Comparative evaluation of deep learning architectures for microbial colony classification in microbiological imaging
Authors: Mohamed Abdelmoaty Ahmed, Rawaf Alenazy, Ahmed AbdelMoety, Hend A. Kamel, Shokri M. Shafik
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundVisual interpretation of microbial colonies on agar plates is an essential task for experienced microbiologists. In high-throughput workflows and large-scale screening, automated image-based methods can provide decision-support capabilities in standardized laboratory imaging setups and consistency. Deep Learning (DL) can support automated identification, but comparative evidence across widely used Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) on the same dataset remains limited.MethodsThis study performed a head-to-head comparison of six CNN architectures—AlexNet, SqueezeNet 1.1, ResNet18, ShuffleNetV2, EfficientNetB0, and MobileNetV2—for microbial colony classification. Using the public AGAR dataset, we processed 5,269 plate images and used the provided annotations to extract 86,045 colony crops from 4,424 countable plates representing five species (Bacillus subtilis (B. subtilis), Escherichia coli (E. coli), Pseudomonas aeruginosa (P. aeruginosa), Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus), and Candida albicans (C. albicans)). Crops were resized to 224 × 224 and augmented (random rotation, horizontal flip, and color jitter). All models were trained under a unified protocol (Adam, learning rate \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$1\times{10}^{-4}$$\end{document}, batch size 32, early stopping) with a split (70% training, 15% validation, 15% testing). Performance was assessed using accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-score.ResultsMobileNetV2 achieved the highest test accuracy (99.40%), followed closely by EfficientNetB0 (99.38%), ShuffleNetV2 (99.19%), and ResNet18 (99.16%), while SqueezeNet 1.1 also showed strong performance (99.09%) and AlexNet yielded the lowest accuracy (98.74%). These findings indicate that, although modern lightweight architectures performed best, ResNet18 and SqueezeNet 1.1 remained highly competitive under the unified evaluation protocol.ConclusionModern CNN architectures, especially MobileNetV2 and EfficientNetB0, offer accurate microbial colony classification on standardized colony crops. Introduction

Analysis of microbial colonies in agar plates is essential in microbiology because it allows the isolation, recognition, and counting of microorganisms in a wide variety of habitats. The method forms the basis of many fields of investigation since researchers can visually examine the morphology of colonies, and growth changes on hard media [1, 2]. Determining the presence and type of pathogenic organisms that cause infection is particularly essential with the help of microbial colony identification in clinical diagnostics [3, 4]. Agar plate analysis is a popular technique in isolation, recognition and quantification of microorganisms in clinical diagnostics and laboratory procedure [5, 6]. Microbial counts play a critical role in the food safety and pharmaceutical industries in the quality control of products, which means that consumables and drugs are of the necessary quality in terms of safety [7]. Nevertheless, the traditional/manual classification of microbial colonies is tedious, subjective, and prone to inconsistency. This kind of manual counting and identification relies on expert interpretation that can cause variability between operators and delay in the turnaround time [8–10]. These constraints have hastened the transition to automated and Deep Learning (DL) based methods for classification and enumeration of colonies.

Classification of a microbial colony based on the traditional methods, which laid the basis for microbiology, has some specific critical challenges that make them ineffective in modern high-throughput and accuracy-demanding laboratory workflows. Its major drawback is the merit of its colony morphology variability. Environmental, media, and biological factors contribute to the inter-colonial variability of shape, size, texture, and pigmentation of colonies, even for different strains of the same species. The range of morphology increases the visual assessment complexity, has high expert level dependency on morphological taxonomy for sub-familial classification, thus manual classification is subjective and error-prone [11–13]. Also, large scale or high throughput analysis cannot be done using traditional techniques. Manual counts and codification are time consuming, laborious and unable to meet the requirements of the current microbiological processes, where the thousands of samples may have to be assayed (or counted) per day [14–16]. These constraints have led to the pressing necessity of automation in order to enhance classification accuracy, reproducibility and efficiency of operation. The latest developments in DL and image processing have demonstrated high potential to address these limitations, because they will facilitate colony detection and classification with high precision, reduced human intervention, and reduce processing time [17–19].

DL and specifically Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) have transformed image analysis in many areas, such as medical imaging, pattern recognition, and biological classification. CNNs are particularly effective in high-dimensional and complex visual data (hierarchical feature extraction) and are especially effective compared to more traditional machine learning models in which features are created manually [19, 20]. In microbiology, CNNs have been used effectively to automate the process of detecting and classifying bacterial colonies, and this has aided in curbing manual labor and subjectivity. The DL methods, in addition to improving the accuracy of classification, can be used to automatically extract informative visual features that the traditional algorithms may neglect [21, 22]. This inherent ability of CNNs allows systems to be scalable for several morphologies present in each type of microbes, and for various imaging conditions.

DL has been applied to classify microbial colonies in numerous studies, with some examples leveraging object detection models like You Only Look Once (YOLO), Cascade Region-Based CNN, and Swin Transformers onto AGAR image datasets [6, 22, 23]. These studies confirm the applicability of DL to colony identification, yet many are focused on building or validating an individual model or architecture. Even though there are some comparative benchmarks, there are limited general like-to-like assessments of multiple CNN architectures on similar protocols, which decreases its applicability to deployment scenarios [12, 24].

This study compares six CNN models (AlexNet, SqueezeNet 1.1, ResNet18, ShuffleNetV2, EfficientNetB0, and MobileNetV2) on the AGAR dataset with five classes of microbial colonies under a standard training scheme. The accuracy, class-wise precision/recall/F1-score, and confusion matrix analysis are used to measure performance so that the model used when rolling out the implementation of microbiology workflows can be selected based on its performance.

Research question

Can image-based DL reliably classify five microbial species from agar-plate colony images, and which architecture—AlexNet, SqueezeNet 1.1, ResNet18, ShuffleNetV2, EfficientNetB0, or MobileNetV2—performs best under a unified protocol on the AGAR dataset?

Recent studies show that DL can automate microbial colony analysis with strong performance across classification, detection (e.g., YOLO-based pipelines), segmentation (e.g., U-Net for counting), and data-efficient training using synthetic augmentation and few-shot learning, as summarized in Table 1. However, many works are constrained by small or custom datasets, narrow species scope, or evaluation of only a single architecture, which makes fair cross-study comparison difficult. Summary of recent studies on DL–based microbial colony analysis

As summarized in Table 1, recent studies have applied DL to multiple stages of agar-plate analysis, including colony classification, detection, and segmentation. Several works report strong performance in controlled settings; for example, CNN-based classification on simplified or low-resolution inputs has been reported to exceed 90–98% accuracy for limited label sets. Two-stage pipelines that combine object detection with transfer learning also achieve promising detection performance (e.g., mAP) alongside high downstream classification accuracy. Other studies highlight data efficiency. They show that using synthetic augmentation and few-shot strategies can boost model performance when labeled data are limited. Overall, these findings suggest that colony morphology holds distinguishing signals that modern CNN architectures can take advantage of under suitable imaging and training conditions.

However, Table 1 also highlights recurring limitations that hinder fair cross-study comparison. Many studies rely on small or institution-specific datasets, narrow problem formulations (e.g., binary tasks or Gram-level grouping), single-species evaluations, or specialized imaging hardware, limiting transferability to routine laboratory workflows. Even when multiple models are compared, differences in preprocessing, resolution, augmentation, and data splits make it difficult to separate the effects of architecture choice from experimental setup. These issues motivate a like-for-like benchmark in which multiple widely used CNN architectures are evaluated under a unified protocol on a public dataset. Accordingly, we compare AlexNet, SqueezeNet 1.1, ResNet18, ShuffleNetV2, EfficientNetB0, and MobileNetV2 using a standardized pipeline on the AGAR dataset.

Methodology

Dataset presentation

The dataset used in this study comprises annotated agar plate images containing microbial colonies. From these plates, individual colony crops were extracted, resized, and augmented to form a dataset suitable for training and evaluating CNN architectures. The dataset is publicly available as the AGAR microbial colony dataset and has been specifically designed to support DL–based detection and classification tasks [6].

The dataset was made up of 5,269 AGAR plate images that were split into three categories: empty (262), countable (4,424), and uncountable (583). Only countable plates, which contain expert-provided colony bounding boxes and species labels, were used for colony cropping and downstream species-level classification. Plates labeled as uncountable or empty do not include colony-level annotations and were therefore excluded from the colony-crop classification task. Empty and uncountable plates are described to contextualize the AGAR dataset and to clarify the scope and limitations of the colony-crop classification task, not because they contribute training samples. Six DL architectures, AlexNet, SqueezeNet 1.1, ResNet18, ShuffleNetV2, EfficientNetB0, and MobileNetV2 were compared on colony crops, mostly sampled by the countable category.

After extracting colony crops from the countable plates only, the colony-crop dataset was stratified by species and split into training, validation, and test sets (70%/15%/15%) to preserve class proportions. As shown in Fig. 1, 70% of the samples constituted a training set, and 15% each was reserved for validation and testing. This split guarantees enough data for model training, and leaves an unbiased subset of the data for hyperparameter tuning and final testing.

Image quality, agar medium, and colony definition

Image quality was assessed indirectly during the AGAR dataset curation process. In the original AGAR dataset, expert microbiologists visually checked and classified agar plate images as countable, uncountable, or empty. Only countable plates, which had distinct and visually separate colonies suitable for manual labeling, were labeled with bounding boxes and species information. These plates were included in the colony-level analysis. Plates that showed excessive colony overlap, blurred boundaries, or insufficient visual separability were labeled as uncountable and excluded. As a result, only images meeting the visual quality standards defined during dataset preparation were used [6].

All microbial cultures in the AGAR dataset were grown on Trypticase Soy Agar (TSA) [6]. This is a non-selective and commonly used general-purpose culture medium. TSA offers a clear and even background, which helps in visualizing the shape, color, and edges of bacterial and fungal colonies [6].

Imaging conditions and subset used

Although the AGAR dataset contains images captured under diverse imaging conditions (including different lighting setups and camera resolutions), this study uses only the low-resolution subset available in the AGAR dataset folder. Specifically, the low-resolution images used in this work correspond to the provided plate categories: Empty (sample IDs 12732–12993), Countable (12994–17417), and Uncountable (17418–18000), where only the countable plates were used for colony-crop extraction and classification [6].

Colony definition and classification input

In this study, each input sample to the classification model corresponds to a single microbial colony instance defined by the expert-provided bounding box and species label from the AGAR “countable” category [6]. Crops were extracted on a per-bounding-box basis; hence, the classification task operates on single-colony images rather than whole-plate images. Plates labeled as “empty” or “uncountable” in AGAR do not include colony-level annotations and were not used for colony-crop classification [6].

All colony crops extracted from bounding boxes of varying sizes and aspect ratios were resized directly to 224 × 224 pixels using “torchvision.transforms.Resize((224,224))” without padding; thus, smaller crops were upsampled, larger crops downsampled, and non-square crops were geometrically transformed to a square input.

No explicit size threshold in millimeters

The AGAR annotations provide colony locations as bounding boxes in image coordinates [6]. Since physical colony diameter in millimeters is not provided in the dataset metadata, this study did not impose an explicit millimeter-based size threshold (e.g., > 3 mm). Instead, the inclusion of colonies follows the AGAR annotation protocol, where colonies annotated by microbiologists on countable plates constitute the set of instances used for training and evaluation [6].

Colony shape and adjacency

Colony morphology changes naturally depending on the species and culture conditions. For example, some colonies are small and dense, while others are larger and translucent. Instead of leaving out irregular morphologies, this study trained the model using the morphological diversity found in AGAR [6]. In cases of close or adjacent colonies, the expert bounding boxes define the target instance; nevertheless, neighboring growth may partially appear near crop boundaries. To reduce bias toward specific orientations or simple geometric cues, geometric augmentation (e.g., rotations/flips) was applied during training.

Given that all crops were resized to 224 × 224 pixels without aspect-ratio preservation, we analyzed the original bounding-box size distribution across species to assess potential resizing-related artifacts.

Colony size distribution and resizing considerations

The initial colony bounding-box size distributions were analyzed across species to measure potential effects of resizing all crops to 224 × 224 pixels without aspect-ratio preservation. Figure 6 shows the per-species colony size distribution expressed as equivalent diameter, indicating clear size differences across classes, with S. aureus and C. albicans generally smaller than E. coli, P. aeruginosa, and B. subtilis. Figures 7 and 8 report the corresponding height and width distributions, which are largely similar within each species, suggesting that most bounding boxes are approximately square and thus limiting geometric distortion from direct resizing. However, smaller colonies undergo greater relative upsampling, so interpolation effects may differ systematically across species; this is acknowledged as a preprocessing limitation.

Workflow overview

The flowchart shown in Fig. 9 illustrates the pipeline followed in this study. It starts with the preparation of data, loading the AGAR dataset (JSON annotations and plate images), and performing preprocessing on the data, which includes cropping colonies and preprocessing transforms, including resizing, flipping, rotating, and normalization. The dataset is further divided into training, validation, and test sets with visualizations of species distribution, dataset splits, colony crops, and annotated plates.Fig. 9Flowchart of the comparative DL pipeline for microbial colony classification on agar plates

Flowchart of the comparative DL pipeline for microbial colony classification on agar plates

Then, in the model selection process, six architectures — AlexNet, SqueezeNet 1.1, ResNet-18, ShuffleNetV2, EfficientNetB0, and MobileNetV2 — are tested with a final classification layer modified to recognize five microbial classes. All models are trained using “CrossEntropyLoss” and the Adam optimizer (lr = 1e-4), with looped forward-backward iterations, validation, and early stopping to reload the optimal weights.

Finally, the evaluation and output stage saves the best model, performs testing on the test set, and reports performance parameters (Accuracy, Precision, Recall, F1-score). The loss and accuracy curves, along with the confusion matrix, are plotted to analyze the results.

Early stopping was based on validation loss, with training terminated if no improvement was observed for five consecutive epochs, and the model weights from the best validation-loss epoch were restored.

Each model was trained once under a fixed random seed to ensure deterministic comparison; reported metrics correspond to a single training run per architecture. Training time and inference latency were not the focus of this study and are therefore not reported.

All models were initialized using their standard pretrained weights and fine-tuned for the five-class colony classification task by replacing the final classification layer.

Training of all six CNN architectures was performed using the same hyperparameter configuration to ensure a fair comparison. The dataset was divided into training (70%), validation (15%), and testing (15%) subsets. A batch size of 32 was used with up to 25 epochs, and a fixed random seed (42) was applied to support reproducibility. The complete set of training hyperparameters used across all models is summarized in Table 3.

The six CNN architectures were chosen to showcase a range of model complexity and computing cost. This goes from early deep CNNs, like AlexNet, to more recent lightweight and efficiency-focused models, such as ShuffleNetV2, EfficientNetB0, and MobileNetV2. This choice allows for a clear assessment of how changes in architecture affect classification accuracy, convergence behavior, and their use in resource-limited laboratory settings.

Deep learning architecture descriptions

The six CNN architectures were chosen to create a compact and representative benchmark across different generations of network design. This category comprises the early high-capacity model AlexNet as well as more recent models like SqueezeNet 1.1, ShuffleNetV2, EfficientNetB0, and MobileNetV2 that prioritize efficiency for deployment under constrained computing constraints. Additionally, it has ResNet18, a popular residual model with good generalization and stable optimization. This selection allows for a fair and clear comparison under a single training method without making the study too extensive. The reasons for choosing each architecture and their intended benefits are outlined in Table 4.

The six CNN architectures evaluated in this study include AlexNet [34, 35], SqueezeNet 1.1 [36], ResNet18 [37], ShuffleNetV2 [38], EfficientNetB0 [39], and MobileNetV2 [40], representing different generations of CNN design with varying depth, parameter effectiveness, and computing complexity.

Evaluation metrics

To evaluate the performance of DL structures in classifying microbial colonies, various popular evaluation measures were employed. These are accuracy, precision, recall, and the F1-score, which are obtained based on the confusion matrix. Accuracy gives the general proportion of the correct classification of colonies as in Eq. (1), whereas precision gives the proportion of true positive predictions among all the positive prediction as in Eq. (2). As mentioned, recall is also called sensitivity, and it is the ratio of the number of correct positive samples to the total number of actually positive samples as expressed as Eq. (3). Since accuracy and recall are often a tradeoff the F1-score of the results in Eq. (4) is considered as harmonic mean of the two to strike a balance. As part of the training, the cross-entropy loss optimization is guided by the loss form described in Eq. (5), where model predictions that are inaccurate are penalized by comparing the model’s predicted class probabilities with the ground-truth labels. Multi-class classification: To calculate the average metrics (mean) across multiple classes, two methods are employed: macro-averaging (unweighted mean across classes) and weighted-averaging (class-frequency weighted mean). Impact of dataset splitting strategy and data leakage analysis

To evaluate how dataset partitioning affects model performance, we compared the baseline colony-level split with a plate-level stratified split. As shown in Table 14, the colony-level split resulted in substantial plate overlap between subsets, with 97.46% of test plates also appearing in the training and/or validation sets, indicating strong inter-subset dependency. In contrast, the plate-level split completely eliminated overlap (0%), ensuring full independence between train, validation, and test partitions. Under this corrected evaluation protocol, MobileNetV2 achieved an overall test accuracy of 99.07%, with consistently high class-wise precision, recall, and F1-scores across all species as shown in Table 15. Compared with the previously reported colony-level split accuracy of 99.40%, this represents a modest reduction of 0.33% points, reflecting a more realistic estimate of generalization performance under plate-level independence. The training and validation curves in Fig. 14(a) and Fig. 14(b) demonstrate stable convergence and minimal overfitting under the plate-level split, while the confusion matrix in Fig. 15 shows limited misclassification, with most errors occurring between E. coli and P. aeruginosa.

Discussion

Summary of key findings

This study conducted a head-to-head controlled comparison of six CNN architectures (AlexNet, SqueezeNet 1.1, ResNet18, ShuffleNetV2, EfficientNetB0, and MobileNetV2) to classify colonies at the colony level based on microbial species using cropped colonies of the AGAR dataset. All models performed well on the test (98.74–99.40% accuracy) across 86,045 colony crops of five species, with MobileNetV2 having the highest test accuracy overall (99.40%) and EfficientNetB0 right behind (99.38%). The architectural improvements aligned with the architectural changes of early CNNs (AlexNet) to more modern efficiency-focused architectures (ShuffleNetV2, EfficientNetB0, MobileNetV2). The metrics by class were consistently high and confusion matrices showed that the remaining errors were localized to a few biologically possible confusions (especially between E. coli and P. aeruginosa).

Comparison with MALDI-TOF MS

Microbial colony classification by DL has recently been proposed as a potent alternative to traditional Matrix-Assisted Laser Desorption/Ionization Time-of-Flight Mass Spectrometry (MALDI-TOF MS) as a tool in diagnostic microbiology. MALDI-TOF MS is considered the gold standard for rapid species identification due to its speed and cost-effectiveness [42]. Biological interpretation of misclassifications

The most common misidentifications were between E. coli and P. aeruginosa, which is a biologically plausible finding considering that they have partially overlapping colony morphologies under TSA conditions. The two species are capable of forming semi-translucent colonies that have diffuse and irregular edges and similar diameters especially during early growth phases, which may cause confusion to automated image-based classification systems [45]. P. aeruginosa is also characterized by heterogeneity of colonies because of biofilm-related features and quorum-sensing control, resulting in wrinkled or mucoid phenotypes that may be visually indistinguishable to E. coli when grown on homogenous media [46, 47].

The minor differences in translucency and edge diffusion are in line with the biochemical and physiological resemblances in their outer membrane structure and nutrient uptake on TSA media [48]. Clinically, while these misclassifications are unlikely to have major diagnostic consequences when combined with confirmatory biochemical or genetic testing, they underscore the challenge of relying solely on morphological features in automated diagnostic pipelines—especially in mixed or early-growth cultures [13].

Future directions

Despite the fact that this study has shown that state-of-the-art CNNs achieved near-ceiling performance under this controlled experimental setting in microbial colony classification at the colony-crop level, there are a number of research avenues that can be pursued to improve clinical applicability, robustness, and real-world use.

First, the work of the future should build on the existing classification-only model and create a complete end-to-end pipeline, incorporating colony detection, segmentation, and classification. In practice in the laboratory, colonies have to be initially localized on full agar plates in difficult conditions including overlapping growth, uneven illumination, glare and condensation artifacts. The addition of detection and segmentation models, and subsequent colony-crop classification would be a more realistic measure of system-level performance and would be more representative of the normal diagnostic processes.

Second, to determine generalizability, external validation in various laboratories, imaging devices, and culture conditions is needed. The AGAR dataset is a carefully selected reference; nevertheless, actual microbiology laboratories vary in incubation duration, imaging equipment, agar media, and handling protocols. It would be useful to measure domain shift by testing trained models on datasets gathered independently by other institutions to ensure their robustness is not limited to a single dataset.

Third, future work should include more species to better show clinical diversity. This category includes mixed cultures and rare pathogens. It should also explore multi-label and hierarchical classification methods. For instance, taxonomic inference can progress from Gram status to genus and then to species-level. Fourth, there should be confidence-aware modeling techniques. These methods include probability calibration, abstention, and out-of-distribution detection to indicate suspicious predictions to an expert. Fifth, lightweight architectures such as MobileNetV2 and EfficientNetB0 should be tested on common hardware platforms using deployment-oriented studies. The testing must be based on the inference latency, memory usage, power consumption, and real-time throughput. Finally, multimodal approaches could combine colony morphology with other diagnostic methods. For example, they could include temporal growth dynamics, spectroscopic signatures, or genomic profiling. This may improve classification robustness and decrease dependence on visual features alone.

Together, these directions position the present work as a foundational classification category within a broader intelligent microbiology pipeline, supporting the gradual transition from manual inspection toward reliable, AI-assisted diagnostic systems.

Conclusion

This study presented a systematic, head-to-head comparison of six DL architectures—AlexNet, SqueezeNet 1.1, ResNet18, ShuffleNetV2, EfficientNetB0, and MobileNetV2—for microbial colony species classification at the colony-crop level using a large, publicly available AGAR dataset comprising 86,045 annotated colony images from five microbial species [6]. All evaluated architectures demonstrated high classification performance with modern lightweight architectures obtaining the best results under a single training and evaluation protocol.

The experimental findings show that MobileNetV2 and EfficientNetB0 consistently achieved the highest overall accuracy (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\approx\:\mathbf{99.40}{\%}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\approx\:\mathbf{99.38}{\%}$$\end{document}, respectively), while maintaining strong class-wise precision, recall, and F1-scores across all species. In contrast, earlier architectures such as AlexNet and SqueezeNet 1.1, although still competitive, exhibited slightly lower accuracy and slower convergence. These results show how architectural design choices, such as depthwise separable convolutions and efficient feature reuse, affect performance while lowering computational costs.

The results of this study should be understood within the specific task scope. The evaluation was performed on pre-extracted single-colony crops derived from microbiologist-annotated, countable agar plates, which represents a controlled classification setting rather than a full plate-level diagnostic workflow. Therefore, while the reported accuracies show the strong ability of CNN-based classifiers to distinguish between different groups, they do not directly reflect overall performance in real-world lab settings. In these environments, challenges like colony detection, segmentation, overlapping growth, and imaging artifacts still play a major role. Based on this, the present study does not purport to be superior to human specialists or existing commercial automated systems. Rather, it shows that CNN architectures are capable of learning species-specific morphological patterns with high reliability in scenarios where clear colony instances are given. These findings support the role of CNN-based classifiers as potential decision-support components within broader automated microbiology pipelines, rather than as standalone replacements for manual review at this stage.

The main message that can be brought out of this work is that the modern lightweight CNN models, especially MobileNetV2 and EfficientNetB0, provide a good trade-off between classification efficiency and the ability to classify the colonies at the colony-level microbial classification. This makes them suitable candidates for integration into resource-constrained environments, such as embedded laboratory instruments or edge-based analysis systems, provided they are coupled with robust colony detection and plate-level processing categories.
