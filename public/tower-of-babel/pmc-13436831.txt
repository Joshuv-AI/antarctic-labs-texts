Mothers’ intentions for adolescent sons’ human papillomavirus vaccination in Japan: Psychosocial factors and relative influences across intention groups
Authors: Mei Ueda, Miwa Imai, Noriko Yamamoto, Mika Kawai, Yukie Kameda
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAs human papillomavirus (HPV) vaccination for men remains non-routine in Japan, this study aimed to explore psychosocial factors associated with mothers’ intentions to vaccinate their adolescent sons, and compared the strength of these correlates across intention groups. A web-based cross-sectional survey targeted mothers with boys aged 11–16 years in Japan. Participants were classified by intention into accepting (strongly/relatively agree), undecided (unknown), and refusing (strongly/relatively disagree) groups. Binary logistic regression compared the accepting versus undecided groups and accepting versus refusing groups. Among 301 mothers, 22.6% were accepting, 57.5% undecided, and 19.9% refusing. Normative beliefs exhibited the strongest association, with the highest odds ratio (OR), when comparing accepting versus undecided (OR = 12.63, 95% CI 6.03–26.47) and accepting versus refusing groups (OR = 26.72, 95% CI 9.69–73.74). This was followed by perceived importance of prevention, best-friend influence, anticipatory regret, and perceived susceptibility. Associations were generally stronger in comparisons between accepting and refusing groups than between accepting and undecided groups. The undecided group reported greater perceived logistical hassle. The refusing group was less likely to have completed children’s routine childhood vaccinations before entering elementary school or eldest son’s influenza vaccination and was less influenced by municipal notifications or doctors’ recommendations. Tailored interventions are needed, including those enhancing subjective norms, perceived prevention value, and risk communication for undecided and refusing mothers; providing logistical support for undecided mothers; and implementing multi‑channel, trust‑building communication strategies for refusing mothers. Japan should prioritize boys’ HPV vaccination through multifaceted strategies addressing these psychosocial barriers.KEYWORDS: HPV vaccine, adolescent boys, mothers, intentions, psychosocial factors, Japan

Introduction

Human papillomavirus (HPV) causes cervical cancer in women and oropharyngeal cancer, anal cancer, and condyloma acuminatum in both genders.1–3 In Japan, the incidence and mortality of cervical cancer continue to increase, particularly among women in their 20s and 30s; the incidence of oropharyngeal cancer in both genders, as well as the mortality of anal cancer and incidence of condyloma acuminatum among men, have also increased.4–6 Globally, approximately one in three men aged over 15 years is infected with genital HPV.7 In Japan, among men aged 20–95 years visiting urology clinics, 25% had any HPV infection and 13% had high-risk HPV.8 Therefore, preventing HPV-related diseases has become increasingly important regardless of gender.

HPV is a sexually transmitted infection, and vaccination during adolescence is an effective preventive measure against HPV-related diseases.1,3 In Japan, HPV vaccination is implemented as a routine, publicly funded immunization for girls in school grades corresponding to the last year of elementary school through the first year of senior high school, using a standard three‑dose schedule at medical institutions; a two‑dose schedule permitted for younger age groups under current recommendations.9 Thus, HPV vaccination for girls began in December 2009 and became a national routine vaccination program in April 2013.9 However, active government recommendations were suspended in June 2013 following reports of adverse events,9 leading to a sharp decline in the vaccination rate to below 1%.10,11 Following reassessments confirming the vaccine’s effectiveness and safety,12,13 active government recommendations resumed in April 2022 after a nine-year suspension.9,14 Nevertheless, the cumulative first-dose vaccination rate among girls born during fiscal years (FYs) 2008–2012 remained low (4.4–49.9%) as of early 2024,15 far below the international standards of 90%.3 Meanwhile, HPV vaccination has been approved for males aged 9 years and older since December 2020.9 In contrast to the routine, publicly funded program for girls, HPV vaccination for boys has not been included in the national routine schedule and is provided as an optional vaccination; it generally follows the same three‑dose (or age‑dependent two‑dose) regimen at medical institutions, with out‑of‑pocket payment required.9 As of October 2024, 45 municipalities offered full or partial subsidies for boys’ vaccination, typically targeting those around 11–16 years of age.9 Therefore, no national data are yet available on HPV vaccination rates among boys. Promoting HPV vaccination among men is essential to prevent HPV-related diseases and strengthen herd immunity, considering the current low vaccination rate among women.3,14,16,17 In fact, many Western countries have achieved HPV vaccination rates of 60–80% in both genders.18,19

In Japan, parental or guardian consent is required for HPV vaccination in individuals under 16 years.20 Parents significantly contribute to decision-making regarding HPV vaccination.21 Mothers often play a central role in the decision-making process, reflecting traditional gender roles in household responsibilities.22 Previous domestic studies have identified psychosocial factors influencing mothers’ intentions to vaccinate their adolescent daughters, including perceived vaccine benefits, recommendations from healthcare professionals (HCPs), best-friend effect, and municipal notifications.23,24 The “best-friend effect” refers to the phenomenon through which vaccination uptake or recommendation by a daughter’s close friend positively shapes the mother’s perception of HPV vaccination and increases willingness to vaccinate, reflecting trusted peer influence rather than negative peer pressure.24 Previous domestic studies have identified that interventions targeting mothers can increase vaccination rates among daughters.20,25 Previous foreign studies have also identified various psychosocial factors of parents’ or guardians’ intentions to vaccinate their adolescent sons against HPV, including mothers’ perceived susceptibility, perceived vaccine benefits, recommendations from HCPs, and concerns about safety and cost; this generally indicates that many of these correlates are similar to those reported for mothers’ intentions to vaccinate their daughters.26–36 However, these findings may not be directly generalizable to Japan. In Japan, the HPV vaccination is implemented as a routine, publicly funded program only for girls, whereas boys receive HPV vaccination as a voluntary, self-paid service, and both girls and boys are vaccinated individually in clinic-based instead of school-based settings. Moreover, the suspension of active governmental recommendations for girls in 2013, following intensive media coverage of alleged adverse events, has led to a prolonged loss of public trust in HPV vaccination. In addition, public and policy discourse has focused mainly on cervical cancer prevention among girls, so the benefits of HPV vaccination for boys and their own cancer risk have been much less emphasized. Within this gender-skewed vaccination program and information environment, the psychosocial patterns shaping Japanese mothers’ intentions to vaccinate their sons may differ from those reported in international studies, highlighting the need for Japan-specific evidence on male-focused HPV vaccination. A recent domestic study by Oka et al.,37 conducted before the FY2022 resumption of active HPV vaccination recommendations, revealed that insufficient knowledge about HPV infection and low perceived risk were negatively associated with mothers’ intentions to vaccinate their children (daughters and/or sons). However, further investigation is needed to clarify the factors associated with mothers’ intentions for their sons’ HPV vaccination in Japan. Machida and Inoue38 examined Japanese women aged 18–25 years who had not received HPV catch-up vaccination and identified three clusters: acceptance (high intention), neutral, and refusal (low intention) groups. Similarly, Oka et al.37 classified mothers’ intentions to vaccinate their children into the agree, neutral, and disagree groups but compared only the agree group with the others without detailing their characteristics. Therefore, most domestic studies focused primarily on adolescent daughters and their mothers, while studies examining mothers’ intentions to vaccinate their adolescent sons remain limited in Japan.

This study aimed to address these gaps by exploring psychosocial factors associated with mothers’ intentions for their sons’ HPV vaccination in Japan and assessing their relative influences in comparisons between accepting and undecided mothers and between accepting and refusing mothers. The results offer evidence to guide targeted interventions to promote boys’ HPV vaccination and strengthen herd immunity in Japan.

Methods

Study design

This study used data from mothers who completed a web-based, self-administered, cross-sectional survey on October 4–11, 2024. The survey targeted parents with adolescent sons in Japan as part of the “HPV-Related Disease Prevention Project.”

Initially, we recruited mothers regardless of their sons’ HPV vaccination status. However, mothers whose eldest sons were already HPV vaccinated (n = 2) were excluded from the present analyses, because the study focused on mothers of unvaccinated eldest sons while examining psychosocial factors associated with vaccination intentions.

The sample size was calculated using G*Power (Ver. 3.1.9.6). Following Oka et al.,37 where the odds ratios (ORs) ranged from 1.9 to 2.0, we assumed the upper limit (OR = 2.0) for this analysis. Assuming Pr (Y = 1|X = 1) = 0.5 under the null hypothesis, α = .05, and power (1-β) = 0.80, the required total sample size was 276. Accounting for attrition and ineligible responses, we targeted 300 mothers (150 with HPV vaccination-eligible daughters born during FY1994–2012 and 150 without).

Survey methods

The online survey was conducted by INTAGE Corporation, Inc., a large commercial survey company that uses its proprietary online panel of voluntarily registered members. Panel members are recruited through web-based advertisements and partner websites and provide sociodemographic information (e.g., gender, age, employment status, annual income, marital status, and residential area) during registration. INTAGE Corporation, Inc. solely managed all personally identifiable information, with all study data anonymized.

For the pre-screening survey conducted from October 4–7, 2024, the company identified mothers aged 30–59 years who met eligibility criteria 1, 2 and 5 from its panel database and then invited them to participate. Within this eligible pool, participants were selected using quota sampling by age group and residential region to approximate the national distribution of mothers in Japan. Participants were informed of the study’s purpose, assured anonymity, and requested to provide informed consent before responding.

At least 1,000 mothers per group (with and without HPV vaccination-eligible daughters) who met eligibility criteria 3–4 received links to the main “Survey on Children’s Health” from October 9–11, 2024. Participants could respond only once and discontinue or withdraw before submission. Participants received a small incentive (goods, e-money, or donations) through the company’s point system. Recruitment ended upon receiving at least 150 valid responses per group (with/without HPV vaccination-eligible daughters; data quality criteria 1–2). Following this, we excluded mothers whose eldest sons were already HPV vaccinated and those who selected the “prefer not to answer” response for key variables (data quality Criterion 3).

Survey contents

The questionnaire items were developed based on previous studies on parental intentions regarding HPV vaccination,23,24,26–37,39–43 and incorporated constructs from the health belief model and theory of planned behavior.44 All survey items were organized into the following categories: mothers’ intentions, participants’ characteristics, knowledge, attitudes, social cognition, and triggers. The primary outcome, mothers’ intentions to vaccinate their eldest son against HPV, was assessed with a single item specifically developed for this study.23,26,27,30,33,34,37,42 All items, including those evaluating knowledge, attitudes, social cognition, and triggers, were adopted from previous studies with minor modifications to adapt to and reflect the Japanese context and HPV vaccination for boys. To ensure that the questionnaire items were easy to understand and answer, we conducted a pretest with 12 individuals prior to the main survey and revised the phrasing of the questions based on their feedback. The full set of items has not yet undergone separate psychometric validation as an independent scale.

Mothers’ intentions

Participants were asked to respond to the statement, “I would like to vaccinate my eldest son against HPV in the future,” using only one of the following options (on a five-point Likert scale): “strongly agree,” “relatively agree,” “unknown,” “relatively disagree,” and “strongly disagree.” Participants were classified by intention: accepting (strongly/relatively agree), undecided (unknown), and refusing (strongly/relatively disagree).

Demographic information was collected (Table 1), and the eldest son’s age in FY2024 was calculated based on his birth FY. Daughters’ HPV vaccination status was also sought from mothers with daughters meeting the eligibility criteria. According to the participants’ reported residential areas, official websites of each municipality were reviewed in October 2024 to determine whether an HPV vaccination subsidy was available for boys of the target age.

Attitudes and social cognition

Three items assessed attitudes toward HPV-related diseases, seven assessed attitudes toward HPV vaccination, and four assessed social cognition (Table 2). Participants selected only one of the following options (five-point Likert scale): “strongly agree” (5 points), “agree” (4 points), “undecided” (3 points), “disagree” (2 points), and “strongly disagree” (1 point). Higher scores indicated stronger perceptions.

Triggers

The following multiple-choice questions were asked: “By whom would you need to be recommended to vaccinate your son against HPV?” (eight sources; Table 2); and “Based on a recommendation from which media source would you decide to vaccinate your son against HPV?” (six media sources; Table 2). Participants selected one of two options for each item: “applicable” or “not applicable.”

Statistical analyses

Categorical variables were presented as absolute numbers and percentages, and continuous variables were presented as medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs). To identify psychosocial factors associated with mothers’ intentions and facilitate the transition from indecision or refusal to acceptance, univariate analyses were performed comparing the accepting group with the undecided group and the accepting group with the refusing group, respectively. Between-group differences were examined using the chi-square or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables and the Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables, with the significance level set at .025 after Bonferroni correction. Variables found to be significant in univariate analyses were tested for multicollinearity using Spearman’s rank correlation. Binary logistic regression models were constructed to compare the accepting group with the undecided group and with the refusing group. In this study, considering the strong correlations among psychosocial variables and the risk of multicollinearity, we conducted a series of multivariable binary logistic regression analyses using four-variable models, wherein mother’s age, presence of daughters, and municipal subsidy availability were included as fixed covariates, and one psychosocial variable was added at a time. These covariates were selected based on previous studies suggesting that they may influence parental intentions regarding their children’s HPV vaccination.45,46 Each psychosocial variable that was statistically significant in the univariate analysis and did not exhibit multicollinearity was then individually entered into the adjusted models. ORs with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported, with the significance level set at .05. In the sub-analysis limited to mothers with HPV vaccination-eligible daughters, a chi-square test examined the association between daughters’ HPV vaccination status and mothers’ intentions to vaccinate their eldest son against HPV. Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 29 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Ethical considerations

The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Ishikawa Prefectural Nursing University on August 19, 2024 (Approval No. Kan 2024–51-3) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, Ethical Guidelines for Life Science and Medical Research Involving Human Subjects, and institutional privacy policy. All participants provided online informed consent prior to responding to the survey.

Results

Participants

Figure 1 shows the study flow of participant selection from the initial panel registry and pre-screening to the main survey and final analysis (n = 301). Of the 8,555 panel members invited to respond to the online survey, 305 mothers provided valid responses that met the initial eligibility and data quality criteria. After applying additional exclusion criteria, 301 mothers were included in the final analysis (150 with HPV vaccination-eligible daughters [49.8%] and 151 without [50.2%]).

Mothers’ intentions

Participants were classified as accepting (22.6%), undecided (57.5%), or refusing (19.9%) (Figure 1).

Demographic characteristics

Table 1 delineates the participants’ characteristics. The median age of mothers was 46 years (IQR: 42–50) and that of their eldest sons was 14 years (IQR: 13–15). Among all participants, 71 (23.6%) were regular employees, 215 (71.4%) had received higher education, 61 (20.3%) had an annual individual income exceeding 2 million yen, and 73 (24.3%) had an annual household income exceeding 7 million yen. Participants were distributed across all 47 prefectures of Japan, and 21 (7.0%) lived in municipalities that offered HPV vaccination subsidies.

Factors associated with mothers’ intentions

Univariate analyses

Univariate associations are presented in Tables 1–2. Compared with both the undecided and refusing groups, the accepting group demonstrated higher median knowledge scores, greater knowledge regarding HPV infection in men, vaccination available for men, and vaccine effectiveness. In addition, they exhibited higher perceived susceptibility, perceived medical severity, and perceived social severity toward HPV-related diseases, as well as higher perceived benefits of HPV vaccination, including the importance of prevention and vaccine effectiveness. Furthermore, the accepting group showed higher normative beliefs, stronger influence of best-friend effects, and higher anticipatory regret. Compared with the undecided group, the accepting group demonstrated greater knowledge regarding HPV-related cancers in men and perceived fewer barriers, particularly regarding hassles in taking sons for vaccination. Compared with the refusing group, the accepting group exhibited a higher proportion of participants with annual individual income exceeding 2 million yen and household income exceeding 7 million yen, and were also more likely to have ensured children’s routine vaccination before enrollment in elementary school and influenza vaccination for eldest sons. Among mothers with HPV vaccination-eligible daughters, daughters’ HPV vaccination rates were higher in the accepting group. In addition, the accepting group demonstrated greater knowledge about HPV infection often being asymptomatic and was more likely to be influenced by recommendations from the son’s family doctor and municipal notifications.

Series of multivariable analyses

We conducted a series of four-variable binary logistic regression models in which mothers’ age, presence of daughters, and municipal subsidy availability were included as fixed covariates and one variable was entered at a time. Adjusted ORs and 95% CIs for each variable are summarized in Tables 3–4. When comparing the accepting group with the undecided and refusing groups, respectively, normative beliefs (OR = 12.63, 95% CI 6.03–26.47; OR = 26.72, 95% CI 9.69–73.74) displayed a particularly strong positive association with acceptance, followed by perceived importance of prevention (OR = 4.78, 95% CI 2.85–8.00; OR = 8.63, 95% CI 4.07–18.30), best-friend effect (OR = 4.11, 95% CI 2.53–6.67; OR = 5.86, 95% CI 2.95–11.62), anticipatory regret (OR = 4.42, 95% CI 2.71–7.23; OR = 5.01, 95% CI 2.80–8.95), and perceived susceptibility (OR = 4.01, 95% CI 2.05–7.86; OR = 9.21, 95% CI 3.57–23.74).

Other psychosocial factors that remained significantly associated with acceptance included perceived vaccine effectiveness and regular employment status. When comparing the accepting group with the undecided group, higher perceived medical severity was additionally associated with acceptance, whereas perceived hassle in taking sons for vaccination was inversely associated with acceptance. When comparing the accepting group with the refusing group, several trigger-related variables were also associated with acceptance, including municipal notifications (OR = 9.33, 95% CI 3.88–22.43), children’s routine vaccination status, recommendations from the son’s family doctor, and eldest son’s influenza vaccination status. Discussion

This study classified mothers into the accepting (22.6%), undecided (57.5%), and refusing (19.9%) intention groups for HPV vaccination for their adolescent sons in Japan. It aimed to explore psychosocial factors associated with mothers’ intentions and compare the strength of these correlates between accepting versus undecided mothers and between accepting versus refusing mothers. Overall, the findings indicate that psychosocial factors play an important role in shaping mothers’ intentions.

Main psychosocial factors influencing acceptance

Normative beliefs exhibited the strongest association, followed by perceived importance of prevention, best‑friend effect, anticipated regret, and perceived susceptibility. This pattern is consistent with previous studies conducted in both Asia and Western countries. In Asian countries, existing literature has highlighted the influence of culture, normative beliefs, and religion on HPV vaccine hesitancy among parents and other populations.47 Previous studies from Western countries have also reported that subjective norms were associated with HPV vaccination intentions and actual vaccination behavior.48,49 Aligned with these findings, this study also confirmed that the HPV vaccination status of sons’ friends positively influenced mothers’ intentions, consistent with a previous foreign study.33 Similarly, a previous domestic study on daughters has reported a “best-friend effect,”24 indicating that, even for sons, peers of the same age and gender may influence mothers’ decisions.

The accepting group also displayed stronger anticipated regret than the other two groups, consistent with previous studies.28,29,34 Anticipated regret is known to be a stronger predictor of vaccination intentions and behaviors other than risk perceptions such as perceived likelihood, severity, or worry.50 Therefore, campaigns that present narratives from men affected by HPV‑related diseases or from partners of women with cervical cancer may enhance normative beliefs and anticipated regret.51

The accepting group also perceived higher susceptibility to HPV‑related diseases than the undecided and refusing groups and greater medical severity of HPV‑related diseases than the undecided group, consistent with previous studies.31,32,34,35,37 Moreover, new characteristics not reported by Oka et al.37 were identified in this study: the accepting group exhibited higher perceptions of importance of prevention, and vaccine effectiveness. Therefore, effective strategies may combine clear risk – benefit communication, peer endorsement messages from other mothers and same‑aged friends, emotionally engaging narratives that elicit empathy and anticipated regret, and interactive support that offers reassurance and opportunities for questions.51

Differences between the undecided and refusing groups

This study identified distinct psychological and behavioral characteristics when comparing the undecided and refusing groups. The undecided group perceived greater logistical hassle in arranging vaccination, consistent with a previous foreign study.52 These situational barriers may be reduced through municipal reminders, school‑based campaigns, and flexible appointment scheduling.51 By contrast, the refusing group showed stronger associations with psychosocial factors than the undecided group and was unlikely to be responsive to municipal notifications, possibly reflecting diminished public trust and persistent distrust following the 2013 suspension.9 For this group, one‑way municipal announcements are insufficient, and communication should instead be channeled through HCPs, schools, and parent peer networks, as well as community events and social media, to enhance credibility and rebuild trust.51,53

In our series of partially adjusted models, employment status also showed a significant association with acceptance. In Japan, non‑regularly employed mothers, who account for a large share of the female workforce, often face irregular working hours, limited opportunities to take leave, and restricted access to reliable health information.54 Therefore, easily understandable online educational materials that can be viewed in a short time, vaccination opportunities available during evenings and weekends, and peer support systems tailored to their needs are needed.51

Persistent concerns across groups

Concerns about cost and adverse reactions were high across all intention groups, with no significant group differences. Financial barriers have been demonstrated to reduce mothers’ intentions to vaccinate their sons in settings without public funding.27,30–32,36 The persistence of concerns about cost in this context emphasizes the need for nationwide subsidies and HCPs to advocate for expanded financial support ensuring equitable access.51 Concern about adverse reactions have been reported to suppress mothers’ intentions to vaccinate their sons against HPV in countries both with and without public HPV vaccination programs.27,28,30,31,34,35,55 This persistent concern possibly reflects the lingering impact of past media reports and limited access to reliable safety data. Rebuilding public trust requires transparent communication of national safety data and empathetic engagement between HCPs and parents.51,56 Providing HCPs with continual training and updated evidence can strengthen their confidence and the persuasiveness of their recommendations.51,56 Encouraging parents whose sons have been vaccinated to share positive experiences may further enhance reassurance and community trust.

The refusing group was also unlikely to respond to recommendations from their son’s family doctor and tended to lack prior vaccinations, including children’s routine vaccination before entering elementary school, eldest son’s influenza vaccination, and daughters’ HPV vaccination, where applicable. These patterns indicate deep‑seated vaccine hesitancy and limited exposure to guidance from HCPs, consistent with previous studies.27,32,52 Therefore, multidisciplinary collaboration among doctors, nurses, and public health nurses is essential to provide consistent messages and repeated, trust‑building contact.51

International comparison of mothers’ intentions

In this study, 22.6% of mothers accepted HPV vaccination for their eldest sons, 57.5% were undecided, and 19.9% refused. Acceptance was lower than the 39.9% reported among mothers with hypothetical sons but higher than the 9.7% reported among mothers with actual sons.37,42 This may partially be attributable to the timing of this survey because it was conducted during policy discussions on HPV vaccination for men9 and expansion of municipal subsidy schemes, potentially raising awareness and perceived feasibility. Internationally, this acceptance level was comparable to those in countries such as China and South Korea, where national routine vaccination program for boys have not been implemented.26,27,36 In Western countries, similar acceptance levels before public subsidy introduction have been followed by substantial increases after implementation of universal or subsidized programs.28,30,32,39,52 Alongside psychosocially informed communication strategies, these comparisons underscore the crucial role of institutional and policy support in promoting HPV vaccination for boys in Japan.

Strengths and limitations

The strength of this study lies in exploring psychosocial factors associated with mothers’ intentions to vaccinate their adolescent sons and comparing the strength of these correlates between mothers who accept vaccination and those who are undecided or refusing. These results offer valuable evidence for developing intention-specific intervention design.

However, several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, selection bias may have occurred because participants were recruited from a voluntary online survey panel, and panel members may differ from mothers who do not join such panels. Although the panel includes mothers from across Japan, these differences may limit the generalizability of our results to the entire population. Second, self-reported measures may be susceptible to recall and social desirability bias. Third, although the questionnaire items were developed or adapted from previous studies, some key constructs (including mothers’ intentions, knowledge, attitudes, social cognition, and triggers) were not fully psychometrically validated; this may limit the precision of their measurement. Fourth, owing to multicollinearity among the psychosocial factors, this study utilized a series of four‑variable models instead of a full multivariable model, and the results represent partially independent effects rather than fully independent effects. Fifth, as this was a cross-sectional web-based survey, causal relationships could not be established. Finally, the characteristics of accepting mothers may not necessarily reflect those of mothers with already vaccinated sons. Future longitudinal studies should address these issues.

Conclusions

This study identified specific psychosocial factors that influence mothers’ intentions for their adolescent sons’ HPV vaccination in Japan, highlighting the central roles of normative beliefs, perceived importance of prevention, best‑friend effect, anticipated regret, and perceived susceptibility. By distinguishing between mothers who were undecided and those who were refusing, the findings reveal that these intention groups face qualitatively different barriers and may require different approaches and support strategies. Therefore, intention‑specific, evidence‑based interventions, which strengthen positive social norms and preventive motivations, address practical barriers for undecided mothers, and build trust and motivation among refusing mothers, are essential. The strategies suggested by this study provide a practical foundation for designing targeted public health communication and policy initiatives to promote boys’ HPV vaccination and contribute to herd immunity in Japan.
