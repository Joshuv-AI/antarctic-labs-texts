On the convergence of the variational quantum eigensolver and quantum optimal control
Marco Wiedmann, Daniel Burgarth, Gunther Dirr, Thomas Schulte-Herbrüggen, Emanuel Malvetti, Christian Arenz

Abstract
When does a variational quantum algorithm converge to a globally optimal problem solution? Despite the large literature around variational approaches to quantum computing, the answer is largely unknown. We address this open question by developing a convergence theory for the variational quantum eigensolver (VQE). By leveraging the terminology of quantum control landscapes, we prove a sufficient criterion that characterizes when convergence to a ground state of a Hamiltonian can be guaranteed for almost all initial parameter settings. More specifically, we show that if (i) a parameterized unitary transformation allows for moving in all tangent-space directions (local surjectivity) in a bounded manner and (ii) the gradient descent used for the parameter update terminates, then the VQE converges to a ground state almost surely. We develop constructions that satisfy both aspects of condition (i) and analyze two commonly employed families of quantum circuit ansätze. Finally, we discuss regularization techniques for guaranteeing gradient descent to terminate, as for condition (ii), and draw connections to the halting problem.

Introduction

The advent of noisy, intermediate-scale quantum devices has sparked an explosion in the development of
variational quantum algorithms (VQAs)

[
1
]
. These algorithms aim to solve an optimization problem by using classical and quantum computing resources in tandem. Namely, a classical optimizer is employed in conjunction with a quantum device to optimize parameters of a quantum circuit to solve a given computational problem. The quantum device is used to assess the quality of the solution at each step while the classical computer provides the parameter update.

VQAs face the challenge that the classical optimization routine often has to navigate a complicated optimization landscape
[
2
,
3
]
. The existence of local optima can hinder the search for the optimal solution, which makes it challenging to develop rigorous criteria for when VQAs solve the optimization problem at hand. At the heart of this challenge lies the nonconvex nature of the optimization problem and the complex interplay between the parameterized quantum circuit and the problem instance
[

Results

2.1
Setting

VQAs
implement a parameterized unitary transformation
U(\bm{\theta})
on a quantum computer.
This unitary is used to estimate a cost function
J(\bm{\theta})
, which is minimized by a classical optimization routine like gradient descent that iteratively updates the parameters according to the update rule

\bm{\theta}_{k+1}=\bm{\theta}_{k}-\gamma\nabla J(\bm{\theta}_{k}).

(1)

Here,
k=0,1,\cdots
labels the iterative step,
\gamma
is the step size of the algorithm, and
\nabla J(\bm{\theta}_{k})
denotes the gradient with respect to the parameters
\bm{\theta}_{k}
.

The
VQE
seeks to find the ground state energy of a Hamiltonian
H
by minimizing the energy expectation value

J(\bm{\theta})=\bra{\psi_{0}}U^{\dagger}(\bm{\theta})HU(\bm{\theta})\ket{\psi_{0}},

(2)

for some initial state
\ket{\psi_{0}}
that is evolved by
U(\bm{\theta})
to prepare the state
\ket{\psi(\bm{\theta})}=U(\bm{\theta})\ket{\psi_{0}}
. Since this cost function is independent of the global phase of
U(\bm{\theta})
, it suffices to consider unitary transformations
U(\bm{\theta})\in\mathrm{SU}(d)
that live in the special unitary group of unitary
d\times d
matrices with unit determinant.

VQEs have a broad range of applications, including computing the ground state energy of molecules in quantum chemistry
[
44
]
and solving combinatorial optimization problems
[
45
]
. When
H=\mathds{1}-\ket{V}\bra{V}
is given by a projector formed by some target state
\ket{V}
, the cost function in (
2
) becomes the fidelity error
J(\bm{\theta})=1-|\langle V|\psi(\bm{\theta})\rangle|^{2}
whose minimization corresponds to a state preparation problem. This is a typical scenario in quantum control where the unitary transformation
U(\bm{\theta})
correspond to the time evolution operator that solves the Schrödinger equation governed by a time dependent Hamiltonian of the form

H(t)=H_{0}+\sum_{j=1}f_{j}(t)H_{j}

[
46
,
47
,
48
]
.
In this setting, the parameters
\bm{\theta}
correspond to some parameterization of the control fields
f_{j}(t)
, such as the amplitudes of piecewise constant controls
[
49
]
or frequencies and phases of the control fields that are expanded in a Fourier series
[
50
]
. Beyond state preparation, the implementation of a quantum logic gate
V
in quantum computing can also be achieved by minimizing the gate error
J(\bm{\theta})=1-|\langle V,U(\bm{\theta})\rangle|^{2}
where here
\langle A,B\rangle=\text{Tr}\{A^{\dagger}B\}
denotes the Hilbert Schmidt inner product.

However, despite the broad utility of minimizing cost functions of the form (
2
) through gradient-descent type algorithms (
1
), criteria for when a parameterized unitary transformation
U(\bm{\theta})
yields convergence to the target (i.e., state or gate) are not known. To address this challenge, we first note that the cost function is a composition consisting of two parts:

1.

A parameterization that maps
M
real parameters
\bm{\theta}
to the element
U(\bm{\theta})
of the special unitary group.

2.

A cost functional
J:\mathrm{SU}(d)\rightarrow\mathds{R}
defined on the manifold of the special unitary group.

As a direct application of the chain rule, the gradient of the cost function with respect to a single parameter
\theta_{j}
can be expressed in terms of the Hilbert-Schmidt inner product of the derivatives of these two components
[
9
,
27
,
51
]
,

\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta_{j}}J(\bm{\theta})=\langle\operatorname{grad}J[U(\bm{\theta})],\Omega_{j}(\bm{\theta})\rangle.

(3)

Here,
\operatorname{grad}J[U(\bm{\theta})]=[H,\ket{\psi(\bm{\theta})}\bra{\psi(\bm{\theta})}]
is the
Riemannian gradient
of the cost function
J
, i.e. the direction tangential to the manifold of steepest increase of
J
and

\displaystyle\Omega_{j}(\bm{\theta})=U^{\dagger}(\bm{\theta})\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta_{j}}U(\bm{\theta}).

(4)

As depicted in Fig.
1
, both quantities live in the tangent space of the special unitary group at the identity given by the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{su}(d)
of skew Hermitian and traceless
d\times d
matrices. We leverage this differential geometric perspective to characterize when gradient descent (
1
) convergences to the global minimum of the cost function (
2
) that corresponds to the desired target state.

2.2
Avoiding suboptimal solutions almost surely

For analytic cost functions and a sufficiently small steps size
\gamma
it is well known that gradient descent always either diverges to infinity or converges to a critical point
\bm{\theta}^{*}
at which the gradient vanishes
\nabla J(\bm{\theta}^{*})=0

[
52
]
. It is further known that the Riemannian gradient of the cost functionals corresponding to equation (
2
) only vanishes at global maxima and saddle points
[
51
]
that correspond to the eigenstates of
H
(see Methods,
section

4
).

Now, if the
\Omega_{j}
’s span the full tangent space for all
\bm{\theta}
, the critical point structure is determined by the Riemannian gradient as the situation that
\operatorname{grad}J
is orthogonal to all
\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta_{j}}U(\bm{\theta})
cannot occur, which we refer to as
local surjectivity
. We note that such maps are known as
submersions

[
53
]
in differential geometry.

Definition 1

(Local surjectivity)
.

We say that a parameterized unitary transformation
U(\bm{\theta})
is locally surjective if

\mathfrak{su}(d)=\mathrm{span}\{\Omega_{j}(\bm{\theta})\mid j=1,...,M\}\ \forall\bm{\theta}\in\mathds{R}^{M}.

(5)

The local surjectivity property ensures that at each point
\bm{\theta}
in the Euclidean parameter space
U(\bm{\theta})
has access to any direction on the manifold of the special unitary group, i.e., by varying
\bm{\theta}
the system can be steered in all tangent space directions. We call points in the optimization landscape where local surjectivity breaks down
singular points
. Local surjectivity is independent of the problem structure defined by
H
but only depends on the parameterized unitary transformation
U(\bm{\theta})
. However, there has been a longstanding debate in the quantum control community whether systems exist for which local surjectivity can be satisfied. The more general problem of the existence of submersions from an open manifold to another manifold has also been discussed in the differential geometry literature
[
54
]
. Below we solve this issue by explicitly constructing unitary transformations that exhibit the local surjectivity property. With further details found in the methods section, we show that then the critical points
\bm{\theta}^{*}
of
J(\bm{\theta})
correspond global optima and saddle points whose Hessian has at least one negative eigenvalue. Such saddle points are known in the classical optimization community as strict saddles points, which are avoided for almost all (i.e., with respect to the Lebesgue measure) initial values by gradient descent provided the gradients are Lipschitz continuous
[
55
]
. We establish the following Theorem.

Theorem
.

Let
H
be a Hamiltonian and
U(\bm{\theta})\in\mathrm{SU}(d)
be a parameterized unitary transformation used as a VQE ansatz to minimize the expectation value (
2
) of
H
. If local surjectivity holds for
U(\bm{\theta})
and the Lie algebra elements
\Omega_{j}(\bm{\theta})
in (
4
) are uniformly bounded, then for almost all initial points
\bm{\theta}_{0}
, a VQE updated by the gradient descent algorithm (
1
) either diverges to infinity, or it converges to a ground state of
H
.

We remark that the uniform boundedness of the
\Omega_{j}(\bm{\theta})
in an arbitrary matrix norm is a technical requirement that ensures the gradients to be Lipschitz continuous (see Methods,
section

4
for details).

The Theorem gives guarantees on the convergence of the
VQE
. In particular, it says that given that the algorithm does not run off to infinity but instead converges to some finite critical point
\bm{\theta}^{*}
, then, except for a few select cases,
that point does indeed correspond to a global optimum of the cost function. In other words, the algorithm almost never converges to a suboptimal solution.

In the few extraordinary cases, the algorithm converges to an excited eigenstate of
H
instead.
However, the theorem guarantees that these cases are rare in the sense that when sampling the initial point from a reasonable distribution, e.g. Gaussian or uniformly from some finite region, the probability of hitting such an initial point is zero.

2.3
Gimbal locks in commonly used parameterized quantum circuits

To gain some intuition for the importance of local surjectivity, we start by considering a single qubit example.

The Hilbert space of a single qubit has complex dimension
d=2
and the Lie algebra of traceless skew Hermitian
2\times 2
matrices
\mathfrak{su}(2)
has real dimension three.
A basis for
\mathfrak{su}(2)
is, up to a prefactor of
i
, given by the Pauli matrices

\sigma_{X}=\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{bmatrix},\ \sigma_{Y}=\begin{bmatrix}0&-i\\
i&0\end{bmatrix},\ \sigma_{Z}=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\
0&-1\end{bmatrix}.

(6)

A common parameterization for
\mathrm{SU}(2)
are the Euler angles, defined in the
X-Y-X
convention as

U(\bm{\theta})=\exp(-i\sigma_{X}\theta_{3})\exp(-i\sigma_{Y}\theta_{2})\exp(-i\sigma_{X}\theta_{1}).

(7)

Indeed, any single qubit unitary can be written in this form up to an irrelevant global phase. However, despite the existence of parameters
\bm{\theta}=(\theta_{1},\theta_{2},\theta_{2})
that achieve any unitary (reachability), local optimization methods may not be able to find the parameters that achieve a desired unitary transformation, which we discuss below.

Assume that the gradient descent update (
1

\bm{\theta}=\left(0,\frac{\pi}{2},0\right)^{T}
at which the Lie algebra elements
\Omega_{j}(\bm{\theta})
are given by
\Omega_{1}(\bm{\theta})=-i\sigma_{X}
,
\Omega_{2}(\bm{\theta})=-i\sigma_{Y}
and
\Omega_{3}(\bm{\theta})=i\sigma_{X}
. Consequently, if the Riemannian gradient points into the
\sigma_{Z}
-direction at this point, then the Lie algebra elements have no suppport in the direction of the Riemannian gradient and the Euclidean gradient will vanish.
As such, the optimization routine terminates at
\bm{\theta}
, even though the optimal solution may not have been reached yet.

The same issue arises on the level of the state
\ket{\psi(\bm{\theta})}
instead of the unitary
U(\bm{\theta})
, which is illustrated in
Figure

2
.
The effective loss of a degree of freedom at certain points in the parameterization landscape is well known as
gimbal lock
for the Euler-angle parameterization of rotations in a three-dimensional
space
[
56
]
, as prominently encountered, e.g., in the Apollo-11 Program
[
57
]
.

Hence the derivatives

2.4
Construction of locally surjective parameterizations

Now that we have established that even common parameterized quantum circuits are plagued by singular points at which gradient descent can get stuck, we introduce alternative parameterizations that do not suffer from this issue, i.e., which are locally surjective.
We will leverage two key insights to construct locally surjective parameterized unitary transformations:

1.

Both the
\mathbb{SU}(d)
-gate ansatz and the product-of-exponentials ansatz have their singular points outside a ball with nonzero radius
R
in the parameter space.

2.

If we divide the parameterization into two parts

U(\bm{\theta})=W(\theta_{k+1},...,\theta_{n})V(\theta_{1},...,\theta_{k}),

(10)

then
\Omega_{1}(\bm{\theta})
to
\Omega_{k}(\bm{\theta})
do not depend on
\theta_{k+1},...,\theta_{n}
.

If one naively multiplies two copies of any of the aforementioned ansätze and restricts the parameter domain of the right copy to a suitably small region around zero, this already eliminates all singular points. The first
d^{2}-1
Lie algebra elements are independent of all other parameters and hence always span
\mathfrak{su}(d)
.
However, one cannot make sure that the optimization routine does not leave the domain.
Therefore, we artificially blow up this domain, e.g. by taking a suitably scaled arctangent.

We further require that the parameterization can in principle reach any unitary up to global phase.
This property is usually called
surjectivity
, or in quantum optimal control contexts it is also called
reachability
of all unitaries. To summarize, we construct a surjective and locally surjective parameterized unitary transformation as follows.

Definition 2

(Composite ansätze)
.

Let
\bm{\theta},\bm{\phi}\in\mathds{R}^{d^{2}-1}
,
V(\bm{\phi})
be either the
\mathbb{SU}(d)
-gate ansatz or the product-of-exponentials ansatz and
R>0
be such that all singular points of
V
are outside the ball of radius
R
in parameter space.
Further, let
W(\bm{\theta})
be any surjective parameterization of
\mathrm{SU}(d)
with uniformly bounded derivatives. In particular, a generalized Euler angle ansatz
[
65
,
66
]
is a suitable choice for
W(\bm{\theta})
.
We construct a surjective and locally surjective parameterized unitary transformation in
\mathrm{SU}(d)
as

U(\bm{\theta},\bm{\phi})=W(\bm{\theta})V\left(\frac{2R}{\pi}\arctan(\bm{\phi})\right),

(11)

where
\arctan(\bm{\phi})
is to be understood as element-wise application of the
\arctan
function.

We refer to this family of ansätze as the
composite ansätze
, as they are composed of two different components
V
and
W
.
The order of
V
and
W
can be interchanged, but we will only prove local surjectivity for this particular ordering.
In the context of quantum optimal control, a composite ansatz corresponds to composing the control pulses of two parts.
In the first part, the controls need to remain bounded, so that we do not rotate with any control Hamiltonian around an angle greater than
\frac{2R}{\pi}
.
The second part can be any control sequence that corresponds to a fully controllable system and that achieves reachability of any target, i.e., for a sufficiently long evolution time and unconstrained control fields
[
67
]
.
If the interactions necessary for each of the exponentials in the composite ansatz (e.g., arbitrary Pauli strings) are not natively supported by the hardware, one can approximate them through Trotterization
[
68
]
.

It remains to find a suitable value for the radius
R
such that the singular points of
V
lie outside the open ball.
For the
\mathbb{SU}(d)
-gate ansatz, an appropriate value is given by the so called
injectivity radius
of the unitary group, which is per definition the largest radius for which the exponential map does not have any singular points inside a ball of radius
R
around the origin. In general, for the unitary group the injective radius is known explicitly
[
69
]
.
For the product-of-exponentials ansatz, one needs to manually compute the singular points and choose the radius accordingly.

Let us illustrate the composite ansatz construction on the toy example of a single qubit from earlier.
Consider the product-of-exponentials ansatz for
\mathrm{SU}(2)

G(\bm{\phi})=\exp(-i\sigma_{X}\phi_{3})\exp(-i\sigma_{Y}\phi_{2})\exp(-i\sigma_{Z}\phi_{1}).

(12)

The Lie algebra elements
\Omega_{j}
can be calculated to be

\displaystyle\Omega_{1}=-i\sigma_{Z}

(13)

\displaystyle\Omega_{2}=-i\cos(2\phi_{1})\sigma_{Y}+i\sin(2\phi_{1})\sigma_{X}

(14)

\displaystyle\Omega_{3}=-i\cos(2\phi_{1})\cos(2\phi_{2})\sigma_{X}

(15)

\displaystyle\hphantom{\Omega_3 = }-i\sin(2\phi_{1})\cos(2\phi_{2})\sigma_{Y}+i\sin(2\phi_{2})\sigma_{Z}

In order to check whether these Lie algebra elements are linearly independent, we compute the determinant

\displaystyle\begin{vmatrix}0&i\sin(2\phi_{1})&-i\cos(2\phi_{1})\cos(2\phi_{2})\\
0&-i\cos(2\phi_{1})&-i\sin(2\phi_{1})\cos(2\phi_{2})\\
-i&0&i\sin(2\phi_{2})\end{vmatrix}

(16)

\displaystyle=-\cos(2\phi_{2}).

Thus, as long as
\phi_{2}<\frac{\pi}{4}
, the
\Omega_{j}
’s span
\mathfrak{su}(2)

We conclude that for a single qubit the parameterized unitary transformation,

\displaystyle U(\bm{\theta},\bm{\phi})=

\displaystyle\exp(-i\sigma_{X}\theta_{3})\exp(-i\sigma_{Y}\theta_{2})\exp(-i\sigma_{Z}\theta_{1})

\displaystyle\cdot\exp\left(-\frac{i}{2}\sigma_{X}\arctan(\phi_{3})\right)

\displaystyle\cdot\exp\left(-\frac{i}{2}\sigma_{Y}\arctan(\phi_{2})\right)

(17)

\displaystyle\cdot\exp\left(-\frac{i}{2}\sigma_{Z}\arctan(\phi_{1})\right),

is locally surjective. This construction effectively doubles the number of necessary parameters. It is natural to ask whether it is possible to find locally surjective parameterizations with even fewer parameters.
Since topological arguments exclude a locally surjective parameterization with exactly
d^{2}-1
parameters
[
70
]
, at least
d^{2}
parameters are required.
Indeed, one can construct a locally surjective parameterized unitary transformation with exactly
d^{2}
parameters using the
Cayley transform

\displaystyle\mathrm{cay}:\mathfrak{u}(d)

\displaystyle\rightarrow\mathrm{U}^{*}(d)

(18)

\displaystyle X

\displaystyle\mapsto(\mathds{1}-X)^{-1}(\mathds{1}+X),

(19)

where we denote by
\mathrm{U}^{*}(d)
the set of unitaries
G
, s.t
\det(G+\mathds{1})\neq 0
and
\mathfrak{u}(d)
is the unitary algebra of all skew Hermitian matrices. It is a diffeomorphism onto its image, implying that there are no singular points on the entire parameter space
[
71
]
.
Even though its image is a proper subset of the unitary group, the Cayley transform can reach any unitary up to an irrelevant global phase factor. Hence, we indeed have found a locally surjective unitary transformation that only requires
d^{2}
parameters.

Corollary
.

Let
X_{j}
,
j=1,...,d^{2}
be a basis of
\mathfrak{u}(d)
and
X(\bm{\theta})=\sum_{j=1}^{d^{2}}\theta_{j}X_{j}
for
\bm{\theta}\in\mathds{R}^{d^{2}}
. Then the parameterized unitary transformation

\displaystyle U(\bm{\theta})=\mathrm{cay}(X(\bm{\theta}))

(20)

is locally surjective where
\text{cay}(\cdot)
denotes the Cayley transform defined in (
18
).

We expect that the parameterized quantum circuit (
20
) that utilizes the Cayley transform can be implemented on quantum computers through quantum signal processing and block encoding strategies
[
72
]
.

3
Discussion

In this work we assumed that the Riemannian gradient can a priori point in any direction.
This is however not the case, as the cost function only depends on the state
\ket{\psi(\bm{\theta})}=U(\bm{\theta})\ket{\psi_{0}}
rather than the unitary itself.
Hence in principle, at any state
\ket{\psi}
we can factor out the stabilizer subgroup, i.e., the group of unitaries that leave
\ket{\psi}
invariant.
For example, we can restrict the exponential map onto the orthogonal complement in
\mathrm{su}(d)
of the commutant of the projector onto the initial state
\ket{\psi_{0}}\bra{\psi_{0}}
.
The resulting manifold only has a dimension of
2(d-1)
instead of
d^{2}-1
.

The problem could further admit additional symmetries, which constrain the Riemannian gradient to an even smaller subalgebra
\mathfrak{g}
.
In this case the optimization could be carried out on the corresponding subgroup
G
, which reduces the dimension of the problem even further.
This is interesting since the number of parameters of a locally surjective parameterized unitary transformation necessarily is at least as large as the dimension of the Lie algebra over which the Riemannian gradient has support.
In the most general case this would be
\mathfrak{su}(2^{n})
, where
n
is the number of qubits employed in the
VQA
, hence its dimension scales exponentially.
One might find problems where the Riemannian gradient only has support in a polynomially sized Lie algebra, in which case it might be better to only parameterize the corresponding smaller Lie group.
We expect that the family of composite ansätze can in principle be generalized to different Lie groups of lower dimension, which would lead to a reduction in the number of optimization variables needed for the parameterization.

However, for some parameterizations that satisfy local surjectivity, gradient descent may not converge at all. To demonstrate this issue, let
U(\bm{\theta})
be the
\mathbb{SU}(d)
-gate ansatz and consider for some very small radius
R
the parameterization
U(\frac{2R}{\pi}\arctan(\bm{\theta}))
.
While this parameterization satisfies all the conditions of the theorem, only unitaries that are sufficiently close to the identity can be reached. Consequently, if the global optimum lies outside this region, the gradient algorithm must escape to infinity.

This problem is not only restricted to parameterized unitary transformations that cannot reach the global optimum but can also occurs when the parameterization is surjective.
We start by explicitly illustrating this behavior on the simpler manifold
\mathrm{U}(1)
, i.e. the unit circle, considering the parameterization
U(\theta)=e^{i\left(\frac{5}{2}\arctan(\theta)+\frac{\pi}{4}\right)}
(see Figure
3
for visualization).
The negative half of the real line is mapped such that it gets arbitrarily close to the point
-1
, but never reaches it.
The positive half however is wrapped around the circle up to the point
-i
, so it reaches
-1
and overlaps with the part that is also reached by the negative half.
This parameterization fulfills all the conditions of the main theorem.
However, if one constructs a cost function such that
1
is the optimum and initializes the gradient descent at a sufficiently large positive value of
\theta
, it will run off to positive infinity, instead of going around the circle to reach the global optimum.

More generally, this behavior may also occur with a composite ansatz. Consider the case where the surjective part
W
reaches a singular point.
As the
V
part is locally surjective by construction, we can still move around the special unitary group in any direction. But we cannot guarantee that we will ever hit a point at which the Riemannian gradient regains support over the derivatives of
W
, meaning that the parameters of
W
might never get updated again. If this does happen, the unitary effectively becomes constraint to a small region in

Figure 3:
Example of a gradient algorithm escaping to infinity for an inappropriate choice of parameterization.
The optimization is done on the complex unit circle
\mathrm{U}(1)
(black).
The real line of parameters (blue) is wrapped around the circle such that the negative half extends up to the point
-1
, but never reaches it. The positive half is wrapped the other way around until
-i
so that it overlaps with the negative half in the third quadrant of the circle.
The increasing radius of the blue spiral only serves visibility purposes.
When a gradient search with the target
1
is initialized at a point
\theta
that is mapped to the third quadrant of the circle, the gradient (red) will always point into the positive direction, driving the parameter towards positive infinity, without the image point on the circle ever reaching
1
.

the manifold around
W(\bm{\theta})
and the parameters of
V
must escape to infinity when the optimum lies outside that region.

If the parameterization was periodic with respect to all parameters, one could argue that the gradient descent effectively never leaves one of the periods and hence, cannot run off to infinity.
In the
U(1)
example discussed above, such a periodic parameterization is given by
U(\theta)=e^{i\theta}
.
The corresponding euclidean cost function is a sine function, for which gradient descent surely finds a global optimum when the step size is chosen small enough. The product-of-exponentials ansatz can in principle exhibit periodicity for a suitably chosen basis, such as the Pauli basis. However it remains unknown if it ever becomes locally surjective.

Convergence of gradient descent can be ensured when the cost function
J
exhibits compact sublevel sets, i.e., the sets
\{\bm{\theta}\in\mathds{R}^{M}\mid J(\bm{\theta})\leq C\}
for all
C
should be compact.
However the VQE cost function given by the expectation value of some finite dimensional Hamiltonian (
2
) cannot have this property as it is bounded.
One can in principle try to enforce the compact sublevel set property by regularizing the cost function.
For example, one could add a penalty term

J^{\prime}(\bm{\theta})=J(\bm{\theta})+\lambda\lVert\bm{\theta}\rVert_{\alpha}^{\alpha}

(21)

with regularization constant
\lambda
and order
\alpha
.
In the machine learing community this strategy is known for
\alpha=1
as L1-regularization while for
\alpha=2
as L2-regularization
[
73
]
.

However, regularizing the cost function comes with its own challenges.
Regardless of how small the regularization constant
\lambda
is chosen, it cannot be guaranteed anymore that the L2-regularized cost function
J^{\prime}
is still free of local minima.
The reason for this is that the negative eigenvalues of the Euclidean Hessian at the critical points can in principle become arbitrarily close to zero.
Any additional positive curvature introduced by the L2-regularization penalty can transform the strict saddles of
J
to a local minimum of
J^{\prime}
.
Moreover, adding the penalty term also shifts the position of the optimum itself.
The critical points
\bm{\theta}^{*}
of
J^{\prime}
now satisfy
\nabla J(\bm{\theta}^{*})=-\lambda\lVert\bm{\theta}\rVert_{2}^{-1}\bm{\theta}
or
\nabla J(\bm{\theta}^{*})=-2\lambda\bm{\theta}
for L1- and L2-regularization, respectively.
It is a priori unclear how far these points, and more importantly the respective states
\ket{\psi(\bm{\theta^{*}})}
and unitaries
U(\bm{\theta}^{*})
, are from their corresponding counterparts of
J
.

In general, it is possible that the termination of the gradient descent and convergence to the global optimum cannot both be guaranteed at the same time as some
VQE
instances may not be computable.
It was recently shown that the decision problem associated to a
VQE
with the product-of-exponentials ansatz and commuting generators is undecidable if a certain conjecture on the existence of solutions to a system of polynomial equations holds true
[
74
]
. In the context of this work this means that we would not be able to decide the halting problem for general
VQE
instances.
It may therefore be necessary to make further assumptions on either the parameterized unitary transformation, or the problem Hamiltonian, to achieve guarantees for termination of the VQE and convergence to an optimal solution.

Concluding remarks:
Although
VQAs
have been studied quite extensively in recent times, their convergence properties still remain relatively poorly understood. The results presented in this work provide a first step towards establishing solution guarantees for VQAs. We showed that under the assumption of local surjectivity, a variational quantum eigensolver updated by gradient descent almost never gets stuck at a suboptimal solution.
Instead, the VQE either escapes to infinity or converges towards a global optimum that corresponds to a ground state of a Hamiltonian.
The assumption of local surjectivity is central to avoid the existence of local minima in the optimization landscape.
Missing directions in the tangent space can lead to the formation of local optima where a gradient-based optimization algorithm might get stuck.

While satisfying local surjectivity does indeed prevent this from happening, it is not an “easy” condition to fulfill.
We showed that many of the commonly used parameterized quantum circuit ansätze are not locally surjective. In light of these challenges we proposed two alternatives, the family of composite ansätze (Eq. (
11
)) and the Cayley transform (Eq.(
20
)) that do satisfy local surjectivity. The composite ansätze consist of two parts — one ensures that local surjectivity is satisfied while the other ensures reachability of all unitaries.
One can build a composite ansatz for example from a
\mathbb{SU}(d)
-gate ansatz and a generalized Euler angle parameterization, which can be implemented on quantum hardware
[
30
,
65
]
.
The Cayley transform on the other hand cuts the number of required parameters almost in half. The implementation of such parameterized unitary transformation on a quantum device through quantum signal processing techniques is left for future study.

4
Methods

4.1
Useful results from classical optimization

The convergence properties of gradient descent algorithms have been widely studied in the classical optimization literature. In particular, a lot is known in the case where the cost function is real analytic, which is usually the case for the cost functions we encounter in
VQAs
.
One can show that as long as the step size is chosen so that the descent fulfills the so called
Wolfe conditions
, the gradient search either diverges to infinity or converges to a critical point
\bm{\theta}^{*}
at which the gradient vanishes (cf. Theorem 4.1 in
[
52
]
),

\displaystyle\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\lVert\bm{\theta}_{k}\rVert=\infty\text{ or }

\displaystyle\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\bm{\theta}_{k}=\bm{\theta}^{*},\,\bm{\theta}^{*}\in\left\{\bm{\theta}\in\mathds{R}^{M}\mid\nabla J(\bm{\theta}^{*})=0\right\}.

(22)

Such critical points can either be local or global extrema, or strict saddle points, depending on the eigenvalues of the Hessian of the cost function.
A critical point
\bm{\theta}^{*}
is a strict saddle if the Hessian
\nabla^{2}J(\bm{\theta}^{*})
at this point has at least one negative eigenvalue, i.e. the smallest eigenvalue of the Hessian
\lambda_{\text{min}}
satisfies
\lambda_{\text{min}}<0

[
29
]
.

While the optimizer can in principle get stuck at a strict saddle point, it can be proven from the stable center manifold theorem under the assumption of Lipschitz continuity of the gradient that almost no initialization of gradient descent will actually do so.

Lemma 1

(cf. Theorem 4 in
[
55
]
and Corollary 2 in
[
29
]
)
.

Let
J:\mathds{R}^{M}\to\mathds{R}
be a twice continuously differentiable function and let
\mathcal{X}^{*}=\{\bm{\theta}^{*}\in\mathds{R}^{M}\mid\bm{\theta}^{*}\text{ is a strict saddle point}\}
be the set of all strict saddles. Assume that
0<\gamma<\frac{1}{L}
, where
L
is the Lipschitz constant satisfying
\lVert\nabla J(\bm{\theta}_{1})-\nabla J(\bm{\theta}_{2})\rVert_{2}\leq L\lVert\bm{\theta}_{1}-\bm{\theta}_{2}\rVert_{2}
for all
\bm{\theta}_{1},\bm{\theta}_{2}\in\mathds{R}^{M}
. Then

\mathrm{Pr}(\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\bm{\theta}_{k}\in\mathcal{X}^{*})=0

(23)

for random initial points
\bm{\theta}_{0}
, where the probability is to be taken with respect to an arbitrary measure that is absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure.

This gives a roadmap to prove the main theorem: establish that the cost function only has global extrema and strict saddle points, then use the theorems above to guarantee that the strict saddles are avoided and the algorithm can only converge to a global optimizer or escape to infinity.

4.2
Uniform boundedness of the Lie-algebra elements

In order to apply
Lemma

1
, we need to make sure that the gradient of the cost function is Lipschitz continuous.
We will show in the next section that this is the case when the Lie algebra elements of the parameterization are uniformly bounded, i.e. if for all
\bm{\theta}\in\mathds{R}^{M}
and all
j=1,...,M
we have
\lVert\Omega_{j}(\bm{\theta})\rVert\leq C
for some constant
C
, which does not depend on
\bm{\theta}
or
j
.
In this section, we show that this is indeed the case for the family of composite ansätze (cf. equation (
11
)) and the Cayley transform.

We begin with the family of composite ansätze.
Since by construction,
\Omega_{1}(\bm{\phi}),...,\Omega_{d^{2}-1}(\bm{\phi})
do not depend on
W
and
\bm{\theta}
, we can analyze
V\left(\frac{2R}{\pi}\arctan(\bm{\phi})\right)
in isolation.
There are two cases: either
V
is a product-of-exponentials ansatz, in which case
\lVert\Omega_{j}(\bm{\phi})\rVert\leq\lVert X_{j}\rVert
in any unitarily invariant norm.
Hence, we can choose
C=\max_{j}\lVert X_{j}\rVert
and get uniform boundedness.
Or
V
is the
\mathbb{SU}(d)
-gate ansatz, in which case we can argue that the
\lVert\Omega_{j}(\bm{\phi})\rVert
is a continuous function, hence it has a finite maximal value on the compact disc with radius
R
around the origin.
Since the composite ansatz only evaluates
V
inside that disc and the arctangent only contributes a factor smaller than one to the derivative, we again obtain uniform boundedness.
For the remaining
\Omega_{d^{2}}(\bm{\theta},\bm{\phi}),...,\Omega_{2(d^{2}-1)}(\bm{\theta},\bm{\phi})
just note that they are given by partial derivatives of
W(\bm{\theta})
up to some unitary factors.
Since the partial derivatives are assumed to be uniformly bounded per definition, the same will hold for the
\Omega s
in an appropriate unitarily invariant norm.

Finally, for the Cayley transform the differential is given by (see Lemma 8.8 in
[
75
]
)

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\mathrm{cay}_{X}:\mathfrak{u}(d)

\displaystyle\rightarrow T_{\mathrm{cay}(X)}\mathrm{U}^{*}(d)

(24)

\displaystyle H

\displaystyle\mapsto 2(\mathds{1}-X)^{-1}H(\mathds{1}+X)^{-1},

(25)

we get that

\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta_{j}}\mathrm{cay}(X(\bm{\theta}))=2(\mathds{1}-X(\bm{\theta}))^{-1}X_{j}(\mathds{1}+X(\bm{\theta}))^{-1}.

(26)

And hence

\Omega_{j}(\bm{\theta})=\mathrm{cay}(X(\bm{\theta}))^{\dagger}\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta_{j}}\mathrm{cay}(X(\bm{\theta}))=2(\mathds{1}+X(\bm{\theta}))^{-1}X_{j}(\mathds{1}+X(\bm{\theta}))^{-1},

(27)

where we used that
\mathrm{cay}(X)^{\dagger}=\mathrm{cay}(-X)
.
Since the spectrum of
X(\bm{\theta})
is purely imaginary, we have for all eigenvalues
\lambda
of
(\mathds{1}+X(\bm{\theta}))
that
\lvert\lambda\rvert\geq 1
.
In particular,
(\mathds{1}+X(\bm{\theta}))
is diagonalizable.
Hence the operator norm fulfills
\lVert(\mathds{1}+X(\bm{\theta}))^{-1}\rVert_{\infty}\leq 1
and we get uniform boundedness by submultiplicativity of the operator norm.

4.3
Details on the proof of the main theorem

We first give an outline of the proof.
The proof starts by establishing that the Euclidean gradient only vanishes under the assumption of local surjectivity if the Riemannian gradient vanishes.
Next, we analyze the Hessian to show that the Riemannian gradient only vanishes at global optima and strict saddles.
Then, we relate the Riemannian Hessian to the Hessian in the Euclidean parameter space to show that the same also holds there under the assumption of local surjectivity.
Finally, this enables us to make use of the results in
section

4.1
to argue that the algorithm either runs off to infinity or converges to a ground state almost surely.

We start by considering a gradient component

\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta_{j}}J(\bm{\theta})=\langle\operatorname{grad}J[U(\bm{\theta})],\Omega_{j}(\bm{\theta})\rangle,

(28)

where the scalar product is meant to be the Hilbert-Schmidt inner product.
The Riemannian gradient of equation (
2
) is given by
\operatorname{grad}J[U(\bm{\theta})]=[H,\ket{\psi(\bm{\theta})}\bra{\psi(\bm{\theta})}]
. Under the assumption that the
\Omega_{j}(\bm{\theta})
’s span
\mathfrak{su}(d)
, the Euclidean gradient
\nabla J(\bm{\theta})
can only be zero at points
\bm{\theta}^{*}
where the Riemannian gradient
\operatorname{grad}J[U(\bm{\theta}^{*})]
vanishes. Consequently, the states
\ket{\psi(\bm{\theta}^{*})}
created at the critical points need to satisfy
[H,\ket{\psi(\bm{\theta^{*}})}\bra{\psi(\bm{\theta^{*}})}]=0
. However, since
H
is diagonalizable, a projector commutes with
H
if and only if it projects into an eigenspace of
H
. Therefore, the states
\ket{\psi(\bm{\theta}^{*})}
must be eigenstates of
H
.

We proceed by calculating the Hessian elements at the critical points

\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\theta_{i}\partial\theta_{j}}J(\bm{\theta}^{*})=-\mathrm{Tr}\{[\Omega_{j},[\Omega_{i},\ket{\psi(\bm{\theta}^{*})}\bra{\psi(\bm{\theta}^{*})}]]H\}

(29)

omitting here the explicit dependence of the
\Omega_{j}
’s on
\bm{\theta}^{*}
.
In order to determine the type of critical point (that is, the eigenvalue structure of the Hessian at a critical point), we parameterize
U(\bm{\theta})=U
in the neighborhood of
U(\bm{\theta}^{*})
by
U=e^{\bm{x}\cdot\bm{B}}U(\bm{\theta}^{*})
. Here we use the short hand notation
\bm{x}\cdot\bm{B}=\sum_{i=1}^{d^{2}-1}x_{i}B_{i}
where
\{B_{i}\mid i=1,...,d^{2}-1\}
is a complete and orthonormal basis for
\mathfrak{su}(d)
. In the neighborhood of
U(\bm{\theta}^{*})
, the cost function is given by

\displaystyle J_{\{B_{i}\}}(\bm{x})

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Tr}\{e^{\bm{x}\cdot\bm{B}}\ket{\psi(\bm{\theta}^{*})}\bra{\psi(\bm{\theta}^{*})}e^{-\bm{x}\cdot\bm{B}}H\}

(30)

\displaystyle=J(\bm{\theta}^{*})+\mathrm{Tr}\{(\bm{x}\cdot\bm{B})^{2}\ket{\psi(\bm{\theta}^{*})}\bra{\psi(\bm{\theta}^{*})}H\}-\mathrm{Tr}\{(\bm{x}\cdot\bm{B})\ket{\psi(\bm{\theta}^{*})}\bra{\psi(\bm{\theta}^{*})}(\bm{x}\cdot\bm{B})H\}+\mathcal{O}(\lVert x\rVert_{2}^{3}),

where we used that the gradient vanishes at a critical point and the subscript
\{B_{i}\}
indicates the basis used. Now, taking
\{B_{i}\}=\{\lambda_{s},\lambda_{k,l},\bar{\lambda}_{k,l}\},1\leq s\leq d-1,1\leq k<l\leq d
, where

\displaystyle\lambda_{s}=\frac{i}{\sqrt{s(s+1)}}\left(\sum_{r=1}^{s}\ket{E_{r}}\bra{E_{r}}-s\ket{E_{s+1}}\bra{E_{s+1}}\right)

(31)

\displaystyle\lambda_{k,l}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}i(\ket{E_{k}}\bra{E_{l}}+\ket{E_{l}}\bra{E_{k}}),

(32)

\displaystyle\bar{\lambda}_{k,l}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\ket{E_{k}}\bra{E_{l}}-\ket{E_{l}}\bra{E_{k}})

(33)

are the generalized Gell-Mann matrices and
\ket{E_{j}}
are the eigenstates of
H
corresponding to the eigenenergy
E_{j}
for
j=1,...,d
, we find (see also Theorem IV.1 in
[
76
]
) that the Hessian
\nabla^{2}J_{\{\lambda_{s},\lambda_{k,l},\bar{\lambda}_{k,l}\}}(0)
at a critical point
\bm{x}=0
is diagonal.
Furthermore, the first
d-1
diagonal entries corresponding to
\lambda_{s}
vanish. The remaining
d^{2}-d
Hessian diagonal elements in the subspace spanned by
\{\lambda_{k,l},\bar{\lambda}_{k,l}\}
, read

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x_{i}^{2}}J_{\{\lambda_{k,l},\bar{\lambda}_{k,l}\}}(0)

\displaystyle=2(\mathrm{Tr}\{B_{i}^{2}\ket{\psi(\bm{\theta}^{*})}\bra{\psi(\bm{\theta}^{*})}H\}-\mathrm{Tr}\{B_{i}\ket{\psi(\bm{\theta}^{*})}\bra{\psi(\bm{\theta}^{*})}B_{i}H\})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(E_{k}-E_{l})(\lvert\braket{\psi(\bm{\theta}^{*})|E_{l}}\rvert^{2}-\lvert\braket{\psi(\bm{\theta}^{*})|E_{k}}\rvert^{2}),

(34)

for some
B_{i}\in\{\lambda_{k,l},\bar{\lambda}_{k,l}\}
. Now if we assign the eigenvalue
E_{1}
to the ground state, i.e.
E_{1}\leq E_{2}\leq\cdots\leq E_{d}
, the critical points
\bm{\theta}^{*}_{\mathrm{min}}
that correspond to a ground state constitute Hessian eigenvalues that are either 0 or positive, i.e.

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x_{i}^{2}}J_{\{\lambda_{k,l},\bar{\lambda}_{k,l}\}}(0)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(E_{k}-E_{l})(\lvert\braket{\psi(\bm{\theta}^{*})|E_{l}}\rvert^{2}-\lvert\braket{\psi(\bm{\theta}^{*})|E_{k}}\rvert^{2})

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}0,&\text{for }E_{k}=E_{l}=E_{1}\\
\frac{1}{2}(E_{l}-E_{1})>0&\text{for }E_{l}>E_{k}=E_{1}\\
0&\text{for }E_{k}>E_{1}.\end{cases}

(35)

Conversely, if we assign
E_{d}
to the largest eigenvalue of
H
, the corresponding Hessian eigenvalues are either 0 or negative. As long as
E_{d}>E_{1}
, all intermediate cases have at least one negative Hessian eigenvalue. In the case of
E_{d}=E_{1}
,
H
was a multiple of the identity and therefore any state is a ground state of
H
. Note that since these arguments hold independently of whether
B_{i}\in\{\lambda_{k,l}\}
or
B_{i}\in\{\bar{\lambda}_{k,l}\}
, each Hessian eigenvalue must appear twice. Moreover, in the
2(d-g)
dimensional subspace, where
g
is the degeneracy of the ground state, spanned by
\{\lambda_{1,l},\bar{\lambda}_{1,l}\mid l>g\}
, the Hessian eigenvalues are non-zero at the critical points. This means that within this subspace there are no singular critical points at which the Hessian is not invertible, which shows that the cost function
J(\bm{x})
is a Morse function within that subspace.

In order to show that all saddles are strict, i.e. in order to establish our theorem, we need to relate the Hessian
\nabla^{2}J_{\{B_{i}\}}(0)
to the Euclidean Hessian
\nabla^{2}J(\bm{\theta}^{*})
at the critical points. In order to do so it suffices to consider only the non-zero subspace spanned by
\{\lambda_{1,l},\bar{\lambda}_{1,l}\mid l>g\}
of
\nabla^{2}J_{\{B_{i}\}}(0)
, where we denote by
E_{l}
the eigenvalue that corresponds to the saddle point we consider. We go on and expand the operators
B_{i}
in equation (
4.3
) in the basis
\{\Omega_{j}\mid j=1,...,d^{2}-1\}
of
\mathfrak{su}(n)
to obtain

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x_{i}^{2}}J_{\{B_{i}\}}(0)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n,m=1}^{d^{2}-1}S_{n,i}S_{m,i}(\mathrm{Tr}\{(\Omega_{n}\Omega_{m}+\Omega_{m}\Omega_{n})\ket{\psi(\bm{\theta}^{*})}\bra{\psi(\bm{\theta}^{*})}H\}

\displaystyle\hphantom{= \sum_{n,m=1}^{d^2-1}}-\mathrm{Tr}\{(\Omega_{n}\ket{\psi(\bm{\theta}^{*})}\bra{\psi(\bm{\theta}^{*})}\Omega_{m}+\Omega_{m}\ket{\psi(\bm{\theta}^{*})}\bra{\psi(\bm{\theta}^{*})}\Omega_{n})H\})

\displaystyle=\sum_{n,m=1}^{d^{2}-1}S_{n,i}S_{m,i}\mathrm{Tr}\{[\Omega_{m},[\Omega_{n},\ket{\psi(\bm{\theta}^{*})}\bra{\psi(\bm{\theta}^{*})}]]H\}

(36)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n,m=1}^{d^{2}-1}S_{n,i}S_{m,i}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\theta_{n}\partial\theta_{m}}J(\bm{\theta}^{*}),

where the coefficients are given by the inner product
S_{n,i}=\mathrm{Tr}\{B_{i}^{\dagger}\Omega_{n}\}
.
Thus, the Hessians at the critical points are related through the transformation

\nabla^{2}J_{\{B_{i}\}}(0)=S^{T}\nabla^{2}J(\bm{\theta}^{*})S,

(37)

where
S_{n,i}
are the elements of the matrix
S
.

It remains to show that
\nabla^{2}J(\bm{\theta^{*}})
has at least one negative eigenvalue, whenever
\nabla^{2}J_{\{B_{i}\}}(0)
does.
Since J depends smoothly on
\bm{\theta}
, the Hessian
\nabla^{2}J(\bm{\theta^{*}})
is symmetric.
Denote by
\sigma_{k}(A)
the k-th eigenvalue of a matrix
A
in ascending order.
A generalization of Sylvester’s law of inertia to rectangular transformations (see Theorem 3.2 in
[
77
]
) states that

\sigma_{1}(\nabla^{2}J_{\{B_{i}\}}(0))=\gamma_{1}\mu_{1},

(38)

where
\sigma_{1}(S^{T}S)\leq\gamma_{1}
and
\sigma_{1}(\nabla^{2}J(\bm{\theta^{*}}))\leq\mu_{1}
.
Since the
\Omega_{j}
span
\mathfrak{su}(d)
, we have that
S
is full rank and therefore all eigenvalues of
S^{T}S
are strictly positive.
Hence,
\gamma_{1}
is positive and
\mu_{1}
is negative whenever
\nabla^{2}J_{\{B_{i}\}}(0)
has at least one negative eigenvalue.
It follows that in that case,
\nabla^{2}J(\bm{\theta^{*}})
also has at least one negative eigenvalue, which ensures that the Euclidean optimization landscape also only has global extrema and saddle points.
Since the Lie algebra elements
\Omega_{j}(\bm{\theta})
are uniformly bounded by assumption, so is the Euclidean Hessian and hence the gradient of the cost function is Lipschitz continuous.
Therefore, we can apply
Lemma

1
to argue that strict saddle points are almost always avoided, which completes the proof.

4.4
Overparemeterization does not remove singularities of the SU(d) gate ansatz

The singular points of the
\mathbb{SU}(d)
-gate ansatz are well known and can be studied in detail.
In particular, it is possible to determine which directions in the tangent space are actually missing at these singular points.
This knowledge, captured in the following Lemma, will allow us to argue that the natural strategies for overparameterization cannot fully eliminate singular points in this case.

Lemma 2
.

The
\mathbb{SU}(d)
-gate ansatz is not locally surjective.
Its singular points are given by

\mathrm{sing}(U(\bm{\theta}))=\left\{\bm{\theta}\in\mathds{R}^{d^{2}-1}\big|\left\{\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2}\mid\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}\in\mathrm{spec}\left(X(\bm{\theta})\right)\right\}\cap\left(2\pi i\mathds{Z}\setminus\{0\}\right)\neq\varnothing\right\}.

(39)

Furthermore at any singular point
\bm{\theta}_{s}\in\mathrm{sing}(U(\bm{\theta}))
, let
\ket{e_{i}}
be an orthonormal eigensystem of
X(\bm{\theta}_{s})
with corresponding eigenvalues
\lambda_{i}
for
i=1,...,d^{2}-1
. We have that

\mathrm{span}\{\Omega_{j}(\bm{\theta}_{s})\}^{\perp}=\mathrm{span}\{i(\ket{e_{i}}\bra{e_{j}}+\ket{e_{j}}\bra{e_{i}}),\ket{e_{i}}\bra{e_{j}}-\ket{e_{j}}\bra{e_{i}}\mid\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j}\in 2\pi i\mathds{Z}\setminus\{0\}\},

(40)

where the orthogonal complement is taken with respect to
\mathfrak{su}(d)
.

Proof.

For the characterization of the singular points, also confer to page 313 of
[
53
]
.
The differential of the exponential map is given by (cf. Theorem 1.7 in
[
78
]
)

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}U_{\bm{\theta}}:\mathds{R}^{d^{2}-1}

\displaystyle\rightarrow T_{\exp(X(\bm{\theta}))}\mathrm{SU}(d)

\displaystyle\bm{y}

\displaystyle\mapsto\mathrm{d}\left(L_{\exp(X(\bm{\theta}))}\right)_{e}\circ\frac{1-\exp(-\mathrm{ad}_{X(\bm{\theta})})}{\mathrm{ad}_{X(\bm{\theta})}}(X(\bm{y}))

(41)

where
L_{\exp(X(\bm{\theta}))}
is the left multiplication by
\exp(X(\bm{\theta}))
on
\mathrm{SU}(d)
,
\mathrm{ad}_{X(\bm{\theta})}
is the adjoint action of
X(\bm{\theta})
on
\mathfrak{su}(d)
and
\frac{1-\exp(-\mathrm{ad}_{X(\bm{\theta})})}{\mathrm{ad}_{X(\bm{\theta})}}
has to be understood as its power series expansion

\frac{1-\exp(-\mathrm{ad}_{X(\bm{\theta})})}{\mathrm{ad}_{X(\bm{\theta})}}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{k}\mathrm{ad}_{X(\bm{\theta})}^{k}}{(k+1)!}.

(42)

Since
L_{\exp(X(\bm{\theta}))}
is a diffeomorphism on
\mathrm{SU}(d)
,
\mathrm{d}\exp_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{y})=0
if and only if

X(\bm{y})\in\text{ker}\left(\frac{1-\exp(-\mathrm{ad}_{X(\bm{\theta})})}{\mathrm{ad}_{X(\bm{\theta})}}\right).

(43)

This kernel can be calculated to be (cf. page 316 of
[
53
]
)

\mathrm{ker}\left(\frac{1-\exp(-\mathrm{ad}_{X(\bm{\theta})})}{\mathrm{ad}_{X(\bm{\theta})}}\right)=\bigoplus_{z\in I}\mathrm{ker}(\mathrm{ad}_{X(\bm{\theta})}-z\mathds{1}),

(44)

where the index set
I
is given by
I=\mathrm{spec}(\mathrm{ad}_{X(\bm{\theta})})\cap(2\pi i\mathds{Z}\setminus\{0\})
.
The eigenvalues of
\mathrm{ad}_{X(\bm{\theta})}
are exactly
\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j}
, which proves equation (
39
).

To calculate the orthogonal complement to the span of the
\Omega_{j}(\bm{\theta}_{s})
at a singular point
\bm{\theta}_{s}
, we use that the orthogonal complement to the range of a linear map is the kernel of its adjoint.
For any
Y\in T_{\exp(X(\bm{\theta}))}\mathrm{SU}(d)
and
\bm{z}\in\mathds{R}^{d^{2}-1}
, we have that

\displaystyle\langle Y,\mathrm{d}U_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{z})\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{k}}{(k+1)!}\mathrm{Tr}\{Y^{\dagger}\exp(X(\bm{\theta}))\mathrm{ad}_{X(\bm{\theta})}^{k}(X(\bm{z}))\}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(k+1)!}\mathrm{Tr}\{\mathrm{ad}_{X(\bm{\theta})}^{k}(Y^{\dagger}\exp(X(\bm{\theta})))X(\bm{z})\}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(k+1)!}\mathrm{Tr}\left\{\left[\mathrm{ad}_{X(\bm{\theta})}^{k}(\exp(-X(\bm{\theta}))Y)\right]^{\dagger}X(\bm{z})\vphantom{\left[\mathrm{ad}_{X(\bm{\theta})}^{k}(\exp(-X(\bm{\theta}))Y)\right]^{\dagger}}\right\}

\displaystyle=\left\langle X^{*}\circ\frac{\exp(\mathrm{ad}_{X(\bm{\theta})})-1}{\mathrm{ad}_{X(\bm{\theta})}}\circ\mathrm{d}\left(L_{\exp(-X(\bm{\theta}))}\right)_{e}(Y),\bm{z}\vphantom{\frac{\exp(\mathrm{ad}_{X(\bm{\theta})})-1}{\mathrm{ad}_{X(\bm{\theta})}}}\right\rangle,

(45)

where
X^{*}
is the adjoint of
X
.
Hence,

\mathrm{d}U_{\bm{\theta}}^{*}=X^{*}\circ\frac{\exp(\mathrm{ad}_{X(\bm{\theta})})-1}{\mathrm{ad}_{X(\bm{\theta})}}\circ\mathrm{d}\left(L_{\exp(-X(\bm{\theta}))}\right)_{e}\

(46)

Since the
X_{j}
were assumed to be a basis of
\mathfrak{su}(d)
,
\mathrm{ker}(X^{*})=\{0\}
. Therefore,

\mathrm{ker}(\mathrm{d}U_{\bm{\theta}}^{*})=\left\{\vphantom{\Omega\in\mathrm{ker}\left(\frac{\exp(\mathrm{ad}_{X(\bm{\theta})})-1}{\mathrm{ad}_{X(\bm{\theta})}}\right)}\exp(X(\bm{\theta}))\Omega\mid\Omega\in\mathrm{ker}\left(\frac{\exp(\mathrm{ad}_{X(\bm{\theta})})-1}{\mathrm{ad}_{X(\bm{\theta})}}\right)\right\}.

(47)

The kernel is the same as the one given in equation (
44
), which concludes the proof.
∎

For the parameterization given in
Equation

8
, two natural strategies for overparameterizations are given by

1.

Using an overcomplete frame
\{X_{j}\mid j=1,...,M>d^{2}-1\}
of
\mathfrak{su}(d)
to construct the Lie algebra element
X(\bm{\theta})=\sum_{j=1}^{M}\theta_{j}X_{j}
.

2.

Multiplying multiple copies of the ansatz, e.g.

V(\bm{\theta},\bm{\varphi})=U(\bm{\theta})U(\bm{\varphi}).

(48)

The following theorem proves that these types of overparameterization also always admit singular points.

Lemma 3
.

(a)

Let
X_{j}
,
j=1,...,M>d^{2}-1
be an overcomplete frame of
\mathfrak{su}(n)
and let

W(\bm{\theta})=\exp(\sum_{j=1}^{M}\theta_{j}X_{j}).

Then this parameterization is not locally surjective, i.e. it has singular points.

(b)

Let
X_{j}
,
j=1,...,d^{2}-1
be a basis of
\mathfrak{su}(d)
. Further let
\bm{\theta},\bm{\varphi}\in\mathds{R}^{d^{2}-1}
and

V(\bm{\theta},\bm{\varphi})=\exp(X(\bm{\theta}))\exp(X(\bm{\varphi})).

If
\bm{\varphi}_{s}
is a singular point of
\exp(X(\bm{\varphi}))
, then
(\bm{\varphi}_{s},\bm{\varphi}_{s})
is a singular point of
V
.

Proof.

(a)

As can be seen from equation (
4.4
), we get two contributions to the kernel of the differential of the parameterization.
First, there is a
M-(d^{2}-1)
-dimensional subspace for which
X(\bm{y})=0
and hence
\mathrm{d}U_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{y})=0
, Secondly, for suitable
\bm{\theta}
there exists a
\bm{y}
such that
X(\bm{y})\neq 0
is in the kernel of
\frac{1-\exp(-\mathrm{ad}_{X(\bm{\theta})})}{\mathrm{ad}_{X(\bm{\theta})}}
.
Therefore we have for suitable choices of
\bm{\theta}
that

\displaystyle\mathrm{dim}\,\mathrm{im}\left(\mathrm{d}U_{\bm{\theta}}\right)

\displaystyle=M-\mathrm{dim}\,\mathrm{ker}\left(\mathrm{d}U_{\bm{\theta}}\right)

\displaystyle<M-(M-d^{2}-1)=d^{2}-1,

(49)

which shows that the differential is not surjective at those points.

(b)

We have that

\displaystyle V^{\dagger}(\bm{\theta},\bm{\varphi})\frac{\partial}{\partial\varphi_{j}}V(\bm{\theta},\bm{\varphi})=

\displaystyle\Omega_{j}(\bm{\varphi})

(50)

\displaystyle V^{\dagger}(\bm{\theta},\bm{\varphi})\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta_{j}}V(\bm{\theta},\bm{\varphi})=

\displaystyle\exp(-X(\bm{\varphi}))\Omega_{j}(\bm{\theta})\exp(X(\bm{\varphi})).

(51)

Furthermore, from
Lemma

2
we know that

\mathrm{span}\{\Omega_{j}(\bm{\varphi}_{s})\}=\mathrm{span}\{i(\ket{e_{i}}\bra{e_{j}}+\ket{e_{j}}\bra{e_{i}}),\ket{e_{i}}\bra{e_{j}}-\ket{e_{j}}\bra{e_{i}}\mid\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j}\in 2\pi i\mathds{Z}\setminus\{0\}\}^{\perp},

(52)

where
X(\bm{\varphi}_{s})\ket{e_{\{i,j\}}}=\lambda_{\{i,j\}}\ket{e_{\{i,j\}}}
.
Choose
i,j
such that
\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j}\in 2\pi i\mathds{Z}\setminus\{0\}
.
Then,

\displaystyle\langle\exp(-X(\bm{\varphi}_{s}))\Omega_{k}(\bm{\varphi}_{s})\exp(X(\bm{\varphi}_{s})),i(\ket{e_{i}}\bra{e_{j}}+\ket{e_{j}}\bra{e_{i}})\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\Omega_{k}(\bm{\varphi}_{s}),i\exp(X(\bm{\varphi}_{s}))(\ket{e_{i}}\bra{e_{j}}+\ket{e_{j}}\bra{e_{i}})\exp(-X(\bm{\varphi}_{s}))\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\Omega_{k}(\bm{\varphi}_{s}),i(e^{\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j}}\ket{e_{i}}\bra{e_{j}}+e^{\lambda_{j}-\lambda_{i}}\ket{e_{j}}\bra{e_{i}})\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\Omega_{k}(\bm{\varphi}_{s}),i(\ket{e_{i}}\bra{e_{j}}+\ket{e_{j}}\bra{e_{i}})\rangle=0.

(53)

And similarly for
\ket{e_{i}}\bra{e_{j}}-\ket{e_{j}}\bra{e_{i}}
.
Therefore,
\exp(-X(\bm{\varphi}_{s}))\Omega_{j}(\bm{\varphi}_{s})\exp(X(\bm{\varphi}_{s}))\in\mathrm{span}\{\Omega_{j}(\bm{\varphi}_{s})\}
and
(\bm{\varphi}_{s},\bm{\varphi}_{s})
is a singular point.

∎
