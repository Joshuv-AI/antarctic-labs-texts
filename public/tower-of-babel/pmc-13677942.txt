Clinical correlates for fluconazole resistance in Candida bloodstream infections
Authors: Zihuan Li, Tian Wang, Mengdie Zhang, Shu Cao, Baohong Liu, Cuiqiong Fan, Guanwen Lin, Yanfen Ming
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCandidemia poses a growing global health threat, with rising fluconazole resistance complicating clinical management. This study aimed to investigate the species distribution and fluconazole resistance profiles of Candida bloodstream infections and identify risk factors associated with fluconazole resistance.MethodsA retrospective study was conducted at a tertiary care hospital in Guangzhou, China, from 2023 to 2025. Candida isolates from blood cultures were identified using MALDI-TOF MS, and antifungal susceptibility testing was performed using the VITEK 2 system. Patients were categorized into fluconazole-resistant and non-resistant groups based on species-specific MIC breakpoints.ResultsA total of 94 Candida bloodstream infection cases were analyzed. The species distribution was: Candida albicans (26.60%), Candida glabrata (23.40%), Candida auris (20.21%), Candida tropicalis (15.96%), Candida parapsilosis (11.70%), and others (2.12%). Fluconazole resistance rates were highest in Candida auris (68.42%), followed by Candida tropicalis (46.67%) and Candida parapsilosis (9.09%). For Candida auris infections, indwelling medical devices (p = 0.017), antifungal use (p = 0.044), and antibacterial use (p = 0.004) were associated with fluconazole resistance. For Candida tropicalis infections, surgical history (p = 0.041) was the only factor associated with fluconazole resistance.ConclusionCandida auris and Candida tropicalis demonstrated alarmingly high fluconazole resistance rates, while Candida albicans and Candida glabrata remained susceptible. Indwelling medical devices, prior antimicrobial exposure and antibacterial exposure were identified as factors associated with fluconazole resistance, particularly in Candida auris infections. These findings indicate that enhanced infection control measures are warranted.

Introduction

Candidemia has emerged as a growing public health concern worldwide, imposing a substantial burden on healthcare systems (1). It is estimated that at least 600,000 cases of bloodstream infections caused by Candida species occur annually, with crude mortality rates reaching as high as 30–40% (2). Beyond the alarming mortality, these infections are also associated with prolonged hospitalization and substantially increased healthcare costs (3–8).

In recognition of the escalating threat posed by fungal infections, the World Health Organization (WHO) published its first Fungal Priority Pathogens List in 2022. This landmark document classified Candida auris (C. auris) and Candida albicans (C. albicans) as critical-priority pathogens, while Candida tropicalis (C. tropicalis) and Candida glabrata (C. glabrata) were designated as high-priority pathogens, underscoring the urgent need for enhanced surveillance, diagnostic capacity, and therapeutic strategies (2). National surveillance data from China between 2018 and 2021 further revealed considerable species-specific variability in antifungal resistance profiles. Notably, among isolates causing candidemia, the highest azole resistance rates were observed in C. tropicalis, with 42.5% resistant to fluconazole and 38.9% resistant to voriconazole (9).

Compounding these epidemiological concerns is the persistently limited antifungal armamentarium. Currently available antifungal agents, including amphotericin B, remain a cornerstone of therapy for severe invasive fungal infections. However, their clinical utility is frequently constrained by significant adverse effects (10). Against this backdrop, the progressive emergence of antifungal resistance, particularly among Candida species, and the recent nosocomial outbreaks of multidrug-resistant C. auris have collectively drawn unprecedented attention to this genus and elevated its priority status in both clinical and research agendas (2). Of particular concern is the increasing prevalence of fluconazole-resistant Candida strains, which have demonstrated the capacity to trigger hospital-based outbreaks accompanied by disproportionately high mortality rates (11, 12). Containing such outbreaks demands rigorous and resource-intensive infection control measures, further highlighting the critical need for robust antimicrobial stewardship programs, rapid diagnostic tools, and novel therapeutic agents to effectively manage this evolving threat.

There are limited data on Candida bloodstream infections in southern China. This study focuses on the species distribution and fluconazole resistance profiles of such infections in this region, aiming to provide an evidence-based reference for the rational selection of antifungal agents and the formulation of infection control strategies.

Materials and methods

Setting

This study was conducted at Guangdong Second Provincial General Hospital, a university-affiliated tertiary care hospital in Guangzhou, Guangdong Province, China, with approximately 1,730 beds. This retrospective study analyzed the number of Candida bloodstream infection cases and the prevalence of fluconazole resistance among these isolates during the period from 2023 to 2025.

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing

Candida isolates were identified to the species level using matrix-assisted laser desorption ionization–time of flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF MS). Antifungal susceptibility testing was performed using the VITEK 2 antifungal susceptibility card, and the results were interpreted according to the Clinical Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) document, 3rd Edition, 2022. For C. auris, fluconazole MIC breakpoints were interpreted according to the CDC-recommended breakpoints (MIC ≥ 32 μg/mL). Quality control strains were tested in parallel, and all results fell within acceptable ranges.

Fluconazole resistance was defined based on species-specific minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) breakpoints as follows: for Candida albicans (C. albicans), C. parapsilosis, and C. tropicalis, MIC ≥ 8 μg/mL; for C. glabrata, MIC ≥ 64 μg/mL; and for C. auris, MIC ≥ 32 μg/mL. In this study, isolates with MIC values at or above the respective breakpoints were classified as resistant, whereas those with MIC values below the breakpoints were classified as non-resistant.

Study population and inclusion criteria

Patients were enrolled if they met both of the following criteria: (1) a confirmed or suspected diagnosis of Candida bloodstream infection with a positive blood culture for Candida species, and (2) receipt of antifungal therapy. Exclusion criteria comprised (1) duplicate cases, (2) specimens considered likely contaminated, (3) absence of antifungal susceptibility testing results, and (4) age younger than 18 years. Based on fluconazole susceptibility, patients were categorized into a fluconazole-resistant group and a non-fluconazole-resistant group.

Data collection

Clinical data of hospitalized patients with Candida isolates from blood cultures were extracted from the hospital-acquired infection intelligent management system (Beijing Ruilifang Medical Information Technology Co., Ltd.). All consecutive candidemia isolates recovered during the study period were included. The inclusion criterion of receipt of antifungal therapy was applied only to the analysis of antifungal exposure as a clinical correlate, not to the selection of isolates, so as to avoid selection bias toward more severe or resistant cases. Duplicate isolates from the same anatomical site and the same patient were excluded, and only the first isolate was retained for analysis. Baseline clinical data, including age, sex, length of hospital stay, and underlying risk factors, were obtained from the electronic medical record system V6.0 (Beijing Jiahui Meikang Information Technology Co., Ltd.). The evaluated risk factors comprised pre-existing comorbidities (hypertension, diabetes, renal insufficiency, hepatic insufficiency, etc.) and the presence of invasive indwelling devices (central venous catheters, urinary catheters, and endotracheal tubes) during hospitalization.

In addition, exposure to antimicrobial agents, including both antifungals and antibacterials, within the two-week period preceding a positive blood culture was recorded. All data were collected prior to the identification of Candida bloodstream infection.

Statistical analyses

All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS statistical software (version 29.0). Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test. For categorical variables, the selection of statistical tests for 2 × 2 contingency tables followed standard criteria based on expected cell counts and total sample size: the Pearson chi-square test was applied when all expected cell counts were ≥5 and total sample size was ≥40; Yates’ continuity-corrected chi-square test was used when any expected cell count was between 1 and 5 (1 ≤ T < 5) and total sample size was ≥40; and Fisher’s exact test was employed when any expected cell count was <1 or total sample size was<40. Counted data were described by the number of cases (percentage), and the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test was used to verify the normality of the data. For continuous variables that followed a normal distribution, the mean ± standard deviation (SD) was used to describe them, and the differences between the groups were assessed using Student’s t test. For continuous variables that did not follow a normal distribution, data were described using the median and interquartile range (IQR, P25, P75), and group comparisons were performed using non-parametric tests, specifically the Mann–Whitney U test. A multivariable logistic regression analysis was conducted for Candida. Given the small sample size and the presence of complete separation in some comparisons, exact logistic regression was used for the multivariable analysis. All statistical analyses were evaluated at the statistical significance level of p < 0.05 (two-sided).

Results

Species distribution and fluconazole resistance profiles of Candida bloodstream infections

The species composition of Candida bloodstream infections is summarized as follows: C. albicans (n = 25, 26.60%), C. glabrata (n = 23, 23.40%), C. auris (n = 19, 20.21%), C. tropicalis (n = 15, 15.96%), and C. parapsilosis (n = 11, 11.70%), with Candida krusei (C. krusei) and Candida famata (C. famata) each accounting for one isolate (1.06%) (Figure 1A). With respect to fluconazole susceptibility, all C. albicans and C. glabrata isolates were classified as non-resistant. Among the other species, fluconazole resistance was detected in 13 of 19 C. auris isolates (68.42%), 7 of 15 C. tropicalis isolates (46.67%), and 1 of 11 C. parapsilosis isolates (9.09%) (Figure 1B). Discussion

Our study provides important insights into the species distribution and fluconazole resistance profiles of Candida bloodstream infections in a tertiary care hospital in southern China. Among 94 candidemia cases, C. albicans (26.60%) remained the most prevalent species, followed by C. glabrata (23.40%), C. auris (20.21%), C. tropicalis (15.96%), and C. parapsilosis (11.70%). Alarmingly, fluconazole resistance rates were high in C. auris (68.42%) and C. tropicalis (46.67%), whereas all C. albicans and C. glabrata isolates remained susceptible. Indwelling medical devices, prior antifungal use, and antibacterial exposure were identified as factors associated with fluconazole resistance, particularly in C. auris infections. These findings carry important implications for empiric antifungal therapy selection and infection control practices.

Consistent with our findings, C. albicans remains the most frequently isolated species, while the proportion of non-albicans Candida species has been increasing (13–15). Geographically, C. albicans predominates in the Americas, Europe, and Africa, whereas C. tropicalis is more commonly isolated in Southeast Asia (16). Although C. albicans generally maintains susceptibility to antifungal therapy, non-albicans Candida species tend to exhibit reduced susceptibility, particularly to azoles (17).

The emergence of resistance in C. parapsilosis represents another serious concern. In our study, the fluconazole resistance rate for C. parapsilosis was 9.09%. Notably, one investigation reported a 36.84% increase in the incidence of C. parapsilosis infections (13). Prior to 2019, only sporadic outbreaks of fluconazole-resistant C. parapsilosis had been documented, yet these reports underscore the urgent need for ongoing resistance surveillance (17, 18).

On a global scale, C. tropicalis has demonstrated prominent azole resistance, with fluconazole non-susceptibility rates ranging from 30 to 35% in published studies (19, 20). In our cohort, however, the resistance rate of C. tropicalis to fluconazole reached 46.67%, which is somewhat higher than previously reported figures (3, 13). Given that azole antifungals remain the mainstay of first-line empirical therapy, the increasing azole resistance among Candida species not only compromises the efficacy of initial treatment but also substantially elevates the risk of therapeutic failure.

Regarding C. auris, our study observed a fluconazole resistance rate of 68.42%, reflecting a markedly high level of azole non-susceptibility. This is consistent with a report from South Africa, where the proportion of C. auris bloodstream infections increased significantly from 17% in 2019 to 31% in 2021 (1, 27). C. auris is recognized as a formidable pathogen due to its ability to contaminate the hospital environment, form biofilms on medical devices, colonize healthcare workers’ hands, and facilitate healthcare-associated outbreaks (21, 22).

In our study, indwelling medical devices, prior antifungal use, and antibacterial exposure were identified as significant risk factors for fluconazole resistance, particularly in C. auris infections. These findings are consistent with previously published reports (23–25). Additionally, the median age of patients in the C. albicans group was 70.00 years (IQR: 54.00–82.00), while that of the non-albicans Candida group was 69.00 years (IQR: 56.00–79.00). In line with other investigations, our study also observed a notably high prevalence of Candida bloodstream infections among older adult patients, further underscoring the heightened vulnerability of this population to invasive candidiasis (26).

The present study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the retrospective, single-center design limits the generalizability of our findings to other populations and healthcare settings. Second, the relatively small sample size, particularly for subgroup analyses of C. tropicalis and C. auris, may have reduced statistical power to detect additional clinical correlates. Third, the absence of molecular characterization, such as genomic sequencing, precludes definitive identification of resistance mechanisms. Fourth, mortality and other clinical outcome data were not collected in this study; therefore, we were unable to assess the impact of fluconazole resistance on patient outcomes, particularly mortality. Given that the clinical significance of resistance is largely mortality-driven, this is a major limitation. The associations identified in this study should therefore be regarded as exploratory microbiological findings rather than conclusions regarding clinical outcomes. Fifth, our study did not capture detailed data on antifungal dosing, duration of therapy, or therapeutic drug monitoring, all of which may influence treatment outcomes.

Conclusion

Candida auris and Candida tropicalis demonstrated alarmingly high fluconazole resistance rates, while Candida albicans and Candida glabrata remained susceptible. Indwelling medical devices, prior antimicrobial exposure and antibacterial use were identified as clinical correlates for fluconazole resistance, particularly in Candida auris infections. These findings indicate that enhanced infection control measures are warranted.
