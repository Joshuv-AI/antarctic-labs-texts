Identification of diagnostic and therapeutic roles of programmed cell death–related proteins in dilated cardiomyopathy: a multi-omics and experimental validation study
Authors: Jili Fan, Lihong Chen, Wentao Shang, Xiaotong Wang, Tian Gan, Xin Tan, Laichun Song, Xiaohong Bo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Dilated cardiomyopathy (DCM) is a clinically heterogeneous cardiac disorder characterized by ventricular dilation and systolic dysfunction, with limited options for mechanism-based diagnosis and targeted therapy. Programmed cell death (PCD), encompassing apoptosis, ferroptosis, pyroptosis, and other regulated mechanisms, has been implicated in the pathogenesis of DCM, yet its diagnostic and therapeutic relevance remains incompletely understood. Here, we performed an integrative multi-omics analysis combining bulk and single-cell RNA sequencing datasets to identify PCD-related molecular features associated with DCM. Through differential gene expression, WGCNA, and six machine learning algorithms, eight core genes (AGTR2, GLI2, HRK, IL10, NQO1, NT5E, SFRP1, and STAT4) were identified and used to construct a predictive model evaluated across five independent cohorts. Immune infiltration and consensus clustering revealed two distinct molecular subtypes with differential immune-metabolic signatures. Single-cell analysis demonstrated cell-type–specific expression, particularly in fibroblasts and immune cells. Drug-gene interaction mapping and molecular docking highlighted decitabine and folic acid as potential therapeutic candidates. Expression of key genes was partially validated at the mRNA and protein levels in both human and mouse myocardium. Notably, given that most transcriptomic datasets represent advanced-stage disease, these findings may reflect shared molecular features of cardiac remodeling rather than early disease-specific mechanisms. This study provides insights into PCD-associated molecular alterations in DCM and offers a basis for future research into molecular stratification and therapeutic targeting.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-52624-2.

Introduction

Dilated cardiomyopathy (DCM) is a major myocardial disease characterized by dilation of the left or both ventricles and impaired systolic function. It is a leading cause of heart failure, arrhythmias, and sudden cardiac death. The pathogenesis of DCM is complex and highly heterogeneous, involving both genetic predisposition due to mutations in key genes such as TTN and LMNA, as well as multiple exogenous triggers including viral infections, immune-inflammatory responses, metabolic disorders, and toxin exposure1,2. Immune activation, programmed cell death, and interstitial fibrosis triggered by cardiomyocyte injury collectively promote myocardial remodeling and functional decline, constituting a major pathological foundation of DCM progression. Although therapeutic advances have been made in recent years in terms of pharmacological treatments, device implantation, and heart transplantation, the overall prognosis of DCM remains unsatisfactory, with a five-year mortality rate approaching 50%. Notably, there is a lack of specific treatments for end-stage heart failure, which severely compromises patient quality of life3. Due to the insidious clinical presentation of DCM, traditional imaging and biochemical markers have limited sensitivity in early detection and dynamic assessment. There is an urgent need to explore molecular mechanisms and key genes closely associated with its pathogenesis and to construct robust and reliable risk prediction models, offering new perspectives and tools for precise subtyping, early intervention, and targeted therapy.

Programmed cell death (PCD) is a form of active cell death regulated by specific molecular mechanisms, playing a critical role in embryonic development, maintenance of tissue homeostasis, and immune surveillance4. When the regulatory network of PCD is disrupted, it may lead to abnormal cell survival or excessive clearance, disturbing tissue microenvironment homeostasis and contributing to the onset and progression of various pathological conditions5,6. Recently, the potential link between PCD and DCM has garnered growing interest.

PCD is an active form of cell death precisely regulated by specific signaling pathways and includes various modalities such as apoptosis, pyroptosis, ferroptosis, cuproptosis, autophagy, immunogenic cell death, NETosis, and disulfidptosis7–9. As a representative form of inflammatory cell death, pyroptosis has been shown to induce membrane pore formation and IL-1β release in cardiomyocytes via the NLRP3/Caspase-1/GSDMD pathway, thereby exacerbating inflammation and myocardial injury in infection-related cardiomyopathies10. Immunogenic cell death activates local immune responses in the myocardium through the release of damage-associated molecular patterns, leading to immune cell recruitment and exacerbated ventricular remodeling, and also plays a pathogenic role in non-infectious cardiomyopathies9. NETosis promotes myocardial tissue necrosis and remodeling via the release of neutrophil extracellular traps, and research indicates its marked upregulation in DCM, where it is strongly correlated with impaired left ventricular function8. Cuproptosis, a newly identified form of PCD, has been shown to be significantly activated in doxorubicin-induced cardiomyopathy models. Aprocitentan has been shown to mitigate cardiac cuproptosis and dysfunction by enhancing SIRT7 expression, which inhibits DLAT accumulation and mitochondrial stress, indicating its promise as a therapeutic agent for heart failure11. Disulfidptosis is a cytoskeleton-destructive form of cell death triggered by disulfide stress. Although research on its role in the heart is still in its infancy, emerging evidence suggests a potential link between disulfidptosis-mediated F-actin depolymerization and myocardial injury under oxidative stress conditions12. Moreover, autophagy, an essential mechanism for maintaining cellular homeostasis, plays a double-edged sword role in cardiac remodeling. Studies have shown that modulation of the USP15-BECN1 axis can effectively alleviate myocardial fibrosis, further supporting the involvement of autophagy in structural and functional remodeling in DCM13. In addition, other forms of PCD, such as apoptosis and ferroptosis, have also been extensively studied in the context of DCM14–16. However, the comprehensive relationship between these distinct forms of PCD and the immune microenvironment in DCM remains unclear, highlighting the need for further investigation to elucidate their interaction mechanisms.

Our research group has previously explored the molecular landscape of DCM by focusing on lactylation-related genes and their diagnostic and immunometabolic relevance17. That study primarily emphasized lactylation-driven transcriptional alterations, molecular subtyping, and fibroblast-centered signaling patterns in DCM. In contrast, the present study shifts the central biological focus toward programmed cell death as a fundamental pathological process underlying myocardial injury and remodeling. By integrating multiple forms of programmed cell death, including apoptosis, ferroptosis, pyroptosis, cuproptosis, and disulfidptosis, this work expands beyond a single post-translational modification and provides a systematic multi-omics investigation of PCD-related genes in DCM. In this study, we integrated differential expression analysis, weighted gene co-expression network analysis, and multiple machine learning algorithms to identify eight PCD-related signature genes and construct a risk prediction model for DCM. Moreover, we systematically evaluated the associations between the expression patterns of these key genes and clinical features, immune microenvironment, and molecular subtypes in DCM patients. We further constructed a ceRNA regulatory network to explore potential post-transcriptional regulatory mechanisms, and proposed feasible individualized therapeutic strategies through drug target prediction and molecular docking analysis. Finally, we validated the mRNA and protein expression levels of key genes in human and mouse myocardial tissues, enhancing the reliability and translational potential of our findings (Fig. 1).

WGCNA identifies DCM-associated gene modules and enriched biological processes

We retrieved mRNA expression profiles of DCM and normal control samples from datasets GSE141910 and GSE57338. PCA in three dimensions (PC1, PC2, and PC3) revealed clear separation between the DCM and control groups, indicating significant transcriptional differences (Fig. 2A and B). The heatmap in Fig. 2C shows the DEGs, including CBS, PRODH, IL10, NQO1, IL20RA, and STAT4, which exhibited significant expression differences between DCM and control samples. Genes such as IL10 and TGFB2 were notably upregulated in DCM, potentially contributing to inflammation and apoptosis. Conversely, genes like SFRP1 and STAT4 were downregulated, suggesting they may exert protective effects against myocardial remodeling. Figure 2E illustrates the topological indices of the network under different soft-threshold powers. A soft-threshold value of 12 met the scale-free topology criterion. Figure 2F shows the relationship between the soft-threshold and mean connectivity, confirming 12 as the optimal parameter for network construction.

The correlation between gene modules and the DCM phenotype is shown in Fig. 2G. The MEblue module was significantly negatively correlated with DCM (R = −0.84, p = 7e−145), indicating downregulation in DCM, whereas the MEyellow module was positively correlated (R = 0.48, p = 2e−32), indicating upregulation. Additional modules including MEgreen and MEturquoise displayed moderate yet statistically significant associations. The MEyellow module was primarily enriched in inflammatory responses, innate immune processes, and immune effector mechanisms, suggesting its strong association with immune activation in DCM patients. The MEbrown module showed enrichment in mitochondrial activity and apoptotic signaling, implicating its genes in the metabolic and apoptotic disturbances characteristic of DCM. Functional terms enriched in the MEgreen module included ER stress and intrinsic apoptosis pathways, suggesting that endoplasmic reticulum dysfunction plays a vital role in DCM pathology. The MEturquoise module was enriched in mitochondrial gene expression and small-molecule metabolic processes, suggesting close links between mitochondrial dysfunction and metabolic disturbances in DCM. The MEgrey module was mainly enriched in inflammation and circulatory system processes, further emphasizing the role of inflammation in DCM (Fig. 2H).

Identification of PCD-related core genes and functional pathway enrichment analysis

Next, to identify cell death-related genes among the DEGs in DCM, we intersected the sets of DEGs, cell death-associated genes, and WGCNA-blue module genes, resulting in 19 overlapping genes (Fig. 3A). These genes are potentially involved in the pathogenesis of DCM, particularly in cell death-related processes. Several of these genes, including IL10, TP63, NQO1, CX3CR1, and FASLG, have been reported to be associated with different forms of cell death, such as apoptosis, pyroptosis, and ferroptosis. The gene interaction network further highlights potential regulatory relationships among these genes (Fig. 3B). Genes such as TGFB2, FASLG, AGTR2, and STAT4 were centrally positioned within the network, suggesting their possible involvement in cell death-related signaling pathways in DCM. Other genes, including IL20RA and GLI2, also exhibited notable interactions, indicating potential roles in coordinated regulatory processes (Fig. 3C). KEGG pathway enrichment analysis showed that these genes were significantly enriched in pathways such as cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction, MAPK signaling, JAK-STAT signaling, FoxO signaling, and apoptosis (Fig. 3D). GO enrichment analysis further indicated involvement in biological processes related to apoptotic signaling, mitochondrial function, and immune regulation (Fig. 3E). These findings suggest that cell death–related genes may contribute to DCM pathogenesis through multiple biological processes, including apoptosis, immune modulation, and metabolic regulation. To further characterize their association with specific programmed cell death pathways, we referred to the classification of PCD-related genes summarized in Supplementary Table 2. The results indicate that these genes are distributed across multiple PCD categories, including apoptosis, ferroptosis, pyroptosis, and immune-associated cell death processes. This distribution suggests that the identified gene set reflects interconnected and potentially coordinated cell death mechanisms, rather than a single pathway-specific effect.

Machine learning-based discovery of key feature genes in DCM

To prioritize informative genes associated with DCM, we applied multiple machine learning algorithms, including random forest (RF), LASSO regression, XGBoost, support vector machine (SVM), and K-nearest neighbors (KNN), within the discovery cohorts (GSE141910 and GSE57338) (Fig. 4A). Among these methods, RF and LASSO showed relatively strong discriminative performance during internal cross-validation (Fig. 4B). The RF error rate plot (Fig. 4C) suggested stable classification performance across iterations. Feature importance ranking in the RF model identified NT5E, IL10, AGTR2, and CX3CR1 as top contributors (Fig. 4D). LASSO regression further refined feature selection by shrinking coefficients with increasing penalty parameter λ, and the optimal λ was determined based on binomial deviance (Fig. 4E–F). By intersecting the features selected by RF and LASSO, eight overlapping genes were identified: AGTR2, GLI2, HRK, IL10, NQO1, NT5E, SFRP1, and STAT4 (Fig. 4G). These genes were distributed across different chromosomes (Fig. 4H), and their expression profiles showed significant differences between DCM and control samples (Fig. 4I). Specifically, AGTR2, GLI2, HRK, NT5E, SFRP1, and STAT4 were significantly upregulated (p < 0.01), whereas IL10 and NQO1 were downregulated, suggesting their potential involvement in inflammation, apoptosis, and cell death–related processes.

Expression correlation patterns of core genes in immune cell infiltration

Correlation dot plot analysis revealed distinct association patterns between core genes and various immune cell types (Fig. 5A). NT5E showed strong negative correlations with several immune cell types, including gamma delta T cells, activated NK cells, and neutrophils, suggesting its potential role in suppressing immune activation. IL10 exhibited a strong negative correlation with resting mast cells but was positively correlated with M2 macrophages, suggesting a dual role in modulating immune responses. Spearman correlation scatter plots further confirmed that NT5E expression was significantly negatively correlated with activated NK cells (R = −0.25, p = 5.4e−05), gamma delta T cells (R = −0.21, p = 0.00082), and activated dendritic cells (R = −0.17, p = 0.0078) (Fig. 5B). NQO1 expression was similarly negatively associated with activated dendritic cells (R = −0.17, p = 0.0038) and monocytes (R = −0.13, p = 0.043). RK showed significant negative correlations with M1 macrophages (R = −0.19, p = 0.0037) and resting mast cells (R = −0.14, p = 0.03) (Fig. S1). Notably, GLI2 was negatively correlated with activated dendritic cells (R = −0.13, p = 0.047) and memory B cells (R = −0.1, p = 0.034), suggesting its possible involvement in immunosuppressive mechanisms.

Consensus clustering reveals molecular subtypes and immune heterogeneity in DCM

To further elucidate the relevance of core genes in DCM, consensus clustering was performed on DCM samples. At k = 2, the consensus matrix revealed two well-defined clusters, suggesting a stable separation into two molecular subtypes: C1 and C2 (Figs. 6A, S2). The cumulative distribution function (CDF) curves of consensus scores across different k values showed that clustering stability plateaued at k = 2 (Fig. 6B). The delta area plot illustrated the largest increase at k = 2 (Fig. 6C), supporting this as the optimal cluster number. These findings confirmed the stable division into subtypes C1 and C2 (Fig. 6D). PCA scatter plot further confirmed subtype separation, with C1 and C2 clearly divided along PC1 and PC2 dimensions, highlighting the robustness of clustering and sample heterogeneity (Fig. 6E). We further evaluated cell death–related gene expression between C1 and C2. Most genes, including NT5E, GDNF, SCG2, GLI2, CX3CR1, AGTR2, TGFB2, STAT4, FASLG, and TP63, were markedly downregulated in C2, while IL20RA, RGR, and PRODH were significantly upregulated (Fig. 6F). These findings were visualized as a heatmap (Fig. 6G), suggesting that the two subtypes may involve distinct biological processes in DCM, with the C1 subtype potentially exhibiting more pronounced inflammatory and cell death-related activity.

ceRNA regulatory network analysis

A ceRNA regulatory network was constructed by integrating multi-database predictions with differential expression analysis, including 8 core mRNAs, several differentially expressed miRNAs, and multiple candidate lncRNAs (Fig. S5). Key nodes with high connectivity included STAT3, MYC, JAK2, and SFRP1. Several miRNAs (e.g., hsa-miR-342-3p, hsa-miR-223-5p, and hsa-miR-34a-5p) were predicted to target multiple genes within the network. In addition, lncRNAs such as H19, LINC00472, and LINC01094 exhibited relatively extensive connectivity.

Analysis of drug associations and target networks for core genes

Drug–gene interaction analysis based on the DGIdb database identified five core genes (STAT4, NT5E, NQO1, IL10, AGTR2) with reported interactions (Fig. 7A). These interactions involved immune-related molecules, metabolic regulators, and clinically used agents. Integration with CTD database results further identified candidate compounds, including decitabine and folic acid (Fig. 7B). Molecular docking analysis showed that folic acid exhibited relatively strong binding affinities with NQO1, NT5E, and AGTR2, while decitabine demonstrated moderate binding capacity with several targets (Table S3). Structural visualization suggested potential interactions between these compounds and key residues within the binding pockets (Fig. S6).

scRNA-seq analysis uncovers cellular heterogeneity and feature gene expression in DCM

A total of 48,422 high-quality cells were included for downstream analysis. UMAP clustering revealed the cellular structure and annotation results, identifying eight major cell types: fibroblasts, endothelial cells, macrophages, T cells, NK cells, smooth muscle cells, monocytes, and pericytes (Fig. 8A). Cell type distribution was relatively balanced across individual samples, though specific subpopulations-such as fibroblasts and macrophages-were markedly enriched in the DCM group (Fig. 8B). Grouping by disease status revealed more concentrated clustering of DCM cells in UMAP space, suggesting DCM-associated remodeling of the myocardial cellular microenvironment (Fig. 8C). Marker gene expression analysis confirmed that each cell subtype exhibited a unique transcriptional signature (Fig. 8D). In single-cell expression analysis of key genes (Fig. 8E), IL10, STAT4, and HRK were mainly upregulated in T cells and myeloid cells, suggesting their involvement in inflammatory activation and immune regulation in DCM. SFRP1, NT5E, GLI2, and AGTR2 were predominantly expressed in fibroblasts, suggesting important roles in fibrosis and tissue remodeling. NQO1 showed high expression in both fibroblasts and endothelial cells, indicating a potential role in oxidative stress and metabolic response.

Construction and validation of a risk prediction model based on core feature genes

A nomogram-based risk prediction model for DCM was constructed using the predefined 8-gene signature derived from the discovery stage (Fig. 9A). Decision curve analysis indicated that the model provided net benefit within a threshold probability range of 0.1–0.6, suggesting potential utility for risk stratification (Fig. 9B). The calibration curve demonstrated good agreement between predicted and observed outcomes (Fig. 9C). To evaluate model generalizability, the fixed gene signature was applied to four independent external cohorts (GSE19303, GSE116250, GSE126569, and GSE165303). In GSE19303, the model achieved an AUC of 0.800 (95% CI 0.630–0.945), indicating moderate-to-good discriminative performance (Fig. 9D). In GSE116250 and GSE126569, the AUC reached 1.000 (Fig. 9E–F), while GSE165303 yielded an AUC of 0.997 (Fig. 9G). Although these results suggest strong separation between DCM and control samples in certain datasets, the near-perfect AUC values should be interpreted with caution, as they may be influenced by factors such as limited sample size, cohort composition, and pronounced transcriptomic differences between failing and non-failing donor hearts. Therefore, these estimates may reflect optimistic performance in specific datasets rather than universally generalizable predictive accuracy. Additionally, the diagnostic performance of each individual feature gene was evaluated across datasets (Fig. 9H–K). Several genes, including NT5E, STAT4, SFRP1, and GLI2, consistently demonstrated relatively high discriminative ability.

Multi-cohort validation supports stable expression of core genes in DCM

To validate the stability of core gene expression across different datasets, we performed external validation using three independent GEO datasets (GSE126569, GSE116250, and GSE165303) to assess the expression levels of AGTR2, GLI2, HRK, IL10, NQO1, NT5E, SFRP1, and STAT4. Except for IL10 in GSE126569, which did not reach statistical significance, all other genes showed significant expression differences across most datasets, with trends consistent with previous bioinformatics analyses (Fig. S7). These findings further support the reliability of the selected core genes as potential contributors to DCM pathogenesis. To further validate gene expression changes at the animal level, we established a doxorubicin-induced DCM mouse model. Histological analysis showed that myocardial tissue in the DCM group exhibited disorganized structure and significant damage (HE staining), along with marked fibrosis (Masson staining). Echocardiography revealed a decline in left ventricular function, confirming successful model construction (Fig. 10A, B). qPCR analysis of mouse myocardial tissue revealed significant upregulation of AGTR2 and GLI2, downregulation of IL10, NQO1, SFRP1, and STAT4, while NT5E showed no significant difference between groups (Fig. 10C). To ensure consistency with transcriptomic trends, we validated the protein expression of AGTR2, GLI2, IL10, and NQO1. GLI2 and AGTR2 protein levels were significantly elevated in DCM tissue, IL10 was markedly reduced, while NQO1 protein levels showed no significant difference (Fig. 10D).

Discussion

In this study, we identified 19 DEGs associated with PCD, and further screened 8 core genes with stable expression patterns and high classification efficiency using multiple machine learning algorithms to construct a DCM risk prediction model. The model demonstrated encouraging discriminative performance across multiple independent validation cohorts, with most AUC values exceeding 0.8. However, these results should be interpreted with caution, particularly in datasets showing near-perfect discrimination, as cohort composition, limited sample size, and pronounced transcriptomic differences between failing and non-failing donor hearts may contribute to optimistic performance estimates. We further integrated clinical phenotype data to construct a nomogram combining risk scores and clinical variables, which demonstrated high calibration accuracy and net benefit, providing a potential tool for molecular characterization and individualized risk stratification of DCM.

Previous studies have demonstrated that various forms of PCD, such as ferroptosis, pyroptosis, and apoptosis, are closely associated with immune activation, fibrotic remodeling, and metabolic dysregulation, contributing to the pathogenesis and progression of cardiovascular diseases, particularly DCM8–10. In this study, we leveraged multiple DCM datasets to develop a gene signature consisting of eight PCD-related genes (AGTR2, GLI2, HRK, IL10, NQO1, NT5E, SFRP1, and STAT4), and conducted comprehensive bioinformatic analyses to explore their characteristics and clinical relevance in DCM progression.

To validate the expression patterns of core genes, RT-qPCR was performed in a DCM mouse model, showing results largely consistent with predictions. Subsequent Western blotting in both human and mouse myocardial tissues revealed protein-level expression trends of AGTR2, GLI2, and IL10 in line with expectations, while NQO1 showed no significant changes at either the mRNA or protein level. This discrepancy suggests that its regulation may involve context-dependent mechanisms, including post-transcriptional or post-translational modulation, and highlights the complexity of its role in cardiac pathology. AGTR2, a downstream receptor of angiotensin II, typically mediates protective effects such as anti-proliferation, pro-apoptosis, and vasodilation. Studies have shown that AGTR2 downregulation is closely associated with myocardial remodeling, increased vascular resistance, and impaired cardiac function under various pathological conditions18. For example, prenatal exposure to PFOS significantly reduces AGTR2 expression, leading to heightened sensitivity to angiotensin II and impaired vasodilation in uterine arteries, thereby increasing cardiovascular risk19. Similar regulatory roles of AGTR2 may be implicated in DCM. GLI2 is a key transcription factor in the Hedgehog signaling pathway and has recently been shown to play important roles in the cardiovascular system.On one hand, persistent activation of GLI2 is closely linked to fibrotic diseases such as systemic sclerosis, where it promotes fibroblast activation and collagen deposition, aggravating tissue fibrosis20. On the other hand, GLI2 signaling regulates the transition of cardiomyocytes from proliferation to maturation in the heart and is upregulated in pathological states such as DCM, suggesting a role in myocardial remodeling21. HRK is a BH3-only protein that plays a critical role in regulating apoptosis. Studies have shown that in treatment-induced senescent melanoma cells, HRK expression is systemically downregulated, thereby allowing BCL-xL to bind BAK, suppressing mitochondrial membrane permeability and preventing apoptosis22. Furthermore, in hepatocyte models lacking HRK, no detectable expression was observed, suggesting its function may be limited in certain cell types23. This mechanism highlights the potential regulatory role of HRK in senescence-associated apoptosis resistance. IL-10 is a key anti-inflammatory cytokine that exerts protective effects after acute myocardial infarction by modulating macrophage phenotypes and fibrotic processes. Its expression levels are associated with inflammation and cardiovascular risk24, indicating its immunoregulatory potential in DCM. NQO1 functions as an essential antioxidant enzyme with cardioprotective effects. On one side, NQO1 is induced through Nrf2 signaling, alleviating myocardial oxidative damage25. Conversely, in high-glucose environments, circ-DAPK1-driven degradation of NQO1 promotes endothelial ferroptosis, indicating its role in ferroptosis regulation in cardiovascular contexts26. On one hand, NT5E produces adenosine, which activates A2A receptors to suppress myocardial inflammation and apoptosis, especially in cirrhotic cardiomyopathy, via NF-κB pathway inhibition27. On the other hand, in the context of liver fibrosis, NT5E regulates hepatic stellate cell senescence and activation by inhibiting AURKA ubiquitination and modulating the p53 signaling pathway28, suggesting its important role in metabolism-related diseases. As an antagonist of the Wnt signaling pathway, SFRP1 deficiency leads to cardiac structural disorganization, fibrosis, and functional deterioration, indicating its protective role in myocardial fibrosis regulation29. In DCM models, elevated sFRP1 expression is also closely associated with increased myocardial cell stiffness30. STAT4 has been implicated in both fibrotic and immune processes in cardiovascular disease. Wang et al. confirmed that blocking STAT4 in kidney fibrosis models downregulates TGF-β–driven fibrotic protein expression, supporting its therapeutic potential31. According to Lu et al., STAT4 expression is elevated in ischemic cardiomyopathy and correlates with immunosuppressive and metabolic signaling, highlighting its value as a prognostic biomarker32. In our current study, the expression of these feature genes was found to be closely associated with molecular characteristics and immune status in DCM patients, suggesting their potential as diagnostic and subclassification biomarkers in DCM. Notably, the inclusion of NQO1 in the feature set was based on its contribution to the multigene signature derived from machine learning models, rather than its independent differential expression alone. This suggests that its predictive value may arise from combinatorial effects with other genes rather than as a standalone biomarker.

To evaluate the diagnostic and predictive value of the constructed feature genes in DCM, we developed a nomogram model integrating the expression of 8 core genes and validated its performance in multiple independent cohorts. The model achieved relatively high AUC values across different datasets, suggesting that the selected genes capture biologically meaningful differences between DCM and control samples. Nevertheless, the near-perfect performance observed in some cohorts should not be overinterpreted, and further validation in larger and more clinically diverse populations is needed. In addition, we further explored the relationship between core gene expression and clinical features, revealing that some genes (AGTR2, NQO1) exhibited age-dependent expression differences, while sex had no significant effect. These findings suggest that the feature genes may not only serve as potential diagnostic markers for DCM, but also participate in age-related pathological processes. Given the heterogeneity and complex pathogenesis of DCM, further studies involving larger sample sizes, diverse etiologies, and multi-dimensional clinical data are warranted to expand the model’s applicability and deepen understanding of its underlying regulatory mechanisms through experimental validation.

The myocardial immune microenvironment plays a complex and pivotal role in the progression of DCM33,34. Notably, our consensus clustering analysis identified two molecular subtypes with distinct immune and transcr.

iptional profiles, suggesting that underlying biological heterogeneity exists even within the analyzed cohorts. Our findings underscore substantial immune heterogeneity in DCM, with distinct immune cell profiles observed between the C1 and C2 molecular subtypes. Subtype C2 exhibited a notable reduction in immune-activating cells—such as γδ T cells, activated NK cells, and M1 macrophages—accompanied by increased infiltration of immature dendritic cells, M0 macrophages, and resting mast cells, indicative of an immunosuppressive or exhausted immune state. This immunosuppressive infiltration pattern was highly consistent with the expression of several core genes, particularly NT5E (CD73), which positively correlated with immunosuppressive cells. NT5E promotes terminal differentiation and dysfunction of T cells via adenosine signaling, and has been shown to predict cardiovascular events and immune dysregulation in hemodialysis patients35. At the cellular level, GLI2 is upregulated in fibroblasts, suggesting its potential role in promoting myocardial fibrosis by activating extracellular matrix synthesis pathways. Previous studies have shown that GLI2, as a downstream transcription factor of the Hedgehog signaling pathway, can upregulate fibrotic molecules such as IL-6 and COL1A1, participating in cardiac remodeling36. Similarly, IL10 is enriched in immune cell clusters and serves as a marker of M2 macrophages. It has been shown to suppress inflammation, alleviate fibrosis, and regulate the immune crosstalk between macrophages and mesenchymal stem cells through a bidirectional feedback mechanism, thereby improving ventricular remodeling37. In addition, STAT4, a central node in inflammatory pathways, is elevated in DCM and correlates with activated immune populations. STAT4 has been shown to enhance IL-12-induced Th1 immunity and contribute to TGF-β-mediated fibrosis, indicating its role in driving cardiac pathological remodeling38,39. In summary, our findings suggest that key genes such as NT5E, GLI2, IL10, and STAT4 may jointly shape the immunosuppressive microenvironment of DCM by modulating T cell exhaustion, immune polarization, and fibroblast activation. These results provide new insights into the immune mechanisms of DCM and lay a theoretical foundation for immunomodulatory interventions.

From a clinical intervention perspective, drug prediction and ceRNA network analysis may provide preliminary insights into potential regulatory interactions and candidate therapeutic targets in DCM. Several core genes identified in this study were associated with known compounds, suggesting possible links between these targets and existing pharmacological pathways. For example, previous studies have reported that the AGTR2 agonist C21 exerts cardioprotective effects in experimental heart failure models, while NT5E (CD73) has been implicated in immunomodulation and cardiovascular risk regulation40,41. In addition, folic acid has been shown to influence fibrotic processes through epigenetic mechanisms, although its effects may be context-dependent and require careful interpretation42,43. These findings provide indirect support for the potential relevance of the identified targets.

It is important to note that the drug-gene interaction and molecular docking analyses in this study are primarily based on computational predictions and indirect associations. Therefore, the translational applicability of these findings remains limited at this stage. Regarding existing evidence, while compounds such as decitabine and folic acid have been investigated in the context of epigenetic regulation, fibrosis, and metabolic modulation, there is currently limited direct experimental or clinical evidence supporting their use specifically in dilated cardiomyopathy. Their potential relevance to DCM is therefore inferred from related biological processes rather than disease-specific validation. Consequently, these findings should be interpreted with caution and considered as preliminary hypotheses. Further in vitro, in vivo, and clinical studies are required to validate the therapeutic potential and safety of these candidate compounds in DCM.

At the non-coding RNA level, the constructed ceRNA network highlighted potential interactions among mRNAs, miRNAs, and lncRNAs, with STAT3 and SFRP1 as central nodes. Previous studies have suggested that lncRNA H19 may regulate cardiac fibrosis and apoptosis via the miR-29b/STAT3 axis, and that STAT3 plays a role in inflammation and cardiac remodelin44–46. These observations are consistent with the predicted regulatory relationships in our analysis. However, it should be emphasized that these findings are primarily based on computational predictions and literature-supported associations. Therefore, they should be interpreted cautiously and regarded as hypothesis-generating rather than definitive evidence of therapeutic mechanisms. Further experimental and in vivo studies are required to validate these interactions and assess their clinical relevance.

Although this study established a DCM prediction model based on PCD-related core genes and demonstrated consistent performance across multiple cohorts, several important limitations should be acknowledged. First, the majority of transcriptomic datasets used in this study were derived from explanted hearts of patients with end-stage heart failure undergoing cardiac transplantation. Therefore, the observed gene expression alterations may primarily reflect advanced pathological remodeling, systemic decompensation, and pharmacological influences, rather than early-stage or disease-initiating mechanisms specific to DCM. In addition, control samples in these datasets were mainly obtained from non-failing donor hearts, which may be affected by confounding factors such as brain death, catecholamine surge, and ischemic time prior to tissue collection. These inherent differences between diseased and control samples represent a well-recognized limitation in human cardiac transcriptomic studies and should be considered when interpreting the results. Second, the doxorubicin-induced cardiomyopathy model used in this study represents a specific form of toxic cardiac injury, which is mechanistically distinct from idiopathic or genetic forms of DCM. Although this model is widely used due to its reproducibility and ability to recapitulate key features such as myocardial fibrosis, oxidative stress, and cell death, it does not fully capture the complex etiological and pathological spectrum of DCM. Therefore, the experimental validation results should be interpreted as supporting evidence for shared pathological processes rather than direct validation of DCM-specific mechanisms. Third, the predictive model constructed in this study is based on myocardial tissue transcriptomic data. Given that endomyocardial biopsy is an invasive procedure with associated clinical risks, such an approach is not applicable for routine screening of DCM in clinical practice. Accordingly, the current model should be regarded primarily as a research tool for molecular characterization and risk stratification, rather than a clinically deployable screening method. Future studies integrating non-invasive biomarkers are required to enhance its translational potential. Fourth, although the model showed strong discriminative performance in several external cohorts, the near-perfect AUC values observed in some datasets may reflect optimistic estimates influenced by limited sample size, cohort composition, and substantial transcriptomic differences between failing and non-failing donor hearts. Therefore, these findings should be interpreted with caution and not considered as definitive evidence of clinical-grade predictive performance. Larger, well-controlled, and prospectively collected datasets are required for more rigorous validation. Fifth, although NQO1 was identified as one of the core feature genes, it did not show consistent changes in experimental validation. This discrepancy may be attributed to post-transcriptional or post-translational regulation, differences between bulk and cell-type-specific expression, or variation in disease stage and experimental context. Therefore, its role should be interpreted cautiously, and it is more appropriately considered as part of a multigene signature rather than an independent biomarker. Finally, DCM is a highly heterogeneous disease with diverse etiologies, including genetic, inflammatory, and toxic causes. Due to the lack of detailed clinical annotation in public datasets, stratified analyses based on specific etiological subtypes could not be performed. Notably, our consensus clustering analysis identified two molecular subtypes with distinct immune and transcriptional profiles, suggesting that biological heterogeneity exists even within the analyzed cohorts. However, the identified gene signatures may primarily reflect shared molecular features of advanced-stage DCM rather than subtype-specific mechanisms. Future studies incorporating well-characterized clinical cohorts are required to further delineate etiology-specific molecular patterns.

In this study, we systematically identified eight core genes closely associated with PCD—AGTR2, GLI2, HRK, IL10, NQO1, NT5E, SFRP1, and STAT4—and constructed a DCM risk prediction model with strong diagnostic performance, demonstrating high stability and generalizability across multiple validation cohorts. Immune infiltration and molecular subtype analyses revealed that these genes may contribute to DCM pathogenesis by modulating immune cell infiltration and cell death pathways. Single-cell analysis showed that the core genes were actively expressed in fibroblasts and immune cells, indicating their crucial roles in myocardial remodeling and inflammation regulation. Furthermore, by integrating ceRNA regulatory networks and drug target prediction, we identified several potential therapeutic agents, offering new insights into personalized treatment strategies for DCM. Experimental validation in both murine and human myocardial tissues further strengthened the credibility of the findings. Overall, this study provides a basis for future investigations into the molecular stratification, pathogenesis and therapeutic target exploration of DCM.

Methods

Data sources and preprocessing

The datasets GSE14191047 and GSE5733848 were obtained from the GEO database and used as the discovery cohort, while GSE1930349, GSE1162550, GSE12656951, and GSE165303 were used as validation cohorts (Table S1) for transcriptomic analysis of DCM and control samples. The majority of myocardial samples in these datasets were derived from left ventricular tissues obtained at the time of cardiac transplantation from patients with end-stage heart failure, predominantly classified as New York Heart Association (NYHA) class III–IV. These samples therefore represent advanced disease states rather than early-stage DCM. Control samples in these datasets were primarily obtained from non-failing donor hearts, which may have been affected by factors such as brain death, catecholamine surge, and ischemic time prior to tissue collection. Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed using the prcomp function to assess the overall distribution and clustering patterns of samples across datasets. A total of 19 categories of PCD-related genes were manually curated from the literature52. After removing duplicates, 1567 PCD-related genes were included for subsequent analysis (Table S2). The research process is shown in Fig. 1.

Identification of differentially expressed genes (DEGs)

The Limma package (Linear Models for Microarray Data) was used to analyze DEGs. Genes significantly differentially expressed between DCM and control groups were selected based on FDR < 0.05 and log2FC ≥ 1, and their expression profiles were visualized using the “heatmap” package.

Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA)

The WGCNA package53 was used to construct a gene co-expression network and identify gene modules associated with the DCM phenotype. Outlier samples were removed using hierarchical clustering dendrograms to ensure data quality. To ensure a scale-free network topology, topological indices and gene connectivity were calculated under various soft-thresholding powers. The optimal soft-thresholding power was selected for network construction. Genes were grouped into modules based on expression similarity using the dynamic tree cutting algorithm. Each module was color-coded, and module–trait relationships were calculated to identify modules significantly associated with the DCM phenotype. Cytoscape was used for visualizing the co-expression relationships among genes within the identified modules.

GO and KEGG pathway enrichment analysis

Functional enrichment analysis of significantly DEGs was performed using DAVID54 and ClusterProfile55 for Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways56. Enriched pathways with p values < 0.05 were identified, and the number and significance of genes involved in each pathway were quantified. Bar plots and Sankey diagrams were used to visualize enriched pathways along with corresponding gene counts and p-values. Pathways were ranked based on -log10(p-value), with color intensity representing statistical significance.

Construction of gene networks and analysis of gene–gene interactions

The ccgraph package was used to construct the network of cell death–related genes. A gene–gene interaction network of cell death–related genes was built using the STRING database. A Circos diagram was employed to map the chromosomal locations of core genes across the entire genome.

Machine learning model development and feature selection

Machine learning–based feature selection and model development were performed exclusively in the discovery cohorts (GSE141910 and GSE57338). Based on the intersecting genes identified from differential expression analysis, WGCNA modules, and programmed cell death (PCD)-related gene sets, multiple classification algorithms were applied, including Random Forest (RF), Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) regression, Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN), Support Vector Machine (SVM), and Gradient Boosting Machine (GBM). Model training and internal evaluation were conducted using stratified tenfold cross-validation within the discovery cohorts to ensure balanced class distribution across folds and to reduce overfitting. Within each cross-validation iteration, the dataset was partitioned into training and validation subsets, and all feature selection procedures (including RF-based importance ranking and LASSO-based regularization) were performed exclusively within the training folds. This approach ensured that no information from the validation folds was used during feature selection, thereby minimizing the risk of data leakage. In addition, any preprocessing steps were conducted independently within each training fold before model fitting, and the same transformation parameters were applied to the corresponding validation fold. Performance metrics, including accuracy, area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), and F-score, were calculated based on the validation folds. The DALEX package was used to visualize model residual distributions, and ROC curves were generated using the pROC package. The intersection of genes selected by RF and LASSO was defined as the final feature gene set. Importantly, this feature selection process was conducted solely within the discovery cohorts, and the resulting gene signature was fixed prior to external validation to avoid information leakage.

Correlation analysis between immune infiltration and key genes

Expression profiles were integrated with immune infiltration scores to systematically evaluate correlations with core genes. Immune infiltration was quantified using the CIBERSORT57 and MCPcounter58 algorithms, and the data were uniformly normalized. Spearman correlation coefficients were calculated to assess associations, with scatter plots constructed using ggplot2 and ggpubr. Density plots were also included to intuitively display the distribution trends of gene expression across immune cell types.

Consensus clustering analysis

Unsupervised consensus clustering was performed using the R package ConsensusClusterPlus59. The K-means algorithm was applied for iterative clustering with a subsampling proportion of 80% and 1000 repetitions to enhance result stability. The number of clusters (k) ranged from 2 to 9, and the optimal k was selected based on clustering performance. The stability of clustering results was evaluated using cumulative distribution function (CDF) curves, and the relative change in CDF area across different k values was calculated. Consensus scores were used to assess clustering consistency and robustness. PCA was conducted to validate clustering reliability by projecting the samples into a lower-dimensional space. The distribution of samples was displayed using PC1 and PC2 to assess the spatial distinctness of the clusters. In addition, gene set variation analysis (GSVA) and immune infiltration data were integrated to further explore the functional and immunological differences among the identified subtypes60.

Construction of ceRNA regulatory network

Target miRNAs for core mRNAs were predicted by integrating multiple databases including miRWalk, TargetScan, and miRDB. Consistently predicted miRNAs with high scores and stable binding sites were selected. Subsequently, upstream lncRNAs regulating differentially expressed miRNAs were systematically predicted using the miRcode and starBase databases. Expression data were used to identify lncRNAs significantly dysregulated in DCM tissues to enhance biological relevance. Based on integrated data, the identified lncRNA–miRNA–mRNA interactions were further filtered to retain those meeting ceRNA interaction criteria, namely co-expression of lncRNAs and mRNAs and the presence of shared competitive miRNA binding sites. The regulatory network was visualized using Cytoscape.

Prediction of drug targets and molecular docking

Drug–gene interaction data were mined using the Drug–Gene Interaction Database (DGIdb)61 and the Comparative Toxicogenomics Database (CTD)62. Initially, the core feature genes were queried in the DGIdb database to obtain information on interacting drugs, interaction scores, targeting levels, and approval status. Based on the downloaded data, bubble plots were generated using R packages (ggplot2, dplyr, and tidyr) to visualize interaction strength, score level, and approval status of drug–gene pairs. The CTD database was further used to query the core genes and construct a drug–gene regulatory network. The coverage density of drugs and the connectivity of each core gene within the network were used to evaluate their potential relevance in the drug intervention map.

Representative candidate small-molecule drugs were selected for molecular docking with corresponding core protein targets. Protein structures were retrieved from the PDB database, if no experimentally resolved structure was available, AlphaFold2 predictions were used. The 3D structures of small-molecule drugs were downloaded from the PubChem database and subjected to energy minimization preprocessing. Molecular docking was carried out using AutoDock Vina, and the docking results were visualized in 3D using PyMOL and Discovery Studio to analyze key binding sites, hydrogen bonds, and hydrophobic interactions.

Single-Cell RNA sequencing data processing and analysis

The DCM single-cell RNA-seq dataset was derived from GSE18385263. Raw data were aligned to the human reference genome using Cell Ranger (10X Genomics) or STAR, and low-quality cells (gene count < 200 or mitochondrial gene proportion > 10%) were filtered out during quality control. Further analyses were carried out using the Seurat package (v4.3.0), applying SCTransform for normalization to reduce technical variability. Dimensionality reduction was initially conducted with PCA to capture key sources of variation, and subsequently visualized using the UMAP algorithm for nonlinear projection. Clustering was conducted using the Louvain algorithm (FindClusters), and cell types were annotated based on known canonical marker genes. The expression profiles of DCM-associated core genes were then extracted, and their spatial distribution across UMAP coordinates was visualized using the Nebulosa package, clarifying their cellular origins and distribution characteristics.

Construction and external validation of the DCM risk prediction model

A nomogram-based risk prediction model for DCM was constructed using the predefined feature genes identified in the discovery stage. The nomogram was developed using the rms R package. Decision Curve Analysis (DCA) was performed to evaluate the potential clinical net benefit of the model across a range of threshold probabilities. Model calibration was assessed using calibration curves to examine the agreement between predicted and observed outcomes. To assess model generalizability, the fixed gene signature was applied to independent external datasets (GSE19303, GSE116250, GSE126569, and GSE165303). These datasets were used exclusively for validation purposes, without any feature reselection, model retraining, or parameter optimization. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were generated, and AUC values were calculated using the pROC package. Additionally, logistic regression models based on individual feature genes were constructed to evaluate their independent predictive performance across validation cohorts.

Correlation analysis with clinical features

Correlation analysis was performed using clinical phenotype data (including age and sex) from the GSE141910 dataset. The expression data of core genes were extracted and modeled in relation to age and sex, respectively. For continuous variables, Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient was used for non-parametric correlation analysis, for binary variables, logistic regression was applied to evaluate the explanatory power of gene expression for sex status. Visualization was performed using the ggpubr and ggplot2 packages to generate correlation scatter plots with marginal distributions and significance labels (p < 0.05).

Human myocardial tissue samples

Human myocardial tissue samples were obtained from patients with DCM and non-failing donor hearts. DCM samples were collected from patients undergoing orthotopic heart transplantation at Wuhan Yaxin General Hospital between January 2023 and December 2024. All patients were diagnosed with end-stage heart failure secondary to DCM, classified as New York Heart Association (NYHA) class III–IV, with severely reduced left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF < 35%). Patients with ischemic cardiomyopathy, significant valvular heart disease, congenital heart disease, or other known causes of cardiomyopathy were strictly excluded to ensure a relatively homogeneous DCM cohort. Left ventricular myocardial tissues were collected from the explanted failing hearts at the time of transplantation. Control samples were obtained from non-failing donor hearts deemed unsuitable for transplantation due to non-cardiac reasons (e.g., trauma or brain death), with no prior history of cardiovascular disease. All tissue samples were collected and stored in the biobank of Wuhan Yaxin General Hospital. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Wuhan Yaxin General Hospital (Approval No.: WAGHMEC-KY-2025010) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants or their legal representatives prior to sample collection.

Establishment of a mouse model of DCM

The experimental animals used in this study were 8-week-old male C57BL/6 J mice (weighing 20–25 g), purchased from the Experimental Animal Center of the Medical College of Soochow University (License No.: SYXK 2021-0065). All animal experiments were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Soochow University (Approval No.: 202408A0374) and were conducted in strict accordance with the Regulations for the Administration of Affairs Concerning Experimental Animals and the Guidelines for the Welfare and Ethical Review of Laboratory Animals, with all efforts made to minimize animal suffering. All animal experiments were performed between August 22, 2024 and October 22, 2024. This study is reported in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines for reporting animal research. Mice were housed in a well-controlled environment with a temperature of 22 ± 2 °C, humidity of 55 ± 5%, and a 12-h light/dark cycle, with free access to food and water. After one week of acclimatization, mice were randomly divided into a normal control group and a DCM model group, with six mice per group. The DCM group was modeled by doxorubicin induction. Doxorubicin was dissolved in 0.9% saline to prepare a 1 mg/mL working solution and administered intraperitoneally at 2.5 mg/kg twice a week for 4 consecutive weeks64. The control group received an equal volume of saline during the same period. At the end of the 4-week modeling period, mice underwent high-frequency echocardiography to evaluate left ventricular systolic function. Mice were then anesthetized with 2% isoflurane and euthanized via cervical dislocation after ensuring a painless state.

Echocardiography in mice

Cardiac ultrasound examinations were performed using a small-animal dedicated imaging system (FUJIFILM VisualSonics Vevo 2100/3100). Mice were fasted for 4–6 h prior to the examination (with free access to water) and anesthetized with 1.5–2.0% isoflurane inhalation to maintain stable vital signs and reduce motion artifacts, with continuous monitoring of body temperature and respiratory rate. Under anesthesia, mice were placed in a supine position on a heated imaging platform (temperature maintained at 36–37 °C), with limbs gently secured using tape. Chest hair was removed and ultrasound gel was applied to the thoracic region. A 30–40 MHz ultrasound transducer was placed along the left sternal border and adjusted to acquire optimal imaging angles. Short-axis (papillary muscle level) and long-axis images were acquired to evaluate left ventricular structure and function. M-mode imaging was used to record dynamic changes of the left ventricle during end-diastole and end-systole, measuring left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF), fractional shortening (LVFS), and end-diastolic diameter (LVEDD). To ensure data reliability, three consecutive cardiac cycles were recorded for each mouse, and the average was used for subsequent analysis.

Histological staining and analysis of mouse myocardial tissue

Mice were euthanized immediately after echocardiography, and heart tissues were rapidly harvested and rinsed with PBS to remove residual blood. The tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h, then dehydrated, cleared, and embedded in paraffin at room temperature. Paraffin-embedded myocardial tissues were sectioned at 4–6 μm using a microtome, mounted on slides, and dried for further processing.

For hematoxylin and eosin (HE) staining, sections were deparaffinized twice in xylene (10 min each), rehydrated through graded ethanol (100%, 95%, 85%, 70%, each for 5 min), and finally rinsed with distilled water. Sections were stained with hematoxylin for about 5 min, washed under running water, differentiated briefly with 1% acid alcohol, and then returned to blue in running water for another 5 min. Cytoplasmic staining was performed with 0.5–1% eosin for 1–2 min, followed by ethanol dehydration and xylene clearing, then mounted for observation.

Histopathological evaluation was performed under light microscopy to examine myocardial cell arrangement, morphological alterations, tissue disruption, and immune cell infiltration.

Masson’s trichrome staining was performed to assess myocardial fibrosis. After deparaffinization and rehydration, nuclei were stained with Weigert’s iron hematoxylin for 5–10 min, followed by rinsing and brief differentiation with acidic solution, and background removal via water wash. Masson’s composite solution was used to stain muscle fibers red and collagen fibers blue for 5–10 min. Differentiation was performed using phosphomolybdic or phosphotungstic acid, followed by counterstaining with light green or orange G to improve contrast. Finally, the sections were dehydrated through graded ethanol and cleared in xylene before mounting, and collagen deposition and fibrosis were evaluated under the microscope.

RNA extraction and quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR)

Mouse myocardial tissues were harvested and snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen. Total RNA was extracted using Trizol reagent, and the purity and concentration were determined with an N50 UV spectrophotometer (Immilen, Germany). cDNA was synthesized from total RNA using a reverse transcription kit (TransGen Biotech, Beijing, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Real-time quantitative PCR was then performed using specific primers and SYBR Green dye (Selleck, Houston, TX, USA) on a qPCR detection system. The cycling conditions were typically set as follows: 95 °C for 30 s (initial denaturation), followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 5–10 s and 60 °C for 30 s (annealing/extension). After the experiment, β-actin was used as the internal control, the 2−ΔΔCt method was applied to quantify mRNA levels of AGTR2, GLI2, IL10, NQO1, NT5E, SFRP1, STAT4, and β-actin (Table 1).

Western blot

Approximately 10–20 mg of human or mouse myocardial tissue was homogenized or sonicated in lysis buffer containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors (brand, catalog number). After incubation at 4 °C for 30 min, the lysate was centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 10–15 min, and the supernatant was collected for further analysis. Protein concentration was determined using the BCA method. Samples were normalized to equal concentrations, mixed with 5 × SDS loading buffer (containing β-mercaptoethanol), and denatured at 95 °C for 10 min. Denatured protein samples were resolved on SDS-PAGE gels (concentration based on target protein size), run at 80 V through the stacking gel and 120 V through the resolving gel. After electrophoresis, proteins were transferred onto a nitrocellulose membrane at 4 °C using 300 mA current for 60–120 min. Membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk for 1 h, followed by overnight incubation at 4 °C with primary antibodies: GLI2 (1:1000, Rabbit, Proteintech), AGTR2 (1:1000, Rabbit, Affinity), NQO1 (1:1000, Rabbit, Proteintech), and IL10 (1:2000, Mouse, Proteintech). On the following day, membranes were washed three times with TBST (10 min each), then incubated for 1 h at room temperature with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:10,000, goat anti-rat IgG, CST 98,164; and goat anti-mouse IgG, CST 91,196). Bands were visualized using ECL reagents and captured with an imaging system. Protein expression levels were normalized to internal controls (GAPDH or β-actin), and band intensities were quantified using ImageJ software.

Statistical methods

Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism version 9.0. All data were presented as mean ± standard deviation (Mean ± SD). Differences between groups were evaluated using independent sample t-tests. Statistical significance was defined as follows: *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, and ns indicating no significant difference. All tests were two-tailed, and a p value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
