Dispersion of first sound in a weakly interacting ultracold Fermi liquid
Thomas Repplinger, Songtao Huang, Yunpeng Ji, Nir Navon, Hadrien Kurkjian

Abstract
At low temperature, a normal gas of unpaired spin-1/2 fermions is one of the cleanest realizations of a Fermi liquid. It is described by Landau’s theory, where no phenomenological parameters are needed as the quasiparticle interaction function can be computed perturbatively in powers of the scattering length a a , the sole parameter of the short-range interparticle interactions. Obtaining an accurate solution of the transport equation nevertheless requires a careful treatment of the collision kernel, as the uncontrolled error made by the relaxation time approximations increases when the temperature T T drops below the Fermi temperature. Here, we study sound waves in the hydrodynamic regime up to second order in the Chapman-Enskog’s expansion. We find that the frequency ω q \omega_{q} of the sound wave is shifted above its linear departure as ω q = c 1 ​ q ​ ( 1 + α ​ q 2 ​ τ 2 ) \omega_{q}=c_{1}q(1+\alpha q^{2}\tau^{2}) where c 1 c_{1} and q q are the speed and wavenumber of the sound wave and the typical collision time τ \tau scales as 1 / a 2 ​ T 2 1/a^{2}T^{2} . Besides the shear viscosity, the coefficient α \alpha is described by a single second-order collision time which we compute exactly from an analytical solution of the transport equation, resulting in a positive dispersion α > 0 \alpha>0 . Our results suggest that ultracold atomic Fermi gases are an ideal experimental system for quantitative tests of second-order hydrodynamics.

II
Transport equation at low temperature

Landau’s theory postulates that a Fermi liquid is described
by a local quasiparticle distribution
n_{\sigma}(\textbf{p},\textbf{r},t)
, which is the number
of quasiparticles of spin
\sigma
having momentum
p
at position
r
and time
t
.
This distribution deviates slightly on average from its value in the
Fermi sea
n^{0}_{\sigma}(p)=\Theta(p_{\rm F}-p)
(where
p_{\rm F}
is the Fermi momentum and
\Theta
the Heaviside distribution), and
the energy
of an arbitrary quasiparticle configuration is expanded to second
ordrer in
\delta n_{\sigma}^{0}=n_{\sigma}-n^{0}_{\sigma}
:

E=E_{0}+\sum_{\textbf{p},\textbf{r},\sigma}\epsilon_{\sigma}^{0}(\textbf{p})\delta n_{\sigma}^{0}(\textbf{p},\textbf{r})\\
+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\textbf{p},\sigma,\textbf{p}^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime},\textbf{r}}f_{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}(\textbf{p},\textbf{p}^{\prime})\delta n_{\sigma}^{0}(\textbf{p},\textbf{r})\delta n_{\sigma^{\prime}}^{0}(\textbf{p}^{\prime},\textbf{r})

(1)

In the general case, the ground state dispersion relation

\epsilon_{\sigma}^{0}
of quasiparticles and the interaction function
f_{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}

are phenomenological parameters usually reexpressed in terms of
an effective mass and Landau parameters. In the case of a
weakly interacting gas with contact interactions, these quantities
can be calculated perturbatively in powers of the coupling constant
g=4\pi a/m

[
12
]
(we use
\hbar=k_{\rm B}=1
throughout this work).
To first order in perturbation theory, we have:

\epsilon_{\sigma}^{\rm 0}(\textbf{p})=\frac{p^{2}}{2m}+g\rho_{\sigma^{\prime}},\quad f_{\uparrow\downarrow}={g}/V,\quad f_{\sigma\sigma}=0

(2)

where
V
is the volume of the gas, and
\rho_{\sigma}
the density of fermions of spin
\sigma
.

The time evolution of the quasiparticle distribution is described by a
transport equation:

\frac{\partial n_{\sigma}}{\partial t}+\frac{\partial\epsilon_{\sigma}}{\partial\textbf{p}}\cdot\frac{\partial n_{\sigma}}{\partial\textbf{r}}-\frac{\partial(\epsilon_{\sigma}+U_{\sigma})}{\partial\textbf{r}}\cdot\frac{\partial n_{\sigma}}{\partial\textbf{p}}=I_{\sigma}

(3)

where
U_{\sigma}
is an external driving field,
\epsilon_{\sigma}(\textbf{p},\textbf{r})=\epsilon_{\sigma}^{\rm 0}(\textbf{p})+\sum_{\textbf{p}^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime}}f_{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}(\textbf{p},\textbf{p}^{\prime})\delta n_{\sigma^{\prime}}^{0}(\textbf{p}^{\prime},\textbf{r})

is the local energy of the quasiparticles, and the collision integral is given in this weakly interacting
limit by Fermi’s golden rule

I_{\sigma}(\textbf{p})=\frac{2\pi g^{2}}{V^{2}}\sum_{\textbf{p}_{2},\textbf{p}_{3},\textbf{p}_{4}}\delta_{\textbf{p}+\textbf{p}_{2},\textbf{p}_{3}+\textbf{p}_{4}}\delta\left(\epsilon_{\sigma}(\textbf{p})+\epsilon_{\sigma^{\prime}}(\textbf{p}_{2})-\epsilon_{\sigma}(\textbf{p}_{3})-\epsilon_{\sigma^{\prime}}(\textbf{p}_{4})\right)\\
\left[(1-n_{\sigma}(\textbf{p}))(1-n_{\sigma^{\prime}}(\textbf{p}_{2}))n_{\sigma}(\textbf{p}_{3})n_{\sigma^{\prime}}(\textbf{p}_{4})-n_{\sigma}(\textbf{p})n_{\sigma^{\prime}}(\textbf{p}_{2})(1-n_{\sigma}(\textbf{p}_{3}))(1-n_{\sigma^{\prime}}(\textbf{p}_{4}))\right].

(4)

In this expression, all the quasiparticle distributions
n
are evaluated at position
r
and time
t
, which reflects the assumption that collisions are local and instantaneous.

As we seek the eigenmodes of the transport equation, we assume that the drive is weak,
slowly varying in both time and space,
and we linearize Eq. (
3
) around the thermal equilibrium distribution
n_{\rm eq}(\epsilon_{0})=1/(1+\textrm{e}^{(\epsilon_{0}-\mu)/T})
.
We focus here on the unpolarized
case so
\mu
is the common chemical potential of the two spin species
and
\epsilon_{0}\equiv\epsilon^{0}_{\uparrow}=\epsilon^{0}_{\downarrow}
.
We also restrict ourselves to excitations of the total density, and define the total driving field

U_{\rm tot}=U_{\uparrow}+U_{\downarrow}
, that we decompose in Fourier
space
U_{\rm tot}(\textbf{r},t)=\text{Re}(U\sum_{\textbf{q}}\textrm{e}^{\textrm{i}(\textbf{q}\cdot\textbf{r}-\omega t)})
.
Restricting to terms of first order in temperature
T
and the drive intensity

U
, the transport equation obeyed by
\delta n(\textbf{p})=n_{\uparrow}(\textbf{p})+n_{\downarrow}(\textbf{p})-2n_{\rm eq}(\epsilon_{0}(\textbf{p}))

is, in Fourier space:

\left(\omega-\frac{\textbf{p}\cdot\textbf{q}}{m}\right)\delta n(\textbf{p},\textbf{q},\omega)+\frac{\partial n_{\rm eq}}{\partial\epsilon_{0}}\frac{\textbf{p}\cdot\textbf{q}}{m}\left(g\delta\rho(\textbf{q},\omega)+U\right)\\
=\textrm{i}I_{\rm lin}

(5)

where
\delta\rho(\textbf{q},\omega)
is the fluctuation of the total density about
\rho_{\rm eq}=\rho_{\uparrow}+\rho_{\downarrow}
(see Eq. (
) below),
and the linearized collision integral
I_{\rm lin}
is given by Eq. (
44
) in Appendix.

For
T\ll T_{\rm F}
, transport occurs in a energy shell of typical depth
T
around the
Fermi energy
\epsilon_{\rm F}

[
47
,
1
]
.
We thus reparametrize the quasiparticle distribution as

\delta n(\textbf{p})=U\frac{\partial n_{\rm eq}}{\partial\epsilon_{0}}\Big|_{\epsilon_{0}=\epsilon_{\rm 0}(\textbf{p})}\nu(\epsilon,\theta)\\
\text{ with }\epsilon=\frac{\epsilon_{\rm 0}(\textbf{p})-\mu}{T}=\frac{p^{2}/2m-\epsilon_{\textrm{F}}}{T}

(6)

where
\theta
is the angle between
p
and
q
, and we have used the equation of state
\mu=\epsilon_{\textrm{F}}+g\rho_{\rm eq}/2
of the weakly interacting Fermi gas. At low temperature, the reduced energy
\epsilon
varies from
-\infty
to
+\infty

p
varies from
0
to
+\infty

Restricting the transport equation to momenta

lying in the relevant energy shell around the Fermi surface,
we obtain, in the limit
T/T_{\textrm{F}}\to 0

\epsilon

\epsilon^{\prime}

(c-\cos\theta)\nu(\epsilon,\theta)-\frac{k_{\textrm{F}}a}{\pi}\cos\theta\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\textrm{d}\epsilon^{\prime}g(\epsilon^{\prime})\int_{0}^{\pi}\sin\theta^{\prime}\textrm{d}\theta^{\prime}\nu(\epsilon^{\prime},\theta^{\prime})\\
+\frac{\textrm{i}}{\omega_{0}\tau}\left[\Gamma(\epsilon)\nu(\epsilon,\theta)+\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\textrm{d}\epsilon^{\prime}\int\sin\theta^{\prime}\textrm{d}\theta^{\prime}\frac{\textrm{d}\phi^{\prime}}{2\pi}\mathcal{N}_{\rm od}(\epsilon,\epsilon^{\prime},u)\nu(\epsilon^{\prime},\theta^{\prime})\right]=-\cos\theta

(7)

where
g(\epsilon)=1/(4\text{cosh}^{2}(\epsilon/2))
is the dimensionless density of states,

\omega_{0}=v_{\textrm{F}}q
is a typical excitation frequency (with
v_{\textrm{F}}=\sqrt{2\epsilon_{\textrm{F}}/m}
the Fermi velocity),
c=\omega/\omega_{0}

is the dimensionless excitation frequency, and

\tau=\frac{\pi}{2ma^{2}T^{2}}

(8)

is a typical collision time. For numerical applications, we replace

\tau_{\sigma}=\frac{\pi}{2m\sigma T^{2}}=\frac{\tau}{4\pi}

(9)

where
\sigma=4\pi a^{2}
is the scattering cross section.
This is because the center of the hydrodynamic to collisionless crossover

\omega_{0}\tau_{\sigma}\simeq 1
rather than
\omega_{0}\tau\simeq 1
.
The diagonal part of the collision kernel in Eq. (
7

\Gamma(\epsilon)=\pi^{2}+\epsilon^{2}.

(10)

This dimensionless function sets the physical quasiparticle lifetime to
\tau_{\rm qp}(\epsilon)=\tau/\Gamma(\epsilon)

The off-diagonal part of the kernel reads

\mathcal{N}_{\rm od}(\epsilon,\epsilon^{\prime},u)={\frac{\mathcal{S}(\epsilon,-\epsilon^{\prime})}{\sqrt{2(1+u)}}-2\frac{\mathcal{S}(\epsilon,\epsilon^{\prime})}{\sqrt{2(1-u)}}}\\
\text{with }\mathcal{S}(\epsilon,\epsilon^{\prime})=\frac{\epsilon-\epsilon^{\prime}}{2}\frac{\text{cosh}\frac{\epsilon}{2}}{\text{cosh}\frac{\epsilon^{\prime}}{2}\text{sinh}\frac{\epsilon-\epsilon^{\prime}}{2}}

(11)

where the angular dependence comes through

The conservation of the number of quasiparticle in a collision provides
a relation between
\Gamma
and
\mathcal{S}
:

\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\textrm{d}\epsilon^{\prime}\mathcal{S}(\epsilon,\epsilon^{\prime})=\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\textrm{d}\epsilon^{\prime}\frac{g(\epsilon^{\prime})}{g(\epsilon)}\mathcal{S}(\epsilon^{\prime},\epsilon)=\frac{\Gamma(\epsilon)}{2}.

(12)

III
Hydrodynamic limit

The hydrodynamic limit is the regime of short collision times

\omega_{0}\tau\ll 1.

(13)

In this regime, collisions bring the quasiparticle distribution back to equilibrium
much faster than the typical time
1/\omega_{0}
at which the sound wave evolves. Only the few components
of the distribution that are not affected by collisions (
i.e.
those that belong to the zero-energy
space of the collision kernel) remain significantly excited.
Eq. (
13
) defines a dynamical regime, accessible from any equilibrium state
of the phase diagram (i.e. for any value of
a
and
T
).
Here we have sent
a
and
T
to 0 at fixed
\omega_{0}\tau

and finally expanded the transport equation in powers of
\omega_{0}\tau
.
To guarantee that the system remains in the hydrodynamic regime,
it is required to set
q/k_{\rm F}=2(T/T_{\rm F})^{2}(k_{\rm F}a)^{2}\omega_{0}\tau_{\sigma}
in such a way that
\omega_{0}\tau_{\sigma}\ll 1
.
For a finite-size system (of size
L
),
q
is limited to
q=\pi/L
which in typical experiments is
\approx 0.01k_{\rm F}
.

The usual expansion to order
O(\omega_{0}\tau)^{1}
yields the transport
coefficients entering the Fermi-liquid version of the Navier-Stokes equations.
Here, we will push to second-order in the hydrodynamic expansion, that is
O(\omega_{0}\tau)^{2}

and retrieve the sound dispersion from the quantum analogue of Burnett’s hydrodynamics
[
43

44
]

To obtain the exact solution of the transport equation in the hydrodynamic limit,
we expand the distribution

\epsilon Q_{n}=Q_{n+1}+\xi_{n}Q_{n-1}\quad\text{with}\quad\xi_{n}\equiv\frac{||Q_{n}||^{2}}{||Q_{n-1}||^{2}}

(16)

We choose
Q_{0}=1

Q_{1}=\epsilon
as the initial condition

IV
First-sound waves

The drive on the right-hand-side of Eq. (
21
) is coupled to
\nu_{0}^{1}
.
The perturbation it generates is therefore symmetric in
\epsilon
,
that is,
\nu_{n}^{l}=0
for all
n
odd. In particular the sound wave
does not generate fluctuations of the energy density:
\nu_{1}^{0}=0
.
To leading order in
\omega_{0}\tau
the system of Eqs. (
23
)–(
24
) describes an ideal hydrodynamic behavior, that is,
an undamped resonance at
\omega_{q}=c_{1}q
with the first
sound velocity

\frac{c_{1}}{v_{\textrm{F}}}=\sqrt{\frac{1+\frac{2k_{\textrm{F}}a}{\pi}}{3}}.

(32)

To study how the resonance deviates from
c_{1}
,
one must compute the set
\vec{\nu}^{2}=(\nu_{n}^{2})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}

of the non-conserved components in the
l=2
subspace, that we write
as an infinite-dimension vector.
Keeping the leading and subleading terms in Equation (
21
) for
l=2
, we obtain

\left(c+\frac{\textrm{i}}{\omega_{0}\tau}\mathcal{M}^{2}\right)\vec{\nu}^{2}=\frac{2}{3}\nu_{0}^{1}\vec{u}_{0}+O(\omega_{0}\tau)^{2}

(33)

where we have introduced the matrix
\mathcal{M}^{2}=(\mathcal{M}_{nn^{\prime}}^{l=2})_{n,n^{\prime}\in\mathbb{N}}
and the unit vectors
(\vec{u}_{n})_{n^{\prime}\in\mathbb{N}}=(\delta_{nn^{\prime}})_{n^{\prime}}
.
We have neglected in Eq. (
33
) the vector
\vec{\nu}^{3}
and the components of
\vec{\nu}^{1}

orthogonal to the zero eigenvector
\nu_{0}^{1}\vec{u}_{0}
:
both are of order
O(\omega_{0}\tau)^{2}
(see Fig.
1
), and hence negligible compared to
the subleading term
c\vec{\nu}^{2}
(of order
O(\omega_{0}\tau)
) in Eq. (
33
).
As is noted in Ref.
[
48
]
, the expansion
in powers of
\omega_{0}\tau
becomes very tedious
as soon as higher Legendre components have to be taken
into account. Fortunately this is not the case in our calculation to order

O(\omega_{0}\tau)^{2}
: the transport equation can be truncated
to
l=2
, and only two collision times are needed to express

\nu_{0}^{2}
from Eq. (
33
):

\nu_{0}^{2}=-\frac{2\textrm{i}\omega_{0}}{3}\nu_{0}^{1}\left[\tau_{\eta}+\textrm{i}\omega_{0}ct_{\eta}^{2}\right]+O(\omega_{0}\tau)^{3}

(34)

where we introduce the viscous collision time
\tau_{\eta}/\tau=\vec{u}_{0}\frac{1}{\mathcal{M}^{2}}\vec{u}_{0}
and
the second-order viscous time
t_{\eta}/\tau=\sqrt{\vec{u}_{0}\frac{1}{(\mathcal{M}^{2})^{2}}\vec{u}_{0}}
.
These two parameters characterize the Burnett hydrodynamical equations on the density and parallel velocity;
a relaxation time approximation would not distinguish them since it amounts to replacing
\mathcal{M}^{2}

by a number. In previous exact calculations,
\tau_{\eta}
has been expressed as an infinite serie
[
14
,
13
]
or as continued fraction
[
49
]
, which converge rather slowly when truncated at
n=n^{\prime}=n_{\rm max}
:
\tau_{\eta}-\tau_{\eta}^{(n_{\rm max})}=O(1/n_{\rm max}^{2})
.
Numerically, we obtain

\tau_{\eta}\simeq 1.079\tau_{\eta}^{(0)}\simeq 1.288\tau_{\sigma}\text{\ \ and\ \ }t_{\eta}\simeq 1.098\tau_{\eta}^{(0)}\simeq 1.311\tau_{\sigma}

(35)

where we have introduced the viscous collision time truncated at
n=0
:
\tau_{\eta}^{(0)}/\tau_{\sigma}=4\pi/\mathcal{M}^{l=2}_{00}=15/4\pi
.

Plugging Eq. (
34
) in Eqs. (
23
)–(
24
), we obtain a quasi-Lorentzian shape for
the density response:

\chi_{\rho}=\frac{mk_{\textrm{F}}}{6\pi^{2}}\frac{1}{c^{2}-\bar{c}_{1}^{2}+\frac{4\textrm{i}\omega_{0}c}{15}\left(\tau_{\eta}+\textrm{i}\omega_{0}ct_{\eta}^{2}\right)}.

(36)

The pole
z_{1}
of
\chi_{\rho}(c)
is located at:

z_{1}=\bar{c}_{1}-\frac{2\textrm{i}}{15}\omega_{0}\tau_{\eta}+\frac{2\omega_{0}^{2}}{225\bar{c}_{1}}(15\bar{c}_{1}^{2}t_{\eta}^{2}-\tau_{\eta}^{2})

(37)

where
\bar{c}_{1}=c_{1}/v_{\textrm{F}}
.
Going back to the physical units, we write the resonance frequency
\omega_{q}=\text{Re}(z_{1})\omega_{0}
,
the main result of this work, as

\omega_{q}\!=\!c_{1}q\left[1+\theta(\bar{c}_{1})\epsilon_{\textrm{F}}^{2}\tau_{\sigma}^{2}\left(\frac{q}{mc_{1}}\right)^{2}\!\!\!+O(\omega_{0}\tau)^{3}\right]

(38)

with
\theta(c)\simeq 0.917c^{2}-0.059
. The first deviation from the linear spectrum
c_{1}q
is thus proportional to
q^{3}
. The dispersion is positive at weak
coupling since
\theta(\bar{c}_{1})\simeq 0.247
for
\bar{c}_{1}=1/\sqrt{3}
.
The function
\theta
vanishes and changes sign at
c_{\rm inv}\simeq 0.25
such that an inversion of the sign of the dispersion may occur in settings where the ratio
c_{1}/v_{\textrm{F}}

is lower than
c_{\rm inv}
.

The damping rate
\Gamma_{q}=-\text{Im}(z_{1})\omega_{0}
of the sound wave is determined
only by the shear viscosity
\eta
in this low-temperature regime:

\Gamma_{q}=\frac{2}{3m\rho}\eta q^{2}\text{ with }\eta=\frac{2}{5}\rho\epsilon_{\textrm{F}}\tau_{\eta}\simeq 0.5153\rho\epsilon_{\textrm{F}}\tau_{\sigma}.

(39)

Our value coincides with the exact calculations of
\eta
done in the context of
3
He
[
15
]

or neutron matter
[
8
]
.
This is a factor
\tau_{\eta}/{\tau_{\eta}^{(0)}}\simeq 1.08
above the value in the relaxation time approximation
of Refs.
[
38
,
28
,
30
]
(called the “variational approximation” therein).
We note that the underestimation consecutive to the approximation is larger than stated before
[
28
,
30
]
.
Conversely, in the Abrikosov-Khalatnikov approximation
[
20
,
47
,
39
]
,
the viscosity is overestimated by a factor
{\tau_{\eta}^{\rm(AK)}}/{\tau_{\eta}}=4\tau_{\eta}^{(0)}/3{\tau_{\eta}}\simeq 1.24
.

V
Thermal conductivity

Equation (
25
) on the energy density
allows us to compute the thermal conductivity of the gas.
At low temperature, the energy current
\nu_{1}^{1}
exists only if the
quasiparticle distribution has components odd in
n
, that is if
there is an energy-imbalance across the Fermi surface,
which is not the case in a sound wave.
We compute this energy current by writing down the transport equation for
n
odd and
l=1
:

c\nu_{n}^{1}+\frac{\textrm{i}}{\omega_{0}\tau}\sum_{n^{\prime}}\mathcal{M}_{nn^{\prime}}^{1}\nu_{n^{\prime}}^{1}-\nu_{n}^{0}-\frac{2}{5}\nu_{n}^{2}=0

(40)

To leading order in
\omega_{0}\tau
, this gives

\frac{\textrm{i}}{\omega_{0}\tau}\mathcal{M}^{1}\vec{\nu}_{\rm odd}^{1}=\nu_{1}^{0}\vec{u}_{1}+O(\omega_{0}\tau)

(41)

where
\vec{\nu}_{\rm odd}^{1}=(0,\nu_{1}^{1},0,\nu_{3}^{1},0,\ldots)
is the odd component of
\vec{\nu}^{1}
,
and we recall that
\mathcal{M}
does not couple the even and odd components. We solve Eq. (
41
)
for
\nu_{1}^{1}
, which yields

\nu_{1}^{1}=-\textrm{i}\omega_{0}\tau_{\kappa}\nu_{1}^{0}\quad\text{with}\quad\frac{\tau_{\kappa}}{\tau_{\sigma}}=\vec{u}_{1}\frac{4\pi}{\mathcal{M}^{1}}\vec{u}_{1}\simeq 0.7478

(42)

Going back to Eq. (
25
) on the energy density, we obtain the heat diffusion equation

\left(\omega+\textrm{i}{D_{\kappa}}q^{2}\right)\nu_{1}^{0}=0\text{ with }D_{\kappa}=\frac{1}{3}{v_{\textrm{F}}^{2}}\tau_{\kappa}\simeq 0.249{v_{\textrm{F}}^{2}\tau_{\sigma}}.

(43)

The thermal conductivity
\kappa
is related to
D_{\kappa}
by
\kappa=c_{V}D_{\kappa}
with

c_{V}=mp_{\rm F}T/3
the heat capacity.
The relaxation time approximation
\tau_{\kappa}^{(0)}/\tau_{\sigma}=15/8\pi
turns out much worse
for the thermal conductivity than for the viscosity,
with the error reaching
25\%
. We are thus in neat
disagreement with the claim of Ref.
[
30
]

that
|\kappa-\kappa_{(0)}|/\kappa<2\%
.

VI
Conclusion

Dispersion of sound was extensively studied in classical gases, where it is very well captured
by second-order (Burnett) hydrodynamics
[
50
]
. We have shown here that a
gas of fermions obeys a very similar kind of Burnett hydrodynamics, although
the presence of a Fermi sea leads to a
1/T^{2}
behavior of the collision time, as
opposed to
\tau\propto\sqrt{T}
in a classical gas. A quantum gas is also affected
by a quantum Vlasov force on the left-hand side of the transport equation.
To leading order in
a
, this force is the gradient of
g\rho(\textbf{r})
, and therefore independent of the quasiparticle momentum

p
. This may not be true to higher order in the interaction strength, and the effect on
hydrodynamic transport equations remains to be investigated.

Experiments on ultracold gases have often measured transport
coefficients
[
23
,
51
,
52
,
53
,
54
,
55
]
,
and thus seem mature to investigate departures from standard Navier-Stokes hydrodynamics.
The dispersion of first sound could be measured
by tracking the variations of the resonance frequency in the density-density
response function with the excitation wavenumber
q
. Alternatively,
this resonance frequency could be compared to the speed of sound known from the equation of state.
Dispersive effects also affect the real time dynamics, such as the equilibration after a quench
[
56
]
,
or the propagation of shock waves
[
42
,
57
,
58
]
.
