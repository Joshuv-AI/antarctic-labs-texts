Equivalent Conditions for Domination of M ⁡ ( 2 , ℂ ) \mathrm{M}(2,{\mathbb{C}}) -sequences
Chang Sun and Zhenghe Zhang

Abstract
. It is well known that a SL ⁡ ( 2 , ℂ ) \mathrm{SL}(2,{\mathbb{C}}) -sequence is uniformly hyperbolic if and only it satisfies a uniform exponential growth condition. Similarly, for GL ⁡ ( 2 , ℂ ) \mathrm{GL}(2,{\mathbb{C}}) -sequences whose determinants are uniformly bounded away from zero, it has dominated splitting if and only if it satisfies a uniform exponential gap condition between the two singular values. Inspired by [ QTZ ] , we provide a similar equivalent description in terms of singular values for M ⁡ ( 2 , ℂ ) \mathrm{M}(2,{\mathbb{C}}) -sequences that admit dominated splitting. We also prove a version of the Avalanche Principle for such sequences.



1.
Introduction

Throughout this paper, we consider
B\in\ell^{\infty}({\mathbb{Z}},{\mathrm{M}}(2,{\mathbb{C}})
. In particular, we fix a large
M>0
so that
\sup_{j\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\|B(j)\|<M
, where
\|A\|
always denotes the operator norm for
A\in{\mathrm{M}}(2,{\mathbb{C}})
. Throughout this paper, unless otherwise stated, all matrices are assumed to be nonzero and to have operator norm bounded above by
M
. We also let
C>0,c>0
be universal constants, where
C
is large and
c
is small. We define

B_{n}(j)=\begin{cases}B(j+n-1)\cdots B(j),&n\geq 1,\\
I_{2},&n=0,\end{cases}

where
I_{2}
is the identity matrix and

B_{-n}(j)=[B_{n}(j-n)]^{-1}=B(j-n)^{-1}\cdots B(j-1)^{-1},\ n\geq 1,

if all matrices involved are invertible. Regarding
\mathrm{M}(2,{\mathbb{C}})
-sequences admitting dominated splitting, the following definition is introduced in
[
AZ
]
and is proved to be appropriate.

Definition 1
.

We say that
B:{\mathbb{Z}}\to\mathrm{M}(2,{\mathbb{C}})
admits if for each
j\in{\mathbb{Z}}
, there are one-dimensional spaces
E^{u}(j)
and
E^{s}(j)
of
{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
with the following properties:

(1)

E^{u},E^{s}
are
B
–invariant in the sense that for all
j\in{\mathbb{Z}}
, it holds that

B(j)[E^{u}(j)]\subseteq E^{u}(j+1)\mbox{ and }B(j)[E^{s}(j)]\subseteq E^{s}(j+1).

(2)

There exist
N\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}
and
\lambda>1
such that

\|B_{N}(j)\vec{u}(j)\|>\lambda\|B_{N}(j)\vec{s}(j)\|

for all
j\in{\mathbb{Z}}
and all unit vectors
\vec{u}(j)\in E^{u}(j)
and
\vec{s}(j)\in E^{s}(j)
.

(3)

\inf_{j\in{\mathbb{Z}}}d\big(E^{u}(j),E^{s}(j)\big)>0
.

(4)

For the same
N
from (2), it holds that
\inf_{j\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\|B_{N}(j)\|>0
.

A similar definition may be found at
[
QTZ
]
for sequence of bounded linear operators on a Banach space. Condition (b) or (c) implies that
E^{s}(j)\neq E^{u}(j)
, hence
{\mathbb{C}}^{2}=E^{s}(j)\oplus E^{u}(j)
for all
j\in{\mathbb{Z}}
. In condition (c),
d(W,V)
denotes a distance between two one-dimensional spaces
W
and
V
of
{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
. For its definition, see equation (
12
) at the beginning of Section
4
. Here we say the space
E^{u}
dominates the space
E^{s}
. As noted in
[
AZ
]
, under conditions (a)-(c), condition (d) actually implies something stronger:

\inf_{j\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\|B_{n}(j)\|>0\mbox{ for all }n\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}.

We shall sometimes use or prove condition (d)’. From now on,
B\in{\mathcal{D}}{\mathcal{S}}
means
B
has dominated splitting.

We have the following equivalent conditions for
{\mathcal{D}}{\mathcal{S}}
-sequences. For any nonzero matrix
A\in{\mathrm{M}}(2,{\mathbb{C}})
, we define
\sigma_{1}(A)=\|A\|
and
\sigma_{2}(A)=\frac{|\det(A)|}{\|A\|}
which are the so-called singular values of
A
. One may find more detailed information about the singular values of
A
at the beginning of Section
4
. Let
\mu>1
and
0<{\varepsilon}<\mu
where
{\varepsilon}
may be arbitrarily small. Then we define:

\displaystyle\mbox{(SVG)}\hskip 14.22636pt\sup_{j\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\left\{\frac{\sigma_{2}(B_{n}(j))}{\sigma_{1}(B_{n+1}(j))},\ \frac{\sigma_{2}(B_{n}(j+1))}{\sigma_{1}(B_{n+1}(j))}\right\}<C\mu^{-n}\mbox{ for all }n\geq 0.

\displaystyle\mbox{(FI)}\hskip 22.76228pt\sup_{j\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\left\{\frac{\sigma_{1}(B_{n}(j))}{\sigma_{1}(B_{n+1}(j))},\ \frac{\sigma_{1}(B_{n}(j+1))}{\sigma_{1}(B_{n+1}(j))}\right\}<C\mu^{(1-{\varepsilon})n}\mbox{ for all }n\geq 1,

where following the terminology of
[
QTZ
]

\mathrm{(SVG)}
stands for singular value gap and
\mathrm{(FI)}
stands for fast invertibility. Our main goal is to prove the following theorem:

Theorem 1
.

Let
B\in\ell^{\infty}({\mathbb{Z}},\mathrm{M}(2,{\mathbb{C}}))
.
B\in{\mathcal{D}}{\mathcal{S}}
if and only if
B
satisfies both
\mathrm{(SVG)}
and
\mathrm{(FI)}
.

Domination is an intensively studied notion in dynamical systems which naturally generalizes the notion of uniform hyperbolicity. It was introduced by and plays a key role in the works of Mañé
[
M
]
and Liao
[
L
]
on Smale’ s stability conjecture. The term
dominated splitting
was introduced by Mañé in
[
M
]
. We refer the readrs to
[
BV
,
BDP
,
P
]
and the referenes therein regarding recent works in smooth dynamical systems involving domination.

In this paper, we focus on equivalent conditions for domination via singular values. For bounded
\mathrm{SL}(2,{\mathbb{C}})
-sequences, we can use conditions (a) and (b) to define uniform hyperbolicity. It is straightforward to see that for such sequences,
\mathrm{(SVG)}
is equivalent to the uniform exponential growth
\mathrm{(UEG)}
condition

\inf_{j\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\|B_{n}(j)\|\geq C\mu^{\frac{n}{2}}\mbox{ for all }n\geq 1.

It is well-known that uniform hyperbolicty of such sequences is equivalent to
\mathrm{(UEG)}
, see e.g.
[
Y
]
or
[
Z
]
for detailed information.

In the context of bounded
\mathrm{GL}(2,{\mathbb{C}})
-sequences whose determinants are unifromly bounded away from zero, one only needs conditions (a) and (b) of Definition
1
to define domination. For such sequences, domination is equivalent to
\mathrm{(SVG)}
, which is easily seen to be equivalent to a weaker form as:

(1)

\sup_{j\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\frac{\sigma_{2}(B_{n}(j))}{\sigma_{1}(B_{n}(j))}<C\mu^{-n},\mbox{ for all }n\geq 1.

Indeed, for such sequences, one can convert them into
\mathrm{SL}(2,{\mathbb{C}})
-sequences by considering

A(j)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\det(B(j))}}B(j).

Then all claimed results above follows from those of
\mathrm{SL}(2,{\mathbb{C}})
-sequences. One may also see
[
BG
]
for related discussion.

In the context of general bounded
\mathrm{GL}(2,{\mathbb{C}})
-sequences,
\mathrm{(SVG)}
is strictly stronger than (
1
). Indeed, by choosing
n=0
, one can easily see that
\mathrm{(SVG)}
implies
\inf_{j\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\|B(j)\|>0
(see e.g. the proof of Lemma
6
) while (
1
) doesn’t gurantee that. However, even for
\mathrm{GL}(2,{\mathbb{C}})
-sequences satisfying
\inf_{j\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\|B(j)\|>0
,
\mathrm{(SVG)}
is still strictly stronger than (
1
). This is because we can let
|\det(B(j))|
to be arbitrarily small. For instance, by choosing
B_{n}(j)
and
B(j+n)
appropriately, one can make
\frac{\sigma_{2}(B_{n}(j))}{\sigma_{1}(B_{n+1}(j))}
arbitrarily large while keeping
\frac{\sigma_{2}(B_{n+1}(j))}{\sigma_{1}(B_{n+1}(j))}
arbitrarily small.
\mathrm{(SVG)}
was first introduced in
[
BM
]
where it is used to show domination for injective operator-valued cocycles, namely
B(j)
’s are allowed to be injective bounded linear operators on a Banach space. In fact, even in the context of
{\mathrm{M}}(2,{\mathbb{C}})
,
\mathrm{(SVG)}
arises naturally in showing the existence of the invariant directions
E^{u}
and
E^{s}
, see the proof of Lemma
8
.

While
\mathrm{(SVG)}
is sufficient to show existence of
E^{u}
and
E^{s}
, it is however not sufficent to gurantee
E^{s}\neq E^{u}
. That is the place where
\mathrm{(FI)}
is used.
\mathrm{(FI)}
was first introduced in
[
QTZ
]
, which if translated into the context of Theorem
1
can be rewritten as:

(2)

\sup_{j\in{\mathbb{Z}},n\geq 1}\frac{\sigma_{1}(B_{n}(j))}{\sigma_{1}(B_{n+1}(j))}<C.

Similar to
[
BM
]
,
[
QTZ
]
considers domination for sequences of bounded linear operators on a Banach space.
\mathrm{(FI)}
is introduced to avoid the injectiveness assumption of
[
BM
]
. We wish to point out that even in the context of general
\mathrm{GL}(2,{\mathbb{C}})
-sequences (hence, one has injectiveness),
\mathrm{(SVG)}
is not suffcient to show
E^{u}\neq E^{s}
, see e.g. Example
1
and its remark. Indeed,
[
BM
]
considered cocycles defined over compact toplogical spaces where injectiveness implies
uniform lower bound of
|\det(B(j))|
in the context of Theorem
1
. Compared with Theorem
1
, the main result of
[
QTZ
]
is stronger in the sense that they considered more general sequences, obtained explicit lower bounds on
d(E^{s},E^{u})
, and adopted a more general version of
\mathrm{(SVG)}
which allows them to remove the uniform boundedness assumption on
\|B(j)\|
.

However, while it is in the simplest context, bounded
{\mathrm{M}}(2,{\mathbb{C}})
-sequences still keep the essential difficulty. Thus it is worthwhile to explore domination in the simplest nontrivial scenario as it allows one to see the dynamics relatively clearly. Compared with
[
QTZ
]
, one of the main advantages of approach is that the proof of Theorem
1
is significantly simpler, which is based on a soft argument used in
[
Z
]
. Basically, instead of obtaining explict lower bound of
d(E^{s}(j),E^{u}(j))
, we prove
E^{s}(j)\neq E^{u}(j)
in the singular case and reduce another unresolved case into this case via embedding the sequence into its hull. This soft argument also allows us to weaken the condition (
2
) to our
\mathrm{(FI)}
. In fact, by our proof one can see that to get
\inf_{j\in{\mathbb{Z}}}d(E^{s}(j),E^{u}(j))>0
, the real competition lies between
\frac{\sigma_{2}(B_{n}(j))}{\sigma_{1}(B_{n}(j+1))}
and
\frac{\sigma_{1}(B_{n}(j))}{\sigma_{1}(B_{n}(j+1))}
. We really want the decay rate of the former beats the growth rate of the latter. In some sense, we have rigidity of the growth rate of
\frac{\sigma_{1}(B_{n}(j))}{\sigma_{1}(B_{n}(j+1))}
: as long as it grows slower than the decaying rate of
\frac{\sigma_{2}(B_{n}(j))}{\sigma_{1}(B_{n}(j+1))}
(which is of exponential rate by
\mathrm{(SVG)}
), it is uniformly bounded above.
One may see the proof of Lemmas
9
and
10
for more detailed information.

On the other hand, even the stronger version of
\mathrm{(FI)}
as stated in (
2
) is automatically satisfied for bounded
\mathrm{GL}(2,{\mathbb{C}})
-sequences with determinants unifromly bounded away from zero. This is due to the following fact:

\sigma_{1}(B_{n}(j+1))\geq\sigma_{1}(B_{n}(j))\sigma_{2}(B(j+n))=\sigma_{1}(B_{n}(j))\frac{|\det(B(j+n))|}{\|B(j+n)\|}\geq c\sigma_{1}(B_{n}(j)).

In summary,
\mathrm{(FI)}
arises naturally if one wants to separate
E^{s}
and
E^{u}
in cases one does not have uniform invertibility of the operators.

We also further explore our techinques and prove the following version of the so-called
Avalanche Principle
for
{\mathrm{M}}(2,{\mathbb{C}})
-sequences. It generalizes
[
Z
, Theorem 5]
and consequently, extends all prior versions of the Avalanche Principle for sequences of
2\times 2
matrices.

Theorem 2
.

Let
B(j):{\mathbb{Z}}\to\mathrm{M}(2,{\mathbb{C}})
be that
0<c<\|B(j)\|<M
for all
j\in{\mathbb{Z}}
. Suppose there is a
\mu>1
large such that for each
j
, it holds that:

(3)

\displaystyle\frac{\sigma_{2}(B(j))}{\sigma_{1}(B(j))}\leq\mu^{-1};

(4)

\displaystyle\frac{\sigma_{1}(B(j+1))\sigma_{1}(B(j))}{\sigma_{1}(B(j+1)B(j))}\leq\mu^{\frac{1}{4}}.

Then
B
has dominated splitting and it holds for each
j\in{\mathbb{Z}}
and each
n\geq 3
that

(5)

\left|\log\|B_{n}(j)\|+\sum_{k=1}^{n-2}\log\|B(j+k)\|-\sum_{k=0}^{n-2}\log\|B(j+k+1)B(j+k)\|\right|\leq Cn\mu^{-\frac{1}{2}}.

Avalanche Principle is first introduced in
[
GS
]
for finite
\mathrm{SL}(2,{\mathbb{R}})
sequences. Together with large deviation type of estimates for the associated Lyapuonv exponent, it is proved to be a powerful tool in establishing quantative continuity of the Lyapunov exponent and the integrated density of states of the associated ergodic Schrödinger operators.
There are numerous generalizations since the original work of Goldstein-Schlag, e.g. orders of the matrices have been generalized from
2
to any
d\geq 2
, real valued matrices to complex valued ones. We refer the readers to the work of Bourgain-Jitomirskaya
[
BJ
, Lemma 5]
, Bourgain
[
B
, Lemma 2.6]
, and Schlag
[
S
, Lemma 1]
for of invertible matrices, and Duarte-Klein
[
DK
, Section 2.4]
for general
{\mathrm{M}}(d,{\mathbb{R}})
-matrices.

However, it was first observed in
[
Z
]
that there is a close relation between uniformly hyperbolic sequence of matrices and the Avalanche Principle in the scenario of
\mathrm{SL}(2,{\mathbb{C}})
-sequences. As a consequence, a relatively short and dynamical proof of the Avalanche Principle was obtained in
[
Z
]
. Likewise, no relation between domination and the Avalanche Principle for squences of possibly non-invertible matrices seemed to be explored (in fact, as far as we know, the work of
[
DK
]
is the only place where non-invertible finite sequences were explored). One of the main purpose of Theorem
2
is to explore such a relation and provide a relatively short and dynamical proof for
{\mathrm{M}}(2,{\mathbb{C}})
-sequences. While it is possible for us to consider general
{\mathrm{M}}(d,{\mathbb{C}})
sequences, we again wish to do things in the simplest nontrivial setup to emphasize the main ideas and key tools behind the proof. The proof is a combination of the techniques we used to prove Theorem
1
and outline of
[
Z
, Section 4]
. Theorem
2
has the potential to be highly useful in establishing Hölder continuity of the Lyapunov exponent for
{\mathrm{M}}(2,{\mathbb{C}})
-cocycles.

To conclude this section, we further explore the following example from
[
AZ
]
. It provides an example in the scenario of bounded
\mathrm{GL(2,{\mathbb{R}})}
-sequences whose norm is uniformly bounded from below where
\mathrm{(SVG)}
holds true and
\mathrm{(FI)}
fails. It also shows that merely
\mathrm{(SVG)}
is not enough to guarantee the separation of
E^{s}
and
E^{u}
.

Example 1
.

Define
\Lambda(j)=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}2^{2-|j|}&0\\
0&2^{-|j|}\end{smallmatrix}\right),D(j)=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&1\\
0&\ 2^{-|j|}\end{smallmatrix}\right)
,
and

B(j):=D(j+1)\Lambda(j)D(j)^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}2^{2-|j|}&-3\\
0&2^{-|j+1|}\end{pmatrix}.

Claim:
B
satisfies conditions (1), (2), and (4) of Definition
1
as well as
\mathrm{(SVG)}
. But
B
does not satisfies (3) or
\mathrm{(FI)}
.

Proof.

By construction,
D
converts the two obivous invariant line section of
\Lambda
to those of
B
, which are:

E^{u}(j)=\mathrm{span}\left\{\binom{1}{0}\right\}\mbox{ and }E^{s}(j)=\mathrm{span}\left\{\binom{1}{2^{-|j|}}\right\}.

One readily checks that for all
j\in{\mathbb{Z}}
and all unit vectors
\vec{u}(j)\in E^{u}(j)
and
\vec{s}(j)\in E^{s}(j)
:

\|B(j)\vec{u}(j)\|\geq 2\|B(j)\vec{s}(j)\|.

Hence, conditions (a) and (b) of Definition
1
are satisfied where in (b) we may choose
N=1
. Moreover, it is clear that
\|B(j)\|\geq 3
so that condition (d) is satisfied. However, by equation (
12
) we have

d(E^{u}(j),E^{s}(j))=2|\det(\vec{u}(j),\vec{s}(j))|\leq 2^{1-|j|}\to 0.

Hence, condtion (c) is not satisfied.

Next, let us explore
\mathrm{(SVG)}
and
\mathrm{(FI)}
. It is straightforward to see that for all
n\geq 2
:

\displaystyle B_{n}(j)

\displaystyle=D(j+n)\Lambda_{n}(j)D(j)^{-1}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\
0&2^{-|j+n|}\end{pmatrix}\cdot\begin{pmatrix}2^{2n-\sum^{n-1}_{k=0}|j+k|}&0\\
0&2^{-\sum^{n-1}_{k=0}|j+k|}\end{pmatrix}\cdot\begin{pmatrix}1&-2^{|j|}\\
0&2^{|j|}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=2^{-\sum^{n-1}_{k=1}|j+k|}\begin{pmatrix}2^{2n-|j|}&-2^{2n}+1\\
0&2^{-|j+n|}\end{pmatrix}.

Let
\|A\|_{\max}=\max_{i,j}\left|a_{ij}\right|
. It is a straightforward calculation to see that for all
A\in{\mathrm{M}}(2,{\mathbb{C}})
:

\|A\|_{\max}\leq\|A\|\leq 2\|A\|_{\max}

Then for all
j\in{\mathbb{Z}}
and
n\geq 2
, we get

\displaystyle\sigma_{2}(B_{n}(j))

\displaystyle=\frac{|\det(B_{n}(j))|}{\|B_{n}(j))\|}\leq\frac{|\det(B_{n}(j))|}{\|B_{n}(j))\|_{\max}}

(6)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2^{-2\sum^{n-1}_{k=1}|j+k|}\cdot 2^{2n-|j|-|j+n|}}{2^{2n-\sum^{n-1}_{k=1}|j+k|}}

\displaystyle=2^{-\sum^{n}_{k=0}|j+k|}

and

(7)

\sigma_{1}(B_{n}(j))=\|B_{n}(j))\|\geq\|B_{n}(j))\|_{\max}\geq 2^{-\sum^{n-1}_{k=1}|j+k|}\cdot(2^{2n}-1),

which implies for all
n\geq 2
:

\displaystyle\sup_{j\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\left\{\frac{\sigma_{2}(B_{n}(j))}{\sigma_{1}(B_{n+1}(j))},\ \frac{\sigma_{2}(B_{n}(j+1))}{\sigma_{1}(B_{n+1}(j))}\right\}

\displaystyle\leq\sup\left\{\frac{2^{-\sum^{n}_{k=0}|j+k|}}{2^{-\sum^{n}_{k=1}|j+k|}\cdot(2^{2(n+1)}-1)},\ \ \frac{2^{-\sum^{n+1}_{k=1}|j+k|}}{2^{-\sum^{n}_{k=1}|j+k|}\cdot(2^{2(n+1)}-1)}\right\}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2^{2(n+1)}-1}

\displaystyle<\frac{1}{2^{2n}}.

For
n=1
, we note that
\sigma_{2}(B(j))<2^{1-|j|-|j+1|}
for all
j\in{\mathbb{Z}}
, which together with (
7
) implies:

\displaystyle\sup_{j\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\left\{\frac{\sigma_{2}(B(j))}{\sigma_{1}(B_{2}(j))},\frac{\sigma_{2}(B(j+1))}{\sigma_{1}(B_{2}(j))}\right\}

\displaystyle\leq\sup\left\{\frac{2^{1-|j|-|j+1|}}{2^{-|j+1|}\cdot(2^{4}-1)},\ \frac{2^{1-|j+1|-|j+2|}}{2^{-|j+1|}\cdot(2^{4}-1)}\right\}

\displaystyle<\frac{2}{15}.

For
n=0
, it is clear that we have
\sigma_{1}(B(j))=\|B(j)\|\geq 3
which implies

\frac{\sigma_{2}(B_{0}(j))}{\sigma_{1}(B(j))}=\frac{\sigma_{2}(I_{2})}{\sigma_{1}(B(j))}=\frac{1}{\|B(j)\|}<\frac{1}{3}.

To sum up, we get for all
n\geq 0
:

\sup_{j\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\left\{\frac{\sigma_{2}(B_{n}(j))}{\sigma_{1}(B_{n+1}(j))},\ \frac{\sigma_{2}(B_{n}(j+1))}{\sigma_{1}(B_{n+1}(j))}\right\}<\frac{1}{2^{2n}},

which is nothing other than
(SVG)
.

Finally,
\|B_{n}(j)\|\leq 2\|B_{n}(j)\|_{\max}\leq 2^{-\sum^{n-1}_{k=1}|j+k|}\cdot 2^{2n+1}
and (
7
) imply for each
n\geq 2
that:

\displaystyle\frac{\|B_{n+1}(j)\|}{\|B_{n}(j+1)\|}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2^{-\sum^{n}_{k=1}|j+k|}\cdot 2^{2n+3}}{2^{-\sum^{n}_{k=2}|j+k|}\cdot(2^{2n}-1)}

\displaystyle=2^{-|j+1|}\cdot\frac{2^{2n+3}}{2^{2n}-1}

\displaystyle\to 0\mbox{ as }j\to\pm\infty,

hence,
\mathrm{(FI)}
fails.
∎

2.
{\mathbb{C}}{\mathbb{P}}^{1}
and Singular Value Decomposition

We first collect some facts about
{\mathbb{C}}{\mathbb{P}}^{1}
from
[
AZ
]
, where
\mathbb{CP}^{1}={\mathbb{C}}\cup\{\infty\}
is the one-dimensional complex projective space, or the Riemann sphere. We mainly use the following projection maps from
\mathbb{C}^{2}\backslash\{\overrightarrow{0}\}\rightarrow\mathbb{CP}^{1}
:

\pi:\mathbb{C}^{2}\backslash\{\overrightarrow{0}\}\rightarrow\mathbb{CP}^{1}\text{ where }\pi\begin{pmatrix}z_{1}\\
z_{2}\end{pmatrix}=\frac{z_{2}}{z_{1}}.

Through this projection, each one-dimensional space in
{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
can be identified with a point in
\mathbb{CP}^{1}
. Hence, we may view a point
z\in\mathbb{CP}^{1}
as the one-dimension space
\operatorname{span}\left\{\binom{1}{z}\right\}
of
{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
. Note
\infty
is considered to be
\operatorname{span}\left\{\binom{0}{1}\right\}
. For instance, by
\vec{v}\in z
, we mean
\vec{v}
is a vector in the one dimensional space
z
. In particular, we let
\vec{z}
denotes a unit vector in
z
. For any non-vector
\vec{v}
, we denote by
\vec{v}^{\perp}
a unit vector that is orthogonal to
\vec{v}
. We use the following metric on
\mathbb{CP}^{1}
:

(8)

d\left(z,z^{\prime}\right)=\begin{cases}\frac{2\left|z-z^{\prime}\right|}{\sqrt{\left(1+|z|^{2}\right)\left(1+\left|z^{\prime}\right|^{2}\right)}},&z,z^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{C}},\\
\frac{2}{\sqrt{1+|z|^{2}}},&z^{\prime}=\infty.\end{cases}

Let
\vec{u}
and
\vec{v}
be two nonzero vectors in
\mathbb{C}^{2}
. We let
\left(\vec{u},\vec{v}\right)\in\mathrm{M}(2,\mathbb{C})
denotes the matrix whose column vectors are
\vec{u}
and
\vec{v}
. Then a direct computation shows that

(9)

d\left(\pi(\vec{u}),\pi\left(\vec{v}\right)\right)=\frac{2\left|\operatorname{det}\left(\vec{u},\vec{v}\right)\right|}{\|\vec{u}\|\cdot\left\|\vec{v}\right\|}

In particular, if
\vec{u}
and
\vec{v}
are two unit vectors, then we have

(10)

d\left(\pi(\vec{u}),\pi\left(\vec{v}\right)\right)=2\left|\operatorname{det}\left(\vec{u},\vec{v}\right)\right|,

which clearly implies that

(11)

d\left(\pi(\vec{u}^{\perp}),\pi\left(\vec{v}^{\perp}\right)\right)=d\left(\pi(\vec{u}),\pi\left(\vec{v}\right)\right).

In other words, if we define
\mathscr{O}(z)=(z)^{\perp}:{\mathbb{C}}{\mathbb{P}}^{1}\to{\mathbb{C}}{\mathbb{P}}^{1}
to be
z^{\perp}:=\pi(\vec{z}^{\perp})
, then
\mathscr{O}

Since one dimensional space can be identified by the points in
\mathbb{CP}^{1}

where
\vec{u}\in U

are nonzero vectors.

Let
A=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}\in\mathrm{M}(2,\mathbb{C})
be a nonzero matrix. Under the projection
\pi
, there is an induced projectivized map of
A
acting on projective space
\left(\mathbb{CP}^{1}\right)\backslash\{\alpha\}
, where
\alpha
is the eigenspace of the 0 eigenvalue of
A
, if such exists. We denote the induced map by

. Then a direct computation shows that

A\cdot z:\left(\mathbb{CP}^{1}\right)\backslash\{\alpha\}\rightarrow\mathbb{CP}^{1},A\cdot z=\frac{c+dz}{a+bz}

Recall
\vec{z}
denotes a unit vector in the one-dimensional space

. Then it holds that

(13)

d\left(A\cdot z,A\cdot z^{\prime}\right)=\frac{|\operatorname{det}(A)|}{\|A\vec{z}\|\cdot\left\|A\vec{z}^{\prime}\right\|}d\left(z,z^{\prime}\right).

In particular, for all

, the set of all

unitary matrices, it holds that

(14)

d(U\cdot z,U\cdot z^{\prime})=d(z,z^{\prime})\mbox{ for all }z,z^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{C}}{\mathbb{P}}^{1}.

Next we collect some facts about singular value decomposition for
A\in\mathrm{M}(2,{\mathbb{C}})
. It is a standard fact that we can decompose

as
A=U\Lambda V^{*}
, where
U,V\in\mathrm{U}(2)
and
\Lambda=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}\sigma_{1}(A)&0\\
0&\sigma_{2}(A)\end{smallmatrix}\right)
. Moreover, we have

(15)

\sigma_{1}(A)=\|A\|=\sup_{\vec{\|}v\|=1}\|A\vec{v}\|,\ \sigma_{2}(A)=\frac{|\det(A)|}{\|A\|}=\inf_{\vec{\|}v\|=1}\|A\vec{v}\|,

which are so-called singular values of
A
. Moreover, column vectors of
V
are eigenvectors of
A^{*}A
where the first column vector corresponds to the most expanding direction of
A
and the second corresponds to the most contracted direction. Notice that two directions are orthogonal. Similarly, column vectors of
U
are eigenvectors of
AA^{*}
, where the first column vector corresponds to the most expanding direction of
A^{*}
and the second corresponds to the most contracted direction.

With some fixed choice of
V

U

V
and
\Lambda

as self-maps on
{\mathrm{M}}(2,{\mathbb{C}})
so that for each

Let
\mathscr{D}=\{A\in{\mathrm{M}}(2,{\mathbb{C}}):\sigma_{1}(A)=\sigma_{2}(A)\}
. Then we have the following simple fact:

Lemma 1
.

For some suitable choices of column vectors of

Proof.

Let

c&d\end{pmatrix}\in{\mathrm{M}}(2,{\mathbb{C}})\setminus\mathscr{D}
. First, we note that
A^{*}A=V\Lambda^{2}V^{*}
which implies
that
\sigma_{1}^{2}(A)
and
\sigma_{2}^{2}(A)
are two eigenvalues of
A^{*}A
.
Hence,

\mathrm{tr}(A^{*}A)=|a|^{2}+|b|^{2}|+|c|^{2}+|d|^{2}=\sigma_{1}^{2}(A)+\sigma_{2}^{2}(A)>2|\det(A)|\mbox{ and}

\sigma_{1}^{2}(A)=\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathrm{tr}(A^{*}A)+\sqrt{\mathrm{tr}(A^{*}A)^{2}-4|\det(A)|^{2}}\right),

which implies that

\sigma_{1}^{2}(A)
, and hence
\sigma_{1}(A)
, are
C^{\infty}
on
{\mathrm{M}}(2,{\mathbb{C}})\setminus\mathscr{D}
. This proves that

\Lambda\in C^{\infty}({\mathrm{M}}(2,{\mathbb{C}})\setminus\mathscr{D},{\mathrm{M}}(2,{\mathbb{C}})).

Let
V^{\prime}
be a matrix that diagonalizes
A^{*}A
.
Then the column vectors of
V^{\prime}
are solutions of the equations

(A^{*}A-\sigma_{1}^{2}(A)I_{2})\binom{x_{1}}{x_{2}}=0\mbox{ and }(A^{*}A-\sigma_{2}^{2}(A)I_{2})\binom{x_{1}}{x_{2}}=0.

In particular, we may choose

to be

\begin{pmatrix}\bar{a}b+\bar{c}d,&\bar{a}b+\bar{c}d\\
\sigma_{1}^{2}(A)-|a|^{2}-|c|^{2},&\sigma_{2}^{2}(A)-|a|^{2}-|c|^{2}\end{pmatrix},

which has nonzero determinant since
\sigma_{1}(A)>\sigma_{2}(A)
. Then we can
choose
V

V(A)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\det(V^{\prime}(A))}}V^{\prime}(A).

This shows that

V\in C^{\infty}({\mathrm{M}}(2,{\mathbb{C}})\setminus\mathscr{D},\mathrm{SU}(2,{\mathbb{C}})).

Finally, recall that column vectors of
U
are eigenvectors of
AA^{*}
whose eigenvalues are again
\sigma_{1}^{2}(A)
and
\sigma_{2}^{2}(A)
. Hence, similar to the process finding
V
, we may first fix a choice of a unit vector
\vec{u}_{1}
for the first colum vector of
U
. The second column vector
\vec{u}_{2}
of
U
is then determined by the facts
\|\vec{u}_{2}\|=1
,
\langle\vec{u}_{1},\vec{u}_{2}\rangle=0
, and
\det(U)=\frac{\det A}{|\det A|}
. Since all operations involved in determining
U
are algebraic operations/equations in entries of
A
,
\bar{A}
, and
\Lambda
, it follows that

U\in C^{\infty}({\mathrm{M}}(2,{\mathbb{C}})\setminus\mathscr{D},\mathrm{U}(2,{\mathbb{C}})).

∎

The most contracted direction

of

is defined as

that is, it is the projection of the second column vectors of

. We also define

to be

u(A)=U(A)\cdot 0:{\mathrm{M}}(2,{\mathbb{C}})\setminus\mathscr{D}\to{\mathbb{C}}{\mathbb{P}}^{1}.

If

. In any case, we always have

. By Lemma

,
s
and

and
C^{\infty}
maps.

B_{n}(j)D(j)=D(j+n)\Lambda_{n}(j)=(\vec{u}(j+n),\vec{s}(j+n))\begin{pmatrix}\prod_{k=0}^{n-1}\lambda_{j+k}^{+}&0\\
0&\prod_{k=0}^{n-1}\lambda_{j+k}^{-}\end{pmatrix}.

Lemma 2
.

Suppose
B
admits dominated splitting. Then there exist

and

such that for all

and all

(22)

which in particular implies

(23)

\sigma_{1}(B_{n}(j))>c\mu^{n}\sigma_{2}(B_{n}(j)).

Proof.

For any
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}
, we let

where

. By (a) of Definition
1

\|B_{k+l}(j)\vec{u}(j)\|=\|B_{k}(j+l)\vec{u}(j+l)\|\cdot\|B_{l}(j)\vec{u}(j)\|.

Same holds true for
\vec{s}(j)

\displaystyle\|B_{mN}(j)\vec{u}(j)\|

\displaystyle=\prod^{m-1}_{k=0}\|B_{N}(j+Nk)\vec{u}(j+kN)\|

\displaystyle\geq\lambda^{m}\prod^{m-1}_{k=0}\|B_{N}(j+Nk)\vec{s}(j+kN)\|

\displaystyle\geq\lambda^{m}\|B_{mN}(j)\vec{s}(j)\|.

If

\|B_{r}(j)\vec{s}(j)\|\leq\|B_{r}(j)\|\leq M^{r}
. Combine all these facts, we obtain

\displaystyle\|B_{n}(j)\vec{u}(j)\|

\displaystyle=\|B_{r}(j)\vec{u}(j)\|\cdot\left\|B_{mN}(j+r)\vec{u}(j+r)\right\|

\displaystyle\geq{\lambda}^{m}\frac{\|B_{r}(j)\vec{u}(j)\|}{\|B_{r}(j)\vec{s}(j)\|}\left\|B_{mN}(j+r)\vec{s}(j+r)\right\|\cdot\|B_{r}(j)\vec{s}(j)\|

Finally, by (

for all

. Hence,

\sigma_{1}(B_{n}(j))\geq\|B_{n}(j)\vec{u}(j)\|>c\mu^{n}\|B_{n}(j)\vec{s}(j)\|\geq c\mu^{n}\sigma_{2}(B_{n}(j)),

Lemma 7

\displaystyle=2|c_{2}|\cdot|\det\big(\vec{s}^{\perp}(A),\vec{s}(A)\big)|

\displaystyle=2|c_{2}|.

On the other hand, it holds that

\displaystyle|c_{2}\sigma_{1}(A)|

\displaystyle\leq\|A\vec{z}\|+\|A\vec{s}(A)\|

\displaystyle\leq\delta+\sigma_{2}(A),

\displaystyle d(u^{\perp}_{n}(j),u^{\perp}_{n+1}(j))<\frac{2M\sigma_{2}(B_{n}(j-n))+2\sigma_{2}(B_{n+1}(j-n-1))}{\sigma_{1}(B_{n+1}(j-n-1))}<C\mu^{-n}.

By (
11

d(u_{n}(j),u_{n+1}(j))=d(u^{\perp}_{n}(j),u^{\perp}_{n+1}(j))<C\mu^{-n},

which implies the existence of
E^{u}:\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{CP}^{1}
such that

\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}u_{n}(j)=E^{u}(j)
and

(28)

d(u_{n}(j),E^{u}(j))<C\mu^{-n}.

Note that by the fact
\mathscr{O}(z)=z^{\perp}
is an isometry (hence, continuous) on
{\mathbb{C}}{\mathbb{P}}^{1}
, we also have

(29)

\lim_{n\to\infty}u^{\perp}_{n}(j)=[E^{u}(j)]^{\perp}

To show that
E^{u}
is
B
-invarint, we again consider two case:

Case u.I
:
\det(B(j))\neq 0
. Set
\vec{v}=\frac{[B^{*}(j)]^{-1}\vec{u}^{\perp}_{n}(j)}{\|[B^{*}(j)]^{-1}\vec{u}^{\perp}_{n}(j)\|}\in[B^{*}(j)]^{-1}\cdot u^{\perp}_{n}(j)
. It clearly holds that

\displaystyle\|B^{*}_{(n+1)}(j-n)\vec{v}\|

\displaystyle=\frac{\|B^{*}_{n}(j-n)B^{*}(j)[B^{*}(j)]^{-1}\vec{u}^{\perp}_{n}(j)\|}{\|[B^{*}(j)]^{-1}\vec{u}^{\perp}_{n}(j)\|}

\displaystyle\leq M\|B^{*}_{n}(j-n)\vec{u}^{\perp}_{n}(j)\|

\displaystyle=M\sigma_{2}(B_{n}(j-n)).

Applying Lemma
7
with
z=[B^{*}(j)]^{-1}\cdot u^{\perp}_{n}(j)
,
A=B^{*}_{(n+1)}(j-n)
, and
\delta=M\sigma_{2}(B_{n}(j-n))
and by
\mathrm{(SVG)}
, we obtain that

d(B^{*}(j)]^{-1}\cdot u^{\perp}_{n}(j),u^{\perp}_{n+1}(j+1))\leq\frac{2\big(M\sigma_{2}(B_{n}(j-n))+\sigma_{2}(B_{n+1}(j-n))\big)}{\sigma_{1}(B_{n+1}(j-n))}<C\mu^{-n}.

Letting
n
tends to
\infty

B^{*}(j)\cdot[E^{u}(j+1)]^{\perp}=[E^{u}(j)]^{\perp}
, which in turn implies

\langle\vec{E}^{\perp}_{n}(j+1),B(j)\vec{E}^{u}(j)\rangle=\langle B^{*}(j)\vec{E}^{\perp}_{n}(j+1),\vec{E}^{u}(j)\rangle=0,

and hence,
B(j)\cdot E^{u}(j)=E^{u}(j+1)
.

Case u.II
:
\det(B(j))=0
. Then it is clear that the most contracted direction
u^{\perp}_{n}(j+1)
of
B^{*}_{n}(j+1-n)=B^{*}_{n-1}(j+1-n)B^{*}(j)
is
\ker[B^{*}(j)]
for all
n\geq 1
which implies

[E^{u}(j+1)]^{\perp}=\ker(B^{*}(j)),

which in turn implies that for all
\vec{v}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{2}

\langle\vec{E}^{\perp}_{n}(j+1),B(j)\vec{v}\rangle=\langle B^{*}(j)\vec{E}^{\perp}_{n}(j+1),v\rangle=0,

and hence
E^{u}(j+1)=\mathrm{Im}(B(j))=B(j)({\mathbb{C}}^{2})
. In particular,
B(j)[E^{u}(j)]\subset E^{u}(j+1)
.

Lemma 9
.

Suppose
\det(B(j))=0
for some
j
. Then
E^{u}(j)\neq E^{s}(j)
for all
j\in{\mathbb{Z}}
.

Proof.

Fix any arbitrarily
j
. We have two different cases: Case I, there is a
j^{\prime}<j
so that
\det(B(j^{\prime}))=0
; or Case II, there is a
j^{\prime}\geq j
so that
\det(B(j^{\prime}))=0
. By
B
-invariance of
E^{u(s)}
and the fact that
B(j^{\prime})E^{u(s)}(j^{\prime})=E^{u(s)}(j^{\prime})
when
\det(B(j^{\prime}))\neq 0
, we may further reduce the two cases to: Case I:
\det(B(j-1))=0
; Case II:
\det(B(j))=0
. For instance in Case I, if we set
j_{0}=\max\{j^{\prime}<j:\det(B(j))=0\}<j-1
, then we have by the facts above
E^{s}(j)=E^{u}(j)
if and only if

E^{s}(j_{0})=B_{j_{0}-j}(j)E^{s}(j)=B_{j_{0}-j}(j)E^{u}(j)=E^{u}(j_{0}).

For simplicity, from now on we write unit vectos in
E^{s}(j)
and
E^{u}(j)
as
\vec{s}(j)
and
\vec{u}(j)
, respectively. Likewise,
\vec{s}^{\perp}(j)
and
\vec{u}^{\perp}(j)
are unit vectors that are orthogonal to
\vec{s}(j)
and
\vec{u}(j)
, respectively.

Case I
. Since
\det(B(j-1))=0
, we have
E^{u}(j)=\mathrm{Im}(B(j-1))
by Remark
2
. Hence,
\frac{B(j-1)\vec{v}}{\|B(j-1)\vec{v}\|}
can be
\vec{u}(j)
for all
\vec{v}\notin\ker(B(j-1))
. In particular, we can choose
\vec{v}=\vec{s}^{\perp}_{n+1}(j-1)
which belongs to the most expanding direction of
B_{n+1}(j-1)
(hence cannot be in
\ker(B(j-1))
).
then we obtain

\|B_{n}(j)\vec{u}(j)\|=\frac{\|B_{n+1}(j-1)\vec{s}^{\perp}_{n+1}(j-1)\|}{\|B(j-1)\vec{v}\|}\geq\frac{\sigma_{1}(B_{n+1}(j-1))}{M}.

Now we let
\vec{s}(j)=c_{1}\vec{s}_{n}(j)+c_{2}\vec{s}^{\perp}_{n}(j)
, where
|c_{1}|^{2}+|c_{2}|^{2}=1
and
|c_{2}|=d(E^{s}(j),s_{n}(j))<C\mu^{-n}
. Then we have

\displaystyle\|B_{n}(j)\vec{s}(j)\|

\displaystyle\leq|c_{1}|\|B_{n}(j)\vec{s}_{n}(j)\|+|c_{2}|\|B_{n}(j)\vec{s}^{\perp}_{n}(j)\|

\displaystyle\leq\sigma_{2}(B_{n}(j))+C\mu^{-n}\sigma_{1}(B_{n}(j)).

Combine the two estimates above together with
\mathrm{(SVG)}
and
\mathrm{(FI)}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{\|B_{n}(j)\vec{s}(j)\|}{\|B_{n}(j)\vec{u}(j)\|}

\displaystyle\leq M\frac{\sigma_{2}(B_{n}(j))+C\mu^{-n}\sigma_{1}(B_{n}(j))}{\sigma_{1}(B_{n+1}(j-1))}

\displaystyle\leq M\left[\frac{\sigma_{2}(B_{n}(j))}{\sigma_{1}(B_{n+1}(j-1))}+C\mu^{-n}\frac{\sigma_{1}(B_{n}(j))}{\sigma_{1}(B_{n+1}(j-1))}\right]

\displaystyle\leq CM\mu^{-n}+C\mu^{-n}\mu^{(1-{\varepsilon})n}

\displaystyle\leq C\mu^{-{\varepsilon}n}<1,

for
n
sufficiently large, which clearly implies that
E^{u}(j)\neq E^{s}(j)
.

Case II
. To show
E^{s}(j)\neq E^{u}(j)
, we equivalently show
[E^{s}(j)]^{\perp}\neq[E^{u}(j)]^{\perp}
. Since
\det(B(j))=0
, we have
E^{s}(j)=\ker(B(j))
by Remark
2
. This is clearly equivalent to
[E^{s}(j)]^{\perp}=\mathrm{Im}(B^{*}(j))
. Hence,
\frac{B^{*}(j)\vec{v}}{\|B^{*}(j)\vec{v}\|}
can be our
\vec{s}^{\perp}(j)
for all
\vec{v}\notin\ker(B^{*}(j))
. In particular, we may choose
\vec{v}=u_{n}(j+1)
which is the most expanding direction of
B^{*}_{n+1}(j-n)=B^{*}_{n}(j-n)B^{*}(j)
(hence cannot be in
\ker(B^{*}(j))
). This implies that

\|B^{*}_{n}(j-n)\vec{s}^{\perp}(j)\|=\frac{\|B^{*}_{n+1}(j-n)\vec{u}_{n}(j+1)\|}{\|B^{*}(j)\vec{v}\|}\geq\frac{\sigma_{1}(B_{n+1}(j-n))}{M}.

On the other hand, recall that we have
d([E^{u}(j)]^{\perp},u^{\perp}_{n}(j))<C\mu^{-n}
where
u^{\perp}_{n}(j)
is the most contracted direction of
B^{*}_{n}(j-n)
. Now we write
\vec{u}^{\perp}(j)=c_{1}\vec{u}^{\perp}_{n}(j)+c_{2}\vec{u}_{n}(j)
where
|c_{1}|^{2}+|c_{2}|^{2}=1
and
|c_{2}|=d([E^{u}(j)]^{\perp},u^{\perp}_{n}(j))
. Then we have

\displaystyle\|B^{*}_{n}(j-n)\vec{u}^{\perp}(j)\|

\displaystyle\leq|c_{1}|\|B_{n}(j-n)\vec{u}^{\perp}_{n}(j)\|+|c_{2}|\|B_{n}(j-n)\vec{u}_{n}(j)\|

\displaystyle\leq\sigma_{2}(B_{n}(j-n))+C\mu^{-n}\sigma_{1}(B_{n}(j-n)).

The same proof as in Case I shows that it holds for all large
n
:

\frac{\|B^{*}_{n}(j-n)\vec{u}^{\perp}(j)\|}{\|B^{*}_{n}(j-n)\vec{s}^{\perp}(j)\|}<1,

which implies that
[E^{s}(j)]^{\perp}\neq[E^{u}(j)]^{\perp}
, hence
E^{s}(j)\neq E^{u}(j)
.

Now we introduce the definition of

\Omega

\Omega

(T,A):\Omega\times\mathbb{C}^{2}\rightarrow\Omega\times\mathbb{C}^{2},(T,A)(\omega,\vec{v})=(T\omega,A(\omega)\vec{v}).

Iterations of dynamics are denoted by
\left(T^{n},A_{n}\right):=(T,A)^{n}

A_{n}(\omega)=\begin{cases}B\left(T^{n-1}\omega\right)\cdots B(\omega),&n\geq 1,\\
I_{2},&n=0,\end{cases}

\displaystyle\mathrm{(FI)}\hskip 56.9055pt\sup_{\omega\in\Omega}\left\{\frac{\sigma_{1}(A_{n}(\omega))}{\sigma_{1}(A_{n+1}(\omega))},\ \frac{\sigma_{1}(A_{n}(T\omega))}{\sigma_{1}(A_{n+1}(\omega))}\right\}<C\mu^{(1-{\varepsilon})n}\mbox{ for all }n\geq 1,

(T\omega)_{n}=\omega_{n+1}\text{ for }\omega=(\omega_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\in\mathcal{B}^{\mathbb{Z}}

Definition 2

s_{n}^{\omega}(j)=s(B^{\omega}_{n}(j))
and
u^{\omega}_{n}(j)=s([B_{n}^{\omega}(j-n)]^{*})
. Then we define
s_{n}(\omega)=s(F_{n}(\omega))
and
u_{n}(\omega)=s(F_{-n}(\omega))
. It is clear that we have
s_{n}(\omega)=s_{n}^{\omega}(0)
and
u_{n}(\omega)=u_{n}^{\omega}(0).

Now for each
\omega
, we could treat the sequence
\left\{B^{\omega}(j),j\in{\mathbb{Z}}\right\}
as the sequence
\{B(j),j\in{\mathbb{Z}}\}
. By Lemma
8
and taking

for each
\omega
, we obtain

Hence, there exist
E^{s},E^{u}:\Omega\rightarrow{\mathbb{C}}{\mathbb{P}}^{1}
such that

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}s_{n}=E^{s}\text{ and }\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}u_{n}=E^{u}

and it holds for all
n\geq 1
that

(35)

\sup_{\omega\in\Omega}d(s_{n}(\omega),E^{s}(\omega)<C\mu^{-n}\mbox{ and }\sup_{\omega\in\Omega}d(u_{n}(\omega),E^{u}(\omega)<C\mu^{-n}.

In particular, for each
\omega
, it holds that

F(\omega)\cdot E^{s}(\omega)=B^{\omega}(0)(E^{s})^{\omega}(0)=(E^{s})^{\omega}(1)=E^{s}(T\omega);

that is,
E^{s}
is
(T,F)
-invariant. Similarly,
E^{u}
is
(T,F)
-invariant as well. Moreover,
F_{n}(\omega)\notin\mathscr{D}
for all
\omega
and all large
n
which together with Lemma
1
implies that
s_{n}(\omega)
and
u_{n}(\omega)
are continuous in
\omega
. By (
35
),
E^{s}
and
E^{u}
are continuous maps. Now assume for the sake of contradiciton that
E^{s}(j)=E^{u}(j)
for some
j
. By the
B
-invariance and the fact
\det(B(j))\neq 0
for all
j
, we obtain that
E^{s}(j)=E^{s}(j)
for all
j
. This in particular means
E^{s}(T^{j}B)=E^{s}(T^{j}B)
. By continuity of
E^{s}(\omega)
and
E^{u}(\omega)
and the fact that
\{T^{j}B:j\in{\mathbb{Z}}\}
is dense in
\Omega
, we obtain that
E^{s}(\omega)=E^{u}(\omega)
for all
\omega\in\Omega
.

On the other hand, applying Lemma
9
to
\tilde{\omega}
, we obtain
E^{s}(\tilde{\omega})\neq E^{u}(\tilde{\omega})
. This clearly contradicts
E^{s}(j)=E^{u}(j)
for some

which concludes the proof of the first part of this lemma.

Now apply Lemma
9
and the part we have just proved to all
B^{\omega}(j)=F(T^{j}\omega)
, we obtain
E^{s}(\omega)\neq E^{u}(\omega)
for all
\omega
. By continuity of
E^{s}
and
E^{u}
and compactness of
\Omega
, we obtain

\inf d(E^{u}(\omega),E^{s}(\omega)>0.

Since
E^{u(s)}(j)=E^{u(s)}(T^{j}B)
, we then obtain

\inf_{j\in{\mathbb{Z}}}d(E^{u}(j),E^{s}(j))>0,

as desired.
∎

Finally we show that
E^{u}
dominates
E^{s}
as stated in condition (b) of Definition

.

Lemma 11
.

There exists a
\lambda>1
and

such that it holds for all

and all unit vectors
\vec{s}(j)\in E^{s}(j)
and
\vec{u}(j)\in E^{u}(j)

Proof.

By Lemma
10
, there is a
\delta>0
such that

for all

. This together with (
27
) and (
28
) implies that for all
n
large:

d(s_{n}(j),E^{u}(j))>\frac{\delta}{2}.

Writing
\vec{u}(j)=c_{1}\vec{s}_{n}(j)+c_{2}\vec{s}^{\perp}_{n}(j)
and
\vec{s}(j)=d_{1}\vec{s}_{n}(j)+d_{2}\vec{s}^{\perp}_{n}(j)

and

, we obtain for all

\displaystyle\frac{\|B_{n}(j)\vec{s}(j)\|}{\|B_{n}(j)\vec{u}(j)\|}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{|d_{1}|\sigma_{2}(B_{n}(j))+|d_{2}|\sigma_{1}(B_{n}(j))}{|c_{2}|\sigma_{1}(B_{n}(j))-|c_{1}|\sigma_{2}(B_{n}(j))}

\displaystyle=\frac{|d_{1}|\frac{\sigma_{2}(B_{n}(j))}{\sigma_{1}(B_{n}(j))}+|d_{2}|}{|c_{2}|-|c_{1}|\frac{\sigma_{2}(B_{n}(j))}{\sigma_{1}(B_{n}(j))}}

The desired result follows by choosing

and

∎

5.
An Avalanche Principle for
{\mathrm{M}}(2,{\mathbb{C}})
-sequences

In this section, we prove Theorem
2
. Recall that by (
16
), for any
Q\in{\mathrm{M}}(2,{\mathbb{C}})
, it holds that

Q=U(Q)\begin{pmatrix}\sigma_{1}(Q)&0\\
0&\sigma_{2}(Q)\end{pmatrix}V^{*}(Q),

where
V(Q)\cdot\infty=s(Q)
and
U(Q)\cdot 0=u(Q)
. Let
E_{1},E_{2}\in{\mathrm{M}}(2,{\mathbb{C}})
. We write
V(E_{2})=(\vec{v}_{1},\vec{v}_{2})
,
U(E_{1})=(\vec{u}_{1},\vec{u}_{2})
, where all vectors are column vectors. We also write

V^{*}(E_{2})U(E_{1})=\begin{pmatrix}c_{1}&c_{2}\\
c_{3}&c_{4}\end{pmatrix}\in\mathrm{U}(2).

Note
V^{*}=V^{-1}
which implies:

(36)

|c_{1}|=|\det(\vec{v}_{2},\vec{u}_{1})|=d(V(E_{2})\cdot\infty,U(E_{1})\cdot 0)=d(s(E_{2}),u(E_{1})).

Lemma 12
.

Let
E_{1},E_{2}\in{\mathrm{M}}(2,{\mathbb{C}})
satisfying

(37)

\sigma_{1}(E_{2}E_{1})>C^{2}\max\left\{\sigma_{1}(E_{1})\sigma_{2}(E_{2}),\sigma_{1}(E_{2})\sigma_{2}(E_{1})\right\}.

Then it holds that

(38)

\displaystyle cd\big(s(E_{2}),u(E_{1})\big)<\frac{\sigma_{1}(E_{2}E_{1})}{\sigma_{1}(E_{2})\sigma_{1}(E_{1})}<Cd\big(s(E_{2}),u(E_{1})\big)

(39)

\displaystyle\left|\frac{\sigma_{1}(E_{2}E_{1})}{\sigma_{1}(E_{2})\sigma_{1}(E_{1})}-d(s(E_{2}),u(E_{1}))\right|<C\max\left\{\frac{\sigma_{2}(E_{2})}{\sigma_{1}(E_{2})},\ \frac{\sigma_{2}(E_{1})}{\sigma_{1}(E_{1})}\right\}

Proof.

By singular value decomposition of
E_{1}
and
E_{2}
, one has

E_{2}E_{1}=U(E_{2})\begin{pmatrix}\sigma_{1}(E_{2})&0\\
0&\sigma_{2}(E_{2})\end{pmatrix}V^{*}(E_{2})U(E_{1})\begin{pmatrix}\sigma_{1}(E_{1})&0\\
0&\sigma_{2}(E_{1})\end{pmatrix}V^{*}(E_{1}),

which implies that

U^{*}(E_{2})\cdot E_{2}E_{1}\cdot V(E_{1})-\begin{pmatrix}c_{1}\sigma_{1}(E_{2})\sigma_{1}(E_{1})&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}0&c_{2}\sigma_{1}(E_{2})\sigma_{2}(E_{1})\\
c_{3}\sigma_{2}(E_{2})\sigma_{1}(E_{1})&c_{4}\sigma_{2}(E_{2})\sigma_{2}(E_{1})\end{pmatrix}.

Since
\mathrm{U}(2)
-matrices preserve the operator norm, triangle inequality yields

(40)

\left|\sigma_{1}(E_{2}E_{1})-c_{1}\sigma_{1}(E_{2})\sigma_{1}(E_{1})\right|<C\max\big\{\sigma_{1}(E_{2})\sigma_{2}(E_{1}),\ \sigma_{2}(E_{2})\sigma_{1}(E_{1})\big\}.

Combining (
40
) with (
37
), we obtain

c|c_{1}|\sigma_{1}(E_{2})\sigma_{1}(E_{1})<\sigma_{1}(E_{2}E_{1})<C|c_{1}|\sigma_{1}(E_{2})\sigma_{1}(E_{1}),

which together with (
36
) clearly implies (
38
). Now divide (
40
) by
\sigma_{1}(E_{2})\sigma_{1}(E_{1})
at both sides we obtain

\left|\frac{\sigma_{1}(E_{2}E_{1})}{\sigma_{1}(E_{2})\sigma_{1}(E_{1})}-d(s(E_{2}),u(E_{1}))\right|<C\max\left\{\frac{\sigma_{2}(E_{2})}{\sigma_{1}(E_{2})},\ \frac{\sigma_{2}(E_{1})}{\sigma_{1}(E_{1})}\right\}

which is nothing other than (
39
).
∎

Corollary 1
.

Let
E_{2},E_{1}\in{\mathrm{M}}(2,\mathbb{C})
be such that
\frac{\sigma_{1}(E_{1})\sigma_{1}(E_{2})}{\sigma_{1}(E_{2}E_{1})}\leq\mu^{1/4}
and
\frac{\sigma_{2}(E_{i})}{\sigma_{1}(E_{i})}\leq\mu^{-1}
for

. Then it holds that

(41)

d\big(s(E_{2}),u(E_{1})\big)>c\mu^{-\frac{1}{4}}.

Proof.

By the given conditions, we clearly have

\displaystyle\sigma_{1}\big(E_{2}E_{1}\big)\geq\sigma_{1}(E_{2})\sigma_{1}(E_{1})\mu^{-\frac{1}{4}}>\mu^{\frac{3}{4}}\max\left\{\sigma_{1}(E_{1})\sigma_{2}(E_{2}),\sigma_{2}(E_{1}))\sigma_{1}(E_{2})\right\}.

Thus the condition of Lemma

is satisfied which in turn implies

d\big(s(E_{2}),u(E_{1})\big)>c\frac{\sigma_{1}(E_{2}E_{1})}{\sigma_{1}(E_{2})\sigma_{1}(E_{1})}\geq c\mu^{-\frac{1}{4}}.

∎

Lemma 13
