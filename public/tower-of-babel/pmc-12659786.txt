Challenges in the Implementation of Blood Culture Parameters to Identify Patients at Low Risk of Infective Endocarditis
Authors: Pietro Valsecchi, Paola Giordani, Elena Seminari, Raffaele Bruno
Published: 2025

To The Editor—We read with great interest the study by Fourré et al. on blood culture parameters for identifying low-risk patients for infective endocarditis (IE) among those with Gram-positive bacteremia [1].

While we appreciate the large cohort size and methodological approach, we wish to highlight several concerns regarding the clinical applicability of this risk stratification strategy.

The fundamental limitation lies in the classification outcome: only 18% of patients (398/2165) were identified as low-risk for IE. This means 82% of patients would still require echocardiography, severely limiting the practical utility for reducing unnecessary cardiac imaging—the study's primary stated goal.

Given that current guidelines already recommend echocardiography for high-risk patients, this approach offers minimal clinical advantage.

More concerning are the safety implications of the low-risk classification.

Among the 398 episodes deemed low-risk, 7% had prosthetic valves, 6% had cardiac implantable electronic devices, and 6% experienced embolic events.

These features are established high-risk factors for IE according to current ESC guidelines and should warrant transesophageal echocardiography regardless of blood culture parameters [2]. Therefore, patients presenting these features should be excluded from the low-risk category, further limiting the impact of this approach.

Moreover, the presence of such patients in the “low-risk” category raises significant safety concerns for clinical implementation.

The 4% misclassification rate (14 IE cases among low-risk patients) is particularly problematic when considering real-world implementation. In routine clinical practice, the absolute number of missed cases could be relevant if these parameters are applied to a large patient population.

Furthermore, there is a discrepancy in the numbers reported in the text and in table 3 for high-risk patients. Specifically, if 1783 patients had either >1 positive out of the initial 4 blood culture bottles or persistent bacteremia for ≥48 hours, the number of patients classified as low risk should be 382.

Additionally, as the authors acknowledge, persistent bacteremia was rare in cases with only one positive blood culture bottle, suggesting that the score's predictive ability rests primarily on the number of initially positive bottles rather than the combination of both proposed criteria.

We believe that for this approach to be safely implemented in clinical practice, patients with prosthetic intracardiac material or presenting with embolic events should be automatically excluded from low-risk classification, regardless of blood culture parameters.

Future validation studies should focus on populations with lower IE prevalence and assess real-world impact on both imaging utilization and patient outcomes.

While the simplicity of this approach is appealing compared to complex scoring systems, the combination of limited clinical impact and safety concerns suggests that this strategy requires significant refinement before clinical implementation, in particular before delivering this message to an audience composed of noninfectious disease specialists.
