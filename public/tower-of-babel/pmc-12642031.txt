Nigerian dramatic arts students’ willingness to train as standardized patients
Authors: Fadekemi Olufunmilayo Oginni, Bamidele Adetokunbo Famurewa, Ajewumi Bili Raji
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundStandardized Patients(SPs) are valid and reliable substitutes for teaching and assessing clinical students in a wide range of clinical scenarios. They are trained to portray clinical case realistically and consistently, and simulate real clinical cases in a standardized manner. We investigated the willingness of undergraduate students of drama and theater art in a Nigerian University to serve as standardized patients and portray clinical scenarios, especially mental illness in comparison with other clinical conditions.MethodsNinety-four undergraduate students of the Department of Dramatic Arts, Obafemi Awolowo University, Ile Ife, Nigeria completed a self-administered questionnaire, participated in a 15-minute introductory session (short talk and video) on the role of SPs in medical education and listened to three clinical vignettes on bronchial asthma, breaking bad news and psychotic disorder respectively. Respondents rated their level of comfort portraying each case on a scale of 0(not comfortable at all) to 10(very comfortable) and provided reason(s) for their responses. Their demographic characteristics, responses to the questionnaires and comforts ratings were summarized using descriptive statistics. Analysis of variance(ANOVA) was used to analyze the mean difference between levels of comfort perceived by respondents across the clinical cases.ResultsThe mean(SD) age of respondents was 21.0(2.9) years, 57.5% were men, and mean(SD) time already spent as dramatic arts undergraduate translated to 3.75(2.6) years. Most(97%) had never heard of the term “Standardized Patient”, none of them could define the term. Most respondents (95.7%) expressed the highest level of comfort of 10 if they were to act as SPs for chronic asthma and breaking bad news. Respondents were more comfortable with portrayal of asthma (7.6(3.3)) and breaking bad news (6.9(3.5)) than portrayal of psychotic disorder (5.0(4.2)) (p < 0.0001). Reasons given for discomfort with psychotic disorder were that the case was complex, critical, emotionally uncomfortable and subject to fatigue.ConclusionsNigerian dramatic art students comprise a potential resource and pool of SPs in Nigeria however, they expressed feelings of discomfort with simulating psychotic disorders as SPs. Their deployment should begin with uncomplicated and common disorders. Additional education and training to decrease discomfort may enable the portrayal of mental health cases as well.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-025-08208-1.Keywords: Standardized patients; Dramatic arts, students, actors; Performing arts; Medical education; Mental health simulation

Background

Advancements and quests for improvement in medical education have brought to the fore the need to allow trainees extensive practice before engaging in clinical activities with real patients [1]. Standardized patients (SPs) are trained individuals who accurately act out the roles of patients with specific medical conditions. They are also known as simulated or programmed patients. The term “standardized” means that these people are specifically instructed and trained to model a “true” patient’s illness in a realistic, consistent and reproducible way [2, 3]. The roles and benefits of SPs in medical education have been widely reported [4]. Consequently, the use of SPs in medical education has become popular in most countries of the world – but not yet in African countries.

The first medical school in Nigeria opened its door in I948 at the then University College, Ibadan [5]. Currently, there are 47 accredited medical schools in Nigeria [6]. As demand increases for more doctors and healthcare providers to care for the growing Nigerian population, medical schools are faced with the challenge of getting enough patients for bedside teaching during ward rounds thus, medical schools are beginning to supplement the traditional methods of teaching with simulation-based learning and use of SPs [7, 8]. While there is no published data on the number of medical schools in Nigeria with SP programme, recent publications in Southwestern Nigeria revealed gradual uptake of SPs by some departments in three of the medical and health profession schools in this region [7, 8]. With the ongoing evolution in Nigerian medical education, the use of SPs is likely to become unavoidable and popular soon.

While a myriad of individuals (including lay persons) may serve as SPs, this role falls readily within the purview of dramatic and theatre artists as well as dramatic arts students who can be gainfully employed thereby [9]. We opine that a focus on the undergraduate dramatic arts population and the introduction of the concept in different schools is desirable for an early buy in. Dramatic artists ordinarily portray various roles in their career, but little is known about the “perceived risks” associated with portraying roles of ill health.

Generally, the Nigerian cultural setting promotes the view that imitating the ill and afflicted is tantamount to inviting the misfortune wittingly or unwittingly on oneself. It is believed, for instance, that such illnesses as are imitated may befall the individual who plays the role later in life [10]. Aina [10] reported that only 15% of reviewed Nigerian films had acting parts that portrayed mental illness, a condition that is still widely stigmatized in the Nigerian cultural setting. Culture, according to Edward Taylor (1871) quoted by Abasiekong (2010) [1, 3, 11] “is that complex whole which includes knowledge, belief, art, morals, law, customs and any other capabilities and habits acquired by man as a member of the society”. Culture has been described as the totality of the ways of life of a people [12]. Nigeria is a multi-cultural and multi-ethnic society, and each of the cultures share certain similarities as well as differences with the other cultures. Each of these cultures, like many other cultures found on the African continent, is very rich and entrenched in myths. Three groups of illnesses were identified in the context of the African culture: Trivial or everyday disease (amenable to home remedies), regular, orthodox diseases (amenable to western scientific therapy) & non-orthodox diseases (not understood or treated successfully through the use of orthodox medication) [4, 13]. Mental illness in Nigeria is an example of what is considered to be non-orthodox [10, 14–16]. Aina’s review of Nigerian films showed a relatively low portrayal of mental illness [10].

As an example, there is a general belief that supernatural forces, witches, evil spirits and even God cause mental illness [12–15] and these beliefs have influenced the attitude of Nigerians towards the mentally ill. A scoping review on knowledge of, and attitude to mentally ill individuals in Nigeria confirmed an enormous knowledge gap and chronic widespread stigmatization [16]. For these reasons, there is bound to be appreciable resistance (especially from the patient) against the use of the mentally ill for teaching and summative assessments of learners [17]. The SP is however a potential resource for teaching medical students about mental illness and others. Recently, the role of SPs has been extended to include the objective assessment of healthcare in real-time because it obviates recall errors/bias among patients in exit interviews and incomplete medical records or missing patient charts [18].

The objective of this study was to explore Nigerian Dramatic Arts trainees’ willingness to serve as SPs. It is hypothesized that dramatic arts students will be less comfortable with portraying mental illness scenarios as SPs in comparison to other clinical conditions. Our findings would have implications for the effective preparation, implementation, and smooth running of SP programs in Nigeria.

Methods

The study recruited all year 2–4 undergraduate students of the Dramatic Arts Department at the Obafemi Awolowo University, Ile-Ife, Nigeria in December 2019. First year students were excluded because they are not fully integrated into departmental activities. A purposive sampling technique was used. Students were recruited by class announcement by one of the authors (ABR). All participants gave informed verbal consents to participate at the onset as approved by the Institution Review Board. Thereafter, respondents completed a content-validated questionnaire, designed purposely for this study (Supplementary file 1) requesting their socio-demographic details, whether they had ever heard the term “Standardized patients (SPs)” before then, and under what circumstance(s). The content of the vignettes was reviewed for clinical accuracy by three medical faculty who had been in practice for 5 to 15 years, and were familiar with the clinical presentations for asthma, adverse drug reactions, and psychosis. Respondents were also asked to write down their understanding of what is meant by SP.

Immediately after completing the questionnaire, a 15-minute introductory session on the role of SPs in medical education was conducted with the aid of a slide presentation (Supplementary file 2) and videos projected to the respondents in a hall. The slide presentation addressed definition of terms, and the purposes, benefits, desired attributes, and training of an SP (See Supplementary File 2). A 5-minute period was allotted for questions and answers to enable respondents obtain clarifications on the talk.

Then, three standardized case vignettes (1) Asthma, (2) Breaking bad news and (3) Psychotic disorder were presented verbally, one at a time in that order. Respondents rated their level of comfort with portraying each case ranged on a scale of 0 (not comfortable at all) to 5 (neither comfortable nor uncomfortable) to 10 (very comfortable) and provided reason(s) for their responses (Fig. 1: full case provided in Supplementary File 1). Our choice of a numeric scale was predicated on respondents’ familiarity with it, its provision of a wide range of output, output amenable to our desired statistical analysis, and the fact that it allows for better discrimination between levels.

The study was registered with the health research ethics committee of Obafemi Awolowo University Teaching Hospitals’ Complex Ile-Ife Osun state Nigeria and approved with ethical clearance certificate number ERC/2019/03/07. The research was carried out in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, and it was not funded by any organization.

All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics Version 26. Respondents’ demographic characteristics and responses to the questionnaires and rating scores of levels of their comforts were summarized using descriptive statistics. Analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to analyze the mean difference between levels of comfort perceived by respondents between the clinical cases. Multiple Analysis of Variance (MANOVA) and logistic regression model were conducted to examine the influence of participants’ demographics on portrayal comfort level across the three cases. Inferential statistical tests and reported p-values were two-sided. Statistical significance was inferred at p value < 0.05 and 95% confidence intervals (95% CI) were reported.

Results

Participants included all 94 out of 132 total Dramatic Arts students at the Obafemi Awolowo University, Ile-Ife. They were 41, 34 and 19 in years 2, 3 and 4 respectively. No students declined to participate. Majority of them were male (M: F = 54:40), 2 to 4 years into their training, mostly of Yoruba ethnicity (84.2%), and Christian faith (88.4%). Their ages ranged from 16 to 32 years, with mean age (SD) of 21 (2.9) years and they had been students in the program for a range of 2 to 12 years (Mean (SD) = 3.75 (2.6) years). 79% of the respondents had no experience in drama or theatre before admission into the university; 97% had never heard of the term “Standardized Patient”, and none of them was able to define it correctly. Previous dramatic arts experience (vocation/hobby) in 21% of respondents had lasted an average (SD) of 3.2 (1.8) years. Those among them who had heard of SPs before came across it in their interactions with friends and family.

Comfort ratings for the three cases are shown in Table 1, and the distribution of comfort scores by case is shown in Fig. 2. For this figure, comfort scores 0 and 10 were retained as the extremes, 5 as the center (5 = neither comfortable nor uncomfortable) while 1–4 and 6–9 were merged to reflect everything in between. Respondents were significantly (p < 0.0001) more comfortable with simulating asthma and breaking bad news than they were with projecting psychotic disorder. Most of the participants provided explanatory comments; indicating that case 1 (asthma) was familiar (8 of 90 respondents) and would therefore be easy to simulate (16/90), while case 3 (psychotic disorder) was complex, critical, uncomfortable and subject to fatigue. A few (13/90) of them indicate readiness to mimic case 3 and this as they said was because they love playing such roles, or would like to be of help to the health care delivery. Other reasons for reluctance across all cases were fear of scenarios inducing recall of unpleasant experiences and unfamiliarity with the clinical presentation; while additional reasons for willingness were professional skillfulness, and desire to impart quality of health care (Tables 2 and 3). 78(83%), 63(67%) and 63(67%) of respondents provided reasons for their expressed level of comfort. Most (30.5%) attributed their willingness to confidence, personal resistance and having been trained to take up any role. 16.4% considered it a privilege to partner with the College of Health Sciences in training the next generation of physicians and contribute or support the efforts to improve health care delivery in the nation by preventing wrong diagnosis (Table 2).

An ANOVA test revealed statistically significant different comfort means (p < 0.0001, f = 11.776) between the cases. A post hoc test was conducted and significant difference was found between scores allotted to Case 1 (Asthma) and Case 3 (Psychotic illness) (p < 0.0001) and between Case 2 (Breaking bad news) and Case 3 (Psychotic illness). No significant difference was found between comfort ratings for Case 1 (Asthma) and Case 2 (Breaking bad news) (p = 0.23).

Multivariate analysis of variance (MANOVA) conducted to examine the influence of participants’ demographic information (gender, age, year of training, state, religion, and ethnicity) on expressed comfort level indicated that these factors were not statistically significant predictors of expressed comfort level across the three cases (p > 0.05).

Multiple linear regression analyses were conducted to examine the influence of the demographic factors (age, gender, ethnic group, religion, and year of training) on participant’ perception of the three clinical cases. For case 1 (asthma) and case 2 (Breaking bad news), the regression model was not statistically significant.While in Case 3 (Psychosis), the regression model was not statistically significant overall (F = 1.718, p = 0.140) however, gender emerged as a significant predictor (B=–1.97, 95%CI [–3.84, − 0.11], p = 0.039), indicating that perception of Case 3 differed significantly by gender with other predictors not statistically significant. Overall, these findings suggest that demographic factors generally did not predict students’ perceptions of the clinical cases with the only exception being gender, which significantly influenced participant’ perceptions of Case 3 (Table 4).

Discussion

Our study found that dramatic art students were willing to contribute to the training of health professionals and were comfortable with straightforward clinical situations such as asthma and breaking bad news, while many of them were reluctant to simulate difficult mental health scenario such as psychosis. Respondents attributed their relative comfort and willingness to simulate the different roles to the skills they have acquired as aspiring dramatic artists, their desire to contribute to the improvement of health care delivery, and, of course, expected remuneration. The reluctance they exhibited in certain cases was attributed to painful recall of unpleasant experiences, and lack of interest. Majority of respondents were, however, significantly less willing to simulate psychotic disorder. Established programs in USA, Turkey and Asia [19–22], one with over 50 years experience [21] alluded to the emotional distress associated with clinical simulation especially in simulation of psychatric illness [22, 23] and sexually transmited diseases [22]. Thus emphasisizing that it is desirous to protect the SPs from these known adverse consequences [20]. Our respondents gave similar reasons for their reluctance about playing the role of patient with psychotic disorder. However, 69% of the respondents who expressed lack of willingness to play the roles were silent about their reasons for reluctance. Their silence may be attributed to a number of reasons which are rooted in cultural beliefs and superstitions that may sound unacceptable in a modern society.

Culture in African, and specifically Nigerian context is known to be very rich and suffused in myths. Jacobs and van Jaarsveldt [24] explored and described the experiences of 11 drama students engaged as SPs for mental health nursing education in South Africa. They reported the serious difficulties that SPs had to navigate to truly portray mental health conditions. Similarly, findings from the present study and their results showed that simulating mental health conditions could render SPs emotionally vulnerable depending on what is demanded of them in the scenario to be portrayed and the SP’s gender. These concerns are often difficult to express but nevertheless real and deep-seated within the affected SPs. The belief that “what you portray or say about yourself will eventually become your lot” is held strongly by many and the fact that they are not readily expressed may not help an already stressed emotional state.

Coming to terms with the physical, psychological, and social needs of the SPs could help decrease the negative outcomes related to the tasks and duties performed by the SPs [20] if annexed properly. Regardless of the reluctant dispositions and unhelpful cultural beliefs, available literature suggests that practical steps like appropriate recruitment, systematic and well-planned SPs education, professional counscelling [24] and interprofessional learning opportunities can help ensure successful outcomes for teachers, coaches, and SPs actor [11]. For example, relaxation exercises enabled SPs to emotionally detach themselves from the difficult scenarios portrayed by them; in addition, the SPs were relaxed and felt a sense of well-being [21].

Awareness on the role of SPs was very low among the respondents, but a brief introduction to the subject was sufficient to stimulate remarkable interests and willingness to serve as SPs in the group. Successful leveraging of dramatic arts students in the role of SPs should commence with simple, familiar clinical presentations, while paying attention to individual student’s level of comfort with case scenarios. While SPs roles are being extended for their many merits, the use of SPs for medical education and assessment of healthcare service delivery in sub-Saharan Africa is still emerging. SPs documented extended roles conform with the expressions of SPs in Dublin, Ireland and Tehran, Iran who alluded to belief in the importance of their roles in health education, enhancing professional competence and improving the quality of clinical evaluation in medical and health sciences [11, 25].

A multidisciplinary approach to health professions education has evolved in a number of developed countries [20–23]. Benefits of engaging SPs include but are not limited to creating “safe” and comfortable environments for learners to practice and polish up their skills without causing any harm to the “real” patients [26]. Conversely, Plaksin et al. [17] reviewed and classified negative risks of being a SP into three, namely; immediate psychological effects, immediate physical effects, and long-term effects. Although this scope of SPs in medical education covers a wide range of subjects/areas – including the use of models, task trainers, mannequin, and others, the focus of this work is on the willingness of Nigerian Dramatic and Theatre Arts trainees towards playing the role of SPs, and the factors that influence their willingness or otherwise.

In Nigeria, the need for SPs in the training of health care professionals is becoming evident. The reasons include patients’ awareness of their rights and demands for privacy; trainers’ consciousness of trainees’ propensity to cause harm; heightened litigation consciousness; specialists care; and the need for trainees to learn under safe conditions. The feasibility and usefulness of SPs in medical education have been reported in HIV/AIDS care as well as sexual and reproductive health in Tanzania and Kenya [27, 28].

While most places use lay persons and not necessarily actors as SPs, the gift of a readily accessible, young, dynamic population cannot be underestimated especially in exploring a “new” field like this. A remarkable level of ignorance about the term “standardized patients” and the role of dramatic arts students in the training of health professionals is not surprising but this definitely calls for a need to generate awareness among the potential pool of dramatic arts students and their instructors. The stack lack of knowledge of dramatic arts students about “standardized patients” may be a true reflection of the nascent stage of simulation-based medical education using SPs in Nigeria and other African countries. Interestingly, this offers a great opportunity for interdisciplinary collaboration between dramatic arts and medicine and related professional disciplines. If judiciously pursued and implemented, it could provide rich opportunities for dramatic and theatre arts students to learn about authentic character portrayal and improvisation in the presence of an informed medical audience. On the other hand, medical students would benefit from objective feedback from their peers, posing as SPs, in a relaxed atmosphere than would be the case in a formal learning environment [24].

Educating the group about SPs in health Professions Education with examples in video clips was structured to bring the practice as close to respondents as possible. We believe that this purpose was achieved to a large extent, as respondents sought relevant clarifications. Respondents’ performances during the question-and-answer session showed great enthusiasm, inquisitiveness and dynamism. The idea of SP was well understood and received at the end of the talk. This finding is similar to the report submitted by Mkonyi and colleagues [28] following a project conducted in Tanzania where arts students were trained as SPs for sexual and reproductive health cases as part of training for nursing, medical, and midwifery students.

The limitations of this study include limited sample size and restriction to a single geopolitical zone of the country. Although content validated, our study instrument was not standardized. There is also a need to explore the dramatic arts trainers’ level of awareness, understanding of the roles of SPs, their fitness for the training curriculum and willingness to collaborate with the College of Health Sciences in the training of health professional students. Our focus on dramatic arts students only is another limitation of this study in the sense that drama students may have been especially susceptible to worry about mental disorders, and other potential SP populations may not be - or may be much more so. Furthermore, it would be helpful to increase our understanding of the factors that impact the comfort or discomfort of dramatic and theatre arts students in playing the roles of SP through interviews.

Conclusion

While most drama students were not familiar with the term “Standardized Patient” prior to the presentation session after a brief orientation they were quite, willing to contribute to health professions education by acting as SPs for uncomplicated and common clinical scenarios. However, they expressed discomfort with simulating mental illness such as psychotic disorders, as a consequence of the unpleasant emotional distress that it could cause them. The cause of such distress may be rooted in cultural beliefs and myths. Since Nigerian dramatic and theatre arts students potentially constitute a ready source of SPs for health professions and education, having demonstrated appreciable willingness to contribute to the training of health professionals, stakeholders in health and medical education in the country should begin to seriously consider the possibility of engaging them in the role of SPs. Introductory training should commence with simple, familiar, clinical presentations, with due attention paid to individual students’ levels of comfort and disease-related experiences. Future research should further explore the specific determinants of comfort level, taking into consideration the place of gender, culture, cultural beliefs, and disease types and more importantly, the way forward in training, empowering, and engaging SPs in handling all case types.
