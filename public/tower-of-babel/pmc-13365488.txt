EV-ready building codes and electric vehicle adoption
Authors: Jiehong Lou, Deb A Niemeier
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEV-integrated building codes are an innovative tool that local governments can use to promote electric vehicle (EV) adoption. Despite their growing use, evidence on their effectiveness remains limited. In this study, we analyze EV registration data from Maryland from 1999 to 2024 to evaluate the impact of local EV-ready building codes on EV adoption. Using a synthetic difference-in-differences design, we find that EV-ready policies increase EV adoption by 0.25 vehicles per census block on average, primarily driven by battery electric vehicles. Our analysis of distributional effects shows that the benefits are concentrated in higher-income communities and areas with a greater share of single-family homes. These findings suggest that while EV-ready building codes effectively support EV adoption, complementary measures are needed to promote more inclusive adoption.Subject terms: Environmental impact, Climate-change mitigation, Energy economics, Interdisciplinary studies

Introduction

The transportation sector is the largest contributor to the greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions in the United States. As of 2021, it accounted for 29% of the nation’s GHG emissions and 27% of its total primary energy consumption1,2. Over the past five years, emissions from transportation have surpassed those from the electric power sector, now accounting for two-fifths of domestic emissions from burning fossil fuels. Between 1990 and 2021, the transportation sector saw the largest absolute increase in GHG emissions compared with other sectors, such as industry, agriculture, residential, and commercial activities3. This rise is driven largely by growing travel demand and the sector’s heavy reliance on petroleum, a trend exacerbated by the low price sensitivity of transportation services. Electrifying the transportation sector, especially through the widespread adoption of electric vehicles (EVs), is therefore a critical and urgent priority for reducing carbon emissions. This shift is also essential for countries to fulfill their Paris Agreement commitments. EVs should become the predominant mode of private car sales within the next 20 years4. In line with this, the United States announced in its second Nationally Determined Contribution (NDC) a commitment to ensuring that EVs comprise 50 percent of all new vehicle sales by 20305.

It is crucial to recognize the pivotal role that state and local governments play in reducing transportation emissions, as most of these emissions occur on roads they own and operate within the U.S. context6. Decarbonizing this sector necessitates proactive involvement and leadership from state and local agencies. Governments generally promote financial incentives for fuel-efficient vehicles, including federal tax credits and state-level financial incentives7–12. Additional incentives also include privileges such as EV access to high-occupancy vehicle (HOV) lanes to encourage the transition away from gas vehicles8. Local governments often actively promote EV adoption through investments in public and private EV charging infrastructure9. An innovative approach that state and local governments are now employing to incentivize EV adoption is through building codes. Traditionally used to “protect public health, safety, and welfare in the construction and occupancy of buildings,” building codes have now become one of the most effective and cost-efficient strategies for decarbonization13. For example, governments have integrated EV-specific requirements into building codes to support EV charger installation initiatives. As of May 1, 2024, eight states, six counties, and 36 U.S. cities (Fig. 1) have updated building codes for EV-capable, EV-ready, and EV-installed provisions.

These building codes—EV-Capable, EV-Ready, and EV-Installed—categorize the degree of electrification in buildings, targeting single-family homes (SFH), multi-family homes (MFH), and commercial buildings, as illustrated in Fig. 1. In this paper, MFH includes both rented and owned multi-family residences, such as apartments. These types of building codes primarily focus on regulations for private-access charging infrastructure.

Installing EV charging infrastructure in SFHs and MFHs presents several challenges, including electrical capacity upgrades, installation costs, planning, collaboration with local authorities, permitting, safety requirements, technical considerations, and future-proofing. Two challenges, in particular, stand out: the strain EV charging infrastructure can place on the electrical grid and the high cost of retrofitting. Installing EV charging infrastructure with high-voltage requirements can overload the grid, necessitating costly electrical capacity upgrades14,15. The expenses associated with these upgrades, combined with labor and other installation costs, significantly inflate the overall installation charges16–18. Studies indicate that integrating EV chargers during initial construction can be 64% to 75% cheaper than retrofitting existing buildings19,20, while some estimates suggest that retrofit costs can be four to eight times higher than deploying chargers during initial construction21,22 for both SFH and MFH building types. Therefore, excessive retrofitting costs act as an obstacle to widespread EV adoption23,24 and require partnership between local governments and private entities to incentivize homeowners and MFH residents.

Incentivizing infrastructure buildup, alongside tax incentives and other non-monetary benefits, can significantly increase EV adoption in the U.S.9,25. Access to private charging infrastructure plays an important role in influencing people’s willingness to purchase an EV, with nearly 80% of plug-in EV users still charge their vehicles at home. Multiple studies conducted worldwide consistently find that home is the most preferred location for charging EVs, followed by the workplace and public charging. Easy access to home charging persuades customers to purchase an EV26–28. The convenience of Level 1 chargers significantly increases the odds of being interested in EVs by approximately 124-136% compared with those lacking access29. Additionally, Level 2 chargers increase the likelihood of EV ownership by approximately 86% and motivate the purchase of larger battery EVs30,31. A Swiss study reports that individuals with private charging are 200% more likely to express strong willingness to purchase an EV compared to those who parked on the street, and 50% more likely than those who parked in shared lots or garages32. Installing Level 2 chargers in MFHs is crucial in reducing the dependence on public Level 2 and direct-current fast charging (DCFC) while also supporting EV growth in these areas33,34. In short, the literature clearly supports that deploying charging stations at homes and in residential areas can significantly contribute to achieving a potential EV coverage of 99%35.

In this paper, we assess the effects of specific EV-ready building code regulations in Howard County, Maryland, on overall EV adoption. Howard County was one of the first jurisdictions in Maryland and one of the 18 early adopters nationwide to implement such policies (see Supplementary Section A for a detailed background on EV-ready policies in the U.S. and Maryland). In Maryland, Frederick County and Prince George’s County followed Howard County’s lead in 2022, preceding the passage of a statewide EV-ready bill in 2023 (Supplementary Fig. 12). Effective January 11, 2019, Howard County Bill CB-76-2018, titled “Electric Vehicle (EV) Charging Station Requirements,” mandated all new one- and two-family dwellings with a garage, carport, or driveway to be EV-ready36. This necessitates all newly constructed buildings to be equipped with electrical wiring for Level 2 or higher EV charging stations, implying at least one EV-ready parking space per unit. Additionally, new MFHs are required to have one EV-installed parking space, which is a pre-installed Level 2 EV charging station, for every 25 units37. Howard County’s bill has received strong support and has contributed to the county achieving the highest per capita ownership of EVs in Maryland38. It has served as a model to fashion similar statutes in other jurisdictions, such as Atlanta (17-0 −1654, amended as 18-0 −1143), NYC (Local Law 130), San Francisco (Ordinance 92-17), Vancouver (By-law 9691 and 9936), and Salt Lake City (Ordinance 20 of 2017), with the closest EV readiness statute being found in Denver (Building Code Section R327)39.

To estimate the causal effect of the EV-ready policy on EV adoption, we employ a difference-in-differences (DID) empirical strategy, the synthetic difference-in-differences (SDID) design, using Howard County as the treatment group, since it was the first county in the state to enact the policy on January 11, 2019. SDID improves causal estimation by leveraging both pre-treatment time periods and cross-sectional units to construct a synthetic counterfactual, enhancing the robustness of our findings40. Our analysis is based on panel EV registration data from 1999 to 2024 at the block level, covering 75,097 EVs across Maryland, with additional sociodemographic variables such as population, education, median household income, median house age, vehicle ownership, etc., from the U.S. Census Block Group level based on the American Community Survey (ACS) five-year estimates.

Our findings indicate that the EV-ready policy led to an increase of 0.25 EVs per census block per year on average, representing a 28% rise compared to the pre-policy period. Furthermore, the policy impact varied between battery electric vehicles (BEVs) and plug-in hybrid electric vehicles (PHEVs), with an increase of 0.253 BEVs and 0.047 PHEVs per census block, respectively. In addition, BEVs exhibited a much faster growth rate relative to the pre-policy period than PHEVs, at 53% versus 12%, respectively. We further find that these policies disproportionately benefit communities with higher incomes and greater concentrations of SFHs. This suggests that more targeted efforts are necessary in underrepresented communities that require additional support to facilitate rapid and equitable EV adoption.

Results

Positive impact of building codes on EV adoption

The synthetic difference-in-differences estimations demonstrate that the EV-ready policy significantly boosts EV adoption, with a positive effect distinguishable from zero at the 1 percent significance level. Specifically, EV adoption in treatment blocks rose by an additional 0.25 EVs compared to control blocks (Table 1). This finding holds across our four model specifications: without control variables (1), accounting for public EV infrastructure and utility companies (2), restricting the sample to observations with available control variables (3), and a reduced sample that controls for public EV infrastructure, utility companies, and all other variables (4). Specification (1) provides the largest sample size. In specification (2), we introduce controls for public EV infrastructure and utility companies to assess whether these factors significantly influence the results. As noted in the Methods section, some control variables from the U.S. Census are unavailable at certain block levels, limiting data availability. Consequently, incorporating additional controls reduces the sample size significantly from 1,830,660 to 726,852 observations. To address this limitation, in specifications (3) and (4), we estimate the model using the reduced sample, where specification (3) excludes additional controls, while specification (4) includes them. Despite differences in sample sizes, the estimates remain highly consistent across all four model specifications, reinforcing the robustness of our findings.

Differential impacts of building codes on BEV and PHEV adoption

We also examined the policy effect separately for different EV types by conducting SDID analyses for BEVs and PHEVs. Our findings, illustrated in Fig. 2, indicate that the policy effect differs between BEVs and PHEVs. Specifically, we observed an increase of 0.253 BEVs and 0.047 PHEVs per census block per year using the 2023 specification, which excludes two counties. Using the 2022 specification with the full sample, we observed consistent outcomes. Given the state averages stock of 0.61 BEVs (2.99 in Howard County) and 0.28 PHEVs (1.03), the increase in BEVs relative to pre-policy period is significantly faster than that of PHEVs due to the policy implementation, at 53% and 12%, respectively.

The colored dots represent estimated effects of the EV-ready policy on BEV and PHEV adoption obtained from running the synthetic difference-in-differences (SDID) specification separately (The sample size is 2,095,675 for the full sample and 1,744,425 for the full sample with utility and charger.). The colored horizontal bars with corresponding colors represent the 95% confidence intervals of the estimations.

The differentiated impact on BEVs and PHEVs is intuitive. The EV-ready policy is particularly relevant to BEV owners. BEVs, unlike PHEVs, rely entirely on electric power and thus require more frequent access to charging stations. This makes BEV owners more dependent on robust charging infrastructure compared to PHEV owners who have the flexibility of a gasoline backup. Consequently, policies designed to enhance charging access directly influence the attractiveness and practicality of BEV ownership, leading to higher adoption rates. This pattern aligns with findings from the literature. Studies finds that PHEV owners are less likely to plug in at home than BEV owners41, that BEV owners rely more on Level 2 home chargers42, and that some PHEV owners may forgo charging altogether if gasoline is available for longer trips or when charging options are limited43. Thus, a more notable policy effect on BEV adoption rates compared to those of PHEVs was observed, and it underscores how well-designed infrastructure policies can support broader climate goals without prescribing specific technological pathways.

Disproportionate policy impacts in high-income, or single-family home communities

To better understand the impact of EV-ready policy on EV adoption, and to determine whether these policies affect targeted areas and who benefits from them, we examine variations across different socioeconomic groups. Specifically, we consider the distribution by area of concentration of SFH, rural/urban areas, as well as by income and race.

In this analysis, SFH covers both of single-family detached and attached homes given the definition from the US Census44. Accordingly, we calculate the percentage of SFH within each block by combining the share of housing units in detached and attached structures, while the percentage of MFH is calculated as 1 minus the share of SFH. Blocks with more than 50% SFHs are classified as SFH-dominant and those with 50% or less as MFH-dominant. For rural and urban classification, we use the percentage of urbanized areas, with 0% defined as rural and 100% defined urban. For income, we classify blocks into low- and high-income groups using the overall sample mean household income ($84,972.41 from Methods) as the threshold. Finally, for race, we classify blocks with 50% or less White population as low-White areas, and those with more than 50% as high-White areas.

We conduct separate regressions for all the groups. The results of these regressions are presented in Fig. 3 accompanied by formal statistical tests reported in Table 3 that evaluate differences in the estimated policy effects across the two groups within each panel. Each panel features black dots that represent the point estimates for the treatment group for each group, while the black vertical bars indicate the 95% confidence intervals of these estimates. Panel a in Fig. 3 and Table 3 demonstrates that the treatment effects on EV adoption increase as the concentration of SFH units rises, as the policy’s influence strengthens when moving from MFH-dominant blocks to SFH-dominant blocks. In MFH-dominant areas, the point estimate of the effect is an increase of 0.084 EVs per block on average, while in regions where the concentration of SFH units is 50% or higher, the effect rises substantially to more than 0.289 EVs per block. This indicates that areas with higher proportion of MFH experienced smaller impacts compared to those with higher proportion of SFH, and the difference in estimated policy effects is statistically significant according to the T-test in Table 3 (p-value = 0.000). It is also worth noting that the error bars are larger for MFH-dominant blocks, likely due to smaller sample sizes in those categories. This aligns with the fact that only 13.5% of the blocks in our sample are MFH-dominant, suggesting a lower representation of MFH-heavy blocks compared to SFH-heavy ones.

Panel b illustrates the impact of the EV-ready policy on rural and urban areas, indicating a greater policy effect in rural regions (where urbanized areas are 0%). The difference in the estimated policy effects between rural and urban areas is approximately 0.04 EVs per block at the 5% significance level according to the T-Test. This finding suggests that rural communities may be more responsive to EV-ready policies, potentially due to higher dependence on personal vehicles and limited access to alternative transportation options compared to urban areas (only 5% of households in rural areas are without a car, compared to 12% in urban areas in our sample). Another explanation is that rural areas typically have a higher share of SFHs (83% compared to 76% in urban areas in our sample), which, based on our analysis, are more responsive to the EV-ready policy.

Panel c demonstrates a discernible increase in treatment effects correlated with rising income levels. For the low-income group, the point of estimate averages 0.157 EVs per block, whereas for the high-income group, the point estimate rises to 0.223 EVs per block. Both estimates are statistically significant at the 1% level, highlighting the robustness of the results. Furthermore, the comparison between low- and high-income groups in Table 3 highlights that the difference in the estimated policy effects is −0.066, at the 1% significance level according to the T-test, confirming that the gap is statistically significant. This finding suggests that higher-income households are more responsive to EV-ready policies, potentially due to greater financial capacity to purchase EVs. By contrast, while low-income households also benefit from the policy, their adoption levels remain lower, underscoring the importance of equity-focused measures to ensure that EV-ready policies do not disproportionately advantage higher-income communities. It is also worth noting that the treatment sample in the low-income group is relatively small compared with the treatment samples in other panels. This is primarily due to the high affluence of the treated county (Howard County), which results in fewer low-income observations in the treatment group. According to U.S. Census data, the median household income in Howard County was $149,763 in 2024, compared with a statewide median of $102,905 (both in 2024 dollars).

Finally, Panel d compares the response to the EV-ready policy between areas with higher concentrations of White residents and those with lower concentrations. Overall, communities with a higher percentage of White residents show slightly lower EV adoption rates in response to the EV-ready policy. And the difference in the estimated policy effects between white-dominant and minority-dominant blocks is significant, according to the T-test in Table 3 (p-value = 0.041).

The distributional analysis suggests that the effects of the EV-ready policy are predominantly observed in areas characterized by high-income population and a higher share of SFHs relative to other dwelling types. A key component of the EV-ready policy aims to promote EV adoption in MFHs by mandating the installation of chargers in complexes with more than 25 units. However, the distributional effects depicted in Panel a indicate that this specific objective has not been completely realized.

We further analyzed housing unit trends in new construction across Maryland and specifically in Howard County. As depicted in Fig. 4a, Maryland experienced an upward trend in new construction, increasing from 18,491 units in 2019 to 19,346 units in 2022, with an annual average of 19,928 units over the four years. In contrast, in Howard County (Fig. 4b), the number of new constructions has fluctuated, with an overall average of 1,037 units per year over the same period. This translates to an average of 0.47 new constructions per census block in Howard County (1,037/2,204 blocks). When considered alongside our finding of an increase of 0.25 EV per census block resulting from the EV-ready policies, these results appear to be consistent and reasonable.

At the state level, the distribution between SFHs and MFHs in Fig. 4c has remained relatively stable, with MFH share accounting for 24–40% in general of new construction, except in 2022 when the share spiked to 56% before returning to previous levels. By contrast, Howard County exhibited much wider variation, with MFHs share ranging from just 2% to as high as 61% of new construction in Fig. 4d. At both the state and county levels, however, the recent upward trend in MFH development (averaging 36% in Maryland and 38% in Howard County during 2010–2024) may not fully translate into the expected policy outcomes. One partial explanation lies in historical patterns: between 1990 and 2010, MFHs accounted for only about 19% of new construction in both Maryland and Howard County (Maryland: 17% in 1990–1999 and 22% in 2000–2009; Howard County: 14% and 25%), reflecting a long-standing baseline of limited MFH development.

Discussion

Our primary objective is to provide an evidence-based evaluation of EV-ready policies. We find that the EV-ready policy in Howard County had a considerable impact, leading to an average increase of 0.25 EVs per census block per year. These findings underscore that EV-ready building codes can serve as a cost-effective and impactful tool to accelerate EV adoption, even in the absence of direct government subsidies. The policy implications are far-reaching. When analyzing the implementation of a policy, the most common strategy is to conduct a cost-benefit analysis. In this case, the primary costs of the EV-ready policy to government are regulatory in nature, involving procedural and administrative expenses. Although difficult to precisely quantify, these costs are minimal compared to the substantial benefits of accelerating the transition to clean transportation.

The benefits of implementing EV-ready policy are threefold. First, these policies may support increased adoption of EVs if applied statewide. Based on our previous estimation of an average increase of 0.25 EVs per block per year due to the EV-ready policy in Howard County, a 28% increase relative to the total adopted EVs during the entire pre-policy period, applying this policy to the entire state could potentially result in up to 21,279 additional EV adoptions over the next five years, assuming the policy had been put into place at the start of 2024. However, we acknowledge that this assumption has its limitations, as factors such as socioeconomic differences, infrastructure variations, and policy enforcement may lead to varying impacts across different counties. This estimate serves only as the upper bound of a potentially optimistic outcome, given that Howard County is a relatively wealthy area in Maryland. To provide a more conservative estimate, we conducted a lower-bound estimate assuming the policy is implemented among lower-income groups, drawing on our distributional analysis in Table 3. This suggests a lower-bound estimate of approximately 13,362 additional EV adoptions over the next 5 years. As of Jan 31, 2026, there are currently 105,102 EVs in Maryland, according to Maryland Department of Transportation (MDOT)’s official figures. Maryland has committed to achieving a target of 600,000 EVs (MDOT, 2023), and this policy alone could contribute between 3.0% and 4.7% of the remaining 450,000 EV target. The increase in EV adoption could likely spur further infrastructure development, such as more charging stations, which could in turn make EV ownership more convenient and attractive to consumers. This positive feedback loop can accelerate the transition to cleaner transportation45.

Second, the policy could provide substantial health benefits by reducing emissions. Electric and hybrid vehicles produce far fewer substantial emissions than conventional vehicles. According to the U.S. Department of Energy46, BEVs produce zero tailpipe emissions, thereby reducing pollutants such as nitrogen oxides (NOx), particulate matter (PM), and volatile organic compounds (VOCs) that contribute to smog and respiratory problems. Additionally, the overall reduction in greenhouse gas emissions is notable. Our back-of-the-envelope calculation, based on Department of Energy data47, suggests that if applied statewide, the EV-ready policy could cut annual emissions by 111,502 metric tons of CO2 equivalent under renewable fuel sources, and by 101,500 metric tons of CO2 equivalent under a mixed fuel scenario, compared to traditional gasoline cars (see Supplementary Table 20). Overall, the implementation of the EV-ready policy can create a cascade of positive effects, from increased EV adoption and enhanced infrastructure to meaningful health and environmental benefits48–50.

Third, it avoids future retrofit costs of electrical wiring and panel systems, as well as the procedural costs of securing permits, whether general electrical permits or specific EV permits in certain counties. Currently, retrofit costs can vary depending on factors such as building type, geography, and the number of parking spaces, as shown in Supplementary Fig. 2. For both SFHs and MFHs, retrofitting can be four to eight times more expensive than deploying chargers during initial construction21,22. By contrast, including EV-ready features at the construction stage accounts for only approximately 0.06% of total construction costs in SFHs and 0.3% in MFHs (see Supplementary Section D), demonstrating that proactive planning is a cost-effective approach51,52. In summary, while there are some direct and indirect costs associated with building code mandates, borne by developers as well as homeowners and buyers, these additional costs remain relatively modest. Our semi-structured interviews with developers and real estate professionals (see Ethics Statement and Supplementary Table 9) further support this finding, reinforcing that EV-ready policies impose relatively low costs compared to other infrastructure requirements. With federal budget constraints phasing out EV tax credits and restricting infrastructure investment53, EV-ready building codes represent a particularly cost-effective and essential policy tool for accelerating EV adoption.

Building on the discussion of the potential benefits and associated costs, our findings demonstrate that the EV-ready policy should be promoted by local governments across the United States. The policy has proven effective in significantly increasing EV adoption rates, as evidenced by our findings in Howard County. By implementing similar policies nationwide, states can not only advance their goals for reducing greenhouse gas emissions but also encourage the development of essential EV infrastructure. This includes the installation of more charging stations, which could make EV ownership more accessible and convenient for consumers. Additionally, the widespread adoption of EV-ready policies can create a positive feedback loop54,55, driving further technological advancements and infrastructure improvements. Therefore, it is imperative that state and local governments prioritize and enact EV-ready regulations to foster a sustainable and cleaner transportation future.

It is important to note several caveats to our results. First, our estimate is based on an affluent county, which should be regarded as an upper bound of potential policy impact if extrapolated to the entire state. While we also provide a lower-bound estimate, we acknowledge that these figures should be interpreted with caution. Nonetheless, our results demonstrate that EV-ready building codes can meaningfully influence EV adoption. Given the wide range of benefits associated with EV adoption, it is encouraging that more local governments are implementing these codes, and it would be even more beneficial if adoption were expanded further. Second, this paper does not account for supply-side effects or private costs associated with implementing EV-ready policies. While such costs exist, they are generally modest. Third, we use the model year or release year of the EV as a proxy for registration year due to the format of the original dataset. Although we were able to simulate a used EV market and obtain consistent and robust results across different scenarios—including a scenario that more closely reflects low-income households—we recognize the limitations of this approach. We encourage future research to further examine the used EV market and refine the methodology to better capture market realities.

Methods

In this paper, we evaluate the impact of specific EV-ready building code regulations implemented in Howard County, Maryland, on overall EV adoption rates. We employ an empirical approach, the synthetic difference-in-differences (SDID) design, using Howard County as the treatment group, as it was the first jurisdiction in the state to enact such a policy on January 11, 2019. This setting offers a valuable quasi-experimental context for identifying the causal effects of a specific subnational EV-ready mandate on EV adoption.

Data

EV registration data

Our primary data include EV registrations from 1999 to 2024 in Maryland, covering both PHEVs and BEVs at the block level, sourced from the Maryland Department of Transportation. This comprehensive dataset records each EV’s model year, make, series name, and block of registration. In total, we have data on 75,097 EVs, with an average of 3 EVs per block (see Supplementary 13a). Since exact registration date were not available, we used two approaches to approximate them. First, we used the vehicle release date to proxy for registration date. We utilize vehicle model year, make and series number to identify its official release date. From these activities, we identified 41% of the data. For EV models without a clear release date from the initial search, social media was used to find the earliest video reviews of the model. The date of the earliest video review was used as an estimated release date for the EV model. We further validate this step by checking the models with both official release dates and social media review dates, we found that most social media review dates were a few months later than the official release dates. Second, we used the model year56 as proxy for registration year, to provide additional robustness analysis.

Public EV infrastructure data

We use public charging station data from the U.S. Department of Energy’s Alternative Fuels Data Center (AFDC), which provides a comprehensive dataset listing each recorded alternative fuel station. We refine these data to include only public, electric charging stations in Maryland. The AFDC dataset includes the opening date for each station, and the earliest listed opening date for a charging station in Maryland is 2011 (Supplementary Fig. 11a). The data used in this analysis were retrieved from the AFDC website on March 1, 2024. We then matched the number of EV stations at the block level with EV registration data by block for the same period (see Supplementary Fig. 11b).

Utility companies

Recognizing the notable impact of various incentives from utility companies in EV adoption, we obtain data on the service boundaries of utility companies for the entire U.S. from the U.S. Energy Information Administration57. From this dataset, we extracted information pertaining only to those companies that provide services in Maryland, identifying 32 utility companies operating within the state. In our study, each block is assigned to a utility company; however, in instances where two companies serve the same block, we retain the company with the larger service territory within that block (see Supplementary Fig. 11c). Additionally, we conduct a robustness check analysis by dropping blocks that have multiple utility companies, we obtain consistent results in Supplementary Table 7.

Other demographic and economic data

We sourced various socioeconomic variables, including population, education, median household income, and median house age, vehicle ownership, etc., from the U.S. Census Block Group level using American Community Survey (ACS) five-year estimates from 2010 and 2023. The block group level represents the most detailed level of data available from the Census. For a comprehensive summary, please refer to Table 4. The rational for adopting this list of variables is based on a systematic literature review identifying potential determinants of EV adoption (see Supplementary Sections G, H and K). Therefore, we include these variables to control for potential confounding factors. In addition, given the importance of charging infrastructure, we include the average distance (in miles) from residents in each block to the nearest public EV charger as an additional variable, following the methodology established in a previous study by the authors58. Additionally, we classify blocks into low- and high-income groups using the overall sample mean household income ($84,972.41 from Table 4) as the threshold.

Empirical strategy

Howard County provides the opportunity to study the combined effects of EV-ready policies on EV adoption from both SFHs and MFHs due to its uniqueness. Our primary empirical strategy to evaluate the EV-ready policy on EV adoption is through the SDID design recently introduced by Arkhangelsky et al59. Unlike the traditional difference-in-differences (DID) methods, which is widely used in applied economics over the last three decades60, SDID was introduced as a new methodology combining features of DID and synthetic control (SC) methods.

The drawback of DID lies in its reliance on the parallel trend assumption, which is often challenging to meet with real-world data. Alternatively, SC strictly limits control groups to those closely matching the treatment group in the pretreatment phase, but it doesn’t accommodate different trends between control and treatment groups.

In contrast, SDID overcomes these limitations by allowing treated and control units to follow different trends before the reform of interest. SDID seeks to optimally generate a matched control unit, significantly relaxing the need for parallel trend assumptions61. The innovation of SDID lies in its inclusion of both pre-treatment time periods and cross-sectional units when constructing a synthetic counterfactual for causal estimation40. It is novel in its use of time and unit weights to create a counterfactual, its inclusion of unit fixed effects (which are absent in SC methods), and its reliance on weighting control observations to achieve parallel trends with treated observations rather than directly matching them40,62.

By incorporating unit and time weights, SDID allows us to compare similar units and time periods, leading to a more robust estimator. With these unit and time weights, we are able to estimate the average causal effects of policy exposure through the two-way fixed effects regression as follows:

Identification and case selection

Our goal is to build a solid evidence base for EV-ready policy and to use Howard County, Maryland as a case study given the availability of EV registration data. We estimate the effects of an EV-ready policy on EV adoption using a SDID framework. We use Howard County as the treatment group, as it was the first county in the state to implement the policy on January 11, 2019. For the control group, we apply two approaches to define the control groups.

In the first approach, we maximize the time horizon, extending the analysis period to October 1, 2023, when the policy was implemented statewide. We retain all observations before October 1, 2023. Since Frederick and Prince George’s Counties also implemented similar policies in July and August 2022, respectively, we exclude observations from these two counties from the control group. This leaves us with 2,202 blocks in the treatment group and 68,206 blocks in the control group. In the second approach, we include all Maryland counties in our sample by shortening the timeline to July 1, 2022, when Frederick County began implementing similar policies. As a result, the treatment group remains at 2,202 blocks, while the control group increases to 81,623 blocks. The drawback of this approach is that it results in the loss of over a year of observations.

Our analysis requires a registration year, which was not included in our dataset. To proxy for registration year, we use either the model year or the release year of the EV. To ensure robust results, we run multiple specifications, adjusting the dates to test the sensitivity of our results. Additionally, this approach rests on the assumption that buyers generally prefer purchasing the newest models available. We were able to validate this assumption by examining the model year data of newly registered EVs in Oregon and Washington. Both states maintain records of EV vehicle registration (new and renewal) and model years. Focusing solely on new registrations, we find that over 85% of buyers selected the most recent models at the time of their initial registration in Panel a and b of Fig. 5. This trend is consistent across both states and supports the validity of our assumption.

To account for this, we conducted a simulation to model the secondary market. Across 1,000 iterations, we obtained the distribution of the estimates, as shown in Fig. 5c. Robustness analysis

To better understand SDID and evaluate its efficacy, we also performed DID and SC analyses and compared their results to those from SDID (see Supplementary Section B). We recognize that when there are pre-treatment differences between treatment and control groups, DID provides an alternative strategy for identifying treatment effects. However, this method relies on the assumption that, in the absence of the EV-ready policy, the initial disparities between treatment and control groups would have remained consistent. Since we didn’t pass the parallel trend test in our case, we rely on SDID as our primary research design. Moreover, SC is most effective when synthesizing an optimal control group in settings where only one unit is treated, by applying weights to pre-treatment period control units. We also conducted the SC analysis to provide additional robustness. The results listed in Table 5 are consistent with main results.

Using model year as a proxy for registration year

In our primary analysis, due to data limitations, we use the official release date as a proxy. In this section, we introduce another proxy, the model year of the EVs. Figure 6 illustrates that both dates provide consistent and closely aligned estimates of the policy’s impact. Specifically, if we use the model year, we observe that the EV-ready policy increases EV adoption by 0.246 EVs per block (Supplementary Table 2), whereas using the official release date yields an estimate of 0.248 EVs. Furthermore, we notice that the effect estimated using the official release date is slightly higher across the three specifications when considering the entire sample up to 2023. However, if we limit our sample to the year 2022, the impact estimated from the official release date is slightly lower.

We also make a few specific adjustments to ensure the robustness of our analysis. Given the peculiarities associated with model years, we specifically excluded the year 2019. This is because model year vehicles for 2019 were often released in the later months of 2018. By omitting 2019, we ensure that all subsequent model years analyzed are strictly post-2019. We also conducted analyses limited exclusively to the years 2021, 2022, and 2023. The results of these analyses are presented in Supplementary Fig. 1 and Supplementary Tables 3-6. In general, all of the results indicate a notable increase in EV adoption, with a range of 0.107 to 0.302 new EVs due to the EV-ready policy.

Potential Impact from the Fleet-based EV

To address concerns that our original dataset may contain outliers, such as blocks with unusually high concentrations of EVs of the same brand, potentially indicating government fleet ownership. We undertook a detailed set of steps to identify and mitigate the influence of fleet-based EVs. Specifically, we flagged brand–model–year combinations within a single block that exceeded 10 vehicles, treating them as likely government procurements, except for Tesla Model 3 and Model Y, which together account for about 35.8% of U.S. EV sales and are predominantly household vehicles (Supplementary Table 10). We then excluded these blocks with potential fleet concentrations and reran our SDID models. As detailed in Supplementary Section F, we find that our results in Table 6 remain highly robust and consistent.
