Estimands in Cardiovascular Device Trials: What Are They and Why Do They Matter?
Authors: Andrew D. Althouse, David J. Cohen, Graeme L. Hickey
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•The word “estimand” means “target of estimation”.•All clinical trials implicitly have estimands, even if not explicitly defined.•Estimands can be particularly important when comparing data across trials.•Estimands are now part of official guidance documents from International Council for Harmonisation, International Organization for Standardization, and Food and Drug Administration.•Clinical trials of implantable medical devices have some unique considerations when defining and targeting estimands.

Background

Clinical trials are an important tool for evaluating the safety and effectiveness of medical treatments, both pharmaceutical treatments and implantable devices. Of course, the analysis and interpretation of clinical trial data can be complicated by issues such as crossover, dropout, and/or use of other treatments during the trial. Even the best-designed and conducted clinical trials are affected by these issues, and many do not make explicit a) how these issues are handled in statistical analysis and b) the effect such issues may have on the interpretation of the study results.

The International Council for Harmonisation (ICH) of Technical Requirements for Pharmaceuticals for Human Use publishes guidance documents on the design, conduct, safety, and reporting of clinical trials. One fundamental guidance document is ICH E9 Statistical Principles for Clinical Trials, first released in 1998.1 In October 2014, the ICH produced a concept paper titled “Choosing Appropriate Estimands and Defining Sensitivity Analyses in Clinical Trials” as an addendum to the existing guidance on statistical principles for clinical trials; the final ICH E9(R1) addendum was released in November 2019.2

The addendum describes a systematic approach to ensure that the trial design and statistical analysis are aligned with the scientific objectives. A key element of this approach is clearly articulating the estimand, or the target of estimation, associated with the study objectives.

To date, much commentary has focused on the application of estimands in clinical trials of pharmaceutical treatments. However, trials of implantable medical devices have some different considerations than trials of pharmaceutical agents. For example: most medical devices are implanted in a single procedure and remain in place and active unless they are removed from the patient, whereas a pharmaceutical agent that is given on a periodic regimen (e.g., daily, weekly, etc) may be temporarily discontinued, have the dose adjusted, or augmented with a “rescue” therapy. These differences in treatment delivery can raise different considerations for the trial estimands, which we will discuss in the text that follows. Furthermore, with the addition of estimands to the 2026 International Organization for Standardization guidance on Good Clinical Practice for clinical investigation of medical devices in human subjects,3 this will be an increasingly important topic for trialists and readers in this space. In this paper, we will introduce the concept of estimands to the structural heart clinical audience, and then provide contextual examples to make this concept approachable.

What Is An Estimand?

ICH E9(R1) defines a clinical trial estimand as “a precise description of the treatment effect reflecting the clinical question posed by the trial objective.” With a clearly defined estimand, one may select a suitable estimator (statistical approach used to estimate the target defined by the estimand) and compute the estimate (the actual numerical value computed by the estimator). A clinical trial estimand should clearly address 5 attributes.1.Population: population of patients targeted by the clinical question2.Treatment(s): treatment of interest and any alternative treatment to which a comparison will be made3.Variable (or endpoint): outcome measure of interest4.Intercurrent events: these are events occurring after treatment initiation that can affect either the interpretation or the existence of the measurements associated with the clinical question of interest. Notably, the phrase “after treatment initiation” could arguably be modified to “after study enrollment” to accommodate the possibility of intercurrent events that might occur before treatment initiation, or in patients who are enrolled in a trial that do not receive the active treatment assignment. Note that an estimand must address both what the intercurrent events are, and what strategy will be employed to handle them in analysis.5.Summary measure: numerical quantity that will be used to evaluate the trial objective

Four of these 5 attributes (population, treatment, variable/endpoint, and summary measure) are generally straightforward to identify for seasoned trialists and readers, even in trials that have not fully implemented the estimand framework. However, we find that intercurrent events are usually the most challenging aspect to define and account for in data analysis. Therefore, we will focus our discussion on issues related to intercurrent events.

Common Types of Intercurrent Events

The most straightforward example of an intercurrent event is death. For any endpoint other than all-cause mortality, death certainly meets the definition of an “event that can affect the interpretation or the existence of the measurements associated with the clinical question of interest.” Consider a trial of mitral valve replacement with an endpoint “resolution of mitral regurgitation (MR) at 30 days”—if any patients have died before the 30-day assessment, they will not have a 30-day visit echocardiogram to assess the primary endpoint. As cardiovascular device trials often treat populations with high morbidity and mortality, death is an inevitable intercurrent event and one that must be accounted for properly in data analysis of nonmortality outcomes to ensure alignment with the trial’s scientific objectives.

Another example is crossover to the other treatment arm, which is particularly important in the context of medical device trials (or perhaps, more generally, in trials of procedural interventions). This is a specific case where device trials may have quite different considerations than pharmaceutical trials. Pharmaceutical trials are typically more amenable to placebo-controlled designs that preserve blinding during the treatment period, making it difficult (in some cases, even impossible) for patients in the control group to cross over to the experimental treatment arm. Conversely, many procedural trials are unblinded or open-label—and can be subject to pressure from patients who may be dissatisfied with their initial assignment and/or their outcome and may wish to cross over to the other treatment arm if that option is available. Consider a trial that randomly assigns patients to an initial procedure versus guideline-directed medical therapy (GDMT). If some patients assigned to the GDMT arm feel that their symptoms have not sufficiently improved or have even worsened after a few months, they may inquire about whether they can receive the procedural option. Although it may be standard clinical practice to permit this crossover, it is important to consider the impact this might have on the observed treatment effect within the trial. Procedural trials that implant devices are also often effectively irreversible—once the device has been implanted, it is going to remain in the patient unless an invasive removal procedure is undertaken, whereas a pharmaceutical treatment typically can be discontinued at any time.

A third example is the addition of other treatments beyond the defined treatment conditions under study. Consider a trial of renal denervation versus sham control, with the primary endpoint of blood pressure 24 weeks after the index procedure. Of course, patients may still be taking antihypertensive medications to control their blood pressure during the follow-up period. The use of the additional medications may alter the interpretation of the current blood pressure measurement—in this case, the differential use of antihypertensive medications in one arm relative to the other could impact the observed difference in blood pressure between the two arms.

Note that although each of these intercurrent events meets the broad definition of “events occurring after treatment initiation that can affect either the interpretation or the existence of the measurements associated with the clinical question of interest,” they present distinct issues. Death is an example of a truncating event—where there are no more data to be collected after the event at all. Crossover to the other treatment arm is a trial-specific event, as the concept of a treatment arm is specific to the setting of a clinical trial. Addition of other treatments is arguably representative of an event that happens in routine clinical practice, inside or outside of a trial setting.

Distinguishing Intercurrent Events from Missing Data

It is also critical to distinguish intercurrent events from the broader topic of missing data. Although an intercurrent event can be a cause of missing data, simply having a missing data point is not an intercurrent event. For example, consider an endpoint that is assessed on a 30-day visit echocardiogram. Data for this endpoint could be missing because the patient has died, or because they did not have their 30-day visit, or perhaps even if they had the 30-day visit but a poor quality image did not allow reading the specific variable of interest. The mere fact that the data are missing is not an intercurrent event—but the underlying cause of the missingness may be an intercurrent event (e.g. death before the 30-day visit).

This distinction is important because the mere presence of some missing data in a data set does not alter the intended scientific question, but how the analysis strategy handles missing data due to intercurrent events might alter the scientific question being answered. Therefore, the strategy for handling missing data due to intercurrent events must be aligned with the intended scientific question.

Strategies for Handling Intercurrent Events

There are 5 commonly acknowledged strategies for handling intercurrent events – not all of which are equally applicable or even sensible for different types of intercurrent events.1.Treatment policy: analyze the outcome regardless of the intercurrent event.2.Composite: the intercurrent event is treated as an additional component of the outcome variable.3.Hypothetical: we wish to know what the outcome would have been in a hypothetical scenario where the intercurrent event did not occur.4.Principal stratum: the inference is restricted to those who would not experience the intercurrent event.5.While-on-treatment/while-alive: use data only up to the time that the intercurrent event occurs (e.g. treatment switching, treatment discontinuation, or death) in the analysis.

Writing an Estimand for a Clinical Trial: Implications of Different Choices

Consider a single-arm trial testing a new bioprosthetic valve for patients with severe mitral regurgitation. The outcome is defined as “freedom from MR at 30 days” and the statistical analysis plan states that this will be reported as a binomial proportion. The clinical question for this trial appears quite simple from a statistical perspective: what proportion of patients treated with the new valve will be free of MR 30 days after the procedure?

What is the correct estimate of the proportion of patients treated with the new valve that are free of MR 30 days after the procedure?

A total of 50 patients had an attempted procedure, and 44 were still alive with a 30-day visit echocardiogram to confirm that they were free of mitral regurgitation. So one possible answer could be 44/50 = 88% of the patients were free of MR 30 days after the procedure.

But might one reasonably argue that patients who did not actually have the valve implanted in the index procedure are not informative regarding the efficacy of the valve in resolving mitral regurgitation? In that case, perhaps the correct answer is 44/49 = 89.8% (excluding the 1 patient who did not have a device implanted during the index procedure from the denominator). Is this appropriate? It depends on the scientific question we are trying to answer; if one argues that the failure to implant the device is not related to the valve’s ability to resolve MR but some other failure mode (e.g. aborted procedure due to anatomical factors that were only identified at the time of surgery), it can be argued that this approach yields a more appropriate estimate of the quantity of scientific interest.

Going further, one might also argue that the purest efficacy estimate of the ability to durably resolve MR should include only patients with evaluable MR at the time point of interest; someone with this viewpoint might report a success rate of 44/47 = 93.6% (excluding both the 1 patient who did not have the valve implanted during the index procedure and the 2 patients who died sometime between the procedure and the 30 day visit). Extending the aforementioned logic, this approach would be defensible if the deaths occurred for some noncardiac reason, perhaps arguing that in the absence of an echo showing mitral regurgitation, we do not know the status of MR for those patients, and they should not be considered as “failures” in the denominator of this calculation.

Which of these is the correct answer? An estimand could be written as follows:“The estimand will quantify the proportion of patients with severe mitral regurgitation who have an attempted procedure with Device X that are still alive with resolution of MR on an echocardiogram 30 days after the attempted procedure, regardless of whether Device X was successfully implanted during the index procedure. Unsuccessful implant procedures and deaths at any time from the implant procedure through 30 days will be considered a failure to resolve MR at the 30-day assessment.”

“The estimand will quantify the proportion of patients with severe mitral regurgitation who have an attempted procedure with Device X that are still alive with resolution of MR on an echocardiogram 30 days after the attempted procedure, regardless of whether Device X was successfully implanted during the index procedure. Unsuccessful implant procedures and deaths at any time from the implant procedure through 30 days will be considered a failure to resolve MR at the 30-day assessment.”

Note that the estimand addresses each component in turn. It defines population, treatment, variable, and summary measure. It addresses 2 different intercurrent events—aborted procedures and deaths before 30 days—noting that both will be considered a failure to resolve MR (utilizing the Composite strategy). Stated in probabilistic notation, the target of estimation may then be defined as:

Pr(Alive With No Residual MR at 30 Days | Attempted Procedure with Device X) or in written words, the “probability that a patient is alive with no residual MR at 30 days, conditional on the fact that they had an attempted procedure.”

It is readily apparent that by including patients who died in the denominator of this proportion, this estimand provides a conservative estimate of the device’s true ability to resolve MR; if there are patients in whom the device worked properly (resolved MR) that died for other reasons before the 30-day assessment, they are treated as failures in the approach outlined above. However, if one considers the objective of a medical procedure as not only fixing a mechanistic problem but keeping patients alive and well, it seems reasonable to consider survival a necessary condition for a patient to be declared a success. The final decision about whether this estimand is in fact the correct estimand to target rests on the argument about the true scientific question. Are we interested in the patient-centric question of “If I undergo this procedure today, what are the chances I’ll be alive with no MR in 30 days?” Or are we more interested in the device-performance question “If the valve can be implanted and is still in place after 30 days, what is the probability the patient will still be free of MR?” Either can be valid, but they answer different questions and should not be compared directly to one another.

In summary, what appears to be a clearly defined analysis may be ambiguous unless the estimand and handling of intercurrent events are clear. Therefore, having a clearly written estimand that explicitly identifies the intercurrent events and the strategies for handling them are essential to prespecifying a valid analysis approach in trial documents such as the protocol and statistical analysis plan.

Discussion

The estimand framework is increasingly recognized and requested by important bodies such as International Organization for Standardization, ICH, and the Food and Drug Administration. It is useful for all parties with an interest in clinical trial design, analysis, and interpretation to become familiar with the terminology and thought process that underlies these concepts. As described in this paper, the concept of intercurrent events and strategies for handling them is quite complex and takes some hard thinking.

The case study illustrates that even in the simple scenario of a single-arm trial with a binary outcome, different approaches to handling intercurrent events can lead to different numerical estimates for analyses that may appear to be reporting the same thing. Using the sample numbers given in Figure 1, one could reasonably argue that “freedom from MR at 30 days” is any one of 44/50 (88%), 44/49 (89.9%) or 44/47 (93.6%) for this example. Precisely defining the estimand helps identify which is the “correct” answer for a particular question by linking the scientific objective to the statistical approach.

We also believe that increased adoption of the estimand framework could enhance the ability to compare numbers across different studies. This is particularly important for single-arm trials evaluating success against a performance goal. As performance goals for single-arm trials are often derived using estimates from previously reported trials, it is important for such trials to have clearly defined estimands so performance goals can be derived based on estimates that target the same estimand as intended for the present trial (or if no such trial has targeted the same estimand, the performance goal derivation may be adjusted to account for this issue). Even outside the setting of a formally tested performance goal, it is often tempting to compare outcomes such as “procedure success” or “device success” or “freedom from mitral regurgitation” across studies. In such an application, the estimand framework has the potential to provide greater clarity about whether estimates from different studies are targeting the same quantity.

In summary, all trials implicitly target estimands whether they formally acknowledge it or not. Explicit use of the estimand framework is critical to ensuring transparency in data analysis and reporting, which will ultimately improve the ability of regulators and clinicians to interpret trial results and make decisions accordingly.
