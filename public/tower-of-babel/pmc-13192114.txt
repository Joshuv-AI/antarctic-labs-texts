Hyperkalemia management in heart failure and chronic kidney disease: a Spanish cost-effectiveness analysis of patiromer informed by the integrated DIAMOND trial and real-world evidence
Authors: A Bayes-Genis, J Butler, E Stawowczyk, M Turner, R D Lewis, I T Lloyd, A Ramirez de Arellano
Published: 2026

The recent DIAMOND clinical trial (ClinicalTrials.gov: NCT03888066) reported that the use of patiromer led to a significant reduction in RAASi discontinuation or dose adjustment, serum potassium levels, and episodes of HK in patients with HF and coexisting CKD. Background

Hyperkalemia (HK), defined as serum potassium level > 5 mmol/L, is frequently observed in patients with heart failure (HF) and concomitant chronic kidney disease (CKD) [1]. A meta-analysis study in 2014 reported that between 32% and 49% of people with HF also have CKD; in real-world settings, approximately 40% of diagnosed HF patients have HK, with a high risk of experiencing recurrent episodes [2]. Both conditions are increasingly prevalent, particularly in older adults or those with additional comorbidities [3].

Patients with HK are at increased risk of adverse clinical outcomes, including chest pain, major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE), hospitalization, renal replacement therapy (RRT), and mortality [4]. The increased risk of HK in HF and CKD means that ~ 50% of this patient group may experience two or more episodes of HK recurrence on an annual basis [1].

Patients with HF and/or CKD are often prescribed renin-angiotensin-aldosterone inhibitors (RAASi) to alleviate HF symptoms, halt disease progression, and improve cardiovascular outcomes (including death and HF hospitalizations). However, RAASi use itself increases the likelihood of patients experiencing HK, particularly in patients with CKD, thus the management of these patients is particularly complex [5, 6]. In this population, HK is often managed with suboptimal RAASi dosing or complete discontinuation [7, 8], leading to an increased likelihood of adverse events [9, 10]. In Spain, HF prevalence is estimated at 4–7%, with a study from 2024 reporting that up to 70% of those on a chronic HF registry in Spain also present with kidney disease [11–15]. In a large study of patients with HK and HF in the UK, HK increased the risk of RAASi discontinuation and mortality, emphasizing the need to monitor and manage serum K+ levels in patients [16]. To support the effective management of HF and facilitate the optimal use of RAASi, the Spanish Society of Cardiology (SEC) and the Spanish Society of Internal Medicine (SEMI) issued a consensus statement in 2022 recommending adherence to the guidelines of the European Society of Cardiology (ESC). These guidelines highlighted the use of K+ binding drugs for the management of HK in patients with HF [17, 18]. Further, the use of K+ binders for treating HK in CKD has recently been endorsed in the 2020 and 2021 Kidney Disease Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) clinical practice guidelines for diabetes management in CKD and blood pressure in CKD, respectively [19, 20].

Patiromer is an oral K+ binding agent with a strong safety and tolerability profile for the long-term management of HK in individuals with HF and CKD [21]. A real-world study published in 2022 showed that over a three-month follow-up period, patiromer treatment maintained or reduced K+ levels in patients with HF [22]. This reduction in K+ levels allowed patients to continue RAASi treatment at the optimal dose and reduced hospitalizations [22]. Data from the OPAL-HK and DIAMOND clinical trials have also supported the established safety and efficacy of patiromer. Patiromer is proven as a treatment for HK, aimed at reducing the likelihood of HK and enabling RAASi treatment to be maintained at optimum levels for a longer period in patients with HF and CKD [23, 24]. To be a viable treatment option, treatments also need to be cost-effective. Patiromer has been shown to be cost-effective in several European countries; however, data from the smaller-scale OPAL-HK trial were used to conduct cost-effectiveness estimates [25–28]. A cost-effectiveness evaluation of patiromer using data from the recent, large-scale, DIAMOND clinical trial has not yet been conducted [23].

In this study, we present a health-economic evaluation of patiromer in treating HK in patients with HF and CKD in Spain, informed by the integrated DIAMOND trial and real-world evidence (RWE) [16, 23].

Methods

Trial design and patient population

The DIAMOND study (ClinicalTrials.gov: NCT03888066) was a placebo-controlled, double-blind, randomized withdrawal trial in patients with HF with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF) and CKD [23, 29]. The trial was designed to evaluate long-term efficacy of patiromer for the control of serum K+ levels. Adults aged ≥ 18 years with class II–IV HF according to New York Heart Association (NYHA) functional classification, with left ventricle ejection fraction ≤ 40%, and concomitant CKD were eligible for participation. Trial inclusion criteria also required HK at screening or a history of HK within 12 months, characterized as serum K+ exceeding 5.0 mmol/L. Patients were excluded if they had severe loss of kidney function according to an estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) of < 30 ml/min/1.73 m2, systolic blood pressure < 90 mmHg or symptoms of hypotension, or any other significant cardiovascular comorbidity unrelated to HF. The inclusion and exclusion criteria are listed in DIAMOND trial protocol [29].

Patients enrolled in the trial were titrated to 8.4 g of patiromer up to three times daily while RAASi therapy was optimized for the duration of a run-in phase of a maximum of 12 weeks. The run-in phase of the DIAMOND study was designed to control K+ levels using patiromer and concurrently allow optimization of RAASi medications (use and dose), including mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists (MRAs) (titrated to 50 mg/day). Following management of serum K+ levels, eligible patients were randomized to two treatment arms either continuing patiromer treatment or undertaking patiromer withdrawal (placebo treatment). Following randomization, serum K+ levels were monitored at frequent intervals, with the primary endpoint of adjusted mean change to serum K+ levels from baseline. Secondary endpoints included HK related outcomes, such as time to first event of HK, cardiovascular death, and cardiovascular hospitalization.

Cost-effectiveness model (CEM)

A previously published cohort state-transition Markov model was adapted to simulate disease progression in Spanish patients with HK, CKD, and HF (Fig. 1) [30]. The model was informed by data from the DIAMOND trial and real-world evidence (RWE) [16, 23]. Cost-effectiveness of patiromer versus standard of care (SoC) was expressed as incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER), in terms of extra cost per year of life saved and extra cost per quality-adjusted life year (QALY) gained. The model followed a monthly cycle, an 80-year lifetime horizon, which assumed that all patients would have died within this period, and captured the perspective of a Spanish health care payer. Future clinical benefits and costs were discounted at 3% per annum, in accordance with Spanish economic evaluation guidelines [31].

Treatment value was captured as individual patients passed through the economic model. The impact of each treatment was reflected through the incidence of HK events and down-titration or discontinuation of RAASi. RAASi use was categorized as optimal (maximum), sub-optimal (down titrated) and off. The level of RAASi use influenced the probability of CKD progression, mortality among CKD patients, and MACE in CKD and HF populations, as well as the occurrence of HK events and hospitalizations in both CKD and HF patients. Other clinical events, such as MACE (defined as coronary heart disease, peripheral arterial disease leading to hospitalization, ischemic stroke, and HF), hospitalization (defined as any hospitalization), and mortality are also captured within the model as transient events, with their probabilities influenced by patient health status (CKD and HF), RAASi, and severity of HK. In the DIAMOND trial, AEs recorded in the blinded treatment phase, including those reported after trial completion, included, hypokalemia (mild-severe), constipation, diarrhea, nausea, hypomagnesemia, AEs leading to patient withdrawal and serious AEs. Incidence of AEs was comparable between the placebo and patiromer groups in the DIAMOND trial and due to low incidence of serious events (13.2% versus 12.3% respectively), these parameters were not included in the current model.

Costs and utilities

Direct medical costs were assigned to each modelled health state and event, and are detailed in Supplementary Appendix C. Spanish-specific sources were used to derive costs and were inflated to 2022 values [40].

Health state costs were assigned to each CKD and HF health state; sourced from published literature. Health resource costs were calculated according to RAASi status (optimal, sub-optimal, and off therapy), and HK event severity (mild HK: K+ > 5.0 to ≤ 5.5 mmol/L; moderate HK: K+ > 5.5 to ≤ 6 mmol/L; and severe HK: K+ > 6 mmol/L). Costs for major events (MACE and hospitalization), ESRD-related events (dialysis access, dialysis complications, and transplant procedure), and HK management were informed by country reference unit costs and published literature. Meanwhile, resource use for HK management was informed by the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) technology appraisal of sodium zirconium cyclosilicate for the treatment of HK (TA599) [41]. Finally, no mortality costs were applied in the model.

Patiromer cost was €7.59 per 8.4 g daily, sourced from the Bot plus Medication database [42]. Annual RAASi costs per patient, categorized by status subgroup and estimated according to average use of the drugs (informed by the clinical expertise of the Spanish members of the study), were €151.13, €75.57, and €0.00 for optimal, sub-optimal and off therapy, respectively. For patients receiving patiromer, continuous use was assumed throughout the patient’s lifetime; therefore, no stopping rule was applied, however treatment discontinuation was applied based on values observed in DIAMOND trial [26]. Monthly probability of patiromer discontinuation was 9.1% in first three months and 1.9% in month four onwards [26].

Baseline utility values were derived from Spanish age-dependent estimates, with health state utilities for CKD and HF, along with event-related disutilities applied to calculate utility per arm per cycle. In absence of exhaustive Spanish utility data published estimates from other countries were used. Detailed utility weights are provided in Supplementary Appendix D.

Country-specific costs and utility inputs are presented in Supplemental Appendix C and D, respectively.

Mean K+ values

The DIAMOND clinical trial included a 12-week run-in phase during which all participants received patiromer to standardize baseline treatment and serum K⁺ levels [23]. Following randomization, mean serum K⁺ levels in the placebo arm were influenced by the residual effects of prior patiromer treatment and therefore do not represent clinically realistic values for the standard of care (SoC) arm in the cost-effectiveness model. To address this misalignment with real-world clinical practice, SoC serum K⁺ values were sourced from a RWE study by Linde et al., [16], which included a large UK cohort of patients with HF and CKD receiving RAASi therapy. In this study, hyperkalemia was defined as serum K⁺ ≥ 5.0 mmol/L, and a weighted average of baseline serum K⁺ levels was used to inform the SoC arm in the cost-effectiveness model (Table 2). This approach is consistent with previous economic evaluations of patiromer by Senni et al., in an Italian population [30].

However, the Linde et al., population was not directly comparable to the DIAMOND trial population [16, 23]. Therefore, the DIAMOND trial data were adjusted using a unanchored matching-adjusted indirect comparison (MAIC) to estimate serum K⁺ levels at screening and at the end of the run-in phase for a DIAMOND population reweighted to match the RWE cohort [16, 43]. These adjusted values informed the baseline and patiromer arms in the cost-effectiveness model (Table 2), a similar approach to a previous economic evaluations of patiromer by Senni et al., in an Italian population [30]. A MAIC was selected as the preferred indirect treatment comparison (ITC) methodology after assessing available approaches, in line with guidance from Signorovitch et al., and NICE DSU Technical Support Document 18 [43, 44]. A MAIC involves reweighting individual patient-level data (IPD) from the index trial to align with aggregate characteristics of the comparator study, thereby enabling estimation of treatment effects in the target population. In this analysis, the DIAMOND IPD was reweighted to match the Linde et al., cohort [16].

A MAIC includes all identified treatment effect modifiers (TEMs) and prognostic factors (PFs) as covariates for adjustment. A review of patiromer publications identified the following variables as TEMs or PFs: age, sex (proportion male), region (proportion in the European region), estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) and diabetes status. Although baseline serum K+ levels were identified as a PF/TEM it was excluded from matching as it was the outcome of interest [16, 23]. Following the matching process, population baseline characteristics showed a high degree of matching (Table 3) (Supplemental Appendix E, Table 1; Fig. 2), with low standardized mean differences between the post-matching DIAMOND population and Linde et al., [16]. This indicates that the MAIC successfully adjusted for differences, enabling a more representative comparison despite initial population imbalance. Some differences were observed in the proportions of patients using angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors (ACEIs), angiotensin receptor blockers (ARBs) and mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists (MRAs) at baseline. Since all patients in the DIAMOND study population were receiving RASSi at baseline, the adjustment of RAASi use is not possible. Similarly, as 99.9% of patients in DIAMOND were using MRAs at baseline, we would not expect to be able to match the populations on this characteristic. These data indicate that while matching was achieved for key variables including age, region, sex, diabetes status, and eGFR, some notable differences between the RWE and adjusted populations remain.

The histogram of patient weights (Supplemental Appendix E, Fig. 1) shows that most patients have weights between zero and one, with a small number of high weight patients. The weighting of the DIAMOND population resulted in an effective sample size of 254, indicating sufficient overlap between the DIAMOND and RWE cohorts, supporting the feasibility of a MAIC to derive adjusted serum K+ levels. While this is a notable reduction from the original population size (878 patients in the DIAMOND trial), an effective sample size (ESS) of 254 remains a sizeable population for the analyses, and any bias introduced by high-weight individuals is likely to be minimal, as there are very few patients with weights greater than three. The larger the ESS, the greater degree of similarity (overlap) between the populations; an ESS has a theoretical maximum equal to the total population size in the index trial, which would indicate total overlap of the populations, while an ESS of zero would indicate no overlap (and render a MAIC infeasible). Although the MAIC ensures comparability of populations and reduces bias in treatment effect estimates compared to a naive comparison, it is associated with increased uncertainty due to the reduced ESS.

Sensitivity analyses

Sensitivity analyses were carried out to assess the robustness of the model. Deterministic sensitivity analysis, a univariate analysis, was conducted by varying individual parameters either according to upper (120%) and lower (80%) bounds of the parameter mean value, or by plausible absolute value ranges. In a probabilistic sensitivity analysis, estimation of the combined impact of parameter variation was conducted. The analysis was run for 1,000 simulations and the convergence was achieved after approximately 100 runs. All model parameters were sampled using beta (probabilities, utilities, and baseline characteristics), normal (clinical inputs and baseline characteristics), log-normal (hazard and odds ratios) or gamma (clinical inputs and costs) distribution. Further details are presented in Supplemental Appendix F.

Subgroup analyses

Analyses were conducted to determine which subgroup patient population would be most beneficial to treat with patiromer, in terms of clinical outcomes and cost, with the aim to inform decision makers on how to optimize its impact within the Spanish healthcare setting. Patients were stratified by CKD severity, based on baseline eGFR; CKD stages 3a-3b (eGFR 30–59 mL/min/1.73m2) and 3b–4 (eGFR 15–44 mL/min/1.73m2). Subsequently, the effect of patiromer on cost and clinical efficacy at different CKD stages was analyzed. Additionally, a subgroup analysis for patients with a history of HK at baseline was conducted.

Results

Base-case cost-effectiveness results

The outcomes of the base-case cost-effectiveness analysis are detailed in Table 4. Compared with SoC, patiromer was associated with gains of 2.05 LYs and 1.61 QALYs. The incremental discounted lifetime cost per patient was estimated at €37,428 and was predominantly driven by the acquisition cost of patiromer (Table 5). The resulting ICER was €23,251 per QALY gained, and €18,241 per LY gained, indicating patiromer in patients with HF and concomitant CKD is likely to be cost-effective in Spain at a willingness-to-pay threshold of €30,000/QALY gained.

Patients on patiromer experienced fewer moderate and severe HK events (12.75 and 0.13), along with reductions in RAASi reinitiations or up-titration (1.39 events), down-titration (0.79 events), and discontinuation (1.73 events), compared with SoC over lifetime (Fig. 2).

Over a lifetime horizon, patiromer was associated with a higher total number of MACE and hospitalizations compared with SoC (Fig. 2), primarily because patients receiving patiromer lived longer. However, annual event rates were lower with patiromer than SoC for both MACE (0.21 versus 0.26 per year) and hospitalization (0.53 versus 0.54 per year). Annual rates were similar between groups for dialysis (0.0084 versus 0.0087 per year), transplant (0.0080 versus 0.0082 per year), and dialysis complications (no difference).

Subgroup analysis

The subgroup analysis allowed us to determine the patient populations in which patiromer would have the greatest economic and clinical benefit. Patiromer treatment is most cost-effective in people with a prior history of HK and those in CKD stages, 3a–b and 3b–4, with ICERs of €23,241, €25,157 and €29,801 per QALY gained and ICER/LY gained of €18,234, €19,234 and €21,731, respectively (Table 6).

Sensitivity analyses

The results of the deterministic and probabilistic sensitivity analysis confirmed base-case results. A deterministic sensitivity analysis was undertaken, and each parameter was varied between the lower and upper bounds of the mean values used in the base case. The ten most influential parameters are depicted in Fig. 4, with K+ levels exerting the greatest impact on the modelled outcomes. Other key drivers include discount rate and health state utilities. The probabilistic sensitivity analysis estimated that treatment with patiromer in Spain would have a 75.6% chance of the ICER falling under the €30,000/QALY gained willingness to pay (WTP) threshold (Fig. 5) [45].

Discussion

HF is a growing global burden for the healthcare system, with prevalence of HF in Spain estimated at 4–7%, and the incidence projected to worsen over coming years due to an aging population [11–15]. Patients with HF often have concomitant CKD, with 70% of this patient population having CKD in Spain [46]. The gold-standard treatment for HF and CKD is RAASi or MRAs, with two thirds of patients in Spain receiving RAASi, however, their use is limited by the occurrence of HK [47]. A recent Spanish study estimated that two thirds of those on MRAs do not reach their target dose, with HK as the principal cause of failure to reach the optimal dose in 10.8% of patients and responsible for 28.9% of all cases of contraindication of MRA use [48]. Consequently, this has an impact on both clinical and economic outcomes. Multiple studies have shown that, in patients with HF and CKD, HK and the suboptimal use of RAASi are associated with increased healthcare resource utilization and costs. These findings underscore the importance of implementing an effective HK management strategy in clinical practice [49, 50].

Patiromer treatment has been approved for the management of HK in Spain since 2017 [51], and improved awareness of its efficacy in controlling HK and increased clinical utilization could lead to improved clinical outcomes in HF and CKD. This study is the first to utilize long-term data from the DIAMOND trial and RWE for the health-economic evaluation of patiromer in patients with HF and concomitant CKD in Spain. Previously, OPAL-HK clinical trial data had been used to demonstrate the safety and efficacy of patiromer in Spanish population with HK [24].

The results presented in this analysis demonstrate that the management of HK in patients with HF and CKD with patiromer is cost-effective in Spain. Treatment with patiromer led to reductions in moderate and severe HK and reduced the number of treatment alterations (up-titrations/down-titrations/discontinuations) compared with SoC. The results are aligned with previous cost-effectiveness studies of patiromer conducted in Europe utilizing OPAL-HK data, where an increase in discounted QALYs and a reduction in RAASi discontinuation were observed [25, 26]. These results are also supportive of a cost-effectiveness analysis of patiromer in Spain that used OPAL-HK clinical trial data [28]. The study by González-Juanatey et al., demonstrated that patiromer use increased QALYs when compared to SoC (5.76 versus 5.57), with cost-effectiveness (ICER of €19,092/QALY gained) mainly being driven by reduction in HK and maintenance of RAASi therapy [28]. Other cost-effectiveness analyses of patiromer have been published in recent years. A 2023 study by Ward et al., in patients with CKD and HK with and without HF also showed an increase in QALYs compared with SoC (6.36 versus 6.16), with an ICER of £14,816 per QALY gained [26]. Patiromer was predicted to have a 100% chance of being cost-effective at a WTP threshold of £30,000 in the UK, driven by a decrease in HK and RAASi stabilization [26]. These studies support the use of patiromer in the management of HK in patients with HF and CKD. Furthermore, the cost-effectiveness analysis based on the DIAMOND trial data confirms its cost-effective in the Spanish HF population with concomitant CKD, particularly in individuals with recurrent HK and early-stage CKD.

Limitations

There were limitations associated with this analysis. Firstly, due to the DIAMOND study design, a legacy effect of patiromer was observed in the SoC arm, resulting in low serum K+ levels compared with their real-world counterparts. To better represent K+ levels, an RWE study was incorporated, and a MAIC was conducted between the DIAMOND and RWE study populations to enhance the accuracy of inputs used in the CEM. The two populations showed some overlap, and following MAIC adjustment, these populations were well aligned on key variables (age, region, sex, diabetes status and eGFR). The final adjusted serum K+ concentrations were similar to those from the DIAMOND trial after matching. However, the results should be interpreted with caution, given the differences in initial populations and methodology between the DIAMOND trial and RWE study [16, 23]. Furthermore, the model estimations of the lifetime impact of patiromer required the extrapolation of clinical outcomes past the observed trial duration of 54 weeks, which naturally introduces uncertainty into the model. In addition, the patiromer effect was based on the mean K+ value after the run-in period (12 weeks), with the assumption that this effect is constant thereafter, and is therefore subject to uncertainty. Data availability of certain clinical parameters and utilities resulted in the application of input figures sourced from country settings other than Spain, such as the UK and Sweden. Another constraint was the limited data available for overall RAASi use; instead, ACE inhibitors, ARBs, and angiotensin receptor-neprilysin (ARN) inhibitors were used as a proxy. Lastly, the model assumed a Spanish payer perspective; however, it does not incorporate indirect costs (e.g., productivity loss or caregiver burden).

Conclusions

HK is associated with a significant clinical burden and reduction in health-related quality of life [52], up to 43% of patients with CKD and/or HF having to reduce or discontinue RAASi [53, 54]. Hence, lowering K+ levels could have a significant impact on reducing the disease burden in this patient population. Guidelines for HK management in Spain exist, however increased awareness among clinicians regarding the benefits associated with K+ lowering treatments, such as patiromer, is required before the full impact of the use of these treatments on alleviating disease burden and savings for healthcare systems can be realized.
