Genomic and phenotypic comparisons reveal lineage-specific traits of the probiotic Saccharomyces boulardii
Authors: Hannah Elena Duffey, Karl Alex Hedin, Hitesh P. Gelli, Troels Holger Vaaben, Morten Otto Alexander Sommer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummarySaccharomyces boulardii is a widely used probiotic yeast, yet its functional distinction from closely related Saccharomyces cerevisiae strains and the consistency of traits across commercial isolates remain unclear. Here, we performed integrated genomic, physiological, metabolic, and host-cell analyses of five S. boulardii isolates and three distinct S. cerevisiae strains. Despite high genomic similarity, S. boulardii exhibited conserved lineage-specific features, including a chromosomal inversion, reduced CAZyme copy numbers, and consistent sequence variation in metabolic pathways. Functionally, S. boulardii isolates showed enhanced acid tolerance, elevated acetate and succinate production, and reproducible immunomodulatory activity, including NF-κB suppression and reduced IL-8 and TNF-α secretion. All isolates consistently activated the aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR), unlike other S. cerevisiae strains, which instead displayed faster growth and greater bile tolerance. Overall, probiotic-relevant phenotypes were conserved across S. boulardii, defining it as a functionally coherent probiotic lineage.

Introduction

Saccharomyces cerevisiae is one of the most extensively characterized eukaryotic model organisms, foundational to studies in genetics, metabolism, and cellular regulation.1 Its well-annotated genome and robust genetic tractability have made it a cornerstone of molecular biology and functional genomics.1,2,3,4 In parallel with its research utility, S. cerevisiae plays a central role in industrial biotechnology.5 It is widely used in baking, brewing, and winemaking, where its high fermentation efficiency, ethanol tolerance, and metabolic plasticity are exploited at commercial scale. More recently, it has become a favored platform for synthetic biology and metabolic engineering, supporting the production of high-value compounds such as biofuels, bioplastics, pharmaceuticals, and nutraceuticals.6,7,8 Its generally recognized as safe (GRAS) status, scalability, and resilience under industrial conditions make it a versatile chassis for diverse biomanufacturing applications.

A closely related taxonomic variant, Saccharomyces cerevisiae var. boulardii, was the first yeast strain commercialized for probiotic use in clinical settings.9 Although classified within the S. cerevisiae lineage, S. boulardii exhibits distinct phenotypic, genomic, and functional characteristics.10,11,12 It is routinely administered for the prevention and treatment of gastrointestinal disorders such as antibiotic-associated diarrhea, Clostridioides difficile infection, and inflammatory bowel disease.9,13 A defining feature of S. boulardii is its ability to remain viable and metabolically active in hostile gastrointestinal environments, including exposure to low pH, bile salts, digestive enzymes, and host temperature.10,11,14 These attributes are less pronounced or absent in many laboratory and industrial S. cerevisiae strains, which typically fail to persist or function effectively in the gut.10,11

Despite this shared ancestry, genomic similarity between S. boulardii and other S. cerevisiae strains does not necessarily imply functional equivalence. Extensive strain-level diversity within S. cerevisiae—driven by ecological niche, domestication history, and selective pressures—has been shown to generate substantial differences in stress tolerance, metabolism, and host interaction.3,4 Accordingly, relatively small but conserved genomic features, including copy number variation, structural rearrangements, or pathway-level sequence variation, may be sufficient to underpin reproducible probiotic-relevant phenotypes.

The commercial use of S. boulardii is dominated by the CNCM I-745 strain, which has been validated in over 90 randomized clinical trials.15 However, several manufacturers’ market probiotic products labeled as S. boulardii using distinct, often proprietary strains, such as CNCM I-1079, CNCM I-3799, and DBVPG 6763. These strains are frequently assumed to be functionally interchangeable, yet their genomic and phenotypic equivalence has not been systematically evaluated. This lack of comparative data complicates regulatory assessment, reproducibility across products, and the rational development of next-generation yeast-based probiotics.16,17,18

Recent studies have begun to highlight conserved immunomodulatory mechanisms associated with S. boulardii, including suppression of pro-inflammatory signaling pathways and activation of the aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR) through yeast-derived metabolites.19,20,21,22,23 Independent work has further demonstrated the importance of strain specificity in yeast probiotics, showing that closely related isolates can differ markedly in metabolic output, stress tolerance, and host-response modulation.20

Based on these observations, this study was guided by three testable hypotheses: (1) that S. boulardii constitutes a functionally coherent lineage within S. cerevisiae despite high overall genomic similarity; (2) that conserved lineage-specific genomic features are associated with reproducible metabolic and immunomodulatory phenotypes relevant to probiotic function; and (3) that these probiotic-relevant traits are conserved across commercially distributed S. boulardii strains rather than being restricted to a single reference isolate.

To test these hypotheses, we performed a comparative analysis of five commercially available S. boulardii isolates and three phylogenetically and industrially distinct S. cerevisiae strains, including baker’s and laboratory variants. By integrating comparative genomics, growth and stress physiology, metabolic profiling, and host-cell assays, this study provides a focused framework for distinguishing lineage-level from strain-level probiotic traits and informs both regulatory evaluation and the development of next-generation yeast-based probiotics.

Results

Comparative genomic analysis reveals conserved and divergent features distinguishing S. boulardii from baker’s and laboratory S. cerevisiae strains

Whole-genome comparisons were performed across five S. boulardii isolates and three S. cerevisiae strains, including two baker’s strains and one laboratory strain. All S. boulardii isolates displayed high genomic conservation, with >99.98% average nucleotide identity (ANI), a genome size of approximately 11.8 Mb, and ∼6,400 predicted protein-coding genes (Figure 1A; Table S1). In contrast, baker’s and laboratory S. cerevisiae genomes were larger (∼12.3 Mb) and each contained more than 100 singleton genes. Gene cluster analysis identified sets of gene clusters consistently present or absent across all S. boulardii isolates relative to the other S. cerevisiae strains (Figure 1A; Table S2). Both cluster sets were sparsely annotated, with most open reading frames lacking functional assignments in current databases. Among the clusters conserved across all S. boulardii isolates, three contained identifiable Pfam domains corresponding to ribosomal protein S19, a Flo11 adhesin-related domain, and a cytochrome c/quinol oxidase subunit.

Analysis of gene copy number across functional categories revealed differences between S. boulardii isolates and the other S. cerevisiae strains. Examination of carbohydrate-active enzyme (CAZyme) families showed that baker’s and laboratory S. cerevisiae strains generally contained equal or higher copy numbers than S. boulardii isolates, with the exception of the AA4 and AA7 families, which were present at higher copy number in S. boulardii (Table S3). Expanded analysis across all annotated enzyme classes identified increased representation of enzymes from 12 functional groups in S. boulardii isolates relative to the other S. cerevisiae strains, including glucan 1,3-β-glucosidases and acid phosphatases (Table S4).

Whole-genome synteny analysis revealed overall chromosomal conservation across all strains, with no large-scale translocations detected. A single 68,263 bp inversion on chromosome XVI (positions 776,149–844,412) was consistently present in all S. boulardii isolates and absent from the other S. cerevisiae strains (Figures 1B and S1). This inversion encompassed several annotated genes, including NCE102, TPO3, LOA1, TAZ1, THI22, and NAT3 (Table S5).

To identify metabolic features distinguishing S. boulardii from other S. cerevisiae strains, we prioritized comparative analysis of protein-coding sequence variation in central carbon metabolism and tryptophan catabolism. Central carbon metabolic pathways were selected because they govern core fermentative and respiratory processes that differed phenotypically between strains in growth and metabolite profiling experiments. Tryptophan catabolism was examined separately because this pathway generates indole-derived metabolites with established relevance to host-microbe interactions and has been implicated in strain-specific functional differences among probiotic yeasts. Within central carbon metabolism, all S. boulardii isolates carried the SDH1 H202Y and F318Y amino acid substitutions in succinate dehydrogenase (Table S6). Within tryptophan catabolism, sequence variation was assessed in the upstream enzymes indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase (BNA2) and tryptophan aminotransferase (ARO8). BNA2 contained two amino acid substitutions (T268S and R371K) present in all S. boulardii isolates and absent from the other S. cerevisiae strains, while ARO8 harbored a conserved E495G substitution across all S. boulardii isolates (Tables S7 and S8). In contrast, no S. boulardii-specific substitutions were detected in downstream pathway enzymes, including pyruvate decarboxylase (PDC1) and aldehyde dehydrogenases (ALD6 and ALD5) (Tables S9–S11).

High-confidence single-nucleotide variant (SNV) analysis revealed very limited isolate-specific variation among S. boulardii isolates. Using stringent filtering criteria appropriate for nanopore-only assemblies, we identified a small number of high-confidence coding-region SNVs that vary among isolates, with Sb CNCM I-745 and Sb CNCM I-3799 each harboring rare isolate-specific variants in a limited number of genes (Table S12). No widespread coding divergence was observed across the remaining S. boulardii isolates, consistent with their high overall genomic conservation.

S. boulardii isolates exhibit enhanced growth and viability under host-relevant conditions

Building on the genomic analyses, we next evaluated the phenotypic performance of S. boulardii isolates under host-relevant conditions. Proliferation and survival were assessed in defined minimal and rich media across a range of temperatures, oxygen levels, pH values, bile concentrations, and simulated gastrointestinal fluids.

To evaluate growth at host-relevant temperatures, S. boulardii isolates were compared with baker’s and laboratory S. cerevisiae strains in defined minimal medium (DMM) and rich medium (YPD) at 30°C, 37°C, and 42°C under aerobic conditions. No significant differences in growth rate were observed between 30°C and 37°C for any strain in DMM (Figure 2A). Growth kinetics was highly similar across S. boulardii isolates and S. cerevisiae strains at both temperatures (Figures S2A and S2B). To approximate anaerobic conditions encountered in the gastrointestinal tract, growth was assessed at 37°C under oxygen limitation. All strains retained the capacity for anaerobic growth (Figures 2B and S2D). Switching from minimal to rich medium increased overall growth rates under aerobic conditions but did not substantially alter relative differences between strains (Figures S2E–S2G). In contrast, under anaerobic conditions in YPD medium, baker’s S. cerevisiae strains exhibited significantly higher growth rates than S. boulardii isolates (Figures S2H and S2I). The laboratory strain CEN.PK113-5D displayed growth behavior more similar to the baker’s strains than to S. boulardii isolates. Relative to aerobic conditions, anaerobic growth rates in DMM were reduced by 20%–45% across all strains (Figure 2C). In YPD, growth reduction under anaerobic conditions was more pronounced for S. boulardii isolates and CEN.PK113-5D (Figure S2E).

Tolerance to gastrointestinal stressors was next assessed by examining growth across a pH range of 1.5–6.0 in DMM and YPD. S. boulardii isolates sustained growth down to pH 3.5 in DMM and pH 2.5 in YPD, whereas baker’s and laboratory S. cerevisiae strains exhibited a pronounced decline in growth below pH 4.5 in DMM and pH 3.5 in YPD (Figures 2D–2G and S3A–S3D). Growth responses were highly consistent among S. boulardii isolates. At pH 3.5 in DMM and pH 4.5 in YPD, S. boulardii isolates exhibited significantly higher area under the curve (AUC) than S. cerevisiae strains (Figures S3E and S3F). In contrast, bile salt tolerance assays (0.3%–2.5%) showed that baker’s and laboratory S. cerevisiae strains tolerated higher bile concentrations than S. boulardii isolates (Figures S4A–S4E).

Finally, cell viability was quantified following exposure to simulated gastric, intestinal, and colonic fluids, as well as oxidative stress induced by 0.1 M hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), using live/dead staining and flow cytometry (Figures S5A and S5B). S. boulardii isolates exhibited substantially higher survival in both gastric and intestinal fluids compared with baker’s and laboratory S. cerevisiae strains, retaining approximately 50% viability, whereas S. cerevisiae strains showed only 0%–10% survival at 6 h in gastric fluids (Figures 2H and 2I). AUC analysis confirmed a significant difference between S. boulardii isolates and S. cerevisiae strains during gastric fluid exposure (Figure S5C), whereas no larger differences were observed among S. boulardii isolates. No major differences in survival kinetics or estimated half-life were observed between strains following exposure to simulated colonic fluid (Figure 2J). Under oxidative stress, baker’s S. cerevisiae strains displayed significantly higher viability than both S. boulardii isolates and the laboratory strain CEN.PK113-5D (Figure 2K).

S. boulardii isolates exhibit distinct central carbon metabolism across culture conditions

Having characterized growth and stress responses, we next examined how the different strains distribute carbon into biomass and extracellular metabolites under fermentative and respiratory metabolism. Biomass accumulation, substrate consumption, and metabolic byproduct formation were profiled over a 72-h time course in 250 mL shake-flask cultures.

In shake flasks, optical density at 600 nm (OD600) increased more rapidly in the commercial baker’s S. cerevisiae strains during the first 24 h, although all strains reached strain-specific stationary-phase plateaus by 72 h (Figure 3A). A decrease in glucose concentration was observed earlier in the baker’s S. cerevisiae strains, with approximately 50% of the initial glucose consumed within 8 h, whereas S. boulardii isolates and the laboratory S. cerevisiae strain completed glucose depletion by 24 h (Figure 3B). Ethanol concentration peaked at 24 h across all strains, with baker’s S. cerevisiae strains reaching higher maximum ethanol concentrations than both the laboratory strain and the S. boulardii isolates (Figures 3C–3G). To further characterize metabolic byproduct profiles, acetate, lactate, and succinate concentrations were quantified over time. S. boulardii isolates accumulated significantly higher levels of acetate than S. cerevisiae strains, reaching approximately 3 g/L by 24 h, compared with 1 g/L in baker’s S. cerevisiae strains, while the laboratory strain produced only trace amounts (Figures 3D–3G and S6). In contrast, lactate concentrations peaked at significantly higher levels in baker’s S. cerevisiae strains than in S. boulardii isolates, reaching approximately 4 g/L versus 2 g/L, respectively (Figures 3E and S6). Both acetate and lactate concentrations declined after 48 h across all strains. Succinate concentrations were significantly higher in S. boulardii isolates than in S. cerevisiae strains, peaking at 48 h and showing a modest decline at 72 h (Figures 3F and S6). The metabolic profiles were highly consistent among S. boulardii isolates.

Under anaerobic conditions, glucose consumption was extended to 120 h, with baker’s S. cerevisiae strains exhibiting earlier substrate depletion relative to the other strains (Figure S7). Under these conditions, ethanol, acetate, and lactate concentrations plateaued across all strains (Figure S7). Consequently, the metabolic differences observed under aerobic conditions were largely attenuated under oxygen limitation (Figure S6).

S. boulardii supernatants selectively modulate inflammatory signaling and epithelial barrier function

Building on the metabolic and growth characterizations, we next evaluated whether culture supernatants from S. boulardii isolates modulate inflammatory responses. To minimize confounding effects from undefined media components, all functional assays were performed using supernatants derived from strains grown exclusively in DMM. Supernatants were collected after 24 h of shake-flask cultivation, corresponding to peak acetate accumulation, and subsequently applied to human epithelial and macrophage cell models to assess immunomodulatory activity.

To examine anti-inflammatory effects in an epithelial context, we first used the human intestinal epithelial cell line HT-29, a well-established model for tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α)-induced cytokine responses. Supernatants from S. boulardii isolates reduced TNF-α-induced interleukin-8 (IL-8) secretion by approximately 25% relative to TNF-α treatment alone (Figure 4A). This response was broadly consistent across isolates. In contrast, supernatants from baker’s and laboratory S. cerevisiae strains did not significantly alter TNF-α-induced IL-8 secretion. These observations were further evaluated in THP-1 monocyte-derived macrophages stimulated with TNF-α or LPS. TNF-α stimulation did not increase IL-8 secretion relative to unstimulated controls; however, supernatants from several S. boulardii isolates and from other S. cerevisiae strains reduced IL-8 levels below baseline (Figure S8C). In contrast, LPS stimulation robustly increased both IL-8 and TNF-α secretion, and all yeast supernatants attenuated these responses (Figure 4B). Notably, S. boulardii supernatants produced a significantly greater reduction in LPS-induced TNF-α secretion compared with other S. cerevisiae strains (Figure 4C).

To assess signaling pathways associated with these responses, AhR and nuclear factor κB (NF-κB) activity were examined using reporter cell lines. In HT29-Lucia AhR reporter cells, supernatants from all S. boulardii isolates increased AhR-dependent luciferase activity by approximately 50% relative to media controls (Figure 4D), whereas supernatants from other S. cerevisiae strains did not significantly activate AhR signaling. NF-κB activity was assessed in THP1-NFκB-Luc2 cells following TNF-α or LPS stimulation. Under TNF-α stimulation, most S. boulardii supernatants significantly reduced NF-κB-dependent luciferase activity; however, this reduction did not reach statistical significance for Sb CNCM I-745 (Figure 4E). Following LPS stimulation, all yeast supernatants reduced NF-κB activity to a similar extent (Figure 4F).

Finally, the effects of live yeast on epithelial barrier integrity were evaluated using differentiated Caco-2 monolayers. Transepithelial electrical resistance (TEER) measurements showed that most S. boulardii isolates significantly increased TEER relative to untreated controls, indicating enhanced barrier integrity (Figures 4G and 4H). This effect was most pronounced for CNCM I-3799, whereas the increase observed for CNCM I-745 did not reach statistical significance. In contrast, baker’s S. cerevisiae strains did not significantly alter TEER.

Probiotic-relevant activities across all eight strains were summarized to enable strain-level comparison (Table S13). Across most assays, S. boulardii isolates showed similar response profiles, whereas baker’s and laboratory S. cerevisiae strains exhibited greater variability. Minor isolate-specific differences were observed, most notably for Sb CNCM I-745 in selected immunomodulatory readouts.

Discussion

This study was guided by three hypotheses: that S. boulardii constitutes a functionally coherent lineage within S. cerevisiae despite high genomic similarity; that conserved lineage-associated genomic features underlie reproducible metabolic and immunomodulatory phenotypes; and that these traits are conserved across commercially distributed S. boulardii isolates. By integrating comparative genomics with physiological, metabolic, and host-cell assays, our findings address each of these questions and clarify how subtle genomic divergence can yield consistent probiotic-relevant functionality.

Although S. boulardii isolates and baker’s and laboratory S. cerevisiae strains share >99% ANI, comparative genomics identified conserved lineage-associated features coinciding with functional divergence. All S. boulardii isolates clustered within the Wine/European clade (subclade 3),4 underscoring that functional differentiation in Saccharomyces often occurs within species boundaries. This context highlights the importance of accounting for extensive strain-level diversity when interpreting comparative studies,3,24 as laboratory, bakery, and industrial strains differ substantially in ploidy, ecological niche, and selective history. Within this framework, S. boulardii isolates exhibited a smaller, more conserved genome and a consistent set of lineage-associated gene clusters absent from the baker’s and laboratory strains. Although many of these loci remain poorly annotated, their conservation across independently sourced isolates suggests functional relevance. Similar genomic signatures have been reported previously,11,20,25 but our analysis extends these findings by explicitly linking them to reproducible phenotypes across multiple commercial isolates.

Physiological profiling revealed that S. boulardii isolates were highly similar to one another across diverse conditions, supporting lineage coherence. Growth at 30°C and 37°C was comparable, contradicting earlier claims of optimal growth at 37°C,9,12,18 which likely reflected comparisons with auxotrophic laboratory strains rather than wild-type S. cerevisiae.10,26 Instead, S. boulardii was distinguished by enhanced acid tolerance and superior survival in simulated gastric and intestinal fluids, consistent with adaptation to transient gastrointestinal exposure rather than superior proliferation. These traits are likely linked to its mannan-rich cell wall,27,28 a feature known to enhance stress resilience.29

Metabolic profiling revealed a consistent lineage-level phenotype characterized by elevated acetate and succinate production under aerobic conditions across all S. boulardii isolates. This profile coincided with conserved SDH1 H202Y and F318Y substitutions absent from the baker’s and laboratory S. cerevisiae strains. While these substitutions have been associated with altered respiratory metabolism and acetate production in S. boulardii,30 our findings demonstrate that this metabolic signature is conserved across multiple commercially distributed isolates and is accompanied by reproducibly elevated succinate levels. Notably, these metabolic differences were strongly oxygen dependent and largely absent under strict anaerobiosis, highlighting environmental context as a key determinant of probiotic mechanism. Given oxygen gradients near the intestinal epithelium,31,32,33 transient aerobic niches may permit localized acetate production in vivo, complemented by microbiota-mediated acetate generation under anaerobic conditions.19

The immunomodulatory activity of S. boulardii appears to depend on both the inflammatory stimulus and the responding host cell type, consistent with modulation of specific signaling contexts rather than broad immunosuppression. In intestinal epithelial cells, where responsiveness to LPS is limited by reduced functional TLR4-MD2 signaling,34 attenuation of inflammatory output is likely dominated by effects on cytokine-driven pathways such as TNF-α signaling. In contrast, myeloid cells respond robustly to LPS through TLR4-dependent activation of NF-κB, and under these conditions S. boulardii exerts stronger suppressive effects, consistent with interference at convergent downstream signaling nodes shared by TNF-α and LPS pathways. While acetate may contribute to this effect via GPR43 signaling and histone deacetylase inhibition,35 the partial immunomodulatory activity observed with other S. cerevisiae strains20,28,36,37 suggests the involvement of additional yeast-derived factors. A defining mechanistic distinction of S. boulardii is its conserved activation of AhR signaling across all tested isolates, a property absent from baker’s and laboratory strains and previously linked to probiotic activity.19 Given AhR’s central role in mucosal immune regulation and epithelial homeostasis,38 this pathway likely represents a mechanistically distinct axis that complements NF-κB modulation. Lineage-specific variants in BNA2 and ARO8 provide a plausible genomic basis for altered production of tryptophan-derived metabolites capable of engaging AhR, although direct identification of the active compounds remains a limitation and an important next step.

The functional relevance of these pathways is supported by epithelial barrier assays, in which only S. boulardii isolates enhanced transepithelial electrical resistance in Caco-2 monolayers. As both acetate signaling and AhR activation promote tight junction assembly and barrier integrity,39,40 their convergence provides a coherent mechanistic explanation for enhanced barrier function independent of growth advantage.

Previous studies of S. boulardii have largely focused on single reference strains or isolated phenotypes, often without accounting for strain-level diversity within S. cerevisiae or integrating genomic, metabolic, and host-response data. By systematically comparing multiple commercially distributed S. boulardii isolates with phylogenetically distinct baker’s and laboratory strains under harmonized conditions, this work demonstrates that probiotic-relevant traits in S. boulardii reflect conserved lineage-associated features rather than idiosyncratic strain effects. The identification of coordinated metabolic and immunomodulatory pathways—linking oxygen-dependent acetate production, AhR activation, and epithelial barrier reinforcement—provides a unifying mechanistic framework that extends current models of yeast probiotic function.

Although isolate-specific genomic variation among S. boulardii was minimal, a small number of high-confidence coding-region SNVs were detected in Sb CNCM I-745 and Sb CNCM I-3799. These variants raise the possibility that subtle genetic differences may contribute to the modestly reduced immunomodulatory responses observed for Sb CNCM I-745, although the limited number of variants and their unknown functional consequences preclude definitive conclusions. Importantly, SNV detection was performed on Nanopore-only, haplotype-collapsed assemblies, which necessitates stringent variant filtering to minimize sequencing error and limits resolution of low-frequency or heterozygous variants; consequently, direct functional validation and higher-accuracy sequencing would be required to establish causality.

Across most assays, S. boulardii isolates otherwise behaved comparably to one another, whereas baker’s and laboratory S. cerevisiae strains displayed greater phenotypic divergence, often differing from each other to a similar extent as from S. boulardii. Together, these findings extend prior single-strain studies by demonstrating that probiotic-relevant functionality in S. boulardii represents a conserved, lineage-level property supported by coordinated genomic, metabolic, and host-interaction features. Overall, the consistency observed across genomic, physiological, metabolic, and immunological analyses supports the functional interchangeability of commercial S. boulardii isolates. As yeast-based probiotics and advanced microbiome therapeutics gain prominence,41,42,43 integrating genomic and functional benchmarks will be essential for regulatory evaluation, quality control, and rational probiotic development.

Limitations of the study

This study has some limitations that should be considered. Genomic analyses were based on haplotype-collapsed long-read assemblies, which limit resolution of intra-strain heterogeneity and low-frequency variants, constraining interpretation of strain-specific mutations. Although consistent metabolic and immunomodulatory phenotypes were observed, causal links between genomic features (e.g., variants in SDH1, BNA2, and ARO8) and functional outputs remain inferential. Metabolite analyses relied on concentration measurements rather than flux-based assessments, limiting insight into metabolic dynamics. In addition, functional assays were performed in vitro under defined conditions, which may not fully capture the complexity of the gastrointestinal environment. While multiple commercial isolates were included, broader strain diversity may reveal additional variability. Future studies integrating higher-resolution genomics, targeted metabolite identification, and in vivo validation will be needed to confirm mechanistic links and physiological relevance.

Strain isolation and culture condition

S. boulardii isolates were selected based on commercial availability across different regions to capture representative strains currently used in probiotic formulations. Baker’s and laboratory S. cerevisiae strains were selected to represent phylogenetically and functionally distinct non-probiotic lineages and were likewise chosen based on regional availability. Two media types—a defined minimal medium and a rich medium—were used to systematically compare strain performance under nutrient-limited and nutrient-replete conditions. S. cerevisiae strains and S. boulardii isolates were isolated from commercial products (Table 1) by streaking onto YPD agar plates and incubating at 37°C for 48 h. High molecular weight yeast DNA extraction

Yeast cells were initially cultured in 25 mL DMM in baffled shake flasks for 24 h. Cells were subsequently harvested by centrifugation and resuspended to achieve an optical density 600 nm (OD600) of approximately 30, corresponding to approximately 3 × 108 cells per aliquot, in DNA/RNA Shield and stored at 4°C prior to DNA extraction. DNA extraction was performed according to the protocol previously described.45

DNA sequencing, assembly and gene calling

The following terminology is used throughout the manuscript: “individual genes” refers to annotated protein-coding loci within a single genome; “orthologous gene clusters” refer to groups of homologous genes identified by pangenome analysis across strains; “singleton genes” denote strain-specific gene clusters present in only one genome; and “functionally annotated gene families” refer to groups of genes classified by shared Pfam, KEGG, or CAZyme annotations. Unless otherwise stated, presence–absence patterns and copy-number variation are reported at the level of orthologous gene clusters. This terminology and level of reporting follow established conventions in the anvi’o comparative framework, in which genome comparisons are primarily conducted at the level of orthologous gene clusters rather than individual gene instances.46

Genomic DNA from eight Saccharomyces strains was sequenced by Plasmidsaurus using Oxford Nanopore Technologies and processed using a standardised assembly and annotation pipeline. Raw reads were quality filtered using Filtlong v0.2.1, discarding the lowest 5% of reads based on a composite Phred quality and length score (with --mean_q_weight 10). High-quality reads were assembled de novo with Flye v2.9.1, using parameters optimised for long read sequencing input. Contigs were annotated with Augustus v3.5.0, using an automatically selected gene model based on the closest reference genome. Open reading frames (ORFs) were queried against the UniProt v2024_04 database using BLAST v2.15.0, and top hits with e-values <0.05 were retained. Assembly structure was visualised using Bandage v0.8.1, and genome completeness was assessed with BUSCO v5.8.0. Assemblies were generated as consensus (single-haplotype) genomes, meaning that heterozygous variants are collapsed into a single representative allele rather than separated into two haplotypes. As a result, allelic variation within strains is not represented in the assemblies, and analyses therefore focus on lineage-level features rather than within-strain heterozygosity. The three S. cerevisiae strains were selected to represent two commercial baker’s strains and one laboratory strain, which together span distinct ecological and genomic clades previously shown to differ in ploidy and metabolic traits.3

Pan genome construction and comparative genomics

A pan-genome was constructed for all eight strains using Anvi’o v8,46 incorporating external gene calls generated by Augustus. Contig databases were annotated with Pfam v37.4 domain families and KEGG orthologs, then merged into a unified genome storage.

Gene clusters were identified via an all-vs-all DIAMOND search, followed by clustering using the Markov Cluster Algorithm with an inflation parameter of 2.0 and a minimum bit-score ratio of 0.5. Clusters were classified as core (present in all genomes), accessory (present in multiple but not all genomes), or singleton (unique to one genome). Comparative analysis identified gene clusters uniquely shared among S. cerevisiae or S. boulardii isolates. Pairwise average nucleotide identity (ANI) was computed using FastANI47 to assess overall genomic similarity.

Whole genome synteny and structural variation

Whole-genome synteny was assessed by aligning the eight yeast assemblies to the S. cerevisiae S288C reference with Minimap2 v2.17, which produces base-level SAM alignments for structural analyses. The resulting SAM files were converted to coordinate-sorted BAM format with Samtools v1.10, for downstream processing. To enable chromosome-scale comparison between strains, we generated 16-chromosome pseudo-genomes for each query with Chroder (bundled with SyRI), which concatenates contigs according to their reference coordinates, and realigned these pseudo-chromosomes to the reference with the same Minimap2 settings. Syntenic blocks and structural rearrangements (inversions, translocations, duplications and indels) were identified with SyRI v1.548 executed on the sorted BAMs. Finally, synteny maps for all strains were visualised with Plotsr v0.2which renders a vertical plot of reference chromosomes against each pseudo-genome, coloring collinear blocks and exposing structural breakpoints. All tools were run with default parameters.

Targeted SNP calling for five S. boulardii isolates (anvi’o, reference-based)

Oxford Nanopore (ONT) FASTQ reads from five S. boulardii isolates were mapped to the S. boulardii CNCM I-745 reference assembly using minimap2 map-ont, and alignments were converted to sorted/indexed BAM files with samtools. An anvi’o contigs database was generated from the CNCM I-745 FASTA (anvi-gen-contigs-database) and annotated with HMM profiles (anvi-run-hmms). Each BAM was profiled against the reference contigs database using anvi-profile with --min-coverage-for-variability 10 (and --cluster-contigs), profiles were merged with anvi-merge, and nucleotide-level variability was exported with anvi-gen-variability-profile --engine NT restricted to the five isolate sample names.

High-confidence SNVs were defined by filtering the exported variability table using anvi’o per-sample fields: sites were retained only if (i) the minimum per-sample coverage across all five isolates at the site was ≥30, (ii) consensus clarity was met in ≥4/5 isolates, defined as departure_from_consensus ≤0.2, and (iii) strong non-reference evidence existed in ≥1 isolate, defined as consensus ≠ reference and departure_from_reference ≥0.8 (with that isolate’s coverage also ≥30).

Growth assessments

S. boulardii isolates and baker’s and laboratory S. cerevisiae strains were inoculated at OD600 0.1 in 200 μL media in CELLSTAR 96-well microtiter plates with a Breathe-Easy sealing membrane. OD600 was measured using a bench-top spectrophotometer. Growth was monitored at 30°C, 37°C, or 42°C in DMM or YPD, respectively. For acid tolerance assay, DMM and YPD were adjusted to pH 1.5, 2.5, 3.5, or 4.5 by dropwise addition of HCl prior to inculcation. Anaerobic conditions were established by streaking and preculture strains in sealed containers with AnaeroGen pouches followed by incubation in microtiter plates in a Coy Anaerobic Chamber (gas mixture, 95% N2 and 5% H2). All media used for anaerobic growth was as pre-reduced for 48 h. Real-time OD600 was measured every 15 min for 48 h using a BioTek Synergy H1 microplate reader. All experiments were performed using three independent biological replicates.

Growth rate estimation

OD600 time-series data were collected from microplate cultures and processed in R and converted to natural logarithms after filtering out non-positive values. For each condition (strain × medium × temperature × oxygen × replicate), we estimated the maximum specific growth rate (μ_max) using a rolling-window linear regression approach. Briefly, linear models of log(OD600) versus time (in hours) were fitted across all contiguous windows of 4–5 data points. For each window, the slope of the regression (h−1) and the corresponding coefficient of determination (R2) were recorded. The window yielding the highest slope (with R2 used as a tie-breaker) was selected as the best estimate of exponential growth. The corresponding μ_max (h−1) and doubling time (dt = ln(2)/μ_max, reported in both hours and minutes) were calculated.

Viability assessments

Pre-cultures were diluted to OD600 of 10 in a final volume of 500 μL using artificial intestinal fluid (pH 6.8), artificial colonic fluid (pH 7.0), artificial gastric fluid (pH 1.5), or 0.1 M H202 prepared in YPD and incubated at 37°C with shaking. At 0, 3, 6, 24, and 48 h, 10 μL of culture was harvested and diluted in 90 μL filtered PBS in a clear-bottom microplate, to achieve an approximate OD600 of 1. Cell viability was assessed using the Yeast Live-or-Dye Fixable Live/Dead Staining Kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Samples were analyzed using a Novocyte Quanteon. Forward scatter (FSC) and side scatter (SSC) were measured with a gain of 400; non-viable cells were detected through PE detection channel with a 586 nm filter (yellow laser) and a gain of 591. A threshold of 6000 was used, and data acquisition continued until 30 μL of the sample was injected. Gates for dead cells identification were defined using heat-killed controls (100°C for 10 min) treated under identical exposure conditions. All experiments were performed using three independent biological replicates.

HPLC analysis of yeast metabolites

Strains were cultured in biological triplicate by diluting overnight cultures to OD600 0.1 in either 500 μL of DMM or YPD in 96 deep-well plates, or 25 mL of DMM in 250 mL baffled shake flask. Aerobic cultures were incubated at 37°C under 250 rpm shaking. Anaerobic cultures were incubated in a sealed container with AnaeroGen pouches at 37°C. Yeast supernatants were obtained by centrifuging cultures at 4,000 × g for 10 min at 8, 24, 48, 72, and 120 h corresponding to early exponential, late exponential, and stationary phases, to capture dynamic changes in metabolite production over time. The supernatants were then stored at −20 °C until further analysis.

(D+)-Glucose monohydrate, ethanol absolute, sodium acetate, sodium L-lactate, and sodium succinate dibasic were quantified by high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) using a Dionex Ultimate 3000 HPLC system with analysis software Chromeleon Metabolites were separated on Bio-Rad Aminex HPx87 column with 5 mM H2SO4 as an eluent at a flow rate of 0.6 mL/min. Column temperature was maintained at 30°C, and detection was performed using a refractive index detector. The limit of detection for all metabolites was 0.1 g/L.

Mammalian cell culture maintenance

The human intestinal epithelial cell line HT-29 and the HT29-Lucia AhR (aryl hydrocarbon receptor) reporter cell line were cultured in McCoy’s 5A (Modified) medium (supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin. Cells were maintained in a humidified incubator at 37°C with 5% CO2.

Human THP-1 monocytes were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium 10% FBS and 1% penicillin–streptomycin and incubated at 37°C with 5% CO2. For macrophage differentiation, cells were seeded at appropriate density and treated with 20 μM phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA) for 24 h, followed by washing with serum-free RPMI-1640 prior stimulation.

HT29-Lucia AhR reporter cells were thawed and passaged twice in the absence of the selection antibiotic, then maintained in compete McCoy’s 5A medium supplemented with 100 μg/mL Zeocin and 1% penicillin–streptomycin. Cells were harvested prior to reaching 90% confluency.

THP-1-Lucia NF-κB (Nuclear factor kappa B) cells were maintained in suspension and were not differentiated with PMA prior to use. Cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 media containing 1 μg/mL puromycin, 10% FBS, and 0.05 mM 2-Mercaptoethanol.

All cell lines were passaged every 2–3 days and used for experiments within 10 passages. All experiments were performed using three to five biological replicates.

Preparation of yeast supernatant

For the preparation of yeast supernatants, strains were diluted to an initial OD600 of 0.1 in 25 mL of DMM in 250 mL shake flasks and incubated at 37°C with shaking at 250 rpm for 24 h. Cultures were prepared in duplicate and pooled at the end of the incubation period. The final OD600 was measured using a bench-top spectrophotometer to ensure similar biomass formulation across strains. Cultures were then centrifuged at 4,000 × g for 10 min, and the resulting supernatants were sterile-filtered through 0.22 μm syringe filters. Filtered supernatants were aliquoted and stored at −20 °C and were thawed only once prior to use. Supernatants were applied at a final concentration of 10% (v/v) in host-cell assays, selected to balance physiological relevance and cell viability based on prior studies.19 Supernatants were not normalised to individual metabolite concentrations, as the objective was to assess integrated strain-specific secretomes produced under identical growth conditions.

LPS and TNFα stimulation of mammalian cells

For HT-29 stimulation, 10,000 cells were seeded in 100 μL complete McCoy’s 5A medium in CELLSTAR 96-well microtiter plate and incubated for 48 h to reach 90% confluency. Subsequently, the media were replaced with 200 μL of fresh McCoy’s 5A media containing 1 μg/mL lipopolysaccharides (LPS from Escherichia coli O111:B4) or 10 ng/mL tumor necrosis factor-α (TNFα), supplemented with 10% (v/v) yeast supernatant. Control groups received 10% (v/v) of DMM. Supernatants were collected at 6- or 24-h post-stimulation.

For THP-1 stimulation, 50,000 cells were seeded in 100 μL complete RPMI-1640 medium with 5 μg/mL PMA in CELLSTAR 96-well microtiter plate and incubated until a 90% confluent monolayer formed. Subsequently, the cells were replaced with 200 μL of fresh RPMI-1640 media containing 1 μg/mL LPS or 10 ng/mL TNFα and 10% (v/v) yeast supernatant. Control groups received 10% (v/v) of DMM. Supernatants were collected at 24-h post-stimulation.

Cytokine quantification by ELISA

Cytokine concentrations in the supernatants from stimulated HT-29 and THP-1 cells were quantified by ELISA. Interleukin-8 (IL-8) was measured using the DuoSet ELISA kit, according to the manufacturer’s protocol. TNFα was quantified using the TNFα ELISA kit. Absorption at 450 nm was measured using a BioTek Synergy H1 microplate reader.

NF-κB activation reporter assay in THP1 NF-kB-Luc2 cells

THP1 NF-kB-Luc2 reporter cells were seeded at 100,000 cells per well in 200 μL complete RPMI-1640 medium in CELLSTAR 96-well microtiter plates. Cells were stimulated with 100 ng/mL LPS or 10 ng/mL TNFα and 10% (v/v) yeast supernatant. Control groups received 10% (v/v) of DMM. After 6 h incubation at 37°C with 5% CO2, 100 μL of samples from each of the wells were transferred to a white 96-well microtiter plate and 100 μL of the Bright-Glo Luciferase Assay reagent, prepared according to the manufacturer’s protocol, was added to the wells. Luminescence was immediately measured on a BioTek Synergy H1 microplate reader.

AhR activation reporter assay in HT29-Lucia cell

HT29-Lucia AhR reporter cells were seeded at 50,000 cells per well in 180 μL medium in CELLSTAR 96-well microtiter plates. After adherence, 20 μL of yeast supernatant, control DMM, or 20 μM 6-formylindolo[3,2-b]carbazole (FICZ) was added per well. Cells were incubated for 48 h at 37°C. Following incubation, 20 μL of cell culture supernatant was transferred to a black 96-well plate with optical bottom. Luminescence was detected by adding 50 μL QUANTI-Luc and immediately measured using a BioTek Synergy H1 plate reader.

TEER analysis of differentiated Caco-2 monolayers co-cultured with live S. boulardii

Caco-2 cells were seeded at 20,000 cells per well on 24-well Transwell inserts (0.4 μm pore size; 0.33 cm2 PET membrane; cellQART) and differentiated for 19–21 days in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin–streptomycin. Media were replaced every other day during differentiation. On the day of the assay, the apical compartment was refreshed with 150 μL of live S. boulardii suspension (1×108 cells/mL; OD600 = 1) in DMEM with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin–streptomycin; the basolateral chamber received 600 μL of cell free matching medium. Monolayers with baseline TEER values deviating by more than 25% from the plate average were excluded from analysis to ensure comparable barrier integrity at assay start. Transepithelial electrical resistance (TEER) was recorded at multiple time points over 24 h using a Millicell ERS-2 voltohmmeter with an STX-04 electrode. Percentage change in TEER at each time point was calculated relative to the 0h post-treatment baseline. The area under the curve (AUC) was determined over the 24-h period to quantify the barrier integrity changes. All conditions were tested using four biological replicates; in cases where monolayers were excluded based on baseline TEER criteria, analyses were performed on the remaining replicates (minimum n = 3).

Quantification and statistical analysis

All experiments were performed with three to five biological replicates and compiled from one to two independent experimental runs. Exact n values are reported in figure legends and supplementary data. No a priori power calculations were performed. All statistical analysis was performed using Graph Pad Prism software (Graph Pad Software Inc.) or RStudio version 4.1.0, utilising the rstatix and DescTools packages. Data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM).

Statistical analyses were conducted using one-way ANOVA followed by either Dunnett’s or Tukey’s post hoc tests, as appropriate, to account for multiple comparisons. A p value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant.
