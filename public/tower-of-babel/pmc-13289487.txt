Missed duodenal tumors at upper endoscopy: a five year retrospective cohort study at West China hospital (2019–2023)
Authors: Yaxin Li, Yuan Li, Zhujun Wu, Linjie Guo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDuodenal tumors constitute 35%-55% of small bowel neoplasms, yet the misdiagnosis rate remains substantial. This study investigated the clinical features of duodenal tumors missed during endoscopic examination.MethodsThis retrospective cohort analysis included patients who were diagnosed with duodenal tumors between 2019 and 2023 at West China Hospital. Demographic data, tumor characteristics, endoscopic findings, and missed diagnosis records were extracted from electronic medical records and telephone follow-ups. The exploratory analysis identified the reasons for the missed diagnosis during endoscopy.ResultsAmong the 307 enrolled patients with duodenal tumors, 36 patients (34 with adenocarcinomas and 2 with neuroendocrine tumors) had undergone previous endoscopic examinations without a definitive diagnosis, yielding a missed detection rate of 11.7%. In all missed cases, the mean number of endoscopic procedures performed prior to definitive diagnosis was 2 (range: 1–8), with an average interval of 11.1 months (range: 3–36 months). The anatomical distribution of missed lesions included the duodenal papilla (44.4%, 16/36), duodenal bulb (25.0%, 9/36), bulb-descending junction (5.6%, 2/36), descending (13.9%, 5/36) and horizontal (5.6%, 2/36) parts. The causes of missed diagnoses included exposure errors (n = 20), judgment errors (n = 5), biopsy errors (n = 8), and unclassified errors (n = 3). The difference in the pattern of missed duodenal tumors between tertiary hospitals and non-tertiary hospitals was not statistically significant (P > 0.05).ConclusionThe missed diagnosis rate of duodenal tumors is high during endoscopic examination. Comprehensive endoscopic observation and improved detection awareness of duodenal tumors are essential in both tertiary and nontertiary hospitals.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12876-026-04883-6.Keywords: Missed diagnosis, Upper gastrointestinal endoscopy, Duodenal tumors, Early diagnosis

Background

Duodenal tumors constitute a rare subgroup of gastrointestinal neoplasms, including adenocarcinomas, squamous cell carcinomas, stromal tumors, neuroendocrine tumors, and lymphomas. They account for approximately 0.3% of all gastrointestinal malignancies [1] and 35–55% of small intestinal tumors [2]. These lesions can develop in any anatomical subdivision of the duodenum. The diagnosis of duodenal tumors is challenging because of the complex anatomical structure of the duodenum and the often occult nature of these tumors. Initial diagnostic delays often lead to complex surgical interventions (e.g., pancreaticoduodenectomy) with increased morbidity risks. Early diagnosis is crucial for improving patient prognosis. Endoscopic examinations constitute the cornerstone of gastrointestinal tumor diagnosis [3]. Although endoscopic techniques have significantly advanced in detecting early gastrointestinal tumors, the misdiagnosis rate remains high, posing a major challenge for endoscopic quality control. Several studies have examined the missed diagnosis rates of gastrointestinal tumors. The reported misdiagnosis rate for esophageal cancer ranges from 5.0% to 14.3% [4–6]. For early gastric cancer, the misdiagnosis rate has been reported to be approximately 22.8% [7], whereas for advanced gastric cancer, the reported rate ranges from 4.6% to 25.8% [8, 9]. However, owing to the relatively low incidence of duodenal tumors and the limited research focus, few studies have focused specifically on missed diagnoses of duodenal tumors.

This retrospective cohort study investigated the clinical features of missed diagnoses of duodenal tumors to offer clinicians diagnostic insights and improve the early detection rates of duodenal neoplasms.

Methods

Study design and data sources

This retrospective cohort study was conducted at West China Hospital of Sichuan University and approved by the hospital’s Ethics Committee. Owing to its retrospective nature, the requirement for informed consent was waived by the Ethics Committee. This study was designed and reported in accordance with the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) guidelines to ensure transparency and completeness in reporting observational research.

Study population

This retrospective cohort study included patients who met the following inclusion criteria: (1) were diagnosed with duodenal tumors via histopathology at West China Hospital between 2019 and 2023; and (2) had undergone upper gastrointestinal endoscopy performed within three years before diagnosis. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) had a history of prior gastrointestinal surgery altering the duodenal anatomy; and (2) had recurrent duodenal tumors. (3) Patients whose medical records were incomplete.

A total of 307 histologically confirmed duodenal tumor cases were included and categorized into two groups:

Correctly detected tumor (CDT) cohort: 271 patients (88.3%) with concordant endoscopic and pathological findings.

Correctly detected tumor (CDT) cohort: 271 patients (88.3%) with concordant endoscopic and pathological findings.

Missed duodenal tumor (MDT) cohort: 36 patients (11.7%) meeting the predefined missed diagnosis criteria.

Missed duodenal tumor (MDT) cohort: 36 patients (11.7%) meeting the predefined missed diagnosis criteria.

Definitions

On the basis of established criteria in previous studies, missed duodenal tumors (MDTs) were defined as cases in which duodenal neoplasms were not identified during upper gastrointestinal endoscopy conducted between 3 and 36 months before the definitive diagnosis. Endoscopic examinations performed within three months before diagnosis were excluded, as these procedures were likely part of diagnostic confirmation rather than routine screening or early detection. Similarly, examinations conducted more than 36 months prior to diagnosis were not considered, as lesions detected beyond this timeframe were presumed to have newly developed rather than been previously missed. Conversely, correctly detected tumors (CDTs) were defined as cases in which duodenal tumors were pathologically confirmed without prior negative endoscopic findings. The missed diagnosis rate was calculated by dividing the number of MDT cases by the total number of eligible patients meeting the inclusion criteria, expressed as a percentage.

We meticulously reviewed all upper gastrointestinal endoscopic reports and corresponding imaging data to identify the causes of missed duodenal tumors, including the following:

Exposure errors: Failure to detect lesions due to inadequate visualization of the duodenal mucosa during endoscopic examination.

Judgment errors: Lesions visible in archived endoscopic images but not initially diagnosed as neoplastic.

Biopsy errors: Lesions documented, reported, and biopsied during endoscopy but with non-neoplastic pathological results.

Biopsy errors: Lesions documented, reported, and biopsied during endoscopy but with non-neoplastic pathological results.

Unclassified errors: Cases where the reason for misdiagnosis was indeterminate owing to a lack of definitive endoscopic and pathological reports.

Additional definitions:

Smoking: Defined as current tobacco use or cessation within the past year.

Drinking: Defined as regular alcohol consumption or cessation within the past year.

The following data were collected for analysis:

Demographic information: Age and sex.

All the data were cross-checked for completeness and accuracy by two independent researchers to minimize the risk of data entry errors.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are described as the means with standard deviations (SDs), supplemented by medians and interquartile ranges (IQRs) for non-normally distributed data. Categorical variables are presented as frequencies (%) and were compared via chi-square tests to delineate baseline characteristics. Patients with missing data were analyzed as an independent subgroup to mitigate selection bias. Given the exploratory nature of this study, primary emphasis was placed on absolute frequency distributions and proportional composition analyses of observational indicators.

All the statistical calculations were performed via IBM SPSS Statistics version 24.0.

Results

Baseline characteristics

This study included 307 patients with pathologically confirmed duodenal tumors, who were classified into the correctly detected tumor (CDT) group (n = 271, 88.3%) and the missed duodenal tumor (MDT) group (n = 36, 11.7%) (Fig. 1). The overall missed diagnosis rate was 11.7% (36/307). A prior diagnosis of familial adenomatous polyposis (FAP) was reported in one patient (0.33%), whereas 20 patients (6.51%) had a history of other malignancies, including colorectal cancer (n = 6), gastric cancer (n = 3), uterine malignancies (n = 3), lung cancer (n = 2), and thyroid cancer (n = 2), and one case each of cardia cancer, breast cancer, kidney cancer, and oral cancer. A significantly greater proportion of patients in the MDT group than in the CDT group had a family history of malignancies (25.0% vs. 11.1%, P = 0.018). The most common indication for endoscopy in both groups was symptom-driven investigation, with approximately 35% of patients presenting with abdominal pain, 22% with abdominal distension, around 10% exhibiting varying degrees of jaundice, and the remainder presenting with other symptoms, including melena, nausea, or vomiting, etc. The degree of tumor differentiation and average number of biopsy specimens did not differ significantly between the MDT group and CDT group. Among the MDT cases, information about operator seniority could be determined in 9 cases. Among which, 7 cases were performed by senior endoscopists (more than 30 years of expertise in endoscopic operations), and one case each by an intermediate (10–20 years expertise in endoscopic operations) and a junior endoscopists (less than 10 years expertise in endoscopic operations). Comparison of missed duodenal tumors at different hospitals

Among the MDT cases, 28 (77.8%) were initially evaluated at tertiary hospitals, whereas 8 cases (22.2%) were from non-tertiary hospitals (Table 3). On average, patients underwent two endoscopic examinations before receiving a definitive diagnosis. There were no significant differences between patients from tertiary hospitals and non-tertiary hospitals in terms of the misdiagnosis pattern of duodenal tumors. However, regarding the anatomical sites of MDTs, cases in tertiary hospitals were more frequently missed at the duodenal papilla and duodenal bulb, whereas in non-tertiary hospitals, MDTs were predominant in the descending part of the duodenum and the duodenal papilla (P = 0.021).

Discussion

Although duodenal tumors are relatively rare, their incidence is increasing, particularly among younger individuals. Epidemiological studies from developed Western countries report an incidence ranging from 3.7 to 5.4 per million person-years [10–13]. Data from the National Cancer Registry of Japan indicate a crude incidence rate of 23.7 cases per million population in 2016 [14]. Histologically, most duodenal tumors are adenocarcinomas, with approximately 80% occurring in the descending part or ampullary region [15, 16]. Other subtypes include gastrointestinal stromal tumors (GISTs) and neuroendocrine tumors (NETs), etc. Although our study included only one patient with familial adenomatous polyposis (FAP), no known risk factors for nonampullary duodenal cancer other than FAP were identified [2]. The literature suggests that FAP increases the lifetime risk of duodenal cancer to approximately 15%-20% [17, 18], underscoring the importance of comprehensive endoscopic surveillance in high-risk populations. In addition to genetic syndromes, accumulating evidence indicates that duodenal tumors often co-occur with other malignancies. Approximately 7%-21% of duodenal tumor patients are diagnosed concurrently with colorectal cancer [19, 20], and the risk of small intestinal cancer is 7.1- to 9.0-fold greater in colorectal cancer patients than in those with other primary malignancies [21]. These associations may be driven by shared mutations in the KRAS, P53, and MMR genes [22, 23]. Even in non-FAP populations, patients with duodenal tumors may develop secondary gastrointestinal malignancies, including gastric cancer [24] and pancreaticobiliary cancer. This multiorgan carcinogenic pattern may be attributed to shared environmental exposures or field cancerization in mucosal stem cells. Therefore, patients with a history of gastrointestinal malignancies should undergo closer surveillance for synchronous or metachronous duodenal tumors. These findings collectively emphasize the necessity of comprehensive oncologic screening in patients diagnosed with duodenal cancer.

In this retrospective cohort study, we assessed the missed diagnosis rate of duodenal tumors and its influencing factors. The overall rate was 11.7%, which is comparable to previously reported missed diagnosis rates of 4.4%–34% for upper gastrointestinal tumors [25–29].

However, most prior studies have focused on esophageal and gastric cancers, with limited data available for duodenal tumors. The complex anatomy and concealed nature of duodenal tumors pose significant challenges for endoscopic detection. As duodenal neoplasms are relatively uncommon, endoscopic examinations often focus more on esophageal and gastric lesions, potentially leading to incomplete duodenal evaluation. In our study, all patients with missed diagnoses underwent an average of two endoscopic examinations before a definitive diagnosis was established, primarily due to inadequate lesion exposure. Lesions in the duodenal papilla are particularly prone to being obscured by incomplete exposure or limited visual fields. Without appropriate patient positioning, repeated flushing, or careful observation, these lesions can be overlooked. Furthermore, some endoscopists may have low sensitivity to early manifestations of duodenal tumors and fail to recognize subtle mucosal abnormalities or small lesions. Single biopsies may not provide representative tissue samples, increasing the risk of missed diagnoses. Additionally, the horizontal and ascending parts of the duodenum are anatomically hidden and often difficult to reach, making them blind spots in endoscopic examination and increasing diagnostic challenges.

Missed diagnoses of duodenal tumors arise from multiple factors. To reduce this risk, endoscopists should remain highly vigilant and systematically examine all duodenal regions, with a particular focus on high-risk areas such as the duodenal papilla and descending part. The use of advanced imaging technologies, including magnifying endoscopy, narrow-band imaging (NBI), and endoscopic ultrasound (EUS), enables endoscopists to more accurately characterize suspicious lesions, thereby reducing the risk of misdiagnosis. Additionally, enhancing physician training, standardizing examination protocols, increasing biopsy sampling when necessary, and implementing stricter screening for high-risk populations can aid in early detection and reduce missed diagnoses.

This study has several limitations that warrant cautious interpretation of the results. First, as a single-center retrospective study, selection bias may affect the reliability of our conclusions. Second, the lack of key endoscopic procedural details, such as endoscopist experience, withdrawal time, and sedation use, limits our understanding of the mechanisms underlying missed diagnoses. Third, the subjective classification of error types (exposure, judgment, and biopsy errors) without standardized diagnostic criteria or blinded expert review introduces a risk of misclassification bias. Fourth, reliance on telephone follow-ups for historical endoscopic data introduces recall bias, particularly regarding the number and timing of prior procedures. Finally, the small sample size (n = 36 missed cases) precluded meaningful subgroup analyses and robust multivariable logistic regression to identify the causes of misdiagnosis, including analyses of the impact of tumor location on detection rates and comparisons between tertiary and non-tertiary hospitals, as indicated by the lack of statistical significance (P > 0.05). Thus, future multicenter large-scale prospective studies incorporating standardized endoscopic documentation are needed to further validate and refine these findings.

Conclusion

This study revealed a significant missed diagnosis rate of 11.7% for duodenal tumors during endoscopic examinations. Enhancing endoscopist training, standardizing examination protocols, and utilizing advanced technologies are necessary for high-quality examinations.
