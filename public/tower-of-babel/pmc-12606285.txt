Two opposing yet complementary ocular dominance plasticities: thalamus strengthens the weak channel while higher cortex listens to the strong signal
Authors: Yazhu Qian, Zhouyuan Sun, Yizhi Wang, Yige Gao, Chencan Qian, Jiawei Zhou, Peng Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Neural mechanisms of ocular dominance plasticity in the adult brain remain elusive. Using high-resolution functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) at 7 T, we investigated ocular dominance plasticity in the visual thalamus and extrastriate visual cortices in response to monocular contrast deprivation (MCD) in human adults. Short-term (3 hours) MCD enhanced the sensitivity of the deprived eye (DE) relative to the non-deprived eye (NDE) in the lateral geniculate nucleus and the ventrolateral pulvinar of the thalamus. Compared to the NDE, the DE became more sensitive in contrast detection and more dominant in binocular combination. On the other hand, MCD reduced DE relative to NDE sensitivity in extrastriate cortices, and DE relative to NDE performance in 3-D shape perception. These findings demonstrate that a homeostatic mechanism in the visual thalamus concurrently operates with a Hebbian-like mechanism in extrastriate cortices to rapidly and adaptively adjust interocular balance to disrupted binocular input.

Introduction

Our brain has the remarkable ability to adapt its function and structure to the constantly changing environment. Monocular deprivation (MD) is a classic paradigm that has been used extensively to study experience-dependent neural plasticity in the visual system. Depriving one eye’s input early in life can lead to unilateral amblyopia1, resulting in profound functional deficits2–5, and structural abnormalities in the early visual areas6–8. Clinically, long-term patching of the fellow eye can fully or partially restore visual acuity of the amblyopic eye9. However, recent laboratory evidence suggests that short-term patching strengthens the patched eye instead10–12. How can these two disparate findings be reconciled?

One hypothesis is that there are two opposing mechanisms operating at different levels of the visual processing hierarchy on different time scales: a rapid homeostatic mechanism at early stages that increases the gain of the deprived eye (DE) over the nondeprived eye (NDE), and a slower, Hebbian-like or learning-type mechanism at higher levels that shifts ocular dominance toward the NDE. In support of this, behavioral evidence suggests a turning point in the direction of ocular dominance change at around 5 h of MD, shifting from DE- to NDE-biased direction13. Moreover, Hebbian-like learning effect can also occur rapidly within a single session, especially for perceptual tasks that engage high-level visual processing14. However, the neural mechanisms underlying these opposing effects remain to be elucidated. While the effect of short-term MD has been extensively studied in the primary visual cortex (V1)15–18, much less is known about the changes in subcortical and extrastriate visual areas.

In the lateral geniculate nucleus (LGN) of the thalamus, inputs from the two eyes are segregated into six alternating layers of monocular neurons. The two ventral layers and the four dorsal layers contain magnocellular (M) and parvocellular (P) cells, respectively. In addition to driving input from the retina, the LGN also receives modulatory input from V119,20, the thalamic reticular nucleus21, and the brainstem22. The dendrites of certain interneurons extend to the interlaminate zone and adjacent ocular layers23–25, which may support binocular inhibition within the LGN26–30. Electrophysiological recordings in the macaque LGN have shown strong contrast adaptation in the M cells31, partly inherited from parasol (M-type) ganglion cells on the retina. A homeostatic effect of MD has also been observed in LGN activity in rodents32, suggesting that a similar mechanism might operate in primates. However, a recent fMRI study reported a significant effect of short-term MD in the ventral pulvinar, but not in the LGN33. Given the small size and low signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) of the LGN, the negative result might reflect a lack of sensitivity rather than a true absence of effect. Moreover, it remains unclear whether the deprivation effect mainly occurs in the M or P subdivisions of the visual thalamus. Similar to the LGN, the ventral pulvinar is also subdivided into an M-biased ventromedial subdivision and P-biased ventrolateral subdivision, which are more strongly connected with the dorsal and ventral streams, respectively34,35. In the superior colliculus (SC) of macaques, inputs from the two eyes are segregated into alternating columns and strata, although neurons in these structures respond robustly to the stimulation of either eye36. While MD early in life can disrupt corticotectal projections5,37, it remains unknown whether short-term MD can influence SC activity in adults.

In cortical visual areas, Binda et al. observed enhanced BOLD responses to high spatial frequency broadband stimuli in the DE relative to the NDE, both in V1 and in extrastriate cortices of the ventral stream10. Spindle oscillations in frontal EEG electrodes during sleep can also predict the consolidation of MD effects38. While these studies suggest the involvement of the higher cortex in ocular dominance plasticity induced by short-term MD, direct evidence of Hebbian-like strengthening of the NDE is still lacking. Moreover, previous investigations have primarily focused on the influence of MD on low-level perception closely linked to V1, such as contrast detection, binocular combination, and binocular rivalry12,16,39. How MD influences higher-level perception associated with the higher cortex remains unclear.

In this study, we aim to investigate how short-term monocular contrast deprivation (MCD) influences ocular dominance at different levels of the visual hierarchy. In experiment 1 (Exp. 1), using 7T fMRI at 1.2-mm isotropic resolution, we examined the effects of 2.5-h MCD on ocular dominance of BOLD responses to high contrast checkerboards in subcortical and cortical visual areas. Here, a high contrast stimulus was intentionally used to enhance the SNR, thereby increasing sensitivity to detect potential deprivation effects in the LGN. Perceptual effects on contrast detection and binocular combination were also assessed by psychophysical methods. In experiment 2 (Exp. 2), before and after 2.5-h MCD, steady-state visually evoked potentials to low- and high-contrast checkerboards were recorded from the occipital pole and retina, to study whether the absence of deprivation effect in V1 in Exp. 1 is a result of response saturation under high contrast condition, and a potential effect of contrast deprivation on the neural activity of retinal ganglion cells. Finally, in experiment 3 (Exp. 3), to reveal the perceptual changes associated with the ocular dominance shift towards NDE in extrastriate visual cortices found in experiment 1, and to investigate whether it could occur at a shorter time scale as suggested by the previous study14, we examined the influence of 1-h MCD on 3-D shape perception.

In the psychophysical tests, contrast detection threshold was first measured for each eye, followed by an assessment of ocular dominance using a binocular phase combination task. Given that the deprivation effect decays exponentially40, we used the method of adjustment to reduce the measurement time. In the monocular contrast detection task (Fig. 1b, left), a sub-threshold sinewave grating was presented monocularly, either to the left or to the right side of fixation. Participants increased the grating contrast until the stimulus was just visible and then reported its location. The contrast detection threshold was defined as the final contrast level. In the binocular phase combination task (Fig. 1b, right), two horizonal sinewave gratings with 22.5° of phase shift above and below the midline were presented dichoptically, participants adjusted the horizontal black line to align with the center of the dark band in the binocularly fused grating. Despite the limited number of trials, psychophysical measurements were highly consistent across repetitions (see Supplementary Fig. 1 for the results of a representative participant).

Each fMRI session consists of two runs of the ocular sensitivity measure and four runs of the ocular bias localizer. In the ocular sensitivity measure (Fig. 1c, left), an 80% contrast sine-wave checkerboard flicker was presented to one or both eyes to measure ocular sensitivity to visual stimuli in subcortical and cortical visual areas. In the ocular bias localizer (Fig. 1c, right), a full contrast square-wave checkerboard flicker was presented alternatively to the two eyes to measure the ocular bias of voxels in the early visual areas. Due to the rapid decay of deprivation effects, participants first performed two runs of ocular sensitivity measure (about 15 min), followed by a T1-w anatomical scan (about 7 min), and then four runs of ocular bias localizer (about 25 min). The ocular bias localizer was scanned in both pre- and post-test sessions to avoid alignment errors from inter-session registration.

Figure 3b shows the LGN responses to monocular and binocular stimuli in the ocular sensitivity measure before (pre) and after (post) 2.5-h MCD. In the monocular stimulus conditions, DE and NDE responses were defined as the ROI-averaged responses of the most visually responsive voxels in the ocular bias localizer to DE and NDE stimuli, respectively. In the binocular stimulus condition, DE- and NDE-biased responses were defined as the ROI-averaged responses in DE- and NDE-biased voxels in the localizer, respectively.

We first performed an omnibus F test in a three-way rm ANOVA with stimulus (monocular/binocular), eye (DE/NDE), and session (Pre/Post) as within-subject factors, and subject as a random effect. The result showed a highly significant interaction between eye and session (F(1,14) = 18.33, p < 0.001, ηp2 = 0.567), which strongly supports an eye-specific deprivation effect in the LGN. We then performed two-way ANOVAs separately for the monocular and binocular stimulus conditions. The eye × session interaction was significant in both the monocular (F(1,14) = 7.09, p = 0.019, ηp2 = 0.336), and binocular (F(1,14) = 8.94, p = 0.009, ηp2 = 0.390) conditions. In the binocular condition, post-hoc t-test also revealed a significant decrease for the NDE-biased response (t(14) = 2.52, p = 0.025, Cohen’s d = 0.650).

To characterize the change in ocular dominance and to account for the fatigue effect of overall signal reduction, we calculated the ratio between DE and NDE responses (DE/NDE ratio) in the pre- and post-test sessions11,44–46. MCD significantly enhanced DE/NDE ratio in both monocular and binocular conditions (Fig. 3c, p = 0.004 and 0.016, respectively). Although the DE and NDE were randomly selected from the dominant and non-dominant eyes of participants, DE response was slightly lower than NDE prior to deprivation. To ensure that the observed deprivation effect was not simply a regression to the mean driven by this pre-existing interocular imbalance, we performed a control analysis in which the voxel selection threshold for the NDE was relaxed (more voxels with weaker sensitivity were selected), such that the pre-deprivation responses of the two eyes were matched in amplitude (see Methods for details). A significant deprivation effect was still observed on DE/NDE ratio (Fig. 3d, p = 0.004 and 0.0005 in monocular and binocular conditions, respectively).

Our results do not agree with the recent study by ref. 33, who reported no significant effect of MD on LGN responses to monocular stimuli. We believe the main reason underlying this discrepancy is the difference in ROI definition: the previous study averaged BOLD signals from a large ROI in the MNI space (e.g., 200 mm3, compared to the average size of human LGNs of about 120 mm3,5,47,48), while the current study selected voxels with the highest sensitivity within individually defined anatomical ROIs in the native space. Give the small size and low SNR of the LGN, a coarse ROI in standard space may lack the sensitivity needed to detect the deprivation effect. In support of this, using the whole anatomical ROI, we also found no significant deprivation effect in LGN responses to monocular stimuli (eye × session interaction: F(1,14) = 0.99, p = 0.335, ηp2 = 0.066). We further evaluated the influence of visual sensitivity and ocular bias of voxels on the deprivation effect. In the monocular stimulus conditions (see Supplementary Fig. 4, left), deprivation effects in the LGN (see Eq. 1 in “Methods”) were significantly stronger in voxels with strong (most responsive voxels as in Fig. 3b) compared to those with weak (the rest of voxels in the anatomical ROI) visual responsiveness (t(14) = 2.6, p = 0.02, Cohen’s d = 0.672). In the binocular condition (see Supplementary Fig. 4, right), deprivation effects in the LGN were significantly stronger in voxels with strong (ocular bias voxels) compared to those with weak (voxels without significant ocular bias) ocular bias (t(14) = 2.95, p = 0.01, Cohen’s d = 0.761).

Given the small size of the LGN, we defined ROIs by the ocular bias localizer within each session to avoid alignment errors from inter-session registration. To demonstrate the influence of inter-session registration on results, we defined the same ROI for pre- and post-test using localizers from both sessions (after careful inter-session registration). Although a significant eye × session interaction can still be observed (F(1,14) = 7.80, p = 0.014, ηp2 = 0.358), the effect size is weaker compared to the results using ROIs defined by the localizer within each session (ηp2 = 0.567). Thus, alignment errors from inter-session registration may influence results, which could be another reason for the null result in the previous study.

MCD enhanced DE relative to NDE sensitivity in V1 to low- but not high-contrast stimuli

For the cortical visual areas, we first examined the effect in V1. Similar to the analysis in the LGN, DE, and NDE responses in the monocular condition were defined as the ROI-averaged responses of visually responsive voxels (significant DE + NDE response in the localizer, see Methods for details) to DE and NDE stimuli, respectively. In the binocular condition, DE- and NDE-biased responses were defined as the ROI-averaged responses of DE- and NDE-biased voxels (voxels with significant DE-NDE responses in the localizer) to the binocular stimulus, respectively.

To test this hypothesis and to investigate whether contrast deprivation can influence neural activity of retinal ganglion cells, we recorded SSVEPs from the occipital pole and retina to 20 and 80% contrast checkerboard flickers before and after 2.5 h of MCD. The amplitude spectrums of SSVEPs at the occipital pole (Oz electrode) showed clear peaks with good SNRs at the 2nd harmonic of stimulus frequency (7.5 Hz), thus we focused our analysis at this frequency. SSVEP amplitude showed a highly significant eye × session interaction in the low-contrast condition (Fig. 6b, c, F(1,11) = 13.53, p = 0.004, ηp2 = 0.552), but not in the high-contrast condition (F(1,11) = 3.83, p = 0.08, ηp2 = 0.258). DE/NDE response ratio exhibited similar results (Fig. 6d, p = 0.001 and 0.051 for low- and high-contrast conditions, respectively). These findings support our interpretation that the absence of the deprivation effect in V1 in the fMRI experiment was a result of response saturation to high contrast stimuli.

To assess whether contrast deprivation affected the neural activity of retinal ganglion cells, we recorded pattern electroretinogram (ERG) from the DE in a subset of participants. SSVEP amplitude showed no significant difference between pre- and post-test sessions, for either the low-contrast (t(6) = 0.249, p = 0.812) or high-contrast condition (t(6) = 0.279, p = 0.79) (see Supplementary Fig. 6). To further test the null hypothesis, we conducted a Bayesian mixed-model ANOVA using SSVEP amplitudes from all trials, with session and contrast as fixed effects, and participant as the random grouping factor. The main effect of session provided strong evidence in favor of the null hypothesis (BF10 = 0.143). The absence of contrast deprivation effects at both the retina and V1 suggests that the observed changes in the LGN likely reflected local gain modulation and binocular inhibition within the nucleus itself.

During MCD, higher cortical areas exclusively received NDE input from V1, while the DE input was suppressed. According to the Hebbian channel re-weighting model of perceptual learning55, performing naturalistic tasks using sensory input from the NDE but not the DE would strengthen the connections between V1 and higher cortex for the NDE channel, while weakening those for the DE channel. To test this hypothesis, we performed a dynamic causal modeling (DCM) analysis to investigate how MCD influenced the effective connectivity between extrastriate visual areas and V156. Given their strong deprivation effect in Exp.1 (Fig. 7a), dorsal visual areas (DVA, including V3ab and LO) were selected as the high-level cortex in this analysis. In the full DCM model (Fig. 7c), V1 receives stimulus-driving input. Fixed connections include the reciprocal connections between V1 and DVA and the self-connections within each area. MCD can modulate the interareal connections for both DE and NDE stimuli. The full DCM model was first estimated individually, and then a parametric empirical Bayesian (PEB) analysis was used for the group-level inference57. Results showed that MCD significantly strengthened the V1-DVA connection for the NDE channel, while weakening the connection for the DE channel.

Finally, to investigate potential interactions between the homeostatic effect in the visual thalamus and the Hebbian-like effect in the extrastriate cortex, we performed a cross-subject correlation analysis between the deprivation effects in subcortical and cortical regions. MCD reduced DE relative to NDE performance in 3-D shape perception

Experiment three aims to investigate the perceptual changes associated with the deprivation effect observed in the extrastriate visual areas (Fig. 7). Given that the deprivation effect was strongest in V3ab and LO (Fig. 7a), we examined how MCD influences the performance of 3-D shape perception, which is closely associated with the functional role of these regions58–60. During deprivation, the dorsal visual areas rely exclusively on monocular cues (e.g., shading, texture) from the NDE for 3D shape perception, while the non-informative DE input is suppressed. Based on this, we hypothesized that the performance of 3-D shape perception would increase for monocular cues presented to the NDE compared to the DE following deprivation. Furthermore, to examine whether Hebbian-like plasticity on high-level perception could occur over a shorter time scale, we tested the effect of just 1 h of MCD on 3-D shape perception.

Experiment 3a investigated the effect of MCD on perceptual grouping of spheres versus cavities, which depends on efficient extraction of 3-D shapes from monocular shading cues61. We hypothesized that enhanced NDE sensitivity relative to the DE in dorsal visual areas would support more efficient 3D shape perception, resulting in better performance on the perceptual grouping task. In experiment 3b, we employed a 3D shape discrimination task that provides a more direct measure of the performance of 3-D shape perception. The visual stimulus was a 3-D object composed of five ellipsoids (Fig. 9b). The 3-D shape of the object was controlled by changing the orientation, position, size, and aspect ratio of the ellipsoids (see Methods for details). In each trial, two 3-D objects were presented sequentially for 200 ms each, separated by an interstimulus interval of 300 ms. The second object was tilted by 15° along the z-axis with respect to the first one. Stimuli were presented to either the DE or the NDE. Participants were instructed to determine whether there was a difference in the 3-D shape of the two objects. The 3-D shape discrimination ability was measured using the d-prime, which showed a significant eye × session interaction (F(1,29) = 5.26, p = 0.029, ηp2 = 0.154). NDE performance increased significantly after deprivation (t(29) = 2.49, p = 0.019, Cohen’s d = 0.454). DE/NDE performance ratio significantly reduced following deprivation (p = 0.018). These findings indicate that following MCD, NDE input supported more accurate 3-D shape perception than input from the DE, consistent with enhanced NDE relative to DE sensitivity in V3ab and LO (Fig. 7a).

Discussion

Using high-resolution 7T fMRI, we investigated the effect of short-term MCD in subcortical and cortical visual areas in human adults. MCD enhanced DE relative to NDE sensitivity in the LGN and the ventrolateral pulvinar of the thalamus, and DE relative to NDE sensitivity in contrast detection and dominance in binocular combination. On the other hand, MCD reduced DE relative to NDE sensitivity in extrastriate visual areas. The effective connectivity from V1 to higher cortex was enhanced for the NDE while reduced for the DE. Consistent with the fMRI results, MCD reduced DE relative to NDE performance in 3-D shape perception. These findings support the existence of two rapid and opposing mechanisms of ocular dominance plasticity that operate concurrently in the visual thalamus and higher cortex.

The primate LGN receives driving input from retina and modulatory input from V1, TRN, and brainstem nuclei, including the SC22. Using a high contrast stimulus, we found no significant effect of contrast deprivation in V1(Fig. 6), retina (see Supplementary Fig. 6), or the SC (Fig. 5). Although neuromodulations from TRN or other brainstem nuclei remains possible, the observed LGN effect should be primarily due to contrast gain modulation and/or binocular inhibition that occurred locally in the LGN. This effect likely reflects a homeostatic mechanism that stabilizes or normalizes LGN activity at the neuronal and network level62,63. Although the population response in the LGN showed enhanced DE relative to NDE sensitivity, it is unlikely that MCD changed the eye preference of single neurons, as the vast majority of neurons in the primate LGN are monocular26. In a recent fMRI study, LGN responses to monocular stimuli showed no significant change after 2-h MCD33. This discrepancy could be due to the differences in ROI definition (whole anatomical ROI vs. most sensitive voxels), and alignment approaches (spatial normalization to standard space vs. within-session registration to independent localizers in native space) (see LGN results for details). Finally, no significant correlation was found between the perceptual and LGN effects. This was likely due to the top-up deprivation in our procedure, causing a difference in the deprivation durations of psychophysical (2.5 h) and fMRI (about 3 h, varied across participants) measurements.

Since our stimuli were high contrast (80%) gratings at a relatively high spatial frequency (2 c.p.d.), BOLD responses in the LGN should primarily reflect activity from the parvocellular subdivision41,64,65. In support of this, the laminar analysis showed that the deprivation effect mainly occurs in the dorsolateral portion of the LGN (Fig. 4), corresponding to the parvocellular subdivision. The ventrolateral or parvocellular portion of the pulvinar also showed DE relative to NDE sensitivity enhancement following deprivation. No significant deprivation effect was found in the ventromedial or magnocellular portion of the pulvinar, nor in the SC, which is also magnocellular in terms of projection and visual response properties66. One possible explanation for the selective influence of parvocellular activity is that the translucent eye patch mainly reduced contrast input to P cells while the M cells were still highly responsive to luminance fluctuations. These findings are consistent with previous studies showing the selective influence of monocular pattern deprivation on binocular rivalry of chromatic stimulus67, BOLD response to high spatial frequency stimuli in the ventral visual stream10, parvocellular neuronal activity in V168, and the loss of high spatial frequency sensitivity in driving the deprivation effects in human behaviorally12. Compared to these previous studies, our work provides direct neuroimaging evidence that pattern deprivation primarily affects parvocellular activity in the visual thalamus.

During MCD, higher-order visual areas exclusively used the NDE input for higher-level visual processing, while the non-informative input from the DE was suppressed. The strengthening of the NDE channel in extrastriate visual areas and their connections from V1 (Fig. 7) can be explained by the Hebbian channel-reweighting model in perceptual learning55,69. Consistent with the fMRI results, our behavioral experiments also revealed enhanced NDE relative to DE performance in 3-D shape perception from monocular shading cues, and it occurred rapidly in just 1 h of MCD. Consistent with our findings, fast within-session learning effects have been found for many perceptual tasks, especially those involved in high-level processing14. Previous studies have shown that the learning-type effect of MD on low-level visual processing occurs at a much longer time scale (over 5 h or multiple days)13,70,71. It is possible that the learning effect in the high-level visual cortex could influence the ocular dominance in V1 through feedback modulations, leading to a slower shift towards NDE dominance after long-term deprivation72. In support of this, single-unit recordings of macaque V1 have found cells exhibiting enhanced activity to the NDE during short-term MD73. The effect in 3-D shape perception was unlikely due to changes in contrast appearance of high contrast stimuli, as it has been shown that the apparent contrast of high-contrast grating was affected little by contrast adaptation74. Although our behavioral and fMRI results were consistent in the direction of the deprivation effect, differences in deprivation duration precluded correlation analysis across participants. Finally, a stronger homeostatic effect in the pulvinar was associated with a weaker Hebbian-like effect in the dorsal visual areas, which may suggest a competitive interaction between the opposing mechanisms of ocular dominance plasticity. A similar inverse relationship was recently observed by Prosper and colleagues when Hebbian and homeostatic plasticity were measured separately with different paradigms75. However, they did not observe a significant interaction when the two effects were induced in close succession. Our results suggest that the interaction of homeostatic and Hebbian mechanisms of ocular dominance plasticity could occur when the two effects were induced simultaneously with MD.

We believe that the main reason for the discrepancy with previous studies on the deprivation effect on V1 BOLD responses is the difference in stimulus contrast10,11. The broadband noise stimulus in the previous studies was presented at a much lower contrast (10% root mean square (RMS) contrast) compared to the present study (40% RMS contrast). Contrast deprivation or adaptation has been shown to induce a horizontal shift (contrast gain change) of V1 contrast response function (CRF)17,76, which might explain the negative result in our study due to the saturation of V1 response at a high-contrast level51,52. Compared to V1, LGN responses are much more linear as a function of luminance contrast50, thus the contrast deprivation effect can still be observed in our LGN results. Using high contrast gratings not only increased the SNR in the LGN, but also has the advantage to saturate V1 responses. By which, we would be able to reduce the influence of V1 feedback, while achieving a measurable deprivation effect in the LGN. This explanation is strongly supported by our SSVEP results (Fig. 6c, d), showing a highly significant deprivation effect in low- but not high-contrast condition.

Although we did not record eye movement in the scanner, the influence of MD on gaze position has been investigated by another study77. In which they found no influence on the stability of gaze position even without requiring participants to maintain fixation on a small stimulus. In the current study, participants were instructed to maintain fixation on a small point throughout the measurements. Taken together with the previous finding, the observed deprivation effects were unlikely due to changes in fixation stability. Even if it had caused fixation instability of the DE, the blurring effect on the retinal image of 2 c.p.d. sinewave gratings should be minimal. Finally, there was an overall reduction of BOLD responses in the pulvinar, SC, and most cortical visual areas. The visual search performance also reduced based on shape-from-shading cues presented to DE. This was likely due to fatigue, as reported by many participants, by the end of the experiment. The fatigue could result from continuous adjustments of convergence and accommodation eye movements and attention mechanisms involved to resolve incompatible binocular inputs during MCD78,79.

To conclude, our findings demonstrate two opposing mechanisms of ocular dominance plasticity that concurrently operate in the visual thalamus and intermediate-level visual cortex in response to disrupted binocular input. Homeostatic contrast gain modulation occurred as early as in the LGN of the thalamus, enhancing DE relative to NDE sensitivity to restore interocular balance in contrast detection and binocular combination. At the same time, a rapid Hebbian-like (or learning-based) mechanism in the higher visual cortex strengthened the NDE channel relative to the DE channel, supporting higher-level visual functions such as 3-D shape perception. After long-term patching, top-down influences from the higher cortex may shift the ocular dominance in V1 towards the non-patched eye. Our findings elucidate the neural mechanisms behind the opposing effects of monocular patching observed in clinical practice versus the literature, underscoring the importance of perceptual training that engages both low- and high-level mechanisms for treating visual disorders like amblyopia.

Methods and materials

Participants

15 (26.3 ± 4.5 years of age, 8 females), 12 (24.8 ± 5.5 years of age, 6 females), 32 (25.3 ± 4.2 years of age, 19 females), and 30 (23.5 ± 4.5 years of age, 17 females) healthy volunteers with normal or corrected-to-normal vision participated in experiment 1, experiment 2, experiment 3a, and experiment 3b, respectively. Two participants in experiment 1 failed to follow task instructions in binocular phase combination, their data were not included in the behavioral results in Fig. 2b. ERGs were recorded in 7 out of 12 participants in experiment 2 (24.7 ± 7.3 years of age, 4 females). Two participants in experiment 3a performed less than 80% accuracy and reported difficulty in the 3-D shape perception task; thus, their data were removed from formal analysis. Based on literature, the influence of short-term MCD on low-level perception is a strong effect10,12,80,81. In experiment 1, the sample size of 15 participants provides 80% power to detect a strong effect (Cohen’s d = 0.8, alpha = 0.05). There has been no previous study on the short-term effect of MCD on high-level visual perception. The sample size of 30 participants in experiment 2a and 2b provides 74% power to detect a medium effect (Cohen’s d = 0.5, alpha = 0.05). The sample size of 12 participants in experiment 3 has 70% power to detect a strong effect (Cohen’s d = 0.8, alpha = 0.05). All procedures involving human participants were conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of the institutional research committee and with the 1964 Helsinki Declaration and its later amendments or comparable ethical standards. Informed consent was obtained from all individual participants included in the study. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Institute of Biophysics, Chinese Academy of Sciences (approval number: [No. 2012-IRB-011]). All ethical regulations relevant to human research participants were followed.

Stimuli and procedures

Visual stimuli were generated in MATLAB (Mathworks Inc.) with Psychophysics Toolbox extensions v3.0 (Brainard, 1997; Pelli, 1997). For psychophysical experiments, stimuli were dichoptically presented through OLED goggles (Goovis G2, 1920 by 1080 pixels @ 60 Hz for each eye). Visual stimuli for fMRI were presented on a rear-projection screen with an MRI compatible projector at 1024 by 768 pixels @ 60 Hz. Participants viewed the screen through a mirror mounted inside the head coil. A pair of prisms and a card-board divider were used for dichoptic presentation. In the ERG/EEG experiment, visual stimuli were presented by a CRT monitor (NESO FS210A, 1024 by 768 pixels @ 60 Hz). Participants used a hand-held occluder to achieve monocular stimulation. Color lookup tables of the OLED goggles, the MRI-compatible projector, and the CRT monitor were calibrated to have linear luminance output. The translucent eye patch is a diffuser, that transmits 80% of light but deprives the spatial pattern. It was not possible to count fingers at 10 cm away from the eye patch.

Experiment 1

Participants measured visual psychophysics and fMRI before (pre-test) and after (post-test) 2.5 h of MCD (Fig. 1a). During deprivation, a translucent eye-patch was applied either to the dominant eye or to the nondominant eye (i.e., randomly selected across observers). After the behavioral post-test, participants wore the eye-patch for another 30 min before the fMRI post-test.

In the contrast detection task, the visual stimulus was a sine-wave checkboard grating (2 c.p.d., 5° of visual angle) counterphase flicking at 4 Hz (Fig. 1b left). On each threshold-measurement trial, the visual stimulus was randomly presented to the left or to the right side of fixation, at 3° of eccentricity from the stimulus center, and in a gray background of the DE or the NDE. The fixation point was presented in both eyes. At the beginning of each trial, the stimulus was presented at a random contrast level below the detection threshold from the previous trial (the starting contrast level for the first trial was determined by a practice session). Participants were asked to remain fixated while increasing the contrast of the stimulus until it was just seen, and then to report its location (left or right) by pressing a button. The contrast of the stimulus was increased in a step size of 0.39%. The final contrast level was recorded as the detection threshold for this trial. On very rare occasions when participants made an incorrect response, an additional trial will be collected. Eight repetitions (i.e., 8 correct trials) were collected per session for testing the thresholds in the DE and 8 for the NDE. The median of the just-seen contrast levels on the correctly reported trials was used as the contrast detection threshold. Participants performed sufficient practice to obtain a stable performance before the formal test.

During binocular phase combination, a pair of full-contrast horizontal sine-wave gratings (0.46 c.p.d., 4.3° by 4.3° of visual angle), with 22.5° of phase-shift above or below the center of the screen, were dichoptically presented (Fig. 1b right). The sine-wave gratings were surround by square frames and diagonal lines to assist binocular convergence. A one-pixel black horizontal reference line in the middle of the grating was presented to both eyes. A trial started with a fixation surround by two dots in each eye as the nonius to check eye convergence. When the four dots were perceived as symmetrical, participants pressed a button, and the dichoptic gratings appeared. They were required to adjust the black reference line (in a step size of 1 pixel) to the center of the dark band of the binocularly perceived sine-wave grating. The phase measured by the black line indicates the perceived phase after binocular combination. To minimize potential positional bias, we performed measures in two stimuli configurations. In one configuration, the grating phase presented to the DE was +22.5° and the phase presented to the NDE was −22.5°; in the other configuration, the order was reversed. We repeated the measures four times for each configuration in each session (a total of eight trials were collected). The observers’ binocular perceived phase was calculated as the median value of these trials. Participants practiced sufficiently to obtain a stable performance before the formal experiment. A previous report showed that this task was reliable in quantifying changes in sensory eye dominance after monocular pattern deprivation82.

Each fMRI session started with two runs of ocular sensitivity measure, followed by an 8-min anatomical scan and then four runs of ocular bias localizer. The ocular sensitivity measure allows us to test the responsiveness of each ROI to stimulation from the deprived and NDE, both before and after MD. This served as the main condition of interest in our pre-post comparisons. The ocular bias localizer refers to a monocular presentation with high-contrast checkerboard, designed to activate eye-specific populations. This condition was used as a localizer to define ROIs with preferential responses to each eye. For the ocular sensitivity measure (Fig. 1c left), a sinewave checkerboard (2 c.p.d., 10° in diameter) with 80% luminance contrast counterphase flickering at 4 Hz was presented either to the DE or to the NDE, or to both eyes. The sine wave checkboard was presented for 12 s in alternation with 8 s of fixation. Each run of the ocular sensitivity measure lasts 8 min. The ocular bias localizer was a full contrast square wave checkerboard pattern (1° of check size, 10° in diameter) counter-phase flickering at 7.5 Hz (Fig. 1c right), presented to the left and right eyes in alternation every 24 s. A 12-s fixation period was included in the beginning and the end of each run. Each localizer run lasts 5.67 min. Participants were instructed to keep fixation during fMRI scanning.

Experiment 2

Before and after 2.5 h of MCD, participants measured visually evoked neural activity from the retina and early visual cortex to a full-field sinusoidal checkerboard (2 c.p.d.) counterphase flickering at 3.75 Hz. The checkerboard flickers were presented at 20 and 80% contrast. Each trial began with an arrow cue above fixation, and participants occluded the eye indicated by the cue. After pressing a button, the checkerboard flicker was presented continuously for 6 s. In the pre- or post-test session, 12 trials per contrast level per eye were collected in four runs. The order of contrast levels and the test eyes was counterbalanced across runs.

Experiment 3a

Before and after 1-h MCD, participants performed a perceptual grouping task based on 3-D shape from shading cues presented to the DE or to the NDE. The visual stimulus consists of a group of discs (0.6° in diameter) in a gray background (Fig. 9a). The surface texture of each disc was defined by a luminance gradient of 100% contrast that can be perceived either as a sphere or a cavity. The spheres were arranged in a closed (“O”) or an open circle (“C”) against a background of cavities. The diameter of the circle was 6°. The “O” shaped configuration consisted of 12 spheres, each of which was slightly shifted around a fixed position. The C-shaped stimulus was constructed by replacing three adjacent spheres in the circle with cavities. The opening was randomly chosen from 12 directions. The task was to determine whether the arrangement of balls was “O” or “C” and respond as soon as possible. A pilot experiment was conducted for each participant to determine the stimulus duration at 90% accuracy, using a 3-down-1-up staircase procedure. The stimulus duration was 198.33 ± 95.65 ms on average across participants.

Experiment 3b

The visual stimulus was a 3-D object composed of five ellipsoids presented on a gray background (Fig. 9b). The stimulus was about 5° in width and height. The size, position, orientation, and aspect ratio of the ellipsoids were randomly generated following normal distributions. In each trial, two 3-D objects were presented sequentially for 200 ms each, separated by 300-ms interstimulus interval. The two objects rotated either 7.5° to the right or to the left along the z-axis, resulting in a difference of 15° between them. The rotation is to avoid participants making judgment based on 2-D contours. In addition, the centroid of 3-D objects shifted 1° from the fixation point in random directions. The task for the participants was to discriminate whether the 3-D shapes of the two objects were the same or different. The discrimination ability was assessed by d prime. In a pilot experiment, discrimination performance was measured at different levels of 3-D shape difference between the two objects. The shape difference with a task difficulty of d prime = 1.5 was determined for each individual and used in the main deprivation experiment. In the main experiment, participants measured their 3-D shape discrimination ability for stimulus presented to DE and NDE before and after 1 h of MCD.

MRI data acquisition

MRI data were acquired on a 7 Tesla MRI scanner (Siemens Magnetom) with a 32-channel receive 1-channel transmit head coil (NOVA Medical), in the Beijing MRI center for Brain Research. T1-w anatomical volume was acquired with a MP2RAGE sequence (0.7-mm isotropic voxels, 256 sagittal 0.7-mm thick slices, FOV = 224 mm, TR/TE = 4000/3.05 ms, FA1 = 4°, FA2 = 5°, TI1 = 750 ms, TI2 = 2500 ms). Functional data were collected with a two-dimensional gradient-echo echo-planar imaging (2D GE-EPI) sequence (1.2-mm isotropic voxels with 31 slices, TE = 22 ms, TR = 2 s, FOV = 180 mm, in-plane GRAPPA factor = 2, nominal FA = 78°, phase encoding direction from anterior to posterior, receiver bandwidth = 1587 Hz/pixel). Functional images with reversed phase encoding direction were acquired for distortion correction. In both pre- and post-test sessions, there were two runs of ocular sensitivity measure (8 min per run), and four runs of ocular dominance localizer (5.67 min per run). To improve data quality, participants used a bite bar to restrict head motion.

MRI data analysis

Preprocessing

MRI data were preprocessed and analyzed with AFNI83, FreeSurfer Version 6.084, ANTs85, and the mripy package (https://github.com/herrlich10/mripy). Preprocessing of EPI data includes slice timing correction, distortion correction (blip-up/down), rigid body motion correction, and per-run scaling as a percent signal change. The anatomical volume was aligned to the mean of preprocessed EPI images (cost function: lpc). For inter-session registration, a 12-parameter linear transformation was estimated between T1w anatomical volumes (cost function: lpa). A midpoint transformation was calculated and applied to the data for each session. To minimize image blur, all spatial transformations were combined and applied to the functional images in one interpolation. General linear models using an HRF with short time-to-peak (Block4 in AFNI) were used to estimate BOLD signal change from baseline for each stimulus condition. Motion correction parameters were included as regressors of no interest. The bias-field corrected T1w-MP2RAGE images were segmented and reconstructed into pial and white matter surfaces in FreeSurfer.

ROI definition and analysis

LGN

The anatomical mask of each LGN was carefully defined on the T1-weighted anatomical images, in which the LGN appeared darker than surrounding tissue. For the ROI of most visually responsive voxels (to analyze monocular responses), we selected 50 voxels (0.6 mm isotropic after interpolation) with the strongest DE + NDE responses within the anatomical mask of the LGN. These visually responsive voxels were defined as those have strong visual responsiveness in Supplementary Fig. 4 the remaining voxels in the LGN mask were classified as having weak visual responsiveness. For the ROI of ocular bias voxels (to analyze binocular responses), we first identified two ocular bias clusters in each LGN with significant or close-to-significant DE-NDE activations in the ocular bias localizer (see Supplementary Fig. 2 shows the ocular bias clusters for all LGNs). 15 voxels with the strongest ocular bias in each cluster were selected as the ocular bias voxels for the analysis of binocular responses. These ocular bias voxels were defined as voxels with strong ocular bias in Supplementary Fig. 4, while the rest of the voxels in the LGN mask were defined as having weak ocular bias. Voxel numbers (15 or 50) were determined by the smallest ROI size across all LGNs. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 7, the statistical results of the LGN were robust to the number of voxels in the ROI.

To control for baseline interocular differences in BOLD activation within the LGN, we implemented a voxel selection procedure based on responses from the localizer. Prior to ranking, only voxels showing significant activation in the localizer run were included: for monocular conditions, this was defined as voxels significantly activated by the DE + NDE; for binocular conditions, voxel selection was based on significant activation for each eye. For each participant, the retained LGN voxels were then independently ranked in descending order of their beta values for deprived-eye and non-deprived-eye stimulation, respectively. A fixed number of voxels (n = 50 for monocular and n = 30 for binocular) showing the strongest response to the DE were selected. For the NDE, the number of selected voxels was incrementally increased (starting from 50 in steps of 10) until the average pre-deprivation BOLD response (n = 280 for monocular and n = 100 for binocular, measured from the main experiment) closely matched that of the DE. The number of voxels for the DE was fixed because, in the pre-deprivation data, responses to the NDE were consistently stronger than those to the DE. Since 50(30) voxels already represented a minimal subset for the DE, we relaxed the voxel selection criterion only for the NDE. This approach ensured that any observed post-deprivation changes were not confounded by baseline asymmetries in signal strength.

Layer profile analysis of the LGN

To be consistent with the activation maps in Fig. 4a, the analysis of layer profile was done in the MNI space. For each LGN, we selected several adjacent coronal slices strongly activated by the functional localizer (with a large cluster of positive LE + RE response). Beta values of all voxels of these slices were projected to the axis perpendicularly to the ventral surface of the LGN, and then averaged to generate the layer profile. The layer profiles of all LGNs were interpolated to the same length for group analysis (5.3 mm, the largest thickness of the LGN across all slices).

Pulvinar and SC

Data analysis for the pulvinar and SC was performed in MNI space. We first defined a spherical mask including the visual thalamus and the upper brainstem. A nonlinear transformation of the subcortical region was then computed from the native space of anatomical volume to the MNI space using ANTs. The high-resolution symmetric MNI template from CIT168 atlas was used for nonlinear warping86. The nonlinear transformation was then applied to the GLM results to transform the functional data from native space to MNI space. The anatomical masks of the Pulvinar and SC were carefully defined on the high-resolution symmetric MNI template. To reduce inter-individual differences and improve detection power in the group-level analysis, spatial smoothing was performed on the beta values of GLM results within the ROI with a 2.83-mm FWHM Gaussian filter. Then, group-level activation maps of stimulus response and deprivation effect in Fig. 4a were generated using standard random-effects analysis (t-test) on the beta maps across participants. ROIs of the SC and pulvinar were defined as voxels with significant group-level activations in the ocular bias localizer (DE + NDE > 0, p < 0.05 uncorrected). As shown in Supplementary Fig. 7, the statistical significance of the pulvinar and SC were consistent across different numbers of voxels in the ROI.

Cortical visual areas

For each cortical visual area, voxels with significant activations in the functional localizer (DE + NDE > 0, p < 0.05 uncorrected) within the retinotopic atlas53,54 were selected as the ROI for analysis of monocular responses. For the analysis of binocular responses in V1, we selected voxels with significant ocular bias (DE-NDE, p < 0.05 uncorrected) within V1 with significant positive activation (DE + NDE > 0, p < 0.05 uncorrected) in the functional localizer. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 7, the statistical significance in V1 was consistent across different number of voxels in the ROI. To generate the group-level activation maps of stimulus response and deprivation effect on the standard surface (std141 in SUMA), a surface-based spatial smoothing with 4-mm FWHM kernel was first applied to the beta maps on the standard surface and then followed by a standard random-effects analysis (t-test) across participants. Family-wise errors of surface clusters were determined by Monte Carlo simulation in AFNI.

Dynamic causal modeling

Effective connectivity between the dorsal visual areas (DVA) and V1 was analyzed with the DCM module of SPM12 (version 2020-Jan-13th). Preprocessed ROI-averaged time series of the pre- and post-test sessions were concatenated as the input dataset. In the full DCM model (Fig. 7c left), V1 receives three driving inputs (Matrix C), including DE, NDE, and binocular stimulus conditions in both pre- and post-test sessions. Modulatory inputs (Matrix B) of deprivation effect include the DE and NDE stimulus conditions only in the post-test session. Fixed connections (Matrix A) were defined as the reciprocal connections between V1 and DVA, and the self-connections within each area. The deprivation effect could modulate the reciprocal connections between V1 and DVA. At the first level, the full DCM model was estimated for each participant56. At the second level, we used Bayesian model reduction, Bayesian model average, and parametric empirical Bayes to estimate the group-averaged connectivity strength and posterior probability57,87.

ERG/EEG data acquisition

ERG and electroencephalography (EEG) were recorded with a 64-channel Neuroscan SynAmps RT system digitized at a sampling rate of 1000 Hz with DC components. A 64-channel Ag-AgCl electrode cap was used for EEG recordings. Data from the posterior parietal and occipital electrodes were recorded. The reference electrode located in-between Cz and CPz. For ERG recordings, a gold foil electrode (a product of Roland) was looped over the lower eye-lid margin of the DE, contacting the lower sclera below the iris. An Ag-AgCl electrode (a product of Roland) was placed on the outer canthus as reference. For both ERG and EEG, the ground electrode was placed on the forehead in-between the two eyes. All electrode impedances were kept below 8 kΩ.

ERG/EEG data analysis

ERGs and EEGs were band­pass filtered from 3 to 30 Hz. Since ERGs were extremely sensitive to eye movements and blinks, we removed trials with a voltage range over 100 μV (eye movements or blinks) or less than 1 μV (flat timecourse due to loosen electrode contact). No artifact rejection was performed on EEGs. To focus on activity from the early visual cortex, only Oz was used for VEP analysis. To avoid the influence of transient responses to steady-state signals, the first 800-ms data from the beginning of each trial were removed. The remaining 5.2-s data were averaged across all trials in each condition. The amplitude spectrum (Fig. 6b) was then generated by fast Fourier transform of the mean time course. The amplitude of the second-order harmonic at 7.5 Hz was taken as the amplitude of the steady-state signal.

Statistical sand reproducibility

For the ROI-averaged responses in LGN and V1, we first performed an omnibus F test of three-way repeated measures (rm) ANOVA with eye (DE/NDE), session (pre/post), and stimulus (monocular/binocular) as the fixed effects, and participants as the random effect. The deprivation effect on ocular dominance was characterized by a significant eye × session interaction. Then, two-way rm ANOVAs with eye and session as within-subject factors were performed separately on the monocular and binocular responses, followed by post-hoc paired t-tests if there was a significant eye × session interaction. Based on the rationale of the seminal paper by Levin et al.88, post-hoc t-tests followed by a significant two-way interaction do not need further correction for family-wise errors. For the monocular responses of all other visual areas, two-way rm ANOVAs with eye and session as within-subject factors were performed. A Bayesian mixed-model ANOVA was used to assess the evidence for a null effect in pattern ERGs.

A non-parametric bootstrap test was used to evaluate the false positive rate of the deprivation effect on the normalized response in the DE relative to (divided by) the response in the NDE. The normalized responses were calculated by the mean of bootstrapped samples (sampled with replacement). The deprivation effect was defined as the difference between the normalized DE/NDE ratio between pre and post-sessions. The bootstrapping procedure was repeated 10,000 times. A null distribution was generated by shifting the mean of the bootstrapped distribution to the test value (0), and then a two-sided p value was derived from the null distribution. The family-wise error of significant clusters on the cortical surface (Fig. 7a) was determined by Monte Carlo simulation in AFNI, corrected within vertices with significant stimulus-evoked activations in the localizer.

Sample sizes, power calculations, and exclusion criteria are described in the “Participants” section. Within-subject designs were used throughout. Statistical analyses and reproducibility assessments followed standard practices for behavioral and fMRI research.
