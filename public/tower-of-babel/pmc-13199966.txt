Understanding smokeless tobacco use in rural United States: A narrative synthesis of contributing factors and prevention efforts
Authors: Mariyam Abbas, Steven A Cohen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesSmokeless tobacco (SLT) use remains disproportionately high in rural regions of the United States, particularly among youth, due to perceptions of lower risk compared with smoking. Despite clear evidence of its addictive potential and serious health consequences, limited attention has been given to the social, cultural, and structural drivers of SLT use in these underserved communities. This review aims to synthesize existing evidence on the prevalence, risk factors, and prevention strategies related to SLT use in rural America.Study designNarrative review.MethodsA narrative synthesis of peer-reviewed studies was conducted to integrate findings across heterogeneous study designs and contexts.ResultsThe literature highlights multiple contributors to SLT use, including ease of access, lower taxation, reduced risk perception, and demographic influences such as age, gender, race/ethnicity, and educational attainment. Cultural acceptance and intergenerational norms further reinforce its use. While interventions such as public health campaigns and behavioral therapies have been attempted, their effectiveness has often been limited by insufficient cultural tailoring and persistent misinformation.ConclusionsSLT use in rural America reflects deeply rooted sociocultural dynamics and structural inequities that are not adequately addressed by existing tobacco control approaches. Sustainable prevention requires culturally informed, community-driven strategies that address local realities while tackling broader determinants of health. These insights underscore the need for more tailored policies and interventions to reduce health disparities in rural populations.Keywords: Smokeless tobacco, Rural health, Health disparities, Tobacco control, Narrative synthesis

Introduction

Rural US, defined by low population density, geographic isolation, and smaller, dispersed communities, faces persistent public health disadvantages. Rural populations experience higher poverty rates, reduced access to healthcare and prevention services, and fewer educational and economic opportunities, factors that contribute to disproportionate burdens of chronic disease and premature mortality [1,2]. Addressing the specific health needs of rural communities remains critical for reducing health disparities and advancing population health equity [3].

Tobacco use including smokeless tobacco (SLT) is consistently higher in rural than urban areas of the United States, exacerbating persistent rural–urban health inequities [[4], [5], [6]]. National surveillance shows elevated use of SLT products in rural regions compared with urban and suburban settings [4,5]. For instance, CDC NHIS 2024 estimates indicate that 4.5 % of rural adults use smokeless tobacco compared with 1.8 % of urban adults, underscoring the scale of rural–urban inequities [7,8]. Recent youth-focused work in high-burden areas such as Appalachia and rural California further documents early initiation, product curiosity, and permissive community norms that normalize SLT experimentation and use [[9], [10], [11], [12]].

SLT products include chewing tobacco, moist snuff, pouched or loose oral tobacco, snus, and other non-combusted oral forms. Earlier drafts of this work referenced e-cigarettes, waterpipes, and beedis because these terms appear in indexing records that sometimes co-occur with SLT search strings; however, because these products are combustible (beedi, waterpipe) or inhaled nicotine aerosols (e-cigarettes) rather than true smokeless products, they were excluded from the analytic focus in this narrative synthesis [[13], [14], [15]]. Globally, more than 350 million people use SLT, and evidence links use to oral cancers, cardiovascular disease, and exposure to carcinogens, nitrosamines, and heavy metals [16,17]. Traditional forms such as betel quid or khainee remain common in South and Southeast Asia, offering comparative insight into cultural embedding of non-combustible tobacco, though their direct relevance to U.S. rural users is context-dependent [18].

Although surveillance documents SLT-related disease risk [19] and U.S. rural tobacco disparities broadly [4,5], less is known about why rural residents-especially youth, men, and socially disadvantaged groups-initiate, sustain, or switch to SLT; how cultural and gendered norms (e.g., rural masculinity, sports, labor identity) reinforce use; and which prevention or cessation strategies are viable in low-resource, geographically isolated settings [9,10,12,[20], [21], [22], [23]]. Evidence from Alaska Native and other Indigenous rural communities further indicates unique cultural meanings and product traditions that influence interventions [[24], [25], [26]].

Policy frameworks exist to guide tobacco dependence treatment globally: Article 14 of the WHO framework convention on tobacco control encourages behavioral and pharmacological support for cessation, but implementation has been uneven [[27], [28], [29], [30]]. Translating such frameworks to rural U.S. settings requires attention to access barriers, sociocultural fit, and uneven policy adoption in states or counties where tobacco agriculture, tax structures, or political climates may dampen regulatory momentum [[31], [32], [33]].

Earlier reviews of SLT interventions [34,35] and tobacco-related health equity [36] showed the need for locally responsive, culturally grounded strategies. Yet targeted syntheses of SLT use in rural U.S. populations remain sparse as much as it is done form combustible cigarettes [37]. The present narrative synthesis draws together the available peer-reviewed literature to [1]: describe reasons rural residents use SLT (including substitution for cigarettes) [2]; summarize demographic and behavioral correlates of SLT use in rural communities; and [3] examine policy, community, and cessation efforts relevant to SLT prevention and control. By integrating findings across epidemiologic, behavioral, and community-engaged studies, we aim to guide rural health promotion, tobacco control programming, and future research.

Methods

The design of this study is a narrative review. To inform our narrative review, we conducted a targeted literature search focused on smokeless tobacco use in rural U.S. populations. An initial exploratory search (September 2022) was run across PubMed, JSTOR, PsycINFO, EBSCOhost, and Scopus to scope terminology, indexing variability, and the range of tobacco product categories linked to rural keywords. Because Scopus returned the broadest capture of peer-reviewed records spanning public health, clinical, behavioral, and social science journals, it was selected as the primary database for structured searching. Reference lists of included papers and forward citation tracking were used to identify additional relevant publications (see Fig. 1).

Flow diagram showing the literature selection process for the narrative synthesis.

The final search strategy (May 2023–January 2024) combined controlled vocabulary and free-text terms for smokeless/oral tobacco products (e.g., “smokeless tobacco,” “chewing tobacco,” “snuff,” “snus,” “oral tobacco,” “dip”) with rural U.S. identifiers (“rural,” “Appalachia,” “frontier,” “rural United States,” “rural health”). Broader nicotine product terms (e.g., e-cigarette, waterpipe, beedi) were initially included to capture mis-indexed studies but were excluded at the analytic stage unless SLT-specific data were presented. No geographic or date limits were applied in the database query; only English-language publications were reviewed in full.

Eligible publications included peer-reviewed journal articles and, where relevant to context, books or reports that presented data or interpretation related to SLT use, correlates, or prevention/cessation efforts in U.S. rural populations. We took an inclusive approach to the operationalization of “rural,” accepting author-defined categorizations (e.g., RUCA codes, census rural, Appalachian counties, Alaska village classifications) to reflect the diversity of rural metrics used in the literature. Exclusion criteria eliminated non–peer-reviewed commentaries, news items, or pieces lacking SLT-relevant data; non-U.S. rural populations without translatable relevance; and studies focused exclusively on combustible products when no SLT data were reported.

Because study designs varied widely (cross-sectional surveys, qualitative interviews, intervention trials, policy analyses), we did not apply a formal risk-of-bias instrument. Extracted data were grouped inductively and iteratively into thematic domains aligned with our guiding questions [1]: motivators and contexts for SLT use (including substitution for cigarettes) [2]; demographic, social, and behavioral correlates; and [3] policy, cessation, and prevention strategies in rural settings. This flexible, interpretive approach follows guidance for narrative synthesis in heterogeneous health promotion literature. [Adapted from Arksey & O'Malley, 2005 and Peterson et al., 2020 [38,39]].

Synthesis approach (SWiM framework)

To enhance transparency and reproducibility, the synthesis followed the Synthesis Without Meta-analysis (SWiM) framework [40]. Studies were grouped by population focus (e.g., rural adolescents, adults, Indigenous or Alaska Native populations), study design (quantitative, qualitative, or mixed-methods), and thematic domain (motivators, correlates, prevention/cessation).

Key study characteristics-author, year, design, sample, and principal findings are summarized in Table 1, linking each thematic conclusion to its supporting evidence. Heterogeneity in study methods and outcome measures was addressed qualitatively by evaluating the direction and consistency of findings across studies rather than statistical pooling. Because of this heterogeneity, no standardised effect metrics or transformations were applied.

Findings from unique cultural or regional contexts (e.g., Alaska Native and Indigenous communities) were explicitly contextualized rather than generalized to the broader rural U.S. population. Data presentation used tabular and visual methods (Table 1, Table 2; Fig. 1, Fig. 2) consistent with SWiM guidance for non-quantitative evidence synthesis.

Results

Rural tobacco burden and SLT prevalence

According to the 2021 CDC MMWR surveillance report 18.7 % (≈46 million) U.S. adults currently used any tobacco product, 3.5 % used smokeless tobacco (≈5.2 million), and 77.5 % of users used combustible products [41]. National data further show that SLT use remains disproportionately higher in rural than urban communities 4.5 % versus 1.8 % and overall tobacco use is 26.2 % versus 17.5 % respectively [7,8]. States with the highest smoking and SLT rates remain among the most rural (West Virginia 20.8 %, Kentucky 17.2 %, Tennessee 17.0 %) [7]. These patterns contribute to persistent rural–urban inequities in tobacco-related disease and mortality. According to national surveillance (NHIS and BRFSS), approximately 2.1% of U.S. adults reported current SLT use in 2021, with prevalence nearly twice as high among rural residents (3.8%) compared with urban counterparts (2.0%) [[4], [5], [6],42].

Regional studies in Appalachia and western rural states further demonstrate clustering of multi-product tobacco use and early uptake of SLT among youth [9,11,12,43]. Longitudinal work indicates that product switching, including movement between cigarettes, SLT, and dual use, occurs in both rural and urban settings, but rural users may have distinct transition pathways influenced by access and social networks [44,45].

Motivators for SLT use and substitution for cigarettes

Economic, social, and perceived risk advantages frequently motivate rural residents to use SLT instead of, or alongside, cigarettes. Lower taxation and cheaper unit cost relative to factory-made cigarettes are consistently associated with lower cessation rates and higher SLT substitution in ecological and modeling studies [20,31,46,47]; however, causal inference is limited by study design. Youth decision-making studies in rural California and Appalachia report that SLT is seen as more discreet (no smoke), acceptable in school- or work-adjacent spaces, and sometimes perceived as less harmful [[9], [10], [11], [12]]. Misperceptions of reduced risk echo broader harm-minimization narratives seen in nicotine product debates [48]. Among Alaska Native and other Indigenous rural communities, historical and ceremonial meanings intersect with contemporary recreational use, complicating cessation messaging [24,26,49].

Gender, rural masculinity, and cultural norms

Rural masculine identities-linked to farming, ranching, manual trades, and sport, recurrently appear in SLT narratives. Helme and colleagues describe how rural teen boys interpret SLT use as a marker of toughness and belonging, reinforced by adult role models [21]. Similar themes emerge in Appalachian youth focus groups, where SLT is normalized in male peer groups and family networks [9,11,48]. Earlier national and rural analyses also show higher SLT use among males and lower education groups [24,43,[50], [51], [52]]. Cultural acceptability in rural settings may reduce perceived stigma and undermine quit motivation [27,53].

Developmental pathways: youth susceptibility, initiation, and progression

Adolescence is a critical window for SLT initiation. Chaffee and colleagues reported longitudinal predictors of susceptibility, initiation, and progression in a rural cohort, highlighting peer exposure and perceived norms [12]. Couch and others similarly found that rural adolescent males weigh family examples, peer behavior, and product flavoring/form factors in SLT decision-making [10]. Helme et al. and Hart et al. add qualitative evidence that intergenerational use and local sports culture normalize experimentation [9,21]. In broader national datasets, youth from rural or lower-resource areas show higher odds of SLT ever use [53]. These early-use trajectories reinforce the need for tailored youth prevention in schools and community programs serving dispersed rural districts.

Structural and cumulative disadvantage

Rural tobacco disparities are shaped by layered structural factors: poverty, limited health infrastructure, geographic isolation, and in some areas, economic dependence on tobacco production or related industries [1,2,33]. Ozga and colleagues proposed a cumulative disadvantage framework linking early disadvantages to higher tobacco risk behaviors across the life course in rural settings [22]. These structural constraints are linked with lower health literacy, limited cessation services, and policy gaps in taxation or flavored product restrictions, which may contribute to the persistence of SLT use [3,31,32].

Intervention and policy efforts in rural settings

Evidence for SLT prevention or cessation in rural U.S. populations is limited but instructive. School- and community-based programs targeting rural adolescent males have demonstrated feasibility and modest behavior change [54,55]. A text-based mobile reduction intervention showed promise among rural and underserved SLT users [56]. The U.S. FDA's Real Cost Smokeless campaign produced measurable changes in beliefs about SLT among rural boys in a randomized field trial [53,57]. Professional advice and structured behavioral counseling remain underused but potentially scalable in low-resource environments [53,58]. Lessons from broader smokeless cessation and cross-national cessation initiatives [34,35,[59], [60], [61]] suggest that multi-component interventions combining culturally framed messaging, behavioral support, and accessible remote delivery modalities may be adaptable to rural U.S. contexts.

Discussion

Taken together, the literature indicates that SLT use in rural US is sustained by intersecting forces, economic accessibility, masculine identity, intergenerational normalization, perceived lower risk, and structural disadvantage [9,10,21,22,24,47]. Interventions that ignore cultural fit or rural delivery constraints tend to underperform [54,56,57]. Future progress will require co-designed rural strategies that integrate local voices, address policy levers (e.g., tax parity, retail density), and incorporate tailored communication that challenges low-risk myths while respecting cultural meanings of use [26,53,62].

SLT use in rural US represents a complex and persistent public health challenge, rooted in factors such as low-risk perception, economic accessibility, and entrenched cultural norms. This narrative synthesis reveals that SLT use is disproportionately prevalent among young, less-educated men in rural areas, particularly among White and Indigenous populations, where masculinity, tradition, and intergenerational use sustain tobacco behaviors. Despite the availability of interventions, including behavioral therapies, media campaigns, and policy efforts, effectiveness has been limited by a lack of culturally nuanced strategies and insufficient attention to social determinants of health.

Evidence from this review highlights the importance of addressing not only individual behavior but also the broader socioecological context. Successful intervention requires acknowledging local cultural values, community norms, and political-economic realities [63]. Approaches such as community co-designed messaging, gender-sensitive interventions, and culturally tailored cessation programs show promise and warrant further evaluation. Drawing lessons from international and cross-cultural interventions, particularly those that center community voice and cultural humility, can guide future efforts.

Based on the reviewed literature, recommendations can be considered in three tiers.

Tier A – Moderate evidence: Behavioral interventions (e.g., individual or group counseling) and combined media plus counseling campaigns have shown measurable reductions in SLT use and quit attempts in rural settings [[54], [55], [56], [57], [58]]. Implementation should prioritize young men and adolescents through schools and community health centers, with outcomes measured via quit rates and risk-perception change.

Tier B – Promising but under-evaluated: Mobile and text-based cessation programs show feasibility and engagement among rural and underserved users [56] but require controlled evaluation to assess sustained cessation impact.

Tier C – Indirect or model-based evidence: Policy levers such as tax parity, retail density restrictions, and flavor bans have strong theoretical support but face feasibility and political barriers in tobacco-dependent rural economies [8,[31], [32], [33]]. Stakeholder-driven policy co-creation and rural coalition leadership are critical for sustainable implementation.

Implementation efforts should include clear metrics, such as quit attempts, reduced SLT prevalence, or increased awareness tailored to each population and intervention channel.

Finally, public health strategies must consider the sociopolitical landscape, including resistance from the tobacco industry, rural economic dependence on tobacco, and policy inertia. Engaging local leaders, healthcare providers, and stakeholders in policy co-creation and implementation can build sustainable support for SLT prevention and cessation. A multifaceted, equity-driven approach that centers rural voices is essential to reducing SLT use and advancing health equity in underserved US communities.

Supporting statement for narrative review

A narrative synthesis approach was chosen due to the heterogeneity of available studies on smokeless tobacco (SLT) use in rural America. The literature spans diverse methodologies (qualitative, epidemiological, and policy-focused) and heterogeneous populations, making a systematic review or meta-analysis infeasible. A narrative approach allows integration of findings across these varied study types to generate actionable insights. Furthermore, the public health issue addressed: SLT use in rural and underserved U.S. populations is both underexplored and not amenable to systematic aggregation of outcomes. The authors bring expertise in tobacco research, behavioral science, and rural health disparities, positioning them to contextualize and synthesize the evidence into a coherent framework for policy and practice.
