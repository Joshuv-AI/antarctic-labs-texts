Synthesis of halide perovskite thin films from an eutectic mixture of Pb(NO3)2, acetamide, and urea for fabricating resistive random-access memory (RRAM)
Authors: Chuong Thanh Huynh, Vy Thao Ha Cao, Tai Duy Nguyen, Tung Thanh Ngo, Em Hoang Danh, Quan Phu Pham, Ngoc Kim Pham, Phuong Tuyet Nguyen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Methylammonium lead iodide (MAPbI3) perovskite is conventionally synthesized in toxic solvents such as dimethylformamide and dimethyl sulfoxide, whose associated health and environmental risks limit large-scale and sustainable production. Building on our previous work, we further developed a synthesis route for MAPbI3 perovskite using a deep eutectic mixture composed of lead nitrate, acetamide, and urea. The resulting perovskite thin film with its intrinsic hysteresis behavior was used to fabricate resistive random-access memory (RRAM). The structure and morphology of the perovskite films were characterized by X-ray diffraction and scanning electron microscopy, respectively, while the operation of RRAM devices was evaluated by current–voltage curve measurement. The perovskite films synthesized using the eutectic mixture exhibited improved phase conversion and greater stability than those fabricated by the conventional sequential deposition method. Electrical characterization of the RRAM devices demonstrated reliable and repeatable current–voltage behavior, with strong endurance and retention performance. Notably, the devices exhibited both analog and digital resistive switching modes, underscoring their potential for multifunctional non-volatile memory and neuromorphic computing. Overall, our findings provided a reduced-hazard solvent pathway for MAPbI3 perovskite synthesis, replacing toxic organic solvents with a low-toxicity deep eutectic system while broadening its applicability to next-generation memory technologies with implications for future sustainable information storage solutions.

Introduction

Organic–inorganic hybrid halide perovskites, with the general formula ABX3 (A = MA+, FA+; B = Pb2+, Sn2+; X = I−, Cl−, Br−), have emerged as versatile materials for optoelectronic devices, light-emitting diodes, and photodetectors.1,2 Their implementation in solar cells has driven power conversion efficiencies beyond 26%,3 owing to strong light absorption, low exciton binding energy, tunable bandgap, and ambipolar charge transport.4 However, most solar cells based on organic–inorganic hybrid halide perovskites exhibit hysteresis in the current density–voltage (J–V) characteristics, which severely affects both device performance and the accuracy of efficiency measurements.5 Numerous experimental and theoretical studies have characterized this hysteresis and proposed underlying mechanisms associated with halide ion migration.6 Since ion migration is also a typical origin of resistive switching behavior, organic–inorganic hybrid halide perovskites have been widely utilized as the active layer and investigated for RRAM applications.7,8

RRAM, also referred to as memristors, has been extensively studied for use in non-volatile memory, logic devices and artificial synapses due to advantages such as simple device architecture, fast switching speed and long data retention.9 A typical RRAM device consists of a simple electrode/active layer/electrode configuration. In general, RRAM can reversibly switch between a high resistance state (HRS) and a low resistance state (LRS), or even between multiple resistance states, under voltage bias.10 Compared with oxide-based RRAM, hybrid halide perovskite-based RRAM offers significant advantages such as lower power consumption, owing to the low formation energy of intrinsic defects and the fast migration of ions. Recent studies have confirmed that the application of organic–inorganic hybrid halide perovskites in RRAM can yield stable resistive switching performance with excellent operational reliability, establishing them as multifunctional materials suitable for diverse technological applications.11–13

Within this family, methylammonium lead iodide (MAPbI3) has emerged as one of the most promising materials due to its outstanding optoelectronic properties. MAPbI3 exhibits a broad absorption spectrum extending to ∼800 nm, a high absorption coefficient (∼105 cm−1), a tunable bandgap, and low temperature processability.2 The material can be synthesized through facile methods and readily forms uniform thin films, making it highly suitable for both optoelectronic and memory devices. Typically, MAPbI3 thin films are fabricated via various solution processing methods such as one-step spin coating, two-step sequential deposition, blade coating, spray coating, and inkjet printing.14

Conventional MAPbI3 fabrication relies on toxic, EU-restricted solvents such as DMF and DMSO,15,16 prompting efforts to identify safer alternatives. Approaches using alternative lead precursors (PbCl2, Pb(OAc)2, Pb(acac)2, PbCO3) or electrochemical deposition in aqueous/ethanol media have been explored, but generally suffer from poor phase control, incomplete conversion, or non-uniform morphology.17,18 Nevertheless, this approach remains constrained by poor conversion efficiency, incomplete transformation of the precursors and intermediate phases into perovskite crystals, and non-uniform surface morphology.18

Other approaches including using alternative solvent systems such as Pb(NO3)2 aqueous precursors have also been considered for the conventional PbI2/DMF:DMSO system. In 2015, Hsieh et al. first demonstrated the formation of perovskite films from a Pb(NO3)2/H2O precursor via a dipping process in methylammonium iodide (MAI) solution prepared in isopropanol. The film formation began with deposition of a Pb(NO3)2 layer, followed by MAI diffusion during immersion. PbI2 was generated through ion-exchange between Pb(NO3)2 and MAI, yielding MANO3 as a byproduct, which was subsequently removed by rinsing with isopropanol. The final MAPbI3 film was obtained via a chemical reaction between the residual PbI2 and excess MAI, starting with the transformation of PbI2 into PbI64− octahedra, into which MA+ cations diffused and coordinated, completing the MAPbI3 perovskite structure. However, the film morphology and conversion efficiency were suboptimal, as the diffusion of MAI was hindered by the initially formed MAPbI3 layer, leaving residual PbI2 and resulting in incomplete conversion. Furthermore, non-uniform nucleation and uncontrolled crystal growth led to poor surface morphology.19

Subsequent studies focused on optimizing processing conditions to enhance conversion efficiency and surface morphology by employing multi-cycle immersion in MAI solution and introducing MACl additives. The presence of MACl, combined with multiple dipping cycles, significantly accelerated the conversion of Pb(NO3)2 to perovskite crystals, facilitated ion diffusion, and improved film morphology.20 For instance, Zhang et al. achieved improved Pb(NO3)2 film density and grain size by controlling relative humidity (<10%), tuning wetting properties, and incorporating additives, thereby producing more uniform MAPbI3 films with fewer pinholes. Despite these advancements, the Pb(NO3)2/H2O precursor system still faces challenges related to viscosity, strict requirements for temperature and humidity control, and prolonged deposition times, which hinder reproducibility and large-scale implementation.21

In this context, we proposed a novel approach employing a deep eutectic solvent (DES) system composed of Pb(NO3)2, acetamide, and urea as the precursor for MAPbI3 film fabrication via a simple two-step spin coating process. Compared to traditional organic solvents, our proposed system is environmentally friendlier with respect to solvent toxicity,22 allowing easy preparation through simple, low-cost techniques suitable for large-scale production. Importantly, such a DES system enables controlled crystallization and tunable film morphology, thereby improving conversion efficiency and surface quality of MAPbI3 films.23

Deep eutectic solvents (DESs), mixtures of hydrogen-bond donors and acceptors capable of dissolving lead-based compounds at low melting points,20–22 represent an attractive alternative solvent class for perovskite synthesis.24–27 In 2003, Abbott et al.28 introduced DES of choline chloride–urea (1 : 2), which could remain liquid at room temperature with a melting point as low as 12 °C. Several studies have demonstrated the potential of DES to dissolve lead-based compounds. For example, DES of choline chloride–urea can dissolve PbSO4, PbO2, and PbO27 while DES from choline chloride and ethylene glycol is capable of dissolving PbSO4, PbCO3, PbO, and PbO2.29 Recently, Lu et al. synthesized CsPbBr3 perovskite nanocrystals using natural deep eutectic solvents as solvents.30 In another work, a glucose–urea DES-assisted calcination strategy was established to synthesize oxygen vacancy-rich BaTiO3 as an efficient support for Ru catalysts.31

We previously reported a preliminary DESs precursor system based on acetamide and urea for MAPbI3 synthesis, under environmentally friendly conditions, further supporting the feasibility of replacing conventional toxic solvents with alternative chemistries.32 In this study, we further develop this concept by formulating a fully eutectic Pb(NO3)2–acetamide–urea (PAU) system that achieves complete liquefaction, and apply it to MAPbI3 thin-film fabrication via a two-step spin-coating process. Beyond the improved precursor chemistry, the specific novelty of the present work lies in (i) verification of the DESs formation mechanism and its partial persistence within the resulting perovskite film, (ii) comprehensive structural, optical, and long-term stability characterization of the resulting films, and (iii) the first integration of this PAU-derived MAPbI3 into RRAM devices, revealing a dual digital/analog resistive switching behavior. This strategy aims to provide a sustainable and less toxic alternative to conventional organic solvents, while enabling controlled crystallization, improving conversion efficiency, and enhancing film morphology. Furthermore, this study evaluates the potential of DESs-derived MAPbI3 films for integration into RRAM, underscoring their applicability in memory technologies.

Experimental section

Materials

Unless otherwise stated, chemicals and materials were purchased on commercial sources. These include fluorine-doped tin oxide (FTO) glass (10 × 10 cm, 15 Ω cm−2, Pilkington, USA), lead nitrate (Pb(NO3)2, Xilong), acetamide (CH3CONH2, Zhanyun), urea (NH2CONH2, Xilong), titanium tetrachloride (TiCl4, Macklin), methylammonium iodide (MAI, Sigma-Aldrich), isopropanol (IPA, Sigma-Aldrich), and ethanol (C2H6O, Chem Sol).

Preparation and evaluation of the eutectic mixture of Pb(NO3)2, acetamide and urea (PAU)

Pb(NO3)2, acetamide and urea were dried in a vacuum at 70 °C for 2 h to eliminate hygroscopic moisture and prevent water contamination in the resulting product. We prepared the eutectic mixture by mixing the dried components in a Pb(NO3)2 : acetamide : urea molar ratio of 1 : 3 : 3 (Fig. 1a) after screening different ratios (e.g., 1 : 1 : 1, 1 : 2 : 2, 1 : 2 : 3, 1 : 3 : 2, 1 : 3 : 3, 2 : 3 : 3…), and magnetically stirring at 60 °C for 3 hours in a sealed vial until a homogeneous liquid slurry formed (Fig. 1b).

(a) Solid-state mixture of Pb(NO3)2, acetamide and urea at a molar ratio of 1 : 3 : 3. (b) Homogeneous eutectic PAU liquid formed after heating at 60 °C. (c) Schematic illustration of the RRAM device (FTO/MAPbI3/Ag).

Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR, Bruker ALPHA II, 4000–500 cm−1) was used to analyze individual components and the resulting mixture for hydrogen bonding and characteristic molecular interactions of the expected eutectic mixture (Fig. 2).

FTIR spectra of acetamide, urea and the eutectic PAU mixture.

Preparation and evaluation of perovskite films

FTO glass substrates were sequentially cleaned with detergent, deionized water, and ethanol in an ultrasonic bath for 30 minutes. A compact TiO2 layer was deposited on the FTO surface via chemical bath deposition (CBD) using a 40 mM TiCl4 solution at 70 °C for 30 minutes, followed by annealing at 500 °C for 30 minutes. Prior to perovskite deposition, the substrate surface was treated with an ultraviolet ozone system (UV-O3) for 20 minutes.

In a typical fabrication process, the perovskite layer was deposited onto the substrate via a two-step spin-coating method. Firstly, the PAU eutectic precursor was spin-coated at 4500 rpm for 15 seconds. Subsequently, an MAI/IPA solution (60 mg mL−1) was drop-cast onto the spinning substrate for 10 seconds, followed by further spin-coating at 5500 rpm for 25 seconds. The resulting perovskite film was then annealed at 120 °C for 20 minutes.

The crystal structure of the MAPbI3 films was examined using X-ray diffraction (XRD, Bruker D8 ADVANCE, Cu Kα radiation) over the 2θ range of 10–70° with a step size of 0.02° s−1. Diffraction patterns were further analyzed by the Rietveld refinement method implemented in FullProf software to simulate patterns and calculate phase composition. In addition, FTIR (Bruker ALPHA II) was employed to detect the possible presence of residual PAU components on the perovskite films after the two-step spin coating process. Optical absorption spectra were measured using an Agilent 8453 UV-vis spectrophotometer. The surface and cross-sectional morphologies of the films were characterized by field emission scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM, Hitachi S4800, Japan). Film stability was evaluated under ambient conditions (23 °C, 30–40% relative humidity). Furthermore, changes in crystallinity and phase composition over time was monitored by re-examining the perovskite films after 30 and 60 days by XRD under identical conditions with diffraction patterns subjected to Rietveld refinement.

Fabrication and characterization of RRAM devices

The 100 nm-thick Ag top electrode was deposited by magnetron sputtering using a shadow mask at a current of 0.1 A for 30 seconds to complete the RRAM device with the architecture FTO/perovskite/Ag (Fig. 1c).

The current–voltage (I–V) characteristics of the fabricated RRAM devices were measured under dark conditions and at room temperature using a Keithley 2400 SourceMeter. Measurements were conducted over the following voltage ranges: −0.5 V → 0.5 V, −1 V → 1 V, −1.5 V → 1.5 V, −3 V → 3 V, and −5 V → 5 V, following a sweep sequence of −V → 0 V → +V → 0 V → −V. Device endurance (cycling stability) and retention (data storage stability) were systematically investigated at a read voltage of 250 mV.

Charge-transport mechanisms were analyzed from the DC I–V curves. The slope S of the ln I–ln V plot was extracted by linear fitting of each region. The regions were further examined using the Schottky-emission model (ln I vs. V1/2) and the space-charge-limited-current (SCLC) model (I vs. V2). Each fit was performed over the voltage window indicated in the corresponding figure, and the coefficient of determination (R2) is reported as a measure of linearity.

Results and discussion

Formulation of the eutectic PAU mixture

FTIR analysis of the PAU mixture displayed a spectrum with broad and intense absorption bands in the range of 3200–3500 cm−1 (Fig. 2), which are attributed to symmetric and asymmetric N–H stretching vibrations of the NH2 group, originating from both acetamide and urea. The shift from corresponding bands around 3300–3400 cm−1 in the spectra of pure acetamide and urea33,34 suggests the formation of hydrogen bonding within the mixture, as well as possible interactions with ions NO3−. The absorption bands in the region of 2750–2850 cm−1 correspond to C–H stretching vibrations of methyl groups present in acetamide. These are primarily associated with acetamide and, to a lesser extent, urea as the corresponding bands in pure liquids usually appear near 1690 cm−1 and around 1670 cm−1, respectively. Shifts or broadening in this region indicate the formation of strong hydrogen bonds, commonly found in eutectic mixtures where CO groups participate in hydrogen bonding with NH2 groups or NO3− ions.35 Bending and wagging vibrations of NH2 groups are observed in the 1500–1600 cm−1 region, contributed by both acetamide and urea. In the range of 1350–1450 cm−1, signals corresponding to CH3 bending modes and the symmetric stretching of NO3− ions are found, compared to symmetric stretching bands around 1350–1380 cm−1 typically exhibited by nitrate ions. Finally, the absorption bands below 1000 cm−1 are associated with fingerprint region vibrations, including NO3− bending modes and other complex vibrational modes involving interactions among the eutectic components.36

The PAU mixture exhibits characteristics of a DESs-like system, formed through the interactions between Pb(NO3)2 and the hydrogen bond-donating molecules acetamide and urea. Upon heating the initial solid-state PAU mixture to 60 °C, extensive hydrogen bonding networks and coordination complexes are established between Pb2+ ions and the amide/urea functional groups. These interactions disrupt the intrinsic crystal lattices of the individual components, resulting in a pronounced melting-point depression that is characteristic of DESs formation.37

We propose that the eutectic formation mechanism proceeds as follows: Pb2+ cations coordinate with nitrogen and oxygen atoms in acetamide and urea, while nitrate anions participate in bridging interactions and hydrogen bonding. In parallel, the cooperative hydrogen bonding network among urea and acetamide molecules further stabilizes these interactions, giving rise to a robust molecular network. This extended network enables the solid mixture to transition into a homogeneous liquid upon moderate heating and maintains the liquid state even at room temperature due to the eutectic nature of the mixture.

The solid–liquid transition reflects the overcoming of the lattice energies of the individual solids through the enthalpic gain associated with hydrogen bonding and coordination interactions. This gives rise to a thermodynamically stable liquid phase with a melting point substantially lower than that of pure Pb(NO3)2, acetamide, or urea. Similar eutectic behavior has been reported for metal salt systems combined with amide and urea, highlighting that the balance between coordination interactions and hydrogen bonding is the key determinant for low temperature liquid-phase formation.38 Molecular dynamics simulations also support the presence of a dynamic yet ordered hydrogen bonded network, contributing to the fluid-like nature of the system.39

FTIR analysis of the perovskite film synthesized from the eutectic PAU mixture (Fig. 2) showed absorption bands in the range of 3200–3300 cm−1, which are attributed to N–H stretching vibrations, primarily originating from the methylammonium (MA+) cation, with potential contributions from NH2 groups present in residual acetamide or urea. In the 2800–2900 cm−1 region, C–H stretching vibrations associated with the methyl group in the MA+ cation are clearly observed. The band in the 1600–1700 cm−1 region is assigned to the bending vibration of the NH3 group, which may also overlap with the CO stretching vibrations from residual acetamide or urea.40 The observed overlap at 1650 cm−1 may arise from the interaction between the CO (urea and/or acetamide) group and Pb(NO3)2 as well as MAI during perovskite synthesis, resulting in the CO peak shifting compared with the eutectic PAU mixture (1659 cm−1).41 The peaks located around 1400–1450 cm−1 correspond to the bending vibrations of CH3 groups and symmetric bending of the NH3 group, which are characteristic signatures of the methylammonium cation.42 Additionally, peaks in the 900–1000 cm−1 range are associated with the rocking modes of CH3 and NH3 groups within the MA+ structure. These spectral features indicate that a residual amount of acetamide and urea from the precursor mixture may remain on the surface or within the bulk of the perovskite film. Altogether, the FTIR characterization strongly indicates that the PAU mixture forms a DESs-like eutectic system stabilized by extensive hydrogen bonding and coordination interactions, which persist partially during perovskite film formation, thereby facilitating the formation of a homogeneous liquid precursor and contributing to the structural integrity and stability of the resulting perovskite films.

Structural characterization of MAPbI3 perovskite film fabricated using the PAU mixture

A tetragonal crystal phase was apparent in the X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns of the MAPbI3 perovskite film synthesized from the PAU precursor. The characteristic peaks at 2θ = 14.15°, 23.40°, 24.52°, 28.57°, and 31.89°, correspond to the (110), (211), (202), (220), and (222) planes, respectively (Fig. 3), confirming that the MAPbI3 films derived from PAU precursor crystallize in the tetragonal phase.43 Notably, the perovskite film derived from the PAU precursor exhibits enhanced phase purity and better crystallization, as evidenced by the markedly stronger intensity of the (110) diffraction peak at 2θ = 14.15°. Meanwhile, the characteristic diffraction signal of PbI2 at 2θ = 12.6° was barely detectable, indicating superior conversion efficiency with the PAU precursor.

X-ray diffraction patterns of perovskite films from the eutectic PAU mixture (the black line shows the experimental XRD pattern and the red one indicates the Rietveld refinement).

The average crystallite size was estimated using the Debye–Scherrer equation:where D is the average crystallite size (nm), K is the shape factor (typically ∼0.9), λ is the X-ray wavelength (Å), β is the full width at half maximum (FWHM) in radians, and θ is the Bragg diffraction angle. Based on the FWHM values of the five most intense diffraction peaks in the sample, the calculated average crystallite sizes for the perovskite films prepared from PAU precursor were 96.59 nm. The phase composition of perovskite films (Table S1) and calculated unit cell parameters (Table S2) suggest that trace amounts of urea might remain in the perovskite films after the two-step spin coating process, implying that the DES mixture was not completely eliminated during the second coating step. In contrast, acetamide most likely evaporated or completely decomposed as it was not detected in the final film.

The XRD pattern of the perovskite film showed the appearance of a peak at 12.12°, which corresponds to the hydrate phase (Fig. 3). This weak hydrate signal could be a result of the presence of urea and acetamide in the precursors.44 Using urea and acetamide in the perovskite synthesized from the PAU precursor results in larger crystallite sizes and enhanced intensities of the characteristic diffraction peaks. These findings indicate that employing multicomponent precursors for perovskite film fabrication improves the crystallite size and promotes the preferential growth of lattice planes associated with the characteristic tetragonal phase (Tables S1 and S2).

The PAU mixture serves as a medium that provides highly stabilized and homogeneously dispersed Pb2+ cations without inducing premature PbI2 crystallization, which leads to uniform PbI2 formation when reacting with MAI during the initial stage. The controlled release of Pb2+ from the PAU phase during the two-step spin coating process promotes a more complete conversion to MAPbI3, enabling a regulated and efficient formation of PbI2 intermediates. The stabilized state of Pb2+ in PAU facilitates uniform diffusion and reaction with MAI, accelerating the overall transformation and minimizing the amount of residual PbI2.

This behavior resembles the mechanism observed when DMSO is added to perovskite precursor solutions to delay PbI2 formation. DMSO forms strong coordination complexes with PbI2, generating PbI2-DMSO adducts or intermediate phases that retard crystallization and nucleation. Such coordination-driven retardation of perovskite crystallization enables enhanced control over nucleation kinetics, ultimately producing high-quality perovskite films.45

UV-vis analysis of the perovskite film derived from the PAU precursor exhibited strong absorption in the visible region, with an absorption edge near 780–800 nm, which is characteristic of MAPbI3 perovskite (Fig. 4). The optical bandgap values, estimated using the Tauc plot method, were found to be 1.53 eV. Notably, these films exhibited broader, higher absorption across the visible to near-infrared (NIR) region, particularly in the 600–800 nm range. This enhanced light-harvesting capability suggests that the PAU precursor improves the optical quality of the perovskite film. The improved absorption is likely associated with better crystallinity (Table S2), or enhanced film uniformity (Fig. 5).

Using SEM to analyze the structure of the MAPbI3 film synthesized from the PAU precursor, we observed an average grain size of about 0.6 µm, with a compact and aggregated morphology in which individual grain boundaries were less distinguishable. Cross-sectional SEM images revealed that the grains are stacked in multiple layers, forming a dense film with an approximate thickness of 4.5 µm. These results suggest that acetamide and urea may act as crystallization modulators, slowing down the perovskite formation process and promoting the growth of larger grains. The delayed conversion mechanism contributes to improved film morphology and increased thickness, which is consistent with the UV-vis absorption spectrum characteristic of thick films as discussed previously. Additionally, the residual urea in the film formed an encapsulating structure that surrounded the perovskite grains, covering both the surface and grain boundaries.

The formation of an ultrathin organic layer encapsulating the perovskite film is likely driven by self-assembly processes governed by strong hydrogen bonding and the surface affinity of the organic molecules during or after perovskite crystallization. Urea contains Lewis-basic functional groups (–NH2 and CO) capable of coordinating with undercoordinated Pb2+ sites on the perovskite surface, effectively passivating surface defects and suppressing nonradiative recombination pathways, in agreement with previous reports on similar systems.46

During nucleation and crystal growth of MAPbI3, urea molecules present in the precursor medium or residual solvent tend to migrate toward grain boundaries and grain surfaces due to the polarity of the amide group. At these locations, urea forms a dense hydrogen bonded network, creating a thin organic shell surrounding the perovskite grains. The DESs-like environment of PAU further promotes this encapsulation process by enabling homogeneous precursor distribution, controlling nucleation, and retaining urea at the surface rather than allowing its incorporation into the bulk film.41

This results in a core–shell like morphology in which the perovskite core is shielded by a stabilizing urea layer, providing moisture resistance and significantly improving film stability. Similar surface reconstruction and shell-stabilization mechanisms have been reported in halide perovskite nanocrystals in Zhu's study47 as well as in various passivation and encapsulation strategies employing surfactants and polymers have also been investigated.48–50 These findings support the interpretation that the urea shell in our system arises naturally from synergistic effects involving coordination chemistry, hydrogen bonding, and the unique PAU precursor environment. The resulting organic overlayer enhances stability by offering physical protection against moisture and environmental degradation while chemically passivating surface defects.

Investigating the stability of perovskite film over time

To probe the stability of the perovskite films prepared from the PAU precursor over time, we subjected three films fabricated and stored under identical conditions at room temperature up to 60 days to XRD analysis (Fig. 6). Throughout the monitoring period, the perovskite samples exhibited strong diffraction peaks at 14.15°, 28.57°, and 31.89°, corresponding to the (110), (220), and (222) planes, which are characteristic signals of the tetragonal perovskite phase.44

X-ray diffraction patterns of perovskite films stored at 23 °C, 30–40% relative humidity over 60 days.

For samples stored after 30 and 60 days, XRD analysis showed that an additional diffraction peak appeared at 12.80°, assigned to the (100) plane of PbI2. The intensity of this peak increased progressively over time, indicating a substantial rise in PbI2 content from about 3.1% initially to 48.0% after 30 days and reaching 85.1% after 60 days, when most of the perovskite had decomposed into PbI2. Concurrently, the intensity of the characteristic perovskite peaks gradually decreased. In the freshly prepared MAPbI3 sample, the perovskite phases accounted for 96.7% in total. This value dropped markedly to about 50.7% and only 13.6% after 30 and 60 days, respectively. This clearly demonstrates that, upon prolonged exposure to air and ambient moisture, the perovskite films undergo a pronounced phase transformation, gradually converting from perovskite to PbI2 (Table 1).51

Evaluation of the applicability of MAPbI3 thin films in RRAM devices

To demonstrate the quality of the perovskite films produced by our PAU precursor-based method, we used them to fabricate RRAM devices and evaluated their performance. The resulting RRAM devices were investigated for their current–voltage (I–V) characteristics under a voltage sweeping sequence of −1.5 V → 0 V → +1.5 V → 0 V → −1.5 V (Fig. 7). In the negative-voltage region, the device exhibited a SET process, corresponding to the transition from the high-resistance state (HRS or OFF) to the low-resistance state (LRS or ON), with a SET voltage (VSET) of −0.63 V (Fig. 7b). The ON/OFF current ratio exceeded 102, indicating reliable switching performance. During the positive voltage sweep, the RESET process was observed, corresponding to the transition from LRS back to HRS with a VRESET = 0.57 V (Fig. 7b), showing a clear single-step RESET behavior. Due to its thicker perovskite layer, the device required a higher voltage to form and rupture conductive filaments (CFs) during both the SET and RESET processes. Nevertheless, our RRAM devices exhibit high stability, a broad memory window, and reliable switching repeatability (Fig. 7c), which can be attributed to the improved crystallinity and internal microstructure quality of the perovskite film.

(a) Linear and (b) logarithmic I–V characteristics of the device, (c) overlaid I–V cycling curves, (d) endurance and (e) retention performance evaluation.

To evaluate the stability of the device, we analyzed the distributions of HRS and LRS values and the repeatable bipolar resistive switching behavior over 100 cycles at a read voltage of 0.25 V (Fig. 7d). Data showed that this device maintained a clear separation between the HRS and LRS states with an ON/OFF current ratio of 103 after 100 cycles. Moreover, the device demonstrated excellent cycle-to-cycle reproducibility of the I–V characteristics, confirming its superior consistency and reliability in data retrieval. Additionally, the temporal stability of the device was evaluated through retention measurements under a constant read voltage of 250 mV, as shown in Fig. 7e, during which the current levels of both resistance states remained stable at approximately 5 × 10−3 A for LRS and 3 × 10−6 A for HRS over a retention time of 104 s, indicating that the device may offer real-time operational durability of up to 10 years.

In addition to low-field resistive switching capability, the device exhibited a high-field memory effect that appears to operate via a different mechanism from the low-field switching behavior. Because the initial voltage range of −3 V to +3 V (Fig. S3) showed no RESET transition in the negative region, a more negative range of −5 V to +3 V was applied to the top electrode to fully evaluate its behavior (Fig. 8).

(a) Linear and (b) logarithmic I–V characteristics of the device with voltage from −5 V to +3 V, (c) overlaid I–V cycling curves, and (d) endurance performance evaluation.

Within the new voltage range of −5 V to +3 V, the SET process occurred under positive bias at VSET = 1.95 V with an ION/OFF ratio of approximately 10, which is significantly lower than that observed in the low-voltage operation range (−1.5 V to +1.5 V). Under negative bias, the RESET process occurs at VRESET = −3.32 V, with an ILRS/HRS ratio of only around 3. The fact that the SET and RESET processes occurred under opposite voltage polarities in this high-voltage regime suggests a fundamentally different switching mechanism from that in low-voltage operation. Moreover, the markedly reduced ION/OFF ratios for both SET and RESET transitions indicate a shift in resistive-switching behavior from digital to analog as the applied voltage increases.

To quantify the switching uniformity, the statistical distributions of the switching voltages and resistance states were analysed (Fig. 9) VSET and VRESET follow narrow distributions centered at −0.64 V and 0.71 V under low-field operation and 1.84 V and −2.75 V under high-field operation. The HRS and LRS remain clearly distinguishable over 100 cycles and their cumulative probability curves show no overlap (Fig. S4).

Statistical distributions of the SET and RESET voltages under low-field and high-field operating conditions. (a and d) Box-and-scatter plots, (b and e) voltage histograms, and (c and f) cumulative probability distributions for the corresponding operating-field ranges.

The reproducibility of the resistive switching was further evaluated on 10 independently fabricated devices (Fig. S5). All 10 devices displayed the characteristic bipolar switching under low-field operation, and the same polarity reversal under high-field operation, indicating that the switching is a reproducible property of the DESs-derived MAPbI3 film. The residual variation is ascribed to the stochastic formation of conductive filaments and to thickness or morphology fluctuations of the spin-coated film.

Analysis of charge transport regimes under low- and high-field operation

Proposed resistive switching mechanism of the device under the operating voltage range of ±1.5 V

Intrinsic defects are inherently present within the perovskite film, including vacancies (VPb, VMA, VI), interstitials (Pbi, MAi, Ii) and anti-site substitutions (PbI, IPb). Among these, VI and Ii exhibit the lowest migration activation energies of 0.25 eV and 0.15 eV, respectively. In contrast, the migration activation energies of the remaining cations and anions in the MAPbI3 lattice namely I−, Pb2+ and MA+ are relatively higher, measured at 0.58 eV, 2.31 eV, and 0.84 eV, respectively. Therefore, the migration of iodine vacancies (VI) is considered the dominant factor governing the resistive switching mechanism.58

When the device is biased within the range of −1.5 V to 1.5 V, the SET process occurs under negative bias at −0.63 V, characterized by an abrupt transition from the HRS to the LRS. The pronounced SET process yields an ION/OFF ratio of approximately two orders of magnitude, a characteristic feature of digital-type memory switching. The digital switching behavior can be attributed to the migration of VI vacancies, which is in good agreement with the observation that the SET process initiates under negative bias, consistent with the findings reported by Yiming Sun et al.59

The formation of conductive filaments, or the SET process, is initiated by applying a negative potential to the top electrode (TE) (Fig. 11a). Under the influence of the electric field, the pre-existing defects in the active layer, such as iodine vacancies (VI), drift toward the TE. With the continuous migration of VI, conductive paths composed of iodine vacancy chains are gradually established, which can be regarded as filamentary conduction channels. This results in a significant enhancement of the device's conductivity. The RESET process occurs upon reversal of the applied electric field polarity at the TE. During this stage, VI migrates back into the perovskite layer toward the TE, rupturing the filaments and thereby switching the device from the LRS to the HRS.60

Proposed resistive switching mechanism of the device under the operating voltage range of −5 V to +3 V

At higher operating voltages ranging from −5 V to 3 V, the electric field applied to the device becomes much stronger than that under the low-field condition (±1.5 V). The I–V characteristics exhibit a reversal in the resistive-switching polarity, with the SET process occurring under positive bias and the RESET process under negative bias. In this regime, the formation of conductive filaments is no longer dominated by VI-mediated pathways but is primarily associated with ionic migration. The strong electric field provides sufficient energy to generate iodine ions and iodine vacancies (VI), or even to induce the diffusion of Ag+ ions from the top electrode toward the bottom electrode, leading to more complex filament formation mechanisms.

The formation of conductive filaments, or the SET process, is initiated by applying a positive potential to the top electrode (TE) (Fig. 11b). Under the influence of the electric field, I− ions are displaced from their original lattice sites, leaving behind positively charged iodine vacancies (VI) within the perovskite layer. These I− ions gradually drift toward the bottom electrode (BE), while the chain of VI vacancies migrates toward the BE. With continued ion migration, conductive paths comprising iodine vacancies are progressively established, which can be regarded as filamentary conduction channels. This leads to a significant increase in the device's electrical conductivity. The RESET process occurs upon reversal of the applied electric field polarity at the TE. During this stage, I− ions migrate back into the perovskite layer toward the BE, where they recombine with the VI vacancies. This recombination disrupts the previously formed conduction channels, rupturing the filaments and thereby switching the device back from the LRS to the HRS.

In addition to the I−/VI mediated mechanism, resistive switching under high electric fields may also proceed via an electrochemical metallization (ECM) mechanism (Fig. 11c). At sufficiently high fields, the applied energy is adequate to drive the migration of Ag+ ions across the perovskite layer. When a positive bias is applied to the TE, electrochemical oxidation of Ag occurs, in which Ag atoms at the TE are oxidized to Ag+ cations. These Ag+ cation species subsequently diffuse into the perovskite matrix or through the grain boundaries of the perovskite layer under the influence of the electric field. Simultaneously, the negative potential at the BE attracts Ag+ cations, which are reduced back to metallic Ag. The reduced Ag atoms accumulate progressively until metallic filaments are formed, bridging the TE and BE, thereby switching the device into the low resistance state (LRS). Upon reversing the bias polarity, the electrochemical dissolution of the metallic filaments takes place. The filaments are disrupted as electrons are withdrawn toward the TE, leading to filament rupture and returning the device from the LRS to the HRS, i.e., the RESET process.

However, both of the aforementioned mechanisms exhibit certain inconsistencies. If the device operated solely through the formation of vacancy-mediated (VI) conductive channels, its memory characteristics should resemble those observed under low field operation. In contrast, when operated under high electric fields, the device follows an analog switching mechanism that is distinctly different from the digital switching behavior observed in the low-field range (−1.5 V to 1.5 V). This discrepancy may arise from the interaction between Ag+ and I−, whereby I− ions migrating toward the top electrode react to form AgIx. The formation of AgIx hinders the reverse migration of I− ions toward the bottom electrode upon field reversal. As a result, a higher voltage is required to initiate the RESET process, and only partial rupture of the conductive filaments formed during SET occurs, leading to a reduced ION/OFF ratio in the RESET state.58 Consequently, the formation of the AgIx interfacial layer not only impedes the RESET process but also explains the contrasting behavior in the 1 V to 1.5 V region when comparing low field and high field operations. While this positive voltage range is sufficient to rupture the preexisting VI filament (RESET) in the low-voltage cycle, the increased interfacial resistance induced by AgIx in the high-voltage cycle shifts the required activation energy for new filament formation (SET) to a much higher threshold.

On the other hand, the formation of Ag filaments also encounters significant constraints. Previous studies have demonstrated that when the film thickness approaches the micrometer scale, the formation of conductive Ag filaments become highly unlikely. In the present case, the film thickness of approximately 4 µm suppresses Ag filament formation due to the long diffusion path of Ag+ ions. Additionally, the organic passivation layer surrounding the grains plays a critical role in limiting the interfacial contact between the Ag electrode and the perovskite layer, thereby significantly reducing Ag+ diffusivity.

Nevertheless, considering the combined effects of film thickness, grain boundaries, and surface passivation, it cannot be excluded that the resistive switching observed in the −5 V to 3 V range may involve a hybrid mechanism, where both VI and Ag+ mediated conductive filaments contribute to the switching behavior. Under such conditions, the large film thickness may hinder complete Ag+ migration across the film, preventing full filament formation and, in turn, affecting the reversibility of the resistive states.

Therefore, to better account for the I–V characteristics, a hybrid switching mechanism is proposed in which both Ag+ ions and VI vacancies cooperatively participate in filament formation within the device (Fig. 11d). When a large positive bias is applied to the top electrode (TE), two parallel processes occur. First, Ag+ ions diffuse from the TE into the perovskite layer and initiate the formation of a conductive filament extending from the TE. Simultaneously, I− ions, driven by the electric field, migrate toward the TE, leaving behind a chain of positively charged iodine vacancies (VI) that gradually drift toward the TE. This process results in the formation of a VI mediated filament extending from the BE to the TE. These two types of filaments subsequently merge to establish a unified conduction path, giving rise to resistive switching behavior governed by the cooperative contribution of both VI vacancies and Ag+ ions.

In addition to the SET process, the RESET process in the device is particularly difficult to achieve, with an ION/OFF ratio of only about three. The incomplete RESET process and the low ION/OFF ratio observed in the device can be further elucidated by considering the interfacial reaction between the Ag top electrode and iodide ions. Under a strong electric field, I− ions migrate toward the top electrode, where they are trapped and chemically react with Ag to form AgIx. This interfacial AgIx layer acts as an ionic reservoir that immobilizes the migrated I−, effectively suppressing their back-diffusion into the perovskite layer and hindering recombination with iodine vacancies. Similar interfacial phenomena have been reported in perovskite-based optoelectronic devices, where AgI formation passivates the interface and reduces ion migration, but at the cost of interfacial conductivity and reversibility.61 In the context of resistive switching, this chemical stabilization translates into a persistent VI mediated conductive filament that cannot be fully ruptured during the RESET operation.

Furthermore, the presence of AgIx alters the local electric field distribution and introduces a significant interfacial resistance, thereby impeding the redissolution of the filament and leading to gradual rather than abrupt conductance changes. This scenario aligns with the analog-type resistive-switching behavior typically observed under high-field operation. A similar field-driven halide migration mechanism coupled with interfacial ion trapping has been described in other perovskite-based RRAM systems, where the interplay between halide ions and vacancies determines the transition between digital and analog switching regimes.58 It is also noteworthy that microstructural factors – such as grain boundaries and defect density – may accelerate the migration of I− toward the electrode, facilitating the formation of AgIx and exacerbating the RESET inefficiency. Once AgIx forms, its limited ionic conductivity and chemical stability prevent rapid dissolution under reverse bias, resulting in a quasi-permanent alteration of the interfacial chemistry. As a consequence, the filament rupture becomes incomplete, leading to low switching contrast and cycle-to-cycle variability. To improve the RESET efficiency, interface engineering strategies such as inserting a thin buffer layer or employing a less reactive top electrode (e.g., Au or carbon) could be adopted to suppress Ag–I interactions while maintaining sufficient electronic contact.

In summary, the formation of AgIx at the perovskite/electrode interface not only governs the kinetics of ion migration but also fundamentally modifies the resistive switching pathway, from a reversible vacancy-mediated mechanism at low bias to a hybrid, chemically stabilized, analog-type switching under strong electric fields.

Conclusion

This study developed MAPbI3 perovskite thin films from a novel Pb(NO3)2–acetamide–urea (PAU) deep eutectic precursor via a simple two-step spin coating process with no requirement of stringent temperature and humidity control. The resulting films exhibited high conversion efficiency with a negligible amount of residual PbI2 and enhanced durability compared to those obtained via conventional sequential deposition methods, which can be attributed to the presence of residual organic components in the precursor that act as a protective layer, effectively protecting the perovskite structure. When integrated into RRAM devices, the films demonstrated reliable bipolar resistive switching with excellent ON/OFF ratios, endurance, retention, and the ability to operate in both digital and analog switching modes. The dual-mode behavior is ascribed to the voltage-activated electrochemical contribution of the reactive Ag electrode on top of intrinsic vacancy-mediated switching, a hypothesis that warrants verification with inert-electrode devices in future work. It should be noted that, while the PAU-based route substantially reduces the hazards associated with volatile toxic organic solvents, it does not eliminate the intrinsic toxicity of the lead-containing precursor, a limitation shared with other high-performance Pb-based perovskite systems. Overall, our findings present a simple PAU precursor-based synthesis of stable, high-quality perovskite films that can broaden the applicability of MAPbI3 from photovoltaics to multifunctional next-generation memory technologies.
