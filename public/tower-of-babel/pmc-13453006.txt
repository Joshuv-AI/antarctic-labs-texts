Uhrf1 in PDGFRα-lineage cells regulates osteophyte formation in osteoarthritis
Authors: Akihiro Jono, Yuta Yanagihara, Hiroshi Sakai, Noritaka Saeki, Tatsuhiko Kutsuna, Tomofumi Kinoshita, Akiyoshi Uezumi, Masaki Takao, Yuuki Imai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryOsteophytes are a characteristic feature of osteoarthritis (OA), and clarifying their molecular regulation may contribute to preventive and therapeutic strategies. Uhrf1 (ubiquitin-like containing PHD and RING finger domains 1), a regulator of DNA methylation maintenance, is indispensable for chondrocyte proliferation and differentiation in the growth plate. Because osteophytes develop via endochondral ossification, we hypothesized that Uhrf1 is involved in osteophyte formation. platelet-derived growth factor receptor α (PDGFRα)-lineage cell-specific Uhrf1-knockout mice showed that Uhrf1 regulates the proliferation and chondrogenic potential of synovial PDGFR-α-lineage cells during osteophyte development, influencing osteophyte formation. Furthermore, experiments using human synovial cells revealed that UHRF1 maintains DNA methylation and identified NLK (nemo-like kinase) as a candidate gene that may be regulated by UHRF1-mediated DNA methylation. These findings suggest that UHRF1 may modulate Wnt signaling and chondrogenic differentiation through the regulation of NLK. Together, these results highlight the role of Uhrf1 in osteophyte formation and provide insight into the mechanisms underlying OA progression, suggesting Uhrf1-mediated pathways as targets for OA.

Introduction

Knee osteoarthritis (OA) is a condition of articular cartilage degeneration that increases in prevalence with age.1,2 Prevention and radical treatment of OA are very important to extend healthy life expectancy.3 Although the pathophysiology and pathogenic mechanisms of OA have been studied for a long time, much of its mechanisms remain elusive. Osteophytes are bony protrusions that form on the edges of the articular surface and are an important component of the staging of OA.4,5 Previously, osteophytes have been considered a biological defense response against joint instability and a byproduct of OA. In recent years, however, MRI studies have reported that osteophyte or chondrophyte formation itself can cause medial meniscal extrusion and is one of the causes of OA.6,7,8 Thus, it has been suggested that osteophytes may contribute to OA pathology, but the role and development of osteophytes in OA remain unclear.9 Therefore, it is important to elucidate the molecular mechanisms of osteophyte formation to investigate novel preventive and therapeutic strategies for OA.

Epigenetics is the concept that expresses the regulation of gene expression without alteration of the genomic DNA sequence. Accumulation of epigenetic abnormalities associated with aging is involved in the onset of many age-related diseases.10,11,12 The pathogenesis of OA, which develops with age, is related to the dysfunction of epigenetic regulation, such as DNA methylation.11,13 Ubiquitin-like containing PHD and RING finger domains 1 (Uhrf1) is an important molecule for the maintenance of DNA methylation and is responsible for recruiting DNA methyltransferase 1 (Dnmt1) to the hemi-methylated site.14,15,16,17 Our previous study revealed that limb-specific Uhrf1-knockout mice exhibited abnormal proliferation and chondrogenesis in the growth plate, resulting in limb growth defects.18 Osteophyte formation begins with synovial-derived cells around the articular cartilage, followed by synovial hypertrophy, chondrogenesis, and ossification.19 Additionally, we have confirmed and reported the columnar structure of cartilage, similar to growth plate cartilage, during osteophyte maturation using a surgically induced OA mouse model.20 Based on these findings, we hypothesized that Uhrf1, which affects the proliferation and chondrogenesis in the growth plate, may play an important role in osteophyte formation. Here, we present findings suggesting that osteophytes may be associated with OA progression and demonstrate that Uhrf1 is involved in osteophyte formation.

Results

Uhrf1 is expressed in PDGFRα-positive cells located in the hypertrophic synovium in the early stages of osteophyte formation

To observe Uhrf1 localization during osteophyte formation, we performed immunohistochemistry against Uhrf1 in osteophytes induced by the destabilization of the medial meniscus (DMM) in the DMM mouse model. Knee joints including osteophytes were collected at 4 days, 1 week, and 2 weeks post-DMM. Uhrf1 expressed within the hypertrophic synovium at day 4 post-DMM, which is a very early stage of OA. Furthermore, Uhrf1 expression was proportional to the number of Ki67-positive proliferating cells and peaked at 1 week post-DMM. A previous report showed that cells expressing platelet-derived growth factor receptor α (PDGFRα), a marker of mesenchymal progenitors in the synovium, are the origin of osteophytes.21 Therefore, we co-stained PDGFRα and Uhrf1 to investigate the relationship between these two molecules. Uhrf1 expression was observed in some of the PDGFRα-positive cells in the hypertrophic synovium (Figure 1B). These results suggest that Uhrf1, which is expressed in PDGFRα-positive cells in the hypertrophied synovium at an early stage of OA, may be involved in osteophyte formation.

PDGFRα-lineage cell-specific Uhrf1-knockout mice exhibited reduced osteophyte formation

To confirm the effect of Uhrf1 on osteophyte formation, PDGFRα-lineage cell-specific Uhrf1-knockout (cKO) mice were generated by crossing PDGFRα-CreER mice with Uhrf1 flox or mTmG mice. Control (Ctrl) and cKO mice were administered tamoxifen for 5 days at 11 weeks of age and then subjected to DMM at 12 weeks of age. Samples were collected 2 weeks after DMM. At first, the recombination efficiency and Uhrf1 suppression were validated in PDGFRα-positive cells isolated by fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) from lower limb muscles, where sufficient cell numbers could be obtained for quantitative analysis. Real-time reverse-transcription PCR (RT-PCR) and immunocytochemical staining revealed that Uhrf1 expression levels in PDGFRα-positive cells were significantly decreased at both mRNA (Figure 2A) and protein levels (Figures 2B and 2C). Importantly, lineage-tracing analysis using PDGFRαCreER/+; mTmGflox/+ reporter mice demonstrated that Cre-recombined PDGFRα-lineage cells were abundantly present within early osteophytes and the surrounding synovium at 1 week after DMM, suggesting that PDGFRα-lineage cells contribute to the cellular population involved in osteophyte initiation (Figure S1). This finding supports that the Cre-mediated recombination occurs in PDGFRα-lineage cells present within early osteophytes and the surrounding synovium in vivo. Histologically, osteophytes at the medial tibial articular surface were smaller in cKO mice than in Ctrl mice (Figure 2D). Quantitative evaluation of osteophyte formation was performed according to our previously reported methods.20 Osteophyte width (Figure 2E), osteophyte area (Figure 2F), and modified maturity score (Figure 2G) were slightly suppressed in cKO mice compared with Ctrl mice at each evaluation point. The area under the curve (AUC) for each item in cKO mice was significantly lower than that in Ctrl mice (Figures 2E, 2F, and 2G). Inhibition of osteophyte formation in cKO mice is maintained until 12 weeks after DMM and exacerbates articular cartilage degeneration

To confirm whether the inhibition of osteophyte formation observed in cKO mice 2 weeks after DMM was maintained even after osteophyte maturation, a 12-week post-DMM model was evaluated in the same manner. Osteophytes at the medial tibial articular surface in cKO mice were slightly smaller than those in Ctrl mice, and cKO mice exhibited more degenerative medial tibial articular cartilage than Ctrl mice (Figure 3A). Osteophyte width (Figure 3B) and osteophyte area (Figure 3C) were significantly lower in cKO mice than in Ctrl mice, indicating that osteophyte formation remained inhibited even 12 weeks after DMM. Notably, this occurred despite no difference in the osteophyte maturity score (Figure 3D) between the groups. These results indicate that Uhrf1 in PDGFRα-lineage cells contributes to osteophyte formation but not to osteophyte maturation over the longer term. Degeneration of articular cartilage was significantly more severe on the medial tibial plateau of cKO mice than in Ctrl mice (Figure 3E), suggesting an association between reduced osteophyte formation in early stage of OA and increased articular degeneration. To determine whether synovial inflammation contributed to the exacerbated cartilage degeneration observed in cKO mice at 12 weeks after DMM surgery, histological evaluation of synovitis was performed. Synovial sections from cKO and Ctrl mice (n = 7 per group) were assessed independently by two observers (AJ and YY), each performing the evaluation twice in a blinded manner. Synovitis was scored according to previously published criteria, including hyperplasia, sublining cellularity, and fibrosis.22 No significant differences were detected between the two groups in any of the evaluated parameters (Figure S2). Loss of UHRF1 in human knee synovial cells reduces DNA methylation and the proliferative and chondrogenic differentiation potential

To further explore the role of Uhrf1 underlying the osteophyte formation process observed in cKO mice, the proliferative potential of human synovial cells collected from OA patients and their ability to differentiate into chondrocytes were analyzed. We knocked down UHRF1 in knee synovial cells using small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) and that confirmed UHRF1 expression in the knockdown cells. As a result, UHRF1 expression was successfully decreased at both mRNA (Figure 4A) and protein levels (Figures 4B and 4C). As UHRF1 is responsible for maintaining DNA methylation, we assessed the yield of enriched methylated DNA, using the methyl-CpG-binding domain (MBD)-based methylated DNA enrichment assay. UHRF1-knockdown cells showed a significantly reduced yield of enriched methylated DNA compared with control cells (Figure 4D). In addition, UHRF1-knockdown cells showed suppressed proliferation (Figure 4E) and chondrogenesis (Figure 4F) as well as the cKO mouse phenotype. UHRF1 deficiency negatively regulates cell development and growth

To elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying the impaired proliferation and differentiation of UHRF1-deficient synovial cells, RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) was performed using control and UHRF1-knockdown human knee synovial cells. Principal component analysis (PCA) demonstrated a clear separation between the control and UHRF1-knockdown cells, indicating distinct transcriptional profiles (Figure 5A). In UHRF1-knockdown cells, 408 genes were significantly upregulated, and 742 genes were significantly downregulated compared with controls (p < 0.01, >2-fold change) (Figure 5B). Although the yield of enriched methylated DNA was reduced following UHRF1 knockdown—an alteration generally expected to derepress gene expression—the number of downregulated genes was higher than that of upregulated ones. This finding suggests that the loss of UHRF1 induces secondary transcriptional dysregulation associated with the disruption of epigenomic stability. Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis using Metascape revealed that the upregulated genes were significantly associated with pathways such as cytokine signaling in the immune system, negative regulation of cell development, and negative regulation of growth (Figure 5C), indicating the induction of anti-proliferative and suppressive transcriptional programs. In contrast, GO enrichment analysis of the downregulated genes showed enrichment in categories including “NABA CORE MATRISOME and NABA MATRISOME ASSOCIATED,” “skeletal system development,” “cell migration,” “cell adhesion,” “growth factor responsiveness,” and “cell population proliferation” (Figure S3). These biological processes are closely related to the maintenance of cellular function, proliferative responsiveness, and tissue-forming capacity, suggesting that UHRF1 deficiency impairs the development and functional potential of human synovial cells. NLK is a candidate gene associated with UHRF1-mediated DNA methylation changes during chondrogenic differentiation

UHRF1 deficiency is expected to reduce DNA methylation and promote gene expression (Figure 6A). Therefore, to confirm the UHRF1 deficiency-specific decrease in DNA methylation regions, we performed a comprehensive analysis of methylated DNA loci, using MBD-seq obtained from the control and knockdown cells. First, MBD-seq identified genes with UHRF1-mediated DNA methylation peaks located within 5 kb of the transcription start site (TSS). Then, comparison among three patients revealed 2,402 genes in common (Figure 6B). Among these 2,402 genes, 42 overlapped with the 408 genes that were upregulated in the RNA-seq results (Figures 6C and S5). GO and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analyses of these 42 genes revealed the enrichment of terms related to cell proliferation, differentiation, and cartilage matrix metabolism, such as negative regulation of TORC1 signaling, the mTOR signaling pathway, β-catenin independent WNT signaling and glycosaminoglycan metabolism (Figures 6D and S4). The genes included in these terms are shown in Figure 6E. In particular, NLK, WNT5B, SIRT7, GNG11, SDC1, and INPP5F have been previously reported to be involved in cell growth and differentiation (Figure 6E).23,24,25,26,27,28 Among them, NLK was the most significantly upregulated gene with reduced DNA methylation, as revealed by the RNA-seq and MBD-seq results (Figures 6F and 6G). Therefore, we conducted a rescue experiment to confirm whether the knockdown of NLK along with UHRF1 would restore β-catenin expression, cell proliferation, or chondrocyte differentiation potential. To investigate whether UHRF1 and NLK influence Wnt-related signaling, β-catenin expression was evaluated by western blotting using UHRF1-knockdown cells and UHRF1/NLK simultaneous knockdown cells. Quantitative analysis revealed that the simultaneous knockdown of UHRF1 and NLK significantly increased β-catenin expression relative to UHRF1-knockdown cells alone, suggesting a partial restoration of signaling activity (Figures 6H and 6I). Although the proliferation ability was not rescued (Figure 6J), knockdown of NLK significantly increased Alcian blue-positive staining areas in the micromass culture that also were affected by UHRF1 knockdown (Figure 6K). Consistent with this phenotypic recovery, real-time RT-PCR analysis demonstrated that the expression of chondrogenic marker genes, including SOX9 and COL10A1, was significantly increased in cells subjected to simultaneous knockdown of UHRF1 and NLK compared with that in UHRF1-knockdown cells, while the expression of other cartilage-related genes showed a similar increasing trend (Figure S6). These findings indicate that the inhibition of NLK partially restores the chondrogenic transcriptional program suppressed by UHRF1 deficiency. Discussion

This study explored the role of Uhrf1 in regulating the proliferation and chondrogenesis of PDGFRα-lineage cells within the synovium during the early stage of synovial hypertrophy, followed by osteophyte formation. The near absence of Uhrf1-positive cells in osteophytes after chondrogenesis suggests that the osteophyte characteristics may be determined at an early stage of their formation (Figure 1A). Our previous study revealed that Uhrf1 serves as an indispensable factor for normal limb growth by regulating chondrocyte proliferation and differentiation in the growth plate.18 This observation supports the idea that UHRF1-dependent regulation is also important for osteophyte formation, which similarly occurs through endochondral ossification. Accumulating evidence from cancer biology shows that UHRF1 supports cell proliferation by maintaining the normal DNA methylation patterns and chromatin structure.29 Consistent with this role, our RNA-seq analysis of human synovial cells demonstrated that UHRF1 knockdown increased the expression of several genes known to suppress cell development and growth (Figure 5C).

Interestingly, despite evidence of reduced DNA methylation following UHRF1 knockdown, many genes were downregulated (Figure 5B), indicating that the loss of UHRF1 does not simply derepress transcription but instead induces widespread epigenetic instability. These findings suggest that UHRF1 functions as an epigenetic stabilizer required for maintaining cellular homeostasis.

To identify genes potentially affected by methylation changes associated with UHRF1 deficiency, we combined MBD-seq and RNA-seq analyses and focused on genes that showed both decreased methylation near their TSSs and increased expression. Among these candidates, NLK showed the strongest upregulation. NLK is known to regulate non-canonical Wnt signaling and interfere with β-catenin-dependent transcription.30,31 Previously, it was reported that upregulation of the non-canonical WNT signaling pathway inhibits endochondral ossification,32 and the non-canonical rather than the canonical WNT signaling pathway regulates the fate of skeletal precursor differentiation.33 In our experiments, knockdown of NLK, which was elevated in association with Uhrf1 deficiency, partially restored β-catenin levels (Figure 6I). These results suggest that increased NLK expression contributes to the altered Wnt signaling observed under UHRF1-deficient conditions.

Functionally, simultaneous knockdown of UHRF1 and NLK restored chondrogenic differentiation, as evidenced by the recovery of Alcian blue staining (Figure 6K) and increased expression of chondrogenic markers such as SOX9 and COL10A1 (Figure S6). In contrast, cell proliferation was not restored by NLK suppression (Figure 6J). This dissociation indicates that the impaired proliferation caused by UHRF1 loss likely reflects global epigenetic dysregulation, whereas NLK more specifically influences the lineage commitment toward chondrogenesis. Together, these findings suggest that UHRF1 deficiency may lead to increased NLK expression through reduced DNA methylation, thereby contributing to chondrogenic differentiation defects.

In vivo, PDGFRα-lineage cell-specific Uhrf1-knockout mice showed sustained suppression of osteophyte formation, even 12 weeks after DMM, accompanied by exacerbated cartilage degeneration on the medial tibial plateau (Figures 3A–3E). Previous studies have suggested that osteophytes may contribute to joint adaptation by compensating for mechanical instability.4,34 However, our data do not establish a direct protective role of osteophytes. Because UHRF1 deficiency simultaneously impairs the cellular proliferation and differentiation capacity, the accelerated OA phenotype may reflect compromised tissue repair mechanisms rather than reduced osteophyte formation itself. Thus, reduced osteophyte formation and OA progression should be interpreted as parallel consequences of disrupted UHRF1-mediated cellular homeostasis.

Taken together, our findings support a model in which UHRF1 contributes to the maintenance of epigenetic homeostasis and proper differentiation of PDGFRα-lineage cells during osteophyte formation. Loss of UHRF1 disrupts this balance and is associated with increased NLK expression, altered Wnt signaling, impaired chondrogenic commitment, and reduced osteophyte formation (Figure 7). These results provide new insight into how epigenetic regulation coordinates PDGFRα-lineage cell behavior during OA pathogenesis and identify the UHRF1-NLK axis as a potential molecular pathway involved in osteophyte development.Figure 7Graphical abstractUHRF1 contributes to the chondrogenic differentiation of PDGFRα-lineage cells during early osteophyte formation stages and is associated with altered NLK expression. Limitations of the study

There are several limitations to this study. First, the DMM model induces osteophyte formation and cartilage degeneration secondary to surgically created medial meniscus instability. Therefore, this model neither allows us to determine whether osteophytes themselves modify the joint mechanics or disease progression nor can distinguish whether the worsened OA observed in Uhrf1-deficient mice is a direct consequence of reduced osteophyte formation or instead reflects the impaired proliferation and functional capacity of PDGFRα-lineage cells within synovium. As such, a causal relationship between osteophytes and cartilage protection cannot be established in this study. In addition, because DMM represents a post-traumatic OA model, the relevance of these findings to early or idiopathic OA remains uncertain. Second, although our data suggest that UHRF1 contributes to the proliferation and chondrogenic differentiation of human synovial cells, the downstream mechanisms are not fully elucidated. While NLK was identified as a candidate mediator linking UHRF1-dependent epigenetic regulation to altered Wnt signaling, the failure of NLK suppression to rescue proliferation indicates that additional epigenetic targets and signaling pathways must be involved. Third, our analyses were primarily based on bulk molecular and functional assays and, thus, do not resolve cellular heterogeneity or lineage dynamics during osteophyte development. Future studies using single-cell approaches and direct validation of gene expression changes within synovial PDGFRα-lineage cells are required. Finally, all animal experiments were performed using male mice, and all human synovial samples were obtained from female patients. Therefore, potential sex-related differences in UHRF1 function and osteophyte formation were not evaluated and should be addressed in future studies.

Experimental model and study participant details

Animals

C57BL/6J mice were obtained from CLEA Japan (Tokyo, Japan). PDGFRαCreER/+ mice, ROSA26-mTmG reporter mice and Uhrf1flox/flox mice were obtained from The Jackson Laboratory Japan, Inc. (Kanagawa, Japan) and EUCOMM. Uhrf1flox/flox mice were crossed with PDGFRαCreER/+ mice to generate Uhrf1flox/flox mice as a Ctrl and PDGFRαCreER/+; Uhrf1flox/flox mice as a cKO. To induce Uhrf1 deletion in adult mice, 11-week-old mice were intraperitoneally injected with 100 μL of tamoxifen solution (20 mg/mL) for 5 consecutive days. To confirm the knockout efficiency of Uhrf1 in cKO mice, 3–4 mice were used for each analysis. For the evaluation at 2 weeks after DMM, 10 mice were prepared for both the control and cKO groups. One mouse from each group was excluded due to patellar dislocation observed at the time of evaluation, resulting in a final sample size of 9 mice per group. For the 12-week post-DMM evaluation, 7 mice were prepared for both the control and cKO groups, and all mice were included in the analysis. To confirm the Cre recombination efficiency of PDGFRαCreER/+ mice, ROSA26-mTmG reporter mice were crossed with PDGFRαCreER/+ mice to generate PDGFRαCreER/+; mTmGflox/+ reporter mice. All experiments and analyses in this study were conducted using male mice.

These mice were housed under controlled conditions at room temperature (23 ± 2°C) and a 12-h light/dark cycle and were provided with water and a standard diet. Experiments using these mice were conducted in accordance with the Ehime University Animal Experiment Committee (Approval No. 37A1-1/16) and in accordance with the Ehime University Animal Experiment Guidelines.

Human samples

The study was conducted with the permission of the Ehime University Ethics Committee (Approval No. 2307001). With informed consent, synovial tissue samples were obtained from seven patients with end-stage knee osteoarthritis (Kellgren–Lawrence grade 4) who underwent total knee arthroplasty and were used for subsequent experiments. All patients were female, with a mean age of 82 years (range, 74–89 years). Only patients with medial knee joint osteoarthritis were included. In vitro experiments were conducted using all or part of the synovial samples derived from the seven patients. The collected synovial membrane was immediately washed with medium, the heat coagulated portion was trimmed, cut into small pieces, and reacted with type 4 collagenase for 2 h, after which suspended tissues were removed using a 40 μm filter and divided into 10 cm dishes. The next day, suspended cells were removed and collected using 0.04% trypsin and stored frozen at −80°C.

Method details

Surgical procedures of osteophyte formation

Osteophyte formation on the medial tibial articular surface was induced in mice by destabilization of the medial meniscus (DMM) at 12 weeks of age as previously reported.35 Briefly, the medial meniscotibial ligament was resected from the tibial attachment using a 25G needle and the anterior angle of the medial meniscus was completely extruded.

Histology and histomorphometry

Mouse knees were harvested and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (Wako, Osaka, Japan) at 90o of flexion for 24 h and then replaced with 100% ethanol for 24 h. Demineralization was then completed with Morse’s solution for 3 days. The paraffin-embedded knee joints were sectioned and stained with Safranin O/Fast green36 and evaluated according to previously reported methods.20 Briefly, mouse knees were sectioned every 100 μm from anterior to posterior. Osteophytes were evaluated in all sections and cartilage degeneration was evaluated in the most severe of all sections. Osteophytes were evaluated by Osteophyte Width, Osteophyte Area, and Modified Maturity Score, and cartilage degeneration was evaluated using the OARSI Score.

Immunofluorescence staining

Immunofluorescence staining was performed on tissue specimens by autoclaving with 0.5% ImmunoSaver (Wako, Osaka, Japan) for 45 min at 85°C and blocking by treatment with a solution of 1% BSA and 0.02% Triton in PBS for 1 h. Primary antibodies were mouse anti-Uhrf1 monoclonal antibody (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, TX, USA; 1:400), rat anti-KI67 monoclonal antibody (Synaptic systems, Goettingen, Germany; 1:400), goat anti-PDGFRα monoclonal antibody (R&D Systems, MSP, USA; 1:400), rat anti-GFP monoclonal antibody (R&D Systems, MSP, USA; 1:400). Tissues were incubated overnight at 4°C. Specimens were then incubated for 1 h at room temperature with secondary antibodies, Alexa Fluor 488 goat anti-mouse IgG (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA; 1:1000), Alexa Fluor 568 goat anti-mouse IgG (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA; 1:1000), Alexa Fluor 488 goat anti-rat IgG (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA; 1:1000), Alexa Fluor 568 goat anti-rat IgG (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA; 1:1000), and Alexa Fluor 555 Donkey anti-goat IgG (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA; 1:1000). Autofluorescence was then blocked by treatment with Vector TrueVIEW Autofluorescence Quenching Kit (Funakoshi, Tokyo, Japan) for 5 min. Finally, VECTASHIELD Vibrance Antifade Mounting Medium with DAPI (Funakoshi, Tokyo, Japan) was used for encapsulation and nuclear staining. The autoclaving step was excluded for cell specimens.

Flow cytometry

Mouse leg muscles were harvested and treated with collagenase type 2 (Worthington Biochemical Corporation, NJ, USA) for 1 h, then filtered through a 40 μm filter to isolate cells. The isolated cells were suspended in 200 μL of washing buffer and fluorescently labeled by adding 5 μL of anti-CD31(Biolegend, SAN, USA), 5 μL of anti-CD45 (Biolegend, SAN, USA), and 15 μL of anti-PDGFRα (R&D Systems, MSP, USA) and reacting at 4°C for 1 h. CD31, CD45-negative and PDGFRα-positive cells were collected using a FACSAria instrument (Becton Dickinson, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA).

Real-time RT-PCR

Total RNA was extracted with ISOGEN (NIPPON GENE, Tokyo, Japan) and a RNeasy spin column kit (QIAGEN, N.V, Venlo, Netherlands). Single-stranded cDNA was synthesized from total RNA using PrimeScript RT Master Mix (Takara Bio Inc., Shiga, Japan), and then real-time RT-PCR was performed using TB Green Premix Ex Taq II (Takara Bio Inc., Shiga, Japan) and Thermal Cycler Dice Real Time System III (Takara Bio Inc., Shiga, Japan) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Gene expression levels were normalized relative to the expression level of the housekeeping gene Rplp13 or GAPDH. Primer sequences are shown in Table S1.

siRNA treatment

Cells were transfected with non-targeted siRNA (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA) and two siRNAs targeting UHRF1 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA) using Lipofectamine RNAiMAX (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. In vitro chondrogenesis assays were performed using 24-well plates, whereas all other experiments involving siRNA transfection were conducted using 6-well plates. For transfection in 24-well plates, 3 μL of Lipofectamine RNAiMAX and 1 μL of siRNA (10 μM) were diluted in 100 μL of Opti-MEM (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA), incubated for 5 min, and added to 500 μL of culture medium per well.

For transfection in 6-well plates, 9 μL of Lipofectamine RNAiMAX and 3 μL of siRNA (10 μM) were diluted in 300 μL of Opti-MEM, incubated for 5 min at room temperature, and then added to 2 mL of culture medium per well.

In vitro proliferation assay

Human knee synovial cells obtained from seven patients were seeded into 6-well plates at a density of 2 × 104 cells per well. The next day, the medium was changed, and siRNA was added. Three days after the initial siRNA addition, medium was changed and siRNA was added, and cells were harvested at day 7 after the initial siRNA addition. Cell counts were performed and calculated as % control with the siCtrl sample as 100%.

In vitro chondrogenesis assay

Human knee synovial cells from five of the seven patients were prepared as micromass cultures at a density of 1 × 105 cells in 10 μL and plated in 24-well plates. After confirming cell attachment, 500 μL of culture medium supplemented with recombinant human BMP-2 and siRNA (100 ng/mL) was added, and the cells were cultured for 21 days to evaluate chondrogenic differentiation. One patient-derived sample was excluded from quantitative analysis because the cells detached from the plate during the experimental period. After 21 days of culture, the cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 5 min. After washing with PBS twice, the fixed cells were rinsed with 3% acetic acid for 5 min, stained with 1% Alcian blue (Sigma-Aldrich) in 3% acetic acid for 30 min, and then washed with 3% acetic acid. Quantitative evaluation was performed by measuring the area of Alcian blue staining using Fiji (v2.3.0) (https://imagej.net/Fiji). To eliminate variation among patient samples, calculations were made as % control with the siCtrl sample set at 100%.

Western blotting

For the experiment confirming siUHRF1 knockdown shown in Figure 4B, human synovial cells from three of the seven patients were treated with siRNA targeting UHRF1 for one week, washed with PBS, and lysed in RIPA buffer (Wako, Osaka, Japan) containing a protease inhibitor (Nacalai Tesque, Inc., Kyoto, Japan). After lysis, samples were crushed using ultrasound at 12,500 bpm for 10 min. Protein concentration was determined using the Pierce BCA protein assay kit (Life Technologies, MA, USA). Samples were applied to SuperSep Ace polyacrylamide gels (Wako, Osaka, Japan) and transferred to PVDF membranes (Bio-Rad Laboratories, CA, USA) by electrophoresis. The membranes were blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin (Wako, Osaka, Japan) in TBS containing 0.05% Tween 20 (TBS-T) for 1 h at room temperature. The membranes were reacted with primary antibodies [anti-Uhrf1 (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, TX, USA; 1:1000), anti-βactin (MBL, Tokyo, Japan; 1:2000), anti-βactin (MBL, Tokyo, Japan; 1:2000)] at 4°C overnight. After washing with TBS-T, membranes were incubated with secondary antibodies, anti–mouse IgG (PROMEGA, WI, USA; 1:5000) for 1 h at room temperature. ECL Prime Western Blotting Detection Reagent (Cytiva, Tokyo, Japan) was used to detect signals, and membranes were imaged using ImageQuant LAS 4000 (GE Healthcare, IL, USA). Quantification of the intensity was determined using Fiji (v2.3.0) (https://imagej.net/Fiji). For the NLK rescue experiment shown in Figure 6H, five patient samples were used and processed using the same protocol as described above. The membranes were reacted with primary antibodies [mouse anti-βcatenin (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, TX, USA; 1:1000), rabbit anti-GAPDH (Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA; 1:2000)], followed by incubation with secondary antibodies, anti–mouse IgG (PROMEGA, WI, USA; 1:5000) and anti–rabbit IgG (PROMEGA, WI, USA; 1:5000).

RNA-seq

Human knee synovial cells from five of the seven patients were seeded into 6-well plates at a density of 2 × 104 cells per well. siRNA was administered the following day, and the cells were harvested after one week. The culture medium was replaced every three days, and siRNA was added twice weekly. Cells were collected using ISOGEN (NIPPON GENE, Tokyo, Japan). RNA extraction and RNA-seq were performed by the Medical Institute of Bioregulation, Kyushu University. The resulting count data files were analyzed with iDEP2.0 using the DESeq2 package. GO enrichment analysis of up-regulated genes (log2FC > 1, p < 0.01) in UHRF1 knockdown cells was performed using Metascape.37 The RNA-seq dataset was deposited in GEO under accession number GSE296850.

Methylated DNA enrichment assay

Human knee synovial cells from three patients, which were a subset of the five patients used for RNA-seq analysis were seeded into 6-well plates at a density of 2 × 104 cells per well. siRNA was administered the following day, and the cells were harvested after one week. The medium was changed every 3 days, and siRNA was added twice/week. DNA was collected using NucleoSpin Tissue kits (Takara Bio Inc., Shiga, Japan) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. DNA was fragmented with a Covaris sonicator and methylated DNA was enriched using the EpiXplore Methylated DNA Enrichment Kit (Takara Bio Inc., Shiga, Japan) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The yield of enriched methylated DNA was quantified by fluorescent labeling with the QuantiFluor dsDNA System (Promega, WI, USA) and measured using a Quantus Fluorometer (Promega, WI, USA).

MBD-seq

MBD-seq is an analytic tool for understanding genome-wide methylated regions using methyl-CpG-binding domain protein 2 (MBD2), which can bind methylated CpG. For MBD-seq analysis, methylated DNA enriched using a methylated DNA enrichment assay was obtained from three patients, which were a subset of the five patients used for RNA-seq analysis, and subjected to sequencing. Libraries for MBD-seq analysis were prepared using the NEBNext Ultra II DNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina and NEBNext Multiplex Oligos for Illumina (96 Unique Dual Index Primer Pairs) (NEB) according to the manufacturer’s instructions and validated for an average size of about 520–620 bp using the Agilent High Sensitivity D1000 ScreenTape kit. The libraries were sequenced using NovaSeq 6000 with 150 bp paired end reads. The reads were mapped using CLC Genomics Workbench Premium (QIAGEN, N.V, Venlo, Netherlands). Peak calling was performed using CLC Genomics Workbench Premium with human knee synovial cells treated with siCtrl as “Treatment” and siUHRF1 as “control” to detect regions of DNA hypomethylation due to UHRF1 deficiency. DNA hypomethylation regions were visualized using Integrative Genomics Viewer (IGV).38 The MBD-seq dataset was deposited in GEO under accession number GSE297163.

Integrated analysis of RNA-seq and MBD-seq data

To detect common genes across three samples, we drew a Venn diagram using the genes with DNA hypomethylation within 5 kb of the TSS by UHRF1 deficiency from the MBD-seq data. To identify the genes whose expression was increased by DNA hypomethylation induced by UHRF1 deficiency, we conducted integrative analysis using RNA-seq and MBD-seq data.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Statistical analysis

Sample sizes were not based on power calculations. The sample sizes were determined empirically based on our previous experience and review of similar experiments in the literature. Statistical analyses were performed using Prism (GraphPad Software Inc, CA, USA). The normality of the two datasets was evaluated using a Kolmogorov-Smirnov test and a Shapiro–Wilk test at a significance level of 0.05. When the normality was or was not confirmed in the groups, a two-tailed Student’s t test or a Mann–Whitney U-test, respectively, was applied. Similarly, for comparisons of three groups, ordinary one-way ANOVA and Tukey’s multiple comparisons tests were used when normality was confirmed, and the Kruskal-Wallis test and Dunn’s multiple comparisons test were used when normality was not confirmed. For all graphs, data are represented as the mean ± s.d. Statistical significance was accepted when p < 0.05.
