Successful VA-ECMO rescue of life-threatening cardiotoxicity following paclitaxel and cisplatin neoadjuvant chemotherapy for laryngeal cancer: a case report
Authors: Yan Qian, Youjun Pan, Zhiru Che, Ke Fang, Yingya Cao, Xiaogan Jiang, Weihua Lu, Qiancheng Xu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNeoadjuvant chemotherapy with paclitaxel and cisplatin (TP regimen) is a cornerstone of treatment for locally advanced laryngeal squamous cell carcinoma. While effective, this regimen can, in rare instances, induce multiple adverse events, including a life-threatening fulminant myocarditis. Characterized by sudden and severe cardiac inflammation leading to cardiogenic shock and arrhythmias, this complication carries a high mortality rate. Its non-specific initial symptoms often mimic more common conditions like acute coronary syndrome, posing significant diagnostic and therapeutic challenges for oncologists and critical care teams.Case presentationWe describe the case of a 59-year-old male with laryngeal cancer who, after completing three cycles of neoadjuvant TP chemotherapy and undergoing a total laryngectomy, presented with acute-onset dyspnea and chest pain. His condition rapidly deteriorated into profound cardiogenic shock, characterized by severe hypotension refractory to maximal vasopressor support, and a third-degree atrioventricular block necessitating temporary cardiac pacing. Diagnostic investigations revealed a severely depressed left ventricular ejection fraction (< 30%) with global hypokinesis on echocardiography and exceptionally high serum high-sensitivity troponin I levels (> 27 ng/mL). The patient developed multi-organ failure, including acute kidney and liver injury requiring continuous renal replacement therapy. After emergent coronary angiography ruled out obstructive coronary artery disease and extensive microbiological screenings were negative, a diagnosis of chemotherapy-induced fulminant myocarditis was established. Veno-arterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (VA-ECMO) was promptly initiated to provide biventricular support and restore end-organ perfusion, alongside high-dose corticosteroids and intravenous immunoglobulin. The patient’s myocardial function substantially improved over eight days, leading to successful weaning from VA-ECMO. He was subsequently discharged with complete cardiac and renal function recovery.ConclusionThis case describes fulminant myocarditis with cardiogenic shock and high-grade atrioventricular block following neoadjuvant paclitaxel-cisplatin chemotherapy for laryngeal cancer. Early initiation of VA-ECMO, together with intensive supportive care and immunosuppressive therapy, was associated with complete recovery of cardiac and renal function. Background

Neoadjuvant chemotherapy is an established strategy for treating locally advanced laryngeal cancer, a common head and neck malignancy [1, 2]. The paclitaxel and cisplatin (TP) combination is a widely used regimen that has proven effective in improving locoregional control and survival rates [3, 4].

While the toxicities of these agents are well-documented, severe cardiotoxicity is uncommon. Paclitaxel is associated with cardiac events, most commonly asymptomatic bradycardia and conduction abnormalities, while severe complications like left ventricular dysfunction are less frequent [5, 6]. For instance, one study found that cardiotoxicity was typically mild (grade 1–2), with no severe cases reported in its cohort [7]. Cisplatin, while primarily known for nephrotoxicity and ototoxicity, can also induce cardiotoxicity. Reported cardiovascular complications include myocardial infarction, heart failure, and arrhythmias, with an estimated incidence of 1.0–6% [6, 8, 9]. Moreover, cisplatin can cause electrolyte disturbances and thromboembolic events that indirectly affect cardiac function [9]. However, the development of fulminant myocarditis leading to cardiogenic shock and high-grade atrioventricular block (AVB) from the TP regimen is exceedingly rare.

Veno-arterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (VA-ECMO) provides temporary mechanical circulatory support for patients with refractory cardiogenic shock. It serves as a bridge to myocardial recovery or other definitive therapies [10–12]. Its role as a potentially lifesaving rescue therapy is well established for refractory shock resulting from various cardiotoxic medication overdoses, such as those involving beta‑blockers and calcium channel blockers [13–15]. Building on this principle, while its use in chemotherapy-induced cardiotoxicity is less common, it is similarly emerging as a critical intervention for these severe cases [16–18].

Herein, we report the case of a laryngeal cancer patient who developed fulminant myocarditis, progressing to cardiogenic shock and third-degree AVB, after three cycles of neoadjuvant TP chemotherapy. He was successfully rescued with the prompt initiation of VA-ECMO. This report highlights a critical and rare complication of a commonly used TP regimen and underscores the potential life‑saving role of VA‑ECMO in this setting.

Case presentation

A 59-year-old male with a history of laryngeal cancer presented with a two-day history of progressive chest pain and dyspnea. He had been otherwise healthy, and his routine health examination earlier this year showed no significant abnormalities, including a normal resting electrocardiogram and unremarkable laboratory tests. Transthoracic echocardiography at that time demonstrated normal left ventricular size and wall thickness, preserved global systolic function with a left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) of 60–65%, and no regional wall motion abnormalities. The right ventricle was normal in size and systolic function, and both atria were of normal dimensions. Cardiac valves had normal morphology and motion, without significant stenosis or regurgitation. There was no pericardial effusion, and the estimated pulmonary artery systolic pressure was within the normal range. He had recently completed three cycles of neoadjuvant chemotherapy with paclitaxel and cisplatin. Following this, he underwent a total laryngectomy with tracheostomy on August 21, 2024 and was discharged on postoperative Day 6. However, he subsequently developed low-grade fever, chest tightness, and anorexia. On the following day (postoperative Day 7), he required urgent readmission to a local hospital for acute dyspnea and hypotension.

An electrocardiogram (ECG) at the local hospital revealed a third-degree AVB. His cardiac biomarkers were markedly elevated: high-sensitivity troponin I (hs-TnI) was 29.128 ng/mL, myoglobin was 280.330 ng/mL, and creatine kinase-MB was 58.270 U/L. The initial diagnosis was cardiogenic shock and respiratory failure, suspected to be secondary to an acute myocardial infarction. Emergent coronary angiography revealed only mild stenosis of the left anterior descending artery, effectively ruling out obstructive coronary disease. A temporary pacemaker (ventricular demand inhibited mode) was implanted. Despite these interventions, his condition deteriorated rapidly with persistent cardiogenic shock and frequent ventricular tachycardia, necessitating transfer to our tertiary care center for potential veno-arterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (VA-ECMO) support. He arrived at our center’s intensive care unit in the early morning of postoperative Day 8 (Hospital Day 1 at our institution).

On admission to our center, the patient was lethargic and critically ill. His vital signs revealed severe hypotension (60/50 mmHg), requiring high-dose triple vasopressor support (norepinephrine 0.89 µg/kg/min, epinephrine 0.36 µg/kg/min, and dopamine 15 µg/kg/min). He had a heart rate of 100 beats/min and was normothermic (36.0 °C). Physical examination revealed coarse breath sounds with bilateral moist rales. Significant laboratory findings (Table 1) included leukocytosis (10.5 × 10⁹/L), neutrophilia (8.2 × 10⁹/L), and an elevated C-reactive protein (114.45 mg/L). Markers of severe cardiac injury and multi-organ dysfunction were prominent. High-sensitivity troponin I (hs-TnI) was > 27.533 ng/mL. Indicators of organ failure included a creatinine of 152.6 µmol/L, lactate dehydrogenase of 7594 U/L, and profoundly elevated liver enzymes (aspartate aminotransferase 3287 U/L, alanine aminotransferase 1733 U/L). Arterial blood gas analysis showed a mixed metabolic acidosis and respiratory alkalosis with hyperlactatemia (5.4 mmol/L) and hyperkalemia (5.7 mmol/L). A point-of-care echocardiogram demonstrated diffuse left ventricular hypokinesis with a severely reduced ejection fraction of < 30% (Video 1), confirming profound cardiogenic shock. Right ventricular systolic function was also markedly impaired. There were no significant valvular lesions, no pericardial effusion, and no intracardiac thrombus or mass. Pulmonary artery systolic pressure could not be reliably quantified due to an inadequate tricuspid regurgitation signal; however, right heart size and interventricular septal motion did not suggest severe pulmonary hypertension. The admission ECG (Fig. 1A) showed an accelerated idioventricular rhythm and a complete right bundle branch block (RBBB).

Our admission diagnoses included fulminant myocarditis, cardiogenic shock, acute respiratory failure, and acute kidney injury. Due to refractory shock and worsening ventricular arrhythmias, the decision was made to initiate VA-ECMO. VA-ECMO was started on Hospital Day 1 via percutaneous cannulation of the femoral artery and femoral vein. Supportive care included mechanical ventilation, systemic anticoagulation, and continuous renal replacement therapy. During the shock and VA-ECMO phase, management focused on circulatory stabilization, with vasopressors/inotropes, invasive mechanical ventilation, and continuous renal replacement therapy, in addition to guideline-consistent systemic anticoagulation for ECMO. Guideline-directed chronic heart failure medications (such as angiotensin‑converting enzyme inhibitors and β-blockers) were temporarily withheld at this stage because of profound hypotension and dependence on vasopressor support.

The diagnosis of fulminant myocarditis was established based on the clinical presentation, rapid deterioration, and evidence of severe myocardial injury in the absence of obstructive coronary disease. Endomyocardial biopsy (EMB) was not performed due to high procedural risks. The patient required VA-ECMO with systemic anticoagulation and had developed shock-related coagulopathy and hepatic dysfunction, which substantially increased the risk of bleeding and ventricular perforation. Furthermore, since high-dose immunosuppressive therapy had already been initiated, biopsy findings were unlikely to alter the acute management plan. An extensive etiological workup was performed to exclude infectious causes. The patient reported no prodromal infectious symptoms. Viral serologies and polymerase chain reaction testing for common cardiotropic viruses (including Epstein-Barr virus, influenza, and Coxsackie B virus) were negative. Next-generation sequencing of a blood sample also failed to identify any causative pathogens. Given the temporal association with his recent chemotherapy and the negative infectious workup, chemotherapy-induced fulminant myocarditis was determined to be the most likely diagnosis. Consequently, adjunctive immunosuppressive therapy was initiated on Hospital Day 2, consisting of intravenous immunoglobulin (20 g/day for 5 days) and high-dose methylprednisolone (160 mg/day, followed by a taper).

By Hospital Day 5, the patient showed signs of significant myocardial recovery (Table 1). Cardiac injury markers declined (hs-TnI to 17.976 ng/mL), and echocardiography revealed improved left ventricle systolic function. On Hospital Day 6, repeat transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated substantial recovery of biventricular systolic function, with an estimated LVEF of approximately 40% and only mild residual segmental hypokinesia. Right ventricular systolic function had normalized, and there remained no significant valvular disease, no pericardial effusion, and no intracardiac thrombus or mass. Pulmonary artery pressure still could not be precisely measured, but there were no echocardiographic signs of severe pulmonary hypertension. An ECG showed a return to sinus rhythm, though the complete RBBB persisted (Fig. 1B). Vasopressors were successfully weaned and discontinued. After 8 days of mechanical circulatory support, the patient passed a weaning trial, and VA-ECMO was successfully decannulated on Hospital Day 8 (clinical course detailed in Fig. 2).

Post-decannulation, he was transitioned to high-flow nasal cannula. His renal function gradually improved, though he initially required intermittent hemodialysis. Once medically stable, he was discharged back to a local hospital for continued rehabilitation on Hospital Day 9. After transfer, low-dose angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitor and low-dose β-blocker therapy were cautiously initiated and titrated as tolerated. At a 2-month outpatient follow-up, the patient reported being in good general condition with no cardiopulmonary limitations. His renal function had fully recovered, and he had resumed his oncological treatment at another institution. A follow-up transthoracic echocardiogram performed at the local hospital reported a normalized LVEF of 55%, normal right ventricular size and function, no regional wall motion abnormalities, no significant valvular pathology, no pericardial effusion, and no intracardiac masses, indicating complete recovery of biventricular function.

Discussion

We report an exceedingly rare case of a 59-year-old male with laryngeal cancer who developed fulminant myocarditis, leading to cardiogenic shock and high-grade AVB, following neoadjuvant TP chemotherapy. The patient was successfully managed with VA-ECMO and adjunctive therapies. This case highlights two critical points: the potential for catastrophic cardiotoxicity with this common chemotherapy regimen and the life-saving role of advanced mechanical circulatory support in managing it.

The diagnosis of fulminant myocarditis was established based on the acute onset of severe biventricular dysfunction, markedly elevated cardiac biomarkers, and profound cardiogenic shock, alongside significant electrocardiographic abnormalities mimicking acute myocardial infarction [19, 20]. After emergent coronary angiography excluded acute myocardial infarction, the temporal relationship with recent chemotherapy, combined with a negative workup for infectious etiologies, strongly implicated chemotherapy as the trigger. Severe malignant arrhythmias, such as ventricular tachycardia, were considered secondary to inflammatory myocardial injury rather than the primary cause of cardiogenic shock. They appeared concurrently with profound biventricular dysfunction and resolved in parallel once systolic function normalized under VA-ECMO and immunosuppressive therapy. This clinical course supports myocarditis‑related electrical instability as the underlying mechanism, rather than arrhythmia‑induced cardiomyopathy.

EMB is the gold standard for confirming myocarditis [21, 22]. The 2025 European Society of Cardiology Guidelines on Myocarditis and Pericardial Diseases recommend EMB in high‑risk cases when histologic or virologic findings are expected to influence management, provided the procedural risk is acceptable [23]. In this patient, several factors made EMB unsafe. First, he presented with refractory cardiogenic shock that required VA‑ECMO and full systemic anticoagulation. Second, severe hepatic injury caused marked coagulopathy, further increasing the risk of bleeding. Third, the expected complication rate for EMB in fulminant myocarditis under mechanical circulatory support can reach 26%, including perforation, tamponade, and fatal arrhythmias [6, 24]. Given the strong temporal association with chemotherapy, the negative results of extensive viral and pathogen screening, and the early initiation of maximal immunosuppressive therapy with high-dose corticosteroids and immunoglobulin, histological information was unlikely to change acute management. The patient achieved rapid clinical recovery, with complete normalization of biventricular systolic function within one week. These factors substantially reduced the ethical and clinical justification for invasive biopsy. Therefore, the diagnosis was established through an integrated assessment of clinical, laboratory and imaging evidence. The findings were consistent with immune-mediated fulminant myocarditis induced by paclitaxel and cisplatin, and the decision-making process adhered to guideline-based evaluation of risk and benefit for EMB.

Paclitaxel and cisplatin are cornerstones in cancer treatment [3, 4], but their cardiotoxic profiles rarely include such a severe presentation [5, 6, 24]. The ST‑segment elevations seen in our patient likely represent a “pseudo‑infarction” pattern resulting from severe myocardial inflammation rather than coronary occlusion [21, 22]. The onset after three cycles and major surgery suggests a potential cumulative effect or a “second hit,” where surgical stress may have exacerbated underlying chemotherapy-induced myocardial damage, a timing consistent with other reports of acute paclitaxel-induced cardiotoxicity [20, 25, 26]. Severe malignant arrhythmias, such as ventricular tachycardia, were considered secondary to this inflammatory myocardial injury, as they appeared concurrently with biventricular dysfunction and resolved as systolic function normalized.

The successful use of VA-ECMO in this context is supported by its established role as a rescue therapy for refractory shock from other cardiotoxic drug overdoses. Systematic reviews and meta-analyses have demonstrated that VA-ECMO is a potentially lifesaving intervention for patients with cardiac arrest or shock refractory to medical therapy from calcium-channel blocker and beta-blocker poisoning [27]. A 2025 systematic review of 157 patients found a pooled survival rate of 63% with VA-ECMO support for calcium-channel blocker toxicity [28]. A broader 2024 meta-analysis of 539 patients on ECMO for acute drug intoxication further confirmed its utility, reporting an overall survival to hospital discharge of 61.0%, which was even higher (68.8%) for patients receiving VA-ECMO [29]. Our case extends this established therapeutic paradigm, demonstrating that VA-ECMO can serve as an equally critical bridge to recovery in severe, refractory cardiogenic shock induced by chemotherapeutic agents, with the potential to achieve excellent long-term outcomes. These findings also suggest that VA‑ECMO should be considered in selected cases of chemotherapy‑related cardiotoxicity, particularly when the underlying myocardial injury is potentially reversible. For agents causing primarily inflammatory or transient injury, such as immune checkpoint inhibitors [16–18], [30–32] or 5-fluorouracil-induced vasospasm [33–36], VA‑ECMO can serve as an effective bridge to recovery. In contrast, for agents causing extensive direct cardiomyocyte necrosis with limited regenerative capacity, such as high‑dose cyclophosphamide [37, 38] or acute anthracycline toxicity [38–40], its role may be primarily as a bridge to more definitive therapies (e.g., durable mechanical circulatory support or transplantation) rather than to full myocardial recovery. Therefore, this case carries several important clinical implications. First, it underscores the need for a high index of suspicion for severe cardiotoxicity in patients receiving TP chemotherapy, particularly when they present with unexplained chest pain, dyspnea, arrhythmias, or hemodynamic instability. Second, it highlights the importance of rapid multidisciplinary coordination among oncologists, cardiologists, intensivists, and ECMO specialists. Third, it supports the early consideration of VA‑ECMO in refractory cardiogenic shock, before irreversible end‑organ damage occurs.

This case report has several limitations. As a single-patient observation, the findings cannot be generalized. The diagnosis of fulminant myocarditis was not confirmed by EMB, which is the gold standard; in this critically ill patient, the procedure was deemed too high-risk due to VA-ECMO support, systemic anticoagulation, and coagulopathy. Despite these constraints, the diagnosis was established from consistent clinical, laboratory, and imaging evidence, but histological confirmation would have increased diagnostic certainty.

Conclusion

Fulminant myocarditis can be a rare but life-threatening complication of chemotherapy, presenting with severe ventricular dysfunction and malignant arrhythmias. Early recognition, prompt initiation of mechanical circulatory support such as VA-ECMO, and tailored immunosuppressive therapy may lead to full recovery of cardiac function. This case highlights the importance of integrating clinical, laboratory, and imaging findings to guide diagnosis and management when histological confirmation is not feasible.
