Oral microbiota-responsive ZIF nanoplatform via double-layer glycans modified combined with PD-1 inhibitor for treatment of microsatellite-stable colorectal cancer
Authors: Yeyang Sun, Xietao Ye, Lin Yang, Shuo Dong, Chen Chen, Yani Wang, Jianan Qiao, Congyan Liu, Yuping Liu, Yan Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractProgrammed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) inhibitors are integral to contemporary cancer immunotherapy. However, their objective response rate in microsatellite-stable colorectal cancer (MSS CRC) remains below 5 %. This limited efficacy is primarily due to insufficient tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs), a consequence of the combined effects of low tumor immunogenicity and impaired dendritic cell (DC)-mediated antigen presentation. The combination of ursolic acid (Ua) and ginsenoside Rg3 (Rg3) enhances TIL infiltration by inducing immunogenic cell death (ICD) in tumor cells and promoting DC maturation. Due to their poor oral bioavailability, an oral ZIF nanoplatform was engineered with a chitosan oligosaccharide (Cos) and inulin (In) bilayer coating [(In/Cos)@Ua-Rg3-ZIF] for targeted delivery of Ua and Rg3 to the CRC microenvironment. Efficient dual-drug co-loading was achieved with a loading efficiency of 24.07 wt%. After oral administration, the (In/Cos) coating protects the formulation from degradation in the upper gastrointestinal tract, while specific enzymatic cleavage by colonic microbiota releases Ua-Rg3-ZIF into tumor tissues. The ZIF platform responds to elevated adenosine triphosphate (ATP) in the tumor microenvironment, enabling controlled release of Ua and Rg3 to enhance cytotoxic T lymphocyte (CTL) levels. In vivo studies demonstrate that combining (In/Cos)@Ua-Rg3-ZIF with α-PD-1 results in an 80.86 % tumor inhibition rate, significantly outperforming monotherapies. This strategy presents an innovative oral delivery system capable of resisting gastrointestinal degradation and facilitating microbiota-responsive drug release, effectively enhancing antitumor immunity and improving the therapeutic outcome of immunotherapy in MSS CRC.Keywords: Gut microbiota-responsive ZIF, Double-layer glycans, Ursolic acid, Ginsenoside Rg3, ICD, Antigen transport, PD-1 inhibitors, Microsatellite-stable colorectal cancer

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is the third-most prevalent malignancy worldwide, with a high mortality rate. Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), notably PD-1 inhibitors, have expanded treatment options for CRC [[1], [2], [3]]. However, their therapeutic efficacy is heavily influenced by the molecular subtypes of tumors. Approximately 85 % of patients are diagnosed with microsatellite-stable CRC (MSS CRC), which exhibits a minimal response to PD-1 blockade therapy [4].

PD-1 inhibitors exert anti-tumor effects by binding to tumor-infiltrating T lymphocytes (TILs). The infiltration of TILs into tumors is critically dependent on the antigen-recognition and antigen-presentation functions of antigen-presenting cells, particularly dendritic cells (DCs) [[5], [6], [7]]. Unfortunately, MSS CRC, characterized by a low tumor mutational burden and deficient antigen presentation, represents a prototypical immunologically cold tumor. This results in sparse TIL infiltration and primary resistance to PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors [8,9]. Enhancing tumor antigen content and promoting DC maturation to boost TIL infiltration and tumor recognition capabilities thus represents an optimal strategy to increase the response rate to PD-1 inhibitors.

Ursolic acid (Ua), a pentacyclic triterpenoid extracted from Actinidia arguta, induces immunogenic cell death (ICD) in tumor cells [10]. This process releases tumor antigens, enabling DCs to more effectively recognize and phagocytose antigens, thereby initiating an immune response [11]. Ginsenoside Rg3 (Rg3), a tetracyclic triterpenoid isolated from ginseng, promotes DC maturation and enhances their antigen-presentation functions [12]. Consequently, the synergistic effects of Ua and Rg3 are expected to elevate tumor antigen levels, promote DC maturation, enhance TIL infiltration into tumor tissues, and enable TILs to effectively recognize and eradicate tumor cells [13,14]. Given that CRC is a gastrointestinal (GI) tumor, oral administration is the preferred route due to its convenience, non-invasiveness, and favorable patient compliance [[15], [16], [17]]. However, the poor bioavailability and distinct physicochemical properties of these two drugs present significant challenges for their effective co-delivery to the tumor site via oral administration [18].

ZIF-90, a zeolitic imidazolate framework (ZIF) material, features a three-dimensional porous structure formed by the coordination of zinc ions with imidazole-2-carboxaldehyde ligands. This structure has an optimal pore and window size, which facilitates the co-encapsulation of Ua and Rg3 [19,20]. Furthermore, ZIF-90 responds to the elevated adenosine triphosphate (ATP) levels in the tumor microenvironment, leading to framework disintegration and controlled release of Ua and Rg3 [21,22]. Thus, ZIF is an ideal carrier for the co-delivery of Ua and Rg3 to the tumor microenvironment.

Due to ZIF's alkali-resistant yet acid-sensitive nature, its use in oral drug delivery systems faces the challenge of gastric acid erosion [23]. Natural glycans have garnered significant attention in drug delivery due to their ability to prevent premature absorption in the upper GI tract [24,25]. Among these, inulin (In) and chitosan oligosaccharide (Cos) are particularly notable for their excellent water solubility and biocompatibility [26,27]. Specifically, the complementary electrostatic interactions between negatively charged In and positively charged Cos enhance coating efficiency and protect the delivery system from gastric acid degradation [[28], [29], [30]]. To implement this strategy, a layer-by-layer (LBL) self-assembly approach was utilized to modify the surface of Ua- and Rg3-loaded ZIF nanoparticles with an In/Cos coating, resulting in the formation of (In/Cos)@Ua-Rg3-ZIF (Fig. 1 A).

Schematic diagram of (In/Cos)@Ua-Rg3-ZIF combined with α-PD-1 in the treatment of MSS CRC. (A) Preparation of (In/Cos)@Ua-Rg3-ZIF. (B) Oral Administration of (In/Cos)@Ua-Rg3-ZIF: in vivo processes and immunotherapeutic effects against CRC.

Upon oral administration, the double-layer glycans of (In/Cos)@Ua-Rg3-ZIF resist degradation by digestive fluids, pass through the upper GI tract, and accumulate in the colon. Microbiota-secreted enzymes subsequently degrade the In/Cos coating, triggering the release of Ua-Rg3-ZIF [31,32]. Notably, the degradation products of glycans modulate the colonic microbiota [33,34]. Once in the tumor tissue, the exposed Ua-Rg3-ZIF nanoparticles disintegrate in response to the elevated ATP levels of the tumor microenvironment, releasing Ua to induce ICD and generate tumor antigens, while Rg3 enhances the antigen-presentation capacity of DCs. The synergistic effects of these two drugs significantly promote TIL infiltration into the tumor. When combined with PD-1 antibodies, (In/Cos)@Ua-Rg3-ZIF demonstrates potent synergistic anti-tumor immunotherapeutic efficacy, overcoming the limitations of low response rates to PD-1 monotherapy in MSS CRC (Fig. 1 B). This study presents an innovative oral delivery platform that integrates microbiota-responsive drug release and immunotherapy for treating MSS CRC.

Materials and methods

Materials, cell lines, and animals

Zinc acetate [Zn (CH3(COO)2·2H2O, 99 % purity] was procured from Aladdin (Shanghai, China). Imidazole-2-carboxaldehyde (2-ICA, 99 % purity), chitooligosaccharides, trioctylamine were purchased from Shanghai Yien Chemical Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Ursolic acid and Ginsenoside Rg3 (purity >98 %) were obtained from Shanghai Yuanye Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Inulin was acquired from Macklin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Antibodies for FACS analysis, including KO525 Fixable Viability Dye, Purified anti-mouse CD16/32 Antibody, PB450 anti-mouse CD45 Antibody, FITC anti-mouse CD3 Antibody, BV510 anti-mouse CD4 Antibody, APC anti-mouse CD8a Antibody, PE anti-mouse Granzyme B Antibody, PC7 anti-mouse IFN-γ Antibody, APC anti-mouse CD11c Antibody, APC-A700 anti-mouse I-A/I-E Antibody, and PE anti-mouse CD86 Antibody were sourced from Biolegend Inc. HMGB1 Rabbit PolyAb and Calreticulin Rabbit PolyAb were sourced from Proteintech Group, Inc.

The mouse MSS CRC cell line CT-26 was obtained from the National Collection of Authenticated Cell Culture (Shanghai, China). CT-26 cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10 % fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1 % penicillin/streptomycin at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere with 5 % CO2. The mouse immature dendritic cell line DC2.4 was purchased from Shanghai Fuheng Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). DC2.4 cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10 % FBS at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere with 5 % CO2. Bone marrow-derived dendritic cells (BMDCs) were induced from the femurs of male Balb/c mice. BMDCs require the additional addition of GM-CSF (20 ng/mL) and IL-4 (10 ng/mL) for induction over 7 days, and the half of medium change was performed on the third and fifth days.

Male Balb/c mice (6–8 weeks old, 18–22 g) were obtained from GemPharmatech Co., Ltd. All animal handling procedures were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Jiangsu Academy of Traditional Chinese Medicine (AEWC-20220920-235).

Preparation of ZIF-90

A DMF solution containing Zn(CH3COO)2·2H2O (0.176 g, 2 mL) and 2-ICA (0.154 g, 8 mL) was slowly mixed and stirred for 5 min. Trioctylamine (600 μL) was added to the solution, followed by continuous stirring for 12 h. Finally, the product was collected by centrifugation (12,000 rpm) for 30 min, washed with an excess of ethanol, and vacuum-dried at 40 °C to obtain ZIF-90.

Preparation of (In/Cos)@Ua-Rg3-ZIF

Ua (5 mg) and Rg3 (10 mg) were added to 10 mL of a methanol solution containing ZIF (10 mg) and stirred in the dark for 24 h. Subsequently, the solution was centrifuged to obtain ZIF co-loaded with Ua and Rg3 (Ua-Rg3-ZIF). The drug-loading capacity of Ua-Rg3-ZIF was determined by high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC, Waters 2695, USA). Finally, an In/Cos polyelectrolyte double-layer was constructed on the surface of Ua-Rg3-ZIF using layer-by-layer self-assembly technology. Briefly, Ua-Rg3-ZIF nanoparticles were dispersed in a 1 mg/mL chitooligosaccharides solution, stirred at 700 rpm at room temperature for 4 h, and then washed three times with PBS after centrifugation (5500 rpm, 4 °C, 15 min). Subsequently, the obtained nanoparticles were suspended in a 1 mg/mL inulin solution, stirred for 4 h, and washed three times with PBS after centrifugation, and (In/Cos)@Ua-Rg3-ZIF was collected.

Characterization of (In/Cos)@Ua-Rg3-ZIF

The size, surface charge, and morphology of ZIF, Ua-Rg3-ZIF, and (In/Cos)@Ua-Rg3-ZIF were measured by dynamic light scattering, transmission electron microscopy, and scanning electron microscopy. The crystal phase of the prepared materials was analyzed using a powder X-ray diffractometer and a micromeritics surface area analyzer. To further confirm the effective fabrication of the bilayer structure, the resulting product was analyzed by infrared spectroscopy to verify the presence of typical peaks of Cos and In.

Stability and drug release of (In/Cos)@Ua-Rg3-ZIF under physiologically simulated gastrointestinal conditions

The protective effects of the In/Cos layer on nanoparticles against simulated gastrointestinal (GI) conditions were evaluated using simulated gastric fluid (SGF, pH 1.2), simulated intestinal fluid (SIF, pH 7.4), and simulated colonic fluid (SCF, pH 6.8). Briefly, equal amounts of Ua-Rg3-ZIF, In@Ua-Rg3-ZIF, Cos@Ua-Rg3-ZIF, and (In/Cos)@Ua-Rg3-ZIF were separately exposed to SGF/SIF/SCF and then incubated at 37 °C with shaking at 100 rpm. At predetermined time points, 500 μL of the sample was extracted. After centrifugation, the liquid supernatant was analyzed by high-performance liquid chromatography and the precipitation was measured by DLS.

Using the same preparation method as for (In/Cos)@Ua-Rg3-ZIF, we co-encapsulated two fluorescent probes, C6 and DiD, within ZIF prior to applying the In/Cos coating, resulting in (In/Cos)@C6-DiD-ZIF. This formulation was orally administered to both normal mice and germ-free mice (in which gut microbiota were depleted via antibiotic treatment). After 24 h, we examined the distribution of C6 and DiD in the colon.

Fluorescently labeled (In/Cos)@Ua-Rg3-ZIF

5 mg of C6 was dissolved in 10 mL of methanol and sonicated for 10 min at room temperature. Following the method in ‘Preparation of (In/Cos)@Ua-Rg3-ZIF’, C6-ZIF and (In/Cos)@C6-ZIF were obtained. 5 mg of DiD was dissolved in 5 mL of methanol and sonicated for 10 min at room temperature. Following the method in ‘Preparation of (In/Cos)@Ua-Rg3-ZIF’, DiD-ZIF and (In/Cos)@DiD-ZIF were obtained. All the above operations were performed in a dark environment.

In vitro experiments

Cell viability of CT-26 In vitro

CT-26 cells were seeded in 96-well plates (1 × 104 cells/well) and cultured for 24 h. Subsequently, the culture medium was removed, and the cells were exposed to Ua at various concentrations (0, 2.5, 5, 10, 20, 50, 100, and 200 μM). After 24 h of incubation, cell viability was assessed using the CCK8 assay.

Maturation of DCs In vitro

The tumor cell-conditioned medium was prepared as follows: CT-26 cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10 % fetal bovine serum (FBS) until they reached approximately 80 % confluence, the supernatant was collected and centrifuged at 2000 rpm for 10 min to remove any cellular debris. The resulting supernatant was further sterile-filtered using a 0.22-μm filter and was used as the tumor cell-conditioned medium for the DC2.4 maturation assays.

DC2.4 cells were seeded in 12-well plates (1 × 105 cells/well) and cultured for 24 h. Cells were treated with Rg3 at concentrations of 10, 20, or 30 μM prepared in tumor cell-conditioned medium. After 24 h of incubation, the cells were harvested, washed with PBS, incubated with anti-CD86, anti-CD40 and anti-MHC-II antibodies for 0.5 h, and analyzed by flow cytometry.

Cellular uptake

CT-26 and DC cells were seeded in a 12-well plate (5 × 104 cells/well) and incubated at 37 °C with 5 % CO2 for 24 h. After different treatments, 4 °C PBS was used to terminate cellular uptake, and DAPI was used to label the cells. Fluorescence microscopy was used to observe the cellular uptake of different drugs. Cells were also collected, and the fluorescence intensity was quantitatively analyzed by flow cytometry.

The level of cytosolic calreticulin and high-mobility group box 1

Initially, CT26 cells were seeded into a 12-well plate and incubated at 37 °C with 5 % CO2 for 24 h. After different treatments, the cells were stained with a primary anti-CRT antibody or anti-HMGB1 antibody and incubated with a secondary goat anti-rabbit antibody. Finally, the nuclei were stained with DAPI, and CRT or HMGB1 expression was subsequently observed by confocal laser-scanning microscopy (CLSM).

In vivo experiments

Orthotopic Transplantation Tumor Model

Mice were randomly divided into different subgroups. 1 × 106 CT-26-Luc cells were transplanted subcutaneously into Balb/c mice. Fourteen days later, the established ectopic colorectal cancer tumors in mice were excised. The tumor tissues were placed in a petri dish containing sterile 1640 medium. The tissues were cut into small, uniformly-sized pieces with a volume of approximately 1 mm3. These pieces were carefully implanted into the mouse colon and fixed with Matrigel. Bioluminescence imaging was performed using an IVIS Spectrum (PerkinElmer) instrument to validate the establishment of tumor models in mice.

Biodistribution

After oral administration of different preparations [PBS, DiD, DiD-ZIF, Cos@DiD-ZIF, (In/Cos)@DiD-ZIF at 30 mg/kg], Balb/c male mice (n = 3, 6–8 weeks old) were imaged at different time points (3 h, 6 h, 9 h, 12 h, and 24 h) in vivo to observe the fluorescence intensity of DiD in the main organs. At 24 h, the mice were euthanized, and the main organs, including the heart, liver, spleen, lung, kidney, and intestine, were collected for ex vivo fluorescence imaging. The intestines were collected at 24 h and prepared as single-cell suspensions for flow cytometry analysis. The relative fluorescence intensities of DiD in DCs, macrophages, T cells and non-immune cells were analyzed to determine the relative uptake of nanoparticles by immune cells. Additionally, fecal samples were collected from each experimental group at predetermined intervals (3, 6, 9, 12, and 24 h post-administration) and subjected to ex vivo imaging to analyze the fluorescence intensity of DiD.

Antitumor efficacy

The tumor model in Balb/c mice was established as described in ‘Orthotopic Transplantation Tumor Model’. After 3 days, mice (n = 6) were treated daily via oral gavage with PBS, Ua-Rg3, (In/Cos)@ZIF, Ua-Rg3-ZIF, and (In/Cos)@Ua-Rg3-ZIF (30 mg/kg). Mice in the α-PD-1 group and the combination group were injected with α-PD-1 (5 mg/kg) via intravenous administration every 4 days. At the end of the antitumor efficacy study, mice were sacrificed, and tumors were collected and weighed. Tumor volume was calculated as follows: width [2] × length × 0.5. Moreover, the tumors were sectioned. These slices were stained with HE, Ki67, and TUNEL respectively to observe the histopathological changes. Apoptosis of tumor cells was measured by flow cytometry. Survival experiments were also conducted.

Flow cytometry analysis

Tumor tissues were collected and digested at 37 °C in a buffer containing 1 mg/mL collagenase I, 1 mg/mL collagenase IV, 0.04 mg/mL DNAase, and 0.4 mg/mL HAase for 5 h. Subsequently, the mixtures were ground and filtered to collect single cells. Single cells were labeled with antibodies and analyzed by flow cytometry.

Safety assessment

To evaluate the in-vivo safety of (In/Cos)@Ua-Rg3-ZIF, the major organs of mice were subjected to HE staining. The serum levels of AST, ALT, and BUN were measured to assess the potentially toxic effects of (In/Cos)@Ua-Rg3-ZIF on the liver and kidney.

16S ribosomal RNA sequencing

Fecal samples were collected from the orthotopic CRC mouse model at the endpoint of treatment. Samples were promptly stored at −80 °C. Subsequently, an analysis of the gut microbiota abundance was conducted.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analysis were performed using GraphPad Prism 8.0 software (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Two-tailed Student's t-test and one-way ANOVA were used to analyze significant differences. A P-value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results and discussion

Fabrication and characterization of (In/Cos)@Ua-Rg3-ZIF

To prepare (In/Cos)@Ua-Rg3-ZIF, Ua and Rg3 were first loaded into the porous structure of ZIF to yield Ua-Rg3-ZIF. The drug loading capacity (DLC) values Ua and Rg3 were 16.72 and 7.35 wt % by HPLC, respectively. To prevent degradation of Ua-Rg3-ZIF in gastric fluid during oral administration, an LBL electrostatic self-assembly strategy was employed [35]. Cationic Cos and anionic In were sequentially coated onto Ua-Rg3-ZIF, forming an In/Cos bilayer modification, resulting in the formation of (In/Cos)@Ua-Rg3-ZIF.

Dynamic light scattering (DLS) measurements revealed that the particle sizes of ZIF, Ua-Rg3-ZIF, and (In/Cos)@Ua-Rg3-ZIF were 104 nm, 122 nm, and 160 nm, respectively (Fig. S1A). During the LBL process, zeta potential changes on the nanoparticle surfaces were measured. The zeta potential shifted from −12.8 mV for Ua-Rg3-ZIF to 14.5 mV for Cos@Ua-Rg3-ZIF, indicating the successful application of the cationic Cos coating. Following the anionic In coating, the zeta potential decreased to −13.4 mV for (In/Cos)@Ua-Rg3-ZIF (Fig. 2A). These alternating zeta potentials, corresponding to the different outer layers, confirm the successful preparation of the LBL coatings. Furthermore, glycan content quantification demonstrated that the LBL strategy significantly enhanced glycan loading onto ZIF (Fig. S1B).

The FT-IR analysis indicated the successful construction of ZIF-90, Ua-Rg3-ZIF-90 and (In/Cos)@Ua-Rg3-ZIF. The ZIF-90's characteristic peak (∼1661 cm−1) remained stable with slight shifts after drug loading and modification. The final spectra showed overlapping peaks of COS and In, such as the broadened hydroxyl (∼3362 cm−1) and C-H stretching vibrations (∼2928 cm−1), confirming successful surface modification. Thus, FT-IR verified the successful creation of (In/Cos)@Ua-Rg3-ZIF with dual drug loading and polysaccharide modification (Fig. 2B and S1C).

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) imaging showed that ZIF exhibited a uniform spherical shape with a diameter of approximately 100 nm. Upon coating, a distinct layer approximately 20 nm thick appeared on the surface of (In/Cos)@Ua-Rg3-ZIF (Fig. 2C). Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) results revealed that ZIF, Ua-Rg3-ZIF, and (In/Cos)@Ua-Rg3-ZIF maintained a dodecahedral shape, with the loading of Ua-Rg3 and modification with In/Cos preserving the characteristic framework of ZIF (Fig. 2D) [36], with no observed disruption of the ZIF structure. Elemental spectrum analysis detected C, N, O, and Zn elements in (In/Cos)@Ua-Rg3-ZIF, confirming the successful synthesis of the modified nanoparticles (Fig. S1D). X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis of (In/Cos)@Ua-Rg3-ZIF did not exhibit the characteristic peaks of Ua and Rg3, suggesting that these compounds are loaded into ZIF in an amorphous form (Fig. 2E).

Additionally, nitrogen adsorption-desorption experiments were conducted to determine the specific surface area and pore volume, and the parameters were calculated using BET and BJH methods [37]. The specific surface area and pore volume of ZIF were found to be 1190.07 m2/g and 1.30 cm3/g, respectively. However, after drug loading (Ua-Rg3-ZIF), both the specific surface area and pore volume significantly decreased to 969.99 m2/g and 1.08 cm3/g, respectively (Fig. 2F and G). This reduction is attributed to the occupation of pore space by the loaded Ua-Rg3. The subsequent In/Cos coating further reduced the specific surface area and pore volume.

To protect against GI degradation, ensuring the oral (In/Cos)@Ua-Rg3-ZIF withstands gastric fluid is crucial before the nanoparticles are delivered to the tumor. Size changes of (In/Cos)@Ua-Rg3-ZIF were measured in simulated gastric fluid (SGF, pH 1.2), simulated intestinal fluid (SIF, pH 7.4), and simulated colonic fluid (SCF, pH 6.8) to assess nanoparticle stability [38]. The results demonstrated that only (In/Cos)@Ua-Rg3-ZIF maintained consistent particle size (Fig. 2H and Fig. S1E). To evaluate the system's ability to prevent premature drug release in the upper GI tract and respond to degradation by colonic microbiota-secreted enzymes and ATP, the drug release profile of the delivery system was examined in various simulated digestive fluids. The results indicated that the cumulative release of both drugs from (In/Cos)@Ua-Rg3-ZIF in SGF was only 20.4 % and 19.4 % of that from uncoated Ua-Rg3-ZIF, highlighting that the bilayer glycan modification significantly improved gastric stability. Additionally, incubation with inulinase, chitosanase, or ATP notably accelerated the drug release rate from (In/Cos)@Ua-Rg3-ZIF, confirming its responsive release capability to both enzymatic degradation and ATP stimulation (Fig. 2I and J). SEM images further illustrate that the dual-layer polysaccharide coating preserves the stability of the drug delivery system in SGF, while specifically responding to target enzymes and ATP by disintegrating (Fig. 2K and Fig. S1F). To evaluate the in vivo gut microbiota-controlled drug release, we used an antibiotic cocktail to establish gut-microbiome-elimination mouse model. As shown in Fig. S2, the results revealed distinct spatial separation of C6 and DiD signals in the colon of normal mice, whereas in germ-free group mice, the two probes showed strong co-localization. This difference can be attributed to the degradation of the In/Cos coating by colonic microbiota in normal mice, leading to differential release and distribution of the two probes.

These findings confirmed that the In/Cos coating of (In/Cos)@Ua-Rg3-ZIF was specifically degraded by colonic microbiota, thereby facilitating drug release within the colon tissue.

In vitro cellular uptake, antitumor efficacy, and antitumor Immune activation of (In/Cos)@Ua-Rg3-ZIF

To investigate the uptake of (In/Cos)@Ua-Rg3-ZIF by CT-26 and DC2.4, coumarin 6 (C6) was used as a fluorescent surrogate for Ua-Rg3 [39]. Fluorescent microscopy and flow cytometry analysis revealed that the uptake rate of C6-ZIF by CT-26 cells was 2.56-fold higher than that of free C6 (Figs. S2, A, B, and C). Similarly, the uptake rate of C6-ZIF by DC2.4 cells was 11.47-fold higher than that of free C6 (Figs. S3, D, E, and F), indicating that Ua-Rg3-ZIF enhances the uptake of Ua-Rg3 by both CT-26 and DC2.4 cells. Notably, the uptake of (In/Cos)@C6-ZIF by both cell types was the highest, likely due to the increased water solubility of the nanosystem after modification with the double-layer glycan coating. The saccharides on the surface exhibit an affinity for cells, promoting the drug uptake process.

To assess the effect on cell viability, a cell-counting kit-8 (CCK-8) assay was performed to evaluate the anti-tumor activity of the nanoparticles. The results indicated that for DC2.4 cells, Ua-Rg3-ZIF had minimal impact on cell viability at low concentrations, while for CT-26 cells, it exhibited significant cytotoxic effects, suggesting that the drug-loaded nanosystem effectively inhibited CT-26 cell proliferation without affecting DC cell viability (Fig. 3A and B). However, compared to the Ua-Rg3-ZIF group, the modified (In/Cos)@Ua-Rg3-ZIF exhibited a reduced inhibitory effect on the proliferation of both cell types (Fig. 3C and D). This observation suggests that the glycan coating undergoes limited intracellular degradation, allowing the nanosystem to remain intact and retain its encapsulated drugs, as confirmed by subsequent experiments.

The low immunogenicity of MSS CRC contributes significantly to the poor response to PD-1 inhibitors. ICD can enhance antigen levels in the tumor microenvironment by releasing damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), which activate immune responses, including the recruitment of DCs for tumor antigen uptake and presentation, thereby promoting TIL infiltration [40]. Key hallmarks of ICD include the translocation of calreticulin (CRT) from the endoplasmic reticulum to the cell surface and the release of high-mobility group box 1 (HMGB1) from the nucleus to the cytoplasm [41]. To assess ICD induction by (In/Cos)@Ua-Rg3-ZIF in CT-26 cells, firstly, we preliminarily evaluated the effects of Ua and Rg3 on the survival rate of CT-26 cells. At the same concentration, the cytotoxicity of Ua is significantly stronger than that of Rg3, indicating that Ua plays a dominant role in inducing immunogenic cell death in tumor cells (Fig. S4). Then CRT and HMGB1 dynamics were analyzed using laser scanning confocal microscopy. Immunofluorescence analysis revealed significantly stronger green fluorescence signals for CRT expression in Ua-Rg3-ZIF-treated cells compared to control cells, indicating enhanced CRT translocation (Fig. 3E). In contrast, HMGB1 release was markedly higher in Ua-Rg3-ZIF-treated cells, as evidenced by weaker red fluorescence signals compared to controls (Fig. 3F). These results suggest that the nanosystem effectively induces ICD in tumor cells, setting the stage for subsequent immune activation.

Following ICD-induced DAMP release and an increase in tumor antigen levels, CTL infiltration still requires DC involvement. However, the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment hinders the antigen-presenting capacity of DCs [42]. To validate whether (In/Cos)@Ua-Rg3-ZIF enhances DC antigen presentation, co-culture models of DC2.4/BMDC with CT-26 cells were established [43]. Flow cytometry analysis revealed that Ua-Rg3-ZIF significantly upregulated the antigen-presenting molecule MHC-II on DCs. Furthermore, marked increases in co-stimulatory molecules CD40 and CD86 were observed, demonstrating that the nanoparticles effectively enhance DC antigen-presentation capacity and facilitate antigen presentation to TILs (Fig. 3G and H).

Biodistribution of (In/Cos)@Ua-Rg3-ZIF

To assess the biodistribution of (In/Cos)@Ua-Rg3-ZIF, an orthotopic MSS CRC animal model was established, and in vivo fluorescence was monitored using IVIS [44]. The (In/Cos)@DiD-ZIF group exhibited significantly higher fluorescence intensity than the DiD-ZIF group at 3, 6, 9, 12, and 24 h, indicating enhanced GI tract accumulation and retention (Fig. 4A). Ex vivo fluorescence imaging and quantification of tumors and major organs at 24 h post-oral gavage revealed a 5.63-fold higher distribution of (In/Cos)@DiD-ZIF in the colon and tumors compared to DiD-ZIF (Fig. 4B and C and S5). The results demonstrated that the (In/Cos)@C6-DiD-ZIF group exhibited the highest fluorescence signal in the colon. This improved distribution is likely due to the dual-glycan coating, which facilitates increased tumor targeting. Fluorescence quantification in the stomach further demonstrated a significantly reduced signal for the (In/Cos)@DiD-ZIF group, measuring only 52.56 % of that observed in the DiD-ZIF group (Fig. 4D). Combined with the quantitative data from the stomach, the results indicated that the In/Cos coating effectively protects (In/Cos)@C6-DiD-ZIF against gastric acid and enzyme degradation. The coating was subsequently degraded by specific enzymes in the colon, facilitating targeted drug release. Further quantitative data of tumor tissue showed that the released drug was then absorbed by the local tumor tissue, thereby exerting its therapeutic effect.

Fluorescence intensity in fecal contents serves as an indicator of the intestinal excretion efficiency of the formulation. The (In/Cos)@DiD-ZIF group showed the strongest fluorescence signal in feces, likely due to the increased accumulation of the nanoplatform in the colon (Fig. 4E and F). Combining in vivo and ex vivo imaging results revealed that the dual-glycan coating significantly improved the nanoplatform's distribution. By minimizing gastric degradation, a higher proportion of nanoparticles successfully reached the colon intact, facilitating targeted delivery to colon tumors and enhancing intratumoral accumulation [45].

To confirm whether (In/Cos)@Ua-Rg3-ZIF effectively delivers drugs to tumor cells, its distribution in DCs, macrophages, and CD45− cells within tumors was analyzed via flow cytometry (Fig. 4G). Compared to the DiD-ZIF group, (In/Cos)@DiD-ZIF demonstrated significantly higher uptake in DCs, macrophages, and CD45− cells, confirming efficient internalization by both immune and tumor cells in vivo [45].

In summary, the dual polysaccharide coating (In/Cos) on Ua-Rg3-ZIF enables precise drug delivery to the colon, protects the nanoparticles from gastric degradation, and promotes tumor-specific accumulation. This approach offers a robust strategy for improving therapeutic efficacy and safety in MSS CRC treatment.

Synergistic antitumor effects of (In/Cos)@Ua-Rg3-ZIF and PD-1 antibody (α-PD-1) in orthotopic MSS CRC transplantation mouse model

To validate the in vivo efficacy of (In/Cos)@Ua-Rg3-ZIF combined with PD-1 inhibitors, an orthotopic MSS CRC mouse model was established. Treatments were administered following the outlined protocol (Fig. 5A) [19]. Survival experiments showed that mice receiving combination therapy had the longest median survival, indicating its potential to prolong survival in patients with MSS CRC (Fig. 5B). After completing the treatment cycle, antitumor efficacy was evaluated using IVIS to monitor bioluminescence emitted by tumor tissues. The results revealed a significant reduction in fluorescence intensity in the combination treatment group compared to the model group (Fig. 5C and D). Tumor volumes, measured after 14 days of treatment, were significantly smaller in the combination therapy group, consistent with the in vivo imaging results, strongly suggesting that the combined treatment effectively inhibited tumor growth (Fig. 5E and Fig. S6). Tumor inhibition analysis demonstrated a 60.80 % inhibition rate for (In/Cos)@Ua-Rg3-ZIF, indicating superior therapeutic efficacy compared to free Ua-Rg3 and uncoated Ua-Rg3-ZIF (Fig. 5F and G). This marked improvement underscores the critical role of the dual-glycan coating in protecting the drug payload and enabling colon-targeted delivery. Notably, the combination therapy group achieved a tumor inhibition rate of 80.86 %, representing a 20-fold enhancement over α-PD-1 monotherapy (4.04 % inhibition, P < 0.001), effectively overcoming the primary resistance of MSS CRC to PD-1 blockade therapy, which typically shows response rates below 5 %. Moreover, combination therapy significantly outperformed (In/Cos)@Ua-Rg3-ZIF monotherapy, highlighting the synergistic effect between nanoplatform-induced immune activation and PD-1 checkpoint inhibition.

To validate tumor cell proliferation inhibition post-treatment, flow cytometry was used to measure CD45-Ki67+ expression in tumor cell suspensions (Fig. 5H and I). Ki67 immunohistochemical staining results further confirmed that combination therapy significantly suppressed tumor proliferation (Fig. 5J). Additionally, H&E staining and TUNEL assays revealed extensive tumor necrosis and increased apoptosis levels in the combination therapy group (Fig. 5K and L). These results demonstrate that (In/Cos)@Ua-Rg3-ZIF combined with α-PD-1 enhances the in vivo response to PD-1 inhibition, exerting potent antitumor effects.

Induction of ICD and enhancement of DCs antigen transport by (In/Cos)@Ua-Rg3-ZIF in vivo

To assess whether (In/Cos)@Ua-Rg3-ZIF synergizes with α-PD-1 in inducing ICD, the expression and localization of CRT and HMGB1 in tumor tissues across treatment groups were examined using immunofluorescence analysis [46]. The results revealed increased CRT expression in the tumor tissues of mice treated with the combination of (In/Cos)@Ua-Rg3-ZIF, while the HMGB1 signal decreased, indicating the ability of (In/Cos)@Ua-Rg3-ZIF to induce immunogenic death of tumor cells (Fig. 6A and B). To further evaluate DC maturation, surface expression levels of CD86 and MHC-II on DCs were quantitatively assessed using flow cytometry [47]. The data demonstrated that the proportion of CD86+ DCs in the (In/Cos)@Ua-Rg3-ZIF group and the combination group was 3.57-fold and 5.6-fold higher, respectively, compared to the model group (Fig. 6C). Similarly, the proportion of MHC-II+ DCs in the (In/Cos)@Ua-Rg3-ZIF group and the combination group was 3.2-fold and 5.44-fold higher, respectively, compared to the model group (Fig. 6D). This substantial enhancement in DC maturation, particularly in the combination group, underpins the significantly increased TIL infiltration observed in subsequent analyses, ultimately contributing to the enhanced antitumor efficacy.

Flow cytometry was employed to analyze TIL levels in tumor tissues. Compared to the model group, α-PD-1 monotherapy had no significant impact on the number of CD3+ TILs, consistent with its limited therapeutic effect. In contrast, (In/Cos)@Ua-Rg3-ZIF significantly increased CD3+ TILs by 4.3-fold relative to the model group, demonstrating its ability to elevate the overall TIL population [48] (Fig. 6E). Notably, combination therapy further enhanced CD3+ TIL infiltration by 5.5-fold compared to the model group. Analysis of T cell subsets revealed a striking 5.6-fold increase in CD4+ T cells specifically within the combination group [49] (Fig. 6F). This substantial enhancement in TILs, particularly the synergistic effect observed in the combination group, directly correlates with the superior DC maturation induced by the same treatments.

To further evaluate the tumor-killing capacity of the recruited TILs, the proportion of T cells expressing granzyme B and IFN-γ in tumor tissues was investigated [50]. Flow cytometry analysis revealed that combination therapy significantly increased the proportion of CD8+ T cells expressing granzyme B and IFN-γ (Fig. 6G and H). This indicates that the recruited TILs not only infiltrated the tumor but also exhibited potent effector functions, characterized by elevated cytotoxic granule production and pro-inflammatory cytokine secretion-key components for direct tumor cell killing and effective antitumor immune responses.

In summary, (In/Cos)@Ua-Rg3-ZIF enhances TIL infiltration into tumors by inducing ICD of tumor cells and improving the antigen-presenting capacity of DCs, thereby potentiating the therapeutic effect of α-PD-1 in MSS CRC.

Impacts of oral (In/Cos)@Ua-Rg3-ZIF on Intestinal microorganisms

The onset and progression of CRC are closely associated with the gut microbiota. Increasing evidence suggests that modulating the gut microbiota enhances the immunomodulatory capacity of immune cells, thereby inhibiting tumor proliferation. After enzymatic degradation and metabolism by colonic bacteria, inulin and chitooligosaccharides exert prebiotic effects that promote intestinal microbial balance [[51], [52], [53]]. To explore the microbiota-modulating capacity of this nanoplatform, 16S rRNA amplicon sequencing was employed. Diversity analysis (Fig. 7A) revealed significantly higher α-diversity indices in the treatment group compared to the model group, indicating an effective amelioration of microbial dysbiosis. Venn diagram analysis (Fig. 7B) further demonstrated that the number of shared OTUs between the treatment group and healthy controls (n = 275) was significantly greater than that between the model group and healthy controls (n = 190), reflecting a microbiota composition more similar to that of healthy individuals following (In/Cos)@Ua-Rg3-ZIF treatment. Both principal coordinates analysis (PCoA) (Fig. 7C) and non-metric multidimensional scaling (NMDS) (Fig. 7D) revealed clear clustering separation between the treatment and model groups, with the microbiota structure shifting toward the healthy control profile.

At the family level (Fig. 7E), (In/Cos)@Ua-Rg3-ZIF treatment significantly reduced the relative abundances of harmful bacteria, such as Bacteroidaceae and Enterobacteriaceae, while increasing the abundance of beneficial bacteria, including Lachnospiraceae and Akkermansiaceae. Genus-level analysis (Fig. 7J) indicated that (In/Cos)@Ua-Rg3-ZIF specifically restored the abundance of probiotic Ligilactobacillus to levels comparable to healthy controls, highlighting its ability to selectively modulate beneficial microbial populations. Notably, the restoration of these particular bacterial taxa, which have been shown to enhance antitumor immunotherapy efficacy by promoting TIL infiltration [54,55], strongly correlates with the significantly enhanced TIL infiltration and functional activation observed in our (In/Cos)@Ua-Rg3-ZIF-treated mice, particularly in the combination group. This suggests that the modulated gut microbiota may contribute to the overall immunotherapeutic efficacy of the nanoplatform.

Safety of the combination therapy of (In/Cos)@Ua-Rg3-ZIF and α-PD-1

To assess the biocompatibility of (In/Cos)@Ua-Rg3-ZIF and α-PD-1, daily monitoring of mouse body weight (Fig. 8A) during the treatment period revealed no significant changes, indicating that long-term administration of (In/Cos)@Ua-Rg3-ZIF and combination treatment did not cause harm. Consistently, no significant differences in serum levels of AST (Fig. 8B), BUN (Fig. 8C), or ALT (Fig. 8D) were observed among the groups, suggesting that (In/Cos)@Ua-Rg3-ZIF and combination treatment did not impact liver or kidney function. Additionally, H&E staining showed no lesions in the major organs of the mice (Fig. 8E). Collectively, these findings demonstrate the safety of the combination of (In/Cos)@Ua-Rg3-ZIF and α-PD-1.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study developed a double-layer glycan-modified delivery system combined with a PD-1 inhibitor for the oral treatment of MSS CRC. The dual-glycan coating of (In/Cos)@Ua-Rg3-ZIF serves two key functions: 1) It protects the delivery system from gastric acid degradation, ensuring colon-specific drug delivery, and 2) The glycans are enzymatically degraded by microbiota-secreted enzymes in the colon, simultaneously modulating gut microbiota homeostasis. Within the tumor microenvironment, elevated ATP levels trigger ZIF disintegration, leading to Ua release, which induces ICD of tumor cells and the release of DAMPs, while synchronized Rg3 release promotes DC maturation, enhancing phagocytic capacity and antigen-presenting functions. Thus, TIL infiltration within the tumor is significantly increased, leading to effective tumor cell killing.

This strategy demonstrates substantial potential in achieving colonic-targeted drug delivery and improving the efficacy of oral treatments for colonic diseases, offering a promising approach to enhance the response rate of immunotherapy in MSS CRC.

Although the proposed (In/Cos)@Ua-Rg3-ZIF platform demonstrates distinct advantages in dual-drug co-loading, colon tumor specific drug release, and synergistic immune activation compared with conventional oral nanocarriers, several limitations remain. The in vivo PK profile of (In/Cos)@Ua-Rg3-ZIF and the molecular pathways underlying dendritic cell activation require further clarification. Future studies will focus on elucidating these pathways and unraveling the intricate interplay between gut microbiota remodeling and antitumor immunity, thereby advancing the translational potential of this microbiota-responsive therapeutic strategy.
