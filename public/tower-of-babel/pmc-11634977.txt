Comparison of predicted and actual dermatitis generated from computed tomography images and dose distribution maps
Authors: Yasuhide Miyabe, Saori Oshiro, Hiroto Seki, Yusuke Muroi, Eriko Kawashima, Megumi Hosoda, Kento Ohashi, Hiromu Yamanaka, Mingliang Shao, Hiromi Sugawara, Jyunetsu Mizoe, Ritsuko Komaki
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Highlights•We have devised a method for creating predictive maps of skin inflammation.•This diagram considers only the effects on the skin based on radiation dosage.•Visual information on skin inflammation before treatment can be provided.•This study demonstrates new approaches to addressing skin inflammation.

Introduction

One of the most commonly reported toxicities associated with radiation therapy is radiation dermatitis. Wong et al. developed guidelines to help address challenges in managing patients with radiation dermatitis [1]. Additionally, numerous investigations have extensively reported the management of radiation dermatitis resulting from radiotherapy [2], [3], [4], [5], [6], [7]. These studies have focused primarily on photon therapy-induced radiation dermatitis, and recent literature on proton therapy-induced radiation dermatitis has been reported [8], [9], [10], [11]. In recent scholarly literature, there have been propositions for the quantification and grading assessment of radiation dermatitis [12]. Kim et al. have attempted to segment the occurrence and morphology of radiation dermatitis based on severity by using deep learning on skin photographs and skin dose distribution data of patients who underwent radiation therapy. This approach provides reproducible and objective assessment for the evaluation of radiation dermatitis. These discussions of radiation dermatitis primarily concern interventions and evaluations after dermatitis manifestation. We hypothesized that predicting the location and severity of dermatitis before radiation therapy initiation could enhance dermatological care provided by medical practitioners. Moreover, providing patients with visual information in advance could be beneficial from the self-care perspective. Therefore, we developed a method to construct a dermatitis-prediction diagram (hereinafter referred to as the “prediction diagram”) using CT images and dose information from the radiation treatment planning system (RTPS). The study aims to validate the prediction diagram by comparing the results of the diagram with the actual condition of patients through empirical validation.

Materials and methods

This study used the SOMATOM Perspective (Siemens Healthineers, Forchheim, Germany) for the therapeutic planning computed tomography, RayStation version 10A (RaySearch Laboratories, Stockholm, Sweden) for the RTPS, and PROTEUS®ONE (Ion Beam Applications, Louvain-la-Neuve, Belgium) for the proton beam therapy equipment. Method for creating the prediction diagram involves using the formulas for calculating the biologically equivalent dose (BED) and EQD2 to determine the dose equivalent to 20 Gy for 2 Gy fractions. The value of α/β was set to 10.0. Herein, “n” indicates the number of treatment sessions, “d” denotes the dose per session, and “D” represents the total dose. Using a similar procedure, we determined the doses equivalent to 30–50 Gy in EQD2.BED(Gy)=nd1+dα/β,EQD2(Gy)=Dd+α/β/2+α/βIn this study, the subsequent discussions on skin dose value will utilize EQD2 as the standard value. The regions of interest (ROIs) were defined as doses of 20–50 Gy in 10 Gy intervals, using the “Create ROI from dose” function. Overlapping areas between each dose-specific ROIs and skin (external) were visually highlighted through color coding. The skin was created by subtracting a 3 mm reduced ROI of the body contour from the body contour. To develop the prediction diagram, the region where the skin overlapped with the ROI of 20 Gy was extracted, which was isotropically expanded by 0.5 mm, using the “ROI algebra” function, and designated as the 20 Gy region. Similarly, overlaps between the 20 Gy region and the ROI of 30 Gy, which was expanded by 0.5 mm isotropically, were identified as the 30 Gy region, and this process was iteratively continued to develop regions up to 50 Gy. This approach avoids the speckled pattern, originating from overlapping of ROIs when extracting the intersected areas without expansion due to RTPS specifications. Subsequently, color coding was sequentially assigned— blue, yellow, red, and purple—for each 10 Gy increment, starting at 20 Gy.

Before commencing the treatment, printed diagrams predicting skin inflammation were provided to nurses and physicians. Additionally, nurses utilized these diagrams to instruct patients on the application sites for topical creams and ointments.

Our Institutional Review Board (IRB Number: 18000006) granted approval for this study on June 17, 2024, assigning it reference number 2024–3. The study was conducted after obtaining informed consent from the participants.

The comparative methodology used in this study comprised two verification processes. Verification 1, the correlation between the subjective assessment of dermatitis grades by three nurses, who primarily conducted skin observations, and the predicted dose map was evaluated in terms of severity. Subjective evaluation of the nurses involved marking the areas corresponding to grades 1 and 2 on the skin observation photographs captured at the end of the treatment. CTCAE version 5.0 was used for grading assessment. Blue lines indicated grade 1 markings, and yellow lines denoted grade 2 markings. Verification 2, prediction diagram and skin observation photographs were compared to visually assess the extent of agreement in the occurrence location and shape of dermatitis. Observers used a method for continuously- distributed test results, where each observation material was scored based on their judgment criteria [13]. In the present study, which employed a continuous scale of 0–100, a confidence score of 100 indicated complete agreement, and a score of 0 denoted complete disagreement. This evaluation was conducted by 12 observers (physicians, nurses, medical physicists, radiologic technologists, and administrative staff). The statistical analysis comprised a one-way analysis of variance and Bonferroni’s multiple comparison test.

This study included four patients who were treated with proton beam therapy; intensity modulated proton therapy using spot scanning technology was performed. Patient A received treatment for laryngeal cancer with 44 GyE/22 fr, along with an additional 26 GyE/13 Fr of localized treatment. Patient B received treatment for lymph node metastasis after thyroid cancer surgery with 44 GyE/22 fr, along with an additional 26 GyE/13 Fr of localized treatment. Furthermore, Patient C received treatment for breast cancer and cervical and supraclavicular lymph node metastases with 50 GyE/25 fr, along with an additional 17.5 GyE/7 Fr of localized treatment. Patient D received treatment for breast cancer subclavian and mediastinal lymph node metastasis with 65 GyE/25 fr. All patients were treated solely with radiotherapy, and they were selected because skin photographs taken at the end of treatment included the entire treated area.

Discussion

This study proposes a method for creating a dermatitis prediction diagram using dose information divided into 10 Gy increments within the 20–50 Gy range and CT images. We used four patients predicted diagrams and post-treatment skin photographs to validate our proposed method. Verification 1 examined the correlation between the skin maps and actual skin reaction severity. Verification 2 assessed inter-observer agreement.

Accurate alignment of the 20 Gy region contours with the actual skin reaction periphery provided successful outcomes for 3 out of 4 patients during verification 1. Additionally, the high-grade skin reaction sites were near the predicted regions. However, the unfavorable outcome in one of the 4 patients studied could be associated with differences in the angles of the prediction diagram and skin confirmation photographs. Moreover, high-grade skin reactions that were not predicted in the diagram were seen in the skin confirmation photographs. Particularly, the linear erythema around the neck was believed to be caused by chronic skin irritation during work due to the patient’s tight uniform.

Verification 2 revealed variations in marking and dose in grade 2. The variability in marking could be attributed to the subjective evaluation by the nurses. A method proposed by Kim et al., which utilizes deep learning to determine the affected areas and severity of radiation dermatitis, could be useful in terms of reproducibility and objectivity to address this issue [12]. The difference between marking and dosage was thought to arise from the inability to depict dose differences within regions color-coded in 10 Gy increments. For example, within the 30 Gy region, doses above 30 Gy to those below 40 Gy are treated as the same, thus not accurately reflecting finer dose variations. This could be the cause of the discrepancies.

Our prediction diagram carries two main benefits. First, this method allows for pre-treatment determination of the locations and severity of dermatitis that manifests in response to high-dose radiation, affording opportunities to plan preventive strategies. Second, by providing patients with a concrete visual representation of the predicted dermatitis, medical practitioners can better explain how radiation doses affect the skin, including the development of dermatitis.

The methodology of this study has several limitations. First, the ROI was enlarged while making the predictive diagram, causing a maximum height of approximately 2.0 mm above the skin surface. Second, verification was based on a small sample size of four patients. Finally, other factors such as genetic skin characteristics influenced by race, comorbidities, concomitant chemotherapy, and non-radiation-related skin irritations like friction were not considered in the study. Further studies are needed in order to clarify the validity of this research.

Conclusion

Even considering this method’s limitations, the prediction diagrams generally demonstrated good agreement with actual skin reactions in cases where no external factors other than the effects of radiotherapy were present. This novel method could provide meaningful visual information and be useful for medical practitioners and patients.
