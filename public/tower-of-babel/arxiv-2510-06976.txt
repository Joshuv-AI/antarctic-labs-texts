Scattering of charged particles within Efros method utilizing oscillator series expansion of wave functions
Ustin M. Yanikov, Vasily A. Kulikov, Andrey M. Shirokov

Abstract
We apply the version of the Efros method utilizing oscillator expansion of wave functions to the Coulomb scattering problem using our recent developments of the HORSE formalism. The approach yields accurate phase shifts and cross sections with significantly reduced computational cost compared to the full HORSE method, while maintaining agreement with exact solutions. These results demonstrate the efficiency of the Efros method and its prospect for applications in ab initio nuclear reaction calculations.



keywords
Quantum scattering theory; HORSE formalism; Efros method; Charged particle scattering

1
Introduction

Static properties of light atomic nuclei are currently calculated within various
ab initio
approaches which do not use any model assumptions about nuclear structure. It is especially worth noting No-Core Shell Model (NCSM)
[
1
]
as one of the most advanced and promising
ab initio
methods which utilizes the harmonic oscillator basis.

The nuclear continuum spectrum has been successfully studied so far within different approaches, e. g.,
R
-matrix method
[
2
]
and
J
-matrix method
[
3
,
4
]
. The formalism of Harmonic Oscillator Representation of Scattering Equations (HORSE)
[
5
,
6
,
7
,
8
]
is a particular version of
J
-matrix method utilizing harmonic oscillator basis which is preferable for many-body nuclear applications. The HORSE is the discrete analogue of
P
-matrix which is the inverse of
R
-matrix.
[
8
]

Various versions of the HORSE method have been successfully applied to the studies of nuclear resonant
states,
[
9
,
10
]
photodisintegration,
[
11
]
three-body continuum within phenomenological cluster models
[
12
,
13
]
and various problems within the Resonating Group Model (RGM)
[
14
,
15
,
16
]
. However, accounting for the
Coulomb interaction between charged particles within HORSE encounters difficulties due to the long-range nature of the Coulomb potential. Several methods have been suggested to
account for Coulomb asymptotics within the oscillator expansion of wave
functions
[
4
,
8
,
17
]
,
however, all
of them have significant
difficulties in combining with many-body
ab initio
approaches like the NCSM for
studying the nuclear continuum.
Recently we have suggested
[
18
]
the method for studying
scattering of charged particles based
on analysis of Coulomb matrix elements in oscillator basis performed in Ref.
[
17
]
,
which is simple and convenient to use within the
HORSE
formalism and is prospective for many-body applications in combination with NCSM.

Unfortunately,
one cannot use the HORSE in combination with NCSM for the studies of nuclear reactions
due to a prohibitive computational cost
as
it requires calculating extremely large number of eigenstates, even for light nuclei reactions. The method proposed recently by V. D. Efros
[
19
]
is based on variational
Hulthén–Kohn
method
[
20
]
and can be used for

ab initio
calculations of
nuclear reactions in combination with NCSM. Recently a modified version of the Efros method
has been suggested
[
21
]
which utilizes the oscillator expansion of
non-Coulomb scattering
wave functions developed within the HOSRE formalism and is well-adapted for the use
in combination with NCSM in many-body applications.
If one uses the full basis of
eigenstates
of the truncated Hamiltonian matrix in the oscillator basis
as short-range functions (SRFs) within the
modified
Efros method,
it appears to be
equivalent to
the
HORSE, however, it allows accounting for
a small
number of
SRFs
to
obtain adequate results.
Using
a small
number of
SRFs
not only
simplifies calculations significantly
but just
opens
possibilities for studying nuclear reactions within the framework of
ab initio
approaches.

In this
contribution,
we
combine our method for accounting for the Coulomb distortion of wave function asymptotics
proposed in Ref.
[
18
]
with the
modified
Efros method
[
19
]
for the studies
of charged particles scattering.
Using model problems, we
demonstrate that this
approach
provides quite accurate results even for small number of SRFs and basis truncations accessible by modern NCSM codes.

2
Coulomb scattering within HORSE formalism

In this section, we sketch the key expressions obtained within HORSE formalism for Coulomb scattering following the approach described in Ref.
[
18
]
. Consider a single-channel scattering of two particles with charges
eZ_{1}
and
eZ_{2}
. We use the conventional partial wave expansion of the wave function and describe the system in terms of partial amplitudes
u_{l}(k,r)
each of which obeys the radial Schrödinger equation with the Hamiltonian
H^{l}
,

H^{l}u_{l}(k,r)=Eu_{l}(k,r).

(1)

Here,
l
is the orbital quantum number,
r
is the distance between the particles,
k=\sqrt{2\mu E}/\hbar
is the momentum,
\mu
is the reduced mass, and
E
is the relative motion energy. The interaction potential between the particles is represented as
V^{l}=V^{Nucl,\,l}+V^{Coul}
, where
V^{Nucl,\,l}
and
V^{Coul}
are nuclear and Coulomb potentials, respectively. The asymptotic behavior of partial amplitudes
u_{l}(k,r)
is described by the superposition of regular
F_{l}(\eta,kr)
and irregular
G_{l}(\eta,kr)
Coulomb wave functions
[
22
]
,

u_{l}(k,r)\xrightarrow[r\to\infty]{}F_{l}(\eta,kr)+\tan{\delta_{l}(k)}\,G_{l}(\eta,kr),

(2)

where

\eta=\mu Z_{1}Z_{2}e^{2}/\hbar^{2}k
is the Sommerfeld parameter and

\delta_{l}(k)
is the phase shift.

Within the HORSE formalism, we expand the partial amplitudes
u_{l}(k,r)
into a series of harmonic oscillator functions
\varphi_{nl}(r)
,

u_{l}(k,r)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{nl}(k)\,\varphi_{nl}(r),

(3)

where

\varphi_{nl}(r)=(-1)^{n}\sqrt{\frac{2n!}{b\Gamma(n+l+\frac{3}{2})}}\left(\frac{r}{b}\right)^{\!l+1}e^{-\frac{r^{2}}{2b^{2}}}\,L_{n}^{l+\frac{1}{2}}\!\!\left(\frac{r^{2}}{b^{2}}\right)\!.

(4)

Here,
\Gamma(x)
is the gamma function
[
22
]
,

L_{n}^{\alpha}(x)
is the associated Laguerre
polynomial
[
22
]
,
b=\sqrt{\hbar/\mu\omega}
is the oscillator radius, and
\omega
is the oscillator frequency. The expansion coefficients
a_{nl}(k)
satisfy an infinite system of linear equations,

\sum_{n^{\prime}=0}^{\infty}(H_{nn^{\prime}}^{l}-\delta_{nn^{\prime}}E)\,a_{n^{\prime}l}(k)=0,

(5)

where
H_{nn^{\prime}}^{l}=T_{nn^{\prime}}^{l}+V_{nn^{\prime}}^{l}
are the Hamiltonian matrix elements in the harmonic oscillator basis with
T_{nn^{\prime}}^{l}
and
V_{nn^{\prime}}^{l}
being the matrix elements of the kinetic
T^{l}
and potential
V^{l}
energies, respectively.

In the harmonic oscillator basis, the kinetic energy matrix
T_{nn^{\prime}}^{l}
is tridiagonal,

\displaystyle T_{nn^{\prime}}^{l}

\displaystyle=0,\quad|n-n^{\prime}|>1,

(6)

\displaystyle T_{nn}^{l}

\displaystyle=\frac{\hbar\omega}{2}\left(2n+l+\frac{3}{2}\right),

\displaystyle T_{n+1,\,n}^{l}=T_{n,\,n+1}^{l}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\hbar\omega}{2}\sqrt{(n+1)\left(n+l+\frac{3}{2}\right)},

with its non-zero elements
T_{nn}^{l}
and
T_{n,\,n\pm 1}^{l}
increasing linearly with
n
for large values of
n
. At the same time, the matrix elements of nuclear potential
V_{nn^{\prime}}^{Nucl,\,l}\to 0
as
n
and/or
n^{\prime}\to\infty
. This allows us to replace
V^{Nucl,\,l}

by
the potential
\widetilde{V}^{Nucl,\,l}
defined by its matrix in harmonic oscillator basis truncated at some N,

\widetilde{V}_{nn^{\prime}}^{Nucl,\,l}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}V_{nn^{\prime}}^{Nucl,\,l}&\text{for $n$ and $n^{\prime}\leq N$},\\
0&\text{for $n$ or $n^{\prime}>N$}.\end{array}\right.

(7)

The matrix elements of Coulomb potential
V^{Coul,\,l}_{nn^{\prime}}
, however, decrease slowly along the main diagonal
[
17
]
and should be accounted for
at large values of radial quantum numbers
n
.
In the Kiev group study
[
17
]
, it was stated that for large values of
n
, the asymptotic
coefficients
a_{nl}^{as}(k)\equiv a_{nl}(k)
(
n\geq N
)
in the expansion (
3
)
fit the three-term recurrence relation (TRR)

T_{n,\,n-1}^{l}a_{n-1,\,l}^{as}(k)+(T_{nn}^{l}+V_{nn}^{ad,\,l}-E)a_{nl}^{as}(k)+T_{n,\,n+1}^{l}a_{n+1,\,l}^{as}(k)=0,

(8)

where the additional Coulomb term

V_{nn}^{ad,\,l}=\hbar\omega\frac{\eta kb}{\sqrt{4n+2l+3}}.

(9)

The calculations, however, demonstrate that TRR (
8
) remains highly accurate even for small
n\sim 0
.
[
18
]
As it was shown in Ref.
[
18
]
, this fact allows one to replace
V^{Coul}
and
T^{l}

by the
operators
\widetilde{V}^{Coul,\,l}
and
\widetilde{T}^{l}
, respectively,
defined by their
matrices in oscillator basis,

\displaystyle\widetilde{V}_{nn^{\prime}}^{Coul,\,l}

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}V_{nn^{\prime}}^{Coul}&\text{for $n$ and $n^{\prime}\leq N$},\\
0&\text{for $n$ or $n^{\prime}>N$},\end{array}\right.

\displaystyle\widetilde{T}_{nn^{\prime}}^{l}

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}T_{nn^{\prime}}^{l}&\text{for $n$ and $n^{\prime}\leq N$},\\
T_{nn^{\prime}}^{l}+V_{nn}^{ad,\,l}\delta_{nn^{\prime}}&\text{for $n$ or $n^{\prime}>N$}.\end{array}\right.

The conventional HORSE formulas are then applied to the problem with Hamiltonian
\widetilde{H}^{l}=\widetilde{V}^{Nucl,\,l}+\widetilde{V}^{Coul,\,l}+\widetilde{T}^{l}
to obtain the phase shift
\delta_{l}(k)
(see Ref.
[
18
]
for details).

The asymptotic coefficients
a_{nl}^{as}(k)
can be expressed as a superposition of two linearly independent solutions of TRR (
8
),
S_{nl}(k)
and
C_{nl}(k)
,

a_{nl}^{as}(k)=S_{nl}(k)+\tan{\delta_{l}(k)}\,C_{nl}(k).

(14)

To satisfy the asymptotics (
2
) of functions
u_{l}(k,r)
, we should define the solutions
S_{nl}(k)
and
C_{nl}(k)
so that

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}S_{nl}(k)\,\varphi_{nl}(r)

\displaystyle=F_{l}(\eta,kr),

(15a)

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}C_{nl}(k)\,\varphi_{nl}(r)

\displaystyle=\widetilde{G}_{l}(\eta,kr)\xrightarrow[r\to\infty]{}G_{l}(\eta,kr),

(15b)

where
\widetilde{G}_{l}(\eta,kr)
is some regular function which fits an inhomogeneous Schrödinger equation
[
8
,
23
]
.

At large
n
, harmonic oscillator functions
\varphi_{nl}(r)
have a delta-like behaviour in the vicinity of classical turning point
r_{turn}=\nu_{nl}b
, where
\nu_{nl}=\sqrt{4n+2l+3}
[
23
]
. Using this property, we obtain the asymptotic expressions for the coefficients
S_{nl}(k)
and
C_{nl}(k)
[
18
]
,

\displaystyle S_{nl}(k)\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}\sqrt{\frac{2b}{\nu_{nl}}}F_{l}(\eta,\nu kb),

(16a)

\displaystyle C_{nl}(k)\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}\sqrt{\frac{2b}{\nu_{nl}}}G_{l}(\eta,\nu kb).

(16b)

The high accuracy of TRR (
8
) allows us to reproduce the coefficients
S_{nl}(k)
and
C_{nl}(k)
[
18
]
. For some starting value
n_{s}
, the coefficients
S_{n_{s}+2,\,l}(k)
and
S_{n_{s}+1,\,l}(k)
are obtained from the asymptotic formulas (
15a
). The coefficients
S_{nl}(k)
(
n=0,\dots,n_{s}
) are then calculated according to TRR (
8
). The same procedure is used to obtain the coefficients
C_{nl}(k)
(
n=0,\dots,n_{s}
).

3
Efros method

The approach to finding solutions
S_{nl}(k)
and
C_{nl}(k)
of TRR (
8
) described in Sec.
2
allows us to apply Efros method
[
19
,
21
]
to Coulomb scattering problem. Suppose that solution of the Schrödinger equation (
1
) has the following form,

\widetilde{u}_{l}(k,r)=F_{l}(\eta,kr)+\tan\delta_{l}(k)\,\widetilde{G}_{l}(\eta,kr)+\sum_{n=0}^{v-1}b_{nl}(k)\beta_{nl}(r),

(17)

where
\beta_{nl}(r)
(
n=0,\dots,v-1
) is the set of
v
linearly independent
SRFs.
The equation (
17
)
includes

(v+1)
unknowns:
v
coefficients
b_{nl}(k)
and
\tan\delta_{l}(k)
. Introduce the vector
c
of unknowns with its components

c_{n}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}b_{nl}(k)&\text{for }n=0,1,\dots,v-1,\\
\tan\delta_{l}(k)&\text{for }n=v.\end{array}\right.

(18)

To find the unknowns
c_{n}
, consider
(v+1)
equations

\langle\bar{\beta}_{nl}|\widetilde{H}^{l}-E|\widetilde{u}_{l}\rangle=0,\quad n=0,\dots,v.

(19)

Here
\bar{\beta}_{nl}(r)

comprise a
set of
(v+1)
linearly independent SRFs. Following
Ref.
[
21
]
,
we
choose the first
v
functions as
\bar{\beta}_{nl}(r)=\beta_{nl}(r)
(
n=0,\dots,v-1
). The last function
\bar{\beta}_{vl}(r)
is arbitrarily chosen so that it satisfies the requirement of linear independence
with the rest
\bar{\beta}_{nl}(r)
with
n\leq v-1
.
Eq. (
19
) can be written in matrix form,

Ac=B,

(20)

where matrix
A
and vector
B
have the following matrix elements and components,

\displaystyle A_{nn^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\langle\bar{\beta}_{nl}|\widetilde{H}^{l}-E|\beta_{n^{\prime}l}\rangle,

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}n=0,\dots,v,\\
n^{\prime}=0,\dots,v-1,\end{array}\right.

\displaystyle A_{nv}

\displaystyle=\langle\bar{\beta}_{nl}|\widetilde{H}^{l}-E|\widetilde{G}_{l}\rangle,

\displaystyle n=0,\dots,v;

(21c)

B_{n}=-\langle\bar{\beta}_{nl}|\widetilde{H}^{l}-E|F_{l}\rangle,\quad n=0,\dots,v.

(22)

Solving the matrix equation (
20
), we obtain
\tan\delta_{l}(k)
and unknown coefficients
b_{nl}(k)
.

As for the choice of
SRFs

\beta_{nl}(r)
, it is natural to use either the set of
v
oscillator functions
\varphi_{n^{\prime}l}(r)
(
n^{\prime}\leq N
), or the set of
v
eigenfunctions
\gamma_{\lambda}(r)
of
truncated Hamiltonian
\widetilde{H}^{tr,\,l}
.
[
21
]
Here, the Hamiltonian
\widetilde{H}^{tr,\,l}
is defined by its
(N+1)\times(N+1)
matrix
\widetilde{H}_{nn^{\prime}}^{tr,\,l}=\widetilde{H}_{nn^{\prime}}^{l}
(
n,n^{\prime}=0\dots,N
) and eigenfunctions
\gamma_{\lambda}(r)
satisfy the following equations,

\widetilde{H}^{tr,\,l}|\gamma_{\lambda}\rangle=E_{\lambda}|\gamma_{\lambda}\rangle,\quad\lambda=0,\dots,N,

(23)

\langle\gamma_{\lambda}|\gamma_{\lambda^{\prime}}\rangle=\delta_{\lambda\lambda^{\prime}},\quad\lambda,\lambda^{\prime}=0,\dots,N,

(24)

where
E_{\lambda}
are the eigenenergies corresponding to eigenfunctions
\gamma_{\lambda}(r)
. The eigenfunctions are convenient to be ordered so that lower indices
\lambda
correspond to lower eigenenergies
E_{\lambda}
, i. e.,
E_{\lambda}\leq E_{\lambda^{\prime}}
if
\lambda<\lambda^{\prime}
.
The set of eigenfunctions is especially convenient for many-body applications in combination of the Efros method with NCSM
since otherwise one needs to construct the SRFs
\beta_{nl}(r)
as non-excited center-of-mass superpositions
with the definite value of the total angular momentum
J
of
the NCSM basis functions which are Slater determinants that are not characterized by a definite value of the total
angular momentum
J
and may have contributions of the center-of-mass excitations.
However, generally,
one can define
the set of SRFs
\beta_{nl}(r)
as
any
set of
v
linearly independent superpositions of
short-range functions.
As for the function
\bar{\beta}_{vl}(r)
, it was
shown
in Ref.
[
21
]
that
it is reasonable to define it as
the first oscillator function outside the truncation boundary
N
, i. e.,
\bar{\beta}_{vl}(r)=\varphi_{N+1,\,l}(r)
. Such a choice is made in order to provide a necessary coupling of the interaction (
n
and
n^{\prime}\leq N
) and the continuum (
n
or
n^{\prime}>N
) regions of the Hamiltonian matrix
\widetilde{H}_{nn^{\prime}}^{l}
.

It is noteworthy that
the modified
Efros method
of Ref.
[
21
]

is equivalent to
the
HORSE approach if
{v=N+1}
. However, the advantage of
the
Efros method is that one can obtain adequate results which converge to numerical solution
if
{v<N+1}

and in many cases even if
v\ll N+1
.
Besides, matrix elements
A_{nn^{\prime}}
and vector components
B_{n}
are readily obtained if one uses expressions (
15
) for
the
Coulomb functions
F_{l}(\eta,kr)
and
\widetilde{G}_{l}(\eta,kr)
with coefficients
S_{nl}(k)
and
C_{nl}(k)

that are easily
numerically calculated as discussed in Sec.
2
. Using
\varphi_{n^{\prime}l}(r)
or
\gamma_{\lambda}(r)
as SRFs
\bar{\beta}_{nl}(r)
, one also obtains finite sums in the
right-hand
sides of expressions (
21
) and (
22
). Thus, Efros method provides a simple and numerically efficient approach to finding phase shifts
\delta_{l}(k)

and wave functions in scattering of charged particles.

4
Results

We use
the Woods–Saxon potential
as the nuclear potential,

V^{Nucl,\,l}=\frac{V_{0}}{1+\exp{\left(\frac{r-R_{0}}{\alpha_{0}}\right)}}+(\mathbf{l}\cdot\mathbf{s})\frac{1}{r}\frac{d}{dr}\frac{V_{ls}}{1+\exp{\left(\frac{r-R_{1}}{\alpha_{1}}\right)}},

(25)

where
\mathbf{l}
and
\mathbf{s}
are the orbital momentum and the spin, respectively. To improve
the
convergence,
as it was shown in Ref.
[
18
]
, it is important to use smothing
[
24
]
of the
truncated
matrix of the
nuclear interaction (
7
), that is
\widetilde{V}_{nn^{\prime}}^{Nucl,\,l}
is replaced by

\mathbb{V}_{nn^{\prime}}^{Nucl,\,l}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\sigma_{n}V_{nn^{\prime}}^{Nucl,\,l}\sigma_{n^{\prime}}&\text{for $n$ and $n^{\prime}\leq N$},\\
0&\text{for $n$ or $n^{\prime}>N$},\end{array}\right.

(26)

where

\sigma_{n}=\frac{1-\exp{\left(-{\left[\frac{\alpha(n-N-1)}{N+1}\right]}^{2}\right)}}{1-\exp{(-\alpha^{2})}}.

(27)

In what follows, the smoothing parameter
\alpha=5
is used.
Note, the Coulomb interaction
\widetilde{V}_{nn^{\prime}}^{Coul,\,l}
[see Eq. (
2
)] should not be smoothed.
Following
the
Efros formalism, we calculate
the
phase shifts
\delta_{l}(k)
and corresponding partial
elastic scattering
cross sections

\sigma_{l}(k)=\frac{4\pi}{k^{2}}(2l+1)\sin^{2}\delta_{l}(k),

(28)

and compare the results with
those
obtained within HORSE method for Coulomb scattering discussed in Ref.
[
18
]
as well as with the results found by numerical integration of Schrödinger equation using Numerov method which
will
be referred
to as exact.

Let us consider
the

p{-}\alpha
scattering phase shifts
in the
p_{3/2}
partial wave.
We use the
Woods–Saxon
potential parameterization suggested in Ref.
[
25
]
with
{V_{0}=-43.0}
MeV,
R_{0}=2.0
fm,
\alpha_{0}=0.70
fm,
V_{ls}=40.0\text{ MeV}\cdot\text{fm}^{2}
,
R_{1}=1.50
fm, and
\alpha_{1}=0.35
fm. The calculations of phase shifts were performed for different
numbers of SRFs

v
and various sets of SRFs

\beta_{nl}(r)
and

\bar{\beta}_{nl}(r)

(note, we always choose the first
v
functions
\bar{\beta}_{nl}(r)
the same as
\beta_{nl}(r)
:

\bar{\beta}_{nl}(r)=\beta_{nl}(r)
for
n=0,\dots,v-1
)
at oscillator energy
\hbar\omega=27
MeV which yields the best results. We started with choosing the set of SRFs
\bar{\beta}_{nl}(r)
as

\bar{\beta}_{nl}^{Eig}(r)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}\gamma_{n}(r),&&n=0,\dots,v-1,\\
\varphi_{N+1,\,l}(r),&&n=v.\end{array}\right.

(29)

We note that the same set of SRFs
\bar{\beta}_{nl}(r)
was used in Ref.
[
21
]
for
the scattering of non-charged particles.
The results are shown in Fig.
1
. One can
see
that
the
Efros method provides adequate phase shifts which converge to their exact values even for small
v=4
. It seems, however, that choosing
v=2
leads to unsatisfying results for
{E>15}
MeV.
This is not surprising since the second (first excited) eigenstate in this case has an energy of 10.25 MeV; hence one
cannot expect an adequate description of the phase shifts at higher energies using only 2 SRFs and the phase shifts start
deviating from the exact ones starting from approximately 11 MeV.
Nevertheless, the
elastic
cross section
\sigma_{l}(k)
,
which, contrary to the phase shifts, is
an observable,
does not differ much from exact values since

in this region of energies
[see Eq. (

)].
The difference from the exact solution can be actually expressed numerically
by calculating the

s_{\sigma}

of the calculated cross section from the exact one
which appears to be quite small for all choices for
v
(see the inset
in

Conclusion

In this
paper, we
developed the description of scattering of charged particles within the version of
the Efros method utilizing the expansion of the scattering wave functions in oscillator functions.
Using model problems which can be exactly solved numerically by other methods, we demonstrated that the proposed approach
provides accurate results for scattering phase shifts and cross sections even when only a relatively small number of short-range
functions is used.
As a result, we obtained
a promising tool for the

ab initio

description of reactions with charged particles
based on the NCSM calculations.
