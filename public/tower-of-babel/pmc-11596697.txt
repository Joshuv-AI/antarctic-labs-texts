Identification and validation of a novel defined stress granule-related gene signature for predicting the prognosis of ovarian cancer via bioinformatics analysis
Authors: Xiaoqi Chen, Qianqian Han, Jing Song, Yongqiang Pu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Ovarian cancer (OC) is a malignant gynecological cancer with an extremely poor prognosis. Stress granules (SGs) are non-membrane organelles that respond to stressors; however, the correlation between SG-related genes and the prognosis of OC remains unclear. This systematic analysis aimed to determine the expression levels of SG-related genes between high- and low-risk groups of patients with OC and to explore the prognostic value of these genes. RNA-sequencing data and clinical information from GSE18520 and GSE14407 in the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) and ovarian plasmacytoma adenocarcinoma in The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) were downloaded. SG-related genes were obtained from GeneCards, the Molecular Signatures Database, and the literature. First, 13 SG-related genes were identified in the prognostic model using least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) Cox regression. The prognostic value of each SG-related gene for survival and its relationship with clinical characteristics were evaluated. Next, we performed a functional enrichment analysis of SG-related genes. The protein-protein interactions (PPI) of SG-related genes were visualized using Cytoscape with STRING. According to the median risk score from the LASSO Cox regression, a 13-gene signature was created. All patients with OC in TCGA cohort and GEO datasets were classified into high- and low-risk groups. Five SG-related genes were differentially expressed between the high- and low-risk OC groups in the GEO datasets. The 13 SG-related genes were related to several important oncogenic pathways (TNF-α signaling, PI3K–AKT–mTOR signaling, and WNT–β-catenin signaling) and several cellular components (cytoplasmic stress granule, cytoplasmic ribonucleoprotein granule, and ribonucleoprotein granule). The PPI network identified 11 hub genes with the strongest interactions with ELAVL1. These findings indicate that SG-related genes (DNAJA1, ELAVL1, FBL, GRB7, MOV10, PABPC3, PCBP2, PFN1, RFC4, SYNCRIP, USP10, ZFP36, and ZFP36L1) can be used to predict OC prognosis.

1. Introduction

Ovarian cancer (OC) is a common malignancy in females, with high recurrence and mortality rates.[1] It is essential to diagnose patients with OC early to improve their prognosis; however, due to the lack of effective screening methods for early detection, the overall survival of patients with OC remains poor.[2] The 5-year survival rate of patients with OC is only 30%.[3] Previous prognostic markers, such as cancer antigen (CA125), tumor stage, and tumor grade have limited their application due to their low efficacy. Reliable biomarkers for predicting the prognosis and underlying mechanisms of chemotherapy resistance in OC are lacking. Therefore, there is an urgent need to identify new, reliable novel biomarkers of OC

Stress granules (SGs) are assemblies of mRNA, RNA-binding proteins, and translation initiation factors formed as non-membrane organelles that respond to stress stimuli.[4–8] Several studies have revealed the link between SG formation and tumorigenesis. Growing evidence has demonstrated that SGs promote cell survival during stress-related damage and regulate tumor cell progression, invasion, metastasis, and drug resistance.[9,10] Thus, SGs are key regulatory hubs and promising therapeutic targets for cancer.[9] However, the specific functions of SG-related genes (SGRGs) in the prognosis of OC remain unclear.

In this study, we performed a systematic analysis to determine the expression levels of SGRGs between high- and low-risk groups of patients with OC and explored the prognostic value of these genes.

2. Methods

2.1. Data acquisition

Gene expression data for 381 patients with OC were obtained from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) using the R package TCGAbiolinks. RNA-sequencing (RNA-seq) data were transformed into fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads (FPKM) format. The corresponding clinical information was obtained from the University of California, Santa Cruz Xena.[11] The count sequencing data of the TCGA-OV dataset were standardized using the R package limma.

RNA-seq and clinical data of the GSE18520[12] and GSE14407[13] datasets (Table 1) were downloaded from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database using the R package GEOquery.[14] The microarray data of GSE18520 and GSE14407 were based on the GPL570 platform. The GSE18520 dataset comprised 53 colorectal cancer (CRC) tissues and 10 normal ovarian plasmacytoma adenocarcinoma (OV) tissues. The GSE14407 dataset comprised 12 OC tissues and 12 normal OV tissues. The GSE18520 and GSE14407 datasets were merged into a combined dataset.

We downloaded stress granule-related phenotype genes from the GeneCards database, Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB), and the literature. We obtained 197, 38, and 10 SGRGs from the GeneCards database,[15] MSigDB,[16] and the literature,[17] respectively. Finally, 199 SGRGs were obtained by intersecting all searched genes.

2.2. Cox regression analysis and prognostic analysis of SGRGs

Cox regression analysis was performed to select SGRGs significantly associated with prognosis, as determined by P < .1. The selected SGRGs were used as key genes for subsequent analyses. The expression of the key genes was used to establish a univariate Cox regression model with a P-value threshold of.1. Based on Cox regression models, independent prognostic factors were used to establish nomograms using the R package “rms” to predict the 1-, 2-, and 3-year survival rates of patients with OC. We assessed the accuracy and resolution of the nomograms using a calibration curve. The R package “ggDCA”[18] was used to draw decision curve analysis (DCA) maps and to evaluate the predictive effect of the nomogram model on the 1-, 3-, and 5-year survival outcomes of patients with OC.

2.3. Prognostic model construction and validation for SGRGs and clinically relevant analysis

The software package “glmnet”[19] performed the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO)[20] logistic regression analysis on the identified SGRGs. The parameters of the LASSO regression were set as seed = 2022 and family = “binomial,” and 1000 cycles were run to prevent overfitting. Using the risk score obtained from the established LASSO model, samples without normal or no prognostic information were divided into high- and low-risk groups. We then visualized the LASSO regression results, displayed the expression of each hub gene in the groups, and compared clinically relevant groups of hub genes.

2.4. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) screened by LASSO regression analysis

We used the R package “sva”[21] to process the GSE18520 and GSE14407 datasets to obtain combined datasets (including 65 OC and 22 control samples). Principal component analysis (PCA)[22] was performed on the processed datasets.

The samples in the TCGA-OV dataset were divided into high- and low-risk groups based on the risk scores obtained from the established LASSO model. TCGA-OV dataset and the merged dataset combined dataset were standardized, and DEGs between the different groups were then identified by the “limma”[23] software package. The significance of each DEG was analyzed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. The genes were considered to be upregulated when the adjusted P < .05 and the log fold change (FC) > .1. When the adjusted P < .05 and the logFC < .1, the genes were considered to be downregulated. The “ggplot2”[24] software package in R was used to draw volcano maps, difference sorting charts, and simple numerical heat maps.

We applied the LASSO model calculation coefficients from the TCGA-OV dataset to the combined datasets to obtain the risk score. After removing normal samples, the disease group samples in the combined datasets were then divided into high- and low-risk groups based on the risk score. Finally, hub genes were compared in the high- and low-risk groups of the TCGA-OV and combined datasets, respectively.

2.5. Gene ontology and Kyoto Encyclopedia Of Genes And Genomes pathway analysis

“clusterProfiler”[25] software was used to perform gene ontology (GO) function enrichment analyses, and an adjusted P < .05 and a false discovery rate (FDR) < .25 were considered significant. Benjamini–Hochberg (BH) was used as the corrective method.

2.6. Gene set enrichment analysis

Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was performed using the “clusterProfiler”[25] R package. According to the default statistical method, the parameters set in this process were seed = 2022, the calculation was repeated 1000 times, and each gene set contained at least 10 genes and a maximum of 500 genes. BH was used as the corrective method. Then, we selected “c2.all.v2022.1. Hs.symbols.gmt” in the MSigDB collection as the reference gene collection. An adjusted P < .05 and FDR < .25 indicated significant enrichment.

2.7. Gene set variation analysis

We constructed a gene set variation analysis (GSVA)[26] heatmap using the “GSVA” R package. We downloaded “h.all.v7.4.symbols.gmt” from the MSigDB database for the GSVA. Statistical significance was set at P < .05.

2.8. Protein–protein interaction (PPI) network

The STRING database was used to construct the PPI network of DEGs. The maximal clique centrality (MCC)[27] algorithm is a widely used performance indicator in bioinformatics. The minimum required score (0.4) was regarded as the threshold. We used “Cytoscape”[28] software (version 3.9.1) to visualize the PPI network model. The GeneMANIA[29] website predicted functionally similar genes among the screened hub genes and constructed an interaction network.

2.9. Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R (version 4.1.0), with continuous variables displayed as the mean ± standard deviation. The Wilcoxon rank-sum test was used to compare the 2 groups. Three or more groups were analyzed using the Kruskal–Wallis test. Survival curves were determined using the Kaplan–Meier method and analyzed with the log-rank test. Chi square test or Fisher exact test was used to compare and analyze the statistical significance between 2 sets of categorical variables. The results were calculated by Spearman correlation analysis to determine the correlation coefficients between different molecules. Statistical significance was set at P < .05 (two-sided). 3.1. Dataset normalization

First, the R package “sva” was used to remove batch effects from the combined GSE18520 and GSE14407 datasets, resulting in a merged GEO dataset. Box plots (Fig. 2A and B) and PCA plots (Fig. 2C and 2D) were used to compare the datasets before and after removing the batch effects. The box plot and PCA plot results indicated that after removing batch processing, the batch effect of the samples in the combined datasets was eliminated.

3.2. Cox regression to identify key SGRGs and related prognostic analysis

We used Cox regression analysis to obtain 17 SGRGs (FBL, ELAVL1, SRSF3, USP10, PABPC3, HNRNPC, HNRNPA2B1, MOV10, OGFOD1, ZFP36, ZFP36L1, PFN1, RFC4, PCBP2, GRB7, SYNCRIP, and DNAJA1). We included the expression levels of the 17 key genes in the univariate Cox regression analysis, constructed a univariate Cox regression model (Table 2), and plotted a forest map (Fig. 3A) and nomogram (Fig. 3B). In addition, we conducted calibration analysis on the nomogram (Fig. 3C). The fitting curves of 1-, 3-, and 5-year survival (Fig. 3C) have a high degree of overlap with the prediction curve, indicating that the diagnostic effect of the Cox model was good. Finally, we used DCA to evaluate the role of the Cox regression model in disease diagnosis and presented the 1-, 3-, and 5-year survival (Fig. 3D–F).

3.3. Prognostic model construction and validation for SGRGs and clinically relevant analysis

To determine the prognostic value of the 17 key genes in the TCGA-OV dataset, we used LASSO regression analysis to build a prognostic model and screened the key genes. A total of 13 hub genes (DNAJA1, ELAVL1, FBL, GRB7, MOV10, PABPC3, PCBP2, PFN1, RFC4, SYNCRIP, USP10, ZFP36, and ZFP36L1) were obtained (Table 3), and LASSO variable trajectories (Fig. 4A) and screening factor plots (Fig. 4B) were plotted. Using the median risk score obtained from the LASSO model as a boundary, the OC group was divided into high- and low-risk groups (Fig. 4C). In addition, we used receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves related to time (1-, 3-, and 5-years) to evaluate survival predictions for the 13 hub genes and LASSO models and used the “timeROC” package to calculate the area under the ROC curve to measure prognosis or predictive accuracy. The results showed that only GRB7 (Fig. 4D), PCBP2 (Fig. 4E), ZFP36L1 (Fig. 4F), ZFP36 (Fig. 4G), MOV10 (Fig. 4H), PABPC3 (Fig. 4I), FBL (Fig. 4J), and the LASSO model (Fig. 4K) accurately predicted survival at 1-, 3-, and 5-years.

3.4. Correlation of hub genes with clinical factors

First, we generated a correlation heatmap (Fig. 5A) and chromosomal mapping (Fig. 5B) of the 13 hub genes. The results showed that MOV10, RFC4, SYNCRIP, DNAJA1, PCBP2, PABPC3, ZFP36L1, USP10, PFN1, GRB7, ELAVL1, ZFP36, and FBL were located on chromosomes 1, 3, 6, 9, 12, 13, 14, 16, 17, 17, 19, 19, and 19, respectively. Finally, a differential analysis of clinical relevance was conducted on the 13 hub genes in subgroups of clinical age, primary tumor output, tumor residual, progression-free interval (PFI) event, clinical stage, race, and venous invasion (Table 4), and a violin chart was drawn. The results were as follows: The expression of FBL, MOV10, and DNAJA1 might be related to clinical age (≤60 and >60 years; P < .05, Fig. 5C, G, and O), while those of ELAVL1, USP10, and PABPC3 might be related to the clinical primary thermal output (partial response (PR) and complete response (CR); P < .05, Fig. 5D, E, F). Furthermore, the expression of ZFP36 and ZFP36L1 may be related to the presence of clinical tumor residuals (P < .05, Fig. 5H and I), whereas that of PFN1 may be associated with the presence of clinical PFI events (P < .01, Fig. 5J). The expression of RFC4 might be related to the clinical stage (III and IV; P < .01, Fig. 5K), whereas that of PCBP2 might be related to race (Asian and Caucasian; P < .01, Fig. 5L). Finally, the expression of GRB7 and SYNCRIP may be associated with the presence of clinical venous invasion (P < .05; Fig. 5M and N).

3.5. DEGs screened by LASSO regression analysis

A total of 3119 DEGs were identified in the TCGA-OV dataset, including 1387 upregulated and 1732 downregulated genes. The DEGs were visualized using a volcano map (Fig. 6A) and a difference ranking chart (Fig. 6B). A heatmap of the 13 hub genes (DNAJA1, ELAVL1, FBL, GRB7, MOV10, PABPC3, PCBP2, PFN1, RFC4, SYNCRIP, USP10, ZFP36, and ZFP36L1) in the high- and low-risk groups of the TCGA-OV dataset was plotted (Fig. 6C). As shown in Figure 6A, FBL, ZFP36, ZFP36L1, GRB7, and PABPC3 were downregulated, whereas DNAJA1, PFN1, SYNCRIP, RFC4, USP10, PCBP2, ELAVL1, and MOV10 were upregulated. We compared the expression levels of the hub genes in the high- and low-risk groups of TCGA-OV (Fig. 6D) and combined datasets (Fig. 6E). Thirteen SGRGs were differentially expressed between the high- and low-risk OC groups in TCGA (Fig. 6D). In contrast, 5 SGRGs (ELAVL1, FBL, GRB7, SYNCRIP, and USP10) were differentially expressed in the GEO datasets (Fig. 6E).

3.6. GO and KEGG pathway analysis

The functions of the hub genes were predicted using GO analysis in Metascape. The results showed that the 13 hub genes were mainly related to the biological processes (BP) of regulation of mRNA stability (GO:0043488), regulation of RNA stability (GO:0043487), regulation of mRNA catabolic process (GO:0061013), and 3′-UTR-mediated mRNA destabilization (GO:0061158); the cellular components (CC) of cytoplasmic stress granule (GO:0010494), cytoplasmic ribonucleoprotein granule (GO:0036464), ribonucleoprotein granule (GO:0035770), and P-body (GO:0000932); the molecular functions (MF) of mRNA 3′-UTR AU-rich region binding (GO:0035925), mRNA 3′-UTR binding (GO:0003730), 14-3-3 protein binding (GO:0071889), and heat shock protein binding (GO:0031072) (Table 5). The GO results are visualized using a bar chart (Fig. 7A) and network diagrams (Fig. 7B). Based on the GO analysis, the z-score corresponding to each gene was calculated using the logFC values of the 13 hub genes in the different analyses between the high- and low-risk groups of the TCGA-OV dataset. The results of the GO/KEGG enrichment analysis combined with logFC were visualized using circular (Fig. 7C) and chord charts (Fig. 7D).

3.9. PPI network

We performed PPI enrichment analysis to better understand the potential biological functions of the 13 hub genes in OC. Among the 13 hub genes, except for the absence of interactions between PABPC3, PFN1, and other genes, all the key genes interacted with at least 1 hub gene, with the highest number of interactions found between ELAVL1 and other genes (Fig. 10A). We used the maximum clique centrality (MCC) to calculate the hub gene scores. Based on this score, we used a gradient color from red to yellow to arrange the hub genes and visualized them using Cytoscape. As shown in Figure 10B, ELAVL1 ranked first. 4. Discussion

OC is the most difficult tumor of the female reproductive system to treat. The leading causes of the high mortality rate in OC are low diagnostic rates in the early stages, recurrence, and chemotherapy drug resistance.[30] Approximately 70% of patients with OC are diagnosed at an advanced stage as they are typically asymptomatic in the early stage.[31] Although the standard treatment is appropriate surgery followed by platinum-based systematic chemotherapy, the long-term survival of patients remains unsatisfactory. Therefore, it is crucial to understand the molecular mechanisms underlying the occurrence and progression of OC. CA125, circulating cell-free DNA (cfDNA), tumor stage, and tumor grade are prognostic markers[32–34]; however, the low efficacy of these biomarkers limits their applications. Therefore, it is essential to determine reliable novel prognostic biomarkers for OC to improve the prognosis and the understanding of the underlying mechanisms.

SGs are dynamically regulated.[4] They occur under stressful conditions or during cancer drug treatment and usually disappear following recovery from stress.[35] Patients with tumors often encounter stressful environments. Dysregulated signaling pathways in cancer contribute to SG formation by modifying canonical molecules in these pathways.[36,37] SGs may play a role in inhibiting cell senescence by recruiting plasminogen activator inhibitor-1, a known promoter of senescence, and regulating the level of specific protein 1, a transcription factor that regulates the expression of SG-nucleating proteins, such as G3BP1, TIA-1/TIAR, eIF4G, hnRNPK, and HuR.[38,39] Moreover, SGs suppress cell apoptosis by sequestering pro-apoptotic factors and inhibiting the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS). In addition, SG formation induced by chemotherapeutic agents is involved in resistance to chemotherapy-induced cell death.[40]

In the present study, we identified 13 SGRGs (DNAJA1, ELAVL1, FBL, GRB7, MOV10, PABPC3, PCBP2, PFN1, RFC4, SYNCRIP, USP10, ZFP36, ZFP36L1) that constituted a credible prognostic predictive gene signature and revealed their effects in OC. There is evidence that 5 SGRGs are associated with OC development. GRB7 is a known adaptor protein involved in signal transduction from the cell surface to specific signaling pathways.[41–43] A previous study demonstrated that the overexpression of GRB7 and GRB7v was more likely to lead to high-grade tumors and exert tumorigenic functions through different signaling pathways in OC cells.[44] Moreover, miR-193a-3p modulates GRB7 in the MAPK/ERK signaling pathway, thereby promoting the oncogenic features of OC cells in vitro and in vivo.[45] Another study reported that targeting the GRB7–ERK–FOXM1 signaling pathway impaired the aggressiveness of OC cells.[46] In the present study, we analyzed the relationship between the expression of 13 SGRGs to predict the prognosis of OC and clinically related characteristics. In agreement with other research findings, our results suggest that GRB7 is accurate in predicting the survival of patients with OC and is also related to clinical venous invasion that can cause tumor metastasis. Our results further support its clinical value as a potential biomarker. A recent study suggested that Apurinic/apyrimidinic endodeoxyribonuclease 1 promotes stress granule formation via Y-Box binding protein 1 phosphorylation in OC.[47] Our research suggests that GRB7 is associated with clinical venous invasion, providing a new perspective on the mechanism of action of GRB7 in OC. In the future, research on the correlation between GRB7 and OC can be conducted from the perspective of clinical venous invasion. PABPC3 is listed among the most mutated genes in malignant ovarian germ cell tumors as a member of a highly conserved family of cytoplasmic poly(A)-binding proteins.[48,49] Interestingly, our data also showed that the predictive accuracy of PABPC3 for the prognosis of ovarian plasmacytomatous adenocarcinoma might be related to its impact on PR and CR in the clinical primary thermal output. Nevertheless, the role and mechanism of PABPC3 in ovarian plasmacytoma adenocarcinoma requires further study. PCBP2 plays an oncogenic role in cancer. A previous study showed that PCBP2 was involved in cell proliferation in bladder cancer.[50] This finding was consistent with our study. PFN1, a G-actin-binding protein, exerts an anti-migratory action on OC cells.[51] USP10 is a member of the Ub-specific protease family. Decreased USP10 expression is an indicator of poor prognosis in OC.[52] Low USP10 expression and a combined USP10/p14ARF low expression were revealed to be independent prognostic factors. Our data indicated that high expression of PFN1 and USP10 may lead to the occurrence of PFI and CR, respectively, in the clinical primary thermal output. In OC, PFN10 and USP10 may play antitumor roles. These results suggest that SGRGs can be used to predict the prognosis of patients with OC.

In addition, we performed GO and KEGG enrichment analyses to determine the functions of the 13 SGRGs. Our findings indicated that the SGRGs were predominantly involved in the CC of cytoplasmic SGs (GO: 0010494), cytoplasmic ribonucleoprotein granules (GO:0036464), and ribonucleoprotein granules (GO: 0035770). Studies have shown that the synthesis of stress particles in cancers increases due to the stressful environment[53] and that stress particles can be a promising therapeutic target.[9] Enrichment analyses revealed that these SGRGs were relevant to OC. In our study, GSEA showed that the pathways of resistin as a regulator of inflammation, clock-controlled autophagy in bone metabolism, hypoxia by DMOG upregulation, and the IL5 pathway were negatively correlated with the 13-gene signature model. Therefore, we speculated that the upregulation of SGRGs might influence the tumor inflammatory microenvironment in OC. The GSVA showed that the genes were significantly enriched in pathways such as TNFα signaling via NFκB, PI3K–AKT–mTOR signaling, p53 pathway, Notch signaling, and Wnt-β-catenin signaling. These signaling pathways also participate in cellular stress response. Therefore, SGRGs may have therapeutic potential. We constructed a PPI network and identified 11 hub genes: DNAJA1, ELAVL1, FBL, GRB7, MOV10, PCBP2, RFC4, SYNCRIP, USP10, ZFP36, and ZFP36L1. Additionally, we observed the strongest interaction between ELAVL1 and other genes. ELAVL1, an important RNA-binding protein, is highly expressed in various tumors. ELAVL1 is not only involved in the occurrence and progression of tumors but is also related to chemotherapy resistance.[54–57]

Despite the meaningful discovery of the prognostic value of SGRGs in OC in this study, there were some limitations. First, the data were obtained from public databases. Although the quality of the data was high, there was a lack of validation studies targeting specific populations, which may have affected the generalizability of the results. Second, the factors we considered may not cover all relevant variables, which may lead to 1-sided conclusions. Due to the lack of experimental validation, we could not confirm the mechanism of action of these genes in OC. Finally, the 3 datasets used in this study may have resulted in unavoidable inter-batch differences or insufficient sample sizes during the analysis, requiring a larger sample size to ensure the reliability and stability of the experiment. In response to these limitations, future studies should expand the sample size, conduct multicenter validation, and delve deeper into the functions of related genes to gain a more comprehensive understanding of their roles in developing OC.

5. Conclusions

In conclusion, we identified 13 SGRGs that provide a novel gene signature for predicting the prognosis of OC and response to chemotherapy. However, further studies (in vivo and in vitro experiments) are necessary to clarify the functions and possible mechanisms of action of these hub genes in OC.
