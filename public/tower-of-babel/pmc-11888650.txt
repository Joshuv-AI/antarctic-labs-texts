Accuracy and Reliability of Oral Exfoliative Cytology in Age Estimation: An Observational Study
Authors: Naseemoon Shaik, Vivek Anne, Saraswathi Saisupraja Mantha, Medapati Murali Jogi Reddy, Vidya Priyadarshini, B Tanuja, Sriharsha Pudi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAim and Objectives:Age and gender identification are critical in forensic investigations, especially for unidentified human bodies. This study explores differences in nuclear size, nuclear area, and nuclear-cytoplasmic ratio between healthy individuals and those with physiological or pathological conditions, aiming to understand how these variations may influence age estimation.Materials and Methods:Following ethical clearance, the study was conducted on 80 participants aged 20–35 years, who were divided into four groups of 20 each, Group 1: Healthy individuals; Group 2: Individuals with physiological variations; Group 3: Individuals with pathological variations; and Group 4: Individuals with acquired conditions. Buccal cell samples were collected using a moistened tongue blade. Measurements of cell size, nuclear dimensions, nuclear area, and the nuclear-cytoplasmic ratio were taken.Results:Significant differences in nuclear and cell sizes were observed among participants with acquired conditions and metabolic disorders compared to healthy controls.Conclusion:The study suggests that systemic diseases and tobacco use (without visible oral lesions) can significantly affect cellular morphology, which may have implications for the accuracy of age determination in forensic analyses.

INTRODUCTION

The cell is the fundamental unit of all living organisms. Normal epithelial tissue undergoes exfoliation as part of its natural turnover. Exfoliative cytology is the microscopic study of shed cells shed from body surfaces, lesions, or fluids. It relies on the natural process of epithelial shedding.

Forensic investigations often require determining the age and gender of unidentified individuals. While DNA can identify gender, age estimation is less straightforward and typically involves less accurate methods, such as skeletal and dental assessments. Forensic Age Estimation (FAE) focuses on determining a person’s chronological age using various techniques, including radiological and dental analysis.[1]

The mouth is ideal for studying aging signs.[2] Oral exfoliative cytology is a simple, non-invasive procedure with high sensitivity and specificity.[3] Most research in this field focuses on pathological conditions; however, this study compares nuclear variations in healthy individuals with those in physiological and pathological states.

METHODOLOGY

After obtaining institutional ethical clearance, 150 subjects aged 20–40 years were initially screened, and 80 eligible participants were selected and divided into four groups of 20 individuals each. The exclusion criteria included subjects under 20 or over 40 years, those on long-term medication, those with oral infectious lesions, and individuals with systemic diseases not covered by the inclusion criteria. Group 1 consisted of healthy individuals; Group 2 included pregnant women (physiological variation); Group 3 involved subjects with metabolic diseases such as diabetes and thyroid disorders (pathological variation); and Group 4 included tobacco users (acquired variation). Written informed consent was obtained from each participant, and details such as age, occupation, medical history, and site investigated were recorded. Oral examinations were conducted using a mouth mirror and light, and subjects rinsed their mouths before sample collection. Exfoliated cells from the right buccal mucosa were collected using a moistened wooden spatula, spread on glass slides, and fixed with 95% ethanol. Samples were stained with Papanicolaou stain and examined under a ZEISS HBO 50 microscope. Images were captured using a CARL ZEISS B × 41 microscope, and an average of 20 clearly defined cells per smear was measured, avoiding distorted or clumped cells. Nuclear and cell diameters were recorded and statistically analyzed.

DISCUSSION

The use of exfoliative cytology in cancer diagnosis was initially introduced by Walsh in 1843. However, its significance in clinical practice remained minimal until Papanicolaou and Traut revolutionized the field with their contributions to the cytological detection of unsuspected uterine cancer.[4] Since then, cytological techniques have expanded, playing an important role not only in gynecology but also in the early detection of various cancers, including oral cancer.[5]

The normal histological structure of the oral epithelium consists of stratified squamous cells. It includes the innermost basal layer (stratum germinativum), followed by the prickle cell layer (stratum spinosum) and the outer cornified layer (stratum corneum). When keratin is present, a granular layer may also be observed. Exfoliative cytology involves scraping the surface epithelium, potentially dislodging cells from all layers, including the basal cells. These basal cells are small, oval-shaped, and typically about five times larger than leukocytes. The nucleus occupies approximately one-third to one-quarter of the cell and is relatively larger compared to other cell types. Basal cells also exhibit basophilic cytoplasm. Prickle cells, found between basal and cornified cells, are larger with an irregular shape and flatter appearance. Their nuclei are smaller and rounder, while the cytoplasm stains in shades ranging from pink to faint orange. Cornified cells are identified by their organophilic staining. As epithelial cells mature, they become compressed and flattened, leading to a decrease in nuclear activity and the condensation of nuclear chromatin into a dense, structureless mass, known as pyknosis. In the final stages of cornification, the nucleus disappears, leaving behind a thin layer of squamous epithelium.[6]

Cellular changes are often observed in patients with endocrine or respiratory conditions, which may reduce the rate of keratinization, leading to an increase in nuclear size or diameter.[7] In smokers, alterations in keratinization are common, often accompanied by epithelial dysplasia and chronic inflammatory infiltrates, which exhibit similar cytological patterns.[8] To date, no studies in the literature have compared all four variables simultaneously. Our results showed statistically significant differences in both nuclear and cell diameters in individuals with acquired conditions and metabolic disorders compared to the control group. However, the differences in nuclear and cell diameters in pregnant women (physiological variation) were not statistically significant when compared to those in the control group.

Limitations

The study’s sample size and age range (20–40 years) may limit the generalizability of the findings to other populations. Excluding individuals with oral infections and long-term medication use may have missed other factors affecting cytomorphometric variations. Additionally, only buccal smears were used, which may not fully capture changes across different oral sites.

CONCLUSION

Systemic conditions and lifestyle factors, such as metabolic disorders and tobacco use, significantly impact buccal cell cytomorphometry. These variations may influence forensic age estimation, emphasizing the need for careful interpretation in individuals with underlying health issues. Further research with a broader sample and additional variables is recommended.
