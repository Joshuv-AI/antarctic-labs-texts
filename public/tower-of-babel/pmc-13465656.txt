Moral courage in health sciences students: a systematic review of challenges, influencing factors, and consequences
Authors: Kosar Kordkatuli, Sare Manoucher, Kosar Bayat, Amirali Alizadeh, Soheyla Kalantari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMoral courage is the critical bridge between ethical knowledge and ethical action in healthcare. While health sciences students often possess high moral sensitivity, they frequently struggle to act on their values due to the “theory-practice gap” and the pressures of clinical hierarchies. The suppression of moral courage not only compromises patient safety but is also closely associated with moral distress and professional burnout among trainees.ObjectivesThis systematic review aims to critically analyze the global evidence regarding the prevalence of moral courage among health sciences students and to identify the barriers, facilitators, and consequences associated with moral courage in educational settings.MethodsThis systematic review was conducted in accordance with PRISMA guidelines and registered in PROSPERO (CRD420251057915). A comprehensive search was performed across PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, the Cochrane Library, and Google Scholar to identify relevant studies published from 2000 to March 2025. The methodological quality of the included studies was assessed using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) critical appraisal tools.ResultsA total of 21 studies, comprising 4,719 participants from diverse cultural contexts, were included. The findings reveal a disciplinary divergence in moral courage: nursing students generally exhibited moderate-to-high levels, whereas medical and dental students reported lower levels, often constrained by rigid hierarchies. The primary barriers identified were the “hidden curriculum,” fear of negative evaluation, and hierarchical power dynamics. Conversely, key facilitators included positive role modeling, a strong sense of moral agency, and supportive peer environments.ConclusionStudents largely perceive the deficit in moral courage as a systemic issue rather than an individual failure. Current educational models often prioritize compliance over advocacy, creating a perceived conflict between academic survival and ethical action for trainees. To foster active moral agents, medical education must reframe moral courage as a trainable “non-technical skill” and implement systemic reforms to dismantle the culture of silence in clinical environments.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-026-09619-4.

Introduction

In the rapidly evolving landscape of modern healthcare, ethical competence is a critical determinant of patient safety and clinical quality. As healthcare systems face increasing complexity, driven by technological advancements, resource scarcity, and global health crises, professionals are frequently confronted with high-stakes ethical dilemmas. While moral sensitivity enables clinicians to recognize these conflicts, it is moral courage that empowers them to act. Defined as the capacity to overcome fear and stand up for core values despite the risk of reprisal [1, 2], moral courage serves as the final barrier against medical error and patient harm [3]. Recent literature underscores that the absence of moral courage does not merely result in silence; it is strongly associated with moral injury and burnout. This phenomenon has become increasingly prevalent among healthcare workers globally in the post-pandemic era [4, 5].

The cultivation of moral courage is particularly precarious during professional training. Health sciences students undergo a rigorous socialization process where they transition from the idealistic environment of the classroom to the hierarchical reality of clinical practice. Here, they encounter a profound Theory-Practice Gap [6]. While formal curricula emphasize advocacy and transparency, the hidden curriculum can implicitly teach that conformity and obedience to superiors are the keys to academic survival [7]. A substantial proportion of trainees, ranging from 40% to 60% in various educational settings, witness unethical behaviors or unprofessional conduct by supervisors but choose to remain silent [8, 9]. This perceived systemic suppression frequently leads students into a state of moral distress. In such situations, trainees often report knowing the right action but feeling institutionally powerless to execute it [10].

Given the high likelihood that students will encounter unethical behavior during their clinical training, moral courage represents an essential clinical competency. However, it remains unclear how often trainees actually deploy this courage when faced with real-world ethical dilemmas. Current educational models frequently fall short in equipping students with the “non-technical skills” required to navigate power gradients and ethical conflicts effectively [6, 11].

While existing research establishes that moral courage is vital for patient advocacy and professional integrity, the literature remains fragmented. Previous reviews have predominantly focused on practicing nurses and nursing students [9, 12] or specific isolated interventions [13], failing to provide a comprehensive synthesis across the broader spectrum of health sciences students. However, emerging primary studies indicate that the struggle for moral courage extends deeply into other clinical disciplines. For instance, medical students frequently grapple with steep hierarchical power dynamics during clinical clerkships. This dynamic often results in reported silence regarding medical errors or unprofessional conduct by attending physicians [14, 15]. Similarly, dental students face unique ethical dilemmas, such as the pressure of clinical quotas, overtreatment, and balancing patient autonomy, which severely test their ethical resilience [16, 17]. Allied health and pharmacy students also encounter significant interprofessional barriers when attempting to advocate for patient safety. Understanding the unique ecosystem of barriers (e.g., hierarchical fear, evaluation anxiety) and facilitators (e.g., resilience, role modeling) specific to the student population is essential. This understanding is a critical step toward bridging the gap between ethical knowing and ethical doing.

To address this gap, this systematic review aims to analyze the global evidence regarding moral courage in health sciences students. Specifically, this study seeks to identify the challenges that inhibit moral courage, the influencing factors that promote it, and the consequences of moral courage for both the student’s professional identity and the healthcare system. The findings may inform the development of evidence-based pedagogical strategies that transform students from passive observers into active moral agents.

Methods

Protocol and registration

We conducted a systematic review of papers published from 2000 to March 12, 2025, to examine the literature regarding moral courage in health sciences students. This study was conducted in accordance with the PRISMA guidelines [18]. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of Golestan University of Medical Sciences (approval number: IR.GOUMS.REC.1403.470). Following a preliminary search, the systematic review protocol was developed and registered in PROSPERO [19] under registration number CRD420251057915.

Literature search

To collect relevant data, a comprehensive search was conducted across international databases, including PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, and the Cochrane Library. The search strategy was structured to combine terms relating to health sciences education (e.g., “health sciences students” OR “nursing students” OR “medical students”) AND terms related to moral courage (e.g., “moral courage” OR “courage”). These terms were applied to the titles and abstracts of records. Additionally, a subject-based search using Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) was performed in PubMed. To ensure comprehensive coverage, the reference lists of eligible studies were manually screened, and supplementary searches were conducted in Google Scholar using the main keywords. Furthermore, the “Cited by” and “Related articles” features were examined to identify additional relevant studies. Detailed search strategies employed across the databases are provided in [Supplementary Table 1].

Study selection and Data extraction

Following the retrieval of records, all citations were imported into EndNote (Version 20, Clarivate Analytics), and duplicates were removed by one reviewer (K.K.). Subsequently, two independent reviewers (S.M. and A.A.) screened the titles and abstracts to identify studies meeting the inclusion criteria. In cases of disagreement, consensus was reached through discussion. When necessary, a third reviewer (K.K.) was consulted to reach a final decision. The full texts of all potentially eligible studies were then retrieved and assessed. Review articles, theses, conference abstracts without full texts, and studies published outside the defined time frame were excluded.

Key data, including the title, first author, publication year, location, study design, sample size, target population, instruments used, and main findings, were independently extracted by two reviewers (S.M. and A.A.). These extracted data were then entered into a Microsoft Excel 2016 spreadsheet for analysis. Due to the methodological heterogeneity of the measurement tools across the primary studies, direct numerical synthesis was not feasible. Therefore, the reported “levels” of moral courage were extracted and categorized. This categorization was based strictly on the specific cut-off points and qualitative interpretations provided by the original authors of each study. Any discrepancies between the reviewers during the screening and data extraction stages were resolved through consultation with the third reviewer (K.K.).

Methodological quality assessment

All included studies underwent quality assessment using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Critical Appraisal Tools. Specific tools were utilized for each study type, including cross-sectional [20], quasi-experimental [21], and qualitative designs [22]. Each appraisal item was rated as “Yes”, “No”, “Unclear”, or “Not Applicable” (NA). To determine the overall quality, the proportion of “Yes” responses was calculated. Studies with ≥ 75% “Yes” responses were classified as having a low risk of bias (high quality). Those scoring between 50% and 74% were categorized as moderate risk, and those with < 50% as having a high risk of bias (low quality) [Supplementary Tables 2–4]. Based on these criteria, 18 studies were classified as high quality, 1 study as moderate quality, and 2 studies as low quality. Visual representations of the quality scores across the different study designs are provided in the supplementary files. All steps of the review process were conducted in strict adherence to PRISMA guidelines to ensure transparency and reproducibility.

Results

Search results

The article selection process is illustrated in [Figure 1]. The initial search across electronic databases identified a total of 1,568 records. Specifically, these included 577 from PubMed, 407 from Web of Science, 550 from Embase, and 32 from the Cochrane Library. Additionally, one record was identified through a manual search in Google Scholar. After removing 611 duplicates, 957 records remained for screening. Of these, 907 were excluded based on title and abstract screening, as they did not meet the eligibility criteria. Consequently, the full texts of 50 articles were assessed for eligibility. Thirty articles were subsequently excluded for the following reasons: failing to address moral courage (n = 13) or not focusing on the target population of health sciences students (n = 17). Ultimately, 21 studies, comprising a total of 4,719 students, fulfilled the inclusion criteria and were included in this systematic review. The characteristics of the included studies are presented in [Table 1].

Discussion

This systematic review synthesized evidence from 21 studies to analyze the moral landscape of health sciences students. Overall, our findings indicate that while students perceive peer support and positive role modeling as primary facilitators of moral action, they consistently identify the hidden curriculum, fear of negative evaluation, and hierarchical power dynamics as primary barriers. These perceptions highlight what has been described in the literature as the moral performance gap. Students often report high moral sensitivity, yet perceive that their transition to moral action is structurally inhibited. Mosavi et al. [6] framed this perceived disconnect not merely as a lack of skill, but as a compromised sense of moral agency. In this context, students report that they possess the cognitive competence to identify ethical breaches but perceive a lack of professional power to intervene.

Institutionalized vulnerability and the survival paradox

The literature consistently identifies fear of reprisal as the primary perceived inhibitor of moral courage. However, a deeper analysis reveals that students often frame this hesitation not as individual timidity, but as a rational response to institutionalized vulnerability. As Caldicott et al. [36] highlighted, trainees frequently perceive a direct tradeoff between academic survival and patient interests. This dynamic creates a perceived conflict where students report that the hidden curriculum implicitly rewards compliance over courageous advocacy.

Critically, this review identifies that students’ experiences of suppression are highly context-dependent. For example, Caldicott et al. [36] noted that the fear of speaking up was not uniform. Instead, it was reported to be significantly heightened in high-stakes, high-hierarchy rotations, such as surgery and obstetrics/gynecology, compared to pediatrics. This variation indicates that students perceive a greater necessity for, and difficulty in exercising, moral courage within specific departmental microcultures. Furthermore, Martinez et al. [38] observed an association between this sense of vulnerability correlates directly with hierarchical rank, with interns reporting more inhibition than residents. This indicates that students perceive the medical hierarchy as a structural barrier that heavily discourages action among the most junior members of the healthcare team.

Importantly, while these specific examples highlight medical trainees, our synthesis reveals that this perceived vulnerability is not exclusive to medical students. Rather, it is commonly reported across health sciences education. Nursing students similarly experience feelings of vulnerability when confronting senior ward staff or hospital managers [28]. Likewise, dental students report a comparable sense of powerlessness when challenging the authority of clinical instructors who evaluate their practical requirements [6]. Thus, the perceived tension between academic standing and ethical action appears to affect multiple disciplines, even though the specific hierarchical actors exerting the pressure may differ.

Professional identity formation: the logic of care vs. cure

The synthesis of findings suggests a divergence in how students perceive their professional identity formation and structural barriers between disciplines. Nursing students, as noted by Bickhoff et al. [23], often report internalizing a patient advocate identity early in their training. This identity often serves as a moral anchor, compelling them to act despite perceived risks. However, they frequently describe their primary barrier to moral courage as manifesting within ward dynamics, such as the fear of alienating senior nurses or physicians in a team-based setting [27, 28].

In contrast, medical students frequently report feeling socialized into an environment where they perceive that clinical competence and hierarchical deference are prioritized over ethical advocacy. For medical students, the perceived structural barrier is highly hierarchical. They feel their academic progression heavily relies on subjective evaluations from attending physicians. Students report that this intense evaluative pressure makes them particularly susceptible to the perceived risks of speaking up compared to other disciplines [39]. This perceived structural difference may explain why Heggestad et al. found that medical students were more prone to a gradual desensitization to ethical issues when they lacked role models to validate their moral intuitions.

Furthermore, our review highlights a distinct perceived structural reality for dental students. Unlike medical or nursing students who operate within large ward hierarchies, dental students report navigating ethical dilemmas tied to their specific educational framework. For instance, they often describe challenges related to fulfilling rigid clinical procedural quotas. For these students, perceived barriers to moral courage are frequently linked to the conflict between patient autonomy (e.g., overtreatment) and the academic pressure to complete graduation requirements. Facilitators for this group often rely heavily on ethical guidance from individual clinical instructors rather than broad team support [6, 40].

If the organizational structure is perceived to fail to support moral courage, what sustains it? Our analysis points to specific protective buffers.

Educational implications: from passive knowledge to active agency

The persistent gap between knowing and doing challenges current educational models. Theoretical ethics classes often fail to replicate the high-pressure reality of clinical dilemmas. Xiao et al. [31] and DeSimone demonstrate that efficacy lies in immersive ethical rehearsal. While we acknowledge that the inherent social disincentives and intrinsic difficulties of moral courage will always remain in real clinical settings, we hypothesize that simulation can provide a psychologically safe environment for students to effectively practice identifying ethical content and formulating their responses. Just as students simulate cardiac arrest management to build procedural proficiency, we propose that they must simulate speaking up to build habitual ethical responses.

We argue that moral courage must be reframed from an optional virtue to a non-technical skill (NTS), assessed with the same rigor as diagnostic reasoning. However, building ethical resilience at the student level is likely futile if the clinical environment remains punitive. Our findings suggest that the penalization of advocacy—often manifesting as poor academic evaluations, ostracization, or public reprimand by superiors—is deeply ingrained in traditional healthcare hierarchies. Addressing these deeply entrenched systems requires a top-down institutional shift rather than relying solely on individual student initiative. Reformation must begin with faculty development, training clinical instructors to actively reward, rather than punish, questioning behavior. Furthermore, institutions must establish structural safety nets, such as secure, anonymous reporting mechanisms and strictly enforced zero-retaliation policies. Without these systemic reforms, demanding moral courage from vulnerable students risks asking them to assume significant professional risk.

Limitations

This systematic review has several limitations. First, the heterogeneity of the measurement tools across the included studies restricted the feasibility of a quantitative meta-analysis. Second, the reliance on self-reported questionnaires in the majority of studies may introduce reporter bias. This not only risks students overestimating their own moral courage in hypothetical scenarios, but it also introduces student bias regarding the actual facilitators and barriers to moral courage. Consequently, the data heavily relies on their subjective perceptions. Third, the geographic distribution of studies was uneven, and the restriction to English and Persian languages may have excluded relevant data from other cultural contexts.

Conclusion

This review concludes that students largely perceive the deficit in moral courage as a reflection of perceived systemic barriers rather than individual deficiency. However, it is important to note that this perception—attributing moral hesitation to external systemic barriers rather than personal shortcomings—is not unique to health sciences students and is frequently at the root of general moral hesitation. The rigid hierarchies and punitive micro-cultures of certain specialties are perceived to force students to choose between their conscience and their career.

Therefore, cultivating moral courage requires a dual evolution: educating students to build ethical resilience, while simultaneously reforming clinical environments to dismantle the perceived culture of silence that penalizes advocacy. Crucially, mitigating these systemic barriers is desirable not because it makes exhibiting moral courage “easier”—as courage inherently implies acting amidst difficulty—but because it is fundamentally desirable to minimize the occurrence of clinical problems that require its exercise in the first place.

To broaden this field, we recommend that future researchers move beyond descriptive, cross-sectional studies reliant on self-reported data. Future scholarship should prioritize interventional research to empirically evaluate the effectiveness of immersive ethical rehearsals and simulation-based training. Additionally, longitudinal studies tracking students as they transition from academic training into independent clinical practice are essential to understand how moral courage is sustained or eroded over a professional lifespan.
