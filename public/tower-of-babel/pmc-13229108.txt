Reply to Ritson-Williams et al. and Loya and van Woesik: Coral replacement research is vital due to uncertainty and unprecedented stress
Authors: Alejandro E. Camacho, David A. Dana, Mikhail Matz
Published: 2026

We appreciate hearing counterarguments (1, 2) to our coral ecological replacement (ER) proposal (3). We share concerns about the state of Caribbean coral ecosystems and the potential risks and uncertainties of more interventionist ecological strategies like ER. However, these worries actually serve to support our proposal for rigorous, democratically informed multistage research exploring ER’s tradeoffs.

The submitted commentary has three broad recurrent themes: 1) ER can cause harm, most notably through unintended introduction of pathogens; 2) it may not work, either because introduced corals would not survive or would be unable to establish symbiosis; and 3) ER may only be considered when all other options are exhausted. We agree on all three accounts. We never called for moving corals between oceans right away, far from it. In our view as well, ER is likely the solution of last resort. But that does not mean that preparatory research and legal work to allow such a solution should be held back until the last minute, until “Caribbean species are ecologically extinct across their entire range.”

In our view, some of the perceived risks that they identify can be addressed relatively easily. First, the risk of pathogen introduction can be reduced by rearing nonnative corals from larvae in near-sterile conditions followed by quarantine in a mesocosm. It is worth noting that there have already been hundreds of nonnative species introductions into the Caribbean (4), so the common Indo-Pacific microbes are likely already there.

Second, at least some Indo-Pacific corals are able to survive in the Caribbean. A mushroom coral Lobactis scutaria has been persisting in Jamaica for decades (5), and a patch of Acropora tenuis (a superrecruiter!) was recently found thriving off the coast of Venezuela (6). Notably, the patch sheltered nineteen species of native invertebrates, indicating that the coral successfully served its ecological function. Still, these corals most likely persisted through asexual reproduction, and mesocosm experiments are needed to confirm that their sexually produced recruits would partner with local algal symbionts. In any event, any uncertainty about each of these risks are most appropriately explored through a careful research program.

We agree that several less interventionist approaches merit parallel investigation and may reasonably be attempted first, including assisted gene flow, genetic rescue, and selective breeding. Yet, like ER, each of these strategies entails substantial uncertainty and faces legal and regulatory hurdles. For that reason, we see little justification for excluding ER from the same portfolio of options. We would welcome an outcome of this exchange that amounts to a clear endorsement of research on all these interventions.

Likewise, we would welcome effective political and legal action to address local anthropogenic stressors highlighted by the commentators, including overfishing and land-based pollution. Scholars and advocates have articulated a range of recommendations for reducing these harms, and some progress has been made (7). Yet these stressors remain politically difficult to tackle, and it is still unclear whether even full implementation of proposed measures would be sufficient to reverse the decline of Caribbean reefs. For this reason, advanced interventions such as ER remain worthy of investigation.

Loya and van Woesik (2) raise an important possibility: that extremely low recruitment may be the historical norm for Caribbean Acropora, and that their former dominance may naturally give way to reefs dominated by boulder corals. If so, the changes now unfolding might represent a natural transition rather than a collapse that must be “fixed” through radical interventions. This is a deep and multifaceted issue that deserves more discussion than we can provide here. Our view, in brief, is that such a transition may indeed occur and could still yield a functioning reef state, but that outcome remains highly uncertain—arguably no less uncertain than the success of ER. Whether we are correct on this or not, that there is disagreement on these scientific questions counsels for the creation of a research and policy framework, not against it.

To conclude, we agree that coral ER is bound to have risks, but these should be weighed against the risk of region-wide reef loss under currently allowed management strategies. In other ecosystem management contexts, researchers have been experimenting for over fifteen years to understand the tradeoffs of advanced interventionist strategies to help deal with a changing world (8, 9). A multistage research program, working with communities and policymakers to explore the tradeoffs of the whole range of advanced interventions including ER, is arguably the most conscientious and responsible way to reduce uncertainties and prepare for the range of futures that these ecosystems and communities may face.
