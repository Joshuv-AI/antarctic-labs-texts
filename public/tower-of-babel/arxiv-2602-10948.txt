Parameterized Complexity of Finding a Maximum Common Vertex Subgraph Without Isolated Vertices
Palash Dey, Anubhav Dhar, Ashlesha Hota, Sudeshna Kolay, Aritra Mitra

Abstract
In this paper, we study the Maximum Common Vertex Subgraph problem: Given two input graphs G 1 , G 2 G_{1},G_{2} and a non-negative integer h h , is there a common subgraph H H on at least h h vertices such that there is no isolated vertex in H H . In other words, each connected component of H H has at least 2 2 vertices. This problem naturally arises in graph theory along with other variants of the well-studied Maximum Common Subgraph problem [ 22 , 23 , 26 ] and also has applications in computational social choice [ 18 , 17 , 16 ] . We show that this problem is NP -hard and provide an FPT algorithm when parameterized by h h . Next, we conduct a study of the problem on common structural parameters like vertex cover number, maximum degree, treedepth, pathwidth and treewidth of one or both input graphs. We derive a complete dichotomy of parameterized results for our problem with respect to individual parameterizations as well as combinations of parameterizations from the above structural parameters. This provides us with a deep insight into the complexity theoretic and parameterized landscape of this problem.

Introduction

The problem of finding a
maximum common subgraph
of two given graphs is well-studied in algorithmic and structural graph theory. The most prominent variants include (i)
Maximum Common Induced Subgraph
, asking to maximize the number of vertices of a common induced subgraph, (ii)
Maximum Common Edge Subgraph
, asking to maximize the number of edges of a common subgraph, and (iii)
Maximum Common Induced Connected Subgraph
, asking to maximize the number of vertices in a common connected induced subgraph. A natural variant of this problem is to ask for a common subgraph with the maximum number of vertices for two input graphs
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
. However, this problem admits a trivial solution: a graph consisting of
\min(|V(G_{1})|,|V(G_{2})|)
-many isolated vertices. Evidently, such a trivial graph maximizes the number of vertices among all common subgraphs.

In this paper, we consider the next natural variant, the problem of
Maximum Common Vertex Subgraph
, which asks to maximize the number of vertices in a common subgraph of two input graphs, but where the common subgraph has no isolated vertices. Interestingly, forbidding isolated vertices makes this problem significantly non-trivial, and in this paper we show that
Maximum Common Vertex Subgraph
is
\mathsf{NP}
-hard
and boasts a rich landscape in parameterized complexity. We study the parameterized complexity of
Maximum Common Vertex Subgraph
under various choices of parameters: the size of the maximum common subgraph, maximum degree, treewidth, pathwidth, treedepth, and vertex cover size.

Apart from being an interesting and natural problem in graph theory in its own right,
Maximum Common Vertex Subgraph
finds its application in graphical variants of the house allocation problem in computational social choice. We state the following motivation. Let
\mathcal{A}
be a set of agents and
\mathcal{H}
be a set of houses. Let
G_{\mathcal{A}}
be a graph with
\mathcal{A}
as the vertex set, where edges depict some social relationship among the agents (e.g. friendship, acquaintances, collaborators, etc). Similarly, let
G_{\mathcal{H}}
be a graph with
\mathcal{H}
as the vertex set, where edges depict some notion of ‘nearness’ (e.g. geographic, transit-accessible, etc). Each agent
a\in\mathcal{A}
has to be allocated a unique house
\phi(a)\in\mathcal{H}
. An agent
a\in\mathcal{A}
is
happy
if at least one of the neighbours of
a
(in
G_{\mathcal{A}}
) is allocated to a neighbouring house (in
G_{\mathcal{H}}
) of
\phi(a)
. The problem of
Maximum Neighbour-Happy House Allocation
asks to find an allocation which maximizes the number of happy agents. Although being a natural extension to other well-studied variants of graphical house allocation
[
18
,
17
,
16
]
, the problem is identical to the
Maximum Common Vertex Subgraph
problem on the graphs
G_{\mathcal{A}}
and
G_{\mathcal{H}}
.

Related Works

The
Subgraph Isomorphism
problem asks whether there exists an injective mapping
\phi:V(H)\to V(G)
from a pattern graph
H
to a host graph
G
such that
\{u,v\}\in E(H)\implies\{\phi(u),\phi(v)\}\in E(G)
. This problem is
\mathsf{NP}
-hard

[
13
]
.
Kann
[
22
]
observes that the problems of
Maximum Common Induced Subgraph
,
Maximum Common Edge Subgraph
and
Maximum Common Induced Connected Subgraph
generalize the
Subgraph Isomorphism
problem, and hence are
\mathsf{NP}
-hard
. This work further studies the approximability of these problem.

Maximum Common Induced Subgraph
is shown to be at least as hard to approximate as Maximum
Clique
. The
Maximum Common Induced Connected Subgraph
is
\mathsf{NPO}

\mathsf{PB}
-complete. Kann also related node- and edge-based formulations via L-reductions, establishing foundational approximation and hardness results.
Khanna et al.
[
23
]
studied the
Largest Common Subtree
problem which asks for a largest tree that appears as a common subgraph in a collection of input trees. They showed that for bounded-degree trees, an approximation ratio of
\mathcal{O}\!\left(\frac{n\log\log n}{\log^{2}n}\right)
is achievable. For unbounded-degree trees, an
\mathcal{O}\!\left(\frac{n(\log\log n)^{2}}{\log^{2}n}\right)
-approximation algorithm is known in the unlabeled case. The same approximation guarantee holds for labeled trees of unbounded degree when the number of distinct labels is
\mathcal{O}(\log^{O(1)}n)
. Further Akutsu et al.
[
1
]
studied the problem for a restricted unordered setting where the maximum outdegree of the common subtree is bounded by a constant.
Yamaguchi et al.
[
32
]
further imposed structural restrictions focusing on tractable cases, in particular, graphs with bounded degree and treewidth and a polynomial number of spanning trees. Exploiting these properties, a polynomial-time algorithm was developed for computing a maximum common connected induced subgraph between a degree-bounded partial
k
-tree and a connected graph whose number of spanning trees is polynomially bounded.

On the structural side, Kriege et al.
[
26
]
proved that computing an
Maximum Common Connected Subgraph
of two biconnected series–parallel graphs (partial 2-trees) remains
\mathsf{NP}
-hard
even when all but one vertex have degree at most three.
Hoffmann et al.
[
15
]
introduced
k
-less subgraph isomorphism, where up to
k
vertices may be deleted before embedding. They developed weakened filtering invariants generalizing degree- and path-based constraints, implemented in a constraint-programming style algorithm.

As we showed, our problem can be equivalently thought of as finding the largest common star forest. Star-packing in graphs is very well-studied. Prieto and Sloper
[
27
]
studied packing
k
vertex-disjoint copies of a graph
H
into
G
, focusing on the case where
H
is a star,
K_{1,s}
. They provided a quadratic kernel for general
s
and a linear kernel with an
\mathcal{O}(2^{5.301k}k^{2.5}+n^{3})
algorithm for
s=2
.
Xi and Lin
[
30
]
investigated maximum
P_{3}
packing problem on claw-free cubic planar graphs, showing it to be
\mathsf{NP}
-hard
even under this structural restriction. In their follow up work
[
31
]
, they prove
\mathsf{NP}
-hardness for
3
-star packing on claw-free cubic graph. They designed a linear-time approximation algorithm covering at least
(3n-8)/4
vertices, leveraging structural properties and local augmentation arguments. Huang et al.
[
19
]
studied
k^{+}
-star packing and improved approximation guarantees, achieving
9/5
for
k=2
and
(k+2)/2

Parameterized Complexity.

In decision problems where the input has size
n
and is associated with a parameter
k
, the objective in parameterized complexity is to design algorithms with running time
f(k)\cdot n^{\mathcal{O}(1)}
, where
f
is a computable function that depends only on
k

[
8
]
. Problems that admit such algorithms are said to be
fixed-parameter tractable
(
\mathsf{FPT}
). An algorithm with running time
f(k)\cdot n^{\mathcal{O}(1)}
is called an
\mathsf{FPT}
algorithm, and the corresponding running time is referred to as an
\mathsf{FPT}
running time
[
7
,
11
]
. Formally, a parameterized problem
L\subseteq\Sigma^{*}\times\mathbb{N}
is said to be
fixed-parameter tractable
if there exists an algorithm
A
, a computable function
f:\mathbb{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{N}
, and a constant
c\in\mathbb{N}
such that for every input
(x,k)\in\Sigma^{*}\times\mathbb{N}
, the algorithm
A
decides whether
(x,k)\in L
in time at most
f(k)\cdot|(x,k)|^{c}
. The class of all such problems is denoted by
\mathsf{FPT}
. Next, we define
parameterized reductions
.

Definition 2

(Parameterized Reduction)
.

Let
A,B\subseteq\Sigma^{*}\times\mathbb{N}
be two parameterized problems.
A
parameterized reduction
from
A
to
B
is an algorithm that, given an instance
(x,k)
of
A
, outputs an instance
(x^{\prime},k^{\prime})
of
B
such that:

1.

(x,k)
is a yes-instance of
A
if and only if
(x^{\prime},k^{\prime})
is a yes-instance of
B
,

2.

k^{\prime}\leqslant g(k)
for some computable function
g
, and

3.

the running time of the algorithm is
f(k)\cdot|x|^{\mathcal{O}(1)}
for some computable function
f
.

For more details, please refer to Cygan et. al
[
7
]
.

Structural Parameters.

Following are the definitions of tree decompositions, treewidth, nice tree decompositions, path decompositions, pathwidth, treedepth, and vertex covers. We start by defining tree decompositions, treewidth and nice tree decompositions.

Definition 3

(Tree Decomposition and Treewidth)
.

Let
G=(V(G),E(G))
be a graph. A tree-decomposition of a graph
G
is a pair
(T=(V(T),E(T)),X=\{X_{t}\}_{t\in V(T)})
, where
T
is a tree where every node
t\in V(T)
is assigned a subset
X_{t}\subseteq V(G)
, called a bag, such that the following conditions hold.

1.

\bigcup\limits_{t\in V(T)}{X_{t}}=V(G)
,

2.

for every edge
\{x,y\}\in E(G)
there is a
t\in V(T)
such that
x,y\in X_{t}
, and

3.

for any
v\in V(G)
the subgraph of
T
induced by the set
\{t\mid v\in X_{t}\}
is connected.

The width of a tree decomposition is
\max\limits_{t\in V(T)}|X_{t}|-1
. The treewidth of
G
is the minimum width over all tree decompositions of
G
and is denoted by
\mathrm{tw}(G)
.

Definition 4

(Nice tree decomposition)
.

A tree decomposition
(T,X)
is called a nice tree decomposition if
T
is a tree rooted at some node
r
where
X_{r}=\emptyset
, each node of
T
has at most two children, and each node is of one of the following kinds:

\vartriangleright

Introduce node
: a node
t
that has only one child
t^{\prime}
where
X_{t}\supset X_{t^{\prime}}
and
|X_{t}|=|X_{t^{\prime}}|+1
.

\vartriangleright

Forget node
: a node
t
that has only one child
t^{\prime}
where
X_{t}\subset X_{t^{\prime}}
and
|X_{t}|=|X_{t^{\prime}}|-1
.

\vartriangleright

Join node
: a node
t
that has two children
t_{1}
and
t_{2}
such that
X_{t}=X_{t_{1}}=X_{t_{2}}
.

\vartriangleright

Leaf node
: a node
t
that is a leaf of
T
, and
X_{t}=\emptyset
.

A tree decomposition of width
\mathrm{tw}
can be transformed into a nice tree decomposition of the same width
\mathrm{tw}
and with
\mathcal{O}(\mathrm{tw}\cdot|V(G)|)
nodes

7
]
. The following is known regarding the treewidth of

-outerplanar graphs
[
29
]
.

Proposition 5
.

A

Pathwidth is a similar parameter to treewidth, except when the underlying graph is a path. Treedepth is yet another structural parameter. These are defined below.

Definition 6

A path decomposition of a graph

P=(X_{1},X_{2},\ldots,X_{r})

\forall u\in V(G)

The width of a path decomposition

The pathwidth of a graph

The treedepth of

\mathrm{td}(G)=\begin{cases}0&\text{if $G$ has no vertices,}\\
\max\limits_{H\in\mathrm{cc}(G)}\{\mathrm{td}(H)\}&\text{if $G$ is disconnected,}\\
\min\limits_{v\in V(G)}\{\mathrm{td}(G-v)\}+1&\text{if $G$ is connected.}\end{cases}

Approximation Algorithms.

An algorithm

.

Definition 9
.

An
\alpha
-approximation algorithm for an optimization problem is a polynomial time algorithm that for all instances of the problem produces a solution whose value is within a factor of
\alpha
of the value of an optimal solution.

We call
\alpha
as the approximation ratio. Typically, for minimization problems,
\alpha>1
and for maximization problem,
\alpha<1

[
29
]
.

Definition 10

(PTAS)
.

A polynomial time approximation scheme (PTAS) is a family of algorithms

, where there is an algorithm for each
\varepsilon>0
, such that
A_{\varepsilon}
is a
(1+\varepsilon)
approximation algorithm (for minimization problems) or a
(1-\varepsilon)
approximation algorithm (for maximization problems), and the running time of

is polynomial in the input size for every constant
\varepsilon
.

Definition 11

(EPTAS)
.

An efficient polynomial time approximation scheme (EPTAS) is a family of algorithms
A_{\varepsilon}
, where there is an algorithm for each
\varepsilon>0
, such that
A_{\varepsilon}
is a
(1+\varepsilon)
approximation algorithm (for minimization problems) or a
(1-\varepsilon)
approximation algorithm (for maximization problems), and the running time of

is
f(1/\varepsilon)\cdot n^{O(1)}
, where

is a computable function depending only on

, where

is the input size.

3
Size of the Common Star Forest as a Parameter

As we will see in Theorem
17
,
MaxComSubG
is an
\mathsf{NP}
-hard
problem. We show that
MaxComSubG
parameterized by the common subgraph size

admits an
\mathsf{FPT}
algorithm.

Theorem 12
.

Given an instance of
MaxComSubG
,
(G_{1},G_{2},h)
, there exists an
\mathsf{FPT}
algorithm parameterized by
h
. The running time of this algorithm is
2^{\mathcal{O}(h)}\cdot n
.

Proof.

Let
H
be a maximum common star forest subgraph of
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
. We need to decide if
|V(H)|\geqslant h
. We start by proving the following result.

Claim 13
.

|V(H)|\geqslant h
if and only if at least one of the following holds true.

1.

G_{1}
and
G_{2}
both have a matching consisting of
\lceil h/2\rceil
edges.

2.

G_{1}
and
G_{2}
both have a common star forest subgraph of size exactly
h
.

{claimproof}

Clearly, if
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
both have a matching (which is also a star forest) of size
\lceil h/2\rceil
or if they have a common star forest of size exactly
h
, then the maximum common star forest would be at least of size
h
, i.e.
|V(H)|\geqslant h
.

Now, we prove the other direction. Let
H
be a common star forest subgraph of
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
with
|V(H)|\geqslant h
, and let the size of the maximum matching in either
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
be less than
\lceil h/2\rceil
. Therefore,
\lceil h/2\rceil
is strictly larger than the size of the maximum matching of
H
. Hence,
H
can have at most
\lceil h/2\rceil-1
many stars. Let the stars of
H
be
S_{1},\ldots,S_{t}
for
t\leqslant\lceil h/2\rceil-1
. For every
i\in[t]
, let
S_{i}
have centre
c_{i}
and leaves
v_{i}^{(0)},v_{i}^{(1)},\ldots,v_{i}^{(y_{i})}.
, for some
y_{i}\geqslant 0
. Notice that the graph induced by
Q=\{c_{1},c_{2},\ldots,c_{t}\}\cup\{v_{1}^{(0)},v_{2}^{(0)},\ldots,v_{t}^{(0)}\}
induce a matching of size
t
in
H
, containing
2t\leqslant 2\lceil h/2\rceil-2<h
nodes. Now, we take all vertices from
Q
and we take
h-2t
vertices from
V(H)\setminus Q
to get a subset
R
of
V(H)
of size exactly
h
.
H[R]
is a subgraph of both
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
of size
h
. Moreover,
H[R]
is a star forest as it contains no isolated vertices; this is because the centres of stars
S_{1},\ldots,S_{t}
, i.e.
c_{1},\ldots,c_{t}
are present in
R
.

Let
S(h)
be the set of all star forests with
h
vertices, without any isolated vertices, and unique up to isomorphism.

Claim 14
.

|S(h)|=2^{\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{h})}
, and this can be enumerated in time
2^{\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{h})}
.

{claimproof}

A
partition
of an integer
t\geqslant 0
is a multiset of
[t]
, such that the elements add up to
t
. Let
P(h)
be the set of partitions of
h
. We show that
|S(h)|\leqslant|P(h)|
by constructing an injective mapping
\phi:S(h)\to P(h)
. Consider
H\in S(h)
to be a star forest with
h
vertices, without isolated vertices. Let the sizes of connected components of
h
be
c_{1},c_{2},\ldots
, where
c_{1}\geqslant c_{2}\geqslant\ldots
. Note that
\sum_{i}{c_{i}}=h
, hence
\{c_{1},c_{2},\ldots\}\in P(h)
. We define
\phi
such that
H\mapsto\{c_{1},c_{2},\ldots\}
. Now, let
\phi(H_{1})=\phi(H_{2})
, where
H_{1},H_{2}\in S(h)
. Hence, the sizes of connected components of
H_{1}
and
H_{2}
are same. As all connected components of
H_{1}
and
H_{2}
are stars,
H_{1}
and
H_{2}
are isomorphic. Hence,
H_{1}=H_{2}
.

This proves that
|S(h)|\leqslant|P(h)|
. Now, as it is known that
|P(h)|\leqslant\exp\left(\pi\sqrt{2h/3}+o(h)\right)=2^{\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{h})}

[
3
]
, we have
|S(h)|=2^{\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{h})}
. Enumerating partitions can be done using a simple recursive algorithm in time
\mathcal{O}(h^{2}\cdot|P(h)|)=2^{\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{h})}
(Lemma
41
, in the Appendix). Since
S(h)
contains all partitions in
P(h)
which do not contain
1
,
S(h)
can be enumerated in the same time.

We now state the algorithm for
MaxComSubG
parameterized by
h
. Let
M
be the graph consisting of
\lceil h/2\rceil
copies of an edge (i.e. a matching of size
\lceil h/2\rceil
). By Claim
13
, the instance is a
yes
-instance if and only if there exists a common star forest subgraph
H
of
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
in
S(h)\cup\{M\}
.

The algorithm enumerates
S(h)
(Claim
14
) and for all
H^{\prime}\in S(h)\cup\{M\}
, it checks whether
H^{\prime}
is a subgraph of
G_{1}
and a subgraph of
G_{2}
. Since
H^{\prime}
is a forest, Alon, Yuster and Zwick
[
2
]
show that this can be done in time
2^{\mathcal{O}(h)}\cdot n
. The algorithm outputs
yes
if for some
H^{\prime}\in S(h)\cup\{M\}
,
H^{\prime}
is a common star forest subgraph of
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
; and
no
otherwise.

The total running time of the algorithm is
2^{\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{h})}\cdot 2^{\mathcal{O}(h)}\cdot n=2^{\mathcal{O}(h)}\cdot n
.
∎

4.1

We start by showing that

Theorem 15
.

is
\mathsf{W[2]}
-hard when parameterized by the minimum of the vertex covers of the two graphs.

Proof.

The parameterized problem

Claim 16

(G,k)

{claimproof}

Forward Direction.
Assume that

(by Lemma

be matched to

Let

Therefore,

contains as a spanning subgraph, a collection of

Remark 19

Theorem 20
.

There exists an
\mathsf{FPT}
algorithm to determine the size of the largest common star forest subgraph of two input graphs
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
, with parameter
k
such that both
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
have a vertex cover of size
\leqslant k
. The running time of this algorithm is
2^{2^{\mathcal{O}(k)}}\cdot n^{\mathcal{O}(1)}

Proof.

Let
V_{1}=V(G_{1})
and
V_{2}=V(G_{2})
. Let
H
be a maximum common star forest subgraph of
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
. The algorithm runs in two phases: the
preprocessing phase
and the
ILP phase
. The preprocessing phase creates multiple ILP instances, each of which is solved in the ILP phase, which finally outputs the best answer among all instances.

Preprocessing phase.

Let
C_{1}
be a minimum vertex cover of
G_{1}
, with
|C_{1}|=a\leqslant k
. Let
I_{1}=V_{1}\setminus C_{1}
. Notice that
I_{1}
is a maximum independent set of
G_{1}
. Now, we create
2^{a}
twin classes partitioning
I_{1}
, one for each
X\subseteq C_{1}
, as follows: define twin class
B^{(1)}_{X}=\{v\in I_{1}\mid N_{G_{1}}(v)=X\}
. Let
H_{1}
be a subgraph of
G_{1}
which is isomorphic to
H
. The number of stars in
H_{1}
must be at most
a
, as every star must contain at least one vertex from the vertex cover
C_{1}
. We classify the stars into two types:

\vartriangleright

Type I stars: the centre of a Type I star is a vertex from
C_{1}
.

\vartriangleright

Type II stars: the centre of a Type II star is a vertex from
I_{1}
.

Note that every leaf of a Type II star belongs to
C_{1}
.

We ‘guess’ the number of Type I and Type II stars. Let there be
p
many Type I and
q
many Type II stars, where
p+q\leqslant a
. Let
T^{(1)}_{1},T^{(1)}_{2},\ldots,T^{(1)}_{p}
be the Type I stars, and
L^{(1)}_{1},L^{(1)}_{2},\ldots,L^{(1)}_{q}
be the Type II stars. There are
\mathcal{O}(k^{2})
guesses for
p
and
q
.

We now ‘guess’ the centres of the Type I stars. Let
c_{1},c_{2},\ldots,c_{p}\in C_{1}
be the centres of
T^{(1)}_{1},T^{(1)}_{2},\ldots,T^{(1)}_{p}
respectively. There are
\mathcal{O}(a^{p})=\mathcal{O}(k^{k})
guesses of
c_{1},c_{2},\ldots,c_{p}\in C_{1}
. The variable
\alpha_{i}
would denote the value of
|V(T^{(1)}_{i})|
, and will be a variable in the ILP we design.

Next we completely ‘guess’ the Type II stars. Notice that two vertices in
B_{X}^{(1)}
, for any
X\subseteq C_{1}
have the same neighbourhood and therefore they are indistinguishable from each other. For
L_{i}^{(1)}
,
i\in[q]
, let
c_{p+i}\in I_{1}
be its centre. We guess

such that

. Note that since all vertices in
B^{(1)}_{X}
are indistinguishable from each other, we can arbitrarily consider any vertex to be
c_{p+i}
. Next, we guess the exact leaves of
L^{(1)}_{i}
, this will be a subset of
X
. For every
i\in q
, there are
2^{a}
choices of
X
and for each
X
, there are

choices of the exact set of leaves of
L^{(1)}_{i}
. Hence in total, there are at most
\sum\limits_{j\in[a]}\binom{a}{|X|}2^{|X|}\leqslant 3^{k}
guesses. Let
\alpha_{p+i}\in\{2,3,\ldots,a+1\}
be the value of
|V(L^{(1)}_{i})|
; this will be a constant in the ILP we design. Also, for every
X\subseteq C_{1}
, let
a_{X}=|B_{X}^{(1)}\setminus\{c_{p+i}\mid i\in[q]\}|
be the number of nodes in
B_{X}^{(1)}
which are not centres of
L^{(1)}_{i}
for
i\in[q]
; this will also be a constant in the ILP we design.

For every other remaining vertex
v
in
C_{1}
which is neither guessed to be a centre of
T_{i}^{(1)}
nor a leaf of
L_{i^{\prime}}^{(1)}
, We guess whether it is a leaf of some

or if it is not a part of
H_{1}
. For every

there are
(p+1)
choices, hence in total there are at most
(p+1)^{a}=\mathcal{O}((k+1)^{k})
many guesses. After this guess, every vertex in
C_{1}
is either uniquely guessed to be a centre of
T_{i}^{(1)}
or a leaf of
T_{i}^{(1)}
or a leaf of
L_{i}^{(1)}
, or not a part of
H_{1}
. Let
\beta_{i}=|T_{i}^{(1)}\cap C_{1}|
, this will be a constant in the ILP we design.

For each
X\subseteq C_{1}
, and
i\in[p]
, we denote by the variable
x_{i,X}
to value
|T_{i}^{(1)}\cap B^{(1)}_{X}|
; this will be a variable in our clause. We also perform a sanity check and discard the set of guesses if they are not consistent with each other.

Now, we perform similar ‘guesses’ with
G_{2}
. Let
H_{2}
be a subgraph of
G_{2}
isomorphic to
H
. Let a minimum vertex cover of
G_{2}
be
C_{2}
, with
|C_{2}|=b\leqslant k
;

is a maximum independent set. This gives us twin classes
B^{(2)}_{Y}=\{v\in I_{2}\mid N_{G_{2}}(v)=Y\}
, for all
Y\subseteq C_{2}
. Let there be
r
many Type I stars
T^{(2)}_{i}
’s, and
s
many Type II star

’s. We ensure

. We guess
d_{1},d_{2},\ldots,d_{r}\in C_{2}
be the centres of
T^{(2)}_{1},T^{(2)}_{2},\ldots,T^{(2)}_{r}

\gamma_{i}
would denote the value of
|V(T^{(2)}_{i})|
. For
L_{i}^{(2)}
,
i\in[s]
, let
d_{r+i}\in I_{2}
be its centre. We guess

such that

. We guess the exact leaves of
L^{(2)}_{i}
in

. Let
\gamma_{r+i}\in\{2,3,\ldots,b+1\}
be the value of
|V(L^{(2)}_{i})|
, and for every

, let
b_{Y}=|B_{Y}^{(2)}\setminus\{d_{r+i}\mid i\in[s]\}|
. For all remaining vertices of

, we guess if it belongs to

for some

or if it is not in

. Let
\delta_{i}=|T_{i}^{(2)}\cap C_{2}|
. For each

, and

, we denote by the variable

Since
H_{1}
and
H_{2}
are a isomorphic to each other, there must exist a permutation
\pi:[p+q]\to[r+s]
such that
\alpha_{i}=\gamma_{\pi(i)}
, for every

. There are

guesses for

Putting everything together, there are
\mathcal{O}\left(\left(k^{2}\cdot k^{k}\cdot 3^{k}\cdot\left(k+1\right)^{k}\right)^{2}\cdot k!\right)=k^{\mathcal{O}(k)}
guesses. For each such guess, we formulate an ILP and solve it to determine if there are corresponding subgraphs
H_{1}
and
H_{2}
and we would like to maximize the total size.

ILP phase.

We fix a guess from the preprocessing phase. Our objective is to maximize

(equivalently

). Since

We now look into the constraints which ensure that

and

are star subgraphs of

and
G_{2}
respectively. Recall that for every

. Therefore, the sum of

over all

must not exceed

. Similarly, for every

, the sum of

over all

must not exceed

.

Now, observe that for
i\in[p]
, for any

,

does not contain any node in

; and therefore,
x_{i,X}=0
. Similarly, for

Next, we compute the size of

Each ILP has

k^{\mathcal{O}(k)}
, the total running time is

over all feasible guesses. For completeness we formally state the ILP below.

\displaystyle\max\sum_{i\in[p]}\alpha_{i}

subject to:

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{i\in[p]}x_{i,X}\leqslant a_{X}

\displaystyle\forall X\subseteq C_{1}

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{i\in[r]}y_{i,Y}\leqslant b_{Y}

\displaystyle\forall Y\subseteq C_{2}

\displaystyle x_{i,X}=0

\displaystyle\forall i\in[p],X\subseteq C_{1}\setminus\{c_{i}\}

\displaystyle y_{i,Y}=0

\displaystyle\forall i\in[r],X\subseteq C_{2}\setminus\{d_{i}\}

\displaystyle\alpha_{i}=\beta_{i}+\sum\limits_{X\subseteq C_{1}}x_{i,X}

\displaystyle\forall i\in[p]

\displaystyle\gamma_{i}=\delta_{i}+\sum\limits_{Y\subseteq C_{2}}y_{i,Y}

\displaystyle\forall i\in[r]

\displaystyle\alpha_{i}=\gamma_{\pi(i)}

\displaystyle\forall i\in[p+q]

Theorem 26
.

MaxComSubG
admits an
\mathsf{FPT}
algorithm with running time
2^{2^{\mathcal{O}(k^{2})}}\cdot n^{\mathcal{O}(1)}
, with parameter
k
when all connected component of both graphs have at most
k
nodes.

Proof.

Let the input be graphs
G_{1}
,
G_{2}
and we want to maximize the size of the common star forest subgraph. Let
n=|V(G_{1})|+|V(G_{2})|
. The
\mathsf{FPT}
algorithm consists of two phases: the
preprocessing phase
and the
ILP phase
. The preprocessing phase computes the necessary values needed for the ILP, which is then solved in the ILP phase.

Preprocessing phase.

First we enumerate all distinct ordered, connected graphs of
k
nodes. These are named
C_{1}
,
C_{2}
, …,
C_{k_{1}}
. This can be done by simply enumerating all possible subsets of edges between
k
vertices. Therefore
k_{1}\leqslant 2^{\binom{k}{2}}
, and this process can be done in time
\mathcal{O}(k_{1})
time.

Let
p_{i}
to be the number of copies of
C_{i}
appearing in
G_{1}
, and let
q_{i}
be the number of copies of
C_{i}
in
G_{2}
, for all
i\in[k_{1}]
. This can be done in
\mathcal{O}(k_{1}\cdot k\cdot n)
time by any simple search algorithm like BFS or DFS. Notice that
(p_{1},\ldots,p_{k_{1}},q_{1},\ldots,q_{k_{1}})
completely determine the graphs
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
.

Next, we enumerate all the set
\{0,1,\ldots,k-1\}^{k}
. Let
S_{1},S_{2},\ldots,S_{k_{2}}
, for
k_{2}=k^{k}
be the ordered tuples, where
S_{\ell}=(s_{\ell}^{(1)},s_{\ell}^{(2)},\ldots,s_{\ell}^{(k)})
,
s_{\ell}^{(j)}\in\{0,1,\ldots,k-1\}
for all
j\in[k],\ell\in[k_{2}]
. This enumeration takes time
\mathcal{O}(k\cdot k_{2})
.

For
i\in[k_{1}]
,
\ell\in[k_{2}]
, we define a
realisation of
S_{\ell}
into
C_{i}
as a subset of edges that induce a star forest in
C_{i}
such that for every
j\in[k]
, there are exactly
s_{\ell}^{(j)}
occurrences of stars of
j
leaves (and 1 centre). Next we compute the boolean variables
\mathsf{br}_{i,\ell}\in\{\text{{\sc true}},\text{{\sc false}}\}
for every
i\in[k_{1}],\ell\in[k_{2}]
.
\mathsf{br}_{i,\ell}
is
true
if there exists a realisation of
S_{\ell}
into
C_{i}
;
\mathsf{br}_{i,\ell}
is
false
otherwise. Since
|V(C_{i})|\leqslant k
, all stars must have at most
k
leaves, and there cannot be more than
k
stars. A simple algorithm to compute
\mathsf{br}_{i,\ell}
can be described as follows. Start by initializing all
\mathsf{br}_{i,\ell}
to false, and then iterate over each
i\in[k_{1}]
: for each subset of edges in
C_{i}
, check if deleting them leaves
C_{i}
with a collection of stars with exactly
s_{\ell}^{(j)}
occurrences of stars of
j
leaves, for
j\in[k]
. This entire procedure takes time
\mathcal{O}(k_{1}\cdot 2^{\binom{k}{2}}\cdot k)=\mathcal{O}(k\cdot k_{1}^{2})
. This concludes the preprocessing phase.

ILP phase.

To solve
MaxComSubG
, we need to remove a subset of edges from
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
such that the subgraph induced by the remaining edges,
H
, is a collection of stars and the number of nodes in
H
is maximized. Now, fix a component
C
of
G_{1}
. We will refer to
H
as a
solution
. Let
C
be a copy of
C_{i}
for some
i\in[k_{1}]
. Let the edges in
H\cap C
induce a star forest where the star with
j\in[k]
leaves appears
s^{(j)}
times. There must be some
\ell\in[k_{2}]
such that
S_{\ell}=(s_{\ell}^{(1)},s_{\ell}^{(2)},\ldots,s_{\ell}^{(k)})=(s^{(1)},s^{(2)},\ldots,s^{(k)})
, i.e. the edges in
H\cap C
is a realisation of
S_{\ell}
into
C
. Moreover, such a realisation exists if and only if
\mathsf{br}_{i,\ell}=\text{{\sc true}}
.

We now introduce the variables for the ILP. Among the
p_{i}
occurrences of
C_{i}
in
G_{1}
, we denote by the integer variable
x_{i,\ell}
, for
i\in[k_{1}],\ell\in[k_{2}]
, the number of components such that a solution
H
induces a realisation of
S_{\ell}
into this connected component. Similarly in
G_{2}
, let
y_{i,\ell}
, for
i\in[k_{1}],\ell\in[k_{2}]
, be the number of occurrences of
C_{i}
such that a solution
H
induces a realisation of
S_{\ell}
into that component. These will be the only variables of the ILP that we would formulate.

Next, we formulate the constraints of the ILP which would ensure that the variables
x_{i,\ell}
and
y_{i,\ell}
correspond to some valid solution. Firstly, for all occurrences
C
of
C_{i}
in
G_{1}
, the edges of a solution
H
must induce a realisation of some
S_{\ell}
,
\ell\in[k_{2}]
into
C
. Therefore, for a fixed
i
, the variables
x_{i,\ell}
must add up to exactly
p_{i}
; and similarly the variables
y_{i,\ell}
must add up to exactly
q_{i}
.

The next criteria we need to ensure is that to only allow realisations of
S_{\ell}
into
C_{i}
when
\mathsf{br}_{i,\ell}=\text{{\sc true}}
. Therefore, for all
i\in[k_{1}],\ell\in[k_{2}]
with
\mathsf{br}_{i,\ell}=\text{{\sc false}}
, we enforce
x_{i,\ell}=y_{i,\ell}=0
.

Finally, for every
j\in[k]
, the edges of a solution
H
must induce equal number of stars with
j
leaves in
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
. The number of such stars induced by the edges of
H
in any occurrence of
C_{i}
in
G_{1}
, with the edges of
H
inducing a realisation of
S_{\ell}
into this component, is
s_{\ell}^{(j)}
. Therefore, the total number of occurrences of such stars induced by the edges
H
in
G
is
\sum\limits_{i\in[k_{1}]}\sum\limits_{\ell\in[k_{2}]}s_{\ell}^{(j)}\cdot x_{i,j}
. Similarly, the total number of occurrences of such stars induced by the edges
H
in
G
is
\sum\limits_{i\in[k_{1}]}\sum\limits_{\ell\in[k_{2}]}s_{\ell}^{(j)}\cdot y_{i,j}
. These two quantities must be equal for each
j\in[k]
. This completes the requirements for the variables to correspond to a valid solution.

We want to maximize the number of nodes in such a solution
H
. We argued that the total number of occurrences of stars with
j
leaves in
H
is
\sum\limits_{i\in[k_{1}]}\sum\limits_{\ell\in[k_{2}]}s_{\ell}^{(j)}\cdot x_{i,j}
. Each such star contains
(j+1)
nodes. Therefore, the total number of nodes in
H
is
\sum\limits_{j\in[k]}\sum\limits_{i\in[k_{1}]}\sum\limits_{\ell\in[k_{2}]}(j+1)\cdot s_{\ell}^{(j)}\cdot x_{i,j}
. This is the objective that we aim to maximize.

Therefore, we have an ILP with
k_{3}=2\cdot k_{1}\cdot k_{2}
variables. This can be solved in time
k_{3}^{\mathcal{O}(k_{3})}\cdot n^{\mathcal{O}(1)}=2^{2^{\mathcal{O}(k^{2})}}\cdot n^{\mathcal{O}(1)}
time
[
7
]
. This dominates all other steps from the preprocessing phase. For completeness, we provide the formal ILP formulation below.

\displaystyle\max\sum\limits_{j\in[k]}\sum\limits_{i\in[k_{1}]}\sum\limits_{\ell\in[k_{2}]}

\displaystyle(j+1)\cdot s_{\ell}^{(j)}\cdot x_{i,j}

subject to:

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{\ell\in[k_{2}]}x_{i,\ell}

\displaystyle=p_{i}

\displaystyle\forall i\in[k_{1}]

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{\ell\in[k_{2}]}y_{i,\ell}

\displaystyle=q_{i}

\displaystyle\forall i\in[k_{1}]

\displaystyle x_{i,\ell}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\forall i\in[k_{1}],\ell\in[k_{2}],\text{ with }\mathsf{br}_{i,\ell}=\text{{\sc false}}

\displaystyle y_{i,\ell}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\forall i\in[k_{1}],\ell\in[k_{2}],\text{ with }\mathsf{br}_{i,\ell}=\text{{\sc false}}

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{i\in[k_{1}]}\sum\limits_{\ell\in[k_{2}]}s_{\ell}^{(j)}\cdot x_{i,j}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{i\in[k_{1}]}\sum\limits_{\ell\in[k_{2}]}s_{\ell}^{(j)}\cdot y_{i,j}

\displaystyle\forall j\in[k]

\displaystyle x_{i,\ell},y_{i,\ell}

\displaystyle\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geqslant 0}

\displaystyle\forall i\in[k_{1}],\ell\in[k_{2}]

∎

Now, we are ready to prove our result with respect the treedepth and maximum degree of both input graphs as parameters.

Corollary 27
.

MaxComSubG
admits an
\mathsf{FPT}
algorithm with parameters
d
and
\Delta
such that treedepth is at most
d
and maximum degree is at most
\Delta
in both input graphs. The running time of the algorithm is
2^{2^{\mathcal{O}(\Delta^{2d})}}\cdot n^{\mathcal{O}(1)}
.

Proof.

We start by showing the following.

Claim 28
.

The size of any connected component of a graph of treedepth at most
d
and maximum degree
\Delta
is at most
\Delta^{d}-1
.

{claimproof}

We proceed by induction on
d
. For
d=0
, the graph must have no vertices, in particular the number of vertices is at most
\Delta^{d}-1=0
. Now, for some
d\geqslant 1
, assume that all graphs of maximum degree at most
\Delta
and treedepth at most
d-1
have connected components of size at most
\Delta^{d-1}-1
. Let
G
be a graph of maximum degree at most
\Delta
and tree depth at most
d
. Every connected component
C
of
G
must have maximum degree at most
\Delta
and treedepth at most
d
. We show that
|C|\leqslant\Delta^{d}-1
.

By definition,
d\geqslant\mathrm{td}(C)=\min\limits_{v\in V(C)}\{\mathrm{td}(C-v)\}+1
. Let
u\in V(C)
be the vertex such that
\mathrm{td}(C)=\mathrm{td}(C-u)+1
. Therefore, every connected component of
C-u
must have treedepth at most
d-1
and there can be no larger than
\Delta^{d-1}-1
. Moreover, these can be at most
\Delta
many connected components in
C-u
as the degree of
u
in
C
is at most
\Delta
. Therefore
|C-u|\leqslant\Delta\cdot(\Delta^{d-1}-1)=\Delta^{d}-\Delta
, and hence
|C|=|C-u|+1\leqslant\Delta^{d}-1
.

Therefore, the
\mathsf{FPT}
algorithm from Theorem
26
directly gives us an
\mathsf{FPT}
algorithm for parameters
\Delta
and
d
and runs in time
2^{2^{\mathcal{O}(\Delta^{2d})}}\cdot n^{\mathcal{O}(1)}
.
∎

Theorem

Our approach is based on the observation that any common subgraph without isolated vertices can be decomposed into a
star forest
, where each star has a centre vertex and possibly several leaves. By first designing a dynamic programming algorithm on nice tree decomposition for finding a maximum-cardinality star forest in a single graph in Lemma
37
, we extend it in Theorem
39
to compute a maximum common subgraph of two graphs.
At a high level, each DP-table entry at a bag encodes a partial star forest formed in the subgraph induced by the vertices processed in the corresponding subtree of the decomposition. For each vertex in the bag, we record its role: unused, a leaf (together with its centre), or a centre (with its current number of attached leaves), thereby describing how it participates in the partial solution. In addition, we maintain a star-count vector that stores how many stars of each size have already been fully formed inside the subtree.

Fix a mask
\sigma

\mathrm{DP}[t_{2},\sigma_{2},\mathbf{c}^{(2)}]=1

Vertex-role compatibility.
For every vertex

\sigma(v)
as follows.

1.

If one of
\sigma_{1}(v)
and
\sigma_{2}(v)
is
Uncovered
.
If a vertex is

(a)

If
\sigma_{1}(v)
and
\sigma_{2}(v)
are both

\sigma(v)=\textsc{Uncovered}

(b)

2.

If both roles

3.

If both

\sigma_{1}

be the number of common leaves of

and

, and the star-count vector is updated by removing the two partial counts and adding one count for the merged star of size

4.

If one of
\sigma_{1}(v)
or
\sigma_{2}(v)
is a Leaf and the other is a Centre.
If one role is
\textsc{Leaf}(u)
and the other is
\textsc{Centre}(d)
, the such a combination is incompatible, since a vertex cannot simultaneously act as a leaf and a centre in the same star forest.

Star-count update.

For each

.

For every node

, mask
\sigma

\mathrm{DP}[t,\sigma,\mathbf{c}]=1
.

Base case (leaf node).
If
t
is a leaf,
H
must be empty, so the DP initialization sets
\mathrm{DP}[t,(\emptyset),(0,\ldots,0)]=1
.

Inductive step.
Assume the claim holds for all children of a node
t
. We prove it for
t
according to its type.

Introduce node.
Let
t
introduce
u
. Let
H^{\prime}=H[V_{t^{\prime}}]
. By induction,
H^{\prime}
corresponds to
\mathrm{DP}[t^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime},\mathbf{c}^{\prime}]=1
. The role of
u
in
H
determines which introduce transition applies, updating roles and counts to
\sigma,\mathbf{c}
. Hence
\mathrm{DP}[t,\sigma,\mathbf{c}]=1
.

Forget node.
If
t
forgets
u
, then
H
is also a star forest in
G_{t^{\prime}}
. By induction, it corresponds to
\mathrm{DP}[t^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime},\mathbf{c}]=1
. The forget transition projects
\sigma^{\prime}
to
B_{t}
, so
\mathrm{DP}[t,\sigma,\mathbf{c}]=1
.

Join node.
Let
H_{i}=H\cap G_{t_{i}}
for
i=1,2
. By induction, these correspond to states
\mathrm{DP}[t_{i},\sigma_{i},\mathbf{c}^{(i)}]=1
. Since both originate from the same
H
, their roles on
B_{t}
are compatible. The join transition merges them and sets
\mathrm{DP}[t,\sigma,\mathbf{c}]=1
.

Thus both directions hold, completing the proof.

Running Time.

Let
k=\mathrm{tw}+1
denote the maximum bag size. For each vertex
v
in a bag, its role can be
Uncovered
,
Centre
(d)
for some
d\in\{2,\ldots,\Delta+1\}
, or
Leaf
(u)
for
u\in B_{t}\setminus\{v\}
or
\textsc{Leaf}(\bot)
. Hence each vertex has at most
1+\Delta+(k+1)=\Delta+\mathrm{tw}+3
possible roles, and the number of masks per bag is at most
(\Delta+\mathrm{tw}+3)^{\mathrm{tw}+1}
. The star-count vector has
\Delta
coordinates, each bounded by
n
, so the number of possible vectors is
\mathcal{O}(n^{\Delta})
. Therefore, the number of DP states per bag is
(\Delta+\mathrm{tw}+3)^{\mathrm{tw}+1}\cdot n^{\Delta}.
Introduce and forget nodes can be processed in time polynomial in (
\mathrm{tw}+\Delta
) per state. At a join node, in the worst case every state of one child must be checked against every state of the other child, leading to a time of
(\Delta+\mathrm{tw}+3)^{2(\mathrm{tw}+1)}\cdot\Delta\cdot n^{\Delta}\log n
for that node. Since a nice tree decomposition has
\mathcal{O}(n\cdot\mathrm{tw})
nodes, the total running time of the algorithm is
\mathcal{O}\big((\Delta+\mathrm{tw}+3)^{2(\mathrm{tw}+1)}\cdot\mathrm{tw}\cdot\Delta\cdot n^{\Delta+1}\cdot\log n\big)
.
∎

We now extend the dynamic programming algorithm of Lemma
37
to compute a maximum common subgraph of two graphs that contains no isolated vertices.

Theorem 39
.

Consider
MaxComSubG
on the input
G_{1}
,
G_{2}
,
h
with parameter
\mathrm{tw}
, such that the treewidths of both graphs are at most
\mathrm{tw}
and the maximum degree of one of the graphs is at most a constant
\Delta
. There exists an
\mathsf{FPT}
algorithm solving
MaxComSubG
, with a running time of
\mathcal{O}\left((\Delta+\mathrm{tw})^{2(\mathrm{tw}+1)}\cdot\mathrm{tw}\cdot\Delta\cdot n^{\Delta+1}\cdot\log n\right)
, where
n=|V(G_{1})+V(G_{2})|
.

Proof.

Let
H
be a maximum common star forest subgraph of
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
, then the maximum degree of

is
\Delta
. Using Lemma
37
, we enumerate all star forest subgraphs of

and

of maximum degree

∎

5
EPTAS for Bounded Degree Planar Graphs

In this section, we examine the problem of
MaxComSubG
, where the input graphs are planar graphs with maximum degree bounded by some constant. Although
MaxComSubG
is
\mathsf{NP}
-hard
when both graphs are planar graphs of bounded degree (by a constant
\Delta=\mathcal{O}(1)
) (Theorem
17
), we show that the problem admits an EPTAS for such inputs. The algorithm is a modification of the Baker’s technique
[
4
]
which sacrifices a small part of optimal solution to break the graph down to
k
-outerplanar graphs, for a small
k
and then solves the problem exactly.

Theorem 40
.

For every
0<\varepsilon<1
, There is a
(1-\varepsilon)
-approximation algorithm, for
MaxComSubG
, on planar graphs with maximum degree at most
\Delta
, with running time
(1/\varepsilon+\Delta)^{\mathcal{O}(1/\varepsilon)}\cdot n^{\Delta+1}\cdot\log n
.

Proof.

We first state the algorithm and then prove its correctness.

The approximation algorithm.

Let
0<\varepsilon<1
, and the input graphs be
G_{1}
,
G_{2}
. Consider a BFS tree
\mathcal{T}
of
G_{1}
. Let
\Delta
be the largest degree in these graphs. Let
L^{(i)}_{1}\subseteq V(G_{1})
be the
i
-th level of vertices in
\mathcal{T}
. Similarly let
L^{(i)}_{2}
be the
i
-th level of vertices in some BFS tree of
G_{2}
. Let
k=\lceil 2/\varepsilon\rceil
.

The algorithm iterates over
r_{1},r_{2}\in\{0,1,\ldots,k-1\}
. The algorithm creates the graph
G_{1}^{(r_{1})}
which is the graph obtained from
G_{1}
by removing the vertices lying in level
L^{(3r_{1}+2)}_{1},L^{(3(k+r_{1})+2)}_{1},L^{(3(2k+r_{1})+2)}_{1},L^{(3(3k+r_{1})+2)}_{1},\ldots
. Similarly it creates the graph
G_{2}^{(r_{2})}
which is the graph obtained from
G_{2}
by removing the vertices lying in level
L^{(3r_{2}+2)}_{2},L^{(3(k+r_{2})+2)}_{2},L^{(3(2k+r_{2})+2)}_{2},L^{(3(3k+r_{2})+2)}_{2},\ldots
.
G^{(r_{1})}_{1}
and
G^{(r_{2})}_{2}
are
3k
-outerplanar graphs and have treewidth at most
9k+1
(due to Proposition
5
). The algorithm now solves
MaxComSubG
on
G_{1}^{(r_{1})},G_{1}^{(r_{2})}
in time
\mathcal{O}\left((\Delta+(9k+1))^{2(9k+2)}\cdot(9k+1)\cdot\Delta\cdot n^{\Delta+1}\cdot\log n\right)
(using Theorem
39
). The algorithm outputs the solution which attains the largest number of nodes, over all choices of
r_{1},r_{2}
. The total running time of the algorithm is
k^{2}\cdot\mathcal{O}\left((\Delta+(9k+1))^{2(9k+2)}\cdot(9k+1)\cdot\Delta\cdot n^{\Delta+1}\cdot\log n\right)=(1/\varepsilon+\Delta)^{\mathcal{O}(1/\varepsilon)}\cdot n^{\Delta+1}\cdot\log n
.

Correctness: guarantee of the approximation factor

Let
\mathsf{OPT}
be the number of nodes of the maximum common star forest subgraph
H
of
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
. Let
SOL
be the number of nodes in the output by the algorithm. Clearly
\textsf{SOL}<\mathsf{OPT}
, as the algorithm finds largest common subgraph of a subgraph of
G_{1}
and a subgraph of
G_{2}
.

Let
S
be a star in a subgraph
H_{1}
of
G_{1}
which is isomorphic to
H
; hence
S
is a star subgraph of
G_{1}
. There exists
i
such that
V(S)\subseteq L^{(i)}_{1}\cup L^{(i+1)}_{1}\cup L^{(i+2)}_{1}
, as a star cannot span across four or more levels. Therefore for at most one
r_{1}\in\{0,1,\ldots,k-1\}
,
G^{(r_{1})}_{1}
does not contain all the vertices of
S
; in such a case we say
r_{1}

stabs

S
. Let
s_{r_{1}}
denote the total number of vertices in all stars of
H_{1}
stabbed by
r_{1}
. Let
r^{*}_{1}\in\underset{r_{1}\in\{0,\ldots,k-1\}}{\argmin}\{s_{r_{1}}\}
minimize the number of nodes of stars in
H_{1}
it stabs. Let
F_{1}
be the forest of stars in
H
corresponding to the stars stabbed by
r^{*}_{1}
, then
|F_{1}|\leqslant\frac{1}{k}\cdot|V(H)|
. Moreover,
H\setminus F_{1}
is a subgraph of
G_{1}^{(r_{1}^{*})}
. Similarly, there exists
r^{*}_{2}\in\{0,\ldots,k-1\}
and a forest of stars
F_{2}
in
H
, such that
|F_{2}|\leqslant\frac{1}{k}\cdot|V(H)|
and
H\setminus F_{2}
is a subgraph of
G_{2}^{(r_{2}^{*})}
. Therefore
H\setminus(F_{1}\cup F_{2})
is a common star forest subgraph of both
G_{1}^{(r_{1}^{*})}
and
G_{2}^{(r_{2}^{*})}
. Therefore, we have

SOL

\displaystyle\geqslant|V(H)\setminus(V(F_{1})\cup V(F_{2}))|\geqslant|V(H)|-|V(F_{1})|-|V(F_{2})|

\displaystyle\geqslant\left(1-\frac{2}{k}\right)\mathsf{OPT}\geqslant(1-\varepsilon)\mathsf{OPT}

∎

6
Conclusion

In this paper, we obtain a complete dichotomy of the parameterized complexity of
MaxComSubG
when the parameters are a combination of the common subgraph size, or vertex cover, maximum degree, treedepth, pathwidth or treewidth of one or both input graphs. In future, it would be interesting to study the scope for designing efficient approximation schemes for the problems with parameterized hardness results - similar to the EPTAS we design for the problem when both input graphs are planar graphs with constant maximum degrees. From the perspective of computational social choice, we also wish to study the problem where the input graphs come from special graph classes like unit disk graphs, etc.
