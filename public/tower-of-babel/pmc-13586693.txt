Single-Nucleus Transcriptomic Atlas Reveals Disease-Associated Cellular States in Human Glomerular Diseases
Authors: Sehoon Park, Jeong Ho Joo, Yaerim Kim, Hyunah Ku, Jae-ik Oh, Jeong Min Cho, Semin Cho, Soojin Lee, Jung Hun Koh, San-Sung Kwon, Yi Rang Na, Jee-Yeon Ryu, Seung Hee Yang, Seungyeup Han, Jang Hee Han, Chang Wook Jeong, Jong-Il Kim, Yong Chul Kim, Seung Seok Han, Hajeong Lee, Kook-Hwan Oh, Jeong Seok Lee, Dong Ki Kim, Hajeong Lee, Hajeong Lee, Seung Seok Han, Yaerim Kim, Jeonghwan Lee, Ji In Park, Jung Tak Park, Jung Nam An, Kyung-Chul Moon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionGlomerular diseases are a major cause of chronic kidney disease and kidney failure worldwide, yet the cellular mechanisms underlying their heterogeneity remain incompletely understood. Single-nucleus RNA sequencing (snRNA-seq) enables high-resolution characterization of transcriptional alterations across diverse kidney cell populations.MethodsWe performed snRNA-seq on snap-frozen kidney biopsy tissues obtained from treatment-naïve patients with minimal change disease (MCD), membranous nephropathy (MN), IgA nephropathy (IgAN), and diabetic kidney disease (DKD), together with control kidney samples. In total, 333,938 nuclei from 37 individuals were analyzed to define disease-associated transcriptional programs across kidney cell types.ResultsUpregulated transcripts formed disease-specific programs that were shared across multiple cell types, whereas downregulated transcripts were largely cell type–specific and conserved across diseases. We identified disease-associated cellular states, including a glomerular epithelial population exhibiting a hybrid transcriptional profile shared between podocytes and parietal epithelial cells (PECs); a podocyte subpopulation with endocytosis-related gene expression, correlated with proteinuria severity and treatment response; and expansion of periostin (POSTN)-expressing myofibroblasts, inversely associated with estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) and spatially colocalized with podocyte injury. Each cellular state was independently recovered in external single-cell cohorts.ConclusionSingle-nucleus transcriptomic profiling of human kidney biopsy tissues reveals disease-specific and shared cellular programs across major glomerular diseases. This resource provides insight into the cellular mechanisms underlying glomerular injury and identifies disease-associated cell states that may contribute to disease progression.

Glomerular diseases significantly contribute to the global burden of kidney disease and frequently lead to kidney failure.1, 2, 3, 4 Although they share common clinical manifestations such as proteinuria and progressive loss of kidney function, they differ considerably in their histopathologic features, mechanisms, and clinical trajectories.5, 6, 7, 8 Understanding the molecular heterogeneity underlying these diseases is therefore critical for improving disease classification and identifying potential therapeutic targets.

Because the kidney is composed of diverse and highly specialized cell populations, disease mechanisms may manifest through cell type-specific transcriptional alterations. Recent advances in single-cell and snRNA-seq have enabled high-resolution characterization of kidney cell populations and injury-associated transcriptional states.9, 10, 11, 12, 13 These approaches have provided important insights into kidney biology and disease mechanisms, including the identification of rare glomerular cell types such as podocytes that are often underrepresented in conventional transcriptomic studies.14 However, most single-cell studies of kidney disease have focused on individual disease entities or tissues obtained from nephrectomy specimens, limiting the ability to compare transcriptional alterations across multiple glomerular diseases using diagnostic human biopsy tissues.

To address this gap, we performed snRNA-seq on snap-frozen kidney biopsy tissues obtained from patients with 4 major glomerular diseases—MCD, MN, IgAN, and DKD—together with control kidney samples. By integrating transcriptomic profiles across disease groups and controls, we aimed to identify both shared and disease-specific transcriptional programs across kidney cell types and to characterize disease-associated cellular states within the human glomerulus.

Methods

Ethical Considerations and Patient Consent

The study protocol was approved by the institutional review board of Seoul National University Hospital (IRB Nos. H-2203-053-1303 and H-1404-117-575). The study samples were collected from the KORNERSTONE project, a multicenter, prospective cohort study and biobank for glomerular diseases, run under the National Biobank Project of the Republic of Korea.15 Patients provided informed consent for the collection of the biospecimens, their use in this study, and their use in a public database. The study was conducted under the National Research Project Single Cell Atlas of Immune Disease (http://www.scaid.org/).

Kidney Tissue Collection

The study subjects were enrolled in the KORNERSTONE project before their first diagnostic kidney biopsy. At our institution, three biopsy cores were typically obtained per patient and first evaluated by an attending nephropathologist for routine clinical diagnostics (light microscopy, immunofluorescence, and electron microscopy). Intact biopsy tissue not required for diagnostic evaluation was immediately snap-frozen at the bedside for biobanking. For snRNA-seq, the remaining cores were used in their entirety; because standard biopsy cores traverse the cortex and may variably extend toward the corticomedullary region, the sequenced material represents mixed cortical regions. We selected patients with preserved kidney function (eGFR ≥ 30 ml/min per 1.73 m2), to minimize disease-nonspecific transcriptomic effects of advanced fibrotic changes. Inclusion required primary MCD with overt nephrotic syndrome, anti-phospholipase A2 receptor antibody-positive MN, IgAN, or DKD without other glomerulonephritis pathologies; patients with urine protein creatinine ratio > 3 g/g (MCD, MN, and DKD) who were immunosuppression-naïve were prioritized to capture active disease. In this study, we included a total of 30 patient kidney samples (from 7 MCD, 6 MN, 13 IgAN, and 4 patients with DKD). Clinical data from the initial visit and treatment initiation were analyzed.

For control kidney tissues, we used the normal kidney cortex obtained during radical nephrectomy surgery for kidney cancer. We carefully reviewed the clinical histories to exclude patients with diabetic or hypertensive kidney disease and selected those without proteinuria or obstructive changes in the kidney. To minimize ischemic bias, an intraoperative gun biopsy was obtained from the exposed kidney before renal artery clamping, away from cancerous lesions. Tissues were snap-frozen and delivered to central storage. A total of 7 control patients were included in the study.

Results

Baseline Characteristics of the Study Subjects

We collected data from 37 participants, including individuals with MCD (n = 7), MN (n = 6), IgAN (n = 13), and DKD (n = 4), as well as a control group (n = 7). All patient samples were obtained from immunosuppressants-naïve individuals with typical clinical presentations, including primary MCD with overt nephrotic syndrome, anti-PLA2R antibody-positive MN, IgAN, or DKD without another diagnosis. Control kidney tissues were obtained from chemotherapy-naïve patients undergoing nephrectomy for renal cell carcinoma, using noncancerous lesions in cortical tissue. The baseline demographics of the study subjects are presented in Supplementary Table S1. The mean spot urine protein creatinine ratio for each condition was 8.09 ± 4.87 g/g for the MCD group, 7.98 ± 6.49 g/g for the MN group, 1.05 ± 1.36 g/g for the IgAN group, and 3.68 ± 1.78 g/g for the DKD group. The control group had no proteinuria. The baseline eGFR was 89.8 ± 26.8 ml/min per 1.73 m2 in the study subjects with typical clinical features.

We analyzed a total of 333,938 nuclei from biopsy tissues (Figure 1a) and identified 12 main cell clusters, annotated based on known canonical markers, including podocytes, PECs, various tubular cells, endothelial cells, fibroblasts, and immune cells (Figure 1b, c). We next examined the distribution of these subtypes across clinical groups and observed pronounced, disease-specific heterogeneity (Figure 1d). To validate these annotations, we compared our data with an external dataset from the Kidney Precision Medicine Project.9 This comparison revealed a high correlation in marker gene expression profiles (Supplementary Figure S1A). Furthermore, upregulated differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in our DKD samples showed a similar pattern to those in the Kidney Precision Medicine Project dataset (Supplementary Figure S1B). Disease-Dominant, Cell Type-Common, Cell Type-Dominant, and Disease-Common DEGs in Glomerular Diseases

Our analysis of DEGs revealed a dichotomous pattern—upregulated DEGs were largely disease-specific but shared across cell types, whereas downregulated DEGs were cell type-specific but common across diseases (Supplementary Table S3).

We identified disease-dominant upregulated gene modules enriched in each condition (Figure 1e and f; Supplementary Table S4). The MCD-dominant module was enriched for transporter-related genes (e.g., SLC13A1, SLC36A2; Module 5). The MN-dominant module included genes associated with epithelial-mesenchymal transition (e.g., SPP1, VIM) and the interferon response (e.g., HIF1A, IFNAR2; Module 8). The IgAN-dominant module was associated with previously reported pathways, including aldosterone-regulated sodium reabsorption (Module 2). In DKD, upregulated modules were associated with differentiation and metabolic processes (Module 15) (Figure 1f; Supplementary Table S5). Representative genes within each module also showed preferential expression in the corresponding disease group at the sample level (Supplementary Figure S2A). Module score analysis confirmed significantly higher in scores in the corresponding disease groups compared with others (Supplementary Figure S2B).

In contrast, downregulated DEGs exhibited a cell type-restricted pattern shared across diseases (Figure 1g and 1h; Supplementary Table S4). In podocytes, genes related to the actin cytoskeleton (e.g., IQGAP2, MICAL2)16,17 and cell-cell adhesion were reduced (Module 12). In PECs, genes negatively regulating mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) signaling were downregulated (Module 14).18 Tubular cells showed decreased expression of metabolic pathways, including fatty acid metabolism, in proximal tubule- and thick ascending limb-specific modules (Modules 1 and 3).19,20 In collecting duct principal cells, the downregulated module included the glucocorticoid receptor and NR4A1. Endothelial cells demonstrated reduced expression of SPRED2, with gene ontology analysis indicating negative regulation of the MAPK cascade (Module 9; Supplementary Table S6).21 For each cell type, module scores were significantly lower in diseased samples than in controls, without differences in unrelated cell types (Supplementary Figure S2C).

Disease-Associated Intermediate Epithelial State Between Injured Podocytes and Activated PECs

Subclustering of glomerular cells identified a distinct population positioned between podocytes and PECs on uniform manifold approximation and projection (Figure 2a).22 These cells coexpressed canonical markers of both lineages along with a distinct transcriptional profile (Figure 2b, Supplementary Figure S3A). A pseudotime analysis further demonstrated the transcriptional proximity of this population to both podocytes and PECs (Figure 2c). This population was enriched for PEC activation and proliferative signaling programs, including MAPK- and epidermal growth factor receptor-related pathways involving ERBB2, EGF, FGFR, and CD24, together with cluster-defining genes such as CASR and PRDM16 (Supplementary Figure S3B and C).18,23 This cluster also showed significant upregulation of genes known to be highly expressed in proliferative PECs (Figure 2d).22 Importantly, the proportion of this cluster was significantly correlated (Pearson's r = 0.35, R² = 0.13, P = 0.031) with clinical severity index that we defined (Figure 2e). Spatial validation reinforced these findings. Xenium in situ demonstrated coexpression of ALDH1A2 and NPHS1 in glomerular diseases (Figure 2f). At the protein level, immunofluorescence staining revealed that WT1, Claudin1 (encoded by CLDN1, Figure 1c), and PRDM16 (Supplementary Figure S3A) were coexpressed in all disease samples (Figure 2g). In addition, coexpression of Podocin (encoded by NPHS2) and CASR was also observed (Figure 2h and Supplementary Tables S7 and S8). Across conditions, both modalities showed increased abundance in glomerular diseases compared with controls (Figure 2i, Supplementary Figure S3E).24

To further characterize the glomerular niche surrounding this population, we performed CellChat analysis using glomerular and peri-glomerular populations together with spatial neighborhood analysis in Xenium data. Intermediate cells showed convergent incoming signaling associated with transforming growth factor (TGF)-β, platelet-derived growth factor, complement, and EGFR–MAPK-related pathways, including epidermal growth factor and neuregulin signaling from multiple glomerular cell populations (Supplementary Figure S3F). Consistent with these findings, Xenium neighborhood analysis demonstrated increased expression of TGFB1, PDGFRB, C3, STAT1, and ETS1 in cells spatially proximal to Intermediate cells, supporting the presence of an active glomerular injury niche associated with epithelial plasticity and PEC activation.

Disease-Specific Gene Expression in Podocytes and Podocytes With Endocytosis Feature

We further focused on podocytes, given their crucial role in glomerular pathophysiology. Comparison of downregulated DEGs between controls and disease groups revealed 2 dominant patterns—genes commonly reduced across all glomerular diseases (n = 101) and genes selectively reduced in DKD (n = 124; Figure 3a). The commonly downregulated DEGs were primarily associated with the regulation of cell migration and the negative regulation of the immune system (Figure 3b). This latter finding is consistent with the prominent interferon-γ response observed in the disease groups (Figure 3c). Upregulated DEGs were enriched in disease-specific gene modules across glomerular diseases, highlighting the strong disease specificity of transcriptional activation programs (Figure 3d). Upregulated DEGs in MN showed enrichment of epithelial-mesenchymal transition-related genes, whereas IgAN was associated with genes involved in cell-substrate junction assembly (Figure 3e). Across diseases, slit diaphragm-associated genes including TJP1 and MAGI2 were consistently downregulated, along with VCL, which contributes to maintenance of the glomerular filtration barrier.26, 27, 28 Additional disease-specific patterns were observed, including detachment-associated genes (NOX4, TCF21, and SLIT2)29, 30, 31, 32 in MCD and regeneration-related genes (PROM1 and PAX8)33,34 in DKD (Figure 3f and Supplementary Figure S4A and B and Supplementary Table S9). The expression of GADD45A, VIM, and CDH6,35, 36, 37 which are known to be associated with epithelial-mesenchymal transition, was found to be specifically elevated in MN.

Podocytes were divided into 3 clusters and annotated using marker genes (Figure 3g and Supplementary Figure S4C). One population was characterized by high expression of genes associated with cubilin and amnionless complex, including LRP2, CUBN, CLCN5, and PRKCA, and was therefore designated the podocyte endocytosis state (Supplementary Figure S4C and D).38 A similar transcriptional pattern cluster was also observed in podocytes in independent external single-cell datasets, including in patients with chronic kidney disease (Supplementary Figure S4E, F, and G).9

Compared with conventional podocytes, podocyte endocytosis showed increased PAX2,39 and reduced expression of CDKN1C,40 PODXL, and NPHS2, together with higher proliferation module scores (Supplementary Figure S4H and I). Importantly, the proportion of podocyte endocytosis cells significantly increased with baseline proteinuria severity (quasibinomial generalized linear model, β = 0.042, P = 0.001, pseudo-R2 = 0.226), whereas patients with persistent proteinuria after treatment exhibited higher podocyte endocytosis module scores, suggesting an association between this transcriptional state and poor treatment response (Figure 3h and i). Accordingly, the gene module score was significantly greater in the MCD and MN groups than in the other glomerular disease groups and the control group (Figure 3j). These findings suggest that the activation of endocytosis-related gene programs in podocytes reflects the severity of proteinuria and may also have prognostic implications. We validated the presence of “podocyte endocytosis” at both the transcriptomic and protein levels through the coexpression of LRP2 and NPHS1 in Xenium in situ and megalin (encoded by LRP2) and WT1 in immunofluorescence (Figure 3k–l and Supplementary Table S7). Additionally, the proportion of podocyte endocytosis tended to be higher in glomerular disease in Xenium in situ and IF (Supplementary Figure S4J and K).

To further characterize the signaling environment associated with podocyte endocytosis, we performed CellChat analysis across glomerular and peri-glomerular populations. Among the inferred pathways, Bradykinin, complement, and interleukin17 signaling showed preferential communication toward the podocyte endocytosis state (Supplementary Figure 4L). Bradykinin signaling originated from multiple glomerular populations, whereas complement and interleukin17 signaling were primarily inferred from juxtaglomerular apparatus cells, PECs, and podocytes. Together, these findings indicate an injury-related signaling microenvironment around podocyte endocytosis cells.

Drug2Cell (Wellcome Sanger Institute, Cambridge, UK)41 identified candidate compounds targeting podocyte endocytosis, including phosphodiesterase-4 inhibitors (Apremilast, Roflumilast, and Crisaborole) and the cyclic guanosine monophosphate-modulating agent isosorbide (Supplementary Figure S4M), suggesting that modulation of cyclic adenosine monophosphate and cyclic guanosine monophosphate pathways may offer promising therapeutic avenues for targeting podocyte endocytosis-related injury.

Stromal Activation and Myofibroblast Expansion Across Glomerular Diseases

Stromal cells (including vascular smooth muscle cells and fibroblasts) were subclustered into 12 clusters (Figure 4a, Supplementary Figure S5A).9 Across glomerular diseases, stromal cells showed consistent enrichment of TGF-β signaling, with the strongest activation observed in the myofibroblast cluster (Figure 4b and c). Trajectory analysis restricted to fibroblast-lineage cells revealed a differentiation continuum from fibroblasts to myofibroblasts present across all disease groups (Figure 4d), consistent with the established role of TGF-β signaling in myofibroblast activation. Along this trajectory, gene programs transitioned from mesenchymal differentiation to extracellular matrix organization, reflecting acquisition of myofibroblast functions (Supplementary Figure S5B). The expression levels of genes corresponding to the trajectory of the myofibroblast-enriched state and myofibroblast-associated genes, such as SULF1, NOX4, and COL16A1, were consistently increased in glomerular diseases (Supplementary Figure S5C). A heatmap analysis of fibroblast-associated cell types (e.g., fibroblasts, medullary fibroblasts, adaptive fibroblasts, and myofibroblasts) using their DEGs revealed a gene module (Module 4) that was commonly upregulated across all glomerular diseases (module 4, Supplementary Figure S5D and Supplementary Table S9). This module was significantly enriched for markers of myofibroblasts, TGF-β signaling-related genes, and genes of the Wnt pathway. Consistent with this, functional analysis of the module showed enrichment for biological processes related to myofibroblast function, such as extracellular matrix organization (Supplementary Figure S5E). The presence of this shared profibrotic pathway indicates that a common fibrotic program is activated across these diverse glomerular diseases.

The myofibroblast gene module score was significantly increased across all glomerular diseases (Supplementary Figure S5F). Importantly, this module score was inversely correlated with eGFR(linear regression r = -0.47, R2 = 0.22, P-value = 0.003), directly linking the fibrotic signature to declining kidney function (Figure 4f). Consistent with this, the proportion of myofibroblasts itself was also higher in the disease groups and was significantly associated with lower baseline (quasibinomial generalized linear model β = −0.013, P-value < 0.001, pseudo-R2 = 0.339) and follow-up eGFR (quasibinomial generalized linear model β = −0.009, P-value = 0.012, pseudo-R2 = 0.171) in regression analyses (Figure 4g and h). These associations persisted throughout the follow-up period, supporting a potential relationship between myofibroblast expansion and progressive kidney dysfunction.

Cell-cell interaction analysis highlighted Periostin pathway as a disease-specific pathway originating from myofibroblasts (Figure 4i). This pathway primarily targeted PECs and, in primary glomerular diseases, also affected podocytes (Figure 4j). POSTN signaling is implicated in wound healing and fibrosis,42,43 and POSTN-expressing fibroblasts were enriched within diseased glomeruli (Figure 4k). Their abundance was significantly associated with both proteinuria severity (R2 = 0.29, P-value = 0.016) and the proportion of podocyte endocytosis (R2 = 0.284, P-value = 0.019; Figure 4l and m). These results indicate that myofibroblasts are common pathogenic cell types in glomerular disease and are associated with a decline in kidney function. Among them, POSTN-expressing myofibroblasts are additionally associated with proteinuria and appear to represent a key cellular component underlying glomerular disease manifestations.

To identify potential therapeutic targets for myofibroblasts, we applied the Drug2Cell framework (Supplementary Figure S5G).41 Among the top-ranked candidate compounds, cetirizine and olaratumab were identified as potential modulators of myofibroblast-associated programs.44 These findings suggest that targeting myofibroblast activity may represent a potential therapeutic strategy for glomerular disease-associated fibrosis.

Discussion

This study presents a single-nucleus transcriptome analysis of human biopsy-confirmed glomerular diseases, comprising 333,938 nuclei obtained from 37 individuals, including 30 treatment-naïve patients with MCD, MN, IgAN, and DKD and 7 controls. By profiling diverse kidney cell populations from diagnostic biopsy cores, our dataset provides a comprehensive view of early disease-associated transcriptional changes across glomerular diseases. Another strength of this study is the inclusion of subjects without substantial GFR reduction, which enabled us to compare cell type-specific transcriptomic alterations in relatively early disease states. Our transcriptome data may enhance the understanding of disease-specific pathophysiologic mechanisms of glomerular diseases, which may lead to the discovery of diagnostic or therapeutic biomarkers and common pathological pathways contributing to the progression of chronic kidney disease. This atlas therefore provides a framework for interpreting cell type–specific transcriptional alterations across major glomerular diseases.

A notable observation from our analysis was the distinct pattern of transcriptional regulation across diseases and cell types. Upregulated genes tended to form disease-dominant modules, whereas downregulated genes were largely organized by cell type. This pattern suggests that disease-specific signaling programs activate distinct transcriptional responses, whereas functional impairment manifests through loss of cell type-specific gene expression. For example, the MN-dominant transcriptional signature was enriched for epithelial-mesenchymal transition and interferon-γ response pathways, consistent with immune-mediated injury associated with phospholipase A2 receptor autoimmunity45,46 and IgAN modules with MAPK signaling, consistent with MAPK and/or extracellular signal-regulated kinase activation in mesangial injury.47 Nevertheless, several upregulated modules also exhibited cell type-restricted patterns that complemented the corresponding downregulated programs within the same cell type. For example, proximal tubule modules paired upregulated transport programs with downregulated metabolic programs, suggesting adaptive tubular hypertrophy; endothelial modules paired vasculogenic activation with loss of MAPK negative regulators.

We also identified a glomerular epithelial population exhibiting a hybrid transcriptional profile shared between podocytes and PECs. Podocytes are terminally differentiated cells that undergo structural injury and transcriptional remodeling under stress, whereas PECs retain proliferative potential and can respond dynamically to podocyte loss. The hybrid epithelial population was enriched in diseased glomeruli and showed activation of epithelial stress- and proliferation-associated programs, suggesting epithelial plasticity and remodeling within the injured glomerular compartment. This observation is consistent with previous studies showing that pathological activation of PEC signaling pathways can lead to abnormal PEC proliferation and contribute to glomerulosclerosis. Spatial transcriptomic analysis further supported the presence of these cells within diseased glomeruli. Although the lineage relationships of these cells cannot be resolved from cross-sectional human biopsy data alone, their shared transcriptional features and spatial localization support the existence of an injury-associated hybrid epithelial state in glomerular disease.

Our study further identified a podocyte subpopulation characterized by expression of genes involved in protein endocytosis, including the cubilin and amnionless complex components LRP2 and CUBN.38,48, 49, 50, 51 Previous studies have suggested that podocytes may activate endocytic pathways under protein overload to maintain filtration barrier integrity. Consistent with this, the podocyte endocytosis program was associated with increased proteinuria and remained elevated in patients with persistent proteinuria after treatment. Recent work suggesting that antinephrin autoantibodies in podocytopathies induce nephrin internalization and glomerular endocytic activity provides a potential mechanistic framework, and the prominent MCD enrichment may reflect this adaptive or injury-associated response to slit diaphragm dysfunction.52 The preferential enrichment of Bradykinin, complement, and interleukin17 signaling toward this population further suggests an injury-associated microenvironment, consistent with podocyte dedifferentiation features. These findings suggest that this transcriptional state may represent an adaptive but injury-associated podocyte response with potential relevance to disease severity and treatment response.

In addition to epithelial injury programs, we observed consistent activation of stromal fibrotic pathways across glomerular diseases. Trajectory analysis suggested differentiation from fibroblasts to myofibroblasts, accompanied by enrichment of TGF-β signaling and extracellular matrix programs. Notably, the abundance of myofibroblasts correlated with declining kidney function even in patients without severe functional impairment at diagnosis, suggesting that fibrotic remodeling begins early in disease progression. Spatial transcriptomic data further revealed enrichment of POSTN-expressing fibroblasts within diseased glomeruli, supporting the existence of localized fibrotic microenvironments that may promote ongoing glomerular injury. Our data extend previous observations by demonstrating these fibrotic programs at single-cell resolution within human biopsy tissues. Drug2cell analysis further identified candidate compounds targeting disease-associated cellular states, providing a framework for future therapeutic hypothesis generation.

The enrichment of distinct pathways in endothelial cells offers potential mechanistic insights into these diseases. For instance, the prominent TGF-β signaling in MN may contribute to the increased glomerular basement membrane thickness characteristic of the disease.53 Furthermore, the upregulation of major histocompatibility complex molecules in both MCD and MN suggests that endothelial cells may activate antigen-experienced T-cells, implicating T-cell involvement in nephrotic syndrome.54,55 Finally, the elevated interferon signature in MCD could help explain its characteristic steroid sensitivity.56

There are several limitations in this study. First, glomerular cells comprised a relatively small proportion of total captured nuclei, reflecting the technical challenges of profiling human diagnostic biopsy tissue. Nevertheless, the absolute number of glomerular nuclei (n = 13,475) was sufficient to identify multiple disease-associated cell states such as podocyte endocytosis. Second, the cohort is heterogeneous with limited samples per disease; however, the use of treatment-naïve diagnostic biopsies allowed us to capture early disease-associated transcriptional programs rarely accessible in human studies and was designed for shared rather than disease-specific classification. Third, our dataset mainly comprises transcriptomic information. Further enhancement by the inclusion of proteomic or epigenetic profiling could expand the insights related to pathophysiologic alterations in glomerular disease.57 Lastly, control tissues were obtained from histologically normal cortex of nephrectomy specimens rather than healthy donor biopsies; subtle transcriptional alterations from tumor microenvironment effects cannot be fully excluded. To minimize these effects, control samples were collected by intraoperative biopsy-gun before renal artery clamping, mimicking native biopsy acquisition. Future validation with living donor or preimplantation biopsies may further confirm disease-associated programs.

In conclusion, we report an extensive snRNA-seq dataset generated from rare and clinically valuable treatment-naïve human kidney biopsy tissues across MCD, MN, IgAN, and DKD. This dataset uniquely captures disease-relevant transcriptomic states of kidney-resident cells at the time of diagnosis, offering a snapshot that is difficult to obtain in other models or settings. Our findings provide novel insights into disease- and cell type-specific alterations in glomerular diseases. As a resource study, this work constitutes a foundational reference atlas that may support future investigations aiming to discover diagnostic markers, therapeutic targets, and mechanistic pathways in human kidney disease. Importantly, because the KORNERSTONE cohort is primarily composed of Korean patients, this dataset also expands the ethnic diversity of currently available kidney single-cell reference resources, which remain heavily weighted toward Western populations.
