Characterization of aberrant alternative splicing landscape in patients with metastatic renal cell carcinoma
Authors: Ameish Govindarajan, Nathaniel Hansen, Benjamin D Mercier, Sara A Byron, Apurva Hegde, Krystine Garcia-Mansfield, Kristin Leskoske, Neal Chawla, Luis Meza, Zeynep Zengin, Regina Barragan-Carillo, Hedyeh Ebrahimi, Daniela V Castro, Nazli Dizman, JoAnn Hsu, Alexander Chehrazi-Raffle, Abhishek Tripathi, Nicholas J Salgia, Sumanta K Pal, Patrick Pirrotte
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeAberrant alternative splicing (AS) events have been implicated in cancer progression; however, their role in metastatic renal cell carcinoma (mRCC) remains underexplored. This study aims to identify AS events associated with clinical benefits from immune checkpoint inhibitors and targeted therapies in mRCC.Materials and methodsWe conducted a retrospective analysis on 101 patients with mRCC who received systemic therapy and underwent RNA sequencing. Patients were divided into subgroups based on ICIs (alone or in combination) and targeted therapies. Responders and non-responders were classified according to Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors V.1.1 criteria. Differential gene expression and splicing analyses were performed between responders and non-responders in each cohort. Novel AS events were analyzed for their potential to generate peptide neoantigens through major histocompatibility complex (MHC) class I binding predictions.ResultsOutlier splicing analysis identified 10 aberrant splice events specific to mRCC. AS analysis revealed 461 differentially spliced events between responders and non-responders in the ICI cohort and 253 in the targeted therapy cohort, with intron retention as the predominant motif. Thirteen unique AS events were enriched in responders, including PTPN6 and ACTN1. Predictive neoantigen analysis identified high MHC class I binding potential in peptides from AS events in IFFO1 and ZNF692. High splice burden was linked to an immunogenic tumor microenvironment, characterized by enriched antigen processing and adaptive immune responses.ConclusionsThis study provides a comprehensive analysis of AS events in mRCC, highlighting intron retention as potential biomarkers for treatment response. Identified AS-derived neoantigens may serve as potential targets for adoptive cell therapy strategies.Keywords: Renal Cell Carcinoma, Immune Checkpoint Inhibitor, Biomarker, Tumor microenvironment - TME

Introduction

The therapeutic paradigm for metastatic renal cell carcinoma (RCC) (mRCC) has rapidly evolved in recent years. Notably, targeted therapies (TTs) such as anti-angiogenic therapies, immuno-oncology (IO) therapies/immunotherapies, such as immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), and combination TT/IO regimens are now entrenched as standard practices in the management of mRCC.1 However, the field still grapples with the scarcity of reliable prognostic or correlative biomarkers to gage the clinical benefit of available mRCC therapies.2 Investigations into potential biomarkers within the mRCC tumor microenvironment (TME), including programmed death-ligand 1 expression and tumor microsatellite instability, have demonstrated inconclusive predictive potential.3,6 One biological property that has received less attention as a potential influencer and predictor of therapeutic response in mRCC is aberrant alternative splicing (AS) events.7

AS is a crucial gene regulatory mechanism vital for enhancing proteomic complexity. In healthy cells, over 95% of pre-messenger RNA (mRNA) synthesized in the human genome undergoes splicing to form mature mRNA.8 Significant errors can occur in the AS process. These errors, or aberrant AS events, can lead to the production of abnormal or truncated protein isoforms. These dysregulated AS events are increasingly recognized as significant contributors to tumorigenesis and metastasis, implicating a substantial role in the development and progression of various cancer types.9,13

Aberrant AS events are known to occur in categorizable motifs. These include exon skipping, 3’ splice site, 5’ splice site, mutually exclusive exons, and, more rarely, intron retention.14 It is well-established that these aberrations of pre-mRNA splicing can significantly alter the marking of exons and the occurrence of AS events overall.15 Thus, aberrant AS events are potentially significant to metastatic processes and subsequent responsiveness to systemic anticancer agents in mRCC. While potential correlative features of aberrant AS events have been observed within other cancers, little literature exists examining AS events in mRCC.7 10 12 13

This study is a novel analysis of AS events in mRCC. By employing multifactorial analysis of tumor-derived RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) data, we delve into the landscape of differentially spliced events in patients with mRCC who achieve response to systemic therapy, demonstrate the ability of novel splice products to be displayed as putative neoantigens, and link high splice burden with a more immunogenic TME in RCC.

Materials and methods

Clinical data collation and outcome assessment for patients with mRCC

Patients with mRCC treated at a National Cancer Institute-designated comprehensive cancer center facility were identified from an institutional review board-approved, prospectively maintained clinical database. Patients with tumor tissue that had undergone paired-normal whole exome sequencing and tumor whole transcriptome sequencing with the OncoExTra assay (Exact Sciences, Madison, Wisconsin, USA) as part of clinical care were analyzed. Patients were divided into subgroups based on the classification of systemic therapies received—an IO subgroup including patients who received an ICI (either alone or in combination with another agent) across any line of therapy and a TT subgroup comprising patients who received an anti-angiogenic or other form of systemic TT (eg, mTOR inhibitor). Objective therapeutic response outcomes were defined by Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST) V.1.1 criteria. Based on previously developed criteria in previous publications, patients achieving complete/partial responses or stable disease for six or more months on therapy were classified as “responders”, whereas patients experiencing progressive disease or stable disease for less than 6 months were considered “non-responders”.16 17

Clinicodemographic features including sex, age, race, and smoking history were collected for each patient (table 1). Clinical variables specific to primary tumors, such as nephrectomy status and tumor, node, metastases classification, were also recorded. For the tissue collection site, we noted whether the tissue was from the primary site (kidney) or a metastatic site. Risk status was defined at the time of initial metastatic presentation according to the International Metastatic RCC Database Consortium criteria. A linear regression model was used to determine correlations between these variables and disease response to targeted and immunotherapeutic treatments.

Transcriptomic sequencing of RCC tumor specimens

RCC tumor specimens were collected for sequencing prior to treatment initiation. Paired-end bulk RNA-seq of these specimens was performed as part of the OncoExTra (previously Ashion GEM ExTra) clinical assay, as previously described.17 FASTQ files were aligned to the hg38 reference genome with STAR (V.2.7.5a) and quantification of gene counts was performed with featureCounts from Subread (V.2.0.0) for use in differential expression analysis.

Differential gene expression analysis of responders versus non-responders to immunotherapy and targeted therapy

Differential gene expression analysis between responders and non-responders within each treatment cohort—IO and TT—was performed with edgeR V.3.38.4 using Benjamini-Hochberg correction for p value adjustment. Genes were filtered to include only those with a counts-per-million value of greater than one across two or more samples prior to differential expression analysis. Downstream Gene Set Enrichment Analysis was then performed using fgsea (V.1.22) for the following molecular signature database (MSigDB) collections: Hallmark, BioCarta, Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG), Reactome, Gene Ontology Biological Processes, and Gene Ontology Molecular Functions obtained through the msigdbr package (V.7.5.1) in R. Pathways with a normalized enrichment score of ≥ |1.5| and a false discovery rate of <0.05 were considered as significantly enriched or de-enriched in responders across treatment cohorts.

Differential and outlier splice event analysis

Detection and quantification of AS events was performed using SplAdder (V.2.3.0) on aligned RNA-seq reads.18 SplAdder detects and quantifies both canonical and novel splice events using the portion of RNA-seq reads that contain splice isoforms, also known as per cent spliced in (PSI). SplAdder output files (.counts.hdf5) were loaded into the Translational Genomics Research Institute’s splicing analysis pipeline, Bisbee.19 Using the PSI value from SplAdder outputs, Bisbee Diff performs a beta binomial analysis to determine whether a splice event is differentially spliced between two groups. For analyses performed within, MaxW was set to 200 and a log-likelihood ratio (LLR) of 8 was used as a threshold to determine significance. Bisbee outlier analysis identifies splice isoforms that are unique in an individual when compared with a reference set, which in this case was the entire Genotype-Tissue Expression (GTEx) tissue library with the exclusion of testis tissue samples, downloaded from Genomic Data Commons, (https://gdc.cancer.gov/about-data/publications/PanCanAtlas-Splicing-2018). The Bisbee outlier detection method parameter was set to 80 with a log likelihood (LL) cut-off of 10. Prediction of resulting protein sequences of the differential splice events was performed in Bisbee using the hg19 genome FASTA file for reference.

Neoantigen prediction of splice events

Identification of human leukocyte antigen (HLA) class I calls from each patient sample was performed with phlat, using the top 14 most frequently observed HLA calls for HLA-I binding predictions.20 NetMHCpan V.4.1 tiled the predicted peptides into lengths of 8–11 amino acids to replicate the length of HLA-I bound peptides.21 NetMHCpan V.4.1 identifies strong and weak binders as those with a rank <0.5 and 2, respectively, which were subsequently run through DeepHLApan to rank the best MHC-I binding peptides based on HLA-peptide binding and score the predicted immunogenicity of the peptide-HLA complex.22 DeepHLApan considers peptide-HLA complexes with a rank ≤20 as a high confidence neoantigen.

Differential gene expression analysis of splice burden-high versus burden-low cases

Patient samples were next classified as splice burden-high or burden-low based on the median number of novel isoform counts across the patient cohort as quantified by SplAdder. Differential gene expression analysis between splice-high and splice-low transcriptomes was performed with edgeR V.3.38.4 as described above. Differentially expressed genes with a log fold change of ≥5 that were enriched in splice burden-high samples were subsequently collated into a 20-gene novel splice burden gene signature. Enrichment of the novel splice burden gene signature in responders to both IO and TT was assessed using the fgsea package.

Results

Patient characteristics of mRCC clinical cohort

Patients with mRCC receiving IO and/or TT regimens who had undergone tumor transcriptomic profiling were retrospectively identified from a single institution. Clinical outcomes were collated and assessed for an initial cohort of 142 patients. However, 35 patients were excluded due to insufficient follow-up or lack of sufficient tissue collection, and an additional 6 were excluded due to low-quality RNA-seq outputs, resulting in 101 patients included in the final cohort (figure 1). Within this population, 91 patients received an IO-based regimen during their course of therapy, 57 of whom were classified as “responders’’ (≥6 mos. of partial or complete disease response) and 34 were “non-responders” (<6 mos. of stable disease or disease progression). Further, 77 patients received a TT across any line of therapy, 59 of whom were classified as “responders” and 18 as “non-responders”. In total, 67 patients received both IO and TT independently during their course of treatment. Full clinicopathologic features of the patient cohort are provided in table 1. Details of therapeutic regimens used to treat patients can be found in online supplemental table 1.

Differential gene expression analysis between responders and non-responders to systemic therapies in mRCC

To identify transcriptomic profiles enriched in patients with mRCC achieving response to IO and/or TT within our real-world cohort, we performed differential gene expression and Gene Set Enrichment Analysis between responders and non-responders within both treatment groups. KLK15, encoding kallikrein-related peptidase 15, was the most differentially enriched gene in IO responders (log fold change (FC)=7.22) whereas MUC5AC, encoding mucin 5AC, was the most differentially enriched gene in IO non-responders (logFC=−11.86) (figure 2A). A similar analysis was performed within the TT cohort (figure 2B). Among genes mapped to a defined gene symbol, DBH—encoding dopamine beta-hydroxylase—was the most enriched gene in TT responders (logFC=5.30). Conversely, CRP (C-reactive protein) was the most enriched gene (excluding those with unmapped gene symbols and pseudogenes) in TT non-responders (logFC=−4.72).

Next, Gene Set Enrichment Analysis was performed within each treatment cohort. Across the referenced MSigDB collections, 146 gene sets were significantly different between responders and non-responders in the IO cohort (n=118 pathways enriched in responders, n=28 pathways enriched in non-responders). Alternatively, within the TT cohort, a total of 357 gene sets were significantly different between responders and non-responders (n=97 pathways enriched in responders, n=260 pathways enriched in non-responders). Of note, the KEGG spliceosome gene set (normalized enirchment score (NES)=−1.54, false discovery rate (FDR)=0.045), the Reactome mRNA splicing gene set (NES=−1.59, FDR=0.026), and the Reactome Processing of Capped Intron Containing Pre-mRNA gene set (NES=−1.62, FDR=0.008) were significantly enriched in non-responders to TT (figure 2C). Conversely, multiple humoral immune response pathways were significantly enriched in responders to IO (figure 2D). When comparing between IO and TT cohorts, multiple pathways were conserved among responders across treatment groups, including Striated Muscle Contraction, Muscle Contraction, Myogenesis, and Ion Homeostasis. Full differential gene expression and Gene Set Enrichment Analysis results comparing responders versus non-responders across both treatment cohorts are provided in online supplemental data 1.

Outlier splicing analysis of mRCC reveals dysregulated splice events compared with healthy cohort

To identify mRCC-specific splice events, Bisbee outlier analysis was performed on patient tumor-derived transcriptomic profiling outputs compared with the complete GTEx tissue library, excluding testis tissue samples. In total, 14,717 alternative splice events were identified as outlier splicing events in at least one patient with mRCC compared with the GTEx reference set. Of those splice events, 10,522 were protein coding and 9,097 were predicted to code for a novel protein sequence. Of particular interest, 10 alternative splice events predicted to code for a novel protein were outliers in at least 80 patients within the mRCC cohort, six of which were intron retention and four of which were exon skipping events. Five of those aberrant splice events occur in POMZP3, EIF4A1, TTYH1, TRMT2B and TCF7 and have a significantly increased PSI in patients with mRCC samples compared with the GTEx reference. The remaining five aberrant splice events occur in RPLP1, SLC8B1, RPLP0, SOD2 and RPLP1 and have a significantly decreased PSI in patients with mRCC samples compared with the GTEx reference.

Differential splicing analysis in IO and TT cohorts uncovers dominant splicing motifs

To ascertain potential AS landscape association with noted differences mRCC systemic treatment response, differential splice analysis was performed between responders and non-responders within each treatment group. Bisbee Diff analysis identified 461 differentially spliced events (LLR>8) between responders and non-responders within the IO cohort, 251 of which encoded for a protein sequence not annotated as canonical in the Ensembl database—deemed novel AS events (figure 3A). 192 of these 251 novel AS events were intron retention, showing an enrichment of intron retention events in responders relative to non-responders (figure 3B,C). Within the TT subgroup, Bisbee analysis identified 253 differentially spliced events (LLR>8), 119 of which encoded for a non-canonical protein sequence (figure 3D). Similar to the IO cohort, 87 of the 119 novel AS events were intron retention, again revealing an enrichment of intron retention events in responders compared with non-responder samples (figure 3E,F).

Novel splice event products are capable of being displayed as neoantigens on HLA molecules

We further explored whether differentially spliced sequences shared between the IO and TT cohorts could serve as putative neoantigenic targets given that both IO and TT are predicated on the activity of microenvironmental constituents, including adaptive immune cells that function in an antigen-restricted manner within the RCC TME. Neoantigen prediction by MHC I binding analysis revealed 84 high confidence neoantigens. Aberrant AS events in ZNF692 and IFFO1 produced the greatest amount of high confidence neoantigens based on binding and immunogenicity scores in IO and TT (figure 4D,E).

Differential gene expression reveals an immunogenic landscape associated with high splice burden tumors

Given that these aberrant AS events have potential to be translated into immunogenic neoantigenic peptides in mRCC, we next explored if novel splice events were associated with changes in immune microenvironmental phenotype. Samples across the entire cohort were classified as novel splice burden-high or burden-low based on the median number of novel splice events per patient as identified by Bisbee analysis. Differential gene expression between splice burden-high and burden-low samples and Gene Set Enrichment Analysis were performed. In total, 4,256 genes were significantly enriched in splice burden-high patients, compared with 2,710 genes in splice burden-low patients (figure 5A).

In line with the finding that novel AS events can be presented on MHC I molecules and thus serve as putative neoantigenic targets for recognition by immune effectors, we identified numerous antigen processing and presentation signatures enriched in splice burden-high patients (figure 5B). Nearly 50 adaptive immune cell pathways were enriched in splice burden-high patients (figure 5C). T-cell responses have canonically been associated with immune-reactive properties in mRCC, and accordingly, multiple T-cell proliferation, activation, and effector pathways were enriched in splice-burden high patients. Notably, however, the adaptive immune pathways most associated with splice burden-high tumors, as determined by normalized enrichment score, were related to humoral immune responses, including B lymphocyte maturation and immunoglobulin production pathways. Specifically, the seven most differentially enriched (and 14 of the top 16) adaptive immune pathways in splice-burden high patients were related to B lymphocyte activity. Full differential gene expression and Gene Set Enrichment Analysis results comparing splice burden-high versus splice burden-low samples are provided in online supplemental data 2.

To connect splice burden back to therapeutic responses, a novel splice burden gene set was developed from differentially expressed genes in novel splice burden-high samples. Within both the IO and TT cohorts, this novel splice burden-high gene set was enriched in responders relative to non-responders (NES=2.39 and 2.07; FDR=8.52×10−23 and 5.10×10–12, respectively; figure 5D). These findings serve to connect the transcriptomic features of tumors with high splice burdens to therapeutic outcomes in mRCC.

Discussion

Biological understanding of the drivers of response to systemic therapy in mRCC remains incomplete. Thus, identification of biomarkers for predicting response to systemic therapies remains a critical obstacle in the management of mRCC. Preliminary analysis outside of the AS landscape has shown some promise. In their exploratory proteomic profiling of a small cohort of patients with mRCC, Simonetti and colleagues previously demonstrated a correlation of plasma receptor activator of nuclear factor kappa-B ligand levels and response to immunotherapy.16 While current limited research has assessed the AS landscape of mRCC, our findings suggest AS as a promising area of exploration for both biomarker discovery and improved biological understanding of the mRCC TME. These investigations into the transcriptomic and aberrant splicing landscape of mRCC reveal key differences in novel splice events between responders and non-responders to systemic therapy, identify gene-specific AS events that may carry potential to predict patient therapeutic response, and correlate splicing events with a more immunogenic TME in mRCC.

When interrogating transcriptomic features of responders and non-responders to systemic therapy, multiple mRNA splicing-associated gene sets were differentially expressed between treatment outcome groups. As such, subsequent investigation of AS events in patients with mRCC was performed. We identified a marked enrichment of intron retention events compared with other splice event motifs, both within the cohort and within subgroups of responders to both IO and TT. Intron retentions have canonically been observed as relatively uncommon alteration events in healthy tissue, whereas we have shown that intron retention events represent the majority of aberrant splicing events in mRCC. Recent research indicates that intron retention plays a crucial role in generating transcriptional diversity in cancers. These events contribute to cancer pathogenesis and therapeutic potential by producing novel protein isoforms and neoepitopes, which can influence the TME and immune response.23 This suggests that intron retention events have a crucial impact on generating heterogeneity within the proteomic landscape of the RCC TME.

Further analysis demonstrated the presence of novel splice events in responders to systemic therapy, including 23 gene-specific splice events differentially enriched in both therapy cohorts. Thirteen of these were enriched in responders to both IO and TT. Notably, splice events generating novel proteins in MOV10 and GTF2H2 have limited literature on their role in tumorigenesis or tumor proliferation in RCC and other cancers, warranting further clinical validation as potential biomarkers for treatment response in RCC. Enriched AS events in ACTN1 were present in responders from both cohorts and have been associated with chemoresistance in other tumor types, including head and neck squamous cell carcinoma and pancreatic cancer, respectively.24 25 However, their occurrence as enriched AS events in any oncological setting remains uncharacterized.26 Aberrant splicing events, such as intron retention, were also previously demonstrated to play a significant role in loss of function.27 PTPN6 had enriched novel splice events in both cohorts and plays an immune suppressive role in glioblastoma. It also serves as a prognostic biomarker in glioblastoma and colorectal cancer and shows promise in predicting IO response.28 29 TMEM106A, which was enriched for novel splice events in responders, acts as a tumor suppressor in RCC, with knockdown leading to increased colony formation within in vitro models.30 Its potential as a biomarker for treatment response in RCC requires further validation.

Among the 23 gene-specific splice events generating novel peptides, eight demonstrated higher expression in opposing responses to systemic therapy. TSR1 and GRHPR showed higher AS expression in responders, with TSR1 negatively correlated with treatment response in gastric cancer, although its role in RCC remains unclear.31 Novel peptide-generating AS events in IFFO1 and ZNF692 were highly expressed in responders to IO and TT. While these genes play roles in tumorigenesis and metastasis in RCC and colorectal cancer, the specific impact of AS events within these genes is not yet understood.32 33

Additionally, recent research has demonstrated that intron retention-derived neoantigens can trigger immune responses and are associated with favorable clinical outcomes in pancreatic cancer.34 High intron retention-derived neoantigenic load has been linked to better overall survival and a more immunogenic TME, characterized by increased tumor-infiltrating immune cells and differential expression of immune checkpoint genes. This indicates that intron retention events contribute to the diversity of the proteome by producing a wide array of neoantigens, which can modulate the immune landscape of the tumor.

In line with these findings in other solid tumors, the products of the aberrant splicing activities identified in the analyses within were predicted to bind as epitopes to MHC antigen presentation molecules, thus representing putative neoantigens and mediators of antitumor immune activity in mRCC. Currently, the study of neoantigenicity in RCC has been a considerable struggle. TMB has often been employed as a surrogate of neoantigen production in solid tumors, yet the TMB of RCC cases is low to moderate compared with other tumor types that respond to IOs.35 36 Indeed, identifying targetable neoantigens in RCC has been a troublesome endeavor. Recent studies have shown the potential for both genomic and transcriptomic events (ie, insertion-deletion mutations and aberrant expression of transposable elements) to yield immunogenic neoantigens in RCC.37,39 Building off of such results, the high prevalence of intron retention events identified in mRCC not only underscores their role in tumorigenesis but also highlights their potential as a novel class of putative neoantigens capable of enhancing antitumor immune function in the mRCC TME.

Novel splice burden was additionally correlated with transcriptomic profiles of the RCC TME to further explore the immunogenic impacts of these AS outputs. Congruent with the finding that these splice events could be processed and presented on MHC molecules, we identified numerous antigen processing and presentation pathways as enriched in splice burden-high patients, further supporting the notion of these AS products as neoantigens. Among our most intriguing findings, however, came with an analysis of the adaptive immune compartment. While cytotoxic T cells have been the primary focus of strategies to understand and augment antitumor immunity in RCC, we identified humoral immune responses as the most significantly enriched pathways in splice burden-high tumors. Numerous B-cell programs were enriched for in splice burden-high tumors. This finding paralleled the upregulation of various humoral immune pathways in patients achieving response to IO within our cohort. Recent work has sought to better understand the antitumor potential of humoral immune responses in RCC. Meylan and colleagues demonstrated that increased tertiary lymphoid structure abundance in clear cell RCCs resulted in greater cancer-specific immunoglobulin production and resultant antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity, further correlating immunoglobulin presence in the TME with improved outcomes to ICIs.40 As this humoral response still occurs in an antigen-restricted manner, the data herein support the role of AS-based neoantigens as potential mediators of humoral immune responses and, more broadly, antitumor immunity.

Due to the nature of our retrospective study, several limitations affect the scope of our analysis. The limited size of our patient cohort poses challenges regarding the generalizability of our findings to the broader population of patients with mRCC. Furthermore, our relatively small cohort prevented us from controlling the number of prior lines of treatment a patient had received. While smaller, more controlled cohorts have previously shown ability to screen for potential biomarkers, we intended our study to be a broad characterization and initial test of feasibility in using our computational methods to profile aberrant AS events in RCC-derived transcriptomic data.16 Patients who received TTs or IOs on their second or later line of treatment were included within their respective cohorts. Demographically, our patient cohort was also relatively homogenous, with a disproportionate majority identifying as white (82.2%), potentially limiting broader applicability of our findings to all patients. The organization of our limited TT and IO cohorts required the grouping together of a very heterogeneous array of disease histologies and modalities of therapy. TT and IO therapies often vary in their mechanisms of action, and we acknowledge the limitation this may pose in the direct clinical applicability of our findings. Modalities of systemic treatment are largely administered agnostic to disease histology in RCC. Despite differences in outcome between each subtype, patients with non-clear cell disease will often receive the same systemic therapies as patients with clear cell disease. Finally, tissue collection sites for transcriptomic and exomic sequencing were not standardized, with samples taken from primary tumors or from metastatic lesions, including the lungs or bone.

Conclusions

This study represents the first comprehensive analysis of AS events in patients with mRCC. Intron retention events were the most common AS motifs profiled and were enriched in patients responsive to both IO and TT. We demonstrate multiple other aberrant AS events were enriched across patient cohorts achieving clinical response to IO and TTs and could potentially serve as predictive biomarkers to systemic therapy response in mRCC. Future work must seek to interrogate aberrant AS products, including those identified herein, as predictive biomarkers to treatment response in controlled retrospective clinical trial datasets and in prospective, single-disease histology cohorts of patients with mRCC receiving uniform treatment or in patients with resected locally advanced RCC receiving adjuvant IO therapy. Immunogenomic analyses revealed these splice products are also capable of presentation on class I MHC molecules and thus can serve as putative neoantigens for adaptive immune cell recognition. Congruently, numerous adaptive immune gene pathways were enriched in patients with mRCC with high splice burden, particularly humoral immune responses. These data suggest aberrant AS-derived neoantigens can serve as potential targets for adoptive cell therapy strategies designed to augment systemic therapy responses for patients with mRCC.
