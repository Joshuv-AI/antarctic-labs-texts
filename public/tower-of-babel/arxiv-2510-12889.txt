Dodoor: Efficient Randomized Decentralized Scheduling with Load Caching for Heterogeneous Tasks and Clusters
Wei Da, Evangelia Kalyvianaki

Abstract
. This paper introduces Dodoor, an efficient randomized decentralized scheduler designed for task scheduling in modern data centers. Dodoor leverages advanced research on the weighted balls-into-bins model with b b -batched setting. Unlike other decentralized schedulers that rely on real-time probing of remote servers, Dodoor makes scheduling decisions based on cached server information, which is updated in batches, to reduce communication overheads. To schedule tasks with dynamic, multidimensional resource requirements in heterogeneous cluster, Dodoor uses a novel load score to measure servers’ loads for each scheduled task. This score captures the anti-affinity between servers and tasks in contrast to the commonly used heuristic of counting pending tasks to balance load. On a 101-node heterogeneous cluster, Dodoor is evaluated using two workloads: (i) simulated Azure virtual machines placements and (ii) real serverless Python functions executions in Docker. The evaluation shows that Dodoor reduces scheduling messages by 55–66% on both workloads. Dodoor can also increase throughput by up to 33.2% and 21.5% by reducing mean makespan latency by 12.1% and 7.2%, and improving tail latency by 21.9% and 24.6% across the two workloads.



1.
Introduction

Data center scheduling is an instrumental management operation driven by the exponential increase in data and application volume in modern data centers. The emergence of big data and AI applications has highlighted the need for efficient task scheduling, making it a vital component for both modern industrial applications
(
Verma et al. 2015
;
Schwarzkopf et al. 2013
;
Burns et al. 2016
)
and research
(
Isard et al. 2009
;
Boutin et al. 2014
;
Gog et al. 2016
)
. In large-scale data centers characterized by high throughput, heavy workloads, and stringent latency Service Level Agreements (SLAs), lightweight stateless distributed schedulers
(
Ousterhout et al. 2013
;
Dogar et al. 2014
;
Wang et al. 2015
)
have been developed to ensure efficient scheduling with low latency. Unlike centralized schedulers, which have complete access to cluster status, decentralized schedulers
(
envoy [n. d.]
;
Anderson and Oanta [n. d.]
;
Garrett 2018
)
typically rely on probing a subset of workers to gather partial information before making scheduling decisions. These schedulers often cast scheduling as a balls-into-bins problem
(
Mitzenmacher 2001
)
and use the power-of-two method for decisions. This approach enhances efficiency, scalability, and reliability by eliminating the need for a comprehensive view of all nodes and schedulers can operate in parallel, independently of each other. However, the quality of decisions based on partial data is generally lower compared to centralized schedulers, which can be optimal in certain settings
(
Tumanov et al. 2016
;
Gog et al. 2016
;
Newell et al. 2021
)
.

These decentralized schedulers depict active servers as bins and tasks as balls assigned to the queues of servers for execution. They usually assume that server workloads are known and updated over time, and the system is considered homogeneous in terms of task size and server capacity. Then, schedulers randomly select two servers to probe and choose the one with fewer pending tasks between the two to balance the load. Various load-balancing task schedulers have been proposed and implemented based on this set-up, such as Nginx
(
Garrett 2018
)
, Envoy
(
envoy [n. d.]
)
, Fingle
(
Anderson and Oanta [n. d.]
)
, YARP
(
mic [n. d.]
)
, and Prequal
(
Wydrowski et al. 2024
)
. The majority of these randomized distributed schedulers assume statelessness, requiring runtime probes for each decision and discarding previous results. Besides, these schedulers often assume homogeneous task requests, which can lead to imbalances in resource allocation, causing resource fragmentation and overallocation
(
Grandl et al. 2014
)
. This limitation affects performance gains from parallelism, as fewer tasks can be executed concurrently due to the servers’ limited resource capacity. Additionally, tasks may be blocked by preceding heavy tasks, a phenomenon known as head-of-line blocking
(
Scharf and Kiesel 2006
)
.

This paper introduces Dodoor, a system for efficient scheduling in heterogeneous cloud environments without runtime probing. Dodoor uses a setting with two variants for the balanced allocation problem (Section
2.1
) and adopts the
alignment score
technique from
(
Grandl et al. 2014
)
to tackle resource imbalance issues. Unlike continuous runtime probing, Dodoor maintains local views of server information, allowing timely scheduling. Server load information is updated by centralized data storage services for each batch of scheduling requests, without blocking ongoing scheduling actions. Furthermore, Dodoor leverages a new load score to enhance scheduling with diverse resource requirements, focusing on tasks’ dynamic resource needs. Dodoor is compatible with various hybrid or hierarchical architectures that combine centralized and decentralized schedulers
(
Delgado et al. 2015
;
Wang et al. 2019
)
, and is easy to integrate with other probing techniques
(
Delgado et al. 2016
;
Ousterhout et al. 2013
)
. Dodoor’s scheduling approach and design are further discussed in Sections
3
and
4
.

To validate Dodoor’s effectiveness, we conduct a comprehensive empirical study on a 101-node heterogeneous cluster. We evaluate our system using two distinct and demanding workloads: a trace of 4,000 virtual machine requests from the Azure Cloud dataset
(
Hadary et al. 2020
)
and a high-throughput benchmark of short-lived, heterogeneous serverless functions selected in
(
Kim and Lee 2019
)
. Results demonstrate that Dodoor significantly improves system efficiency by: 1) reducing the number of scheduling RPC messages by up to 66%; 2) boosting cluster throughput by up to 33.2%; 3) significantly cutting the average and P95 task makespan latencies (up to 12.1% and 24.6%); and 4) achieving the most balanced resource utilization across all tested schedulers. Section
6
presents Dodoor’s comprehensive evaluation.

In summary, this paper makes the following contributions:

•

We design and implement Dodoor, a novel decentralized scheduler that significantly reduces communication overhead by replacing per-request probing with a batched, centrally-pushed caching mechanism inspired by recent advances in the balls-into-bins model
(
Los and Sauerwald 2023a
;
Berenbrink et al. 2008
;
Berenbrink et al. 2012
)
.

•

We propose a new anti-affinity Resource-Load (RL) score that captures the relationship between heterogeneous task requirements, node capacities, and current server loads, leading to higher-quality placement decisions than traditional schedulers that rely solely on count of requests-in-flight (RIF).

•

We conduct a comprehensive and rigorous evaluation on a 101-node heterogeneous cluster using two distinct workloads: a real-world Azure VM trace and a high-throughput benchmark of short-lived serverless functions, each demonstrating significant performance improvement.

Background and Motivation

The background concepts that motivate and inform the design of our system are reviewed in this Section. Section
2.1
introduces the balls-into-bins problem, its mathematical model, and randomized solutions that are widely used for modeling load balancing problem in the cloud. Section
2.2

2.4

2.1.
Balls-into-bins Problem

The balls-into-bins problem, also known as balanced allocation, is a well-known model and widely used for load balancing
(
Anderson and Oanta [n. d.]
;
Garrett 2018
;
envoy [n. d.]
;
Ousterhout et al. 2013
;
Wydrowski et al. 2024
)
, hashing
(
Pagh and Rodler 2004
;
Kirsch and Mitzenmacher 2008
)
and network pruning
(
Elhoushi et al. 2021
;
Wang et al. 2025
;
Przewlocka-Rus et al. 2022
)
. It is defined as allocating
m
balls into
n
bins and it aims to minimize the gap between the maximum load and the average load per bin with high probability (often denoted as (w.h.p.)). In task scheduling scenarios involving load balancing, it is generally assumed that
m>>n
, where
m
can be represented as scheduled tasks count and
n
as the number of computing servers.

The simplest baseline is
single choice
where each ball is placed into a uniformly randomly selected bin. This strategy yields a (w.h.p.) gap of

\Theta\!\big(\sqrt{(m\log n)/n}\big)
above the average load, which can be significant when
m
is large
(
Mitzenmacher 2001
)
. A well-known improvement is
power-of-
d
choices
where for each ball,
d
bins are uniformly sampled at random, and among them the least-loaded bin is selected to place the ball.
This eliminates the dependence on
m
in the high-probability gap and yields a gap of

\Theta\!\big(\tfrac{\log\log n}{\log d}\big)
, a dramatic reduction compared to the single choice.
The common case
d=2
(
power-of-two
) is widely deployed in practice (e.g., Nginx
(
Garrett 2018
)
, Envoy
(
envoy [n. d.]
)
, Twitter
(
Anderson and Oanta [n. d.]
)
, Microsoft YARP
(
mic [n. d.]
)
, and YouTube Prequal
(
Wydrowski et al. 2024
)
).

Beyond the uniform setting, several variants have been studied.
In the
weighted
model, balls have weights and bin load is the sum of weights to reflect heterogeneous task demands
(
Talwar and Wieder 2007
;
Peres et al. 2010
;
Berenbrink et al. 2012
)
.
The
(1+\beta)
process mixes single choice and two choices (use two choices with probability
\beta
) and achieves a gap

\Theta\!\big(\tfrac{\log n}{\beta}\big)
in the weighted setting
(
Peres et al. 2010
)
.
Moreover, decisions do not need to have perfectly fresh information. In
stale
or
batched
models, loads are updated only periodically
(
Dahlin 1999
;
Mitzenmacher 2000
)
.
In the
b
-
batched
setting
(
Berenbrink et al. 2012
)
, where loads are updated once per batch of size
b
, the power-of-two process attains a tight (w.h.p.) gap of

\Theta\!\big(\tfrac{\log n}{\log\log n}\big)
when
b=n
, and a gap of
\Theta(b/n)
for larger batches
b=\Theta(n\log n)
.
Combining batching with weights,
(
Los and Sauerwald 2023a
)
shows that applying power-of-two directly in the weighted
b
-batched model preserves these bounds,
and that the
(1+\beta)
process further improves the gap to

O\!\big(\sqrt{(b/n)\,\log n}\big)
for
b\in[\,2n\log n,\,n^{3}\,]
when
\beta
is chosen on the order of
\sqrt{(n/b)\,\log n}
.

Besides the weight or batch variant setting, multidimensional balls-into-bins problem is also proposed and studied
(
Broder and Mitzenmacher 2005
;
Narang et al. 2011
)
, which allows balls and bins to have multiple attributes to be balanced together. The performance of both the power-of-two and
1+\beta
has been analyzed in
(
Broder and Mitzenmacher 2005
)
. This extension allows for the allocation of balls with a
d
-dimensional 0-1 weight vector and defines the dimensional load of bins as the maximum load across all dimensions. Furthermore, several variants of the traditional balls-into-bins setting have been studied such as, bins with different sampling rates
(
Los et al. 2023
)
, placement based on incomplete information
(
Los and Sauerwald 2022
)
, permitting dynamic deletion and reinsertion of balls by bins
(
Bansal and Kuszmaul 2022
)
, and enabling balls to be swapped and reallocated across bins
(
Becchetti et al. 2016
)
.

2.2.
Power-of-two in Modern Schedulers

Load balancing task schedulers in data centers can be categorized as centralized and decentralized. Centralized schedulers, such as
(
Isard et al. 2009
;
Gog et al. 2016
;
Verma et al. 2015
;
Tumanov et al. 2016
;
Cortez et al. 2017
;
Garefalakis et al. 2018
)
, are designed to maintain access to the status of servers and clusters and to make decisions on a global scale. This allows for flexibility in implementing either heuristic or optimal scheduling strategies tailored to various metrics and workloads. However, the centralized approach can hinder the scalability of scheduling, as all information from large-scale clusters must be stored, updated, and synchronized across schedulers and servers. This challenge is particularly pronounced for online services like search, ads, and video, which have strict latency requirements and demand high throughput, resulting in extremely high frequency of global data updates and read operations. Such scalability challenges are common in production systems. For instance, the default Kubernetes scheduler can experience significant overhead spikes in large-scale clusters (>5,000 nodes), and become the throughput bottleneck
(
K8S [n. d.]
)
.

Inspired by the balls-into-bins (also known as balanced allocation) problem, designing request scheduling in a decentralized manner using the power-of-two approach has been widely adopted. Many open-source load balancing systems, including Nginx
(
Garrett 2018
)
, Fingle
(
Anderson and Oanta [n. d.]
)
, and Envoy
(
envoy [n. d.]
)
, utilize the power-of-two for request scheduling, which randomly probes the number of pending requests on two servers and dispatches the next request to the server with fewer pending requests. Such schedulers that employ this random process can be categorized as randomized scheduler.

However, applying the balls-into-bins model for load balancing usually conceptualize each task as uniform balls, and servers with standard containers as bins. These assumptions fit popular distributed data processing frameworks, such as
(
Apache Software Foundation 2010
;
Zaharia et al. 2010
)
, which bundle server resources as slots and maintain separate task queues per server slot for later execution. However, modern data centers and today’s heterogeneous workloads exhibit significant variability in their server resource capacities and task demands. For instance, individual tasks could require multiple types of resources. Assigning slots with equivalent resources bundle can lead to server resource fragmentation and wastage as discussed in
(
Grandl et al. 2014
)
or negatively impact task parallelism and executions with head-of-line blocking
(
Scharf and Kiesel 2006
)
. In addition to resource capacity and demand, task execution time depends on hardware (e.g., CPU frequency) or resource constraints. Additionally, the implementations of power-of-two schedulers require probing the server status prior to scheduling, which introduces additional messages and communication overhead. Furthermore, race conditions among schedulers could occur if messages are lost, affecting the overall reliability of the system.

2.3.
Variants of Randomized Scheduler

The challenges of directly applying the balls-into-bins model for scheduling, as summarized above, have been identified in recent years, leading to the proposal of a set of variants of randomized schedulers as solutions.

The concept of late-binding was introduced by Sparrow
(
Ousterhout et al. 2013
)
in applying power-of-two algorithms to data center task scheduling. This technique has since been adopted by other decentralized scheduler prototypes, such as those in
(
Wang et al. 2019
;
Zhang et al. 2020
)
. The late-binding mechanism enhanced with proactive cancellation is illustrated in Figure
1(a)
. The process begins when the scheduler randomly selects two servers and places a task reservation on both. This contrasts with the standard power-of-two approach, which only places a reservation on the single least-loaded server. When one of the two reservations reaches the head of a server’s waiting queue, that server sends a confirmation message to the scheduler. The scheduler then confirms the task ready to be executed and proactively cancels the reservation on the other.

Late-binding can be viewed as a greedy algorithm to minimize makespan as it ultimately selects the first server with the least wait time for task execution. With late-binding, servers that are considered more imbalanced with poorer parallelism will automatically be bypassed, as their load is self-reduced through proactive cancellation. So, the head-of-line blocking caused by task and server heterogeneity can be mitigated. According to
(
Ousterhout et al. 2013
)
, even the application of late-binding alone can achieve near-optimal response times for short task placement compared to centralized schedulers. However, recent studies indicate that late-binding with proactive cancellation can significantly increase tail latency for highly variable workloads, such as those encountered in serverless environments
(
Kaffes et al. 2021
)
.

Furthermore, late-binding can add more communication overheads into the scheduling process due to the need to deliver confirmation and cancellation messages, as shown in Figure
1(a)
. Processing these additional messages increases the utilization of RPC clients, and can cause potential race conditions between servers and the schedulers, leading to duplication/omission of task executions.

To address this issue, Google recently proposed Prequal
(
Wydrowski et al. 2024
)
as the default scheduler for YouTube services. Prequal is designed to perform probing asynchronously to scheduling to avoid increased runtime overheads. After scheduling tasks, each Prequal scheduler sends probe messages to random servers for their status. These probes’ results are cached locally by the scheduler and are used for subsequent requests. This design ensures that probing does not delay scheduling. Additionally, Prequal introduces a new placement strategy called hot-cold lexicographic (HCL) that makes scheduling decisions based on the cached old probed server load information, the number of pending tasks, referred to as requests-in-flight (RIF) by Prequal, the estimated task latency, and the global RIF counts distribution.

Although Prequal’s designs effectively address problems from late-binding, they have inherent shortcomings which could hurt their performance. First, Prequal’s schedulers still need to probe servers for each query, which can introduce overheads and increase the scheduler’s workload. Further, probing messages are sent in a distributed manner, leading to duplicate probes from different schedulers for the same server. Additionally, since the freshness of probed results can affect scheduling performance, Prequal’s scheduler employs a heuristic maintenance strategy to delete probed results regularly for each scheduler. This approach heavily depends on human experience to tune related parameters. Further, it requires a high update frequency of this scheduler-local data, which can be shared across multiple scheduling threads, leading to potential race conditions and inconsistent scheduling outcomes. When there are insufficient local probed results, either due to over-deletion or scheduler’s failure recovery, tasks will be placed on random servers, potentially causing imbalances. Finally, Prequal’s reliance solely on server-side load signals is a significant limitation. By ignoring both the specific resource demands and execution duration differences of tasks and the actual capacities of servers, it fails to utilize all available information, which leads to a degradation in scheduling quality.

Furthermore, Microsoft’s YARP (Yet Another Reverse Proxy) load balancer also uses the power-of-two as its default scheduling strategy. In YARP, server status, including RIF, is periodically updated to YARP clients and utilized by their power-of-two load balancer. YARP’s fixed periodic asynchronous probing helps reduce the probing overhead similar to Prequal. However, it lacks flexibility and overlooks the relationship between probing frequency and the cluster QPS (referring to the request arrival rate). Unlike Prequal, which adjusts deletion and probe operations based on queries, YARP does not dynamically adapt to varying throughput levels. Additionally, periodic probing messages, which scale exponentially with the number of servers and clients, can lead to network bandwidth contention. Finally, like other standard implementations of randomized load balancers and schedulers, as in
(
Garrett 2018
;
Anderson and Oanta [n. d.]
)
, they solely rely on the RIF counts to balance load among servers with the same detrimental sequences discussed above.

In summary, the existing design for probe-based randomized schedulers reveals a trade-off. Strategies like late-binding (e.g., Sparrow) and runtime probing introduce communication overhead at the scheduler. In contrast, asynchronous probing schedulers (e.g., YARP, Prequal) avoid this dispatch delay but introduce the secondary challenge of managing the probed results effectively. Besides, for both variants, the heterogeneity of task resources, durations, and server capacities is ignored in scheduling. On the other hand, the variants of the balls-into-bins model presented in Section
2.1
, which have been largely overlooked in practical systems, can offer promising solutions and directions for better modeling practical load balancing problems.

2.4.
Scope and Proposal

Motivated by these observations, we present
Dodoor
, a distributed scheduler that employs the novel theoretical variants of power-of-two.
Dodoor
targets low-latency, high-throughput scheduling in clusters with multi-dimensional resource heterogeneity (CPU, memory, duration) of task demand and node capacity, especially for short-to-medium-lived tasks and bursty arrivals. The core idea is to replace per-request runtime probing with a centrally pushed, locally cached view of node state and to make lightweight selections based on the power-of-two choice. We further introduce an anti-affinity resource-load (RL) score that accounts for task demand, duration, and node capacity. This design reduces scheduling RPCs and decision latency while preserving balance and placement quality under stale—yet regularly refreshed—cluster views.

3.1.
System Model

We study the task-level scheduling problem. It is defined as placing a set of computing tasks
t_{i},i\in\{0...m\}
to servers
s_{j},j\in\{0...n\}
in the data center, where
m
and
n
refer to the count of tasks and servers. Each task has varying CPU and memory requirement. The model can be expanded to include additional resources such as disk and GPU, which we leave for future exploration.

Each task,
t_{i}
, has resource requirements denoted as a vector
\vec{r_{i}}=[CPU,memory]
. Its execution times on different servers are denoted by a duration vector as
\vec{d_{i}}==[d_{i1},d_{i2},d_{i3},...,d_{in}]

The maximal capacity of resources for server
s_{j}
is given by the capacity vector
\vec{C_{j}}=[CPU,memory]
.
s_{j}
also maintains a single tasks queue with all task in flight denoted by
\vec{Q_{j}}=[t_{1},t_{2},t_{3},...,t_{k}]
, where
k_{j}
indicates its number of uncompleted tasks (also noted as requests-in-flight, RIF in
(
Wydrowski et al. 2024
)
). The total requested resources of the server
s_{j}
is denoted as its resource load vector
\vec{L_{j}}=\sum_{i}r_{i}
. The server also tracks the total estimated duration of these tasks, which gets marked as
D_{j}=\sum_{i=1}^{k_{j}}d_{ij}
, where
d_{ij}
stands for estimated duration for
t_{i}
over
s_{j}
.

In addition, the cluster will keep track of the total number of scheduling decisions
p

b

p\equiv((p+1)\pmod{b})
after each scheduling.

As discussed in Section
2
, Dodoor’s scheduling is based on the batched balls-into-bins model studied in
(
Los and Sauerwald 2023a
)
and employs the power-of-two discussed in Section
2.1
. We choose it over the
(1+\beta)
approach in order to avoid tuning
\beta
to decide 1 or 2 choices for each placement. Unless specified otherwise, the batch size
b
defaults to half the number of available nodes to balance the update frequencies and freshness of the scheduler’s local load cache. The Dodoor scheduler is designed on two core principles: a)
Asynchronous Load Updates
: assigns tasks without real-time probing, instead it updates local load cache only once per batch; b)
Weighted Task Accommodation
: schedule tasks that have varying execution duration or resource requirements.

Each scheduler keeps its local servers’ load cache map
L
(for resource load vector) and
D
(total duration of uncompleted tasks). Upon receiving a task, the scheduler randomly selects two candidate servers (noted as A and B) and then calculates the
loadScore
, which is used for evaluating the anti-affinity between the two servers and
t_{i}
, and finally selects the candidate with lower
loadScore
.

We adopt the alignment score from Tetris
(
Grandl et al. 2014
)
to prioritize assigning multi-dimensional server resources to scheduled tasks, rather than using task queue length to measure workload. The alignment scores in Tetris are computed by multiplying the resource vector of the scheduled task with the vectors of the available capacity of server candidates. However, unlike Tetris, which schedules tasks to free servers only for immediate execution, Dodoor is designed to allow tasks to "early-bind" to servers, enabling the oversubscription of requested resources. Therefore, in contrast to the alignment score, which indicates the affinity between tasks and servers, we design a new score, resource load, which represents anti-affinity of resources demands and capacities. The resource load score is defined as the inner product of the servers’ resource load vector
L_{j}
and the task requesting resource vector
r_{i}
over the
L2
norm of the server’s capacity, expressed in Equation
1
and implemented as the
RL
function in Algorithm
1
, for given task
t_{i}
over server
s_{j}
with
k
resource types:

(1)

RL(\vec{r_{i}},\vec{L_{j}},\vec{C_{j}})=\frac{\vec{r_{i}}^{T}\cdot\vec{L_{j}}}{\sum_{k}C_{jk}^{2}}

In addition to resource load, the Dodoor scheduler leverages timing information to estimate a task’s makespan, a method also used by Prequal. Differing from Prequal which only counts the total duration of servers’ RIF, Dodoor also considers potential heterogeneity of scheduled task’s duration across servers. It uses a refined total duration calculated as the sum of the server’s RIF duration,
D_{j}
, and the targeted request duration,
d_{ij}
. Thus, the final load scores for candidate server
s_{j}
with another candidate server
s_{p}
for the scheduled task
t_{i}
can be viewed as a normalized linear combination of resource load scores and total duration, incorporating an optional tunable weight
\alpha
for the total duration. This is defined by the
loadScore
function:

\text{loadScore}_{ij}=\frac{RL_{j}}{RL_{j}+RL_{p}}\cdot(1-\alpha)+\alpha\cdot\frac{D_{j}+d_{ij}}{D_{j}+d_{ij}+D_{p}+d_{ip}}

As discussed before, load scores quantify the anti-affinity between a task and the server candidate. The server with the lowest load score is selected. The parameter
\alpha
is set to 0.5 by default to evenly balance the duration of scheduled tasks and the overall resource balance in the cluster, which can potentially cause delays for subsequent tasks.

Similarly to Kubernetes
(

Data Store

Similar to ETCD
(
kubernetes 2016a
)
, the key-value store for Kubernetes cluster data, Dodoor employs a lightweight global datastore service to store and manage shareable load information. It is a lightweight, write-dominated aggregator that prioritizes low overhead over strong consistency, used to centrally manage shareable load information. The system monitors the number of scheduling decisions, denoted as
p
in Section
3.1
, and collects load signal changes from servers and schedulers. Once a batch of size
b
is complete, the system proactively pushes the entire load vector to the scheduler. Since schedulers never query the store on the hot path, this push-based design eliminates active pulling and probe maintenance overheads required by systems such as Prequal and YARP (as discussed in Section
2
).

The service can be built atop a high-performance Key-value layer (e.g., RocksDB
(
Dong et al. 2021
)
). Internally, it maintains a table mapping each node address to its load vector
\vec{L}
and total duration of uncompleted task
D
, and exposes a minimal API for updating. Servers publish their latest view via
overrideNodeState
, which replaces the stored vector, while schedulers report incremental effects via
addNewLoad
. The store combines these streams by overriding with the most recent server view and applying scheduler deltas; the count of
addNewLoad
calls serves as the ground truth for
p
and drives the batch trigger at
b
requests. Unlike schedulers with a global view of cluster status
(
Schwarzkopf et al. 2013
;
Burns et al. 2016
;
Boutin et al. 2014
)
, the data store is strictly
push-only
and batched: schedulers consume best-effort snapshots that may be stale. Consequently, there are no cross-services consistency or synchronization requirements between schedulers and the data store. This significantly simplifies scaling, eases fail-over, and avoids making the store a read bottleneck, while preserving the load-awareness needed for decentralized placement.

Scheduler

The main process of the scheduler service is designed to execute the scheduling algorithms outlined in Algorithm
1
and passively waits for the updates of load caches from data store for each
b
requests in the clusters. It also monitors scheduling decisions and, after reaching the mini-batch size of schedules, sends the newly added loads for the scheduled servers to the data store using the
addNewLoad
API. This update of load deltas is beneficial because servers only send updates once tasks are completed. If all tasks executed on the server are lengthy, the cached load in the data store may become outdated, especially if many new requests are sent before the first task finishes. The mini-batch size for calling
addNewLoad
is set no larger than
b/(\text{num\_scheduler})*2
to ensure that every scheduler’s update is included in the next batch’s results.

4.2.
Server Execution

Each server instance maintains a queue of pending tasks, allowing the scheduler to early bind them before execution. This approach is similar to slot-based distributed frameworks like Hadoop
(
Apache Software Foundation 2010
)
, but it contrasts with containerized frameworks such as Kubernetes
(
kubernetes 2016b
)
, which halt the scheduling process if no suitable nodes are available.

Tasks are executed in a First-Come, First-Served (FCFS) order. However, multiple tasks can run concurrently on a single server, up to the number of CPU cores and limited by the hardware resources available for task execution. While this FCFS model can lead to head-of-line blocking for subsequent tasks, it also prevents starvation and avoids additional delays for large tasks at the front of the queue.

Furthermore, Dodoor is designed to natively support hierarchical mini-clusters as outlined in
(
Wang et al. 2019
)
. Due to the lack of dependencies between schedulers and servers, each server can be mapped to different schedulers and data stores within its own mini-cluster. For enhanced flexibility and scalability, an operator can divide the entire system into multiple mini-clusters, each with a unique configuration. This can improve the overall reliability of the cluster, an area we have left for future exploration.

4.3.
Cluster Operation and Reliability

The system is designed to enable efficient operation and seamless scaling, with a robust failure recovery mechanism. To scale the cluster up or down by adding or removing servers and schedulers, operators should first use the register/unregister APIs in Section
4.1
to update the load vectors in centralized data services. These operations can be encapsulated into command-line tools for easy operation, similar to Kubectl in Kubernetes. After updating server information, the data services can execute the
updateNodeState
operation to provide the latest information to the schedulers, reset current batches, and immediately start or stop sending requests to newly added or removed schedulers. This operation also allows schedulers to start or stop assigning tasks to new or removed servers if they have been added or removed from the schedulers’ load maps
L
.

We believe this framework can be considered more reliable than other centralized or decentralized scheduling systems. No critical system issue can be triggered by a single instance failure because all APIs are designed asynchronously with error handling. Additionally, Dodoor schedulers do not manipulate or maintain a local view of centralized data, which mitigates the risk of scheduler failure. Provisioning or recovering new schedulers is straightforward, as they can be registered with the data store without incurring performance loss. In contrast, systems like Sparrow, Prequal, and YARP maintain scheduler-local load information, making recovery more challenging. For instance, Prequal may experience cold start issues because it relies on scheduler-local probe results for scheduling. If the probed results disappear due to scheduler service shutdown, purely random selection may occur. Also, a failure in the Sparrow scheduler can lead to tasks being rejected or duplicated for execution, as servers require extra confirmation and active cancellation from the scheduler based on its cached enqueue decisions, as claimed in the special case in
(
Ousterhout et al. 2013
)
.

We assume the data store can be built atop a well-designed storage layer that provides robust support for failure handling, such as RocksDB
(
Dong et al. 2021
)
. For even greater availability, the data store itself can be replicated similarly to
(
Hunt et al. 2010
;
kubernetes 2016a
;
DeCandia et al. 2007
)
.

However, a key advantage of Dodoor’s architecture is that it does not require a complex, highly-available central data plane to function effectively. Failures in the data store service will not cause a catastrophic system-wide crash, unlike centralized schedulers that depend on a constantly accessible global store. If the data store becomes temporarily unavailable, schedulers continue to operate using their last-known cached view of the cluster state. The quality of placement decisions may gradually degrade as this information becomes stale, but the system remains fully operational. Recovery is simple and automatic. Once the data store restarts, it will naturally rebuild its load tables from the ongoing signals published by servers and schedulers. As soon as the next batch of scheduling decisions is completed, the store will push a fresh, up-to-date snapshot to all schedulers, immediately restoring the quality of placement decisions with no negative long-term impact. This graceful degradation and seamless recovery highlight the operational simplicity and resilience of our push-based design.

Additionally, failure handling for the servers is expected to be similar to that of other distributed frameworks, allowing for the re-enqueuing and execution of unfinished tasks if a server goes down and detected. An interesting observation is that even if a server is unexpectedly terminated without notifying the schedulers and data store, it can automatically be soft-pinned out. This occurs because the scheduler’s load at the data store can only be incremented by the scheduler without deletion by server overriding, which means the likelihood of that server being selected decreases over time.

(

To improve the reproducibility of randomized schedulers, we use the task ID as the seed for random number generation during placement and send the tasks across schedulers following a Poisson distribution with varying QPS (arrival rates per second)

•

Our experiments are conducted on a testbed of 101 physical hosts built on the CloudLab platform

Each scheduler and data store was configured to process 8 RPC messages in parallel, allowing for up to 40 concurrent scheduling decisions. We also limit each scheduler to a maximum of 16 RPC clients for each server port. Before running each experiment, 100 short standard requests are sent as a warm-up to initialize these services’ RPC clients. The formal experiments commence after all the warm-up requests are completed.

A primary motivation for our work is to address hardware heterogeneity. Therefore, unlike existing studies that often rely on homogeneous machines, our cluster was intentionally heterogeneous. It consists of four different node types from CloudLab’s Utah site, with the ratio of nodes selected to mirror their natural distribution in the whole site. These nodes cover a broad range of capacities, and their detailed specifications are provided in Table

We conduct experiments with varying QPS and collect the following metrics from scheduler and node side: 1) RPC counts processed by all schedulers, which directly reflects the workload of schedulers; 2) cluster throughput, calculated by dividing the experiment’s wall time by the number of processed requests; 3) the mean and p95 tasks’ end-to-end (e2e) makespan latency; 4) the mean and p95 scheduling latency, which represents the overhead caused by the scheduler, calculated as the difference between the task makespan and the tracked lifetime on the server side; 5) server’s resource utilization, as each server records the utilization of CPU and memory per 10 seconds.

Figure

Firstly, Dodoor significantly reduces RPC messages by 55% and 66% compared to the PoT and Prequal schedulers, respectively, while incurring only a 33% overhead for local caching updates. As discussed in Section
2.2
, PoT requires two additional messages for runtime probing before scheduling. In contrast, Prequal needs to send 3 asynchronous post-scheduling messages to update each scheduler’s local cache, which can lead to redundant probing of the same node by different schedulers. In contrast, Dodoor utilizes a data store service to maintain servers’ states through passive updates from both schedulers and servers. It then actively pushes these updates to schedulers for each batch to reduce the workload of schedulers by eliminating unnecessary duplicate probing.

Secondly, we observe a significant improvement in the quality of scheduling decisions, a benefit derived from the new scheduling policy that accounts for the heterogeneity of server capacities and task demands. As shown in Figure
4

Even at low QPS, Dodoor demonstrates a latency reduction of 0.5% and 1.3% by decreasing task waiting times. This ultimately leads to a throughput improvement ranging from 5.9% to 33.2%, achieved by reducing the total experiment wall time. Furthermore, we note that while Prequal consistently outperforms PoT in tail latency—demonstrating the advantages of HCL—when QPS > 1, its average latency and overall throughput do not show a significant advantage in these experiments.

While Prequal can generate a higher RPC message workload and occasionally exhibits lower throughput, our experiments demonstrate that its performance involves significant trade-offs aimed at reducing scheduling overhead through asynchronous probing and local caching, which are its primary design objectives. Prequal consistently achieves the lowest average scheduling latency. However, as QPS increases, Dodoor’s mean scheduling overhead decreases, which due to a reduced risk of blocking during cache updates, allowing it to eventually outperform the random scheduler. Although the random scheduler theoretically incurs no processing overhead, it can still suffer from imbalanced placements that lead to scheduling delays due to contention among RPC clients targeting the same server. Regarding the P95 scheduling overhead, we observe considerable variance among the schedulers, with PoT consistently underperforming compared to the others, primarily due to the additional runtime probing.

Related Work

Load Balancing Models

The balls-into-bins problem, also known as balanced allocation (described in Section
2.1
), is a foundational mathematical model for load balancing. Seminal work between 1994 and 1996 introduced and extensively analyzed randomized solutions to this problem. This pioneering research, which received the ACM Theory and Practice Award in 2020, has inspired numerous practical applications. These include task scheduling in data centers
(
Ousterhout et al. 2013
;
Anderson and Oanta [n. d.]
;
envoy [n. d.]
;
Garrett 2018
;
Wydrowski et al. 2024
)
, hash table implementations
(
Pagh and Rodler 2004
;
Kirsch and Mitzenmacher 2008
)
, low-congestion circuit routing
(
Cole et al. 1998
)
, and more recently, neural network pruning and post-training quantization
(
Elhoushi et al. 2021
;
Wang et al. 2025
;
Przewlocka-Rus et al. 2022
)
. Subsequent research has extended the basic model, providing theoretical analyses for more complex scenarios. These extensions address dynamic environments with ball or bin insertions and deletions
(
Aamand et al. 2021
;
Berenbrink et al. 2016
)
, concurrent batched placements
(
Berenbrink et al. 2012
)
, self-stabilizing re-allocation
(
Los and Sauerwald 2023c
;
Becchetti et al. 2016
)
, and decision-making with delayed (
\tau
) steps
(
Los and Sauerwald 2023b
)
or incomplete information
(
Los and Sauerwald 2022
)
. Further variations account for heterogeneity, such as balls with varying weights
(
Berenbrink et al. 2008
;
Talwar and Wieder 2007
;
Berenbrink et al. 2012
)
, multiple dimensions
(
Broder and Mitzenmacher 2005
;
Narang et al. 2011
)
, and biased bin sampling for varied capacities
(
Wieder 2007
;
Los et al. 2023
)
. Dodoor can be conceptualized as an application that synthesizes several of these variants. It operates in a batched setting
(
Berenbrink et al. 2012
)
, handles balls with dynamic weights (represented by the load score in Algorithm
1
), and permits the removal of tasks (balls). Dodoor employs the power-of-two approach, which has been proven to maintain controlled load balance under such batched conditions
(
Los and Sauerwald 2023a
)
.

Cluster Scheduling

Task scheduling in large-scale data centers is a well-studied field, driven by the industrial demand to maximize resource utilization and minimize task latency. Some applications favored centralized, monolithic schedulers that model scheduling as a deterministic optimization problem
(
Isard et al. 2009
;
Gog et al. 2016
;
Tumanov et al. 2016
;
Narayanan et al. 2021
)
. While these systems can produce high-quality placement decisions, they often incur significant overhead, which limits their scalability. To address this, shared-state architectures were introduced
(
Schwarzkopf et al. 2013
;
Boutin et al. 2014
;
kubernetes 2016b
;
Vijayakumar et al. 2024
)
, improving scalability by replicating the scheduler. However, the need of runtime synchronization either with the centralized repository of cluster state or other scheduler replications can create a performance bottleneck. This limitation led to the development of fully distributed schedulers based on the balls-into-bins model. Sparrow applied the power-of-two paradigm with late-binding to reduce scheduling overhead (see Section
2.2
). Other works have proposed hybrid schedulers that combine centralized and distributed strategies partially or use hierarchical layers to manage diverse workloads
(
Delgado et al. 2015
;
Delgado et al. 2016
;
Karanasos et al. 2015
;
Wang et al. 2019
)
. While Dodoor utilizes a centralized data store to cache cluster status, it fundamentally operates as a distributed scheduler. Unlike shared-state schedulers, Dodoor’s schedulers run independently without requiring synchronization or a consistent global view for every decision. Instead, they are stateless and simply receive passive, batched updates. The data store itself does not need to provide strong consistency guarantees; its only role is to aggregate information and push updates once per batch. This design imposes low performance requirements on the central component, making it easy to scale and maintain, and clearly distinguishes Dodoor from the shared-state schedulers.

Despite the theoretical benefits of randomization, applying these schedulers in production has revealed practical challenges. Prequal
(
Wydrowski et al. 2024
)
noted that the runtime probing required by power-of-two approaches can still introduce significant overhead, and argued that Sparrow’s use of reservations and late-binding "would incur undesirable overheads in our environment." The primary motivation for our system, Dodoor, is similar to that of Prequal: to design an efficient, low-overhead distributed scheduler without probing overhead. During development, our own implementation of Sparrow’s confirmation and cancellation logic was prone to race conditions and deadlocks, causing duplicate task executions. Given these implementation challenges, we exclude it from our baseline comparisons. Instead, we evaluate Dodoor against Prequal and a standard power-of-two implementation, as both are more representative of approaches used in production systems. We believe this comparison more effectively demonstrates the impact of our work.

8.
Conclusion

This paper introduced Dodoor, a highly efficient, randomized decentralized scheduler designed to tackle the challenges of task placement in modern, heterogeneous data centers. We identified that existing schedulers often suffer from high communication overhead and make suboptimal decisions by ignoring the rich heterogeneity of both tasks and cluster resources. Dodoor addresses these issues through two key innovations: a batched, centrally-pushed caching mechanism that eliminates redundant per-request probing, and a novel anti-affinity score that enables smarter placement decisions. Our comprehensive evaluation on a 101-node physical, heterogeneous cluster demonstrated the significant benefits of Dodoor’s design. Dodoor paves the way for applying theoretical balls-into-bins insights—batched choices, weighted loads, and stale views—to a practical scheduler design.
