Assessing the environmental impacts of upcycling recycled aggregates into concrete with consideration of resource flows and substitution effects
Authors: Jeonghyun Kim, Hongseok Yang, Namho Kim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study evaluated the properties of concrete incorporating repeatedly recycled aggregates through laboratory experiments. In parallel, it quantitatively analyzed the environmental and economic impacts of repeated recycling of waste concrete, taking into account the policy and infrastructural context of South Korea, where landfilling of construction and demolition waste is virtually prohibited and recycling is mandatory. The analysis considered three recycling scenarios. In the first scenario, waste concrete was recycled as road base material through all three cycles. In the second scenario, it was recycled once as concrete aggregate and once as road base material. In the third scenario, it was recycled twice as concrete aggregate. Environmental impacts were assessed using five indicators, including global warming potential, abiotic depletion potential, ozone depletion, acidification potential and eutrophication potential. Economic feasibility was evaluated based on total material costs. The experimental results showed that concrete containing recycled aggregates maintained similar slump and compressive strength compared to natural aggregate concrete across the two cycles of recycling. However, repeated recycling as concrete aggregate led to increased environmental burdens and higher total costs. This outcome was mainly attributed to the lower recovery rate of recycled coarse aggregate for concrete, which resulted in a larger volume of surplus recycled fine aggregate that required additional processing. Among the three scenarios, the first scenario involving only road base reuse exhibited the most favorable performance in both environmental and economic aspects.Keywords: Circular construction, Recycling optimization, Recycled concrete aggregate, Concrete waste, Closed-loop recyclingSubject terms: Engineering, Environmental sciences, Environmental social sciences

Introduction

The construction industry is one of the main contributors to global resource consumption and greenhouse gas emissions. To achieve the sustainable development goals (SDGs), various technological and institutional approaches are required to reduce its environmental impacts. Among construction materials, concrete is the most widely used in the world. Due to the large consumption of cement, water, and aggregates, and the high energy demand during production, concrete contributes significantly to carbon emissions and environmental burdens. In particular, natural aggregates (NA), an essential component of concrete, are being increasingly depleted. This depletion has led to various environmental issues, such as ecosystem destruction, water pollution, and dust generation1. Since the 1990s, South Korea has faced challenges related to the imbalance in NA supply and environmental degradation. In response, the government has enacted the Aggregate Extraction Act2, the Act on the Promotion of Saving and Recycling of Resources3, and later the Act on the Promotion of Recycling of Construction Waste4. These regulations have strengthened restrictions on NA extraction and made the recycling of construction and demolition (C&D) waste mandatory. As a result, the use of recycled aggregates (RA) has increased, and their use is legally required in certain large-scale construction projects.

Within this regulatory context, many studies have investigated the environmental and economic performance of replacing NA with RA. However, the literature presents mixed outcomes. For example, one study found that producing RA from C&D waste results in approximately 50% lower environmental impacts compared to producing NA from crushed stone5. In contrast, another study reported that while RA significantly reduces abiotic depletion potential (ADP), their global warming potential (GWP) can be up to twice as high6, which has been attributed to the more complex and energy-intensive RA production process. The use of RA in concrete also shows ambiguous environmental and economic advantages. Some studies have found that recycled aggregate concrete (RAC) does not offer clear environmental benefits compared to natural aggregate concrete (NAC), nor does it outperform NAC in terms of material performance or production cost7–10. In contrast, Martínez-Lage et al.11 reported that using 100% RA slightly increases greenhouse gas emissions and eutrophication but significantly reduces waste generation and the depletion of non-renewable resources. These conflicting findings stem from different assumptions and system boundaries applied in life cycle assessments (LCA). If indirect environmental effects such as avoided landfill use, emissions from landfill operation, or CO₂ uptake by waste concrete are not considered, the environmental benefits of RA may be underestimated12.

Among various influencing factors, transport distance is one of the most critical determinants of the environmental and economic performance of RA and RAC. The location of recycling facilities and the level of local infrastructure also play important roles. A study on the Galicia region in Spain estimated that the use of 100% RA increased costs by around 11% due to insufficient local recycling facilities and an inefficient logistics system 11. Another study showed that when the transport distance of NA exceeds 250 km, RA becomes environmentally advantageous. However, when the distance is less than 22 km, NA may have a lower environmental impact13. These findings support the view that RA is not always environmentally superior, and that quantitative assessments of their feasibility must incorporate local infrastructure conditions. In South Korea, with its small land area and well-established recycling infrastructure, studies that assume long transport distances and low recycling rates may not accurately reflect the actual resource circulation system. Considering the specific conditions in Korea, existing studies on the environmental performance of concrete show several notable limitations. First, some studies assume that waste concrete is landfilled14, even though the recycling rate for waste concrete in Korea has exceeded 99.9% from 2006 to 2023, and the landfilling rate is virtually negligible. This assumption fails to reflect the Korean resource circulation system and may compromise the validity of the analysis. Second, most existing studies focus on a simplified process in which concrete is used once, then demolished, and subsequently either recycled or landfilled. As a result, they overlook actual material flows at construction sites, such as the recovery rate of recycled coarse aggregates (RCA), the applicability of recycled fine aggregates (RFA), and cases in which the use of RCA in concrete leads to the use of NA in backfill or road base layers. Given that resource recovery from waste concrete is now standard practice and most waste concrete is recycled, comparing the environmental performance of RA and NA based on unrealistic landfilling scenarios is disconnected from current realities. Instead, it is more appropriate to focus on how RCA can be effectively utilized.

Therefore, this study aims to quantitatively analyze the environmental and economic performance of concrete recycling under the actual conditions in South Korea and to propose effective resource circulation scenarios. To this end, three recycling scenarios were developed based on the utilization pathways of waste concrete. Each scenario considers material flow, the generation of recycling by-products, and potential storage, and integrates avoided impacts into both environmental and economic assessments.

Methodology

The LCA methodology used in this study follows the ISO 14040 and ISO 14044, and consists of four main phases: goal and scope definition, life cycle inventory analysis (LCI), life cycle impact assessment and interpretation of results. To ensure a fair comparison of the environmental impacts of concretes with different recycling cycles, it is necessary that each concrete mix not only meets the quality standards required for practical use in construction but also exhibits comparable performance. Therefore, prior to the LCA analysis, laboratory mix experiments were conducted to ensure that concretes made with aggregates from different recycling cycles achieved similar performance.

Materials and methods

According to national standards, KS F252715, in South Korea, RCA for concrete must meet a maximum water absorption of 3.0% and a minimum oven-dry density of 2.5 g/cm3, which are equivalent to the criteria for natural coarse aggregate (NCA). RCA that satisfies these criteria is commercially produced through multiple crushing and screening processes16. However, twice-recycled aggregate is generally not available from commercial facilities, primarily due to practical constraints. Therefore, in this study, NCA was first used to cast NAC under laboratory conditions, which was then crushed and acid-treated to produce RCA that meets industrial specifications after one recycling cycle (RCA1) and after two recycling cycles (RCA2). Goals and scope definition

The objective of this study is to find an efficient approach to recycling waste concrete in terms of environmental and economic performance. To achieve this, three scenarios were developed in which waste concrete undergoes up to three successive recycling cycles using different strategies. In the first scenario (S1), all waste concrete is recycled exclusively as road base material. In the second scenario (S2), the concrete is recycled once as aggregate for concrete and then reused as road base material. The third scenario (S3) involves recycling the waste concrete twice as aggregate for concrete. The functional unit was defined as the production of 1 m3 of concrete, along with the associated resource flows, including recycling processes, storage and substitution by road base applications.

As noted in the introduction, landfilling of waste concrete is virtually not practiced under current conditions in South Korea. Therefore, this study assumes that all waste concrete is fully converted into RA—namely, RCA and RFA. If the RCA is not used in concrete, an equivalent mass is assumed to be utilized as base material for roads. This assumption was intended to more realistically reflect the effects of resource substitution and circular resource flows on environmental performance. Based on these conditions, the system boundary of this study includes the following processes: the production of raw materials used for concrete and road base layers, including natural coarse and fine aggregates (NCA, sand), RAs (RCA1, RCA2, RFA), water, cement, and plasticizer. Transportation of materials was excluded from the system boundary, given the negligible environmental impact expected under high urban density and compact land area of South Korea, as well as the difficulty of accurate quantification. Previous studies indicate that the average transport distance from quarries does not exceed 10 km19, and the distance from demolition sites to recycling facilities typically ranges from 30 to 40 km20,21. These distances are considered to have a minimal contribution to the overall environmental impact.

According to Korean national statistics, approximately 80% of RA is used as backfilling or road base materials, whereas less than 10% is applied in concrete or concrete products16. Thus, S1 can be regarded as the most typical and frequent recycling pathway in the Korean context. More recently, a regulation was introduced in Korea that provides floor area ratio incentives for buildings incorporating RAC22. This can be interpreted as a policy initiative by the government to actively encourage the use of RCA in concrete, which in turn provides a practical basis for S2 and S3 where RCA is incorporated into concrete. Overall, the three scenarios presented in this study can be considered to adequately reflect current construction practices in Korea and serve as a suitable framework for comparing and evaluating the potential impacts of different recycling strategies.

Results

Experimental work

The slump and compressive strength test results for NAC, RAC1, and RAC2 are shown in Table 5. All concrete mixes exhibited slump values between 120 and 150 mm, indicating sufficient workability for placement. The 28-day compressive strengths ranged from 32.8 to 33.7 MPa, showing nearly equivalent performance across the mixes. According to previous studies, RAC generally shows reduced mechanical properties compared to NAC, primarily due to the presence of attached mortar on RCAs28,29. This reduction becomes more pronounced with an increasing number of recycling cycles30. To mitigate such effects and maintain performance comparable to NAC, modifications such as increasing the dosage of superplasticizer or adjusting the cement content are often necessary31,32. In the present study, however, no significant degradation was observed in RAC1 or RAC2 compared to NAC. This is attributed to the use of high-quality RCA that meets the KS F2527 standard, which imposes quality requirements equivalent to those for NCA. These findings are consistent with other domestic studies utilizing RCA that complies with the KS F2527 criteria33,34.

Environmental impacts

Figure 2 illustrates the total environmental impacts of each scenario. The black numbers inside or next to the bars indicate the percentage share of each process within the scenario. For example, in the GWP of S1, concrete production accounts for approximately 83%. The red numbers above the bars in S2 and S3 represent the percentage change in total impact compared to S1, indicating that the GWP in S2 and S3 increased by approximately 3.3% and 6.6%, respectively.

Environmental impacts for each scenario: (a) global warming potential; (b) abiotic depletion potential; (c) ozone depletion; (d) acidification potential; (e) eutrophication potential.

Across all impact categories (GWP, ADP, ODP, AP and EP), S1 exhibits the lowest overall environmental burden, while S3 shows the highest. Total GWP values were 1356.7 kg CO₂-eq for S1, 1402.1 kg CO₂-eq for S2 and 1446.5 kg CO₂-eq for S3, showing a progressive increase. Similar trends were observed for ADP, OD, AP, EP. When broken down by application, concrete production, road base, and storage, the GWP associated with concrete production in S2 and S3 was 2.8% and 5.5% higher, respectively, than in S1. This same trend is reflected in the other impact categories. When RCA was recycled once (S2) and twice (S3) in concrete, the corresponding increases were: ADP by 7.3% and 14.5%, ODP by 50.6% and 101.2%, AP by 9.3% and 18.5%, and EP by 9.6% and 19.1%. While concrete production remained the primary contributor to GWP (over 80% in all scenarios), the environmental burden associated with producing RA (i.e. RCA and RFA), categorized under the storage, contributed more significantly to ADP, ODP, AP and EP. The LCI data indicate that the crushing and screening processes required to produce RA from waste concrete are more energy-intensive and complex than the extraction of NA (NCA and sand). Even after accounting for the avoided impacts of substituting NA with RA, a significant environmental burden remains within the overall system. These findings suggest that repeatedly producing high-quality RCA-c for use in next-generation concrete results in greater environmental impacts from processing, which outweigh the benefits of resource recycling. Therefore, within the boundaries of this study, recycling waste concrete for road base applications and using NCA for concrete production appears to be a more environmentally sustainable approach.

Economic assessment

The cost analysis is presented in Fig. 3a–c. The total cost increases progressively across the scenarios, from KRW 259,393 in S1 to KRW 263,603 in S2 and KRW 267,624 in S3, indicating that repeated recycling of waste concrete as RCA-c leads to a gradual rise in overall costs.

Cost breakdown by process for each scenario: (a) Concrete production; (b) Road base layer; (c) Stored materials (RCA, RFA).

The concrete production accounts for approximately 93–97% of the total cost in all scenarios, largely due to the use of high-cost materials such as cement, sand, and plasticizer. In scenarios S2 and S3, the use of RCA-c, which is less expensive than NCA, led to a marginal cost reduction of about 0.5% per scenario. Costs associated with the road base application increased in proportion to the number of recycling cycles. The cost rose from approximately KRW 7839 in S1 to KRW 11,507 in S2 and KRW 15,176 in S3, representing increases of 46.8% and 93.6%, respectively. This rise is attributed to the substitution of RCA with higher-cost NCA in the road base layer, as RCA was utilized to concrete production. The storage category reflects the economic impact of surplus RA not used in concrete or road base applications. In S1, 435 kg of RCA and 960 kg of RFA remained after road base use, yielding a cost offset of –KRW 507 due to their potential to replace NCA and sand. In contrast, storage-related costs in S2 and S3 rose to KRW 1190 (+ 334%) and KRW 2886 (+ 669%), respectively. This increase is due to the lower recovery rate of RCA-c compared to RCA-r, which reduces its effectiveness in substituting for NCA. Moreover, the increased production of RFA, whose unit cost is higher than that of sand, contributed to the overall rise in system costs. These results suggest that while the use of RCA-c offered marginal cost savings in the concrete production, no significant economic advantage was observed at the system level. Although RCA-r and RCA-c can effectively replace NCA as cost-efficient alternatives, RFA was an important factor of increased total cost, due to its higher production cost. Particularly in S2 and S3, the combination of low RCA-c recovery rates and increased RFA generation led to substantial increases in storage-related costs compared to S1. These findings suggest the significance of RCA quality, recovery efficiency, and allocation strategy in determining the economic feasibility of recycling systems. Reducing RFA generation and increasing the recovery of high-quality RCA may be worth considering in the development of future recycling strategies.

Discussion

The experimental results of this study demonstrate that all concrete samples achieved sufficient workability and met the target compressive strength. In particular, no reduction in the fresh and hardened properties was observed with increased recycling cycles. Unlike the trends reported in previous studies, this suggests that when high-quality RCA is used, the technical performance of concrete can be reliably maintained even with repeated recycling of waste concrete as RCA-c. However, despite the stable technical performance, both environmental and economic assessments indicated a gradual decline in performance or increased burdens with repeated recycling. Specifically, (i) Environmental loads increased across all impact categories (GWP, ADP, OD, AP, EP) in the order of Scenario S1 < S2 < S3.

(ii) Total costs also rose modestly from approximately KRW 259,000 in S1 to KRW 268,000 in S3.

(iii) The amount of RFA requiring additional treatment increased.

(iv) The substitution of road base materials with higher-cost NCA led to reduced cost-effectiveness.

These findings are closely linked to the production process of RCA, which involves greater complexity and energy consumption due to the heterogeneous nature of C&D waste. In particular, current industrial standards require RCA-c to meet the same water absorption and oven-dry density criteria as NCA. Achieving these performance requirements necessitates multiple crushing stages, which in turn increases energy consumption and generates by-products such as RFA. From a global perspective, several countries classify RCA into different grades based on its physical properties, with corresponding limitations on concrete maximum strength or the allowable RCA replacement ratio. For example, Japanese standards categorize RCA for concrete into three quality levels: low35, medium36, and high37. Low-quality RCA is applied in non-structural elements where high strength and durability are not required, whereas medium-quality RCA is mainly used in structures not subject to drying shrinkage or freeze–thaw actions. Such grading and usage restriction approaches, which are adopted in several countries, are well summarized in a study by Tam et al.38. Under the KS F 2573, Korea standards previously classified RCA into three grades based on water absorption. However, implementing grade-specific applications considered impractical due to on-site management challenges, and the related provisions were eventually abolished. This experience underscores the importance of balancing the environmental and economic performance of RCA with its practical utilization efficiency. Looking forward, the development of more effective grading criteria and management systems will be essential. By applying such strategies, production processes could be optimized by selecting low- or medium-quality RCA when suitable, which could in turn reduce unnecessary multi-stage crushing and help decrease the generation of environmentally burdensome RFA. Moreover, such an approach may offer practical advantages in regions where recycling infrastructure is less developed, by providing a more flexible and feasible pathway to promote effective resource utilization.

Transport distance is a key factor influencing the environmental performance of RAs. Although this study did not explicitly account for transportation impacts, it is important to recognize that sources of NAs are expected to become gradually distant from urban centers due to resource depletion. Under such conditions, the environmental advantages of using RAs produced and consumed within cities are likely to become more important over time.

Conclusions

This study developed recycling scenarios for concrete waste and quantitatively evaluated their environmental and economic performance. The main findings are summarized as follows:

RAC incorporating RCA recycled once and twice, which met the quality requirements of the Korean Industrial Standards, showed no deterioration in performance. Both slump and compressive strength remained comparable to those of NAC. This suggests that, with proper quality control, repeated recycling of waste concrete as RCA-c can maintain the technical performance of concrete.

This highlights the need for a comprehensive approach to recycling strategies that takes into account not only technical feasibility but also the broader system, such as resource flows, material quality, and processing routes.

This conclusion is specific to the scenarios considered in this study and should not be interpreted as a general claim that downcycling of C&D waste is always more beneficial than upcycling in environmental and economic terms. The findings of this study are primarily based on publicly available LCI data and cost information from Korea. Future research could be improved by incorporating more detailed, site-specific, and measurement-based information. Additionally, this study did not account for factors such as material transport distances, the utilization of RFA, or the combined use of industrial by-products (e.g. slag, incineration ash). Indirect environmental benefits, including reduced landfill use, ecosystem preservation, and natural resource conservation, were also beyond the scope of this analysis. Incorporating these variables in future studies could improve the realism and reliability of circular resource strategies.
