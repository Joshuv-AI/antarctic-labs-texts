A study of the optimum injection rate of carbonic acid during matrix acidizing of carbonate reservoirs: Implications for reducing CO2 emissions
Authors: Abdulameer Almalichy, Murtada Saleh Aljawad, Zoltan Turzo, Ahmed Al-Yaseri
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study investigates the process of optimizing the injection rates of carbonic acid during matrix acidizing in carbonate formations, highlighting its impact on wormhole formation, dissolution, and the changes in rock petrophysical properties. Indiana limestone core samples, with porosity ranging from 14 % to 16 % and permeability from 7 to 12 mD, were flooded using diffrent injection rates (0.5, 1, 2, and 5 cm³/min) to determine the optimal injection velocity. The carbonic acid is prepared by mixing 70 % fresh water with 30 % sc-CO2 at 1500 psi and room temperature. A micro-CT scan was employed to visualize the wormhole patterns, measure its size, and calculate the optimum Damköhler Number. Moreover, inductively coupled plasma (ICP) was used to measure the calcium concentrations from effluents collected at different intervals. Two models were used to ascertain the optimum injection rate; the first model based on PVBT as a function of injection rate, indicated that 2 cm³/min is the optimum injection rate with 22 pore volumes to breakthrough (PVBT). The second model considered the relationship between PVBT and interstitial velocity (Vi), leading to an optimum injection rate of 1.7 cm³/min, corresponding to an interstitial velocity of 1 cm/min. Calculations of the optimum Damkhöler number reveal a value significantly different from the 0.29 proposed by Fredd and Fogler. This study provides valuable insights into the utilization of CO2 to enhance well productivity as a replacement for conventional acids.Keywords: Carbonic acid, Matrix acidizing, Wormhole, Damköhler number, Optimum injection rate

Introduction

The gradual increase in carbon dioxide (CO2) emissions is causing severe environmental and economic concerns regarding global climate change. Utilizing large volumes of CO2 in industrial projects can reduce greenhouse gas emissions [[1], [2], [3]]. In the last decade, much focus has been directed toward CO2 injection enhanced oil recovery (EOR) and carbon geo-storage (CCS) applications as promising methods to achieve zero-net carbon [[4], [5], [6]]. Using carbonic acid, which consists of fresh water and CO2, as a stimulation acid of carbonate reservoirs should reduce emissions and tubular corrosion, which is common in HCl acid stimulation.

Matrix acidizing is a stimulation technique used to remove the damaged zone around the well formation caused by the drilling and completion process; the intended acid is pumped into the wellbore during the acidizing process at a pressure below the formation breakdown pressure, avoiding formation fractures. When injected acid penetrates carbonate formations, it dissolves carbonate minerals and generates flow channels called wormholes that enhance well productivity [[7], [8], [9], [10]].

Creating a long and dominant flow channel called a wormhole is desirable during matrix acidizing. Finding the optimum injection rate is the key to achieving the longest penetration radius with minimum acid volumes. Experimentally, the optimum injection rate is evaluated when the acid generates a wormhole in a core sample with the minimum injected acid pore volume, a parameter called the minimum pore volume to breakthrough (PVBT). The optimum injection rate is affected by many factors, such as rock minerals, acid type, formation temperature, permeability, and core dimensions. Coreflooding experiments of the selected rock and acid type are usually conducted to find the optimum injection rate and the corresponding PVBT. The selection of the acid concentration, acid volume, and injection rate are all based on this fundamental characteristic. Thus, a similar study should be conducted on carbonic acid to understand its optimum injection conditions, which is an environmentally friendly replacement for hydrochloric acid [[11], [12], [13], [14]].

Numerous researchers have looked at the optimum injection rate theoretically and experimentally. Wang et al. were the first to experimentally determine the optimal injection rate through acid core flooding experiments. They found that the optimum value varies based on the injection rate, rock mineralogy, reaction temperature, and acid concentration. The results indicate that rock mineralogy has the most significant impact on the optimum injection rate. Higher injection rates are needed for formations with higher temperatures. Furthermore, the study revealed that increasing the acid viscosity or decreasing the acid diffusivity could be advantageous. According to the theory, the wormhole radius will decrease, decreasing the amount of acid needed to achieve a specific penetration [8,15].

Bazin et al. studied the effect of core length, acid concentration, and injection rate on wormhole propagation. He found that the wormhole propagation rate is proportional to u2/3, where u is the acid injection rate. Furthermore, researchers found the maximum wormhole depth on long cores, which was determined by a balance between convection and acid consumption. They also identified the optimal injection rate, which varies with core length, marking the transition between convection-limited and mass transfer-limited regimes [9,16].

Buijse studied the wormhole formation mechanism in carbonate formation and proved that the core geometry significantly influences leak-off properties and wormhole competition, impacting the upscaling of lab results to field scale. Wormhole growth rate, breakthrough volumes, etc., vary with core size. Larger-diameter cores exhibit more developed side branches and lower breakthrough volumes. The results indicate that wormhole models that depend on laboratory flooding experiments' outcomes should be used cautiously [11,17].

Buijse and Glasbergen developed a semi-empirical model to determine the relationship between acid injection rate and the propagation velocity of wormholes. This model aims to fit experimental data from acidizing core flooding experiments. Two essential parameters that can be obtained from the core flooding experiment and fit into the model are the optimum interstitial velocity and the associated PVBT. The study revealed that upscaling the optimum injection rate from a linear core flow experiment to field conditions is not straightforward. Pumping the acid at its maximum rate while avoiding the fracturing pressure could be the best option in a radial flow scenario [18]. Later on, another model was developed by Furui et al., which is based on enhancing Buijse and Glasbergen's semi-empirical model. This study upscaled the lab-scale core flooding experiments to field conditions [19,20].

Fredd and Fogler studied how the reaction rate of the acid and transport phenomena work together to generate wormholes using the Damköhler number concepts. They found that the optimum Damköhler number was 0.29 regardless of the acid system and rock type. The study revealed that a low injection rate leads to unwanted face dissolution, and the acid injected is consumed at the sample inlet. At a slightly higher injection rate, the acid enters the sample and generates a wormhole, but with a loss of acid on the wormhole wall leading to an increase in the acid PVBT; this type of wormhole is called a conical-shaped wormhole. At an intermediate injection rate, a single, less branched, dominant wormhole is generated with minimum pore volume to break through; this desirable type of wormhole is called a dominant wormhole. At a higher injection rate, the acid goes to the tiny pores, which means the acid contacts more surface area, resulting in more acid consumption and branched flow channels; this type of wormhole is called a ramified wormhole [21].

Dong et al. also studied the optimal injection rate in carbonate acidizing by considering the influence of rock lithology, acid concentration, temperature, and rock-pore-size distribution in their model. He approved that the model accurately predicts optimal rates for acidizing-core flood experiments compared to experimental results. Furthermore, the study reveals that wormhole-propagation velocity is directly proportional to the acid-diffusion coefficient for diffusion-limited reactions under optimal conditions, as confirmed experimentally and theoretically. Since the model was not limited by flow geometry, it can be applied to field scales, as demonstrated in the study [22,23].

Talbot and Gdanski analyzed different experimental results using hydrochloric acid, considering various factors such as temperature, acid concentration, core aspect ratio, porosity, and permeability. The authors developed a universal function to describe all the data, enabling the collapse of the data into a master curve. Regardless of the aspect ratio, this master curve makes it possible to predict the optimum flow rate for acid wormhole experiments on various rocks [24,25].

Later, Dong developed a novel wormhole propagation model for acidizing processes, explicitly examining the characteristics of wormholes as they reach specific lengths in core plugs. He discovered, through numerical simulations and lab experiments, that when a wormhole reaches a critical length, its propagation velocity becomes stable because of the establishment of a consistent fluid loss profile. The authors show that the propagation speed is influenced by various factors, including the distribution of pore sizes in the rock, the overall rate at which reactions occur, and the diffusion coefficient. He incorporated an optimal tip flux model into a mechanistic growth model [26].

Aljawad used a two-scale continuum model to investigate the impact of temperature on acid stimulation in carbonate reservoirs, specifically examining limestone, dolomite, and formations with mixed mineralogy. The key findings emphasized that the heat generated during the reaction had a negligible effect on the propagation of wormholes, whereas the temperature of the acid injected significantly impacted its efficiency. Increasing the temperature of the acid enhanced the effectiveness of dolomite stimulation but decreased the efficiency of limestone. At low temperatures, acid targets limestone more than dolomite in separate layers, while at elevated temperatures, it penetrates both layers with equal intensity. Wormhole propagation was enhanced in cases with mixed mineralogy, resembling the behavior observed in pure calcite under high-temperature conditions [27,28].

The main goal of this study is to determine the optimum injection rate of carbonic acid during matrix acidizing in carbonate formations, a knowledge that has yet to be disseminated. This involves investigating different injection rates and their impact on the creation of wormholes, dissolution rate, and the changes in petrophysical properties. The study aimed to identify the injection rate that produces the most efficient wormhole with minimal PVBT through four single-core acidizing experiments. The study was supplemented by several lab techniques such as helium porosimetry for porosity measurement, X-ray diffraction (XRD) for mineral composition analysis, computerized tomography (CT) scanning for wormhole visualization and dimension measurement, and inductively coupled plasma (ICP) for calcium (Ca+2) concentration analysis in effluent samples.

Experimental methods and materials

Experimental procedure

As shown in Fig. 1, the experimental procedure begins by measuring two critical petrophysical properties: porosity, which determines the void space within the rock, and permeability, which measures the ability of fluids to flow through the rock. The core sample's dimensions were measured using the caliper and then weighed in dry conditions, followed by measuring the porosity using the helium porosimeter and rough estimating the permeability using the Autoscan. The core sample was dried at 100 °C overnight in an oven. Vacuum the core sample for 3 h, then saturate it with fresh water with 2000 psi for 24 h. Core flooding follows, as detailed in the core flooding procedure section, wherein the differential pressure (DP)and pore volume to breakthrough (PVBT) were recorded besides collecting effluent samples for ICP analysis. The core samples were dried in the oven at 100 °C for 24 h. Computerized Tomography (CT) scans were then used to visualize the wormhole and measure its size. As the last step in the laboratory work, the porosity was measured after the acidizing and Effluent samples collected during the core flooding underwent Inductively Coupled Plasma (ICP) analysis.

The experimental workflow outlined above integrates various techniques to study flooding in carbonate formations using carbonic acid.

Core flooding procedure

In this study, carbonic acid was prepared for testing by mixing 30 % CO2 and 70 % fresh water using a high-pressure/high-temperature accumulator. The procedure involved filling accumulator No.2. with CO2 until the pressure reached 650 psi, followed by precise filling of accumulator No.1. with 700 cm3 of fresh water. Both accumulators were then connected to the esco pump according to the schematic in Fig. 3, ensuring proper alignment. The connection between the accumulators was closed, valve No.2., and the pump was set to constant pressure mode to reach 650 psi, with close monitoring to prevent overpressurization. Subsequently, the pump was switched to constant flow rate mode, and the connection between accumulators, valve No.2., was opened to initiate fresh water transfer to the CO2 accumulator at a controlled rate of 50 cm3/min until reaching the targeted volume of 750 cm3. After that, disconnect the accumulator containing the resulting carbonic acid, accumulator No.2., and manually mix for 10 min to ensure thorough incorporation of CO2 into the fresh water. Finally, the prepared accumulator was placed inside an oven set to 60 °C to allow sufficient time for equilibration and reaching the specified temperature for optimal performance. At the outset, the core flooding experiments started by covering the core sample with a shrink tube, loading it into the core holder, and placing it in the oven. A confining pressure of 3500 psi and a back pressure of 2000 psi were applied. Ahead of each experiment, fresh water was pumped at rates of 1, 2, 4, and 8 cm3/min, respectively, and the differential pressure was recorded to calculate the water permeability of the core sample before the flooding experiments. Injection experiments were performed using an HP/HT esco pump at four different injection rates (0.5, 1, 2, 5 cm3/min). The injection was stopped after a breakthrough occurred. Effluent samples were collected at each of the two-pore volumes for the ICP test to measure the Ca+2 concentration.

Computerized tomography (CT) scan

Computed tomography is a non-invasive method that utilizes X-rays to produce a sequence of 2D cross-sectional images of the rock sample. The program can generate a 3D model of the sample from the images. Software may analyze the images to identify the rock sample's porosity, permeability, mineral composition, wormhole presence, and pore size distribution. The CT scan comprises four components: an X-ray source system, a mechanical motion system, a data-gathering system, and an image production system [29,30]. The wormhole length can be objectively established by acquiring the standard curve [31]. The X-ray source emits X-rays that penetrate and weaken as they pass through the rock, a process influenced by mineral density. A detector captures the weakened X-rays to generate a projected image. The rock sample is rotated 180 or 360° to create a sequence of projection images, as described by Shafiq [32]. In our study, the CT scan was conducted after the flooding experiments to visualize the wormhole and estimate its volume using pergeous software.

Inductively coupled plasma (ICP)

Inductively Coupled Plasma (ICP) is a versatile analytical technique for detecting trace components in many types of samples. It integrates the ideas of atomic emission spectroscopy and mass spectrometry [33]. Calcium, a common element found in carbonate rocks, is essential in influencing the efficiency of acid treatments. Accurate monitoring of calcium content is crucial for improving acid formulations and forecasting reservoir response. ICP-OES is a favored analytical method for elemental analysis because of its high sensitivity, precision, and capability to examine many elements simultaneously [34]. Calcium is a significant component of carbonate minerals like calcite and dolomite, which comprise most carbonate reservoirs. The dissolution of calcium-rich minerals during matrix acidizing substantially impacts the permeability and porosity of the rock, ultimately affecting reservoir productivity. Accurate measurement of calcium levels before and after acid treatment is crucial for assessing the effectiveness of the stimulation procedure [35].

When considering the outcomes of the two methods proposed by Wang and the other one proposed by Ref. [18], it is obvious that both approaches aim to identify the injection rate to obtain the breakthrough with minimum acid volume. Wang's method utilizes experimental observation and curve fitting to determine the optimal injection rate. In this study, which is found to be 2 cm³/min, the rate was established based on experimental data.

However, Buijse and Glasbergen's model presents a more detailed and systematic comprehension of how the injection rate, interstitial velocity, and wormhole growth are interconnected. Their method produces a different and more accurate injection rate. Based on our experiments, the optimum injection rate was 1.7 cm³/min, with an optimal interstitial velocity of 1 cm/min. These values are obtained through thorough calculations that consider various factors, including sample diameter, porosity, injection rate, and pore volume to break through.

As compared with several researchers have investigated the optimal injection rates for various acid treatments in limestone formations, revealing a spectrum of outcomes [[37], [38], [39], [40]]. For example, one study determined that employing 3.4 wt% HCl at 75 °C required an injection rate of 3.5 cm³/min to achieve optimal results. However, at 50 °C, this rate dropped significantly to 1 cm³/min [8]. In contrast, employing foam acid, comprising 15 wt% HCl and 2 vol% foaming agent, at 60 °C significantly reduced the optimal injection rate to 1.5 cm³/min [31]. Another investigation exploring the efficacy of 10 % acetic acid at 121 °C indicated a further decrease in the optimal injection rate to 1.2 cm³/min [14]. Conversely, the utilization of 10 wt% methanol sulfamic acid (MSA) at 80 °C resulted in an increased injection rate of 6 cm³/min, notably dropping to 2 cm³/min at room temperature [41]. Interestingly, gelled acid, in-situ gelled acid, and regular acid (all at 5 wt% HCl) exhibit the same optimal injection rate at 120 °C, which stands at 7 cm³/min, meaning that changing the acid type didn't affect the optimum injection rate. However, the required volume for a breakthrough was significantly different for all treatments [42].

Damkohler number

The Damköhler number measures the ratio between the overall rate of acid dissolution and the rate of acid convection [43]. Fredd and Fogler first introduced the damköhler number by conducting different theoretical and experimental investigations on a wide range of acid systems, and they observed that the optimum Damköhler number was 0.29. In matrix acidizing, the Damköhler number is essential in determining the dissolution patterns of carbonate formation. Two cases of the Damkohler number formula exist based on the reaction rate. The first case is mass transfer limited, which indicates that the movement of carbonic acid (H₂CO₃) from the solution to the calcite surface (where the solid and liquid meet) occurs at a slower rate compared to the reaction on the surface. The other case is reaction rate limited, which indicates that the surface reaction rate (the process of calcite dissolving) is the slowest step [21,44]. At a low injection rate of 0.5 cm³/min (Fig. 12D), face dissolution did not occur due to carbonic acid's relatively slow reaction kinetics. However, this condition led to the formation of a branched wormhole, resulting in the highest wormhole volume compared to other experiments. Increasing the injection rate slightly to 1 cm³/min (Fig. 12C) resulted in a notable acid loss on the wormhole's walls, leading to conical-shaped wormholes with multiple branches within the core. A dominant, less branched wormhole was formed with minimal pore volume required for the breakthrough at an intermediate injection rate of 2 cm³/min (Fig. 12B). This type of wormhole, known as a dominant wormhole, exhibited the lowest wormhole volume.

Further increasing the injection rate to 5 cm³/min (Fig. 12A) resulted in the acid penetrating tiny pores, increasing surface area contact and higher acid consumption. This condition led to ramified wormholes characterized by branched flow channels. These findings indicate that the injection rate plays a crucial role in shaping the morphology and volume of wormholes formed during acid injection processes. Intermediate injection rates promote the formation of less branched, dominant wormholes, whereas higher and lower rates lead to more branched structures.

ICP analysis

Calcium is the major constituent of carbonate minerals such as calcite and dolomite, which comprise most carbonate reservoirs. Calcium-rich minerals dissolution during matrix acidizing significantly influences rock permeability and porosity, thereby impacting reservoir productivity. In this study, the effluent samples were taken every two pore volumes in each experiment. Then, the ICP test was conducted to determine the calcium concentration. As we can see in Fig. 14, increasing the injection rate from 0.5 cm3/min to 2 cm3/min resulted in a significant increase in calcium concentration from 2770 ppm to 7070 ppm, while at the greatest injection rate of 5 cm3/min, the calcium concentration significantly dropped to 5146 ppm. The 2 cm3/min injection rate yielded the greatest calcium concentration of 7070 ppm, indicating the most effective rate for promoting calcium dissolution in the specified experimental conditions. The ICP test results, plotted in Fig. 14, agree with the results presented in Fig. 8, as the 2 cm3/min experiment resulted in the fastest breakthrough of a wormhole.

Conclusion

The comprehensive experimental study on carbonic acid injection into carbonate formations has yielded several significant findings that enhance our understanding of the process and its optimization. Below are the key findings derived from the presented results.

1.Optimum Injection Rate: two models were presented to find the optimum injection rate, one based on the injection rate proposed by Wang. It's observed that the optimum injection rate lies at 2 cm³/min, and another is based on the interstitial velocity of acid proposed by Buijse and Glasbergen, considers the relationship between PVBT and interstitial velocity (Vi), as expressed in Equations (3), (4), (5), (6), (7), (8). By calculating parameters such as efficiency factor (Weff), B-factor (WB), and wormhole growth rate (Vwh), the model determines an optimum interstitial velocity of 1 cm/min, corresponding to an optimum injection rate of 1.7 cm³/min. We can speculate that changes in the temperature and pressure might change the optimum injection rate and velocity. Wang's model determines the optimum injection rate based on the lowest PVBT obtained experimentally. However, due to the limitations of the number of experiments that can be conducted, the optimum obtained from Wang's method might not be the actual optimum. Therefore, Buijse and Glasbergen's semi-empirical model, whose parameter can be obtained from few experimental data and the true optimum PVBT can be determined.
