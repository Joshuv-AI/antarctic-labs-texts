Forward and Inverse Problems in Nonlinear Acoustics


Abstract
Résumé The importance of ultrasound is well established in the imaging of human tissue. In order to enhance image quality by exploiting nonlinear effects, recently techniques such as harmonic imaging and nonlinearity parameter tomography have been put forward. As soon as the pressure amplitude exceeds a certain bound, the classical linear wave equation loses its validity and more general nonlinear versions have to be used. Another characteristic property of ultrasound propagation in human tissue is frequency power law attenuation, leading to fractional derivative damping models in time domain. In this contribution we will first of all dwell on modeling nonlinearity on the one hand and fractional damping on the other hand. Moreover we will give an idea on the challenges in the analysis of the resulting PDEs and discuss some parameter asymptotics. Finally, we address a relevant inverse problems in this context, the above mentioned task of nonlinearity parameter imaging, which leads to a coefficient identification problem for a quasilinear wave equation.



1
Introduction

The difference between linear and nonlinear acoustic wave propagation becomes apparent by a steepening of waves, see Figure
1
. This is due to a pressure dependent wave speed, which is higher in compressed areas and lower in low density regions. This fact is visible in the wave profiles from both a space and from a time perspective, and can be also be read off from the PDEs, cf. (
2.7
) below. Another way of characterizing nonlinearity is by looking at contributions in frequency domain in case of a sinusiodal excitation, which in the linear case results in a response at the excitation frequency only, whereas in the nonlinear case, so-called higher harmonics are generated, see Figure
2
. Also this effect is very well visible in the governing mathematical models, cf. (
5.4
).

2.1
Physical Principles

The main physical quantities in acoustics are

\bullet
acoustic particle velocity
\mathbf{v}
;

\bullet
absolute temperature
{\vartheta}
;

\bullet
acoustic pressure
p
;

\bullet
heat flux
{\bm{q}}
;

\bullet
mass density
\varrho
;

\bullet
entropy
{\eta}
;

These are usually decomposed into mean (zero order) and fluctuating (first order) parts

\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{v}_{0}+\mathbf{v}_{\sim}=\mathbf{v}\,,\qquad p=p_{0}+p_{\sim}\,,\qquad\varrho=\varrho_{0}+\varrho_{\sim}\,,\qquad\text{etc.}

and subject to the following physical laws

—

momentum conservation =
Navier Stokes equation (with
\nabla\times\mathbf{v}=0
) :

{\varrho}\Big({\mathbf{v}_{t}}+\nabla({\mathbf{v}}\cdot{\mathbf{v}})\Big)+\nabla{p}=\Big(\tfrac{4\mu_{V}}{3}+\zeta_{V}\Big)\Delta{\mathbf{v}}

—

mass conservation = equation of continuity :

{\displaystyle{\varrho_{t}}+\nabla\cdot({\varrho}{\mathbf{v}})=0}

—

entropy equation :

{\displaystyle{\varrho}{\vartheta}({\eta}_{t}+{\mathbf{v}}\cdot\nabla{\eta})=-\nabla\cdot{\bm{q}}}

—

equation of state :

{\displaystyle{\varrho_{\sim}}=\frac{1}{c^{2}}{p_{\sim}}-\frac{1}{\varrho_{0}c^{4}}\tfrac{B}{2A}{p_{\sim}}^{2}-\frac{a}{\varrho_{0}c^{4}}\Big(\frac{1}{c_{V}}-\frac{1}{c_{p}}\Big){{p_{\sim}}_{t}}}

—

Gibbs equation :
{\displaystyle{\vartheta}d{\eta}=c_{V}d{\vartheta}-{p}\frac{1}{\varrho^{2}}d{\varrho}}

where
c_{p}
/
c_{V}
is the specific heat at constant pressure / volume, and

\zeta_{V}
/
\mu_{V}
the bulk / shear viscosity.
The missing 6th equation in this system governing the 6 unknown functions
{\mathbf{v}}
,
{p}
,
{\varrho}
,
{\vartheta}
,
{\bm{q}}
,
{\eta}
is the relation between temperature and heat flux, which (like the pressure – density relation) is subject to constitutive modeling.
Classically, Fourier’s law

{\bm{q}}=-K\nabla{\vartheta}

(2.1)

(with
K
denoting the thermal conductivity) has been employed for this purpose. However this is known to lead to the infinite speed of propagation paradox.
For this reason, a relaxation term has been introduced, that leads to the
Maxwell-Cattaneo law

\tau{\bm{q}}_{t}+{\bm{q}}=-K\nabla{\vartheta},

(2.2)

which contains the relaxation time
\tau
as an additional parameter and
allows for so-called “thermal waves” (also called the second sound phenomenon).

2.2
Classical Models of Nonlinear Acoustics

In the linear case, where quadratic and higher order terms (e.g., products of first order terms) are skipped, it suffices to consider momentum and mass conservation along with the equation of state, and the velocity can be eliminated by taking the divergence in momentum conservation and subtracting the time derivative of mass conservation. Upon substitution of the (linearized) pressure – density relation, this leads to the well-known second order wave equation
p_{tt}-c^{2}\Delta p=0
.
In the nonlinear case, the same procedure, but retaining quadratic terms,
(that is, application of the so-called Blackstock’s scheme in nonlinear acoustics) leads to the classical models of nonlinear acoustics.

—

Kuznetsov’s equation

[
46
,
45
]

{{p_{\sim}}_{tt}-c^{2}\Delta p_{\sim}-\delta\Delta{p_{\sim}}_{t}=\left(\tfrac{B}{2A\varrho_{0}c^{2}}p_{\sim}^{2}+\varrho_{0}|\mathbf{v}|^{2}\right)_{tt}}

where

\varrho_{0}\mathbf{v}_{t}=-\nabla p

for the
particle velocity
\mathbf{v}

and the
pressure
p
, i.e.,

{\psi_{tt}-c^{2}\Delta\psi-\delta\Delta\psi_{t}=\Bigl(\tfrac{B}{2A\,c^{2}}(\psi_{t})^{2}+|\nabla\psi|^{2}\Bigr)_{t},}

(2.3)

since
\nabla\times\mathbf{v}=0
hence

\mathbf{v}=-\nabla\psi

for some velocity potential
\psi

—

The
Westervelt equation

[
54
]
, which is obtained from Kuznetsov’s equation via the approximation
\varrho_{0}|\mathbf{v}|^{2}\approx\frac{1}{\varrho_{0}c^{2}}({p_{\sim}})^{2}
that corresponds to neglecting non-cumulative nonlinear effects

{{p_{\sim}}_{tt}-c^{2}\Delta p_{\sim}-\delta\Delta{p_{\sim}}_{t}=\frac{1}{\varrho_{0}c^{2}}\Bigl(1+\tfrac{B}{2A}\Bigr){p_{\sim}^{2}}_{tt}}

(2.4)

Here
\delta=h(\text{Pr}(\tfrac{4}{3}+\tfrac{\zeta_{V}}{\mu_{V}})+\gamma-1)
is the diffusivity of sound ; (containing the thermal diffusivity
h
and the Prandtl number Pr) and

\tfrac{B}{A}

is the nonlinearity parameter.

2.3
Advanced Models of Nonlinear Acoustics

Taking into account further physical effects requires to consider temperature, heat flux and entropy as variables as well.
Modeling in nonlinear acoustics is a highly active field and only a small portion of the resulting PDE models has been subject to rigorous mathematical investigation so far.
We here mention a few examples of these.

—

The
Jordan-Moore-Gibson-Thompson
JMGT equation
[
13
,
27
,
50
]

\tau\psi_{ttt}+\psi_{tt}-c^{2}\Delta\psi-(\delta+\tau c^{2})\Delta\psi_{t}=\left(\tfrac{B}{2Ac^{2}}(\psi_{t})^{2}+|\nabla\psi|^{2}\right)_{t}

(2.5)

where
\tau
is the relaxation time, results from replacing Fourier’s law (
2.1
) of heat conduction by (
2.2
) ; later on, we will also encounter fractional versions thereof.
The mentioned second sound phenomenon can be seen by considering the auxiliary quantity

z:=\psi_{t}+\frac{c^{2}}{\delta+\tau c^{2}}\psi
, which solves a weakly damped wave equation

z_{tt}-\tilde{c}\Delta z+\gamma z_{t}=r(z,\psi)

with
\tilde{c}=c^{2}+\tfrac{\delta}{\tau}
,
\gamma=\tfrac{1}{\tau}-\tfrac{c^{2}}{\delta+\tau c^{2}}>0
and a lower order term
r(z,\psi)
.

—

The
Blackstock-Crighton
BCBJ equation
[
5
,
8
,
15
]

\left(\partial_{t}-a\Delta\right)\left(\psi_{tt}-c^{2}\Delta\psi-\delta\Delta\psi_{t}\right)-ra\Delta\psi_{t}=\left(\tfrac{B}{2Ac^{2}}(\psi_{t}^{2})+\left|\nabla\psi\right|^{2}\right)_{tt}

(2.6)

where
a=\frac{\nu}{\mbox{Pr}}
is the thermal diffusivity and
r
another constant.

Both models can be related to the classical Kuznetsov equation (
2.3
) by – first of all just formally – setting the parameters
\tau
and
a
to zero.
We will take a look at the mathematical justification of these parameter limits in Section
3
.

The amount of literature on the analysis of these and some other advanced models of nonlinear acoustics is vast and we do not attempt to provide an overview that could claim completeness in any sense.
Some selected references are

,

]
.

Just to get an idea of the mathematical challenges arising in the analysis (referring to the above quoted literature for details) let us take a look at the question of well-posedness of a prototypical initial-boundary value problem
for the Westervelt equation as the most simple of the above mentioned models

\displaystyle u_{tt}-c^{2}\Delta u-b\Delta u_{t}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{\kappa}{2}(u^{2})_{tt}

\displaystyle\mbox{ in }\Omega

\displaystyle\frac{\partial u}{\partial n}

\displaystyle=g

\displaystyle\mbox{ on }\partial\Omega

\displaystyle u(t=0)=u_{0}\,,\ u_{t}(t=0)

\displaystyle=u_{1}

\displaystyle\mbox{ in }\Omega,

where we use the mathematically common notation
u
for the PDE solution, which plays the physical role of the acoustic pressure.
Differentiating out the quadratic term and a slight rearrangement leads to

{(1-\kappa u)}u_{tt}-c^{2}\Delta u-b\Delta u_{t}=\kappa(u_{t})^{2}

which reveals the fact that the coefficient of the second time derivative depends on
u
itself.
This leads to potential degeneracy for
u\geq\frac{1}{\kappa}
and similarly affects the other models (Kuznetsov, Jordan-Moore-Gibson-Thompson, Blackstock-Crighton).
The typical approach of proving well-posedness therefore relies on smallness of the state and proceeds as follows :

—

employ energy estimates to obtain a bound on
u
in
C(0,T;H^{2}(\Omega))
;

—

use smallness of
u
in
C(0,T;H^{2}(\Omega))
and the embedding
H^{2}(\Omega)\to L^{\infty}(\Omega)
to guarantee

1-\kappa u\geq\underline{\alpha}>0
;

—

apply a combination of these arguments in a fixed point scheme.

This also illustrates state dependence of the effective wave speed, since a further rearrangement allows to rewrite the equation as

u_{tt}-\tilde{c}^{2}\Delta u-\tilde{b}(u)\Delta u_{t}=f(u)

with

\tilde{c}(u)=\frac{c}{\sqrt{1-\kappa u}},\quad\tilde{b}(u)=\frac{b}{1-\kappa u},\quad f(u)=\frac{\kappa(u_{t})^{2}}{1-\kappa u},

(2.7)

as long as
1-\kappa u>0
(otherwise the equation loses its validity as a wave propagation model).

3
Parameter asymptotics

In this section we point to a few analytical results on limits that interrelate the models in Section
2
. We will be explicit about function space settings and mathematical tools only in part for the first example (the limit as
\tau\searrow
in the JMGT equation, cf. Section
3.1
). The other limits will be discussed briefly and collectively in Section
3.2
; details can be found in the cited papers.

3.1
Vanishing relaxation time in the
Jordan-Moore-Gibson-Thompson equation

Considering a family of solutions
(\psi^{\tau})_{\tau\in(0,\overline{\tau}]}
to

\tau\psi^{\tau}_{ttt}+\psi^{\tau}_{tt}-c^{2}\Delta\psi^{\tau}-b\Delta\psi^{\tau}_{t}=\left(\tfrac{B}{2Ac^{2}}(\psi^{\tau}_{t})^{2}+|\nabla\psi^{\tau}|^{2}\right)_{t}

with
b=\delta+\tau c^{2}
, the typical question to be answered is, whether and in which function spaces a limit
\psi^{0}
of
\psi^{\tau}
as
\tau\searrow 0
exists and under which conditions it can be shown to solve Kuznetsov’s equation (
2.3
).

Some comments are in order.

—

We will consider the “Westervelt type” and the “Kuznetsov type” equation ; without and with the gradient nonlinearity
|\nabla\psi|^{2}_{t}
.

—

For
\tau=0
(classical Westervelt and Kuznetsov equation) the reformulation of the linearization as a first order system leads to an analytic semigroup and maximal parabolic regularity.
These properties do not hold any more with
\tau>0
, that is, the equation loses its “parabolic nature”.
This is consistent with physics, corresponding to a transition from infinite to finite propagation speed. As
\tau\to 0
the PDE changes from hyperbolic to parabolic.

—

As in the classical models (
2.3
), (
2.4
), potential degeneracy can be an issue, since we have

\displaystyle\tau\psi^{\tau}_{ttt}+\psi^{\tau}_{tt}-c^{2}\Delta\psi^{\tau}-b\Delta\psi^{\tau}_{t}=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\kappa}{2}(\psi^{\tau}_{t})^{2}+|\nabla\psi^{\tau}|^{2}\right)_{t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\kappa\psi^{\tau}_{t}\psi^{\tau}_{tt}+|\nabla\psi^{\tau}|^{2}_{t}

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow\quad\tau\psi^{\tau}_{ttt}+{(1-\kappa\psi^{\tau}_{t})}\psi^{\tau}_{tt}-c^{2}\Delta\psi^{\tau}

\displaystyle-b\Delta\psi^{\tau}_{t}=|\nabla\psi^{\tau}|^{2}_{t}.

The plan of the analysis is to first establish well-posedness of the linearized equation along with energy estimates and use these results to prove well-posedness of the Westervelt or Kuznetsov type JMGT equation for
\tau>0
by a fixed point argument.
Relying on uniform in
\tau
bounds obtained in the energy analysis and (weak) compactness of bounded sets in the underlying solution spaces, we then take limits as
\tau\to 0
.
We refer to
[
6
,
33
,
34
]
for details and only sketch the above steps in a concrete function space setting for the Westervelt type case, while mentioning in passing that an extension to the Kuznetsov type JMGT equation requires higher order energy estimates.

We start by considering the linearized problem with variable coefficient
\alpha

\displaystyle\begin{cases}{\tau}\psi_{ttt}+\alpha(x,t)\psi_{tt}-c^{2}\Delta\psi-b\Delta\psi_{t}=f\quad\mbox{ in }\Omega\times(0,T),\\[2.84526pt]
\psi=0\quad\mbox{ on }\partial\Omega\times(0,T),\\[2.84526pt]
(\psi,\psi_{t},\psi_{tt})=(\psi_{0},\psi_{1},\psi_{2})\quad\mbox{ in }\Omega\times\{0\}.\end{cases}

(3.1)

Under the assumptions

\displaystyle\alpha(x,t)\geq\underline{\alpha}>0\ \mbox{ on }\Omega\ \ \mbox{ a.e. in }\Omega\times(0,T).

(3.2)

\displaystyle\alpha\in L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{\infty}(\Omega))\cap L^{\infty}(0,T;W^{1,3}(\Omega)),

(3.3)

\displaystyle f\in H^{1}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega)).

(\psi_{0},\psi_{1},\psi_{2})\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)\cap H^{2}(\Omega)\times H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)\cap H^{2}(\Omega)\times H_{0}^{1}(\Omega).

(3.4)

we obtain the following result

Theorem 3.1
.

Let

,

The solution fullfils the estimate

\displaystyle\|\psi\|_{W,\tau}^{2}:=

\displaystyle{\displaystyle\tau}^{2}\|\psi_{ttt}\|^{2}_{L^{2}L^{2}}+{\tau}\|\psi_{tt}\|^{2}_{L^{\infty}H^{1}}+\|\psi_{tt}\|^{2}_{L^{2}H^{1}}+\|\psi\|^{2}_{W^{1,\infty}H^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C(\alpha,T,{\tau})\left(|\psi_{0}|^{2}_{H^{2}}+|\psi_{1}|^{2}_{H^{2}}+{\tau}|\psi_{2}|^{2}_{H^{1}}+\|f\|^{2}_{L^{\infty}L^{2}}+\|f_{t}\|^{2}_{L^{2}L^{2}}\right).

As standard for proving well-posedness of evolutionary PDEs, we combine a Galerkin discretization with energy estimates and weak limits ; however, due to the need for higher regularity,

\diamondsuit

This allows to prove well-posedness of the Westervelt type JMGT equation

\displaystyle\begin{cases}{\tau}\psi_{ttt}+(1-\kappa\psi_{t})\psi_{tt}-c^{2}\Delta\psi-b\Delta\psi_{t}=0\,\quad\mbox{ in }\Omega\times(0,T),\\[2.84526pt]

3.2
Some further parameter limits

—

Vanishing diffusivity of sound
\delta\searrow 0
in
Kuznetsov’s equation (
2.3
) :

\psi^{\delta}_{tt}-c^{2}\Delta\psi^{\delta}-{\delta}\Delta\psi^{\delta}_{t}=\left(\tfrac{B}{2Ac^{2}}(\psi^{\delta}_{t})^{2}+\left|\nabla\psi^{\delta}\right|^{2}\right)_{t}

with an undamped quasilinear wave equation

\psi_{tt}-c^{2}\Delta\psi=\left(\tfrac{B}{2Ac^{2}}(\psi_{t})^{2}+\left|\nabla\psi\right|^{2}\right)_{t}

(3.5)

as a limiting problem, cf.
[
36
]
.
The challenge here lies in the fact that
\delta>0
is crucial for global in time well-posedness and exponential decay in

space dimensions.
As a byproduct for
\delta=0
we recover results (in particular on required regularity of initial data) from
[
18
]
on the undamped Westervelt equation and obtain a new result on local in time well-posedness of the undamped Kuznetsov equation (
3.5
).
A similar limiting analyisis for
\delta\searrow 0
has been carried out for the JMGT equation (
2.5
) in
[

]
.

—

Vanishing thermal diffusivity in the Blackstock-Crighton equation (
2.6
) :

Here the aim is to recover solutions to Kuznetsov’s equation (
2.3
) as a limit for
a\searrow 0
of solutions
\psi^{a}
to

\left(\partial_{t}-{a}\Delta\right)\left(\psi^{a}_{tt}-c^{2}\Delta\psi^{a}-\delta\Delta\psi^{a}_{t}\right)-ra\Delta\psi^{a}_{t}=\left(\tfrac{B}{2Ac^{2}}({\psi^{a}_{t}}^{2})+\left|\nabla\psi^{a}\right|^{2}\right)_{tt}

cf.
[
43
]
.
Note that this process also requires one integration with respect to time. To obtain sufficiently regular solutions, (as needed for the underlying arguments for nondegeneracy), consistency of the initial data is thus needed

\psi_{2}-c^{2}\Delta\psi_{0}-\delta\Delta\psi_{1}=\tfrac{B}{Ac^{2}}\psi_{1}\psi_{2}+2\nabla\psi_{0}\cdot\nabla\psi_{1}.

—

Limit
\alpha\nearrow 1
of the differentiation order in fractional JMGT equations :

Finally, we also consider families of time-fractional versions of the Jordan-Moore-Gibson-Thompson equation (
2.5
), such as

\tau^{{\alpha}}\partial_{t}^{2+{\alpha}}\psi^{\alpha}+\psi^{\alpha}_{tt}-c^{2}\Delta\psi^{\alpha}-(\delta+\tau^{{\alpha}}c^{2})\Delta\partial_{t}^{{\alpha}}\psi^{\alpha}=\left(\tfrac{B}{2Ac^{2}}(\psi^{\alpha}_{t})^{2}+|\nabla\psi^{\alpha}|^{2}\right)_{t}

and convergence of their solutions to one of (
2.5
) (with fixed positive

), cf.
[
37
]
; see also (

Like in the
\tau\searrow 0
limit, the leading order time derivative changes ; however here this happens in a continuous manner.
One of the key tasks in this work was the derivation of proper models from physical balance and constitutive laws. This brings us to the topic of the next section.

Fractional attenuation models in ultrasonics

As Figure
6

Since physical balance equations by their nature only contain integer derivatives, fractional (or more generally, nonlocal) modeling happens on the level of constitutive laws.
In acoustics this can be done in terms of

—

pressure – density relation and/or

—

temperature – heat flux relation

Below we will partly describe the derivation of such models in the simpler linear setting ; nonlinear versions can be obtained by inserting the constitutive equations into the balance laws and applying the procedure mentioned at the beginning of Section
2.2
, leading to second (or higher) order in time wave equations.

As pressure – density relation often rely on analogy to viscoelasticity (which was one or the historical origins of fractional modeling
[
12
]
), we start with a brief recap on fractional modeling there.

4.1
Fractional models of (linear) viscoelasticity

In continuum mechanics, the deformation of a body is described by

—

the equation of motion (resulting from balance of forces)

\varrho\mathbf{u}_{tt}=\mbox{div}\sigma+\mathbf{f};

—

the characterization of strain as the symmetric gradient of displacements (in a geometrically linearized setting)

\epsilon=\frac{1}{2}(\nabla\mathbf{u}+(\nabla\mathbf{u})^{T});

—

a constitutive model, that is, a stress-strain relation.

In here,
\mathbf{u}
is the vector of displacements,

\sigma
and
\epsilon
are the stress and strain tensors and
\varrho
is the mass density.

Classical examples of constitutive models are

\displaystyle\mbox{Hooke's law (linear elasticity) :}

\displaystyle\sigma=b_{0}\epsilon

\displaystyle\mbox{Newton model :}

\displaystyle\sigma=b_{1}\epsilon_{t}

\displaystyle\mbox{Kelvin-Voigt model :}

\displaystyle\sigma=b_{0}\epsilon+b_{1}\epsilon_{t}

\displaystyle\mbox{Maxwell model :}

\displaystyle\sigma+a_{1}\sigma_{t}=b_{0}\epsilon

\displaystyle\mbox{Zener model :}

\displaystyle\sigma+a_{1}\sigma_{t}=b_{0}\epsilon+b_{1}\epsilon_{t}

with coefficients
a_{i}
,
b_{i}
.
Replacing the first time derivatives in there by fractional ones
\partial_{t}^{\beta}
(cf. Section
4.4
) with
\beta\in[0,1)
, one arrives at (time-)fractional versions

\displaystyle\mbox{fractional Newton model :}

\displaystyle\sigma=b_{1}\partial_{t}^{\beta}\epsilon

\displaystyle\mbox{fractional Kelvin-Voigt model :}

\displaystyle\sigma=b_{0}\epsilon+b_{1}\partial_{t}^{\beta}\epsilon

\displaystyle\mbox{fractional Maxwell model :}

\displaystyle\sigma+a_{1}\partial_{t}^{\alpha}\sigma=b_{0}\epsilon

\displaystyle\mbox{fractional Zener model :}

\displaystyle\sigma+a_{1}\partial_{t}^{\alpha}\sigma=b_{0}\epsilon+b_{1}\partial_{t}^{\beta}\epsilon

\displaystyle\mbox{which are comprised in the general model class :}

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{N}a_{n}\partial_{t}^{\alpha_{n}}\sigma=\sum_{m=0}^{M}b_{m}\partial_{t}^{\beta_{m}}\epsilon

cf.
[
3
,
12
]
.

4.2
Fractional models of (linear) acoustics via pressure – density relation

As we have already seen in Section
2
, the fundamental laws of acoustics are first of all

—

balance of momentum

\varrho_{0}\mathbf{v}_{t}=-\nabla p+\mathbf{f}

—

balance of mass

\varrho\nabla\cdot\mathbf{v}=-\varrho_{t}

—

combined with an equation of state ;
the most simple linear one is

\frac{\varrho_{\sim}}{\varrho_{0}}=\frac{p_{\sim}}{p_{0}}

and analogously to the general viscoelastic law above, can be generalized to

\sum_{m=0}^{M}b_{m}\partial_{t}^{\beta_{m}}\frac{\varrho_{\sim}}{\varrho_{0}}=\sum_{n=0}^{N}a_{n}\partial_{t}^{\alpha_{n}}\frac{p_{\sim}}{p_{0}}.

Inserting the equation of state into a combination of the balance laws that eliminates the velocity leads to fractional acoustic wave equations.
Some of the most commonly used instances are as follows
[
24
,
51
]
.

—

Caputo-Wismer-Kelvin wave equation (fractional Kelvin-Voigt) :

p_{tt}-b_{0}\Delta p-b_{1}\partial_{t}^{\beta}\Delta p=\tilde{f}\,,

—

modified Szabo wave equation (fractional Maxwell) :

p_{tt}-a_{1}\partial_{t}^{2+\alpha}p-b_{0}\Delta p=\tilde{f}\,,

—

fractional Zener wave equation :

p_{tt}-a_{1}\partial_{t}^{2+\alpha}p-b_{0}\Delta p+b_{1}\partial_{t}^{\beta}\Delta p=\tilde{f}\,,

—

general fractional model :

{\textstyle\sum_{n=0}^{N}a_{n}\partial_{t}^{2+\alpha_{n}}p-\sum_{m=0}^{M}b_{m}\partial_{t}^{\beta_{m}}\Delta p=\tilde{f}\,.}

We refer to
[
40
, Chapter 7]
for their well-posednesss analysis.

4.3
Fractional models of nonlinear acoustics via temperature – heat flux relation

Recall that the classical Fourier law (
2.1
) leads to an infinite speed of propagation paradox, which can be amended by substituting it with the Maxwell-Cattaneo law (
2.2
), allowing for “thermal waves” (aka the second sound phenomenon).
However, this can lead to a violation of the 2nd law of thermodynamics and moreover is unable to reproduce the fractional power law frequency dependence of attenuation as relevant in ultrasonics.
Some research in physics has therefore been devoted to an appropriate and physically consistent modeling of the temperature – heat flux relation.
This can be achieved by “interpolating” between (
2.1
) and (
2.2
),
using fractional derivatives
[
14
,
23
,
49
]
:

(GFE)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad(1+\tau^{\alpha}\partial_{t}^{\alpha}){\bm{q}}(t)=

\displaystyle-K\nabla{\vartheta}.\hphantom{{\tau_{\vartheta}^{1-\alpha}}\partial_{t}^{1-\alpha}}

(GFE I)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad(1+\tau^{\alpha}\partial_{t}^{\alpha}){\bm{q}}(t)=

\displaystyle\,-K{\tau_{\vartheta}^{1-\alpha}}\partial_{t}^{1-\alpha}\nabla{\vartheta}

(GFE II)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad(1+\tau^{\alpha}\partial_{t}^{\alpha}){\bm{q}}(t)=

\displaystyle\,-K{\tau_{\vartheta}^{\alpha-1}}\partial_{t}^{\alpha-1}\nabla{\vartheta}

(GFE III)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad(1+\tau\partial_{t}){\bm{q}}(t)=

\displaystyle\,-K{\tau_{\vartheta}^{1-\alpha}}\partial_{t}^{1-\alpha}\nabla{\vartheta}.

Using these in place of (
2.2
) leads to the models

\displaystyle\tau^{\alpha}\partial_{t}^{\alpha}\psi_{tt}+(1-\kappa\psi_{t})\psi_{tt}-c^{2}\Delta\psi-\tau^{\alpha}c^{2}\partial_{t}^{\alpha}\Delta\psi-\delta\Delta\psi_{t}+\partial_{t}|\nabla\psi|^{2}=0

(4.1)

\displaystyle\tau^{\alpha}\partial_{t}^{\alpha}\psi_{tt}+(1-\kappa\psi_{t})\psi_{tt}-c^{2}\Delta\psi-\tau^{\alpha}c^{2}\partial_{t}^{\alpha}\Delta\psi-\delta\partial_{t}^{2-\alpha}\Delta\psi+\partial_{t}|\nabla\psi|^{2}=0

\displaystyle\tau^{\alpha}\partial_{t}^{\alpha}\psi_{tt}+(1-\kappa\psi_{t})\psi_{tt}-c^{2}\Delta\psi-\tau^{\alpha}c^{2}\partial_{t}^{\alpha}\Delta\psi-\delta\partial_{t}^{\alpha}\Delta\psi+\partial_{t}|\nabla\psi|^{2}=0

\displaystyle\tau\psi_{ttt}+(1-\kappa\psi_{t})\psi_{tt}-c^{2}\Delta\psi-\tau c^{2}\Delta\psi_{t}-\delta\partial_{t}^{2-\alpha}\Delta\psi+\partial_{t}|\nabla\psi|^{2}=0

that have been analyzed in
[

.

4.4
Fractional derivatives

To clarify what we mean by
\partial_{t}^{\alpha}
in the models above, we provide a brief intermission on some of the most commonly used fractional derivative concepts.

With the Abel fractional integral operator

I_{t_{0}}^{\gamma}f(x)=\frac{1}{\Gamma(\gamma)}\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\frac{f(s)}{(t-s)^{1-\gamma}}\,ds,

a fractional (time) derivative can be defined by either

\displaystyle{}^{RL}_{t_{0}}D^{\alpha}_{t}f=\frac{d\ }{dt}I_{t_{0}}^{1-\alpha}f\quad\mbox{Riemann-Liouville derivative}\text{ or }

\displaystyle{}^{DC}_{t_{0}}D^{\alpha}_{t}f=I_{t_{0}}^{1-\alpha}\frac{df}{ds}\quad\mbox{Djrbashian-Caputo derivative}

These are nonlocal operators and have a definite starting point
t_{0}
.
The R-L derivative is defined on a larger function space that the D-C one. However, the R-L derivative of a constant is nonzero ; it even exhibits a singularity at initial time
t_{0}
.
This is why the D-C derivative, which maps constants to zero, is often preferred in physical applications.
Some recent books on fractional PDEs (also containing many classical references on fractional differentiation) are
[
26
,
40
,
44
]
.

The nonlocal and causal character of these derivatives provides the models in which they are used with a “memory”.
In the context of inverse problems, it is important to note that therefore initial values are tied to later values and hence can be better reconstructed backwards in time than in integer derivative models.

Some of the challenges in the analysis of fractional PDEs, in particular in deriving energy estimates, are as follows.

—

The chain rule identity
u\,u_{t}=\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}u^{2}
is lost ;

—

Likewise, there is no product rule of differentiation.

“Substitutes” for these devices in the fractional derivative case are

—

the chain rule inequality

{w(t)}\textup{D}_{t}^{\alpha}w(t)\geq\tfrac{1}{2}(\textup{D}_{t}^{\alpha}w^{2})(t)

for
w\in W^{1,1}(0,T)

[
2
]
;

—

coercivity of the Abel integral operator

\int_{0}^{T}\langle\textup{I}^{1-\alpha}w(s),w(s)\rangle\,ds\geq\cos(\tfrac{\pi(1-\alpha)}{2})\|w\|_{H^{-(1-\alpha)/2}(0,T)}^{2}

for
w\in H^{-(1-\alpha)/2}(0,T)

[
19
,
53
]
;

—

the Kato-Ponce inequality

\|f\,g\|_{W^{\rho,r}(0,T)}\lesssim\|f\|_{W^{\rho,p_{1}}(0,T)}\|g\|_{L^{q_{1}}(0,T)}+\|f\|_{L^{p_{2}}(0,T)}\|g\|_{W^{\rho,q_{2}}(0,T)}

for
f,\,g
such that the right hand side is finite,
0\leq\rho\leq\overline{\rho}<1
,

1<r<\infty
,
p_{1}
,
p_{2}
,
q_{1}
,
q_{2}\in(1,\infty]
, with
\frac{1}{r}=\frac{1}{p_{i}}+\frac{1}{q_{i}}
,
i=1,2
; see, e.g.,
[
22
]
.

5
Describing nonlinear wave propagation in frequency domain by
multiharmonic expansions

Motivated by the characteristic appearance of higher harmonics in nonlinear acoustics, cf. Figure
2
, we now provide a mathematical formulation of this physical fact.

To this end, we recall the linear wave equation

u_{tt}-c^{2}\Delta u=r,

(5.1)

where using a harmonic excitation
r(x,t)=\textrm{Re}(\hat{r}(x)e^{\imath\omega t})

and a harmonic ansatz for
u

u(x,t)=\textrm{Re}\left(\hat{u}(x)e^{\imath\omega t}\right)

leads to the Helmholtz equation

-\omega^{2}\hat{u}-c^{2}\Delta\hat{u}=\hat{r}.

Replacing (
5.1
) by the Westervelt equation

u_{tt}-c^{2}\Delta u-b\Delta u_{t}=\kappa(u^{2})_{tt}+r

(5.2)

and still using a harmonic excitation
r(x,t)=\textrm{Re}(\hat{r}(x)e^{\imath\omega t})
, we expect a response at multiples of the fundamental frequency
\omega
.
We therefore use a multiharmonic expansion of
u

u(x,t)=\textrm{Re}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\hat{u}_{k}(x)e^{\imath k\omega t}\right),

(5.3)

which is mathematically justified by completeness of the system
(e^{\imath k\omega\cdot})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
in

L^{2}(0,T)

as well as the fact that indeed a periodic solution to the Westervelt equation exists
[
28
]
.

Theorem 5.1
.

For
b,c^{2},\beta,\gamma,T>0
,
\kappa\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
, there exists
\rho>0
such that for all
r\in L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega)
with
\|r\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}\leq\rho
there exists a unique solution

\displaystyle u\in\,X:=H^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))\cap H^{1}(0;T;H^{3/2}(\Omega))\cap L^{2}(0;T;H^{2}(\Omega))

of

\displaystyle\begin{cases}u_{tt}-c^{2}\Delta u-b\Delta u_{t}=\kappa(x)(u^{2})_{tt}+r\quad\mbox{ in }\Omega\times(0,T),\\[5.69054pt]
\beta u_{t}+\gamma u+\partial_{\nu}u=0\quad\mbox{ on }\partial\Omega\times(0,T),\\[5.69054pt]
u(0)=u(T)\,,\ u_{t}(0)=u_{t}(T)\quad\mbox{ in }\Omega,\end{cases}

and the solution fulfills the estimate

\displaystyle\|u\|_{X}\leq\tilde{C}\|r\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}.

Inserting the ansatz (
5.3
) into (
5.2
) leads to the coupled system

\displaystyle m=1:

\displaystyle-\omega^{2}\hat{u}_{1}-(c^{2}+\imath\omega b)\Delta\hat{u}_{1}=\hat{r}{\color[rgb]{0.5,0.5,0.5}\ -\frac{\kappa}{2}\omega^{2}\sum_{k=3:2}^{\infty}\overline{\hat{u}_{\frac{k-1}{2}}}\hat{u}_{\frac{k+1}{2}}}

(5.4)

\displaystyle m=2,3\ldots:

\displaystyle-\omega^{2}m^{2}\hat{u}_{m}-(c^{2}+\imath\omega mb)\Delta\hat{u}_{m}=-\frac{\kappa}{4}\omega^{2}m^{2}\sum_{\ell=1}^{m-1}\hat{u}_{\ell}\hat{u}_{m-\ell}

\displaystyle\color[rgb]{0.5,0.5,0.5}{\displaystyle-\frac{\kappa}{2}\omega^{2}m^{2}\sum_{k=m+2:2}^{\infty}\overline{\hat{u}_{\frac{k-m}{2}}}\hat{u}_{\frac{k+m}{2}}}.

Note that the quadratic nonlinearity corresponds to a discrete autoconvolution in time.
Skipping the grey terms corresponds to the simplification of dropping
Re
in (
5.3
) and leads to a triangular system that can be solved by forward substitution in a sequence of Helmholtz problems.

6
Inverse problems

In this section we mainly focus on an innovative quantitative imaging methodology, based on some of the models in the previous sections, namely acoustic nonlinearity parameter tomography ANT.
The key motivation for this comes from the experimentally proven qualification of the B/A parameter in (
2.3
), (
2.4
), (
2.5
) or (
2.6
) as a characterizing and distinguishing material property of biological tissues.
Thus, viewing
\kappa=\tfrac{1}{\varrho c^{2}}(\tfrac{B}{2A}+1)
as a spatially varying coefficient in the Westervelt equation, it can be used for medical imaging.
While the physical and engineering literature on ANT has already been developping over many years, with initializing work going back to the 1980’s and 90’s,
[
4
,
10
,
11
,
25
,
56
]
,
mathematical results, as they are crucial for enabling any kind of advanced medical imaging technology, have here only been started off recently.
In particular, we point to
[
1
,
52
]
for results on uniqueness from the Neumann-Dirichlet map in the Westervelt equation and to
[
20
]
, which investigates and highlights the usefulness of geometric optics solutions to Westervelt equation for this imaging problem.
In our own work
[
38
,
39
,
29
,
41
,
42
]

we mainly focused on uniqueness from a single boundary observation and on convergence of Newton’s method in the Westervelt equation as well as
in
[
55
]
on linearized uniqueness and conditional stability of the inverse problem for the BCBJ model (
2.6
).

With the Westervelt equation as a forward model, the inverse problem of nonlinearity parameter imaging reads as follows.

Reconstruct
\kappa(x)
in

\displaystyle\bigl(u-\kappa(x)u^{2}\bigr)_{tt}-c_{0}^{2}\Delta u+Du=r\quad\mbox{ in }\Omega\times(0,T)

(6.1)

\displaystyle\partial_{\nu}u+\gamma u

\displaystyle=0\mbox{ on }\partial\Omega\times(0,T),\quad u(0)=0,\quad u_{t}(0)=0\quad\mbox{ in }\;\Omega

(with excitation
r
) from observations

g=u\quad\mbox{ on }\Sigma\times(0,T)

on a transducer array
\Sigma\subset\overline{\Omega}
, which we assume to be a surface or collection of discrete points.

The attenuation term
D
can be defined by one of the following common fractional models.

\displaystyle\text{Caputo-Wismer-Kelvin : }\qquad D=-b\Delta\partial_{t}^{\beta}\quad\mbox{ with }\beta\in[0,1],\ \ b\geq 0

\displaystyle\text{fractional Zener : }\qquad D=a\partial_{t}^{2+\alpha}-b\Delta\partial_{t}^{\beta}\quad\mbox{ with }a>0,\ b\geq ac^{2},\ 1\geq\beta\geq\alpha>0,

\displaystyle\text{space fractional Chen-Holm : }\qquad D=b(-\Delta)^{\tilde{\beta}}\partial_{t}\quad\mbox{ with }\tilde{\beta}\in[0,1],\ b\geq 0.

Since also the speed of sound and the attenuation coefficient depend on the underlying tissue type, they can be considered as additional unknowns in a multicoefficient inverse problem, see, e.g.,
[
42
,
29
]
.

Chances and challenges of this inverse problem result from

—

the difficulties in the analysis of the forward model (as well as its numerics) that have already been pointed to in Section
2
;

—

the fact that the unknown coefficient
\kappa(x)
appears in the nonlinear term ;

—

\kappa(x)
being spatially varying whereas the data
g(t)
is in the
“orthogonal” time direction ;
this is well known to lead to severe
ill-conditioning of the inverse problem.

On the other hand, nonlinearity helps by adding information.
In particular the generation of higher harmonics and the fact that
\kappa
appears in each of the equations in (
5.4
) shows that information multiplies due to nonlinearity.
As a consequence, it is in fact possible to achieve enhanced uniqueness results for the inverse problem formulated above, which we here illustrate by a uniqueness result for a general spatially variable coefficient from a single observation, while in the linear setting, this type of data would at most provide identifiability of piecewise constant coefficients.

6.1
Linearized and local nonlinear uniqueness

To this end, recalling (
5.4

the inverse problem in frequency domain, using the abbreviations

\vec{u}=(\hat{u}_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\quad B_{m}(\vec{u})=\frac{1}{4}\sum_{\ell=1}^{m-1}\hat{u}_{\ell}\hat{u}_{m-\ell}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=m+2:2}^{\infty}\overline{\hat{u}_{\frac{k-m}{2}}}\hat{u}_{\frac{k+m}{2}},

\text{tr}_{\Sigma}\hat{u}_{m}\end{array}\right)

y_{m}:=\left(\begin{array}[]{l}\hat{r}\text{ if }m=1\ /\ 0\text{ if }m\geq 2\\

\displaystyle F_{m}^{\prime}(\kappa,\vec{u})(\underline{d\kappa},\underline{d\vec{u}})=

\displaystyle F_{m}^{\prime}(0,\vec{u})(\underline{d\kappa},\underline{d\vec{u}})=\left(\begin{array}[]{l}-\bigl(\omega^{2}m^{2}+(c^{2}+\imath\omega mb)\Delta\bigr)\underline{d\hat{u}}_{m}+\omega^{2}m^{2}\,\underline{d\kappa}\,B_{m}(\vec{u})\\

Theorem 6.1
.

The homogeneous linearised
(at

\vec{u}=\phi(x)\vec{\psi}

m\in\mathbb{N}
