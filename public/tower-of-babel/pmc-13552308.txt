Beyond the feedback: a student-directed case-based collaborative learning Review committee
Authors: Jessica R. Newman, Danny Hernandez, Selina Gierer, Dorothy Hughes
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThe curriculum of the Doctor of Medicine program at The University of Kansas School of Medicine includes a substantial portion of content delivered via small-group, case-based collaborative learning (CBCL) activities. As critical stakeholders in continuous quality improvement in their education, medical students are poised to make contributions to curriculum enhancement, though they are often underutilized resources.MethodsWithout a faculty-led curriculum revision committee, we piloted a student-led Case-Based Collaborative Learning Review Committee (SCRC) to systematically incorporate student feedback into curriculum revision efforts by reviewing three CBCL activities. The SCRC provided structured anonymous edits and feedback to faculty authors. Qualitative (thematic) data analysis used mixed methods to assess the intervention’s impact and develop guidance for future iterations.ResultsSessions were well-received by participating students and receiving faculty based on satisfaction surveys. Thematic analysis of student-generated suggested edits revealed five primary categories of revisions: simple edits, pre-work recommendations, relevancy of social constructs, clarification, and ‘need more’.ConclusionOverall, the intervention demonstrated that student-led review was feasible and yielded valuable multi-level feedback on small-group learning activities, such as CBCL. Further research could help determine if similar work could be feasible and effective for lectures and flipped classroom material and whether such continuous quality improvement should be required in our medical education curriculum processes.

Introduction

The University of Kansas School of Medicine (SOM) Active-learning, Competency-based, Excellence-driven (ACE) curriculum is an integrated block structure based on organ systems that systematically introduces foundational and clinical content across four years. The ACE curriculum is designed to train students to navigate the evolving landscape of medical practice, understand and engage effectively in health care systems to better serve patients, competently work within interdisciplinary teams to improve health outcomes, and integrate complex technologies while maintaining a strong patient-physician relationship to promote excellence in patient care. A substantial portion of Phase I (years 1 and 2) is delivered via small-group learning activities, including problem-based learning (PBLs) and case-based collaborative learning (CBCLs), totaling 4–8 h weekly [1].

Each CBCL includes self-directed pre-work (readings and/or videos), a 2-hour small-group session led by clinical faculty using written case and discussion prompts, and a 1-hour large-group session led by the CBCL author using PowerPoint to reinforce content with additional clinical cases [1]. Delivering this content requires substantial resources: a 10–20-page document for students with clinical cases and prompts, a detailed facilitator guide, a 25- to 50-slide PowerPoint, and approximately 35 formative short answer or multiple-choice questions. CBCLs encourage learners to be self-directed by completing pre-work, and the session in total encourages learning via social constructivism, in which the medical knowledge gains are co-constructed through peer discussion. The use of authentic clinical cases and tasks reflects situated learning theory, inferring that learning is most effective and transferable when it occurs in a situation similar to that which it will be applied in the future, with directed multiple-choice questions offered post-activity to capitalize on retrieval practice. Despite the volume of material and vital nature of the content, this material is often created and reviewed by only 1 or 2 faculty authors, limiting the opportunity for improvements from other perspectives. While the block director is ultimately responsible for the content of each block, their bandwidth is also limited, as they additionally serve as educators, managers, and administrators for the course. Ultimately, in our SOM, Foundational Science Deans oversee each block’s content, though the ability to provide granular feedback and quality review for each individual learning activity is also limited by the volume of their administrative duties. While students can provide feedback on each block’s curriculum, comments on specific learning activities are diluted when combined with overall weekly or comprehensive course feedback, diminishing the insightfulness of specific comments to CBCL authors. Additionally, while authors have content expertise, time constraints and knowledge of the broader evolving curriculum can make it challenging for CBCL authors to make constructive and expeditious alterations. By comparison, students are exposed to the entirety of team-based learning activities sequentially in real time. These system limitations prompted the vision for the incorporation of current students into a student-led CBCL Review Committee (SCRC) pilot program to continuously improve CBCLs.

Medical students, critical stakeholders in continuous quality improvement in medical school education, are often an untapped resource in the process of curriculum enhancement [2]. Existing evaluations of faculty and curriculum quality may consist solely of end-of-course evaluations and questionnaires, impeding timely improvements [3]. Although faculty may view provided foundational science instruction as thorough, students may still perceive gaps in their preparation for USMLE Step exams. Burk-Rafel & Santen, et al. identified a correlation between student-identified gaps in their institution’s curriculum and lowest-scoring USMLE Step 1 categories. This study found that only 17.9% of students perceived their institution’s preclinical coursework helpful in preparing for their exam [4]. This perceived disconnect often drives students to create their own supplementary learning resources requiring extensive additional time when they are already investing nearly 80 h per week for an average of five weeks for Step 1 preparation [4]. More senior medical students, currently engaging with and applying curricular content, may offer valuable insights into which materials best prepare them for subsequent course blocks and clinical clerkships. Several studies have demonstrated including students in curricular and coursework reform not only supports alignment with national exam preparation and clinical readiness, but it enhances confidence in the curriculum’s ability to achieve these goals and benefits academic instutions [2,5–7]. Karakitsiou et al. argued that just as being a ‘good teacher’ is more than being a good lecturer, there are multiple medical student learner roles, including ‘keeping up with the curriculum’, ‘curriculum evaluator’, and ‘resource material consumer’ [8]. Recognizing these points, many medical schools have implemented student-driven curricular planning, development, and review [2,5–7,9]. Such programs have increased student input, brought institutions in closer alignment with their learning objectives, and been proven ‘instrumental’ in synthesizing class-wide reviews into actionable curricular improvement steps [2].

The proposed pilot SCRC initiative could be situated within a co-production framework, whereby students and faculty design and collaboratively refine educational experiences. The SCRC also functions as a continuous quality improvement (CQI) mechanism by providing structured, iterative, and explicit feedback on CBCL materials, addressing limitations of traditional block evaluations that may dilute and over generalize specific learning activity feedback. Together, co-production and CQI offer a conceptual foundation for understanding how student-led review processes could enhance curriculum quality, strengthen student–faculty partnerships, and support curricular refinement and the development of future educators. Reflective practice models offer a third relevant theoretical lens for this initiative. The capacity to critically examine past experience to generate insight and improve future performance, reflection has been recognized as a core professional competency in medical education [10]. As SCRC members revisited CBCLs they had previously completed as novice learners, they were prompted to critically re-examine content from a more advanced stage of clinical understanding, surfacing gaps not readily apparent to faculty. This structured retrospective process parallels Kolb’s experiential learning cycle, in which concrete experience, completing the CBCL the prior year, was followed by reflective observation and abstract conceptualization (revising) before active application (submitting feedback to faculty for review) [11]. By embedding reflection into the review process, the SCRC positioned students not only as curricular contributors but as active practitioners of reflective, practice-based learning and improvement. While student curriculum review groups which provide course-level feedback and suggestions have been described previously, there is limited data on feasibility or efficacy of teams’ capabilities in editing specific learning activities. This study describes a novel approach to real-time student involvement in curricular change through structured review of individual case-based collaborative learning activities [12]. The objectives of this study were threefold: 1) to pilot a novel, student-led CBCL Review Committee (SCRC) to assess the feasibility of structured, real-time student review of individual small-group learning activities; 2) to characterize, through thematic analysis, the types and cognitive complexity of feedback generated by student reviewers; and 3) to evaluate faculty and student committee members’ perceptions of the review process’s value, constructiveness, and equity of partnership.

Materials and methods

A mixed-methods non-experimental observational pilot study was conducted at our School of Medicine within the Undergraduate medical pre-clinical curriculum. A novel SCRC with three CBCLs were utilized to bring student feedback to the forefront, maximizing its potential to improve these learning activities for future learners. CBCLs were chosen via purposive convenience sampling to represent different years and content areas. Two CBCLs were chosen from year one (one from Molecular and Cellular Medicine and one from Infection, Blood, and Immunity) and one from the year 2 block Brain, Mind and Behavior. Our qualitative outcomes were themes of suggestions from the SCRC. Our quantitative outcomes were the perceived value of the SCRC review as articulated by CBCL authors and associated block directors and satisfaction ratings from participating student committee members collected via a 5-point Likert scale survey.

Data collection tools and techniques: to pilot our novel SCRC intervention, faculty, and student comments from each of three pilot CBCLs were compiled by the Office of Medical Education into Excel documents for each case and provided for student review. There were two participant groups: medical student volunteers were recruited via email from a student leader in the SOM surgical and internal medicine near-peer teaching programs. Eligibility: students who had completed the blocks in which the pilot CBCLs fell. After receiving a detailed explanation of the study purpose via email, verbal informed consent was obtained from students prior to their participation in the pilot given the small participant number. Prior to their first session, each student was also required to complete an asynchronous pre-learning reinforcing the purpose and goal for CBCLs in the SOM curriculum. Participant group size: during each of the SCRC meetings, which took place between September and December 2023, four to six student members along with one faculty facilitator (one of the PIs) reviewed one CBCL over a two-hour period. The facilitator began the session by opening the small group document in tracked changes for suggested edits and comments to preserve student anonymity. One student served as record-keeper, adding the edits and comments while other students examined feedback and the cases and suggested edits. The facilitator served to keep time, encourage participation, and answer questions about content via the small group facilitator guide but did not make independent suggestions for edits. The edited small-group document was then returned to the CBCL author and block director for review as a ‘tracked changes’ version along with comments related to any suggested modifications for pre-work learning and/or associated large-group activities which were not included in the small group document, or more overarching suggestions for the small-group itself (Appendix Figure A1).

The tracked changes documents from the SRCR were uploaded to Dedoose (10.1.5) [13], and the research team (3 coders) used a content analysis approach, conducting inductive coding to identify patterns, develop a codebook, then organizing codes into themes and subthemes. Final themes agreed upon at consensus meetings and were listed in order of cognitive difficulty, with spelling and grammar first and more complex topics last. Any coding discrepancies among the three researchers were resolved through discussion until consensus was reached, and the codebook was iteratively revised throughout the coding process to reflect emerging patterns using Dedoose.

Our quantitative research questions addressed by a Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap) survey for faculty, and paper survey, for students were: 1) Would CBCL faculty (author and block director) find SCRC review beneficial to the curriculum, beneficial to the reviewed CBCLs, and constructive; and secondarily, how would they feel about the students’ role in CBCL review? Also, 2) What would SCRC student member perceptions be of their influence on curricular change, how reviewed CBCLs may benefit from their work, and their role in the review process; and, secondarily, how would their evaluations differ from those of their peers? The survey was developed by the authors and not previously validated.

The first quantitative question was addressed using data from a Likert-style REDCap survey for CBCL authors and block directors. The second quantitative question was evaluated using data from a Likert-style paper survey distributed to student committee members. Descriptive statistics (frequencies and means) were used to summarize Likert-scale responses for both the faculty and student surveys. Ethical approval for this study was obtained from the University of Kansas Medical Center Institutional Review Board who deemed the project exempt from full committee review under the designation of quality improvement.

Results

Six medical students participated in the pilot SCRC committee, collectively dedicating six hours to review three CBCLs from separate curricular blocks. All students had completed at least their M1 year and therefore had previously completed the curriculum they were reviewing. CBCL feedback and edit suggestions were provided to each of the three CBCL authors and associated block directors. Perception of the committee’s utility from both student participants and faculty receiving feedback was positive. Based on an average score of 3.86 on a 5-point scale, faculty felt the curriculum benefited from SCRC review. With a score of 4 out of 5, faculty felt their specific CBCL benefited from review and that the review was constructive. The majority of faculty answered the final question, scoring 3.67 out of 5 that the SCRC student members were equal partners in the review process. These scores indicated that faculty were generally receptive to student suggestions on how to improve CBCL content and suggested that it could be worthwhile continuing the SCRC review process into the future.

The SCRC student members felt positive overall about their leadership in this review process. They felt their influence changed the curriculum (3.67), that the curriculum benefited from their efforts (3.83), and that they were equal partners with faculty in curricular improvement (3.83). The students also indicated a high level of consensus, with an average score of 1.83 on the question asking whether they felt their evaluation differed from that of their peers. These scores suggest that students had similarly positive perceptions of this process to faculty, and continued student engagement in such a review process could be possible.

In our analysis of types of suggested changes from the student committee, five primary themes emerged, two with sub-themes (listed in order of progressive cognitive complexity): Edits-Typos, Pre-Work, Relevancy of Social Constructs, Clarification, and Need More. The theme, Clarification included four sub-themes: Reorder/Reorganize, Format, Add or Draw Attention to Learner Tools, and Rephrasing. The most complex theme was Need More and included six sub-themes: Elaboration on Existing Tasks, Additional Tasks, Additional Facilitator Guidance, Additional Case, Slides, and Visual. The themes Edits-Typos and Pre-Work were simple in nature. Students felt that minor spelling or grammatical mistakes should be corrected to maximize accuracy. They also made recommendations for improving pre-work, not only recommending the addition of material that students should complete prior to arriving at CBCL activities, but also recommending changes to the length, complexity, or duration of those materials to increase the likelihood that students would complete pre-work and come to the learning activity better prepared.

The theme Relevancy of Social Constructs included thoughtful suggestions related to the relevancy of a wide range of concepts, from demographic information to social determinants of health. Students would sometimes recommend the removal of references to these concepts, whereas in other cases they recommended more meaningful engagement.

Clarification’s four sub-themes were Reorder/Reorganize, Format, Add or Draw Attention to Learner Tools, and Rephrasing. These types of suggested changes differed from Edits-Typos in that they were larger and sometimes more complicated. For example, in a CBCL there are several tasks students must complete, and the SCRC sometimes felt those tasks could be posed in a different sequence to build knowledge more effectively. The SCRC also drew attention to mnemonics and other learner tools that the CBCL could use to make case information and questions clearer.

Need More was by far the most complex theme, with the sub-themes Elaboration on Existing Tasks, Additional Tasks, Additional Facilitator Guidance, Additional Case, Slides, and Visual. The SCRC found that some tasks in CBCL merited further explanation or more detail so that students could effectively complete them. The three ‘additional’ sub-themes (tasks, facilitator guidance, and case) focused on either breaking down material that was too large and needed to be tackled in smaller chunks or providing more information to students beyond just clarifying. The sub-themes Slides and Visual indicated that SCRC students thought faculty could better illustrate concepts and reasoning by adding those types of materials.

Discussion

Student participation in curriculum development can positively impact institutional culture, generate actionable suggestions for curricular improvement, and increase interest in academic medicine. Our findings extend this literature by demonstrating the feasibility and perceived value of a student-led committee which also focused on real-time, activity-level review of case-based collaborative learning sessions. Despite notable benefits for both students and faculty, only a minority of schools have implemented a formal structure that guarantees real-time student involvement in curricular reform [5–7,9,12,14–16].

Student curriculum partnerships can improve general student satisfaction and trust between students and faculty. Harvard was successful in launching Pathways Education Representatives (Ed Reps) in their new curriculum to foster a continuous quality improvement partnership between students and faculty. Ed Reps has cultivated, and organized student and faculty feedback related to learning activities and participated in other SOM related initiatives. In a survey of 222 students, 91% reported the Ed Reps had a positive impact on the curriculum and made real-time improvements to courses. Similarly, 81% and 91% of faculty in academic year 2015 and 2016, respectively, felt the committee had a positive impact on the curriculum in facilitating change, and 88% felt the committee had a positive impact on learning activities specifically [17]. A Student Workgroup on Medical Education (SWME) was established at one Belgian institution to provide feedback on design, contribute ideas, and participate in future curriculum planning [9]. The SWME formed solutions at the student level and posed issues to the faculty-led educational committee. When surveyed, students reported participation fostered ethical decision-making and problem-solving skills [9]. In Gheihman et al’.s exploratory study of programs utilizing clinical-year curricular coproduction, they found that 99% of both student and faculty survey respondents reported benefit from coproduction models, regardless of the level of partnership [12]. These perceived improvements included those in the program culture and increased student learning experiences in medical professionalism. Perelman School of Medicine reported improvement in their ability to implement changes based on student feedback when they shifted from relying on course evaluation surveys to requiring faculty and administrators to gather weekly in-person feedback from peer-elected students [3]. Co-production positioned learners as essential partners rather than passive recipients, a perspective also reflected in our finding that students felt like ‘equal partners in the review process’. Both faculty (3.67/5) and students (3.83/5) rated students as equal partners in the review, suggesting the SCRC succeeded in repositioning students from passive recipients of curriculum to active co-developers, consistent with prior co-production literature describing similar shifts in perceived partnership [10]. Consistent with a CQI model, the SCRC provided structured, iterative, activity-level feedback that addressed a key limitation of block-level evaluations which led to dilution of activity-specific comments within broader course feedback. The five thematic categories identified, ranging from simple corrections to substantive elaboration or the addition of new material, demonstrated that this structured process yielded the kind of granular, actionable input which CQI processes were designed to generate. Faculty ratings that specific CBCLs benefited from review (4/5) and that feedback was constructive further support the alignment between iterative review structure and the practical utility of its output.

Finally, the progression of student-generated themes from simple (spelling, pre-work) to complex (elaboration, additional cases) suggested that as students engaged more deeply with previously completed material, their feedback moved from surface correction toward substantive reflection on gaps in their own prior learning, consistent with reflective practice models in which retrospective, critical examination of experience yields increasingly sophisticated insight.

Student curriculum groups can also generate explicit practical feedback. Faculty at Johns Hopkins University described recommendations by Student Curriculum Review Team realistic and actionable. In this pilot, faculty case authors rated the committee feedback as uniformly constructive. Additionally, faculty and block directors who received feedback from the SCRC found benefit. Within the three pilot sessions students were able to escalate from lower cognitive difficulty edits, including identifying missed spelling and grammatical errors, to more complex subjects such as social determinants, clarifications of task questions and background information and even suggestions for additional cases or tasks to help reinforce concepts from objectives that were addressed in the CBCL summary large group didactic or in related areas of the course.

Our pilot SCRC was feasible to enact with a small student group volunteering their time and finding short-term benefit in their contribution. There was minimal faculty effort required, for each meeting one faculty member (who was not a content expert on the topic) facilitated the student group, serving as a timekeeper and resource for student questions. This effort could be reframed depending on faculty interest as the facilitator position could be performed by graduate-level learners, residents or even trained senior medical students. A novel aspect of this committee was its focus on providing faculty with specific, actionable edits using material commentary and ‘tracked changes’. This resulted in a more streamlined and efficient review process for specific learning activities, rather than more inexplicit global course-level feedback.

An additional benefit of student involvement in curriculum review and improvement is that this deeper dive into academic medicine may lead the student to be an active participant in medical education as a practicing physician. The University of Illinois College of Medicine-Chicago developed a Student Curricular Board that led to increasing the interest in academic careers in medicine in addition to improving preparedness for that career path for the students involved [12]. In our small pilot, student participants commented ‘Students participating in CBCLs will appreciate knowing that students worked to improve them and that their input matters’ and that ‘Participating in student education and medical education is something I hope to be involved in after residency. Participating now in curriculum review feels really rewarding’.

Though this is notably a small pilot program in one SOM, given feasibility, and positive feedback, the committee work will be expanded in the coming AY. Similarly, in our pilot SCRC members commented ‘Having an input in the curriculum is important. I think more students should be involved and student actively participating in CBCLs will appreciate knowing students worked to improve them and their input matters’ and ‘I really enjoy helping with CBCL review. Participating in student education and medical education is something I hope to be involved with after residency/medical school. Participating now in curriculum review feels really rewarding!’

There were some notable limitations to this study. Regarding scale, this project was small pilot, with voluntary participation, which only evaluated three CBCL activities in the pre-clinical curriculum. Flipped classroom activities, lectures and PBLs were not trialed, thus applicability to other styles of learning activities may not be as feasible. Although this pilot examined the feasibility of only three CBCL review sessions, we proposed a cadence for reviewing each CBCL over a series of several years, with the possibility of expanding to include additional learning activities if sufficient volunteer student and facilitator interest emerged. Additionally, this program was completed at a single academic institution, which may limit applicability to other institutions or programs. Furthermore, the degree to which suggested edits were included in the next iteration of the block, and comparisons in learning outcomes for related small group questions were not ascertained. Future research should examine multiple teaching modalities across additional medical schools to better assess the broader applicability of this student committee model on program culture, as well as to consider objective evaluation of learning outcomes and the overall educational impact of such a review committee on the curriculum.

Conclusion

Student participation in curriculum development has shown a positive impact on culture, enriched learning experiences, and impactful exposure to medical education. Our Student CBCL Review Committee (SCRC) received positive feedback about the committee’s utility. Students reported that participation empowered them to make meaningful curriculum contributions, to communicate complex concepts more effectively, and enhanced their learning experience overall. Initiatives like the SCRC could provide a better understanding of student learning and introduce students to the rewarding experience of participating in medical education as practicing physicians. The committee is unique in its goals, investment in continuous quality improvement of learning activities, and via the depth of involvement in curriculum change for students.

As we expand the work of the committee in the coming AY, a faculty lead was established to organize CBCLs for review and scheduling student participants and faculty facilitators. Three students are participating in this organization of the group including facilitating student engagement, scheduling appointments, and organizing committee documents and feedback for review further increasing their leading roles in medical education. Participation in the SCRC will now be recognized as a qualifying activity for a Leadership and Medical Education Certificate of Distinction. Opportunities for further study include determining the feasibility at additional institutions using case-based collaborative learning and investigating the degree to which SCRC suggested edits to materials are made, including study of attitudes and perceptions of edits not chosen to not be incorporated. In the time since this project’s inception, the use of large language models in the creation and review of educational materials has grown substantially. Future work might explore whether AI-assisted review can complement or enhance stakeholder-driven review processes such as the one described here, particularly with respect to efficiency, though relative value of human stakeholder engagement, including collaborative and professional development benefits warrants further investigation. Additionally, it would be prudent to determine if committee review/improvement of learning materials improves learning outcomes on the related material. Given its feasibility, positive reception, and the substantial opportunity for flexible learning activity review by invested students, we recommend similar committees at other Schools of Medicine.
