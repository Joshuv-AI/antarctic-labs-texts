Severe hypercalcemia in a cat following treatment with antibiotic impregnated calcium sulfate carrier material for chronic pyogranulomatous panniculitis
Authors: Chris Greiner, Lauren Retallack, H Grady Bailin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCase summaryA 3-year-old, female spayed domestic shorthair with 7 months of pyogranulomatous panniculitis was referred after culture isolation of Actinomyces species. Despite initial improvement, clinical resolution was not achieved with the dosage and duration of clindamycin therapy previously reported as adequate for actinomycosis. Surgical resection with antibiotic impregnated calcium sulfate carrier material for local delivery of ampicillin-sulbactam was pursued. The patient developed severe hypercalcemia (21.6 mg/dl, reference interval 7.8–11.3) after surgery, resolving after 7 days of 0.9% saline diuresis and bisphosphonate therapy. Mycobacterium abscessus was isolated from tissue culture. After 5 months of clarithromycin therapy, clinical resolution of mycobacteriosis panniculitis was achieved.Relevance and novel informationThis case documents the management of a potential complication of calcium sulfate carrier material, a local antibiotic delivery material that is increasingly used for chronic soft and osseous tissue infection management. This case documents the management of a medically refractory case of infectious feline panniculitis specifically as a result of complex bacterial culture interpretation specific to rapid-growing non-tuberculosis mycobacterium.Keywords: Hypercalcemia, calcium sulfate, panniculitis, mycobacteria, actinomyces

Introduction

Calcium sulfate hemihydrate (CaSO4) is a biocompatible material originally proposed as a bone defect filling agent that has found use as a carrier material for auto-resorbing pharmaceutical delivery, particularly in the release of local antimicrobial therapy to treat chronic infections.1–9 After CaSO4 implantation, symptomatic hypercalcemia is a rarely reported complication in humans.10–17 This complication in clinical veterinary cases has yet to be published.

Feline panniculitis is frequently reported secondary to bacteria of the class Actinomycetes – that is, Actinomyces, Mycobacterium and Nocardia.18–25 These bacteria manifest chronic pyogranulomatous nodular inflammation with draining tracts, whose treatment requires months of antibiotic therapy and, frequently, surgical debridement. Despite a similar clinical appearance, treatments vary between the genera because of marked differences in antibiotic susceptibility.18–23

This case report documents the association of severe hypercalcemia after implantation of antibiotic impregnated CaSO4. In addition, this case documents feline panniculitis with both Actinomyces species and Mycobacterium abscessus culture isolates, highlighting a complex infectious disease diagnosis.

Case description

A 3-year-old, female spayed domestic shorthair cat presented to her family care veterinarian in November 2023 for evaluation of abdominal alopecia and crusting. The cat lived indoors only with no other pets. Relevant medical history included a jejunotomy in December 2022 to remove obstructive foreign material and negative retroviral testing in January 2023. A preliminary diagnosis of superficial pyoderma was made, although nodular subcutis was also noted. Between November 2023 and February 2024, the cat received three injections of cefovecin and tapering prednisolone for abdominal pruritus with self-excoriations. A hydrolyzed protein diet was prescribed alongside a 1-month course of prednisolone (1.3 mg/kg PO q48h) for suspected allergic dermatitis. Remission of the clinical signs was achieved for approximately 1 month.

Signs recurred in March 2024. Prednisolone was restarted and amoxicillin-clavulanate (16.7 mg/kg PO q12h, Clavamox; Zoetis) prescribed but discontinued owing to diarrhea. In April 2024, pending referral to a dermatologist, a draining wound with thickened subcutaneous tissue was identified. A commercial laboratory (IDEXX) aerobic culture isolated Actinomyces species. Clindamycin (6.7 mg/kg PO q12h) was prescribed with an intended minimum duration of 8 weeks.

At the first dermatology consult in June 2024, the owner reported a 75% reduction in lesions. On examination, the cat’s ventral abdomen had 8–10 focal hemorrhagic crusts with hemopurulent draining tracts to subcutaneous fibrosis. A skin-scrape cytology found pyogranulomatous inflammation with vacuolated macrophages and possible branching hyphae. Oral amoxicillin prescriptions at doses from 41.3 mg/kg PO q8h to 27.5 mg/kg PO q12h were trialed but met with intolerable gastrointestinal adverse effects. Clindamycin per os was restarted with topical clindamycin added. An August 2024 recheck found unchanged draining tracts with subcutaneous thickening in a ‘Y’ configuration. Repeat cytology documented persistent pyogranulomatous inflammation with vacuolated macrophages. Topical oxytetracycline/polymyxin B (q12–24h, Terramycin; Zoetis) replaced topical clindamycin and surgical debridement was recommended because of insufficient resolution with medical management.

On 16 September 2024, the patient underwent en bloc resection of the abdominal lesions and placement of CaSO4 for local aminopenicillin therapy. A ‘Young Wellness’ biochemistry panel with complete blood count (Catalyst One Chemistry Analyzer, ProCyte Dx Hematology Analyzer; IDEXX) was performed before anesthesia (Table 1), finding a mild normocytic, normochromic, non-regenerative anemia (hematocrit 26.9% [reference interval (RI) 28.2–52.7]) and a mild elevation in symmetric dimethylarginine (15 μg/dl [reference interval (RI) 0–14]) with normal total protein (6.6 g/dl [RI 6.3–8.8]) and albumin (3.0 g/dl [RI 2.6–3.9]). This panel did not include serum total calcium or phosphorus. A commercial CaSO4 carrier material (Kerrier; Kerrier) was combined with the recommended quantity of ampicillin-sulbactam: approximately 11.6 g of CaSO4 beads were closed within the wound bed. Subsequent in vitro studies documenting short elution times of ampicillin from calcium sulfate have prompted the manufacturer to discontinue its recommended use.26,27 The patient was discharged without immediate complication the following day.

Tissue samples were submitted to an academic diagnostic laboratory (Michigan State University Veterinary Diagnostic Laboratory). Hematoxylin and eosin staining identified multifocal to coalescing pyogranulomatous panniculitis and dermatitis with intralesional filamentous bacteria (Figure 1a). Follow-up Gram (Figure 1b), acid-fast (Figure 1c) and periodic acid-Schiff staining found weak Gram-positive and weak acid-fast positive filamentous bacilli within the centers of pyogranulomatous foci. Fungal organisms were not observed with periodic acid-schiff staining. The aerobic culture isolated M abscessus, with susceptibility sensitivities to linezolid, amikacin, cefoxitin and clarithromycin. No growth occurred in anaerobic and fungal cultures.

On postoperative day 9, the patient presented to the emergency service of the referral hospital for hyporexia and lethargy since discharge. On examination, the patient was found to be severely dehydrated with 12% body weight loss. Hematology (ProCyte Dx Hematology Analyzer; IDEXX) found hemoconcentration and a mild leukocytosis (white blood cells 20.15 k/μl [RI 2.87–17.02, neutrophils 16.46 k/μl [RI 2.30–10.29], monocytes 1.05 k/μl [RI 0.05–0.67]). Serum biochemical analysis (Catalyst One Chemistry Analyzer; IDEXX) found severe total hypercalcemia (>16 mg/dl, 21.6 mg/dl on serum dilution [RI 7.8–11.3]), confirmed via venous blood gas (Element POC; Antech Diagnostics) with ionized hypercalcemia (2.77 mmol/l [RI 1.21–1.51]), with normal phosphorus (6.6 mg/dl [RI 3.1–7.5]). In addition, a mild-moderate azotemia (creatinine 1.88 mg/dl [RI 0.5–1.9], blood urea nitrogen 77 mg/dl [RI 16–36]) was identified alongside an increase in serum total protein (8.6 mg/dl) from baseline with unchanged albumin (Table 1). Urinalysis after 12 h of intravenous fluids found a urine specific gravity of 1.020 with occasional granular casts plus slight (2–5/hpf) pyuria and hematuria. The cat was admitted to the hospital for 0.9% saline diuresis with venous blood gas monitoring, nasogastric tube placement for enteral nutrition and medical support, including continued clindamycin therapy. During hospitalization, incision effusion was identified and a partial dehiscence of the surgical site developed (Figure 2), passively removing an unquantified volume of CaSO4.

On day 3 of hospitalization, zoledronate 0.2 mg/kg IV was administered for persistent severe ionized hypercalcemia (1.91 mmol/l) despite rehydration and resolved azotemia. Clindamycin was discontinued and empiric clarithromycin 18.8 mg/kg PO q12h was started based on the preliminary aerobic culture. On day 7 of hospitalization, the patient’s ionized calcium was within the reference interval (1.45 mmol/l), her appetite was normal and the surgical site adequately healed for at-home bandage changes. The patient was discharged that day.

At follow-up 1 week later, the patient’s ionized calcium remained normal (Table 1). Her ventral abdomen healed over the next 3 months, and clarithromycin was continued for an additional 2 months, discontinuing in March 2025. In November 2025, the patient was acutely vomiting and diagnosed with a gastrointestinal foreign body obstruction. An emergent gastrotomy was performed to relieve the obstruction; no abnormalities were found incidental to the laparotomy. As of December 2025, the owner reports the patient has returned to a normal routine after the healing of the incision, with clinical resolution of granulomatous panniculitis.

Discussion

Hypercalcemia after application of CaSO4 in humans is typically transient and asymptomatic, but a symptomatic hypercalcemia incidence of 0.27–0.9% is reported, occurring within 1–2 weeks of application. This results in days of hospitalization for calcium correction through saline diuresis, bisphosphonate and/or calcitonin therapy, and occasionally dialysis.10–17 This complication is associated with parathyroid hormone (PTH) suppression, normal PTH-related peptide (rp), normal calcidiol, low calcitriol and calcium dysregulating comorbidities including renal disease, calciuresis-inhibiting medications, disuse osteoporosis and excessive calcium intake.10–17,28 The proposed mechanism of hypercalcemia is the rate of elemental calcium (1 g of calcium sulfate hemihydrate contains 276.1 mg of elemental calcium) releasing from the CaSO4 exceeding the patient’s calcium excretory and sequestration capacities.10–15,17 The quantity of CaSO4 implanted is not consistently associated with hypercalcemia.10,11,14,17,28 Pharmacokinetic data of implanted CaSO4 in healthy cats are limited: Maxwell et al 5 found 4/6 cats developed mild aclinical hypercalcemia (median total calcium 10.7 mg/dl [RI 8.8–10.2) with implanted elemental calcium doses of approximately 10.0 mg/kg. This patient received an estimated elemental calcium dose of approximately 880 mg/kg with concurrent mycobacteriosis granulomatous panniculitis, a potential calcium-dysregulating condition.29–31 Similar severe hypercalcemia in one cat after implantation of CaSO4 without obvious calcium dysregulating comorbidities was presented as an abstract at the 2025 ACVS Surgery Summit. 32

Regarding this current case, administration of zoledronate was extrapolated from human studies of CaSO4-associated symptomatic hypercalcemia where the calcium dysregulating comorbidities are addressed with bisphosphonates.10–16 In cats, bisphosphonates have demonstrated a favorable short-term safety profile and serum calcium reduction for idiopathic, chronic kidney disease and granulomatous hypercalcemia.33–36 Further study is needed to determine whether bisphosphonate therapy is indicated in cats with CaSO4-associated hypercalcemia in addition to saline diuresis with supportive care.

Actinomyces is a genus of genetically heterogeneous bacteria defined by a shared phenotype – Gram-positive, non-acid-fast, branching, sometimes filamentous, bacillus–which are normal mucosal flora.18,37–39 M abscessus is a rapid-growing non-tuberculous mycobacterium—weak Gram-positive, weak acid-fast positive, bacillus – which is a ubiquitous opportunistic pathogen that can mimic actinomycosis in presentation.20–22,24,40 Both laboratories attempted matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight for identification but neither achieved an acceptable match. The commercial laboratory (IDEXX) used Gram-stain morphology for identification and the academic laboratory (Michigan State University Veterinary Diagnostic Laboratory) performed extended protein extraction for identification. Initial misidentification of M abscessus for Actinomyces species, is likely as M abscessus can express overlapping phenotypic characteristics with aerotolerant Actinomyces species, and phenotypic methods of identification are less accurate for both genera.18,20,37,41–43 Similar misidentification of rapid-growing non-tuberculous mycobacterium for actinomycosis has been reported in a dog. 43

Limitations of this report include lack of evaluation of the cat’s serum calcium before her September 2024 surgery as well as lack of evaluation of serum PTH, PTHrp and calcidiol/calcitriol. Actinomyces and Mycobacterium-associated granulomatous disease have been identified as causes of mild hypercalcemia (ionized calcium ⩽1.67) in cats due to macrophage production of calcitriol and PTHrp.29–31,44,45 Based on this case report, it cannot be stated that the CaSO4 implantation was the sole cause of the severe hypercalcemia as granulomatous disease is a potential precipitating factor. The patient’s normal total calcium serum biochemistry in December 2022 and November 2025 ruled out incidental idiopathic hypercalcemia. The owner denied exposure to cholecalciferol and unwitnessed toxin ingestion was considered unlikely.

Conclusions

Severe hypercalcemia may be a complication of CaSO4 implantation in cats. CaSO4 usage in cats should include careful perioperative monitoring of serum calcium, and preoperative PTH, PTHrp, calcidiol/calcitriol assessment should be considered if there are comorbidities known to impact calcium regulation. Further study into the incidence rate of CaSO4-associated hypercalcemia in cats is warranted, with a focus on precipitating patient factors.

Feline actinomycosis and mycobacteriosis panniculitis may appear similar. A definitive diagnosis of the infective agent can prove difficult even with positive culture isolates and histopathologic evaluation. Persistent ‘actinomycosis’ lesions in cats, despite appropriate antibiotic therapy, should be re-interrogated for the presence of rapid-growing non-tuberculous mycobacterium.
