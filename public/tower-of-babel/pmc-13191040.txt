What is the meaning of R in AMR surveillance?
Authors: S Peter Borriello
Published: 2026

The term AMR (antimicrobial resistance) is used extensively, not only among professionals in human and veterinary medicine and environmental and food microbiology, but also by policy makers, official national and international bodies, non-governmental organizations (NGOs) and the public. Despite its widespread use, most do not realize that the term resistance has multiple meanings. The definition used for resistance of food-borne pathogens (FBPs) surveillance differs from that used for clinical surveillance of the same pathogens. Consequently, it is difficult to compare the two datasets, and erroneous conclusions can be reached about the risk of acquisition of untreatable infection. These definitions and meanings will be described in terms of bacterial resistance (although it would be equally applicable to other microbes). First, the meaning that is generally understood is that the bacterial infection is unlikely to be treatable by usual therapeutic doses of the antibiotic to which resistance is expressed. In this case resistance is determined by the clinical breakpoint (CBP). It can also mean something unrelated directly to human clinical medicine, and used to describe a decreased susceptibility (due to acquisition of a resistance mechanism) compared with that expected for the least susceptible of the wild-type population of that bacterial species. This is referred to as an epidemiological cut-off value (ECOFF) to distinguish it from a CBP. The setting of CBPs is more complex than that of determining ECOFFs, requiring an integration of the wild-type susceptibility range of a pathogen, assessment of the pharmacodynamics (antibiotic effects over time)/pharmacokinetics (changes in antibiotic concentrations over time), and experience of clinical outcome when a particular antibiotic is used to treat infection with a known pathogen. The purpose of the CBP is to determine the likelihood of treatment success (the strain is susceptible) or failure (the strain is resistant) when the antibiotic is used at the recommended dose for a site of infection.

In general, ECOFF resistance values tend to be lower than CBPs. This discordance between values can range from close, for example Campylobacter jejuni and tetracycline where the ECOFF is >1 mg/L whereas the CPB is >2 mg/L, to major differences: for example Salmonella enterica and meropenem where the ECOFF is >0.125 mg/L whereas the CBP is currently >8 mg/L.

Finally, with the increasing availability of veterinary clinical breakpoints it can mean that the infection caused by that veterinary pathogen for a given animal species is unlikely to respond to treatment with that antibiotic.

This variable definition of resistance/resistant leads to misunderstanding, particularly among policy makers and NGOs, and among the public when these specialist publications are reported on. An attempt to exemplify this is presented in Box 1, which is a fictitious conversation between a Minister and a senior Civil Servant based in style on a British Broadcasting Corporation comedy series called ‘Yes Minister’. Alhough light-hearted, it is making an important point related to clarity of definition and understanding of a scientific term. Some commissioners of AMR surveillance have received resistance data and published reports on that resistance on the understanding it meant clinical resistance, unaware that an ECOFF definition of resistance was used (or its distinction from a CBP), and the determination of resistance did not necessarily equate to clinical resistance. The use of the term resistance/resistant in official reports based on ECOFF values is common among food safety agencies. This is often due to their lack of awareness of the need to stipulate a requirement for CBPs to be assessed, and that providers of surveillance data to food safety agencies are usually those involved in environmental and/or veterinary microbiology, where ECOFFs have been traditionally used (particularly due to the difficulty of setting veterinary CBPs for such a variety of animal species). As awareness increases that a distinction between resistance based on ECOFFs and that based on CBPs needs to be made, some have adopted the approach of making a qualifying statement in official reports that those microorganisms reported as resistant are not necessarily clinically resistant (for example, the European Food Safety Authority, EFSA, and more recently the UK Food Standards Agency). Where the problem becomes most acute is in One-Health type reports: particularly those reporting susceptibility data on FBPs isolated from food-producing animals, food and patients. EFSA has taken the approach of comparing ECOFFs with combined human isolate CPBs for resistance and for intermediate resistance (now referred to as increased dose susceptible) data, whereas the UK One-Health report compares the ECOFF and CBP data, but draws attention to the distinction. The joint Swedish Veterinary Antimicrobial Resistance Monitoring and Swedish Antibiotic Sales in Human Medicine reports make clear that direct comparisons of resistance are hampered because different interpretative criteria are used for bacteria from humans and animals, but have partially circumvented the problem by using human CBP data for some animal derived Escherichia coli to compare resistance rates with human isolates.

The European Committee on Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing, in trying to distinguish between ‘resistance’ determined by ECOFF values and clinical resistance have developed and defined the term ‘microbial resistance’ for resistance derived from ECOFF values, which is used in EFSA reports but is unlikely to be helpful for general improved understanding by non-specialists (see Box 1). To a large degree, relatively convoluted approaches have recently been taken to prevent general misunderstanding of the term ‘resistance’ in official surveillance reports on FBPs and disclaimers made in those reports that the use of the term resistant does not necessarily mean clinical resistance. It would be more appropriate and informative for commissioners of AMR surveillance data to stipulate that providers submit quantitative data and indicate both ECOFF and CBP values. This would allow presentation of both clinical resistance and general changes in susceptibility of the population of that bacterial species, and facilitate comparisons with clinical AMR data.

For future consideration of terminology, there could be merit in distinguishing the different meanings of resistance by, for example, having the designations epidemiological resistance, clinical resistance and veterinary resistance. There could also be merit in ensuring that, when professional committees give consideration for appropriate distinguishing nomenclature for this important word, customers of resistance data and experts in communication of science are also involved.
