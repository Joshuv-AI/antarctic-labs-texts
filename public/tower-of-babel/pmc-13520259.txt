Microfracture effectively fills majority of talus osteochondral lesions at 4–6 months in patients with concomitant chronic lateral ankle instability: a second-look arthroscopy study of staged surgery
Authors: Zhidong Zhao, Shengxuan Li, Pengtao Shi, Chen Yang, Zhijiang Li, Yanpeng Zhao, Qi Quan, Min Wei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMicrofracture (MF) is the first-line treatment strategy for Osteochondral lesions of the talus (OLT), while its clinical outcomes have been reported, the early-stage (4–6 months) healing effects of MF for OLT has been poorly characterized. This study investigated the early MF repairing outcomes in patients with concurrent OLT and CLAI treated with staged surgery.MethodsWe retrospectively evaluated 34 consecutive patients with concomitant OLT and CLAI who underwent staged surgery, consisting of first-stage MF for OLT and second-stage modified Brostrom-Gould repair with second-look arthroscopy. Repair quality was assessed using the Ferkel and Cheng staging system and the International Cartilage Repair Society (ICRS) score. Preoperative and postoperative magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) was evaluated using the Hepple classification. Clinical outcomes were assessed using the American Orthopaedic Foot and Ankle Society (AOFAS) and visual analog scale (VAS) scores. Spearman correlation analysis was used to examine associations among preoperative variables, postoperative clinical scores, and second-look repair grades.ResultsAll lesions showed effective filling at second-look arthroscopy; however, four patients with contiguous lesions involving zones 4 and 1 underwent revision MF because of poor integration in anterior zone 1. According to the Ferkel and Cheng system, repair quality was Grade A in 2.9%, Grade B in 70.6%, Grade C in 20.6%, and Grade D in 5.9% of cases. MRI demonstrated nearly intact cartilage surfaces with persistent subchondral bone edema. Hepple classification showed a trend toward improvement (Z = − 1.83, p = 0.070). AOFAS and VAS scores improved significantly by the second procedure. Preoperative subchondral bone cysts and lesion number were not significantly associated with repair quality. No significant correlation was found between arthroscopic repair grades and clinical scores at early follow-up.ConclusionMF achieved satisfactory early filling in most OLTs at 4–6 months in patients with concomitant CLAI undergoing staged surgery. However, integration in anterior zone 1 warrants particular attention in lesions extending across zones 4 and 1.Case seriesLevel of evidence, 4.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13018-026-06879-0.

Introduction

Osteochondral lesions of the talus [1–10] comprise a common condition that significantly affects quality of life and activity levels, particularly in younger individuals. Achieving superior therapeutic outcomes through minimally invasive techniques is a key goal for both patients and physicians [11]. Currently, microfracture is the first-line treatment for talus osteochondral lesions, with satisfactory short- and mid-term clinical results [12–14]. Meanwhile, the sustainability of the repaired tissues remains a concern has been recognized in the field for it primarily consists of fibrocartilage rather than native hyaline cartilage [2, 15]. Under such circumstances, various augmentation methods have been explored to enhance the repair efficacy of microfracture for talus osteochondral lesions [16–21]. Therefore, understanding the dynamic repair process of microfracture for talus osteochondral lesions and identifying potential influence factors, especially the early-stage, may provide clues for targeted intervention, optimizing treatment outcomes and improving clinical decision-making.

Second-look arthroscopy allows direct visual assessment of repaired tissues and is a crucial tool for evaluating microfracture efficacy [22–25]. Previous second-look arthroscopic studies [26, 27] conducted at mean follow-up intervals of 1 and 3.6 years, examined the durability of microfracture repair and reported a 36% incomplete healing rate which strongly correlated with clinical American Orthopaedic Foot and Ankle Society (AOFAS) scores. However, if it was the primary repair outcome or a secondary effect of postoperative activity remains unclear. There is a specific lack of data on early follow-up in existing studies, which further highlights the necessity of this research and fills the existing evidence gap in related fields.

Clinically, over 33% of patients with chronic lateral ankle instability (CLAI) have concomitant OLT [28–30]. Staged surgery is an established management approach in the literature, and comparative studies have reported differences in functional outcomes and operative time between staged and single-stage management [31]. During the study period (2015–2018), staged surgery was the treatment strategy used in our clinical practice for patients with concomitant OLT and CLAI. Therefore, a series of cases of concomitant OLT and chronic lateral ankle instability (CLAI) received staged surgery (first-stage arthroscopic microfracture for OLT, second-stage modified Brostrom-Gould ligament repair with second-look arthroscopy), which provide a valuable opportunity to investigate the early-stage healing effects of MF.

The present study aimed to characterize the early-stage (4–6 months) repair of OLT treated with microfracture in patients with concurrent CLAI undergoing staged surgery. Specifically, we described (1) macroscopic fill and integration via second-look arthroscopy, (2) concurrent magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) morphology, and (3) preliminary patient-reported and radiographic outcomes. We also investigated potential factors influencing repair outcomes and correlations between repair grades and clinical scores.

Materials and methods

Ethical approval and patient selection

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the XX Hospital (S2021-637-01). A cohort of 34 patients with concurrent talus osteochondral lesions and chronic lateral instability who underwent staged surgery between 2015 and 2018 were included (Fig. 1). The first arthroscopic inspection, debridement of the OLT and microfracture procedures were performed, followed by 4–6 months of rehabilitation. All patients then underwent modified Brostrom-Gould ligament repair during which second-look arthroscopy was conducted to evaluate the quality of microfracture repair.Fig. 1A total of 38 patients were assessed for eligibility, 4 were excluded, and according to the surgical approach, 34 patients were included in the final analysis

A total of 38 patients were assessed for eligibility, 4 were excluded, and according to the surgical approach, 34 patients were included in the final analysis

The inclusion criteria were patients with CLAI who met the International Ankle Consortium standards [32]. All patients had a history of at least one significant ankle sprain, with the initial injury occurring ≥ 12 months prior and the most recent episode > 3 months before enrollment. All reported ankle “giving way,” recurrent sprains, and/or sensations of instability. They were between 18 and 60 years old and had a positive an anterior drawer test. OLT was confirmed by radiograph, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), and physical examination and had clear clinical symptoms that did not respond after 3 months of nonsurgical treatment (including immobilization, brace, and rehabilitation). The foot alignments were normal. In all cases, the lesion size was no more than 150 mm2 (diameter ≤ 15 mm). The included patients received the staged procedure.

Exclusion criteria comprised patients with systematic inflammatory, metabolic (e.g., gout), or rheumatic diseases, as well as those patients with a history of ankle joint injections. No restrictions were set on concurrent tibial lesion (talus alone or combined with tibial lesion), or the presence of subchondral bone cysts. All the patients received the second arthroscopy surgery and there is no lost to follow up. All surgical procedures (both first-stage microfracture and second-look arthroscopy) were performed by a single experienced surgeon. Intraoperative measurement of osteochondral lesion size was independently conducted by two senior experienced surgeons using a custom-made calibrated probe (1.0 mm scale). In cases of inter-observer discrepancy (> 0.2 cm2), the two surgeons re-evaluated the lesion together to reach a consensus, and the final value was used for analysis.

Figure 1 shows the flow diagram of this study.

Data collection

Patient demographics and clinical data, including age, gender, injury history, injury mechanism, body mass index (BMI), duration of symptoms, lesion location, and lesion size, were recorded. Preoperative imaging, including ankle X-rays, computed tomography, and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), was conducted before both the first and second surgeries.

Surgical techniques and postoperative rehabilitation

Surgical procedure

Following preoperative preparation, standard anteromedial and anterolateral portals were established. A comprehensive arthroscopic inspection of the joint was performed to assess osteochondral lesions, lateral ligament injuries, and other conditions. A limited synovectomy was conducted to expose the lesions.

Unstable cartilage and osteochondral fragments were debrided until a stable cartilage rim was identified. Perpendicular articular margins were created using a ring-shaped curette to resect lesion edges. Microfracture awls with different angles were used to perform subchondral bone penetrations at approximately 3–4 mm intervals and depths. Bone marrow bleeding and fat release were assessed. After completing the procedure, the portals were closed, and an ankle brace was applied in the neutral position.

Second-stage surgery

Second-stage surgery was performed 4–6 months after the first-stage microfracture, as planned during initial treatment.

Preoperative evaluation included physical examination (anterior drawer test, talar tilt test), ankle MRI, and clinical scoring (AOFAS, VAS) to assess ligament instability, cartilage repair status, and functional outcomes. The same anteromedial and anterolateral portals from the first-stage surgery were used to avoid additional tissue trauma. First, a comprehensive arthroscopic inspection was performed to evaluate the repair quality of OLT (filling rate, integration with native cartilage, macroscopic appearance) using Ferkel-Cheng and ICRS classification systems. After completing the second-look assessment, modified Brostrom-Gould ligament repair was performed via an open surgical technique, the lateral collateral ligament complex was dissected, shortened, and reinserted into the fibular tip with suture anchors (3.0 mm, Arthrex, USA) to restore lateral ankle stability. The inferior extensor retinaculum was then advanced to reinforce the repaired ligament. Revision microfracture was performed in cases of poor integration. The procedure included debridement of unstable repair tissue, creation of fresh perpendicular articular margins with a ring-shaped curette, and re-performance of subchondral bone penetrations using microfracture awls. Abrasion arthroplasty was additionally performed for severely unstable tissue, involving gentle abrasion of the lesion surface to promote bone marrow bleeding and integration (as shown in Fig. 3F, K-L).

Postoperative rehabilitation

Standardized rehabilitation began on the first postoperative day, following protocols used in previous studies [13, 33]. Passive ankle flexion and extension (Towel stretch and Stepping on the hard board: about 20 times per day from postoperative day 1, gradually increasing to 600 times per day by week 4) was conducted from day 1 to 4 weeks after procedure. Active joint ROM exercise, Muscle strength exercise and Gait training were conducted from 5 to 12 weeks. Muscle strength training, Balance, proprioception, and mobility training were subsequently introduced. A protective brace was used for 4 weeks, with partial weight-bearing permitted at 6 weeks. The brace was gradually removed over 12 weeks. All patients were permitted to resume daily life activities 4–6 months after the first arthroscopy.

Arthroscopic, clinical, and radiological evaluations

Arthroscopic images and video recordings from both procedures were analyzed. Ferkel and Cheng [34] and ICRS [35] arthroscopic classifications were used for grading, with assessments performed at two time points: (1) First-stage surgery (pre-microfracture): baseline OLT grading and initial lesion size measurement; (2) Second-stage surgery (second-look arthroscopy): post-repair grading. Intraoperative lesion size was independently measured by two senior attending surgeons using a custom-made calibrated probe (1.0 mm scale). For inter-observer discrepancies > 0.2 cm2, consensus was reached via joint re-evaluation, and the final value was adopted. All grading was conducted by two independent observers blinded to preoperative findings, with kappa analysis for inter-observer agreement. Lesion location was recorded using a nine-box grid (Fig. S1).

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) was performed at two time points: (1) 1 week before first-stage surgery (baseline) for Hepple classification [18]; (2) 1 week before second-stage surgery (4–6 months after first stage) to evaluate radiological repair outcomes.

Clinical assessments included American Orthopaedic Foot and Ankle Society (AOFAS) scoring and visual analog scale (VAS) for pain, recorded at two time points: (1) Baseline: 1 week before first-stage surgery; (2) Pre-second-stage surgery: 1 week before the second procedure.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was conducted using SPSS version 22 (IBM Corp.). Normality was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Normally distributed data are presented as mean ± standard deviation, whereas non-normally distributed data are expressed as median (minimum–maximum).

Paired t tests were used to compare preoperative and postoperative AOFAS scores. The Mann–Whitney U test was applied to compare VAS scores, Ferkel and Cheng staging, and Hepple classification between first- and second-look arthroscopy. Benjamini–Hochberg was used for control multiplicity adjustment. Spearman correlation analysis was performed to assess the relationships between preoperative variables and postoperative repair grades (Ferkel and Cheng staging and ICRS grades). The kappa consistency test was used to assess the agreement between two blinded evaluators regarding the grading of osteochondral lesions of the talus on magnetic resonance imaging and during arthroscopy surgery. A two-sided p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient demographics and preoperative data

A total of 34 patients with OLT and concomitant CLAI managed with staged surgery were enrolled, including 15 (44.1%) males and 19 (55.9%) females. The mean age was 38.82 ± 13.06 years (range: 16–60), and the mean BMI was 26.81 ± 3.98 kg/m2 (range: 20.6–35.8). The mean lesion size was 0.89 ± 0.34 cm2 (range: 0.4–1.5). Symptom duration ranged from 12 to 120 months.

Of the patients with OLT and concomitant CLAI managed with staged surgery, 34(100%) had a traumatic injury history (e.g., sprain and falls). Equal numbers of patients with OLT and concomitant CLAI managed with staged surgery (n = 17, 50%) had left and right ankle involvement.

A total of 22 (64.7%) patients had a single-zone osteochondral lesion, whereas 12 (35.3%) patients had lesions in two or more zones. Among these, six patients had continuous injury zones, while the remaining six had separate lesions. Zone 4 was the most commonly affected area (91.2%).

A total of 17 (50%) patients with OLT and concomitant CLAI received staged surgery had subchondral bone cysts. The kappa values for preoperative and postoperative MRI assessment of Hepple classification were 0.813 and 0.822. Based on the Hepple classification, 11 (32.4%) patients had grade 2A lesions, five (14.7%) patients had grade 2B lesions, one (2.9%) patient had grade 3 lesions, and 17 (50%) patients had grade 5 lesions. Discussion

The most important finding of this study was that fibrocartilage can effectively fill the majority of micro-fractured OLT by 4–6 months in patients with concomitant CLAI who undergo staged surgery. Preoperative factors such as subchondral bone cysts, BMI, and the number of osteochondral lesions did not significantly influence the general repair outcomes. However, incomplete integration between repaired tissues and native cartilage in anterior zone 1 in cases with lesions extending across zones 4 and 1 may catch our attention. Postoperative MRI at the early stage exhibited an intact cartilage surface with residual subchondral bone edema, whereas Hepple classification showed no significant changes before and after surgery.

This study focused on microfracture due to its widespread use in treating talus osteochondral lesions. While previous studies have evaluated its long-term clinical outcomes and repair durability [12, 26], the early-stage repair effects remain poorly understood. This study was conducted in patients with OLT and concomitant CLAI who underwent staged surgery [36–39]. This staging strategy provided a valuable opportunity to evaluate talar osteochondral repair outcomes and facilitate timely intervention when necessary. Meanwhile, we must be aware that the unsolved ligament instability may have potential influence on osteochondral lesion healing. Specifically, the frayed, unstable tissues between newly repaired and native tissues primarily observed in anterior zone 1 in cases with concurrent osteochondral defects in zones 1 and 4 was a crucial reason for revision which has not been previously reported. One possible explanation is that zone 1 experiences high contact stress during ankle dorsiflexion and extension. Furthermore, concurrent lateral ankle instability may contribute to altered ankle motion trajectories and biomechanical changes [40]. Prior studies have shown that patients with concomitant chronic ankle instability and osteochondral lesions of the talus exhibit increased dorsiflexion and eversion moments, along with elevated anterior and medial ankle joint reaction forces during stair descent [28, 41]. These findings suggest that addressing both ankle instability and osteochondral lesions simultaneously may be beneficial. Certainly, one-staged procedure strategy was widely adopted nowadays [31, 33, 42].

The investigated time points of 4–6 months after microfracture were relatively uniform in the cohort, providing a robust basis for characterizing the early-stage repair outcomes. According to the current findings, preoperative factors such as BMI, age, and subchondral cysts did not significantly influence the healing effects of microfracture, indicating its early-stage repair efficacy. The BMI (≥ 30 vs. < 30 kg/m2) and age (≥ 60 years) groupings were referenced from previous studies [12, 43, 44]. Although prior research has suggested that BMI and age may serve as prognostic factors for clinical outcomes after microfracture, our results are not contradictory. In the current short-term study (4–6 months), preoperative factors (e.g., BMI, age, subchondral bone cysts) did not significantly impact the early filling effect of microfracture. However, previous long-term studies have reported that higher BMI and advanced age are associated with reduced durability of repaired fibrocartilage over 5–10 years [26]. Thus, it is plausible that these factors primarily affect long-term tissue durability rather than the initial repair phase, but this hypothesis requires confirmation through long-term follow-up of the current cohort.

AOFAS and VAS scores of patients with OLT and concomitant CLAI managed with staged surgery showed significant improvement from the first to the second procedure. Additionally, the arthroscopic repair outcomes observed in the second-look procedure did not correlate significantly with clinical scores, which contradicts previous studies suggesting a close relationship between the quality of repaired tissue and clinical scores [26]. This discrepancy may be attributed to differences in the time points investigated.

Meanwhile, we have to acknowledge the limited reliability of AOFAS scoring system. A key limitation of this study is the reliance on AOFAS and VAS for clinical outcome assessment. The AOFAS score was developed decades ago and has well-documented limitations: it overemphasizes objective joint range of motion and radiographic findings while underrepresenting patient-reported symptoms. Additionally, its responsiveness to early cartilage repair is poor compared to ankle-specific PROMs such as FAOS. This may explain why we observed no significant correlation between arthroscopic repair grades and clinical scores the AOFAS may not capture subtle improvements in cartilage function or residual symptoms related to integration defects. Future studies evaluating early cartilage repair should incorporate validated PROMs to better align objective repair findings with patient-reported outcomes.

Previous second-look arthroscopy studies [26, 27] conducted at a mean of 12 months and 3.6 years reported 36% of lesions as incompletely healed. Synthesizing these findings, we speculate that incomplete healing occurs later in the usage phase rather than during the initial repair phase. This insight highlights the necessity to monitor the dynamic changes of microfracture-mediated repair processes and identify potential influencing factors for targeted intervention.

Postoperative MRI results revealed abnormal subchondral bone signals in the treated zone than preoperative signals, likely representing immature tissue following microfracture. The trend toward improvement in Hepple classification (p = 0.070) suggests that radiological repair may be ongoing at 4–6 months, and longer follow-up is needed to confirm whether this trend translates to significant structural improvement. These findings are consistent with prior studies reporting persistently high MRI signals in the long term [45–47]. Nevertheless, the relationship of “filled defect” of MRI with ankle functional success need further elucidation, especially the long-term data.

Overall, early-stage second-look arthroscopy provides a direct assessment of microfracture-mediated repair effects in the staged treatment context. While this study focuses on the filling effects of microfracture, previous research has examined the durability of repaired tissue. These findings are complementary and contribute to a broader understanding of microfracture efficacy. Ultimately, it is important to note that the population of patients who underwent two-stage surgery in the current study comprised only a subset of cases between 2015 and 2018, and we capitalized on this opportunity to characterize the repair outcomes. During the study period, staged surgery was an established management approach in literature and was used in our clinical practice.

This study has several limitations. First, as a retrospective case series of 34 patients, it is subject to selection bias and limited statistical power. However, given that concurrent talar osteochondral lesions and lateral ligament injuries account for over 33% of ankle sprains [48], the number of patients undergoing early-stage (4–6 months) second-look arthroscopy is relatively scarce. This study provides valuable insights into the early repair effects of microfracture. Second, due to the limited sample size, subgroup analyses could not be conducted to obtain additional statistical findings. However, despite the small sample size, this study identified critical factors that may play a vital role in enhancing microfracture repair efficacy, which is valuable for clinical practice. Third, the unaddressed CAI in staged surgery may lead to the poor integration in anterior zone 1 in patients with OLT and concomitant CLAI managed with staged surgery with lesions extending from zones 4 to 1. Moreover, the effects of early-stage unsatisfactory integration in anterior zone 1 on mid- and long-term functional scores as well as the underlying mechanisms and potential targeted modifications, require further clarification.

Conclusion

Microfracture effectively fills the majority of talus osteochondral lesions (OLT) in the early stage (4–6 months) in patients with concomitant chronic lateral ankle instability (CLAI) who undergo staged surgery. However, the integration of repaired tissue in anterior zone 1 requires special attention in patients with contiguous OLTs involving zones 4 and 1. Its generalization to patients with isolated OLT or single-stage surgery needs further elucidation.
