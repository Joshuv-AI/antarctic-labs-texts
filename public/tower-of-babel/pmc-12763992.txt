Clinical outcomes after switching from omalizumab to anti-IL-5/IL-5R biologics in severe asthma: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Fatma Arzu Akkuş, Fatih Çölkesen, Tuğba Önalan, Mehmet Emin Gerek, Mehmet Kılınç, Şevket Arslan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSevere asthma (SA) is a heterogeneous disease composed of various clinical phenotypes, and criteria for selecting the most appropriate biological agent remain unclear. Therefore, when optimal control cannot be achieved with the initial biological therapy, switching between biological drugs is often performed.MethodsThis real-world study evaluated patients with severe asthma who initiated omalizumab treatment. Based on their treatment response, patients were divided into two groups: those who continued omalizumab and those who switched to mepolizumab or benralizumab. Clinical data were evaluated before biological treatment, after omalizumab therapy, and following the second biological therapy. Additionally, factors influencing the decision to switch and the effectiveness of the switch were analyzed.ResultsOf the total 51 patients, 45.1% (n = 23) switched to a second biological agent due to inadequate response to the initial treatment. In the “Switch” group, baseline forced expiratory volume in one second (FEV₁) levels were significantly lower, while blood eosinophil counts (BEC) and exacerbation frequency were higher (p < 0.05). Although limited clinical improvement was observed after omalizumab treatment, significant improvements in asthma control test (ACT) scores, FEV₁, BEC, exacerbations, and hospitalizations were noted following the second biological therapy (p < 0.001). Low baseline FEV₁, high BEC, and frequent exacerbations were the main predictors of the decision to switch.ConclusionsIn severe asthma patients who do not achieve adequate control with omalizumab, switching to interleukin-5 (IL-5) targeted biological therapies may provide clinically significant improvements. Baseline clinical parameters can serve as useful predictors for the need to change biological treatment.Keywords: Benralizumab, Biologic therapy, Eosinophils, Mepolizumab, Omalizumab, Severe asthma, Switching

Introduction

Severe asthma (SA), is a heterogeneous disease comprising various clinical phenotypes [1]. When identifying individuals expected to benefit from treatments targeting type 2 pathways, clinical features, biomarker profiles, and comorbidities should be carefully evaluated [2]. Based on these assessments, patients must be classified precisely. However, some SA patients may be eligible for biological therapies targeting either the allergic or eosinophilic phenotypes. Although personalized treatment is the ultimate goal, the initially selected therapy may not fully eliminate the risk of exacerbations and asthma symptoms in all patients [3].

There are multiple biological agents with overlapping eligibility criteria for the treatment of SA [4]. For patients who meet prescription criteria for several biologics, particularly those with allergic phenotypes and elevated blood eosinophil levels, there is no clear guideline on how to choose the most appropriate agent. Clinical features such as nasal polyps or allergic rhinitis may help to better characterize the patient phenotype and predict clinical response to biologics [5]. Available biologic treatment options for patients in Turkey include monoclonal antibodies targeting immunoglobulin E (IgE) (omalizumab, available in Turkey since 2008), interleukin-5 (IL-5) (mepolizumab, since 2019), and the IL-5 receptor (benralizumab, since 2023). Omalizumab is the first biological agent approved for the treatment of severe allergic asthma associated with high total serum IgE levels in our country. A previous study suggested that omalizumab could be considered as a first-line treatment due to its demonstrated efficacy and safety [6]. Although the efficacy of omalizumab in severe asthma has been demonstrated, some patients exhibit a poor response and therefore require alternative biological therapies [7]. The efficacy of a biological agent should be assessed after 4 to 6 months of treatment based on parameters such as asthma control, exacerbation history, and lung function. However, there are no clear guidelines to determine the most effective biological alternative or the optimal timing for switching therapies [5].

In this study, we aimed to evaluate real-world data of patients who either continued omalizumab treatment due to an adequate clinical response or switched to mepolizumab or benralizumab because of an inadequate response to initial omalizumab therapy.

Methods

Study design

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Necmettin Erbakan University (approval no: 2025/5663). Between January 2015 and January 2025, 97 patients who initiated omalizumab as the first biological agent for SA at the Department of Adult Immunology and Allergy were screened. Since the focus of this study was on switching biological therapies, patients who discontinued omalizumab before the introduction of mepolizumab, those with incomplete data records, and those lost to follow-up were excluded. A total of 51 patients were included: those who continued omalizumab treatment with an adequate clinical response and those who switched to mepolizumab or benralizumab due to inadequate response to omalizumab. Patients continuing omalizumab were designated as the “Omalizumab-continued” group, while those who switched to mepolizumab or benralizumab were defined as the “Switch” group.

Patients

The study population consisted of patients with SA whose asthma remained poorly controlled despite at least 6 months of daily maximal inhalation therapy combined with other controller medications or oral corticosteroids (OCS) in the previous year [8]. Omalizumab treatment was initiated in patients with total serum IgE levels ranging from 30 to 1500 IU/mL and evidence of sensitization to at least one perennial allergen. Patients who experienced frequent severe exacerbations and/or uncontrolled symptoms after at least 6 months of omalizumab treatment were considered to have omalizumab failure and subsequently switched to mepolizumab or benralizumab. Prior to starting mepolizumab, patients had blood eosinophil counts of at least 150 cells/µL at baseline and at least 300 cells/µL in the previous 12 months; for benralizumab, a minimum blood eosinophil count of 300 cells/µL was required before initiation. Biological treatment outcomes were evaluated after at least 6 months following the switch.

Patients who were switched from omalizumab to a different biological therapy were those who demonstrated inadequate treatment response based on parameters such as exacerbation frequency, symptom control, lung function tests, and patient satisfaction. In patients with insufficient control under omalizumab, a clinical observation period of approximately one month was implemented following the sixth dose, during which treatment response was evaluated. Patients who did not achieve adequate clinical benefit were then switched to mepolizumab or benralizumab.

Measurements

Demographic data (age, gender, comorbidities, asthma history, smoking habits) were obtained from patient files, and laboratory test results along with clinical parameters were accessed from electronic medical records. Laboratory data included blood eosinophil count (BEC) and total serum IgE levels. Atopic status was assessed based on sensitization to perennial allergens demonstrated by skin prick tests or specific serum IgE measurements. The Asthma Control Test (ACT) was used to evaluate asthma control over the past 4 weeks. An ACT score of ≤ 19 is considered clinically significant for poor asthma control [9]. Pulmonary function test (PFT) parameters recorded included forced expiratory volume in one second (FEV1) and forced vital capacity (FVC).

Asthma exacerbations were defined as episodes requiring outpatient treatment with systemic corticosteroids (intramuscular, intravenous, or oral) or hospitalizations due to asthma. Hospitalizations were classified as cases where asthma was the primary diagnosis. Regular oral corticosteroids were not used; exacerbations were managed with short courses of systemic corticosteroids in line with local clinical practice.

Statistical analysis

Data entry and statistical analyses were performed using SPSS for Windows, version 18.0 (SPSS Inc. Chicago, IL, USA). Prior to analysis, the accuracy of the data was verified, and missing or extreme values were assessed. The normality of distribution for continuous variables was evaluated using both visual methods (histograms, probability plots, and Q-Q plots) and analytical methods (Shapiro–Wilk test). For descriptive statistics, non-normally distributed continuous variables were summarized as medians with interquartile ranges (IQR). Categorical variables were presented as frequencies (n) and percentages (%). Comparisons between groups were conducted using the Mann–Whitney U test for non-normally distributed continuous variables and the chi-square (χ²) test for categorical variables. For comparisons of paired data, the Wilcoxon Signed-Ranks test was applied. To identify potential predictors of switching from omalizumab to a second biologic, logistic regression analysis was performed. The results were expressed as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). The goodness-of-fit of the logistic regression model was assessed using the Hosmer–Lemeshow test. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant in all analyses.

Results

Clinical characteristics

A total of 51 patients who initiated omalizumab as their first biological treatment for SA were included in the study. Among these, 28 patients (54.9%) demonstrated a sufficient clinical response to omalizumab and continued the treatment (“Omalizumab-continued” group). The remaining 23 patients (45.1%) switched to mepolizumab or benralizumab due to inadequate response to omalizumab (“Switch” group). In the “Switch” group, total duration of biological treatment, baseline blood eosinophil count (BEC), and the number of exacerbations in the last 6 months were significantly higher, whereas forced expiratory volume in one second (FEV₁) was significantly lower.

Clinical factors associated with switching from omalizumab

When clinical factors related to switching biologic therapy were analyzed, baseline FEV₁ (L) was identified as a significant determinant in the decision to switch from omalizumab to other biologic agents (Table 3). Each one-unit increase in FEV₁ was associated with an approximately 18-fold decrease in the likelihood of switching (OR: 18.2, p = 0.002). Conversely, each one-unit increase in exacerbation frequency and BEC increased the probability of switching by 1.99-fold and 1.014-fold, respectively (OR: 1.152 and 1.005; p = 0.014 and 0.001, respectively).

Treatment outcomes in patients switched to second biologic

In patients who “Switch” group, significant improvements were observed in clinical and laboratory parameters both after omalizumab treatment and following the second biologic agent. Initially elevated BEC decreased after omalizumab therapy and showed a markedly greater reduction following the second biologic treatment (p < 0.001). Similarly, statistically significant improvements were noted in ACT scores, FEV₁, FVC, exacerbation frequency, and hospitalizations after both treatments (p < 0.001). Discussion

Biologic agents provide effective treatment options for SA. However, disease control may remain suboptimal in some patients despite biologic therapy, with recurrent exacerbations occurring. Consequently, switching between biologics may be necessary. In this study, we demonstrated that switching to mepolizumab or benralizumab after insufficient response to first-line omalizumab treatment could lead to improved clinical outcomes. Although patients in the “Switch” group showed significant improvements in BEC, ACT scores, FEV₁, FVC, exacerbation rates, and hospitalizations following omalizumab, these improvements were clinically insufficient, warranting a switch. The transition to IL-5 pathway-targeted therapies was found to be both statistically and clinically effective. In the “Switch” group, the significantly higher number of exacerbations in the 6 months preceding omalizumab treatment, along with significantly lower baseline FEV₁ (L), suggests that asthma was more severe in this group. Low baseline FEV₁, higher exacerbation number, and elevated BEC were identified as independent predictors for the necessity of switching to an alternative biologic after omalizumab.

In addition to inadequate asthma control, comorbidities such as chronic rhinosinusitis with nasal polyps (CRSwNP) have been reported to influence the decision to switch between biologics [10]. Previous studies have demonstrated that patients who switched biologic treatments exhibited higher sputum and blood eosinophil counts as well as lower FEV₁ values [11, 12]. Similarly, in our study, the “Switch” group showed significantly higher BEC and lower FEV1 (L) compared to the group continuing omalizumab. Furthermore, a prior study reported that patients who switched biologics experienced more frequent exacerbations and had more severe disease [10]. Similarly, in this study, the “Switch” group had a higher number of exacerbations during the 6 months prior to baseline and a longer total duration of biologic treatment. These results suggest that patients requiring a switch may have higher levels of T2 high inflammation and, consequently, more severe disease. Therefore, BEC may serve as a potential biomarker for assessing disease severity in this context.

In this study, baseline parameters such as low FEV1, high exacerbation count, and elevated BEC were found to be associated with an increased likelihood of switching to a second biologic after omalizumab. Our findings are consistent with existing literature, as BEC -a diagnostic marker of type 2 inflammation- is known to be associated with asthma exacerbations [13]. A previous study also demonstrated a significant relationship between exacerbation frequency and decline in lung function among patients with SA. It has been suggested that recurrent exacerbations may accelerate the loss of pulmonary function [14]. The association between decreased FEV1 and increased likelihood of switching in this study suggests that patients with better lung function in the early stages may have a higher probability of achieving clinical control with omalizumab. Moreover, a high exacerbation frequency not only reflects treatment insufficiency but may also accelerate lung function decline, thereby influencing the need to switch biological therapies. Although these three variables showed significant associations in univariate analysis, their significance was lost when evaluated together in multivariate analysis. Nonetheless, these findings suggest that the response to biological therapy is multifactorial and highlight the importance of considering multiple parameters collectively in clinical decision-making.

A previous study demonstrated that patients with severe eosinophilic asthma responded favorably to mepolizumab, regardless of prior omalizumab use. This response was associated with a reduction in exacerbations and oral corticosteroid (OCS) use, along with improvements in asthma control and quality of life [15]. Other studies investigating the efficacy of mepolizumab in patients whose asthma was inadequately controlled with omalizumab have also reported significant improvements in asthma control [16, 17]. Similarly, a separate study on switching from omalizumab to benralizumab found that benralizumab had beneficial effects on symptom control, lung function, annual exacerbation rates, and BEC [18]. In this study, the number of patients receiving benralizumab in our cohort was limited, precluding definitive conclusions regarding comparative efficacy. Notably, NK cell dysfunction has been observed mainly in severe forms of asthma [19]. However, benralizumab induces antibody-dependent cell-mediated cytotoxicity, leading to depletion of eosinophils via activation of natural killer (NK) cells [20]. These distinct pharmacodynamic and immunological mechanisms may partly explain potential differences in clinical outcomes between benralizumab and other IL-5–targeted therapies.

Omalizumab has been reported to reduce BEC in patients with SA and to be particularly effective in reducing exacerbations among those with high eosinophil levels [21]. In this study, a reduction in BEC was observed following omalizumab treatment in patients who underwent switching; however, this decrease became markedly more pronounced after transitioning to mepolizumab or benralizumab. Additionally, although improvements in ACT scores, FEV₁, FVC, exacerbation rates, and hospitalization frequency were observed after omalizumab in the “Switch” group, these improvements did not reach clinically meaningful levels for most patients. Consequently, a change in therapy was required, and treatment with mepolizumab or benralizumab led to statistically and clinically significant improvements in these parameters. These findings suggest that switching from omalizumab to IL-5–targeted biologics may result in clinically meaningful benefits in patients with high BEC and overlapping allergic and eosinophilic asthma phenotypes.

This study has several limitations, including its retrospective design and small sample size. The limited sample size, in particular, may have affected the results of the multivariate analyses. Another important limitation is that the decision to switch treatment was at the discretion of the treating physician, and some patients were initiated on omalizumab during a time when alternative biologics were not yet available. In addition, fractional exhaled nitric oxide (FeNO) data were not routinely available due to the retrospective design, which may have limited further assessment of type 2 inflammation. These factors may have influenced clinical decision-making and treatment responses. Despite these limitations, we believe that the findings of this real-world study are valuable. The proportion of SA patients who continue with their first biologic therapy versus those who switch remains unclear. Therefore, larger real-life studies are needed to better understand patient-related factors contributing to differences in treatment decisions and responses.

Conclusions

In conclusion, some patients with SA may present with characteristics that make them eligible for more than one type of biologic therapy. When an adequate response is not achieved with first-line treatment, switching between biologics may lead to better clinical outcomes. In line with this, our study found that transitioning from anti-IgE therapy to mepolizumab or benralizumab was an effective treatment option for SA patients whose disease was not controlled with omalizumab. Furthermore, switching from omalizumab to IL-5–targeted therapies was performed safely after a 1-month washout period in cases of uncontrolled disease. This study demonstrates that initiating a second biologic agent can improve clinical outcomes when the initial biologic fails to provide sufficient response.
