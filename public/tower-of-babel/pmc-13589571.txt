Mosquito diversity, range-expanding species, and host-feeding patterns across Virginia’s heterogeneous landscapes
Authors: John A Muller, Krisangel López, Joanna M Reinhold, Manette Tanelus, Chloé Lahondère, Sally E Paulson, Chad E Mire, Albert J Auguste
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractUnderstanding how mosquito community, host use, and environmental drivers vary across landscapes is critical for predicting vector‑borne disease risk. Virginia spans multiple ecoregions and broad elevational gradient, yet statewide surveillance remains limited and concentrated near urban centers. To address surveillance gaps, we surveyed mosquitoes across four ecologically distinct sites in Virginia during 2022 to evaluate species diversity, bloodfeeding patterns, and environmental predictors of occurrence. Using CDC light traps, gravid traps, and supplemental aspiration, we collected 7,781 mosquitoes representing 34 species. Community composition varied significantly among sites: the Coastal Plain exhibited the greatest abundance and richness, while montane and urban sites supported lower but distinct assemblages. Several medically important and invasive species—including Aedes albopictus (Skuse), Aedes japonicus (Theobald), Culex pipiens/quinquefasciatus, Culiseta inornata (Williston), and the range-expanding Culex coronator (Dyar & Knab) and Culex nigripalpus (Theobald) were detected, providing new regional records beyond previously documented localities. Bloodmeal analysis from 18 specimens revealed predominantly mammalian feeding, with white‑tailed deer (Odocoileus virginianus [Zimmermann]) comprising over 80% of identified hosts across taxa. Avian and amphibian hosts occurred at lower frequencies, indicating diverse host–vector interactions relevant to enzootic transmission. Random forest models identified elevation and trap type as the strongest predictors of species-specific occurrence, with climatic factors exerting secondary effects. Together, these findings show that mosquito distribution and host selection in Virginia are shaped by interacting ecological and environmental drivers, underscoring the need for expanded, ecologically informed surveillance to improve assessments of vector‑borne disease risk in the region.

Introduction

Mosquito surveillance plays a vital role in public health management and vector control for several reasons (Schwab et al. 2018, Wilson et al. 2020). Foremost is disease prevention, as mosquitoes are vectors for a variety of pathogens, including malaria parasites, dengue, Zika, West Nile (WNV), Eastern Equine encephalitis, and yellow fever (Foster and Walker 2019, Madewell 2020, Wilson et al. 2020) viruses. Monitoring mosquito populations allows health authorities to detect early signs of outbreaks, enabling intervention and implementation of disease prevention strategies (da Cruz Ferreira et al. 2017). Surveillance also provides critical data on the distribution and abundance of mosquito species. As climate change alters mosquito distribution and breeding patterns, surveillance becomes even more critical for detecting shifts in disease risk and adapting public health strategies accordingly (Laporta et al. 2023, Muller et al. 2024).

To understand mosquito ecology, behavior and roles in their ecosystem, it is essential to characterize their feeding patterns (Takken and Verhulst 2013). Feeding patterns can inform efforts to track and control the spread of vector borne diseases such as malaria, dengue, and Zika (Omondi et al. 2015). Knowledge of mosquito host feeding patterns can allow public health officials to design more effective mosquito control programs (Reddy et al. 2011). Tracking feeding patterns also allows epidemiological research to better trace disease outbreaks and increase understanding of transmission dynamics.

Virginia encompasses five distinct ecoregions including the Coastal Plain, Piedmont, Blue Ridge, Valley and Ridge, and the Appalachian Plateau. Elevation in the state ranges from sea level in the east to 1,746 meters at Mount Rogers. This broad elevational gradient offers a valuable natural setting to study how geographic variation influences the distribution of vectors. The Virginia Department of Health conducts mosquito surveillance centered around urban centers such as Virginia Beach, Richmond, and Fairfax (Virginia Department of Health 2019). Given the ongoing impacts of climate change and expanding ranges of vector species, there is a critical need for statewide surveys that inform public health strategies and support the development of effective risk assessments (Loetman 2024). Therefore, we conducted mosquito surveys at four sites during 2022 using a combination of Gravid traps, CDC light traps, and opportunistic vacuum aspirations. We also linked these surveys to various environmental variables to determine what abiotic factors influence distribution and abundance of various mosquito species in the Commonwealth of Virginia.

Materials and Methods

Site Descriptions

Cavalier Wildlife Management Area is a conservation area located in the southeastern corner of Virginia in the county of Chesapeake (Fig. 1). Covering approximately 4,550 acres, habitats consist of forested wetlands, seasonally flooded swamps, bottomland hardwoods, and managed coastal forests. Featherfin Wildlife Management Area spans nearly 2,800 acres spread across Prince Edward, Appomattox, and Buckingham counties within Virginia’s Piedmont region. The landscape is characterized by mixed hardwood forests, planted loblolly pine stands, agricultural fields, small drainages, and extensive wetlands associated with the Appomattox River. Big Survey Wildlife Management Area covers approximately 7,500 acres in Wythe County, within the Valley and Ridge physiographic province. The site consists of mountainous ridges separated by narrow valleys and drains into the Reed and Cripple Creek watersheds, and is dominated by oak–hickory forest with patches of yellow pine. Sampling in Blacksburg occurred within residential neighborhoods near Merrimac, in Montgomery County, situated between the Blue Ridge and Allegheny Mountains. This site represents an urban–suburban landscape interspersed with green spaces, hardwood forests, riparian corridors, and backyard vegetation.

Mosquito Collection

Mosquito sampling was carried out over five-months, from May 15 to October 1, 2022, with each site visited for three consecutive days per month. At each location, four CDC light traps baited with carbon dioxide (CO2) (BioQuip Products, Rancho Dominguez, CA) and four gravid traps baited with maple leaf-infused water (BioQuip Products) were deployed. Traps were deployed in the same spot each month. Additionally, vacuum aspiration with a homemade aspirator was used to supplement collection once a day. Traps were set at dusk and retrieved at dawn. Mosquitoes in each container were sorted into pools based on species using a dissecting microscope and the North American Mosquito guide (Darsie and Ward 2005).

Diversity Analysis

We calculated five diversity variables including species richness (the number of species), individual abundance (total number of individuals per species), Shannon-diversity indexes (H’ [Krebs 1989]), Simpson Evenness index (E1/D [Simpson 1949], and the expected number of species based on species accumulation curves (Chao et al. 2020, Chao and Hu 2023).

Bloodmeal Analysis

To determine host feeding choices, any mosquito that showed engorged abdomens were collected separately. Whole mosquitoes were processed as previously described (Reinhold et al. 2023) using a Qiagen DNeasy Blood & Tissue Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany), following kit instructions. DNA was then amplified using primers from (Kitano et al. 2007) targeting the 16S gene. Visually detectable PCR amplicons were then sent to Genewiz (South Plainfield, NJ) for Sanger sequencing. Sequences were then identified using NCBI BLAST and data collected for samples with a minimum identity of 85% (Altschul et al. 1990).

Random Forest Modeling

All analyses were performed in Rstudio (R Core Team 2025) using the randomForest (v4.7–1.1), caret (v6.0–94), tidyverse (v2.0.0), and randomForestExplainer (0.10.1) packages (Liaw and Wiener 2002, Kuhn 2008, Wickham et al. 2019, Paluszynska et al. 2020). Predictor variables were downloaded and included (Trap Type, Humidity, Daily Temperature [High, low, and mean], Julian Day, Monthly Precipitation [Current and Previous Month], Monthly Mean Temperature, and site Elevation [PRISM Climate Group 2025]). The data were randomly partitioned into training (70%) and testing (30%) sets using stratified sampling via “createDataPartition” to preserve class proportions (seed = 123). Model performance was quantified using overall accuracy and the confusion matrix computed with caret. Predictor importance was summarized by mean decrease in accuracy and mean depth of tree.

Results

Diversity Analysis

Mosquito communities differed among the four surveyed sites (Table 1). Cavalier exhibited both the highest total abundance (6583 individuals) and the greatest species richness (31 species), followed by Featherfin (427 individuals, 14 species), Blacksburg (533 individuals, 12 species), and Big Survey (204 individuals, 13 species). Expected species richness followed the same rank order, with Cavalier predicted to have 33.91 ± 13.06 species, Blacksburg: 15.93 ± 7.18, Big Survey: 16.14 ± 12.19, and Featherfin: 16.92 ± 7.35. Shannon diversity was highest at Cavalier (H′ = 0.78) and Big Survey (H′ = 0.71), followed by Blacksburg (H′ = 0.62), and Featherfin (H′ = 0.62). The most abundant species varied by site: Cs. inornata dominated Cavalier, Culex restuans were predominant at both Blacksburg and Featherfin, and Aedes triseriatus was the most common species at Big Survey. Four invasive mosquito species were detected (Aedes albopictus, Aedes japonicus, Culex coronator, and Culex pipiens/quinquefasciatus) (Table 2). Aedes albopictus, Ae. japonicus, Ae. triseriatus, and Culex restuans were predominantly captured in gravid traps whereas Ae. vexans, Anopheles crucians, Coquillettidia perturbans, and Culiseta inornata were primarily collected using CDC light traps (Table 2).

Bloodmeal Analysis

Bloodmeal analysis was successfully performed on 18 of the 28 mosquito specimens representing nine species (Table 3). White-tailed deer was the most frequently identified host, constituting the sole or primary bloodmeal source for Aedes sticticus, Ae. triseriatus, Anopheles punctipennis, Anopheles quadrimaculatus (n = 4), and Culex erraticus (n = 2). Culex restuans had both mammalian (White-tailed deer, n = 2) and avian bloodmeals (n = 3). Uranotaenia sapphirina from Cavalier fed on an anuran, representing the only documented amphibian host in our study.

Random Forest Modeling

Random forest analysis revealed species‐specific differences in the influential predictors indicated by minimum depth and mean decrease in accuracy (Table 4). Trap type emerged as the top predictor for Ae. triseriatus, Ae. vexans, Ae. japonicus, Coquillettidia perturbans, and Cx. restuans, with mean depths (2.0–2.66) and high contributions to model accuracy (mean decrease in accuracy ranging from 7.48 to 31.76). In contrast, elevation was the dominant predictor for Ur. sapphirina, Anopheles crucians, An. quadrimaculatus, Cx. erraticus, Cx. pipiens/quinquefasciatus, Cx. territans, and Cs. inornata, consistently showing low minimum depths (1.36–2.18) and high importance values (mean decrease in accuracy up to 22.0). Climatic variables also played substantial secondary roles across taxa. Measures of temperature (monthly mean temperature, monthly temperature, or daily low temperature) frequently ranked as second most important predictors, particularly for Ur. sapphirina, An. quadrimaculatus, Cx. pipiens/quinquefasciatus, and Cs. inornata. Humidity was especially influential for An. punctipennis, where it was the primary predictor. Overall, species tended to be defined by combinations of climatic and location-specific conditions.

Discussion

We identified several mosquito species of public health concern, including Culex coronator and Cx. nigripalpus representing new regional records. Culex coronator is a recognized vector of WNV (Mackay et al. 2008), and St. Louis encephalitis virus (Aitken et al. 1964), while Cx. nigripalpus is implicated in the transmission of WNV (Vitek et al. 2008), St. Louis encephalitis virus (Day and Curtis 1993), and EEEV (Corrin et al. 2021). Cx. coronator was first detected in Virginia in 2016, with additional records in 2019 and 2020 from Suffolk County (Akaratovic and Kiser 2017). A single Cx. nigripalpus was reported in 2017, also in Suffolk County (Akaratovic et al. 2021). Our findings document the first detections outside of Suffolk County and highlight the ongoing range expansion of both species. The most frequently encountered mosquito species during our study were Cs. inornata and Cx. pipiens/quinquefasciatus (Table 1). Culiseta inornata has been linked to Cache Valley virus (Corner et al. 1980), California encephalitis virus (Morgante and Shemanchuk 1967), and Jamestown Canyon virus (Shepard and Armstrong 2023). Culex pipiens can transmit WNV, Rift Valley fever virus, Sindbis virus, and heartworm (Dirofilaria immitis) (Reeves et al. 1942, Meegan et al. 1980, Dohm et al. 2002, Molaei et al. 2006, Michalski et al. 2010). The climate variables identified in our Random Forest Modeling suggests that continued warming or shifts in precipitation may facilitate further range expansions of these medically important species, which would likely drive corresponding changes in pathogen distributions (Laporta et al. 2023, Muller et al. 2024).

Bloodmeal analysis revealed a predominance for mammalian feeding, with white-tailed deer representing the primary host for most engorged females. This pattern is consistent with the high local abundance of deer across study sites. Avian hosts were detected at lower frequencies and primarily in Cx. restuans, an ornithophilic species implicated in enzootic WNV transmission among passerine birds. The detection of an anuran bloodmeal from Ur. sapphirina further highlights the diversity of host use within the mosquito community. Although the primers we used are sufficiently specific to identify most bloodmeals by excluding species outside the geographic range of our study sites, they do not have the taxonomic resolution necessary to identify to the species level of some groups (eg passerine birds). Collectively, these results demonstrate that mosquito–host interactions vary among taxa.

Random forest modeling provided quantitative support for the importance of both habitat structure and environmental gradients in shaping species distributions. The strong influence of trap type provides behavioral context that complements recent work on landscape heterogeneity and mosquito physiology in southwestern Virginia. In particular, VanderGiessen et al. (2026) demonstrated that urbanization, elevation, and vegetation structure shape mosquito abundance and sugar-feeding prevalence. Our models included only broad-scale environmental variables and did not include any fine-scale predictors, such as vegetation density or litter depth. Future studies incorporating finer-scale variables would likely improve the predictive power of models.

Our study demonstrates that mosquito diversity, host use, and environmental drivers vary across Virginia’s heterogeneous landscapes. Coastal and low-elevation habitats supported the highest abundance and richness, while upland and urban sites hosted distinct but less diverse assemblages. The widespread occurrence of medically important species, combined with predominantly mammalian feeding and environmentally structured distributions, highlights the complex ecological context underlying vector-borne disease risk in the region. Expanding similar statewide, ecologically informed monitoring efforts will be critical for detecting range shifts, identifying emerging vectors, and improving risk assessments as climate and land-use changes continue to reshape mosquito communities across the Mid-Atlantic.
