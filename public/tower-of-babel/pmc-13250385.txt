Randomized Controlled Trial on the Effectiveness of a Telehealth Gamification App for Self‐Management in Patients With Inflammatory Bowel Disease
Authors: Narges Norouzkhani, Mahbobeh Faramarzi, Ali Bahari, Javad Shokri Shirvani, Hamed Tabesh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundInflammatory bowel diseases (IBDs) are chronic, relapsing‐remitting disorders that significantly impair quality of life and increase healthcare utilization. Although effective therapies are available, suboptimal adherence and insufficient monitoring often compromise disease control. Digital health interventions that incorporate gamification may strengthen patient engagement and promote sustained self‐management.ObjectiveTo assess the efficacy of the “My IBD Buddy” gamified mobile telemedicine platform vs. standard care in enhancing clinical and patient‐reported outcomes among adults with IBD.MethodsA multicenter, parallel‐group, two‐arm exploratory randomized controlled trial (RCT) was conducted over 24 weeks. Adults with IBD were randomized to receive either the My IBD Buddy intervention—featuring disease activity tracking, medication adherence support, personalized education, and gamified self‐management modules—or standard care. Primary endpoints were disease‐specific quality of life and self‐efficacy; secondary endpoints included disease knowledge, mental health status, and additional health‐related metrics.ResultsAt week 12, the intervention group exhibited statistically significant improvements in self‐efficacy (p < 0.001, SMD = 0.30) and disease knowledge (p = 0.001, SMD = 0.40) compared with the control group. Although quality‐of‐life scores improved, the differences were not statistically significant at this stage. By week 24, all primary and key secondary outcomes showed statistically significant differences, including substantial improvements in quality of life, self‐efficacy (p < 0.001, SMD = 0.61), and disease knowledge (p < 0.001, SMD = 0.61). Retention in the intervention group was high at 92%.ConclusionA gamified, theory‐driven telemedicine platform significantly enhanced patient‐reported outcomes, disease knowledge, and engagement in IBD care. These results support incorporating gamified digital self‐management solutions into routine clinical practice as a scalable, patient‐centered strategy for managing chronic disease.Trial RegistrationIRCT20200613047757N1; registered on November 16, 2021. Introduction

Inflammatory bowel diseases (IBDs), including Crohn's disease (CD) and ulcerative colitis (UC), affect approximately 1.4 million individuals in the United States [1]. IBD is a chronic, relapsing–remitting disorder characterized by alternating periods of flares and remission, with clinical manifestations such as abdominal pain, diarrhea, hematochezia, fatigue, and extraintestinal symptoms [1, 2]. These symptoms significantly reduce quality of life [3] and contribute to elevated healthcare utilization and substantial direct and indirect costs [3, 4]. Despite the availability of effective pharmacologic therapies, optimal outcomes remain elusive for many patients. This is due not only to the lack of efficacious treatments for certain subgroups [4] but also to persistent barriers such as nonadherence [5], delayed initiation of appropriate therapy, insufficient monitoring, medication‐related adverse effects [6], inadequate patient education [7], limited shared decision‐making [8], and restricted access to high‐quality IBD care [9].

IBD management typically involves routine in‐person follow‐ups; however, disease flares often arise unpredictably and may not align with scheduled visits. While urgent consultations can be arranged, limited availability frequently delays diagnostic assessment and therapeutic intervention. In clinical settings, many IBD centers rely on telephone triage to initiate diagnostics and treatment [9]. A recent report noted that IBD centers manage an average of 125 patient calls daily, presenting logistical obstacles to timely care delivery [9]. Standard care often centers on provider‐initiated treatment decisions, restricting patient autonomy during acute exacerbations. Notably, Robinson et al. [8] demonstrated that UC patients who self‐initiated treatment based on symptom recognition experienced shorter relapses and fewer medical visits, highlighting the need for enhanced symptom monitoring and timely therapeutic initiation.

Telemedicine has emerged as a promising approach in chronic disease management, including IBD. Early pilot studies suggest that telemedicine is both feasible and acceptable to IBD patients [10] and can improve disease activity indices and quality of life [11]. A large European RCT showed that telemedicine facilitated faster therapeutic initiation, reduced relapse duration, and improved quality of life compared with standard care [12]. However, many early telehealth platforms required in‐home monitoring equipment or depended on static web portals, contributing to high attrition rates [13].

A recent systematic review and meta‐analysis by [14] confirmed that e‐health interventions enhance patient knowledge, quality of life, and treatment adherence in IBD, but also emphasized persistent engagement‐related challenges.

Gamification has garnered interest as a strategy to address these challenges in digital health. Defined as the incorporation of game‐like features—such as points, badges, and progress tracking—into non‐gaming contexts, gamification has been shown to improve user motivation, adherence, and long‐term participation in chronic disease self‐management [15, 16]. In IBD [17] and [16] emphasized the benefits of user‐centered, needs‐driven gamified designs, which support autonomy, competence, and relatedness—core constructs of Self‐Determination Theory—thereby improving adherence and outcomes. Furthermore [18], reported that a digital IBD management platform integrating continuous monitoring, patient–provider communication, and self‐management tools significantly improved objective biomarkers and patient satisfaction over 6 months. These findings suggest that embedding gamification within telemedicine may reduce attrition and yield sustained clinical improvements.

Despite these promising insights, no previous RCT has systematically integrated gamification with a behavior change framework to enhance engagement, autonomy, and self‐management in IBD. Furthermore, the extent to which such interventions deliver lasting improvements in both patient‐reported outcomes and objective clinical indicators remains uncertain.

This multicenter RCT aimed to evaluate the efficacy of “My IBD Buddy,” a gamified telemedicine platform, in comparison with standard care, in improving disease‐specific quality of life, self‐efficacy, patient knowledge, and other clinical outcomes among adults with IBD. We hypothesized that participants receiving the intervention would exhibit significant improvements across these outcomes, along with higher retention and engagement levels, compared with standard care.

“My IBD Buddy” integrates disease activity monitoring, medication adherence reminders, tailored educational content, and gamified self‐management tools via a secure mobile application. The platform is designed to empower patients in disease management while enabling timely clinician oversight. This trial evaluates the clinical efficacy, user engagement, and feasibility of this approach within routine IBD care, as outlined in the subsequent sections.

Methods

This RCT was conducted in accordance with the protocol published by Norouzkhani et al. (2024), ensuring methodological rigor and transparency. The trial evaluated the effectiveness of a gamified self‐management intervention delivered via a mobile application compared with a control condition. Details regarding the study design and statistical methodology are available in the protocol article [15]. This report adheres to the CONSORT 2010 guidelines for the reporting of stepped randomized trials [19].

Development Process and Pilot Study Design

A gamified mobile application, My IBD Buddy, was developed to facilitate self‐management in patients with IBD [16]. The development process followed the ADDIE instructional design model—Analysis, Design, Development, Implementation, and Evaluation—and incorporated the eight behavior‐change components of the Sukr Wheel framework: Fun, Esteem, Growth, Motivation, Sustainability, Socialization, Self‐Representation, and Self‐Management. Multidisciplinary input from gastroenterologists, psychologists, nurses, medical informatics specialists, and UI/UX designers ensured both content validity and usability.

The final Android‐based platform comprised eight modules: user registration and profile; symptom tracking (five submodules—interaction, psychological, medical/clinical, educational, and nutritional monitoring); medication management; laboratory results; colonoscopy preparation; educational resources; and support services. Gamification elements included points, badges, levels, progress bars, leaderboards, customizable avatars, narrative storytelling, and push notifications.

User interface development progressed from low‐fidelity sketches to high‐fidelity wireframes in Figma, underwent heuristic evaluation by five usability experts (resulting in the resolution of 50 unique issues), and was programmed using Android Studio 2022. Multimedia content included VideoScribe animations, instructional videos, and calming audio features. Alpha testing by the development team addressed functionality‐related bugs.

In a 14‐day beta phase, 22 patients with IBD engaged with the app for at least 15 min per day. Usability, assessed using the System Usability Scale (SUS), yielded a mean score of 80.68, indicating high usability.

Settings and Locations

Participants were recruited from academic medical centers and private outpatient adult gastroenterology and hepatology clinics in Iran. Information about the study was disseminated via phone calls and electronic brochures. Each participating site serves more than 500 IBD patients annually. Potential participants were approached by email, phone, or during clinical consultations with their gastroenterologist. To expand outreach, the Inflammatory Bowel Disease Association was also informed. After eligibility was confirmed, patients who expressed interest provided written informed consent.

Intervention

The intervention arm received structured education and disease management via a mobile application over 6 months. During the study, the control group continued with standard care at the discretion of the treating physician. This randomized clinical trial followed a two‐arm parallel design (Figure 2).

Flowchart of study participants. Inflammatory bowel diseases: inflammatory bowel disease. Control Group: standard care group. Intervention Group: self‐management intervention using a gamified application in addition to standard care.

Following informed consent, participants were stratified by disease type, disease activity, and clinical site. They were then allocated to the intervention or control group as per the randomization procedure. In the intervention group, a self‐care platform based on a customized mobile gamification system was provided in addition to standard care. This Android‐based application was designed using the Wheel of Sukr framework [20] and the ADDIE instructional model [21, 22, 23].

Patient information and support needs were assessed to individualize the intervention [24, 25, 26, 27], and the development and validation of educational content were conducted by prior studies [28]. Participants completed follow‐up questionnaires and submitted related data every 12 weeks. A psychologist also provided remote training and counseling focused on mental health, contributing to a comprehensive, multidisciplinary care model.

Sample Size Calculation and Data Collection Procedures

Sample size estimation was performed using the “sampsi” module in Stata 2015 and the Minimal Clinically Important Difference (MCID) approach. Calculations were based on a repeated‐measures analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) model, incorporating three time points: baseline, 12 weeks, and 24 weeks. Parameters included a two‐sided significance level (α = 0.05), statistical power (1 − β = 0.80), and an inter‐measurement correlation coefficient (ρ = 0.30), as reported in prior literature.

The MCID for the primary outcome—the total score on the Inflammatory Bowel Disease Questionnaire (IBDQ)—was set at 16 points (Supplementary Table 2). Effect‐size estimates were drawn from validated studies. An anticipated 20% attrition rate over the follow‐up period was included in the calculation, resulting in a final sample size of 105 participants per group (210 total).

Adult patients with confirmed IBD were recruited in person by trained research coordinators at three tertiary gastroenterology and hepatology centers in Iran. Following eligibility screening and informed consent, participants were enrolled and scheduled for data collection at baseline, 12 weeks, and 24 weeks.

Data collection methods varied by study arm. In the intervention group, all self‐reported outcomes—including disease activity indices, quality of life, anxiety and depression, self‐efficacy, and disease‐specific knowledge—along with app‐specific usage metrics (e.g., symptom tracking frequency, medication reminder adherence, lab result uploads, nutrition diary entries, and educational module completion)—were captured directly through the My IBD Buddy mobile application and stored automatically on a secure backend server.

In the control group, equivalent outcome data were collected using a secure online survey system or structured telephone interviews conducted by trained staff. Responses were manually entered into the database and verified through double‐entry procedures to ensure accuracy.

Randomization

Patients were randomly assigned to the intervention or control group using a stratified block randomization sequence generated in WINPEPI, with individuals serving as the randomization unit. All eligible IBD patients from participating centers were invited to join the trial. An independent study administrator generated the allocation sequence and prepared 210 sealed, opaque, sequentially numbered envelopes, each containing a group assignment evenly distributed across the two arms.

Participants were informed of their group allocation via email or social media and received instructions for accessing the assigned program or completing the appropriate online survey.

Masking

Given the intervention's interactive nature and distinct format across groups, participant blinding was not feasible. Similarly, blinding was not possible for research personnel involved in eligibility screening, data collection, mobile application orientation, and follow‐up procedures. However, treating physicians conducting in‐person visits and data analysts responsible for statistical analysis remained blinded to group allocation.

Statistical Methods

All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 22.0 and Stata version 17.0. All tests were two‐sided, with a predefined significance level of 0.05.

Primary Analysis

Repeated‐measures ANCOVA was used to analyze the four primary outcomes. Each outcome was compared between groups at weeks 12 and 24. For each time point, an ANCOVA model included group allocation as a fixed factor and the baseline value of the respective outcome as a covariate to control for initial between‐group differences. Following estimation of group effects, effect size metrics—including adjusted mean difference (MD) and standardized mean difference (SMD)—were computed along with their corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs).

To evaluate statistical assumptions, normality and homogeneity of variance between the two groups were assessed separately for weeks 12 and 24. All primary analyses were performed using Stata version 17.0 and SPSS version 22.0.

Secondary and Post Hoc Analysis

Secondary statistical analyses—based on secondary outcomes—were conducted using repeated‐measures ANCOVA, following the same analytical framework applied to the primary outcomes.

Subgroup analyses were performed to examine the influence of potential covariates, including age (< 40 vs. ≥ 40 years), sex, IBD subtype (CD vs. UC), disease duration (< 7 vs. ≥ 7 years), and education level (diploma or lower vs. bachelor's degree or higher). For each subgroup, the effect size index was reported as the mean difference between comparison groups.

To account for potential imbalances in key baseline covariates that could influence outcome estimates, multivariable linear regression models were applied. These models adjusted for age, sex, IBD subtype, disease duration, smoking status, and education level.

Variables were included in the adjusted model if they showed significant between‐group differences in the demographic and covariate summary table—defined as a mean difference exceeding 20% of the standard deviation for continuous variables or a difference > 10% in category proportions for categorical variables. The adjusted model took the form of a repeated‐measures ANCOVA with additional covariates.

Effect size estimates from the unadjusted models were subsequently compared with those derived from the adjusted models to evaluate the robustness of findings.

Sensitivity Analysis

This component assessed the potential influence of loss to follow‐up and missing outcome data at weeks 12 and 24. As all primary outcomes were continuous variables, missing data were addressed using multiple imputation (MI) via the chained equations method [29], with 10 imputed datasets generated.

After imputation, two analytic strategies were compared: per‐protocol (PP) or complete case analysis (CCA), and intention‐to‐treat (ITT), which incorporated imputed data for participants lost to follow‐up.

All MI procedures were conducted in Stata version 17.0 using the “mi set,” “mi register,” “mi impute,” and “mi estimate” commands. Subsequent analyses were carried out in both Stata version 17.0 and SPSS version 22.0 to verify the robustness of the results.

Ethical Considerations

The study protocol was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of Mashhad University of Medical Sciences in November 2021 (IR.MUMS.REC.1400.230) and conducted by qualified personnel. The potential benefits outweighed any associated risks, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants without coercion. The protocol was registered with the Iranian Registry of Clinical Trials (IRCT20200613047757N1) and is publicly accessible via the OSF registry (https://doi.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/AWFY9). The reporting of results followed the CONSORT 2010 guidelines.

Results

Participant Flow

A total of 586 patients were screened for eligibility. Among these, 41 (7.0%) did not meet the inclusion criteria, 299 (51.02%) did not respond to the invitation, and 36 (6.14%) declined participation, citing reasons such as lack of time, involvement in other studies, or perceived lack of benefit from research participation. Ultimately, 210 eligible individuals provided written informed consent and were randomized to either the intervention group (n = 99) or the standard care control group (n = 111) (Figure 4).

CONSORT flowchart for patient appointment process.

In the intervention arm, two participants did not install the mobile application and were excluded before follow‐up. Of the 97 who initiated the intervention, four (4.12%) were lost to follow‐up: one due to pregnancy and disease recurrence, one due to a preference for in‐person education, and two for unspecified reasons.

In the control arm, nine participants (8.11%) were lost to follow‐up: seven declined continued participation, and two were lost for unspecified reasons.

At week 12, completed questionnaires were received from 93 participants in the intervention group and 102 in the control group. At week 24, follow‐up was completed by 91 intervention participants and 95 control participants, yielding per‐protocol analyses of 91 and 95 individuals, respectively.

Baseline and Clinical Characteristics

Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics for both study groups are summarized in Table 1. Overall, groups were comparable at baseline, confirming successful randomization. The only statistically significant differences were observed in marital status and education level; marital status was not considered clinically meaningful, while education level was included as a covariate in post hoc analyses. The mean participant age was 35.91 ± 10.43 years, with a mean disease duration of 8.68 ± 5.77 years. Women accounted for 53.81% of the sample, and 50.95% had not attained a university‐level education. CD was present in 53.33% of participants, and 40.95% had active disease at baseline. Immunosuppressive therapy was reported by 60.48%, and 54.76% were receiving biologic agents. Detailed distributions of baseline variables—including age, sex, education level, marital status, disease type, and disease duration—are provided in Supplementary Table 3. No statistically significant between‐group differences were identified, except for education level (all p > 0.05).

Primary Outcomes

Table 2 summarizes the distribution of all primary outcomes at baseline, week 12, and week 24, along with adjusted MDs, SMDs, 95% CIs, and p values.

In Supplementary Figures 1–4, mean changes for each outcome are plotted across baseline, week 12, and week 24, with 95% CI error bars.

Quality of Life (QOL): At week 12, the adjusted MD between groups was 3.9 (95% CI: –1.1 to 8.9; p = 0.123), indicating a non‐significant difference. By week 24, the adjusted MD increased to 13.8 (95% CI: 8.6–19.0; p < 0.001), favoring the intervention group. The corresponding SMD at week 24 was 0.41 (95% CI: 0.12–0.70), reflecting a small‐to‐moderate effect size.

Disease Activity Index: The intervention group showed significantly lower disease activity at both follow‐up points. At week 12, MD = –0.19 (95% CI: –0.30 to –0.09; p = 0.001); at week 24, MD = –0.35 (95% CI: –0.44 to –0.16; p < 0.001). Corresponding SMDs indicated moderate effect sizes.

Anxiety: Scores were significantly lower in the intervention group at both follow‐up time points. At week 12, MD = –2.0 (95% CI: –2.4 to –1.5; p < 0.001); at week 24, MD = –3.2 (95% CI: –3.8 to –2.7; p < 0.001). Effect sizes at both time points reflected large reductions in anxiety.

Depression: A modest but statistically significant reduction was observed at week 12 (MD = –0.8; 95% CI: –1.6 to –0.1; p = 0.03), which increased at week 24 (MD = –2.6; 95% CI: –3.1 to –2.1; p < 0.001), with the SMD indicating a large effect.

All p‐values were derived from ANCOVA models adjusted for baseline scores.

Results of Secondary Outcomes

For self‐efficacy, the intervention group showed mean scores approximately 5 points higher than the control group at week 12, and about 7 points higher at week 24. These differences were statistically significant at both follow‐up points. At week 24, the intervention's effect on self‐efficacy was classified as moderate.

Regarding disease‐specific knowledge, the intervention group exceeded the control group by approximately 16 points at week 12 and 32 points at week 24. These differences were statistically significant at both time points. By the end of the study, the intervention's effect on knowledge was considered large.

Detailed outcome statistics are provided in Supplementary Table 3. Supplementary Figures 5 and 6 depict mean changes in each secondary outcome across baseline, week 12, and week 24 for both groups, with error bars representing 95% CIs.

Complementary Analyses

To address baseline differences in education level, education was dichotomized (high school diploma or lower vs. bachelor's degree or higher) and included as a covariate in ANCOVA models. Adjustment for education did not materially alter primary outcome estimates. For instance, the adjusted MD for QOL at week 24 was 14.2 (95% CI: 9.0–19.4), and for the disease activity index was –0.31 (95% CI: –0.45 to –0.17). Anxiety and depression outcomes remained virtually unchanged after adjustment.

Subgroup analyses by age, sex, disease type, disease duration, and education level (Supplementary Tables 4–7) revealed no significant interaction effects for most primary outcomes. Exceptions were noted in the disease activity index, where greater improvements were observed among participants aged ≥ 40 years and among women.

Sensitivity Analysis

PP and ITT analyses, performed after MI, produced highly consistent results (Table 3). Loss to follow‐up had minimal impact on outcome estimates, with changes in MDs below 15% and SMDs below 5%. The direction of effects consistently favored the intervention, except for disease activity, where the magnitude was attenuated.

Discussion

The primary objective of this study was to assess the effectiveness of the “My IBD Buddy” remote self‐care system compared with standard care in adults with IBD. The observed improvements are clinically meaningful, as a theory‐informed digital platform can shift patients from passive care recipients to active participants in disease management—an especially valuable approach in chronic, relapsing–remitting conditions with substantial psychological and functional burdens. Sustained gains in disease‐specific quality of life, self‐efficacy, and patient knowledge suggest that well‐designed digital interventions can support both disease control and long‐term patient engagement.

Early research by Robinson et al. [8] demonstrated that structured self‐management after hospital discharge in patients with UC reduced relapse duration and healthcare utilization [12], while maintaining quality of life and coping abilities without increasing outpatient visits or anxiety [10]. However, those regimens lacked continuous digital engagement—a key differentiator that likely contributed to the more durable improvements observed in this trial. By integrating real‐time monitoring, personalized feedback, and interactive educational modules, the present intervention offered a persistent layer of support absent in prior models, thereby reinforcing sustained behavioral change.

Web‐based platforms, such as those assessed by Elkjaer et al. [13], provided disease‐specific content and self‐treatment algorithms for patients with UC, yet yielded mixed clinical results. The Short Inflammatory Bowel Disease Questionnaire (SIBDQ) and the Simple Clinical Colitis Activity Index (SCCAI) were used to evaluate disease severity [30]. Despite some favorable findings, the Danish cohort exhibited no improvement in adherence and experienced higher relapse rates in the web‐based group, although relapse durations were shorter [13]. The Irish cohort demonstrated improvements in knowledge, general health, vitality, and psychosocial functioning, but no significant change in disease‐specific quality of life [13]. Additionally, web‐platform users initiated more email and telephone contacts than controls, indicating increased engagement without an enhanced clinical benefit [31].

In a subsequent open‐label trial involving 95 UC patients, a web‐guided mesalamine regimen achieved 86% adherence over 3 months and was associated with reductions in disease activity indices and fecal calprotectin levels [13]. In contrast, the IBD Home Automated Telemanagement (HAT) system [32] led to improvements in clinical activity indices, quality of life, and disease‐specific knowledge, but was evaluated in small cohorts (27 and 47 participants), limiting statistical robustness. Although the UC HAT randomized trial included individualized action plans and educational content, its overall effect on disease activity was modest. Notably, group differences in baseline immunosuppressant use (56% vs. 27%, p < 0.05) and lower baseline quality of life in the intervention group further confounded interpretation [32, 33].

As seen in other TELE‐IBD studies, reliance on subjective tools—such as the Crohn's Disease Activity Index (CDAI) and the Seo index—while pragmatic, limits precision due to weak correlation with objective inflammatory markers, especially in CD [34]. In contrast, the observed improvements in the present trial can be understood through established behavioral frameworks. Gamification elements—such as progress tracking, badges, and personalized feedback—supported the development of competence and autonomy, two core constructs in Self‐Determination Theory. Visible indicators of progress reinforced patients' belief in their ability to manage illness, aligning with Bandura's Social Cognitive Theory, which emphasizes mastery experiences as drivers of sustained behavioral change. These mechanisms likely mediated the gains in self‐efficacy and adherence documented in our cohort.

This interpretation is consistent with emerging empirical evidence. For instance [18], demonstrated that a digitally enabled IBD management platform—featuring continuous monitoring, bidirectional communication, and self‐management tools—significantly improved fecal calprotectin levels, short health status scores, and patient satisfaction over 6 months. Similarly [17], highlighted that needs‐driven, user‐centered design—aligned with patient‐identified priorities—enhances adoption and long‐term engagement by avoiding generic, non‐personalized content. In line with these findings, Norouzkhani et al. [15, 16] reported that gamified, mobile‐based self‐management interventions—developed using structured instructional models such as ADDIE and guided by behavior‐oriented frameworks like Sukr—can improve motivation, enhance adherence, reduce disease activity, and yield measurable gains in quality of life and mental health. Systematic evidence from [14] further supports the role of e‐health in enhancing quality of life and disease‐related knowledge while reducing healthcare utilization, but also underscores a critical gap in leveraging gamification to sustain engagement. The novelty and added value of the current approach lie in directly addressing these limitations. From a healthcare systems perspective, integrating platforms such as My IBD Buddy could support scalable, guideline‐concordant care delivery, especially in resource‐constrained settings with limited specialist availability. Observed reductions in healthcare utilization in similar e‐health models [14] suggest potential cost offsets, underscoring the need for formal economic evaluation in future studies.

Limitations and Future Directions

This study has several limitations. First, the sample size was underpowered to detect small but clinically relevant differences in disease activity, limiting the depth of subgroup analyses. Second, although attrition remained within expected limits, retention in telemedicine trials for IBD varies substantially (7%–60%) [30], highlighting the persistent challenge of sustaining engagement. Third, recruitment from tertiary referral centers may limit generalizability to community‐based populations with less severe disease and fewer resources. Fourth, the 24‐week follow‐up period was sufficient to capture early effects but too short to evaluate the durability of outcomes over time. Fifth, objective biomarkers—including fecal calprotectin and C‐reactive protein (CRP)—were not included as primary endpoints, and mucosal healing was not assessed due to the cost and invasiveness of endoscopy.

Future research should address these limitations by implementing pragmatic trials in more diverse healthcare settings, with larger sample sizes, longer follow‐up durations, and inclusion of objective markers in outcome assessments. Economic evaluations will also be essential to determine the cost‐effectiveness and scalability of gamified digital self‐management systems for IBD.

Conclusion

This RCT demonstrates that My IBD Buddy—a gamified, theory‐informed self‐care platform—can significantly enhance disease‐specific quality of life, self‐efficacy, and patient knowledge in adults with IBD, while promoting sustained engagement. These findings underscore the potential of thoughtfully designed digital interventions to shift patients from passive recipients to active collaborators in chronic disease management, addressing both clinical and psychosocial dimensions of IBD.

While the 24‐week follow‐up and modest sample size limited detection of smaller yet clinically relevant changes in disease activity, the observed improvements highlight the value of integrating behavioral theory and gamification into digital health strategies. Future studies should validate these findings in larger, more heterogeneous populations, over extended timeframes, and with objective biomarkers, in conjunction with formal economic analyses to assess cost‐effectiveness and scalability.

By merging behavioral science with digital innovation, this study advances the growing body of evidence supporting personalized, engaging, and scalable self‐management solutions as a central component of modern chronic care.
