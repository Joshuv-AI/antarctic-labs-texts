Associations Between Vision and Hearing Impairment and Cardiovascular Diseases: A Longitudinal Cohort of Middle‐Aged and Older Adults in China
Authors: Jie He, Xiaoyu Tang, Minglan Jiang, Xiaowei Zheng
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBased on the severity of cardiovascular disease (CVD) and sensory impairment in China and the lack of research on this, the aims of our study were to assess the impact of hearing impairment (HI), visual impairment (VI), and concurrent HI and VI (termed dual sensory impairment) on CVD in elderly people, based on a representative sample of China.Methods and ResultsWe used data from the CHARLS (China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study) for our survey of 11 332 participants. We calculated hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% 95% CIs for CVD, stroke, and cardiac events by using Cox proportional hazards models. HI and VI status were collected through self‐reported questions. During the 7‐year follow‐up, a total of 2156 participants experienced CVD (including 745 stroke and 1605 cardiac events). Compared with the reference, individuals with VI had higher risk of CVD (HR, 1.24 [95% CI, 1.09–1.40]). Individuals with HI also had higher risk of CVD than those without HI (HR, 1.20 [95% CI, 1.09–1.33]). Compared with participants without VI and HI, individuals with dual sensory impairment had a 1.35‐fold increased risk of CVD (HR, 1.35 [95% CI, 1.16–1.57]). In addition, individuals with dual sensory impairment also had increased risk of stroke and cardiac events.ConclusionsOur study shows that HI and VI have a combined effect on the incidence of CVD. Based on the high prevalence of CVD around the world, early detection of sensory disorders and the adoption of appropriate measures may contribute to prevent CVD and reduce the burden of clinical diagnosis and treatment of CVD.Keywords: cardiac events, cardiovascular disease, hearing impairment, stroke, vision impairmentSubject Categories: Aging, Cardiovascular Disease, Epidemiology, Lifestyle, Primary Prevention

Clinical Perspective

What Is New?

Our research is the first to explore the impacts of hearing impairment, visual impairment, and dual sensory impairment on cardiovascular disease risk among Chinese middle‐aged and elderly adults.

What Are the Clinical Implications?

Hearing and visual impairment have a combined effect on the incidence of cardiovascular disease. Our findings suggest that consideration of sensory impairment and appropriate treatments such as use of glasses and hearing aids should be a target for further research to reduce cardiovascular disease risk.

The proportion of people aged 60 years and older in the world is on the rise. The World Health Organization estimated that the number of people aged 60 years and older will double (2.1 billion) by 2050. 1 Data released by the National Bureau of Statistics show that at the end of 2022, there were 280.04 million people aged 60 years or over in China, of whom 209.78 million people are aged more than 65 years. 2 With the continuous aging of the population, the incidence and mortality rates of various chronic diseases are also increasing. 3 Among them, cardiovascular disease (CVD) is a leading cause to global mortality. It is estimated that CVD will cause 23.3 million deaths by the year 2030. 4 In China, in terms of the proportions of disease mortality among urban and rural residents, CVD is at the top of the list. 5 Meanwhile, with the continuous progress of aging, the burden of global hearing impairment (HI) and vision impairment (VI), termed dual sensory impairment (DSI) is also gradually increasing. 6 , 7

Over the past 40 years, we have had a general understanding of how to prevent and treat CVD and its risk factors, and mortality from CVD has decreased in some high‐income countries. 8 However, most low‐income countries did not show a reduction in cardiovascular mortality. Cost‐effective delivery of treatment remains a major challenge for low‐income countries. In China, although we have taken many preventive measures and many cardiovascular technologies have been at world‐leading level, compared with other countries, there is still a high incidence of CVD and a large number of people with CVD risk factors: the number of people with hypertension, diabetes, or dyslipidemia has reached hundreds of millions. 9 , 10 Studies have shown that the deaths caused by chronic diseases in China account for 87% of total deaths. The burden of chronic diseases has accounted for approximately 70% of the total burden of diseases in China. 11 Further, more than 80% of the residents aged ≥60 years suffered from at least 1 chronic disease in China. 12 People with CVD require lifelong treatment with multiple drugs to prevent CVD, thus posing a significant challenge for the prevention and treatment of CVD. If risk factors for cardiovascular disease are effectively controlled, the incidence of CVD could be greatly reduced. Further research is needed to formulate more effective strategies to improve CVD awareness, treatment, and control. Recent studies suggest that HI is linked to CVD via vascular mechanisms, but epidemiological associations remain unclear. 13 In a study of a nationally representative sample of US adults, 3 in 5 people with VI had multiple CVD risk factors; the 2 most prevalent risk factors among those with VI were hypertension and obesity. 14 Thus, HI, VI, and DSI may be associated with CVD in middle‐aged and elderly people.

Concurrent DSI is strongly prevalent in the elderly. HI currently affects around 20% of the global population. 15 According to the World Health Organization 2021 World Report on Hearing, by 2050, nearly 2.5 billion people are projected to have some degree of hearing loss, and at least 700 million will require hearing rehabilitation services. 16 VI is also very common globally. In 2020, it was estimated that moderate or severe VI would affect over 200 million people around the world. 17

Many previous studies have shown that VI and HI alone have an impact on CVD, indicating that they may be a potential risk factor affecting the occurrence of CVD. 18 , 19 However, previous studies included United States and Korea individuals, and the impact of VI, HI, and DSI on the incidence rate of CVD in the Chinese population has not been fully elucidated. This study aimed to assess the relation between VI, HI, DSI, and CVD among Chinese middle‐aged and elderly adults, based on the data from the CHARLS (China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study). Results from this study may inform public health efforts and identify treatment targets to prevent CVD, facilitating clinical diagnosis and treatment of CVD.

METHODS

Ethical approval for all the CHARLS waves was granted from the Institutional Review Board at Peking University. The approval number for the main household survey, including anthropometrics, is IRB00001052‐11015; the approval number for biomarker collection was IRB00001052‐11014. The details of the CHARLS data are available on the website (http://charls.pku.edu.cn/en).

Study Population

CHARLS is a longitudinal survey representing households and individuals aged 45 years and above in China. It is an ongoing cohort using the multistage probability to proportional to scale sampling method in 2011. 20 , 21 In 2011, 17 708 participants from 28 provinces in China were surveyed (baseline, Wave 1) and followed every 2 to 3 years, using face‐to‐face computer technology to assist personal interviewing in their homes. Three subsequent follow‐up surveys were conducted in 2013 to 2014 (Wave 2), 2015 to 2016 (Wave 3), and 2017 to 2018 (Wave 4) among survivors. 22

Of 17 708 participants at baseline, individuals who met all the following criteria were included in the study: age≥45 years; available information about sensory impairment (SI) in Wave 1, free of history of cardiac events and stroke in Wave 1, and without loss to follow‐up or death in follow‐up. Finally, a total of 11 332 individuals were eligible for subsequent analysis (Figure 1).

Assessment of Sensory Impairment

HI and VI status were collected through self‐reported questions. 23 The assessment of VI includes the following questions: (1) “How good is your vision for seeing things at a distance (with glasses or corrective lenses), like recognizing a friend from across the street?” and (2) “How good is your vision for seeing things up close (with glasses or corrective lenses), like reading ordinary newspaper print?” For each question, the responses can be divided into “excellent,” “very good,” “good,” “fair,” and “poor.” A response to either of the 2 questions as “fair or poor” was judged as VI. 23 One question was included to assess HI, “Is your hearing excellent, very good, good, fair, or poor (with a hearing aid if you normally use it and without if you normally don't).” We identified participants as having HI when they responded fair or poor to this question. When HI and VI were both present, respondents were regarded as having DSI. 23 By asking “Do you have vision or/and hearing problems?” we categorized SI into 4 groups: no sensory impairment, VI only, HI only, and DSI.

Assessment of CVD

The primary outcome of the present study was incident CVD (stroke or cardiac events), and the secondary outcomes were cardiac events and stroke, separately. Incidence of stroke or cardiac events was defined as new events that occurred from Wave 1 to Wave 4, based on self‐reported physician's diagnosis (“Has a doctor ever told you that you had any adverse cardiovascular events [angina, acute myocardial infarction, severe arrhythmias, heart failure, coronary heart disease, or other heart problems] or stroke?”), following previous reported studies in CHARLS. 24

Covariates Assessments

Covariate data were collected at baseline including age, sex, living place (urban versus rural), educational level (illiteracy, primary school, middle school, high school or above). Health‐related factors included drinking status (never drinking versus ex‐drinking versus current drinking), smoking status (never smoking versus ex‐smoking versus current smoking), body mass index (BMI) (weight in kilograms divided by the square of height in meters), the presence or absence of other chronic diseases (dyslipidemia, diabetes, hypertension, cancer, arthritis, chronic lung disease, kidney disease, liver disease, digestive system disease). “Ever smoking” included reports of any smoking in the past, and “never smoking” never having smoked. “Ever drinking” represents having had an alcoholic beverage at some point, and “never drinking” not having any alcoholic beverage in the past. For dyslipidemia, the primary assessment tool is a fasting lipid panel that consists of total cholesterol, low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol, high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol, and triglycerides. 21 Dyslipidemia was defined as triglycerides ≥200 mg/dL, total cholesterol ≥240 mg/dL, low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol ≥160 mg/dL, and high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol <40 mg/dL based on the 2016 Chinese guidelines for dyslipidemia in adults. 22 Diabetes was defined as hemoglobin A1c ≥6.5% or fasting blood glucose ≥126 mg/dL, or 2‐hour blood glucose ≥200 mg/dL in the oral glucose tolerance test, or treatment for diabetes, or self‐reported history of diabetes. 22

Statistical Analysis

All participants were divided into 4 groups: no SI (Group 1), VI only (Group 2), HI only (Group 3), and DSI (Group 4). Participants' baseline characteristics were presented as mean±SD for continuous variables, as numbers and percentages for categorical variables. Demographic and clinical characteristics were compared between the 4 subgroups by ANOVA or Kruskal–Wallis test for continuous variables and χ2 test for categorical variables. Kaplan–Meier curves and the log‐rank test were used to compare the cumulative risk of events among the different groups. We computed hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% CIs for CVD, stroke, and cardiac events by using Cox proportional hazards models among those without reported history of cardiovascular events in Wave 1. The models were performed using 3 incremental levels of covariate adjustments. Model 1 adjusted for age. Model 2 adjusted for age, sex, living place, education level, BMI, smoking status, and drinking status. Model 3 adjusted for age, sex, living place, education level, smoking status, drinking status, and medical history (diabetes, hypertension, cancer, chronic lung disease, kidney disease, liver disease, arthritis, digestive disease, and asthma). 22 , 25 Subgroup analyses were further performed to evaluate the association between DSI and the risk of CVD according to sex, age, living place, smoking, drinking, history of hypertension and diabetes subgroups, and BMI subgroups. In sensitivity analysis, we computed Cox proportional hazards models and assessed the influence of death as a competing risk for CVD in our interpretations by performing competing risk analyses. The proportion of missing data on covariates ranged from 0% to 4.8%, and multiple imputation with the Markov chain Monte Carlo method was performed to assign any missing covariate data. Statistical analysis was conducted using SAS statistical software version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA).

DISCUSSION

This study of 11 332 participants explored the relationships between HI, VI, and DSI and the incidence of CVD. Our analyses of this nationally representative sample of Chinese adults suggested that HI, VI, and DSI increase the risk of CVD. Results remained significant after considering relevant confounders. Our study addresses the gap in knowledge linking sensory impairments with CVD risk in the Chinese population.

The prevalence rates of sensory loss in the current study are similar to other population‐based studies of vision and hearing loss and DSI in China. 26 , 27 , 28 Other studies, such as those in the United States, show that about 5% of adults ages 45 to 54 years have disabling hearing loss. The rate increases to 10% for adults ages 55 to 64 years; 22% of those ages 65 to 74 years and 55% of individuals 75 years and older have disabling hearing loss. 29 VI estimates varied between 1.6% and 24.8% for those <65 years and between 2.2% and 26.6% for those 65 years or older. 30 DSI affected up to 15% of adults, with the highest prevalence among adults age 80 years and older. 31 , 32 Overall the reported prevalence of VI in China is similar, whereas the prevalence of HI is lower and the prevalence of DSI is higher.

Previous studies have focused on the relationship between SI and mortality, but few have investigated the effect of DSI on cardiovascular morbidity. Jung and colleagues found in a Korean longitudinal study that VI and HI were associated with increased risks of myocardial infarction, stroke, and all‐cause mortality in patients with type 2 diabetes with hazard ratio increasing 2.5 fold for myocardial infarction, 2.9‐fold for stroke, and 4.6‐fold for all‐cause mortality when compared with those without VI and HI. 33 In a previous study exploring the effects of VI and HI on CVD, Liljas et al. found that men who could not hear and did not use a hearing aid had greater risks of incident CVD, particularly incident stroke, and CVD mortality than men who could hear. 34 An additional cohort study showed that higher risk of cardiovascular mortality was evident in those with VI in a dose–dependent manner, and this association remained significant after adjustment for confounders including cardiovascular risk factors. 18

There are a few notable differences in our research. First, most of these studies were conducted in the United States, the United Kingdom, South Korea, and other countries, and our study subjects were Chinese. Second, previous studies mostly focused on VI or HI or DSI, 35 whereas ours assessed all components, only VI, only HI, and also DSI. Third, previous research focused on the impact of SI on quality of life, 36 cognitive function, 37 and mortality, 35 but our study focused on CVD.

The underlying mechanisms by which VI and HI could lead to increased risk of CVD may include the following. First, physical activity has been widely recognized as a preventive measure against a wide range of chronic diseases, including CVD. However, for people with VI or HI, engaging in traditional physical activity can be difficult. Some studies had found that adults with VI are more likely to be physically inactive than adults without VI, 14 and moderate or greater HI in older adults is associated with lower levels of physical activity. 38 Lack of physical activity may lead to an increased risk of CVD, thus highlighting the need to provide appropriate physical activity opportunities for adults with VI. 39 Second, previous studies suggested that the association between HI and CVD may be mediated by smoking and atherosclerosis risk factors 40 ; this study observed an association that remained significant after adjustment for smoking and CVD‐related comorbidities. Third, cognitive impairment, which is associated with HI, 41 may be a link between SI and increased CVD risk. Associations between hearing loss and cognitive impairment have been reported. 42 , 43 , 44 , 45 , 46 These may be explained by sensory underload (lack of intellectual stimulation reducing cognitive ability), 43 attentional demands of sensory measurement (measurement of hearing loss is sensitive to negative age differences in cognitive processes such as sustained attention and discrimination), 43 or some common cause (hearing loss and cognitive function are both measures of the physiological architecture of the brain). 41 , 45 , 47 Fourth, SI affects daily communication and cognitive abilities in older adults, which may result in psychiatric disorders including depression, anxiety, and schizophrenia, increasing psychological burden, affecting the mental health of older adults, hindering information exchange and social participation, and consequently affecting physical activity, resulting in altered physical functioning. Meanwhile, some psychological studies have shown that negative psychological factors affect neurotransmitters such as norepinephrine and 5‐hydroxytryptamine, causing vasoconstriction disorders, altering blood rheology, causing insufficient blood supply, ischemia, and hypoxia, leading to CVD. 48 Fifth, SI may also lead to impaired understanding of medical treatments and compliance with medications over the long term, thus leading to increased risk of CVD events. Last, other factors such as subclinical cerebrovascular disease and CVD, atherosclerosis, and chronic vascular inflammatory states may contribute to CVD events in individuals with sensory impairment, but these could not be taken into account in the analysis. 33

The SI addressed in this study constitutes modifiable risk factors for prevention of CVD. The Information, Coordination, Preparation and Emotional Support model states that the most common causes of VI in older adults include presbyopia, cataracts, glaucoma, and diabetic retinopathy with age‐related macular degeneration. 49 An ophthalmological consultation should be recommended once vision loss is detected in an older adult. VI caused by cataract can be restored through surgery, and VI caused by presbyopia can be corrected through the wearing of glasses. 50 In addition, a prior study suggested that hearing aid fitting may be associated with a subsequent improvement in older adult‐specific quality of life, possibly through enhanced communication opportunities. 51 HI can be improved by hearing aids but also by some changes in the living environment and lifestyle, such as reducing environmental noise and speaking loudly and clearly. The risk of CVD may be reduced to a certain extent by carrying out targeted treatment (such as wearing glasses and using hearing aids) and lifestyle changes.

Further, early screening for VI and HI may allow more adequate time for health care workers to develop treatment strategies to alleviate or mitigate the effects of vision and hearing impairment. Regular follow‐up should also be carried out for patients who have been provided with visual and hearing aids to assess the effectiveness of these aids and to help improve the quality of life of elderly people. 52

This study has important limitations to consider. The research was based on the CHARLS study, which is a nationally representative longitudinal cohort study. Judgment of whether VI and HI are present is based on participants' self‐reports, which may contribute to information bias. Second, CHARLS subjects included people over 45 years old and did not collect information on previous health status, which may have an impact on health conditions that could influence CVD risk. Finally, this study is observational and its results may be disturbed by unmeasured confounders and limited to establish causal effects. Therefore, our study should be considered as generating hypotheses for further research.

Conclusions

In conclusion, the current study provides evidence that VI and HI and DSI are associated with CVD in Chinese middle‐aged and elderly adults. Early screening for vision and hearing and effective interventions to reduce impairment of VI and HI may be targets for the prevention of CVD.
