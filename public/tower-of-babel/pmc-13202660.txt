Prevalence of overweight and obesity among children in Greenland: a growing concern
Authors: Emma Vang Frandsen, Ida Hove Christensen, Michael Lynge Pedersen, Stine Byberg
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTo investigate the prevalence of overweight and obesity among children aged 3 to 10 years in Greenland. Further, to describe the prevalence of overweight and obesity according to sex, place of residence and age, including change over time. In a cross-sectional study, we included 4715 children aged 3–10 years, using data from the national electronic medical records. BMI was calculated and children were categorised into age and sex-specific weight classes according to cut-off values established by the International Obesity Task Force. In 2023, 37.1% of children in Greenland aged 3–10 years were classified as overweight or obese (12.6% obese). The proportion of children with normal weight was 61.1%. We saw no noticeable differences in the distribution of weight classes according to place of residence and sex. Further, our study illustrates a significant rise in the prevalence of overweight and obesity at the time of school entry, increasing from 7.5% in 1980 to 33.4% in 2023. This study demonstrates a high prevalence of overweight and obesity among children in Greenland, and further highlights a striking increase at school entry over recent decades. These findings underscore the urgent need for early prevention efforts and continued nationwide surveillance.Keywords: Overweight, obesity, children, body mass index, Greenland, Nuuk, Inuit

Introduction

Childhood obesity has emerged as a matter of serious public health concern throughout the world as its prevalence has reached epidemic proportions [1]. Overweight and obesity in childhood is associated with increased morbidity and mortality later on in life. Once obesity has become established, achieving long-term treatment outcomes is challenging [2]. It is therefore essential that childhood obesity is prevented. Furthermore, lifestyle programs can effectively reduce overweight levels in children and mitigate obesity in adolescents [3,4].

Reflecting global trends, the increasing rates of childhood obesity in Greenland are of great concern. Greenland is the world's largest Island and is located in the Arctic. In recent decades, Greenland has adopted a more Western lifestyle, and the transition from a hunter society to a more Westernised lifestyle has led to changes in dietary habits and reduced physical activity [5].

The prevalence of overweight and obesity among children in Greenland has been surveyed regularly at school entry. In the capital Nuuk, the prevalence of children classified as overweight or obese at school entry nearly tripled from 7.5% in 1980 to 21.1% in 1999 [6]. From 2000 to 2018, the prevalence remained relatively stable at around 17% [6–8]. Whether the curve has flattened remains unclear. However, previous studies have been limited by small sample sizes, restricted to weight measurements at school entry and largely confined to Nuuk rather than covering Greenland more broadly. A nationwide EMR system was implemented in Greenland from 2015 to 2017, and this enabled registration of countrywide estimates and significantly more anthropometric data are now available.

The aim of this study was to investigate the prevalence of overweight and obesity among children aged 3 to 10 years in Greenland in 2023. Further, to describe the prevalence of overweight and obesity according to sex, place of residence and age, including change over time.

Methods

We extracted the most recent height and weight measurement registered in the national EMR system, in an anonymous extract of children born between 2011 and 2020, with a permanent address in Greenland at the time of data extraction (May 2023). Furthermore, information on age, sex, place of birth, place of residence, height, weight and dates of height and weight measurements were extracted from the EMR. Age was defined as the child's age at the time of weight measurement. Ethnicity or Inuit heritage is not registered in the EMR, and it is therefore not possible to extract this information. Approximately 90% of the Greenlandic population are of Inuit descent, and approximately 10% are migrants from predominantly Denmark and Asia.

Schoolchildren in Greenland visit the public health nurse for routine health examinations at school entry (the year of their 6th birthday). Among others, height and weight measurements are obtained at the school health examinations and recorded in the EMR.

Since 2021, dental practitioners have begun to register weight and height data of children attending dental services in the national EMR system [9]. This initiative has expanded the amount of available data to include more childhood age groups as well as more measurements for each child, as well as children living outside Nuuk, depending on access to dental services.

We classified the children into age- and sex-specific weight classes based on the International Obesity Task Force (IOTF) cut-off values of BMI proposed by Cole 2000 [10], in compliance with other similar studies on childhood obesity. BMI was divided into: thinness, normal weight, overweight and obesity. These weight classes are equivalent to the adult weight classes, with thinness corresponding to an adult BMI below 18.5 kg/m2 at age 18 years. Overweight corresponds to an adult BMI above 25 kg/m2 and obesity corresponds to an adult BMI above 30 kg/m2 [10].

We calculated the prevalence of the different weight classes for all children aged 3–10 years, stratified by sex and by place of residence (the capital Nuuk vs. the rest of Greenland, and among children living in towns vs. settlements). Furthermore, we compared the prevalence results calculated in this study with the results from previous prevalence studies, performed in children living in Greenland to assess whether or not the prevalence of overweight and obesity was increasing in 2023.

Statistical analysis was performed using Stata version 18.0. Chi-square test was used to compare distributions of weight classes among groups. p-value ≤ 0.05 was used to denote the significance level.

Ethics

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee for Scientific Research Greenland (KVUG 2016-09) in Greenland and the Agency of Health and Prevention in Greenland.

Results

The study population initially consisted of 4851 children aged 3–10 years, born between 2011 and 2020, who were registered in the EMR and had a permanent residence in Greenland at the time of data extraction. A total of 75 children were excluded due to missing data on height, weight or the date of measurement. Additionally, data were reviewed for implausible anthropometric values, resulting in the exclusion of 30 children with abnormal height or weight measurements. We assessed whether height and weight were abnormal by using growth charts based on Greenlandic children [11]. Children whose weight or height fell below the 3rd percentile or above the 97th percentile were manually reviewed to avoid the inclusion of potential input errors. One child was excluded because of missing data on sex. Children were also excluded if there were more than 183 days between measurements of height and weight, as this could have resulted in inaccurate BMI calculations. After all exclusion criteria were applied, the final study population comprised 4715 children aged 3–10 years, equivalent to 75% of all children living in Greenland aged 3–10 years in 2023 [12].

In contrast, the present study showed a marked increase to 33.4% in 2019–2023, the highest prevalence reported to date among children at school entry in Nuuk.

Discussion

Our results reveal a pronounced and concerning increase in the prevalence of overweight and obesity among children in Greenland.

In our study, the prevalence of overweight and obesity among children in Greenland aged 3–10 years in 2023 was 37.1%. This is the first study to assess the prevalence of overweight and obesity in a sample representing children from all of Greenland, limiting the possibility for direct comparisons with earlier national studies.

Our study finds that a lower proportion of overweight and obese children live in Nuuk (34.9%) compared with children living outside Nuuk (38.3%). Consistent with our findings, Budtz et al. also found a higher proportion of overweight and obesity in children living in the rest of Greenland compared to children living in Nuuk [8]. From the schoolchildren's survey in 2018, children living outside Nuuk generally had a higher intake of sugar-sweetened soft drinks [14]. Foods and beverages with high sugar content are often a significant factor in weight gain and may explain the higher prevalence of overweight and obesity in children outside Nuuk. On the other hand, children living outside Nuuk were more active in their daily lives and tended to eat more homemade food [14].

Additionally, a higher prevalence of overweight and obesity was observed among children aged 3–4 years, followed by a decline towards 7 years of age and a subsequent increase thereafter, as seen in Figure 2. This pattern may reflect the physiological phenomenon of BMI rebound, whereby BMI decreases during early childhood, reaches a nadir between 4 and 7 years of age, before increasing again throughout later childhood [15]. This may help explain the relatively higher prevalence observed in the youngest age groups. Importantly, while this pattern may partly explain variation across age groups, the prevalence of overweight and obesity remains high throughout childhood, as shown in Figure 2, indication that excess weight is already established in early childhood. This underscores the importance of early preventive efforts at preschool age, including the promotion of healthy diet and lifestyle behaviours.

Since the first study on childhood obesity in Greenland in 1980, the prevalence of overweight and obesity among children at school entry has risen from 7.5% to 33.4% in 2023 [6].

Overall, overweight and obesity have increased markedly in Greenland, including among adults [16]. The increased prevalence of overweight and obesity in Greenland is most likely multifactorial, with a significant effect of the adoption of a more Western lifestyle in recent decades. Population surveys among adult Greenlanders found that less Greenlandic food is consumed and substituted by more processed imported food [17]. Furthermore, physical activity levels have also been declining [14,17].

The unique genetic composition of the Inuit includes genetic adaptations that may play a role in metabolising and utilising the traditional diet, which is rich in animal fats and proteins and low in carbohydrates. The genetic composition of the Greenlandic Inuit may predispose them to overweight and obesity when exposed to a lifestyle with greater and more consistent access to calories than in the traditional diet, especially when combined with a more sedentary lifestyle and a carbohydrate-rich diet [18]. One example is the TBC1D4 variant, which substantially increases the risk of type 2 diabetes among homozygous carriers. Controlled dietary interventions showed that adherence to a traditional low-carbohydrate diet improved glycaemic control, reduced LDL:HDL cholesterol ratio and promoted modest weight loss compared with a Western diet. These findings highlight that genetic susceptibility interacts with dietary changes, and that the transition towards westernised eating patterns may exacerbate metabolic risk in genetically predisposed individuals [19].

Furthermore, an “obesity gene” has also been identified in the Greenlandic Inuit. Homozygous carriers of the ADCY3 gene in general have 7.3 kg/m2 higher BMI than non-carriers [20]. However, as the variant is found in only 2–3% of the population, it cannot explain the high overall prevalence of overweight and obesity.

In addition, a large proportion of the Greenlandic Inuit population has some degree of European admixture, which may further dilute the impact of Arctic-specific genetic variants.

It is also possible that additional, currently unidentified genetic variants contribute to the development of obesity in the Greenlandic population; however, evidence for such variants remains lacking.

Other countries

In comparison to the Inuit child population in Nunavik, Canada [21], our study found a higher prevalence of overweight and obesity. In Nunavik, the combined prevalence of overweight and obesity was 26.9% (6.6% obese) among children aged 8–14 years between 2005 and 2010, while the combined prevalence of overweight and obesity in our study was 37.1% (12% obese).

However, the age groups are not directly comparable, as the Canadian study included older children (8–14 years), whereas our study focused on younger children aged 3–10 years. Furthermore, the Canadian study was conducted between 2005 and 2010. This temporal difference limits direct comparability, as prevalence trends may have changed in Canada over time, similar to the increase observed in Greenland. Around the same time as the Canadian study, Schnohr et al. (2000–2004) reported a combined prevalence of overweight and obesity of 21.7% among children at school entry in Greenland, slightly lower than in Nunavik [6].

Given the drastic increase from 2000 to 2004 to our study in 2023, one can speculate whether the prevalence among Inuit children in Canada has also increased over time, following a similar trend observed in Greenland. To our knowledge, no recent studies have been conducted among Inuit populations in Canada.

Our study found that 33.4% of 5.5–7.5-year-old children in Greenland were overweight or obese. Compared with the Nordic countries, the prevalence of overweight and obesity among 7-year-old children was 13.4% in Denmark, 23.4% in Sweden and 22.1% in Finland [22]. Thus, the prevalence of overweight and obesity in Greenland differs markedly compared to the other Nordic countries. In the HBSC international report from 2021 to 2022, it is stated that the highest levels of physical inactivity were found in Greenland, where almost 60% of adolescents were classified as physically inactive. In contrast, fewer than 19% in Denmark and 22% in Sweden were physically inactive, while the proportion was even lower in Finland, where less than 10% were classified as physically inactive [23]. Among 10- to 12-year-old children, dietary patterns differ markedly across the Nordic region. In Greenland, 25% of children report daily consumption of sugar-sweetened soft drinks, whereas the corresponding prevalence in Denmark is only 5.5%. Daily consumption of sweets and chocolate follows the same pattern, reaching 27% in Greenland but only 8% in Denmark. Almost half of Danish and Swedish children (both 45%) eat vegetables every day, compared with just 26% of Greenlandic children [24–26].

Overall, Greenlandic children stand out for their high daily intake of sugar-sweetened beverages and confectionery, combined with a relatively low intake of vegetables. At the same time, physical inactivity is more prevalent among Greenlandic children than among children in any other Nordic country. Together, these lifestyle factors likely play a significant role in the high rates of overweight and obesity observed among Greenlandic children.

Strengths and limitations

To our knowledge, this is the first nationwide study investigating the prevalence of overweight and obesity among children in Greenland. Using the EMR, we were able to access and include data for 75% of all children aged 3–10 years in Greenland, representing both urban areas and remote settlements. The large sample size and broad geographical coverage are major strengths of the study, in contrast to previous studies with small sample sizes and limited to school entry data from Nuuk. However, the proportion of missing data in settlements was larger than in the towns. This is likely attributed to the limited access to healthcare services in the rest of Greenland. Greenland is the world's largest island, but it has a very low population density. Many settlements are isolated and can only be reached by boat or helicopter, making it difficult to provide easy access to healthcare services.

Nevertheless, the included children from settlements are likely to represent a largely random subset rather than a systematically selected group. Inclusion was primarily driven by the presence of healthcare personnel rather than by characteristics of the children. Thus, the included children can be considered broadly representative of the settlement population, and it is unlikely that inclusion of the remaining children would have substantially changed the overall obesity prevalence.

As previously mentioned, ethnicity or Inuit heritage is not registered in the EMR, and it is therefore not possible to distinguish between Inuit and non-Inuit children in the present analyses. Approximately 10% of the population in Greenland are migrants, which may introduce some heterogeneity in the study population. However, given the relatively small proportion of non-Inuit individuals, this is unlikely to substantially influence the overall findings, particularly in relation to genetic predisposition.

BMI is an index of weight to height (kg/m2) commonly used as a measure of excess body fat in a population [27]. In children, BMI varies strongly with age and sex. Therefore, age- and sex-specific cut-offs were applied to classify children as thin, normal weight, overweight or obese.

The applicability of BMI in Inuit populations has been debated, as differences in body proportions, with Inuit people having shorter extremities relative to torso length, may lead to a systematic overestimation of adiposity when using standard BMI cut-offs [28,29]. However, the IOTF cut-off values applied in this study have been suggested as the most appropriate for assessing the prevalence of overweight and obesity in Inuit children [21]. Importantly, because identical IOTF cut-offs have been used in previous Greenlandic studies, comparisons of prevalence and weight categories over time remain internally valid, despite potential limitations of BMI as a measure of adiposity.

A limitation of the present study is that information on socioeconomic status and lifestyle factors was not available in the dataset used for the analyses. Consequently, we were unable to examine the potential influence of these factors on the prevalence of overweight among children in Greenland. Socioeconomic and lifestyle factors are known to play an important role in the development of childhood overweight [30–32]. Future studies including more detailed information on socioeconomic and lifestyle determinants may provide further insight into these associations.

Perspectives and conclusion

In conclusion, our study showed that the prevalence of overweight and obesity among children in Greenland has increased markedly since 1980. According to our study, the prevalence was 37.1% of children aged 3–10 years being either overweight or obese.

The increasing prevalence of overweight and obesity among children in Greenland has reached alarming levels with far-reaching consequences not only for the individuals affected but also for the society and the healthcare system. Alongside the rising rates among children, a similar increase in the prevalence of overweight, obesity and chronic diseases (e.g. type-2 diabetes and cardiovascular diseases) is observed among adults [16,18,33]. This development places a growing pressure on the Greenlandic healthcare system, both in terms of resources and finances, and will become increasingly difficult to manage unless preventive measures are implemented.

The findings of this study emphasise the urgent need for the establishment of comprehensive programs for prevention and intervention. These should target both children and adults. Such efforts are crucial in breaking the intergenerational obesity and thereby diminishing the long-term burden on the healthcare system.
