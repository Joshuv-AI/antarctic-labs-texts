Improving T cell expansion by optogenetically engineered bacteria-loaded MMP-2-responsive cyclophosphamide for antitumor immunotherapy
Authors: Jiawen Chen, Renjiang Kong, Yuzhi Qiu, Sihan Chen, Yidi Liu, Xi Yu, Xiangliang Yang, Yan Zhang, Yanhong Zhu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The efficacy of antitumor immunotherapy is closely associated with the expansion of tumor-infiltrating CD8+ T cells. However, within the tumor microenvironment, CD8+ T cells often exhibit reduced proliferation due to persistent exposure to tumor antigens. The cytokine IL-2 is a potent growth factor that can drive the expansion of tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes. While its clinical application has been severely limited by systemic toxicity and in vivo instability. To address these challenges, we have developed a dual-responsive system (EcNIL-2@UCNP/Gel-CTX) leveraging the hypoxic tropisms of E. coli Nissle 1917(EcN). This system is capable of producing IL-2 in situ upon near-infrared (NIR) irradiation and releasing low-dose cyclophosphamide (CTX) in response to matrix metalloproteinase-2 (MMP-2) in the tumor microenvironment. The EcNIL-2@UCNP/Gel-CTX system not only drives the expansion of CD8+ T cells and boost the activity of NK cells but also reduces Treg cell populations, thereby remodeling the immune microenvironment and eliciting robust tumor-specific immune responses in H22 subcutaneous tumors in mice and confers long-term protection against tumor rechallenge by promoting the generation of durable memory T cells. Our findings provide an both light and tumor microenvironment responsive platform for enhanced cancer immunotherapy.Graphical Abstract We have developed a dual-responsive system, EcNIL-2@UCNP/Gel-CTX, which enables in situ interleukin-2 (IL-2) production under near-infrared (NIR) irradiation and releases low-dose cyclophosphamide (CTX) in response to matrix metalloproteinase-2 (MMP-2) within the tumor microenvironment. This system not only enhances CD8+ T cell expansion and NK cell activity but also suppresses regulatory T cell (Treg) populations, effectively remodeling the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment. In murine H22 subcutaneous tumor models, EcNIL-2@UCNP/Gel-CTX elicited potent tumor-specific immune responses and conferred long-term protection against tumor rechallenge by inducing durable memory T cell generation. Introduction

Immune checkpoint therapy relies on the activation of cytotoxic T lymphocytes that tumors exploit to evade immune destruction, and has shown notable therapeutic benefit in clinical cancer management [1]. However, its clinical benefits are only observed in certain patient subsets and limited to specific tumor types [2, 3]. Studies indicate that tumor infiltrating CD8+ T cells often exhibit decreased proliferation and cytotoxic capability due to continuously stimulation of tumor antigens and inflammatory factors within solid tumors [4]. Moreover, many inhibitory cells in the tumor microenvironment, such as, regulatory T cells (Tregs), release immunosuppressive cytokines to further impair and inhibit the anti-tumor functions of CD8+ T cells [5]. These multiple resistance mechanisms highlight the pressing need for effective combinational strategies that target these resistance pathways and improve clinical responses.

IL-2 is tightly correlated to T cell proliferation and effector functions, such as potentiating the expansion of CD8+ T cells [6]. IL-2 was approved by the US Food and Drug Administration for the treatments of melanoma and renal cell carcinoma. However, its clinical application is limited by significant systemic toxicity and the short half-life time [7, 8]. This limitation highlights the need for advanced delivery systems that increase IL-2 concentrations specifically at tumor sites, minimizing toxicity, dosing frequency, and improving CD8+ T cell proliferation [9]. Although nanovectors had increased the targeted delivery of IL-2 to some extent, physical and physiological barriers within the body limit its delivery efficiency [10].

Certain anaerobic or facultative anaerobic bacteria present a pronounced hypoxia tropism, enabling the selective accumulation and colonization within both solid and metastatic tumors [11]. A variety of bacteria, including Salmonella, Escherichia coli (E. coli), Clostridium, and Bifidobacterium have been developed for tumor treatments [12]. Attenuated bacteria hold innate immunomodulatory effect, activating immune cells and promoting cytokine release [13]. In addition, these bacteria can be conveniently modified by genetic or chemical methods to serve as tailored drug-delivery vehicles [14]. With development of synthetic biology, E. coli has been engineered as a programmable living vector for producing cytokines, immunomodulators, or chemotherapeutics in situ, thereby improving both safety and therapeutic efficacy [15]. The production of IL-2 by genetically-engineered tumor-targeted bacteria may largely decrease the systemic toxicity of IL-2 [16]. Moreover, the production of IL-2 by engineered bacteria within the tumor may overcome its short half-life during circulation [17]. Therefore, localized production of IL-2 brings a promising strategy for driving CD8+ T cell proliferation for tumor cell killing.

Cyclophosphamide (CTX), a chemotherapeutic drug, has been included in various regimens for its immunomodulatory effects [18–20]. Low-dose CTX (≤ 20 mg/kg) selectively reduces Treg number and function while enhancing CD8⁺ T and NK cell activity [21–24]. IL-2 activates effector T cells but also expands CD25⁺ Tregs, attenuating antitumor immunity [25, 26]. The combination of IL-2 with low-dose CTX elicited synergistic effects, amplifying IL-2-mediated immune activation [27–30]. Preclinical and clinical studies show that CTX prevents IL-2–induced Treg expansion, remodels their function, and improves the immune milieu, thereby amplifying IL-2-mediated antitumor responses [31, 32]. However, the “on-demand” production of IL-2 and release of CTX in situ remained a challenge. Herein, a both light and matrix metalloproteinase-2 (MMP-2) response system (EcNIL−2@UCNP/Gel-CTX) was prepared, producing IL-2 upon near-infrared (NIR) light irradiation, and release of low-dose cyclophosphamide (CTX) responsive to MMP-2. A blue light-sensitive system for IL-2 expression by integrating the EL222 sensor gene and the IL-2-mCherry fusion gene were transferred into the pET20b plasmid (pET20b-EL222-IL-2-mCherry). The mCherry protein serves as a reporter, enabling the construction of an IL-2 expression system in E. coli. Due to the limited tissue penetration of blue light, we synthesized upconversion nanoparticles (UCNPs) that convert NIR light to blue light. Additionally, gelatin nanoparticles loaded with low-dose CTX, responding to MMP-2, were synthesized using a one-phase condensation method. By employing EDC/NHS amide coupling, UCNPs and Gel-CTX were conjugated onto EcNIL−2, creating a drug-loaded engineered bacterial system (EcNIL−2@UCNP/Gel-CTX). This system allows for NIR-induced in situ expression of IL-2, alongside the MMP-2 responsive release of CTX. Strong antitumor immune responses were induced in vitro and in vivo (Fig. 1).

Results and discussion

Preparation and characterization of EcNIL−2@UCNP/Gel-CTX

In this study, the recombinant plasmid pET20b-EL222-IL-2/mCherry was constructed, in which blue light-sensitive EL222 and IL-2 gene sequences were inserted into plasmid pET20b, and mCherry was used as a reporter gene (Fig. S1). The recombinant plasmid was transfected into EcN to obtain EcNIL−2 with the ability of expressing the secretory IL-2 under blue light-irradiation. Due to the shallow penetration of blue light, UCNPs with the ability to convert deep-penetrating NIR into blue light were conjugated onto the surface of EcNIL−2. At the same time, Gel loaded with CTX, was coupled with EcNIL−2 via EDC/NHS chemistry to construct an EcNIL−2@UCNP/Gel-CTX with both light and MMP-2 response abilities.

TEM results showed that UCNPs were dispersed uniformly in hexagonal form (Fig. 2A). As shown in Fig. 2B and C, the size of UCNP was 66.91 ± 1.87 nm and the zeta potential was 24.53 ± 0.45 mV. The size of spherical Gel-CTX particle was 207.67 ± 4.61 nm, and zeta potential was − 3.39 ± 0.57 mV by DLS. After coupling UCNP/Gel-CTX with EcNIL−2, the size of EcNIL−2@UCNP/Gel-CTX was 1827.67 ± 127.77 nm, and zeta potential was − 1.13 ± 0.07 mV. Under NIR irradiation, the blue light emission from the EcNIL−2@UCNP and EcNIL−2@UCNP/Gel-CTX were similar to that from the UCNPs (Fig. 2D). While no blue light emission was found in the EcNIL−2 and Gel-CTX groups. These results indicated that UCNPs were successfully conjugated with bacteria and the conjugation does not affect the light conversion ability of UCNPs.

To detect the co-location of EcNIL−2 and CTX, CTX in Gel was replaced by FITC and the membrane of EcNIL−2 was stained with DiI. The images under laser confocal microscopy showed Gel-FITC attached onto the bacteria (Fig. 2E). CTX drug loading rate was 5.06% in Gel using HPLC method, which indicated that the prepared nanoparticles have high drug loading efficiency. As shown in the Figure S2, the particle size remained unchanged across all media, demonstrating excellent dimensional stability in both PBS and serum-containing environments. According to the high expression of MMP-2 in the tumor microenvironment, 10 µg/mL MMP-2 were added into Gel-CTX solution. Thereafter, the release behavior of CTX in Gel-CTX nanoparticles was detected by HPLC, and the release curves of CTX with or without MMP-2 were drawn, respectively (Fig. 2F). CTX released from Gel-CTX achieves 70% in 24 h after treatment with 10 µg/mL MMP-2, indicating that Gel-CTX exhibited good sustained-release properties.

As shown in the growth curve (Figure S3), all three groups (EcNIL−2, EcNIL−2@UCNP, and EcNIL−2@UCNP/Gel-CTX) exhibited a typical bacterial growth trend, including an initial lag phase (0–2 h), onset of logarithmic growth (4–6 h), and rapid exponential increase at 8 h. Importantly, the engineered strains maintained growth kinetics comparable to wild-type EcN, indicating that genetic modification and nanoparticle conjugation had no significant effect on the growth rate of EcNIL−2.

The fluorescence signal of mCherry could be detected after blue light illumination (BL+), and increased in a time-dependent manner by analysis of flow cytometry, which demonstrated that the blue-light responsive EcNIL−2 was successfully constructed (Fig. S4). Moreover, in Fig. S5A, the fluorescence signal of mCherry in the EcNIL−2 (BL+) group was higher than that in the EcNIL−2@UCNP (NIR+) group, which may be attributed to the partial loss of energy during light conversion. Furthermore, western blot results showed that the expression of mCherry both in bacteria and their supernatants in the EcNIL−2 (BL+) and EcNIL−2@UCNP (NIR+) groups (Fig. S5B). Altogether, after coupling with UCNP, the EcNIL−2 can express and secrete IL-2 upon NIR irradiation. After 30 min of NIR irradiation, the fluorescence intensity of mCherry significantly increased (Fig. S6). Moreover, the fluorescence intensity of mCherry in the EcNIL−2@UCNP/Gel-CTX group increased in a time and power-dependent manner, which indicates that IL-2 expression can be controlled precisely (Fig. 2G and S7). The images under the confocal microscope showed the similar results (Fig. 2H). The IL-2 levels in the supernatant of EcNIL−2@UCNP/Gel-CTX (NIR+) reached 718.08 pg/mL, as determined by ELISA (Fig. 2I). These results indicate that EcNIL−2@UCNP/Gel-CTX has super capability to induce the expression of IL-2 under NIR.

Immunoactivation of EcNIL−2@UCNP/Gel-CTX in vitro

Tumor infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) are tightly associated with the efficiency of tumor immunotherapy [33, 34]. While IL-2 played a crucial role in the expansion of TILs, especially in T cells and NK cells. In this study, TILs were separated from tumors, and incubated with EcNIL−2@UCNP/Gel-CTX under NIR irradiation and MMP-2 (10 µg/mL) to analyze the activation of immune cells.

The activation of T cells requires both the engagement of the T cell receptor (TCR) with antigens presented by antigen-presenting cells (APCs) and the interaction of the co-stimulatory receptor CD28 on T cells with the co-stimulatory ligands B7-1/B7-2 on APCs [35]. Tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) are typically stimulated by antigen-presenting cells (APCs) in the tumor microenvironment. Therefore, TILs isolated from a cancer biopsy can be greatly expanded by adding IL-2, bypassing the interaction with APCs [36]. In this study, TILs collected from tumors were directly incubated with the supernatant of EcNIL−2@UCNP/Gel-CTX after NIR irradiation and MMP-2 digestion. As shown in Fig. 3A, the number of CD8+ T cells increased significantly in the EcNIL−2@UCNP (NIR+) and EcNIL−2@UCNP/Gel-CTX (NIR+, MMP- 2+) groups. Among them, CD8+ T cells in the EcNIL−2@UCNP/Gel-CTX (NIR+, MMP-2+) group are higher than other groups, presenting 2.07 and 1.73-fold higher than PBS and EcNIL−2 groups, respectively (Fig. 3B). Altogether, the combination of NIR-induced IL-2 and low dose CTX can activate T cells significantly. Moreover, NK cells (CD49b+) activation in the EcNIL−2@UCNP/Gel-CTX (NIR+, MMP-2+) group presented the similar trends to that of T cells (Fig. 3C and D). IL-2 is generally considered promoting T-cell proliferation and enhancing effector T-cell function, yet unexpectedly inducing immune tolerance and promoting Tregs development. As shown in Fig. 3E and F, the ratio of Tregs increased in the EcNIL−2@UCNP (NIR+) group. While Tregs (CD25+/Foxp3+) were largely inhibited by the supernatant of EcNIL−2@UCNP/Gel-CTX due to low dose CTX release after MMP-2 digestion.

IFN-γ and Granzyme B (Gra B) were the key markers of active T cells and NK cells. IFN-γ in the EcNIL−2@UCNP/Gel-CTX (NIR+, MMP-2+) group was 2.45, 1,85 and 1.24-fold higher than that in the PBS, EcNIL−2 and EcNIL−2@UCNP (NIR+) groups, respectively (Fig. 3G). The level of Gra B in the EcNIL−2@UCNP/Gel-CTX (NIR+, MMP-2+) group was 2.71, 1.69 and 1.37-fold higher than the PBS, EcNIL−2 and EcNIL−2@UCNP (NIR+) groups, respectively (Fig. 3H). Altogether, NIR-inducing IL-2 combined with low dose CTX can effectively activate TILs, and promote TILs to secrete antitumor elements.

To exclude the direct anti-tumor effect, EcNIL−2@UCNP/Gel-CTX were co-cultured with H22 cells in vitro. No significant changes in cell viability were found (Fig. S8). Afterwards, to mimic the immune cells killing tumor cells in vivo, TILs after different treatments were co-incubated with H22 cells. As shown in the Fig. 3I, the cytotoxicity in the EcNIL−2@UCNP/Gel-CTX (NIR+, MMP-2+) group achieved to 21.77% higher than 15.93% in the EcNIL−2@UCNP (NIR+) group. The result provided evidence that the ability of TILs to kill tumor cells was significantly enhanced in the EcNIL−2@UCNP/Gel-CTX (NIR+, MMP-2+) group.

The biodistribution of EcNIL−2@UCNP/Gel-CTX in tumor-bearing mice

As shown in Fig. 4A, the bio-distribution of EcNIL−2@UCNP/Gel-CTX in vivo was detected by IVIS imaging system after staining with IR-780. At different time points, the fluorescence signal increased over time, and achieved to the peak at 48 h (Fig. 4B). Moreover, the major organs, such as heart, liver, spleen, lung, kidney, and tumor were resected for analysis of fluorescence signal at 72 h. The results showed that the fluorescence intensity in tumors was far higher than that in other tissues (Fig. 4C and D). After intravenous administration of EcNIL−2@UCNP/Gel-CTX (Fig. S9), blood CFUs declined sharply within the first 12 h and became nearly undetectable within 24–72 h. Low levels of colonies were detected in normal organs such as the heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney within 72 h. In contrast, tumor tissues exhibited markedly higher bacterial accumulation. These findings demonstrate the tumor-targeting ability of EcNIL−2@UCNP/Gel-CTX.

In vivo antitumor efficacy of EcNIL−2@UCNP/Gel-CTX (NIR+)

The antitumor effects of EcNIL−2@UCNP/Gel-CTX (NIR+) were further evaluated in vivo. Eight groups were divided as follows: PBS, CTX, EcNIL−2, EcNIL−2 (NIR+), EcNIL−2@UCNP, EcNIL−2@UCNP (NIR+), EcNIL−2@UCNP/Gel-CTX, and EcNIL−2@UCNP/Gel-CTX (NIR+). The experiment design is shown in Fig. 4E, no significant difference was found between PBS and CTX groups, which indicated low dose CTX had no significant anti-tumor efficacy. Tumors were inhibited in EcNIL−2, EcNIL−2 (NIR+), EcNIL−2@UCNP and EcNIL−2@UCNP/Gel-CTX to some extent, which may be attributed to the poor immunogenicity of EcN. Tumor suppression was achieved at 62.20% and 85.05% in EcNIL−2@UCNP (NIR+) and EcNIL−2@UCNP/Gel-CTX (NIR+) groups, respectively (Fig. 4G). Both tumor images and tumor weight data showed consistent results (Fig. 4F and H). The results indicated that IL-2 combined with low-dose CTX could inhibit the growth of H22 tumors. After staining by H&E, TUNEL and Ki67 staining, the tumor tissues in the EcNIL−2@UCNP/Gel-CTX (NIR+) group showed the weakest cell proliferation (Fig. 4I).

In vivo anti-tumor immune activation of EcNIL−2@UCNP/Gel-CTX (NIR+)

To further investigate the antitumor mechanism of EcNIL−2@UCNP/Gel-CTX (NIR+), DCs maturation in lymph nodes was analyzed by flow cytometry after treatments on the 3rd day after the last irradiation (after second i.v. injection). As shown in Fig. 5A and B, CTX alone cannot induce DCs maturation, which is similar to the PBS group. DCs maturation was found to some extent in the EcNIL−2 (NIR+), EcNIL−2, EcNIL−2@UCNP, and EcNIL−2@UCNP/Gel-CTX groups. CD80+ CD86+ DCs ratio in EcNIL−2@UCNP (NIR+) and EcNIL−2@UCNP/Gel-CTX (NIR+) groups was 1.74 and 1.84-fold higher than PBS group, respectively. CD8+ T cells in tumor-draining lymph nodes (TDLN) in EcNIL−2@UCNP (NIR+) and EcNIL−2@UCNP/Gel-CTX (NIR+) groups achieved at 42.8% and 43.9%, respectively, higher than other groups (Fig. 5C). The highest ratio of CD8+/CD4+ T cells was found in the EcNIL−2@UCNP/Gel-CTX (NIR+) group (Fig. S10).

To evaluate the immune response induced by EcNIL−2@UCNP/Gel-CTX (NIR+) in H22 tumor-bearing mice, CD8+ T and CD4+ T cells in tumors were analyzed by flow cytometry on the 5th day after the last irradiation. As shown in Fig. 5D and E, there was no significant difference in the infiltration of CD8+ T cells among the EcNIL−2, EcNIL−2 (NIR+), EcNIL−2@UCNP, and EcNIL−2@UCNP/Gel-CTX groups, while all showed slightly higher levels compared to both the PBS and CTX groups. The highest ratio of CD8+ T cells was presented in the EcNIL−2@UCNP/Gel-CTX (NIR+) group, which was 2.73, 1.73 and 1.29-fold higher than that in the PBS, EcNIL−2, and EcNIL−2@UCNP (NIR+) groups, respectively. A similar tendency was found in CD4+ T cells in deferent groups (Fig. S11). The ratio of NK cells in tumor tissues in the EcNIL−2@UCNP (NIR+) group was also higher than that in other groups (Fig. 5F, S12). Altogether, the combination of IL-2 and low dose CTX greatly promoted the infiltration and activation of T cells and NK cells.

Moreover, fluorescence intensity of Foxp3, a marker of Tregs, increased in the EcNIL−2@UCNP (NIR+) group, whereas it largely decreased in the EcNIL−2@UCNP/Gel-CTX (NIR+) group after immune-fluorescence staining, which may be attributed to the fact that low dose CTX inhibit Tregs induced by IL-2 (Fig. 5G). Fluorescence intensity of Granz B and IFN-γ in the EcNIL−2@UCNP/Gel-CTX (NIR+) group was higher than that in other groups, which was consistent with the results in vitro.

To explore the systemic immune activation mediated by EcNIL−2@UCNP/Gel-CTX (NIR+) in H22 tumor-bearing mice, spleen tissues were harvested and homogenized to obtain single-cell suspensions for analysis. The proportions of CD8+T cells and CD4+T cells were detected by flow cytometry. The test results are shown in Fig. 5H, after treatment with EcNIL−2@UCNP/Gel-CTX (NIR+), the proportion of CD8+ T cells in splenic T cells was 15.6%, which was significantly higher than the 8.33% in the PBS group (Fig. 5I). The ratio of CD8+ T to CD4+ T cells in the EcNIL−2@UCNP/Gel-CTX (NIR+) group was higher than other groups (Fig. 5J). The results indicated that EcNIL−2@UCNP/Gel-CTX (NIR+) treatment can significantly increase the infiltration of CD8+T cells in the spleen of mice, causing a systemic immune response.

Moreover, the level of IFN-γ in the EcNIL−2@UCNP/Gel-CTX (NIR+) group, was 2.92 and 2.65-fold higher than that in PBS and CTX groups, respectively (Fig. 5K). And TNF-α was 2.07 and 1.96-fold higher than that in PBS and CTX groups, respectively (Fig. 5L). Treg-related cytokines IL-10 and TGF-β in the EcNIL−2@UCNP/Gel-CTX (NIR+) group decreased by 1.15 and 1.01-fold compared to that in the EcNIL−2@UCNP (NIR+) group (Fig. 5M and N). Altogether, EcNIL−2@UCNP/Gel-CTX (NIR+) activated the immune response in TME and improved the tumor immune suppression microenvironments.

Long-term immune memory

To investigate the immune memory induced by EcNIL−2@UCNP/Gel-CTX (NIR+), experiments were designed as shown in Fig. 6A. T cells in spleen were analyzed on the 7th day after the last treatment in part of the mice. Tumors were resected on the 8th day after the last treatment in the other tumor-bearing mice. On the 18th day after the last treatment, H22 cells were rechallenged subcutaneously on the opposite side. As shown in Fig. S13, the significant antitumor effect of EcNIL−2@UCNP Gel-CTX (NIR+) was found after three administrations. Tumor inhibition achieved to 92.2% after administration of EcNIL−2@UCNP/Gel-CTX (NIR+) 3 times.

As shown in Fig. 6B and C, T memory cells in spleen could be induced by EcNIL−2@UCNP (NIR+) and EcNIL−2@UCNP/Gel-CTX (NIR+). The highest immune memory was found in the EcNIL−2@UCNP/Gel-CTX (NIR+) group. Moreover, lymphocytes collected from the spleen were treated with H22 tumor antigen from ultrasound-induced cell lysis, showing significant specific H22 cells killing effects, which indicates EcNIL−2@UCNP/Gel-CTX (NIR+) could effectively train the immune memory (Fig. 6D).

Biosafety analysis

Biochemical analysis was carried out to detect alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), lactic dehydrogenase (LDH), creatine kinase (CK), urea nitrogen (BUN), and creatinine (CREA) in sera for evaluating systemic safety of EcNIL−2@UCNP/Gel-CTX(NIR+). As shown in Fig. 7A-F, those blood biochemical parameters were in the normal range. Mouse body weight (Fig. S14) maintained stability during the monitoring period, and the staining of different tissues by H&E (Fig. 7G) showed no significant changes after treatment with EcNIL−2@UCNP/Gel-CTX (NIR+). Therefore, EcNIL−2@UCNP/Gel-CTX (NIR+) presented good biocompatibility in vivo.

Conclusion

Tumor-infiltrating CD8+ T cells, which are closely associated with the efficacy of antitumor immunotherapy, are often insufficient for effectively killing tumor cells within the tumor microenvironment. The cytokine IL-2 can drive the expansion of activated T populations. However, its clinical application is limited by systemic toxicity and in vivo instability. To address these limitations, we constructed a genetically engineered bacteria-based system (EcNIL−2@UCNP/Gel-CTX) that responds to near-infrared (NIR) light and matrix metalloproteinase-2 (MMP-2) for cancer immunotherapy. Specifically, IL-2 is secreted upon exposure to NIR light, while the release of cyclophosphamide (CTX) is triggered by MMP-2 from the EcNIL−2@UCNP/Gel-CTX system. This dual-responsive design expands CD8+ T cells, and NK cells within the tumor while inhibiting Tregs, thereby eliciting robust anti-tumor immune responses and long-lasting immune memory in mice. Compared with previously reported CTX/IL-2 combination therapies, the EcNIL−2@UCNP/Gel-CTX platform offers several advantages. Foremost is its dual-responsive controlled-release mechanism, which enables spatiotemporal coordination of CTX and IL-2 delivery within the immunosuppressive TME. By localizing IL-2 release to sites of TME while restricting CTX activation to the core of solid tumors, EcNIL−2@UCNP/Gel-CTX minimizes peripheral exposure, thereby widening the therapeutic index. Methods

Bacteria and plasmid

Escherichia. coli Nissle1917 (EcN) was used in this study. The IL-2 sequences incorporated in the plasmid construct is murine IL-2 (Mus musculus), which was selected to match the immune system of BALB/c mice used in the study. The recombinant plasmid (pET20b-EL222-IL-2-mCherry) was constructed by Wuhan Miaoling Biotechnology Co. Ltd, and was transfected into EcN by electroporation (2.5 kV, 25 µF, 200 Ω, 5 ms). Plasmid-transfected EcN (EcNIL−2) was cultured in Luria-Bertani (LB) agar plates or LB medium supplemented with antibiotics (100 µg∙mL− 1 ampicillin). The engineered bacteria were cultured in a shaking incubator (180 rpm) at 37 °C. The detailed plasmid information was shown in Fig. S1.

Synthesis and surface modification of NaYF4:Yb, Tm@NaYbF4,Nd@NaGdF4 (UCNPs)

The synthesis and modification of UCNPs in this study were performed according to the previous reports [37, 38].

Preparation of Gel-CTX

Type A gelatin (0.1 g) and CTX (8 mg) were dissolved in 10 mL of deionized water at 45℃ under stirring to ensure full swelling. Subsequently, 150 µL of Tween-20 were added under stirring. The pH was adjusted to 7.0 using a 0.5 M NaOH. Afterwards, approximately 7 mL of Na2SO4 solution (0.5 M) was added dropwise to achieve a continuous turbidity. Then 1 mL of isopropanol was added to obtain the clear solution. Thereafter, 25% glutaraldehyde was added into the mixture by a constant flow injection pump at a rate of 0.05 mL/min under the stirring speed of 800 rpm for 15 min. The unreacted glutaraldehyde and the rest of the unloaded drugs were removed using an ultrafiltration centrifugation method. The prepared gelatin nanoparticles were then lyophilized for storage.

Drug release and stability profile of Gel-CTX

The content of free CTX was determined using HPLC with an Elite C18 column, and a detection wavelength was set at 197.0 nm. The mobile phase consisted of a mixture of acetonitrile and water at a ratio of 30:70. The flow rate was maintained at 1.2 mL/min.

Preparation of EcNIL−2@UCNP and EcNIL−2@UCNP/Gel-CTX

UCNPs (40 mg), Gel-CTX (40 mg), EDC (27 mg) and sulfo-NHS (14 mg) were added into MES buffer (10 mL, 0.1 M, pH 6.0), and then stirred at room temperature for 4 h. After washing with PBS for 3 times, 109 CFU EcNIL−2 in PBS were added into the deposition, and stirred at room temperature for 12 h. EcNIL−2@UCNP/Gel-CTX were obtained after removing the remaining UCNPs and Gel-CTX by centrifugation (8000 rpm, 10 min). EcNIL−2@UCNP/Gel-CTX were resuspended in PBS or LB culture medium. EcNIL−2@UCNP as the control was prepared without Gel-CTX.

Characterization of EcNIL−2, UCNPs, Gel-CTX, EcNIL−2@UCNP and EcNIL−2@UCNP/Gel-CTX

The emission spectra of EcNIL−2, UCNPs, Gel-CTX, EcNIL−2@UCNP and EcNIL−2@UCNP/Gel-CTX were measured by the time-resolved fluorescence spectrometer (FluoroMax+, Japan) with an additional 808 nm laser. Those particles were determined by HT7700 TEM (Hitachi, Japan) at a voltage of 100 kV and the size and zeta potential were tested by Nano Zetasizer ZS90 (Malvern, UK).

Gelatin nanoparticles loaded with FITC (Gel-FITC) and EcNIL−2 labeled with DiI dye were coupled as described above to obtain EcNIL−2@UCNP/Gel-FITC. The co-localization of the bacteria and gelatin nanoparticles was observed under a confocal microscope.

The activity of EcNIL−2@UCNP/Gel-CTX

EcNIL−2, EcNIL−2@UCNP, and EcNIL−2@UCNP/Gel-CTX were incubated at 37°C in LB medium at a volume ratio of 1:100. At five time points (0, 2, 4, 6, and 8 h), 100 µL of diluted culture was spread onto Amp-resistant agar plates, followed by incubation at 37°C for colony imaging and enumeration.

Light-response expression analysis of EcNIL−2, EcNIL−2@UCNP and EcNIL−2-UCNPs in vitro

After irradiation by blue light (475 nm) for 0 min, 30 min, 60 min, 120 min, and 180 min, bacterial suspensions were taken out for detecting the expression of IL-2. The fluorescence intensity of mCherry was then measured using flow cytometry.

EcNIL−2, EcNIL−2@UCNP, and EcNIL−2@UCNP/Gel-CTX bacterial suspensions were prepared for irradiation with NIR light for 30 min, respectively. And then fluorescence images were taken using confocal microscope (Olympus, Japan). One mL of EcNIL−2 and EcNIL−2@UCNP bacterial suspensions were added to 1.5 mL Eppendorf tubes, respectively. The EcNIL−2 group was induced by blue light, while the EcNIL−2@UCNP group was irradiated with 808 nm NIR at 1 W/cm2. The fluorescence signal of mCherry was detected at the different timepoints (0, 30, and 60 min) using IVIS Lumina XR (Caliper Life Sciences, American).

The expression of IL-2 was analyzed by Western blot. Anti-mCherry antibody (Proteintech, China) at the 1:1000 dilution ratio was added into the lysis of blue light-induced EcNIL−2, NIR-induced EcNIL−2@UCNP, and their supernatants at 4℃ overnight, respectively. Horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibody was added and incubated for 1 h at room temperature. Chemiluminescence imaging system (ChemiDoc XRS, Bio-Rad, USA) was used to analyze the electrophoretic bands.

The expression level of IL-2 was further analyzed using a commercial mouse IL-2 ELISA kit. Groups were divided as follows: EcNIL−2, EcNIL−2 (NIR+), EcNIL−2@UCNP, EcNIL−2@UCNP (NIR+), EcNIL−2@UCNP/Gel-CTX, and EcNIL−2@UCNP/Gel-CTX (NIR+), where (NIR+) indicates illumination with NIR light for 2 h. After centrifugation, the bacteria and the supernatant were collected. Subsequently, the content of IL-2 in both the bacteria and the supernatants was detected according to the kit manual, respectively.

EcNIL−2 and EcNIL−2@UCNP/Gel-CTX (5 mL, 109 CFU/mL) were respectively added into LB culture medium with Amp (100 µg/mL). After NIR irradiation for 30 min, 150 µL of EcNIL−2@UCNP/Gel-CTX solution were taken out, and fluorescence signal was detected by flow cytometry at 0 h, 0.5 h, 1 h, and 2 h to investigate the expression level of mCherry-IL-2 fusion protein. The process was repeated for 3 cycles.

Collecting tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) and in vitro experiments

One day before tumor engraftment, the right dorsal flanks of 30 male BALB/c mice were clipped and depilated. On day 0, 2 × 10⁶ H22 cells in 100 µL PBS were injected subcutaneously into the same site. When tumors reached 8–10 mm in diameter, they were aseptically excised, diced into 1–2 mm³ fragments, and digested with 0.8 mg/mL collagenase. The digest was passed through a 300-mesh sieve, gently pushed through with a syringe plunger, and washed with PBS to obtain a single-cell suspension.

To isolate tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs), 5 mL of lymphocyte separation medium was placed in a 15 mL conical tube and overlaid with an equal volume of the tumor single-cell suspension. After centrifugation (3000 rpm, 10 min), four layers were presented from bottom to top: red blood cells, transparent separation medium, diluted buffer, and a whitish lymphocyte layer. The lymphocyte layer was carefully collected, washed three times with washing buffer, and finally resuspended in RPMI-1640 complete medium for use.

Groups were divided as follows: PBS, CTX, EcNIL−2, EcNIL−2 (NIR+), EcNIL−2@UCNP, EcNIL−2@UCNP (NIR+), EcNIL−2@UCNP/Gel-CTX (MMP-2+) and EcNIL−2@UCNP/Gel-CTX (NIR+, MMP-2+). The NIR + group was irradiated with NIR for 30 min each time and then cultured for 2 h, and this process was repeated three times. MMP-2 was added to the MMP-2 + group, and the final concentration was adjusted to 10 µg/mL. At the same time, a blank hole (only adding culture medium) and a control hole (only adding H22 cell suspension) were set, viability using Cell Counting Kit 8 (CCK8) (Dojindo, Japan).

The TILs were co-incubated with bacterial supernatant (filtration with 0.22 μm membrane) after different treatments for 48 h. Cells were collected and stained by differently labeled antibodies. After washing with PBS, the infiltration of CD8+ T, Treg and NK cells ratios were analyzed by flow cytometry. CD8+ (anti-CD3-FITC, anti-CD8-PE/Cy7), CD4+ T cells (anti-CD3-FITC, anti-CD4-Bv421), NK cells (anti-CD49b-APC), and Tregs (anti-CD3-PC/7, anti-CD4-Bv42, anti-CD25-FITC, and then with anti-Foxp3-Alexa Fluor 647 after disrupting nucleus with True-Nuclear™ Transcription Factor Buffer Set) were stained respectively for 30 min. The Gra B within the cells was also measured using a commercial ELISA kit. At the same time, the content of IFN-γ in the supernatant was quantified using a commercial ELISA kit. And then the TILs (1 × 105 cells) treated with different materials were co-incubated with 1 × 104 H22 tumor cells in RPMI 1640 medium without FBS and antibiotics for 5 h. The killing ability of the differently treated TILs against the tumor cells was assessed using an LDH release assay kit.

Animals

Male BALB/c mice (18–20 g) were purchased from Liaoning Changsheng Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Liaoning, China). Mice were housed in cages under constant environmental conditions (22 ± 2°C, 55 ± 5% relative humidity, and a 12 h light-dark cycle). H22 tumor-bearing mice were prepared by subcutaneous injection of H22 cells (2 × 106 cells per mouse) into the flanks of BALB/c mice.

All animals received human care in compliance with the Principles of Laboratory Animal Care Formulated by the National Society of Medical Research. All animal studies were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Huazhong University of Science and Technology (IACUC Number: 4142).

Biodistribution of EcNIL−2@UCNP/Gel-CTX

To investigate the distribution of EcNIL−2@UCNP/Gel-CTX in vivo, IR780-stained EcNIL−2@UCNP/Gel-CTX (1 × 106 CFU) were injected into H22 tumor-bearing BALB/c mice via i.v. At different time points (0 h, 2 h, 6 h, 12 h, 24 h, 48 h, and 72 h) after injection, fluorescence images were taken by IVIS Lumina XR. The major organs and tumors were collected from mice at 72 h after administration of EcNIL−2@UCNP/Gel-CTX for fluorescence imaging.

In the meanwhile, mice (three per time point) were intravenously injected via the tail vein with approximately 1 × 106 CFU of EcNIL−2@UCNP/Gel-CTX. Blood, tumors and other tissues were harvested at 6 h, 12 h, 24 h, 48 h, and 72 h post-injection. After washing with PBS, tissue homogenization was performed using a tissue grinder. The homogenates were temporarily stored at 4℃ and transferred to − 20℃ for long-term preservation. Serial dilutions of the blood and homogenates were prepared, and 100 µL of each dilution was spread onto pre-prepared solid agar plates containing ampicillin using a sterile spreader. The plates were incubated at 37℃ for 24 h, and then the colony-forming units (CFU) on each plate were counted.

Evaluating antitumor effects in vivo

When the volume of tumor achieved to 100–150 mm3, the mice were divided into eight groups: PBS, CTX, EcNIL−2, EcNIL−2@UCNP, EcNIL−2@UCNP/Gel-CTX, EcNIL−2 (NIR+), EcNIL−2@UCNP (NIR+), and EcNIL−2@UCNP/Gel-CTX (NIR+). The mice were injected intravenously with PBS (100 µL), CTX (10 mg/kg in 100 µL), EcNIL−2 (106 CFU in 100 µL PBS), EcNIL−2@UCNP (106 CFU in 100 µL PBS), or EcNIL−2@UCNP/Gel-CTX (106 CFU in 100 µL PBS) 2 times on the 0th and 6th day. NIR light illumination (808 nm, 1 W/cm− 2, 10 min) was performed on the 2nd, 3rd, 4th, 8th, 9th, and 10th day, respectively. The size of the tumor and the body weight of the mice were measured every day. Tumor volume was calculated by the following formula: 0.5 × length × width2.

After illumination on the 14th day, 3 mice per group were sacrificed to obtain lymph node cells for flow cytometry analysis of DC maturation. At the end of treatments, the tumors were weighed and photographed. The major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney) were collected for pathological analysis, and the tumors and spleens were collected for flow cytometry analysis.

Histochemical and Immunofluorescence staining

At the end of experiments, major organs and tumor tissues were collected and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h at room temperature. Pathological changes in heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney tissues were detected by H&E staining. Immunohistochemical staining was conducted to determine the expression of Ki67 (a proliferation marker). TUNEL immunofluorescence staining (for detecting apoptotic cells) was carried out in Wuhan Sevicebio Technology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China). The images were captured with a light microscope (NIKON, Japan) and a fluorescence microscope, respectively. IFN-γ, Gra B, and Foxp3 in tumor tissues were determined via immunofluorescence assay.

Tumor immune cell analysis in vivo

TDLNs were crushed with a syringe plug to extrude cells and filtered through a 200-mesh nylon mesh, and the cell pellet was collected and resuspended with PBS. Spleens were crushed with a syringe plug and filtered through a 200-mesh nylon mesh, the pellet was resuspended in erythrocyte lysate for 2 min, and then the cell pellet was collected and resuspended with PBS. Tumor tissues were chopped and digested in 1640 medium containing 0.8 mg/mL collagenase at 37 °C for 1 h, then pipetted and filtered through a 300-mesh nylon mesh. The pellet was resuspended with erythrocyte lysate for 2 min, and the cell pellet was collected and resuspended with PBS. In the TDLN group, cells were stained with anti-CD45-APC/cy7, anti-CD3-FITC, anti-CD4-BV421, and anti-CD8-PE/Cy7 for lymphocyte analysis. For DC analysis, cells were stained with anti-CD80-PE, anti-CD86-APC, and anti-CD11c-FITC. In the spleen group, cells were stained with anti-CD3-FITC, anti-CD4-BV421, and anti-CD8-PE/Cy7 for lymphocyte analysis. In the tumor group, cells were stained with anti-CD45-APC/Cy7, anti-CD3-FITC, anti-CD4-BV421, anti-CD8-PE/Cy7, and anti-CD49b-APC for lymphocyte analysis.

Analysis of inflammatory factors in sera and blood biochemistry

At the end of the experiments, blood samples collected from mice were centrifuged (3000 rpm, 10 min) to obtain sera. After dilution with PBS, part of sera was used to measure the levels of TNF-α, IFN-γ, TGF-β and IL-10 by the kits according to the manuals. The other part was used to determine the levels of CREA, AST, ALT, BUN, LDH, and CK using an automatic biochemical analyzer (Mindary, China) in the hospital of Huazhong University of Science and Technology.

Detecting long-term immune memory effects

H22 tumor-bearing mice were divided into eight groups same to the aforementioned antitumor experiment. The mice were administrated intravenously with PBS (100 µL), CTX (10 mg/kg in 100 µL), EcNIL−2 (106 CFU in 100 µL PBS), EcNIL−2@UCNP (106 CFU in 100 µL PBS), or EcNIL−2@UCNP/Gel-CTX (106 CFU in 100 µL PBS) on the 0th, 6th, and 12th day. NIR light illumination (808 nm, 1 W/cm2, 10 min) was performed on the 2nd, 3rd, 4th, 8th, 9th, 10th, 14th, 15th and 16th day, respectively. Three mice from each group were sacrificed on the 23rd day, and their spleens were harvested for obtaining a suspension of splenic lymphocytes. Memory T cells in spleens were analyzed by staining with anti-CD3-FITC, anti-CD4-BV421, anti-CD8-PE/Cy7, anti-CD44-PE, and anti-CD62L-PerCP-Cy5.5. To obtain the antigen-specific splenic lymphocytes, the lymphocytes were co-incubated with antigens (80 µg/mL) from H22 cells treated with ultrasound for 72 h. And then antigen-specific splenic lymphocytes were co-incubated with H22 cells for 5 h. The cytotoxic activity of the splenic lymphocytes against the H22 cells was then assessed using a lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) release assay kit.

The tumors were surgically removed from the mice in the EcNIL−2@UCNP/Gel-CTX (NIR+) group on the 24th day and rechallenged with 2 × 106 H22 cells subcutaneously injected into the opposite backside on the 34th day. The untreated mice at the same age were injected with 2 × 106 H22 cells as controls (n = 5). At the end of experiments (on day 52), mice were sacrificed, and their tumors, spleens, and lymph nodes were harvested, weighed, and photographed.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 8.0 software, and the results were presented as mean (s. e. m). Two-tailed Student’s T-test or one-way ANOVA followed by Turkey’s test was performed. p < 0.05 was considered to be significantly different. (*p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, and ****p < 0.0001, ns: no significant difference.)
