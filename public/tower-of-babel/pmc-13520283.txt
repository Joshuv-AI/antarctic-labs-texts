Letter to the Editor: “Organ donation after ECPR: ethical challenges and clinical implications”
Authors: Jose Moya Sánchez, Beatriz Domínguez-Gil, Jose Miguel Pérez Villares, Mario Royo-Villanova
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

We read with great interest the narrative review by Rajsic et al. on organ donation after extracorporeal cardiopulmonary resuscitation (ECPR) [1]. We broadly agree with the ethical framework presented by the authors, particularly that ECPR must be initiated exclusively with therapeutic intent and that decisions to limit or withdraw life-sustaining treatment must remain clinically independent from any subsequent consideration of donation. Our comments are therefore intended as a constructive refinement of several aspects of the discussion from the perspective of mature, protocol-driven donation after circulatory determination of death (DCDD) programmes.

The ethical questions identified by the authors are legitimate and deserve careful consideration, including the transition from rescue to organ preservation, time-sensitive logistics, communication with families, determination of death, and adherence to the dead donor rule (DDR). These issues should not be overlooked. Our concern is narrower: repeated references to “pressure”, “implicit bias”, “conflicts of interest”, “instrumentalization”, and “erosion of public trust”, without sufficient contextualization, may inadvertently suggest that the prospect of organ donation introduces suspicion into clinical decision-making. Financial, resource-related, reputational, and programmatic incentives deserve scrutiny and appropriate oversight; however, their existence should not in itself be taken as evidence that individual clinical decisions are influenced by donation. In established DCDD programmes, these potential pressures are addressed through procedural safeguards. The clinical team determines that treatment is no longer beneficial. Only after that independent, documented, patient-centred decision does the donor coordination team explore the patient’s values and previously expressed wishes and, where appropriate, protect the patient’s opportunity to donate.

This distinction also matters for the role of donor coordinators. Coordinators should neither determine futility nor influence prognostication. Their role is to ensure legality, traceability, family support, communication, and adherence to the separation between end-of-life decisions and donation procedures. Time-sensitive logistics are inherent to deceased donation, as they are to trauma, stroke, myocardial infarction, and sepsis. Urgency should therefore not be conflated with undue influence. The appropriate response is robust protocols ensuring that logistical requirements never precede or modify the clinical decision to withdraw or withhold treatment.

We also suggest greater precision regarding communication. Donation should not be raised before the family has understood the clinical situation, prognosis, and independent decision to limit treatment. Conversely, once that decision has been understood, withholding information about donation may deny the patient and family the opportunity to act on previously expressed values. The objective should therefore be sequenced communication by trained professionals: first clarity about prognosis and treatment limitation, then transparent discussion of donation when clinically and ethically appropriate. This is the approach followed in Spain, although it is often assumed that the country’s normative presumed consent framework facilitates this process. Importantly, despite Spain being legally an opt-out system, clinical practice places considerable emphasis on identifying the donor’s wishes, values, and previously expressed preferences through discussions with relatives. In practice, donation and premortem interventions do not proceed without explicit written informed consent from the family or legal representative.

The review’s account of the DDR also requires clarification. Rajsic et al. state that patients should not undergo “interventions for the purpose of organ transplantation” until after death has been formally determined [1]. This wording is broader than the classical DDR principle that vital organs should not be removed before death. The distinction is relevant in controlled DCDD, where certain premortem procedural or preservative interventions may be ethically and legally permissible when separately authorised and when they do not cause death or compromise appropriate end-of-life care. Conflating organ recovery with all preparatory or preservative measures may therefore broaden the DDR beyond its literal formulation.

The discussion of normothermic regional perfusion (NRP) should likewise be calibrated to current evidence. Concerns about cerebral reperfusion and the permanence principle are legitimate, particularly in thoraco-abdominal NRP (TA-NRP), and rigorous technical safeguards remain essential. Several mechanistic and clinical studies from our group have addressed this question. Intracranial arterial pressure measured at the circle of Willis did not increase after initiation of abdominal or TA-NRP despite restoration of regional aortic pressures [2]; 99mTc-HMPAO scintigraphy showed no tracer uptake in the brainstem or cerebral hemispheres in 20 DCDD donors undergoing NRP [3]; and more recent clinical evidence supported the absence of brain perfusion during TA-NRP when supra-aortic vessels were appropriately cross-clamped and vented to atmospheric pressure [4]. Importantly, independently generated findings are consistent with these observations. In a porcine TA-NRP model, Glorion et al. found no restoration of intracranial blood flow or brain electrical activity despite collateral supra-aortic blood flow [5]. In a prospective cohort of 43 human donors, Tur et al. found no cerebral blood flow by transcranial Doppler and no evidence of brain function by pupillometry or bispectral index monitoring after TA-NRP initiation [6]. In addition, Frontera et al. independently reported no restoration of cerebral blood flow during TA-NRP [7]. These convergent data do not eliminate the need for protocolisation, objective verification, audit, independent replication, and continued research; they shift the debate toward technical standardisation and demonstrable compliance with the permanence principle and the DDR.

Finally, the moral burden experienced by professionals should not be attributed to donation alone. It arises from cardiac arrest, ECPR, devastating neurological injury, prognostic uncertainty, prolonged dying, and the complexity of end-of-life care in the intensive care unit. Expert coordination can reduce, rather than amplify, this burden by clarifying roles, supporting families, and preserving the temporal and ethical sequence of decisions.

In summary, the review offers a valuable ethical framework, and we share many of its central principles. We believe that some of the risks appropriately identified by Rajsic et al. may benefit from additional contextualization when considered in the setting of mature DCDD and NRP programmes with established procedural safeguards. A key principle is clear: ECPR is initiated for the patient’s potential benefit; the decision to limit treatment is independent of donation; and, once this sequence is respected, organ donation may represent not instrumentalization, but respect for the patient’s values and a final act of solidarity.
