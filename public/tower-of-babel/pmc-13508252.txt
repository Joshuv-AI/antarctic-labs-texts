Phase evolution and magnetic properties of (Ce,Nd)–Fe–B prepared using microwave-assisted approach
Authors: Harshida Parmar, Mikael Broders, Ikenna C. Nlebedim, Xubo Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
A bottom-up synthesis strategy combining microwave-assisted oxide formation with a reduction-diffusion process has been developed to produce (Ce,Nd)–Fe–B powders. A full compositional range from 0 to 100% Ce was successfully synthesized, and a detailed investigation of phase evolution of the oxides and alloys as a function of Ce concentration was studied, and the reduction diffusion mechanism was also identified. Powder XRD, thermal analysis and TEM confirm the formation of a (Ce,Nd)2Fe14B phase and show nanostructure features. The coercivity of the nanoparticles varied from 0.4 to 9.1 kOe depending on the composition of Ce–Nd–Fe–B, which is related to the intrinsic magnetic properties of (Ce,Nd)2Fe14B with different Ce content and the formation of secondary phases. Additionally, coercivity was improved in Ce–Fe–B particles doped with Cu, Al, Pr, Dy, and Nd.

Introduction

To date, Nd–Fe–B magnets display the best magnetic properties at room temperature and are widely applied in energy technologies and electromobility.1–3 Typical Nd–Fe–B magnets contain about 30 wt% of the critical rare-earth (RE) elements, such as Nd and Pr. For high-temperature applications, some heavy REs, such as Dy and Tb, are often incorporated into magnets. However, the critical RE such as Nd, Pr, Dy, and Tb, are source-limited and costly, and present significant sustainability challenges.4,5 Partial substitution of Nd with the resource-abundant and cost-effective cerium (Ce) has emerged as a promising strategy for more efficient use of critical RE.6–12 However, Ce2Fe14B has a lower saturation magnetization (Js = 11.7 KG), weaker magnetocrystalline anisotropy (HA = 26 kOe) and lower Curie temperature (TC = 150 °C), compared to Nd2Fe14B (Js = 16 kG, HA = 76 kOe and TC = 312 °C).13 Proper chemical composition design and microstructure engineering can substantially enhance the magnetic performance of Ce–Fe–B magnets. Partial replacement of Ce by Nd or Pr, such as (Ce,Nd)2Fe14B, can significantly enhance the intrinsic magnetic properties, hence the performance of target magnets. With control of chemical composition, microstructure, and grain-boundary chemistry, Nd–Ce-Fe–B magnets can achieve high coercivity, remanence, and (BH)max.14,15 This can lead to a balanced substitution strategy that leverages cerium's low cost and abundance while maintaining required magnet performance for a desired application.16

Microstructural design is important to develop Ce–Nd–Fe–B magnets. Du et al.17 demonstrated significant coercivity enhancement in sintered Nd–Ce–Fe–B magnets through Tb-based grain-boundary diffusion without notable remanence loss, attributed to the formation of refined, Dy/Tb-rich boundary layers. Sepehri-Amin et al.18 and Li et al.19 reported that eutectic RE–Cu diffusion (e.g., Pr–Cu, Nd–Cu) markedly improves coercivity in hot-deformed Nd–Ce-Fe–B by promoting continuous grain-boundary phases and improved grain isolation. Moreover, Tang et al.20 demonstrated that Ce-substituted hot-deformed Nd–Fe–B can achieve thermally stable high coercivity through tailored boundary-phase engineering. These results indicate the important role of microstructural control and boundary-phase design in developing high-performance Ce-based magnets.

Conventional sintering and top-down mechanically milling approaches often struggle to produce nanoscale, compositionally uniform microstructures required for effective Ce substitution. As a result, bottom-up synthesis methods have gained traction for producing chemically homogeneous Nd–Ce–Fe–B nanoparticles with tunable Ce : Nd ratios.21 Techniques such as wet-chemical synthesis, mechanochemical processing, microwave-assisted methods, and reduction-diffusion routes allow control over particle size, morphology, crystallinity, and composition, all of which are critical for optimizing hard magnetic properties.22,23 These bottom-up approaches may also be more energy efficient and have reduced negative environmental impacts when scaled for magnet production.24,25 However, the synthesis of Ce-substituted Nd–Fe–B magnets via a wet-chemical bottom-up approach remains largely unexplored. A systematic investigation of the complete compositional range from Ce2Fe14B to Nd2Fe14B through gradual substitution of Ce by Nd has not yet been reported. Very limited studies have reported on the synthesis of Ce-containing Nd–Fe–B using this route,21,26 indicating the need for further research to better understand the processing and resulting phase evolution, microstructure, and magnetic properties.

In this work, we employed a combustion chemical synthesis route to prepare oxide precursors, followed by a reduction diffusion reaction to produce (Ce,Nd)–Fe–B powders with 20, 40, 60, 80, and 100% Nd substitution. Detailed investigations were carried out for the effect of Ce concentration on phase evolution in oxides as well as in the alloys along with the reaction mechanism of reduction-diffusion. We demonstrate that Ce can be progressively substituted by Nd to form the desired (Ce/Nd)2Fe14B tetragonal phase in nanoparticles. Additionally, we show that introducing dopants such as Dy, Pr, Nd, Cu and Al into Ce2Fe14B can improve magnetic properties.

Experimental

Synthesis process

Ce17−xNdxFe77B8 alloys with nominal compositions x = 0, 3, 6, 9, 12, and 17 were synthesized. In addition to the base composition Ce17Fe77B8, a series of doped alloys were synthesized by introducing Nd, Dy, Pr, Al, and Cu as dopants. The investigated compositions included Ce15Nd2Al2Fe77B8, Ce15Nd2Cu1Fe77B8, Ce15Nd2Al2Cu1Fe77B8, Ce15Dy2Cu1Fe77B8, Ce15Dy2Al2Cu1Fe77B8, Ce15Pr2Cu1Fe77B8, and Ce15Pr2Al2Cu1Fe77B8. All required metal nitrates, boric acid, and glycine were procured from Millipore Sigma and used as received. Stoichiometric quantities of the nitrate precursors and boric acid (pre-dissolved in 4 N HNO3) were dissolved in deionized water. Glycine was added at a total metal salts-to-glycine molar ratio of 1 : 1.4 and formed a homogeneous precursor solution. The mixture was subjected to microwave-assisted combustion using a domestic microwave oven (Panasonic NN-SB65NS) operated at maximum 1200 W for 12 min. Microwave dielectric heating rapidly decomposed nitric acid, evaporated volatiles, and generated a brick-colored glycine metal gel. Simultaneously, boron reacted with hydroxide and formed B(OH)4. Continued heating initiated a nitrate glycine redox reaction, producing ultrafine homogeneous mixed oxides without selective precipitation.27

The oxide powders were mixed with CaH2 (90–95%, Sigma-Aldrich) at a mass ratio of 1 : 1.1, pelletized, and reduced in a vacuum furnace (∼10−2 torr) at 900 °C for 2 h. CaH2 was selected as the reducing agent due to its ease of mixing and hydrogen release during heating, which promotes in situ pulverization and accelerates reduction relative to bulk Ca. CaO was the primary solid by-product. CaO byproduct and residual Ca were removed by repeated washing in an NH4Cl/methanol solution under magnetic stirring for 10 min. The resultant metallic alloy powders were subsequently dried under vacuum.

Characterization techniques

X-ray diffraction (XRD) measurements were conducted using a Bruker (D8) X-ray diffractometer (Cu Kα radiation) in a 20°–80° 2θ range and 0.01° step size. The tetragonal (Ce,Nd)2Fe14B (space group P42/mnm) and α-Fe (space group Im3m) phases were taken as the starting model. Rietveld refinement was performed on the powder XRD data. The microstructure of the powders was examined by a FEI Tecnai G2-F20 Scanning transmission electron microscope (200 kV). Magnetic hysteresis loops were obtained at a magnetic field up to 70 kOe using a Quantum Design Superconducting Quantum Interference Device (SQUID). The powders were aligned in wax by heating to 325 K in the SQUID magnetometer, while applying a 1 kOe magnetic field. After cooling to 300 K, hysteresis loops were measured. Thermomagnetometry (mTGA) was conducted using a Netzsch STA 449 F1 thermogravimetric analyzer (Selb, Germany) equipped with a pair of permanent magnets to generate a static magnetic field gradient. In this setup, changes in the sample's apparent weight arise from variations in magnetic force associated with changes in bulk magnetization, enabling qualitative identification of magnetic ordering and disordering transitions.28–30 Measurements were performed using Al2O3 crucibles over the temperature range of 30–600 °C to determine the Curie temperature. Differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) was carried out from 30 to 900 °C to identify phase transformations. Both mTGA and DSC measurements were conducted under a flow of ultra-high purity helium (20 mL min−1) with a heating rate of 20 °C min−1.

Ce–Nd–Fe–B oxides were synthesized using microwave-assisted combustion process. Rapid heating was achieved by microwave irradiation and the related thermal energy drives the chemical reaction. Microwave processing can reduce processing time and enhance product quality since microwaves can transfer energy throughout the entire volume of the material. In this case, energy transfer occurs at a molecular level. Powder XRD was performed to investigate the oxide phase formation, followed by Rietveld refinement to analyze the mass fraction of each phase (Table 1 and Fig. 1).

It was observed that the presence of Ce and Nd content affects the phase formation and mass fraction. The high temperatures needed to form the oxide powders were achieved by exposure to microwave radiation and the exothermic redox reaction between the metal nitrate (oxidizer) and glycine (fuel). The products were Fe3O4, FeO, Ce0.5Fe1.95O3.025, CeFeO3, CeBO3, CeO2, NdFeO3, and NdBO3 and Nd0.5Fe1.5O3 after the reaction. The change in Fe3O4 mass fraction elucidates the impact of Ce and Nd concentration. It not only exploited the formation of Ce and Nd oxides with Fe and B but also impacted the Fe3O4 mass fraction. Fe3O4 formation exhibits a non-monotonic dependence on Ce3+ concentration, with maximum yield at x = 12 where Ce was 20% lowest levels. While high Ce3+ suppresses Fe3O4 by limiting Fe3+ reduction, a trace amount provides optimal redox balance and nucleation control. In the complete absence of Ce for x = 17, loss of this stabilization leads to reduced Fe3O4 formation due to uncontrolled reaction conditions.31

Reaction mechanism of reduction-diffusion

The phase evolution of the Ce–Nd–Fe–B–O systems during CaH2-assisted reduction was temperature-dependent and governed by both thermodynamic and kinetic constraints. An earlier study has determined the reduction-diffusion mechanism for Nd–Fe–B. At temperatures above 400 °C, Fe–O was first reduced and followed by rare-earth decomposition and reduction at 600–750 °C, resulting in the formation of Nd2Fe14B compound.27,32 Similarly, in Ce–Nd–Fe–B–O, four major exothermic peaks were observed in DSC analysis at ∼330 °C, ∼675 °C and 800–835 °C. Fig. 2 shows the DSC profile (a) for Ce–Fe–B–O + CaH2 without Nd presence (x = 0) and that (b) for Ce–Nd–Fe–B–O + CaH2 with 20% Nd substitution (x = 3). As shown in earlier study, the exothermic peak at 327 °C for both compositions indicates the reduction of iron oxides (Fe3O4/FeO → α-Fe), while rare-earth-containing phases CeO2, CeFeO3, and NdFeO3 undergo partial decomposition, and form intermediate oxides (Ce2O3, Nd2O3) alongside CaO and H2. At this stage, borate phases (CeBO3 and NdBO3) remain largely stable due to the strong B–O bonding. Increasing the temperature to 670 °C results in substantial progress in the reduction reactions, with CeO2 and Nd-containing oxides further reduced to the metallic state. Simultaneously, Nd reacts with H2 and forms NdH2 in Nd-rich compositions. The coexistence of metallic Fe, and Ce formed the intermetallic CeFe2 phase, while the borate decomposed and released elemental boron into the system.

Isochronal DSC of (a) Ce–Fe–B oxides + CaH2 and (b) Ce–Nd–Fe–B oxides + CaH2.

At elevated temperatures (808–836 °C), the reaction transitioned from reduction-dominated behavior to diffusion- and phase-forming behavior. Initially, NdH2 decomposed, forming Nd. Parallel boron diffusion into the Fe-rare-earth matrix enables the nucleation of the thermodynamically stable 2 : 14 : 1 phase, Ce2Fe14B or (Ce,Nd)2Fe14B in Nd-substituted systems. The systems with Nd substitution and the one without show the same peak positions for iron oxide reduction and rare-earth oxide decomposition. However, the diffusion temperature seems to increase from 819 °C to 835 °C with Nd substitution, which can be attributed to the higher formation temperature of the Nd-based 2 : 14 : 1 phase.33 At 900 °C, the final phases include Ce2Fe14B (or (Ce,Nd)2Fe14B), CeFe2, and α-Fe, with minor amounts of unreduced CeO2 and Ce2O3. The persistence of these oxide phases suggests kinetic limitations related to incomplete reduction, particularly for CeO2, which is highly thermodynamically stable.

Increasing Nd content in (Ce17−xNdx)Fe77B8 from x = 6 to 17, (Ce,Nd)2Fe14B phase fraction increases from ∼70 wt% to ∼95.9 wt%, with concurrent suppression of CeFe2 and α-Fe. The Nd-rich (x = 17) composition eliminates CeFe2 and reduces α-Fe to ∼1 wt%, indicating the stabilization of Nd2Fe14B. The diminished formation of CeFe2 also limits excess Fe segregation, resulting in significantly improved 2 : 14 : 1 phase purity. A similar observation has been reported by Lee et al. in both of their studies on the bottom-up synthesis of Ce-based RE2Fe14B magnets. The authors observed the presence of α-Fe and CeFe2 secondary phases in the Ce2Fe14B composition.21,26 However, upon partial substitution of Ce with Nd (Ce0.5Nd0.5 and Ce0.3Nd0.7), the fraction of the α-Fe and CeFe2 secondary phases decreased significantly, while the (Ce,Nd)2Fe14B (2 : 14 : 1) phase became predominant.

As shown in Table 2, the lattice parameters of the 2 : 14 : 1 phase increase systematically with Nd substitution (a = 8.7536 Å, c = 12.0974 Å for x = 0 to a = 8.8057 Å, c = 12.1988 Å for x = 17). It confirms the formation of a substitutional solid solution with the P42/mnm structure. Low χ2 (1.1–1.8) and acceptable R-Bragg values indicate reliable refinement. Overall, Nd enrichment shifts the phase equilibrium toward the hard-magnetic 2 : 14 : 1 field, suppresses secondary phases, and stabilizes the tetragonal structure.34

Morphological analysis

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) analysis of the sample with x = 6 (Fig. 4) reveals predominantly irregular, equiaxed particle morphologies. The particles are nanoscale, with an average size below 100 nm, though the microstructure exhibits a wide distribution characterized by the coexistence of small particles and significantly larger ones. This broad dispersion indicates non-uniform growth kinetics during synthesis. The reduction diffusion process likely enables simultaneous nucleation and grain growth, leading to heterogeneous particle development.

Bright-field transmission electron microscopy (TEM) image of the (Ce,Nd)2Fe14B phase in Ce11Nd6Fe77B8, showing nanoscale particles with sizes below 100 nm.

Magnetic thermogravimetric analysis

Magnetic thermogravimetric analysis (mTGA),35 performed in a TGA under a magnetic field gradient, was used to examine the evolution of magnetic behavior across the (Ce,Nd)–Fe–B compositional series. The mTGA curves (SI Data S.3) show apparent mass changes at the Curie temperature, corresponding to the ferromagnetic → paramagnetic transition of the 2 : 14 : 1 phase. The Curie temperature increases systematically from 156 °C in Ce-rich compositions36 to 323 °C for x = 17 (Fig. 5), demonstrating the strong sensitivity of magnetic ordering to Nd substitution. Ce-rich alloys have lower Curie temperatures characteristic of Ce2Fe14B, due to the small unit cell volume and a weak 4f-3d exchange coupling between rare-earth and Fe sublattices.15,37 Nd substitution enlarges the unit cell size and strengthens the rare-earth-transition-metal exchange interactions to increase the Curie temperature toward that of Nd2Fe14B (∼320 °C). The systematic transition shift confirms continuous tuning of magnetic exchange through rare-earth substitution within the (Nd,Ce)2Fe14B lattice.

Magnetic property analysis

Magnetic properties of the Ce–Nd–Fe–B alloys were analyzed as a function of Ce/Nd substitution. Fig. 6 shows the coercivity of all the samples before and after the removal of the CaO by-product, M–H loops for samples before and after CaO removal are shown in the SI data S4(a) and (b), respectively. In the as-reduced state (CaO present), x = 0 exhibits very low coercivity (∼0.4 kOe), consistent with the weak magnetocrystalline anisotropy of Ce2Fe14B and the presence of soft magnetic phase α-Fe.13,26 As the Nd substitution for Ce increases, the coercivity also increases, reaching 9.1 kOe at x = 17 (Fig. 6). This trend follows intrinsic anisotropy scaling, as Nd2Fe14B possesses a substantially higher anisotropy field than Ce2Fe14B.38,39

Coercivity (Hc) and high-field magnetization (M at 70 kOe) as a function of composition (x) in Ce17−xNdxFe77B8. CaO removal significantly decreases coercivity across all compositions, with the effect becoming more pronounced at higher x. Magnetization increases monotonically with composition.

Fig. 6 also shows reduced coercivity values for the samples after chemical removal of the CaO by-product, reaching a maximum of ∼5.4 kOe at x = 17. This reduction in coercivity primarily originates from changes in the intergranular boundary phase, not from changes in composition. During reduction-diffusion, CaO forms discrete inclusions that are heterogeneously distributed within the powder, acting as a non-magnetic insulating barrier and partially suppressing exchange coupling and magnetostatic interactions between adjacent magnetic particles. Such magnetic decoupling and isolation enhance coercivity by disrupting magnetic phase connectivity and limiting the propagation of magnetization domains. Removal of CaO promotes direct particle-to-particle contact, strengthening interparticle magnetostatic and exchange interactions and facilitating magnetization reversal, thereby reducing coercivity despite improved phase purity. Particle modification during washing, including possible surface oxidation of fine particles, may further contribute to coercivity degradation.22,40 A distinct trend in coercivity was observed: the difference between values measured before and after CaO removal increased with x. This is because, as the fraction of the hard magnetic phase (2 : 14 : 1) increases (increasing x), the system becomes more magnetically connected, making coercivity increasingly sensitive to CaO removal due to enhanced inter-particle magnetic interactions.

In contrast, magnetization increases substantially and continuously with CaO removal, rising from ∼95 emu g−1 for x = 0 to ∼144 emu g−1 for x = 17 (Fig. 6). The increase in magnetization is due to reduced dilution effect, i.e., the elimination of the non-magnetic contribution from CaO. Also, it is related to the composition shifting towards Nd2Fe14B, which has higher saturation magnetization than Ce2Fe14B. Consequently, both magnetization (Fig. 6) and remanence (Fig. 7) increase monotonically with x.

Remanence as a function Nd content in Ce17−xNdxFe77B8 before and after CaO byproduct removal. It increases with increasing x.

Dopant effects on Ce–Fe–B

Coercivity enhancement in Ce2Fe14B-based alloy powders is governed by both intrinsic magnetocrystalline anisotropy and extrinsic microstructural isolation of magnetic phases. These effects can be tuned through chemical modification via incorporation of secondary alloying elements, including Nd–Cu, Pr–Cu, Dy–Cu, Nd–Al–Cu, Pr–Al–Cu, Dy–Al–Cu. The base Ce17Fe77B8 alloy exhibits low intrinsic coercivity (∼0.4 kOe) due to the low magnetocrystalline anisotropy of Ce2Fe14B and formation of α-Fe.38,41,42 Incorporating secondary alloys resulted in a systematic increase in coercivity.

The coercivity trends shown in Fig. 8 reveal a clear interplay between intrinsic magnetic anisotropy and microstructural modification. Specifically, substitution with higher-anisotropy rare-earth elements (Nd, Pr, Dy), combined with Cu- and Al-containing additions, significantly enhances coercivity, demonstrating that both compositional design and microstructural control are effective in improving magnetic hardness in Ce–Fe–B.

Coercivity of Ce–Fe–B-based alloys with rare-earth substitution (Nd, Pr, Dy) and Cu/Al additions. Coercivity increases with rare-earth substitution in the order Nd < Pr < Dy, reflecting increasing intrinsic anisotropy. The addition of Al alongside Cu further enhances coercivity in Nd- and Pr-containing systems, while showing negligible effect in Dy-containing compositions.

With rare earth substitution, a systematic trend is observed in which coercivity increases in the order Nd < Pr < Dy, indicating the increased contribution of the intrinsic magnetocrystalline anisotropy of these elements. Dy-doped 2 : 14 : 1 has the strongest anisotropy, resulting in the highest resistance to magnetization reversal, hence the highest coercivity in the Dy-doped sample.

In addition to rare-earth substitution, alloying with Cu significantly improves coercivity across all systems, likely through microstructural effects such as modifications in phase distribution and magnetic isolation. The further addition of Al alongside Cu produces an additional increase in coercivity for Nd- and Pr-containing compositions, suggesting enhanced control over microstructural modification of magnetic phase connectivity or local chemistry.38,41 However, this effect is negligible in Dy-containing samples, where coercivity appears to be limited primarily by intrinsic anisotropy rather than microstructure.

Overall, the results highlight two governing mechanisms: intrinsic anisotropy, dictated by rare-earth selection, and extrinsic microstructural tuning, enabled by alloying additions, i.e., Cu and Al. While both contribute to coercivity enhancement, their relative importance changes with composition: microstructural effects are more influential in lower-anisotropy systems, whereas intrinsic anisotropy dominates at higher performance levels.

Conclusion

This work demonstrates the development of a microwave-assisted approach to the synthesis of Ce–Fe–B based magnets. A series of Ce17−xNdxFe77B8 nanoparticles were prepared through microwave-assisted oxide synthesis followed by reduction-diffusion processing. The effect of Ce and Nd concentration on oxide phase formation and the mechanism of reduction-diffusion were studied. Results indicate that partial replacement of Ce by Nd enhanced the stabilization of the tetragonal R2Fe14B phase and reduced the fraction of secondary magnetic phases such as CeFe2 and α-Fe. With increasing Nd content, the Curie temperature, coercivity, and magnetization increase in the Ce17−xNdxFe77B8 system. It was found that coercivity in Ce–Fe–B-based alloys is governed by the interplay of intrinsic anisotropy, phase evolution, and magnetic phase connectivity. Substitution with higher-anisotropy rare earth elements (Nd → Pr → Dy) increases coercivity accordingly. Alloying Cu and Al further enhanced coercivity through microstructural modification, particularly in lower-anisotropy systems, while their influence diminishes in Dy-containing compositions where intrinsic anisotropy dominates. This study shows that overall, optimal performance requires balancing compositional design with control of magnetic connectivity.
