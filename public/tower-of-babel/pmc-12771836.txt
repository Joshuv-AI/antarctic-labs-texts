Genome-wide identification of AAAP gene family and expression analysis under abiotic stresses in Avena sativa
Authors: Lei Ling, Shulei Wang, Han Zhang, Fangyu Yang, Lina Qu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The amino acid/auxin permease (AAAP) gene family plays a vital role in plant growth and abiotic stress responses. In this study, we advance current knowledge of the regulatory mechanisms associated with the AAAP gene family through bioinformatic analysis of the whole genome of Avena sativa. We identified 145 members of the AAAP family in Avena sativa, then investigated these genes (AsAAAPs) in terms of their chromosomal localisation, phylogenesis, conserved motifs, structure and replication. Many abiotic stress response elements were detected in the promoter region of AsAAAPs. RT-qPCR analysis revealed that AAAPs genes play a role in regulating plant responses to salt and saline stress. By highlighting the biological functions of AsAAAPs, this study provides a theoretical basis for further exploration of these important genes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-025-12319-w.

Introduction

Amino acid/auxin permeases (AAAPs) are ubiquitous in eukaryotes and play important roles in the growth and development [1]. AAAPs have been widely identified in living organisms, including animals, plants and fungi [2]. The first member of the AAAP gene family was identified in Arabidopsis [3]. Members of this family encode electrochemical potential-driven transporters that can transport auxins (indole-3-acetic acid), c-aminobutyric acid, single l-amino acids and multiple amino acids [4]. Amino acids are crucial for maintaining normal growth and development as well as regulating the metabolism, structure, and biosynthesis of various compounds in eukaryotes [5, 6].

Previous research has shown that OsAAP6 affects the distribution of amino acids during the early stages of seed development in Oryza sativa [7]; NtAAP2 is involved in asparagine, aspartate, glutamine and glutamate transport in Nicotiana tabacum [8]; and AtLHT2 is involved in amino acid transport in spore cells and regulating pollen development in Arabidopsis thaliana [9]. The present study provides evidence of possible roles of the AAAP family in growth, development, and signal transduction of A.sativa.

The plant family Poaceae comprises more than 10,000 species [10]. Among them, Avena sativa (oat), a cereal crop with high nutritional value, is widely grown in temperate regions [11, 12]. As a rich taxonomic research object, the genus Avena includes many wild species that exhibit traits with great significance for breeding new varieties of cultivated oat species [13]. Three ploidy levels have been recognised in this genus (diploid, tetraploid, and hexaploid), with most being wild type [14].

In recent years, AAAPs have been reported in many plants, including Moso bamboo and Cerasus humilis [4, 15]. High-quality genome sequences for A.sativa were reported in 2021 and 2022 [16, 17]; however, the AAAP gene in A.sativa (AsAAAP) has not been explored in detail. Therefore, in this study, we present a comprehensive analysis of the AAAP gene family in A.sativa, laying the foundation for further research on this important gene family.

Materials and methods

Identification of AAAPs in A.sativa

We downloaded annotated gene and protein data of A.sativa (https://wheat.pw.usda.gov/GG3/node/922, accessed on 3 March 2021) (Table S1) and used the hidden Markov model and BLAST method to identify AAAPs [18]. BLASTP and BLASTN were used to determine AAAP sequences in A.thaliana and O.sativa to initially identify AAAPs in A.sativa [1]. We then verified that the candidate genes harboured sequences encoding conserved domains typical of the AAAP family using the SMART database (http://smart. Embl-heidelberg.de; accessed on 6 March 2021). Protein molecular weights, isoelectric point (pI) values and other information for members of the AAAP family in A.sativa were predicted through ExPASy (https://www.expasy.org/tools; accessed on 5 April 2021) [19]. Redundant sequences were manually removed.

Phylogenetic analysis of AAAP genes

We used MEGA6 to generate a neighbour-joining phylogenetic tree [20]. In total, 145 AAAPs were identified in the whole genome of A.sativa. We used Clustal X 2.1 to create multiple sequence alignments that subsequently served as the input for building the phylogenetic tree. A.thaliana, O.sativa and A.sativa were then classified according to distance homology.

Multiple alignment, gene structure and conserved motif analysis of AAAPs

We used Jalview (https://www.jalview.org) to perform multiple protein sequence alignment of AAAPs in A.sativa [21]. Additionally, we used MEME software (https://meme.ebi.edu.au/meme/intro.html; accessed on 16 April 2021) to identify the conserved motifs and domains, where the maximum number of motifs was set to 15 [22]. A selection of 15 motifs covers most biologically significant conserved sequences and facilitates analysis. The prediction results were downloaded as a MEME XML output file then uploaded with the genome annotation file to TBtools to visualise the genetic structure [23].

Evolutionary analysis and gene replication analysis of AAAPs

BLASTP and OrthoMCL (http://orthomcl.org/orthomcl/about.do#release; accessed on 10 June 2021) were then used to search for potential homologous gene pairs in A.sativa (E < 1e− 5, top five matched pairs) [24]. Based on the A.sativa genome annotation information, the chromosomal localization and gene density distribution (Bin size: 100,000) of AsAAAP family genes were mapped using TBtools v2.118 software. To further explore genomic evolutionary features, intraspecific collinearity analysis of A.sativa was performed using the Advanced Circos module of TBtools v2.118, and cross-species collinearity patterns between A.sativa and the model plants Arabidopsis thaliana were analyzed using the One-Step MCScanX-Super Fast module (CPU for BlastP: 2, E-value: 1e− 3, Num of BlastHits: 5). We also calculated the ka/ks value using TBtools (Toolbox for Biologists, v2.118) software (Table S2).

Analysis of cis-elements in the AAAP promoter region

To enhance our understanding of the AAAP gene family, we analysed the promoter elements of AAAP. We performed promoter analysis of sequences within 1500 base pairs (bp) upstream of the starting codon (ATG) after excluding any sequences overlapping adjacent genes. Tools hosted on the PlantCARE database were used to search for sequences and order elements in the promoter region (http://bioinformatics.psb.ugent.be/webtools/plantcare/html; accessed on 30 June 2021) [25] (Table S3).

cDNA synthesis and RNA extraction and quantification via real-time fluorescence-based PCR

The entire procedure prior to real-time PCR complied with MIQE guidelines [26]. First, total RNA was extracted from whole seedlings using an RNA extraction kit (DP430 TianGen Biotech, Beijing, China) and DNA contamination was removed after DNaseI treatment. Then, the RNA was run on a 1.0% (w/v) agarose gel to assess RNA quality. The cDNA synthesised by reverse transcription was stored at −80 ℃. Real-time PCR included three main steps: denaturation at 94–98 ℃; annealing at 50–65 ℃; and final extension at approximately 72℃. DNA polymerase aided the synthesis of new DNA chains (using dNTPs) from the 3’ end of the primer bound to the template (cDNA) (Table S4). In the experiment, we used RNA samples with three biological replicates. The three amplification steps were performed 30 times to exponentially amplify the target DNA fragments. The DNA fragments were heated at 72 ℃ for 5–10 min to ensure full amplification, after which PCR products were analysed using gel electrophoresis with 2% agarose to assess the amplification efficiency (Table S5). A RT-qPCR melt curve was used to analyse the expression of AAAPs in A.sativa. AAAP expression was normalised against that of β-actin (internal control) and expression levels were estimated using the 2−ΔΔCt method. Primers were designed according to AAAP CDS using Primer Express 3.0 [27].

Plant material and treatments

The A.sativa plant material and treatments used in this study were obtained from (yanmaiCV). We planted the seeds in a 3:1 (w/w) mixture of sand and soil, irrigated and germinated with half-strength Hoagland solution once every two days in a greenhouse [28]. Seedlings grow at 18 °C during the night and 25 °C during the day, at a relative humidity of 65%−80% with a 14/10-h photoperiod (daytime, 06:00–20:00; nighttime, 20:00–06:00) and a light intensity of 200–230 µmol m− 2s− 2. Germinated seedlings were treated with 200 mM sodium chloride (salt), with the prepared solution poured onto the soil after four weeks. We repeated this treatment five times. Samples were frozen instantly in liquid nitrogen and stored at − 80 ℃ prior to RNA extraction.

Results

AAAPs identified in A. sativa

A total of 145 AAAPs (AsAAAP1-AsAAAP145) were identified in A.sativa located on chromosomes Chr1A-Chr7D. The length of AsAAAPs encoding the corresponding proteins ranged from 360 bp to 4632 bp, with molecular weights between 18.25 and 109.11 kDa. The theoretical pI ranged from 4.75 to 10.11, with an average pI of 8.05. Detailed information on AAAP proteins is provided in Table S1.

The AAAP gene family system in A.sativa

Based on a comparative analysis of A.thaliana, O.sativa and the 145 AAAP gene sequences of A.sativa, we constructed an evolutionary tree of the AAAP gene family in A.sativa (Fig. 1) [1]. Based on the AAAP family in A.thaliana and O.sativa, the 145 AAAP protein sequences identified in this study were divided into eight subfamilies: ATLa, ANT, ATLb, LHT, ProT, GAT, AAP and AUX. Members of the ANT subfamily accounted for only 11.03% of all sequences. The AAP subfamily encompassed 30.34% of all genes, with all other subfamilies containing fewer genes (23.45%, ATLa; 29.66%, ATLb; 13.10%, LHT; 18.62%, ProT; 19.31%, GAT; 18.62%, AUX). We did not count the genes repeatedly and did not consider multiple subfamily counts when calculating the percentage of AAAP members in each subfamily. Furthermore, AAAPs in O.sativa were mainly concentrated in AAP group, and A.sativa and O.sativa were closely related.

Motif analysis of the AAAP family in A.sativa

Motif analysis revealed 15 different motifs in the 145 AAAPs identified in A.sativa (Fig. 2). Most members shared the same sequence (motif1-8–9); however, most members of the subfamily ProT shared a different sequence (motif1-3–8-2–5). Motif1 and motif10 were present in most AAAPs. Motif9 was detected in groups ATLa, ANT, ATLb and LHT; motif5 was identified in groups ProT and GAT; and motif10 appeared in groups AAP and AUX. Identical structural distribution and motif sequences were detected within individual groups, which may be associated with the molecular functions of the groups.

Structural analysis of the 145 AAAPs revealed that the number of introns ranged from 1 to 12, with 117 and 24 genes containing only 2 and 3 introns, respectively, and the other 4 genes comprising 4 or more introns. The number of exons in the AAAPs ranged from 1 to 13; the AAP subfamily included 1–6 exons, whereas the ATLa, ANT, ATLb, LHT, ProT, GAT and AUX subfamilies contained 6–13 exons. Most genes within a group showed a similar genetic machinery. Among these, AsAAAP29 contained 12 introns and 13 exons. Groups ATLa and AAP exhibited the longest (11518 bp) and shortest AsAAAP29 sequences, respectively.

Chromosome localisation and AAAP replication in A.sativa

Among the 21 chromosomes harbouring the 145 AAAPs identified in A.sativa (Fig. 3), Chr3D, Chr5C and Chr6C contained the lowest and highest numbers of AAAPs, respectively. A total of 103 AAAPs were associated with 99 segmental duplications under the gene counts and 58 AAAPs were identified in 38 tandem replication pairs, with 18, 28 and 21 pairs of chromosomal replication relationships identified for chr1A-1D, chr2A-2 C, and chr2D-3 A, respectively. Among all replication events, the highest chromosome replication relationship was observed in 67, 61 and 59 pairs in groups A, C and D, respectively. Genetic replication events were concentrated on Chr2 and Chr6 in A.sativa (Table S2).

Based on the evolutionary analysis of AAAPs in A.sativa, we explored the collinearity between AAAPs in A.sativa and A.thaliana (Fig. 4). The results revealed 46 collinear gene pairs in A.sativa and A.thaliana, with pairs mainly distributed on Chr3C and Chr4A. In A.thaliana, ChrD exhibited the highest homology, with 18 homologous genes, whereas ChrC showed the lowest homology, with 13 homologous genes. Similarly, in A.sativa, ChrD showed the highest homology, with 18 homologous genes, whereas ChrC showed the lowest homology, with 13 homologous genes. Additionally, 15 genes were homologous with ChrA. These finding indicate that the AAAP gene emerged prior to differentiation between monocotyledonous and dicotyledonous plant species.

Cis-acting regulatory elements in the promoter region of AAAPs in A.sativa

We identified 95 genes involved in the ABRE (ABA) response system, including 34 genes associated with light-responsive elements, 63 genes containing gibberellin-responsive elements, 39 genes with auxin-responsive elements and 11 genes involved in regulating endosperm-specific negative expression. Promoter cis-component analysis revealed that multiple cis-elements in AsAAAPs (AsAAAP122, AsAAAP58, AsAAAP53 and AsAAAP108) were associated with light-responsive (G-box, MRE), drought-induction, gibberellin-responsive (GARE, P-box), and abscisic acid (ABRE) elements, respectively (Figure S1). The presence of cis-regulatory elements suggests that these hormones may regulate AsAAAP.

Expression pattern of AAAPs in A.sativa under abiotic stress conditions

We selected 16 genes and examined their expression under two stress conditions using RT-qPCR (Fig. 5A). The 16 genes selected from each subgroup consisted of two representative genes. Almost all members of the AAAP family were significantly downregulated following 24 h of salt or saline stress treatment. Members of the ANT (AsAAAP74/76) subgroup showed an upward trend under salt and saline treatments. Gene expression was highest at 12 h of stress treatment, then decreased significantly at 24 h of stress treatment. Members of the subgroup ATLb (AsAAAP127/129) were significantly expressed at 1 and 12 h under salt and saline treatment, respectively. Members of the AAP (AsAAAP42/43) subgroup showed an overall downward trend under both stress conditions. The expression of GAT subfamily members showed no significant change at 12 and 24 h of stress treatment. Difference in gene expression under salt and alkali stress reveal the ability of plants to respond to stress. Figure 5B and C show photographs of the phenotypes of four-week-old oat plants under salt and saline stress at different time points, indicating poorer growth with prolonged stress. This effect may be related to the expression of AAAPs.

Discussion

In this study, we identified 145 AAAP genes in A.sativa encoding proteins with 1080–13,896 amino acids and pI values ranging from 4.75 to 10.11 (Table S1). Previous studies have reported AAAP proteins with 83–653 amino acids and pI values of 4.91 to 10.30 in Moso bamboo [15], as well as AAAP proteins with pI values of 4.45 to 11.24 in maize [29]. In Cerasus humilis, the AAAP protein length ranges from 335 to 573 amino acids, with pI values of 5.43–9.38 [4]. These extensive variations in pI and protein size suggest that AsAAAPs are well-suited for plant adaptation to different environments. Moreover, the pI range of Gramineous crops is larger than that of Rosaceae [4, 29].

The identified AsAAAPs include 1–12 introns, with AsAAAP29 comprising 12 introns (Fig. 2). Except for five genes (PeAAAP4,16, 43, 48, and 51) without introns, the number of introns in Moso bamboo ranges from 1 to 10 [15]. Thus, AsAAAPs contain variable types of introns and a relatively conserved gene structure. Furthermore, AAAP gene duplication analysis revealed 46 genome-wide duplications in A.sativa and A.thaliana (Fig. 4). Moreover, in the AAAP genes of A.sativa, segmental duplications (61) had a greater effect than tandem duplications (38) (Fig. 3). Previous research detected 32 segmental duplications and five tandem duplications in maize [29], suggesting that expansion of the grass AAAP gene family mainly relies on segmental duplication. The conserved domains in these AAAPs exhibited high similarity with several pairs of conserved domains identified in other plants. For example, the AAAP gene families in Capsicum annuum and foxtail millet (Setaria italica) exhibit functional similarities; however, both comprise unique and highly conserved motifs [30, 31]. Although similarities exist between the functions and convered domains of different plant AAAP genes, some differences are also observed.

Promoter analysis revealed that 141 members contained cis-elements associated with cold, drought, light, gibberellin, and abscisic acid (ABA) responses. ABA is involved in stomatal movement, with plants adjusting their ABA levels according to stress severity [32]. The present study revealed that genes belonging to different AAAP subfamilies in A.sativa exhibited varying expression profiles under abiotic stress at different post-treatment periods. In addition, the expression levels of most AAAPs in A.sativa containing promoter elements associated with ABA (ABRE) were higher 12 h after salt stress treatment than at 1 and 6 h after salt stress treatment. These results suggests that AAAP plays a crucial regulatory role in plant responses to abiotic stress. Moreover, the functions of AsAAAPs identified in this study can serve as a reference for further research on other AAAP genes.

The expression of gene family members in the AsAAP subfamily decreased over time under saline stress. Most members of the AsAAP subfamily exhibit periods of significantly reduced gene expression in Setaria italica L. [31]. Genes of the AsAAP subgroup contain cis-regulatory elements such as the gibberellin-responsive P-box. As saline stress reduces gibberellin-related signalling, the activity of P-box dependent genes is also affected, suggesting that the P-box element links hormone regulation with the saline stress response in plants.

Conclusion

In this study, we identified 145 members of the AAAP gene family through whole-genome bioinformatic analysis of A.sativa. Further investigation of AAAP genes in terms of their chromosomal localisation, phylogenesis, conserved motifs, gene structure, and gene replication revealed that many response elements related to abiotic stress were localised in the promoter region of AsAAAP genes. Moreover, the P-box element decreased in crops under saline stress. This study provides a theoretical basis for further research on the biological functions of the AAAP gene family in A.sativa.
