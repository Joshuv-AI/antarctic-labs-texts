Bimekizumab as an Effective Treatment in Darier Disease: Clinical Outcomes of a Single-Center Retrospective Analysis of Patients Treated with Biologics
Authors: David L. Ranzinger, Paul Schmidle, Matthias Seifert, Sophia Wasserer, Kristin Technau-Hafsi, Cristina Has, Kilian Eyerich, Anna C. Pilz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionDarier disease (DD) is a rare autosomal dominant genodermatosis with limited systemic therapeutic options. Retinoids are the gold standard therapy, but long-term application is frequently limited by adverse events. Case reports suggest that biologicals approved for psoriasis vulgaris or atopic dermatitis may be beneficial in DD.MethodsIn this retrospective study, patients with DD who presented to the Department of Dermatology and Venerology, University Hospital Freiburg, between 2019 and 2024 were identified, and clinical efficacy, patient-reported outcomes, and histological features were analyzed in the patients treated with biologic agents in routine care.ResultsIn total, 15 patients (female: 11, male: 4) with a mean age of 48.20 ± 9.16 years were treated with biologic agents. The three patients who received the type 2 inhibitors dupilumab or lebrikizumab worsened or remained unchanged and discontinued therapy by week 12. Patients treated with the interleukin-17A/F inhibitor bimekizumab showed reduction of the Investigator Global Assessment score and body surface area from a mean of 3.58 ± 0.56 at baseline to 1.80 ± 0.82 at week 24 (p < 0.001) and from 32.00 ± 19.17% to 13.60 ± 9.05% (week 24; p < 0.001), respectively. The Dermatology Life Quality Index score decreased from 18.45 ± 9.87 to 4.78 ± 6.12 (week 24; p < 0.001). Additionally, on histopathology, the total numbers of dyskeratoses showed a trend towards reduction (31.17 ± 10.19 at baseline to 13.67 ± 13.25 at week 12; p = 0.062) with a decrease in the number of grains (10.50 ± 6.77 to 3.33 ± 3.88; p = 0.031).ConclusionsIn summary, these real-world data indicate that bimekizumab may be a beneficial and relatively safe treatment option in DD.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s13555-026-01890-z.

Key Summary Points

Why carry out this study? Darier disease (DD) is a rare hereditary disease, and systemic treatment options are very limited, mainly restricted to retinoids, which show limited efficacy and a high potential of therapy-restricting adverse events. Therefore, there is an unmet need to explore new treatment options for patients suffering from DD. This study represents a retrospective assessment of 5 years of routine care data from 15 patients with DD treated with biologic agents at a tertiary university center in Germany, to evaluate whether different biologics are effective in the treatment of DD. What was learned from the study? Biologic agents targeting the T helper cell 2 axis did not lead to improvement of skin lesions in a small, heterogeneous group of three patients with DD. Bimekizumab, an interleukin (IL)-17A and IL-17F inhibitor, reduced physician and patient-reported disease severity scores in 10 of 12 patients. Bimekizumab may be an effective and relatively safe treatment option for patients with DD.

Introduction

Darier disease (DD) (also known as dyskeratosis follicularis or ATP2A2-EDD), is a rare autosomal dominant genodermatosis with an estimated prevalence of approximately 1:36,000 to 1:100,000, which was first described independently by Darier and White in 1889 [1]. Clinically DD presents predominantly in seborrheic regions with erythematous brownish hyperkeratotic papules and plaques, wart-like papules, and pits on the hands, and it regularly affects the nails. In addition, oral and genital mucosal as well as ophthalmic involvement may be present [2, 3]. The disease course is relapsing–remitting with exacerbations commonly triggered by heat, sweating, ultraviolet (UV) exposure, and mechanical friction, often peaking during the summer months [4, 5]. Moreover, patients frequently experience recurrent bacterial and viral superinfections particularly with Staphylococcus aureus and herpes simplex virus [6, 7]. DD leads to a substantial reduction of quality of life with social isolation due to symptoms such as pruritus and malodor [3, 8]. Beyond cutaneous involvement, increased rates of neuropsychiatric disorders, including a lifetime prevalence of major depression of approximately 30%, as well as an elevated risk of heart failure and type 1 diabetes have been described [9–11].

DD is caused by heterozygous loss-of-function mutations in the ATP2A2 gene, which encodes the sarco/endoplasmic reticulum calcium adenosine triphosphatase type 2 isoform (SERCA2). These mutations lead to disrupted intracellular calcium homeostasis in keratinocytes, resulting in loss of desmosomal adhesion, dyskeratosis and a T-cell predominant inflammatory immune infiltrate [4, 12]. T cells are the key inflammatory cells in psoriasis and atopic dermatitis pathogenesis, producing interleukin (IL)-17A, IL-17F, IL-4, and IL-13. Targeting these cytokines through biologic therapies has tremendously transformed the management of psoriasis and atopic dermatitis in the last decade [13, 14]. But these biologic agents have also been reported to be partly effective in genodermatoses such as congenital ichthyosis [15] in which T-cell-driven inflammation rather displays a secondary feature. Up to date, systemic therapeutic options in DD remain very limited with retinoids being considered the most effective therapy, but long-term application is frequently limited by side effects such as cheilitis, hyperlipidemia, hepatotoxicity, and possible exacerbation of underlying mood disorders [4]. Case reports and case series have suggested beneficial effects of biological substances that are approved for psoriasis vulgaris and atopic dermatitis in DD, but the used drugs were heterogeneous, and the outcomes were controversial [16–18].

In this study, we present a retrospective single-center analysis of 15 patients with DD treated with biologic therapy at the Department of Dermatology and Venereology of the University Hospital Freiburg between 2019 and 2024, with a focus on clinical efficacy and patient-reported outcomes.

Methods

Patients with DD who presented to the Department of Dermatology at the University Hospital Freiburg between 2019 and 2024 were identified via the International Classification of Diseases Version 10 (ICD-10) diagnosis code Q82.8, and the diagnosis of DD was confirmed by review of the patients’ medical files. A subset of 11 patients additionally participated in the Freiburg Translational Dermatology Initiative, a noninterventional registry in which patients were evaluated at predefined time points (baseline, 4 weeks, 12 weeks, 6 months, and thereafter every 6 months) [19]. Treatment choices were part of routine care. Biologic treatment was offered to all patients with DD who qualified for systemic treatment on basis of the severity of their skin lesions and who had either used systemic retinoids unsuccessfully in the past or had other reasons why systemic retinoids were unsuitable. The choice of biologic was made jointly by the individual physician and patient and was based on pathophysiological rationale, available case reports, and patient preference, also considering potential side effects. None of the patients qualified for biologic therapy based on a separate on-label indication (e.g., bronchial asthma, atopic dermatitis, psoriasis, psoriatic arthritis). Since the available pathophysiological evidence predominantly pointed to involvement of the Th17 axis and the most effective blockade of this pathway was sought, patients were preferentially offered anti-IL-17A/F treatment. Before treatment initiation, patients were counseled in detail regarding the off-label use, uncertain efficacy, and the risk of infections, particularly fungal infections in the case of IL-17A/F inhibition, and written informed consent was obtained. If biologic agents were administered, the on-label doses and intervals approved for psoriasis vulgaris or atopic dermatitis were used.

Disease severity was evaluated using the physician-reported outcomes body surface area (BSA; in %), Investigator’s Global Assessment (IGA; 0: clear, 1: almost clear, 2: mild, 3: moderate, 4: severe), and, as an exploratory secondary measure, the Darier Area and Severity Index (DASI). The DASI score is a newly developed, not yet validated, composite disease severity index based on principles derived from the Psoriasis Area and Severity Index (PASI) [20]. The score was designed to quantify overall disease burden by integrating lesion severity of erythema, erosion, and hyperkeratosis on a scale of 0–4 and the percentage of affected body surface area on a scale from 0–6 (0: no involvement, 1: < 10%, 2: 10–29%, 3: 30–49%, 4: 50–69%, 5: 70–89%, 6: 90–100%) across the four predefined anatomical regions: head and neck, upper limbs, trunk, and lower limbs (Supplementary Fig. 1). Patients included into the Freiburg Translational Dermatology Initiative additionally completed several patient-reported outcome measures, including the Dermatology Life Quality Index (DLQI) [21], Patient Global Assessment (PGA; 0: clear, 1: almost clear, 2: mild, 3: moderate, 4: severe), peak pruritus numeric rating scale (0–10; within the last 24 h) [22], and questionnaires assessing happiness (0–10) and treatment satisfaction [23, 24]. The latter was assessed with the single question: “How satisfied are you overall with the therapy, that you are receiving for your skin disease?”, which was adapted from the DermSat-7, a validated patient-reported outcome instrument, and answered on a five-point Likert scale ranging from “not at all satisfied” to “very satisfied” [25, 26].

In patients participating in the Freiburg Translational Dermatology Initiative, lesional biopsies were obtained for research purposes at the baseline visit and again 12 weeks after treatment initiation, from the same lesional body site. As described in Jonsson et al. [19], a 6 mm punch biopsy was taken, and one third was fixed in formalin, and the remaining two thirds were stored in the biobank of the registry. Biopsies were taken from erythematous, hyperkeratotic papules at week 0, and at week 12, from the same region directly adjacent to the prior biopsy site. Formalin-fixed tissue (npatients = 6) was embedded in paraffin, cut in 2.5 μm sections, dewaxed, rehydrated, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin using standard methods. A dermatopathologist assessed the quantitative degree of total dyskeratoses as well as corps ronds (dyskeratoses in deeper epidermal layers with a central pyknotic nucleus and a surrounding perinuclear halo) and corps grains (dyskeratoses with scant cytoplasm and a highly condensed, barely visible nucleus located in the stratum corneum). In addition, the amount of lymphocytes and neutrophils using a semiquantitative scoring system ranging from 0 to 3 (0: none, 1: mild, 2: moderate, 3: severe) was analyzed.

The study was approved by the ethical committee of the University of Freiburg (25-1202-S1-retro) and was performed in accordance with the Helsinki Declaration of 1964, and its later amendments.

Descriptive statistics are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) for continuous variables and as absolute numbers with corresponding proportions for categorical variables. Statistical significance was assessed using linear mixed-effects models with visits as fixed effect and patients as random effect to account for within-subject correlations of repeated measurements. Patients with incomplete follow-up data were retained in the analysis. The Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used to evaluate statistical significance in patients treated with dupilumab and lebrikizumab, as well as in histological assessments. A two-sided p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Data analysis and visualization were performed using Python version 3.11.5, employing the libraries Pandas (version 2.0.3), NumPy (version 1.24.3), SciPy (version 1.11.1), Seaborn (version 0.12.2), and Matplotlib (version 3.7.2).

Results

Patient Characteristics

A total of 40 patients with a confirmed diagnosis of DD presenting to the Department of Dermatology and Venerology of the University Hospital Freiburg between 2019 and 2024 were identified (Fig. 1). Of this cohort, 25 patients received systemic therapy, with 15 patients being treated with biologic agents, including bimekizumab (n = 12), dupilumab (n = 2), and lebrikizumab (n = 1) without other concomitant systemic treatment of DD. Among these 15 patients, 11 were female, and 4 were male, and the mean age amounted to 48.20 ± 9.16 years (Table 1; Supplementary Table S1). 12 of 15 patients had received previous systemic treatments such as systemic retinoids (73.3%, 11/15) and ablative procedures (46,7%, 7/15) such as surgical dermabrasion (Table 1). 13 of 15 patients reported recurrent bacterial cutaneous infections during disease exacerbations in the past. Four patients had previously developed complications associated with systemic retinoid therapy, including elevated liver enzymes and keratoconjunctivitis sicca, and four reported a history of depression. Treatment Outcome of Patients with Dupilumab or Lebrikizumab

In the three patients treated with dupilumab or lebrikizumab, skin lesions of DD either worsened during treatment or remained unchanged (Fig. 2a). Consequently, all patients discontinued therapy by week 12. The IGA increased from 2.0 ± 1.0 at baseline to 3.0 ± 0.0 at week 12 (p = 0.180; Fig. 2b), the BSA involvement rose from 9.67 ± 9.07% to 16.0 ± 2.65% (p = 0.500; Fig. 2c) and the DASI score increased from 7.0 ± 4.3 at baseline to 11.83 ± 2.22 at week 12 (p = 0.180; Fig. 2d). When comparing individual DASI scores over time, no improvement was seen. Discussion

In this retrospective single-center analysis of 15 patients with DD treated with biologic agents, a divergent outcome depending on the cytokine axis targeted was observed. Whereas all 3 patients receiving the type 2 inhibitors dupilumab or lebrikizumab worsened or remained unchanged and discontinued therapy by week 12, 10 of 12 patients treated with the IL-17A/F inhibitor bimekizumab showed improvement in both physician-assessed (IGA, BSA, DASI) and patient-reported outcomes (DLQI, PGA, peak pruritus) over at least 24 weeks.

As is typical for a tertiary-referral DD population, most of the patients included into the analysis had previously received systemic retinoids and ablative procedures with unsatisfactory results, and several had developed retinoid-associated complications such as keratoconjunctivitis sicca, hypertriglyceridemia, and hepatotoxicity, underscoring the unmet need for effective and better-tolerated long-term treatment options [4, 27].

In our analysis, all three patients treated with type 2 inhibitors (dupilumab or lebrikizumab) did not show an improvement of their skin disease. However, favorable responses to type 2 inhibitors (dupilumab, tralokinumab) have been reported in two female patients with DD, paralleling observations in Hailey–Hailey disease (HHD) [17]. HHD is a clinically and pathophysiologically related acantholytic genodermatosis in which clinical improvement under dupilumab [28–33], tralokinumab [34, 35], and lebrikizumab [36, 37] has been described in several case reports and case series with a total of at least 23 treated patients. Interestingly, in transcriptome analyses of lesional skin from patients with HHD a predominantly Th17 signature without evidence of a Th2 signal has been observed [38]. Hence, it is currently thought that Th2 inhibition acts rather indirectly in HHD through modulation of intracellular calcium concentrations in keratinocytes [28]. Th17-driven inflammation has been reported in DD by several independent groups. Amar et al. showed enrichment of IL-17 signaling in transcriptome data of lesional versus nonlesional skin in patients with DD, and Roth-Carter et al. reported increased expression of IL17A in patients with DD compared with control patients [6, 38]. Ettinger et al. demonstrated upregulated Th17 related genes with elevated numbers of Th17 cells in lesional versus nonlesional skin of patients with DD [39]. They also reported clinical amelioration of skin lesions in three patients treated with anti-IL23/-IL17 treatment (guselkumab and secukinumab) that paralleled normalization of the inflammatory gene signature. In addition, Giorgio et al. observed marked improvement of cutaneous manifestations in three out of four patients treated with brodalumab, an IL-17 receptor A antagonist [40]. However, the durability of anti-IL-17 inhibition response in DD remains uncertain. Ettinger et al. showed that the response may be transient and dependent on treatment duration [16]. The patients in our cohort who were successfully treated showed sustained improvement through at least week 24, but later relapse cannot be excluded, and long-term follow-up is required to properly evaluate efficacy. Nevertheless, four patients of our cohort maintained disease control for 13–28 months under continued treatment. In the analysis presented herein, all patients received biologic agents without concomitant therapy, but combination approaches may further optimize outcomes. Low-dose systemic retinoids, which target the epidermal compartment, have been used together with IL-17 inhibition in DD and could be synergistic with anti-inflammatory biologic therapy [40]. However, retinoids remain the established first-line therapy, and biologics should be considered primarily if retinoids alone have failed or are unsuitable. To date, the relative efficacy of biologics and retinoids in DD has not been directly assessed. A systematic comparison with retinoids or with historical efficacy data would therefore be a valuable avenue for future research. Importantly, bimekizumab was well tolerated in patients analyzed here with no treatment discontinuations due to adverse events and no increase in cutaneous bacterial and/or viral superinfections. Concerning fungal complications, mucosal candidiasis occurred in two patients and was treated with topical amphotericin B, while one patient had a tinea corporis, requiring systemic terbinafine treatment. These fungal complications represent a known class-effect risk associated with IL-17 pathway inhibition, particularly under dual IL-17A/F blockade [41]. Given the impaired skin barrier and predisposition to secondary infections in DD, fungal screening prior to and throughout the treatment course should be considered when using IL-17A/F inhibitors in patients with DD. In patients at elevated risk for fungal infections, e.g., those with pronounced intertriginous involvement, obesity, diabetes mellitus, or a history of recurrent fungal infection, selective IL-17A or IL23p19 inhibition may be preferred. Finally, given the high prevalence of depression and other neuropsychiatric comorbidities in DD [9], it is worth noting that effective control of inflammatory skin disease with biologics has been associated with improvements in comorbid anxiety and depression in psoriasis, raising the possibility of secondary psychological benefit that warrants prospective evaluation in DD [42].

Limitations of this study include its retrospective design, although documentation of responses, including objective and subjective assessment, was relatively standardized. This was partly due to the high participation rate of 73.3% in the Freiburg Translational Dermatology Initiative. As treatment was not randomly allocated but guided by physician and patient preference in the context of pathophysiological considerations and available case report data, selection bias cannot be excluded. Nevertheless, real-world data may provide complementary or even more generalizable evidence compared with controlled clinical trial settings. In addition, we do not provide sufficient long-term data and cannot draw conclusions regarding potential disease modification. The DASI used here is a newly introduced instrument that still requires formal validation. However, it has only be employed as an exploratory secondary measurement tool. The single-center setting limits generalizability, but the large catchment area of our tertiary referral center allowed the analysis of a comparatively large cohort. As DD characteristically flares during summer, the timing of treatment initiation could confound efficacy estimates. But, although six patients started biologic therapy between July and September, the remaining patients began outside the summer months, and the prolonged treatment period of at least 24 weeks may attenuate seasonal effects across the cohort. The lack of response to type 2-targeted therapy observed in the three patients described here should be interpreted with caution, given the small sample size, the heterogeneous composition of this subgroup, and the absence of a documented atopic history (e.g., normal serum IgE levels; Supplementary Table S1). A type 2-targeted approach may therefore still be more beneficial in patients with a Th2-dominant endotype.

Conclusions

In summary, these real-world data suggest that bimekizumab may be an effective and relatively safe treatment option for patients with DD. Given the rarity of the disease and the inherent difficulty of conducting prospective randomized trials in this setting, well-characterized real-world cohorts such as ours provide an important evidence base to inform clinical practice and to guide the design of future studies.
