Adverse events in paediatric spinal deformity surgery – 12-year review of specialist spinal deformity practice
Authors: Edward J Bayley, Shreya Srinivas, Matthew Bellamy, Adya Ranjan, Dominic M Luttrell, Michael Athanassacopoulos, Anthony L R Michael, Lee M Breakwell, Ashley A Cole
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsAdverse events following paediatric spinal deformity surgery in the UK suffers from significant reporting gaps, as current data relies predominantly on voluntary incident submissions. This retrospective study aims to comprehensively assess the incidence of all surgical adverse events at a specialized paediatric spinal orthopaedic centre.MethodsA retrospective review of prospectively collected data from January 2010 to December 2022 systematically evaluated perioperative and postoperative adverse events. Patients were analyzed by scoliosis aetiology and adverse events were stratified according to the Clavien-Dindo classification system. Adverse events were further grouped by common themes including implant-related, infection-related, and neurological events.ResultsA total of 871 paediatric spinal deformity patients undergoing 1,020 operations with at least two years follow-up were identified. Adolescent idiopathic scoliosis was the most common primary pathology with 545 patients (62.6%). Overall, 324 patients (37.2%) experienced an adverse event. Adverse events were categorized by severity: 14.8% grade 1, 5.3% grade 2, 1.1% grade 3A, 14.6% grade 3B, 1.1% grade 4, and 0.1% grade 5. Implant-related issues were most frequent (n = 113, 13.0%), followed by curve progression (n = 33, 3.8%) and persistent pain (n = 33, 3.8%). Neurological events included four complete cord injuries (0.5%) and four incomplete cord injuries which fully recovered (0.5%). One perioperative death occurred.ConclusionIn this cohort of paediatric spinal deformity surgeries, over one-third of patients experienced adverse events, predominantly of low severity requiring minimal or no further intervention. Implant-related adverse events were most frequent, while the incidence of both complete and incomplete spinal cord injury, as well as mortality, remained reassuringly low across all procedures.Cite this article: Bone Jt Open 2026;7(10):1365–1374.Keywords: Scoliosis, Adverse event, Complication, Paediatric, Outcome, Spinal surgery

Introduction

The accurate documentation and analysis of surgical complications and adverse events are fundamental to improving patient care and safety in paediatric spinal deformity surgery.1 These outcomes enable the healthcare professionals involved in their care to better understand prevalence rates and their subsequent impact on patient outcomes.1 However, despite the critical importance of such information, there remains a concerning absence of a comprehensive, centralized database dedicated to documenting these complications systematically.2

The challenge is further compounded by the relatively infrequent nature of major complications and their heterogeneous presentation, making meaningful analysis particularly difficult. While various reporting mechanisms exist, each has notable limitations.1 The Scoliosis Research Society (SRS) has maintained a morbidity and mortality database since 1965, requiring its members to self-report complications as part of their annual membership obligations.3 However, this requirement can be circumvented through a financial penalty and, importantly, not all practising spinal surgeons in the UK maintain SRS membership.

The British Spine Registry (BSR) represents a national initiative designed to capture all spinal procedures performed across the United Kingdom (UK) and is financially incentivized by a best practice tariff.4 Yet, like many registries, its utility is inherently limited by the quality and completeness of data entry. Crucially, the reporting of complications within the BSR remains non-mandatory, potentially leading to significant under-reporting.

We conducted a comprehensive review of our patient records, encompassing all paediatric spinal surgical procedures performed at our trust over a 12-year period. This is a comprehensive modern analysis and report of all adverse events that occurred in paediatric spinal deformity surgery performed between January 2010 and December 2022 with a minimum two-year follow-up. By systematically quantifying the frequency and severity of such events, this study aims to highlight any key areas of concern, identify patterns that may contribute to adverse outcomes, enhance the informed consent processes, and could potentially inform improved strategies for prevention and offer future directions of reporting.

Methods

Study design and patient selection

An observational study was conducted using retrospectively collected data from our institutional spine surgery database. We included all patients who underwent spinal procedures for scoliosis deformity between January 2010 and December 2022. A local hospital patient administration system was also checked to ensure all patients were identified. All included patients had a minimum follow-up period of two years.

Data collection

A comprehensive review of all clinical records was performed by a senior spinal surgeon (EJB) and/or supervised clinical research student (MB, DML, AR). Multiple data sources were accessed to ensure the completeness. These included Electronic Document Management System (EDMS) (CareFlow; System C, UK), Impax radiology imaging system, BSR records, legacy paper records (pre-2016), operative notes, other local medical records, nursing documentation, and the departmental morbidity and mortality database (from 2020 onwards). Since 2020, all adverse events have been prospectively recorded in a dedicated morbidity and mortality database, regularly reviewed during departmental governance meetings, and so have been prospectively collected.

Patient classification

Patients were grouped based on aetiology to capture complication and adverse event profiles in similar types of surgery. The categories used included: adolescent idiopathic scoliosis (AIS) (age 10 to 18 years), neuromuscular scoliosis (including cerebral palsy), early onset scoliosis (younger than and including up to age 9 ), and syndromic scoliosis (including osteogenesis imperfecta (OI)).

To prevent duplicate counting in some cases where diagnostic overlap occurred (e.g. growing rod surgery in neuromuscular conditions, fusion rather than growing rods in a nine- to ten-year-old), each patient was assigned to a single best-fit category based on their primary pathology by a senior spinal surgeon (EB/SS).

Patient characteristics

We identified 871 patients undergoing a total of 1,020 spinal operations from 2010 to 2022 with minimum two years’ follow-up. Mean age at time of surgical procedure was 13.7 years (2 to 20).

Outcome measures

The primary outcome measure was the occurrence of all recordable adverse events at a minimum of two years. The collection of all adverse events instead of only complications was chosen to capture the entirety of untoward events encompassing minor or potentially expected postoperative sequelae that would impact the patient’s recovery and experience. While both the number of patients and number of operations were recorded, these were clearly differentiated in the analysis. The overall grade of the surgical complication was defined by the Clavien-Dindo Classification of Surgical Complications (Table I).5 Adverse events fitting into the primary outcome were categorized into key themes to allow analysis and improve clinical applicability, which included but were not limited to: mortality, neurological, wound-related, implant-related, respiratory, infection, persistent pain, postoperative ileus (gastrointestinal), nonunion, curve progression, proximal junctional kyphosis (PJK), neurological and scar paraesthesia, and ‘other’ complications.

Ethical consideration

This study was conducted as a service evaluation project and was registered with our institution’s Clinical Audit Department at the Sheffield Children’s Hospital. As per national guidelines and institutional policy, formal ethical approval was not required for this retrospective review of routinely collected clinical data.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to analyse complication rates within and between diagnostic categories. Descriptive and thematic analysis was conducted using SPSS (v29.0, IBM, USA). Continuous variables were reported as means with SDs or medians with the range or IQR as appropriate. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. The primary outcome was the frequency of adverse events graded according to the Clavien-Dindo classification. Secondary outcomes included the rate of return to the operating theatre, adverse event groupings, and specific complication types.

Results

Across the 871 patients, 545 (62.6%) had AIS, 81 (9.3%) had early onset scoliosis, 116 (13.3%) neuromuscular conditions, and 129 (14.8%) had syndromic scoliosis (including 22 patients with OI) (Table II). Overall, 324 patients (37.2%) experienced an adverse event during or after an operation (Table III). Most of these were classified as grade 1 (14.8%, n = 129), representing minor deviations from the normal postoperative course that required no subsequent intervention (Figure 1). In the remaining cases, we found that 46 patients (5.3%,) required pharmacological intervention, including blood transfusions (grade 2). Notably, no patients in the AIS group required a postoperative transfusion, however there were 127 patients (14.6%) who required surgical, endoscopic, or radiological intervention under general anaesthetic (grade 3B). Patients who required intervention (not under general anaesthetic) and those who experienced a life-threatening complication (requiring intensive care unit (ICU) admission – grade 4A) were a small proportion and mainly in the nonidiopathic patient groups. Complication/adverse event type

Overall, implant-related complications were the most frequent adverse events, affecting 113 patients (13.0%) with 127 (14.6%) patients requiring return to theatre under general anaesthetic (GA) across all groups (Figure 2). Curve progression and persistent pain each occurred in 33 patients (3.8% respectively), while wound-related complications were observed in another 30 patients (3.4%). Notably, fracture-related complications were rare, occurring in only three patients (0.3%). Neurological complications were also uncommon (2.9%): four patients (0.5%) experienced complete cord injuries, and four patients (0.5%) sustained incomplete cord injuries with subsequent full recovery. All complications across the whole cohort can be found summarized in Table III. Significant complications, grade 3 or higher, occurred in 149 patients (17.1%). The most common reason for returning to theatre across all groups was implant-related issues in 74 cases (8.5%), followed by curve progression in 21 (2.4%), infection in seven (0.8%), and PJK in two (0.2%).

Adverse events by patient group and diagnosis

Given the diverse clinical presentations between the included patient groups and varying aetiologies, we also conducted an analysis of adverse events analysed by primary diagnosis. This analysis was done to understand and summarize how adverse events and outcomes might differ across the distinct sub-groups.

Adolescent idiopathic scoliosis

Of this cohort, 135 AIS patients (135/545, 24.8%) experienced postoperative adverse events, with 35 (6.4%) having a second surgical procedure (Table IV). These patients had undergone surgery at a mean age of 13.6 years (SD 1.4), with procedures involving a median average of 11 fused vertebral levels (range 4 to 16 levels). In patients experiencing adverse events, most corrections were performed through a posterior approach (n = 126, 93.3%), whereas nine patients (6.7%) underwent anterior deformity correction.

There were no deaths or spinal cord injuries in the AIS group. Most adverse events (80 patients, 14.7%) involved grade 1 events, which were any deviations from the normal postoperative course not requiring treatment such as persistent back pain, implant-related reasons (prominence/implant to rod disengagement) or transient neuropraxia that did not require operative interventions. Significant complications, grade 3 or higher occurred in 36 (6.6%) with return to theatre in 35 (6.4%).

Grade 2 complications, requiring pharmacological treatment, occurred in 19 patients (3.5%). No postoperative transfusion occurred, and most pharmacological interventions were for persistent pain or infection. Only one case of infection required metalwork removal, with the remainder treated with antimicrobials which resolved. More severe complications affected 36 patients with 1 grade 3A (postoperative eosinophilic colitis) and 35 patients (6.4%) with Grade 3B.

Of the 35 patients requiring secondary intervention after the primary procedure (grade 3B), 18 were due to implant-related issues (including one for infection requiring metalwork removal), ten for progression of the curvature, four for wound breakdown, and one neurological complication after a dural tear/CSF leak. In addition, one patient had an implant-related fracture and one nonunion (0.2% each). Persistent pain was the second most frequent complication, occurring in 29 patients (5.2%), with one additional case of persistent pain combined with mental health concerns (0.2%). Persistent pain was intervened on in ten cases (1.8%).

Curve progression affected 11 patients (2.0%) with wound healing complications affecting eight patients (1.5%). As above, ten patients had further surgery for progression with extension of their fusion. Paraesthesia and scar paraesthesia were equally common, each occurring in ten patients (3.6%). Neurological complications (excluding cord injury) were reported in five patients (0.9%), while cosmetic issues affected three patients (0.6%). Respiratory complications, including respiratory infection, affected three patients (0.6%). Other complications included one case each (0.2%) of constipation, GI issues, nerve injury, nonunion, over-correction, and visual impairment (with recovery).

Early onset scoliosis

Of the study cohort, 73 EOS patients (73/81, 90.2%) experienced a postoperative adverse event (Table V). Significant complications, grade 3 or higher occurred more often with 52 patients (64.2%), and return to theatre in 48 (59.3%). Of these patients, 42 (57.5%) had congenital scoliosis, 13 (17.8%) had idiopathic scoliosis, and 18 (24.7%) had an underlying syndrome. Only four patients (5.4%) had anterior and posterior surgery on the same day with the rest undergoing only posterior correction. Mean age at surgery was 9.9 years (SD 18.9).

Other adverse events included neurological (including cord injury) (n = 3, 3.7%), systemic infections (n = 3, 3.7%), and PJK (n = 2, 2.5%).

Neuromuscular scoliosis

Adverse events occurred in 42 patients (42/116, 36.2%) with neuromuscular scoliosis (Table VI). Discussion

There is a recognized inconsistency and under-reporting of adverse events in surgery,6 but the general consensus is that it is important to know the incidence of adverse events that can occur after the surgical treatment of scoliosis, in order to allow informed operative planning, patient counselling, and ongoing efforts to improve the safety of patient care.7 At first glance, it may appear that our reported adverse event or complication rates are high, however we have included outcomes that others may not see as a true complication, e.g. pain or cosmetic unhappiness. Our paper aims to provide a comprehensive review of any adverse outcome, as perceived by the patient, to allow appropriate perioperative counselling as per the principles of Montgomery consent.8

We have used the term ‘adverse event’ instead of ‘complication’ because we have reported every unfavourable occurrence that was noticed during or after the surgical intervention. There is a wide variability in adverse outcomes associated with the operative treatment of paediatric scoliosis. Reports so far are from surgeon-reported databases,3 or from retrospective case studies and audit reports.9,10

Adverse events should ideally be defined before the collection of data, and the timing and severity should always be reported. In our unit, this approach has helped us to design a simple recording form following our initial review, and allowed us to prospectively collect data from 2020. While there are validated spinal surgery-specific adverse event forms that have been shown to maximize adverse event detection,11 it would be difficult to gather the data retrospectively. Therefore, we have used a simpler, Clavien-Dindo classification, which has been validated for various surgical specialities and has high inter-rater reliability.12 As discussed, this groups the complications based on the intervention required to resolve them, which in turn defines the severity.

We report a minimum two-year follow-up because this is the standard practice in our unit. We would have expected to capture any medium-term outcomes because, as a tertiary referral centre with a regional deformity team, they will be referred to our adult or paediatric services. It is not possible to discuss every adverse event and complication in depth; however, we have picked out some themes running through our clinical outcomes.

We have used the broad category of implant-related adverse events or complications to include screw malpositioning, screw head/rod disengagement, screw pullout, and rod breakage, even if they did not require revision procedure or intervention. Only 18 of the 38 implant-related complications required intervention under GA in the AIS group, but this was higher in the early onset group (32 of 48 cases with implant-related problems required further intervention).

Our cohort included 81 early onset patients; within this group, the greatest number of complications was implant-related (n = 32, 44%). In a similar retrospective study conducted in Japan, with 88 early onset patients, implant-related failures were also the highest reason for complications, with 72% of their EOS cohort developing complications related to implant failure.13 It is therefore important to note the common occurrence of implant-related complications in this category in the literature, which also demonstrates that our adverse event rate is at an acceptable level.

In our cohort of AIS patients, there were 18 implant-related return to theatre episodes, which gave an overall percentage of 3.3%. In general, it is accepted that the freehand pedicle screw insertion technique has a malposition rate of up to 20%,14 yet implant malposition resulting in significant symptoms remains low.15 Our implant malposition rate and return to theatre remain acceptable in comparison.

New neurological deficit from spinal cord injury associated with spinal surgery remains low,16 with around one in 400 reported for paediatric idiopathic scoliosis patients.17 We have no reported spinal cord injuries in our idiopathic cohort, and so this is not an unrealistic finding in our group of 535 AIS patients. Additionally, all these patients were cared for on a ward pathway after their surgery, showing that this standard of care is safe, and does not lead to delayed cord injuries due to noninvasive blood pressure monitoring.

We have seen a curve progression rate of around 2% in our AIS patients. This could be in part explained by the department’s focus on selective thoracic fusion (STF) where possible, rather than instrumenting both curves, and it is lower than the reported revision rates in the literature18 . It has been shown that with STF, decompensation of the coronal deformity can occur in a significant number of cases and requires further surgery in around 6% of cases,19 but this is usually not progressive in the majority of cases and, in addition, offers the benefit of sparing the lumbar motion segment in the long term.20 However, unhappiness with cosmesis is also more likely to be associated with STF because of the need for under-correction to achieve a balanced spine, which we have classed as an adverse event.21

In the early onset scoliosis group, we have not specifically separated our results into those undergoing short fusion and hemivertebrectomy from those undergoing growing rod constructs. Curve progression is expected in this group when compared with those having surgery at an older age, and will include those who are having crankshaft as well as simple progression of associated curves. The practice in our unit is to use spinal bracing treatment options for most early onset patients, as this has shown favourable results to stabilize and reduce progression of the curvature.22 Bracing may avoid surgery in many patients and, in others, delay progression until the child is at an age where a single final fusion can be performed. By consequence, growing rod instrumentation is considered only in the more complex high-risk groups which would include larger and stiffer curves. This could potentially explain the higher adverse event rate in this group.

Overall, our adverse events are higher in syndromic and NM scoliosis, with lower rates in idiopathic scoliosis similar to other reports,3,16,17,23-25 with no cases of permanent blindness, low rates of cord injury (0.5%), and one death (nonidiopathic). There are also few cases of nonunion across the groups, although it could be argued they may present at later follow-up.

The highest rate of surgical complications was noted in the syndromic scoliosis group, which would reflect not only the complex nature of the curves but also the presence of abnormal bone/skeletal dysplasia in this population. Given the diverse nature of diagnoses that are included, it is not possible to make any specific conclusions in this cohort for individual conditions. Specifically, syndromic scoliosis was associated with the highest rate of cord injury and the most severe medical complications/systemic response, even when compared with neuromuscular cohort. These outcomes demonstrate the need to highlight the medical risks in these patients when consenting for surgery,23 and the need for combined care with paediatricians perioperatively to mitigate the severity and range of potential medical complications.26

With regard to neuromuscular patients, large cohort studies have shown that this group has a very high occurrence of intraoperative and non-surgical adverse events in the perioperative period, with reported rates as high as 11%.27 In our unit, the aim is for patients to return to the ward within 48 hours of surgery; despite this, we have noted a low return to high-dependency unit/reintubation rates compared with reports in the literature of up to 18% of reintubation.27

In the SRS morbidity and mortality database review, this group sustained the highest rate of infections (3.2% to 3.9%).3 Similarly, we noted a higher rate of wound-related problems (6%) but a lower return to theatre or surgical adverse event occurrence. This suggests that if these patients are managed well from a medical and nutritional point of view, the rate of surgical adverse events can be kept low.

We have likely overestimated our incidence of wound infections and wound healing problems because we have not used a formal microbiological diagnosis as a primary criterion. Any mention of the need for wound checks, antibiotics by primary care, or the need for specialist dressings have all led to the wound healing being classed as an adverse event, with antibiotic use leading to a diagnosis of wound infection, even if we have not seen the patient. Being more aware of the early postoperative interactions of our patients with primary care teams has improved our practice in terms of communication with patients/carers. It has also prompted primary care physicians to contact the surgical team early if there are any wound problems. This will allow appropriate documentation and antibiotic stewardship to prevent unnecessary treatment, or prevent later antibiotic resistance.

Limitations of our study include its retrospective nature, and the fact that we may not have recorded all long-term complications unless treated again at our institution. We have assumed that any subsequent procedures performed out of area have been captured on BSR because it is the departmental policy to engage fully and record all procedures in that database. This is audited annually to prove compliance. We have not included blood loss specifically, but have considered the need for postoperative transfusion as a surrogate measure. Intraoperative spinal cord monitoring events that were not associated with a postoperative deficit were not collected. Other perioperative complications such as constipation and electrolyte imbalance, which may have affected the length of stay, but not the outcome, were also not looked at specifically. Finally, we acknowledge that the adjustment of spinal alignment and the subsequent biomechanical stress on non-fused segments may contribute to adjacent segment disease (ASD). While our study reports clinical adverse events at a minimum of two years, this follow-up duration is a recognized limitation that prevents a definitive long-term analysis of ASD incidence. Future studies should prioritize a complete prospective design to ensure standardized, complete outcome reporting, as well as assessing the longitudinal impact on adjacent-level disease and the comparative efficacy of different spinal implant variations between sub-groups. Scaling these through national and international collaborations will further improve the generalisability and conclusions.

In conclusion, this study reports a single-centre experience over 12 years and gives a narrative summary of all outcomes that patients may be disappointed with after surgery. The mortality rate (0.1%) and occurrence of spinal cord injury (< 1%) across all types of scoliosis in our series are low. This will allow appropriate counselling of patient expectations when discussing surgical options in the management of scoliosis.

Take home message

- With regard to paediatric spinal deformity surgery, overall adverse event rate was 37.2%, with marked variations between aetiological groups. Most adverse events were minor deviations from normal care with significant complications (Clavien-Dindo 3+) in 17.1% and return to theatre in just under 15%.

- Nonidiopathic cases experienced more severe complications requiring subsequent intervention, especially syndromic scoliosis.

- The most frequent reason for intervention across all groups was implant-related complications, with 8.5% of all patients needing a return to theatre for this under general anaesthetic. Severe outcomes, including permanent neurological injuries or death, occurred rarely (in less than 1% of cases for each).
