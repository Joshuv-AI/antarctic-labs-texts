Physicochemical and mechanical properties of gallium oxide-, cerium oxide-, and graphene oxide-modified mineral trioxide aggregate: an in vitro study
Authors: Gaurav Patri, Sonali Bansal, Harshita Lath
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMineral trioxide aggregate (MTA) is widely used in endodontics for its excellent sealing ability and bioactivity. However, its prolonged setting time, challenging handling, and limited early strength restrict clinical efficiency. This in-vitro study evaluated the effect of incorporating gallium oxide (Ga₂O₃), cerium oxide (CeO₂), and graphene oxide (GO) nanoparticles on the physicochemical and mechanical properties of MTA.MethodsNanoparticles (1 wt%) were homogeneously blended with MTA powder before hydration. Eighty specimens were prepared and divided into four groups (n = 20 each): unmodified MTA (control), MTA + Ga₂O₃, MTA + CeO₂, and MTA + GO. Tests for setting time, dimensional change, solubility, and pH were performed on six specimens per group, while compressive strength was assessed using ten specimens per group, in accordance with ANSI/ADA Specification No. 57 and ISO 6876:2012 standards. Data were analyzed using one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post hoc test (p ≤ 0.05).ResultsAll nanoparticle-modified groups exhibited significantly reduced initial and final setting times compared with control MTA (p < 0.001). Dimensional change improved in all modified groups at 24 h and remained consistent at 30 days. Compressive strength increased over time for all nanoparticle-modified cements, with CeO₂ + MTA and Ga₂O₃ + MTA showing the highest values at 30 days. Solubility outcomes were nanoparticle-dependent: Ga₂O₃ + MTA demonstrated the lowest long-term solubility, whereas GO + MTA and CeO₂ + MTA showed slightly higher early values. All formulations maintained an alkaline pH, with CeO₂ + MTA and Ga₂O₃ + MTA showing the highest levels.ConclusionsIncorporating 1 wt% Ga₂O₃, CeO₂, or GO nanoparticles into MTA enhanced setting characteristics, compressive strength, dimensional change, and alkalinity. Solubility changes were nanoparticle-specific rather than universally reduced. These findings indicate that targeted nanoparticle modification can improve MTA’s physicochemical performance in endodontic applications.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Keywords: Cerium oxide, Dental cements, Endodontics, Gallium oxide, Graphene oxide, Mineral trioxide aggregate, Physicochemical properties

Introduction

Mineral trioxide aggregate (MTA) is a cornerstone material in endodontics, widely used for pulp capping, apexification, perforation repair, and root-end fillings because of its proven biocompatibility and excellent sealing ability [1]. It mainly consists of tricalcium silicate, dicalcium silicate, tricalcium aluminate, and bismuth oxide. Upon hydration, it releases calcium hydroxide, which reacts with phosphate ions to precipitate hydroxyapatite, thereby enhancing marginal adaptation and sealing effectiveness [2].

Despite these advantages, MTA presents several drawbacks, including a prolonged setting time, difficult handling, and sensitivity to washout or material displacement before full setting, particularly in moist environments [3, 4]. These shortcomings have been linked to procedural difficulties and variable clinical success, with reported failure or retreatment rates ranging from 10 to 20% in cases involving exposure to moisture or blood contamination during placement [4]. This is largely attributed to the negative impact of blood and moisture on the material’s sealing ability, marginal adaptation, and mechanical strength during the critical early setting period [4, 5]. Although modified MTA formulations have demonstrated partial improvements in handling and setting behaviour [6], issues related to initial mechanical instability and material solubility remain unresolved [3].

Nanotechnology offers a promising strategy to address these limitations. Nanoparticles, owing to their high surface area and distinct physicochemical characteristics, can accelerate hydration, reduce porosity, and enhance mechanical and biological performance in dental cements [7, 8]. Among these, Gallium oxide (Ga₂O₃) exhibits antimicrobial activity, potentially through mechanisms similar to Ga3⁺, which disrupts bacterial iron metabolism [9, 10]; cerium oxide (CeO₂) displays antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties that may promote cellular protection and repair [11]; and graphene oxide (GO) provides mechanical reinforcement, improved hydration kinetics, and antimicrobial potential [8].

While several studies have examined the incorporation of different nanoparticles into calcium silicate–based cements, most have focused on single additives or limited physicochemical parameters [6, 7, 12, 13]. Although GO has been shown to enhance microhardness and microbial resistance in tricalcium silicate–based cements [8], systematic and comparative evaluations of Ga₂O₃ and CeO₂ incorporation into MTA remain scarce. Notably, this is the first study to investigate the effects of gallium oxide (Ga₂O₃) within an MTA formulation, offering new insights into its potential physicochemical contributions. To the best of our knowledge, no previous research has directly compared Ga₂O₃, CeO₂, and GO nanoparticles within a uniform MTA matrix. Given their distinct chemical functionalities—antimicrobial (Ga₂O₃) [9, 10], antioxidant (CeO₂) [11], and reinforcing (GO) [8]—these nanoparticles may exert unique influences on hydration kinetics, structural integrity, and long-term stability [6, 8, 12]. Therefore, this study aimed to comparatively evaluate the effects of Ga₂O₃, CeO₂, and GO nanoparticles on the setting time, dimensional change, solubility, and compressive strength of MTA. The null hypothesis was that nanoparticle incorporation would not significantly alter these physicochemical or mechanical properties compared with unmodified MTA.

Methodology

Sample size determination

The overall sample size for this in-vitro study was estimated using the World Health Organization (WHO) sample size calculation formula, considering a 95% confidence level (α = 0.05) and 80% statistical power (β = 0.20). Preliminary data on compressive strength variations between experimental groups were used to estimate the minimum number of required specimens, yielding a total of 80 samples (n = 20 per material group) to ensure adequate statistical power. In compliance with international testing standards (ANSI/ADA Specification No. 57 and ISO 6876:2012), six specimens per group were used for assessing setting time, dimensional change, solubility, and pH, while ten specimens per group were allocated for compressive strength testing, as recommended for mechanical evaluation of dental cements. This design ensured statistical adequacy for each test while maintaining alignment with ISO-prescribed minimum sample requirements.

Ethical approval

This study was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee of Kalinga Institute of Medical Sciences, KIIT University, Bhubaneswar, India (Ref. No.: KIIT/KIMS/IEC/1246/2024; dated 16 April 2024).

Experimental Group Allocation

Group I: MTA only (Control; Angelus, Brazil).

Group II: MTA + Gallium oxide (Ga₂O₃, 30 nm, 1 wt%).

Group III: MTA + Cerium oxide (CeO₂, 30 nm, 1 wt%).

Group IV: MTA + Graphene oxide (GO, 30 nm, 1 wt%).

A nanoparticle concentration of 1 wt% was selected based on prior studies demonstrating favorable dispersion and enhancement of physicochemical properties in calcium silicate-based systems [8, 14]. In Groups II–IV, nanoparticles (1 wt% of MTA) were weighed and added to MTA powder. The powders were then dry mixed manually to ensure homogeneous distribution using a mortar and pestle [15]. The nanoparticle‑modified powder was then mixed with sterile distilled water (3:1 powder‑to‑liquid ratio) and spatulated manually on a glass slab using a sterile spatula until a homogeneous paste was obtained. This manual mixing technique is described in nanoparticle‑modified MTA studies and is consistent with standard laboratory protocols [14].

Material characterization

X‑ray Diffraction (XRD)

The crystallographic structures of the nanoparticle-modified Mineral Trioxide Aggregate (MTA) groups were evaluated using an X-ray diffractometer (Bruker D8 Advance, Bruker Corporation, Germany). Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.5406 Å) was employed at 40 kV and 40 mA. Scanning was performed over a 2θ range of 5°–90° with a step size of 0.029649°. XRD analyses were conducted on set cement specimens (after 24 h of curing at 37 °C and 100% humidity) to identify crystalline phase compositions and detect any structural changes induced by nanoparticle incorporation [12].

Physicochemical evaluation

Setting time

The initial and final setting times were determined according to ANSI/ADA Specification No. 57 and ISO 6876:2012 standards. Stainless steel ring molds (10 mm diameter × 1 mm height) were filled with freshly mixed material from each group (n = 6 specimens per group), a sample size consistent with ISO and ADA guidelines for physical testing of dental cements. Specimens were incubated at 37 ± 1 °C and 95% relative humidity to replicate clinical conditions. A Gillmore needle apparatus (100 ± 0.5 g; 2.0 ± 0.1 mm cylindrical tip) was gently lowered vertically onto the specimen surface at 30-s intervals. The initial setting time was defined as the point at which the needle ceased to leave a complete circular indentation, while the final setting time corresponded to the moment when no visible indentation was observed. Timing was recorded using a calibrated digital stopwatch to ensure measurement precision under controlled environmental conditions [13, 16].

Dimensional change

Dimensional change was assessed in accordance with ISO 6876:2012 at two time intervals: 24 h and 30 days. Cylindrical specimens (3.58 mm height × 3.00 mm diameter) were prepared using stainless steel molds, with n = 6 samples per group per timepoint, in line with ISO guidelines. After placement, samples were incubated at 37 °C and 95% relative humidity for 24 h to allow complete setting. Specimen surfaces were then standardized using 600-grit silicon carbide sandpaper under continuous water irrigation. The initial length (C) of each specimen was measured using a calibrated digital caliper (precision: 0.01 mm), after which samples were stored individually in 20 mL distilled water at 37 °C for either 24 h or 30 days. Solubility

Solubility testing was conducted in accordance with ISO 6876 guidelines. Stainless steel ring molds (1.50 mm thickness × 7.75 mm internal diameter) were filled with freshly mixed material (n = 6 per group) and incubated at 37 °C with 95% relative humidity for a duration equivalent to three times the respective group’s setting time, ensuring complete initial reaction. Following this period, specimens were weighed to obtain the initial hydrated mass (PH₀), then dried in a desiccator for 24 h and reweighed to determine the initial dry mass (PD₀). Each ring was then suspended by nylon thread and immersed individually in 30 mL of distilled water at 37 °C for 30 days. After incubation, samples were again weighed in their hydrated state (PH₃₀d), re-desiccated for 24 h, and weighed to determine the final dry mass (PD₃₀d). pH Measurement

The pH of the storage medium from the dimensional change test was recorded at both 24 h and 30 days to assess the alkalinity profile of each material. Measurements were performed using a calibrated digital pH meter with a precision of ± 0.01, standardized against pH 7.0 and 9.0 buffer solutions prior to each session. All readings were obtained at a controlled ambient temperature of 25 ± 2 °C, using freshly collected aliquots of the immersion water to ensure accurate reflection of the material’s ion release behavior [13]. The storage solution was renewed before each pH measurement (24 h and 30 days) to prevent ion accumulation and saturation effects, as recommended in previous physicochemical evaluation protocols of calcium silicate–based cements [17].

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 27.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). The Shapiro–Wilk test was used to assess the normality of data distribution (p > 0.05 was considered normally distributed), and Levene’s test evaluated the homogeneity of variances.

For intergroup comparisons at each timepoint, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed for normally distributed data with equal variances, followed by Tukey’s honestly significant difference (HSD) post hoc test to identify pairwise group differences. When the assumptions for ANOVA were violated, the Kruskal–Wallis test was used, with Dunn’s post hoc test applied for multiple comparisons.

To assess within-group differences over time (i.e., between 24 h and 30 days), paired t-tests were used for parametric data, and the Wilcoxon signed-rank test was applied for non-parametric data. A two-tailed p-value ≤ 0.05 was considered statistically significant in all tests.

Results

X-Ray diffraction analysis

X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns of unmodified and modified mineral trioxide aggregate (MTA) are presented in Fig. 1A–D. The diffractograms demonstrate the characteristic crystalline phases of hydrated MTA along with the incorporation of the respective modifiers.

X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns of unmodified and modified mineral trioxide aggregate (MTA), showing (A) unmodified MTA, (B) MTA + Ga₂O₃, (C) MTA + CeO₂, and (D) MTA + GO. Characteristic MTA phases (Ca(OH)₂, C₃S/C₂S, C₃A, and C₄AF) are identified, with major peaks labelled along with their crystallographic planes (hkl), while additional reflections corresponding to Ga₂O₃, CeO₂, and GO confirm successful incorporation without alteration of the MTA crystalline phases

The unmodified MTA (Fig. 1A) exhibited prominent reflections at 18.1°, 29.4°, 32.2°, 41.2°, and 47.3° (2θ), corresponding to Ca(OH)₂ (001), C₃S/C₂S (211), C₃S (300), C₃A (400), and C₄AF (004), respectively. Among these, the three most intense reflections were observed at 29.4°, 32.2°, and 18.1° (2θ), indicating the predominance of calcium silicate phases and portlandite within the hydrated MTA matrix.

In the Ga₂O₃-modified group (Fig. 1B), all characteristic MTA reflections were preserved without any detectable peak shift, suggesting the absence of phase alteration. Additional distinct reflections were observed at 31.7°, 45.3°, and 54.8° (2θ), corresponding to the (401), (600), and (115) crystallographic planes of β-Ga₂O₃, confirming successful incorporation of the gallium oxide phase into the MTA matrix.

Similarly, the CeO₂-modified group (Fig. 1C) retained the characteristic MTA peaks, with supplementary reflections at 28.6°, 33.1°, 47.5°, and 56.3° (2θ), assigned to the (111), (200), (220), and (311) planes of cubic-phase CeO₂. The relatively sharp and well-defined peaks indicate the crystalline nature of cerium oxide within the composite.

In the GO-modified group (Fig. 1D), the MTA reflections remained unchanged, indicating structural stability of the base material. A broad, low-intensity diffraction peak centred at approximately 26.5° (2θ), corresponding to the (002) plane of graphene oxide, was observed. This broadening is characteristic of layered carbon structures with reduced long-range crystalline order, confirming the presence of GO in an exfoliated or partially disordered form.

Overall, the absence of peak shifts or disappearance of characteristic MTA reflections across all modified groups suggests that nanoparticle incorporation did not adversely affect the crystalline integrity of the MTA matrix, while the additional reflections confirm successful phase integration of Ga₂O₃, CeO₂, and GO.

Setting time

The initial and final setting times differed significantly among the experimental groups (p < 0.001 for both comparisons; Table 1). The Control–MTA group exhibited the longest initial setting time (209.83 ± 0.75 min), reflecting its known delayed setting behavior. Among the nanoparticle-modified groups, GO + MTA demonstrated the shortest initial setting time (40.83 ± 1.72 min), followed by CeO₂ + MTA (59.67 ± 1.03 min) and Ga₂O₃ + MTA (61.00 ± 1.27 min), all of which were significantly faster than the control (p < 0.001). Final setting times showed a similar significant reduction, with Control–MTA again recording the highest value (239.33 ± 0.82 min), followed by GO + MTA (199.83 ± 0.41 min), CeO₂ + MTA (109.50 ± 1.38 min), and Ga₂O₃ + MTA (82.50 ± 1.76 min), indicating an overall acceleration of the setting process in the presence of all three nanoparticles.

Dimensional change

Dimensional change was assessed at 24 h and 30 days and reported as a percentage relative to the initial specimen length (Table 1). At 24 h, all nanoparticle-modified groups exhibited significantly greater expansion compared to Control–MTA (0.85 ± 0.06%). The highest dimensional change was observed in the Ga₂O₃ + MTA group (1.28 ± 0.04%), followed by CeO₂ + MTA (1.24 ± 0.01%) and GO + MTA (1.22 ± 0.01%). Post hoc analysis confirmed that all modified groups differed significantly from the control (p < 0.001).

At 30 days, dimensional change values remained relatively stable across all groups. Control–MTA (0.91 ± 0.07%) and CeO₂ + MTA (0.92 ± 0.06%) showed nearly identical values, with no statistically significant difference between them. Although GO + MTA (1.21 ± 0.02%) and Ga₂O₃ + MTA (1.27 ± 0.03%) continued to exhibit higher dimensional change compared to Control–MTA, the overall intergroup comparison did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.057), and therefore these differences were not considered statistically significant.

Control–MTA exhibited the highest load-bearing capacity (233.13 ± 55.12 N), significantly greater than all nanoparticle-modified groups according to Tukey’s post hoc test. Among the modified groups, Ga₂O₃ + MTA recorded the highest load (127.21 ± 32.88 N), followed by CeO₂ + MTA (108.40 ± 28.75 N) and GO + MTA (54.00 ± 12.45 N).

At 30 days, all modified groups showed marked increases in maximum load. CeO₂ + MTA reached the highest value (468.24 ± 74.20 N), followed closely by Ga₂O₃ + MTA (450.14 ± 89.16 N) and GO + MTA (295.54 ± 60.28 N). Compressive strength

At 24 h, compressive strength values differed significantly across the groups (p = 0.026; Table 2). Control–MTA had the highest early strength (18.55 ± 4.60 MPa), which was significantly greater than all nanoparticle-modified groups (Tukey’s post hoc test). Among the modified groups, Ga₂O₃ + MTA exhibited higher strength (10.12 ± 2.86 MPa) than CeO₂ + MTA (8.62 ± 2.08 MPa), while GO + MTA recorded the lowest value (4.29 ± 1.22 MPa).

At 30 days, all nanoparticle-modified groups demonstrated substantial increases in compressive strength. CeO₂ + MTA achieved the highest value (37.26 ± 8.10 MPa), followed by Ga₂O₃ + MTA (35.82 ± 7.42 MPa), GO + MTA (23.51 ± 6.58 MPa), and Control–MTA, which declined to 16.04 ± 5.35 MPa. Despite these improvements, the differences between groups at 30 days were not statistically significant (p = 0.080), and no post hoc testing was performed.

Percent strength change over time

The relative change in compressive strength between 24 h and 30 days is summarized in Table 2. GO + MTA exhibited the greatest increase in strength over time (+ 448%), followed by CeO₂ + MTA (+ 332%) and Ga₂O₃ + MTA (+ 254%), indicating substantial material maturation. Conversely, Control–MTA showed a decrease in compressive strength (− 13.5%), suggesting possible degradation or limited post-setting reinforcement in the unmodified material.

Solubility

Solubility values, expressed as percentage weight loss, differed significantly among the groups at both 24 h and 30 days (p < 0.001 for both; Table 1). At 24 h, Ga₂O₃ + MTA exhibited the highest solubility (1.61 ± 0.01%), followed by GO + MTA (1.55 ± 0.02%) and CeO₂ + MTA (1.45 ± 0.01%), while Control–MTA had the lowest value (1.42 ± 0.01%). Tukey’s post hoc test confirmed that the differences among these groups were statistically significant.

At 30 days, solubility values increased in most groups. GO + MTA showed the highest solubility (1.77 ± 0.02%), while Ga₂O₃ + MTA demonstrated the lowest value (1.45 ± 0.00%). These changes indicate enhanced long-term stability in the Ga₂O₃-modified group. All intergroup differences at this timepoint were also statistically significant (p < 0.001).

pH Evaluation

The pH of eluates differed significantly among groups at both 30 days (desiccation conditions) and 24 h (immersion conditions) (p < 0.001 for both; Table 1).

Under desiccation at 30 days, CeO₂ + MTA exhibited the highest pH (10.50 ± 0.01), followed by Ga₂O₃ + MTA(10.10 ± 0.01), GO + MTA (9.60 ± 0.01), and Control–MTA (9.00 ± 0.01). All pairwise differences were statistically significant according to Tukey’s post hoc test.

A similar pattern was observed under immersion conditions at 24 h: CeO₂ + MTA again showed the highest pH (10.60 ± 0.01), followed by Ga₂O₃ + MTA (10.00 ± 0.01), GO + MTA (9.80 ± 0.01), and Control–MTA (8.60 ± 0.01). These findings highlight the superior alkalinity potential of the nanoparticle-modified groups compared to unmodified MTA.

Discussion

Mineral trioxide aggregate (MTA) remains a cornerstone in endodontics due to its biocompatibility, sealing ability, and bioactivity, making it suitable for pulp capping, apexification, and root-end fillings [12]. However, its clinical performance is often hindered by drawbacks such as delayed setting, challenging handling, and reduced mechanical integrity during early hydration, particularly in moist or time-sensitive settings [4, 13, 16]. To address these limitations, this study explored the incorporation of three mechanistically distinct nanoparticles—gallium oxide (Ga₂O₃), cerium oxide (CeO₂), and graphene oxide (GO)—into MTA at a fixed 1 wt% concentration. These additives were selected based on their potential to improve hydration kinetics, structural resilience, and chemical stability [8, 14].

To date, no peer-reviewed studies have evaluated Ga₂O₃ or CeO₂ incorporation within MTA formulations, although CeO₂ has shown promising biological outcomes in MTA-like systems [11, 18]. Existing research on these oxides has largely been confined to mesoporous bioceramic systems [18]. Ga₂O₃ is noted for its antimicrobial effects via iron metabolism disruption [9, 10, 19], CeO₂ for its redox-stabilizing antioxidant activity [18], and GO for its ability to reinforce structure and accelerate hydration [8, 20]. By systematically comparing these nanoparticles under uniform test conditions, the present study addresses a critical knowledge gap and provides a reference point for future biological validation.

In this study, Ga₂O₃, CeO₂, and GO were introduced into the MTA matrix at a uniform concentration of 1 wt%, based on earlier reports suggesting that low-level nanoparticle incorporation can influence hydration behavior and mechanical integrity without disrupting the bulk chemistry of the host material [8, 12, 14, 16, 20]. The experimental design adhered to international testing standards (ANSI/ADA and ISO 6876:2012), evaluating key physicochemical parameters including setting time, dimensional change, solubility, pH, and compressive strength [16]. Standardized methods such as Gillmore needle penetration, caliper-based length measurement, and mechanical load testing ensured compatibility with existing literature and reliability in comparative interpretation [12, 20]. The 30-day observation period was chosen to capture both early-stage performance and longer-term property evolution. Previous studies have shown that nanoparticle-doped cements can undergo delayed strength maturation due to extended hydration and densification processes [20, 21]. Inclusion of pH and solubility measurements adds further clinical relevance, reflecting how the modified cements might interact with surrounding tissues and fluids in clinical practice [12, 21, 22].

The results of this study demonstrated that incorporating nanoparticles into MTA significantly reduced both initial and final setting times across all experimental groups compared with the unmodified control group (p < 0.001) (Table 1). Among the tested formulations, GO-modified MTA showed the most pronounced reduction, aligning with earlier reports in which GO accelerated hydration through its large surface area and reactive oxygen-containing functional groups [8, 12, 20]. The addition of CeO₂ and Ga₂O₃ also contributed to faster setting, likely by promoting early calcium ion interactions and modifying the ionic balance during the hydration process [18]. These observations are consistent with previous studies that reported nanoparticle-mediated acceleration of setting reactions in tricalcium silicate–based systems [8, 20].

All nanoparticle-modified groups exhibited significantly improved dimensional change compared with the control group at 24 h (p < 0.05), while at 30 days the values remained higher than the control, although the differences were not statistically significant (p = 0.057) (Table 1). This outcome is favorable, as dimensional change is critical for maintaining the long-term sealing ability of MTA. GO again demonstrated the greatest improvement, possibly due to its ability to reduce matrix porosity and reinforce the structural framework of the set cement [8, 20, 21]. CeO₂ and Ga₂O₃ may have exerted similar effects through refined particle packing and modulation of the hydration front, thereby minimizing void formation and matrix disruption [18, 20]. Comparable enhancements in dimensional change have been documented in other oxide-reinforced calcium silicate–based materials [12].

Compressive strength testing revealed that all nanoparticle-modified groups exhibited statistically significant improvements compared with unmodified MTA at 24 h (p < 0.05), and strength values further increased across all modified groups after 30 days, although intergroup differences were not statistically significant (p = 0.080) (Table 2). Among the experimental formulations, GO-modified MTA showed the greatest overall increase, consistent with previous reports in which GO enhanced mechanical integrity through matrix densification and microstructural reinforcement [8, 20, 21]. CeO₂- and Ga₂O₃-modified groups also demonstrated superior strength development, likely resulting from improved phase stabilization and optimized internal packing during hydration and matrix formation [18]. These findings are in line with trends reported for other oxide-based cementitious systems subjected to similar mechanical testing conditions [12, 20].

Solubility testing revealed that nanoparticle modification influenced material behavior in a time-dependent manner (Table 1). At 24 h, GO- and Ga₂O₃-modified groups exhibited slightly higher solubility than the control, while by 30 days, Ga₂O₃-modified MTA demonstrated the lowest mass loss, indicating improved long-term stability (p < 0.05). These outcomes suggest that nanoparticle incorporation alters early hydration kinetics but enhances matrix consolidation over time. The overall reduction in long-term solubility observed in the modified groups may be attributed to improved internal cohesion, reduced microstructural defects, and enhanced matrix compactness imparted by the nanoparticles [8, 12, 18, 20, 21]. Similar findings have been reported in previous studies where nanoparticle additives improved hydration efficiency and reduced porosity in tricalcium silicate–based systems [8, 16, 21].

All groups maintained an alkaline pH throughout the 30-day evaluation period, with significantly higher values in the modified groups compared with the control (p < 0.05) (Table 1). Among the experimental formulations, CeO₂- and Ga₂O₃-modified MTA displayed the highest pH levels, which may be linked to their influence on sustained calcium ion release and prolonged hydration reactions [8, 12, 18, 20]. GO also maintained alkaline conditions, supporting previous findings that associate graphene-based additives with enhanced ion exchange and bioactive potential [20, 21]. The maintenance of strong alkalinity across all modified groups is favorable, as this property has been correlated with antibacterial potential and enhanced biological compatibility in calcium silicate–based materials [20, 21].

While these results are promising, several limitations should be acknowledged. This analysis was limited to physicochemical and mechanical properties, without assessing cellular behavior, inflammatory potential, or long-term biocompatibility. Although prior studies have suggested beneficial biological effects of GO and CeO₂ in comparable matrices [8, 18, 21], direct clinical extrapolation remains uncertain without further in vitro or in vivo validation. The study employed a fixed 1 wt% nanoparticle concentration based on previous optimization data [8, 12, 14, 16]; exploring a broader range may reveal dose-dependent responses or thresholds. Additionally, all testing was conducted under controlled laboratory conditions, which do not fully replicate intraoral factors such as microbial exposure, salivary flow, or thermal fluctuations. Future investigations should incorporate additional analyses such as radiopacity testing, scanning electron microscopy coupled with energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (SEM–EDS), water absorption assessment, three-point bending strength, and calcium ion release to obtain a more comprehensive understanding of the material behavior. Evaluating dimensional change using volumetric methods such as Micro-CT or optical scanning, particularly in artificial saliva, would provide clinically relevant insight into material performance under oral conditions. Future research should also investigate the biological performance of Ga₂O₃- and CeO₂-modified MTA, focusing on pulp cell viability, inflammatory modulation, and mineralization capacity. Their known antioxidant and antimicrobial properties in non-dental applications [18] warrant direct evaluation in endodontic models. Further work should also assess different concentrations, interactions with bonding systems and obturation materials, and in vivo validation under oral conditions.

Conclusion

Incorporation of gallium oxide, cerium oxide, and graphene oxide nanoparticles into mineral trioxide aggregate significantly enhanced its physicochemical and mechanical characteristics without compromising its alkaline pH. All nanoparticle-modified formulations exhibited reduced setting times and improved dimensional change, with long-term increases in compressive strength and reduced solubility compared to unmodified MTA. Among them, graphene oxide produced the fastest setting and maintained high alkalinity, cerium oxide achieved the greatest compressive strength, and gallium oxide showed superior dimensional change. These findings suggest that nanoparticle modification can address key limitations of conventional MTA, potentially improving its clinical handling and long-term performance. Clinically, such enhancements may translate into more predictable outcomes in pulp capping, apexification, perforation repair, and root-end filling procedures, particularly in cases requiring rapid setting and durable sealing.
