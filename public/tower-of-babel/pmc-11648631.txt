Prenatal social disadvantage is associated with alterations in functional networks at birth
Authors: Ashley N. Nielsen, Regina L. Triplett, Lourdes M. Bernardez, Ursula A. Tooley, Max P. Herzberg, Rachel E. Lean, Sydney Kaplan, Dominique Meyer, Jeanette K. Kenley, Dimitrios Alexopoulos, David Losielle, Aidan Latham, Tara A. Smyser, Arpana Agrawal, Josh S. Shimony, Joshua J. Jackson, J. Philip Miller, Marcus E. Raichle, Barbara B. Warner, Cynthia E. Rogers, Chad M. Sylvester, Deanna M. Barch, Joan L. Luby, Christopher D. Smyser
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SignificanceSocial disadvantage relates to variations in early trajectories of brain development. Yet, the role and significance of exposure during the prenatal period, when brain systems begin to rapidly emerge, are poorly understood. Here, we demonstrate that prenatal social disadvantage is associated with alterations in functional connectivity that are present at birth and are distributed across functional networks and subcortical structures key to executive function and behavioral adaptation. This evidence that deviations in brain development associated with social disadvantage begin in utero suggests that interventions to promote adaptive brain development and reduce psychiatric risk may need to begin prenatally and underscore the importance of social supports for expectant parents that reduce exposures associated with social disadvantage.

Social disadvantage, including reduced household income and neighborhood disadvantage, is associated with higher risk for poor developmental, psychiatric, educational, and occupational outcomes across the lifespan (1). Social disadvantage is particularly impactful when experienced in early life (2), presumably because adverse exposures in early childhood alter or disrupt complex neural processes that occur during typical early brain development (3). Further still, indirect prenatal exposure through social disadvantage experienced by mothers during pregnancy may alter fetal brain development and yield early deviations in brain structure, function, metabolism, and connectivity through hormonal, microbiome, genetic, epigenetic, and immune factors that cross the placenta and fetal blood–brain barrier (4). These early neural alterations may, in turn, contribute to predisposition toward risk or resilient developmental trajectories (2–4). Recent work from our group demonstrated that the relation between prenatal exposure to social disadvantage and brain development is evident in the first weeks of life, with greater exposure associated with smaller brain volumes (5) and aberrant white matter microstructure (6). Herein, we investigate whether prenatal exposure to social disadvantage is associated with variation in infant functional networks to contribute to the characterization of alterations in the emerging organization of large-scale brain systems that underlie elevated risk for poor outcomes.

Intrinsic activity across large-scale brain systems is spatiotemporally structured (7) such that activity of distant, but functionally related, brain areas are correlated at rest (i.e., functional connectivity [FC]), and these brain areas coordinate to form functional networks (8). Functional network organization supports complex cognitive, social, and emotional behavior (9); constrains learning and plasticity (10); and can vary across individuals (11) and in disease (12). These networks can be observed in the first weeks of life (13) and serve as foundational precursors that are refined over development (14). Alterations in FC associated with social disadvantage have been identified in children and adolescents (15–19). Further, some alterations have clinical significance and mediate the links between social disadvantage and subsequent behavioral and socioemotional outcomes (15–17, 19). The amygdala has been consistently implicated (15–17), but recent work has delineated the broader impact of social disadvantage, including brain-wide alterations in FC across both association networks important for executive function and sensorimotor networks key to adaptively interacting with the environment (18, 19).

One hypothesized link between FC and social disadvantage is that the quality, predictability, and nature of a child’s experiences in their environment during early development alter the coactivation and coordination of brain areas over time (20). Another possibility is that prenatal exposure to social disadvantage initiates these changes in emerging functional networks, with alterations in FC beginning in utero and persisting into childhood and adolescence rather than being driven by postnatal experiences alone. Given the limited social mobility in the United States and countries around the world (21), childhood social disadvantage is frequently preceded by maternal social disadvantage during pregnancy, making it difficult to disentangle the effects of pre- and postnatal exposure. Without measuring FC at birth, extant studies linking social disadvantage and FC in children have been unable to capture the contribution of prenatal social disadvantage to observed differences in functional brain organization. Further, characterizing the effects of prenatal social disadvantage on FC across the brain at birth may illuminate adaptations that confer early vulnerability and increase adversity-linked risk for psychopathology.

The current study includes a cohort of 399 mother–infant dyads participating in the Early Life Adversity, Biological Embedding, and Risk for Developmental Precursors of Mental Disorders (eLABE) study. Initially recruited through a larger investigation (22), mothers were identified in the first trimester of pregnancy and oversampled for low income. Prenatal exposure to social disadvantage was assessed during all three trimesters using demographic and survey measures and analyzed using factor analysis to derive a latent measure (encompassing parental education, health insurance, income, neighborhood disadvantage, nutrition) (23). Of the 385 infants imaged during natural sleep within the first weeks of life, a cohort of 261 healthy, full-term infants had at least 10 min of low head-motion resting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) data acquired to measure FC across the cortex, subcortex, and cerebellum. Multivariate machine learning techniques were implemented to extract patterns of infant FC that vary according to the latent measure of social disadvantage experienced by mothers during pregnancy. Critically, a separate validation sample of 151 infants with high rates of maternal social disadvantage participating in the Prenatal Cannabis Use and Development of Offspring Brain and Behavior During Early Life (CUDDEL) study were used to independently validate the findings identified in the eLABE sample. Multiple strategies were employed to interrogate the importance of individual functional networks and subcortical structures for the detection of variation in infant FC related to prenatal social disadvantage. Last, we examined whether either prenatal psychosocial stress (i.e., depression, anxiety, racial discrimination, stressful life events) or differences in brain maturity may contribute to the relationship between prenatal social disadvantage and variation in infant FC.

Results

Prenatal exposures were assessed using demographic and survey measures during all three trimesters and combined to derive a latent measure of prenatal social disadvantage (23) and align with previous eLABE studies (5, 6). Maternal health insurance status (private, public, underinsured), education (<high school, high school graduate, college graduate, postgraduate), national area deprivation index percentile (24), Income-to-Needs Ratio (25), and Healthy Eating Index (26) were combined to quantify prenatal social disadvantage in the full eLABE cohort (SI Appendix, Fig. S1 and Table S1). Description of maternal and infant characteristics of the eLABE sample is provided in SI Appendix, Tables S2 and S3. Linear support vector regression (SVR) was implemented to identify multivariate patterns of infant FC that carry information about prenatal exposure to social disadvantage. SVR is a sensitive and powerful tool to extract the multivariate relationship between a set of many features (here, functional connections) and labels (here, prenatal social disadvantage) in a training sample which can subsequently be applied to make predictions about unseen test individuals. Patterns of brain-wide FC (i.e., all 60,031 functional connections among 333 parcels and 14 subcortical regions; Fig. 1) that best capture variation in FC related to prenatal social disadvantage in the eLABE sample were identified with SVR and initially evaluated within sample using 10-fold cross-validation. Covariates with potential to influence estimates of FC and/or the relationship between FC and social disadvantage (e.g., head motion, minutes of data, age at scan, maternal medical risk, prenatal tobacco and cannabis exposure) were regressed separately from training and testing sets. Variation in prenatal social disadvantage was associated with multivariate patterns of FC at birth (R = 0.43, R2 = 0.18, P < 0.001, SI Appendix, Fig. S2) in the eLABE sample (Fig. 2A). SI Appendix, Table S4 details the extent to which infant FC is associated with individual components of the social disadvantage latent factor and when combined as a composite.

Study overview. Prenatal social disadvantage was measured as a latent composite of maternal education, health insurance status, income-to-needs ratio, area deprivation index, and a healthy eating index. Infant FC was measured by imaging neonates within the first weeks of life, extracting fMRI timeseries from 333 parcels covering 12 functional networks and 14 subcortical regions, and calculating the temporal correlation between each pair of regions and/or parcels. In a 10-fold cross validation framework, SVR was used to train a model to detect differences in prenatal social disadvantage from patterns in infant FC. Trained models were evaluated and interrogated during the testing phase. (1) As part of the 10-fold cross validation framework, the trained model was evaluated using the 10% of the eLABE sample left out of training and infant FC was used to estimate prenatal social disadvantage for the left-out sample. Variance explained (R2) was quantified by comparing the estimated social disadvantage from FC and true social disadvantage across all individuals in the eLABE sample. (2) An independent validation sample was used to evaluate the generalizability of the trained model and infant FC was used to estimate economic disadvantage for the CUDDEL sample. Ten estimates of social disadvantage were generated for each individual in the CUDDEL sample corresponding to the 10 folds of cross-validation. Variance explained (R2) was quantified by comparing the average estimate of social disadvantage based on FC with the true Income-to-Needs Ratio across all individuals in the CUDDEL sample. (3) To determine whether each individual functional network contains FC that varies with social disadvantage, SVR was used to train a model to detect differences in prenatal social disadvantage using only the FC from a single functional network (within and between network connections). Variance explained (R2) was quantified by comparing the estimated social disadvantage based on FC from a single functional network and the true social disadvantage across all individuals in the eLABE sample. (4) To interrogate how each individual functional network contributes to the estimation of prenatal social disadvantage from brain-wide patterns of FC, FC from a single functional network (within network and between network connections) or a single parcel/subcortical ROI (similar to a seed map) was randomly permuted across the left-out 10% of subjects during the testing phase of 10-fold cross validation, removing any variation related to prenatal social disadvantage. Reduction in variance explained (ΔR2) was quantified by comparing the variance explained when estimating prenatal social disadvantage using with and without permuted functional connections.

External Validation in a Separate, Independent Sample.

Patterns of FC related to social disadvantage identified in the eLABE sample were used to estimate prenatal exposure to adversity in a separate cohort of infants with high rates of maternal socioeconomic disadvantage (Income-to-Needs Ratio: mean = 0.78; SD = 2.17; range = 0.07 to 11.0) from the CUDDEL study (n = 151). Infants in CUDDEL were imaged at similar ages using identical imaging parameters and procedures. Because the CUDDEL study did not acquire all measures used to operationalize social disadvantage, estimates of social disadvantage based on infant FC were instead validated against an individual’s true economic disadvantage (i.e., inverse of the Income-to-Needs Ratio during pregnancy) to demonstrate that identified patterns of FC generalized beyond the eLABE cohort. The Income-to-Needs Ratio is a significant contributor to the latent social disadvantage variable (23) (SI Appendix, Fig. S1), and economic disadvantage is correlated with the estimates of social disadvantage based on FC in eLABE (R = 0.53, Fig. 2B). Critically, patterns of infant FC related to social disadvantage in the eLABE cohort were robust and significantly generalized to the CUDDEL sample (P = 0.004, SI Appendix, Fig. S2) to predict prenatal economic disadvantage (R = 0.18, Fig. 2B). Patterns of FC related to prenatal economic disadvantage were also identified in eLABE with SVR and were successfully applied to the CUDDEL sample to provide direct validation and further demonstrate generalizability (SI Appendix, Fig. S3).

The Role of Individual Functional Networks in Estimating Prenatal Social Disadvantage.

Feature selection and feature permutation were implemented to interrogate the specific functional networks (14) that contained variation related to prenatal social disadvantage. First, selecting only functional connections from a single network (i.e., within-network, between-network, and network-to-subcortex) revealed that each network contained FC related to social disadvantage and the variance explained was better than expected by chance for almost all networks (except visual and cinguloparietal; Fig. 3A). Estimation of social disadvantage differed by functional network, but network size has been consistently found to be related to performance with multivariate machine learning methods (27). When the same number of parcels were randomly subselected from each functional network, estimation of social disadvantage from FC still differed between functional networks and appeared greatest in association networks such as the frontoparietal (FP), dorsal attention (DAN), and ventral attention (VAN) networks (Fig. 3B). Second, the size of the effect of removing the contribution of a single functional network (i.e., permuting FC from each network before estimating an individual’s prenatal social disadvantage) was small (ΔR2 = −0.05), but differed across functional networks (Fig. 3C). Disrupting FC from the FP, DAN, VAN, retrosplenial (RST), and somatomotor networks negatively impacted the detection of differences in social disadvantage from FC more than expected by chance (Fig. 3D), suggesting these networks contain unique information not represented elsewhere in the brain. FC related to prenatal social disadvantage is present across multiple functional networks, but the effect differs and is more pronounced in select association networks and somatomotor networks.

The Role of Individual Subcortical Structures in Estimating Prenatal Social Disadvantage.

A similar approach was extended to interrogate which subcortical structures contribute to the detection of social disadvantage from infant FC. Disrupting FC from the amygdala or the putamen negatively impacted the detection of differences in social disadvantage from whole-brain FC more than expected by chance (Fig. 4A). The size of the effect of disrupting FC from a single subcortical structure was small (ΔR2 = −0.005), but was greater than when disrupting FC from a matched number of connections. Because amygdala FC has been frequently implicated in studies of social disadvantage in older children (15–17), we examined the relationship between amygdala FC at birth and prenatal social disadvantage. As observed in older children, prenatal social disadvantage was associated with reduced FC between the amygdala and medial prefrontal cortex at birth (blue arrow in Fig. 4B). In addition, prenatal social disadvantage was associated with reduced amygdala FC to association cortices and increased FC to sensorimotor cortices (yellow arrow in Fig. 4B). However, this pattern was not unique to the amygdala—all subcortical-cortical FC examined shared a similar spatial pattern of relationships to social disadvantage (Fig. 4C) characterized by reduced FC with association networks and increased FC with sensorimotor networks.

Benchmarking Variation in Infant FC Related Social Disadvantage.

The magnitude and spatial pattern of variation in infant FC related to social disadvantage was also considered in relation to variation in infant FC related to both maternal stress during pregnancy and infant brain maturation as potential confounds or mediators of this relationship. Prenatal exposure to psychosocial stress was also assessed during pregnancy (23). Prenatal depression symptoms, perceived stress, the frequency and severity of previous adverse life events, and experienced racial discrimination (SI Appendix, Table S2) were combined to quantify prenatal psychosocial stress. Prenatal social disadvantage was modestly correlated with prenatal psychosocial stress (R = 0.35, P < 0.001) and patterns of brain-wide FC related to prenatal psychosocial stress were identified with SVR, but psychosocial stress was a relatively poorer predictor of variation in infant FC (R = 0.17, R2=0.03, P = 0.005).

Prenatal social disadvantage was associated with earlier delivery and, in turn, infants with younger gestational age at birth (R = −0.26, SI Appendix, Fig. S4A) and younger postmenstrual age (PMA) at scan (R = −0.22), potentially creating a sample in which social disadvantage and brain maturity are confounded. Previous work has demonstrated that infant FC varies substantially according to maturity defined by PMA at scan (14) (here, R2 = 0.51), though this variation is predominantly associated with time since birth (R2 = 0.39) rather than that during gestation (R2 = 0.05). Expanding the maturity covariates to account for potential nonlinear relationships did not impact the estimation of prenatal social disadvantage (R2 = 0.19). Restricting the sample to infants born at 39 to 40 gestational weeks (n = 152) reduced the relationship between maturity and social disadvantage (R = −0.14, P = 0.09), but also did not negatively impact detection of prenatal social disadvantage from infant FC (R2 = 0.26, SI Appendix, Fig. S4B). Finally, feature interrogation revealed limited overlap between the functional networks, parcels, and subcortical regions identified as important for capturing prenatal social disadvantage and those capturing differences in brain maturity (SI Appendix, Fig. S5) and when considered independently, the size and directionality of these effects were largely dissimilar (SI Appendix, Fig. S6).

Discussion

The main objective of this study was to determine whether associations between prenatal exposure to social disadvantage and FC are evident in the first weeks of life. Our findings indicate that the functional organization of large-scale brain systems shortly after birth relates to a latent measure of social disadvantage experienced by mothers during pregnancy. Brain-wide patterns in infant FC were associated with differences in prenatal exposure to social disadvantage and, importantly, the identified patterns generalized to an independently collected validation sample. We saw evidence that prenatal social disadvantage was associated with FC in all functional networks and subcortical structures. However, FC from the FP, DAN, VAN, RST, and somatomotor networks and the amygdala and putamen were needed to best estimate an individual infant’s exposure to social disadvantage. As observed in older children, amygdala FC was related to prenatal social disadvantage, with similar associations also found across other subcortical structures. Identified patterns of FC related to prenatal social disadvantage could not be explained by differences in exposure to psychosocial stress during pregnancy nor differences in infant brain maturity. Our findings shed light on how common forms of adversity experienced during fetal development are linked to variation in the foundations of functional brain organization. These findings point to in utero beginnings of vulnerabilities in functional brain development related to adaptations to the maternal prenatal environment during gestation.

The current study adds to a growing literature demonstrating the powerful influence of the prenatal maternal environment on fetal brain development by illuminating the associations between prenatal social disadvantage and the foundations of functional brain organization observed at birth. Prenatal exposure to social disadvantage is associated with elevated stress (28), greater exposure to environmental toxins, and altered inflammatory (29) and metabolic (30) processes, and may shape emerging FC through indirect mechanisms resulting from the altered uterine environment (4). For example, maternal cortisol production during pregnancy is known to be elevated in the presence of social disadvantage (31), with elevated maternal cortisol predicting amygdala FC in infants (32). Multiple mechanisms of gene-environment interplay could undergird the observed findings, as genetic predisposition for high stress reactivity may amplify the effect of prenatal social disadvantage in both mothers and offspring (33). During gestation, fetal brain maturation occurs rapidly through multiple interrelated mechanisms (e.g., neural migration, differentiation, synaptic pruning, myelination, synaptic plasticity) (34), and exposure to social disadvantage during this window of heightened capacity for change could alter or disrupt one or more of these processes with dramatic, cascading consequences for brain development (3). We identified alterations in FC indicative of differences in the propagation of intrinsic brain activity (7) in the first weeks of life, earlier than has been previously reported (15–19), demonstrating that indirect exposure to social disadvantage during the fetal period plays a role in the associations between early adversity and brain development. This early brain activity and related large-scale organization are a key factor that constrains how the brain responds to task demands (35) and learns and adapts to the environment (10). Thus, differences in functional brain organization related to social disadvantage present at birth may reflect altered prenatal programming and shape how developing children respond to and learn from their surroundings. Greater social disadvantage was associated with weaker FC for some connections and networks and stronger FC for others (Fig. 4B and SI Appendix, Fig. S6). These adaptations may prove advantageous for continued development in adverse environments, but come with tradeoffs that increase long-term risk for poor mental health, educational, and occupational outcomes (36). Characterization of the long-term clinical significance, malleability to intervention, and persistence over time of the identified FC associated with prenatal social disadvantage is important and remains to be determined.

While the effects of social disadvantage were brain-wide and distributed across functional networks and subcortical structures, the association between prenatal social disadvantage and infant FC differed and was more pronounced in select functional networks and subcortical structures, consistent with differential vulnerability to prenatal social disadvantage across the cortex and subcortex. Differences in vulnerability to the prenatal environment likely arise through the complex interaction between the genetic programming and ordered developmental processes underlying the heterogeneous cytoarchitecture across the brain and the mechanistic action of maternal factors associated with social disadvantage. For example, effects of DNA methylation and other epigenetic mechanisms differ by cell type (37) and receptor density for stress hormones and inflammatory cytokines vary across the brain (38). Because metabolic rates vary across the brain [e.g., increased aerobic glycolysis in the default mode network (DMN)] (39), stress hormone-induced changes in energy demand (40) may contribute to patterns of intrinsic activity that differ by functional network. Our findings suggest that the identified pattern of altered FC may point to where these mechanisms have the greatest effect in the neonatal brain. Further, neurodevelopment occurs at different rates across the brain, with maturation occurring earlier and faster in sensorimotor brain systems and later and slower in association brain systems (34). Maternal inflammation associated with social disadvantage can activate developing microglia (41) and may interact with the process of pruning excess neurites and phagocytosis of dying neurons that occurs during fetal brain development (42), potentially occurring at different rates in early vs. late developing brain systems. Several later developing association networks (FP, DAN, VAN) appeared to be differentially impacted by prenatal social disadvantage, indicating these early prenatal mechanisms may initiate the cascade of further deviations when developmental change becomes more rapid.

Amygdala FC, the focus of many studies of early life adversity, was related to prenatal social disadvantage. The amygdala may be particularly vulnerable to early life adversity given the increased number of glucocorticoid receptors in this region (43) and is of particular interest due to its established link to psychiatric disorders (44). Altered amygdala FC has been frequently identified in older children exposed to social adversity, such that parental education during infancy (15), income-to-needs during preschool (17), and childhood neighborhood socioeconomic status (18) have all been associated with weaker FC between the amygdala and medial prefrontal cortex. In the current study, amygdala FC at birth was differentially important for estimating prenatal exposure to social disadvantage and, specifically, weaker FC between the amygdala and medial prefrontal cortex is consistent with prior work in older children. Yet, greater social disadvantage was also associated with stronger FC between the amygdala and sensorimotor systems indicating that prenatal exposures may alter the balance of activity across multiple brain networks in different ways. Further, this pattern was similar across subcortical structures, potentially suggesting either shared vulnerability or a shared mechanism of exposure. This finding parallels what has been observed in infant brain structure, as prenatal social disadvantage is associated with smaller amygdala volumes at birth, but similar effects are also present across subcortical structures (5). Broadening the scope of future investigations of early life adversity to examine similarities across the subcortex and cerebellum may be necessary to shed light on the broader impact and shared mechanisms of adverse environmental exposures.

In older children, variation in FC related to social disadvantage has been shown to be clinically and behaviorally meaningful. Alterations in amygdala FC mediate relationships between childhood social disadvantage and later negative mood/depression symptoms (17), anxiety problems (18), and less prosocial behavior (15). Further, altered patterns of FC across sensorimotor and association networks mediate the relationship between social disadvantage and cognitive delays (19). These alterations in FC are thought to reflect a history of adverse exposures that alter coactivation across the lifespan (19). Brain-wide effects of social disadvantage (18, 19) have been attributed to environmental differences in cognitive stimulation/deprivation that drive lower-level sensory and perceptual processes and cascade to impact higher-level processes like executive function (19). Our findings suggest that the maternal prenatal environment may initiate this process, shaping the foundations of functional brain organization upon which later experience-dependent processes build. Importantly, FC related to prenatal social disadvantage in brain systems subserving cognitive and socioemotional capacities is evident before differences in behavior can be detected, potentially representing a useful early risk marker and target for intervention for subsequent clinical and behavioral outcomes.

Our findings should be viewed in light of the study limitations. First, this study is correlational and cannot demonstrate that the observed alterations in infant FC were caused by prenatal exposure to social disadvantage. Second, this study cannot disentangle prenatal social disadvantage, and the indirect exposures that occur through changes in the uterine environment, from postnatal social disadvantage and the direct exposures that occur through experiences during the first weeks of life. Prenatal and postnatal social disadvantage are strongly correlated (45), though may impact infant FC through different neural mechanisms (i.e., indirect vs. direct exposures). The study aimed to minimize postnatal exposure by acquiring FC from infants shortly after birth, but it remains possible that postnatal experiences (first days and weeks of life) may contribute to the observed patterns of FC. Third, given the relationship between prenatal exposure to social disadvantage and earlier delivery, it cannot be entirely ruled out that the identified alterations in FC are in part due to differences in brain maturity related to shorter gestation. However, we demonstrated that, after controlling for and restricting the variation related to gestational age at birth, patterns of FC related to social disadvantage remained. Fourth, multivariate pattern classification techniques are a powerful tool to detect subtle and complex differences in neuroimaging data, but also come with challenges (27). These approaches utilize multivariate information to prioritize classification of individuals over the explanation of the underlying mechanisms (27) and are not well-suited to compare effect sizes or characterize directionality of associations between prenatal social disadvantage and infant FC (46). Explanatory approaches would complement these findings to further characterize how prenatal exposures are associated with FC at birth. In addition, because these approaches are sensitive to multivariate patterns but are agnostic to the source of these patterns, the observed estimation of social disadvantage from infant FC could be artificially enhanced by variation attributable to other disadvantage-related confounding variables and/or information leakage because social disadvantage was defined using the full eLABE sample. To limit these concerns, we demonstrated that 1) the patterns of FC identified with SVR could estimate social disadvantage in an independent validation sample, 2) differences in psychosocial stress which frequently accompanies social disadvantage did not contribute to estimates of social disadvantage from FC, 3) the latent factor was unchanged (R = 0.99) when individuals in the test set were excluded, and 4) patterns of FC could be identified to estimate social disadvantage when defined using a simple composite generated separately in the training and testing sets. Last, longitudinal neuroimaging and follow-up assessment of socioemotional, cognitive, and other behavioral outcomes are necessary to illuminate the significance of early alterations in FC related to prenatal social disadvantage for brain and behavioral development.

Conclusions

The current study reveals that prenatal exposure to social disadvantage is related to functional brain organization shortly after birth. Our group has demonstrated that associations between social disadvantage and brain development begin in utero and are evident at birth across three modalities, including brain volumes, white matter microstructure, and now FC (5, 6). During a developmental window marked by rapid brain maturation and heightened neural plasticity, the finding that FC varies according to prenatal social disadvantage highlights the potency of the prenatal environment and importance of more focused attention to these exposures in fetal development. This potential importance is underscored by the cascading impact of these early exposures on subsequent brain maturation. These findings point to a critical need to protect pregnant people from such exposures to support fetal brain development. The earliest possible identification of these processes opens opportunities for earlier and potentially more effective prevention. Interventions that reduce social disadvantage and increase access to social supports for expectant parents should be urgently considered as a key target to protect fetal brain development.

Methods

Study Design and Population.

eLABE.

This cohort has been previously reported on in Luby et al. (23), Triplett et al. (5), and Lean et al. (6) and is described below. In this longitudinal, observational, multiwave, multimethod collaboration, a cohort of pregnant women who participated in a large-scale study of preterm birth (22) within the Washington University in St. Louis March of Dimes Prematurity Research Center were recruited from September 1, 2017, to February 28, 2020. Women from the parent study (n = 663) were invited to participate in this investigation with the following exclusion criteria: multiple gestation, infections known to cause congenital disease (e.g., syphilis), and/or alcohol or drug use other than tobacco and marijuana. A total of 395 eligible participating mothers completed assessments during each trimester of pregnancy and at delivery. Medical data from mothers and their 399 singleton offspring (four mothers had two singleton births during the recruitment period) were collected from questionnaires and medical record review. In order to assess the contributions of racial and ethnic discrimination and inequities, pregnant mothers’ self-reported race and ethnicity were extracted from the medical record. The following options were provided for race: American Indian/Alaskan Native, Asian, Black or African American, Native Hawaiian/Pacific Islander, White, unknown, or other (free text), and the following options for ethnicity: Hispanic/Latina, non-Hispanic/Latina, or unknown/not applicable. Exclusion criteria included premature birth (<37 wk’ gestation), neonatal intensive care unit admission for more than 7 d, birth weight less than 2,000 g, or evidence of brain injury on MRI. Anatomic MR images were reviewed by a neuroradiologist (J.S.S.) and pediatric neurologist (C.D.S.) and subjects were excluded for brain injury. After exclusion and data quality criteria were applied, 261 mother–infant dyads were included in the current analysis (see fMRI sample below). Study procedures were reviewed and approved by the Washington University Institutional Review Board. Written informed consent was obtained for each participant, with written parental informed consent for each infant.

CUDDEL.

This study was approved by the Human Studies Committees at Washington University in St. Louis, and informed consent was obtained from a parent of all participants. Neonatal participants were recruited as a part of the parent study CUDDEL. Pregnant mothers were recruited and enrolled between the 2nd to 3rd trimesters. The current study focused on neonatal MRI performed shortly after birth. Inclusion criteria for the current study included speaking English, mother age 18 y or older, and cannabis use at least once during the subject’s lifetime. Exclusion criteria included women with alcohol or other substance abuse (cannabis use was not excluded), premature birth (<37 wk’ gestation), neonatal intensive care unit admission for more than 7 d, birth weight less than 2,000 g, or evidence of brain injury on MRI. Both cases (infants born to women that indicated prenatal cannabis use) and controls (infants born to women that indicated no prenatal cannabis use) were included. Participants answered questions from the Cannabis Use Disorder Identification Test–Revised (47) that asks about frequency of cannabis use with a Likert scale: never (0), monthly or less (1), 2 to 4 times a month (2), 2 to 3 times a week (3), 4+ times a week (4). Cases reported continuous cannabis use for all three trimesters (Trimester 1: mean = 1.06, SD = 1.35; Trimester 2: mean = 0.93, SD = 1.32; Trimester 3: mean = 0.78, SD = 1.25). Controls stopped cannabis use once pregnant (confirmed with urine drug screen). Anatomic MR images were reviewed by a neuroradiologist (J.S.S.) and pediatric neurologist (C.D.S.) and subjects were excluded for brain injury. Collection of MRI data for this study is ongoing. Of the 175 participants that have been scanned for CUDDEL, 151 were included in the current analyses after exclusion and data quality criteria were applied as in eLABE. A subset of this sample has been previously reported on in Nielsen et al. 2023 (14).

Measures.

Components and generation of latent factors for prenatal social disadvantage and psychosocial stress.

Previous work employed a confirmatory factor analysis to derive two latent prenatal social disadvantage and psychosocial stress constructs to predict birthweight and gestational age at birth (23). The following maternal measures were included in the social disadvantage variable: health insurance status (grouped by private insurance or public or no insurance), highest educational level (less than high school, high school graduate, college graduate, postgraduate), Income-to-Needs Ratio (25) in each trimester, national Area Deprivation Index percentile at birth (24), and the Healthy Eating Index (26). The Healthy Eating Index assesses whether maternal diet aligns with the Dietary Guidelines for Americans, asking about the frequency of consumption of different food groups to ascertain the adequacy of nutrients (fruits, vegetables, grains, dairy, proteins, fats) and moderation of other components (added sugars, sodium, saturated fats). Socioeconomic factors are frequently associated with diet quality in the US (48) which we used as rationale for inclusion in the latent factor social disadvantage. The following maternal measures were included in the psychosocial stress variable: Perceived stress scale (49) and Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale (50) in each trimester, Stress and Adversity Inventory (STRAIN) (51), and Everyday Discrimination Scale (52). Both the severity and number of endorsed adverse experiences on the STRAIN were included as separate components.

Complete details of the construction and validation of the structural equation modeling (SEM) used to generate the two latent factors for social disadvantage and psychosocial stress are provided in Luby et al. (23). Briefly, these factors were derived in the full sample (n = 399) using a confirmatory factor analysis to predict gestational age at birth using SEM (SI Appendix, Fig. S1). Models were estimated using the maximum likelihood method with robust SE which allows for estimation in the setting of missing data.

Unfortunately, these latent factors cannot be generated separately in the training and testing sets avoiding all risk of data leakage (53), because different patterns of missingness would invalidate the interpretation of the generated factors. Nevertheless, we generated the social disadvantage latent factor for each fold of 10-fold cross-validation excluding the individuals in the testing sample (n = 26) and found that these estimates were nearly perfectly correlated with those generated from the full sample (min R = 0.9982, max R = 0.9991). Thus, the potential for inflation of the estimation of social disadvantage from FC due to data leakage resulting from the inclusion of the test individuals in the generation of the social disadvantage factor is likely small.

Maternal comorbidities and exposures.

A maternal medical risk score was calculated for each participant using questionnaires and medical record review (54) as a sum of weighted comorbidities, including advanced maternal age, cardiac disease, and preeclampsia, with higher scores predicting increased risk of severe morbidity or mortality and poor infant health. Frequency of tobacco and marijuana use (none, some, or heavy) was self-reported on questionnaires at each trimester. At the discretion of the treating clinician, a subset of mothers underwent urine drug screens during prenatal clinical care. Marijuana exposure (any vs. none) was, therefore, based on self-report and/or a urine drug screen result positive for tetrahydrocannabinol metabolites. Because marijuana exposure and tobacco use are not included in the maternal medical risk index, they were independently evaluated as covariates of interest. Covariates were selected to align with those used by Triplett et al. (4).

Neuroimaging acquisition.

Sequences and acquisition protocols have been previously reported in Nielsen et al. (14) and Sylvester et al. (13) and a brief description is provided below. Both the eLABE and CUDDEL samples were imaged using the same procedures and same MRI sequences. After feeding, the infant was swaddled and positioned in a head-stabilizing vacuum fix wrap. A nurse familiar with neonate transport and resuscitation was present at all MRI scans. Heart rate and blood oxygenation were measured continuously throughout all scans, and infants were monitored visually via video. Based on visual monitoring through a camera, infants slept through scans as indicated by eye closure and minimal movements. Imaging was performed without sedating medications using a Siemens 3T Prisma scanner and 64-channel head coil. A T2-weighted image (sagittal, 208 slices, 0.8 mm isotropic resolution, TE = 563 ms, TR = 4,500/3,200 ms for eLABE) was collected. For the resting state fMRI, functional imaging was performed using a blood-oxygen-level-dependent (BOLD) gradient-recalled echo-planar multiband (MB) sequence (72 slices, 2.0 mm isotropic resolution, TE = 37 ms, TR = 800 ms, MB factor = 8). Spin-echo field maps were obtained [at least one anterior–posterior (AP) and one posterior-anterior (PA)] during each session with the same parameters. Between two and nine fMRI BOLD scans were acquired, depending on how the infant tolerated the scan (mean 3.75 runs). Scans were collected in both the AP and PA direction; a typical scan session included two AP runs and two PA runs. The scans were 420 frames, which is 5.6 min in length.

fMRI sample.

Nearly all of the 399 infants in the eLABE sample were imaged (n = 385). MRI scans were not performed for 14 neonates due to the COVID-19 pandemic (March 2020). fMRI data were acquired for 350/385 neonates. A total of 66 infants were excluded due to premature birth (gestational age <37 wk, n = 45), high-grade brain injury (n = 13), neonatal intensive care unit admission >7 d (n = 32), low birth weight (<2,000 g n = 1), and identification of congenital anomaly in infancy (n = 1). Of the remaining 284 neonates, 23 infants had fewer than 10 min of low motion fMRI data, leaving a sample of 261 neonates. We report the framewise displacement in mm (mean = 0.19; SD = 0.16; range = 0.04 to 1.2) and the amount of retained low-motion data in minutes (mean = 16.6; SD = 4.4; range = 10.1 to 41.9) for the included sample.

Image preprocessing and FC processing.

The processing pipelines utilized here have been previously reported in Nielsen et al. (14) and Sylvester et al. (13) and a brief description is provided below. Both the eLABE and CUDDEL samples were processed in an identical manner to limit potential differences due to analysis across cohorts, incorporating surface-based strategies to account for individual and developmental differences in cortical folding (55). fMRI preprocessing included correction of intensity differences attributable to interleaved acquisition, bias field correction, intensity normalization of each run to a whole brain mode value of 1,000, linear realignment within and across runs to compensate for rigid body motion, and linear registration of BOLD images to the adult Talairach isotropic atlas. Neonates were registered: BOLD to individual T2 to group-average T2 from the eLABE cohort to 711-2N Talairach atlas. Field distortion correction was performed, using the FSL TOPUP toolbox (http://fsl.fmrib.ox.ac.uk/fsl/fslwiki/TOPUP) and applied with FSL’s applytopup.

Following initial processing, fMRI data were censored at FD > 0.25 mm, with the additional restriction that only epochs of at least three consecutive frames FD < 0.25 mm were included. This FD threshold was selected after taking into account the smaller radius of infants’ heads (56) and reviewing motion traces in multiple subjects (57). In order to be included in the study, a minimum of 10 min (750 frames) of data were required. We calculated the number of frames retained after censoring and the average FD across all frames (precensoring) for each individual subject and evaluated a covariate of interest.

Next, we used the Melbourne Children’s Regional Brain Atlases [MCRIB (55)] as a surface-based neonatal parcellation approach to generate surfaces for each subject. Volumetric resting-state BOLD timeseries were mapped to subject-specific surfaces using established procedures adapted from the Human Connectome Project as implemented in Connectome Workbench 1.2.3. MCRIB was also used to parcellate the subcortex for each subject to generate the volumetric resting-state BOLD timeseries for left and right amygdala, hippocampus, putamen, pallidum, caudate, thalamus, and cerebellum.

After mapping to the surface, each dataset underwent rs-fc preprocessing. In the initial iteration, the data were processed with the following steps: a) demean and detrend within run, band-pass filtering (0.005 Hz < f < 0.1), interpolation of censored frames, b) multiple regression with nuisance timeseries including white matter, ventricles, extra-axial cerebrospinal fluid, and whole brain, as well as 24-parameter Friston expansion regressors derived from head motion. fMRI data were aligned across subjects into the “fs_LR32k” surface space using spherical registration. Time courses for surface data were smoothed with geodesic 2D Gaussian kernels (σ = 2.25 mm).

FC network construction.

For each participant, whole-brain FC was generated by extracting the timeseries from 333 previously defined surface parcels (58) (Fig. 1) and 14 subcortical regions and calculating the Fisher z-transformed Pearson correlation between each pair of parcels. For each individual, this yields a 347 × 347 correlation matrix. We used definitions of functional networks derived in healthy adults (58) that include processing networks like the auditory, visual, somatomotor-hand, somatomotor-mouth, retrosplenial, and control and other association networks like the frontoparietal, cingulo-opercular, dorsal attention, ventral attention, salience, default mode, cingulo-parietal.

Multivariate Machine Learning Procedures.

In alignment with the procedures reported in Nielsen et al. (14), SVR with a linear kernel was implemented to identify multivariate patterns of FC that carry information about prenatal exposure to social disadvantage. SVR is a sensitive and powerful tool to extract the multivariate relationship between a set of many features (here, functional connections) and labels (here, prenatal social disadvantage) in a training sample which can subsequently be applied to make predictions about unseen test individuals. Ten-fold cross-validation was used to identify patterns of FC in the training set (i.e., 90% of the eLABE sample) and test whether these patterns could accurately discriminate differences in social disadvantage in the remaining individuals in the test set (i.e., 10% of the eLABE sample). To minimize the impact of confounding covariates with the potential to influence FC or the relationship between social disadvantage and FC, we controlled for PMA at scan, sex, amount of data, mean FD (prescrubbing), maternal medical risk, tobacco use, and marijuana use. Within each fold of 10-fold cross-validation, linear models were fit to best capture the relationship between the regressors and FC from each functional connection (60,031) and the relationship between the regressor and social disadvantage, separately in both the training set and testing set. The residuals from these models were then used as the features and labels for training and testing with SVR. Ten-fold cross-validation was repeated with 100 random partitions of the dataset into training and test sets to ensure that performance was not due to serendipitous random seeding (SI Appendix, Fig. S7). Deidentified data used for training (FC matrices, social disadvantage, covariates) are publicly available (59).

Validation in the CUDDEL Sample.

Predictive approaches like SVR are designed to optimize prediction and could potentially identify multivariate patterns of FC that do not generalize to describe the impact of early adversity outside of the original training sample (27). Cross-validation alone is not a sufficient test of the robustness of data-driven, multivariate predictive models (60). Because the CUDDEL study did not collect all measures used in the eLABE sample, training to identify and evaluate patterns of FC related to prenatal social disadvantage was slightly modified. First, because the CUDDEL sample did not incorporate some of the measures used as covariates (e.g., maternal medical risk), the SVR model was generated without regressing covariates. Second, the CUDDEL sample did not acquire all measures that comprise the latent factor composite social disadvantage variable. Thus, only the Income-to-Needs Ratio was used to evaluate whether estimates of social disadvantage generated from infant FC were related to true exposure to social disadvantage. For each fold of cross-validation, the multivariate patterns of FC identified in the eLABE sample were also applied to estimate the social disadvantage of the individuals in the validation sample (CUDDEL). This generates a single estimate of social disadvantage based on FC for each individual in the eLABE sample and 10 estimates of social disadvantage based on FC (i.e., for each fold of 10-fold cross-validation) for each individual in the validation sample. Performance was quantified by calculating the correlation between the estimated social disadvantage and actual Income-to-Needs Ratio (i.e., R2) in the eLABE and validation samples separately. To evaluate whether this performance was better than expected by chance, null models were generated by identifying patterns of FC related to social disadvantage that was permuted before training in the eLABE sample. Estimates of social disadvantage based on permuted data were then correlated with prenatal Income-to-Needs. This process was repeated 500 times and the observed correlation between estimates of social disadvantage based on FC and prenatal Income-to-Needs was compared to the distribution of correlations generated with null models to produce a P-value.

Because the Income-to-Needs Ratio was acquired in both samples, a separate validation of the patterns of infant FC related to Income-to-Needs was also conducted. For each fold of cross-validation, the multivariate patterns of FC identified in the eLABE sample were also applied to estimate the prenatal Income-to-Needs Ratio of the individuals in the validation sample (CUDDEL). This generates a single estimate of Income-to-Needs Ratio based on FC for each individual in the eLABE sample and 10 estimates of the Income-to-Needs Ratio based on FC (i.e., for each fold of 10-fold cross-validation) for each individual in the validation sample. Performance was quantified by calculating the correlation between the estimated and actual Income-to-Needs Ratio (i.e., R2) in the eLABE and validation samples separately.

Detection of Social Disadvantage with FC from Individual Functional Networks.

Feature selection (i.e., restricting the number of features used for training), as detailed in Nielsen et al. (14), was used to test the extent to which the FC from a single network might capture the variance in FC related to social disadvantage. For each network, all connections associated with parcels from a single functional network (e.g., within the FP and between the FP and the rest of the brain) were subselected and then only those features were used to detect evidence of prenatal exposure to social disadvantage. False discovery rate was used to correct for the multiple comparisons (12 networks).

To further disentangle the relationship between evidence of prenatal exposure to social disadvantage, network size, and network identity, we compared the detection of social disadvantage from FC when the same number of parcels were selected from each functional network. A number of parcels was randomly subselected from a single functional network, from a single parcel to the maximum number of parcels in a network (e.g., for DMN = 41). Only the FC from the connections associated with those sampled parcels was then used as features for estimation of social disadvantage from FC (number of features: 333-13,653). This process was repeated 25 times for each network and each parcel number. The average variance explained as a function of sampled parcel number was generated for each functional network.

Detection of Social Disadvantage with Permuted FC from Individual Networks and Subcortical Structures.

Feature permutation (i.e., manipulating a restricted set of features used for testing), as detailed in Nielsen et al. (14), was used to investigate whether a given set of features was necessary to capture the brain-wide patterns of FC that facilitate the detection of differences in prenatal exposure to social disadvantage. Permutation sensitivity analysis disrupts the true relationship between social disadvantage and FC present in a limited set of features by permuting these features across the subjects in the test set. The model trained to detect social disadvantage using all the features is then applied to the test set containing permuted features. Permuting these features will negatively impact the estimation of social disadvantage with brain-wide FC if a set of features is uniquely important for detection of social disadvantage. This approach was applied for functional networks and subcortical structures as described below.

Networks.

For each network, all connections associated with parcels from a single functional network (e.g., within the CO and between the CO and the rest of the brain) were subselected and these features were then permuted across subjects in the test set as described above. To ensure that the impact of permuting the FC of a given network was due to network identity and not network size, the impact of permuting a matched set of randomly selected connections was also examined. For each network, we generated a matched set of functional connections randomly selected from the matrix and these features were then permuted across subjects in the test set. Fifty matched randomly selected feature sets were generated for each network. P-values were calculated for each network by comparing the detection of social disadvantage (R2) when features from a single network were permuted and when randomly selected features were permuted. False discovery rate was used to correct for the multiple comparisons (12 networks).

Subcortical structures.

The connections associated with a single subcortical structure (left and right) were also selected and these features were then permuted in the test set as described above. The impact of permuting a matched set of randomly selected connections was also examined. One thousand matched randomly selected feature sets were generated as the null for all subcortical structures. P-values were calculated for each subcortical structure by comparing the detection of social disadvantage (R2) when features from a single subcortical structure are permuted and when randomly selected features are permuted. False discovery rate was used to correct for the multiple comparisons (seven subcortical structures).

Benchmarking Variation in Infant FC Related Social Disadvantage.

Individual components of social disadvantage.

SVR was used to determine the extent to which multivariate patterns of infant FC carried information about the individual components of the Social Disadvantage latent factor in isolation. SVR was used for the continuous variables (Income-to-Needs, Area Deprivation Index, Healthy Eating Index) and support vector classification as used for the binary and categorical variables (Insurance: public vs. private; Education: less than college graduate vs. college graduate or more). Regression models were generated with and without the covariates used above (PMA at scan, sex, amount of data, mean FD (prescrubbing), maternal medical risk, tobacco use, and marijuana use).

Composite of social disadvantage.

SVR was used to build additional models to identify patterns of FC related to equally weighted composites of the components of the latent factor of social disadvantage. Using only the subjects that did not have any missing data, a composite of social disadvantage was created using all components (n = 184) and another composite was created excluding the Healthy Eating Index (n = 223). To generate a composite, the components were z-scored and averaged together. To avoid potential leakage across the training and test sets, the individuals in the test set were excluded to calculate the mean and SD used to z-score the components. Regression models were generated with and without the covariates used above (PMA at scan, sex, amount of data, mean FD (prescrubbing), maternal medical risk, tobacco use, and marijuana use).

Brain maturity vs. social disadvantage.

Because an infant’s age at scan has previously been shown to be related to infant FC, further analyses were conducted to demonstrate that differences in brain maturity alone did not yield patterns of FC that can estimate social disadvantage. First, we built models to estimate measures of brain maturity (PMA at scan, gestational age at birth, chronological age since birth) using SVR in this sample for context.

Though the models that identify patterns of FC related to social disadvantage above control for linear relationships with PMA at scan, it is possible that nonlinear or more complex relationships with brain maturity could inflate performance. Thus, we next expanded the maturity covariates (gestational age at birth, chronological age since birth, PMA at scan, gestational age at birth^2, chronological age since birth^2, PMA at scan^) to account for these potential relationships and examine the effect on estimation of social disadvantage from infant FC.

Because greater social disadvantage was associated with earlier delivery, a younger gestational age at birth, and a younger PMA at scan (SI Appendix, Fig. S4A), we also determined whether social disadvantage could be estimated from infant FC in an age-restricted sample that only included infants born between 39 to 40 wk gestation (n = 152). As above, we used SVR to identify patterns of FC related to prenatal social disadvantage.

Last, permutation sensitivity analyses were performed at the level of parcels for both the detection of social disadvantage and PMA at scan from variation in infant FC. As above, the connections associated with a single parcel (analogous to a seed map) were selected and these features were permuted across subjects in the test set, removing the information related to either social disadvantage or PMA at Scan. Five hundred matched randomly selected features were generated as the null for all parcels. P-values were calculated for each parcel separately for social disadvantage and PMA at scan by comparing the variance explained when features from a single parcel are permuted and when randomly selected features are permuted. False discovery rate was used to correct for the multiple comparisons. The distribution of parcels important to detect social disadvantage and PMA at scan from FC and their overlap are depicted in SI Appendix, Fig. S5.

Maternal psychosocial stress vs. social disadvantage.

Maternal psychosocial stress is potentially related to social disadvantage and/or infant FC. Thus, we examined how this variable contributed to the observed detection of prenatal social disadvantage from infant FC. SVR was used to determine the extent to which multivariate patterns of FC carried information about maternal psychosocial stress.
