Promoting physical activity at Danish schools targeting youth who are not in employment, education or training (NEET): a qualitative study
Authors: Johanne Triantafyllou Lorenzen, Sofie Rolff Carlsen, Charlotte Demant Klinker, Julie Hellesøe Christensen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPhysical activity is associated with both physical and mental health, but many youths do not meet physical activity recommendations. Particularly, youth who are Not in Employment, Education or Training (NEET) are less physically active than their peers. In Denmark, the “Forberedende Grunduddannelse” (FGU; Preparatory Basic Education and Training) programme was established in 2019 to prepare youth who are NEET for secondary education or employment. While schools are generally considered ideal for physical activity promotion, knowledge about physical activity promotion at schools targeting youth who are NEET is limited. In this study, we investigated how schools targeting youth who are NEET can support students in being physically active. Specifically, we identified contextual influences on physical activity promotion and intervention- and implementation strategies used by schools to promote physical activity.MethodsWe conducted qualitative, semi-structured interviews with three school leaders and focus groups with 18 teachers and 41 students from three FGU schools in Denmark. The data was analysed using reflexive thematic analysis.ResultsWe developed five themes: 1) Accommodating diverse student needs to increase inclusion; 2) Utilising physical activity to achieve the schools’ core purpose; 3) Building organisational structures that support physical activity; 4) Improving accessibility of physical activity facilities; and 5) Prioritising physical activity given the legal framework. Our findings suggest that physical activity promotion aligns with these schools’ core purpose and that schools can accommodate students’ diverse needs and resources by emphasising inclusivity and flexibility in physical activities. Physical activity promotion is, however, poorly supported by national legal regulations and schools’ organisational and physical environments challenge implementation of activities into daily practices.ConclusionsWhile some of the results echo those of general educational settings, they also suggest that school-based physical activity programmes should be tailored to the specific characteristics of this youth group and the organisational structures of the schools to succeed. Our findings may be used to inform future school-based physical activity programmes targeting youth who are NEET.Keywords: Health promoting schools, Health promotion, Inequalities, Qualitative methods, Young adults

Background

Physical activity (PA) is important for both physical and psychosocial health among youth. In the short term, PA affects self-esteem, cognitive- and academic performance, as well as risk of anxiety and depression [1], while, in the longer term, PA affects the risk of type 2 diabetes, cardiovascular disease and certain types of cancer [2]. Yet, many youth do not meet current recommendations for PA, and participation in PA generally declines with age [3, 4]. In particular, youth who are Not in Employment, Education, or Training (NEET) tend to be less physically active and less likely to participate in sports than other youths [5, 6]. In 2023, youth who are NEET made up 11.2% of 15–29 year olds in the EU [7], and in addition to being less physically active, this group also has a higher risk of poor self-rated health [8], mental illness and being over- or underweight [6] compared to youth in employment or education. The correlation between NEET status and health is bidirectional, as poor health increases the risk of being NEET [9], while being outside of education and the labour market has detrimental effects on health [10]. Therefore, inclusion of youth who are NEET in the educational system as well as in health promotion initiatives, including PA promotion initiatives, can positively influence their health and well-being and possibly support the transition into education or labour.

Initiatives aimed at engaging youth who are NEET in education and/or the labour market vary internationally and include, for instance, job search assistance, apprenticeships and vocational education, and training to improve skills and qualifications [11]. In Denmark, the “Forberedende Grunduddannelse” (FGU; Preparatory Basic Education and Training) programme was introduced in 2019 to offer a flexible and individually tailored programme for youth who are NEET. The FGU programme provides a comprehensive approach to student skill development, emphasising the importance of improving both academic, social and personal skills. FGU schools should provide an inclusive environment with a combination of academic and vocational teaching, work placement and counselling [12]. These characteristics resemble international programmes for youth who are NEET, which often combine multiple elements such as education, work placement, advice and support [13]. The FGU consolidation act describes a set of ‘didactical principles’ for teaching, including a principle about health promotion, which states that health promotion, such as PA initiatives, must be integrated into the school day, e.g., offering opportunities for PA as part of school lessons, breaks and/or as after-school activities [12, 14]. However, a recent report about health and well-being of students at FGU schools showed that 60% do not meet the WHO recommendations for PA, 39% are rarely or never physically active during school hours, and 33% primarily engage in sedentary activities during their spare time [15]. These numbers are notably higher compared to students attending upper secondary or vocational education [16, 17]. For example, 30% of vocational education students do not meet the WHO recommendations [16]. The findings indicate that FGU schools have not yet been successful with implementing PA initiatives and improving students’ PA as prescribed by the didactical principle and highlight the importance of increasing PA in this particular youth group to achieve the immediate and long-term benefits of PA.

Educational settings are generally viewed as ideal for health promotion, including PA promotion, due to their ability to reach a large number of youths from diverse social backgrounds [18]. In particular, school-based PA interventions based on whole-school approaches, such as the Health Promoting School (HPS) framework, have shown promise in promoting PA [19]. The HPS framework, developed by the WHO in the 1980 s, describes a comprehensive, whole-school approach to health promotion by integrating health promotion into the entire ‘school community’ [20, 21]. Thereby, all (organisational) levels of a school, including school policies, curriculum, the social and physical environment as well as engagement with the surrounding community and health services, are relevant to promoting student health [21]. Although whole-school approaches have shown improvement in students’ PA, implementing school-based PA interventions and making lasting changes remains a challenge [22], stressing the need to better understand “the many factors that could be modified to advance the implementation experience” [23]. Consequently, studies have identified several influences on implementation of school-based PA, including the school’s physical environment and facilities [24–26], resources in terms of economy and time (e.g., for preparation), opportunities for competence development [24, 25, 27, 28], priority and time for PA in the curriculum [26, 29], support from school leaders [27], knowledge, motivation and self-efficacy among teachers [25, 28, 29] and students’ response to PA (e.g., motivation and enjoyment of PA) [24]. However, because most existing research has focused on primary and secondary schools, it is unclear whether these findings apply to schools targeting youth who are NEET, such as FGU schools in Denmark and similar educational settings internationally. Therefore, the current study explored how schools can promote PA among youth who were recently NEET by answering the following research question:

How can schools targeting youth who are NEET support their students in being physically active during school hours?

The research question was addressed by exploring contextual influences on PA promotion in the FGU school setting and current intervention- and implementation strategies used by schools to promote PA. We included perspectives of school leaders, teachers, and the students themselves. Based on the analysis, we discuss implications for PA promotion in educational settings aimed at youth who are NEET.

Methods

Setting

The Danish FGU programme is a flexible educational programme targeting youth (aged 15–25 years) who are NEET, which aims to provide students with sufficient personal, social, and academic skills to enter upper secondary education or obtain unskilled employment [12]. The programme offers three educational tracks: an academic track that prepares students for general upper secondary education; a vocational track that prepares students for vocational education; and an occupational track that provides students with work experience and prepares them for unskilled employment. Youth are referred to the FGU programme by their local municipality, which – in collaboration with the young person – is also responsible for preparing an individually tailored educational plan specifying the student’s goals with attending FGU and determining educational track and duration of the programme, which typically varies between six months and two years [12]. In 2024, there were 27 institutions offering the FGU programme, covering a total of 85 individual schools [30], and the total student population was approx. 9,000 [31].

Study design and participants

We conducted a qualitative interview study among school leaders, and teachers and other pedagogical staff (hereafter referred to collectively as ‘teachers’) and students to gain an in-depth understanding of the FGU school setting in relation to PA promotion. Participants were recruited from three FGU schools (schools A, B and C) that participate in the Move FGU intervention, and the data collected was, therefore, also used to inform the intervention development. Schools A and C were relatively small with student populations between 100 and 150, while School B was a larger school with approx. 225 students. The schools were located in three municipalities across Zealand, Denmark.

Data were collected through semi-structured interviews and focus group interviews. The leader of each school was invited for an individual interview, while teachers and students were recruited for focus groups in collaboration with the school leaders. Focus groups were chosen for teachers and students as this is a well-established qualitative method for collecting in-depth data within health research, which especially among youth can foster a safe environment for expressing experiences and opinions freely [32]. There were no specific inclusion criteria for teachers and students; however, we encouraged the schools to recruit a heterogeneous group of teachers and students with regards to educational tracks, experience, motivation, and interest in PA. Data collection

We developed separate interview guides for school leaders, teachers, and students. The interview guides were developed based on the WHO HPS framework [21] to ensure that they covered important contextual influences and strategies for PA promotion in the FGU school setting. More specifically, the guides addressed the eight ‘HPS standards’ described by the WHO: 1) Government policies and resources; 2) School policies and resources; 3) School governance and leadership; 4) School and community partnerships; 5) School curriculum; 6) School social-emotional environment; 7) School physical environment; and 8) School health services [21]. The weighing of the standards depended on the interviewees’ position, for example, interview guides for school leaders particularly inquired about governance, while student interview guides focused more on daily school life and activities. Student focus groups included an exercise with individual reflection time followed by facilitated sharing, grouping and discussion of influences on PA during school hours, to make focus groups more engaging and support all participants in sharing their experiences. Data collection took place in April-June 2024. All interviews and focus groups were conducted at the schools during school/work hours and teachers and students were given time off to participate. Interviews with school leaders lasted in average 34 minutes (range 25–42 min.), teacher focus groups 49 minutes (range 45–51) and student focus groups 39 minutes (range 27–53 min.). All interviews and focus groups were conducted by JTL and JHC, audio-recorded and transcribed verbatim.

Analytical framework and approach

Data were analysed using reflexive thematic analysis inspired by the analytical steps described by Braun and Clarke [33, 34]. First, to become familiar with data and generate preliminary ideas for analysis, all transcripts were read thoroughly by JTL and JHC. Then, the same two authors coded data based on whether it could be considered (a) a contextual influence affecting PA in the FGU school setting, or (b) a strategy employed by schools to promote PA.

In the coding of contextual influences on PA promotion we applied a deductive approach, drawing on Nilsen and Bernhardsson’s 12 context dimensions, identified in a review of frameworks describing contextual determinants for implementation [35]. Thus, codes were categorised according to these context dimensions, which are placed under three levels of aggregation: (1) micro: characteristics related to the individuals intended to benefit from PA promotion, in this case, students attending FGU schools; (2) meso: characteristics related to the FGU schools, including organisational culture and climate, readiness to change, support, and structures; and (3) macro: characteristics related to the wider environment ‘beyond’ the schools, including policies, guidelines, and legislation. Some dimensions, including social relations and support, financial resources, leadership, time availability, feedback, and physical environment, may be placed under multiple levels of aggregation [35]. Categories that pertained to these dimensions were placed under the micro, meso or macro level according to respondents’ own framing. For example, if the physical environment, such as the school’s facilities, was pointed to as a contextual influence on PA promotion, it was placed under the meso level, as this is a characteristic related to the school.

In the coding of strategies used by the schools to promote PA, we applied an inductive, data-driven approach, which allowed us to be explorative in relation to current school practices and considerations related to PA promotion. Although we did not investigate the implementation of a predefined school-based PA programme, we were inspired by the concepts of intervention- and implementation strategies. We used intervention strategies to denote specific activities employed by schools to increase students’ PA [36], e.g., walk and talks or regular yoga sessions. We used implementation strategies to describe the methods and approaches used by schools to enhance adoption, implementation, and sustainment of these activities [37], e.g., increasing student acceptance towards PA promoting activities by gradually increasing the complexity of yoga exercises. In the analyses, we refer collectively to intervention- and implementation strategies as ‘strategies’, which encompass any approach that is actively used to enhance implementation of a school-based PA-related activity. Following the initial coding, similar codes were grouped into categories that captured common overarching strategies (e.g., ‘safe and inclusive physical activities’).

The coding and categorisation of all relevant data as either a contextual influence or strategy was followed by the development of interpretative themes to answer the research question. Themes resonated across the empirical material, i.e., they encompassed categories related to both contextual influences and strategies. Finally, these themes were reviewed and refined based on discussions between all authors and supplemented by illustrative quotes [34, 38]. The thorough analytical process and its reporting, in which we have made the analytical steps and categorisations transparent and presented illustrative excerpts from diverse interview participants, strengthen the trustworthiness of the analysis [39]. Preliminary findings and interpretations were regularly discussed in the author group to support reflexivity and creativity in the analytical process, and the final themes and interpretations were considered in relation to the empirical material [33].

Ethics

The study was reviewed and approved by the Capital Region of Denmark’s legal centre for personal data handling (journal number P-2023–14520). All data were processed and stored in accordance with the EU General Data Protection Regulation and national Danish data protection legislation. The study did not require approval from the Committees on Health Research Ethics for the Capital Region of Denmark (reference number F-23060092), as this is not mandated for social health science research in Denmark. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. For students, the consent forms were read aloud and explained thoroughly to support their informed choice to participate. After the consent forms were explained, three students chose not to participate in the study and, therefore, returned to their class.

Results

The analysis resulted in the development of five themes, pointing to how schools can support students’ PA in the FGU school setting: 1) Accommodating diverse student needs to increase inclusion; 2) Utilising PA to achieve the schools’ core purpose; 3) Building organisational structures that support PA; 4) Improving accessibility of PA facilities and 5) Prioritising PA promotion given the legal framework. Theme 1: accommodating diverse student needs to increase inclusion

At the micro level, we found that characteristics related to the specific student group attending FGU schools were an important contextual influence on how schools approached PA promotion. According to school leaders and teachers, many students were referred to FGU because they experienced psychosocial challenges, such as ADHD and anxiety, which also affected their participation in physical activities:

“We have a very large overrepresentation of vulnerable students (…). Some are used to being active, to cycle a lot or whatever they do. They will easily be able to participate in [physical activities]. But we have a lot who are challenged by their diagnoses in one way or another.” (leader, school A).

For these reasons, some students found it difficult and overwhelming to participate in physical activities in larger groups. A school leader explained how most students willingly participated in activities conducted “in smaller groups with a teacher [they] feel safe around”, but if teachers planned an activity, which required that they “need to get eighty people into a bus, then (…) it becomes difficult” (school A). Teachers also noted the challenge of planning physical activities as some students felt uncomfortable and anxious if their schedule changed and they did not know their programme in advance. Moreover, in terms of both current habits and past experiences with PA, a substantial proportion of students were described as ‘out of shape’ and ‘unfamiliar’ with PA, which resulted in lack of motivation and a general reluctancy to participate in PA. A teacher described students as “very estranged towards physical activity” and explained that “the idea of walking, let’s say, just two kilometres is a giant leap for them” (school C). This was further elaborated by a student who explained that school break activities consisted of “going to [shop name] and buying snacks. (…) That’s the only thing that happens. Otherwise, people just sit and wait. We’re not active at all in this place.” (school A). According to school leaders and teachers, the reluctancy to participate in PA was for many students caused by prior negative experiences, for example from primary school.

Schools employed different strategies to accommodate students’ diverse psychosocial needs, resources and familiarity with PA. For instance, all activities were carefully planned, included different options for participation, and programmes were communicated to students plenty of time in advance:

“(…) when we have organised these big activity days, we have actually made an effort to announce them in advance and explain what was expected (…), and then we have tried to plan it so that there were different ways of participating. So, there were those [students] who could go all out (…), but it was also possible to participate without having to compete with someone and without having to talk to someone you didn’t know.” (teacher, school A).

Other strategies included creating recognizability and slow progression in activities, e.g., by creating a routine that students became familiar with. A teacher described how she had introduced her students to yoga by beginning with “doing it once a week (…) and [starting] with chair-yoga” and now, as they were “approaching the end of the year, we are actually lying on yoga mats and doing half an hour of yoga every morning in the classroom and almost everyone participates.” (school C).

Overall, the characteristics of the student group attending FGU had a profound influence on how schools approached PA promotion. The schools employed deliberate and tailored strategies to adjust activities to the specific student group and ensure inclusion of as many students as possible.

Theme 2: utilising physical activity to achieve the schools’ core purpose

Related to the macro level of context, we found that across all schools, leaders and teachers considered PA promotion to contribute to achieving the overall purpose of FGU as an educational programme, i.e., to prepare students for further secondary education or unskilled employment. A school leader elaborated:

“I simply believe that [PA] can help give them some self-esteem and some joy and inner motivation (…). I just think that they would get a much better life and much more energy and confidence in the fact that they can actually get to a place where life is better than being on early retirement or having a lonely life or something.” (school C).

Accordingly, PA was viewed as a potentially important tool in building students’ self-competence and motivation and, thereby, improving their chances of successfully moving on from FGU. This underlines the holistic understanding of student health, well-being and academic performance, which was emphasised by leaders and teachers across all schools and, as a result, experienced by students: “Here, there is a bit more consideration of the whole person, and of course we have some challenges, you carry a heavy load, so both you as a person and your education are taken care of” (student, school B). A narrow focus on academic performance was not considered beneficial, as personal development and well-being, including physical health and -activity, was seen as equally important to prepare students for life after FGU. This was considered particularly important because many students came from somehow disadvantaged backgrounds, e.g., low income or newly arrived refugee families. School leaders therefore believed that the schools had “a role in presenting [students] with how to obtain a good life” (school A) with the hope that positive experiences from their school time, e.g., with being physically active, would “settle within” the students and support their future well-being.

The holistic approach to student health, well-being and learning was further reflected in the schools’ strategies for promoting PA. Physical activities were often used to facilitate other outcomes, such as strengthening students’ social skills and relationships and creating a sense of belonging to the school. Across all schools, leaders and teachers explained how the social and community aspects of school life were particularly important for the student group at FGU schools and that physical activities, therefore, also had a social purpose. A teacher explained that PA was used to “create a forum where [students] are part of something (…) and feel how nice it is to belong to a community” and how, in that sense, “physical activity can be a third place (…), which is different from what they usually gather around” (school A). A student similarly considered PA a mean for social interaction:

“That’s why I think [physical] activities are good. Because that’s how we bond with each other. Community, of course. So, that’s it. We need it.” (school A).

In sum, PA was considered a tool for increasing students’ self-confidence, motivation and social relations in the short term, while potentially also increasing students’ future engagement in PA in the longer term. PA promotion thus aligned well with the schools’ core purpose of building students’ personal, social and academic skills and, thereby, preparing them for either entering secondary education or obtaining employment, and navigating everyday life.

Theme 3: building organisational structures that support physical activity

At the meso level, we found the organisational structures of the schools to be an important contextual influence, which affected opportunities for PA promotion in everyday school life. Due to the different aims of the three educational tracks at FGU, the content of the school day varied significantly between tracks. This resulted in a mainly sedentary school day for students attending the academic track and a more active and physically demanding school day for students attending the vocational and occupational tracks, as explained by some students:

Researcher: “Are there times during the school day where you are mostly sedentary?” Student 1 and 2: “Most of the day.” Student 3: “That’s definitely the case in the IT class [academic track]. (…) I think the construction class [vocational track] are a bit more on their feet, at least I hope so.” Student 1 [directed at a student from the construction class]: “You guys are always out working.” (school C).

Some teachers considered the different PA levels across educational tracks to result in “(…) huge differences in how big the need [for PA promotion] is” (school B) and stressed that, because of physically demanding lessons, some students needed to rest during breaks. Moreover, the different content of the educational tracks resulted in diverse class schedules and daily structures, all in all challenging the implementation of school-wide PA promotion initiatives. Nevertheless, regarding both school-wide and track-specific PA promotion initiatives, teachers across all schools emphasised that an important strategy for promoting PA would be to integrate it in the weekly schedule “so that it becomes a permanent part of [what we do]” and “[students] develop an interest in being physically active and doing sports” (school A).

However, particularly at one school, the leader and some teachers considered the vocational teaching to be hardly compatible with scheduled PA because it would disrupt daily compulsory tasks, such as preparing lunch for the entire school. Such tasks were considered essential for both the school functioning and for the individual student’s well-being. A teacher described how the daily routine of preparing lunch contributed to his students’ well-being, because “they do something together every single day” and “it’s easy for them to be part of this community, and the well-being associated with the community around producing something every day.” (school B).

PA initiatives were thus in some cases considered to potentially interfere with important class structures and difficult to make fit with the diverse schedules of the vocational classes. The school leader was aware that “the challenge will be to design track-specific solutions for each individual class” and, therefore, highlighted that a relevant strategy to succeed with promoting students’ PA is “all about how to integrate physical activity into every [class] and every daily routine” (school B).

In sum, the schools faced distinct challenges in promoting PA due to the varied daily structures and teaching purposes of the educational tracks. To tackle these challenges, tailoring initiatives and making PA fit into the everyday routines of each educational track was suggested as one potential strategy, while others considered the establishment of school-wide organisational structures, such as integrating PA into the weekly schedule, a more effective strategy to increase PA among the entire student group.

Theme 4: improving accessibility of physical activity facilities

The physical environment of the schools, including the physical surroundings, facilities, and equipment, was also experienced as a contextual influence at the meso level, which affected opportunities for PA promotion. The physical environments of the schools were vastly different. At two schools, the facilities were experienced as unsuitable for conducting physical activities by both students, teachers and school leader. A student described that “there is nothing, I mean, if we’re talking about facilities, there is nothing at all” (school A). As a strategy to address the lack of facilities, one of the schools sometimes rented facilities outside of the school to do physical activities:

“It doesn’t have to be anything big, but to have options for activities, and we don’t have that. So, we rent the badminton centre and pay to go to the gym or the yoga centre, where we’ve had a course with 10 sessions with one of our classes, and so on. So, we constantly have to find these things. It’s very difficult for us to get access to the local swimming pool, for instance.” (leader, school C).

Expenses related to renting made it difficult to prioritise due to the schools’ limited financial resources, and, together with transport time consumption, challenged opportunities for PA promotion at these two schools. In contrast, one school had great facilities according to both the school leader, teachers, and students. However, most facilities were placed in a building belonging to the ‘sport class’ and primarily used by this class. In fact, some of the students attending other classes had never seen or used the facilities and, therefore, the facilities did not support PA for most of the student group. Teachers were aware that students were unlikely to use the facilities unless they were specifically invited and stressed that “someone has to take the lead, either the teacher goes over there and brings some of the students, otherwise no one [participates]” (school B). Students agreed that “it makes a big difference when the teacher [participates]” (school B), emphasising the importance of active teacher involvement as a strategy to support and encourage student PA. Additionally, the physical placement of facilities was also considered to influence whether the students would use them. A teacher whose students were generally inactive remarked that having facilities for PA visible and close by was preferable because “it’s a bit easier to get [the students] pushed outside when there’s actually something to get them out to” (school B).

In sum, besides access to appropriate PA facilities, facilities should be both visible, familiar and close by to also encourage usage by students who are not usually physically active. Active teacher involvement could be an important strategy in improving accessibility by ensuring students’ familiarity with PA facilities and supporting their use.

Theme 5: prioritising physical activity promotion given the legal framework

The FGU consolidation act and its description of the aims of the programme provide the legal framework for working with promotion of student health, including PA, at the macro level. Although the act includes PA promotion as a didactical principle, which should be integrated into everyday school life, the brief and general description lacks specific guidelines for implementation. This left individual schools and leaders with the task of interpreting and applying the principle in practice, which resulted in difficulties as well as differences in strategies across schools:

“First of all, the legislation states that we need to focus on exercise and nutrition. So, there is a profile related to that on all FGU schools. There are no specific guidelines about whether it should be two hours a week or [something else], so in that sense, there’s no guidelines on what exactly we should do. But it’s obvious that we somehow have to support them, and the exercise part has been difficult for us to deliver.” (leader, school A).

Despite the mentioning of PA in the legal framework, none of the schools had developed a policy or action plan describing their strategies for PA promotion. Across all schools, teachers pointed to the importance of leadership for succeeding with promoting students’ PA, for instance, a teacher stressed how “the management has to decide that [PA promotion] is something we want to do” (school A).

Especially at two of the schools, teachers and students experienced the lack of financial resources and time allocation as a challenge for implementing PA initiatives. A student explained that “it feels a bit like ‘where’s the time for it?’. Because it’s fun, I love exercise and I think it’s great, especially team sports. It’s really great, it’s just not very relevant when there’s no time for it.” (school A). School leaders attributed the lack of prioritisation of time and money for PA to micro level influences, such as students facing numerous challenges besides physical inactivity, e.g., mental health issues, which meant that other areas were prioritised above PA. Also, macro level influences, such as the recent process of establishing FGU as an educational programme, were mentioned as very time- and resource consuming, as remarked by a school leader: “It has been a question of what to focus on. Because it has been a huge development process (…), where we have been focused on other things [than PA]” (school B). Furthermore, a teacher pointed out how “[PA] is not something [students] are tested in, it’s not in the curriculum in that sense” (school A). Thus, although PA is part of a didactical principle of the FGU programme, physical education was not included in the national FGU curriculum, which might also contribute to de-prioritisation of PA compared to other subjects.

At the third school, however, both time and financial resources had to some degree been prioritised to promoting students’ PA, with strategies including a weekly time slot allocated to mandatory PA for all students as well as a course for all teachers on how to incorporate health behaviour, including PA, into everyday teaching practices.

Overall, the broad and unspecific macro level legal framework provided little direction on how schools could support students’ PA during school hours. This resulted in different approaches to and prioritisations of PA, with PA promotion being dependent on leader support and prioritisation, often resulting in de-prioritisation in everyday school life.

Discussion

This study explored how Danish FGU schools targeting youth who are NEET can support their students in being physically active during school hours by identifying contextual influences on PA promotion as well as strategies employed by schools to promote PA given these influences. We generated five themes that together pointed to how students could be supported in being physically active in the FGU school setting, and in the following, we discuss these themes by returning to the WHO HPS framework, which we used as a starting point for the study. We discuss the findings in relation to, firstly, governance structures and, secondly, school operations and community partnerships [40].

Successful implementation of school-based health promotion, including PA promotion, that is based on HPS principles requires commitment and investment in resources across different levels of governance, including government and school level [21]. Although PA is widely acknowledged as beneficial for learning, integrating PA into the school day is often perceived as taking time away from schools’ core purpose in terms of developing students’ academic skills and preparing for exams, particularly in secondary school [28, 41]. At the government level, this perception constitutes a common barrier to implementation of school-based PA programmes in general educational settings, such as primary and secondary schools [26, 29]. However, our findings suggested that such an understanding is less pronounced in the FGU school setting. Specifically, informants stressed that students’ personal and social development were core purposes at FGU schools which were equally important for preparing them for further education or employment, and PA was viewed as a valuable tool to this end. For instance, PA was used to facilitate the development of students’ social skills and relations. This finding aligns with a study by Høy and colleagues that investigated employment programmes utilising PA as an element to prepare youth who are NEET for employment. Here, PA was also found to help participants “become part of a community and to meet other young people in a similar situation” [41]. The fact that PA promotion was viewed as aligning with the core purpose of FGU schools may contribute to easier implementation of PA promotion programmes in this type of educational setting, as school-based interventions are more likely to be integrated, prioritised and sustained if they align directly with a school’s mission [42]. This is supported by findings that teachers are more likely to dedicate time to PA promotion if they understand the relationship between students’ academic performance, health and well-being and, thereby, recognise how PA can contribute to the school’s core purpose [43].

Despite aligning with the core purpose of FGU schools, our findings showed that the absence of more specific requirements on how to implement PA into daily routines posed a challenge for prioritising PA promotion. As the legal framework did not provide any guidelines on how to integrate PA in school practices, school leaders and teachers tended to prioritise other, more clearly defined educational requirements. This echoes findings internationally from primary and secondary school settings, where an absence of political incentives to PA promotion has been identified as a barrier for implementation [26], while a political mandate for 45 min daily physical activity in Danish primary and lower secondary schools was found to reverse a trend of decreasing student physical activity [44]. Further, teachers’ knowledge of political regulations regarding PA has been identified as a facilitator [43]. We found that, because of the lack of political incentives and regulations, the prioritisation of resources in terms of time and money for PA promotion was influenced by school level governance, i.e. it depended on the individual school leader and, therefore, varied between schools. Other studies and implementation frameworks have also identified leadership support and engagement as essential for successful implementation of school-based PA programmes [27, 42, 43]. Thus, our findings suggest a need for more detailed national regulations to fully support a HPS approach to PA promotion in the FGU school setting; a point supported by the WHO’s recommendations to establish national policies and guidelines for PA, encourage weekly PA and active learning as part of the national curriculum, and allocate financial and human resources for PA [40].

While policies on various governmental levels lay the groundwork for school-based health promotion, the daily responsibility for implementing and supporting HPS initiatives lies within the individual school’s operations, including the curriculum, the socio-emotional and physical environments and school-health services, ideally supported through relevant community partnerships [21]. The current study suggested that FGU schools have a particular focus on the social-emotional environment due to the psychosocial characteristics of the student group, which constituted an important influence on how schools could approach PA promotion. Therefore, it was crucial to foster an inclusive and supportive environment for PA, where physical activities were tailored to students’ psychosocial needs and resources, e.g., by allowing for flexible participation options and establishing predictable routines. This differs from findings from educational settings targeting the general youth population, e.g. secondary schools, where characteristics related to the student group itself are rarely explored or identified as an influence on schools’ opportunities for implementing PA promotion initiatives [23]. However, our findings echo those of the study by Høy and colleagues, who found that youth comparable to the students in our study preferred fixed schedules, fixed groups of people, and ‘smooth transitions’ into new activities, e.g., by being provided adequate information, meeting peers and instructors, and seeing the physical setting in advance, when attending PA programmes [41]. The importance of focusing on inclusivity to accommodate students with diverse needs and resources is also stressed in the WHO’s recommendations for strengthening PA in school systems [40]. Based on our findings, this recommendation may be of particular importance at FGU schools compared to more general educational settings.

Although FGU schools were aware of how to adjust physical activities to accommodate the needs and resources of students, building appropriate organisational structures that supported the implementation of such activities into everyday school practices was identified as a challenge in the current study. Informants pointed to different needs and opportunities for PA promotion across educational tracks due to their diverse structures and content. Some believed that successful, long-term implementation of PA required integration into the weekly schedule with allocation of specific time slots to PA. This aligns well with an HPS approach to health promotion that implies using the curriculum as a structure to support student health [21], and, specifically concerning PA promotion, suggests that PA and active learning are integrated into various parts of the curriculum (besides physical education lessons) [40]. Findings from general educational settings have also shown that many teachers prefer physical activities that are short and easy to integrate into existing teaching and can be combined with or integrated into the curriculum [43]. Nonetheless, some informants in our study felt that this would interfere with their daily vocational tasks, potentially causing unnecessary stress and negatively affecting students’ mental well-being. Thus, based on our findings, integrating PA into the curriculum may be more challenging in the FGU school setting because of its distinct organisation with some educational tracks having a strong focus on vocational tasks with specific deadlines. Therefore, an approach that allows adjustment to educational track (and class) may be beneficial in this educational setting. However, the characteristics of the student group at FGU imply that any approach to integrate PA into the school day should consider students’ need for predictable routines and structures.

Finally, we found the schools’ physical environment to influence opportunities for PA promotion. Similarly to studies from general educational settings [24–26], a lack of appropriate facilities and equipment for PA was a major challenge to implementation, and establishing partnerships with local sport centres could be expensive and time consuming. Importantly, our findings also indicated that having physical access to appropriate facilities is not sufficient to encourage all students to be more physically active, as knowledge of facilities and social support influence the use of facilities. Perceived accessibility does not necessarily reflect objectively measured access to facilities and can be influenced by socio-personal factors such as knowledge, awareness, cultural background and social in- or exclusion [45]. Especially, students who are unfamiliar with PA may need support and encouragement to use facilities and, based on our findings, this could be obtained by teachers taking the lead and encouraging students to participate as well as actively participating in physical activities themselves.

Strengths and limitations

A major strength of this study lies in its focus on a youth group and an educational setting where there is a scarcity of knowledge about PA practices and -promotion. The study thus contributes to bridging a gap in current literature on school-based PA promotion while expanding on knowledge from general educational settings. The inclusion of different types of informants allowed us to explore diverse perspectives on PA promotion in the FGU school setting. Moreover, because informants came from three different schools with varying sizes, locations and physical surroundings we were able to explore both common and unique contextual influences and strategies for PA promotion. The consistency of findings across schools, however, emphasises that FGU schools share many of the same contextual influences and strategies for PA promotion, which enhances the transferability of the study. Nonetheless, a potential limitation to the study is the possibility that the schools chose to participate based on a special interest in PA, e.g., they viewed PA promotion as more important than other schools or found it particularly challenging due to their specific contextual circumstances. The fact that school leaders were responsible for recruiting teachers and students for the focus groups in this study made it unclear how participants were chosen and whether they were representative for the entire group of teachers and students regarding their opinions on PA promotion. For instance, the student focus groups had a large overweight of boys, which may have impacted discussions due to gender differences in experiences and preferences for PA.

Conclusions

This study explored how Danish FGU schools targeting youth who are NEET can support their students in being physically active during school hours. The findings point to both advantages and challenges related to school-based PA promotion in this unique educational setting. Importantly, we found that implementation of school-based PA aligned well with the schools’ core purpose of fostering both academic, social and personal development of students. Yet, the absence of specific legal requirements related to PA promotion posed a challenge to its prioritisation and implementation in daily school life. Moreover, the organisational structure of FGU schools, including the diverse educational tracks, required PA promotion initiatives to be flexible and designed to fit into the curriculum of each track. The findings also showed that PA promotion required tailored initiatives emphasising inclusivity and flexibility to accommodate participation of students with various mental health challenges. Also, we found a need for improved access to PA facilities and equipment, which, ideally, should be accompanied by proper introduction and support from teachers to ensure use by all students regardless of familiarity and prior experiences with PA. In sum, the study underscores the importance of a flexible and context adaptable approach to PA promotion in educational settings targeting youth who are NEET, such as a whole-school approach to PA that influences the whole school system, including the unique characteristics of the students, the school organisational structures and the wider political and legal regulations.
