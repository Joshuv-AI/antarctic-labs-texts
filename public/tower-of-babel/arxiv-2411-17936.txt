Stealthy Multi-Task Adversarial Attacks
Jiacheng Guo, Tianyun Zhang, Lei Li, Haochen Yang, Hongkai Yu, Minghai Qin, Cleveland State University Western Digital Research Corresponding Authors

Abstract
Deep Neural Networks exhibit inherent vulnerabilities to adversarial attacks, which can significantly compromise their outputs and reliability. While existing research primarily focuses on attacking single-task scenarios or indiscriminately targeting all tasks in multi-task environments, we investigate selectively targeting one task while preserving performance in others within a multi-task framework. This approach is motivated by varying security priorities among tasks in real-world applications, such as autonomous driving, where misinterpreting critical objects (e.g., signs, traffic lights) poses a greater security risk than minor depth miscalculations. Consequently, attackers may hope to target security-sensitive tasks while avoiding non-critical tasks from being compromised, thus evading being detected before compromising crucial functions. In this paper, we propose a method for the stealthy multi-task attack framework that utilizes multiple algorithms to inject imperceptible noise into the input. This novel method demonstrates remarkable efficacy in compromising the target task while simultaneously maintaining or even enhancing performance across non-targeted tasks — a criterion hitherto unexplored in the field. Additionally, we introduce an automated approach for searching the weighting factors in the loss function, further enhancing attack efficiency. Experimental results validate our framework’s ability to successfully attack the target task while preserving the performance of non-targeted tasks. The automated loss function weight searching method demonstrates comparable efficacy to manual tuning, establishing a state-of-the-art multi-task attack framework.



Introduction

Figure 1:
An example for the stealthy attack on semantic segmentation task. The SMTA framework preserves the performance of other non-targeted tasks.

Deep Neural Networks (DNNs) are known for their impressive performance across a wide range of tasks
(
LeCun et al. 1998
;
Hinton et al. 2012
;
Dahl et al. 2011
;
Andor et al. 2016
)
, yet they are also vulnerable to an inherent vulnerability to adversarial attacks
(
Madry et al. 2018
)
. These attacks involve making subtle, often imperceptible, perturbations to input data, which can cause DNNs to produce incorrect outputs
(
Nguyen, Yosinski, and Clune 2015
;
Carlini et al. 2016
;
Madry et al. 2018
)
. The implications of such attacks are particularly concerning in safety-critical applications, where even a small misjudgment can lead to catastrophic outcomes
(
Goodfellow, Shlens, and Szegedy 2014
)
. For instance, in autonomous driving systems, an adversarial attack that alters the perception of a stop sign as a yield sign could result in a serious accident
(
Lu, Sibai, and Fabry 2017
)
.

While research on adversarial attacks has focused on single-task scenarios, where the goal is to compromise the performance of a model on a specific task traditionally, there is a growing interest in the domain of multi-task learning (MTL)
(
Caruana 1997
;
Evgeniou and Pontil 2004
;
Crawshaw 2020
)
. MTL allows a shared network to learn multiple related tasks simultaneously, improving efficiency and generalization while reducing computational costs
(
Caruana 1997
;
Sener and Koltun 2018
;
Crawshaw 2020
)
. In this way, we only need to train a combined network to solve different but related tasks instead of training multiple networks separately. MTL significantly improves the training efficiency and reduces the computational cost with much less trained parameters
(
Evgeniou and Pontil 2004
;
Xu et al. 2016
)
. However, the introduction of MTL frameworks also opens new avenues for adversarial attacks, where attackers might target multiple tasks within a shared network
(
Xu et al. 2016
;
Guo et al. 2020
;
Ghamizi et al. 2022
)
.

Existing research in multi-task adversarial attacks (MTA) primarily aims to degrade the performance of all tasks simultaneously by generating adversarial perturbations that affect the entire network
(
Moosavi-Dezfooli et al. 2017
;
Poursaeed et al. 2018
;
Ghamizi et al. 2022
)
. While this approach effectively disrupts overall performance, it does not consider scenarios where certain tasks are of higher security priority than others.
Attackers should strategically prioritize targeting critical tasks (with higher security priority) while minimizing impact on non-critical ones. This approach maximizes the effectiveness of the attack on high-value targets and reduces the risk of premature detection by a defender. By avoiding disruption to non-critical tasks, attackers aim to delay the activation of defense mechanisms, thereby extending their window of opportunity to compromise essential tasks.
For instance, in an autonomous driving system, misinterpreting critical objects such as traffic signs or pedestrians can be far more dangerous than minor errors in depth estimation
(
Liu et al. 2023
)
. Consequently, there is a certain need for an approach that allows for selective task targeting, where the attacker can compromise a high-priority task while preserving or even enhancing the performance of non-targeted tasks.

In this paper, we address this challenge by introducing a novel concept termed “Stealthy Multi-Task Attack” (SMTA). The SMTA framework is designed to subtly alter the input image with minimal noise, effectively degrading the performance of a targeted task while ensuring that other non-targeted tasks remain unaffected or even perform better.
Figure
1
shows an example SMTA of an image in the Cityscape dataset. The left four images are original RGB images and visualized results of three tasks (semantic segmentation, part segmentation, and disparity). The RGB image on the top right is the adversarially attacked image targeting the semantic segmentation task. It can be seen that the semantic segmentation result is significantly degraded (the second image on the right) while the other two tasks remain almost identical to the non-attacked results.
It is intriguing to observe the disparate performance across three downstream tasks, given that all three tasks share both the attacked input image and the entire backbone network.
Furthermore, we propose an automated approach to search for the optimal weighting factors in the loss function design to protect the performance of non-targeted tasks while attacking the targeted task. This method can dynamically adjust the loss function based on varying input data, significantly reducing the training time compared to manually exploring different weighting factor combinations.

Overall, our main contributions are listed as follows:

•

We propose a concept for the qualified multi-task adversarial attack, which is called “Stealthy Multi-task Attack” (SMTA). In this scenario, by slightly changing the input image (e.g., 1-2 pixel levels), the performance of the
targeted
task is significantly impacted while other
non-targeted
tasks have strictly equal or better performance.

•

We design a novel formulation for SMTA to maximize the attacking capability following the proposed concept. The formulation leads to a problem in which the loss function is a weighted sum of individual losses for each task, but the weighting factors might be negative.

•

We propose a method to automatically search for optimal weighting factors in the loss function we design. This automated method can also adjust the loss function according to different inputs, saving a significant amount of time compared to manually scanning the combination of weighting factors.

•

Experiments show that our SMTA framework can be applied to multiple multi-task datasets (i.e., NYUv2 and Cityscapes) and attacking algorithms (i.e., projected gradient descent (PGD) and iterative fast gradient sign method (IFGSM)). To the best of our knowledge, this is the first standard and baseline for targeted adversarial attacking of multi-task learning systems.

Related Work

Multi-task learning

Multi-task learning (MTL) is a machine learning paradigm that simultaneously learns multiple related tasks, offering benefits such as improved data efficiency, reduced overfitting, and enhanced generalization
(
Caruana 1997
;
Xu et al. 2016
;
Crawshaw 2020
)
. MTL has gained popularity in various fields, including natural language processing and computer vision
(
Zhang et al. 2022
;
Yu et al. 2024
)
. Recent surveys categorize MTL techniques into areas such as regularization, relationship learning, feature propagation, optimization, and pre-training
(
Evgeniou and Pontil 2004
;
Hinton et al. 2012
;
Zhang et al. 2019
;
Guo et al. 2024
)
. MTL approaches can be broadly classified into joint training and multi-step training methods depending on task relations
(
Zhang et al. 2022
;
Ghamizi et al. 2022
)
. The field has evolved from traditional approaches to deep learning and pretrained foundation models, with recent developments focusing on task-promotable and task-agnostic training, as well as zero-shot learning capabilities
(
He et al. 2020
;
Yu et al. 2024
)
. MTL has also been explored in distributed and streaming machine learning contexts, highlighting its versatility for future applications
(
Nassif et al. 2020
)
.

Adversarial attacks

Adversarial attacks have emerged as a significant challenge for deep neural networks (DNNs), which are highly vulnerable to perturbations that can lead to incorrect predictions
(
Lu, Sibai, and Fabry 2017
;
Sun et al. 2024
)
. These perturbations, though often imperceptible to human observers, can drastically alter the output of a model, revealing critical weaknesses in DNN architectures
(
Nguyen, Yosinski, and Clune 2015
;
Carlini and Wagner 2017
;
Madry et al. 2018
)
.

One of the earliest and most influential methods for generating adversarial attacks is based on gradient optimization.
(
Moosavi-Dezfooli et al. 2017
)
introduced the concept of universal adversarial perturbations, which could fool the classifiers by manipulating the gradient of the loss function. This work set the stage for numerous subsequent approaches that exploit gradient information to craft attacks that are both effective and difficult to detect. Adversarial attacks in MTL introduce unique challenges as they require balancing the performance of multiple tasks within a single model. As the first work considering adversarial attacks with MTL,
(
Chen et al. 2018
)
addressed the issue of gradient imbalance in MTL by proposing GradNorm, a gradient normalization technique that ensures balanced loss optimization across tasks. This technique indirectly contributes to the robustness of MTL models by preventing any single task from dominating the training process.
(
Guo et al. 2020
)
extended the concept of adversarial attacks to the multi-task domain with the introduction of MTA. This paper announced that multiple tasks could be attacked simultaneously by generating multi-task perturbations.
(
Ghamizi et al. 2022
)
explored the broader implications of adversarial attacks and robustness within MTL, digging into the potential benefits and limitations. Their work underscores the complexity of achieving robustness in MTL, where the interactions between tasks can complicate the defense against adversarial attacks.

Recently, adversarial attacks are not confined to traditional image classification tasks; they have been successfully applied to various specialized domains.
(
Du et al. 2022
)
explored the impact of attacks on object detection in aerial imagery, demonstrating that these attacks can effectively disrupt the performance of detectors in the real world.
(
Chen, Wang, and Crandall 2022
)
developed semantically stealthy adversarial attacks for segmentation models, where the perturbations are crafted to maintain the visual quality of the images while inducing significant model errors.

To counter the growing threat of adversarial attacks,
(
Madry et al. 2018
)
proposed a robust optimization approach that formulates the adversarial defense problem as a min-max problem to enhance model robustness to adversarial attacks. Additionally,
(
Mao et al. 2021
)
introduced a novel defense mechanism that leverages natural supervision to reverse the effects of adversarial attacks. This method relies on naturally occurring patterns in the data to guide the model back to correct predictions.

Methodology

Problem Formulation

Consider that we have a dataset with shared input data and multiple ground-truth labels corresponding to different tasks. For input data
x
, we have the ground-truth label
y=(y_{1},y_{2},...,y_{i},...,y_{m})
, where
y_{i}
denotes the ground-truth for the
i
-th task. Also, we have a pretrained multi-task model for this dataset, with the loss function corresponding to the
i
-th task
L_{i}(x,y_{i})
.

Definition:
A
Stealthy Multi-Task Attack (SMTA)
is an adversarial attack to the multi-task model such that the performance of the targeted task is degraded, and the performance of all other tasks needs to be maintained.

Assume
i_{t}
the targeted task, SMTA can be formulated as

\displaystyle\underset{\delta}{\text{maximize}}

\displaystyle L_{i_{t}}(x+\delta,y_{i_{t}}),

(1)

\displaystyle\text{subject to}

\displaystyle L_{i}(x+\delta,y_{i})\leq L_{i}(x,y_{i})~\text{for}~i\neq i_{t},

\displaystyle||\delta||_{p}\leq\epsilon,x+\delta\in\mathcal{B},

where
\delta
is the adversarial noise,
\epsilon
is a value to constraint the strength of the adversarial noise, and
\mathcal{B}
is the box constraint to ensure that we derive a valid image after adding the adversarial noise.

We call the attack without the first constraint,
L_{i}(x+\delta,y_{i})\leq L_{i}(x,y_{i})~\text{for}~i\neq i_{t},
a
non-stealthy
multi-task attack.

As there are many complex constraints in problem (
1
), it is difficult to solve it directly. We relax problem
1
to

\underset{||\delta||_{p}\leq\epsilon,x+\delta\in\mathcal{B}}{\text{maximize}}~~L(x+\delta,y)=\sum_{i=1}^{m}w_{i}L_{i}(x+\delta,y_{i}),

(2)

where
w_{i}
is the weighting factor corresponding to the
i
-th task.
w_{i}
is positive if
i=i_{t}
, and
w_{i}
is negative if
i\neq i_{t}
. When we solve problem (
2
), we can guarantee to find a solution that is feasible to problem (
1
) by adjusting the weighting factors manually or using an automated searching method. The details of adjusting the weighting factors will be discussed in the next subsection.

In the adversarial attack of a multi-task model, attacking one task could negatively influence other tasks. As a result, the performance of non-targeted tasks could also be significantly affected when the targeted task is attacked. To maintain the performance of non-targeted tasks, we set a positive weighting factor for the targeted task and negative weighting factors for the non-targeted tasks when solving problem (
2
). The positive weighting factor can degrade the performance of the targeted task, while the negative weighting factors can compensate for the performance degradation of non-targeted tasks caused by attacking the targeted task. If the magnitudes of negative weighting factors are too small, the performance of non-targeted tasks will still be degraded. In this case, the first constraint in problem (
1
) is not satisfied. Suppose the magnitudes of negative weighting factors are too large, then the performance of non-targeted tasks will be much better than their performance before the attack, and the solution of problem (
2
) will be feasible to problem (
1
). However, the strength of attacking the targeted task will be sacrificed.

In our proposed SMTA framework, we aim to search for a combination of weighting factors that maximize the performance degradation of the targeted task without sacrificing the performance of non-targeted tasks. We propose two methods to search the weighting factors for different tasks:
manual method
and
automated method
.

The first method is to adjust the weighting factors manually. In this method, we fix the weighting factor of the targeted task and manually tune the weighting factors of the non-targeted tasks. Specifically, we start by setting the weighting factors to a small magnitude. If the first constraint in problem
1
is not satisfied for some tasks after the attack, we increase the magnitudes of weighting factors corresponding to these tasks in the next trial. We keep doing this until all the tasks satisfy the constraint. The manual method can guarantee to find a feasible solution to the problem (
1
). However, it takes a lot of trials to find a group of weighting factors that leads to a feasible solution, which means tuning the hyper-parameters takes a long time.

The second method is to search for the weighting factors automatically. Different from the manual method in which weighting factors are constant during the adversarial attack, in the automated method weighting factors are dynamically updated in every step of the attack. For every non-targeted task, we adjust its corresponding weighting factor based on the difference between its current loss and original loss without attack. We include a step size for adjusting the weighting factors, once the constraint corresponding to a task is satisfied we multiply its step size by an attenuation coefficient to accelerate the convergence of its weighting factor. The details of the automated search method to adjust weighting factors are summarized in Algorithm
1
. Unlike the manual method takes a long time to tune the hyper-parameters, the automated method can efficiently find a feasible solution for SMTA with a proper step size and attenuation coefficient.

Experiments

To evaluate the performance of the proposed SMTA framework, we provide two approaches to search the optimal task weighting factors:
manual
method and
automated
method. Two attack algorithms are applied: projected gradient descent (PGD)
(
Chen and Wainwright 2015
)
and iterative fast gradient sign method (IFGSM)
(
Kurakin, Goodfellow, and Bengio 2018
)
on each method. We evaluate the performances of two weight searching methods through a proposed loss function design.

Experimental Setup

We evaluate our proposed framework using the NYUv2
(
Silberman et al. 2012
)
and Cityscapes
(
Cordts et al. 2016
)
datasets, both of which are suitable for multi-task scenarios. To expedite training, the images in the NYUv2 dataset are all resized to
288\times 384
, and those from the Cityscapes dataset are resized to
256\times 512
.

To demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed SMTA framework, we apply two attack algorithms: PGD and IFGSM, for both non-stealthy and stealthy attacks. A “non-stealthy attack” refers to targeting a specific task without regard for the impact on non-targeted tasks, causing significant degradation in non-targeted tasks as well. In contrast, a “stealthy attack” ensures that while the targeted task is attacked, the non-targeted tasks maintain performance equal to or better than their non-attacked state. For the PGD attack, we use both
L_{2}
and
L_{\infty}
norms, while for the IFGSM attack, we employ the
L_{\infty}
norm. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first baseline that proposes a stealthy attack method integrated with the MTL framework. All experiments were conducted using two NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3090 GPUs.

Experimental Procedure

Adversarial attacks negatively impact all tasks in a multi-task network, so it is crucial to identify the optimal combination of weighting factors to ensure the targeted task is effectively attacked while the non-targeted tasks maintain performance levels equal to or better than the non-attacked model.
To evaluate the SMTA framework, we propose an evaluation standard that focuses on the attack effectiveness of the targeted task, provided that the non-targeted tasks remain unaffected.

In the non-stealthy attack scenario, the weighting factor for the targeted task is set to 1, while other tasks are set to 0. This setup demonstrates that attacking a targeted task also significantly degrades the performance of non-targeted tasks. Specifically, when “strong adversarial attacks” alter more than 2 pixels

In the manual weight searching method, we explore optimal weighting combinations to maximize attack effectiveness. We observe that assigning negative weights to non-targeted tasks can significantly enhance attack performance. Thus, the optimal weighting factor combination lies between the original and negative weight settings, allowing the targeted task to be effectively attacked while preserving the performance of other tasks. Algorithm
1
, which outlines the automated weight search procedure, dynamically adjusts weighting factors based on loss function changes. Key hyperparameters include the step size and attenuation coefficient for weighting factors. By choosing appropriate values for these parameters, the automated method achieves attack performance comparable to the manual approach.

Main Results and Analysis

For the NYUv2 dataset, we evaluate the performance of the SMTA framework using mean intersection over union (mIoU), absolute error (aErr.), and mean angular distances (mDist.) for each respective task. For the Cityscapes dataset, the evaluation metrics include mIoU for segmentation and part segmentation tasks, and aErr. for disparity estimation. All basic settings follow
(
Liu et al. 2022
)
.

Table
1
shows the results of the non-stealthy attack, as well as the proposed manual and automated attack methods for each task using the PGD
L_{\infty}
attack on the NYUv2 dataset with different perturbation levels. Note that when
\epsilon
is set to 0, it represents the baseline model without any adversarial attack. Even when a specific targeted task is chosen, all non-stealthy attack results show that non-targeted tasks are also significantly affected by the attack. For SMTA to be deemed effective, the performance of non-targeted tasks must remain unaffected, showing equal or better performance compared to the baseline model while the targeted task is attacked.

Overall, both the manual and automated methods demonstrate significant attack efficacy. For each perturbation level
\epsilon
, we attack each task (considered the “targeted task”) while preserving the other two tasks’ performance to be equal or better. In most cases, the proposed stealthy attack method achieves comparable results on the targeted task compared to the non-stealthy attack while safeguarding the non-targeted tasks. On the one hand, the attack effectiveness is evident even when only a single pixel is altered, and at this level, the changes are completely imperceptible to the naked eye. Specifically, when altering 1 or 2 pixels, both the manual and automated methods achieve similar attack performance on the targeted task as the non-stealthy attack while fully protecting the non-targeted tasks. When increasing the perturbation to 4 pixels (i.e.,
\epsilon=8/255
), the performance difference between the manual, automated methods, and the non-stealthy attack on targeted tasks remains within an acceptable range, though the attack effect becomes visually apparent. Besides, the automated weight searching method achieves nearly the same attack effectiveness as the manual method but is more efficient, saving significant time on parameter tuning.

Ensuring that non-targeted tasks retain their original performance, the results show that SMTA is more effective in attacking the targeted tasks than the non-stealthy method in most cases. The automated search method achieves comparable effectiveness to the manual method, validating SMTA as a stable and state-of-the-art framework.

Conclusion

In this paper, we introduce a novel stealthy multi-task attack framework to selectively target specific tasks within a multi-task learning environment while preserving or even enhancing the performance of non-targeted tasks.
Our method leverages multiple attack algorithms to introduce imperceptible perturbations, effectively compromising the targeted task without negatively impacting other tasks.

Furthermore, we propose an automated approach to optimize the loss function by dynamically adjusting the weighting factors, significantly improving the efficiency of the attack process compared to manual tuning.
Experimental results demonstrate that our SMTA framework successfully attacks the selected task while safeguarding non-targeted tasks across various multi-task datasets and attack methods.
This work not only provides a new standard for evaluating multi-task adversarial attacks but also opens up new possibilities for developing more sophisticated and targeted adversarial strategies in multi-task learning systems.
