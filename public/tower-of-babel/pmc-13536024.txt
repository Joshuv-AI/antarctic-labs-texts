Lock solutions for the prevention and treatment of catheter-related bloodstream infections in newborns: a systematic review and meta-analysis protocol
Authors: Giulia Maiocco, Sonia Deantoni, Elena Maggiora, Alessandra Coscia, Francesco Cresi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionBloodstream infections remain a major cause of morbidity and mortality for infants admitted to neonatal intensive care units (NICUs), with central venous catheter use representing one of the principal risk factors. Among the strategies developed to prevent and manage catheter-related bloodstream infections (CRBSIs) and central line-associated bloodstream infections (CLABSIs), catheter-lock therapy (CLT) has emerged as a particularly promising approach. CLT is defined as the instillation of antimicrobial or antibiotic solutions into the catheter lumen, which are then left in place for a defined period, and has been adopted in adult and paediatric populations with satisfactory results. However, evidence supporting CLT efficacy and safety in newborns is still limited and fragmented. A rigorous synthesis of the available data is therefore critically needed.Methods and analysisThis systematic review will follow the methodological recommendations of the Cochrane Collaboration, in accordance with Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines. The primary outcome will be the frequency of CRBSI and CLABSI in newborns receiving CLT in NICUs, compared with those receiving placebo or no lock therapy. Secondary outcomes will include mortality, duration of NICU stay, catheter survival rate and catheter dwell time, antibiotic exposure and safety profiles. A Population, Intervention, Comparison, and Outcome framework will be used to guide the inclusion criteria. A comprehensive search strategy has been developed across the most relevant electronic databases, together with grey literature. Search queries have been tailored for each specific database. Backward and forward citation strategies will be used. No restrictions regarding language or publication period will be applied. The systematic search will be conducted on 1 May 2026. All study designs presenting original data will be considered for inclusion—randomised controlled trials, cohort studies, case-control studies, case reports and surveys. Studies without original data will be excluded; however, the reference list of relevant reviews will be screened manually. Two reviewers will independently screen titles and abstracts, extract data and assess the risk of bias of each included study using standardised tools. In the event of disagreement, a third reviewer will be consulted. The quantitative synthesis will involve aggregation of comparable data, with results presented in tabular and graphical form. The effects of CLT will be evaluated separately according to its use as prophylaxis or as treatment. A subgroup analysis will be conducted according to the type of locking solution employed. Where at least two studies are judged sufficiently homogeneous, a meta-analysis will be performed. The GRADE (Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development and Evaluation) approach will be applied to assess the certainty of the evidence for clinically relevant outcomes.Ethics and disseminationAs this protocol pertains to a systematic review based on secondary data from published studies, formal ethical approval is not required. We anticipate completing the systematic review within 6 months. On conclusion, the findings will be submitted for publication in peer-reviewed journals indexed in PubMed. Introduction

Central venous catheters (CVCs) are essential components of care in critically ill patients, providing reliable access for medication delivery, parenteral nutrition and haemodynamic monitoring. However, the presence of a central line is consistently identified as one of primary risk factors for the development of sepsis in both adult and paediatric intensive care units.1 2

Two distinct entities are commonly described regarding sepsis in critical settings: catheter-related bloodstream infection (CRBSI) and central line-associated bloodstream infection (CLABSI). CRBSI refers to a clinically confirmed infection in which the catheter is proven to be the source of bacteraemia, typically demonstrated through paired blood cultures or differential time to positivity. In contrast, CLABSI is a surveillance definition, used primarily for infection control and epidemiological purposes, referring to any laboratory-confirmed bloodstream infection occurring in a patient with a central line in place within the preceding 48 hours, in the absence of another identifiable source.3

Over the past decades, numerous strategies have been developed to prevent and manage CRBSI and CLABSI, including strict adherence to aseptic technique, care bundles, antimicrobial-impregnated catheters and optimised catheter maintenance protocols.4 5 Among these approaches, catheter-lock therapy (CLT)—defined as the instillation of antimicrobial or antibiotic solutions into the catheter lumen, which are then left in place for a defined period—has gained increasing attention. CLT may be employed either as a prophylaxis, to prevent microbial colonisation of the intraluminal catheter surface, or as treatment, to eradicate established infections.6 In adult and paediatric populations, several studies and meta-analyses have demonstrated the efficacy of this technique in reducing infection rates and preserving catheter function.7–9

In neonatal intensive care units (NICUs), the risk of CRBSI and CLABSI is particularly high due to the immunologic immaturity and weak skin barrier of preterm infants; moreover, consequences of sepsis can be devastating for such a fragile population. For these reasons, some authors have recently proposed the implementation of antimicrobial or antibiotic CLT protocols in neonatal settings.10–12 However, given the extreme vulnerability of newborns admitted to NICUs, CTL must demonstrate robust clinical efficacy alongside an exceptional safety profile, with no meaningful adverse effects.

A systematic review and meta-analysis of the existing literature is therefore warranted to consolidate current knowledge and potentially guide the safe, evidence-based adoption of CLT in neonatal practice.

Methods and analysis

This systematic review will follow the methodological recommendations of the Cochrane Collaboration, in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines.

No restrictions regarding language or publication period will be applied. All study designs presenting original data will be considered for inclusion: randomised controlled trials (RCTs) as well as non-RCTs (i.e., cohort studies, case-control studies, case reports, and surveys). Publications without original data, such as reviews, editorials, comments, letters and book chapters, will be excluded. However, the reference list of relevant reviews will be screened manually to identify any additional potentially eligible studies. Studies based on animal or in vitro models will also be excluded.

Search strategy

The search strategy will include the following electronic databases: PubMed/Medical Literature Analysis and Retrieval System Online (MEDLINE), Embase, The Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL), Scopus, Google Scholar, Global Index Medicus, Web of Science and Cumulative Index to Nursing and Allied Health Literature (CINAHL).

Grey literature will also be explored, including the following sources: MedXRiv, Bielefeld Academic Search Engine (BASE), Connecting Repositories (CORE) and Zenodo.org.

Clinical trial registries will also be searched for recently completed trials, including ClinicalTrials.gov and the WHO International Clinical Trials Registry Platform (ICTRP).

Tailored search queries have been developed for each database and are summarised in online supplemental table 1.

A hybrid search strategy will be employed, combining structured database searches with backward snowballing citation searches, to maximise the identification of relevant primary studies.13

The five most cited relevant studies identified through the systematic search will be screened on PubMed for citing articles (forward snowballing).14 Forward snowballing complements database searches by following citations forward from known relevant papers to capture recent, terminologically diverse or poorly indexed studies that keyword searches tend to miss, thereby improving recall and assisting in establishing literature saturation. In addition, the reference list of relevant reviews will be screened manually to identify any further potentially eligible studies.

This systematic literature search will be conducted on 1 May 2026.

Studies screening and data extraction

Search results from different databases will be imported into Rayyan, where duplicates will be identified and removed. Each article will be independently screened by two authors with previous experience in the field of neonatal intensive care. First screening stage will use titles and abstracts to remove irrelevant reports based on eligibility and PICO criteria. When uncertainty about inclusion of the study arises, the study paper will proceed to the full-text selection stage.

Remaining studies will be retrieved, and independently examined in full text by the two authors.

A third review author will be involved at the end, to resolve possible conflicts.

A standardised data extraction form on Excel will be used to report study characteristics, participants, interventions, comparisons and outcomes (online supplemental table 2). Two authors will independently extract relevant data from the included studies and assess their methodological quality. When necessary, corresponding authors of relevant studies will be contacted to obtain missing data or to clarify unclear information. The extracted data will be then cross-checked, and any discrepancies addressed through discussion by the full review team until consensus is reached.

Risk of bias in individual studies

Two reviewers will independently assess each included study for risk of bias. Any disagreement will be referred to a third reviewer for resolution. Validated tools will be used, accordingly to the study design: RoB-2 for randomised controlled trials,15 ROBINS-I for non-randomised trials of intervention,16 and Newcastle-Ottawa Scale for observational studies.17 The risk of bias due to missing results will be evaluated using a within-study approach, applying the ROB-ME tool.18

Data synthesis and meta-biases

All included studies will be synthesised narratively in a structured manner, grouping them properly according to relevant characteristics, resulting in a qualitative synthesis.

Where appropriate, a quantitative synthesis will be conducted by pooling comparable data, with results (including measures of distribution, mean, SD and effect size, when available) presented in tabular and graphical form.

The effects of CLT will be evaluated separately according to its use as prophylaxis or as treatment.

A subgroup analysis will be conducted according to the type of locking solution employed.

Where at least two studies are judged sufficiently homogeneous in terms of intervention type, population and outcome definition, a meta-analysis will be performed using a random-effects model with Hartung-Knapp adjustment. Cochran’s Q test will be used to assess statistical heterogeneity across studies, considering p<0.05 indicative of significant heterogeneity. I² will also be reported to quantify the proportion of variability attributed to heterogeneity rather than chance.

Effect sizes will be expressed as risk ratios or ORs, for dichotomous outcomes, and as mean differences or standardised mean differences, for continuous outcomes. Where available, adjusted effect estimates from observational studies will be preferentially extracted.

In the presence of rare events or zero-event studies, appropriate statistical methods (eg, continuity correction or alternative modelling approaches) will be considered.

Where sufficient studies are available (n≥10), publication bias will be assessed using funnel plots and, where appropriate, formal statistical tests. Sensitivity analyses will be conducted to evaluate the robustness of the findings. All statistical analyses will be performed using R or equivalent statistical software.

Confidence in cumulative evidence

The GRADE (Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development and Evaluation) approach will be applied to assess the certainty of evidence for each clinically relevant outcome, which will be independently evaluated by two reviewers.19 Should the number of included studies be limited, the potential for publication bias will be carefully considered.20

Patient and public involvement statement

Patients and the public were not involved in the design, conduct, or reporting of this systematic review protocol. Given the nature of the study—a synthesis of existing published literature—direct patient or public involvement was not considered feasible at the protocol stage. Future dissemination of findings will aim to include formats accessible to patients, families and the general public.

Discussion

Sepsis remains one of the most serious and life-threatening conditions affecting infants admitted to NICUs, with CVC use representing one of its principal risk factors. Among the strategies proven effective in preventing and managing catheter-related sepsis in adults and children, CLT holds considerable promise. However, its use in neonatal settings remains limited. The unique physiological vulnerability of NICU patients requires caution prior to implementing this approach. Concerns include the potential selection of antimicrobial resistance when antibiotic-based solutions are used, while alternative agents—such as ethanol—raise safety issues should even minimal amounts enter the systemic circulation. Furthermore, neonatal catheters typically have small lumens (<5 Fr) and are often non-tunnelled, making direct comparison with protocols used in other age groups problematic. Consequently, CTL in NICUs has so far remained confined to experimental settings rather than routine clinical practice. In this context, a rigorous evidence-based synthesis of the existing literature has the potential to address the critical knowledge gaps that currently impede the implementation of lock therapy in neonatal care.

Ethics and dissemination

As this protocol pertains to a systematic review based on secondary data from published studies, formal ethical approval is not required. We anticipate completing the systematic review within 6 months. On conclusion, the findings will be submitted for publication in peer-reviewed journals indexed in PubMed. This systematic review protocol has been registered in PROSPERO (International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews) under registration number: CRD420251176308.
