Behavioral parent training interventions to improve health outcomes of preschool children with ADHD symptoms from Chinese disadvantaged families: study protocol for a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Dan Li, Xiaorui Feng, Yuan Yuan, Yanwei Li, Li Zhang, Teng Tian, Xinyang Wu, Dandan Wu, Jiaqi Yao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBehavioral parent training (BPT) interventions are effective for preschoolers with attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD). However, BPT is rarely implemented in Chinese populations; very few parents receive BPT for improving health outcomes of their children’s ADHD symptoms in hospitals, especially those from disadvantaged families. BPT interventions urgently require assessments for adaption and effectiveness with Chinese caregivers.MethodsA sample of 128 parents, each with a child aged 4–6 years diagnosed with ADHD, will be randomly assigned to either receive up to 10 sessions of group-based BPT (gBPT) or support meetings conducted by psychiatrists in a hospital. Assessments will occur at baseline, posttreatment, and 12-week follow-up. The main goal is to determine how gBPT affects the reduction of ADHD symptoms in children. Additionally, the study will also examine the influence of gBPT on parental stress, parental skills, and the overall quality of family life. Qualitative interviews with a semi-structured format will evaluate the advantages and challenges of the intervention.DiscussionThis study will be the first RCT to evaluate the efficacy of modified gBPT among low socioeconomic status families in China. This study will contribute to a greater focus on individual therapy effect and thus improve the understanding of subgroups of ADHD families. Suppose our finding confirms the impact of gBPT on ADHD symptoms, as well as advances the viability and receptiveness of gBPT among families with low SES in Chinese culture. In that case, the results should enhance understanding of gBPT and provide valuable clinical experience regarding the use of gBPT.Trial registrationChiCTR2400082158, Registered 22 March 2024, https://www.chictr.org.cn/showproj.html?proj=210819Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13063-025-09156-3.Keywords: Behavioral parent training, Randomized controlled trial, Attention deficit hyperactivity disorder, Disadvantaged families, Parenting

Introduction

Background

Attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) is a neurodevelopmental disorder that usually appears in young children and is defined by signs of excessive activity, lack of focus, and impulsiveness, which disrupt daily activities and impair occupational functioning [1]. ADHD prevalence in children is estimated to be 7.2% (95% CI, 6.7–7.8%) [2] and 6.4% (95% CI, 6.2–7.0) in China mainland [3]. Particularly, the ADHD rate for Chinese preschoolers has increased from 2.47% [4] in 2016 to 5% [5] in 2022. More than two-thirds of those children will still have symptoms in adulthood [6, 7], resulting in diverse negative effects such as functional impairment [8], antisocial behavior [9], and substance abuse [10], leading to a serious economic burden for their families and society.

Triggers for ADHD are believed to be genetic, environmental factors, and parent–child interactions [11, 12]. Approximately 10%–40% of ADHD variance can be attributed to environmental factors like family conflict, hostility, and chaos in the home [13], given that high parenting stress contributes to a worsening in child behavior problems [14]. Notably a reciprocal relationship may exist between children with ADHD and the family environment. On the one hand, the difficulties experienced by families with children with ADHD can impact parent–child interactions, the overall home environment, and elevate parental stress levels [15]. Interpersonal conflicts, depression, isolation, and negativity are more common in these families [16], along with a poorer quality of life both socially and environmentally [17]. On the other hand, poor family environments can lead to hyperactive-impulsive behaviors and inattentive behaviors in children, which predict ADHD symptoms in those children [18]. A longitudinal study showed that 4.5 years-old children in families with high conflict, and hostility would have ADHD symptoms at the age of 7 [19]. Furthermore, the risk of developing ADHD symptoms is higher when early childcare experiences are negative [20]. Accordingly, home environmental interventions are urgently needed for both parents and children to reduce the risk of developing ADHD and obtain positive outcomes.

One effective approach of home environmental improvement that continues to be explored is behavioral parent training (BPT) interventions, which have greater evidence of effectiveness with preschoolers than for children of other ages [21]. BPT is based on social learning principles [22] and assists parents in adopting effective parenting strategies that promote positive behaviors in children, by using group-based parent-training (gBPT) [23], Parent–Child Interaction Therapy (PCIT) [24, 25], or the Positive Parenting Program (Triple P) [26]. In BPT interventions, parents learn certain parenting skills to handle and solve problematic behaviors, improve family functioning, and modify the behavior of children with ADHD [27], which in turn lead to a decrease in children's behavioral problems, and an increase in caregivers’ self-efficacy on parenting. Compared with individual parent interventions, gBPT for parent behavioral improvements are more promising considering the time and labor input from both health professionals and parents [28].

Behavioral parent training interventions in different cultural contexts

BPT interventions are considered first-line treatment and have been widely verified to be effective for parents of children with ADHD in the USA, including improvements in child behavior [16], parent–child relationship [29], family quality of life [30], and lower parenting stress [31]. The primary objective of BPT interventions is to improve parenting skills, which are highly dependent on cultural contexts [32]. However, relative few trials have been conducted in the Chinese culture, Gao [33] found a positive outcome for BPT on reducing parents’ reports of their children’s externalizing behavior problems (F = 6.13, P < 0.05). The Group Positive Parenting Program [34] was effective with Chinese children with developmental disabilitiesa of preschool age because there were higher levels of child behavioral improvement, parental stress, dysfunctional discipline styles, and parental conflict scores in the intervention group. These intervention programs conducted in a Chinese cultural context were effective for externalized behavior problems, although they were not specific targeted at ADHD.

It is crucial that effect of BPT interventions in Chinese populations be further examined because Chinese families may experience higher levels of engagement barriers, including the scarcity of experienced therapists who can deliver quality services, and inclusivity matters. In addition, ADHD children raised in Confucian culture encounter more significant challenges regarding adjustment difficulties due to cultural norms and expectations [35]. It appears that Chinese parents are not easily swayed by the concept of “ignorance of negative behavior,” as the Chinese proverb says: “A child’s misbehavior reflects parental failure in upbringing” (子不教父之过). Therefore, to avoid publicly admitting their own parenting failings, parents attempt to use methods such as criticism, corporal punishment, or violence to quickly end children's negative behaviors [36]. Parents value a sense of authority in front of their children and expect them to be obedient, although they are not good at encouraging children to express their opinions or cultivating their independence. Researchers found that children with an authoritarian mother’s parenting style were more likely to display inattentive/hyperactive symptoms at age 5 [37]. Therefore, it is worth exploring whether Chinese parents can accept and apply strategies for improving parenting skills in interventions such as praising, active parenting, and ignoring children’s negative behaviors. To achieve positive outcomes for parents, modified gBPT interventions are urgently needed.

Behavioral parent training interventions and socioeconomic status

Meta-analyses of the association dimensions between socioeconomic status (SES) and ADHD indicate that children from low SES families are, on average, 1.85 to 2.21 times more likely to be diagnosed with ADHD compared to their peers from high SES families [38]. Research suggests that high socioeconomic stress can reduce parents' sense of control over their environment [37]. Low socioeconomic status is associated with severe ADHD symptoms, oppositional behaviors, aggressive behaviors, and nonsocial behavior in children [39].

Although interventions that assist parents and alleviate low SES-related challenges can improve ADHD symptoms in children [40], and there is evidence attesting to the efficacy of gBPT making improvements in both child and parent health outcomes [23, 41, 42], many questions remain regarding its broader applicability in families with different socioeconomic statuses. Low SES families may benefit less from parent training, and parents in low-SES families may have insufficient capacity for learning, emotion regulation, and cognitive control [43], leading to a low BPT engagement [44]. Moreover, children from disadvantaged families showed less improvement at a 1-year follow-up after treatment [45, 46]. However, a meta-analysis [47] demonstrated cognitive behavioral therapy had greater effects in reducing stress and depression for socioeconomically disadvantaged parents; BPT helped them gain greater control over their children’s home behavior. Accordingly, healthcare providers must gain a better understanding of parents’ characteristics and dimensions of cognition, concerns, and educating behaviors to offer the appropriate type of support and resources, which can ultimately empower parents to effectively manage their children's health outcomes [48].

Study objectives

This protocol aims to evaluate the intervention effects of gBPT with parents who have a child 4–6 years of age with ADHD symptoms. Specifically, it includes three core objectives:

To determine in a Chinese culture whether ADHD symptoms will differ posttreatment and at 12-week follow-up with gBPT vs. the support group (Sg).

To determine in a Chinese culture whether ADHD symptoms will differ posttreatment and at 12-week follow-up with gBPT vs. the support group (Sg).

To determine in a Chinese culture whether parents’ stress and family quality of life will differ posttreatment and at 12-week follow-up with gBPT vs. the Sg.

To determine in a Chinese culture whether parents’ stress and family quality of life will differ posttreatment and at 12-week follow-up with gBPT vs. the Sg.

To explore the feasibility and acceptability of gBPT among low SES families, and the implementation potential of gBPT conducted by health providers in a Chinese culture.

To explore the feasibility and acceptability of gBPT among low SES families, and the implementation potential of gBPT conducted by health providers in a Chinese culture.

We hypothesized that

H1: gBPT would lead to a significant decrease in ADHD symptoms among children. and parental stress, increase family quality of life, and enhance parenting competence compared with the support group.

H1: gBPT would lead to a significant decrease in ADHD symptoms among children. and parental stress, increase family quality of life, and enhance parenting competence compared with the support group.

Exploratively, this study aims to ascertain the individual intervention effect of gBPT on ADHD symptoms, as well as advance the viability and receptiveness of gBPT among families with low SES in Chinese culture.

Methods

Study design and settings

A single-site, two-arm RCT will be conducted with a 1:1 allocation: (1) guided gBPT and (2) Sg after randomization. The overall study design is shown in Fig. 1. The study will be conducted at the Children’s Hospital of Nanjing Medical University, which is classified as a tertiary class A facility and equipped with over 1700 beds [49]. The intervention team is composed of psychiatrists and psychologists with training in gBPT. The day-to-day operational team, comprising the clinical intervention implementation physicians, conducts weekly progress meetings to track subject enrollment metrics and resolve data discrepancies. This study has been prepared following the Standard Protocol Items: Recommendations for Interventional Trials guidelines (Supplementary File 1: The SPIRIT checklist).

Participants, inclusion, and exclusion criteria

Participants

Participant recruitment will commence in January 2024 and continue until the targeted sample size was got. Recruitment efforts will include lectures, posters, and leaflets distributed in kindergartens affiliated with Nanjing Xiaozhuang University and the Children's Hospital of Nanjing Medical University. Parents interested in participating in the study can register and enter their children’s information by scanning the QR code provided on the leaflets or posters.

Inclusion criteria

To be eligible for the study, the inclusion criteria are (a) parents with a child 4–6 years of age; (b) parents with a child independently diagnosed with ADHD by a licensed psychologist, psychiatrist, and/or pediatric neurologist; (c) child enrolled in preschool; and (d) low SES families (total family SES rating score below 3).

Exclusion criteria

The exclusion criteria are (a) parents recently separated or currently undergoing divorce proceedings, (b) parents with a child who has suffered any other significant losses in the last 12 months (death of a parent or sibling), (c) parents recently experienced or currently undergoing other family changes, and (d) parents with a child receiving other psychological interventions during the time of trial.

Recruitment, screening, and randomization

Participants will be screened in person, by telephone, or via video-conference for interest and eligibility. Those who pass the screening and are interested in participating will sign the informed consent. To improve engagement, incentives will be provided during the weekly interventions, including free childcare and instant consultations on WeChat. A 500 RMB gift card will be awarded to participants who attend all 10 sessions and the follow-up meeting.

When baseline data is collected, a random assignment will be made between the gBPT and the SG for eligible participants. Our randomization algorithm will use permuted block sizes of 2–4 to achieve balanced group sizes of 1:1 and maintain allocation concealment. This randomization and allocation procedure will be handled by a statistician independent of the trial, and the entire process will be hosted via the website http://www.randomization.com. There will be no stratification factors for the randomization. There will be no special criteria for discontinuing or modifying allocated interventions. Upon agreement to participate in the trial, participants will receive an opaque envelope containing a randomly generated group allocation code.

Data management and monitoring

A patient-centered remote intelligent clinical research platform will support data collection and management by TRIAL DATA company (TD) in Shanghai, China (https://www.trialdata.cn/). Data management will be performed by the principal investigator and statistician from Nanjing Xiaozhuang University. Independent data monitoring committee (DMC) is established and consists of several experts in the field of public health, which is responsible for data safty.

Primary outcome data will be assessed by teachers of ADHD children using the TD App. Secondary outcome data reported by parents will be accomplished in the hospital using the TD App under the guidance of therapists. All the questionnaire data will be will collected by TD App, which will be kept by a statistician in data files both online and on a local disk using a password, which will be backed up weekly. The group and participants will be given a unique number respectively to make sure individual information can not be identified. Once all the data collection is finished, the dataset will be locked and downloaded to be analyzed by a statistician.

Interventions

Group-based behavioral parent training interventions

Participants who are assigned to the gBPT group will receive a manual-guided consultation conducted by psychiatrists in the Children’s Hospital of Nanjing Medical University. The consultation will last for approximately 1 h and will include information about this trial, advice on methods for interacting with children with ADHD, and health-promoting recommendations. The aim is to increase parents’ understanding of BPT interventions.

Drawing from previous research, the therapy comprises 10 modules, each lasting 1.5 h and conducted in groups of 6–8 participants over 10 weeks. Additionally, a follow-up meeting lasting 2 h will be held 12 weeks after the completion of the 10th module. The gBPT will be implemented every Friday evening in the Children’s Hospital of Nanjing Medical University for 1.5 h. Interventions will be conducted by licensed psychotherapists who have completed the PBT intervention training before treating subjects. The summarized module topics are listed in Table 1 along with detailed information on the treatment program. To address the cultural challenges, we have adapted the gBPT program to better fit the cultural context of Chinese families. Specific adaptations include (a) Incorporating culturally relevant examples and scenarios in training materials. (b) Emphasizing the importance of authoritative parenting styles that balance discipline with emotional support. (c) Modifying intervention strategies to align with Chinese cultural values, such as the use of praise and positive reinforcement.

Support group

Participants assigned to the Sg will also receive consultations (1.5 h each) conducted by psychiatrists every Saturday evening in the Children's Hospital of Nanjing Medical University for 10 sessions. In contrast to the gBPT group, the provider of Sg will act only as a facilitator to assist participants in developing connections with one another and emphasizing similarities between participants [52].

Patient public involvement plan

Patient and Public Involvement (PPI) in research is described as research being carried out “with” or “by” patients and members of the public rather than “to,” “about,” or “for” them [53]. As evidenced by Selby et al. [54], PPI integration reduces trial dropout rates by 18%. Aligned with Patient engagement in clinical practice guidelines [55], Patient and Public Involvement (PPI) Plan has been incorporated, outlining the following actions: (1) Quarterly workshops with a patient advisory group will refine intervention protocols and outcome measurement tools. (2) We'll share the results through easy-to-understand summaries with pictures, using real-name verified WeChat group, including 1–2 patient representatives, PI, and coordinators, all signing Privacy Protection Agreements. (3) De-identified progress summaries will be shared with the public through the WeChat official account.

Measurements

Clinicodemographic and socioeconomic characteristics

We will collect data on sex, age, disease diagnosis, and treatment from the children; from the parents data on sex, age, family structure, religion, and family socioeconomics. Family SES will be collected as confidential research data with no sharing or analysis during treatment; it will include maternal education level (0 = primary, 1 = secondary, 2 = tertiary); total household wealth quartiles (0–3); and marital status (1 = married, 0 = other) [43]. Low family SES will be defined as a total score below 3.

Primary outcomes

The Conners’ Teacher Rating Scale-Revised (CTRS-R) is used for evaluating ADHD symptoms from teachers’ perceptions [56]. It contains 28 items covering 3 domains: Inattention, Hyperactivity/Impulsivity, and Oppositional behaviors. Each item is related on a 4-point Likert scale, ranging from 0 (not at all true) to 3 (very much true). The Chinese version of this questionnaire [57] has high validity (0.896), and the Cronbach's α is 0.941, while the test–retest reliability is 0.544 (P < 0.01). The norms of CTRS-R can be applied to Chinese children, and the scales will be filled out by their teachers in kindergartens.

Secondary outcomes

The Strength and Difficulties Questionnaire (SDQ) [58] is used for assessing behaviors, emotions, and relationships assessed by teachers. The Parenting Stress Index-Short Form (PSI-SF) [59] is a brief assessment of parenting stress. Perceived parenting competence is measured using the Parenting Sense of Competence Scale (PSOCS) [60] by two subscales, parenting satisfaction, and efficacy. The revised Chinese version of the Family Quality of Life Scale (FQLS) [61] developed by the Beach Center on Disability of the University of Kansas [62] was adopted.

Parent participation engagement includes the completion of treatment, within-session activities (sharing opinions, asking questions and active involvement) and satisfaction in interventions. Client Satisfaction Questionnaire-8 (SCQ-8) [63] will be used for measuring satisfaction during the intervention, including eight questions. The Working Alliance Inventory-Short Revised (WAI-SR) [64] provides an assessment of the therapeutic alliance (see the details of the questionnaires in Supplementary file 2).

A brief semi-structured interview will be conducted to assess the benefits and difficulties of the intervention. The interview includes three open-ended statements: “If you obtained benefits from the treatment, please explain what has been achieved,” “Please tell me about the difficulties when you applied the parenting skills from the intervention,” and “If your child has changed his/her behaviors, please tell me in what ways it has happened.” Figure 2 presents the schedule of enrolment, interventions, and assessments.

Analytic plan

Power and sample size

To our knowledge, only a handful of BPT studies have undertaken the assessment of treatment effects on an individual level, including both child- and parent-level outcomes [65]. The effect size varied depending on the outcome measurement, intervention strength and duration, the severity of children's ADHD symptoms, and so on. Previous research on BPT for ADHD has reported moderate to large effect sizes (Cohen’s d = 0.4 ~ 0.7) [50, 66]. We assume the margin of d = 0.6 effect sizes. With a statistical power of 0.80 and a two-sided significance level of 0.05, the minimum sample size should be approximately 90 (45 for each group). Chacko [67] reported in a review that among 54 studies, at least 51% of families requiring BPT withdrew before completing treatment, with a main attrition rate of 20%. Many clients discontinue treatment before the fifth session [68]. SES was a significant factor that increased the attrition rates to about 28% [44]. Zhang et al. reported a 20% intervention attrition rate among low-SES families in China. [69]. Considering a drop rate of 28%, a sample size of 128 (64 per group) is required.

Statistical analysis

All statistical data will be appropriately analyzed using StataMP 17 (Stata Corp, College Station, TX, USA) with appropriate commands. No interim data analysis will be conducted to maintain study integrity and prevent biases. The primary and secondary outcomes of BPT interventions will be analyzed using the mixed-effects models [70] with the interaction of group and time. The covariates at random include time, children's age, and baseline scores of The Conners' Teacher Rating Scale-Revised. Cohen's d will be estimated for the appropriate effect size metric. P values will be set at 0.05.

Individual treatment effects will be estimated using Bayesian additive regression trees on ADHD severity and family SES scores, using a back-fitting algorithm [71]. Bayesian additive regression trees avoid over-emphasizing interactions between variables and help find linear relationships.

Treatment satisfaction and therapeutic alliance will be analyzed using the t-test. Qualitative data from these interviews will be analyzed using thematic analysis to identify common themes and patterns related to the intervention's impact. An integration of qualitative findings with quantitative results will be provided a comprehensive understanding of the intervention's effectiveness.

Missing data and multiple testing

An sensitivity analysis will be carried out under the missing-at-random assumption, aimed at scrutinizing potential biases introduced by missing data. If the primary outcome exhibits a non-significant effect and the estimated effect size is below 0.6, an explanatory analysis will be performed to elucidate potential contributing factors to the null findings, such as poor adherence to the intervention protocol or heterogeneity effects.

False Discovery Rate (FDR) [72] will be addressed to limit the number of false discoveries when testing multiple hypotheses. The FDR will be set to 0.05, which will yield reliable, well-calibrated inferences for the study [73].

An analysis of the Complier Average Causal Effect (CACE) will be performed on the intervention arms to evaluate protocol compliance, which is the difference in outcomes between participants who complied with the intervention and those who would have complied if assigned to the treatment. “Full” participation is defined as receiving at least five interventions, which is the minimum number of sessions recommended for optimum results [74]. However, for participants who will miss certain sessions, we will provide videos for them to review, they will be required to achieve a minimum accuracy threshold of ≥ 75% on post-session quizzes to qualify for ‘full participation’ status.

Discussion

gBPT interventions have emerged as a potentially effective approach for modifying parental behavior. These interventions aim to achieve positive changes in children's behaviors, skills, and socialization. Moreover, they offer social support and coping strategies that cater to the health needs of the parents. It is crucial that gBPT be well studied in China, especially concerning cultural adaptation, socioeconomic status, adherence, satisfaction, effect size, and limitations. This study will evaluate the effect of gBPT vs. Sg for disadvantaged families with children with ADHD in a Chinese culture, which has rarely been researched to date. In addition, the results from this study will provide diverse perspectives and shared cultural experiences for using gBPT, and serve to broaden the applicability, particularly within families with low socioeconomic status. The results will be published according to the CONSORT statement in peer-reviewed journals.

This study has several limitations. First, the trial’s small sample size may limit the external validity of the study. Second, there is no blinding option for the clinical assessment, which is a problem common in BPT studies. To minimize potential biases, assessors will be trained to ensure consistency in the assessment process. Additionally, teachers will be informed about the study's purpose but will be kept blinded to group allocation. This approach aims to reduce the impact of subjective biases on the outcome measures. Third, self-report scales will be used to assess parental stress and family quality of life, and children's ADHD symptoms will be reported by teachers, whereas neurocognitive parameters and actual academic performance will not be measured. As a result, participant recall bias may occur. In conclusion, this study will enhance our understanding of gBPT and provide valuable clinical experience for the use of gBPT among diverse socioeconomic statuses in China. This study, thus, will represent an important contribution to evidence-based BPT targeted at disadvantaged families because the findings from this trial will allow us to further investigate and improve gBPT. By incorporating these cultural adaptations, we aim to improve the acceptability and efficacy of the intervention for Chinese families, ultimately contributing to better health outcomes for children with ADHD symptoms. The cost-effectiveness of gBPT and other treatments may be the focus of future research.

This study commences with participant recruitment in March 2025 and is estimated to be terminated by June 2026. We anticipate the follow-up to end by December 2026. Pre-intervention assessments are being made. The current protocol date is 2 Aug 2025, and the version is Version 3.0.
