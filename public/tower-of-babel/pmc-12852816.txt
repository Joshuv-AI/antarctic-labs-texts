Continental outflow shapes the circum-Antarctic pattern of summertime atmospheric mercury depletion zones
Authors: Zhouqing Xie, Fange Yue, Hélène Angot, Haicong Zhan, Hongwei Liu, Biao Tian, Aurélien Dommergue, Olivier Magand
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAtmospheric mercury depletion events (AMDEs) are a unique phenomenon in polar mercury cycling, involving intense atmospheric mercury oxidation and deposition that amplify marine mercury sinks. The Southern Ocean, a critical hotspot for environmental mercury exposure, exhibits AMDEs whose characteristics and distribution patterns remain unclear. This study presents the observational dataset of atmospheric gaseous elemental mercury (GEM) across the circum-Antarctic Southern Ocean. We observed pronounced summertime AMDEs that drove high spatial heterogeneity in circumpolar GEM distribution. By combining Generalized Additive Models (GAM) simulations with multi-platform observational evidence, we demonstrate that the Antarctic continental outflow largely shapes the circumpolar distribution of summertime AMDEs, concentrating these events within the convergence zones of katabatic winds. This work highlights the critical role of land-sea coupling effects in Antarctic atmospheric chemistry, and underscores the need to systematically assess the implications of these processes for mercury bioavailability across Southern Ocean ecosystems.Subject terms: Atmospheric chemistry, Environmental monitoring

Introduction

Mercury (Hg) is a highly neurotoxic heavy metal pollutant whose long-range atmospheric transport makes it a pervasive global contaminant1,2. Chronic environmental exposure to mercury can lead to bioaccumulation and toxicity, causing irreversible damage to the nervous, immune, cardiovascular systems, and even death. Polar regions are highly sensitive to environmental Hg exposure3,4. In Antarctica, the Southern Ocean serves as a critical habitat for diverse biota, where Hg entering the marine environment can be methylated by microorganisms, enhancing its bioavailability and leading to bioaccumulation and biomagnification across food webs5. Evidence of Hg bioaccumulation has been documented in a wide range of species, including penguins, seabirds, fish, krill and seals6–9. In addition, the Southern Ocean is also a major sink for global atmospheric Hg10. A recent study presented that Hg burial in the Antarctic Ross Embayment and open ocean increased three- and eightfold, respectively, over the past 12,000 years11.

Atmospheric Mercury Depletion Events (AMDEs) represent a unique phenomenon in polar atmospheric Hg cycling. These events predominantly occur in spring and are primarily driven by the heterogeneous photochemical reactions involving reactive halogen species (mainly bromine) produced at interfaces such as sea ice, snowpack, frost flowers, and sea salt aerosols, leading to the rapid oxidation of gaseous elemental mercury (GEM)12–16. This process results in a pronounced decline in GEM concentrations, enhances atmospheric mercury oxidation and subsequent deposition, and increases mercury input to terrestrial and marine surfaces, thereby attracting significant scientific attention. AMDEs were first discovered in 1995 at Alert Station in the Arctic and have since been widely observed across various Arctic sites3,12. In contrast, due to Antarctica’s remoteness, research on AMDEs in this region remains scarce. The first documented evidence of springtime AMDEs in Antarctica was reported by Ebinghaus et al. at Neumayer Station17.

Notably, recent observations have revealed that AMDEs can also occur in winter and in summer in Antarctica18,19. For instance, Sprovieri et al. and Temme et al. observed the summertime GEM depletion phenomena, along with the concurrent GOM elevations in the Antarctic Terra Nova Bay and Neumayer station, respectively20,21. Angot et al. observed multi-day summer AMDEs over the Antarctic Plateau18. Unlike springtime events, these cannot be explained solely by bromine-driven oxidation, and their underlying chemical mechanisms remain unclear18. The occurrence of AMDEs outside spring further enhances annual atmospheric Hg deposition, potentially increasing biological Hg exposure—particularly during the polar summer breeding season. However, summer AMDEs in Antarctica have so far been detected primarily inland, while observations over the Southern Ocean—the key breeding and foraging habitat for Antarctic wildlife—remain extremely limited. The characteristics and distribution of summer AMDEs in this critical region, therefore, remain unknown, hindering robust assessments of Antarctica’s Hg budget and associated ecological risks.

The Antarctic environment differs fundamentally from the Arctic, being centered on a vast, high-altitude continent with an average elevation exceeding 2300 m. This unique geography drives distinct atmospheric processes that influence Hg cycling. In summer, photochemical reactions on the Antarctic Plateau snowpack generate a highly oxidative atmosphere characterized by elevated concentrations of oxidants such as NO2, OH and RO222–24. In addition, radiative cooling along the ice sheet slopes produces strong, cold downslope katabatic winds that transport air masses from the interior to the coast. These katabatic winds can extend horizontally for thousands of kilometers, facilitating strong land-sea interactions25,26. Previous observations at Antarctic land-based stations have shown that Antarctic katabatic wind transport can influence isotopic signals of gaseous and particulate Hg in the nearshore atmosphere27,28. However, the implications of these processes for Hg dynamics over the Southern Ocean remain unclear, particularly at the Antarctic-wide scale.

To address these questions, this study presents the circumpolar observational dataset of atmospheric GEM over the Southern Ocean, obtained during the 33rd Chinese National Antarctic Research Expedition. These data reveal the spatial characteristics of GEM distribution around Antarctica, filling a critical data gap in polar Hg research. Building on these measurements, we performed observational data analysis with generalized additive model simulations to elucidate (1) the circumpolar distribution patterns of summer AMDEs in the Southern Ocean, and (2) the role of Antarctica’s unique land-sea atmospheric coupling in driving this phenomenon.

Results and discussion

Circum-Antarctic distribution characteristics of GEM and AMDEs

Here, we present the circum-Antarctic observations of GEM at a 5-min time resolution over the Southern Ocean, conducted from 26 November 2016 to 10 March 2017. The spatial coverage included the Ross Sea (150°W–160°E), Amundsen Sea (90°W–150°W), Bellingshausen Sea (60°W–90°W), Weddell Sea (30°W–60°W), Riiser-Larsen Sea (0°E–30°E), Cosmonauts Sea (30°E–60°E), Davis Sea (75°E–105°E), and D’Urville Sea (120°E–150°E). GEM concentrations ranged from 0.20 to 1.80 ng/m3, with a mean value of 0.88 ± 0.23 ng/m3 and a median value of 0.88 ng/m3. The distribution histogram shows that GEM concentrations were primarily within 0.80–0.90 ng/m3 (26.1%), followed by 0.90–1.00 ng/m3 (21.9%), 0.70–0.80 ng/m3 (16.3%) and 1.00–1.10 ng/m3 (11.5%) (Supplementary Fig. S1).

During this expedition, several summertime AMDE episodes were observed across the circumpolar Southern Ocean (Fig. 1a, b), based on the definition of Antarctic AMDEs as GEM concentrations below 0.60 ng/m3, following Pfaffhuber et al. and Angot et al.29,30. These summer AMDEs accounted for 7.6% of the total GEM observation period in this study. This proportion exceeds the previously reported 5% occurrence rate of spring AMDEs at the Antarctic Troll Station30, indicating that AMDEs represent a non-negligible phenomenon in the Southern Ocean during summertime.

The spatial distribution of GEM exhibited pronounced heterogeneity across the eight marine sectors surrounding Antarctica. The highest average GEM concentration (1.03 ± 0.23 ng/m3) was recorded in the Cosmonauts Sea (30–60°E; Fig. 2), nearly 35% higher than the lowest average concentration (0.76 ± 0.32 ng/m3, t-test: p < 0.01) observed in the Ross Sea (Fig. 2). To further elucidate spatial distribution patterns and hotspot regions of summertime AMDEs over the Southern Ocean, we calculated the percentage of time AMDEs occurred across the eight circum-Antarctic marine sectors (Table 1). The results reveal pronounced regional contrasts in AMDEs frequency. The Ross Sea exhibited the highest proportion of summertime AMDEs (29%), corresponding to its lowest average GEM concentrations, followed by the Davis Sea (5%). In contrast, the Amundsen, Weddell, and D’Urville Seas all showed AMDEs occurrences below 1%, while no events were detected in the Cosmonauts, Bellingshausen, and Riiser-Larsen Seas. These findings highlight substantial spatial heterogeneity in the distribution of summertime AMDEs across the Southern Ocean, which underlies the marked spatial variability in atmospheric GEM concentrations during the summer months.

The role of land-sea interactions in the circum-Antarctic distribution of GEM

Why do summertime AMDEs exhibit pronounced spatial heterogeneity around Antarctica? Previous studies have identified polar sea-ice zones as the primary regions for springtime AMDEs, where photochemically produced reactive bromine species at sea-ice interfaces drive significant GEM oxidation and depletion. However, 72-h backward air-mass trajectories during our circumpolar GEM observations reveal that low GEM concentrations predominantly corresponded to air masses originating from the Antarctic continent rather than from marine regions (Supplementary Fig. S2). This finding is further supported by Concentration-Weighted Trajectory (CWT) analysis, which shows that GEM-depleted air masses (CWT values < 0.6 ng/m³) mainly originated from the Antarctic Plateau and western highland areas (Fig. 3a). These CWT results are consistent with the statistical analysis of GEM concentrations: both mean and median GEM levels decrease progressively with increasing mean terrain altitude along the corresponding 72-h backward air-mass trajectories (TERRMSL, indicative of Antarctic Plateau-originating air masses, Fig. 3b). When TERRMSL exceeds 2500 m, both mean and median GEM concentrations drop below 0.6 ng/m3, confirming strong linkages between high-altitude inland air masses from the Antarctic continent and AMDEs. In contrast, no apparent relationship is observed between GEM concentrations and local sea-ice fraction at the cruise observation sites (Fig. 3c). Across the circum-Antarctic observation period, the corresponding 72-h backward trajectories spent on average up to 35.9% over land (Traj_land), with a mean TERRMSL value of 616 m. For the Ross Sea region—where frequent AMDEs were observed (Table 1)—the average Traj_land and TERRMSL values are even higher (51.9% and 817 m, respectively). Meanwhile, due to substantial summer sea-ice melt and the avoidance of dense sea-ice areas during the voyage, the mean sea-ice fraction is only 6.9% over the entire observation period. The results collectively suggest that Antarctic geomorphological differences play a key role in influencing the occurrence of summertime AMDEs.

The results show that TERRMSL and atmospheric mixing depth (MIXDEPTH) together account for over 40% of the variability in GEM concentrations (Table 2).

Regarding geophysical surface types, TERRMSL has the greatest contribution to GEM variability (21.5%), significantly higher than that of other geophysical surface types, including Open-water and Sea-ice. This underscores the crucial role of air masses from the Antarctic continent in driving GEM variations around Antarctica. This influence is supported by the relationship between GEM concentrations and the proportion of time the corresponding 72-h backward trajectories spent over land (Traj_land) during the cruise, which shows that Traj_land typically increased to above 0.8 when summer AMDEs occurred (Supplementary Fig S3). Moreover, the partial response curve for TERRMSL shows a sharp decline in GEM concentrations when TERRMSL exceeds 2000 m, suggesting that air masses predominantly originating from the Antarctic highlands via katabatic winds are closely linked to GEM depletion (Fig. 4a). This finding is also consistent with the results of CWT analysis and the observed relationship between GEM and TERRMSL (Fig. 3). It is noteworthy that while katabatic wind transport occurred widely across Antarctica during the cruise, the most significant GEM depletions were observed primarily in the coastal areas of the Antarctic Ross Sea and Prydz Bay (Fig. 3a). These regions are “confluence zones”, located near the broad upslope areas of the Ross Ice Shelf and Amery Ice Shelf at 70°E26. In these zones, katabatic winds are intensified as air masses converge while moving from the high interior of the ice sheet, with maximum mean annual wind speeds in the lowest 100 m exceeding 20 m/s26. These zones also represent areas of concentrated northward mass flux26. Such conditions help preserve the original chemical state (e.g., concentration-depleted conditions) of atmospheric Hg as it is transported from inland Antarctica to the Antarctic coastal areas, and even to the nearshore Southern Ocean, with an influence range of ~20–60 km from the continent, as revealed by the observed GEM variations during the cruise.

Regarding meteorological factors, atmospheric mixing depth (MIXDEPTH) emerges as a key contributor, accounting for 19.2% of the GEM variation—second only to TERRMSL among all variables. The partial response curve for MIXDEPTH reveals that at very low levels (<400 m), GEM concentrations decline sharply with decreasing MIXDEPTH (Fig. 4b). Observational data further indicate that summertime AMDEs episodes are consistently associated with simultaneous decreases in MIXDEPTH (Supplementary Fig. S4a). This suggests two possible mechanisms: low atmospheric mixing depths may either trap Antarctic inland GEM-depleted air masses within the boundary layer, or facilitate the accumulation of atmospheric oxidants, thereby enhancing GEM oxidation. Notably, TERRMSL exhibits a negative relationship with MIXDEPTH, with both average and median MIXDEPTH values remaining low (~200 m) when TERRMSL exceeds 500 m (Supplementary Fig. S4b). This observation aligns with previous studies showing that the Antarctica Plateau maintains a persistently low planetary boundary layer height during the summer. Although the wind shear associated with katabatic winds can generate mechanical turbulence, the inhibitory effect of thermal stability driven by the radiative cooling of the surface snowpack typically far outweighs the lifting effect of mechanical turbulence. As a result, mechanical turbulence is confined to a very thin layer near the surface, ultimately leading to a shallow and stable boundary layer31–33. Consequently, the observed decrease in MIXDEPTH, which is likely closely linked to the transport of air masses from the Antarctic Plateau via katabatic winds, can further facilitate summertime AMDEs.

Based on the air mass trajectories analysis, GAM simulations, and supporting observational evidences, we demonstrate that the continental outflow of air masses from the Antarctic highland areas via katabatic winds can largely influence the circum-Antarctic pattern of GEM, and shape the distribution of summertime atmospheric mercury depletion zones across the Southern Ocean.

Potential mechanisms leading to a strong association between continental outflow and circum-Antarctic distribution of AMDEs

Building on the findings above, we explored the mechanisms through which air masses from the Antarctic highlands contribute to the pronounced spatial heterogeneity of GEM in the circum-Antarctic region. We observed a pronounced increase in gaseous oxidized mercury (GOM) concentrations (peaking at 201 pg/m³) in the Ross Sea region, concurrent with a decline in GEM concentrations (Supplementary Fig. S5). Backward trajectory analysis indicates that the air masses primarily originated from the Antarctic Plateau34. These results indicate that significant oxidation of GEM likely occurs over the Antarctic continent in summer, and subsequent transport of GEM-depleted air masses to the marginal seas contributes to the observed summer AMDEs. To further assess this mechanism, we analyzed three representative summertime AMDEs (E1–E3, Fig. 5) and identified three complementary processes involving continental outflow that could lead to GEM depletions:

The first process is near-surface GEM oxidation over the Antarctic Plateau and subsequent transport via katabatic winds. During the AMDEs around 4 February 2017 (E2 in Fig. 5), 72-h backward trajectories indicate that air masses originated from the upper troposphere or lower stratosphere (>4000 m), intruded over the Antarctic Plateau, and were subsequently advected toward the Southern Ocean by near-surface katabatic winds (Fig. 6a). Simultaneous GEM depletions were observed both at Dome C (Concordia Station, 75°06’ S, 123°20’ E, 3220 m above sea level) and over the Ross Sea, reaching similar concentrations (0.2–0.3 ng/m3, Fig. 6c). The 72-h trajectories at Dome C during this period remained within the near-surface layer (Fig. 6b), suggesting that GEM oxidation predominantly occurred close to the surface rather than aloft. Angot et al. reported similar summer AMDEs at Dome C driven by local oxidation18, while Song et al. confirmed through modeling that bromine-mediated two-step oxidation dominates GEM depletion at this site35. Observations of BrO levels (2-3 pptv) at Dome C by Frey et al.24 exceed the model upper limit (1 pptv) used by Song et al.35, with even higher BrO in the free troposphere. Holmes et al. also presented that the summer Antarctic troposphere exhibits elevated concentrations of reactive bromine, and high annual mean Hg(0) oxidation rates by bromine radical using GEOS-Chem model simulation36. In addition, the summer lower troposphere over the Antarctic Plateau is highly oxidizing, driven by photochemically produced reactive NOx, HONO, OH, HO2, RO2, and O323,37,38. Thus, bromine-initiated GEM oxidation in the near-surface atmosphere of the Plateau is both chemically and observationally plausible.

Supporting this interpretation, snowmass (total snow storage on land) data along 72-h backward trajectories from the cruise track show elevated values during summer AMDEs, suggesting a possible contribution from snowpack photochemistry (Fig. 5). The concurrent O3 increase observed during this event—unlike the depletions seen in springtime AMDEs—can be attributed to persistent inputs of O3-rich air from the upper troposphere/lower stratosphere and in situ photochemical O3 production from NOx emissions over the Antarctic interior39,40.

The second process is GEM oxidation in the lower troposphere over coastal Antarctica and ice-shelf regions. During the AMDEs around 1 February 2017 (E1 in Fig. 5), air masses primarily traversed the Ross Ice Shelf and coastal Antarctica within the lower troposphere. Long-term BrO observations at coastal Antarctic stations show that the snowpack on ice shelves and coastal regions is a significant source of reactive bromine41. GOME-2 BrO vertical column density maps for this period show elevated BrO levels along these trajectories (Supplementary Fig. S6). Therefore, GEM oxidation driven by coastal and ice-shelf bromine emissions, followed by lower-tropospheric transport, is another plausible pathway for summer AMDEs. In this case, due to limited O3-rich air intrusion from the upper troposphere or lower stratosphere, O3 decreased synchronously with GEM, consistent with catalytic loss via halogen chemistry (E1 in Fig. 5).

The third process is direct transport of GEM-depleted air from the upper troposphere/stratosphere. During the AMDEs around 13 February 2017 (E3 in Fig. 5), trajectories indicate that air masses from the Antarctic upper troposphere/lower stratosphere were transported at high altitudes toward the coast and then to the observation site via katabatic winds (Supplementary Fig. S7). Brooks et al. reported elevated gaseous oxidized mercury (GOM) at the South Pole linked to vertical transport from the Antarctic Plateau42. Holmes et al. used the GEOS-Chem model to analyze summer GEM depletion events at the Antarctic Neumayer Station, which were accompanied by increases in GOM and O3. They attributed these events to bromine oxidation of Hg(0) in the Antarctic troposphere and the subsequent subsidence of the produced GOM and O336. In addition, Frey et al. also observed higher BrO in the free troposphere than near the surface24. Thus, GEM oxidation in the upper troposphere followed by large-scale transport of GEM-depleted air to the Southern Ocean is also likely. The lack of O3 depletion during this event can again be explained by the continuous supply of O3-rich air from the upper layers.

In summary, our combined observations and trajectory analyses suggest that summer GEM depletion over the Southern Ocean results from bromine-initiated oxidation occurring across multiple Antarctic environments: (1) the near-surface atmosphere of the Plateau, (2) coastal and ice-shelf regions, and (3) the upper troposphere/lower stratosphere. The depleted air masses are then advected to the Southern Ocean via upper-level intrusions and katabatic winds. Reactive bromine likely originates from both snowpack emissions and upper-atmospheric inputs. The variable O3 behavior during AMDEs reflects the changing contributions from bromine-driven O3 loss, and O3 replenishment from O3-rich air intrusions and in situ photochemical production. These processes collectively explain the observed summer AMDEs and the associated O3 response in a consistent framework.

It is noteworthy that Shah et al. proposed a new chemical mechanism for atmospheric mercury cycling and incorporated it into the GEOS-Chem global atmospheric chemistry model for comparison with observational data43. Their study revealed that Br and OH contribute comparably to the global net oxidation of Hg(0) to Hg(II), while O3 serves as the primary oxidant for Hg(I), facilitating efficient Hg(0) oxidation via OH43. However, Shah et al. did not perform region-specific simulations for Antarctica. Given the inflow of upper tropospheric air masses over the Antarctic continent, along with active photochemical processes occurring on the snowpack surface during summer—which can enhance O3 and OH concentrations—the relevance of this chemical mechanism in the Antarctic region merits further investigation.

Implications for the Antarctic Hg cycle

As noted above, GEM on the Antarctic continent undergoes significant photochemical oxidation during the summer polar day, forming atmospheric oxidized Hg (Hg(II)), which has higher water solubility and a shorter atmospheric residence time. Once produced, Hg(II) can be transported to the surrounding Southern Ocean via katabatic winds, where high humidity and increased precipitation in the marine atmosphere promote its deposition. These processes suggest that the Antarctic continent acts as a significant source of atmospheric Hg(II) in summer, while the adjacent Southern Ocean serves as an important sink. Katabatic winds play a critical role as a transport pathway connecting these sources and sink regions.

It is noteworthy that summer coincides with the breeding season and that the Southern Ocean serves as a vital habitat for Antarctic species, including krill, penguins, whales, and seals. Key colonies of Adélie and emperor penguins, such as those in Victoria Land and Amanda Bay, are located near the Ross Sea—the world’s largest marine protected area—and Prydz Bay, areas identified in this study as typical confluence zones of katabatic winds and sites of significant GEM depletion26. Additionally, spatial analyses of Hg concentrations in Adélie penguin feathers have shown the highest Hg levels in the Ross Sea region44.

These findings suggest that the transport of Hg(II) from the Antarctic interior via katabatic winds may influence Hg exposure levels in high-latitude Southern Ocean ecosystems during critical breeding periods. Furthermore, based on the circumpolar GEM observations of this study, we emphasize the potential of Antarctic land-sea interactions to impact atmospheric chemistry in the nearshore Southern Ocean, warranting further investigation.

Methods

Study site

We conducted continuous circum-Antarctic observations of GEM over the Southern Ocean from November 26, 2016, to March 10, 2017, as part of the 33rd Chinese National Antarctic Research Expedition aboard the icebreaker R/V Xuelong. The study area encompassed most of the seas surrounding the Antarctic continent and was divided into eight regions: the Ross Sea (150°W–160°E), Amundsen Sea (90°W–150°W), Bellingshausen Sea (60°W–90°W), Weddell Sea (30°W–60°W), Riiser-Larsen Sea (0°E–30°E), Cosmonauts Sea (30°E–60°E), the Davis Sea (75°E–105°E), and D’Urville Sea (120°E–150°E). These regions span a latitude range exceeding 20 degrees (58°S–79°S). The observations encompassed diverse hydrological environments, including coastal Antarctic areas, sea-ice zones, and open waters. The cruise track is shown in Fig. 1.

Experimental methods

Atmospheric GEM was measured using a TekranTM 2537B Hg analyzer based on the principle of cold vapor atomic fluorescence spectroscopy (CVAFS). Detailed descriptions of the methodology can be found in Yue et al.45 and are only briefly presented here. The air inlet for the Hg analyzer was mounted at the front of the R/V Xuelong (~30 m above the sea surface) to minimize contamination from the ship’s exhaust plume. Atmospheric Hg was continuously collected on two gold cartridges operating alternately at a constant flow rate of 1 L·min−1, thermally desorbed at 550 °C, and detected via CVAFS. The inlet system was equipped with two soda lime tubes and two 0.45-μm Teflon filters to remove moisture and coarse particles. Daily calibration of the analyzer was conducted using an internal Hg permeation source, while external calibrations were performed via manual injections of known GEM concentrations using a TekranTM 2505 unit before and after the cruise. Both external calibrations demonstrated accuracy exceeding 94%. The detection limit (DL) of the TekranTM 2537B instrument for GEM measurement is below 0.10 ng·m−3.

Continuous measurements of Gaseous Oxidized Mercury (GOM) were carried out during the subsequent 34rd Chinese National Antarctic Research Expedition using an automated mercury speciation system. The system comprised a TekranTM 2537B Hg analyzer coupled with a TekranTM 1130 GOM unit. Operationally, GOM was defined as the fraction of oxidized mercury captured by a KCl-coated annular quartz denuder. Measurements were taken at a 2-h temporal resolution. The sampling flow rate for GOM was maintained at 7 L·min⁻¹, and coarse particles larger than 2.5 μm were removed at the inlet using a heated impactor. The method detection limit (DL) for GOM was estimated to be below 0.89 pg·m⁻³ based on sampling volumes. Although no calibration standards are available for GOM, its 1σ precision is ~15%46. More details of this method can be found elsewhere46.

Laurier et al.47 and Beck et al.48 have shown that contamination from vessel emissions typically occurs when the apparent wind direction angle (AWD) falls within the range of 120°–240°. In this study, 91% of the AWD values were outside this range, and most instances of elevated GEM concentrations corresponded to sparse AWD points (Supplementary Fig. S8a). In addition, no correlation was observed between GEM and CO levels, with elevated CO generally corresponding to low GEM concentrations (Supplementary Fig. S8b). These findings suggest that the impact of vessel exhaust emissions on GEM measurements during this cruise was minimal.

Other ancillary data and analysis

Ozone (O3) and carbon monoxide (CO) were continuously measured using a Thermo FisherTM 49i O3 analyzer and a Thermo FisherTM 48i CO analyzer, respectively. Navigation parameters were obtained from onboard monitoring systems.

Hourly data for sea-ice fraction (the proportion of sea-ice coverage per grid cell, Sea-ice), the land‒water–ice index (LWI), solar radiation (SUNFLUX, W/m2), and snowmass (total snow storage on land, kg/m2) were sourced from the assimilated meteorological dataset of the Goddard Earth Observing System (GEOS)—the second Modern-Era Retrospective Analysis for Research and Applications (MERRA-2), with a horizontal resolution of 2° × 2.5°. The HYSPLIT transport and dispersion model from the NOAA-Air Resources Laboratory, driven by Global Data Assimilation System (GDAS) meteorological data (1° × 1°), was used to generate 72-h backwards trajectories of air masses for the entire cruise. Considering that the GEM observations in this study were conducted at ~35 m above sea level, the height of the backward trajectory was set to 50 m to approximate the observation altitude. A total of 1648 trajectories were generated, enabling detailed statistical analyses of the air mass characteristics. These included the average values of land surface altitude (TERRMSL, m), land coverage fraction (Traj_Land), relative humidity (RH), air temperature (Airtemp, K), atmospheric mixing depth (MIXDEPTH, m), wind speed (WS, m), atmospheric pressure (P, hPa), and snowmass (total snow storage on land, kg/m2) encountered along each trajectory arriving at the ship location.

Atmospheric GEM measurements obtained at Concordia Station on the Antarctic Plateau were used to support the hypothesis of GEM-depleted air masses originating from inland regions (Fig. 6c). As detailed in previous studies18,30, continuous monitoring was carried out using a Tekran 2537 automated analyzer following strict quality assurance and control procedures.

The results effectively captured GEM variability throughout the cruise, indicating good model performance (Supplementary Fig. S9a). Both predictor categories satisfied the AIC (Supplementary Fig. S10d) and demonstrated signification contributions (p < 0.001).

Model validation

The reliability of the GAM was systematically evaluated using multiple methods. First, a 5-fold cross-validation test was conducted to assess the model’s accuracy51. In this test, four subsets were used to fit the model, while the remaining subset was reserved for prediction. This process was repeated 5 times to ensure each subset was tested50. The results showed good agreement between the GAM and the cross-validated predictions (slope = 0.97, R2 = 0.94), demonstrating the model’s robustness (Supplementary Fig. S9b)52. Additionally, the following diagnostic methods were applied to test key assumptions of the GAM-homogeneity, normality, and independence: (1) quantile‒quantile (Q‒Q) plots comparing sampling quantiles against theoretical quantiles, (2) scatterplots of residuals against linear predictors to assess the distribution and trend of residuals, and (3) histograms of residuals to evaluate the normality of residuals49,50.

The Q‒Q plot indicated that the GAM performed well around the average GEM concentration (Supplementary Fig. S10a). The histogram of residuals closely resembled a normal distribution, indicating that model errors were random (Supplementary Fig. S10b). Furthermore, the scatterplot of residuals vs. linear predictors showed that residuals were generally centered around zero, with a random distribution and no discernible trend, suggesting unbiased predictions (Supplementary Fig. S10c). Overall, these analyses confirmed the GAM’s reliability and the appropriateness of the selected predictor variables.
