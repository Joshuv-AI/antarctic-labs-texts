Universality of Rényi Entropy in Conformal Field Theory
Yuya Kusuki, Hirosi Ooguri, Sridip Pal

Abstract
We use the thermal effective theory to prove that, for the vacuum state in any conformal field theory in d d dimensions, the n n -th Rényi entropy S A ( n ) S_{A}^{(n)} behaves as S A ( n ) = f ( 2 ​ π ​ n ) d − 1 ​ Area ⁡ ( ∂ A ) ( d − 2 ) ​ ϵ d − 2 ​ ( 1 + O ⁡ ( n ) ) S_{A}^{(n)}=\frac{f}{(2\pi n)^{d-1}}\frac{{\rm Area}(\partial A)}{(d-2)\epsilon^{d-2}}\left(1+O(n)\right) in the n → 0 n\rightarrow 0 limit when the boundary of the entanglement domain A A is spherical with the UV cutoff ϵ \epsilon . The theory dependence is encapsulated in the cosmological constant f f in the thermal effective action. Using this result, we estimate the density of states for large eigenvalues of the modular Hamiltonian for the domain A A . In two dimensions, we can use the hot spot idea to derive more powerful formulas valid for arbitrary positive n n . We discuss the difference between two and higher dimensions and clarify the applicability of the hot spot idea. We also use the thermal effective theory to derive an analog of the Cardy formula for boundary operators in higher dimensions.

I
Introduction and Summary

In conformal field theory (CFT) there are universal formulas that describe how physical quantities depend on the theory. Arguably, the most famous example is the Cardy formula for
2d
CFT
[
1
]
, which estimates the density of states
\rho(\Delta)
as a function of the conformal dimension
\Delta
as,

\displaystyle\int_{\Delta-\delta}^{\Delta+\delta}d\Delta^{\prime}\rho(\Delta^{\prime})

(1)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{c}{48\Delta^{3}}\right)^{1/4}\exp\left(2\pi\sqrt{\frac{c}{3}\Delta}+O(1)\right),

where
c
is the central charge of the theory. The formula is valid for
\Delta\gg c
if we choose
\delta=O(1)

[
2
,
3
]
.

The method of thermal effective theory introduced in
[
4
,
5
,
6
]
and further developed in
[
7
,
8
]
turned out to have powerful applications to many problems in CFT including the generalization of the Cardy formula to higher dimensions
[
4
,
9
]
, the supersymmetric indices
[
10
]
, the asymptotic properties of
(
heavy
)^{3}
OPE coefficients
[
7
]
, the internal and spatial symmetry resolutions of partition functions
[
8
,
11
]
, their subleading terms
[
12
]
, and effects of boundaries and defects
[
13
,
14
,
15
,
16
]
. Compactifying

d
-dimensional CFT on the thermal circle
\mathbb{S}^{1}_{\beta}
at temperature
\beta^{-1}
, the high temperature limit

\beta^{d-1}\ll{\rm vol}(\Sigma_{d-1})

is described by a quantum field theory
on
\Sigma_{d-1}
with a mass gap
m_{\text{gap}}=1/\beta
. It is convenient to use the Kaluza-Klein (KK) ansatz for the
d
-dimensional metric
G_{\mu\nu}
on
\mathbb{S}^{1}_{\beta}\times\Sigma_{d-1}
as,

\displaystyle G_{\mu\nu}dx^{\mu}dx^{\nu}

\displaystyle=g_{ij}(\vec{x})dx^{i}dx^{j}+\mathrm{e}^{2\phi(\vec{x})}\left(d\tau+A_{i}(\vec{x})dx^{i}\right)^{2}

(2)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{e}^{2\phi(\vec{x})}\left[\hat{g}_{ij}(\vec{x})dx^{i}dx^{j}+\left(d\tau+A_{i}(\vec{x})dx^{i}\right)^{2}\right],

where
\tau
is the Euclidean time with the period
1
and
\hat{g}_{ij}=e^{-2\phi}g_{ij}
is the Weyl-invariant metric on
\Sigma_{d-1}
. Due to the conformal invariance in
d
dimensions, the thermal partition function and related physical observables depend on the KK scalar
\phi
only through the Weyl anomaly
[
7
]
and does not contribute to the leading terms in the
\beta\rightarrow 0
limit, which we study in this paper. Thus, we can safely ignore the
\phi
dependence. The rest of
the low energy effective action can be expanded in
the power of derivatives as

S[\hat{g},A]=\int d^{d-1}x\sqrt{\hat{g}}\left(-f+c_{1}\hat{R}+c_{2}F^{2}+\cdots\right),

(3)

where

\hat{R}
is the scalar curvature for the metric
\hat{g}_{ij}
, and
F_{ij}
is the field strength of the KK gauge field,
F_{ij}=\partial_{i}A_{j}-\partial_{j}A_{i}
. The first term measures the volume of
\Sigma_{g-1}
. When
A_{i}=0
, it gives

\int d^{d-1}\sqrt{\hat{g}}=\beta^{1-d}\ {\rm vol}(\Sigma_{d-1}).

(4)

The cosmological constant
f
multiplying to it has several important physical interpretations as explained in
[
7
]
. When
d=2
,
the Cardy formula (
1
) shows that
f
is given by the central charge
c
as,

f=\frac{2\pi c}{12}.

(5)

The thermal effective theory is also useful when the temperature
\beta^{-1}
is
spatially dependent. Under certain conditions,
which we will clarify in this paper,
leading contributions to the thermal effective action come from the regions where the local temperature is high, and we only need to evaluate the integral of the Lagrangian density in these regions.
In
[
7
]
, this “hot spot idea” was developed and successfully applied to derive the universal formula for OPE coefficients.

In this paper, we use the thermal effective theory to study properties of the Rényi entropy defined by

S^{(n)}_{A}=\frac{1}{1-n}\log{\mathrm{Tr}}\rho_{A}^{n},

(6)

where
\rho_{A}
is the reduced density matrix for a subsystem
A
.
In
[
17
]
,
it was conjectured that the Rényi entropy has the universal behavior in the
n\rightarrow 0
limit as

S^{(n)}_{A}\sim\frac{f}{n^{d-1}}\ g(A),

(7)

where the dependence of the CFT is encapsulated in the cosmological constant
f
, and

g(A)
depends only on the geometry of the subsystem
A
. In this paper, we recast the argument in Section 3.1 of
[
17
]
in the language of the thermal effective theory, which allows us to justify their use of the position-dependent temperature, to take into accout boundary and curvature effects, and to estimate subleading terms in the small
n
expansion.

In Section
II
of this paper, we verify the universal behavior (
7
) when the boundary of
A
is spherical and determine
g(A)
when
\epsilon^{d-2}\ll{\rm Area}(\partial A)
as,

g(A)=\frac{{\rm Area}(\partial A)}{(2\pi)^{d-1}(d-2)\epsilon^{d-2}}.

(8)

In Section
III
, we generalize the thermal effective theory to include effects of boundaries
and estimate subleading terms to eq. (
7
) in the small
n
expansion as

S^{(n)}_{A}=\frac{f}{n^{d-1}}\ g(A)+O\left(\frac{1}{n^{d-2}}\right),~~~n\ll 1.

(9)

As an application of the universal formula (
9
),
we study the entanglement spectrum

d_{\lambda}
defined by

S_{A}^{(n)}=\sum_{\lambda}d_{\lambda}\mathrm{e}^{-2\pi n\lambda},

(10)

in Section
IV
.
For
\lambda\gg 1
,
we estimate

d_{\lambda}
for large
\lambda
as,

\displaystyle d_{\lambda}\ {\sim}\ \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi d}}\left(\frac{d-1}{d-2}\frac{f}{\lambda^{d+1}}\frac{\text{Area}({\partial}A)}{\epsilon^{d-2}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2d}}

(11)

\displaystyle\times\exp\left[\frac{d}{(d-2)^{\frac{1}{d}}(d-1)^{1-\frac{1}{d}}}\left(\frac{f\lambda^{d-1}\text{Area}({\partial}A)}{\epsilon^{d-2}}\right)^{\frac{1}{d}}\right]\,.

In two dimensions, we can use the hot spot idea to derive a result stronger than the combination of eqs. (
8
) and (
9
), which is applicable to arbitrary
n
as,

S^{(n)}_{A}=\frac{c}{6}\left(\frac{1}{n}+1\right)\log\frac{\left|A\right|}{2\epsilon}+s_{a}+s_{b},

(12)

where we followed the prescription of

[
18
,
19
]
to impose conformal boundary conditions at the end points
a
and
b
of the subsystem
A
(see Fig.
1
)
and

s_{a}=\log\braket{0|a}
is the boundary entropy of
a
, introduced in
[
20
]
. This formula was originally found by using a conformal map to relate
S^{(n)}_{A}
to the annulus partition function
[
19
]
. The leading term in (
12
) can also be captured using the twist field approach
[
21
]
.

One might expect a similar formula for arbitrary
n
in higher dimensions.
However, it turns out that the hot spot idea is not directly applicable in such a case because of the behavior of the effective temperature
\beta^{-1}
near the boundary of the entanglement domain, as we will explain in Section
V
. This does not necessarily mean that eq. (
12
) does not generalize to higher dimensions, but it suggests that the hot spot idea has a certain limitation.
We discuss the necessary conditions for the applicability of the hot spot idea and show that it can be used consistently for
the computation in
[
7
]
and that the universal formula for OPE coefficients derived there using the hot spot idea remains valid.

As another application of the thermal effective theory, in Section
VI
we generalize the Cardy formula to higher dimensional boundary conformal field theories (BCFTs).
We end this paper with a discussion on future directions of research in Section
VII
.

Towards the completion of this work, we received
[

]
, where the conjecture (
7
) was tested for holographic CFTs using the AdS/CFT correspondence. The thermal effective theory approach in this paper is more general and applicable to any CFT, whether or not it is unitary or has a holographic description, as far as the Rényi entropy is well-defined.

II
Universality of Rényi Entropy

Consider the
n
-th Rényi entropy for a spherical entanglement domain
A
of radius
R
in a
d
-dimensional CFT on Minkowski space
\mathbb{R}^{1,d-1}
with the following metric,

ds^{2}=-dt^{2}+dr^{2}+r^{2}d\Omega^{2}_{d-2},

(13)

where
d\Omega_{d-2}
is the metric of the unit transverse sphere
\mathbb{S}^{d-2}
.
To calculate the Rényi entropy using the thermal effective theory,
it is useful to consider the following coordinate transformation
[
23
]
,

\displaystyle t

\displaystyle=R\frac{\sinh(\tau/R)}{\cosh u+\cosh(\tau/R)},

(14)

\displaystyle r

\displaystyle=R\frac{\sinh u}{\cosh u+\cosh(\tau/R)}.

After the Euclidean rotation,
\tau\rightarrow-i\tau
, the metric takes the following form,

ds^{2}=\Xi^{2}\left(d\tau^{2}+R^{2}(du^{2}+\sinh^{2}u\ d\Omega_{d-2}^{2})\right),

(15)

where
\Xi=\left(\cosh u+\cos(\tau/R)\right)^{-1}

and the periodicity in
\tau
is
2\pi Rn
.
By removing the factor
\Xi
by the Weyl invariance and rescaling
\tau
so that its period is
1
,
the CFT is defined on
\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{H}^{d-1}
with the metric in the KK form (
2
),

ds^{2}=d\tau^{2}+\frac{1}{(2\pi n)^{2}}\left(du^{2}+\sinh^{2}ud\Omega_{d-2}^{2}\right).

(16)

To define the reduced density matrix,
it is necessary to decompose the Hilbert space of the CFT into the one over
A
and the one over its complement
\bar{A}
.
Here, we perform the decomposition by introducing a physical boundary between the subsystem
A
and
\bar{A}
, placing the cutoff surface at
r=R-\epsilon
.
In the hyperbolic coordinates,
this cutoff surface is located at
u=\log(2R/\epsilon)
in
\mathbb{H}^{d-1}
, to the leading order in
(R/\epsilon)
.
In other words, the
cutoff corresponds to a regularization of the infinite volume of
\mathbb{H}^{d-1}
.

We now consider the thermal effective theory on
\mathbb{S}^{1}_{\beta}\times\mathbb{H}^{d-1}
.
Since the mass gap
\beta^{d-1}

is much smaller than the regularlized volume
of
\mathbb{H}^{d-1}
,
the thermal effective theory can be described by a local action.
In particular, we can calculate the Rényi entropy
by the thermal effective action (
3
).
Here, the inverse temperature

\beta

is proportional to the replica number
n
.
Expanding in the power of
n
,
the leading term is

-f\int d^{d-1}x\sqrt{\hat{g}}=\frac{f}{(2\pi n)^{d-1}}\frac{{\rm Area}(\partial A)}{(d-2)\epsilon^{d-2}},

(17)

in the limit of
n\rightarrow 0
and then
\epsilon^{d-2}\ll{\rm Area}(\partial A)
, and the subleading bulk term

c_{1}\int d^{d-1}x\sqrt{\hat{g}}\hat{R}=-\frac{(d-1)c_{1}}{(2\pi n)^{d-3}}\frac{{\rm Area}(\partial A)}{\epsilon^{d-2}},

(18)

is suppressed by
n^{2}
compared to the leading term.
In these and later equations, we only exhibit the leading terms in
(R/\epsilon)
since
\epsilon
is the UV cutoff.
Note that the powers of
(R/\epsilon)
are the same for both, which will become important when we discuss the applicability of the hot spot idea in Section
V
.

There are also contributions from the boundary at
u=\ln(2R/\epsilon)+O(\epsilon)
.
As we will see in Section
III
, the leading boundary contribution is of the order
1/n^{d-2}
, suppressed by one power of
n
compared to the leading bulk term (
17
).
Thus, the
n\to 0
limit of the Rényi entropy is given by the following universal formula including the estimate of the leading correction,

S_{A}^{(n)}=\frac{f}{(2\pi n)^{d-1}}\frac{{\rm Area}(\partial A)}{(d-2)\epsilon^{d-2}}+O\left(\frac{1}{n^{d-2}}\right).

(19)

So far, we have considered the case when the boundary of
A
is spherical. It is natural to conjecture that eq. (
19
)
generalizes to arbitrary shapes of
A
as far as it is connected.
In Section
VII
, we will discuss a possible way to prove this conjecture.

In two dimensions, the polynomial divergence
in (
17
) is replaced by a logarithmic divergence,

-f\int d^{d-1}x\sqrt{\hat{g}}=-\frac{f}{\pi n}\log\frac{|A|}{2\epsilon}=-\frac{c}{6n}\log\frac{|A|}{2\epsilon}.

(20)

Including the estimate of the boundary terms, we obtain

S^{(n)}_{A}=\frac{c}{6n}\log\frac{\left|A\right|}{2\epsilon}+O(1),~~~n\ll 1.

(21)

This reproduces with the
n\to 0
limit of the universal formula (
12
) for the Rényi entropy in two dimensions.
Note that the denominator appears as
2\epsilon
instead of
\epsilon
,
because to match the convention used in higher dimensions,
we follow the convention shown in Fig.
1
,
where the boundary size is taken to be
2\epsilon
rather than
\epsilon
, which is the standard convention in
2d
CFT.

As a test of the universal formula (
19
), let us consider a holographic CFT,
where the cosmological constant
f
in the thermal effective action is given
[
7
]
as

f=\frac{(4\pi l_{\mathrm{AdS}})^{d-1}}{4G_{N}d^{d}},

(22)

where
l_{\mathrm{AdS}}
is the AdS radius,
and
G_{N}
is the
d+1
-dimensional Newton constant.
Thus, the
n\to 0
limit of the Rényi entropy is given by

S^{(n)}_{A}=\left(\frac{2l_{\mathrm{AdS}}}{n}\right)^{d-1}\frac{\text{Area}({\partial}A)}{4G_{N}d^{d}(d-2)\epsilon^{d-2}}+O\left(\frac{1}{n^{d-2}}\right),

(23)

This result agrees with the holographic computation of Rényi entropy based on
[
24
,
22
]
.
This can also be regarded as a consistency check of (
22
) (We can perform a scheme independent check by comparing coefficients of the logarithmic divergences in even dimensions.)

III
Contribution from Boundary of Entanglement Domain

Figure 1:
Regularization prescription in
2d
CFT: By inserting a physical boundary of size
2\epsilon
at the entangling surface,
we decompose the CFT Hilbert space.

To regularize the calculation of the Rényi entropy, we insert a physical boundary between the entanglement domain

A
and its complement
\bar{A}
. First, we need to specify the boundary condition.
In
2d
CFT, it has been studied in
[
18
,
19
]
. Assuming that
the boundaries
a
and
b
at the two end-points of
A
(as shown Fig.
1
) are conformal, preserving
one half of the bulk conformal symmetry,
the boundary contributions to
the Rényi entropy (
12
) are given by

S^{(n)}_{A}=\frac{c}{6}\left(1+\frac{1}{n}\right)\log\frac{\left|A\right|}{2\epsilon}+s_{a}+s_{b},

(24)

where
s_{a}=\log\braket{0|a}
is the boundary entropy of
a
, introduced in
[
20
]
.

To generalize this to higher dimensions, we need to
take into account boundary terms in
the thermal effective action,

\displaystyle S[\hat{g},A]

\displaystyle=\int d^{d-1}x\sqrt{\hat{g}}\left(-f+c_{1}\hat{R}+c_{2}F^{2}+\cdots\right)

(25)

\displaystyle+\int d^{d-2}x\sqrt{\hat{\sigma}}\left(-g+b_{1}\hat{K}+b_{2}\hat{R}+\cdots\right),

where
\sigma
is the induced metric on the boundary and
K
is the trace of the extrinsic curvature
K_{ab}=\nabla_{a}n_{b}
where
n
is a unit vector normal to the boundary.
The leading contribution from the boundary effective action is

-g\int d^{d-2}x\sqrt{\hat{\sigma}}=-\frac{g}{n^{d-2}}\frac{{\rm Area}(\partial A)}{(\pi\epsilon)^{d-2}}.

(26)

This is suppressed by one power of
n
relative to the bulk leading term (
17
) as indicated in (
19
).

Let us examine the boundary leading contribution (
26
) explicitly in a
2d
CFT.
Unlike the computation of the bulk leading contribution (
20
),
the boundary leading contribution is given by a zero-dimensional integral, so no logarithmic divergence appears.
Thus, the contribution of the boundary to the Rényi entropy is simply given by a constant
2g
.
Here, the factor of 2 accounts for the fact that the entangling surface is disconnected in
2d
CFT.

In two dimensions, there are only two local terms one can write using Weyl-invariant metric, as follows:

\displaystyle S[\hat{g},A]

\displaystyle=-f\int d^{d-1}x\sqrt{\hat{g}}-2g\int d^{d-2}x\sqrt{\hat{\sigma}}.

(27)

Thus, we obtain in the
\epsilon\to 0
limit for arbitrary
n
,

S^{(n)}_{A}=\frac{c}{6}\left(1+\frac{1}{n}\right)\log\frac{\left|A\right|}{2\epsilon}+2g.

(28)

In this expression,
g
plays the role of the boundary entropy.
Investigating
g
in various examples of higher-dimensional CFTs
is an interesting future direction.
In particular, since the holographic dual
of the lattice regularization introduced here
is provided in
[
25
]
,
it would be intriguing to use this framework
to compute
g
in holographic CFTs.

IV
Entanglement Spectrum

The density matrix
\rho
can be written in terms of modular Hamiltonian
K
:

\rho=\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-2\pi K}}{{\mathrm{Tr}}\ \mathrm{e}^{-2\pi K}}

(29)

The eigenvalues of
\rho
lie in
[0,1]
. Consequently, the eigenvalues of
K
lie in
[0,\infty)
. We would like to estimate the density/degeneracy of low-lying eigenvalues of
\rho
. They correspond to the large eigenvalues of
K
. See
[
26
,
27
,
28
]
for the computation of the density of large eigenvalues of
K
in two dimensions and
[
29
]
for the holographic computation of the same in higher dimensions.

We recall the derivation of the Cardy formula for the high-energy density of states of a CFT on spatial slice
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
,
from the leading term in the
\beta\to 0
expansion
of the partition function of the CFT on the manifold
\mathbb{S}^{1}_{\beta}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
; see, for example,
[
1
,
7
,
8
,
11
,
13
,
14
,
15
]
. In other words, the expansion of
{\mathrm{Tr}}\ \mathrm{e}^{-\beta H}
in
\beta
provides the density of large eigenvalues of
H
.
Now that we have identified the leading term
in the
n
expansion of the
n
-th Rényi entropy i.e. we know
{\mathrm{Tr}}\ \mathrm{e}^{-2\pi nK}
in the
n\to 0
limit,
we can determine the entanglement spectrum asymptotically, i.e. the large eigenvalues of
K
,
in the same spirit as the Cardy formula. In particular, the (
19
) can be written as:

\displaystyle S_{A}^{(n)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\lambda}d_{\lambda}\mathrm{e}^{-2\pi n\lambda}

(30)

\displaystyle=\exp\left[\frac{f}{(2\pi n)^{d-1}}\frac{\text{Area}({\partial}A)}{(d-2)\epsilon^{d-2}}+O\left(\frac{1}{n^{d-2}}\right)\right]\,.

Here the sum over
\lambda

on the right-hand side of the first line runs over the eigenvalues
\lambda
of
K
and
d_{\lambda}
is the degeneracy of the eigenvalue
\lambda
.

The inverse Laplace transform gives

\displaystyle d_{\lambda}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\gamma-i\infty}^{\gamma+i\infty}dy\ \left(\sum_{\lambda^{\prime}}d_{\lambda^{\prime}}\mathrm{e}^{-y\lambda^{\prime}}\right)\mathrm{e}^{y\lambda}

(31)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\gamma-i\infty}^{\gamma+i\infty}dy\ S_{A}^{(n=\frac{y}{2\pi})}\mathrm{e}^{y\lambda}.\,

Because of the factor
\mathrm{e}^{y\lambda}
, the large
\lambda
asymptotics of
d_{\lambda}
is expected to come from the region near
y=0
in the saddle point approximation, where we can use (
30
) for
S_{A}^{(n=\frac{y}{2\pi})}
.
The saddle is at

\lambda_{*}=f\frac{(d-1)\text{Area}({\partial}A)}{(d-2)\epsilon^{d-2}}y^{-d}\,

and gives us with the estimate for
d_{\lambda}
for
\lambda\gg 1
as,

\displaystyle

\displaystyle d_{\lambda}\ {\sim}\ \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi d}}\left(\frac{d-1}{d-2}\frac{f}{\lambda^{d+1}}\frac{\text{Area}({\partial}A)}{\epsilon^{d-2}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2d}}

(32)

\displaystyle\times\exp\left[\frac{d}{(d-2)^{\frac{1}{d}}(d-1)^{1-\frac{1}{d}}}\left(\frac{f\lambda^{d-1}\text{Area}({\partial}A)}{\epsilon^{d-2}}\right)^{\frac{1}{d}}\right]\,.

We can express this limit more precisely if we assume analyticity in
n
.
In particular, we can use Ingham’s Theorem
[
30
]
(see Theorem IV.21.1 in
[
31
]
for a textbook exposition) to obtain the following:

\displaystyle\sum_{\lambda=0}^{\Lambda}d_{\lambda}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\left(\frac{d-2}{(d-1)d^{d-1}}\frac{\epsilon^{d-2}}{f\Lambda^{d-1}\text{Area}({\partial}A)}\right)^{\frac{1}{2d}}

(33)

\displaystyle\times\exp\left[\frac{d}{(d-2)^{\frac{1}{d}}(d-1)^{1-\frac{1}{d}}}\left(\frac{f\Lambda^{d-1}\text{Area}({\partial}A)}{\epsilon^{d-2}}\right)^{\frac{1}{d}}\right.

\displaystyle\left.+~O\left(\Lambda^{\frac{d-2}{d}}\right)\right]\times\left(1+o(1)\right).

This is consistent with the naive formula (
32
) for the asymptotic spectrum when integrated against a window function supported on
[0,\Lambda]
in the
\Lambda\to\infty
limit. A similar result has been obtained in the context of the Cardy formula in Appendix C of
[
32
]
and in
[
2
]
.

V
Applicability of Hot Spot Idea

In two dimensions,
the universal formula (
12
) for the Rényi entropy
originates from the
\epsilon\to 0
limit, rather than the
n\to 0
limit. That is why the formula is applicable to arbitrary
n
not just in the
n\rightarrow 0
limit.
Can we find a similar formula in higher dimensions?

Let us consider the following coordinates,

ds^{2}=d\tau^{2}+\frac{1}{(2\pi n\sin\theta)^{2}}\left(d\theta^{2}+\cos^{2}\theta d\Omega_{d-2}^{2}\right),

(34)

where
0\leq\theta\leq\pi/2
.
They are related to the coordinates (
16
) by the coordinate transformation
\sinh u=\cot\theta
with a conformal transformation removing a conformal factor.
This metric shows the spatial
dependence
\beta(\theta)\sim\sin\theta
of the temperature
1/\beta
, which diverges linearly
at
\theta=0
.
It was suggested in
[
7
]
that,
when the temperature has spatial dependence and diverges at some points,
the thermal effective action is dominated by contributions from the regions near the divergence, which they called the hot spot.

In the current setup, the hot spot is expected to be in the vicinity of
\theta=0
where

\beta=0
.
In order for the hot spot idea to work,
the higher-order terms in the effective action must be suppressed in the region.
With the spatial dependence of the temperature given by,

\beta^{2}=\mathrm{e}^{-2\phi(\vec{x})},

(35)

where
\phi(\vec{x})
is given by (
2
),
the Ricci scalar
\hat{R}
is evaluated as

\displaystyle\hat{R}

\displaystyle=\beta^{2}R+2(d-2)\beta^{2}\nabla^{2}\log\beta

(36)

\displaystyle+(d-3)(d-2)\beta^{2}{\partial}_{a}\log\beta{\partial}^{a}\log\beta.

Therefore, higher order terms are suppressed only if the logarithmic derivatives of
\beta
do not diverge too quickly.
Indeed, this is the case for the scalar curvature in two dimensions because of the
(d-2)
factor in the second and third terms on the right-hand side in the above equation.
However, this is not the case in higher dimensions.
For example, in three dimensions,
\nabla^{2}\log\beta
diverges as
\beta^{-2}

at the hot spot, and the scalar curvature remains finite
\hat{R}=-6
. Thus,
the higher-order terms contribute in the same order
as the cosmological constant term, and the high temperature expansion of the thermal effective action fails near the hot spot.

This is related to the fact that both the cosmological term (
17
) and the Einstein-Hilbert term (
18
) in the thermal effective action have the same power in
(R/\epsilon)
, as we observed earlier.
On the other hand, they have different powers in
n
, which is why the small
n
expansion of the thermal effective action is valid.

The fact that the leading contribution
to the Rényi entropy for arbitrary
n
can be universally expressed
in terms of the central charge,
seems to be a unique property in two dimensions.
Our result suggests that, in higher dimensions, the Rényi entropy for general
n
depends on the details of the theory (See
[
33
]
for related comments in the context of holography).

In contrast, in the set-up of the paper
[
7
]
,
\beta

vanishes quadratically in the coordinate corresponding to
\theta
, and
\beta^{2}\nabla^{2}\log\beta
and
\beta^{2}\partial_{a}\log\beta\partial^{a}\log\beta
vanishes at the hot spot. Therefore, the hot spot idea can be consistently applied there, and the universal OPE formula discovered in the paper remains unaffected by the subtlety we point out here.

VI
Asymptotic Density of Boundary States

In two dimensions, the comparison of the open and closed string channels for the annulus zero-point function gives the following asymptotic formula for the density of states
\rho(\Delta)
in the open string Hilbert space with the
boundary condition
a
,

\displaystyle\int_{\Delta-\delta}^{\Delta+\delta}d\Delta^{\prime}\rho(\Delta^{\prime})

(37)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{c}{96\Delta^{3}}\right)^{1/4}\exp\left(2\pi\sqrt{\frac{c}{6}\Delta}+2s_{a}+O_{\delta}(1)\right)\,,

The above formula is valid in the regime
\Delta\gg c
and
\delta=O(1)
.
The subscript in
O_{\delta}(1)
indicates that the error is in the range bounded by
\left[\log(2\delta-1),\log(2\delta+1)\right]
, which depends on
\delta>1/2

[
28
]
.

To generalize this to higher dimensions, consider the
CFT on
\mathbb{S}^{1}_{\beta}\times\mathbb{S}_{+}^{d-1}
, where
\mathbb{S}_{+}^{d-1}
is a hemisphere
with a
\mathbb{S}^{d-2}
boundary.
The first few terms in the
\beta
expansion of the effective action are given by

\displaystyle S[\hat{g},A]

\displaystyle=-f\frac{\mathrm{vol}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})}{2\beta^{d-1}}-g\frac{\mathrm{vol}(\mathbb{S}^{d-2})}{\beta^{d-2}}

(38)

\displaystyle+c_{1}\frac{(d-2)(d-1)\mathrm{vol}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})}{2\beta^{d-3}}+\cdots.

By performing the inverse Laplace transform with the saddle point approximation,
we obtain the higher-dimensional generalization of (
37
),

\displaystyle\log\rho(\Delta)=f^{\frac{1}{d}}\cdot\frac{d}{d-1}

\displaystyle\left(\frac{(d-1){\rm vol}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})}{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{d}}\Delta^{\frac{d-1}{d}}

(39)

\displaystyle+f^{\frac{2-d}{d}}g\cdot{\rm vol}(\mathbb{S}^{d-2})

\displaystyle\left(\frac{(d-1)\mathrm{vol}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})}{2}\right)^{\frac{2-d}{d}}\Delta^{\frac{d-2}{d}}

\displaystyle+O(\Delta^{\frac{d-3}{d}}).

VII
Discussion

In this paper, we use thermal effective theory to find universal properties of the Rényi entropy in the limit of
n\rightarrow 0
. Our results suggest several future research directions:

•

In this paper, we discussed how the thermal effective action is modified in the presence of spatial boundaries and used
the result to estimate the Rényi entropy, the entanglement spectrum, and the asymptotic density of boundary states.
We expect many other applications of the thermal effective theory to BCFT. An interesting direction may be to use it to generalize the known universality in two dimensions

[
34
,
35
]
to higher dimensions.

•

In this paper, we restricted our attention to the case when the boundary of the entanglement domain
A
is spherical. It would be interesting to see if the universal formulas such as eq. (
9
) generalize to arbitrary shapes of
A
as far as it is connected. A possible approach may be to deform the Weyl invariant metric
\hat{g}_{ij}
and use the thermal effective action to understand the responses of the Rényi entropy and
other physical quantities.

•

If CFT has a global symmetry
G
, we can turn on the gauge field and use the method developed in

[
36
,
37
,
8
]
to obtain the symmetry resolution of the Rényi entropy
[
38
,
39
,
40
,
41
]
, as

\lim_{n\rightarrow 0}\left(S^{(n)}_{A}(r)-S^{(n)}_{A}\right)=\log\frac{d_{r}^{2}}{\left|G\right|},

(40)

where
S^{(n)}_{A}(r)
is the
n
-the Rényi entropy within the irreducible representation
r
,
d_{r}
is the quantum dimension of
r
, and
\left|G\right|
is the order of the group
G
.
Our result (
40
) is the first step towards the higher dimensional generalization of the results for
2d
CFTs
[
28
]
, where it was shown that
the equation holds for arbitrary
n
.
