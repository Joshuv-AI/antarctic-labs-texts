Factors associated with knowledge, attitude and practice of micronutrients among adolescents in Madhyabindu Municipality: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Tripti Shrestha, Poonam Pokhrel, Milan Thapa, Kiran Paudel, Sandesh Bhusal, Tara Ballav Adhikari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionMicronutrients, which include essential vitamins and minerals required in trace amounts, play crucial roles in various physiological processes, such as immune function, cognitive development and overall health. Several previous studies in Nepal have addressed general nutrition and malnutrition trends, few have specifically explored micronutrient-related knowledge and dietary behaviours, particularly among adolescents with attention given to micronutrients in particular. Therefore, this study aims to assess the level of awareness, perceptions and dietary practices related to micronutrients among adolescents.MethodsA school-based cross-sectional study was conducted between October and December 2022 among 755 adolescents in Madhyabindu Municipality, Nepal. The study employed multistage random sampling with a validated self-administered questionnaire distributed to participants. Statistical analyses, including χ2 tests and logistic regression, were performed with significance set at p values <0.05 and <0.001 via the SPSS V.16.0.ResultsAdequate knowledge of micronutrient-rich foods was observed in 51.52% of the participants, whereas 47.94% had positive attitudes and 42.38% demonstrated good consumption practices. Higher knowledge levels were linked to higher grades (adjusted OR (AOR)=1.47, 95% CI 0.90 to 2.37), belonging to the advantageous ethnic group (AOR=2.40, 95% CI 1.14 to 3.65), maternal education (primary: AOR=1.91, 95% CI 1.06 to 3.47; secondary: AOR=3.02, 95% CI 1.44 to 6.32) and curriculum nutrition content (AOR=1.92, 95% CI 1.30 to 2.81). Good dietary practices were associated with ethnicity (AOR=3.59, 95% CI 1.89 to 6.79), family income (AOR=2.14, 95% CI 1.18 to 3.86), maternal occupation (AOR=1.73, 95% CI 1.05 to 2.86) and paternal occupation (AOR=1.71, 95% CI 1.01 to 2.90).ConclusionThis study highlights a significant gap between adolescents’ knowledge and their actual consumption or the dietary practices related to micronutrients. There is a pressing need for targeted educational interventions, particularly those involving parents. Enhancing school curricula to include comprehensive and detailed education on micronutrients—especially folate, zinc and vitamins C and D—is crucial for improving adolescent health outcomes.

Background

Micronutrients are essential vitamins and minerals that are required in small quantities and play pivotal roles in various physiological processes, from immune function to cognitive development.1 2 Minerals are vital for development, bone health, fluid balance, blood clotting and many other activities, whereas vitamins are required for energy production, immune system function, blood clotting and other functions.3 4 Micronutrients naturally occur in foods such as meat, eggs, milk, legumes, fruits and vegetables.5 6 Adolescence is a critical developmental phase that not only establishes lifelong health foundations but also increases vulnerability to micronutrient deficiencies (MNDs) due to heightened nutritional demands and greater autonomy in dietary choices.7 8 MNDs are caused by insufficient dietary intake or impaired absorption due to infections, disease or inflammation.9 MNDs include deficiencies in both macro and trace minerals as well as fat-soluble and water-soluble vitamins.4 6 MNDs remain a major global public-health problem: Anaemia affected an estimated 30% of non-pregnant women worldwide in 2019 (over 500 million people), with adolescents and women of reproductive age among the most vulnerable groups. Vitamin A deficiency continues to be a leading cause of preventable childhood blindness and is concentrated in lower income regions, with South Asia carrying a large share of the global burden.10 Zinc deficiency and inadequate zinc intake are also important regional problems in South and South-East Asia, where dietary patterns and high phytate diets contribute to population risk.11

In low and middle-income countries, adolescents frequently experience growth problems, such as stunting, anaemia and/or MNDs.12 The most prevalent deficiencies globally include iron, iodine, zinc, folate, vitamins A, D, E, C, thiamine, riboflavin, vitamin B12, calcium, selenium and fluoride.5 However, the most widespread deficiencies of MNDs are iron, iodine, folate, vitamin A and zinc.13 Vitamin A deficiency causes anaemia, dry eyes and night blindness, making it the leading cause of preventable childhood blindness.4 14 Vitamin D and calcium deficiencies can contribute to syndromes such as osteoporosis, rickets and bone fractures.5 6 Other water-soluble vitamins are attributed to conditions such as scurvy, pellagra, birth defects15 and anaemia.4 Mineral and vitamin deficiencies are silent epidemics that affect people of all ages, genders and risk groups. They have a significant effect on morbidity, mortality and quality of life by aggravating infectious and chronic disorders (including hypothyroidism, colorectal cancer and cardiovascular diseases), as do specific MND disorders.16 Micronutrients help in the health and development of children and adolescents during their time of growth. The MNDs in children and adolescents include iron, iodine, vitamin A and zinc.17 In Nepal, the burden of MNDs remains substantial. According to the Nepal Demographic and Health Survey (NDHS) 2022, anaemia affects 34% of adolescents aged 10–19 years, with higher prevalence among females.18 National surveys and assessments show that micronutrient problems persist in Nepal. The Nepal National Micronutrient Status Survey (NNMSS) 2016 documented substantial prevalence of iron deficiency and other micronutrient shortfalls among children, adolescents and women (eg, iron deficiency and iron-deficiency anaemia were important findings reported in the NNMSS report).19 The NDHS 2022 likewise highlights continuing nutritional concerns among adolescents and young women.18

Adolescence is also a period in which lifelong dietary habits are established.20 Owing to their heightened vulnerability, adolescents require particular attention to their nutrition during this stage, especially in the context of rapid lifestyle changes and dietary shifts.7 8 17 Multiple factors, including personal behaviour, the social environment,12 literacy level and family dynamics, shape adolescents’ nutritional status. Most notably, key behavioural determinants,21 defined in this study as knowledge,7 22 attitudes23 and practices24 related to micronutrient-rich foods, play a significant role in overall health outcomes. Nutritional knowledge, combined with a positive attitude towards healthy eating, is necessary to promote healthier eating habits at an early age.25 26 Gathering data on adolescents’ micronutrient consumption patterns is critical for promoting healthier eating behaviours and breaking the intergenerational cycle of malnutrition.1 In Nepal, MNDs represent a growing challenge within the broader context of the triple burden of malnutrition. School settings offer an ideal environment for introducing and monitoring nutrition-focused interventions, making this a key population for targeted health promotion strategies. This study explores the status and factors influencing adolescents’ awareness, perception and consumption of micronutrient-rich foods. Understanding these behavioural determinants is crucial to ensuring the health and well-being of this generation and those to come.27

Methods

Study area and design

This school-based analytical cross-sectional study was conducted among adolescents in the Madhyabindu Municipality located in the Nawalpur district of western Nepal. The local level was purposively selected from among the eight local levels in Nawalpur. This location represents a semiurban setting with a mixed agricultural and service-based economy. The population is predominantly Hindu, with Janajati and Brahmin/Chhetri communities constituting the major ethnic groups. Agriculture remains an important livelihood, with rice, maize, wheat and seasonal vegetables being commonly produced, while a substantial proportion of households rely on non-agricultural income sources, including business, service-sector employment and foreign labour migration, particularly among male household members.28 The municipality has access to basic education and primary healthcare services, including public schools and government health facilities, though variations in household income, parental education and occupation may influence food availability, dietary diversity and nutrition-related knowledge. These sociodemographic and cultural characteristics provide an important context for understanding adolescents’ knowledge, attitudes and practices (KAP) regarding micronutrient-rich food consumption.

Sampling and study sample

Multistage random sampling was used to select the adolescents from the selected schools. The sample size was estimated to be 755, which was calculated via Cochran’s formula,

n=Zα2pqd2+Zα2pqN

with a 95% CI, an assumed proportion (p) of 50%29 and a 5% margin of error (d). The sample size was also adjusted, with a design effect (deff) of 2 and a 10% non-response rate. The proportion of 50% was assumed with the aim of generating robust findings through sufficiently powered method due to variability of the actual prevalence in various studies conducted in Nepal alongside lack of such studies, especially for school-based research studies in the study area.30 A separate list of private and public schools was prepared using the updated information on secondary schools, totalling a pool of 64 schools (19 private and 45 public) obtained from the municipal education section. Primary schools and schools with fewer than 50 students in grades 8, 9 and 10 were excluded from the study. This was done to ensure that each selected school could contribute an adequate number of participants for meaningful statistical analysis aiming robust results. The final list of schools was narrowed down to 18 (5 private and 13 public). From this list, three private and six public schools were selected through simple random sampling using the lottery method to ensure transparency and minimise selection bias. The list of eligible students was obtained from official school enrolment registers through coordination with the municipal education section and the administrations of the selected schools. All students enrolled in grades 8, 9 and 10 during the study period were considered eligible. Data collection was carried out in close collaboration with class coordinators, and each school was visited two to three times due to huge sample and led to include students who were absent during earlier visits.

Development of questionnaire

A semistructured questionnaire was developed specifically for this study, guided by its objectives and informed by a comprehensive literature review. The tool drew on instruments used in previous studies assessing nutrition-related KAP among adolescents in Nepal and similar contexts.31–35 Consultations were held with experienced faculty members in public health and experts working in adolescent nutrition and health promotion to ensure content relevance and conceptual clarity. The initial version of the questionnaire was prepared in English, then translated into Nepali and back-translated into English to maintain semantic equivalence. Linguistic and contextual validation was performed through expert review by academicians and field professionals with over a decade of experience in nutrition and adolescent health. The questionnaire included sections on sociodemographic characteristics, parental information, awareness and perceptions of micronutrients and dietary practices related to micronutrient-rich foods. Each knowledge and practice item was scored dichotomously (‘1’ for correct/positive and ‘0’ for incorrect/negative responses). Attitude-related items were assessed using a 3-point Likert scale (agree, neutral and disagree), with higher scores reflecting positive attitudes towards micronutrient-rich dietary behaviours.

Pretesting was conducted among 10% of the total sample size in a non-study school to assess clarity, appropriateness and reliability. The composite Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for the overall final questionnaire was 0.75, indicating acceptable internal consistency. Additionally, internal consistency was calculated separately for each subscale knowledge (22 items), attitude (12 items) and practice (12 items) to evaluate reliability within each construct. All subscales demonstrated satisfactory reliability. Based on pretest feedback, minor wording adjustments were made to enhance comprehension and flow. The final version of the questionnaire was used for the main data collection and is available in online supplemental file S1.

Data collection

The data were collected between October 2022 and December 2022 using a self-administered questionnaire. Participants were approached through their respective schools, which had been purposively selected based on prior coordination with local education authorities. After obtaining permission from school administrations, information about the study objectives, procedures and ethical considerations was shared with class teachers, who served as focal persons for the process. Parental consent was obtained in written form for the adolescents under 18 years of age.

The questionnaires were distributed and collected under the supervision of local supervisors/class teachers during regular school hours. This school-facilitated approach, combined with clear communication about study aims and support from teachers, enabled full participation from all eligible students. Out of the 755 adolescents approached, all completed the questionnaire. The school-based, teacher-supervised distribution and collection of questionnaires, clear communication of objectives and institutional support likely facilitated complete participation.

Criteria for sample collection

Inclusion criteria

The study included adolescents aged 12–18 years who were enrolled in schools with at least 50 students in grades 8, 9 and 10 collectively.

Exclusion criteria

Primary schools and adolescents who were unable to participate due to illness or emergencies during the data collection period were excluded from the study.

Study measures

The data were collected using a semistructured self-administered questionnaire for assessing the sociodemographic and organisational characteristics and status of the behavioural determinants of the study participants after proper standardised verbal instructions regarding the questionnaire were given. The parental education was grouped into illiterate, literate, primary level (grades 1–5) and secondary level and above (grades ≥6), based on the standard Nepali education structure and consistent with classifications used in prior Nepal-based nutrition studies.36 Parental occupation responses were reviewed and recoded into broader categories to ensure sufficient cell sizes for multivariable modelling. For fathers, foreign employment emerged frequently and was retained as a separate category; for mothers, low-frequency categories (including foreign employment) were merged under ‘others.’ This recoding approach is consistent with Nepal-based adolescent nutrition studies and standard analytic practices in epidemiological research.31 32 37 The Shapiro–Wilk normality test was used to examine the normality of the composite KAP scores. Since the distributions of all three variables followed an approximately normal distribution (p>0.05), mean and SD values were used to define cut-off points for categorisation (adequate/inadequate knowledge, positive/negative attitude and good/poor practice).

Knowledge

Knowledge refers to adolescents’ familiarity with, awareness of and understanding of micronutrients, sources of micronutrient-rich foods, micronutrient functions and MNDs and was categorised into adequate and inadequate levels on the basis of a cut-off score (38.48±12.31) obtained from the Wilk-Shapiro normality test.

Attitude

Attitude refers to adolescents’ opinions regarding their perceived susceptibility to and severity of MNDs and the perceived barriers to and benefits of micronutrient-rich foods, which were categorised as positive or negative on the basis of the cut-off score (65.38±15.41) obtained from the Wilk‒Shapiro normality test.

Practice

Practice refers to the consumption patterns of various foods rich in vitamins and minerals among adolescents, which were categorised as good or poor on the basis of a cut-off score (58.33±17.20) obtained from the Wilk‒Shapiro normality test.

Statistical analyses

Descriptive statistics, including frequencies and percentages, were used to summarise the data. χ2 tests were used to examine associations between sociodemographic status and organisational characteristics and the various behavioural determinants of KAP. All independent variables were initially examined using Pearson’s χ2 test to assess their crude associations with KAP outcomes. Variables significant at the χ2 level were then entered into bivariate logistic regression to obtain crude ORs (CORs). Only those variables that remained statistically significant in the bivariate analysis were subsequently included in the multivariate logistic regression models to estimate adjusted ORs (AORs) and 95% CIs. A logistic regression model was used to estimate ORs and 95% CIs for factors associated with adequate knowledge, positive attitudes and good practices. Statistical significance was set at p values of <0.05 and <0.001. The goodness-of-fit of the logistic regression model was assessed using the Hosmer-Lemeshow test. All the statistical analyses were conducted via SPSS V.16.0. The dataset can also be assessed as part of online supplemental file S2.

Ethical consideration

Ethical approval for the study was obtained from the Institutional Review Committee of Manmohan Memorial Institute of Health Sciences, Tribhuvan University (MMIHS IRC 892; Ref 79/156). Informed consent was obtained from the parents of the adolescents in written form before the data were collected through coordination with the school principal. Prior approval was sought from the municipality’s education sector and the school administration before data collection. Written assent was obtained from the adolescents themselves in the case of adolescents under 18 years of age. Participants were clearly informed about the study’s objectives in an understandable manner, and the confidentiality and anonymity of the data were strictly maintained throughout the research process. The Strengthening the Reporting of Observation Studies in Epidemiology cross-sectional reporting guidelines was also used to ensure the quality of reporting and transparency and have been uploaded as online supplemental file S3.

Micronutrient knowledge

Figure 1 illustrates the respondents’ knowledge of various micronutrients. A majority (82.5%) identified vitamin A as an essential micronutrient, while only 27.5% recognised folate and 33.8% identified zinc as an essential micronutrient. Most respondents (84.2%) were aware that citrus fruits are sources of vitamin C, and 76.7% recognised yellow, red and green vegetables as sources of vitamin A. Furthermore, approximately 82% identified sunlight as a source of vitamin D. A large proportion (91.7%) knew that iodised salt is a source of iodine. Approximately 49% were knowledgeable about dry fruits such as cashews, raisins, walnuts and almonds as sources of iron, and approximately 34% identified asafoetida, coriander leaves and cumin seeds as sources of zinc. Micronutrient consumption practices

Figure 2 summarises the respondents’ dietary practices related to the consumption of micronutrient-rich foods. Almost half (47.7%) of the participants reported consuming meat or fish occasionally, whereas 46.8% consumed eggs sometimes. In contrast, 39.5% consumed milk and dairy products daily. One-third of the respondents consumed green leafy vegetables daily, whereas 44.2% consumed legumes and lentils daily. A smaller proportion (13%) reported daily consumption of dry fruits and nuts, whereas 23.4% consumed fresh fruits or fruit juices daily. Additionally, 42.2% sometimes included lemon with their meals.

Knowledge, attitude and practice distributions

Figure 3 presents KAP related to micronutrient-rich foods among adolescents. The chart differentiates between ‘good’ and ‘poor’ ratings in each category. The prevalence of adequate knowledge regarding micronutrients, sources of micronutrients, their importance and problems associated with deficiencies was 51.52% (95% CI 47.95 to 55.09). Similarly, positive attitudes towards micronutrient-rich foods were reported by 47.94% (95% CI 44.37 to 51.51) of the adolescents. The practices associated with the consumption of these foods were found to be good for 42.38% (95% CI 38.85 to 45.91) of the respondents.

Factors associated with KAP outcomes

Table 2 presents summary statistics for multivariate logistic regression of behavioural determinants such as KAP with demographic characteristics. Respondents in grade 10 had higher odds of adequate knowledge compared with those in grade 8; however, the association was not statistically significant (AOR=1.47, 95% CI 0.90 to 2.37). Compared with other ethnicities, Brahmin/Chettri adolescents had more than two times the odds (AOR=2.40, 95% CI 1.14 to 3.65) of having adequate knowledge. Adolescents with literate mothers (AOR=1.91, 95% CI 1.06 to 3.47) and those with primary (AOR=2.18, 95% CI 1.14 to 4.15) or secondary education or higher (AOR=3.02, 95% CI 1.44 to 6.32) had significantly greater odds of adequate knowledge. Exposure to micronutrient topics in school nearly doubled the odds (AOR=1.92, 95% CI 1.30 to 2.81).

Compared with grade 8 adolescents, grade 9 adolescents had significantly lower odds (AOR=0.60, 95% CI 0.40 to 0.90) of having a positive attitude towards micronutrient-rich foods. Adolescents from the Brahmin/Chettri ethnic group had significantly greater odds (AOR=2.70, 95% CI 1.52 to 4.78) of having a positive attitude towards micronutrient-rich foods. Adolescents exposed to nutrition topics at school had three times (AOR=3.69, 95% CI 1.76 to 7.76) higher odds of having positive attitudes, with this result being highly significant.

Adolescents from the Brahmin/Chettri ethnic groups exhibited over three times (AOR of 3.59, 95% CI 1.89 to 6.79) higher odds of good practices associated with the consumption of foods with micronutrients. Adolescents from families in the income bracket $353.53–$471.36 were over two times (AOR=2.14, 95% CI 1.18 to 3.86) more likely to practice well than those with a monthly family income ≤ $117.84. Adolescents whose mothers and fathers were involved in some kind of business and service sector were 1.73 times (AOR=1.73, 95% CI 1.05 to 2.86) and 1.71 times (AOR=1.71, 95% CI 1.01 to 2.90) more likely to have good practice compared with those whose parents relied on agriculture.

Discussion

This study explored the status and factors influencing the behavioural determinants associated with micronutrient-rich foods among adolescents. Our findings add to the limited literature on the KAP associated with micronutrient-rich foods among adolescents, a subject often overlooked in favour of broader studies on general nutrition and balanced diets. Notably, this study revealed that only half of the adolescents had adequate knowledge of micronutrient-rich foods, with significant associations with grade level, ethnicity, maternal education and the inclusion of micronutrient topics in the school curriculum. This pattern likely reflects unequal access to educational resources, differences in parental literacy and variability in exposure to nutrition information within households and schools in the local context. Moreover, approximately 48% of the adolescents exhibited a positive attitude toward consuming micronutrient-rich foods, with significant associations with grade and ethnicity. Approximately 42% demonstrated good practices in consuming micronutrient-rich foods, which were significantly associated with ethnicity, family income and parental occupation.

The proportion of adolescents with adequate knowledge is consistent with studies from Bangladesh23 and Kenya,26 which reported similar levels of knowledge among adolescents. However, this finding contradicts previous research conducted in Nepal,31 Bangladesh38 India39 and Trinidad and Tobago,40 which reported lower knowledge levels. Although Mirmiran et al25 and Bhavesh et al41 reported evidence of higher levels of knowledge among most respondents, our findings suggest that adequate knowledge was found among just half of the adolescents. This rather contradictory result may be due to the inclusion of a few micronutrients, such as vitamin A, iron and iodine, only in studies performed in Iran25 and India.41 Fewer than one-third (27.8%) of the adolescents knew that micronutrients are required in less quantity, similar to a nationwide Nepal study in which 26.04% understood this.34 Most respondents (82.5%) were aware that vitamin A is an essential micronutrient, but awareness of folate (27.5%) and zinc (33.8%) was low, echoing findings from a previous study.34 These relationships may partly be explained by the fact that the curriculum in these studies did have nutrition and micronutrients for students.

In the present study, adolescents from higher grades were more likely to have adequate knowledge than adolescents from lower grades; however, this association was not statistically significant after adjustment for confounding variables. Nevertheless, the observed direction of association is consistent with other studies linking knowledge levels to age and grade.31 It is somewhat surprising that the adolescents from grade 9 were more likely to have inadequate knowledge than the adolescents from grade 8 were. This rather intriguing finding can be explained by the inclusion of a nutrition curriculum under grade 8, which was absent in grade 9 in the particular study area. In Nepal, curriculum content is often grade-specific and examination-oriented, and students may not revisit nutrition topics in higher grades, leading to erosion of retained knowledge over time. This can be further attributed to the fact that students who had topics related to micronutrients under the nutrition curriculum included were more likely to have adequate knowledge regarding micronutrient-rich foods. These results mirror those of previous studies from Nepal42 and India,43 which showed that tailored nutrition education can effectively improve adolescents’ knowledge, attitudes and behaviours. This has also been evident in a health literacy intervention study, which clearly speaks regarding the importance of integrating educational interventions related to nutrition into the school curricula.44

This study revealed that adolescents from the Brahmin/Chhetri ethnic group were significantly more likely to have adequate knowledge of micronutrients, which is consistent with the findings of Manandhar et al, who reported higher knowledge levels among advantageous ethnic groups (AOR=1.7, 95% CI 0.7 to 4.1).31 In the Nepali context, advantageous ethnic groups often have higher parental education levels, greater exposure to health information and relatively better socioeconomic conditions, which may facilitate improved nutrition-related knowledge among adolescents. Conversely, adolescents from disadvantaged ethnic groups may face structural barriers such as food insecurity, limited dietary diversity and restricted access to health education, influencing their knowledge and perceptions of micronutrients. This research also revealed that adolescents from nuclear families (AOR=1.75, 95% CI 1.18 to 2.60) were more likely to have adequate knowledge than were those from extended families, contrary to our findings. Adolescents residing in nuclear households may receive more direct parental supervision, communication, and individualised attention regarding education and health, whereas those living in extended households may experience diluted caregiving responsibilities and decision-making related to food and health practices. Maternal education was significantly associated with adolescents’ knowledge, in line with studies from Bangladesh38 and Italy,45 where adolescents with literate or more educated parents had higher odds of possessing good knowledge. These relationships may be explained in part by the fact that parents with higher levels of education might have given more information on nutrition to their children. However, no such association was found in a study on balanced diet knowledge among Nepalese adolescents, possibly because of its urban setting.31

Adolescents’ attitudes towards micronutrient-rich foods were generally suboptimal, reflecting limited perceived importance of micronutrients despite partial curricular exposure. However, prevailing cultural food norms, taste preferences, peer influence and the increasing availability of inexpensive ultra-processed foods may negatively shape adolescents’ attitudes towards micronutrient-rich foods, despite awareness of their benefits. This result differs from the yields of investigations performed in India,39 Iran,25 Nigeria46 and Kenya26 but is broadly consistent with earlier observations from Bangladesh.38 These participants with positive attitudes may benefit from the curriculum arrangements, which included nutrition as part of study at schools.

With respect to micronutrient-rich foods, this study revealed that just over an edge of four-tenths of adolescents had good practices in which they consumed food groups as part of their meals. In the local setting, adolescents’ dietary practices are largely determined by household food availability, parental food choices and economic constraints, limiting adolescents’ ability to translate knowledge into practice. Seasonal agricultural production patterns and reliance on staple-based diets may further reduce access to diverse micronutrient-rich foods. This study produced results that corroborate the findings of a great deal of the previous works performed in Bangladesh,23 where a similar proportion of adolescents practised satisfactory diet and nutritional habits. This finding contrasts with those of previous studies conducted in India,39 47–49 Bangladesh,38 Iran25 and Slovenia,50 which reported micronutrient intake below the recommended dietary allowance, leading to deficiencies in nutrients such as folate, fluoride, calcium, anaemia, vitamin A and vitamin B12.

Adolescents from the Brahmin/Chhetri ethnic group were more than three times more likely to consume micronutrient-rich foods than those from other ethnic groups. Additionally, the odds of having good practices among respondents were two times greater for those whose family income bracket was $353.53–$471.36 than for those whose monthly income bracket was in the lowest range after adjusting for other explanatory variables in our study. These findings are consistent with the findings of different studies33 51 conducted in Nepal, which revealed that socioeconomic factors are associated with MND or malnutrition. Adolescents whose parents were involved in the business and service sector were more likely to consume micronutrient-rich foods than those whose parents were in agriculture, supporting evidence from Nepal32 and India (R2=0.07, p<0.01)48 where the initial findings suggest greater malnutrition among children whose families practised agriculture as their major occupation. The occupation of the father was not found to be associated with practice status. An association was found between paternal education and practice status, which aligns with findings from Chiplonkar et al in India.48 In Nepal, households dependent on subsistence agriculture often prioritise staple food production over dietary diversity, while families engaged in salaried employment or business may have greater purchasing power and market access to micronutrient-rich foods such as fruits, vegetables and animal-source foods. This study has several strengths. It is one of the few studies in Nepal to focus exclusively on adolescents’ KAP regarding micronutrients, addressing a substantial evidence gap in low-resource settings. The sampling process incorporated a design effect for clustering and accounted for non-response to ensure adequate statistical power and representativeness. We also acknowledge certain limitations to our study findings. This study does not address the quantity of micronutrient-rich food consumed relative to daily dietary requirements, which represents a significant area for further research. The data were collected through a recall and self-reporting methods, which may be prone to recall and social desirability biases, potentially leading participants to over-report or under-report their actual dietary behaviours and may not reflect the proper patterns of consumption of micronutrient-rich foods among adolescents. Moreover, since dietary recall relies on memory and subjective interpretation, the precision of reported dietary patterns may vary depending on individual comprehension and recall ability. Additionally, the study area was selected purposively, which may introduce selection bias and limit the generalisability of the findings to adolescents in other municipalities or regions of Nepal with different socioeconomic, cultural or dietary contexts. However, within the selected municipality, schools and participants were recruited using random sampling procedures to minimise bias at the participant level. Additionally, the focus was solely on natural food sources, excluding supplements that might also contribute to micronutrient intake. Moreover, the study was limited to school-going adolescents; thus, those not enrolled in school settings, who may have different micronutrient consumption patterns, were excluded. The cross-sectional nature of the data limits the determination of the associations reported in this study.

Conclusion

This study highlights significant gaps in adolescents’ KAP related to micronutrient-rich foods, underscoring the need for targeted educational interventions. While half of the respondents demonstrated adequate knowledge, a substantial portion exhibited negative attitudes and poor dietary practices. These findings suggest that improving knowledge alone may not be sufficient to change behaviours. It is also crucial to foster positive attitudes towards micronutrient consumption to encourage better dietary habits. Sociodemographic and socioeconomic factors play crucial roles in shaping adolescents’ nutritional KAP, with parental education and income strongly influencing dietary behaviours. Therefore, nutrition intervention programmes should not only target adolescents but also involve parents to increase the overall dietary quality of their families. Additionally, schools should consider expanding their curricula to include comprehensive and detailed education on micronutrients, particularly folate, zinc and vitamins C and D. Municipalities should prioritise the development of inclusive, community-based nutrition interventions that offer informal education and counselling for both parents and adolescents, especially in socioeconomically disadvantaged areas.
