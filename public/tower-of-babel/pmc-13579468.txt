Molecular Evidence of Autochthonous Environmental Acquisition of Fonsecaea Pediatric Infection in the United States
Authors: Maria G Segura, Brian Schwem, Rama Murthy Sakamuri, Carolyn Z Grimes, Maria Horne, Audrey Wanger, Thao T Truong, Joshua A Lieberman, Anastasia P Litvinseva, Luis Ostrosky-Zeichner, Dallas J Smith
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract Fonsecaea species cause chromoblastomycosis and phaeohyphomycosis, but links between environmental exposure and infection are poorly defined. We describe a 4-year-old boy with finger swelling after oak tree laceration in Houston, Texas. Broad-range polymerase chain reaction identified F. pedrosoi or F. monophora, and metagenomic sequencing of tree bark and soil detected Fonsecaea DNA.Keywords: Fonsecae, chromoblastomycosis, phaeohyphomycosis, fungi infection, osteomyelitis

INTRODUCTION

Fonsecaea species are dematiaceous fungi that can cause chromoblastomycosis, a chronic cutaneous and subcutaneous infection that occurs after inoculation through a break in the skin [1]. Chromoblastomycosis is recognized by the World Health Organization as a neglected tropical disease [2]. Fonsecaea species can also cause phaeohyphomycosis, a fungal infection caused by dematiaceous fungi with diverse clinical presentations including skin manifestations [3]. These diseases occur more commonly in subtropical and tropical regions and locally acquired cases have been reported in the United States, although the U.S. epidemiology is not well understood. During 2017–2021, 667 chromoblastomycosis and phaeohyphomycotic abscesses were identified using national administrative data discharge diagnosis codes, with higher prevalences occurring in ages ≥65, men, in U.S. Northeast census region, and in rural areas [4]. During 2016–2021, national hospitalization data identified 690 patients hospitalized with a chromoblastomycosis and phaeohyphomycotic abscess discharge code, with 5% having lymphoedema and a 3% in-hospital mortality rate [5].

Chromoblastomycosis usually begins as a small firm, red, or gray papules or macules that progresses to small nodules, eventually evolving to nodular, verrucous, tumorous, or scar-like morphologies, giving a polymorphic presentation that often mimics other dermatological conditions [6]. The most common skin manifestation of phaeohyphomycosis is a subcutaneous cyst, but it may also manifest as nodules or plaques [7]. Chromoblastomycosis often occurs in immunocompetent individuals, whereas those who are immunocompromised have increased risk of phaeohyphomycosis [7]. Trauma, penetrating injury, and agricultural occupation are the most common risk factors for Fonsecae infections [6, 8]. Infection can lead to fibrosis, secondary bacterial infections, chronic lymphedema, and squamous cell carcinoma in advanced cases [6]. Treatment options for Fonsecae species infections are multimodal, usually including antifungal treatment and surgery.

Systematic environmental sampling of dematiaceous fungi in the United States has not been completed, and ecological niches are relatively unknown. Globally, infections caused by dematiaceous fungi have been associated with certain plants and hydrocarbon-polluted environments [9]. Sequencing of environmental samples can provide supportive evidence to link clinical infections to their source, particularly for molds that are difficult to isolate by culture. We present a case of phaeohyphomycosis in a previously healthy 4-year-old boy.

CASE REPORT

The patient was a previously healthy 4-year-old boy born in the United States who was admitted for evaluation of suspected osteomyelitis. Approximately 1 month before admission, he struck his left fifth finger against the trunk of an oak tree while on a swing in Houston, Texas, resulting in a laceration involving the nail bed. The wound was treated at home with topical antibiotic ointment and bandaging. He remained afebrile and initially asymptomatic.

Two weeks before admission, the bandages were removed, and the wound appeared to be healing, with improvement in swelling and erythema. However, during the week before admission, the finger became progressively erythematous and swollen. One day before admission, the patient struck his hand against a table at the same site of the initial injury, after which he developed severe pain, worsening swelling and erythema, and purulent drainage from the fingertip (Figure 1).

Fonsecaea infection on left fifth finger demonstrating swelling, erythema, and purulent drainage from the fingertip. Fourth finger demonstrating healing scratches related to initial injury.

On admission date, he presented to the emergency department. Radiographs demonstrated soft-tissue swelling and findings concerning for osteomyelitis, including cortical destruction and erosive changes of the distal tuft of the fifth distal phalanx, a small ossific fragment superiorly, and diffuse soft-tissue swelling. Laboratory evaluation revealed a normal white-cell count and C-reactive protein level, a mildly elevated erythrocyte sedimentation rate (18 mm/h), and a negative nasal methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus polymerase chain reaction (PCR). He was transferred to our institution for pediatric hand surgery evaluation. Magnetic resonance imaging with contrast confirmed acute osteomyelitis. The patient underwent irrigation and debridement of the left fifth finger. Operative findings included erosion of the distal phalanx with scant purulence. Swabs were obtained for routine aerobic and anaerobic bacterial cultures, acid-fast bacilli, and fungal cultures, and tissue was submitted for histopathologic examination. Empiric intravenous cefazolin (100 mg/kg/day) was initiated, and the patient showed clinical improvement.

He was transitioned to oral cefadroxil (100 mg/kg/day) after 4 days of intravenous antibiotics and was discharged home to complete a 6-week course of cefadroxil therapy with outpatient pediatric infectious disease follow-up. Routine operative cultures grew oxacillin-resistant Staphylococcus epidermidis, which was felt to represent contamination given the clinical response in the absence of targeted therapy. Anaerobic, fungal, and acid-fast bacilli cultures showed no growth.

Histopathologic examination of soft tissue obtained during surgery revealed abundant budding hyphae on Grocott methenamine silver staining, initially interpreted as concerning for Candida species. One week after admission, the patient's finger showed partial clinical improvement, with persistent erythema and swelling. Given the absence of a definitive organism on culture, fungal and bacterial broad range PCR testing was performed on tissue from paraffin-embedded tissue. After shared decision-making, antifungal therapy was deferred pending results. Two weeks after admission, broad-range fungal PCR detected Fonsecaea monophora or Fonsecaea pedrosoi from both 28S and Internal Transcribed Spacer genes; broad-range bacterial 16S PCR again identified Staphylococcus epidermidis. Upon reevaluation after obtaining fungal PCR results, the histopathology findings were reinterpreted as pigmented mold fragments rather than yeast (Figure 2).

Grocott-Gomori methenamine silver stain of a biopsy of the soft tissue of left small finger distal phalanx demonstrating pigmented hyphae.

The patient completed 6 weeks of cefadroxil. At 1 month follow-up, the patient had moderate residual erythema of the finger but no swelling. Radiographs demonstrated widening, sclerosis, and irregular contour of the distal tuft of the fifth distal phalanx, with increased sclerosis compared with prior imaging and persistent soft-tissue swelling. Oral itraconazole (10 mg/kg twice daily) was prescribed; initiation was delayed 1.5 months after admission because of insurance barriers and family travel. As of the first 4 weeks of treatment with itraconazole, the patient remained clinically stable, afebrile, and pain-free, with therapeutic itraconazole levels. The therapeutic plan is to continue itraconazole for 24 weeks.

ENVIRONMENTAL SAMPLING AND METAGENOMICS

Bark and soil surrounding the oak tree associated with trauma were sent to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) for metagenomic testing (Supplementary Appendix). Total DNA was extracted from the bark scrapings, soil, and their suspensions in phosphate-buffered saline. The Internal Transcribed Spacer and 28S regions of rDNA were PCR amplified and sequenced using the Oxford Nanopore platform. Reads mapping to Fonsecaea species were found in both the soil (161 reads) and bark (20 reads).

DISCUSSION

Fonsecaea infections, while relatively rare in the United States, can be locally acquired and cause substantial morbidity, including in pediatric patients. Our investigation used innovative diagnostic tools for the detection of fungal DNA in the environment at the site of trauma and presumptive link to a clinical infection. Our findings support further work to understand dematiaceous fungi ecological niches in the presence of a strong epidemiological link and clinical infection.

The geographical distribution of dematiaceous fungi is largely unknown globally and in the United States, but limited data suggest some may be climate-sensitive whereas others are more widespread. F. pedrosoi infections are commonly acquired in tropical regions whereas F. monophora infections are thought to be more widespread [9, 10]. Case reports of likely local acquisition of Fonsecaea have been reported as far north as New York and Pennsylvania in the United States [11]. Although some of these cases were attributed to F. pedrosoi, molecular methods, the only reliable way to distinguish between Fonsecaea species, were not commonly used. Our case investigation adds to the limited understanding of the geographical distribution of dematiaceous fungi and supports parts of Texas may be endemic for Fonsecaea species. Previous Fonsecaea infections or causative fungi have been associated with certain plants (eg, thorns of Mimosa pudica, several species of the Palmacea family including Madagascar palm house plant, Jurubeba, Murta tree, Tucum tree, Vassourinha tree, Bacuri tree, babassu coconut) and hydrocarbon-polluted environments such as wood treated with phenolic preservatives, toxic mine waste, oil-polluted soils), but the ecological niches and association with certain plant matter of dematiaceous fungi are not well understood [9]. Our detection of Fonsecaea species on the oak tree adds to the literature of potential host of dematiaceous fungi.

Fonsecaea infections can lead to long-term complications and disability, including in young, immunocompetent hosts [6]. Delayed diagnosis and treatment can result in chronic soft-tissue fibrosis, chronic lymphedema, secondary infection, or the development of malignancy [12]. Our 4-year-old patient without underlying conditions had an insidious but acute osteomyelitis only 1 month after exposure. Early diagnosis, either via direct microscopy, histopathology, or molecular technology, is crucial to prevent bone invasion, chronic inflammation leading to squamous cell carcinoma, or refractory disease.

Fonsecaea infections, although rare in the United States, can cause substantial morbidity in infected persons and are often misdiagnosed and inappropriately treated. Our case investigation provides the first presumptive environmental link with molecular evidence to a clinical Fonsecaea infection in the United States and adds to our understanding of the geographical distribution and ecological host of Fonsecaea species. Prospective surveillance can help identify epidemiological associations with Fonsecaea infections to inform targeted environmental sampling and environmental niche modeling.
