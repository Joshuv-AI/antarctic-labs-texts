A narrative review of barrier-matched engineering of macrophages for solid tumor immunotherapy: design logic, translational barriers and clinical outlook
Authors: Jian Wu, Jiabao Dong, Yi Shen, Haoyun Yu, Miyang Xu, Jiangfeng Zhang, Wei Wei, Xiongfeng Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and ObjectiveMacrophages are being investigated as adoptive cell therapies for solid tumors because they can migrate into tissues, phagocytose tumor cells, present antigen, secrete cytokines, and interact with stromal and other myeloid cells. However, their plasticity is also a liability. Once transferred into solid tumors, macrophages are exposed to hypoxia, metabolic stress, suppressive cytokines, and tumor-derived signals that may reduce phagocytosis, dampen inflammatory activation, and redirect them toward tumor-associated macrophage (TAM)-like, tumor-supportive phenotypes. This review examines engineered macrophage therapy through a barrier-matched framework.MethodsPubMed, Web of Science, Scopus, and ClinicalTrials.gov were initially searched from database inception to 24 December 2024, with a supplementary search update conducted through 21 July 2026. Search terms covered engineered macrophages, chimeric antigen receptor macrophages (CAR-M), solid tumors, the tumor microenvironment, phagocytosis checkpoints, macrophage backpacks, nanoparticle macrophages, and stem-cell-derived macrophages. Peer-reviewed original studies, clinical trial reports, high-quality reviews, and registered trials directly relevant to macrophage- or myeloid-cell-based cancer immunotherapy were selected. No formal risk-of-bias assessment or meta-analysis was performed.Key Content and FindingsSolid tumors impose five interacting barrier layers on macrophage therapy: physical barriers, metabolic barriers, immunosuppressive barriers, anti-phagocytic barriers, and phenotypic-plasticity barriers. These include restricted infiltration through dense extracellular matrix and abnormal vasculature, hypoxia, lactate accumulation, extracellular adenosine (eAdo)-A2A receptor (A2AR) signaling, suppressive tumor microenvironmental cues, do-not-eat-me checkpoints such as CD47-SIRPα, and TAM-like reprogramming after transfer. Engineering strategies have therefore shifted from simple cell transfer toward barrier-matched design. Some approaches add tumor-directed recognition, as in CAR-M or ligand-modified macrophages. Others aim to preserve inflammatory or phagocytic activity, deliver local payloads, attenuate inhibitory signaling, define scalable cell sources, or improve release and safety testing. The evidence base remains uneven. CAR-M has entered early clinical testing, whereas many nanoparticle-armed, backpack-based, stimulus-responsive, and genome-edited systems remain largely preclinical.ConclusionsEngineered macrophage therapy should be guided by the dominant barrier layer in a given solid tumor setting rather than by the number of modifications added to the cell product. Future development requires clearer links between engineering modules, barrier-adapted translational readouts, manufacturing feasibility, persistence, safety, and rational trial design.

Introduction

Solid tumors remain difficult targets for adoptive cell therapy, despite the clinical success of engineered immune cells in hematologic malignancies (1-3). Unlike hematologic malignancies, which arise in blood, bone marrow or lymphoid tissues, solid tumors impose anatomical and functional barriers. Effector cells must reach the lesion, cross abnormal vessels, penetrate extracellular matrix (ECM) and remain active in a heterogeneous tissue containing tumor cells, hypoxic and nutrient-depleted regions, stromal barriers and tumor-associated macrophage (TAM)-rich immunoregulatory niches (1,3-6). Chimeric antigen receptor T-cell (CAR-T) therapy has made this clear: antigen recognition alone is often insufficient when the effector cell cannot enter, persist or function within the lesion (1-3).

Macrophages provide one route into this problem. They are abundant myeloid cells in many solid tumor microenvironments and can perform functions relevant to tumor control, including phagocytosis, antigen presentation, cytokine secretion, matrix regulation and crosstalk with adaptive immune cells (7,8). Their tissue-infiltrating biology makes them attractive for therapies that must operate inside complex tumor sites (8,9). The challenge is equally clear: a transferred macrophage must not only reach the lesion, but also remain functional after entering it (10).

Macrophage plasticity creates the central therapeutic dilemma. Although the M1/M2 model is an oversimplification, TAMs in many established solid tumors are enriched for M2-like, immunoregulatory and tissue-repair programs associated with tumor growth, angiogenesis, immune evasion and therapy resistance (4,5). In solid tumors, IL-10, TGF-β, CSF-1, hypoxia, lactate and adenosine can weaken inflammatory activation and bias macrophages toward tumor-supportive states (4,5,8,11,12). Engineered macrophage therapy must therefore preserve or restore antitumor function within this suppressive setting.

This review is organized around the barriers that limit macrophage therapy in solid tumors and the engineering strategies designed to address them. We link each strategy to its intended function, evidence strength and remaining translational uncertainty, so that tumor targeting, phenotype stabilization, phagocytosis, cargo-enabled engineering, cell sourcing and early clinical development are discussed as connected problems.

Barrier-matched engineering framework

In this review, solid-tumor constraints are organized into five interacting barrier layers: physical, metabolic, immunosuppressive, anti-phagocytic and phenotypic-plasticity barriers (Figure 1, Table 1). These layers include restricted infiltration through ECM and abnormal vasculature, hypoxia, lactate, eAdo-A2AR signaling, nutrient deprivation, IL-10, TGF-β, CSF-1, suppressive myeloid niches, CD47-SIRPα and CD24-Siglec-10 checkpoints, TAM-like reprogramming and limited functional persistence. Table 1 links each layer to matched engineering strategies, barrier-adapted readouts and translational risks. The framework is not meant to rank platforms. It asks whether an engineering module corrects the dominant barrier in the intended solid-tumor context. Methods

This narrative review summarizes evidence on engineered macrophages for solid tumor immunotherapy, with emphasis on barrier-matched design, tumor recognition, phenotype stabilization, phagocytosis enhancement, cargo-enabled engineering, manufacturing, safety and clinical translation. PubMed, Web of Science, Scopus and ClinicalTrials.gov were initially searched from database inception to 24 December 2024, and a supplementary search update was conducted through 21 July 2026, using terms related to engineered macrophages, chimeric antigen receptor macrophages (CAR-M), solid tumors, tumor microenvironment, phagocytosis, CD47-SIRPalpha, CD24-Siglec-10, macrophage backpacks, nanoparticle macrophages and stem-cell-derived macrophages. We prioritized peer-reviewed original studies, clinical trial reports, high-quality reviews and registered clinical trials directly related to macrophage- or myeloid-cell-based tumor immunotherapy. No formal risk-of-bias assessment or meta-analysis was performed. The search strategy is summarized in Table S1.

Biological rationale for macrophage-based therapy in solid tumors

Macrophages as tissue-infiltrating effector cells

Macrophages are professional phagocytes with diverse developmental origins and tissue-specific programs (8,13). Tumor macrophage populations include tissue-resident and monocyte-derived cells, and their states are shaped by cytokines, metabolites, hypoxia, stromal contact and tumor-cell interactions (4,5,8). The M1/M2 model remains useful for in vitro polarization, but it does not capture the diversity of TAM states in solid tumors (5,8). This matters because transferred macrophages enter a microenvironment that can alter their phenotype, location and effector function.

Macrophage-based therapy is attractive because macrophages can remove antibody-opsonized or receptor-recognized tumor cells, present antigen through major histocompatibility complex (MHC) class II, release inflammatory cytokines and chemokines, and interact with matrix, vasculature and hypoxic or necrotic tumor regions (7-10). These functions can make macrophages both effector cells and regulators of the tumor immune microenvironment. Earlier activated-macrophage transfer and IFN-γ-associated activation studies already showed that macrophage effector states could be manipulated therapeutically before modern CAR-M platforms (14,15).

Why macrophage transfer alone is insufficient

Macrophage transfer alone is unlikely to overcome the main barriers in solid tumors. Natural chemotaxis may enrich macrophages in inflamed or damaged tissues, but it does not ensure selective tumor accumulation or stable contact with malignant cells (9). Ex vivo activation can fade after infusion as hypoxia, suppressive cytokines and tumor-derived metabolites push macrophages toward TAM-like or tissue-repair programs (4,5,8,11,12). CD47-SIRPα and CD24-Siglec-10 signaling can further suppress engulfment, while dense matrix, abnormal perfusion and high interstitial pressure restrict intratumoral distribution (1,16,17).

These limitations explain the shift from macrophage transfer alone to engineered macrophage products. Engineering can add tumor-directed recognition, preserve antitumor or phagocytic activity, deliver local cues or cargo, and reduce inhibitory signaling. In this framework, each modification is judged by the barrier layer it is meant to address (Figure 1, Table 1). Table 2 compares major strategy classes by barrier fit, applicable scenario, advantage and limitation. This keeps engineering claims tied to function and helps separate durable mechanism-linked activity from passive carriage or material-induced effects (18-21).

Engineering tumor recognition and tumor targeting

The targeting problem in solid tumors

Tumor antigen heterogeneity is a major obstacle for adoptive cell therapy in solid tumors (1-3). Antigen expression can vary within a lesion and across metastatic sites, leaving antigen-low or antigen-negative clones after single-target pressure (1,2). For macrophage-based therapies, this affects tumor contact, phagocytosis and downstream activation because many platforms depend on antigen-triggered engagement (9,22). Single-cell and CAR-engineering studies support considering target distribution, receptor design and cell-type-specific effector programming together (23-25).

CAR-M and antigen-directed recognition

CAR-M is a direct example of antigen-directed macrophage engineering (Figure 2). By adding an antibody-derived recognition domain, CAR-M links tumor-antigen binding to macrophage outputs such as phagocytosis, cytokine release and antigen presentation (9,10). Representative targets and evidence stages are summarized in Table 3 and include HER2, mesothelin, carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA), GPC3, CD147, GD2, CSPG4, VEGFR2, c-MET, DLL3, GPA33 and IL13Rα2. The evidence is uneven: most targets remain supported mainly by cell-line or mouse-model data, whereas peer-reviewed clinical experience is still concentrated in HER2-positive advanced solid tumors (26). CAR-M improves tumor-directed contact, but it does not remove antigen heterogeneity or on-target/off-tumor risk.

Aptamer, peptide and surface-decoration strategies

Aptamer, peptide and other surface-decoration strategies address tumor recognition without permanently rewiring the macrophage genome (Figure 2). Nucleic acid aptamers, peptide ligands and lipid-anchored modules can be displayed on macrophage membranes to increase tumor contact (53-56). Examples include KTP-modified M1 macrophages in osteosarcoma and GPC3-, AS1411- or dual-aptamer-based systems in other solid tumor models (53-56). The appeal is modularity. The limitation is durability: surface ligands may shed, internalize, lose orientation or become masked by serum proteins after infusion. A 2026 supramolecular glycoengineering study extends this logic: adamantane-labeled macrophages recognized β-cyclodextrin-tagged tumor cells through host-guest pairing, while the labeling procedure also supported an activated macrophage phenotype (57).

For translation, these systems require direct measurement of ligand retention, functional persistence, off-tumor binding, macrophage viability, migration and batch reproducibility. They are adaptable early-stage targeting strategies, not mature replacements for genetically encoded CAR-M.

Magnetic and material-assisted localization

Material-assisted strategies shift the problem from molecular recognition to spatial control (Figure 2). Magnetic nanoparticles, photothermal particles and microrobot-like systems can be loaded into or associated with macrophages to support guidance, imaging, local activation or cargo delivery (58,59). These systems are strongest as proof of concept and may be most useful in accessible, regionally confined or image-guided settings.

These targeting and localization strategies are therefore not interchangeable. In the five-layer framework, CAR-M and aptamer or peptide decoration mainly fit the tumor-contact and engulfment interface of the anti-phagocytic barrier, whereas magnetic and material-assisted approaches primarily fit the physical barrier of spatial delivery. CAR-M is most appropriate when antigen-dependent contact or engulfment is limiting. Aptamer or peptide decoration is better suited to modular targeting, but needs direct evidence of ligand retention and off-tumor binding. Magnetic and material-assisted approaches address spatial delivery rather than antigen recognition. They may fit regional, image-guided or physically accessible lesions, but should not be presented as general solutions for disseminated or deep tumors without biodistribution data (Figure 2, Table 2).

The translational distance remains substantial. Deep-lesion guidance, cell-material tracking, biodistribution, clearance, degradation and toxicity all require direct testing (58-66).

TME-induced phenotype instability and stabilization strategies

M1/M2 concepts and their limitations

The antitumor activity of transferred macrophages depends not only on tumor contact but also on phenotype stability after infusion. In the five-layer framework used here, this section focuses on the phenotypic-plasticity barrier and its overlap with immunosuppressive and metabolic pressure in the TME (Figure 3). The M1/M2 model remains useful as a functional shorthand: M1-like macrophages are generally associated with inflammatory cytokine release, antigen presentation and antitumor immunity, whereas M2-like macrophages are associated with tissue repair, angiogenesis, immunosuppression and tumor support (4,5,8). However, TAM states in solid tumors are more diverse than this binary model suggests and vary with tumor type, spatial location, metabolic state and treatment history (5,8). Therefore, therapeutic evaluation should focus not only on marker expression but also on retained phagocytosis, antigen presentation, cytokine kinetics, T-cell activation and phenotype stability after TME exposure.

Ex vivo activation and epigenetic/metabolic modulation

Ex vivo activation and targeted modulation strategies seek to keep transferred macrophages on the antitumor side of this functional spectrum (Figure 3). Activation cocktails containing IFN-γ and innate immune stimuli such as poly(I:C) can enhance macrophage inflammatory function in murine tumor models (67,68). HDAC6 inhibition provides a more targeted example: in melanoma models, HDAC6i-treated macrophages suppressed STAT3-associated M2-like polarization, maintained a more inflammatory phenotype and improved antitumor responses (67).

Metabolic modulation extends this logic beyond short-term activation. Macrophage phenotype and metabolism are tightly linked, and tumor-derived lactate, eAdo-A2AR signaling, hypoxia and nutrient stress can weaken inflammatory function (11,12,69). Direct evidence in adoptive macrophage therapy remains limited. The translational question is not simply how much activation can be induced. It is whether the induced state persists under TME pressure without causing tissue injury. Cytokine kinetics, toxicity and post-transfer phenotype stability should therefore be reported together (70,71).

Two recent CAR-M studies make this point more concrete. SLC38A2-overexpressing anti-HER2 CAR-Ms increased glutamine uptake and enhanced phagocytosis of HER2-positive breast tumor cells, suggesting that nutrient-transporter tuning can support macrophage effector function under metabolic stress (72). A complementary in vivo mRNA-engineering strategy co-delivered CAR and IFN-γ transcripts to tumor-associated macrophages using macrophage-preferential lipid-assisted polymeric nanoparticles, generating antigen-specific CAR-M-like macrophages while helping maintain an M1-like state under TME pressure (73).

Trained-immunity priming provides a recent example of this logic. In small-cell lung tumor models, β-glucan-primed DLL3-CAR-Ms linked antigen recognition to epigenetic and metabolic reprogramming, stronger phagocytosis and cytokine production, and improved activity with PD-L1 blockade (50).

Cellular backpacks and controlled stimulation

Cellular backpacks are surface-associated materials designed to release cytokines, drugs or immunostimulatory molecules while remaining attached to the therapeutic cell (Figure 3). In this framework, their relevance is strongest when they address the phenotypic plasticity or immunosuppressive barrier by providing sustained local cues after the macrophage enters the TME (74). IFN-γ backpacks, bacterial backpacks and multilayer polymer systems illustrate this concept (74,75). Bacteria-based backpacks are notable because bacterial components can stimulate innate immune pathways, while the macrophage carrier may improve tumor-directed localization and limit systemic exposure (75).

Recent programmable backpack designs add external control to this idea: ultrasound-triggered bacteria-macrophage hybrids released CCL21 and were linked to macrophage repolarization, ECM remodeling and improved tumor suppression in solid tumor models (76).

The same modularity creates a practical bottleneck. For a macrophage-backpack product, the cell and attached material both need characterization: cargo loading, attachment stability, release kinetics, effects on macrophage migration or viability, and batch reproducibility. These assays determine whether a backpack system can move beyond proof-of-concept studies (Table 4).

Thermal, ultrasound and hypoxia-responsive systems

Stimulus-responsive systems are intended to control macrophage activation in space and time (Figure 3). They therefore connect trigger sensing with phenotypic-plasticity control. Heat-inducible macrophages can couple inflammatory polarization to external thermal stimulation (77). Ultrasound-triggered platforms can release drugs from antibubble-loaded macrophages in vivo (78). Hypoxia-inducible lentiviral systems can restrict therapeutic gene expression preferentially to low-oxygen regions, a common feature of solid tumors (79). The rationale is to use either an external trigger or a tumor-associated cue to reduce systemic activation while strengthening local macrophage function.

The limiting issue is not whether these systems can be triggered in principle, but whether activation is reliable, local and measurable in vivo. Heat delivery may be uneven in deep lesions, ultrasound must balance release with tissue safety, and hypoxia-inducible circuits must be tested for leakage and incomplete activation across oxygen gradients (77-79). Useful readouts include trigger delivery, local transgene or payload release, cytokine kinetics, spatial distribution and off-tumor activation.

Enhancing phagocytosis and effector function

CAR-M design principles

CAR-M design cannot be treated as a direct copy of CAR-T design. In the five-layer framework, these designs mainly act at the anti-phagocytic and tumor-contact interface by coupling antigen engagement to macrophage effector outputs rather than to T-cell cytotoxic programs (Figure 4). Macrophage products should support phagocytosis, inflammatory cytokine release, antigen presentation and TME remodeling. This is why CD3ζ-containing CAR-M designs have been evaluated alongside phagocytosis- or myeloid-oriented domains such as FcRγ, MEGF10, BAI1 and MerTK, as well as 2B4-DAP12-containing constructs in HSPC-derived CAR macrophages (9,10,22,80).

The CAR-M evidence base has moved from proof-of-concept engulfment to designs that combine antigen recognition with phenotype control, defined cell sourcing or altered signaling strength. In addition to HER2, mesothelin and CEA, recent solid-tumor targets include GPC3, c-MET, VEGFR2, CD147, GD2, CSPG4, DLL3, GPA33 and IL13Rα2 (Table 3). Some designs also use CAR activation to reshape the local lesion, for example by coupling HER2 recognition to CD147/MMP-associated ECM remodeling rather than treating ECM as a separate antigen target (10,22,43,44,48,80-82). M1-polarized, CAR-monocyte, integrin/FcγRI-based, HSPC-derived and lentivirus-produced primary human CAR-M systems illustrate the widening design space (27,46,82-84). Recent reviews still describe the platform as early and limited by antigen selection, persistence, manufacturing, safety and clinical efficacy (85-90). GPA33-targeted CAR-M work in colorectal tumor models adds a useful but still early example. The product was generated from THP-1-derived macrophage-like cells and showed antigen-dependent phagocytosis, M1-like polarization and tumor inhibition in immunodeficient xenografts (51).

CAR-M mechanisms beyond direct engulfment

The therapeutic effect of CAR-M is not limited to direct engulfment (Figure 4). Antigen recognition can trigger phagocytosis, but engulfed tumor material may also support antigen presentation, cytokine release, chemokine production and endogenous T-cell recruitment (9,10,26). This multifunctional profile explains why CAR-M is often discussed as a TME-remodeling strategy. The unresolved issue is durability: whether these immune-remodeling effects are strong enough to produce tumor regression without additional combinations. HER2-directed CAR-M plus PD-1 blockade and CAR-M/CAR-T coculture studies support testing mechanism-led combinations rather than generic add-ons (91,92).

This mechanistic rationale is now being tested clinically, but the detailed translational interpretation is best considered together with trial design, patient selection and pharmacodynamic evidence in the Clinical translation section.

Targeting inhibitory phagocytosis signals

Tumor cells can evade macrophage engulfment by expressing inhibitory ligands that restrain phagocytosis, making checkpoint relief a direct anti-phagocytic-barrier strategy (Figure 4). CD47 binds SIRPα and provides a prototypical do-not-eat-me signal, whereas CD24 engages Siglec-10 and can similarly suppress macrophage-mediated phagocytosis (16,17). Blocking these pathways can enhance macrophage-dependent tumor clearance, but systemic blockade may also affect normal tissues because these molecules are not restricted to malignant cells. For this reason, engineering strategies increasingly aim to confine anti-phagocytic blockade to transferred macrophages or tumor lesions.

Current approaches differ in how directly they modify the macrophage product. Some act on the transferred cell, including CRISPR-mediated SIRPα disruption and SIRPα-silencing CAR-M designs (93,94). Others act at the tumor-cell interface, including CD47/CD24 blockade, dual-targeting D-peptides and peptide-antibody assembly systems (32,95,96). Membrane-fusion systems such as ARMFUL add another route by inserting functional components into macrophage membranes (97). The shared lesson is that macrophage products may need both a positive recognition signal and relief from inhibitory phagocytosis signaling, while still balancing normal-cell and hematologic risks (98). Across these examples, checkpoint relief refers to both tumor-interface blockade and macrophage-intrinsic editing or silencing, rather than to a single modality. A 2026 ovarian tumor study adds direct support for this combination logic, reporting that CD47 antibody blockade increased the phagocytic and antitumor activity of anti-HER2 CAR-Ms against HER2-positive ovarian tumor cells (99).

Macrophage-intrinsic regulators as candidate engineering targets

Beyond receptor design and phagocytosis-checkpoint blockade, macrophage-intrinsic pathways may also shape therapeutic function. TAM-reprogramming targets such as CSF1R, PI3Kγ, TREM2 and MARCO are mainly supported by pharmacologic or antibody-mediated modulation of endogenous TAMs rather than adoptive macrophage engineering. Here, NLRP6 and MerTK are useful recent examples: NLRP6 deficiency has been linked to enhanced macrophage-mediated phagocytosis in hepatocellular tumor models, whereas MerTK-positive macrophage states have been associated with melanoma progression and immunotherapy resistance (100,101). These findings nominate pathways rather than ready-made platforms.

Their value is to broaden the engineering question beyond receptor design. Future products may need to tune intrinsic state regulators as well as tumor recognition and phagocytosis checkpoints. Each added component, however, increases the need to prove viability, retained antitumor function after TME exposure, safety and manufacturing consistency (Tables 4,5).

Cargo-enabled functional engineering of macrophages

Drug-loaded macrophages and nanoparticle carriers

Cargo loading adds a second engineering layer by altering macrophage behavior after tumor entry rather than using the cell only as a carrier. Nanoparticles, vesicles or microbial components can reinforce inflammatory polarization, improve tumor-cell engagement, provide local immune stimulation, or add controlled release and imaging functions in solid tumor tissue (53,54,58).

Several examples show this function-oriented logic. Copper sulfide nanoparticle-redirected macrophages combine macrophage recruitment with photothermal and immune-modulating effects in melanoma models. In osteosarcoma, K7M2-targeting peptide-modified M1 macrophages carrying ZIF-8-encapsulated pexidartinib increase tumor affinity while pexidartinib reduces myeloid-derived suppressor cell (MDSC)-mediated immunosuppression and supports macrophage-mediated antitumor activity (53). GPC3-targeted macrophages carrying drug-loaded exosomes combine tumor recognition with vesicle-mediated drug delivery (54). Their value depends on improved localization, tumor-cell engagement, immune activation or local therapeutic output, not cargo transport alone. Hybrid cell membrane-coated nanoparticles and macrophage-modulating nanomedicine support this delivery-and-reprogramming framework (64,102,103).

Virus-armed macrophages provide another cargo-enabled route to phagocytosis relief: macrophages carrying oncolytic adenovirus-loaded ZIF-8 downregulated tumor CD47/CD24, improved macrophage phagocytosis and reinforced M1-type polarization in preclinical breast tumor models (104).

Local scaffold designs extend this principle to CAR-M persistence and regional activity. In a 3D-printed GelMA implant, IL13Rα2-targeted CAR-Ms and SIRPα-EVs were combined to improve local macrophage viability, provide sustained locoregional release and attenuate CD47-mediated phagocytic inhibition in postoperative tumor models (52).

Bacterial loading and microbial immunostimulation

Bacteria-associated macrophage systems use microbial immunogenicity as a functional stimulus. In backpack designs, bacteria attached through adhesive nanocoatings provide sustained innate immune stimulation, helping transferred macrophages maintain an inflammatory phenotype and repolarize endogenous TAMs in solid tumor models (75). Cryo-shocked macrophage shells can also camouflage attenuated Salmonella, reducing peripheral immune clearance while improving tumor enrichment and immune activation (105). These approaches are relevant here because the microbial component is intended to shape macrophage activation, localization and immune remodeling, but they require infection-control and localization endpoints (75,105).

Controlled-release microbial systems have also become more elaborate. In one design, NIR-triggered GSDMD-N pyroptosis released oncolytic bacteria and inflammatory signals from engineered macrophages, creating a biphasic antitumor mechanism that also raises clear requirements for trigger specificity and inflammatory-toxicity testing (106).

Microbial activity is both the therapeutic signal and the main safety concern. Bacteria must stimulate local immunity without causing systemic infection or excessive inflammation (75,105). Key tests include attenuation stability, delayed release, antibiotic sensitivity, biodistribution, cytokine induction and host immune clearance. Macrophage-associated systems add a separation risk: the carrier cell may die, migrate or be cleared while the bacterium proliferates or is released outside the tumor. Translation therefore requires inflammatory-toxicity assays, bacterial tracking, release-kinetic studies, rescue-antibiotic testing and tumor-type-specific delivery routes, not tumor inhibition alone (Table 5).

Cell sources, manufacturing and quality control

Cell source is a design variable. The same CAR, cytokine circuit or cargo-loading strategy may behave differently in patient monocytes, engineered monocytes, differentiated macrophages, HSPC-derived macrophages or pluripotent-stem-cell-derived macrophages. Starting cell choice affects editability, maturation, inflammatory responsiveness, tumor trafficking, batch consistency, release time and regulatory testing.

Autologous monocyte-derived macrophages

Autologous monocyte-derived macrophages are the most clinically visible source. CT-0508 uses patient monocytes that are collected, differentiated ex vivo and engineered to express an anti-HER2 CAR (26). This route reduces alloimmune concerns and fits individualized cell-therapy manufacturing, but variability remains. Patients with advanced solid tumors are often heavily pretreated, and their monocytes may differ in number, fitness and inflammatory responsiveness. Manufacturing time also matters, so autologous products fit settings with feasible release windows and pre-treatment target confirmation.

HSPC- and pluripotent-stem-cell-derived macrophages

CAR-monocytes, HSPC-derived macrophages and hPSC/iPSC-derived macrophages address different translational barriers. CAR-monocytes may simplify production and exploit monocyte trafficking before tissue differentiation, but proliferation control, differentiation fate and inflammatory safety remain open questions (37). HSPC-derived CAR macrophages offer a route to more standardized production after progenitor engineering and myeloid differentiation, although differentiation efficiency and maturity still matter (80). hPSC- or iPSC-derived macrophages offer a potentially expandable source and have been engineered to express CARs or cytokines in preclinical systems (107-109). Off-the-shelf development requires testing for genomic stability, residual pluripotency, maturation state, allogeneic compatibility, and post-TME potency (80,107-110). Source choice should match clinical use: speed and patient specificity for autologous monocytes, scale for stem-cell-derived products, and early tissue trafficking for monocyte-based products (108,109,111-114).

The same source question also applies to non-CAR macrophage programming. ACE-engineered iPSC-derived human macrophages showed stronger inflammatory polarization and reduced melanoma, breast and head-and-neck tumor xenograft growth relative to unmodified iPSC-derived macrophages, illustrating how a scalable source and an intrinsic activation program can be engineered together (115).

Release criteria and potency assays

Release testing should confirm identity, engineering status and mechanism-matched function, not viability and sterility alone (Table 4). Identity assays may include CD14, CD11b, CD68, CD64 and HLA-DR plus CAR expression or other engineered modules. Potency assays should match design: antigen-dependent phagocytosis for CAR-M, cytokine release or T-cell activation for immune-stimulatory products, cargo retention/release for material-loaded macrophages, and phenotype stability after tumor-conditioned exposure for products designed to resist TME reprogramming. Safety testing should cover endotoxin, replication-competent vectors, residual editing components, off-target phagocytosis, material toxicity and excessive cytokine release as appropriate (Table 4).

Nonviral mRNA delivery is beginning to enter CAR-M manufacturing and in situ engineering discussions. An oxidized LNP platform optimized for human macrophages generated primary human HER2-CAR-Ms ex vivo and produced antigen-specific tumor-cell killing in ovarian tumor coculture, supporting mRNA delivery efficiency and expression duration as release-relevant attributes for transiently engineered products (116). Two 2026 in vivo LNP studies extend this direction: fluorinated LNPs delivered CAR mRNA to macrophages in solid tumor models and enhanced anti-PD-L1 therapy, while F4/80-targeted MPLA-LNPs generated TYRP1-CAR-expressing macrophages in a murine choroidal melanoma model (117,118).

Clinical translation

The clinical evidence base is still narrow, and CT-0508 remains the most informative peer-reviewed human dataset for engineered macrophage therapy in solid tumors (26). In this first-in-human phase 1 trial, 14 patients with HER2-overexpressing advanced solid tumors received autologous anti-HER2 CAR-M without lymphodepleting chemotherapy (26). The study was designed primarily to assess safety and manufacturability rather than efficacy.

The early signal was mixed. CT-0508 could be manufactured and administered; no dose-limiting toxicities, grade 3/4 cytokine-release syndrome or immune effector cell-associated neurotoxicity syndrome were reported, and serial biopsies supported tumor trafficking, TME remodeling and CD8+ T-cell expansion (26). However, no RECIST-defined objective responses were reported in the full study text, median progression-free survival was short, stable disease at 8 weeks was concentrated in HER2 3+ tumors, and no meaningful activity was observed in the HER2 2+ cohort (26). Thus, CT-0508 supports clinical feasibility and on-mechanism pharmacodynamic activity, but it also shows that antigen-directed CAR-M monotherapy has not yet produced durable tumor regression in most treated solid tumors.

Other programs test whether cell state, target choice, route or tumor setting can improve this profile. CT-0525 extends the HER2 program to CAR-monocytes: autologous CD14+ monocytes are Ad5f35-transduced to express anti-HER2 CAR, manufactured within 1 day, given without conditioning, and evaluated for safety, tolerability and manufacturing feasibility in six participants with HER2-overexpressing solid tumors (37). Sponsor updates add caution: Carisma prioritized CT-0525 over CT-0508 and stopped new CT-0508 recruitment, then discontinued CT-0525 and the broader anti-HER2 ex vivo program after completing phase 1 enrollment without entering the planned third cohort (33,34). These are pipeline and translational constraints, not peer-reviewed efficacy failures. Other registered or early programs include HER2-targeted CAR-M for gastric cancer with peritoneal metastases, RR-M01/BM-1901 for HER2-low or HER2-positive tumors, c-MET macrophage programs, and SY001 in mesothelin-positive ovarian cancer; mature peer-reviewed outcomes remain sparse (28-30,38,42,48,49,119).

This clinical map shows why translation should not be equated with target nomination. Few trials have mature peer-reviewed results, and ongoing or early registered programs should not be labeled as failures. The more useful question is whether a product matches the dominant barrier in the selected tumor. CT-0508 reached tumors and remodeled immune features, but tumor regression was limited, implicating antigen density, cell exposure, persistence, suppressive TME pressure and treatment route as determinants of durable activity (26). Company-reported CT-0508 plus pembrolizumab data were also too small to define a combination strategy: among the first three regimen-level-1 patients, no dose-limiting toxicity was reported, two had progressive disease and one had stable disease with one target lesion reduced by about 46% (34). CT-0525 similarly paired faster manufacture and higher planned dose with later program discontinuation, underscoring that feasibility and early biological activity do not establish a registrational path (33,34). RR-M01/BM-1901, mesothelin and c-MET programs remain exploratory until peer-reviewed outcomes are available (28,29,38,40,42,48,49,119).

The clinical message should remain modest. Early data support manufacture, administration and biological activity, but not durable efficacy in solid tumors (26). Development should test whether each feature improves a measurable barrier, such as tumor trafficking, antigen-dependent engagement, persistence, TME remodeling or combination responsiveness.

Safety, toxicity and regulatory considerations

On-target/off-tumor phagocytosis

Safety becomes a separate problem once macrophages are engineered to recognize, engulf and remodel tissue. On-target/off-tumor risk is relevant to targets such as HER2, CD147, VEGFR2 and c-MET (Table 3) (26,46,49). For macrophage products, injury may involve antigen-dependent phagocytosis, inflammatory cytokine release and recruitment of endogenous immune cells, not only direct cytotoxicity (10,26). Target selection should therefore include normal-tissue expression, antigen-density thresholds and functional off-tumor assays.

Safety testing should follow the engineering design. CAR-M requires normal-tissue expression data and functional coculture assays. Material-loaded macrophages require biodistribution, retention, release kinetics and viability testing (53,54,58,78). Bacteria-associated systems need additional controls for bacterial persistence, immune clearance, antibiotic sensitivity and inflammatory activation (75,105). A useful safety package should therefore be matched to the product rather than copied from a generic cell-therapy template (Table 5).

Cytokine toxicity and inflammatory overshoot

Inflammation is part of the intended activity of many engineered macrophage products, but it should be treated as a controllable function rather than a signal to maximize. Activated macrophages can release TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, IL-12, type I interferon-related mediators and chemokines that may support TME remodeling and adaptive immune recruitment (10,26). The same activity can become unsafe if cytokine release is systemic, prolonged or poorly reversible.

This is especially relevant for macrophages carrying bacterial components, IFN-γ backpacks or inducible cytokine modules (74,75,105). Heat-, ultrasound- and hypoxia-responsive systems may reduce systemic exposure, but only if basal leakiness, trigger-dependent activation and cytokine kinetics are measured directly (77-79). Early trials should therefore include cytokine monitoring and predefined management strategies, particularly when safety signals may come from both the cell and the delivered payload.

Genetic engineering, cell source and combination-product regulation

Material- or microbe-associated macrophage products add a final translational complication: the therapeutic product is no longer only a cell. Backpacks, nanoparticles, antibubbles, magnetic particles and bacterial cargos can all contribute to activity, but they also change what must be characterized before clinical use (58,74,75,78,105). The relevant tests should cover both the macrophage and the added component, including identity, viability, loading efficiency, attachment or retention, payload release, sterility, endotoxin, potency and in vivo fate. If the material changes cell migration, activation or biodistribution, it becomes part of the mechanism and part of the safety profile (Tables 4,5).

Barrier-related toxicity

Barrier-matched engineering can also introduce barrier-related safety liabilities, especially for strategies designed to improve physical-barrier penetration, inflammatory recruitment, tissue retention or local activation (Table 5). Because macrophages naturally enter inflamed and damaged tissues, engineered products could accumulate in non-malignant inflamed sites, release cytokines locally, remodel stroma, or engulf antigen-positive normal cells when targets are shared. This risk differs from classic CAR-T cytotoxicity: tissue injury may arise from phagocytosis, stromal remodeling, bystander inflammation and recruitment of endogenous immune cells. Assessment should combine normal-tissue antigen atlases, inflamed-tissue models, primary-cell phagocytosis assays, biodistribution and spatial pathology. Mitigation may include antigen-density tuning, local delivery, transient expression, controllable activation circuits, dose optimization and tumor-triggered payloads.

Future directions

The next stage of engineered macrophage therapy should begin with the barrier, not the module. Solid tumors do not limit adoptive cell therapy in one way. Antigen-rich but immune-excluded tumors, hypoxic lesions, stromal pancreatic tumors, peritoneal metastases and myeloid-dominant tumors each pose different problems. CARs, phagocytosis-checkpoint edits, cytokine backpacks, responsive circuits and cargo systems should therefore be judged by the barrier they address, not by their novelty as engineering features.

This barrier-first view also changes reporting standards. Tumor location, antigen density, stromal architecture, resident macrophage biology and myeloid composition shape the task assigned to a product. Future studies should pair tumor-growth data with localization, phenotype stability, phagocytosis, cytokine kinetics, antigen presentation and effects on endogenous T cells. Clinical trials need the same discipline: if intratumoral remodeling is proposed, biopsies, immune profiling, spatial analysis and product tracking should be built into the evidence package (23,120-124). Recent 2026 reviews and a Translational Cancer Research study of macrophage-mediated IL-12 and SIRPα-Fc delivery support this mechanism-led view of local payload delivery (125-128).

Combination therapy should also follow mechanism. Checkpoint blockade is most rational when a macrophage product increases antigen presentation, T-cell infiltration or PD-L1 expression. Chemotherapy or radiotherapy may help when it increases antigen release, inflammatory signaling or susceptibility to phagocytosis. Timing, toxicity and effects on transferred macrophages still need direct testing. Preclinical CAR-M and PD-1 blockade synergy supports this mechanism-led logic (91).

Strengths and limitations of this review

This review has several strengths. It organizes engineered macrophage therapy through a barrier-matched translational framework rather than listing platforms as isolated technologies. It integrates evidence from CAR-M, surface-decorated macrophages, cargo-loaded systems, cellular backpacks, responsive circuits, phagocytosis-checkpoint attenuation, stem-cell-derived macrophages and early clinical trials. It also links engineering strategies to translational readouts, including tumor localization, phenotype stability, phagocytosis, antigen presentation, cytokine kinetics, biodistribution, safety and manufacturing comparability.

This review also has limitations. It is a narrative review and does not include formal risk-of-bias assessment or meta-analysis. The evidence base is heterogeneous, and many platforms remain supported mainly by in vitro or mouse-model data. Clinical evidence is still limited, especially outside HER2-directed CAR-M products. Therefore, the proposed barrier-matched framework should be viewed as a conceptual and translational guide rather than a validated clinical decision algorithm.

Conclusions

Engineered macrophages should be understood as functional immune-cell products, not interchangeable carriers. Their value lies in tissue entry, phagocytosis, antigen handling and local immune remodeling, but these same features make activity sensitive to TME-driven reprogramming, variable persistence and context-dependent safety. CT-0508 shows that CAR-M products can be manufactured, administered and biologically active in patients, whereas most other platforms remain preclinical, registry-level, abstract-level or company-reported (29,38,42,48,49,119). The CT-0508/CT-0525 sequence also shows that technical feasibility is not enough for a sustainable clinical program. The central question is whether a product reaches the relevant tumor compartment, maintains its intended state and changes the barrier it was designed to address (89,90,125,126,128-132).
