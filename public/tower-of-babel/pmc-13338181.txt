An intelligent decision model for optimizing industrial power consumption using q-fraction fuzzy information
Authors: Muhammad Ahmed, Shahzaib Ashraf, Muhammad Saqib, Ayele Tulu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe minimization of power consumption poses a very important challenge to industries which aim at reducing power consumption and achieving their ecological sustainability objectives. The study proposes a superior decision-making model of the q-fractional fuzzy logic with reference to energy consumption in the industrial setting. The proposed model is the combination of multiple criteria including the power requirement of a machine, operating efficiency, temporal limitations and energy prices. The framework is concerned with uncertainties and imprecision in energy consumption data using the concepts of the q-fractional fuzzy aggregation operators, e.g., Weighted Average (WA), Weighted Geometric (WG), Ordered Weighted Average (OWA), Ordered Weighted Geometric (OWG), Hybrid Average (HA) and Hybrid Geometric (HG). The model will seek to attain high level of quality and cost efficient strategies in production due to energy saving. The result of its performance is confirmed by its case studies and simulations and has a huge decrease in energy consumption as compared to the traditional methods of optimization. These findings indicate how this other q-fractional fuzzy set can be a feasible and intelligent technology to manage energy during the industrial processes towards ecological sustainability, and it must be based on minimal human interjections as much as feasible. The empirical findings show that the advised q-fraction fuzzy verdict paradigm significantly reduces industrial power usage and outperforms the competitive CoCoSo mode in terms of reliability and effectiveness.Subject terms: Energy science and technology, Engineering, Environmental sciences, Environmental social sciences, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

The practical system has increased gradually become more complex, making it difficult for decision-makers to choose the best option among two or more alternatives. Nevertheless, the option of making a decision always exists, but it is never easy1. Outlining the objectives, inspiration, and perspectives that should be taken into account by the company when acting in the market remains a difficult task even for many organizations. Decision-making caters to different goals at the same time and this is one of the challenges of the decision-maker2. In situations where the criteria are not congruent, it may be problematic to figure out what exactly is the optimal solution to a certain situation. This means that decision-makers will be able to formulate more appropriate and realistic strategies.

A q-fractional fuzzy set (q-FFS) is an advanced mathematical tool for handling risk in decision-making3. It refines traditional fuzzy sets by integrating three critical components: the membership degree, non-membership degree, and hesitancy degree4. All these aspects enable the development of an optimal and precise representation of uncertainty, particularly in high-ambiguity or hesitation contexts5. Specifically, this paper establishes that the theory ofq-FFSs guarantees better solution accuracy and reliability where the degree of membership, non-membership, or both may be less clearly defined.

Review of literatures

The primary advantage of using q-FFSs is optimal for the calculations of a number of operations, including multiple criteria decision- making and diagnosis in the medical domain. Consequently, it helps to reduce ambiguous decision-making and delays to minimize the formulation of wrong decisions, as this can lead to confusion and hasty decision-making. Conservation of electricity in the use of power in industries is very important for saving energy costs and utilizing available power resources effectively. Many problems in this area are addressed using Multiple Criteria Group Decision Making (MCDM) tools6, which involve dealing with uncertainty and ambiguity. These characteristics can be applied to the use of Fuzzy sets, especially for proposed Fuzzy sets for handling uncertainties and fuzziness in data7.

Fuzzy sets were introduced to the world by Zadeh in 19658. A fuzzy set (FS) is a concept developed by allowing each element of a set to have a membership range that falls within the interval of 0 and 1. Unlike classical sets where an element either belongs or does not belong to a set with a specific degree,9 fuzzy sets automatically consider the fuzzy nature and uncertainty, providing capabilities to address many real-life complexities encountered in industrial decision-making processes.

Intuitionistic fuzzy sets (IFS) are an extension of the concept of fuzzy sets first introduced by Atanassov in 198310 with some changes. IFS is defined by two parameters: a membership function and a non-membership function. The membership function gives a measure of how much an element is in the set, and the non-membership function gives a measure of how much an element is out of the set11. Originally, the parameterized measure of credibility in the aggregated membership and non-membership degrees of an object or set does not exceed one12. That is why the additional dimension analyzed makes IFS especially useful when working with vague and unpredictable information13.

A q-rung orthopair FS14 is an even more generalization of fuzzy sets, intuitionistic fuzzy sets15, and Pythagorean fuzzy sets, with additional flexibility on uncertainty and imprecision. It is defined in terms of a membership degree and non-membership degree to satisfy a particular condition. The membership degree and non membership degree of any element of a q-ROFS in a universe are defined in the set [0, 1]16. With increasing values of q, the model has increased tolerance and q-ROFS is quite suitable to more realistic scenarios17. The difference of q-ROFS is that the value of the qth power of the membership degree and the value of the qth power of the non-membership degree are required to be less than or equal to 1. Hence, higher absolute values of provide the model more ’margin of error’ and enable the model to operate on a wider spread of cases which are possible in the real situation18.

Compared to its earlier forms, the design of the q-ROFS provides better capability to categorize and regulate complex and uncertain information19. This is achieved by reducing some of the constraints regarding the values of membership and non-membership to open up more possibilities for modeling uncertain and imprecise data information20.

The problems with the existing approach led to the creation of the Linear Diophantine Fuzzy Set (LDFS)21. For instance, in traditional FS theory, extreme values cannot be described as belonging to a set22. The addition of two parameters for the LDFS allowed for better monitoring of data that contains a wide range of outliers. While the approach had more favorable convergence, it made the parameters more restrictive in use, narrowing down the applicability to specific problems only. This could reduce the potential for applying the LDFS23 to other more complex problems involving causes that intertwine parameter constraints or where oddly valued parameters are critically influential in decisions.

FFS is another concept that is characterized by enhancement over the conventional FS using fractional calculus. In a classical FS, every element has a degree of membership that varies from 0 to 124. FFSs extend this further by including fractional-order operators, including but not limited to derivatives, integrals of non-integer order, into the membership functions. Thus, there is a possibility to describe more intricate, memory-related, or hereditary characteristics of systems, which seems to be one of the strong claims of fractional fuzzy sets, especially in cases when the traditional FS approach may be insufficient to model the long and dynamic behaviors of systems25.

In decision-making, fractional fuzzy sets are beneficial as they interpret the complex relationships and uncertainties in decision-making effectively. When considering the first type of decision criteria,26 it is important to mention that the decision criteria often depend on each other or have a historical context. By applying the membership functions of a fraction, fractional fuzzy sets can consider these dependencies more effectively. Fractional derivatives, known for their memory effects, play a crucial role in this process. For example, in multi-criteria decision-making, fractional fuzzy sets can provide a better way to assign more weight to dynamically or historically influenced criteria than dominant criteria27, resulting in improved accuracy and reliability. Additionally, variables measured as fractional fuzzy sets enhance the representation of vagueness and uncertainty in real-world problems, thereby assisting decision-makers in making informed evaluations of options and selecting the best course of action.

Aq-FFS is a specialized extension of a fractional FS. Presently, the q-FFS aggregation model is in the currently developing stages. These emerging structures are improvements to FS theory and are more effective than possibility-probability distribution models at dealing with fuzzy data. Comparing the various classes of fuzzy aggregation operators, one may state that the fraction of q fuzzy aggregation approach to aggregation possesses more capability to solve common problems in decision-making applications28. This methodology adds a parameter that modifies the (decision-making) process by regulating the aggregation of single measurements, which makes the solutions of greater quality in fields where the balance between performance, cost, or environmental damage is important, e.g., in the optimization of processes like in factories, one example is energy consumption. The power of the model based on the q-FFS aggregation is that it allows industries to make well-informed decision within an environment of uncertainty or data variability thereby benefiting to all stakeholders.

The suggested technique of the q-FFS aggregation overcomes classical fuzzy models, as it also enables the combination of different fuzzy numbers without losing the blankness, uncertainness, and insignificance of real-life data. It provides an opportunity to obtain a broader set of information, which are the keys to decision-making activities aimed at automating and optimizing many activities, including regulating power consumption in the large-scale segment. It allows industries to study a number of possible scenarios of different levels of risk and attainable predictions are rather stable which allows for reasonable decisions. In this way, industries can solve difficult and changing tasks and avoid wasting resources and polluting the environment.

Motivation of the study

Modern industrial operations face growing pressure to optimize power consumption due to rising energy costs, stricter environmental regulations, and the need for operational efficiency. Traditional decision-making approaches often rely on precise data assumptions that fail to account for the inherent uncertainties, incomplete information, and dynamic fluctuations characteristic of real-world industrial environments. While conventional fuzzy logic methods provide some capability for handling vague data, they lack the flexibility to model complex, nonlinear uncertainties and expert hesitancy effectively. Similarly, established multi-criteria decision-making techniques like CoCoSo, though useful for evaluating competing objectives, do not adequately address the sophisticated uncertainty structures present in industrial energy systems29. This critical gap necessitates an advanced decision framework that can better capture and adapt to the ambiguous, hesitant, and fractional nature of industrial data. The proposed model of q-FFS will meet this demand through the introduction of a tunable parameter, namely: the parameter q that allows having a more finer-grained control on the aspects of uncertainty representation, over their classical fuzzy counterparts. Considering the combination of this upgraded fuzzy methodology with the multi-criteria decision analysis, our model offers an enhanced accuracy of decisions in energy optimization and aligns with the nature of the modern industrial landscape, unpredictable and chaotic in many ways, and will eventually contribute to the better-informed and more sustainable power management strategy.

Critical need of more advanced method of energy consumption modeling with capability of modeling the complex and dynamic nature of modern industrial system brings about the development of q-FFSs. Although basic FS theory offers essential processing abilities with respect to imprecise information, it has shown to lack sufficient functioning when faced with such relationships that contain high non linearity and a time-varying nature to uncertainty that characterizes the usage patterns in energy real life entities. The cost of the proposed approach of q-FFS will have greater mathematical flexibility to describe these complex system behaviors more accurately that allows power consumption to be examined and optimized in industrial application. The technological step solves the increasing need of the decision-support tool that takes the complex nature of uncertainties in modern energy management situations.

Research gap

It has become increasingly challenging to maximize power utilization in today’s data-driven industrial environments due to the inherent imprecision, ambiguity, and changeability of operational parameters. Though they provide useful analytical frameworks, current multi-criteria decision-making (MCDM) approaches, like the CoCoSo methodology, sometimes fail to replicate human mistake, hesitation, and higher-order uncertainty all of which are critical in real-time industrial contexts. These disadvantages are particularly apparent in modern smart industrial systems, where dynamic factors including insufficient sensor data, machine learning predictions, fluctuating energy prices, and sustainability goals influence energy consumption decisions. Since real-world selection ambiguity is nonlinear and fractional, the current techniques mostly depend on classical, intuitionistic, pythagorean, and Fermatean fuzzy approaches that assume linear or stable uncertainty structures. Therefore, these methods might result in less-than-ideal or unduly strict choices that aren’t well suited to actual industrial circumstances. By adding a tunable parameter q that regulates the degree of uncertainty modeling, the suggested study fills this gap by introducing a q-fraction fuzzy decision model, which improves the expressiveness and flexibility of fuzzy logic. This approach improves decision resilience by enabling a broader portrayal of disparate data, partial truths, and expert hesitancy. When used to manage industrial power use and compared to CoCoSo, the model performs better in managing intricate, unpredictable energy usage scenarios.

Fractional fuzzy sets (FFS) are an advanced generalization of classical fuzzy sets that incorporate a fractional-order parameter (often denoted by q) into the membership function to provide more granular control over uncertainty and vagueness in decision-making. Unlike classical fuzzy sets where each element has a membership degree [0, 1], FFS modify the behavior of membership functions through fractional calculus-inspired transformations, allowing nonlinear scaling of uncertainty, hesitancy, and partial truth values. Fractional fuzzy sets offer several key advantages over traditional fuzzy logic frameworks: FFS can better represent real-world ambiguity and vagueness, especially in systems where human reasoning, sensor errors, or imprecise measurements play a role. The fractional parameter q enables dynamic adjustment of the fuzzy system’s responsiveness, making it adaptable to different application scenarios and decision environments. By providing a finer representation of the uncertainty spectrum, FFS often lead to more robust and accurate decision outcomes especially in multi-criteria decision-making and optimization problems. In fields such as industrial automation, energy management, and intelligent systems, FFS improve interpretability and adaptability in uncertain environments. We have included a detailed definition of fractional fuzzy sets, elaborated on their mathematical structure involving a tunable parameter q, and highlighted their significance in modeling nonlinear uncertainty and hesitation. This addition strengthens the theoretical foundation of our proposed model and clarifies how FFS extend classical fuzzy logic to address the complex, uncertain nature of industrial power consumption data.

Case study

Power consumption reduction is especially important for companies who would like to cut their expenditure and meet the environmental objectives. However, for optimizing the energy consumption equations within a manufacturing environment, it is not always easy to identify the kind of strategies that should be adopted, in the consideration of various influence factors and decision variables. This paper aims to gain an understanding of how a q-FF aggregation information-based decision model can be used in a large scale manufacturing facility decision making to evaluate and rank energy-saving options regarding power usage.

The manufacturing facility had a problem with high energy costs and inefficient control of power usage because it had no good methods for monitoring and analyzing its usage in the real time. Various power optimization alternatives were available except that the decision making was affected because each of the factors affected power consumption in different ways. This realization grew from the necessity to have a single appraisal model that would allow for the proper ranking of such options.

The purpose of this particular case study was to use a q-FF aggregation information-based decision model, to decide on the use of power conservation by assessing the four dimensions of the energy saving on five diverse options. The objective was to define the measures, best suited for minimizing energy usage, which would not negatively impact organizational performance.

Energy Consumption (EC) is defined as the total electric energy used in industry by different equipment and processes in a manufacturing unit and is expressed in kilowatt-hours. Fixed costs are a crucial that defines business operating costs and effectiveness, thus being an important factor that defines profitability and feasibility of industrial processes. The causes of high energy consumption include lack of efficiency of mechanisms, ineffective time management of the production process, and inadequate regularity of equipment maintenance. Hence, there is the prognosis that specific policy solutions such as the replacement of less efficient equipment with more efficient ones, the use of real-time energy monitoring, and effective load management could help to reduce costs, increase production performance, and support the achievement of sustainable objectives in EC.

Operational Efficiency (OE) captures the ability of machinery and the manufacturing process to utilize energy to create value, capturing efficiency levels within manufacturing. A higher OE means that the machinery is at an optimum, that is using less energy than it would under inefficient conditions; a low OE depicts inefficiencies; losses by way of inefficient or worn up machinery, or by way of unmaintainable vessels or conduits, or by way of cumbersome processes. Elements of OE include implementing superior automation, servicing equipment frequently, and enhancing processes that require less energy, enhancing production rates and cutting expenses to make the industrial processes cheaper and environmentally friendly.

Peak Demand Impact (PDI) This analyzes the degree to which a host of energy management measures influences the energy demand of a facility at its highest at some time in the chosen time span. Hence managing PDI is important since peak demand costs are usually a large part of industrial energy costs. High peak demand may be attributed to demand that is directed towards standby of intensive energy equipment; this causes a surge in the overall electricity usage which attracts overload charges from suppliers. The methods for decreasing the PDI consist in load shifting when assuming energy-intensive tasks in the non-peak hours and demand response systems which help to divide the loading more evenly. This paper has highlighted how the reduction of PDI assists in achieving maximum energy utilization, lower bills and ensures that the framework does not yield pressure hence cutting costs for the organization.

Implementation Cost (IC) refer to total capital investment necessary to install each of the energy optimization option consisting of expenses like purchase of; Alarm system, Converter, UPS, new lighting equipment among others, Installation charges, Integration charges and Employee training expenses. It is important in decision making because it is a direct measure of how economically viable these measures are. A high IC can scare investors away even though its benefits are potentially long term in nature while a low IC product may attract investors much faster as its benefits are more immediate. If IC is reported alongside projected energy savings and efficiency improvements, then the chosen solutions also effectively compare well with the budget constraints to provide a good value proposition for what can be good energy management and by extension general operations.

These attributes were assessed by the decision model for five energy-saving plans to identify the overall optimal method of saving power. Alternative A was tailored to incorporate Variable Frequency Drives (VFDs) for motor control; VFDs change speed according to load demand, thus avoiding energy wastage in unnecessary high speeds. In contrast, Alternative B was to integrate a Real-Time EMS to monitor and control energy use throughout a facility continuously. Alternative C involved installing high efficient motors to upgrade existing conventional machinery for the purpose of improving efficiency of key equipment. A new element called Load Shifting Strategy was introduced under Alternative D to reduce the tendency of using power during peak hours by shifting the load to other off-peak hours with higher supply capacity. At long last, it is now suggested by Alternative E the installation of Smart Lighting Systems in production areas to maximize occupancy control and recycled natural daylight so as to effect considerable energy reduction. Any change was evaluated in terms of Energy Consumption, Operational Efficiency, Peak Demand Impact and Implementation Cost of each solution in the context of the available to choose from alternatives. Sensitivity analysis

The sensitivity analysis of the proposed q-FFS based multi-criteria decision-making model was carried out to evaluate the effect of changing the weight vectors of the criteria on the final ranking of alternatives. Various weight scenarios were considered, including equal weights (where all criteria were treated with the same level of importance), biased weights (where certain criteria were given higher importance than others), and moderately adjusted distributions to simulate realistic decision-making environments. The primary objective was to assess whether such variations would lead to a change in the optimal solution. Across all tested scenarios, the top-ranked alternative remained unchanged, demonstrating the stability and robustness of the model. Although minor shifts were observed in the ranking positions of non-optimal alternatives, the consistency of the best-performing option highlights the models resilience to fluctuations in decision-maker preferences. Figure 7 presents a visual comparison through a bar chart (or line graph) illustrating how the scores or ranks of each alternative varied across scenarios. This discussion has assured the validity that the q-FFS-based technique provides the credible and consistent results, despite different assumptions, which means that this is a powerful instrument addressing complex decision-making under uncertain circumstances.

Conclusion

This study presented a novel extension to fuzzy decision-making by introducing the q-Fractional Fuzzy (qFF) framework and developing a set of corresponding aggregation operators, namely: qFFWA, qFFOWA, qFFHA, qFFWG, qFFOWG, and qFFHG. These operators are designed to more effectively capture and model the uncertainty, hesitation, and partial knowledge commonly encountered in real-world decision-making environments. We integrated these operators into a modified CoCoSo framework, resulting in a more flexible and adaptive model for addressing Multiple Attribute Decision-Making (MADM) problems. The proposed model enhances decision accuracy and robustness by accounting for fractional-order fuzziness, which traditional fuzzy and MCDM methods fail to fully accommodate. A mathematical example demonstrated the models practical utility, showing improved reliability over classical approaches.

Limitations of the proposed work: While the proposed method shows promising results, several limitations remain:

Parameter sensitivity: The effectiveness of qFF-based operators depends on the proper selection of the q-parameter. However, the method does not currently include a data-driven or adaptive strategy for optimal parameter tuning, which may affect result consistency in different applications.

Computational complexity: The incorporation of multiple q-fraction operators increases the computational load, especially when applied to large-scale datasets or high-dimensional decision problems.

Dependence on expert input: Like many fuzzy systems, this method relies on subjective expert input to define membership values and criteria weights. Inconsistent or biased input may influence final decisions, particularly in highly uncertain environments.

Limited comparative validation: While the model is tested against the CoCoSo method, its comparative performance against a wider range of MCDM techniques (e.g., EDAS, MARCOS, VIKOR) needs further exploration to validate its general superiority.

Domain-specific adaptability: The model may require domain-specific customization (e.g., in healthcare or environmental policy) to function optimally, as general-purpose fuzzy rules may not capture context-specific nuances.

To sum it up, q-FF framework is yet another major stride in the direction of decision science by overcoming major shortcomings of traditional MCDM methods. Although it has some limitations, the approach has formed a powerful platform to create more adaptive, flexible, and intelligent decision-support systems capable of operating well within uncertainty- thereby having a broad scope of applicability in the contemporary environments which are a complex and dynamic system. Finally, the paper concludes giving fundamentals operating principles of q-FF systems which result into formations of new aggregation operators: q-FFWA, q-FFOWA, q-FFHA, q-FFWG, q-FFOWG and q-FFHG. They are defined by key characteristics and theorems that have been shown through numerous studies to significantly expand the effectiveness of each operator in cases of decision-making.To address the challenges involved in Multiple Attribute Decision Making (MADM) we have introduced a detailed decision-making model that accommodates the inclusion of these operators within the CoCoSo computational tools. This approach skillfully balances advantages and disadvantages, allowing for a more comprehensive assessment of the available options.

Nevertheless, decision-making with ambiguity and incomplete information remains a hard problem for classic CoCoSo approaches, in which often provide inconsistent results. In light of these challenges, we propose a slight alteration to the CoCoSo technique that incorporates q-Fractional Fuzzy sets into selecting and ranking criteria since the CoCoSo technique is better suited for managing uncertainty and strengthening decision dependability than regular Fuzzy-set MCDM methods. In this study, the mathematical example of the suggested approach also affirms the effectiveness of our approach on solving MADM problems. Thus, the outcomes indicate not only that our method is very useful for such type of situations but at the same time it is very effective tool for solving practical tasks connected with real life decisions. The main emphasis of future studies will concern enhancing decision-making approaches within the scope of qFF. To enhance the efficiency and adaptability of the decision making systems, we would like to consider the use of multiple operators including the EDAS technique and the generalized geometric and average operators. This continuous investigation has the potential of greatly assisting in the development of robust approaches that can help address difficult decision making challenges. Our future work is to create even more precise and accurate instruments for decision makers, using the opportunities of qFF systems and studying different ways. In the end, this will promote better results across a variety of applications by enabling stake holders to traverse difficult environments with more assurance. The dedication to innovation in this field is a reflection of the continuous demand for practical solutions in a world that is becoming more unpredictable.
