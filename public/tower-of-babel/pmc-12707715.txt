Middle meningeal artery embolization for migraine: A review
Authors: Deborah Boyett, Nadine Marie Khoury, Nathan Shlobin, Marianna Shnayderman Yugrakh, Sean Lavine, Grace Kim Mandigo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background: Migraine and chronic headaches are highly prevalent disorders representing billions in healthcare spending and significantly reduced quality of life. The middle meningeal artery (MMA) is an established target in intracranial pathologies, and MMA embolization may be an innovative and promising therapeutic approach in the management of chronic headaches.Methods: This review of the literature aims to provide an in-depth analysis of the current state of knowledge regarding the effectiveness, safety, and potential implications of surgical targeting of the MMA, with particular emphasis on MMA embolization in the treatment of migraines and chronic headaches. PubMed was systematically queried for studies of MMA Embolization for the treatment of headache.Results: The trigeminovascular system is uniquely implicated in the pathogenesis of migraine headaches. Significant improvement in symptoms has been shown when targeting the neural, chemical, and vascular components of this system. The MMA has demonstrated unique behavior during and after migraines, distinct from that of other intracranial vessels. Reports of MMA perturbance have demonstrated prolonged improvements in migraine-induced pain without major adverse effects.Conclusion: The MMA’s unique role in the pathophysiology of migraines renders it a unique target for therapeutic intervention. Early studies revealing clinical migraine benefit after MMA disruption underscore the potential therapeutic value of MMA embolization. MMA embolization has been demonstrated to be safe and feasible.

INTRODUCTION

Migraine is a prevalent neurological disorder, ranking as the third highest cause of disability globally for individuals under 50, and affecting approximately one billion people.[22,30,49] In the United States, migraine and severe headache impact 1 in 6 Americans and 20% of women, with a stable prevalence over 19 years.[1] Headache consistently ranks as the fourth or fifth most common reason for emergency department visits, comprising estimated annual US healthcare costs of 3.2 billion associated with outpatient visits, 700 billion for emergency room (ER) visits, and 375 million for inpatient hospitalizations.[6,17,26]

This burden is amplified in patients with chronic migraine (15 or more headache days per month): Messali et al.[36] found that when compared with patients suffering of episodic migraines (less than 15 headache days per month), those with chronic migraines had significantly higher rates of visits to both primary care providers and neurologists, and over a three-fold increase in annual aggregate costs. This significant difference persisted after stratifying the cost by direct sources (healthcare expenses) and indirect sources (productivity loss).[36] This is further supported by the Chronic Migraine Epidemiology and Outcomes Study, which surveyed over 13,000 patients with recurrent migraines and found that 32.7% reported negative effects on one or more career areas, and 32.1% reported that their migraines contributed to concerns for long-term financial security. These findings are more glaring when stratified by patients with chronic migraines. In this population, 58.4% reported negative impacts on one or more career domains and 57.4% reported long-term financial concerns due to their migraines.[7] In addition to lost wages, migraine and chronic headache significantly affect patients’ quality of life and can be associated with opioid and barbiturate dependence.[20] Moreover, Seng et al. underscore the association between symptoms of depression and chronic migraine, revealing a link between severe migraine disability and anxiety or severe depression.[48]

The spectrum of migraine disorder ranges from episodic headaches to status migrainosus, an extended attack resistant to medical intervention, to chronic migraine with near-daily headache that can be refractory to multiple preventive therapies in some. The pathophysiology of migraines is not entirely understood; decades of research have revealed nuanced interactions between the trigeminovascular system and neuronal modulation, influenced by genetic factors.[25] Though the exact steps that lead to activation of trigeminal nociceptors are still under investigation, there is consensus that migraine pain is mediated by the trigeminovascular pathway with nociceptors at the dura and meningeal vessels. Middle meningeal artery (MMA) embolization is an endovascular procedure that embolizes the vessels supplying this nociceptive pathway. Although conventionally employed for the treatment of chronic subdural hematomas (cSDHs),[10] embolization has been shown to relieve headaches, possibly due to its dampening of trigeminovascular nociception, although further research is needed to confirm these preliminary findings. MMA embolization has therefore emerged as a candidate for headache management, specifically in patients with chronic migraine or severe headache that has failed to respond to conventional treatments.[14,23,37,40-42,56] This review explores literature supporting the role of the MMA in migraine pathophysiology and as a potential target for treatment, including via surgical ligation, craniotomy, and embolization of the MMA.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

PubMed was queried for prospective or retrospective studies of MMA Embolization using search conditions including (“middle meningeal artery embolization” OR “middle meningeal artery”) AND (“headache” OR “migraine”). A total of 203 papers were generated from the search. Studies that assessed patients’ headaches after embolization as an endpoint were included. Review articles were excluded. Considering the relatively novel use of MMA embolization, case reports and case series were included. One study was excluded due to a broad primary endpoint that prevented the direct assessment of headache response.

DISCUSSION

MMA implicated in migraine physiology

There are several ways in which the MMA is implicated in headache disorders. The dura mater is innervated by the trigeminal nerve and richly supplied with pain-sensitive nerve (C fibers and Aδ) fibers, the largest density of which is found in meningeal blood vessels.[4,31] These meningeal nociceptors have the unique capability to bridge intracranial and extracranial spaces, as observed in animal models in which axons of dural nociceptors cross the arachnoid space to reach the periosteum and even pericranial muscles.[32,51] Data from awake craniotomies have established that mechanical and electrical stimulation of the dura can induce pain in the sensory territories of the V1 and V3 divisions of the trigeminal nerve, and more specifically, stimulation of large meningeal blood vessels is also associated with headache, whereas stimulation of areas remote from vessels was not often associated with headache.[18,39] The trigeminal nerve branches are in turn supplied by the MMA, and stimulation or irritation of these nerves can also lead to the perception of headache pain. This neuronal and vascular interplay encompassing both peripheral and central nervous system components is known as the “trigeminovascular system.”

Historically, the vascular component of migraines has been studied for over a century, tracing back to Dr. Harvey Cushing, who noted “striking dilatation of the temporal arteries” in The Special Field of Neurological Surgery: 5 Years Later.[9,19] However, conflicting results regarding regards to MMA dilation have been reported, ranging from no dilation, to ipsilateral dilatation on the affected side, to bilateral dilation as measured on magnetic resonance angiography (MRA). More striking is the MMAs distinct response to administration of sumatriptan. Asghar et al. used Calcitonin Gene-Related Peptide (CGRP) to induce migraine symptoms and concurrent dilatation of the MMA and middle cerebral artery (MCA) on the ipsilateral side.[2] After administration of sumatriptan, they observed a reduction in the diameter of the MMA, without a concurrent reduction in the diameter of the intracranial vessels. Khan et al.[25] similarly used MRA to measure changes in vessel circumference after inducing migraine with Cilostazol. The study found that only the MMA displayed significant side-specific dilation at migraine onset. Conversely, intracerebral vessels (internal carotid, basilar, MCA) exhibited dilation bilaterally without preference for the affected side. Furthermore, 27 h after migraine onset without treatment, the MMA showed persistent dilation bilaterally, which coincided with patient reports of late-stage bilateral head pain. Some have been tempted to view this observed neurovascular dilatation as a secondary “epiphenomenon,” resulting from (CGRP) release alongside reflexive parasympathetic activation. However, this view ignores the observation that the MMA is unique from intracranial circulation in its vasodilatory response and timing, underlying its distinct correlation with the nociceptive pathways inherent to migraine.[25,33]

Nevertheless, MMA vasodilation alone does not appear to explain migraine pain, and with the turn of the century, studies began placing increasing focus on neurochemical effectors of nociceptors. It is now generally accepted that headache is initiated by the introduction of inflammatory molecules and CGRP to the dura.[24] CGRP, released by the trigeminal nerve, is an immunoreactive marker and potent vasodilator of intracranial blood vessels.[34,35] Fibers containing CGRP are abundant within the walls of cerebral arteries in the circle of Willis, and studies have shown that CGRP is released by stimulation of meningeal afferents.[12,52] Furthermore, the dura demonstrates hypersensitivity to mechanical stimulation after induction of CGRP, and this chemically induced dural hypersensitivity could underlie the exacerbation of migraine pain with Valsalva, movement, and position.[4,5,50]

Pharmacological treatments for migraine, including triptans, ergot alkaloids, and CGRP monoclonal antibodies, seek to disrupt the pathway between the peripheral and central components of the trigeminovascular system to prevent and abort migraine. Triptans block the central release of CGRP, and ergot alkaloids block neuropeptide release in the meninges.[29] Both have been demonstrated to reduce CGRP plasma concentrations and are effective at aborting migraine. CGRP monoclonal antibodies prevent migraine by inhibiting the activation and sensitization of thinly myelinated Adelta meningeal nociceptors.[46] These monoclonal antibodies do not readily cross the blood–brain barrier, and their site of action is likely extracranial. Despite their consistent results and wide use in migraine prevention and treatment, 30– 40% of patients do not respond to anti-CGRP monoclonal antibodies, and some do not respond to multiple classes of migraine preventive therapies, underscoring the need for further research and treatment modalities.[3,43]

MMA embolization has the potential to diminish trigeminal ganglion stimulation, modulate neurovascular function, and reduce CGRP release. By interrupting the inflammatory cascade and devascularizing their site of action at the meninges, MMA embolization may alleviate migraine symptoms and provide significant relief. MMA embolization is already being used to address the inflammatory cascade in the treatment of cSDH. Specifically, cSDH formation involves the release of inflammatory mediators and vascular growth factors, leading to neomembrane formation and fragile, leaky blood vessels within the hematoma. MMA embolization targets these vessels within the dural membranes, cutting off the blood supply that feeds the hematoma and promoting its growth.[28] This, in turn, reduces the release of inflammatory substances and slows the progression of the hematoma. Several studies have observed positive outcomes after MMA embolization for cSDH, including reduced recurrence rates and improved neurological outcomes.[11,44,47] While these studies primarily focus on hematoma resolution and neurological improvement, the reduction in recurrence suggests that the underlying inflammatory process is also being addressed. Further research is needed to fully elucidate the specific effects of MMA embolization on dural inflammation in cSDH patients. One small study performed endoscopic visualization following MMA embolization for cSDH demonstrated occlusion of both dural and sinusoidal arteries supplying the outer cSDH membrane.[38] This resulted in a visible color change in the outer membrane, suggesting ischemia. The resulting reduction in blood supply likely exerts a therapeutic anti-inflammatory effect by mitigating hyperemia, decreasing capillary permeability, and suppressing the release of inflammatory mediators. This therapeutic approach of targeting vascular supply to reduce inflammation is also utilized in other medical contexts, such as vasoconstricting nasal sprays for rhinitis and geniculate artery embolization for osteoarthritis of the knee.[21,45]

Migraine relief with surgical interventions to the MMA

Surgical interventions have shown promise in managing and alleviating headache symptoms, particularly in cases of severe or refractory migraines. In a rat model of migraine, heat coagulation of the MMA has been shown to significantly decrease plasma CGRP level and c-fos (a marker of neuronal activation) expression in the trigeminal nucleus caudalis compared with a control.[57] Physiologically, a portion of the MMA has been shown to supply the trigeminal ganglion, which is hypothesized to play a critical role in headache sensation and targeted therapy. The trigeminal nerve may supply dural unmyelinated C and catecholaminergic fibers, which contribute to dural nociception. Consequently, targeting the MMA may result in attenuation of trigeminal hyperexcitability known to contribute to headache sensation. In fact, intra-arterial injection of lidocaine into the MMA has also been shown to improve headaches of various etiologies. Qureshi et al. documented four patients suffering from chronic intractable headache and status migrainosus who exhibited significant improvements in both pain and Migraine Disability Assessment Test scores up to 3 months after injection of lidocaine in the MMA.[41] Patients reported an immediate reduction in headache intensity immediately following the injection, and the improvement lasted longer than the short half-life of lidocaine, suggesting a potential relationship with central desensitization. The group has published case reports, series, and a prospective trial demonstrating similar promising results.

Furthermore, several small studies have reported positive outcomes with MMA ligation, further emphasizing the artery’s role in headache generation. Elisevich et al.[13] highlight mechanical stimulation as a cause of migraine in their case report on an unusual head presentation attributed to post-traumatic dural adhesion and tethering of the MMA. The patient’s headache syndrome was further exacerbated by contrast injection and ballooning of the artery and resolved with administration of intra-arterial lidocaine and subsequent (open) surgical removal of the MMA.[13] Zhaomin et al. performed MMA and STA ligation in 10 patients with severe migraines.[56] The greater superficial petrosal nerve adjacent to the foramen spinosum was also ligated. Follow-up of 2–18 years later showed no recurrences, suggesting the potential usefulness of MMA ligation for treating intractable migraines. In addition, in patients undergoing craniotomy versus coiling for subarachnoid hemorrhage, there was a significant improvement in preexisting migraine in both groups at a follow-up of 12–114 months, with >50% reduction in migraine attacks.[53] The effect was significantly more pronounced in patients undergoing craniotomy, with 77.4% reporting no further migraine attacks.

Reduction of dural circulation by MMA occlusion may lead to a decrease in pulse wave amplitude, typically associated with the pulsating pain characteristics of migraines. This reduction could also contribute to decreased endothelial leaking and lowered release of inflammatory mediators secondary to decreased blood flow and reduced vasodilation. The interruption of dural trigeminal nerve branches traveling with meningeal arteries influences migraine development at a neural level, as discussed previously. Neuropeptides and pain modulators, including CGRP, originating from the trigeminovascular complex, may no longer be capable of release from dural vessels through these nerve fibers. While these data suggest an improvement in migraine frequency after craniotomy, it is well known that newly acquired headaches, especially tension-type headaches, can occur after craniotomy as well, underscoring the complex and multifaceted nature of post-surgical headache dynamics.

There is some evidence that embolization of the MMA is emerging as a promising treatment for chronic headaches. A retrospective study on patients undergoing MMA embolization for cSDH demonstrated improvement for the majority of patients who suffered from pre-subdural headaches for at least 2 years. This effect was observed for up to 489 days, indicating a potential role for MMA embolization in enhancing the quality of life for individuals affected by chronic headaches.[8] This retrospective observational study employed liquid embolic for embolization, and further research is needed to establish the efficacy of the procedure and the type of embolic across diverse headache etiologies and patient populations. Safety profile and economic considerations

Surgical intervention for migraine may offer a means of reducing the economic burden associated with both inpatient and outpatient medical care. Faber et al. demonstrated that surgical treatment of migraine (via peripheral nerve decompression) resulted in approximately $2,000 annual savings in medication costs, fewer primary care visits, and reduced lost work or childcare days.[15] Their study reported a fourfold reduction in total migraine treatment costs post-surgery, coupled with significant improvements in quality of life and disability assessments. It is important to note that while these findings are promising, surgical treatment for migraine is still considered experimental and requires further investigation. Innovative treatments addressing both the central and peripheral mechanisms of migraine are needed to address the significant portion of patients who do not find relief with existing therapies.

MMA embolization is a minimally invasive outpatient procedure with a well-established safety profile, supported by numerous retrospective studies and systematic reviews.[27,54] The procedure can be performed under moderate sedation or general anesthesia, accessing the arterial system via either the radial or femoral approach. Various embolic agents, including n-butyl cyanoacrylate (n-BCA) glue, Onyx, coils, and polyvinyl alcohol particles, have been used, with a slight preference for polyvinyl alcohol (PVA).[16,27,55] While complications are rare, meticulous attention to potential intracranial anastomoses and the origin of the ophthalmic artery is critical during both open surgical and endovascular MMA procedures. Future directions

While MMA embolization for headaches demonstrates promising results, the field remains in its nascent stages. Several key questions and challenges need to be addressed. Current studies are limited by small sample sizes, heterogeneous patient selection criteria, variable procedural techniques, and a lack of standardized outcome measures, which makes it difficult to draw definitive conclusions. Most recently, Qureshi et al., have published the first IRB-approved prospective study [Table 1] in seven patients.[40] They employed an 11-point numeric rating scale to ascertain patient response and assess improvement in the severity of headaches in seven patients with headaches associated with subarachnoid hemorrhage. Although the study is limited by a small sample size, all patients exhibited improvement – a promising result that bolsters the hypothesized role of the MMA in migraine pathophysiology and targeted therapy. Moreover, long-term follow-up data are crucial to understanding the durability of headache relief following MMA embolization. Rigorous, randomized controlled trials are essential to establish the efficacy of MMA embolization as a treatment for migraine and chronic headache. Several clinical trials are in progress testing the utility of MMA embolization. Most are for the management of cSDH and include the DAMMET, MEMBRANE, PREMMA, OTEMACS, STEM, CHESS, EMPROTECT, and EMBOLISE trials, as noted by Mortezaei et al.[37] For intractable headache and migraine, two ongoing trials have focused on the utility of MMA embolization: “MMA Embolization for Refractory Chronic Migraine” (NCT06735833), is a multi-center, prospective study testing the safety and efficacy of TRUFILL n-BCA liquid embolic for bilateral embolization in patients with refractory and/or chronic migraine, with an estimated enrollment of 50 adults in centers along the eastern United States. The study has a primary outcome measure of “change from baseline” after 28 days, with secondary outcome measurements in 3–6 months. The second trial, “Efficacy and Safety of MMA Embolization for Treatment of Intractable Migraine (FAST-EM)” (NCT06029153), based in Guizhou, China, measured headache attack frequency after MMA embolization with coil for patients suffering from persistent, chronic migraines for at least 3 months.

CONCLUSION

Surgical targeting of the MMA, particularly MMA embolization emerges as a compelling therapeutic avenue for chronic headaches, offering a minimally invasive approach. While early evidence suggests positive outcomes, ongoing research is required to refine patient selection criteria, standardize procedural techniques, and establish the long-term efficacy and safety of MMA embolization across diverse headache subtypes. This review underscores the potential of MMA embolization as a valuable addition to the armamentarium for chronic headache management, warranting continued investigation, and clinical application.
