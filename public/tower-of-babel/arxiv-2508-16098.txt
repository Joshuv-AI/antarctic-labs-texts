On the Dbar method and direct linearization approach of the lattice KdV type equations
Leilei Shi, Cheng Zhang, Da-jun Zhang

Abstract
The purpose of this paper is to bridge the gap between the Dbar method and the direct linearization approach for the lattice Korteweg-de Vries (KdV) type equations. We develop the Dbar method to study some discrete integrable equations in the Adler-Bobenko-Suris list. A Dbar problem is considered to define the eigenfunctions of the Lax pair of the lattice potential KdV equation. We show how an extra parameter is introduced in this approach so that the lattice potential modified KdV equation and lattice Schwarzian KdV equation are derived. We also explain how the so-called spectral Wronskians make sense in constructing the H3 ( δ ) (\delta) , Q1 ( δ ) (\delta) and Q3 ( δ ) (\delta) equations. Explicit formulae of multi-soliton solutions are given for the derived equations, from which one can see the connections between the direct linearization variables ( S ( i , j ) S^{(i,j)} and V ⁡ ( p ) V(p) ) and the eigenfunctions and their expansions respectively at infinity and a finite point. Keywords: Dbar method; direct linearization approach; lattice KdV type equation; soliton solution Mathematics Subject Classification: 37K60

Then, Dbar problems were employed by Ablowitz, Bar Yaacov and Fokas in a series of work

In particular, as mentioned in

Dbar problem is of crucial importance in solving the Kadomtsev-Petviashvili (KP) II equation using the IST scheme since
the equation (KP II) “was the first case the inadequacy of the Riemann-Hilbert
formulation of the IST was discovered.”

The direct linearization (DL) approach was first introduced by
Fokas and Ablowitz in 1981
[

]
.
As pointed out by the authors (Fokas and Ablowitz) in
[

[
42
]
.
In DL scheme, a linear integral equation plays a central role,
whose solution is used to define eigenfunction as well as the potential function.
It can be verified that the eigenfunction as well as potential function satisfy certain Lax pair
and in this way solutions of the nonlinear equation defined by the Lax pair can be formulated
by the integral equation.
As pointed out in
[

]
, the verification,
“in sprit, similar to that of Zakharov and Shabat” in 1974
[
47
]
.
In addition to Lax pairs and solutions, the Gel’fand-Levitan-Marchenko (GLM) equation
can also be constructed from the linear integral equation
[

]
.
The DL scheme and Dbar problem can be connected via the generalized Cauchy integral formula
(also known as the Cauchy-Pompeiu formula),
with examples given in
[
16
]

Fokas-Ablowitz’s DL scheme was extended to the KP equation,

[

,

,

,

,

Recently, the scheme was also extended to the case of elliptic solitons

Soon after

, the DL scheme of Fokas and Ablowitz was developed by

,

In the Dutch version nonlinear equations can be constructed together with their solutions,

It allows the treatment of discrete as well as continuous equations on one and the same

Its effectiveness has been proved in particular in the investigation of integrable lattice equations,

Recently, an elliptic version of this DL scheme was extended for

The paper
[
47
]
in 1974 by Zakharov and Shabat

The paper was reformulated in

using the language of the Riemann-Hilbert problem.
In

they called the method of

Dressing method provides an algorithm for constructing exact solutions

It is “purely local in the coordinates” and
“avoids the requirement of decay of solutions of infinity”, as described in
[

]
.

The combination of dressing method and Dbar problem
was introduced in
[

]
and
[
9
]
.
Both papers included high dimensional integrable equations as examples.

[

introduced a connection with the GLM equations and showed how exact solutions were obtained,

Apart from the formulation in

They used a Dbar problem together with the associated linear integrable equation

By assuming the eigenfunction satisfies a spectral problem under consideration,
and utilizing asymptotic property of the the eigenfunction, they determined

For the KdV equation, they introduced a so-called

which turns out to be an entire function with respect to the spectral parameter.
By this spectral Wronskian they could define a squared eigenfunction symmetry

This method was later described with more details
and illustrated with the KdV hierarchy and Toda hierarchy in
[
28
]
.
In this method the potential functions are formulated in a more natural way,
and time evolution is introduced not by “dressing”
but by using the “spectral Wronskian” (cf.

]
and
[
10

2
The Dbar problem and Cauchy-Pompeiu integral formula

For a function
f(p)
,
p\in\mathbb{C}
, let us introduce the notation

\bar{\partial}=\partial_{\bar{p}}=\frac{\partial}{\partial\bar{p}}=\frac{1}{2}(\partial_{p_{1}}+\mathrm{i}\partial_{p_{2}}),

(2.1)

where
\mathrm{i}
is the imaginary unit,
p=p_{1}+\mathrm{i}p_{2}\in\mathbb{C}
,

(p_{1},p_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
and
\bar{p}
stands for the complex conjugate of
p
.
A function
f(p)
is analytic on a domain
\Omega
if and only if
\bar{\partial}f(p)=0,~p\in\Omega
.
A
\bar{\partial}
(Dbar) problem means any problem that consists of finding a function

\psi(p)
in the complex plane from a
\bar{\partial}
equation
involving
\psi(p)
and
\bar{\partial}\psi(p)
, e.g.

\bar{\partial}\psi(p)=\psi(p)R(p),

(2.2)

where
R(p)
is some given function.
It is more general than a Riemann-Hilbert problem
where
\psi(p)
is analytic everywhere in
\mathbb{C}

except on a contour where it has a jump.
An extension of (
2.2
) is

\bar{\partial}\psi(p)=\psi(\omega(p))R(p),

(2.3)

which is called a quasi-local
\bar{\partial}
equation, where

\omega(p)
is a function of
p
, e.g.
\omega(p)=-p
.

The generalized Cauchy integral formula
(also called the Cauchy-Pompeiu integral formula, due to Pompeiu
[
39
]
)
reads (see
[
3
]
)

\displaystyle\psi(p)=\frac{1}{2\pi{\rm i}}\int_{\partial\Omega}\frac{\psi(\mu)}{\mu-p}\,d\mu+\frac{1}{2\pi{\rm i}}\int_{\Omega}\frac{\bar{\partial}\psi(\mu)}{\mu-p}\,d\mu\wedge d\bar{\mu},

(2.4)

where
\partial\Omega
is a simple closed contour enclosing the finite region
\Omega
.
Note that the generalized Cauchy integral formula also holds on an affine algebraic Riemann surface
[
21
]
.
Thus, finding a solution of the
\bar{\partial}
equation (
2.3
)
can be converted to solving the linear integral equation

\displaystyle\psi(p)=\frac{1}{2\pi{\rm i}}\int_{\partial\Omega}\frac{\psi(\mu)}{\mu-p}\,d\mu+\frac{1}{2\pi{\rm i}}\int_{\Omega}\frac{\psi(\omega(\mu))R(\mu)}{\mu-p}\,d\mu\wedge d\bar{\mu}.

(2.5)

Solutions of the same Dbar equation are usually not unique,
but one can characterize the uniqueness as discussed in
[
24
]
.
We assume
\psi(p)
has an expansion at infinity:

\psi(p)=\sum^{N}_{j=-\infty}\psi^{(j)}p^{j},

(2.6)

where
\sum^{N}_{j=0}\psi^{(j)}p^{j}
denotes the principle part of
\psi(p)
at infinity.
Then, on one side, letting
\Omega\to\mathbb{C}
in (
2.5
), one obtains

\psi(p)=\sum^{N}_{j=0}\psi^{(j)}p^{j}+\frac{1}{2\pi{\rm i}}\int_{\mathbb{C}}\frac{\psi(\omega(\mu))R(\mu)}{\mu-p}\,d\mu\wedge d\bar{\mu}.

(2.7)

On the other side, it is natural to assume the homogenous form of the above equation, i.e.

\psi(p)=\frac{1}{2\pi{\rm i}}\int_{\mathbb{C}}\frac{\psi(\omega(\mu))R(\mu)}{\mu-p}\,d\mu\wedge d\bar{\mu},

(2.8)

has always zero solution
\psi(p)=0
.
Thus, for two solution
\psi_{1}(p)
and
\psi_{2}(p)
of the same Dbar equation (
2.3
),
if they have same principle parts at infinity, then we assume
\psi_{1}(p)=\psi_{2}(p)
.
This agrees with the uniqueness of solutions of the integral equation (
2.7
).

In the case of integrable systems,
\psi(p)
is usually normalized by setting

\psi(p)\to 1
as
p\to\infty
, i.e.

\psi(p)\sim 1+O({1}/{p}),~~~p\to\infty.

It then follows from (
2.7
) that

\psi(p)=1+\frac{1}{2\pi{\rm i}}\int_{\mathbb{C}}\frac{\psi(\omega(\mu))R(\mu)}{\mu-p}\,d\mu\wedge d\bar{\mu}.

(2.9)

If replacing
\psi(p)
by
\rho\psi(p)
where
\rho
is some plane wave factor,
the above equation takes a form of the linear integral equation employed
by Fokas and Ablowitz in their DL approach
[
12
]
.
Thus, it is understood that the Dbar problem and the related methods
are closely related to the Fokas-Ablowitz DL approach.
For some (2+1)-dimensional equations, their DL schemes were also given
when they are solved via the IST scheme based on Dbar problems
[
16
]
.

3
The Dbar approach to the lpKdV equation

In this section, we will extend the Dbar approach to the discrete setting by introducing a quasi-local Dbar problem
with a discrete plane wave factor.
We will demonstrate that the solution of this Dbar problem satisfies
the Lax pair of the lpKdV equation.
In addition, we will explore the connection between the solution of this Dbar problem
and some
\{S^{(0,j)}\}
.

3.1
Quasi-local Dbar problem for the lpKdV

We adopt a specific quasi-local Dbar problem

\displaystyle\bar{\partial}\phi\left(p\right)=\phi\left(-p\right)R\left(p\right),\qquad p\in\mathbb{C},

(3.1)

\phi(p)=1+p^{-1}\phi^{(-1)}+p^{-2}\phi^{(-2)}+\cdots,\qquad\text{as}\quad p\rightarrow\infty.

(3.2)

4
The lpmKdV equation and lSKdV equation

In this section, we proceed to explore how the lpmKdV equation and lSKdV equation
arise from our approach. In particular, we give two formulations for the lSKdV equation.

4.1
The lpmKdV equation

Assume
c\in\mathbb{C}
is any finite point where
\phi(p)
is analytic.
Thus
\phi(p)
admits an expansion in a neighbourhood of

In the following we will derive these three equations directly from

and its deformations.
In addition, we will reveal the connection between the parameter

(\delta)

and the so-called “spectral Wronskian” that played a crucial role

28

5.1
H3
(\delta)
equation

For the Dbar problem (
3.1
), its spectral Wronskian can be introduced as (cf.
[
27
]
)

\displaystyle W(\psi,\phi)=\begin{vmatrix}\psi(p)&\phi(p)\\
\psi(-p)&\phi(-p)\end{vmatrix},

(5.1)

where
\psi(p)
and
\phi(p)
are two solutions of (
3.1
).
It is easy to check that
\bar{\partial}W(\psi,\phi)=0
, which means

W(\psi,\phi)
is an entire function of
p
on the complex plane.
For
\phi(p)
being a solution of the Dbar problem (
3.1
) with
R(p)
defined in (
3.3
),
it is easy to see that
(\alpha+p)\widetilde{\phi}(p)
is also a solution of the same Dbar problem.
These two solutions compose a spectral Wronskian

\displaystyle D(p)=\begin{vmatrix}\phi(p)&(\alpha+p)\widetilde{\phi}(p)\\
\phi(-p)&(\alpha-p)\widetilde{\phi}(-p)\end{vmatrix}.

(5.2)

Due to the symmetric form of the plane wave factor
R(p)

(as well as the multi-dimensionally consistent property of the related lattice equations),
the above
D(p)
can be equivalently defined as

\displaystyle D(p)=\begin{vmatrix}\phi(p)&(\beta+p)\widehat{\phi}(p)\\
\phi(-p)&(\beta-p)\widehat{\phi}(-p)\end{vmatrix}.

(5.3)

In fact, they are connected by
M(p)\phi(p)=0
in the Lax pair (
3.7
).

Thus we have

\displaystyle\bar{\partial}D(p)=0,

(5.4)

i.e.
D(p)
is an entire function of
p
on the complex plane.
Noticing (from (
3.2
)) the asymptotic behavior

\displaystyle D(p)\rightarrow-2p,\quad\text{as}\quad p\rightarrow\infty,

(5.5)

and also noticing that
D(p)
is odd with respect to
p
, i.e.
D(p)=-D(-p)
,
we are immediately led to

\displaystyle D(p)=-2p,

(5.6)

in light of the Liouville Theorem.

Inserting
p=c
in
D(p)
, we have

\displaystyle D(c)=\begin{vmatrix}\phi(c)&(\alpha+c)\widetilde{\phi}(c)\\
\phi(-c)&(\alpha-c)\widetilde{\phi}(-c)\end{vmatrix}=-2c,

(5.7)

and noticing that
\phi(p=c)=\phi_{c}^{(0)}
we obtain

\displaystyle(\alpha-c)\phi_{c}^{(0)}\widetilde{\phi}_{-c}^{(0)}-(\alpha+c)\widetilde{\phi}_{c}^{(0)}\phi_{-c}^{(0)}=-2c.

(5.8)

Meanwhile, from (
5.3
) we have

\displaystyle(\beta-c)\phi_{c}^{(0)}\widehat{\phi}_{-c}^{(0)}-(\beta+c)\widehat{\phi}_{c}^{(0)}\phi_{-c}^{(0)}=-2c.

(5.9)

We now construct the H3(
\delta
) equation.
First, we define
\mathrm{v}
through the following linear combination of the H3(0) solution

:

\displaystyle\mathrm{v}(c)

\displaystyle=C_{1}v_{c}+C_{2}v_{-c}

\displaystyle=C_{1}\theta(c)\phi_{c}^{(0)}+C_{2}\theta^{-1}(c)\phi_{-c}^{(0)},

(5.10)

where
v_{c}
is defined by (
4.8
) through
\phi_{c}^{(0)}
,
and
C_{1},C_{2}
are constants.
Next, through a calculation we find the following expression:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{A}\mathrm{v}(c)\widetilde{\mathrm{v}}(c)+\frac{1}{A}\widehat{\mathrm{v}}(c)\widetilde{\widehat{\mathrm{v}}}(c)-\frac{1}{B}\mathrm{v}(c)\widehat{\mathrm{v}}(c)-\frac{1}{B}\widetilde{\mathrm{v}}(c)\widehat{\widetilde{\mathrm{v}}}(c)

\displaystyle=~

\displaystyle\frac{C_{1}^{2}\theta^{2}(c)}{(c-\alpha)(c-\beta)}\Xi_{1}+\frac{C_{1}C_{2}}{c^{2}-\alpha^{2}}(\Xi_{2}+\widehat{\Xi}_{2})-\frac{C_{1}C_{2}}{c^{2}-\beta^{2}}(\Xi_{3}+\widetilde{\Xi}_{3})+\frac{C_{2}^{2}\theta^{-2}(c)}{(c+\alpha)(c+\beta)}\Xi_{4},

(5.11)

where

\displaystyle\Xi_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(c-\beta)\phi_{c}^{(0)}\widetilde{\phi}_{c}^{(0)}+(c+\beta)\widehat{\phi}_{c}^{(0)}\widehat{\widetilde{\phi}}_{c}\!\!{}^{(0)}-(c-\alpha)\phi_{c}^{(0)}\widehat{\phi}_{c}^{(0)}-(c+\alpha)\widetilde{\phi}_{c}^{(0)}\widehat{\widetilde{\phi}}_{c}\!\!{}^{(0)},

\displaystyle\Xi_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(c-\alpha)\phi_{c}^{(0)}\widetilde{\phi}_{-c}^{(0)}+(c+\alpha)\widetilde{\phi}_{c}^{(0)}\phi_{-c}^{(0)},

\displaystyle\Xi_{3}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(c-\beta)\phi_{c}^{(0)}\widehat{\phi}_{-c}^{(0)}+(c+\beta)\widehat{\phi}_{c}^{(0)}\phi_{-c}^{(0)},

\displaystyle\Xi_{4}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(c+\beta)\phi_{-c}^{(0)}\widetilde{\phi}_{-c}^{(0)}+(c-\beta)\widehat{\phi}_{c}^{(0)}\widehat{\widetilde{\phi}}_{-c}\!\!\!\!\!{}^{(0)}-(c+\alpha)\phi_{-c}^{(0)}\widehat{\phi}_{-c}^{(0)}-(c-\alpha)\widetilde{\phi}_{-c}^{(0)}\widehat{\widetilde{\phi}}_{-c}\!\!\!\!\!{}^{(0)}.

According to (

\displaystyle\Xi_{1}=0,\quad\Xi_{2}=-2c,\quad\Xi_{3}=-2c,\quad\Xi_{4}=0.

(5.12)

Thus we arrive at

\displaystyle\frac{1}{A}\mathrm{v}(c)\widetilde{\mathrm{v}}(c)+\frac{1}{A}\widehat{\mathrm{v}}(c)\widehat{\widetilde{\mathrm{v}}}(c)-\frac{1}{B}\mathrm{v}(c)\widehat{\mathrm{v}}(c)-\frac{1}{B}\widetilde{\mathrm{v}}(c)\widehat{\widetilde{\mathrm{v}}}(c)=-4cC_{1}C_{2}\left(\frac{1}{A^{2}}-\frac{1}{B^{2}}\right),

(5.13)

where

are defined as in (

This is the ABS H3
(\delta)

Concluding remarks

The purpose of this paper is to bridge the gap between the Dbar method and the
DL approach for lattice KdV type equations.
This has been achieved through formulating these equations in the Dbar method
and investigating the relations between the eigenfunction (and its expansions)
and the DL variables
S^{(i,j)}
and
V(p)
.

We have extended the Dbar (spatial transform) method (introduced by Jaulent and Manna in 1980s)
to the lpKdV equation (
3.15
).
We introduced the Dbar problem (
3.1
),
accompanied with the asymptotic assumption (
3.2
) and the discrete plane wave factor (
3.3
),
which defines the eigenfunction
\phi(p)
of the Lax pair (
3.7
) of the lpKdV equation.
On the other hand, in light of the Cauchy-Pompeiu integral formula, solving the Dbar problem (
3.1
)
can be converted to solving the linear integral equation (
3.22
).
The later is the starting point as well as the central role of the Fokas-Ablowitz DL approach (cf.
[
12
]
).
As usual, expansion of the eigenfunction
\phi(p)
at infinity can lead to the construction of the lpKdV equation,
which has been described in Sec.
3
.
However, as we have shown in Sec.
4
, expansion of
\phi(p)
at a finite point
c
can
give rise to the lpmKdV equation and the lSKdV equation.
Moreover, we have presented in Sec.
4.2.2
a new formulation for the lSKdV equation,
which seems not have been reported in the DL approach or the Cauchy matrix approach.
To obtain the ABS equations with
\delta
-terms, namely,
the H3
(\delta)
, Q1
(\delta)
and Q3
(\delta)
equations,
we have made use of the so-called spectral Wronskian and its double parameter generalization
(see
D(p,q)
in (
5.28
)).
They enable us to introduce the
\delta
-terms.
We have also shown how the NQC equation and the NQC
(\delta)
equation arose from our approach,
which corresponds to the formulation via eigenfunctions given in
[
44
]
.
More than that, in our construction, Q1
(\delta)
is obtained through a direct derivation,
rather than via a degeneration from Q3
(\delta)
(cf.
[
31
,
44
]
).
In Sec.
6
we have presented explicit formulae for
N
-soliton solutions
for the derived discrete equations.
These formulae enable us to elaborate the connection between
\phi(p)
(and its expansions at infinity
and at a finite point
c
) and the DL variables
S^{(i,j)}
and
V(c)
.
Their correspondences are pointed out in Remark
4
in Sec.
6
.

In this paper, we derived the NQC equation that was formulated by
S(a,b)
in the
DL approach or the Cauchy matrix approach (see (
A.15
)).
The formulation of
S(a,b)
in our paper is given by (
5.38
) together with
D(p,q)

defined in (
5.28
) by
\phi(p)
and
\phi(q)
.
Although
\phi(p)
can be formulated as in (
6.22
), corresponding to
the DL variable
V(p)
(see (
A.11
)),
we cannot recover
S(p,q)
from
D(p,q)
together with (
6.22
).
However, we would like to point out that
S(p,q)
defined as in (
A.8
)
can be formulated from a new Dbar problem, which will be explored in a follow-up paper.
In the future, we will also extend our investigation to the Dbar method for the lattice Boussinesq equations
and the lattice KP type equations,
in particular, to pursue its potential utility in seeking the lattice Boussinesq
\delta
-type equations.

Appendix A
Cauchy matrix approach to the lattice KdV type equations

In what follows we sketch how the lpKdV, lpmKdV, lSKdV and NQC equations
arise as closed forms from the Cauchy matrix approach.
One can refer to
[
31
]
or Chapter 9 of
[
22
]
for details.
We adopt the notations used in
[
31
]
.

We start from a Sylvester equation

\boldsymbol{M}\boldsymbol{K}+\boldsymbol{K}\boldsymbol{M}={{\boldsymbol{r}}}\boldsymbol{c}^{T},

(A.1)

where

\boldsymbol{K}=\mathrm{diag}(k_{1},k_{2},\cdots,k_{N}),~~{\boldsymbol{r}}=(\rho_{1},\rho_{2},\cdots,\rho_{N})^{T},~~\boldsymbol{c}=(c_{1},c_{2},\cdots,c_{N})^{T},

(A.2)

\rho_{i}
is the plane wave factor defined as

\rho_{i}=\biggl(\frac{p+k_{i}}{p-k_{i}}\biggr)^{n}\biggl(\frac{q+k_{i}}{q-k_{i}}\biggr)^{m}\rho^{0}_{i},

(A.3)

c_{i}
,
k_{i}
and
\rho^{0}_{i}
are constants,
\boldsymbol{M}
is a“dressed” Cauchy matrix

\boldsymbol{M}=(M_{i,j})_{N\times N},~~M_{i,j}=\frac{\rho_{i}c_{j}}{k_{i}+k_{j}}.

(A.4)

Define the master functions

S^{(i,j)}=\boldsymbol{c}^{T}\,\boldsymbol{K}^{j}(\boldsymbol{I}+\boldsymbol{M})^{-1}\boldsymbol{K}^{i}{\boldsymbol{r}},~~~i,j\in\mathbb{Z},

(A.5)

which compose an infinite symmetric matrix, i.e. (also see
[
50
]
)

S^{(i,j)}=S^{(j,i)},~~~i,j\in\mathbb{Z}.

(A.6)

It can be proved that
\{S^{(i,j)}\}
obey dynamical recurrence relations:

\displaystyle p\widetilde{S}^{(i,j)}-\widetilde{S}^{(i,j+1)}=pS^{(i,j)}+S^{(i+1,j)}-\widetilde{S}^{(i,0)}S^{(0,j)},

(A.7a)

\displaystyle pS^{(i,j)}+S^{(i,j+1)}=p\widetilde{S}^{(i,j)}-\widetilde{S}^{(i+1,j)}+S^{(i,0)}\widetilde{S}^{(0,j)},

(A.7b)

\displaystyle q\widehat{S}^{(i,j)}-\widehat{S}^{(i,j+1)}=qS^{(i,j)}+S^{(i+1,j)}-\widehat{S}^{(i,0)}S^{(0,j)},

(A.7c)

\displaystyle qS^{(i,j)}+S^{(i,j+1)}=q\widehat{S}^{(i,j)}-\widehat{S}^{(i+1,j)}+S^{(i,0)}\widehat{S}^{(0,j)}.

(A.7d)

In addition to
S^{(i,j)}
, we define

S(a,b)=\boldsymbol{c}^{T}(b\boldsymbol{I}+\boldsymbol{K})^{-1}(\boldsymbol{I}+\boldsymbol{M})^{-1}(a\boldsymbol{I}+\boldsymbol{K})^{-1}{\boldsymbol{r}},~~a,b\in\mathbb{C},

(A.8)

which also has the symmetric property

S(a,b)=S(b,a)

(A.9)

and obeys the shift relations

\displaystyle 1-(p+b)\widetilde{S}(a,b)+(p-a)S(a,b)=\widetilde{V}(a)V(b),

(A.10a)

\displaystyle 1-(q+b)\widehat{S}(a,b)+(q-a)S(a,b)=\widehat{V}(a)V(b),

(A.10b)

where

\displaystyle V(a)=1-\boldsymbol{c}^{T}(a\boldsymbol{I}+\boldsymbol{K})^{-1}(\boldsymbol{I}+\boldsymbol{M})^{-1}{\boldsymbol{r}}=1-\boldsymbol{c}^{T}(\boldsymbol{I}+\boldsymbol{M})^{-1}(a\boldsymbol{I}+\boldsymbol{K})^{-1}{\boldsymbol{r}}.

(A.11)

With the help of the symmetric properties (
A.6
) and (
A.9
),
lattice equations can be obtained as closed forms of the recurrence relations (
A.7
) and (
A.10
).
We list them below:

•

lpKdV equation
(w=S^{(0,0)})
:

(p+q+w-\widehat{\widetilde{w}})(p-q+\widehat{w}-\widetilde{w})=p^{2}-q^{2};

(A.12)

•

lpmKdV equation
(v=1-S^{(0,-1)})
:

p(v\widehat{v}-\widetilde{v}\widehat{\widetilde{v}})=q(v\widetilde{v}-\widehat{v}\widehat{\widetilde{v}});

(A.13)

•

lSKdV equation
(z=S^{(-1,-1)}-\frac{n}{p}-\frac{m}{q})
:

\frac{(z-\widetilde{z})(\widehat{z}-\widehat{\widetilde{z}})}{(z-\widehat{z})(\widetilde{z}-\widehat{\widetilde{z}})}=\frac{q^{2}}{p^{2}};

(A.14)

•

NQC equation:

\frac{1-(p+b)\widehat{\widetilde{S}}(a,b)+(p-a)\widehat{S}(a,b)}{1-(q+b)\widehat{\widetilde{S}}(a,b)+(q-a)\widetilde{S}(a,b)}=\frac{1-(q+a)\widehat{S}(a,b)+(q-b)S(a,b)}{1-(p+a)\widetilde{S}(a,b)+(p-b)S(a,b)}.

(A.15)
