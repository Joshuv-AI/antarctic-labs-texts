Chronotype and academic procrastination: the chain mediating role of future self-continuity and self-control
Authors: Jingjing Song, Yilan Lei, Yi Zhang, Xintong Hu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAlthough studies indicate that chronotype might be correlated with procrastination, the specific mechanism of that correlation has not been fully discussed.MethodsThis study investigated the associations of future self-continuity and self-control with the relationship between chronotype and academic procrastination by conducting a questionnaire survey of 774 college students.ResultsA negative correlation was observed between chronotype and academic procrastination, and analyses suggested that future self-continuity and self-control may serve as statistical mediators, both individually and as a chain of mediation.ConclusionsThe present study enriches and deepens our understanding of the potential mechanisms underlying the association between chronotype and academic procrastination, and provides a novel perspective that may inform approaches to the prevention and intervention of academic procrastination.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40359-025-03884-6.Keywords: Chronotype, Future self-continuity, Self-control, Academic procrastination

Introduction

Procrastination means voluntarily postponing a scheduled action, even though the delay will worsen the situation [1]. Procrastination is a prevalent problem, most commonly examined in academic contexts as academic procrastination [2]. Academic procrastination may affect academic performance through individuals’ ineffective meta-cognitive strategies [3, 4]. Procrastination may also contribute to harmful health consequences. For example, bedtime procrastination is an important risk factor associated with insufficient sleep [5].

In recent years, the relationship between chronotype and general procrastination [6–11], especially bedtime procrastination [12–15], has attracted increased attention. These studies indicated a similar relationship whereby eveningness, who prefers a later bedtime and later rising time, tends toward procrastination, particularly bedtime procrastination. The tendency to procrastinate may be due to the lower self-control shown by eveningness [7, 10, 11]. Additionally, a meta-analysis [16] revealed that bedtime procrastination had a moderately positive association with eveningness. This may be explained by the fact that eveningness is associated with a preference for later bedtimes [17], exacerbated by procrastination.

While studies have revealed that chronotype might be associated with procrastination, the specific mechanism has not been thoroughly explored. Studies have verified that morningness is associated with better performance and that eveningness is associated with worse performance in school [18, 19]. Because procrastination is a crucial factor in academic performance [3] and few researchers have directly discussed how chronotype affects academic procrastination [20], it is essential to discuss the possible mechanism underlying the relationship between chronotype and academic procrastination based on the existing research.

Self-control is essential for achieving long-term goals but is a relatively limited cognitive resource [21]. People with greater self-control have a lower tendency to procrastinate [10]. The results from meta-analyses also confirmed that self-control is the most significant personality variable for predicting procrastination [1, 22, 23].

Sirois and Pychyl try to understand procrastination from the perspective of the temporal self and emotion-regulating processes. People tend to procrastinate when facing aversive tasks, and the effects of procrastination are usually on the future self. From a temporal perspective, procrastination reflects not only the priority of immediate mood over future benefits but also the prioritisation of the present self over the future self. When individuals yield to immediate gratification, they limit their ability to project themselves into the future and neglect the well-being of their future selves [24]. In this case, the future self is more like someone else, a stranger [24–26]. Studies have verified that people with greater future self-continuity indeed procrastinate less [25, 26].

Based on the existing research, this study explored the effect of future self-continuity and self-control on the relationship between chronotype and academic procrastination. Despite these separate lines of evidence, prior work has not examined these constructs within a single, integrated framework. In particular, little research has considered how chronotype may relate to future self-continuity, nor how future self-continuity and self-control might jointly account for the link between chronotype and academic procrastination. Addressing this conceptual gap, the present study brings together these perspectives to provide a theoretically grounded explanation for this association. Together, Hershfield’s model of future self-continuity and Identity-Based Motivation theory suggest that connecting the future self to one’s present identity can facilitate identity-consistent self-regulation, providing a coherent theoretical basis for our chain mediation hypothesis. We expect this study to provide fresh insight into how to prevent and intervene in cases of academic procrastination.

Chronotype and academic procrastination

Chronotype is defined as an individual’s differences in the timing of sleep-wake cycles. Human chronotypes can be largely divided into morningness, eveningness, and intermediate-type. Morning-types are characterized by earlier sleep and wake times, whereas evening-types are characterized by later sleep and wake times [27, 28]. Chronotype is associated with individual genetic variation [29] and is relatively stable [30], though it changes across the lifespan: morningness is prevalent in childhood and older age, eveningness emerges in adolescence, and a shift back to morningness occurs in adulthood [31].

As mentioned above, the relationship between chronotype and procrastination has received increasing attention. Research indicates that evening-types generally show avoidant rather than decisional procrastination, which has been related to the perception that performance is better later in the day [6]. In addition, several studies have found that morningness is associated with lower procrastination, while eveningness is associated with higher procrastination. Researchers have speculated that lower self-control is one of the possible reasons [7, 10, 11]. Evening-type individuals often adjust their schedules to meet early school or work demands. These adjustments create gaps between preferred and actual sleep times, leading to circadian misalignment [32]. Eveningness may require additional resources for self-control to cope with circadian misalignment [32], and such persons may therefore procrastinate more [11, 13].

The mediating role of future self-continuity

Chandler defined self-continuity as the degree to which people regard their past, present, and future selves as a continuum [33]. Unlike Chandler, Hershfield focused more on individuals’ perceptions of their present and future selves. He proposed future self-continuity to describe perceptions of the degree of congruence between their present and future selves. In addition, the future self-continuity hypothesis posits that individuals regard their future selves as strangers, distinct from their present selves [34]. Subsequently, Hershfield proposed a theoretical model of future self-continuity. Specifically, he noted that the similarity, vividness, and positivity of future selves collectively affect future self-continuity [35]. This theoretical model also suggests that perceiving the future self as connected to one’s present identity may have broader motivational implications, foreshadowing identity-based explanations discussed later. At present, future self-continuity is often conceptualized as a trait in measurement studies, emphasizing reliable assessment, and as a state in experimental research, emphasizing effective manipulation [34–38].

Given that chronotype shapes individuals’ temporal orientation, time perspective provides a theoretical entry point to understand how chronotype may relate to future self-continuity. Recent research has found that chronotype is significantly correlated with time perspective: morningness is generally linked to a future-oriented perspective, while eveningness is generally linked to present-hedonic and present-fatalistic perspectives [39, 40]. Time perspective not only reflects how individuals organise psychological time [41] but also shapes identity-related thinking [42]. Young people with a future time perspective tend to actively address identity-related challenges and invest in academic or career development [43, 44]. This temporal–identity pattern conceptually links time perspective to future self-continuity, insofar as future-oriented individuals are more likely to see their future self as part of their current identity. According to Hershfield’s theoretical model of future self-continuity, the more positively individuals view their future selves, the more easily they connect their future selves with their present selves [35]. Thus, in the absence of any direct evidence linking chronotype with future self-continuity, we speculated, drawing on indirect evidence from this model, that morningness may be associated with greater future self-continuity, whereas eveningness may be associated with lower future self-continuity.

Blouin-Hudon and Pychyl offered initial empirical evidence that people with lower future self-continuity tend to procrastinate [25]. They argue that individuals with low future self-continuity view their future selves as strangers [24] rather than extensions of the present self, which increases procrastination. The discontinuity between present and future selves may arise partly because of a failure of the imagination. Furthermore, individuals who score higher on vivid mental imagery and positive affect exhibit greater future self-continuity. Vivid imagination increases positive affect, which helps individuals perceive continuity between their present and future selves [25]. Based on this idea, a future self focused mental imagery intervention was designed to reduce procrastination. The results showed that vivid mental images of the future self were crucial for cultivating affective empathy and enhancing future self-continuity [26].

The mediating role of self-control

Chronotype is significantly correlated with self-control: eveningness is associated with lower self-control, whereas morningness is associated with higher self-control [7, 10, 39, 45]. Johnson and Bloom examined the five-factor model of personality in relation to procrastination and found that only conscientiousness and neuroticism were significantly associated with procrastination [46], with conscientiousness negatively correlated with procrastination [47]. Later studies showed that the link between conscientiousness and lower procrastination is mainly due to its self-control component [48, 49]. Subsequently, theories about procrastination, such as temporal motivation theory [50] and conceptual models [51], have incorporated individual differences in self-control into their theoretical models.

Chain mediation of future self-continuity and self-control

Individuals with higher future self-continuity think more about the future consequences of their present actions. Such future-oriented thinking predicts greater self-control, which is linked to better academic performance [52]. In addition, the correlations between all three components of future self-continuity (similarity, vividness, positivity) and self-control were positive and significant [53].

Identity-Based Motivation theory proposes that self-identity is dynamic. Individuals interpret situations in ways consistent with their current identities and prefer actions that align with those identities [54]. It suggests that people commit precious time and spirit to identity-consistent behaviours and that linking the future self with the present self can be viewed as another approach to self-control [55]. Thus, procrastination may, at least in part, reflect difficulties in maintaining future self-continuity and exercising identity-consistent self-control.

Methods

Participants

A cross-sectional design was employed in this study. Participants were university students who were recruited via an online questionnaire using convenience sampling from September to November 2023. Participants were told that participation was voluntary and anonymous and that the data would be kept confidential and used for scientific research only. We collected 838 questionnaires in China and obtained 774 valid data points after excluding those with obvious regular, random, missing, or incorrect values, for an effective rate of 92.36%. An a priori G*Power analysis (multiple regression, f² = 0.20, α = 0.05, power = 0.80, 8 predictors) indicated a required sample of 485, confirming our sample size was sufficient. Participants were aged between 18 and 25 years. Their demographic information is summarized in Table 1.

Measures

Chronotype

Chronotype was quantitatively assessed using the 19-item Chinese version [56] of the Morningness–Eveningness Questionnaire [57], which has demonstrated good reliability and validity in previous studies. The total score ranges from 16 to 86: 16–41 indicates eveningness, 42–58 indicates intermediate type, and 59–86 indicates morningness, with higher total scores reflecting a greater inclination to morningness. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient in the present sample was 0.79.

Academic procrastination

The Chinese version [58] of the Procrastination Assessment Scale-Students [2], which consists of 18 items rated on a Likert scale from ‘1 = never’ to ‘5 = always’, where the higher the score is, the greater the degree of academic procrastination, was used in this study. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient in the present sample was 0.91.

Future self-continuity

This study employed the Chinese version [59] of the Future Self-Continuity Questionnaire [60], which was revised by Zhang et al. It has 10 items with a Likert scale from ‘1 = very little’ to ‘6 = very much’, with higher scores being associated with greater future self-continuity. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient in the present sample was 0.82.

Self-control

The Chinese version [61] of the Self-Control Scale [62] was employed in our study, in which there are 19 items with a Likert scale from ‘1 = not at all’ to ‘5 = completely’, with higher scores being related to stronger self-control. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient in the present sample was 0.87.

Data analysis

SPSS 26.0 was used for descriptive statistics and correlation analysis. Mediation analysis was performed using Model 6 of the PROCESS macro for SPSS version 3.5 [63]. A Monte Carlo power analysis for indirect effects, based on the observed path coefficients, indicated adequate power to detect all key indirect effects (power > 0.89), exceeding the conventional 0.80 threshold.

Prior to the mediation analyses, regression assumptions were examined. All variables showed acceptable skewness and kurtosis (|skewness| < 1, |kurtosis| < 2), suggesting approximate normality. Variance inflation factors (VIFs < 2.0) indicated no multicollinearity. Breusch–Pagan tests indicated heteroscedasticity in two regression equations (p < 0.01), while the third was not significant (p = 0.95). Bootstrapping with 5000 repetitions was applied to address any deviations from the normality of residuals or homoscedasticity. Five outliers were detected, however, results were robust when analyses were conducted with and without these cases, so all data were retained to preserve sample integrity. Indirect effects were evaluated with 5,000 bias-corrected bootstrap resamples (95% CI).

Results

Common method bias

As all measurements in the study were based on self-reports, Harman’s single-factor method was employed to check for common method bias. The results showed that the maximum factor variance explained was 17.42%, which is considerably less than 40%, indicating the lack of any serious common method bias.

Discussion

The study examined the roles of future self-continuity and self-control in the association between chronotype and academic procrastination, suggesting that they may sequentially mediate this relationship. This study firstly examined the relationship between chronotype and future self-continuity, and the results suggested that morningness may be linked with greater future self-continuity, whereas eveningness may be linked with lower future self-continuity. The present study offers insights into the potential mechanisms relating chronotype to academic procrastination and suggests a novel perspective that may be valuable for developing preventive and interventive strategies in future studies.

Chronotype was significantly negatively correlated with academic procrastination, with morningness being less likely to be related to academic procrastination and eveningness being more likely to be related to academic procrastination, which is consistent with the results of existing studies [9, 10, 20]. Hess et al. found that neuroticism mediates the relationship between chronotype and academic procrastination. In particular, tense arousal—a component of neuroticism linked to fear responses—may heighten emotional instability among evening-types, which could increase procrastination as a way to avoid failure-related anxiety. This is the first study to directly explore the relationship between chronotype and academic procrastination and the effect of neuroticism on this relationship [20]. In addition, eveningness may naturally start later in the day to complete tasks that need to be completed. Some individuals interpret this natural delay as a sign of failure, triggering self-critical thoughts and heightened task-related concerns. These reactions may foster avoidance and, consequently, greater procrastination [8]. The finding underscores that explanations of the link between chronotype and academic procrastination should incorporate not only personality-related factors, such as self-control, but also cognitive and affective mechanisms.

This study is, to the best of our knowledge, the first to examine the association between chronotype and future self-continuity. Morningness was linked to higher future self-continuity and eveningness to lower levels, consistent with our hypothesis. We previously speculated that chronotype may affect future self-continuity through the temporal mechanism of time perspective, but this remains to be empirically tested. Morning-type individuals tend to be more future-oriented, engage in more active thinking, and plan for long-term goals [64]. They are more actively engaged in the exploration of the self and in the formation of self-identity [42, 55]. In contrast, evening-type individuals tend to focus on immediate gratification or adopt a fatalistic attitude toward the present, which may reduce engagement with their future self [43]. Previous research shows that the link between chronotype and time perspective depends on age and sex [65]. Morningness is typically associated with a stronger future perspective, whereas eveningness is linked to a stronger present perspective. These patterns are more pronounced in younger individuals—especially young men—and weaker or absent in older or female populations. Building on this, although our study did not directly measure time perspective and only assessed future self-continuity, it is plausible that the observed association between chronotype and future self-continuity may also vary depending on demographic factors. Given that our sample consisted primarily of young university students, the observed effects may mirror patterns more commonly reported among younger populations. Future research should include broader age ranges and both sexes to examine these potential moderating effects.

Furthermore, our findings are consistent with Hershfield’s theoretical model of future self-continuity, which provided the basis for our initial hypothesis. Future work should examine whether chronotypes differ in perceived similarity, vividness, and positivity of the future self. It is also important to test whether these components relate differently to self-control and academic procrastination across chronotypes. These exploratory efforts, grounded in this model, extend its theoretical implications to chronotype-related outcomes from a temporal-self perspective. In addition, intervention implications in terms of Hershfield’s three components—perceived similarity, vividness, and positivity—provides a clearer theoretical basis for understanding how chronotype-related differences in future self-continuity may be addressed.

There was a negative correlation between future self-continuity and academic procrastination, which is consistent with the findings of other studies [25, 26]. When people perceive high future self-continuity, they make better decisions about their future [34]. Our findings can be understood in light of the Identity-Based Motivation theory [54], which posits that greater connectivity and congruence between the present and future self enhance individuals’ motivation to engage in actions that support their future self-identities. When individuals realise that present tasks help construct future identities, they proactively overcome present difficulties to accomplish the task [66, 67] and may therefore avoid procrastination.

Consistent with previous studies, our results suggest that morning-type individuals tend to show higher self-control, whereas evening-type individuals tend to show lower self-control [7, 9–11]. Many studies have shown that morningness performed better than eveningness in various activities that depend on self-control, for example, cognitive performance [68], academic performance [18, 19], physical health [69, 70], and emotional regulation [71]. They also cautiously mentioned that they could not infer causal relationships from their cross-sectional, non-experimental design. It is also possible that self-regulation difficulties make bed and rise times later [7]. Some researchers note that both chronotype and self-control may have constitutional bases, complicating causal inferences. Self-control is a relatively stable trait [72] that develops with executive maturation [29, 73], whereas chronotype is 45–54% heritable [74] and linked to multiple physiological outcomes [75, 76]. These patterns suggest that chronotype may be more biologically driven than self-control, supporting the idea that chronotype could precede self-control in a causal sequence [39].

The present study indicated that self-control was negatively related to academic procrastination. Although many studies have confirmed that self-control reduces procrastination [1, 77], how self-control reduces procrastination remains unclear [78, 79]. The temporal decision model proposes that task willingness depends on process utility (the experience of performing the task) and outcome utility (the value of the outcome). Based on this model, self-control may reduce procrastination by regulating negative emotions, focusing attention on positive outcomes, or shifting attention to alter the relative weight of utilities. These pathways, however, require further empirical testing [80]. It should be noted that students’ procrastination is often associated with emotional and motivational factors, insufficient organizational skills or self-control, and the tendency to adopt maladaptive learning strategies [3, 4], all of which have been found to relate to ineffective studying and lower academic achievement.

Our study showed that future self-continuity and self-control statistically mediate the association between chronotype and academic procrastination. There was a positive correlation between future self-continuity and self-control, consistent with other studies [52, 53]. For long-term decisions, one person needs to have one identity, which requires linking the present self with the future self [35]. Meanwhile, to reap the greatest long-term returns, resisting the temptation to pursue instant gratification usually requires a degree of self-control [81]. Because future consequences influence both future self-continuity and self-control, shifting attention from the present to the future may help students exercise self-control and regulate their behaviour for better academic outcomes [52]. Researchers identified two prerequisites to the decision to study without procrastination: motivation and a study plan. People with high future self-continuity tend to perceive that current behaviours are linked to future development, which in turn may be associated with greater study motivation. Effective self-control, in turn, is likely to support the implementation of a study schedule. Hence, academic procrastination-related studies should concern themselves with the role of both future self-continuity and self-control [82].

This study has several limitations. First, because this study employed a cross-sectional design, causal inferences cannot be drawn from the observed associations. Future research employing longitudinal or experimental designs is needed to establish temporal precedence and clarify potential causal pathways. Second, the research mainly used self-assessment questionnaires. Future studies should also incorporate objective measures, such as combining self-report chronotype questionnaires with sleep diaries or actigraphy. Finally, the generalizability of our findings is constrained by the demographic characteristics of our sample, which consisted mainly of young university students. Chronotype [31] and time perspective [65] vary across age, and future studies including broader age ranges are needed to determine whether the chronotype–FSC association is consistent across developmental stages. Although sex differences in chronotype and time perspective have been reported in some populations [65], the present study was not designed to examine sex as a moderator, and future work with more demographically diverse samples may clarify whether these effects vary across sex. Additionally, cultural factors—such as variations in time perspective [83] and self-construal [84] —may also shape how strongly individuals connect with their future selves, suggesting that the chronotype–FSC link may not generalize uniformly across cultural contexts.

Before concluding, it is worth emphasizing that interventions derived from Hershfield’s model can be articulated in theoretically precise and empirically testable ways [85]. Imagery-based tasks [25, 26] and structured writing prompts [86]—both shown to strengthen future self-continuity—represent concise formats suitable for experimental evaluation. A key direction for future research is to determine which underlying mechanism is most malleable for evening-type individuals, such as whether enhancing future-self vividness through episodic future thinking or increasing perceived similarity via brief trait-reflection exercises yields stronger effects. Further work may also assess whether these mechanism-focused interventions can reduce procrastination among evening-types.

Conclusions

This study investigated how future self-continuity and self-control relate to the association between chronotype and academic procrastination. Analyses suggested a potential chain mediation involving both variables. Individual differences in chronotype should be taken into account when designing prevention and intervention strategies for academic procrastination. These results provide implications that interventions targeting future self-continuity could be considered as a potential approach to addressing eveningness-related academic procrastination.
