Hybrid magnetic-electric actuation for enhanced motion control and multi-surface operation of Janus microrobots
Authors: Ido Rachbuch, Sinwook Park, Yuval Katz, Touvia Miloh, Gilad Yossifon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study presents a hybrid actuation framework for enhanced and automated control of Janus particle microrobots across multiple surfaces. Under various magnetic and electric field combinations, Janus particles act as active microscale agents with diverse propulsion and navigation modes on a single surface, while their electric response additionally provides frequency-modulated functionalities such as reversible cargo loading. Here, we show that by further leveraging magnetic levitation and electrostatic trapping at a microchamber ceiling against gravity, these particles can transition between surfaces on demand, extending their mobility beyond the limitations of many traditional microrobots which are surface-bound. This approach grants access to three-dimensional workspaces without continuous surface contact, enabling capabilities such as vertical obstacle crossing, navigation of elevated surfaces, discrete surface patterning, and cargo transport between surfaces. Together, these developments establish a versatile micro-robotic platform applicable in microfluidic and lab-on-a-chip systems, and may further inspire microrobot designs utilizing controlled inter-surface transitions.

Introduction

Hybrid microrobots, combining various energy sources for propulsion and steering (e.g., chemical and magnetic1, optical and electric2, acoustic and magnetic3), have demonstrated significant potential for environmental and biomedical applications4 due to their enhanced operational robustness, complementary actuation, and ease of integrating diverse functionalities, including drug delivery, bio-sensing and pollutant degradation5,6. Among these, electrically powered microrobots offer distinct capabilities, such as selective cargo transport7,8, immunosensing9, and local stimulation10,11, which can be tuned by adjusting the applied alternating current (AC) frequency. Meanwhile, magnetically powered microrobots have been extensively studied due to their remote operability with high precision under various forms of magnetic fields12, as well as across diverse environmental conditions, including varying medium properties (e.g., pH and conductivity)13 and resistive fluid flows14. Recognizing the complementary advantages of these two modalities, hybrid systems that combine magnetic and electric powering can offer increased versatility in both motion control and functional operation. For instance, magnetic actuation can compensate for the quenching of electrokinetic propulsion with increasing solution conductivity15,16, which effectively ceases propulsion at conductivities exceeding ≈0.3 mS cm−1. However, other electric field-driven functions (e.g., reversible cargo loading) remain effective in both low- and high-conductivity (mS cm−1) conditions17, enabling synergistic interactions unattainable by magnetic actuation alone. Thus, to fully realize the potential of such a hybrid system, it is essential to understand the various possible combinations of the contributing components, each with its own benefits, and to integrate them effectively for optimized control.

In this study, we focus on metallo/magneto-dielectric Janus particles (JPs)—asymmetric microspheres partially coated with a hemispherical metal cap—which serve as one of the simplest microrobot models for exploring hybrid magnetic and electric actuation18,19. These particles can self-propel under uniform AC electric fields via induced-charge electrophoresis (ICEP)15 or self-dielectrophoresis (sDEP)20,21, depending on the AC frequency, while also responding to magnetic fields for steering22 and rolling23. A key feature of JPs is their ability to act as mobile microelectrodes7, enabling selective cargo manipulation and field-controlled interactions with both synthetic and biological targets, including polystyrene beads8,9, bacteria10 and mammalian cells11,24.

Within the wider field of microrobotics, propulsion modes can be grouped into three broad categories. The first category includes swimmers25—oscillatory, pulsed, or rotating field-driven microrobots that navigate through bulk fluids by generating net thrust, often via helical geometries26 or induced asymmetric flow27,28. These can offer full three-dimensional (3D) mobility, but typically require complex fabrication methods, such as two-photon polymerization26, and in some cases are not trivial to control for precise trajectory following28. The second category comprises levitated microrobots, which achieve precise 3D positioning through spatial field-gradients (e.g., magnetic29–31 or acoustic32) and feedback control, enabling simultaneous lifting against gravity and lateral translation. While powerful, such systems typically rely on specialized multi-source setups and sustained high-magnitude gradient fields (e.g., 102–103 mT m−1 magnetic field gradients30,31,33) which may demand high power compared to uniform-field actuation. The third category, where JPs are typically situated, consists of “surface-assisted” or “surface-bound” microrobots12, which rely on continuous interaction with a nearby physical boundary to generate net translational motion (e.g., via rolling, walking34, or sDEP propulsion21). This constraint, combined with gravitational sedimentation, usually confines their motion to two-dimensional (2D) workspaces. Several studies have demonstrated 3D maneuverability of surface-bound microrobots by utilizing 3D surface topographies, e.g., by climbing inclined boundaries35 or traversing closed tubular walls36. While these approaches are effective, they still rely on continuous surface contact, such that the surface geometry dictates possible trajectories and ultimately limits versatility, for example, when encountering high vertical walls.

Here, we address these constraints and employ diverse magnetic and electric field combinations to expand the operational versatility of JPs in two main directions. First, we demonstrate enhanced and automated motion control on a single surface by characterizing and integrating several conventional actuation mechanisms into closed-loop navigation, including electric propulsion with magnetic steering, and fully magnetic rolling and steering. In the latter case, we also incorporate an orthogonal electric field component, which induces alignment and surface interactions that increase translation speeds and enable stabilized cargo manipulation capabilities, as demonstrated in our recent work17. Additionally, we introduce a navigation scheme that combines magnetic rolling about a fixed rotation axis (thereby modeling simplified operating conditions using a permanent magnet with a single rotation axis) with perpendicular electric propulsion. Notably, these control strategies rely on uniform electric fields generated via simple parallel-plate electrodes, and uniform magnetic fields (~3.5 mT) requiring power levels well below the capacity of the field generator used in this study (see Supplementary Fig. 9 and Supplementary Note 4). Second, we extend the mobility of JPs beyond traditional surface-bound motion through a distinct 2.5-dimensional (2.5D) actuation paradigm. By combining magnetic levitation with electrostatic trapping against gravity, we achieve controlled transitions between the floor and the ceiling of a microchamber, as well as intermediate surfaces. This requires the use of magnetic gradient fields only for inter-surface transitions while allowing the same uniform-field actuation modes to operate on multiple surfaces, thereby effectively increasing the available workspace and enabling programmable trajectories that bridge surface-confinement and fully 3D mobility, without the need for sustained gradient fields, complex microrobot designs, or specialized surface topographies.

The resulting 2.5D navigation approach supports a range of functional operations and practical tasks. We experimentally demonstrate capabilities such as crossing vertical walls and navigating on their surface, patterning of the surface at discrete locations while avoiding the surrounding area (e.g., for localized in vitro drug delivery during rolling via biochemical surface treatment36–38), and executing “pick-and-place” cargo transport between separate compartments. Furthermore, the ability to perform controlled transitions between the bottom and top substrate while dwelling at selected locations may be relevant in several other microscale application scenarios. These include, for example, localized and target-specific (e.g., by approaching a single cell from above and interacting with its upper surface11) sensing and stimulation (e.g., bio/chemical9,39–41, electrical10,11,42,43, mechanical44–46, thermal47,48) by the microrobot, and targeted deposition of particles and cargo for 3D micro-assembly49.

Results

Hybrid magnetic-electric actuation in a 3D workspace

Figure 1A illustrates the experimental workspace with some of the various combinations of electric and magnetic field actuations for controlling the motion of metallo/magneto-dielectric Janus particles (JPs) both in-plane and out-of-plane, enabling them, for example, to cross barriers or descend onto elevated surfaces. These combinations include electric propulsion with magnetic steering, and magnetic rolling and steering enhanced by electric-field induced alignment and surface interaction. A uniform AC electric field applied between parallel ITO-coated glass slides drives the in-plane self-propulsion of JPs. Depending on the applied frequency, JPs propel with either their dielectric (polystyrene) side or metal-coated (Cr-Ni-Au) side facing forward, exhibiting induced-charge electrophoretic (ICEP) or self-dielectrophoretic (sDEP) propulsion, respectively. ICEP propulsion occurs at lower frequencies (~103–104 Hz), while sDEP propulsion becomes dominant at higher frequencies (~104–105 Hz), beyond a critical transition point (depending on solution conductivity and particle size), where propulsion directions reverses20. At even higher frequencies, in the MHz range, both mechanisms are suppressed, leaving only electro-orientation and electrostatic interactions with the substrate (electrode). The magnetic field, generated by a commercially available electromagnet system (MagnebotiX), provides enhanced versatility. Experiments were performed with JPs of 10- and 27-μm diameter within designated microchambers, in a baseline KCl solution with a conductivity of ~4 µS cm−1. Under these conditions, 10-µm JPs exhibited ICEP-sDEP transition at a critical frequency of ~2–3 kHz, whereas 27-μm JPs showed this transition at ~1–2 kHz. The motion of JPs was controlled in both open-loop and closed-loop configurations. For open-loop control, we developed an integrated software and hardware system (see Methods section) to simultaneously manage the AC electric field and the magnetic field, allowing intuitive manual manipulation via keyboard inputs (Fig. 1B and Supplementary Fig. 1). Magnetic actuation parameters included field direction, magnitude, and rotation frequency (for magnetic rolling), while electric propulsion velocity and alignment depended on voltage and frequency, enabling versatile control of motion in various directions and magnitudes. By adjusting these parameters through our control system, we achieved precise microrobot navigation at the surface, and additionally utilized magnetic field gradients for inter-surface transitions. For closed-loop control, we incorporated high-level operations such as real-time visual tracking and path planning. Desired paths were defined as sequences of linearly connected waypoints, which could be arbitrarily selected or derived from predefined shapes.

Since the JPs were not premagnetized in a fixed direction, the thin ferromagnetic nickel (Ni) layer of their metallic hemisphere was dynamically magnetized by the applied magnetic fields (up to 20 mT) during manipulation, with some remanent magnetization persisting due to magnetic hysteresis. Previous studies36 examined hysteresis loops of sputtered Ni films on similar Janus microspheres, magnetized to saturation under strong fields up to 1.8 T, and reported a coercivity of ~20 mT. This implies that the modest field strengths used here were sufficient to repeatedly alter the magnetization of the particles. Under uniform (i.e., gradient-free) magnetic fields, the induced magnetization is dominated by the shape anisotropy of the metallic hemisphere, driving the JP into energetically favorable orientations. The long (easy) axis of the hemisphere, which lies approximately parallel to the JP’s dielectric-metal interface (see Supplementary Fig. 3), tends to align with the field direction. Similarly, the uniform AC electric field between the parallel ITO electrodes induces an electric dipole in the gold (Au) cap of the metallic hemisphere, favoring alignment of the same axis with the field. Due to the JP’s axial symmetry around its perpendicular short axis, it can still rotate freely about this axis without disturbing alignment. Thus, both the magnetic and electric fields impose a single rotational constraint, each reducing the particle’s rotational degrees of freedom (DOFs) by one (see Supplementary Fig. 4). When applied simultaneously in non-parallel directions, the combined constraints reduce the available rotational DOFs from three to one. However, since the electric field is fixed along the z-axis (normal to the ITO substrates), two remaining DOFs can still be utilized by the magnetic field to induce rotations about the z-axis and the JP’s symmetry axis. Given that electric propulsion drives translation toward one of the hemispheres, this arrangement enables magnetic field-induced steering and rolling in orthogonal directions, minimizing interference and fostering a synergistic interaction between the two mechanisms.

Figure 2A shows the typical case of fully magnetic rolling and steering, where motion is directed by setting the magnetic field rotation axis perpendicular to the desired trajectory, as enabled by the arbitrary field generator used here or by other coil configurations such as Helmholtz pairs. Figure 2B presents the same configuration combined with a high-frequency electric field, introducing several effects. The electric field acts on the induced electric dipole at the Au layer of the JP and strongly aligns its dielectric-metal interface orthogonal to the substrate, thereby suppressing wobbling and enabling stabilized cargo loading while rolling17. Additionally, it enhances the effective contact with the substrate due to electrostatic attraction50, resulting in increased linear velocity for the same magnetic field rotation frequency. This increase in speed naturally raises the likelihood of overshooting corners in the trajectory, which can be reduced by adjusting the rotation rate. The rolling velocity versus rotation rate with and without the electric field is further quantified in Fig. 2E. Notably, the electric field also shifts down the magnetic rolling step-out frequency, especially for 27-μm JPs. Step-out occurs when the magnetic torque is insufficient to keep the particle’s rotation synchronized with the rotating magnetic field51. Without the electric field, this limit stems mostly from viscous drag exerted by the surrounding fluid and hydrodynamic wall interactions. With the addition of the field, the observed shift indicates larger resistance to rotation, which could be attributed to the increased electrostatic interactions with the substrate. However, by balancing the AC voltage with the magnetic field magnitude and rotation rate, the combined actuation can be utilized for its advantages (e.g., higher speed, effective cargo loading) while avoiding the step-out regime.

Figure 2C demonstrates electric propulsion governed by either ICEP or sDEP, determined by the AC field frequency, with guidance by magnetic steering. This strategy is particularly advantageous for selective cargo transport and sensing, as the electric propulsion couples with reversible dielectrophoretic (DEP) trapping mechanisms, either positive (pDEP) or negative (nDEP), depending on the cargo type and AC frequency7,8. The direction of motion is controlled by adjusting the azimuthal angle Φ of the planar magnetic field component Bxy, which is parallel to the substrate. This approach relies on the alignment of the JP’s dielectric-metal interface about the z-axis, such that Bxy is roughly perpendicular to the propulsion direction. However, since the alignment is not exact, precise azimuthal control cannot be achieved by simply setting the desired Φ, and instead requires feedback strategies. Here, we implemented basic proportional (P-) control (see Methods), resulting in trajectories with gently curved turns rather than sharp corners, and overshoots that increased with linear velocity. The accuracy of this method can be readily improved with a PID controller.

The inclination angle of the magnetic field relative to the z-axis, Θ (see Fig. 7B), also plays a crucial role. While a fully planar field (Θ=90°) might seem ideal for steering, our experiments revealed that propulsion efficiency can vary significantly with Θ. Certain angles maximize velocity, whereas others, including Θ=90°, can cause a complete halt. Supplementary Movie 8 demonstrates this effect, showing two JPs subjected to identical fields where changes in Θ result in one particle propelling efficiently while the other remains stationary. Theoretically, this variability cannot be explained by misalignment of the metallic hemisphere relative to the substrate, since the electric field enforces alignment of the interface plane with the z-axis. This was confirmed by measurements of the hemisphere’s tilt angle α relative to the substrate (Supplementary Fig. 5), showing that α remains nearly constant under an electric field regardless of Θ. Instead, the observed differences may result from small rotations of the JP about its symmetry axis, normal to the interface plane, as propulsion efficiency may be sensitive to inhomogeneities in the Au coating or local defects that interfere at specific angles. To mitigate this in practice, Θ was manually calibrated for each microrobot and frequency regime to identify an optimal value prior to closed-loop navigation. This step could also be automated in future implementations.

Figure 2D presents another navigation approach, referred to as cross-axis motion, in which magnetic rolling is restricted to a single axis and combined with frequency-controlled orthogonal electric mobility via ICEP and sDEP propulsion. Unlike the steering approach in Fig. 2C, this method alternates between magnetic and electric propulsion modes to achieve discrete movements in four cardinal directions (left, right, up, down), resulting in a characteristic “staircase” trajectory. This strategy prioritizes simplicity and hardware accessibility over trajectory precision, enabling implementation with a minimal setup. Namely, a single permanent magnet mounted on a DC motor for rotation about a fixed axis, and a function generator with three interchangeable outputs (see example in Supplementary Fig. 7 and Supplementary Movie 9). Furthermore, since the JP maintains an approximately constant orientation while moving, it can consistently approach samples or cargo from any of the four directions, either with the apex of one of its hemispheres or with its interface region—each providing distinct interaction modes.

For x-axis (left-right) motion, the magnetic rotation frequency ω was fixed at ωfast = 5 Hz (300 rpm) in alternating directions, while a high-frequency electric field (5 MHz) suppressed orthogonal electric mobility, eliminating the need to toggle the voltage between electric propulsion and magnetic rolling modes. y-axis (up-down) motion was achieved by setting the appropriate AC frequency for ICEP and sDEP, with the magnetic field remaining active and rotating at a slower rate of ωslow = 0.5 Hz (30 rpm). Keeping the magnetic field active is crucial for maintaining y-axis alignment during electric propulsion, as random orientation shifts tend to occur without it. This is also inevitable in setups based on a permanent magnet, which cannot be deactivated. Additionally, simply stopping the magnetic field’s rotation and holding it at its settled position would result in random inclination angles, often hindering electric propulsion due to the alignment sensitivity described previously. Instead, slow rotation maintains propulsion by continuously sampling favorable angles, albeit at the cost of a slight diagonal displacement instead of a purely perpendicular one. Furthermore, it often helps in suppressing local immobilization of the JP caused by surface absorption or interactions with contaminants.

Figure 2F presents the trajectory tracking errors (Chamfer distance, see Methods) and average motion speeds for each control strategy, measured across repeated trials of the lemniscate path with different JPs and actuation parameters. Additional data and statistical summaries, including the Hausdorff distance metric, are available in Supplementary Fig. 8 and Supplementary Table 3. The correlation between higher speed and larger errors (lower accurcay), particularly evident for the steering approach, highlights the expected trade-off. However, overall, the errors can be considered low, as the maximum Chamfer distance remained below 5 μm, approximately half the microrobot size (10 µm), and average Hausdorff distances were on the order of ~7 µm, indicating high positional accuracy. The optimal operation region corresponds to maximum speed and minimum error. Among the control strategies, magnetic rolling alone achieved the lowest average errors, while its combination with the electric field performed nearly equivalently (average Chamfer distances of ~0.7 and ~1 µm, respectively), but attained higher average translation speeds under similar magnetic field rotation rates, as described before. Magnetically steered electric propulsion reached comparable average velocities (up to ~45 µm s−1), though with a larger growth of tracking error at higher speeds. Cross-axis motion exhibited the least favorable speed-accuracy trade-off, with lower average speeds and moderately higher errors. Nevertheless, it maintained generally low errors (Chamfer distance <3 μm) and consistently reached all waypoints, demonstrating robust performance despite its relative precision limits. Further optimization of this actuation mode could be achieved, for example, by introducing velocity modulation through dynamic adjustments of the magnetic field rotation rate and AC voltage.

Closed-loop repeatability vs. open-loop response

While our control system targets a single actively controlled particle, other particles may be present in the area and thus exposed to the same global (i.e., effectively homogenous across the workspace) magnetic and electric fields. Such passively influenced particles exhibit diverse responses in velocity, directionality and propulsion efficiency, resulting in unpredictable trajectories. This variability primarily arises from particle inhomogeneity, including size distribution, fabrication inconsistencies, and the accumulation of microscopic debris on individual particles. Additional factors include variations in initial particle orientations and differing local particle-surface interactions, which can cause temporary immobilization or abrupt changes in direction.

We leverage this behavior to evaluate the robustness of our closed-loop control strategies in comparison to both uncontrolled particles responding to shared fields, and open-loop sequences without feedback. The actively controlled particle consistently maintained its trajectory across repetitions, whereas uncontrolled particles exhibited shifts, scaling or rotations. Panels G–J extend this comparison to open-loop sequences (using 10-µm JPs), where predefined commands were repeated at fixed intervals. Quantitative analysis of trajectory repeatability for the presented sessions and additional trials, using the first repetition as the reference path (see Methods), is summarized in Supplementary Tables 4 and 5. Closed-loop strategies maintained low tracking errors across repetitions (average Chamfer distance of ~1.1 µm, and Hausdorff distance of ~4.7 µm), while both uncontrolled responses and open-loop sequences exhibited substantially larger errors (average Chamfer distances of 14.6 and 6.2 µm, and Hausdorff distances of 53.8 and 19.7 µm, respectively). For the uncontrolled response, these deviations can be traced to distinct factors in each actuation mode. For example, in hybrid magnetic rolling, small differences in particle size and heterogenous responses to the electric field alignment caused by debris or defects on individual particles result in varying linear velocities for the same rotation frequency. By contrast, in magnetically steered electric propulsion, variations in initial particle orientations produce rotated trajectories, while differences in optimal magnetic inclination angles lead to varying motion speeds and distorted trajectories. For the open-loop response, such difference is inherently expected, since without feedback disturbances result in accumulated deviations.

These results confirm that our closed-loop implementations are both adaptable and reliable for programmed navigation tasks, whereas open-loop sequences—like the uncontrolled response—are prone to drift and fail to achieve consistent outcomes. At the same time, beyond validating our single-particle control framework, these experiments provide a clear illustration of a core challenge in microrobotics, which is parallel navigation of multiple agents either identically or independently under global shared fields. Addressing this challenge was not the focus of the present study, although a conceptual example of separated control (via adjustment of the magnetic field inclination angle during electric propulsion) is presented in Supplementary Movie 8, as described above. However, this demonstration highlights why we emphasize targeted actuation of a single active microrobot, which is still capable of highly localized interventions. Additionally, it shows that our control method may be extended to small particle-groups, with inherent variability, as long as a selected leading particle is being actively tracked and controlled. In the following section, we shift the attention into extending this control paradigm to operation within a 3D workspace.

Magnetic and electric field-based 2.5-dimesnional manipulation

Here, we employ magnetic field gradients to levitate JPs, and use electrostatic trapping at the chamber ceiling to hold them against gravity, enabling their controlled release and descent by switching off the electric field. We demonstrate that the same actuation mechanisms used at the chamber floor can be applied to the top surface, allowing to perform diverse 2.5-dimensional (2.5D) trajectories. While magnetophoresis can achieve full 3D manipulation by volumetric positioning of magnetized objects in viscous liquids, the relatively weak magnetization of our JPs, induced at their thin Ni layer, cannot generate sufficient force for significant lateral translation in addition to levitation. This limitation becomes particularly evident when operating with distant magnetic sources over a large workspace, especially with electromagnets, due to the practical constraints on field strength and operation time, which are limited by the risk of overheating when increasing the currents. Thus, we use the term “2.5D” to describe movement in 3D space that remains partially constrained to discrete planes or surfaces, yet unlocks additional motion capabilities.

Figure 4A depicts the dynamic lifting process of the JP that ends with its attachment to the chamber ceiling. Initially, the microrobot rests at equilibrium on the floor, with the heavier, metal-coated hemisphere facing downward. At t = 0, the magnetic field is activated, with both the field vector B and intensity gradient ∇|B| oriented at the +z direction, mimicking the effect of a single permanent- or electromagnet operating from above. The metallic hemisphere, with magnetic dipole m in its Ni layer, rapidly aligns with the field by the magnetic torque T = m × B, while upward translation of the JP is driven by the magnetophoretic force F = (m ⋅ ∇)B. With the dipole aligned (i.e., m || B), and assuming a point dipole, this reduces to F = m∇|B|. Assuming low remanence and coercivity, where induced magnetization is linear and dominates over magnetic hysteresis (i.e., m ∝ B), the relation becomes F ∝ ∇(|B|2). In both cases, the force is directed along ∇|B| at the +z direction, yielding the desired upward pulling. To generate sufficient force to overcome gravity and achieve lifting within reasonable intervals (~20 s), relatively high field magnitudes were required, demanding high coil currents (see Supplementary Fig. 9). For 10-µm JPs, lifting typically required ~15 mT fields with ~2000 mT m−1 gradients, while heavier 27-µm JPs needed ~17.5 mT fields and ~2500 mT m−1 gradients. This reflects the small fraction of the magnetic Ni layer relative to the total particle volume. Greater lifting forces could be obtained, for instance, by placing stronger dedicated magnets directly above the sample, or by increasing the Ni content of the microrobot. During ascent, the JP progressively shifts out of the image’s focal point, appearing larger and blurrier, until reaching the ceiling. At that point, activation of the AC electric field generates a quasi-electrostatic attraction force sufficient to hold the JP against its weight once the magnetic lifting is switched off.

The vertical motion of the JP during both lifting and descent phases was assumed to follow a straight path along the z-axis (normal to the image plane), with minimal lateral displacement. By measuring three independent lift and descent sequences of 10-µm JPs (see Source Data File), we observed average lateral drifts of ~10.3 µm during lifting and ~1.2 µm during descent from the initial XY position on the bottom or top surface, respectively, to the final position on the opposite surface. Both values are within the scale of the JP diameter, supporting the validity of the fully vertical translation assumption. The slightly larger drift observed during lifting likely originates from a small mismatch between the expected and actual magnetic field gradient produced by the field generator.

Figure 4B illustrates the concept of 2.5D trajectories, following a simple rectangular (box) path. In this example, the microrobot is actuated on the bottom surface along the first two edges, lifted and held at the top surface to traverse the remaining edges, and finally descends back to the starting point once the electrostatic force is disengaged. The realization of such a trajectory with manual control is demonstrated in Fig. 4C–E and Supplementary Movie 3, showcasing various modes of actuation. Namely, magnetic rolling with a high-frequency electric field (Fig. 4C), which is necessary for continuous ceiling trapping, ICEP propulsion guided by magnetic steering (Fig. 4D), and the same with sDEP propulsion (Fig. 4E). An important effect to account for during magnetic rolling on the top surface is the inversion of the rotation axis, as the rolling boundary inducing symmetry-breaking lies on the opposite side relative to the microrobot. It should also be noted that while the magnetic actuation setup used here technically enables superimposed magnetic field rotation with upward lifting gradients for inverted rolling, such implementation requires rapidly shifting high coil currents for prolonged periods, stretching hardware limits. In contrast, the proposed approach relying on electrostatic force to carry the weight, eliminates the need for these strong sustained gradients, allowing to use low-magnitude uniform fields for rotation.

To explore the limits of the electrostatic trapping mechanism, we thoroughly investigated its capability to hold the particle at the ceiling against gravity. Varying the AC frequencies, we performed a voltage scan, from higher to lower voltage, to determine the threshold voltage (VT) at which the JP escapes the electrostatic trap and falls down (Supplementary Movie 4). The particles were not intentionally propelled during this experiment, yet exhibited different levels of electrokinetic motion depending on the applied frequency. In most cases, near VT the propulsion was very weak, with the velocity approaching zero. The results of this scan, depicted in Fig. 4F, can be directly translated to the corresponding electric field magnitude if divided by the chamber height of ~120 μm. In the baseline low-conductivity (~4 μS cm−1) solution, for 10- and 27- μm JPs, VT remained at a minimum plateau in most of the examined frequency range (beyond 2 kHz), before rising significantly at high frequencies approaching 10 MHz, close to the upper limit of the measurement range (20 MHz). When comparing to bare polystyrene (PS) particles in the same solution conductivity, we observed a significant rise in VT for those particles also at low frequencies below 10 kHz. We suspected that this rise resulted from the weaker electrostatic force acting on the smaller induced dipole within the dielectric PS compared to the JP’s Au-coated hemisphere, revealing an attenuation of the force in general at these frequencies.

Examining the threshold in higher solution conductivities of 0.05, 0.5, and 1 mS cm−1 confirmed the same behavior observed with PS particles, with VT increasing markedly at lower frequencies. Additionally, increasing conductivity progressively narrowed the low-voltage plateau and shifted it toward higher VT values. At these conductivities, the threshold at many sub-MHz frequencies exceeded the 20 Vpp limit of our setup, preventing measurement of stable attachment. Nevertheless, a distinct minimum in VT persisted around 5 MHz (circled in Fig. 4F), where voltages remained below 20 Vpp across all conductivities, though still gradually increasing with conductivity. This identifies 5 MHz as a robust operating point for idle ceiling-attachment and inverted magnetic rolling, whereas ICEP- and sDEP-based propulsion on the ceiling remain limited to lower-conductivity media.

The experimental trends were qualitatively captured by our theoretical model (Fig. 4G), based on the equivalent dipole of a metallo-dielectric Janus particle52,53. This model considers the RC frequency of the induced electric double layer54, electrode screening55, and the Maxwell-Wagner high-frequency dispersion56 (details in Supplementary Note 1). The surface conductance Ks was used as a fitting parameter, with the value of 100 nS showing the best agreement with experimental data. This relatively large value compared to previous experimental estimations (e.g., Ks = 2.3 nS for 4.8-μm polystyrene beads57) likely reflects additional contributions not accounted for in our simplified formulation, such as the particle-wall interactions. Thus, Ks is regarded as an effective parameter. However, the model generally reproduces the observed increase in VT at lower frequencies with increasing solution conductivity, and captures the plateau region at intermediate frequencies.

We further measured the effect of magnetic rolling speed on VT under the baseline conductivity of 4 μS cm−1. Various magnetic field rotation rates were examined at fixed AC frequencies of 750 kHz and 5 MHz, where electric propulsion is minimized. Compared to the idle state (without rolling), we observed a shift of 1–2 V at lower rotation rates, which levels off at around 10 Hz (Supplementary Fig. 6). This shift is minor relative to the differences observed in varying AC frequencies, and is therefore likely due to increased disturbances of the electrostatic equilibrium, raising the likelihood of detachment. This behavior indicates that magnetic rolling on the ceiling remains effective across the examined rotation rates (up to 40 Hz), provided that the AC voltage remains within a safe range relative to the measured threshold values.

Figure 5A presents an interplanar navigation session with electrically enhanced magnetic rolling, tracing the initials “TAU”, where the letter “A” segment occurs on the chamber ceiling. Starting on the bottom surface, this trajectory involves one transition per motion plane, with automated application and deactivation of the required magnetic and electric fields for the lifting and descent phases. The high-frequency (5 MHz) electric field is inherently needed for electrostatic trapping at the ceiling, and is therefore inseparable from the control scheme. Figure 5B demonstrates how automated interplanar transitions can be harnessed to produce discrete patterns on one selected surface. This could be practically useful in scenarios where the moving microrobot exerts an effect on the substrate (e.g., “writing”58 or localized stimulations11,43), enabling separated interventions at desired locations while avoiding unnecessary interactions in other areas. Furthermore, directing microrobots to specific positions on the upper substrate and releasing them on-demand resembles the operation of a “pick-and-place” manipulator, with potential applications for example in targeted cargo transport between compartments59 and in the assembly of microstructures60,61.

Accomplishing this type of trajectories in a fully closed-loop manner would require knowing the exact moment the microrobot reaches the top surface when being lifted, and the moment it reaches the bottom after descending. This could likely be achieved with a sequence of calibrations and image processing methods. However, to avoid additional complexity, we employed a more convenient approach, adding one open-loop component to our control algorithm while keeping it fully automated. By measuring the approximate time it takes for a JP to be lifted and to descend, we simply added an adjustable standby phase during each of these events, after which control was resumed. For example, ~15–20 s for lifting and ~18 s for descending were typical values in our setup for 10-μm JPs, while 27-μm JPs generally required shorter intervals of ~10–20 s to lift and ~4.5 s to descend. Theoretical calculations, accounting for gravity, buoyancy, drag and wall effects62,63, as well as non-uniform metallic coating due to physical vapor deposition64,65 (see Supplementary Note 2 and Supplementary Table 1), estimated sedimentation times of 17.8 s for 10-μm JPs and 4.8 s for 27-μm JPs when dropped from the chamber ceiling, demonstrating a strong correlation with the observed values. Small deviations can be attributed to heterogenous particle sizes, fabrication inconsistencies, and observational uncertainties due to manual visual assessment of particle focus stabilization upon reaching the floor.

Figure. 5C displays automated obstacle crossing via magnetic rolling on the ceiling, conducted in a specialized chamber containing several U-shaped SU-8 structures (see Supplementary Fig. 2). These structures, reaching a uniform height of ~50 μm within the ~120-μm chamber gap, were wide enough so that a single wall could effectively block direct or lateral passage on the bottom, while still permitting crossing from above. Their transparency also enabled continuous visibility and tracking of the microrobot when positioned above. Therefore, they could serve either as 3D obstacles, or as elevated platforms for descending upon and traveling atop the structure itself (Fig. 5F). The crossing trajectory began on the bottom surface, with the first waypoint placed within the obstacle’s upper surface, emphasizing that the microrobot could not reach it from below due to collision with the wall. We then manually switched to the next waypoint to continue with the programmed path, in which the microrobot was lifted to the ceiling and guided toward the obstacle, where it successfully crossed from above. Upon reaching the opposite side, the microrobot was automatically lowered by switching off the electric field. Finally, before settling at the target destination, another unreachable waypoint within the obstacle was tested, reaffirming that crossing from below was obstructed. Throughout this session, the image focus was fixed at an intermediate level between the bottom and the top surfaces, to minimize visual distortions caused by the obstacle edges and to ensure continuous tracking.

During our initial crossing trials, several challenges and effects were observed, as shown in Fig. 5D, E and Supplementary Movie 6. When approaching the obstacle from the top substrate, the microrobot encountered resistance from a force that slowed its motion and pushed it back when propulsion was halted. Then, upon entering the middle region directly above the obstacle, the microrobot could lose attachment to the ceiling, causing it to fall onto the surface. After overcoming these areas and successfully crossing, the microrobot accelerated in velocity as it moved away from the obstacle region, mirroring the resistive force encountered in the initial approach. These phenomena arise from electrokinetic effects caused by the presence of the obstacle structure under the applied electric field, as revealed by the numerical simulation shown in Fig. 5E. The obstacle, made of a dielectric material with sharp corners at its edges, locally deforms the field induced by the voltage difference between the parallel ITO electrodes. This deformation causes the field to bypass the sides of the structure, reducing its strength in the middle region while amplifying it near the edges, where significant electric field gradients are formed. Given the low-conductivity solution and high AC frequencies used (500 kHz–5 MHz), this effect generates positive dielectrophoretic (pDEP) forces on the JP, oriented toward the local maxima of the electric field intensity |E|. The DEP forces act along the intensity gradient (∇|E|) streamlines near the top, creating the observed repulsion and attraction effects. In some cases, the forces are strong enough to overcome the electrostatic attraction at the top and trap the JP at one of the corners. However, this was avoided by applying higher voltages (e.g., 15 Vpp) and moving the JP quickly past the edges.

The trajectory in Fig. 5F involves descent onto the SU-8 structure rather than crossing it, followed by navigation atop its surface via magnetic rolling to a target waypoint. In an additional manually controlled session (see Supplementary Movie 6), the microrobot was further guided back from the upper surface of the structure to the chamber floor by rolling off its edge. This capability demonstrates that 3D structures within the microchamber comprising multiple levels can be navigated by first transitioning to the ceiling and then descending onto the desired level or a higher one, from which the microrobot can proceed downward in a stepwise manner, similar to descending stairs.

Depending on the solution conductivity and electric-field frequency, different types of cargo exhibit either positive DEP (pDEP) or negative DEP (nDEP) response, leading to accumulation in regions of electric field maxima or minima on the JP surface, respectively. In previous studies7,8, we have shown that pDEP-responsive cargo is mainly trapped along the metallo-dielectric interface of the JP or at the equator of its PS hemisphere, whereas nDEP-responsive cargo mostly accumulates at the equator of the metal-coated hemisphere and, to a lesser extent, under the PS hemisphere (Fig. 6B, C). Additionally, we have previously shown that magnetic rotation induces strong hydrodynamic shearing forces at the bottom and top poles of the JP near the metallo-dielectric interface17. Therefore, to minimize shear-induced detachment, cargo loading is preferred along the equator of the metallic or PS hemisphere (via nDEP and pDEP, respectively), where such forces are reduced.

In our experiments, the synthetic PS microspheres exhibited nDEP response in the baseline solution conductivity of 4 µS cm−1 at frequencies \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\ge$$\end{document}≥400 kHz (see Supplementary Fig. 10), whereas live E. Coli bacteria have previously been reported66 to exhibit only pDEP response in the 103–107 Hz frequency range under similar conditions, with the transition to nDEP occurring only at higher conductivities approaching ~10 mS cm−1. Accordingly, the transport of bacteria was carried out using pDEP, consistent with these prior observations and with the need to operate within the relatively low-conductivity regime in which electrostatic ceiling trapping remains effective.

Before introducing a physical separation between the cargo compartments, we first investigated transport across a “virtual wall” (Supplementary Fig. 11 and Supplementary Movie 7), simulating a boundary that prevents crossing from the bottom surface. These experiments, performed at higher optical magnification (40x), provided clear visualization of cargo pickup, ceiling transport, and release, over short distances. The coupling of cargo loading onto the JP with a sustained electric field requires accounting for the electrostatic force that attracts the JP to either the bottom or top surface during the initiation of lift and descent sequences. To enable inter-surface transitions without losing cargo, the AC voltage was temporarily lowered, just long enough to release the JP from the surface while still maintaining cargo trapping. Although some cargo briefly detached, it was rapidly recovered upon restoring the voltage. Hence, the timing of this voltage modulation was critical, as it had to be sufficient for complete disengagement of the JP from the surface, yet short enough to retain the cargo. This process is highlighted in Supplementary Fig. 11, showing the separation and reattachment of PS microspheres during the lift sequence, in correlation with the applied fields.

Building on these principles, we next addressed physical barriers, demonstrating cargo transport across the same 50-µm-tall SU-8 wall used in the previous navigation experiments, now serving as a separator between compartments (Supplementary Movie 7). A high concentration of cargo was introduced outside the U-shaped structure, from where it was collected and carried by the JP microrobot into the inner region confined by the structure by crossing the wall from above. Figure 6D displays the transport process of PS microspheres across the barrier, capturing the sequential steps of cargo pickup, lifting, ceiling navigation over the wall, descent, and release in the goal region on the bottom. The same procedure was repeated with live E. Coli bacteria, as illustrated by the image sequence and corresponding schematics in Fig. 6E, F. In this case, the solution was a low-conductivity (~6 µS cm−1) buffer with physiological osmolarity (see Methods), allowing to maintain both bacterial and mammalian cell viability during operation while providing the necessary conditions for the microrobot’s electrokinetic function. Upon electric-field activation, the bacteria became vertically aligned due to electro-orientation, and were trapped on the JP’s PS hemisphere and in the gap between the metallic hemisphere and the substrate, consistent with an exclusive pDEP response. The viability of the transported bacteria was confirmed by their motility after release.

To outline the operational boundaries of the cargo transport mechanism, we have quantified the cargo loading capacity and its bidirectional coupling with microrobot mobility (Supplementary Fig. 12). Cargo trapping limits and the effect of loaded cargo on motion speed during surface motion were characterized in a previous work8 for electrically propelled JPs, revealing a balance between hydrodynamic shearing and DEP trapping forces that determines the maximum number of loaded cargos, as well as a correlation between increased amounts of cargo and reduced translation speeds due to Stokes drag and cargo-wall interactions. While a similar balance of forces affects cargo loading during magnetic rolling under high-frequency electric fields, the underlying rotational shearing, which was also previously described17, is inherently different. Therefore, additional analysis was required to establish the relevant constraints for this mode. In terms of cargo size, to allow unhindered rolling motion of the JP carrier, the effective diameter of a single cargo should not exceed that of the JP and preferably be smaller, so that surface contact is maintained during lateral motion. Accordingly, we used PS microspheres of 1.1, 3, and 5 µm in diameter as cargo for a 10-μm JP carrier. This size range also encompasses both the 1.1-μm particles and the bacteria used in the qualitative demonstrations (measured at 4.4 ± 1.1 × 1.8 ± 0.3 μm, mean ± SD).

Supplementary Fig. 12A shows the maximal number of stably loaded cargos as a function of cargo size and magnetic field rotation rate (up to 12 Hz), starting from a rotation rate of zero which corresponds to maximized idle-state loading obtained through slow-motion intervals and stationary phases. A clear nonlinear inverse correlation is observed between the maximal number of loaded cargos (i.e., cargo loading capacity) and the rotation rate, as expected due to increased hydrodynamic shearing at higher rotation rates. For example, the average maximal number of loaded 1.1-μm cargos was ~56 at idle and stabilized at ~9 cargos for rotation rates ≥4 Hz, compared to ~22 3-μm cargos at idle which decreased to ~3 at 4 Hz and 0–1 at 12 Hz. Since all cargo particles exhibited nDEP response (at ~4 μS cm−1, 5 MHz), stable cargos were predominantly localized around the metallic hemisphere and closer to its apex at higher rotation rates, consistent with the locally reduced shearing in this region (Supplementary Fig. 12C). Notably, while the 1.1- and 3-μm cargos were reliably released from the JP upon turning off the electric field, the 5-μm-cargo particles were generally not easily detached from the JP or from one another, even at higher rotation rates or with additional mechanical agitation, likely due to nonspecific adhesion forces (e.g., van der Waals interactions) under the present experimental conditions. Consequently, 5-μm particles were not included in this analysis. However, they were included in the subsequent analysis of linear translation velocity at constant rotation rates (2–3 Hz) as a function of the number of loaded cargos (Supplementary Fig. 11B), where rolling motion could still be assessed. This analysis shows that increased cargo numbers cause a gradual reduction in velocity beyond a certain threshold, which could be associated with a lowering of the magnetic step-out frequency, as synchronous rotation is imposed up to this point. The measured reduction was up to 20% relative to the velocity without cargo. For 5-μm cargos, an increase in velocity was observed at around six loaded particles, yet this effect may be attributed to the formation of a non-spherical JP-cargo complex with a larger effective diameter, which in this specific configuration resulted in enhanced rolling efficiency (Supplementary Fig. 12C). To further analyze the out-of-plane cargo loading and transport capacity, we have measured the number of cargos successfully lifted to the microchamber ceiling by the JP microrobot as a function of the electric-field toggle interval between off and back on, which is applied during the initiation of magnetophoretic lifting (Supplementary Fig. 12D, E). As previously noted, this modulation is required to release the JP from the bottom surface while maximizing payload retention.

Our analysis identifies a critical retention window, which varies between JPs, cargo sizes and the initial amount of cargo. Insufficient intervals prevent the JP from overcoming electrostatic surface attraction, whereas excessive intervals lead to levitation of the JP with minimal fractions of the initial payload, as cargo is temporarily released and the DEP trapping distances become too large upon restoration of the electric field. An optimal interval (typically measured at 4–5 s) maximizes cargo retention, yet requires fine-tuning due to the narrow time window spanning sub-seconds. Here, we demonstrated successful lifting of up to 14 1.1-μm particles and 11 3-μm particles under optimal intervals. These values do not represent a rigid limit, as the process can be further investigated with additional voltage or frequency modulations and with improved timing control. Notably, we have also managed to levitate four 5-μm-cargo particles with the JP under the same magnetic (17.5 mT, 2500 mT m−1) and electric (15 Vpp, 5 MHz) field outputs (Supplementary Movie 7). These particles are roughly equivalent in mass (~68.7 pg per particle, 274.88 pg in total) to 375 1.1-μm particles or 18 3-μm particles, indicating that the payload weight of the PS cargos itself was not a limiting threshold in these experiments. Heavier types of cargo may require stronger electric and magnetic fields for trapping and levitation. Furthermore, while the present analysis focuses on nDEP trapping, pDEP-based cargo loading is expected to exhibit similar trends, particularly when cargo is localized along the equator of the PS hemisphere. Trapping at the metallo-dielectric interface is expected to be more sensitive to increased rotation rates due to the locally higher levels of shearing. Further investigation of additional cargo types and solution conductivities is left for future work.

Discussion

We have integrated versatile hybrid magnetic and electric actuation modes for metallo/magneto-dielectric Janus particle (JP) microrobots, introducing capabilities that overcome the constraints of traditional surface-bound motion. By implementing and characterizing motion control strategies on a single surface, we established a robust foundation for precise and repeatable operation. Building upon this groundwork, we introduced a higher level of motion involving multiple surfaces by featuring interplanar transitions and 2.5-dimensional (2.5D) trajectories via magnetic levitation and electrostatic trapping, extending JP mobility into a three-dimensional (3D) workspace without requiring continuous surface contact or sustained high-magnitude gradient fields.

These advancements provide a basis for several practical applications in microrobotics. Multi-surface navigation enables vertical obstacle crossing and transitioning between separate compartments, allowing the microrobot to perform “pick-and-place” operations, which were demonstrated here for both synthetic and living biological cargo. In practical laboratory settings, this capability enables the controlled transport of specific (bio-)particles, cells or analytes between isolated local environments, which are separated by physical barriers and accessed exclusively through an upper passage (Fig. 1F). It may be useful, for example, to deliberately produce and examine interactions between precisely controlled amounts of various species. Beyond transport, controlled surface contact with precise closed-loop positioning can enable localized patterning (Fig. 1E) and targeted sensing or stimulus (e.g., chemical, electric, or mechanical) by the microrobot while leaving untargeted areas unaffected. This level of precision is particularly relevant for applications such as localized in-vitro drug delivery (e.g., via microrobot surface treatment36–38) or single-cell stimulation (e.g., electroporation10,11 or mechanical perturbation44,67), where minimizing off-target exposure and cross-contamination is highly beneficial. The same functionality may further be employed for targeted particle deposition, enabling hierarchical 3D micro-assembly. Such capabilities are unattainable, for example, by conventional surface-rollers. While such rollers can climb inclined or curved surfaces, such as the ceilings of cylindrical compartments, their position relies on continuous motion for pressing against the wall. If motion ceases, the roller would detach and fall due to gravity. By comparison, our approach allows the microrobot to dwell while upside-down, navigate to a precise position and detach from the surface in a controlled and programmable manner. This enables extended periods for observation and position correction. Additionally, using the ceiling as a transitional plane enables microrobots to navigate multi-surface 3D architectures by descending to lower levels and returning to the top as needed (Fig. 1D), overcoming barriers that typically limit surface-bound agents.

In terms of robotic operation, our system effectively provides three DOFs for task-level control, with two positional DOFs in the XY plane and one orientational DOF corresponding to the in-plane orientation of the JP’s metallo-dielectric interface line. Positional motion is inherently coupled to this orientation, since in electric propulsion the interface line dictates propulsion direction, while in magnetic rolling the rotation axis sets the interface orientation. When the microrobot is stationary, however, the interface can be oriented in-place using the magnetic field. A fourth orientational DOF can be accessed when the electric field is disabled, allowing control of the out-of-plane inclination angle of the interface line (see Supplementary Figs. 4 and 5). The additional vertical surface-transition axis (z) is discrete, and can be treated as a separate motion axis rather than a continuous DOF (Fig. 1C). For some applications, such as the “pick-and-place” transport, this reduced number of DOFs relative to full 3D position and orientation control can be considered beneficial, as it simplifies the control scheme.

Compared to other hybrid actuation modalities, such as acousto-magnetic or opto-electric systems, which may offer advantages like fully 3D motion68 or local confinement of agents for decoupled control69, the present work establishes multi-surface operation and its associated task capabilities as a distinct mode of microrobot actuation. This mode is naturally enabled by the combined use of magnetic and electric fields, since magnetic fields are well suited for both levitation and orientation control, while electric fields enable togglable surface attachment and reversible robot-target interactions, including the cargo loading and release demonstrated here. To our knowledge, surface transitioning was not achieved using acoustic or optical fields, as well as alternative hybrid approaches, likely due to the absence of an inherent, switchable and reversible surface-trapping mechanism analogous to the electrostatic trapping employed here.

While the demonstrations presented in this work were performed in a microchamber of 120-μm height using ITO-coated substrates, the proposed framework is not limited to this specific material or geometry. ITO was selected to enable simultaneous electric-field actuation and optical imaging, yet alternative conductive substrates may be utilized, provided that surface adhesion is minimized (e.g., via thin passivation layers) and at least one boundary (bottom or top) remains optically transparent for imaging. Electric-field actuation scales naturally with chamber size, as larger lateral extents require only proportionally larger electrode areas, while vertical scaling can be achieved by adjusting electrode spacing and applied voltage to preserve field strength. Magnetic actuation scalability is governed primarily by the available magnetic workspace. In this study, the MagnebotiX system provided a hemispherical workspace of ~10 mm in diameter, far exceeding the 120-μm chamber height and allowing the workspace to be further enlarged. Larger workspaces can also be realized with alternative actuation platforms such as expanded coil arrays, nested Helmholtz coils, or actuated permanent magnet configurations12. The requirement for low-conductivity media for full utilization of electric propulsion and functionality is a known trade-off in electrokinetic actuation. However, solutions such as low-conductivity physiological buffers extend the applicability of these mechanisms in in vitro environments. The use of hybrid actuation provides further compensation, as magnetic rolling remains viable in high-conductivity solutions, with electric field-driven alignment, ceiling-attachment and cargo loading still functioning up to a certain threshold. Additionally, strong dielectrophoretic (DEP) forces near sharp structures such as the SU-8 walls used here can affect microrobot behavior and complicate control. This challenge was addressed by modeling the environment, characterizing the effects and tuning control parameters accordingly. Further optimization may involve the design of shorter, smoother or thinner walls, as long as those still provide effective separation of compartments.

Future work could enhance performance through more adaptive control methods (e.g., PID or model-predictive control), automation of task-related components such as cargo collection and release, and reduced levitation and descent intervals by the incorporation of dedicated electromagnets or actuated permanent magnets positioned at the top and bottom of the chamber. This could also be achieved with stronger magnetization of the microrobots, either by increasing their magnetic volume fraction or by pre-magnetizing the material under powerful fields.

Overall, this work highlights the transformative potential of hybrid control systems in overcoming conventional mobility limits and expanding functionality, while preserving simple microrobot design and predominantly low-power uniform-field actuation. Furthermore, while this study employs Janus particles, the underlying principles of controlled inter-surface transitions can be extended to additional microrobot designs. For instance, robots featuring internal cavities70,71, porous structures72 or alternative cargo manipulation modalities73,74 could utilize the multi-surface navigation approach to transport payloads through various physical mechanisms, beyond the dielectrophoretic (DEP) trapping used here. The DEP mechanism itself can also be further optimized using tailored structures such as micro-bowls75. The proposed methodology, therefore, provides a conceptual basis for the development of additional microrobotic systems capable of operating within multi-surface environments.

Methods

Preparation of Janus particles and microchamber systems

Metallo/magneto-dielectric JPs with diameters of 10 and 27 μm were fabricated by coating fluorescent polystyrene particles (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) with 15 nm of chromium (Cr), followed by the deposition of 55 nm nickel (Ni) and 15 nm gold (Au) layers using an electron-beam evaporator (PVD20-EB, Vinch technologies). The coated JPs were released via sonication in deionized (DI) water and rinsed three times with DI water before being suspended in the selected medium. The baseline medium was a low-conductivity potassium chloride (KCl) solution (~4 μS cm−1) with 0.025–0.05 %v/v Tween-20 (Sigma-Aldrich) as a non-ionic surfactant to minimize adhesion to the substrate. The microchamber system consisted of a circular microfluidic chamber formed by a spacer positioned between two indium tin oxide (ITO)-coated glass slides (Delta Technologies)9. ITO was selected to enable simultaneous electric field application and optical imaging. However, the system is not inherently limited to ITO substrates and is compatible with alternative conductive materials. To further reduce particle adhesion to the substrate, both ITO-coated glass slides were coated with a 20-nm-thick silicon dioxide (SiO2) layer using a sputter coater (PVD20-S, Vinch technologies). The thin spacer, which defined a 10-mm-diameter microchamber, was constructed from a 120-μm-thick double-sided tape (3 M), which was cut using an electronic cutting machine (Silhouette Cameo 3, Silhouette America Inc.). Two inlet holes (~1 mm in diameter) were drilled at the top of the chamber to introduce JPs, cargo particles and bacteria via manual pipetting. Above the holes, an additional solution was filled within a surrounding silicone (Grace-Bio) or polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) reservoir (~2 mm in height, 10 mm in diameter) to prevent evaporation of the solution and minimize flow within the chamber during the experiment. In some microchambers, U-shaped wall structures with a thickness of ~50 μm were fabricated on the bottom ITO-coated glass slide using standard photolithography techniques with SU-8 negative photoresist (SU-8-2025, MicroChem)76. These structures served as physical barriers separating adjacent regions.

Experimental setup

The microfluidic chip was placed on a Nikon TI inverted epi-fluorescent microscope (Nikon, Japan) equipped with an Andor Neo camera (Andor, Belfast, UK). Electric signals were generated using a single-channel function generator (Agilent 33250A, Keysight Technologies, Inc., Santa Rosa, CA, USA), controlled via a GPIB-USB interface. The signals were sine waves at frequencies ranging from 1 kHz to 20 MHz and peak-to-peak voltages (Vpp) of up to 20 V. Magnetic actuation was carried out using the MagnebotiX MFG-100-i field generator (MagnebotiX AG, Zurich, Switzerland)77, which consists of an array of eight electromagnets positioned above the sample workspace (see Supplementary Fig. 1), and is capable of producing both magnetic field gradients and gradient-free uniform fields. Gradient fields (15–17.5 mT, 2000–2500 mT m−1) were used exclusively for JP levitation, while uniform idle fields were applied for steering, and uniform rotating fields were applied for magnetic rolling, with a typical magnitude of 3.5 mT and rotation rates of up to 50 Hz. Power consumption by the system (Supplementary Fig. 9 and Supplementary Note 4) was estimated from computed coil currents required to apply various magnetic field and gradient combinations.

Realization of open- and closed-loop control

The control system was based on a custom Python program that managed the camera and actuation hardware. The AC function generator was operated by setting the voltage and frequency parameters and toggling its output. Similarly, the MagnebotiX field generator was controlled by specifying magnetic field components, which the system translated into corresponding coil currents. The acquisition module recorded experimental data, including the applied magnetic and electric fields at each time step. For manual (open loop) control, keyboard inputs were used to adjust the magnetic field for steering, rolling and lifting and to toggle the electric field for propulsion and surface-trapping. For closed-loop control (see Fig. 7), the system tracked the position and velocity of the microrobots using the CSRT tracker from the OpenCV library, with additional image processing for precise center-point extraction. A dynamically updated region of interest (ROI) was defined around each tracked microrobot, enabling efficient tracking of multiple particles while actively controlling only a selected one. Navigation was guided by a sequence of waypoints forming a desired path, either manually marked on the live image feed or sampled from predefined geometric shapes (e.g., rectangle, triangle, and lemniscate). For motion on a single surface, the feedback signal was defined as the distance vector d (in pixels) from the tracked microrobot’s position to the target waypoint, and the microrobot’s velocity vector v. The positional error was defined as the magnitude of the distance vector, |d|, and the alignment error was defined as the angle between d and v. A waypoint was considered reached when the positional error |d| fell below a constant threshold radius R. For out-of-plane (z-axis) transitions, the location of the waypoint on the top or bottom surface prompted the controller to initiate lift or descent sequences when needed, and to use an inverted rotation axis when rolling upside-down on the chamber ceiling. For these transitions, a predetermined timing protocol was employed rather than vertical (z-axis) position feedback. Each hybrid control strategy was implemented through a tailored algorithm (Fig. 7B–D) applied at every frame update, synchronized with the camera frame rate (typically 20 fps). The algorithms included dynamically tunable parameters, such as the rotation frequencies for magnetic rolling. Given the low Reynolds number regime, where particle inertia is negligible, the system’s response to control signals was effectively immediate.Fig. 7Closed-loop control scheme.A Flow chart of the higher-level decision process applied at each frame update. The magnetic rolling algorithm (Fig. 7B) was defined in a straightforward manner, relying on the MagnebotiX system’s capability of generating arbitrary magnetic fields rotating around any given rotation axis. For a target waypoint positioned at a distance d from the microrobot, the required rotation axis to achieve translation in the direction of d is given by Rax = ẑ × d, where ẑ is perpendicular to the image plane. To add some level of velocity control, the rotation frequency ω was made proportional to |d|, slowing the JP as it approached the target to avoid overshooting. Two selected values ω1 and ω2 defined a linear range [ω1, ω2] from which ω was sampled as a function of |d|, such that ω = ω1 + (|d|/|d|max)(ω2 – ω1), where |d|max is the maximal possible distance in the trajectory. Selecting ω1 = ω2 yields a constant rotation frequency. For magnetically steered electric propulsion (Fig. 7C), a semi-linear proportional (P-) controller was used. If the velocity vector v deviated from the target direction d by an error angle αerr exceeding a predetermined threshold (1–4°), the orientation was corrected by rotating the planar component of the magnetic field Bxy at an angular velocity Ω [° s−1] proportional to αerr. The controller was tuned using a single parameter KP, such that: Ω = KPαerr. A typical value was KP = 2, corresponding to a rotation rate of 2° s−1 per degree of error. For orthogonal cross-axis motion (Fig. 7D), the algorithm measured the x-axis (Δx) and y-axis (Δy) distances to the target and prioritized motion to reduce the greater error. To prevent diagonal drifts, the algorithm also reoriented the trajectory toward the line connecting the current and previous waypoints if the distance from that line exceeded a predefined threshold. Whether ICEP or sDEP is used to achieve positive or negative y-axis motion depends on the JP’s initial metallic-side orientation, which is set at the start of the control signal. To address this without requiring additional image processing steps to distinguish between the JP’s hemispheres, which are not always visually distinct, we implemented a dynamic calibration method. By assuming an initial state and monitoring the JP’s y-axis velocity, the controller automatically switched between ICEP and sDEP if the observed propulsion direction opposed the assumption, updating the correct orientation in memory. This calibration was typically needed only once prior to a navigation session, in which case it did not affect trajectory accuracy. However, it could occasionally reoccur during trajectory following if the JP became temporarily stuck or abruptly flipped orientation, resulting in a minor displacement error. The same applied to sessions where calibration was not performed in advance. For example, across three independent sessions using 27-µm JPs, the average deviation associated with calibration was ~52 µm, about twice the particle diameter. This corresponds to a small fraction of the typical trajectory scale, which was at least an order of magnitude larger.

Quantification of control performance

Trajectory accuracy, speed, and repeatability of the JP microrobots’ motion were quantified using nearest-neighbor-based error metrics. Accuracy was measured by the Chamfer and Hausdorff distances between the actual tracked trajectory and desired reference path78–80. Two complementary sets of nearest-neighbor Euclidean distances were computed—(i) from each tracked position to the closest point on the reference path, and (ii) from each point along the reference path to the nearest tracked position. This approach ensures that every point of both sets is considered, and accounts for differences in sampling density. The Chamfer distance, representing overall similarity between point sets, was defined as the average of these distances, while the Hausdorff distance, which is sensitive to outliers, was defined as the maximum distance, representing the largest local deviation (see also Supplementary Note 3). Average velocity was computed as the total trajectory length divided by the completion time, to evaluate speed-accuracy trade-offs. For repeated trajectories, comparing actively controlled particles to passively influenced or open-loop controlled particles, the first traversal of the path served as the reference, allowing quantification of repeatability for subsequent repetitions using the same metrics. Statistical summaries (mean ± standard deviation) were reported for each case. For comparison across control conditions (e.g., closed-loop vs. open-loop), the overall mean Chamfer and Hausdorff distances were calculated by averaging the subgroup means within each condition (see Supplementary Tables 4 and 5 for individual subgroup statistics).

Measurement of alignment angles

27-μm Janus particles with a fluorescent polystyrene hemisphere were introduced into the microchamber and visualized using an epi-fluorescent microscope with a 20x objective lens. Images were captured with an exposure time of 50 ms. The tilt angle α of the JPs’ dielectric-metal interface relative to the bottom substrate was determined by analyzing fluorescence intensity using the following equation81: α = 2sin−1(√I*). Here, I* is the non-dimensional areal integrated intensity around the JP, calculated by normalizing the measured average pixel intensity I̅ with the extreme cases of the particle’s metallic (Imin) and dielectric (Imax) hemispheres facing downward: I* = _(I̅ - Imin)/(Imax - Imin). Reference measurements of Imin and Imax were obtained by applying specific magnetic and electric fields to stabilize the particles at known angles in equilibrium. Due to inconsistent intensity measurements in high angles approaching 180°, Imax was estimated based on the intensity measured at 90°, using the approximation Imax ≅ 2I̅90° - Imin. This method provided reliable angle estimations up to 90°, which was the relevant range for this study. The orientation angle β of the JP’s interface relative to the x-axis in the image plane was measured by applying a binary threshold to isolate the bright hemisphere, detecting the bright contour and extracting its primary axis direction using image moments with the OpenCV library.

Threshold voltage scan

Janus particles (10- and 27-μm diameter) were magnetically levitated to the chamber ceiling. Once at the top, particles were held against gravity by electrostatic trapping, applying an initial high voltage (≥10 Vpp) at 5 MHz to ensure firm attachment while suppressing lateral motion. To determine the threshold voltage (VT), the AC frequency was fixed at a selected value, and the voltage was decreased gradually by steps of 1 Vpp until particle detachment occurred, indicating that the threshold was at ~1 Vpp above the detachment point. At frequencies where ICEP or sDEP propulsion occurs, particles were propelled laterally on the ceiling with progressively decreasing speeds as the voltage was lowered, until detachment. For bare polystyrene (PS) particles, lifting was achieved by manually flipping the chamber, allowing gravity to bring the particles to the ceiling. The voltage was applied to secure attachment before the chamber was flipped back. In both cases, equilibrium stability was tested by lightly tapping the table, reducing the likelihood of particles adhering to the surface due to local interactions, irrespective of the applied voltage. A similar protocol was used to assess the impact of lateral magnetic rolling on the threshold, with the magnetic rotation rate fixed each time while maintaining rolling motion throughout the voltage-lowering sequence. The AC frequency was set to either 750 kHz or 5 MHz in this case.

Numerical simulations

Simulations were performed in COMSOLTM 5.3 to qualitatively analyze the deformation of the electric field between the parallel ITO electrodes caused by the obstacle structure and the JP. Using a 2D model, the geometries comprised a rectangular domain representing the space between the electrodes (120-μm height and 1200 μm (Fig. 5E) or 300 μm (Fig. 6C) width), with a rectangle (50-μm height and 230-μm width) subtracted at the lower edge to represent the obstacle (Fig. 5E), or a circle of 10 µm diameter subtracted at 300 nm (Fig. 6C) or 60 μm (inset of Fig. 6C) above the substrate (with opposing electrically insulating and conducting boundary conditions applied to its left and right halves) to represent the JP. The problem was analyzed under quasi-steady DC conditions, focusing solely on the qualitative shape of the electric field magnitude, as well as its streamlines (Fig. 6C) or its gradient streamlines (Fig. 5E) under high-frequency AC voltage. This approximation is valid at high frequencies beyond the RC time of the induced electric double layers. Accordingly, a stationary model (electric currents) was used, solving the Laplace equation for the electrostatic potential. The boundary conditions included electric potentials of 0 V (ground) at the lower electrode and 7.5 V (Fig. 5E) or 5 V (Fig. 6C) at the upper electrode. For the dielectric obstacle, electrically insulating boundary conditions were applied at its edges. For the JP, this condition was applied at the dielectric PS hemisphere, while a floating potential condition was applied to the conductive metal hemisphere to simulate an ideal conductor.

Preparation of synthetic and biological cargo

Synthetic 1.1-µm-cargo particles (Polystyrene microspheres, Fluoro-Max, Thermo Scientific) were suspended in the baseline ~4 μS cm−1 KCl solution with 0.05% Tween-20 (Sigma-Aldrich) and introduced into the microfluidic chamber at high concentration near the injection point, forming distinct regions of high and low concentrations. E. Coli bacteria (MP 289, obtained from Prof. Yechezkel Kashi, Technion) were cultured at 37 °C on LB agar plates or in LB broth (50 mL culture tubes) to serve as a biological cargo. Bacteria were collected from a colony or from the liquid culture using a pipette, washed three times with the selected medium, and then introduced into the microchamber in the same manner. The medium was either a low-conductivity physiological buffer (Fig. 6E, F, composition described below) or a 1:20 diluted PBS (Sigma-Aldrich) with a measured conductivity of ≈0.94 mS cm−1 (Supplementary Fig. 11D, E).

Low-conductivity physiological buffer

The buffer used in bacterial experiments was prepared to maintain cell viability while providing low conductivity suitable for electrokinetic manipulation82. Its composition was HEPES (2385 mg L−1), sucrose (80,700 mg L−1), and dextrose (4500 mg L−1), yielding a conductivity of ~6 μS cm−1 while preserving physiological osmolarity to support both bacterial and mammalian cells. The buffer was previously verified to maintain high viability of mammalian cells (HeLa MDA-MB-231) for 1 h, comparable to PBS buffer (87 vs. 93%, respectively)24.

Quantitative analysis of cargo loading capacities

Polystyrene microspheres (Fluoro-Max, Thermo Scientific) with diameters of 1, 3, and 5 μm were suspended in the baseline ~4 μS cm−1 KCl solution and introduced into the microchamber. Stable cargo loading as a function of the magnetic field rotation rate was quantified by first loading the JP microrobot under slow-motion intervals to maximize cargo attachment, and then applying magnetic rolling at a constant rate in random directions until unstable cargos detached. The remaining cargos were released by switching off the electric field and counted. The effect of payload on motion speed was evaluated by measuring the linear translation velocity under a constant magnetic field rotation rate with varying numbers of loaded cargos. Lifting capacity and the influence of the electric-field toggle interval on cargo retention were assessed by applying magnetophoretic levitation with systematically varied electric-field switching durations, until the JP microrobot and retained cargos were successfully lifted to the microchamber ceiling and counted. Data series were obtained from consecutive measurements on the same JPs under comparable initial conditions.
