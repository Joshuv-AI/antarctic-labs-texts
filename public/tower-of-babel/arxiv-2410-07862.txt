DYNAMICAL AND INVARIANCE ALGEBRAS OF THE d d -DIMENSIONAL DUNKL-COULOMB PROBLEM
C. QUESNE

Abstract
It is shown that the rich algebraic structure of the standard d d -dimensional Coulomb problem can be extended to its Dunkl counterpart. Replacing standard derivatives by Dunkl ones in the so( d + 1 d+1 ,2) dynamical algebra generators of the former gives rise to a deformed algebra with similar commutation relations, except that the metric tensor becomes dependent on the reflection operators and that there are some additional commutation or anticommutation relations involving the latter. It is then shown that from some of the dynamical algebra generators it is straightforward to derive the integrals of motion of the Dunkl-Coulomb problem in Sturm representation. Finally, from the latter, the components of a deformed Laplace-Runge-Lenz vector are built. Together with the Dunkl angular momentum components, such operators insure the superintegrability of the Dunkl-Coulomb problem in Schrödinger representation.



Keywords: quantum mechanics, Dunkl derivative, deformed Lie algebras, superintegrability

Introduction

In 1950, Wigner introduced a deformed version of the Heisenberg algebra in quantum mechanics

More recently, some specific exactly-solvable quantum systems wherein the ordinary derivatives are replaced by Dunkl ones have been worked out. Much interest has been devoted, for instance, to the Dunkl oscillators in one, two, and three dimensions

The purpose of the present paper is to extend the rich algebraic structure of the standard Coulomb problem (for a review see, e.g.
[

The paper is organized as follows. In Section 2, we introduce the

2
d
-dimensional Dunkl-Coulomb problem

The
d
-dimensional Dunkl-Coulomb model is defined by the Hamiltonian

H=-\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{D}^{2}-\frac{\alpha}{r},

(2.1)

where
r=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d}x_{i}^{2}\right)^{1/2}
,
\alpha>0
, and

\mathbf{D}^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}D_{i}^{2}

(2.2)

is the
d
-dimensional Dunkl-Laplacian operator, defined in terms of the Dunkl derivatives

D_{i}=\partial_{i}+\frac{\mu_{i}}{x_{i}}(1-R_{i}),\qquad i=1,2,\ldots,d,\qquad\mu_{i}>0.

(2.3)

Here,
R_{i}
is the reflection operator defined by

R_{i}f(x_{i})=f(-x_{i})

(2.4)

and
\partial_{i}f=\partial f/\partial x_{i}
. For future purposes, we note that

\displaystyle[D_{i},x_{j}]=\delta_{i,j}(1+2\mu_{i}R_{i}),

\displaystyle\{R_{i},x_{i}\}=\{R_{i},D_{i}\}=0,

(2.5)

\displaystyle[R_{i},x_{j}]=[R_{i},D_{j}]=[R_{i},R_{j}]=0\qquad\text{if $i\neq j$}.

It is also worth observing that the change of standard derivatives into Dunkl ones is accompanied by a change of scalar product, which is now defined by

<g|f>=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\cdots\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}g^{*}(\mathbf{x})f(\mathbf{x})|x_{1}|^{2\mu_{1}}|x_{2}|^{2\mu_{2}}\cdots|x_{d}|^{2\mu_{d}}dx_{1}dx_{2}\cdots dx_{d}.

(2.6)

The Schrödinger equation corresponding to (
2.1
) reads

H\Psi(\mathbf{x})=\left(-\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{D}^{2}-\frac{\alpha}{r}\right)\Psi(\mathbf{x})=E\Psi(\mathbf{x}).

(2.7)

Instead of the latter, one may consider the eigenvalue problem

K\Psi(\mathbf{x})=\alpha\Psi(\mathbf{x}).

(2.8)

for the operator
K
defined by

K=-\frac{r}{2}\mathbf{D}^{2}-Er.

(2.9)

This corresponds to the so-called Sturm representation for the Coulomb problem, wherein the energy
E
is considered fixed, while
\alpha
becomes the eigenvalue.

3
Dynamical algebra of the Dunkl-Coulomb problem

To start with, let us introduce three operators generalizing some operators known for the three-dimensional standard Coulomb problem
[
22
,
23
]

\Gamma_{0}=\frac{1}{2}r(-\mathbf{D}^{2}+1),\quad\Gamma_{d+1}=\frac{1}{2}r(-\mathbf{D}^{2}-1),\quad T=-{\rm i}\biggl(\mathbf{r}\cdot\mathbf{D}+\frac{d-1}{2}+\sum_{i}\mu_{i}R_{i}\biggr).

(3.1)

It is straightforward to show that they satisfy the commutation relations

[\Gamma_{0},\Gamma_{d+1}]={\rm i}T,\qquad[\Gamma_{0},T]=-{\rm i}\Gamma_{d+1},\qquad[\Gamma_{d+1},T]=-{\rm i}\Gamma_{0},

(3.2)

showing that they generate an so(2,1) algebra.

Let us now introduce the components of the Dunkl angular momentum in
d
dimensions,

J_{ij}=-J_{ji}=-{\rm i}(x_{i}D_{j}-x_{j}D_{i}),\qquad i,j=1,2,\ldots,d.

(3.3)

Its square can be shown to be given by

\mathbf{J}^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{ij}J_{ij}^{2}=-\mathbf{x}^{2}\mathbf{D}^{2}+(\mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{D})^{2}+\mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{D}\biggl(d-2+2\sum_{i}\mu_{i}R_{i}\biggr).

(3.4)

On comparing the latter with the Casimir operator of the so(2,1) algebra generated by
\Gamma_{0}
,
\Gamma_{d+1}
, and
T
, namely

Q^{2}=\Gamma_{0}^{2}-\Gamma_{d+1}^{2}-T^{2},

(3.5)

we get the relation

Q^{2}=\mathbf{J}^{2}+\biggl(\frac{d-3}{2}+\sum_{i}\mu_{i}R_{i}\biggr)\biggl(\frac{d-1}{2}+\sum_{j}\mu_{j}R_{j}\biggr).

(3.6)

Let us also consider three sets of
d
operators, defined by

\begin{split}A_{i}&=-\frac{1}{2}x_{i}\mathbf{D}^{2}+D_{i}\biggl(\mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{D}+\frac{d-3}{2}+\sum_{j}\mu_{j}R_{j}\biggr)-\frac{1}{2}x_{i},\\
M_{i}&=-\frac{1}{2}x_{i}\mathbf{D}^{2}+D_{i}\biggl(\mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{D}+\frac{d-3}{2}+\sum_{j}\mu_{j}R_{j}\biggr)+\frac{1}{2}x_{i},\\
\Gamma_{i}&=-{\rm i}rD_{i},\end{split}.

(3.7)

with
i=1,2,\ldots,d
.

The commutation relations of the so(2,1) generators (
3.1
) with the four sets of operators defined in (
3.3
) and (
3.7
) are easily obtained in the form

\begin{split}&[\Gamma_{0},J_{ij}]=[\Gamma_{d+1},J_{ij}]=[T,J_{ij}]=0,\\
&[\Gamma_{0},A_{i}]=[\Gamma_{d+1},M_{i}]=[T,\Gamma_{i}]=0,\\
&[\Gamma_{d+1},A_{i}]=-[\Gamma_{0},M_{i}]={\rm i}\Gamma_{i},\\
&[T,A_{i}]=[\Gamma_{0},\Gamma_{i}]={\rm i}M_{i},\\
&[T,M_{i}]=[\Gamma_{d+1},\Gamma_{i}]={\rm i}A_{i},\end{split}.

(3.8)

and we note that they coincide with those obtained for the corresponding operators in the standard Coulomb problem, i.e., for
\mu_{i}=0

[
22
,
23
]
.

On finally considering the commutation relations of operators (
3.3
) and (
3.7
) among themselves, we obtain some relations that remain the same as for the standard Coulomb problem, namely

[A_{i},A_{j}]=-[M_{i},M_{j}]=-[\Gamma_{i},\Gamma_{j}]={\rm i}J_{ij},

(3.9)

as well as some relations that are changed by the appearance of some reflection operator on the right-hand side, namely

\begin{split}&[J_{ij},J_{kl}]={\rm i}[\delta_{i,k}J_{jl}(1+2\mu_{k}R_{k})+\delta_{i,l}J_{kj}(1+2\mu_{l}R_{l})\\
&\phantom{[J_{ij},J_{kl}]}\quad+\delta_{j,k}J_{li}(1+2\mu_{k}R_{k})+\delta_{j,l}J_{ik}(1+2\mu_{l}R_{l})],\\
&[J_{ij},A_{k}]={\rm i}(\delta_{i,k}A_{j}-\delta_{j,k}A_{i})(1+2\mu_{k}R_{k}),\\
&[J_{ij},M_{k}]={\rm i}(\delta_{i,k}M_{j}-\delta_{j,k}M_{i})(1+2\mu_{k}R_{k}),\\
&[J_{ij},\Gamma_{k}]={\rm i}(\delta_{i,k}\Gamma_{j}-\delta_{j,k}\Gamma_{i})(1+2\mu_{k}R_{k}),\\
&[A_{i},M_{j}]={\rm i}\delta_{i,j}T(1+2\mu_{i}R_{i}),\\
&[A_{i},\Gamma_{j}]={\rm i}\delta_{i,j}\Gamma_{d+1}(1+2\mu_{i}R_{i}),\\
&[M_{i},\Gamma_{j}]={\rm i}\delta_{i,j}\Gamma_{0}(1+2\mu_{i}R_{i}).\end{split}.

(3.10)

In addition, we have some commutation or anticommutation relations involving reflections,

\begin{split}&[R_{i},\Gamma_{0}]=[R_{i},\Gamma_{d+1}]=[R_{i},T]=0,\\
&[R_{i},J_{jk}]=0\qquad\text{if $i\neq j,k$},\\
&\{R_{i},J_{jk}\}=0\qquad\text{if $i=j$ or $i=k$},\\
&[R_{i},A_{j}]=[R_{i},M_{j}]=[R_{i},\Gamma_{j}]=0\qquad\text{if $i\neq j$},\\
&\{R_{i},A_{j}\}=\{R_{i},M_{j}\}=\{R_{i},\Gamma_{j}\}=0\qquad\text{if $i=j$}.\end{split}.

(3.11)

In the standard Coulomb problem, the
\frac{1}{2}(d+2)(d+3)
operators
\Gamma_{0}
,
\Gamma_{d+1}
,
T
,
J_{ij}=-J_{ij}
(
1\leq i<j\leq d
), and
A_{i}
,
M_{i}
,
\Gamma_{i}
, (
i=1,2,\ldots,d
) can be identified with the generators
L_{ab}=-L_{ba}=L_{ab}^{\dagger}
,
a,b=1,2,\ldots,d+3
, of an so(
d+1
,2) algebra, whose commutation relations are given by

[L_{ab},L_{cd}|={\rm i}(g_{ac}L_{bd}+g_{ad}L_{cb}+g_{bc}L_{da}+g_{bd}L_{ac}),

(3.12)

where
g_{ab}={\rm diag}(1,1,\ldots,1,-1,-1)
. The identification is the following one:

\begin{split}&L_{ij}=J_{ij},\qquad i,j=1,2,\ldots,d,\\
&L_{i,d+1}=A_{i},\qquad L_{i,d+2}=M_{i},\qquad L_{i,d+3}=\Gamma_{i},\qquad i=1,2,\ldots,d,\\
&L_{d+1,d+2}=T,\qquad L_{d+1,d+3}=\Gamma_{d+1},\qquad L_{d+2,d+3}=\Gamma_{0}.\end{split}

(3.13)

With the same identification, it turns out from (
3.2
), (
3.8
), (
3.9
), and (
3.10
) that the operators (
3.1
), (
3.3
), and (
3.7
), obtained by changing standard derivatives into Dunkl ones, still satisfy an equation of type (

) provided we interpret

as the operator-dependent diagonal matrix
g_{ab}={\rm diag}(1+2\mu_{1}R_{1},1+2\mu_{2}R_{2},\ldots,1+2\mu_{d}R_{d},1,-1,-1)
. In addition, the adjoint

is now calculated with respect to the new scalar product (
2.6
) and relations (
3.11
) have to be taken into account, leading to

\begin{split}&[R_{i},L_{ab}]=0\qquad\text{if $a,b\neq i$},\\
&\{R_{i},L_{ab}\}=0\qquad\text{if $a=i$ or $b=i$}.\end{split}

(3.14)

4
Invariance algebra of the Dunkl-Coulomb problem in Sturm representation

From its definition (
2.9
), it is obvious that the operator
K
, which is diagonalized in Sturm representation, is a linear combination of the generators
\Gamma_{0}
and
\Gamma_{d+1}
of the so(2,1) subalgebra considered in section 3,

K=\frac{1}{2}(1-2E)\Gamma_{0}+\frac{1}{2}(1+2E)\Gamma_{d+1}.

(4.1)

From commutation relations (
3.8
), it therefore follows that

[J_{ij},K]=[B_{i},K]=0,

(4.2)

where
B_{i}
is defined by

B_{i}=\frac{1}{2}[(1-2E)A_{i}+(1+2E)M_{i}],

(4.3)

which from (
3.7
) amounts to

\displaystyle B_{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\biggl[-x_{i}\mathbf{D}^{2}+2D_{i}\biggl(\mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{D}+\frac{d-3}{2}+\sum_{j}\mu_{j}D_{j}\biggr)+2Ex_{i}\biggr]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\biggl[-x_{i}\mathbf{D}^{2}+2\biggl(\mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{D}+\frac{d-1}{2}+\sum_{j}\mu_{j}D_{j}\biggr)D_{i}+2Ex_{i}\biggr].

(4.4)

Hence, the
\frac{1}{2}d(d+1)
operators
J_{ij}=-J_{ji}
and
B_{i}
are integrals of motion of the Dunkl-Coulomb problem in Sturm representation.

Their commutation relations directly follow from equations (
3.9
) and (
3.10
) and they are given by

\begin{split}&[J_{ij},J_{kl}]={\rm i}[\delta_{i,k}J_{jl}(1+2\mu_{k}R_{k})+\delta_{i,l}J_{kj}(1+2\mu_{l}R_{l})\\
&\phantom{[J_{ij},J_{kl}]}\quad+\delta_{j,k}J_{li}(1+2\mu_{k}R_{k})+\delta_{j,l}J_{ik}(1+2\mu_{l}R_{l})],\\
&[J_{ij},B_{k}]={\rm i}(\delta_{i,k}B_{j}-\delta_{j,k}B_{i})(1+2\mu_{k}R_{k}),\\
&[B_{i},B_{j}]=-2{\rm i}EJ_{ij}.\end{split}

(4.5)

We also get a direct consequence of (
3.11
), namely

\begin{split}&[R_{i},J_{jk}]=0\qquad\text{if $i\neq j,k$},\\
&\{R_{i},J_{jk}\}=0\qquad\text{if $i=j$ or $i=k$},\\
&[R_{i},B_{j}]=0\qquad\text{if $i\neq j$},\\
&\{R_{i},B_{j}\}=0\qquad\text{if $i=j$}.\end{split}

(4.6)

In addition, the operators
J_{ij}
and
B_{i}
satisfy the relations

J_{ij}B_{k}+J_{jk}B_{i}+J_{ki}B_{j}=0,\qquad 1\leq i<j<k\leq d,

(4.7)

and

\mathbf{B}^{2}=K^{2}+2E\biggl[\mathbf{J}^{2}+\biggl(\frac{d-1}{2}+\sum_{i}\mu_{i}R_{i}\biggr)^{2}\biggr].

(4.8)

The proof of equation (
4.7
) is a straightforward consequence of definitions (
3.3
) and (
4.4
). To demonstrate equation (
4.8
), let us first calculate the square of a component of
\mathbf{B}
, for instance
B_{1}
. From definition (
4.4
), as well as from commutation or anticommutation relations (
2.5
), one gets

\displaystyle B_{1}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}x_{1}^{2}\mathbf{D}^{4}-\biggl[x_{1}\biggl(\mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{D}+\frac{d+1}{2}\biggr)D_{1}+\frac{1}{2}\biggl(\mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{D}+\frac{d-1}{2}\biggr)+Ex_{1}^{2}\biggr]\mathbf{D}^{2}

\displaystyle\quad{}-x_{1}D_{1}\mathbf{D}^{2}\biggl(-\mu_{1}R_{1}+\sum_{j\neq 1}\mu_{j}R_{j}\biggr)-\biggl(\mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{D}+\frac{d-1}{2}\biggr)\mathbf{D}^{2}\mu_{1}R_{1}

\displaystyle\quad{}-\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{D}^{2}\biggl(\sum_{j}\mu_{j}R_{j}\biggr)(1+2\mu_{1}R_{1})+\biggl(\mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{D}+\frac{d-1}{2}\biggr)\biggl(\mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{D}+\frac{d+1}{2}\biggr)D_{1}^{2}

\displaystyle\quad{}+(2\mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{D}+d)D_{1}^{2}\sum_{j}\mu_{j}R_{j}+D_{1}^{2}\biggl(\sum_{j}\mu_{j}R_{j}\biggr)^{2}

\displaystyle\quad{}+2Ex_{1}\biggl(\mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{D}+\frac{d-1}{2}\biggr)D_{1}+2Ex_{1}D_{1}\biggl(-\mu_{1}R_{1}+\sum_{j\neq 1}\mu_{j}R_{j}\biggr)

\displaystyle\quad{}+E\biggl(\mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{D}+\frac{d-1}{2}+\sum_{j}\mu_{j}R_{j}\biggr)(1+2\mu_{1}R_{1})+E^{2}x_{1}^{2}.

(4.9)

A similar calculation leads to the square of the other components of
\mathbf{B}
. On summing all the results, one obtains

\displaystyle\mathbf{B}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\biggl\{\mathbf{x}^{2}\mathbf{D}^{4}+(2\mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{D}+d-1-4E\mathbf{x}^{2})\mathbf{D}^{2}+2\mathbf{D}^{2}\sum_{i}\mu_{i}R_{i}

\displaystyle\quad{}+4E\biggl[2(\mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{D})^{2}+(2d-3)\mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{D}+\frac{d(d-1)}{2}\biggr]+4E(4\mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{D}+2d-1)\sum_{i}\mu_{i}R_{i}

\displaystyle\quad{}+8E\biggl(\sum_{i}\mu_{i}R_{i}\biggr)^{2}+4E^{2}\mathbf{x}^{2}\biggr\}.

On the other hand, the square of operator (

\displaystyle K^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\biggl\{\mathbf{x}^{2}\mathbf{D}^{4}+2\biggl(\mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{D}+\frac{d-1}{2}+\sum_{i}\mu_{i}R_{i}+2E\mathbf{x}^{2}\biggr)\mathbf{D}^{2}

\displaystyle\quad{}+4E\biggl(\mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{D}+\frac{d-1}{2}+\sum_{i}\mu_{i}R_{i}\biggr)+4E^{2}\mathbf{x}^{2}\biggr\}.

(4.11)

It only remains to combine equations (

5
Invariance algebra of the Dunkl-Coulomb problem in Schrödinger representation

On considering

H=\frac{1}{r}(K-\alpha)+E.

(5.1)

instead of
K
, it is obvious that the angular momentum components
J_{ij}
remain integrals of motion:

[J_{ij},H]=0.

(5.2)

This is not the case, however, for the components of
\mathbf{B}
, which have to be transformed. As we plan to show now, the operators
\tilde{A}_{i}
, defined by

\tilde{A}_{i}=B_{i}+x_{i}(H-E),

(5.3)

have that property, namely

[\tilde{A}_{i},H]=0.

(5.4)

From (
5.1
) and (
5.3
), this amounts to showing that

[B_{i},H]+[x_{i},H](H-E)=0.

Since

[B_{i},H]=\biggl[B_{i},\frac{1}{r}(K-\alpha)+E\biggr]=\biggl[B_{i},\frac{1}{r}\biggr](K-\alpha)=\biggl[B_{i},\frac{1}{r}\biggr]r(H-E)

(5.7)

equation (
5.5
) is equivalent to

\biggl[B_{i},\frac{1}{r}\Biggr]r=-D_{i}.

(5.8)

On inserting definition (
4.4
) in the left-hand side of (

), we get

\biggl[B_{i},\frac{1}{r}\biggr]=\biggl[-\frac{1}{2}x_{i}\mathbf{D}^{2}+D_{i}\biggl(\mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{D}+\frac{d-3}{2}+\sum_{j}\mu_{j}R_{j}\biggr),\frac{1}{r}\biggr],

where

\begin{split}&\biggl[D_{i},\frac{1}{r}\biggr]=-\frac{x_{i}}{r^{3}},\\
&\biggl[\mathbf{D}^{2},\frac{1}{r}\Biggr]=-\frac{2}{r^{3}}\biggl(\mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{D}+\frac{d-3}{2}+\sum_{j}\mu_{j}R_{j}\biggr),\\
&\biggl[\mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{D},\frac{1}{r}\biggr]=-\frac{1}{r}.\end{split}

Hence, equation (

) is fulfilled, so that the components of
\mathbf{\tilde{A}}_{i}

On combining equation (

where

with
\mathbf{J}=\mathbf{x}\times\mathbf{p}
(see, e.g., equation (3) of Ref.
[
25
]
.

6
Conclusion

In the present work, we have shown that the rich algebraic structure of the standard three-dimensional (or, more generally,
d
-dimensional) Coulomb problem can be extended to its Dunkl counterpart.

To start with, we have proved that the replacement of standard derivatives by Dunkl ones transforms the so(
d+1
,2) dynamical algebra of the standard
d
-dimensional Coulomb problem into a deformed algebra with similar commutation relations, except that the metric tensor becomes dependent on the reflection operators and that there are some additional commutation or anticommutation relations involving the latter.

We have then shown that from some of the dynamical algebra generators, it is straightforward to derive the integrals of motion of the Dunkl-Coulomb problem in Sturm representation.

From the latter, we have then built the components of a deformed LRL vector, which, with the Dunkl angular momentum, provide integrals of motion of the Dunkl-Coulomb problem in Schrödinger representation and insure its superintegrability.

Studying the detailed action of the operators introduced in the present paper on the Dunkl-Coulomb Hamiltonian eigenstates would be an interesting subject for future work.
