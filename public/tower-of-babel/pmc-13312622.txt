Short-term clinical and radiographic outcomes after isolated medial patellofemoral ligament reconstruction for recurrent and first-time traumatic patellar dislocation with severe trochlear dysplasia, increased tibial tuberosity–trochlear groove distance, patella alta and lower extremity torsion deformities
Authors: Yi Hua, Hua Wang, Xu Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo report the clinical and radiographic outcomes of isolated medial patellofemoral ligament (MPFL) reconstruction in patients with recurrent or first-time traumatic patellar dislocation accompanied by multiple anatomical risk factors.MethodsThis retrospective study included 27 patients (27 knees) with a mean age of 22.70 ± 6.27 years (range, 14–35 years) who underwent isolated MPFL reconstruction between June 2019 and December 2023. All patients presented with severe trochlear dysplasia (Dejour types B-D), increased tibial tuberosity-trochlear groove (TT-TG) distance (>20 mm), patella alta (Caton-Deschamps index ≥ 1.2), and lower extremity torsion deformities (femoral anteversion ≥ 25° or external tibial rotation ≥ 30°). Clinical outcomes were evaluated using International Knee Documentation Committee (IKDC), Lysholm, Kujala, and Tegner scores. Radiographic parameters (patellar tilt angle (PTA) and lateral patellar displacement (LPD)) were measured on CT scans preoperatively and postoperatively. Return to sports, patient satisfaction (numeric rating scale 0–10 and categorical grades), redislocation rate, and complications were recorded at final follow-up.ResultsMean follow-up was 3.14 ± 1.15 years (range, 1-4.42 years). The mean TT-TG distance was 22.22 ± 1.58 mm (range, 20.32–25.91 mm), the mean Caton-Deschamps index (CDI) was 1.41 ± 0.18 (range, 1.22–2.01), the mean femoral anteversion angle was 29.28° ± 3.88° (range, 25.26°-35.41°) and the mean external tibial rotation angle was 35.42° ± 4.54° (range, 30.62°-47.07°). All functional scores improved significantly (P < .001): Kujala (49.30 ± 9.36 to 87.85 ± 4.59), Lysholm (51.41 ± 9.61 to 88.63 ± 5.71), IKDC (44.46 ± 8.69 to 83.04 ± 3.86), and Tegner score (from 2.00 ± 1.03 to 3.67 ± 0.92). PTA decreased from 24.02 ± 7.07° to 7.31 ± 3.45° and LPD decreased from 10.55 ± 4.33 mm to 0.11 ± 6.28 mm (P < .001). One patient had postoperative subluxation and one experienced redislocation after direct trauma. Mean subjective satisfaction was 8.72.ConclusionsIsolated MPFL reconstruction provides satisfactory short-term clinical outcomes with a low redislocation rate in patients with patellar instability and multiple severe anatomical risk factors.

Introduction

Patellar instability predominantly affects young and active individuals and can cause significant functional limitations [1, 2]. Although it is often encountered as a sports-related injury after traumatic patellar dislocation, its etiology is multifactorial and includes underlying bony abnormalities and generalized joint laxity [3]. The medial patellofemoral ligament (MPFL) is the primary soft-tissue restraint against lateral patellar displacement and is disrupted in up to 90% of first-time dislocation [4–6]. MPFL reconstruction has become the mainstay for surgical treatment because of its reliable clinical results and low redislocation rate [7, 8].

Several anatomical risk factors are strongly associated with recurrent instability, including increased tibial tuberosity–trochlear groove (TT-TG) distance, trochlear dysplasia, patella alta, and abnormal lower-extremity torsion [9–12]. Whether MPFL reconstruction should be combined with corrective bony procedures, including tibial tuberosity osteotomy (TTO), trochleoplasty, or distal femoral osteotomy, remains controversial [13]. Bony procedures restore normal anatomy but carry risks of nonunion, infection, fracture, and thromboembolism [14–16]. Numerous studies have shown good outcomes with isolated MPFL reconstruction even in the presence of bony abnormalities [13, 17–19]. However, data on isolated MPFL reconstruction in patients with multiple concurrent severe risk factors are still limited.

The purpose of this study was to evaluate the short-term clinical and radiographic outcomes of isolated MPFL reconstruction in patients with recurrent or first-time traumatic patellar instability who had severe trochlear dysplasia, increased TT-TG distance, patella alta, and lower extremity torsion deformities. We hypothesized that isolated MPFL reconstruction would yield satisfactory results in this high-risk population.

Methods

This retrospective study was approved by the Ethical Committee of our hospital (No. 2025237 K) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. All patients who underwent primary isolated MPFL reconstruction for patellar dislocation between June 2019 and December 2023 were screened.

Inclusion criteria

(1) first-time traumatic or recurrent patellar dislocation (RPD); (2) isolated MPFL reconstruction; (3) complete clinical and preoperative imaging data (lateral radiographs and full-length lower-limb CT); (4) patella alta (Caton-Deschamps index ≥ 1.2), TT-TG distance > 20 mm, severe trochlear dysplasia (Dejour type B, C, or D); (5) lower-extremity torsional deformities (femoral anteversion ≥ 25° or external tibial rotation ≥ 30°).

Exclusion criteria

(1) skeletally immature patients; (2) previous knee surgery; (3) concomitant bony procedures (trochleoplasty, femoral or tibial osteotomy); (4) concomitant other ligament injuries and generalized joint laxity; (5) follow-up <1 year.

Radiological assessment

The measurements of all radiological parameters were conducted by two trained authors (Y.H. and X.Y.). Patellar height was measured with the Caton-Deschamps index (CDI) [20]. TT-TG distance and PTA were measured according to Dejour et al. [21]. Trochlear dysplasia was classified using the Dejour system, types B-D were considered severe [22]. Femoral anteversion and external tibial rotation angles were measured using previously validated methods [23]. Postoperative CT scans were routinely obtained before hospital discharge (mean 5 days, range 3–7 days) to evaluate patellofemoral alignment and tunnel positions. Interrater reliability was assessed with the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC), with values < 0.4 defined as poor, 0.4–0.75 as good, and > 0.90 as excellent. Intra-rater reliability was not separately calculated because only a single set of measurements per observer was used for analysis.

Clinical evaluation

Patient data, including age, sex, affected side, and body mass index, were collected. Patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) included the Tegner, Lysholm, Kujala, and International Knee Documentation Committee (IKDC) scores. Satisfaction was assessed with both a 4-point categorical scale (very satisfied, satisfied, partially satisfied, not satisfied) and a numeric rating scale (0–10). Complications (redislocation, infection, stiffness, fracture, etc.) were recorded. Data were collected at clinic visit, by mail, or by telephone.

Surgical technique

All procedures were performed by the senior author using a standardized isolated double-bundle MPFL reconstruction technique. Spinal anesthesia and nonsterile tourniquet were routinely used in all patients. First, a diagnostic knee arthroscopy was conducted to evaluate trochlear and patellar surfaces, loose body, patellar tracking, and other concomitant intra-articular injuries. The ipsilateral semitendinosus tendon was harvested as the standard autograft for all cases.

Patellar fixation

The patellar periosteum was exposed through a 3 cm incision along the medial patellar edge. Following exposure of the patellar periosteum, two guidewires were inserted into the proximal third and midpoint of the patella. Two 15-mm-deep patellar bone tunnels were then drilled along the guidewires using a 5-mm reamer. The midpoint of the autograft was then fixed to the patella through the two suture anchors.

Femoral fixation

A 3 cm incision was made in the adductor tubercle which was identified through palpation. The femoral graft fixation point was located at the Schöttle point [24]. A tunnel was then drilled to a depth of 30 mm using a 5-mm cannulated drill. Then, the graft ends were introduced into the femoral tunnel. Femoral graft fixation was performed using a 6 × 25 mm interference screw with the knee flexed to 90°. Patellar stability and tracking were checked again throughout full knee flexion and extension. The wound was routinely irrigated and sutured.

Rehabilitation

Weight-bearing was allowed as tolerated from postoperative day 1. Quadriceps strengthening began immediately after surgery. Range of motion was progressed to 90°by 4 weeks and full range of motion by 2 to 3 months. Return to running and jogging at 3 months and full sports at 6 months.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed with SPSS Statistics software ( Version 25.0, IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables are presented as mean ± SD or median (range). Pre- and postoperative PROMS and radiographic parameters were compared using paired t-test for normally distributed statistics or Wilcoxon signed-rank test for non-normally distributed statistics. P ≤ .05 was considered significant.

Results

Patient characteristics

Twenty-seven patients (27 knees) met the inclusion criteria (Fig. 1). Mean age was 22.70 ± 6.27 years (range 14–35). Fourteen patients had first-time traumatic dislocation and 13 had recurrent dislocation. 37% of patients were male and mean BMI was 22.62 ± 3.52 kg/m2 (range, 15.22–31.48). 63% of the patients were affected in the left knee. The mean follow-up for the cohort was 3.14 ± 1.15 years (range, 1.00-4.42 years).

Clinical and radiographic outcomes

All PROMs improved significantly (Table 2). Kujala score, from 49.30 ± 9.36 to 87.85 ± 4.59 (P < .001); Lysholm score, from 51.41 ± 9.61 to 88.63 ± 5.71 (P < .001); Tegner score, from 2.00 ± 1.03 to 3.67 ± 0.92 (P < .001); and IKDC score, from 44.46 ± 8.69 to 83.04 ± 3.86 (P < .001).

Mean subjective satisfaction score was 8.72 ± 1.48. Six patients (22%) were very satisfied, 16 (59%) satisfied, and 4 (15%) partially satisfied, and 1 (4%) not satisfied due to activity-related pain that resolved with rest (Table 3).

Complications

Two patients (7.4%) reported mild anterior knee pain in cold/rainy weather. One patient (3.7%) had activity-related pain that resolved with rest. One (3.7%) superficial wound infection at the harvest site healed with dressings. One (3.7%) deep knee infection required debridement and resolved. One patient (3.7%) had postoperative patellar subluxation that did not require revision. One patient (3.7%) sustained a redislocation 17 months postoperatively after direct valgus trauma during basketball, revision MPFL reconstruction was performed. This patient did not return to sport for fear of re-dislocation. Overall complication rate was 18.5%, redislocation rate was 3.7% (traumatic)/ 7.4% (including subluxation). No patellar fracture or stiffness occurred.

Discussion

This study demonstrated that isolated MPFL reconstruction can achieve good short-term clinical results in a challenging cohort with multiple severe anatomical risk factors. 93% of patients returned to sports, and the 7.4% redislocation rate is comparable to rates reported in literatures and is acceptable given the high-risk profile .

Over the past two decades, MPFL reconstruction has become a fundamental procedure for treating patellar dislocation due to its low re-dislocation rate and favorable clinical outcomes [25]. Currently, there remains controversy regarding whether patients with bony deformities require correction through combined surgical techniques, such as TTO, derotational femoral osteotomy (DFO), or trochleoplasty [26]. Erickson et al. [17] reported that isolated MPFL reconstruction resulted in satisfactory clinical outcomes and low re-dislocation rates regardless of trochlear dysplasia, CDI, and TT-TG distance at 1 and 2 years follow-up. In a large cohort study of 211 patients with a minimum 3-year follow-up, Sappey-Marinier et al. [13] demonstrated that isolated MPFL reconstruction is safe and effective in the treatment of RPD, with a low failure rate of 4.7%. Both Hong et al. [18] and Dennis et al. [19] have reported favorable clinical outcomes with isolated MPFL reconstruction at 5-year follow-up. The current study reported the outcomes of isolated MPFL reconstruction for patellar instability with multiple bony deformities. A systematic review by Phillips et al. [25] reported a failure rate of 0–33% after isolated MPFL reconstruction. A meta-analysis by Vivekanantha et al. [27] reported a low redislocation rate of 3.1% after isolated MPFL reconstruction. In this study, isolated MPFL reconstruction also demonstrated a low redislocation rate of 7.4% in treating patients with multiple bony deformities.

Tibial Tuberosity Osteotomy (TTO)

Although the mean TT-TG distance in this series was 22.22 mm, and the mean CDI was 1.41, indicating the need for a TTO procedure. However, isolated MPFL reconstruction produced excellent outcomes. Recent systematic reviews and meta-analyses have questioned the additional benefit of TTO when TT-TG is only moderately elevated. A systematic review conducted by Song et al. [28] demonstrated that a TTO procedure may not provide additional benefit for patients with increased TT-TG distance. A meta-analysis by Vivekanantha et al. [27] demonstrated comparable clinical outcomes and return to sport rates between isolated MPFL reconstruction and combined MPFL reconstruction with TTO procedure. TTO is associated with higher complication rates, such as deep infection, tibial fracture, osteotomy nonunion, and thromboembolic events [14, 29]. A large-sample study by Markes et al. [30] reported that isolated MPFL reconstruction had the lowest complication rates (5.4%) compared to isolated TTO procedures (7.5%) and combined TTO with MPFL reconstruction procedures (7.1%). Furthermore, Meng et al. [31] also noted that the TTO procedure may prolong the postoperative recovery period and increase the rate of secondary operations for removing the internal fixation devices. Our findings support isolated MPFL reconstruction as a safer, more cost-effective first-line option in this population.

Lower-extremity torsional deformities

Torsional malalignment is an important risk factor. Over the past decade, derotational distal femoral osteotomy (DDFO) has become an increasingly popular surgical procedure for treating RPD, aiming to correct excessive femoral anteversion. In 2015, Nelitz et al. [15] first reported satisfactory outcomes of combined MPFL reconstruction and DFO in patients with excessive femoral torsion. Both Zhang et al. [32] and Hao et al. [33] reported that MPFL reconstruction combined with DDFO resulted in better outcomes than MPFL reconstruction without DDFO in patients with excessive femoral torsion. Currently, the optimal threshold for derotational osteotomy remains debated. While some scholars consider the minimum threshold for femoral anteversion to be 25°, others argue for a threshold of 30° [34]. Zhang et al. [32] adopted a minimum threshold of 30°, with an average anteversion angle of 34°, while Hao et al. [33] used a minimum threshold of 25°, with an average anteversion angle of 32.6°. In our cohort the mean femoral anteversion was 29.28°, milder than in studies reporting superior results with combined derotational osteotomy.

Similarly, tibial torsion contributes to the pathogenesis of patellar instability. Qiao et al. [35] found that increased tibial torsion (> 30°) was associated with inferior PROMs following combined MPFL reconstruction and TTO. Derotational High Tibial Osteotomy (DHTO) is designed to correct excessive tibial torsion. However, studies reporting the outcomes of DHTO for patellar instability with severe tibial torsion are limited. In 2010, Fouilleron et al. [36] reported that DHTO leads to satisfactory outcomes for patients with severe tibial torsion.

A recent systematic review by Ferreira et al. [16] demonstrated that DHTO was associated with a high complication rate (13.8%), with the most common complications being pain and symptoms related to the peroneal nerve. A systematic review by Hao et al. [37] reported a complication rate of 10.6% for DDFO, with the most common complications being pain (60%) and stiffness (20%). In this study, 8 knees had severe femoral anteversion, and 24 knees had severe tibial torsion. Satisfactory clinical outcomes were observed with isolated MPFL reconstruction, and we do not recommend routine derotational osteotomy in patients with borderline torsional values, given the higher complication rates (10.6–13.8%) and prolonged rehabilitation associated with these procedures.

Severe trochlear dysplasia

Trochlear dysplasia is a major contributor to instability [9]. A biomechanical study by Amis et al. [38] found that lateral patellar stability was significantly reduced in simulated trochlear dysplasia but was improved by trochleoplasty (TP). Frings et al. [39] found that MPFL reconstruction combined with TP resulted in satisfactory short-term clinical outcomes without progressive cartilage degeneration. In a long-term follow-up (10 to 20 years), Dejour et al. [40] reported that combined MPFL reconstruction and sulcus-deepening TP provided satisfactory outcomes with no or minimal patellofemoral osteoarthritis. However, a meta-analysis by Leclerc et al. [41] reported notable risks of patellofemoral arthritis (27%) and stiffness (7%) following the TP for patellofemoral instability. It remains controversial whether TP increase the risk of patellofemoral osteoarthritis. No direct comparative studies exist between isolated MPFL reconstruction and combined MPFL reconstruction and TP in patients with severe dysplasia. Our short-term results suggest that isolated MPFL reconstruction can stabilize the patella even in Dejour B-D dysplasia. Longer follow-up is needed to assess osteoarthritis risk.

Exclusion of habitual patellar dislocation

This study included patients with RPD and first-time traumatic patellar dislocations but excluded those with habitual patellar dislocation (HPD). Research indicates that compared to RPD and traumatic patellar dislocations, HPD is often associated with more severe bony deformities of the knee [42–44]. However, the more prominent pathological feature of habitual patellar dislocation is lateral soft tissue contracture of the knee, with a key treatment approach being comprehensive release of the contracted lateral soft tissues [45, 46]. In contrast, patients with recurrent or first-time traumatic patellar dislocations rarely present with lateral soft tissue contracture of the knee.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, it is a single-center retrospective analysis with a small sample size and no formal power calculation. The heterogeneous inclusion of both first-time and recurrent dislocations may introduce bias. Potential selection bias (single-center design) and recall bias (PROMs collected by telephone/mail) were present, outcome assessors were not blinded and sensitivity analyses were not performed. The study was underpowered to detect rare events such as redislocation. Second, the mean follow-up of 3.14 years is relatively short, mid- to long-term outcomes, particularly the risk of patellofemoral osteoarthritis, may differ. Third, postoperative imaging was incomplete in five patients, potentially introducing selection bias. Another limitation is the timing of postoperative CT imaging, which was performed prior to hospital discharge. This early assessment may be influenced by acute postoperative changes such as edema or soft-tissue swelling, potentially affecting the radiographic measurement. Longer-term imaging would be valuable to confirm the stability of these radiographic outcomes, despite the satisfactory short-term clinical results observed. Finally, no control group (isolated MPFL vs. combined procedures or patients without bony abnormalities) was included. Future multicenter randomized controlled trials are necessary to confirm these findings.

Conclusion

Isolated MPFL reconstruction is an effective and safe treatment for recurrent and first-time traumatic patellar dislocation in patients with multiple severe anatomical risk factors, providing significant functional improvement, and a low redislocation rate at short-term follow-up.
