In Silico toxicity assessment of Euphorbia hirta
Authors: Prabhu Subramani, Senthil Murugappan, Gajalakshm Ganeshi, Sumathi Jones, Devi Mani
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground:Herbs have evolved as an alternative and safer option in deriving antibiotics to combat antimicrobial resistance. Euphorbia hirta is a tropical weed. It possesses many beneficial medicinal values; the present study was undertaken to determine in silico toxicity of Eurphorbia hirta.Materials and Methods:In silico toxicity estimation was carried out using The Toxicity Estimation Software Tool (TEST). LC50 threshold was calculated using TEST software based on predictions from each model and the consensus average of the component models.Results:The phytoconstituents 3, 7, 11, 15-tetramethyl-2-hexadecen-1-ol, 9, 12, 15-octadecatrienoic acid, 9-octadecenamide, henicosane, hentriacontane, hexadecanoic acid, hexadecanoic acid, methyl ester, phytol, squalene, tetracosane, and Urs - 12- 2n-24-oic acid, 3-oxo-, methyl ester are nontoxic.Conclusion:Phytochemical constituents of Euphorbia hirta exhibit an acceptable toxicity limit for drug production and preclinical and clinical appraisal.

INTRODUCTION

Infectious diseases are widespread in the population, and the current use of antibiotics has shown that many bacteria are developing resistance to the conventionally prescribed antibiotics; therefore, there is a need to develop newer and more effective antibiotics.[1] Herbal preparations have always been used to treat health problems; plants like neem, tulsi, turmeric, and aloe vera are widely used in ancient systems of medicine to cure infections.[2] A few herbal preparations have similar efficacy as conventional antibiotics and have less or no side effects compared to artificial preparations, so the development of herbal preparations as antibiotics for human consumption will be safe and nontoxic.[3]

Euphorbia hirta is a tropical weed commonly found in South Asia. It possesses antimicrobial, anti-inflammatory, antidiabetic, and antioxidant properties[4] and is also used to treat asthma[5] and to increase platelet count in dengue fever.[6] The study of Euphorbia hirta’s chemical makeup showed that it contains numerous substances with potential medical benefits. Unfortunately, there is a lack of information regarding the toxicity of this plant. Therefore, this study aimed to assess the virtual toxicity of Euphorbia hirta when taken orally.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

The raw form of e. Hirta was analyzed separately by GC-MS using a hp-5 ms capillary column (30 m ˆ 250 μm, i.E., 0.25 μm film thickness) in an Agilent 6890 n gas chromatograph (Agilent Technologies, Palo Alto, CA, USA) coupled to a water GCT premier mass spectrometer (Waters Corporation, Milford, ma, USA). The carrier gas was helium with a constant flow rate of 3 ml/min. The oven temperature was initially kept at 70 c for 3 min and then ramped at 10*c/min to 240*c. The temperature was gradually increased from 8*c/min to 300*c and held isothermally for 9 minutes. An amount of 1.0 μl of the sample solutions was injected in the splitless mode. Mass spectra were obtained over the scan range m/z 40–600. The compounds were identified by comparison of their mass spectra with those of the NIST 05 l mass spectral library. The spectral match factor limit was set at 600, and any components with a match factor less than 600 were not considered; phytochemistry revealed the presence of 19 active components in the extract.

In silico toxicity estimation was performed using the toxicity estimation software tool (test), which was designed to facilitate the estimation of chemical toxicity using quantitative structure activity relationship (QSARs) methodologies.[7] Lc50 threshold was calculated using test (https://www.Epa.Gov/chemical-research/toxicity-estimation-software-tool-test) software based on predictions from each model and the consensus average of the component models.[8] The hierarchical technique, the single model method, the group contribution method, the consensus method, and the nearest neighbor method are all different approaches used in this study.

T.E.S.T. allows to estimate the value for several toxicity endpoints:

• Oral rat LD50 (amount of chemical in mg/kg body weight that is lethal to 50% of rats after oral ingestion).[9] The endpoint of the oral rodent LD50 is the measure of the compound (chemical mass per rodent body weight) that destroys half of the rodents when administered orally.

The oral LD50 was determined using four different methods for the chosen compound, and the results were analyzed.

Hierarchical method: The toxicity for a given query compound is estimated using the weighted average of the predictions from several different models. The various models are achieved by employing Ward’s method to partition the training set into clusters that are structurally similar. The models are created beforehand before the actual execution of the program.Single-model method: Predictions are made using a multilinear regression model that is fit to the training set (using molecular descriptors as independent variables) using a genetic algorithm-based approach. The regression model is created before the actual execution of the program.Group contribution method: Predictions are made using a multilinear regression model fitted to the training set (using molecular fragment numbers as independent variables). The regression model is developed prior to the execution of the program.Nearest neighbor method: The predicted toxicity is estimated using the average of the three chemicals in the training set that are most similar to the test chemical.Consensus method: The predicted toxicity is estimated by taking the average of the predicted toxicities from each of the above QSAR methodologies.

RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

The toxicity assessment software tool is used to predict the toxicity values of chemical compounds. This tool is cost-effective, time-consuming, and supported by the authorities. This software also reduces the use of animals and enables the prediction of compounds prior to in vivo animal testing. Table 2 shows that the oral LD50 of phytoconstituents, which are 5000–15,000 mg/kg, are nontoxic and can be easily administered to rodents. According to the toxicity scale of Hodge and Sterner, the phytoconstituents 3, 7, 11, 15-tetramethyl-2-hexadecen-1-ol, 9, 12, 15-octadecatrienoic acid, 9–octadecenamide, henicosan, hentriacontane, hexadecanoic acid, hexadecanoic acid, methyl ester, phytol, squalene, tetracosan, and urs- 12- 2n-24-oic acid, 3-oxo-, methyl ester are nontoxic. The compounds alpha-amyrin, beta-amyrin, beta-sitosterol, diethyl phthalate, gamma-sitosterol, and vitamin E are slightly nontoxic. The phytoconstituents stigmastane 3-5 diene and tetracosane are moderately nontoxic. Phytoconstituents that have been determined to be nontoxic can be advanced to subsequent studies.

CONCLUSION

Toxicity assessments indicate that the phytochemical composition of Euphorbia hirta is within safe limits for preclinical and clinical drug development.
