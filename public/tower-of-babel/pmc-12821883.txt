Associations of weight-derived markers with mortality in patients with Corona virus disease 2019: evidence from hospitals and patients
Authors: Yanqiu Li, Shuang-Shuang Song, Hang Ruan, Cancan Gong, Yingjie Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesThis study investigated the relationship between weight-derived markers and in-hospital mortality in patients with Corona Virus Disease 2019 (COVID-19).MethodsVarious body composition including Weight, Body Mass Index (BMI), Body Fat Percentage (BFP), Whole-Body Fat Mass (WBFM), Lean Body Mass (LBM), and Basal Metabolic Rate (BMR) were calculated based on height, weight, gender, and age. In-hospital mortality served as the primary clinical outcome. The associations between these indicators and patient prognosis were evaluated using a crude model, a logistic Model adjusted for confounders, and a Propensity Score Matching (PSM) model. The corresponding 95% confidence intervals (95% CI) and odds ratio (OR) values were calculated. Additionally, four machine-learning predictive models (Decision Tree Classifier, Random Forest, Gaussian Naive Bayes, Gradient Boosting Classifier) were developed to assess the clinical utility of weight-derived markers.ResultsA total of 509 patients with COVID-19 were included in the study. Among the weight-derived markers, only BMI consistently demonstrated a protective effect against in-hospital mortality (crude model: OR (95% CI) = 0.84 (0.77–0.92); adjusted model 1: OR (95% CI) = 0.84 (0.77–0.92); PSM: OR (95% CI) = 0.85 (0.74–0.97), all P < 0.05). Restricted Cubic Spline regression indicated significant nonlinear associations between BMI, Weight, LBM, and WBFM with in-hospital mortality (P for overall < 0.05). Conversely, no significant nonlinear associations were observed between BFP, BMR, and in-hospital mortality. The BMI-based Random Forest prediction model effectively forecasted in-hospital mortality (ROC (95% CI) = 0.84 (0.76–0.92)).ConclusionsHigher BMI was associated with reduced in-hospital mortality in patients with COVID-19, with the BMI-based predictive model demonstrating strong predictive capabilities.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12890-025-04049-2.

Introduction

COVID-19, short for Corona Virus Disease 2019, is caused by the severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2). During the COVID-19 pandemic, all 21 countries experienced excess mortality in 2022, ranging from 8.6 to 116.2 per 100,000 population, with the acknowledgment that rates were not directly comparable across countries [1]. The World Health Organization (WHO) has identified COVID-19 as a significant global public health challenge, with global cumulative deaths surpassing 7 million by August 2024 [2]. WHO data further underscores persistent disparities in COVID-19 fatality rates among various regions, age demographics, and populations with underlying health conditions. Increasingly, factors related to body composition and weight are being recognized as potential modifiers of mortality risk in post-pandemic analyses. The continuing high disease burden, combined with uncertainties surrounding the influence of weight indices on outcomes, emphasizes the importance of investigating mortality-related risk factors in current research efforts.

Recent clinical studies exploring mortality risks in COVID-19 patients have encountered a controversial issue concerning the prognostic impact of obesity. Some studies suggest that obese patients exhibit improved outcomes, characterized by prolonged survival and lower mortality rates [3, 4]. On the other hand, certain research findings propose that obesity does not significantly influence the prognosis of patients with COVID-19 [5, 6], and may even contribute to poorer outcomes [7]. Consequently, the examination of obesity-related factors, including broader weight-derived markers, in predicting outcomes for COVID-19 patients has become a significant area of interest in recent times.

While obesity is generally associated with an elevated risk of various diseases, recent animal and clinical research has introduced the concept of the “obesity paradox” [8]. Some studies have utilized body mass index (BMI) as a marker of obesity and have observed that obese individuals with sepsis do not exhibit a worse prognosis compared to normal-weight counterparts [9, 10]. This unexpected phenomenon challenges the traditional understanding of obesity-related complications. However, relying solely on BMI for obesity assessment may overlook crucial differences in body composition [11], suggesting the need to investigate the impact of weight-derived markers on the prognostication of patients with COVID-19.

Several studies have demonstrated that various body components, including Body Fat Percentage (BFP), Lean Body Mass (LBM), Whole-body fat mass (WBFM), Basal Metabolic Rate (BMR), and others, can be accurately estimated through mathematical formulas utilizing parameters such as height, age, sex, and body weight [12–15]. By examining the impact of these indicators on the in-hospital prognosis of patients with COVID-19, we can further elucidate the contribution of different body components to patient outcomes.

Materials and methods

Study design

This study was a single-center, retrospective cohort study that enrolled patients with COVID-19 treated at the Department of Respiratory and Critical Care Medicine in Yantai Yuhuanding Hospital from December 2022 to October 2023. Sample size and power analysis

The sample size for the study was predetermined, given the retrospective design. Post-hoc power analyses were performed using PASS 15.0 software to assess the study’s statistical power. The calculated power from the analysis was determined to be 0.95.

Ethical considerations

This study was conducted in strict compliance with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki (2013 revision) and all applicable national/institutional regulations for research involving human subjects. All study protocols were reviewed and approved by the Ethics Review Committee of Yantai Yuhuangding Hospital (Approval Number: 2024 − 1181). Given the retrospective nature of the research—where data were extracted from de-identified, routinely collected electronic health records (EHRs)—the ethics committee determined that written informed consent from patients was unnecessary, as the study posed no foreseeable risk to participants and did not involve interaction with or intervention on living subjects. To ensure the strict protection of patient confidentiality, the following measures were implemented throughout the study:

Data de-identification: Before data extraction, all personally identifiable information (PII) was permanently removed from EHRs, including but not limited to patients’ full names, identification numbers (e.g., national ID or hospital registration numbers), dates of birth, home addresses, and contact information. Only non-identifiable study identifiers (e.g., unique, anonymized serial numbers) were used to link data during analysis.

Secure data storage: All de-identified study data (including electronic spreadsheets and database files) were stored on encrypted secure servers managed by the hospital’s medical informatics department. Access to these servers required multi-factor authentication, and only members of the research team with pre-approved roles (principal investigator, data analysts) were granted access.

Data use restriction: All study data were used exclusively for the purposes of this research and were not shared with any third parties (e.g., external institutions, commercial entities) without additional ethical approval. Upon completion of the study, the de-identified data will be archived in the hospital’s secure research data repository for 10 years (per institutional policy) before permanent destruction.

Study population, inclusion, and exclusion criteria

The diagnostic criteria for the study cohort with clinical features of COVID-19 included symptoms such as dry throat, sore throat, cough, and fever, alongside positive results from various pathogenetic and serological tests, namely: (1) positive nucleic acid test for COVID-19 in respiratory specimens (nasopharyngeal swabs, pharyngeal swabs, sputum, tracheal aspirations) or other samples using nucleic acid amplification within 24 h of hospital admission; (2) Positive detection of COVID-19 antigen in respiratory specimens using colloidal gold and immunofluorescence; (3) isolation and culture of COVID-19 from respiratory or fecal specimens; and (4) at least a 4-fold increase in COVID-19-specific IgG antibody levels during the acute phase in serological testing. Exclusion criteria encompassed pregnant individuals and minors under the age of 18.

Measurement of weight and height

Undergo measurements in the morning, after an overnight fast (≥ 8 h) and prior to any meals or oral fluid intake. Weight and height were used to calculate BMI (kg/m²). Measurements were obtained via standardized protocols, with adjustments for patients admitted to the intensive care unit (ICU) to ensure accuracy despite their clinical status:

For non-ICU patients: Patients were instructed to remain calm for 10 min before assessment, avoid vigorous activity, and wear light clothing (no heavy accessories). Standing height was measured with a wall-mounted stadiometer (precision: 0.1 cm): patients removed shoes/socks, stood upright with heels together, toes separated by ~ 60°, and heels/sacrum/mid-scapular region in contact with the stadiometer column. The head was held erect (upper earlobe edge aligned with the lower orbital rim), and height was recorded to the nearest 0.1 cm. Weight was measured with a digital scale (precision: 0.1 kg): patients stood barefoot at the scale’s center, and weight was recorded once stable.

For ICU patients (due to immobility or use of life-support equipment): Recumbent length (proxy for standing height): Measured with a calibrated non-stretchable tape (precision: 0.1 cm) by two trained staff. Patients were placed in supine position with legs extended; the tape was aligned from the head’s vertex to the heel’s inferior edge (midline or parallel to midline, if devices obstructed the path). Measurements were recorded to the nearest 0.1 cm. Recumbent length was used for BMI calculation (note: recumbent length is typically 0.5–2 cm longer than standing height, but this difference is consistent and does not affect intra-cohort comparisons). Weight measurements were taken utilizing bed-integrated digital scales or portable bed scales, which were properly tared to adjust for the weight of the bed and any medical devices attached. Measurements were recorded to the nearest 0.1 kg.

Calculation of weight-derived markers

In the present study, weight-derived markers and serum osmolarity were calculated using validated formulas from established literature, with detailed equations provided in Supplementary Table 1 for clarity. BMI was computed as the ratio of weight (kg) to height squared (m²), a standard metric for assessing weight status in clinical research [8]. Lean Body Mass (LBM), representing non-adipose tissue mass, was estimated using a formula specific to East Asian adults, incorporating weight (kg) and height (cm) as input variables [12]. BFP, which quantifies the proportion of fat mass relative to total body weight, was calculated using a validated equation that integrates BMI, age, and sex (1 for males, 0 for females) [13]. WBFM, the absolute quantity of fat mass in kilograms, was derived by multiplying total body weight (kg) by BFP [14]. BMR, reflecting resting daily energy expenditure, was estimated using the Mifflin-St Jeor equation, with separate formulations for males and females that account for weight (kg), height (cm), and age (years) [15].

Collection and categorization criteria for covariates

In this study, we also gathered additional potential prognostic factors for patients with COVID-19, including demographic parameters such as age, gender, and lifestyle habits, as well as markers of disease severity like Oxygen Saturation/Fraction of Inspired Oxygen ratio (SpO2/FiO2 ratio), Clinical Pulmonary Infection Score (CPIS), age-adjusted Charlson Comorbidity Index (aCCI), mean arterial pressure (MAP), partial pressure of arterial carbon dioxide (PaCO2), creatinine, blood urea nitrogen (BUN), serum osmolarity, and others. All laboratory findings were recorded as the initial measurements taken within the first 24 h of hospital admission. The CPIS was utilized to evaluate the severity of pulmonary infection in patients with COVID-19 [16]. The aCCI served as a composite score to assess the impact of comorbid conditions on patient prognosis [17]. Blood counts were performed concurrently, and serum osmolarity, a key indicator of plasma solute concentration essential for assessing fluid-electrolyte balance, was calculated based on plasma levels of sodium (Na⁺), potassium (K⁺), glucose (Glu), and urea. This calculation utilized a well-established formula that integrates these electrolytes and metabolites [18].

Variables were grouped based on existing literature and prior knowledge. Sex was categorized as male and female according to biological sex. Age was divided into “elderly” and “non-elderly” categories using a threshold of 60 years. Partial pressure of carbon dioxide was derived from blood gas analyses conducted at admission and segmented into two groups using a cut-off value of 50 mmHg. Additionally, comorbidities present in patients upon admission were documented and grouped into six subcategories, which included cerebrovascular disease, cardiovascular disease, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, hypoproteinemia (Albumin ≤ 35 g/L), and hyperlactatemia (Lactate ≥ 2 mmol/L).

Clinical outcome

In this study, the selected outcome measures served as short-term prognostic indicators for patients with COVID-19. These indicators comprised in-hospital mortality, length of hospital stay, total duration of oxygen therapy, and requirement for mechanical ventilation (MV). Among these, in-hospital mortality was identified as the primary outcome, referring to all-cause mortality during the period between patient admission and discharge. Secondary outcomes included the duration of hospitalization, length of oxygen therapy, and the need for MV.

Testing for nonlinear relationships and adjustment for confounding factors

In this study, RCS regression was utilized to explore potential nonlinear relationships, with regression nodes placed at the 5th, 35th, 65th, and 95th percentiles [19]. The Generalized Linear Model (GLM) was applied with a logit link function to analyze the association between changes in Weight-Derived Markers and the binary response variable. To enhance the robustness of the findings, sensitivity analyses were conducted using propensity score matching (PSM) with a 1:1 ratio and a caliper value of 0.01. Finally, E-value calculations were executed to further evaluate the study outcomes [20].

Exploring the clinical value of weight-derived markers

This study compared the clinical value of Weight-Derived Markers in several ways. First, Weight-Derived Markers were ranked for importance using the Boruta algorithm in machine learning, which is a Random Forest-based feature selection algorithm used to filter out features with high correlation with the target variable [21]. Second, the receiver operating characteristic (ROC), area under ROC curve (AUC), and 95% confidence intervals (CI) were calculated. The predictive performance of different metrics was compared using the DeLong test [22]. To explore the clinical application value of weight-derived markers, the best metrics were selected, and four machine learning models based on BMI - Gradient Boosting Classifier (GBC), Random Forest (RF), Gaussian Naive Bayes (GNB), and Decision Tree Classifier (DTC) models were built for predicting in-hospital mortality. The dataset was divided into training and validation sets in an 80:20 ratio, and the models were trained on the training set and validated on the validation set. Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) logistic regression was performed for feature selection and optimal parameter tuning using grid search (GridSearchCV). The logistic model was separately constructed using the GLM function, and the corresponding net return scenarios were calculated and plotted using the “rmda” package for decision curve analysis (DCA). The machine learning model predictions were explained using SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) [23]. Finally, the predictive performance of the models was further assessed in different disease subgroups.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses for this study were conducted using Stata 17 (StataCorp, LLC; College Station, TX, USA), R software (version 4.3.0), and Anaconda3 (Anaconda, Inc). In this study, continuous variables conforming to a normal distribution are presented as mean ± standard deviation, whereas those deviating from a normal distribution are summarized as median (interquartile range [IQR]; Q1, Q3). Q1 (first quartile) represents the 25th percentile (25% of values ≤ Q1), and Q3 (third quartile) represents the 75th percentile (75% of values ≤ Q3). A t-test was employed for comparing two groups when the variables adhered to normal distribution, while the variance chi-squared test was utilized. If the data followed a normal distribution but not a variance chi-squared, the Welch t-test was used for comparisons between two groups. For data that deviated from a normal distribution, the Wilcoxon rank sum test was applied for intergroup comparisons. Supplementary Fig. 1 illustrates the normality test results for weight-related indicators. Statistical significance was defined as a two-sided P-value < 0.05, indicating statistical significance in the analysis.

Results

Baseline data and clinical characteristics of study participants

A total of 509 patients with COVID-19 were included in this study, of whom 405 eventually recovered (defined as complete resolution of COVID-19-related symptoms, normal oxygenation on room air, and meeting hospital discharge criteria) or showed clinical improvement (defined as resolution of key symptoms such as persistent fever or severe cough, improvement in oxygenation [peripheral oxygen saturation (SpO2) ≥ 94% on room air without supplemental oxygen, or transition from advanced respiratory support to lower-level oxygen therapy or complete discontinuation of oxygen therapy]), while 104 succumbed to disease progression. Table 1 provides an overview of the demographic characteristics, disease severity, and weight-related indicators of the study participants. The study population consisted predominantly of older adults (n = 446, 87.6%), with a higher proportion of males (n = 328, 64.4%) and a minority of smokers (n = 117, 23.0%). Statistically significant differences were observed between the survivor and non-survivor groups regarding age distribution and disease severity indicators (SpO2/FiO2 ratio, CPIS, aCCI, PaCO2, Creatinine, BUN, serum osmolarity) (all P < 0.05). Non-survivors were more likely to be older (350 (86.4%) vs. 96 (92.3%), P < 0.001) and exhibited more severe disease. Additionally, statistically significant difference in comorbidities, specifically cardiovascular disease, hyperlactatemia, and hypoproteinemia, were observed between the two groups (all P < 0.05).

The association between Weight-Derived markers and In-Hospital mortality

After screening with Boruta’s algorithm, it was found that among the six weight-derived markers, BMI had the most substantial impact on in-hospital mortality (Fig. 2b). By plotting the ROC curve and calculating the AUC, LBM was selected as the reference group due to its lower predictive performance (AUC (95% CI) = 0.53 (0.47–0.59)). Among all weight-derived markers, BMI exhibited the highest predictive performance (AUC (95% CI) = 0.63 (0.56–0.69), with P-value for DeLong’s test < 0.01, as shown in Table 3. This was followed by WBFM (AUC (95% CI) = 0.61 (0.55–0.67), P-value for DeLong’s test > 0.05), Weight (AUC (95% CI) = 0.59 (0.52–0.65), P-value for DeLong’s test < 0.01), BFP (AUC (95% CI) = 0.55 (0.49–0.61), P-value for DeLong’s test > 0.05), and BMR (AUC (95% CI) = 0.54 (0.48–0.60), P-value for DeLong’s test > 0.05). Furthermore, this study explored potential nonlinear associations between different variables and in-hospital mortality. RCS regression revealed significant nonlinear relationships between BMI, Weight, LBM, and WBFM, and in-hospital mortality (all P for overall < 0.05, Fig. 2c). In contrast, no significant nonlinear associations were observed between BFP and in-hospital mortality, as well as between BMR and in-hospital mortality.

BMI identified as an independent protect factor for In-Hospital mortality

The crude model without adjustment for confounding factors and the GLM corrected for different confounders were utilized to assess the impact of weight-derived markers on in-hospital mortality (Fig. 3). Across all models, BMI consistently demonstrated a protective effect on in-hospital mortality (all OR < 1, P < 0.05). Specifically, in the unadjusted model, an increase in BMI was associated with a protective effect on in-hospital mortality (OR (95% CI) = 0.84 (0.77–0.92), P < 0.001). In the three adjusted models addressing demographic factors, disease severity, and comorbidities, BMI consistently displayed a protective effect (adjusted model 1: OR (95% CI) = 0.84 (0.77–0.92), P < 0.001; adjusted model 2: OR (95% CI) = 0.88 (0.80–0.97), P < 0.05; adjusted model 3: OR (95% CI) = 0.86 (0.78–0.94), P < 0.01, Fig. 3). No significant multicollinearity was observed among the variables in any of the models (Supplementary Tables 2–7). Furthermore, in the PSM model, BMI continued to demonstrate a protective effect on in-hospital mortality in patients with COVID-19 (OR (95% CI) = 0.85 (0.74–0.97), P < 0.05). The corresponding E-values of the BMI for in-hospital mortality were calculated using STATA, with a point estimate of 1.66 and a confidence interval of 1.40. None of the other weight-derived markers exhibited consistent results across the models. Lastly, no significant associations were observed between weight-derived markers and secondary clinical outcomes (Supplementary Table 7).

The predictive model based on BMI can effectively forecast in-hospital mortality

To further investigate the clinical utility of the most effective parameter among weight-derived markers, namely BMI, a predictive model was developed and its efficacy was evaluated across various comorbidity subgroups. Predictor variables such as “SpO2/FiO2 ratio”, “BUN”, “Creatinine”, “CPIS”, “aCCI”, “BMI”, and “Smoker”were selected after screening using a LASSO regression model with 10-fold cross-validation (Supplementary Fig.3 a, b). The optimal parameter configurations were fine-tuned using different machine learning algorithms in the validation set (SupplementaryFig.3 c-f), with the RF model exhibiting the most favorable predictive performance in the validation set (RF model: ROC (95% CI) = 0.84 (0.76–0.92), Fig. 4a). DCA curve indicated that the RF model offered superior net benefit in prediction when compared to other models (Fig. 4b). Furthermore, the SHAP, a tool for interpreting machine learning models, elucidated the role of BMI in the prediction model, demonstrating that higher BMI levels were associated with a reduced risk of mortality in patients with COVID-19 (Fig. 4c). Furthermore, the BMI-based RF prediction model exhibited robust predictive performance (all AUC > 0.8, Fig. 4d-i) across different comorbidity subgroups.

Discussion

The objective of this study was to explore the association between various weight-derived indicators and in-hospital mortality in individuals with COVID-19. The study ensured result robustness through applications of a crude GLM, GLM models adjusting for confounding factors, and a PSM model. Predictive models were also developed to assess the clinical relevance of these indicators. The findings suggest that BMI independently predicts in-hospital mortality among COVID-19 patients, as demonstrated by univariate and multivariate GLM regression analyses. Moreover, RCS regression illustrated a nonlinear correlation between BMI and in-hospital mortality. The BMI-based clinical prediction model effectively forecasted in-hospital mortality in individuals with COVID-19. Sensitivity analysis using E-value further corroborated the robustness of the results.

This study explored various physiological metrics derived from body weight calculations, including LBM, BMR, BFP, WBFM, and BMI, to account for differences in body tissue composition based on physiological parameters. The findings revealed that only an escalation in BMI consistently acted as a protective factor for in-hospital mortality, across the original, confounder-corrected BMI model, and the PSM model. In contrast, none of the other weight-related indicators demonstrated a significant impact on in-hospital mortality post-adjustment for confounders. This suggests that factors such as total body weight excluding fat, total adipose tissue weight, and body fat percentage did not significantly influence in-hospital mortality in patients with COVID-19. Given these observations, the study suggests that the commonly termed “obesity paradox” may represent a BMI paradox.

This study confirms that individuals with higher BMI have a reduced risk of in-hospital mortality in patients with COVID-19, a finding that is consistent with earlier clinical investigations [3, 24, 25]. For instance, a study in Japan involving 1,105 COVID-19 patients and utilizing all-cause mortality within 29 days as the primary outcome, revealed a lower mortality rate in patients with BMI ranging from 11 to 25 kg/m2 [24]. Similarly, in individuals with non-severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 viral pneumonia, a BMI of 35 kg/m2 or higher was associated with a protective effect against in-hospital mortality [25]. Additionally, a multicenter retrospective study corroborated this “obesity paradox” phenomenon in COVID-19 patients admitted to ICU [3]. Specifically, among 2,931 patients hospitalized in ICU for COVID-19, those with a BMI of 40 kg/m2 or greater displayed better survival outcomes at 2 years [3].

The influence of adipose tissue on COVID-19 outcomes, particularly in overweight patients and its association with in-hospital mortality, necessitates a comprehensive mechanistic exploration due to its role in the observed obesity paradox. From the perspective of the interaction between adipose tissue and SARS-CoV-2, the severity of COVID-19 is influenced by complex interactions with viral pathophysiology, metabolism, and immunity, with variations based on the specific context. Angiotensin-converting enzyme 2 (ACE2) has been identified as the primary entry receptor for the virus, while transmembrane serine protease 2 (TMPRSS2) plays a critical role as an entry factor for SARS-CoV-2 [26]. The expression of the ACE2 gene in visceral and subcutaneous adipose tissue is higher than in lung tissue [27]. Therefore, we posit that this characteristic potentially establishes adipocytes as a “viral reservoir,” where low-level viral replication may occur, subsequently reducing systemic viral load by sequestering the virus away from vital organs such as the lungs and heart during the acute phase.

From an energy reserve perspective, a high BMI is often considered an indicator of physical obesity, where an elevated BMI signifies the presence of excess fat accumulation in the body. Individuals classified as obese typically possess greater metabolic reserves than those with a normal weight, thereby better preparing them to cope with the heightened catabolic demands characteristic of acute and critical illnesses [26]. From an immunomodulatory perspective, infection with SARS-CoV-2 leads to a cytokine storm characterized by the release of inflammatory mediators including tumor necrosis factor α (TNF-α), interferon β (IFN-β), and interleukin 6 (IL-6) [25]. Adipose tissue secretes a variety of hormones and cytokines, known as adipokines, which play critical roles in regulating metabolic and immune functions [28]. In a study investigating adipose cell involvement in Fibrotic-Like Pulmonary Radiographic Patterns in COVID-19 patients and its association with adverse outcomes, IL-6 levels were inversely correlated with increasing BMI [30]. Furthermore, adipocytes exert a protective effect by secreting cytokines. Leptin, a proinflammatory cytokine produced by adipocytes, triggers inflammatory responses by binding to leptin receptors on the surface of immune cells. Serum leptin levels are positively correlated with total body fat mass. Research by Bornstein et al. [28] indicated that leptin concentration in critically ill septic patients who survived was three times higher than in those who did not survive, suggesting that elevated leptin levels may be associated with reduced mortality rates.

The lack of similar protective effects for BFP and WBFM is not contradictory to the mechanisms based on adipose tissue but rather stems from their distinct physiological meanings and measurement characteristics. BMI combines moderate obesity and maintained lean body mass, whereas BFP quantifies relative fat proportion and WBFM measures absolute fat quantity [8, 13, 14]. In our study cohort, patients with high BFP but low lean body mass did not exhibit a benefit in mortality rates, as these individuals may have excess fat with dysfunctional features. Meanwhile, measurements of WBFM may be affected by factors like patient edema, potentially distorting the actual fat content. Furthermore, the measurement of BFP and WBFM relies on formula-based estimates, which may introduce bias, whereas BMI provides a direct and stable measurement. This underscores that BMI better reflects the functional status of adipose tissue, rather than simply fat quantity, aligning with the protective mechanisms. Overall, the synergistic effect of adipose tissue through “viral sequestration (mediated by ACE2) - energy support (lipolysis for energy provision) - immune modulation (leptin/IL-6 balance)” demonstrates a protective advantage, especially evident during the acute phase of COVID-19.

Some studies have suggested that elevated BMI may not be correlated with prognosis in patients with COVID-19 and could potentially be detrimental [5–7]. These discrepancies in findings may be due to several factors. Firstly, the timing of data collection differed in the mentioned studies. Cases with negative outcomes were mainly recorded about a year after the initial COVID-19 outbreak, during a period when the virus may have exhibited higher pathogenicity. In contrast, the study population in this investigation spanned from December 2022 to October 2023, during which time the virus had evolved and mutated, potentially leading to reduced pathogenicity while maintaining increased transmissibility [29]. Secondly, racial variations in the obesity paradox may exist [30]. Previous studies have reported a U-shaped relationship between BMI and mortality in critically ill patients, indicating that both very high and very low BMI levels are associated with an increased risk of death [31]. East Asian populations generally have fewer individuals in the extremely obese category compared to populations in Europe and the United States [32, 33]. Notably, this study did not include extremely obese individuals, which could contribute to the divergence in results compared to other clinical investigations. Additionally, secondary clinical analyses in this study did not identify significant associations between higher BMI and length of hospital stay, duration of oxygen therapy, and the necessity for mechanical ventilation.

Limitation

Certain limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the generalizability of findings may be constrained by COVID-19 strain mutations—ongoing viral evolution alters transmissibility and pathogenicity, complicating cross-context applicability. Second, comorbidity heterogeneity among patients underscores the need for future subgroup analyses to clarify how different comorbid backgrounds might modify results [34]. Thirdly, being an observational study, we are unable to make definitive causal conclusions regarding the link between higher BMI and decreased in-hospital mortality. Additionally, the precise mechanisms behind the obesity/BMI paradox in the context of COVID-19 remain partially understood. To bridge these knowledge gaps, forthcoming clinical investigations should utilize sophisticated statistical techniques to emulate experimental designs. These methods could include target trial emulation, PSM, regression discontinuity design, and other pertinent approaches [8, 35]. Fourth, body composition markers (e.g., BFP, WBFM) were calculated via formulas rather than directly measured, introducing potential errors inherent to formula-based estimates. Lastly, the lack of data on nutritional status, inflammatory biomarkers (e.g., IL-6 or adiponectin levels), and specific antiviral treatments are important confounding factors that could influence both baseline BMI and study outcomes, and their absence should be noted as a limitation. Notably, while prior studies have hypothesized adipose tissue’s metabolic and immunomodulatory roles in critical illness, targeted COVID-19-specific basic and clinical research is still needed to validate these mechanisms.

Conclusions

In the single-center retrospective study of hospitalized COVID-19 patients, higher BMI was significantly linked to lower in-hospital mortality and identified as a key predictor. Given the study’s limitations—single-center design, retrospective nature, and focus on severe cases—these findings may not apply to mild or asymptomatic patients or diverse populations. Future multicenter prospective research with broader cohorts is necessary to confirm the BMI-COVID-19 mortality association and assess its generalizability across different patient groups.
