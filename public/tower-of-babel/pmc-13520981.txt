Impact of adsorption-based solidification on the pharmaceutical performance and antidyslipidemic efficacy of simvastatin–ezetimibe SNEDDS
Authors: Sani Ega Priani, Iyan Sopyan, Anis Yohana Chaerunisaa, Gofarana Wilar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSimvastatin and ezetimibe are widely used in combination therapy for dyslipidemia; however, their poor aqueous solubility limits oral bioavailability. This study aimed to develop and characterize liquid and solid self-nanoemulsifying drug delivery systems (L-SNEDDS and S-SNEDDS) containing simvastatin and ezetimibe and to evaluate the impact of adsorption-based solidification on their physicochemical characteristics, dissolution behavior, and antidyslipidemic efficacy. An optimized L-SNEDDS composed of Capryol® 90, Cremophor® RH40, and Transcutol® HP was successfully converted into S-SNEDDS by adsorption onto Neusilin® US2. The optimized L-SNEDDS exhibited rapid self-emulsification, high transmittance, and a globule size of 24.47 ± 0.55 nm, whereas S-SNEDDS readily reconstituted into a nanoemulsion with a globule size of 35.51 ± 0.32 nm. Adsorption-based solidification did not alter the chemical integrity of either drug and maintained both drugs predominantly in an amorphous or molecularly dispersed state. S-SNEDDS markedly increased the apparent solubility of both drugs, and both formulations substantially enhanced drug dissolution compared with the drug suspension, although dissolution from S-SNEDDS was slightly lower than that from L-SNEDDS. In a Poloxamer 407-induced dyslipidemia model, both formulations significantly reduced total cholesterol, LDL-C, and triglyceride levels compared with the dyslipidemic control and produced greater lipid-lowering effects than the drug suspension, while no significant differences were observed between L-SNEDDS and S-SNEDDS. These findings suggest that adsorption-based solidification preserved the pharmaceutical and therapeutic performance of the optimized L-SNEDDS while providing the practical advantages of a solid dosage form, supporting S-SNEDDS as a promising oral lipid-based delivery system for simvastatin–ezetimibe combination therapy.Keywords: SNEDDS, Solidification, Simvastatin, Ezetimibe, Mesoporous carrier

Introduction

Dyslipidemia remains one of the major modifiable risk factors for atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease, with elevated low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) representing the primary therapeutic target for reducing cardiovascular events (Liu et al., 2022; Su et al., 2021). Current clinical guidelines recommend statins as first-line therapy, while ezetimibe is commonly added when LDL-C targets cannot be achieved with statin monotherapy because the two agents act through complementary mechanisms by inhibiting cholesterol synthesis and intestinal cholesterol absorption, respectively. Consequently, the simvastatin–ezetimibe combination has become an established therapeutic strategy for patients requiring intensive lipid lowering (Banach et al., 2025; Kim et al., 2022; Ya'qoub et al., 2023).

The synergistic effect of simvastatin and ezetimibe has led to the development of fixed-dose combination (FDC) products to enhance lipid-lowering efficacy while improving treatment adherence (Ferri et al., 2023; Kim and Weon, 2021; Thangaraju et al., 2021). Despite the demonstrated clinical efficacy and regulatory approval of simvastatin–ezetimibe FDC tablets, both drugs possess intrinsic biopharmaceutical limitations (Knapik-Kowalczuk et al., 2019). Simvastatin and ezetimibe are classified as Biopharmaceutics Classification System (BCS) class II drugs, in which dissolution-rate-limited absorption contributes to variable systemic exposure (Bashir et al., 2023; Elkhayat et al., 2023). Simvastatin exhibits low oral bioavailability (<5%) because of poor aqueous solubility and extensive first-pass metabolism, whereas ezetimibe, despite undergoing enterohepatic recirculation, also requires adequate gastrointestinal solubilization for efficient absorption (Abd-Elghany et al., 2024; Bhandari et al., 2020; Sun et al., 2023). These intrinsic biopharmaceutical limitations highlight the need for advanced formulation strategies to improve the solubility and oral absorption of both drugs, with lipid-based drug delivery systems emerging as a promising approach (Priani et al., 2025a).

Among lipid-based drug delivery systems, self-nanoemulsifying drug delivery systems (SNEDDS) have attracted considerable attention because of their ability to improve the oral bioavailability of poorly water-soluble drugs (Dhaval et al., 2022; Jin et al., 2025). SNEDDS are isotropic mixtures that spontaneously form nanosized oil-in-water emulsions upon contact with gastrointestinal fluids, thereby enhancing drug solubilization and potentially improving oral absorption (Jvus et al., 2024). In addition, SNEDDS may facilitate lymphatic transport and reduce variability in oral absorption (Buya et al., 2021; Cherniakov et al., 2015; Zhang et al., 2021). Unlike nanoemulsions, SNEDDS are supplied as an anhydrous preconcentrate, providing greater formulation stability and simpler manufacturing (Buddhadev et al., 2024; Verma et al., 2022). However, their practical application is often limited by challenges such as leakage, interactions with capsule shells, handling difficulties, and broader concerns related to formulation stability and manufacturability, which are particularly critical in the context of simvastatin–ezetimibe co-delivery, where dose uniformity during storage and distribution is essential (Kim et al., 2026; Uttreja et al., 2025). Conversion into solid SNEDDS (S-SNEDDS) via adsorption onto porous carriers has emerged as an attractive strategy to address these limitations while preserving self-emulsifying properties (Galatage et al., 2023; Gul et al., 2024; Priani et al., 2025d; Uttreja et al., 2025).

In our previous study, a quality-by-design (QbD)-based liquid SNEDDS (L-SNEDDS) containing simvastatin and ezetimibe was successfully optimized and demonstrated enhanced dissolution performance. However, it remains unclear whether adsorption-based solidification can preserve the pharmaceutical and therapeutic performance of the optimized liquid formulation after conversion into a solid dosage form. In particular, the effects of solidification on redispersion behavior, solid-state properties, physicochemical characteristics, accelerated stability, and in vivo antidyslipidemic efficacy have not been established for this drug combination. Therefore, the present study converted the optimized L-SNEDDS into a solid SNEDDS (S-SNEDDS) by adsorption onto Neusilin® US2 and systematically compared the liquid and solid formulations with respect to redispersion characteristics, solid-state properties, physicochemical performance, accelerated stability, apparent solubility, dissolution behavior, and in vivo antidyslipidemic efficacy.

Materials and methods

Materials

Materials used in this study included simvastatin (Shangyu Jingxin Pharmaceutical, procured through PT. Kimia Farma) and ezetimibe (kindly provided by PT. Dexa Medica). Peceol®, Maisine®, Labrafac™ Lipophile WL, Capryol™ 90, Labrafil® M 1944, and Transcutol HP from Gattefossé were kindly provided as complimentary samples via the distributor PT. Menjangan Sakti. Tween 20, Tween 60, Tween 80, Cremophor® EL, Cremophor® RH 40, propylene glycol, polyethylene glycol (PEG) 400, and glycerin from BASF were kindly provided as complimentary samples through PT. Nutrindo Interfarma Sejahtera and Neusilin US2 was kindly provided by Fuji Chemical Industries. Methanol and acetonitrile (Merck), as well as a diagnostic kit for blood lipid analysis, were also used in this study. Male Wistar rats (8 weeks old, weighing 250 ± 50 g) were used for the antidyslipidemic study.

Preparation and characterization of L-SNEDDS SIM/EZE

The L-SNEDDS was prepared using an optimized formulation previously developed through a D-optimal mixture design approach, as described in our previous study (Priani et al., 2025b). The optimized formulation consisted of Capryol 90 (23.8%), Cremophor RH 40 (31.2%), and Transcutol HP (45.0%), with simvastatin (40 mg/g) and ezetimibe (20 mg/g) incorporated, corresponding to a single-dose amount of 0.5 g. The L-SNEDDS was prepared by dissolving simvastatin and ezetimibe in Capryol 90 under magnetic stirring at 600 rpm for 15 min, followed by the addition of Cremophor RH 40 and Transcutol HP, with continued stirring at the same speed for an additional 15 min, until a homogeneous mixture was obtained. The resulting system was then sonicated in a bath sonicator for 15 min to ensure complete homogenization. The physicochemical characteristics of the optimized L-SNEDDS were evaluated through a series of tests.

Emulsification time

One milliliter of L-SNEDDS was added to 500 mL of distilled water at 37 ± 0.5 °C under gentle stirring (50 rpm), and the time required to form a clear or slightly bluish emulsion was recorded (Mohite et al., 2024).

Percent transmittance, globule size, polydispersity index (PDI), and zeta potential

To determine percent transmittance (%T), globule size, polydispersity index (PDI), and zeta potential, the formulation was diluted 100-fold with distilled water. Percent transmittance was measured at 638 nm using a UV–Vis spectrophotometer, whereas globule size, PDI, and zeta potential were measured using a Nano Particle Analyzer SZ-100 (HORIBA Ltd., Kyoto, Japan) based on the dynamic light scattering (DLS) principle (Hamdy et al., 2024; Zafar et al., 2022).

Cloud point

The diluted L-SNEDDS (100×) was gradually heated from 25 to 80 °C, and the temperature at which turbidity appeared was recorded. A decrease in transmittance (<90%) confirmed cloud formation (Arshad et al., 2022; Chaudhuri et al., 2022).

Robustness to dilution

L-SNEDDS was diluted 50-, 100-, and 1000-fold in distilled water, 0.1 N HCl, and phosphate buffer (pH 6.8), and the samples were observed after 24 h for phase separation, drug precipitation, and changes in transmittance (Buddhadev et al., 2024; Jahan et al., 2023).

Drug content

A 100 μL volume of L-SNEDDS was diluted to 50 mL with methanol and analyzed using a validated HPLC method. Separation was performed on a Purospher RP-18 column using an isocratic mobile phase of phosphate buffer (pH 4.5) and acetonitrile (35:65) at a flow rate of 1 mL/min, with UV detection at 238 nm (Górniak et al., 2022).

Optimization of solid SNEDDS (S-SNEDDS SIM/EZE)

Solid SNEDDS (S-SNEDDS) containing simvastatin and ezetimibe were prepared using Neusilin US2 as the solid carrier via physical adsorption (Fig. 1). Four formulations were developed by varying the L-SNEDDS: Neusilin US2 ratio (1:1, 1:0.75, 1:0.5, and 1:0.25). The preparation was carried out by gradually adding L-SNEDDS into Neusilin US2 under continuous mixing until a homogeneous, free-flowing powder was obtained. The resulting S-SNEDDS formulations were evaluated for appearance and flow properties. Angle of repose was determined by the fixed funnel method, while Carr's index and Hausner ratio were calculated from bulk and tapped density measurements. The formulation with the most favorable characteristics was selected for further analysis (Bhagwat et al., 2021; Buddhadev et al., 2024).

Physicochemical characterization of S-SNEDDS

Solid-state characterization

Solid-state properties were evaluated using three complementary techniques: Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FT-IR), Differential Scanning Calorimetry (DSC), and Powder X-ray Diffraction (PXRD). FT-IR analysis was performed by dispersing the sample in KBr and compressing it into a pellet, followed by spectral acquisition over the wavenumber range 4000–400 cm−1 at a resolution of 1 cm−1 using an IR Prestige-21 spectrophotometer (Buddhadev et al., 2024; Tashish et al., 2023). For DSC, samples were sealed in aluminum crucibles and heated from 30 to 300 °C at 10 °C/min under a nitrogen flow of 40 mL/min using a Shimadzu DSC-60 Plus instrument. PXRD analysis was conducted by scanning the sample over a 2θ range of 5°–80° at 10°/min using a Rigaku Miniflex 600 diffractometer (Cu-Kα radiation, 40 kV, 15 mA). All analyses were carried out on simvastatin, ezetimibe, Neusilin US2, physical mixtures, and S-SNEDDS (Tashish et al., 2023).

Morphological analysis

Surface morphology was examined using SEM (TM3000, Hitachi). Approximately 1–2 mg of sample was mounted on double-sided carbon tape and analyzed at an accelerating voltage of 5 kV. Observations were conducted for simvastatin, ezetimibe, Neusilin US2, and S-SNEDDS (Dangre et al., 2022).

Globule size, polydispersity index (PDI), and zeta potential

S-SNEDDS was diluted with distilled water (1:100), followed by filtration and centrifugation at 4000 rpm to minimize interference from undispersed Neusilin® US2 carrier particles during dynamic light scattering (DLS) analysis. The resulting supernatant was analyzed to determine globule size, PDI, and zeta potential (Bhagwat et al., 2021).

Drug content

A total of 150 mg of S-SNEDDS was dispersed in 50 mL of methanol and stirred to ensure complete extraction of simvastatin and ezetimibe. The resulting suspension was filtered to remove the insoluble Neusilin US2 carrier, and the drug content in the filtrate was quantified using a validated HPLC method (Górniak et al., 2022).

Solubility study of S-SNEDDS

An excess amount of S-SNEDDS (equivalent to the drug content) was dispersed in distilled water. The mixture was vortexed and then equilibrated using a water bath shaker for 24–48 h. The resulting suspension was filtered through a polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) membrane filter, and the drug concentration in the filtrate was determined using HPLC. The same procedure was applied to a physical mixture of simvastatin and ezetimibe as a control (Schmied et al., 2022).

Accelerated stability study

Accelerated stability studies were conducted by storing the formulations in a climatic chamber maintained at 40 ± 2 °C and 75 ± 5% RH for 3 months. Following storage, the formulations were characterized for globule size, polydispersity index (PDI), zeta potential, drug content, and powder X-ray diffraction (PXRD) to evaluate their physicochemical stability. Differences between the initial (0-month) and stored (3-month) formulations were analyzed using a paired Student's t-test, with statistical significance set at p < 0.05 (Fitria et al., 2021).

In vitro dissolution study

Dissolution studies were performed using a USP type II apparatus in three dissolution media: 0.1 N HCl, acetate buffer pH 4.5, and phosphate buffer pH 6.8, maintained at 37 ± 0.5 °C with a paddle rotation speed of 50 rpm. Samples were withdrawn at predetermined time intervals of 10, 20, 30, 40, 50, 60, and 90 min. The concentrations of simvastatin and ezetimibe in the dissolution samples were quantified using a validated HPLC method. For comparison, dissolution studies were conducted on suspensions of the pure drugs and on a commercially available tablet containing the same active ingredients (Górniak et al., 2022; Nazlı et al., 2021).

In vivo antidyslipidemic study

All animal procedures were approved by the Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Medicine, Universitas Islam Bandung (Approval No. 030/KEPK-Unisba/VI/2025) and conducted in accordance with applicable institutional and national guidelines for the care and use of laboratory animals.

Optimization of Poloxamer 407-induced dyslipidemia model

Prior to the efficacy study, the optimal dose of Poloxamer 407 for dyslipidemia induction was determined by evaluating serum total cholesterol levels 12 h after intraperitoneal administration of Poloxamer 407 at doses ranging from 100 to 1000 mg/kg body weight (Gardouh et al., 2020; Khafagy et al., 2023; Younas et al., 2024).

Antidyslipidemic activity evaluation

For the antidyslipidemic activity study, healthy male Wistar rats were randomly assigned to six groups: normal control, dyslipidemic control, L-SNEDDS, S-SNEDDS, drug suspension, and the reference product, Vytorin® (10 mg ezetimibe/20 mg simvastatin; Merck Sharp & Dohme, USA). The number of animals per group was determined using the Federer formula, resulting in four rats per group. Animals were fasted overnight before the experiment. Dyslipidemia was induced in all groups except the normal control by intraperitoneal injection of the optimized dose of Poloxamer 407. Twelve hours after induction, blood samples were collected via the retro-orbital plexus to confirm the establishment of dyslipidemia through serum lipid analysis. Animals that failed to develop dyslipidemia following Poloxamer 407 administration, as confirmed by serum lipid analysis, were predefined for exclusion; however, no animals met this criterion. Subsequently, all treatment groups received orally equivalent doses of simvastatin and ezetimibe based on the human therapeutic dose (20 mg/10 mg) following standard body surface area conversion to rats. L-SNEDDS, S-SNEDDS, the drug suspension, and the Vytorin® suspension were prepared to provide identical amounts of both drugs and were administered by oral gavage at 2.25 mL/kg body weight at 12 and 36 h post-induction. The drug suspension was prepared in 0.5% (w/v) sodium carboxymethyl cellulose (CMC-Na), whereas Vytorin® tablets were finely pulverized and suspended in distilled water immediately before administration. At 60 h, blood samples were collected again, allowed to clot for 30 min, and centrifuged at 4000 rpm for 10 min to obtain serum. Serum total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) levels were determined using commercial enzymatic diagnostic kits. Data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) (Gardouh et al., 2020; Khafagy et al., 2023; Younas et al., 2024).

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using Shapiro–Wilk and Levene's tests to assess normality and homogeneity of variance, respectively. Normally distributed and homogeneous data were analyzed using one-way ANOVA, whereas normally distributed but heteroscedastic data were analyzed using Welch ANOVA. When significant differences were detected (p < 0.05), appropriate post hoc tests were performed to identify differences among groups.

Results and discussion

Preparation and characterization of L-SNEDDS SIM/EZE

The optimized L-SNEDDS formulation obtained from our previous QbD-based study was successfully reproduced in the present work. The formulation, hereafter referred to as L-SNEDDS SIM/EZE, consisted of Capryol 90 as the oil phase, Cremophor RH40 as the surfactant, and Transcutol HP as the cosurfactant (Priani et al., 2025b). Capryol 90 was selected for its superior ability to simultaneously solubilize simvastatin and ezetimibe, which is essential for achieving high drug loading while maintaining a single isotropic phase (Chauhan et al., 2024). Cremophor RH40 and Transcutol HP were selected not only for their high solubilization capacity toward both drugs but also for their excellent emulsification performance with Capryol 90, enabling rapid self-emulsification and the formation of a nanoemulsion upon aqueous dilution (Buddhadev et al., 2024). The resulting formulation was a clear, homogeneous, yellowish liquid. The physicochemical characteristics of the optimized L-SNEDDS are summarized in Table 1, and all evaluated parameters met the predefined acceptance criteria.

The formulation exhibited rapid self-emulsification, with an emulsification time of 34.33 ± 1.53 s, and produced a highly transparent nanoemulsion upon dilution, as confirmed by a transmittance of 99.3 ± 0.30%. A mean globule size of 24.47 ± 0.55 nm was obtained, indicating successful formation of a nanosized emulsion with a large interfacial surface area, which is conducive to enhanced drug solubilization and gastrointestinal absorption (Park et al., 2022). The PDI value of 0.27 ± 0.03 indicates acceptable globule-size uniformity in oral SNEDDS formulations (Ahmad and Hafeez, 2023). The L-SNEDDS exhibited a moderately negative zeta potential (−22.64 ± 3.43 mV), consistent with values commonly reported for lipid-based SNEDDS stabilized with nonionic surfactants (Chen et al., 2025). In addition, the cloud point was 61.83 ± 1.04 °C, well above physiological temperature, indicating adequate thermal stability of the nonionic surfactant system during oral administration (Mohite et al., 2024). No phase separation or drug precipitation was observed in any dilution or medium after 24 h, and transmittance values remained above 90% across all conditions. These findings confirm that the nanoemulsion maintained its physical integrity under simulated gastrointestinal conditions and indicate a low tendency for drug precipitation upon dilution, which is critical for ensuring consistent drug availability along the gastrointestinal tract (Shrivastava et al., 2022). Drug content analysis yielded values of 101.67 ± 1.88% for simvastatin and 100.11 ± 0.93% for ezetimibe, confirming uniform drug incorporation and the absence of drug degradation during formulation preparation.

Optimization of solid SNEDDS (S-SNEDDS SIM/EZE)

The optimized L-SNEDDS SIM/EZE was converted into a solid form by physical adsorption onto Neusilin US2, a porous magnesium aluminometasilicate with high surface area and oil absorption capacity (2.7–3.4 mL/g), selected for its ability to efficiently immobilize lipid-based systems while retaining rapid re-emulsification upon aqueous contact (Alshadidi et al., 2022; Anas Al Tahan et al., 2025). This approach was preferred over spray drying and melt granulation as it requires neither organic solvents nor thermal processing, minimizing the risk of drug or lipid degradation (Nazlı et al., 2021; Uttreja et al., 2025).

Four L-SNEDDS:Neusilin US2 ratios (1:1, 1:0.75, 1:0.5, and 1:0.25, w/w) were evaluated. As shown in Table 2, FS1, FS2, and FS3 yielded free-flowing powders within the oil absorption capacity range of Neusilin US2, whereas FS4, corresponding to a liquid loading of 4.0 g/g adsorbent, produced a paste-like mass, indicating that the lipid phase exceeded the effective adsorption capacity of the carrier. This finding highlights the importance of matching liquid loading with the adsorption capacity of the carrier, as incomplete adsorption may result in non-uniform distribution of the liquid formulation within the adsorbent and potentially affect dose uniformity. Among the powder formulations, FS3 exhibited the most favorable micromeritic properties (Carr's Index: 17.36 ± 2.73%; Hausner Ratio: 1.21 ± 0.04), indicating good flowability. The relatively poor flowability of FS1 and FS2 may be attributed to excess unloaded Neusilin US2, which retains its native, highly porous, and irregular surface characteristics, thereby increasing interparticulate interactions and cohesiveness. At the FS3 ratio, more complete occupation of the mesoporous matrix by the lipid phase is thought to moderate these surface properties, thereby improving powder flow. Accordingly, FS3 was selected as the optimal S-SNEDDS SIM/EZE for further evaluation (Buddhadev et al., 2024; Yilmaz Usta et al., 2023). The adsorption capacity of the carrier is an important consideration during S-SNEDDS development because excessive liquid loading may result in incomplete adsorption and non-uniform distribution of the liquid formulation within the adsorbent, potentially affecting dose uniformity. Therefore, optimizing the L-SNEDDS: Neusilin US2 ratio is essential to achieve efficient adsorption while maintaining acceptable powder characteristics for further pharmaceutical development.

Solid-state characterization

Solid-state characterization was performed to evaluate the effect of adsorption-based solidification on the optimized S-SNEDDS. Pure simvastatin and ezetimibe were used as reference materials to identify their characteristic solid-state properties, whereas Neusilin US2 and the physical mixture served as comparator samples to distinguish changes associated with the carrier and simple physical mixing from those resulting from the solidification process. FTIR, DSC, and PXRD analyses were subsequently employed to assess the chemical integrity and solid-state properties of the optimized S-SNEDDS.

Neusilin US2 displayed the characteristic Si—O stretching band at ∼1000–1100 cm−1 (Kumar Panda et al., 2024). These characteristic bands remained detectable in both the physical mixture and S-SNEDDS without the appearance of new bands or significant peak shifts. Compared with the physical mixture, S-SNEDDS exhibited markedly attenuated drug-related bands, particularly in the fingerprint region, which is attributed to the adsorption of the liquid SNEDDS within the porous Neusilin US2 matrix, spectral overlap with the carrier, and molecular dispersion of the drugs throughout the porous network rather than the formation of new chemical bonds (Dangre et al., 2022; Galatage et al., 2023).

FTIR spectra of pure components, physical mixture, and S-SNEDDS.

DSC thermograms showed sharp endothermic peaks for pure ezetimibe and simvastatin at approximately 161 °C and 137 °C, respectively, corresponding to their crystalline melting points (Modhave et al., 2020; Tulain et al., 2021). The physical mixture exhibited a single endothermic peak at approximately 118 °C, indicating eutectic formation between the two drugs, consistent with previous reports (Bahetibieke et al., 2022). In contrast, the characteristic melting peaks of simvastatin and ezetimibe were completely absent in the S-SNEDDS thermogram (Fig. 3), indicating that both drugs were no longer present as free crystalline phases in the system. This observation is consistent with the S-SNEDDS preparation process, in which simvastatin and ezetimibe were first solubilized in the lipid phase and subsequently adsorbed into the porous network of Neusilin US2 during solidification. Consequently, both drugs are presumed to exist in a molecularly dispersed or amorphous state within the carrier matrix (Dangre et al., 2022; Shrivastava et al., 2022). In addition, confinement of the drug molecules within the mesoporous structure of Neusilin US2 may further inhibit molecular mobility and crystal growth, thereby reducing the tendency for recrystallization (Bavnhøj et al., 2024). This interpretation was further corroborated by PXRD analysis, which provided complementary evidence of reduced crystallinity in the S-SNEDDS.

FTIR spectra of pure components, physical mixture, and S-SNEDDS.

PXRD analysis was performed to complement the DSC results by directly evaluating changes in the solid-state properties of the formulation (Fig. 4). Pure simvastatin and ezetimibe exhibited characteristic sharp diffraction peaks, confirming their crystalline nature and consistent with previous reports (Sopyan et al., 2024; Tulain et al., 2021). In contrast, Neusilin US2 displayed a broad halo pattern, characteristic of an amorphous mesoporous material (Dangre et al., 2022). The characteristic diffraction peaks of both drugs remained visible in the physical mixture, indicating that simple blending did not alter their crystalline state. However, these peaks disappeared completely in the S-SNEDDS diffractogram, which exhibited a diffuse halo pattern, indicating a marked reduction in drug crystallinity after solidification. This observation is consistent with the DSC results and suggests that both drugs were molecularly dispersed and/or present in an amorphous state within the lipid formulation adsorbed into the porous network of Neusilin US2 (Dangre et al., 2022; Galatage et al., 2023). Although the disappearance of diffraction peaks may also be influenced by confinement of the drugs within the mesoporous carrier, the combined DSC and PXRD findings indicate the absence of detectable crystalline drug domains in the S-SNEDDS.

Morphological analysis

The surface morphology of simvastatin, ezetimibe, Neusilin US2, and S-SNEDDS was examined by scanning electron microscopy (SEM) (Fig. 5). Pure simvastatin and ezetimibe exhibited distinct crystalline morphologies, whereas Neusilin US2 consisted of spherical porous particles, consistent with its mesoporous structure. In contrast, S-SNEDDS appeared as irregular agglomerates with a rough surface, while the characteristic crystalline morphology of both drugs was no longer observed. The surface pores of Neusilin US2 became less distinct after solidification, suggesting that the liquid SNEDDS was successfully adsorbed into and onto the porous carrier. These observations are consistent with the FTIR, DSC, and PXRD results, confirming successful solidification of L-SNEDDS without detectable crystalline drug particles (Buddhadev et al., 2024; Kazi et al., 2020).

Globule size, polydispersity index (PDI), and zeta potential

The globule size, polydispersity index (PDI), and zeta potential of S-SNEDDS were evaluated after reconstitution. Compared with L-SNEDDS, the globule size increased slightly from 24.47 ± 0.55 nm to 35.51 ± 0.32 nm, likely due to adsorption of the liquid formulation within the mesoporous Neusilin® US2 matrix and the presence of the water-insoluble carrier, which may slightly delay formulation release during redispersion (Chen et al., 2025; Shahba et al., 2021). Despite this slight increase, the PDI remained comparable between L-SNEDDS (0.27 ± 0.03) and S-SNEDDS (0.25 ± 0.01), indicating that adsorption-based solidification did not adversely affect the uniformity of the reconstituted nanoemulsion. It should be noted that particle size analysis was performed on the supernatant after filtration and centrifugation to minimize interference from undispersed Neusilin® US2 particles during DLS measurement. Although this sample preparation improved measurement reliability, it may have reduced the contribution of larger aggregates and should therefore be considered when interpreting the measured globule size and PDI. Nevertheless, both formulations remained within the nanometer size range (<100 nm), indicating that the self-nanoemulsifying performance was preserved following solidification.

The zeta potential changed from −22.64 ± 3.43 mV for L-SNEDDS to −16.63 ± 1.63 mV after adsorption-based solidification. This slight reduction in the magnitude of the negative surface charge may reflect changes in the interfacial characteristics of the reconstituted droplets arising from interactions between the lipid formulation and the mesoporous Neusilin® US2 carrier during adsorption and subsequent reconstitution (Chen et al., 2025). However, because both formulations are stored as anhydrous preconcentrates and form nanoemulsions only after contact with gastrointestinal fluids, the zeta potential of the reconstituted droplets should not be interpreted as a direct indicator of storage stability (Kamble and Shaikh, 2022).

Drug content

The S-SNEDDS exhibited drug content values of 99.37 ± 0.40% for simvastatin and 99.41 ± 2.03% for ezetimibe, indicating uniform drug distribution within the formulation. Since no pharmacopeial acceptance criteria are currently available for SNEDDS formulations, the measured drug contents were evaluated relative to the theoretical drug content. The values, which were close to 100%, suggest that the solidification process maintained accurate drug loading without significant drug loss while also achieving satisfactory formulation homogeneity following adsorption onto Neusilin US2 (Galatage et al., 2025).

Solubility study of S-SNEDDS

Pure simvastatin and ezetimibe exhibited low aqueous solubilities of 5.37 ± 1.05 and 1.90 ± 0.14 μg/mL, respectively. Incorporation into S-SNEDDS markedly increased the apparent solubility of simvastatin and ezetimibe to 3328.54 ± 217.59 and 1176.84 ± 73.01 μg/mL, respectively. This enhancement is primarily attributed to the lipid-based formulation's ability to maintain both drugs in a solubilized state within the oil–surfactant matrix prior to spontaneous nanoemulsion formation upon aqueous dilution. Furthermore, the reduced crystallinity and molecular dispersion of the drugs within the solidified formulation, as evidenced by DSC and PXRD analyses, may increase apparent solubility by minimizing crystalline barriers to dissolution. Consequently, the improved apparent solubility may facilitate drug dissolution in the gastrointestinal tract and enhance oral absorption (Kamble and Shaikh, 2022).

Accelerated stability study

The thermodynamic stability of L-SNEDDS SIM/EZE has been previously established and reported (Priani et al., 2025b). Therefore, the accelerated stability evaluation in the present study focused on the S-SNEDDS formulation, the newly developed solid system. Accelerated stability of S-SNEDDS was evaluated after storage at 40 ± 2 °C/75 ± 5% RH for 3 months. No noticeable changes in appearance were observed throughout storage. Following reconstitution, the globule size, PDI, and zeta potential remained comparable before and after storage, with values of 35.51 ± 0.32 and 36.48 ± 4.26 nm, 0.25 ± 0.01 and 0.31 ± 0.02, and −16.63 ± 1.63 and −15.53 ± 0.64 mV, respectively. Likewise, ezetimibe content remained essentially unchanged (99.41 ± 2.03% vs. 98.37 ± 0.77%), indicating that the physicochemical characteristics of S-SNEDDS were well preserved during accelerated storage. PXRD patterns obtained after accelerated storage (Fig. 6) remained comparable to those of the freshly prepared S-SNEDDS, with no appearance of new crystalline reflections, indicating that no significant changes in the crystalline state of simvastatin and ezetimibe occurred during storage (Kamble and Shaikh, 2022).

In vitro dissolution study

As simvastatin and ezetimibe require different compendial dissolution conditions, dissolution testing was performed in three physiologically relevant media (0.1 N HCl, acetate buffer pH 4.5, and phosphate buffer pH 6.8) to simultaneously evaluate the combined formulation (Nazlı et al., 2021). In all media, both L-SNEDDS and S-SNEDDS exhibited markedly higher dissolution than the drug suspension, consistent with the enhanced apparent solubility previously observed for both drugs following SNEDDS formulation (Fig. 7). Upon aqueous dispersion, spontaneous nanoemulsion formation generated nanosized globules with a large interfacial surface area, facilitating rapid drug release while maintaining the drugs in a solubilized state (Buya et al., 2021; Park et al., 2022). These findings were further supported by quantitative dissolution parameters (Table 3). Across all dissolution media, both SNEDDS formulations consistently exhibited substantially higher AUC₀–₉₀, dissolution efficiency (DE₀–₉₀), and Q30 values than the drug suspension, confirming their superior dissolution performance.

Compared with L-SNEDDS, S-SNEDDS exhibited slightly lower dissolution, particularly in 0.1 N HCl. This trend was reflected by lower AUC₀–₉₀ and DE₀–₉₀ values for S-SNEDDS than for L-SNEDDS in the acidic medium. This reduction is likely associated with the additional desorption step required to release the lipid formulation from the mesoporous Neusilin US2 matrix prior to nanoemulsion formation (Bhagwat et al., 2021). Furthermore, interactions between the adsorbed lipid formulation and the silanol-rich surface of Neusilin US2 may slightly retard formulation release, an effect that appears more pronounced under acidic conditions (Kumar Panda et al., 2024; Williams et al., 2014). Nevertheless, the dissolution profiles of L-SNEDDS and S-SNEDDS were comparable in acetate buffer pH 4.5 and phosphate buffer pH 6.8, indicating that solidification largely preserved the dissolution-enhancing properties of the SNEDDS system. A slight decline in dissolved ezetimibe after reaching the maximum concentration was observed in 0.1 N HCl, which may be associated with transient supersaturation followed by partial drug precipitation after aqueous dispersion, although precipitation kinetics were not specifically investigated in this study (Lestari and Sukmawati, 2025; Park et al., 2020; Priani et al., 2025c). Accordingly, this interpretation remains hypothetical and should be confirmed by dedicated precipitation studies. Although dissolution was evaluated using three physiologically relevant compendial media, the inclusion of biorelevant media in future studies may provide additional insight into formulation behavior under gastrointestinal conditions.

Overall, S-SNEDDS retained a substantial dissolution advantage over the drug suspension while providing the practical benefits of a solid dosage form. Although these findings suggest improved oral drug availability, dissolution testing alone cannot predict therapeutic efficacy; therefore, the formulations were further evaluated for antidyslipidemic activity.

In vivo antidyslipidemic activity

The antidyslipidemic activity of the developed formulations was evaluated using an acute Poloxamer 407 (P407)-induced dyslipidemia model. This model has been widely employed for the preclinical evaluation of lipid-lowering agents and drug delivery systems because it rapidly and reproducibly induces marked hyperlipidemia by inhibiting lipoprotein lipase, resulting in pronounced elevations in circulating cholesterol and triglycerides (Hernandez-Baixauli et al., 2023; Younas et al., 2024). The marked dyslipidemia produced by this model makes it particularly suitable for evaluating combination lipid-lowering formulations, as combination therapy is clinically recommended for patients requiring intensive LDL-C reduction or those who fail to achieve lipid targets with statin monotherapy (Grundy et al., 2019). Because the P407 doses used to induce acute dyslipidemia vary considerably among published studies, an initial dose-optimization study was conducted to determine an appropriate induction dose for the subsequent efficacy evaluation.

Optimization of Poloxamer 407-induced dyslipidemia model

Preliminary dose optimization showed that intraperitoneal administration of 300 mg/kg Poloxamer 407 (P407) produced marked hypercholesterolemia (320.90 ± 3.91 mg/dL), comparable to that achieved with 500 mg/kg (318.10 ± 10.53 mg/dL) and 1000 mg/kg (316.33 ± 18.50 mg/dL). Therefore, 300 mg/kg was selected for the subsequent efficacy study to establish a robust dyslipidemia model while avoiding unnecessary administration of higher doses. This dose is also consistent with previous studies employing the P407-induced acute dyslipidemia model (Sobhy et al., 2025).

Antidyslipidemic efficacy of L-SNEDDS and S-SNEDDS

The optimized P407-induced dyslipidemia model was subsequently used to evaluate the antidyslipidemic efficacy of the developed formulations. The study included a normal control group (healthy rats without P407 induction), a dyslipidemic control group (P407-induced untreated rats), a drug suspension, L-SNEDDS, S-SNEDDS, and a commercial reference tablet (Vytorin®). The drug suspension served as a conventional formulation control to evaluate the therapeutic contribution of the SNEDDS delivery system, whereas the commercial reference tablet (Vytorin®) was included as a clinically relevant comparator. Twelve hours after P407 administration, all induced animals exhibited serum total cholesterol concentrations exceeding 200 mg/dL, confirming successful induction of dyslipidemia. At the end of treatment (T60), the dyslipidemic control showed significantly higher total cholesterol (TC), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), and triglyceride (TG) levels than the normal control (p < 0.05), whereas no significant difference was observed in HDL-C. These findings are consistent with the mechanism of P407-induced dyslipidemia, in which inhibition of lipoprotein lipase impairs the clearance of triglyceride-rich lipoproteins, resulting in rapid accumulation of circulating triglycerides and cholesterol (Hernandez-Baixauli et al., 2023; Peng et al., 2022).

Both L-SNEDDS and S-SNEDDS significantly reduced TC, LDL-C, and TG compared with the dyslipidemic control (p < 0.01) and achieved significantly greater reductions in these lipid parameters than the drug suspension (Table 4, Fig. 8). Although the mean TC and LDL-C values were lower in both SNEDDS groups than in the reference product group, these differences were not statistically significant (p > 0.05) and should therefore be interpreted with caution. Likewise, no statistically significant differences were observed between L-SNEDDS and S-SNEDDS for any lipid parameter. These findings suggest that the solidification process was not associated with a measurable reduction in therapeutic efficacy under the present experimental conditions. The absence of statistically significant differences between L-SNEDDS and S-SNEDDS, despite the modestly lower in vitro dissolution of S-SNEDDS, suggests that the reduction in dissolution was insufficient to produce a measurable difference in pharmacological response under the present experimental conditions. This finding may also reflect the more dynamic gastrointestinal environment in vivo, where physiological factors such as bile secretion and intestinal motility could partially offset the additional desorption step required by S-SNEDDS (Bhagwat et al., 2021; Gidwani et al., 2024).

The enhanced antidyslipidemic activity of both SNEDDS formulations is consistent with the previously demonstrated improvements in apparent solubility and dissolution, suggesting that improved drug solubilization may have contributed to the observed therapeutic efficacy. Following oral administration, the formulations spontaneously generated nanosized emulsions that increase the interfacial surface area and maintain the drugs in a solubilized state, which may facilitate drug absorption. In addition, lipid-based formulations have been reported to facilitate intestinal drug uptake and may promote lymphatic transport of the lipophilic drug fraction, potentially reducing the impact of first-pass metabolism, although these mechanisms were not directly investigated in the present study. Furthermore, the improved dissolution of ezetimibe may increase the amount of dissolved drug available to inhibit the intestinal NPC1L1 transporter, thereby contributing to the observed lipid-lowering effect (Buya et al., 2020; Chauhan et al., 2024). Overall, these findings suggest that conversion of L-SNEDDS into a solid dosage form was not associated with a measurable reduction in antidyslipidemic efficacy under the present experimental conditions despite a slight reduction in in vitro dissolution after solidification. Together with its improved physicochemical stability and pharmaceutical handling characteristics, S-SNEDDS may represent a promising solid lipid-based delivery system for the co-delivery of simvastatin and ezetimibe.

Nevertheless, the present findings were obtained using an acute Poloxamer 407-induced dyslipidemia model, and their applicability to chronic dyslipidemia requires further investigation. In addition, although the enhanced pharmaceutical performance was associated with improved antidyslipidemic efficacy, pharmacokinetic studies are warranted to further elucidate the relationship between the formulations' in vitro and in vivo performance. The sample size (n = 4 per group), determined using the Federer formula, was adequate for an exploratory evaluation of efficacy but may have limited the ability to detect modest between-group differences. Therefore, the absence of statistically significant differences between treatment groups should not be interpreted as evidence of equivalent efficacy. Further studies incorporating chronic efficacy assessment, larger cohorts, and comprehensive toxicological evaluation will help strengthen the translational potential of the proposed S-SNEDDS.

Conclusions

The optimized simvastatin–ezetimibe L-SNEDDS was successfully converted into a solid formulation by adsorption onto Neusilin® US2 while retaining its self-nanoemulsifying properties upon reconstitution. Adsorption-based solidification preserved the critical physicochemical characteristics of the optimized liquid formulation, maintained both drugs in a predominantly amorphous or molecularly dispersed state, and substantially improved their apparent solubility and dissolution compared with the drug suspension. Although S-SNEDDS exhibited a modest reduction in dissolution relative to L-SNEDDS, no statistically significant differences in antidyslipidemic efficacy were observed between the liquid and solid formulations under the present experimental conditions. These findings demonstrate that adsorption-based solidification successfully preserved the pharmaceutical performance of the optimized L-SNEDDS while maintaining comparable antidyslipidemic efficacy under the present experimental conditions. Further pharmacokinetic, long-term efficacy, and toxicological studies are warranted to support the translational development of S-SNEDDS as an oral lipid-based delivery system for poorly water-soluble combination drugs.
