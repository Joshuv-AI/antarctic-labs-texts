Compound Selection Decisions: An Almost SURE Approach
Jiafeng Chen and Lihua Lei and Timothy Sudijono and Liyang Sun and Tian Xie

Abstract
. This paper proposes methods for producing compound selection decisions in a Gaussian sequence model. Given unknown, fixed parameters μ 1 : n \mu_{1:n} and known σ 1 : n \sigma_{1:n} with observations Y i ∼ N ​ ( μ i , σ i 2 ) Y_{i}\sim\textsf{N}(\mu_{i},\sigma_{i}^{2}) , the decision maker would like to select a subset of indices S S so as to maximize utility 1 n ​ ∑ i ∈ S ( μ i − K i ) \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i\in S}(\mu_{i}-K_{i}) , for known costs K i K_{i} . Inspired by Stein’s unbiased risk estimate (SURE), we introduce an almost unbiased estimator, called ASSURE , for the expected utility of a proposed decision rule. ASSURE allows a user to choose a welfare-maximizing rule from a pre-specified class by optimizing the estimated welfare, thereby producing selection decisions that borrow strength across noisy estimates. We show that ASSURE produces decision rules that are asymptotically no worse than the optimal but infeasible decision rule in the pre-specified class. We apply ASSURE to the selection of Census tracts for economic opportunity, the identification of discriminating firms, and the analysis of p p -value decision procedures in A/B testing.

2.2.
Intuition for

Inspecting (
2.1

: For differentiable

\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim\textsf{N}(\mu,\sigma^{2})}[(Y-\mu)F(Y)]=\sigma^{2}\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim\textsf{N}(\mu,\sigma^{2})}[f(Y)].

We can rearrange to obtain

\mathbb{E}[(Y-K)F(Y)-\sigma^{2}f(Y)]=(\mu-K)\mathbb{E}[F(Y)].

Thus,
(Y-K)F(Y)-\sigma^{2}f(Y)

\Phi\left({\frac{\mu-\delta}{\sigma}}\right)=\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{1}(Y>\delta)].

Having reduced the problem in this way, by rescaling if necessary, we may assume

\displaystyle 0=\hat{W}^{\prime}(\hat{\beta})

\displaystyle\approx\hat{W}^{\prime}(\beta^{*})+\hat{W}^{\prime\prime}(\beta^{*})(\hat{\beta}-\beta^{*}).

\displaystyle=\hat{W}^{\prime}(\beta^{*})-W^{\prime}(\beta^{*})+\hat{W}^{\prime\prime}(\beta^{*})(\hat{\beta}-\beta^{*}).

Finally, we consider a leave-one-out stability argument to control

4.1.
Other observation distributions

The approach—finding an estimator for
W
and optimizing this estimator over
decisions—can be extended to settings where the observation distribution is
non-Gaussian. For certain exponential families like the Poisson distribution and
exponential distribution, one can find an exactly unbiased estimate for the welfare
W
.
The intuition for these cases is to exploit a formula akin to
Tweedie’s formula. We focus on the Poisson setting as it
frequently appears in empirical Bayes and compound decisions.

22

In this setting, let
\mu_{i}\geq 0
and

. Again, associated with

K_{i}
and auxiliary information

23

For

\widehat{W}_{\mathsf{Poi}}(\beta):=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(Y_{i}\mathbf{1}\left\{Y_{i}\geq\delta(Z_{i};\beta)+1\right\}-K_{i}\mathbf{1}\left\{Y_{i}\geq\delta(Z_{i};\beta)\right\}\right)

.

Proposition 2
.

The Poisson

estimator

is unbiased for the welfare

(Regret upper bounds for the Poisson Case)
.

Let

We prove the upper bound by appealing to the similar empirical process theory arguments as

further relaxed by. Mirroring the argument of the Gaussian case, we have a matching lower

(Matching Regret Lower Bounds)

Fix costs

Remark 2
.

Consider the case

4.2.
Complex Decisions

We discuss how to extend the
ASSURE
framework to accommodate
complex, non-separable decision rules
\delta(\beta,Z_{1:n},Y_{-i})
which may depend on
the outcomes and auxiliary information of other units. For example, an analyst might
consider making decisions using empirical Bayes posterior mean estimates

\mathbb{E}_{\hat{G}}[\mu_{i}\mid\sigma_{i},Y_{i}]
and flexible machine-learning models

\hat{f}(X_{i})
that estimate the regression
\mathbb{E}[Y_{i}\mid X_{i}]
. In both of these cases,
it would be desirable to estimate
\hat{G}
and model
\hat{f}
using the data
itself.

By a similar argument to
Proposition

1
, one can create a near unbiased
estimator of the welfare of this decision rule using a leave-one-out cross fitting
construction. Redefine the
ASSURE
summand (
2.5
)
with the decision rule
\delta(\beta,Z_{1:n},Y_{-i})
, so that

w_{h}(Y_{i};\beta,Z_{i},Y_{-i}):=(Y_{i}-K_{i})\operatorname{Csinc}\left({\frac{Y_{i}-\delta(\beta,Z_{1:n},Y_{-i})}{\sigma_{i}h}}\right)-\frac{\sigma_{i}}{h}\sinc\left({\frac{Y_{i}-\delta(\beta,Z_{1:n},Y_{-i})}{\sigma_{i}h}}\right).

Then,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}w_{h}(Y_{i};\beta,Z_{i},Y_{-i})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{E}\left[w_{h}(Y_{i};\beta,Z_{i},Y_{-i})\ |\ Y_{-i}\right]\right]

\displaystyle\approx\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mu_{i}\mathbb{E}\left[\Phi\left(\mu_{i}-\delta(\beta,Z_{1:n},Y_{-i})\right)\right],

which can be interpreted as the average welfare of using the non-separable decision rule.

Proposition

1
shows that the bias in the above approximation is small.

Given this observation, a simple construction is the following. Let
m_{\hat{G}_{1}}^{(-i)}(y,\sigma)
be a posterior mean function obtained by applying an empirical
Bayes method on the data
(Y_{-i},\sigma_{-i})
. Let
m_{\hat{G}_{2}}^{(-i)}(y,\sigma)

denote the same for a different empirical Bayes method. Finally, let
\widehat{f}^{(-i)}

denote a machine learning model trained using the data
(Y_{-i},X_{-i})
. Consider the
class of decision rules which selects a unit whenever the ensemble prediction of these
models is greater than the implementation cost:

(4.4)

b_{1}m_{\hat{G}_{1}}^{(-i)}(Y_{i},\sigma_{i})+b_{2}m_{\hat{G}_{2}}^{(-i)}(Y_{i},\sigma_{i})+b_{3}\widehat{f}^{(-i)}(X_{i})\geq K_{i}

where the parameters
\beta=(b_{1},b_{2},b_{3})
are restricted to be in the unit simplex.
Extensions to multiple empirical Bayes posterior mean models or machine learning models is
straightforward. By the monotonicity of the posterior mean function, the left hand side of
(
4.4
) is increasing in
Y_{i}
: the decision is equivalent to
Y_{i}\geq\delta(\beta,Z_{1:n},Y_{-i})
for some function
\delta
. As a result,
ASSURE
can be used to tune the parameter
\beta
. Under mild stability
assumptions and for large enough samples,
ASSURE
is expected to
perform no worse than the constituent models since the decision class nests each model
individually. This ensemble class thus immediately enables
ASSURE

to improve on any fixed empirical Bayes decision method and illustrates the utility of the
framework. We defer a detailed theoretical analysis of the ensembling method for future
work.

\displaystyle\norm{\norm{\bb{G}_n}_{\cl{F}}^*}_{\psi_{2}}\lesssim E^{*}\norm{\bb{G}_n}_{\mathcal{F}}+n^{-1/2}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\norm{\sup_{f \in\cl{F}} |f(Z_i) - \E[f(Z_i)] |}_{\psi_{2}}^{2}\right)^{1/2}

(B.9)

\displaystyle\leq E^{*}\norm{\bb{G}_n}_{\mathcal{F}}+n^{-1/2}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\norm{F(Z_i) + \E[F(Z_i)]}_{\psi_{2}}^{2}\right)^{1/2}

Where we used the fact that if
|f|\leq|g|
then
\norm{|f(X)|}_{\psi_{2}}\leq\norm{|g(X)|}_{\psi_{2}}
for any random variable. Using the triangle inequality for the Orlicz norm and the fact that
\norm{X}_{\psi_{2}}\geq\mathbb{E}|X|
and
\norm{C}_{\psi_{2}}\leq C/\sqrt{\ln 2}
for any constant
C,
we can bound

\displaystyle\norm{F(Z_i) + \E[F(Z_i)]}_{\psi_{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\norm{F(Z_i)}_{\psi_{2}}+\norm{\E[F(Z_i)]}_{\psi_{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\norm{F(Z_i)}_{\psi_{2}}+\mathbb{E}[F(Z_{i})]/\sqrt{\ln 2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\norm{F(Z_i)}_{\psi_{2}}.

This gives a final bound by a constant multiple of

E^{*}\norm{\bb{G}_n}_{\mathcal{F}}+n^{-1/2}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\norm{F(Z_i)}_{\psi_{2}}^{2}\right)^{1/2},

from which the desired claim holds.
∎

Lemma B.3
.

For any constant random variable
C
,
\norm{C}_{\psi_{2}}\leq C/\sqrt{\ln 2}
. For any random variable
Y,\norm{X}_{\psi_{2}}\geq\mathbb{E}|X|
.

Proof.

Recall that for any random variable
X
,

\norm{X}_{\psi_{2}}=\inf\left\{c:\mathbb{E}\psi_{2}\left(\frac{|X|}{c}\right)\leq 1\right\}.

For the first claim, taking

∎

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbb{E}_{\textsf{Rad}_{p}^{\otimes n}}\left[\operatorname{\mathsf{Regret}}_{n}(a_{1:n})\right]-\mathbb{E}_{\textsf{Rad}_{p}^{\otimes n}}\left[\operatorname{\mathsf{Regret}}_{n}(a_{1:n})\mathbf{1}(A^{c})\right]}{\mathbb{P}_{\textsf{Rad}_{p}^{\otimes n}}(A)}

(B.53)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\mathbb{E}_{\textsf{Rad}_{p}^{\otimes n}}\left[\operatorname{\mathsf{Regret}}_{n}(a_{1:n})\right]-\mathbb{P}_{\textsf{Rad}_{p}^{\otimes n}}(A^{c})}{1-\mathbb{P}_{\textsf{Rad}_{p}^{\otimes n}}(A^{c})}.

It is easy to see that
\mathbb{P}_{\textsf{Rad}_{p}^{\otimes n}}(A^{c})
is exponentially small in
n
for all
p
in a small neighborhood of
\frac{1}{2}.
We will focus on analyzing the term
\mathbb{E}_{\textsf{Rad}_{p}^{\otimes n}}\left[\operatorname{\mathsf{Regret}}_{n}(a_{1:n})\right].
We have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\textsf{Rad}_{p}^{\otimes n}}\left[\operatorname{\mathsf{Regret}}_{n}(a_{1:n})\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{G^{\otimes n}}\left\{\sup_{\beta}W(\beta)-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mu_{i}\mathbb{E}\left[a_{i}(Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n})\right]\right\}

\displaystyle\geq\sup_{\beta}\mathbb{E}_{G^{\otimes n}}W(\beta)-\mathbb{E}_{G^{\otimes n}}\left[\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mu_{i}\mathbb{E}\left[a_{i}(Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n})\right]\right]

Since
W(\beta)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mu_{i}\mathbb{P}(Y_{i}\geq\beta)
and
\mu_{i}
are independent, observe that

\sup_{\beta}\mathbb{E}_{G}W(\beta)=\mathbb{E}_{G}\mathbb{E}_{X}[\mu\mathbf{1}\left\{\mathbb{E}[\mu\mid Y]>0\right\}],

which follows from monotonicity of the posterior expectation in
Y.
This is the
welfare of the Bayes-optimal decision (see e.g.
7
). Therefore, we
have lower bounded
\sup_{\mu_{1:n}\in\Theta}\operatorname{\mathsf{Regret}}_{n}(a_{1:n})
by the Bayes regret
of any prior
\textsf{Rad}_{p}^{\otimes n}
up to some additive and multiplicative factors
which are exponentially small in
n
.

We will finish the proof using Le Cam’s two point argument. By Lemma
E.1
we may restrict to decision rules which are monotone:
a_{i}(Y)=\mathbf{1}\left\{Y_{i}>\delta_{i}(Y_{(-i)})\right\}
. Let
m_{G}(y)=\mathbb{E}_{G}[\mu\mid y]
be
the posterior mean and
a_{G}^{*}(y)=\mathbf{1}\left\{m_{G}(y)>0\right\}
be the Bayes optimal
decision for a prior
G.
We can equivalently write the Bayes regret as

\displaystyle R(G,a_{1:n})

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}_{G}\left[\mu_{i}\left(a_{G}^{*}(Y_{i})-a_{i}(Y_{1:n})\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}_{G}\left[m_{G}(Y_{i})\left(a_{G}^{*}(Y_{i})-a_{i}(Y_{1:n})\right)\right].

We claim that the Bayes regret can also be written as a weighted classification loss

(B.54)

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}_{G}\left[\left|m_{G}(Y_{i})\right|\mathbf{1}\left\{a_{G}^{*}(Y_{i})\neq a_{i}(Y_{1:n})\right\}\right].

To see this, consider the case where
m_{G}(Y_{i})>0
so that
a_{G}^{*}(Y_{i})=1
. Then

\displaystyle m_{G}(Y_{i})\left(a_{G}^{*}(Y_{i})-a_{i}(Y_{1:n})\right)

\displaystyle=|m_{G}(Y_{i})|\left(1-a_{i}(Y_{1:n})\right)

\displaystyle=\left|m_{G}(Y_{i})\right|\mathbf{1}\left\{a_{G}^{*}(Y_{i})\neq a_{i}(Y_{1:n})\right\}.

In the other case where
m_{G}(Y_{i})<0
so that
a_{G}^{*}(Y_{i})=0
, then

\displaystyle m_{G}(Y_{i})\left(a_{G}^{*}(Y_{i})-a_{i}(Y_{1:n})\right)

\displaystyle=-m_{G}(Y_{i})a_{i}(Y_{1:n})

\displaystyle=\left|m_{G}(Y_{i})\right|a_{i}(Y_{1:n})

\displaystyle=\left|m_{G}(Y_{i})\right|\mathbf{1}\left\{a_{G}^{*}(Y_{i})\neq a_{i}(Y_{1:n})\right\}.

Next, consider two priors
G_{0}:=\textsf{Rad}^{\otimes n}_{1/2-h/\sqrt{n}}
and
G_{1}:=\textsf{Rad}^{\otimes n}_{1/2+h/\sqrt{n}}
for
h
to be chosen later. Let
c^{*}_{G_{i}}
be the zero of the posterior mean
m_{G_{i}}(y)
for
i=0,1.
By monotonicity of the decision rule
a_{i}(Y_{1:n})
, observe that

(B.55)

\mathbf{1}\left\{a_{G_{0}}^{*}(Y_{i})\neq a_{i}(Y_{1:n})\right\}+\mathbf{1}\left\{a_{G_{1}}^{*}(Y_{i})\neq a_{i}(Y_{1:n})\right\}\geq\mathbf{1}\left\{c^{*}_{G_{0}}<Y_{i}<c^{*}_{G_{1}}\right\}.

Next, define
w(y)=\min\left(\left|m_{G_{0}}(y)\right|,\left|m_{G_{1}}(y)\right|\right)
and
q(\mathbf{y})=\min(p^{\otimes n}_{G_{0}}(\mathbf{y}),p^{\otimes n}_{G_{1}}(\mathbf{y}))
where
p^{\otimes n}_{G_{i}}(\mathbf{y})
is the joint density of the data
Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n}
under the prior
G_{i}
. Let
p_{G_{i}}(y)
be the marginal density of a single
y
under prior
G_{i}
. Finally, let
p^{(-j)}_{G_{i}}(\mathbf{y})
be the joint density of the data
Y_{1},\dots,Y_{j-1},Y_{j+1},\dots,Y_{n}
, under
G_{i}
. Then,

\displaystyle R(G_{0},\delta)+R(G_{1},\delta)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}_{G_{0}}\left[\left|m_{G_{0}}(Y_{i})\right|\mathbf{1}\left\{a_{G_{0}}^{*}(Y_{i})\neq a_{i}(Y_{1:n})\right\}\right]

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}_{G_{1}}\left[\left|m_{G_{1}}(Y_{i})\right|\mathbf{1}\left\{a_{G_{1}}^{*}(Y_{i})\neq a_{i}(Y_{1:n})\right\}\right]

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}w(y_{i})\mathbf{1}\left\{c^{*}_{G_{0}}<y_{i}<c^{*}_{G_{1}}\right\}q(\mathbf{y})dy_{1}\dots dy_{n}.

Inspecting the integral, first notice that

q(\mathbf{y})\geq\min\left(p_{G_{0}}(y_{i}),p_{G_{1}}(y_{i})\right)\min\left(\prod_{j\neq i}p_{G_{0}}(y_{j}),\prod_{j\neq i}p_{G_{1}}(y_{j})\right).

Thus,

(B.56)

R(G_{0},\delta)+R(G_{1},\delta)\geq\alpha_{n-1}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{c^{*}_{G_{0}}}^{c^{*}_{G_{1}}}w(y_{i})\min\left(p_{G_{0}}(y_{i}),p_{G_{1}}(y_{i})\right)dy_{i}

where

\alpha_{n-1}:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n-1}}\min\left(p_{G_{0}}^{(-i)}(\mathbf{y}_{-i}),p_{G_{1}}^{(-i)}(\mathbf{y}_{-i})\right)d\mathbf{y}_{-i}.

Notice that
\alpha_{n-1}=1-\textsf{TV}(p_{G_{0}}^{(-i)},p_{G_{1}}^{(-i)})\geq 1-\sqrt{\frac{n-1}{2}\textsf{KLD}(p_{G_{0}},p_{G_{1}})}
by Pinsker’s and tensorization of KL divergence. By the data processing inequality for divergences,

\textsf{KLD}(p_{G_{0}},p_{G_{1}})\leq\textsf{KLD}(G_{0},G_{1})=\frac{2h}{\sqrt{n}}\ln\frac{\frac{1}{2}+\frac{h}{\sqrt{n}}}{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{h}{\sqrt{n}}}.

The last equality follows from the formula for the Bernoulli KL divergence. Then for large enough
n
,
\textsf{KLD}(p_{G_{0}},p_{G_{1}})\leq 16h^{2}/n
and so

\alpha_{n-1}\geq 1-h\sqrt{\frac{8(n-1)}{n}}.

Taking
h=1/4
we have
\alpha_{n-1}\geq\alpha:=1-\sqrt{2}/2.
Next, observe that the posterior distribution
m_{\textsf{Rad}_{p}}(y)
is given by the explicit formula

\frac{pe^{2y}-(1-p)}{pe^{2y}+(1-p)}

with
c^{*}_{\textsf{Rad}_{p}}=\frac{1}{2}\ln\frac{1-p}{p}.
Thus,

c^{*}_{G_{0}}=-c^{*}_{G_{1}}=\frac{1}{2}\ln\left(1+\frac{2\frac{h}{\sqrt{n}}}{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{h}{\sqrt{n}}}\right).

Using the inequality
\ln(1+x)\geq x/2
for small
x
, for
n
large enough we find that

c^{*}_{G_{0}}\geq\frac{1}{2}\ln\left(1+\frac{8h}{\sqrt{n}}\right)\geq\frac{2}{\sqrt{n}}.

Furthermore, it is not difficult to see that on
[c^{*}_{G_{1}},c^{*}_{G_{0}}]
, for
n
large enough, we have

w(y)\geq\frac{1}{2}|c_{G_{0}}^{*}-y|.

This follows by noting
m_{\textsf{Rad}_{p}}(y)\geq\frac{1}{2}(y-c^{*}_{\textsf{Rad}_{p}})
on
[c^{*}_{\textsf{Rad}_{p}},c^{*}_{\textsf{Rad}_{p}}+\epsilon]
and
m_{\textsf{Rad}_{p}}(y)\leq\frac{1}{2}(y-c^{*}_{\textsf{Rad}_{p}})
on
[c^{*}_{\textsf{Rad}_{p}}-\epsilon,c^{*}_{\textsf{Rad}_{p}}]
, for some small
\epsilon
. Lastly,
\min\left(p_{G_{0}}(y_{i}),p_{G_{1}}(y_{i})\right)
is bounded above by an absolute constant
c
on the interval
[c^{*}_{G_{1}},c^{*}_{G_{0}}]
. Combining these items together, we have

\displaystyle R(G_{0},\delta)+R(G_{1},\delta)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\alpha c}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{-c^{*}_{G_{0}}}^{c^{*}_{G_{0}}}w(y_{i})dy_{i}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\alpha c}{2}\int_{-c^{*}_{G_{0}}}^{c^{*}_{G_{0}}}|c_{G_{0}}^{*}-y|dy

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\alpha c}{2}\frac{c^{*2}_{G_{0}}}{4}=\Omega(n^{-1}).

Recall from Eq. (
B.53
) that

\sup_{\mu_{1:n}\in\Theta}\operatorname{\mathsf{Regret}}_{n}(a_{1:n})\geq\max\left(\frac{R(G_{0},\delta)-\mathbb{P}_{G_{0}}(A^{c})}{1-\mathbb{P}_{G_{0}}(A^{c})},\frac{R(G_{1},\delta)-\mathbb{P}_{G_{1}}(A^{c})}{1-\mathbb{P}_{G_{1}}(A^{c})}\right).

For
n
large enough,
\mathbb{P}_{G_{0}}(A^{c}),\mathbb{P}_{G_{1}}(A^{c})=O(e^{-n}).
Thus,

\displaystyle\sup_{\mu_{1:n}\in\Theta}\operatorname{\mathsf{Regret}}_{n}(a_{1:n})

\displaystyle\geq\max\left(R(G_{0},\delta),R(G_{1},\delta)\right)-O(e^{-n})

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}\left(R(G_{0},\delta)+R(G_{1},\delta)\right)-O(e^{-n})

\displaystyle=\Omega(n^{-1}),

as desired.
∎

Lemma E.1

(Reduction to monotone decision rules)
.

Fix
\sigma_{1:n},K_{1:n}
. Let
a_{i}(Y_{i};Y_{-i})\in[0,1]
be a decision rule and
assume
a_{i}(\cdot,Y_{1:n})
is not almost surely zero or almost surely one. There
exists a threshold rule
a_{i}^{*}(\cdot;Y_{-i})=\mathbf{1}(Y_{i}>\delta_{i}(Y_{-i}))
, where

\delta_{i}(Y_{-i})\in\mathbb{R}
, such that

\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{1:n}}[(\mu_{i}-K_{i})a_{i}(Y_{i};Y_{-i})]\leq\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{1:n}}[(\mu_{i}-K_{i})a_{i}^{*}(Y_{i};Y_{-i})]

for all
\mu_{1:n}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Proof.

Let

\gamma_{0}(Y_{-i})=\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{i}=K_{i}}[a_{i}(Y_{i};Y_{-i})\mid Y_{-i}]\in(0,1).

Define
a_{i}^{*}(y;Y_{-i})=\mathbf{1}(y\geq c(Y_{-i}))
such that

\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{i}=K_{i}}\left[{\mathbf{1}(Y_{i}\geq c(Y_{-i}))\mid Y_{-i}}\right]=\gamma_{0}(Y_{-i}).

Note that
a_{i}^{*}(y;Y_{-i})
is the uniformly most powerful test for
H_{0}:\mu_{i}\leq K_{i}
with
conditional size

\gamma_{0}(Y_{-i})
, against
H_{1}:\mu_{i}>K_{i}
.
Likewise,
1-a_{i}^{*}(y;Y_{-i})
is the UMP test when we swap the null and the
alternative.
a_{i}(y;Y_{-i})
is a randomized test that has the same conditional size

\gamma_{0}(Y_{-i})
.

Let
\mu_{i}>K_{i}
, then

\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{i}}[a_{i}(Y_{i};Y_{-i})\mid Y_{-i}]\leq\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{i}}[a_{i}^{*}(Y_{i};Y_{-i})\mid Y_{-i}]

since
a_{i}^{*}
is weakly more powerful than
a_{i}
. Conversely, if
\mu_{i}<K_{i}
, then

\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{i}}[a_{i}(Y_{i};Y_{-i})\mid Y_{-i}]\geq\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{i}}[a_{i}^{*}(Y_{i};Y_{-i})\mid Y_{-i}]

since
1-a^{*}
is more powerful than
1-a_{i}
. Thus

(\mu_{i}-K_{i})\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{i}}[a_{i}(Y_{i};Y_{-i})\mid Y_{-i}]\leq(\mu_{i}-K_{i})\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{i}}[a_{i}^{*}(Y_{i};Y_{-i})\mid Y_{-i}].

The conclusion follows by integrating out
Y_{-i}
.
∎

Lemma E.2

(Properties of Sinc Kernel)
.

The function
x\mapsto\frac{1}{h}\sinc\left(\frac{x}{h}\right)
is bounded above by
Ch^{-1},
and further has a derivative bounded uniformly by
Ch^{-2}
for an absolute constant
C.

Proof.

The first claim follows since
|\sinc(y)|\leq\pi^{-1}
per our definition. For the second, differentiating gives
h^{-2}\sinc^{\prime}\left(\frac{x}{h}\right)
where

\text{sinc}^{\prime}(y)=\frac{y\cos y-\sin y}{\pi y^{2}}.

An application of l’Hopital’s rule shows that this function is zero at zero. From this and continuity, it is not difficult to see that
\text{sinc}^{\prime}(y)
is uniformly bounded by some absolute constant. This gives the second claim.
∎

Proposition E.1

(
68
)
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be some real-valued function where
\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim\textsf{N}(\mu,1)}[|f(Y)|]<\infty
.
Define
m:\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}
as
m(z)=\int f(y)\varphi(y-z)\,dy
. If
m
is entire, then
there is some
\mu\in\mathbb{R}
where

\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[f(Y)]\neq\varphi(\mu).

Proof.

Towards contradiction, suppose
m(\mu)=\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[f(Y)]=\varphi(\mu)
over
\mu\in\mathbb{R}
.
Since
\varphi(\cdot)
is analytic, we must have
m(z)=\varphi(z)
over
\mathbb{C}
. Let

z=it
, we have that for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
,

\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(y)\varphi(y)e^{ity}dye^{t^{2}/2}=e^{t^{2}/2}\implies\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(y)\varphi(y)e^{ity}dy=1

Note that

\int|f(y)\varphi(y)|dy=\int|f(y)|\varphi(y)\,dy=\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim\textsf{N}(0,1)}[|f(Y)|]<\infty.

Thus
f\varphi
is
L^{1}
. By the Riemann–Lebesgue lemma, the Fourier transform
vanishes at infinity:

0=\lim_{|t|\to\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(y)\varphi(y)e^{ity}dy=1.

This is a contradiction.
∎
