Assessing Unmeasured Confounding in Observational Research via Bayesian Semiparametric Modeling
Authors: Xinyi Xu, Steven N. MacEachern, Bo Lu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
It is challenging to infer a causal relationship with observational data. Untestable assumptions of ignorability or exchangeability are often utilized to facilitate causal effect estimation, which opens the door to arguments for and against the validity of a causal conclusion. In their 1959 seminal paper, Cornfield and colleagues provided sophisticated statistical reasoning for causal inference with observational data and made a key technical contribution to sensitivity analysis. Cornfield’s idea paved the way for the development of modern tools for assessment of causality if ignorability assumptions fail. Compared with classical/frequentist approaches, Bayesian methods for sensitivity analysis are less fully developed. In this commentary, we first introduce a Bayesian semiparametric model for causal inference, then present a sensitivity analysis strategy for the Gaussian process model. Our method is easily interpretable and avoids restrictive parametric outcome assumptions. It can also be applied to both population-level and conditional causal effects.

Introduction

It is challenging to infer a causal relationship on the basis of observational data due to the lack of randomization. As a consequence, analysts often turn to assumptions of ignorability or exchangeability in order to provide an estimate of the average treatment effect (Imbens and Rubin, 2015). However, such assumptions are untestable with the observed data. Some would go so far as to say that the assumptions are unexaminable on the basis of the observed data. The lack of randomization and reliance on important and unverifiable assumptions opens the door to arguments for and against the validity of a causal conclusion. The classic example is the heated debate of 70 years ago, as to whether smoking in a causal agent for lung cancer. In their seminal paper, Cornfield and colleagues synthesized epidemiological and experimental evidence. They systematically addressed criticism against the cigarette-lung cancer relationship, examined the evidence from multiple perspectives, found it to be compelling, and concluded that the totality of the evidence strongly supported the existence of a causal relationship between smoking and lung cancer (Cornfield, Haenszel, Hammond, Lilienfeld, Shimkin, and Wynder, 1959).

Cornfield and colleagues’ paper provides a superb example of sophisticated statistical reasoning that goes far beyond the algorithmic “data in, conclusion out” analyses that too commonly appear in the literature. In addition to their reasoned arguments and refutation of various alternative explanations, they made a key technical contribution to sensitivity analysis: for a binary unmeasured confounder to fully explain away an observed risk ratio r, the confounder must be associated with both the exposure and the disease by risk ratios at least as large as r. This result marked a major step forward as it provided a rigorous quantitative framework in which to assess the impact of unmeasured confounding. A large value of r can only be explained by the presence of a strong unmeasured confounder – or a real effect.

Cornfield’s idea paved the way for the development of the modern sensitivity analysis that is an essential part of any assessment of causality. His technical result supports the general strategy for a sensitivity analysis: Posit the value of certain sensitivity parameters that quantify the magnitude of unmeasured confounding. Combine these parameters with the observed data to infer the magnitude of the true causal effect. Vary the value of these parameters over a range that is judged to be plausible (or, alternatively, ask whether the value of these parameters needed to erase any causal effect are plausible).

Sjölander, Gabriel, and Ciocănea-Teodorescu (2022) distinguishes between two types of sensitivity analysis. The first follows Cornfield, considering an unmeasured confounder, U, that is sufficient for confounding control, and parameterizes its association with the exposure and the outcome. In modern work, U need not be binary. The second focuses on sensitivity parameters that directly quantify the deviation from ignorability/exchangeability. This approach has the advantage of not requiring the researcher to explicitly consider an unmeasured confounder. However, the resulting sensitivity parameters are more abstract and more difficult to explain, especially to a non-technical audience. In this note, we follow Cornfield, considering models with an unobserved confounder.

Sensitivity analysis can be conducted for classical/frequentist analyses and for Bayesian analyses. On the classical side, Rosenbaum’s approach and the more recently developed E-value see routine use (Ding and VanderWeele, 2016; Rosenbaum, 2010). With these approaches, one specifies a particular non-zero association between U and the exposure/outcome, calculates bounds for the causal effect, and sees whether there is a qualitative change in the causal conclusion. The specified association is often varied to find the minimal association required to change the causal conclusion. On the Bayesian side, there has been tremendous growth over the past two decades. The growth has been driven, in part, by the methods’ ability to quantify uncertainty and their probabilistic interpretation of the causal effect (Roy, Lum, Zeldow, Dworkin, III, and Daniels, 2018; Hahn, Murray, and Carvalho, 2020). Perhaps more importantly, the methods are driven by modeling. As a consequence, they are amenable to modelling latent causal structures, to smoothly transitioning from estimation to prediction, to addressing non-constant causal effects, and to handling shifting relationships between measured and unmeasured covariates, exposures, and outcomes. The methods also provide insight into the performance of classical sensitivity analysis when used to assess causality.

Given their potential, Bayesian methods in this area are underdeveloped. In this commentary, we explore a novel sensitivity analysis in a Bayesian semiparametric framework. In Section 2, we introduce a Bayesian semiparametric model for causal inference under the assumption of ignorability. This is followed with a brief extension to sensitivity analysis in the non-ignorable setting in Section 3. The final Section contains parting comments.

A Bayesian Semiparametric Model for Causal Inference

This Section briefly reviews a propensity-score-based Gaussian process model proposed in Xu, MacEachern, and Lu (2023). Following their notation, we denote the observed data as (Yi,Xi,Ti), where Yi is a response variable, Xi is a vector of observed covariates and Ti is a binary treatment indicator variable (1 for treated and 0 for non-treated) for the ith individual, i=1,⋯,n. A nonparametric conditional mean model for the outcome is postulated as Yi=g(Xi,Ti)+εi, where the random error εi is assumed to be normally distributed with mean zero and unknown variance σ2. The εi are taken to be mutually independent.

Under the ignorability assumption, the potential outcomes depend on the covariates only through the propensity score, following an argument similar to that in Wu, Xu, Tong, Jiang, and Lu (2021). We drop the subscript and describe the propensity score and conditional mean in terms of the covariate x and treatment t. The propensity score is e(x)=P(T=1|x). The mean function can be simplified into the following semiparametric form (1)g(x,t)=f(e(x))+Δ(e(x))×t, where f captures the response surface for the control group and the function Δ captures the treatment effect. Note that the treatment effect may be heterogeneous with respect to e(x).

We assume that the functions f and Δ are continuous, and we quantify our uncertainty about them with Gaussian process (GP) priors. That is, on the interval (0,1), (2)f(·)∼GP(μf(·),K(·,·))andΔ(·)∼GP(μΔ(·),K(·,·)), where μf(·) and μΔ(·) are the mean functions and K(·,·) is the covariance function. The framework is quite general. In practice, we suggest the use of a relatively simple mean function that is capable of capturing large-scale trends and stylized features of f and Δ. The Gaussian processes pick up departures from these trends and features, giving the model full support among continuous functions for both f and Δ. Considerations of pathwise properties of f and Δ guide the choice of K(·,·).

We have explored a variety of implementations of this model. One which has produced good results uses a polynomial form for μf and μΔ, namely μf(e)=H(e)⊤α and μΔ(e)=H(e)⊤β, with H(e) the same low-dimensional polynomial (e.g., H(e)⊤=(1,e,e2,e3)⊤) for both. This ensures that the mean functions for the treatment and control groups, μf(e) and μf(e)+μΔ(e), can be any pair of linear functions of H(e) (e.g., any pair of cubic polynomials). Along with this, we ensure smoothness of the realized mean functions f(·) and f(·)+Δ(·) by taking the covariance function K(·,·) to be of the squared-exponential form, that is, K(e,e′)=γ2exp(−12l2|e−e′|2), where |e−e′|2 is the squared Euclidean distance between e and e′.

To complete the model, we need distributions on the hyperparameters. We have found success with weakly informative normal priors on α and β and inverse-gamma priors on γ, l and σ2. These parameters and the functions f and Δ can be updated through an MCMC algorithm. The Bayes estimator of the population average treatment effect (PATE) under squared error loss is ΔˆGP=1n∑i=1nμ˜Δ(e(Xi)), where μ˜Δ(e(Xi)) is the posterior mean of the treatment effect function Δ at e(Xi). This estimator resembles the common matching estimator when the parameter l in the covariance function goes to 0 and the mean functions μf and μΔ are constants with independent uniform priors.

The model leads to much more. Making use of an alternative parameterization, as is often done with ANOVA models, allows one to model a “grand mean” (f) and departure from it (±Δ). This form easily allows one to write an exchangeable model for treatment and control or for a set of treatments where none is distinguished as the control. The various parameterizations facilitate the elicitation of prior information that matches the given setting. The information needed to characterize the global behavior of the mean and its local features can be quite different for the overall mean (e.g., the control or grand mean) than for the treatment effect function (Hahn, Carvalho, Puelz, and He, 2018). The Bayesian nature of the model and its flexibility provide predictive distributions as a function of e(x). This allows one to consider alternative estimands, such as the population average treatment effect, whether for the given population or for a population with a different distribution over covariates and hence over propensity scores. It also allows one to provide estimates under different loss functions.

Computation for the model can be enhanced with strategies that have proven useful for work with Gaussian processes (Banerjee, Dunson, and Tokdar, 2012; Hoffmann and Onnela, 2025). Efficient computation leads to rapid fits of the model, even for large data sets. Of more relevance here, it gives us an easy way to perform sensitivity analysis.

A Sensitivity Analysis Strategy Based on Bayesian Semiparametric Modeling

Sensitivity analysis is an essential component of causal arguments that are based on observational data. We begin with the semiparametric Gaussian process model of Section 2. Our approach is similar to that of Dorie, Harada, Carnegie, and Hill (2016) who posit a binary unmeasured confounder U and examine its impact on the estimand of interest. We depart from Dorie et al.’s method by allowing the prevalence of U to vary across a range of values and by allowing non-binary U.

Suppose that there is an unmeasured confounder, U, which impacts both the treatment selection and the outcome. The relationship between the unmeasured confounder U and the observed covariates X is specified by some probability model p(U|X). In some situations, it is plausible to assume that U is independent of X, for example, when U represents the portion of the unobserved covariate that is not explained by the observed covariates. In such situations, we can place a probability distribution to U, that is, U|θU∼p(U|θU), where U may follow a binary distribution, or a distribution such as the normal, t, or binomial. This distribution does not depend on X. In the case of a binary U, U∣X∼i.i.d.Bernoulli(πu), and we would consider a variety of values for πu – say 0.1, 0.3, 0.5, 0.7, and 0.9. This allows us to assess the robustness of the causal estimates in different prevalence scenarios. Alternatively, U may be related to the observed covariate X. We have found that relatively simple models such as linear regression or logistic regression can be effective. In this situation, the model for p(U|θU) is modified to p(U|X,θU), where θU could be the regression coefficients. Again, we recommend consideration of a range of values for θU.

Assuming that ignorability is satisfied with the addition of U, we write models for the treatment assignment and the outcome that incorporate U. We introduce two sensitivity parameters, ζt and ζy, to capture the association of U with the treatment assignment T and the outcome Y, respectively. We consider a probit model for the binary treatment indicator, T, that is linear in the observed covariates, X, and the unmeasured confounder, U, so T∣X,U∼Bernoulli(Φ(XTβt+ζtU)), where βt is the vector of unknown coefficients. Simplicity at this stage of the model appears to work well, but there is scope for building a more complex model, especially for the impact of X on the distribution of T. The continuous outcome Y is assumed to follow a normal distribution with variance σ2 and is modeled as Y∣T,X,U,σ2∼N(f(e)+Δ(e)T+ζyU,σ2), where e is the propensity score described in Section 2. If U is a continuous unmeasured confounder instead of a binary one, the functional relationship of U in the above representations can vary. Our ability to reparameterize by mapping U to another continuous covariate suggests that we stick to a relatively simple functional form for U.

The sensitivity analysis is carried out over a grid of values for the sensitivity parameters. The computational algorithm is as follows:

The credible intervals from Step 3 are used to make decisions on whether the treatment effects are substantial, after accounting for potential unmeasured confounders. This includes checking to see whether 0 is included in the interval and also examining the size of the effect in the interval. This strategy can be adapted to account for binary or ordinal outcomes for Y by replacing the normal model for Y with an appropriate form. The models are easily modified to include prognistic scores in the outcome regression model, something that we have seen improve performance (Xu et al., 2023). Computational implementation remains important. As suggested by Dorie et al. (2016), the end-state for one grid cell can be used to seed the MCMC sampler in an adjacent cell, because a small change in the sensitivity parameters is expected to have only a minor effect on the posterior distribution. This alleviates many concerns with convergence of the MCMC sampler.

Sensitivity analyses are most useful when their implications can be communicated clearly, especially to readers who may be less familiar with statistical modeling. Graphical summaries can help to visualize the analyses. In particular, we use contour plots to show how estimated treatment effects (PATE and CATE) vary over a grid of sensitivity parameters ζt and ζy. We center the display at (ζt,ζy)=(0,0), which corresponds to the ignorability assumption, and visualize departures from that baseline. To aid interpretation, we scale the axes so that values of ζt and ζy can be compared directly to the marginal effects of observed covariates. This anchors the sensitivity parameters in familiar quantities and provides a practical benchmark for judging what levels of unmeasured confounding would be needed to meaningfully change the estimated effects.

Discussion

The big data era has ushered in automated data collection, and with this has come a wealth of observational data. The resulting data sets are often large and contain a wide variety of recorded variables. They provide a rich resource for extracting causal information in research fields where randomized experiments are difficult or impossible to conduct. This lack of randomization presents a major challenge for validating an apparent causal effect. Tools for elucidating the impact of potential unmeasured confounding, such as sensitivity analysis, are critically important for completing a causal argument. Cornfield et al. (1959) was instrumental in laying the foundation for the quantitative evaluation of unmeasured confounding.

In this commentary, we propose a Bayesian sensitivity analysis strategy, built upon a semiparametric regression framework using Gaussian process priors. It is inspired by the idea of postulating an unmeasured confounder and its association with both the treatment and the outcome. This echoes the original thoughts in Cornfield et al. (1959), which were later made popular and further developed by Rosenbaum. Our method is advantageous in the following ways: first, it adopts a Bayesian framework that can effectively incorporate the uncertainty from various components of the model, both observed and unobserved. In particular, it naturally includes the missing U which is easily included in the MCMC sampler used to fit the model. Second, it uses semiparametric modeling to avoid restrictive parametric assumptions on the outcome. Third, the interpretation is easy, as the practitioners can link U to important factors that are not included in the dataset and can contrast this with observed confounders. Lastly, our method is comprehensive as it can evaluate the sensitivity for both PATE and CATE, which has strong potential for applications with heterogeneous causal effects.
