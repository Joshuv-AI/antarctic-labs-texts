Substance use depiction in women characters: Content analysis of systematically selected Hindi movies over three decades
Authors: Swati Kedia Gupta, Snehil Gupta, Udit Kumar Panda, Alok Agrawal, Atul Ambekar, Ravindra Rao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:Globally, proportion of women using psychoactive substances has increased substantially.Aim:We aimed to assess the extent and pattern of depiction of alcohol and other substance use by women in Hindi-language movies and analyse the trends of depiction over the three decades selected for the study (I: 1961–1970; II: 1981–1990; and III: 2001–2010).Methods:We selected the top five grossing movies for each year of the three decades. Across the decades, we compared quantitative variables to assess for changes in portrayal of alcohol and other psychoactive substance use by women and a qualitative content analysis was also performed.Results:Of the 150 movies analyzed, 36 movies (24%) had scenes (n = 79) depicting any form of psychoactive substance use by women. The cumulative duration of these scenes was approximately 4.5 hours. In majority of the scenes (74.4%), female lead was shown to be consuming any form of substance, with alcohol being the most dominant (41.1%). Enjoyment emerged as the most cited reason especially in decade III (26.6%). Tobacco use decreased by decade III; however, portrayal of alcohol changed with more use of beer and wine. This decade also had a significant proportion of scenes set in parties, bar etc., and the context was to show the “coolness” or independent nature of the women.Conclusion:There seems to be an increasing trend of use of alcohol and other psychoactive substance use, amongst female characters in Bollywood, with negative consequences rarely shown. The impact of these depictions on Indian viewers especially women needs further study.

INTRODUCTION

The proportion of women by using psychoactive substances has increased substantially over years.[12] A recent national survey also found that a sizeable proportion of women in India use psychoactive substances.[3] Assessing factors influencing the initiation of substance use can help in designing appropriate preventive strategies in women. Studies show that multiple factors influence the initiation of alcohol or other substances, one of that is mass media, especially movies. The role of media in the initiation of substance use can be explained by various theories such as cultivation theory and social cognitive theory.[45] In India, Hindi-language movies dominate the Indian film industry, contributing to about 33% of the entertainment industry revenue.[6] Films in India are often likened to a potent cultural stimulant profoundly influencing societal norms and values and vice-versa.[7]

Despite the influence of movies on the viewers, data on depiction of alcohol and substance use by women in movies is limited and mostly from western countries. For example, a content analysis of USA movies and movies from Argentina and Mexico found that while female smoking depiction was significantly less than that of male characters, there were no gender differences for alcohol use depiction.[8] Similarly, analysis of 50 movies on Netflix revealed that 13 movies depicted women by using psychoactive substances.[9] Most studies, however, focus on general depiction of alcohol or other psychoactive substances without detailed analysis of context of substance use by women. A recent descriptive study of purposively chosen 22 movies found drug use portrayal to be common in men, young adults (both gender), and in individuals of affluent families.[10]

Literature on substance use by women in Hindi movies is scant, and exists primarily as either opinion papers, commentaries, or as content analysis of purposively selected movies.[111213] To the best of our knowledge, no systematic studies have been conducted to specifically understand the same along with its context and changing trends of portrayal over time. The current study was planned keeping this background in mind. The specific objectives of the study were: (a) to quantify (by number and duration) the scenes, in which alcohol/drug use by a female character is depicted in the movies; (b) to conduct content analysis of those scenes; and (c) to analyses, over the three chosen decades (1961–1970; 1981–1990; and 2001–2010), the trends in portrayal of alcohol and drug use by female characters in Hindi movies.

METHODOLOGY

We chose top five movies that had the highest box office collections from each year of the following decades: 1961–1970; 1981–1990; and 2001–2010. Information on the top five grossing movies was obtained from Wikipedia and the website http://muvyz.com/boxoffice (functional at the time of data collection). The Box office earnings can be considered as an indirect sign of the viewership and hence the reach of the movie.

Detailed methodology can be found in our previous work.[14] The second (1981–1990) and third (2001–2010) were the decades pre and post economic liberalization in the country. And the first decade (1961–1970) were chosen to assess long-term trends. Hundred fifty movies (50 movies per selected decade) were chosen, and their hard copies or online versions, whichever was available easily, were accessed. The list of movies is attached as Supplementary File 1. Each movie was analyzed at two levels. At the first level, the movies were distributed to seven selected raters though stratified random sampling (stratified by the three decades). These raters were asked to note down all scenes in that women were shown to be either consuming any form of psychoactive substance or were intoxicated. At the second level, four of the authors performed content analysis of the selected scenes. The four raters initially analyzed few scenes together to achieve consensus on rating. In case of doubt, discussions were held to arrive at a consensus. After performing content analysis of the scenes selected at first level, each author watched the entire movie allotted to them to ensure that no scene was missed. Data for the study was collected from January 2018 to May 2019.

Content analysis of the scene

We defined a scene as “series or sequence of dialogue and action at a single location or single point in time.” Visual, audio/dialogue, and the combined audio-visual depiction of psychoactive substances in women were considered for inclusion in the study. The psychoactive substances included all drugs, including tobacco, and alcohol. Visual depiction meant visual scenes of either the psychoactive substance itself, related paraphernalia (such as bottles of alcohol or lighter), or of the woman being shown as intoxicated in the scene. Audio depiction included mention of psychoactive substance by a woman, either in dialogue or in the lyrics of a song.

For each depiction of psychoactive substance use by women, the second-level raters made a note of the following aspects: (1) time duration of the depiction, (2) audio, visual or audio-visual depiction, (3) depiction in narration or in song, (4) type of depiction–substance itself, paraphernalia, intoxicated female without depiction of substance, showing brand in background, or showing alcohol shop in background, (5) Specific type of depiction (spirits, local liquor, beer, wine, combination of any of these; cigarettes, Bidi, Hookah, cigar, Bong, etc.), (6) setting of depiction (home, restaurant or bar, workplace, party/club or casino, etc.), (7) locale of depiction (India or outside India, and whether urban or rural), (8) type of message (pro-use, anti-use, mixed or no message), and (9) whether characters shown to consume alcohol or drugs.

The following information about the woman shown using psychoactive substance was also collected: type of character (heroine, female in negative character (popularly called as “vamp”), mother of any primary character, etc.), context of use (enjoyment or partying, romance, forgetting loved one, warding off negative emotions, alcohol/drug taken as challenge, evoke laughter, etc.), associated emotions (happy, sad, irritable, anxious, etc.), and whether they indulged in high-risk behavior (driving under influence, forced into sex, indulging in crime, etc.).

We started the study after getting permission from the Ethics committee of our institute.

Data compilation and analysis

The second-level raters entered the data in Google Docs, with each scene considered as one data point, and conducted quantitative analysis using Microsoft Excel. Descriptive analysis for the quantitative variables was conducted and then the variables, across the three decades, were compared to assess for any changes in the portrayals.

We also performed qualitative content analysis for all the scenes in which female characters consuming alcohol/drugs was depicted. For each decade, the analysis was performed separately. Keywords were coded for each description separately and then grouped to create themes for each decade. Data from the quantitative and qualitative analyses were used to assess trends of depiction of women using psychoactive substance in cinema across the three decades.

RESULTS – QUANTITATIVE ANALYSIS

Of the 150 movies analyzed, 36 movies (24%) had scenes (n = 79) depicting any form of psychoactive substance use by women. The cumulative duration of the 79 scenes was about 282 min (approximately 4.5 hours). Two movies (“Purab and Paschim” and “Chaalbaaz”) had maximum number of scenes with psychoactive substance and portrayal of the heroine as the user in first decade and second decade, respectively. The scenes were evenly distributed across movies in the third decade.

Content analysis

As shown in Table 1, the medium of depiction was primarily visual (n = 44, 55.7%), followed by audio-video depiction (n = 33, 41.8%). Of the latter, most of the scenes (n = 24, 72.7%) contained dialogues and visual depiction. In most scenes (n = 72, 91.1%), the substance itself was shown, with alcohol being the most common (n = 38, 41.1%), followed by smoked tobacco, (n = 21, 26.6%). No brand of alcohol was shown. Two scenes each, depicted Cannabis use (accidently or for recreation) and sleep medicine (as self-medication). The characters were shown to consume substances primarily at home (n = 23, 29.1) or in bars or restaurants (n = 16, 20.3%). Most scenes were primarily shot in India (n = 58, 73.4%).

Characters, context of use and high-risk behaviors

As shown in Table 2, the lead female character (heroine) was shown to be consuming any form of psychoactive substance in majority of the scenes (n = 59, 74.7%), followed by the female in negative role (n = 14, 17.7%). About 43 scenes (54.4%) depicted the character in intoxicated state, mostly because of actual use or feigning intoxication to embarrass family members. Most of these characters belonged to upper socio-economic status (n = 50, 63.3%). Where the context of the use of psychoactive substance could be inferred (57 scenes, 72.2%), it was consumed primarily for enjoyment (n = 21, 26.6%), followed by need toward off negative emotions (n = 11; 13.9%). Some other reasons for substance use included increasing confidence, enjoying intimacy with a partner, or to evoke humor. Only 12 scenes (15.2%) depicted high-risk behavior following psychoactive substance use.

Trend analysis

Trend analysis is reported in Table 3. In the first decade, 11 movies had scenes depicting substance use by women, followed by 15 movies in the second decade, and 10 movies in the third decade. The number of scenes in each decade was 24, 30, and 25 respectively. Compared to the first decade, there was an increase in the number of scenes in the second decade, which again decreased a bit in third decade. However, it is of note that in the first decade, more than 50% scenes were contributed by a single movie (“Purab and Paschim”). The average depiction time per scene of substance use also increased from 2.6 min in the first decade to 4.1 min in the third decade. Across the decades, alcohol use depiction increased (6 scenes in the first decade to 18 scenes in the second decade), while that of tobacco decreased (from 12 scenes in the first decade to only two in the third decade). Portrayal of other psychoactive substance use emerged by the third decade. In the first decade, majority of scenes were shot inside home of affluent families (n = 12, 50%) and/or outside India (n = 14, 58.3%) and by the third decade, most scenes were filmed within the country (n = 16, 64%) and in public places like pubs, bars, and so on (n = 16, 64%). While in most scenes, the implied messages were either mixed or difficult to decipher, more scenes in the third decade depicted use of psychoactive substances for enjoyment as compared to the first decade.

RESULTS-QUALITATIVE ANALYSIS

The description provided by the second-level rates for each movie was analyzed to assess the common trends in the manner of psychoactive depiction by women in each decade.

The first decade (1961–1970)

In the 1960s, the genre of the movies was primarily drama and love stories in which the heroine played an important role, with more screen time, and was seen to be integral in upholding the values of righteousness and repute of the family. She was usually portrayed as coy, demure, and dependent on their male counterparts. Drinking and smoking by women were portrayed to be part of western culture and alien to Indian culture. “Wearing revealing dresses,” “loose” morality was associated with drinking and smoking, again to depict western culture. Most instances of use were shot at home setting. Alcohol was the predominantly used psychoactive substance. The context of drinking or smoking by women were: as a female in negative character (while scheming with villain); being offered a drink for some “legitimate” reasons such as dealing with cold (“Sangam”); being deceived into consuming intoxicants; to embarrass someone by bringing disrepute to family name “Intequam”) or to break off a marriage (“Gharana”).

The second decade (1981–1990)

This decade was dominated, in general, by action movies. Less screen time was given to female characters except in two movies (“Chaalbaaz” and “Khoon bhari maang”). In majority of movies, women were seen just as “romantic partners” and/or were “abducted by villain” to threaten the hero. An increase in the number of movies depicting smoking and drinking by women was observed. The trend of equating “western” and “morally corrupt” women with drinking and smoking continued in this decade as well. Females in negative characters were shown to drink and smoke without any “particular reason” as well as in scenes where there was scheming/plotting against the hero/heroine. The heroine would be seen resorting to alcohol use, when she had to be shown going “wayward,” being deceived by someone. Use in a negative emotional state was rare. Interestingly, tobacco or cigarette use was only shown in two movies.

The third decade (2001–2010)

The genre again shifted to drama, comedy, and love stories with women playing significant roles in the movies—at times, being wooed by more than one hero. In this decade, women were depicted as educated, independent, and bashful and the distinction between heroine and the women in negative character blurred. The same character when shown in positive light would be shown to be drinking to have fun and when shown in negative light was shown to be drinking to accentuate her “villainy”/negative nature (e.g., in the movie “Race”). Moral strings attached to women drinking, by and large, stopped in this decade and alcohol consumption was used as a marker of the woman being independent and progressive. In one movie (“Kal Ho Na Ho”), the heroine who was otherwise a non-drinker and a nerd, goes to a bar to drink to prove her “coolness.” Unlike previous decades, women were shown to drink to enjoy, sing and dance, and generally, to have fun. More scenes showed women drinking in public places such as bar, restaurants, or pubs. As compared to men, more women were shown to be intoxicated, though they did not engage in high-risk behaviors. In this decade, the hero or protagonist usually did not look down upon women who used alcohol. Keeping in trend with male characters, smoking depictions declined.

DISCUSSION

The present study was conceptualized to quantify the depiction of tobacco, alcohol, and drugs by women in top-grossing Hindi movies and to conduct in-depth analysis across three decades. We followed the Standards for Reporting Qualitative Research (SRQR) guidelines for reporting this study.[15] The checklist of the guidelines can be seen in the Supplementary File 2. The strength of the study is that it is possibly the first study to systematically assess the portrayal of alcohol and drugs by women in Hindi movies. Our study found a total of 1877 scenes containing any form of psychoactive substance use, out of which 79 scenes (4.2%) depicting alcohol or substance use by female characters were analyzed. The scenes were spread across 36 films (24%) with a total running time of approximately 4.5 hours, that amounts to 1.25% of the combined duration of all the movies, which needs to be seen in context of women occupying less screen-time as compared to their male counterparts.[16]

Alcohol was the most used substance by women, followed by tobacco. The depiction was primarily visual in nature. In most scenes, the lead actress was depicted with substance use and was often shown to be tipsy. Similar trends have been observed in other studies assessing alcohol and substance use depictions by male actors in movies. With respect to alcohol use, the context was primarily enjoyment, unlike men where alcohol use was toward off negative emotions as found in our previous study.[14] Tobacco, mostly used by young actresses, accompanied alcohol use and, in general, did not have any context, which is unlike that found in Hollywood movies, where the actress smoked as much as the male lead and the purpose of smoking would be to control emotions or cope with stress.[1718] It is also contrary to factual scenario in India, where 14% of adult females reported use of any form of tobacco products.[19] Only four scenes depicted consumption of cannabis and sleep medicine, which is in line with other studies, where portrayal of other drugs is lesser as compared to alcohol and tobacco.[20] The context of cannabis use, in the form of Bhang, was recreational or accidental and was shown in a positive light. In India, Bhang is the only form of cannabis that is legal as well as socially acceptable. On the other hand, Ganja (marijuana) has been depicted to portray hippie culture. Like the study was conducted by Bhatia et al.,[10] sleep-medication (possibly benzodiazepines) use was shown as a form of self-medication and had an associated negative connotation.

The trend analysis highlighted two aspects—generic changes in representation of female characters and specific changes in their patterns of alcohol or substance use. We observed that in the first decade post-independence, women played an important role in the storyline and had significant screen-time. Also, there was a clear dichotomy between the actresses. The lead actress upheld the values and morals of the family, was passive or subservient to the male character. However, the actress in the company of the villain (also known as vamp) was more “westernized” in terms of clothing, substance use habits and was more outspoken. In the second decade, a rise in action films and popularity of “angry young man” reduced women to “objects” to satisfy the male gaze.[21] This perception underwent a substantial transformation in the third decade, portraying heroines as independent individuals, enjoying alcohol alongside their male counterparts for fun and pleasure (e.g., in movies like “Love Aaj Kal,” “Bunty aur Bubly”). This could be understood as the effect of post-liberalization, which witnessed a change in the way movies were received and an increase in number of women going to cinema halls.[2223] Moreover, at the turn of the century, Indian society also witnessed a change in its views about gender equality, dating, sexuality, women being offered better education and employment opportunities, and the breakdown of the black and white dichotomy.[24]

In decade 1 and 2, alcohol or drug use by women was used to typecast her as either bad, sad, or cool, without looking at the complexities of her character. The lead actress would not smoke, drink, or party, whereas the woman playing negative character used alcohol, indulged with random men, wore revealing clothes and partied.[25] Wherever alcohol or substance use was shown, it was to reflect westernization or hippie culture. For instance, in the 1970s film “Purab aur Paschim,” the lead actress born and brought up in western world considered smoking, drinking, or partying as normative behavior. But when she comes back to India, she imbibes its values and beliefs by wearing traditional attire (which is the saree) and throws away cigarette and alcohol befitting an “Indian” woman. By the third decade, drinking, and smoking on screen became acceptable and “cool,” which resembled a similar transition in American movies in 1930s, wherein alcohol use by women was symbolized as matter-of-fact modernity.[26]

As observed in other studies and in parallel with the real-world changes, our study also found an increase in scenes depicting female characters consuming alcohol or other psychoactive substances over the three decades.[327] Although use of alcoholic beverages went up across the three decades, use of tobacco decreased as evidenced in other studies, reflecting the growing awareness and the effect of the 2005 regulation on tobacco depiction in mass media by Ministry of Health and Family Welfare.[1428] Thus, it can be inferred that alcohol use and smoking are getting “normalized” irrespective of the gender and are no more associated with morality or negative repercussions. Further research is needed to understand the implication of this phenomenon in girls and young women.

It is crucial to consider specific limitations when interpreting the results. A formal analysis to evaluate inter-rater reliability was not conducted, potentially leading to variations in interpretation among second-level raters. To address this limitation, efforts were made to enhance consistency by having the second-level raters collaboratively review 10–15 scenes, fostering a shared understanding in the interpretation of scenes. Another limitation was exclusion of parallel cinema/Art movies, as they would not be part of top grossing movies but may have a more significant impact on the audience watching it. Moreover, our study has analyzed movies only till 2010 and it would be interesting to assess what changes have occurred in the last decade, especially with advent and easier accessibility of hard drugs.

CONCLUSION

In conclusion, this study sheds light on the evolving portrayal of alcohol and drug use by women in Bollywood cinema across three decades. Initially, female characters’ engagement with alcohol and drugs was heavily stereotyped and used to delineate moral boundaries or signify cultural foreignness. Over time, however, the representation of substance use by women has transitioned from negative connotations to a more normalized and nuanced depiction. The trend toward normalization of substance use in films could have diverse social impacts, warranting further investigation into its influence on audiences.
