Covariance scanning for adaptively optimal change point detection in high-dimensional linear models
Haeran Cho, Housen Li

Abstract
This paper investigates the detection and estimation of a single change in high-dimensional linear models. We derive minimax lower bounds for the detection boundary and the estimation rate, which uncover a phase transition governed the sparsity of the covariance-weighted differential parameter. This form of inherent sparsity captures a delicate interplay between the covariance structure of the regressors and the change in regression coefficients on the detectability of a change point. Complementing the lower bounds, we introduce two covariance scanning-based methods, McScan and QcSan, which achieve minimax optimal performance (up to possible logarithmic factors) in the sparse and the dense regimes, respectively. In particular, QcScan is the first method shown to achieve consistency in the dense regime and further, we devise a combined procedure which is adaptively minimax optimal across sparse and dense regimes without the knowledge of the sparsity. Computationally, covariance scanning-based methods avoid costly computation of Lasso-type estimators and attain worst-case computation complexity that is linear in the dimension and sample size. Additionally, we consider the post-detection estimation of the differential parameter and the refinement of the change point estimator. Simulation studies support the theoretical findings and demonstrate the computational and statistical efficiency of the proposed covariance scanning methods.

Introduction

The change point problem in linear models has received great attention over the past several decades, owing to its widespread applicability across diverse scientific and practical disciplines, including genomics, climatology, finance and economics. Numerous methods have been developed that achieve consistency in (multiple) change point detection, both in multivariate settings (e.g.
Csörgo and Horváth, 1997
;
Bai and Perron, 1998
) and high dimensional scenarios (e.g.
Lee et al., 2016
;
Kaul et al., 2019
;
Wang et al., 2021b
;
Rinaldo et al., 2021
;
Cho and Owens, 2024
;
Xu et al., 2024
).

When faced with the high dimensionality, many existing methods rely on
\ell_{1}
-regularised estimators of local regression parameters for change point detection, thereby requiring the sparsity of regression parameters.

Gao and Wang, 2022
propose CHARCOAL, a projection-based method that instead assumes sparsity on the
differences
of regression parameters. However, CHARCOAL is applicable only when the dimension is strictly smaller than the sample size.
More recently,
Cho et al., 2025
introduce McScan, a multiscale covariance scanning approach that achieves consistent change point detection with both statistical and computational efficiency. McScan does not require exact sparsity of the regression parameters or their differences and accommodates ultra-high dimensional settings. It operates by examining the
\ell_{\infty}
-norm of the contrasts in local sample covariances between regressors and responses, and is thus implicitly better suited to sparse regimes.
Minimax lower bounds for the change point problem have been investigated, but only in the sparse setting when the
\ell_{0}
-norm of regression parameters (or their differences) is bounded by the square root of the dimension (see
Rinaldo et al., 2021
and
Cho et al., 2025
).

Despite these advances, several questions remain to be answered for the change point problem in high dimension linear models. In particular, it is hitherto unknown whether it is possible to consistently detect the change point when the change is
non-sparse
. More fundamentally, it is yet to be understood which notion of sparsity — or more generally, which structural assumption — determines the
intrinsic difficulty
in the detection and estimation of change points.
In this paper, we provide first answers to these questions under a Gaussian linear model with at most one change (AMOC): The observations
(Y_{t},\mathbf{x}_{t})\in\mathbb{R}^{1+p},\,t\in[n]:=\{1,\ldots,n\}
, are distributed as

\displaystyle Y_{t}=\begin{cases}\mathbf{x}_{t}^{\top}\bm{\beta}_{0}+\varepsilon_{t}&\text{for \ }1\leq t\leq\theta,\\
\mathbf{x}_{t}^{\top}\bm{\beta}_{1}+\varepsilon_{t}&\text{for \ }\theta+1\leq t\leq n,\end{cases}

(1)

where
\mathbf{x}_{t}\sim_{\text{iid}}\mathcal{N}_{p}(\mathbf{0},\bm{\Sigma})
with non-negative definite
\bm{\Sigma}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
and independently,
\varepsilon_{t}\sim_{\text{iid}}\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})
for all
t
.
We denote the differential parameter by
\bm{\delta}=\bm{\beta}_{1}-\bm{\beta}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
, the maximum variability of the response by
\Psi=\max(|\bm{\Sigma}^{1/2}\bm{\beta}_{0}|_{2},|\bm{\Sigma}^{1/2}\bm{\beta}_{1}|_{2},\sigma)
, and the minimal segment length by
\Delta=\min(\theta,n-\theta)
.
As a convention, we write
\theta=n
, when
\bm{\beta}_{0}=\bm{\beta}_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
, corresponding to the case of no change.
By focusing on the AMOC situation under (
1
), we separate the two
“orthogonal”
aspects of the problem: The design of an adaptively optimal procedure for change point detection and estimation, which is the main focus of this paper, and the development of algorithms for multiple change point detection. While our theoretical analysis is framed under independence and Gaussianity, we also discuss extensions to accommodate temporal dependence or non-Gaussianity.

We summarise our main contributions to the growing literature on change point analysis in high-dimensional linear models, which also serve to outline the structure of the paper.

(ii)

The first method for consistent change point detection in the dense regime.

We first show that McScan
(
Cho et al., 2025
)
, which scans the
\ell_{\infty}
-aggregation of the contrasts in local sample covariance between regressors and responses, is minimax optimal in the sparse regime up to the factor of
\sigma_{*}^{-1}
(
Section

3.1
), where
\sigma_{*}
denotes the smallest
non-zero
eigenvalue of
\bm{\Sigma}
, i.e. McScan achieves optimality in the sparse regime even when
\bm{\Sigma}
is rank deficient.
For the dense regime, we introduce the
quadratic covariance scanning
(QcScan) that evaluates the
\ell_{2}
-aggregation of the local sample covariance contrasts.
QcScan is the first method that achieves minimax optimality, up to a factor of
\sigma_{*}^{-1}\sqrt{\log\log(n)}
, in detection and estimation of the change point in the dense regime even when
\bm{\Sigma}
is rank deficient, which is in contrast to existing change point methods that require
\mathsf{Cov}(\mathbf{x}_{t})
to have bounded spectrum.
As a by-product, we also obtain an estimator for
|\bm{\Sigma}\bm{\delta}|_{2}^{2}
, which serves as a surrogate of the strength of the signal
\bm{\delta}^{\top}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{\delta}
for the change point problem in (
1
). See
Section

3.2
.

(iii)

Adaptation to inherent sparsity.
We introduce OcScan which, by combining McScan and QcScan, adapts to the unknown level of sparsity and thus achieves optimal statistical performance uniformly across all
\mathfrak{s}
, providing a complete treatment of the change point detection problem in high-dimensional linear models. See
Section

4.1
.

(iv)

Refinement of change point localisation.
We propose a procedure for further refinement of the change point estimator by incorporating the direct estimation of the differential parameter alongside covariance scanning. This refinement can improve the estimation rate by up to a factor of
\sqrt{p}
, provided that
\bm{\delta}
itself is sufficiently sparse. See
Section

4.2
.

(v)

Computational efficiency.
In contrast to a plethora of existing methods that require repeated estimation of local regression parameters, the proposed methods based on covariance scanning, namely McScan, QcScan and OcScan, exhibit ideal worst-case computation complexity of
O(np)
, see
Section

4.3
.
This demonstrates that for the detection and estimation of a change point, computationally costly operations may be avoided without losing statistical efficiency.

We further corroborate our theoretical findings with simulation studies in
Section

5
, and provide all proofs and technical details in the appendix.

2.1
Isotropic case

Let us define a model space under (
1
) as

\mathcal{P}_{\bm{\Sigma},\sigma}^{\mathfrak{s},n,p}(\tau)=\Bigl\{\mathsf{P}_{\theta,\bm{\beta}_{0},\bm{\beta}_{1}}\;:\;\{(\mathbf{x}_{t},Y_{t})\}_{t\in[n]}\sim\mathsf{P}_{\theta,\bm{\beta}_{0},\bm{\beta}_{1}}\text{ \ such that \ }\mathbf{x}_{t}\sim_{\text{iid}}\mathcal{N}_{p}(\mathbf{0},\bm{\Sigma})\text{ and}\\
\text{independently \ }\varepsilon_{t}\sim_{\text{iid}}\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}),\;Y_{t}=\mathbf{x}_{t}^{\top}\bigl(\bm{\beta}_{0}\cdot\mathbb{I}_{\{t\leq\theta\}}+\bm{\beta}_{1}\cdot\mathbb{I}_{\{t>\theta\}}\bigr)+\varepsilon_{t},\\

Theorem 1
.

Let
\sigma>0
and
1\leq\Delta\leq n/4
, and suppose that
\bm{\Sigma}=\mathbf{I}
.

(i)

If
\mathfrak{s}\log\bigl(1+\tfrac{p}{\mathfrak{s}^{2}}\vee\tfrac{\sqrt{p}}{\mathfrak{s}}\bigr)\leq\sqrt{\tfrac{25}{2}\log\left(\tfrac{17}{12\sqrt{2}}\right){\Delta}}
and
0<\tau\leq\tfrac{2}{5}\log\left(\tfrac{17}{12\sqrt{2}}\right)
, then

\inf_{\widehat{\theta}}\;\sup_{\mathsf{P}\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathbf{I},\sigma}^{\mathfrak{s},n,p}(\tau)}\;\mathsf{P}\left(\bigl|\widehat{\theta}-\theta(\mathsf{P})\bigr|\geq{\Delta}\right)\;\geq\;\frac{1}{25}.

(ii)

Let
\tau
be a large enough constant, or
\tau\to\infty
(at an arbitrary rate) as
n\to\infty
. If
\mathfrak{s}\log\bigl(1+\tfrac{p}{\mathfrak{s}^{2}}\vee\tfrac{\sqrt{p}}{\mathfrak{s}}\bigr)\leq\sqrt{\tfrac{5}{2}\log\left(\tfrac{3}{2\sqrt{2}}\right)\tfrac{\Delta}{\tau}}
, then

\inf_{\widehat{\theta}}\;\sup_{\mathsf{P}\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathbf{I},\sigma}^{\mathfrak{s},n,p}(\tau)}\;\mathsf{P}\left(\bigl|\widehat{\theta}-\theta(\mathsf{P})\bigr|\geq\frac{\sigma^{2}\mathfrak{s}\log\left(\frac{3}{2\sqrt{2}}\right)\log\left(1+\frac{p}{\mathfrak{s}^{2}}\vee\tfrac{\sqrt{p}}{\mathfrak{s}}\right)}{5|\bm{\delta}|_{2}^{2}}\right)\;\geq\;\frac{1}{4}.

Theorem
1
provides the minimax lower bounds on both the detection boundary and the rate of estimation for the change point problem in (
1

\bm{\Sigma}=\mathbf{I}
, the sparsity is determined by
\mathfrak{s}=|\bm{\Sigma}^{1/2}\bm{\delta}|_{0}=|\bm{\delta}|_{0}=\mathfrak{s}_{\delta}

\mathfrak{s}<\sqrt{p}

(

Cho et al., 2025

. Notably, one striking observation in the dense regime is that the minimax rate of estimation becomes a trivial one when

Remark 2.1
.

In the high-dimensional change point detection literature, the phase transition with respect to sparsity has been noted in the context of mean change detection, see
Enikeeva and Harchaoui, 2019
,
Liu et al., 2021
and
Pilliat et al., 2023

Theorem

is a first of its kind for the linear model setting where the cross-sectional dependence is present in

(whose expectation is piecewise constant as
\mathsf{E}(\mathbf{x}_{t}Y_{t})=\bm{\Sigma}\bm{\beta}_{0}+\bm{\Sigma}\bm{\delta}\cdot\mathbb{I}_{\{t\geq\theta+1\}}

Upper bounds

3.1
Sparse regime

Cho et al., 2025
propose McScan, a data segmentation method for the multiple change point problem in linear models.
Motivated by the observation that
\mathsf{E}(\mathbf{x}_{t}Y_{t})
contains the information about local parameter vector, it searches for change points by scanning the local covariance between
Y_{t}
and
\mathbf{x}_{t}
.
Avoiding the costly computation of local parameter estimates via
\ell_{1}
-regularision, it is highly efficient computationally and moreover, attains better statistical efficiency over a wider class of change point problems when compared to the state-of-the-art, by relaxing the conditions of exact sparsity imposed on the regression or differential parameters.

In the AMOC setting of (
1
), we consider the detector statistic

\displaystyle\bar{T}_{k}=|\mathbf{X}^{\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{Y}}(k)|_{\infty}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{k(n-k)}{n}}\left|\frac{1}{n-k}\sum_{t=k+1}^{n}\mathbf{x}_{t}Y_{t}-\frac{1}{k}\sum_{t=1}^{k}\mathbf{x}_{t}Y_{t}\right|_{\infty},

(3)

where
\mathbf{X}=[\mathbf{x}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{n}]^{\top}
and
\widetilde{\mathbf{Y}}(k)=\bigl(\widetilde{Y}_{1}(k),\ldots,\widetilde{Y}_{n}(k)\bigr)^{\top}
with

\displaystyle\widetilde{Y}_{t}(k)

\displaystyle=-\sqrt{\frac{n-k}{nk}}Y_{t}\cdot\mathbb{I}_{\{t\leq k\}}+\sqrt{\frac{k}{n(n-k)}}Y_{t}\cdot\mathbb{I}_{\{t>k\}}.

(4)

We propose to evaluate
\bar{T}_{k}
at strategically selected locations for the detection and estimation of the change point (if any).
For this, we adopt the advanced optimistic search proposed by
Kovács et al., 2024
, see Algorithm
1
for the generic pseudo code applicable with any detector statistic denoted by
V_{k}
.
It first evaluates
V_{k}
on a dyadic grid denoted by
\mathcal{D}
, for a given trimming parameter
\varpi
, and identifies the maximiser
k^{*}
.
If
V_{k}
does not exceed some threshold
\zeta
, we quit the algorithm while if
V_{k}>\zeta
, we perform a divide-and-conquer-type search through calling the function
OS
over the interval determined with
k^{*}
and its neighbours on the dyadic grid.
Through this, we iteratively compare
V_{k}
at dynamically selected search locations based on prior evaluations and discard the sections of the data that are not likely to contain any change point.
Replacing the conventional exhaustive search, this adaptive strategy significantly reduces the total computational complexity from linear to logarithmic, while maintaining the statistical efficiency.

Remark 4.1
.

Adapting to unknown sparsity in estimation and inference has been actively explored in the high-dimensional statistics literature.
On the change point front,
Wang and Feng, 2023
establish the distributions of max- and sum-type statistics for testing for a change in the mean of high-dimensional panel data, as well as their asymptotic independence, which enables sparsity-adaptive testing.
For this problem, aggregation based on a cross-sectional grid has also been considered for change point detection
(
Enikeeva and Harchaoui, 2019
;
Liu et al., 2021
;
Pilliat et al., 2023
;

;
Moen et al., 2024

.
For linear models,
Verzelen and Gassiat, 2018
propose a sparsity-adaptive estimator of the explained variance which achieves optimality by combining a quadratic form of the response and the square-root Lasso estimator.

Remark 4.2
.

The change point problem in linear models can be viewed as a special case of the change point problem in covariances, which allows us to place the performance of our proposed methods in a context. This is particularly fruitful in elucidating the role of terms such as

and

Compared to the state-of-the-art methods
(
Wang et al., 2021a
;

Moen, 2024
)
that target different types of changes in covariances, our OcScan consistently achieves equal or superior performance across a variety of structural settings. For instance, OcScan attains a sharper detection boundary than the projection-based method of
Wang et al., 2021a
, with an improvement factor of

Runtime and memory

We analyse the computational complexity of our methods in terms of both runtime and memory. As a pre-processing step, we compute and store the following quantities:

\displaystyle a_{0}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{t=1}^{n}|\mathbf{x}_{t}|_{2}^{2},

(13a)

\displaystyle r_{k}

\displaystyle:=\sum_{t=1}^{k}Y_{t}^{2}\text{ \ and \ }{\bm{S}}_{k}:=\sum_{t=1}^{k}\mathbf{x}_{t}Y_{t}\quad\text{for}\quad k\in[n],

Proposition 10

Appendix A
Connection to the covariance change detection problems

The change point problem in linear models can be viewed as a special case of changes in covariances. Specifically, the AMOC model (
1
) is equivalent to

\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{x}_{t}\\
Y_{t}\end{bmatrix}\sim_{\mathrm{ind.}}\begin{cases}\mathcal{N}_{p+1}\left(\mathbf{0},\bm{\Sigma}_{0}\right)&\text{for \ }1\leq t\leq\theta,\\
\mathcal{N}_{p+1}\left(\mathbf{0},\bm{\Sigma}_{1}\right)&\text{for \ }\theta+1\leq t\leq n,\end{cases}\quad\text{ with }\quad\bm{\Sigma}_{i}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\Sigma}&\bm{\Sigma}\bm{\beta}_{i}\\
\bm{\beta}_{i}^{\top}\bm{\Sigma}&\bm{\beta}_{i}^{\top}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{\beta}_{i}+\sigma^{2}\end{bmatrix}.

This perspective allows us to draw connections with the literature on covariance change detection in high dimensions.

Wang et al., 2021a
proposed a projection-based binary segmentation method and established its consistency.
Their Assumption 3 on detection requirement translates to our setting as

\displaystyle\Delta\kappa^{2}\gtrsim B^{2}p\log(n)

(A.1)

where
B=\max(\|\bm{\Sigma}\|,\Psi^{2})
and
\kappa=\|\bm{\Sigma}_{1}-\bm{\Sigma}_{0}\|
, which satisfies

\displaystyle\max(|\bm{\Sigma}\bm{\delta}|_{2}^{2},|\bm{\beta}_{0}^{\top}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{\beta}_{0}-\bm{\beta}_{1}^{\top}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{\beta}_{1}|^{2})\leq\kappa^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2\max(|\bm{\Sigma}\bm{\delta}|_{2}^{2},|\bm{\beta}_{0}^{\top}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{\beta}_{0}-\bm{\beta}_{1}^{\top}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{\beta}_{1}|^{2}).

To ease comparison, assume that
\mathsf{Var}(Y_{t})
remains invariant before and after the change, i.e.

\bm{\beta}_{0}^{\top}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{\beta}_{0}=\bm{\beta}_{1}^{\top}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{\beta}_{1}.

(A.2)

Ignoring logarithmic factors, our detection requirement (
5
) is weaker than (
A.1
) by a factor of
p/\min(\|\bm{\Sigma}\|\sigma_{*}^{-1}\mathfrak{s},\mathfrak{s}_{*})
, with
\mathfrak{s}_{*}=|\bm{\Sigma}\bm{\delta}|_{0}
, and (
8
) is weaker by a factor of
\sqrt{p}
.
Thus, with OcScan (
Section

4.1
), we achieve an improvement by a factor of

\displaystyle\frac{p}{\min(\|\bm{\Sigma}\|\sigma_{*}^{-1}\mathfrak{s},\,\mathfrak{s}_{*},\,\sqrt{p})}.

It is important to note that the requirement (
A.1
) is minimax optimal, up to
\log(n)
, for the covariance change point problem in general
(
Wang et al., 2021a
, Lemma 3.1)
.
The improved detection performance of OcScan arises from leveraging the specific structure of the linear model, which also enables the careful analysis of the phase transition with respect to the inherent sparsity
\mathfrak{s}
.
Additionally, the estimation rate achieved by our refined procedure described in
Section

4.2
, nearly matches the minimax lower bound of order
\kappa^{-2}B^{2}
reported in
Wang et al., 2021a
.

Moen, 2024
investigated the minimax optimality in covariance change point testing over the class of parameters that are
d
-sparse
for any
d\in[p]
, defined as

\displaystyle\Lambda^{d}_{\max}(\bm{\Sigma}_{1}-\bm{\Sigma}_{0}):=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{v}:\,|\mathbf{v}|_{2}=1,\\
|\mathbf{v}|_{0}=d\end{subarray}}|\mathbf{v}^{\top}(\bm{\Sigma}_{1}-\bm{\Sigma}_{0})\mathbf{v}|,

which implies that the leading eigenvector of
\bm{\Sigma}_{1}-\bm{\Sigma}_{0}
is sparse with
d
non-zero elements.
Their (computationally intractable) test matches the minimax lower bound on the detection boundary, which requires

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\Lambda^{d}_{\max}(\bm{\Sigma}_{1}-\bm{\Sigma}_{0})}{\Lambda^{d}_{\max}(\bm{\Sigma}_{0})\vee\Lambda^{d}_{\max}(\bm{\Sigma}_{1})}\right)^{2}\Delta\;\gtrsim\;d\log(ep/d)\vee\log\log(8n)

(A.3)

for
some

d
.
Some elementary calculations show that

\displaystyle\Lambda^{d}_{\max}(\bm{\Sigma}_{0})\vee\Lambda^{d}_{\max}(\bm{\Sigma}_{1})\geq\max\left\{\Psi^{2},\max_{i\in[p]}(\bm{\Sigma})_{i,i}\right\},

\displaystyle\Lambda^{d}_{\max}(\bm{\Sigma}_{1}-\bm{\Sigma}_{0})\leq\|\bm{\Sigma}_{1}-\bm{\Sigma}_{0}\|\leq\|\bm{\Sigma}_{1}-\bm{\Sigma}_{0}\|_{F}\lesssim|\bm{\Sigma}\bm{\delta}|_{2}+|\bm{\beta}_{1}^{\top}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{\beta}_{1}-\bm{\beta}_{0}^{\top}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{\beta}_{0}|,

where
(\bm{\Sigma})_{i,i^{\prime}}
denotes the element in the
i
th row and
i^{\prime}
th column of
\bm{\Sigma}
.
To facilitate the comparison between (
A.3
) and our results, we relate the notion of
d
-sparsity and the sparsity of
\bm{\Sigma}\bm{\delta}
, as
d=\mathfrak{s}_{*}+1
.
Then, again assuming (
A.2
), our requirement that either (
5
) or (
8
) holds, is weaker than (
A.3
) by a factor of
\max(d/\sqrt{p},1)
, save logarithmic factors.
That is, the detection boundary of OcScan is more favourable in the scenario where
d\geq\sqrt{p}
.

Finally,
Dette et al., 2022
investigated the covariance change point estimation problem by imposing the sparsity directly on
\bm{\Sigma}_{1}-\bm{\Sigma}_{0}
.
For estimation consistency, their proposed method requires that

|\bm{\Sigma}_{j}|_{\infty}\lesssim 1
,
(\bm{\Sigma}_{j})_{i,i}\lesssim 1
, for
i\in[p+1]
and
j\in\{0,1\}
, and

\min\bigl\{|(\bm{\Sigma}_{0}-\bm{\Sigma}_{1})_{i,j}|:(\bm{\Sigma}_{0}-\bm{\Sigma}_{1})_{i,j}\neq 0\bigr\}\Delta^{2}\;\gtrsim\;n^{3/2}\log(p\vee n),

which translates to
\min\bigl\{|(\bm{\Sigma}\bm{\delta})_{i}|:(\bm{\Sigma}\bm{\delta})_{i}\neq 0\bigr\}\Delta^{2}\gtrsim n^{3/2}\log(p\vee n)
, in our setting.
This is stricter than our requirement (
5
), which is
|\bm{\Sigma}\bm{\delta}|_{\infty}^{2}\Delta\gtrsim\log(p\log(n))
, since
1/\sqrt{\Delta}\leq n^{3/2}/\Delta^{2}
due to
\Delta\leq n
.

Lemma B.2
.

For
\bm{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
, let
\mathsf{P}_{\bm{u}}
denote the centred Gaussian distribution with covariance matrix

\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{I}&\bm{u}\\
\bm{u}^{\top}&\bm{u}^{\top}\bm{u}+\sigma^{2}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{(p+1)\times(p+1)}.

If
|\bm{u}|_{2}=|\bm{v}|_{2}=\kappa<\sigma/\sqrt{2}
, then

\mathsf{E}_{\mathsf{P}_{\bm{0}}}\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathsf{P}_{\bm{u}}}{\mathrm{d}\mathsf{P}_{\bm{0}}}\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathsf{P}_{\bm{v}}}{\mathrm{d}\mathsf{P}_{\bm{0}}}\right)=\left(\left(1-\frac{\langle\bm{u},\bm{v}\rangle}{\sigma^{2}}\right)^{2}-\left(\frac{\kappa^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}\right)^{2}\right)^{-1/2}.

Proof.

It follows from a general result for centred multivariate Gaussians (see e.g. Lemma 11 in
Cai and Guo, 2017
). For completeness, we give below a direct computation:

\displaystyle\mathsf{E}_{\mathsf{P}_{\bm{0}}}\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathsf{P}_{\bm{u}}}{\mathrm{d}\mathsf{P}_{\bm{0}}}\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathsf{P}_{\bm{v}}}{\mathrm{d}\mathsf{P}_{\bm{0}}}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathsf{E}_{\mathsf{P}_{\bm{0}}}\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathsf{P}_{\bm{u}}^{\bm{Y}|\mathbf{x}}\mathrm{d}\mathsf{P}_{\bm{u}}^{\mathbf{x}}}{\mathrm{d}\mathsf{P}_{\bm{0}}^{\bm{Y}|\mathbf{x}}\mathrm{d}\mathsf{P}_{\bm{0}}^{\mathbf{x}}}\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathsf{P}_{\bm{v}}^{\bm{Y}|\mathbf{x}}\mathrm{d}\mathsf{P}_{\bm{v}}^{\mathbf{x}}}{\mathrm{d}\mathsf{P}_{\bm{0}}^{\bm{Y}|\mathbf{x}}\mathrm{d}\mathsf{P}_{\bm{0}}^{\mathbf{x}}}\right)=\mathsf{E}_{\mathsf{P}_{\bm{0}}}\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathsf{P}_{\bm{u}}^{\bm{Y}|\mathbf{x}}}{\mathrm{d}\mathsf{P}_{\bm{0}}^{\bm{Y}|\mathbf{x}}}\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathsf{P}_{\bm{v}}^{\bm{Y}|\mathbf{x}}}{\mathrm{d}\mathsf{P}_{\bm{0}}^{\bm{Y}|\mathbf{x}}}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{det}\left(\mathbf{I}-\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\left(\bm{u}\bm{v}^{\top}+\bm{v}\bm{u}^{\top}\right)\right)^{-1/2}

\displaystyle=\left(\left(1-\frac{\langle\bm{u},\bm{v}\rangle}{\sigma^{2}}\right)^{2}-\left(\frac{\kappa^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}\right)^{2}\right)^{-1/2},

where the last equality can be seen from the fact that

\left(\mathbf{I}-\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\left(\bm{u}\bm{v}^{\top}+\bm{v}\bm{u}^{\top}\right)\right)(\bm{u}\pm\bm{v})=\left(1-\frac{\langle\bm{u},\bm{v}\rangle}{\sigma^{2}}\mp\frac{\kappa^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}\right)(\bm{u}\pm\bm{v}).

∎

Remark B.1
.

Note that in Lemma
B.2
the condition
\kappa<\sigma/\sqrt{2}
is only needed to ensure the invertibility of the matrix
\mathbf{I}-\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\left(\bm{u}\bm{v}^{\top}+\bm{v}\bm{u}^{\top}\right)
. In the special case of
\kappa^{2}+\langle\bm{u},\bm{v}\rangle=\sigma^{2}
, by the same calculation, we have

\mathsf{E}_{\mathsf{P}_{\bm{0}}}\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathsf{P}_{\bm{u}}}{\mathrm{d}\mathsf{P}_{\bm{0}}}\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathsf{P}_{\bm{v}}}{\mathrm{d}\mathsf{P}_{\bm{0}}}\right)=\left(1-\frac{\langle\bm{u},\bm{v}\rangle}{\sigma^{2}}+\frac{\kappa^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}\right)^{-1/2}.

However, if
\kappa^{2}+\langle\bm{u},\bm{v}\rangle>\sigma^{2}
, we have
\mathsf{E}_{\mathsf{P}_{\bm{0}}}\left(\tfrac{\mathrm{d}\mathsf{P}_{\bm{u}}}{\mathrm{d}\mathsf{P}_{\bm{0}}}\tfrac{\mathrm{d}\mathsf{P}_{\bm{v}}}{\mathrm{d}\mathsf{P}_{\bm{0}}}\right)=\infty
.

B.2.3

For notational convenience, we assume that there is no rounding in determining the dyadic search locations and the probe points.
Throughout, we omit the subscript ‘Q’ where there is no confusion.

We verify the claims in the theorem for

\displaystyle c=\sqrt{2}C_{0}+1,\,c_{0}\geq\max(16c,128C_{0}),\,c_{1}\geq 8\sqrt{3}C_{0}\text{ \ and \ }c_{2}\geq 96C_{1}.

Step 0.

Step 1.
