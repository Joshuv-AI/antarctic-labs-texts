The vocal side of empathy: neural correlates of pain perception in spoken complaints
Authors: Maël Mauchand, Jorge L Armony, Marc D Pell
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn the extensive neuroimaging literature on empathy for pain, few studies have investigated how this phenomenon may relate to everyday social situations such as spoken interactions. The present study used functional Magnetic Resonance Imaging (fMRI) to assess how complaints, as vocal expressions of pain, are empathically processed by listeners and how these empathic responses may vary based on speakers’ vocal expression and cultural identity. Twenty-four French participants listened to short utterances describing a painful event, which were either produced in a neutral-sounding or complaining voice by both in-group (French) and out-group (French Canadian) speakers. Results suggest that the perception of suffering from a complaining voice increased activity in the emotional voice areas, composed of voice-sensitive temporal regions interacting with prefrontal cortices and the amygdala. The Salience and Theory of Mind networks, associated with affective and cognitive aspects of empathy, also showed prosody-related activity and specifically correlated with behavioral evaluations of suffering by listeners. Complaints produced by in- vs out-group speakers elicited sensorimotor and default mode activity, respectively, suggesting accent-based changes in empathic perspective. These results, while reaffirming the role of key networks in tasks involving empathy, highlight the importance of vocal expression information and social categorization processes when perceiving another’s suffering during social interactions.Keywords: empathy, fMRI, accent, prosody, social communication

Introduction

Neuroimaging research on empathy—the state resulting from the observation and representation of another’s feeling (Preston and de Waal, 2002)—has identified brain networks and processes associated with the appraisal of suffering in others (de Vignemont and Singer, 2006; Jauniaux et al., 2019). Most of this literature involves participants evaluating the pain of a person they see from images of humans in painful situations (e.g. a hand caught in a door; Fan and Han, 2008; faces expressing pain, Mathur et al., 2010) or in dynamic multimodal settings (e.g. film excerpts, Borja Jimenez et al., 2020; experiencing social exclusion in a cyberball game, Eisenberger, 2012). Here, we sought to shed new light on the neural responses associated with empathic processes to vocal (auditory) signals communicated in everyday speech, such as when people listen to third-party complaints about painful events.

To date, many studies report that witnessing others suffer elicits activity in two critical regions of the salience network: the dorsal anterior cingulate (dACC) and anterior insula (AI) (Mathur et al., 2010; Masten et al., 2011; Preis et al., 2015). This network is sometimes referred to as the ‘pain matrix’ or ‘core empathy network’ because of its involvement in perceiving pain both in the self and in others (Jackson et al., 2006; Fan et al., 2011; Lamm et al., 2011). More generally, dacC and AI tend to be mobilized during the perception of salient and emotionally relevant stimuli that engage the receiver’s attention (Legrain et al., 2011; Perini et al., 2018). In addition, empathy experiments have highlighted the role of areas involved in Theory of Mind (ToM) such as the temporoparietal junction (TPJ) and superior temporal cortex (STC), together with the medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC) and posterior cingulate (PCC) from the default mode network (DMN) (Cheon et al., 2011; Li et al., 2014; Kim et al., 2017). While some consider ToM separate from empathy per se (Vö et al., 2006; Kanske et al., 2015), many define empathy as a dual stream process composed of distinct but inter-related affective (salience) and cognitive (ToM) components (Sessa et al., 2014; Cuff et al., 2016). Less commonly, a somatomotor component of empathy involving the mirror neuron system, somatosensory, motor and pre-motor areas has been described, potentially indicating a more reactive ‘resonance’ of the observer with the pain of the observed (Baird et al., 2011; Borja Jimenez et al., 2020).

Research has long demonstrated that the empathy network is modulated by social proximity, affiliation and group belonging (Cheon et al., 2010; Hollan, 2012). Neurocognitive investigations of sociocultural differences in empathy to another person’s pain have yielded mixed findings: some have reported increased in-group preference in affective but not cognitive responses (Xu et al., 2009; Fourie et al., 2017), group biases in ToM (Mathur et al., 2010; Cheon et al., 2011) or even culture-dependent empathic networks (Mathur et al., 2012). These differences may be explained by the way culture, familiarity and other factors are defined across experiments (Avenanti et al., 2005; Contreras-Huerta et al., 2014; Sheng et al., 2014), yielding context-dependent results (Xiang et al., 2018; Schurz et al., 2021). Despite this variability, the scope of empathy research remains narrow, as much of the literature is restricted to the first-hand perception of pain in others (i.e. directly witnessing a painful event). This focus leaves out an important context for eliciting empathy in more mundane, daily interactions in which speakers report and vocally express their pain or suffering, such as when people complain.

Complaining is a ubiquitous social act whose main purpose is to elicit empathy to affiliate and strengthen bonds with the listener (Drew, 1998; Acuña-Ferreira, 2002; Edwards, 2005). Beyond verbally describing painful events, complaints communicate suffering through the speaker’s tone of voice or prosody; acoustic modulation of the speaker’s pitch, voice quality and rhythm provide salient affective cues that reconstruct their emotive state to share it with others (Selting, 2010; Rao, 2013; Mauchand and Pell, 2021). In the brain, the processing of affective prosody engages cortico-subcortical networks centered around the emotional voice areas (EVAs), comprising the primary auditory cortex (PAC) and parts of the STC situated postero-laterally to the PAC (Ethofer et al., 2012; Schirmer, 2018; Mauchand and Zhang, 2023). These regions interact with the amygdala during attentional capture of emotionally relevant sounds, as well as the orbitofrontal cortex (OFC) for in-depth analysis of the affective meaning of vocal signals (Frühholz et al., 2016; Grandjean, 2021). In addition, the AI appears to be specifically involved when subjects actively empathize with emotional or painful voices, while ACC activity remains unclear (Lang et al., 2011; Kanel et al., 2019).

Although no fMRI experiment has been conducted using spoken complaints, there are strong indications that prosody plays a critical role in the neurocognition of empathy (Aziz-Zadeh et al., 2010; Regenbogen et al., 2012; Meconi et al., 2018). Recent neurophysiological evidence from event-related potentials shows that as people evaluate a speaker’s suffering when listening to third-party complaints in French, statements produced in an emotive, complaining voice elicit a stronger early brain potential in the 175- to 450-ms time window (peaking 240 ms after speech onset) when compared to similar statements expressed in a neutral manner (Mauchand and Pell, 2022b). It was argued that this neural response reflects the rapid detection and registration of the high salience of complaining vocal signals, which simulate the acoustic properties of pain vocalizations, triggering empathy-related processes as listeners process the linguistic content of the complaint (Mauchand and Pell, 2022b).

However, consistent with the idea that complaining is a cultural ritual influenced by social proximity and identity (Boxer, 1993; Katriel, 1991), the ERP data further revealed that the nature and time course of empathic responses to spoken complaints depended on the speaker’s accent (Mauchand and Pell, 2022b). Listeners rapidly infer information about a speaker’s identity from their accent (e.g. geographical/cultural background), which is known to produce various ‘downstream’ effects on the semantic encoding and socio-emotional evaluation of utterances (Van Berkum et al., 2008; Adank et al., 2015; Jiang et al., 2018, 2020; Hernández et al., 2019; Laukka and Elfenbein, 2020). In Mauchand and Pell (2022b), French listeners judged complaints expressed by speakers with either a French (in-group) or Québécois (French Canadian/out-group) accent; the results showed that group membership triggered distinct affective and cognitive responses linked to empathy. In-group complaints selectively increased early motivational responses compared to all other utterance types, whereas out-group complaints elicited slower, more effortful interpretation processes. While further study and replication of these results are needed, they underscore the critical importance of both the speaker’s expressive behavior and their (vocal) identity in empathy-related brain processes (Mauchand and Pell, 2022b).

To continue progress in this vital but unexplored area in the literature, this study employed fMRI to identify the neural systems at play during the empathic processing of spoken complaints. Our primary objective was to compare how emotive (‘complaining’) voices, which are socially constructed to feign the speaker’s pain to trigger empathy in the listener, engage the neural system relative to complaints produced in a neutral tone and how this brain activity relates to explicit evaluation of speaker suffering. As a secondary objective, we investigated whether these processes are influenced by the cultural identity/proximity of the speaker to the listener (French participants listened to complaints expressed by in-group/French and regional out-group/Québécois speakers). It was predicted that complaining prosody would increase activation of the EVAs and their connected network when compared to neutral statements, as well as induce both affective and cognitive empathic responses within the salience and ToM networks. These patterns of activity should be modulated by initial categorization of the speaker’s culture (accent); this could increase stimulus processing demands for out-group accents in voice-sensitive areas (Adank et al., 2015; Jiang et al., 2018). In addition, we hypothesized that affective (and potentially cognitive) responses to vocal signals of pain would be enhanced for in-group speakers, thus engaging distinct processing pathways in the empathic appraisal of vocal signals (Mathur et al., 2010; Cheon et al., 2011; Mauchand and Pell, 2022b).

Methods

Participants

Twenty-four French-speaking adults were recruited in the region of Montréal, Canada (13 females, age: M = 26.04, s.d. = 3.99). All participants were born and had lived in France until they were at least 18 years of age; to limit habituation to the regional Québécois accent (Sumner and Samuel, 2009), participants had lived less than 2 years in the province of Québec. All participants were right-handed and reported no history of major psychiatric or neurological illness or speech/hearing problems. Participants voluntarily consented to take part and received a $50 compensation upon completion of the study that was ethically approved by the Faculty of Medicine Institutional Review Board (McGill University).

Stimuli

Stimuli were 160 short French utterances (M = 1.29 s, s.d. = 0.29 s) from a corpus created and validated in two previous studies (Mauchand and Pell, in review; 2021). The utterances were statements describing a situation in which a third-party agent caused the suffering of the speaker (e.g. ‘Il a dit que j’étais stupide/He said I was stupid’). Statements were then manipulated to vary across two orthogonal, binary factors of Prosody and Accent. For the Prosody manipulation, each statement was produced twice, first in a neutral, fact-stating manner (Neutral condition) and then with a complaining tone of voice (Complaint condition). For the Accent manipulation, utterances were produced by four French (in-group condition) and four Québécois (out-group condition) speakers. Each group of speakers was composed of two men and two women with acting experience, matched for age and education. Recordings were digitally captured in a sound-attenuated chamber with a high-quality head-mounted microphone onto a Tascam recorder (sampling rate of 44.1 kHz, 16-bit, mono, .wav format). They were then edited in Praat (Boersma and van Heuven, 2001) into short .wav audio files and normalized to a peak intensity of 70 dB.

The stimuli used in the present study were selected from an original set of 672 perceptually validated utterances (see Mauchand and Pell, 2021 for details). Utterance sets that were most representative of complaints or neutral productions according to listeners from each cultural group were selected. In total, 160 utterances were presented in the fMRI study (2 Accents × 4 Speakers × 10 Statements × 2 Prosodies). Previous analyses of the stimuli revealed that complaints are acoustically (and perceptually) differentiated from their neutral counterparts across several affect-related acoustic parameters, including increased mean pitch and pitch variability and changes in voice quality, which denote the heightened emotivity of complaining speakers (Mauchand and Pell, 2021, 2022b).

fMRI scanning parameters

The fMRI scanning was performed at the Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI) on a 3T Siemens retro-fitted Prisma system with a 64-channel head coil. Functional images were acquired across four runs of 6:26 min using a multi-band accelerated pulse with a factor of 6. Each functional run continuously acquired 405 volumes (72 slices per volume, interleaved acquisition; field-of-view = 208 × 208 mm2, matrix = 104 × 104, voxel size = 2 × 2 × 2 mm3; Repetition Time (TR) = 927 ms; Time to Echo (TE) = 30 ms). The first five scans of the run were discarded due to T1 saturation. After the first two runs, a high-resolution T1-weighted image was acquired (voxel size 0.9 mm3, 208 slices, TR = 2300 ms, TE = 2.32 ms) for anatomical co-registration and normalization.

fMRI task procedure

In each run, 40 stimuli (10 In-group/Neutral, 10 In-group/Complaint, 10 Out-group/Neutral, 10 Out-group/Complaint) together with 10 null events were presented in a pseudo-random, fully balanced order (equal number of first-order transitions between each of the five event types), such that the same speaker would not be heard more than two utterances in a row to avoid potential carry-over effects. Each stimulus was presented only once to each participant. Stimuli were presented binaurally via fMRI-adapted, sound-attenuating earbuds covered by foam pads to minimize scanner noise. Sound levels were tested and adjusted for participant comfort during a practice run presenting four utterances (not included in the main task). During the presentation of each stimulus, participants viewed a fixation cross on the screen. Immediately after utterance presentation, participants were prompted via an image to evaluate ‘How hurt the speaker felt’ (‘À quel point cette personne se sent-elle blessée?’). They responded using a four-button box at their right hand, ranging from ‘Not at all’ (‘Pas du tout’) on the leftmost button to ‘Very much’ (‘Énormément’) on the right. Participants were required to answer within a 3.5-s window (Response Time: M = 1.47 s, s.d. = 0.72 s). Jitter was naturally introduced between trials by leaving a black screen during the time remaining after the response, with an added fixed interstimulus interval of 2.5 s. During null events, participants viewed a fixation cross for 3 s with no auditory stimulus or instruction presented. An example trial is illustrated in Figure 1.

fMRI pre-processing and analysis

fMRI volumes were pre-processed and analyzed using SPM12 (www.fil.ion.ucl.ac.uk/spm/). Functional images were spatially realigned to the first volume of the third run, which immediately followed the anatomical scan, and normalized to the MNI 152 template. Images were then smoothed using a 5-mm Full Width at Half Max kernel. A whole-brain, full-factorial univariate general linear model was applied voxel-wise to the blood-oxygen-level-dependent signal. Single-subject data were analyzed at the first level by modeling each trial a boxcar with the onset and duration of the stimulus convolved with the canonical hemodynamic response function, with Accent and Prosody as interacting two-level factors. Null events were not included in the analysis. The perceptual rating was included as a parametric modulator, modeled as a zero-duration event at the onset of the stimuli, to assess activity directly related to participants’ judgments. To account for potential effects of head movement between volumes, motion parameters obtained during realignment were entered as regressors in the model. Mixed-effect analyses were conducted at a second level by performing t-tests on each contrast acquired during the first-level analysis of all subjects. Significance levels of resulting images were thresholded at P < 0.001 voxel-wise and Family-Wise-Error (FWE) corrected at P < 0.05 cluster-wise.

Results

Behavioral results

Behavioral responses (i.e. ratings of speaker suffering) were fitted into a linear mixed-effect model using lme4 (Bates et al., 2015) and lmerTest (Kuznetsova et al., 2017) packages in R software (R Core Team, 2018). Prosody (neutral/complaining) and Accent (in-group/out-group) were entered as interacting fixed factors; Participant and Statement were added as random factors, and Prosody-by-Participant, Accent-by-Statement and Prosody-by-Statement were added as random slopes. In-group Neutral utterances were chosen as the intercept in the model. The analysis revealed a significant effect of Prosody (β = 0.97, standard error (se) = 0.14, t(15.87) = 6.98, P < 0.001), showing that speakers were judged to be suffering more when producing statements in a complaining vs neutral tone of voice. There was also a marginal effect of Accent (β = 0.40, se = 0.18, t(6.77) = 2.27, P = 0.059), suggesting a trend for participants to rate Out-group speakers as suffering more than In-group speakers overall. Discussion

The present results reveal distributed patterns of brain activity underlying the processing of speaker suffering from spoken complaints. Beyond extending knowledge of how empathy-related brain areas respond to expressive speech acts, results highlight that emotive utterances reliably evoke processes of affect sharing and mentalizing driven mainly by speech prosody, although these processes are sensitive to speech cues referring to the speaker’s identity.

Prosody-driven empathic responses

Complaining prosody, as an ostensive emotive auditory signal, activated the EVAs bilaterally: the greater acoustic expressivity of complaints compared to neutral utterances increased activation in primary auditory cortices, as well as secondary auditory processing across the entire STC (Schirmer, 2018; Mauchand and Zhang, 2023). Co-activation of auditory cortices with bilateral amygdala suggests that complaints are not just acoustically salient but also emotionally relevant to the listener (Pannese et al., 2016). Meanwhile, the combined involvement of amygdala with STC, insula and orbitofrontal regions is known to be associated with in-depth analysis of the affective significance of emotional signals and their contextual implications (Grandjean, 2021).

Increased activation of these brain regions implies that emotive prosody acts as a communicative device to elicit the involvement of the listener in complaining interactions (Caffi and Janney, 1994; Selting, 2010). For the speaker, it allows the reconstruction of a strong feeling (suffering) to serve as a communicative act directing the listener’s affective state and behavior (Scarantino, 2017). For the listener, it serves as social information that can be processed in a context- and task-relevant manner (Van Kleef, 2009); in this case, a signal to empathize with the speaker. This finding complements recent neurophysiological data which highlighted the rapid uptake and neural differentiation of prosodic information in a similar set of complaining and neutral utterances, a process that seems to initially prioritize emotive vocal information in complaints (Mauchand and Pell, 2022b). Together, these results underline the critical role of prosody in conveying motivationally relevant, affective information that is immediately available to guide perception and affective cognition.

Neural mechanisms for evaluating prosody, possibly linked to empathy, extended beyond voice-sensitive areas, as indicated by modulated activity in the ToM network: the left TPJ was more activated for complaints, while prefrontal regions and precuneus were more deactivated for neutral utterances. The ToM network is typically involved in mentalizing and intention processing efforts (Lombardo et al., 2010; Young et al., 2010; Molenberghs et al., 2016). It has been described as a critical network for understanding the feelings of others and to reason about the causes and consequences of those feelings (Schurz et al., 2021). Here, the TPJ in particular appeared to play a critical role in mediating the effect of complaining prosody on participant’s impressions of speaker suffering: complaint-related activation in the left TPJ significantly overlapped with its parametric involvement in behavioral responses. While the right TPJ is traditionally more associated with cognitive empathy (Hétu et al., 2012; Mai et al., 2016), the left TPJ has also been shown to directly affect behavioral performance in ToM-related tasks similar to the present experimental design (Samson et al., 2004; Spunt and Adolphs, 2014; Kanske et al., 2015). Its co-activation with the amygdala in guiding behavior based on prosody also suggests that forming impressions about a complaining speaker requires inferential processing, not only about the affective content of speech but also about how these cues are linked to the speaker’s mental state.

The perception of suffering from speech cues also correlated with activity in the dACC and AI, reaffirming and extending the central role of the salience network in the perception of pain (Rotge et al., 2015; Jauniaux et al., 2019). Whereas the ToM network is often associated with cognitive empathy, salience network activity may reflect a more affective component of the phenomenon, driven by a vicarious experience of other’s suffering (Fan et al., 2011). This experience affected behavioral ratings, such that increased signal in the network was associated with higher ratings of speaker pain, as has been reported in several studies. Complaining prosody appeared to partially activate the salience network when compared to neutral speech, marginally overlapping with the parametric modulation of the left AI. This suggestive finding is in line with previous evidence highlighting the role of the AI in the behavioral evaluation of affective prosody (Lang et al., 2011; Sachs et al., 2018; Kanel et al., 2019). Still, it underlines that while the salience network is critical in the empathic processing of suffering from voices, its sensitivity is not limited to prosodic signals and may interface with other characteristics of the attended stimuli. In our study, all utterances described an event that involves speaker suffering in various situations and from different speakers. The parametric activation of the salience network as a function of pain evaluation reveals its sensitivity to stimuli that are salient in the speaker’s perspective, rather than specific unidimensional manipulations (Perini et al., 2018).

Effects of speaker identity on empathic appraisal

Although speakers’ prosody largely governed behavioral decisions and activated ‘classic’ emotion processing and empathy networks regardless of culture, accent-based contrasts revealed noteworthy differences. These patterns strongly suggest that perceived group characteristics (in-group vs out-group speaker) alter how vocal signals are appraised and evoke different forms of empathic perspectives in the listener.

Building on the dual definition of affective (salience network) and cognitive (ToM) empathy, most studies investigating empathy from a cross-cultural perspective have reported in-group biases in affective empathy, associated with increased dACC and AI activity for in-group pain (Cheon et al., 2010). Interestingly, in the present study, listening to in-group speakers did not yield greater brain activity in these areas overall, and neither did in-group complaints compared to out-group complaints. One possible explanation for these differences is that every utterance presented in our study was a description of a painful scenario. The presence of a constant, objective cue for judging speaker pain based on the semantic message could have diminished prosody- and culture-based effects within the salience network. Instead, here, in-group voices activated the supplementary motor cortex (SMA), a region adjacent to the ACC. Often grouped with mid-cingulate and ACC, the SMA is strongly associated with affective empathy and has been reported to show greater activity when subjects exhibit racial biases in response to other’s suffering (Xu et al., 2009; Sheng et al., 2014). With regard to voice perception, the SMA is critically involved in sensorimotor representations of speech (Froese and González-Grandón, 2019) and high-arousal emotions (Wildgruber et al., 2002; Scott et al., 2010) and appears to be sensitive to accent-based in-group preferences (Jiang et al., 2018).

The idea that utterances produced by perceived in-group (vs out-group) members involved sensorimotor mechanisms to a greater extent is further suggested by widespread bilateral activity across motor, primary somatosensory and association cortices. While these areas are not usually associated with acoustic processing, they may suggest a form of mirroring of the speaker’s described experience (Baird et al., 2011). Such sensorimotor activity has indeed been reported in empathy when seeing (Avenanti et al., 2005), reading, (Yang and Shu, 2014) and hearing pain (Froese and González-Grandón, 2019). Not limited to direct action perception (Landmann et al., 2011), it does seem sensitive to in-group biases (Avenanti et al., 2010) and stimulus familiarity and preference (Pereira et al., 2011). The description of speaker suffering in a familiar accent may thus facilitate the vicarious engagement of the listener with that experience, although this does not directly affect their impressions of suffering (Chen et al., 2012). This cross-modal sensory involvement appears to extend to visual mental imagery, as suggested by enhanced activity in lingual gyrus and visual cortices (Spence and Deroy, 2013; Yang and Shu, 2014). Additionally, the distribution of the activation pattern for in-group accents across the dorsal attention network could exemplify more reactive, stimulus-driven attention, in opposition to the default mode modulations shown for out-group utterances.

Indeed, the processing of an out-group accent clearly showed reduced deactivation activity in the mPFC and PCC, two key nodes of the DMN together with the right TPJ. Although DMN functioning is traditionally associated with resting-state activity and reduced cognitive demands (Greicius et al., 2003), growing evidence points to its role in social cognition and ToM (Li et al., 2014; Schurz et al., 2021). Engaging the DMN may reflect self-referential processing (Gusnard et al., 2001), a critical function when forming impressions of others’ personality and to predict their behavior (Hassabis et al., 2014; Knyazev et al., 2021). While DMN is often influenced by culture, many studies actually report greater deactivation of DMN regions for out-group compared to in-group empathy (Mathur et al., 2012; Cheon et al., 2013), mentalizing (Adams et al., 2010) as well as during the (non-empathic) processing of accented speech (Hernández et al., 2019), a pattern usually attributed to a more cognitively demanding processing of out-group signals.

The fact that we observed the opposite pattern suggests that out-group accents in our study did not exact comprehension-related processing costs but may have instead triggered different types of empathic thinking than for members of the in-group. PCC, mPFC and TPJ are known to be involved during social stereotyping (Quadflieg and Macrae, 2011; Contreras et al., 2012), potentially reflecting the integration of associative memory and propositional beliefs about in- and out-group members (Gawronski and Bodenhausen, 2006; Mauchand and Pell, 2022a). This introspective processing may have been enhanced by the social context of our participants: as recent French immigrants in Québec, their exposure and integration to the out-group may have affected their empathic reactions and biases differently than in traditional in- vs out-group experimental settings (Zhou et al., 2022), a difference also visible in their behavioral decisions (Mauchand and Pell, 2022a). Interestingly, default mode involvement was complemented by increased activity in STC, AC and right amygdala, with enhanced right AC activity specifically for out-group complaints, a pattern commonly reported when processing unfamiliar accents (Adank et al., 2015; Jiang et al., 2018). Localized changes within the EVAs could partly reflect differences in the acoustic-affective nature of out-group utterances, given that complaints expressed by Québécois speakers tend to exhibit increased pitch variation and are perceived as angrier and more surprised than French complaints (Mauchand and Pell, in review; 2021).

The inclusion of two cultural groups in our study shows that while emotive prosody is the most potent signal governing how listeners empathize from spoken complaints, accent perception entails group-specific appraisal of speech signals in an empathy-related task. One can speculate that in-group perception activates a dorsal stream, characterized by more reactive and immersive empathic processing and possibly including sensorimotor resonance and mental imagery of the pain expressed in the spoken statements. Meanwhile, out-group processing involves a ventral stream associated with more elaborative, mentalizing efforts based on self-referential intention processing and stereotype-based inference. This distinction shows an important overlap with other perspective-based models of empathy, which distinguish between the ‘how’ (dorsal stream) and the ‘why’ (ventral stream) of mentalizing (Spunt and Adolphs, 2014). Similarly, adopting an involved empathic perspective has been shown to activate sensorimotor areas, while a more detached perspective preferentially activates TPJ and mPFC (Borja Jimenez et al., 2020). Our results suggest that the perception of group membership (accent) can trigger this change of perspective and associated processing routines and even somewhat alter the behavioral outcome of pain evaluation (Jiang et al., 2020).

Limitations and future directions

While providing novel insights into the phenomenon of empathy, interpretation of these data is limited by the relatively small participant sample and asymmetrical conditions, as only French (and not Québécois) participants were tested. Replicating these results with a larger sample and comparing other cultural groups would increase their robustness. In addition, limits arise from the complexity of complaints as a socially constructed communication signal and their relationship to pain/suffering, making it difficult to compare how the present neural findings relate specifically to pain perception, despite our efforts to carefully define task demands and to validate the stimuli. Future studies may be better able to link the brain activity we observed to the perception of real pain and/or related empathic processes in new contexts and using other modalities.

Conclusion

Our study sheds new light on how people likely empathize with others using a ‘thin slice’ of everyday social interactions as an example: third-party complaints. Consistent with the speaker’s intentions, complainers who use highly expressive and emotive voices elicit strong empathic responses in the brain, activating EVAs and the Salience and ToM networks, which correlated with listener impressions of speaker suffering. However, the speaker’s identity (accent) also appears to guide the listener’s perspective as the brain appraises signals of pain (although only marginally influencing perceptual impressions of suffering): in-group speech enacted brain mechanisms for generating a more involved, sensorimotor perspective, whereas out-group complaints seemed to engage mechanisms for deriving a more detached, mentalizing perspective of out-group pain. Taken together, these results point to a complex array of sensory, affective and cognitive mechanisms that underlie the phenomenon of empathy in vocal communication, while showing that these responses are shaped by the characteristics of affective signals, social–relational factors and contextual constraints.
