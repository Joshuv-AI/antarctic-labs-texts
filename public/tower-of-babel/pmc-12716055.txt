A cost-benefit analysis of adult immunization programs across ten countries: Modeling the socioeconomic value of immunization for older populations compared to no vaccination
Authors: Sulayman Chowdhury, Simon Brassel, Hania El Banhawi, Eleanor Bell, Margherita Neri, Lotte Steuten
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTGlobally aging demographics necessitate proactive public health interventions like adult immunization programs, but comprehensive estimates of their socioeconomic value are lacking or narrow in perspective, risking suboptimal decision-making. To estimate the socioeconomic value of adult immunization programs for influenza, pneumococcal disease, herpes zoster, and respiratory syncytial virus across ten countries. Benefit-cost analysis using static disease models and a reference case framework to quantify net monetary benefits of immunization programs using different monetization approaches for non-monetary benefits. Australia, Brazil, France, Germany, Italy, Japan, Poland, South Africa, Thailand, US. Based on the lowest and highest valuations of non-monetary benefits, across all immunization programs, the mean benefit-cost ratios (BCRs) ranged from 5 to 19, suggesting substantial positive returns. Mortality-focused disease programs like influenza (BCRs 6–22) and pneumococcal disease (BCRs 15–33) showed the highest returns. The emerging respiratory syncytial virus program also demonstrated high potential positive returns (BCRs 6–16). Herpes zoster programs which primarily affect quality of life also yielded positive returns with BCRs of 2–4. Across all data points, 95% of estimated BCRs exceeded the cost-neutral 1.0 threshold. Adult immunization programs for influenza, pneumococcal disease, respiratory syncytial virus and herpes zoster consistently show positive returns on investment across a diversity of countries. Funding these programs provides long-term benefits and may help to mitigate upcoming global demographic challenges.KEYWORDS: Cost-benefit, economic evaluation, vaccines, public health, healthy aging, modeling, international

Introduction

Today’s global health and social care systems are under increasing strain. Demographic changes and aging societies1,2 are driving incidence and prevalence rates of non-communicable diseases.3 This compounds the unprecedented demand for health services post-pandemic which is further fueled directly and indirectly by past and ongoing “tripledemic” waves of COVID-19, influenza, and Respiratory Syncytial Virus (RSV).4

In such a situation, prevention including adult immunization offers great potential value. For example, childhood immunization programs in low- and middle-income countries have demonstrated a high return on investment,5,6 with estimates of over $50 in societal return per $1 spent. Furthermore, since 1974, vaccination has averted 154 million deaths, of which 146 million were among children younger than 5 y,7 and a recent study showing 1.1 million child deaths prevented in the US from vaccination programs.8 While these numbers are objectively impressive, most of the value of vaccination literature focuses either on younger populations or on health technology assessments (HTA) from a narrow health system perspective. This leaves a knowledge gap regarding the broader value that adult immunization programs may return to society on an international scale.

This knowledge gap risks suboptimal decision-making and potentially underfunding of adult immunization programs. For example, in the U.K., it has been shown that existing narrow value frameworks systematically undervalue the existing vaccine pipeline,9 a pipeline which is increasing in scope of target diseases and robustness.10 Underfunding of adult immunization and limited coverage reduces population access and consequently may exacerbate future health system strain. Therefore, taking a broader perspective in economic evaluation is essential when estimating the socioeconomic value and returns of these programs,11 because broader value elements such as productivity costs can be included.

This study aims to fill this knowledge gap. We estimate the societal costs and benefits associated with influenza, pneumococcal disease (PD), herpes zoster (HZ), and RSV in ten countries, where publicly provided. While for some of these diseases, national immunization programs exist in most of the countries in focus, gaps remain, particularly for vaccines against HZ and RSV. We apply a reference case-based benefit-cost analysis (BCA) framework12 to quantify and compare the broader net monetary benefits of current or emerging future adult immunization programs across this diverse set of countries. This analysis can help guide investment and policy decisions about adult immunization programs.

Materials and methods

Program selection & study design

Program selection

We conducted a BCA to evaluate the societal value of existing, publicly provided, adult immunization programs looking across ten countries: Australia, Brazil, France, Germany, Italy, Japan, Poland, South Africa, Thailand, and the United States. Country selection was determined through a deliberate process to balance representation according to geography and World Bank income group classification13 with data availability and study complexity. This resulted in a selection of ten countries of which 70% represented upper income groups and 30% middle income groups. All included immunization programs are outlined in Table 1. The analysis compared vaccination scenarios implementing immunization programs for influenza, PD, HZ and RSV to a scenario of no vaccination.

Immunization program specifications

Immunization program specifications, such as age of vaccination and which product/dosage to model, were determined by the respective government’s recommendations/provisions at the time of analysis (2022–early 2023) and are shown in Table 1.

Vaccine characteristics were modeled using efficacy data for the specification of the PD, HZ and RSV models and effectiveness data for influenza. To account for the seasonal effectiveness of influenza, we averaged effectiveness data published by the Centers for Disease Control (CDC) in the US for the quadrivalent influenza vaccine14 and applied it, to maintain consistency, across all nations.

The exception for inclusion criteria was RSV, where we modeled emerging programs based on the recommendations in countries where there was a public recommendation and price for both RSV vaccines, respiratory syncytial virus prefusion F protein – based candidate vaccine (RSVPreF3 OA) and RSVpreF, which could be identified at the time of data collection. This was the case for the US,15,16 where the RSV program had already been initiated, and Germany,17 where both vaccines were approved, and prices were publicly available. Between both products, we averaged the vaccine efficacy due to the uncertainty of vaccine product usage. Detailed vaccine product characteristics and references can be found in Appendix A.

Disease model structure

The general model used a life-table modeling approach, similar to the Papillomavirus Rapid Interface for Modeling and Economics (PRIME)18 which allowed us to simulate vaccination programs within multiple country settings following the natural life course of different country populations.

The individual decision tree structures for influenza, PD and HZ (Figure 1) were based on Talbird et al.19 with the RSV model closely aligning with influenza and being developed de-novo. We chose static over dynamic models for pragmatic reasons due to the number of countries and vaccination programs considered. Consequently, the transmission value (prevention value delivered to the non-vaccinated through herd immunity) was not captured. While each model was parameterized to reflect individual country settings where possible, our aim to produce results that are comparable across countries requires the prioritization of consistency in modeling and outcome metrics. This led to trade-offs with the vaccine and country-specificity of our models.

All decision tree models.

The definition of emergency department (ED) cases in the model was aligned with the definition used in each country and, where appropriate, separated from hospitalizations and outpatient appointments. In some countries, the ED cases are included in hospitalizations while in others these were considered (emergency) outpatient appointments.

The cycle structure for both archetypes is yearly, with results being calculated over the lifetime of eligible cohorts or until age 100. The structures followed one of the two model archetypes which reflect the vaccination age and cohort recommendations across our countries:

Closed single-cohort archetype to model one birth cohort: For example, the model follows a cohort of 50-y-olds until the age of 100 or death. With yearly cycles, this would be a maximum time horizon of 50 y, or 50 cycles. This model structure was utilized for both PD and HZ, where the age recommendations for all applicable countries are a single cohort with no definite repeat vaccinations after the base year. This model type aggregates costs and benefits therefore only for a single age cohort, with costs applying in the first cycle for one-time vaccinations and benefits aggregating throughout all cycles.

Benefit-cost framework

Benefits and costs were aggregated following Robinson et al.12 Figure 2 provides an overview of the resulting benefit-cost framework and the approaches used to monetize the costs and benefits of the modeled programs.

Benefit-cost framework.

Aggregation of costs

The costs of all vaccination programs are based on direct medical costs, e.g., the cost of the vaccine and its administration by a health care professional, and direct non-medical costs, such as an assumed 2-h productivity loss for the person getting vaccinated.

Indirect medical costs, such as treatment of side effects from the vaccination, are also included. Based on pain management recommendations and country-specific expert feedback, we assume that all vaccinations require two paracetamol or non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAID) tablets to treat localized swelling/pain but do not consider medical costs for treating other side effects due to their rarity and lack of country-specific data.

Aggregation of benefits

Benefits are aggregated within three categories: mortality risk reduction, non-fatal health risk reduction and changes in time use. The methodology of benefit monetization related to mortality risk reduction and non-fatal health risk reduction depended on the vaccine-preventable disease.

For influenza, PD and RSV, which significantly impact mortality, we used the Value of a Statistical Life (VSL) for valuing the prevention of fatal cases and the cost of illness (COI) approach for non-fatal cases with productivity benefits as outlined in the reference case.12 The VSL estimate provides a single monetary value for preventing the loss of life, irrespective of the remaining life expectancy and includes broader value elements such as productivity and leisure.20 In contrast, the value of a statistical life-year (VSLY), which is derived from the VSL, assigns a value to each additional year of life gained, by multiplying the VSLY with the expected remaining life years of the person at the point of the averted death, it is therefore sensitive to the age at which the fatal event occurs. VSLY acts as an age-adjustment of VSL and is appropriate for programs where the populations affected are predominantly younger or older12 as in our analysis. VSL and VSLY valuations thus provide a reasonable lower (VSLY) and upper (VSL) bound of the value that society places on mortality risk reduction for influenza, PD and RSV programs. VSL values for 2022 were only available for the US21 and Australia.22 We computed values for other countries through a value transfer approach following the methods outlined in23 using GNI per capita values from the World Bank Open Data, 2023, assuming an income elasticity of one as used in previous studies23–25 and due to all included countries being classified as high-income or upper-middle income by the World Bank.26 The VSLY values were then derived by dividing the VSL by the undiscounted future life expectancy at the average age of the country’s adult population25 to age-adjust the VSL value.

For HZ, which predominantly affects quality of life rather than survival, we estimated the averted QALY losses from a HZ case and postherpetic neuralgia (PHN) as a potential complication and valued them using a variety of methods. For the upper bound, we valued the QALY with the value of a statistical life year (VSLY). For the lower bound, we first valued the QALY loss using different multiples of Gross Domestic Capital per Capita (GDP per capita) using the factors of 1, 1.5 and 3 times the GDP per capita,27 then averaged these valuations.

All monetary model inputs were inflated or deflated in their local currency using consumer price indices28,29 and converted to 2022 international dollars $ using purchasing power parity (PPP) adjusted exchange rates30 following the second approach in the methodology outlined in Turner et al.31

Data collection

We structured the data collection along four main categories: disease outcome incidence rates, disease outcome mortality rates, medical costs of disease outcomes and immunization program specifications, including the relevant vaccine characteristics.

We aimed to collect country-specific data on disease incidence rates (in the absence of vaccination if programs were already implemented), mortality rates, and medical costs wherever possible and used proxies and assumptions where necessary. Where available, official primary data from nationally provided open-source databases were used. If the former was unavailable, we used secondary data from studies conducted within those geographies, otherwise, proxy data and assumptions were used as a last resort. For immunization program specifications, we would use national governmental sites for immunization schedules or WHO’s immunization schedule32 when not found.

Additionally, labor market data was collected and used to estimate productivity losses using the human capital method.33,34 We used labor force participation rates,35 adjusted by unemployment rates,36 to calculate the expected value of lost daily wages for relevant age groups in all countries and apply this to the average workdays lost for a disease or time taken for vaccination.

Vaccine efficacy and waning rates were collected and utilized across all applicable countries rather than local effectiveness rates due to a lack of data availability, with influenza being the exception where we apply an average effectiveness rate across countries, allowing standardization and a greater degree of comparison in our results. Vaccine uptake rates were based on the most recent coverage data found within a country. A summary of program/vaccine input parameters and sources is given in Appendix A: Immunization Program Inputs.

Sensitivity analyses

One-way deterministic sensitivity analyses were conducted by varying all parameters ±20% from baseline values to examine their impact on the BCR for each program and assess the effect of parameter uncertainty. The only exception was for time discount rates where we varied the parameter by ±50%.

The emerging RSV program also demonstrated potential for positive economic returns. Based on the model results in Germany and the U.S., the BCRs for RSV immunization range from $6.2 (median: $6.2, IQR: $2.2) using VSLY to $16.0 (median: $16.0, IQR: $4.8) using VSL per $1 spent, corresponding to mean net benefits per vaccinated individual ranging from $1,260 (median: $1,260, IQR: $555) to $3,646 (median: $3,646, IQR: $1,238).

Discussion

This study assessed the socioeconomic value of four adult immunization programs across ten countries using a benefit-cost analysis. Across the range of the programs modeled, we estimated an average BCR ranging between $5 (average across all lower bound valuations) and $19 (average across all upper bound valuations) per $1 spent. Furthermore, from 58 different estimates that were generated based on different valuations of non-monetary outcomes, 95% exceed the breakeven threshold of 1, where program returns exceed their costs.

These results complement a recent narrative literature review37 on the broader value of adult immunization programs. The positive socioeconomic returns are driven by the significant health benefits of vaccination, including reduced mortality, quality of life impacts and productivity which translate into substantial societal value when appropriately valued.

These findings add to the growing body of evidence supporting the economic rationale for investing in adult immunization programs. Most economic evaluations of the programs we analyze–whether cost-effectiveness or cost-utility analyses–have found them to be cost-effective from a health system perspective,38,39 demonstrating good value for their fiscal investment.

Our study, using a benefit-cost analysis (BCA) from a societal perspective, shows that the monetized societal benefits–primarily from reduced mortality and morbidity–far exceed the direct costs of running these programs. While the order of magnitude of these returns is comparable to childhood immunization programs,5,6 adult immunization remains underappreciated.37,40,41

It is important however to distinguish between value generation (as estimated in cost-effectiveness and benefit-cost analysis) and net cost impacts. As for most interventions, healthcare payers may still incur a net cost in implementing immunization programs, as the direct expenditures may exceed the immediate cost offsets within the healthcare system. Nevertheless, healthcare and government decision-makers are tasked with maximizing value for money, not minimizing expenses. The BCA results clearly show these programs’ broader societal value, positioning adult immunization among the most impactful public health interventions healthcare payers and governments can undertake.42

This emphasizes the urgent need for governmental bodies to consider the importance of adult immunization programs for the future, as even within our country selection, many programs were not implemented. Successful immunization programs require sufficient and stable funding as the socioeconomic returns of said programs include long-term benefits. However, governmental budgets are often short-term and aligned with political cycles,43,44 creating a misalignment which may hinder the implementation of these programs if governments do not appropriately consider the long-term benefits of population health, especially with the costs of lifetime vaccination being relatively low45 compared to the benefits. These benefits include averted fatalities, reduced use of healthcare resources and the potential for more economic productivity in older groups which will help mitigate international demographic challenges including increasingly strained healthcare systems and aging populations.

This analysis also underscores the importance of accounting for uncertainty in different approaches to valuing mortality and morbidity risk reduction when assessing the societal value of immunization programs within a BCA.

To address this uncertainty, we use the VSL as an upper bound and the VSLY as a lower bound, with VSLY de-facto serving as an age-adjusted alternative.12 The choice of valuation method significantly impacts the results. For instance, using VSL to value mortality results in BCRs that are 2–3 times higher than those derived using VSLY. Similarly, when valuing QALYs for the HZ program, the BCRs are four times higher with the VSLY approach compared to using a country’s average GDP per capita multiplier.

Further uncertainty arises from extrapolating VSL/VSLY values from US estimates using established methods for global analyses,23–25 as most countries – except the US and Australia–did not report their own 2022 VSL values. We did not use older VSL estimates due to ongoing debate over the validity of inflating past values.

The resulting value range reflects a likely minimum and maximum, capturing different societal preferences and willingness to pay for health outcomes. The resulting valuations are relatively high as they are based on societal willingness to pay and encompass broader societal benefits of immunization programs that extend beyond direct health impacts.12,46,47 This differs from the more commonly used supply-side thresholds in cost-utility modeling for valuing QALYs.

These findings underscore the need for careful consideration of valuation methods, the incorporation of broader value elements and the importance of assessing the value of immunization programs through different economic evaluation methods and from different perspectives, which traditionally do not use VSL and use more narrow methodologies such as cost-utility analyses.

Immunization programs that prevent fatal events seem to generate larger amounts of value than those programs that prevent mostly morbidity. However, as future societies will live and work longer, being in good health for longer will become more valuable in the future. In addition, engaging in non-market activities at older ages (e.g. informal caregiving) creates considerable economic value which is not observed using standard national accounting measures.48 Hence, all preventive programs will increase in value in line with demographic changes.

While the results demonstrate the overall economic value of adult immunization programs, there is a need for continued research and more robust and comprehensive data collection. Sensitivity analyses identified disease incidence rates, case fatality rates, vaccine costs, and discount rates to be key drivers of results. As these may vary across different settings and over time, ongoing monitoring and evaluation of immunization programs, considering local epidemiological and economic factors, are crucial for ensuring accurate assessments of their economic value and well-informed decision-making. Future research should focus on refining economic models, incorporating emerging data on the broader value and new immunization programs, and exploring the potential impact of new vaccination technologies (e.g. combination vaccines) and strategies (e.g. complementary pharmacy-based delivery) to optimize the delivery and economic benefits of adult immunization programs.

Strength and limitations

To our knowledge, this is the first benefit-cost model of its kind to attempt to capture the socioeconomic value of multiple adult immunization programs across multiple countries using various valuation methods. Other models have often focused on a single disease, nation or younger populations.5,6,19,49,50 We employ cohort models for each recommendation found in each country and model a diverse set of countries to increase the robustness and generalizability of our aggregated average returns.

The main limitation of the analysis was that country-specific data are not available for all countries, vaccination programs or value elements. Notably, the availability of disease incidence (in the presence of no vaccination) and mortality rates for older adult populations as well as vaccines effectiveness varied widely. Additionally, only a limited set of all relevant value elements of vaccination, as outlined in previous studies,51–54 was fully incorporated, namely, the impact on mortality and cost-offsets to the health system.

More broadly, the lack of country-specific data may be seen as a limitation, particularly when it concerns vaccine effectiveness rates. This is especially relevant for the flu programs and lower- and middle-income countries (Brazil, Thailand and South Africa). When extrapolating data from other countries we used the US as the starting point to ensure consistency and standardization in our approach, allowing for a more comparable analysis between all countries. We also perform a sensitivity analysis on the efficacy rate to address this with results shown in tornado diagrams in Appendix B. Further, the lack of 2022 VSL values for most countries meant we had to impute those from the US which may lead to an overestimation of the BCR as the US VSL is relatively high.

We partially captured a number of other broader elements: vaccines’ impact on the quality of life of vaccinated individuals and productivity benefits to vaccinated individuals and their caregivers. Other relevant value elements such as disease transmission value including “herd protection,” impact on antimicrobial resistance and impact on carers were not included due to data and model limitations.37 Furthermore, specific data on older populations such as the time spent on and value of informal activities including caregiving duties would likely paint a fuller picture of the socioeconomic returns and likely increase the overall BCR and NMB per vaccination course. Data for most other broader value elements were sparse or non-existent for all countries modeled and had to be excluded for this purpose. This may affect the results of the various diseases in different ways for diseases that primarily impact morbidity, such as HZ, these elements would likely have greater relative value than for more fatal diseases modeled using VSL.

Another limitation in the use of static models is that these prevent capturing value elements such as transmission effects or reinfections. While noting that such data is also not widely available, excluding this likely underestimates the BCRs of the Influenza, RSV and PD programs.

Finally, monetizing mortality risk reduction using VSL generates high value estimates for pneumococcal, influenza, and RSV programs in older adults. As with any economic valuation approach, VSL has recognized limitations: it is sometimes misinterpreted as the value of a life rather than of small changes in mortality risk, and locally derived estimates are often lacking,55 requiring value transfer23 and introducing uncertainty. VSL also does not account for remaining life expectancy, which may be a particular consideration in older populations.

To address these issues, we additionally report VSLY estimates to provide a more conservative benchmark; actual societal willingness to pay likely lies between the two. Using VSL remains consistent with recent methodological guidance, and presenting both measures enables decision-makers to assess the impact of alternative welfare assumptions on the results.

Conclusions

Adult immunization programs for influenza, PD, RSV and HZ consistently show positive returns on investment across a diversity of countries. Funding these programs provides short- and long-term benefits and may help to mitigate upcoming global demographic challenges. Further robust research and open-source data are needed to quantify more comprehensively the broader value of these and other emerging immunization programs. Policymakers are recommended to consider the long-term value of such programs and the necessity of their implementation in light of the demographic and health challenges facing aging societies worldwide.
