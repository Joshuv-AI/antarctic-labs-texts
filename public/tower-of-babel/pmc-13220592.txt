Classification of spinal tuberculous infection, pyogenic infection and spinal metastasis from magnetic resonance imaging using machine learning
Authors: Apiruk Sangsin, Piyapong Khumrin, Peem Sarasombath, Hideki Murakami, Permsak Paholpak, Nuttaya Pattamapaspong, Wongthawat Liawrungrueang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDifferentiating between spinal tuberculosis, pyogenic (bacterial) spondylitis and spinal metastasis remains a major diagnostic challenge because their radiological features often overlap. Delayed or incorrect diagnosis may lead to inappropriate treatment, permanent disability or death.ObjectiveTo develop and evaluate deep learning models for automated classification of spinal tuberculosis, pyogenic infection, and spinal metastasis using magnetic resonance imaging (MRI).MethodsT2-weighted sagittal MRI scans from 120 patients (40 per disease group) with pathologically or microbiologically confirmed diagnoses between 2014 and 2019 were retrospectively analyzed. Lesion regions were manually annotated by radiologists, and data were split into 80% training and 20% testing sets at the patient level. Extensive data augmentation (rotation ± 5°, zoom 1.1–1.2×, shearing ± 5°, grid distortion 2 × 2) was applied to mitigate overfitting. Three models were trained and compared: (1) a single-layer perceptron baseline, (2) a custom dense neural network (2 × 1024 neurons), and (3) pre-trained convolutional neural networks (ResNet50, VGG16, InceptionV3). Model performance was evaluated using accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-score on both whole and segmented images.ResultsAfter augmentation, 1,000 synthetic samples were generated per class. The baseline model achieved 27–33% accuracy, whereas the dense and pre-trained models achieved 98–100% accuracy on the test set. Although pre-trained networks demonstrated marginally higher performance, the difference compared with the dense model was not statistically significant. Activation heatmaps revealed inconsistent localization of attention regions, suggesting potential overfitting and limitations in visualization interpretability.ConclusionDeep learning models demonstrated strong potential in distinguishing between spinal tuberculosis, bacterial spondylitis, and spinal metastasis on MRI. However, the near-perfect performance likely reflects dataset homogeneity and augmentation effects rather than full generalization. External, multi-center validation and improved interpretability methods (e.g., Grad-CAM) are warranted to confirm clinical applicability and ensure reliable decision support for radiologists.Keywords: Spinal tuberculosis, Pyogenic spondylitis, Spinal metastasis, Deep learning, Magnetic resonance imaging, Artificial intelligence

Introduction

Spinal tuberculosis, pyogenic (bacterial) spondylitis, and spinal metastasis are three distinct yet radiologically overlapping spinal pathologies. Differentiating among these conditions remains a major clinical challenge, as they often present with similar imaging and clinical features. Delayed or inaccurate diagnosis can lead to inappropriate treatment, neurological deterioration, permanent disability, or even death. In certain cases, such as early tuberculous infection before abscess formation or when intervertebral discs remain uninvolved, paraspinal lesions may mimic metastatic masses, making distinction difficult on imaging alone [1]. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is widely used for assessing spinal infections and neoplasms. Although MRI provides high soft-tissue contrast and characteristic features, such as disc preservation in early tuberculosis and disc destruction in pyogenic spondylitis, these features often overlap in atypical cases [2, 3]. Consequently, accurate diagnosis frequently requires expert interpretation and histopathological confirmation. Rapid and accurate differentiation is essential to guide timely management, minimize complications, and optimize clinical outcomes.

Artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning (ML) have recently emerged as transformative tools in medical imaging and spine diagnostics [4]. By automatically identifying complex imaging patterns, AI models can support clinicians in recognizing subtle distinctions that may be visually imperceptible. In musculoskeletal imaging, AI has been applied to tasks such as tumor detection, fracture classification, and degenerative disease evaluation, demonstrating promising improvements in efficiency, consistency, and diagnostic accuracy [5]. Several studies have explored AI-based MRI classification of spinal tumors, distinguishing between tumor types with accuracies ranging from 80% to 90% [6–9]. However, AI applications in differentiating spinal infections and metastasis remain underexplored. One major limitation in previous studies is the small sample size, which constrains model generalization and increases the risk of overfitting [10]. To mitigate this, data augmentation techniques—such as geometric and photometric transformations—are widely used to expand datasets and enhance model robustness [11, 12].

Another promising approach in modern AI is transfer learning, which utilizes pre-trained deep convolutional networks as a foundation for new classification tasks [13]. It is the easiest and fastest way to leverage the representational power of pre-trained deep networks. Tuning a network with transfer learning is typically much faster and easier than training a new network from scratch with randomly initialized weights [14, 15]. This strategy significantly reduces the need for large labeled datasets and allows models to leverage learned features from large-scale image databases. Pre-trained architectures, including AlexNet [16], GoogLeNet [17], ResNet18, and ResNet50 [18], have been successfully applied in diverse medical imaging domains, achieving high accuracy and computational efficiency.

To our knowledge, no previous study has developed a deep learning model capable of simultaneously classifying spinal tuberculosis, bacterial spondylitis, and spinal metastasis on MRI. Therefore, this study aimed to develop and evaluate a deep learning–based classification model capable of differentiating these three pathologies using sagittal T2-weighted MRI images. By addressing the current diagnostic ambiguity, this research seeks to reduce delays in management, unnecessary diagnostic procedures, and healthcare costs, while supporting physicians in achieving more accurate, efficient, and objective diagnoses.

Methods and materials

Patient recruitment and data collection

Patients diagnosed with spinal tuberculosis, pyogenic (bacterial) spondylitis, or spinal metastasis who underwent T2-weighted sagittal MRI between 2014 and 2019 at Chiang Mai University Hospital (Thailand) were retrospectively included. Diagnosis in all cases was pathologically, microbiologically, or molecularly confirmed through tissue biopsy, culture, or GeneXpert testing. Each result was independently verified by a musculoskeletal pathologist with over five years of experience. Exclusion criteria included: Unconfirmed or uncertain diagnosis; Incomplete MRI sequences; or Poor image quality due to motion artifacts or inadequate signal resolution. This inclusion strategy ensured that only clinically and histopathologically validated cases were analyzed, maintaining dataset reliability and diagnostic accuracy.

MRI acquisition protocol

MRI data were obtained following a standardized institutional imaging protocol, employing T2-weighted sequences in the sagittal plane. This sequence was selected for its superior visualization of vertebral bodies, intervertebral discs, and paraspinal soft tissue changes. All MRI scans were reviewed by a musculoskeletal radiologist (≥ 5 years of experience) to confirm diagnostic features and exclude motion or artifact-distorted images.

Computational environment

All model development and analysis were conducted using Python 3.6 on Google Colaboratory (Google Research, Mountain View, CA, USA). Libraries employed included TensorFlow, Keras, NumPy, OpenCV, and imgaug for data augmentation and preprocessing. The computational environment setup is provided as supplementary material to enable reproducibility and transparency for future research.

Image preprocessing and segmentation

MRI images were manually segmented by radiologists to define regions of interest (ROI) corresponding to lesions and adjacent vertebral levels (Fig. 1). Lesion images: Cropped regions encompassing the infected or metastatic vertebral body and adjacent levels. Normal control regions: Cropped areas from unaffected vertebrae within the same sagittal slice. To enhance image quality, histogram equalization was performed to normalize intensity and improve tissue contrast. Both the original whole images and the segmented lesion images were used for training and evaluation to compare diagnostic performance across contexts.

(a) Original sagittal T2-weighted MRI image; (b) cropped image covering the lesion area and adjacent vertebral levels; (c) cropped normal control image

Preprocessing pipelines were adapted to conform with the input specifications of ResNet50, VGG16, and InceptionV3 models.

Data augmentation

To address the limited dataset size and mitigate overfitting, data augmentation was applied using a randomized but reproducible (fixed-seed) augmentation protocol. Augmentation settings were selected to simulate realistic anatomical and acquisition variations without distorting pathology. The applied transformations included: Rotation: ±5° (probability = 70%), Zoom: 1.1–1.2× ,Shearing: ±5°, Grid distortion: 2 × 2, Skew: 0.1 magnitude and Resizing: 256 × 256 pixels All images underwent histogram normalization to prevent brightness bias. For each diagnostic class (tuberculosis, pyogenic infection, metastasis), 1,000 augmented samples were generated. The dataset was then divided at the patient level into 80% training and 20% testing subsets, ensuring that data from the same patient did not appear in both sets.

Model architecture and training

We developed and evaluated three deep learning configurations: Baseline (Perceptron) model – a single fully connected layer serving as a benchmark. Dense neural network – consisting of two fully connected layers (each 1024 neurons) with ReLU activation, dropout (rate = 0.5), and a softmax output for three-class classification. Pre-trained convolutional neural networks (CNNs) – ResNet50 [18, 19],, VGG16 [18, 20, 21], and InceptionV3, fine-tuned using transfer learning on our dataset. All pre-trained networks were initialized with ImageNet weights and adjusted via transfer learning to optimize classification of spinal pathologies. Input images were standardized to 256 × 256 pixels, and batch normalization was employed for training stability. Training was performed for 50 epochs with a batch size of 16, using categorical cross-entropy as the loss function and the Adam optimizer (learning rate = 1 × 10⁻⁴). Early stopping and dropout regularization were implemented to prevent overfitting. A 10% validation split from the training set was used for hyperparameter tuning. Both whole-image and segmented-image datasets were used to compare model robustness under different contexts.

Evaluation metrics

Model performance was evaluated on the independent test set using multiple quantitative metrics to ensure comprehensive analysis: Accuracy, Precision, Recall (Sensitivity), F1-score, Macro and weighted averages Confusion matrices were generated to visualize classification performance across the three diagnostic categories. Additionally, model interpretability was explored using activation heatmaps to identify areas contributing most to classification decisions. To assess model reliability, we also performed 5-fold cross-validation on the augmented dataset, reporting mean accuracy ± standard deviation.

Ethical considerations

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Ethics Committee and Institutional Review Board of the Faculty of Medicine, Chiang Mai University (IRB number: ORT-2564-08032). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to data inclusion.

Results

Patient data and MRI characteristics

A total of 120 patients were included: 40 cases of pyogenic (bacterial) spondylitis, 40 cases of spinal metastasis, and 40 cases of spinal tuberculosis. Each class was augmented with 1,000 synthetic samples to address dataset limitations and balance class representation (Fig. 2). Representative MRI findings for each pathology are illustrated: Pyogenic infection (Fig. 2a): Epidural abscess with posterior paraspinal phlegmon, vertebral body erosion, and disc space narrowing. Spinal tuberculosis (Fig. 2b): Reduced disc space, blurred paradiscal margins, paravertebral soft tissue thickening (with or without calcification), and vertebral body collapse in advanced stages. Spinal metastasis (Fig. 2c): Cortical destruction, heterogeneous marrow signal, and soft tissue extension compressing the thecal sac. Normal spine (Fig. 2d): Intact vertebral alignment and uniform signal intensity.

Model training and evaluation overview

The augmented dataset was split 80:20 at the patient level to avoid data leakage between training and test sets. Five experiments were conducted using both whole MRI images and segmented lesion images. Models included: Dummy (Perceptron) model – simple baseline with random-like performance. Dense neural network – two hidden layers with 1024 neurons each, dropout rate 0.5 and Pre-trained CNNs – ResNet50, VGG16, and InceptionV3, fine-tuned using transfer learning.

Activation heatmap analysis

To assess model interpretability, activation heatmaps were generated for representative cases (Figs. 3, 4, 5 and 6). The color scale ranged from red (high attention) to purple (low attention). Ideally, red areas should align with the lesion zone; however, the following trends were observed: In spinal tuberculosis (Figs. 3 and 4), red activations appeared diffusely distributed, including non-lesional areas, while lesion areas often showed weaker (green/yellow) intensity. In pyogenic infection (Figs. 5 and 6), a similar mismatch was observed; red activation frequently appeared in normal vertebral regions, not corresponding to the abscess site. This lack of correlation between heatmap intensity and true lesion locations suggests that the models, while highly accurate numerically, may not focus on clinically meaningful features, potentially indicating overfitting or non-anatomical pattern recognition. Our findings and the results show that deep learning models, particularly dense networks and pre-trained CNNs, achieved very high classification performance (99–100%) in distinguishing among the three spinal diseases. The dummy model exhibited poor predictive ability, confirming the non-random effectiveness of the dense and pre-trained architectures. Despite the high quantitative results, activation heatmaps lacked consistent anatomical localization, implying limited interpretability. These results demonstrate feasibility and proof of concept, but also underscore the need for external validation using independent, multi-institutional datasets before clinical translation.

Discussion

The spine is the central load-bearing structure of the axial skeleton and plays a vital role in protecting the spinal cord, nerve roots, and cauda equina [22]. Differentiating spinal tuberculosis, pyogenic spondylitis, and spinal metastasis on imaging remains a major clinical challenge, as these entities frequently share overlapping radiologic features despite differing in etiology, management, and prognosis [23]. Although each disease exhibits characteristic findings—such as disc preservation in early tuberculous infection, disc destruction with abscess formation in bacterial spondylitis, and heterogeneous marrow replacement in metastasis. Their imaging appearances often overlap, especially in early or atypical presentations [24]. These diagnostic ambiguities contribute to delays in appropriate management and increase the risk of irreversible neurological complications.

Errors in diagnostic radiology are not uncommon. Studies indicate that up to 70% of radiologic errors are related to “missed findings”, reflecting the inherent difficulty of visual detection and interpretation [25]. Consequently, artificial intelligence (AI) has emerged as a promising adjunct for medical imaging interpretation. Advances in machine learning (ML) and deep learning (DL) enable computers to process complex image features beyond human perception, thereby enhancing diagnostic accuracy, workflow efficiency, and clinical decision support [26].

In comparison with previous studies, the previous research has applied deep learning models to tumour classification and lesion detection within spinal imaging. Maki et al. achieved the highest specificity of 82% in distinguishing between spinal schwannoma and meningioma on MRI [6]. Jung et al. demonstrated 63.4% accuracy using MRI features for spinal cord diffuse midline gliomas [7]. Additionally, two studies delved into the diagnosis of spinal tumors. Fan et al. explored lung cancer spinal bone metastasis diagnosis, reporting an accuracy of 85.9% using the Chan-Vese algorithm, which outperformed other methods [27]. Liu et al. conducted a study on risk prediction via spinal MRI, achieving an accuracy of 78.7% [28]. However, there have been no publications in the past decade concerning the utilization of MRI and AI for spinal infection detection. In 2017, a study by Wang et al. successfully trained a convolutional neural network using a “Siamese architecture” to accurately detect all spinal metastasis lesions. This represents one of the many approaches to lesion detection [29]. According to the objective of this study, our aim was to develop machine-learning or deep-learning algorithms capable of classifying spinal tuberculous infections, pyogenic infections, and metastatic carcinoma of the spine on MRI. Through the utilization of machine learning, our work introduced a novel technique for simultaneously distinguishing between various spinal disorders. This approach involves handling the local image gradient orientation properties of each structure using machine learning. Chen et al. demonstrated that the 3D AlexNet network outperforms traditional segmentation methods in the imaging diagnosis of prostate cancer in terms of training time, parameter count, and network performance evaluation [30]. The study by Yang et al. demonstrated that AlexNet and GoogLeNet outperformed traditional machine learning methods in glioma grading [31]. Liu et al. employed the ResNet18 model to identify early femoral head necrosis from MRI scans, achieving a high detection rate [31]. Another study by Odusmai et al. demonstrated that the ResNet50 model, when analyzing features of Alzheimer’s disease from MRI data, outperformed other known models [32].

Our study developed a deep learning framework capable of distinguishing spinal tuberculosis, pyogenic infection, and spinal metastasis using T2-weighted sagittal MRI images. Three architectures were compared: a baseline (dummy) model, a dense fully connected network (1024 neurons × 2 layers), and pre-trained CNNs (ResNet50, VGG16, InceptionV3). The dense and pre-trained models both achieved near-perfect accuracy (99–100%) across training and test datasets. While this result demonstrates the feasibility of AI in identifying distinguishing patterns among these spinal diseases, the absence of statistically significant differences between models suggests that high accuracy may reflect the controlled dataset and augmentation effects rather than true generalization capability. Indeed, the dummy model performed at near-random accuracy (27–33%), confirming that the high scores of the dense and pre-trained models were not coincidental but must nonetheless be interpreted cautiously. Furthermore, activation heatmap analysis revealed that model attention was not consistently localized to the lesion sites, suggesting that the networks may have relied on non-anatomical or spurious image features. Such patterns are indicative of overfitting, where the model learns dataset-specific cues instead of disease-specific radiological markers.

Although quantitative results were excellent, interpretability findings highlight that model reliability and clinical explainability remain limited. These findings are consistent with reviewer observations and similar reports in medical AI literature emphasizing that high accuracy does not necessarily imply clinical validity.

In terms of practical implications and model efficiency, our findings demonstrate that a dense network with 1024 neurons can achieve comparable performance to complex, pre-trained CNNs, suggesting that simpler models may suffice for structured, high-contrast medical imaging tasks. The use of data augmentation proved crucial for achieving these results, as it mitigated overfitting by increasing the apparent sample diversity. Additionally, the observation that model accuracy was nearly identical between whole-image and cropped-image training sets implies that manual cropping and segmentation may not be essential for achieving robust classification. This feature could simplify future deployment in clinical workflows. However, the apparent plateau in accuracy gains between the dense and pre-trained architectures underscores the limitations of dataset homogeneity and class balance. A larger, more diverse dataset encompassing variations in MRI scanners, protocols, and patient demographics would be essential to validate these models for real-world use.

The limitations and future direction of this study have several important limitations. First, the small sample size (120 patients) and single-center data source restrict generalizability. Although augmentation increased the apparent dataset size, it did not introduce new biological variability. Second, the lack of external validation remains a critical limitation. Third, the inconsistent activation heatmap distributions demonstrate that the models, despite high accuracy, may not rely on pathophysiologically meaningful features. Future studies will adopt Grad-CAM and Integrated Gradients to improve interpretability and quantify lesion overlap metrics. Finally, given that all cases were confirmed histopathologically, the models have yet to be tested on ambiguous or early-stage infections where clinical benefit would be most relevant. Future directions include expanding to multi-sequence MRI datasets (T1, T2, STIR) for richer feature extraction, implementing radiomics-based hybrid AI models to combine handcrafted and learned features and establishing prospective clinical validation with radiologist-AI collaboration to assess workflow integration and diagnostic accuracy. Clinical and research significance of despite its limitations, this study provides proof of concept for the use of AI-assisted MRI classification of spinal pathologies.

Such systems could serve as decision-support tools for radiologists and spine surgeons, aiding in the rapid triage of patients and reducing diagnostic delays. Importantly, our findings underscore that AI performance metrics must always be accompanied by interpretability and external validation to ensure safety and clinical reliability. With appropriate validation, AI-assisted MRI analysis could complement radiologic expertise, improve diagnostic consistency, and facilitate earlier, more accurate management of spinal diseases.

In summary, our deep learning models successfully distinguished spinal tuberculosis, pyogenic infection, and spinal metastasis on MRI with extremely high accuracy. However, imperfect lesion localization, small dataset size, and lack of external testing limit immediate clinical applicability. This study highlights both the potential and pitfalls of AI in spinal imaging offering a foundation for future multi-center, interpretable, and clinically validated models.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates the potential of deep learning–based MRI analysis for the automated classification of spinal tuberculosis, pyogenic spondylitis, and spinal metastasis. The proposed model achieved high diagnostic accuracy, underscoring the feasibility of AI-assisted tools in supporting orthopaedic, neurosurgical, and radiological decision-making. However, despite the promising results, the current findings should be interpreted as a proof of concept, limited by the small, single-center dataset. Future research should focus on multi-center collaborations, incorporation of larger and more diverse datasets, and integration of explainable AI techniques to ensure clinical reliability, generalizability, and safe adoption in real-world spine diagnostics.
