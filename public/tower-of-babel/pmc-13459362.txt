How is shared decision making used in the treatment of patients with liver disease? a scoping review
Authors: Haeun Kang, Da Young Ryu, Yeonsoo Jang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundShared decision making (SDM) is an approach in which healthcare providers and patients collaboratively consider the best available evidence when making treatment decisions; patients are also supported in evaluating available options and forming informed preferences. However, evidence regarding treatment decision making contexts involving SDM for patients with liver disease remains limited. Therefore, this scoping review analyzes the contextual environments in which SDM is implemented during the treatment of patients with liver disease and examines the characteristics of patient participation in the SDM process.MethodsThis study focused on treatment decision making contexts (context) involving SDM (concept) among patients with liver disease (population). Following the Arksey and O’Malley scoping review method, structured searches of articles published until March 2025 were conducted in four electronic databases: PubMed, Cumulative Index to Nursing and Allied Health Literature, Embase, and Web of Science. The search terms used were “shared decision making” and “liver disease.” Eight studies were ultimately included in the review.ResultsSDM was applied in treatment contexts, including medication initiation, surgical decision making, and the selection of therapeutic approaches such as hepatic resection and radiofrequency ablation. Five (62.5%) out of the eight studies included surgery as an option, and four (50.0%) focused on patients with liver cancer. When the patients with liver disease participated in the SDM process, they were presented with treatment options, provided information about these options, and asked to express their preferences. However, only one study evaluated decision making outcomes.ConclusionsCancer-related and surgical decision making contexts were the most frequent targets for SDM in this review. However, patient decision aids (PDAs) are currently unavailable for any liver disease. Additionally, only one study included a process for re-evaluating prior decisions. Future research and clinical practice should incorporate mechanisms for re-evaluating prior decisions and support the development of PDAs for liver disease.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13643-026-03227-5.Keywords: Shared decision making, Liver disease, Patient participants, Scoping review

Background

Shared decision making (SDM) is a collaborative process in which patients and healthcare providers (HCPs) share the best available evidence and discuss patients’ informed preferences when making healthcare decisions [1]. SDM has several components, including presenting treatment options, sharing information about the advantages and disadvantages of each option, identifying patient preferences based on tailored information, and making decisions that reflect these preferences [2, 3]. These major processes can be classified into three parts: inviting patients to participate in decision making (team talk), sharing information, and discussing preferences regarding available options (option talk), and reaching a decision (decision talk). These parts influence each other and form a continuous cycle from deliberation to active listening [2]. Therefore, understanding the applications of SDM requires focusing on the decision making process, rather than simply determining whether SDM occurred.

SDM has increasingly been used in various clinical contexts across the continuum of care, from diagnosis to management. After diagnosis, the treatment process is particularly important because it involves complex factors, including the quality of life, costs, and preferences regarding potential side effects. Previous studies have identified specific clinical situations and demonstrated the positive impact of SDM. In emergency situations, patients often prefer physicians’ determinations over engaging in SDM, with decisions frequently driven by the surgeon’s expertise [4]. By contrast, SDM has been more widely implemented in patients with chronic conditions such as cancer, or those requiring complex treatment regimens [5–7]. These findings suggest that incorporating patient preferences and considering various treatment options over long-term care are essential, and that SDM may enhance treatment satisfaction and clinical outcomes in this population.

Patients with liver disease, particularly those with cirrhosis or liver cancer, require long-term management. These patients may also experience various complications such as hepatic encephalopathy, esophageal variceal bleeding, or hepatorenal syndrome [8]. Although many chronic diseases follow relatively predictable clinical courses, liver disease can involve acute clinical changes and temporary cognitive changes associated with these complications. These changes can affect patients’ ability to participate in decision making at different time points. Consequently, in liver disease management, the SDM process may be more dynamic than in other conditions and may require more deliberation and frequent re-evaluation. As such, a circular and recursive SDM model is adequate to analyze the decision making process of patients with liver disease. This model does not describe the process as a linear progression but as a “team–option–decision” cycle, thus allowing for immediate and iterative adjustments required by clinical changes in liver disease.

To support the application of the SDM process, standardized patient decision aids (PDAs) could be used because they help align healthcare decisions with patients’ personal values and preferences [9]. Major health organizations, such as the UK National Health Service (NHS), the UK National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE), and the Ottawa Hospital Research Institute (OHRI), have developed PDAs and presented the effects for various conditions such as cancer and cardiovascular disease. However, the standardized PDAs for patients with liver disease remain relatively limited [10–12]. As the efficacy of PDAs has been demonstrated in other fields, there is a need to expand the scope of PDA application to patients with liver disease as well. Therefore, as an initial step in developing PDAs related to liver disease, it is important to understand how the SDM process is implemented in this patient population. This understanding may help address the unique challenges of liver disease and support the delivery of patient-centered care.

However, evidence comprehensively examining how the SDM is conducted or identifying the key characteristics related to the SDM within the clinical contexts of liver disease remains limited compared with other diseases. To develop efficient strategies for the application of SDM, the trend of SDM application needs to be identified. As such, a scoping review is more appropriate at this initial stage than a systematic review [13]. Additionally, conducting a scoping review for SDM in patients with liver disease is essential. Therefore, this review analyzes the clinical contexts in which SDM is practiced among patients with liver disease and examines the characteristics of patient involvement in the SDM process.

Methods

This study focuses on treatment decision making contexts involving SDM among patients with liver disease by following the Arksey and O’Malley scoping review method [14].

Study design

Based on the Arksey and O’Malley scoping review method [14], this study addressed the following research question guided by the PCC (population, concept, context): “How is SDM (concept) conducted within treatment contexts (context) for patients with liver disease (population)?” This review adheres to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses Extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) [15].

Study eligibility

The analysis included studies investigating SDM among patients with liver disease. To ensure comprehensive coverage, no restrictions were placed on publication year. As there is no international consensus for SDM yet, this study used the definition of the SDM model [2] that describes it as a collaborative process that provides information on various options and ensures that decisions are based on the best available evidence aligned with patient preferences and contexts.

Studies with insufficient data presentation (e.g., review articles, editorials, letters, errata, short surveys, preprints, notes, articles in press, book chapters, as well as conference abstracts, papers, and reviews) were excluded from the analysis. Additional exclusion criteria included studies not published in English; studies that did not primarily target liver diseases; and studies involving pediatric populations, pregnant women, or nonhuman subjects. Studies published in languages other than English were excluded because SDM is a context- and analysis-sensitive process; this exclusion was necessary to ensure accurate data extraction and comprehensive information during translation. Furthermore, because this review focused on the interactive process of SDM and used the definition of the SDM model [2], studies that lacked a clear description of patient participation or interaction with HCPs during the SDM process were excluded. Studies that only investigated patient preferences without describing actual SDM activities or that did not directly mention SDM were also excluded.

Information sources and search strategy

Structured searches for articles published until March 2025 were conducted on four electronic databases: PubMed, Cumulative Index to Nursing and Allied Health Literature, Embase, and Web of Science. The search terms and strategies were developed with the assistance of a research librarian. Medical Subject Heading (MeSH) terms related to SDM and liver diseases were used. To ensure a comprehensive search, the term “liver” was expanded to include “hepatic” and “hepato,” and the term “disease” was expanded to include “failure” and specific disease names. These terms were then combined using the appropriate Boolean operators for each database with no restrictions applied to the publication period. The search strategy included terms such as “shared decision making” in “((decision* OR choice*) AND (make* OR making*)) OR decision making) AND (share* OR sharing*)” and “decision making, shared [MeSH]” or “liver diseases” such as in “(liver OR hepatic* OR hepato*) AND ((disease* OR disorder* OR failure*) OR (fatty* OR transplantation* OR abscess* OR neoplasm* OR carcinoma* OR adenoma*)),” “liver diseases [MeSH],” “cirrhosis,” and “hepatitis.” Supplementary Material 1 provides a full overview of the search terms.

Study selection process

Two reviewers (HEK and DYR) independently screened the titles and abstracts according to the predefined eligibility criteria, after discussing and establishing a shared understanding of the SDM concept. Although some studies appeared relevant based on titles and abstracts, they were subsequently excluded owing to data limitations. The remaining studies were assessed for eligibility based on their SDM coverage by the same reviewers. If an agreement was not reached between the two reviewers, a third reviewer (YJ) was consulted. Finally, eight studies were included in this review (Fig. 1) and are listed in Supplementary Material 2.

Data extraction

To ensure data accuracy, data extraction was performed by one researcher (HEK) using data organization tables. The two other researchers (DYR and YJ) cross-checked the extracted data. The extracted data included the following: (1) general characteristics (author, year, country, diagnosis of liver disease, research design, and sample size divided between the patient and the HCP side); (2) a summary of the decision process (purpose, decision process, and outcomes of the reviewed studies); and (3) components of the SDM process.

Data analysis and synthesis

Because liver disease progresses dynamically and necessitates frequent reassessments, this study evaluated the SDM process using the SDM model [2] to capture its interactive and cyclical characteristics. Based on the SDM model, a descriptive analysis was conducted to examine the decision making process and outcomes of the reviewed studies. The “Decision Making Process” section examined the participation level in treatment-related decisions. The “Tailored Information and Discussion Topics” section analyzed the process by which the HCPs and patients engaged in discussions regarding the patient’s disease status, the benefits and risks associated with the presented treatment options, available support resources, personalized goal setting, and other relevant decision making factors. The “Outcomes” section examined the results related to decision making that reflected the patient’s subjective decisional values and preferences. The components of the SDM process were analyzed to examine how well they were applied based on the SDM model according to the following elements: offering treatment options, providing tailored information about the patient’s individual status, presenting information about available treatment options, discussing the decisional objective factors, deliberation, identifying the patient’s subjective decisional values, and evaluating the outcomes of the decision.

Results

General characteristics

Out of the eight studies, four (50.0%) examined the use of SDM among patients with liver cancer. Regarding the liver disease diagnoses, the participants in one study had Child–Pugh class A or B cirrhosis and were presumed to have early-stage hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) in the SDM process [16]. As the participants in this study were considered to have early-stage HCC [16], three studies involved patients with early- or mid-stage HCC [16–18], while one involved patients with early, mid, or advanced/unresectable HCC stages [19]. Additionally, three studies involved patients with hepatitis [20–22] and one involved patients with hepatocellular adenoma [23]. Summary of decision making process

Among the included studies, two qualitative studies were identified: one described the experiences of patients [19] and the other identified main decision making attributes [22]. One mixed-method study involved assessing the current SDM level from the perspectives of observers, patients, and physicians, as well as interviewing patients to identify potential needs and opportunities for improvement [23]. Among the three cohort studies, one involved examining patients’ choices between hepatic resection and radiofrequency ablation (RFA) and identifying patient thresholds based on information about the benefits and risks of the treatment options in a hypothetical context [16]. Another cohort study determined the sustained virologic response and adherence rates across care models [20]. The remaining study explored patients’ selection among antiviral medications after providing standardized drug information [21]. Two quantitative studies evaluated the effects of SDM interventions on patients’ understanding of the disease or treatment, as well as decision self-efficacy, decision satisfaction, and decisional conflict [17, 18] (Table 2).

Components of the SDM process

All eight reviewed studies provided patients with more than one treatment option for their diagnosed illness and information regarding the proposed options, such as the expected goals, benefits, and risks of each treatment plan [16–20, 22, 23]. Except in cases involving hypothetical scenarios [16], drug profiles [21], or unclear information exchange [20], the HCPs shared patient individual status information and discussed various objective factors that could be considered in decision making. Although three studies did not report whether sufficient time or opportunities were provided for the deliberation process [16, 19, 21], all studies identified patients’ subjective decisional values, and the patients and HCPs made shared decisions based on their preferences [16–20, 22, 23]. In one quasi-experimental study, the follow-up outcomes of the treatment decisions through SDM were evaluated three months after discharge using a decision regret measure [17] (Table 3).

Discussion

This study reviewed eight studies to analyze the clinical contexts in which SDM was implemented for patients with liver disease and examine the characteristics of patient participation in the SDM process. The major findings can be summarized as follows: (1) the HCPs primarily consisted of multidisciplinary teams (MDTs), whereas the involvement of supportive individuals, such as family members or relatives of the patients, was not clearly reported; (2) notably, in patients with liver disease, SDM was applied in various treatment decision making situations such as medication initiation or selection, surgical decision making, and as part of cancer treatment process; and (3) the commonly used components of the SDM process included providing options with information on associated risks and benefits, identifying patient preferences, and making decisions consistent with those preferences. However, the evaluation of decision outcomes and the continuity of the SDM process were limited.

In this review, SDM for patients with liver disease was primarily conducted using MDTs of HCPs, with limited reporting on the participation of additional individuals, such as family members. This finding about MDTs is consistent with previous research indicating that patients with complex care needs often collaborate with MDTs [7]. However, to fully conduct SDM from multiple perspectives, beyond the individual level, its implementation should be supported at the health system level. For example, this can be achieved by resolving time constraints in clinics to improve deliberation, training HCPs in SDM, and promoting a patient-centered culture. The limited reporting on the involvement of patients’ support networks might restrict the understanding of how social networks influence the implementation of SDM in real-world settings. Relatives can play both supportive and conflicting roles in decision making. Supportive roles involved providing emotional encouragement or considering treatment options together; however, conflicting roles were reported when patients felt marginalized in the process or pressured to accept a specific option [24]. One study also reported that Asian patients emphasized the influence of their family and support networks through decision making [25]. Healthcare decisions often occur in relational contexts rather than being made solely by individual patients [26]. Therefore, the SDM process should be understood in the context of social networks.

According to this review, SDM operationalization and social dynamics also seem to differ depending on the context of the disease. The application of SDM primarily focused on cancer treatment and surgical decision making. These results may be attributed to the fact that the treatment options for cancer are diverse [27, 28] and that surgery is a critical decision point [26], which influences the quality of life and several aspects of patients’ lives. By contrast, the use of SDM in non-cancerous liver diseases requiring complex medical management, such as advanced cirrhosis, was relatively limited. Patients with liver disease are more vulnerable to the social stigma related to viral hepatitis or alcohol use compared with patients with other chronic diseases [29–31]. This concern can be a barrier to SDM by discouraging patients from communicating their symptoms or preferences to HCPs.

This review indicated that participation of MDTs was another difference between liver cancer and non-cancerous liver disease. MDTs focused on patients with liver cancer rather than those with non-cancerous liver disease, according to the reviewed studies. Additionally, the “tailored information about patients’ individual status” component was included in all studies on liver cancer (excluding hypothetical contexts), as opposed to viral hepatitis studies. The reason may be that cancer treatment can be individualized based on specific cancer staging and physiological parameters while viral hepatitis treatment often relies on standardized antiviral regimens and guidelines [27, 28, 32, 33]. As the SDM approaches for patients with non-cancerous diseases may differ from those for patients with liver cancer, the strategies for an effective SDM process should be tailored to patients’ health status, including the participation of an MDT. However, the use of standardized PDAs for supporting SDM is not fully established for liver disease, unlike other diseases. This lack of clinical application, in turn, limits the development and validation of such tools. Therefore, the development of standardized PDAs tailored to liver disease would be a meaningful step toward effectively applying SDM in this population. These PDAs may benefit from including key features, such as prognostic visualization and cognitive status consideration, at critical decision points like transplant eligibility determination. Additionally, digital PDAs can be utilized to improve accessibility, personalization, and engagement by providing up-to-date interactive information and enabling data-driven monitoring of patient preferences [34]. However, there are risks associated with digital exclusion, usability issues, unverified content, and privacy concerns. Furthermore, alternatives such as in-person support, offline printed materials, or face-to-face assistance should be considered for patients with limited digital literacy or access.

This review selected studies based on the definition of SDM from the three-talk SDM model [2] and analyzed the extent to which the components of the model were implemented. While the components of “team talk,” “option talk,” and “decision talk” were commonly included in the reviewed studies, reporting on the deliberation component remained limited. This may suggest that decision making processes were closer to clinician-guided approaches than to fully realized SDM. Given that the three-talk SDM model is an iterative and cyclical process, this review also examined the “evaluation of decisions”; however, this component was identified in only one study with a short follow-up period. Compared with a review of SDM in oncology [6], which found that one study operationalized an SDM model, this review found that the use of all SDM components remains limited. However, while a previous review of oncology studies identified that the “assessment of patient and family understanding” is the least implemented component, this review found the “evaluation of outcomes” to be the least used. This difference may be interpreted based on the SDM models used for analysis, but a direct equivalent comparison is difficult because of the heterogeneity and number of reviewed studies. Nevertheless, this broad comparison suggests that a structured SDM framework for the full implementation of SDM components is important in clinical settings.

Furthermore, because SDM is a complex and recursive process [2, 35], the “evaluation of outcomes” should be considered for patients with liver disease who require long-term treatment. Accordingly, the SDM model may need adaptation for hepatology—for example, by incorporating decision pathways for cognitive-impairment screening and surrogate decision making, together with routine evaluation of clinical outcomes and SDM processes. In addition to implementing SDM components, the qualitative application of SDM is also important, as a gap often exists between HCPs’ self-reported and observed behaviors [36]. Not only the evaluation of SDM outcomes but also the assessment of the SDM process (e.g., satisfaction or decisional conflicts) was limited in most reviewed studies. Therefore, evaluating the quality of SDM in aspects of clinical and patient-reported health outcomes, as well as the process itself, and expanding deliberation may help ensure that patient values are meaningfully integrated into decision making beyond power dynamics.

To the best of our knowledge, this is one of the earliest scoping reviews examining the characteristics of the SDM process among patients with liver disease. However, it has several limitations. First, the included studies were limited to English-language articles published in four databases and gray literature was excluded to ensure peer review and the quality of evidence. This approach may have led to potential publication bias. Because this review followed the definition of the interactive SDM model, the SDM-related search terms may have been conceptually narrow, potentially introducing conceptual bias. Considering heterogeneity of study, limitation in coding, and the unintentionally small number of included studies, this study was conducted as a scoping review to identify how SDM is applied in practice, rather than providing an overview of SDM for liver disease. Future research should therefore broaden search strategies to capture alternative SDM definitions and related decision-support approaches. Second, the search strategy did not differentiate between liver diseases. As a result, there was heterogeneity in the study design and outcomes of the included studies, which likely limited the generalizability of the findings and the breadth of the conclusions regarding the effectiveness of SDM.

Further research is needed to explore how SDM is implemented across different types of liver diseases by including a larger number of studies to provide sufficient evidence. As only one randomized controlled trial was identified among the included studies, more research should be conducted to provide evidence to objectively compare health outcomes between SDM and non-SDM groups. Additionally, it is important to explore how liver-specific cognitive impairments such as hepatic encephalopathy affect decisional capacity, the timing of SDM, and the necessity of surrogate decision makers. SDM in cognitively vulnerable populations frequently emphasizes self-involvement or at least that of relatives [37]. Therefore, such evidence would help establish a base to develop PDAs to support liver diseases-specific SDM, which are currently lacking from official clinical guidelines.

Conclusions

SDM involves the active participation of HCPs, patients, and family members in the treatment process, thereby supporting patient-centered decision making and enhancing the equity and autonomy in interactions between HCPs and patients [1–3]. However, this review indicates a lack of research on SDM focused on the liver disease population apart from liver cancer or surgical contexts. To address this gap, expanding the application of SDM to a wider range of liver diseases, particularly for patients with non-cancerous liver diseases, is essential. This can be achieved by focusing on the development and validation of liver disease-specific PDAs that address social concerns, as the most promising intervention type to facilitate more evidence-based SDM implementation. Based on these findings, applying SDM in clinical practice should be guided by established SDM models to ensure that all components of this process are not addressed. Prospective studies evaluating SDM implementation in clinical settings can also support its application. Rather than short-term assessments, long-term longitudinal studies evaluating patient experience, including the “evaluation of decisions” component, which was limited in this review, would be beneficial. Such studies may contribute to tracking and improving outcomes related to liver-specific acute or cognitive changes. In addition, other decisional changes affected by societal barriers could be supported through the development of digital PDAs. These developments could enable more patient-centered and accessible treatment. To increase the frequency of SDM application, the development of PDAs for liver disease should be prioritized, along with generating evidence on the effectiveness of SDM in this population. However, further studies are needed to evaluate the implementation and effectiveness of SDM approaches in various clinical contexts to enhance health outcomes and treatment efficacy in patients with liver diseases.
