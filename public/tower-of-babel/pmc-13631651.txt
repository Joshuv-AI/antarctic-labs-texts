Genomic insights into carbapenemase- and extended-spectrum β-lactamase–producing gram-negative bacteria in aquatic and terrestrial animal reservoirs in the United Arab Emirates
Authors: Hazim O Khalifa, Temesgen Mohammed, Hazem Ramadan, Afra Abdalla, Ann Mary Joseph, Natassia Mannina, Uday Kishore, Arve Lee Willingham
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe global spread of carbapenemase- and extended-spectrum β-lactamase (ESBL)–producing Gram-negative bacteria represents a major One Health challenge, driven by interconnected human, animal, and environmental reservoirs. However, genomic data on antimicrobial-resistant bacteria associated with aquatic and terrestrial animals in the United Arab Emirates (UAE) remain scarce, limiting assessment of their potential role in resistance dissemination. This study investigated the phenotypic and genomic characteristics of resistant Gram-negative bacteria recovered from these animal reservoirs.MethodsSixty aquarium-associated samples were collected, yielding 69 Gram-negative isolates. Antimicrobial susceptibility testing, phenotypic detection of ESBL and carbapenemase activity, molecular screening of resistance genes, and whole-genome sequencing of selected isolates were performed. Phylogenetic analyses were conducted to assess evolutionary relatedness with global strains.ResultsThe isolates were mainly identified as Citrobacter, Pseudomonas, Acinetobacter, and Escherichia coli. Resistance to β-lactam antibiotics was widespread, with 87% of isolates confirmed as ESBL producers and 37.7% classified as multidrug resistant. Carbapenemase activity was detected in 14.5% of isolates. Genomic analyses revealed diverse ESBL, AmpC, and carbapenemase genes, including blaCTX-M-15, blaCTX-M-55, blaCMY-82, blaNDM-1, and for the first time multiple OXA-type carbapenemases (blaOXA-51, blaOXA-421, and blaOXA-848, blaOXA-1005, blaOXA-211-like, and blaOXA-396-like), frequently associated with mobile genetic elements. ESBL-producing E. coli ST602 and ST10 clustered closely with human, food, wildlife, and environmental strains from national and international sources.ConclusionAquatic and terrestrial animals in the UAE serve as important reservoirs for clinically relevant antimicrobial resistance. These findings underscore the need for integrated One Health genomic surveillance to mitigate the transboundary spread of high-risk resistant lineages.Keywords: Antimicrobial resistance, One health, Carbapenemase-producing bacteria, Extended-spectrum β-lactamases, Genomic surveillance, Animal reservoirs, Mobile genetic elements

Introduction

The escalating threat of antimicrobial resistance (AMR) has emerged as a critical global public health challenge, with multidrug-resistant (MDR) pathogens—particularly carbapenemase- and extended-spectrum β-lactamase (ESBL)–producing bacteria—posing a serious risk to effective disease management [1], [2], [3], [4]. Of particular concern are Gram-negative bacteria, including members of the order Enterobacterales, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, and Acinetobacter baumannii, which have developed resistance to a wide range of conventional and broad-spectrum antimicrobials, including last-resort agents such as carbapenems [4], [5], [6]. Without urgent and coordinated interventions, AMR-associated mortality is projected to reach 10 million deaths annually by 2050, surpassing deaths caused by cancer [7]. Recent global estimates indicate that bacterial AMR was associated with approximately 4.95 million deaths in 2019, of which 1.27 million were directly attributable to resistant infections [8], with the highest mortality burdens reported in regions such as western Sub-Saharan Africa (27.3 deaths per 100,000 population) [8].

Although extensive research has explored the prevalence and genetic basis of ESBL- and carbapenemase-producing bacteria across human, livestock, and environmental sectors [1], [9], substantial knowledge gaps remain regarding their occurrence in less-studied but increasingly important reservoirs, including aquatic and exotic animals and their associated environments. Investigating ESBL- and carbapenemase-producing Gram-negative bacteria in aquatic animals and aquarium ecosystems is therefore critical for understanding the role of controlled aquatic environments in the persistence and dissemination of AMR.

Aquatic animals, encompassing both marine and freshwater species, can act as reservoirs and vectors for resistant Gram-negative bacteria, facilitating the transmission of AMR determinants between animals, humans, and the environment [10]. Aquariums, which maintain diverse aquatic species within shared or interconnected water systems, represent unique microcosms where antimicrobial resistance can emerge, persist, and spread [11]. Factors such as water recirculation systems, biofilm formation, bioaccumulation of antimicrobial residues, and interspecies interactions may promote the maintenance and evolution of resistance mechanisms [12]. In addition, the routine use of antimicrobial agents for disease prevention and treatment in aquarium settings can impose selective pressure, favoring the survival and amplification of ESBL- and carbapenemase-producing bacteria [13], [14].

The presence of ESBL- and carbapenemase-producing bacteria in aquarium environments raises important concerns not only for aquatic animal health but also for public health, as aquarium personnel, researchers, and visitors may be exposed to resistant pathogens through direct contact, aerosols, or waterborne transmission [15]. Understanding the ecology, prevalence, and genetic characteristics of these resistant bacteria in aquarium settings is therefore essential for informing targeted biosecurity measures, improving antimicrobial stewardship, and minimizing the risk of spillover into natural aquatic ecosystems.

In the United Arab Emirates, several studies have documented the burden of AMR in human and veterinary sectors [2], [3], [16], [17]; however, data on antimicrobial resistance in aquatic animals and their environments remain scarce. To the best of our knowledge, this study is the first to investigate the prevalence and genetic characteristics of ESBL- and carbapenemase-producing Gram-negative bacteria in aquatic animals and aquarium-associated environments in the UAE. Moreover, by integrating phenotypic characterization, molecular detection, whole-genome sequencing, and phylogenomic analyses, our current study provides novel insights into potential transmission dynamics across animal, environmental, and human sectors at both national and global scales.

Materials and methods

Sampling and bacterial isolation and identification

In total, 60 samples were obtained from an Aquarium in United Arab Emirates, during routine animal health surveillance and environmental monitoring in March 2024. The sampling set included 22 rectal swabs, 17 fecal samples, 20 water samples, and one sample collected from the feeding station. All specimens were aseptically collected into sterile containers and transported to the laboratory under refrigerated conditions. Upon receipt, samples were homogenized in buffered peptone water and incubated overnight at 37 °C. Following enrichment, aliquots were streaked onto MacConkey agar supplemented with cefotaxime (4 μg/mL) using a sterile 10 μL inoculation loop and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. Colonies exhibiting distinct morphological characteristics, including variations in size and color, were selected and subcultured onto nutrient agar plates for purification, followed by incubation at 37 °C for an additional 24 h. Pure isolates were preserved in tryptic soy broth containing 25% glycerol and stored at −80 °C for long-term preservation. Bacterial identification and confirmation were performed using the Microgen GN-ID A and B identification panel test kits (Microgen, UK). The rectal and fecal samples used in this study were originally collected by the Aquarium veterinary staff during routine health surveillance in March 2024. These pre-existing biological materials were subsequently provided to the researchers for retrospective analysis.

Antimicrobial sensitivity testing

The antimicrobial susceptibility profiles of the bacterial isolates were determined using the Kirby–Bauer disk diffusion technique in accordance with the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute recommendations [18]. E. coli ATCC 25922 was employed as the quality control reference strain. The testing panel covered multiple classes of antimicrobial agents, including β-lactam antibiotics such as ampicillin (AMP, 10 μg), amoxicillin–clavulanic acid (AMC, 20/10 μg), cefoperazone (CFP, 75 μg), ceftriaxone (CRO, 30 μg), cefoxitin (FOX, 30 μg), meropenem (MEM, 10 μg), and imipenem (IPM, 10 μg). In addition, quinolones (ciprofloxacin [CIP, 5 μg] and nalidixic acid [NAL, 30 μg]), aminoglycosides (gentamicin [GEN, 10 μg] and amikacin [AMK, 30 μg]), as well as tetracycline (TET, 30 μg) and chloramphenicol (CHL, 30 μg) were included in the susceptibility assessment.

Phenotypic detection of extended-spectrum β-lactamase production

Phenotypic confirmation of ESBL activity was performed using the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute–recommended double disk synergy test (DDST). In this assay, ceftriaxone (CRO; 30 μg) and ceftazidime (CAZ; 30 μg) disks were applied both alone and in combination with clavulanic acid (CA; 10 μg). ESBL production was considered present when the inhibition zone surrounding either ceftriaxone or ceftazidime increased by 5 mm or more in the presence of clavulanic acid compared to the corresponding antibiotic disk alone, in accordance with CLSI criteria [18].

Phenotypic detection of carbapenemase production

After selective isolation, culture plates were incubated aerobically at 37 °C for 24 h. Colonies exhibiting distinct morphological features were further purified by subculturing onto nutrient agar and incubated under identical conditions to obtain pure bacterial isolates. All purified isolates were subsequently evaluated for carbapenemase production using the modified carbapenem inactivation method (mCIM), following established protocols [19]. In brief, a loopful of bacteria from an overnight culture was inoculated into 2 mL of tryptic soy broth, mixed thoroughly, and incubated with a 10 μg meropenem disk at 37 °C for 4 h. Following incubation, the disk was removed and placed onto Mueller–Hinton agar previously inoculated with a standardized suspension (0.5 McFarland) of E. coli ATCC 25922. Plates were incubated at 37 °C for 18–24 h, and carbapenemase activity was determined by assessing the inhibition zone diameter surrounding the meropenem disk.

DNA preparation and PCR analysis

DNA was extracted using the boiling lysate method, as described in previous studies [1], [9]. Briefly, an overnight bacterial culture grown on MHA was mixed with 100 μL of sterile distilled water and boiled for 8 min. The bacterial suspension was then shaken, centrifuged, and the supernatant was used as a template for PCR. Genetic characterization of the isolates was performed through PCR, as outlined in previous studies using single or multiplex PCR, as detailed in the Supplementary Table S1 [1], [20], [21], [22], [23]. Multiplex PCR was used for the detection of blaCTX-M, blaSHV, blaOXA, and blaTEM genes. All blaCTX-M positive isolates were further tested to identify groups one, two, and nine using multiplex PCR [1].

Whole Genome Sequencing (WGS) analysis

Whole genome sequencing (WGS) was performed on 21 selected isolates that were phenotypically positive to carbapenemase and/or ESBL-production and/or showed resistance to broad range of antibiotics. This analysis aimed to validate the phenotypic results and provide further insight into the molecular mechanisms of resistance. DNA was extracted using the commercial Wizard® Genomic DNA Purification Kit from Promega (Wisconsin, USA), according to the manufacturer's instructions. The sequencing was conducted by the commercial service provider Novogene, using the Illumina NovaSeq platform (PE150). Paired-end sequencing reads from the isolates were de novo assembled using the SPAdes assembler [24]. The assembled genomes were subsequently screened for antimicrobial resistance genes, plasmid replicon and multilocus sequence types (MLST) using ResFinder v4.7.2, PlasmidFinder and MLST v2.0, respectively, through the Center for Genomic Epidemiology (CGE) pipeline. These analyses were also performed using the corresponding ABRicate tools implemented on the Galaxy platform (https://galaxy-main.usegalaxy.org/), applying a minimum identity and coverage threshold of 80%.

The genetic context of the blaCTX gene was examined using a combination of nucleotide BLAST analysis (https://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Blast.cgi) and ISfinder (https://isfinder.biotoul.fr/) to detect associated insertion sequence (IS) elements. Assembled contigs were annotated using Prokka [25], and the genomic regions harboring blaCTX were subsequently compared and visualized using Easyfig (https://mjsull.github.io/Easyfig/).

WGS-based phylogenetic analysis was performed using single-nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) analysis and hierarchical clustering (HierCC) based on the core genome multilocus sequence typing (cgMLST) scheme [26]. Raw FASTQ files of the study isolates were uploaded to EnteroBase (https://enterobase.warwick.ac.uk/species/index/ecoli) and analyzed in comparison with publicly available E. coli genomes in Enterobase derived from diverse sources. For lineage-specific analysis, the six isolates belonging to sequence type ST602 were compared with 180 publicly available ST602 genomes, while the single isolate assigned to ST10 was compared with 159 ST10 genomes representing various sources. All seven study isolates were phylogenetically analyzed alongside 143 publicly available isolates from UAE in EnteroBase, using the E. coli K-12 reference genome (substrain MG1655; Biosample ID. SAMN43493033) for mapping and comparative analysis. The Newick file of the phylogenetic tree was visualized with iTOL [27].

Results

Bacterial isolation and characterization

From the 60 aquarium-associated samples analyzed, a total of 69 Gram-negative bacterial isolates were recovered. Identification revealed predominance of Citrobacter spp. (n = 21) and Pseudomonas spp. (n = 20), followed by Acinetobacter spp. (n = 14) and E. coli (n = 9). Additional isolates included Proteus spp. (n = 3), Aeromonas hydrophila (n = 1), and Achromobacter sp. (n = 1).

Phenotypic characterization of the resistance pattern

Phenotypic antimicrobial susceptibility testing revealed a high prevalence of resistance among the Gram-negative bacterial isolates recovered from aquatic and terrestrial animals in the United Arab Emirates (Fig. 1). The highest resistance rates were observed against ampicillin (91.3%) and cefoxitin (89.9%), indicating widespread resistance to β-lactam antibiotics. Resistance to ceftriaxone was also substantial (56.5%), while lower but notable resistance was detected against amoxicillin/clavulanic acid (43.5%) and cefoperazone (26.1%). Carbapenem resistance was detected at comparatively low frequencies, with imipenem resistance observed in 7.2% of isolates and meropenem resistance in 5.8%. The latter corresponded to four isolates, all belonging to the genus Acinetobacter: A. soli harboring blaEBR-4, A. schindleri harboring blaNDM-1 and blaOXA-1005, A. johnsonii harboring a blaOXA-211-like gene, and A. pittii harboring blaOXA-421. These findings suggest limited but concerning circulation of carbapenem-resistant strains within animal reservoirs.

Phenotypic antimicrobial resistance profile of Gram-negative bacterial isolates from aquatic and terrestrial animals in the United Arab Emirates. The bar chart illustrates the percentage of isolates resistant to each tested antibiotic, including β-lactams, carbapenems, quinolones, tetracyclines, chloramphenicol, and aminoglycosides. High resistance rates were observed for ampicillin and cefoxitin, while carbapenems and aminoglycosides showed the lowest resistance frequencies.

Among non-β-lactam antibiotics, resistance to nalidixic acid was relatively high (58%), while moderate resistance levels were observed for tetracycline (31.9%), chloramphenicol (31.9%), and ciprofloxacin (29%). In contrast, resistance to aminoglycosides remained low, with amikacin resistance detected in 5.8% of isolates and gentamicin resistance in only 4.3%, indicating preserved activity of this antimicrobial class.

A high level of MDR was observed, with 37.7% of the isolates exhibiting resistance to at least one antibiotic in three or more antimicrobial classes, indicating widespread MDR among the recovered Gram-negative bacteria (Fig. 1). Phenotypic detection of ESBL production using the DDST revealed that 87% of the isolates were ESBL producers, while 13% were non-ESBL producers (Fig. 2A). Further comparison of the phenotypic and genomic data revealed that all MDR isolates were ESBL producers. Furthermore, carbapenemase production assessed by mCIM showed that 14.5% of the isolates were positive for carbapenemase production, whereas 85.5% were negative (Fig. 2B).

Genotypic characterization of the β-lactam resistance

Genotypic analysis by PCR revealed the presence of several β-lactamase–encoding genes among the studied Gram-negative isolates (Fig. 2C). The most frequently detected gene was blaSHV (31.9%), followed by blaTEM (26.1%), blaCTX-M (13.0%), and blaOXA (4.3%). Overall, 53.6% of the isolates were negative for the four β-lactamase gene families targeted by the PCR assays. This finding does not exclude the presence of other β-lactamase genes or alternative resistance mechanisms, including AmpC production, reduced membrane permeability, efflux-pump activity, and alterations in penicillin-binding proteins. All detected blaCTX-M genes belonged exclusively to the blaCTX-M group 1, indicating the predominance of this clinically important lineage among ESBL-producing isolates.

Analysis of β-lactam resistance gene combinations demonstrated diverse genetic patterns within the isolates (Fig. 2D). The most common gene combination was blaSHV/blaTEM (15.9%), followed by single-gene carriage of blaSHV (13.0%) and blaTEM/blaCTX-M (8.7%). Less frequent patterns included blaCTX-M alone (4.3%), blaOXA alone (1.45%), blaSHV/blaOXA (1.45%), and blaSHV/blaTEM/blaOXA (1.45%). More than half of the isolates (53.6%) did not harbor any of the screened β-lactam resistance genes.

The sequenced isolates represented diverse bacterial species, including E. coli, Citrobacter freundii, P. aeruginosa, Acinetobacter spp., A. hydrophila, Achromobacter spanius, and Empedobacter brevis.

Among E. coli, all sequenced isolates belonged predominantly to ST602, with one isolate identified as ST10. The E. coli ST602 isolates consistently harbored blaCTX-M-15 in combination with blaTEM-1B, explaining their ESBL-positive phenotype. These isolates also carried multiple non-β-lactam resistance genes, including aminoglycoside resistance genes (aph(3″)-Ib, aph(6)-Id), trimethoprim resistance (dfrA14), tetracycline resistance (tet(A)), quinolone resistance (qnrS1), sulfonamide resistance (sul2), and the multidrug efflux gene mdf(A). Plasmid analysis revealed the presence of IncFIB replicons, including IncFIB(AP001918) and IncFIB(K)_1_Kpn3, supporting plasmid-mediated dissemination of ESBL and multidrug resistance traits. The E. coli ST10 isolate similarly carried blaCTX-M-15 alongside multiple resistance genes and a broader plasmid repertoire (IncFIB, IncFIA, IncB/O/K/Z, and Col156), consistent with its MDR phenotype.

Two Citrobacter freundii isolates, both belonging to ST370, exhibited identical resistance gene profiles, including blaCTX-M-55 and blaCMY-82, explaining the ESBL phenotype and resistance to third-generation cephalosporins. These isolates additionally carried genes conferring resistance to aminoglycosides, trimethoprim, phenicols, tetracyclines, macrolides, quinolones, sulfonamides, and disinfectants (qacEΔ1), highlighting extensive multidrug resistance. The detection of IncR and Col440I plasmids suggests plasmid-mediated accumulation and maintenance of resistance determinants.

The P. aeruginosa isolates belonged to ST892 and ST5817. All P. aeruginosa isolates were phenotypically ESBL-positive, phenotypically positive by mCIM indicating carbapenemase production, and carried intrinsic and acquired β-lactam resistance genes, including blaPDC, the AmpC β-lactamase variants (blaPDC-3 and blaPDC-12) and OXA-type carbapenem-hydrolyzing β-lactamases (blaOXA-396-like and blaOXA-848). These findings explain the ESBL and carbapenemase production observed phenotypically, despite the absence of classical CTX-M genes. The isolates also carried fosA and catB7, conferring resistance to fosfomycin and chloramphenicol, respectively. Plasmid analysis identified IncQ2 replicons in ST5817 isolates, indicating potential horizontal gene transfer capacity.

Several Acinetobacter species were identified, including A. schindleri, A. pittii, A. baumannii, A. soli, and A. johnsonii. Notably, the A. schindleri isolate carried blaNDM-1, together with blaOXA-1005, and was phenotypically positive by mCIM, confirming the presence of a carbapenemase and demonstrating strong concordance between phenotypic and genotypic results. Other Acinetobacter species harbored intrinsic OXA-type carbapenem-hydrolyzing β-lactamases (e.g., blaOXA-51 in A. baumannii, blaOXA-421 in A. pittii, blaOXA-211-like in A. johnsonii) and ADC-type cephalosporinases (blaADC-6 and blaADC-150), explaining their ESBL and carbapenemase production phenotypes, and reduced β-lactam susceptibility. The A. soli isolate carrying subclass B1 metallo-beta-lactamase blaEBR-4 gene and tet(X2) gene highlights the presence of rare and emerging resistance determinants, including tigecycline resistance. Additionally, A. pittii carried, besides the OXA-213 family carbapenem-hydrolyzing class D β-lactamase blaOXA-421, the blaMOC gene, a member of the XUS family subclass B1 metallo-β-lactamases, indicating the coexistence of both serine- and metallo-β-lactamase–mediated resistance mechanisms. In contrast, one carbapenemase-producing A. soli isolate was identified that did not harbor any known carbapenemase-encoding genes, suggesting the involvement of alternative resistance mechanisms or yet uncharacterized carbapenemase determinants.

The A. hydrophila isolate (ST2524) carried cphA subclass B2 metallo-beta-lactamase encoding gene, blaAQU a class C β-lactamase, and blaOXA-12 a class D β-lactamase, consistent with intrinsic and acquired β-lactam resistance mechanisms commonly described in Aeromonas spp. Although ESBL-positive phenotypically, the resistance profile reflects species-specific β-lactamase expression rather than classical ESBL genes.

The A. spanius isolate carried macrolide- and sulfonamide-resistance genes (mph(G), mef(C), sul2), in addition to qacF, supporting a multidrug-resistant phenotype despite the absence of detectable β-lactamase genes; however, its phenotypic ESBL positivity likely reflects intrinsic reduced susceptibility to β-lactams, the activity of non-classical β-lactam resistance mechanisms, or limitations in the current genomic databases for this species. Similarly, E. brevis isolate was phenotypically ESBL-positive despite the absence of detectable β-lactamase–encoding genes by genomic analysis, which may be attributed to intrinsic β-lactam resistance mechanisms, reduced outer-membrane permeability, overexpression of chromosomally encoded enzymes, or the presence of uncharacterized or highly divergent ESBL genes not captured by current resistance databases.

Genetic context of blaCTX-M gene

Analysis of the genomic environment surrounding blaCTX-M in E. coli revealed a highly conserved genetic structure among the sequenced isolates, showing strong similarity to the reference plasmid sequence CP145658.1 (Fig. 3). In all blaCTX-M-positive E. coli isolates, the gene was identified as blaCTX-M-15 and was embedded within a mobile genetic element–rich region characterized by multiple insertion sequences and recombination-associated genes.

Genetic environment of blaCTX-M-15 in Escherichia coli isolates.

Comparison of the genomic regions surrounding blaCTX-M-15 in E. coli isolates (S35R, S37R, S39R, S48C, S50R, S51R, and S53R) and the reference plasmid CP145658.1. Arrows indicate gene orientation. The blaCTX-M-15 gene is located within a conserved mobile genetic element–rich region flanked by insertion sequences, with plasmid-associated genes such as repB supporting plasmid-mediated dissemination. Shaded areas represent nucleotide identity (80–100%) between sequences.

Upstream of blaCTX-M-15, the presence of ISKpn19/IS3-family elements and recombinase genes was observed, which are known to facilitate the mobilization and expression of CTX-M-type ESBL genes. Downstream regions included plasmid-associated genes such as repB, indicating plasmid localization and supporting the role of horizontal gene transfer in the dissemination of blaCTX-M. The conserved synteny across isolates (S35R, S37R, S39R, S48C, S50R, S51R, and S53R) and the high nucleotide identity (>80–100%) suggest clonal spread or acquisition from a common mobile genetic platform.

Additionally, the co-localization of blaCTX-M-15 with other resistance-associated genes, including qnrS1, highlights the potential for co-selection of β-lactam and quinolone resistance under antimicrobial pressure. The preservation of this genetic structure across isolates from different sources underscores the stability and successful dissemination of this resistance module.

Phylogenetic relationship of E. coli isolates

WGS–based phylogenetic analysis was performed using SNP analysis and HierCC derived from cgMLST scheme to investigate the genetic relatedness of the six E. coli isolates belonging to sequence type ST602. These isolates were compared with 180 publicly available ST602 genomes retrieved from international databases (Fig. 4). The analysis revealed that the six ST602 isolates from this study clustered tightly together, exhibiting minimal allelic and SNP differences, indicative of a highly clonal population. Notably, the study isolates clustered most closely with a publicly available genome (BioSample: SAMEA7281004), which was recovered from human feces in the Netherlands in 2013. In addition, the study isolates showed close phylogenetic relationships with several other ST602 genomes recovered from environmental and wildlife sources, including SAMN47130252, isolated from surface water (river/lake water) in Sweden in 2023, and SAMN12500120 and SAMN40183531, both recovered from wild rodents (Mus musculus) in Portugal in 2020.

Furthermore, we investigated the evolutionary context of the E. coli ST10 isolate recovered in this study (S53R). The ST10 isolate was compared with a diverse collection of publicly available ST10 genomes representing multiple sources and geographic regions worldwide (Fig. 5). Phylogenetic analysis revealed that the ST10 isolate clustered within a widely distributed global ST10 lineage, demonstrating close genetic relatedness to several human- and animal-derived isolates. Notably, S53R showed the highest genetic similarity to a human clinical isolate (BioSample: SAMN04357621), which was recovered from human blood in the United Kingdom in 2010. The close clustering of these isolates, despite differences in source, host, and geographic origin, highlights the broad dissemination and genetic stability of the ST10 lineage over time.

Discussion

This study provides an initial assessment of the diversity and antimicrobial resistance profiles of Gram-negative bacteria recovered from aquatic and terrestrial animals in the United Arab Emirates, highlighting the presence of clinically relevant resistance phenotypes in non-human reservoirs. The predominance of Citrobacter spp., Pseudomonas spp., Acinetobacter spp., and E. coli among the recovered isolates is consistent with previous reports describing these genera as common inhabitants of aquatic environments and animal-associated niches, as well as important opportunistic pathogens capable of acquiring antimicrobial resistance [28].

Phenotypic susceptibility testing revealed high resistance to β-lactam antibiotics, particularly ampicillin and cefoxitin, with substantial resistance also observed to third-generation cephalosporins. These findings align with the widespread dissemination of β-lactam resistance mechanisms among Gram-negative bacteria in animal and environmental settings and reflect sustained selective pressure outside clinical environments [1], [9]. In contrast, carbapenem resistance was detected at lower frequencies; however, the identification of carbapenem-resistant isolates in animal-associated samples is noteworthy, as carbapenems are critically important antimicrobials and resistance in non-clinical reservoirs remains a growing concern [1]. Resistance to non-β-lactam antibiotics, including quinolones and tetracyclines, was also observed at moderate levels, while aminoglycosides largely retained activity. This heterogeneous resistance pattern suggests differential selective pressures and antimicrobial usage across animal and environmental compartments. The detection of multidrug resistance in nearly 37.7% of isolates further emphasizes the accumulation of resistance traits across multiple antimicrobial classes. Such MDR profiles in animal and aquatic environments may facilitate the maintenance and dissemination of resistance genes through environmental pathways, food chains, and direct contact [29].

Notably, the high proportion of ESBL-producing isolates (87%) indicates that ESBL production is a dominant resistance mechanism among the recovered Gram-negative bacteria. ESBL-producing organisms have been increasingly reported in animals and aquatic environments worldwide, reinforcing concerns regarding their role in the broader epidemiology of antimicrobial resistance [1], [9]. Additionally, the detection of carbapenemase activity in a subset of isolates highlights the need for continued surveillance, even in settings where carbapenem resistance appears infrequent.

PCR-based screening revealed that blaSHV and blaTEM were the most frequently detected β-lactamase genes, while blaCTX-M was identified at a lower frequency, with all detected blaCTX-M genes belonging to blaCTX-M group 1. This finding is consistent with global trends, where blaCTX-M group 1—particularly blaCTX-M-15—has emerged as the dominant ESBL lineage across human, animal, and environmental reservoirs [30]. The predominance of blaSHV/blaTEM combinations further suggests the circulation of older ESBL-associated lineages alongside more recently emerged CTX-M variants, reflecting a heterogeneous resistome within animal-associated bacterial populations.

More than half of the isolates were negative for the screened β-lactamase genes, despite exhibiting phenotypic ESBL activity. This discrepancy highlights the limitations of targeted PCR assays and suggests the contribution of intrinsic β-lactamases, chromosomal AmpC enzymes, non-classical ESBLs, or regulatory and permeability-associated mechanisms, particularly among non-Enterobacterales such as Pseudomonas, Acinetobacter, Achromobacter, and Empedobacter spp. Similar observations have been reported in environmental and animal isolates, where phenotypic resistance is not always fully explained by known resistance genes [31].

Whole-genome sequencing provided deeper resolution of resistance mechanisms and revealed a diverse array of ESBL- and carbapenemase-encoding genes across multiple bacterial species. The detection of blaCTX-M-15–producing E. coli ST602 and ST10 carrying IncF-family plasmids aligns with their recognized role as high-risk clones and efficient vehicles for plasmid-mediated dissemination of ESBLs and multidrug resistance [32], [33]. Similarly, the identification of Citrobacter freundii ST370 harboring blaCTX-M-55 and blaCMY-82 highlights the role of opportunistic Enterobacterales in accumulating and maintaining complex resistance gene arrays via mobile genetic elements.

Of particular concern we identified for the first time carbapenemase-producing organisms across several clinical species, including Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Acinetobacter spp. and Aeromonas hydrophila. The detection of blaNDM-1 in A. schindleri, together with OXA-type carbapenemases in P. aeruginosa and Acinetobacter spp., represents one of the first reports of such carbapenemase-producing bacteria from animal and environmental sources in the UAE. These findings are particularly alarming given the critical importance of carbapenems in human medicine and the limited therapeutic options available for infections caused by such organisms [4], [6]. The presence of rare and emerging resistance determinants, including blaEBR-4, blaMOC, and tet(X2), further emphasizes the role of animal-associated and environmental niches as reservoirs for novel and underrecognized resistance genes. The coexistence of serine- and metallo-β-lactamases within single isolates, as observed in A. pittii, highlights the increasing genetic complexity of resistance and raises concerns regarding future diagnostic and therapeutic challenges [1], [4], [6], [9], [19]. Further in-depth characterization of the carbapenemase-producing Enterobacterales and non-fermenting Gram-negative isolates identified in this study will be undertaken in future research to elucidate their full genetic makeup, transmission dynamics, and potential public health impact.

Analysis of the genetic context of blaCTX-M-15 in E. coli revealed a highly conserved and mobile resistance module shared among all sequenced isolates, showing strong similarity to the reference plasmid sequence CP145658.1. In all blaCTX-M-positive isolates, the gene was embedded within a mobile genetic element–rich region characterized by insertion sequences and recombination-associated genes, supporting its plasmid-mediated dissemination. The presence of ISKpn19/IS3-family elements upstream of blaCTX-M-15, together with recombinase genes, is consistent with previously described genetic arrangements that enhance the mobilization, expression, and horizontal transfer of CTX-M-type ESBL genes [34]. Downstream localization of plasmid-associated replication genes such as repB further confirms plasmid carriage and highlights the central role of conjugative plasmids in the global spread of blaCTX-M-15 [35]. The high degree of synteny and nucleotide identity (>80–100%) observed across isolates from different sources suggests either recent clonal expansion or acquisition from a common mobile genetic platform. Moreover, the co-localization of blaCTX-M-15 with additional resistance genes, including qnrS1, indicates the potential for co-selection of β-lactam and quinolone resistance under antimicrobial pressure, thereby promoting the persistence and successful dissemination of this resistance module across animal-associated and environmental settings [1].

Whole-genome sequencing–based phylogenetic analyses provided important insights into the evolutionary relationships and dissemination dynamics of ESBL-producing E. coli lineages identified in this study. The tight clustering of the six ST602 isolates, characterized by minimal SNP and allelic differences, indicates a highly clonal population, suggesting either recent dissemination from a common ancestor or sustained circulation of a conserved lineage within animal-associated reservoirs. Such low genomic diversity has been widely reported among successful ESBL-producing E. coli lineages and is often associated with efficient adaptation and persistence across hosts and environments [17], [32], [36].

The close phylogenetic relationship between the animal-derived ST602 isolates and a human fecal isolate from the Netherlands (SAMEA7281004, 2013), as well as with ST602 isolates recovered from environmental water in Sweden and wild rodents in Portugal, highlights the transboundary and cross-sectoral nature of this lineage. These findings strongly support the concept that ESBL-producing E. coli ST602 is not restricted to a single host or ecological niche but circulates among humans, animals, wildlife, and environmental compartments [33]. Similar patterns of global dissemination of ESBL-producing E. coli across disparate reservoirs have been described for other high-risk clones, emphasizing the interconnectedness of resistance reservoirs under a One Health framework [37]. Phylogenetic comparison with UAE-derived genomes further demonstrated that the study ST602 isolates are closely related to locally recovered ST602 isolates from environmental (2021) and food (broiler carcass, 2023) sources, indicating the circulation of a locally established ST602 lineage within the UAE. This finding suggests ongoing exchange between animal, food, and environmental reservoirs and reinforces the need for integrated national surveillance strategies that include non-clinical settings [17].

In parallel, analysis of the ST10 isolate (S53R) revealed its placement within a widely distributed global ST10 lineage, showing close relatedness to a human bloodstream isolate from the United Kingdom (2010). ST10 is recognized as a highly adaptable lineage frequently associated with both commensal and pathogenic E. coli, and has been implicated in the global dissemination of ESBL and multidrug resistance genes across humans, animals, and food-producing systems [38]. The persistence of this lineage over time and across geographic regions highlights its evolutionary success and zoonotic potential. Notably, the clustering of the ST10 isolate with poultry- and food-associated isolates in the UAE, as well as within a broader clade that included ST1585, suggests circulation of related lineages across the food production continuum. Such clade-level relatedness, rather than strict sequence-type confinement, has been increasingly recognized in cgMLST- and SNP-based analyses and reflects shared evolutionary backgrounds and ecological adaptation rather than recent clonal expansion alone [17], [38].

Conclusion

This study provides comprehensive characterization of carbapenemase- ESBL–producing Gram-negative bacteria recovered from aquatic and terrestrial animals in the UAE, integrating phenotypic resistance profiling, targeted molecular detection, whole-genome sequencing, and phylogenetic analyses. The findings demonstrate that animal-associated and environmental niches in the UAE harbor a diverse range of clinically relevant Gram-negative bacteria exhibiting high levels of β-lactam resistance, multidrug resistance, and most critically, carbapenemase production. The high prevalence of ESBL-producing isolates, dominated by blaCTX-M group 1 genes, together with the detection of multiple carbapenemase determinants—including blaNDM-1 and OXA-type carbapenemases—underscores the growing complexity of antimicrobial resistance mechanisms circulating outside clinical settings. Notably, this study represents one of the first detailed genomic reports of carbapenemase-producing Gram-negative bacteria from animal and environmental sources in the UAE, highlighting the expansion of critically important resistance determinants into non-human reservoirs. WGS revealed that resistance dissemination is driven by both successful clonal lineages and mobile genetic elements, particularly plasmids carrying blaCTX-M-15 and associated resistance genes. Phylogenetic analyses further demonstrated that ESBL-producing E. coli lineages identified in this study are closely related to strains recovered from humans, food, wildlife, and the environment at both national and global scales. These findings provide compelling genomic evidence for cross-sectoral circulation and reinforce the interconnectedness of antimicrobial resistance reservoirs across the human–animal–environment interface. Collectively, the results of this study emphasize the critical importance of incorporating aquatic and terrestrial animals, wildlife, food, and environmental sources into national antimicrobial resistance surveillance programs. A coordinated One Health approach, supported by genomic epidemiology, is essential to monitor the emergence and spread of high-risk resistant clones and to inform targeted interventions aimed at mitigating the public health impact of antimicrobial resistance in the UAE and beyond.
