PET imaging of extradigital glomus tumor: a case report and literature review
Authors: Regina Hopson, Patricia Connor, James Bennett, Scott Sommerville
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTExtradigital glomus tumors are uncommon and challenging to diagnose. The patient had debilitating pain, and equivocal findings on examination and other soft tissue imaging modalities, without diagnosis or effective treatment, despite consulting a broad range of medical specialties. An incidental finding on fluorine-18 fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography (18F-FDG PET) of a thigh lesion located at the site of 8 years of undiagnosed and treatment resistant pain led to a diagnosis of glomus tumor. The PET findings ultimately facilitated complete surgical excision and definitive cure. This case presents the utility of 18F-FDG PET as an imaging modality in patients with otherwise unexplained localized pain, to identify a lesion and lead to more timely diagnosis and treatment.KEYWORDS: Glomus tumor, positron emission tomography, computed tomography, diagnostic imaging, chronic pain, musculoskeletal pain, 18F-FDG PET, PET imaging

Introduction and literature review

A glomus tumor is an uncommon benign perivascular neoplasm originating from smooth muscle cells of the glomus body, an arteriovenous anastomosis surrounded by smooth muscle and neural cells that regulates heat [1–4]. Most commonly found in the digital extremities and usually subungual, glomus tumors are characterized by intense episodes of pain out of proportion to the size of the tumor, often radiating away from the lesion, exquisite pinpoint tenderness and cold hypersensitivity [2,4–8]. Definitive treatment is complete surgical excision [1,2,4,7,9]. Malignancy is rare [4,9].

Plain radiograph, computed tomography (CT), magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and ultrasonography (US) features of glomus tumor are well established. Plain radiograph classically shows a mass with well-demarcated scalloped erosion of adjacent bone [2,4,5,7–11]. CT reveals a well-demarcated lytic lesion, minimal peripheral sclerosis and no periosteal reaction [2,10], with rapid contrast enhancement [10]. MRI features typically are low to intermediate T1-weighted signal, hyperintense T2-weighted signal and avid enhancement with gadolinium [2,5–14]. Finally, US features include solid, well-defined, hypoechoic oval nodules with erosion of bone [9–14], internal venous and arterial flow, and sometimes the vascular stalk sign on doppler [10–14].

This paper discusses a recent case of an extradigital glomus tumor of the thigh and the utility of fluorine-18 fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography (18F-FDG PET) in presentations of paroxysmal pain and an impalpable lesion with equivocal findings on routine soft tissue imaging modalities.

Case summary

A mid-40s male experienced 6 years of gradually worsening, episodic, non-radiating pain that had plateaued for a further 2 years, in the left proximal anteromedial thigh, without an initial precipitating factor. He described pinpoint tenderness to light palpation and disproportionate pain when ascending steps or standing from a seated position. At the peak of symptoms, an intense cramp or spasm of the muscles of the anteromedial thigh occurred. Symptoms were temporarily improved by some forms of physical activity, particularly swimming, but were non-responsive to analgesia, including anti-inflammatory and neuropathic agents. Over time, increasing pain resulted in a pronounced compensatory gait pattern with secondary left anterior thigh disuse muscle wasting.

Exploratory MRI of the thigh at 3 years (Figure 1) and 6 years (Figure 2) post symptom onset failed to reveal a cause. The MRI reports described features of hamstring origin tendinopathy with intrasubstance tearing, and edema in the vastus muscles suspicious for myositis of the anterior compartment, respectively. The report of the latter of the two earlier MRIs provided a differential that that finding could be due to field inhomogeneity, which in that location would most likely be due to femoral artery pulsation artifact given that vessel’s proximity.

Seven years after the onset of pain, new shortness of breath at rest resulted in a cardiac MRI with the identification of enlarged pulmonary nodules, ultimately diagnosed as pulmonary sarcoidosis. PET imaging (Figures 3 and 4) was employed with the intention of characterizing the extent of systemic sarcoidosis. Incidentally, a small, atypical area of tracer avidity was identified within the left proximal anteromedial thigh, correlating with the patient’s described pain location. It was presumed to be small nodal sarcoidosis.

18F-FDG-PET image at 7 years post symptom onset.

On specialist orthopedic examination, the lesion was impalpable. Apart from quadriceps wasting and tenderness at the site correlating to the PET-avidity in the thigh, examination of the leg was otherwise normal.

Repeat MRI (Figure 5) and diagnostic US were obtained to assess for the suitability of hookwire localization to facilitate excision under anesthesia. This MRI report described the classic, although not specific, glomus tumor features of hyperintensity on T2 weighted imaging and slight hyperintensity on T1. On retrospective review of the two earlier exploratory MRIs these features could be observed, albeit they were subtle and the lesion smaller, and confounded by femoral artery pulsation artifact.

Axial PDFS MR image at 8 years post symptom onset.

Ultrasound-guided hookwire localization occurred and complete excision followed. On histopathological examination, the lesion measured 10 mm × 8 mm × 7 mm, was clear of all margins and showed features of a glomus tumor. This was characterized by sheets and nests of monomorphic cells intermixed with small vascular spaces, and set within a predominantly hyalinized fibrous stroma. The tumor cells exhibited round nuclei, fine nuclear chromatin, and moderate eosinophilic cytoplasm. Malignant features were not identified. On immunohistochemistry, the cells showed positive expression of smooth muscle actin and caldesmon stains. The recovery post-excision has been remarkable; at 1 month and 10 months post excision, the pain was non-existent, and with rehabilitation exercises, muscle mass is returning and gait normalizing.

Discussion

Extradigital glomus tumors present a diagnostic conundrum, with patients suffering ongoing symptoms, often for years, while other more likely diagnoses are pursued.

There are 23 prior cases of glomus tumors of the thigh reported in the literature. Of these, five were impalpable [13,15–18], nine were described as immobile masses [19–27], while this feature was not published for the remaining nine [28–32]. Where the lesion is impalpable on clinical exam, the difficulty of diagnosing and managing these lesions increases; imaging is vital to locate the offending lesion, inform differentials and aid excision planning. The anesthesia required for excision removes the ability to localize the lesion to pain and in our case ultrasound-guided hookwire localization pre-anaesthetic was utilized.

While the plain radiograph, CT, MRI and US findings of a glomus tumor are well established, there is minimal literature on the use of PET imaging characteristics to aid in diagnosis and excision planning. In our case, the incidental finding that the lesion was PET-avid demonstrates the potential utility of 18F-FDG PET as an imaging modality for assisting in the investigative pathway for a patient with unexplained significant pain with an unremarkable examination and no overt abnormality on other imaging modalities.

To our knowledge, none of the published cases of glomus tumors of the thigh had a PET scan or utilized PET as an investigation tool or diagnostic aid. More generally, there have been only five case reports of glomus tumor that also report 18F-FDG PET findings. Two glomus tumors of the axilla have been reported separately to have had 18F-FDG PET: one showed intense avidity [33]; the other, a mildly avid lesion [10]. Shao presented a benign glomus tumor of the trachea that had intense avidity on 18F-FDG PET [34]. A case report of multiple primary lung glomus tumors reports high avidity on 18F-FDG PET [35]. Finally, a 2017 case report of glomangiomatosis, presenting as multiple lower leg and ankle lesions, reported the lesions as avid on 18F-FDG PET [36].

In the time since the histopathology of glomus tumors was first described in 1924 [4], the advancements in radiological imaging modalities and availability have been remarkable. This case highlights the potential utility of 18F-FDG PET as an imaging modality to aid diagnosis in the patient who presents with a history of paroxysmal pain, tenderness to palpation but no palpable mass, MRI findings that may not be overtly obvious or are consistent with a variety of soft tissue lesions, and otherwise equivocal investigations. Glomus tumor ought to be on the list of differentials in such a case and PET would, we suggest, be highly useful in supporting that differential and further aiding more timely and appropriate management.

Conclusion

Extradigital glomus tumor ought to be a differential in patients with paroxysmal pain out of proportion to lesion size (on examination or imaging), where no other more likely explanation is present. Early imaging of the symptomatic region, particularly in the absence of a palpable lesion, including 18F-FDG PET, could lead to more timely diagnosis, excision and cure.

Patient perspective

The pain started off minor, then for many years was debilitating. Test after test and specialist after specialist with no diagnosis or any indication as to how much worse the pain would get. It was the consequential effects that had the greatest impact on my life and caused me to continue to seek medical opinions. Over the years, my left thigh gradually became weaker as I favored my right leg and avoided activities, attempting to limit the pain. Daily activities that I previously had taken for granted, including standing up, crossing the road, getting out of the car and walking up stairs, came with severe pain. Actively avoiding some activities to minimize pain had a significant detrimental impact on my health, work and life generally.

Having the PET scan was the turning point because it finally led to treatment and definite cause for the pain. The improvement I felt after the tumor was removed was instant and complete. I have slowly regained strength in my left thigh, and I am no longer avoiding activities that once caused me pain.
