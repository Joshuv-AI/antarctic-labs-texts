Exposure to tobacco smoke, concurrent short-term symptoms of respiratory, allergic, cardiovascular morbidities and health-services use among young adults in Kuwait
Authors: Saeed Akhtar, Sarah Almutairi, Wadhah Alhajri, Sarah Alrashidi, Nouf Almutairi, Ghadeer Almutairi, Manar Aladwani, Lulowa Al-Sanafi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundExposure to tobacco smoke remain major public health concerns due to its’ association with multiple morbidities and increased health-services use. This cross-sectional study aimed to, (i) assess the prevalence of exposure to tobacco smoke (first-hand and second-hand) among students at a public university; (ii) assess the prevalence of tobacco smoke-related concurrent morbidities; (iii) assess the prevalence of health-services use (≥ 1 vs. none) during the past one-year; and (iv) evaluate the association between demographics, tobacco smoking, concurrent morbidities, and health-services use during the past year.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted in October 2022 among undergraduate students at Kuwait University using a structured electronic questionnaire. Participants were recruited as a sample of convenience. The prevalences (%) of exposure to tobacco smoke (1st hand and 2nd hand at home) were estimated. Self-reported respiratory symptoms, physician-diagnosed morbidities, and health-services use (≥ 1 visit vs. none) during the past year were assessed. Multivariable logistic regression analysis was conducted to examine the association between sociodemographics, lifestyle factors, concurrent morbidities, and health-services use during the past year. The adjusted odds ratios (OR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) were used to interpret the final model.ResultsOf 1,323 participants, the majority were Kuwaiti (87.5%) and female (75.8%). The prevalence of exposure to first-hand tobacco smoke was 13.4% (177/1,323), while 50.8% (672/1,323) reported exposure to second-hand tobacco smoke at home. Overall, the prevalence of exposure to tobacco smoke was 55.9% (740/1323). The reported respiratory symptoms were shortness of breath (59.1%), cough (46.3%), chest pain (26.9%), and difficulty during exercise (21.6%). Physician-diagnosed conditions included asthma (16.8%), eczema (11.2%), and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (1.4%). Of the participants, 44.6% (590/1323) used the health-services at least once during the past year. In the multivariable model, after adjusting for effects of age and sex, participants were significantly more likely to report at least one instance of health-services use during the past year if they reported cough (adjusted OR = 2.10; 95% CI: 1.66–2.64; p < 0.001), shortness of breath (adjusted OR = 1.30; 95% CI: 1.01–1.67; p = 0.044), nausea/vomiting (adjusted OR = 2.52; 95% CI: 1.79–3.54; p < 0.001), or asthma (adjusted OR = 1.95; 95% CI: 1.43–2.65; p < 0.001). However, tobacco smoking (active and/ or secondhand) was not significantly associated with health services use at least once during the past year in this evaluation.ConclusionsThis study reports high prevalence of second-hand tobacco smoke exposure at home among young adults, along with high levels of respiratory and allergic morbidities. Although tobacco exposure (first and/ or secondhand) itself was not directly associated with health-services use at least once in the past year, smoking-related symptoms and ill-health conditions were significantly associated with health-care use at least once during the past year. Public health interventions aimed at reducing exposure to tobacco smoke may help decrease related morbidities and subsequent health-services use among young adults.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12913-026-15530-x.

Introduction

Tobacco use remains a major global public health challenge. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), tobacco causes approximately 6 million deaths each year worldwide and accounts for about one in every ten adult deaths. If current trends continue, nearly 500 million people alive today are expected to die prematurely due to tobacco-related diseases [1, 2]. Smoking often begins early in life, with about 90% of smokers initiating tobacco use during their teenage years, making adolescence and early adulthood critical periods for prevention [3, 4].

In the Middle Eastern countries, tobacco use among adolescents and young adults has become an important public health concern. Previous studies reported that around 30% of university students were current smokers in Bahrain, Saudi Arabia, and Kuwait [5–8]. Another study found that 31% of Kuwaiti male students and 3% of female students were current smokers, with a mean smoking initiation age of 17.5 years [9]. University students represent a critical population because smoking behaviors established during early adulthood may persist throughout life and contribute to chronic diseases. However, many college students have limited awareness of the harmful effects of tobacco, and perceptions of what constitutes a “smoker” may influence smoking behavior and cessation efforts [9–11].

Tobacco smoking has been linked to several adverse health outcomes, including respiratory symptoms, increased medical visits, emergency department use, and hospitalizations [12, 13]. Adolescents exposed to tobacco smoke may also experience increased respiratory problems and higher health-care utilization. Despite growing evidence, data on the relationship between tobacco exposure, morbidity, and health-services use among young adults remain limited in many Middle Eastern countries, particularly in Kuwait. Therefore, this cross-sectional study aimed to; (i) assess the prevalence of exposure to tobacco smoke (first-hand and second-hand) among students at a public university; (ii) assess the prevalence of tobacco smoke-related concurrent morbidities; (iii) assess the prevalence of health-care use (≥ 1 vs. none) during the past year; and (iv) evaluate the association between demographics, tobacco smoking, concurrent morbidities, and health-care utilization during the past year. Understanding these relationships may help inform targeted screening and counseling strategies for young adults and contribute to efforts aimed at reducing the burden of chronic disease and health-services use in this and other similar settings.

Methods

Study design, setting, and participants

This cross-sectional study was conducted in October 2022 among young adults enrolled at Kuwait University. Data were collected using a self-administered questionnaire distributed to male and female students of any nationality (Kuwaiti and non-Kuwaiti). The objectives of the study were to: (i) assess the prevalence of exposure to tobacco smoke (first-hand and second-hand) among students at a public university; (ii) assess the prevalence of tobacco smoke-related concurrent morbidities; (iii) assess the prevalence of health-care use (≥ 1 vs. none) during the past year; and (iv) evaluate the association between demographics, tobacco smoking, concurrent morbidities, and health-care utilization during the past year.

Questionnaire

A structured self-administered questionnaire was developed in both English and Arabic. The questionnaire consisted of 24 items including dichotomous (yes/no) and multiple-choice questions. It comprised two sections. Section I included close-ended questions on participants’ sociodemographics and lifestyle characteristics, including gender, age (in completed years), and nationality (Kuwaiti or non-Kuwaiti). Participants were also asked about their monthly family income in Kuwaiti Dinars (KD) (less than 500, 500–999, 1000–1500, or more than 1500) and marital status (single, married, divorced, or widowed). In addition, participants were asked about the college they were enrolled in and their field of study. Section II included questions related to tobacco smoking. Participants were asked whether they or any of their parents smoked and what type of tobacco products they used (cigarettes, water-pipe, vape, or others). Additional questions assessed the age at which participants first smoked, factors that encouraged smoking initiation, the duration (years) of smoking, and the approximate number of cigarettes smoked per day. Participants were also asked whether they had been informed about the health effects of smoking, including yellow teeth, wrinkles, unpleasant odor, lung cancer, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, peptic ulcer disease, bladder cancer, and cardiovascular disease. Current smokers were further asked whether they had ever considered or attempted to quit smoking and their level of interest in quitting (strongly interested, somewhat interested, slightly interested, or not interested at all). They were also asked whether any family members had advised them to quit smoking.

Participants were asked whether they had experienced any of the following symptoms during the past year: cough, chest pain, shortness of breath, oral infections, difficulty exercising, lip discoloration (darker contour), nausea/vomiting, or heartburn. They were also asked whether they believed any of these symptoms were related to tobacco consumption. Furthermore, participants reported whether they had sought medical care or had been admitted to the hospital or undergone surgery during the past year. Additionally, they were asked whether they had been diagnosed with asthma, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (chronic bronchitis or emphysema) (COPD), or eczema during the past year. Finally, participants were asked to rate their overall health as excellent, very good, good, fair, or poor. The questionnaire was reviewed by two faculty members, and their feedback was taken into account in the final version of the questionnaire (Supplement 1. Questionnaire English version).

Sampling, sample size, and data collection

The target population for this study comprised young adults in Kuwait, including both males and females of any nationality (Kuwaiti or non-Kuwaiti). Data were collected over five days, from 16 to 20 October 2022, from students enrolled in six colleges at Kuwait University: Medicine, Pharmacy, Engineering and Petroleum, Business Administration, Life Sciences, and Education. Approval was obtained from the deans of the selected colleges to approach students for participation. The research team consisted of seven students who were divided into three data-collection groups. Each group was assigned two colleges to visit. Class schedules were obtained from the Office of the Vice Dean for Student Affairs of each college. Permission to approach students was obtained from the lecturers of each class via Microsoft Teams. We used the convenience sampling method to enroll the study participants. The questionnaire was distributed by members of the research team through a QR code at either the beginning or the end of the class session. Students were given approximately 10 min to complete the questionnaire.

For this cross-sectional study, a minimum sample size of 990 participants was estimated to assess the prevalence of self-reported health-services use with a 95% confidence level (CI) and a 5% margin of error, assuming a prevalence of health-services use of 20% in the target population. To account for potential refusals, a design effect of 1, and the need to generate estimates across age–sex groups, the sample size was increased to 1,200 participants. This sample size was also adequate to examine associations between the outcome and most potential exposures with a prevalence of at least 25% of the general population.

Ethics

The research protocol, consent form, and data collection instrument were approved by the Ethics Committee of the Health Sciences Center of Kuwait University (# 272/ dated 25/1/2021). The questionnaire included an informed consent statement explaining the purpose of the study and indicating that participation was completely voluntary. Participants were informed that no personal identifying information would be collected and that the survey was anonymous to ensure confidentiality of the data. The consent statement also indicated that participants could decline participation or withdraw from the study at any time without any negative consequences.

Statistical analysis

Data collected through the QR-code questionnaire were exported to a Microsoft Excel file and subsequently imported into Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS, version 29) for analysis. Descriptive statistics, including frequencies and percentages (%), were calculated to summarize the sociodemographic and lifestyle characteristics of the study participants. The prevalence of health-service use during the past year was also calculated. The Chi-squared test was used to evaluate the statistical significance of bivariate associations between sociodemographic variables, lifestyle factors (including exposure to tobacco smoke—first-hand, second-hand, or both), concurrent morbidities, and health-service use. Univariable logistic regression analysis was conducted to estimate the magnitude of the associations between variables that were significantly related to health-service use during the past year in the Chi-squared analysis (p ≤ 0.200) [14]. Simulation studies have indicated that this approach is reasonable, in that use of a 0.20 or higher alpha (α) level instead of a 0.05 level for confounder selection can make the difference between acceptable and poor performance of statistical testing for confounder selection. Using this more inclusive threshold at the bivariate screening stage helps reduce the risk of excluding potentially important predictor variables (i.e., minimizing Type II error) before multivariable model development [14–16]. Variables showing statistical significance (p ≤ 0.200) in both the Chi-squared analysis and univariable logistic regression analysis were considered for inclusion in the multivariable logistic regression model. Previously suggested backward stepwise deletion procedure was used to develop the final multivariable model [17]. Missing data were handled using listwise deletion. Variables that remained statistically significant (p < 0.05) and demonstrated a biologically plausible relationship with the outcome variable were retained in the final model. Adjusted odds ratios (ORs) and their 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated to interpret the final model.

Results

Sociodemographic characteristics

Of 1350 students invited to participate, 1323 (response rate = 98%) students consented and completed the questionnaire. The common reasons cited for non-participation were ‘not interested’ (11), ‘busy in other matter’ (14) or ‘don’t have time’ (2). In this sample, 42.7% of the participants were aged between 18 and 20 years, 32.4% were between 21 and 22 years of age and 24.9% were 23 years old or older. Most participants were females (75.8%), Kuwaiti (87.5%), the majority (83.7%) were single, 14.1% married, and 2.1% were divorced or widowed. Of participants, 51.5% came from families having an income of more than 1500 Kuwaiti Dinars per month. Participants belong to colleges offering undergraduate degrees either in humanities (53.1%) or science (46.9%) subjects (Table 1a).

Prevalence of current smoking, morbidities, and health-services use

The prevalence of 1st hand smoking among young adults was 13.4% (177/1323). Of participants, the prevalence of 2nd hand smoking (one and/ or both parents smokers) was 50.8% (672/1323). Overall, the prevalence of exposure to tobacco smoke (1st and/ or 2nd hand) was 55.9% (740/1323). Of participants, 44.6% (590/1323) used the health-services at least once during the past year. The characteristics of smokers such as the age at which they started smoking, dose and duration of smoking, and the intent of quitting smoking, etc. in the study sample are given in Appendix 1. Of the participants, reportedly majority (59.1%) had shortness of breath, followed by cough (46.3%), chest pain (26.9%), and difficulty in exercise (21.6%). The distributions of other self-rated and physician-diagnosed morbidities including asthma (17.3%), eczema (11.2%), and COPD (1.4%) are in Table 1b.

Chi-squared analysis and univariable logistic regression analysis of healthcare services use during the past one year

Chi-squared analysis of sociodemographics of the participants showed that age (p = 0.008), sex (p < 0.001), and marital status (p = 0.046) were significantly associated with health services use at least once during the past year. Chi-squared analysis of current smoking status and self-related and physician-diagnosed morbidities showed that smoking status (p < 0.001), having cough (p < 0.001), chest pain (p < 0.001), shortness of breath (p < 0.001), and oral infections (p < 0.001), nausea/ vomiting (p < 0.001), heartburn (p = 0.001), and asthma (p < 0.001) in the past one year were significantly associated with the use of health services at least once (Table 2).

Univariable logistic regression analysis showed the variables significantly associated with the use of health-services at least once during the past one-year among young adults were age between 21 and 22 years (unadjusted OR = 1.46,; CI: 1.14–1.89) or 23 years old or older (unadjusted OR = 1.32; CI: 1.00-1.73; p = 0.008), female sex (unadjusted OR = 1.60; CI: 1.23–2.07; p < 0.001), being married (unadjusted OR = 1.39; CI: 1.01–1.89; p = 0.047), self-smoking (unadjusted OR = 0.50; CI: 0.36–0.70; p < 0.001), parental smoking (unadjusted OR = 1.03; 0.95% CI: 0.83–1.28; p = 0.798) and having cough (unadjusted OR = 2.20; CI:1.76–2.75; p < 0.001), shortness of breath (unadjusted OR = 1.78; CI:1.42–2.23; p < 0.001), chest pain (unadjusted OR = 1.70; CI:1.33–2.17; p < 0.001), and oral infections (unadjusted OR = 2.28; CI: 1.50–3.48; p < 0.001), as well as nausea and vomiting (unadjusted OR = 3.20; CI: 2,36-4.35; p < 0.001), heartburn (unadjusted OR = 1.82; CI:1.28–2.57; p < 0.001), and asthma (unadjusted OR = 1.93; CI:1.44–2.57; p < 0.001) (Table 3).

Multivariable logistic regression model

The final multivariable logistic regression model of the factors associated with the health services use at least once among young adults during the past one year showed a statistically significant relationship with age (age 21–22 years, adjusted OR = 1.55; CI:1.19–2.02, p = 0.001; age ≥ 23 years, adjusted OR = 1.36; CI:1.02–1.82; p = 0.037) in comparison with young adults in age range 18–20 years. Of the self-rated or physician-diagnosed morbidities significantly associated with healthcare use among the participants were self-reported cough (adjusted OR = 2.09; CI:1.66–2.63; p < 0.001), shortness of breath (adjusted OR = 1.31; CI:1.01–1.68; p = 0.039), nausea/vomiting (adjusted OR = 2.62; CI:1.87–3.68; p < 0.001) or physician-diagnosed asthma (adjusted OR = 1.90; CI:1.40–2.57; p < 0.001) (Table 4).

Discussion

The present study assessed the prevalence of health-services use among young adults in Kuwait and examined its association with tobacco smoking and selected morbidities. A total of 1,323 students from Kuwait University participated in this cross-sectional study using a self-administered questionnaire.

The prevalence of current first-hand smoking among participants was 13.4%, while 50.8% reported exposure to second-hand smoke at home due to parental smoking. Overall, 55.9% of participants were exposed to tobacco smoke through first-hand and/or second-hand smoking. Health service utilization at least once during the past year was reported by 44.6% of participants. After adjusting for age and tobacco smoke exposure, cough, shortness of breath, nausea/vomiting, and asthma remained independently associated with health service utilization at least once during the past year.

The prevalence of current first-hand smoking in this study (13.4%) is consistent with previous findings among high school students in Kuwait [18], but lower than estimates reported in studies from China, Iran, and Pakistan [19–21]. Similarly, the overall prevalence of tobacco smoke exposure (55.9%) is comparable to that reported in a previous Kuwaiti study [18], and other regional studies [22, 23]. Similarities and differences between these findings and those of the present study may be attributed to differences in age distribution, sociodemographic characteristics, and cultural norms across the study populations. In particular, the stigma associated with smoking among females in many settings may have contributed to underreporting [22].

In the present study, a high proportion of participants reported experiencing respiratory and related symptoms during the past year. The most commonly reported symptom was shortness of breath (59.1%), followed by cough (46.3%), chest pain (26.9%), and difficulty in exercising (21.6%). Additionally, the prevalence of physician-diagnosed asthma, eczema, and COPD among participants was 17.3%, 11.2%, and 1.4%, respectively. The high prevalence of shortness of breath observed in this study is consistent with findings from a previous study conducted among students at the University of Minnesota in 2004, which reported that 64.6% of participants who smoked on 21 or more days during the previous month experienced at least one day of shortness of breath, compared with 42.7% among non-smokers [23]. In contrast, an earlier study assessing the prevalence of asthma among young adults aged 15–34 years reported a prevalence of 2.2% [24]. The higher prevalence of asthma observed in the present study may be related to differences in study populations, environmental exposures, or increasing levels of air pollution in recent years.

Previous studies conducted among high-school students in Kuwait reported a prevalence of eczema as14.8% [25, 26], which is slightly higher than the prevalence observed in the present study (11.2%). These differences may be attributed to variations in the age groups included in the respective studies. Similarly, the prevalence of COPD in this study (1.4%) was lower than that reported in older populations. For example, a recent study reported a prevalence of 4.2% among individuals aged 40–50 years in Korea [27]. Another study conducted in China estimated the overall prevalence of COPD to range between 1.2% and 8.9%, with an average prevalence of 5.9%, with significantly lower prevalence among individuals younger than 35 years [28]. Previous research has also shown that COPD prevalence is considerably higher among elderly populations and smokers [29]. Recently a study from Qatar showed higher odds of chest pain among the participants living in households with two or more smokers [30]. In the backdrop of growing evidence of respiratory and allergic morbidities among young adults exposed to tobacco smoke through first-hand and/ or second-hand routes and increasing burden of health-service use needs concerted efforts by the public health authorities.

The final multivariable logistic regression model showed that participants were significantly more likely to use health-care services during the past year if they were aged ≥ 21 years or reported symptoms such as nausea/vomiting, cough, shortness of breath, or physician-diagnosed asthma. These findings are consistent with the results of previous studies indicating that respiratory and gastrointestinal symptoms are strong predictors of health-care utilization. For instance, a recent study reported that health-care costs associated with chronic cough are largely driven by clinic consultations [31]. Similarly, a study conducted in Bangladesh among individuals aged ≥ 15 years who reported persistent cough for at least three weeks found that 61.9% of participants sought medical care [32]. Another study reported that patients with nausea and vomiting had significantly higher rates of hospitalization, emergency department visits, and physician consultations compared with those without such symptoms [33]. In addition, shortness of breath has been identified as one of the most commonly reported symptoms associated with increased health-care utilization [34]. Furthermore, asthma has been consistently associated with increased use of health-care services. A study conducted in Singapore among patients with asthma reported that approximately half of the respondents had at least one episode of acute health-care utilization during the previous year [35]. Similarly, data from the United States indicated that in 2007, there were approximately 1.75 million asthma-related emergency department visits and 456,000 asthma-related hospitalizations [36]. These findings support the results observed in the present study of avoidable economic burden on country’s health services calling for the necessary attention of public health authorities and overall society at large.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several notable strengths. First, the study included a relatively large sample size (n = 1323), which enhances the statistical power of the analysis. Second, the study collected information on both participants’ smoking behaviors and parental smoking status, allowing assessment of exposure to both first-hand and second-hand tobacco smoke. Third, the study examined multiple respiratory and physician-diagnosed morbidities in relation to health-care utilization. However, several limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the study used a convenience sampling method; therefore, the results may not be fully generalizable to all young adults in Kuwait. However, Kuwaiti young adults share broadly similar lifestyles, educational backgrounds, and access to healthcare services. Therefore, we believe that the study findings are reasonably generalizable beyond the study sample to the wider population of Kuwaiti young adults, although some caution should be exercised when extrapolating the results to other populations. Second, data on health-care utilization and symptoms during the past year were collected using a self-administered questionnaire and are therefore subject to recall bias. Third, the majority of participants in this study were female (75.8%). Owing to prevailing social and cultural norms and the limited social acceptability of smoking among females, smoking may have been underreported. This reporting bias may partly explain the inverse or null association between tobacco smoking and health-service utilization observed in both the univariable and multivariable analyses. Finally, data on concurrent morbidities were self-reported and were not clinically validated using medical records or physician verification. However, given that the study population comprised young adults and university students, the likelihood of disease misclassification is expected to be minimal.

Conclusions

This study found among young adults, a moderate prevalence of 1st -hand tobacco smoking (13.4%), high prevalences of 2nd -hand tobacco smoke exposure through parental smoking at home (50.8%) and high prevalence of exposure to tobacco smoke from any source including 1st -hand and/ or 2nd -hand (55.9%). The study also revealed relatively high prevalences of tobacco smoking related respiratory and allergic morbidities, including shortness of breath, cough, chest pain, difficulty in exercising, nausea/vomiting, and physician-diagnosed asthma and eczema. In addition, a substantial proportion of participants (44.6%) reported health-services use at least once during the past year.

Although self-smoking and/or parental smoking were not independently associated with increased health service utilization in the multivariable analysis, smoking-related respiratory and allergic morbidities were independently associated with increased health service utilization. These findings underscore the need for targeted public health interventions to reduce exposure to both first-hand and second-hand tobacco smoke among young adults. Supporting the implementation of tobacco-free policies in academic institutions may help reduce tobacco exposure, the burden of smoking-related respiratory and allergic morbidities, and the subsequent demand for health services. Future studies should evaluate the effectiveness of these interventions in reducing tobacco exposure and improving health outcomes in this and similar settings.
