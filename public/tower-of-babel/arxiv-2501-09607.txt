A posteriori error estimates for the Lindblad master equation
Paul-Louis Etienney, Rémi Robin, Pierre Rouchon

Abstract
We are interested in the simulation of open quantum systems governed by the Lindblad master equation in an infinite-dimensional Hilbert space. To simulate the solution of this equation, the standard approach involves two sequential approximations: first, we truncate the Hilbert space to derive a differential equation in a finite-dimensional subspace. Then, we use discrete time-step to obtain a numerical solution to the finite-dimensional evolution. In this paper, we establish bounds for these two approximations that can be explicitly computed to guarantee the accuracy of the numerical results. Through numerical examples, we demonstrate the efficiency of our method, empirically highlighting the tightness of the upper bound. While adaptive time-stepping is already a common practice in the time discretization of the Lindblad equation, we extend this approach by showing how to dynamically adjust the truncation of the Hilbert space. This enables fully adaptive simulations of the density matrix. For large-scale simulations, this approach can significantly reduce computational time and relieves users of the challenge of selecting an appropriate truncation. Furthermore, as a special case, our method naturally applies to Hamiltonian (unitary) dynamics.

1.1
Presentation of the problem and main contributions

In this article, we focus on simulating open quantum systems obeying the Lindblad master equation
[
20
,
12
,
8
]
. This equation models the evolution of an open quantum system weakly coupled to a Markovian environment
[
4
]
. We are particularly interested in the case where the underlying Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
is infinite-dimensional, with a particular focus on bosonic modes, also known as quantum resonators, or cavities.
More precisely, the Lindblad equation reads as follows:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\rho})

\displaystyle=-i[{\bf H},\boldsymbol{\rho}]+\sum_{i}\mathcal{D}_{{\mathbf{\Gamma}}^{i}}(\boldsymbol{\rho}),

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\boldsymbol{\rho}(t)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\rho}(t)),\quad\boldsymbol{\rho}(0)=\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0},

(1)

where
\boldsymbol{\rho}
is a density operator, that is a self-adjoint positive semidefinite operator of trace one,
{\bf H}
is the Hamiltonian of the system, which is a self-adjoint operator, the bracket denotes the commutator, and the dissipators
\mathcal{D}_{{\mathbf{\Gamma}}^{i}}(\boldsymbol{\rho})
act on
\boldsymbol{\rho}
as

\mathcal{D}_{{\mathbf{\Gamma}}}(\boldsymbol{\rho})={\mathbf{\Gamma}}\boldsymbol{\rho}{{\mathbf{\Gamma}}}^{\dagger}-\frac{1}{2}\left({{\mathbf{\Gamma}}}^{\dagger}{\mathbf{\Gamma}}\,\boldsymbol{\rho}+\boldsymbol{\rho}\,{{\mathbf{\Gamma}}}^{\dagger}{\mathbf{\Gamma}}\right).

(2)

In the case of infinite dimensional Hilbert space and unbounded operators
{\mathbf{\Gamma}}^{i}
and/or
{\bf H}
, special care is needed to define the solution of the equation, we refer to
[
9
,
6
]
for the general case or
[
11
]
for the specific case of bosonic modes.

We are interested in computing an approximation of the solution
\boldsymbol{\rho}(T)
of
Eq.

1
at a final time
T>0
.
To this aim, two intermediate steps are classically performed.

Step 1.

We begin by selecting a finite-dimensional subspace
\mathcal{H}_{N}\subset\mathcal{H}
. Our goal is to define a finite-dimensional approximation of the solutions of
Eq.

1
within this subspace. Let
\mathbf{P}_{N}
denotes the orthogonal projector onto
\mathcal{H}_{N}
. We consider the corresponding truncated operators:

\displaystyle{\bf H}_{N}=\mathbf{P}_{N}{\bf H}\mathbf{P}_{N},\quad{{\mathbf{\Gamma}}^{i}}_{N}=\mathbf{P}_{N}{\mathbf{\Gamma}}^{i}\mathbf{P}_{N},\quad\forall i\in\mathbb{N}.

(3)

For a single bosonic mode, the Hilbert space is given by
\mathcal{H}=\{\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}u_{n}\ket{n}\mid\sum_{n}|u_{n}|^{2}<\infty\}\simeq l^{2}(\mathbb{N})
, where the canonical orthonormal basis is provided by the Fock states
(\ket{n})_{n\geq 0}
, and the corresponding dual basis is
(\bra{n})_{n\geq 0}
. In this setting, a common method is to truncate the Fock basis, namely consider

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{N}=\operatorname{Span}\{\ket{n}\mid 0\leq n\leq N\},\quad\mathbf{P}_{N}=\sum_{i=0}^{N}\ket{i}\bra{i}.

(4)

Next, we define the following Lindbladian for both density operators on
\mathcal{H}
and on
\mathcal{H}_{N}
.

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{N}(\boldsymbol{\rho})

\displaystyle=-i[{\bf H}_{N},\boldsymbol{\rho}]+\sum_{i}\mathcal{D}_{{{\mathbf{\Gamma}}^{i}}_{N}}(\boldsymbol{\rho}).

(5)

Thus, we can define the approximated solution
{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}(t)
by

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}(t)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{L}_{N}({\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}(t)),\quad{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}(0)=\mathbf{P}_{N}\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0}\mathbf{P}_{N}.

(6)

It is important to keep in mind that
\mathbf{P}_{N}\boldsymbol{\rho}(t)\mathbf{P}_{N}
is not equal in general to
{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}(t)
; but we expect
{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}(t)
to be an approximation of
\boldsymbol{\rho}(t)
. For the sake of simplicity, we assume that
\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0}
has support in
\mathcal{H}_{N}
, that is
\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0}=\mathbf{P}_{N}\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0}\mathbf{P}_{N}
(see
Remark

1
for the general case).

Step 2:

Approximate the solution of the linear differential equation
Eq.

6
using an Ordinary Differential Equation (ODE) solver. In the following, we will denote by
\mathcal{F}_{\delta t}
one step of the considered scheme. Let us give an example with the first order explicit Euler scheme, which reads as follows

\displaystyle e^{\delta t\mathcal{L}_{N}}(\boldsymbol{\rho})\approx\mathcal{F}_{\delta t}(\boldsymbol{\rho})=\boldsymbol{\rho}+\delta t\mathcal{L}_{N}(\boldsymbol{\rho}).

(7)

Taking
N_{step}
and
\delta t
such that
T=N_{step}\delta t
, we compute iteratively
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\rho}}_{n+1,\delta t}=\mathcal{F}_{\delta t}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\rho}}_{n,\delta t})
with
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\rho}}_{0,\delta t}=\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0}
, that is
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\rho}}_{N_{step},\delta t}=\mathcal{F}_{\delta t}^{N_{step}}(\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0})
. At this point, we expect
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\rho}}_{N_{step},\delta t}
to be close to
\boldsymbol{\rho}(T)
.

These two steps are completely standard and can be easily performed using a library for quantum simulation like
Qutip

[
16
]
,
Qiskit dynamics

[

]
,
Dynamiqs

[

]
,
QuantumOptics.jl

[

]
,

The time solver scheme is usually more complex and powerful than a first order explicit Euler scheme. For instance, it can be a Runge-Kutta scheme or a structure preserving one, like
[
5
,
1
,
26

. The space discretization method we employed in step 1 is commonly known as a Galerkin approximation. For a detailed study of the convergence and limitation of this method to the Schrödinger equation, we refer to
[
10
]
. In this article, we do not consider non-linear space discretization methods, such as reduced rank or tensor network methods, nor do we address stochastic unraveling schemes and cumulant expansions.

This paper focuses on providing computable bounds on the distance between the approximations
{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}(T)
or
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\rho}}_{N_{step},\delta t}
and the true solution
\boldsymbol{\rho}(T)
. The error
\|{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}(T)-\boldsymbol{\rho}(T)\|_{1}
is referred to as the (space-)truncation error, while
\|{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}(T)-\tilde{\boldsymbol{\rho}}_{N_{step},\delta t}\|_{1}
is the time discretization error. Previous works, such as
[
30
]
, have addressed (while not being the main contribution of the paper) the space truncation error for finite-dimensional Hamiltonian systems coupled to chains of bosonic modes, using time-adaptive density matrix renormalization group (t-DMRG) methods. Our approach differs in several important ways. First, our proposed method applies to systems that are themselves infinite-dimensional, such as bosonic modes. Second, it is suitable for Lindblad dynamics, not just closed systems. See
Appendix

B
for details.

Other works focus on
a priori
error analysis, typically for specific Hamiltonian systems (e.g.
[
28
,
22
]
); an exception is the recent study of Lindblad equations in
[
24
]
. However, these studies do not provide explicit usable computable estimates, but rather asymptotic convergence rates. Even when constants can be found, they are typically not explicit and the resulting bounds are not sharp. Conceptually,
a priori
estimates use bounds depending on the exact solution
\boldsymbol{\rho}(t)
or its regularity (for example the norm of
\boldsymbol{\rho}(t)
in a well-chosen Sobolev space), whereas
a posteriori
estimates only depend on the computed solution
{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}(t)
or
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\rho}}_{N_{step},\delta t}
. While the first allows proving convergence and rates of convergence, the second is much sharper in practice to control the error of a given simulation.

Many examples considered in this work are motivated by the bosonic code community, a subfield of quantum error correction devoted to the robust encoding of quantum information in bosonic modes. In this setting, one encounters a broad class of Hamiltonians and dissipative generators, frequently expressed as polynomials in the creation and annihilation operators. In the experimental platform of circuit quantum electrodynamics, sum of unitary operators of the form
e^{i\eta{\bf q}}+e^{-i\eta{\bf q}}
naturally arise, where
{\bf q}
denotes the position operator. Such terms originate from the Josephson junction nonlinearity, whose interaction energy is proportional to the cosine of the flux. The simulation of dissipative cat-qubits
[
21
]
, as well as several proposals for implementations of Gottesman–Kitaev–Preskill (GKP) qubits
[
13
]
, is often subtle. Indeed, these qubits deliberately exploit the large dimensionality of the underlying infinite-dimensional Hilbert space in order to encode quantum information with enhanced protection.

The techniques and estimates developed here are, however, not restricted to bosonic systems. They extend directly to other quantum many-body settings—for instance, to spin lattices—as briefly discussed at the end of
Section

2
.

2
A posteriori
truncation error estimates for Lindblad equation

In this section, we assume that we have access to the continuous-in-time solution
({\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}(t))_{0\leq t\leq T}
of
Eq.

6
. Note that for all
0\leq t\leq T
,
{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}(t)\in\mathcal{K}^{1}_{+}(\mathcal{H}_{N})
.

The key to obtain our estimates resides in the fact that the flow of the Lindblad equation contracts the trace norm. Let us first recall a classical result.

Proposition 1
.

[
18
, Lemma 1]

Let
\mathcal{M}
be a Completely Positive Trace Preserving (CPTP) map, and
\boldsymbol{\sigma}\in\mathcal{K}_{s}^{1}
. Then,

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{M}(\boldsymbol{\sigma})\|_{1}\leq\|\boldsymbol{\sigma}\|_{1}.

(12)

Proof.

\boldsymbol{\sigma}
is self-adjoint, thus we can decompose it into a positive part and negative part, so that
\boldsymbol{\sigma}=\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{+}-\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{-}
, with
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{+}\geq 0
,
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{-}\geq 0
and
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{-}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{+}=0
. Thus,

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{\sigma}\|_{1}

\displaystyle=\|\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{+}-\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{-}\|_{1}=\operatorname{Tr}\left(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{+}\right)+\operatorname{Tr}\left(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{-}\right).

(13)

Then, we compute

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{M}(\boldsymbol{\sigma})\|_{1}

\displaystyle=\|\mathcal{M}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{+})-\mathcal{M}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{-})\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\|\mathcal{M}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{+})\|_{1}+\|\mathcal{M}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{-})\|_{1}\quad\text{(triangle inequality)}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Tr}(\mathcal{M}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{+}))+\operatorname{Tr}(\mathcal{M}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{-}))\quad\text{($\mathcal{M}$ is completely positive)}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Tr}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{+})+\operatorname{Tr}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{-})\quad\text{($\mathcal{M}$ is trace preserving)}

\displaystyle=\|\boldsymbol{\sigma}\|_{1},

(14)

which concludes the proof.
∎

The flow of the Lindbladian, that is
\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0}\mapsto e^{t\mathcal{L}}\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0}
for any
t\geq 0
, is CPTP. Hence, it contracts the trace norm.

Lemma 1
.

We have the following estimate

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{\rho}(t)-{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}(t)\|\leq\|\boldsymbol{\rho}(0)-{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}(0)\|_{1}+\int_{0}^{t}\left\|\left(\mathcal{L}-\mathcal{L}_{N}\right){\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}(s)\right\|_{1}ds.

(15)

Proof.

The evolution of
\mathbf{r}(t)=\boldsymbol{\rho}(t)-{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}(t)
can be written as

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\mathbf{r}(t)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\rho}(t))-\mathcal{L}({\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}(t))+\mathcal{L}({\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}(t))-\mathcal{L}_{N}({\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}(t))

\displaystyle=\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{r}(t))+(\mathcal{L}-\mathcal{L}_{N})({\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}(t)).

(16)

Then, using Duhamel’s principle, we get

\displaystyle\mathbf{r}(t)=e^{t\mathcal{L}}\mathbf{r}(0)+\int_{0}^{t}e^{(t-s)\mathcal{L}}\left(\left(\mathcal{L}-\mathcal{L}_{N}\right){\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}(s)\right)ds.

(17)

Taking the trace-norm and applying the triangle inequality gives

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{r}(t)\|_{1}\leq\|\mathbf{r}(0)\|_{1}+\int_{0}^{t}\left\|\left(\mathcal{L}-\mathcal{L}_{N}\right){\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}(s)\right\|_{1}ds.

(18)

∎

As a consequence, we can define the estimator
\xi
as

\displaystyle\dot{\xi}(t)=\|\left(\mathcal{L}-\mathcal{L}_{N}\right){\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}(t)\|_{1},\quad\xi(0)=\|\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0}-{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}(0)\|_{1},

(19)

and we get
\xi(t)\geq\|\boldsymbol{\rho}(t)-{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}(t)\|_{1}
for all
t\geq 0
.

.

\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0}

provides an

Application to spin lattices

One family of examples satisfying the first requirement is spin lattices in the thermodynamic limit, where the truncation retains only a finite number of spins. Consider a
d
-dimensional lattice indexed by
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
(or a subset thereof), with each site
j
having a finite-dimensional local Hilbert space
\mathbb{C}^{d_{j}}
(e.g.,
d_{j}=2
for spin-1/2 systems). Given a finite cubic domain
\Lambda_{N}\subset\mathbb{Z}^{d}
of linear size
N
, the truncated Hilbert space is the finite tensor product
\mathcal{H}_{N}=\bigotimes_{j\in\Lambda_{N}}\mathbb{C}^{d_{j}}
. The infinite system is understood in the thermodynamic limit as
N\to\infty
, or more rigorously in the operator-algebraic framework via the inductive limit of local algebras.

For physically relevant spin lattices, the Lindbladian
\mathcal{L}
is typically a sum of local terms:
\mathcal{L}=\sum_{j}\mathcal{L}_{j}
, where each
\mathcal{L}_{j}
acts non-trivially only on a finite number of neighboring spins (e.g., nearest-neighbor or finite-range interactions). The truncated Lindbladian
\mathcal{L}_{N}
retains only those terms
\mathcal{L}_{j}
that are supported in
\Lambda_{N}
. Crucially, the difference
(\mathcal{L}-\mathcal{L}_{N})({\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}(t))
involves only boundary terms—interactions crossing the boundary
\partial\Lambda_{N}
of the truncation region. More precisely, if the interactions have range
r
(i.e.,
\mathcal{L}_{j}
couples at most
r
-th nearest neighbors), then
\mathcal{L}({\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}(t))
can be represented as an operator on a slightly larger finite region
\Lambda_{N+r}
. The error
\|(\mathcal{L}-\mathcal{L}_{N})({\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}(t))\|_{1}
can thus be numerically computed, as it only involves operators on the finite-dimensional space
\mathcal{H}_{N+r}
.

3
Application 1: estimates for polynomial operators on bosonic modes

3.1
General case

In this section, we consider the case where
\mathcal{H}=l^{2}{(\mathbb{N})}^{\otimes m}
, and the Hamiltonian and the dissipators are polynomials in creation and annihilation operators. We show that for these systems, the computation of
\mathcal{L}({\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}(t))
is simple. First, we treat the case of a single mode before discussing the generalization to several modes.

Definition 1
.

An (unbounded) operator
{\mathbf{\Gamma}}
on
l^{2}(\mathbb{N})
is a polynomial in the creation and annihilation operators of degree
d
if there exists a (non-commutative) polynomial of degree
d\in\mathbb{N}
of the form
{Q}[X,Y]=\sum_{i+j\leq d}\nu_{i,j}X^{i}Y^{j}
such that
{\mathbf{\Gamma}}={Q}[{\bf a},{\bf a^{\dagger}}]=\sum_{i+j\leq d}\nu_{i,j}{\bf a}^{i}{\bf a^{\dagger}}^{j}
.

If the Hamiltonian and the dissipators are polynomials in
{\bf a}
and
{\bf a^{\dagger}}
, the corollaries of the next proposition show that it is always possible to compute explicitly
\mathcal{L}({\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}})
.

Proposition 2
.

Let
{Q}[X,Y]
be a polynomial of degree
d\geq 1
, then

\displaystyle{Q}[{\bf a},{\bf a^{\dagger}}]\mathcal{H}_{N}\subset\mathcal{H}_{N+d}.

(20)

Proof.

Using that
[{\bf a},{\bf a^{\dagger}}]=\operatorname{\mathbf{Id}}
, we can reduce
{Q}[{\bf a},{\bf a^{\dagger}}]
to the following form

\displaystyle{Q}[{\bf a},{\bf a^{\dagger}}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i+2j\leq d}\lambda_{i,j}{\bf a}^{i}\mathbf{N}^{j}+\mu_{i,j}{\bf a^{\dagger}}^{i}\mathbf{N}^{j},

(21)

where
\mathbf{N}={\bf a^{\dagger}}{\bf a}
is the photon number operator. If we apply the operator
{Q}[{\bf a},{\bf a^{\dagger}}]
on an element of
\mathcal{H}_{N}
, the first part of the sum remains in
\mathcal{H}_{N}
. As
\mathcal{H}_{N}
is invariant under the action of
\mathbf{N}
,
{\bf a^{\dagger}}^{i}\mathbf{N}^{j}\mathcal{H}_{N}\subset\mathcal{H}_{N+i}
. Hence,
{Q}[{\bf a},{\bf a^{\dagger}}]\mathcal{H}_{N}\subset\mathcal{H}_{N+d}
.
∎

Example 1
.

For any
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{N}\in\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{H}_{N})
, we can decompose
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{N}=\sum_{0\leq i,j\leq N}\sigma_{i,j}\ket{i}\bra{j}
, and we get indeed that
{\bf a^{\dagger}}\boldsymbol{\sigma}
belongs to
\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{H}_{N+1})
.

Corollary 1
.

Let
{\bf H}
be a polynomial operator of degree
d
in
{\bf a}
and
{\bf a^{\dagger}}
. Then, for every operator
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{N}\in\mathcal{K}_{s}^{1}(\mathcal{H}_{N})
:

\displaystyle-i[{\bf H},\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{N}]=-i[{\bf H}_{N+d},\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{N}].

(22)

Proof.

Using
Proposition

2
,
{\bf H}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{N}=\mathbf{P}_{N+d}{\bf H}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{N}
. Besides,
\mathbf{P}_{N+d}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{N}=\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{N}
, so that
{\bf H}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{N}={\bf H}_{N+d}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{N}
.
∎

Remark 3
.

We recall that as
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{N}\in\mathcal{K}_{s}^{1}(\mathcal{H}_{N})
and
\mathcal{H}_{N}\subset\mathcal{H}
, one has
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{N}\in\mathcal{K}_{s}^{1}(\mathcal{H})
.

Corollary 2
.

Let
{\mathbf{\Gamma}}
be a polynomial operator of degree
d
in
{\bf a}
and
{\bf a^{\dagger}}
. Then, for every density operator
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{N}\in\mathcal{K}_{s}^{1}(\mathcal{H}_{N})
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{{\mathbf{\Gamma}}}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{N})=\mathcal{D}_{{\mathbf{\Gamma}}_{N+2d}}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{N}).

(23)

Proof.

Using
Proposition

2
, one has
{\mathbf{\Gamma}}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{N}{\mathbf{\Gamma}}^{\dagger}={\mathbf{\Gamma}}_{N+d}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{N}{\mathbf{\Gamma}}_{N+d}^{\dagger}
. In general
{\mathbf{\Gamma}}^{\dagger}{\mathbf{\Gamma}}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{N}
is not equal to
{\mathbf{\Gamma}}_{N+d}^{\dagger}{\mathbf{\Gamma}}_{N+d}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{N}
(a counter-example is given in
Section

3.2.4
). Nevertheless, we have
{\mathbf{\Gamma}}^{\dagger}{\mathbf{\Gamma}}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{N}={\mathbf{\Gamma}}_{N+2d}^{\dagger}{\mathbf{\Gamma}}_{N+2d}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{N}
, which ensures that
\mathcal{D}_{{\mathbf{\Gamma}}}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{N})=\mathcal{D}_{{\mathbf{\Gamma}}_{N+2d}}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{N})
.
∎

Extension to several bosonic modes is rather straightforward. Let
{Q}[\mathbf{X_{1}},\mathbf{Y_{1}},\ldots\mathbf{X_{m}},\mathbf{Y_{m}}]
be a polynomial of degree
d
. We easily generalize
Proposition

2
to
m
modes and obtain

\displaystyle{Q}[{\bf a}_{1},{\bf a}^{\dagger}_{1},\ldots,{\bf a}_{m},{\bf a}^{\dagger}_{m}]\mathcal{H}_{N_{1},\ldots N_{m}}\subset\mathcal{H}_{N_{1}+d,\ldots,N_{m}+d}.

(24)

As a consequence,
\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{N})
coincides with
\mathcal{L}_{N+d}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{N})
, where
d=\max(d_{{\bf H}},2\max_{i}(d_{i}))
with
d_{{\bf H}}
the degree of a polynomial generating the Hamiltonian,
d_{i}
the degree of
{\mathbf{\Gamma}}_{i}
, and
N+d
denotes the m-uplet
(N_{1}+d,\ldots N_{m}+d)
.

3.2
Some examples

We showed that it is always possible to compute the space estimate of
Section

2
expressing
\mathcal{L}_{N+d}
,
\mathcal{L}_{N}
and
\boldsymbol{\rho}_{N}
on
\mathcal{K}_{s}^{1}(\mathcal{H}_{N+d})
, and neglecting the time-discretization errors.
In this part, we investigate some explicit examples to provide an intuition on the estimates. In
Sections

3.2.3
and
3.2.5
, we also compare our estimate against the truncation error to assess the degree of overestimation in the upper bound. We also assume that
\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0}\in\mathcal{K}^{1}_{+}(\mathcal{H}_{N})
in all these examples.

3.2.1
Frequency driven damped oscillator -
{\bf H}=u(t){\bf a^{\dagger}}{\bf a}
and
\mathcal{D}_{{\bf a}}

The Hamiltonian
u(t){\bf a^{\dagger}}{\bf a}
for a scalar function
u(t)
is not responsible for any truncation error as
\mathbf{P}_{N}
commutes with
\mathbf{N}={\bf a^{\dagger}}{\bf a}
(
\mathbf{N}
being diagonal in the Fock basis). As a consequence,
i[{\bf H}-{\bf H}_{N},{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}]=0
.

Concerning the dissipator, it can be noticed that
{\bf a}{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}=\mathbf{P}_{N}{\bf a}\mathbf{P}_{N}{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}
and
{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}{\bf a^{\dagger}}={\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}\mathbf{P}_{N}{\bf a^{\dagger}}\mathbf{P}_{N}
. Besides,
{\bf a^{\dagger}}_{N}{\bf a}_{N}{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}={\bf a^{\dagger}}{\bf a}{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}
, so that
\mathcal{D}_{{\bf a}}({\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}})=\mathcal{D}_{{\bf a}_{N}}({\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}})
.
As a consequence,
\mathcal{L}_{N}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{N})=\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{N})
for every
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{N}\in\mathcal{K}^{1}_{s}(\mathcal{H}_{N})
and the truncation is not responsible for any error. Both the truncation error and our estimate are null.

3.2.2
Driven oscillator -
{\bf H}=u(t)({\bf a}+{\bf a^{\dagger}})

We have

\displaystyle i[{\bf H}-{\bf H}_{N},\boldsymbol{\rho}_{N}]

\displaystyle=i\mathbf{P}_{N}^{\perp}{\bf H}{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}+h.c.

\displaystyle=i\left(\sum_{n=N}^{\infty}\sqrt{n+1}\ket{n+1}\bra{n}+\sum_{n=N+1}^{\infty}\sqrt{n+1}\ket{n}\bra{n+1}\right)\boldsymbol{\rho}_{N}+h.c.

\displaystyle=i\sqrt{N+1}\ket{N+1}\bra{N}\boldsymbol{\rho}_{N}+h.c.\ .

(25)

Hence, we have to compute

\|[{\bf H}-{\bf H}_{N},{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}(t)]\|_{1}=|u(t)|\\
\times\operatorname{Tr}\left(\sqrt{\left(i\sqrt{N\!+\!1}\ket{N\!+\!1}\bra{N}{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}(t)+h.c.\right)\left(i\sqrt{N\!+\!1}\ket{N\!+\!1}\bra{N}{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}(t)+h.c.\right)^{{\dagger}}}\right).

(26)

Using
{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}\ket{N+1}=0
, we have

\displaystyle\left(i\sqrt{N+1}\ket{N+1}\bra{N}{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}(t)+h.c.\right)\left(i\sqrt{N+1}\ket{N+1}\bra{N}{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}(t)+h.c.\right)^{{\dagger}}

\displaystyle={\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}\ket{N}\bra{N}{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}+\ket{N+1}\bra{N}{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}^{2}\ket{N}\bra{N+1}.

(27)

As
{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}\ket{N}
and
\ket{N+1}
are orthogonal vectors, we get

\sqrt{{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}\ket{N}\bra{N}{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}+\ket{N\!+\!1}\bra{N}{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}^{2}\ket{N}\bra{N\!+\!1}}\\
=\sqrt{{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}\ket{N}\bra{N}{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}}+\sqrt{\ket{N\!+\!1}\bra{N}{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}^{2}\ket{N}\bra{N\!+\!1}}.

(28)

Then, as for a rank one symmetric matrix
S
,
\operatorname{Tr}\left(\sqrt{S}\right)=\sqrt{\operatorname{Tr}\left(S\right)}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\|[{\bf H}-{\bf H}_{N},{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}(t)]\|_{1}=

\displaystyle=|u(t)|\sqrt{N+1}\Bigl(\sqrt{\operatorname{Tr}\left({\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}\ket{N}\bra{N}{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}\right)}+\sqrt{\bra{N}{{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}(t)}^{2}\ket{N}}\Bigr)

\displaystyle=2|u(t)|\sqrt{N+1}\sqrt{\bra{N}{{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}(t)}^{2}\ket{N}}.

(29)

From a numerical point of view, the estimate is computationally cheap to compute, as one only needs to compute the Hilbert norm of the N
th
row of
\boldsymbol{\rho}_{N}
. Hence, the space estimate obtained in
Lemma

1
gives

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{\rho}(t)-{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}(t)\|_{1}\leq\int_{0}^{t}2|u(s)|\sqrt{N+1}\sqrt{\bra{N}{{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}(s)}^{2}\ket{N}}ds.

(30)

3.2.3
Dissipative cat-qubit -
{\mathbf{\Gamma}}={\bf a}^{2}-\alpha^{2}\operatorname{\mathbf{Id}}
, with
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}

The dissipator
{\bf a}^{2}-\alpha^{2}\operatorname{\mathbf{Id}}
is used for the stabilization of dissipative cat-qubits, see
[
21
]
. Well-posedness and convergence toward the codespace
\operatorname{Span}\{\ket{\pm\alpha}\bra{\pm\alpha},\ket{\pm\alpha}\bra{\mp\alpha}\}
is proved in
[
3
]
.

Expression of the estimate

One starts considering

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{{\mathbf{\Gamma}}}({\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}(t))-\mathcal{D}_{{\mathbf{\Gamma}}_{N}}({\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}(t))

\displaystyle=-\frac{\alpha^{2}}{2}\big(\sqrt{(N\!+\!1)(N\!+\!2)}

\displaystyle\quad\times(\ket{N\!+\!2}\bra{N}{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}(t)+{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}(t)\ket{N}\bra{N\!+\!2})

(31)

\displaystyle+\sqrt{N(N\!+\!1)}(\ket{N\!+\!1}\bra{N\!-\!1}{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}(t)

\displaystyle\quad+{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}(t)\ket{N\!-\!1}\bra{N\!+\!1})\big),

(32)

which is an operator supported on
\operatorname*{Span}\{{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}\ket{N},{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}\ket{N-1},\ket{N+1},\ket{N+2}\}
. After some computations, postponed to
Appendix

C
, we get

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{D}_{{\mathbf{\Gamma}}}({\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}})-\mathcal{D}_{{\mathbf{\Gamma}}_{N}}({\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}})\|_{1}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{N\!+\!1}\frac{\alpha^{2}}{2}

\displaystyle\quad\times\Biggl(\operatorname{Tr}\left(\sqrt{{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}\Big(\sqrt{N}\ket{N\!-\!1}\bra{N\!-\!1}+\sqrt{N\!+\!2}\ket{N}\bra{N}\Big){\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}}\right)

(33)

\displaystyle+\sqrt{N}\bra{N\!-\!1}{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}^{2}\ket{N\!-\!1}+\sqrt{N\!+\!2}\bra{N}{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}^{2}\ket{N}\Biggr).

(34)

Note that this expression is not expensive to numerically compute, as it only requires performing elementary operations on the last two rows of
{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}
and computing the eigenvalues of a rank 2 matrix.

4
Application 2: estimates for Lindbladian with unitary operators involved

4.1
Unitary operators

In this section, we compute the
a posteriori
truncation estimate given in
Lemma

1
for Lindbladians that involve either a unitary operator as Hamiltonian or more interestingly unitary operators as jump operators.

We are interested in operators for which we know the exact finite truncation. More precisely, we assume that analytic formulas for the coefficients of
\mathbf{U}_{N}=\mathbf{P}_{N}\mathbf{U}\mathbf{P}_{N}
are available. For example, the matrix elements
\bra{i}e^{i\eta X}\ket{j}
, for any
\eta\in\mathbb{R}
, and
(i,j)\in\mathbb{N}^{2}
, admit an explicit (and numerically stable) formula, see e.g.
[
27
, Appendix E.3]
. Hence, we have access to the truncated operator
\mathbf{U}_{N}
exactly. The key estimate for this section is the following Lemma.

Lemma 2
.

Let
\mathbf{U}
be a unitary operator on
\mathcal{H}
, and
\mathbf{M}_{N}
be an operator on
\mathcal{H}_{N}
. We have the following norm equality:

\|\mathbf{P}_{N}^{\perp}\mathbf{U}\mathbf{M}_{N}\|_{1}=\operatorname{Tr}\left(\sqrt{\mathbf{M}_{N}^{\dagger}(\operatorname{\mathbf{Id}}_{N}-\mathbf{U}_{N}^{\dagger}\mathbf{U}_{N})\mathbf{M}_{N}}\right).

(37)

Proof.

We write
\mathbf{U}
using the space decomposition
\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}_{N}\oplus\mathcal{H}_{N}^{\perp}
:

\displaystyle\mathbf{U}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{U}_{N}&\mathbf{P}_{N}\mathbf{U}\mathbf{P}_{N}^{\perp}\\
\mathbf{P}_{N}^{\perp}\mathbf{U}\mathbf{P}_{N}&\mathbf{P}_{N}^{\perp}\mathbf{U}\mathbf{P}_{N}^{\perp}\end{pmatrix}.

(38)

Since
\mathbf{U}
is unitary,
\mathbf{P}_{N}(\mathbf{U}^{\dagger}\mathbf{U})\mathbf{P}_{N}=\operatorname{\mathbf{Id}}_{N}=\mathbf{U}_{N}^{\dagger}\mathbf{U}_{N}+(\mathbf{P}_{N}^{\perp}\mathbf{U}\mathbf{P}_{N})^{\dagger}(\mathbf{P}_{N}^{\perp}\mathbf{U}\mathbf{P}_{N})
. Hence,
(\mathbf{P}_{N}^{\perp}\mathbf{U}\mathbf{P}_{N})^{\dagger}(\mathbf{P}_{N}^{\perp}U\mathbf{P}_{N})=\operatorname{\mathbf{Id}}_{N}-\mathbf{U}_{N}^{\dagger}\mathbf{U}_{N}
.
As a consequence,

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{P}_{N}^{\perp}\mathbf{U}\mathbf{M}_{N}\|_{1}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Tr}\left(\sqrt{\mathbf{M}_{N}^{\dagger}(\mathbf{P}_{N}^{\perp}\mathbf{U}\mathbf{P}_{N})^{\dagger}(\mathbf{P}_{N}^{\perp}\mathbf{U}\mathbf{P}_{N})\mathbf{M}_{N}}\right)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Tr}\left(\sqrt{\mathbf{M}_{N}^{\dagger}(\operatorname{\mathbf{Id}}_{N}-\mathbf{U}_{N}^{\dagger}\mathbf{U}_{N})\mathbf{M}_{N}}\right).

(39)

∎

Note that the symmetric operator inside the square root of
Eq.

39
can be computed and has support on
\mathcal{H}_{N}
.

Hamiltonian error involving a unitary operator

While being quite specific, let us assume that
{\bf H}
is also unitary. In this case

\displaystyle\|[{\bf H}-{\bf H}_{N},{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}]\|_{1}\leq 2\|({\bf H}-{\bf H}_{N}){\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}\|_{1}=2\|\mathbf{P}_{N}^{\perp}{\bf H}{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}\|_{1}.

(40)

As a consequence, we can use
Lemma

2
to compute the error.

Dissipator error involving a unitary operator

We split into three parts the dissipator’s error
(\mathcal{D}_{\mathbf{U}}-\mathcal{D}_{\mathbf{U}_{N}})({\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}})
, with
\mathbf{U}
a unitary operator on
\mathcal{H}
.

\displaystyle\|(\mathcal{D}_{\mathbf{U}}-\mathcal{D}_{\mathbf{U}_{N}})({\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}})\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\|\mathbf{U}{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}\mathbf{U}^{\dagger}-\mathbf{U}_{N}{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}\mathbf{U}_{N}^{\dagger}\|_{1}

\displaystyle+\|\mathbf{U}^{\dagger}\mathbf{U}{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}-\mathbf{U}_{N}^{\dagger}\mathbf{U}_{N}{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}\|_{1}

(41)

\displaystyle+\|{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}\mathbf{U}^{\dagger}\mathbf{U}-{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}\mathbf{U}_{N}^{\dagger}\mathbf{U}_{N}\|_{1}.

(42)

We start by the second and third term of the right-hand side of
41
.

\displaystyle\|{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}\mathbf{U}^{\dagger}\mathbf{U}-{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}\mathbf{U}_{N}^{\dagger}\mathbf{U}_{N}\|_{1}

\displaystyle=\|\mathbf{U}^{\dagger}\mathbf{U}{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}-\mathbf{U}_{N}^{\dagger}\mathbf{U}_{N}{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}\|_{1}

\displaystyle=\|{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}-\mathbf{U}_{N}^{\dagger}\mathbf{U}_{N}{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}\|_{1}

(43)

\displaystyle=\|(\operatorname{\mathbf{Id}}_{N}-\mathbf{U}_{N}^{\dagger}\mathbf{U}_{N}){\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}\|_{1}.

(44)

This expression can be numerically computed. Focusing on the remaining term, we get

\|\mathbf{U}{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}\mathbf{U}^{\dagger}-\mathbf{U}_{N}{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}\mathbf{U}_{N}^{\dagger}\|_{1}\leq\|\mathbf{P}_{N}^{\perp}\mathbf{U}{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}\mathbf{U}^{\dagger}\mathbf{P}_{N}^{\perp}\|_{1}+\|\mathbf{P}_{N}\mathbf{U}{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}\mathbf{U}^{\dagger}\mathbf{P}_{N}^{\perp}\|_{1}+\|\mathbf{P}_{N}^{\perp}\mathbf{U}{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}\mathbf{U}^{\dagger}\mathbf{P}_{N}\|_{1}.

(45)

The last two terms are equal and can be handled using
Eq.

37
with
\mathbf{M}_{N}={\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}\mathbf{U}_{N}^{\dagger}
:

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{P}_{N}^{\perp}\mathbf{U}{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}\mathbf{U}^{\dagger}\mathbf{P}_{N}\|_{1}

\displaystyle=\|\mathbf{P}_{N}^{\perp}\mathbf{U}{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}\mathbf{U}^{\dagger}_{N}\|_{1}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Tr}\left(\sqrt{\mathbf{U}_{N}{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}\mathbf{U}_{N}^{\dagger}(\operatorname{\mathbf{Id}}_{N}-\mathbf{U}_{N}^{\dagger}\mathbf{U}_{N})\mathbf{U}_{N}{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}\mathbf{U}_{N}^{\dagger}}\right).

The remaining term is
\|\mathbf{P}_{N}^{\perp}\mathbf{U}{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}\mathbf{U}^{\dagger}\mathbf{P}_{N}^{\perp}\|_{1}
. As
{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}\geq 0
, we also have
\mathbf{P}_{N}^{\perp}\mathbf{U}{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}\mathbf{U}^{\dagger}\mathbf{P}_{N}^{\perp}\geq 0
. Hence, the trace norm and the trace of these operators coincide. Then,

\displaystyle\operatorname{Tr}\left(\mathbf{P}_{N}^{\perp}\mathbf{U}{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}\mathbf{U}^{\dagger}\mathbf{P}_{N}^{\perp}\right)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Tr}\left(\mathbf{U}{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}\mathbf{U}^{\dagger}\mathbf{P}_{N}^{\perp}\right)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Tr}\left(\mathbf{U}{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}\mathbf{U}^{\dagger}(\operatorname{\mathbf{Id}}-\mathbf{P}_{N})\right)

\displaystyle=1-\operatorname{Tr}\left(\mathbf{P}_{N}\mathbf{U}{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}\mathbf{U}^{\dagger}\mathbf{P}_{N}\right).

(46)

Eventually, we get the following estimate, that can be numerically computed:

\displaystyle\|(\mathcal{D}_{\mathbf{U}}-\mathcal{D}_{\mathbf{U}_{N}})({\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}})\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq 2\|(\operatorname{\mathbf{Id}}_{N}-\mathbf{U}_{N}^{\dagger}\mathbf{U}_{N}){\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}\|_{1}+\|\mathbf{P}_{N}^{\perp}\mathbf{U}{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}\mathbf{U}^{\dagger}_{N}\|_{1}+1-\operatorname{Tr}\left(\mathbf{U}_{N}{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}\mathbf{U}_{N}^{\dagger}\right).

(47)

4.2
Application to a more complex example

In this section, we study a more complex example involving both unitary operators and polynomials in creation and annihilation operators. More precisely, we study the following dynamics introduced in
[
27
]
for dissipative GKP stabilization:

\frac{d}{dt}\boldsymbol{\rho}=\sum_{k=0}^{3}\mathcal{D}_{{\mathbf{\Gamma}}_{k}}(\boldsymbol{\rho}),

(48)

with

\displaystyle{\mathbf{\Gamma}}_{0}=\mathcal{A}\,e^{i\eta{\bf q}}\left(\operatorname{\mathbf{Id}}-\epsilon{\bf p}\right)-\operatorname{\mathbf{Id}},\quad\mathbf{R}=e^{i\pi{{\bf a^{\dagger}}{\bf a}}/2},\quad{\mathbf{\Gamma}}_{k}=\mathbf{R}^{k}\,{\mathbf{\Gamma}}_{0}\,\mathbf{R}^{-k},

(49)

where
\mathcal{A},\eta
and
\epsilon
are given constant real numbers. We recall that
{\bf q}
and
{\bf p}
are the position and momentum operators whose definition is recalled in
Section

1.2
. Note also that
\mathbf{R}
is unitary and
\mathbf{R}^{4}=\operatorname{\mathbf{Id}}
. To lighten a little the notations, we introduce
\mathbf{U}=e^{i\eta{\bf q}}
, and
\mathbf{Q}=\mathcal{A}\left(\operatorname{\mathbf{Id}}-\epsilon{\bf p}\right)
.

Our goal is obtaining a good upper bound on
\|\mathcal{L}({\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}})-\mathcal{L}_{N}({\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}})\|_{1}
that can be numerically computed. Because the dissipator
{\mathbf{\Gamma}}_{0}
involves both the polynomial
\mathbf{Q}
and the unitary operator
e^{i\eta{\bf q}}
, we cannot directly apply the results of
Section

3
or
Section

4.1
.

Tools

Let us first state a generalization of
Eq.

37
. Assume
\mathcal{H}_{N_{1}}\subset\mathcal{H}_{N_{2}}
, and let us consider
\mathbf{U}
a unitary operator on
\mathcal{H}
, and
\mathbf{M}_{N_{2}}\in\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{H}_{N_{2}})
. Note that
\mathbf{P}_{N_{1}}^{\perp}=\operatorname{\mathbf{Id}}-\mathbf{P}_{N_{1}}=\operatorname{\mathbf{Id}}-\mathbf{P}_{N_{2}}+\mathbf{P}_{N_{2}}-\mathbf{P}_{N_{1}}
. Besides as
\mathbf{P}_{N_{2}}^{\perp}
and
\mathbf{P}_{N_{2}}-\mathbf{P}_{N_{1}}
are orthogonal projectors with orthogonal ranges, we have

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{P}_{N_{1}}^{\perp}\mathbf{U}\mathbf{M}_{N_{2}}\|_{1}

\displaystyle=\|\mathbf{P}_{N_{2}}^{\perp}\mathbf{U}\mathbf{M}_{N_{2}}\|_{1}+\|(\mathbf{P}_{N_{2}}-\mathbf{P}_{N_{1}})\mathbf{U}\mathbf{M}_{N_{2}}\|_{1}.

Besides, using
Eq.

37
, we get

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{P}_{N_{1}}^{\perp}\mathbf{U}\mathbf{M}_{N_{2}}\|_{1}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Tr}\left(\sqrt{\mathbf{M}_{N_{2}}^{\dagger}(\ \operatorname{\mathbf{Id}}_{N_{2}}-\mathbf{U}_{N_{2}}^{\dagger}\mathbf{U}_{N_{2}})\mathbf{M}_{N_{2}}}\right)+\|(\mathbf{P}_{N_{2}}-\mathbf{P}_{N_{1}})\mathbf{U}\mathbf{M}_{N_{2}}\|_{1}.

(50)

Dissipator error

Let us start the analysis with the error term coming from
{\mathbf{\Gamma}}_{0}
.

\displaystyle(\mathcal{D}_{\mathbf{U}\mathbf{Q}-\operatorname{\mathbf{Id}}}-\mathcal{D}_{\mathbf{P}_{N}\mathbf{U}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{P}_{N}-\operatorname{\mathbf{Id}}_{N}})({\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}})

\displaystyle=\operatorname{\mathbf{I}}_{1}-\frac{1}{2}(\operatorname{\mathbf{I}}_{2}+\operatorname{\mathbf{I}}_{2}^{\dagger}),

(51)

with

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbf{I}}_{1}

\displaystyle=(\mathbf{U}\mathbf{Q}-\operatorname{\mathbf{Id}}){\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}(\mathbf{Q}^{\dagger}\mathbf{U}^{\dagger}-\operatorname{\mathbf{Id}})-\mathbf{P}_{N}(\mathbf{U}\mathbf{Q}-\operatorname{\mathbf{Id}}){\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}(\mathbf{Q}^{\dagger}\mathbf{U}^{\dagger}-\operatorname{\mathbf{Id}})\mathbf{P}_{N},

(52)

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbf{I}}_{2}

\displaystyle=(\mathbf{Q}^{\dagger}\mathbf{U}^{\dagger}-\operatorname{\mathbf{Id}})(\mathbf{U}\mathbf{Q}-\operatorname{\mathbf{Id}}){\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}-\mathbf{P}_{N}(\mathbf{Q}^{\dagger}\mathbf{U}^{\dagger}-\operatorname{\mathbf{Id}})\mathbf{P}_{N}(\mathbf{U}\mathbf{Q}-\operatorname{\mathbf{Id}}){\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}.

(53)

We start with
\operatorname{\mathbf{I}}_{1}
:

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbf{I}}_{1}

\displaystyle=(\mathbf{U}\mathbf{Q}{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}\mathbf{Q}^{\dagger}\mathbf{U}^{\dagger})+{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}-({\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}\mathbf{Q}^{\dagger}\mathbf{U}^{\dagger})-(\mathbf{U}\mathbf{Q}{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}})

\displaystyle\quad-\Bigl[(\mathbf{P}_{N}\mathbf{U}\mathbf{Q}{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}\mathbf{Q}^{\dagger}\mathbf{U}^{\dagger}\mathbf{P}_{N})+{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}-({\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}\mathbf{Q}^{\dagger}\mathbf{U}^{\dagger}\mathbf{P}_{N})-(\mathbf{P}_{N}\mathbf{U}\mathbf{Q}{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}})\Bigr]

\displaystyle=\mathbf{U}\mathbf{Q}{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}\mathbf{Q}^{\dagger}\mathbf{U}^{\dagger}-\mathbf{P}_{N}\mathbf{U}\mathbf{Q}{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}\mathbf{Q}^{\dagger}\mathbf{U}^{\dagger}\mathbf{P}_{N}-(\mathbf{P}_{N}^{\perp}\mathbf{U}\mathbf{Q}{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}+h.c.).

(54)

Similarly,

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbf{I}}_{2}

\displaystyle=(\mathbf{Q}^{\dagger}\mathbf{U}^{\dagger}-\operatorname{\mathbf{Id}})(U\mathbf{Q}-\operatorname{\mathbf{Id}}){\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}-\mathbf{P}_{N}(\mathbf{Q}^{\dagger}\mathbf{U}^{\dagger}-\operatorname{\mathbf{Id}})\mathbf{P}_{N}(\mathbf{U}\mathbf{Q}-\operatorname{\mathbf{Id}}){\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}\

\displaystyle=(\mathbf{Q}^{\dagger}\mathbf{Q}-\mathbf{P}_{N}\mathbf{Q}^{\dagger}\mathbf{U}_{N+1}^{\dagger}\mathbf{P}_{N}\mathbf{U}_{N+1}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{P}_{N}){\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}-\mathbf{P}_{N}^{\perp}\mathbf{U}\mathbf{Q}{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}-\mathbf{P}_{N}^{\perp}\mathbf{Q}^{\dagger}\mathbf{U}^{\dagger}{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}.

(55)

Thus,

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbf{I}}_{1}-\frac{1}{2}(\operatorname{\mathbf{I}}_{2}+\operatorname{\mathbf{I}}_{2}^{\dagger})

\displaystyle=\mathbf{A}_{1}+\mathbf{A}_{2}+\mathbf{A}_{3}+\mathbf{A}_{4},

(56)

with

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}_{1}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{U}\mathbf{Q}{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}\mathbf{Q}^{\dagger}\mathbf{U}^{\dagger}-\mathbf{P}_{N}\mathbf{U}\mathbf{Q}{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}\mathbf{Q}^{\dagger}\mathbf{U}^{\dagger}\mathbf{P}_{N},

(57)

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}_{2}

\displaystyle=-(\mathbf{Q}^{\dagger}\mathbf{Q}-\mathbf{P}_{N}\mathbf{Q}^{\dagger}\mathbf{U}_{N+1}^{\dagger}\mathbf{P}_{N}\mathbf{U}_{N+1}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{P}_{N}){\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}},

(58)

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}_{3}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{P}_{N}^{\perp}\mathbf{U}\mathbf{Q}{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}+h.c.\,,

(59)

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}_{4}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{P}_{N}^{\perp}\mathbf{Q}^{\dagger}\mathbf{U}^{\dagger}{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}+h.c.\,

(60)

We then apply the triangle inequality. It remains to find a way to compute the trace norm of each of the
A_{i}
.
First, we get

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{A}_{1}\|_{1}\leq\|\mathbf{P}_{N}^{\perp}\mathbf{U}\mathbf{Q}{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}\mathbf{Q}^{\dagger}\mathbf{U}^{\dagger}\mathbf{P}_{N}^{\perp}\|_{1}+2\|\mathbf{P}_{N}^{\perp}\mathbf{U}\mathbf{Q}{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}\mathbf{Q}^{\dagger}\mathbf{U}^{\dagger}\mathbf{P}_{N}\|_{1}.

(61)

The second term of the right-hand side of
Eq.

61
can be numerically computed using
Eq.

50
, with
\mathbf{M}_{N_{2}}
being
\mathbf{Q}{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}\mathbf{Q}^{\dagger}\mathbf{U}^{\dagger}\mathbf{P}_{N}\in\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{H}_{N+1})
. The first term is handled as follows:

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{P}_{N}^{\perp}\mathbf{U}\mathbf{Q}{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}\mathbf{Q}^{\dagger}\mathbf{U}^{\dagger}\mathbf{P}_{N}^{\perp}\|_{1}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Tr}\left(\mathbf{P}_{N}^{\perp}\mathbf{U}\mathbf{Q}{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}\mathbf{Q}^{\dagger}\mathbf{U}^{\dagger}\mathbf{P}_{N}^{\perp}\right)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Tr}\left(\mathbf{P}_{N}^{\perp}\mathbf{U}\mathbf{Q}{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}\mathbf{Q}^{\dagger}\mathbf{U}^{\dagger}\right)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Tr}\left(\mathbf{Q}{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}\mathbf{Q}^{\dagger}\right)-\operatorname{Tr}\left(\mathbf{P}_{N}\mathbf{U}\mathbf{Q}{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}\mathbf{Q}^{\dagger}\mathbf{U}^{\dagger}\mathbf{P}_{N}\right).

Then,
\mathbf{A}_{2}
and
\mathbf{A}_{3}
can be numerically computed as an element of resp.
\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{H}_{N+2})
and
\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{H}_{N+1})
. For
\mathbf{A}_{4}
, we use the Heisenberg-picture evolution of
{\bf p}
under the Hamiltonian
{\bf q}
to get

\displaystyle\mathbf{Q}^{\dagger}\mathbf{U}^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{A}(\operatorname{\mathbf{Id}}-\epsilon{\bf p})e^{-i\eta{\bf q}}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{A}e^{-i\eta{\bf q}}e^{i\eta{\bf q}}(\operatorname{\mathbf{Id}}-\epsilon{\bf p})e^{-i\eta{\bf q}}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{A}e^{-i\eta{\bf q}}(\operatorname{\mathbf{Id}}-\epsilon{\bf p}-\epsilon\eta{\bf q}).

(62)

So that we obtain

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{A}_{4}\|_{1}\leq\mathcal{A}\|\mathbf{P}_{N}^{\perp}e^{-i\eta{\bf q}}(\operatorname{\mathbf{Id}}-\epsilon{\bf p}-\epsilon\eta{\bf q}){\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}\|_{1}.

(63)

As a consequence, we get
\|(\mathcal{D}_{{\mathbf{\Gamma}}_{0}}-\mathcal{D}_{\mathbf{P}_{N}{\mathbf{\Gamma}}_{0}\mathbf{P}_{N}})({\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}})\|_{1}\leq f({\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}})
with

\displaystyle f({\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}})

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Tr}\left(\mathbf{Q}{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}\mathbf{Q}^{\dagger}\right)-\operatorname{Tr}\left(\mathbf{P}_{N}\mathbf{U}\mathbf{Q}{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}\mathbf{Q}^{\dagger}\mathbf{U}^{\dagger}\mathbf{P}_{N}\right)+2\|\mathbf{P}_{N}^{\perp}\mathbf{U}\mathbf{Q}{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}\mathbf{Q}^{\dagger}\mathbf{U}^{\dagger}\mathbf{P}_{N}\|_{1}

\displaystyle+\|(\mathbf{Q}^{\dagger}\mathbf{Q}-\mathbf{P}_{N}\mathbf{Q}^{\dagger}\mathbf{U}_{N+1}^{\dagger}\mathbf{P}_{N}\mathbf{U}_{N+1}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{P}_{N}){\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}\|_{1}

\displaystyle+\|\mathbf{P}_{N}^{\perp}\mathbf{U}\mathbf{Q}{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}\|_{1}

\displaystyle+\mathcal{A}\|\mathbf{P}_{N}^{\perp}\mathbf{U}^{\dagger}(\operatorname{\mathbf{Id}}-\epsilon{\bf p}-\epsilon\eta{\bf q}){\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}\|_{1}.

(64)

To compute the errors due to the dissipators
{\mathbf{\Gamma}}_{k}
,
1\leq k\leq 3
, note that as the unitary
\mathbf{R}
commutes with
{\bf a^{\dagger}}{\bf a}
, it commutes with its spectral projectors
\mathbf{P}_{N},\mathbf{P}_{N}^{\perp}
and
\mathbf{P}_{N+1}
. One can then check that

\displaystyle(\mathcal{D}_{{\mathbf{\Gamma}}_{k}}-\mathcal{D}_{\mathbf{P}_{N}{\mathbf{\Gamma}}_{k}\mathbf{P}_{N}})({\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}})=\mathbf{R}^{k}(\mathcal{D}_{{\mathbf{\Gamma}}_{0}}-\mathcal{D}_{\mathbf{P}_{N}{\mathbf{\Gamma}}_{0}\mathbf{P}_{N}})(\mathbf{R}^{-k}{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}\mathbf{R}^{k})\mathbf{R}^{-k}.

(65)

Hence,

\displaystyle\|(\mathcal{D}_{{\mathbf{\Gamma}}_{k}}-\mathcal{D}_{\mathbf{P}_{N}{\mathbf{\Gamma}}_{k}\mathbf{P}_{N}})({\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}})\|\leq f(\mathbf{R}^{-k}{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}\mathbf{R}^{k}).

(66)

Eventually, we deduce that

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{L}({\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}})-\mathcal{L}_{N}({\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}})\|_{1}\leq\sum_{k=0}^{3}f(\mathbf{R}^{-k}{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}\mathbf{R}^{k}).

(67)

4.3
Cosine operator

We provide here an additional estimate related to unitary operators. In superconducting circuits, Josephson Junctions
[
17
]
are commonly used non-linear elements. Their effect on the system Hamiltonian is typically to introduce a term of the form
\cos(\mathbf{O})
(see e.g.,
[
29
]
), where
\mathbf{O}
is a self-adjoint operator, usually a sum of position and/or momentum operators acting on different modes. We assume that we have access to the truncation of the unitary operators
(e^{i\mathbf{O}})_{N}
,
(e^{2i\mathbf{O}})_{N}
, and their Hermitian conjugates, which is feasible for linear combinations of position and momentum operators. Denoting
\mathbf{U}=e^{i\mathbf{O}}
, we have
\cos(\mathbf{O})=\frac{\mathbf{U}+\mathbf{U}^{\dagger}}{2}
.

Let us show that we can compute
\|(\cos(\mathbf{O})-{(\cos(\mathbf{O}))}_{N}){\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}\|_{1}
. Using the decomposition
\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}_{N}\oplus\mathcal{H}_{N}^{\perp}
, we introduce the notations:

\displaystyle\mathbf{U}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{U}_{N}&\mathbf{P}_{N}\mathbf{U}\mathbf{P}_{N}^{\perp}\\
\mathbf{P}_{N}^{\perp}\mathbf{U}\mathbf{P}_{N}&\mathbf{P}_{N}^{\perp}\mathbf{U}\mathbf{P}_{N}^{\perp}\end{pmatrix}\eqqcolon\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{A}&\mathbf{C}\\
\mathbf{B}&\mathbf{D}\end{pmatrix}.

(68)

We can now compute

\displaystyle\|(\cos(\mathbf{O})-{(\cos(\mathbf{O}))}_{N}){\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}\|_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\|((\mathbf{U}+\mathbf{U}^{\dagger})-(\mathbf{U}_{N}+\mathbf{U}_{N}^{\dagger})){\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}\|_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\|(\mathbf{P}_{N}^{\perp}\mathbf{U}+\mathbf{P}_{N}^{\perp}\mathbf{U}^{\dagger}){\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}\|_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{Tr}\left(\sqrt{{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}(\mathbf{B}^{\dagger}+\mathbf{C})(\mathbf{B}+\mathbf{C}^{\dagger}){\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{Tr}\left(\sqrt{{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}(\mathbf{B}^{\dagger}\mathbf{B}+\mathbf{C}\mathbf{C}^{\dagger}+\mathbf{B}^{\dagger}\mathbf{C}^{\dagger}+\mathbf{C}\mathbf{B}){\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}}\right).

(69)

As in
Eq.

37
, we have the following equalities that are obtained using
\mathbf{U}^{\dagger}\mathbf{U}=\mathbf{U}\mathbf{U}^{\dagger}=\operatorname{\mathbf{Id}}
:

\displaystyle\mathbf{B}^{\dagger}\mathbf{B}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{\mathbf{Id}}-\mathbf{A}^{\dagger}\mathbf{A},

(70)

\displaystyle\mathbf{C}\mathbf{C}^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{\mathbf{Id}}-\mathbf{A}\mathbf{A}^{\dagger}.

(71)

Hence, it remains to obtain
\mathbf{C}\mathbf{B}
and its Hermitian conjugate. To this aim, we simply use that
\mathbf{P}_{N}\mathbf{U}^{2}\mathbf{P}_{N}=\mathbf{A}^{2}+\mathbf{C}\mathbf{B}
. As a consequence, we obtain the following expression:

\displaystyle\|(\cos(\mathbf{O})-{(\cos(\mathbf{O}))}_{N}){\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}\|_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{Tr}\left(\sqrt{{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}(\operatorname{\mathbf{Id}}-\mathbf{U}_{N}^{\dagger}\mathbf{U}_{N}+{(\mathbf{U}^{2})}_{N}-(\mathbf{U}_{N})^{2}+h.c){\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}}\right).

(72)

5.1
Dynamical reshapings, single mode

Having an error estimate of the space truncation error not only allows us to bound the simulation’s final error but also to monitor it throughout the time integration process. With this, we can detect when the truncated space is too small –causing significant error– or overly large –resulting in wasted resources–. With this in mind, we developed an adaptive solver that dynamically adjusts the truncation size.
For the simulation on one bosonic mode, we propose the following algorithm which takes the following inputs:

\mathcal{L}_{N}

5.2
Dynamical reshapings, several modes

When dealing with
m
bosonic modes, the previous algorithm can be straightforwardly implemented by replacing the scalar
N
with the
m
-tuple
(N_{1},\ldots,N_{m})
. By fixing
n_{\pm}=(n_{1,\pm},\ldots,n_{m,\pm})
, we obtain a direction to expand and reduce the size of the density matrix. However, the reduced Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{(N_{1},\ldots,N_{m})}=\{\otimes_{j=1}^{m}\ket{k_{j}}\mid 0\leq k_{j}\leq N_{j}\}
is not always a good choice for simulations. For example, instead of bounding the number of excitations of each mode separately, we can bound the total number of excitations, leading to the following definition:
\mathcal{H}_{N_{\textrm{tot}}}=\{\otimes_{j=1}^{m}\ket{k_{j}}\mid 0\leq\sum_{j=1}^{m}k_{j}\leq N_{\textrm{tot}}\}

3.2.5

({\bf a^{\dagger}})^{2}{\bf b}+{\bf a}^{2}{\bf b^{\dagger}}

\frac{{\bf a^{\dagger}}{\bf a}}{2}+{\bf b^{\dagger}}{\bf b}
, it leads to the natural choice of truncation

. Note that in
Fig.

2
, where the truncated Hilbert space is
\mathcal{H}_{(N_{1},N_{2})}
, the truncation error is mostly determined by biggest
N_{\textrm{cat}}
such that
\mathcal{H}_{N_{\textrm{cat}}}\subset\mathcal{H}_{N_{1},N_{2}}
.

\|{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}(t)-\mathbf{P}_{N-n_{-}}{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}(t)\mathbf{P}_{N-n_{-}}\|_{1}
to the estimate, this is the jump one can see.

6
Time and space-time estimates

Up to this point, we have focused solely on the space-truncation error. In this part, we do not assume that we have access to
({\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}(t))_{t\geq 0}
but only to the discrete time trajectory
(\boldsymbol{\rho}_{n,\delta t})_{n\geq 0}
. Note that the space estimate we derived in
Lemma

1
cannot be easily combined with a time estimate. Indeed, if
\mathcal{L}
is an unbounded operator, it is not continuous from
\mathcal{K}^{1}
to
\mathcal{K}^{1}
. This implies that
\|\mathcal{L}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\rho}})-\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\rho})\|_{1}
might be large even for arbitrarily small
\|\tilde{\boldsymbol{\rho}}-\boldsymbol{\rho}\|_{1}
, preventing a naive approximation of the integral in
Lemma

1
by a
\mathcal{K}^{1}
-approximation of
({\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}(t))_{t\geq 0}
. We address this issue by presenting space-time estimates. We start by proving that the total distance between the continuous solution
e^{T\mathcal{L}}\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0}
and the space and time discretized approximation
\mathcal{F}_{\delta t}^{N_{step}}(\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0})
is smaller than the sum of errors made in each time-step: assuming that
N_{step}\delta t=T
, we have the following inequalities that start with a telescoping argument:

\displaystyle\|e^{T\mathcal{L}}\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0}-\mathcal{F}^{N_{step}}_{\delta t}(\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0})\|_{1}

\displaystyle=\left\|\sum_{n=0}^{N_{step}-1}e^{(N_{step}-n)\delta t\mathcal{L}}\mathcal{F}^{n}_{\delta t}\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0}-e^{(N_{step}-n-1)\delta t\mathcal{L}}\mathcal{F}^{n+1}_{\delta t}\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0}\right\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{n=0}^{N_{step}-1}\left\|e^{(N_{step}-n-1)\delta t\mathcal{L}}(e^{\delta t\mathcal{L}}\mathcal{F}^{n}_{\delta t}\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0}-\mathcal{F}^{n+1}_{\delta t}\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0})\right\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{n=0}^{N_{step}-1}\left\|e^{\delta t\mathcal{L}}\mathcal{F}^{n}_{\delta t}\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0}-\mathcal{F}^{n+1}_{\delta t}\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0}\right\|_{1},

(75)

where we used that the CPTP map
e^{\delta t\mathcal{L}}
contracts the trace-norm.
If we denote again
\boldsymbol{\rho}_{n,\delta t}=\mathcal{F}_{\delta t}^{n}\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0}
, we got

\displaystyle\|e^{N_{step}\delta t\mathcal{L}}\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0}-\mathcal{F}^{N_{step}}_{\delta t}(\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0})\|_{1}\leq\sum_{n=0}^{N_{step}-1}\left\|(e^{\delta t\mathcal{L}}-\mathcal{F}_{\delta t})\boldsymbol{\rho}_{n,\delta t}\right\|_{1}.

(76)

To compute a bound on the terms inside the sum, we restrict ourselves to the following cases:

•

Time-independent Lindbladian with a time solver based on
k^{th}
order Taylor truncation.

•

Time-dependent Lindbladian with a first order explicit Euler scheme.

6.1
Time-independent Lindbladian with
k^{th}
order Taylor scheme

For a fixed integer
k\geq 1
, we define the following discrete scheme that is simply the truncation of the Taylor expansion of
e^{\delta t\mathcal{L}_{N}}
, which we call the
k^{\text{th}}
order Taylor scheme:

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{\delta t}(\boldsymbol{\rho})=\sum_{j=0}^{k}\frac{\delta t^{j}}{j!}\mathcal{L}_{N}^{j}(\boldsymbol{\rho}).

(77)

Then, we have

Lemma 3
.

For any
\boldsymbol{\rho}\in\mathcal{K}_{s}^{1}(\mathcal{H}_{N})
that belongs to the domain of
\mathcal{L}^{k+1}
, we have

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{F}_{\delta t}(\boldsymbol{\rho})-e^{\delta t\mathcal{L}}(\boldsymbol{\rho})\|_{1}\leq\left\|\mathcal{F}_{\delta t}(\boldsymbol{\rho})-\sum_{j=0}^{k}\frac{\delta t^{j}}{j!}\mathcal{L}^{j}(\boldsymbol{\rho})\right\|_{1}+\frac{\delta t^{k+1}}{(k+1)!}\left\|\mathcal{L}^{k+1}(\boldsymbol{\rho})\right\|_{1}.

(78)

Before proving this Lemma, note that together with
Eq.

76
, it implies that

\displaystyle\|e^{T\mathcal{L}}\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0}-\mathcal{F}^{N_{step}}_{\delta t}(\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0})\|_{1}\leq\sum_{n=0}^{N_{step}-1}\left(\left\|\sum_{j=0}^{k}\frac{\delta t^{j}\mathcal{L}^{j}(\boldsymbol{\rho}_{n,\delta t})}{j!}-\mathcal{F}_{\delta t}(\boldsymbol{\rho}_{n,\delta t})\right\|_{1}+\frac{\delta t^{k+1}}{(k+1)!}\left\|\mathcal{L}^{k+1}(\boldsymbol{\rho}_{n,\delta t})\right\|_{1}\right).

(79)

Remark 4
.

In the case where
\mathcal{H}
is finite-dimensional and
\mathcal{H}_{N}=\mathcal{H}
, we have
\|\sum_{j=0}^{k}\frac{\delta t^{j}}{j!}\mathcal{L}^{j}(\boldsymbol{\rho})-\mathcal{F}_{\delta t}(\boldsymbol{\rho})\|_{1}=0
. Consequently, the estimate contains only the terms
\frac{\delta t^{k+1}}{(k+1)!}\|\mathcal{L}^{k+1}(\boldsymbol{\rho}_{n,\delta t})\|_{1}
.

Proof of
Lemma

3
.

With the triangle inequality, we get

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{F}_{\delta t}(\boldsymbol{\rho})-e^{\delta t\mathcal{L}}(\boldsymbol{\rho})\|_{1}\leq\left\|\mathcal{F}_{\delta t}(\boldsymbol{\rho})-\sum_{j=0}^{k}\frac{\delta t^{j}}{j!}\mathcal{L}^{j}(\boldsymbol{\rho})\right\|_{1}+\left\|\sum_{j=0}^{k}\frac{\delta t^{j}}{j!}\mathcal{L}^{j}(\boldsymbol{\rho})-e^{\delta t\mathcal{L}}(\boldsymbol{\rho})\right\|_{1}.

(80)

To deal with the second term, we use the integral form of the remainder of the Taylor expansion

\displaystyle\left\|e^{\delta t\mathcal{L}}(\boldsymbol{\rho})-\sum_{j=0}^{k}\frac{\delta t^{j}}{j!}\mathcal{L}^{j}(\boldsymbol{\rho})\right\|_{1}

\displaystyle=\left\|\frac{1}{k!}\int_{0}^{\delta t}\mathcal{L}^{k+1}(e^{s\mathcal{L}}\boldsymbol{\rho})(\delta t-s)^{k}ds\right\|_{1}

\displaystyle=\left\|\frac{1}{k!}\int_{0}^{\delta t}e^{s\mathcal{L}}\mathcal{L}^{k+1}(\boldsymbol{\rho})(\delta t-s)^{k}ds\right\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{k!}\int_{0}^{\delta t}\|\mathcal{L}^{k+1}(\boldsymbol{\rho})(\delta t-s)^{k}ds\|_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{\delta t^{k+1}\|\mathcal{L}^{k+1}(\boldsymbol{\rho})\|_{1}}{(k+1)!}.

(81)

∎

6.2
Time-dependent Lindbladian with a first order explicit Euler scheme.

Let us now consider a time-dependent Lindbladian that we denote
\mathcal{L}(t,\cdot)
. For
t_{1}\leq t_{2}
, the flow of the Lindbladian is the bounded linear map
\Phi(t_{1},t_{2},\cdot):\mathcal{K}^{1}\to\mathcal{K}^{1}
characterized by the following set of equations for all
\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0}
smooth enough
2
2

2

See e.g.
[
11
, Section 3.2]
:

\displaystyle\partial_{t_{2}}\Phi(t_{1},t_{2},\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0})=\mathcal{L}(t_{2},\Phi(t_{1},t_{2},\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0})),\quad\Phi(t_{1},t_{1},\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0})=\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0},\quad\forall t_{2}\geq t_{1}

(82)

The first order explicit Euler scheme is

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{\delta t}(t,\boldsymbol{\rho})=\boldsymbol{\rho}+\delta t\mathcal{L}_{N}(t,\boldsymbol{\rho}).

(83)

The applications
\boldsymbol{\rho}\mapsto\Phi(t_{1},t_{2},\boldsymbol{\rho})
are CPTP map, so
Eq.

76
is modified as follows:

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{\Phi}(0,T,\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0})-\boldsymbol{\rho}_{N_{step},\delta t}\|_{1}\leq\sum_{n=0}^{N_{step}-1}\|\boldsymbol{\Phi}(t_{n},t_{n+1},\boldsymbol{\rho}_{n,\delta t})-\mathcal{F}_{\delta t}(t_{n},\boldsymbol{\rho}_{n,\delta t})\|_{1},

(84)

with
t_{n}=n\delta t
and

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\rho}_{n+1,\delta t}=\mathcal{F}_{\delta t}(n\delta t,\boldsymbol{\rho}_{n,\delta t}),\quad\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0,\delta t}=\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0}.

(85)

Lemma 4
.

The norm of the error on each time step is bounded by

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{\Phi}(t_{n},t_{n+1},\boldsymbol{\rho}_{n,\delta t})-\mathcal{F}_{\delta t}(t_{n},\boldsymbol{\rho}_{n,\delta t})\|_{1}\leq\delta t\sup_{t_{n}\leq s\leq t_{n+1}}\|(\mathcal{L}(s,\cdot)-\mathcal{L}(t_{n},\cdot))\boldsymbol{\rho}_{n,\delta t}\|_{1}

\displaystyle+\frac{\delta t^{2}}{2}\sup_{t_{n}\leq s\leq t_{n+1}}\|\mathcal{L}(s,(\mathcal{L}(t_{n},\boldsymbol{\rho}_{n,\delta t})))\|_{1}+\delta t\|(\mathcal{L}(t_{n},\cdot)-\mathcal{L}_{N}(t_{n},\cdot))\boldsymbol{\rho}_{n,\delta t}\|_{1}.

(86)

Proof.

To lighten the notation, we focus on the first time-step and denote
\boldsymbol{\rho}_{t}=\Phi(t,0,\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0})
. We introduce the error between the exact solution and the first order euler scheme using
\mathcal{L}
and not
\mathcal{L}_{N}
:

\displaystyle\mathbf{r}(t)=\boldsymbol{\rho}_{t}-\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0}-t\mathcal{L}(0,\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0}).

(87)

Taking the time derivative, we have,

\displaystyle\dot{\mathbf{r}}(t)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{L}(t,\boldsymbol{\rho}(t))-\mathcal{L}(0,\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{L}(t,\mathbf{r}(t))+\mathcal{L}(t,\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0}+t\mathcal{L}(0,\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0}))-\mathcal{L}(0,\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0}).

(88)

Using Duhamel principle, we get

\displaystyle\mathbf{r}(t)=\boldsymbol{\Phi}(0,t,\mathbf{r}(0))+\int_{0}^{t}\boldsymbol{\Phi}(s,t,\mathcal{L}(s,\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0}+s\mathcal{L}(0,\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0}))-\mathcal{L}(0,\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0}))ds.

(89)

As
\Phi(s,t,\cdot)
contracts the trace norm and
\mathbf{r}(0)=0
, we get

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{r}(t)\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{t}\|\mathcal{L}(s,\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0}+s\mathcal{L}(0,\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0}))-\mathcal{L}(0,\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0})\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{t}\|(\mathcal{L}(s,\cdot)-\mathcal{L}(0,\cdot))(\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0})\|_{1}+\|s\mathcal{L}(s,\mathcal{L}(0,\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0}))\|_{1}ds.

(90)

Hence,

\displaystyle\|e(\delta t)\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\delta t\sup_{0\leq s\leq\delta t}\|(\mathcal{L}(s,\cdot)-\mathcal{L}(0,\cdot))\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0}\|_{1}+\frac{\delta t^{2}}{2}\sup_{0\leq s\leq\delta t}\|\mathcal{L}(s,(\mathcal{L}(0,\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0})))\|_{1}.

(91)

As

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Phi}(0,\delta t,\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0})-\mathcal{F}_{\delta t}(0,\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0})

\displaystyle=\mathbf{r}(\delta t)+\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0}+\delta t\mathcal{L}(0,\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0})-\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0}-\delta t\mathcal{L}_{N}(0,\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0})

\displaystyle=\mathbf{r}(\delta t)+\delta t(\mathcal{L}(0,\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0})-\mathcal{L}_{N}(0,\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0})),

(92)

we use a triangle inequality to finish the proof of
Lemma

4
.
∎

Note that in the time-independent case, we recover

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{r}(\delta t)\|_{1}\leq\frac{\delta t^{2}}{2}\|\mathcal{L}(0,\mathcal{L}(0,\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0}))\|_{1}+\delta t\|(\mathcal{L}(0,\cdot)-\mathcal{L}_{N}(0,\cdot))\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0}\|_{1}.

(93)

corresponding to
Lemma

3
for
k=1
.

It is important to notice that this estimate requires prior knowledge on the regularity of
s\mapsto\mathcal{L}(s,\cdot)
. A typical case is when the Lindbladian (or part of it) is of the form
u(t)\mathcal{L}_{0}
where
\mathcal{L}_{0}
is time-independent. For example, if
u
is a
\mathcal{C}^{1}
function, then

\displaystyle\sup_{0\leq s\leq\delta t}\|(\mathcal{L}(s,\cdot)-\mathcal{L}(0,\cdot))\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0}\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\delta t\|u^{\prime}\|_{L^{\infty}([0,\delta t])}\|\mathcal{L}_{0}(\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0})\|_{1},

(94)

\displaystyle\sup_{0\leq s\leq\delta t}\|\mathcal{L}(s,(\mathcal{L}(0,\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0})))\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq|u(0)|\|u\|_{L^{\infty}([0,\delta t])}\|\mathcal{L}_{0}^{2}(\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0})\|_{1}.

(95)

6.3.1
Frequency driven damped oscillator revisited -
{\bf H}=u(t){\bf a^{\dagger}}{\bf a}
and
\mathcal{D}_{{\bf a}}

Let us now generalize the truncation estimate obtained in
Section

3.2.1
. We assume that we use a first order explicit Euler scheme for the time integration, and that
u(t)
is a
\mathcal{C}^{1}
function.
Then, following
Section

6.2
, we can bound the error between the continuous solution
(\boldsymbol{\rho}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
and the discrete time approximation with step-size
\delta t
,
(\boldsymbol{\rho}_{n,\delta t})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, by

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{\rho}_{n\delta t}-\boldsymbol{\rho}_{n,\delta t}\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\delta t^{2}\|u^{\prime}\|_{\infty}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\|[{\bf a^{\dagger}}_{N}{\bf a}_{N},\boldsymbol{\rho}_{n,\delta t}]\|_{1}

\displaystyle+\frac{\delta t^{2}}{2}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\|\mathcal{D}[{\bf a}_{N}](\mathcal{L}_{N}(k\delta t,\boldsymbol{\rho}_{k,\delta t}))\|_{1}+\|u\|_{\infty}\|[{\bf a^{\dagger}}_{N}{\bf a}_{N},(\mathcal{L}_{N}(k\delta t,\boldsymbol{\rho}_{k,\delta t}))]\|_{1}.

(96)

6.3.2
Driven oscillator revisited -
{\bf H}=u(t)({\bf a}+{\bf a^{\dagger}})

In
Section

3.2.2
, we had the space estimate

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{\rho}_{t}-{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}(t)\|_{1}\leq\int_{0}^{t}2|u(s)|\sqrt{N+1}\sqrt{\bra{N}{{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{(N)}}(s)}^{2}\ket{N}}ds.

(97)

To obtain a space-time estimate for a first order explicit Euler scheme, we get from
Lemma

4

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{\rho}_{n\delta t}-\boldsymbol{\rho}_{n,\delta t}\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\delta t^{2}\|u^{\prime}\|_{\infty}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\|[{\bf a}_{N+1}+{\bf a^{\dagger}}_{N+1},\boldsymbol{\rho}_{k,\delta t}]\|_{1}

\displaystyle+\delta t^{2}\|u\|_{\infty}\sum_{k=0}^{n}|u(k\delta t)|\|[{\bf a}_{N+2}+{\bf a^{\dagger}}_{N+2},[{\bf a}_{N+1}+{\bf a^{\dagger}}_{N+1},\boldsymbol{\rho}_{k,\delta t}]]\|_{1}

\displaystyle+2\delta t\sum_{k=0}^{n}|u(k\delta t)|\sqrt{N+1}\sqrt{\bra{N}{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{k,\delta t}}^{2}\ket{N}}.

(98)

Note that it is needed to perform computation in the space
\mathcal{K}^{1}_{s}(\mathcal{H}_{N+2})
for the second sum.

6.3.3
General bosonic modes

In this short subsection, we explain how to extend the result of
Section

3
, and briefly show that for a bosonic mode with polynomials in creation and annihilation, we can also compute the space-time estimate. We only focus on the one mode case, the generalization to several modes is similar to
Section

3.1
.
As a consequence of
Corollaries

1
and
2
, we get:

Corollary 3
.

If the Hamiltonian
{\bf H}
and the dissipators
{\mathbf{\Gamma}}_{i}
, are polynomials in
{\bf a}
and
{\bf a^{\dagger}}
, of degree
d_{{\bf H}}
and
d_{i}
resp., then defining
d=\max(d_{H},2\max_{i}(d_{i}))
we have:

\displaystyle(\mathcal{L}-\mathcal{L}_{N})(\boldsymbol{\rho}_{N})

\displaystyle=(\mathcal{L}_{N+d}-\mathcal{L}_{N})(\boldsymbol{\rho}_{N})\in\mathcal{K}_{s}^{1}(\mathcal{H}_{N+d}).

(99)

This naturally extends to

\displaystyle(\mathcal{L}^{k}-\mathcal{L}_{N}^{k})(\boldsymbol{\rho}_{N})

\displaystyle=(\mathcal{L}_{N+kd}^{k}-\mathcal{L}_{N}^{k})(\boldsymbol{\rho}_{N})\in\mathcal{K}_{s}^{1}(\mathcal{H}_{N+kd}).

(100)

Hence, we can always numerically compute the estimate of
Lemma

3
for an order
k
scheme by embedding
\boldsymbol{\rho}_{n,\delta t}
in
\mathcal{K}_{s}^{1}(\mathcal{H}_{N+(k+1)d})
, and computing the trace norm of
(\mathcal{L}^{j}_{N+(k+1)d}-\mathcal{L}^{j}_{N})(\boldsymbol{\rho}_{n,\delta t})
for
1\leq j\leq k
and of
\mathcal{L}^{k+1}_{N+(k+1)d}\boldsymbol{\rho}_{n,\delta t}
in this finite dimensional space.

7
Conclusion

In this article, we provided
a posteriori
computable error bounds for the space-truncation error and/or time discretization. We have demonstrated numerically the efficiency of our approach for a large class of problems involving bosonic modes.

Several promising avenues for future research are worth pursuing:

1.

In
Section

4
, we have demonstrated how space estimates can be applied to various operators related to unitary operators. Extending these methods to other types of systems with different dissipators would be a valuable next step.

2.

We have not addressed the proof of convergence of the approximations toward the solution
(\boldsymbol{\rho}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
in this work. This problem has been initiated for time discretization with unbounded operators in
[
26
]
and in
[
24
]
for space discretization, but space-time discretization remains an open question.

3.

A powerful property that would be beneficial to prove with
a posteriori
error bounds is an efficiency result. Specifically, while we know that the error is smaller than the estimate, it would be advantageous to prove the existence of a constant such that the estimate is controlled by this constant times the error. Although such a property may not hold universally, identifying verifiable criteria that ensure its validity would be a significant advancement.

4.

We believe that maintaining and extending the library
dynamiqs_adaptive
(see
Section

5.3
) should be a valuable asset for the community. Several improvements, both on the numerical and algorithmic side could be implemented. In particular, general support of the space-time estimates would be very interesting.

5.

In this article, we have restricted ourselves to linear approximations for space discretization, specifically using a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{N}
. Many recent works, however, consider non-linear approximations, such as low-rank approximations, tensor networks, and many others. These settings are fundamentally different as they do not yield a Lindblad equation on the approximation manifold. Nevertheless, investigating
a posteriori
error estimates in these contexts would be extremely interesting and could provide valuable insights.

6.

Another worth considering extension is the stochastic unraveling of the Lindblad master equation. In this case, providing either strong or weak
a posteriori
estimates on the truncation and/or time discretization errors has not been explored yet.
