A novel amide corrosion inhibitor derived from waste cooking oil for mild steel in hydrochloric acid
Authors: Khalid M. Abed, Osama A. Mohsen, Abdullah G. Al-Issawi, Mahmood W. Faraj, Najah M. Al-Shuwaiki, Sunarti Abd Rahman, Adeeb Hayyan, Wan Jefrey Basirun, Bhaskar Sen Gupta, Jehad Saleh, M. Zulhaziman M. Salleh, Zulhelmi Amir, Yousef Mohammed Alanazi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study investigates the transformation of waste cooking oils into amides to serve as corrosion inhibitors for carbon steel in hydrochloric acid environments. Fourier-transformed infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) and proton nuclear magnetic resonance (1HNMR) methods were then used to analyze the presence of amides inhibitors. The application of inhibitor to carbon steel prior to immersion in hydrochloric acid was effective in delaying the corrosion rate and increasing resistance towards corrosion by forming a protective barrier on the surface. The effects of temperature and inhibitory ratio on the efficacy of corrosion inhibition under various conditions were also determined. The maximum effectiveness of 88.5% was attained at 298 K with an inhibitor ratio of 2000 ppm. Kinetic analysis at different temperatures was also carried out to determine the response mechanisms of inhibitors. According to the inhibitor thermal adsorption isotherm, the steel surface is complexed based on the Langmuir adsorption isotherm. Inhibition and corrosion investigations were also performed by using electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS). Scanning electron microscopy, energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy and atomic force microscopy study established the existence of a protective adsorbed film of inhibitor molecules over the metal surface. This study contributes to the advancement of science and engineering pertaining to improved corrosion resistance under acidic conditions and the mitigation of used cooking oils.

Introduction

The detrimental effects of metal corrosion have become an issue of global importance. Corrosive environments are present in nearly every industry, from acid washing and steel processing to pickling and petrol processing, as well as for the quality and productivity-related removal of rust from alloy surfaces and many more1. The deterioration of a material (often a metal) via oxidation in the environment is known as corrosion2,3. When exposed to the environment, corrosion can be classified into (i) dry or chemical corrosion and (ii) wet or electrochemical corrosion. The different types of dry or chemical corrosion are oxygen or oxidation corrosion, hydrogen corrosion, and liquid metal corrosion4.

The annual global loss of metallic corrosion is more than $2 trillion, and experts believe 25–30% of that could be avoided if proper corrosion protection is applied. A construction project with insufficient planning will eventually result in corroded structures that must be replaced, which is not only a waste of natural resources but also contrary to the efforts of long-term global sustainability. In addition to the economic effects, corrosion may cause safety hazards, loss of life, extra indirect costs, and structural damage. In the United States alone, corrosion causes losses between $8 to $126 billion a year. The main reasons of these are downtime, overdesign, product loss, efficiency loss, and contamination5,6

Various methods can be used to mitigate corrosion such as chemical modification of the metal surface, changing the structural design of the metal, alteration of the corrosive environment and the oxidation potential of the metal in the environment, and employing corrosion inhibitors7. However, the current available techniques possess limited applications, due to the huge expenses and challenges of conversion into a corrosion-resistant material. In industrial applications, the alteration of these process environment is unsuitable as the metal needs to be exposed to a certain reactive media. While anti-corrosion coating is one of the technologies that could also increase the emission of carbon dioxide (CO2)8,9.

The scientific community is now facing an increasing interest in resources optimization and reduction of environmental impact, both for new and existing production processes. The research on these cross-cutting topics is growing in an exponential manner in many areas of research. Generally, to develop such ambitious research, many scientists have proposed into recycling of waste as raw materials for both productions and conversion of existing processes10,11. This is a difficult path, as the industries demand the acceleration to reach these goals immediately. In this context, the waste cooking oil stands out as a promising solution due to its availability, low cost, and eco-friendly nature12.

Most cooking oils (also known as edible oils) originates from plants and animals. The Canadian government defines edible oil as any food substance which should not come from any dairy product, and regardless of its composition, source or origin, that is completely manufactured for human consumption or partially derived from fats or oils other than that of milk. Various fatty acids such as mono- and polyunsaturated fatty acids along with the saturated fatty acids are found in cooking oils. Some examples of cooking oils are palm oil, Coconut oil, Olive oil, Soybean oil, Canola oil, Sunflower oil, and Sesame oil, have widespread use due to the remarkable oxidation stability13. Reheating or reusing cooking oil could increase the acidity and the cholesterol levels in the human body14–16. In the UK, the collection of waste cooking oils is controlled by the Environment Agency. The collection of used cooking oils should be disposed through a licensed business by the environmental agency as a waste carrier. Used cooking oil is a waste generated widely by all households, restaurants, and the food industry. It is estimated that each person in the European Union generates 5 kg of used cooking oils per year, totaling 2.5 million metric tons17–21. Therefore, the efficiency of waste cooking oils as a corrosion inhibitor was investigated in this study and was further evaluated of its potential in this field.

The growing environmental concern has caused corrosion mitigation solutions to shift their attention to the fabrication of green corrosion inhibitors in recent years. Usually, these inhibitors come from natural, renewable, and biodegradable sources such waste-derived compounds, biopolymers, and plant extracts. Green corrosion inhibitors are more environmentally friendly, less harmful, and frequently less expensive than traditional synthetic inhibitors. They also effectively safeguard metals in harsh corrosive conditions. Global tendencies to reduce the environmental impact and encourage safe industrial practices are in line with this sustainable strategy13,22–24. This study aims to address a research gap in the literature, as used cooking oils have not received sufficient attention as a source for the preparation of amide inhibitors. To our knowledge, this study is one of the first attempts to explore the potential of used cooking oils in this context, which lends it a novelty and contributes to the development of sustainable and environmentally friendly solutions in the field of corrosion inhibitors.

Waste cooking oil collected from restaurants was converted into a corrosion inhibitor by the conversion into fatty acid amides (FAA). This study aims to explore the role of aliphatic amides in corrosion inhibition based on the proton (1H) NMR spectroscopy. The inhibitor’s efficacy was tested using mild steel immersed in a hydrochloric acid medium in the presence and absence of the inhibitor. The impact of the corrosion inhibitor on the metal surface was investigated by the gravimetric method, EIS methods, SEM, EDX, and AFM. This approach emphasizes the possibilities of using waste materials for sustainable, ecologically friendly corrosion protection solutions while producing glycerol as the byproduct that is used for many applications.

Experimental method

Material

The material used was used cooking oil, 2% NaCl solution, laboratory filter papers, chloroform, absolute ethanol, 1 M HCl, aqueous NH3 solution, glass beaker, heater, Reaction cooling system, mild steel (3.5*4.5 cm).

Purification

To purify the used cooking oil, laboratory filter paper was used to eliminate any suspended impurities in the oil. Then 100 ml of oil we mixed with a NaCl solution and placed on the heater while stirring for 15 min, left for an hour and then dried it for twenty minutes in an oven. it was left to cool to ambient temperature, then dissolved in Chloroform solution for thirty minutes at 80 °C. The hot mixture was filtered, followed by thermal evaporation of the chloroform using a rotary evaporator, leading to the purified cooking oil25.

Esterification of fatty acid

Esterification is the formation of an ester from the combination of an acid (in this case, fatty acids) with an alcohol (such as ethanol). The reaction is typically achieved using a strong acid catalyst such as sulfuric acid or a Lewis acid like p-toluene sulfonic acid. The reaction conditions such as reaction time and temperature are optimized to enhance the esterification reaction and minimizing the unwanted side reactions. The resulting product is a mixture of fatty acid esters, which are present in the plant oil as the ester forms of the fatty acids, often used in bio-based coatings with anticorrosive properties 26. In this experiment, 85 ml of purified cooking oil was mixed with 250 ml of absolute ethanol solution for 12 h, at 85° C with continuous mixing at 510 rpm. After an hour, five drops of sulfuric acid we added to catalyze the reaction to completion 27–29.

Amidation of fatty acid esters

Amidation is a process of converting the fatty acid esters into fatty acid amides by replacing the ester group (−COO−) with an amide group (−CONH−). The amidation reaction typically involves the use of ammonia (NH3) as the aminating agent. The reaction can be carried out under various conditions, such as elevated temperatures and pressures, to promote the formation of fatty acid amides. Catalysts, such as transition metal catalysts or enzymes, may be employed to enhance the reaction rate and selectivity. Two analytical techniques such as high-performance liquid chromatography or gas chromatography can be used to observe the reaction progress and completion. At this stage, we mixed the fatty acid ester obtained from the esterification reaction with 100 ml of ammonia solution for 48 h, at of 85 °C and 510 rpm. The reaction requires continuous cooling, so we connected the reaction flask with a cooling system25,30. Figure 1 illustrates the preparation of fatty acid amide (FAA) corrosion inhibitor. The conversion of used cooking oils shows that the esterification process yields approximately 81%, glycerol as the byproduct. Meanwhile, the amidation step yields 72% and the yield of the total amide product is approximately 0.60–0.76 kg per 1 kg of used cooking oil. In addition, byproducts such as glycerol, methanol, and water were added.Fig. 1Scheme of synthesis.

Characterization

Fourier transformed infrared spectroscopy (FTIR)

FTIR is an analytical technique employed to identify the functional groups of polymeric, organic, and inorganic materials. The chemical properties of samples can be monitored through FTIR technique using infrared light. All FTIR was measured using BRUKER Tensor27\Germany25,27.

Proton nuclear magnetic resonance (1HNMR)

The 1HNMR analysis was conducted using solutions containing synthesized products and (dimethyl sulfoxide) solvent. BRUKER NMR-400 MHz spectrometer was used to confirm the final product.

Surface analyses

Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM) was used to investigate the surface morphology and particle size distribution of mild steel specimens. This test was done using Zeiss MERLIN Compact SEM at Alkhour Electron Microscopy Unit, Baghdad. The sample was immersed in 1 M HCl solution, no extra chemicals were used throughout the test, with the inhibitor under study present at 2000 ppm with an exposure time of 12 h. Under the same condition, Energy Dispersive X-ray Spectroscopy (EDX) (Hitachi S-3400 N with Thermonoran EDS analyzer) and Atomic Force Microscopy (AFM) were used to analyze the composition and surface morphology of corrosion products on carbon steel samples both with and without an inhibitor.

Kinetic study

The Arrhenius equation is generally used to relate the reaction rates, particularly the corrosion rates with the temperature. The estimation. of the corrosion rate at different temperatures could determine the activation energy (Ea) of the process. The Ea is defined as the minimum amount of energy required to initiate the corrosion reaction. In the corrosion process, Ea represents the energy required to dissolve the mild steel atoms in the acidic solution. The Ea is inversely proportional to the corrosion rate; where higher Ea results in a lower rate of reaction, or a slower corrosion rate. Electrochemical test

Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) is an electrochemical technique that can be used to assess the corrosion rate. Electrochemical cells consist of three electrodes represented by the steel specimen, saturated calomel electrode, and a platinum foil which served as the working (WE), reference (RE), and counter electrodes (CE), respectively. The cell volume can accommodate 300 ml of acidic solution at 323 K. The different ratios of the prepared inhibitor ranging from 250 to 2000 ppm, were added into the solution to examine the inhibitor performance. An inhibitor-free acidic solution was used as the control in these experiments. All the tests were conducted after immersion of the WE in the acidic solution for 10 min. A Corr Test Workstation (Corr Test, China) was used to conduct the EIS experiments. The EIS was conducted at the open circuit potential (OCP) with 10 mV perturbation amplitude at 10 points per frequency decade over a frequency range of 10 kHz to 10 mHz.

Effect of inhibitor concentration

Figure 6a shows the effect of inhibitor concentration (FAA) on the corrosion rate at different temperatures from 293 to 333 K. The results reveal that the corrosion rate decrease with the addition of FAA at any temperature, as these inhibitors form inhibitive protected layers on the mild steel surface against corrosion. Figure 6b provides better visualization of the IE % of the FAA at different concentration and temperatures. The results show that the IE % increases with the inhibitor concentration and decreases with temperature, consistent with previously reported temperature-dependent behavior of similar organic inhibitors48. This result is predictable as the inhibitor minimizes the corrosion rate of mild steel acidic solution via adsorption onto the metal surface and formation of a preventive layer14,49.Fig. 6Effect of the inhibitor concentration on (a) corrosion rate (CR) and (b) inhibition efficiency (IE%) at different temperatures.

Effect of the inhibitor concentration on (a) corrosion rate (CR) and (b) inhibition efficiency (IE%) at different temperatures.

At a given temperature, the IE % increases as the concentration of inhibitor increases. For example, the IE % at the highest concentration (2000 ppm) is more than double the efficiency at the lowest concentration (250 ppm) at 293 K50,51. While at a given concentration, the IE % increases as the temperature decreases, e.g. at the highest concentration (2000 ppm), the IE % at 293 K is about 88% higher than the efficiency at 323 K. From these results, it was found that the highest achieved efficiency is 88.5% at 293 K with an inhibitor concentration of 2000 ppm52,53. These results reveal that the mild steel corrosion rate was reduced effectively by the FAA inhibitor, and its efficiency can be improved by increasing the ratio and decreasing the temperature. According to the experimental results, the inhibitory efficacy increases at 2000 ppm. When the metal’s adsorption sites on the surface become saturated, fewer active sites are available for new inhibitor molecules, therefore further concentration increases are unlikely to result in a noticeable improvement in efficiency. Excessively high concentrations of inhibitor molecules might cause them to combine or form multilayers, which can lower the protective film’s homogeneity and perhaps make corrosion prevention less effective. This result emphasizes the significance of tailoring inhibitor concentration for real-world applications and is consistent with adsorption-based inhibition mechanisms24,54.

EIS is a useful tool for studying metal corrosion and inhibitor performance, as widely demonstrated in studies involving plant-based and synthetic inhibitors63. The impedance data from the EIS tests provides some insights on the mechanisms of inhibitor and electrochemical processes. In this research, we investigate the effect of a concentration-dependent corrosion inhibitor on the impedance response of a metal surface41. The Nyquist plots in Fig. 9a is consistent with present studies on metal corrosion in various acidic conditions41,64. The Nyquist plots for the mild steel corrosion in acidic solution (1 M HCl) at 293 K are shown in Fig. 9a, in the absence and with different concentrations of FAA. Comparison with other green corrosion inhibitors

Although the utilization of used cooking oil as a corrosion inhibitor is an innovative and environmentally friendly approach, it is important to compare it with other previously studied plant-based corrosion inhibitors that have demonstrated high inhibition efficiency in harsh environments.

For example, studies have shown that plant extracts such as aloe vera, ginger, henna, and some essential oils are capable of forming a protective layer on metal surfaces, effectively reducing the rate of corrosion. In a study of aloe vera, an inhibition efficiency of up to 82.28% was achieved at a concentration of 300 mg/L in a saline aqueous medium, which is significantly higher than conventional synthetic inhibitors67.

Essential oils extracted from plants such as cinnamon and cypress have also demonstrated inhibition efficiency of up to 98% in hydrochloric acid, where active compounds such as phenols, aldehydes, and ketones interact with the metal surface and form an insulating layer68.

In contrast, used cooking oil consists of residual fatty acids, such as linoleic and oleic acids, which form a nonpolar aqueous layer that prevents oxidants from reaching the metal surface69. However, its inhibitory efficiency remains lower than that achieved by pure plant extracts rich in active compounds, especially in acidic conditions or when contaminants such as water or short-chain acids are present69.

It is worth noting that some studies have indicated that the introduction of amino acids such as lysine and arginine into used cooking oil improves its inhibitory efficiency. Still, this efficiency is negatively affected by the simultaneous presence of water and organic acids.

A study by Abd El-Lateef et al.70 achieved a high inhibition efficiency of up to 98.6% in 1 M hydrochloric acid using a plant-based surfactant prepared from soybean oil via transesterification and gas flow over the oil surface to produce a carboxylic ether70. This emulsion forms an organized micelle layer that inhibits the diffusion of aggressive ions towards the carbon steel surface. This performance is among the highest values ​​reported for “green” corrosion inhibitors at present70.

Additionally, natural deep eutectic solvents (NADES) prepared from sugar-based NADES were used as a green corrosion inhibitor for carbon steel in 0.5 M sulfuric acid53. The formulation achieved a maximum inhibition efficiency of 82% at 50 mmol/L and 298 K, by forming an electrostatic double layer that prevents ionic access to the metal surface. This study53 further demonstrates the potential of functionalizing simple natural product-based materials for corrosion inhibition without the use of toxic compounds53.

As shown in Table 7, the efficiency of plant inhibitors varies depending on the nature of the active compounds and the polarity of the medium. Phenolic compounds (such as cinnamon oil) or emulsions with a well-distributed micelle (such as soybean surfactant) outperform other alternatives. However, the current study presents a new approach that combines the recycling of used cooking oil with its chemical conversion into a fatty amide with higher polarity than the free fatty acids themselves. Conclusions

This study aims to investigate the possibility of converting used cooking oils into amide compounds for use as effective corrosion inhibitors for carbon steel in hydrochloric acid environments. Analysis of the FTIR and 1H NMR spectra shows that the compound under study follows the spectral pattern of aliphatic amides, where these compounds are characterized by the signals of the -NH group at high displacements, as well as the carbonyl effects on the adjacent methylene group. The partitioning pattern reflects the complexity of the chemical environment of the compound, providing valuable information about the FAA inhibitor molecular structure. SEM, EDX and AFM analyses revealed that FAA molecules inhibit corrosion by forming a thin layer on the metal surface. The FAA derived from waste cooking oil is an active inhibitor. The maximum effectiveness of 88.5% was attained at 298 K with an inhibitor ratio of 2000 ppm. The IE% %, ∆H, and ∆S, and Ea increase with the inhibitor concentration. However, the IE % decreases with the increase in temperature. These findings could be useful in improving the strategies for optimizing the inhibitor uses in industrial applications. The Langmuir adsorption isotherm accurately describes the FAA inhibitor adsorption on the surface of mild steel. The analysis of the percentage increase from the impedance data shows the concentration-dependent effectiveness of the FAA inhibitor. An increase in the inhibitor concentration greatly enhances the imaginary resistance, showing that the FAA inhibitor is most likely to form a preventive layer that enhances the surface’s ability to resist corrosion. The development of eco-friendly amide-based inhibitors from used cooking oil offers effective corrosion protection, provides numerous environmental benefits, and contributes to the advancement of the sustainable corrosion research field.
