The Interplay between Utility and Risk in Portfolio Selection We are very grateful to Cosimo Munari for stimulating discussions on the material. We also thank the participants of the London-Oxford-Warwick workshop and the Financial Mathematics seminar at King’s College London for their comments.
Leonardo Baggiani, Martin Herdegen, Nazem Khan

Abstract
We revisit the problem of portfolio selection, where an investor maximizes utility subject to a risk constraint. Our framework is very general and accommodates a wide range of utility and risk functionals, including non-concave utilities such as S S -shaped utilities from prospect theory and non-convex risk measures such as Value at Risk. Our main contribution is a novel and complete characterization of well-posedness for utility-risk portfolio selection in one period that takes the interplay between the utility and the risk objectives fully into account. We show that under mild regularity conditions the minimal necessary and sufficient condition for well-posedness is given by a very simple either-or criterion: either the utility functional or the risk functional need to satisfy the axiom of sensitivity to large losses. This allows to easily describe well-posedness or ill-posedness for many utility/risk pairs, which we illustrate by a large number of examples. In the special case of expected utility maximization without a risk constraint (but including non-concave utilities), we show that well-posedness is fully characterised by the asymptotic loss-gain ratio , a simple and interpretable quantity that describes the investor’s asymptotic relative weighting of large losses versus large gains.



Keywords
: portfolio selection, utility maximization, risk measures, sensitivity to large losses, asymptotic loss-gain ratio

1
Introduction

In this paper, we revisit portfolio selection in a one-period setting. An investor trades in a market consisting of one riskless asset and
d
risky assets. Denote by
\bar{S}_{0}\coloneqq(S^{0}_{0},\ldots,S^{d}_{0})
the initial prices, assumed to be positive constants, and by
\bar{S}_{1}\coloneqq(S^{0}_{1},\ldots,S^{d}_{1})
the terminal prices, which are assumed to take values in some integrable space of positions
L
. Given an increasing utility functional
\mathcal{U}:L\to[-\infty,\infty)
and a decreasing risk functional
\mathcal{R}:L\to(-\infty,\infty]
, the investor solves the optimization problem

\sup_{\bar{\vartheta}\in\mathbb{R}^{1+d}}\mathcal{U}(\bar{\vartheta}\cdot\bar{S}_{1})\hskip 10.00002pt\text{subject to}\hskip 10.00002pt\begin{cases}\bar{\vartheta}\cdot\bar{S}_{0}=w,\\
\mathcal{R}(\bar{\vartheta}\cdot\bar{S}_{1})\leq R_{\max},\end{cases}

(1.1)

where
w>0
denotes her initial wealth,
R_{\max}
is the maximum allowable risk and portfolios
\bar{\vartheta}\in\mathbb{R}^{1+d}
are parameterized in numbers of shares. To exclude trivially ill-posed or well-posed cases, we assume that the market is arbitrage-free and
\mathcal{U}(Y)<\mathcal{U}(\infty)\coloneqq\lim_{y\to\infty}\mathcal{U}(y)
for all
Y\in L
. We refer to the problem (
1.1
) as utility-risk or
(\mathcal{U},\mathcal{R})
portfolio selection in the sequel.

Our aim is to understand the interplay between utility and risk in portfolio selection, and to identify
necessary
and
sufficient
conditions on the utility/risk pair
(\mathcal{U},\mathcal{R})
that ensure existence of an optimizer in utility-risk portfolio selection
across all markets
.

Motivation

There are several reasons to study such
market-independent well-posedness
. These are best appreciated once the following observation is made. As we will see in Theorem
4.7
, for the majority of utility/risk pairs
(\mathcal{U},\mathcal{R})
— in particular, for every utility/risk pair used in practice —
(\mathcal{U},\mathcal{R})
-portfolio selection falls into one of two regimes: either (
1.1
) admits a maximizer for every market; or there exists a market and a sequence of portfolios
(\bar{\vartheta}_{n})_{n\geq 1}\subset\mathbb{R}^{1+d}
with

\bar{\vartheta}_{n}\cdot\bar{S}_{0}=w,\hskip 10.00002pt\mathcal{R}(\bar{\vartheta}_{n}\cdot\bar{S}_{1})\leq R_{\max}\hskip 10.00002pt\textnormal{and}\hskip 10.00002pt\mathcal{U}(\bar{\vartheta}_{n}\cdot\bar{S}_{1})\to\mathcal{U}(\infty).

From the
investor’s perspective
, it is desirable to know that, regardless of the market model, an optimizer exists within the feasible set. From the
regulator’s perspective
, market-independent well-posedness guarantees that the “bliss point”
\mathcal{U}(\infty)
cannot be attained in any market. In this sense, the risk constraint is genuinely effective, ensuring that utility cannot be driven to its supremum except in the presence of arbitrage.

From a
theoretical perspective
, it is both mathematically and economically interesting to identify the precise conditions under which these two regimes occur. Finally, from a
computational perspective
, the existence of an optimizer is crucial: one can meaningfully attempt to compute or approximate solutions, rather than face an ill-posed problem in which utility diverges to
\mathcal{U}(\infty)
.

Literature Review

The literature on utility-risk portfolio selection spans several decades and covers many different settings. We focus on papers that study some form of market-independent well-posedness. For clarity, we organize the discussion into two strands: (i) one-period markets and (ii) multi-period and continuous-time models. Within each, we first review the unconstrained utility maximization problem (that is, with
\mathcal{R}\equiv 0
), before turning to the risk-constrained case.

As our main interest lies in one-period markets, this part of the review is more comprehensive, while the discussion of multi-period and continuous-time results is included for completeness.

One-Period Markets

Despite its practical relevance, the theory of utility-risk portfolio selection in one-period markets is surprisingly incomplete. To the best of our knowledge, the most general result concerning unconstrained utility maximization is
Föllmer and Schied 2016
(originally published in 2002), which shows that (
1.1
) admits a unique optimizer for
\mathcal{U}(Y)=\mathbb{E}[u(Y)]
when
u:\mathbb{R}\to[-\infty,\infty)
is continuous, strictly increasing, strictly concave, and either bounded above or satisfies
u(a)=-\infty
for some
a<0
. These conditions, however, are far from necessary and in particular exclude the non-concave
S
-shaped utility functions of
prospect theory
introduced by
Kahneman and Tversky 1979
.

Bernard and Ghossoub 2010
and
He and Zhou 2011a
study portfolio selection under
cumulative prospect theory
without a risk constraint in a one-period setting with one risk-free and one risky asset. Unlike ordinary prospect theory, cumulative prospect theory incorporates a
distortion function
that perturbs the probability measure by overweighting small probabilities, consistent with behavioural biases. In particular,
He and Zhou 2011a
introduce the notion of a
large-loss aversion degree
(LLAD) for strictly increasing and twice differentiable utilities
u
that are convex on
(-\infty,0)
and concave on
(0,\infty)
, defined as

k\coloneqq\lim_{y\to\infty}\frac{-u(-y)}{u(y)}\in[0,\infty],

whenever the limit exists. They use
k
to characterize well-posedness of their optimization problem. Up to a change in sign, LLAD coincides with the
asymptotic loss-gain ratio
, which we later identify as a fundamental structural quantity in our framework. Interpreting their results (namely, Theorem 2) in a setting without probability distortion (like ours) reveals that if
k=\infty
, then (
1.1
) is well-posed, while if
k<\infty
, then one can construct a market in which (
1.1
) becomes ill-posed. Their analysis, however, is restricted to a single risky asset.

In the risk-constrained setting, the only established existence results concern the well-studied case of
mean
-risk portfolio selection, i.e.,
\mathcal{U}(Y)=\mathbb{E}[Y]
with portfolio returns in place of terminal wealth. The seminal contribution is due to
Markowitz 1952
, who studied the problem

\sup_{\pi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\ \mathbb{E}[X_{\pi}]\hskip 10.00002pt\text{subject to}\hskip 10.00002pt\textnormal{Var}(X_{\pi})\leq\tilde{v}_{\max},

(1.2)

of maximizing expected excess return subject to a variance constraint. Here,
\pi
are portfolio weights in fractions of total wealth,
X_{\pi}
is the portfolio excess return, and
\tilde{v}_{\max}
the variance bound. Markowitz showed that (
1.2
) admits a unique closed-form solution. Equivalently, one may express (
1.2
) in terms of portfolios
\bar{\vartheta}
parameterized in numbers of shares:

\sup_{\bar{\vartheta}\in\mathbb{R}^{1+d}}\ \mathbb{E}[\bar{\vartheta}\cdot\bar{S}_{1}]\hskip 10.00002pt\text{subject to}\hskip 10.00002pt\begin{cases}\bar{\vartheta}\cdot\bar{S}_{0}=w,\\
\mathrm{Var}(\bar{\vartheta}\cdot\bar{S}_{1})\leq v_{\max},\end{cases}

(1.3)

with
v_{\max}=w^{2}\times\tilde{v}_{\max}
. This reformulation shows explicitly that mean-variance optimization fits naturally into the
(\mathcal{U},\mathcal{R})
framework.

Rockafellar et al. 2006
extended this setup by replacing the variance (more precisely, the standard deviation) with more flexible
generalized deviation measures
, such as the lower semideviation or mean absolute deviation. They established existence of an optimizer under mild regularity assumptions. Generalized deviations, however, are not risk functionals in our sense because they fail to be monotone: a position
X
may dominate
Y
almost surely yet be assigned greater deviation.

For this reason, a further strand of research has replaced the variance constraint with a risk functional
\mathcal{R}
, often taking the form of
Value at Risk
or
Expected Shortfall
; see, for example,
Campbell et al. 2001
;
Rockafellar and Uryasev 2002
;
Alexander and Baptista 2002
;
Ciliberti et al. 2007
and
Adam et al. 2008
, among many others.

If
\mathcal{R}
is
positively homogeneous
and
cash-invariant
, then the problem

\sup_{\pi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\ \mathbb{E}[X_{\pi}]\hskip 10.00002pt\text{subject to}\hskip 10.00002pt\mathcal{R}(X_{\pi})\leq\tilde{R}_{\max},

(1.4)

is equivalent to
(\mathcal{U},\mathcal{R})
-portfolio selection with
\mathcal{U}
given by the mean. In this setting,
Herdegen and Khan 2022
proved that (
1.4
), and hence (
1.1
) with
\mathcal{U}(Y)=\mathbb{E}[Y]
, is well-posed if and only if
\mathcal{R}
coincides with the
worst-case risk measure
. However, once positive homogeneity is dropped, this characterization no longer applies; cf.
Herdegen and Khan 2025
. Moreover, in general, the problem

\sup_{\pi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\ \mathcal{U}(X_{\pi})\hskip 10.00002pt\text{subject to}\hskip 10.00002pt\mathcal{R}(X_{\pi})\leq\tilde{R}_{\max},

(1.5)

is
not
equivalent to (
1.1
). Establishing such an equivalence requires additional care; cf. Lemma
2.11
below.

In summary, the results in the extant literature cover only particular cases: unconstrained utility maximization under restrictive assumptions, or risk-constrained problems in the special case of mean-risk optimization. A general theory that characterizes precisely when utility-risk portfolio selection admits solutions across all one-period markets has not been addressed in the literature before.

Multi-Period and Continuous-Time Markets

The theory is more complete in multi-period and continuous-time settings. Unconstrained utility maximization has been studied extensively, most notably by
Kramkov and Schachermayer 1999
and
Schachermayer 2001
in continuous time, and by
Rásonyi and Stettner 2005
in multiple periods.
Kramkov and Schachermayer 1999
and
Schachermayer 2001
showed that, under standard no-arbitrage assumptions, market-independent well-posedness is characterized by growth restrictions on the utility function, expressed via
asymptotic elasticity
. These conditions are both necessary and sufficient for the existence of an optimal portfolio. In discrete time,
Rásonyi and Stettner 2005
obtained related sufficiency results, providing growth bounds that ensure existence under no-arbitrage, though without a corresponding necessity result. For a more detailed review, see Remark
5.7
(b).

Portfolio selection under cumulative prospect theory without a risk constraint is also more developed in multi-period and continuous-time markets than in the one-period case. In complete continuous-time markets,
Jin and Zhou 2008
analyze optimal investment for behavioural agents with
S
-shaped preferences and probability distortion, identifying sufficient market-independent conditions for well-posedness and deriving near-explicit optimal terminal wealth distributions. In incomplete discrete-time multi-period markets,
Carassus and Rásonyi 2015
study the optimal investment problem under cumulative prospect theory and provide sufficient market-independent conditions for well-posedness. In both cases, however, necessity results are lacking.

Risk-constrained utility maximization has also been analyzed in greater detail than in the one-period framework. The first paper to directly embed risk management objectives into a utility-maximizing framework is due to
Basak and Shapiro 2001
who work in a continuous-time complete market with a
Value at Risk
constraint. They show in particular that the constraint may induce larger losses than in the unconstrained problem. A more theoretical analysis is provided by
Gundel and Weber 2008
, who investigate when the risk constraint is binding for expected utility maximizing agents in general continuous-time models under a
utility-based shortfall risk
constraint. Further works on risk-constrained utility maximization include
Gabih et al. 2009
;
He and Zhou 2011b
;
He et al. 2015
;
Wei 2018
, and more recently
Ghossoub and Zhu 2025
.

Overall, while multi-period and continuous-time utility-risk portfolio selection is now relatively well understood, the corresponding one-period theory remains fragmented. Our goal in this paper is to close this gap by identifying the exact market independent conditions on
\mathcal{U}
and
\mathcal{R}
that ensure the existence of an optimizer in (
1.1
).

Contribution

Our main result, Theorem
4.7
,
shows that under mild regularity assumptions on
\mathcal{U}
and
\mathcal{R}
, utility-risk portfolio selection is well-posed if
either the utility functional or the risk functional is
sensitive to large losses
, in the sense that

\mathbb{P}[Y<0]>0\ \Rightarrow\ \textnormal{either}\ \begin{cases}\mathcal{R}(\lambda Y)>0&\text{for all sufficiently large }\lambda>0,\\
\mathcal{U}(\lambda Y)<0&\text{for all sufficiently large }\lambda>0.\end{cases}

It is also a necessary condition: if both
\mathcal{U}
and
\mathcal{R}
fail to be sensitive to large losses, then there exists a market in which the supremum in (
1.1
) equals
\mathcal{U}(\infty)
and is not attained.

This result reveals a fundamental and previously unrecognized interplay: it is sufficient for
either
the utility
or
the risk functional to penalize large losses in order to ensure well-posedness, but if
both
fail to do so, the problem becomes ill-posed.

The property of sensitivity to large losses has been rigorously analyzed in
Herdegen et al. 2024
. They provide easily checkable criteria for a broad class of utility and risk functionals to decide whether or not they are sensitive to large losses. A key example is when the utility functional is given by expected utility,
\mathcal{U}(Y)=\mathbb{E}[u(Y)]
, where
u:\mathbb{R}\to[-\infty,\infty)
is increasing, normalized, upper semicontinuous, unbounded, and concave on
(0,\infty)
. In this case (see Theorem
5.3
), a sharp dichotomy arises based on the
asymptotic loss-gain ratio
:

\mathrm{ALG}(u)\coloneqq\limsup_{y\to\infty}\frac{u(-y)}{u(y)}\in[-\infty,0].

•

If
\mathrm{ALG}(u)=-\infty
or
\mathcal{R}
is sensitive to large losses, then (
1.1
) admits a maximizer, which is unique if
u
is strictly concave and
\mathcal{R}
is convex.

•

If
\mathrm{ALG}(u)>-\infty
and
\mathcal{R}
fails to be sensitive to large losses, then there exists a market for which the supremum in (
1.1
) equals
u(\infty)
and is unattainable.

The asymptotic loss-gain ratio
\mathrm{ALG}(u)
quantifies the investor’s asymptotic aversion to large losses relative to large gains. It is both intuitive and easy to compute. A value of
-\infty
corresponds to a situation where large losses are penalized far more heavily than large gains are rewarded. In contrast, finite values of
\mathrm{ALG}(u)
reflect a more symmetric treatment of tail outcomes, which may fail to sufficiently discourage downside risk.

Finally, we also consider the less common problem of
risk minimization under a utility constraint
, that is, minimizing
\mathcal{R}
subject to a fixed initial wealth and a minimum utility requirement. While it is more natural, from an economic standpoint, to treat utility as the objective and risk as a constraint, the reverse formulation arises in settings such as capital requirement reduction or liability-driven investment. This alternative perspective can be treated almost symmetrically within our utility-risk framework; see Section
4.3
for further discussion.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows.
Section
2
introduces the framework and formalizes market-independent (weak) well posedness. Section
3
analyzes weak well posedness, while Section
4
contains the main theoretical contributions, establishing minimal conditions for market-independent well posedness of utility-risk portfolio selection. Section
5
illustrates the scope of the results through examples, with a focus on expected utility. Section
6
concludes with directions for future research. Counterexamples highlighting the sharpness of our results are given in Appendix
A
, and all proofs are collected in Appendix
B
.

2
The Problem

Throughout the paper, we consider a one-period economy in which uncertainty about the terminal state of the world is modelled by an atomless probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
. Denote by
L^{1}
the space of integrable random variables, and by
L^{\infty}
the space of essentially bounded random variables. Let
L
be a Riesz space such that
L^{\infty}\subset L\subset L^{1}
and assume it is closed under
weak tail domination
, i.e., for every non-negative random variable
Y
, we have
Y\in L
whenever there exists
Z\in L
such that
\mathbb{P}[Y>y]\leq\mathbb{P}[Z>y]
for all
y\geq 0
. This implies in particular that a non-negative random variable
Y
lies in
L
if there is
Z\in L
with
Y\leq Z

\mathbb{P}
-a.s. This is a very mild condition that is satisfied by all
L^{p}
spaces for
p\in[1,\infty]
, and more generally, by all Orlicz spaces and Orlicz hearts.

Elements of
L
represent
payoffs
of financial positions at the terminal date, with the convention that positive values correspond to profits.

2.1
Utility and Risk

The reward of a financial position is captured by a
utility functional

\mathcal{U}
, while its riskiness is evaluated using a
risk functional

\mathcal{R}
. The numerical values assigned by these functionals are not directly important; only the induced ordering of positions, reflecting
preferences
, is relevant. We adopt an axiomatic framework. To that end, a functional
\mathcal{H}:L\to[-\infty,\infty]
is said to be:

•

normalized
if
\mathcal{H}(0)=0
;

•

decreasing
if
\mathcal{H}(Y)\geq\mathcal{H}(Z)
for all
Y,Z\in L
with
Y\leq Z

\mathbb{P}
-a.s.;

•

increasing
if
\mathcal{H}(Y)\leq\mathcal{H}(Z)
for all
Y,Z\in L
with
Y\leq Z

\mathbb{P}
-a.s.

Moreover, if
\mathcal{H}
is increasing or decreasing, then we set
\mathcal{H}(\infty)\coloneqq\lim\limits_{y\to\infty}\mathcal{H}(y)\in[-\infty,\infty]
.

Definition 2.1
.

A
utility functional
is any increasing functional
\mathcal{U}:L\to[-\infty,\infty)
that is normalized and satisfies
\mathcal{U}(Y)<\mathcal{U}(\infty)
for all
Y\in L
.
A
risk functional
is any decreasing functional
\mathcal{R}:L\to(-\infty,\infty]
that is normalized.

A few comments regarding the above definition are in order. The assumption that no position is assigned infinite utility or negative infinite risk is completely standard in the literature. The condition
\mathcal{U}(Y)<\mathcal{U}(\infty)
reflects non-satiation and expresses that no position
Y\in L
is as desirable as receiving unbounded deterministic wealth. It will play an important technical role in some of our proofs. By constrast, we do not impose the condition
\mathcal{R}(Y)>\mathcal{R}(\infty)
as this would exclude the important case of no risk constraint, i.e.,
\mathcal{R}=0
. Finally, the normalization assumption on
\mathcal{U}
and
\mathcal{R}
is for notational convenience and without loss of generality, provided that the utility and risk of the null position are finite. Indeed, given any functional
\tilde{\mathcal{H}}:L\to[-\infty,\infty]
with
\tilde{\mathcal{H}}(0)\in\mathbb{R}
, the normalized version defined by

\mathcal{H}(Y)\coloneqq\tilde{\mathcal{H}}(Y)-\tilde{\mathcal{H}}(0)

preserves the underlying preference structure: for any
Y,Z\in L
,
\tilde{\mathcal{H}}(Y)<\tilde{\mathcal{H}}(Z)
if and only if
\mathcal{H}(Y)<\mathcal{H}(Z)
.

Our definition of a utility and a risk functional requires minimal structure. For certain results, however, we require additional axioms. A functional
\mathcal{H}:L\to[-\infty,\infty]
satisfies:

•

positive homogeneity
if
\mathcal{H}(\lambda Y)=\lambda\mathcal{H}(Y)
for all
Y\in L
and
\lambda\in(0,\infty)
;

•

negative star-shapedness
if
\mathcal{H}(\lambda Y)\leq\lambda\mathcal{H}(Y)
for all
Y\in L
and
\lambda\in(1,\infty)
; or equivalently, if
\mathcal{H}(\lambda Y)\geq\lambda\mathcal{H}(Y)
for all
Y\in L
and
\lambda\in(0,1)
;

•

positive star-shapedness
if
\mathcal{H}(\lambda Y)\geq\lambda\mathcal{H}(Y)
for all
Y\in L
and
\lambda\in(1,\infty)
; or equivalently, if
\mathcal{H}(\lambda Y)\leq\lambda\mathcal{H}(Y)
for all
Y\in L
and
\lambda\in(0,1)
;

•

cash-additivity
if
\mathcal{H}(Y+c)=\mathcal{H}(Y)+\mathcal{H}(c)
for all
Y\in L
and
c\in\mathbb{R}
;

•

concavity
if
\mathcal{H}(\lambda Y+(1-\lambda)Z)\geq\lambda\mathcal{H}(Y)+(1-\lambda)\mathcal{H}(Z)
for all
Y,Z\in L
and
\lambda\in(0,1)
; it satisfies
strict concavity
if the inequality is strict whenever
\mathbb{P}[Y\neq Z]>0
;

•

convexity
if
\mathcal{H}(\lambda Y+(1-\lambda)Z)\leq\lambda\mathcal{H}(Y)+(1-\lambda)\mathcal{H}(Z)
for all
Y,Z\in L
and
\lambda\in(0,1)
; it satisfies
strict convexity
if the inequality is strict whenever
\mathbb{P}[Y\neq Z]>0
;

•

quasi-concavity
if
\mathcal{H}(\lambda Y+(1-\lambda)Z)\geq\min\{\mathcal{H}(Y),\mathcal{H}(Z)\}
for all
Y,Z\in L
and
\lambda\in(0,1)
; it satisfies
strict quasi-concavity
if the inequality is strict whenever
\mathbb{P}[Y\neq Z]>0
;

•

quasi-convexity
if
\mathcal{H}(\lambda Y+(1-\lambda)Z)\leq\max\{\mathcal{H}(Y),\mathcal{H}(Z)\}
for all
Y,Z\in L
and
\lambda\in(0,1)
; it satisfies
strict quasi-convexity
if the inequality is strict whenever
\mathbb{P}[Y\neq Z]>0
;

•

cash-concavity
if
\mathcal{H}(\lambda Y+(1-\lambda)c)\geq\lambda\mathcal{H}(Y)+(1-\lambda)\mathcal{H}(c)
for all
\lambda\in(0,1)
,
Y\in L
, and
c\in\mathbb{R}
;

•

cash-convexity
if
\mathcal{H}(\lambda Y+(1-\lambda)c)\leq\lambda\mathcal{H}(Y)+(1-\lambda)\mathcal{H}(c)
for all
\lambda\in(0,1)
,
Y\in L
, and
c\in\mathbb{R}
;

•

the upper Fatou property
if
\mathcal{H}(Y)\geq\limsup\limits_{n\to\infty}\mathcal{H}(Y_{n})
whenever
Y_{n}\to Y

\mathbb{P}
-a.s.,
Y_{n},Y\in L
, and
\lvert Y_{n}\rvert\leq Z

\mathbb{P}
-a.s. for some
Z\in L
;

•

the lower Fatou property
if
\mathcal{H}(Y)\leq\liminf\limits_{n\to\infty}\mathcal{H}(Y_{n})
whenever
Y_{n}\to Y

\mathbb{P}
-a.s.,
Y_{n},Y\in L
, and
\lvert Y_{n}\rvert\leq Z

\mathbb{P}
-a.s. for some
Z\in L
;

•

law-invariance
if
\mathcal{H}(Y)=\mathcal{H}(Z)
whenever
Y,Z\in L
have the same distribution.

Each axiom introduced above has a natural economic interpretation and plays a role in our analysis. Concavity of a utility functional reflects standard risk aversion, while convexity of a risk functional encodes preference for diversification. Both are foundational in utility and risk theory; cf.
Föllmer and Schied 2016
and the references therein. Quasi-concavity and quasi-convexity are weaker shape restrictions that still encode diversification. Negative star-shapedness and positive star-shapedness are also weaker conditions. They impose diminishing returns to scale, and are more behaviourally plausible than positive homogeneity, which assumes linear scaling. We refer to
Landsberger and Meilijson 1990
for examples of negatively star-shaped utility functionals and to
Castagnoli et al. 2022
for a recent presentation of positively star-shaped risk functionals.

Cash-concavity and cash-convexity, though less common in the literature, are weaker than full concavity or convexity, and imply star-shapedness by taking constants equal to zero. Cash-additivity generalizes the familiar cash-invariance property used in
coherent risk measures

Artzner et al. 1999
(see Proposition
B.1
), but is less common for utility functionals, except in specific frameworks such as Yaari’s dual theory
Yaari 1987
and variational preferences
Maccheroni et al. 2006
. Note that cash-additivity together with negative/positive star-shapedness implies cash-concavity/convexity; cf. Proposition
B.2
.

Finally, the upper and lower Fatou properties are mild regularity conditions that ensure stability under limits. In particular, the lower Fatou property is standard in the literature on risk functionals, where it is often referred to simply as the Fatou property. Law-invariance expresses the idea that only the distribution of outcomes matters, which is a standard assumption in models of preference. In certain settings, law-invariance implies the upper/lower Fatou property; see, for instance,
Jouini et al. 2006
.

Examples of utility functionals such as classical (von Neumann-Morgenstern) expected utility and
S
-shaped utilities, as well as risk functionals such as (loss) Value at Risk and (adjusted) Expected Shortfall are discussed in Section
5
.

2.2
Market Models

A one-period financial market on
L
with a risk-free asset can be represented by a price process
\bar{S}=(S^{0}_{t},\ldots,S^{d}_{t})_{t\in\{0,1\}}
, where
d\in\mathbb{N}
,
S^{i}_{0}\in(0,\infty)
denotes the initial price of asset
i
and
S^{i}_{1}\in L
denotes its random terminal price. We assume asset
0
to be
risk-free
so that
S^{0}_{1}=(1+r)S^{0}_{0}
, where
r\in(-1,\infty)
denotes the risk-free rate. As we are interested in portfolio selection, we may assume without loss of generality that all assets are
normalized
so that
S^{i}_{0}=1
for all
i
.

We will only consider market models
\bar{S}
that do not admit arbitrage; that is, there does not exist a portfolio
\bar{\vartheta}\in\mathbb{R}^{1+d}
, expressed in
numbers of shares
, such that

\bar{\vartheta}\cdot\bar{S}_{0}\leq 0,\hskip 10.00002pt\bar{\vartheta}\cdot\bar{S}_{1}\geq 0\;\mathbb{P}\textnormal{-a.s.}\hskip 10.00002pt\textnormal{and}\hskip 10.00002pt\mathbb{P}[\bar{\vartheta}\cdot\bar{S}_{1}>0]>0.

Indeed, if arbitrage opportunities were present, any rational agent would exploit them rather than engage in a constrained optimization problem.

As we assume that market models
\bar{S}
do not admit arbitrage, we may further assume without loss of generality that each market is
non-redundant
; in the sense that no risky asset can be synthesized by a linear combination of the other assets. This in turns gives that all the payoffs
S^{0}_{1},\ldots,S^{d}_{1}
are linearly independent as elements of the Riesz space
L
. We denote the collection of all normalized, arbitrage-free and non-redundant market models on
L
by
\mathbb{S}(L)
.

In order to study portfolio selection it is convenient to switch to excess returns. For a normalized market model
\bar{S}=(S^{0}_{t},\ldots,S^{d}_{t})_{t\in\{0,1\}}\in\mathbb{S}(L)
, we denote the corresponding
excess returns
by
X=(X^{1},\ldots,X^{d})
, where

X^{i}\coloneqq S^{i}_{1}-S^{0}_{1}\in L,\hskip 10.00002pti\in\{1,\ldots,d\}.

We denote the collection of all excess returns corresponding to
\mathbb{S}(L)
by
\mathbb{X}(L)
. Note that there exists a bijection between the sets
\mathbb{S}(L)
and
\mathbb{X}(L)\times(-1,\infty)
given by
\bar{S}\mapsto(X,r)
. For this reason and in a slight abuse of notation, we will refer to any element of
\mathbb{X}(L)
also as a market.

For a given market
X\in\mathbb{X}(L)
, we can describe a portfolio by a vector
\pi=(\pi^{1},\dots,\pi^{d})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, where
\pi^{i}
denotes the fraction of wealth invested in asset
i
, and the corresponding
excess return
portfolio is given by
X_{\pi}:=\pi\cdot X.

Note, that describing portfolio selection in terms of
\pi
and
X
is not fully equivalent to describing it in terms of
\bar{\vartheta}
and
\bar{S}
, as with
\pi
and
X
alone, we can neither recover the initial value
w
of the portfolio nor the interest rate
r
. However, it is easy to check that there exists a bijection between pairs
(\bar{\vartheta},\bar{S})
and quadruplets
(w,r,\pi,X)
. This bijection lies at the heart of our analysis.

Remark 2.2
.

The set
\mathbb{X}(L)
can be described also without referring first to
\mathbb{S}(L)
as the set of all excess return vectors
X=(X^{1},\ldots,X^{d})
— where
d\in\mathbb{N}
and
X^{i}\in L
— that are arbitrage-free in the sense that there does not exist
\pi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
with

\pi\cdot X\geq 0\;\mathbb{P}\textnormal{-a.s.}\hskip 10.00002pt\textnormal{and}\hskip 10.00002pt\mathbb{P}[\pi\cdot X>0]>0,

and non-redundant in the sense that
X^{1},\ldots,X^{d}
are linearly independent.

2.3
Optimization Problem

We consider an agent who trades in a a financial market and wants to maximize utility subject to a risk constraint. Let
\mathcal{U}
be a utility functional and
\mathcal{R}
be a risk functional. Given a market model
\bar{S}\in\mathbb{S}(L)
, an initial wealth
w\in(0,\infty)
and a maximal risk threshold
R_{\max}\in[\mathcal{R}(w(1+r)),\infty)
,
(\mathcal{U},\mathcal{R})
-portfolio selection is the optimization problem

\sup_{\bar{\vartheta}\in\mathbb{R}^{1+d}}\mathcal{U}(\bar{\vartheta}\cdot\bar{S}_{1})\hskip 10.00002pt\text{subject to}\hskip 10.00002pt\begin{cases}\bar{\vartheta}\cdot\bar{S}_{0}=w,\\
\mathcal{R}(\bar{\vartheta}\cdot\bar{S}_{1})\leq R_{\max}.\end{cases}

(2.1)

It is natural to take
R_{\max}\in[\mathcal{R}(w(1+r)),\infty)
, as this is the largest interval that guarantees the feasibility of the
riskless portfolio

\bar{\vartheta}=(w,0,\ldots,0)
, that allocates all wealth
w
to the riskless asset. Indeed, this portfolio has cost
w
at time
t=0
and risk
\mathcal{R}(w(1+r))
.

Remark 2.3
.

If the risk functional is trivial, i.e.,
\mathcal{R}\equiv 0
, then the risk constraint becomes vacuous. In this case, the portfolio selection problem (
2.1
) reduces to a utility maximization problem with no risk constraint,

\sup_{\bar{\vartheta}\in\mathbb{R}^{1+d}}\mathcal{U}(\bar{\vartheta}\cdot\bar{S}_{1})\hskip 10.00002pt\text{subject to}\hskip 10.00002pt\bar{\vartheta}\cdot\bar{S}_{0}=w.

Although
\bar{S}
satisfies the no-arbitrage condition, the optimization problem (
2.1
) need not admit a solution in general; see Example
A.1
for a counterexample. For this reason, it is important to study under which conditions it is
well-posed
, i.e., the problem admits an optimizer. To this end, it turns out to be helpful to first answer the question under which conditions (
2.1
) is
weakly well posed
in the sense that the supremum does not reach the absolute bliss point
\mathcal{U}(\infty)
.

Definition 2.4
.

(\mathcal{U},\mathcal{R})
-portfolio selection is
well posed for the market

\bar{S}\in\mathbb{S}(L)
if, for every initial wealth
w\in(0,\infty)
and risk threshold
R_{\max}\in[\mathcal{R}(w(1+r)),\infty)
, there exists at least one solution to (
2.1
).

Definition 2.5
.

(\mathcal{U},\mathcal{R})
-portfolio selection is
weakly well posed for the market

\bar{S}\in\mathbb{S}(L)
if, for every initial wealth
w\in(0,\infty)
and risk threshold
R_{\max}\in[\mathcal{R}(w(1+r)),\infty)
, the supremum in (
2.1
) is less than
\mathcal{U}(\infty)
.

It is immediate that well-posedness implies weak well-posedness. The converse, however, need not hold, even if
\mathcal{U}
satisfies the upper Fatou property and
\mathcal{R}
satisfies the lower Fatou property; see Example
A.2
.

Of particular interest is the
market-independent
version of the previous definitions.

Definition 2.6
.

(\mathcal{U},\mathcal{R})
-portfolio selection is
market-independent (weakly) well posed
if
(\mathcal{U},\mathcal{R})
-portfolio selection is (weakly) well-posed for every market
\bar{S}\in\mathbb{S}(L)
.

The key question that we want to answer is: What properties must
\mathcal{U}
and
\mathcal{R}
satisfy in order to ensure that
(\mathcal{U},\mathcal{R})
-portfolio selection is market-independent (weakly) well posed?

Remark 2.7
.

Note that while from an investor’s perspective it may be more natural to study the problem for a fixed market
\bar{S}\in\mathbb{S}(L)
, from a regulator’s perspective, the model-independent formulation is more relevant because they must specify the risk constraint
without
knowing the market model in advance.

2.4
Reparametrization of the Problem

In order to answer the question of (weak) well-posedness, it turns out to be helpful to to first reparametrize the optimisation problem (
2.1
) in terms of excess returns. To this end, let
X\in\mathbb{X}(L)
denote the excess returns corresponding to the market
\bar{S}
. For
w\in(0,\infty)
and
r\in(-1,\infty)
, define the transformed utility functional
\mathcal{U}_{w,r}:L\to[-\infty,\infty)
and transformed risk functional
\mathcal{R}_{w,r}:L\to(-\infty,\infty]
by

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}_{w,r}(Y)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\mathcal{U}(w(1+r)+wY)-\mathcal{U}(w(1+r)),

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{w,r}(Y)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\mathcal{R}(w(1+r)+wY)-\mathcal{R}(w(1+r)).

Note that these are well-defined as
\mathcal{U}(w(1+r))\in[0,\infty)
and
\mathcal{R}(w(1+r))\in(-\infty,0]
by normalization, monotonicity and co-domains of
\mathcal{U}
and
\mathcal{R}
. Both functionals
\mathcal{U}_{w,r}
and
\mathcal{R}_{w,r}
inherit the key structural properties of
\mathcal{U}
and
\mathcal{R}
, respectively. This is formalized in Proposition
B.3
in Appendix
B
. In particular,
\mathcal{U}_{w,r}
is a utility functional and
\mathcal{R}_{w,r}
is a risk functional.

Prior to the reparametrization of (
2.1
), it is useful to recall that
X_{\pi}\coloneqq\pi\cdot X
denotes the excess return of a portfolio
\pi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
parametrized in
fractions of wealth
. By Proposition
B.4
, the
(\mathcal{U},\mathcal{R})
-portfolio selection problem (
2.1
) is equivalent to the
(\mathcal{U}_{w,r},\mathcal{R}_{w,r})
-portfolio selection problem defined by

\sup_{\pi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathcal{U}_{w,r}(X_{\pi})\hskip 10.00002pt\text{subject to}\hskip 10.00002pt\mathcal{R}_{w,r}(X_{\pi})\leq\tilde{R}_{\max},

(2.2)

where
\tilde{R}_{\max}\coloneqq R_{\max}-\mathcal{R}(w(1+r))\in[0,\infty)
. Analogously to Definitions
2.4
and
2.6
, we now introduce the notions of (weak) well posedness for
(\mathcal{U}_{w,r},\mathcal{R}_{w,r})
-portfolio selection, both in a fixed market and in the market-independent setting.

Definition 2.8
.

Let
w\in(0,\infty)
and
r\in(-1,\infty)
. Then
(\mathcal{U}_{w,r},\mathcal{R}_{w,r})
-portfolio selection is
well posed for the market
X\in\mathbb{X}(L)
if, for every
\tilde{R}_{\max}\in[0,\infty)
, there exists at least one solution to the optimization problem (
2.2
).

Definition 2.9
.

Let
w\in(0,\infty)
and
r\in(-1,\infty)
. Then
(\mathcal{U}_{w,r},\mathcal{R}_{w,r})
-portfolio selection is
weakly well posed for the market
X\in\mathbb{X}(L)
if, for every
\tilde{R}_{\max}\in[0,\infty)
, the supremum in (
2.2
) is less than
\mathcal{U}_{w,r}(\infty)
.

Definition 2.10
.

Let
w\in(0,\infty)
and
r\in(-1,\infty)
. Then
(\mathcal{U}_{w,r},\mathcal{R}_{w,r})
-portfolio selection is
market-independent (weakly) well posed
if the optimization problem (
2.2
) is (weakly) well posed for every market
X\in\mathbb{X}(L)
.

The key observation is that market-independent (weak) well-posedness of
(\mathcal{U},\mathcal{R})
-portfolio selection is equivalent to market-independent (weak) well-posedness of
(\mathcal{U}_{w,r},\mathcal{R}_{w,r})
-portfolio selection
for every

w\in(0,\infty)
and
r\in(-1,\infty)
. This equivalence is formalized in the next lemma, which follows directly from Proposition
B.4
.

Lemma 2.11
.

The following are equivalent:

(a)

(\mathcal{U},\mathcal{R})
-portfolio selection is market-independent (weakly) well posed.

(b)

(\mathcal{U}_{w,r},\mathcal{R}_{w,r})
-portfolio selection is market-independent (weakly) well posed for all
w\in(0,\infty)
and
r\in(-1,\infty)
.

3
Weak Well-Posedness of
(\mathcal{U},\mathcal{R})
-Portfolio Selection

In this section, we seek to answer the question under which conditions the optimization problem (
2.1
) is market-independent weakly well posed. Inspired by Lemma
2.11
, we proceed in two steps. We first fix an initial wealth
w\in(0,\infty)
and an interest rate
r\in(-1,\infty)
and see what kind of properties on
\mathcal{U}_{w,r}
and
\mathcal{R}_{w,r}
are necessary and sufficient to guarantee that
(\mathcal{U}_{w,r},\mathcal{R}_{w,r})
-portfolio selection is market-independent weakly well posed. We then identify the corresponding conditions on the original functionals
\mathcal{U}
and
\mathcal{R}
that ensure that these properties are satisfied
uniformly
across all pairs
(w,r)
, thereby characterizing the market-independent weak well-posedness of
(\mathcal{U},\mathcal{R})
-portfolio selection.

3.1
Weak Well-Posedness of
(\mathcal{U}_{w,r},\mathcal{R}_{w,r})
-Portfolio Selection

Throughout this section, we fix an initial wealth
w\in(0,\infty)
, an interest rate
r\in(-1,\infty)
as well as a utility functional
\mathcal{U}_{w,r}
and a risk functional
\mathcal{R}_{w,r}
.

We begin our discussion by formulating the
negation
of weak well-posedness, which is easier to interpret.

Definition 3.1
.

The market
X\in\mathbb{X}(L)
admits
(\mathcal{U}_{w,r},\mathcal{R}_{w,r})
-arbitrage
if there exists a sequence
(\pi_{n})_{n\geq 1}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
of portfolios and a constant
\tilde{R}\in[0,\infty)
such that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathcal{U}_{w,r}(X_{\pi_{n}})=\mathcal{U}_{w,r}(\infty)\hskip 10.00002pt\textnormal{and}\hskip 10.00002pt\mathcal{R}_{w,r}(X_{\pi_{n}})\leq\tilde{R}\ \textnormal{ for all }n\in\mathbb{N}.

The notion of
(\mathcal{U}_{w,r},\mathcal{R}_{w,r})
-arbitrage
is inspired by the concept of
\rho
-arbitrage
; see
Herdegen and Khan 2025
. Indeed, if
\mathcal{U}_{w,r}
is the mean and
\tilde{R}=0
, then both definitions coincide. The terminology is motivated by the fact that when
\mathcal{U}_{w,r}
is the mean,
\mathcal{R}_{w,r}
the worst-case risk measure, and
\tilde{R}=0
, then
(\mathcal{U}_{w,r},\mathcal{R}_{w,r})
-arbitrage is equivalent to ordinary arbitrage. However, in general, the presence of
(\mathcal{U}_{w,r},\mathcal{R}_{w,r})
-arbitrage is not necessarily a sign of inefficiency of the market itself but rather indicates an inefficiency of the risk functional
\mathcal{R}_{w,r}
to properly capture risk for preferences described by the utility functional
\mathcal{U}_{w,r}
.

One can show that any sequence
(\pi_{n})_{n\geq 1}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
of portfolios generating
(\mathcal{U}_{w,r},\mathcal{R}_{w,r})
-arbitrage must necessarily be unbounded; see Proposition
B.5
. Together with Proposition
B.6
this implies that if
\mathcal{R}_{w,r}
satisfies the lower Fatou property and positive star-shapedness, and
X\in\mathbb{X}(L)
admits
(\mathcal{U}_{w,r},\mathcal{R}_{w,r})
-arbitrage, then there exists a non-zero portfolio
\pi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
whose risk remains non-positive under arbitrary scaling.

Proposition 3.2
.

Assume
\mathcal{R}_{w,r}
satisfies the lower Fatou property and positive star-shapedness. If
X\in\mathbb{X}(L)
admits
(\mathcal{U}_{w,r},\mathcal{R}_{w,r})
-arbitrage,
then there exists a portfolio
\pi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\}
such that

\mathcal{R}_{w,r}(\lambda X_{\pi})\leq 0,\text{ for all }\lambda>0.

Since any
X\in\mathbb{X}(L)
is arbitrage-free and non-redundant, it follows that
\mathbb{P}[X_{\pi}<0]>0
for every
\pi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\}
. Thus, Proposition
3.2
establishes a direct link between the existence of
(\mathcal{U}_{w,r},\mathcal{R}_{w,r})
-arbitrage and the notion of
sensitivity to large losses
. In what follows, we introduce an analogous version of this definition for utility functionals.

Definition 3.3
.

\mathcal{U}_{w,r}
(resp.
\mathcal{R}_{w,r}
) is
sensitive to large losses
if, for every
Y\in L
satisfying
\mathbb{P}[Y<0]>0
, there exists
\lambda_{Y}\in(0,\infty)
such that
\mathcal{U}_{w,r}(\lambda Y)<0
(resp.
\mathcal{R}_{w,r}(\lambda Y)>0
) for all
\lambda\in(\lambda_{Y},\infty)
.

In parallel to the concept of well-posedness, it turns out to also be useful to consider a weak version of the above notion.

Definition 3.4
.

\mathcal{U}_{w,r}
(resp.
\mathcal{R}_{w,r}
) is
weakly sensitive to large losses
if, for every
Y\in L
with
\mathbb{P}[Y<0]>0
,
\limsup\limits_{\lambda\to\infty}\mathcal{U}_{w,r}(\lambda Y)<\mathcal{U}_{w,r}(\infty)
(resp.
\liminf\limits_{\lambda\to\infty}\mathcal{R}_{w,r}(\lambda Y)>\mathcal{R}_{w,r}(\infty)
).

It is clear that sensitivity to large losses implies weak sensitivity to large losses, however, the converse is not always true, see Example
A.4
.

The concept of sensitivity to large losses was introduced and studied in detail in
Herdegen et al. 2024
. The financial interpretation is that although scaling magnifies gains, it also amplifies losses, and at some point, very large losses outweigh very large gains. The notion of weak sensitivity to large losses is a relaxation of the former property. It merely requires that the utility (resp. risk) of
\lambda Y
stays strictly below
\mathcal{U}_{w,r}(\infty)
(resp. strictly above
\mathcal{R}_{w,r}(\infty)
) as
\lambda\to\infty
, without necessarily crossing the zero threshold.

Examples of risk functionals that are sensitive to large losses include Loss Value at Risk and Adjusted Expected Shortfall; see Sections
5.2.1
and
5.2.2
, respectively. Moreover, expected utility for many choices of (concave or non-concave) utility functions is sensitive to large losses; see Section
5.1
.

It is clear from Proposition
3.2
that sensitivity to large losses is exactly what is required for
\mathcal{R}_{w,r}
to make
(\mathcal{U}_{w,r},\mathcal{R}_{w,r})
-portfolio selection weakly well posed. The following result shows that weak sensitivity to large losses for
\mathcal{U}_{w,r}
is an alternative condition.

Lemma 3.5
.

(\mathcal{U}_{w,r},\mathcal{R}_{w,r})
-portfolio selection is market-independent weakly well posed if one of the following conditions is satisfied:

(a)

\mathcal{U}_{w,r}
is weakly sensitive to large losses.

(b)

\mathcal{R}_{w,r}
satisfies the lower Fatou property, positive star-shapedness and sensitivity to large losses.

The converse of Lemma
3.5
does not hold in general; see Example
A.3
for a counterexample. However, under the additional assumption of law-invariance for either
\mathcal{U}_{w,r}
or
\mathcal{R}_{w,r}
, the converse
does
hold. Note that the result contains two either-or conditions that need to be satisfied either for the utility or the risk functional.

Theorem 3.6
.

Suppose
\mathcal{R}_{w,r}
satisfies positive star-shapedness, the lower Fatou property, and either
\mathcal{U}_{w,r}
or
\mathcal{R}_{w,r}
are law-invariant. Then the following statements are equivalent:

(a)

(\mathcal{U}_{w,r},\mathcal{R}_{w,r})
-portfolio selection is market-independent weakly well-posed.

(b)

Either
\mathcal{U}_{w,r}
is weakly sensitive to large losses or
\mathcal{R}_{w,r}
is sensitive to large losses.

3.2
Weak Well-Posedness of
(\mathcal{U},\mathcal{R})
-Portfolio Selection

We now return to the characterization of market-independent weak well-posedness of
(\mathcal{U},\mathcal{R})
-portfolio selection. First, for the convenience of the reader, we restate the notions of (weak) sensitivity to large losses for
\mathcal{U}
and
\mathcal{R}
.

Definition 3.7
.

\mathcal{U}
(resp.
\mathcal{R}
) is
sensitive to large losses
if, for every
Y\in L
with
\mathbb{P}[Y<0]>0
, there exists
\lambda_{Y}\in(0,\infty)
such that
\mathcal{U}(\lambda Y)<0
(resp.
\mathcal{R}(\lambda Y)>0)
) for all
\lambda\in(\lambda_{Y},\infty)
.

Definition 3.8
.

\mathcal{U}
(resp.
\mathcal{R}
) is
weakly sensitive to large losses
if, for every
Y\in L
with
\mathbb{P}[Y<0]>0
,
\limsup\limits_{\lambda\to\infty}\mathcal{U}(\lambda Y)<\mathcal{U}(\infty)
(resp.
\liminf\limits_{\lambda\to\infty}\mathcal{R}(\lambda Y)>\mathcal{R}(\infty)
).

It is shown in Proposition
B.3
that if
\mathcal{U}
(resp.
\mathcal{R}
) is (weakly) sensitive to large losses, then so is
\mathcal{U}_{w,r}
(resp.
\mathcal{R}_{w,r}
) for every
w\in(0,\infty)
and
r\in(-1,\infty)
. Similarly, if
\mathcal{U}
(resp.
\mathcal{R}
) fails to be weakly sensitive to large losses, then neither is
\mathcal{U}_{w,r}
(resp.
\mathcal{R}_{w,r}
) for any such
w
and
r
. Together with Proposition
B.11
, this implies that under cash-convexity,
\mathcal{R}
is sensitive to large losses if and only if
\mathcal{R}_{w,r}
is sensitive to large losses for every
w\in(0,\infty)
and
r\in(-1,\infty)
.

Recalling that by Lemma
2.11
,
(\mathcal{U},\mathcal{R})
-portfolio selection is market-independent weakly well posed if and only if
(\mathcal{U}_{w,r},\mathcal{R}_{w,r})
-portfolio selection is market-independent weakly well posed for all
w\in(0,\infty)
and
r\in(-1,\infty)
, we can now recast Theorem
3.6
for the original functionals
\mathcal{U}
and
\mathcal{R}
, provided that we can identify suitable conditions on
\mathcal{U}
and
\mathcal{R}
that ensure the transformed utility and risk functionals
\mathcal{U}_{w,r}
and
\mathcal{R}_{w,r}
satisfy the assumptions of Theorem
3.6
for each
w\in(0,\infty)
and
r\in(-1,\infty)
. While the Fatou property and law-invariance directly transfer from
\mathcal{U}
and
\mathcal{R}
to
\mathcal{U}_{w,r}
and
\mathcal{R}_{w,r}
we need to assume the stronger cash-convexity for
\mathcal{R}
to obtain star-shapedness for
\mathcal{R}_{w,r}
; see Proposition
B.3
for details.

With this we can now state the first main result of this paper. Note that it contains two either-or conditions, for either the utility or the risk functional.

Theorem 3.9
.

Suppose
\mathcal{R}
satisfies cash-convexity and the lower Fatou property, and either
\mathcal{U}
or
\mathcal{R}
are law-invariant. Then the following statements are equivalent:

(a)

(\mathcal{U},\mathcal{R})
-portfolio selection is market-independent weakly well-posed.

(b)

Either
\mathcal{U}
is weakly sensitive to large losses or
\mathcal{R}
is sensitive to large losses.

4
Well-Posedness of
(\mathcal{U},\mathcal{R})
-Portfolio Selection

We now return to the study of market-independent well-posedness. As in the case of weak well-posedness, we proceed in two steps, first fixing an initial budget
w\in(0,\infty)
and an interest rate
r\in(-1,\infty)
and characterizing the market-independent well-posedness of
(\mathcal{U}_{w,r},\mathcal{R}_{w,r})
-portfolio selection, before turning to the original functionals
\mathcal{U}
and
\mathcal{R}
.

4.1
Well-Posedness of
(\mathcal{U}_{w,r},\mathcal{R}_{w,r})
-Portfolio Selection

Throughout this section, we fix an initial wealth
w\in(0,\infty)
, an interest rate
r\in(-1,\infty)
as well as a utility functional
\mathcal{U}_{w,r}
and a risk functional
\mathcal{R}_{w,r}
.

Having developed the theory of market-independent weak well-posedness of
(\mathcal{U}_{w,r},\mathcal{R}_{w,r})
portfolio selection, we now focus on the market-independent well-posedness. Notice that if
\mathcal{U}_{w,r}
satisfies the upper Fatou property,
\mathcal{R}_{w,r}
satisfies the lower Fatou property, the market
X\in\mathbb{X}(L)
does not admit
(\mathcal{U}_{w,r},\mathcal{R}_{w,r})
-arbitrage, and the maximizing sequence in Equation (
2.2
) is
bounded
for every
\tilde{R}_{\max}\in[0,\infty)
, then
(\mathcal{U}_{w,r},\mathcal{R}_{w,r})
portfolio selection is well-posed for
X
. It turns out that this boundedness is guaranteed so long as either
\mathcal{U}_{w,r}
is sensitive to large losses, or
\mathcal{R}_{w,r}
is sensitive to large losses and positively star-shaped.

Proposition 4.1
.

Assume
\mathcal{U}_{w,r}
satisfies the upper Fatou property and
\mathcal{R}_{w,r}
satisfies the lower Fatou property. If either
\mathcal{U}_{w,r}
is sensitive to large losses, or
\mathcal{R}_{w,r}
is positively star-shaped and sensitive to large losses, then
(\mathcal{U}_{w,r},\mathcal{R}_{w,r})
-portfolio selection is market independent well-posed.

We now want to combine the previous result with Theorem
3.6
. To this end, we need to deal with the issue that Theorem
3.6
requires
\mathcal{U}_{w,r}
to be weakly sensitive to large losses, whereas Proposition
4.1
requires
\mathcal{U}_{w,r}
to be sensitive to large losses. This motivates the following definition.

Definition 4.2
.

\mathcal{U}_{w,r}
(resp.
\mathcal{R}_{w,r}
) is
sensitivity equivalent
if, it is weakly sensitive to large losses if and only if it is sensitive to large losses.

It turns out that the class of sensitivity equivalent utility functionals is very broad, including, for example, all cash-additive or cash-concave functionals; see Proposition
B.11
. Moreover, it encompasses a wide range of (concave and non-concave) expected utility functionals (see Proposition
5.2
).

By employing the notion of sensitivity equivalence and imposing the upper Fatou property on
\mathcal{U}_{w,r}
, we obtain a
complete
description of market-independent well-posedness for
(\mathcal{U}_{w,r},\mathcal{R}_{w,r})
portfolio selection. Moreover, under these slightly stronger assumptions, market-independent well-posedness coincides with market-independent weak well-posedness.

Theorem 4.3
.

Assume
\mathcal{U}_{w,r}
satisfies the upper Fatou property and sensitivity equivalence,
\mathcal{R}_{w,r}
satisfies the lower Fatou property and positive star-shapedness, and either
\mathcal{U}_{w,r}
or
\mathcal{R}_{w,r}
is law-invariant. Then the following statements are equivalent:

(a)

(\mathcal{U}_{w,r},\mathcal{R}_{w,r})
-portfolio selection is market-independent well-posed.

(b)

(\mathcal{U}_{w,r},\mathcal{R}_{w,r})
-portfolio selection is market-independent weakly well-posed.

(c)

Either
\mathcal{U}_{w,r}
or
\mathcal{R}_{w,r}
is sensitive to large losses.

We note an important corollary for the case that the utility functional is the mean, which satisfies the upper Fatou property and law-invariance but is not weakly sensitive to large losses.

Corollary 4.4
.

Assume
\mathcal{U}_{w,r}(Y)=\mathbb{E}[Y]
. Let
\mathcal{R}_{w,r}
be a risk functional that is positively star-shaped and satisfies the lower Fatou property. Then
(\mathcal{U}_{w,r},\mathcal{R}_{w,r})
-portfolio selection is market-independent (weakly) well posed if and only if
\mathcal{R}_{w,r}
is sensitive to large losses.

Remark 4.5
.

Corollary
4.4
becomes particularly transparent once the subscripts
w
and
r
are omitted. It shows that if
\mathcal{R}
is a positively star-shaped risk functional satisfying the lower Fatou property, then the “Markowitz-type” optimization problem

\sup_{\pi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\,\mathbb{E}[X_{\pi}]\hskip 10.00002pt\textnormal{subject to}\hskip 10.00002pt\mathcal{R}(X_{\pi})\leq\tilde{R}_{\max},

admits a solution for every
\tilde{R}_{\max}\in[0,\infty)
and every market
X\in\mathbb{X}(L)
if and only if
\mathcal{R}
is sensitive to large losses.

4.2
Well-Posedness of
(\mathcal{U},\mathcal{R})
-Portfolio Selection

We now return to the characterization of market-independent well-posedness of
(\mathcal{U},\mathcal{R})
-portfolio selection. First, for the convenience of the reader, we restate the notion of sensitivity equivalence for
\mathcal{U}
, which we also formulate for
\mathcal{R}
.

Definition 4.6
.

\mathcal{U}
(resp.
\mathcal{R}
) is
sensitivity equivalent
if, it is weakly sensitive to large losses if and only if it is sensitive to large losses.

It is shown in Proposition
B.3
that if
\mathcal{U}
(resp.
\mathcal{R}
) is sensitive to large losses, then so is
\mathcal{U}_{w,r}
(resp.
\mathcal{R}_{w,r}
) for every
w\in(0,\infty)
and
r\in(-1,\infty)
. Conversely, if
\mathcal{U}
(resp.
\mathcal{R}
) fails to be weakly sensitive to large losses, then neither is
\mathcal{U}_{w,r}
(resp.
\mathcal{R}_{w,r}
) for any such
w
and
r
. In particular, if
\mathcal{U}
(resp.
\mathcal{R}
) is sensitivity equivalent, then
\mathcal{U}
(resp.
\mathcal{R}
) is sensitive to large losses if and only if
\mathcal{U}_{w,r}
(resp.
\mathcal{R}_{w,r}
) is sensitive to large losses for every
w\in(0,\infty)
and
r\in(-1,\infty)
.

Recalling that by Lemma
2.11
,
(\mathcal{U},\mathcal{R})
-portfolio selection is market-independent well posed if and only if
(\mathcal{U}_{w,r},\mathcal{R}_{w,r})
-portfolio selection is market-independent well posed for each
w\in(0,\infty)
and
r\in(-1,\infty)
, we can now recast Theorem
4.3
for the original functionals
\mathcal{U}
and
\mathcal{R}
, provided that we can identify suitable conditions on
\mathcal{U}
and
\mathcal{R}
that ensure that the transformed utility and risk functionals
\mathcal{U}_{w,r}
and
\mathcal{R}_{w,r}
satisfy the assumptions of Theorem
4.3
for each
w\in(0,\infty)
and
r\in(-1,\infty)
. While the Fatou property, law-invariance and sensitivity equivalence transfer directly from
\mathcal{U}
and
\mathcal{R}
to
\mathcal{U}_{w,r}
and
\mathcal{R}_{w,r}
we need to assume the stronger cash-convexity for
\mathcal{R}
to obtain star-shapedness for
\mathcal{R}_{w,r}
; see Proposition
B.3
for details.

With this we can now state the main result of this paper.

Theorem 4.7
.

Assume
\mathcal{U}
satisfies the upper Fatou property and is sensitivity equivalent, and
\mathcal{R}
satisfies the lower Fatou property and cash-convexity. Suppose either
\mathcal{U}
or
\mathcal{R}
is law-invariant. Then the following statements are equivalent:

(a)

(\mathcal{U},\mathcal{R})
-portfolio selection is market-independent well-posed.

(b)

(\mathcal{U},\mathcal{R})
-portfolio selection is market-independent weakly well-posed.

(c)

Either
\mathcal{U}
is sensitive to large losses or
\mathcal{R}
is sensitive to large losses.

Moreover, if one of the above condition holds,
\mathcal{U}
is in addition strictly quasi-concave and
\mathcal{R}
is quasi-convex, then for every market
\bar{S}\in\mathbb{S}(L)
, initial wealth
w\in(0,\infty)
and risk threshold
R_{\max}\in[\mathcal{R}(w(1+r)),\infty)
, the optimization problem (
2.1
) admits a unique maximizer.

Theorem
4.7
provides a sharp and general criterion for market-independent well-posedness of utility-risk portfolio selection: well-posedness holds if and only if at least one of the utility or risk functionals is sensitive to large losses. This result highlights a fundamental trade-off. Either the investor’s preferences must exhibit sufficient aversion to downside risk, or the imposed risk constraint must be strong enough to curb excessive risk-taking. The criterion is minimal and robust, relying only on sensitivity to large losses and mild regularity properties.

4.3
Risk Minimization

In most practical investment problems, the agent seeks to maximize utility while remaining within acceptable risk levels, as in the
(\mathcal{U},\mathcal{R})
portfolio selection problem. However, in certain settings, it is more appropriate to consider problems in which the investor directly
minimizes risk
, subject to a
target utility
constraint. Although the literature on risk minimization is relatively limited compared to that on utility maximization, several notable contributions exist.

Rockafellar et al. 2002
developed an abstract scheme for determining optimality in problems of minimizing deviation or risk.
Ruszczyński and Shapiro 2006
address the problem in a broader setting, establishing conditions under which the minimization of a convex risk functionals is well-posed, and
Gianin and Mastrogiacomo 2015
studied the problem for quasiconvex risk functionals. More recently,
Embrechts et al. 2022
investigate a
robust
risk minimization framework, showing in particular that Value at Risk (VaR) is not robust in the context of risk minimization, thereby questioning its suitability as a risk constraint. Finally,
Guedes et al. 2023
solve the minimum portfolio risk optimization problem for seven different risk functionals using linear programming under cardinality constraints.

We proceed to show how our results translate to the setting of risk minimization. To this end, let
\mathcal{R}:L\to(-\infty,\infty]
be a decreasing, normalized functional such that
\mathcal{R}(Y)>\mathcal{R}(-\infty)
for all
Y\in L
, and
\mathcal{U}:L\to[-\infty,\infty)
an increasing, normalized functional. Given a market model
\bar{S}\in\mathbb{S}(L)
, an initial wealth
w\in(0,\infty)
and a minimal utility level
U_{\min}\geq\mathcal{U}(w(1+r))
,
(\mathcal{R},\mathcal{U})
portfolio selection is the optimization problem

\inf_{\bar{\vartheta}\in\mathbb{R}^{1+d}}\mathcal{R}(\bar{\vartheta}\cdot\bar{S}_{1})\hskip 10.00002pt\text{subject to}\hskip 10.00002pt\begin{cases}\bar{\vartheta}\cdot\bar{S}_{0}=w,\\
\mathcal{U}(\bar{\vartheta}\cdot\bar{S}_{1})\geq U_{\min}.\end{cases}

(4.1)

In principle our theory carries over by considering the utility functional
-\mathcal{R}
and the risk functional
-\mathcal{U}
. A closer look, however, reveals that the symmetry is not perfect: in utility maximization we make the assumption
\mathcal{R}_{\max}\geq\mathcal{R}(w(1+r))
on the range of the risk constraint, whereas in risk minimization, we make the assumption
U_{\min}\geq\mathcal{U}(w(1+r))
(rather than the symmetric
U_{\min}\leq\mathcal{U}(w(1+r))
) on the range of the utility constraint. While this is perfectly reasonable from a financial perspective, it brings the new problem that if the utility constraint is chosen too high, no solution to (
4.1
) might exist for the sole reason that there does not exist any portfolio satisfying the utility constraint. Of course, this has nothing to do with well-posedness in the proper sense.

In order to deal with this issue, we therefore need to consider a slight modification of the analogues to Definition
2.6
for
(\mathcal{R},\mathcal{U})
portfolio selection.

Definition 4.8
.

(\mathcal{R},\mathcal{U})
portfolio selection is market-independent (weakly) well-posed if, for every market
\bar{S}\in\mathbb{S}(L)
, initial wealth
w\in(0,\infty)
and utility target
U_{\min}\geq\mathcal{U}(w(1+r))
such that

\{\bar{\vartheta}\in\mathbb{R}^{1+d}:\bar{\vartheta}\cdot\bar{S}_{0}=w\ \textnormal{and}\ \mathcal{U}(\bar{\vartheta}\cdot\bar{S}_{1})\geq U_{\min}\}\neq\emptyset,

the optimization problem (
4.1
) admits at least one solution (the infimum in (
4.1
) is not
\mathcal{R}(\infty)
).

With this modified definition in place, we now obtain the following analogue of Theorem
4.7
.

Theorem 4.9
.

Assume
\mathcal{R}
satisfies the lower Fatou property and is sensitivity equivalent, and
\mathcal{U}
satisfies the upper Fatou property and cash-concavity. Suppose either
\mathcal{U}
or
\mathcal{R}
is law-invariant. Then the following statements are equivalent:

(a)

(\mathcal{R},\mathcal{U})
-portfolio selection is market-independent well-posed.

(b)

(\mathcal{R},\mathcal{U})
-portfolio selection is market-independent weakly well-posed.

(c)

Either
\mathcal{U}
is sensitive to large losses or
\mathcal{R}
is sensitive to large losses.

Moreover, if one of the above conditions holds,
\mathcal{R}
is in addition strictly quasi-convex, and
\mathcal{U}
is quasi-concave, then for every market
\bar{S}\in\mathbb{S}(L)
, initial wealth
w\in(0,\infty)
and target utility
U_{\min}\geq\mathcal{U}(w(1+r))
with

\{\bar{\vartheta}\in\mathbb{R}^{1+d}:\bar{\vartheta}\cdot\bar{S}_{0}=w\ \textnormal{and}\ \mathcal{U}(\bar{\vartheta}\cdot\bar{S}_{1})\geq U_{\min}\}\neq\emptyset,

(
4.1
) admits a unique minimizer.

5
Examples

We now specialize to the case where
\mathcal{U}
is an
expected utility
functional, and identify concrete conditions on the corresponding
utility function
that ensure
\mathcal{U}
is sensitivity equivalent and sensitive to large losses. These structural properties, combined with key features of widely used risk functionals, allow us to characterize precisely when risk constrained expected utility maximization is market-independent well-posed. The results are summarized succinctly in Table
2
.

5.1
Expected Utility

A utility function
u
captures an agent’s preferences over monetary outcomes.

Definition 5.1
.

A function
u:\mathbb{R}\to[-\infty,\infty)
is called a
utility function
if it is increasing,
u(0)=0
,
u(\infty)\coloneqq\lim_{y\to\infty}u(y)>u(z)
for all
z\in\mathbb{R}
and
\limsup_{y\to\infty}u(y)/y<\infty
.

It is natural to assume that
u
is increasing, reflecting a preference for more wealth, and normalized so that
u(0)=0
. The non-satiation condition,
u(\infty)>u(y)
for all
y\in\mathbb{R}
, means that utility grows strictly at infinity, i.e., no finite outcome is ever as desirable as becoming arbitrarily wealthy. Finally, the asymptotic condition
\limsup_{x\to\infty}u(y)/y<\infty
ensures that
u
grows at most linearly at infinity, thereby ruling out excessively risk-seeking behaviour. This is a weaker condition than concavity and allows for a broad class of preferences. In particular, our definition encompasses classical concave (von Neumann–Morgenstern) utility functions, the negatively star-shaped utilities studied by
Landsberger and Meilijson 1990
, and the
S
-shaped utilities central to prospect theory in
Kahneman and Tversky 1979
. Thus, our framework is flexible enough to accommodate both traditional and nontraditional models of preferences.

For a utility function
u
, the
expected utility
of
Y\in L
is given by

\mathcal{E}_{u}(Y)\coloneqq\mathbb{E}[u(Y)].

This is a utility functional. The asymptotic property
\limsup_{y\to\infty}u(y)/y<\infty
implies that there exists
a,y_{0}\in(0,\infty)
such that
u(y)\leq ay
for all
y\in(y_{0},\infty)
, and hence ensures that the expectation
\mathbb{E}[u(Y)]\in[-\infty,\infty)
is well-defined.

If
u
is upper semicontinuous, then the expected utility functional
\mathcal{E}_{u}
satisfies the upper Fatou property (see Proposition
B.12
) and if
u
is negatively star-shaped on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
and unbounded, then
\mathcal{E}_{u}
is sensitivity equivalent.

Proposition 5.2
.

If
u
is an unbounded utility function that is negatively star-shaped (e.g. concave) on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
, then
\mathcal{E}_{u}
is sensitivity equivalent.

However, when
u
is bounded,
\mathcal{E}_{u}
may fail to be sensitivity equivalent; cf. Example
A.4
. Additionally,
\mathcal{E}_{u}
is always law-invariant.

The only remaining ingredient needed to apply Theorem
4.7
is a characterization of when
\mathcal{E}_{u}
is sensitive to large losses. This is precisely the content of
Herdegen et al. 2024
, which we reproduce for convenience: for any utility function
u
that is negatively star-shaped on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
, the functional
\mathcal{E}_{u}
is sensitive to large losses if and only if
\limsup_{y\to\infty}u(-y)/u(y)=-\infty
. We refer to this quantity as the
asymptotic loss-gain ratio
of
u
, defined by

\mathrm{ALG}(u)\coloneqq\limsup_{y\to\infty}\frac{u(-y)}{u(y)}\in[-\infty,0].

This ratio measures the degree to which large losses are penalized relative to large gains. It allows us to determine whether
\mathcal{E}_{u}
is sensitive to large losses. Applying Theorem
4.7
now yields the following.

Theorem 5.3
.

Let
u
be an unbounded, upper semicontinuous utility function that is negatively star-shaped (e.g. concave) on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
. Assume
\mathcal{R}
is cash-convex and has the lower Fatou property.

(a)

If
\textnormal{ALG}(u)=-\infty
or
\mathcal{R}
is sensitive to large losses, then for any market
\bar{S}\in\mathbb{S}(L)
, initial wealth
w\in(0,\infty)
and maximal risk threshold
\mathcal{R}_{\max}\in[\mathcal{R}(w(1+r)),\infty)
, there exists a maximizer to the risk constrained expected-utility-maximization problem

\sup_{\bar{\vartheta}\in\mathbb{R}^{1+d}}\mathcal{U}(\bar{\vartheta}\cdot\bar{S}_{1})\hskip 10.00002pt\text{subject to}\hskip 10.00002pt\begin{cases}\bar{\vartheta}\cdot\bar{S}_{0}=w,\\
\mathcal{R}(\bar{\vartheta}\cdot\bar{S}_{1})\leq R_{\max}.\end{cases}

Moreover, there exists at most one maximizer if
u
is strictly concave and
\mathcal{R}
is quasi-convex.

(b)

If
\textnormal{ALG}(u)>-\infty
and
\mathcal{R}
is not sensitive to large losses, then there exists a market
\bar{S}\in\mathbb{S}(L)
such that for any initial wealth
w\in(0,\infty)
, one can construct a sequence of portfolios
(\bar{\vartheta}_{n})_{n\geq 1}\subset\mathbb{R}^{1+d}
satisfying

\mathbb{E}[u(\bar{\vartheta}_{n}\cdot\bar{S}_{1})]\to u(\infty)\hskip 10.00002pt\textnormal{and}\hskip 10.00002pt\begin{cases}\bar{\vartheta}_{n}\cdot\bar{S}_{0}=w,\\
\mathcal{R}(\bar{\vartheta}_{n}\cdot\bar{S}_{1})\leq\mathcal{R}(w(1+r)),\end{cases}\textnormal{for all }n.

Theorem
5.3
provides a precise criterion for market-independent well-posedness of risk-constrained expected utility maximization: either the utility function must exhibit strong aversion to losses (captured by
\mathrm{ALG}(u)=-\infty
), or the risk functional must be sufficiently responsive to downside risk (i.e., sensitive to large losses). This result highlights a fundamental interplay between preferences and risk constraints: either may compensate for deficiencies in the other to ensure existence of an optimizer. In particular, it allows us to characterize exactly when unconstrained expected utility maximization is market-independent well-posed.

Corollary 5.4
.

Let
u
be an unbounded, upper semicontinuous utility function that is negatively star-shaped (e.g. concave) on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
.

(a)

If
\mathrm{ALG}(u)=-\infty
, then for any market
\bar{S}\in\mathbb{S}(L)
and initial wealth
w\in(0,\infty)
, there exists a maximizer to

\sup_{\bar{\vartheta}\in\mathbb{R}^{1+d}}\mathbb{E}[u(\bar{\vartheta}\cdot\bar{S}_{1})]\hskip 10.00002pt\text{subject to}\hskip 10.00002pt\bar{\vartheta}\cdot\bar{S}_{0}=w.

Moreover, there exists at most one maximizer if
u
is strictly concave.

(b)

If
\textnormal{ALG}(u)>-\infty
, then there exists a market
\bar{S}\in\mathbb{S}(L)
such that for any initial wealth
w\in(0,\infty)
, one can construct a sequence of portfolios
(\bar{\vartheta}_{n})_{n\geq 1}\subset\mathbb{R}^{1+d}
satisfying

\mathbb{E}[u(\bar{\vartheta}_{n}\cdot\bar{S}_{1})]\to u(\infty)\hskip 10.00002pt\textnormal{and}\hskip 10.00002pt\bar{\vartheta}_{n}\cdot\bar{S}_{0}=w,\ \textnormal{for all }n.

A few remarks are in order.

Remark 5.5
.

Although we focus on expected utility, the analysis extends to other utility functionals, such as the
classical certainty equivalent

\mathcal{C}_{u}(X):=u^{-1}(\mathbb{E}[u(X)]),

and Bühlmann’s
u
-mean certainty equivalent

(
Bühlmann 1970
)
; see
Herdegen et al. 2024
. Notably,
\mathcal{C}_{u}
is (strictly) quasi-concave whenever
u
is (strictly) concave. However, to maintain clarity and highlight the central message of the paper, we restrict attention to expected utility due to its foundational role in decision theory.

Remark 5.6
.

Examples of unbounded, upper semicontinuous utility functions that are negatively star-shaped on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
and satisfy
\mathrm{ALG}(u)=-\infty
include:

•

the exponential utility
u(y)=1-e^{-ay}
for
a>0
;

•

the power utility

u(y)=\begin{cases}\frac{y^{\gamma}}{\gamma},&\text{if }y\geq 0,\\
-\infty,&\text{if }y<0,\end{cases}

where
\gamma\in(0,1)
;

•

the
S
-shaped utility

u(y)=\begin{cases}y^{\alpha},&\text{if }y\geq 0,\\
-(-y)^{\beta},&\text{if }y<0,\end{cases}

(5.1)

for parameters
0<\alpha<\beta<1
.

In contrast, unbounded, upper semicontinuous utility functions that are negatively star-shaped on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
and satisfy
\mathrm{ALG}(u)>-\infty
include:

•

the linear utility function
u(y)=ay
for
a>0
;

•

the
S
-shaped utility (
5.1
) with parameters satisfying
0<\beta\leq\alpha<1
;

•

any utility function that is unbounded from above but bounded from below.

Remark 5.7
.

(a) Corollary
5.4
generalizes earlier results in the literature. For instance,
Föllmer and Schied 2016
(first published in 2002) show existence and uniqueness under the assumptions that
u
is continuous, strictly increasing, strictly concave, and either bounded above or identically
-\infty
on
(-\infty,a)
for some
a<0
; in both cases
\mathrm{ALG}(u)=-\infty
. Likewise,
He and Zhou 2011a
establish that in a market with one risky and one riskless asset, and for twice differentiable utilities that are convex on
(-\infty,0)
and concave on
(0,\infty)
, a maximizer exists if and only if the
large-loss aversion degree

k\coloneqq\lim_{y\to\infty}\frac{-u(-y)}{u(y)},

is infinite; whenever
k
exists, it coincides with
-\mathrm{ALG}(u)
.

(b) The dichotomy in Corollary
5.4
is conceptually related to the market-independent well posedness results of
Kramkov and Schachermayer 1999
and
Schachermayer 2001
. In continuous time semimartingale markets, for a utility function
u
that is strictly increasing, strictly concave, continuously differentiable on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
, satisfies the Inada conditions and takes the value
-\infty
on
\mathbb{R}_{-}
,
Kramkov and Schachermayer 1999
showed that the
asymptotic elasticity
condition

\mathrm{AE}(u)\coloneqq\limsup_{y\to\infty}\frac{yu^{\prime}(y)}{u(y)}<1

is necessary and sufficient for the existence and uniqueness of an optimizer.
Schachermayer 2001
extended this to utilities on the entire real line, proving that
reasonable asymptotic elasticity
,

\mathrm{AE}_{+\infty}(u)\coloneqq\limsup_{y\to\infty}\frac{yu^{\prime}(y)}{u(y)}<1\hskip 10.00002pt\textnormal{and}\hskip 10.00002pt\mathrm{AE}_{-\infty}(u)\coloneqq\liminf_{y\to-\infty}\frac{yu^{\prime}(y)}{u(y)}>1,

is necessary and sufficient provided the value function lies strictly below
u(\infty)
.

In discrete time,
Rásonyi and Stettner 2005
obtained the most general results to date in multi-period models. For increasing, concave utilities, they established existence of an optimizer under
either
of the following growth conditions:

•

There exist constants
\tilde{x}>0
and
\gamma\in(0,1)
such that for all
x\geq\tilde{x}
and
\lambda\geq 1
, one has
u(\lambda x)\leq\lambda^{\gamma}u(x)
; (when
u
is continuously differentiable, this is equivalent to
\mathrm{AE}_{+\infty}(u)<1

(
Kramkov and Schachermayer 1999
, Lemma 6.3)
).

•

There exist constants
\tilde{x}\leq 0
and
\alpha>0
such that for all
x\leq\tilde{x}
and
\lambda\geq 1
, one has
u(\lambda x)\leq\lambda^{1+\alpha}u(x)
; (when
u
is continuously differentiable, this is equivalent to
\mathrm{AE}_{-\infty}(u)>1
by
Frittelli and Gianin 2004
).

However, their results are not necessary and assume finiteness of the value function, which can fail for unbounded
u
.

5.2
Risk Functionals

Theorem
5.3
shows that irrespective of the risk functional
\mathcal{R}
, the condition
\mathrm{ALG}(u)=-\infty
guarantees market-independent well-posedness of
(\mathcal{E}_{u},\mathcal{R})
-portfolio selection. We now turn to the complementary regime where
\mathrm{ALG}(u)>-\infty
, and aim to identify risk functionals
\mathcal{R}
whose sensitivity to large losses is sufficient to
counterbalance
the weak loss aversion of
\mathcal{E}_{u}
.

5.2.1
Loss Value at Risk

The first class of risk functionals we consider is a generalization of
Value at Risk
(VaR), one of the most widely used risk functionals. For
Y\in L^{1}
and a confidence level
\alpha\in[0,1)
, the Value at Risk of
Y
at level
\alpha
is defined as

\textnormal{VaR}^{\alpha}(Y)\coloneqq\begin{cases}\inf\{m\in\mathbb{R}:\mathbb{P}[m+Y<0]\leq\alpha\},&\text{if }\alpha\in(0,1),\\
\esssup(-Y),&\text{if }\alpha=0.\end{cases}

While simple and intuitive, VaR completely ignores the behaviour of losses beyond the reference quantile. To address this limitation,
Bignozzi et al. 2020
introduced the
Loss Value at Risk
(LVaR), which incorporates information about the entire left tail of the loss distribution through a benchmark function.

Let
\beta:(-\infty,0]\to[0,1)
be an increasing function, interpreted as a
threshold distribution for acceptability
. The Loss Value at Risk of a position
Y\in L^{1}
with benchmark loss distribution
\beta
is defined by

\mathrm{LVaR}^{\beta}(Y)\coloneqq\sup_{\ell\in(-\infty,0]}\left\{\mathrm{VaR}^{\beta(\ell)}(Y)+\ell\right\}.

This is a risk functional that is cash-convex and satisfies the lower Fatou property. Moreover,
\mathrm{LVaR}^{\beta}
is sensitive to large losses if and only if
\inf_{\ell\in(-\infty,0]}\beta(\ell)=0
, by
Herdegen et al. 2024
. In particular, if
\alpha\in(0,1)
and
\beta\equiv\alpha
, then
\mathrm{LVaR}^{\beta}\equiv\mathrm{VaR}^{\alpha}
is not sensitive to large losses.

5.2.2
Adjusted Expected Shortfall

This class of risk functionals is a generalization of
Expected Shortfall
(ES), which is currently endorsed by the Basel framework. For
Y\in L^{1}
and a confidence level
\alpha\in(0,1]
, the Expected Shortfall of
Y
at level
\alpha
is defined as

\textnormal{ES}^{\alpha}(Y)\coloneqq\begin{cases}\frac{1}{\alpha}\int_{0}^{\alpha}\textnormal{VaR}^{u}(Y)\,\mathrm{d}u,&\text{if $\alpha\in(0,1)$,}\\
\mathbb{E}[-Y],&\text{if }\alpha=1.\end{cases}

While ES improves upon VaR by accounting for the average loss beyond the
\alpha
-quantile, it does not differentiate between losses with identical means but different tail behaviours. To address this limitation,
Burzoni et al. 2022
introduced a new class of risk functionals that refine ES by incorporating tail sensitivity.

Let
g:(0,1]\to[0,\infty]
be a decreasing function with
g(1)=0
, called a
risk profile
. The
adjusted Expected Shortfall
of
Y\in L^{1}
with respect to
g
is defined as

\mathrm{ES}^{g}(Y):=\sup_{\alpha\in(0,1]}\{\mathrm{ES}^{\alpha}(Y)-g(\alpha)\}.

This is a (cash-)convex risk functional that satisfies the lower Fatou property. By
Herdegen et al. 2024
,
\mathrm{ES}^{g}
is sensitive to large losses if and only if
g
is finite everywhere. In particular, if
\alpha\in(0,1)
,
g=\infty
on
(0,\alpha)
and
g=0
on
[\alpha,1]
, then
\mathrm{ES}^{g}\equiv\mathrm{ES}^{\alpha}
is not sensitive to large losses.

5.2.3
Risk Functionals Based on Loss Functions

The remaining examples are based around the theme of
loss functions
, which mirror utility functions but in reverse. Whereas utility functions measure satisfaction from gains, loss functions quantify the
disutility
from losses.

Definition 5.8
.

A function
\ell:\mathbb{R}\to(-\infty,\infty]
is called a
loss function
if it is increasing,
\ell(0)=0
, and
\limsup_{y\to-\infty}\ell(y)/y<\infty
.

It is natural to require that
\ell
is increasing because more severe losses should never incur a smaller penalty. The normalization
\ell(0)=0
fixes the baseline, ensuring that the absence of loss carries no penalty. The asymptotic condition
\limsup_{y\to-\infty}\ell(y)/y<\infty
imposes a restriction on the left tail. As outcomes become arbitrarily favorable (that is, as
y\to-\infty
), the loss function cannot decrease faster than linearly. This is a weaker condition than convexity. Finally, we define the asymptotic loss-gain ratio of
\ell
as

\mathrm{ALG}(\ell)\coloneqq\limsup_{y\to\infty}\frac{\ell(y)}{\ell(-y)}\in[-\infty,0],

so that if
u
is a utility function, then
\ell\coloneqq-u
is a loss function with
\mathrm{ALG}(\ell)=\mathrm{ALG}(u)
.

Expected Weighted Loss

The
expected weighted loss
of a position
Y\in L^{1}

with respect to
\ell
is defined by

\textnormal{EW}^{\ell}(Y)\coloneqq\mathbb{E}[\ell(-Y)].

The expected weighted loss
\textnormal{EW}^{\ell}
is a risk functional that satisfies: the lower Fatou property, if
\ell
is lower semicontinuous; (cash-)convexity, if
\ell
is convex; and sensitivity to large losses if and only if
\mathrm{ALG}(\ell)=-\infty
, provided that
\ell
is positively star-shaped (e.g. convex) on
\mathbb{R}_{-}
, by
Herdegen et al. 2024
.

Shortfall Risk Measures

Shortfall risk measures were first introduced as a case study in
L^{\infty}
by
Föllmer and Schied 2002
. Here, we work instead on the space
L^{1}
. Let
\ell:\mathbb{R}\to(-\infty,\infty]
be a loss function and define the associated
acceptance set

\mathcal{A}_{\ell}:=\{Y\in L^{1}:\textnormal{EW}^{\ell}(Y)\leq 0\},

where
\textnormal{EW}^{\ell}(Y)\coloneqq\mathbb{E}[\ell(-Y)]
denotes the expected weighted loss with respect to
\ell
. The
shortfall risk measure associated with
\ell
is then the functional
\textnormal{SR}^{\ell}:L^{1}\to(-\infty,\infty]
defined by

\textnormal{SR}^{\ell}(Y):=\inf\{m\in\mathbb{R}:Y+m\in\mathcal{A}_{\ell}\}=\inf\{m\in\mathbb{R}:\textnormal{EW}^{\ell}(Y+m)\leq 0\}.

It can be interpreted as the cash-additive analogue of
\textnormal{EW}^{\ell}
, in the sense that it is cash-additive and
\textnormal{SR}^{\ell}(Y)\leq 0
if and only if
\textnormal{EW}^{\ell}(Y)\leq 0
.

The shortfall risk measure
\textnormal{SR}^{\ell}
is a risk functional that satisfies: the lower Fatou property, if
\ell
is lower semicontinuous; and cash-convexity, if
\ell
is positively star-shaped. Moreover, by
Herdegen et al. 2024
, if
\ell(y)>0
for every
y\in(0,\infty)
and
\ell
is positively star-shaped on
\mathbb{R}_{-}
, then
\mathcal{R}_{\ell}
is sensitive to large losses if and only if
\mathrm{ALG}(\ell)=-\infty
.

OCE Risk Measures

Optimised Certainty Equivalents (OCEs) were first introduced in
Ben-Tal and Teboulle 1986
and later linked to risk measures on
L^{\infty}
in
Ben-Tal and Teboulle 2007
. We follow
Cheridito and Li 2009
. Let
\ell
be a real-valued convex loss function with
\ell(y)\geq y
for all
y\in\mathbb{R}
and define the OCE risk measure associated with
\ell
as the map

\mathrm{OCE}^{\ell}(Y):=\inf_{\eta\in\mathbb{R}}\left\{\mathbb{E}[\ell(\eta-Y)]-\eta\right\},\hskip 10.00002ptY\in H^{\Phi_{\ell}},

where
H^{\Phi_{\ell}}
is the Orlicz heart corresponding to the
Young function

\Phi_{\ell}:=\ell|_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}

H^{\Phi_{\ell}}\coloneqq\{Y\in L^{\Phi}:\mathbb{E}[\Phi_{\ell}(c|Y|)]<\infty\textnormal{ for all }c>0\}.

By
Cheridito and Li 2009
,
\mathrm{OCE}^{\ell}
is a (cash-)convex risk functional that satisfies the lower Fatou property. Moreover, it is sensitive to large losses if and only if
\liminf_{y\to\infty}\ell(y)/y=\infty
and
\limsup_{y\to-\infty}\ell(y)/y=0
, by
Herdegen et al. 2024
. This condition implies
\mathrm{ALG}(\ell)=-\infty
but is not equivalent; cf.
Herdegen et al. 2024
.

Conclusion and Outlook

In this paper, we have developed a new perspective on the interplay between utility and risk in one-period portfolio selection. For a utility functional
\mathcal{U}
and a risk functional
\mathcal{R}
, we showed that
(\mathcal{U},\mathcal{R})
-portfolio selection is well posed in
every
arbitrage-free market provided that at least one of the two functionals satisfies the axiom of
sensitivity to large losses
. This reveals that, despite their different roles,
\mathcal{U}
and
\mathcal{R}
are in a precise sense two sides of the same coin. Furthermore, if either
\mathcal{U}
or
\mathcal{R}
is law-invariant, then sensitivity to large losses is not only sufficient but also necessary for market-independent well posedness; see Theorem
4.7
.

The classical case of unconstrained expected-utility maximization, encompassing both traditional concave and non-concave
S
-shaped utilities, emerges as a special case of our framework. In this setting, well posedness is governed by whether the
asymptotic loss-gain ratio
of
u
is infinite; see Corollary
5.4
.

A key strength of our theory is that it yields
simple, verifiable
necessary and sufficient conditions for market-independent well posedness in one-period models, under only mild assumptions on the utility and risk functionals. This stands in contrast to much of the existing literature, where well-posedness typically requires strong regularity assumptions on the utility function or restrictive conditions on the market models.

Our results also open several avenues for further exploration, particularly on the computational and numerical side. For instance:

•

Consider the
S
-shaped utility

u(y)=\begin{cases}y^{\alpha},&y\geq 0,\\
-(-y)^{\beta},&y<0,\end{cases}

which is sensitive to large losses if and only if
0<\alpha<\beta<1
. In this case, one-period expected-utility maximization is always well posed. What do the corresponding optimal portfolios look like under elliptical or empirical return distributions? How tractable are they computationally?

•

The risk functional
L^{1}\ni Y\mapsto\mathbb{E}[Y^{-}]
is sensitive to large losses, implying that the
mean-shortfall
problem

\sup_{\pi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\ \mathbb{E}[X_{\pi}]\hskip 10.00002pt\text{subject to}\hskip 10.00002pt\mathbb{E}[X_{\pi}^{-}]\leq R_{\max}

is well posed in every arbitrage-free market (see Remark
4.5
). How does this compare, both theoretically and empirically, to classical mean-variance portfolio selection?

Beyond computational questions, the problem of identifying
market-dependent
conditions for well-posedness of
(\mathcal{U},\mathcal{R})
-portfolio selection remains open; that is, for a given utility/risk pair, exactly which markets admit a well-posed optimization problem? In the important special case where
\mathcal{U}
is the mean and
\mathcal{R}
is Expected Shortfall at level
\alpha
, well-posedness holds for every market that admits an
equivalent martingale measure
whose Radon-Nikodým derivative is bounded above in
L^{\infty}
by
1/\alpha
(see the discussion in the Introduction and
Herdegen and Khan 2022
). We plan to address the general market-dependent case separately, as it is technically more challenging and less easily verified.

Another promising direction for future research is to extend our characterization of well-posedness to the setting of
robust utility
, where preferences are evaluated under model uncertainty by considering the worst-case expected utility over a set of plausible probability measures. Prior works, such as
Nutz 2016
and
Meireles-Rodrigues and Rásonyi 2021
, study robust utility maximization in discrete time, while
Gundel and Weber 2007
also incorporate additional risk constraints.

Example A.1
.

This example shows that
(\mathcal{U},\mathcal{R})
-portfolio selection can be ill-posed. We take
\mathcal{U}
to be the mean and quantify risk
\mathcal{R}
via Value at Risk at first, and then Expected Shortfall for some level
\alpha\in(0,1)
(both are defined in Section
5.2
).

Consider the market
\bar{S}=(S^{0}_{t},\ldots,S^{d}_{t})_{t\in\{0,1\}}
where
S^{0}_{0}=1
,
S^{0}_{1}=1+r
for some
r\in(-1,\infty)
,
S^{i}_{0}\in(0,\infty)
and
S^{i}_{1}=S^{i}_{0}+R^{i}
for
i\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
, where the return vector
R\coloneqq(R^{1},\ldots,R^{d})
has a multivariate Gaussian distribution with mean vector
\mu\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\{r\mathbf{1}\}
and positive definite covariance matrix
\Sigma\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
. Define the
maximal Sharpe ratio
as

\textnormal{SR}_{\max}\coloneqq\sqrt{(\mu-r\mathbf{1})^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}(\mu-r\mathbf{1})}\in(0,\infty).

•

Let
L=L^{1}
,
\mathcal{U}\equiv\mathbb{E}
,
\mathcal{R}\equiv\mathrm{VaR}^{\alpha}
for some
\alpha\in(0,1)
, and note that
\bar{S}\in\mathbb{S}(L)
. Let
Z
be a standard Gaussian random variable with mean
0
and variance
1
. If
\textnormal{SR}_{\max}\geq\mathrm{VaR}^{\alpha}(Z)
, then by
Herdegen and Khan 2022
, there exists a sequence of portfolios parameterized in fractions of total wealth
(\pi_{n})_{n\geq 1}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}[\pi_{n}\cdot(R-r)]=\infty\hskip 10.00002pt\textnormal{and}\hskip 10.00002pt\mathrm{VaR}^{\alpha}(\pi_{n}\cdot(R-r))\leq 0\ \textnormal{for all }n.

Fix
w\in(0,\infty)
,
R_{\max}\in[\mathcal{R}(w(1+r)),\infty)
and define the corresponding sequence of portfolios parameterized in numbers of shares
\bar{\vartheta}_{n}=(\vartheta^{0}_{n},\ldots,\vartheta^{d}_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{1+d}
by
\vartheta^{i}_{n}\coloneqq w\pi^{i}_{n}/S^{i}_{0}
for

and
\vartheta^{0}_{n}\coloneqq w(1-\pi^{1}-\ldots-\pi^{d})
. Then,
\bar{\vartheta}_{n}\cdot\bar{S}_{0}=w
and
\bar{\vartheta}_{n}\cdot\bar{S}_{1}=w(1+r)+w\pi_{n}\cdot(R-r)\ \mathbb{P}
-a.s.
Whence, (
2.1
) is ill-posed for the market
\bar{S}
, because

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}[\bar{\vartheta}_{n}\cdot\bar{S}_{1}]=\infty\hskip 10.00002pt\textnormal{and}\hskip 10.00002pt\mathrm{VaR}^{\alpha}(\bar{\vartheta}_{n}\cdot\bar{S}_{1})\leq R_{\max}\ \textnormal{for all }n.

•

Let
L=L^{1}
,
\mathcal{U}\equiv\mathbb{E}
and
\mathcal{R}\equiv\mathrm{ES}^{\alpha}
for some
\alpha\in(0,1)
. Let
Z
be a standard Gaussian random variable with mean
0
and variance
1
. If
\textnormal{SR}_{\max}\geq\mathrm{ES}^{\alpha}(Z)
, then by arguing as above, the
(\mathcal{U},\mathcal{R})
portfolio selection problem (
2.1
) is not well-posed for the market
\bar{S}
.

Example A.2
.

In this example we construct a utility functional
\mathcal{U}
that satisfies the upper Fatou property, a risk functional
\mathcal{R}
that satisfies the lower Fatou property and a market
\bar{S}\in\mathbb{S}(L)
such that
(\mathcal{U},\mathcal{R})
-portfolio selection is weakly well posed for
\bar{S}
but not well posed for
\bar{S}
.

Let
L=L^{1}
. Define the utility functional
\mathcal{U}:L\to\mathbb{R}
by

\mathcal{U}(Y)=\begin{cases}\essinf Y,&\text{if }Y\geq 0\ \mathbb{P}\text{-a.s.,}\\
f(\mathbb{E}[Y]),&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

where
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is an increasing upper semicontinuous function with
f(a)>0
for some
a>0
, and for all
y\in\mathbb{R}
,
f(y)\leq y
and
f(y)<f(\infty)\coloneqq\lim_{z\to\infty}f(z)<\infty
. For example, one can take
f(y)=1-\exp(-y)
for
y\in\mathbb{R}
. Let
\mathcal{R}\equiv 0
. Note that
\mathcal{U}
satisfies the upper Fatou property,
\mathcal{U}(\infty)=\infty
and
\mathcal{R}
satisfies the lower Fatou property. Let
\bar{S}=(S^{0}_{t},S^{1}_{t})_{t\in\{0,1\}}
where
S^{0}_{0}=S^{1}_{0}=1
,
S^{1}_{0}=1
and
S^{1}_{1}=1+X^{1}
. Assume
X^{1}\in L
is unbounded from below and
\mathbb{E}[X^{1}]>0
so
\bar{S}\in\mathbb{S}(L)
.

Now for
w\in(0,\infty)
and
R_{\max}\in[0,\infty)
, the optimization problem (
2.1
) is equivalent to

\sup_{\vartheta^{1}\in\mathbb{R}}\mathcal{U}(w+\vartheta^{1}X^{1}),

where
\vartheta^{1}
and
w-\vartheta^{1}
are the number of shares invested in the risky and riskless asset, respectively. Since
f
is increasing,
\mathbb{E}[X^{1}]>0
and there exists
a>0
such that
f(a)>0
, any maximizing sequence
(\vartheta^{1}_{n})_{n\geq 1}\subset\mathbb{R}
to this problem must satisfy
\lim_{n\to\infty}\vartheta^{1}_{n}=\infty
. Thus,
(\mathcal{U},\mathcal{R})
-portfolio selection is not well-posed for the market
\bar{S}
as the supremum
f(\infty)
is not attained. However,
(\mathcal{U},\mathcal{R})
-portfolio selection is weakly well posed for
\bar{S}
since
f(\infty)<\mathcal{U}(\infty)
.

Example A.3
.

This example shows that there exists a positively star-shaped risk functional
\mathcal{R}_{w,r}
that satisfies the lower Fatou property but is not sensitive to large losses, and a utility functional
\mathcal{U}_{w,r}
that is not weakly sensitive to large losses, for which
(\mathcal{U}_{w,r},\mathcal{R}_{w,r})
-portfolio selection is market-independent weakly well posed.

For notational simplicity, we omit the subscripts
w
and
r
in what follows.

Set
\Omega=[0,1]
, with the Borel
\sigma
-algebra and the Lebesgue measure
\mathbb{P}
. Let
A=[0,1/2]
and
L=L^{\infty}
. Define the functionals
\mathcal{U}:L\to\mathbb{R}
and
\mathcal{R}:L\to(-\infty,\infty]
by

\mathcal{U}(Y)=\essinf(Y\mathds{1}_{A})\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002pt\mathcal{R}(Y)=\begin{cases}0,&\text{if }Y\mathds{1}_{A^{c}}\geq 0\ \mathbb{P}\text{-a.s.},\\
\infty,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Note that
\mathcal{R}
is a positively star-shaped risk functional that satisfies the lower Fatou property but is not sensitive to large losses, and
\mathcal{U}
is a utility functional that is not weakly sensitive to large losses. We claim that there is no market in
\mathbb{X}(L)
that admits
(\mathcal{U},\mathcal{R})
-arbitrage. Indeed, if the market
X\in\mathbb{X}(L)
does admit
(\mathcal{U},\mathcal{R})
-arbitrage, then there would exist a sequence of portfolios
(\pi_{n})_{n\geq 1}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
and a constant
\tilde{R}\in[0,\infty)
such that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathcal{U}(X_{\pi_{n}})=\infty\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002pt\mathcal{R}(X_{\pi_{n}})\leq\tilde{R}\ \textnormal{for all }n.

But this would mean that for each
n
,
X_{\pi_{n}}\geq 0\ \mathbb{P}
-a.s. and
\mathbb{P}[X_{\pi_{n}}>0]>0
, in contradiction to the fact that the market
X
is arbitrage-free in the classical sense.

Example A.4
.

In this example we show that the expected utility functional
\mathcal{E}_{u}
(defined in Section
5.1
) may not be sensitivity equivalent if the utility function
u
is bounded.

Let
u:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be defined by

u(y)=\begin{cases}1-\mathrm{e}^{-y},&\text{if }y\geq 0,\\
\mathrm{e}^{y}-1,&\text{if }y<0.\end{cases}

Then
\mathcal{E}_{u}
is weakly sensitive to large losses. Indeed, if
Y\in L
and
\mathbb{P}[Y<0]>0
, then

u(\lambda Y)\xrightarrow{\mathbb{P}\textnormal{-a.s.}}Z\coloneqq\begin{cases}1,&\{Y>0\},\\
0,&\{Y=0\},\\
-1,&\{Y<0\},\end{cases}

and by the Dominated Convergence Theorem,

\lim_{\lambda\to\infty}\mathcal{E}_{u}(\lambda Y)=\mathbb{E}[Z]=\mathbb{P}[Y>0]-\mathbb{P}[Y<0]<1=\mathcal{E}_{u}(\infty).

However,
\mathcal{E}_{u}
is not sensitive to large losses since the limit can remain positive. For instance, if the gain region dominates the loss region, that is
\mathbb{P}[Y>0]>\mathbb{P}[Y<0]
, then

\lim_{\lambda\to\infty}\mathcal{E}_{u}(\lambda Y)>0.

Proposition B.1
.

If
\mathcal{R}:L\to(-\infty,\infty]
is a cash-additive risk functional, then for all
Y\in L
and
c\in\mathbb{R}
,
\mathcal{R}(Y+c)=\mathcal{R}(Y)+\alpha c
, where
\alpha\coloneqq\mathcal{R}(1)\in(-\infty,0]
.

Proof.

First note that
\mathcal{R}(c)\in\mathbb{R}
for all
c\in\mathbb{R}
. Indeed, if
c\geq 0
, then by monotonicity, normalization and co-domain of
\mathcal{R}
,
\mathcal{R}(c)\in(-\infty,0]
. If
c<0
and
\mathcal{R}(c)=\infty
, then by cash-additivity, normalization and the fact
\mathcal{R}(-c)\in\mathbb{R}
, we have
0=\mathcal{R}(c-c)=\mathcal{R}(c)+\mathcal{R}(-c)=\infty
, which is absurd. Thus, the function
\mathbb{R}\ni c\mapsto\mathcal{R}(c)
is real-valued, monotone and additive. Cauchy’s functional equation with monotonicity implies that
\mathcal{R}(c)=\mathcal{R}(1)c
. The result then follows by plugging this linear form back into cash-additivity.
∎

Proposition B.2
.

If
\mathcal{H}:L\to[-\infty,\infty]
is cash-additive and negatively/positively star-shaped, then it is cash-concave/convex.

Proof.

Let
Y\in L
,
c\in\mathbb{R}
and
\lambda\in(0,1)
. If
\mathcal{H}
is cash-additive and negatively star-shaped, then

\mathcal{H}(\lambda Y+(1-\lambda)c)=\mathcal{H}(\lambda Y)+\mathcal{H}((1-\lambda)c)\leq\lambda\mathcal{H}(Y)+(1-\lambda)\mathcal{H}(c),

where the equality is by cash-additivity and the inequality follows from negative star-shapedness. Thus,
\mathcal{H}
is cash-concave. Similarly, if
\mathcal{H}
is cash-additive and positively star-shaped, then it satisfies cash-convexity.
∎

Proposition B.3
.

Let
\mathcal{U}
be a utility functional,
w\in(0,\infty)
and
r\in(-1,\infty)
. Then,
\mathcal{U}_{w,r}
is a utility functional. Moreover:

(1)

If
\mathcal{U}
has the upper Fatou property, then so does
\mathcal{U}_{w,r}
.

(2)

If
\mathcal{U}
is law-invariant, then so is
\mathcal{U}_{w,r}
.

(3)

If
\mathcal{U}
is cash-additive, then so is
\mathcal{U}_{w,r}
.

(4)

If
\mathcal{U}
is concave, then so is
\mathcal{U}_{w,r}
.

(5)

If
\mathcal{U}
is cash-concave, then
\mathcal{U}_{w,r}
is negatively star-shaped.

(6)

If
\mathcal{U}
is (weakly) sensitive to large losses, then so is
\mathcal{U}_{w,r}
.

(7)

If
\mathcal{U}
is not weakly sensitive to large losses, then neither is
\mathcal{U}_{w,r}
.

(8)

If
\mathcal{U}
is sensitivity equivalent, then so is
\mathcal{U}_{w,r}
.

Proof.

It is not difficult to verify
\mathcal{U}_{w,r}
is a utility functional. Moreover, we omit the proofs of statements (1)–(4) as well as statement (7), because they are elementary. That leaves statements (5), (6) and (8) whose proofs are given below.

(5) Assume

is cash-concave. Let

,

(6) Assume

The argument to show that

(8) Suppose

is weakly sensitive to large losses. Then, by (7),

Proposition B.4
.

(with inverse

\mathcal{U}_{w,r}
is sensitive to large losses, the argument in Proposition
B.7
(which we do not repeat) implies the existence of
Y\in L
with
\mathbb{P}[Y<0]>0
and a sequence
(\lambda_{n})_{n\geq 1}\subset\mathbb{R}
with
\lambda_{n}\to\infty
such that
\lambda_{n}Y\geq X_{\pi_{n}}\ \mathbb{P}
-a.s., for all
n\geq 1
. By monotonicity this implies

for all

eventually since

is a maximizing sequence. But this contradicts our assumption that

Therefore, the maximizing sequence

must be bounded. By restricting to a subsequence and relabelling, we may assume that this sequence converges to a limit
\pi^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. It follows from the upper Fatou property of
\mathcal{U}_{w,r}
and the lower Fatou property of
\mathcal{R}_{w,r}
that
\pi^{*}
is a maximizer to (
2.2
). Therefore,
(\mathcal{U}_{w,r},\mathcal{R}_{w,r})
-portfolio selection is market-independent well-posed.
∎

Proposition B.11
.

Let
\mathcal{U}
be a utility functional. If
\mathcal{U}
is cash-additive or cash-concave, then
\mathcal{U}
is sensitivity equivalent.

Proof.

To show sensitivity equivalence, it suffices to show that if
\mathcal{U}
is not sensitive to large losses, then it is not weakly sensitive to large losses.

Suppose
\mathcal{U}
is cash-additive. Then,
\mathcal{U}(\infty)=\infty
and
\mathcal{U}(1)\in(0,\infty)
by Proposition
B.1
and the fact
\mathcal{U}(Y)<\mathcal{U}(\infty)
for all
Y\in L
. Assume there exists
Y\in L
with
\mathbb{P}[Y<0]>0
and a sequence
(\lambda_{n})\subset\mathbb{R}
with
\lambda_{n}\to\infty
such that
\mathcal{U}(\lambda_{n}Y)\geq 0
for all
n
. Fix
\varepsilon>0
so that
\mathbb{P}[Y+\varepsilon<0]>0
and let
Z\coloneqq Y+\varepsilon
. Then,
\mathbb{P}[Z<0]>0
and by Proposition
B.1
,

\mathcal{U}(\lambda_{n}Z)=\mathcal{U}(\lambda_{n}Y+\lambda_{n}\varepsilon)=\mathcal{U}(\lambda_{n}Y)+\mathcal{U}(1)\lambda_{n}\varepsilon\to\infty.

Whence,
\mathcal{U}
is sensitivity equivalent.

Now suppose
\mathcal{U}
is cash-concave, and hence, negatively star-shaped. Assume there exists
Y\in L
with
\mathbb{P}[Y<0]>0
and a sequence
(\lambda_{n})\subset\mathbb{R}
with
\lambda_{n}\to\infty
such that
\mathcal{U}(\lambda_{n}Y)\geq 0
for all
n
. By negative star-shapedness of
\mathcal{U}
,
\mathcal{U}(\lambda Y)\geq 0
for all
\lambda\in(0,\infty)
. Let
\varepsilon>0
be such that
\mathbb{P}[Y+\varepsilon<0]>0
and set
Z\coloneqq Y+\varepsilon
. Then, by the cash-concavity and monotonicity of
\mathcal{U}
, for any
\eta\in(0,1)
,

\mathcal{U}(\lambda Z)=\mathcal{U}(\eta\lambda\varepsilon+(1-\eta)(\tfrac{\lambda}{1-\eta}Y+\lambda\varepsilon))\geq\eta\mathcal{U}(\lambda\varepsilon)+(1-\eta)\mathcal{U}(\tfrac{\lambda}{1-\eta}Y)\geq\eta\mathcal{U}(\lambda\varepsilon).

Therefore,
\lim_{\lambda\to\infty}\mathcal{U}(\lambda Z)\geq\lim_{\eta\to 1}\eta\mathcal{U}(\infty)=\mathcal{U}(\infty)
. Whence,
\mathcal{U}
is sensitivity equivalent.
∎

. That (a) implies (b) is trivial. Finally (b) implies (c) as a consequence of Theorem

Proposition B.12
.

If
u
is an upper semicontinuous utility function, then
\mathcal{E}_{u}
satisfies the upper Fatou property.

Proof.

Assume
(Y_{n})_{n\geq 1}\subset L
,
Y\in L
,
Y_{n}\to Y\ \mathbb{P}
-a.s., and
\lvert Y_{n}\rvert\leq Z\ \mathbb{P}
-a.s. for some
Z\in L
. Since
u
is upper semicontinuous and
Y_{n}\to Y\ \mathbb{P}
-a.s.,

u(Y)\geq\limsup_{n\to\infty}u(Y_{n})\ \mathbb{P}\textnormal{-a.s.}\hskip 10.00002pt\textnormal{and hence}\hskip 10.00002pt\mathbb{E}[u(Y)]\geq\mathbb{E}[\limsup_{n\to\infty}u(Y_{n})].

(B.1)

Moreover, since
\lvert Y_{n}\rvert\leq Z\ \mathbb{P}
-a.s.,
u
is increasing and
u(0)=0
, it follows that
u(Y_{n})\leq u(Z)\ \mathbb{P}
-a.s., and because
\mathbb{E}[u(Z)]<\infty
, the reverse Fatou lemma implies

\mathbb{E}[\limsup_{n\to\infty}u(Y_{n})]\geq\limsup_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}[u(Y_{n})].

From this inequality and the inequality established in (
B.1
), we have

\mathcal{E}_{u}(Y)\geq\limsup_{n\to\infty}\mathcal{E}_{u}(Y_{n}).\qed

Proof of Proposition
5.2
.

Suppose there exists
Y\in L
with
\mathbb{P}[Y<0]>0
and a sequence
(\gamma_{n})_{n\geq 1}\subset\mathbb{R}
with
\gamma_{n}\to\infty
such that
\mathcal{E}_{u}(\gamma_{n}Y)\geq 0
for all
n
. Then,
\ell\coloneqq\limsup_{x\to\infty}u(-x)/u(x)>-\infty
by
Herdegen et al. 2024
, and by normalization,
\ell\leq 0
. This implies
\lim_{y\to\infty}u(y)=\infty
, for otherwise
\lim_{y\to\infty}u(y)\in(0,\infty)
, and in this case, the only way
\ell\in(-\infty,0]

\lim_{y\to-\infty}u(-y)\in(-\infty,0]
, but this would contradict the unboundedness of

. Now by the definition of

, there exists a sequence

\lambda_{n}\to\infty
such that

. Set
