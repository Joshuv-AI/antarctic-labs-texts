Low-rank approximation of analytic kernels
Marcus Webb Dedicated to the memory of Nicholas J. Higham

Abstract
Many algorithms in scientific computing and data science take advantage of low-rank approximation of matrices and kernels, and understanding why nearly-low-rank structure occurs is essential for their analysis and further development. This paper provides a framework for bounding the best low-rank approximation error of matrices arising from samples of a kernel that is analytically continuable in one of its variables to an open region of the complex plane. Elegantly, the low-rank approximations used in the proof are computable by rational interpolation using the roots and poles of Zolotarev rational functions, leading to a fast algorithm for their construction.

3
Cauchy–Zolotarev numbers

The Cauchy–Zolotarev numbers, a new concept, provide the rapid decay in Theorem
1.1
and are defined as follows.

Definition 3.1

(Cauchy–Zolotarev Numbers)
.

Let
E
and
F
be disjoint, closed sets in
\widehat{\mathbb{C}}
, with Radon measures
\mu
and
\nu
, and let
1\leq p,q\leq\infty
. Then for
n=0,1,2,\ldots
, the
n
th Cauchy–Zolotarev number is

Z_{n}\left(L_{\mu}^{p}(E),L_{\nu}^{q}(F)\right)=\inf_{\phi\in\mathcal{R}_{n}}\left\|\frac{\phi(z)^{-1}\phi(y)}{y-z}\right\|_{L_{\mu}^{p}(E)\to L_{\nu}^{q}(F)},

(3.1)

where
\mathcal{R}_{n}=\mathcal{R}_{n,n}
is the set of all rational functions of type
(n,n)
.

These numbers are related to both the classical Zolotarev numbers in Equation (
1.8
) and the Cauchy transform in Equation (
2.4
). Indeed, the case
n=0
is,
Z_{0}(L_{\mu}^{p}(E),L_{\nu}^{q}(F))=\|\mathcal{C}\|_{L_{\mu}^{p}(E)\to L_{\nu}^{q}(F)}
, and for
n\geq 1
, the relationship is encapsulated in the following lemma.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
and
s\in\{0,1,\ldots,n\}
. Then

Z_{n}(L_{\mu}^{p}(E),L_{\nu}^{q}(F))\leq Z_{n-s}(E,F)\,\cdot\,Z_{s}(L_{\mu}^{p}(E),L_{\nu}^{q}(F)).

(3.2)

Proof.

Consider
\phi(\xi)=\phi_{n-s}(\xi)\phi_{s}(\xi)
, where
\phi_{n-s}\in\mathcal{R}_{n-s}
,
\phi_{s}\in\mathcal{R}_{s}
. Then

\displaystyle\left\|\frac{\phi(z)^{-1}\phi(y)}{y-z}\right\|_{L_{\mu}^{p}(E)\to L_{\nu}^{q}(F)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sup_{\|g\|_{p}=1}\left\|\int_{E}\frac{\phi(z)^{-1}\phi(y)g(y)}{y-z}\,\mathrm{d}\mu(y)\right\|_{L_{\nu}^{q}(F)}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sup_{\|g\|_{p}=1}\|\phi_{n-s}^{-1}\|_{L_{\nu}^{\infty}(F)}\,\left\|\int_{E}\frac{\phi_{s}(z)^{-1}\phi_{s}(y)\left(\phi_{n-s}(y)g(y)\right)}{y-z}\,\mathrm{d}\mu(y)\right\|_{L_{\nu}^{q}(F)}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sup_{\|g\|_{p}=1}\|\phi_{n-s}^{-1}\|_{L_{\nu}^{\infty}(F)}\,\left\|\frac{\phi_{s}(z)^{-1}\phi_{s}(y)}{y-z}\right\|_{L_{\mu}^{p}(E)\to L_{\nu}^{q}(F)}\left\|\phi_{n-s}(y)g(y)\right\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\|\phi_{n-s}^{-1}\|_{L_{\nu}^{\infty}(F)}\,\|\phi_{n-s}\|_{L_{\mu}^{\infty}(E)}\,\left\|\frac{\phi_{s}(z)^{-1}\phi_{s}(y)}{y-z}\right\|_{L_{\mu}^{p}(E)\to L_{\nu}^{q}(F)}.

Now if we take the infimum over all possible
\phi_{n-s}
and
\phi_{s}
, we obtain the desired inequality.
∎

The case

corresponds to the bound

Z_{n}(L_{\mu}^{p}(E),L_{\nu}^{q}(F))\leq Z_{n}(E,F)\,\|\mathcal{C}\|_{L_{\mu}^{p}(E)\to L_{\nu}^{q}(F)}.

(3.3)

This is sufficient to show that the Cauchy–Zolotarev numbers decay extremely quickly if
\mathcal{C}
is bounded, because so do the classical Zolotarev numbers. Following the brief discussion in the introduction, let us discuss two important special cases for
E
and
F
. If
E
and
F
are two disjoint disks in the complex plane, then
Z_{n}(E,F)=\rho(E,F)^{-n}

starke1992near
. In particular, for
R>1
,

Z_{n}(\{|z|\leq 1\},\{|z|\geq R\})=R^{-n}.

(3.4)

The Zolotarev numbers are invariant under Möbius transformations, so the result extends to any two disjoint disks. The rate of decay can be explicitly bounded for the case of two disjoint intervals, too.

Theorem 3.3

(Beckermann–Townsend
beckermann2019bounds
)
.

Let
-\infty\leq a<b<c<d\leq\infty
. Then for
n=1,2,\ldots
,

Z_{n}([a,b],[c,d])\leq 4\exp\left(\frac{-n\pi^{2}}{\log(16\gamma)}\right),\qquad\gamma=\frac{|c-a||d-b|}{|c-b||d-a|}.

The Cauchy–Zolotarev numbers can be bounded using double integrals, as follows. For compactness of notation, and because we will use these spaces later, we use the Bochner spaces,
L_{\mu}^{p}\left(E;\,L_{\nu}^{q}(F)\right)
, of Bochner measurable functions
Z
on
E
with values in
L_{\nu}^{q}(F)
, satisfying finiteness of the norm,

\|Z\|_{L_{\mu}^{p}\left(E;\,L_{\nu}^{q}(F)\right)}=\left(\int_{E}\left(\int_{F}|Z(z,y)|^{q}\,\mathrm{d}\nu(z)\right)^{p/q}\,\mathrm{d}\mu(y)\right)^{1/p},

(3.5)

in the cases
1\leq p,q<\infty
and appropriate suprema when
p
or
q
is infinite
diestel1977vector
.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
Z\in C(F\times E)
define an operator
\mathcal{Z}:L_{\mu}^{p^{*}}(E)\to L_{\nu}^{q}(F)
for
p,q\in[1,\infty]
, where
p^{*}
is the Hölder dual exponent, satisfying
\tfrac{1}{p}+\tfrac{1}{p^{*}}=1
. Then

\|\mathcal{Z}\|_{L_{\mu}^{p^{*}}(E)\to L_{\nu}^{q}(F)}\leq\|Z\|_{L_{\mu}^{p}\left(E;\,L_{\nu}^{q}(F)\right)}.

Proof.

We can work through this directly from the definition. For
p\in[1,\infty]
and
q\in[1,\infty)
,

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{Z}\|_{L_{\mu}^{p^{*}}(E)\to L_{\nu}^{q}(F)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sup_{\|g\|_{p^{*}}=1}\left(\int_{F}\left|\int_{E}Z(z,y)\,g(y)\,\mathrm{d}\mu(y)\right|^{q}\,\mathrm{d}\nu(z)\right)^{1/q}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sup_{\|g\|_{p^{*}}=1}\int_{E}\left(\int_{F}\left|Z(z,y)\right|^{q}\,\mathrm{d}\nu(z)\right)^{1/q}\,|g(y)|\,\mathrm{d}\mu(y)\qquad\text{ (Minkowski's inequality)}.

The result now follows by Hölder’s inequality. For
q=\infty
, the proof is similar.
∎

The Cauchy transform operator is not necessarily bounded for any given
p
and
q
. The choice
p=1
,
q=\infty
always yields bounded
\mathcal{C}
as long as
E\cap F=\emptyset
, in which case Lemma
3.4
implies

Z_{n}(L_{\mu}^{1}(E),L_{\nu}^{\infty}(F))=\sup_{y\in E,z\in F}\left|\frac{\phi(z)^{-1}\phi(y)}{z-y}\right|\leq\frac{Z_{n}(E,F)}{\inf_{y\in E,z\in F}|z-y|}<\infty.

(3.6)

Some elegant special cases can be derived from Equation (
3.3
) and Lemma
3.4
. For example, with the notation of Theorem
3.3
, we can show that
\|\mathcal{C}\|_{L^{2}(a,b)\to L^{2}(c,d)}\leq\log^{1/2}(\gamma)
, so

Z_{n}(L^{2}(a,b),L^{2}(c,d))\leq 4\,\log^{1/2}(\gamma)\,\exp\left(\frac{-n\pi^{2}}{\log(16\gamma)}\right).

(3.7)

However, in the context of Theorem
1.1
, we are often constrained by
\mathcal{K}^{\prime}
in our choice of
p
and
q
, and in some of the examples of Section
6
,
\mathcal{C}
must be unbounded. This is fatal for the case
n=0
, but does not affect the validity of the main result for
n\geq 1
, as shown in the following Lemma.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
-\infty<b<c<d<\infty
and let
\mu
be a Radon measure on
[c,d]
. Then
Z_{0}(L_{\mu}^{2}(c,d),L^{1}(-\infty,b))=\infty
, but for
n\geq 1
,

Z_{n}(L_{\mu}^{2}(c,d),L^{1}(-\infty,b))\leq Z_{n-1}([c,d],[-\infty,b])\,\frac{1}{2}\,\mu([c,d])^{1/2}\,\log\left(\frac{d-b}{c-b}\right).

Proof.

For the first assertion, we note that,

\displaystyle Z_{0}(L_{\mu}^{2}(c,d),L^{1}(-\infty,b))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sup_{\|g\|_{2}=1}\int_{-\infty}^{b}\left|\int_{c}^{d}\frac{g(y)}{y-z}\,\mathrm{d}\mu(y)\right|\,\mathrm{d}z

(3.8)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\mu([c,d])^{-1/2}\int_{-\infty}^{b}\int_{c}^{d}\frac{1}{y-z}\,\mathrm{d}\mu(y)\,\mathrm{d}z,

(3.9)

by taking
g\equiv\mu([c,d])^{-1/2}
and noting that
y>z
in the integrand. Then by Fubini’s Theorem,

Z_{0}(L_{\mu}^{2}(c,d),L^{1}(-\infty,b))\geq\mu([c,d])^{-1/2}\int_{c}^{d}\int_{-\infty}^{b}\frac{1}{y-z}\,\mathrm{d}z\,\mathrm{d}\mu(y)\geq\mu([c,d])^{1/2}\int_{-\infty}^{b}\frac{1}{d-z}\,\mathrm{d}z=\infty.

(3.10)

For the second assertion, choose
\phi(\xi)=\xi-t
, where
t=b+\sqrt{(d-b)(c-b)}
. If
Z(z,y)=\phi(z)^{-1}(y-z)^{-1}\phi(y)
, then by Lemma
3.4
with
p=2
,
q=1
, we have

\displaystyle Z_{1}(L_{\mu}^{2}(c,d),L^{1}(-\infty,b))

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left(\int_{c}^{d}\left(\int_{-\infty}^{b}|Z(z,y)|\,\mathrm{d}z\right)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mu(y)\right)^{1/2}.

Then, using partial fractions to evaluate the inner integral,

\displaystyle\left(\int_{c}^{d}\left(\int_{-\infty}^{b}|Z(z,y)|\,\mathrm{d}z\right)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mu(y)\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left(\int_{c}^{d}\left(\int_{-\infty}^{b}\frac{y-t}{(t-z)(y-z)}\,\mathrm{d}z\right)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mu(y)\right)^{1/2}

(3.11)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\int_{c}^{d}\log^{2}\left(\frac{y-b}{t-b}\right)\,\mathrm{d}\mu(y)\right)^{1/2}

(3.12)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mu([c,d])^{1/2}\sup_{y\in[c,d]}\left|\log\left(\frac{y-b}{t-b}\right)\right|.

(3.13)

Now we can substitute the form of
t
into the logarithm. Since
y\to\log\left(\frac{y-b}{t-b}\right)
is a monotonic function, its extrema are attained at the endpoints. For this choice of
t
, the endpoint values are
\pm\frac{1}{2}\log\left(\frac{d-b}{c-b}\right)
,
which yields the desired bound on
Z_{1}
. To bound
Z_{n}
for
n\geq 1
, use Lemma
3.2
with
s=1
.
∎

4
The Grothendieck dual operator

The operator
\mathcal{K}^{\prime}:H(F)\to C(D)
in Theorem
1.1
appears to be an unusual choice, but we will see here how it can be thought of naturally as a kind of dual operator to
\mathcal{K}
. The type of duality required is due to Grothendieck
grothendieck1953certains
, Köthe
kothe1953dualitat
and Silva
silva1950funcoes
, and does not appear to be well known among Numerical Analysts, despite the attempt made in
rubel1969functional
to introduce it to our repertoire. We refer to this duality as Grothendieck duality and refer to
\mathcal{K}^{\prime}
as the
Grothendieck dual
of
\mathcal{K}
.

4.1
Examples

To motivate Grothendieck duality, we begin with two examples. The first is

K(x,y)=\frac{m!}{(y-x)^{m+1}},\qquad x\in D=[1,N],\quad y\in E=[-N,-1]

(4.1)

where
m
is an integer. For each
x\in D
,
K(x,\cdot)
is analytic in
F^{\prime}
where
F=[1,N]
. Its Grothendieck dual operator
\mathcal{K}^{\prime}:H([1,N])\to C([1,N])
is

\mathcal{K}^{\prime}[h](x)=\frac{m!}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{\Gamma}\frac{h(\xi)}{(\xi-x)^{m+1}}\,\mathrm{d}\xi=h^{(m)}(x),

(4.2)

for
h\in H([1,N])
. The second example is

K(x,y)=\log(y-x),\qquad x\in D=[-N,-1],\quad y\in E=[1,N].

(4.3)

For each
x\in D
,
K(x,\cdot)
is analytic in
F^{\prime}
where
F=[-\infty,-1]
. The action of the the Grothendieck dual operator
\mathcal{K}^{\prime}:H([-\infty,-1])\to C([-N,-1])
is given below, but proved in Section
A
.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
K
be as in Equation (
4.3
) and
h\in H([-\infty,-1])
, then for
x\in[-N,-1]
,

\mathcal{K}^{\prime}[h](x)=\int_{-\infty}^{x}\frac{a_{1}}{z-x-1}-h(z)\,\mathrm{d}z,

(4.4)

where
a_{1}=\lim_{z\to\infty}zh(z)
.

4.2
Grothendieck duality

Recall Definition
2.1
, which defined the spaces
H(F)
and
H(F^{\prime})
. Endow
H(F^{\prime})
with the topology of uniform convergence on compact subsets of
F^{\prime}
, and endow
H(F)
with the inductive limit topology, which means that
h_{m}\to h
in
H(F)
as
m\to\infty
if there exists an open set
U\supset F
such that, for all sufficiently large
m
,
h_{m}\in H(U)
and
h_{m}\to h
in
H(U)
.

Let
H^{\prime}(F)
be the space of continuous linear functionals on
H(F)
. Silva proved that
H^{\prime}(F)
is isomorphic to
H(F^{\prime})
(as topological vector spaces, if
H^{\prime}(F)
is endowed with the strong topology) by the map
\Phi:H(F^{\prime})\to H^{\prime}(F)
,

\left(\Phi[g]\right)[h]=\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{\Gamma}g(\xi)\,h(\xi)\,\mathrm{d}\xi,\qquad g\in H(F^{\prime}),\quad h\in H(F),

(4.5)

where
h
is analytic in an open set
U
containing
F
and
\Gamma\subset U\setminus F
is any finite sum of rectifiable Jordan curves with winding numbers satisfying
\mathrm{Ind}_{\Gamma}(F)-\mathrm{Ind}_{\Gamma}(U^{\prime})=1

silva1950funcoes
. The inverse operator is
\Phi^{-1}[\varphi](y)=\varphi[(y-z)^{-1}]
, where
\varphi\in H^{\prime}(F)
and
y\in F^{\prime}
. If
F
is a smooth contour then
\Phi[g]
depends only on the jump of
g\in H(F^{\prime})
across
F
, and
\Phi[g]
can be interpreted in a manner more familiar to Numerical Analysts, as a
hyperfunction

sato1959theory
.

In
grothendieck1953certains
, Grothendieck generalised Silva’s duality theorems to general complementary sets
F
and
F^{\prime}
, and to weakly holomorphic functions with values in a locally convex vector space
V
. Specifically,
H(F^{\prime};V)\cong H^{\prime}(F;V^{\prime})
, where
V^{\prime}
is the continuous dual space of
V
. We won’t go into the details here about the strict definition of
H(F^{\prime};V)
— we proved Theorem
1.1
without appealing to Grothendieck’s theory — because we merely need to know that the kernel
K
in Theorem
1.1
lies in
H(F^{\prime};\,L_{\lambda}^{2}(D))
(after subtracting off
K(x,\infty)
if
\infty\in F^{\prime}
, which does not change
\mathcal{K}^{\prime}
), and

H(F^{\prime};\,L_{\lambda}^{2}(D))\cong H^{\prime}(F;\,L_{\lambda}^{2}(D)).

(4.6)

From these two facts, we deduce the existence of a corresponding distributional kernel
K^{\prime}\in H^{\prime}(F;\,L_{\lambda}^{2}(D))
. Specifically, there exists a distribution on analytic functions
K^{\prime}
such that

\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{\Gamma}K(x,\xi)\,h(\xi)\,\mathrm{d}\xi=\int_{F}K^{\prime}(x,z)\,h(z)\,\mathrm{d}\nu(z),

(4.7)

for all
h\in H(F)
. There are two interpretations of the space
H^{\prime}(F;\,L_{\lambda}^{2}(D))
in this equation, one through Grothendieck duality and one through the Gelfand triple (or rigged Hilbert space),

H(F;\,L_{\lambda}^{2}(D))\subset L_{\nu}^{2}(F;\,L_{\lambda}^{2}(D))\subset H^{\prime}(F;\,L_{\lambda}^{2}(D)),

(4.8)

each with a corresponding kernel. Setting
h(z)=(y-z)^{-1}
for a fixed
y\in F^{\prime}
yields the key relationship,

K(x,y)=\int_{F}\frac{K^{\prime}(x,z)}{z-y}\,\mathrm{d}\nu(z).

(4.9)

If the distribution
K^{\prime}
is actually a function, then we can estimate the norm of
\mathcal{K}^{\prime}
as follows.

Lemma 4.2
.

If
K^{\prime}\in L^{p}_{\nu}(F;\,L_{\lambda}^{q}(D))
, where
p,q\in[1,\infty]
, then

\|\mathcal{K}^{\prime}\|_{H_{\nu}^{p^{*}}(F)\to L_{\lambda}^{q}(D)}\leq\|K^{\prime}\|_{L_{\nu}^{p}(F;\,L_{\lambda}^{q}(D))},

where
p^{*}
is the Hölder dual exponent, satisfying
\tfrac{1}{p}+\tfrac{1}{p^{*}}=1
.

Proof.

This follows from Equation (
4.7
) and Lemma
3.4
.
∎

5
Computation by rational interpolation

The low-rank approximant used in the proof of Theorem
1.1
is a Hilbert–Schmidt operator with kernel

K_{n}(x,y)=\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{\Gamma}K(x,\xi)\,\left(1-\frac{\phi(y)}{\phi(\xi)}\right)\frac{1}{y-\xi}\,\mathrm{d}\xi,

(5.1)

where
\phi(\xi)=\prod_{i=1}^{\ell}(\xi-q_{i})/\prod_{k=1}^{m}(\xi-p_{k})
for some
\ell,m\leq n
. This formula bears a resemblance to the Hermite integral formula for rational interpolation with prescribed poles.

Theorem 5.1

(Hermite integral formula
(
walsh1935interpolation
, Thm 8.2)
)
.

Let
\Omega
be a bounded open subset of
\mathbb{C}
whose boundary
\Gamma
is a finite sum of rectifiable Jordan curves. Let
g\in H(\Omega)
,
q_{1},\ldots,q_{\ell}\in\Omega
and
p_{1},\ldots,p_{m}\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{q_{1},\ldots,q_{\ell}\}
. There exists a unique
r\in\mathcal{R}_{\ell-1,m}
that interpolates
g
at
\{q_{1},\ldots,q_{\ell}\}
(in the Hermite sense if points are repeated) and has poles at
\{p_{1},\ldots,p_{m}\}
, which for
y\in\Omega
satisfies

r(y)=\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{\Gamma}g(\xi)\,\left(1-\frac{\phi(y)}{\phi(\xi)}\right)\frac{1}{\xi-y}\,\mathrm{d}\xi.

Theorem
5.1
indeed implies, after changing the orientation of the contour and reversing the sign of the
\xi-y
term, that the low-rank kernel given in the proof of Theorem
1.1
is a rational interpolant of
K(x,\cdot)
, with interpolation nodes and poles that are independent of
x
. The roots and poles in the proof are chosen so that
\phi
is optimal for
Z_{n}(L_{\mu}^{2}(E),L_{\nu}^{p}(F))
. If the roots of
\phi
are distinct, then

K_{n}(x,y)=\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}K(x,q_{j})\,\phi_{j}(y),\text{ where }\phi_{j}(y)=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=1\\
i\neq j\end{subarray}}^{\ell}\frac{y-q_{i}}{q_{j}-q_{i}}\,\prod_{k=1}^{m}\frac{q_{j}-p_{k}}{y-p_{k}}.

(5.2)

To evaluate
K_{n}
in an efficient and numerically stable manner, use the barycentric form
higham2004numerical
;
webb2012stability
,

K_{n}(x,y)=\phi(y)\,\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}\frac{K(x,q_{j})\,w_{j}}{y-q_{j}},\text{ where }w_{j}=\frac{\prod_{k=1}^{m}(q_{j}-p_{k})}{\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=1\\
i\neq j\end{subarray}}^{\ell}(q_{j}-q_{i})}.

(5.3)

Now the only thing standing between us and a practical algorithm is the choice of
\phi
. The case of two disjoint intervals on the extended real line has an explicit solution due to Zolotarev.

Theorem 5.2

(Zolotarev
zolotarev1877application
,
(
wilber2021computing
, p. 20)
)
.

Let
\phi(z)
be the Zolotarev rational function for

Z_{n}([a,b],[c,d])
, where
-\infty\leq a<b<c<d\leq\infty
. Then the zeros
q_{1},\ldots,q_{n}
and poles
p_{1},\ldots,p_{n}
of
\phi
are

q_{j}=T\left(-\mathrm{dn}\left[\frac{2j-1}{2n}\mathrm{K}(\Xi),\Xi\right]\right),\quad p_{j}=T\left(\mathrm{dn}\left[\frac{2j-1}{2n}\mathrm{K}(\Xi),\Xi\right]\right),

(5.4)

where
\Xi=\sqrt{1-1/\tau^{2}}
,
\tau=-1+2\gamma+2\sqrt{\gamma^{2}-\gamma}
,
\gamma
is as in Theorem
3.3
,
\mathrm{K}
is the complete elliptic integral of the first kind
(
NIST:DLMF
, 19.2.8)
,
\mathrm{dn}(z,\Xi)
is the Jacobi elliptic function of the third kind
(
NIST:DLMF
, 22.2.6)
, and

T
is the Möbius transformation such that

T(-1)=a,T(-1/\tau)=b,T(1/\tau)=c,T(1)=d
.

In this case,
q_{1},\ldots,q_{n}
are distinct, so formula (
5.3
) can be used to evaluate the kernel
K_{n}
. However, for other sets
E
and
F
, the roots of the optimal
\phi
may not be distinct. In such cases, the rational interpolant is of Hermite type and requires partial derivatives of
K
with respect to
y
. Indeed, for
R>1
, the optimal Zolotarev rational function for
Z_{n}(\{|z|\leq 1\},\{|z|\geq R\})
is
\phi(\xi)=\xi^{n}
.

However, the rational function
\phi
with
q_{j}=\mathrm{e}^{2\pi\mathrm{i}j/n}
and
p_{j}=R\mathrm{e}^{2\pi\mathrm{i}j/n}
is asymptotically optimal, in the sense that
\lim_{n\to\infty}\sup_{y\in E,z\in F}|\phi(z)^{-1}\phi(y)|^{1/n}=\rho(E,F)^{-1}

walsh1965hyperbolic
;
starke1992near
. Asymptotically optimal rational functions with distinct roots can be computed using conformal mapping to an annulus (if possible)
walsh1965hyperbolic
;
starke1992near
, Bagby’s iteration
bagby1969interpolation
, or the recent algorithm of Trefethen and Wilber
trefethen2025computation
based on the AAA-Lawson algorithm applied to the sign function on
E\cup F
.

Of course, suboptimal
\phi
leads to suboptimal interpolants. The following suboptimal version of Theorem
1.1
places the bounds of Little–Reade type (based on polynomial approximation) in the context of the present work
little1984eigenvalues
(see also
(
townsend2015continuous
, Thm 7.1)
).

Proposition 5.3
.

Let
K\in C(D\times[-1,1])
be as in Theorem
1.1
, such that
K(x,\cdot)
is analytically continuable to the open interior of a Bernstein ellipse with parameter
R>1
. Then for
n=1,2,\ldots
, there exists a rank-
n
kernel
K_{n}\in C(D\times E)
such that, for any
p\in[1,\infty]
,

\|\mathcal{K}-\mathcal{K}_{n}\|_{L_{\mu}^{2}(-1,1)\to L_{\lambda}^{2}(D)}\leq\frac{2R^{-n}}{1-R^{-2n}}\,\left\|\mathcal{C}\right\|_{L_{\mu}^{2}(-1,1)\to L_{\nu}^{p}(F)}\,\|\mathcal{K}^{\prime}\|_{H_{\nu}^{p}(F)\to L_{\lambda}^{2}(D)},

where
\mathcal{C}
is the Cauchy transform in Equation (
2.4
).

Proof.

Apply Theorem
1.1
and take
\phi(\xi)=T_{n}(\xi)\in\mathcal{R}_{n,0}\subset\mathcal{R}_{n}
, which corresponds to polynomial interpolation at the roots of
T_{n}
. To bound the resulting suboptimal Zolotarev term, start with the formula
\phi(z)=\tfrac{1}{2}(\zeta^{n}+\zeta^{-n})
where
z=\tfrac{1}{2}(\zeta+\zeta^{-1})
with
|\zeta|\geq 1

(
NIST:DLMF
, 18.5.1)
. Then for
y\in[-1,1]
,
z\in F
,

\frac{\phi(z)^{-1}\phi(y)}{y-z}=\frac{2\zeta^{-n}}{1+\zeta^{-2n}}\cdot C(z,y)\cdot T_{n}(y),\quad\text{(where }C\text{ is the kernel of }\mathcal{C}).

(5.5)

To conclude, note that
|\zeta|\geq R
for all
z\in F
since
F
lies outside the ellipse.
∎

Remark 5.4
.

If
F\subset\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
then the denominator can be changed to
1+R^{-2n}
because
|T_{n}(z)|\geq\tfrac{1}{2}(R^{n}+R^{-n})
for
z\in F
. If
\mathcal{C}
is unbounded then
\phi(\xi)=(\xi-t)T_{n-1}(\xi)
can be used as in Lemma
3.5
.

6.1
Cauchy matrices and tensors

Consider a Cauchy matrix
A\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}

A_{i,j}=\frac{1}{x_{i}+y_{j}},\qquad x_{i}\in D,\quad y_{j}\in E,

(6.1)

where
D
and
E
are compact subsets of the complex plane such that
(-D)\cap E=\emptyset
. To apply Theorem
1.1
, we take the kernel
K(x,y)=\frac{1}{x+y}
, with
\lambda
and
\mu
being discrete counting measures on
\{x_{i}\}
and
\{y_{j}\}
. For each
x\in D
,
K(x,\cdot)
can be analytically continued to
\widehat{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\{-x\}
, so we can take
F=-D
. The Grothendieck dual of
\mathcal{K}
is
\mathcal{K}^{\prime}:H(-D)\to C(D)
, given by

\mathcal{K}^{\prime}[h](x_{i})=\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{\Gamma}\frac{h(\xi)}{x_{i}+\xi}\,\mathrm{d}\xi=h(-x_{i}),

(6.2)

where
\Gamma
is any suitable contour within the region of analyticity of
h
, winding once around
-D
(and zero times around
E)
. It is clear that
\mathcal{K}^{\prime}
merely reflects
h
across the origin, so if we take
\mathrm{d}\nu(z)=\mathrm{d}\lambda(-z)
, then

\|\mathcal{K}^{\prime}\|_{H_{\nu}^{2}(-D)\to L^{2}_{\lambda}(D)}=1
. Therefore, by Theorem
1.1
,

\displaystyle\sigma_{n+1}(A)\leq\|\mathcal{K}-\mathcal{K}_{n}\|_{L^{2}_{\mu}(E)\to L^{2}_{\lambda}(D)}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle Z_{n}(L_{\mu}^{2}(E),L_{\nu}^{2}(-D))

(6.3)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle Z_{n}(E,-D)\,\|(y-z)^{-1}\|_{L_{\mu}^{2}(E)\to L_{\nu}^{2}(-D)},

(6.4)

by Lemma
3.4
. Since
\|(y-z)^{-1}\|_{L_{\mu}^{2}(E)\to L_{\nu}^{2}(-D)}=\|(y+z)^{-1}\|_{L_{\mu}^{2}(E)\to L_{\lambda}^{2}(D)}=\sigma_{1}(A)
, we recover the Beckermann–Townsend results exactly for Cauchy matrices
beckermann2019bounds
.

Now consider a Cauchy tensor
A\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N\times N}
,

A_{i,j,k}=\frac{1}{w_{i}+x_{j}+y_{k}},\qquad(w_{i},x_{j})\in D,\quad y_{k}\in E,

(6.5)

where
E
is a compact subset of
\mathbb{C}
and
D
is a compact subset of
\mathbb{C}^{2}
such that
(-\Sigma D)\cap E=\emptyset
, where
\Sigma D=\{w+x:(w,x)\in D\}
. Then we take the kernel
K((w,x),y)=\frac{1}{w+x+y}
, with
\lambda
and
\mu
counting measures as above. For each
(w,x)\in D
,
K((w,x),\cdot)
can be analytically continued to
\widehat{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\{-(w+x)\}
. Therefore, we take
F=-\Sigma D
. The Grothendieck dual of
\mathcal{K}
is
\mathcal{K}^{\prime}:H(-\Sigma D)\to C(D)
and we have

\mathcal{K}^{\prime}[h]((w,x))=\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{\Gamma}\frac{h(\xi)}{w+x+\xi}\,\mathrm{d}\xi=h(-w-x)

(6.6)

The norm is slightly more challenging to bound. If we define a Radon measure
\nu
on
-\Sigma D
by

\nu(U)=\mu\left(\{(w,x)\in D:-w-x\in U\}\right),

(6.7)

for all open sets

, then
\mathcal{K}^{\prime}:H_{\nu}^{2}(-\Sigma D)\to L_{\lambda}^{2}(D)
is an isometry. Therefore, by Theorem
1.1
,

\|\mathcal{K}-\mathcal{K}_{n}\|_{L^{2}_{\mu}(E)\to L^{2}_{\lambda}(D)}\leq Z_{n}(L_{\mu}^{2}(E),L_{\nu}^{2}(-\Sigma D))\leq Z_{n}(E,-\Sigma D)\,\|(y-z)^{-1}\|_{L_{\mu}^{2}(E)\to L_{\nu}^{2}(-\Sigma D)}.

(6.8)

Just as with the Cauchy matrices,
\|(y-z)^{-1}\|_{L_{\mu}^{2}(E)\to L_{\nu}^{2}(-\Sigma D)}=\|\mathcal{K}\|_{L_{\mu}^{2}(E)\to L_{\lambda}^{2}(D)}
, so we recover the results of Shi and Townsend

based on displacement structure of the Cauchy tensor.

6.3
Twisted Hankel transform matrix

The discrete Hankel transform is defined by the matrix
johnson1987improved
,

J_{i,j}=\mathrm{J}_{\nu}\left(\frac{\omega_{i}\omega_{j}}{\omega_{N+1}}\right),\qquad i,j=1,2,\ldots,N,

(6.16)

where
\omega_{1},\ldots,\omega_{N+1}
are the first
N+1
positive roots of
\mathrm{J}_{\nu}
, the Bessel function of order
\nu\geq 0

(
NIST:DLMF
, §10)
. For simplicity, we take
\nu=0
. A new class of fast algorithms for the discrete Hankel transform (to appear in a forthcoming publication) rely on the low-rank approximation of the kernel

K(x,y)=\mathrm{H}_{0}^{(1)}(xy)\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}xy},\qquad x\in[0,1],\qquad y\in[\omega_{1},\omega_{N}],

(6.17)

where
\mathrm{H}_{0}^{(1)}
is the Hankel function of the first kind. For each
x\in[0,1]
,
K(x,\cdot)
is analytic in
\mathbb{C}\setminus(-\infty,0]
, where
\mathrm{H}_{0}^{(1)}
has its principle branch cut. Therefore, we can take
D=[0,1]
,
E=[\omega_{1},\omega_{N}]
,
F=[-\infty,0]
. Its Grothendieck dual
\mathcal{K}^{\prime}:H([-\infty,0])\to C([0,1])
is

\mathcal{K}^{\prime}[h](x)=\frac{2}{\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{-\infty}^{0}\mathrm{J}_{0}(xz)\,\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}xz}\,h(z)\,\mathrm{d}z.

(6.18)

The proof of this is similar to that of Equation (
6.11
). The two consequential things to note are, that
\mathrm{H}_{0}^{(1)}(\xi)
has a jump of
-4\mathrm{J}_{0}(\xi)
over the negative real axis
(
NIST:DLMF
, 10.11.5)
, and, crucially,

\mathrm{H}_{0}^{(1)}(\xi)\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\xi}=\mathcal{O}(\xi^{-1/2})\quad\text{ as }\xi\to\infty\text{ with }|\mathrm{arg}(\xi)|<\pi-\delta,

(6.19)

by
(
NIST:DLMF
, 10.7.8)
. This second fact ensures that the contour integrals depending on
R
will converge to zero as
R\to\infty
. Therefore, as a member of
H^{\prime}([-\infty,0];L_{\lambda}^{2}([0,1]))
, the Grothendieck dual has kernel
K^{\prime}(x,z)=\frac{2}{\pi\mathrm{i}}\mathrm{J}_{0}(xz)\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}xz}
, so we can use Lemma
4.2
to bound its norm

\|\mathcal{K}^{\prime}\|_{H^{1}([-\infty,0])\to L_{\lambda}^{2}([0,1])}=\sup_{z\leq 0}\frac{2}{\pi}\left(\int_{0}^{1}|\mathrm{J}_{0}(xz)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\lambda(x)\right)^{1/2}=\frac{2}{\pi}\lambda([0,1])^{1/2},

(6.20)

because
\mathrm{J}_{0}(0)=1
. By Lemma
3.5
, we have

Z_{n}(L_{\mu}^{2}(\omega_{1},\omega_{N}),L^{1}(-\infty,0))\leq\frac{1}{2}\,\log\left(\frac{\omega_{N}}{\omega_{1}}\right)\,\mu([\omega_{1},\omega_{N}])^{1/2}\,Z_{n-1}([\omega_{1},\omega_{N}],[-\infty,0]).

(6.21)

Therefore, by Theorem
1.1
, in the case that
\lambda
and
\mu
are counting measures,

\|\mathcal{K}-\mathcal{K}_{n}\|_{L_{\mu}^{2}([\omega_{1},\omega_{N}])\to L_{\lambda}^{2}([0,1])}\leq\frac{N}{\pi}\,\log\left(\frac{\omega_{N}}{\omega_{1}}\right)\,Z_{n-1}([\omega_{1},\omega_{N}],[-\infty,0]).

(6.22)

6.4
Beta-Cauchy matrices

Positive-definite Hankel matrices of the following form appear in the change-of-basis matrices between classical orthogonal polynomials
townsend2018fast
. A family of fast algorithms for performing the matrix-vector product depends on low-rank approximation of the matrix,

A_{ij}=\mathrm{B}(i+j+\alpha,\beta),\qquad i,j=0,\ldots,N,

(6.24)

where
\alpha,\beta>0
,
\beta\notin\mathbb{N}
, and
\mathrm{B}
is Euler’s beta function,
\mathrm{B}(\alpha,\beta)=\Gamma(\alpha)\Gamma(\beta)/\Gamma(\alpha+\beta)

(
NIST:DLMF
, 5.12)
. To apply Theorem
1.1
, we take

K(x,y)=\mathrm{B}(x+y+\alpha,\beta),\qquad x,y\in[0,N],

(6.25)

with
\lambda
and
\mu
the counting measures on
\{0,1,\ldots,N\}
. For each
x\in\{0,\ldots,N\}
,
\xi\mapsto K(x,\xi)
is analytic for
\xi\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{-(\alpha+x+k):k\in\mathbb{Z}_{\leq 0}\}
, so we can take

D=[0,N]
,
E=[0,N]
,
F=[-\infty,-\alpha]
. We use the partial fraction expansion
(
bateman1953higher
, p. 8)
,

\mathrm{B}(\alpha,\beta)=\frac{1}{\Gamma(1-\beta)}\,\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{\Gamma(k+1-\beta)}{\Gamma(k+1)}\,\frac{1}{\alpha+k},

(6.26)

to obtain

K(x,y)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{w(k,\beta)}{x+y+\alpha+k},\qquad w(k,\beta)=\frac{\Gamma(k+1-\beta)}{\Gamma(k+1)\,\Gamma(1-\beta)}.

(6.27)

For intuition in what follows, it is useful to note by Stirling’s formula that
w(k,\beta)\sim k^{-\beta}/\Gamma(1-\beta)
for large
k
. The Grothendieck dual operator is, for
h\in H([-\infty,-\alpha])
,

\mathcal{K}^{\prime}[h](x)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}w(k,\beta)\,h(-\alpha-k-x)=\sum_{k=x}^{\infty}w(k-x,\beta)\,h(-\alpha-k).

(6.28)

An argument similar to that in Section
A
is technically required to preclude hiccoughs at infinity. If we choose the space
H_{\nu}^{1}([-\infty,-\alpha])
, where
\nu
is the discrete weighted measure,
\nu(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}|w(k,\beta)|\,\delta(z+k+\alpha)
, then

\mathcal{K}^{\prime}[h](x)=\int_{-\infty}^{-\alpha}K^{\prime}(x,z)\,h(z)\,\mathrm{d}\nu(z),

(6.29)

where, for
x\in\{0,1,\ldots,N\}
and
z\in\{-\alpha,-1-\alpha,-2-\alpha,\ldots\}
,

K^{\prime}(x,z)=\begin{cases}\frac{w(-z-\alpha-x,\beta)}{|w(-z-\alpha,\beta)|}&\text{ if }x+z+\alpha\leq 0\\
0&\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

(6.30)

The weighting of the discrete measure
\nu
has provided a uniformly bounded
K^{\prime}(x,z)
, since for
k\geq x
,

|K^{\prime}(x,-k-\alpha)|=\left|\frac{\Gamma(k+1-x-\beta)}{\Gamma(k+1-\beta)}\frac{\Gamma(k+1)}{\Gamma(k+1-x)}\right|=\prod_{\ell=0}^{x-1}\frac{k-\ell}{k-\ell-\beta}=\prod_{\ell=0}^{x-1}\frac{1}{1-\frac{\beta}{k-\ell}}.

(6.31)

This expression is strictly decreasing with
k
, so using Lemma
4.2
we can bound the norm of
\mathcal{K}^{\prime}
by

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{K}^{\prime}\|_{H^{1}_{\nu}([-\infty,-\alpha])\to L_{\lambda}^{2}([0,N])}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sup_{z\in\{-\alpha,-1-\alpha,\ldots\}}\left(\sum_{x=0}^{N}|K^{\prime}(x,z)|^{2}\right)^{1/2}

(6.32)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{x=0}^{N}|K^{\prime}(x,-N-\alpha)|^{2}\right)^{1/2}

(6.33)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{x=0}^{N}\left(\frac{w(x,\beta)}{w(N,\beta)}\right)^{2}\right)^{1/2}.

(6.34)

The Cauchy transform
\mathcal{C}:L_{\mu}^{2}([1,N])\to L_{\nu}^{1}([-\infty,-\alpha])
can also be bounded. By Lemma
3.4
,

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{C}\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left(\int_{0}^{N}\left(\int_{-\infty}^{-\alpha}|y-z|^{-1}\,\mathrm{d}\nu(z)\right)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mu(y)\right)^{1/2}=\left(\sum_{y=0}^{N}\left(\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{|w(k,\beta)|}{y+k+\alpha}\right)^{2}\right)^{1/2}.

(6.35)

Theorem
1.1
and Lemma
3.2
imply

\sigma_{n+1}(A)\leq Z_{n}([-\infty,-\alpha],[0,N])\left(\sum_{y=0}^{N}\left(\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{|w(k,\beta)|}{y+k+\alpha}\right)^{2}\right)^{1/2}\left(\sum_{x=0}^{N}\left(\frac{w(x,\beta)}{w(N,\beta)}\right)^{2}\right)^{1/2}.

(6.36)

N=100
,
\alpha=1/2
,
\beta=1/2
. The interpolation nodes and poles for the interpolants corresponding to the purple triangles were computed in a similar manner to those in Figure

trefethen2025computation

7
Conclusion

This paper provides a new theorem to bound the best low-rank approximation error of a matrix or operator whose kernel is continuous and analytically continuable in one of its variables to a larger region of the complex plane. Key concepts introduced are the Cauchy–Zolotarev numbers and the Grothendieck dual operator. We described practical techniques to bound the error, showed that rational interpolation computes low-rank approximations that achieve the bounds, and confirmed the theory with several numerical examples.

The core of the proof is the operator decomposition
\mathcal{K}=\mathcal{K}^{\prime}\,\mathcal{C}
, where
\mathcal{C}:L_{\mu}^{2}(E)\to H(F)
is a Cauchy transform operator and
\mathcal{K}^{\prime}:H(F)\to C(D)
is the Grothendieck dual operator of
\mathcal{K}
. This reveals a latent displacement structure in
\mathcal{K}
, since
\mathcal{C}
satisfies the displacement equation,

\mathcal{M}_{F}\,\mathcal{C}-\mathcal{C}\,\mathcal{M}_{E}=\mathcal{L},

(7.1)

where
\mathcal{M}_{E}[g](y)=T(y)\,g(y)
,
\mathcal{M}_{F}[h](z)=T(z)\,h(z)
, and
\mathcal{L}[g](z)=-\sqrt{T^{\prime}(z)}\,\int_{E}\sqrt{T^{\prime}(y)}g(y)\,\mathrm{d}\mu(y)
(a rank-1 operator), for any Möbius transformation
T
such that
T(E\cup F)
is bounded, because

\frac{T(z)-T(y)}{z-y}=\sqrt{T^{\prime}(z)}\,\sqrt{T^{\prime}(y)}.

(7.2)

The rapid decay of the singular values of
\mathcal{C}
, and hence those of
\mathcal{K}
, now follows from
beckermann2019bounds
;
clouatre2025lifting
.

The proof can easily be generalised to bound
\|\mathcal{K}-\mathcal{K}_{n}\|_{L_{\mu}^{q}(E)\to L_{\lambda}^{r}(D)}
for any
q,r\in[1,\infty]
. The case
q=1
,
r=\infty
corresponds to the ‘max norm’, a particularly tractable case. I have carefully avoided discussing higher-order singularities such as
K(x,y)=(y-x)^{-2}
. In this case
\mathcal{K}^{\prime}[h](x)=h^{\prime}(x)
, the norm of which cannot easily be bounded using the techniques of this paper. A Sobolev space,
W_{\nu}^{p,s}(F)
, could be introduced, upon which
\mathcal{K}^{\prime}
is more obviously bounded, but then one is left to bound what could be called Cauchy–Sobolev–Zolotarev numbers,
Z_{n}(L_{\mu}^{2}(E),W_{\nu}^{p,s}(F))
.

The duality of
H(F)
and
H(F^{\prime})
discussed in Section
4
directly impacts Potential Theory and approximation of analytic functions. The standard potential-theoretic methods of bounding the integral in the Hermite integral formula for a function
f
with singularities within the set
F
lead to an error bound for each contour
\Gamma
, which for fixed
n
often tends to infinity as
\Gamma
approaches
\partial F

levin2006potential
. Grothendieck duality takes
\Gamma
out of the equation (after all, the equation is invariant under the choice of
\Gamma
), resulting in a single error bound involving a norm of
f
, interpreted as an element of
H^{\prime}(F)
, multiplied by a Cauchy–Zolotarev number (or its polynomial analogue). This is the topic of a future paper.

The choice of interpolation points and poles is ripe for investigation. More research on computation of Zolotarev rational functions associated with continuous and, more importantly, discrete subsets of
\widehat{\mathbb{C}}
is needed, since Trefethen and Wilber’s algorithm is not 100% reliable
trefethen2025computation
. Figure
4
highlights the potential of direct computation of the optimal rational functions for Cauchy-Zolotarev numbers. When
F
is unknown, the best approach to finding good points and poles may involve the AAA algorithm
nakatsukasa2018aaa
applied to
K
. Developing a practical interpolation scheme in this setting could have significant consequences, impacting any algorithm employing low-rank approximation of analytic kernels.

Proof of Lemma
4.1

Proof.

Fix
x\in[-N,-1]
. Since
h
is analytic at and tends to zero at infinity (by the definition of
H([-\infty,-1])
), there exists a Laurent expansion
h(z)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{a_{n}(x)}{(z-x-1)^{n}}
,
convergent for all
|z-x-1|>R
for some
R>0
. Set
0<\varepsilon<x+1
(sufficiently small), consider the standard keyhole contour,
\Gamma=\Gamma_{+}\cup\Gamma_{-}\cup\Gamma_{R}\cup\Gamma_{\varepsilon}
, where
\Gamma_{\pm}=[-\sqrt{R^{2}-\varepsilon^{2}}+x,x]\pm\varepsilon\mathrm{i}
,
\Gamma_{\varepsilon}=\{x+\varepsilon\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta}:\theta\in[-\pi/2,\pi/2]\}
,
\Gamma_{R}=\{x+R\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta}:\theta\in[-\pi+\delta,\pi-\delta]\}
,
where
\delta
is set so that
\Gamma_{R}
touches
\Gamma_{\pm}
at its endpoints.
By residue calculus,
\mathcal{K}^{\prime}[(z-x-1)^{-1}](x)=-\log(1+x-x)=0
, so we can assume that
a_{1}=0
. We calculate,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{\Gamma_{-}\cup\Gamma_{+}}\log(\xi-x)h(\xi)\,\mathrm{d}\xi

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{-\sqrt{R^{2}-\varepsilon^{2}}+x}^{x}\log(z-\mathrm{i}\varepsilon-x)h(z-\mathrm{i}\varepsilon)-\log(z+\mathrm{i}\varepsilon-x)h(z+\mathrm{i}\varepsilon)\,\mathrm{d}z

\displaystyle\to

\displaystyle-\int_{-\infty}^{x}h(z)\,\mathrm{d}z\qquad\text{ as }\varepsilon\to 0\text{ and then }R\to\infty,

because the jump across the negative axis for the logarithm is
2\pi\mathrm{i}
. We further calculate,

\displaystyle\left|\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{\Gamma_{\varepsilon}}\log(\xi-x)h(\xi)\,\mathrm{d}\xi\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{len}(\Gamma_{\varepsilon})}{2\pi}\sup_{\theta\in[-\pi/2,\pi/2]}|\log(\varepsilon\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta})h(x+\varepsilon\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta})|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\sqrt{\log^{2}(\varepsilon)+\frac{\pi^{2}}{4}}\sup_{\theta\in[-\pi/2,\pi/2]}|h(x+\varepsilon\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta})|,

which tends to zero as
\varepsilon\to 0
. Since
a_{1}=0
, we have that
h(\xi)=O(\xi^{-2})
as
|\xi|\to\infty
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{\Gamma_{R}}\log(\xi-x)h(\xi)\,\mathrm{d}\xi

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi+\delta}^{\pi-\delta}\log(R\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta})h(x+R\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta})R\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta}\,\mathrm{d}\theta,

which tends to zero as
\varepsilon\to 0
and then
R\to\infty
.
∎
