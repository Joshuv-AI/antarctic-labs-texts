Universal Reasoner: A Single, Composable Plug-and-Play Reasoner for Frozen LLMs
Jaemin Kim, Hangeol Chang, Hyunmin Hwang, Choonghan Kim, Jong Chul Ye

Abstract
Large Language Models (LLMs) have demonstrated remarkable general capabilities, but enhancing skills such as reasoning often demands substantial computational resources and may compromise generalization. While Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning (PEFT) methods offer a more resource-conscious alternative, they typically require retraining for each LLM backbone due to architectural dependencies. To address these challenges, we propose Universal Reasoner (UniR) —a modular, composable, and plug-and-play reasoning module that can be used with larger frozen LLMs to provide specialized reasoning capabilities with a shared or aligned token space. Specifically, UniR decomposes the reward into a standalone reasoning module trained in a decoupled manner using verifiable rewards, effectively translating trajectory-level signals into token-level guidance. Once trained, UniR is combined with frozen LLMs at inference time by simply adding its output logits to those of the backbone. This additive structure enables modular composition: multiple UniR modules trained for different tasks can be jointly applied by summing their logits, enabling complex reasoning via composition. Furthermore, UniR demonstrates weak-to-strong generalization, where reasoning modules trained on smaller models effectively guide much larger LLMs in the same model family, and generalize across domains such as in vision language models and medical reasoning. Experiments on mathematical reasoning and machine translation show that UniR surpasses existing fine-tuning methods. Code is open-sourced at https://github.com/hangeol/UniR

Introduction

Recent Large Language Models (LLMs), such as OpenAI-o1
(
OpenAI, 2024
)
, DeepSeek-R1
(
DeepSeek-AI, 2025
)
, Kimi-1.5
(
Team et al., 2025
)
, BOLT
(
Pang et al., 2025
)
, and Open-Reasoner-Zero
(
Hu et al., 2025
)
, have demonstrated remarkable capabilities in complex reasoning tasks. These models leverage Chain-of-Thought (CoT)
(
Wei et al., 2022
)
to elicit step-by-step reasoning processes from LLMs, thereby enhancing their ability to tackle intricate problems. A key driver of this progress has been Reinforcement Fine-Tuning (RFT), which aligns model outputs with desired criteria, often human preferences, through reinforcement learning such as PPO
(
Schulman et al., 2017
)
, DPO
(
Rafailov et al., 2023
)
, GRPO
(
Shao et al., 2024
)
, etc.

Unfortunately, adapting these powerful LLMs to specialize in specific domains or further enhance their reasoning abilities through RFT presents significant hurdles. The process is often prohibitively expensive due to the substantial computational and memory resources required for fine-tuning large-scale models. While Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning (PEFT) techniques like LoRA
(
Hu et al., 2022
)
have been proposed to mitigate these costs, they introduce their own limitations: (1) Model Dependency: LoRA modules strongly depend on specific model architectures, limiting their transferability across models of different sizes (e.g., from 3B to 14B). (2) Poor Linearity Support: Combining multiple domain-specific LoRA adapters through linear operations lacks robust theoretical support and frequently yields unpredictable outcomes
(
Wang et al., 2023
)
.

To address these challenges, we introduce
UniR
(
Uni
versal
R
easoner), a novel method that decouples reasoning capability into a specialized reasoning module, independently trained and designed to enhance the capabilities of a backbone LLM.
This reasoning module can be integrated into a backbone model without accessing its internal layers or parameter dimensions, provided that the two models share a tokenizer or that their token spaces are properly aligned.
This approach not only fosters modularity by preserving the core capabilities of the backbone model, but also enables effective weak-to-strong generalization, in which a compact reasoning module can enhance larger models. Moreover, this modularity opens avenues for combining distinct reasoning modules across multiple domains, such as visual mathematical reasoning and medical reasoning.
Overall, UniR offers a training- and transfer-efficient, adaptable, and robust solution for enhancing reasoning in LLMs without compromising their core capabilities.

The core innovation of UniR lies in its decoupling of reward model training from policy updates.
While many alignment methods rely on preference datasets, UniR is designed to leverage explicit,
predefined rewards that are often available for tasks with verifiable solutions, such as mathematical problem-solving or machine translation. We propose to model these typically trajectory-level rewards, or rewards over a full response, as a sum of log-probabilities derived from our decomposed reasoning module. This formulation effectively translates a global reward signal into a sequence of implicit token-level guidance signals that steer the reasoning process. The reasoning module is then trained to maximize this reward using a policy gradient algorithm, ensuring that only the smaller reasoning module is updated while the backbone policy (LLM) remains unchanged. During inference, reasoning module provides guidance to a backbone model by combining their outputs, typically by adding their respective logits before sampling.
Furthermore, because the guidance occurs at the logit level, multiple reasoning modules trained for distinct tasks can be composed through simple logit addition—enabling composite probabilistic reasoning without additional training or architectural modification. Our contributions are summarized as follows:

•

Transferability Across Model Sizes.

Although our approach trains only small models, the learned reasoning module can be effectively transferred to larger models at inference time. For example, a 1.5B reasoning module trained alongside a 3B backbone can be used in conjunction with a 14B model, providing token-level guidance that improves the larger model’s performance without requiring fine-tuning of the 14B model itself.

•

Composability.

Our method allows seamless composition of multiple small reasoning modules specialized for specific domains or tasks. These specialized models can then be selectively deployed based on the domain context, enabling modular and efficient inference pipelines where domain expertise is encapsulated in lightweight, task-specific policies.

•

Superior Performance on
Diverse Tasks.
Our experiments show that UniR substantially outperforms conventional fine-tuning methods, showcasing its efficacy and robustness in enhancing LLM capabilities on challenging mathematics, translation, and even medical benchmarks.

2
Related Work

Enhancing LLM Reasoning.

Improving LLM reasoning is key for complex reasoning and understanding
(
Durante et al., 2024
)
. One prominent strategy involves Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) on specialized, often small, datasets curated to foster reasoning skills;
these datasets are either distilled from larger models
(
Open Thoughts, 2025
)
or meticulously selected from high-quality examples
(
Muennighoff et al., 2025
;
Ye et al., 2025
)
.
Recently, Reinforcement Learning (RL)-based Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) and related methods
(
Shao et al., 2024
;
Liu et al., 2025b
)
have shown promise in enhancing LLM reasoning capabilities using rule-based rewards without requiring a separate value function. Furthermore, the concept of verifiable rewards is extended by using generative models to provide soft reward signals for unstructured tasks
(
Su et al., 2025b
)
.
Our work leverages verifiable rewards to improve the reasoning ability of LLMs by training a lightweight, specialized reasoning module for tasks that require complex reasoning.

Modular and Efficient Inference Guidance.

To adapt LLMs efficiently, Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning (PEFT) methods like LoRA
(
Hu et al., 2022
)
are widely adopted. However, PEFT modules are architecturally constrained by the model’s internal dimensions, which restricts their transferability across LLMs of varying scales.
This limitation has spurred interest in plug-and-play modularity and inference-time guidance, where a separate module steers a frozen backbone.
Early approaches like TART
(
Bhatia et al., 2023
)
attach a separate module to process embeddings from a frozen backbone, primarily for classification tasks. More recent works focus on generative capabilities. Reasoning Vectors
(
Zbeeb et al., 2025
)
utilizes Task Arithmetic
(
Ilharco et al., 2022
)
to modify the parameters of the target model by adding a vector derived from the difference between RL and SFT models. Recent techniques focus on finer-grained, token-level guidance
(
Xu et al., 2024b
;
Yang et al., 2023
;
Ouyang et al., 2025
)
. RAST
(
Ouyang et al., 2025
)
shares a similar logit-level motivation but employs a contrastive strategy, requiring the subtraction of logits from a small base model and its RL-trained counterpart to guide the target LLM (see Appendix
D.3
for empirical comparison). GenARM
(
Xu et al., 2024b
)
also employs a token-level guiding module; however, it relies on a surrogate reward model trained on human preferences. In contrast, UniR trains a reasoning module to maximize verifiable rewards. In addition, several works
(
Mudgal et al., 2023
;
Han et al., 2024
)
train a value function using temporal difference learning. Instead, UniR directly trains the reasoning module using a policy gradient algorithm, collaborating with a frozen backbone.

3
Problem Definition

A growing trend in enhancing LLM reasoning is Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR), where models are guided by predefined and automatically checkable signals, such as correctness or rule-based verification. This approach has been adopted in models such as DeepSeek-R1
(
DeepSeek-AI, 2025
)
, and recent RLVR research has demonstrated its effectiveness across diverse domains
(
Su et al., 2025a
;
Zhao et al., 2025
)
. Following this direction, we focus on tasks where performance can be assessed reliably via such verifiable rewards. We denote the predefined reward as
r(x,y)
for a given prompt
x
from a dataset
\mathcal{D}
and a generated output sequence
y
. Standard RL fine-tuning aims to find an optimal policy,
{\pi_{\theta}}^{*}(y|x)
, that maximizes the expected reward while remaining close to a backbone policy (
{\pi_{b}}(y|x)
), typically a pre-trained base LLM. This objective is formulated as:

{\small\max_{{\pi_{\theta}}}\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D},y\sim{\pi_{\theta}}(y|x)}[r(x,y)]-\beta{\mathbb{D}_{KL}}[{\pi_{\theta}}(y|x)||{\pi_{b}}(y|x)],}

(1)

where
{\mathbb{D}_{KL}}[\cdot||\cdot]
represents the Kullback-Leibler divergence, and
\beta>0
controls the strength of the regularization towards the backbone policy
{\pi_{b}}
. The optimal policy
{\pi_{\theta}}^{*}(y|x)
for this objective can be expressed in a closed form as
(
Ziebart et al., 2008
;
Rafailov et al., 2023
)
:

{\pi_{\theta}}^{*}(y|x)=\frac{1}{Z(x)}{\pi_{b}}(y|x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\right),

(2)

where
Z(x)=\sum_{y}{\pi_{b}}(y|x)\exp(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x,y))
is a partition function. A key challenge in directly applying Eq. (
2
) for autoregressive generation is that the trajectory-level reward
r(x,y)
can be obtained only after the full sequence
y
is complete. However, it does not provide token-specific feedback during the generation process. For fine-grained guidance of an LLM’s reasoning steps, per-token signals are more desirable. Theoretically, the optimal token-level decision rule for the objective in Eq. (
1
) can be expressed using the optimal soft Q-function,
Q^{*}(a_{t}|s_{t})
where
s_{t}=(x,y_{<t})
is the current state and
a_{t}=y_{t}
is the next token. This
Q^{*}(a_{t}|s_{t})
represents the maximum expected future return following the optimal policy
{\pi_{\theta}}^{*}
. The optimal token-level policy is then given by
(
Haarnoja et al., 2017
;
Ziebart et al., 2008
)
:

\displaystyle\log{\pi_{\theta}}^{*}(y_{t}|x,y_{<t})=

\displaystyle\log{\pi_{b}}(y_{t}|x,y_{<t})+

(3)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\beta}Q^{*}(y_{t}|x,y_{<t})-\log Z^{\prime}(x,y_{<t}),

where
Z^{\prime}(x,y_{<t})
is the per-step partition function
(
Uehara et al., 2025
;
Haarnoja et al., 2017
)
. However, guiding with
Q^{*}
is impractical since it requires knowledge of the optimal policy
{\pi_{\theta}}^{*}
itself. This challenge motivates the need to bridge observable trajectory-level rule-based rewards with a practical formulation that enables effective token-level guidance.

4
UniR: Universal Reasoner

To circumvent the direct estimation of
Q^{*}
, our core idea is to learn a separate, typically smaller, reasoning module, denoted as
{\pi_{r}}(y|x)
with parameters
\phi
. This policy is specifically trained to capture the desired reasoning or task-specific behaviors that lead to high rewards. We then use this learned reasoning module to guide a larger LLM.

4.1
Modeling Trajectory Rewards via the Reasoning Module

To connect trajectory-level reward
r(x,y)
to our reasoning module
{\pi_{r}}
and enable its use in a token-by-token guidance mechanism, we posit that the reward can be represented by the sum of log-probabilities of the reasoning module
{\pi_{r}}
:

\frac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)=\sum_{t=1}^{|y|}\log{\pi_{r}}(y_{t}|x,y_{<t};\phi).

(4)

Here,
\beta
is the same temperature parameter from Eq. (
1
). This formulation implies that sequences
y
assigned a higher sum of token-level log-probabilities by
{\pi_{r}}
should correspond to sequences achieving higher rewards
r(x,y)
. The feasibility of representing a reward function via the log-likelihood of a policy is supported by theoretical work in
(
Xu et al., 2024b
)
, which claims that reward can be represented using the parameterization
\log{\pi_{r}}(y|x)
for some probability distribution
{\pi_{r}}(y|x)
. This assumption is further supported by recent works
(
Li et al., 2025
;
Wulfmeier et al., 2024
)
that language models trained via next-token prediction implicitly learn endogenous reward signals.

Since
\log{\pi_{r}}(y|x)=\sum_{t=1}^{|y|}\log{\pi_{r}}(y_{t}|x,y_{<t})
by the chain rule, this suggests that a sufficiently expressive reasoning module
{\pi_{r}}
can indeed model any trajectory-level reward function
r(x,y)
.
By substituting the definition from Eq. (
4
) into the optimal policy form of Eq. (
2
), we derive the structure of our guided policy for autoregressive generation of the next token
y_{t}
. This translates to combining their next-token predictions:

\displaystyle\log{\pi_{\theta}}(y_{t}|x,y_{<t})=

\displaystyle\log{\pi_{b}}(y_{t}|x,y_{<t})+

(5)

\displaystyle\log{\pi_{r}}(y_{t}|x,y_{<t})-\log Z^{\prime}(x,y_{<t}),

where
Z^{\prime}(x,y_{<t})
is the per-step partition function. Accordingly, the logits from the frozen backbone
{\pi_{b}}
and the learned module
{\pi_{r}}
can be separable to produce the logits for the guided policy
{\pi_{\theta}}
.

This additive and separable guidance mechanism extends to scenarios involving multiple reward criteria. Instead of training a single monolithic model to jointly optimize for all objectives, UniR allows for the training of several distinct reasoning modules
\{{\pi_{r}}^{1},\cdots,{\pi_{r}}^{N}\}
, each specialized for a different reward function
r_{i}(x,y)
. At inference, these specialized modules can be combined by taking a weighted sum of their logits and adding them to the backbone model’s logits:

\displaystyle\log{\pi_{\theta}}(y_{t}|x,y_{<t})\propto

\displaystyle\log{\pi_{b}}(y_{t}|x,y_{<t})+

(6)

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}\alpha_{i}\log{\pi_{r}}^{i}(y_{t}|x,y_{<t}),

where
\alpha_{i}\geq 0
controls the influence of each reasoning module
{\pi_{r}}^{i}
. This approach offers flexibility, as the weights
\alpha_{i}
can be adjusted at inference time to explore dynamic optimization across multiple objectives without any retraining. This additive combination of logits is not a heuristic but the principled solution to a standard KL-regularized optimization problem,
\max_{{\pi_{\theta}}}\mathbb{E}_{y\sim{\pi_{\theta}}}[\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}r_{i}(x,y)]-{\mathbb{D}_{KL}}({\pi_{\theta}}\|{\pi_{b}})
, a common framework for multi-objective alignment
(
Abdolmaleki et al., 2020
;
Dekoninck et al., 2023
;
Xu et al., 2024b
)
. The goal is to find the optimal policy
{\pi_{\theta}}(y|x)\propto{\pi_{b}}(y|x)\prod_{i}\exp(\frac{1}{\beta}\alpha_{i}r_{i}(x,y))
. By substituting our reward definition from Eq. (
4
), taking the logarithm of this solution directly yields the linear combination of logits in Eq. (
6
).

4.2
Training
{\pi_{r}}
with GRPO

The parameters
\phi
of the reasoning module
{\pi_{r}}
are trained to maximize the expected predefined reward
r(x,y)
, not the sum of its own log-probabilities defined in Eq. (
4
). We employ Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)
(
Shao et al., 2024
)
for this purpose. For each input
x\sim\mathcal{D}
, we generate a group of
G
candidate responses
\{y^{1},\cdots,y^{G}\}
by sampling from
{\pi_{\theta}}(y^{i}|x;\phi_{old})
, where
\phi_{old}
denotes the parameters of the reasoning module from the previous iteration. For each generated sequence
y^{i}
, we compute its external reward
r(x,y^{i})
(denoted
r_{i}
for brevity) and then calculate the normalized advantage
A_{i}
:

\small A_{i}=\frac{r_{i}-\text{mean}(\{r_{1},r_{2},\cdots,r_{G}\})}{\text{std}(\{r_{1},r_{2},\cdots,r_{G}\})}.

(7)

In GRPO, the objective function for updating the parameters
\phi
of the reasoning module is then:

\displaystyle\small\mathcal{L}_{\text{GRPO}}(\phi)=

\displaystyle-\mathbb{E}_{x,y^{i}}\Bigg[\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}\Bigg\{\min\Bigg(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y^{i}|x)}{\pi_{\theta}(y^{i}|x;\phi_{old})}A_{i},\;

(8)

\displaystyle\text{clip}\left(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y^{i}|x)}{\pi_{\theta}(y^{i}|x;\phi_{old})},\;1-\epsilon,\;1+\epsilon\right)A_{i}\Bigg)

\displaystyle-\beta_{KL}{\mathbb{D}_{KL}}[{\pi_{\theta}}(y^{i}|x;\phi)||{\pi_{\theta}}(y^{i}|x;\phi_{0})]\Bigg\}\Bigg].

Here,
\epsilon
is the clipping hyperparameter from PPO-clip,
\phi_{0}
is initial value of
\phi
, and
\beta_{KL}
controls the KL-regularization term.
The learning direction is determined by the ratio
\frac{{\pi_{\theta}}(y^{i}|x,\phi)}{{\pi_{\theta}}(y^{i}|x;\phi_{old})}
, which simplifies to
\frac{{\pi_{r}}(y^{i}|x,\phi)}{{\pi_{r}}(y^{i}|x;\phi_{old})}
as the terms from the
{\pi_{b}}
cancel out, thereby the gradient
\nabla_{\phi}\mathcal{L}_{\text{GRPO}}
only affects the parameters of
{\pi_{r}}
since
{\pi_{b}}
is frozen.

4.3
Interpreting
{\pi_{r}}
with
Q^{*}

Drawing on recent theoretical advances
(
Xu et al., 2024b
)
, we assert that for any reward
r(x,y)
and coefficient
\beta
, there exists an equivalent reward function
\hat{r}(x,y)
—inducing the identical optimal policy—that can be exactly decomposed into token-level log-probabilities of a distribution
{\pi_{r}}
. We provide the formal statement and proof in Lemma
A.1
. Given this valid decomposition, we show that optimizing
{\pi_{r}}
via GRPO to maximize the reward implicitly forces
{\pi_{r}}
to learn the optimal soft Q-function.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
{\pi_{\theta}}^{*}
be the unique optimal policy for the KL-regularized objective in Eq. (
1
). If the reasoning module
{\pi_{r}}
is trained such that the guided policy
{\pi_{\theta}}
converges to
{\pi_{\theta}}^{*}
, then the log-probability of the reasoning module at each step recovers the scaled optimal soft Q-function:

\log{\pi_{r}}(y_{t}|x,y_{<t})=\frac{1}{\beta}Q^{*}(y_{t}|x,y_{<t}),

(9)

where
Q^{*}(y_{t}|x,y_{<t})
is the optimal soft Q-function corresponding to the reward
r(x,y)
.

Proof.

See Appendix
A
for the detailed proof.
∎

Importantly, this interpretation does not require
{\pi_{r}}
to be a strong standalone language model or a near-optimal policy by itself. The backbone
{\pi_{b}}
supplies the general language prior, while
{\pi_{r}}
contributes reward-aligned token-level directions through logit modulation.

Empirical Validation.

While Theorem
4.1
relies on the assumption of convergence to the optimal policy, strictly checking this equality is intractable. However, the theorem implies a testable hypothesis: if
{\pi_{r}}
effectively approximates the scaled
Q^{*}
, it should assign higher summed log-probabilities (
\sum_{t}\log{\pi_{r}}
) to trajectories with high rewards (
r=1
) compared to those with low rewards (
r=0
).
To validate this, we analyze the generated responses on the GSM8k test set. As detailed in Appendix
E.1
, we observe a strong alignment where correct responses receive significantly higher scores. Notably, this separation becomes more pronounced after UniR training. This empirical finding suggests that the reasoning module successfully internalizes the reward signal, supporting our theoretical framework.

5.1
Experimental Settings

We evaluate UniR on two tasks: mathematical problem-solving and machine translation. Mathematical problem-solving requires explicit, step-by-step reasoning, while machine translation involves implicit reasoning based on domain-specific knowledge and semantic understanding. These tasks provide complementary benchmarks for assessing our decomposed reasoning module.

Models and Baselines.
Our experiments utilize models from Qwen2.5
(
Yang et al., 2024a
)
and Llama3.2
(
Grattafiori et al., 2024
)
. We train the reasoning module
{\pi_{r}}
alongside a 3B-scale backbone
{\pi_{b}}
in two settings: (1)
{\pi_{b}}
as Qwen2.5-3B with
{\pi_{r}}
as Qwen2.5-1.5B, and (2)
{\pi_{b}}
as Llama3.2-3B with
{\pi_{r}}
as Llama3.2-1B. For the transferability experiment, we use Qwen2.5-14B and Llama3.1-8B as backbone models. Unless stated otherwise, all models are Instruct variants. For baselines, we apply GRPO to fine-tune the entire backbone model (e.g., the 3B-scale
{\pi_{b}}
model) directly on each task using the same predefined rewards. Baseline tuning is implemented through either full model tuning or LoRA-based fine-tuning.

Dataset and Reward Formulation.

For mathematical reasoning tasks, we use the Math-12k
(
Lightman et al., 2023
;
Liu et al., 2025b
)
and GSM8k
(
Cobbe et al., 2021
)
datasets. The reward

is determined by the rule-based correctness of the final answer, following the methodology in Dr.GRPO
(
Liu et al., 2025b
)
. For machine translation, we use the IWSLT 2017 dataset
(
Cettolo et al., 2017
)
for English-to-German and German-to-English translation, employing BLEU
(
Papineni et al., 2002
)
and CometKiwi
(
Rei et al., 2022
)
as reward during training. Following MT-R1-Zero
(
Feng et al., 2025
)
, we combine lexical and semantic metrics for balanced training.

Evaluation Metrics.
We evaluate mathematical reasoning on five benchmarks: GSM8k, MATH-500
(
Lightman et al., 2023
)
, AIME24

)

5.2
Enhancing Reasoning Ability

Superior Performance under Stochastic Decoding.

As shown in Table
2
, our framework consistently achieves substantial improvements over GRPO baselines across both in-distribution and out-of-distribution benchmarks. Specifically, with the Llama backbone, UniR achieves an average score of 33.3, surpassing GRPO Full (25.4). Similarly, with the Qwen backbone, UniR achieves 42.7, outperforming the baselines. This trend extends to machine translation (Table
2
), where UniR improves BLEU scores by
+1.77
(EN
\rightarrow
DE) over GRPO Full, confirming its generalization capability beyond explicit reasoning tasks.

Robustness to Decoding Strategies.

A critical observation in our experiments explains the performance gap compared to reported baselines in other literature. We observed that standard GRPO exhibits high sensitivity to sampling temperature. To investigate this, we compared performance under greedy decoding (
T=0
) versus sampling (
T=0.6
).
As shown in Appendix Table
17
and
17
,
while GRPO Full achieves competitive scores at
T=0
(e.g., reaching 77.3% on GSM8k with Llama), its performance degrades significantly as temperature increases. In contrast, UniR maintains robust performance with minimal degradation. For instance, in the Llama setting, GRPO Full drops from 42.4% (
T=0
) to 36.1% (
T=0.6
) on MATH-500, whereas UniR demonstrates stability, achieving 49.2% (
T=0
) and 48.8% (
T=0.6
). This suggests that fully fine-tuned baselines tend to suffer from mode collapse—overfitting to a narrow, high-reward trajectory that becomes brittle under stochastic sampling. In contrast, UniR preserves the frozen backbone as a robust prior to maintain diversity while applying logit modulation to steer the reasoning process, offering a more reliable solution for realistic deployment scenarios.

Necessity of Reward Alignment.
To verify that the gains stem from our reward optimization and not merely ensemble effects, we compare UniR against a naive logit ensemble (Backbone + Pre-trained Module). As shown in the results, the naive ensemble performs worse than the UniR method. This highlights the need for our reward alignment process, which is crucial for transforming the reasoning module into an effective guide.

To investigate the advantages of integrating multiple specialized reasoning capabilities enabled by UniR, we design a composite task: translating German math problems from the MGSM dataset

{\pi_{b}}

\log{\pi_{b}}+\alpha\log\pi^{\text{Math}}_{r}+(1-\alpha)\log\pi^{\text{Trans}}_{r},

(10)

where

\alpha=0

Beyond this baseline clarification, we observe that increasing the weight

For the LOS task, UniR achieves an accuracy of 67.77% and an F1-score of 16.25%, representing a dramatic improvement over the baseline of 9.64% and 5.92%, respectively. Moreover, we evaluate composability of our framework in the medical setting.
While this composition decreases accuracy compared to using each module individually, it consistently improves F1-scores. Furthermore, the combined inference generalizes to the unseen mortality prediction task, which is designed for predicting mortality in binary classification, yielding substantial gains over the baseline without task-specific training. These results suggest that complementary knowledge from different tasks can be integrated, which is beneficial in medical reasoning as their combination provides a richer basis for risk estimation, extending even to mortality. 6
Conclusion

We introduce Universal Reasoner (UniR), a modular plug-and-play module that enhances LLM reasoning by directly optimizing verifiable rewards. By decoupling reasoning from the backbone, UniR not only demonstrates superior robustness compared to standard RL fine-tuning but also achieves strong transferability across model scales. Furthermore, UniR facilitates the combination of modules specialized for different tasks. UniR demonstrates generalization across different modalities and domains, highlighting its potential to integrate multiple domain-specific modules. We provide a detailed discussion on limitations in Appendix
C
.

Impact Statement

This paper presents work whose goal is to advance the field of machine learning, specifically focusing on efficient and modular reasoning for Large Language Models (LLMs). While we demonstrate the efficacy of UniR in medical reasoning, we emphasize that these experiments are strictly for methodological validation. Models enhanced via our framework should not be deployed in clinical or critical decision-making environments without rigorous safety testing, potential bias mitigation, and human oversight, as they rely on the underlying frozen backbone which may still exhibit hallucinations or inherent biases.

Appendix A
Theoretical Justification

A.1
Reward Parameterization

To justify the reward parameterization in Eq. (
4
), which decomposes the trajectory-level reward function into a token-level sum, we first formally prove that this parameterization is equivalent and expressive for any reward function within our optimization objective.

Lemma A.1

(Theorem 3 in
Xu et al. (2024b)
)
.

For any trajectory-level reward function
r(x,y)
and any
\beta>0
, there exists an equivalent reward function
\hat{r}(x,y)
and a probability distribution
{\pi_{r}}(y|x)
such that
\hat{r}(x,y)=\beta\log{\pi_{r}}(y|x)
, where
r(x,y)
and
\hat{r}(x,y)
induce the identical optimal policy
{\pi_{\theta}}^{*}
for the KL-regularized objective in Eq. (
1
).

Proof.

As shown in Eq. (
2
), the unique optimal policy
{\pi_{\theta}}^{*}
for the KL-regularized objective has the closed-form solution. Following Theorem 3 in
Xu et al. (2024b)
, we define two reward functions
r(x,y)
and
\hat{r}(x,y)
as equivalent if they induce the same optimal policy. This holds if they differ only by a function
f(x)
that is independent of the trajectory
y
:

\displaystyle\hat{r}(x,y)=r(x,y)-f(x).

(11)

This is because the
f(x)
term is absorbed into the partition function
Z(x)
.

Our goal is to find a function
f(x)
that allows
\hat{r}(x,y)
to be written in the form
\beta\log{\pi_{r}}(y|x)
for some valid probability distribution
{\pi_{r}}(y|x)
. Let us define a probability distribution
{\pi_{r}}(y|x)
using the given reward function
r(x,y)
:

\displaystyle{\pi_{r}}(y|x):=\frac{\exp(r(x,y)/\beta)}{\sum_{y^{\prime}}\exp(r(x,y^{\prime})/\beta)}

(12)

By construction,
\sum_{y}{\pi_{r}}(y|x)=1
, so
{\pi_{r}}
is a valid probability distribution.

Let us define our new reward function
\hat{r}(x,y)
using this
{\pi_{r}}(y|x)
, as required by the lemma:

\displaystyle\hat{r}(x,y):=\beta\log{\pi_{r}}(y|x).

(13)

Substitute the definition of
{\pi_{r}}(y|x)
into this equation:

\displaystyle\hat{r}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\beta\log\left(\frac{\exp(r(x,y)/\beta)}{\sum_{y^{\prime}}\exp(r(x,y^{\prime})/\beta)}\right)

(14)

\displaystyle=\beta\left[\log\left(\exp\left(\frac{r(x,y)}{\beta}\right)\right)-\log\left(\sum_{y^{\prime}}\exp\left(\frac{r(x,y^{\prime})}{\beta}\right)\right)\right]

(15)

\displaystyle=r(x,y)-\beta\log\left(\sum_{y^{\prime}}\exp\left(\frac{r(x,y^{\prime})}{\beta}\right)\right)

(16)

\displaystyle=r(x,y)-f(x),

(17)

where
f(x)=\beta\log\left(\sum_{y^{\prime}}\exp(r(x,y^{\prime})/\beta)\right)
. Therefore,
\hat{r}(x,y)
and
r(x,y)
are equivalent, as they induce the identical optimal policy
\pi_{\theta}^{*}
. This proves that any reward function
r(x,y)
has an equivalent counterpart
\hat{r}(x,y)
that can be represented as a scaled log-probability of a distribution
{\pi_{r}}
.

∎

Corollary A.2
.

The trajectory-level reward
\hat{r}(x,y)
proven in Lemma
A.1
can be decomposed into a sum of token-level log-probabilities.

Proof.

By the chain rule of probability, trajectory-level distribution
{\pi_{r}}(y|x)
can be decomposed as:

\displaystyle\log{\pi_{r}}(y|x)=\sum_{t=1}^{|y|}\log{\pi_{r}}(y_{t}|x,y_{<t}).

(18)

Substituting this into the result from Lemma
A.1
:

\displaystyle\hat{r}(x,y)=\beta\log{\pi_{r}}(y|x)=\beta\sum_{t=1}^{|y|}\log{\pi_{r}}(y_{t}|x,y_{<t}).

(19)

Dividing by
\beta
, we arrive at the form used in our paper Eq. (
4
):

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\beta}\hat{r}(x,y)=\sum_{t=1}^{|y|}\log{\pi_{r}}(y_{t}|x,y_{<t}).

(20)

This formally justifies that our parameterization in Eq. (
4
) is not a limiting assumption, but a fully general way to represent any reward function for our objective.
∎

A.2
Interpretation of
{\pi_{r}}
at the Optimum

In this section, we derive the token-level structure of the optimal policy
\pi_{\theta}^{*}
and use it to motivate the design of our guided policy
\pi_{\theta}
.

From Lemma
A.1
and Corollary
A.2
, we substitute the equivalent reward
r(x,y)
(omitting the
\hat{\cdot}
for clarity) its token-decomposed log-probability from Eq. (
2
):

\displaystyle\pi_{\theta}^{*}(y|x)

\displaystyle\propto\pi_{b}(y|x)\exp\left(\sum_{t=1}^{|y|}\log{\pi_{r}}(y_{t}|x,y_{<t})\right)

(21)

\displaystyle=\pi_{b}(y|x)\prod_{t=1}^{|y|}{\pi_{r}}(y_{t}|x,y_{<t})

(22)

\displaystyle=\prod_{t=1}^{|y|}\pi_{b}(y_{t}|x,y_{<t}){\pi_{r}}(y_{t}|x,y_{<t})

(23)

By definition, the trajectory-level policy
\pi_{\theta}^{*}(y|x)
is also a product of its own token-level conditional policies:
\pi_{\theta}^{*}(y|x)=\prod_{t}\pi_{\theta}^{*}(y_{t}|x,y_{<t})
. By matching the terms inside the product for each step
t
, we derive the token-level optimal policy defined in Eq. (
3
).

Motivated by this derivation, we define our trainable policy
\pi_{\theta}
to have this exact mathematical structure, but replacing the ideal
{\pi_{r}}
with our learnable reasoning module
{\pi_{r}}(\cdot;\phi)
in Eq. (
5
).

Theorem A.3
.

Let
\pi_{\theta}^{*}
be the unique optimal policy for the KL-regularized objective Eq. (
1
) with reward
r(x,y)
. The token-level distribution of
\pi_{\theta}^{*}
is given by Eq. (
3
):

\displaystyle\log\pi_{\theta}^{*}(y_{t}|x,y_{<t})=\log\pi_{b}(y_{t}|x,y_{<t})+\frac{1}{\beta}Q^{*}(y_{t}|x,y_{<t})-\log Z^{\prime}(x,y_{<t}),

(24)

where
Q^{*}(y_{t}|x,y_{<t})
is the optimal soft Q-function for the reward
r(x,y)
and
Z^{\prime}
is the per-step partition function. As the KL-regularized objective drives
\pi_{\theta}\to\pi_{\theta}^{*}
, we can formally interpret the trained reasoning module’s output as the scaled optimal soft Q-function:

\displaystyle\log{\pi_{r}}(y_{t}|x,y_{<t})=\frac{1}{\beta}Q^{*}(y_{t}|x,y_{<t})

(25)

Proof.

The objective in Eq. (
1
) has a unique optimal solution
\pi_{\theta}^{*}
. Let us assume the optimization is successful and
\pi_{\theta}
has converged to
\pi_{\theta}^{*}
:

\displaystyle\log\pi_{\theta}(y_{t}|x,y_{<t};\phi)=\log\pi_{\theta}^{*}(y_{t}|x,y_{<t}).

(26)

Now, substitute the definitions from Eq. (
3
) and Eq. (
5
) into this equality. Then, we get:

\displaystyle\log{\pi_{r}}(y_{t}|x,y_{<t};\phi)=\frac{1}{\beta}Q^{*}(y_{t}|x,y_{<t}).

(27)

This proves that the our reasoning module
{\pi_{r}}(\cdot;\phi)
is learning to approximate the scaled optimal soft Q-function via the KL-regularized optimization.
∎

B.3.3

Collaborative decoding is a technique that improves generation by combining outputs, such as probability distributions or logits, from multiple models at each step. Building on Speculative Decoding
(
Leviathan et al., 2023
)
, Collaborative decoding via Speculation (CoS)
(
Fu et al., 2025
)
theoretically guarantees that the inference speed is never slower than standard collaborative decoding. Since our UniR framework utilizes the summation of logits, we seamlessly apply the CoS approach to enhance inference speed. We employ the Llama model family and the reasoning module
\pi_{r}^{Math}
trained on GSM8k dataset. To evaluate inference speed, we generate 100 tokens and measure the average token generation time across the entire GSM8k test benchmark.

Appendix C
Limitations

Our framework has three primary limitations. First, as a guidance module, UniR’s performance is inherently bounded by the latent capabilities of the frozen backbone model. Second, while additive composition is grounded in multi-objective optimization, combining modules with conflicting reward signals may lead to negative interference, as analyzed in Appendix
J.5
. Finally, although UniR is designed to be architecture-agnostic, tokenizer mismatch presents a practical challenge. Our cross-tokenizer experiment using EVA alignment (Appendix
D.2
) demonstrated that while imperfect vocabulary mapping limits absolute performance, UniR successfully transfers reasoning patterns. We view the development of more robust tokenizer alignment techniques as a promising direction to fully unlock UniR’s cross-architecture potential.

To further validate the effectiveness of UniR, we compare it against inference-time guidance approaches: Reasoning Vectors
(
Zbeeb et al., 2025
)
and RAST
(
Ouyang et al., 2025
)
. These methods represent the most relevant methodological alternatives to our framework as they also aim to transfer reasoning capabilities to a target model without full fine-tuning.

Reasoning Vectors:
This approach utilizes Task Arithmetic
(
Ilharco et al., 2022
)
to manipulate model weights directly. Following the original methodology, we define the reasoning vector
\tau
as the difference between the parameters of a Reinforcement Learning model (
\theta_{RL}
) and a Supervised Fine-Tuning model (
\theta_{SFT}
) (or base model). This vector is added to the backbone parameters with a scaling factor
\lambda
:
\theta_{new}=\theta_{base}+\lambda(\theta_{RL}-\theta_{SFT})
. In our setup, we used the GRPO-trained model as
\theta_{RL}
and the backbone as
\theta_{SFT}
, with a scaling factor of
\lambda=2
.

RAST:
RAST applies guidance in the logit space but relies on a contrastive strategy. It computes a logit difference by subtracting the logits of a small base model from those of its RL-trained counterpart. This difference is then added to the target model’s logits. This approach requires maintaining two auxiliary models (small base + small RL) during inference.

We controlled for model scale to ensure a fair comparison. All methods utilized Qwen2.5-3B as the target backbone model and Qwen2.5-1.5B variants (base or GRPO-trained) as the guidance. The GRPO training settings for the source models were identical to those used for UniR. The results on five mathematical benchmarks are summarized in Table
11
.
UniR achieves an average accuracy of 42.7%, outperforming both Reasoning Vectors (37.8%) and RAST (36.3%).

E.1
Analysis of the Reward Decomposition

To empirically validate our core assumption that the trajectory-level reward
r(x,y)
can be expressed as the sum of log-probabilities from the reasoning module
{\pi_{r}}
(Eq.
4
), we evaluate our Llama-based model on the GSM8k test set. For each generated response, we compute
\sum_{t}\log{\pi_{r}}(y_{t}|x,y_{<t})
using the reasoning module and group the responses according to whether the final answer is correct (
r=1
) or incorrect (
r=0
). To examine the effect of training, we compare outputs from the pre-trained module and the UniR-trained module.

As shown in Figure
5
, correct responses consistently achieve higher summed log-probabilities than incorrect ones in both settings. This correlation provides empirical evidence that the reasoning module internalizes the external reward signal by assigning higher probability to trajectories that lead to correct outcomes. Moreover, while the pre-trained module already shows moderate separation between correct and incorrect responses, this separation becomes substantially more pronounced after UniR training, indicating that our framework strengthens the module’s ability to distinguish successful reasoning paths from unsuccessful ones.

F.1
Computational Efficiency in Training

VRAM Usage.
Due to its structural decomposability, our framework substantially reduces VRAM usage by avoiding gradient updates to the large backbone model. While LoRA also reduces memory usage by restricting updates to a small subset of parameters, it still incurs substantial activation memory costs since the full computation graph must be stored for backpropagation
(
Zhang et al., 2023
)
. In contrast, our approach updates only the lightweight reasoning module, drastically minimizing activation memory requirements and enabling significantly larger batch sizes or sequence lengths under the same hardware constraints.

We examine peak memory usage as a function of the number of tokens processed per step. Input length was fixed to 250 tokens (plus up to 5 output tokens), and batch size was gradually increased under an 80GB VRAM limit. As shown in Figure
6
, UniR supports batch sizes up to 128, whereas LoRA and full fine-tuning are limited to 64 and 32, respectively. Although sequence length was fixed in this experiment, activation memory generally scales with both batch size and sequence length, making UniR more flexible for large-scale training. Moreover, UniR remains compatible with efficiency techniques such as quantization, caching, and adapter-based methods (e.g., LoRA applied to the reasoning module itself).

F.2
Reward Stabilization and Training Dynamics

Reward variance.
A notable empirical finding during training is that UniR not only achieves higher rewards compared to GRPO but also exhibits a smaller reward variance, as shown in Figure
7
. Since the advantage calculation in GRPO normalizes the unbiased advantage
r_{i}-\text{mean}(\{r_{1},\dots,r_{G}\})
by its standard deviation (Eq. (
7
)), lower reward variance amplifies the magnitude of the effective advantage
A_{i}
. This means even small deviations from the mean reward translate into strong learning signals.

Gradient norm.
We further analyze the gradient norm dynamics of UniR and GRPO Full over training steps on GSM8K and Math-500 using Qwen model. Specifically, we compute the average gradient norm every 100 training steps and report the maximum value within each 100-step window in parentheses in Table
13
. These results demonstrate that UniR avoids the gradient explosions observed in GRPO Full, highlighting its training stability across both datasets.

These dynamics relate to the “Question-level difficulty bias” identified by
(
Liu et al., 2025b
)
, where highly consistent rewards induce strong learning directions and thus lead to unstable policy updates. Indeed, GRPO often suffers from training instabilities when encountering policy collapse that can result in overfitting on
too easy
or
too hard
samples. By maintaining a fixed backbone model
{\pi_{b}}
, UniR avoids such collapses and produces consistent responses with lower reward variance. This structural stability allows UniR to turn strong learning signals into constructive updates, rather than unstable divergence.

In this section, we examine the effectiveness of employing a pre-trained small reasoning model, specifically DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-1.5B, directly as the reasoning module
\pi_{r}
within the UniR framework. Unlike our main experiments where
\pi_{r}
is trained from a base model using verifiable rewards, this analysis explores whether a model already specialized via supervised fine-tuning (SFT) can serve as an effective guide.

As shown in Table
18
, using the distilled 1.5B reasoning model to guide the Qwen2.5-3B backbone improves mathematical reasoning performance even without additional UniR-specific training. Furthermore, when we apply UniR fine-tuning to this pre-trained module, the performance further increases. This result demonstrates that UniR is not only effective for training reasoning capabilities from scratch but can also be used as a powerful alignment tool to further enhance existing specialized models.

Appendix H
Declaration of LLM Usage

Our core methodology involves utilizing pre-trained LLMs in two key roles: (1) as a frozen backbone/reference model (
{\pi_{b}}
) whose generation process is guided, and (2) potentially as the architecture for the smaller, trainable reasoning policy (
{\pi_{r}}
) itself. The interaction between these LLM components, specifically the combination of their logits to form a guided policy, is a central and original part of our proposed method. We also use LLMs as evaluation methods, as described in the paper.
In addition, LLMs were used during the writing stage only for language polishing and expression refinement. They were not used for research ideation, methodological design, or initial draft writing. All conceptual contributions and scientific content are solely the responsibility of the authors.

Appendix I
Prompt Template

For all models in the Llama family, we modified the default chat template by removing the knowledge cutoff and the generation time, as these fields vary depending on the time of execution and thus hinder reproducibility, and are also irrelevant to the task. For models in the Qwen family, however, we retained the default chat template as provided.

The prompt template for mathematical problems follows the style of DeepSeek-R1
(

, we instructed the model to output its answer within a boxed LaTeX environment. This format was adopted to
ensure that various valid answer formats were correctly recognized and evaluated.

I.2

For translation, inspired by the findings of MT-R1-Zero
(

I.3
Compositional Task.

We instructed the model to solve math problems written in German and to perform reasoning and solving in English. The reasoning process was placed within
<
think
>
tags, and the final answer was enclosed within
<

>
tags, as illustrated in Figure
13
. Using the prompt shown in Figure
14
, we evaluated the responses with GPT-4.1-nano-2025-04-14 on two dimensions: translation quality and math accuracy. Translation quality measures how well the response was translated into English, while math accuracy assesses accuracy using ground truth for a given problem.

While rule-based evaluation is often used for math accuracy due to the structured format of answers, we chose to use an LLM-based evaluator. This is because the math module is explicitly trained to follow a specific output format, whereas the translation module is not. Therefore, using a rule-based approach would unfairly favor the math side. To ensure a fair comparison between translation and problem-solving accuracy, we opt for GPT-4.1-nano as an impartial judge capable of understanding semantic correctness beyond strict formatting.
