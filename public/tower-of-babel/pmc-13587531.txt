Collective capacity building for emotionally charged work: a grounded theory of strategies for maintaining meaning and well-being in emotionally charged work
Authors: Sofie Østergaard Jaspers, Sara Wienke Christiansen, Emma Dybdal Andersen, Anne-Marie Martin, Ida E H Madsen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeEmotionally charged work—encompassing emotional demands and emotional labour - has been associated with ill health and alienation. While the consequences of such work has been widely theorized, the prevention of negative consequences remains undertheorized.MethodsIn this study, we adopt a classical grounded theory approach to (a) Explore the main concern of employed people regarding emotionally charged work and (b) Generate a grounded theory that could inform sustainable organization of emotionally charged work and the prevention of compassion fatigue. Initial purposeful sampling included eight employees and managers from psychiatric hospitals. As subsequent theoretical sampling required broader diversity, we conducted interviews with 15 additional participants across varied occupations, including ministers of religion, military personnel, social care workers, educators, IT managers, and others.ResultsUsing open coding and constant comparison, we developed a grounded theory of collective capacity building for emotionally charged work. We found that the main concern of participants was to maintain meaningful working lives and in the context of emotionally charged work, they achieved this by building collective capacity for this work.ConclusionOur findings offer theoretical and practical guidance for sustainably organizing work in occupations marked by high levels of emotionally charged work.Keywords: Burnout, Classic Grounded Theory, emotional demands, emotional labour, job demands and resource theory, meaning in work, organizational prevention of emotional demands

Introduction

The emotional demands, emotional labour, critical incidents, moral distress, and compassion fatigue of emotionally charged work have been linked to poor mental health in previous research (Framke et al., 2021, 2023; Grandey & Gabriel, 2015; Madsen et al., 2022; Møller et al., 2023; Pihl-Thingvad et al., 2022). European data show that emotionally charged work is highly prevalent in occupations involving contact with students, patients or other types of clients, and relational work. Common occupations include the pedagogical sector, healthcare and social care services, among social workers, within education, as well as in policing, prisons, and emergency services (Sjöberg, 2025). More detailed data from a Danish national survey of around 30,000 employees show that in these occupations, between 26% and 53% reported experiencing high emotional demands at work (Arbejdstilsynet, 2023).

Emotionally charged work can be seen as a broader concept encompassing different traditions for studying emotions at work by drawing on affective events theory (Weiss & Cropanzano, 1996). According to affective events theory, “environmental features influence affect primarily making affective events […] more or less likely” (Weiss & Cropanzano, 1996, p.11). Furthermore, the affective reactions to these events depend on patterns in interpretation of these events that are generalisable across individuals.

Until now the main part of theory in regards to emotionally charged work have focused on the consequences of this type of work, such as stress and burnout. However, as these conditions are widely prevalent in the European workforce (Belli & De Keulenaer, 2025), and to guide preventive efforts, there is a need to shift the focus from describing the potential negative outcomes of emotionally charged work towards theorizing a sustainable way of organizing this work that forms the pillars of many societies. In the following we briefly present the current dominant theoretical perspectives on the possible consequences of emotionally charged work, and thereafter move on to possible theoretical perspectives to guide prevention in terms of sustainable organization of this work.

Current theoretical perspectives on consequences of emotionally charged work

Emotional labour and emotional demands

The probably most influential perspective is the seminal work of sociologist Arlie Hochschild's theory of emotional labour (Hochschild, 1983). Emotional labour refers to the management of feeling to create a publicly observable facial and bodily display that is sold for a wage and therefore has exchange value. Hochschild distinguishes between surface acting, in which individuals modify outward expressions without changing inner feelings, and deep acting, where individuals attempt to align their internal emotions with organizational expectations. Hochschild highlights how emotional labour can lead to emotional dissonance, a mismatch between felt and displayed emotions that may have psychological costs. The concept of emotional labour is reflected in much of the later research within organizational psychology of emotional demands, as a specific type of job demand e.g., van Vegchel et al. (2004) or Zapf (2002).

Compassion fatigue

The related concept compassion fatigue was proposed in the 1990's in nursing science by Carla Joinson and refers to a state of emotional, physical, and psychological exhaustion resulting from prolonged exposure to others' suffering, particularly in caregiving and helping professions (Joinson, 1992). It is often conceptualized as the “cost of caring” and is closely related to secondary traumatic stress, arising from indirect exposure to trauma through empathetic engagement with clients (Figley, 1995). The concept highlights how sustained empathic involvement can diminish caregivers' capacity for compassion over time, potentially leading to reduced well-being and professional functioning. It is commonly discussed alongside, but distinct from, burnout, as it is more directly tied to trauma exposure rather than other organizational stressors.

Current theoretical perspectives on prevention of negative consequences of emotionally charged work

A central perspective to guide prevention is the Job Demands and Resources (JD-R) model of burnout that was proposed in 2001 as a theoretical lens for understanding the development of burnout and possible pathways for prevention (Demerouti et al., 2001). The Job Demands–Resources (JD–R) theory conceptualizes all work characteristics as either job demands (aspects requiring sustained effort and associated with costs) or job resources (aspects that support goal attainment, reduce demands, and foster growth). In its current form, the model posits two core processes: a health impairment process, where high demands lead to strain and burnout, and a motivational process, where resources promote engagement and performance. Recent extensions emphasize dynamic interactions, including buffering effects (resources mitigate demands) and gain/loss spirals over time, as well as the inclusion of personal, social, and organizational resources (Bakker & Demerouti, 2017).

While the JDR theory contributes to the broader understanding of the development of negative outcomes such as stress and positive outcomes such as motivation and engagement, the authors of the JDR theory also point to the need for identification of specific resources to counterbalance specific demands like emotional demands (Demerouti et al., 2001).

There is therefore a need to develop theory to guide sustainable organization of emotionally charged work. We used a Classical Grounded Theory (Glaser & Strauss, 1967; Glaser, 1978, 1992) approach to a) Explore the main concern of employed people in relation to emotionally charged work and b) Generate a grounded theory that could inform sustainable organization of emotionally charged work and the prevention of compassion fatigue.

Methods

Classic Grounded Theory is inductive in nature and enables researchers to systematically conceptualize latent social patterns and structures in the substantive area of study (Berterö, 2012; Glaser & Strauss, 1967; Glaser, 1992). It is characterized by focusing the analysis on two questions: (1) What is the main concern of the informants and (2) how do they resolve it? (Glaser, 1992; Simmons, 2022). This entails a broad scope for the study, because the researcher cannot know beforehand the main concern of the informants. To guide the sampling, the concept of a substantive area is therefore used. The substantive area is the context or topic-area where these formal questions are posed (Glaser, 1978). For the present study, the substantive area was emotionally charged work in organizations. While Classic Grounded Theory is presented as ontologically neutral (Kempster & Parry, 2011; Simmons, 2022), we situate this study in a critical realist tradition (Bhaskar, 1975).

Sampling

In Classic Grounded Theory sampling, data collection and analysis happen concurrently (Glaser & Strauss, 1967). The sampling initially purposefully targets the substantive area under study and analysis is simultaneously carried out as new data are collected. The emerging theory guides the subsequent sampling to progressively close gaps in the comprehension of the substantive area (Glaser, 1992).

In line with this methodology, we collected the initial data in an area where the phenomenon to be studied (emotionally charged work) would be highly prevalent: among employees or managers working in public psychiatry in Denmark. The codes emerging from this data informed further sampling to address gaps in the data. One example was the code of meaning in work. The psychiatry workers drew heavily on the narrative of “helping others” when ascribing meaning to their working lives. In the subsequent sampling, we therefore targeted participants whose work tasks did not consist in “helping others”, e.g., work that involved sanctioning. We continued to collect data until reaching theoretical saturation.

We recruited the initial participants through work environment professionals in a psychiatric unit. From then on, the recruitment strategies were as diverse as the participants. We recruited through social partners, our own network, social media (Facebook, Instagram and LinkedIn), an occupational physician who put us in touch with a patient in treatment for work-related stress, and, finally, direct contact to companies.

Data collection methods

As the tables show, the data collection consisted of 23 interviews with workers in different occupational sectors and positions. The first three authors of this paper conducted all of the interviews and were equally involved in the analytical process. For the initial eight interviews, we used an interview guide built to capture the range of emotions experienced at work and the actions taken by participants to navigate these. The interview guide consisted of open-ended questions relating to the substantive area of the study: emotionally charged work in organizations.

The initial eight interviews were conducted face-to-face. The first question addressing this topic was assisted by artefacts in the form of eight cards with different emotions stated on them. The interview participants were asked to choose three emotions (three cards) that resonated with their experiences with work and describe a situation in which each emotion arose. The first 7 cards stated the following words; joy, disgust, insecurity, excitement, frustration, meaningfulness, and anger. The last card was left blank, allowing participants to opt for other emotions.

The ongoing analysis of the first eight interviews informed subsequent theoretical sampling (aimed at developing the theory) and the interview guide was adjusted to collect data that would address data gaps around the three emerging categories; (1) the role of emotions in work, (2) emotions and collegial relationships, and (3) meaning in work. We asked participants to relate to the previous findings, with questions such as: “Other people have highlighted helping others through work as meaningful to them. Does this resonate with you and how?” The majority of these interviews were held face-to-face, but three interviews were conducted through a video call, due to the participants preferring this format.

Throughout the data collection and analysis, memos were used to examine developing thoughts and capture new theoretical ideas about the data (Glaser, 1978). An example of this is that participants would experience (negative) emotions in relation to clients/patients differently from (negative) emotions in relation to co-workers. Being aware of this preconception, we made sure to phrase questions about emotions in work in a way that did not make this distinction on behalf of the participant.

Ethics

All informants received written information about the aim of the study, their anonymity in participating and the processing of personal data and verbally consented to participate before we conducted the interviews and started recording.

The study was conducted in accordance with applicable Danish legislation for GDPR. We carried out the study in accordance with the legal basis for research (Regulation-2016/679-EN-gdpr-EUR-Lex GDPR, article 9, stk. 2, litra j) for sensitive personal data and the legal basis for tasks carried out in the public interest or services to public authorities (Regulation-2016/679-EN-gdpr-EUR-Lex GDPR, article, 6, stk. 1, litra e) for regular personal data. In line with these regulations written informed consent was not required; therefore, oral informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to their participation in the study.

In Denmark, the scientific ethics committee only requires approval for the collection of biological data, not qualitative data. The study has been notified to and approved by Danish Data Protection Agency under the joint notification of The National Research Center for the Working Environment (approval no. 2015-57-0074). For further details, we refer to the Danish Research Ethics Committees site: https://www.videnskabsetik.dk/ansoegning-til-etisk-komite/overblik-over-anmeldelsespligten/hvad-skal-ikke-anmeldes, specifically the subsection: “What Should Not Be Reported.” This is why we could not use an independent review board or an institutional review board for receiving ethical approval.

To minimize risk of re-traumatizing participants, the research team received supervision from a licenced psychologist with experience in traumatology during the data collection phase. Furthermore, we made sure to acquire and convey information to participants regarding possible support provided by their employer or relevant organization, in case they were affected by their participation.

Analysis

All interviews were audio recorded and notes were written during or shortly after the interview. The first 10 interviews were transcribed verbatim manually, while the remaining interviews were transcribed using a transcription software and selected passages were proofread using the audio recording. Initial stages of coding was conducted manually and in discussion between authors SJ, EA and SW, later stages of coding was undertaken in NVivo 12.

Stage 1: substantive coding

Substantive coding involved starting with open coding, moving to selective coding around the core category, then constant comparison and conceptualizing in relation to the core category (Glaser, 1978; Hallberg, 2006). In open coding, all actions are coded as individual incidents. By constantly comparing these incidents across the data, indicators and concepts are generated.

During the initial phase of the data collection, the research group used open coding that helped inform us of what data was needed. After each interview, three members of the research group memoed and discussed—first individually and subsequently together—the main concern of the interviewees and how they resolved this concern. In both cases, the focus was on what the interviewees describe that they do in relation to the substantive area. The emphasis was therefore on behaviours and actions. The analytical task was to identify the indicators that characterize these actions and conceptualize what was happening. Through this continuous comparison, some key concepts emerged that were consistent among the eight initial interview participants.

One concept was the function of emotions in work. The job tasks required interviewees to actively and continuously display, regulate and understand their own (and others') emotions. In other words, the performance of their assigned work tasks required employees to use their emotions. A physiotherapist in a children's psychiatric hospital expressed it like this:

You could say that we use ourselves as our tool. It's our personality and our way of being and our way of creating relationships with other people, so it also feels a little extra hard when it falls short, because it's not just my hammer that doesn't work that I need to replace, it's me as a person that doesn't work. I7 Physiotherapist, health and safety representative

Another concept that emerged was emotions related to collegiality. Employees' emotions were related to their co-workers because they were heavily dependent on each other to solve their work tasks. A chief physician in a forensic ward went as far as describing the feelings for his colleagues as love, although in a non-romantic sense: “i t's where you have that common destiny and where you feel that we wish each other well” (Chief Physician, I3).

Finally, the concept of the meaningfulness of the work emerged. Employees described how the meaningful part of their work was helping other people. This was both a motivating factor and a central part of their job satisfaction, but also posed a potential challenge to their wellbeing, when the ability to help others was threatened due to poor organizational conditions. A charge nurse explains what she found meaningful in her job: “actually, it's all the difficult things. It's the fact that you have succeeded in moving the patient from a bad place to a slightly better place” (Charge Nurse I5).

The new data enabled us to analyse incidents and actions related to emotions in work in different contexts, e.g., where emotions were not a part of the professional language. Progressing from open to selective coding, we coded the interviews focusing on participants' actions concerning emotions in work, continuously comparing indicator to indicator, indicator to concept and concept to concept in order to identify action/behavioural patterns and the emergence of concepts. The core category of Capacity Building for emotionally charged work emerged and selective coding was performed on all the material.

Stage 2: theoretical coding

Classic Grounded Theory stipulates that if multiple concepts are identified in the material, the researcher should identify a theoretical code that organizes the theory. In Classic Grounded Theory, there are different theoretical code families, but the different families are not mutually exclusive and often overlap (Glaser, 1978). Identification of the theoretical code is indicated by fit, meaning that all the collected data must align. The 6 Cs theoretical code organizes the Theory of Building Capacity. The 6 Cs are: causes, consequences, contingencies, context, covariates and conditions related to the central concept.

Findings: collective capacity building for emotionally charged work

Across all the types of jobs represented in this study, the participants shared one main concern: having meaningful working lives. They actively created meaning in their work. The more emotionally charged the work was, the more this meaning was created through building collective capacity; the core category of this theory. This collective capacity for emotionally charged work also served as a buffer against negative health consequences such as burnout. Emotionally charged work can be understood as arising in situations related to a work task with a particular potential to elicit emotions in the worker.

Building a collective capacity for emotionally charged work entailed a range of strategies used to stay in their line of work “in the long run”. The capacity building is characterized as collective because the strategies were embedded in relations between co-workers, peers or others around the participants.

Building this collective capacity was a strategy used across different lines of work. Increasingly emotionally charged work led to increasing use of this strategy. One example is the traffic warden, who had often experienced conflicts with car owners. For him, meaning existed in creating traffic accessibility in public areas, e.g., in front of embassies or kindergartens. He maintained this meaning by employing strategies for collective capacity building that we will unfold in the following sections.

The Theory of Collective Capacity Building presents the causes, consequences, contingencies, conditions, and covariance that employees navigate in context of their emotionally charged work in order to build their collective capacity. The findings are presented around the 6Cs of this grounded theory as illustrated in Figure 1. The context of the grounded theory is paid work as this was specified already in choosing the substantive area of the study. The core horizontal line of boxes explains the core of the theory: that the main cause of collective capacity building is participants' concern with creating meaningful working lives and the main consequence of an adequate capacity is meaningful working lives and the absence of illness. In the following sections we will unfold the last three boxes: contingencies, covariances and conditions of the core category. These three boxes relate to each other as illustrated in Figure 1: the capacity building is contingent on three strategies in the upper box. The need to employ these strategies covaries with the intensity and/or frequency of emotionally charged work. And finally, there are organizational conditions that influence both the intensity and frequency of the emotionally charged work and the possibility of employing the three strategies.

Contingencies—strategies for collective capacity building

The collective capacity for emotionally charged work was contingent on employees seeking affirmation, building collegiality, and creating space for emotional recovery.

Seeking affirmation involves actively searching for indications that their work was “good enough”. Affirmation could be obtained from several sources: recognition from colleagues and management, verbal acknowledgement from clients/customers, or simply registering an actual effect of one's work (e.g., the improved condition of a patient). A head nurse in a psychiatric hospital for adolescents described how she actively used her extensive knowledge in psychiatric care to search for subtle signs of improvement in patients that would be invisible for laymen to create meaning in her work. She consciously applied this strategy, because the other sources of affirmation, e.g., a simple “thank you” or visible improvements, were not always available in her job:

At the moment we have a girl, who keeps showing us how bad she feels, because she’s afraid that if she gets better, she’ll be discharged. She has to make sure to feel bad, in order to stay here. So we try to talk to her about how we won’t discharge you if you take this one pill. And even if you look like you’re getting better, we know you still feel like shit inside. It’s these kinds of things that give me meaningfulness and joy. But it takes professional skills to have these talks with patients. I5 charge nurse, psychiatric hospital for adolescents

Building collegiality consists of actively building trust and relationships with colleagues. Building collegiality served three purposes in relation to capacity building for emotionally charged work: (1) taking over for tasks when colleagues were low in their emotional energy, (2) providing social support after emotionally intense tasks, and (3) using differences in personal preferences to distribute tasks accordingly, thereby diminishing the emotional charge of the situation. The following example illustrates a team that has built collegiality for years enabling the staff to take over for each other in tasks that demand a high level of emotional energy, in this case a conflictual situation with a patient in a forensic unit:

I know my staff don’t hold back, and I’ve tried speaking to a patient and realizing afterwards that I had tunnel vision. But then a staff member comes to me and says “you need to step to the side”, and I was very offended in the situation, I thought “what the hell, I’m talking to this patient?”. But in the end, he was right; it was the right thing to do. I was standing too close and escalating the conflict. I thought I was de-escalating, but that’s the thing about security, having a safe environment where people aren’t afraid to tell me when I’m in danger. But it requires a high degree of mutual trust – that we want the best for each other. I3 Chief psychiatrist, forensic psychiatric hospital

Participants build collegiality over years of working together, and this entails knowing each other well, personally as well as professionally.

Creating space for emotional recovery involves planning pauses or seeking out ad hoc pauses from the emotionally charged work, either inside or outside of working hours. The recovery can include social contact or being solitary as this minister of religion practiced following three extraordinarily tragic funerals in a row:

I think [it looks] like a plant that just wilts, because it didn’t get enough water. But then I have some very good colleagues who let me forward my calls to them for a few days, when I just need to sleep. And it’s different how people react to this kind of thing. Some people need to do some gardening and some people find a hobby. I’m very fond of streaming. Completely shutting down. Sitting there and playing Candy Crush and being a total zombie. And luckily that’s legitimate in a profession like this. I14 Minister of religion

The pause was unplanned, but possible because of the high degree of autonomy in her work. The need for emotional recovery is proportional to the frequency and intensity of the emotionally charged situations, meaning that low intensity situations should require a shorter break.

Covariance: the need for capacity building varies by the frequency and intensity of emotionally charged work tasks

Certain tasks or situations are more emotionally charged than others. Varying frequency of emotionally charged work is also experienced. Yet, it is not the frequency and/or intensity of the emotionally charged work itself, but rather the lack of access to preventive strategies that cause emotional strain.

More intense and/or frequent emotionally charged work requires greater capacity to maintain meaningful working lives and avoid negative health effects. Therefore, seeking affirmation, building collegiality, and creating space for emotional recovery become crucial, when the work is perceived as more frequently and/or intensively emotionally charged.

One example is a forensic psychiatric nurse who takes care of some of the most complex and dangerous patients and therefore experiences a high level of intensity and frequency of emotionally charged situations. Despite the highly emotionally charged work, he judges that he could easily work until retirement provided that he is able to apply his specialized knowledge and develop the care. Applying specialized knowledge and developing care can be seen as variants of being able to continuously get affirmation that the work is good enough.

Conditions affecting the possibility of building capacity and the frequency and intensity of emotionally charged work

Seeking affirmation, building collegiality, and creating space for emotional recovery depend on three organizational conditions: the quality of the core task, resources (personnel, time, and skills), and task discretion.

These three conditions enable collective capacity building for emotionally charged work. Moreover, they also affect the intensity and frequency of emotionally charged situations directly. This is represented in the last aspect of the theory of Collective Capacity Building (see Figure 1) with the two intertwined arrows from the conditions and frequency/intensity box. The intertwined arrows indicate that the conditions can also influence the frequency and intensity of emotionally charged situations directly. When conditions are unfavourable (low quality of the core task, low level of resources, and/or low task discretion), there is an increase in the frequency and/or intensity of emotionally charged situations.

The quality of the core task refers to the perceived level of quality in the core tasks of the role. The quality of the core task is typically decided based on the overall strategy of an organization, e.g., focusing on high levels of customer satisfaction, cheap products or—in public administration—helping as many citizens as possible with a limited budget. This condition mainly affects the possibility of seeking affirmation, but also to a minor extent the possibility of building collegiality and creating space for emotional recovery. For example, seeking affirmation can be difficult when the quality of the core task is low, and this would negatively affect the possibility of capacity building.

This is illustrated in the case of the social worker in the occupational area (I19). The management wanted to reduce the case management time and ordered the social workers to close cases fast and not spend too much time on the challenges faced by the citizens. The social worker perceives this as entailing a lower quality of case management because it is more difficult to help the citizen get employment. This limits the sources of affirmation that the work undertaken is “good enough” because affirmation from both the management and the citizens is not possible and she must therefore choose one of the two.

The quality of the core task also directly affects the intensity and frequency of emotionally charged situations by placing the employee in situations where the recipient of their work is unsatisfied or has unmet needs, typically resulting in conflictual situations or the employee witnessing suffering.

An example is the case of the social worker in the occupational area, where the low quality of her core task led to a situation with high emotional charge. She is the contact person for a citizen in a vulnerable position and the citizen cannot—due to the legislation in the area—get the help she needs. The supervisor of the social worker decides that the citizen should not even get her scheduled counselling, because there is no chance that she will obtain the cash benefits. However, the social worker believes that cancelling the counselling would be detrimental to her relationship with the citizen and to her future help-seeking:

I thought it was the worst treatment ever, and I’ll admit I cried that day. Sometimes you cry in this job, and I 100 percent did that day. It’s this kind of thing that can happen sometimes that you take home with you and that makes you feel like the worst social worker ever. And you’re not, but that’s how you feel in that moment, and if you had too many of those days, you wouldn’t want to be in this job. I23 Social worker in the occupational area, left her line of work

In the eyes of the social worker, the quality in the way the case was managed was poor, causing an emotionally intense situation where she feels insufficient and feels guilty in relation to the citizen.

The second condition, resources, in terms of stable personnel, time and skills, affects the possibility of capacity building in terms of availability. Available resources affect the possibility of using all three strategies for building collective capacity.

An example is the forensic psychiatric unit, where interviewees describe how years of good staffing and stability in the staff group has facilitated a trusting and close relationship between the personnel (I1, I2, I3). Conversely, a lack of resources can translate into discontinuity in the staff group, which limits the possibility of “building collegiality”.

Availability of resources also directly affects the frequency and/or intensity of emotionally charged situations through several mechanisms. Firstly, a lack of skills can lead to insufficiently managed work tasks where the recipient of their work is unsatisfied or has unmet needs. Secondly, a lack of stability in staff can concentrate difficult work tasks with the experienced personnel facing increased frequency and intensity of emotionally charged work. Lastly, a lack of time leads to conflictual situations because time pressure fosters increased waiting times and impedes addressing pressing needs.

The most illustrative case of resources affecting the frequency and intensity of emotionally charged work is the nursery teacher who has left her job after being on sick leave with burnout (I22). When describing what led up to her sick leave, she begins with explaining a severe lack of staffing during her introductory period. One of the three teachers was temporary, and they increased the number of children per group from 9 to 15 without an increase in staffing during the months of her introductory period. One of the new children had a mother who had difficulties in letting go of her child and was allowed to stay with the child for a month in the nursery, which led to conflicts:

And her child was running around, [her presence] was more of a nuisance than a help. The stress level was elevated that month, because we had a child there that we couldn’t control […] And at that point I actually broke down and cried. I22 Nursery teacher, left her job after being on sick leave with burnout

The increase in demands without additional resources led to more intense emotionally charged situations, in this case because employees did not have the resources to handle a complicated introduction with a new child, and the lack of resources provokes a more nervous attitude from the mother, which limits their chances of handling the relationship with the mother adequately.

The last condition, task discretion, pertains to the autonomy the worker has to decide when and how a work task is solved. This condition affects mainly the possibility of creating space for emotional recovery, and to a minor extent building collegiality and seeking affirmation.

The level of task discretion is decisive for being able to decide when and how to take breaks for emotional recovery, and when task discretion is low, the space for emotional recovery can only be created outside of working hours if the person's personal life permits it.

While most of the examples we find of emotional recovery are indeed outside of working hours (e.g., in the car after work, see Table II), a minister explains how he has the decision-making authority to decide to turn off the phone during transport time to visits, to get a break from the emotionally charged situations (I15).

Task discretion also influences the frequency and/or intensity of emotionally charged situations directly in that task discretion enables the employee to distribute emotionally charged tasks over time, to alternate between emotionally charged work and other tasks, and to postpone emotionally charged tasks if the emotional energy is low.

An example is the CEO of an IT start-up Company who explained she experienced intense emotionally charged situations in her personnel management task because of a conflictual relationship with an employee. However, she did not have the necessary task discretion to fire this employee because of the financial situation of the company:

It can definitely affect me a lot. It can ruin my day for sure. One thing I struggle with is my authority; he has a hard time respecting me […] If I had the money to do so, I would have fired him long ago. I12 CEO of an IT-start-up

Her limited task discretion in relation to personnel management increased the frequency of emotionally charged situations of daily conflictual encounters with this employee.

We have now looked at a number of conditions and strategies for building collective capacity for emotionally charged work. In the following, we will discuss the implications of these findings for theory and practice.

Discussion

This article has presented a Classic Grounded Theory of collective capacity building for emotionally charged work. The theory provides a framework for understanding the dual aspects of emotionally charged work as both a stressor and a source of meaning at work. The theory places meaning at work at the centre, as the main concern of the participants of this study. The more emotionally charged the work is the more articulated the need for building capacity for this work. The capacity building is contingent on three strategies that informants actively employed to be able to maintain meaning and stay in their line of work: seeking affirmation, building collegiality and creating space for emotional recovery. The need for collective capacity covaries with the intensity and frequency of the emotionally charged work. Finally, three organizational conditions affect both the intensity and frequency of the emotionally charged work and the possibility of employing the three strategies for building collective capacity.

Hereby the Theory of collective capacity building for emotionally charged work both differs from and extends concepts in earlier research. In line with Classical Grounded theory existing theories can be interwoven into the emerging theory, once it has emerged (Hallberg, 2010). Specifically, the Theory of collective capacity building for emotionally charged work extends current research on organizational resources and job crafting by describing tailored practices in relation to emotionally charged work. The Job Demands and Resources (JDR) theory posits that on the one hand the frequency and/ severity of job demands may lead to health impairments and on the other hand job resources are functional in achieving work goals, regulate the impact of job demands and foster motivation (Bakker et al., 2023). A recent focus in the JDR theory has been on job-crafting where employees actively seek out resources in their work. In the presented grounded theory this is found in the form of capacity building – an active act from employees in emotionally demanding work that allows them to maintain meaning and good health. The presented grounded theory extends the JDR theory by proposing specific job resources such as individually tailored at-work recovery from emotionally demanding tasks and role clarity in the form of organizational affirmation of when the task is performed “good enough”. The theory also differs from the JDR theory in proposing that some organizational resources (conditions: job-autonomy, high quality of the core task and resources in terms of time and stable personnel) bear a double-role in both shaping the intensity and frequency of emotionally demanding work and at the same time affecting the possibility of employing demand-specific job-resources. In line with the Conservation of Resources Theory (Hobfoll et al., 2018) that stipulates that you need to invest resources to maintain resources, these conditions might be a place to start for organizations where emotionally charged work is prevalent and who want to create resource caravan pathways and avoid resource loss spirals.

The strategy “building collegiality” in collective capacity building for emotionally charged work shares characteristics with social support – the probably most investigated specific job resource for buffering negative consequences of emotional demands (e.g., Chang & Cho, 2021; Eriksson et al., 2021; Fisher et al., 2022; Granek et al., 2016; Kracen et al., 2020; Portela et al., 2015; Van Daalen et al., 2009; Wazqar, 2019). It adds to emerging literature that investigates the quality of this social support in relation to emotionally charged work and the specific function it serves e.g., in allowing for task rotation between colleagues (Andersen et al., 2025) and collective emotion regulation (Monier, 2020).

Despite the above mentioned contributions, the study also has certain limitations. The study relies solely on individual interviews. Compared to data from group interviews, we might have identified collective strategies that are not socially accepted in participants' respective workgroups. Compared to data from participant observation, we might have overlooked socially unacceptable or maladaptive strategies such as substance use as found in the ethnographic study of Monier (2020). It is also important to note that the study was conducted in Denmark that has a high level of social security net. This might mean that participants have placed comparatively more importance on meaning at work as economic security is broadly secured.

Practical implications of a grounded theory for capacity building for emotionally charged work

Experiencing high emotional demands at work has consistently been linked to increased risk of sick leave, disability pension, and being diagnosed with depression (Framke et al., 2021, 2023, 2024; Madsen et al., 2022; Wieclaw et al., 2008). The theory of collective capacity building suggests that it is not desirable to try to prevent all emotionally charged situations at work, because emotionally charged situations can also stimulate motivation and in combination with the right strategies or resources contribute to meaningful working lives. Even frequent and/or intense emotionally charged situations can serve as a source of meaning and a sustainable working life, provided that the capacity for emotionally charged work is sufficiently high. Nevertheless, it cannot be ruled out that there may be an upper limit to the extent of emotionally charged work that can be offset by an increased capacity.

The theory outlines two primary pathways for either expanding employees' capacity to manage or directly reduce the frequency and intensity of emotionally charged work. Both can be understood as forms of primary prevention against conditions such as burnout or compassion fatigue. While the data indicate that not all emotionally intense situations at work are avoidable, e.g., exposure to patient death in certain healthcare settings, others, like conflict-laden interactions with clients, citizens, or patients, can often be mitigated.

The first pathway to prevention targets the reduction of emotionally charged situations themselves. This is influenced by three interrelated conditions: (1) the quality of task performance, (2) the availability of adequate resources, such as staffing levels, time, and skills, and (3) the degree of control and influence employees have over their work tasks.

The second prevention pathway involves building capacity for managing emotional demands. One illustrative example emerged from a forensic psychiatric ward, where staff were able to distribute patients among themselves based on their relational skills and personal comfort levels. Some employees felt better equipped to work with patients convicted of paedophilia, while others preferred engaging with individuals convicted of homicide. This capacity-building practice was made possible by strong collegial relationships characterized by mutual trust and deep interpersonal knowledge—a dynamic captured in the strategy of “building collegiality.”

Importantly the study was conducted based on individual interviews entailing findings of strategies possible from an individual or group level perspective. The guiding principle in occupational health and safety is to anchor initiatives on an organizational level where possibilities of eliminating risk is higher (Kjærgaard et al., 2025; Schulte et al., 2024). We therefore strongly encourage further research in operationalizing and testing the theoretically proposed strategies in organizational initiatives such as e.g., systematic venting within working hours as a structure supporting “creating space for emotional recovery”.

Conclusion

Drawing on the theory of collective capacity building for emotionally charged work, we find that specific organizational practices can simultaneously create meaningful working lives and buffer against adverse outcomes such as ill health and compassion fatigue. The theory thus offers a framework for organizing work in a sustainable manner within settings characterized by high levels of emotionally charged work. The presented theory aligns with existing theory of Job demands and Resources and offers guidance for future organizational interventions for managing and preventing emotional demands.
