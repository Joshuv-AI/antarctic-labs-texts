Mechanical stiffness orchestrates distinct regulation of MRTFs and YAP/TAZ transcriptional cofactors in hepatocytes
Authors: Brenda Selene Torres‐Ortiz, Daniel Pérez‐Calixto, Jorge Carretero‐Ortega, Aida Gabriela Guzman‐López, Diana Cristina Pinto‐Dueñas, Mariana Hernández‐Juárez, Alejandra Jiménez‐Escobar, Adriana Rodríguez‐Hernández, Beatriz Díaz‐Bello, Nathalia Serna‐Márquez, Miguel Ángel Peña‐Rico, Laura Gómez‐Romero, Francisco Gabriel Vázquez‐Cuevas, Genaro Vázquez‐Victorio
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
During liver fibrosis, hepatocytes respond to progressive increases in tissue stiffness by undergoing cytoskeletal reorganisation. This has important consequences such as immune cell infiltration, mainly triggered by the activation of YAP and TAZ proteins. However, other proteins that respond to mechanical modifications remain poorly characterised; in particular, the myocardin‐related transcription factors A and B (MRTFs). Here, we referred to these four proteins as mechanical responsive transcriptional cofactors (MRTcoF). We analysed the regulation of MRTcoF proteins in fibrotic livers induced by carbon tetrachloride (CCl4) and observed that MRTcoF were differentially regulated during liver fibrosis, predominantly in hepatocytes. Transcriptome analysis suggested that fatty acid and glucose metabolisms, cell proliferation and signalling pathways were regulated by transcriptional enhanced associate domain (TEAD)‐ and serum response factor (SRF)‐transcriptional programmes in CCl4‐treated livers. Then, we evaluated the protein expression levels of MRTcoF in primary hepatocytes (PH) cultured on tissue culture petri dishes (TCPD). After culture, we observed upregulation of MRTcoF, and downregulation of TAZ after protein increase. Interestingly, despite the increase in MRTFB protein levels, no accumulation of MRTFB was detected in the nucleus. Culture on soft polyacrylamide hydrogels (PAA HGs) attenuated the activation of MRTcoF, having specific implications in the transcriptional regulation of target genes. In contrast, pharmacological inhibition of MRTcoF was not sufficient to halt transcriptional regulation. In summary, a progressive increase of stiffness activates MRTcoF transcriptional complexes in hepatocytes, suggesting a key role of mechano‐transduction during liver fibrosis.

Introduction

During liver fibrosis, the damaged parenchyma triggers the infiltration of immune cells that activates hepatic stellate cells (HSCs) to myofibroblasts, the main hepatic cells that sustain fibrogenesis. In parallel, the extracellular matrix (ECM) deposition causes a progressive increase of stiffness [1, 2]. Paradoxically, a rigid environment seems to take place before the accumulation of ECM deposits in liver fibrosis [3, 4]. In addition, crosslinking and deposition of ECM spread from the periphery toward the centre of the hepatic lobule with a significant increase in rigidity at the borders, generating a heterogeneous response to stiffness in the hepatic cells [5]. In particular, inflammation is promoted by hepatocytes through the activation of mechanotransduction proteins known as yes‐associated protein 1 (YAP1) and WW‐domain‐containing transcription regulator 1 (WWTR1), or TAZ, in liver fibrosis [6, 7]. It seems that hepatocytes somehow respond to early mechanical modifications and participate in the fibrogenesis process by promoting immune cell infiltration that subsequently activates HSC.

Proteins known as myocardin‐related transcription factor A and B (MRTFA and MRTFB) are transcriptional cofactors that respond to actin polymerisation, and regulate contractility, fibrogenesis and epithelial‐mesenchymal transition (EMT) [8]; these processes are important events during a fibrotic process. Also, YAP and TAZ proteins are members of the polarity‐associated HIPPO pathway, which regulates cell proliferation and differentiation. Also, YAP/TAZ proteins respond to actin dynamics [9]. MRTFs and YAP/TAZ proteins have an intricate crosstalk, and cooperatively regulate the transcription of multiple genes such as CTGF, CYR61, ACTA2, MYC among several others [10, 11, 12]. The activation of the four proteins, named mechanical responsive transcriptional cofactors (MRTcoF), has significant consequences in the regulation of ECM deposition, actin contractility, cell proliferation and differentiation [13]. MRTcoF signalling plays an important role in complex physiological processes from early development to fibrosis [14, 15, 16].

In the liver, HSCs regulate fibrosis through the activation of MRTFA and YAP cofactors, controlling the expression of several profibrotic proteins [17, 18]. Pharmaceutical inhibition of these cofactors significantly attenuates ECM deposition and the expression of profibrotic regulators [18, 19]. In hepatocytes, the activation of YAP and TAZ cofactors have a fundamental role in the appropriate response to damage during liver fibrosis [6, 20, 21]. Hepatocytes respond to increase of stiffness by enhancing actin contractility, downregulating HNF4A protein and activating extracellular signal‐regulated kinase (ERK), PI3K and YAP signalling [5, 22, 23]. Despite the central role of MRTcoF in mechanotransduction, nonetheless, their contribution has not been elucidated in hepatocytes. Thus, we aimed to explore the simultaneous regulation of MRTcoF in CCl4‐treated fibrotic livers, and in rat primary hepatocytes cultured on soft and stiff PAA HGs. We analysed the regulation of multiple MRTcoF target genes under different mechanical conditions, and performed a transcriptome analysis to visualise a general landscape of cellular processes promoted by MRTcoF during liver fibrosis.

Results

MRTcoF are upregulated in hepatocytes during liver fibrosis

It has been demonstrated that stiffness regulates the activation of MRTcoF in liver fibrosis; however, their simultaneous regulation has not been characterised entirely [17, 18]. Hence, we evaluated the simultaneous regulation of MRTcoF in two rat fibrotic models of one and two months of CCl4 treatment [24]. In parallel, we explored the mechanical modifications by Masson's trichrome staining, IHC immunodetection and microindentation tests. Collagen deposits were detected in one‐ and two‐month models showing a characteristic peripheral accumulation at the hepatic lobules, as reported previously [5] (Fig. 1A and Fig. S1A). Both fibrotic models were also positive to ACTA2 protein, suggesting transdifferentiation of HSC [3]. To corroborate the increase of stiffness caused by the CCl4‐induced damage, we decided to perform a relaxation test to measure the mechanical properties of the two‐month model. It has been previously reported that G′ modules no longer modify from one month of CCl4 treatment in rats [3]. The pure elastic modulus obtained by microindentation tests was referred to as a pure elastic module (E i), as previously reported [25]. Control livers (mock) yielded a mean stiffness value of 1.57 ± 0.42 kPa, whereas CCl4‐treated livers showed a significant increase in their E i modulus with a mean value of 5.12 ± 2.04 kPa (Fig. 1B). Similar values had been reported previously in rat, mouse and human hepatic samples [3, 26].

Having confirmed a significant increase of stiffness in our model, we then explored the presence of MRTcoF in fibrotic livers by IHC immunodetection. Interestingly, MRTFA protein showed cytoplasmic and nuclear localisation in mock and CCl4‐treated livers; however, the signal was stronger in both CCl4 models (Fig. 1C and Fig. S1B). MRTFB protein was also present in hepatocytes showing a faint nuclear signal with a moderate increase in fibrotic models. YAP/TAZ proteins also showed cytoplasmic and nuclear localisation with an important increase in both CCl4 models; YAP and TAZ share a WW domain that is recognised by the antibody used in the immunodetection as shown in immunoblots (Fig. 1C,D). To confirm the expression of MRTcoF proteins shown by IHC, we analysed protein levels by immunoblotting nuclear extracts in mock and CCl4‐treated livers. MRTFs presented a significant increase in a one‐month CCl4 model; TAZ protein tended to increase as well (Fig. S1C,D). However, only MRTFA and TAZ proteins were significantly upregulated in the two‐month model (Fig. 1D,E). YAP protein levels did not present an increase in both fibrotic models; nevertheless, YAP showed also a significant signal in mock livers. All MRTcoF transcripts were upregulated in both CCl4 models (Fig. S2A–D). These data suggest there is a sustained upregulation of MRTFA and TAZ proteins during liver fibrosis despite the transcriptional upregulation of the four MRTcoF genes.

MRTcoF‐associated genes are differentially expressed in liver fibrosis

To understand the transcriptional role of MRTcoF during liver fibrosis, we analysed RNA sequencing data from a previously reported two‐month rat model treated with CCl4 (GSE110096) [27]; the CCl4 treatment was performed similarly to our model of fibrosis (an administration of three times a week). We identified 4250 genes differentially expressed (DEG, Log2FC > |1| and p‐adj < 0.05) in CCl4‐treated livers compared to mineral oil‐treated (Fig. S3A,B and Table S1). To pinpoint potential direct targets of MRTcoF in humans, rat genes were mapped to their human orthologues using Ensembl through the biomart r package version 2.58.2 (Table S2) [28]. We then focused on genes containing at least one cis‐regulatory element for serum response factor (SRF) or TEAD1‐4 within a 3000 bp region upstream of the transcription start site (promoter regions). SRF and TEADs transcription factors act as the primary nuclear effectors of MRTFs and YAP/TAZ signalling, respectively [13].

Using an R/Bioconductor workflow, promoter regions were retrieved from the human genome, and motif scanning was performed using position weight matrices from the JASPAR database [29]. Specifically, canonical SRF and TEAD family motifs, CArG box and TEAD‐binding element (TBE), respectively, were scanned across promoter sequences using tfbstools version 1.40.0 [30], and genes containing at least one significant motif match for SRF and/or TEAD1–4 were retained. This analysis identified 3625 genes harbouring putative binding sites for SRF and/or TEAD1–4 (Fig. 2A–C and Table S3). Notably, 85.3% DEGs in CCl4‐treated livers were associated with MRTcoF. Among these genes, 2564 contained putative binding sites exclusively for TEAD factors, whereas 1061 genes harboured binding sites for both SRF and TEAD factors. This transcriptome analysis highlights the extensive contribution of MRTcoF‐associated transcriptional programmes during liver fibrosis.

A Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis of the 3625 identified genes was performed to pinpoint cellular processes promoted by MRTcoF (Fig. 2D,E and Fig. S3C). Interestingly, we found enrichments in biological processes related to fatty acid and steroid metabolism, glucose metabolism, cellular components, cell proliferation and signalling pathways. Fatty acid metabolism has been previously described as a cellular process regulated by MRTFA in the liver [31]. Also, YAP/TAZ proteins have been involved in lipid metabolism in hepatocytes [6]. Biotransformation phase I and gluconeogenesis enzymes have been described to be regulated by YAP [32]. Genes related to cell adhesion, cytoskeleton and ECM components are well‐known targets of MRTcoF [9, 33]. Proliferation of hepatocytes during liver regeneration is known to be sustained by YAP/TAZ signalling [6] but MRTFs are direct effectors of growth factors through ERK signalling [33]; the Myc gene showed an increase in our CCl4 models. Also, we found enrichments for several signalling pathways. Interleukin, NFκB, TGF‐β, cAMP and small GTPases signalling components were differentially expressed; however, important signalling for liver physiology such as WNT, NOTCH and circadian clock were also regulated (Table S4). Senescence and autophagy differential regulation is associated with liver fibrosis and cancer [34]. Downregulation of the complement elements could be related as an attempt to reduce inflammation [35]. Transcriptome analysis suggests a relevant contribution of MRTcoF in liver fibrosis regulating multiple important components associated with fatty acid and glucose metabolism, cell cycle and signalling pathways.

To validate the transcriptome analysis, we evaluated specific enriched transcripts obtained from the mock vs CCl4 models. Semiquantitative (RT‐PCR) and quantitative (qPCR) PCR assays were performed to validate differential expression. Significant results are influenced by template quality, primer design, cycle number, annealing temperature, among others; then we decided to perform both methodologies. First, we chose TUBB and DUSP genes, reported recurrently as positive target genes for MRTcoF [10, 11, 33] (Fig. 3A,B and Fig. S4B). For TUBULINS, the differential expression of Tubb2b was significant in qPCR, and Tubb2a, Tubb2b and Tubb6 were differentially expressed in RT‐PCR. For DUSP phosphatases, Dusp8 presented a significant increase in CCl4 model via RT‐PCR but a tendency to an increase of Dusp5, Dusp8 and Dusp16 was observed in qPCR whereas Dusp10 and Dusp16 showed also a tendency of differential expression in CCl4‐induced fibrotic livers in RT‐PCR. Unexpectedly, Cyp genes appeared to be differentially regulated in our transcriptome analysis (Fig. 3A). Again, Cyp1a2 showed a significant downregulation in fibrotic livers via RT‐PCR. In contrast, Cyp1a1, Cyp1a2 and Cyp8b1 presented a tendency to decrease in qPCR and RT‐PCR (Fig. 3B and Fig. S4B). It was reported that Cyp8b1 was downregulated by the formation of actin filaments in the liver [32]. Correlations between transcriptome analysis and semi‐ and quantitative PCRs strengthen the certainty of the transcriptome results. We consider that most tendencies are prone to significance.

Furthermore, we evaluated the differential regulation in characterised MRTcoF target genes related to actin cytoskeleton, EMT, ECM and inflammation obtained from the mock vs CCl4 models (Fig. 3C and Fig. S5A–F). Actin‐associated genes Flna, Cfl1 and Fhl2 presented a significant increase in qPCR and RT‐PCR in fibrotic livers. Also, ECM‐related transcripts Itgb1 and Col1a1 were upregulated (Fig. 3C and Fig. S5B,C). The regulator gene of inflammation, the Ctgf gene, showed a tendency mainly in RT‐PCR; despite being described to be strongly regulated during liver fibrosis (Fig. S5D) [6, 7]. Similarly, the upregulation of EMT genes Snai1 and Snai2 were not differentially expressed in RT‐PCR but the increase of Snai1 transcript was significant in qPCR (Fig. 3C and Fig. S5E) [36, 37]. The Baat transcript was the only gene related to liver functionality that presented a downregulation in RT‐PCR (Fig. S5F). All these data suggest that an increase of stiffness upregulates MRTcoF, correlating with the differential expression of previously characterised target genes and genes with new roles in metabolism, subcellular components and signalling that participate during liver fibrosis.

MRTcoF are differentially regulated by stiffness in PH

Hepatocytes lose their epithelial and functional characteristics in stiff culture conditions such as in a commonly used TCPD, developing a partial EMT state [22]. In TCPD, HNF4A and CDH1 proteins were downregulated, whereas VIM and CDH2 proteins were upregulated in PH, cultured up to five days compared to freshly isolated rat hepatocytes (FH) (Fig. 4A). ACTB protein was upregulated, and this correlated with the presence of stress fibres after 72 h of culture (Fig. 4B); ACTB gene is transcriptionally regulated by MRTFs [8]. TUBB protein and ponceau staining served better as loading controls (Fig. 4A,C). These results confirmed that PH lose their epithelial characteristics in stiff cultures.

We then evaluated the protein expression of MRTcoF (Fig. 4C). MRTFs and YAP protein levels were significantly upregulated from the second day after culture compared to FH (Fig. 4D), but TAZ protein showed an upregulation within the interval of 3–5 days. This increase of protein levels may be likely supported by transcriptional regulation, so we decided to explore the transcript levels of MRTcoF via semiquantitative RT‐PCR, as shown in Fig. 4E. Mrtfa, Mrtfb and Yap1 transcripts were upregulated up to seven days after culture (Fig. 1F). Again, Wwtr1 transcript levels, which encodes TAZ protein, were upregulated at an interval of 2–5 days. These results suggest that stiff conditions, such as TCPD, induce an upregulation of MRTcoF at transcriptional and translational level in PH; however, TAZ protein is the only cofactor upregulated temporarily.

The upregulation of MRTcoF protein levels in PH ought to correlate with their accumulation at the nucleus [13]. We therefore evaluated the subcellular localisation of MRTcoF on glass coverslips, a stiff condition as well (Fig. 5A). We observed that YAP/TAZ proteins were localised faintly at the nucleus in the first 3 h after culture (with a mean of 815.9 ± 105.5 CTCF) but showed a sustained nuclear localisation up to 72 h with a mean of 1878 ± 363.7 CTCF (Fig. 5B); again, YAP and TAZ proteins share a common WW domain recognised in immunofluorescence. We also performed cell fractionation experiments confirming the accumulation of YAP protein in the nuclear fraction after 24 h of culture (Fig. 5C,F). For TAZ cofactor, protein is only accumulated in both fractions after 72 h (Fig. 5C,G).

MRTFA protein was present in the nucleus as soon as 3 h of culture with a mean of 2952 ± 527.0 CTCF and maintained up to 72 h (with a mean of 4106 ± 501.6 CTCF) in glass coverslips (Fig. 5A,B). However, MRTFA protein was not accumulated in the nuclear fraction in FH, as it is shown in Fig. 5C,D. Remarkably, MRTFB protein was localised faintly in the nucleus of PH even at 24 nor 72 h after culture (Fig. 5A,B). In cell fractionation, there was no significant accumulation in the nuclear fraction (Fig. 5C,E); however, accumulated MRTFB was significantly retained in the cytoplasmic fraction after 72 h. Notably, there is no evidence of the cytoplasmic arrest of MRTFB protein in epithelial cells so far. Results suggest a good correlation between protein upregulation and nuclear localisation for MRTFA, YAP and TAZ proteins where nuclear accumulation of MRTFA and YAP proteins is possible 24 h after culture. Nevertheless, the accumulation of MRTFA/YAP/TAZ proteins occurred until 72 h (Fig. 5H). Also, there is a clear resistance of MRTFB cofactor to localise in the nucleus despite the significant increase of protein levels at stiff conditions. All these data suggest a differential localisation of MRTcoF and this may be relevant in the responses of the hepatocytes to mechanical modifications.

Softness attenuates the activation of MRTcoF in PH

The loss of polarity, the activation of actin cytoskeleton dynamics in TCPD and the upregulation of MRTcoF, may be a direct consequence of the stiffness. We therefore decided to control mechanical conditions of culture by using PAA HGs with 0.5, 1, 5 and 20 kPa elastic modules as described in [38]. As expected, PH were confined in 0.5 kPa PAA HGs from a mean area of 309 ± 0.010 μm2 at 24 h up to 555 ± 0.029 μm2 at 72 h of culture (Fig. 6A,B); a similar confinement was observed in 1 kPa showing a mean area of 714 ± 0.029 μm2 at 72 h. By contrast, spreading was promoted as 5 kPa having a mean area of 13 750 ± 1.00 μm2 at 24 h of culture, and it reached a mean of 32,803 ± 2.504 μm2 at 72 h (Fig. 6B). We also evaluated circularity in PAA HGs (Fig. 6C). Again, a significant decrease of circularity was evident as 5 kPa after 24 h of culture (0.732 ± 0.015), having a value of 0.523 ± 0.036 at 72 h. These results suggest that PH respond to a stiffness of 5 kPa through a significant increase of spreading and decrease of circularity, losing their epithelial morphology. Then, we evaluated MRTcoF protein levels in PH cultured at different mechanical stiffness, where spreading was described, after 72 h of culture (Fig. 6D,E and S6A,B). MRTFs protein levels increased in 0.5 kPa HGs but it was less than in 5 kPa HG and TCPD. YAP and TAZ protein levels showed a significant increase from 5 kPa PAA HGs, remaining low in 0.5 kPa HGs (Fig. 6D–F). It is known that Rho/ROCK signalling is an important mechanical response in PH [5, 22]. So, we evaluated the effects of the Y27632 inhibitor in PH cultured in 5 kPa PAA HGs. A tendency to inhibit the increase of YAP and TAZ protein levels was only observed at concentrations as high as 100 μm of Y27632 (Fig. 6F,G). It seems that MRTcoF upregulation was attenuated only in PH cultured at 0.5–1 kPa HGs where spreading was restrained.

Also, we explored nuclear localisation of MRTcoF in soft (0.5 kPa) and stiff (5 kPa) PAA HGs (Fig. 6H,I). MRTFA protein is localised in the nucleus in stiff HGs at 24 (with a mean of 6258 ± 576.4 CTCF) and 72 h (with a mean of 5842 ± 265.0 CTCF) whereas nuclear localisation was faint at soft HGs at 72 h (with a mean of 1865 ± 762.7) after culture. Once again, MRTFB protein was poorly localised in the nucleus at both conditions showing no significant differences, with the exception of stiff HGs at 72 h (Fig. 6I); this could be a direct consequence of the increase in protein levels. YAP/TAZ proteins were slightly present in the nucleus in soft kPa HGs at 24 h (with a mean of 9541 ± 1418), and translocation was upregulated in stiff HGs at 72 h (with a mean of 19,241 ± 3206) (Fig. 6H,I). Attenuation of YAP protein nuclear translocation has been previously described in PH [39]. In parallel, we explored actin filaments close to partial bile canaliculi to explore polarised PH in soft HG (Fig. 6J). All these data suggest that softness restrains MRTcoF upregulation but it seems that protein translocation was strongly inhibited (Fig. 6K).

MRTcoF‐dependent genes are differentially regulated by stiffness in PH

Attenuation in the activation of MRTcoF may differentially regulate their target genes. Thus, we decided to evaluate the specific regulation of SRF, ACTB, CAV1, ITGB1, FHL2 and LMNA/C proteins in PAA HGs (Fig. 7A); all these proteins depend on MRTcoF transcriptionally [8]. Interestingly, LMNA/C protein levels were unmodified in all conditions despite being a well‐known MRTFs target gene [10]. ITGB1 and FHL2 protein levels were downregulated in soft HG compared to FH but increased in TCPD compared to soft HGs. SRF and CAV1 protein levels were upregulated at stiff HGs compared to soft HGs. ACTB protein was upregulated in soft conditions compared to FH (Fig. 7B); TUBB protein was used as a loading reference.

MRTcoF protein levels and the transcriptional regulation of their target genes are not inhibited completely by softness, so we used the MRTFs inhibitor CCG‐203971 (CCG) to fully downregulate the transcription of MRTcoF target genes in combination with soft HGs (Fig. 7C) [16, 40]. We also evaluated the TGF‐β inhibitor SB431542 but it did not have an effect on MRTcoF upregulation (Fig. S6C); it has been reported that TGF‐β regulates the stability of TAZ and MRTFs [41, 42]. Ctgf and Cyr61 transcripts were significantly downregulated at soft HGs compared to TCPD whereas the decrease of Talin and Amotl2 was moderate. Lmna transcript levels decreased as well, contrary to the observation in protein levels (Fig. 7C,D). Gadd45a transcript did not present any regulation by stiffness. Nevertheless, the use of a CCG inhibitor did not significantly affect the transcript levels of MRTcoF target genes at soft conditions (Fig. 7D), similar to Y27632 inhibitor. Conversely, CCG inhibitors had an effect on TAZ, ACTB and FHL2 protein levels at a concentration of ≥ 75 μm in TCPD but had no effects on ITGB1 and LMNA/C proteins (Fig. S6D). However, LMNA/C, VIN and ACTB protein levels were diminished with 50 μM of CCG treatment in soft conditions (Fig. S6E). Nonspecific effects of the CCG inhibitor may be explained because this inhibitor affects the global transcriptional regulation through the inhibition of RNA pol II [43].

To evaluate that activation of MRTcoF has important consequences in hepatocytes. We analysed the traction forces of PH in 10 kPa HGs, as previously described in [39]. In Fig. 7F, PH presented a partial increase of traction forces (strain energy, 10−13 J) from a mean of 6.3 ± 3.38 at 24 h to a mean of 19.8 ± 7.73 at 72 h of culture. This may be explained by the increase of actin cytoskeleton‐related proteins, such as ACTB and ITGB1, in PH cultured at stiff HGs (10 kPa), where MRTFA, YAP and TAZ proteins are translocated in the nucleus [8, 9]. In general, all data suggest that the regulation of MRTcoF by stiffness in PH has a significant attenuation in the activation of their target genes and contractility (Fig. 7G).

Verteporfin promotes higher forms of MRTcoF in PH

Again, to attempt the inhibition of the activation of MRTcoF in PH, we performed a pharmacological approach by using verteporfin (VP), a commonly used inhibitor of transcriptional activity of YAP/TAZ proteins, in combination with the CCG inhibitor [44]. Interestingly, the use of ≥ 1 μm VP for 48 h downregulated MRTFs and YAP at protein level although transcripts remained unmodified (Fig. 8A,C and Fig. S7A,B). However, TAZ protein levels were significantly reduced at a concentration of 50 μm of CCG (Fig. 8B). TUBB protein levels were also downregulated at 2.5 μm of VP, likely as a consequence of its proteotoxic effects by promoting higher molecular weight complexes [45, 46] (Fig. 8C). There is evidence that VP is a photoresist, promoting protein crosslinking by generating free oxygen radicals [47]. Next, we explored whether the effect of VP is a consequence of the MRTcoF crosslinking. In immunoblot assays, YAP and MRTFA proteins presented higher forms (> 70 and > 110 kDa, respectively) with ≥ 1 μm VP at both 4 h and 48 h of treatment (Fig. 8D), and this correlated with the downregulation of specific molecular weights. Higher forms suggest protein multimerisation derived from crosslinking.

YAP protein localises at liquid–liquid phase separation (LLPS) subcellular aggregates in hepatocytes [48]. We observed that MRTFA and YAP cofactors, and their transcriptional partners SRF and TEAD proteins, were present at supernatants (soluble) and pellets (insoluble) in PH (Fig. S7E). This localisation might facilitate MRTcoF crosslinking. Then we evaluated whether VP has an effect on YAP and MRTFA proteins at insoluble fractions; both proteins accumulated at pellets by VP in a concentration‐dependent manner (Fig. S7C,D). Perinuclear enrichment positive to MRTFA were formed after 4 h incubation of 10 μm VP in PH (Fig. 8E). To confirm crosslinking effects of VP, we used N‐acetyl‐cysteine (NAC) to quench oxygen radicals [46]. As shown in Fig. 8F, 10 mm of NAC blocked the formation of YAP and MRTFA higher forms with 1 μm VP. These data suggest that MRTcoF proteins are susceptible to crosslink by the photoresists VP, forming higher forms. There is not enough evidence to associate higher molecular forms induced by VP, as a direct posttranslational modification [47, 49]. Nonspecific effects of VP and CCG inhibitors must be reconsidered in pharmaceutical protocols assuming that the specific inhibition of MRTcoF is caused by the use of these two molecules.

Discussion

Here, we found a correlation with the increase in stiffness and the upregulation of MRTcoF during liver fibrosis, with an increase of E i about 5.12 ± 2.04 kPa. In CCl4‐treated livers, MRTFA and TAZ proteins increased significantly. YAP protein was not upregulated but it was already present in the nucleus of mock animals, and MRTFB was only upregulated significantly in one month of treatment. Nonetheless, all transcripts of MRTcoF increased in CCl4 models, and it was the first time that simultaneous upregulation of MRTcoF was described particularly in the hepatocytes during fibrosis (Fig. 1C and Fig. S1B), although there was already a similar report in kidney fibrosis [16]. Interestingly, lower bands (< 150 kDa) for MRTFA protein present in fibrotic livers are related to desumoylation that activates MRTFA (Fig. 1D and Fig. S1C) [50]. This may imply a significant role of the hepatocytes in the response to mechanical modifications during liver fibrosis through MRTcoF, as suggested previously [5, 6]. Interestingly, hepatic tissue stiffness is increased in patients with MASLD and in a murine model of MASH. In both cases, lipid metabolism as well as pro‐inflammatory and pro‐fibrotic transcriptional programmes are regulated by YAP/TAZ proteins [51, 52].

To understand the contribution of the hepatocytes in liver fibrosis, we analysed the transcriptome of a published fibrotic model that used a similar experimental protocol [27] and observed that the 85.3% of differentially regulated transcripts (3625 genes) were common targets for SRF or TEADs, the main transcriptional factors of MRTcoF. The GO analysis revealed that fatty acid and glucose metabolism, cellular components, cell proliferation and signalling pathways were differentially expressed. Interestingly, MRTFA KO has been associated with the resistance to develop steatosis [31], and YAP/TAZ proteins were associated with fatty metabolism programmes in hepatocytes [6]; however, there is important evidence about the role of MRTFs in lipid metabolism [53, 54]. Gluconeogenesis has been described to be regulated by YAP in the liver [32], and MRTFs have been linked to glucose metabolism as well [51, 55]. It is known that proliferation is sustained by YAP and TAZ in regenerating hepatocytes [6]. There is an important relationship between proliferation and lipid metabolism mediated by SRF signalling [33]. Regulators of the circadian clock are highly associated with liver metabolism and are known to be regulated by MRTFs [56, 57, 58]. Differential regulation of ligands, receptors and coreceptors of WNT signalling may have important consequences in the metabolism and proliferation of the liver [59]. Also, there was a differential regulation in several cellular processes such as survival, senescence and autophagy (Tables S3 and S4). Autophagy controls YAP/TAZ protein degradation and, as a direct consequence, proliferation in the liver [60]. Oncogenes such as BRAF, MYC and CCND1, and tumour suppressor proteins such as TP53, CDKN1A and CDKN2B appeared as MRTcoF target genes; these signatures correlate with the carcinogenesis and metastasis promotion known to MRTcoF [11, 12]. The significance of our transcriptome analysis suggests a relevant role of MRTcoF, mediated by SRF and TEAD factors, in response to mechanical modifications during liver fibrosis.

The Tubb and Dusp genes were confirmed as potential targets of MRTcoF [10, 11]. Pocaterra et al. demonstrated that an exacerbated actin polymerisation inhibited the expression of Cyp1a2, Cyp8b1, Oat and Glul transcripts in the liver, as we observed in our analysis (Fig. 3B and Fig. S4A, Table S3). In addition, genes related to ECM deposition and actin contractility such as Flna and Itgb1 showed differential expression as well [17, 19, 61]. The proinflammatory Ctgf gene was partially upregulated in the analysis, as previously described in liver fibrosis [6, 7]. Interestingly, the differential regulation of Snai1 and Snai2 is highly associated with the proliferation response of hepatocytes [62]. So, confirmation of DEGs from the current transcriptome analysis suggests that MRTcoF are regulating not only well‐described targets involved in actin contractility, ECM deposition and proliferation but also new important targets related to fatty acid and glucose metabolism, biotransformation and complement system, among others (Table S4).

Again, this is the first time that simultaneous regulation of MRTcoF in PH is reported. We observed that TAZ protein upregulation was temporal and reached its highest levels as of 72 h of culture, increasing significatively at > 5 kPa HGs. TAZ protein has been upregulated in human MASLD and controlled the secretion of IHH [63]; Ihh gene was differentially regulated in the transcriptome analysis. MRTFs seem to be promptly upregulated after culture even in 0.5 kPa HGs; in particular MRTFA was translocated to the nucleus after 3 h in TCPD (Fig. 5A). YAP protein was slightly localised in the nucleus in soft HGs and significant translocation was observed as of 5 kPa HGs. Nonetheless, MRTFA, YAP and TAZ proteins were attenuated in soft HGs (0.5 and 1 kPa). Activation of MRTcoF correlated with the loss of circularity and the increment of cell area in PH cultured in > 5 kPa HGs; it also correlates with an increase of actin contractility measured by TFM [22] (Fig. 7E). MRTFB protein was reluctant to accumulate significantly in the nucleus at stiff conditions. This cytoplasmic localisation of MRTFB may help to understand the partial mesenchymal phenotype of PH acquired in stiff conditions [64]. MRTFB protein is essential for EMT because it is the principal activator of SNAI1 and it cooperates with MRTFA protein to regulate SNAI2 [37, 65]. In addition, it was recently reported that CAP2 protein seems to restrain MRTFs in the cytoplasm in heart tissue [66] but accumulation of MRTFA at the nucleus was reduced in CAP2−/− MEFs [67]. In PH, upregulation of MRTcoF correlates with the increase of spreading and actin contractility triggered by the gradual escalation of stiffness.

Differential activation of MRTcoF targets has to be taken into consideration in vitro because inhibition of mechanical response seems to be partial and a full inhibition is not completed. There are pharmacological and mechanical attempts, and the use of both strategies seem to be a good approach [5, 22, 68]. However, the specificity of every molecule should be reconsidered owing to their nonspecific effects. In PH, CCG inhibitors required high concentrations to downregulate MRTcoF protein targets in TCPD (> 75 μm); however, CCG molecules have been reported as inhibiting global transcriptional activation at ≥ 25 μm [43]. Similarly, VP inhibitors presented an important nonspecific effect by promoting protein crosslinking at > 1 μm [47]. The specificity of MRTFs and YAP/TAZ inhibitors must be taken into consideration in further protocols, and the use of controlled mechanical conditions such as PAA HGs and microfluidic platforms [5, 69] appear to be the best alternatives to control the activation of MRTcoF in vitro. Even using both methodologies was not sufficient to inhibit transcriptional activation of some MRTcoF‐dependent genes. It seems that a full inhibition may be performed in parallel with the blockage of actin polymerisation, contractibility or SRC/ERK/PI3K pathways (latrunculin B, blebbistatin and dasatinib/U0126/LY294002, respectively) because all these signals are upstream of the activation of MRTcoF [22, 70]. Finally, we demonstrated that the response of MRTcoF to stiffness depends on magnitude and time in hepatocytes, and this effect correlated with differential expression of their target genes. This evidence suggests that the progressive response of hepatocytes in liver fibrosis is an intricate process that depends on the extent of ECM deposition and progression, where mechanical sensitive factors form a variety of transcriptional complexes that regulate metabolism, proliferation and survival, and the response to mechanics and signalling pathways.

Animals and induction of fibrosis with CCl4

CRL‐WI Wistar rats from the Instituto de Fisiología Celular‐UNAM and Instututo de Neurobiología‐UNAM, were housed in an 8 m2 room with ventilation and noise control. Animals (four per cages) were exposed to 12 h light/12 dark cycle and feeding ad libitum, as the Mexican Official legislation NOM‐062‐ZOO‐1999 indicates. The induction of fibrosis was done as previously reported [24]. Briefly, 250 g males of an age between 6–8 weeks received CCl4 in sterile corn oil and 0.5 mL of the mixture (1 : 3) was administered via intraperitoneal injection three times a week. The mock control group of animals received only corn oil. This protocol was approved by the bioethics committee of Instituto de Neurobiología‐UNAM (project number 84‐A). Approximately 1 cm3 liver pieces were removed for cell fractionation and RNA extraction. Animals were sacrificed after one or two months of treatment with CCl4.

Histological staining and immunohistochemistry assays (IHC)

Liver sections were fixed in 10% formalin for subsequence processing. Liver slices of 5 μm were obtained using standard methods. Masson's trichrome staining was used to outline collagen fibres with blue [24]. For IHC assays, samples were permeabilised with 0.1% of Tween 20 and incubated with 2% of BSA (blocking solution) at RT. The primary antibody was incubated at RT in blocking solution and Linker and Tracer reagents were used subsequently (Cat. No. KP50). Afterwards, samples were incubated with amber‐coloured DAB (Cat. No. K047) at RT and were contrasted with Gill's hematoxylin. The evaluation was done under histopathological criteria. All primary antibodies used in immunodetection assay are listed in Table S6.

Mechanical characterisation

Relaxation tests were performed using the FT‐MTA03 Micromechanical Testing and Assembly System (FemtoTools AG, Zürich, Switzerland) and analysed as mentioned in [25]. Time vs force data were obtained with a FT‐S200 tip (a glass spherical tip with a 50 μm diameter, a measurement range of ± 200 μN, and a maximum resolution of 0.0005 μN at a sampling rate of 10 Hz). The relaxation curves were normalised at the maximum force value and fitted to the generalised Maxwell model using the cross‐validation criterion to determine the approximation grade (n) of the relaxation modulus E n (t). We used the long‐term stiffness E l of the relaxation modulus to compare the stiffness modifications between mock and CCl4‐treated livers as the long‐term stiffness represents the pure elastic behaviour when E l = E(t → ∞). For both conditions, liver sections were measured in at least 10 different positions.

Hepatocytes isolation and culture

For primary hepatocytes isolation, the bioethics committee of the Facultad de Ciencias‐UNAM approved the project PI_2019_02_004. Rat hepatocytes were isolated using the collagenase perfusion method described previously [71]. Hepatocytes were collected and purified by isodensity Percoll® centrifugation. Fresh isolated hepatocytes (FH) were suspended in William's E medium. For culture, isolated cells were seeded in attachment medium (DMEM‐F12 supplemented with 2 mm GlutaMAX, 1 mm sodium pyruvate, 10 mm HEPES, 0.5 μg·mL−1 amphotericin B, 1X ITS, and 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) plus Penicillin–Streptomycin) for 3 h at standard culture conditions (5% CO2 and 37 °C). After attachment, either PAA HGs or TCPD, hepatocytes were maintained in the feeding medium (FBS‐free attachment medium). Inhibitors were incubated for 4 h or 48 h at concentrations mentioned in each experimental condition.

Protein extraction and immunoblotting

For protein analysis, FH and PH were rinsed once with pre‐chilled DPBS and subsequently lysed with RIPA buffer (50 mm Tris–HCl pH 7.4, 150 mm NaCl, 1 mm EDTA, 0.5% sodium deoxycholate, 0.1% SDS, and 1% Nonidet) plus cOmplete™. Liver sections were fractionated and lysed with RIPA. Lysates were centrifuged at maximum speed and soluble fraction (supernatant) was collected; the insoluble fraction (pellets) was also collected when mentioned. Proteins were separated by SDS polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS‐PAGE) and transferred to a PVDF membrane. Primary antibodies were incubated overnight and horseradish peroxidase (HRP)‐coupled secondary antibodies against heavy and light (H + L) chains of immunoglobulin G (IgG) were incubated at RT. Enhanced chemiluminescence assay (ECL) was used for protein detection. All primary antibodies used in immunodetection assay are listed in Table S6.

Semiquantitative RT‐PCR assays

Liver pieces, FH or PH were resuspended with TRIzol reagent or prepared using the Direct‐zol RNA miniprep kit (Cat. No. R2052; Zymo Research, Irvine, CA, USA) to obtain purified RNA. Synthesised first‐strand cDNA was prepared by using M‐MLV Reverse Transcriptase. PCR reactions were set up using Taq DNA polymerase (Cat. No. 10342020). Each amplicon was amplified at different annealing Tm and cycles to verify specificity and saturation. PCR products and immunoblots were visualised in a ChemiDoc XRS System. Densitometry analysis was performed using free fiji software. The primer sequences are listed in Table S7.

Quantitative PCR (qPCR) assays

Gene expression profiles were evaluated using SYBR Green chemistry (NZYSpeedy qPCR Green Master Mix), 50 ng of cDNA per sample, and analysed in a StepOne™ Real‐Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems,Foster City, CA, USA). The amplification conditions were performed as follows: DNA polymerase activation and initial cDNA denaturation at 95 °C for 10 min, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 15 s and annealing/extension for 30 s at 55–61 °C as annealing temperature (T m) for each target gene. The Gapdh gene was used as an internal control, while nuclease‐free water was included as a negative control instead of cDNA. Relative gene expression was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method. The primer sequences are listed in Table S7.

Immunofluorescence assays

Immunofluorescence performed in PH cultured on PAA HG or glass coverslips is described as done previously [23]. Hepatocytes were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde at 37 °C for 20 min. Fixed cells were permeabilised with 0.1% Triton X‐100 at RT. After permeabilisation, samples were incubated in 10% of horse serum in DPBS (blocking solution) at RT, and primary antibodies were incubated in blocking solution ON. Alexa594‐coupled, Alexa 488‐coupled and Cy3‐coupled secondary antibodies against H + L IgG were used for primary antibody detection. Alexa 488‐coupled phalloidin was used for F‐actin detection. Nuclei were stained with DAPI and samples were mounted with Mowiol®. All primary antibodies used in immunodetection assay are listed in Table S6. Deconvoluted fluorescence images were captured using a Leica Thunder Imaging System or TCS SP8 laser scanning confocal microscope (63X objective; Leica Microsystems, Wetzlar, Germany). Also, a confocal laser scanning microscope (63X objective, ZEISS LSM 800) and Fluoview FV10i (60X objective, Olympus) were used for imaging capture. Images were edited by using free fiji software.

Cell fractionation

Pieces from mock and CCl4‐treated DPBS‐perfused livers were cut approximately 1 cm3 and placed into a pre‐chilled dounce grinder tube with a homogenisation solution (250 mm of sucrose, 3 mm of Imidazole at pH 7.4 plus cOmplete™) for cell fractionation as previously described [72]. Isolated and cultured hepatocytes were resuspended in the homogenisation solution and placed into a pre‐chilled dounce grinder tube. Samples were homogenised with a pestle attached to a power drill until nuclei were separated. Then, cellular extracts were centrifuged at 620 g for 10 min. The pellet (nuclear) and supernatant (cytoplasmic) fractions were lysed with RIPA buffer (50 mm Tris–HCl pH 7.4, 150 mm NaCl, 1 mm EDTA, 0.5% sodium deoxycholate, 0.1% SDS, and 1% Nonidet) plus cOmplete™ for 2 h at 4 °C in agitation. Lysates were centrifuged at maximum speed for 10 min to remove insoluble material.

Fabrication of polyacrylamide hydrogels (PAA HGs)

PAA HGs were polymerised as described previously [38], and elastic modules were previously confirmed in [25]. Specific combinations of acrylamide:bisacrylamide (0.5, 1, 5 and 20 kPa) were deposited and polymerised with ammonium persulfate/TEMED on glass coverslips, which were previously functionalised with a solution of 2% of TMSPMA and 1% acetic acid in absolute ethanol for 20 min at room temperature. After polymerisation, 1 mm of sulfo‐SANPAH was activated at the surface of PAA HGs with a 365 nm UV crosslinker (UVP crosslinker, AnalytikJena) for 15 min. Then, active PAA HGs were incubated in 0.1 mg·mL−1 of rat tail collagen type I in 50 mm HEPES overnight at 4 °C. Petri dishes or coverslips were used as stiff controls.

Differential expression analysis

RNA sequencing data from the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) with accession number GSE110096 was used [27]. Samples corresponding to mineral oil‐(mock) and CCl4‐treated rat livers were included in the analysis. Raw FASTQ files were initially assessed for quality using fastqc (v0.12.1) [73]. Adapter trimming and quality filtering were performed with fastp (v0.23.4) [74]. Cleaned reads were aligned to the rat reference genome (mRatBN7.2) using star (v2.7.11b) [75] in two‐pass mode to improve splice junction detection. Alignment quality was evaluated using qualimap (v2.3) [76]. Gene‐level read counts were generated with featureCounts (v2.0.8) using the Rattus_norvegicus.GRCr8.115.gtf annotation file.

Differential expression analysis between mock and CCl4‐treated livers was performed in R (v4.4.2) using the DESeq2 package (v1.46.0) [77]. Genes displaying an adjusted P value < 0.05 and an absolute log2 fold change ≥ 1 were considered differentially expressed. For cross‐species regulatory analyses, rat genes were mapped to their human orthologues using the biomaRt R package (v2.58.2) [28]. To identify putative MRTcoF transcriptional targets, an R/Bioconductor workflow was implemented. Promoter regions were defined as the 3000 bp upstream of the transcription start site and retrieved from the human genome assembly (hg38). Motif scanning was performed using position weight matrices from the JASPAR database [29] for SRF and TEAD family transcription factors (TEAD1–4), implemented through the tfbstools package (v1.40.0) [30]. Motif occurrences were considered significant when they reached a relative PWM score ≥ 80% of the maximal score and an associated P‐value < 1 × 10−4. Genes containing at least one significant motif match for SRF and/or TEAD1–4 within the defined promoter regions were retained for downstream analyses.

Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was performed using clusterProfiler (v4.10.1) [78] with Gene Ontology (GO) annotations to identify biological processes enriched among MRTcoF‐associated gene sets. Differential expression analysis was executed within a reproducible Nextflow pipeline (v25.04.7.5955) [79] using docker (v25.0.1) containers [80]. The pipeline is publicly available at: https://github.com/INMEGEN/Pipelines_Inmegen/tree/Principal/QDEA‐RNAseq.

Fluorescence intensity quantification and densitometry analysis

Fluorescence intensity quantification of one slide and Z‐stack images captured by confocal microscopy were obtained with the fiji software. Both images were converted to 8‐bits. For Z‐stack images, a maximum intensity projection was applied to the image stack. Automatic regions of interest (ROI) were obtained around the nucleus on the fluorescent channel corresponding to DAPI. The selected ROIs were added to the ROI manager and were subsequently applied to the protein of interest fluorescence channel for obtaining the Area, Mean and Integrated density. Background ROIs of the background of each image were also obtained to calculate the Corrected Total Cell Fluorescence (CTCF). For protein band quantification, the enhanced chemiluminescence was acquired and digitalised with the ChemiDoc XRS System and the densitometry analysis was obtained with the fiji software using the Analyze > Gels option. Finally, it was divided by the percentage of density of its corresponding loading control band.

Traction force microscopy (TFM)

PAA HGs were polymerised as described previously in Fabrication of polyacrylamide hydrogels (PAA HGs). In addition, 0.12% (v/v) of 0.2 μm fluorescent microspheres was added to the specific combinations of acrylamide : bisacrylamide for 10 kPa PAA HGs. HGs were allowed to polymerise in 20 mm glass‐bottom dishes (Cat. 801001, NEST). After protein crosslinking, HGs were sterilised with DPBS plus antibiotics. PH were cultured for 24 and 72 h in the feeding medium. A Zeiss Confocal laser scanning microscope (LSM 800) with a 20X objective was used. LSM 800 was customised with a cell chamber that maintained a 5% CO2 atmosphere at 37 °C. DIC fields of PH were selected, and a stack of DIC and fluorescent images was captured from the focused cell to a depth of 10 μm on the HGs surface. Then PH were detached by adding SDS carefully to 1%. After adding SDS, the upper and lower limits of the Z‐stack were adjusted due to the expansion of the PAA HGs.

Free software fiji was used for TFM analysis. PH borders were limited by DIC images. Fluorescent microspheres beneath the cells were selected and projected. A pair of images were generated from the bead projections, before and after the PH were detached with SDS. The Z‐stack images then aligned to correct shifts stemmed from HGs expansion, using linear stack alignment with SIFT plug‐in. TFM analysis was performed as previously described in [81]. Briefly, the Particle Image Velocimetry (PIV) plug‐in with the normalised correlation coefficient algorithm was used. For images measuring 200 × 200 μm, the smallest interrogation window was set at 5.4 μm with a correlation threshold of 0.6. The Fourier Transform Traction Cytometry (FTTC) plug‐in was subsequently used to calculate traction stress and generate the heat map. FTTC was run with a Poisson's ratio of 0.48, a regularisation factor of 0 and Young's modulus of 10 kPa. To calculate strain energy, PIV and FTTC x and y displacements, as well as stress components, were utilised. The actual interrogation window area was calculated and multiplied by stress components to obtain the force. Displacements were then converted to meters. Then, the equation described in [82] was used to obtain strain energy.

Statistical analysis

The statistical analysis was performed using graphpad prism (graphpad Software Version 11, Expiration: June 30, 2026). For multiple comparisons, one‐way analysis of variance (one‐way ANOVA) followed by the Tukey or Dunnett post hoc test were employed to calculate statistical significance. Alternatively, for comparison between two groups paired and unpaired Student's test (t) was used. Differences were considered statistically significant when P values were equal to or less than 0.05 but particular P values are specified in some experiments. All statistical analyses are shown in detail in Table S5. S.E.M is shown in the mean of the experimental conditions described in the text.
