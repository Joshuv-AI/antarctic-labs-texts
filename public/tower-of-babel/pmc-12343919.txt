Impact of Instagram and TikTok influencer marketing on perceptions of e-cigarettes and perceptions of influencers in young adults: a randomised survey-based experiment
Authors: Julia Vassey, Erin A Vogel, Jennifer B Unger, Junhan Cho, Dayoung Bae, Scott I Donaldson, Jon-Patrick Allem
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPairing e-cigarettes with healthy lifestyle contexts in influencers’ promotional social media posts may increase e-cigarette use risk among young adults. This study examined the effects of e-cigarette and healthy lifestyle content on young adults’ perceptions of influencer credibility, harm perceptions of, and susceptibility to use, e-cigarettes.MethodsIn this survey-based online, repeated-measures experiment (2023), California young adults (N=1494, Mage=23 (SD=1.8); 63% female, 51% Hispanic) were randomly shown 10 (total) Instagram and TikTok videos, featuring influencers promoting e-cigarettes alongside healthy lifestyle activities (experimental group), or a healthy lifestyle activity alone (control). After watching each video, participants rated perceived influencer credibility (eg, honesty). After watching all videos, harm perceptions of e-cigarettes were assessed among all participants and susceptibility to e-cigarette use was assessed among e-cigarette never-users. Harm perceptions and susceptibility outcomes were compared between groups (experimental vs control) and between participants who perceived influencers as credible versus non-credible, using binomial generalised linear mixed effects models.ResultsParticipants in the experimental group were more likely to report lower harm perceptions (adjusted OR (AOR) 1.12; 95% CI 1.03; 1.21) and higher susceptibility to e-cigarette use (AOR 1.22, 95% CI 1.11; 1.34), than participants in the control condition. Similar results were found when influencers were perceived as credible, but not when they were perceived as non-credible.ConclusionsSocial media influencers’ posts promoting e-cigarette use along with healthy activities may contribute to young adult e-cigarette use. Decreasing perceived credibility of influencers could decrease the negative effects of their promotional posts.Trial registration numberNCT06433466.

Introduction

Promotional tobacco-related content posted by influencers on behalf of tobacco brands is present on social media platforms (eg, Instagram, TikTok) popular among Generation Z (ie, those 11–26 years of age in 2024).15 This content exists despite Instagram’s and TikTok’s community guidelines that restrict paid advertisements or promotions of tobacco products, including influencer marketing.68 For example, a national survey of adolescents and young adults aged 13–26 (N=1163) showed that among those who reported seeing e-cigarette-related content on social media, over 40% reported seeing celebrity or influencer posts.9 Other studies have shown that young adult never-users of e-cigarettes exposed to tobacco content from celebrities or influencers (vs non-exposed) were more likely to initiate tobacco product use, including e-cigarettes, a year later,10 while adolescent e-cigarette never-users were at higher odds of susceptibility to e-cigarette use.11 Consistent with social learning theory (SLT),12 13 the findings may suggest that consumers perceive influencers as trusted, credible sources of information and adopt behaviours (eg, e-cigarette use) through observing and modelling influencers.14 Understanding how influencers’ promotional posts for tobacco products affect youth can inform targeted policies to counter such marketing.

Since consumers perceive influencers as authentic, trustworthy, relatable and credible,15 brands have increased their reliance on influencers,15 16 especially microinfluencers and nanoinfluencers (users with about 1000–100 000 followers). Brands, including e-cigarette companies, offer paid partnerships to microinfluencers and nanoinfluencers to market their products,17 because of the cost-effectiveness of these non-celebrity influencers (vs celebrities). Financial compensation may motivate social media content creators to collaborate with tobacco brands and promote harmful content on social media.

Prior research has explored the effects of celebrity and non-celebrity influencer marketing on youth perceptions and use patterns of e-cigarettes.1821 One survey experiment found that young adults who viewed Instagram posts of non-celebrity influencers promoting e-cigarettes and recognised that the posts were promotional (based on tobacco brand sponsorship disclosure) had more negative perceptions of influencers than participants who did not recognise posts as promotional.21 Another experiment assessing the impact of nicotine warning labels in e-cigarette promotions by microinfluencers found that, regardless of the warning label, perceiving microinfluencers as credible (eg, honest, trustworthy and knowledgeable) was associated with lower harm perceptions and greater susceptibility to e-cigarette use among adolescents.20 Consistent with the Prototype Willingness Model (PWM), these findings suggest that young people are more willing to engage in a health-risky behaviour (eg, e-cigarette use) when they have a positive view of the prototypical person (eg, an influencer) who engages in the behaviour.20 22 23

Influencers sometimes promote e-cigarettes on social media alongside lifestyle posts featuring healthy activities (eg, exercising), which could make e-cigarette content more appealing to social media users and contribute to substance use among youth.2 24 Similar marketing tactics were used by tobacco brands in the past to advertise combustible cigarettes next to sports-related imagery featuring models.25 However, the effects of e-cigarette promotion in these healthy lifestyle contexts on perceptions and use patterns of e-cigarettes, and on perceptions of influencers, among youth remain unknown.

This study assessed the impact of posts from social media microinfluencers, promoting e-cigarettes alongside healthy lifestyle activities, on perceived influencer credibility, harm perceptions of, and susceptibility to use, e-cigarettes among young adults. Harm perceptions of, and susceptibility to use, e-cigarettes could provide insights into reasons for use or intention to use e-cigarettes among youth.19 20 26 Seeing influencers using e-cigarettes in positive contexts such as healthy lifestyle behaviour may reduce youths’ harm perception of e-cigarettes, which is consistent with SLT.12 13 We hypothesised that young adults who viewed videos of microinfluencers promoting e-cigarettes alongside healthy lifestyle activities (experimental condition) would report lower harm perceptions of e-cigarettes (H1) and higher susceptibility to e-cigarette use (H2), compared with those who viewed videos of microinfluencers showing healthy lifestyle alone (control condition). We also tested an interaction between the experimental condition and influencer credibility consistent with the PWM, hypothesising that young adults who perceived influencers as credible in the experimental condition (vs control) would report lower harm perceptions of e-cigarettes (H3) and higher susceptibility to e-cigarette use (H4).22 23

Materials and methods

Participants and procedures

In May–June 2023, young adults (18–24 years of age) living in California were recruited by YouGov (via online advertising, social media and website sign-ups) to participate in a survey on tobacco-related attitudes and behaviours. YouGov, a research panel agency, has been used in prior research to survey young adults about their tobacco-related attitudes and behaviours.27 28 Respondents (N=1500) were matched to a sampling frame based on gender, age, race and education. This frame was a politically representative model of US adults based on the American Community Survey. The matched cases were weighted using propensity score matching. Participants were provided with a survey URL link. After screening eligible, participants provided informed consent and completed the survey online. At the end of the survey, participants were debriefed via a screen prompt about risks of exposure to tobacco content on social media associated with e-cigarette and other substance use. To ensure data quality, YouGov conducted fraud detection to check for duplicate IP addresses, bots or speeding. Low-quality responses were replaced with new responses. Each participant was offered a credit that could be redeemable toward a gift card, equivalent to US$1.50.

Experimental stimuli development

The experimental videos were created from Instagram and TikTok posts of 10 microinfluencers who had disclosed (in profile descriptions or captions to posts) that they were sponsored by e-cigarette companies.2 Tobacco sponsorship disclosures were not shown in the videos. These microinfluencers had public accounts, had posted e-cigarette content, and, in some instances, had posted e-cigarette use alongside a healthy lifestyle activity. To ensure that the content of the stimuli only differed by the characteristics of interest, the posts in the treatment condition were altered (ie, removing the e-cigarette from view) to serve as stimuli in the control condition. To create homogeneous stimuli (ie, to ensure that influencers were perceived similarly by age and gender), 10 undergraduate students reviewed the stimuli, answering the questions about the perceived gender and age of the influencers (online supplemental table 1). Posts with the highest rating agreement and highest rating scores (8 or above) were selected for the experiment (five male and five female profiles, perceived age range: 21–30). The videos featured influencers working out in a gym or outdoors, doing yoga and hiking. The influencers were notified via Instagram messaging about the use of their content in a research study.

Experimental design

Each participant viewed 10 posts (10 s videos featuring 10 different microinfluencers) assigned using simple randomisation. The posts differed across two conditions: (1) influencers promoting e-cigarettes alongside a healthy lifestyle activity (experimental condition) or (2) influencers promoting a healthy lifestyle activity alone (control, figure 1). The perceived influencer credibility was assessed after each video. Harm perceptions of, and susceptibility to use, e-cigarettes were assessed immediately after participants watched all the videos (online supplemental file 1).

Measures

Harm perceptions of and susceptibility to e-cigarette use

Harm perceptions of e-cigarettes were measured (among all participants including users and non-users of e-cigarettes) using one validated item from the Population Assessment of Tobacco and Health (PATH) survey.29 Responses to the question, ‘Do you think vaping e-cigarettes is harmful to your health?’ were measured on a Likert scale, ranging from ‘definitely yes’ (1) and ‘probably yes’ (2) to ‘probably not (3)’ and ‘definitely not (4)’. A higher score represented lower harm perception (online supplemental table 1). Consistent with prior research,19 the measure was dichotomised with responses ‘definitely yes’ coded as ‘higher harm perception’ and responses ‘probably yes’, ‘probably not’ or ‘definitely not’ coded as ‘lower harm perception’. Susceptibility to use e-cigarettes was measured (among never-users of e-cigarettes), using the validated three-item scale adapted from PATH.29 Three items: (1) Have you ever been curious about vaping e-cigarettes?, (2) Do you think you will try vaping e-cigarettes soon? and (3) If one of your best friends were to offer you an e-cigarette, would you use it? were combined into one variable. Consistent with prior research,30 the measure was dichotomised with responses ‘definitely not’ to all items coded as ‘not susceptible’ and responses ‘probably not’, ‘probably yes’ or ‘definitely yes’ coded as ‘susceptible’.

Perceived influencer credibility

The perceived influencer credibility for six personality traits (ie, honesty, trustworthiness, knowledge, intelligence, attractiveness and popularity) were assessed for each of the 10 microinfluencers after each of the 10 videos using a 0–100 scale validated in prior research.19 20 31 online supplemental table 2 shows the scores per each of the 6 traits in each of the 10 videos featuring microinfluencers. The items representing the six traits were combined (by computing the mean of the six trait scores) into one variable because the items were closely related, α=0.97. The mean ‘influencer credibility perception’ score (ie, the mean of the six trait scores) was computed for each video.

Participant characteristics

The participants reported sociodemographic characteristics (ie, age, sex assigned at birth, sexual orientation, race and ethnicity), past-month substance use (ie, cigarettes, e-cigarettes (not included for assessment of susceptibility among e-cigarette never-users), cannabis and alcohol), frequency of social media use and exposure to celebrity, or influencer, pro-e-cigarette content on social media in the past 30 days.

Statistical analyses

Main effects models

To test the hypotheses, we used main effects models, interaction effects models and multigroup analyses. To test H1 and H2, we examined the main effects of the experimental condition (ie, exposure to videos of microinfluencers promoting e-cigarettes alongside healthy lifestyle activities) on harm perceptions of e-cigarettes (H1) and susceptibility to e-cigarette use (H2), (assessed after all the videos), adjusting for perceived influencer credibility (assessed after each of the 10 videos featuring microinfluencers). We used binary logistic mixed effects regression models to assess harm perceptions among all the participants who completed the experiment (N=1494) and susceptibility among never-users of e-cigarettes (n=962). Repeated measures in study conditions (ie, perceptions of influencer credibility) were modelled as random effects (ie, a random intercept). This approach was chosen because prior research suggested that a substantial bias could exist when stimulus repetitions go unanalysed as a random effect.32

Interaction effects models and multigroup analyses

To test the moderating effects of perceived influencer credibility on associations of the experimental condition (ie, exposure to videos of microinfluencers promoting e-cigarettes alongside healthy lifestyle activities) with harm perceptions of, and susceptibility to use, e-cigarettes, the interaction term of experimental condition X perceived influencer credibility (as a fixed predictor) was added to the subsequent interaction mixed effects models (that also included perceived influencer credibility as a random intercept).

To interpret the significant interaction effects, multigroup analyses using mixed effects models were performed to assess the effects of the experimental condition on harm perceptions of e-cigarettes (H3) and susceptibility to e-cigarette use (H4) stratified by the level of influencer credibility perceptions (ie, high or the influencer credibility perception score at or above the mean, vs low, or the score below the mean). In all the main effects, interaction effects and stratified models, we organised the data in the long format, treating influencer credibility as a single variable across all 10 videos.

Covariates

All models were adjusted for fixed effects of sociodemographic characteristics, substance use, social media use and exposure to celebrity or influencer pro-e-cigarette content described in the Participant characteristics section above. Covariates were assessed before the exposure to the experimental stimuli. The choice of covariates was guided by prior research because these covariates were expected to be associated with the outcome variables.1820 Adjustment for self-reported exposure to e-cigarette content posted by celebrities or influencers accounted for participants’ familiarity with this type of content on social media (prior to assessment of the perceived influencer credibility in the experiment).

Supplementary sensitivity analyses

To verify robustness of the findings, we performed post hoc analyses to assess the effects of the experimental condition on harm perceptions of, and susceptibility to use, e-cigarettes using different cut-off values of the influencer credibility perception score. We stratified the samples by the level of the perceived influencer credibility: mean plus 1 SD and mean minus 1 SD cut-off values (ie, high: perception scores ≥mean + 1 SD; low: scores≤mean −1 SD).

We also assessed harm perceptions of, and susceptibility to use, e-cigarettes as dichotomised outcomes when responses of ‘definitely not’ and ‘probably not’ were coded as ‘lower harm perception’ and ‘not susceptible’, respectively, and responses of ‘probably yes’ and ‘definitely yes’ were coded as ‘higher harm perception’ and ‘susceptible’, respectively. We also assessed each of the three susceptibility items separately, dichotomising responses in each item as ‘definitely not’ versus ‘probably not,’ ‘probably yes’, or ‘definitely yes’. We used binary logistic mixed effects regression models to conduct sensitivity analyses for all dichotomised outcomes. Finally, we applied proportional odds (cumulative link) mixed models to assess harm perception of e-cigarettes as an ordinal outcome.

Statistical significance and power analysis

Adjusted ORs (AORs) with 95% CIs were reported with statistical significance at p<0.05 (two tailed) after applying Benjamini-Hochberg multiple testing corrections to control for the false discovery rate at 0.05. Sample sizes for all experimental conditions are reported in figure 2. Based on the a priori power analysis calculated in G*Power V.3.1, we would have achieved 95% power on the sample size of 793 participants to assess harm perceptions of, and susceptibility to use, e-cigarettes. Statistical analyses were conducted using R V.4.2.2. The dataset and the code are publicly available.

Results

Sample characteristics

Among 1500 randomised young adults, 1494 completed the experiment. The participants were Mage=23 (SD=1.8); 63% (n=935) female; 51% (n=766) Hispanic; 18% (n=272) reported using e-cigarettes in the past 30 days; 17% (n=260) reported past 30-day exposure to pro-e-cigarette-related social media content posted by celebrities or influencers (table 1).

Main effects models

H1 and H2 were supported. The main effect of the experimental condition (influencer e-cigarette promotion alongside healthy lifestyle activities) on harm perceptions of e-cigarettes (H1) was significant, after adjusting for perceptions of influencer credibility. Participants (N=1494) in the experimental condition reported higher odds of lower harm perceptions of e-cigarettes (AOR 1.12; 95% CI 1.03; 1.21, table 2). The perceived influencer credibility modelled as a random intercept had a SD of 0.48, indicating variability of the outcome across different levels of this predictor and suggesting that it contributes to differences in the outcome beyond the fixed effects. The main effect of the experimental condition on susceptibility to e-cigarette use (H2) was also significant, after adjusting for perceptions of influencer credibility. Participants (never-users of e-cigarettes, N=962) in the experimental condition reported higher odds of susceptibility to e-cigarette use (AOR 1.22; 95% CI 1.11;1.34, table 2).

Interaction effects models and multigroup analyses

We found significant interaction effects of the experimental condition X perceived influencer credibility on harm perceptions of, and susceptibility to use, e-cigarettes (table 2 and figure 3). Among the participants (n=804) who perceived microinfluencers as credible (≥mean of the influencer credibility perception score), 55% in the experimental condition vs 31% in the control condition had lower harm perceptions of e-cigarettes, (p<0.001). Among the participants (n=690) who perceived microinfluencers as non-credible (< the mean of the influencer credibility perception score), 18% in the experimental condition vs 31% in the control condition had lower harm perceptions of e-cigarettes, (p<0.001). Among never-users of e-cigarettes who perceived microinfluencers as credible (n=462), 68% in the experimental condition vs 48% in the control condition were susceptible to e-cigarette use (p<0.001), (figure 3).

Proportion of participants with lower harm perceptions of e-cigarettes and proportion of participants susceptible to e-cigarette use in the experimental and control conditions. Higher influencer credibility perception score represents the values at or above the mean. Lower influencer credibility perception score represents values below the mean. Differences in proportions between experimental and control conditions among participants who reported higher or lower influencer credibility perception score were compared using two-sample test for equality of proportions with continuity correction.

H3 and H4 were supported for the multigroup analyses of associations between the experimental condition and each outcome, stratified by the influencer credibility perception score (table 2). Among the participants (n=804) who perceived microinfluencers as credible, those in the experimental condition had higher odds of lower harm perceptions of e-cigarettes (AOR 2.31; 95% CI 2.08; 2.56) compared with those in the control condition (H3). Among participants who perceived microinfluencers as non-credible (n=690), those in the experimental condition had lower odds of lower harm perceptions of e-cigarettes (AOR 0.52; 95% CI 0.46; 0.59) compared with those in the control condition.

Among never-users of e-cigarettes who perceived microinfluencers as credible (n=516), participants in the experimental condition had higher odds of susceptibility to use e-cigarettes (AOR 1.94; 95% CI 1.69; 2.22) compared with the participants in the control condition (H4), (table 2). Among never-users of e-cigarettes who perceived microinfluencers as non-credible (n=446), there was no significant difference in susceptibility between experimental and control conditions.

Supplementary sensitivity analyses

Results of the sensitivity multigroup analyses on the samples stratified by the level of the perceived influencer credibility using mean+1 SD and mean–1 SD cut-off values remained consistent with the results of the post hoc analyses. Results of the sensitivity analyses with alternatively coded harm perceptions and susceptibility outcomes remained consistent and are reported in online supplemental tables 3 and 4. In the main effects models, only the effect for curiosity about using e-cigarettes, but not intentions to use them, was significant. Results for the interaction effect and stratified analysis models remained consistent with those for the combined susceptibility outcome (online supplemental table 5).

Discussion

Using a randomised survey-based experiment, this study examined non-celebrity influencer marketing tactics of promoting e-cigarettes in healthy lifestyle contexts (eg, exercising) in Instagram or TikTok videos on harm perceptions and susceptibility to e-cigarette use among young adults. Adding to prior research that explored the effects of celebrity or non-celebrity influencer marketing on youth perceptions and use patterns of e-cigarettes,1821 this study demonstrated that microinfluencer promotion of e-cigarettes in healthy lifestyle contexts affects young adult perceptions of e-cigarettes and that influencer perception moderates the e-cigarette perceptions.

Participants who viewed videos of microinfluencers promoting e-cigarette use paired with a healthy lifestyle were more likely to report lower harm perceptions of, and higher susceptibility to use, e-cigarettes. The findings support SLT, which emphasises learning through observation and imitation of others’ behaviours (eg, influencers).12 13 Seeing influencers showing e-cigarettes alongside healthy lifestyle behaviour may reduce risk perception of, and increase interest in, e-cigarettes. However, the results for the main effects models in the sensitivity analyses showed higher odds for curiosity about e-cigarette use, but not intentions to use e-cigarettes. Although social media might not produce immediate intentions to use e-cigarettes among never-users, it still might raise their curiosity and openness toward e-cigarette use. This is consistent with the PWM, which posits that young people may engage in a risky behaviour under certain circumstances even if they do not explicitly intend to.22 23

Participants in the treatment condition were also more likely to report lower harm perceptions of, and higher susceptibility to use, e-cigarettes (including curiosity and intentions to use) if they perceived microinfluencers as credible, but less likely to report lower harm perceptions of, and intentions to use, e-cigarettes if they perceived microinfluencers as non-credible. These findings also support the PWM,22 23 suggesting that people are more willing to engage in a health risk behaviour to the extent that they have a positive view of the prototypical person who performs that behaviour. While influencers may advertise unhealthy products to pursue commercial interests, consumers of their content (including youth) may perceive these influencers as trusted, credible and authentic sources.33 The findings suggest that exposure to such promotional content could contribute to initiation of, experimentation with, or continued use of, e-cigarettes among youth.

Influencer e-cigarette-related marketing on social media is not sufficiently regulated by platforms’ community guidelines or federal legislation. Although most popular social media platforms (eg, Facebook, Instagram, YouTube, Twitter, TikTok, Reddit and Snapchat) prohibit paid advertisements for tobacco products, few (ie, Facebook, Instagram and TikTok) explicitly prohibit influencers from promoting nicotine/tobacco products.8 19 However, influencers still promote e-cigarettes on the platforms (ie, Facebook, Instagram and TikTok) that have restrictions in place and do not always comply with federal marketing regulations.23436 According to the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) and the Federal Trade Commission (FTC) guidelines, if influencers have any material connection with a brand—meaning that they have been paid or given something of value to tout a product or service—such relationships must be disclosed in their social media posts.37 Yet, enforcement is inconsistent, and many influencers’ posts have ambiguous or absent disclosures of their partnerships with brands.2 Influencers who promote products on behalf of tobacco brands are also required to restrict access to e-cigarette marketing content to only individuals who are at or above the federal minimum age (ie, 21) of sale of tobacco products. However, microinfluencers have not been consistently applying youth age restrictions to their social media accounts.2

Regulating influencer marketing features (eg, prohibiting depiction of healthy lifestyle context next to e-cigarette products) could be challenging because it could fall under the umbrella of free speech and be protected by the First Amendment of the US Constitution.38 However, federal agencies and social media users (including youth) should be informed about such marketing tactics, especially if such content is youth-appealing. Authorities could consider prohibiting e-cigarette brands from hiring influencers under a certain age,3942 ban influencer advertising of e-cigarettes altogether, or penalise brands and influencers for non-compliance with federal marketing regulations such as restricting access to e-cigarette marketing content to youth, following the FTC guidelines for sponsorship disclosures or the FDA guidelines for nicotine warning labels. Further improvement in the enforcement of social media community guidelines is also necessary.43 44 Social media platforms could use efficient auto-detection algorithms to find and eliminate e-cigarette marketing content.34 Platforms could also strengthen the enforcement of existing age verification policies for users viewing e-cigarette-related content,4547 as well as guidelines for the disclosures of sponsored partnerships. In addition, platforms could strengthen and enforce existing monetisation (ie, earning revenue through content or ads) policies by demonetising or removing financial incentives for posting content that promotes e-cigarettes.8 Because influencers can be perceived as trusted sources of information,15 16 public health authorities could harness influencers for antitobacco messaging and in health literacy campaigns about the harmful effects of e-cigarette use or disseminating other positive public health messages.33 48

Limitations

These results may not generalise from California to young adults in other regions in the USA. However, concerns about generalisability are partially mitigated by the fact that the ethnic diversity of the sample represents the projected future ethnic diversity of the USA.49 The measures in this study were assessed at one point in time, and duration of exposure to the experimental stimuli was limited (100 s total). Repeated exposures at multiple points in time might have produced larger and longer-lasting effects. While the experimental stimuli were created from actual Instagram and TikTok posts featuring microinfluencers promoting e-cigarette products, the survey did not use an actual Instagram or TikTok interface to simulate the real-life social media user experience. We did not assess harm perceptions of, and susceptibility to use, e-cigarettes prior to the stimuli exposure, which could have introduced some uncertainty in whether the effects should be attributed to the experimental condition or pre-existing attitudes towards e-cigarettes. This concern was mitigated by randomising participants to experimental conditions and controlling for self-reported past-month e-cigarette use (not included for assessment of susceptibility among e-cigarette never-users), cigarette, cannabis and alcohol use, which capture behavioural patterns associated with openness to substance use.

Conclusions

This study showed that the influencer marketing tactic of depicting a healthy lifestyle alongside e-cigarette promotion in Instagram or TikTok videos may contribute to lower harm perceptions of, and higher susceptibility to use, e-cigarettes among young adults. Future research should examine the prevalence of other contexts and themes shown alongside nicotine and tobacco or other substance-related product promotions. Research also should assess the effects of this content on perceptions of substance use and influencer perceptions among youth with different sociodemographic characteristics, as well as whether tobacco companies use influencers to introduce new or promote existing e-cigarette products in violation of premarket authorisation requirements. Future research should also examine how sponsorship disclosures affect the perceived credibility of influencers in posts promoting e-cigarettes paired with contexts such as a healthy lifestyle or other activities (eg, videogaming). Our findings indicate the need for further regulation of tobacco-related influencer marketing on social media.
