Different methods for including retardation in hadronic interactions
M. De Sanctis

Abstract
A study of the retardation contributions to the hadronic quark interaction is performed in the coordinate space elaborating a classical electrodynamics procedure. The possibility of constructing a corresponding quantum operator is critically analyzed also performing some numerical matrix element calculations. A comparison of the model with the Feynman diagram calculation at tree level is studied, showing a substantial physical correspondence of the two models.

Introduction

The study of hadronic systems in terms of interacting constituent quarks still
represents an interesting field of investigation,
due to the nonperturbative character of QCD and to the difficulties
of the lattice calculations.

Focusing our attention on heavy
q~\bar{q}
systems, we recall that
the dynamics of the hadronic system can be reproduced by means of a relativistic
wave equation in which the constituent quarks interaction depends on
a specific potential.
In this respect, we cite here a series of works developed by the author
in the framework of a three-dimensional reduction of a two-body Dirac equation

[
1
,
2
,
3
,
4
,
5
,
6
,
7
]
.
The interested reader can find, particularly in
[
2
]
, the references to other
relativistic models for hadronic systems.

It has been possible to show that a good reproduction of the charmonium data
can be obtained by taking a
vector
interaction term,
that is reminiscent of the underlying QCD dynamics, plus a scalar (or mass) term
that reproduces the correct spin-orbit splittings and can be related
to the scalar resonances of the hadronic spectrum
[
3
,
5
]
.
However, the main role in the interaction is played by the vector term,
that can be represented by a Coulombic potential,
i. e.
given by the interchange of a massless particle, regularized by a chromo-electric charge distribution of the interacting quarks
[
1
]
.

The interaction vector potential is usually derived in the framework of a three-dimensional
relativistic wave equation, that is obtained by
reducing
the four-dimensional
Bethe-Salpeter equation
[
8
,
9
]
.
This treatment, that was originally developed for the study of
electromagnetically

bound systems, allows to add the retardation corrections by means of a cumbersome procedure

[
10
]

that relies on the smallness of the coupling constant and, for this reason,
is not straightforwardly
applicable in the case of strongly interacting particles.

Retardation corrections have been also calculated with
other terms of relativistic corrections
that are added, perturbatively, to a standard Schrödinger Hamiltonian

[
11
,
12
]
.
But this technique is now superseded by fully relativistic calculations.

In the standard
instantaneous
or
unretarded
approach,
the bound system is studied by means of
a relativistic three-dimensional equation
in which the interaction is represented by
a potential operator that depends only on the interquark distance

r
, completely ignoring the retardation effects of the interaction.
As it will be recalled in Subsect.
1.2
, this modelization,
also studied in more detail in Ref.
[
4
]
,
does not violates relativity

per se
.
Phenomenologically,
it is able to reproduce accurately the charmonium data.

Nevertheless, it is worthwhile to investigate the possible effects related to retardation,
taking into account that retardation
is generally considered a specific characteristic of particle interactions in field theory
and could give nonnegligible contributions in strongly interacting systems.

This point represents the main motivation of the present work.
In other words, we try to study different modelizations of the retardation effects
that can be used in hadronic constituent quark interacions.
The use of coordinate space will be emphasized in order to understand in a more intuitive way the contribution of the different terms.

We try to construct a retarded interaction
operator
by using a procedure that relies
on classical electrodynamics, more precisely, on the well-known Liénard-Wiechert (LW) construction, also assuming constant momenta of the interacting particles.

A series expansion of this operator is performed at the end of the procedure to calculate
numerically the operator matrix elements.

Finally, we compare our model with the tree-level Feynman diagram calculation,
showing a substantial physical agreement of the two approaches.
In this respect, the present work helps to understand the physical meaning of retardation
in coordinate space and, in this way, motivates further calculations
that can be developed directly in momentum space,
starting from the tree-level Feynman diagram interaction.

The problem faced in this article presents a high level of complexity, mainly related to
the treatment of time in microscopic systems and to the formulation of
a relativistic quantum model that could represent an
alternative

to standard quantum field theories.
No definitive conclusions will be drawn.
The logical and mathematical processes are shown in detail, not only for pedagogical reasons,
but also to allow the reader to criticize and improve the construction of whole model.

The contents of the work are organized as follows.
In Subsect.
1.1
we introduce the notation and the definitions that
are used in the article.
In Subsect.
1.2
we recall the construction of a relativistic interaction model

without
retardation, commenting on some related developments.
In Sect.
2
the classical derivation, based on the LW construction,
is explained.
Then, in Sect.
3
the corresponding quantum operator is constructed
in different forms, also studying some properties of this operator
by means of test numerical calculations.
Finally, in Sect.
4
, we study the relationship between our model and
the interaction given by the Feynman diagram at tree-level.
In the App.
A
, the expression of the Harmonic Oscillator (HO) wave functions
(used for the test calculations) is given.
The form of the momentum space matrix elements for the Feynman interaction
matrix elements is also outlined.

1.2
Relativistic interaction without retardation

In this subsection we recall synthetically
that retardation is not strictly necessary to formulate a relativistic interaction model.
This point has been discussed in Ref.
[
4
]
considering the relativistic (integral) wave equation
of that model, written in the momentum space.

Here we note that in the coordinate space, one can always define the CM interparticle distance

r
in a relativistic invariant form, by using a standard trick
[
13
]
.
Let us consider, in a generic reference frame, the 4-vector distance between two events:

r^{\mu}=(r^{0},\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle r$}})

(11)

where the time component
r^{0}
can take
any
form and
\textstyle r
is the interparticle spatial distance.

We introduce the
projected
4-vector distance

\rho^{\mu}=r^{\mu}-\left({\frac{r_{\nu}P^{\nu}}{M}}\right){\frac{P^{\mu}}{M}}

(12)

that, in the CM, takes the form

\rho^{\mu}_{{}_{CM}}=(0,\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle r$}}_{{}_{CM}})~.

(13)

In this way, the CM interparticle invariant distance is written as

r=\sqrt{-\rho^{\mu}\rho_{\mu}}

(14)

where, in the
l. h. s.
, the label
CM
has not been written explicitly.
Note that the last equation defines the variable that is used in the
instantaneous potential functions.

As a side remark, we mention the possibility of introducing an interaction given
by the sum of an unretarded contribution plus a retarded term,
as it is done in the Coulomb gauge in quantum electrodynamics
[
14
]
.
We shall not develop this point in the present paper.

2
The standard LW construction

The problem of retardation has been faced in the context
of classical electrodynamics

[
15
,
16
]
,
highlighting that the finite speed of light,
that originates retardation, does not allow to write down an
exact
Hamiltonian
for two (or more) interacting particles.
The retardation effects are taken into account as terms of relativistic corrections
in an expansion of the Hamiltonian in powers of
p/m
.
In this work we try to follow a different method,
writing an (initially) exact expression for the retarded interaction
by dropping the acceleration terms that,
classically, are related to the radiation emission.
This last effect. in a quantum model,
should be treated apart, in a different way.

Along the derivation, we take into account that
the expression for the interaction obtained by means of the classical calculation
must be transformed into a quantum operator for our study
of hadronic systems.
This last point will be then developed in Sect.
3
.

We start our calculation deriving the expression of the
retarded distance

between two interacting particles.
Then, we shall use this quantity in the LW retarded interaction.

We bear in mind that, in the context of standard quantum mechanics,
the
standard distance
between two particles is represented by an Hermitian operator
given by the difference of the position operators of the two particles,
at the same time.
On the other hand, we want to derive the
retarded distance
operator
whose expression depends (as it will be shown) on the
standard distance
and on the

momenta
of the two particles.
We perform the following derivation according to a classical procedure

[
15
,
16
]
,
with the aim of obtaining, finally, a quantum operator in which the time variable
does not explicitly appear.

In a given reference frame, the particle 1,
at the time
t^{\prime}
and
from the spatial point
\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle r$}}_{1}^{\prime}=\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle r$}}_{1}(t^{\prime})
, “emits” the vector field.
The emission event is defined as
r_{1}^{\prime\mu}=(t^{\prime},\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle r$}}_{1}^{\prime})
.
The field is “observed” (
i. e.
“received”) later,
at the time
t>t^{\prime}
, in the spatial point
\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle r$}}_{o}
;
in this way, we can define the observation event

r_{o}^{\mu}=(t,\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle r$}}_{o})
.
At the same time

, the position of the particle 1 is
\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle r$}}_{1}=\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle r$}}_{1}(t)
,
that defines the event

We can introduce the

4-vector distance:

r^{\mu}=r_{o}^{\mu}-r_{1}^{\mu}=(0,\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle r$}})

(15)

where
\textstyle r
represents the

between

.
Note that, in the classical theory,
\textstyle r
depends on the time
t
through
\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle r$}}_{1}

For the following developments, recall that, at the time

\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle v$}}_{1}

Given that the speed of the light (

The emission time and position are usually called

Considering the time difference

We make the

from now on
:

r^{\mu}=r_{2}^{\mu}-r_{1}^{\mu}=(0,\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle r$}})=(0,\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle r$}}_{2}-\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle r$}}_{1})~.

(31)

Furthermore, the particle 2 moves with
the constant velocity
\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle v$}}_{2}
, being
u_{2}^{\mu}

the corresponding 4-velocity.
In this way, the invariant interaction at the time
t
takes the standard form

W_{12}=q_{2}u_{2}^{\mu}A_{\mu}(t,\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle r$}})

(32)

where
A_{\mu}(t,\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle r$}})
is given by Eq. (
28
).

We develop the last expression in the following way.
First, in order to construct the quantum model in Sec.
3
,
we can drop the dependence on the (generic) time
t
,
because in the Schrödinger representation, the operators do not depend on time.
Furthermore, we specialyze the interaction to a
q

\bar{q}
system
.
In this case,
the product of the two strong charges gives

q_{1}q_{2}=-{\frac{4}{3}}\alpha_{s}~.

(33)

where
-4/3
represents the quark color factor and

\alpha_{s}
is the strong effective coupling constant.
We obtain:

W_{12}=-{\frac{4}{3}}\alpha_{s}u_{1}^{\mu}u_{2}^{\nu}g_{\mu\nu}\cdot{\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle r$}}^{2}+{\left({\frac{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle r$}}\cdot\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle p$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle p$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle p$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle p$}}_{1}}{m}}\right)}^{2}}}}

(34)

Now we have to consider the contribution
W_{21}
that is obtained from Eq. (
34
)
by interchanging the two particles.
Then, we sum the two contributions multiplying by a factor
1/2
to avoid double counting:

W_{ret}={\frac{1}{2}}(W_{12}+W_{21})

(35)

that gives the general expression

W_{ret}(\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle r$}},\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle p$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle p$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle p$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle p$}}_{1},\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle p$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle p$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle p$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle p$}}_{2})=-{\frac{4}{3}}\alpha_{s}u_{1}^{\mu}u_{2}^{\nu}g_{\mu\nu}\cdot{\frac{1}{2}}\left({\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle r$}}^{2}+{\left({\frac{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle r$}}\cdot\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle p$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle p$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle p$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle p$}}_{1}}{m}}\right)}^{2}}}}+{\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle r$}}^{2}+{\left({\frac{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle r$}}\cdot\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle p$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle p$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle p$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle p$}}_{2}}{m}}\right)}^{2}}}}\right)~.

(36)

Furthermore,
for practical calculations of bound state properties,
as anticipated in Subsect.
1.1
,
we use the CM reference frame, where we can introduce the relative momentum
\textstyle p

of Eq. (
5
).
In this frame the interaction can be written
in the following form:

W_{ret}(\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle r$}},\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle p$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle p$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle p$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle p$}})=-{\frac{4}{3}}\alpha_{s}u_{1}^{\mu}u_{2}^{\nu}g_{\mu\nu}\cdot V_{ret}(\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle r$}},\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle p$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle p$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle p$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle p$}})

(37)

where we have introduced the
spatial function

V_{ret}(\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle r$}},\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle p$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle p$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle p$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle p$}})={\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle r$}}^{2}+\left({\frac{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle r$}}\cdot\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle p$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle p$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle p$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle p$}}}{m}}\right)^{2}}}}~.

(38)

In the last equation, the denominator of
V_{ret}(\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle r$}},\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle p$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle p$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle p$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle p$}})
represents the
invariant retarded distance
of the two particles.
In the
classical theory
, the 4-velocity product of Eq. (
37
) gives :

u_{1}^{\mu}u_{2}^{\nu}g_{\mu\nu}=1+{\frac{2\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle p$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle p$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle p$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle p$}}^{2}}{m^{2}}}~

(39)

but, in the quantum model discussed in the next section, the 4-velocities will be
replaced by the Dirac matrices of the two particles.

3
The quantum operator for bound state calculations

In this section, starting from the retarded interaction of Eqs. (
37
)
and (
38
), we construct a quantum operator to be used in a reduced two-body Dirac equation.
To this aim, we replace the 4-velocities
u_{i}^{\mu}
of Eq. (
37
)
with the Dirac matrices of the two particles.
For clarity, we write here the expression that will be elaborated and studied in the
remainder of the work:

W_{ret}(\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle r$}},\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle p$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle p$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle p$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle p$}})=-{\frac{4}{3}}\alpha_{s}\gamma_{1}^{\mu}\gamma_{2}^{\nu}g_{\mu\nu}\cdot V_{ret}(\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle r$}},\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle p$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle p$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle p$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle p$}})~,

(40)

with the spatial factor given by Eq. (
38
).

Note that in the static case (
\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle p$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle p$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle p$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle p$}}=0
) the retardation
effects disappear.
In the general case,
the retardation effects
reduce
(in absolute value) the strength of the interaction.

The expression of
W_{ret}(\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle r$}},\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle p$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle p$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle p$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle p$}})
of the last equation
is apparently simple
but cannot be directly “interpreted” as a quantum
operator due to the non-Hermitian character of
V_{ret}(\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle r$}},\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle p$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle p$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle p$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle p$}})
.
We have the task to introduce, from that expression
(obtained by means of a classical procedure), a corresponding Hermitian quantum operator.
This operator will have, in any case, a nonlocal form; it means that the retardation effects
involve the wave function of the system over all the space.

The procedure to obtain an Hermitian operator is
not unique
.
In any case, it consists in
choosing an order
of the operators
\textstyle p
and
\textstyle r

so that an Hermitian operator is finally obtained.
We shall describe in detail the following procedure because it allows to calculate
some test
matrix elements in a simple way, without introducing singular terms.
Another procedure is also incidentally outlined, without performing specific calculations.

First, we consider the following non-Hermitian radial momentum operator
[
17
]
:

p_{r}=-{\frac{i}{r}}\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle r$}}\cdot\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle p$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle p$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle p$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle p$}}~.

(41)

For a wave function
\psi(r,\theta,\varphi)=<r,\theta,\varphi|\psi>

in spherical coordinates, we have

\begin{split}<r,\theta,\varphi|p_{r}|\psi>&=-i{\frac{\partial}{\partial r}}\psi(r,\theta,\varphi)~,\\
<\psi|p_{r}^{\dagger}|r,\theta,\varphi>&=+i{\frac{\partial}{\partial r}}\psi^{*}(r,\theta,\varphi)~.\end{split}

(42)

Furthermore, we note that the operator
p_{r}^{\dagger}p_{r}

is
Hermitian.
For further developments, this operator
can be written, with standard procedures, in the form

p_{r}^{\dagger}p_{r}=\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle p$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle p$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle p$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle p$}}^{2}-{\frac{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle L$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle L$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle L$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle L$}}^{2}}{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle r$}}^{2}}}~.

(43)

For
\textstyle r
and
\textstyle p
of Eq. (
38
)
we shall use the following operator ordering:

\left({\frac{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle r$}}\cdot\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle p$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle p$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle p$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle p$}}}{r}}\right)^{2}\rightarrow\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle p$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle p$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle p$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle p$}}\cdot\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle r$}}{\frac{1}{r^{2}}}\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle r$}}\cdot\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle p$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle p$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle p$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle p$}}=p_{r}^{\dagger}p_{r}~.

(44)

In this way
we can rewrite Eq. (
40
) in the form:

W_{ret}(\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle r$}},\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle p$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle p$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle p$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle p$}})=-{\frac{4}{3}}\alpha_{s}\gamma_{1}^{\mu}\gamma_{2}^{\nu}g_{\mu\nu}\cdot{\frac{1}{r\sqrt{1+{\frac{p_{r}^{\dagger}p_{r}}{m^{2}}}}}}~.

(45)

The previous expression is not yet Hermitian but we can perform a series expansion
in powers of
p_{r}^{\dagger}p_{r}/m^{2}
and write each term of the series in Hermitian form.
To this aim we make use of the following operator reordering:

{\frac{1}{r}}\left(p_{r}^{\dagger}p_{r}\right)^{k}\rightarrow(p_{r}^{\dagger})^{k}{\frac{1}{r}}(p_{r})^{k}~.

(46)

In consequence, the interaction can be expanded in the following way

W_{ret}(\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle r$}},\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle p$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle p$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle p$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle p$}})=-{\frac{4}{3}}\alpha_{s}\gamma_{1}^{\mu}\gamma_{2}^{\nu}g_{\mu\nu}\cdot\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}c_{k}\left({\frac{p_{r}^{\dagger}}{m}}\right)^{k}{\frac{1}{r}}\left({\frac{p_{r}}{m}}\right)^{k}

(47)

where the
c_{k}
are the coefficients of the Taylor expansion:

{\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+x}}}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}c_{k}x^{k}~,

(48)

or, more explicitly

c_{k}=(-1)^{k}{\frac{(2k-1)!!}{(2k)!!}}~.

(49)

We can now study some developments of the previous analysis.
First, we recall that, instead of the pure Coulombic potential,
it is possible to use a regularized function

U(r)
that is introduced in a model in which the quarks are considered as
extended sources of the interaction field.
In general, we can perform the following formal replacement in Eq. (
47
):

{\frac{1}{r}}\rightarrow U(r)

(50)

where
U(r)
indicates a
generic
(pure Coulombic or regularized) spatial potential function.
In particular the author successfully used the following regularized function
[
3
]
:

U(r)={\frac{1}{r}}\text{erf}\left({\frac{r}{2d}}\right)

(51)

that is obtained considering a Gaussian color charge distribution of the source particles.
Some test calculations performed with the pure Coulombic interaction and with the regularized one will be discussed below.

Incidentally.
we also show here a different operator ordering that can be used to obtain an Hermitian interaction operator

W_{ret}(\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle p$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle p$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle p$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle p$}},\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle r$}})
.
Starting directly from Eq. (
45
), we define:

W_{ret}(\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle r$}},\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle p$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle p$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle p$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle p$}})=-{\frac{4}{3}}\alpha_{s}\gamma_{1}^{\mu}\gamma_{2}^{\nu}g_{\mu\nu}\cdot\left[1+{\frac{p_{r}^{\dagger}p_{r}}{m^{2}}}\right]^{-1/4}U(r)\left[1+{\frac{p_{r}^{\dagger}p_{r}}{m^{2}}}\right]^{-1/4}

(52)

where the operator
p_{r}^{\dagger}p_{r}
has been given explicitly in Eq. (
43
).
Also in Eq. (
52
) it is possible to take for
U(r)

a generic potential function.
We note that Eq. (
52
) seems more compact than Eq. (
47
).
However, for practical calculations, one has to perform, also in this case,
a series expansion in powers of
p_{r}^{\dagger}p_{r}/m^{2}
.
The calculation of the corresponding matrix elements is not
easy to handle and, for this reason, we do not use the interaction of Eq. (
52
) in this work.

We now discuss a test calculation of some matrix elements of the spatial operator
that appears in Eq. (
47
), with the generalization
of Eqs. (
50
) and (
51
).
To this aim, we disregard the Dirac matrices and the color coupling, defining
the spatial operator
S
as:

S=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}c_{k}\left({\frac{p_{r}^{\dagger}}{m}}\right)^{k}U(r)\left({\frac{p_{r}}{m}}\right)^{k}~.

(53)

We study the matrix elemants

<n_{b},l|S|n_{a},l>

where
n_{b},~n_{a}
represent the energy quantum number of the states and
l
is
the orbital angular momentum quantum number.
The matrix elements do not depend on the third component of the angular momentum.
For this reason, the corresponding quantum number has been dropped.
For bound states with real radial functions
R_{n,l}(r)
, using Eq. (
42
),
the matrix elements
of
S
can be written in the form:

<n_{b},l|S|n_{a},l>=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}c_{k}{\frac{1}{m^{2k}}}\int_{0}^{\infty}r^{2}drR_{n_{b},l}^{(k)}(r)U(r)R_{n_{a},l}^{(k)}(r)

(54)

where
R_{n,l}^{(k)}(r)
represents the
k
-th derivative of the radial function.
For our test calculation we use HO wave fuctions in coordinate space
whose expression is
given in Eqs. (
74
) and (
75
).
By using the adimensional variable
s
and the HO
scale
\bar{p}
,
the matrix elements take the form

<n_{b},l|S|n_{a},l>=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}c_{k}\left({\frac{\bar{p}}{m}}\right)^{2k}\int_{0}^{\infty}s^{2}ds\hat{R}_{n_{b},l}^{(k)}(s)U\left({\frac{s}{\bar{p}}}\right)\hat{R}_{n_{a},l}^{(k)}(s)~.

(55)

The integrals of the previous expression can be calculated analytically for the
pure Coulombic case. For the regularized
U(r)
of Eq. (
51
) the integrals are
performed numerically.
The first term of the series (that is the term with
k=0
) gives the unretarded interaction.
By considering the calculations of the work
[
3
]
, the value of
\bar{p}
has been fixed,
indicatively, at the value
\bar{p}=
0.5 GeV;
the value of
d
in the regularized interaction of Eq. (
51
),
at the value
d=0.15
fm and the quark mass at
m=1.27
GeV.
Some results are shown in Table
1
.
The sum over
k
has been performed up to
k=8
with an accuracy of the order of
6\%
.
The difference betweeen the
unretarded
and
retarded
calculation is
not big but should be taken into account in more refined models for the charmonium
and bottomonium spectra.

4
A comparison with Feynman model

In this section we discuss a point of conceptual interest, that is the comparison
of our model of interaction (developed in the coordinate space)
with the results of the Feynman diagram (at tree-level) that are given in the momentum space.
Due to the complexity of the problem, we shall only study the pure Coulombic case.
We shall consider the interaction of Eq. (
40
) and (
38
)
without chosing, at the beginning, a specific
operator ordering.

To develop the calculation it is necessary to recall some standard procedures.
In the
unretarded

(or
static
) case, that is obtained
by setting
\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle p$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle p$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle p$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle p$}}=0
in Eq. (
38
),
we have a pure Coulombic interaction, in the form

W_{unr}(\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle r$}})=-{\frac{4}{3}}\alpha_{s}\gamma_{1}^{\mu}\gamma_{2}^{\nu}g_{\mu\nu}\cdot{\frac{1}{r}}

(56)

In the momentum space, we have

\hat{W}_{unr}(\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle q$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle q$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle q$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle q$}})=<\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle p$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle p$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle p$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle p$}}_{b}|W_{unr}(\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle r$}})|\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle p$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle p$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle p$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle p$}}_{a}>

(57)

where
\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle q$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle q$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle q$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle q$}}=\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle p$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle p$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle p$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle p$}}_{a}-\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle p$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle p$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle p$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle p$}}_{b}
is the interaction 3-momentum transfer.
Specifically,
\hat{W}_{unr}(\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle q$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle q$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle q$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle q$}})
is obtained by means of a standard Fourier Transform:

\begin{split}\hat{W}_{unr}(\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle q$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle q$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle q$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle q$}})&={\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{3}}}\int d^{3}r\exp(i\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle q$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle q$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle q$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle q$}}\cdot\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle r$}})W_{unr}(\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle r$}})\\
&=-{\frac{4}{3}}\alpha_{s}\gamma_{1}^{\mu}\gamma_{2}^{\nu}g_{\mu\nu}\cdot{\frac{1}{2\pi^{2}}}\cdot{\frac{1}{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle q$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle q$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle q$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle q$}}^{2}}}\end{split}

(58)

We recall that, in
\hat{W}_{unr}(\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle q$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle q$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle q$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle q$}}),
the dependence on
\textstyle q

only
,
corresponds to the local, unretarded character of
W_{unr}(\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle r$}})
.

On the other hand, from the tree-level Feynman diagram
[
14
]
, one obtains
for the interaction amplitude in the momentum space, the following expression:

\hat{W}_{Fey}(\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle p$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle p$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle p$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle p$}}_{b},\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle p$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle p$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle p$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle p$}}_{a})=-{\frac{4}{3}}\alpha_{s}\gamma_{1}^{\mu}\gamma_{2}^{\nu}g_{\mu\nu}\cdot{\frac{1}{2\pi^{2}}}\cdot{\frac{1}{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle q$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle q$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle q$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle q$}}^{2}-(\Delta\epsilon)^{2}}}

(59)

We note that the last expression, by means of the dependence on
q^{\mu}q_{\mu}

(see Eqs. (
8
), (
9
) and (
10
)),
is
standardly
covariant.
In particular, the presence of the on-shell energy transfer
\Delta\epsilon
,
that does not appear in Eq. (
58
),
represents the
non-local and retarded character of the interaction.
In this respect, analogously to Eq. (
57
), it is possible to write

\hat{W}_{Fey}(\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle p$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle p$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle p$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle p$}}_{b},\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle p$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle p$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle p$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle p$}}_{a})=<\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle p$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle p$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle p$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle p$}}_{b}|W_{Fey}|\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle p$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle p$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle p$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle p$}}_{a}>

(60)

but the form of the nonlocal operator
W_{Fey}
cannot be directly determined.
The matrix elements of
W_{Fey}
with HO wave functions can be calculated as
shown in Eq. (
77
).

In order to make a comparison with our retardation model, we write
the on-shell energy difference in the following way

\Delta\epsilon=-~{\frac{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle q$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle q$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle q$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle q$}}\cdot(\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle p$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle p$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle p$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle p$}}_{a}+\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle p$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle p$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle p$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle p$}}_{b})}{\epsilon(\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle p$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle p$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle p$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle p$}}_{b})+\epsilon(\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle p$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle p$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle p$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle p$}}_{a})}}~.

(61)

In consequence, the Feynman interaction amplitude of Eq. (
59
)
can be rewritten in the equivalent form

First,
in our model we have the Jacobian factor

This factor can be related to the covariant normalization of the states
that should be used when calculating matrix elements with momentum space wave functions.
In any case, this discrepancy should not be related to relevant physical properties.

Second,

\begin{split}\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle p$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle p$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle p$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle p$}}&\rightarrow{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle p$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle p$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle p$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle p$}}_{b}+\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle p$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle p$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle p$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle p$}}_{a}\right)~,\\

Unfortunately, it is not easy to find an operator ordering in the coordinate space that gives
the result that is obtained by means of the replacements of Eq. (

We can tentatively conclude that our model of retardation, based on the LW construction,
has the same physical content as the Feynman theory.
The main difficulty of the problem consists in
translating
the classical expression into a quantum one, choosing a suitable operator ordering.
In the Feynman diagram one has two asymptotically
free
particles, that corresponds,
in our LW approach, to take particles with constant momenta.

Starting from these considerations, we can say that
further investigation efforts should be oriented to calculate the matrix elements
of the Feynman theory directly in the momentum space by using
bound state wave functions, as indicated in Eq. (
77
).
The mathematical properties and the physical
contents of the theory should be studied, analyzing the influence
of the retardation efects on the hadronic observables.
