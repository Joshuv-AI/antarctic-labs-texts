Single-cell data analysis of lung cancer brain metastasis reveals SEC61G serves as a potential risk indicator
Authors: Yunjing Li, Yuanyuan Zhang, Wenxin Tian, Zhixun Zhao, Lei He
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLung adenocarcinoma is a highly malignant and life-threatening disease, with brain metastases representing a major cause of mortality in patients with lung cancer. Although extensive research has focused on interpreting single-cell data from lung cancer brain metastases, most studies emphasize the immune microenvironment, leaving the molecular mechanisms and pathways involving epithelial cells under explored. In this study, we investigated epithelial cells using single-cell data derived from lung cancer brain metastases (comprising six lung cancer and six lung-to-brain metastasis samples) and identified a significant enrichment of the protein-folding pathway in brain metastases, with SEC61G emerging as a particularly prominent contributor. Subsequent in silico replication in clinical samples and public cohort confirmed that SEC61G can serve as a biomarker for brain metastases in lung cancer. These results provide novel insights into the molecular mechanisms driving brain metastases in lung cancer and underscore the potential of SEC61G as both a therapeutic target and a diagnostic marker. Our findings advance understanding of the metastatic process in lung adenocarcinoma and provide a foundation for developing targeted therapies aimed at improving patient outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-026-05341-1.Keywords: Lung cancer, Brain metastasis, scRNA-seq, SEC61G, Risk indicator

Introduction

Lung cancer is one of the most common malignancies worldwide and remains the leading cause of cancer-related mortality. Among the histological subtypes of non-small cell lung cancer, lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) is the most frequently diagnosed, followed by squamous cell carcinoma [1]. Despite significant advancements in alternative therapies, including targeted treatments and immunotherapies for patients with LUAD over the past few decades, the average 5-year survival rate remains alarmingly low, at < 20% [2].

The brain is one of the most common sites of distant metastasis in patients with lung cancer [3]. Alarmingly, nearly 50% of patients develop brain metastases as the disease progresses. The prognosis for these patients is poor, with a median survival time of only 4–5 months [4]. Currently, the main treatment approach involves a combination of surgery and radiotherapy; however, outcomes remain suboptimal [5]. Therefore, a deeper understanding of the mechanisms driving brain metastases in lung cancer is crucial for improving both survival rates and quality of life.

Given that brain metastasis is a major complication of lung cancer, identifying the distinct characteristics of lung cancer brain metastases is essential for improving treatment strategies. Tumor heterogeneity within a single tumor refers to the presence of diverse subpopulations of cancer cells that exhibit significant differences in genomic features (e.g., somatic mutations and epigenetic modifications) and tumor biology (e.g., transcriptomics, proteomics, and metabolomics) [6]. Metastatic tumor cells typically consist of both clones from the primary tumor and more differentiated, invasive clones with secondary mutations. These cells possess self-renewal capacity, can develop treatment resistance, and may lead to cancer recurrence. They can also be characterized as distinct subpopulations [7]. Over successive passages, these cells interact with the “tumor ecosystem”—the surrounding tumor microenvironment (TME)—during their evolution, ultimately promoting metastasis and enabling immune evasion [8].

Conventional sequencing methods, such as bulk sequencing, capture average signals across a population of cells, making it difficult to specifically characterize tumor cell heterogeneity. In contrast, single-cell sequencing offers a powerful solution by enabling the analysis of individual cells, thus facilitating more precise exploration of tumor biology and the development of personalized treatment strategies. This technique has proven invaluable across various contexts, including developmental studies, the creation of human cell atlases, and cancer research [9, 10]. Single-cell sequencing enables molecular profiling of distinct subpopulation, allowing for the identification of biomarker differences between metastatic and primary tumors. Comparative single-cell sequencing analyses of primary lung tumors and brain metastatic lung tumors can help identify key genomic differences within analogous cell subtypes. These differences may reveal biomarkers that can specifically identify lung cancer cells with the potential to metastasize to the brain. This approach could significantly enhance our understanding of the molecular drivers of brain metastasis and improve the ability to predict and target metastasizing lung cancer cells.

In this study, we analyzed the publicly available dataset (GSE131907) [11], which encompasses single-cell sequencing data from six primary lung cancer samples and six lung-to-brain metastasis samples. By comparing gene expression profiles between these two groups, we identified the SEC61G gene as potentially associated with brain metastasis in patients with lung cancer. This finding offers a new avenue for exploring the molecular mechanisms underlying brain metastasis and highlights the potential role of SEC61G in this process.

Materials and methods

Single-cell RNA dataset and analysis

The single-cell RNA sequencing dataset was download from GEO database ( https://ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi?acc=GSE131907). The sample was devided into exploration and in silico replication groups, detailed group was supplied as a supplementary Table 1. Another single-cell RNA sequencing dataset GSE223499 was also downloaded (https://ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi?acc=GSE223499) for in silico replication the potential genes, the sample were selected without KRAS or STK11 mutation, detailed information including primary and brain metastasis were supplied as a supplementary Table 2.

Quality control was performed on the original gene-cell-barcode matrix using the following criteria: mitochondrial gene percentage < 0.15, unique molecular identifiers between 1000 and 25,000, and gene counts > 500. After filtering, the expression matrix of each selected sample was normalized using the NormalizeData function from the Seurat package. FindVariableFeatures function in the Seurat package was employed to identify genes with significant cell-to-cell variation. Subsequently, the RunPCA function was used to compute the principal components (PCs). To select a subset of significant PCs, the JackStraw and PCElbowPlot functions of Seurat were utilized. For cell clustering analysis and data visualization, the FindClusters function was used, and the RunTSNE function was employed to generate a t-SNE visualization of the cells. The identity of each cluster was annotated based on the expression of known marker genes.

Batch effects were corrected using harmony (https://github.com/immunogenomics/harmony) with sample as the grouping variable. PCA was performed on scaled data, and the top 50 principal components were used for RunHarmony integration. UMAP was run with reduction = “harmony” and dims = 1:50, followed by neighbor graph construction (FindNeighbors, same Harmony reduction and dims) and clustering (FindClusters) at a resolution of 0.3.

Copy number variation (CNV) assessment of cells was conducted using the inferCNV package (inferCNV of the Trinity CTAT Project; https://github.com/broadinstitute/infercnv). CNVs in epithelial cells were calculated using the average gene expression of immune cells (B cells) as a reference. Default parameters were applied (cutoff = 0.1). The CNV score was calculated as the mean of CNV values across regions. If the CNV score > 0.1, the epithelial cells was regarded as malignant cells, the cells with CNV score < 0.1, the epithelial cells was regarded as normal cells.

Differential expression was performed by FindMarker function in Seurat, genes with |logfoldchange|>1 and adjusted p value < 0.05 was regarded as significant changed genes.

Hallmark unfolded protein response (UPR) genes were defined as the core UPR genes (XBP1, ATF4, CHOP, ATF6, PERK, IRE1). The UPR pathway activity score for each single cell was quantified using AUCell (https://github.com/aertslab/AUCell) based on the expression of these core UPR genes. The median AUCell score of all cells within each group was used to represent the UPR pathway activity between groups.

Human tissue samples from primary lung tumor and brain metastatic tumor

The tissues of primary lung tumor were collected from lung cancer patients who admitted to our hospital for cough, bloody phlegm, chest discomfort or pain and received the surgical resection of tumors. The tissues of brain metastasis tumor were collected from the recurrent lung cancer patients who admitted to our hospital for headache, limb movement disorders, epileptic seizures, and require surgery to alleviate symptoms.

Quantitative PCR (qPCR)

SEC61G expression levels were evaluated in both murine and human samples using qPCR. Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (15596026CN, Invitrogen, Massachusetts, USA), and cDNA was synthesized using the PrimeScript RT reagent Kit (RR037A, Takara, Japan). qPCR was carried out using SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (4309155, Invitrogen, Massachusetts, USA) on a QuantStudio 5 Real-Time PCR system. Expression levels of SEC61G were normalized to the housekeeping gene GAPDH. PCR primer sequences used were as follows: SEC61G forward, 5′-AAAGGACTCCATTCGGCTGGTT-3′; SEC61G reverse, 5′-CAAAGAAGCCAATGAATCCC-3′; GAPDH forward, 5′-GAGAAGGCTGGGGCTCATTT-3′; GAPDH reverse, 5′-AGTGATGGCATGGACTGTGG-3′.18 Relative expression levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method.

Public dataset analysis and in silico replication

To further validate the difference between primary lung adenocarcinoma and brain metastasis bulk RNA sequencing data. 6 brain metastasis RNA expression data (TPM) were downloaded from GEO database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi?acc=GSE141685). Another lung adenocarcinoma bulk RNA sequencing data were downloaded from Xena data center of UCSC (https://xenabrowser.net/datapages/?cohort=TCGA%20Lung%20Adenocarcinoma%20(LUAD)&removeHub=https%3 A%2 F%2Fxena.treehouse.gi.ucsc.edu%3A443), after filter with clinical feature (clinical label: M0), 342 primary lung bulk data were obtained (supplementary Table 3). Difference between primary lung adenocarcinoma and brain metastasis data was performed with Mann-Whitney U test.

Finally, the prognostic value of SEC61G in lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) was evaluated using the Gene Expression Profiling Interactive Analysis (GEPIA, http://gepia.cancer-pku.cn/) online tool. All 239 LUAD patients from the TCGA-LUAD cohort (http://gepia.cancer-pku.cn/detail.php?gene=SEC61G) were stratified into high and low SEC61G expression groups based on the median expression level of SEC61G. Kaplan-Meier survival curves were plotted for over-all survival (OS), and the log-rank test was used to compare survival differences between groups.

Data analysis

PCR data analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism (version 9). Statistical comparisons between two groups were conducted using the Student’s t-test. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All experiments were repeated at least three times, and data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD).

Results

Landscape of cell types in lung and brain

Based on single-cell sequencing data from primary lung cancer and brain metastasis samples, our analysis revealed clear differences in cellular composition. The overall study design and the number of cells analyzed per sample are illustrated in Fig. 1A and B. Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) revealed multiple cell populations, including B lymphocytes, endothelial cells, epithelial cells, fibroblasts, mast cells, myeloid cells, natural killer (NK) cells, oligodendrocytes, T lymphocytes, and unidentified cells (Fig. 1C). Each major cell type exhibited classic markers (Fig. 1D).

Notably, brain metastasis samples showed a higher proportion of epithelial cells, mast cells, and oligodendrocytes, indicative of an increased presence of tumor cells compared with that in primary lung cancer samples (Fig. 1E). In contrast, primary lung cancer samples were enriched with immune cells, including B, NK, myeloid, and T cells, as well as endothelial cells, suggesting more pronounced immune infiltration within the TME.

Identification of tumor cells in lung and brain

Building on the enrichment of epithelial cells in brain metastases, we utilized the inferCNV algorithm to analyze CNVs in epithelial cells, using the CNV of B pymphocytes cells as a control. By applying CNV scores, we were able to distinguish malignant cells within the epithelial population (Fig. 2A), using B cell CNVs as the reference. The results revealed a higher proportion of malignant cells in brain metastatic tumors (Fig. 2B).

UMAP analysis was performed to differentiate malignant epithelial cells in primary and metastatic brain tumors (Fig. 2C–E). Cluster 2 included malignant cells from both brain metastases and primary lung tumors. This cluster exhibited a similar gene expression pattern, likely minimizing gene expression variability unrelated to metastasis across different samples. Further cell trajectory analysis within Cluster 2 revealed that differentiation pathways differed between the groups. Specifically, lung cancer cells appeared to be at a relatively earlier stage in their developmental trajectory (Fig. 2F, G).

Differences in tumor cells between lung and brain

Subsequently, differential gene expression between brain metastasis samples and primary lung tumor samples within Cluster 2 was further investigated. A large number of differentially expressed genes were obtained (Fig. 3A), those with a log fold change > 2 were then subjected to gene set variation analysis (Fig. 3B). Notably, the Unfolded Protein Response (UPR) pathway was significantly upregulated in brain metastasis samples than in primary lung tumors (Fig. 3B).

To further investigate the UPR signaling pathway, the expression of UPR-related genes was compared between primary lung tumor and brain metastasis samples (Fig. 3C). Further more, the core UPR genes (XBP1, ATF4, CHOP, ATF6, PERK, IRE1) was compared to confirm the activation of UPR pathway. The score of core UPR gene in metastasis brain was significant higher than primary lung group (Fig. 3D). Among them, SEC61G was the most noteworthy, exhibiting the greatest change in expression levels and the lowest p-value (Fig. 3E), suggesting its potential as a biomarker for brain metastasis.

Exploration of SEC61G in clinical samples and public database

Further, in the in silico replication samples from GSE131907, high expression of SEC61G in brain was also observed (Fig. 4A). Moreover, similar result was observed in epithelial cells from another single cell dataset (Fig. 4B, data from GSE223499) and bulk RNA data (Fig. 4C, data from GSE141685 and TCGA). Besides, we also performed the qPCR and analyzed the relative expression of SEC61G in three primary lung samples and three brain metastasis samples from our internal cohort. We set GAPDH as internal control, and found expression of SEC61G in the brain metastasis samples was found significantly higher than those in primary lung samples (Fig. 4D). Meanwhile, the IHC result also showed the higher expression of SEC61G in metastasis samples. from our internal cohort (Fig. 4E). To further investigate the prognostic significance of SEC61G in LUAD, we performed survival analysis using the GEPIA platform and found lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) patients with high SEC61G expression exhibited significantly shorter disease-free survival (DFS) than those with low SEC61G expression (log-rank P = 0.015, Fig. 4F). These findings suggested that high SEC61G expression may serve as a potential risk factor for shortened DFS in LUAD patients.

Discussion

In this study, we utilized a publicly available single-cell sequencing dataset [11], comprising six primary lung tumors and six lung-to-brain metastatic tumors, to identify potential biomarkers associated with brain metastasis. Our findings revealed a higher proportion of epithelial cells, oligodendrocytes, and malignant cells in brain metastasis samples, whereas primary tumors exhibited a greater abundance of endothelial and immune cells. These observations align with the expected characteristics of primary and metastatic lung tumors, supporting the dataset’s accurate representation of these tumor types. Through UMAP analysis, we identified a cell cluster common to both primary lung and brain metastatic tumor samples. By focusing on this cluster for differential analysis, we were able to minimize the impact of sample heterogeneity.

Our differential gene analysis of this cell cluster revealed significant upregulation of several pathways in brain metastatic tumors compared with that in primary lung tumors, including the UPR, PI3K-AKT-mTOR signaling, Interferon Alpha Response, Heme Metabolism, Xenobiotic Metabolism, WNT Beta-Catenin Signaling, and KRAS Signaling. Conversely, pathways such as the P53 Pathway, MYC Targets V1, Oxidative Phosphorylation, TGF-Beta Signaling, MYC Targets V2, Apoptosis, Apical Surface Glycolysis, and Notch Signaling were notably downregulated in brain metastases. These findings highlight the molecular changes associated with brain metastasis in lung cancer and suggest potential diagnostic targets.

Notably, we observed a marked down-regulation of the CNTNA2 and PDCD1LG2 genes in brain metastatic cells, which are primarily involved in the Notch signaling pathway and cell adhesion. This down-regulation may facilitate easier cell detachment, a characteristic feature of brain metastatic tumors. Additionally, we identified a significant up-regulation of the SEC61G gene, which is associated with the UPR pathway. The UPR is a cellular response to endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress, and the Sect. 61 complex plays a key role in protein translocation within the ER. Activation of the UPR, often observed in various tumor types, may contribute to tumor survival, proliferation, and metastasis, suggesting that SEC61G could be an important factor in brain metastasis.

The SEC61G gene, also known as the Sect. 61 translocon γ subunit, is an integral component of the Sect. 61 complex. This complex is a heterotrimeric channel protein, consisting of the Sect. 61 α, β, and γ subunits [12]. Notably, SEC61G expression has been found to be upregulated in many cancers [13, 14]. Recent studies have highlighted the overexpression of SEC61G as a poor prognostic factor, by promoting tumor metastasis in lung cancer [15] and in other malignancies [16]. In cervical cancer, silencing SEC61G gene expression inhibits proliferation and invasion of cervical cells [15]. SEC61G may promote tumor cell metastasis through its association with the MAPK signaling pathway [15], the E2F1 gene [17], or mechanisms of immune evasion [18]. In our study, we identified SEC61G as a brain metastatic factor. Both the dysregulation in our data and public cohort and survival analysis suggest that high SEC61G expression may serve as a poor prognosis risk factor in LUAD patients.

Limitation

Firstly, the data is from publicly dataset, the experimental set only included 6 specimens, which may be influenced by individual factors. Although further in silico replication sets confirmed SEC61G, the overall sample size is still relatively limited. Secondly, the compositional shifts between primary lung and BM (including endothelial/immune representation) can be heavily influenced by sampling site, dissociation protocols which will influenced the percentage result. Thirdly, the genetic differences in the UPR pathway are statistically analyzed at the cellular level, cell-level testing without accounting for patient-of-origin risks inflated significance. Finally, the samples of qPCR and IHC still need to be further expanded to demonstrate the reliability of SEC61G.

Summary

These results suggest that the SEC61G gene could serve as a potential risk indicator for brain metastasis in lung cancer. Lung cancer brain metastasis is a major cause of mortality, and patients with this condition have a poorer prognosis. Early identification of a tendency towards brain metastasis in lung cancer could be significant for improving clinical treatment.
