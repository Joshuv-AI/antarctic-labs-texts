Bilateral Hutch diverticula presenting as “Mickey Mouse bladder” in a child: A case report
Authors: Imen Kalfat, Cyrine Saadi, Souha Laarif, Hela Oueslati, Abir Boussetta, Taher Gargeh, Youssef Hellal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Congenital bladder diverticula are rare in children. The Mickey Mouse bladder refers to a specific radiological sign that appears in bilateral paraureteral diverticula. We describe an 11-year-old boy presented with lower urinary symptoms. Imaging studies showed bilateral Hutch diverticula without vesicoureteral reflux. Urodynamic studies showed detrusor overactivity with bladder-sphincter dyssynergia. The patient was managed conservatively with clean intermittent catheterization and anticholinergic medication, and he had a significant clinical response without surgery. This case emphasizes the role of functional studies and supports conservative management in cases where bladder dysfunction is the major underlying problem.

Introduction

Bladder diverticula are defined as hernias of the bladder mucosa through a defect in the detrusor muscle. Congenital paraureteral diverticula, also known as Hutch diverticula, are a rare congenital anomaly in children and are predominantly found in males.1 They are often incidentally discovered during the evaluation of urinary tract infections, vesicoureteral reflux (VUR), or voiding dysfunction.2

Bilateral Hutch diverticula may give rise to a characteristic radiologic finding on voiding cystourethrography (VCUG), known as the “Mickey Mouse bladder.”

We describe a case of bilateral Hutch diverticula, which presented with lower urinary tract symptoms, and was successfully managed conservatively after the identification of associated bladder dysfunction.

Case presentation

An 11-year-old boy with no antenatal urinary tract abnormalities was referred for lower urinary tract symptoms, which were characterized by straining and double voiding for the past two years. He had a history of a single febrile urinary tract infection and was otherwise continent.

Physical examination was unremarkable, no sacral dimple was observed. Neurological evaluation was normal. Serum creatinine was normal.

Given the absence of VUR or recurrent infections and the presence of significant functional bladder disorder, conservative management was undertaken with clean intermittent catheterization, anticholinergic therapy and baclofen for dyssynergia.

At follow-up, the patient showed marked improvement in symptoms and reduction in post-void residual urine.

Discussion

Congenital bladder diverticula occur due to weakness of the detrusor muscle in a specific area and are predominantly found close to the ureteral hiatus. They can be congenital, acquired, or iatrogenic in nature. While they are often benign, large diverticula can cause urinary stasis, recurrent urinary tract infections, VUR, lithiasis, or voiding dysfunction.

Bilateral paraureteral diverticula are rare and can give the typical “Mickey Mouse bladder” appearance on VCUG, which is a specific diagnostic feature. Ultrasound has moderate sensitivity (about 60%), while CT and MRI scans have slightly higher sensitivity (about 70%). However, VCUG is the most sensitive test for the diagnosis of diverticula and the evaluation of associated VUR. Ultrasound findings include round or hypoechoic outpouchings from the bladder base or near the ureteral orifice.3

Endoscopic examination of the lower urinary tract can give additional information about the anatomy. Cystoscopy allows for accurate localization of the neck of the diverticulum, measurement of its width, evaluation of its relationship to adjacent ureteral orifices, and examination of the urethral and bladder mucosa. This information can be particularly useful when planning surgery.4

At present, there are no universally accepted surgical recommendations for primary pediatric bladder diverticula. However, surgical diverticulectomy is advised in symptomatic patients, particularly in the setting of recurrent infections, marked residual urine, obstruction, lithiasis, or VUR. Diverticula exceeding 3 cm in size are likely to be associated with incomplete bladder emptying and complications and may therefore be amenable to surgery.5

On the other hand, small, asymptomatic diverticula found incidentally can be treated conservatively. Careful observation with periodic clinical evaluation, urinalysis, and renal-bladder ultrasound is thought to be sufficient in the absence of VUR. Prophylactic antibiotics are not indicated in the absence of reflux. Moreover, in the absence of VUR, repeated VCUG is not generally recommended for follow-up to prevent unnecessary radiation exposure.

Urodynamic studies are, however, important in selected patients. They can demonstrate bladder outlet obstruction, detrusor underactivity, elevated post-void residual urine volume, and detrusor overactivity. Although it is unclear whether dysfunctional voiding is a cause or consequence of congenital diverticula, functional abnormalities can play a major role in determining management.

In the current case, urodynamic studies revealed the presence of detrusor overactivity with bladder-sphincter dyssynergia, indicating that the abnormal bladder dynamics played a role in the incomplete emptying. Tailored conservative management with clean intermittent catheterization and anticholinergic treatment led to significant symptomatic relief without the need for surgical intervention.

We acknowledge the limitation posed by the absence of pelvic floor physical therapy in current management protocols for voiding dysfunction, and we advocate for its inclusion in future protocols, given its potential benefits in the management of neurogenic bladder.

This case illustrates the need for a combined anatomical and functional approach. Even in the setting of dramatic anatomical findings such as the “Mickey Mouse bladder,” management should be individualized, and conservative management may be indicated when bladder dysfunction is the primary pathological process.

Conclusion

Bilateral Hutch diverticula with the “Mickey Mouse bladder” appearance is a rare finding in children. This case illustrates that assessment and management of underlying bladder dysfunction can result in clinical improvement and prevent the need for unnecessary surgery. Functional assessment should be a critical component of the management plan in children with congenital bladder diverticula.
