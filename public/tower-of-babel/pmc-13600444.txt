Jerome Cornfield: Some Memories
Authors: Susan S. Ellenberg
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Jerome Cornfield was a leading biostatistician of the mid-20th century, held in extremely high regard both by fellow statisticians and medical researchers. He was also beloved for his warmth, charm and wonderful sense of humor. I was fortunate to begin my career working with him; in this piece, I share some memorable experiences.

In 1971, as I was planning a break from teaching high school math to start a family, I was approached by my friend Janet Wittes, who had just completed her doctorate in statistics and was working as a research associate for Jerry Cornfield, who had recently retired from NIH. She and Cornfield offered me an opportunity to provide part-time programming support for the research they were doing, funded by an NIH grant. The idea was that I’d begin work in early summer, once the school year was over, and work until the baby came in late October. I wasn’t sure about this—I had taken a couple of programming courses but was certainly not an experienced programmer—but Janet said the work I’d be doing was in APL (A Programming Language), an interactive program that required mathematical rather than programming knowledge. So, I agreed.

I was surprised to learn that Jerry was entirely self-taught as a statistician. His only degree was a B.A. in History from New York University. Yet he had an extraordinarily productive career, beginning at the Bureau of Labor Statistics, where he worked with Wassily Leontief on input-output economic theory (many thought he should have shared Leontief’s Nobel Prize), then at the National Cancer Institute and finally as chief of the Biostatistics Branch at the then-National Heart Institute. During his career at the National Institutes of Health, he became internationally recognized as a leading statistician in the area of human health. Throughout his professional lifetime, and for years after, in discussions about knotty statistical problems, one would frequently hear, “Well, Jerry always said...”—no need to add the surname.

Cornfield was wonderful to work with. He had a great sense of humor (about which more later) and was always very clear about how I should proceed with our work. He was in inveterate pipe smoker in those days, and despite the fact that I was asthmatic and allergic to smoke, I developed a Pavlovian positive response to the smell of smoke as I walked down the hall to our small suite of offices, because it meant that he was actually in the office and I would be able to review my work with him.

He was also very empathetic. Once, one of his visitors, noting my presence in the office but not involved in their discussions, directed me to get him a cup of coffee. I was not a coffee drinker at the time so had no idea how to operate the coffee maker. But I dutifully wandered into the break room; Cornfield, embarrassed that his guest assumed I was the secretary, quickly followed me, saw me looking confusedly at the coffee maker, and made the coffee himself. Cornfield was much in demand to lecture, attend conferences, participate on advisory panels and collaborate with leading statistical and medical colleagues, so he traveled frequently. Once, when I was the only one in the office, I answered the phone and the caller, in a heavily accented voice, asked to speak to Professor Cornfield. Since he was not there, I took a message—from Professor Neyman. I had just started my statistics studies and was awed—when Jerry returned, I gave him the message and asked incredulously, “Is that the Neyman from the LEMMA?!” That generated a hearty laugh from Jerry—I had imagined Neyman was from an earlier century.

Many of the early NIH statisticians—Sam Greenhouse, Max Halperin, Tavia Gordon, among others, were often in the office to visit Jerry. Nathan Mantel was there most frequently. I first met him when he wandered into Jerry’s office while I was reviewing my computer output with him, and started talking about a restaurant he’d been to recently. Jerry turned to him and said sharply, “Nathan, we are working—go away!” I was horrified that he would talk to this famous statistician in this way in front of me (I was only 25 or 26) but soon learned that even the secretaries talked to Nathan that way. Nathan was brilliant—Jerry said he had the best statistical intuition of anyone he knew—but was not very socially adept. (Fundamentally, though, Nathan was a very kind person and helped me out numerous times as I became more involved in statistics and less in programming.)

Jerry sometimes invited Janet and me to accompany him to local meetings. I remember one in particular; it was a meeting of the Steering Committee of the Coronary Drug Project, a major clinical trial being conducted by the then-National Heart Institute. Most of the other participants were cardiologists, and I was very impressed by seeing how critical a role Jerry played in this important trial, and how much his medical colleagues respected him. He served as Vice President of the American Heart Association, in addition to his presidencies of the American Statistical Association and the American Epidemiologic Society.

As my delivery date approached, Jerry and Janet wanted me to continue being part of their team, and they got me an IBM Selectric typewriter and a modem to connect to the George Washington University mainframe computer via our dial telephone. This allowed me to do my work from home; I would get a baby sitter once a week or so and bring my output into the office to review with Jerry. By this time, I was pretty excited about the role of the statistician in medical research and decided to pursue a doctoral degree in statistics.

Jerry’s initial NIH grant was with the University of Pittsburgh, but after a year he switched his affiliation to the George Washington University (GWU), meaning he no longer had to fly to Pittsburgh once a week to teach and meet with students. That was 1972, the origin of the GWU Biostatistics Center. With new grants coming into the office, and Janet departing for New York City, more people were hired, and we needed a bigger workspace. We moved to an entire floor in a Bethesda office building—there were a few offices along the perimeter, but the interior was completely open, so most of us worked at tables in this big open space. As I was given more responsibilities as a junior statistician on new projects, I had fewer opportunities for interaction with Jerry, but he was always there to provide advice—and to entertain us with amusing anecdotes from his many collaborations.

I mentioned Jerry’s wonderful sense of humor. I cannot think of a better example than a letter to the editor he wrote to The American Statistician in 1963,† motivated by an article that was critical of something he had published (Cornfield, 1963). Here it is, in its entirety:

Dear Sir:

In an article entitled Smoking and Lung Cancer by Joseph Berkson, appearing in your October issue the following statement appears: “From some publications [9] [12] on the present subject one may receive the impression that statisticians lie awake nights devising precious statistical arguments (no matter how biologically fanciful) which obscure those data that embarrass the theory they have chosen to propagate.” The reference [12] is to an article by myself and colleagues expressing certain views of which Dr. Berkson apparently disapproves.

The extent to which my nocturnal habits are a matter of scientific interest is not clear to me, but I am nevertheless happy to assure Dr. Berkson that they are not at all as he has imagined them.

Sincerely yours,

Jerome Cornfield

† Letter reprinted with permission of the American Statistical Association.

As wide-ranging as Jerry’s knowledge and interests were, they did not extend to popular culture. One anecdote shared at the memorial symposium held for Jerry in 1981 related to his work with the epidemiologist Ernst Wynder on the relationship between smoking and lung cancer, one of his most important contributions in the area of observational studies. Jerry was meeting with Wynder at the latter’s office in New York City when Wynder (a well-known man-about-town) announced that he had to leave for an appointment—he had a lunch date with Kim Novak. Jerry reportedly responded, “Kim Novak? Who’s he?”

Jerry was diagnosed with pancreatic cancer in 1977. He had a Whipple procedure that removed all visible cancer, and after recovering from surgery was back to his normal, energetic self for about a year, after which the cancer recurred. His illness limited the Biostatistics Center’s efforts to obtain new grants, and I had to look elsewhere for a position when the grant I had been working on ended. Shortly before he died, I visited him in the hospital. Jerry expressed some concern that the new job I planned to take might not allow me to continue to learn more about statistics and encouraged me to look for a position with a statistical mentor. That advice stayed with me; several years later, when I had the opportunity to go to work for Richard Simon, an eminent and highly productive statistician at the National Cancer Institute, I grabbed it, beginning my 11-year tenure at the NIH.

Jerry died in 1979 at the far-too-young age of 67. Shortly after his death, a group of us began planning a memorial symposium, with talks to cover Jerry’s many contributions to both statistics and public health. The proceedings of this two-day symposium, held at the NIH in 1981, were published as a special issue of Biometrics the following year (Proceedings of “Current topics in biostatistics and epidemiology.” A memorial symposium in honor of Jerome Cornfield, 1982). At a banquet held after the first day of the symposium, attendees shared personal reminiscences. I learned new things about Jerry from the comments of those who had known and worked with him for far longer than I had: his approach to driving that could terrify his passengers, his interest in hiking and mountain climbing, and his wide-ranging intellectual interests beyond statistics. The transcript of these remarks is a delight to read; space does not permit including all of it, but I do want to conclude this remembrance with the lyrics to a song written for Jerry at his NIH retirement celebration in 1967 and sung again at the banquet. It’s set to the tune of “The Sorcerer’s Song” from the Gilbert and Sullivan operetta The Sorcerer. (If you are not familiar with that song, you should listen to it here: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HpHaWw1O-NU) to fully appreciate the following lyrics, which note the many areas in which Jerry worked:
