Optimizing postharvest quality of myrtle (Myrtus communis L.) essential oil extracts: effects of temperature and storage duration
Authors: Jamileh Khosravani, Mehdi Hosseinifarahi, Mohsen Radi, Sharareh Najafian
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study investigates the effects of storage conditions on the chemical composition and stability of Myrtus communis essential oil, collected from Firuzabad, Fars Province. A factorial design was employed with three storage temperatures (25 °C, 4 °C, and − 20 °C) and six storage durations (1–6 months). Essential oils were extracted from air-dried plant material via hydro-distillation and analyzed using gas chromatography (GC) and gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS). Principal component analysis (PCA), combined with specific selection criteria (SC), was applied to interpret the data effectively. The results indicate that lower storage temperatures, particularly − 20 °C, tend to better preserve esters such as linalyl acetate over six months, while monoterpene hydrocarbons such as α-pinene and 1,8-cineole appear relatively stable even at 25 °C. Refrigeration at 4 °C provided intermediate preservation. Some degradation of compounds may occur through hydrolysis, oxidation, or temperature-dependent rearrangements, although these mechanisms remain speculative as direct evidence from GC-MS was not obtained. Overall, the findings suggest that controlling storage temperature is important for maintaining the chemical integrity and potential therapeutic properties of M. communis essential oil, with implications for its use in pharmaceutical and cosmetic applications.

Introduction

Myrtus communis L. commonly known as myrtle, is an aromatic evergreen shrub belonging to the Myrtaceae family, widely distributed across the Mediterranean basin and parts of Asia. Its essential oil is renowned for its therapeutic properties, including antimicrobial, antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and antifungal activities, making it highly valuable in pharmaceutical, cosmetic, and food industries [1]. The chemical composition of myrtle essential oil is predominantly characterized by monoterpenes such as α-pinene, 1,8-cineole, linalool, and myrtenyl acetate, which are responsible for its distinctive fragrance and biological activities [2]. The chemical composition of M. communis essential oils is also influenced by genetic variability. In a study by Usai et al. [3], the essential oils from different M. communis genotypes were analyzed, revealing distinct profiles of volatile compounds in the berries, which further supports the idea of selecting specific cultivars for desired chemical properties. Additionally, studies on the essential oils of M. communis from Algeria [4] and Sardinia [5] have also shown varying antimicrobial and antioxidant activities, reinforcing the plant’s versatility for industrial and medicinal use.

However, the chemical profile and quality of essential oils are influenced by several factors, including environmental conditions, harvest time, and notably, storage conditions [6]. The stability and functional integrity of essential oil compounds are significantly affected by storage conditions, particularly temperature and duration. Studies have shown that exposure to high temperatures and prolonged storage can lead to evaporation, isomerization, and polymerization of volatile compounds, resulting in altered chemical composition and reduced bioactivity [7]. For instance, the essential oils of Thymus vulgaris and Lavandula angustifolia exhibited considerable changes in their chemical profiles when stored under inappropriate temperature and light conditions, leading to diminished antioxidant and antimicrobial properties [8]. Evaluating the impact of long-term storage at different temperatures can provide practical guidelines for maintaining the quality of myrtle EO during commercial production and distribution. In every scientific study, researchers rely on established methods and sources from previous studies to ensure consistency and comparability of results. In our research, the temperatures of 25 °C, 4 °C, and − 20 °C have been widely used in various studies involving different plant species, including recent publications, such as those from 2023 [9]. These temperatures were selected due to their widespread application and established credibility in the scientific community. By using these temperatures, we aim to ensure that our findings are comparable with previous research, providing a robust context for understanding the effects of storage conditions on plant compounds. Furthermore, these temperature conditions allow for a more comprehensive examination of how storage influences the phytochemical and functional integrity of the studied plants. This study aims to systematically investigate the effects of temperature (25 °C, 4 °C, and − 20 °C) and storage duration (up to 6 months) on the yield and chemical composition of M. communis essential oil. The findings will contribute to developing evidence-based storage strategies to preserve the essential oil’s quality, ensuring its optimal application in various industries (Fig. 1).

Materials and methods

Plant material collection, Identification, and drying process

The medicinal plant M. communis was collected from Firuzabad in Fars Province, Iran. In this study, M. communis samples were collected from open areas in Firuzabad, Fars Province, located at the following coordinates: 28°49′N, 52°27′E. The elevation of the collection site is 1304 m above sea level. The site is situated along the Firuzabad road, near the vicinity of the village of Moshegan, and is in the region of the Tange Tikab watercourse. The collection was conducted in compliance with local environmental guidelines and regulations for sustainable harvesting of medicinal plants. No protected areas were involved, and no specific permissions were required for the non-destructive collection of plant material in public lands. We adhered to ethical practices to minimize any ecological impact during the sampling process. The samples were transferred to the Fars Province Research Center, where they were identified by Mr. Ahmad Hatami. The voucher specimen has been assigned the number 16,268, and it was stored in the standard herbarium at the Bakhsh Research Center. Mr. Hatami, the head of the herbarium, conducted the identification and provided the herbarium code. After collecting the aerial parts of the plants, they were prepared for drying at an appropriate time. The preparation process not only accelerates the drying but also positively impacts its quality. In this regard, unnecessary parts were removed, and the main parts were divided into suitable pieces. Once the plants were prepared for drying, their aerial parts were dried in the shade (indirect sunlight) for 2 weeks. EO of dried samples (100 g) was extracted by hydro distillation method for 3 h, using a Clevenger type device according to the method mentioned in the British Pharmacopoeia [10].

Storage conditions of EO samples

The EO samples were dehydrated using anhydrous sodium sulfate and stored in tightly sealed brown glass vials to minimize exposure to light and air. The vials were firmly closed for all three storage conditions (refrigerator at 4 °C, freezer at −20 °C, and room temperature at 25 °C) to prevent oxygen ingress. Although oxygen flushing equipment was not available, all samples were handled and stored under identical conditions to ensure the absence of direct light and minimal oxygen exposure. The stored EO samples were monitored monthly using GC and GC–MS analyses for six consecutive months [2].

Analysis of the essential oils GC

The essential oils were analysed on an Agilent gas chromatograph Model 7890-A, equipped with a HP-5 column (30 m ×0.32 mm; 0.25 μm), programming from 60 °C to 210 °C at 4 °C/min, raised at 20 °C/min to 240 °C (8.5 min hold). Nitrogen as carrier gas (1.0 ml/min); injection in split mode (1:50); injector and detector temperature were 250 and 280 °C, respectively, we used a Flame Ionization Detector (FID) [2].

GC-MS analysis

The essential oils were analyzed using an Agilent gas chromatograph (Model 7890 A) coupled with an Agilent mass spectrometer (Model 5975 C) equipped with an HP-5MS column (30 m × 0.25 mm, film thickness 0.25 μm). Helium was used as the carrier gas at a flow rate of 1.0 ml/min. Mass spectra were recorded in electron impact mode at 70 eV, with an electron multiplier voltage of 1500 V and an ion source temperature of 230 °C. Data acquisition was performed in full scan mode over the m/z range 45–550. The oven temperature program was the same as that described for GC analysis.

Identification of essential oil components

The essential oil components were identified by comparing their mass spectra with published data and Adams (2001), and by calculating their retention indices (RI) relative to a homologous series of n-alkanes (C8–C28) using the Van den Dool and Kratz Eq. (1963). Identification was further confirmed, when possible, by comparison with authentic standards available in our laboratory. The reported percentages represent relative peak areas in the chromatogram rather than absolute concentrations. All compound names and stereochemistry were checked according to IUPAC nomenclature [11, 12].

Principal Component Analysis (PCA)

Principal Component Analysis (PCA) is an eigenvector-based technique primarily used to minimize data complexity by reducing the number of variables. This method converts the original variables into a smaller set of principal components that account for most of the data’s variance. PCA aims to resolve data conflicts by organizing multiple variables into distinct components, with the first component capturing the highest possible variance, followed by additional components that represent decreasing levels of variance. In this study, component rotation was performed using Prism 9 software. The key compounds in each principal component were determined based on the selection criterion (SC), calculated using a specific formula [13]:

Statistical design and analysis

The experiment was conducted using a factorial arrangement based on a completely randomized design with three replications. Factor A was temperature with three levels (25 °C, refrigerator temperature 4 °C, and freezer temperature − 20 °C), and Factor B was storage duration (one to six months after distillation of the essential oil).

Data analysis was performed using SAS 9.1.3 software. Mean comparisons were conducted using Duncan’s multiple range test at a 5% significance level. Graphs were generated using Excel 2019 and GraphPad Prism 9 software. Additionally, Pearson correlation analysis between the essential oil components of each plant was performed using SPSS 19 software.

Results and discussion

Essential oil composition after distillation

A total of 37 compounds were identified in the essential oil immediately after distillation. The major components, based on chromatographic peak areas, were α-Pinene (46.66%), 1,8-Cineole (21.61%), Linalool (11.47%), α-Terpineol (4.28%), Linalyl acetate (3.62%), Limonene (2.82%), Geranyl acetate (1.78%), and α-Terpineyl acetate (1.17%) (Table 1; Figs. 2 and 3). Minor variations in other compounds were negligible. These major compounds account for approximately 93.42% of the total essential oil, indicating their dominance and likely contribution to the plant’s therapeutic properties. It should be noted that the reported values represent relative percentages. Therefore, an apparent increase or decrease in a compound’s relative percentage may result from changes in other compounds rather than absolute changes in its concentration. Without data on the total oil yield or absolute compound amounts, it is not possible to distinguish between chemical degradation and physical evaporation.

Principal component analysis of essential oil components

Samples and data

A total of 54 samples (3 storage temperatures × 3 replicates × 6 months) were included in the PCA analysis of essential oil components of M.communis. PCA was performed to visualize the effect of storage temperature on the overall chemical profile, as well as to identify correlations among major compounds.

Room temperature-stored samples

PCA of 35 compounds revealed three components with eigenvalues ≥ 1, explaining 44.24%, 19.56%, and 18.30% of the total variance, cumulatively 82.10%. PC1 captured the major compounds (Linalool, 1,8-Cineole, Linalyl acetate, Limonene, α-Terpineol, α-Pinene), PC2 reflected ester-related compounds (Geranyl and Citronellyl acetate), and PC3 captured minor features (α-Terpineyl acetate). Samples were clearly separated in PC space according to their chemical composition. Correlation analysis identified two main compound groups: one with high mutual correlations (1,8-Cineole, Linalool, Linalyl acetate), and a second group with independent variation (α-Pinene, α-Terpineol, α-Terpineyl acetate) (Fig. 4).

Refrigerator-stored samples (4 °C)

PCA on 34 compounds identified three components with eigenvalues ≥ 1, explaining 46.33%, 22.89%, and 14.30% of variance (total 83.52%). PC1 was dominated by Limonene, 1,8-Cineole, Linalool, Linalyl acetate, Citronellyl acetate, and Geranyl acetate; PC2 by α-Pinene; and PC3 by α-Terpineol. Sample scores indicated clear differentiation: samples #15, #18, #21 had high positive PC1 scores, while #14, #17, #20 had high negative PC1 scores. Sample #11 had a high PC2 score, reflecting the influence of α-Pinene. Correlation analysis showed strong positive relationships among 1,8-Cineole, Geranyl acetate, and Citronellyl acetate (r = 0.83–0.93) (Fig. 5).

PCA revealed two components with eigenvalues ≥ 1, explaining 60.52% and 20.62% of variance (total 81.14%). Sample distribution in the PC1-PC2 space revealed distinct clusters corresponding to chemical profile differences. Correlation analysis highlighted strong positive correlations among major compounds (r = 0.69–0.98), while α-Pinene and α-Terpineol were less correlated with other compounds (Figs. 6 and 7).

Effect of storage conditions on the essential oil composition and stability of Myrtus communis

These major compounds account for approximately 93.42% of the total essential oil, indicating their dominance and likely contribution to the plant’s therapeutic properties. It should be noted that the reported values represent relative percentages. Therefore, an apparent increase or decrease in a compound’s relative percentage may result from changes in other compounds rather than absolute changes in its concentration. Without data on the total oil yield or absolute compound amounts, it is not possible to distinguish between chemical degradation and physical evaporation.

The variation in essential oil composition observed in this study is consistent with previous reports emphasizing the influence of environmental conditions, storage parameters, and extraction methods on M. communis phytochemicals [14]. Essential oil profiles are strongly influenced by geographical and climatic factors, affecting both chemical composition and biological activity 13. Seasonal changes also impact concentrations of monoterpenes, particularly α-pinene and 1,8-cineole, which were the dominant compounds in our samples [15].

Interestingly, our findings differ markedly from a study investigating M. communis essential oil in the context of food preservation [16]. While α-pinene (46.66%) and 1,8-cineole (21.61%) were the major compounds in our samples, their study reported myrtenyl acetate (15.5%) and β-linalool (12.3%) as the dominant constituents. They also observed lower concentrations of 1,8-cineole (9.9%) and α-pinene (4.4%), along with the presence of compounds such as myrtenol (1.2%), which were absent or present only in trace amounts in our study. These differences likely result from variations in environmental conditions, plant genotype, phenological stage at harvest, and extraction methodologies [16].

Previous studies have highlighted the cytotoxic and antimigratory properties of M. communis essential oil, attributing its bioactivity to dominant monoterpenes [17]. This aligns with our results, where the high α-pinene and 1,8-cineole content suggests potential therapeutic applications. Analyses of trace elements further reinforce its nutritional and medicinal significance [18].

Storage conditions play a crucial role in essential oil stability. Prolonged storage at high temperatures induces oxidative degradation of sensitive compounds such as linalool and α-terpineol, affecting therapeutic efficacy [19]. Storage temperature also influences insecticidal and toxicological properties, with shifts in chemical composition impacting bioactivity [20]. Regional variation contributes to compositional differences; for example, M. communis essential oil from southern Algeria exhibited significant differences in physicochemical properties compared to Mediterranean populations, highlighting ecological and geographical effects [21].

In this study, long-term stability of M. communis essential oil was examined under ambient (25 °C), refrigerated (4 °C), and frozen (−20 °C) conditions. Storage temperature significantly influenced the stability of key constituents. At 25 °C, α-pinene increased from 46.66% to 48.90% over six months (Table 2), possibly due to volatilization of labile compounds or chemical rearrangement processes, as reported in Thymus daenensis essential oil [22]. Similarly, 1,8-cineole showed a moderate increase under ambient conditions, consistent with temperature effects on monoterpenes in Lavandula officinalis essential oil [7]. Both freezing and room temperature storage preserved α-pinene and 1,8-cineole in the short term, although freezing is preferred for long-term storage.

Compared to 25 °C, refrigeration (4 °C) moderately slowed the degradation of volatile compounds, although minor losses were still observed. α-Pinene and 1,8-cineole remained relatively stable, while minor fluctuations occurred in linalool and linalyl acetate [24]. Freezing (−20 °C) was the most effective strategy, minimizing oxidation, hydrolysis, and volatilization, and preserving α-pinene, 1,8-cineole, limonene, linalool, and linalyl acetate with negligible changes over six months [9]. Previous studies on medicinal plant essential oils similarly report refrigeration and freezing as the most effective methods for maintaining oil integrity [2, 23–25].

Note on relative percentages

All reported values are expressed as relative percentages. Apparent increases or decreases in a compound may reflect changes in other compounds rather than absolute changes. Without data on total oil yield or absolute compound amounts, it is not possible to definitively distinguish between chemical transformations and physical evaporation.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates the significant influence of storage temperature on the stability and chemical composition of M. communis essential oil. The effects of storage conditions varied depending on the type of compound. Freezing at −20 °C was most effective for preserving esters such as linalyl acetate, helping to maintain their integrity over six months. In contrast, monoterpene hydrocarbons such as α-pinene and 1,8-cineole were relatively stable even at 25 °C, showing similar or slightly better preservation compared to lower temperatures. Refrigeration at 4 °C provided intermediate preservation, with some degradation occurring for certain compounds. While degradation of some compounds may involve processes such as hydrolysis, oxidation, or temperature-dependent rearrangements, these mechanistic interpretations remain speculative, as direct evidence from GC-MS for intermediates or oxidation products was not obtained. Overall, the findings indicate that controlling storage temperature is important for maintaining the chemical stability and potential therapeutic efficacy of M. communis essential oil, but the optimal condition depends on the target compounds. For long-term preservation of esters, freezing is recommended. Future studies over longer periods (e.g., one year or more) under consistent temperature conditions are necessary to more accurately assess essential oil stability. These results have practical implications for the commercial production and storage of essential oils, ensuring their effectiveness in pharmaceutical, cosmetic, and other applications.
