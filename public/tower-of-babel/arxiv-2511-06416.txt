Online Subspace Learning on Flag Manifolds for System Identification
Dian Jin, Jeremy Coulson

Abstract
Data-driven control methods based on subspace representations are powerful but are often limited to linear time-invariant systems where the model order is known. A key challenge is developing online data-driven control algorithms for time-varying systems, especially when the system’s complexity is unknown or changes over time. To address this, we propose a novel online subspace learning framework that operates on flag manifolds. Our algorithm leverages streaming data to recursively track an ensemble of nested subspaces, allowing it to adapt to varying system dimensions without prior knowledge of the true model order. We show that our algorithm is a generalization of the Grassmannian Recursive Algorithm for Tracking. The learned subspace models are then integrated into a data-driven simulation framework to perform prediction for unknown dynamical systems. The effectiveness of this approach is demonstrated through a case study where the proposed adaptive predictor successfully handles abrupt changes in system dynamics and outperforms several baselines.

2.1
Subspace geometry

A Grassmannian is the set of all subspaces in
\mathbb{R}^{p}
with dimension
d
denoted by
\mathrm{Gr}(p,d)\coloneqq\{\mathbf{U}
a linear subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{p}:\dim\mathbf{U}=d\}.

Flag manifolds generalize the Grassmannians by considering sequences of nested subspaces of increasing dimension.
Let
p\geq 2
be an integer and
q_{1:d}\coloneqq(q_{1},\dots,q_{d})
with
0<q_{1}<\cdots<q_{d}<p.

A flag of signature
(p,q_{1:d})
is a nested sequence of linear subspaces
\{\mathbf{U}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{d}
of
\mathbb{R}^{p}
satisfying

\{0\}\subset\mathbf{U}_{1}\subset\cdots\subset\mathbf{U}_{d}\subset\mathbb{R}^{p}

with
\dim\mathbf{U}_{j}=q_{j},j=1,\dots,d
. We denote this flag by
\mathbf{U}_{1:d}
.
The set of all such flags forms a smooth manifold
\mathrm{Flag}(p,q_{1:d})

(
40
)
.
We also denote
q_{0}=0,q=q_{d},q_{d+1}=p
.
We can represent flags as points on the Stiefel manifold
\mathrm{St}(p,q)=\{U\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times q}:U^{\top}U=I\}
consisting of orthonormal matrices.
Define for each
k=1,\dots,d
,
U_{k}\in\mathrm{St}(p,q_{k}-q_{k-1})
such that
[U_{1}\cdots U_{k}]
is an orthonormal basis of
\mathbf{U}_{k}
. We must note that under these notations,
\mathbf{U}_{k}
is not the subspace spanned by
U_{k}
.
Then
U_{1:d}\coloneqq[U_{1}\cdots U_{d}]
is a representative of
\mathbf{U}_{1:d}
, called Stiefel representative. Throughout this paper we will abuse the notation to write
U_{1:d}\in\mathrm{Flag}(p,q_{1:d})
.
If the signature is
(p,q_{1})
, then
\mathrm{Flag}(p,(q_{1}))=\mathrm{Gr}(p,q_{1})
.
The chordal distance of two subspaces
\mathbf{A}\in\mathrm{Flag}(p,(m)),\mathbf{B}\in\mathrm{Flag}(p,(n))
is defined by
\mathrm{d}(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B})=(\sum_{i=1}^{\min(m,n)}\sin^{2}\theta_{i})^{1/2}

(
39
, Theorem 12)
, where
\theta_{i}
is the
i^{\text{th}}
principal angle
(
18
)

between
\mathbf{A}
and
\mathbf{B}
.
We present a simple example to illustrate the difference between flags and subspaces.

Example 1
.

Consider the canonical basis
e_{1}=(1,0,0,0),e_{2}=(0,1,0,0),e_{3}=(0,0,1,0),e_{4}=(0,0,0,1)
of
\mathbb{R}^{4}
. Let
\mathbf{U}_{1}=\mathrm{span}\{e_{1}\},\mathbf{U}_{2}=\mathrm{span}\{e_{1},e_{2}\},\mathbf{U}_{3}=\mathrm{span}\{e_{1},e_{2},e_{3}\}
, then
\mathbf{U}_{1:3}\in\mathrm{Flag}(4,(1,2,3))
, and a Stiefel representation of this flag is the matrix
U=\begin{bmatrix}e_{1}&e_{2}&e_{3}\end{bmatrix}
.
Note that even if two bases span the same subspace, the hierarchical structures of their flags may be different. Let
V=\begin{bmatrix}e_{1}&e_{3}&e_{2}\end{bmatrix}
, then clearly
\mathrm{im~}V=\mathrm{im~}U
. However,
\mathbf{V}_{1}=\mathrm{span}\{e_{1}\},\mathbf{V}_{2}=\mathrm{span}\{e_{1},e_{3}\},\mathbf{V}_{3}=\mathrm{span}\{e_{1},e_{2},e_{3}\}
, which implies
\mathbf{V}_{2}\neq\mathbf{U}_{2}
, hence
\mathbf{V}_{1:d}\neq\mathbf{U}_{1:d}
.

3
Problem Setup & Motivation

The goal of this paper is to estimate an unknown, time-varying subspace
\mathbf{U}^{t}
from streaming data. More precisely, for each
t\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
, let

\mathbf{U}^{t}\in\bigcup_{j=1}^{d}\mathrm{Gr}(p,q_{j}),

where
0<q_{1}<\cdots<q_{d}<p
. We assume that we have access to an upper bound
q_{d}
on the dimension of all subspaces
\mathbf{U}^{t}
. Note that the upper bound
q_{d}
may not be tight in the sense that there may be no time instance
t\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
where
\dim\mathbf{U}^{t}=q_{d}
. We assume that at each
t\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
, we measure the data
w_{t}=v_{t}+\eta_{t},

where
v_{t}\in\mathbf{U}^{t}
, and
\eta_{t}
is noise.
We wish to obtain estimates
\widehat{\mathbf{U}}^{t}
such that

\mathrm{d}(\widehat{\mathbf{U}}^{t},\mathbf{U}^{t})\leq\epsilon_{\textup{tol}}

for some user-defined tolerance
\epsilon_{\textup{tol}}>0
, i.e., we seek estimates that are “close” to the true subspaces.
We refer to this problem as
learning

\mathbf{U}^{t}
or
tracking

\mathbf{U}^{t}
.
In the case when
d=1
and
q_{d}
is known, there are efficient algorithms
(
38
;
3
;
29
)
for robustly learning
\mathbf{U}^{t}
. However, in many practical settings the true dimension of the underlying subspaces are
unknown
and may even vary over time (e.g., online dynamic mode decomposition for time-varying dynamical systems
(
41
)
, direction of arrival analysis
(
28
)
).
Solving this problem is critical for online system identification and control: a fixed, mis-specified subspace dimension yields biased estimates, slow adaptation, and brittle decisions
(
23
)
(see Figure
3
). Learning a nested hierarchy of subspaces (flag) online gives rank-on-demand models that track changing complexity, enabling efficient prediction, reliable change-point detection, without prior knowledge of the true order
(
30
)
.

4
Online subspace learning on flag manifolds

Inspired by recent advances in subspace learning for online system identification
(
29
)
, we propose a method for solving the learning problem in Section
3
, which consists of estimating the evolving subspace
\mathbf{U}^{t}
using optimization on the flag manifold. Each element of the flag manifold is an ensemble of subspaces that can be used for downstream tasks such as data-driven prediction. Thus, our algorithm contributes to a data-to-prediction pipeline that continuously adapts to streaming data and the changing complexity of the system (see section
5.2
).

4.1
Gradient descent on flag manifolds with streaming data

Let
\{\mathbf{U}^{t}\}_{t\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}}
be a sequence of subspaces with each
\mathbf{U}^{t}\in\bigcup_{j=1}^{d}\mathrm{Gr}(p,q_{j})
. At each time
t\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
we observe a data sample
w_{t}=v_{t}+\eta_{t}
, where
v_{t}\in\mathbf{U}^{t}
and
\eta_{t}
is noise.
Let
T\geq 1
. For each
t\geq T-1
, let
W_{t}=[w_{t-T+1}~\cdots~w_{t}]\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times T}
be the window of the most recent
T
data samples.
For each
t\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
and
j\in\{1,\dots,d\}
, we define the cost function
g_{W_{t}}^{j}\colon\mathrm{Gr}(p,q_{j})\to\mathbb{R}
by

g_{W_{t}}^{j}(\mathbf{U})=\norm{W_{t} - \Pi_{\bmU}W_{t}}_{F}^{2},

(2)

where
\|\cdot\|_{F}
is the Frobenius norm. Minimizing (
2
) over
\mathbf{U}\in\mathrm{Gr}(p,q_{j})
is known as principal component analysis
(
6
, Section 2.4)
and entails finding the
q_{j}
-dimensional subspace that best fits the data in the sense of minimizing the projection error. It is well-known that principal component analysis admits a closed-form solution
(
13
)
given by the top
q_{j}
singular vectors (principal vectors) of
W_{t}
.
One approach to solving the proposed problem would be running
d
separate optimizations on each
\mathrm{Gr}(p,q_{k})
and select a suitable dimension, but this is computationally expensive and would yield optimal subspaces
\mathbf{V}_{j}^{t}:=\argmin_{\mathbf{U}\in\mathrm{Gr}(p,q_{j})}g_{W_{t}}^{j}(\mathbf{U})
that are not nested for
j=1,\dots,d
.
The lack of nestedness has been shown
(
30
)
to cause poor performance when using subspaces for tasks such as robust subspace recovery.
Instead, we propose optimizing over
\mathrm{Flag}(p,q_{1:d})
.
For each
t\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
we define

f_{W_{t}}(\mathbf{U}_{1:d})=\norm{W_{t} - \frac{1}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\Pi_{\bmU_i}W_{t}}_{F}^{2}

(3)

for all
\mathbf{U}_{1:d}\in\mathrm{Flag}(p,q_{1:d})
.
One key difference of optimizing (
3
) instead of (
2
) and is that we obtain a nested sequence of subspaces which can be leveraged for downstream tasks (e.g., data-driven prediction) via ensembling methods
(
11
)
(see Section
5.2
).
The operator
\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\Pi_{\mathbf{U}_{i}}
demonstrates an ensemble of projections onto subspaces of different dimensions, which is called the “flag trick” in
(
30
, Definition 5)
. Similar to (
2
), minimizers of (
3
) can be computed in closed form by computing the top-
q_{d}
principal vectors of
W_{t}

(
30
, Theorem 4)
.
However, storing the sample covariance
W_{t}W_{t}^{\top}
and compute the top-
q_{d}
eigenvectors at every
t

leads to expensive memory and time complexities for large-scale problems
(
2
)
,
motivating an online recursive approach.
Furthermore, one could consider minimizing different functions or modifications of (
3
) (e.g., with regularization) for which no closed-form solutions exist, further motivating iterative updates.
Based on results in optimization on flag manifolds
(
40
;
42
)
, we perform Riemannian gradient descent
(
6
, Section 4.3)
for (
3
).
The goal of Riemannian gradient descent is to seek a minimizer of the function (
3
) by iterative updates. In particular, for each new data sample
w_{t}
, we perform
K>0
steps along the gradient of (
3
). Suppose that the initial point is
\widehat{\mathbf{U}}^{T-1}_{1:d}
(we use the superscript

since we have to collect
T
data points to form the first window
W_{T-1}
). For each fixed

,
we update

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathbf{U}}^{t,k+1}_{1:d}=\mathrm{Exp}_{\widehat{\mathbf{U}}^{t,k}_{1:d}}\left(-\alpha_{t}\mathrm{grad}~f_{W_{t}}(\widehat{\mathbf{U}}^{t,k}_{1:d})\right),

(4)

where

,
\alpha_{t}>0
is the step size, and we denote

\widehat{\mathbf{U}}^{T-1,0}_{1:d}=\widehat{\mathbf{U}}^{T-1}_{1:d}
,

\widehat{\mathbf{U}}^{t+1}_{1:d}\coloneqq\widehat{\mathbf{U}}^{t,K}_{1:d}
,

\widehat{\mathbf{U}}^{t+1,0}_{1:d}=\widehat{\mathbf{U}}^{t+1}_{1:d}
, for all
t\geq T-1
.
The Riemannian gradient
\mathrm{grad}~f_{W_{t}}
is a vector field on the manifold, and
\mathrm{Exp}_{\widehat{\mathbf{U}}^{t,k}_{1:d}}
is the exponential map, which maps the tangent vector
-\alpha_{t}\mathrm{grad}~f_{W_{t}}(\widehat{\mathbf{U}}^{t,k}_{1:d})
back to the manifold.
Riemannian gradient and exponential map can be computed using only matrix algebra
(
40
)
.

\mathrm{Gr}(p,q)
.
Therefore, we have
\widehat{\mathbf{U}}^{t,k}=\widehat{\mathbf{V}}^{t,k}
for all
t\in\{T-1,T,\dots\}
and
k\in\{0,\cdots,K-1\}
, hence
\mathcal{A}_{1}
and
\mathcal{A}_{2}
are equivalent.
∎

Under assumptions in
(
29
, Assumptions 1-4)
, all convergence guarantees and finite-sample analysis of
(
29
, Theorem 1)
directly extend to Algorithm
1

(
29

derives finite-sample upper bounds on the distance

. In the future work, we wish to study finite-sample convergence guarantees for Algorithm

.
To evaluate the effectiveness of Algorithm

6
Conclusion

We proposed the Flag Recursive Online Tracking algorithm for online system identification and data-driven prediction that adapts to time-varying systems by recursively updating a nested hierarchy of subspaces. This enables the predictor to accommodate unknown or changing system dimensions without prior model information.
We proved that FRONT is a natural generalization of Grassmannian Recursive Algorithm for Tracking.
Numerical studies demonstrated that the proposed predictor effectively tracks abrupt changes in dynamics and consistently achieves better prediction error than most baselines even under high noise-to-signal ratio.
Future work focuses on finite sample convergence of Algorithm
1
for nontrivial flag signatures and integrating the proposed flag-based learning framework into an adaptive data-to-control pipeline.
