Cognitive reserve, frailty status, and risk of neurodegenerative diseases: a prospective cohort study
Authors: Xiaxuan Huang, Yitong Ling, Shanyuan Tan, Zihong Bai, Si Shen, Hao Wang, Jun Lyu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Cognitive frailty has emerged as an important concept in research and clinical practice, yet the combined effect of cognitive reserve and frailty on neurodegenerative disease risk remains unexplored. This study included 346,025 UK Biobank participants followed for up to 15 years. Cognitive reserve indicators were generated using latent class analysis based on educational level, occupational achievement, confiding in others, social contact, leisure activities, and television viewing time. The primary outcome was neurodegenerative disease, with secondary outcomes including Parkinson’s disease, Alzheimer’s disease, and all-cause dementia. During a median follow-up of 13.7 years, 5,590 new cases of neurodegenerative diseases were diagnosed. Compared to non-frail individuals, pre-frail and frail individuals had 1.47-fold (95% CI: 1.39-1.55) and 2.74-fold (95% CI: 2.46-3.06) increased risk, respectively, while high cognitive reserve conferred protection (HR = 0.82, 95% CI: 0.76-0.87). In joint effect analysis, individuals with low levels of cognitive reserve and frailty had the highest risk (HR = 3.13, 95% CI: 2.70-3.63), demonstrating significant additive interaction. Cross-sectional neuroimaging analyses showed that lower cognitive reserve levels was associated with reduced total brain volume (β = -0.161), reduced hippocampal volumes (βleft = -0.085, βright = -0.097), and increased white matter hyperintensities (β = 0.045). These findings emphasize maintaining cognitive reserve and managing frailty as modifiable factors for preventing neurodegenerative diseases.

Introduction

With the global aging population, the prevalence of neurodegenerative diseases, such as Alzheimer’s disease (AD)1 and Parkinson’s disease (PD)2 has increased substantially. It is estimated that by 2050, the global incidence of dementia related to these and other diseases will reach 152 million individuals3. Currently, there are no methods to cure neurodegenerative diseases and their associated progressive cognitive, behavioral, and functional impairments, with primary therapeutic strategies focusing on prevention4,5. Proactive management of age-related factors including frailty, diabetes, hypertension, and sarcopenia is strongly advocated6. Consequently, the early intervention of covert and progressive neurological deterioration presents a significant challenge.

The earliest research on cognitive reserve was inspired by Stern’s observation that adults with higher education levels typically develop dementia later in life7. Cognitive reserve (CR), as a hypothetical construct, refers to the brain’s structural and dynamic capacity to buffer against atrophy and pathology8,9. It cannot be observed or directly measured and is frequently assessed using proxy measurements, such as education, intelligence, and occupational complexity, which have been confirmed to contribute to developing more resilient neuronal networks10. Higher early education, cognitively demanding occupations, engagement in intellectually stimulating activities, maintenance of strong social connections, and participation in leisure activities involving intellectual engagement have all been reported to contribute to cognitive reserve11. Therefore, this study attempts to integrate these factors to construct accessible cognitive reserve variables to capture potential associations between CR and neurodegenerative diseases.

Frailty, as a geriatric clinical syndrome, is characterized by multi-system functional decline, decreased homeostatic reserve, and increased vulnerability to stressors12,13. Frailty can be operationalized through different approaches, including the physical frailty phenotype, which captures core biological aging processes, and cumulative deficit indices that encompass broader health domains. The physical frailty phenotype has been extensively validated for predicting cognitive decline and neurodegenerative diseases. Previous research examining the association between cognitive performance and frailty among 20,857 citizens from 12 countries found that individuals with pre-frailty and frailty faced greater risk of cognitive impairment14. Recent studies have expanded the definition of frailty, acknowledging that cognitive impairment coexists with physical frailty, thus proposing the preliminary concept of cognitive frailty, which is considered to play a significant role in clinical outcomes and predicting cognitive decline15. However, recent evidence exploring the relationship between cognitive reserve and frailty in relation to AD and related dementias (ADRD) has primarily focused on specific outcomes with limited sample sizes16, therefore, these studies have not fully captured the broader implications for other neurodegenerative diseases or the underlying mechanisms involved.

Given preliminary evidence suggesting cognitive reserve is reflected in hippocampal and gray matter volumetric changes, as demonstrated by Vuoksimaa and colleagues17, who established that general cognitive ability in young adults moderates the relationship between hippocampal volume and episodic memory, with a positive correlation between memory performance and hippocampal volume observed specifically in individuals with low cognitive reserve, with memory and hippocampal volume positively correlated in the low reserve group. Therefore, this study further explores changes in brain micro and macro structures at low cognitive reserve levels to extend their potential role in promoting neurodegenerative diseases.

Utilizing UK Biobank’s enriched cognitive reserve variables and high-quality neuroimaging data, this study comprehensively examines associations among cognitive reserve, frailty, and neurodegenerative diseases, emphasizing potential synergistic effects between cognitive reserve and frailty. We further investigate whether cognitive reserve-brain structural alteration correlations constitute the underlying foundation of this association.

CR, frailty status and neurodegenerative diseases

During a median follow-up of 13.7 years, 5590 participants developed incident neurodegenerative diseases (ND), comprising 4300 cases of primary neurodegenerative diseases (Primary ND) and 775 cases of vascular neurodegenerative diseases (Vascular ND). Specific subtypes included 3637 cases of ACD, 1966 cases of PD, and 1557 cases of AD (Figure S1). Table 2 presents the hazard ratios for associations between frailty status, cognitive reserve level, and various ND outcomes. Notably, individuals with pre-frail and frail status exhibited significantly elevated risks of neurodegenerative diseases compared to non-frail individuals. Joint effect of CR, frailty status and neurodegenerative diseases

In the joint effect analysis, using individuals with non-frail status and high CR as the reference group, we systematically evaluated the combined impact of frailty status and CR level on ND risk. Results demonstrated a clear dose-response relationship between frailty severity and CR levels. Specifically, among pre-frail individuals, those with moderate levels of CR had an HR of 1.54 (95% CI: 1.40–1.70) for ND, while those with low levels of CR had an HR of 1.72 (95% CI: 1.57–1.89). In fully frail individuals, the risk increased more substantially, with HRs of 3.10 (95% CI: 2.54–3.79) for moderate levels of CR and 3.13 (95% CI: 2.70–3.63) for low levels of CR (Table 3). Notably, among frail individuals, the proportion of neurodegenerative disease incidence attributable to low levels of CR was 6.05% (95% CI: 3.19-8.90%), indicating that individuals with both frailty and low levels of CR constitute the highest risk group for neurodegenerative disease development. In subtype analyses of ND, we observed consistent risk elevation trends, with vascular ND and ACD showing the most pronounced risk increases in individuals with frailty and low levels of CR, with HRs of 7.61 and 4.20, respectively. For AD and PD, the HRs were 3.76 and 2.95, respectively. Further interaction analysis revealed significant additive interaction between frailty status and CR level. For participants with frailty and low levels of CR, the RERI was 0.49, with an AP of 0.16, suggesting that the interaction between frailty and low levels of CR leads to an additional 0.49 relative excess risk of neurodegenerative disease, accounting for 16% of the total risk in this population (Table S4). All E values were close to or exceeded the generally accepted robustness threshold range, supporting the credibility and robustness of the observed associations (Table S5).

Sensitivity and subgroup analyses

Sensitivity analyses further substantiated the robustness of our findings, as results remained significant despite adjustment for additional potential confounders. The associations persisted in models further adjusted for comorbidity and medication histories, when analyses were restricted to participants aged 55 years and older, and after excluding individuals who developed neurodegenerative diseases within the first three years of follow-up. Additionally, competing risk analyses treating death as a competing event reinforced the reliability of the association between cognitive reserve combined with frailty status and neurodegenerative disease incidence (Tables S6–9). Subgroup analyses revealed stronger associations among middle-aged and elderly males, white participants, and obese individuals. Consistent risk estimates were observed across all subgroups (Table S10), although no statistically significant interactions were identified with any potential effect modifiers (all interaction P > 0.05). In addition, the results of separate analyses of each CR-related factor and neurodegenerative disease and its subtype were analyzed sequentially (Table S11).

Influence of CR levels on macro- and micro-structural brain changes

After controlling for potential covariates and multiple comparisons, neuroimaging analysis revealed significant associations between cognitive reserve and brain structural alterations related to neurodegenerative diseases, as shown in Fig. 2. Compared to high CR levels, low CR was significantly associated with reduced volumes of total brain (β = -0.161), white matter (β = -0.059), gray matter (β = -0.070), left hippocampus (β = -0.085), and right hippocampus (β = -0.097), but with increased WMH volume (β = 0.045). Furthermore, low levels of CR demonstrated significant associations with most regional brain structural indices, including cortical and subcortical area, thickness, and volume, particularly in bilateral parahippocampal, cingulate, middle and inferior temporal lobes, and cuneus regions. Mediation analysis

Figure 3 elucidates the complex interplay between CR levels, frailty status, and ND risk. In models adjusted for age, sex, TDI, ethnicity, smoking status, alcohol intake status, BMI, CR explained 2.10% of the mediating effect between frailty status and ND, while frailty mediated 26.58% of the relationship between CR levels and ND. This bidirectional mediation suggests a synergistic effect between cognitive reserve and frailty status, indicating that their combined influence on neurodegenerative disease risk may be more pronounced than their independent effects. Discussion

In this study, we explored the associations between neurodegenerative diseases and their subtypes with CR levels and frailty status. Compared to low CR levels, high CR levels were associated with a reduced neurodegenerative disease risk, while pre-frail and frail individuals exhibited significantly elevated risks compared to non-frail counterparts. Additionally, low CR levels showed significant associations with neurodegenerative-related structural changes, including global and regional brain volume reductions, increased white matter hyperintensities, revealing potential neurobiological substrates of cognitive reserve. As hypothesized, mediation analysis confirmed important synergistic effects between cognitive reserve and frailty status in identifying neurodegenerative diseases.

Frailty has emerged as one of the most challenging health burdens associated with population aging, with risk factors spanning broad domains including sociodemographics, clinical, lifestyle, and biological factors18,19. Increasing epidemiological evidence and clinical trials demonstrate that frailty, as an age-related condition throughout the life course, is associated with a higher risk of adverse outcomes13,20. Our findings emphasize pre-frailty and frailty status critical role in predicting neurodegenerative disease risk. Notably, frail individuals demonstrated substantially elevated risk for neurodegenerative disease subtypes, including a 3.27-fold increased risk for ACD, 2.61-fold for AD, and 2.84-fold for PD. These results further support addressing physical frailty importance for early brain cognitive aging prevention.

Correspondingly, cognitive reserve serves as an important proxy for explaining individual differences in cognitive function following brain aging and irreversible damage. Recent studies have adopted more readily measurable surrogate indicators—including educational attainment, occupational complexity, social engagement, and mental-physical stimulation10. These indicators consider multiple factors of cognitive functional reserve constructed through long-term brain accumulation and experience. Emerging evidence preliminarily confirms that individuals with higher cognitive reserve may exhibit enhanced resilience against cognitive decline and neuropathology21. Consistent results were observed in this study, namely that individuals with high levels of cognitive reserve, compared to low cognitive reserve levels, were associated with lower risks of neurodegenerative diseases, particularly in vascular-origin subclinical subtypes. This may stem from multiple neurobiological mechanisms. High cognitive reserve may counteract vascular pathology by promoting healthy cerebrovascular networks. Long-term cognitive activities and learning processes stimulate increased cerebral blood flow, subsequently enhancing vascular permeability and maintenance of blood-brain barrier function22. Additionally, cognitive reserve may attenuate the impact of vascular-origin damage through neuroplasticity mechanisms23. High cognitive activity promotes formation of new synaptic connections between neurons, when original neural networks are compromised by vascular damage, these alternative neural pathways can maintain cognitive function24.

A recent review proposed cognitive frailty as a critical intersection between physical and cognitive decline15. Age-related cognitive and brain aging may reduce the brain’s resistance to injury, thereby accelerating cognitive deterioration. Our study extends this finding based on preliminary evidence from a large community cohort study16. Low cognitive reserve significantly increases the risk of neurodegenerative diseases at the level of frailty, and consistent results have been observed in PD, AD, and ACD, suggesting that this combined effect may offset multiple potential causes. Recent cross-sectional studies indicate lower educational attainment and primary occupations with low cognitive demands and limited social support significantly increase frailty likelihood, highlighting cognitive reserve proxies’ crucial role in predicting frailty25. Additionally, a cohort study of elderly residents in suburban Japan found that individuals who self-reported at least one day of leisure-time physical activity per week had significantly reduced risk of AD compared to their less active counterparts. Also, Yang W and colleagues were the first to confirm the association between high cognitive reserve and reduced dementia risk in a prospective study11. We propose that frailty and cognitive impairment share common risk factors and biological mechanisms. From a neurobiological perspective, this joint effect involves multi-level integration rather than merely independent influences. Early life experiences mediate brain plasticity by regulating neuronal signaling pathways that affect synaptic function and neural connectivity26,27. High cognitive reserve may counteract these processes through enhancing neural compensation28, promoting network reorganization29, and improving responses to oxidative stress30. Our findings suggest the cognitive reserve-frailty interaction exhibits a bidirectional relationship, simultaneous maintenance of cognitive compensatory mechanisms and frailty alleviation may form a “dual protection” against neurodegenerative diseases, providing valuable preclinical identification insights.

Of note, a significant contribution of this study is the comprehensive consideration of cognitive reserve and its associations with brain structural changes. We identified significant associations between cognitive reserve and alterations in both micro and macrostructures of the brain, with low cognitive reserve levels particularly correlated with atrophy in total brain volume, white matter, and gray matter volumes. From a neuropathophysiological perspective, when healthy neurons fall below a specific threshold, the brain dynamically recruits other regions and networks through functional rerouting mechanisms to maintain cognitive function, with this compensatory hyperactivation typically manifested as enhanced bilateral activation of the prefrontal cortex31. This dynamic reserve model reflects the brain’s adaptive response to neurodegenerative pathology. Individuals with high cognitive reserve demonstrate greater efficiency and flexibility in neural networks, responding more rapidly to pathological degeneration9. Additionally, individuals with higher cognitive reserve tend to possess superior socioeconomic conditions and healthier behavioral patterns, factors that synergistically decelerate the progression of brain pathology and enhance neuronal resilience to damage. The complexity of this association necessitates further exploration through longitudinal neuroimaging studies.

Our study extends existing literature by providing new evidence on the joint effects of cognitive reserve and frailty on neurodegenerative disease risk and associated brain structural abnormalities. This study’s strengths include long-term follow-up with large sample size, comprehensive coverage of clinical and subclinical neurodegenerative outcomes, high-quality brain imaging, and thorough consideration of potential confounding factors. Several limitations must be acknowledged. First, cognitive reserve remains a hypothetical concept lacking standardized assessment methods; our study excluded cognitive performance from reserve measurement, as it likely reflects rather than drives cognitive reserve, and substantial follow-up data for comprehensive assessment was unavailable11,32. Second, self-reported health indicators may introduce reporting and recall bias33. Third, we assessed physical frailty phenotype rather than broader frailty indices. While the physical phenotype captures core biological aging processes and enables clearer distinction from cognitive factors, future research employing multidimensional frailty indices would provide valuable complementary evidence. Fourth, although neurodegenerative diseases and subtypes were primarily diagnosed through medical records, subtype misclassification may occur, necessitating validation via neuroimaging or additional biomarkers34. Fifth, because of the cross-sectional nature of the brain imaging data, we cannot establish causal relationships between cognitive reserve and brain structural changes. Moreover, alterations in brain morphometry may be confounded by other predisposing factors beyond cognitive reserve. Sixth, being an observational study, residual confounding factors could not be entirely eliminated despite extensive adjustments, allowing false-positive associations to persist. Finally, the UK Biobank’s healthy volunteer bias may limit generalizability and contribute to underestimation of observed associations, as participants may not represent the broader population35. Missing data for cognitive reserve factors may also affect result robustness, though complete case analysis was used to minimize imputation-related biases. Future studies should consider utilizing imputation methods to improve the generalizability of findings across diverse populations.

In conclusion, cognitive reserve and frailty synergistically influence neurodegenerative disease risk through additive interactions. Low levels of cognitive reserve combined with frailty conferred the highest risk, while high levels of cognitive reserve provided significant protection across disease subtypes. Lower cognitive reserve levels was associated with brain structural abnormalities, supporting underlying mechanisms. These findings establish cognitive reserve and frailty as modifiable targets for risk stratification and preventive interventions against neurodegenerative diseases.

Methods

Study design and participants

The UK Biobank is a large population-based prospective cohort study encompassing 22 assessment centers across England, Scotland, and Wales. Between 2006 and 2010, the study recruited over 500,000 participants aged 37–73 years36. At baseline enrollment, extensive information was collected through touchscreen questionnaires, interviews, physical measurements, health records, and biological samples, with participants completing detailed questionnaires on demographics, lifestyle choices, and medical conditions37. Since 2014, all participants have been invited to undergo neuroimaging assessments. The study protocol adheres to NHS Northwest Multicenter Research Ethics Committee-approved ethical standards, with participants consenting to health record access.

Neurodegenerative outcomes

The primary outcome of this study was the diagnosis of any neurodegenerative disease. Primary or vascular neurodegenerative diseases, as well as specific neurodegenerative diseases (such as PD, AD, and all-cause dementia) were considered as secondary outcomes38. The definition of diagnosis was mainly based on the corresponding International Classification of Diseases Coding System (ICD-10) code in the hospital admission record (primary or secondary hospital diagnosis) or death registry record (Table S17). This methodology is grounded in the utilization of high-quality, clinically recognized data sources, ensuring reliable definitions of neurodegenerative conditions34. Prior validations within the UK Biobank have demonstrated that these diagnostic procedures yield a positive predictive value of 84.5% for all-cause dementia and 84.0% for PD when benchmarked against comprehensive clinical assessments39. Follow-up time was calculated from baseline assessment to the earliest occurrence of incident neurodegenerative disease diagnosis, death from any cause, loss to follow-up, or administrative censoring date. We identified incident cases through linkage to hospital inpatient records and national mortality registries using ICD-10 diagnostic codes. We applied the recommended UK Biobank data provider-specific censoring dates to ensure consistent data handling across different regions40,41.

Assessment of cognitive reserve

The definition of CR was generated based on information from the baseline examination on six factors associated with cognitive function, including higher education level, higher occupational attainment, more social contacts, more frequent confiding in others, more regular participation in leisure activities, and less time watching television11. These factors are frequently-used proxies of CR that encompass mental and social stimulation across early life (education), midlife (occupation), and late life (social and leisure activities), with each component supported by extensive literature demonstrating independent contributions to cognitive reserve32,42,43.

Education level

Participants We defined education level based on the maximum years of schooling corresponding to these qualifications self-reported their educational level at baseline as follows: (1) no educational qualifications; (2) Certificate of Secondary Education or equivalent, O levels or equivalent; (3) National Vocational Qualification, Higher National Diploma, Higher National Certificate or equivalent; (4) A/AS levels or equivalent, other professional qualifications; or (5) college/university degree.

Television watching time

Participants were asked, “How many hours do you spend watching TV in a day?” The total hours of TV watching per day (hours/day) were summarized and categorized into four categories: (1) ≥ 4, (2) 3 to 3.9, (3) 2 to 2.9, and (4) < 2 hours/day.

Occupation attainment

Participants were asked to indicate their occupation and employment status according to the 2000 standard occupational classification system developed by the UK Office for National Statistics. Occupations were coded as 8-digit occupation codes. Participants were grouped according to the first four digits of their occupational code using the generally accepted socio-economic classification (SEC)44 as an ordinal variable for classification and inference, from SEC-1 to SEC-8, with lower values indicating occupations requiring more skills and training. The simplified SEC was rated as an ordinal variable.

Social contact

Participants were required to indicate how often they made or received friend/family visits, with responses categorized into four categories: (1) about once a month or less; (2) about once a week; (3) 2 to 4 times a week; and (4) almost every day.

Confiding in others

The answer to the question “How often do you confide in people close to you?” was defined as never, about once a month, 1-4 times a week, or almost every day.

Leisure activities

Participants were asked to indicate one or more activities they participated in weekly or more frequently (including sports clubs or gyms, bars or social clubs, religious groups, adult education classes, or other group activities), and responses were categorized as ≤1, 2, or 3–5 leisure activities per week.

Based on previous studies, the calculation of the CR index adopted a latent class analysis method45,46, which identifies unobservable clusters by classifying multiple observable variables into exclusive latent categories to determine the best-fitting model. This approach generated CR indicators from six reserve-related factors. Details on missing data for CR status populations are in Table S18. Likelihood ratio statistic G2, Akaike information criterion (AIC), and Bayesian information criterion (BIC) were used for model selection, with lower values indicating a more reasonable model. A three-latent class model was selected based on statistical properties and posterior classification uncertainty (Table S19-20, Fig. S4). Latent class 1 exhibited high levels of CR-related variables, with participants demonstrating favorable education, occupational achievement, television viewing habits, confiding frequency, and social contact. Latent class 2 showed moderately favorable levels across all CR-related factors. Latent class 3 primarily comprised individuals with secondary education or less, characterized by low occupational achievement, limited leisure activities, extended daily television viewing, and moderate confiding and social contact frequencies. These categories corresponded to high, medium, and low CR levels, respectively.

Assessment of physical frailty

In this study, the physical frailty phenotype was rigorously defined based on previously published literature and data available within the UK Biobank to assess the severity of frailty (Table S21). The physical frailty phenotype comprised five criteria: weight loss, exhaustion, weakness, low physical activity, and slow walking speed. Weakness was objectively assessed using a hydraulic hand dynamometer, while the other criteria were self-reported via questionnaires and coded as binary (yes/no). Frailty severity status was calculated by summing the number of criteria met, with scores ranging from 0 to 5. Consistent with prior literature, participants were classified into three mutually exclusive groups based on the number of criteria fulfilled: frail (meeting three or more criteria), pre-frail (meeting one or two criteria), and non-frail (meeting no criteria)47. The physical frailty phenotype was selected to facilitate the examination of the distinct interactions between frailty and cognitive reserve. Compared to broader frailty indices48, this phenotype minimizes potential confounding from cognitive and psychological factors, thereby providing a clearer understanding of the biological pathways linking frailty to neurodegenerative disease risk49–51.

Assessment of Brain MRI

MRI data were obtained using a Siemens Skyra 3 T scanner with a 32-channel RF head coil at the Stockport Recruitment Center. Imaging-derived phenotypes (IDPs) were obtained from functional MRI analyses performed with the Brain software library, and were made available to authorized researchers52. This study’s IDPs covered whole-brain and regional indices, including white matter hyperintensities (WMH), gray (GM) and white matter volumes (WM), total brain volume, and subcortical volume. Regional indices included area, volume, and mean thickness of 68 cortical regions from the Desikan-Killiany atlas53. WMH were log-transformed and other MRI indices were z-score normalized.

Covariates

Baseline covariates were collected via touchscreen at the UK Biobank Assessment Center, including demographic variables (age, sex, ethnicity), socioeconomic factors (Townsend Deprivation Index (TDI) and education, categorized as high for college/university degrees or professional qualifications, and low for others), lifestyle factors (smoking status as never/previous/current smokers; alcohol intake as never/previous/current drinkers), and vascular variability factors (BMI, metabolic syndrome (MetS), defined by the presence of three or more criteria: central obesity, diabetes, hypertension, low HDL, and high triglycerides). The study also considered comorbidities associated with frailty, including coronary heart disease, heart failure, stroke, hypertension, and diabetes, diagnosed using ICD-10 codes, self-reported and self-reported medication use.

Statistical analysis

One-way ANOVA analysis of variance and chi-square test were used to compare the differences according to CR levels. The Shapiro-Wilk test was used to assess the normality of continuous variables. As all continuous variables showed non-normal distribution, they were expressed as median with interquartile range (IQR). The baseline characteristics of categorical variables were expressed as numerical values (percentages). We employed multiple imputation with chained equations (MICE), which treats each variable with missing values as the dependent variable in a regression model, with the remaining variables serving as predictors. Ten imputed datasets were generated to ensure adequate statistical power and stability of estimates.

After verifying non-violation of the proportional hazard assumption via Schoenfeld residual test, multivariate Cox proportional hazard models with follow-up time as time scale assessed associations between cognitive reserve, frailty, and incident neurodegenerative disease risk. Results were presented as HRs with 95% CIs. Kaplan-Meier curves visualized cumulative neurodegenerative disease incidence by cognitive reserve status, using low CR as reference. Model 1 was adjusted for age at baseline and sex. Model 2 was adjusted for terms in model 1, Townsend deprivation index, ethnicity, smoking status, alcohol intake status and BMI. Model 2 was chosen as the primary model. To comprehensively evaluate cognitive reserve levels and frailty’s combined effect on neurodegenerative disease risk, we created nine distinct exposure groups by combining frailty status (non-frail/pre-frail/frail) with cognitive reserve levels (high/moderate/low), using participants with high cognitive reserve and no frailty as reference. Among frail participants, we calculated the population attributable fraction (PAF) to assess the proportion of neurodegenerative disease cases that could be prevented if low cognitive reserve were eliminated54. This highlights the significance of improving cognitive reserve levels as an intervention target for this high-risk group. To assess potential frailty status-cognitive reserve interaction, we calculated relative excess risk due to interaction (RERI) and attributable proportion (AP) based on Cox proportional hazard models. RERI quantifies additional neurodegenerative disease risk attributable to frailty status and cognitive reserve synergistic effect beyond individual effects, while AP represents the proportion of total disease risk in the doubly-exposed group attributable to this interaction. Finally, we calculated the E-value to assess the robustness of our findings while accounting for potential unmeasured confounding55,56.

To assess the robustness of our findings, we conducted multiple sensitivity analyses. First, we drew on expertise in the frailty and brain health field and existing literature to identify potential confounders of the causal relationship between frailty and brain health. Potential confounders were grouped together into nodes when they shared similar concepts, and a directed acyclic graph (DAG) was constructed using the open-access software DAGitty V.3.057,58 (Fig. S5). We further adjusted the model to account for additional comorbidities (cardiovascular disease, hypertension, diabetes, stroke, and cancer) and medication use (antihypertensive medications, lipid-lowering drugs, and aspirin) to mitigate potential confounding. Second, to minimize bias related to follow-up duration, we restricted analyses to participants aged 55 years and older at baseline. Third, we employed the Fine-Gray competing risk model, treating death as a competing event, to validate our primary findings. Fourth, to address potential reverse causality, we excluded participants who developed neurodegenerative diseases within the first three years of follow-up. Additionally, we conducted comprehensive subgroup analyses to examine whether the joint effects of frailty and cognitive reserve levels on neurodegenerative disease risk differed by baseline characteristics, including age, sex, ethnicity, and BMI ( < 30/ ≥ 30 kg/m²). Specifically, we performed age-stratified analyses by comparing participants aged <65 years with those aged ≥65 years at baseline to assess how these age groups may influence the protective effects of cognitive reserve and frailty on neurodegenerative disease risk. For each subgroup, we tested for multiplicative interactions using the Cox proportional hazards model and calculated corresponding interaction p-values to assess statistical significance.

Linear mixed-effects models were used to assess associations between cognitive reserve levels and both micro- and macro-structural brain features, with comprehensive adjustment for confounding variables. Prior to analysis, predictor multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factors. Cognitive reserve was specified as fixed effect, while UK Biobank assessment center was incorporated as random effect to account for potential clustering. To address multiple comparisons, all p-values were adjusted using Benjamini-Hochberg procedure to control false discovery rate (FDR).

Mediation analyses were conducted to explore the direct and indirect pathways linking cognitive reserve levels, frailty, and neurodegenerative diseases. In recognizing the complexity of the interrelationships among CR levels and frailty, we implemented a bidirectional mediation analysis59,60: in Model A, CR levels was designated as the predictor (X), frailty as the mediator (M), and neurodegenerative diseases as the outcome (Y); conversely, in Model B, frailty served as the predictor, with CR levels as the mediator. The first model assesses the pathway where CR levels influences frailty, which, in turn, impacts neurodegenerative disease risk; the second model reverses this directionality, examining how frailty may subsequently affect CR levels and its relationship to disease risk. By contrasting these models, we aimed to elucidate the symmetry in interactions between these constructs, thereby acknowledging the potential for both CR levels and frailty to influence neurodegenerative disease risk independently. Logistic regression was used to analyze the relationship between CR levels and frailty, while survival regression models assessed the joint influence of CR levels and frailty on neurodegenerative disease risk. The significance of mediation effects was evaluated through bootstrapping with 1000 iterations, yielding robust confidence intervals for indirect effect estimates and ensuring a reliable understanding of the pathways through which CR levels and frailty interact to influence neurodegenerative disease development. Additionally, we performed sensitivity analyses using the “CMAverse” package, which implements alternative regression-based and weighting-based causal mediation approaches, further validating our findings across different analytical frameworks61.

All p values were reported as two-sided tests with significance defined as P < 0.05. R software version 4.1.0 was used for statistical analyses.
