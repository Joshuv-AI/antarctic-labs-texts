Optimal Preservation Method for Long-term Preservation in ddH2O and Starvation Adaptive Mechanisms of Flavobacterium columnare Revealed by Multi-omic Analysis
Authors: Shengnan Gao, Liang Zhong, Yuxuan Zhang, Ruoxi Zhu, Yanwen Shao, Runsheng Li, Yongtao Zhu, Wenlong Cai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAquatic bacterial pathogens cause recurrent diseases in aquaculture. However, their survival dynamics and adaptive mechanisms in nutrient-limited aquatic environments remain poorly understood. In this study, we used Flavobacterium columnare, the causative agent of columnare disease, as an example to study multi-level (morphological, genomic, transcriptomic, epigenomic) adaptive changes during prolonged starvation. Additionally, we proposed an alternative long-term preservation strategy for this pathogen. We stored F. columnare across four preservation water conditions and three temperatures for 12 months, and found that double-distilled water (ddH₂O) (at 22℃ and 28℃) and pond water (at 22℃) effectively maintained bacterial viability. Starvation induced a rhizoid-to-smooth colony morphological shift, with no significant genomic mutations detected. Epigenetically, the CAYNNNNNRTG motif exhibited significantly reduced DNA methylation in smooth morphotypes relative to rhizoid ones. Transcriptomic analysis identified 851 differentially expressed genes (DEGs; 418 upregulated, 433 downregulated) in smooth F. columnare, with upregulated DEGs enriched in cell wall/membrane biogenesis and replication/recombination/repair, and downregulated DEGs associated with translation/ribosomal biogenesis and energy production. Notably, denitrification-related genes (e.g., narT, CuNir, norD) and transposase genes (ISL3, IS1182 families) were significantly upregulated, whereas ribosomal protein and type VI secretion system genes were downregulated. Integrative methylome-transcriptome analysis revealed C⁶ᵐAYNNNNNRTG hypomethylation as a remarkable epigenetic signature associated with starvation adaptation in F. columnare, targeting gene regulatory regions to remodel expression networks. Our findings establish a practical F. columnare preservation method and elucidate key starvation-adaptive mechanisms, informing targeted interventions to mitigate this pathogen’s nutrient-deprivation tolerance in natural aquatic systems.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00248-026-02756-9.Keywords: Flavobacterium columnare, Preservation, Starvation, Methylation, Transcriptome, Novel transportation method

Introduction

Flavobacterium columnare is a pathogenic bacterial species that poses a significant threat to the aquaculture industry by causing columnaris disease in various fish species [1]. Preserving the viability and virulence of this bacterium during long-term storage is essential for conducting research, developing diagnostic tools, and formulating effective control strategies against columnaris disease [2]. Typical long-term storage strategies encompass cryopreservation [3] and the utilization of specialized preservation media including nutrient agar slants, broth cultures, and specialized formulations such as Stuart’s or Amies transport media [4, 5]. However, it is evident that they exhibit limitations in terms of stringent storage temperature. In addition, we found traditional transport methods of F. columnare, such as cold-chain shipping or preservation in semi-liquid media, often face challenges in sustaining bacterial viability over extended periods, leading to reduced infectivity and unreliable results. Therefore, the exploration of alternative strategies for the extended preservation and transportation of F. columnare is necessary.

In natural and aquaculture aquatic environments, F. columnare frequently encounters nutrient limitation, a key environmental stressor that elicits adaptive cellular responses in bacteria. Prior research into F. columnare’s starvation responses has focused primarily on cellular-level changes, revealing that nutrient limitation drives a gradual, consistent shift in the bacterium’s population morphology [6]. These changes were proved by both morphological and ultrastructural observations [7]. Similar colony morphotype alterations have also been documented in F. columnare exposed to nutrient scarcity and lytic phages [8, 9]. It was proposed that F. columnare may prioritize essential functions, optimize resource utilization, and enhance stress tolerance during periods of nutrient limitation by reprogramming their cellular processes [10]. Despite these insights, the multi-level molecular mechanisms underlying F. columnare’s adaptive survival under prolonged nutrient limitation remain largely uncharacterized.

In this study, we evaluated the survival of F. columnare in four different media (i.e., mineral solution, pond water, double distilled water [ddH2O], and phosphate-buffered saline [PBS]) and three temperatures (i.e., 22 ℃, 28 ℃ and 4 ℃) and found that F. columnare can survive in ddH2O for up to one year without genetic alteration concerns, representing a simple and effective preservation strategy with potential for F. columnare short-term transportation or preservation. We further investigated the morphological, genomic, transcriptomic, and epigenomic mechanisms underpinning F. columnare’s adaptive survival in this nutrient-limited condition. These findings advance our understanding of F. columnare’s environmental survival strategies and provide a foundational basis for developing targeted interventions to mitigate columnaris disease transmission in natural and aquaculture aquatic systems.

Materials and Methods

F. columnare Culture and Storage Conditions

The F. columnare strain, designated FCMF, was originally isolated from diseased mandarin fish (Siniperca chuatsi) in Sichuan province, China in 2022 [11]. The F. columnare cells were grown under a constant agitation of 125 rpm at 28 °C for 24 h to reach stationary phase.

Long-term storage was implemented by incubating F. columnare cultures under twelve storage conditions for a duration of 52 weeks. Specifically, ten mL bacterial suspension at stationary phase (OD600=0.60) was harvested and centrifuged at 5000 rpm for 10 min. The supernatant was removed, and the pellets were resuspended in 10 mL of corresponding sterile solution under each temperature condition. These conditions included four types of media (i.e., mineral solution, pond water, ddH2O, and PBS ) under three temperatures (i.e., 22 ℃, 28 ℃, and 4 ℃). Pond water was collected from a freshwater ornamental fish pond on the City University of Hong Kong campus. To eliminate indigenous microorganisms while preserving soluble components, the pond water was sterilized by autoclaving (121 °C, 20 min) prior to use. To ensure reproducibility, the concentrations of key ions (including Ca²⁺, Mg²⁺, K⁺ and Fe⁺) in all four preservation media were measured. Complete compositional analysis of mineral solution, pond water, ddH₂O, and PBS was provided in Supplementary Material 2, Table S1. Each treatment group was set in triplicate. During long-term survival experiments, samples from each storage condition were collected at different time points (i.e., 0, 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 52 weeks post-inoculation), and the bacterial viability was quantified by the standard plate-counting method. In total, 24 samples were analyzed per storage condition (3 replicates × 8 time points).

Bacterial Virulence Test

After 52-week storage, the cultures preserved in ddH2O at 22 ℃ were utilized as representative samples to assess the virulence. Three isolates including original F. columnare stock (OF, parental strain), F. columnare revived directly from ddH2O (WF, starved) and F. columnare subcultured once from WF in MS broth (SF, recovered) were tested for their virulence in freshwater medaka adults (Oryzias melastigma). Fifteen fish (weight mean ± SD: 0.43 ± 0.27 g) were exposed to 24 h bacterial culture in 1 L aquariums to reach a final concentration of 2.10 to 4.90 × 106 CFU/mL. As a negative control treatment, 15 fish were exposed to sterile ddH2O without bacterial inoculum in 1 L aquariums. Each group was done in triplicate. Fish were monitored daily for signs of disease and mortality for 2 weeks. Cumulative mortality of fish was used as an indicator of bacterial virulence.

The present experiment was carried out in strict accordance with the Hong Kong Special Administrative Region (HKSAR) guidelines for research on animals, with relevant animal licenses [Ref No.: (24 - 53) in DH/HT&A/8/2/5 Pt.14] and animal ethics approval (City University Animal Ethics Approval, A-0402).

Minimum Inhibitory Concentration Experiment for Susceptibility Test

Minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs) of oxytetracycline, florfenicol, and enrofloxacin against F. columnare isolates were determined using broth microdilution. Bacterial suspensions (~ 10⁷ CFU/mL) were added to 96-well plates containing two-fold serial dilutions of antimicrobials in MS broth. After incubation at 28 °C for 48 h, MICs were recorded as the lowest concentrations inhibiting visible growth, with DMSO controls and triplicate samples included.

Colony and Cellular Morphological Changes

Morphological changes were monitored periodically using stereomicroscopy and confocal microscopy, aligned with viability assessment time points. At each interval, colony morphology was first documented macroscopically and then examined in detail using a Nikon SMZ25 stereomicroscope (Tokyo, Japan). For cellular morphology, 20 µL of culture was placed on a glass slide and observed under a Nikon Eclipse Ti2 microscope (Tokyo, Japan). Bacterial cell length was measured from 200 randomly selected cells per time point.

DNA and RNA Extraction and Sequencing

Single colonies of the rhizoid morphotype (from pre-storage cultures) and smooth morphotype (from 52-week storage samples) were selected and grown individually in MS broth at 28 °C for 24 h, respectively. Genomic DNA and total RNA samples were extracted from the fresh bacteria culture by using TaKaRa MiniBEST Universal Genomic DNA Extraction Kit Ver.5.0 (Takara Shuzo Co., Ltd., Shiga, Japan) and RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany), respectively. For each morphotype, three biological replicates (independent colonies selected from separate plates, cultured individually) were processed for RNA-seq. Nanopore genomic DNA libraries were constructed from 1 µg of high-quality genomic DNA by utilizing the library preparation kit of Ligation sequencing gDNA – Native Barcoding Kit 24 V14 (SQK-NBD-114.24, ONT, Oxford, United Kingdom). Long-read sequencing was performed on the MinION (ONT) platform using R.10.4.1 Flow Cell (FLO-MIN114, ONT). In addition, RNA samples with integrity > 7.5 were used for further sequencing on the MGISEQ-2000 platform (MGI Tech Co., Ltd., Shenzhen, China) with pair-end read sizes of 100 bp. The sequencing statistics were summarised in Supplementary Material 2, Table S2.

Variant Calling

The resulted pod5 files from the Nanopore sequencing were base-called with Dorado v0.4.1 (https://github.com/nanoporetech/dorado) with the “dna_r10.4.1_e8.2_400bps_sup@v5.0.0” model. The basecalled FASTQ reads were aligned to our in-house assembled F. columnare reference genome (BioProject Accession No. PRJNA1247036) using Minimap2 aligner [12]. After alignment, the generated SAM files were converted to BAM format and then sorted and indexed using Samtools [13]. The prepared reads from Rhizoid and Smooth group were mapped to the F. columnare genome assembled by our lab previously [14], followed by Bcftools-driven “single nucleotide polymorphism (SNPs)” and “insertion and deletion (InDels)” identification, with ploidy set to 1 [13]. Copy number variants (CNVs) were quantified by normalizing reads coverage at genomic loci in whole-genome sequencing data using ggplot2 v3.4.0 package [15] in R. Structural variants (SVs) were also identified by Sniffles2 software (https://github.com/fritzsedlazeck/Sniffles). To enhance the reliability of variant calls, only those with quality scores > 30 were retained.

The nanopore sequencing datasets were also used to determine genome-wide methylation profiles. High-confidence methylated sites and motifs were identified following our previous study [14]. High-confidence modifications were filtered by requiring [Whole Genome Sequencing (WGS) methylation level – Whole Genome Amplification (WGA) methylation level] > 25%. Enriched motifs were detected with STREME (p < 0.05), and motif-level methylation differences between the rhizoid and smooth groups were assessed via Wilcoxon Signed-Rank Test.

Differentially methylated sites were identified using Modkit’s ‘dmr’ function, with significance thresholds set at MAP-based p < 0.05 and methylation change > 10%.

Regulatory regions were defined as: (i) 1,000 nt upstream of the first gene in an operon, and (ii) 200–1,000 nt intergenic sequences for inter-gene distances between two nearby operons that are < 1,000 nt [16]. Downstream gene prediction was subsequently performed via the Operon Mapper web service (https://biocomputo.ibt.unam.mx/operon_mapper/). Following the identification of CDS and regulatory regions, the methylated sites at single-base resolution were mapped to these genomic features using Bedtools [17]. Subsequently, we identified the top 10 methylation-associated genes for 6 mA, 5mC, and 4mC modifications based on their p-value rankings and visualized them by ggplot2 in R.

Transcriptomic Analysis

The raw reads were trimmed of adaptor sequences using SOAPnuke software [18] and low-quality reads were filtered out using Trimmomatic v0.36 (https://github.com/usadellab/Trimmomatic). The processed reads were mapped to the F. columnare genome (BioProject Accession No. PRJNA1247036) using Bowtie2 v2.5.2 (https://github.com/BenLangmead/bowtie2). The alignment statistics were obtained using featureCounts [19]. Comparisons between two morphotypes were performed using the DESeq2 package v1.36.0 [20]. Genes with an FDR-adjusted p-value < 0.05 and log2 |fold-change| > 1 were identified as differentially expressed. Functional annotation and gene ontology were also conducted using BLAST2GO PRO software [21]. The visualization and downstream analysis were performed by OmicShare (https://www.genedenovo.com/).

Combined Analysis of DNA Methylome and Transcriptome

To investigate potential correlations between the DNA methylome and transcriptome, Pearson correlation analyses were performed to compare the significantly changed methylation levels in CDS and regulatory regions, with the differentially expressed corresponding gene levels. Differential methylation levels at single-base resolution for specific motif were also analysed using the ‘dmr’ function of Modkit, followed by Pearson correlation analysis with corresponding transcriptome data. The downstream visualization was performed by ggplot2 in R software.

Statistical Analyses

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 10.1.2 software (Boston, Massachusetts USA, https://www.graphpad.com/). Significant differences in cumulative mortality curve were determined by log-rank (Mantel - Cox) test. For the bacterial length assay and qPCR validation, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Tukey’s post hoc test was used to analyse differences among groups. A p-value < 0.05 was considered significant.

Results

Changes in the Population of F. columnare in Different Conditions During 52-Week Storage

Following 52 weeks of storage, F. columnare exhibited the highest survival rate in ddH₂O at 22 °C (Fig. 1). Across all tested media, survival duration was generally longer at 22 °C relative to other temperatures, with PBS as the only exception. Notably, no viable cells were recovered from any medium following 4 weeks of storage at 4 °C, except in PBS (Fig. 1d). Among the media evaluated, long‑term survival was comparatively high in pond water (1.48 × 10³ CFU/mL) and ddH₂O (1.59 × 10³ CFU/mL) at the 52-week time point (Fig. 1b, c). Conversely, no cells were recovered from mineral solution after 52 weeks of storage (Fig. 1a), and complete mortality of F. columnare occurred within 2 weeks in PBS (Fig. 1d).

Cellular and Colony Morphological Change of F. columnare During 52-Week Storage in ddH2O at 22 °C

During the storage in ddH2O at 22 °C, we found the length of individual bacteria progressively decreased until they became completely coccoid in shape during 52 weeks (Fig. 2a). The distribution of bacterial cell lengths gradually shifted towards a predominance of cells measuring under 5 μm, departing from the initial prevalence of lengths exceeding 5 μm (Fig. 2b). These results indicated the length of F. columnare was significantly reduced over the 52-week storage period. Furthermore, F. columnare cultures stored in ddH2O began to exhibit two distinct colony morphotypes on MS agar starting at week 4, which we described as rhizoid-morphotype and smooth-morphotype, respectively (Fig. 2c, d). The proportion of the smooth-morphotype colonies increased gradually over time, and by approximately week 32, the smooth morphology became the sole colony morphology observed. Colony morphology changes in other preservation media were shown in Supplementary Material 2, Fig. S1.

Virulence and Antimicrobial Susceptibility Changes Following 52-Week Storage in ddH2O at 22 °C

Based on the above findings, ddH₂O was identified as the optimal media for the long-term preservation of F. columnare. To assess changes in virulence, challenge experiments were conducted on freshwater medaka fish using three F. columnare isolates stored in ddH₂O for 52 weeks. Cumulative mortality (CM) varied across isolates, ranging from 33% to 74%. The SF isolate showed virulence comparable to the OF strain, while the WF isolate exhibited significantly attenuated virulence. This observation suggests that starved bacteria require subculturing to fully restore pathogenicity (Fig. 3). To investigate the relationship between colony morphology and virulence recovery, we examined the colony morphology of WF and SF isolates on MS agar. Both WF and SF consistently exhibited the smooth morphotype (Supplementary Material 2, Fig. S2), indicating that the restoration of full virulence in SF occurred independently of colony morphology reversion. The smooth morphotype represented a starvation-adaptive state that retained full virulence capacity, which can be activated by nutrient supplement. In addition, antimicrobial susceptibility testing revealed no significant differences in the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) values of oxytetracycline, florfenicol, and enrofloxacin among the three isolates (Supplementary Material 2, Table S3).

Few Genetic Variations Occurred in Smooth-Morphotype F. columnare Following Long-Term Nutrient Limitation

Given the gradual predominance of the smooth morphotype over the 52-week storage period, we hypothesized that the smooth-shape F. columnare was the adaptive morphotype to nutrient starvation. To explore the underlying adaptive mechanism, we compared genetic variations between the smooth and rhizoid morphotypes.

Genomic reads coverage of the two morphotypes exhibited a strong positive correlation (Pearson’s r = 0.983, p < 0.0001) across 500-bp bins (Supplementary Material 2, Fig. S3), confirming minimal copy number variation following long-term nutrient limitation (Fig. 4a). In addition, a total of 15 SNPs and 11 InDels were identified in smooth morphology, implying that such a limited number of variants could be possibly attributed to random sequencing errors (Fig. 4b; Supplementary Material 2, Table S4). No structural variants were identified. The small number of variants suggested that the genome of F. columnare remained largely conserved during nutrient starvation.

DNA Methylation Level of CAYNNNNNRTG Motif was Significantly Altered in Smooth-Morphotype F. columnare Following Long-Term Starvation

Methylated motif analysis identified eight significantly enriched motifs: CAT⁶ᵐATG, GAA⁶ᵐANNNNNNNNTGG, GA⁶ᵐATTC, GC⁶ᵐAGA, ATCG⁶ᵐAT, C⁶ᵐAYNNNNNRTG, C⁵ᵐCNGG, and CGTA⁴ᵐCG (Fig. 5a). Among these motifs, only C⁶ᵐAYNNNNNRTG motif exhibited a significant reduction in methylation under starvation conditions, indicating its sensitivity to nutrient stress. At single-base resolution, 168, 37, and 8 differentially methylated sites were detected for 6 mA, 5mC, and 4mC, respectively (Fig. 5b).

Genes associated with the top 10 methylated sites (ranked by p‑value) were predominantly involved in metabolic processes, DNA repair, and transposition (Fig. 5c). Notably, tRNA 5‑hydroxyuridine methyltransferase (TrmR) - a key transmethylase in translational regulation and bacterial stress response - exhibited significantly decreased methylation levels, suggesting a potential role of DNA methylation in modulating F. columnare adaptation to starvation.

Identification and Functional Enrichment of Differentially Expressed Genes Between two Morphotypes by Transcriptome Analysis

To further unravel the molecular mechanisms underlying the adaptive shift to the smooth morphotype, we performed transcriptomic analysis to compare gene expression profiles between the smooth and rhizoid morphotypes of F. columnare. Principal component analysis (PCA) revealed distinct clustering of the two morphotypes based on their gene expression profiles, confirming significant differences in transcriptional activity (Fig. 6a). A total of 851 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified in the smooth morphotype relative to the rhizoid morphotype, with 418 up-regulated and 433 down-regulated genes (Fig. 6b; Supplementary Material 1).

To elucidate the potential functions of these DEGs, functional enrichment analysis was performed using the Cluster of Orthologous Groups of proteins (COG) database. COG classification showed that up-regulated DEGs were mainly enriched in “cell wall/membrane/envelope biogenesis” and “replication, recombination and repair,” while down-regulated DEGs were predominantly associated with “translation, ribosomal structure and biogenesis” and “energy production and conversion” (Fig. 6c). These results indicate that the smooth morphotype undergoes transcriptional reprogramming to prioritize structural remodeling and DNA maintenance under nutrient limitation, while downregulating energy-consuming processes related to translation and metabolism.

Integrated Analysis of the DNA Methylome and Transcriptome

Integrated analysis of methylome-transcriptome relationships revealed distinct regulatory patterns between the two F. columnare morphotypes. Single-base resolution comparison showed no significant correlation between DEGs and methylation changes in either coding regions (Supplementary Material 2, Fig. S5a) or their regulatory elements (Supplementary Material 2, Fig. S5b).

Notably, integrated methylome-transcriptome analysis focusing on the C⁶ᵐAYNNNNNRTG motif revealed motif-specific epigenetic regulatory patterns in F. columnare. In the smooth morphotype, the mRNA expression of the C⁶ᵐAYNNNNNRTG-specific methyltransferase was significant down-regulated (***p < 0.001; Fig. 8a). Single-base resolution analysis further showed that the majority of C⁶ᵐAYNNNNNRTG motif sites exhibited decreased methylation level (Fig. 8b). No significant correlation was detected between mRNA expression and methylation changes in the coding regions of C⁶ᵐAYNNNNNRTG-related genes (Fig. 8c). However, a strong negative correlation (r = -0.94, p < 0.05) was observed between gene expression and methylation changes in the regulatory regions of these genes (Fig. 8d). These results indicated that hypomethylation of the C⁶ᵐAYNNNNNRTG motif served as a remarkable epigenetic signature associated with nutrient stress adaptation in F. columnare, preferentially targeting the regulatory regions of specific genes to remodel gene expression networks.

Discussion

Previous studies have reported that the viability of bacterial species, including R. solanacearum, A. tumefaciens, P. fluorescens, Salmonella spp. and E. coli O157:H7, can be maintained for extended period in ddH2O [2, 22]. The results of the present study extended this observation and demonstrated that ddH2O was also a suitable medium for long-term preservation of F. columnare under ambient temperature (22–28℃). Although F. columnare exhibited morphological alterations and virulence attenuation with prolonged storage, our findings indicated that these phenotypic changes were reversible through subculture. Similar results were reported in other bacterial species that motility in vibrio sp. strain S14 and growth capacity in Enterococcus faecalis were restored following nutrient supplementation after periods of nutrient limitation [23, 24]. This ability to rapidly recover from starvation-induced states confers a significant ecological advantage to F. columnare, enabling it to quickly reestablish pathogenicity and infect hosts once nutrients become available in natural aquatic environments.

In our study, F. columnare stored in ddH2O exhibited better survival rates than the seemingly “nutrient-rich” substrates, such as mineral solution and PBS. The differential survival of F. columnare in mineral solution and PBS might be explained by two distinct mechanisms: substrate-accelerated death and energy-dependent osmoregulation failure, respectively. In mineral solution, F. columnare exhibited survival comparable to ddH₂O during the first 16 weeks, but no viable cells were recovered at 52 weeks. This delayed mortality pattern was possibly due to mechanisms similar to the classical “substrate-accelerated death” phenomenon described by Postgate and Hunter [25]. Mineral solution contained metabolically accessible ions (Mg²⁺, Ca²⁺, Fe²⁺) but lacked organic carbon sources. These ions triggered low-level activation of transport systems and metal-dependent enzymes, allowing short-term survival but gradually depleting residual energy reserved without replenishment, ultimately leading to ATP exhaustion and death by 52 weeks. In PBS, rapid death (complete mortality within 2 weeks) was a possible result from collapse of ion homeostasis during starvation. Under nutrient-replete conditions, Flavobacterium species tolerate NaCl up to 4% [26, 27] because ATP fuels Na⁺/H⁺ antiporters and compatible solute synthesis. However, during starvation in PBS, cells lack energy for these continued processes. Schink et al. (2024) demonstrated that carbon starvation led to ion homeostasis collapse, triggering osmotic swelling and membrane permeabilization. Crucially, replacing inorganic salts with a non-permeating osmoprotectant reduced death rates fourfold by eliminating the ATP burden of ion homeostasis [28]. This possibly explained why ddH₂O containing no ions provided optimal preservation. It should be noted that the pond water media, while compositionally characterized, represented an environmental sample with inherent variability that cannot be fully standardized. Thus, caution should be taken for the generalizability of the result. Overall, these results indicated that storage of F. columnare cells in ddH2O at regular ambient temperature maintains a high survival rate. This method offerred a simple, cost-effective and feasible strategy for long-term preservation of F. columnare, with practical implications for laboratory maintenance and resource-sharing related short-term transportation on this pathogenic bacterium.

Long-term preservation in ddH₂O subjects F. columnare to a starvation state, a stressor that typically induces differentiation into resistant forms to sustain viability in nutrient-depleted conditions [29]. In the present study, F. columnare cells progressively shortened over time and eventually adopted a coccoid shape. This aligned with prior observations of F. columnare coiling under stress [7], whereas in other bacteria species the cell morphology could change and generally become shorter, even coccoid [30, 31]. Morphological changes at the cellular level frequently alter colony phenotypes [6]. Consistent with this, our results showed that F. columnare colonies on MS agar differentiated into two morphotypes, with smooth colonies first appearing at week 4. Their absence in the initial population suggested that phenotypic switching may be involved in their emergence, a phenomenon supported by Arias et al. [7] who documented starvation-induced morphological changes in individual F. columnare cells. The smooth morphotype gradually increased in frequency and became the sole phenotype by week 32, indicating it as the adaptive phenotypic state of F. columnare in response to starvation. Once established, the gradual dominance of smooth morphotypes might reflect a survival advantage under continued starvation.

To elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying this starvation adaptation, we conducted a comprehensive multi-omics analysis comparing smooth and rhizoid F. columnare across three levels: genomic variation, DNA methylation (epigenetics), and transcriptome profiling. Genetic variations encompass a broad spectrum of DNA structural alterations, including single nucleotide variants (SNVs/SNPs), small insertions/deletions (InDels; ≤ 50 bp), and structural variations (SVs; > 50 bp, e.g., inversions, translocations, and copy number variations [CNVs]) [32–34]. Notably, minimal genetic variation was detected in the starvation-induced smooth morphotype. This was consistent with expectations, as bacterial mutation rates are inherently low, and natural selection favors enhanced replication fidelity to maintain genome stability [33, 35].

DNA methylation is a key bacterial epigenetic mechanism regulating virulence, biofilm formation, and host colonization [36]. In our study, only the C⁶ᵐAYNNNNNRTG motif exhibited significantly reduced methylation following long-term storage, revealing a motif-specific hypomethylation response to starvation. Single-base resolution analysis showed that differentially methylated genes were enriched in metabolic processes, DNA repair, and transposition pathways - consistent with prior studies that nutrient stress triggered epigenetic reprogramming to optimize energy utilization and maintain genome stability [37, 38]. A notable observation was the decreased methylation of trmR, which encodes tRNA 5-hydroxyuridine methyltransferase, an enzyme critical for translational fidelity and stress response [39]. This hypomethylation likely modulated protein synthesis under starvation, potentially by altering tRNA modification patterns to prioritize the expression of stress-responsive genes [40]. Similar methylation-dependent adaptive responses have been observed in eukaryotes under environmental pressure [41, 42]. These findings supported the emerging paradigm that DNA methylation acts as a dynamic environmental sensor, with motif- and gene-specific changes orchestrating adaptive strategies.

Transcriptomic analysis identified 851 DEGs in the smooth morphotype compared with rhizoid morphotype. COG functional enrichment analysis revealed that most of the up-regulated DEGs were associated with function classification of “cell wall/membrane/envelope biogenesis” and “replication, recombination and repair”, indicating a response directed at enhancing structural integrity and genetic maintenance under stress [43, 44]. Conversely, down-regulated DEGs were enriched in “translation, ribosomal structure and biogenesis” and “energy production and conversion,” reflecting a suppression of energy-intensive processes. This downregulation was a conserved stress response, conserving cellular resources by redirecting energy toward essential survival functions [45, 46]. Notably, DEGs involved in denitrification and transposase activity were significantly up-regulated in the smooth morphotype. Denitrification enables anaerobic respiration using nitrate/nitrite as alternative electron acceptors, a metabolic strategy that enhances survival in nutrient limitation environments [47]. F. columnare genome harbors the denitrification pathways [48]. F. columnare’s upregulation of denitrification genes suggested a shift to anaerobic metabolism - a strategy observed in other denitrifying pathogens under stress [49, 50]. Upregulation of transposases further indicated adaptive genomic plasticity, consistent with reports that stressors such as nutrient availability [51], temperature fluctuations [52], and UV exposure [53] induce transposition to promote survival. In contrast, DEGs encoding ribosomal proteins, type VI secretion system (T6SS) components, and most transcriptional regulators were downregulated in the smooth morphotype. Ribosomal protein synthesis is energetically costly, so its suppression under starvation is a conserved resource-saving mechanism [54]. Consistent with this, nitrogen or carbon limitation has been shown to reduce the expression of ribosomal genes and translation-related factors, reflecting a metabolic shift from growth to maintenance [55, 56]. Similarly, down-regulation of transcriptional regulators suggests repression of non-essential pathways to save energy. Although multiple secretion systems exist in Flavobacterium [48], T6SS was commonly identified in Bacteroidota [57] and mediates interbacterial competition [58] and metal acquisition [59]. Its down-regulation here aligns with reports that T6SS activity is often nutrient-dependent, as seen in Vibrio parahaemolyticus where it is induced in rich media conditions [60]. These results collectively indicated that F. columnare abandons redundant functions (e.g., interbacterial competition) to reduce energy expenditure and prioritize survival during starvation.

Integrated methylome-transcriptome analysis revealed that starvation in F. columnare triggered a targeted epigenetic response, characterized by selective hypomethylation of regulatory regions. The C⁶ᵐAYNNNNNRTG motif functioned as a key regulatory switch, exhibiting marked hypomethylation accompanied by down-regulation of its corresponding methyltransferase - a pattern analogous to GATC motif regulation during oxidative stress in Salmonella [16]. A robust inverse correlation (r = -0.94) between regulatory region methylation and gene expression suggested this CAYNNNNNRTG motif as a possible starvation-responsive epigenetic trigger. Our model proposed: (i) immediate suppression of motif-specific methyltransferase expression, (ii) subsequent focal demethylation of regulatory elements, and (iii) selective reprogramming of downstream gene expression-collectively enhancing F. columnare’s starvation stress resilience.

These findings raised important questions about the evolutionary conservation of motif-specific responses. C⁶ᵐAYNNNNNRTG motif appears uniquely tuned for nutrient limitation in F. columnare. Future studies should explore whether this motif’s regulatory role extends to other Bacteroidota, and whether its manipulation could enhance bacterial fitness in aquaculture settings.

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. First, our transcriptomic and epigenomic analyses compared the starvation-adapted smooth morphotype at week 52 with the original rhizoid morphotype. This single-endpoint design captures the stabilized adaptive state, but the temporal dynamics of molecular reprogramming during the transition period warrant further investigation (weeks 4–32). In addition, the medaka model may not fully reflect virulence dynamics in other economically important fish due to differences in host virulence specificity.

Conclusion

This study validated a novel long-term preservation and transportation strategy for F. columnare using ddH₂O at 22 °C. Multi-omics analysis was used to reveal the adaptive response of F. columnare to starvation during the prolonged preservation. The genome remains largely stable, while targeted epigenetic reprogramming occurred, marked by significant demethylation of the CAYNNNNNRTG motif in regulatory regions. Transcriptomic analysis further revealed a dual response: up-regulation of cell-wall biogenesis, denitrification, and transposase activity to enhance stress resilience and metabolic flexibility, coupled with down-regulation of ribosomal synthesis, T6SS, and energy-intensive processes to conserve resources. Integrated methylome-transcriptome analysis indicated that gene expression was partly regulated through motif-specific hypomethylation, with C⁶ᵐAYNNNNNRTG acting as a key epigenetic switch. These findings not only provide a practical, cost-effective preservation protocol for F. columnare but also advance our understanding of how bacterial pathogens epigenetically and transcriptionally rewire their physiology to survive nutrient stress.
