The Evaluation of Clinical and Dermatoscopic Features of Scalp Nevi in Adults
Authors: Hatice Gamze Demirdağ, Elif Demirci Saadet
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionThe dermatoscopic appearance of scalp tumors, especially melanocytic scalp lesions, differs from that on other body areas. Few studies have revealed the dermatoscopic characteristics of scalp nevi.ObjectivesThis study aimed to establish the clinical and dermatoscopic features of scalp nevi in adults and to compare dermatoscopic patterns according to the demographic and clinical features.MethodsThis prospective study included 129 adult patients with 253 nevi. Demographic data of patients, clinical characteristics, and dermatoscopic features were recorded and compared.ResultsThe study enrolled 86 females (67%) and 43 males (33%) with a median age of 33.6 ± 11.3 years. The eclipse/reverse eclipse patterns were observed more frequently, while the papillomatous pattern was notably less common among younger patients than in older patients (P<0.001). The mean diameter of nevi was smaller in reticular pattern and larger in eclipse/reverse eclipse patterns (P<0.001). Vascular structures were present in 35.2% (N=89) of the nevi. While vessels were not observed in reticular pattern, vessels were present in 70% of the papillomatous pattern (P<0.001). Perifollicular hypopigmentation was observed in 53.8% (N=136) of the scalp nevi, and it was seen in almost all eclipse/reverse eclipse and reticular patterns (P<0.001).ConclusionsDermatoscopic evaluation of scalp nevi provides more detailed information, helps to make a correct diagnosis, and facilitates patient management compared to clinical examination alone. Increasing awareness of the clinical characteristics and dermatoscopic features of scalp nevi can potentially decrease unnecessary surgical excisions.

Introduction

The scalp expresses a unique anatomical part with a rich vascular and lymphatic supply and a higher concentration of pilosebaceous follicles [1]. It has been demonstrated that melanocytic tumors on the scalp have distinct characteristics compared to those found on the trunk and extremities. For instance, melanoma on the scalp tends to have a poorer prognosis than thickness-matched melanoma located on the trunk.

Scalp nevi were reported to have a 13.7% prevalence in a prior study [2] and a 33.6% prevalence in a study of 795 subjects, and the 21–30 age group had the highest incidence of scalp nevi with 41.7% [3]. The patients cannot be aware of the presence of a nevus on the scalp due to its localization. In a research study, 25.2% of the volunteers interviewed reported being aware of having a scalp nevus [4]. When a scalp nevus is noticed, it may be a source of anxiety, and unnecessary excisions can be done due to their clinical appearance and uncertain anamnesis. Consequently, accurately diagnosing scalp nevi requires a comprehensive evaluation and understanding of related dermatoscopic, clinical, histopathological, and epidemiological characteristics to differentiate melanocytic nevi from melanoma [2].

Dermatoscopy is a noninvasive imaging method that improves the accuracy of diagnosing melanoma and other melanocytic tumors. However, the detailed dermatoscopic criteria for melanocytic tumors on the scalp, specifically melanocytic nevi, have not been extensively documented [5]. The structure of scalp nevi changes depending on the patient’s age, and the anatomic location affects the dermatoscopic features of lesions [1]. Moreover, there have been only a limited number of studies assessing the variations in dermatoscopic patterns of scalp nevi in both adult and pediatric populations, and most studies of scalp nevi have been performed in children [2,3,5–7]. Unfortunately, the available data are inadequate for future meta-analyses.

Objectives

This study aimed to establish the clinical and dermatoscopic features of nevi located on the scalp in adults and to compare dermatoscopic patterns according to the demographic and clinical features.

Methods

We conducted a prospective study involving patients who visited the dermatology outpatient clinic in two different hospitals in Ankara between August 2022 and July 2023. Among the patients who consecutively applied to the outpatient clinics, 129 adult patients with 253 scalp nevi were included in the study. Demographic characteristics of patients, Fitzpatrick skin type, eye and hair color, skin cancer history, anatomical localization of nevi on the scalp, nevus diameter, and number of nevi were recorded.

The ethical approval was granted by the Clinical Research Ethics Committee of Medicana International Ankara Hospital (date: 30 June 2022; no. 18). The study was conducted following the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki, the Patient Rights Act, and relevant ethical guidelines. An informed consent form for research was obtained from all patients participating in the study.

For each participant, clinical and dermatoscopic photographs of nevi were taken and stored. Dermatoscopic images were obtained using a Dermlite DL4 dermatoscope and a standardized digitalized dermatoscopy monitoring device (FotoFinder, FotoFinder Systems GMBH; Germany). Patients lacking high-quality dermatoscopic images, having missing clinical data, and/or under 18 years of age were excluded from the study.

The dermatoscopic images of scalp melanocytic nevi were assessed by one dermatoscopy-experienced dermatologist (HGD) and classified based on distinctive morphological patterns, including globular, papillomatous, homogeneous, reticular, eclipse, reverse eclipse, mixed, and atypical patterns (Figure 1). The classification of these dermatoscopic features was based on detailed morphological characteristics described in the current data: globular: presence of brown globules; papillomatous: extended widely light brown or darkened pigmentation and comma-shaped vessels; homogeneous: brown or black structureless pigmentation and including blue homogeneous pigmentation (blue nevus); reticular: dark brown pigment network; eclipse-central hypopigmentation presenting with a peripheral network exhibiting perifollicular hypopigmentation; reverse eclipse: characterized by a target-like shape with a globular darker center, a tan uniform inner ring, and a reticulated darker outer ring; mixed: involves a combination with two features (e.g., reticulo-globular, reticular-homogeneous); atypical pattern: not showing features with any described patterns [2–6]. Vascular patterns were classified as linear-irregular (serpentine), curved (comma-shaped), dotted, looped (hairpin), branched, and clod. In this study, as there was only one atypical nevus pattern, it could not be included in the statistical analyses in terms of dermatoscopic patterns and other variables.

The reverse eclipse pattern was described as identical to Cockade nevus features in some dermatoscopy literature. Eclipse and Cockade nevi have similar histopathological findings, so it is believed that they could represent two distinct clinical variants of the same phenomenon [8]. For statistical analysis, the eclipse pattern and reverse eclipse pattern were grouped.

Minimum and maximum values, mean, and standard deviation values are given for numerical variables, and frequency and percentage values are given for categorical variables. Kruskal-Wallis rank analysis was used to analyze numerical variables. Pearson’s chi-square test and Fisher’s exact test were used in the analysis of categorical variables. Analyses were performed with R 4.4.2 (R Core Team, 2024) program. P<0.05 was considered significant.

Results

The patient characteristics, nevus features, and dermatoscopic features of nevi are presented in Table 1. Only one subject reported a personal history of nonmelanoma skin cancer, and none of them reported a family history of melanoma.

The most frequent pattern was papillomatous. In the homogeneous pattern group, 7/46 (15.2%) nevi were detected as blue nevi. The comparison of dermatoscopic patterns with the detailed data is shown in Table 2. The eclipse/reverse eclipse pattern was significantly more frequent, whereas the papillomatous pattern was significantly less prevalent in younger patients than in older patients (P<0.001). The globular, homogeneous, and mixed patterns were found more frequently in males (P=0.01). The mean diameter of nevi was smaller in reticular pattern and larger in eclipse/reverse eclipse patterns (P<0.001). More than one vessel type was observed in the same nevus. While vessels were not observed in reticular pattern, vessels were present in 70% of the papillomatous pattern (P<0.001). Nearly 90% of the vessels observed in papillomatous nevi were curved (53.4%) and linear vessels (36.3%). There was no perifollicular hypopigmentation in almost any papillomatous nevi but was seen in almost all in eclipse/reverse eclipse and reticular patterns (P<0.001). There was no statistically significant difference in terms of skin type, eye color, hair color, and nevus location with dermatoscopic nevus patterns.

Discussion

The most common dermatoscopic pattern was papillomatous, which was seen in about one third of nevi in this study, and it was notably more common in older patients. Similar to our study, Hoffman et al. found that the majority of scalp nevi in adults tend to be mainly nodular and have a light pigmentation or are skin-colored, featuring either a smooth or papillomatous surface [1]. The eclipse/reverse eclipse pattern was observed more frequently in younger patients, similar to other studies [5,9]. The eclipse nevi are benign compound nevi and are seen more frequently on the scalp of children [8,10,11]. The mean diameter of nevi was found to be larger in eclipse/reverse eclipse patterns. While these nevi might look different from the usual uniformly colored round nevus, their varied characteristics, such as border irregularity, larger diameter, and irregular pigmentation, should not raise concerns for melanoma, and excision is not necessary [11,12]. Changes in the eclipse nevi may occur during one’s lifetime. In young adulthood, the central area of eclipse nevi may become more raised, whereas in childhood, they are typically flat and exhibit central hypopigmentation. When the peripheral pigmentation fades over time, the elevated or nodular, hypopigmented nevus that is usually seen in adults eventually occurs [2]. The studies showed that the eclipse pattern was more frequently observed in patients with atypical mole syndrome and might be an indicator of a higher total body nevus count in the pediatric age group [5,10].

In the present study, the second most common dermatoscopic pattern was reticular, and this type of nevus was small. In a study on trunk-extremity nevi, reticular, homogeneous, and mixed types of reticular-homogeneous patterns were found in individuals over the age of 15 years, with reticular-homogeneous being the most common type [13]. A multicenter study showed that scalp nevi exhibited a significantly higher prevalence of a typical symmetrical reticular pattern, globules distributed regularly, and blue structureless pigmentation like blue nevi compared with scalp melanoma. Scalp melanomas, on the other hand, were strongly linked to atypical pigment networks, atypical pseudonetworks, black irregular structureless areas, and blue-white structures [9]. We did not observe any vessel structures in these nevi with a reticular pattern. In another multicenter study on cases with scalp lentiginous melanomas and histopathologically-confirmed benign yet clinically ambiguous pigmented lesions (solar lentigos, lichen planus-like keratoses, seborrheic keratoses, and actinic keratoses), the strongest independent predictors of malignant lesions were chaos of color, pigmented reticular lines, increased density of vascular network, and perifollicular gray circles [14]. Scalp melanoma tends to exhibit a more aggressive clinical behavior, with increased recurrence rates and a higher mortality compared to melanoma in other anatomical areas [15]. If the reticular pattern of a scalp nevi is chaotic and/or vascular, malignancy should be suspected.

The globular pattern was detected less frequently in our study. It is the most frequent dermatoscopic pattern of scalp nevi in pediatric age [7,10]. Zalaudek et al. examined 1268 melanocytic nevi of 50 individuals located on the body except specific sites like face/head and palms/soles. They found that globular patterns were significantly more common in the youngest age group, with a rate of 82%. The predominance of the globular patterns decreased with aging [13].

Homogeneous pattern of scalp nevi was among the most common patterns, with a rate of 18.2%. In a study, the homogeneous pattern was 11.2% in all age groups, and it was notably lower in individuals under 15 years old who had atypical mole syndrome [5]. In this study, 15.2% (7/46) of the homogeneous pattern and 2.8% (7/253) of the total scalp patterns were blue nevi. While the rate of blue nevi was found to be higher (27/105; 25.7%) in a multicenter study [9], the rate in Zalaudek et al.’s [2] study (2/124; 1.6%) was closer to our study. Almost all of them revealed a structureless blue pigmentation by dermatoscopy.

The presence of vessels was highest in papillomatous pattern (70%), and most of them (90%) were curved and linear-irregular vessels. In a study that investigated the dermatoscopic and clinical features of papillomatous nevi located on the trunk, arms, and legs in an age range of 1–82 years, vascular structures were present in 115 (70.6%). Comma vessels were the predominant vessel type; however, dotted and atypical or polymorphous vessels were also present [16]. In another study on dermatoscopic patterns in intradermal nevi, of the head-neck nevi (N=16), 81.3% showed exophytic papillary structures, and vessels were present in 56.3% [17]. Consistent with our research, comma-shaped vessels (66.6%) and linear-irregular vessels (22.2%) were found to be the most commonly occurring vascular structures in dermal nevi. Polymorphous vessels were observed less frequently in the head and neck area (11.1%) than in the trunk region (58.3%) [17]. Vessel types may play an important role in special locations, including scalp nodular lesions. Nodular melanomas without specific dermatoscopic features, atypical vascular pattern, or multiple irregular, polymorphous vessels may be present [18]. The recent findings from the International Dermoscopy Society study regarding the distinction between thin/thick nodular melanomas and various nonmelanoma nodular lesions confirmed that blue irregular structureless areas, along with serpentine and dotted vessels, served as indicators for all nodular melanomas when compared with nonmelanoma nodular lesions [19].

In this study, the number of scalp nevi was higher in males, consistent with the literature [3,4]. The number of scalp nevi plays an important role. We did not perform a total body nevus count in our study; however, some studies have reported data on this issue. Results from a study involving individuals of all ages revealed that the number of melanocytic nevi on the scalp increased in parallel with the total body melanocytic nevi count and the total body atypical nevi count. This indicates that a comprehensive examination of the scalp is necessary, especially for people who are at increased risk of melanoma or who have over 30 melanocytic nevi [3].

Nearly half of the nevi had perifollicular hypopigmentation. This is the characteristic feature of most scalp nevi in children and causes the appearance of irregular and scalloped borders and variegated pigmentation in melanocytic nevi [10,20]. Scalp nevi with network patterns were frequently associated with central hypopigmentation or perifollicular hypopigmentation. In research conducted by Stanganelli et al., hypopigmentation was observed to be perifollicular in 15 (19.2%) and central in 14 (18%) of the melanocytic nevi [9]. Hypopigmentation areas were commonly seen in both nevi and melanomas; however, hypopigmentation in nevi was predominantly localized perifollicular or at the center, whereas hypopigmentation in melanomas tends to be multifocal or diffuse [9]. When hypopigmentation is observed within the nevus, it appears circular. In contrast, when the hypopigmentation is situated at the outer border of the nevus, it creates a semicircular pattern that leads to a notch in the lesion’s outline. This occurrence was noted in nevi featuring terminal hairs as well as those containing vellus hairs. Histopathologically, the number of junctional melanocytes, the amount of pigment within keratinocytes, the count of melanocytes in the papillary dermis, and the presence of melanophages were decreased surrounding hair follicles in comparison with the rest of the nevus [21].

Our study included only adult patients; pediatric patients were excluded. Larger case series including pediatric patients may be helpful to provide more information on and comparisons between scalp dermatoscopic patterns. The study was conducted as a clinical-dermatoscopic analysis, so we did not excise scalp nevi in our study. No definitive conclusion can be drawn regarding nevi exhibiting atypia in histopathology. Another limitation is the restriction to two centers. Future studies, involving multiple centers and observers, that combine clinical, dermatoscopic, and histopathological analysis may shed further light on this issue.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study demonstrates the clinical and dermatoscopic patterns of scalp nevi in adults. Dermatoscopic examination of scalp nevi provides more detailed information, enables accurate diagnosis, and helps to manage patients compared to clinical examination alone. Evaluation of dermatoscopic features can prevent unnecessary excisions, especially in nevi with concerning clinical features. Nonetheless, all suspicious lesions should be biopsied to exclude malignancy.
