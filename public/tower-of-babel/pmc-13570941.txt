National Utilization of Navigation and Robotics in Inpatient Spinal Fusion Remains Low but is Increasing, 2016–2022
Authors: Mitchell K. Ng, Leonidas E. Mastrokostas, Paul G. Mastrokostas, Gregorio Baek, Matthew Meade, Mohamed Said, Pemla Jagtiani, Jad Bou Monsef, Afshin E. Razi, Barrett Woods, Mark F. Kurd, Ian David Kaye, Zachary Wilt, Thomas D. Cha, Alan S. Hilibrand, Alexander R. Vaccaro, Gregory D. Schroeder, Christopher K. Kepler, Andrew P. Alvarez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Study DesignRetrospective cohort study.ObjectivesAdoption patterns in spinal fusion remain poorly defined at the national level. This study aimed to evaluate national adoption trends of navigation and robotics in spine fusion from 2016 to 2022.MethodsThe National Inpatient Sample was queried for adult spinal fusion hospitalizations between 2016 and 2022. Procedures were categorized as navigation-assisted, robotic-assisted, or conventional. Outcomes included frequency of utilization, complications, and discharge disposition. National estimates were generated using survey weights, and outcomes were compared across groups.ResultsOf 2,057,450 inpatient spinal fusions from 2016 to 2022, 152,155 (7.4%) incorporated technology—120,525 (5.9%) navigation and 31,630 (1.5%) robotics. Technology utilization increased from 2.7% in 2016 to 13.3% in 2022 (navigation 2.30% to 10.39%; robotics 0.36% to 2.90%). Mean unadjusted complication rates were 20.9% for conventional cases, 25.6% for navigation-assisted cases, and 22.4% for robotic-assisted cases, with non-routine discharge also varying across cohorts and spinal regions. However, these findings should be interpreted cautiously, as selection bias and unmeasured differences in case complexity likely influenced technology use and associated outcomes. Further study with more granular clinical data is needed to better define the relationship between these technologies and perioperative outcomes.ConclusionsThis national study characterized trends in navigation/robotic-assisted spinal fusion. Although adoption remained modest, robotics expanded at a steeper trajectory than navigation. Despite observed differences in unadjusted complication profiles across cohorts, these findings should be interpreted cautiously given likely selection bias and unmeasured case complexity, and prospective studies are needed to determine long-term outcomes.

Introduction

Computer-assisted technologies such as navigation and robotics are becoming increasingly embedded within orthopaedic surgery, offering the potential to improve accuracy, reproducibility, and safety.1,2 Their advantages have been well documented in total hip/knee arthroplasty, where use of these are associated with enhanced implant alignment, reduced variability, albeit with unclear effect on clinical outcomes.3,4 By contrast, the role of navigation and robotics in spine surgery is less well defined, particularly in the inpatient setting where procedures are technically demanding, resource-intensive, and associated with significant morbidity.5-7

In spine surgery, both technologies were initially adopted to improve the precision of pedicle screw fixation.8,9 Early studies emphasized their ability to address challenges related to variable anatomy, visualization, and trajectory planning. Over the past decade, however, industry investment and competition among device manufacturers have driven rapid expansion of these platforms. 6 Modern robotic systems now aim to facilitate not only pedicle screw placement but also facetectomy, minimally invasive interbody cage placement, and in some cases, elements of laminectomy.10-13 These broader applications reflect an effort to transform robotics from a single-task adjunct into a more comprehensive surgical workflow tool, aligning with wider commercial strategies to differentiate platforms and capture market share.14,15

Despite this momentum, large-scale evaluation of real-world utilization and outcomes across inpatient spinal fusion remains limited. 16 Most published series are derived from single institutions, proprietary registries, or selected patient populations, leaving critical gaps in our understanding of national adoption patterns, complication profiles, and how robotics compares with navigation in routine practice.7,17 As hospitals weigh the cost and efficiency implications of adopting these technologies which are now offered by several manufacturers, the need for robust comparative data has become increasingly relevant.14,18

To this end, this study aimed to use a national inpatient database to: (1) evaluate temporal trends in the use of navigation and robotics in spinal fusion procedures between 2016 and 2022; (2) compare perioperative complication rates among navigation-assisted, robot-assisted, and conventional cases; and (3) examine procedure- and location-specific differences in outcomes. We hypothesized that use of navigation and robotics would rise steadily over time, with robotics demonstrating the fastest growth, given expanding industry support, increasing surgeon adoption beyond pedicle screw fixation, and parallels already established in arthroplasty.

Materials and Methods

Data Source

We performed a retrospective observational study using the National Inpatient Sample (NIS) from 2016–2022, maintained by the Healthcare Cost and Utilization Project (HCUP). The NIS is a 20% stratified sample of discharges from U.S. community hospitals and excludes rehabilitation and long-term acute care facilities.

Study Population and Cohort Construction

Adult hospitalizations (≥18 years) with a spinal fusion performed during the index admission were identified using International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision, Procedure Coding System (ICD-10-PCS) procedure fields. To comprehensively characterize case mix, all relevant ICD-10-PCS procedure codes were queried across spinal fusion categories, spinal region (cervical, thoracic, lumbar), surgical approach (anterior, posterior, combined), and invasiveness (open, minimally invasive). Admissions were included if at least one qualifying spinal fusion code was present on the day of admission.

Technology Identification (Navigation vs Robotic)

Use of navigation and robotic assistance was identified from ICD-10-PCS technology designations for computer-assisted and robotic-assisted procedures involving the spine/trunk region. Each admission was flagged as navigation-assisted, robotic-assisted, or neither. When both technologies were coded within the same admission, the case was classified as robotic, consistent with prior literature. 19

Exclusion Criteria

We excluded admissions with: age <18 years; missing or invalid core demographic variables (sex, race, age, income quartile), hospital characteristics (region, bed size, teaching status), or discharge disposition; nonelective admissions; procedures not performed on the day of admission; missing payer data; or negative length of stay. In keeping with HCUP guidance, small cell sizes (≤10) were suppressed in reporting.

Complication Definitions

Perioperative complications were identified using ICD-10-CM/PCS codes. Cardiovascular events were analyzed individually—including transfusion, venous thromboembolism, acute post-hemorrhagic anemia, and myocardial infarction. Respiratory complications were grouped (pneumonia, pulmonary embolism), and neurological complications were grouped (cerebrovascular accident, nervous system complication, and cerebrospinal fluid leak/dural tear). Additional individual complications captured included mechanical complication, dysphagia, acute kidney injury, and sepsis. A binary adverse events variable was defined as the presence of at least one complication. Discharge disposition was classified as routine (discharge to home) or non-routine (transfer to another short-term hospital, skilled nursing facility, intermediate care facility, or home health care).

Variables and Outcomes

Patient demographics (age, sex, race, income quartile), primary payer, hospital characteristics (region, bed size, teaching status), comorbidity burden quantified by the Elixhauser Comorbidity Index, spinal region (cervical, thoracic, and lumbar), fusion level (single-level vs multilevel), surgical approach (anterior vs posterior), and invasiveness (open vs minimally invasive) were abstracted. Primary utilization outcomes included annual proportions of navigation and robotic-assisted fusions among all fusions. Complication groupings and the composite adverse events variable were assessed across groups as defined above; inpatient mortality during the index hospitalization was evaluated as a separate outcome and was not included in the adverse events variable. Discharge disposition was analyzed as routine versus non-routine.

Statistical Analysis

All analyses incorporated survey weights, strata, and cluster variables to generate national estimates per HCUP recommendations. Categorical variables were compared using survey-weighted Rao–Scott chi-square tests; continuous variables were compared using survey-weighted Wald F tests from design-based linear models (design-based ANOVA). Annual adoption trends for navigation and robotic assistance were summarized as weighted proportions by year; compound annual growth rates (CAGR) from 2016 to 2022 were also calculated. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05. Analyses were conducted in R (version 4.4.0; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Patient and Hospital Characteristics

Mean age was highest in navigation (62.43 years) compared with robotic (62.11) and conventional (60.86; P < 0.001). Sex distribution showed small but statistically significant differences (male: 46.7% conventional, 45.3% navigation, 46.2% robotic; P = 0.001). Race and income quartiles varied modestly (e.g., White: 80.6% conventional, 83.8% navigation, 82.5% robotic; P < 0.001). Robotic cases were concentrated in the Northeast (27.4%) and South (43.3%) compared with navigation (12.2% and 35.3%) and conventional (18.1% and 41.7%; P < 0.001). Navigation was more often performed at urban teaching hospitals (81.5% vs 75.6% conventional and 78.2% robotic; P < 0.001), while robotic skewed toward small hospitals (34.7% vs 21.6% navigation and 24.8% conventional; P < 0.001). Comorbidity burden was highest in navigation (Elixhauser mean 2.27) vs robotic (2.07) and conventional (2.03; P < 0.001; Table 1).

Perioperative Complications by Region

Across regions, crude complication profiles showed distinct but consistent patterns. In cervical fusion, adverse events were higher with technology assistance than with conventional approaches (navigation 24.6% and robotic 32.8% vs conventional 15.0%; P < 0.001), driven in part by acute post-hemorrhagic anemia (11.9% navigation and 22.4% robotic vs 4.0% conventional; P < 0.001); navigation also had higher transfusion and venous thromboembolism than conventional (4.4% vs 1.0% and 1.1% vs 0.4%, respectively; P < 0.001), and non-routine discharge was more common with technology assistance (43.0% navigation and 55.2% robotic vs 23.6% conventional; P < 0.001). In thoracic fusion, overall adverse events were high across cohorts (50.7% conventional, 56.5% navigation, 50.1% robotic; P < 0.001) with substantial anemia and transfusion rates, but neurologic complications were lower with robotic than with navigation or conventional techniques (3.5% vs 6.5% and 4.7%; P < 0.001). In lumbar fusion, overall adverse events were similar across cohorts (21.1% conventional, 21.6% navigation, 20.3% robotic; P = 0.419); however, robotic assistance was associated with fewer mechanical complications (2.3% vs 3.3% navigation and 3.8% conventional; P < 0.001) alongside a higher non-routine discharge rate (41.2% vs 36.2% navigation and 36.3% conventional; P = 0.003), while neurologic Discussion

This study evaluated national trends in the use of navigation and robotics for inpatient spinal fusion between 2016 and 2022. We found that, although utilization of these technologies increased during the study period, overall adoption remained modest, representing a minority of inpatient cases with the conventional approach accounting for the vast majority of spinal fusions performed. However, in the context of advanced technologies, navigation was consistently more common than robotics, though robotic-assisted fusion demonstrated more rapid growth in recent years. While perioperative complication rates, discharge disposition, and length of stay were similar among patients undergoing navigated and robotic fusion, technology groups had less favorable crude outcome profiles compared to those seen in conventional spinal fusions. Importantly, these unadjusted comparisons should be regarded as descriptive in nature, as technology cohorts likely include patients with more severe underlying pathology who are undergoing more extensive and complex fusion procedures. Notably, crude complication rates in the robotic cohort also declined over time, which may in part reflect increasing familiarity with the technology as institutional and surgeon experience matured. Taken together, our findings suggest that, despite growing enthusiasm from industry and expanding indications for advanced intraoperative technologies, inpatient spine adoption has not mirrored the trajectory seen in other orthopaedic subspecialties. Further study using more granular clinical data is needed to better define how these technologies relate to perioperative outcomes.

Our first objective was to assess national utilization trends. The steady but low overall use of navigation and robotics in spinal fusion contrasts with arthroplasty, where adoption has been swift and more widespread. 20 In spine surgery, however, inpatient use of either robotics and/or navigation remained well below 10% of fusions throughout the study period. This slower trajectory likely reflects a combination of factors. First, the cost of robotic platforms remains high, both in capital acquisition and per-case maintenance. 21 Most navigation or robotic systems have an average fixed cost of $1-1.5 million U.S. dollars, with additional disposable supplies costing ∼$1,500 a case.22-24 A retrospective review of 360 patients undergoing lumbar fusion found cost per quality-of-life adjusted years (QALY) at 1 year for open, MIS, and robot-assisted cases was $296,600, $116,000, and $593,000 respectively. 21 Second, the learning curve for spine robotics may be steeper compared to arthroplasty, given the variability of spinal anatomy and the broad range of procedures technology might be applied. This ranges from pedicle screw placement in large deformity cases including atlantoaxial and spinopelvic fixation, minimally invasive percutaneous screw fixation/interbody cage placement, and more recently endoscopic spine procedures.23-27 Finally, reimbursement structures rarely provide incentives for the use of advanced technologies in spine surgery, making hospitals more cautious in their investments. 21 Despite these barriers, our overall findings demonstrate a consistent upward trend, suggesting gradual but deliberate integration of these tools into clinical practice.

Our second aim was to examine crude perioperative outcomes across conventional, navigated, and robotic spinal fusion. Somewhat discordant with the current literature, there was variability in complication rates between conventional and advanced surgical approaches in the present work. In a national database study conducted by Ansari et al that compared perioperative outcomes between 11,889 patients undergoing conventional versus 689 patients undergoing computer navigation assisted posterior cervical fusion, there were no differences in complication rates amongst the cohorts. 28 Importantly, however, that study used propensity score matching to improve comparability between cohorts, whereas the present analysis reflects unadjusted national comparisons. As such, the higher crude complication rates observed in our navigation- and robotics-assisted cohorts may in part reflect confounding by indication, whereby these technologies are preferentially used in patients with more severe pathology and in more extensive or technically complex fusion procedures. This interpretation is supported by the broader case mix seen in our technology cohorts, including a greater proportion of multilevel and anatomically complex cases. While several studies have demonstrated that navigation and robotics may improve technical accuracy, such as reducing pedicle screw breach rates or limiting radiation exposure to the surgeon and staff, these benefits may not translate into measurable outcome differences at the population level, particularly when assessed in administrative datasets without detailed operative and clinical granularity.29,30 Further studies incorporating risk-adjusted methodologies, such as propensity-matching and prospective design, are required to better define the relationship between these technologies and perioperative outcomes.

Regarding the use of advanced technologies in thoracic fusions, the present work demonstrated increased posthemorrhagic anemia and blood transfusions in patients undergoing surgery with robotic and navigation assistance. These findings may potentially be explained by the use of advanced surgical technologies in larger, more complex constructs (e.g., multi-level lumbar or thoracolumbar procedures) where the platform’s precision is most valuable.31,32 These operations inherently involve longer operative times and greater blood loss, which would increase the likelihood of postoperative anemia. 33 A similar trend was seen in our lumbar fusion cohort—patients undergoing conventional lumbar fusion generally had decreased rates of transfusion compared to patients undergoing robotic or navigation assisted lumbar fusion which similarly can be explained by the use of advanced surgical technologies in multi-level complex constructs. In addition, the increased neurologic complication rate observed with navigation compared to conventional and robotic assisted thoracic fusion may be partly explained by the technical challenges of this region.34-36 Registration errors and line-of-sight limitations are more pronounced in the thoracic spine, and navigation is often selected for complex deformity cases, which inherently carry higher risk.37,38 Robotic systems are also susceptible to registration errors; however, prior studies suggest that they may improve the consistency of pedicle cannulation and screw placement.34-38 Whether this translates into a reduced risk of neurologic injury in thoracic anatomy cannot be determined from the present administrative dataset.

Of note, it is possible that the advantages of these technologies manifest more clearly in long-term outcomes, such as reduced revision rates or improved construct durability, which are not captured in administrative databases. Another consideration is patient selection: surgeons may preferentially employ navigation or robotics in more complex cases, potentially offsetting any immediate benefit with higher baseline risk. 39 Thus, although crude inpatient outcomes differed across technology cohorts, these findings should not be interpreted as evidence that navigation or robotics are intrinsically associated with worse perioperative outcomes, particularly given likely differences in patient selection and case complexity.

Our third objective was to assess potential differences in hospital resource utilization, including length of stay and discharge disposition. The greater proportion of patients with non-routine discharge in our robotic and navigation assisted cohorts is likely due to increased complication rates observed in these groups. In addition, between our robotic and navigation assisted cohorts, we did not detect clinically meaningful differences between groups. This aligns with economic analyses reporting increased operative time and equipment costs without demonstrable inpatient savings. 40 Advocates for robotics and navigation often point to potential long-term efficiencies, including reduced radiation exposure, lower revision rates, and more predictable outcomes in anatomically complex cases. 41 Early reports also suggest that robotics can facilitate tasks beyond pedicle screw placement, including facetectomy, interbody cage insertion, and even elements of decompression, such as laminectomy.26,27,36,39,40 However, these potential applications remain nascent, and population-level data confirming cost savings or improved outcomes are limited.

There are several potential limitations of the study. First, the reliance on an administrative database introduces the potential for coding inaccuracies, particularly in distinguishing between navigated and robotic cases. Furthermore, physicians may have unintentionally omitted billing codes indicative of robotic or navigation system use as there is no additional reimbursement for these procedures. Second, only inpatient spinal fusions were captured, excluding outpatient procedures, which are increasingly common and may represent an early setting for technology adoption. Importantly, the NIS is restricted to the U.S. population, and findings from this study may not be generalizable to other countries or healthcare systems. Third, the database does not include granular clinical details, such as radiographic screw accuracy, radiation exposure, or patient-reported outcomes, all of which are critical in assessing the full impact of navigation and robotics. Additionally, surgeon-level variables (e.g. orthopaedic versus neurosurgery, experience, the specific robotic/navigation system, minimally invasive versus endoscopic approach, case complexity, and institutional infrastructure) could not be accounted for. Accordingly, the observed differences in complication rates should be considered hypothesis-generating rather than causal and are best interpreted as signals that warrant further evaluation in studies with more granular clinical data and appropriate risk adjustment. Despite these limitations, this is the first study to characterize and quantify the real-world utilization trends of navigation and robotics in spine surgery cases. Future research should leverage prospective registries and multicenter collaborations to capture more granular clinical and radiographic outcomes, as well as long-term cost-effectiveness. In particular, evaluating whether robotics and navigation meaningfully reduce revision rates, reoperations, or long-term complications will be essential to justify the substantial investments required for widespread adoption.

Conclusion

While there has been substantial interest surrounding use of navigation guidance and robotic spine surgery, to our knowledge this is the first study to characterize and quantify national trends. In this analysis of 2,057,450 inpatient U.S. spinal fusions performed between 2016 and 2022, only 7.4% incorporated advanced technology, including 5.9% with navigation and 1.5% with robotics. Utilization increased substantially during the study period, rising from 2.7% in 2016 to 13.3% in 2022. Complication rates were comparable across groups: 20.9% for conventional fusion, 25.6% for navigation, and 22.4% for robotics, though trends diverged, with navigation complications increasing over time and robotics demonstrating a decline. Region-specific analyses showed differences in unadjusted outcome profiles across technology cohorts, particularly in thoracic and lumbar procedures, although these findings should be interpreted cautiously given likely differences in case selection and procedural complexity. Despite rapid growth, overall adoption remains lower than expected, underscoring the need for prospective studies to clarify long-term outcomes, cost-effectiveness, and the evolving role of these technologies in complex spine surgery.
