Halophytes as holobionts: disentangling the contribution of plant genotype and environmental factors to the associated microbiome of hydro- and xerohalophytes
Authors: Eloísa Pajuelo, Elena Romano-Rodríguez, Enrique Mateos-Naranjo, Noris J Flores-Duarte, Ignacio D Rodríguez-Llorente, Susana Redondo-Gómez
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Soils of hydrohalophytes are enriched in ammonia oxidizing archaea, whereas soils of xerohalophytes are enriched in adaptive bacteria.•Endophytes of functional hydrohalophytes and xerohalophytes are both genotype-specific and site-specific.•Core endophytes include halophilic bacteria Kushneria, Halomonas, Pseudoalteromonas and Zunongwangia. Moreover, hydrohalophytes harbour marine halophilic bacteria, whereas xerohalophytes harbour haloalkalophilic bacteria.•Factors driving endophytes populations are: halophyte type > halophyte species > soil characteristics > seasonality.Keywords: Hydro- and xerohalophytes, Rhizosphere soils, Bulk soils, Endophytes, Microbial communities, Driving factors

Introduction

Halophytes are getting great interest in research due to their ecological importance and multiple benefits (Parida et al., 2019). From an ecological point of view, they colonize both coastal and inland saline soils, where they provide key ecosystemic services due to the preservation of coastal sediments, habitats for birds and other fauna, CO2 sinks, etc. (Bueno and Cordovilla, 2021; Bibi et al., 2025). Moreover, some halophytes were proposed in soil reclamation and in conservation of protected areas (Turcios et al., 2021; Singh et al., 2023). For instance, Spartina maritima and S. densiflora were proposed for metal immobilization (Mesa et al., 2015; Paredes Páliz et al., 2017; Redondo-Gómez, 2013) and desalination (Sarath et al., 2021).

On the other hand, some halophytes are sources of pharmaceutical products (Giordano et al., 2021; Ferreira et al., 2022). Mesembryanthemum crystallinum, Aster Tripolium and Crithmum maritimum have antioxidant, antiinflammatory, antidiabetic or antimicrobial activities (Ibtissem et al., 2012; Meot-Duros and Magné, 2009; Ruberto et al., 2000), as well as cosmetic applications (Ibtissem et al., 2008). Finally, some halophytes such as Salicornia, Salicornia, Mesembryanthemun crystallinum, Crithmum maritimum, etc. can be eaten in salads, fried or in different recipes due to salty flavor, nutritional properties and high content of beneficial omega-3 and omega-6 unsaturated fatty acids (Meot-Duros and Magné, 2009; Ríos et al., 2020).

The current environmental situation related to climate change is threatening many populations of halophytes worldwide (Yang et al., 2024; Touchette et al., 2019). Elevated temperatures and longer drought periods, together with higher salinity and high levels of CO2, affect the survival of these plants and can contribute to the expansion of some invasive species (Borges et al., 2021).

Plants are considered holobionts and the microbiota associated to halophytes is a key agent in their resilience against changing environmental conditions (Afzal et al., 2019; Mesa-Marín et al., 2019a). Many publications study the microbial populations associated with halophytes (Gao et al., 2022; Zhao et al., 2024; Dai et al., 2025). Rhizosphere and endophytic microbes of halophytes play an important role in adaptation to extreme saline environments, drought, increasing temperatures and pollution (Abdelfadil et al., 2024; Mateos-Naranjo et al., 2024; Pajuelo et al., 2023). This can be achieved through different mechanisms, including the acquisition of nutrients (P, K, N), the secretion of phytohormones, the optimization of root respiration and water use efficiency, the secretion of siderophores, the control of plant stress via reduction of ethylene, the induction of the systemic defense response, the accumulation of osmotic and antioxidant substances, etc. (Mesa-Marín et al., 2018, 2019a, 2019b; Rodríguez-Llorente et al., 2019). Moreover, microbial populations are critical for understanding the dynamics of the cycles of elements in soil, decomposition and recycling of organic matter and CO2 sequestration (Zhao et al., 2024). In addition, halophytes are a source of new bacterial taxons including Vibrio palustris, Kushneria endophytica, Pseudoalteromonas rhizospherae, Rosellomorea acthrocnemi, etc. (Lucena et al., 2017; Navarro-Torre et al., 2018, 2020, 2021). Many of these bacteria display excellent plant growth promoting (PGP) activities and can be considered as biotools for improving the growth of crops under saline conditions (Pajuelo et al., 2021; Redondo-Gómez et al., 2021; Mateos-Naranjo et al., 2025).

A raising interest is being developed in understanding the whole microbiota associated to halophytes. A recent study by Colchado-López et al. (2022) presented a meta-analysis of published datasets derived from metabarcoding technology analyzing the community composition patterns of the rhizosphere associated bacteria of 13 angiosperm plant species (halophytes and xerophytes) from five countries. However, in the mentioned study, the comparison established was between halophytes (in general, plants inhabiting saline environments regardless of hydro- or xerohalophytes) and xerophytes (plants growing in arid environments, not necessarily halophytes). In this study, the authors suggested that the main factors modeling microbial communities are host plant family, plant ecological strategy and the abiotic environment. In another study, the authors analyze the bacterial assemblage disentangling the relative importance of seasonality, zonation and halophytes, without distinguishing between different functional halophytes (Camacho-Sánchez et al., 2022). Thus, there are no studies comparing the microbial populations associated with functional types of halophytes, considering their natural distribution in clearly contrasted ecological habitats, which allowed us to divide them into hydrohalophytes and xerohalophytes (Gul et al., 2013; Yuan et al., 2019). In particular, hydrohalophytes are found in aquatic conditions, or wet soils such as mangroves and salt-marshes inland. Xerohalophytes survive in dry habitats with saline soil and are considered extremophiles, because they germinate and reproduce under high-salinity conditions (Gul et al., 2013; Yuan et al., 2019). Our study aims to fill this gap of knowledge, specifically, study the bacterial assemblage and compartmentalization in halophytes of coastal and inland salt marshes, and disentangle the relative importance of seasonality, habitat abiotic and biotic conditions, and halophytes genotype. Hence, the following objectives will be assessed: a) studying the microbial populations associated to three compartments, i.e., bulk and rhizosphere soils and endophytes, of three species of hydrohalophytes and three species of xerohalophytes, and b) investigating the driving factors that determine the microbial populations associated to both types of halophytes.

Materials and methods

Study species

Halophytes from the Amaranthaceae were used as study species, since this is the family containing the highest number of halophytic genera with about 22 % of total representation (Flowers and Colmer, 2015). The hydrohalophytes selected were the shrubby species Atriplex portulacoides (L.), Salicornia perennis (Mill.) A.J. Scott ssp. perennis Scott., and Suaeda vera Forssk. ex J.F. Gmel. The plants were identified with the collaboration of the Hebarium of the Univesity of Sevilla (CITIUS) according to Castroviejo et al. (1990). These three species are found in the Atlantic coastal ecosystems of the Iberian Peninsula with tidal influence or with wet soil in inland salt lakes (Castroviejo et al., 1990). Thus, A. portulacoides is frequently the physiognomic dominant on well-drained and upper marshes, often fringing channels and pools that are flooded at full tide (Chapman, 1950), and S. vera is normally found at upper saltmarshes and permanent inland saline and brackish lakes, ponds and pools (Fernandez-Illescas et al., 2010; Herrero and Castañeda, 2015). Whilst S. perennis ssp. perennis is an intertidal macrophyte that grows in regularly flooded and open low marshes (Castellanos et al., 1994).

The xerohalophytes selected were Atriplex glauca L., Anabasis articulata (Forssk.) Moq. and Halocnemum strobilaceum (Pall.) M. Bieb., all are found on dry habitats (i.e., arid, semi-arid inland areas with unpredictable rainfall) with saline soil, characteristics found in Mediterranean coastal areas from the Southeast arid extreme of the Iberian Peninsula (i.e. Almeria, Murcia; Castroviejo et al., 1990). Specifically, A. glauca and H. strobilaceum are found at coastal saltmarshes and saline reedbeds as well as at inland salt marshes (Álvarez-Rogel et al., 2000; Hegazy et al., 2007; Zorb et al., 2013), while A. articulata is in permanent inland saline and brackish lakes, ponds and pools, sparsely- or un-vegetated habitats on mineral substrates or coastal dunes and sandy shores (Lev-Yadun, 2006; Veste et al., 2008; Qasem, 2015).

All six species are obligate halophytes and show optimal growth c. 170 mM NaCl (Glenn and O’Leary, 1985; O’Leary et al., 1986; Khan et al., 2000; Davy et al., 2006; Redondo-Gómez et al., 2007; Pyankov et al., 2010).

Sampling areas

The samples of the hydrohalophytes species Atriplex portulacoides, Salicornia perennis and Suaeda vera, were obtained in independent plots (three per species) located in three marshes in the Gulf of Cádiz (SW, Spain): i) natural area of Marismas de Isla Cristina and ii) Natural area of Marismas del Guadiana, both integrated into the Guadiana estuary and located in the Southwestern extreme of Huelva province with a surface of 2525 ha. And iii) Natural Area of Marismas del Río Piedras associated with Río Piedras estuary with a surface of 2595 ha (Huelva, Spain). The Mediterranean climate in this region is characterized by a strong seasonality, with dry and hot summers, and mild wet winters. These salt marshes constitute a natural mesotidal enclave of fluvial-tidal modelling and coastal feeding. Tides are semidiurnal with a mean range of 2.10 m and a mean spring tidal range of 2.97 m, representing a range of 0.40 to 3.37 m above Spanish Hydrographic Zero (Castellanos et al., 1994). In each of the three marshes, a population of each of the hydrohalophyte species was sampled (Table 1, Fig. 1).

The subtropical Mediterranean desert climate is characterized by extreme aridity, with average annual rainfall of <250 mm, which does not reach 200 mm in some areas of Cabo de Gata. This lack of rainfall is accompanied by sunshine, with >3000 hours per year, and high evapotranspiration (Capel Molina, 1990; Matamala, 2007).

Sampling protocol

We collected soil samples and plant tissues in the monospecific plots of all halophytes considering seasonality (spring, coinciding with the period of maximum plant vegetative development, and autumn, during the fructification). Samples were collected in November 2022 (autumn) and March 2023 (spring). In each plot, we randomly selected three healthy and completely developed adult plants from the center of the species patch. Each one of the samples was in fact a mix of three adult plants found in the same patch separated by at least 5 m. The tissues of these plants were mixed together to make a single representative sample.

A total of 108 rhizosediment (soil-root interface) samples were collected (18 plots (three per species) x 2 seasons x 3 replicates) shaking the roots. Each set of three-point samples with the same conditions were pooled in a sterile 100 ml plastic bottle, preserved at 4 °C and immediately transported to the laboratory. Thereafter, samples were stored at −80 °C until DNA extraction.

Furthermore, the aerial parts from the three plants (each sample containing three fragments of plant material of 2–3 cm length) were collected and superficially disinfected by immersing first in 70 % (v/v) ethanol for 1 min while shaking and then in a 3 % sodium hypochlorite solution for 15 min with gently shaking. Finally, they were washed four times in sterile distilled water (Navarro-Torre et al., 2017). Surface sterilization control was done by rotating the pieces of plant tissues on the surface of TSA plates with the help of sterile tweezers and incubating the plates for 24 h at 28 °C. No colonies were developed on the surface of the agar. Disinfected samples were crushed separately with a sterile mortar in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C until DNA extraction.

Finally, bulk reference soil samples (15 cm depth) were collected in unvegetated sites in three points and at least one meter from the following vegetated patches (a reference soil per species, see Table 1): HA1, HS2, HV3, XG1, XA1, and XH1. These samples were transported and processed in a similar manner to that described for the rhizosediment samples.

A fraction of each rhizosediment and soil samples was stored at −20 °C until the subsequent determination of physicochemical measurements.

Soil physicochemical measurements

The soil samples were dried in oven at 37 °C until constant weight. They were grinded and sieved through a 2 mm sieve. The pH (Crison pH/mV p-506, Spain) and electrical conductivity (Crison-522, Spain) were determined in aqueous extracts (1/2.5 and 1/5 w/V, respectively) after 30 min of equilibrium at 25 °C (Porta Casanellas, 1986). Determination of oxidizable organic C and phosphate in solution (Thermo Nicolet Evolution 300 UV–Visible Spectrophotometer, Germany) were performed according to the methods described by Murphy and Riley (1962), respectively, whereas available P was measured according to Olsen et al. (1954). Determination of total C, N and S was performed using the Dumas method (CNS-Trumac de LECO, Spain; Etheridge et al., 1988). In all cases, three replicates were measured for each sample.

DNA isolation and library preparation

Total genomic DNA from samples was extracted using the CTAB method (Doyle and Doyle, 1987). DNA concentration and purity was monitored on 1 % agarose gels and the concentration adjusted to 1ng/μL using sterile water. The V3-V4 regions of 16S rRNAs were amplified using about 10 ng template DNA, 2 μM of specific primers (F: 5′-CCTAYGGGRBGCASCAG-3′ and R: 5′-GGACTACNNGGGTATCTAAT-3′), and the Phusion® High -Fidelity PCR Master Mix (New England Biolabs). PCR products were mixed with SyBr-green loading buffer, electrophoresed on 2 % agarose gel and purified with Qiagen Gel Extraction Kit (Qiagen, Germany) according to the instructions of the manufacturer.

Sequencing libraries were generated usingTruSeq® DNA PCR- Free Sample Preparation Kit (Illumina, USA) following manufacturer's recommendations. The library quality was assessed using the Qubit@ 2.0 Fluorometer (Thermo Scientific) and real-time PCR for quantification, and with the Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100 system for size distribution detection. At last, the library was sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq platform and 250 bp paired-end reads were generated.

Data analysis

Quality control

Paired-end reads according to their unique barcode were truncated for eliminating barcodes and primers using Python (V3.6.13) and adaptors were removed through cutadapt (V3.3). Trimmed sequences were merged using FLASH (V1.2.11, http://ccb.jhu.edu/software/FLASH/; Magoč and Salzberg, 2011) to produce raw tags. Quality filtering of the raw tags was performed using the fastp software (Version 0.23.1; Chen et al., 2018) and the high-quality clean tags were compared against the reference Silva database (https://www.arb-silva.de/) using the UCHIME algorithm (http://www.drive5.com/usearch/manual/uchime_algo.html; Edgar et al., 2011) to detect chimera sequences, which were removed by using the vsearch package (V2.16.0) (https://github.com/torognes/vsearch/; Rognes et al., 2016).

OTU cluster, species annotation and phylogenetic relationships construction

For OTUs production, sequence’s analysis was performed by Uparse software (Uparse v7.0. 1001, http://drive5.com/uparse/; Edgar, 2013). Sequences with ≥ 97 % similarity were assigned to the same OTUs. Representative sequence for each OTU was annotated using the Silva Database (http://www.arb- silva.de/; Quast et al., 2012) based on the Mothur algorithm. To establish the phylogenetic relationship of different OTUs and the difference of the dominant species in different samples or groups, multiple sequence alignment was conducted using the MUSCLE software (Version 3.8.31, http://www.drive5.com/muscle; Edgar, 2004). Raw data have been released to the Sequence Read Archive database of the NCBI (SRA-NCBI) with the following identification codes: SubmissionID: SUB15656311; BioProject ID: PRJNA1347384.

Analysis of relative abundance

The sequences annotated as chloroplasts and mitochondria were manually removed and Alpha and Beta diversity were obtained after removing these reads. OTUs abundance information was normalized regarding the sample with the least sequences. The top 15 taxa of each sample at two taxonomic ranks (phylum and genus) were selected to plot the distribution histogram of relative abundance in Perl through SVG function. The abundance of top 35 taxa of each sample at the level of genus was used to draw the heatmap using R pheatmap function. Venn and Flower diagrams were produced in R for visualization of common and unique information between different samples or groups.

Alpha diversity, Beta diversity and community structure

Alpha diversity was assessed through 5 indices, including Observed_species, Chao1, Shannon, Simpson and ACE. The indices Chao1 and ACE were selected to identify community richness, whereas the indices Shannon and Simpson were used to identify community diversity. Alpha diversity analysis was performed using R software (Version 4.0.3).

Beta diversity analysis, assessed through the Whitaker index calculated with R, was used to evaluate differences in species complexity of samples. Principal Coordinate Analysis (PCoA) based on unweighted unifrac distance matrices, was used to visualize the distance between samples. Moreover, to study the similarity among different samples, a cluster tree was constructed using the Unweighted Pair-group Method with Arithmetic Mean (UPGMA).

To identify biomarkers associated with the different parameters (type of halophytes, soils samples and season), a Linear Discriminant Analysis Effect Size (LDA-LEfSe) method was applied at genus level. Features were considered to be significant with a p-value cutoff of 0.05.

Finally, an Anosim test was used to reveal the community structure differentiation, in order to establish whether the differences between groups were significantly greater than the differences within one group. The test was performed with Vegan package within R.

Results

General features of sequencing

The project allowed sequencing 8173,002 raw paired ends reads of 250 bp, which generated 5940,709 clean reads after merging and trimming. The number of clean reads ranged from 80,740 (in the sample RXA1) to 227,621 (in the sample SXA5) (Supplementary Information Table S1). The percentages of nucleotides with QC > 20 and QC > 30 were 98.84 % and 95.82 % on average. After annotation using the Silva database, a total of 72 phyla, 167 classes, 384 orders, 532 families, 1051 genera and 483 species were identified at > 97 % identity.

Alpha diversity of bacterial populations associated with hydro- and xerohalophytes

Alpha diversity was assessed by different indices (Observed_species, Simpson, Shannon, Chao1, and ACE). Considering all the hydrohalophytes together (PH, RH, SH) as compared to the xerohalophytes together (PX, RX, SX), (Table 2), the first conclusion is the much higher richness and diversity in soil samples (both rhizosphere RH and RX, and bulk soils SH and SX) as compared to endophytes (PH and PX). Moreover, according to all indices, endophyte populations of hydrohalophytes showed a higher Alpha diversity as compared to xerohalophytes, as can be seen in the Supplementary information Figure S2 showing the boxplots of four different indices (Observed_species; Chao1; Shannon; and Simpson). In all cases the value of the median is higher for endophytes of the hydrohalophytes as compared to those of xerohalophytes. However, depending on the index, the difference is little (Observed_species) or much higher (Simpson), probably because the later considers not only richness but also evenness.

Differences of Alpha diversity within groups of halophytes: influence of the plant species

When analyzing the three halophytes in each group, we can obtain Alpha-diversity indices for a particular species, regardless of the sampling site (Table 3). According to most indices, the species with the highest and lowest diversity of endophytes were Salicornia perennis and Halocnemum strobilaceum, respectively. About rhizosphere soils, the conclusion depends on the index considered. The samples RHV corresponding to Suaeda vera displayed the highest alfa diversity according to most indices (observed_species, chao1 and ACE), whereas RXH corresponding to Halocnemum strobilaceum showed the lowest alfa diversity in the rhizosphere soils considering the same indices. Again, Shannon and Simpson indices behaved different, with the highest values corresponding to Anabasis articulata. Regarding the bulk soils, the soil with the highest Alpha diversity was the one near the population 2 of Salicornia perennis (SHS2). Since no plants were growing on this soil, it is supposed not to be influenced by root exudates, so the diversity may reflect a characteristic of the sampling site, although the physicochemical parameters of this soil did not show particularly noticeable parameters (as will be described later). On the other hand, the bulk soil with the lowest alfa diversity was that close to Halocnemum strobilaceum.

Influence of the sampling sites: Alpha diversity in individual samples

The complete data of the various indices related to Alpha diversity (Supplementary information Table S2) show a high variability, reflecting many factors influencing bacterial populations (plant species, location, and plant compartment: tissues, rhizosphere or bulk soils). Regarding endophytes richness (Observed_species), data ranged from 5 in the Suaeda vera sample PHV2 to a maximum of 54 in the PHS1 sample corresponding to Salicornia perennis. Since different results were obtained with different indices, the population of Salicornia perennis PHS1 (Duque) was the one with the highest richness according to three indices (Observed_species, Chao1 and ACE). In contrast, the sample of Suaeda vera PHV2 showed the lowest endophytes’ richness according to the same indices. In general, the values of the indices Shannon and Simpson behaved in a different manner and the sample with the lowest values was Halocnemun strobilaceum, population Carraleona (PXH2).

Samples of rhizosphere soils showed a much higher value of the parameter Observed_species, ranging from 341 in the sample RHV1 (Suaeda vera in the location of Duque) to 81 in RXH1 corresponding to Halocnemum strobilaceum population of Guadalentin. When considering the data of all indices together, the population RHV1 of Suaeda vera (Duque) was the one with the highest Alpha diversity, whereas RXH1 (Halocnemum strobilaceum, Guadalentin) stood out with the lowest Alpha diversity.

Regarding the samples of bulk soil, SHA2 (corresponding to bare soil near the population Estadio of Atriplex portulacoides) showed the highest richness according to all the indices, whereas the sample SXH6 corresponding to bare soil near the population Ecoparque of Halocnemum strobilaceum showed the lowest richness (observed_species, 34) and also according to chao1 and ACE.

Beta diversity

Beta diversity of samples was calculated to assess how ‘similar’ or ‘dissimilar’ the samples were and how they grouped. The Whitaker index (Table 4) indicated slightly higher Beta diversity of hydrohalophytes with respect to xerohalophytes in endophytes and rhizosphere soils, whilst in bulk soils, there was a slightly higher dispersion in the three samples of xerohalophytes, reflecting also the variable nature of the sampling sites (saline pans, saline cliffs and beach areas). Regarding particular species, the highest Beta diversity of endophytes was found for Anabasis articulata and the lowest one for Atriplex glauca. Regarding the rhizosphere soils, the highest Beta diversity was detected in the three populations of Halocnemum strobilaceum and the lowest one between the three populations of Anabasis articulata. It is noteworthy that the rhizosphere soils of A. articulata showed the lowest Beta diversity but the plants showed the highest Beta diversity of endophytes.

The distance between samples was visualized in a 2-D plot using Principal Coordinate Analysis (PCoA) based on unweighted unifrac distances matrices (Fig. 2). These data compute all the species independently of the number of reads to consider also underrepresented taxons. The two components of the axes together accounted for 19.4 % of the variability. The bidimensional plot of data corresponding to the hydrohalophytes together (PH) and the xerohalophytes together (PX) (Fig. 2.A) showed that all plant samples grouped very close, i.e., the similarity between the endophytes populations was much higher than that of soil samples (both rhizosphere soils and bulk soils). On the other hand, we can notice great distance between the rhizosphere and bulk soils, compared to endophytes, confirming again the selective role of the plants at the time of allowing particular bacteria entering their tissues. The PCoA analysis for specific species of halophytes (Fig. 2. B) revealed that the endophytes of all species also grouped together. On the other hand, the rhizosphere soils of Halocnemum strobilaceum were the ones with the highest dispersion, and the bulk soils close to xerohalophytes showed also higher dissimilarity than the bulk soils close to hydrohalophytes.

Taxonomy of endophytes associated to hydro- and xerohalophytes

In the first general analysis, we studied the taxonomy of the types of hydro- and xerohalophytes, regardless of the plant species or sampling locations, i.e., we compared the groups PH (endophytes of hydrohalophytes together) and PX (endophytes of xerohalophytes together). At the level of phylum, Supplementary Information Figure S2 shows those representing over 0.1 % of the relative abundance. Proteobacteria represented the most abundant phylum, with over 75 % and 84 % of total reads in hydro- and xerohalophytes, respectively. The second phylum in every case was Bacteroidota for hydrohalophytes (2.7 % abundance) and Firmicutes for xerohalophytes (with abundance of 2.6 %). Cyanobacteria was the third most represented phylum for both types of halophytes (with 1.2 and 0.8 % relative abundances, respectively). Actinobacteriota was the fourth most abundant phylum in hydrohalophytes (0.8 %), whereas in xerohalophytes this position corresponded to Bacteroidota (0.4 %). Other phyla with abundance over 0.1 % corresponded to Verrucomicrobiota and Acidobacteriota.

At the level of genus (Fig. 3.A), total 93 genera were found to be endophytes in all populations of hydrohalophytes whereas 59 total genera were found in xerohalopytes. We could see a core microbiome of halophytes, both hydro- and xerohalophytes. The set of common genera to both types of halophytes consisted of 10 taxons, whereas other 18 genera were present only in hydrohalophytes and 6 genera were associated specifically to xerohalophytes (Fig. 3.B). For this study, only genera with >0.1 % relative abundance were considered. Regarding the common genera, the most abundant in both types of halophytes was Kushneria. However, there was a difference between hydrohalophytes, for which the second most prevalent genus was Vibrio (only present in hydrohalophytes), and xerohalophytes, in which the second most abundant genus was Halomonas. In addition to these major genera, Pseudoalteromonas and Pseudomonas were found in both types of halophytes although at different abundance. Other genera common to both types of halophytes were Zunongwangia, and Luteolibacter to a lesser extent. In addition, Marinilactibacillus, Bacteroides, Salinisphaera and Psychrobacter were also present in both functional halophytes at lower abundance (< 0.1).

There were genera found specifically or much abundantly in only one of the halophyte types. Regarding the hydrohalophytes, the second most abundant genus was Vibrio, followed by Marinomonas. The intertidal characteristics of the habitats of hydrohalophytes can determine the presence of these marine bacteria, which were almost absent in the xerohalophytes studied. The genus Mesonia was also found at low abundance, together with Thioclava found in hydrohalophytes in Huelva but not in xerohalophytes of the Mediterranean area.

In the case of xerohalophytes, Marinilactibacillus, Salinicola, Alkalicoccus and Alkalibacterium were remarkably more abundant or specific. Besides, we could find three genera of Gram positive haloalkalophilic bacteria, namely, Marinilactibacillus, Alkalicoccus and Alkalibacterium. Also, the Gram-negative genus of haloalkalophilic bacteria Aliidomarina was found at low abundance. Additionally, a cyanobacterium with similarity to Synechococcus_CC9902 was found only in xerohalophytes.

Influence of plant genotype in the recruitment of endophytes (interspecific variation)

In the following study, we looked at the influence of the halophyte species on the population of endophytes (Fig. 3. C). Within the hydrohalophytes, Salicornia perennis showed the most different endo-microbiota. The most prevalent genus was Vibrio, followed by Pseudoalteromonas, Marinomonas and Zunongwangia. By contrast, other genera such as Kushneria and Halomonas, which were very abundant in other species of halophytes, were underrepresented in S. perennis, with relative abundances around 0.1 % or below. By contrast, the other two hydrohalophytes (Atriplex portulacoides and Suaeda vera) showed more similar endophytes profile: for A. portulacoides, Kushneria was the most abundant genus, followed by Marinomonas and Halomonas. For S. vera, Halomonas was the most abundant genus, followed by Zunongwangia and Kushneria. Other minor genera were Mesonia (present in one population of Salicornia perennis) and Buchnera (present in one population of Suaeda vera).

Regarding the xerohalophytes, the three species showed marked abundance of two main genera, Kushneria and Halomonas, followed by Pseudoalteromonas. In addition, these plants specifically attracted Salinicola in elevated proportion. The endomicrobiota of Anabasis articulata stood out for the high relative abundance of Marinilactibacillus and Synechococcus. These genera were absent or low represented in other xerohalophytes. Arthobacter (a genus of Gram-positive rods of the phylum Actynomicecota) and the family Micrococcaceae were also found. The microbiome of Halocnemum strobilaceum was equally dominated by Kushneria and Halomonas. The third genus in abundance, much far from the others, was Marinilactibacillus (with 2.5 % abundance) and Alkalicoccus (1.5 %). Again, we could see a preponderance of Gram-negative endophytes and the presence of these moderately halophilic Gram-positive microorganisms whose optimum pH is between 8 and 9.5 according to the pH of these soils, as we will discuss later.

Intraspecific variations in the core microbiome of hydro- and xerohalophytes: unraveling generalists and specialists

The intraspecific comparison of the endomicrobiota of the individual samples (Figs. 3. D-I) showed that Atriplex portulacoides population 1 (PHA1, Duque) harbored the highest richness, with 24 different OTUs of endophytes, followed by PHA2 (Estadio) with 15 OTUs, and finally the population of Terron (PHA3) showed the lowest diversity with 8 OTUs. Venn diagrams (Fig. 3. D) showed that there were 5 genera found in the three populations, namely: Kushneria, Halomonas, Zunongwangia, Marinomonas and Cutibacterium. These genera could be considered as generalists for this plant species. However, Cutibacterium (formerly Propionibacterium) are residents of the human skin and may reflect contamination although it is surprising their presence in three independent populations of the halophyte. Moreover, C. acnes was preponderantly found in air samples (Li et al., 2024). For its part, Vibrio, which showed a relatively high abundance, was present in PHA1 and PHA3. In addition, 26 genera were found only in one of the three populations. For instance, in the population PHA1, Microbacterium and Brevundimonas were found exclusively, whereas in the population PHA2, Rubivirga and Fulvimarina were present in an exclusive manner.

The 3 populations of Salicornia perennis (Fig. 4. E) showed a core microbiome formed by 4 genera, namely, Vibrio, Pseudoalteromonas, Mesonia and Leeuwenhoekiella. The population PHS1 (Duque) showed the highest richness, with a total of 53 genera identified. Among these, 29 genera were only found in this population and included Oleibacter, Acinetobacter, Fulvivirga, Labrenzia and Alterythrobacter, among others. We can highlight the presence of many Gram-negative bacteria as endophytes of this halophyte. In the population PHS2, 12 genera were found specifically, including Tunicatimonas and Peredibacter. In addition, the population PHS3 showed the lowest number of endophytes (8 genera), three of them were specifically found in this population, namely Synechococcus, Clade_1a and Candidatus_Puniceispirillum.

Influence of environmental conditions (soil properties, soil microbiota and seasonality)

Influence of soil physicochemical parameters

Two types of soil samples were investigated, bulk soils near the plants and rhizosphere soils. In the case of bulk soils, a common sample (SHA1, SHS2, and SHV3 for the hydrohalophytes; and SXG1, SXA1 and SXH1 for the xerohalophytes) was utilized as the control for the halophytes, since it was not associated with any plant. Regarding pH (Table 5), the soil samples corresponding to the hydrohalophytes showed lower pH (between 6.97 and 7.99) as compared to that of xerohalophytes (between 8.03 and 9.13). This can determine the appearance of some genera of haloalkalophilic bacteria in the rhizosphere of xerohalophytes, as we discussed previously. Moreover, the pH of all samples of rhizosphere soils displayed certain alkalinization regarding the same samples of bulk soils (rows highlighted in light gray regarding those in dark gray in Table 5).

The electrical conductivity (EC) displayed a wide range, from 0.20 mS/cm in the sample of Atriplex glauca population Ermita to the maximum value of 32.07 mS/cm in the sample of Halocnemum strobilaceum collected in Calarreona (extremely saline ecvironment). This reveals a high difference in soil salinity. The differences between the electric conductivity between rhizosphere and bulk soils could also depend on the plant. In the case of Suaeda vera, Anabasis articulata and Halocnemum strobilaceum, there was an increase in this parameter respect to bulk soils (ranging from 75 to 100 %). By contrast, Atriplex portulacoides showed a decrease in the EC value regarding the bare soil.

About oxidizable organic matter and oxidizable organic C, the maximum and minimum levels corresponded to Salicornia perennis (Estadio, with 3.55 %) and Atriplex glauca (Ermita, with 0.16 %). In most samples analyzed the content of organic matter increased in the rhizosphere soil respect to bulk soils between 40 and 65 % (Table 5).

The content of total P and available P (Olsen) also showed wide variations, from very poor soils such as the sample RXG2 (corresponding to A. glauca) to very high concentrations in the sample SXA1 (corresponding to A. articulata). In fact, this sample may reflect pollution by some P compounds, since it is close to the industrial zone Fabriquilla.

Regarding the concentration of N, the values oscillated between 0.04 and 0.68 %. Moreover, the concentration of N followed a proportion with organic C, being the ratio C:N between 4 and 8.75, which are very low values in soils.

The concentrations of S also spanned from undetectable levels to the highest concentrations in the three samples of the rhizosphere soils of the xerohalophyte Halocnemum strobilaceum, and in bare soils close to these plants (Table 5).

Soil microbiota

A global analysis at the level of phylum revealed that there was a greater similarity between rhizosphere soils, which grouped together in the UPGMA cluster tree (Fig. 4. A). The most different microbial profile was that of bulk soils of xerohalophytes.

Bulk soils showed different numbers of identified taxa, ranging from 82 OTUs in the soil near to Halocnemum strobilaceum to 3091 in the soil close to Atriplex portulacoides, indicating a large variation in the richness and abundance of the different bulk soils. The results of the 35 most abundant genera in bulk soils (Fig. 4. B) showed an outstanding effect on the sampling site. Since bulk soils are supposed not to be influenced by roots exudates, the results indicate a marked effect of the soil type site since fully different heatmaps were observed in bulk soils near the hydrohalophytes (coastal saltmarshes, Huelva) as compared to soils close to xerohalophytes (particularly, inland saltmarshes of Almería and Murcia).

Bulk soils close to A. portulacoides and S. perennis showed moderate abundance of the archaea Candidatus_Nitrosopumilus and Natronomonas, together with unidentified_Actinomarinales. In general, we can observe a predominance of archaea in bulk soils close to the hydrohalophytes.

Bulk soils surrounding Atriplex glauca showed moderate abundance of Flavobacterium and Pontibacter, and to a lower extent of Allorhizobium and Sphingomonas. In particular, the presence of Ralstonia, Delftia and Stenotrophomonas was outstanding in bulk soils close to Anabasis articulata. Bulk soils close to Halocnenum strobilaceum showed moderate abundance of Sphingomonas. It is important to highlight that in all bulk soils there was very low abundance of genera such as Kushneria, Halomonas, Vibrio, Marinomonas, Marinilactibacillus, Stenotrophomonas, Alkalibacterium, Salinimonas, etc., highly represented in plant tissues.

The rhizosphere behaves like an interface in which some genera present in bulk soils are still represented (Sphingomonas, Candidatus_Nitrosopumilus) together with the presence of genera of endophytes (such as Kushneria, Halomonas, Pseudomonas, Luteolibacter) (Fig. 4.B). The rhizospheres of A. portulacoides were enriched in Bacillus and Candidatus_Nitrosopumilus, which can be related to the attractive role of root exudates for microorganisms of the N cycle.

The rhizosphere soils of Salicornia perennis were particularly enriched in Ignavibacterium, whereas the rhizospheres of Suaeda vera showed many genera that were also found as endophytes, such as Halomonas, Pseudomonas, Sphingomonas, Pontibacter and Cellvibrio. A similar situation was found for the rhizospheres of Atriplex glauca, where Kushneria, Oceanobacillus, Luteolibacter and Salinimicrobium were predominant. Other bacteria with lower representation were Pseudomonas, Cellvibrio, Truepera and Flavobacterium. Since all these bacteria were also found as endophytes, it seems that the plant recruits and selects these genera in the rhizosphere.

The rhizosphere soils of Anabasis articulata were enriched in Candidatus_Nitrosopumilus, Rhodocytophaga, Devosia and the clade Allorhizobium-Neorhizobium-Pararhizobium-Rhizobium. Many of these microorganisms are involved in the cycle of N in the rhizosphere. In addition, Rhodocytophaga is characterized by containing radiation resistant species (Park et al., 2020).

Finally, the rhizosphere of the xerohalophyte Halocnemum strobilaceum population Guadalentín (RXH1) was particularly enriched in several genera of enteric bacteria (Escherichia-Shigella, Bacteroides, Parabacteriodes, Faecalibacterium, Prevotella), probably reflecting fecal contamination due to the activity of animals in this environment or specific sample. However, Bacteroides were also found at low abundance as endophytes of Suaeda vera and Anabasis articulata, in accordance with previous reports in other halophytes (Gao et al., 2022).

Not everyone can go in

The Venn diagrams of Fig. 4.C show that 51 OTUs were common in samples of plant tissues, rhizosphere soils and bare soils of hydrohalophytes. In addition, 62 OTUS were shared between plant tissues and rhizosphere soils, whereas only 4 OTUs were present both in plant tissues and bulk soils. The situation can be visualized as a concentric representation in which the bulk soils harbored in total 6138 OTUs; in the rhizosphere soil there was a recruitment of bacteria and total OTUs identified increased to 13,475 OTUs; whereas only 338 of them entered the three species of hydrohalophytes, suggesting that there was a strict selection for the endophytes (Liu et al., 2017). In particular, 221 OTUS were found only as endophytes, not being present in soils. Analogously, for xerohalophytes, only 23 OTUs were shared between the three compartments, i.e., plant tissues, rhizosphere soils and bulk soils (Fig. 4.D). Thirty-eight OTUs were common between plants and rhizosphere soils and no OTUs were found in common between plants and bulk soils. The scheme of Fig. 4.D shows that 2739 OTUs were present in bare soils, whereas all the xerohalophytes recruited 7336 different OTUs in total and only 407 of these sequences were present inside all the populations of xerohalophytes.

The same study was conducted for particular species (Supplementary Information Figure S3). The number of genera that are simultaneously in bulk soil, rhizosediment and as endophytes varies with the plant species, ranging from 1 genus in Halocnemum strobilaceum to 25 in Salicornia perennis. Besides, we can also observe common genus between the rhizosediment and endophytes which were absent in the bulk soil. These genera ranged from 13 in the case of Atriplex glauca to 32 in the case of Salicornia perennis. Moreover, there were genera shared between the endophytes and the bulk soil but not with the rhizosediment, ranging from 1 (Halocnemum strobilaceum) to 14 (Salicornia perennis). Surprisingly, there were few plant endophytes for each plant species not present in soil samples, neither in rhizosediments, nor in bulk soils.

Seasonality

Samples were collected again in the same sites in the next spring season to test the effect of seasonality. Fig. 5 shows a heatmap of the 35 most abundant genera present in bulk soils and rhizosphere soils in autumn (Fig. 5. A) and spring (Fig. 5. B). First conclusion is the appearance of 9 common genera in both seasons, although at different relative abundance, including Halomonas, Ralstonia, Delftia, Pseudomonas, Devosia, Salinimicrobium, Cellvibrio and Candidatus_Nitrosopumilus. The absence of some genera could be attributed to the fact that it represents the 35 most abundant genera in each season. Furthermore, since bulk soils are supposed not to be influenced by roots exudates, the results indicate a marked effect of the sampling site since fully different heatmaps were observed in bulk soils near the hydrohalophytes (close to the SW coast of Spain, Huelva) as compared to soils close to xerohalophytes (in the more interior zone with respect to the coast, Almería).

The analysis by LDA-LefSe (Fig. 5.C) determined the most different taxa at the level of genus) between the bacterial communities in autumn and spring, both in bulk and rhizosphere soils of both types of halophytes. In bare soils close to hydrohalophytes (SH) in spring there was an enrichment of the ammonia oxidizing archaea Candidatus_Nitrosopumilus, whereas in rhizosphere soils of the hydrohalophytes (RH) there was an enrichment of Cenarchaeum and Haladaptatus, both extremophile archaea (Roh et al., 2010). These results reveal the preference of hydrohalophytes for recruiting archaea in their rhizospheres.

By contrast, in xerohalophytes, many more genera showed enhanced populations in spring. In bulk soils (SX) predominated Sphingomonas, Bradyrhizobium, Deinococcus, Clavibacter, Methylobacterium_Methylorubrum, Acidaminococcus, Fermentimonas and Actinokineospora. Endophytes in the spring season

The 20 most abundant genera of endophytes found in the six halophytes are shown in Fig. 5.D. Main genera previously detected in the autumn season were still conserved, for instance, Halomonas, Kushneria, Zunongwangia, Pseudoalteromonas, Vibrio, Marinilactobacillus, Alkalicoccus, Bacteroides, and Arthrobacter. Another aspect is the existence of microorganisms of the N biogeochemical cycle also in the spring samples, including Candidatus_Nitrosopelagicus, Rhizobium and Aquabacterium. The presence of these genera as plant endophytes is not very common. Moreover, there was outstanding the appearance of new genera not previously detected or detected at much lower abundance in autumn. For instance, among Gram-negatives, Ralstonia, Cupriavidus, Sphingomonas, and Delftia were not found at a relevant abundance as endophytes of plants in the fall samples.

Driving factors of halophytes endophytes: contribution and hierarchy

In order to establish the contribution of different factors, an analysis of similarity (Anosim) was used to see the variability of the inter-group community structure (Table 6). For the hydrohalophytes, the comparison of endophytes with rhizosphere and bulk soils produced high positive R values indicating high dissimilarity (p < 0.05). The same conclusion was reached for xerohalophytes. This points to a predominant role of plant genotype for the selection of endophytes. The comparison of bulk and rhizosphere soils gave low R values, suggesting a higher similarity among the structure of these communities but not at a significant level (p > 0.05). The same situation was found for xerohalophytes. Finally, the comparison of hydro- and xerohalophytes suggested that the similarity of the communities decreased in the direction plants>rhizosphere soils >bulk soils, although only the data of rhizosphere soils were significant. In fact, the most similar groups according to the value of R were endophytes of hydro- and xerohalophytes, although the value of p did not allow us to conclude this significantly.

Discussion

The analysis of the bacterial communities associated to plants is critical to understand their adaptation to environmental conditions (Camacho-Sánchez et al., 2022). In particular, halophytes are known to recruit specific microbial populations which help plants to tolerate salinity stress (Xiong et al., 2020; Kumar et al., 2023). In this context, our work has approached the study of the factors shaping the bacterial populations associated to two functional halophytes.

Analysis of Alpha and Beta diversity

For hydrohalophytes, data of Alpha diversity followed the trend rhizosphere soils ˃ bare soils ˃ endophytes, whereas in xerohalophytes the sequence was bare soils ˃ rhizosphere soils ˃ endophytes. Bacterial richness and diversity in the rhizosphere was higher under the influence of the plants due to the attractive action of root exudates (Vives-Peris et al., 2020). Similar results were reported for other species, for instance Suaeda corniculata and S. salsa (Peng et al., 2022). However, diverse indices consider different approaches for estimating Alpha diversity, since some consider equally more and less represented taxa, whereas others consider both the presence of a particular taxa and its relative abundance. In this regard, higher richness in the rhizosphere soi of hydrohalophytes (with higher chao1 and Observed_species) but higher diversity was observed in xerohalophytes (according to Simpson and Shannon indices).

On the other hand, only a small fraction of all these microorganisms present in the rhizosphere finally enters the plant as endophytes. This means that there is a second selective process by which the plant allows the entrance of only certain bacteria (Etesami et al., 2014; Hereira-Pacheco et al., 2021). Again, a higher Alpha diversity of endophytes could be found in hydrohalophytes compared to xerohalophytes, which could indicate a tighther selection of endophytes by the later. Considering particular species, Salicornia perennis standing out with the highest endophytes diversity, whereas Halocnemum strobilaceum was the xerohalophyhte with the lowest diversity. This can be also related to the lower number of reads for this plant, since the rarefaction curve for these samples still showed a steeped profile (Supplementary information Figure S4), and also by the fact that many reads could not be assigned to a particular taxon at 97 % identity in the Silva database.

Regarding Beta diversity of endophytes, the populations of hydrohalophytes showed higher dissimilarity. This could reflect a wider capacity of interaction with many different microorganisms in these functional halophytes, depending on the surrounding soil, whereas xerohalophytes may be more selective regarding the microorganisms allowed to enter their tissues (Etesami et al., 2014; Hereira-Pacheco et al., 2021). Accordingly, certain specificity of particular halophytes for specific genera of bacteria have been described (Afzal et al., 2019). For instance, Acinetobacter, Arthrobacter, Bacillus, Kocuria, Oceanobacillus, Paenibacillus, Pseudomonas and Virgibacillus were relevant in Cressa cretica, Salicornia brachiate, Suadea nudiflora and Sphaeranthus indicus grown in coastlines (Arora et al., 2014). By contrast, Altererythrobacter, Hoeflea, Labrenzia, Marinilactibacillus, Microbacterium, Salinicola and Vibrio were the most represented genera in Halimione portulacoides from saltmarshes (Fidalgo et al., 2016). Also, a higher Beta diversity was found among the rhizosphere soils of hydrohalophytes with regard to those of xerohalophytes, whereas in bulk soils, there was a slightly higher dispersion in the three samples of xerohalophytes, reflecting also the variable nature of the sampling sites (saline pans, saline cliffs and beach areas).

Environmental factors

We analyzed the relationship of the different populations of halophytes with the physicochemical characteristics of the soils and soils microbiota.

Soil characteristics

Soil pH is one of the factors most driving microbial populations (Fan et al., 2018). Whereas pH values in sediments of hydrohalophytes were around neutrality, alkalinization was observed in the soils of xerohalophytes. This fact can be related to the activity of both the microbial communities and the composition of plant root exudates. In the microbiota of xerohalophytes, the presence of genera such as Alkalicoccus, Alkalibacterium and Aliidomarina can correlate with the increase of pH (see Supplementary Information Figure S5). In fact, several works describe the recruitment of specific microorganisms by plants in alkaline soils (Lei et al., 2025; Lopes et al., 2021). Other factor with the foremost influence is the electric conductivity, which reflects the degree of salinity and spanned from 0.2 to 32 mS/cm in the different samples. Sometimes, an increase in salinity in the vicinity of plants as reported for other halophytes such as Suaeda salsa (Wang et al., 2021a), whereas other times there is a decrease in salinity. In fact, some halophytes have been proposed for soil desalinization due to their capacity to absorb salt from rhizosphere soil by a mechanism of salt accumulation in the plant (Hu et al., 2024).

Regarding the organic matter, in general, soils of the halophytes were richer than those of xerohalophytes, probably due to the fluvial-marine nature of the estuaries which favor the accumulation of organic matter in intertidal sediments. In addition, an increase of the content of organic matter was found in rhizosphere soils regarding the corresponding unvegetated bulk soils. The secretion of plant roots exudates contributes to the increase of organic matter and supports the growth of microbial populations, whereas the recycling activity of microorganisms favour plant growth and further root exudation (Lin et al., 2025; Pausch et al., 2024). Concerning the N content, the soils showed very low levels of the C:N ratio (between 5 and 9). Values below 20 promote N mineralization and make N available to plants, whereas values over 30 promote N immobilization by microorganisms (Brust, 2019). Accordingly, microorganisms of the N biogeochemical cycle were identified in the rhizosphere of plants and as endophytes, particularly ammonia oxidizing archaea.

Soil microbiota

The microbiota of soils reflected those differences in the physicochemical properties. Bulk soils close to hydrohalophytes were particularly enriched in archaea, such as Candidatus_Nitrosopumilus and Natronomonas. Candidatus_Nitrosopumilus is an archaeon found in marine environments (Qin et al., 2016). They are key microorganisms that require salt for growth and are involved in the N cycle since they oxidize ammonia to nitrite (Wright and Lehtovirta-Morley, 2023). Natronomonas is a genus of haloalkaliphilic archaea (Falb et al., 2005; Yin et al., 2020). In contrast, bulk soil close to xerohalophytes were enriched in robust and adaptive genera of bacteria, some of which display plant growth promoting properties. Flavobacterium is traditionally known for its pathogenic character for fish (Wahli and Madsen, 2018). However, some recently described strains of the plant rhizosphere have confirmed their role as PGP, biocontrol and alleviation of plant stress (Seo et al., 2024). The genus Pontibacter, belonging to the family Hymenobacteraceae (phylum Bacteroidetes) is known for its adaptation to extreme environments (salt marshes, deserts) and their potential as plant growth-promoting rhizobacteria (Dastager et al., 2011). In addition, Sphingomonas is common in soils and are Gram-negative facultative rods with high versatility and the capacity of degrading xenobiotic compounds or immobilize heavy metals, whilst some strains display plant growth promoting activities (Asaf et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2022; Mayozon et al., 2023). For its part, the clade Allorhizobium-Neorhizobium-Pararhizobium-Rhizobium comprises several genera of diazotrophs that fix nitrogen in nodules formed on roots of legumes (de Lajudie et al., 1998). Other bacteria highly abundant in bulk soils of xerohalophytes were Ralstonia, Delftia and Stenotrophomonas. These are also Gram-negative rods with high metabolic versatility. Ralstonia can be related to plant disease. R. solanaceum is a plant pathogen widely distributed and causes wilt and important yield losses in tomato, pepper, potato, tobacco, etc. (Li et al., 2023). Delftia comprises diverse strains with agricultural relevance, including plant-growth promotion, and industrial application, bioremediation of hydrocarbon-contaminated soils or heavy metals immobilization (Braña et al., 2016; Bhat et al., 2022). Stenotrophomonas is a genus widely found in soils, particularly in association with plants and they show promotion of plant growth and protect plants from attack of phytopathogens such as Ralstonia pseudosolanacearum (Tanaka et al., 2025). Moreover, they have an important ecological role in nitrogen and sulfur cycles, can degrade many xenobiotics and have the potential for metal bioremediation (Ryan et al., 2009).

In summary, soils of the Atlantic coast close to hydrohalophytes were enriched in marine halophilic archaea, whereas versatile and adaptive bacteria were predominant in soils of the mediterranean area (close to xerohalophytes). As for metabolic functions, it was noteworthy the abundance of taxons related to N cycle in soils close to both types of halophytes, while in soils close to xerohalophytes, there were metabolic versatile bacteria able to thrive in harsh environments, degrade xenobiotic compounds, tolerate heavy metals and interact with plants.

Some of the microorganisms found in bulk soils also extend to the rhizosphere of functional halophytes. Candidatus_Nitrosopumilus and Bacillus were prevalent in the rhizospheres of hydrohalophytes. As commented above, Candidatus_Nitrosopumilus is an ammonia-oxidizing archaea and many Bacillus strains display N2 fixing capacity, besides a great number of other plant growth promoting abilities and biocontrol activity (Tiwari et al., 2019). Moreover, Bacillus species form spores that can survive under harsh conditions for long periods of time due to the formation of spores (Hashem et al., 2019; Valencia-Marín et al., 2024). The rhizosphere of Salicornia perennis stood out for the relative abundance of Ignavibacterium, a genus within the phylum Chlorobi that participates in the decomposition of complex polymers such as cellulose, hemicellulose and chitin in the rhizosphere (Bei et al., 2021).

Rhizosphere soils associated to xerohalophytes correlated with high abundance of robust bacteria such as Pseudomonas and Sphingomonas, together Halomonas and Kushneria, found as endophytes. The rhizosphere of Anabasis articulata was enriched in microorganisms of the N cycle: Devosia is a genus widely distributed in many different environments (Talwar, el al., 2020). Some species are diazotrophs and able to form nodules with aquatic legumes (Rivas et al., 2003). The clade Allorhizobium-Neorhizobium-Pararhizobium-Rhizobium are typically endosymbionts within legume nodules and Candidatus_Nitrosopumilus is an ammonia-oxidizing archaeon (Kraft and Canfield, 2022). These results point to a very active N metabolism in the rhizosphere soils of xerohalophytes.

Identification of a core of endophytes in hydro- and xerohalophytes

In spite of the high abundance of microorganisms in the rhizosphere, not everyone can go inside the plants. Three different filters have been proposed for plants controlling the endophyte populations entering from the rhizosphere; i.e., the exudation of particular compounds from roots attract some microorganisms while suppress others; rhizoplane acts as a “gate-keeper”, the bacteria that cannot effectively colonize the roots do not enter the endosphere; and finally, the plant immune system allows the survival of particular bacteria by suppressing the defense response (Liu et al., 2017). In addition, some endophytes can be vertically transmitted through seeds (Sascha et al., 2014; Sanz-Puente et al., 2025) or enter the plants through the stomata, flowers, wounds, etc., although these entrance mechanisms are less effective compared to the entrance of rhizosphere microorganisms through the rhizoplane (Liu et al., 2017).

The comparison of endophytes in both types of functional halophytes revealed the presence of common and specific genera. The core of genera was mainly constituted by Kushneria, Halomonas, Pseudoalteromonas and Zunongwangia. These are Gram-negative moderate halophilic bacteria frequently isolated from saline environments (Sánchez-Porro et al., 2009; ; Dragojević et al., 2023). Many species within these genera are frequently isolated from the rhizosphere or as endophytes of halophytes (Navarro-Torre et al., 2018; 2019; Meinzer et al., 2023; Fidalgo et al., 2017). For instance, Halomonas can tolerate salt concetrations of 12 %, are catalase-positive, which allows them to manage oxidative stress, and possess salt-in and salt-out systems allowing them to adapt to hypersaline environments (Sánchez-Porro et al., 2009).

By their part, Pseudoalteromonas and Pseudomonas, both halotolerant Gram-negative, are ubiquitous and highly adaptive endophytes (Parrilli et al., 2021; Alattas et al., 2024). Some other genera show very low representation, including Luteolibacter, Gram-negative rods that display a broad capacity for the degradation of various organic compounds (Kim et al., 2015; Shen et al., 2023). Recently, the species L. rhizospherae was isolated from the rhizosphere of Ulmus (Shen et al., 2023). At this point, we would like to draw attention to the core endophytes of both hydro- and xerohalophytes were Gram-negative bacteria, which seems to indicate a preference of halophytes, both from intertidal sites and inland saltmarshes, for hosting diderms.

Looking specifically at the hydrohalophytes, it is outstanding that Vibrio is the second most abundant genus. Vibrio are Gram-negative marine halophiles with important implications for the C cycle in estuaries and marshes, in addition to the pathogenic nature of some species for humans and animals (Grimes, 2020). Furthermore, Vibrio spartinae and V. palustris were described as endophytes of halophytes and show plant growth promoting activities (Navarro-Torre et al., 2017; Mateos-Naranjo et al., 2020). Marinomonas and Mesonia were also present in hydrohalophytes. They are moderately halophilic bacteria isolated from marine and tidal environments known for their environmental adaptation to low temperature and high pressure, as well as high metabolic versatility and production of secondary metabolites (Nedashkovskaya et al., 2003; Sung et al., 2017; Xue et al., 2022). Marinomonas spartinae was first isolated from the halophyte Spartina maritima grown in the Odiel saltmarshes (Lucena et al., 2016), a region close to Duque and Terron sampling sites. Also, Thioclava was found in the population of Terron. It belongs to the family Rhodobacteriaceae formed by marine sulfur-oxidizing bacteria (Sorokin et al., 2005). The presence of metal contamination (particularly As, Cu, Fe, and Cd) of these sediments in the Odiel saltmarshes (Mesa et al., 2016) probably favored the presence of these sulfur-oxidizing bacteria with 17 metal resistant determinants (Yin et al., 2023). In this sense, the intertidal characteristics of the habitats of hydrohalophytes can determine the presence of these all these genera of marine halotolerant bacteria, which were absent in the xerohalophytes studied. These bacteria are characterized by the tolerance towards metals and/or xenobiotics and can help plants to counteract the effect of toxic compounds in coastal environments (Mesa et al., 2015, 2019a, 2019b).

Regarding the xerohalophytes, it was outstanding the specific present of the Gram-negative Salinicola (Halomonadaceae), as described for other members of the Amaranthaceae (Mora-Ruiz et al., 2015; Fidalgo et al., 2019). Nevertheless, one of the most remarkable facts concerning the endomicrobiota of xerohalophytes is the presence of several genera of Gram-positive bacteria, including Marinilactibacillus, Alkalicoccus and Alkalibacterium. Xerohalophyyes thrive in arid saline environments. Due to the thick peptidoglycan layer and other mechanisms such as sporulation, Gram-positive bacteria can better tolerate desiccation, as compared to their Gram-negative counterparts (Bale et al., 1993). In addition, all these bacteria are known for their moderate halophilic character and also for their tolerance towards alkaline pH (up to 9.5–10.5, depending on the species; Ishikawa et al., 2009; Zhao et al., 2017; Yoon et al., 2004). Also, the Gram-negative genus Aliidomarina is reported to tolerate salt and alkali (Yang et al., 2022). In this regard, we must consider the higher pH values found in the soils of the xerohalophytes (discussed later), which could explain the existence of these endophytes. On the other hand, we would also like to carry attention of the presence of cyanobacteria as endophytes of xerohalophytes. Although this fact is documented (Dragojević et al., 2023), to our knowledge the genus Synechococcus has not been described as a plant symbiont. Cyanobacteria can establish symbiosis with plants and provide additional N acting as plant growth promoting bacteria (Álvarez et al., 2023).

Inter and intraspecific variations in the endomicrobiota of functional halophytes

The study investigated also the interspecific and intraspecific variations in the populations of endophytes. The specific endophytes found in one population may reflect the adaptation of plants to the conditions of particular environments. For example, whitin the hydrohalophytes, the species with the most different profiles nf endophytes was Salicornia perennis, with elevated abundance of Vibrio and Pseudoalteromonas. In addition, this hydrohalophyte harbored specific endophytes, including Oleibacter, Fulvivirga, Alterythrobacter and Labrenzia, all of them Gram negative marine bacteria (Teramoto et al., 2011; Bae et al., 2022; Bibi et al., 2014; Kwon et al., 2007). Labrenzia has important roles in marine ecology and form biofilms, which could be involved in plant root colonization (Zaynab et al., 2021). For its part, Oleibacter and Alterythrobacter display capacities for oil degradation or metal resistance, suggesting that they could help plants to adapt to polluted coanvironments (Teramoto et al., 2011; Cheng et al., 2017). Peredibacter is a genus of bacterial predators previously classified as Ddellovibrio and Bacteriovorax (Davidov and Jurkevitch, 2004). Peredibacter was described in the rhizosphere of millet (Mattoo et al., 2021), but to our knowledge, this genus has not been previously described as endophyte and can have importance in the competence given their bacterial predator nature.

Within the xerohalophytes, Anabasis articulata was the species showing the most dissimilar endophytes profile harboring Marinilactibacillus, Arthrobacter and Synechoccocus. The species Arthrobacter pascens was isolated from the rhizosphere of Suaeda fruticosa and showed active phosphate solubilization and bacteriocin and siderophore production (Ullah and Bano, 2015). Some strains can display ACC deaminase activity, alleviating plant stress under saline conditions (Barnawal et al., 2014). These characteristics reflect its role a plant growth promoting bacterium. Marinilactibacillus are also Gram-positive bacteria from marine environments (Ishikawa et al., 2003) which combine moderate halophilic character with resistance to alkaline pH and low temperatures, together with lactic acid fermentation ability. On the other hand, Synechococcus was only present in A. articulata. The phylum Cyanobacteria, when present, was at very low abundance (Dragojević et al., 2023; Yamamoto et al., 2020).

In conclusion, a core microbiome of both, hydro- and xero-halophytes, can be deduced, composed of moderate halophilic Gram-negative bacteria Kushneria, Halomonas, Pseudoalteromonas, Pseudomonas and Zunongwangia. In addition to these common genera, the core microbiome of hydrohalophytes was enriched in Vibrio and Marinomonas, marine halophilic species abundant in intertidal habitats. For its part, the core microbiome of xerohalophytes included also Marinilactibacillus, Alkalicoccus, Alkalibacterium, and Aliidomarina, the three later probably reflecting adaptation to haloalkaline environments, together with Arthobacter and Bacillus, Gram-positives not found in hydrohalophytes and according to the arid nature of these environments. These results could suggest that these genera do not constitute a core microbiome of this species but were maybe related to specific soil physiochemistry, flooding, pollution or other environmental circumstances, and can be considered as specialists. In Kandelia obovata, for example, a core genome of generalists was present in most plants but with low abundance, whereas there was high abundance of specialists which were involved in the optimal adaptation of plants to specific conditions, particularly high temperature (Zhou et al., 2023; Nethmini et al., 2025). These particular rare endophytes, i.e., taxa that show very low abundance, can be determinant in the adaptation of plants to particular changing conditions in the environment (Harrison et al., 2021). Moreover, we also want to highlight the high amount of new bacterial species described in the microbiome of halophytes. These bacteria are considered as potential biotools for improving the resilience of diverse plants, including crops, towards salinity (Mesa et al., 2015; Zhou et al., 2017; Redondo-Gómez et al., 2021; Dif et al., 2022).

Seasonality

The appearance of some new genera among the most abundant could be related to adaptation of the plants to seasonal environmental conditions such as mobilization of contaminants, availability of nutrients, availability of water (which condition the salinity), temperature, biotic stress, etc. (Ge et al., 2025).

Minor changes were observed in bulk soils and rhizosphere soils of hydrohalophytes, with prevalence of ammonia oxidizing archaea and Actinomarinales. Probably the tidal influence buffer the influence of seasons.

By contrast, greater changes were observed in bare soils close to xerohalophytes. It was noticeable the appearance of some putative plant pathogens, such as Clavibacter and Ralstonia. Clavibacter is a genus of Gram-positive rods, and the species C. michiganensis is a phytopathogen causing the bacterial canker of tomato (Nandi et al., 2018). Contrastingly, Actinekineospora is a genus that contains some biocontrol species such as A. fastidiosa, whose secondary metabolites have insecticidal activities (Raguvaran et al., 2025). In this particular, it is likely that the plants attract these bacteria as a protection against insect predation. Moreover, the presence of Sphingomonas is common in rhizosphere soils. These are Gram-negative facultative rods with high versatility, capacity of degradation of xenobiotic compounds or immobilization of heavy metals and plant growth promoting activities (Asaf et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2022; Mazoyon et al., 2023). Other robust bacteria were Deinococcus, able to tolerate high indices of radioactivity (Liu et al., 2023). The presence of Fermentimonas and Methylobacterium-Methylorubrum reveals the role of soil microbiota in the C cycle. Fermentimonas is a genus of facultative anaerobe Gram-negative rods able to produce methane in anaerobic conditions (Tan et al., 2025). For its part, Methylobacterium is a genus of Gram-negative rods able to use one-C compounds such as methanol. Moreover, many species are endophytes of plants and display PGP abilities, including the ability to fix nitrogen (Wang et al., 2021b; Zhang et al., 2021). Besides, some other microorganisms involved in the N biogeochemical cycle were abundant, including Bradyrhizobium (a symbiotic diazotroph in nodules of legumes; Ormeño-Orrillo and Martínez-Romero, 2019). Acidaminococcus is a genus of Gram-negative cocci able to grow on aminoacids as the sole source of energy, which could reflect the attraction by root exudates (Rogosa, 1969). These data reflect complex dynamics in bare soils close to xerohalophytes, particularly focused in tolerance towards harsh environmental conditions and high versatility in the use of different C and N sources, which was reinforced in spring.

In the rhizosphere of xerohalophytes, several genera display high abundance in spring. Cellvibrio is a genus known for its ability to degrade cellulose, hemicellulose, xylan and chitin, probably providing additional organic matter in these poor soils (Wu and He, 2015; Gardner, 2024). As we commented previously, Pseudomonas is one of the most abundant microorganisms in the rhizosphere of plants, including halophytes, and many strains display excellent PGP and biocontrol activities (Leontidou et al., 2020; Flores-Duarte et al., 2022; Alattas et al., 2024), which can counteract the presence of plant pathogens such as Clavibacter and Ralstonia. Klebsiella was also described in the rhizosphere of several plants (Liu et al., 2016; Yu et al., 2025). The strain Klebsiella sp. D5A isolated from maize rhizosphere was tolerant towards salt and alkali (Liu et al., 2016), the same characteristics as the soils of xerohalophytes in our study. In general, we have observed a richer and more diverse microbial population associated with xerohalophytes in spring, with a wider functionality including salt tolerance, tolerance to environmental stresses (xenobiotics, heavy metals, radiation), multiple functions in the C and N cycles, and recycling complex polymers in the rhizosphere, as well as putative plant growth promoting and biocontrol activities. It is likely that the plants, through roots exudates, can alter the species composition and diversity of the rhizosphere communities and enhance beneficial metabolic pathways to ensure growth during the spring season even in these poor soils.

The population of endophytes is affected to a lesser degree for seasonality, with many genera conserved since autumn. In both functional halophytes, Ralstonia, Delftia, Sphingomonas, Cupriavidus and Aquabacterium appeared as new endophytes. Ralstonia can be related to plant disease (Wang et al., 2023) suggesting a higher biotic stress in the spring season. Cupriavidus metallidurans is related to metal tolerance and immobilization (Mergeay et al., 2003; Janssen et al., 2010). This bacterium can interact with plants and alleviate metal stress (Clavero-León et al., 2021). Furthermore, some species have plant growth promoting activities, including C. plantarum (Arroyo-Herrera et al., 2020). Delftia and Sphingomonas also include plant-growth promoting strains and can participate in pollution bioremediation (Braña et al., 2016; Bhat et al., 2022; Asaf et al., 2020), whereas Aquabacterium is a denitrifying bacterium in soils with low C/N ratio similar to those found in our study (Xu et al., 2022).

Concerning type-specific genera, in hydrohalophytes it is noteworthy the presence of Rhizobium, a typical nitrogen fixing symbiont of legumes with good PGP properties (Pereira et al., 2024; Bartoli et al., 2020), whereas Candidatus_nitrosopelagicus is an ammonia-oxidizing archaeon found in open ocean (Santoro et al., 2015). It seems clear the preference of these halophytes for recruiting microorganisms related to the N cycle, both bacteria and archaea. By contrast, in xerohalophtes we could detect the presence of some Gram positives rarely found in fall including Bacillus, Micrococcus and Bifidobacterium. Bacillus is a widely reported plant endophyte with many plant growth promoters and biocontrol species, whereas some strains of Micrococcus were also endophytes of plants (García-Cárdenas et al., 2022). To our knowledge, the presence of Bifidobacterium as plant endophytes was not reported and may reflect fecal animal residues, since they are well known members of the gut microbiota.

Limitations of the work

On the one hand, the collection of only one representative sample of bare soil for each plant species can superopose the effect of plant species effects with site/plot. However, since the bare soil is not associated to any plant species, the three samples of hydrohalophytes (collected all of them in near areas of the Atlantic coast) and the three samples of bare soils of xerohalophytes (Mediterranean coast), the samples of bare soils can be considered as a triplicate, although the collection of three samples in each location would have increased the robustness of the data.

On the other hand, in spite that all the statistics was referred to the sample with the least reads, the number of reads was lower especially in the populations of Halocnemum strobilaceum, which can be seen in the corresponding rarefaction curves that show a steep profile (Supplementary Information Figure S4). This value probably reflects that many species remain to be discovered, since the rarefaction curve for this particular sample showed a steeped profile. In addition, we observed we detected a higher percentage of genera classified as “other”, especially in the spring season, due to the lack of proper identification of some sequences in the Silva database.

Conclusions

According to the data presented we can conclude that the microbial populations of endophytes of halophytes can be both genotype-specific and site-specific. The halophytes strictly select the endophytes among the soil bacterial populations. Moreover, some endophytes were common to both types of halophytes, particularly moderate halophilic bacteria such as Kushneria, Halomonas, Pseudoalteromonas and Zunongwangia. The presence of particular and/or minor genera can represent specialists for a better adaptation to specific environmental conditions of the sampling sites. For instance, haloalkalophilic bacteria (Alkalicoccus, Alkalibacterium, Aliidomarina) were found mostly as endophytes of xerohalophytes according to alkaline soil pHs. In addition, some Gram positives such as Bacillus and Arthrobacter were more abundant in xerohalophytes. By contrast, marine halophilic bacteria (Vibrio, Marinomonas) were associated with hydrohalophytes. The selection process for endophytes starts in the rhizosphere, an interface in which bacteria from surrounding bulk soils and endophytes coexist. Whereas in hydrohalophytes, the rhizospheres were enriched in halotolerant bacteria and ammonia oxidizing archaea, in xerohalophytes, they predominated microorganisms adapted to haloalkaline soils and harsh environments (tolerant to xenobiotics, heavy metals, radiation, etc.). Several genera, even at moderate or low abundance, have known plant growth promoting activities whereas others seem to have key roles in the C and N metabolisms in soils and in recycling the organic matter in the rhizosphere. On the other hand, seasonality had a larger effect on microbial populations in soils than on endophytes, despite the fact that some new endophytes were detected in the spring season such as Ralstonia, probably reflecting biotic stress, together with Delftia, Sphingomonas or Cupriavidus which may be involved in plant defense against abiotic and/or biotic stresses.

A model for the contribution of plant genotype and environmental factors to the associated microbiome of hydro- and xerohalophytes is depicted in Fig. 6. Considering the data presented it is proposed that the type of halophyte (hydro- or xerohalophyte) and the particular plant species are the main driving factors in shaping the structure of the associated microbial community, followed by soil properties and soil microbiota, whereas the season has a lower contribution, affecting more the soil communities, whereas the plant endophytes were affected in a lesser extent.

Declaration of competing interest

Halophytes are of great interest for their ecosystemic benefits and valuable secondary metabolites. However, they are threatened by factors associated with climate change and human activity. In this context, their associated microbial communities are crucial for their survival. Despite that, there are no comparative studies on microbial communities associated two types of functional halophytes: hydro- and xero-halophytes, living in marine and arid saline environments. This study aimed to fill this gap of knowledge by studying the microbial populations associated to six halophytes in two regions and two seasons. The results showed strong attraction of bacteria and archaea to the rhizosphere, although further tight selection limits the number of endophytes. These microorganisms play vital roles, including promoting plant growth, participating in the carbon and nitrogen metabolisms, and recycling organic matter. Moreover, many of the bacteria associated to halophytes are important agrotools to improve crop resilience under increasing soil salinity.
