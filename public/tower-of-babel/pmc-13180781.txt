Knowledge Traffic in Learning Health Systems: A Conceptual Framework for Organizational Phenotyping and Translational Governance
Authors: Octavian C. Ioachimescu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundThe translational journey from biomedical discovery to population health is increasingly complex, requiring coordinated navigation across scientific, clinical, and operational domains. While the Learning Health System (LHS) is a unifying platform developed to accelerate knowledge cycles, evidence generation, testing, and application, its role in governing translational flows across different organization types remains insufficiently specified.ObjectiveThis perspective article proposes a conceptual, structured approach and taxonomy that links translational spectrum (T 0–T 5) to the health system phenotype and identifies the LHS as the governance and operational layer that enables safe, efficient, and speed‐appropriate translation from T 4 to T 5, where competing translational pathways converge. The proposed phenotyping schema is aimed at optimizing LHS configuration and translational performance.MethodsWe synthesize prior work on translational science, LHS design, and organizational capability into an analytic typology (LHS1–LHS4) based on innovation generation and adoption, and their specific investigative capabilities. This new organizational typology shifts the LHS concept from a descriptive model to an actionable design and governance tool, enabling organizational leaders to align infrastructure, strategy, and translational intent.ResultsHealth systems differ in how they generate, test, and adopt new knowledge, yet all require explicit governance, prioritization, data infrastructure, stakeholder engagement, and improvement capacity to move evidence into routine care and population impact. The LHS1–LHS4 are presented as descriptive phenotypes with distinct capability profiles and operationalizations, highlighting the central role of LHS governance in aligning priorities, resources, and implementation pathways.ConclusionsBy explicitly linking system phenotype to translational function, leaders can build a robust foundation for operational decision‐making and targeted investments, while clarifying the rights, rules, and permissions needed for safe knowledge movement, and offering a basis for evaluation, implementation, and further empirical testing.

Introduction

Even for agile, high‐reliability, learning or functionally mature organizations, fast‐paced technological advances and breakthrough innovations represent significant barriers to nimble adaptation, successful implementation and dissemination, up‐scaling, integration, monetization or impact on its constituents. Translational research is a multi‐disciplinary and pluri‐transitional endeavor, which moves research findings across a complex spectrum constituted by various settings, specific languages, different stages, distinct methodologies and study designs. The starting point or first transition is represented by the original ideation, inception or funding opportunity (T 0), progressing to laboratory studies (T 1), then to human specimen‐based translational (T 2) and clinic‐based studies (T 3), all the way to community members (T 4) and general population (T 5). The latter transition or translation is often accomplished via health policies, laws, regulations or societal norm changes.

The Learning Health System (LHS) has emerged as a unifying paradigm to address this complexity by integrating data, science, and practice into continuous cycles of improvement. In an LHS, knowledge generation and application are embedded within routine operations, enabling iterative learning and adaptation [1, 2, 3]. Despite its conceptual appeal, the LHS literature remains largely descriptive, with limited specification of how organizational structures operate as operational governance systems, particularly at critical translational joints. One such junction occurs at the transition from T 4 (evidence in real‐world clinical settings) to T 5 (population‐level adoption and impact), where innovations must compete for attention, resources, and implementation bandwidth. At this interface, health systems must make explicit decisions regarding prioritization, adoption, scaling, and sustainment. These decisions are not uniform across organizations but are shaped by the system's underlying capabilities, mission, and assigned roles in the broader innovation ecosystem.

This perspective article presents a conceptual framework that addresses this gap via three distinct contributions: (1) it reframes the translational continuum as a complex map or network of intersecting roads rather than a linear pipeline; (2) it proposes a four‐phenotype taxonomy (LHS1–LHS4) for classifying LHS by their position on the map; and (3) it argues that the LHS framework can serve as the governance architecture (the traffic roundabout) that optimizes knowledge flow at the T 4–T 5 transition for all LHS phenotypes. As such, this work aims to reposition the LHS as a structured conceptualization of a practical, decision‐oriented framework for aligning translational strategy with organizational identity and capability.

Translational Spectrum: Path, Avenue or Speedway?

Translational research is an investigative endeavor that aims to cross a particular step in the development spectrum for specific diagnostic capabilities, therapeutic targets, or diseases. Its goal is to bridge the gap between scientific discoveries and practical, real‐world applications in medicine and health(care) [4]. Translational research often involves collaboration between scientists, innovators, clinicians, patients, family, community, and society members, policymakers, local, state, and federal government officials, and other stakeholders. This broad spectrum of expertise, engagement, action, and feedback loop has been at the foundation of what was coined as “team science” approach. The traditional, linear pipeline model for translational research can be mended by replacing it with a serial‐parallel system of communicating vessels, each one of them representing different types of research (laboratory, translational, clinical, public health, population science), and benefiting from customized local firepower or support systems to ensure adequate resources for bidirectional flow of information and knowledge exchange between neighboring loops [5, 6].

This perspective article suggests that the translational continuum from T 0 to T 5 is not a meandrous, linear passageway in the unknown, neither a large, busy urban boulevard, nor a straight highway designed for speed. Instead, it looks more like a complex set of intersecting avenues, roads or streets. The trip is not a small and dangerous trail in the wilderness. A small discovery track is likely not very desirable, either: we often need larger throughways for scaling up, for moving along other people, tools and equipment. In fact, the system may benefit from well‐designed and timed intersections, thoughtful discussions, collaborative meetings, or transdisciplinary “cross‐pollinations”, leveraging different areas of expertise, various technological approaches, or expanded knowledge translation capabilities. Conversely, one may wish that the journey of innovation was a straight speedway or highway (Figure 1). While this is very‐very rarely the case, some (if not most) discoveries require us to deliberately slow down, so that certain favorable characteristics are weighted, enhanced, prioritized, capitalized, or highlighted, while existing or emergent flaws or pitfalls are not overlooked, minimized, or underestimated. Some innovations need thorough testing and retesting in various conditions and separate settings. The innovation journey also needs to intersect with other scientific discovery trips, and a highway does not provide the best model for that.

Graphic representation of three conceptualizations of the journey for research and discovery, i.e., trail (left), avenue (top) or speedway (right), all with final outputs arriving to the roundabout (at T 4), the final organizational platform ultimately responsible for delivering the benefit to the members of the society (T 5).

We assert that the dominant mode in the discovery journey is represented by complex road trees and street maps, with many one‐ or two‐way, small or medium‐sized avenues intersecting at various angles (i.e., with knowledge flows that are slower or faster), and at different points in time. Some streets are disappointingly slow. Some roads lead to dead ends, as some lines of investigation may be fruitless or may become so over time. Certain ways successfully continue after well‐lit, traffic lights equipped and clearly signaled intersections to make it from inception, initial ideation or funding opportunity (T 0—see Figures 1 and 2), progressively to T 1, T 2, T 3, ultimately to T 4 intersections. The T 4 transition is the final phase in the organizational roundabout of competing priorities and resources for the respective healthcare system, research and development division, health sciences university or innovation hub (Figure 2). The intersection requires optimized design and explicit navigational rules, rights and permissions, so that the innovation does not stall, crash or gets deprioritized, and it benefits from a swift transition to the end‐beneficiary, the member of the society (T 5), with the goal of better healthcare and health for all.

Learning Health Systems (LHS) Governance: Ultimate Roundabout Intersection for Knowledge Traffic?

The concept of a LHS was established nearly two decades ago by the Institute of Medicine (now National Academy of Medicine) [1], as a unifying commitment to accelerated speed of generation and to wider use of new evidence by leveraging rapid advances in data science and other technologies, ultimately resulting in improved clinical care and better health for all. In other words, a LHS is a microcosm in which science, informatics systems, incentives, and culture are optimally aligned for innovation and continuous improvement, with best practices seamlessly embedded in the delivery process [1]. A LHS deals with general processes related to new knowledge generation (big or small, breakthrough discovery or incremental change), high‐fidelity implementation, broad dissemination, calibrated adaptation, good memory generation, swift retrieval, and systematic application of past learned experiences for sustainment. Functional LHS can bring together finely orchestrated resources, standardized processes and feedback loops that align scientific discovery and innovation, technology, education, training and clinical practices to integrate them as active elements in an evidence‐based continuum. One salient and nearly universal segment in the LHS arc or cycle is represented by the implementation phase, which represents an essential component of the entire system. The term LHS is deliberately broad and non‐prescriptive, ultimately allowing user, context and methodology‐level adaptations. Perhaps because of this, LHS have evolved over time into different models (participative, embedded researcher type, pragmatic trial or programmatic type) [7], which allows managers, leaders, strategists, scholars and practitioners alike to design and model their LHS according to local resources, scale, scope, local culture, institutional mission, strategic aspirations and tactical goals. Further, the “fuzzy” nature of LHS may represent a strength of the current body of literature, providing open passage to further refinements and by not constricting business model innovation by dominant research “traditions”, mandatory frameworks, models or strategic approaches. The LHS build on the necessity to implement change management and leadership by using strong engagement strategies (increasing awareness, participation, active involvement, sustained and successful advocacy) with its stakeholders, including patients, healthy community members and their families. We described elsewhere [6] that the LHS plant may entail separate iterative cycles of improvement for various missions (e.g., clinical care, education, research, community service, etc.), and that a translational enterprise such as Clinical and Translational Science Award (CTSA) hubs or other local translational science engines may serve best the stakeholders' needs by creating these structures and functionalities [5, 8]. Irrespective of the mechanism, an organization should consider building such a core infrastructure in order to be able to design, build and catalyze their functions and optimally calibrate long‐term sustainment.

The article posits that the Learning Health System (LHS) is a useful framework to establish and apply specific navigational rights, rules and permissions in the complex roundabout where different innovation avenues come together at T 4, so that velocity, effectiveness and harm‐free navigation are ensured for successful translation to T 5 (Figure 2). If one considers the advantages of roundabout intersections (assumed here by highly‐functional LHS), they are conveniently represented by: (1) uninterrupted, continuous flow, with more consistent movement, which can significantly reduce delays, traffic congestion or stalling; (2) reduced conflict or risk of collision, with fewer impact points where discovery paths cross; (3) improved overall safety, despite a local slow down; (4) environmental benefits and improved efficiencies (vs stalled or stopped traffic). The LHS must find the optimization mechanisms and functional coordination of the knowledge traffic in the roundabout intersection, so that effective, efficient and fast translation happens consistently for all innovations that make it to this stage, i.e., from positive outcomes in human clinical studies and trials, all the way to the population at large. Operationalizing navigational governance in LHS requires clear definitions of the specific rights (who can initiate, manage, and evaluate change), rules (evidence thresholds, prioritization criteria, speed norms), and permissions (regulatory, institutional, and operational approvals).

While QI (Quality Improvement) programs, D&I (Dissemination and Implementation) frameworks, and innovation incubators each address parts of the same challenge, they typically operate as distinct functional units. The LHS framework is distinguished by its integration of these functions into a single governance cycle, linking evidence generation, implementation, feedback, and learning/adaptation, and by its explicit mandate to serve all stakeholders from clinicians to community members. This integrative function is precisely what is needed at the T 4–T 5 transition, where multiple streams of evidence must be adjudicated against competing clinical priorities. At T 4 and T 5. the LHS mechanisms are both bottleneck fixers and opportunity enhancers, as the intersection is the area where multiple innovations compete simultaneously, while resources (financial, workforce, attention, time) are constrained, and organizational priorities must be explicitly aligned. The effectiveness of this navigation depends on how well the LHS governance system: (1) aligns innovation with system priorities, (2) coordinates activities across clinical, operational, and policy domains, and (3) balances and optimizes speed, safety, and equity in the system.

LHS Phenotyping by Innovation Generation and Adoption

Prior efforts have been focused on the differentiating characteristics of academic versus non‐academic LHS. However, the internal generation of new knowledge in research, in education, or in healthcare operations is likely not a fair or clear‐cut partitioning criterion. We propose here that LHS can be characterized on the axis of discovery and innovation as different phenotypes (LHS1–LHS4, Figure 3) based on their intersection with the innovation topography (Table 1), and that LHS structures and functional mechanisms should be designed, operationalized, and maintained as catalysts and robust navigational systems for all innovation‐friendly and forward‐looking LHS types.

The relationship between the LHS traffic and speed regulation (and illustrated as roundabout intersections in Figures 1, 2, 3) and the general characteristics of the LHS is complex and varies greatly based on the local context. The LHS general type varies from what we coined an LHS4 phenotype, i.e., purely clinical enterprises and conglomerates, able to advance externally or internally generated evidence from T4 and beyond, via very agile and efficient clinical operations; to an LHS3 type, represented by private healthcare systems able also to conduct clinical trials as part of their mission and operations; to an LHS2 type, the so‐called “academoid” health systems able to conduct human studies from T 2 and beyond; and finally to an LHS1 phenotype, represented by traditional academic health science universities, research and development departments or innovation incubators generating internal de novo evidence from systematic investigation that spans the entire translational spectrum (Figure 3).

The LHS1–LHS4 phenotype classification was derived using a cartesian coordinate system with two dimensions: (1) the depth of internal innovation generation, ranging from de novo, basic science investigation at T 0, all the way to late‐stage clinical trials at T 4, and, arguably, practice‐to‐evidence and QI‐type value‐added (see overlapping ellipses in Figure 3); and (2) the relationship between internal and external evidence adoption (Figure 4). The commonality of these LHS phenotypes is represented by adequate management of knowledge streams, either externally sourced or internally produced (i.e., LHS architecture and capabilities), at the T 4–T 5 transition. As described above, this derivation logic produced four distinct LHS profiles. Figure 4 shows that the traditional academic medical centers (AMC*) may have outstanding internal evidence or scientific discovery generation, while the adoption in practice and application toward the health of its community may be anywhere between low, moderate, or high. Conversely, AMC* may not be agile enough in surveying the environment and in applying swiftly the latest discoveries in their own clinical practice and health promotion programs, in their targeted investments or ongoing strategic calibrations. Figure 4 also depicts the dominant phenotype of the traditional community hospitals (CH*) and legacy teaching hospitals (TH*), which may generate internal evidence on a limited scale, but may have low or moderate external evidence adoption rates, respectively. These three traditional categories can be clearly delineated from the LHS1–LHS4 phenotypes, which are aligned in Figure 4 by their ability to generate and implement internal evidence, and having in common the high functionality of traffic regulation by roundabout mechanisms. The latter ensure high and swift adoption of external evidence and moderate to high adoption of internally generated new knowledge in various LHS. The LHS phenotype characterization helps leaders clarify the organization's identity, current and future capabilities, and determines where strategic investment is most critical. An LHS1 system must govern well T 0–T 1 handoffs (laboratory to early translational investigations), as well as T 4–T 5 junctures; an LHS4 system, by contrast, should concentrate its infrastructural architecture and investment entirely at T 4–T 5 level, with a strong externally‐facing innovation intelligence function to harvest and rapidly implement externally generated evidence. Health system leaders, strategists, and LHS designers should first determine their organization's phenotype, then use the Table 2 as a capability audit tool, identifying gaps between current infrastructure and the competencies their phenotype requires to support successful T 4 → T 5 translation.

Consider a large integrated health network with no research mission or capabilities, but a robust QI infrastructure—an LHS4. Under the proposed framework, the organization should: (1) invest in a robust externally‐facing innovation intelligence unit that monitors T 3–T 4 trial results in its priority disease areas (examples: should they invest in CAR‐T or CAR‐NK therapies and specialized teams in their cancer centers? should they add infusion units using CAR‐T therapy in some of their rheumatology clinics?); (2) establish rapid‐cycle implementation protocols within its LHS infrastructure to move evidence from T 4 to T 5 within 12–18 months; and (3) forgo investment in early‐phase research infrastructure (T 0–T 2), instead forming partnerships with LHS1 or LHS2 institutions for upstream knowledge generation and swift handoffs to clinical application. A typical LHS3 institution may have already invested in a comprehensive Clinical Research Unit, capable of running efficiently high‐volume phase 2 and 3 trials, participating in various multicentric studies, being a strong presence in many investigative consortia and registries, enhancing not only their scholarly mission, but also strengthening their research revenue streams. An LHS2 phenotype may be one in which research capabilities are well aligned for all types of clinical investigations (phase 1–4), and perhaps for some translational research, but very little in basic research. Such an organization would want to make sure it aligns its resources with upstream, breakthrough innovations, and to position its infrastructure for fit, alignment, efficiency and speed of delivery to its stakeholders. These examples contrast with an LHS1 academic medical center, where organizational governance must also manage T0 → T1 transitions, intellectual property (IP) decisions, linked translational investigations, phase 2–4 clinical trials, and Clinical and Translational Science Award (CTSA) hub alignment [9, 10, 11].

Let's say a health system is trying to adopt a late‐stage intervention with established efficacy in clinical trials but variable real‐world uptake (e.g., use of GLP‐1 agonists for weight loss interventions). In an LHS1 or LHS2 environment, the key challenge may be integrating new evidence with existing research and innovation infrastructure. In an LHS3 or LHS4 environment, the challenge is more likely to be operational: aligning informatics, frontline workflow, incentives, measurement, contracting or managed care provisions, and stakeholder communication so that adoption is reliable and equitable. In both cases, the LHS functions as the coordinating mechanism, but the emphasis differs by phenotype. This example brings up specific, practical questions: who has decision rights, what metrics define success, how evidence is reviewed, how workflows are redesigned, and how outcomes are fed back for improvement.

In summary, this perspective article proposes an integration of the translational spectrum with the LHS framework, applicable not only to academic institutions, research and development departments, innovation hubs, research incubators, university and community teaching hospitals or research institutes, but to all health systems (albeit of different phenotypes). The article proposes that the LHS plant is a great infrastructural platform for optimized translational navigation, using the broad knowledge bases, tools and frameworks of translational science and its contiguous disciplines. Such a structure should be able to provide the necessary facilitation of flow, navigational rules and sets of permissions, directional clarity and organization, so that the innovation travels successfully beyond T 4 to T 5, to the end‐beneficiary, the general population. The intersection, public forum, agora or simply the traffic roundabout of the LHS is the necessary final navigational infrastructure and functional controller created deliberately and purposefully by the organization for the research enterprise, innovation incubator or their specific investigative endeavors.
