A Qualitative Exploration of the Decision Not to Take Part in Colorectal Cancer Screening Among Women Living in Lower Socio‐Economic Areas
Authors: Nicholas Clarke, Lucy Hayden, Alan Smith, Linda Sharp, Christian von Wagner, Therese Mooney, Pamela Gallagher
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundOrganized colorectal (CRC) screening programmes offer an opportunity to reduce the burden of the disease on populations. CRC screening participation among women is substantially lower than breast or cervical cancer screening. Uptake is also lower in areas of greater deprivation which could exacerbate inequalities in CRC; however, few studies have been conducted with women resident in lower socio‐economic areas who were invited for screening but did not participate. The aim of this study was to explore women's decisions not to take part in Ireland's population‐based BowelScreen programme.MethodsSemi‐structured interviews were conducted with women resident in lower socio‐economic areas, who had not participated in BowelScreen. Deductive thematic analysis, guided by the Theoretical Domains Framework (TDF: 14 domain version), using the framework approach was conducted.ResultsFifteen women from lower socio‐economic areas in Ireland who had not participated in BowelScreen were interviewed. Six TDF domains were identified as influencing non‐participation; (1) Environmental Context and Resources; stressful life events and lack of healthcare professional recommendations, (2) Memory, Attention and Decision Making; delayed decision making and deciding screening is not personally relevant, (3) Knowledge; poor knowledge negating need for screening, (4) Emotions; disgust, embarrassment and fear, (5) Skills; low self‐efficacy and instruction complexity, and (6) Social influence; did not encourage testing or was overridden by emotion and poor knowledge.ConclusionsGreater consideration should be given to the targeting of interventions towards specific determinants of non‐participation within subgroups of the population, in particular non‐participant women living within lower socio‐economic areas. Tackling environmental stressors, emotional factors, lack of healthcare professional support and the perception that feeling healthy equates with not needing screening could help improve CRC screening uptake among women living in low socioeconomic areas.

Introduction

CRC is the third most commonly diagnosed cancer and the second most common cause of cancer related mortality in women worldwide, with an expected 63% increase in incidence and 73% increase in mortality worldwide by 2040 [1]. Organized population‐based cancer screening involves systematically inviting an identified population to undergo a screening test at defined intervals to identify early stage or precancerous lesions [2, 3]. CRC screening programmes have been established in many countries [3, 4] with the aim of reducing mortality from, and, where possible, incidence of those cancers in the population.

The European Union's Beating Cancer Plan aimed to ensure that 90% of the EU's population has access to colorectal, breast and cervical cancer screening by 2025 [5], but importantly also focused on inequalities across and within member states. Uptake in CRC screening programmes remains suboptimal in most European countries, with lower uptake associated with younger age, living alone [6] and living in the most deprived locations [7], highlighting the need for improving uptake overall and reducing differentials [8, 9].

Disparities in participation also exist by gender. Uptake in non‐invasive screening methods, such as fecal immunochemical test (FIT) and guaiac fecal occult blood test (gFOBT), is higher among females compared to males [10, 11], while some reviews report lower female uptake using invasive based methods (colonoscopy and flexible sigmoidoscopy) [11]. It is also noteworthy that, among females, uptake of CRC screening is substantially lower than breast or cervical cancer screening uptake [12, 13, 14], despite the fact that, in the European Union, there are more than four times the number of deaths from CRC in females than from cervical cancer [15].

Irelands Women's Health Action Plan [16] identified equity of access to cancer screening as an issue among women. Ireland has three national organized cancer screening programmes (for breast, cervical and colorectal cancer), however uptake in colorectal cancer screening (46% [17]) is substantially lower than breast (70% [18]) or cervical (73% [19]) cancer screening in Ireland and internationally [12, 13], but is particularly low for first time invitees (31% among females) [17].

Female non‐participation in colorectal cancer screening is linked to negative attitudes, beliefs, and emotions about screening and cancer, adverse social influences and perceptions of healthcare, and overconfidence in personal health [20, 21, 22, 23]. Bhatia et al. found that women with diabetes are less likely to undergo screening than non‐diabetic women [24]. In the UK, non‐participation in flexible sigmoidoscopy screening was also associated with greater deprivation, ethnicity, smoking, and obesity—factors that are additionally linked to higher risk of FOBT positivity and screen‐detected adenomas [25]. However, few qualitative studies have specifically focused on reasons for non‐participation among females from lower socio‐economic backgrounds. Greater understanding of screening determinants among population subgroups could inform the need for targeted strategies to improve uptake.

This study aimed to explore the decisions of women living in lower socioeconomic areas not to take part in Ireland's population‐based BowelScreen programme. Interventions developed and designed using evidence‐based and theoretically guided approaches may have greater potential to improve uptake in target populations [26]. This study therefore employed the Theoretical Domains Framework (TDF; an integrated framework of theories of potential influences on behavior) [27] to systematically identify screening determinants, thereby providing a future pipeline to intervention design using linked tools such as the Behavior Change Wheel (BCW) and taxonomy (BCT) [28, 29].

Methods

A qualitative design was used to explore the views, experiences and decision‐making processes of female non‐participants living in low‐socio‐economic areas when invited to participate in Ireland's national BowelScreen programme. BowelScreen is a biennial population‐based call‐recall programme, employing the faecal immunochemical test (FIT), and has been in operation since 2012 [17]. The programme systematically builds a register of invitees from Department of Social Protection sources, while also encouraging self‐registration. BowelScreen invites, by post, all eligible individuals on the register between the ages of 59–70 1 to take part. Those who consent, by phone, to take part are sent a FIT kit. If individuals do not make contact, a postal reminder is sent after 8 weeks. Upon completing the FIT and posting to a laboratory, individuals receive results within 3–4 weeks. Those with a negative result are re‐contacted after 2 years for repeat screening. Individuals who test positive are offered a diagnostic colonoscopy. Screening and subsequent tests, and treatment (if cancer is detected) is free of charge [17]. BowelScreen provides participants with culturally appropriate, accessible communication materials in easy‐to‐read formats, and photo or video stories. These are delivered through print and online channels and tailored to different health literacy levels. National media campaigns run year‐round, with a stronger focus during bowel cancer awareness month. This is complemented by intermittent awareness campaigns from partner organizations including the Marie Keating Foundation and the Irish Cancer Society. This study received ethical approval from Dublin City University Research Ethics Committee (DCUREC/2023/192A).

Theoretical Framework

The 14‐item version of the TDF [27] was employed to guide the semi‐structured interview and data analysis. The interview guide sought to tap into each TDF domain, namely: knowledge, skills, social/professional role and identity, beliefs about capabilities, optimism, beliefs about consequences, reinforcement, intentions, goals, memory attention and decision processes, environmental context and resources, social influences, emotion and behavior regulation.

Participants

Participants were invitees of the BowelScreen programme. We sought to recruit women 59 years of age and over who had been invited but had not taken part in any round of screening and who lived in a lower socio‐economic area (defined below). In determining the sample size for the study, we anticipated a low response rate given the target population. Accordingly, a purposive sample of 1200 eligible women was drawn from the BowelScreen register.

Sampling and Recruitment

Sampling was carried out within specific areas of high deprivation identified via the Pobal Deprivation Index [30]. The index classifies each small geographical area in the country according to the characteristics of the resident population (including demographic profile, social class composition (percentages of: primary and third level education, households headed by professional, managerial, semi‐skilled or unskilled workers, and mean number of persons per room) and the labor market situation). It contains eight categories ranging from extremely disadvantaged too extremely affluent. This study focused on three categories: areas of extreme disadvantage, very disadvantaged and disadvantage (which represents 8% (402,907 individuals) of the Irish population). These areas were identified within the programme database and samples of eligible women currently resident in these areas (who had not taken part in the programme) were sent a study invitation letter (signed by the BowelScreen programme clinical director). Those interested in taking part could contact the university researcher directly; they were ensured that anything they said in the interview would be confidential and not shared with the screening programme. In compensation for their time and potential expense, participants were offered a €30 shopping voucher. One reminder letter was sent by the programme after 6–8 weeks.

Data Collection

All interviews were conducted (by L.H. (PhD)) over the phone (during March to June 2023), who is a female psychologist and experienced qualitative researcher. Interview participants provided informed consent prior to undertaking the interview. Interviews lasted 14–59 minutes (mean = 28 minutes), were audio‐recorded and transcribed verbatim. Fieldnotes were taken after each interview. Transcripts were not returned to participants for review.

Data Analysis

Transcripts were anonymized, imported into NVivo, and analyzed using the framework approach; this involved familiarization, construction of a thematic framework (the TDF), indexing, and sorting data and reviewing data extracts [31]. A deductive approach to coding was used in which transcripts were coded line by line to TDF domains. Five interviews were independently double coded by two researchers (L.H. & N.C.). Researchers discussed the coding to reach a consensus on attributing data to the TDF. L.H. completed coding and analysis. All participant names have been replaced with pseudonyms.

Environmental Context and Resources

Some women highlighted the influence of their general practitioner (GP) on their healthcare decisions and, specifically, how a recommendation from their GP could have influenced their participation 3 . All women, except one, reported participation in one or both of the breast and cervical cancer screening programmes, valuing these in terms of looking after their health (some had precancerous cervical cells identified via CervicalCheck). The importance placed in other cancer screening programmes (“I have all my checks—every one of them” (Clarie, 59–64)) did not extend to BowelScreen, primarily because BowelScreen was not recommended or conducted by a trusted healthcare practitioner, or because of the nature of the test (home‐based faecal testing).

Participants reported various environmental stressors at the time of their BowelScreen invitation which influenced their decision not to take part in BowelScreen, including mental health difficulties, experiencing critical life events (including personal injury, death of a family member and critical illness of a family member), pre‐existing physical conditions, and other priorities such as work responsibilities. Other women recalled how living with, or caring for someone with health conditions meant bowel screening was not a priority in their lives; one woman described the screening invitation as “a bit of extra struggle” (Noreen, 59–64) (Table 2).

One participant perceived her constipation as a barrier to conducting the test. Others perceived the BowelScreen materials as not fully informative in relation to how to complete the test, while another reported that the waiting time for the results was a barrier to participation.

I often heard about [bowel cancer] but I didn’t go into it, you know what I mean?(Brenda, 65–69)

Others reported some knowledge about bowel cancer via experiences with family and friends who had the disease. In some cases, these experiences were perceived in a negative light (Table 2) and did not motivate a decision to take part sometimes influenced by emotional factors. One woman with first‐hand experience of caring for a relative with bowel cancer, described her sense of disgust with the test, “literally it made me feel sick, the thoughts of doing it” (Catherine, 65–69); (Emotions).

For others, their decision was influenced by a perception that screening was only for those with a family history of bowel cancer, poor diet (“I think diet has a lot to do with it myself, basically.” (Grainne, 65–69)), or having conditions that would increase one's risk.I’m delighted it’s free for people but it’s not for me. It’s for other people who have some sort of complaint.(Noreen, 59–64)

Most participants reported having very limited knowledge about the testing procedure or the BowelScreen programme.

Emotion

Some women reported being fearful of the outcome of the test, which was also described as fear of the unknown, and this influenced their decision not to take the FIT (Table 2).I think, fear, fear would definitely be my first [reaction to the invitation], fear of the unknown, I don’t want to know.(Sophie, 59–64)

In other cases, women's affect (including self‐consciousness and embarrassment) influenced their decision, although for Grainne (below) the passage of time has reduced this sense (perhaps as a result of other events in her life such as having bowel problems and the passing of an adult child);It was just embarrassing. It’s embarrassing for a person like me, but then again as you get older, it wouldn’t be embarrassing to me now.(Grainne, 65–69)

Several women also reported visceral feelings of disgust such as feeling “a turn in their stomach” or “putting your fingers down a chalkboard” at the thought of doing the test. Some expressed a sense of regret after their decision to not take the test (Table 2).

You had to read through the thing and to make sure you were doing it right and making sure that everything was right and, as I say, I just felt it was a bit cumbersome to do.(Mary, 59–64)

Social Influences

Women differed in their willingness to discuss bowel screening with others. Some had not spoken to others about the test.It is not something that you would be discussing what you had to do. It is kind of a very private test.(Mary, 59–64)

Others spoke with friends and family (who believed bowel screening was important) but said this did not influence their decision because they had “no problems” and therefore did not need the test (Knowledge). One woman (Janice, 65–69) described how encouragement from family was overridden by a sense of disgust, a fear of being sick (Emotions) and concerns about not completing the test correctly (Skills).

Some who lived alone reported they rarely spoke to family or neighbors and lacked opportunity to discuss bowel cancer screening, but recognized that having such opportunity may have influenced their decision to take part;Maybe if I’d known someone that had bowel cancer or, you know, I would have said to myself “Oh feck it” you know, “It’s important to get this done.” Then I probably would be more inclined to do it.(Noleen, 59–64)

In general women reported their partners did not influence their participation decision, often reporting they also did not take part. Some women's partners had encouraged them to take part (due to experiencing bowel symptoms, and in the case of a husband's family history of bowel cancer). However, partners' encouragement did not translate to action, primarily because of fear and embarrassment (Emotions), and critical life events (Environmental context and resources).

Discussion

As far as we are aware, this is the first qualitative study to specifically focus on women from lower socio‐economic backgrounds who have decided not to take part in population‐based FIT‐based CRC screening. Six TDF domains were identified as influencing decisions about screening participation, and in a number of cases domains were inter‐related.

Environmental Context and Resources

Stressful life events were an important influence on interviewees' decision not to take part in FIT screening. Marinucci et al.’s review reported that, from a social determinants of health perspective, system level screening inaccessibility, in combination with the stresses and complexities of everyday living, places screening as a low priority for many groups [32]. Some studies have reported on the association of stressful life events with lower CRC screening uptake [33, 34], although particular events may be associated with increased screening (death of someone other than a partner) [34]. While not modifiable, the impact of these events on women's health behavior is an important factor warranting further investigation, particularly in the area of social support [33].

All participants, bar one, had completed breast and/or cervical cancer screening (often expressing the importance of these programmes to their health), and some indicated that, had their GP recommended BowelScreen participation, they would be more inclined to do the test. Work elsewhere shows healthcare provider recommendations of CRC screening can increase uptake [35]. While specific challenges exist in the Irish health system in relation to (lack of) integrated electronic patient records, the ongoing establishment of a digital health framework for Ireland [36] could facilitate integration of screening recommendations from GPs, and targeted uptake interventions across screening programmes (including Diabetic Retina Screening where evidence suggests that a diagnosis of diabetes increases risk of CRC [37]), particularly among lower socioeconomic populations. Marinucci et al. point out that in the absence of strong patient HCP relationships, combined with suboptimal health literacy, access and perceived value of screening health information is compromised [32]. However, it should also be noted that CRC uptake interventions delivered in community settings (i.e., lay health educators, pharmacies, religious and community groups) have been reported as somewhat more effective compared to those delivered through primary care contexts [38]; suggesting that a range of approaches, and multifactorial approaches, will be best placed to incrementally increase uptake, particularly among lower socioeconomic groups.

Given the substantially higher uptake of breast and cervical screening in Ireland, appointments and contact points within these programmes offer a practical opportunity to prompt women to participate in BowelScreen. Consistent cross‐programme messaging may help reinforce the importance of bowel screening among women who already engage with breast and cervical cancer screening services.

Memory, Attention and Decision Process

Some participants had poor memory of receiving the BowelScreen invitation, while others paid little attention to it. Others thought about the invitation, but then ultimately did not act. There may be an opportunity within these decision‐making timeframes to encourage a decision to take the test. Robb et al. have reported that a 2 week deadline, in invitation letters, for return of kits significantly increased uptake and reduced the need to issue reminders; the authors pointed out this is a highly cost effective strategy [39]. A second reminder delivered at the appropriate time could incrementally improve uptake among those women from lower SES areas. Reminder messaging could aim to overcome emotional, knowledge and social influence based‐factors identified here.

Knowledge

Many women believed they were not susceptible to CRC as they did not have risk factors and this was a factor in their decision not to take part in the screening programme. Le Bonniec et al. have also reported not having symptoms led people to consider that they did not need screening [40]. Similarly, we have reported that defensive information processing is a specific barrier to uptake, with self‐exemption (believing that screening is not relevant) being an independent influence on non‐participation [21]. While targeting every barrier to screening may not be possible, increasing knowledge and awareness about what, and who CRC screening is for (particularly those who do not have symptoms or risk factors), among the asymptomatic target population is essential [40], particularly—as suggested here—among lower socio‐economic populations. Interventions should directly address the misconception that feeling healthy and not having risk factors negates the need for preventive screening, emphasizing that CRC screening is an effective way of preventing cancer (cases or deaths) in asymptomatic individuals.

Previously we tested two interventions to increase uptake in BowelScreen (one provided a FIT kit with a reminder letter and the other tackled defensive beliefs (self‐exemption and denial) in the supporting screening literature) and noted an increase in screening participation among women from lower socio‐economic areas when the interventions were combined [41]. Although the result was not statistically significant, it does signal a potential avenue to increase uptake among women from lower socio‐economic areas [41].

Emotion

It is not surprising that emotional factors influenced non‐participation decisions (including fear of cancer and bowel cancer screening, embarrassment and disgust) among women living in lower SES areas; this has been observed in numerous studies [41]. Tackling fear, embarrassment, and disgust requires efforts to overcome these emotions—potentially through mass media campaigns, community endorsements, patient navigation and advice. This could include social norm‐based interventions where non‐participants are encouraged to engage in screening through high uptake messages [42] which are tailored to overcome negative emotions and beliefs, while clearly expressing the benefits of participation, particularly if these messages come from trusted/credible healthcare sources such as GPs [43].

Skills

Some women had low self‐efficacy around completing the FIT or perceived the instructions as complex. A review of interventions in CRC screening, report that experience narratives, which “demonstrate” the testing procedure, led to increased self‐efficacy, facilitating decision making and greater confidence in testing [44].

Social Influence

While many women reported that others had recommended screening participation, this did not encourage a decision to take the test. Social influences were often over‐ridden by other factors such as disgust, feeling well, or having no symptoms. Social norms interventions may also play a role by improving intentions to screen through the provision of information about the number of people who have previously taken part (although it should be acknowledged that the evidence on this is largely for screening intentions rather than behavior) [42].

Strengths and Limitations

While we aimed for data saturation, recruitment was challenging and saturation has likely not been achieved, although as pointed out by Hennick and Kaiser, saturation in qualitative studies can be achieved in a narrow range, specifically 9–17 interviews, particularly in studies with a relatively homogenous study population and narrowly defined objectives, all of which is the case within our study (non‐participant women from lower SES areas) [45]. We acknowledge that some of the interviews were short (2 interviews lasted 14 and 17 minutes) perhaps reflecting less awareness of BowelScreen among women in Ireland compared to cervical or breast cancer screening. We also acknowledge that had we achieved higher participant numbers we may have identified additional domains within the TDF. However, we also highlight the value of information power [46]—separate to the issue of saturation, in that higher information power can be achieved with a narrow aim, dense specificity (characteristics specific to the study aim), applied theory, strong dialog and specific case analysis, all of which may require lower sample sizes. Accessing such a specific population could only be achieved through sampling from the BowelScreen database, so while only 15 women took part, all are verified BowelScreen non‐participants. In this regard we targeted, but did not recruit women from the most deprived category, namely extremely disadvantaged. The invitation to take part in an interview came from the BowelScreen programme, and while we did assure potential participants that their views would not be shared with the screening programme, the invitation process may have influenced women's willingness to be interviewed. Despite the challenges of engaging this population and the scarcity of research on this low‐uptake group, our study adds important evidence to inform efforts to improve colorectal cancer outcomes among women living in socio‐economically disadvantaged areas in Ireland and beyond.

Clinical Implications

A number of factors should be addressed in tackling low uptake in CRC screening among women living in lower socioeconomic areas. Multifactorial approaches to increasing uptake could focus on (1) improving healthcare professional recommendations, particularly from the perspective of general practitioners and pharmacists who are perceived as trusted sources of healthcare information, (2) targeting misconceptions related to feeling healthy and not having risk factors which often negate the need for CRC screening, (3) greater integration of screening recommendations from other trusted screening programmes (Breast and Cervical), (4) additional reminders that focus on emotional and knowledge based barriers, and (5) implementation of social norm and experience narrative based interventions.

Conclusions

Greater consideration should be given to the targeted design and implementation of interventions that address specific determinants of non‐participation within distinct subgroups of the population, in particular women living within lower socio‐economic areas. Our findings suggest that a multi‐faceted approach addressing environmental, psychosocial, and healthcare system factors may be effective for improving CRC screening uptake among women living in low socioeconomic areas.
