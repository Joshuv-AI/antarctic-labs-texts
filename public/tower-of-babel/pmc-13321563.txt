Abiotic stress-responsive transcription factor derived-SSR markers reveal conserved regulatory genes and genetic structure in two cultivated jute species
Authors: Rakesh Bhowmick, Kanti Meena, Thribhuvan R, Kumar Nishant Chourasia, Jitendra Kumar Meena, Jiban Mitra, Gouranga Kar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundJute (Corchorus spp.) is an important natural bast-fibre crop, but its productivity is severely affected by abiotic stresses such as drought, salinity, and waterlogging. Improvement of stress resilience in jute requires functional molecular markers directly linked to regulatory genes governing stress responses. The present study aimed to develop such resources through an integrated transcriptome-driven approach in the two cultivated species, Corchorus capsularis and C. olitorius.ResultsAnalysis of publicly available RNA sequencing datasets identified 1889–6376 and 808–6259 stress-responsive differentially expressed genes under various abiotic stress conditions in C. capsularis and C. olitorius, respectively. From these datasets, 370 differentially expressed transcription factor genes harboring microsatellite motifs were identified in C. capsularis, while 348 such genes were identified in C. olitorius. A total of 728 and 702 simple sequence repeat markers were developed from these transcription factor genes in C. capsularis and C. olitorius, respectively. The majority of markers (77% in C. capsularis and 74% in C. olitorius) were located in regulatory untranslated regions, predominantly enriched with A/T-rich di- and tri-nucleotide motifs. Major stress-responsive transcription factor families, including MYB, basic helix–loop–helix, NAC, WRKY, AP2/ethylene-responsive factor, and homeobox, were highly represented. Synteny analysis revealed 173 conserved syntenic transcription factor–simple sequence repeats gene pairs within collinear chromosomal regions, indicating strong evolutionary conservation of regulatory frameworks between the two species. Population structure analysis revealed three genetic clusters (K = 3) broadly corresponding to species identity, with some admixture in C. olitorius, which may reflect shared ancestry or limited marker resolution rather than definitive gene flow. Pairwise genetic distance values ranged from 0.091(CIN-540 and CEX-39) to 0.773 (CIN-534 and OIN-578), reflecting substantial intraspecific diversity.ConclusionsThis study provides a comprehensive set of transcription factor-derived simple sequence repeat markers tightly associated with stress-responsive regulatory genes in jute. They offer potential advantages over anonymous genomic markers and represent valuable genomic resources for marker-assisted breeding, genomic selection, and the development of climate-resilient jute cultivars.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-08843-w.

Introduction

Jute (Corchorus spp.) is an economically important bast-fibre crop cultivated predominantly in India and Bangladesh, supporting rural livelihoods and providing raw materials for textiles, ropes, and biodegradable packaging. The two major cultivated species, Corchorus capsularis L. (CC) and Corchorus olitorius L. (CO), account for > 95% of global jute production. Due to heightened global efforts to reduce synthetic plastics and increase reliance on sustainable biomaterials, demand for jute fibre has surged. Beyond its direct economic value, jute cultivation provides substantial ecosystem services such as soil structure improvement, reduced erosion, enhanced carbon sequestration, and diversified cropping systems that support biodiversity [1].

One of the most challenging aspects for enhancing crop productivity is abiotic stresses, including drought, salinity, and waterlogging across developmental stages, which impair physiological processes, disrupt water and ion homeostasis, increase reactive oxygen species (ROS), reduce photosynthesis, and alter hormone-signalling pathways [2–4]. The frequency and severity of these stresses are rising under global climate change, especially in tropical and subtropical agro-ecosystems. Similarly, several studies in jute have reported that abiotic stress hampers seed germination, plant growth, affects photosynthesis and its pigments etc. have been reported [5–9], ultimately reducing the fibre yield and quality. However, CC and CO differ in their responses—CC exhibits relatively greater salinity and drought tolerance, whereas CO performs better under water-deficit conditions [9, 10]. These contrasting responses underscore the need to decode species-specific molecular tolerance mechanisms.

Transcription factors (TFs) act as master regulators that sense upstream stress signals (hormones, ROS, Ca²⁺, MAPK cascades) and orchestrate genome-wide transcriptional responses through downstream effector gene modulation [11, 12]. Major TF families such as AP2/ERF, NAC, MYB, WRKY, bZIP and bHLH play key roles in stomatal regulation, osmotic adjustment, ROS scavenging, cell-wall modification, and hormone regulation during salinity, drought, hypoxia and metal stresses [13, 14]. Notably, coding-sequence variation in TF genes often has pleiotropic effects on multiple stress-responsive pathways. SSRs embedded within TF genes, therefore, capture regulatory loci with potential genome-wide impacts on adaptation [15, 16]. Previous work in wheat and kenaf has demonstrated that transcriptome-derived SSRs from stress-related genes are superior for diversity analysis and mapping stress tolerance QTLs compared to genomic SSRs [17, 18].

Despite jute’s agricultural and industrial importance, molecular marker resources are limited compared to major cereals and pulses. Existing jute SSR marker panels are predominantly genomic and lack functional annotation. No comprehensive SSR resource linked to abiotic stress-responsive transcription factors currently exists for jute, representing a significant gap for accelerating stress-adaptive breeding. Recent public availability of high-quality genome sequences and RNA-seq datasets for both cultivated jute species provides an unprecedented opportunity to mine functional markers at scale.

This study integrates five complementary analyses to: (1) identify stress-responsive transcription factor genes using transcriptome mining, (2) develop and characterize SSR markers within these TFs, (3) map TF-SSR loci to chromosomal positions, (4) assess evolutionary conservation through comparative synteny, and (5) evaluate genetic diversity and population structure across CC and CO germplasm. This integrated framework delivers functional genomic resources for marker-assisted breeding and provides mechanistic insights into regulatory evolution and stress adaptation in jute.

Materials and methods

Differential gene expression

To study expression of genes, publicly available RNA sequencing reads were retrieved from the SRA database of NCBI. Details of the reads used in our study are given in Supplementary Table S1. Expression of the genes was studied in response to three different abiotic stresses, namely drought, waterlogging and salinity [19, 20]. The quality control of raw reads was evaluated using FastQC (version 0.11.9). Subsequently, the Trimmatic-version 0.36 program was used via command line to eliminate adapters and low-quality reads from the raw data. After trimming raw reads, the quality of the filtered data was re-assessed using FastQC (version 0.11.9), and gene expression was measured by Kallisto (v0.46.0), which employs a pseudo-alignment method to expedite the alignment process. The genome sequences of CC var. ‘Huangma 179’ (HM179) and CO var. ‘Kuanyechangguo’ (KYCG) obtained from https://www.bigd.big.ac.cn/gwh, with accession numbers GWHBCLC00000000 and GWHBCLB00000000, respectively were used as a reference. RNA-seq reads were quantified using the Kallisto program to obtain transcript-level abundance estimates (est_counts) for each sample. Gene-level expression values were subsequently generated using the tximport package, which aggregates transcript-level estimates into gene-level counts suitable for downstream analysis. Differential gene expression analysis was performed using DESeq2 [21] using VST normalization. The analysis of each study was conducted separately for each dataset using a design formula of ~ condition. To assess potential hidden sources of variation within each dataset, principal component analysis (PCA) was performed on variance stabilized expression data. In all cases, samples clustered primarily according to biological condition rather than any unintended grouping, indicating minimal batch effects. Therefore, no additional batch correction was applied. Genes with an absolute log2 fold change greater than 1 and an adjusted p-value less than 0.05 were considered significantly differentially expressed.

Transcription factor identification

To identify transcription factor (TF) genes among the differentially expressed genes (DEGs), three complementary approaches were employed. First, all DEGs were analyzed using iTAK (v1.7) [22], which classified them into various transcription factor families and regulatory protein categories. Subsequently, the putative TF genes identified by iTAK were subjected to BLASTP searches against the Plant Transcription Factor Database (PlantTFDB v4.0; http://planttfdb.gao-lab.org/) to validate and refine their classification with an E-value cutoff of ≤ 1e − 5, minimum sequence identity of 50%, and query coverage of at least 50%. Finally, the candidate TF genes were further examined using the NCBI Conserved Domain Database (CDD, https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/cdd/) to confirm the presence of conserved transcription factor domains, thereby ensuring accurate identification. Genes lacking conserved TF domains were excluded. To ensure high-confidence identification, only those genes that were consistently supported by all approaches were retained for final classification, thereby minimizing false positives and improving the reliability of transcription factor annotation.

SSR mining in stress-responsive transcription factor genes

To identify simple sequence repeat (SSR) markers within the differentially expressed transcription factor (TF) genes, Krait software [23] was employed. The search parameters were set to detect repeat motifs ranging from di- to hexanucleotides, with a minimum repeat number of five. For compound SSR identification, two SSRs were considered part of the same compound locus only if they were separated by less than 100 nucleotides. SSR locations within gene structures (5′-UTR, CDS, 3′-UTR) were determined using gene annotation files. Primer pairs flanking the identified SSRs were designed using Primer3 integrated within Krait, with the following parameters: amplicon size of 100–300 bp and primer melting temperature (Tm) between 55 °C and 65 °C. Polymorphism information content (PIC) was calculated for each SSR locus to assess marker informativeness using the standard formula PIC = 1 − Σpi², where pi represents the frequency of the i-th allele at a given locus. Allele frequencies were estimated by considering all alleles observed across the genotypes, with each diploid genotype contributing two alleles per locus. Expected heterozygosity (He), also referred to as gene diversity, was calculated based on allele frequencies using the formula He = 1 − Σpi². Observed heterozygosity (Ho) was estimated as the proportion of heterozygous individuals at each locus.

Chromosomal localization of stress-responsive TFDSSRs

Chromosomal localization of the stress-responsive TFDSSRs was determined based on their genomic coordinates obtained from the gene annotation file. The physical distribution of these markers across chromosomes was visualized using the “Gene Location Visualize” module in TBTools (v1.098) [24].

Synteny and orthology study of differentially expressed TF genes

Dual synteny analysis was performed to examine the collinearity and syntenic relationships of SSR-containing differentially expressed transcription factor (TF) genes between CC and CO. MCScanX (v0.8) [25] was used to identify collinear and syntenic gene pairs between the two genomes. The resulting syntenic relationships were visualized through a dual synteny plot generated using the Dual Synteny Plot View module of TBtools. In addition, the orthologous relationships of stress-responsive genes harboring SSR motifs were investigated between CC and CO. For this, OrthoFinder (v2.5.13) [26] was employed to identify genome-wide orthologous gene pairs, from which stress-responsive TF genes containing SSR markers were subsequently filtered for comparative analysis.

Gene ontology analysis

For Gene Ontology (GO) classification, functional annotation of the differentially expressed genes was performed using eggNOG-mapper (v2.1.7) [27] to obtain the corresponding GO terms and IDs. The annotated genes were then categorized into different GO Level 2 functional groups (Biological Process, Molecular Function, and Cellular Component) using TBtools.

DNA extraction and genotyping

Genomic DNA was isolated from fresh leaf tissues utilizing the CTAB method [28]. About 100 mg of leaf tissue was pulverized in liquid nitrogen and reconstituted in 700 µL of CTAB extraction buffer (2% CTAB, 20 mM EDTA, 100 mM Tris-HCl, 1.4 M NaCl and 0.2% β-mercaptoethanol). Following a 30-minute incubation at 65 °C, the aqueous phase containing DNA was isolated utilizing dichloromethane. DNA was subsequently precipitated using isopropanol and rinsed with 70% ethanol. The genetic diversity of 15 CO (OIN-185, OIN-204, OIN-259, OIN-328, OIN-375, OIN-513, OIN-578, OIN-783, OIN-817, OIN-869, OIN-943, OIN-1078, OEX-21, OEX-31, OEX-35) and 15 CC (CIN-531, CIN-532, CIN-533, CIN-534, CIN-535, CIN-536, CIN-537, CIN-538, CIN-539, CIN-540, CEX-39, CEX-54, CEX-62, CMU-41, CMU-49) was evaluated using 22 polymorphic markers. Twenty-two polymorphic SSR markers were selected based on: (1) even chromosomal distribution, (2) clear PCR amplification patterns, (3) maximum allelic variation across the germplasm panel, and (4) representation across diverse TF families. These 22 markers were experimentally validated through PCR amplification and allele sizing against the 30 accessions (Supplementary Table S2). The PCR reaction (20 µL) included 50 ng DNA, 10 µL of 2x PCR master mix, 200 µM dNTPs, 1 µL each of primers, and nuclease-free water. Thermal cycler conditions: 94 °C for 5 min, 35 cycles of 94 °C for 45 s, 52–61 °C for 45 s, and 72 °C for 1 min, with a final extension at 72 °C for 7 min. PCR products were separated on a 3.5% agarose gel and visualized under UV light.

Population structure analysis

Population structure was investigated using the Bayesian clustering algorithm implemented in STRUCTURE v2.3.4 [29]. The analysis was performed under the admixture model with correlated allele frequencies to infer the most likely number of genetic clusters (K) among the 30 jute accessions representing CC (n = 15) and CO (n = 15). The number of possible clusters (K) was tested from K = 1 to K = 10, with 10 independent runs for each K value to ensure consistency and avoid local optima. Each run consisted of a burn-in period of 100,000 iterations, followed by 100,000 Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) iterations. The most likely number of clusters was identified using the Evanno ΔK method [30], based on the rate of change in the log probability of data between successive K values. The STRUCTURE output files were processed through the StructureSelector web server [31] using the Clumpak algorithm [32] to summarize and align replicate clusterings. The ancestry coefficient values (Q-matrix) for the best K were used to generate a population structure bar plot.

Genetic distance and neighbor joining clustering

Allele size data from 22 SSR loci were used to assess genetic relationships among the genotypes. Missing values, originally coded as “999”, were treated as missing data and excluded from pairwise comparisons. Genetic distances between genotypes were calculated using a Nei genetic distance [33] based on the proportion of shared alleles, which is appropriate for co-dominant SSR data. For each pair of genotypes, the proportion of shared alleles was computed across all loci, and genetic distance was estimated as one minus the proportion of shared alleles. A Neighbor-Joining (NJ) dendrogram was constructed from the resulting distance matrix to infer genetic relationships among the genotypes. The analysis was performed using Python-based computational tools (Bio.Phylo module).

Principal component analysis

Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was performed to assess genetic structure and variation among genotypes using multivariate analysis. The diploid SSR allele data were transformed into a numerical matrix by representing both alleles per locus. PCA was conducted using the scikit-learn library in Python, and the first two principal components (PC1 and PC2) were used to visualize genetic relationships. The percentage of variance explained by each principal component was calculated and displayed on the axes. Genotypes were plotted in a two-dimensional space, and color coding was applied to distinguish CC and CO accessions.

Results

Stress-responsive transcriptome and transcription factor mining

Comprehensive transcriptome analysis of CC and CO subjected to drought, waterlogging, and salinity stress revealed substantial and stress-specific differential gene expression. For this study, we utilized previously reported RNA-seq datasets retrieved from the NCBI database and analyzed gene expression responses under waterlogging, drought, and salinity stress conditions (Table 1). In CC, waterlogging resulted in 4,835–6,376 DEGs across the time course, compared with 1,889 DEGs under drought and 1,908–5,672 under salinity. Likewise, CO exhibited 4,979–6,259 DEGs during waterlogging, 563 under drought, and 808–1,996 under salinity, indicating a species and stress-specific amplitude of transcriptional reprogramming. Within these DEGs, transcription factors (TFs) represented a prominent functional category, reflecting their critical role in reconfiguring stress-responsive gene regulatory networks. Notably, CC possessed 105–381 differentially expressed TFs (DE-TFs), while CO harbored 44–384 DE-TFs under different stress conditions.

Classification of transcription factor families

Classification of the transcription factors harboring SSRs revealed both shared and species-specific patterns of regulatory gene enrichment in CC and CO. In both species, the MYB, bHLH, AP2/ERF, NAC, HB, WRKY, and C2C2 families represented the dominant groups, reflecting their well-established involvement in abiotic stress signalling and transcriptional reprogramming (Fig. 1). While CC showed higher representation for MYB (40), bHLH (32), AP2/ERF (25), NAC (23), and HB (23), CO exhibited slightly elevated counts in WRKY (23), C2C2 (24), bZIP (13), and LOB (9), suggesting differences in regulatory pathways between species under stress conditions. Interestingly, several TF families, including OFP, FAR1, Jumonji and GeBP, were detected exclusively in CO, whereas CPP and SNF2 were unique to CC, indicating species-specific regulatory expansions possibly shaped by divergent ecological adaptation.

SSR distribution and motif composition

To further translate these findings into functional genomic resources, all differentially expressed transcription factor (DE-TF) sequences were mined for simple sequence repeats (SSRs), enabling the discovery of transcription factor-associated molecular markers directly linked with stress regulation. A total of 64–214 SSR-containing TF genes were identified in CC and 29–214 in CO, with the highest abundance consistently observed during waterlogging stress in both species (Table 1). A total of 370 and 348 differentially expressed transcription factors were found to harbor SSR motifs in CC and CO, respectively. The enrichment of SSR loci in regulatory genes suggests their potential utility in dissecting allelic variation underlying stress tolerance mechanisms. A total of 728 SSRs were identified within 370 DE-TF genes of CC, whereas 702 SSRs were detected across 348 DE-TF genes in CO, indicating a slightly higher abundance and density of regulatory SSRs in CC. Analysis of SSR localization within gene structures revealed that the majority of SSRs occurred in untranslated regions (UTRs) in both species. In CO, 301 SSRs were detected in the 5′-UTR and 220 in the 3′-UTR, while CC possessed 288 and 276 SSRs in the 5′- and 3′-UTRs, respectively. A smaller proportion of SSRs resided within coding sequences (CDS), accounting for 181 sites in CO and 164 in CC, suggesting an evolutionary preference for SSR accumulation in regulatory regions where they may influence transcriptional modulation without altering coding potential (Fig. 2A, B).

Motif type distribution revealed that di-nucleotide repeats constituted the predominant SSR class in both species, with counts of 497 in CO and 528 in CC. Tri-nucleotide repeats were the next most abundant category (198 and 188, respectively), aligning with the expected enrichment of 3-bp motifs in genes to preserve reading frame integrity. Higher-order motifs (tetra- and penta-nucleotide SSRs) were rare, comprising < 1% of the total SSRs in both genomes and reflecting selective constraints against length variation in coding or regulatory elements (Fig. 2C).

Motif-specific comparison further demonstrated that A/T-rich di-nucleotide repeats were strongly dominant, with AA/TT, AG/CT, and AT motifs contributing to the majority of SSR loci in both species. CC showed slightly higher representation for AA/TT (313 vs. 286) and AG/CT (108 vs. 117), while CO harbored more AT repeats with a modest difference. Among tri-nucleotide motifs, AAG/CTT was the most prevalent in both species (53 in CC and 48 in CO), reinforcing their evolutionary stability in regulatory gene regions (Fig. 2D).

Chromosomal distribution

The chromosomal localization of differentially expressed transcription factor genes harboring SSRs revealed an uneven distribution across the genomes of both cultivated jute species. In CO, a total of 348 DE TF–linked SSRs were mapped, with the highest representation on Chr04 (72 genes; 149 SSRs), followed by Chr06 (61 genes; 118 SSRs) and Chr02 (50 genes; 95 SSRs). In CC, 370 DE TF–SSRs were assigned to chromosomes, with Chr04 again showing the greatest accumulation (93 genes; 173 SSRs), followed by Chr03 (52 genes; 102 SSRs) and Chr05 (52 genes; 116 SSRs). Lower densities were observed on Chr07 in both species, while CO also showed 17 genes (31 SSRs) assigned to two unanchored scaffolds (GWHBCLB00000136 and GWHBCLB00000276), reflecting incomplete genome anchoring in this species (Figs. 3 and 4). The relatively higher representation of DE TF–linked SSRs on certain chromosomes, particularly Chr04 in both genomes, indicates the presence of marker-dense regions rather than definitive regulatory hotspots, as this observation is based on absolute counts without normalization for chromosome length or gene density. A closer examination of the physical distribution patterns revealed that DE-TF genes with SSRs tend to occur in closely spaced clusters rather than being randomly scattered across the genome. These clusters were predominantly detected on Chr04, Chr03, Chr05, and Chr06 of CC and on Chr04 and Chr06 of CO. Within these regions, multiple stress-responsive TF loci were located within short genomic intervals, often in tandem or near-tandem arrangements, suggesting localized genomic organization and possible co-regulation. Such TF-rich clusters may indicate the presence of co-regulated transcriptional hubs that enable a coordinated response to abiotic stress signals.

Comparative synteny and orthology

Dual synteny mapping between the seven chromosomes of CC and CO revealed a strong collinear relationship across the two cultivated jute genomes. A total of 173 syntenic DE-TF genes harboring SSRs were identified within conserved syntenic blocks, demonstrating a high level of evolutionary preservation of regulatory loci associated with stress adaptation. The syntenic links were predominantly concentrated among Chr04, Chr03, and Chr06 in both species, where several dense TF clusters were observed, indicating the presence of a transcriptional regulatory gene-dense region that have been selectively retained following divergence of the two species. (Fig. 5A). Classification of these 173 syntenic DE transcription factor genes harboring SSRs revealed a broad representation across 35 TF families, reflecting the complex regulatory network underlying abiotic stress responses in jute. The most abundant families were MYB and bHLH (18 gene pairs each), followed closely by WRKY (17), MADS-box (13), and AP2/ERF (11) transcription factors, which together accounted for nearly half of all conserved pairs. Several other stress- and development-related TF families such as HSF, SBP, HB, C2C2, bZIP, and AUX/IAA were also substantially represented, whereas a few families including GRAS, TCP, TUB, HMG, and RWP-RK were present as single syntenic pairs (Fig. 5B). Out of the 173 syntenic gene pairs, 155 pairs were also identified as orthologous, while 18 pairs did not show orthologous relationships. Among the identified orthologous DE TF–SSR gene pairs, 49 in Corchorus capsularis and 31 in C. olitorius were found to be localized within the Chromosome 4 hotspot regions. The high proportion of overlapping pairs suggests that most syntenic genes are conserved at both the genomic and sequence levels.

Gene ontology analysis

Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis of differentially expressed TF genes harbouring SSRs revealed their involvement in a wide range of cellular and regulatory functions in both CC and CO (Fig. 6). Within the Biological Process category, the predominant terms included cellular process (230 in CO, 246 in CC), biological regulation (221 in CO, 241 in CC), and response to stimulus (144 in CO, 153 in CC), emphasizing the central role of these TFs in stress perception and signalling. Additional BP terms, such as developmental process and reproductive process, were also enriched, indicating contributions to growth and developmental adjustments under stress. Stress-specific functions such as immune system process, homeostatic process, and detoxification were also represented, although at a lower magnitude.

For the Molecular Function category, the most abundant annotation was transcription regulator activity (188 in CO, 214 in CC), consistent with the dataset being TF-focused. Significant representation was also observed for binding functions (128 in CO, 150 in CC), including nucleic acid interactions necessary for transcriptional modulation. Smaller but important contributions from catalytic, transporter, and molecular transducer activities further suggest functional versatility in response regulation.

Within the Cellular Component category, the major assignment was linked to cellular anatomical structure (183 in CO, 189 in CC), followed by protein-containing complexes, implying that many of these TFs operate as components of multi-protein transcriptional regulatory complexes.

Polymorphic SSR markers and allele characterization

From the pool of 728 and 702 SSRs identified in CC and CO (Supplementary Table S3, S4), 22 SSR markers were experimentally validated based on clear polymorphism, chromosomal coverage, and TF family representation (Supplementary Table S2). The allele number per locus ranged from 2 to 4. Markers such as TFDSSR3 and TFDSSR5 amplified the highest number of alleles (4 alleles), whereas several markers, including TFDSSR2, TFDSSR6, TFDSSR9, TFDSSR12, TFDSSR13, TFDSSR15, TFDSSR16, TFDSSR18, TFDSSR19, TFDSSR20, and TFDSSR21, were biallelic. Minor allele frequency (MAF) varied widely, from 0.0167 (TFDSSR5) to 0.4138 (TFDSSR12), indicating the presence of both rare and relatively frequent alleles. Polymorphic information content (PIC) values ranged from 0.18 (TFDSSR6) to 0.67 (TFDSSR3), with several markers, including TFDSSR1, TFDSSR3, TFDSSR4, TFDSSR5, TFDSSR7, TFDSSR8 and TFDSSR11, showing PIC values above 0.55 and thus high discriminatory power. The observed heterozygosity (Ho) and expected heterozygosity (He) were estimated across 22 SSR loci to assess genetic variation among the studied genotypes. The expected heterozygosity (He) ranged from 0.180 to 0.676, with a mean value of 0.482, indicating moderate levels of genetic diversity within the population. In contrast, the observed heterozygosity (Ho) was consistently low across loci, ranging from 0.000 to 0.071 with an average of 0.009. Most loci exhibited little to no heterozygosity, suggesting high homozygosity among majority of genotypes. However, it is important to note that agarose gel electrophoresis has limited resolution for detecting small allele differences, particularly in the range of 2–4 bp. Therefore, closely sized alleles may not be fully resolved using this method. Despite this limitation, the observed polymorphism was sufficient for comparative analysis and genetic diversity assessment. The use of higher-resolution techniques such as polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis or capillary electrophoresis could further improve allele discrimination in future studies. These results confirm that the TFDSSR markers are informative and suitable for genetic diversity and population structure analyses in Corchorus species.

STRUCTURE analysis and cluster determination

Population structure was evaluated using the Bayesian clustering approach implemented in STRUCTURE, and the most likely number of clusters (K) was determined using the Evanno ΔK method in StructureSelector. The ΔK plot revealed a prominent peak at K = 3 (ΔK = 21.68), indicating that three genetically distinct clusters best represent the population structure in the dataset. A minor secondary peak at K = 7 (ΔK = 11.57) suggests the presence of potential sub-structuring within major clusters, but the magnitude of the main ΔK peak strongly supports K = 3 as the optimal clustering solution (Fig. 7).

STRUCTURE analysis at K = 3 suggested genetic differentiation between CC and CO. Most CC individuals were primarily associated with red cluster, whereas CO individuals were mainly associated with blue cluster, with some degree of overlap observed in a few samples. Overall, the STRUCTURE results suggest the presence of major genetic groupings, which are broadly supported by PCA and Neighbor-Joining analyses, while indicating that finer-scale relationships require cautious interpretation.

Genetic distance and clustering

The NJ dendrogram resolved the genotypes into 7 distinct clusters (Fig. 8), revealing both species-specific grouping and mixed associations. The genetic distance among genotypes ranged from 0.091(CIN-540 and CEX-39) to 0.773 (CIN-534 and OIN-578), indicating the presence of both closely related and highly divergent accessions within the panel.

The Neighbor-Joining dendrogram based on Nei’s genetic distance grouped the 30 jute genotypes into seven distinct clusters (Fig. 8A). One genotype, CIN-533, remained unclustered and did not group with any of the defined clusters, indicating its distinct genetic position. Clusters I and II consisted exclusively of genotypes belonging to Corchorus capsularis, forming relatively compact groups that reflect close genetic relationships within this species. In contrast, Cluster III comprised entirely of C. olitorius genotypes, indicating clear species-level differentiation. Clusters V and VII each contained four genotypes and exhibited mixed composition, with one C. capsularis genotype and three C. olitorius genotypes in each cluster. This pattern suggests partial genetic overlap or shared allelic composition between the two species in these clusters. Overall, the clustering pattern demonstrates a general separation between C. capsularis and C. olitorius genotypes, with occasional mixed clustering likely reflecting genetic proximity or shared ancestry among certain accessions.

Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was performed to assess the genetic relationships among the 30 jute genotypes based on SSR marker data (Fig. 8B). The first two principal components (PC1 and PC2) explained 32.59% and 21.47% of the total variation, respectively, accounting for a cumulative variation of 54.06%. The PCA plot revealed a clear separation between genotypes of Corchorus capsularis and C. olitorius. Genotypes belonging to C. capsularis were grouped within a relatively compact region of the plot, indicating lower genetic dispersion and higher similarity among accessions. In contrast, C. olitorius genotypes were more widely distributed across the PCA space, reflecting greater genetic diversity within this species. Specific genotype-level comparisons further supported this consistency. For example, CMU-41 and CMU-49 were closely associated in the NJ dendrogram and also appeared in close proximity in the PCA plot. Similarly, genotypes such as OIN-204, OIN-1078, and OIN-375 were positioned on separate branches in the dendrogram and were also widely separated in PCA space, reflecting substantial genetic divergence. Likewise, OIN-783 and OIN-259 appeared as divergent genotypes in both analyses, indicating unique genetic backgrounds. Although minor differences in the relative positioning of some genotypes were observed between the two methods, the overall clustering pattern remained consistent. These differences can be attributed to the methodological distinctions between distance-based clustering and multivariate ordination. Overall, the agreement between PCA and NJ dendrogram analyses provides robust evidence for species-level differentiation and highlights greater genetic variability within C. olitorius compared to C. capsularis.

Discussion

Abiotic stresses such as drought, salinity, and waterlogging are among the key environmental factors limiting the productivity of jute (Corchorus spp.). Understanding the genetic and regulatory basis of stress tolerance in the two cultivated species, CC and CO, is essential for developing climate-resilient cultivars. In this study, we combined transcriptome-wide differential expression, transcription factor (TF) identification, SSR mining, chromosomal mapping, dual synteny, and population structure analysis to discover and characterize transcription factor-derived SSR (TFDSSR) markers associated with abiotic stress response. The integration of these datasets provides valuable insights into regulatory evolution and genetic diversity in cultivated jute. Transcriptomic analysis revealed broad and stress-specific transcriptional responses, with waterlogging inducing the highest number of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in both species. Such massive transcriptional reprogramming under hypoxia has also been reported in tomato roots and Gossypium purpurascens, where waterlogging or salt exposure activated anaerobic metabolism, ROS detoxification, and hormone crosstalk [34, 35]. Species-specific differences were evident: CC showed greater transcriptional activation under drought and salinity, whereas CO exhibited a stronger response to waterlogging. These patterns align with phenotypic evidence showing relatively higher salt and drought tolerance in CC [8, 9] and improved flooding tolerance in CO [10]. Such contrasting expression amplitudes reflect differential adaptive strategies that evolved under distinct ecological and domestication histories. Among DEGs, a large cohort encoded TFs belonging mainly to the MYB, bHLH, NAC, WRKY, AP2/ERF, HB, and C2C2 families. These TFs act as central regulators of abiotic stress signalling, modulating osmotic adjustment, ROS scavenging, and hormone-responsive pathways [11, 13, 36]. Our analysis revealed that CC is preferentially enriched with MYB and NAC transcription factor families, whereas CO harbors a higher representation of WRKY and C2C2 members. Lineage-specific expansion of TF families has been documented in plants broadly, where such diversification contributes to adaptive capacities under environmental stress [37]. In cotton, asymmetric expansion and diversification of NAC genes during polyploidization have been reported [38], and the WRKY family shows extensive stress-associated diversification [39]. Similarly, genome-wide characterization of WRKY TFs in kenaf indicates expansion within stress-responsive pathways [24]. The strong representation of bHLH and AP2/ERF families supports their well-known role in integrating ABA and ethylene signalling to control transcriptional cascades during salinity and hypoxia stress [40, 41].

A total of 1,430 SSRs were identified across stress-responsive TFs—728 in CC and 702 in CO. The predominance of di- and tri-nucleotide repeats, particularly A/T-rich motifs (AA/TT, AG/CT, AAG/CTT), mirrors the SSR composition in kenaf and Sudan grass transcriptomes [16, 42]. The majority of these SSRs occurred in untranslated regions (UTRs), which frequently influence promoter activity, mRNA stability, and translation efficiency [43]. Such localization suggests that variation in SSR repeat number may fine-tune TF expression, thereby contributing to stress adaptation. The scarcity of tetra- and penta-nucleotide motifs (< 1%) is consistent with selective constraints on open reading frames and aligns with SSR profiles in other plant transcriptomes [16]. The slightly higher SSR abundance in CC could reflect stronger regulatory plasticity under salinity and drought conditions.

Chromosomal mapping revealed that TF-linked SSR genes are unevenly distributed, with clustering on specific chromosomes. Notably, chromosome 4 emerged as a shared differentially expressed regulatory gene-dense region in both jute genomes, while chromosomes 3, 5, and 6 in CC and chromosome 6 in CO showed additional dense regions. Such non-random chromosomal distribution of transcription factor genes has also been observed in other plant species. In rice (Oryza sativa), the distribution of WRKY transcription factor family members are located across all 12 chromosomes but are not evenly distributed, with some chromosomes carrying more WRKY loci than others, indicating biased chromosomal localization of stress-responsive TFs in the genome [44, 45]. Similar distribution pattern of bHLH TF family in tomato (Solanum lycopersicum), across the 12 chromosomes, which is consistent with the idea that stress-related TF genes cluster uniformly spread across the genome [46]. The conserved enrichment of TFDSSRs on chromosome 4 in jute suggests that this region may represent a gene-rich area potentially associated with stress-responsive regulatory elements. This regulatory gene-dense region provides a logical starting point for fine-mapping and marker–trait association studies.

Dual synteny analysis revealed strong collinearity between CC and CO, identifying 173 syntenic TF-SSR gene pairs positioned within conserved chromosomal blocks. The most abundantly represented transcription factor families-MYB, bHLH, WRKY, and AP2/ERF-are widely recognized as evolutionarily conserved regulators of plant stress responses across angiosperms [13]. The high degree of syntenic conservation and coding-sequence similarity observed among these orthologous TFs indicates strong evolutionary constraint, suggesting that adaptive divergence between the two Corchorus species is unlikely to be driven by extensive protein-coding sequence variation. Importantly, the conservation of SSR motifs within these orthologous transcription factors further supports the notion of preserved regulatory architecture. Together, these findings imply that any species-specific functional differentiation, if present, is more likely to involve subtle regulatory modulation—such as quantitative changes in gene expression—rather than major structural divergence of the transcription factors themselves. Collectively, this evolutionary stability highlights the translational potential of the identified TF-SSRs across Corchorus species. The small subset of syntenic but non-orthologous gene pairs may reflect gene duplication events, lineage-specific divergence, or genome rearrangements. These results highlight that although synteny and orthology are distinct concepts, they can show considerable correspondence in conserved genomic regions. Despite the close evolutionary relationship and high sequence conservation between Corchorus capsularis and C. olitorius, differences in chromosomal localization of transcription factor genes can be attributed to genome structural variations. Comparative genomic studies have revealed extensive synteny between the two species, along with large-scale rearrangements such as inversions and translocations that can reposition orthologous genes onto different chromosomes [4]. In addition, gene duplication and lineage-specific expansion events may further influence gene distribution across chromosomes. Therefore, the observed differences in gene localization likely reflect genome evolution rather than a lack of sequence conservation between the two jute species.

Gene Ontology (GO) analysis highlighted significant enrichment in response to stimulus, biological regulation, and cellular process under the Biological Process category, and transcription regulator activity and DNA-binding under Molecular Function. These categories correspond to central regulatory functions governing stress perception and signal integration. Similar enrichment patterns were observed in cotton and maize under abiotic stress, where TFs orchestrated the ROS and hormone networks required for homeostasis [12, 47]. Terms such as homeostatic process and detoxification align with nitric oxide- and ROS-based signalling modules that confer salt tolerance [48]. The detection of developmental and reproductive process terms also reflects the known role of TFs in balancing growth and defence trade-offs [11]. Overall, the GO profile underscores that the SSR-harbouring TFs identified here act at the nexus of stress signalling and developmental control.

To further evaluate the effectiveness and informativeness of the developed markers, we assessed their polymorphism across the jute panel. The 22 validated SSR markers proved moderately polymorphic across our jute panel. Alleles per locus ranged from 2 to 4, which is lower than in some other studies. For example, Aljuhani et al. [49] reported an average of 7 alleles per locus using eight SSRs on 72 C. olitorius accessions, and Zhang et al. [50] found 3–15 alleles per primer (avg 6.6 loci amplified per primer) in 58 jute accessions. These discrepancies likely reflect sample size and marker choice: our SSRs were derived from transcription factor genes (possibly more conserved), and our panel was smaller. Despite this, several of our SSRs had high informativeness. The PIC values we observed (0.18–0.67, with seven markers > 0.55) are broadly in line with the literature. Saha et al. [51] found a mean PIC of 0.34 (with some > 0.5) across 43 SSRs, and Adeyemo et al. [52] reported a mean PIC ≈ 0.42 (range 0.06–0.73) for 13 SSRs in C. olitorius. High PIC of 0.67 in our study, indicats high discriminatory power of our markers. Overall, the PIC and allele-number variation we observed confirm that these SSRs can capture genetic diversity, even if absolute allele counts per locus were modest. A limitation of this study is the validation of SSR markers using only 22 loci and allele resolution based on 3.5% agarose gel electrophoresis, which has limited ability to distinguish small allele size differences (2–4 bp). Although agarose gels are widely used for genotyping due to their cost-effectiveness and simplicity, this approach may lead to underestimation of allelic diversity and heterozygosity, as closely sized alleles can be scored as identical. Consequently, the reported diversity estimates should be considered conservative. Future studies using higher-resolution methods such as polyacrylamide or capillary electrophoresis would improve allele discrimination.

Our diversity indices show moderate expected heterozygosity (He) but extremely low observed heterozygosity (Ho). We found a mean He ≈ 0.48, higher than values reported in some larger germplasm surveys [53]. The higher He in our study is likely due to pooling two species, which increases overall allelic diversity. In contrast, Ho was near zero (mean 0.009) across loci. This very low heterozygosity stands in stark contrast to some other jute studies: Adeyemo et al. [52] reported mean Ho ≈ 0.60 in Nigerian C. olitorius cultivars. Such a difference likely stems from germplasm differences. Our accessions appear to be largely inbred or fixed lines, whereas Adeyemo’s panel may have included outbred landraces or hybrids.

Population structure analysis identified three major clusters (K = 3), clearly separating C. capsularis and C. olitorius genotypes, with a third cluster representing shared ancestry or sub-structuring. This is in agreement with previous SSR-based studies, which consistently report species-level differentiation in jute germplasm [50]. Similar multi-cluster patterns have also been observed in larger datasets, where primary clusters correspond to species, with additional sub-clusters reflecting geographic origin or breeding history [53]. The presence of individuals with mixed ancestry in the present study may indicate shared ancestral polymorphism or limited marker resolution rather than true recent admixture, particularly given the relatively small number of loci used.

Our NJ dendrogram and PCA corroborated the STRUCTURE clustering. The NJ tree produced seven sub-clusters (with one genotype unclustered), which largely separated the two species. Clusters I–III were species-pure (I–II = C. capsularis; III = C. olitorius). Clusters V and VII were mixed (each containing one C. capsularis and three C. olitorius genotypes), indicating partial overlap. This partial mixing aligns with the STRUCTURE green component that spanned species. PCA showed a tight cluster of C. capsularis genotypes versus a broad scatter of C. olitorius samples – implying greater genetic diversity within C. olitorius. A similar pattern has been reported elsewhere: cultured C. olitorius germplasm often harbors more diversity than the narrower C. capsularis pool. For instance, Zhang et al. [50] noted broad similarity coefficients (0.52–0.91) among jute accessions, consistent with high diversity. In our PCA, pairs of genotypes that clustered together in NJ (e.g. CMU-41/49, OIN-783/259) also grouped closely in PC space, giving confidence in these relationships. Minor differences between methods are expected: distance-based trees and ordination each have strengths. Overall, both approaches agreed on the main signal: species-level differentiation, with C. olitorius showing wider dispersion.

The TFDSSR markers characterized in this study represent functional, gene-anchored tools directly linked to regulatory genes mediating abiotic stress tolerance. Their distribution within conserved TF families and syntenic regions enhances their value for marker-assisted selection (MAS) and genomic selection (GS). Such genic SSRs may offer advantages to anonymous genomic SSRs because they directly tag loci with known biological functions. Future research should validate these markers through expression-trait association, quantitative trait loci (QTL) mapping, and genome-wide association studies (GWAS). Integrating these functional markers with high-throughput genotyping, phenomics, and CRISPR-based editing will accelerate the development of stress-tolerant jute cultivars. As the frequency and intensity of climatic extremes increase, these conserved regulatory markers provide a foundation for breeding resilient and sustainable bast-fibre crops.

Conclusion

This integrated transcriptome-to-molecular marker study delivers a comprehensive functional genomic resource for advancing jute improvement under abiotic stress. By systematically mining stress-responsive transcription factor genes for SSR variation, mapping regulatory gene- dense regions, assessing evolutionary conservation, and characterizing germplasm diversity, we have identified 1430 SSR markers in two jute species and validated TF-derived SSR markers with superior polymorphism. These markers directly tag loci involved in abiotic stress adaptation and enable molecular breeding strategies for developing resilient cultivars. The documented population structure and genetic diversity provide a framework for parental selection and introgression breeding. High syntenic conservation of TF-SSRs across CC and CO supports cross-species marker utility and genomic selection applications. Broadening the marker panel coupled with large-scale phenotyping and QTL mapping, will enhance the identification and utilization of functional genomic markers. This will accelerate stress-adaptive crop improvement in jute and support broader sustainability goals for natural fiber production.
