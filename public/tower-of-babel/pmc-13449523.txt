Effects of two community-based multimodal exercise programs on physical activity, performance, fall prevention, and quality of life in older adults: a randomized clinical trial
Authors: Kazi Md Azman Hossain, Md. Zahid Hossain, Sharmila Jahan, K. M. Amran Hossain, Ehsanur Rahman, Md. Kabir Hossain, Ahmadullah Hil Galeb, Mst Sumya Akter, Jannatul Ferdous Rikti, Md. Feroz Kabir
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAging involves a gradual physiological decline that affects physical activity, performance, fall risk, and quality of life in older adults. Exercise is a recommended intervention to counteract these effects; however, the comparative effectiveness of sensorimotor and strengthening exercise programs in community settings remains limited. Therefore, we aimed to evaluate the effectiveness of two community-based multimodal exercise programs (CMEPs) in improving physical activity, performance, fall prevention, and quality of life in older adults.MethodsA multicenter, randomized clinical trial was conducted with 140 older adults randomly assigned to CMEP-I (sensorimotor exercise) or CMEP-II (strengthening exercise). Both interventions involved 24 supervised sessions over 8 weeks. Outcomes were measured at baseline and post-intervention using the Physical Activity Scale for Elderly (PASE), Short Functional Performance Battery (SPPB), Modified Falls Efficacy Scale (MFES), and 36-Item Short-Form Survey (SF-36).ResultsBoth interventions led to improvements in outcome measures over time. CMEP-I showed significantly superior improvements to CMEP-II in physical activity (PASE, MD = 12.40; 95% CI: 5.92 to 18.88; ηp2 = 0.626), functional performance (SPPB, MD = 0.26; 95% CI: −0.17 to 0.68; ηp2 = 0.137), fall prevention (MFES, MD = 0.37; 95% CI: 0.28 to 0.46; ηp2 = 0.325), and quality of life (SF-36, MD = 3.07; 95% CI: 2.23 to 3.91; ηp2 = 0.642). No serious adverse events were reported.ConclusionsSensorimotor exercise (CMEP-I) showed greater benefits across all outcomes compared with the strengthening exercise (CMEP-II). These findings support the inclusion of sensorimotor CMEPs in community health initiatives as accessible, low-cost strategies for promoting independence and healthy aging in resource-limited settings.Trial registrationThis trial is registered prospectively in the Clinical Trials Registry-India CTRI/2025/03/083260 (Registered on 24/03/2025).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13063-026-09825-x.

Introduction

Demographics are shifting globally, with a trend toward an aging population. Projections show that the number of adults 65 years and older will double to 1.5 billion by 2050. Meanwhile, those 80 years and older are expected to triple from 2019 to 2050, reaching 426 million [1]. Aging gradually impacts physiological systems, causing tissue breakdown, hormonal decline, inflammation imbalance, and a slower metabolism. These changes lead to decreases in nerve density, bone mass, muscle strength, and functional performance, which contribute to frailty and sarcopenia. About 10% of people aged 65 and older and 25–50% of those aged 85 and older are affected [2]. As a result, fall-related injuries account for 38.19% of hospitalizations and 84.6% of deaths among older adults, leading to reduced physical activity, difficulty with daily tasks, and a lower quality of life [3, 4].

In such circumstances, a physical exercise program is a crucial preventive measure to mitigate age-related decline and enhance function in these individuals. The benefits of exercise on physical function and performance are well established [1]. Randomized controlled trials showed that exercise improves risk factors for lifestyle-related diseases and premature death [5–7]. Observational studies indicate that physically active individuals have up to a 72% lower risk of premature all-cause mortality compared with inactive individuals [5, 8]. Current guidelines emphasize that physical activity is a “miracle cure” because it can prevent and help manage many diseases [9]. Commonly recommended exercise approaches include stretching, aerobic training, resistance strengthening, sensorimotor exercise, and endurance activities, which have been shown to improve physical fitness, reduce fall risk, and promote healthy aging [3, 9]. Older adults with poor mobility should perform exercise at least 3 times per week to strengthen major muscle groups, improve balance, and reduce the risk of falls. Those unable to exercise due to poor health should still engage in physical activity according to their abilities [9]. Various exercise interventions have been implemented among healthy and non-healthy older adults across different settings, including community environments, residential care homes, and private homes, with support from professionals or trained facilitators, yet their diversity often leads to inconsistent findings and difficulties in comparing outcomes across studies [1, 5]. However, cost-effective, evidence-based exercise should be integrated into healthcare to improve functional independence, prevent falls, and enhance older adults’ quality of life, with more prospective clinical trials needed to determine the most effective community-level programs [1, 10]. Therefore, this study was designed to evaluate two cost-effective community-based multimodal exercise programs (CMEPs)—sensorimotor and strengthening for older adults.

Sensorimotor exercise is widely recognized as a comprehensive form of balance training that focuses on correcting muscular imbalances, enhancing proprioceptive and somatosensory input, and optimizing motor control through adaptations in the central nervous system [11]. Older adults need to continuously develop these skills to effectively detect obstacles, process sensory information, and generate optimal motor responses during daily activities. The coordinated function of the sensorimotor, vestibular, and visual systems is essential for maintaining dynamic and static stability and balance, and for reducing fall risk. Evidence showed that sensorimotor exercises improve gait and postural control in individuals with Parkinson’s disease, enhance balance and proprioception in those with diabetes, and benefit healthy older adults by increasing physical activity level, functional performance, fall prevention, and quality of life [3, 12, 13]. Strengthening exercise also plays a crucial role in maintaining muscle mass, muscular strength, and functional capacity, thereby mitigating the adverse effects of frailty and sarcopenia. Progressive strengthening training has been shown to enhance neuromuscular function, improve balance, reduce fall risk, and support healthy aging by promoting greater independence and quality of life [3, 10].

Despite extensive evidence supporting the benefits of exercise, direct comparative studies of sensorimotor versus strengthening exercise programs among community-dwelling older adults remain rare. Most previous studies have investigated these interventions separately rather than assessing their relative effectiveness. There are also differences in exercise intensity, duration, and frequency among older adults, which remain inconsistent, and many research interventions are poorly designed [9, 14]. Therefore, this study aimed to compare the effectiveness of well-structured sensorimotor and strengthening exercises within a CMEPs framework [15]. By implementing a structured, clearly defined intervention protocol, this research seeks to determine which approach most effectively improves activities of daily living, enhances functional performance, reduces fall risk, and improves quality of life among community-dwelling older adults. We hypothesized that the community-based sensorimotor exercise program (CMEP-I) would yield greater improvements in outcome measures than another program for community-dwelling older adults.

Methods

Study design and ethics

This study was an assessor- and participant-blinded, multicenter, randomized clinical trial involving two groups: CMEP-I, the sensorimotor exercise group, and CMEP-II, the strengthening exercise group, with a 1:1 ratio by block randomization conducted between July 2025 and February 2026. Each group participated in 24 intervention sessions over 8 weeks. All outcome measurements were taken at two points: baseline and after the 8-week intervention. The study was approved by the Department of Physiotherapy and Rehabilitation at Jashore University of Science and Technology on February 22, 2025. Additionally, this study was prospectively registered as a randomized clinical trial in the Clinical Trials Registry-India (CTRI) on March 24, 2025. Consequently, the research protocol was published on November 14, 2025 [15]. Before participating in this study, participants were informed about the study details, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Sample size calculation

G*Power, version 3.1.9.7 (University of Kiel, Kiel, Germany), was used to determine the required sample size. Based on the calculations, 110 participants were required to achieve an effect size (ES) of 0.54, an α level of 0.05, and a power of 0.80 [16]. Therefore, each group required 55 participants. After accounting for a 20% dropout rate, we increased the number of participants in each group to 70, bringing the total estimated number of participants to 140. Although no single primary outcome was specified, the chosen effect size reflects moderate, clinically meaningful improvements in functional performance and health-related outcomes in older adults, as indicated by similar studies [16]. After reaching the 140-target participant count, recruitment was discontinued.

Participants

Responsible health personnel recruited all participants using a fair initial screening process based on the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Individuals were included if (1) male or female community-dwelling older adults aged 60–80 years, (2) physical independence level ≥18 points according to a 12-item Composite Physical Function (CPF) scale [17], (3) able to walk without walking aids, and (4) Mini-Mental Status Examination (MMSE) score ≥18 [14]. Individuals were excluded if (1) experiencing severe visual disturbances, (2) any soft-tissue or joint injury or fractures within the last 3 months [14], (3) severe heart-disease diagnosis or uncontrolled systemic arterial hypertension, (4) cancer diagnosis and receiving palliative care, (5) taking psychotropic, antiarrhythmic, or hypnotic drugs [3, 13].

Participant recruitment

One hundred of 140 eligible participants were recruited through online and offline advertisements, announcements at religious institutions, and flyers from four community clinics in Jashore, Bangladesh. Initially, interested and eligible participants had informational discussion sessions at their nearest study sites about the study details, plan, objectives, risks, and benefits, followed by a question-and-answer segment. Then, participants completed a written informed consent form before enrollment and could withdraw from the study at any time without affecting their treatment. We ensured that participant recruitment was fair and impartial.

Randomization and blinding

After confirming participant eligibility and obtaining written informed consent, randomization was performed using a simple 1:1 allocation method through the online platform (http://randomizer.org) by maintaining confidentiality [15]. Participants were randomly assigned to two groups, CMEP-I and CMEP-II. The research assistant handled the randomization process but did not take part in the intervention or data collection. Participants, research assistants, assessors, and data statisticians remained blinded to group assignments to ensure unbiased results. However, the nature of the intervention prevents blinding healthcare providers who delivered it. Participants were informed about the interventions but not their specific group allocation. The study was overseen by a trial manager and an independent monitoring team to ensure proper coordination and adherence to the protocol.

Study procedure

Eligible participants were screened in community settings and provided with a study overview and a written consent form. Then, participants were contacted for baseline assessments and the intervention after group allocation. Depending on their group, participants received either a sensorimotor or a strengthening exercise program (CMEP-I or CMEP-II) for 8 weeks. After the 8-week intervention, blinded assessors who worked with each group conducted post-intervention assessments. The study was managed by a dedicated study site responsible for daily operations, including participant recruitment, intervention delivery, data collection, and protocol compliance. A Study Steering Committee provided overall oversight, meeting regularly to review progress, uphold ethical and scientific standards, and guide study conduct. Other support groups helped with logistics, scheduling, and quality control of the intervention. Weekly interim reviews monitored study progress and participant safety and addressed any issues. The procedures for enrollment, assessment, and intervention were maintained in accordance with the predefined study protocol.

Intervention

Eight certified physiotherapists with over 10 years of clinical experience conducted two CMEPs for the study participants. The researcher supervised the interventions and assessments to ensure adherence to the study protocol, but was not directly involved in delivering the exercises. Both intervention groups completed 24 sessions over 8 weeks, with sessions held in small groups of two participants. Certified physiotherapists supervised each session to ensure proper execution and adherence to protocol. Each session started with a warm-up, followed by aerobic exercises, stretching, and the assigned intervention: sensorimotor or strengthening exercises, depending on the group. The session ended with a cool-down. A detailed intervention plan is provided in Table 1 [1, 13, 18–20]. The exercise procedures, dosages, and progression plans were carefully maintained to ensure consistency between the two intervention groups. Intervention fidelity and adherence monitoring

All exercise sessions for both groups were video-recorded, and 20% were randomly selected for review by an independent investigator using standardized checklists to assess protocol adherence, exercise technique, and interventionist competence. Participant adherence was tracked through session attendance logs for all participants in both groups. The number, length, and form of interventions were monitored using a checklist. This method ensured precise monitoring of intervention delivery and participant compliance without overlap or redundancy.

Assessments

Participants were scheduled for baseline assessments during the week before intervention started (week 0). Baseline assessments included sociodemographic data and outcome measurements. Our outcome measurements were selected as four key outcomes, without ranking or designating primary and secondary outcomes, to provide a broader view of the intervention. After the baseline assessment, the first intervention session began within the same week. Post-intervention outcome assessments were conducted after week 8, once the intervention was complete. Independent assessors, blinded to group assignment, carried out all evaluations. They had undergone specialized training, demonstrated inter-rater reliability, and received supervision to ensure consistency and accuracy. To assess blinding integrity, outcome assessors completed a masked assessment form (OA-MF) in which they recorded their best guess of group assignment, their confidence level, and the rationale for their judgment.

Physical activity

Physical activity was assessed using the Physical Activity Scale for the Elderly (PASE), a validated questionnaire that measures physical activity over the past week in older adults. The PASE score was derived from self-reported participation in leisure, household, and work-related activities, with scores ranging from 0 to 400 or higher, where higher scores indicated greater physical activity levels. This scale demonstrated strong reliability, with an intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) ranging from 0.66 to 0.99, confirming its reliability as a measure of habitual physical activity in older adults [19].

Functional performance

Functional performance was assessed using the Short Functional Performance Battery (SPPB), a validated tool that evaluates physical ability in older adults through three main performance-based tests. The battery included (1) a Balance Test with side-by-side, semi-tandem, and tandem stance assessments (scored 0–4); (2) a Gait Speed Test, where participants completed a 4-m walk (scored 0–4); and (3) a Sit-to-Stand Test, involving five repeated chair rises (scored 0–4). Higher scores indicated better functional performance, with a total score ranging from 0 to 12. This scale showed excellent reliability, with ICCs ranging from 0.81 to 0.91 [21, 22].

Fall prevention

Fall risk was assessed using the Modified Falls Efficacy Scale (MFES), a validated 14-item questionnaire that evaluates an individual’s confidence in performing daily activities without falling. Each task was rated on a 10-point scale (0–10). The MFES covers activities in both indoor and outdoor environments, providing a comprehensive measure of fall-related self-efficacy. Higher scores indicate greater confidence and less fear of falling. This scale showed high reliability, with an ICC of 0.95, supporting its application in fall risk assessment among older adults [19, 23].

Quality of life

Quality of life was assessed using the 36-Item Short-Form Survey (SF-36), a validated questionnaire that measures health-related quality of life across eight areas: physical functioning, role limitations due to physical health (role-physical), bodily pain, general health, vitality (energy/fatigue), social functioning, role limitations due to emotional problems (role-emotional), and mental health. Higher scores indicate better health-related quality of life, with values from 0 to 100. This scale showed strong psychometric properties, with an ICC of 0.90, making it a dependable measure of overall well-being in older adults [24, 25].

Safety and adverse effects management

Participant safety was carefully monitored throughout the study. Four independent monitoring staff members and a qualified physician observed participants during and after intervention sessions to detect any unexpected responses. All adverse events, including pain, skin irritation, discomfort, and injuries, were systematically recorded in structured logs and documented in SOAP notes. Participants experiencing discomfort were advised to contact their physiotherapist or healthcare provider for proper management. Continuous monitoring enabled early detection and management of adverse events.

Data management, monitoring, and confidentiality

All participant data were kept confidential. Each participant was assigned a unique ID, and datasets were stored in encrypted formats. Electronic records were secured on password-protected servers, while paper records were verified for accuracy. Double data entry reduced errors. Access to identifiable data was limited to the principal investigator, trial manager, and designated monitors. Four independent monitors supervised recruitment, intervention, and adverse event reporting to ensure adherence to the protocol and data integrity. The final dataset was accessible only to authorized personnel, and all findings were reported in aggregate using de-identified data to maintain confidentiality and meet ethical standards.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS version 25.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), while categorical variables were shown as frequencies and percentages. Normality was tested with the Shapiro–Wilk test and Q-Q plot inspection. Intervention effects were analyzed with repeated-measures ANOVA to evaluate within-group changes over time, between-group differences, and time-by-group interactions. Where significant interaction effects were observed, post hoc pairwise comparisons of estimated marginal means were conducted with Bonferroni adjustment to control for multiple testing across outcome measures. Outcomes were reported as mean ± SD, along with T and F values, 95% confidence intervals, and partial eta squared (ηp2) as a measure of effect size. Analyses followed the intention-to-treat principle, including all 140 randomized participants. Missing data were handled using multiple imputation under a missing-at-random assumption. Fully conditional specification (FCS) was used to generate 20 imputed datasets, incorporating baseline demographic variables, group allocation, and all repeated outcome measures in the imputation model. Pooled estimates were derived according to Rubin’s rules. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05*, p < 0.01**, and p < 0.001***.

Participants and public involvement

Participants and the general public did not participate in designing, reporting, or sharing the results of this study.

Results

Participant flow and baseline characteristics

A total of 140 community-dwelling older adults enrolled in this study. Participants were randomly assigned to two intervention groups: CMEP-I (n = 70) and CMEP-II (n = 70). During the intervention period, 13 participants did not complete the post-intervention assessment, resulting in 64 participants completing the CMEP-I program and 63 completing the CMEP-II program. Baseline demographic, lifestyle, and clinical characteristics of the participants are summarized in Table 2. Overall, the two groups were comparable across all measured baseline variables. The average age of participants was in the mid-sixties in both groups, and the gender distribution was comparable, with just over half male participants in each group. The average body mass index was about 27 kg/m2 in both groups, indicating similar anthropometric profiles. Likewise, cardiovascular indicators, including resting heart rate and both systolic and diastolic blood pressure, were very similar between the groups. Regarding lifestyle characteristics, most participants in both groups reported never smoking, infrequent physical activity, and typically sleeping 7–8 h per night. Additionally, roughly half of the participants were still actively working, and the most commonly reported fall history involved one or two previous falls. The prevalence of common chronic conditions, such as hypertension, diabetes mellitus, osteoarthritis, rheumatoid arthritis, and other comorbidities, was also similarly distributed between the two groups. This indicates that the randomization process successfully created two demographically and clinically similar groups before the intervention, thereby enhancing the internal validity of subsequent between-group comparisons. Changes in outcome measures over time

As shown in Table 3, baseline characteristics revealed very minimal differences between the CMEP-I and CMEP-II groups for all outcome measures, indicating that the groups were comparable at study entry. Repeated-measures ANOVA showed significant main effects of time for all outcomes (all p < 0.001), suggesting improvements in both groups over the 8-week intervention period. Significant group × time interaction effects were also observed, indicating that the amount of change over time differed between the groups. Physical activity

Physical activity (PASE) scores were comparable between groups at baseline (MD = −0.47; 95% CI: −7.04 to 6.10). After the 8-week intervention, both groups showed increases in PASE scores; however, the CMEP-I demonstrated superior improvements compared to the CMEP-II group (MD = 12.40; 95% CI: 5.92 to 18.88). A highly significant group × time interaction was observed (F = 231.364, p < 0.001, ηp2 = 0.626), showing that the increase in physical activity over time differed between the groups.

Functional performance

Functional performance (SPPB) scores were comparable between groups at baseline (MD = −0.37; 95% CI: −0.82 to 0.07). After the 8-week intervention, both groups showed increases in SPPB scores; however, the CMEP-I demonstrated superior improvements compared to the CMEP-II group (MD = 0.26; 95% CI: −0.17 to 0.68). Consequently, a highly significant group × time interaction was observed (F = 21.899, p < 0.001, ηp2 = 0.137), indicating that the pattern of change over time differed between groups.

Fall prevention

Fall prevention (MFES) scores were comparable at baseline (MD = −0.05; 95% CI: −0.14 to 0.05). After the 8-week intervention, MFES scores increased in both groups, with the CMEP-I demonstrating superior improvements compared to the CMEP-II group (MD = 0.37; 95% CI: 0.28 to 0.46), reflecting greater confidence in performing daily activities without falling. A highly significant group × time interaction was also observed (F = 66.587, p < 0.001, ηp2 = 0.325), indicating that the improvements in fall-related self-efficacy differed between the groups over time.

Quality of life

Health-related quality of life (SF-36) scores did not differ significantly between groups at baseline (MD = −0.80; 95% CI: −1.60 to 0.01). After the 8-week intervention, both groups showed improvements in SF-36 scores; however, the CMEP-I demonstrated superior improvements compared to the CMEP-II group (MD = 3.07; 95% CI: 2.23 to 3.91). A highly significant group × time interaction was observed (F = 247.122, p < 0.001, ηp2 = 0.642), suggesting that improvements in quality of life over time differed between the two groups.

Brief details of each outcome subdomain, along with their corresponding measurements and statistical analyses, are included in Supplementary file 1. These additional results provide a more detailed view of the intervention effects and aid in a clearer interpretation of the study findings.

Complications and adverse events

A small number of minor adverse events were observed during the intervention period. Overall, six participants reported transient complications, with a slightly higher occurrence in the CMEP-I group (4 participants) compared to the CMEP-II group (2 participants). The reported events mainly involved temporary musculoskeletal discomfort, lower-limb fatigue, mild dizziness, or balance issues during exercise sessions. These events were mild to moderate and were effectively managed with brief rest periods, temporary adjustments to exercise intensity, or short breaks from training. Importantly, all symptoms resolved on their own without medical treatment, and no serious adverse events or long-term problems were reported. Despite these minor issues, all affected participants were able to safely continue and complete the intervention, showing that the exercise programs were generally well tolerated and safe under supervised conditions (Table 4). Discussion

This randomized clinical trial provides robust evidence that a structured, community-based multimodal exercise program can significantly enhance physical activity, functional performance, fall prevention, or fall-related self-efficacy, and health-related quality of life among community-dwelling older adults. Across the 8-week intervention period, both exercise approaches produced significant improvements in functional health outcomes; however, the sensorimotor exercise program (CMEP-I) consistently demonstrated superior benefits across all outcome measures. Importantly, beyond statistical significance, the magnitude of the observed effects indicates clinically meaningful functional improvements that may translate into greater independence, safer mobility, enhanced community participation, and improved overall well-being among older adults. The absence of significant baseline differences between groups strengthens the internal validity of the findings and confirms that the observed changes were attributable to the interventions rather than pre-existing group disparities. Furthermore, the significant time effects and group × time interactions identified across all outcome measures indicate that the trajectory and magnitude of improvement differed substantially between the two intervention approaches.

Physical activity, measured using the PASE, improved significantly in both groups; however, the sensorimotor program produced substantially greater overall gains than the strengthening program. The post-intervention between-group mean difference was 12.40 points (95% CI: 5.92 to 18.88), accompanied by a very large interaction effect size (ηp2 = 0.626), indicating a strong and clinically meaningful effect of the sensorimotor-focused intervention on habitual physical activity behavior. Physical activity represents a critical determinant of healthy aging because it directly influences metabolic health, functional independence, psychosocial well-being, and long-term disability risk in older adults [19, 26]. The greater improvement observed in the sensorimotor group may be attributable to the multidimensional characteristics of sensorimotor exercise [27]. Unlike isolated strengthening exercises, sensorimotor exercises simultaneously target balance control, proprioception, neuromuscular coordination, and postural stability. These physiological and neuromotor adaptations may enhance movement confidence, reduce perceived barriers to activity participation, and improve the efficiency and safety of daily movement patterns [1, 13, 27]. Enhanced proprioceptive feedback and neuromuscular control likely contributed to greater participation in routine and recreational physical activities by reducing movement uncertainty and fear associated with instability. The very large effect size suggests that the intervention influenced not only exercise capacity but also behavioral participation in daily physical activities, which represents a clinically meaningful outcome for healthy aging. Previous evidence has similarly demonstrated that multimodal balance-focused exercise programs increase habitual physical activity by enhancing perceived movement competence and reducing fear of movement among older adults [13, 28]. The present findings reinforce this evidence and suggest that sensorimotor-focused multimodal exercise may be particularly effective in promoting active lifestyles among older adults in community settings.

Functional performance, assessed using the SPPB, also improved significantly over time. Although the post-intervention between-group mean difference was relatively modest (MD = 0.26; 95% CI: −0.17 to 0.68), the interaction effect size remained moderate (ηp2 = 0.137), indicating meaningful differences in functional improvement between interventions over time. Both sensorimotor and strengthening-based exercise programs can enhance basic functional abilities, including balance, gait speed, and lower-extremity strength. Improvements in SPPB scores likely reflect physiological changes, including increased muscle strength, improved motor unit recruitment, and enhanced neuromuscular coordination. Strengthening exercises primarily target major lower-extremity muscle groups, particularly the quadriceps and hip stabilizers, which are vital for mobility-related tasks such as walking, climbing stairs, and sit-to-stand transfers. In contrast, sensorimotor exercises predominantly improve postural regulation, dynamic balance, and movement control, thereby facilitating more efficient and coordinated functional movement patterns. Because both physiological mechanisms contribute to functional mobility, comparable improvements observed in overall SPPB scores across groups align with previous evidence demonstrating that both resistance and balance-focused exercise programs can significantly improve lower-extremity functional performance among older adults in community settings [13, 28, 29].

Fall prevention outcomes further highlight the superior benefits of the sensorimotor approach. The CMEP-I group demonstrated significantly greater improvements in MFES scores, with a between-group mean difference of 0.37 points (95% CI: 0.28 to 0.46) and a large interaction effect size (ηp2 = 0.325). This large effect size indicates clinically meaningful enhancement in confidence during daily functional activities, which is highly relevant because fear of falling itself is an independent predictor of activity restriction, physical deconditioning, social withdrawal, functional decline, and future fall risk among older adults [30]. Sensorimotor exercise directly targets several physiological systems associated with fall prevention. Balance exercises enhance postural reflexes, proprioceptive sensitivity, vestibular integration, anticipatory postural adjustments, and reactive balance control, all of which are essential for maintaining stability during dynamic movement. Repeated exposure to progressively challenging balance tasks may also improve central sensory integration and adaptive motor responses to unexpected perturbations. Clinically, these adaptations may help interrupt the cycle of fear of falling, reduced activity participation, progressive deconditioning, and declining functional independence commonly observed in aging populations. These combined physical and psychological adaptations likely account for the greater improvements in fall-related self-efficacy observed in the sensorimotor group [27, 28]. Similar findings have been reported in previous studies, demonstrating that balance-focused and sensorimotor exercise significantly reduces fall risk and fear of falling among older adults [13, 27, 31].

Health-related quality of life outcomes demonstrated a similar pattern, with both interventions improving SF-36 scores, although the sensorimotor program resulted in significantly greater improvements, with a post-intervention mean difference of 3.07 points (95% CI: 2.23 to 3.91) and a very large interaction effect size (ηp2 = 0.642). The magnitude of this effect suggests that the intervention influenced multiple interconnected dimensions of health beyond physical functioning alone, including psychosocial well-being, emotional health, vitality, social participation, and self-perceived health status. Improvements were consistently observed across all SF-36 domains, particularly physical functioning, social functioning, mental health, and role-emotional components. From a clinical standpoint, enhanced quality of life in older adults is especially meaningful because it reflects improved independence, greater social participation, increased emotional resilience, and enhanced perception of overall health and functional capability. Sensorimotor exercise may provide broader psychological and psychosocial benefits due to increased cognitive engagement, movement variability, coordination demands, attentional processing, and social interaction associated with group-based exercise participation. The very large effect size observed for SF-36 outcomes further indicates that the intervention produced substantial multidimensional benefits likely to be meaningful in real-world community functioning and healthy aging [27].

Subdomain analysis

Detailed analysis of the outcome subdomains provides additional insights into the multidimensional physiological and functional effects of the two exercise interventions. Improvements on the PASE scores were observed across all three domains—leisure, household, and work-related activities—indicating enhanced overall participation in daily physical activity following the intervention. The pattern of improvement suggests that the CMEP-I program produced greater gains in the leisure and household activity domains, which are closely associated with community participation, recreational movement, mobility confidence, and coordinated functional activity performance. In contrast, work-related activities demonstrated either comparable or slightly greater improvement in the CMEP-II group, implying that interventions emphasizing muscular strengthening may better support sustained or repetitive physical tasks that rely more on muscular endurance and force-generating capacity. Similarly, improvements were observed across all SPPB subcomponents, including balance, gait speed, and chair-stand performance. The CMEP-I intervention demonstrated more pronounced improvements in balance performance, consistent with exercise approaches that emphasize postural regulation, neuromuscular coordination, proprioceptive integration, and sensorimotor adaptability. Improvements in gait speed were observed in both groups, with relatively similar magnitudes, suggesting that both approaches contribute to improved walking efficiency and functional mobility. Conversely, chair-stand performance demonstrated relatively greater improvement in the CMEP-II group, consistent with the physiological adaptations associated with strength-focused exercise programs that enhance lower-extremity muscle force and functional power during sit-to-stand activities. Collectively, these findings suggest that sensorimotor and strengthening-based interventions may influence distinct yet complementary physiological pathways: sensorimotor exercise primarily targets neuromuscular coordination and postural regulation, whereas strengthening exercise predominantly enhances muscular force generation and structural support. Analysis of fall prevention or fall-related self-efficacy using the MFES, covering 14 everyday functional activities, further revealed significant improvements in perceived confidence across tasks such as dressing, bathing, meal preparation, transfers, indoor and outdoor ambulation, and community mobility. Although both interventions improved confidence across most activities, tasks requiring dynamic balance, environmental negotiation, multidirectional movement, and coordinated postural adjustments demonstrated greater improvement in the CMEP-I group, reflecting the important contribution of sensorimotor and balance-oriented adaptations in enhancing perceived movement stability during complex functional activities. Conversely, several routine tasks requiring lower-extremity stability and physical support demonstrated comparable, and sometimes greater, improvements in the CMEP-II group, suggesting that increased muscular strength may enhance perceived physical safety and movement control during physically demanding movements. A comparable multidimensional pattern was also observed across the SF-36 health-related quality-of-life domains, including physical functioning, role limitations due to physical health, bodily pain, general health perception, vitality, social functioning, emotional role limitations, and mental health. The CMEP-I intervention demonstrated greater improvements across all SF-36 domains, likely reflecting the combined effects of improved balance performance, improved mobility confidence, greater participation in daily activities, and broader psychosocial adaptation. Overall, the subdomain analyses indicate that both interventions produced meaningful improvements across multiple aspects of physical activity, functional performance, fall-related confidence, and quality of life. However, the CMEP-I appears to provide broader benefits in balance control, dynamic mobility, and activity participation, while the CMEP-II program has particular advantages in muscular strength, functional stability, and force-dependent functional tasks. These findings highlight the complementary physiological mechanisms underlying the two exercise approaches—namely, neuromuscular coordination and sensorimotor regulation versus muscular force production and structural stability—and emphasize their potential to improve functional independence and overall well-being in community-dwelling older adults.

The physiological mechanisms underlying these improvements are likely multifactorial. Sensorimotor exercise simultaneously stimulates the somatosensory, vestibular, and visual systems, thereby strengthening neural pathways involved in balance regulation, postural control, and coordinated movement execution. This neural adaptation may improve proprioceptive precision, sensorimotor integration, movement efficiency, and central nervous system responsiveness during dynamic functional activities [13, 27, 28]. In contrast, strengthening exercises predominantly induce musculoskeletal adaptations, including increased muscle cross-sectional area, improved force production, and greater joint stability [5, 9]. When implemented within a structured multimodal exercise framework, these physiological adaptations may interact synergistically to enhance mobility, independence, functional safety, and overall well-being. The larger effect sizes observed in the sensorimotor-focused program further suggest that interventions that simultaneously target both neural and musculoskeletal systems may yield broader, more clinically relevant functional benefits than interventions that emphasize muscular strengthening alone. Importantly, these findings have major implications for clinical practice and public health, particularly within South Asia and other low- and middle-income regions where access to rehabilitation and geriatric exercise services is often limited. The large effect sizes observed for physical activity, fall prevention, and quality of life outcomes suggest that community-based sensorimotor multimodal exercise may represent a clinically effective, scalable, and cost-efficient strategy for promoting healthy aging at the population level. Because the interventions required relatively minimal equipment and were successfully implemented in community settings, they may be feasible to integrate into primary healthcare systems, community rehabilitation services, geriatric wellness programs, senior community centers, and population-based healthy aging initiatives. From a practical perspective, incorporating sensorimotor components into a routine community exercise program may help reduce fall-related healthcare burden, preserve long-term functional independence, improve psychosocial well-being, and potentially delay disability progression among older adults. Therefore, the present findings support the inclusion of structured sensorimotor multimodal exercise interventions as part of evidence-based healthy aging strategies, particularly in resource-limited community settings.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several key strengths. The randomized design with assessor and participant blinding improves internal validity and reduces the risk of measurement and selection bias. The multicenter recruitment strategy and relatively large sample size for a community-based intervention increase the reliability of the findings. The use of validated outcome measures—including PASE, SPPB, MFES, and SF-36—enabled a thorough assessment of both physical and psychosocial aspects of healthy aging. Additionally, the standardized detailed intervention protocol and monitoring of intervention fidelity ensured consistency across physiotherapists and study sites. The inclusion of intention-to-treat analysis further strengthens the robustness of the results by addressing potential attrition.

Several limitations should also be recognized. First, the study was conducted in a specific geographic area in Bangladesh, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other cultural or healthcare settings. Second, the intervention lasted 8 weeks; although this period was enough to observe significant improvements, the long-term sustainability of these benefits remains uncertain. Third, although participant adherence was tracked via attendance records, objectively measuring physical activity outside intervention sessions was not possible, which may have affected the results. Finally, multiple outcomes were evaluated simultaneously, which could increase the risk of type I error despite statistical corrections. Future research should include longer follow-up periods, use wearable devices for objective activity monitoring, and conduct cost-effectiveness analyses to better assess the potential for wider implementation of community-based exercise programs.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that structured community-based multimodal exercise programs can improve physical activity levels, functional performance, fall-related confidence, and health-related quality of life among community-dwelling older adults. Both the sensorimotor and strengthening exercise interventions produced beneficial outcomes; however, the sensorimotor-focused program showed comparatively greater improvements across all outcome measures. These findings may be explained by enhanced neuromuscular coordination, improved balance control, increased movement confidence, and greater participation in daily activities associated with sensorimotor training. The findings highlight the potential value of incorporating sensorimotor components into community-based exercise strategies to promote healthy aging and functional independence in older populations. Given its relatively low cost, accessibility, and adaptability to community settings, the CMEP may represent a feasible approach for supporting healthy aging, particularly in low-resource settings. Integration of such programs into community and primary healthcare services could contribute to fall-risk reduction, maintenance of functional ability, improvement in quality of life, and potentially reduced healthcare burden associated with age-related functional decline.
