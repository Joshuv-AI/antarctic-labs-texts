Preoperative nutritional support in children with Hirschsprung disease: a prospective multicenter open-label randomized controlled trial
Authors: Hong-yi Zhang, Ke Chen, Yanmin Zhang, Tingting Gao, Yuanyuan Huang, Liang Ge, Yuhang Yuan, Guofeng Zhang, Heying Yang, Hongxia Ren, Jianghua Zhan, Weijue Xu, Xiang Liu, Tianqi Zhu, Jiexiong Feng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Preoperative undernutrition is prevalent among children with Hirschsprung disease (HSCR). To evaluate whether preoperative nutritional support reduces the incidence of postoperative HSCR-associated enterocolitis (HAEC), we conducted a prospective, multicenter, open-label randomized controlled trial. The trial was initiated on January 1, 2021 and completed on October 31, 2022. It was carried out across seven tertiary hospitals in China. A total of 110 patients admitted for pull-through surgery for HSCR were enrolled. Eligible participants were randomly assigned 1:1 via a centralized web-based randomization system (www.cbdps.com), which generated the allocation sequence. Specifically, 55 patients were allocated to the preoperative nutritional support (PNS) group and 55 to the standard medical care (SMC) group. Surgeons and patients were not blinded to group assignment. The primary outcome was the incidence of HAEC at 3 months after surgery. At 3 months postoperatively, the incidence of HAEC in the PNS group was significantly lower compared to the SMC group. Specifically, the HAEC incidence was 7.27% (4/55) for the PNS group versus 29.09% (16/55) for the SMC group. This resulted in an absolute risk reduction of 21.82% (95% confidence interval: −35.64% to −7.99%; p = 0.003). No adverse events were documented. These results demonstrate that preoperative nutritional support substantially reduces early HAEC incidence in pediatric HSCR patients following pull-through surgery. Trial Registration: ClinicalTrials.gov NCT04598841.

Introduction

Hirschsprung disease (HSCR) is a congenital disorder affecting approximately 1 in 5,000 live births. It is characterized by the absence of ganglion cells in the affected bowel segment, leading to functional obstruction1. The definitive treatment for HSCR is pull-through surgery, which involves the removal of the aganglionic bowel to restore normal intestinal function2.

The optimal timing for single-stage pull-through surgery in HSCR remains a topic of considerable variability and ongoing debate3–5. Most surgeons agree that patients with HSCR should be nutritionally optimized prior to surgery4. However, knowledge regarding the nutritional status of HSCR patients remains limited6. A previous multicenter cross-sectional study from our institution highlighted two critical findings: (1) preoperative nutritional risk screening demonstrated a high prevalence of undernutrition among HSCR patients at the time of admission for pull-through surgery, and (2) low weight-for-age z-scores were independently associated with an increased risk of postoperative HSCR-associated enterocolitis (HAEC)7.

According to the ESPEN (The European Society for Clinical Nutrition and Metabolism) guidelines, patients at high risk of undernutrition and scheduled for major abdominal surgery should receive 7 to 14 days of preoperative nutritional support, preferably enteral nutrition or oral nutritional supplements8. Similarly, the Chinese expert consensus on the diagnosis and treatment of HSCR underscores the importance of tailored nutritional interventions for undernourished HSCR patients prior to surgery9. However, standardized nutritional management protocols for this population remain lacking9. Key challenges include optimizing nutritional status within a constrained preoperative timeframe and identifying evidence-based strategies to improve surgical outcomes and postoperative recovery.

In this work, we have established a feasible protocol for preoperative nutritional screening and intervention targeting undernourished patients with HSCR. Through the clinical trial, we assessed the impact of preoperative nutritional support on clinical outcomes following pull-through surgery. Here, we show that preoperative nutritional support substantially decreases the incidence of postoperative HAEC and promotes recovery in pediatric HSCR patients undergoing pull-through surgery.

Results

Study population

Figure 1 illustrates the participant flow in this multicenter prospective study. Between January 2021 and October 2022, 154 consecutive pediatric patients with HSCR were recruited across seven participating centers. Of these, 136 patients (88.3%) screened positive for nutritional risk (STRONGkids score>3). After applying the exclusion criteria, 26 patients were excluded for the following reasons: (1) failure to meet inclusion criteria (n = 9), (2) not undergoing definitive pull-through surgery (n = 5), or (3) parental refusal to participate (n = 12). This yielded 110 eligible patients, who were randomized via computer-generated allocation to two groups: 55 received preoperative nutritional support (PNS group) and 55 received standard medical care (SMC group).Fig. 1Trial CONSORT flow diagram.

To address potential covariate confounding and enhance result robustness, covariate-adjusted analyses were conducted (STable 1, 3, 7, Supplemental information). After full adjustment, the risk ratio persisted at 0.19 (95% CI: 0.06 to 0.59; p = 0.004) with an odds ratio of 0.12 (95% CI: 0.03 to 0.51; p = 0.004) (Table 2). These findings robustly confirm the consistent superiority of PNS over SMC in reducing HAEC risk, suggesting PNS may provide clinically significant protection against postoperative HAEC.

Secondary outcomes

Anthropometric measurements

On the day of surgery, anthropometric assessments revealed significant differences between the PNS and SMC groups. Specifically, the PNS group exhibited a significantly higher WAZ compared to the SMC group, with a median WAZ of 0.78 (IQR, −0.31 to 2.05) versus 0.39 (IQR, −0.58 to 1.16) in the SMC group (p = 0.003) (Fig. 2E). Notably, the PNS group had a significantly higher BAZ than the SMC group, with a median BAZ of 0.26 (IQR, −1.06 to 1.10) versus −0.74 (IQR, −2.08 to 0.41) in the SMC group (p = 0.004) (Fig. 2F). In terms of HAZ, no significant difference was observed between the two groups, with the median HAZ was 0.40 (IQR, −0.73 to 1.72) in the PNS group and 0.54 (IQR, −1.54 to 1.74) in the SMC group (p = 0.659)(Fig. 2G). Collectively, these findings indicate a significantly improved preoperative nutritional status in the PNS group, particularly regarding weight and body fat composition. During postoperative recovery, WAZ, BAZ, and HAZ were comparable between groups, with no significant differences (all p > 0.05; Fig. 2).

Preoperatively, the PNS group had a significantly longer length of stay (LOS) than the SMC group, with a median of 10.00 days (IQR, 9.00 to 12.00) versus 7.00 days (IQR, 5.00 to 10.00) in the SMC group (p < 0.001) (Table 3). In contrast, the PNS group demonstrated a shorter postoperative LOS, with a median 10.00 days (IQR, 7.00 to 11.00) compared to 12.00 days (IQR, 9.00 to 16.00) in the SMC group (p < 0.001). Notably, the total LOS was comparable between the two groups, with a median of 20.00 days (IQR, 18.00 to 22.00) in both the PNS and SMC groups (p = 0.567) (Table 3). The extended preoperative LOS in the PNS group may be attributed to the additional time required for nutritional interventions and preoperative optimization of patients’ conditions, which likely contributed to their faster postoperative recovery and reduced complication rates.

Postoperative complications

At one month postoperatively, the PNS group experienced 5 total complications (9.09%), including 3 HAECs, 1 anastomotic stenosis, and 1 incisional infection. In contrast, the SMC group had 13 complications (23.64%): 10 HAECs, 1 anastomotic fistula, and 2 incisional infections. The overall complication rate was significantly lower in the PNS group, with an absolute risk difference of −14.60% (95% CI: −28.10% to −1.00%; p = 0.039) (Table 3).

At three months, the PNS group had 8 complications (14.55%): 4 HAECs, 2 anastomotic stenoses, and 2 fecal soiling. The SMC group had 20 complications (36.36%): 16 HAECs, 1 anastomotic stenosis, 2 fecal soilings, and 1 case of persistent constipation. The between-group difference in overall complication rates remained significant (−21.82%, [95% CI: −37.58% to −6.06%]; p = 0.009) (Table 4).

Readmissions within one month postoperatively occurred in 3 patients (5.45%) in the PNS group (all due to HAEC) and 5 patients (9.09%) in the SMC group (Table 3). At three months, 5 patients (9.09%) in the PNS group were readmitted (4 due to HAEC, 1 due to anastomotic stenosis), versus 8 patients (14.55%) in the SMC group (7 due to HAEC, 1 due to anastomotic stenosis) (Table 4).

Subgroup analysis and sensitivity analysis

We conducted sensitivity analyses on the outcome indicators, and the results suggested that the conclusions of this study are highly robust, specifically that PNS can effectively reduce the incidence of HAEC within three months after surgery. To address potential center effects, we added generalized linear mixed models (GLMM) treating center as a random effect. For the primary outcome (HAEC), the odds ratio remained significant (OR = 0.17, 95% CI: 0.05 to 0.62, p = 0.011) (Table 2). For detailed results, see Supplemental information (STable 1, 2).

Subgroup analyses were conducted by stratifying HSCR subtype (STable 4), STRONGkids score (STable 5), and study center (STable 6). We found that the PNS group showed a trend toward lower incidence rates of HAEC and total complications across all subgroups. Specifically, in children with short-segment HSCR, PNS reduced the incidence of HAEC by 15.13% at one month postoperatively (95% CI: −29.83% to −0.44%, p = 0.044) (STable 4-2-1) and by 22.45% at three months postoperatively (95% CI: −38.24% to −6.66%, p = 0.005) (STable 4-2-2). Although other subgroup analyses did not reveal significant statistical differences due to the limited sample size, the trend that the PNS group was superior to the SMC group in reducing the incidence of postoperative complications (especially HAEC) and shortening postoperative hospital stay was consistent across all subgroups.

Discussion

This multicenter randomized controlled trial provides the Level I evidence to evaluate the effect of preoperative nutritional support on outcomes following pull-through surgery for Hirschsprung disease. We established that preoperative nutritional support significantly reduces postoperative complications, particularly enterocolitis, while concurrently enhancing perioperative nutritional status and accelerating postoperative recovery.

HAEC occurs in up to 50% of patients within months after pull-through surgery, with a mortality rate of 1–10%10. Despite advances in diagnosis and treatment, HAEC continues to contribute significantly to HSCR-related mortality11. Undernutrition, prevalent in HSCR patients preoperatively, is a critical risk factor due to its association with low hemoglobin and albumin levels, which may contribute to the development of HAEC. A previous study retrospectively analyzed 624 patients who were admitted for HSCR surgery. The rates of patients with a WAZ of −1 or lower, indicating mild, moderate, or severe undernutrition risk, were 18.1%, 10.6%, and 5.8% respectively7. This observation revealed that undernutrition is a silent epidemic among pediatric patients with HSCR, and it is often underrecognized by many surgeons and caregivers.

The 2017 ESPEN Surgical Nutrition Guidelines and their 2021 practical update emphasize the importance of preoperative nutritional support in reducing surgical risks and improving recovery8. HSCR patients with undernutrition are recommended to receive personalized nutritional intervention before the pull-through procedure, allowing sufficient time for metabolic and physiological improvements9. However, there remains a paucity of evidence regarding preoperative nutritional support in these patients. In this study, we developed and implemented a practical PNS protocol based on previous research and our clinical experience. We used the STRONGkids assessment to screen those at high risk of undernutrition, and adopted a WAZ value of ≥ −1 as the target for preoperative nutritional intervention12.

On the day of surgery, after the personalized nutritional intervention was implemented, the PNS group demonstrated significantly higher average WAZ and BAZ values compared to the SMC group. This clearly indicated a more favorable nutritional state within the PNS group. At one and three months after surgery, the incidence of HAEC in the PNS group was significantly lower than that in the SMC group. Moreover, the PNS group had a significantly lower incidence of overall complications. Additionally, one month after the surgery, the PNS group had a shorter postoperative hospital stay. Overall, these findings suggest that PNS not only enhances the nutritional status of patients but also reduces postoperative complications and promotes recovery.

Notably, the PNS group exhibited higher serum albumin levels at one month postoperatively. Serum albumin, a key indicator of nutritional status, plays critical roles in transporting minerals, hormones, and fatty acids, as well as maintaining capillary oncotic pressure13. Low preoperative albumin levels are associated with increased perioperative risk13. Elevated albumin levels at one month suggest enhanced hepatic synthesis or nutritional status in the PNS group, though long-term (three-month) effects were absent. Moreover, prealbumin levels in the PNS group were numerically higher than those in the SMC group at one month, although the difference did not reach statistical significance, partly due to prealbumin’s short half-life and its high sensitivity to systemic inflammation and physiological stress14,15. The increased prealbumin levels in the PNS group might play a role in alleviating intestinal inflammation, thereby potentially lowering the risk of HAEC16. Conducting serial measurements of prealbumin at shorter intervals may better reflect the effects of PNS on the changes in prealbumin levels. Additionally, PNS may modulate gut microbial composition, influencing metabolic, hormonal, and neurological processes in the intestine, thereby mitigating postoperative enterocolitis severity17. However, this proposed mechanism requires further validation.

Our work has several limitations. Firstly, there is no commonly accepted preoperative nutritional support protocol for HSCR patients. In this study, preoperative nutritional support was provided to reduce the nutritional deficit between the expected and actual calorie count. This protocol effectively reduced the incidence of postoperative HAEC. We recognize that alternative PNS modalities, or their combinations with postoperative nutritional support, may lead to even more favorable outcomes. Therefore, future research should investigate whether more optimized nutritional protocols can further reduce the risk of HAEC.

Secondly, the primary endpoint of this study was set at three months after surgery, as up to 50% of patients who develop postoperative HAEC and other complications, such as anastomotic leakage, intestinal obstruction, within this period. This early occurrence of HAEC and other complications has been shown to be predictive of long-term prognosis of HSCR18,19. Nevertheless, as some patients may still develop HAEC even beyond this timeframe. To comprehensively evaluate the effects of PNS, long-term outcomes, including nutritional status, defecation function, and overall quality of life, require systematic monitoring and assessment.

Thirdly, although centralized randomization ensured overall group balance, the absence of center-level stratification may introduce inter-center bias in small-scale multicenter trials. However, statistical adjustments for center effects and heterogeneity assessments supported the robustness of the primary outcomes. Consistent trends toward PNS-associated reductions in HAEC incidence were observed across all centers, though statistical significance was limited by small per-center sample sizes. Future trials investigating rare outcomes may benefit from implementing center-stratified randomization.

Lastly, while the open-label design may introduce unintended biases20, the diagnosis of HAEC cases was independently verified by pediatric surgeons blinded to group assignments. These surgeons applied standardized diagnostic criteria, strengthening the validity of the findings. Furthermore, comprehensive sensitivity analyses (STables 1, 2, 7) confirmed result robustness against potential adverse effects. Additional bias impact analyses specifically assessed the consequences of unblinding. These methodological measures consistently demonstrated PNS’s superiority over SMC in reducing HAEC risk.

In conclusion, this study establishes that preoperative nutritional support significantly reduces postoperative complications, improves nutritional biomarkers, and accelerates recovery in pediatric Hirschsprung disease patients undergoing pull-through surgery. These findings provide Level I evidence supporting the integration of structured nutritional interventions into standard preoperative protocols for this population.

Methods

This trial was conducted in full accordance with the requirements of Good Clinical Practice, the Declaration of Helsinki, and the local laws and regulations in China. The full protocol was registered on ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT04598841). This study received ethical approval from the independent ethics committees of Tongji Hospital, Anhui Provincial Children’s Hospital, Shanghai Children’s Hospital, Xi’an Children’s Hospital, Tianjin Children’s Hospital, the First Affiliated Hospital of Zhengzhou University, and Children’s Hospital of Shanxi. Informed consent was obtained in writing from the parents or legal guardians of all participating children. In 2020, before the trial initiation, a protocol amendment was submitted. This amendment entailed altering the primary outcome to the incidence of HAEC three months after surgery. This change was made because it represents a more clinically relevant and impactful outcome for HSCR patients. Additionally, the assessment of direct and indirect healthcare expenditures was removed from the secondary outcomes. The Institutional Review Board (IRB) reviewed and approved these modifications. The study was reported in accordance with the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) guidelines. The CORSORT checklist, trial protocol, and statistical analysis plan are provided in the Supplementary Information.

Trial design and patients

This multicenter prospective open-label randomized controlled trial was conducted at seven tertiary hospitals in China between January 1, 2021, and October 31, 2022. Baseline characteristics and sample sizes are detailed in Table 1.

This study recruited patients aged 1 month to 5 years with confirmed HSCR admitted for surgery. Upon admission, all patients underwent nutritional risk screening using the STRONGkids tool, administered by dedicated nutrition management teams at each center12,21. These teams included registered dietitians, nutrition-specialized attending physicians, and nurses, who completed the STRONGkids questionnaire through face-to-face interviews. Based on the scores, patients were stratified as low risk (score 0), medium risk (score 1-3), or high risk (score 4-5). Only those classified as high risk were included in the study cohort.

Exclusion criteria included: Preoperative or intraoperative diagnosis of total colonic aganglionosis or total intestinal aganglionosis. Preoperative presence of severe, life-threatening conditions, such as HAEC. Concurrent severe gastrointestinal malformations requiring surgical intervention, including but not limited to Meckel’s diverticulum or intestinal duplication. Patients requiring reoperation, such as those with a history of pull-through or ostomy procedures.

The discharge criteria included no need for intravenous fluid, tolerance of oral diet, a well-healed surgical incision, and no fever (temperature <38 °C) for a minimum of 24 hours before discharge22.

Prior to pull-through surgery, all patients underwent barium enema, anorectal manometry, and rectal suction biopsy to confirm the diagnosis of HSCR, with definitive confirmation provided by postoperative histopathological examination23.

The length of the aganglionic segment was evaluated preoperatively by barium enema and subsequently confirmed by histopathological analysis of surgical specimens, via intraoperative frozen-section examinations, in accordance with the clinical guidelines established by the Chinese Research Group of Hirschsprung Disease9. Histopathological examination of surgical specimens confirmed HSCR in all patients postoperatively.

HSCR subtypes were classified anatomically based on the proximal extent of aganglionosis: Short-segment HSCR: Aganglionosis confined to the rectosigmoid colon proximal to the dentate line. Long-segment HSCR: Aganglionosis extending beyond the sigmoid colon but not involving the entire colon24,25.

The diagnosis of HAEC was established using a validated clinical scoring system26,27. This scoring tool incorporates 16 parameters across five domains: medical history, clinical signs and symptoms, physical examination findings, radiological imaging, and laboratory investigations. A HAEC score of 10 or greater indicated a diagnosis of HAEC26,27.

Anthropometric data, including weight-for-age z-scores (WAZ), height-for-age z-scores (HAZ), BMI, and BMI-for-age z-scores (BAZ), were calculated using WHO Anthro and AnthroPlus software28. Data collection was managed through a secure, web-based electronic data capture system. An independent Data and Safety Monitoring Board was established to oversee trial conduct, ensure data privacy and security, and perform final data analysis.

Randomization and masking

Participants were randomized via a centralized, web-based system (www.cbdps.com), generating allocation sequences in a 1:1 ratio. Assignments were concealed until participant identifiers were entered. Due to the inherent nature of the intervention, blinding of surgeons and patients was not feasible. However, to minimize bias, data collection personnel were independent of those involved in assessing eligibility, recruiting participants, and evaluating clinical outcomes. Additionally, the diagnosis of HAEC during follow-up was independently verified by a pediatric surgeon not otherwise involved in the study.

Preoperative nutritional management

Participants were randomly assigned to either the control group, receiving standard medical care (SMC), or the intervention group, receiving preoperative nutritional support (PNS). In the PNS group, a nutrition team collaborated with the nursing team to conduct daily assessments of caloric intake, feeding type, route, volume, and composition. Enteral nutrition tolerance was evaluated based on clinical signs and the presence of reducing substances in stool, which may indicate incomplete digestion or absorption. Adjustments to feeding volumes and intervals were made as needed in cases of intolerance.

For the PNS group, energy intake targets were calculated based on the Dietary Reference Intake (DRI) for age and sex. Participants with an initial weight-for-age z-score (WAZ) < -1 were targeted for a total energy intake of 130–150 kcal/kg/day, aiming to achieve a WAZ ≥ -1 before surgery22,29,30. For those with an initial WAZ ≥ -1, the target energy intake was set at 110–130 kcal/kg/day, with a minimum intervention period of 7 days22.

When breastfeeding alone did not meet the target caloric intake, a high-calorie dense formula was added to expressed breast milk. Formula-fed infants or those on complementary feeding received a high-calorie dense formula to address nutritional gaps. Infants under 12 months were provided with Alfare (Nestlé, Switzerland), offering 800 kcal and 21.5 g of protein per 1000 mL, while children aged ≥12 months received PEPTAMEN JUNIOR (Nestlé, Switzerland), providing 1000 kcal and 30.1 g of protein per 1000 mL. If oral intake was insufficient or poorly tolerated, enteral feeding via nasogastric tube was initiated to meet fluid and energy requirements31.

In contrast, the SMC group did not follow a mandatory preoperative feeding protocol. They received standardized education and adhered to a traditional preoperative enteral feeding regimen, with the recommended energy intake set at 80–110 kcal/kg/day (100% DRI). Actual intake and feeding schedules were determined by the parents.

Both groups underwent daily colonic irrigation to ensure complete removal of residual stool from the intestines. Postoperatively, both groups received total parenteral nutrition from the day before surgery until two days postoperatively, during which oral nutrition was withheld. After this period, a gradual transition from parenteral to total enteral nutrition was implemented without a mandatory feeding protocol, maintaining the recommended caloric intake at 90–100% of the DRIs32.

Outcomes

The primary outcome of this study was the incidence of HAEC at three months after surgery. This choice of the primary outcome was influenced by two key factors. Firstly, among patients developing postoperative HAEC, up to 50% experienced their first episode within the initial 3 months after surgery33,34. Secondly, the occurrence of HAEC during this period is predictive of the long-term prognosis in patients with HSCR18,19.

The secondary outcomes consisted of the overall complication rates, the readmission rates, the postoperative hospital length of stay (LOS), anthropometric measurements taken during follow-up visits, along with hematological and biochemical markers that reflect nutritional status (such as serum albumin, prealbumin, and hemoglobin). The selection of the secondary outcomes was based on their clinical relevance and prior literature that investigated the prevalence of undernutrition and the clinical outcomes among patients with HSCR7,22.

Statistical analysis

The sample size was determined based on the anticipated incidence of HAEC at three months postoperatively. In this study, the seven participating centers reached a consensus that the HAEC incidence in the control group was set at 30%. This is consistent with a study from a large tertiary children’s care center in China, where researchers reviewed 304 HSCR patients and found that the incidence of post-HAEC was 29.9% (91/304)18. In another multi-center study that we participated in, which explored enhanced recovery after surgery during the perioperative period of HSCR, among the infants who received preoperative nutritional support, the complications including post-HAEC - HAEC were 6.8%22. Based on these considerations, we assumed a 30% post-HAEC incidence in the control group and a reduction by 23% (absolute risk reduction) in the intervention group. With a two-sided α of 0.05 and 80% power, 86 patients were required. Accounting for an anticipated 10% dropout rate, the target enrollment was 95 patients. Due to actual enrollment, 110 patients were ultimately included, with 55 randomized to each group. Post-hoc power analysis confirmed 87% power to detect the observed HAEC reduction (28.6% vs. 5.4%, p = 0.002) using Z-Test with unpooled variance.

The primary analysis was based on the intention-to-treat population, which included all randomized patients diagnosed with HSCR. Study results and baseline characteristics were summarized using descriptive statistics. Binary outcomes were assessed using the χ² test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate; risk differences are reported with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) calculated via Newcombe’s score method or exact confidence intervals. Continuous measurements and outcomes were compared using t-tests for normally distributed data or Wilcoxon rank-sum tests for non-normally distributed data, with between-group differences reported as mean differences and 95% CIs.

To assess covariate effects on primary outcomes, adjusted analyses were performed using modified Poisson regression and multivariate logistic regression, with adjustment for center effects and surgical approach35. Subgroup analyses were conducted by HSCR subtype (long-segment vs. short-segment) and STRONGkids score (4 vs. 5)35,36. All post-hoc and subgroup analyses were exploratory and did not include α adjustment for multiple comparisons. All tests were two-sided, with statistical significance defined as P < 0.05. Analyses were conducted using SAS 9.4.
