Orbit-blocking words in free groups
Lucy Koch-Hyde, Siobhan O’Connor, Éamonn Olive, Vladimir Shpilrain

Abstract
. By strengthening known results about primitivity-blocking words in free groups, we prove that for any nontrivial element w w of a free group of finite rank, there are words that cannot be subwords of any cyclically reduced automorphic image of w w . This has implications for the average-case complexity of Whitehead’s problem.



1.
Introduction

Let
F_{r}
be a free group with a free generating set (a. k. a.
basis
)
x_{1},\ldots,x_{r}
.
Call an element
u\in F_{r}

primitive
if there is an automorphism of
F_{r}
that takes
x_{1}
to
u
.

For a given element
w\in F_{r}
, we call a word
v=v(x_{1},\ldots,x_{r})

w
-orbit-blocking (or just orbit-blocking if
w
is clear from the context) if for any automorphism
\varphi\in Aut(F_{r})
, the word
v
cannot be a subword of cyclically reduced
\varphi(w)
.

Primitivity-blocking
words are the same as
x_{1}
-orbit-blocking. The existence of primitivity-blocking words easily follows from Whitehead’s observation that the Whitehead graph of any cyclically reduced primitive element of length
>2
has either an isolated edge or a cut vertex. More about this in Section
2
.

In this paper, we first show that the idea of blocking primitivity can be generalized from cyclically reduced primitive elements to some primitive elements that are not necessarily cyclically reduced, see Lemma
1
in Section
3
. Also in Section
3
, we offer an algorithm for detecting primitivity-blocking words in
F_{2}
. We give various nontrivial examples of primitivity-blocking words in
F_{r}
with
r>2
, but algorithmically detecting primitivity-blocking words in
F_{r},r>2
remains an open problem.

Based on Lemma
1
, we show in Section
4
that for any nontrivial
w\in F_{r}
, there are
w
-orbit-blocking words, thus giving a complete solution of Problem (F40) from
[
3
]
. Previously
[
10
]
, we gave a solution in the special case
r=2
based on a very special and explicit description of bases in
F_{2}
.

In the concluding Section
5
, we apply our main result to show that the average-case time complexity of a variant of Whitehead’s problem is constant.

2.
Background

The
Whitehead graph

Wh(w)
of
w\in F_{r}
has
2r
vertices that correspond to
x_{1},\ldots,x_{r},x_{1}^{-1},\ldots,x_{r}^{-1}
. For each occurrence of a subword
x_{i}x_{j}
in the word
w\in F_{r}
, there
is an edge in
Wh(w)
that connects the vertex
x_{i}
to the vertex

x_{j}^{-1}
; if
w
has a subword
x_{i}x_{j}^{-1}
, then there is an
edge connecting
x_{i}
to
x_{j}
, etc. There is one more edge (the
external edge): this is the edge that connects the vertex corresponding to the last letter of
w
to the vertex corresponding to the inverse of the
first letter.

It was observed by Whitehead himself (see
[
19
]
or
[
15
]
) that the Whitehead graph
of any cyclically reduced primitive element
w
of length
>2
has either an isolated edge or a cut vertex, i.e., a vertex that, having been removed from the graph together with all incident edges, increases the number of connected components of the graph. A short and elementary proof of this result was recently given in
[
9
]
, and a more general case of primitive elements in subgroups of
F_{r}
was recently treated in
[
1
]
.

Thus, for example, if the Whitehead graph of
w
(without the external edge) is complete (i.e., any two vertices are connected by at least one edge), then
w
is primitivity-blocking because in this case, if
w
is a subword of
u
, then the Whitehead graph of
u
, too, is complete and therefore does not have a cut vertex or an isolated edge. Examples of primitivity-blocking words are:
x_{1}^{n}x_{2}^{n}\cdots x_{r}^{n}x_{1}
(for any
n\geq 2
),
[x_{1},x_{2}][x_{3},x_{4}]\cdots[x_{n-1},x_{n}]x_{1}^{-1}
(for an even
n
), etc. Here
[x,y]
denotes
x^{-1}y^{-1}xy
.

Primitivity blocking was used for the first time in
[
2
]
to show that for any
k
, there is
w\in F_{r}
that is not a product of
\leq k
primitive elements.

In
[
10
]
, we showed that for any nontrivial
w\in F_{2}
, there are
w
-orbit-blocking words. This was based on an explicit description of bases (equivalently, of automorphisms) of
F_{2}
from
[
5
]
.

3.
Blocking primitivity

Definition 1
.

Call a basis of
F_{r}
Conjugacy Class Length Reduced, or CCLR, if there is no element
g\in F_{r}
such that conjugating each element of the basis by
g
decreases the sum of the lengths of the basis elements.

Note that every basis of
F_{r}
has a CCLR basis in its conjugacy class.
Since there is a one-to-one correspondence between bases and automorphisms of
F_{r}
, we will also refer to CCLR automorphisms as those corresponding to CCLR bases.

Then we have the following technical result of independent interest.

Lemma 1
.

Let
B
be a CCLR basis of
F_{r}
. Then no primitivity-blocking word appears as a subword of any element in
B
.

Proof.

Fix
u
in
B
. We will show it does not contain a primitivity-blocking subword.
If
u
is cyclically reduced, then it cannot contain a primitivity-blocking subword as we mentioned in Section
2
.
Suppose now that
u
is not cyclically reduced.

Let
p
be the first letter appearing in
u
.
Since
u
is not cyclically reduced,
p^{-1}
is the last letter appearing in
u
. Now consider two cases:

1.
Every element of the basis
B
either starts with
p
or ends with
p^{-1}
(or both). In this case, conjugating every element of
B
by
p
(i.e., taking every
z\in B
to
p^{-1}zp
) will decrease the total length of elements of
B
contradicting the fact that
B
is CCLR.

2.
There is an element
v\in B
that neither starts with
p
nor ends with
p^{-1}
. Then consider the element
w=uv
.
Since
v
has neither left nor right cancellation with
u
, this
w
is cyclically reduced. Furthermore, since both
u
and
v
belong to the same basis of
F_{r}
,
w
must be primitive in
F_{r}
. Since
w
is primitive and cyclically reduced, it, and thus
u
as well, cannot contain a primitivity blocking subword. This contradiction completes the proof.
∎

Even though Lemma
1
suffices to prove our main result (Theorem
3
in Section
4
), we believe the following generalization might be useful, too.

Definition 2
.

We call a basis
B

Relatively Cyclically Reduced
, or
RCR
, if there is no letter
p
such that every element of
B
begins with
p
and ends with
p^{-1}
.

First we need the following

Lemma 2
.

Suppose
w,v\in F_{r}
such that
w
and
v
are not powers of the same element. Let
u(m)=w^{m}vw^{-m}
.
Then there exists
m\geq 0
such that the following are true:

1. The first letters of
u(m)
and
w
are the same.

2. The last letters of
u(m)
and
w^{-1}
are the same.

Proof.

Suppose by way of contradiction that for all
m
,
u(m+1)=wu(m)w^{-1}
completely cancels either
w
or
w^{-1}
.
It follows that
|u(m+1)|\leq 2|w|+|u(m)|-2|w|=|u(m)|
.
Since the lengths form a non-increasing positive sequence, there must be some
k
such that for all
i\geq k
,
|u(i)|=|u(i+1)|
.
That is, the sequence must eventually become constant and thus
\{u(i)\}_{i=0}^{\infty}
must then eventually form a cycle of equal length words.
Since conjugation is a one-to-one map, the entire sequence must be a cycle and so
u(0)=v
must be a member of the cycle.
Therefore each conjugation cycles
v
by the length of
m
.
Thus
w
and
v
are powers of the same element, a contradiction.
∎

Proposition 1
.

Let
B
be a RCR basis of
F_{r}
, where
r\geq 3
, and let
w\in B
.
Then
w
does not contain any primitivity-blocking subwords.

Proof.

If
w
is cyclically reduced then it does not contain a primitivity blocking subword.
Thus assume that
w
begins with
p
and ends with
p^{-1}
.

Since
B
is RCR, there exists
u\in B
such that it either does not begin with
p
or it does not end with
p^{-1}
.
We will assume, without loss of generality, that
u
does not begin with
p
.
Let
v\in B
be an element such that
v\neq w
and
v\neq u
.
Take the word
z=u^{m}vu^{-m}
with the
m
given by Lemma
2
.
The word
wz
is a primitive cyclically reduced word containing
w
.
∎

Note that in
F_{2}
, an RCR basis can contain a primitivity-blocking word.
For instance, the basis
\{aba^{-1},ba^{-1}\}
is RCR, but contains
aba^{-1}
, which is primitivity-blocking in
F_{2}
.

Proposition
1
can be generalized further, as follows.

Definition 3
.

We say that a basis

is

if there exists a proper subset

such that there are words

that do not begin with the same letter.

Proposition 2
.

Let
B
be a
R_{1}
basis of
F_{r}
, where

, and let

.
Then
u
does not contain a primitivity-blocking subword.

Proof.

If
B
is RCR, then we are done.
Thus we will proceed with the assumption that
B
is not RCR, and let
u\in B
start with a letter
p
and end with
p^{-1}
. We are going to construct a new basis
B^{\prime}\ni u
that is RCR.

Since

is not RCR, each basis element of

begins with
p
and ends with

.

We will assume, without loss of generality, that
(B-X)
contains just one element, call it
\beta
.
Let

be a word beginning with a letter
q\neq p
.
We will consider two cases:

Case 1.

\beta=u
.

Then for each
\gamma\in X
, let

be the value given by Lemma

such that
w^{m_{\gamma}}\gamma w^{m_{\gamma}}
begins with

and ends with

.
let

In this case we construct the basis

from

by constructing the basis

and then applying the automorphism that maps

to
w^{-m}\alpha w^{m}
and maps

to itself.
Then

is a RCR basis containing

.

Case 2.

\beta\neq u
.

In this case we construct the basis
B^{\prime}=X\cup\{w^{|\beta|}\beta\}
.
Then
B^{\prime}
is a RCR basis containing

.
∎

To show how subtle the situation with blocking primitivity can be, we include the following examples.

Proposition 3
.

(a)

In the group
F_{2}
, the words
x_{1}^{k}x_{2}^{k}
are primitivity-blocking for any
k\geq 2
.

(b)
In the group
F_{r}
,
r\geq 3
, the words
x_{1}^{k}\ldots x_{r}^{k}
are not primitivity-blocking for any
k\geq 1
.

Proof.

(a)
Theorem 1 of
[
5
]
says that in a cyclically reduced primitive word in
F_{2}
, at least one of the generators either only appears with the power
1
or the power
-1
. Thus,
x_{1}^{k}x_{2}^{k}
cannot be a subword of a cyclically reduced primitive element if
k\geq 2
.

(b)
Let
r\geq 3
. The word
u=x_{r}^{k}x_{2}
is obviously primitive in
F_{r}
for any
k\geq 1
. Applying to
u
the automorphisms
x_{r}\mapsto x_{i}^{k}x_{r}x_{i}^{-k}
,
~x_{j}\to x_{j}
if
j\neq r
, for
i=1,\dots,r-1
in order gives the element

x_{1}^{k}\dots x_{r}^{k}x_{r-1}^{-k}\dots x_{1}^{-k}x_{2},

which is a cyclically reduced primitive word containing
x_{1}^{k}\dots x_{r}^{k}
as a subword.
∎

We note that the Whitehead graph
Wh(u)
of the word
u=x_{1}^{k}\ldots x_{r}^{k}
,
k\geq 2
, is a circuit on all
2r
vertices since the external edge is included in the Whitehead graph. Therefore,
Wh(u)
does not have an isolated edge or a cut vertex. However, without the external edge, the graph
Wh(u)
is not a circuit and it does have cut vertices. Thus, there is no contradiction with the fact that
u
can be a subword of a primitive word in
F_{r}
,
r\geq 3
.

Part (b) of Proposition
3
can be generalized as follows:

Proposition 4
.

Let
X
and
Y
be disjoint proper subsets of the generators of
F_{r}
,
r\geq 3
. Denote by
\langle X\rangle
and
\langle Y\rangle
the subgroups of
F_{r}
generated by
X
and
Y
, respectively.
Let
w=w_{X}w_{Y}
, where
w_{X}\in\langle X\rangle
,
w_{Y}\in\langle Y\rangle
. Then
w
is not a primitivity-blocking word.

Proof.

We can assume, without loss of generality, that
X\cup Y
is the complete generating set. Since
r\geq 3
, we can also assume that, say,
|Y|\geq 2
.

Now select
y\in Y
such that there is no cancellation in
w_{Y}y
or in
yw_{Y}^{-1}
. Consider the word
v=w_{Y}^{-1}w_{X}w_{Y}y
. This word is cyclically reduced and contains
w
as a subword. To show that
v
is primitive we apply the automorphism that conjugates all generators in
X
by
w_{Y}
. Since
Y
and
X
are disjoint, this is indeed an automorphism.

Applying this automorphism to
v
gives
v^{\prime}=w_{X}y
. This element is obviously primitive, and therefore so is
v
. Thus,
w
is not a primitivity-blocking.

∎

We note that the shortest primitivity-blocking word in
F_{2}
is
x_{1}^{-1}x_{2}x_{1}
. Note that the Whitehead graph (with or without the external edge) of this word does have a cut vertex.

It is natural then to look for the shortest primitivity-blocking words in
F_{r}
for
r>2
. Below we are going to use a recent result of Ascari
[
1
]
to construct these words.

We need some preliminaries first.

Definition 4
.

For a free group
F_{X}
with a basis
X
we define the automorphisms
\varphi_{(A,a)}
that depend on a generator
a\in X
and a set of generators
A\subseteq X^{\pm 1}\setminus\{a\}^{\pm 1}
, as follows:

\varphi_{(A,a)}(x_{i})=\begin{cases}x_{i}&x_{i}\notin A,x_{i}^{-1}\notin A\\
x_{i}a&x_{i}\in A,x_{i}^{-1}\notin A\\
a^{-1}x_{i}&x_{i}\notin A,x_{i}^{-1}\in A\\
a^{-1}x_{i}a&x_{i}\in A,x_{i}^{-1}\in A\\
\end{cases}

The automorphisms given by
\varphi_{(A,a)}
are
Whitehead automorphisms
.

Definition 5
.

A Whitehead automorphism
\varphi(A,a)
is
fully reducing
on a word
w
if applying it to the cyclic word
w^{\circ}
cancels every added
a
and
\varphi(A,a)(w)\neq w
.

Then one has:

Theorem 1

(Ascari
[
1
]
)
.

Let
w\in F_{X}
be a cyclically reduced primitive word such that
|w|_{X}>1
.
Then there is a Whitehead automorphism
\varphi
that is fully reducing on
w
.

Using this result, we can prove

Lemma 3
.

Let
w
be a word in
F_{r}
,
r\geq 2
, such that
|w|_{X}>1
and for every word
v
containing
w
as a subword, either:

•

There is a fully reducing Whitehead automorphism
\varphi
such that
w
is a subword of
\varphi(v)
.

•

There are no fully reducing Whitehead automorphisms on
v
.

Then
w
is primitivity-blocking.

Proof.

Assume by way of contradiction that
w
is a subword of
u
, where
u
is a cyclically reduced primitive word.
By Theorem
1
, there is a Whitehead automorphism
\varphi
which is fully reducing on
u
, thus there is a Whitehead automorphism
\varphi_{0}
which is fully reducing on
u
, such that
w
is a subword of
\varphi_{0}(u)
. Then

u_{1}=\varphi_{0}(u)
is another cyclically reduced primitive word containing
w
, and thus we can iterate this process to get a sequence of cyclically reduced primitive words each containing
w
.
This sequence decreases in length and thus must end with a single generator
x
.
It follows that
w
is a subword of
x
, which contradicts our assumption that
\|w\|_{X}>1
.
∎

Now we can produce the shortest primitivity-blocking word in any
F_{r}
,
r>2
:

Corollary 1
.

The word
w=x_{1}x_{2}\dots x_{r-1}x_{r}^{2}x_{r-1}\dots x_{2}x_{1}^{-1}
is the shortest primitivity-blocking word in
F_{r}
,
r>2
.

Proof.

It is the shortest because any generator
x_{i}
should occur (as
x_{i}
or
x_{i}^{-1}
) in
w
at least twice since otherwise, either
w
itself is primitive or
wx_{k}
is primitive for some
x_{k}
.
However, if
w
itself is not cyclically reduced, then this argument may not be sufficient; we know this from the example of
w=x_{1}^{-1}x_{2}x_{1}
, which is primitivity-blocking in
F_{2}
. This, however, is specific to
F_{2}
. If
r>2
, then no element with just one occurrence of some
x_{i}
(or
x_{i}^{-1}
) can be
primitivity-blocking. Indeed, suppose, say,
w=x_{1}w^{\prime}x_{1}^{-1}
, where in
w^{\prime}
some
x_{i}
,
i\neq 1
, occurs just once. Let
j\neq 1,~j\neq i
.
Then
x_{j}wx_{j}
is a cyclically reduced primitive element, so

is not primitivity-blocking. ∎

In contrast, we have

Remark 1
.

The word
w=x_{1}x_{2}\dots x_{r-1}x_{r}^{2}x_{r-1}\dots x_{2}x_{1}
is not primitivity-blocking in any
F_{r}
,
r\geq 2
.
Indeed, for
r=2
,
w
is a subword of
x_{2}x_{1}x_{2}^{2}x_{1}
, which is cyclically reduced primitive.

For
r\geq 3
, let
u=x_{2}x_{3}\dots x_{r-1}x_{r}^{2}x_{r-1}\dots x_{3}x_{2}
.
We will now consider the word
v=ux_{1}ux_{1}x_{3}
.
This

clearly contains

as a subword and is cyclically reduced.
Apply the automorphism
\varphi:x_{1}\mapsto u^{-1}x_{1},x_{i}\to x_{i},~i\geq 2
.
Then
\varphi(v)=x_{1}^{2}x_{3}
, a primitive element, and therefore so is

.

3.1.
Detecting primitivity-blocking words

In light of the results above, it is natural to ask:

Problem 1
.

Is there an algorithm that, for a given word
u\in F_{r}
, would decide whether or not
u
is primitivity-blocking?

Below we give a solution for
r=2
. First we will need the following fact about the structure of primitivity-blocking words in
F_{2}
that builds on a result of
[
5
]
. Let
a
and
b
be free generators of
F_{2}
.

Lemma 4
.

Let
w
be a positive word in
F_{2}
whose first syllable is
b^{n}
. Let
q
be the largest power of
b
contained in
w
as a subword. If
n<q
, then
w
is primitivity-blocking if and only if
b^{-n}w
is. If
n=q
then
w
is primitivity-blocking if and only if
aw
is. Analogous statements hold if
b^{n}
is the last syllable.

Proof.

We will argue assuming that
b^{n}
is the first syllable.

Suppose that
b^{-n}w
is primitivity-blocking. Then since
b^{-n}w
is a subword of
w
,
w
is primitivity-blocking. Similarly, if
w
is primitivity-blocking, then so is
aw
.

Now suppose that
n<q
and
w^{\prime}=b^{-n}w
is not primitivity-blocking. Then there exists
v
such that
vw^{\prime}
is cyclically reduced and primitive with no cancellation between
v
and
w^{\prime}
. This
w^{\prime}
starts with
a
by our hypothesis. Theorem 1 of
[
5
]
then implies that the last syllable of
v
is
b
to a power at least as large as
q-1
. Since
n\leq q-1
,
w
is a subword of
vw^{\prime}
and is therefore not primitivity-blocking.

Finally, suppose that
n=q
and
w
is not primitivity-blocking. Then there exists
v
such that
vw
is cyclically reduced and primitive with no cancellation between
v
and
w
. If the last syllable of
w
is
b^{k}
for some
k<q
, then by the argument above we may consider
wb^{-k}
instead. Let us abuse notation to call this new word
w
. Suppose there is a power other than
q
on
b
somewhere in
w
. Then Theorem 1 of
[
5
]
implies that
v
ends with
a
, so
aw
is a subword of
vw
and is therefore not primitivity-blocking. Suppose every exponent on
b
in
w
is 1. Again Theorem 1 of
[
5
]
implies that
v
ends with
a
. Suppose every power on
b
in
w
is
q\neq 1
. Then Theorem 1 of
[
5
]
does not necessarily imply that
v
ends with
a
but it does imply that every exponent on
a
in
w
is 1. Then either
w
ends in
b^{q}
so
awb
is primitive, or
w
ends in
a
so
awb^{q+1}
is primitive.
∎

Theorem 2
.

There is an algorithm that decides, for a given word
u\in F_{2}
of length
n
, whether or not
u
is primitivity-blocking and has the worst-case time complexity
O(n^{2})
.

Proof.

Call our algorithm
\mathcal{A}
and let
F_{2}
have generators
a
and
b
. After each step we will replace
u
by the result of that step and still call it
u
.

Step 1.
\mathcal{A}
checks whether there is a generator that either only appears with the power
1
or the power
-1
. If not, then Theorem 1 in
[
5
]
implies that
u
is primitivity-blocking, so we stop. Otherwise
\mathcal{A}
swaps
a
with
b
and/or swaps
a
with
a^{-1}
if necessary so that
a
only appears with the exponent
1
in
u
.

Step 2.
\mathcal{A}
checks whether all the powers of
b
in
u
have the same sign. If not, then Theorem 1 of
[
5
]
implies that
u
is primitivity-blocking, so we stop. Otherwise
\mathcal{A}
swaps
b
with
b^{-1}
if necessary to make all the powers on
b

Step 3.
\mathcal{A}
checks whether there exists an integer
t
such that every exponent on
b
in
u
belongs to
\{t,t+1\}
except for any
b
s appearing before the first occurrence of
a
or after the last one. (If there are two such integers, take
t
to be the smaller one.) If not, then
u
is primitivity-blocking by Theorem 1 of
[
5
]
.

Step 4.
\mathcal{A}
checks whether there is a power of
b
appearing before the first
a
or after the last
a
, that is greater than
t+1
. If so, then
u
is primitivity-blocking by Theorem 1 of
[
5
]
.

Step 5. Suppose
b^{k}
is the power of
b
appearing before the first
a
. Then
\mathcal{A}
checks whether
k
is the largest exponent on
b
appearing in
u
. If it is, then
\mathcal{A}
replaces
u
by
au
. Otherwise it replaces
u
by
b^{-k}u
. This does not change the primitivity-blocking property by Lemma
4
. Again using Lemma
4
, if the exponent on
b
appearing after the last
a
is less than
t
(including if it is 0), then
\mathcal{A}
changes that power to
t
. Note that at the end of this step, our word is of the form
ab^{m_{1}}\dots ab^{m_{p}}
, where each
m_{i}
is in
\{t,t+1\}
.

Step 6.
\mathcal{A}
applies the automorphism
a\mapsto ab^{-t}
to the word
u
obtained as a result of Step 5. Let us call this automorphism
\varphi
. Suppose
u
is a subword of another word, call it
w
. We will show that
\varphi(w)
contains
\varphi(u)
and thus applying
\varphi
does not affect the property of
u
being primitivity-blocking or not. Recall that
u
ends with
b^{t}
(or
b^{t+1}
). Let us mark the syllable in
w
in which this
b^{t}
appears as
\hat{b}^{m}
. Note that
m
is at least
t
(or
t+1
, respectively). The following syllable in
w
(if there is one) is either
a
to a positive power or
a
to a negative power. In the first case,
\varphi
reduces
\hat{b}^{m}
to
\hat{b}^{m-t}
, so all of
\varphi(u)
appears in
\varphi(w)
. In the second case,
\varphi
leaves
\hat{b}^{m}
unchanged, so again all of
\varphi(u)
appears as a subword in
\varphi(w)
.

Step 7.
\mathcal{A}
swaps
a
with
b
and checks whether the word
u
has two syllables or less. If it does, then it is of the form
b^{m}
or
b^{m}a
for some non-negative
m
. These are both subwords of
b^{m}a
, which is a cyclically reduced primitive word, so neither are primitivity-blocking and thus
\mathcal{A}
stops and returns that
u
is not primitivity-blocking. If the word
u
has more than two syllables, then
\mathcal{A}
goes to Step 3.

Now we note that Step 5 of the algorithm
\mathcal{A}
adds at most 1 to the length of the word and Step 6 adds exactly
-pt
to the length of the word. Thus, as long as
p
and
t
are not both
1
, repeating steps 3 through 7 decreases the length of the word. If
p
and
t
are both
1
, the word must be exactly
ab
after Step 5, and then after Step 6 it will be
a
, so we will stop at Step 7. Thus,
\mathcal{A}
always terminates.

Each step takes at most linear time in
n
(the length of the input word), and the number of repetitions of steps 3 through 7 is also bounded by
n
, so the worst-case time complexity of
\mathcal{A}
is
O(n^{2})
.
∎

4.
Blocking automorphic orbits

Now we establish the main result of the paper.

Theorem 3
.

Let
w\in F_{r}
have length
k
.
Let
\{v_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k+1}
be a sequence of primitivity-blocking words such that there is no cancellation between adjacent terms of the sequence, nor between
v_{k+1}
and
v_{1}
. Then
\prod_{i=1}^{k+1}v_{i}
is
w
-orbit-blocking.

Proof.

Let
\varphi
be an automorphism of
F_{r}
.
Let
\bar{\varphi}
be a CCLR automorphism in the conjugacy class of
\varphi
.
Suppose
w=a_{1}\dots a_{k}
, where each
a_{i}
is a letter.
Then each
\bar{\varphi}(a_{j})
does not contain any
v_{i}
by Lemma
1
.
Thus if some
v_{i}
appears in
\bar{\varphi}(w)
, it must contain a boundary between adjacent
\bar{\varphi}(a_{j})
s.

Consider
\bar{\varphi}(w)
as a cyclic word. Since there are
k
boundaries between adjacent
\bar{\varphi}(a_{j})
s, there are at most
k
non-intersecting words
v_{i}
in
\bar{\varphi}(w)
.

Note that
\varphi(w)
and
\bar{\varphi}(w)
may differ, but they are in the same conjugacy class, so the cyclic reduction of
\varphi(w)
also contains at most
k
non-intersecting
v_{i}
s.
Therefore, no cyclically reduced element in the orbit of
w
can contain the product of all
k+1
words
v_{i}
.
∎

A sequence of primitivity-blocking words mentioned in the statement of Theorem
3
can be built as follows. Take a particular primitivity-blocking word, e.g.
u=x_{1}^{2}x_{2}^{2}\cdots x_{r}^{2}x_{1}
(see Section
2
). Let
v_{i}=u
for all
i=1,\ldots,k+1
. This sequence of
v_{i}
obviously satisfies all conditions of Theorem
3
.

5.
Average-case complexity of the Whitehead problem

Here we address computational complexity of the following version of the Whitehead problem on automorphic conjugacy in
F_{r}
: given a fixed
u\in F_{r}
, decide, on an input
v\in F_{r}
of length
n
, whether or not
v
is an automorphic image of
u
. We show that
the average-case complexity of this version of the Whitehead problem is constant if the input
v
is a cyclically reduced word.

This version is a special case of the general Whitehead algorithm that decides, given two elements
u,v\in F_{r}
, whether or not
u
can be taken to
v
by an automorphism of
F_{r}
. The worst-case complexity of the Whitehead algorithm is unknown in general (cf.
[
3
, Problem (F25)]
) but is at most quadratic in
\max(|u|,|v|)
if
r=2
, see
[
16
]
and
[
13
]
.

Thus, the version of the Whitehead problem that we consider here is different in that the input consists of just one element
v
, and complexity of an algorithm that solves the problem is a function of the length of
v
, while the length of
u
is considered constant.

To construct an algorithm with constant average-case complexity, we use the same approach that we used in
[
10
]
in the special case where the rank
r
of the ambient free group is 2. All we need for this approach to go through is the presence of
w
-orbit-blocking words for every nontrivial
w
, and this is provided by Theorem
3
. Therefore, we are not going to reproduce the whole argument from
[
10
]
, but rather will give just a sketch.

Our algorithm will be a combination of two different algorithms running in parallel: one is fast but may be inconclusive, whereas the other one is conclusive but relatively slow. This idea actually goes back to
[
11
]
.

A fast algorithm
\mathcal{T}
would detect a
u
-orbit-blocking subword
B(u)
of a (cyclically reduced) input word
v
, as follows. Let
n
be the length of
v
. The algorithm
\mathcal{T}
would read the initial segments of
v
of length
k
,
k=1,2,\ldots,
adding one letter at a time, and check if this initial segment has
B(u)
as a subword. This takes time bounded by
C\cdot k
for some constant
C
, see
[
14
]
.

The “usual” Whitehead algorithm, call it
\mathcal{W}
, would reduce the length of the input word
v
by applying elementary Whitehead automorphisms, until the length cannot be reduced any further. This part takes time quadratic in
|v|
.

Denote the obtained element of minimum length in the orbit of
v
by
\bar{v}
. If
|\bar{v}|\neq|u|
, then
\mathcal{W}
stops and reports that
v
is not in the automorphic orbit of
u
. If
|\bar{v}|=|u|
, then the algorithm
\mathcal{W}
would apply all possible sequences of elementary Whitehead automorphisms that do not change the length of
\bar{v}
to see if any of the resulting elements equals
u
. This part may take exponential time in
|\bar{v}|=|u|
, but since we consider
|u|

constant with respect to
|v|
, exponential time in
|u|
is still constant with respect to
|v|
. Thus, the whole time that the algorithm
\mathcal{W}
takes is quadratic in
|v|
.

To address complexity of the algorithm
\mathcal{T}
, we use
a result of
[
4
]
saying that the number of (freely reduced) words of length
L
with (any number of) “forbidden” (i.e.,
u
-orbit-blocking) subwords grows exponentially slower than the number of all freely reduced words of length
L
.

In our situation, we have at least one
B(u)
as a forbidden subword. Therefore, the probability that the initial segment of length
k
of the word
v
does not have
B(u)
as a subword is
O(s^{k})
for some
s,~0<s<1
. Thus, the average time complexity of the algorithm
\mathcal{T}
is bounded by

\sum_{k=1}^{n}C\cdot k\cdot s^{k},
which is bounded by a constant.

Based on these observations, we obtain the following

Theorem 4
.

Suppose possible inputs of the above algorithm
\mathcal{A}
are cyclically reduced words that are selected uniformly at random from the set of cyclically reduced words of length
n
. Then the average-case time complexity (a.k.a expected runtime) of the algorithm
\mathcal{A}
, working on a classical Turing machine, is
O(1)
, a constant that does not depend on
n
. If one uses the “Deque” (double-ended queue) model of computing
[
6
]
instead of a classical Turing machine, then the “cyclically reduced” condition on the input can be dropped.

We refer the reader to
[
10
]
for more rigorous details of the proof.

6.
Shorter orbit-blocking words

Even though the very presence of orbit-blocking words is sufficient to establish constant average-case complexity of the version of Whitehead’s problem in Section
5
, the size of the constant matters, too, which motivates us to look for shorter orbit-blocking words.

Orbit-blocking words for a word
w
offered in the statement of Theorem
3
have length at least
3|w|
. We are now going to look for shorter orbit-blocking words.

Proposition 1
.

There is an algorithm with time complexity
O(n)
that takes a word
w
as the input and outputs a word
v
that blocks the orbit of
w
, such that when
w
is sampled uniformly at random from all cyclically reduced words of length
n
, the expected length of
v
is less than
n
.

To prove this, we will need another result on primitivity-blocking words.

Definition 6
.

We call a word
w
slender if there exists a generator
x_{1}
such that
x_{1}^{\pm 1}
appears as a subword of
w
at most once.

Theorem 5
.

Let
\varphi
be a CCLR automorphism of
F_{r}
, where
r\geq 3
.
Let
w
be a slender word.
Then
\varphi(w)
does not contain a primitivity-blocking subword.

Proof.

Assume first that some
x=x_{i}
appears in
w
exactly once.
If
\varphi(w)
is cyclically reduced then
\varphi(w)
is a cyclically reduced primitive word, thus it cannot contain a primitivity-blocking subword.
Suppose now that
\varphi(w)
is not cyclically reduced.
Let
p
be the first letter of
\varphi(w)
, and thus
p^{-1}
is the last letter of
\varphi(w)
.

Let
B
be the basis associated with
\varphi
, i.e., the basis consisting of
\varphi(x_{j})
.
If some element
y
of the basis
B
besides
\varphi(x)
neither begins with
p
nor ends with
p^{-1}
, then
wy
is a cyclically reduced primitive word.
Otherwise, since
r\geq 3
and
\varphi
is CCLR, there exists
z\in\left(B\setminus\varphi(x)\right)^{\pm 1}
that does not begin with
p
.
Then let
s
be a basis element not equal to
z
or
\varphi(x)
, and let
u=wz^{m}sz^{-m}
, where
m
is the value given by Lemma
2
.
Then
u
is a cyclically reduced primitive word containing
w
, so
w
is not
primitivity-blocking.

Now suppose that for some
x=x_{i}
, there is no letter
x
or
x^{-1}
in
w
.
Then let
v=wx
. From the argument above,
v
is not primitivity-blocking, so there is a cyclically reduced primitive word containing
v
, and therefore also
w
. Therefore,
w
is not primitivity-blocking.
∎

Following the same logic as in the proof of Theorem
3
, if we have a partition of a cyclic word
w^{\circ}
into
k
slender words, then a sequence of
k+1
primitivity-blocking words without cancellation forms an orbit-blocking word for
w
.

We will now define the following simple linear time algorithm
\mathcal{A}
that gives the size of a possible partition into slender words. This

\mathcal{A}
finds the longest slender prefix of
w
, chops it off, then repeats with the new word until there is nothing left.
Then it outputs the number of repetitions.

Algorithm 1
.

Let
w=vw^{\prime}
where
v
is the largest slender prefix of
w
. Then let

\mathcal{A}(w)=\begin{cases}0,&w=1\\
1+\mathcal{A}(w^{\prime}),&w\neq 1.\end{cases}

If we take our primitivity-blocking word of length
2r
described in Corollary
1
,
v=x_{1}x_{2}x_{3}\dots x_{r}^{2}\dots x_{3}x_{2}x_{1}^{-1}
, then
x_{1}
and
x_{1}^{-1}
will cancel out in
v^{2}
.
Thus we apply an automorphism that swaps
x_{1}
with its inverse to get another primitivity-blocking word of length
2r
, call it
v^{\prime}
, that does not yield any cancellations when multiplied by
v
.
Our short orbit-blocking words are then the product of
\mathcal{A}(w)+1
primitivity-blocking words alternating between
v
and
v^{\prime}
to prevent cancellation.
Clearly, this has the total length
(\mathcal{A}(w)+1)\cdot 2r
.

In order to approximate the average length of the above orbit blocking words, we define a constant
c(r)
as follows:

Definition 7
.

Let
w
be a reduced word sampled uniformly from the set of reduced words of length
n
in the free group
F_{r}
.
Then

is defined as the expected length of the smallest prefix of

in which each generator appears (either negative or positive) at least twice, as
n\rightarrow\infty
.

Proof of Proposition
1
.

Our short orbit-blocking words are of length
(\mathcal{A}(w)+1)\cdot 2r
.
We can estimate the expected value of
\mathcal{A}(w)
in terms of
c
.
Let
w
be a random word uniformly selected from the words of length
n
.
Then

(1)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(\mathcal{A}(w)\right)\sim\left\lceil\dfrac{n}{c}\right\rceil\sim\dfrac{n}{c}

\displaystyle\mathrm{as\,\,}n\rightarrow\infty.

Thus the expected length of the orbit blocking words is asymptotically equivalent to
\dfrac{2r}{c(r)}n
.
Therefore, to prove Proposition
1
, it is sufficient to show that
\dfrac{2r}{c(r)}<1
, i.e.,
c(r)>2r
.

We can see calculating

as a variation on the coupon collector problem (

), and the double dixie cup problem (

In particular it is the

P(x,y)=\dfrac{1}{2r-1}\begin{cases}1&x=y\\

This accounts for the fact that

Since we do not distinguish between positive and negative forms of the generators for counting, this means any given distinct generator is twice as likely to follow as an equal generator.
This constitutes the difference between the above and the double dixie cup problem.
In the double dixie cup problem (and the coupon collector problem) successive draws are independent events, whereas here they are not.
