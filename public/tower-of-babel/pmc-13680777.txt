Shifting From Event Rates to Time at Home After Hospitalization for Heart Failure With Reduced Ejection Fraction: Trends and Equity in Days Alive and Out of Hospital in Singapore
Authors: Sibo Liu, Nicholas Graves, Audry Shan Yin Lee, Chun Fan Lee, Sameera Senanayake, Mark Yan Yee Chan, Khung Keong Yeo, Derek John Hausenloy, Sanjeewa Kularatna
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND:Conventional postdischarge monitoring for heart failure (HF) with reduced ejection fraction focuses on readmission and mortality, but these end points may not summarize cumulative patient-centered health. We examined whether secular trends in conventional end points translated into improved days alive and out of hospital (DAOH), and whether trends differed by age, sex, and ethnicity.METHODS:Using the Singapore Cardiovascular Longitudinal Outcomes Database, we conducted a population-based cohort study of patients discharged alive after first HF with reduced ejection fraction hospitalization during 2011 to 2019. We estimated risk-adjusted annual trends in 30-day and 1-year DAOH, all-cause and HF-specific readmission, and all-cause and cardiovascular mortality, and tested trend heterogeneity by age, sex, and ethnicity with multiplicity adjustment.RESULTS:Among 10 147 patients (median age, 68.9 years; 31.5% women; 65.4% Chinese; 20.0% Malay), risk-adjusted 30-day DAOH did not change materially (annual change, 0.01 [95% CI, −0.03 to 0.05] d/y; P-trend=0.554), whereas 30-day HF-specific readmission declined, with no change in 30-day all-cause readmission, all-cause mortality, or cardiovascular mortality. In contrast, 1-year DAOH improved from 291.7 to 301.0 days (1.16 [95% CI, 0.57–1.75] d/y; P-trend <0.001). In parallel, 1-year HF-specific readmission, all-cause mortality, and cardiovascular mortality declined, while 1-year all-cause readmission remained stable. Improvements were not uniform across population subgroups. Declines in HF-specific readmission were steeper in men than women at 30 days and 1 year, and 1-year DAOH gains were greater among Malay than Chinese patients after multiplicity adjustment.CONCLUSIONS:Improvement was seen in 1-year but not 30-day DAOH after first HF with reduced ejection fraction hospitalization, alongside reductions in 1-year HF-specific readmission, all-cause mortality, and cardiovascular mortality. Dissimilar slopes highlight potentially inequitable benefits. DAOH may provide a patient-centered complement to conventional end points for monitoring burden and equity.

Heart failure (HF) remains a major global health challenge that imposes substantial clinical and economic burdens on patients and health systems through frequent hospitalizations, impaired quality of life, and high premature mortality.1,2 The annual global cost of HF was estimated at US$284.17 billion and is projected to rise further.3 Accordingly, trends in HF outcomes are routinely monitored to assess progress, benchmark care, and inform policy. Conventional monitoring of postdischarge outcomes has centered on mortality and readmission rates, yet these end points often show discordant temporal patterns and can be difficult to translate into a patient-centered summary of cumulative burden. Evidence from a meta-regression suggests marked improvements in survival over the past 4 decades, whereas all-cause readmission rates remained high and largely unchanged.4 Interpreting trends is further complicated by substantial heterogeneity across eras, health systems, and subgroups. Recent evidence suggests survival gains may be slowing or reversing in some settings,5 and readmission trends vary widely across regions and data sources.6–9 Moreover, temporal patterns can differ by sociodemographic strata,7 thereby challenging equity-focused surveillance and policy design.

As health systems increasingly prioritize patient-centered outcomes, evaluative frameworks in cardiovascular medicine have broadened beyond isolated clinical events to capture what matters most to patients after discharge.10 This shift naturally raises a fundamental question for patients, clinicians, and health systems: “Do patients actually spend more time at home after hospitalization?” Days alive and out of hospital (DAOH), also referred to as home-time or hospital-free days, directly addresses this question by integrating survival and recurrent hospital use into a single, intuitive measure that reflects the cumulative burden.11 First introduced as a primary end point in the ESCAPE (Evaluation Study of Congestive Heart Failure and Pulmonary Artery Catheterization Effectiveness) trial, DAOH has since been adopted across clinical trials and observational studies for intervention efficacy evaluation and hospital performance benchmarking.12–14 However, despite growing utilization of DAOH, few studies have formally examined risk-adjusted trends in DAOH after HF hospitalization or whether changes differ across population subgroups. Population-based reports from New Zealand and Sweden have described changes in DAOH over time as an exploratory outcome but offered limited formal inference for trend and equity.15,16 Consequently, it remains unclear whether secular changes in readmission and mortality after HF hospitalization have translated into improved DAOH, and whether any gains are shared equitably by age, sex, and ethnicity.

In this nationwide cohort study, we quantified risk-adjusted temporal trends in postdischarge DAOH at 30 days and 1 year after an index hospitalization. We compared these trends with contemporaneous changes in conventional end points, including all-cause and HF-specific readmission and all-cause and cardiovascular mortality. Finally, we examined whether temporal trends differed by age, sex, and ethnicity to evaluate whether improvements were shared equitably within the population.

Methods

The data that support the findings of this study were obtained through the Singapore Cardiovascular Longitudinal Outcomes Database (SingCLOUD) data governance process and are not publicly available. Data may be obtained by qualified researchers with approval from the SingCLOUD Governance Committee and completion of applicable institutional requirements and data-use agreements. The statistical code is available from the corresponding author on reasonable request, subject to institutional approval where required.

Data Sources

The SingCLOUD is a national cardiovascular data registry that integrates clinical, administrative, laboratory, procedural, prescription, and financial records from public sector sources for research and innovation. Detailed information on governance, architecture, security, and data quality has been published previously.17 The SingCLOUD is listed on ClinicalTrials.gov (URL: https://www.clinicaltrials.gov; Unique identifier: NCT03760705). Data sets are linked using encrypted, deidentified tokens to create longitudinal records at the individual level. Diagnoses are coded using Australian adaptations of the International Classification of Diseases (ICD; ICD–Ninth Revision, Clinical Modification and ICD-Tenth Revision, Australian Modification). Ethical approval was obtained from the Duke-NUS Medical School institutional review board (NUS-IRB-2023-548).

Study Cohort

We identified patients with an initial hospitalization between January 1, 2011 and December 31, 2020 and discharged alive with a primary HF diagnosis code. HF was defined by ICD–Ninth Revision, Clinical Modification codes 428, 398.91, 402.01, 402.11, 402.91, 404.01, 404.03, 404.11, 404.13, 404.91, and 404.93; ICD-Tenth Revision, Australian Modification codes I50.1 to I50.9, I11.0, I13.0, and I13.2. The index admission was the first inpatient HF episode after a minimum 3-year HF-free look-back period since January 1, 2008. Because capture of left ventricular ejection fraction >40% became sparse and inconsistent after an administrative change in data collection from 2016 onward, we restricted the cohort to HF with reduced ejection fraction. This restriction was made to ensure a consistent cohort across calendar years rather than for clinical reasons. We excluded patients aged <18 years and those discharged on the same day as the index hospitalization (Figure S1). Comorbidity/medical history had minimal missingness (n=5; 0.05%) and was imputed as absence of condition. All other variables were complete. Baseline demographic and clinical variables were obtained from SingCLOUD according to available source-data fields, and ethnicity was analyzed using the registry categories of Chinese, Malay, Indian, and Others.

Outcomes of Interest

We evaluated postdischarge outcomes at 30 days and 1 year after an index HF hospitalization, including DAOH (primary outcome), all-cause readmission, HF-specific readmission, all-cause mortality, and cardiovascular mortality. Outcomes were ascertained via deterministic linkage of Ministry of Health inpatient records and the national death registry within SingCLOUD. Dates and causes of death were obtained from the death registry. Cardiovascular mortality was defined by underlying causes in ICD chapter Diseases of the Circulatory System (ICD, Ninth Revision, Clinical Modification 390–459; ICD, Tenth Revision, Australian Modification I00–I99). DAOH was calculated by subtracting total hospitalized days and days lost to death from prespecified time windows (Figure S2). Hospital stays overlapping the window boundary were truncated at the window’s end. All cohort members had complete follow-up for these outcomes.

Statistical Analyses

Baseline characteristics were summarized by calendar year of the initial HF hospitalization. Standardized mean differences quantified between-period differences, and values ≥0.1 were considered significant.18 Continuous variables were reported as mean±SD or median with interquartile range (IQR), and categorical variables as n (%). To estimate risk-adjusted rates and temporal trends, we fitted multivariable logistic regression models for readmission and mortality. Because DAOH is bounded by the length of the follow-up window and exhibits point masses at 0 and the maximum, we calculated percentage DAOH (%DAOH) and modeled it with zero-one-inflated beta regression. All models adjusted for a prespecified core set of covariates, including age, sex, ethnicity, implant device, chronic kidney disease, coronary artery disease, prior myocardial infarction, hyperlipidemia, prior stroke, diabetes, hypertension, peripheral artery disease, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, and the admitting hospital. Calendar year was entered as a linear continuous predictor. To assess whether trends differed by age, sex, and ethnicity, we included year×subgroup interaction terms. Yearly risk-adjusted rates were estimated through marginal standardization. To improve interpretability of temporal slopes, we reported average marginal effects for a 1-year increase estimated from contrasts of margins. Temporal slopes were expressed on the absolute scale as percentage-points per year (pp/y) for all outcomes and additionally as days for DAOH. The primary analysis window was 2011 to 2019, and calendar year 2020 was included only in sensitivity analyses to assess the robustness of findings to potential pandemic-era deviations. Overall temporal trend and subgroup heterogeneity in trend slopes were tested for all outcomes as prespecified research questions. For pairwise subgroup contrasts, we applied Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (BH-FDR) control to limit false-positive findings due to multiple comparisons. Two-sided P<0.05 was considered statistically significant. Detailed statistical methods are provided in Supplemental Methods. Study findings were reported in accordance with the Reporting of Studies Conducted Using Observational Routinely Collected Health Data (RECORD) recommendations.19 Analyses were conducted in R 4.2.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing) and Stata/MP 17 (StataCorp LLC).

Sensitivity Analyses

We conducted 2 sensitivity analyses. First, to assess the robustness of main findings to potential pandemic-era deviations, we extended the analysis window to include index hospitalizations in 2020 and fitted models with an additional indicator for calendar year 2020 to estimate an immediate level change. Second, to examine how estimated trends changed when between-hospital variation and changes in where patients received care for first HF hospitalization were allowed to contribute to the results, we refitted models without hospital fixed effects. Detailed model specifications are provided in the Supplemental Methods.

Results

Study Population

There were 10 147 initial hospitalizations in the primary analysis cohort. Median age was 68.9 years (IQR, 59.3–78.9), and 3193 (31.5%) were women. Ethnic distribution was Chinese 6633 (65.4%), Indian 1059 (10.4%), Malay 2034 (20.0%), and Others 421 (4.1%). Patients presented at an older age for their first admission over the study period. The most prevalent comorbidities were hypertension 7471 (73.6%), hyperlipidemia 6641 (65.5%), and diabetes 5472 (53.9%). Hypertension became more common over time (Table 1; Table S1). At 30 days, 2576 patients (25.4%) had at least 1 all-cause readmission and 921 (9.1%) were readmitted for HF. All-cause mortality occurred in 285 (2.8%) and cardiovascular mortality in 207 (2.0%) patients. Overall, 27 (0.3%) patients had 0% DAOH and 7494 (73.9%) had 100% DAOH. Mean 30-day DAOH was 27.8±5.3 days, and the median was 30 days (IQR, 29–30). By 1 year, 6854 patients (67.5%) had at least 1 all-cause readmission and 3090 (30.5%) had HF-specific readmission; there were 2002 (19.7%) all-cause and 1339 (13.2%) cardiovascular deaths. Nine patients (0.1%) had 0% DAOH and 2973 (29.3%) had 100% DAOH. Mean 1-year DAOH was 308.5±102.2 days, and the median was 358 days (IQR, 324–365; Table S2). Distributions of 30-day and 1-year DAOH were available in Figure S3.

Trends in 30-Day Risk-Adjusted Outcomes

Between 2011 and 2019, there was no change in 30-day %DAOH (annual change 0.04 pp/y [95% CI, −0.10 to 0.18]; P-trend=0.554). Contemporaneously, all-cause readmission remained stable (−0.26 [95% CI, −0.62 to 0.11] pp/y; P-trend=0.171) while HF-specific readmission fell from 10.6% to 7.8% (−0.36 [95% CI, −0.61 to −0.11] pp/y; P-trend=0.004), which implies a shifting pattern in readmission causes. There was no change in all-cause (0.00 [95% CI, −0.14 to 0.14] pp/y; P-trend=0.950) and cardiovascular mortality (−0.03 [95% CI, −0.15 to 0.09] pp/y; P-trend=0.644; Figure 1; Table 2). There was no evidence of slope heterogeneity in 30-day DAOH by age, sex, or ethnicity after multiplicity adjustment (Figure 2; Figure S4). Dissimilar trends in 30-day HF-specific readmission was observed in men versus women (P-interaction=0.038): rates decreased from 11.4% to 7.2% (−0.53 [95% CI, −0.82 to −0.23] pp/y) in men versus remained at 9.0% throughout the study period (0.00 [95% CI, −0.42 to 0.42] pp/y) in women, suggesting a potential narrowing and reversed sex gap (Figures S4B and S5C).

Trends in 1-Year Risk-Adjusted Outcomes

There was improvement in 1-year %DAOH from 79.9% to 82.5% (annual change 0.32 pp/y [95% CI, 0.16–0.48]; P-trend<0.001), which is equivalent to 291.7 to 301.0 days (1.16 [95% CI, 0.57–1.75] d/y; Table S3). Over the same period, all-cause readmission did not change (−0.32 [95% CI, −0.71 to 0.06] pp/y; P-trend=0.100), whereas HF-specific readmission decreased from 34.1% to 27.0% (−0.88 [95% CI, −1.27 to −0.49] pp/y; P-trend<0.001). Both all-cause and cardiovascular mortality exhibited improvements, with all-cause mortality decreased from 23.0% to 16.6% (−0.81 [95% CI, −1.13 to −0.48] pp/y; P-trend<0.001) and cardiovascular mortality declined from 15.8% to 10.8% (−0.63 [95% CI, −0.92 to −0.35] pp/y; P-trend<0.001; Figure 1; Table 3). A steeper decrease in 1-year HF-specific readmission was seen in men versus women (P-interaction=0.044): rates declined from 35.3% to 26.3% in men (−1.13 [95% CI, −1.59 to −0.67] pp/y) compared with 31.4% to 28.7% in women (−0.33 [95% CI, −0.99 to 0.33] pp/y; Figures S6B and S7C). Ethnicity-related slope heterogeneity was observed for %DAOH (P-interaction=0.027) and HF-specific readmission (P-interaction=0.020). After BH-FDR adjustment, a Malay-Chinese difference in %DAOH slope remained significant (BH-FDR adjusted P=0.039): the improvement in %DAOH was more substantial in Malay patients (0.65 [95% CI, 0.33–0.97] pp/y; or 2.37 [95% CI, 1.22–3.52] d/y) than in Chinese patients (0.20 [95% CI, 0.01–0.40] pp/y; or 0.74 [95% CI, 0.03–1.44] d/y; Figure 3B; Figure S6C; Table S3). As for HF-specific readmission, no level-specific contrasts remained significant after multiplicity control (Figures S6C and S7B).

Sensitivity Analyses

Baseline characteristics were generally similar between patients with index admissions in 2019 and 2020 (Table S4). In the sensitivity analysis including calendar year 2020, estimated annual changes were very similar to the primary analysis and the presence or absence of a temporal trend was unchanged across outcomes. The level-change term for the COVID period was not statistically significant for any 30-day or 1-year outcome. Patterns of sex-specific heterogeneity were broadly consistent with the primary analysis: disparate slopes by sex were again seen for 30-day (P-interaction=0.017) and 1-year HF-specific readmission (P-interaction=0.043), and a difference also appeared for 30-day all-cause readmission (P-interaction=0.003), which we view as exploratory. For ethnicity, slope heterogeneity persisted for 1-year %DAOH (P-interaction=0.026), whereas evidence for heterogeneity in 1-year HF-specific readmission weakened (P-interaction=0.070). The Malay-Chinese difference in the 1-year %DAOH trend remained statistically significant after BH-FDR correction (BH-FDR adjusted P=0.045; Tables S5 and S6). In the sensitivity analysis without hospital fixed effects, the overall pattern of which outcomes and subgroups showed significant trends, or heterogeneity, was similar to the primary analysis. In addition, modest decreasing trends in all-cause readmission at 30 days and 1 year were observed, suggesting that some apparent population-level improvement in all-cause readmission may reflect shifts in where patients receive care rather than within-hospital changes in practice. Because these models do not account for clustering within hospitals, this finding is considered descriptive and hypothesis-generating (Tables S7 and S8).

Discussion

Improvement was seen in 1-year but not 30-day DAOH after an initial HF hospitalization in Singapore between 2011 and 2019. The improvement in 1-year DAOH coincided with declines in HF-specific readmissions, all-cause mortality, and cardiovascular mortality, whereas at 30 days improvement in conventional end points was confined to HF-specific readmission and DAOH remained unchanged. We observed steeper declines in HF-specific readmission among men than women at both time horizons, and greater improvement in 1-year DAOH among Malay versus Chinese patients, suggesting that system-level gains may not accrue uniformly across population subgroups.

The improvement in 1-year DAOH likely reflects the cumulative effects of evolving HF management and care pathways, quality-improvement initiatives, and broader changes in cardiovascular prevention and risk profiles, which would also be expected to show improvements in conventional end points. By contrast, 30-day DAOH may be less sensitive to modest changes in HF-specific readmission, particularly when early postdischarge mortality is a dominant driver of DAOH in short horizons and is already relatively low compared with international benchmarks.20 A related consideration is that the population-level benefits of guideline-directed medical therapy often accrue over time, because the initiation and dose optimization typically occur over weeks after discharge.21 Several temporal patterns warrant further investigation to advance integrated and equitable care. In particular, the combination of stable all-cause readmission rates alongside declining HF-specific readmissions is consistent with studies reporting postdischarge rehospitalizations shifting towards non-HF causes,7 underscoring the need for holistic multimorbidity management in aging HF populations. Steeper declines in HF-specific readmission among men versus women may reflect persistent gaps in guideline-directed medical therapy use22 and referral to specialist HF care.23 Prior studies in Asian HF cohorts have reported higher symptom burden and greater frailty among Malay patients.24 This may suggest greater baseline burden, which in turn could leave more room for improvement in cumulative time at home when care improves. The greater improvement in 1-year DAOH among Malay versus Chinese patients may therefore reflect broader gains dispersed across multiple components of postdischarge care rather than a single dominant change in conventional end points. Singapore’s health-system changes during the 2010s, including expansion of subsidized community-based chronic disease care, introduction of universal MediShield Life coverage, and scale-up of primary care networks, may also have improved access and continuity of cardiovascular care.25 However, our current data do not allow us to determine whether such changes differentially benefited specific ethnic subgroups, and this can be an important question for future research.

Although the absolute increase in risk-adjusted 1-year DAOH may seem numerically modest, it should not be dismissed as trivial in context. A prior population-based report from New Zealand described a larger gain in 1-year DAOH from 244.9 to 274.2 days between 1988 and 2008.15 However, that study calculated DAOH from the date of index admission rather than discharge, thereby incorporating in-hospital mortality and index length of stay, both of which can materially influence admission-anchored DAOH. By contrast, the present analysis focused on postdischarge outcomes and therefore does not capture improvements during the index hospitalization that may also contribute substantially to overall time at home. In addition, the New Zealand study spanned ≈20 years from earlier eras, whereas this study covered a shorter, more contemporary 8-year period, during which there was likely less room for large secular gains. More broadly, population-level improvements in DAOH would be expected to be smaller than trial-based intervention effects because the benefits of evolving HF therapies and care pathways can be attenuated in routine practice by incomplete uptake, delayed titration, tolerability constraints, and competing multimorbidity.22,26 In the absence of an established minimal clinically important difference in this specific context, we therefore interpret the observed increase as moderate in absolute magnitude but potentially clinically meaningful at the health-system level, particularly in light of the concurrent declines in HF-specific readmission and mortality.

To our knowledge, this is the first study to quantify risk-adjusted secular trends in DAOH, to relate these patterns to contemporaneous changes in conventional end points, and to assess equity of trends. Two prior population-based HF studies reported descriptive improvements in DAOH over time and suggested potentially divergent patterns across subgroups.15,16 However, DAOH was considered an exploratory outcome, and limited formal inferences can be made for trend and equity. A study in neurology examined risk-adjusted trends in Healthy Days at Home, a similar metric to DAOH after emergency department visits.27 However, the adjusted means were estimated from linear regression and do not accommodate the bounded, skewed distribution of time-at-home measures with excess point masses at the boundaries. We used zero-one-inflated beta regression to better reflect these distributional features.

Beyond functioning as an easily communicable and patient-centered summary that can be readily derived from administrative databases,11 DAOH has important implications for health-system performance assessment, policy evaluation, and population-level outcome monitoring. First, DAOH is particularly useful for tracking temporal trends and policy effects when readmission and mortality move in different directions, thereby providing a single overall signal. This can be especially relevant to settings such as the US Hospital Readmissions Reduction Program, where conventional metrics can yield mixed or difficult-to-interpret patterns.28,29 Although Singapore did not seem to use an analogous hospital-level readmission penalty, mortality and readmission rates were monitored by the Ministry of Health, and DAOH may still offer complementary insights and support transparency in care quality. Second, DAOH has potential value for public reporting and value-based payment programs, as illustrated by recent US work showing that home-time meaningfully reclassifies hospital performance compared with existing Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services metrics.30 Moreover, DAOH may provide a useful complement for population-level monitoring by summarizing net postdischarge experience directly in intuitive units of days at home as compared with conventional outcomes. Composite end points such as hospitalization or death have long been used to summarize overall clinical burden, yet commonly used time-to-first-event analyses only address the first event, thereby ignoring recurrent hospitalizations and time spent in hospital, and their interpretation may be driven by the more frequent rather than the more clinically important component.31 Hierarchical approaches such as the win ratio were developed to address some of these limitations by giving priority to outcomes such as death over hospitalization.32 However, these methods remain treatment-comparison metrics rather than absolute measures of patient health. In addition, DAOH can be sensitive to subtle but clinically meaningful improvements dispersed across multiple components of care. For example, small shifts in event rates, event timing, and length of stay may each seem modest in isolation yet collectively translate into meaningful gains in days at home. Our finding of greater 1-year DAOH improvement among Malay than Chinese patients, despite broadly similar trends in conventional end points, is consistent with this possibility, although we were not able to formally decompose the individual contributions of event frequency, timing, and length of stay. Finally, emerging studies have applied quality of life–adjusted DAOH in HF trials,33 which may serve as a pragmatic precursor to full quality-adjusted life year–based cost-effectiveness analysis, offering decision-makers an early sense of whether interventions are bringing added health benefits at acceptable costs. Collectively, these features position DAOH as a pragmatic, patient-centered, and policy-relevant outcome for monitoring population-level HF burden, benchmarking hospital performance, assessing care equity, and informing effectiveness and cost-effectiveness.

Interpreting DAOH and related metrics requires careful attention to definitions and analysis. Although terms such as DAOH, home-time, and hospital-free days are sometimes used interchangeably, readers should confirm how each study defines the measure, including the selected time zero (eg, randomization, admission, or discharge) and whether days spent in extended care facilities (eg, skilled nursing facilities, rehabilitation units, or nursing homes) are counted as at home or as institutional care. Analytic methods should also reflect both the distribution of DAOH and the estimand of interest (eg, mean versus median versus quantiles), recognizing the trade-offs between simplicity and more complex mixture approaches.34 Finally, although there have been efforts to elicit the minimal clinically important difference for DAOH and similar outcomes,35,36 consensus has not been established, and the threshold likely depends on disease setting, baseline risk, treatment context, and follow-up horizon.

Limitations

Our study has several limitations. First, although we controlled multiplicity for pairwise subgroup contrasts using BH-FDR adjustment, our overall evaluation still involved multiple outcomes, time horizons, and interaction assessments. Accordingly, residual type I error inflation is possible, and subgroup findings should be interpreted as exploratory. Second, while we conducted a sensitivity analysis including an immediate level-change term for 2020 to contextualize potential pandemic-era deviations, some patients admitted in late 2019 accrued part of their follow-up during 2020. Because our goal was to investigate temporal trends and subgroup heterogeneity rather than to estimate causal effects of COVID or related policies, we retained all pre-COVID index admissions and avoided causal interpretation of the pandemic-era estimates. Third, although SingCLOUD was developed through extensive data harmonization across multiple sources and benchmarked against an established quality-improvement registry, some degree of misclassification remains possible in this study because cohort identification, readmission ascertainment, comorbidity measurement, and cause-specific outcome classification relied partly on routinely collected administrative coding. Fourth, we lacked individual- or neighborhood-level socioeconomic measures, other social determinants of health, and consistently captured granular clinical variables such as natriuretic peptide levels and NYHA class. Residual confounding and unmeasured heterogeneity may therefore remain. Finally, the cohort was restricted to patients with HF with reduced ejection fraction because of data availability, and the findings may not generalize to other HF phenotypes. Future studies comparing secular trends across HF phenotypes would help determine whether the temporal patterns observed here are specific to HF with reduced ejection fraction or reflect broader changes in HF care.

Conclusions

Improvement was seen in 1-year but not 30-day DAOH after first HF with reduced ejection fraction hospitalization, alongside reductions in readmission and mortality. Subgroup trend differences in DAOH and HF-specific readmissions highlight potentially inequitable benefits. DAOH may provide a patient-centered complement to conventional end points for monitoring postdischarge burden and equity.
