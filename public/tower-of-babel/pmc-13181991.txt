‘What would happen if I die in a foreign country?’: Cancer-related personal uncertainty and its implications for health equity among Indonesian migrant domestic workers
Authors: Madhu Neupane Bastola, Jeffry Oktavianus, Margo Turnbull
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundUncertainty, when perceived as a danger, can severely undermine a cancer patient’s recovery, resilience, and quality of life. Managing this uncertainty is therefore vital to well-being. While research on uncertainty and coping is growing, little attention has been given to vulnerable groups, such as migrant domestic workers (MDWs). Therefore, this study explores how Indonesian MDWs in Hong Kong experience and navigate the uncertainties arising following cancer diagnosis.MethodThis qualitative study is based on semi-structured interviews with 12 Indonesian MDWs diagnosed with cancer or being under observation due to suspicion of cancer (e.g., having cysts or lumps) in Hong Kong. The participants were selected by using a combination of purposive and snowball sampling. The interviews conducted in Bahasa Indonesia were audio-recorded, transcribed, translated into English, and analyzed employing reflexive thematic analysis.ResultsOur analysis, focusing on personal uncertainty, showed two primary forms: psychosocial and existential. Our participants managed these through four strategies, particularly reducing threatening uncertainty, maintaining uncertainty perceived as comfortable, increasing uncertainty to avoid distressing certainty, and adapting to chronic uncertainty.ConclusionTheir narratives of experiencing and managing uncertainty reveal resilience and a quiet strength that restored hope and agency amid adversity. These findings highlight how structural inequities shape illness experiences and point to the need for equity-oriented cancer care for migrant workers.Keywords: Uncertainty, Uncertainty management, Migrant domestic worker, Indonesia, Cancer, Hong kong

Introduction

Cancer remains one of the leading causes of morbidity and mortality worldwide. In 2022, there were an estimated 20 million new cancer cases and 9.7 million deaths globally [1]. Moreover, 53.5 million people were alive within five years after a cancer diagnosis, and the annual incidence is projected to reach 35 million by 2050 if current trends continue. These figures reflect not only population growth and ageing but also persistent inequities in prevention, early detection, and access to quality care across regions and income levels because people in low-income countries are at higher risk of dying from the disease due to late diagnosis and inadequate access to quality treatment [2].

Studies have documented the physical burden of cancer [3, 4], but its psychological toll is equally substantial. Meta-analytic evidence indicates that between 8% and 24% of adults with cancer meet criteria for depression depending on assessment methods and disease stage, and extensive reviews consistently show high rates of anxiety, adjustment disorders, and broader psychological distress across the cancer continuum [5, 6]. This psychological distress is driven by multiple factors, including symptom burden, treatment uncertainty, financial strain, social role disruption, and prognostic ambiguity, and is associated with poorer quality of life and clinical outcomes [5, 6].

One of the key drivers of this distress in the health and healthcare domain is uncertainty, defined as “a perception of doubt or not knowing that is brought about by cognitive and pre - cognitive ways of knowing” [7]. This type of uncertainty stems from scientific, practical, and personal sources [8]. It is also strongly linked with cancer due to its nature as a potentially life-threatening disease [9] that requires people to deal with overwhelming, ambiguous, complex, or insufficient information and make difficult decisions [10, 11].

Research suggests that uncertainty can be perceived as either an opportunity or a threat [12]. Some people describe uncertainty as an eye-opener [13] that prompts personal growth and new perspectives on values and the importance of life [14]. Alternatively, it can be perceived as a danger or a threat, and be linked with aversive experiences such as heightened anxiety, disappointment, and diminished willingness to engage in social interactions [15] increased fear of cancer recurrence [16, 17], fear of death [14, 18, 19], and maladaptive practices such as substance abuse, self-blame, and delay in treatment [20, 21]. Collectively, these factors can potentially contribute to a reduced quality of life for some cancer patients [22].

Prior research shows that migrant workers are disproportionately vulnerable and disadvantaged in terms of health. They are less likely to participate in screening programs than non-migrant populations, mainly due to limited financial resources, language barriers, and low health literacy [23]. These disparities often lead to delayed diagnoses [24] and poorer prognoses [25]. Within this broader population, female migrant domestic workers (MDWs) face even greater vulnerabilities. Their precarious employment, marked by restrictive contracts, low wages, and sometimes substandard working conditions, renders them susceptible to both physical and mental abuse [26, 27]. These vulnerabilities can also create significant barriers to accessing cancer screening and care [28]. Consequently, MDWs’ experiences of cancer are shaped by a complex interplay between structural constraints and personal perceptions. Taken together, these factors weaken their capacity to assert and negotiate their rights [29] and create distinct barriers to accessing timely screening and treatment services [30, 31].

These structural constraints profoundly shape MDWs’ perceptions of vulnerability and agency. These challenges are further compounded by varying laws governing healthcare access across locations, which may restrict their eligibility for services where they work or require them to pay for their own care if they return to their country of origin [28, 32]. When confronted with serious illness, they face an irresolvable dilemma: their ability to access treatment depends on their capacity to work, yet their capacity to work depends on their access to treatment. Unfortunately, financial barriers often prevent them from returning home for medical care [32, 33]. Moreover, MDWs remain susceptible to workplace exploitation, discrimination, financial insecurity, and employer control over their healthcare access [29, 34]. Therefore, while uncertainty is a common experience among cancer patients [22], the uncertainty confronting MDWs is amplified by their precarious legal and economic conditions, limited social support networks, and the constant threat of job loss.

When individuals, including MDWs, experience uncertainty, they take action. According to the theory of communication and uncertainty management (TCUM), individuals tend to employ various strategies to manage or reduce uncertainty, especially when uncertainty is perceived as a threat [12]. Strategies in which patients actively adjust to their disease in order to mitigate the adverse effects of disease-related uncertainty are referred to as adaptive coping strategies [35]. Some adaptive coping strategies that cancer patients have found effective include viewing uncertain situations positively [16, 36], balancing hope and despair, reevaluating what is meaningful, living in the moment, optimizing self-care [13], and accepting the situation [35]. Moreover, embracing a fighting spirit, believing in God or a higher power, avoiding or limiting information exposure [37], participating in clinical trials [15], and sharing experiences publicly [16] have been reported to have positive impacts on individuals’ health-related quality of living, illness experiences, and sense of belonging. However, it is worth noting that while some strategies can lead to positive outcomes, others may maintain or even increase uncertainty [38]. In contrast to adaptive coping strategies, actions taken to manage the stressors associated with uncertainties are called emotion-oriented coping strategies [39]. Prior studies have demonstrated that some emotion-oriented strategies that can compromise cancer patients’ resilience and quality of life include mental or behavioral disengagement or denial [39, 40], anxious preoccupation, pessimistic feelings [37], focus on what is lacking [16], and the avoidance of social interactions [12]. Interestingly, despite a growing body of literature on cancer-related distress and uncertainty, most studies focus on general populations [16, 37, 39]. Limited attention has been devoted to uncertainty experienced by MDWs whose employment-related constraints, language environments, and cross-border family obligations complicate diagnosis disclosure, treatment decisions, and continuity of care.

Guided by the frameworks of uncertainty taxonomy and TCUM, this research seeks to fill this gap by investigating uncertainties experienced by MDWs and their coping strategies through interviews with Indonesian workers in Hong Kong. Hong Kong is an appropriate research locale as it is one of the most popular destinations for MDWs from the Philippines and Indonesia [41]. Indonesian MDWs in Hong Kong are also arguably in a more disadvantaged position relative to their Filipino counterparts, as they tend to have less awareness of their employment rights, as well as a lower educational background and English proficiency, which may affect their ability to access healthcare [28]. The qualitative approach adopted in this study is particularly well-suited to capturing the nuanced ways in which MDWs navigate uncertainty, as it allows for an in-depth examination of how structural constraints influence their perceptions, management strategies, and decision-making processes. By using this qualitative lens, this study not only addresses a significant gap in the literature but also contributes to a broader discussion on migration and cancer [42–44], as well as the experience and management of uncertainty [45–48]. Moreover, insights from this research can inform policy changes and targeted interventions, including promoting stronger psychosocial support networks to help MDWs cope with cancer-related uncertainty.

Theoretical framework

To understand perceptions of uncertainty and coping strategies, this research is informed by the taxonomy of uncertainty [8] and TCUM [12, 38]. According to Han et al. [6], uncertainty can arise from various sources across scientific, practical, and personal domains relevant to patients or clinicians (see Fig. 1). Scientific uncertainty encompasses ambiguities in diagnosis, prognosis, causal explanations, and treatment recommendations for a disease. Moreover, practical uncertainty arises from the structures and processes of care, such as the competence of healthcare providers, the quality of care, and the procedures required to access treatment. In contrast, personal uncertainty is conceptualized as a sense of discomfort with oneself, associated with the implications of health conditions for patients, and has both cognitive and affective dimensions [8, 11, 49]. Personal uncertainty is further categorized into psychosocial (associated with employment, finances, housing, relationships, or social stigma) or existential (associated with the indeterminate nature of existence and the certainty of death) uncertainty [8, 9, 11, 50].

Varieties of uncertainties in healthcare (Han et al., 2011)

Recent attention within Western medicine has been directed towards the personal aspect of uncertainty due to a growing recognition of patients as holistic beings [51, 52], whose healing must address not only the body but also the mind, spirit, and community [53]. Failure to navigate personal uncertainty can result in demoralization, characterized by feelings of hopelessness, helplessness, loss of motivation, social isolation, and even existential distress (i.e., a sense of threat to life) [13]. Despite its profound implications for disease recovery and the growing emphasis on patients’ holistic needs, personal uncertainty has received less research attention than scientific and practical uncertainty. This may be because of the challenges associated with explicitly defining personal uncertainty and resolving it through the provision of information [50, 54]. Therefore, we decided to focus on personal uncertainty because it is particularly salient in the context of MDWs to capture their socio-existential precarity and understand how their health crises intersect with their vulnerability.

Unlike practical uncertainty, which is linked with more tangible dimensions such as structures of treatment and information sharing, personal uncertainty is connected with fundamental aspects of human experience, which may require an ongoing renegotiation of meaning [19] and a recalibration of one’s worldview in response to disruptive experiences [50]. The TCUM [12, 38] reflects this complexity and posits that responses to uncertainty are situated, temporal, and shaped by emotional reactions. Uncertainty can be appraised negatively as a danger or threat, positively as a challenge or an opportunity, neutrally as inconsequential or irrelevant, and in a combined manner as both a danger and an opportunity. Nonetheless, individuals will take actions to manage these uncertainties.

According to Brashers [38], individuals may employ various strategies to manage uncertainty, depending on their appraisals, emotional responses, circumstances, and available resources. Based on her own and other research, Brashers [38] discussed four primary strategies: (a) reducing uncertainty, (b) maintaining uncertainty, (c) increasing uncertainty, and (d) adapting to chronic uncertainty. When uncertainty is perceived as a threat or source of danger, individuals often attempt to reduce it by seeking additional information or clarification. Conversely, when uncertainty is viewed more positively, particularly when the alternative is distressing certainty, such as confirmation of a terminal illness, it can become a preferable psychological state [55]. In such cases, individuals may choose to maintain uncertainty as a coping mechanism, shielding themselves from emotional distress by avoiding medical screening, ignoring or discounting information, withdrawing from social interactions, suppressing thoughts, or intentionally forgetting painful realities [56]. When certainty itself becomes intolerable, people may instead increase uncertainty by seeking out contradictory or hopeful information, such as accounts of miraculous recoveries or alternative treatments in the face of a poor prognosis [18, 55]. Finally, adapting to chronic uncertainty involves accepting uncertainty as an enduring aspect of life [38]. Individuals employing this approach may tolerate ambiguity, redefine life goals, adjust their decisions, rely on faith or spirituality, or seek social and emotional support to coexist with ongoing uncertainty.

Despite growing recognition of the profound impact of personal uncertainty on cancer patients, research in this area has largely overlooked vulnerable and marginalized populations such as MDWs. The small body of work that has been published focuses on permanent residents or citizens in countries such as the United States [14], the United Kingdom [57], Denmark [52], Sweden [13], and Israel [18]. These studies have demonstrated that the process of experiencing uncertainty can ultimately help cancer patients to reevaluate the meaning of life, live in the moment [13], and even experience existential maturity (i.e., the understanding of the key aspects of a person’s being) [14]. However, other studies have reported that personal uncertainty can lead to a sense of social isolation, guilt, and disappointment [18]. Some cancer patients may need continued support even after the treatment is over to deal with their existential and emotional needs [57]. The findings from these studies cannot be extrapolated to MDWs due to the precarious conditions under which they work. To address this gap, we set out to explore the personal uncertainties experienced by Indonesian MDWs and the coping strategies they employ to navigate them. Drawing on Han et al.’s taxonomy of uncertainty and Brashers’ TCUM, these frameworks informed both the interview guide and the analytical lens of the study. In the interviews, they sensitized our exploration of how participants understood and narrated uncertainties arising from cancer, particularly psychosocial and existential uncertainties, as well as how they attempted to manage them in everyday life. In the analysis, the frameworks guided our attention to two interconnected dimensions: first, the forms of personal uncertainty represented in participants’ accounts, and second, the communication and coping strategies through which they reduced, maintained, increased, or adapted to uncertainty. At the same time, our analysis remained open to inductive insights emerging from participants’ narratives.

Methods

Study setting

Hong Kong has a well-established system of employing MDWs from other Asian nations, with the Philippines and Indonesia being the primary countries of origin [34]. As of 2024, there were approximately 368,000 MDWs employed in Hong Kong, 55% originating from the Philippines and 42% from Indonesia (Hong Kong Government, 2025). Indonesian MDWs typically have only junior or senior-level school education, limited English proficiency and knowledge about their labor rights, weak social ties, and a history of serial migration [34, 58]. Additionally, their practice of Islam, associated with dietary and clothing restrictions, may pose further challenges [58]. Therefore, they are arguably at a more disadvantaged position than their Filipino counterparts, who are more proficient in English, better educated, aware of their labor rights, and enjoy stronger social ties [58]. Recent studies have shown that Indonesian MDWs often experience trauma [59] and their language barriers profoundly affect their social integration, psychological adaptation, and access to healthcare [60]. These distinct challenges can influence how they experience and navigate personal uncertainties associated with the diagnosis and treatment of cancer.

Ethical statement

Ethical approval for the study was obtained from the Institutional Review Board of the Hong Kong Polytechnic University (HSEARS20250627003). All participants provided written informed consent prior to enrollment in the study. All interviews were audio-recorded with participant permission for later transcription and analysis. Interview transcripts and their corresponding audio files were assigned pseudonyms to anonymize participants.

Sampling and procedure

Participants were selected through purposive and snowball sampling methods. Purposive sampling was employed to identify Indonesian women legally working as MDWs in Hong Kong and diagnosed with cancer or being under observation due to suspicion of cancer (e.g., having cysts or lumps). Recruitment was facilitated through an informal support group for Indonesian domestic workers with cancer. The authors shared information with group members about the study, including its eligibility requirements, details of participation, and the ethical protections in place. These group members then helped identify eligible participants within their networks using snowball sampling and assisted in arranging convenient times and locations for the interviews. Twelve MDWs from Indonesia who were either undergoing or had completed treatment volunteered to participate (see Table 1). Participants had completed junior or senior school level education, were aged between 40 and 52 (M age = 49.74), and a majority had been diagnosed with breast cancer. No identifying details were collected, and participants were assigned pseudonyms to maintain confidentiality.

Research design and data collection

In this research, we employed a phenomenological approach informed by an interpretivist epistemology to explore the lived experiences of personal uncertainty among Indonesian MDWs diagnosed with or having a suspicion of cancer [61]. In line with the approach, we conducted semi-structured interviews using guides informed by prior research [33, 62]. Prompts were designed to foster empathy, establish rapport, and ensure participant comfort and credibility. The interview guide covered several key topic areas, including how participants first noticed health problems, their experiences of seeking and receiving a diagnosis, how they communicated the diagnosis to employers and family members, the treatments they underwent, and the difficulties they encountered throughout the diagnosis and treatment process. Interviews often evolved into conversations, with follow-up questions tailored to the specific experiences shared by the MDWs. The interviews were conducted by a trained postgraduate research assistant in Bahasa Indonesia, the participants’ native language.

The interviews were conducted between June and July 2024 at locations chosen by the participants. The interview durations averaged 42 min (range: 27 to 59 min). Participants received a HK$150 shopping voucher as a token of appreciation after the interview.

Data analysis

The interviews were analyzed using reflexive thematic analysis, aided by NVivo 13. We followed this approach because it is a theoretically flexible method of analysis compatible with experiential approaches to qualitative research, which allowed us to combine deductive and inductive coding [63]. Theoretical concepts regarding personal uncertainty [8] and uncertainty management [12, 38] informed the coding process. The analysis followed Braun and Clarke’s [63] six-phase approach. The second and third authors were familiar with the data. The second author, a native Bahasa Indonesia speaker, also reviewed the transcripts to ensure accuracy of transcriptions and translations produced by a research assistant. The first author familiarized herself with the data by repeatedly reading the entire dataset, led the analysis, and discussed each stage in the process with the second and third authors. This process ensured the rigor and quality of the analytical process through collaborative sense-making and refinement in thinking [64]. The codes, subthemes, and themes evolved and changed throughout the coding process as a result of our interpretative engagement with and evolving understanding of the data [65]. Analytic sufficiency was reached when no new themes and subthemes were generated in the analysis. This saturation was reached after interviewing 12 MDWs. This process yielded various themes and subthemes associated with personal uncertainty and management strategies.

Researcher positionality

We all come to inquiry with views, attitudes, and ways of thinking. Our histories, personal perspectives, and subjectivities may coincide with our actual inquiries [66]. Therefore, as qualitative inquirers, we are attentive to intersubjectivity, diverse worldviews, and positions [66]. Our research team consisted of a Nepali female, an Indonesian male, and a white Australian female, all of whom held PhDs from international universities. The first author is a patient-turned-scholar with experience in mixed-methods research. Therefore, she provided valuable insider perspectives on the experiences of cancer patients, as their experiences also reflect her own. The second and third authors have been researching migrant health and health communication in Hong Kong since 2020. During the research process, we engaged in continuous reflexive discussions to explore how our backgrounds influenced how we collected, interpreted, and presented the data. We aimed to amplify participants’ voices while being mindful of our roles and perspectives as researchers.

Trustworthiness and rigor

This manuscript was prepared in accordance with the COREQ reporting guideline for qualitative studies based on interviews. The authors’ diverse preunderstandings, including the first author’s firsthand experience with cancer, contributed to the critical reflections and analysis, thereby enhancing trustworthiness. In addition, trustworthiness has been augmented by representative quotes from participants, giving voice to their experiences. Moreover, to ensure rigor, we have clearly described participants’ characteristics, research setting, and the process of data gathering and analysis. Furthermore, we used theoretical categories and discussed codes and themes for collaborative sense-making.

Results

MDWs’ personal uncertainties raised by cancer

Our analysis employed both inductive and deductive coding, guided by Han et al.’s [8] taxonomy of uncertainty. This approach developed two broader themes of personal uncertainty experienced by Indonesian MDWs: psychosocial uncertainty (fearing job termination and financial instability, and undergoing treatment far from family) and existential uncertainty (dreading unpredictable health consequences, and confronting death and dying).

Cancer diagnosis and treatment as psychosocial uncertainty

We developed two subthemes (i.e., fearing job termination and financial instability, and undergoing treatment far from family) related to psychosocial uncertainties.

Fearing job termination and financial instability

Generally, MDWs are the primary income earners for their families. Fear of job termination and financial uncertainty were prominent in the discussion and influenced the way our participants delayed disclosing their health issues to their employers. For example, Wendy did not want to inform her new employer about her condition because she was “afraid [she] would be terminated”. Lily was worried that “they would send [her] back if they found out”. However, they felt compelled to inform their employers when they could no longer hide their medical problem because of their condition. For example, Tiffany’s reluctance to tell her employer and the resulting delay in seeking professional care worsened her condition:

By 2019, the lump had grown […], but I was scared to tell because I did not want to lose my job […] in 2021, […] I told my friend, went to the hospital […] and told my employer […] In July 2022, when I finally saw the doctor, they were upset that I had not come sooner.

Talking about her financial responsibilities, Linda stated, “Our primary goal here is to earn money […] we have so many people depending on us […] That is why many are scared to speak up”. Wendy posed a rhetorical question, “What if I got sick, needed money for my child’s education, and then I lost my job?” These quotes highlight how the likely diagnosis of cancer can increase MDWs’ financial uncertainty and delay in seeking health care. Despite their worries, our participants were relieved to report receiving positive reactions and reassurances from their employers. For example, when Iris shared the cancer diagnosis identified through free screening, her employer reassured her by saying, “Don’t be afraid. Just take this letter to the hospital tomorrow”.

Undergoing treatment far from family

Our participants, due to their fears about access to treatment if they returned home, often reported having to choose between being with their families during challenging times and accessing quality treatment in Hong Kong. Most participants reported that due to their inability to afford expensive treatment in Indonesia, they opted to receive treatment in Hong Kong despite uncertainty about how the treatment would proceed without the support of their loved ones. Rose recounted:

They all cried and urged me to return to Indonesia for treatment, worrying about who would take care of me in Hong Kong […] I told them it was not practical […] as the cost would be too high, over 400 million rupiahs.

Despite her family’s request to return home, Nora decided to stay in Hong Kong for treatment “after considering the differences in treatment quality”. Although Alice had a desire to undergo the surgery in Indonesia, she “had the surgery [in Hong Kong] after considering the cost and quality of treatment back home”. This decision-making was influenced by their ability to access heavily subsidized oncology services in Hong Kong, as well as some coverage through the mandatory employer-provided insurance. For example, Iris stated that she and her family were not worried “about hospital matters because everything would be covered by insurance”. These accounts illustrate how unique structural constraints regarding the healthcare system in Indonesia compelled MDWs to navigate life-threatening illness without physical support and care from their loved ones.

Cancer diagnosis and treatment as an existential uncertainty

Our participants’ existential uncertainty started not only with the diagnosis but with the suspicion of cancer, as they dreaded unpredictable health consequences and had to confront the fear of death and dying.

Dreading unpredictable health consequences

Understandably, the diagnosis of cancer was a terrifying and shocking experience for our participants. They shared their experiences of feeling worried, having “sleepless nights” (Rose), being “terrified, shocked and confused” (Rachel), and “sweating cold” (Wendy) on receiving the diagnosis. The need to process the devastating news mostly alone, compounded their fear. Iris stated:

Being far from family, you start to worry about what will happen during the surgery. That’s why I was so stressed before the first surgery; my blood pressure kept fluctuating.

Uncertainty about health made Rachel ponder “[her] future, [her] family, and [her] need to work because [she] was devastated by the cancer diagnosis.” Linda and Alice were worried because they encountered online information stating “breast cancer patients only survive for five years”. Notably, their worry extended beyond their own future to encompass deep concern for their families.

Confronting death and dying

The diagnosis of cancer brought our participants closer to the reality of death and dying. They were “scared of dying” (Rachel). From religious perspectives, Jennifer felt unprepared for the end and was worried about dying far from home, leaving her family behind. Therefore, she “always prayed that if anything were to happen to [her], let it be after [she] returned to Indonesia”. Tiffany informed her friends about her diagnosis because she was worried that “something might happen to [her] and there would be no one to take care of things”. These quotes illustrate that a profound sense of uncertainty regarding death and dying in a foreign land, leaving their families unsupported, engulfed our participants.

Managing uncertainty

Our participants employed various strategies to cope with the uncertainty, reflecting the four dimensions suggested by Brashers (2013): reducing uncertainty, maintaining uncertainty (avoiding information, selective ignoring, and social withdrawal), increasing uncertainty, and adapting to chronic uncertainty (coming to terms with the diagnosis; focusing on the positive; turning to spirituality; focusing on healthy diet, rest and exercise; managing stress; and seeking instrumental and emotional support).

Reducing uncertainty

Our participants reported heightened anxiety upon suspecting or being diagnosed with cancer. For example, Wendy recounted:

I was sad and scared because of the possibility of cancer […] Eventually, I was referred for a biopsy […] The next day, I was called back. I hoped the nurse would not shock me with bad news. I did not have anyone with me […] I was taken in and shown the computer […] At that moment, I was sweating cold, but the nurse said not to be afraid because it was an early stage.

To reduce uncertainty, they sought information by consulting online resources, communicating with healthcare professionals, and reaching out to friends and family members. Five participants described turning to the Internet. Alice browsed the Internet to confirm whether her symptoms indicated anything serious before visiting a doctor. She stated, “I searched online and found some similarities to my condition, which prompted me to get it checked out”. Other participants browsed Google to “find information about the causes” (Sally), learn about “how to stay healthy” (Jennifer), and “what foods to avoid” (Wendy), and be well-informed about “the side effects” (Rose). They also utilized online resources to verify or supplement information provided by healthcare professionals. For example, Iris said, “The doctor had already told me not to worry, that everything would be fine as long as I followed the process. I wanted to confirm that it was not dangerous”.

Similarly, Alice, when faced with difficulties in understanding medical information, sought clarification from a doctor friend in Indonesia. Rachel also sought clarification from medical professionals by asking, “Why do I have to have surgery? Why do I need two surgeries instead of just one?” Although the explanation provided was satisfactory, she continued to seek reassurance from peers, ultimately finding comfort in experiences shared by those who had already undergone a similar procedure. Tiffany and Rose relied on information provided by healthcare providers. Tiffany asked the doctor whether she could “be cured”. Rose always consulted the doctor about what she should eat and what she should avoid because “every case is different”. Upon discovering a lump through self-examination, Linda consulted the Indonesian Consulate Office to inquire about the free screening service. These accounts demonstrate that our participants sought out and utilized various sources of information to reduce their uncertainty.

Maintaining uncertainty

Some of our participants described engaging in activities that were consistent with the notion of maintaining uncertainty, as discussed in this section.

Avoiding information

Some of our participants avoided information to shield themselves from potentially overwhelming or distressing information. For example, Tiffany stopped searching online because “sometimes, reading things online can make you worry too much”, and Lisa thought such a search “would just add to [her] burden”. By deliberately limiting their exposure to potentially discouraging information, these participants maintained a higher level of uncertainty about their treatment and prognosis. As a form of indirect information avoidance, they also discounted negative facts, as stated by Tiffany:

We had seen information online about how the survival rate for cancer was around five years, which was really discouraging, but I decided to trust in God and keep going.

By discounting distressing information, Tiffany preserved her hope and emotional stability. It should be noted that Tiffany navigated her uncertainty through two methods: reducing some uncertainty with expert advice from her oncologist and maintaining some uncertainty by avoiding online information that could cause unnecessary distress.

Selective ignoring

They also engaged in selective ignoring of health issues as a way to maintain their uncertainty when they noticed something was wrong with their body. The ignoring was often rationalized by attributing symptoms to less severe causes, such as “an internal uterine device” (Sally) or “phone radiation” (Rose). Patricia explained, “I did not pay attention to being sick because I did not have time to look at my body,” while Tiffany shared, “My friend had been urging me to see a doctor since 2018, but I was too scared”. By dismissing and ignoring their symptoms, they were able to reduce their anxiety temporarily. However, their uncertainty remained unresolved, which could potentially increase the risk to their health.

Social withdrawal

Our participants also engaged in social withdrawal to avoid the distress of talking about cancer. Linda maintained her emotional stability and a sense of identity by confiding in only trusted individuals:

People’s perceptions of breast cancer vary, and I did not want anyone to think negatively about me. That would affect my happiness […] I shared my condition with those I knew would be supportive, not because I wanted sympathy, but to protect my mental well-being […] I did not want my mental state to suffer because of someone else’s negative comments.

Wendy chose not to share her experience, even with her family:

I did not want my family, friends, or anyone else to know. Even my friends who sit here every Sunday did not know because I was really down at that time, and I did not want my secret to be known.

While nondisclosure can sometimes be intended to protect loved ones, Wendy’s account suggests a strategy of maintaining uncertainty regarding her status to protect herself by keeping her illness a ‘secret’ during a time when she felt ‘really down’. By limiting or restricting communication about their illness, both women sought to shield themselves from potentially adverse reactions. However, such withdrawal may have intensified their struggle with uncertainty.

Increasing uncertainty

In some situations, our participants were not comfortable with their state of certainty and preferred to be more uncertain. For example, Linda had fear because she had heard many people saying cancer cannot be cured, and cancer patients live for five years on average. Therefore, she kept searching for information to contradict such discouraging certainty and found that “cancer, especially at stage one, can be cured.” The certainty about cancer as an incurable illness was further weakened when her employer’s doctor friend said, “Oh, she is stage one? She can definitely recover”. When she joined a group in Indonesia, she also “learned about a survivor who had lived for 20 years […] That gave [her] hope that we can fight cancer”. The alternative information regarding cancer survivors bolstered her confidence about recovery.

Adapting to chronic uncertainty

Our analysis identified six themes that illustrate stages of how participants adapted to chronic uncertainty: (a) coming to terms with the diagnosis, (b) focusing on the positive, (c) turning to spirituality, (d) focusing on a healthy diet, rest, and exercise, (e) managing stress, and (f) seeking support.

Coming to terms with the diagnosis

Although our participants initially found the diagnosis of cancer overwhelming and devastating, they indicated that they gradually came to terms with the realities of illness. For example, Iris told herself, “You can do this. It is not too late,” and decided to do her best and follow the doctor’s advice. Rachel remained calm because she knew that “if [she] panicked, [her] immune system might drop, making [her] sicker and unhappier.” Rose had mentally prepared herself since she first suspected cancer. She “even cut [her] long hair short because [she] knew that chemotherapy would cause hair loss.” Linda, initially feeling down after the diagnosis, accepted it and made peace with herself by looking up to those who had survived the ordeal. She told herself, “Even if I cry tears of blood, the cancer won’t go away. Why not accept it and make peace with myself?” Similarly, Patricia found no point in worrying, as she recognized the ultimate reality of life: “You live once, you die once.” Alice faced her cancer with optimism because she believed that “things will get better.” These quotes suggest that coming to terms with a diagnosis helped our participants cope with the uncertainty associated with their indeterminate future, as well as death and dying.

Focusing on the positive

Our participants reported that after coming to terms with the diagnosis, they began to focus on the positive aspects. For example, Iris was grateful because “the cancer was caught early”. Others felt thankful to their employer for “allowing me to rest completely during my sick leave” (Iris) and being like “friends or siblings to me” (Rose). All our participants felt fortunate to have access to affordable, high-quality healthcare in Hong Kong and appreciated the healthcare providers for their understanding and support. For example, Sally stated, “I am thankful to God for giving me this trial in a place where I received such good care”. Rachel was very grateful because “none of [her] surgeries cost [her] a single cent.” They also appreciated the support they received from employers, healthcare providers, or fellow MDWs.

Turning to spirituality

Some of the participants turned to spirituality to find meaning and purpose. They perceived that accepting the situation rather than questioning it and believing in God can significantly reduce stress. Rose stated, “Everything has a solution, and God has a purpose for everything […] I found a level of happiness that I never had before the diagnosis.” Sally “was resigned to God’s will and trusted that whatever happened was for the best”. Iris comforted herself by saying, “God willing, I’m strong. God willing, I will be fine. God willing, I can handle it”. Rose also resorted to God to comfort herself about losing her breast, as she said. “If I had to lose a breast, I would be okay with it because I knew my body belonged to God.” Alice “decided to trust in God and keep going.” These comments suggest that our participants’ faith in God became a powerful source of comfort and resilience, allowing them to transform fear and loss into acceptance, hope, and a renewed sense of inner peace amidst the turmoil of illness.

Focusing on a healthy diet, rest, and exercise

After going through a harrowing ordeal, our participants seemed convinced that a healthy, nutritious diet, rest, and exercise would help minimize uncertainty about their health. Sally, for instance, consistently sought medical advice regarding “what [she] should eat and what [she] should avoid.” Drawing on recommendations from peers within her support group, Rachel “took good care of [her] nutrition by drinking date water infusions, honey, and special milk.” Based on their experiences, they advised their colleagues to “make sure to save some for yourself [to] buy healthy food” (Rachel) and be mindful of “what you eat” (Tiffany). Patricia started avoiding sweet, salty, and fried foods and prioritized meat, vegetables, and fruits on alternate days. Emphasizing the necessity of prioritizing health over personal preference, she suggested: “If you want to be healthy, you might have to eat things you do not like.” Recognizing the value of rest and exercise, Rachel “started taking naps during the day” whenever she had that opportunity, and Jenifer invested in massage therapy, “to take care of [her] health.” Underscoring the importance of holistic well-being, Wendy advised her colleagues to “keep praying, stay motivated, maintain a healthy diet, and get enough rest!” In embracing these self-care practices, our participants reclaimed a sense of agency over their health and well-being.

Managing stress

Our participants recognized that managing stress was not only a critical component of their recovery process but also a key strategy in minimizing the chances of cancer recurrence. For example, one recounted that her oncologist reminded her to manage her stress. Rachel tried her best “to stay positive, not let stress affect [her] work.” Linda “just focused on following through with the treatment” without worrying about how her health would progress. She urged other MDWs not to worry too much about treatment because they “are legally protected and getting sick is not a crime”. Similarly, Alice “focused on maintaining a positive mindset and following the treatment plan”. Recognizing the overuse of phones as a source of stress, Wendy and Lily cautioned their colleagues, “to reduce staying up too late on your phones.” These reflections underscore how, by consciously managing stress and cultivating a positive outlook, our participants adapted to uncertainty.

Seeking instrumental and emotional support

Our participants reported seeking support from various sources to manage their uncertainty. For example, Rose requested her employer to accompany her to the hospital because she was “not very fluent in Cantonese or English”. Since Wendy could not dare to inform her new employer that she had cancer again, she called her “previous employer for help.” Our participants also sought emotional support from their employers. Their employers’ reassuring words, such as “You are not alone in this experience” (Iris), “You will be okay” (Rose), and “You need to stay strong to recover quickly” (Rachel), comforted them.

They also sought support from healthcare providers. For example, Sally and Wendy asked the hospital staff to communicate everything to their employers to avoid “any misunderstandings”. When our participants expressed worries, their healthcare providers reassured them by saying “Do not worry” (Iris) and “You will be healthy again” (Rachel). When Linda was anxious about losing her breast, her doctor consoled her by saying, “If the lumps shrink after chemotherapy, we will not need to remove the breast.” When Lily informed her oncologist that her visa was going to expire in six months, “he made an immediate booking for an early operation.”

They also received instrumental and emotional support from their fellow workers in Hong Kong. For example, for Iris, her friends were like her family, and she “always asked for support”. Other participants shared that their friends accompanied them to the hospital and assisted them in purchasing daily necessities. They also sought assistance from non-governmental organizations in Hong Kong, such as Peduli Sehat and the Indonesian Breast Cancer Foundation, to access free screening programs. The group helped them “stay strong” (Jennifer) emotionally by “getting advice from others with similar experiences” (Alice). Despite being far away, they also sought emotional support from their families by asking them to pray for their recovery and well-being. Before her surgery, Sally “called [her] children, [her] husband, and [her] mother to ask for their prayers.” This spiritual support reassured them and helped them maintain hope despite the uncertainty of their cancer journey.

Discussion

This research utilized Han et al.’s (2011) taxonomy of uncertainties and Brashers’ [38] theory of communication and uncertainty management to explore personal uncertainties experienced and coping strategies by Indonesian MDWs diagnosed with cancer in Hong Kong. The findings suggest that MDWs encounter significant psychosocial and existential uncertainty related to both the diagnosis and treatment of cancer. To cope with these challenges, our research participants engaged in (a) reducing uncertainty when it was perceived as threatening, (b) maintaining uncertainty when it provided more comfort than the possibility of negative certainty, (c) increasing uncertainty when negative certainty was discouraging, and (d) adapting to chronic (i.e., ongoing) uncertainty by accepting it as an inherent aspect of life.

Our participants’ experiences of personal uncertainty are consistent with the findings from previous research on cancer patients [13, 16, 47]. However, their experiences of personal uncertainty are distinctively shaped by their unique experiences as MDWs. First, they expressed pronounced psychosocial uncertainty regarding the risk of job termination and subsequent financial instability. This anxiety stemmed from their reliance on continued employment to access healthcare in Hong Kong and their responsibility as sole breadwinners supporting families in Indonesia. This observation aligns with prior studies on Filipino MDWs in Taiwan [32], Hong Kong [28], and Macau [67], which similarly highlight the precarious link between employment, healthcare access, and familial obligations. Second, as transnational MDWs required to reside in their employers’ homes, they experienced the uncertainty of undergoing treatment without physical presence and support from their loved ones because they would not be able to afford treatment if they returned to their home country. This finding is supported by previous research indicating that oncology services in Indonesia are often inaccessible due to geographical barriers, economic constraints, and inadequate health infrastructure [68].

Given the profound fear associated with cancer, our participants also experienced existential uncertainties, particularly regarding unpredictable health outcomes and the prospects of death and dying. While these findings are consistent with previous research on cancer-related existential concerns [13, 14, 18], our study offers a unique perspective as our participants expressed apprehension about dying in a foreign land, echoing the experiences of migrants who have permanently relocated to other countries [69].

To manage personal uncertainty, our participants employed various coping strategies, with seeking information to reduce uncertainty and adapting to uncertainty being more prominent. They sought information from online resources, healthcare providers, as well as friends and support groups in Hong Kong to understand their illness, the required treatment, treatment-induced side effects, and effective lifestyle changes, supporting the findings of existing research [15, 51]. The initial sense of instability and uncertainty created a condition that fostered new meaning, understanding, and post-traumatic growth (e.g., information seeking, positive lifestyle changes, increased appreciation for life, and spiritual transformation) in our participants, corroborating the findings of a scoping review [70]. These strategies helped them to shift away from feelings of hopelessness and helplessness toward optimism, acceptance, positivity, self-care, stress management, spirituality, and the development of emotional resilience. This finding aligns with the previous research, which has associated positive reframing and acceptance with perceived improved quality of life [13, 36, 37, 40, 71, 72]. Our participants’ sense of purpose, driven by the desire to recover, continue working, and provide financial support to their families, appeared to be instrumental in helping them move forward by positively coping with their uncertainties. The presence of this drive is significant, as a diminished sense of purpose has been linked to increased worries and anxieties [17].

Furthermore, although some scholars consider fatalism and spirituality to be maladaptive coping strategies [73], for our participants, these were adaptive strategies. By turning to spirituality, our participants recognized the impermanence of all situations (including life itself), developed confidence to face difficulties, accepted whatever transpired, maintained hope for positive outcomes, and planned for the future. Our findings are in line with a growing body of research that views religious faith or spirituality as an adaptive strategy because it can promote cancer patients’ psychosocial adjustment [18], provide a sense of meaning, peace, and hope in adverse situation [74], and contribute to holistic health, containing physical, mental, social, and spiritual dimensions [52].

Individuals experiencing serious illness often rely on others to help them navigate their uncertainties. Our participants also sought support from their employers, healthcare providers, friends, and organizations in Hong Kong, as well as family members in Indonesia. For our participants, employers’ support was apparently more important than that of friends and close relatives, contrasting with the finding of previous research, which reported support from family and friends to be the most significant [14, 71]. This finding can be attributed to the MDWs’ obligation to reside in their employers’ homes and the limited physical contact they have with their loved ones. Fortunately, most of them had supportive employers who treated their employees as ‘whole persons’ and fostered affective bonds [75]. Additionally, such a bond might have been consolidated by the MDWs’ desperate need and desire to keep their visas to continue treatment in Hong Kong. Our participants also found healthcare providers highly responsive to their needs, in line with the principles of patient-centered care [19] and shared decision-making [76]. Moreover, the support from close friends and community members enabled them to express their vulnerabilities, develop self-esteem and mutual understanding, and foster hope, corroborating the findings of previous research [17, 18]. Notably, engaging with other women who had similar experiences enabled them to understand their situations better, express their hopes and fears more openly, receive emotional support, become resilient, and look forward to the future in alignment with their situated commitments, structural constraints, emotions, and needs [26, 75].

However, some participants who were still grappling with personal uncertainties employed emotion-based strategies, such as avoiding distressing or overwhelming information, ignoring illness symptoms, and engaging in social withdrawal to create a defensive buffer as a means of self-protection from the negative influences of hurtful queries and comments, corroborating the findings of a previous study [35]. These strategies can also be explained in terms of the stages of grief and grieving, with the participants using these emotion-based strategies still being in the phases of denial, anger, or depression [77]. Ignoring the illness symptoms was particularly associated with their conditions as MDWs, which required them to prioritize care for others (i.e., their employers and family members back home) over their own needs [32]. By making this equity gap visible, our findings highlight the need to advance health equity to ensure the fundamental right to health for marginalized migrant populations.

Limitations and implications

This study offers empirical evidence on the psychosocial and existential uncertainties faced by Indonesian MDWs during cancer diagnosis and treatment in Hong Kong. From a health equity perspective, the uncertainties described by participants reflect not only the burdens of cancer but also forms of structural violence produced through restrictive migration governance and insufficiently responsive health and social care systems. For MDWs, uncertainty is intensified by conditions such as employment dependency, temporary status, live-in arrangements, and limited protection during illness. These findings suggest that cancer-related vulnerability among migrant workers is socially and institutionally produced, rather than simply individual. Accordingly, responsibility should rest not only on workers’ resilience or informal support networks, but also on health systems, employers, and policymakers to reduce avoidable barriers to diagnosis, treatment, communication, and continuity of care.

There are three limitations associated with our study. First, our study involved the purposive selection of a modest, homogeneous sample of Indonesian MDWs, which limits the transferability of our findings to MDWs from other sending countries. Second, given the sensitive and emotionally arousing nature of the topic, we interviewed participants only once and could not trace how their perception of uncertainty and its management changed over time. Third, we chose to focus only on personal uncertainty, although other uncertainties are equally important. Nevertheless, the findings reported here yield several important clinical, policy, and research implications.

Clinical implications

Although participants’ sense of agency was initially disrupted by their diagnosis, they demonstrated remarkable persistence through adaptive coping strategies, such as prioritizing self-care, managing stress, cultivating resilience, and embracing spirituality. These findings suggest that awareness-raising and counseling interventions, particularly those incorporating spiritual dimensions, may help MDWs reframe their experiences, identify new priorities, and discover meaning throughout their cancer journey [52, 74].

Policy implications

Given that MDWs often hesitate to seek medical advice due to fears of job loss, it is crucial to provide them with accessible information about health and employment rights, advocacy and support services, and emergency contacts in host locations. This information would be most effectively provided in their native language during pre-departure training. The consulate of the sending country should remain well-informed about the MDWs’ situation to ensure ongoing support for their well-being throughout their stay in Hong Kong. Third, participants highlighted the significant benefits of experience-sharing opportunities facilitated by grassroots welfare groups and community organizations, underscoring the value of involving these groups in co-designing culturally relevant awareness and counseling resources for MDWs with cancer [69]. Fourth, these findings underscore the continued need to strengthen processes of remigration [32] to home countries, especially when employment has had to end due to serious illness. This will require strong collaboration between labor-sending and labor-receiving nations, including intermediaries such as employment agencies, to achieve good migration governance [78] that is mutually beneficial for all parties involved and address structural violence stemming from employment precarity [26]. Additionally, to promote health equity for MDWs, sustainable systems need to be developed by sending countries that would allow individual workers to choose to return home for treatment without fearing the costs of care or the implications for the families of their loss of income.

Research implications

While these results provide preliminary insights into the lived experiences of Indonesian MDWs with cancer, longitudinal research is needed to substantiate these findings and more fully understand the resilience and adaptive capacities of this population in the context of serious illness. Given the scarcity of research in this area, further studies with larger and more diverse samples are essential to deepen our understanding of MDWs’ uncertainties, coping strategies, and support systems across cultural contexts. Therefore, future studies can explore other forms of uncertainty, as workers with cancer may also experience different types of uncertainty.
