Spectrally controlled dissipation in a target subsystem
Man Yin Cheung, Mona Berciu, Kyle Monkman

Abstract
We present a microscopic Hamiltonian time-evolution on an ancilla+target system that evolves the target to a steady state. Using this description, we demonstrate the potential of a spectrally controllable dissipator: Depending on the energy scale of the target, the subsystem reaches the intended steady state or remains partially trapped in the initial state. For a steady state which is pure, this protocol can function as an autonomous qubit reset. We can also choose a mixed steady state so that this functions as a tunable mixed-state preparation. With a particular dissipative condition, we guarantee the equilibration to the steady-state with spectral measure theory. This type of spectral control over dissipation is not present in the common Lindblad description of open systems. Our construction establishes a new design principle for engineered dissipation and opens a pathway toward tunable autonomous quantum control.



I
Introduction

Non-unitary operations have tremendous potential for use in quantum technology. On the one hand, they can be used to describe the imperfections of experiments which are not fully isolated from the environment
Raimond and Haroche 2006
;
Breuer and Petruccione 2002
. They also describe operations which are necessary for quantum technology such as qubit-reset
Aamir et al. 2025
, mixed-state preparation
Li et al. 2025
and quantum error correction
Li et al. 2024
;
Shtanko et al. 2025
;
Lachance-Quirion et al. 2024
. Many of these operations are implemented using a combination of measurement, unitary operations and even engineered bath coupling
Maurya et al. 2024
;
Wong et al. 2019
;
Harrington et al. 2022
. The bath coupling operations are widely described using the Lindblad description or non-hermitian physics.

One critical limitation of a Lindblad master equation is that it does not consider the bandwidth of the bath model
Breuer and Petruccione 2002
;
Monkman and Berciu 2024
. For example, consider a decay process from a state
|1\rangle
to a state
|0\rangle
. In a Lindblad description, the decay process always will occur regardless of the energies of the two states. In a realistic bath model, as described in this article, the decay process only occurs if the energies are sufficiently within the bath spectrum. Adding this additional spectral consideration would allow for a fuller description of the physics where some system states are within the bath spectrum and some are not.

In this article, we consider using an ancilla+target microscopic Hamiltonian description to implement a non-unitary equilibration operation on a target subsystem. The ancilla subsystem is infinite in size while the target is finite. This allows the ancilla to slowly dissipate the target to a final steady state density matrix. Critically, the process is largely dependent on the spectrum of the ancilla. We use spectral measure theory
Webb and Olver 2021
;
De Oliveira 2009
to prove that that if the target energy levels are sufficiently within the bath spectrum, there will be a guaranteed equilibration to a target steady state. We compare this spectral-dependent model to the Lindblad description in Figure
1
.

The ability to spectrally control dissipation enables a range of autonomous operations on the target subsystem
Maurya et al. 2024
;
Wong et al. 2019
;
Harrington et al. 2022
. When the steady state is pure, this enables a spectrally gated state-reset, enabled when the state energy falls within the bath spectrum. If the steady state is a mixed state, then the same principle applied for mixed-state preparation. More broadly, such controllable baths could serve as a design primitive for autonomous error mitigation. Utilizing spectral control of dissipation goes beyond the models in this article. Here we demonstrate this in concept in a model with a continuous bath spectrum.

We suggest two applications of the microscopic dissipative model in this article. In both cases, one can vary the energy of internal states to spectrally control dissipation. Firstly, this model can be used as a generic description of a dissipation process, even when the details of the bath are not known in microscopic detail. These spectrally dependent dissipative systems can come in the form of phonon baths
Kitzman et al. 2023
, spin baths
Ghosh et al. 2011
, and electromagnetic cavity modes
Naghiloo et al. 2019
;
Murch et al. 2012
. Our model can describe a target state which equilibrates to a pure or mixed steady state. In particular, it may be useful when the width of the bath spectrum is relevant, a scenario that Lindblad does not cover.

The second possible application is through direct engineering of the described couplings. The couplings operate on two quantum numbers, the ancilla
|j\rangle
and the target
|\phi\rangle
, written together as
|j\rangle\otimes|\phi\rangle
. Therefore the most direct applications are those which allow simultaneous ’hopping’ of the ancilla+target state. That is, a direct coupling between
|j\rangle\otimes|\phi\rangle
and
|i\rangle\otimes|\psi\rangle
. An illustrative example is a spin-flip hopping on an optical lattice
Bloch et al. 2008
, taking a particle from position 1 with spin up to position 2 with spin down i.e.
|j=1\rangle\otimes|\uparrow\rangle
to
|j=2\rangle\otimes|\downarrow\rangle
. The second example, which may be useful for quantum computing, is qudit-qubit couplings
Aamir et al. 2025
;
Li et al. 2024
where the qudit states are the ancilla acting an operation on the qubit. The required couplings are of the form
(\sigma^{+}\otimes Q+\sigma^{-}\otimes Q^{\dagger})
, a raising/lowering of the qudit and qubit operation Q. For finite ancilla system size, the process will act the same as the infinite case until the quantum information reaches the system boundary

.

II
State reset and mixing

Now we define the dissipative process. The joint ancilla+target Hilbert space is defined by two quantum numbers
|j\rangle\otimes|\phi\rangle
. Then the dissipative Hamiltonian is defined by the Hamiltonian

\displaystyle H=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\bigg(J_{j}|j+1\rangle\langle j|

\displaystyle\otimes

\displaystyle|\phi_{j+1}\rangle\langle\phi_{j}|+\text{h.c.}+

(1)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\mu_{j}|j\rangle\langle j|\otimes|\phi_{j}\rangle\langle\phi_{j}|\bigg)

,

|0\rangle\otimes|\phi_{0}\rangle

|1\rangle\otimes|\phi_{1}\rangle

We start with two theorems which we prove in the supplementary material with spectral theory and the Riemann-Lebesgue lemma. Consider the case where

\displaystyle J_{0}=C\

,

\displaystyle\ J_{j}=B\text{ for }j\geq 1

\displaystyle\mu_{0}=\mu\

,

\displaystyle\ \mu_{j}=0\text{ for }j\geq 1,

(2)

\displaystyle H=2Bh=\begin{pmatrix}\mu&C&0&0&\dots\\
C&0&B&0&\dots\\
0&B&0&B&\dots\\
0&0&B&0&\dots\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots\\
\end{pmatrix}

(3)

The time-evolution of

It is important to understand the physicality of the above Hamiltonian. For an example, consider
|j\rangle
as position and
|\phi_{j}\rangle
as spin degree of freedom. Then let
|\phi_{0}\rangle=|\uparrow\rangle
and
|\phi_{j}\rangle=|\downarrow\rangle
for

. Then this Hamiltonian takes a spin-up state at site zero and ’hops’ it into a larger part of the chain which is spin-down. The spin-up particle will only fully dissipate into the spin down chain if the energy levels (determined from
\mu
,

,

|0\rangle\otimes|\downarrow\rangle
, a spin down state at site zero, is not acted upon. Thus, this is a very targeted operation on a specific part of the Hilbert space.

The matrix
h
, defined in eq. (
3
), is a finite-rank perturbation of a free Jacobi matrix
\Delta
, defined as
\Delta=h[B=C,\mu=0]
. The spectral theory of the free
\Delta

\displaystyle n_{j}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e_{j}e_{j}^{\dagger}

\displaystyle P

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}(e_{2j}e_{2j}^{\dagger}-e_{2j+1}e_{2j+1}^{\dagger}).

(4)

Here
n_{j}
is analogous to an on-site occupation number and

is a parity-like operator. The expectation value of

is known as the even-odd-difference

;
Cinnirella et al. 2024
.

Then we have two theorems on dynamical time evolution that we prove in the supplemental material. Both of these theorems rely on the decay conditions that effectively allow the state to ’decay’ or move throughout the chain. In mathematical terms, this means that that there is no discrete spectra outside of the absolutely continuous spectral interval. For

, the condition is

\displaystyle 2|1-C_{B}^{2}|<||\mu_{B}|-\sqrt{\mu_{B}^{2}+4C_{B}^{2}-4}|

(5)

where
\mu_{B}=\mu/B
and
C_{B}=C/B
. If

. Another important case is if
\mu=0

. This condition, shown in Figure
1

.

Using this we have the first theorem:

Theorem 1

Consider the time evolution of an arbitrary initial state
e_{j}
for some finite
j
and where
H
satisfies (
5
). Then

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to+\infty}\langle{n}_{j}(t)\rangle=0.

(6)

Example 1: Qubit-reset.
We will now use Theorem 1 to purify/reset a quantum state. Assume that
B
,
C
, and
\mu
satisfy the decay condition eq. (
5
). Let the target subspace be two-dimensional with orthonormal basis states
|0\rangle
and
|1\rangle
. We start with the target in a mixed state

\displaystyle\rho_{AT}(t=0)=\frac{1}{2}|0\rangle\langle 0|\otimes(|0\rangle\langle 0|+|1\rangle\langle 1|)

(7)

so that

\displaystyle\rho_{T}(t=0)=\frac{1}{2}(|0\rangle\langle 0|+|1\rangle\langle 1|).

(8)

Then for the chain mapping, we let
|\phi_{0}\rangle=|1\rangle

|\phi_{j}\rangle=|0\rangle

\rho_{T}(t)=\left(\frac{\langle{n}_{0}(t)\rangle}{2}\right)|1\rangle\langle 1|+\left(\frac{2-\langle{n}_{0}(t)\rangle}{2}\right)|0\rangle\langle 0|.

(9)

This results in

\displaystyle\rho_{T}(t\rightarrow\infty)=|0\rangle\langle 0|.

(10)

Theorem 2

Consider time evolution of an arbitrary initial state
e_{j}
for some finite
j
and where
H
has
\mu=0
and

. Then

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to+\infty}\langle{P}(t)\rangle=0.

(11)

Example 2: Qubit-mixing
We will now use Theorem 2 to mix a quantum state. With a similar basis, we start in an initially pure state

\displaystyle\rho_{AT}(t=0)=|0\rangle\langle 0|\otimes|1\rangle\langle 1|.

(12)

so that

\displaystyle\rho_{T}(t=0)=|1\rangle\langle 1|.

(13)

This implies that

\displaystyle\rho_{T}(t)=\left(\frac{1+\langle P(t)\rangle}{2}\right)|1\rangle\langle 1|+\left(\frac{1-\langle P(t)\rangle}{2}\right)|0\rangle\langle 0|.

(14)

Then, using Theorem 2, we have the time evolved steady target state as

\displaystyle\rho_{T}(t\rightarrow\infty)=\frac{1}{2}(|0\rangle\langle 0|+|1\rangle\langle 1|).

(15)

Importantly, this example can be done with non-orthogonal target states
|a\rangle
and
|b\rangle

\displaystyle\rho_{T}(t\rightarrow\infty)=\frac{1}{2}(|a\rangle\langle a|+|b\rangle\langle b|).

III
Controllable dissipation.

In this section, we emphasis the idea of spectrally controllable dissipation. We start by looking at a spontaneous decay process. We describe it by a Lindblad Master equation and show that it does not describe spectral control, whereas the microscopic model presented in this article does.

IV
Discussion

In order to simulate more complex time evolved processes, one needs additional theory on block-Jacobi matrices. The block form in eq. (
20
) was only solvable with spectral theory in
Webb and Olver 2021
because it equally coupled the bath to all eigenstates of the system. In general, incorporating block-diagonal matrices could allow various time scales and steady state density matrices.

To determine system size scaling, one can look to the speed of quantum information on a chain. For a particle on a chain with hopping
B
, the maximum group velocity is
v_{\text{max}}=2B

Calabrese and Cardy 2006
. Then the system size for a time evolution t is approximately determined by
N=v_{\text{max}}t=2Bt
. One can understand this by looking at the occupation number
\langle n_{j}\rangle
which is plotted in Figure
3
. The system parameters are
\mu=0.5,\ B=1,\ C=0.5
. The occupation of particles mostly reaches the boundary at time
t\approx 40
and position
j\approx 80=(2B)\times(40)
. Then for the most part, the system acts as an infinite system until
t\approx 40
.

Further, consider
\langle n_{0}(t)\rangle
starting from
e_{0}
with
C<B
. Then it is known
Monkman and Berciu 2024
that
\langle n_{0}(t)\rangle\approx\exp(-\frac{C^2}{B}t)
. Therefore, for system size
N
, we can approximately make the expectation value as small as
\langle n_{0}(t)\rangle\approx\exp(-\frac{C^2}{B^2}N)
before the particle wave reaches the boundary. For example, for
C=1
,
B=2
and
N=10
, we can implement
\langle n_{0}(t)\rangle\approx\exp(-2.5)\approx 0.08
.

V
Conclusion

In this article, we have outlined an ancilla+target time evolution. Since operation on the target comes from tracing out the ancilla, it is completely-positive-trace-preserving map on the target subsystem. We can implement a steady state density matrix which is pure or mixed. Critically, this evolution is enabled by the spectral properties of the system.

There are two potential applications of this work. The first is to model the time evolution of any system coupled to a bath. In this case, we would only know the microscopic details of the target while the bath is somewhat unknown. The second possibility is through engineering these couplings on a qubit-qudit system, where the qubit is the target and the qudit is ancilla. This may be useful for qubit-reset and mixed-state preparation while it may also have potential for error correction.
