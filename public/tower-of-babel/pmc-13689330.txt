War’s invisible wounds: prevalence and determinants of post-traumatic stress disorder among Gaza women amidst the war: a mixed-method study
Authors: Fatima Masoud Al-Amouri, Ayman S Abutair, Shorouq Morjan, Ola Anabtawi, Dua’a Osta Ali, MoezAlIslam Faris, Manal Badrasawi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe war of 2023–2025 has brought unprecedented devastation to the Gaza Strip, intensifying the psychological toll on the Gazan population. Families were torn apart, and millions were forced to flee their homes. Women in Gaza often carry substantial caregiving responsibilities during war and displacement, which may increase their vulnerability to psychological distress. This study aimed to assess the burden and severity of post-traumatic stress symptoms among displaced women in Gaza and to examine their association with food insecurity, displacement-related conditions, and socioeconomic vulnerability during the ongoing war.MethodsThis mixed-methods study assessed the burden of post-traumatic stress symptoms in a convenience sample of displaced women in Gaza during the war. For the quantitative part, data were collected using a structured survey in Google Forms, which included sociodemographic and socioeconomic characteristics, displacement-related experiences, food insecurity, traumatic events, and post-traumatic stress symptoms. For the qualitative part, semi-structured, face-to-face interviews were carried out in Arabic by trained researchers with field experience.ResultsThe study included 503 displaced Gaza women (29.4 ± 7.3 years); most were married, unemployed, and living below the deep poverty line. The average duration of displacement was nearly one year, and most participants resided in tents under severe overcrowding and food shortages. Almost all women included in this study (98.2%) had severe PTSD symptom scores on the IES-R, with a mean score of 61.5 ± 9.5. Severe food insecurity affected about 71% of participants and was significantly associated with higher post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) symptom severity scores (p < 0.001). Higher PTSD symptom scores were also significantly associated with marital status (being divorced or widowed), family structure (having extended family), living conditions (living in a tent or shelters) during displacement, and food unavailability. Qualitative findings based on interviews with 34 women revealed five overarching themes: poverty and inequity, food as a luxury, wounds you cannot see, on the move, nowhere to belong, and small acts of resilience.ConclusionsThe findings highlight the profound psychological burden experienced by displaced women in this study during the ongoing war. Continuous exposure to violence, loss, displacement, hunger, and deprivation we closely interwind with severe post-traumatic stress symptoms and boarder psychological distress among women included in this study. Given that PTSD symptoms were assessed using a screening instrument rather than a diagnostic clinical interview, these findings should be interpreted as indicating a high burden of trauma-related distress within the study sample. They nevertheless underscore the urgent need for comprehensive humanitarian and psychosocial support for affected women.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12939-026-02952-6.Keywords: Gaza, Post-traumatic stress symptoms, Displacement, War crime, Famine, Psychological toll

Introduction

Gaza Strip, home to nearly 2.34 million Palestinians, continuously experiences widespread destruction and human suffering and is often described as “the world’s largest open-air prison.” Since the Nakba in 1948, Palestine, especially Gaza, has endured frequent wars, air raids, and incursions, resulting in thousands of deaths, injuries, displacement, and destruction of infrastructure and health facilities [1].

The most recent war in Gaza, which erupted in October 2023, unleashed unprecedented assaults from the air, sea, and land, affecting the densely populated area of 365 square kilometers, housing more than 2.34 million people. It claimed over 67,000 Palestinian deaths, with women and children comprising 70% of the victims, and resulted in more than 169,000 injuries. The devastation destroyed over 90% of homes, universities, hospitals, and other vital infrastructure [2, 3]. Over a million people were displaced from northern regions to the southern coastal area of Al-Mawasi, constituting less than 3% of Gaza’s total area. They faced severe shortages of water, healthcare, food, and medicine. The collapse of sewage networks and accumulated garbage contributed to disease outbreaks among displaced populations [2].

By mid-2024, the humanitarian crisis had deepened, with over 80% of Gaza’s population displaced and overcrowded shelters struggling to provide necessities [4–7]. Gaza has experienced one of the world’s worst recent food crises due to the blockade, displacement, and destruction of food production facilities, pushing the situation from food insecurity toward famine [8, 9]. Furthermore, economic devastation has heightened poverty and unemployment, with families struggling to meet basic needs [8]. Amid such conditions, exposure to violence, destruction, loss, displacement, and deprivation has created a pervasive atmosphere of trauma and fear, intensifying psychological distress among individuals and communities. Consequently, many residents are grappling with severe psychological distress, including post-traumatic stress symptoms, anxiety, and fear, as they navigate the constant threat of violence, loss, displacement, and deprivation [10].

Post-traumatic stress symptoms are common among war-affected populations, with an estimated prevalence of post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD)-related symptoms of around 26.5%, most of whom live in low- and middle-income countries [11]. PTSD manifests after intense psychological distress, whether from war, assault, or disasters, through persistent and intrusive reactions that cause significant functional impairment. According to the DSM-5, PTSD is characterized by intrusive traumatic memories, avoidance behaviors, negative alterations in mood and cognition, and hyperarousal following trauma exposure [12, 13].

Perceived exposure to the perils of war refers to the extent to which individuals feel personally threatened, as well as threatened by loved ones and their sense of safety. Greater intensity and frequency of traumatic experiences are strongly linked with higher PTSD severity. Studies consistently show that war exposure significantly impacts civilian mental health, with increased levels of post-traumatic stress symptoms and other mental health problems during conflict. Moreover, lower socioeconomic status, limited emotional support, female gender, and having children are associated with higher PTSD symptom levels in conflict settings [14].

Women, in particular, are among the most affected groups during wartime, bearing the greatest burden of caregiving and survival responsibilities. As primary caregivers, they are responsible for their families’ health, safety, and daily sustenance under extreme conditions. They are often forced to leave their homes to live in overcrowded shelters, further exacerbating suffering due to a lack of necessities. In such circumstances, women endure not only direct physical dangers but also long-term psychological, social, and economic consequences, resulting in enduring psychological trauma [15].

Despite the growing body of global research on war-related mental health outcomes, evidence on the psychological impact of the current war, particularly among women, remains limited. Women’s unique social roles, repeated displacement, and constant exposure to traumatic events position them at heightened risk for PTSD and related psychological distress. Understanding the burden, severity, and determinants of post-traumatic stress symptoms in this population is essential to inform psychosocial interventions and humanitarian response planning. Accordingly, this mixed-methods study aimed to assess the severity and burden of PTSD symptoms among displaced women in the Gaza Strip during the ongoing war and to explore factors associated with PTSD symptoms, including displacement-related experiences and food insecurity. The qualitative component was included to provide contextual understanding of women’s broader lived experiences during war and displacement, including psychological distress, food insecurity, and socioeconomic hardship.

Methods

Study design

This study employed a cross-sectional mixed-method design targeting Palestinian women residing in the Gaza Strip amidst the war that extended from October 2023 to October 2025. For the quantitative component, data were collected between May and June 2025 using a structured digital survey administered with the support of trained local field researchers in Gaza. Data were entered primarily through Google Forms and, in areas with poor or absent internet connectivity, through KoboToolbox, which allowed offline data entry and later synchronization once internet access became available. For the qualitative part, the one-to-one interviews were conducted between June and July 2025.

Study participants and sampling

Study population and setting

The study population consisted of Palestinian women residing in the five governorates of the Gaza Strip: Gaza City, North Gaza, Deir al-Balah, Khan Yunis, and Rafah. For the quantitative component, a convenient non-probability sampling technique was applied, and participants were recruited based on availability and willingness to participate. A probability-based sampling approach was not feasible because of the ongoing war, repeated population displacement, security constraints, and the absence of an up-to-date sampling frame for displaced women in Gaza. Therefore, convenience sampling was adopted to enable data collection across different displacement settings and governorates under highly constrained field conditions. This approach facilitated the inclusion of displaced women from different geographical areas and displacement contexts within the Gaza Strip.

The quantitative sample was restricted to women aged 18–45 years, which corresponds to the reproductive age group and the primary caregiving age range most affected by displacement-related household and childcare responsibilities during the war. This restriction was also applied to reduce heterogeneity related to age-related chronic illness and life-stage differences that could confound psychological and socioeconomic outcomes. Comparison with available population-level indicators from the Palestinian Central Bureau of Statistics (PCBS) suggests that the study sample broadly reflects Gaza’s high pre-war levels of female unemployment and deep poverty. However, married and university-educated women were somewhat overrepresented in the sample compared with national data, which should be considered when interpreting the representativeness of the findings [16].

For the qualitative component, 34 displaced women aged 18–65 years were included, residing in tents, shelter centers, or other temporary displacement settings across different areas of the Gaza Strip. This broader age range was intentionally used to capture variation in lived experiences across different life stages. Participants were purposively selected to ensure variation in displacement experiences, living conditions, and socioeconomic hardship. Initial participants were identified among women who had completed the quantitative survey and agreed to be contacted for follow-up interviews, as well as through local field researchers and community contacts. Additional participants were recruited through snowball sampling to enhance diversity of experiences until thematic saturation was reached.

Women were eligible to participate if they were aged ≥ 18 years and had resided in the Gaza Strip throughout the war period. Women were excluded if they had a prior diagnosis of mental disorders or severe chronic illnesses, such as cancer or kidney failure.

Sample size

For the quantitative component, the required sample size was initially calculated using the single population proportion formula. Assuming a 95% confidence level (Z = 1.96), a 5% margin of error, and an expected prevalence of PTSD symptoms of 35% based on prior studies in war-affected populations [17, 18], the minimum sample size was estimated at 350 participants. After adding 10% to account for possible non-response, the target sample was increased to 385 women. However, because the study aimed not only to estimate the burden of post-traumatic stress symptoms within the study sample, but also to examine associations between sociodemographic factors, displacement characteristics, food insecurity, and PTSD symptoms, a larger sample was deemed necessary to ensure sufficient statistical power for association analyses. Therefore, the final target sample size was set at approximately 500 women.

For the qualitative component, participants were purposively selected to capture variation in displacement experiences, including differences in living conditions, exposure to war-related events, and psychological experiences, rather than based on symptoms severity alone. The qualitative sample included women identified from the survey respondents as well as additional women recruited through local contacts and snowball referral. Data collection continued until thematic saturation was reached, which occurred after approximately 34 in-depth interviews. The integration of quantitative and qualitative data provided a more comprehensive understanding of women’s broader lived experiences during war and displacement, including the contextual factors surrounding psychological distress, food insecurity, and socioeconomic hardship.

Data collection

For For the quantitative component, eligible women were invited to participate through face-to-face approaches supported by trained local researchers. Only individuals who provided informed consent were included in the study. Data collection was conducted using a structured digital survey. Responses were entered primarily through Google Forms; however, in areas with poor or absent internet connectivity, the survey was administered via KoboToolbox, a mobile platform that supports offline data entry and automatic synchronization once an internet connection becomes available. This approach helped improve accessibility by enabling participation from women living in displacement centers and remote areas with limited access to stable internet.

The questionnaire was developed based on the study objectives and relevant literature, and underwent a multistep review process to enhance clarity, relevance, and cultural appropriateness. Content validity was assessed by eight academic experts, including three specializing in women’s health, three in nutrition, and two in public health, who reviewed the questionnaire for item clarity, relevance, comprehensiveness, and contextual suitability. Their feedback led to revisions in item wording, simplification of selected questions, and modification of culturally sensitive phrasing to improve clarity and appropriateness for the study setting. Following expert review, the questionnaire was piloted with a small sample of women (n = 10) to assess clarity, acceptability, and feasibility. The pilot also helped identify items that were difficult to understand or required further linguistic refinement. Feedback from the pilot phase was incorporated into the final version of the questionnaire before full-scale data collection.

The final version of the questionnaire included sections on sociodemographic and socioeconomic characteristics, displacement-related experiences, food insecurity and traumatic events, and post-traumatic stress symptoms (see supplementary file 1). The psychological impact of traumatic events was assessed using the Impact of Event Scale–Revised (IES-R), a validated self-report questionnaire designed to measure post-traumatic stress symptoms [19]. The IES-R consists of 22 items, each scored on a 5-point Likert scale from 0 “not at all” to 4 “extremely”. The total score ranges from 0 to 88, with higher scores indicating greater post-traumatic stress symptom severity. The IES-R score was also classified into 4 levels, from normal (0–23) to mild or clinical concern PTSD (24–32), a moderately probable diagnosis of PTSD (33–36), and severe PTSD (≥ 37) [20, 21]. The tool has demonstrated high reliability and validity, with Cronbach’s alpha values of 0.89, indicating excellent internal consistency [22, 23].

Food insecurity was assessed using the Food Insecurity Experience Scale (FIES), a validated tool developed by the Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO) to measure individuals’ access to adequate food [24]. The FIES evaluates experiences of food insufficiency over the past 12 months, including worries about food availability, inability to acquire nutritious food, limited food variety, meal skipping, and instances of going hungry. The scale consists of 8 dichotomous (yes/no) items that capture both the physical and emotional dimensions of food insecurity. The FIES has demonstrated high reliability and cross-cultural validity, making it suitable for use in diverse populations and contexts, including displacement and conflict settings [25].

For the qualitative part, semi-structured, face-to-face interviews were carried out in Arabic by trained researchers with field experience. Eligible women were approached in person in displacement settings by local field researchers, informed about the purpose of the interview, and invited to participate if they were willing to share their experiences. The interview guide was developed from the literature on food security, displacement, and psychosocial health, and reviewed by two experts in nutrition and mental health. Guiding questions addressed: (i) food availability and access; (ii) income hardship and coping strategies; (iii) humanitarian assistance; (iv) socioeconomic challenges and displacement-related characteristics; and (v) PTSD-related symptoms. Interviews lasted approximately 20–30 min and were audio-recorded after obtaining informed consent. Given the highly unstable and distressing wartime context, interviews were intentionally kept relatively brief to reduce participant burden while still allowing women to describe key aspects of their displacement, food insecurity, and psychological experiences. Interviews were conducted in private settings when possible. When full privacy could not be ensured, researchers sought the quietest and least interrupted available space and did not proceed if the setting was judged to compromise participant comfort or confidentiality.

Statistical analyses

Data were analyzed using SPSS version 27. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize participants’ sociodemographic characteristics, displacement experiences, food insecurity, and post-traumatic stress symptoms. Continuous variables were reported as means ± standard deviations (SD), and categorical variables as frequencies and percentages. Because PTSD symptom scores were not normally distributed, bivariate associations between PTSD symptom scores and categorical independent variables were examined using the Mann–Whitney U test for binary variables and the Kruskal–Wallis test for variables with more than two categories. Correlations between PTSD symptom scores and continuous variables were assessed using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient. Statistical significance was set at p ≤ 0.05.

For the qualitative component, interviews were transcribed verbatim in Arabic. Three professional researchers with expertise in PTSD, women’s health, and maternal/reproductive health independently analyzed the transcripts in three separate rounds. Each researcher coded and identified preliminary themes, which were then compared and discussed collectively. Through iterative discussions, the team refined subthemes and themes, ensuring they aligned with the study objectives. Significant words and phrases were categorized into major themes that reflected women’s lived experiences of psychological distress, food insecurity, and displacement.

Ethical considerations

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Board (IRB) at An-Najah National University, State of Palestine. Measures were taken to protect participants’ privacy, anonymity, and confidentiality throughout the study. All interviews were anonymized, and no identifying details were included in the transcripts. Verbal informed consent was obtained from participants prior to administering the structured survey. For the qualitative component, audio-recorded verbal informed consent was obtained immediately before commencing the semi-structured interviews. Participants were informed about the voluntary nature of participation, their right to decline any question, and their right to withdraw at any time without consequence. Given the sensitive nature of the study topic and the potential for emotional distress when discussing war-related experiences, interviewers were trained to conduct data collection respectfully and to discontinue interviews if participants became visibly distressed or wished to stop. Interviews were conducted in private settings whenever possible. When a fully private setting was not feasible, interviewers identified the most private space available, confirmed that participants were comfortable continuing, and avoided pursuing sensitive topics if privacy or safety could not be reasonably ensured. Due to the disruption of health and mental health services during the ongoing war, formal psychological referral pathways were not consistently available at the time of data collection.

Results

Sociodemographic characteristics

The mean age was 29.45 ± 7.32 years (range: 18–45). The majority of women were married (83.3%) and lived in the city (82.3%) (Table 1). The average number of family members was 5.51 ± 2.56 (range: 2–15). Their mean family income was 906.9 ± 1093 NIS, and among the minority who work, 4.6% work in the private sector, 2% in the governmental sector, and 6.6% in other sectors (Table 1).

Displacement characteristics

The mean number of displacement episodes among participants was 5.13 ± 3.66 (range: 1–30) times. The mean duration of displacement was 11.72 ± 4.60 months (range: 1–20). The mean number of individuals sharing the same living space during displacement was 18.60 ± 20.03 (range: 0–300) individuals. The majority of displaced women (82.9%) were living in tents, while 11.9% were living in a family or friend’s house, and 5% were living in a school or temporary shelter. In addition, 86.3% were accompanied by family members during displacement. Regarding the source of food during displacement, only 34% reported cooking with available raw materials, 12.1% buy food and supplies personally, and 87.1% rely on meals cooked and provided through charitable community kitchens (tekyeh), which served as an important food source for displaced families during the war. However, most meals were either canned or pre-cooked (68.2%), and 79.3% of participants reported receiving ongoing food support through humanitarian assistance programs, such as food parcels, bread distribution, and cash-based food assistance provided by UNRWA, the World Food Programme, and local charitable initiatives. Regarding the regular availability of food, 2.2% reported that food was always available in sufficient quantities, 3% reported that food was available but without variety, 12.3% reported that food was available most of the time, 18.9% reported that food was not available, and 63.6% reported that food was available only at times.

Traumatic events experienced during the war period

Notably, prolonged hunger due to food scarcity was the most frequently reported traumatic event (88.5%) during the war, followed by hunger among their children during displacement (73%) and spending time alone without any family or acquaintances (60%; Fig. 1).

Post-traumatic stress symptoms

Based on the IES-R severity classification used in this study, 98.2% of women in the sample were categorized as having severe PTSD symptoms (IES-R ≥ 37), with a mean total score of 61.54 ± 9.52 (range: 23–79).

Food insecurity

The mean score on the FIES was 6.90 ± 1.51 (0–8), with 70.6% severe food insecure, 25.2% moderate food insecure, and only 4.2% food secure.

The relationship between post-traumatic stress symptom severity and sociodemographic, displacement characteristics, and food insecurity experience

Significant associations were observed between post-traumatic stress symptom severity and several sociodemographic and displacement-related characteristics (Table 2). PTSD symptom scores differed significantly by marital status (p < 0.001), with the highest median scores observed among divorced and widowed women, followed by married women, whereas single women had the lowest median score. PTSD symptom severity was also significantly higher among women living in extended families compared to those living in nuclear families (p = 0.033).

Regarding displacement-related characteristics, women residing in tents or schools/temporary shelters had higher PTSD symptom scores than those staying in family or friends’ houses (p < 0.001). Food availability status was also significantly associated with PTSD symptom severity (p < 0.001), with the highest scores observed among women reporting having no food at all, whereas lower scores were observed among those reporting that food was always available in sufficient quantities or available in sufficient quantities without variety.

In contrast, women who reported cooking with available raw materials (p = 0.002) and those who bought food and supplies personally (p = 0.009) had lower PTSD symptom scores than those who did not.

Through inductive thematic analysis, five themes and their sub-themes were identified, each reflecting the multidimensional challenges of displacement: (1) Between poverty and inequity, (2) When food becomes a luxury, (3) Mental health and psychosocial distress, (4) Displacement and living conditions, and (5) Coping strategies and adaptation.

Theme 1: Between poverty and inequity

Theme 1.1: No income, soaring prices

Nearly all women described severe deterioration in their family’s financial situation. The combination of job losses, liquidity constraints, and skyrocketing prices left households unable to meet even basic needs.

There is no firewood available, and even if you find some, it is too expensive. My children go out and bring sticks from the street or cardboard so that we can cook something. We cannot afford to buy it. (P20)

My husband hasn’t worked at all since the war began. We have no income. (P16)

Prices are unimaginable. A single tomato or cucumber costs 10 shekels, if you can even find them. An onion costs 50 shekels for a slice. How can anyone live like this? (P34)

Theme 1.2: When inequality multiplies

Participants highlighted those inequalities existed before the war, but the ongoing war and displacement further amplified these disparities. Access to aid, work, and essential resources became even more uneven. At the same time, some traders and market monopolies took advantage of scarcity, raising prices and limiting the availability of goods, compounding the challenges faced by already vulnerable households. Perceptions of favoritism, nepotism, and corruption in aid delivery were common.

Aid is not fairly distributed. Whoever has connections gets it. Most of the time, they give it to relatives or their own group, and others are left with nothing. (P20)

Very unfair. There is no system, no oversight, no government distributing fairly. (P7)

Theme 2: When food becomes a luxury

This theme reflects the intense food insecurity experienced by displaced women in the Gaza Strip, highlighting both the physical scarcity of food and dependence on external aid due to extreme economic hardship and full siege.

Theme 2.1: No access, no choice

Participants emphasized that food was often unavailable in local markets or prohibitively expensive, leaving families with extremely limited quantities. Many households went on days surviving on small pieces of bread, while adults often sacrificed their portions for their children. The quality of available food was also a concern: families depended on canned goods, which sometimes expired or caused digestive discomfort, or on charity-provided food, which was not always sufficient or sterile.

We eat mostly lentils. Everyone gets a plate, but no one feels full. If flour were available, at least bread could feed everyone — but there is no flour. (P2)

We adults eat less so the children can eat first. (P7)

Sometimes the food quality is okay, but sometimes it’s burnt or damaged from the ashes. We eat whatever we can just to fill the child’s stomach. (P20)

It [cans] had preservatives, and whenever we ate it, it upset our stomachs and made us feel sick…. but we had no choice. (P1)

Theme 2.2: Reliance in desperation

With income completely depleted and prices soaring, families had no alternative but to rely on humanitarian aid. Participants highlighted that aid, while crucial, was often insufficient, unreliable, or unevenly distributed.

Right now, there is no assistance at all… not even from local charities. If there were, it would have made things a little easier. (P34)

Whenever anything is distributed nearby, we run with the children to get it. We have no other option. (P21)

Theme 3: Wounds you cannot see

The psychological toll of displacement and violence was profound, reflected in severe post-traumatic stress symptoms and emotional distress. Women reported post-traumatic stress symptoms, including anxiety, hypervigilance, intrusive memories, and depressive symptoms. The loss of loved ones, constant bombing, and witnessing death contributed to overwhelming emotional distress.

I feel there is no safety. People tell me to sleep, but I can’t. (P33)

It [war] ruined me. I’m much more irritable than before, easily overwhelmed and exhausted; I scrutinize every little thing. I’m under constant psychological pressure, I remain irritable, and I keep shouting. (P16)

I saw my neighbor and her children killed in front of me. The day before, she was sitting in my house…. That was the hardest moment of my life. (P18)

Anxiety, tension, and fear from the rockets and the evacuations. (P1)

Our mental state is extremely difficult, unbearable. There are no words to describe it, war, death, killing, destruction. Psychologically, we are completely exhausted. (P20)

Look, both my husband’s family and my own are proud people, and we never wanted to resort to aid. But during this period, we were deeply humiliated — we were forced to, because there was no other choice. (p17)

Theme 4.: On the move, nowhere to belong

Theme 4.1: Fleeing under fire

Displacement was often chaotic, frightening, and physically exhausting, contributing to intense psychological distress. Families fled under bombardment, carrying minimal belongings on their backs, walking long distances in the absence of transport, or facing unaffordable costs. The journey itself became a scene of danger, fear, and survival.

They were shooting at us while we were fleeing… the first time we were displaced, it was hard to carry our things and leave the house. (P5)

I still remember the moment, the bombing, the fire in front of us, and we just ran, I don’t know how we survived. (P9)

The last time I fled, I had to walk under shooting. No transportation, and the prices were impossible. (P14)

Mostly it was walking, everything on our backs. (P7)

Living the unlivable

Living conditions in displacement sites, either tents or hospital/school corridors, were described as degrading and unbearable. Families endured extreme heat, poor sanitation, lack of clean water, overcrowded tents, pests, and lack of privacy. Many lived in temporary shelters of nylon sheets and cloth, stripped of dignity and necessities.

We suffered from the lack of fresh water, even salty… we had to walk long, long, long distances just to get it. (P31)

Until now, I don’t even have a proper tent, just nylon sheets and blankets. (P18)

It was so hot inside the tent we couldn’t even escape from the heat. (P25)

The bathrooms were terrible, shared, and never like your own home, no matter how much you cleaned. (P26)

There’s no privacy at all. It’s not even a separate tent just for us, it’s one tent, and you could say there are more than 13 of us all living inside it. (P10)

Theme 5: Small acts of resilience

Participants described how the war disrupted education and daily life, forcing families to seek creative ways to cope amid ongoing hardship and psychological distress. Children’s schooling was interrupted, while households resorted to improvised substitutes for essential items, from sanitary pads to medicine.

Theme 5.1: Lost classrooms

Formal schooling collapsed under bombardment. Parents tried to step in as teachers, but persistent displacement, attacks, and overwhelming daily challenges left many children without consistent education.

I used to teach my little girl; she’s in fourth grade… Now she goes twice a week to a small learning center that opened nearby. (P24)

No education since the truce ended… my daughter should be in 10th grade but missed both 10th and 11th. Now she’s entering “Tawjihi (the Palestinian General Secondary Education Certificate Examination required for university admission)” having forgotten her studies. (P18)

Theme 5.2: Survival creativity

With no access to basic goods, women improvised substitutes for hygiene products, fuel, and even medicine. This creativity reflected both resilience and the desperation of surviving without essentials.

When clementines came in season, I dried and ground the peel to use like deodorant. I even bought adult diapers, cut and sewed them into sanitary pads. (P7)

For the children’s medicine, we went back to natural herbs — sage, rosemary, whatever we could find. It is the best we have (P23)

Discussion

This study aimed to examine the burden and severity of post-traumatic stress symptoms and their relationship with food insecurity, displacement, and sociodemographic factors among displaced women in the study sample during wartime in the Gaza Strip, using a mixed quantitative and qualitative approach to explore the multidimensional challenges of displacement. The findings showed a high burden of post-traumatic stress symptoms in the study sample, with symptom severity significantly associated with poor displacement conditions and food insecurity. The qualitative findings provided contextual insight into how economic collapse, food insecurity, repeated displacement, and insecurity shaped women’s psychological distress during the ongoing war.

In the current study, a strikingly high proportion of participants had severe post-traumatic stress symptom scores on the IES-R, indicating substantial psychological distress in the study sample. However, these findings should be interpreted cautiously. Data were collected during an ongoing war, and the study used the IES-R as a screening instrument rather than a diagnostic clinical interview. This makes it difficult to clearly distinguish between trauma-related symptoms and distress arising from chronic adversity such as hunger, displacement, and poor living conditions. Therefore, the elevated scores observed in this study are better understood as reflecting severe post-traumatic stress symptoms and broader psychological distress rather than clinically confirmed PTSD. In addition, while some participants were exposed to acute traumatic events, such as witnessing injury or death, many were also living under prolonged stressors including food insecurity, repeated displacement, and inadequate shelter. The observed symptom burden may therefore reflect the combined psychological impact of both acute trauma and chronic adversity, which the IES-R is not designed to differentiate.

Previous studies in conflict-affected settings have similarly documented high levels of post-traumatic stress symptoms among civilians exposed to war-related trauma and loss, including Sudan and Ukraine [18, 26]. However, comparisons should be interpreted with caution due to differences in assessment methods, as the present study assessed PTSD symptoms using the IES-R screening instrument, whereas some previous studies relied on diagnostic criteria or structured clinical interviews. However, comparisons should be made cautiously because studies differ in context, timing, and methods of assessment, and some have relied on diagnostic criteria or structured clinical interviews rather than symptom screening tools. In the present study, the qualitative findings complemented the quantitative results through the theme “Wounds you cannot see,” in which women described persistent fear, intrusive memories, emotional exhaustion, and a continuous sense of insecurity. These narratives suggest that women’s distress was shaped not only by direct exposure to violence and loss but also by the ongoing strain of surviving under repeated displacement and deprivation.

Living conditions during displacement emerged as a major correlate of symptom severity. Women living in tents exhibited significantly higher PTSD symptom scores than those staying with relatives or in rented spaces. This finding suggests that the psychological burden of war may be intensified by the material and social conditions of displacement itself, including overcrowding, lack of privacy, poor sanitation, exposure to harsh weather, and uncertainty about safety and stability. This finding are consistent with previous studies from Gaza [27, 28], which highlight the disproportionate psychological burden borne by displaced women [27]. Other conflict zones, such as Sudan [29, 30], Ukraine [31], and Syria [32], also identified higher levels of post-traumatic stress symptoms among women due to differential trauma exposure and societal roles. This finding suggests that the psychological burden of war may be intensified by the material and social conditions of displacement itself, including overcrowding, lack of privacy, poor sanitation, exposure to harsh weather, and uncertainty about safety and stability.

Food insecurity demonstrated a significant positive relationship with post-traumatic stress symptom scores. Socioeconomic vulnerability appeared to play a central role in shaping women’s exposure to food insecurity; even when food was physically available, it was often unaffordable because of limited income. This likely intensified both material deprivation and psychological distress. In our sample, about 96% of participants were classified as moderately to severely food insecure, a burden markedly higher than pre-war estimates in the Gaza Strip, where approximately 60% of the population experienced moderate to severe food insecurity [33]. In addition, participants who reported relying on available raw materials for cooking or buying food and supplies personally had lower post-traumatic stress symptom scores than their counterparts. Similar associations between food insecurity and poorer mental health outcomes, including PTSD symptoms, have been reported among Syrian refugee mothers in Lebanon [34]. The qualitative findings added important context to this relationship through the theme “When food becomes a luxury,” which captured women’s experiences of deprivation, helplessness, and emotional strain in trying to secure food for their families. Participants described skipping meals so their children could eat and consuming poor-quality or spoiled foods out of necessity. These narratives suggest that food insecurity in this context was not only a marker of economic hardship, but also a psychologically distressing experience in itself, particularly when women were unable to meet their children’s basic needs. This interpretation is consistent with previous research showing that food insecurity exacerbates psychological distress among Gazan women, especially mothers, for whom hunger and the inability to provide food for their children create an additional emotional burden [35]. Notably, the impact of hunger extended beyond chronic deprivation to include direct war-related distress. Prolonged hunger due to lack of food was the most frequently reported shocking event during the war, followed by witnessing children’s hunger during displacement. Together, these findings suggest that food insecurity, in this setting, functioned not only as a socioeconomic stressor but also as part of the broader traumatic environment in which women were living.

When analyzing the relationship between post-traumatic stress symptom scores and marital status, divorced women exhibited the highest symptom levels, followed by widowed and married participants, while single women had the lowest. This pattern may reflect the compounded psychological burden of caregiving and economic responsibility during wartime, particularly in the absence of a partner. Similar findings were reported among Syrian refugees, where divorced women also showed the highest burden of PTSD, followed by widowed, married, and single participants [36]. In insecure and economically deprived settings, caregiving responsibilities may further intensify stress and psychological vulnerability [37]. The qualitative findings added important context to this pattern through the theme “Between poverty and equity.” Women described total income loss, soaring prices, and the impossibility of meeting basic needs. The burden appeared especially acute for widowed and divorced women, who lacked spousal support and often carried full responsibility for their families’ survival. Participants also described inequitable aid distribution and corruption as additional sources of distress. Together, these experiences suggest that socioeconomic deprivation and perceived unfairness may have intensified feelings of helplessness and hopelessness, contributing to higher post-traumatic stress symptom severity among women facing both social and economic marginalization. These findings should also be understood within the gendered realities of war and displacement, where women often carry primary caregiving responsibilities and the daily burden of securing food, maintaining household needs, and protecting children under conditions of profound insecurity.

An important socioeconomic finding in this study was the coexistence of relatively high educational attainment with widespread unemployment and poverty among participants. Although more than 93% of women had completed secondary school or university education, nearly 87% were unemployed and most were living below the deep poverty line. This apparent contradiction likely reflects the severe economic disruption caused by the ongoing war, including destruction of workplaces, loss of livelihoods, displacement, and limited employment opportunities, rather than a lack of educational qualifications. These findings underscore the extent to which the ongoing war can erode the socioeconomic benefits typically associated with educational attainment and contribute to heightened economic vulnerability among women.

The study also found that age was positively associated with higher post-traumatic stress symptom scores within the quantitative sample. Although the relationship between age and post-traumatic stress symptoms appears to be context-specific, several studies have reported inconsistent findings, with some showing lower symptom levels with increasing age [26, 38, 39], while others reported greater severity among older displaced individuals during the Ukrainian-Russian conflict [40]. In the present study, which included women aged 18–45 years in the quantitative component, higher symptom scores were observed among women in the older age range within this sampled group. This pattern may reflect the cumulative burden of caregiving responsibilities, repeated displacement, and prolonged exposure to war-related stressors over time. At the same time, this finding should be interpreted cautiously given the relatively narrow age range of the quantitative sample.

Despite the profound psychological toll of war and the disruption of daily life, women in the qualitative sample demonstrated resilience through adaptive coping and efforts to preserve dignity under extreme adversity. They described trying to maintain a sense of normalcy for their families despite displacement, insecurity, and scarcity. In line with previous literature showing that coping strategies can support psychological endurance in wartime [41]. In the present study, women described trying to maintain a sense of normalcy for their children despite displacement and insecurity. With schools destroyed and formal education halted, some mothers attempted to continue their children’s education at home or through informal learning spaces. This effort to preserve education may be understood not only as a practical coping strategy, but also as a way of protecting children’s routines, identity, and future during prolonged crisis. Previous research among young Palestine refugee children in Gaza following the May 2021 escalation documented high levels of stress and trauma-related symptoms, highlighting the vulnerability of children to the psychological consequences of repeated exposure to violence and displacement [42]. Recent studies have also shown that schools, once associated with joy and learning, have become spaces marked by trauma, grief, and uncertainty, while children nevertheless continue to view education as a pathway to dignity, survival, and national identity [43]. Women also described improvising alternatives to scarce daily necessities, reflecting both material deprivation and adaptive resourcefulness. These narratives illustrate how coping in this context often involved practical ingenuity shaped by scarcity, rather than emotional recovery from distress. Previous reports from Gaza similarly show that women’s daily routines during wartime become centered on basic survival within overcrowded and resource-poor displacement settings [44]. Similar findings among Syrian and Palestinian refugee women in Lebanon identified resilience as a protective factor that helps women navigate daily hardship and psychological strain through adaptive strategies and social support [45]. Taken together, these findings suggest that resilience among women in this study coexisted with severe post-traumatic stress symptoms and should be understood as survival under constraint rather than recovery from trauma.

This study has several limitations. Its cross-sectional design limited the ability to establish causal relationships between the study variables. The use of self-reported measures for post-traumatic stress symptoms and food insecurity may have introduced recall or social desirability bias. The use of convenience sampling for the quantitative component and snowball sampling for the qualitative component may have introduced selection bias and limited the generalizability of the findings beyond the study sample. Although participants were recruited from different governorates across the Gaza Strip, the sample may not fully reflect the broader population of women in Gaza. Women experiencing more severe displacement, poverty, and food insecurity may have been overrepresented due to the humanitarian context and the practical constraints of data collection during the war, while women in less accessible areas may have been underrepresented. The temporal and contextual constraints of data collection during an ongoing war may also have influenced participants’ responses, particularly regarding food availability and emotional states. The absence of pre-war baseline data limited the ability to determine the extent to which the observed post-traumatic stress symptom burden was attributable specifically to the ongoing war versus cumulative prior trauma exposure. In addition, information on participants’ personal physical injuries sustained during the conflict was not collected, limiting our ability to assess their potential influence on psychological distress and post-traumatic stress symptoms. Although this study focused on post-traumatic stress symptoms, it did not examine other gender-specific dimensions of women’s wartime experiences, such as pregnancy-related challenges, reproductive health needs, or menstrual hygiene management during displacement, which may also shape psychological distress. Finally, although the qualitative interviews enriched understanding of women’s lived experiences, they were conducted under highly distressing and constrained conditions, which may have limited participants’ ability to fully articulate sensitive psychological or emotional experiences.

Despite these limitations, this study has several strengths. It captured data in real time during one of the most devastating wars in Gaza’s recent history and focused on a highly vulnerable yet understudied population of displaced women. By using a mixed-methods design and validated instruments, the study provided both quantitative and qualitative insight into the multidimensional factors associated with post-traumatic stress symptoms, including displacement conditions, food insecurity, and socioeconomic hardship. It also contributes to the limited literature on women’s lived experiences during the ongoing 2023–2025 war in Gaza and may help inform humanitarian and mental health responses in similar conflict settings.

Conclusion

The findings of this study highlight the acute and chronic stressors experienced by the displaced women included in this study during the 2023–2025 war in the Gaza Strip. Continuous exposure to these intersecting stressors was associated with a high burden of post-traumatic stress symptoms among the women included in this study, many of whom carried the dual burden of survival and caregiving under extreme adversity. Beyond documenting a high burden of post-traumatic stress symptoms in this study sample, this study highlights how chronic wartime stressors, particularly food insecurity, repeated displacement, and deteriorating living conditions, were closely intertwined with women’s psychological distress, underscoring the need to understand trauma in this context not only as exposure to acute violence but also as a prolonged experience of deprivation and survival under siege.

These findings underscore the urgent need for comprehensive humanitarian and psychosocial interventions of affected women. Strengthening access to mental health care, ensuring food security, and improving living conditions are essential to support recovery and resilience.

Declarations

Ethics approval and consent to participate

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Board (IRB) at An-Najah National University, State of Palestine. Measures were taken to protect participants’ privacy, anonymity, and confidentiality throughout the study. All interviews were anonymized, and no identifying details were included in the transcripts. Verbal informed consent was obtained from participants prior to administering the structured survey. For the qualitative component, audio-recorded verbal informed consent was obtained immediately before commencing the semi-structured interviews. Participants were informed about the voluntary nature of participation, their right to decline any question, and their right to withdraw at any time without consequence. Given the sensitive nature of the study topic and the potential for emotional distress when discussing war-related experiences, interviewers were trained to conduct data collection respectfully and to discontinue interviews if participants became visibly distressed or wished to stop. Interviews were conducted in private settings whenever possible. When a fully private setting was not feasible, interviewers identified the most private space available, confirmed that participants were comfortable continuing, and avoided pursuing sensitive topics if privacy or safety could not be reasonably ensured. Due to the disruption of health and mental health services during the ongoing war, formal psychological referral pathways were not consistently available at the time of data collection.
