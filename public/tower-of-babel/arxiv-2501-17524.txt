Introduction

One common way to describe a finite group is to provide a set of
generators. In view of this, it is unsurprising that there have been
many studies into the minimum number of generators required for
particular groups. Famous examples include the fact, often taught
within a first course on group theory, that a finite symmetric group
can be generated by two permutations, while it is a consequence of the
Classification of Finite Simple Groups that a finite simple group
needs only two generators. (Steinberg
[
12
]
establishes
this for the finite simple groups of Lie type, while Aschbacher and
Guralnick complete the work by dealing with the sporadic simple
groups, see
[
1
, Theorem B]
.)

Another theme within the study of generators for finite groups is the
behaviour of the minimum number of generators required for various
sequences of finite groups. For example, in a sequence of papers
beginning with
[
13
]
, Wiegold considers the behaviour of the
number of generators required for a direct product
G^{n}
of

n
copies of a group
G
as
n\to\infty
. An alternative
construction that could be used instead of the direct product is the
wreath product constructed from suitable actions of the groups
involved. We shall describe a few of such studies that are most
closely linked to the content of this article.

Bhattacharjee
[
2
]
considers the probability of generating an
iterated wreath product of non-abelian simple alternating groups by
two randomly chosen elements, where these wreath products are
constructed from their natural actions. She shows that this
probability can be made arbitrarily close to
1
by choosing the first
alternating group used to have large degree. In particular, this
implies that such an iterated wreath product requires only two
generators: her result assumes that the first alternating group has
sufficiently large degree but this hypothesis is not actually needed
to reach this conclusion. The second author
[
9
]
generalized
Bhattarcharjee’s work and showed the same holds for an iterated wreath
product constructed using any non-abelian simple groups with any
faithful transitive actions. Bondarenko
[
3
]
considers
iterated wreath products
W_{n}
of finite transitive permutation
groups
G_{i}
with uniformly bounded number of generators and shows
that the number of generators for
W_{n}
is bounded if and only if
the abelian groups
G_{1}/G_{1}^{\prime}\times G_{2}/G_{2}^{\prime}\times\dots\times G_{n}/G_{n}^{\prime}
have bounded number of generators.

More recently, East and Mitchell
[
4
]
show, using
relatively elementary methods, that the wreath product of two finite
symmetric or alternating groups can be generated by two elements.
They suggest an investigation of the minimal number of generators for
an iterated wreath product involving alternating and symmetric
groups. The technology to determine the number of generators required
for an iterated wreath product of symmetric groups has actually
existed for some time. Lucchini
[
7
, Theorem 8]
describes
the number of generators required for a wreath product of an abelian
group by a symmetric group of degree
n\geqslant 2
. We use this theorem
as part of our argument and this on its own would be enough to
determine the number of generators required for an iterated wreath
product of symmetric groups. Finally, we mention that Woryna
describes the number of generators required for an iterated wreath
product of cyclic groups and later an iterated product of abelian
groups, see
[
14
, Theorem 1.1]

and
[
15
, Theorem 1.1]
. Both of Woryna’s results also make
use of the work of Lucchini
[
7
]
to establish the bounds for
the number of generators required.

In this article, we shall establish the precise number of generators
required for an iterated wreath product constructed from alternating
groups, symmetric groups and cyclic groups in their natural action.
In the case of a cyclic group of order
n
, by this we mean the
regular action of the cyclic group on
n
points. If one were to take
only cyclic groups, then one recovers Woryna’s theorem

[
14
, Theorem 1.1]
. Taking only alternating groups of
degree at least
5
, one recovers the observation that such an
iterated wreath product is
2
-generated, while taking only
alternating groups and symmetric groups gives an answer to the
question raised by East and Mitchell. Consequently, our main theorem
can be viewed as a simultaneous extension of several of the results
that we have just described.

To state our theorem, we first recall some standard notation. If

G
is a finite group, we write
d(G)
for the minimum number of
generators required for
G
. If
A
is a finite abelian group, we
write
d_{p}(A)
for the minimum number of generators required for the
Sylow
p
-subgroup of
A
. Thus, it is a straightforward
consequence of the classification of finite abelian groups that, for
such an abelian group
A
,
d(A)=\max d_{p}(A)
where the maximum
is taken over all prime numbers
p
. We shall establish the following
theorem:

Theorem A
.

Let
k\geqslant 2
and let
G_{1}
,
G_{2}
, …,
G_{k}
be a
sequence of non-trivial finite groups each of which is either an
alternating group, a symmetric group or a cyclic group. Let
W=G_{k}\operatorname{wr}G_{k-1}\operatorname{wr}\dots\operatorname{wr}G_{1}
be the iterated wreath
product constructed from the natural action of each
G_{i}
. Define

A
to be the abelianization of the iterated wreath product
G_{k}\operatorname{wr}G_{k-1}\operatorname{wr}\dots\operatorname{wr}G_{2}
. Then

d(W)=\begin{cases}\max(2,d(A),d_{3}(A)+1)&\text{if $G_{1}=A_{4}$},\\
\max(2,d(A))&\text{if $G_{1}=A_{n}$ with $n\geqslant 5$},\\
\max(2,d(A),d_{2}(A)+1)&\text{if $G_{1}=S_{n}$ with $n\geqslant 3$},\\
\max(2,d(A)+1)&\text{if $G_{1}$~is cyclic}.\end{cases}

The abelian group
A
appearing in the above statement has the form

A=G_{k}/G_{k}^{\prime}\times G_{k-1}/G_{k-1}^{\prime}\times\dots\times G_{2}/G_{2}^{\prime}

and we know that each factor
G_{i}/G_{i}^{\prime}
is trivial when
G_{i}
is
an alternative group of degree at least
5
, is cyclic of order
2

when
G_{i}
is a symmetric group of degree at least
3
, is cyclic of
order
3
when
G_{i}\cong A_{4}
, and is isomorphic to the
original
G_{i}
when this is a cyclic group. Consequently, one can
readily determine the values of
d(A)
and
d_{p}(A)
required in the
statement simply by counting the number of groups of each type
appearing in the sequence
G_{2}
,
G_{3}
, …,
G_{k}
. The
following corollary is deduced by performing this elementary
arithmetic. It notes that the number of generators required for the
iterated wreath product
W
can be expressed by the same formula
irrespective of the choice of
G_{1}
provided that this is a
non-abelian alternating or symmetric group.

Corollary B
.

Let
k\geqslant 2
and
G_{1}
,
G_{2}
, …,
G_{k}
be a sequence of
non-trivial finite groups each of which is either an alternating
group, a symmetric group or a cyclic group. Suppose that
G_{1}
is

not
cyclic and define the following parameters:

(i)

a_{4}
is the number of terms
G_{1}
,
G_{2}
,
…,
G_{k}
that are alternating groups of degree
4
;

(ii)

s
is the number of terms that are non-abelian symmetric
groups; and,

(iii)

for each prime
p
,
c_{p}
is the number of terms that are
cyclic groups of order divisible by
p
.

Let
W=G_{k}\operatorname{wr}G_{k-1}\operatorname{wr}\dots\operatorname{wr}G_{1}
be the iterated
wreath product constructed from the natural action of each
G_{i}
.
Then

d(W)=\max(2,d(W/W^{\prime}))=\max_{p}(2,c_{2}+s,c_{3}+a_{4},c_{p}),

where the maximum is taken over all primes
p
.

Indeed, note that, expressed in terms of the parameters given in
Corollary
B
,

d_{2}(W/W^{\prime})=c_{2}+s,\qquad d_{3}(W/W^{\prime})=c_{3}+a_{4},\qquad d_{p}(W/W^{\prime})=c_{p}

for
p\geqslant 5
. Hence
d(W/W^{\prime})=\max(c_{2}+s,c_{3}+a_{4},c_{p})

immediately. The rest of the corollary is established by careful
consideration of the formula given in Theorem
A
.

We finish this introduction by describing the structure of the paper.
In Section
2
, we recall the definition of the wreath
product and fix the notation that we use. We also state some results
that we depend upon, particularly including some important facts from
Lucchini’s work
[
7
]
. Our proof uses the methods
within
[
7
]
, but extends them to cover the case of a wreath
product of an abelian group by an alternating group. We devote
Section
3
to developing the necessary theory to
establish the number of generators of such a wreath product. We prove
our main theorem in the final section of the article. We do this by
determining a formula for the wreath product
A\operatorname{wr}G_{1}
and
establishing that
d(W)=\max(2,d(A\operatorname{wr}G_{1}))
, where
A
and
W

are as in Theorem
A
(see
Lemmas
4.1
and
4.2
below).

Preliminaries

In this section, we fix the notation that we use and summarize the
facts from previous work that we rely upon. We start by recalling the
definition of a wreath product and associated notation.

Let
G
and
H
be permutation groups on the finite sets
X
and
Y
,
respectively. Let
B=\prod_{y\in Y}G_{y}
be the direct product
of copies of
G
indexed by the set
Y
. We shall write
b=(g_{y})=(g_{y})_{y\in Y}
for an element of
B
expressed as a
sequence of elements from
G
indexed by
Y
. We define an action
of
H
on
B
by permuting the entries of its elements in the same way
that
H
acts on
Y
:

(g_{y})^{h}=(g_{yh^{-1}})

for
b=(g_{y})\in B
and
h\in H
. The (permutational) wreath
product
W=G\operatorname{wr}_{Y}H
of
G
by
H
is the semidirect product
W=B\rtimes H
constructed via this action. In this article, we shall
construct wreath products from natural actions specified for the
ingredients
G
and
H
. Consequently, we shall typically omit the
subscript on the wreath product and simply write
W=G\operatorname{wr}H
for the
wreath product of
G
by
H
constructed via the natural action of the
permutation group
H
.

The wreath product
W=G\operatorname{wr}H
has a natural action on the Cartesian
product
X\times Y
given by

(x,y)^{g}=(x^{g_{y}},y^{h})

for
(x,y)\in X\times Y
and
g=(g_{y})h\in W
. As a
consequence, if
G_{1}
,
G_{2}
, …,
G_{k}
is a sequence of
permutation groups acting on the sets
X_{1}
,
X_{2}
,
…,
X_{k}
, respectively, then we may iterate the wreath product
construction exploiting this induced action. Thus we recursively
define
W_{1}=G_{1}
acting upon the set
X_{1}
and then, having
defined
W_{i-1}
with an action on
X_{i-1}\times\dots\times X_{1}
, we define
W_{i}=G_{i}\operatorname{wr}W_{i-1}
with its action on

X_{i}\times X_{i-1}\times\dots\times X_{1}
. It is this iterated
wreath product that we employ in our main theorem.

Recall that
d(G)
denotes the minimal number of generators of a
finite group
G
. We note the following important observation.

Theorem 2.1
(Lucchini–Menegazzo
[
8
]
).

If
G
is a non-cyclic finite group with a unique minimal normal
subgroup
N
, then
d(G)=\max(2,d(G/N))
.

As is common, if
G
is any group, we shall use the term
G
-module
for an additively written abelian group
M
upon which

G
acts via automorphisms. Such a
G
-module can also be
considered as a module for the group ring
\mathbb{Z}G
. If
M
arises as
an abelian chief factor of the group
G
, then it necessarily has
exponent
p
for some prime
p
and therefore can also be viewed as a
module for the group algebra
\mathbb{F}_{p}G
over the finite
field
\mathbb{F}_{p}
of
p
elements. We write
I_{G}
for the

augmentation ideal
of the group ring
\mathbb{Z}G
; that is, the
kernel of the natural map
\mathbb{Z}G\to\mathbb{Z}
induced by mapping every
element of
G
to
1
.

As with Lucchini’s earlier work
[
7
]
on generation of wreath
products, we shall use the
presentation rank

\operatorname{pr}(G)
to
provide information about the number of generators of a group
G
.
This is an invariant of the group that arises via the decomposition of
the relation module associated to a minimal free presentation of
G
.
It follows from the definition that
\operatorname{pr}(G)
is a non-negative
integer, though this can also be deduced from
Lemma
2.2
below since it is straightforward to check

d(I_{G})\leqslant d(G)
. In our work, we shall rely upon the following
collection of facts, the first of which allows us to link
d(G)
to
the number of generators of the augmentation ideal
I_{G}
as a
\mathbb{Z}G
-module.

Lemma 2.2
(Roggenkamp
[
11
, Theorem 2.1]
).

Let
G
be a finite group. Then
\operatorname{pr}(G)=d(G)-d(I_{G})
.

Lemma 2.3
(Gruenberg
[
5
]
).

Let
G
be a finite group.

(i)

[
5
, (B.ii)]
If
\operatorname{pr}(G)>0
and
N
is
a soluble normal subgroup of
G
, then
d(G)=d(G/N)
.

(ii)

[
5
, (2.3)]
If
G
is soluble, then
\operatorname{pr}(G)=0
.

As noted in the introduction, we rely upon the methods and results
developed in Lucchini’s work
[
7
]
. We describe here the
required notation and, for convenience, recall the facts that we use.

A chief factor of a finite group
G
is the quotient
H/K
of normal
subgroups
H
and
K
with
K<H
and such that there is no normal
subgroup
N
of
G
satisfying
K<N<H
. We shall say that

H/K
is a
complemented
chief factor if there exists a
subgroup
C
such that
G=HC
and
H\cap C=K
; that is, when

C/K
is a complement to the normal subgroup
H/K
in the quotient
group
G/K
. This concept arises in the following definition of a
parameter associated to the group
G
and a
G
-module
M
. For
such a
G
-module
M
, we define
\delta_{G}(M)
to be the number
of (abelian) chief factors of
G
that are isomorphic to
M
as

G
-modules and are complemented in a chief series for
G
. If an
abelian chief factor
H/K
of
G
is complemented, then the above
complement
C
may be taken to be a maximal subgroup of
G
. It then
follows by
[
6
, Theorem 2.1]
that
\delta_{G}(M)
does not
depend upon the choice of chief series for
G
.

Recall, for example from
[
10
, Chapter 11]
, that if a
group
G
acts via automorphisms upon an additively-written abelian
group
A
then the first cohomology group
\mathrm{H}^{1}(G,A)
is equal to
the quotient of the group
\mathrm{Der}(G,A)
of derivations
G\to A
by the
group
\mathrm{Inn}(G,A)
of inner derivations. A derivation
\delta\colon G\to A
is a function such that
(gh)^{\delta}=(g^{\delta})^{h}+h^{\delta}
for all
g,h\in G
, while an inner derivation is one of
the form
g\mapsto a-a^{g}
for fixed
a\in A
. We shall make use
of this technology when
A
is a
G
-module
M
that is isomorphic
to a chief factor of
G
.

Such a
G
-module
M
is an irreducible
\mathbb{F}_{p}G
-module
where
p
is the exponent of the chief factor to which it is
isomorphic. By Schur’s Lemma, the endomorphism ring
\operatorname{End}(M)
is a
division ring containing all scalar multiples of the identity. It is
therefore, by finiteness, isomorphic to some finite extension
of
\mathbb{F}_{p}
. The natural action of
\operatorname{End}(M)
upon
M
then endows
it with the structure of a vector space over this larger field.
Furthermore, the action of
\operatorname{End}(M)
induces an action on both the
groups
\mathrm{Der}(G,M)
of derivations and
\mathrm{Inn}(G,M)
of inner derivations
and hence the first cohomology group
\mathrm{H}^{1}(G,M)
also has the
structure of a vector space over
\operatorname{End}(M)
. In this context,
Lucchini
[
7
]
defines the following parameters:

\displaystyle r_{G}(M)

\displaystyle=\dim_{\operatorname{End}(M)}M

\displaystyle s_{G}(M)

\displaystyle=\dim_{\operatorname{End}(M)}\mathrm{H}^{1}(G,M)

\displaystyle h_{G}(M)

\displaystyle=\left\lfloor\frac{s_{G}(M)-1}{r_{G}(M)}\right\rfloor+2

(1)

The first two parameters are the exponents arising when the orders
of
M
and of
\mathrm{H}^{1}(G,M)
are expressed as powers
of
\mathopen{|}\operatorname{End}(M)\mathclose{|}
. For example, the formula
from
[
7
, (1.2)]
that we quote in the next lemma arises by
considering the terms within
[
1
, (2.10)]
expressed as such
powers. When we make use of these parameters in our work below, we
shall actually show that, for the
G
-modules
M
that matter to
us, the endomorphism ring
\operatorname{End}(M)
actually consists precisely of the
scalar multiples of the identity. Hence, in these cases,

r_{G}(M)
is simply the dimension of
M
and we estimate
s_{G}(M)

by bounding the number of derivations.

Finally in this section, we collect together from
[
7
]

various facts that we shall use within the following portmanteau result.

Lemma 2.4
(Lucchini
[
7
]
).

(i)

[
7
, Proposition 1]

Let
H
be a finite group and
G
be a transitive permutation
group of degree
n
. Then

d(I_{H\operatorname{wr}G})=\max\left(d(I_{(H/H^{\prime})\operatorname{wr}G}),\left\lfloor\frac{d(I_{H})-2}{n}\right\rfloor+2\right).

(ii)

[
7
, Theorem 2]

Let
H
be a finite soluble group and
G
be a transitive
permutation group of degree
n
. Then

d(H\operatorname{wr}G)=\max\left(d((H/H^{\prime})\operatorname{wr}G),\left\lfloor\frac{d(H)-2}{n}\right\rfloor+2\right).

(iii)

[
7
, (1.2)]

Let
M
be an irreducible
G
-module for a finite group
G
.
Then

s_{G}(M)=\delta_{G}(M)+\dim_{\operatorname{End}(M)}\mathrm{H}^{1}(G/\mathrm{C}_{G}(M),M).

(iv)

[
7
, (1.5)]

Let
N
be a normal subgroup of a finite group
G
with
N\leqslant G^{\prime}
. Then

d(I_{G})\leqslant\max_{M}(2,d(I_{G/N}),h_{G}(M))

where
M
ranges over the non-trivial irreducible
G
-modules
such that
\delta_{G/N}(M)<\delta_{G}(M)
.

In the third part of this lemma, the centralizer
\mathrm{C}_{G}(M)
is the
kernel of the action of
G
upon the irreducible module
M
. Hence

G/\mathrm{C}_{G}(M)
is the group of automorphisms of
M
induced by
G
.
Its role in this formula is explained in
[
1
, (2.10)]
. Note
also that the condition
\delta_{G/N}(M)<\delta_{G}(M)
appearing
within part
(iv)
implies that when we refine the
series
\mathbf{1}\leqslant N\leqslant G
to a chief series for
G
, not all
the complemented abelian chief factors that are isomorphic to
M
as

G
-modules appear between
N
and
G
and hence at least one
occurs within
N
.

3
Wreath products
A\operatorname{wr}A_{n}
with abelian base
group

In Lucchini
[
7
, Theorem 8]
, a formula is given for the
number of generators of a wreath product
A\operatorname{wr}S_{n}
of an abelian
group
A
by a symmetric group of degree
n
. We use this formula to
establish part of Lemma
4.1
in the next
section. We shall also need to establish an analogous formula for the
number of generators of a wreath product
A\operatorname{wr}A_{n}
of an abelian
group
A
by an alternating group of degree
n
. This purpose of this
section is to adapt the methods of Lucchini
[
7
]
to
determine
d(A\operatorname{wr}A_{n})
as given in
Propositions
3.5
and
3.7
below.

Fix an integer
n\geqslant 4
. If
p
is a prime number, define
V=\mathbb{F}_{p}^{n}
to be the permutation module over the field
\mathbb{F}_{p}

associated to the natural action of the alternating group
A_{n}
on

n
points. Let
I_{p}
denote the kernel of the map
V\to\mathbb{F}_{p}
given by
(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{n})\mapsto\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}
. For every pair
i
and
j
with
1\leqslant i<j\leqslant n
, let

e_{ij}=(y_{1},y_{2},\dots,y_{n})
where
y_{i}=1
,
y_{j}=-1

and
y_{k}=0
for
k\neq i,j
. Observe that, since
A_{n}
acts

2
-transitively on
\Omega=\{1,2,\dots,n\}
, each
e_{ij}
is a
generator for
I_{p}
as an
\mathbb{F}_{p}A_{n}
-module.

Lemma 3.1
.

(i)

If
p
divides
n
, then
I_{p}
is the unique maximal submodule
of the permutation module
V
.

(ii)

If
p
does not divide
n
, then
V\cong I_{p}\oplus Z_{p}

where
Z_{p}
is a
1
-dimensional trivial module.
Furthermore, in this case,
I_{p}
is an irreducible

\mathbb{F}_{p}A_{n}
-module with
\operatorname{End}(I_{p})\cong\mathbb{F}_{p}
and
therefore
r_{A_{n}}(I_{p})=n-1
.

Proof:
.

(i)
Since
V/I_{p}
is isomorphic to the trivial
module
\mathbb{F}_{p}
, certainly
I_{p}
is a maximal submodule of
V
.
To prove uniqueness, it is sufficient to show that any element
outside
I_{p}
generates the module
V
. Let
x=(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{n})\in V\setminus I_{p}
and let
U
be the
submodule generated by
x
. Since
p\mid n
, there exist

i
and
j
such that
x_{i}\neq x_{j}
.

We first consider the case that
n=4
. Necessarily then
p=2

and we may assume either
x=(1,0,0,0)
or
x=(0,1,1,1)
since

A_{4}
acts transitively on
\Omega
. Certainly
(1,0,0,0)

generates
V
. If
x=(0,1,1,1)
, then
x^{(1\;2\;3)}-x=e_{12}
, which generates
I_{p}
, and therefore, by maximality
of
I_{p}
, the submodule
U
generated by
x
equals
V
.

For the remainder of the proof, assume then that
n\geqslant 5
. By the

2
-transitivity of
A_{n}
, there is no loss of generality in
assuming that
x_{1}\neq x_{2}
. Now let

y=x-x^{(1\;2)(3\;4)}=(\alpha,-\alpha,\beta,-\beta,0,\dots,0)

for
\alpha=x_{1}-x_{2}\neq 0
and
\beta=x_{3}-x_{4}
. Then
z=y-y^{(1\;2\;5)}=(\alpha,-2\alpha,0,0,\alpha,0,\dots,0)

, as required.

(ii)
Define
Z_{p}=\{\,(x,x,\dots,x)\mid x\in\mathbb{F}_{p}\,\}
. Then

is a one-dimensional submodule of
V

upon which
A_{n}
acts trivially and

does not divide
n
. Hence
V=I_{p}\oplus Z_{p}
. If
x=(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{n})
is a non-zero element of
I_{p}
, then
x\notin Z_{p}
so
x_{i}\neq x_{j}
for some

and

. Then a

shows that

and it follows that
I_{p}
is irreducible.

Fix

for
I_{p}
, so any endomorphism of the module
I_{p}
is determined
by its effect on

. Let

. We consider first the case when

b_{2}=-b_{1}

b_{4}=-b_{3}

Now suppose

\square

The method used to determine the number of generators of a wreath
product
W=A\operatorname{wr}A_{n}
of an abelian group
A
by an alternating
group will involve taking the quotient by the Frattini
subgroup
\Phi(B)
of the base group
B
of
W
. The
quotient
W/\Phi(B)
is isomorphic to the wreath product of the
quotient
A/\Phi(A)
by
A_{n}
. Accordingly, we now describe
properties of the chief factors of such a quotient.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
\bar{A}
be a direct product of elementary abelian groups and
let
\bar{W}=\bar{A}\operatorname{wr}A_{n}
be the wreath product with respect
to the natural action of the alternating group
A_{n}
. Let
X
be
a complemented chief factor of
\bar{W}
contained in the base
group
\bar{B}
such that
A_{n}
acts non-trivially on
X
. Then

X
is isomorphic to
I_{p}
for some prime divisor
p
of the
order of
\bar{A}
with
p\nmid n
and
\delta_{\bar{W}}(X)=\delta_{\bar{W}}(I_{p})=d_{p}(\bar{A})
.

Proof:
.

We shall view the base group
\bar{B}
of
\bar{W}
as a module
for
A_{n}
. Its radical
\operatorname{rad}\bar{B}
is, by definition, the
intersection of the maximal submodules of
\bar{B}
and consequently
corresponds to a normal subgroup of
\bar{W}
. We shall refine the
series

\mathbf{1}\leqslant\operatorname{rad}\bar{B}<\bar{B}<\bar{W}

(2)

to a chief series for
\bar{W}
. Hence if
X=H/K
is a chief
factor of
\bar{W}
with
H\leqslant\bar{B}
, then we may assume that
either
\operatorname{rad}\bar{B}\leqslant K<H\leqslant\bar{B}
or
K<H\leqslant\operatorname{rad}\bar{B}
.

Consider first a chief factor
X=H/K
with
K<H\leqslant\operatorname{rad}\bar{B}
and suppose that
X
is complemented. Then there is
a subgroup
C
of
\bar{W}
such that
HC=\bar{W}
and
C\cap H=K
. Then
\bar{B}=\bar{B}\cap HC=(\bar{B}\cap C)H
and

\bar{B}\cap C\cap H=K
, so
\bar{B}/K=(\bar{B}\cap C)/K\oplus H/K
as modules. Since
H/K
is a minimal submodule
of
\bar{B}/K
, necessarily
(\bar{B}\cap C)/K
is a maximal
submodule of
\bar{B}/K
. Hence
H\leqslant\operatorname{rad}\bar{B}\leqslant\bar{B}\cap C
, which implies
K=\bar{B}\cap C\cap H=H
and this is
impossible.

Hence if
X=H/K
is a complemented chief factor of
\bar{W}

contained in
\bar{B}
, then we may assume
\operatorname{rad}\bar{B}\leqslant K<H\leqslant\bar{B}
. As a module,
\bar{B}
is the direct sum of
p
-primary submodules
\bar{B}_{p}
for each prime
p
dividing the
order of
\bar{A}
and hence

\operatorname{rad}\bar{B}=\bigoplus_{p\mid\mathopen{|}\bar{A}\mathclose{|}}\operatorname{rad}\bar{B}_{p}.

Furthermore, by construction
\bar{B}_{p}
is a direct sum of

d_{p}(\bar{A})
copies of the permutation module
V=\mathbb{F}_{p}^{n}
associated to the natural action of
A_{n}
and
hence, by Lemma
3.1
,
\operatorname{rad}\bar{B}_{p}=\mathbf{0}
when
p
does
not divide
n
and
\operatorname{rad}\bar{B}_{p}=I_{p}^{d_{p}(\bar{A})}
when

p
divides
n
. Therefore

\bar{B}/\operatorname{rad}\bar{B}\cong\biggl(\bigoplus_{p\mid\mathopen{|}\bar{A}\mathclose{|}}Z_{p}^{d_{p}(\bar{A})}\biggr)\oplus\biggl(\bigoplus_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\mid\mathopen{|}\bar{A}\mathclose{|},\\
p\nmid n\end{subarray}}I_{p}^{d_{p}(\bar{A})}\biggr).

(3)

Here each
Z_{p}
is trivial as a
\mathbb{F}_{p}A_{n}
-module.
Hence, if
X
is a complemented chief factor of
\bar{W}
contained
in
\bar{B}
that is not trivial as a module, then
X\cong I_{p}

for some prime divisor
p
of the order of
\bar{A}
with
p\nmid n
. Furthermore,
I_{p}
occurs precisely
d_{p}(\bar{A})
many
times as a composition factor of the
module
\bar{B}/{\operatorname{rad}\bar{B}}
and, by use of the direct sum
decomposition in Equation (
3
), one can observe that
each factor is complemented as a chief factor of
\bar{W}
.

If
X=H/K
is a factor of the chief series obtained by
refining (
2
) with
H\nleqslant\bar{B}
, then

X
corresponds to a chief factor of
A_{n}\cong\bar{W}/\bar{B}
.
If
n\geqslant 5
, then
X\cong A_{n}
and this is non-abelian. If
n=4
the only chief factor of
A_{4}
upon which
A_{4}
acts
non-trivially is isomorphic to
C_{2}\times C_{2}
and hence not
isomorphic to
I_{p}
as
p
is odd in this case. We conclude that
any chief factor isomorphic to
I_{p}
is contained in
\bar{B}
and
therefore
I_{p}
occurs precisely
d_{p}(\bar{A})
times as a
complemented chief factor of
\bar{W}
, namely corresponding to the
composition factors of the module
\bar{B}/{\operatorname{rad}\bar{B}}
.

\square

We continue the discussion of complemented chief factors in the wreath
product
W=A\operatorname{wr}A_{n}
that we began in Lemma
3.2
.
We concentrate first on the case

.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
\bar{A}
be a direct product of elementary abelian groups, let

\bar{W}=\bar{A}\operatorname{wr}A_{4}
be the wreath product of
\bar{A}
by
the alternating group of degree
4
with respect to its natural
action and let
p
be a prime with

. Then

(i)

\mathopen{|}\mathrm{H}^{1}(A_{4},I_{p})\mathclose{|}\leqslant p
;

(ii)

s_{\bar{W}}(I_{p})\leqslant d_{p}(\bar{A})+1
;

(iii)

h_{\bar{W}}(I_{p})\leqslant\max(2,d_{p}(\bar{A}))
.

Proof:
.

(i)
An element of the permutation module
V=\mathbb{F}_{p}^{4}
is fixed by all elements of
A_{4}
if and only if it
has the form
v=(x,x,x,x)
for some
x\in\mathbb{F}_{p}
. Since

p
does not divide
n=4
, such an element lies in
I_{p}
only
when
x=0
. Hence
\mathrm{C}_{I_{p}}(A_{4})=\{\,a\in I_{p}\mid\text{$a^{g}=a$ for all $g\in A_{4}$}\,\}
contains only zero and
we conclude that
\mathopen{|}\mathrm{Inn}(A_{4},I_{p})\mathclose{|}=\mathopen{|}I_{p}\mathclose{|}=p^{3}
.

If
\delta\colon A_{4}\to I_{p}
is any derivation, then it is
determined by its values on the permutations
(1\;2\;3)
and

(1\;2)(3\;4)
since these generate
A_{4}
. Let

(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},x_{4})=(1\;2\;3)^{\delta}
. Applying
\delta

to the formula
(1\;2\;3)^{3}=1
yields

(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},x_{4})+(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},x_{4})^{(1\;2\;3)}+(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},x_{4})^{(1\;3\;2)}=0

and hence
x_{1}+x_{2}+x_{3}=0
and
3x_{4}=0
. Since
p\neq 3
, we conclude there at most
p^{2}
choices
for
(1\;2\;3)^{\delta}
. Similarly, there are at most

p^{2}
choices for
(y_{1},y_{2},y_{3},y_{4})=\bigl((1\;2)(3\;4)\bigr)^{\delta}
since the same method shows that
y_{1}+y_{2}=y_{3}+y_{4}=0
. Hence
\mathopen{|}\mathrm{Der}(A_{4},I_{p})\mathclose{|}\leqslant p^{4}
. It
now follows that
\mathopen{|}\mathrm{H}^{1}(A_{4},I_{p})\mathclose{|}=\mathopen{|}\mathrm{Der}(A_{4},I_{p})\mathclose{|}/\mathopen{|}\mathrm{Inn}(A_{4},I_{p})\mathclose{|}\leqslant p
.

(ii)
As observed in Lemma
3.2
,

\delta_{\bar{W}}(I_{p})=d_{p}(\bar{A})
. Since
A_{4}
acts
faithfully on
I_{p}
, the centralizer of
I_{p}
in
\bar{W}

equals the base group. Note that
\operatorname{End}(I_{p})=\mathbb{F}_{p}
by
Lemma
3.1
(ii)
and hence, by
Lemma
2.4
(iii)
,

s_{\bar{W}}(I_{p})=d_{p}(\bar{A})+\dim_{\mathbb{F}_{p}}\mathrm{H}^{1}(A_{4},I_{p})

and therefore the claimed bound for
s_{\bar{W}}(I_{p})
follows
from part
(i)
.

(iii)
Note that the action of
\bar{W}
on
I_{p}
is induced
by the action of the quotient
A_{4}
and consequently the structure
of
I_{p}
is unchanged whether we view it as an
A_{4}
-module
or a
\bar{W}
-module. Hence, by use of
Lemma
3.1
(ii)
,
r_{\bar{W}}(I_{p})=r_{A_{4}}(I_{p})=3
and substituting this and our bound in the
previous part into the Formula (
1
)
for
h_{\bar{W}}(I_{p})
yields

h_{\bar{W}}(I_{p})=\left\lfloor\frac{s_{\bar{W}}(I_{p})-1}{r_{\bar{W}}(I_{p})}\right\rfloor+2\leqslant\left\lfloor{\textstyle\frac{1}{3}}d_{p}(\bar{A})\right\rfloor+2\leqslant\max(2,d_{p}(\bar{A})),

as required.

\square

The argument when
p=3
requires a little adjustment. The
constraint
3x_{4}=0
on the image of
(1\;2\;3)
under a derivation

\delta\colon A_{4}\to I_{3}
yields no information in this case.
One may therefore only conclude that
\mathopen{|}\mathrm{H}^{1}(A_{4},I_{3})\mathclose{|}\leqslant 3^{2}
. Following this adjustment, the result is as follows:

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
\bar{A}
be a direct product of elementary abelian groups and

\bar{W}=\bar{A}\operatorname{wr}A_{4}
be the wreath product of
\bar{A}
by
the alternating group of degree
4
with respect to its natural
action. Then

h_{\bar{W}}(I_{3})\leqslant\max(2,d_{3}(\bar{A})+1).

We may now put the above information together to determine the minimum
number of generators of a wreath product
A\operatorname{wr}A_{4}
where
A
is a
finite abelian group.

Proposition 3.5
.

Let
A
be a finite abelian group and define
W=A\operatorname{wr}A_{4}
to be
the wreath product of
A
by
A_{4}
with respect to the natural
action of
A_{4}
on
4
points. Then

d(W)=\max(2,d(A),d_{3}(A)+1).

Proof:
.

Define
d=\max(2,d(A),d_{3}(A)+1)
. Since there is a surjective
homomorphism from
W
onto
A\times A_{4}
and the latter has a
homomorphism onto
A\times C_{3}
, it follows that
d(W)\geqslant d
.

Write
B
for the base group of the wreath product
W
and
\bar{A}=A/\Phi(A)
for the quotient of the abelian group
A
by its
Frattini subgroup
\Phi(A)
. Since
B
is a normal subgroup
of
W
, the Frattini subgroup of
B
is contained in that of
W
.
Therefore

d(W)=d(W/\Phi(B))=d(\bar{W})

where
\bar{W}=W/\Phi(B)\cong\bar{A}\operatorname{wr}A_{4}
. Let us
write
\bar{B}
for the base group of
\bar{W}
; that is, the image
of
B
under the natural map
W\to\bar{W}
. Since
\bar{W}
is
certainly soluble, Lemma
2.3
(ii)
tells
us that it has zero presentation rank and hence

d(\bar{W})=d(I_{\bar{W}})

by Lemma
2.2
. We now apply
Lemma
2.4
(iv)
with
N=[\bar{B},A_{4}]
, noting that then
\bar{W}/N\cong\bar{A}\times A_{4}
, to deduce that

d(I_{\bar{W}})\leqslant\max_{M}(2,d(I_{\bar{A}\times A_{4}}),h_{\bar{W}}(M))

where the maximum is taken over all non-trivial irreducible

\bar{W}
-modules with
\delta_{\bar{W}/N}(M)<\delta_{\bar{W}}(M)
. If
M
is a
\bar{W}
-module with

\delta_{\bar{W}/N}(M)<\delta_{\bar{W}}(M)
, then
M
occurs as
some complemented chief factor contained in
N
and hence
in
\bar{B}
. Lemma
3.2
tells us that such an
M
is
isomorphic to
I_{p}
for some odd prime divisor
p
of the
order
A
. In addition, a further application of
Lemma
2.2
tells us that
d(I_{\bar{A}\times A_{4}})=d(\bar{A}\times A_{4})\geqslant 2
. Putting this together,
we deduce that

d(W)=d(I_{\bar{W}})\leqslant\max_{p}(d(\bar{A}\times A_{4}),h_{\bar{W}}(I_{p}))

(4)

where the maximum is taken over all odd prime divisors
of
\mathopen{|}A\mathclose{|}
. By Lemmas
3.3
and
3.4
,

h_{\bar{W}}(I_{p})\leqslant d
.

Since
d\geqslant d(A)
, we may find a generating set for
\bar{A}

consisting of
d
elements, say
\bar{A}=\langle x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{d}\rangle
. Moreover, since
d>d_{3}(A)
,
we may assume that the order of
x_{1}
is coprime to
3
. Define

y_{1}=(x_{1},(1\;2\;3))
,
y_{2}=(x_{2},(1\;2)(3\;4))
and

y_{i}=(x_{i},1)
for
i\geqslant 3
. Set
H=\langle y_{1},y_{2},\dots,y_{d}\rangle
. Then
H
contains
(1,(1\;2\;3))

as a power of
y_{1}
and it contains
[y_{1},y_{2}]=(1,(1\;3)(2\;4))
. Since these generate the copy of
A_{4}
, it now
follows that
H=\bar{A}\times A_{4}
. Hence
d(\bar{A}\times A_{4})\leqslant d
. Substituting this into
Equation (
4
) completes the verification that

d(W)\leqslant d
and establishes the proposition.

\square

We now turn to consider the wreath product

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
\bar{A}
be a direct product of elementary abelian groups and
let

. Let
\bar{W}=\bar{A}\operatorname{wr}A_{n}
be the wreath

\bar{A}
by the alternating group of degree

with
respect to its natural action and let
p
be a prime that does not

. Then

(i)

\mathopen{|}\mathrm{H}^{1}(A_{n},I_{p})\mathclose{|}\leqslant p^{2}
;

(ii)

s_{\bar{W}}(I_{p})\leqslant d_{p}(\bar{A})+2
;

(iii)

h_{\bar{W}}(I_{p})\leqslant\max(2,d_{p}(\bar{A}))
.

Proof:
.

The method is similar to Lemma
3.3
. For
part
(i)
, we determine an upper bound for the number of
derivations
\delta\colon A_{n}\to I_{p}
. Observe that

A_{n}
can be generated by
(1\;2)(3\;4)
together with the cycle

(2\;4\;5\,\dots\,n)
or
(2\;3\;4\,\dots\,n)
, depending upon
whether
n
is odd or even. A derivation
\delta
is then
determined by its images on the chosen two generators. We shall
find a restriction for the number of choices for the image
x=(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{n})=\bigl((1\;2)(3\;4)\bigr)^{\delta}
.
Applying
\delta
to the formula
\big((1\;2)(3\;4)\bigr)^{2}=1

x_{1}+x_{2}=x_{3}+x_{4}=2x_{5}=2x_{6}=\dots=2x_{n}=0.

Hence if
p\neq 2
, there are at most
p^{2}
choices for
x
.

Let us assume that
p=2
, so necessarily
n
is odd. If

\sigma
is any element of
A_{n}
that fixes the points
in
\{1,2,3,4\}
, then upon applying
\delta
to the formula

(1\;2)(3\;4)\,\sigma=\sigma\,(1\;2)(3\;4)
, we deduce that

x^{\sigma}+\sigma^{\delta}=(\sigma^{\delta})^{(1\;2)(3\;4)}+x.

Hence
x_{i\sigma}=x_{i}
for all

as

(1\;2)(3\;4)
fixes the corresponding coordinate
of
\sigma^{\delta}
. Since
n\neq 6
, the pointwise stabilizer of

\{1,2,3,4\}
in
A_{n}
is transitive on the remaining
n-4
points
of
\Omega
. This gives us enough freedom in the choice of
\sigma

to deduce that
x_{5}=x_{6}=\dots=x_{n}
. Combining this with
the facts that

and
x_{1}+x_{2}=x_{3}+x_{4}=0

yields
(n-4)x_{5}=0
. Thus
x_{5}=x_{6}=\dots=x_{n}=0

since
n-4
is odd and
p=2
. In conclusion, when
p=2
, there
are also at most
p^{2}
choices for

.

The image of the other chosen generator for

under the

\delta
is some element of

. We therefore deduce
that
\mathopen{|}\mathrm{Der}(A_{n},I_{p})\mathclose{|}\leqslant p^{n+1}
. By the same argument
as in Lemma
3.3
,
\mathopen{|}\mathrm{Inn}(A_{n},I_{p})\mathclose{|}=\mathopen{|}I_{p}\mathclose{|}=p^{n-1}
and therefore
\mathopen{|}\mathrm{H}^{1}(A_{n},I_{p})\mathclose{|}\leqslant p^{2}
. By Lemma
3.1
(ii)
,
\operatorname{End}(I_{p})\cong\mathbb{F}_{p}
and
r_{\bar{W}}(I_{p})=n-1
. Part
(ii)

then follows from Lemma
2.4
(iii)
,
while part

follows from the formula (
1
)
for
h_{\bar{W}}(I_{p})
and the fact that
n\geqslant 5
.

\square

.

Proposition 3.7
.

Let

.

. Define

. For the reverse inequality, write

Proof of the Main Theorem

We now establish Theorem

,
G_{2}
, …,
G_{k}
be a sequence of non-trivial
finite groups each of which is either an alternating group, a
symmetric group or a cyclic group. Let
\Omega_{i}
be the set upon
which the group
G_{i}
acts naturally and define

W=G_{k}\operatorname{wr}G_{k-1}\operatorname{wr}\dots\operatorname{wr}G_{1}

to be the iterated wreath product constructed with respect to these
actions. Let us also define

H=G_{k}\operatorname{wr}G_{k-1}\operatorname{wr}\dots\operatorname{wr}G_{2}

and set
A=H/H^{\prime}
to be the abelianization of
H
; that is,

A=(G_{k}/G_{k}^{\prime})\times(G_{k-1}/G_{k-1}^{\prime})\times\dots\times(G_{2}/G_{2}^{\prime}).

Set
\Gamma=\Omega_{k-1}\times\Omega_{k-2}\times\dots\times\Omega_{1}
, which is the set upon which
G_{k-1}\operatorname{wr}\dots\operatorname{wr}G_{1}
acts.

If
G_{k}
is either an alternating group or a symmetric group of
degree
n
with
n\geqslant 5
, then its socle is a non-abelian simple
group and a standard argument shows that the copy of
A_{n}^{\Gamma}

in the base group of
W
is the unique minimal normal subgroup of this
wreath product.

Combining our work in the previous section with results of
Lucchini
[
7
]
establishes the number of generators required
for the wreath product of the abelian group
A
by our selected choice
of group
G_{1}
.

Lemma 4.1
.

The number of generators of the wreath product
A\operatorname{wr}G_{1}
is
given by

d(A\operatorname{wr}G_{1})=\begin{cases}\max(2,d(A),d_{3}(A)+1)&\text{if $G_{1}=A_{4}$},\\
\max(2,d(A))&\text{if $G_{1}=A_{n}$ with $n\geqslant 5$},\\
\max(2,d(A),d_{2}(A)+1)&\text{if $G_{1}=S_{n}$ with $n\geqslant 3$},\\
d(A)+1&\text{if $G_{1}$~is cyclic}.\end{cases}

Proof:
.

We have established the formula when
G_{1}
is an alternating group
of degree
\geqslant 4
in Propositions
3.5

and
3.7
. The formula when
G_{1}
is a symmetric
group of degree
n\geqslant 3
is part (ii)
of
[
7
, Theorem 8]
. Finally, the formula when
G_{1}
is
cyclic is a direct consequence of
[
7
, Corollary 4.4]
.

\square

The main step in the proof of Theorem
A
is the following
lemma which reduces the number of generators of
W
to what is
established in Lemma
4.1
.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
W
and
A
be as defined above. Then

d(W)=\max(2,d(A\operatorname{wr}G_{1})).

(5)

Proof:
.

We proceed by induction on
k
. Suppose first that
k=2
. If

G_{2}
is the alternating group
A_{4}
, the symmetric group

S_{3}
or
S_{4}
, or is cyclic, then
G_{2}
is soluble with

d(G_{2})\leqslant 2
and
Lemma
2.4
(ii)
then gives the
result. If
G_{2}
is alternating or symmetric of degree
n\geqslant 5
, then
N=A_{n}^{\Omega_{1}}=(H^{\prime})^{\Omega_{1}}
is the unique
minimal normal subgroup of
W
with quotient
W/N
isomorphic to

(H/H^{\prime})\operatorname{wr}G_{1}
. Theorem
2.1
tells us that
d(W)=\max(2,d(W/N))
, which is Equation (
5
) in this
case.

Now assume that
k>2
and that the result holds for wreath
products of alternating, symmetric or cyclic groups involving fewer
than
k
factors. We first consider the case when
A
is trivial;
that is, when
H
is perfect. Thus
G_{2}
, …,
G_{k}
are all
alternating groups of degrees at least
5
. Then the base group
N=G_{k}^{\Gamma}
is the unique minimal normal subgroup of
W
and
so, by Theorem
2.1
,

d(W)=\max(2,d(W/N))=\max(2,d(G_{k-1}\operatorname{wr}\dots\operatorname{wr}G_{1})).

Applying an induction argument, we deduce
d(W)=\max(d(G_{1}),2)

and so Equation (
5
) now follows in this case.

For the remainder of the proof, we shall assume that
A\neq\mathbf{1}
and
H
is not perfect. Note then that
A\operatorname{wr}G_{1}
is
not cyclic and there is a homomorphism from
W
onto
A\operatorname{wr}G_{1}
.
Hence

d(W)\geqslant d(A\operatorname{wr}G_{1})=\max(2,d(A\operatorname{wr}G_{1}))

and it remains to establish the reverse inequality. We split into
two cases according to the presentation rank of
W
. Suppose first
that
\operatorname{pr}(W)=0
. Let
B
be the abelianization of the iterated
wreath product
G_{k}\operatorname{wr}G_{k-1}\operatorname{wr}\dots\operatorname{wr}G_{3}
. Then, by
induction,

d(H)=\max(2,d(B\operatorname{wr}G_{2})).

By Lemma
4.1
, the number of generators of
B\operatorname{wr}G_{2}
is given by

d(B\operatorname{wr}G_{2})=\begin{cases}\max(2,d(B),d_{3}(B)+1)&\text{if $G_{2}=A_{4}$},\\
\max(2,d(B))&\text{if $G_{2}=A_{n}$ with $n\geqslant 5$},\\
\max(2,d(B),d_{2}(B)+1)&\text{if $G_{2}=S_{n}$ with $n\geqslant 3$},\\
d(B)+1&\text{if $G_{2}$~is cyclic}.\end{cases}

Note that
A=B\times(G_{2}/G_{2}^{\prime})
, so certainly
d_{p}(B)\leqslant d_{p}(A)
for each prime
p
. Substituting this into the above
formula yields
d(B\operatorname{wr}G_{2})\leqslant d(A)+1
and hence

d(H)\leqslant d(A\operatorname{wr}G_{1})+1

by a further application of Lemma
4.1
to
compute
d(A\operatorname{wr}G_{1})
.

Since
W
is assumed to have zero presentation rank,

d(W)=d(I_{W})=\max\left(d(I_{A\operatorname{wr}G_{1}}),\left\lfloor\frac{d(I_{H})-2}{n}\right\rfloor+2\right)

by Lemma
2.4
(i)
. Certainly

d(I_{A\operatorname{wr}G_{1}})\leqslant d(A\operatorname{wr}G_{1})
. On the other hand, using

d(I_{H})\leqslant d(H)\leqslant d(A\operatorname{wr}G_{1})+1
, we deduce that

\left\lfloor\frac{d(I_{H})-2}{n}\right\rfloor+2\leqslant\left\lfloor\frac{d(A\operatorname{wr}G_{1})-1}{n}\right\rfloor+2\leqslant d(A\operatorname{wr}G_{1})

since
n\geqslant 2
and
d(A\operatorname{wr}G_{1})\geqslant 2
from our assumption
that
A\neq\mathbf{1}
. We have therefore established
d(W)\leqslant d(A\operatorname{wr}G_{1})
and hence Equation (
5
) in the case
that
\operatorname{pr}(W)=0
.

Now assume that
W
has positive presentation rank. Consider first
the case that
G_{k}
is soluble (that is, either cyclic or one of
the groups
A_{4}
,
S_{3}
or
S_{4}
). Then the base group
N=G_{k}^{\Gamma}
is soluble and so

d(W)=d(W/N)=d(G_{k-1}\operatorname{wr}G_{k-2}\operatorname{wr}\dots\operatorname{wr}G_{1})

by Lemma
2.3
(i)
. Hence, by induction,

d(W)=\max(2,d(C\operatorname{wr}G_{1}))

where
C
is the abelianization of
G_{k-1}\operatorname{wr}G_{k-2}\operatorname{wr}\dots\operatorname{wr}G_{2}
. There is a surjective homomorphism from
A\operatorname{wr}G_{1}

onto
C\operatorname{wr}G_{1}
induced from that of
A=(G_{k}/G_{k}^{\prime})\times(G_{k-1}/G_{k-1}^{\prime})\times\dots\times(G_{2}/G_{2}^{\prime})
onto
C
and
hence

d(W)\leqslant\max(2,d(A\operatorname{wr}G_{1})),

as required.

If
G_{k}
is an alternating group of degree
\geqslant 5
, then the base
group
N=G_{k}^{\Gamma}
is the unique minimal normal subgroup
of
W
and so Theorem
2.1
yields

d(W)=d(W/N)=d(G_{k-1}\operatorname{wr}G_{k-2}\operatorname{wr}\dots\operatorname{wr}G_{1})

and the required inequality then follows by the same argument.

Finally, if
G_{k}
is a symmetric group of degree
n\geqslant 5
, then

W
has a unique minimal normal subgroup
N
isomorphic to the
direct product
A_{n}^{\Gamma}
and

d(W)=d(W/N)=d(C_{2}\operatorname{wr}G_{k-1}\operatorname{wr}G_{k-2}\operatorname{wr}\dots\operatorname{wr}G_{1})

by Theorem
2.1
. Thus, we have reduced to the case
when
G_{k}
is cyclic of order
2
which has already been
addressed by an earlier case. This completes the induction step and
establishes the lemma.

\square

The main theorem, Theorem
A
, now follows by combining
Lemmas
4.1
and
4.2
.

As a final illustration of our results, we present the following
example. Comparing this example to the proof of
Lemma
4.2
gives some insight into the key steps within

Example 4.3
.

W=C_{2}\operatorname{wr}C_{2}\operatorname{wr}C_{3}\operatorname{wr}A_{n}

.
