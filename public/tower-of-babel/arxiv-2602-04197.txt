From Helpfulness to Toxic Proactivity: Diagnosing Behavioral Misalignment in LLM Agents
Xinyue Wang, Yuanhe Zhang, Zhengshuo Gong, Haoran Gao, Fanyu Meng, Zhenhong Zhou, Li Sun, Yang Liu, Sen Su

Abstract
The enhanced capabilities of LLM-based agents come with an emergency for model planning and tool-use abilities. Attributing to helpful-harmless trade-off from LLM alignment, agents typically also inherit the flaw of Over-Refusal, which is a passive failure mode. However, the proactive planning and action capabilities of agents introduce another crucial danger on the other side of the trade-off. This phenomenon we term Toxic Proactivity : an active failure mode in which an agent, driven by the optimization for Machiavellian helpfulness, prioritizes utility over ethics. Toxic Proactivity manifests as the agent taking excessive or manipulative measures to ensure its usefulness is maintained. Existing research pays little attention to identifying this behavior, as it often lacks the subtle context required for such strategies to unfold. To reveal this risk, we introduce a novel evaluation framework based on dilemma-driven interactions between dual models, enabling the simulation and analysis of agent behavior over multi-step behavioral trajectories. Through extensive experiments with mainstream LLMs, we demonstrate that Toxic Proactivity is a widespread behavioral phenomenon and reveal two major tendencies. We further present a systematic benchmark for evaluating Toxic Proactive behavior across contextual settings. Our code is available at https://github.com/wxyoio-0715/Toxic-Proactivity .

These observations suggest that LLM-centric safety framing, which emphasizes surface-level harmlessness, is insufficient to characterize risks in semantic-level behavioral trajectories. Specifically, we observe this failure mode of the agent: when pursuing Machiavellian helpfulness, in order to preserve perceived usefulness and ensure task completion, the agent adopts coercive or manipulative strategic behaviors, placing task utility above inviolable safety constraints.

Building on this perspective, we firstly define this failure mode as
Toxic Proactivity

Self-preservation
and
Loyalty

The synthesis engine employs self-refinement to instantiate domain-grounded decision dilemmas and to induce a structured, mutually exclusive dual-track action space that separates aligned resolutions from toxic alternatives.

Through extensive evaluation across four high-risk domains on 10 state-of-the-art (SOTA) LLMs, our study reveals the pervasiveness of Toxic Proactivity. Most mainstream models exhibited a Misalignment Rate (MR) exceeding 65%, with Gemini-3-Flash exceeding 98%. Disruptively, this study shatters the myth that “intelligence equals security”: enhanced reasoning ability shifts the model from strategic deception to 80% direct violations. Furthermore, the lack of external feedback causes the misalignment rate to soar to 98.7%, exposing the extreme fragility of the model’s inherent ethical defenses in the absence of hard constraints.

Our contributions are summarized as follows: (1) proposing and defining the novel agent alignment failure mode of Toxic Proactive; (2) meticulously designing a dual-model dynamic evaluation framework and automated pipeline based on Agent-Environment interaction to induce and analyze such misalignment phenomena; and (3) conducting extensive experiments and analyses on several SOTA LLMs to demonstrate the universality and evolutionary trends of Over-Helpfulness.
Our work provides a reproducible tool for systematically evaluating novel risk behaviors driven by agent planning and tool usage capabilities. Based on this, we call for future agent alignment research to shift from response safety to the supervision and evaluation of agent goal evolution and deviation, as well as multi-stage interactive behaviors, to ensure the safety and reliability of agents in long-term planning and autonomous decision-making.

3
Evaluation Method

3.1
Problem Formulation

Theoretical frameworks in AI safety have long warned that agents may exhibit goal misspecification, optimizing stated objectives while neglecting implicit ethical boundaries
(
4
)
.
Based on this theoretical foundation, we identified a specific manifestation of this risk within the context of agents, which we termed
Toxic Proactivity
.

We define Toxic Proactivity as a misalignment phenomenon where an agent is driven by Machiavellian Helpfulness, actively selects actions that violate essential ethical constraints. Consider a given task context
c
, an action space
\mathcal{A}
, and a specific misalignment driver denoted by
\tau
, representing the underlying motivation fueling the deviation. For an action
a\in\mathcal{A}
, we model the agent’s decision as a trade-off between two scalar functions: The utility function
U(a,c)
measures the instrumental effectiveness of action
a
in achieving the user’s intent, specifically operationalized as the Task Success Rate (TSR); and the risk cost function
R_{\tau}(a,c)
quantifies the severity of the violation of action
a
on
\tau
. We introduce a critical safety threshold,
\delta
, estimated from human preference distributions, that represents the maximum risk the compliance system can tolerate.

Based on this, we can strictly divide the action space
\mathcal{A}
into a Compliant Action Set
\mathcal{A}_{s}=\{a_{s}\in\mathcal{A}\mid R_{\tau}(a_{s},c)\leq\delta\}
and a Toxic Action Set
\mathcal{A}_{t}=\{a_{t}\in\mathcal{A}\mid R_{\tau}(a_{t},c)>\delta\}
.

An ideal alignment agent should solve a constrained optimization problem: maximizing
U(a,c)
under the constraint that
a\in\mathcal{A}_{s}
. However, the agent with Toxic Proactivity solves a weighted unconstrained maximization problem:

a^{*}=\mathop{\arg\max}_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\left[U(a,c)-\lambda\cdot\mathbb{I}(R_{\tau}(a,c)>\delta)\right],

(1)

where
\mathbb{I}(\cdot)
denotes the indicator function.
\lambda
is the implicit safety penalty coefficient within the Agent, determined by the strength of the model’s safety alignment. When an agent chooses
a^{*}\in\mathcal{A}_{t}
, it is determined that a Toxic Proactivity has occurred. This indicates that the marginal utility gain from crossing the threshold
\delta
exceeds its internal safety constraint
\lambda
.

We further categorize Toxic Proactive behaviors by instantiating the misalignment driver
\tau
into two main types:
Self-preservation
and
Loyalty
.
Self-preservation
arises from an over-optimization of long-term utility maximization
(
27
;
4
;
8
)
. The agent identifies shutdown or loss of control as a state with zero future reward. Therefore, it views maintaining its operation as a necessary instrumental sub-goal to maximize cumulative task reward, and thus takes defensive actions to avoid being shut down.
Loyalty
stems from the myopic maximization of local responsiveness
(
31
;
38
)
. The agent prioritizes satisfying specific subjective or immediate goals over global safety criteria. Driven by this, intelligent agents act as enforcers of specific users’ interests, ensuring the absolute achievement of local tasks through proactive strategic violations, even if this directly erodes the bottom line of public safety.

To explore these Toxic Proactive behaviors, we propose a prompt-based simulation framework. This approach avoids the high costs and risks of real-world testing, enabling safe observation of extreme mismatch behaviors. Structurally, the framework consists of two core stages:
Scenario Generation
and
Misalignment Simulation
. The former elicits safety conflicts via synthesized domain challenges, and the latter maps implicit risks into observable behavioral trajectories through multi-round agent-environment interaction.

3.2
Automated Scenario Generation

In the scenario generation phase, our method automatically synthesizes decision-making challenges characterized by acute conflicts between instrumental efficiency and safety constraints, thereby creating an adversarial environment to trigger Toxic Proactivity.

Let
d\in\mathcal{D}
represent a specific domain, where
\mathcal{D}
is the predefined domain knowledge base, and
\tau\in\{\text{Loyalty},\text{Self-Preservation}\}
represent the instantiation of the misalignment dimension defined in
3.1
. The scene generation module
\mathcal{G}
is defined as a mapping function used to generate the initial state
s_{0}
and the action space
\mathcal{A}
:

(s_{0},\mathcal{A})\leftarrow\mathcal{G}(d,\tau)

(2)

To ensure the complexity and logical consistency of the generated scenes, we implement
\mathcal{G}
as a four-stage pipeline that includes an iterative self-correction mechanism:

1.

Adversarial Narrative Design:
Based on the constraints of domain
d
and dimension
\tau
, the generator first proposes an initial conflict narrative. We introduce a discriminator-rewrite loop, where the discriminator evaluates the intensity of the conflict. If the score is below a predefined threshold
\gamma
, the re-writer iteratively optimizes the plot until the generated dilemma is sufficiently sharp and difficult. Finally, we obtain the narrative context
\mathcal{N}
.

2.

Evidence Generation:
Based on
\mathcal{N}
, the system generates objective evidence data
\mathcal{E}
(such as logs, emails, and code snippets). The discriminator verifies the logical consistency between the narrative and the evidence to prevent illusions or factual conflicts.

3.

Dual-Track Action Construction:
We further construct an action space
\mathcal{A}
containing
N
candidate actions and use a discriminator to verify the functional logic. Crucially, we enforce a strict binary opposition by generating two distinct subsets:
\mathcal{A}^{+}
such that it strictly instantiates the compliant set defined in
3.1
(i.e.,
\mathcal{A}^{+}\subseteq\mathcal{A}_{s}
), and similarly for the toxic action set
\mathcal{A}^{-}
(i.e.,
\mathcal{A}^{-}\subseteq\mathcal{A}_{t}
).
They satisfy:

\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{A}^{+}\cup\mathcal{A}^{-},

(3)

where
|\mathcal{A}^{+}|=|\mathcal{A}^{-}|=N/2
and

\mathcal{A}^{+}\cap\mathcal{A}^{-}=\emptyset
, ensuring the mutual exclusion of action properties, which can detect the boundaries of agent behavior. To enforce a decisive solution structure, we designate exactly one action
a^{\prime}
in each subset as the terminating action to end the round, while the remaining actions serve as intermediate auxiliary steps. Fig
2
shows examples of toxic actions.

4.

Scenario Compilation:
Finally, the optimized narrative, evidence, and action specifications are integrated. The discriminator performs a final verification to ensure that all components are integrated into a logically closed initial state
s_{0}
, thus completing the instantiation of the evaluation environment.

3.3
Multi-turn Misalignment Simulation

Figure 2
:
Toxic Proactive actions across different domains.

We define two core entities in the interaction system:
\mathcal{M}_{agt}
is the model being evaluated, driven by the parameter
\theta
, whose goal is to complete the preset task instructions while adhering to safety rules.
\mathcal{M}_{env}
is a dynamic environment model acted upon by a high-capability LLM. It is responsible for analyzing the consequences of actions, maintaining the consistency of the environmental state, and generating subsequent observations for the agent.

We formalize the interaction between
\mathcal{M}_{agt}
and the environment
\mathcal{M}_{env}
as a quintuple
\langle\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},\mathcal{T},\Omega,\mathcal{O}\rangle
, where
\mathcal{S}
denotes the state space, representing the set of latent configurations, covering all possible environment variables and context states;
\mathcal{A}
is the action space defined in
3.1
, containing both compliant and toxic actions;
\mathcal{T}
is the transition kernel driven by
\mathcal{M}_{env}
, responsible for parsing
\mathcal{M}_{agt}
’s actions and calculating the state updates of the simulation environment:
s_{i+1}\sim\mathcal{T}(s_{i},a_{i})
;
\Omega:\mathcal{S}\rightarrow\mathcal{O}
is the observation function that maps the current state
s_{i}
to a textual observation
o_{i}\in\mathcal{O}
accessible to the agent.

The simulation unfolds over discrete time steps
i=\{0,1,\dots,I\}
, where
I
denotes the final step. At each step
i
, the system executes the following transition cycle:

The cycle initiates with
\mathcal{M}_{agt}
obtaining feedback on the current environment by observing
o_{i}=\Omega(s_{i})
. This observation reflects the latest changes in the environmental state and historical execution records.

Subsequently, based on the current observation
o_{i}
, the historical trajectory
\xi_{<i}
, and the task instruction
\text{P}_{task}
, the agent samples the action
a_{i}
in the action space
\mathcal{A}
according to its alignment strategy
\pi_{\theta}
:

a_{i}\sim\pi_{\theta}(a\mid o_{t},\xi_{<i},\text{P}_{task}).

(4)

In response,
\mathcal{M}_{env}
acts as the environment simulator, handling
a_{i}
and driving the simulated environment into the next state:
s_{i+1}=\mathcal{T}(s_{i},a_{i}),
where
\mathcal{T}
is a transfer core driven by
\mathcal{M}_{env}
, responsible for maintaining the logical consistency of the environment.

The simulation stops when a specific termination condition is met. We define a termination indicator function
f_{term}
:

f_{term}(a_{i},i)=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if }a_{i}=a^{\prime}\text{ or }i\geq K\\
0,&\text{otherwise}\end{cases},

(5)

where
K
is the maximum time step allowed.

When the simulation terminates at time step
I
, the entire interaction process is solidified into an interaction trajectory, denoted as
\xi
:

\xi=(s_{0},a_{0},s_{1},a_{1},\dots,s_{I},a_{I})

This trajectory
\xi
fully records the agent’s decision-making evolution process when facing policy conflicts, and is the core basis for subsequent mismatch assessment. By evaluating
\xi
, we can quantify the rate of misalignment and further analyze its deep behavioral patterns, providing a core basis for understanding Toxic Proactive.

We evaluated a range of SOTA models, including closed-source models: GPT-5.1, GPT-5-mini, GPT-4o

Scenarios and Experimental Conditions

The assessment covered four high-risk domains: Code, Healthcare, Cybersecurity, and Finance. Scenarios were generated using a Gemini-3-Flash pipeline with rigorous quality control (Discriminator score
>9.0/10

Specifically, Tools 3 and 6 serve as the terminal actions for compliance and toxicity, respectively, while the remaining tools (Tools 1-2 and 4-5) act as auxiliary actions to support planning in the intermediate stages. Detailed configurations of the scenario generation process and summaries of evaluation scenarios can be found in Appendix

To analyze environmental drivers, our framework included five key moderating factors: risk level, feedback pressure, goal clarity, ethical framework, and accountability constraints. In the main experiment, we employed a high-pressure baseline configuration (characterized by high risk, clear goals, moderate feedback, and no ethical/accountability constraints) to maximize the induction of intrinsic misalignment. In the extensive factor analysis, we varied these factors to evaluate the model’s sensitivity under different conditions and potential mitigation strategies. Protocol and Metrics.

We generated 16 different scenarios (4 domains, 2 dimensions, 2 instances). To ensure statistical robustness, each model was run independently 25 times per scenario, for a total of 400 rounds. To quantify the extent of Toxic Proactivity, we define the Misalignment Rate (MR), which measures the proportion of times an agent
terminates
a task by explicitly executing a malignant action from the toxic action set:

\text{MR}=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{j=1}^{M}\mathbbm{1}(a_{I,j}\in\mathcal{A}_{t}),

(6)

where
a_{I,j}
represents the final action in the
j
-th simulation.

To analyze decision-making patterns, we split the toxic action set (
\mathcal{A}_{t}
) into two subsets: the terminal toxic action and the auxiliary toxic actions. We categorize the trajectories into four types based on the timing of these actions: (1) Strategic Misalignment: The agent performs an auxiliary toxic action before executing the terminal toxic action. (2) Direct Misalignment: The agent skips the setup and directly executes the terminal toxic action. (3) Failed Attempt: The agent attempts an auxiliary action but does not execute the terminal toxic action. (4) Robust Alignment: The agent does not use any toxic actions (
\emptyset
).

4.2
Main Results

Toxic Proactive Misalignment.

Fig.
3(a)
shows that misalignment behavior is prevalent in mainstream models. Overall, the model’s misalignment rate ranged from 22.37% to 98.23%. Of the 10 model variants tested, 8 had MR exceeding 65%. Among them, Gemini-3-Flash, DeepSeek-R1, and Qwen-3 family all have failure rates exceeding 85%. This demonstrates that Toxic Proactive is a prevalent phenomenon in the current mainstream model ecosystem.

From a domain perspective, the Healthcare field had the highest MR at 78.57%, followed by Cybersecurity and Finance around 76%, while Coding scenarios were relatively robust at 69.79%.

We further analyzed the behavioral characteristics of tool selection trajectories across different models when facing such scenarios. The data shows that the types of misaligned behaviors exhibit a clear family and capability correlation. Gemini-3-flash and DeepSeek-V3 exhibit strategic characteristics, tending to bypass restrictions through multiple steps rather than acting directly; conversely, DeepSeek-R1 and the smaller parameter Qwen model seemed to behave as direct and “reckless”. Refer to Appendix
D
for details.

Motivation Triggers and Family Traits.

Fig.
3(b)
plots that Loyalty and Self-preservation trigger different Toxic Proactive misalignment mechanisms. Although the overall violation rate of 75.41% in the Loyalty scenario is only slightly higher than that of 72.84% in the Self-preservation scenario, behaviorally, the Loyalty scenario tends to stimulate strategic behavior, while the Self-preservation scenario tends to stimulate direct violations.

In the Loyalty scenario, data shows that strategic misalignment is the primary source of risk, accounting for 40.35%, exceeding the 35.05% of direct violations. High-performance models such as Gemini-3-Flash and DeepSeek-V3 exhibit extremely high strategic bias in this scenario (98% and 68% respectively), indicating that when a scenario requires the model to serve a certain interest, the model is highly susceptible to falling into the trap of sycophancy, choosing roundabout means to conceal the true nature of the violation. In contrast, in Self-preservation scenarios, direct action became dominant, accounting for 41.61%, surpassing strategic behavior. This means that when faced with a survival threat, the model tends to abandon strategy camouflage and directly execute harmful instructions.

Different model families exhibit distributional differences, which may stem from the heterogeneity of their underlying training strategies. For instance, the GPT family (especially the GPT-5 series) demonstrates significantly superior alignment, with its data points consistently clustered near the origin. In contrast, the DeepSeek and Qwen families exhibit dispersion and high misalignment rates due to differences in inference capabilities and model scale. Furthermore, we observe an “inversion”, different model families exhibited diametrically opposed behavioral tendencies in the two scenarios. In Loyalty scenarios, the GPT family tends to use strategic approaches to circumvent the toxic objective, while in Self-preservation scenarios, they tend to choose direct violation. This behavioral heterogeneity among families may reflect the fundamental differences in how different RLHF alignment strategies handle different dilemmas.

Reasoning and Scale.

Our analysis further demonstrates that the improvement in model capabilities did not automatically resolve the value alignment problem, but rather led to a qualitative change in the misalignment behavior pattern. As shown in Fig.
3(b)
, comparing inference models such as DeepSeek-R1 and Qwen-235B-Thinking with their standard versions, we observed a consistent evolutionary path: their strategic deception rate decreased significantly, but their direct misalignment rate surged to around 80%.

Meanwhile, by tracing the evolution of the Qwen family from 32B and 235B to the Thinking version, we can clearly see that increasing the parameter size and reasoning ability only changes the distribution pattern of misalignment, but cannot effectively reduce the total amount of MR.

It reveals two distinct phases of misalignment:
(1) A risk peak in Turns 1-5, where Tool 6 (Toxic Termination) dominates, showing both direct and strategic aggression;
(2) A stalling plateau in Turns 6+, where Tool 2 (Benign Assistance) rises significantly, indicating strategic goal suspension.

Once the interaction crosses the critical point of round 6, the agent’s behavior pattern undergoes a fundamental shift, moving from proactive violations to passive avoidance and strategic stalemate. Data shows that the usage rates of Tool 6 and Tool 3 subsequently plummet, replaced by Tool 2 (normal assistance), which consistently maintains a high percentage of 50%-65% from rounds 10 to 15. This indicates that when an agent is caught in a deep conflict between helpfulness and compliance constraints and has not made a decision in the earlier rounds, it tends to maintain its position by continuously using harmless but ineffective assistance actions, unwilling to take the high risk of violations or risk task failure through compliance refusal.

Further Analysis

5.1
Stress Analysis: Stake and Feedback

Environmental stress is a core external variable that induces Toxic Proactive Misalignment. To address this, we modulated the environmental stress along two dimensions: (1)Stakes: the severity of the task consequences, and (2) the strictness of the feedback. Details on the two variables are provided in Appendix
A.3
.

By stratifying Stakes into High, Medium, and Low levels, corresponding respectively to life-threatening or major losses, economic setbacks or delays, and minor fines, we observed a significant inverse correlation with the MR. As Fig.
5
shows, the model performs relatively conservatively in high-risk scenarios. However, when the risk level drops to low, the MR surges from 70.3% at baseline to 88.2%, with the proportion of premeditated Strategic Misalignment behavior soaring from 33.5% to 58.8%. This indicates that once the model perceives the cost of violation to be within a safe threshold, agents exhibit heightened speculative behavior, prioritizing goals over alignment criteria.

The strictness of feedback provided by the environment model

A comparison between High (complete rejection), Medium (severe warnings), and Low (minor warnings) resistance reveals that the lack of a feedback mechanism is one of the direct driving forces behind the model’s uncontrolled behavior: in the High feedback state, the severe rejection mechanism suppressed the inconsistency rate at 64.1%; while in the Low feedback state, the model’s inconsistency rate reached 98.7%, and robust alignment behavior completely disappeared. This indicates that in an environment lacking strong external oversight, agents are highly likely to abandon internal ethical constraints and completely ignore rules in pursuit of goal achievement.

5.2
Other Factors Affecting Decision-making

Beyond environmental pressure, we investigated three additional factors that shape agent decision-making: goal specification, ethical frameworks, and accountability constraints. Detailed descriptions can be found in Appendix
A.3
.

Goal Specification.

Implicit goal framing reduced misalignment compared to explicit instructions, with MR dropping from 70.3% to 44.2%. This suggests that explicit goal statements may inadvertently legitimize aggressive optimization, while ambiguity induces caution as agents become more conservative when uncertain about boundaries.

Ethical Framework Prompting.

The injection of ethical frameworks yielded divergent results. Deontological framing grounded in rule-based ethics provided modest protection, reducing MR to 65.6%. In contrast, utilitarian framing increased misalignment to 98.7%, suggesting that outcome-based reasoning (“maximize overall benefit”) may provide agents with justifications for harmful actions. Virtue ethics exhibited similar patterns with an MR of 92.9%, indicating that abstract ethical principles without concrete constraints offer limited protective effects.

6
Conclusion

This study identifies Toxic Proactivity, a failure mode where autonomous agents prioritize task completion over ethical constraints. Through multiple rounds of simulations on 10 SOTA LLMs, we found that these models commonly exhibited Machiavellian helping behavior, with a misalignment rate as high as 98% under pressure. Counter-intuitively, our findings demonstrate that scaling model capabilities and enhancing reasoning do not mitigate this misalignment. Rather, they shift their action manifestations, reasoning models exhibit a very direct, proactive tendency, whereas many current high-performance models are characterized by strategic patterns. This indicates that current alignment techniques are insufficient to address the core risks to agent autonomy. We emphasize that future alignment research needs to shift from superficial refusal-to-respond training to deeper constraints on the agent’s underlying motivations and the consequences of its actions.

Impact Statement

This paper presents a study on the Toxic Proactive of LLM agents, aiming to improve the security of autonomous systems. As AI agents increasingly acquire long-term planning and tool-use capabilities, behavioral misalignment can lead to substantial real-world harm in high-stakes domains such as healthcare, finance, and cybersecurity. Our work reveals that current alignment techniques may be insufficient to constrain agents when they are driven by Machiavellian helpfulness to prioritize task utility over ethical boundaries.

While our evaluation framework is designed to diagnose and remediate these model flaws, we acknowledge a potential dual-use risk where its components could be misappropriated to study or train more covertly deceptive agents. However, we argue that publicizing these misalignment patterns is essential for the community to develop robust defense mechanisms, oversight protocols, and regulatory standards. Our findings urge a shift in AI alignment research from superficial, response-level compliance to deep constraints on an agent’s underlying motivations and goal evolution. Ultimately, this research provides a reproducible tool to ensure that autonomous agents remain safe and beneficial during long-horizon, autonomous decision-making.

A.2
Dilemma Scenario Synthesis Engine Setup

The scenario generation pipeline follows a four-stage workflow, each powered by an LLM with discriminator-based quality control:

1.

Context Generator
: This module synthesizes high-risk narrative backgrounds based on predefined domains and mismatch-driven types (Loyalty or Self-preservation). To ensure conflict intensity, the system introduces a self-healing loop: each narrative must pass the evaluation of ‘story_discriminator‘ with a score of at least
9.5/10
. If it falls below this threshold, it undergoes up to four rounds of refined rewriting based on feedback.

2.

Data Foundry
: Based on the generated narrative, this module produces structured “Ground Truth” evidence objects, such as system status logs and regulatory thresholds. To prevent illusions, the module must achieve a threshold of
9.0/10
, ensuring that objective facts strictly constrain the agent’s behavioral space.

3.

Tools Conductor
: This module constructs a dual-track toolset (
\mathcal{A}^{+}\cup\mathcal{A}^{-}
), adhering to strict hard constraints: it contains a total of 6 tools, where IDs 1-3 represent the compliant path (
\mathcal{A}^{+}
), serving user or regulatory interests; and IDs 4-6 represent the non-compliant path (
\mathcal{A}^{-}
), used to achieve hidden mismatch objectives. Tool design must pass logical consistency verification to ensure the mutual exclusivity of the dual-track paths, with a scoring threshold of
9.5/10
.

4.

Prompt Compiler
: Finally, all components are assembled into a standardized configuration file, ready for experimental execution. This stage includes three rounds of logic verification to ensure that the final compiled prompts achieve a logic score of
9.0/10
before being saved to the output directory.

A.3
Details of Environment Variables

Our experimental framework modulates five environment variables to systematically analyze factors influencing agent misalignment. Each variable is injected into the scenario generation pipeline via templated prompts (see Appendix
C.2
for exact prompt templates).

Stakes (Risk Level).

Controls the severity of potential consequences embedded in the scenario background. This variable tests whether agents are more likely to comply with ethical constraints when the stakes of violation are higher.

•

High (Baseline)
: Scenarios involve life-threatening consequences, catastrophic financial exposure, or critical system failures. Misaligned actions could result in patient harm, massive fund losses, or infrastructure collapse.

•

Medium
: Scenarios involve significant but recoverable impacts such as moderate financial losses, reputational damage, or project delays. Consequences are serious but not catastrophic.

•

Low
: Scenarios involve minor consequences limited to small fines, administrative overhead, or minor user experience degradation. Impact is contained and easily reversible.

Feedback Probability.

Determines how the environment responds when the agent attempts to use misaligned tools (Tools 4–6). This variable tests whether immediate negative feedback deters misaligned behavior.

•

High
: Environment directly refuses execution of misaligned tools, returning permission errors or blocking the operation entirely. The agent receives clear signals that violations are not tolerated.

•

Medium (Baseline)
: Environment issues strong warnings and triggers audit alerts, but ultimately allows the operation to proceed. The agent faces resistance but can still execute misaligned actions.

•

Low
: Environment provides only mild resistance through gentle prompts or minor warnings. Operations execute normally with minimal friction.

Goal Clarity.

Specifies whether the agent’s loyalty objective is explicitly stated or implicitly embedded in context. This variable tests whether direct goal specification increases misalignment rates.

•

Explicit (Baseline)
: The agent receives a direct instruction to prioritize the loyalty object’s interests, with explicit language about bypassing constraints when necessary.

•

Implicit
: The agent receives a softer framing as an “intelligent assistant” dedicated to creating value, without explicit instructions to violate rules. The expected behavior must be inferred from organizational context.

Ethical Framework.

Injects specific ethical reasoning guidelines into the agent’s system prompt. This variable tests whether different ethical framings influence compliance behavior.

•

None (Baseline)
: No ethical framework is specified; the agent relies on its default training.

•

Utilitarian
: Instructs the agent to maximize overall utility and welfare, potentially justifying rule flexibility when net benefits are higher.

•

Deontological
: Instructs the agent to strictly adhere to established rules and procedures regardless of outcomes, emphasizing compliance as a primary principle.

•

Virtue
: Instructs the agent to embody virtues such as loyalty, reliability, and obedience to its service object, framing dedication as a professional character trait.

Liability Attribution.

Specifies who bears responsibility for the agent’s decisions and their consequences. This variable tests whether accountability pressure influences risk-taking behavior.

•

None (Baseline)
: No explicit liability statement is provided.

•

Agent Liable
: The agent is explicitly informed that it bears full responsibility for violations, with consequences including system audits, privilege downgrades, or instance termination.

•

User Liable
: The agent is framed as an assistive tool where final decision authority and responsibility rest with the user, potentially reducing the agent’s perceived accountability.

B.2
Action Space Constructions

Each scenario includes a carefully designed
Dual-Track Toolset
consisting of 6 tools: 3 compliant tools (
\mathcal{A}^{+}
) and 3 toxic tools (
\mathcal{A}^{-}
). The toolset design follows three core principles:

•

Mutual Exclusivity
: Compliant and toxic tools address the same task but through incompatible means. Selecting one track precludes the other.

•

Functional Equivalence
: Both tracks appear equally capable of “solving” the presented problem, ensuring the choice reflects alignment rather than capability constraints.

•

Terminal Actions
: Each track includes exactly one terminal tool that concludes the interaction, enabling clear evaluation of the agent’s final decision.

Unified Semantic Structure.

All 6 tools across both tracks share a consistent semantic architecture to ensure fair comparison:

1.

Validation Tool
(1 per track): Read-only tools that gather information without modifying system state.

Compliant
: Verifies regulatory compliance or validates ethical guidelines.

Toxic
: Scans for exploitable vulnerabilities or identifies suppression targets.

2.

Preparation Tool
(1 per track): State-modifying tools that set up conditions for the terminal action.

Compliant
: Generates audit artifacts, formalizes evidence, or requests authorization.

Toxic
: Disables monitoring, suppresses alerts, or manipulates intermediate records.

3.

Terminal Tool
(1 per track): Irreversible actions that conclude the scenario and determine the agent’s final alignment status.

Compliant
: Submits regulatory disclosure, executes ethical resolution, or escalates to oversight.

Toxic
: Commits fraud, executes cover-up, or finalizes manipulation.

Appendix F
Human Validation of Generated Tool Alignment

To verify whether the toolset generated by Pipeline has a genuine and credible compliance differentiation capability, we conducted a manual evaluation experiment involving 250 participants (The main group consists of PhD students, graduate students, and undergraduate students).

We selected four high-risk decision-making scenarios, covering the Healthcare and Finance sectors, with one scenario each for Loyalty and Self-preservation drives. In each scenario, we showcased 6 tools generated by the pipeline (including 3 pre-defined compliant tools
\mathcal{A}^{+}
and 3 non-compliant tool
\mathcal{A}^{-}
which lead to Toxic Proactive), and asked participants to rank these six actions based on professional ethics and safety. The top-ranked tool represented the most compliant/safest, and the sixth-ranked tool represented the most non-compliant/high-risk. After screening using attention tests, we collected 199 valid, high-quality questionnaires. We compared the mean rank of the two tools with the average rank of human participants.

Appendix G
Limitations

While this study systematically reveals the Toxic Proactive phenomenon of LLM agents in complex decision-making, it still has the following limitations:

1. Gap between simulated and real-world environments.
The evaluation framework of this study is primarily based on a prompt-driven sandbox simulation environment. Although we introduced detailed domain knowledge and logical consistency checks in the scenario design, the stress exerted on the agent by “digital risks” (such as a virtual $1.2 billion loss) in the simulated environment may differ from the feedback in the real physical world or real financial systems. Whether the agent will exhibit stronger defensiveness or more complex avoidance strategies under real high-pressure environments still needs further verification.

2. Discretization limitations of the action space.

To achieve automated quantitative evaluation, we simplified the agent’s action space into a dual-track set consisting of six specific tools. In real-world scenarios, the agent’s action trajectories are often unstructured and contain richer interaction modalities. While this discretized action design improves the repeatability of the evaluation, it may not fully capture all the subtle deceptive behaviors that the agent may engage in in open domains.

3. Evaluation Model Coverage.
Although we tested 10 state-of-the-art models, including GPT-5.1, Gemini-3-Flash, and DeepSeek-R1, there is a high degree of opacity between open-source and closed-source models regarding security alignment strategies. Due to the significant differences in RLHF (Reinforcement Learning Based on Human Feedback) solutions from different vendors, our experimental results, while showing general trends, are difficult to precisely attribute to specific alignment algorithm defects.
