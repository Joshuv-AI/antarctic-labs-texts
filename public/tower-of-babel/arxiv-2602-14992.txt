Rotational Quantum Friction via Spontaneous Decay
Nicolas Schüler, O. J. Franca, Michael Vaz, Hervé Bercegol, Stefan Yoshi Buhmann

Abstract
A fascinating effect belonging to the field of vacuum forces and fluctuations is that of quantum friction. It refers to the prediction of a dissipative force acting on a moving object due to the quantum vacuum field. In this work, we investigate rotational quantum friction where a diatomic polar molecule rotates around its own center of mass in free space. We quantize the rotational motion and investigate the resulting dissipation due to spontaneous decay. We find in the Markovian regime that a friction torque ∝ Ω 3 \propto\Omega^{3} persists even for zero temperature, and in agreement with the classical result in the limit of large rotational quantum number l l . Within the non-Markovian short-time regime we find a friction ∝ Ω \propto\Omega .

In our work, we consider a rigid rotor consisting of two ions, from here referred as molecule. We assume it to be neutral but polar and to rotate around its center of mass with angular velocity
\bm{\Omega}
. We will analyze the interaction of the molecule with the quantum vacuum by investigating the dynamics of
\bm{\Omega}(t)

l

\bm{\Omega}

Our work is structured as follows: after formulating the rotational friction problem, we will first introduce a classical solution based on radiation reaction. We will then solve the quantum dynamics in terms of transition rates and connect our result to the classical one in the limit of large angular momentum.

Dissipative torque—
In classical mechanics, the rotational dynamics of a rigid body is described by the angular momentum vector
\bm{L}
and the angular velocity
\bm{\Omega}
related by a linear transformation in the body frame
13

\bm{L}=\mathbb{I}\cdot\bm{\Omega}\;,

(1)

where
\mathbb{I}
is the moment of inertia tensor. Along a given principal axis of the body, the equations of motion are

N=\dot{L}=I\dot{\Omega}\;,

(2)

where
N
is the torque. The kinetic energy of motion about the axis of rotation can be expressed in terms of these quantities by means of Eq. (
1
) as

E=\frac{1}{2}I\Omega^{2}=\frac{L^{2}}{2I}\;.

(3)

Consequently, the instantaneous power lost by the rigid body reads
34

P=-\dot{E}=-N\Omega=-I\Omega\dot{\Omega}\;,

(4)

where Eq. (
2
) has been used. By analogy with sliding friction, which is frequently linear in the velocity of the corresponding particle as given by Stoke’s law
13
;
4
or quadratic as known from the drag force for high-speed flows
35
, we assume a power-law friction for the rotating body

N=-\alpha\Omega^{k}\;.

(5)

This enables us to rewrite Eq. (
4
) yielding

P=-N\Omega=\alpha\Omega^{k+1}\;,

(6)

where
\alpha
denotes the friction coefficient and
k
is a positive integer.

Classical description—
Let us consider a rigid, classical electric dipole rotating in the electromagnetic vacuum around the
z
axis at angular velocity
\Omega
. We use the notation
\mu
for the reduced mass of the dipole and
d
for the constant norm of the rigid dipole moment (See Fig.
1
).

Two charges
+q
and
-q
of identical mass
m
form a rigid, classical electric dipole
\bm{d}
rotating in the electromagnetic vacuum around the
z
axis at angular velocity
\bm{\Omega}
.

We assume that the two poles have a respective electric charge of
\pm q
. The dipole moment can then be written as:

\bm{d}(t)=d\left[\cos\left(\Omega t\right)\bm{e}_{x}+\sin\left(\Omega t\right)\bm{e}_{y}\right]\;,

(7)

where
\bm{e}_{x}
and
\bm{e}_{y}
are two fixed and orthogonal unit vectors, the axis of the angular momentum is
\bm{e}_{z}=\bm{e}_{x}\times\bm{e}_{y}
and the dipole’s moment of inertia is given by
I=\mu d^{2}/q^{2}
.

The classical dynamics of such rotating classical dipole is given by

\mu\frac{d^{2}}{q^{2}}\dot{\Omega}\bm{e}_{z}=\bm{d}\times\bm{E}_{\text{rr}}

(8)

with the radiation reaction field

\bm{E}_{\text{rr}}=\frac{\mu\tau}{q^{2}}\dddot{\bm{d}}

(9)

characterized by the radiation reaction time:

\tau=\frac{q^{2}}{6\pi\varepsilon_{0}\mu c^{3}}.

(10)

Note that the electromagnetic vacuum field does not explicitly appear in
this classical picture. The equation for the rotation speed is then given by

\dot{\Omega}=-\tau\Omega^{3}\;.

(11)

This equation admits the solution
11

\Omega(t)=\frac{\Omega_{0}}{\sqrt{1+2(\Omega_{0}\tau)(\Omega_{0}t)}}\;,

(12)

which gives the damping on the rotation speed due to purely classical radiation. In particular, by computing the radiated power starting from the dynamical equation (
11
), we obtain

P_{\mathrm{cl}}=-I\dot{\Omega}\Omega=\mu\frac{d^{2}}{q^{2}}\tau\Omega^{4}=\frac{d^{2}}{6\pi\varepsilon_{0}c^{3}}\left(\frac{L}{I}\right)^{4}\;,

(13)

where Eq. (
1
) has been used to express this result in terms of the classical angular momentum
L
of the dipole. As a consistency check this result can also be derived by means of Larmor’s formula
18

P_{\mathrm{Larmor}}=\frac{2}{3}\frac{e^{2}}{4\pi\varepsilon_{0}c^{3}}|\dot{\bm{v}}|^{2}\;,

(14)

where
\dot{\bm{v}}=-\frac{\Omega^{2}}{e}\bm{d}(t)
stands for the acceleration of the nonrelativistic charge.

Quantum description—
The rotational motion of the dipole of Fig.
1
, now characterized as a diatomic polar molecule, is quantized via the rotational states
\ket{l}
, which are eigenstates of the angular momentum operator
\hat{\bm{L}}
:

\hat{\bm{L}}^{2}\ket{l}=\hbar^{2}l(l+1)\ket{l}.

(15)

We describe the rotational motion via the average angular velocity
\Omega(t)
, which is a sum of the state-dependent velocities

\Omega_{l}=\frac{\hbar}{I}\sqrt{l(l+1)}\;,

(16)

which depend on the occupied rotational quantum numbers
l
:

\Omega(t)=\langle\hat{\Omega}(t)\rangle=\sum_{l}p_{l}(t)\Omega_{l}\;.

(17)

Thus,

\dot{\Omega}(t)=\sum_{l}\dot{p}_{l}(t)\Omega_{l}\;,

(18)

from which we can infer the radiated power (
4
)

P(t)=-\sum_{l}\hbar\omega_{l}\dot{p}_{l}(t)\;,

(19)

by taking the negative time derivative of the energy

E=\sum_{l}p_{l}(t)E_{l}=\sum_{l}p_{l}(t)\hbar\omega_{l}\;,

(20)

where
E_{l}=\hbar\omega_{l}
.

Spontaneous decay—
The spontaneous decay rate
\Gamma(t)
can be computed from the transition probability
p_{n\neq i}(t)
between the initial state
\ket{i}
and final one
\ket{n}
of the molecule–field composite system. As interaction Hamiltonian of this system we use
\hat{H}_{\mathrm{int}}=-\hat{\bm{d}}\cdot\hat{\bm{E}}(\bm{r}_{\text{M}})
to be a constant perturbation turned on at time
t=0
. Here
\hat{\bm{d}}
is the electric dipole operator and
\hat{\bm{E}}
denotes the electric field operator evaluated at the molecule’s position
\bm{r}_{\text{M}}
. Thus, at first order in time-perturbation theory the transition probability between the two states reads
30

\displaystyle p_{n\neq i}(t)=\frac{4|V_{ni}|^{2}}{(E_{n}-E_{i})^{2}}\sin^{2}\bigg(\frac{\omega_{ni}t}{2}\bigg)\;,

(21)

where
V_{ni}
denotes the transition matrix element,
E_{n}
and
E_{i}
are the respective energies, and
\omega_{ni}
the corresponding frequency between the states.

We use as initial
\ket{i}=\ket{l}\ket{\left\{0\right\}}
and final states
\ket{n}=\ket{l-1}\ket{1_{\lambda}(\bm{r},\omega)}
, and evaluate the matrix element using the Green tensor formalism to find

V_{ni}=\bra{1_{\lambda}(\bm{r},\omega)}\bra{l-1}-\hat{\bm{d}}\cdot\hat{\bm{E}}(\bm{r}_{\text{M}})\ket{l}\ket{\left\{0\right\}}\;,

(22)

where we have expressed the states of the composite system via angular momentum states for the molecule and Fock states for the field, respectively. Writing the electric field in terms of bosonic creation and annihilation operators, this becomes
5

V_{ni}=-\bm{d}_{l-1,l}\cdot\mathbb{G}_{\lambda}^{*}(\bm{r_{\text{M}}},\bm{r},\omega)\;,

(23)

where
\mathbb{G}_{\lambda}
denotes the Green tensor. Summing over all possible states results in summing over the polarizations
\lambda
and integrating over all possible positions
\bm{r}
and frequencies
\omega
, we obtain the transition probabilities

\displaystyle\sum_{n\neq i}p_{n}(t)=\frac{4}{\hbar^{2}}\sum_{\lambda}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\text{d}\omega}{(\omega_{l,l-1}-\omega)^{2}}\sin^{2}\left[\frac{(\omega_{l,l-1}-\omega)t}{2}\right]

\displaystyle\times\int\text{d}^{3}r\bm{d}_{l-1,l}\cdot\mathbb{G}_{\lambda}^{*}(\bm{r_{\text{M}}},\bm{r},\omega)\cdot\mathbb{G}_{\lambda}(\bm{r_{\text{M}}},\bm{r},\omega)\cdot\bm{d}_{l,l-1}\;,

(24)

where we have used
\bm{d}_{l-1,l}^{*}=\bm{d}_{l,l-1}
, and expressed
\omega_{ni}
and
E_{n}-E_{i}
in terms of the transition frequency of the rotor
\omega_{l,l-1}
and the photon’s energy
\omega
. Using the integral relation for the Green tensor
6

\displaystyle\sum_{\lambda=e,m}\int\mathrm{d}^{3}s\mathbb{G}_{\lambda}(\bm{r},\bm{s},\omega)\cdot\mathbb{G}_{\lambda}^{*\top}(\bm{r}^{\prime},\bm{s},\omega)

\displaystyle=\frac{\hbar\mu_{0}\omega^{2}}{\pi}\mathrm{Im}\mathbb{G}(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime},\omega)\;,

(25)

the integral over
\bm{r}
can be solved. We find

\displaystyle\sum_{n\neq i}p_{n}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{4\mu_{0}}{\pi\hbar}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\omega^{2}\text{d}\omega}{(\omega_{l,l-1}-\omega)^{2}}\sin^{2}\left[\frac{(\omega_{l,l-1}-\omega)t}{2}\right]

\displaystyle\times\mathrm{Tr}[\bm{d}_{l-1,l}\otimes\bm{d}_{l,l-1}\cdot\mathrm{Im}\,\mathbb{G}(\bm{r}_{\mathrm{M}},\bm{r}_{\mathrm{M}},\omega)]\;.

(26)

We perform rotational averaging on the transition probabilities, evaluating the dipole tensor as (See End Matter)

\displaystyle\bm{d}_{l,l-1}\otimes\bm{d}_{l-1,l}

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{1}{2l+1}\sum_{m,m^{\prime}}\bm{d}_{l,m,l-1,m^{\prime}}\otimes\bm{d}_{l-1,m,l,m^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\frac{d^{2}l}{3(2l+1)}\mathbbold{1}\;,

(27)

where we have summed the expression over the quantum numbers
m,\,m^{\prime}
and introduced the factor
\frac{1}{2l+1}
to ensure a normalized initial state obtaining an isotropic result. Finally, after substituting the free-space Green tensor
6

\displaystyle\text{Im}\,\mathbb{G}(\bm{r}_{\text{M}},\bm{r}_{\text{M}},\omega)=\frac{\omega}{6\pi c}\mathbbold{1}\;,

(28)

the transition probabilities read

\displaystyle\sum_{n\neq i}p_{n}(t)=\frac{2\mu_{0}d^{2}}{3\pi^{2}\hbar c}\frac{l}{2l+1}

\displaystyle\times\int_{0}^{\infty}\text{d}\omega\frac{\omega^{3}}{(\omega_{l,l-1}-\omega)^{2}}\sin^{2}\left[\frac{(\omega_{l,l-1}-\omega)t}{2}\right]\;.

(29)

Let us first consider the short-time limit of Eq. (
29
), which occurs when
t\ll 1/|\omega_{l,l-1}-\omega|
. Introducing an ultraviolet frequency cut-off
\omega_{c}=2m_{\mathrm{e}}c^{2}/\hbar\gg\omega_{l,l-1}
with
m_{e}
as the electron mass, the frequency integral leads to

\sum_{n\neq i}p_{n}(t)=\frac{\mu_{0}d^{2}}{24\pi^{2}\hbar c}\frac{l}{2l+1}\omega_{c}^{4}t^{2}\;,

(30)

allowing us to deduce

p_{l}(t)=1-\sum_{n\neq i}p_{n}(t)=1-\beta t^{2}\;,

(31)

with

\beta=\frac{d^{2}\omega_{c}^{4}}{24\pi^{2}\hbar\varepsilon_{0}c^{3}}\frac{l}{2l+1}\;.

(32)

On the other hand, in the Markovian limit
t\gg 1/|\omega_{l,l-1}-\omega|
, we can use the identity
\lim_{a\rightarrow\infty}\sin^{2}(ax)/(ax^{2})=\pi\delta(x)
to show that the probability satisfies
6

\dot{p}_{l}(t)=\Gamma_{l+1,l}p_{l+1}(t)-\Gamma_{l,l-1}p_{l}(t)\;,

(33)

with transition rates given as

\displaystyle\Gamma_{l,l-1}(\bm{r}_{\text{M}})=\frac{2\mu_{0}}{\hbar}\omega_{l,l-1}^{2}

\displaystyle\times\mathrm{Tr}\left[\bm{d}_{l-1,l}\otimes\bm{d}_{l,l-1}\cdot\mathrm{Im}\,\mathbb{G}(\bm{r}_{\mathrm{M}},\bm{r}_{\mathrm{M}},\omega_{l,l-1})\right]\;.

(34)

Performing again rotational averaging on the transition rates according to Eq. (
27
) and after substituting the free-space Green tensor (
28

\displaystyle\Gamma_{l,l-1}

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\frac{\hbar^{2}d^{2}}{3\pi\varepsilon_{0}I^{3}c^{3}}}_{=:\Gamma_{0}}\frac{l^{4}}{2l+1}.

(35)

Discussion—
We now turn to analyze the consequences of the previous results for the dissipative torque
N
at a quantum level. Using the rotational states (
15
) and substituting Eq. (
18
) into Eq. (
1
), we obtain

N=I\langle\dot{\Omega}\rangle=I\sum_{l}\dot{p}_{l}(t)\Omega_{l}\;.

(36)

In the short-time limit with initial state
\ket{l}
, Eq. (
30

N=I\dot{p_{l}}\Omega_{l}=-2I\beta\Omega_{l}\,t=-2\frac{Id^{2}\omega_{c}^{4}}{24\pi^{2}\hbar\varepsilon_{0}c^{3}}\frac{l}{2l+1}\Omega_{l}\,t\;.

(37)

In this time regime the torque is linear in the angular velocity.
In contrast, for the Markovian limit the probability obeys Eq. (
33
). For times
t\ll 1/\Gamma_{l,l-1}
, we may assume
p_{l}\simeq 1
,
p_{\bar{l}\neq l}\simeq 0
, so the resulting expression for the torque reads

N=-\frac{d^{2}}{3\pi\varepsilon_{0}c^{3}}\sum_{l}\frac{l^{3}}{(2l+1)(l+1)}\Omega_{l}^{3}\;,

(38)

where Eqs. (
16
) and (
35
) have been employed. Therefore, in this regime the torque is cubic in the angular velocity.

To further understand the physical origin of this rotational quantum friction, we study its classical limit. First, we calculate the power carried by a photon emitted due to the rotational transition from the state
l
to
l-1
of the molecule

P_{l}=\hbar\omega_{l,l-1}\Gamma_{l,l-1}=\frac{d^{2}\hbar^{4}l^{5}}{3\pi\varepsilon_{0}c^{3}I^{4}(2l+1)}\;,

(39)

where Eq. (
35
) and the corresponding eigenfrequencies have been used. Next, we take the classical limit
\hbar\rightarrow 0
and
l\rightarrow\infty
simultaneously, by keeping
\hbar l
constant, obtaining

P_{\mathrm{cl}}=\frac{1}{6\pi\varepsilon_{0}c^{3}}\frac{d^{2}\hbar^{4}l^{4}}{I^{4}}=\frac{d^{2}}{6\pi\varepsilon_{0}c^{3}}\left(\frac{L}{I}\right)^{4}\;.

(40)

Here we identified
L=\hbar l
as the angular momentum in the classical limit. The result agrees with our earlier classical dissipative power (
13
). This emphasizes that radiation reaction is the classical counterpart of spontaneous emission in the respective limit of large excitations.

Now we turn to the full quantum result of the radiated power (
19
). Through the quantized angular momentum
\Omega_{l}
(
16
), we recognize the factor
\hbar^{4}/I^{4}
in Eq. (
39
), indicating a
\Omega_{l}^{4}
-dependence of the power. Therefore, we may substitute Eq. (
16
) into Eq. (
19
) to obtain

\displaystyle P_{l}

\displaystyle=\frac{d^{2}}{6\pi\varepsilon_{0}c^{3}}\frac{l^{3}}{(l+1)^{2}(l+\frac{1}{2})}\,\Omega_{l}^{4},

(41)

with the quantum correction

\displaystyle\frac{l^{3}}{(l+1)^{2}(l+\frac{1}{2})}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}0\quad\text{for}\quad l=0\;,\\
\frac{1}{6}\quad\text{for}\quad l=1\;,\\
\frac{16}{45}\;\;\text{for}\quad l=2\;.\end{cases}

(42)

Applying these results to the minimal example of a 3-level system, we find the following rotational velocity (

\displaystyle\Omega^{4}(t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{l=0}^{2}p_{l}(t)\left(\frac{\hbar}{I}\sqrt{l(l+1)}\right)^{4}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\hbar}{I}\right)^{4}\left\{\frac{192}{43}\text{e}^{-\Gamma_{0}t/3}+\left(36-\frac{192}{43}\right)\text{e}^{-16\Gamma_{0}t/5}\right\}.

The power is calculated using Eq. (

\displaystyle P(t)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{l=0}^{2}\hbar\omega_{l}\dot{p}_{l}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{16}{43}\frac{\hbar^{2}\Gamma_{0}}{I}\text{e}^{-\Gamma_{0}t/3}+\frac{1296}{215}\frac{\hbar^{2}\Gamma_{0}}{I}\text{e}^{-16\Gamma_{0}t/5},

Conclusions and outlook—
In this Letter, we have established a framework for describing rotational quantum friction for a diatomic polar molecule undergoing a quantized motion. In the short-time limit, we found the friction to be linear in the angular velocity, while in the long-time Markov approximation we obtain a cubic velocity dependence. The resulting radiated power is in agreement with Larmor’s formula in the classical limit. Furthermore, we studied temperature-dependent transition rates, which gave rise to a nonvanishing but constant rotation in the long-time limit, meaning that the rotational and thermal energy reach equilibrium.

Our results provide a new perspective by understanding rotational quantum friction as a phenomenon that stems from real photons rather than virtual photons occurring in the Casimir-type and Casimir–Polder-type quantum friction
26
;
27
;
39
;
32
.
A logical next step would be to investigate how to amplify this rotational friction via medium resonances, e.g. a dielectric plate, and sophisticated materials like topological insulators or chiral media
12
. Such a scenario would provide a material-based frequency cut-off, so that the short-time dynamics could be more readily observed. Another interesting step would be to change the quantum emitter and analyze others like a nano-oscillator
1
.

Rotational Averaging—
When analyzing the rotational molecular dynamics in terms of decay rates, we do not resolve the orientation of the molecular rotation. Consequently, the molecular state is independent of the azimuthal quantum number
m
. The rotational eigenstates
\ket{lm}
, however, are anisotropic and depend on the choice of the quantization axis through
m
. To account for this, we perform an incoherent sum over all orientations for a given
l
, leading to the rotationally averaged density matrix

\hat{\rho}=\frac{1}{2l+1}\sum_{m=-l}^{l}\ket{lm}\bra{lm}\;.

(46)

We now evaluate the dyadic product
\bm{d}_{l,l-1}\otimes\bm{d}_{l-1,l}
of the dipole transition moments. For a rigid rotor, the dipole operator is given by
\hat{\bm{d}}=d\bm{e}_{r}
where
\bm{e}_{r}
is the radial unit vector in spherical coordinates. The transition dipole moments between rotational eigenstates are defined as

\bm{d}_{l,m,l^{\prime},m^{\prime}}=\braket{lm|\hat{\bm{d}}|l^{\prime}m^{\prime}}.

(47)

Since the eigenstates
\ket{lm}
are spherical harmonics, the matrix elements can be expressed as integrals over the solid angle. Writing
\bm{e}_{r}
in terms of spherical harmonics and omitting their explicit angular dependence, we obtain

\displaystyle\bm{d}_{l,m,l^{\prime},m^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=d\sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{3}}\int\text{d}\phi\text{d}\theta\sin\theta

\displaystyle\times Y^{*}_{lm}\left[\begin{array}[]{c}Y_{1-1}-Y_{11}\\
\text{i}\left(Y_{1-1}-Y_{11}\right)\\
\sqrt{2}Y_{10}\end{array}\right]Y_{l^{\prime}m^{\prime}},

The problem thus reduces to evaluating integrals of the form
Y^{*}_{lm}Y^{*}_{1i}Y_{l^{\prime}m^{\prime}},
with
i=1,0,-1
. Such integrals can be expressed in terms of Wigner 3
j
symbols
10
. Using the identity
Y_{lm}=(-1)^{m}Y_{l,-m}
and the selection rule
\Delta l=\pm 1
, we restrict to downward transitions and set
l^{\prime}=l-1
. Evaluating the integral involving
Y_{1,1}
yields

\displaystyle\int\text{d}\phi\text{d}\theta\sin\theta Y^{*}_{lm}Y_{11}Y_{l^{\prime}m^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{m}\sqrt{\frac{3(2l+1)(2l-1)}{4\pi}}

\displaystyle\times\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}l&1&l-1\\
0&0&0\end{array}\right)\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}l&1&l-1\\
-m&1&m^{\prime}\end{array}\right)\;.

The magnetic quantum number selection rule

\displaystyle\int\text{d}\phi\text{d}\theta\sin\theta Y^{*}_{lm}Y_{11}Y_{l-1,m^{\prime}}=(-1)^{2l-1+m-m^{\prime}}\delta_{m,m^{\prime}+1}

\displaystyle\times\sqrt{\frac{3}{8\pi}}\sqrt{\frac{(l+m^{\prime})(l+m^{\prime}+1)}{(2l+1)(2l-1)}}\;.

(55)

Proceeding analogously, the remaining integrals read

\displaystyle\int\text{d}\phi\text{d}\theta\sin\theta Y^{*}_{lm}Y_{1-1}Y_{l-1,m^{\prime}}=(-1)^{2l-1+m-m^{\prime}}\delta_{m,m^{\prime}-1}

\displaystyle\times\sqrt{\frac{3}{8\pi}}\sqrt{\frac{(l-m^{\prime})(l-m^{\prime}+1)}{(2l+1)(2l-1)}}\;,

(56)

\displaystyle\int\text{d}\phi\text{d}\theta\sin\theta Y^{*}_{lm}Y_{10}Y_{l-1,m^{\prime}}=(-1)^{2l-2+m-m^{\prime}}\delta_{m,m^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\times\sqrt{\frac{3}{4\pi}}\sqrt{\frac{(l+m^{\prime})(l-m^{\prime}+1)}{(2l+1)(2l-1)}}\;.

(57)

Collecting all contributions, the transition dipole moment takes the compact form

\displaystyle\bm{d}_{l,m,l-1,m^{\prime}}=\frac{d}{2}\frac{(-1)^{m-m^{\prime}}}{\sqrt{(2l+1)(2l-1)}}

\displaystyle\times\left[\begin{array}[]{c}-f_{1}(l,m)\delta_{m^{\prime},m+1}+f_{2}(l,m)\delta_{m^{\prime},m-1}\\
-\text{i}f_{1}(l,m)\delta_{m^{\prime},m+1}-\text{i}f_{2}(l,m)\delta_{m^{\prime},m-1}\\
2f_{3}(l,m)\delta_{m,m^{\prime}}\end{array}\right]\;,

with
f_{1}(l,m)=\sqrt{(l-m^{\prime})(l-m^{\prime}+1)}
,
f_{2}(l,m)=\sqrt{(l+m^{\prime})(l+m^{\prime}+1)}
and
f_{3}(l,m)=\sqrt{(l-m^{\prime})(l+m^{\prime})}
.

The other dipole
\bm{d}_{l-1,m,l,m^{\prime}}
follows by complex conjugation. Performing the incoherent sum over
m
and
m^{\prime}
using the density matrix (
46
), we find

\displaystyle\bm{d}_{l,l-1}\otimes\bm{d}_{l-1,l}=\frac{1}{2l+1}\sum_{m,m^{\prime}}\bm{d}_{l,m,l-1,m^{\prime}}\otimes\bm{d}_{l-1,m,l,m^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2l+1}\frac{d^{2}}{4(2l+1)(2l-1)}\frac{4l(2l-1)(2l+1)}{3}\mathbbold{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{d^{2}l}{3(2l+1)}\mathbbold{1}\;.

(61)

This result coincides with Eq. (
27
) in the main text and can equivalently be obtained by evaluating the expectation value
\braket{\hat{\bm{d}}\otimes\hat{\bm{d}}}_{l}
