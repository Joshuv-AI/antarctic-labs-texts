Distance Exceptional Graphs and the Curvature Index
Sawyer Jack Robertson, Finn Southerland, Erlang Surya

Abstract
A graph G = ( V , E ) G=(V,E) on n n vertices is said to be distance exceptional if the equation D ​ x → = 1 → D\vec{x}=\vec{1} admits no solution x → ∈ ℝ n \vec{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n} , where D ∈ ℝ n × n D\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n} is the shortest path distance matrix of G G . These graphs were first studied by Steinerberger in the context of a notion of discrete curvature (“Curvature on graphs via equilibrium measures,” Journal of Graph Theory , 103(3), 2023). This work has led to several open questions about distance exceptional graphs, including: What is the structure of such graphs? How can they be characterized? How rare are they? In this paper, we investigate these questions through the lens of a graph invariant we term the curvature index . We show that a graph is distance exceptional if and only if this invariant vanishes, and we develop a calculus for this invariant under graph operations including the Cartesian product and graph join. As a result, we recover and generalize a number of known results in this area. We show that any graph G G can be realized as an induced subgraph of a distance exceptional graph G ′ G^{\prime} . Moreover, in many cases, this embedding is an isometry. In turn, this leads to a number of methods for constructing distance exceptional graphs.

Beyond the work presented herein, there are many promising directions for future research. For instance, these authors feel it would be interesting to investigate more deeply the structure of graphs with infinite curvature index. It would also be interesting to explore further applications of the curvature index in constructing distance exceptional graphs with additional properties, such as planarity or bounded degree.

We remark additionally that this framework leads to natural conjectures on the behavior of the curvature index in the setting of Erdős–Rényi random graphs. In the regime where

The curvature index and graph operations

In this section, we formalize the curvature index for a connected graph, and examine its behavior under the graph operations of interest in this article: Cartesian product, vertex coalescence, and graph join. The following proposition, to which we alluded in the introduction, asserts a geometric constraint on the images of unit sum curvature potentials.

\lambda_{1}\leq\lambda_{2}\leq\dotsc\leq\lambda_{n}
denote the eigenvalues of
D
, together with an orthonormal set of corresponding eigenvectors
\vec{u_{1}},\vec{u_{2}},\dotsc,\vec{u_{n}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Then

\displaystyle\iota(G)^{-1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{(\vec{u_{i}}^{\top}\vec{1})^{2}}{\lambda_{i}}\in\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}.

(6)

We interpret the right-hand side as an extended real-valued expression, according to the conventions
\frac{1}{\infty}=0
,
\frac{1}{0}=\infty
, and
\frac{0}{0}=0
.

Proof.

We first prove statement
(i)
as follows. Assume that
G
is not distance exceptional and that
G
admits a curvature potential with nonzero sum. Then there exists some
\vec{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that
\vec{1}^{\top}\vec{x}=1
and
D\vec{x}=\iota(G)\vec{1}
. Therefore we have that

\displaystyle\vec{1}^{\top}\kappa

\displaystyle=n\vec{1}^{\top}(D^{+}\vec{1})

\displaystyle=\frac{n}{\iota(G)}\vec{1}^{\top}(D^{+}D\vec{x})

But since
G
is not distance exceptional,
\vec{1}\in\mathrm{range}(D)
, so
D^{+}D\vec{x}=\vec{x}
, and thus

\displaystyle\frac{n}{\iota(G)}\vec{1}^{\top}(D^{+}D\vec{x})

\displaystyle=\frac{n}{\iota(G)}\vec{1}^{\top}\vec{x}=\frac{n}{\iota(G)},

so that
Eq.

5
holds. Next, assume that
G
is not distance exceptional and that all curvature potentials of
G
have zero mean. Since
G
is not distance exceptional,
\kappa=D^{+}(n\vec{1})
is the unique vector of minimal Euclidean length such that
D\kappa=n\vec{1}
. In particular,
\vec{1}\in\mathrm{range}(D)
. But since
\vec{1}\notin D[\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\vec{1}^{\perp}]
, it must hold that
\vec{1}^{\top}\kappa=0
. Thus
Eq.

5
holds as an extended real number. Next we consider statement
(ii)
. First assume that
G
is not distance exceptional and that
G
admits a curvature potential with nonzero sum. Then there exists
\vec{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that
\vec{1}^{\top}\vec{x}=1
and
D\vec{x}=\iota(G)\vec{1}
. Since
\vec{1}\in\mathrm{range}(D)
,
\vec{1}\perp\ker D
, and thus it holds
\vec{1}^{\top}\vec{x}=\vec{1}^{\top}D^{+}(\iota\vec{1})
. Then we have

\displaystyle 1

\displaystyle=\vec{1}^{\top}\vec{x}=\iota(G)\vec{1}^{\top}(D^{+}\vec{1})=\iota(G)\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{(\vec{u_{i}}^{\top}\vec{1})^{2}}{\lambda_{i}},

where the right-hand side is finite (via the conventions of statement
(ii)
) since
\mathrm{ker}(D)\perp\mathbb{R}\vec{1}
. Therefore
Eq.

6
holds. In the case that
G
is not distance exceptional and all curvature potentials of
G
have zero mean, then there exists
\vec{x}\neq 0
such that
D\vec{x}=\vec{1}
, but for any such
\vec{x}
, in particular
D^{+}\vec{1}
, it holds
\vec{1}^{\top}\vec{x}=0
. In turn,

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{(\vec{u_{i}}^{\top}\vec{1})^{2}}{\lambda_{i}}

\displaystyle=\vec{1}^{\top}(D^{+}\vec{1})=\vec{1}^{\top}\vec{x}=0,

from which
Eq.

6
holds using the convention
\iota(G)^{-1}=0
. Finally, if
G
is distance exceptional, then there exists some
\vec{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
\vec{1}^{\top}\vec{x}=1
and
D\vec{x}=\vec{0}
. In particular,
\vec{x}
belongs to the eigenspace of
D
with eigenvalue
\lambda=0
, which is nontrivial, and thus for at least one such eigenvector, say
\vec{u_{1}}
, it holds that
\vec{u_{1}}^{\top}\vec{x}\neq 0
. By convention, the right-hand side of
Eq.

6
evaluates to
\infty
, and the claim follows.
∎

We remark that graphs for which
\iota(G)=\infty
do exist. For a minimal example, we may take
G
to be one of the graph joins
\mathsf{K}_{3}+(3\mathsf{K}_{1})
or
\mathsf{K}_{2}+(4\mathsf{K}_{1})
, where
n\mathsf{K}_{1}
consists of

disjoint and isolated vertices. Then one may verify that
D
is nonsingular and the image

has mean zero so that indeed
\iota(G)=\infty
.

In the remainder of this section, we provide a set of results which show that
\iota(G)
behaves in a relatively friendly manner under graph operations, allowing us to compute its value in a number of situations.

Theorem 3.4

(Cartesian products)
.

Let
G,H
be connected graphs. Then
\iota(G\,\Box\,H)=\iota(G)+\iota(H)
, with
\iota(G\,\Box\,H)=\infty
if either
\iota(G)=\infty
or
\iota(H)=\infty
.

Proof.

First assume that
\iota(G),\iota(H)<\infty
. Let
\vec{x}_{G}\in\mathbb{R}^{|V(G)|}
and
\vec{x}_{H}\in\mathbb{R}^{|V(H)|}
be such that
\vec{1}^{\top}\vec{x}_{G}=1
,
D(G)\vec{x}_{G}=\iota(G)\vec{1}
, and similarly for
H
. Define
\vec{x}_{{G\,\Box\,H}}:=\vec{x}_{G}\otimes\vec{x}_{H}\in\mathbb{R}^{|V(G)||V(H)|}
where
\otimes
denotes the Kronecker product of matrices. Then
\vec{1}^{\top}\vec{x}_{{G\,\Box\,H}}=1
, and by properties of the distance matrix under Cartesian products (see
[
1
]
), it holds that

\displaystyle D({G\,\Box\,H})\vec{x}_{{G\,\Box\,H}}

\displaystyle=(D(G)\otimes J+J\otimes D(H))(\vec{x}_{G}\otimes\vec{x}_{H})

\displaystyle=(D(G)\vec{x}_{G})\otimes(J\vec{x}_{H})+(J\vec{x}_{G})\otimes(D(H)\vec{x}_{H})

\displaystyle=\iota(G)\vec{1}\otimes\vec{1}+\vec{1}\otimes\iota(H)\vec{1}

\displaystyle=(\iota(G)+\iota(H))\vec{1}.

Next assume, without loss of generality, that
\iota(G)=\infty
. If
\iota(H)\neq 0
, then by the preceding argument,
G\,\Box\,H
is not distance exceptional and
\iota(G\,\Box\,H)\neq 0
. Otherwise, assuming
\iota(H)=0
, we claim that
\iota(G\,\Box\,H)\neq 0
. To this extent, let
\vec{y}
be such that
D({G\,\Box\,H})\vec{y}=0
. Write

\displaystyle(\vec{y}_{G})_{u}

\displaystyle=\sum_{v\in V(H)}\vec{y}_{(u,v)},\quad u\in V(G),

\displaystyle(\vec{y}_{H})_{v}

\displaystyle=\sum_{u\in V(G)}\vec{y}_{(u,v)},\quad v\in V(H).

Then

\displaystyle(D({G\,\Box\,H})\vec{y})_{u,v}

\displaystyle=\sum_{u^{\prime}\in V(G)}d_{G}(u,u^{\prime})(\vec{y}_{G})_{u^{\prime}}+\sum_{v^{\prime}\in V(H)}d_{H}(v,v^{\prime})(\vec{y}_{H})_{v^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=(D(G)\vec{y}_{G})_{u}+(D(H)\vec{y}_{H})_{v}.

Thus it must hold that
D(G)\vec{y}_{G}=c\vec{1}
and
D(H)\vec{y}_{H}=-c\vec{1}
for some
c\in\mathbb{R}
. Regardless of
c
, since
\iota(G)=\infty
, it must hold that
\vec{1}^{\top}\vec{y}_{G}=\vec{0}
. Therefore

\displaystyle\vec{1}^{\top}\vec{y}

\displaystyle=\sum_{u\in V(G)}(\vec{y}_{G})_{u}=0,

i.e., by
Lemma

2.1
,
\iota(G\,\Box\,H)\neq 0
, i.e.,
G\,\Box\,H
admits a curvature potential. Finally, we claim that
\iota(G\,\Box\,H)=\infty
. To see this, suppose
D({G\,\Box\,H})\vec{y}=\vec{1}
, and let
\vec{y}_{G},\vec{y}_{H}
be as before. Then

\displaystyle 1

\displaystyle=(D({G\,\Box\,H})\vec{y})_{u,v}=(D(G)\vec{y}_{G})_{u}+(D(H)\vec{y}_{H})_{v},

so that
D(G)\vec{y}_{G}\propto\vec{1}
. Since
\iota(G)=\infty
, it must hold that
\vec{1}^{\top}\vec{y}_{G}=0
, and thus

\displaystyle\vec{1}^{\top}\vec{y}

\displaystyle=\sum_{u\in V(G)}(\vec{y}_{G})_{u}=0.

This completes the proof.
∎

We remark that
Theorem

3.4
recovers and generalizes the result of
[
5
, Thm. 6]
which considered only the case
\iota(G)=\iota(H)=0
. The next result concerns the procedure of connecting two graphs
G,H
at a vertex, which was investigated in
[

]
.

Theorem 3.5

(Vertex coalescence)
.

Let
G,H
be connected graphs. Let
u_{0}\in V(G)
and
v_{0}\in V(H)
be fixed vertices, and let
G\,\circ_{u_{0},v_{0}}\,H
denote the vertex coalescence of
G
and
H
at
u_{0}
and
v_{0}
, respectively. Then

\displaystyle\iota(G\,\circ_{u_{0},v_{0}}\,H)

\displaystyle=\iota(G)+\iota(H).

If either
\iota(G)=\infty
or
\iota(H)=\infty
, then
\iota(G\,\circ_{u_{0},v_{0}}\,H)=\infty
.

Proof.

Fix an enumeration of
V(G)
and
V(H)
such that
u_{0},v_{0}
appear first. Write
G\,\circ\,H:=G\,\circ_{u_{0},v_{0}}\,H
for brevity. Assume that
\iota(G),\iota(H)<\infty
. Then there exist vectors
\vec{x}_{G},\vec{x}_{H}
with
\vec{1}^{\top}\vec{x}_{G}=\vec{1}^{\top}\vec{x}_{H}=1
and
D(G)\vec{x}_{G}=\iota(G)\vec{1}
,
D(H)\vec{x}_{H}=\iota(H)\vec{1}
. Write

\displaystyle\vec{x}_{G}^{\top}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\alpha_{G}&\vec{y}_{G}^{\top}\end{bmatrix},\quad\vec{x}_{H}^{\top}=\begin{bmatrix}\alpha_{H}&\vec{y}_{H}^{\top}\end{bmatrix},\quad\alpha_{G},\alpha_{H}\in\mathbb{R}.

Order the vertices of
G\,\circ\,H
as

\displaystyle V(G\,\circ\,H)=\{w\}\cup(V(G)\setminus\{u_{0}\})\cup(V(H)\setminus\{v_{0}\}),

where
w\in V(G\,\circ\,H)
is the vertex appearing after
u_{0},v_{0}
are identified. Define
s=|V(G)|-1
,
t=|V(H)|-1
. Denote by
{D(G)}^{\prime}
(resp.
{D(H)}^{\prime}
) the principal submatrix of
{D(G)}
(resp.
{D(H)}

V(G)\setminus\{u_{0}\}
(resp.
V(H)\setminus\{v_{0}\}

u_{0}
(resp.
v_{0}
). Let
\vec{x}_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{s}
denote the vector of distances between
u_{0}
and
V(G)\setminus\{u_{0}\}

\vec{y}_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{t}
denote the vector of distances between
v_{0}
and
V(H)\setminus\{v_{0}\}
. With this setup, the distance matrix
D=D(G\,\circ\,H)
takes the form

\displaystyle D=\begin{bmatrix}0&\vec{x}_{1}^{\top}&\vec{y}_{1}^{\top}\\
\vec{x}_{1}&{D(G)}^{\prime}&\vec{x}_{1}\vec{1}^{\top}+\vec{1}\vec{y}_{1}^{\top}\\
\vec{y}_{1}&\vec{1}\vec{x}_{1}^{\top}+\vec{y}_{1}\vec{1}^{\top}&{D(H)}^{\prime}\end{bmatrix}.

(7)

since for
u\in V(G)\setminus\{u_{0}\}
and

d_{G\,\circ\,H}(u,v)=d_{G}(u,u_{0})+d_{H}(v_{0},v)
. Now let
\vec{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{1+s+t}
be the vector given by

\displaystyle\vec{x}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}x_{w}\\
\vec{y}_{G}\\
\vec{y}_{H}\end{bmatrix},\quad x_{w}\in\mathbb{R},\,y_{G}\in\mathbb{R}^{s},\,y_{H}\in\mathbb{R}^{t}.

(8)

To guarantee that
\vec{1}^{\top}x=1
, we put

\displaystyle x_{w}=1-\vec{1}^{\top}\vec{y}_{G}-\vec{1}^{\top}\vec{y}_{H}=1-(1-\alpha_{G})-(1-\alpha_{H})=\alpha_{G}+\alpha_{H}-1.

Then we have that

\displaystyle D\vec{x}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}0&\vec{x}_{1}^{\top}&\vec{y}_{1}^{\top}\\
\vec{x}_{1}&{D(G)}^{\prime}&\vec{x}_{1}\vec{1}^{\top}+\vec{1}\vec{y}_{1}^{\top}\\
\vec{y}_{1}&\vec{1}\vec{x}_{1}^{\top}+\vec{y}_{1}\vec{1}^{\top}&{D(H)}^{\prime}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}x_{w}\\
\vec{y}_{G}\\
\vec{y}_{H}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\vec{x}_{1}^{\top}\vec{y}_{G}+\vec{y}_{1}^{\top}\vec{y}_{H}\\
\vec{x}_{1}x_{w}+{D(G)}^{\prime}\vec{y}_{G}+\vec{x}_{1}\vec{1}^{\top}\vec{y}_{H}+\vec{1}\vec{y}_{1}^{\top}\vec{y}_{H}\\

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\iota(G)+\iota(H)\\
\vec{x}_{1}\,x_{w}+\bigl(\iota(G)\vec{1}-\alpha_{G}\vec{x}_{1}\bigr)+\vec{x}_{1}(\vec{1}^{\top}\vec{y}_{H})+\vec{1}(\vec{y}_{1}^{\top}\vec{y}_{H})\\
\vec{y}_{1}\,x_{w}+\vec{1}(\vec{x}_{1}^{\top}\vec{y}_{G})+\vec{y}_{1}(\vec{1}^{\top}\vec{y}_{G})+\bigl(\iota(H)\vec{1}-\alpha_{H}\vec{y}_{1}\bigr)\\
\end{bmatrix}.

since, by construction,
\alpha_{G}\vec{x}_{1}+{D(G)}^{\prime}\vec{y}_{G}=\iota(G)\vec{1}
, and similarly
\alpha_{H}\vec{y}_{1}+{D(H)}^{\prime}\vec{y}_{H}=\iota(H)\vec{1}
. Continuing, we have

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\iota(G)+\iota(H)\\
\vec{x}_{1}\,x_{w}+\bigl(\iota(G)\vec{1}-\alpha_{G}\vec{x}_{1}\bigr)+\vec{x}_{1}(\vec{1}^{\top}\vec{y}_{H})+\vec{1}(\vec{y}_{1}^{\top}\vec{y}_{H})\\
\vec{y}_{1}\,x_{w}+\vec{1}(\vec{x}_{1}^{\top}\vec{y}_{G})+\vec{y}_{1}(\vec{1}^{\top}\vec{y}_{G})+\bigl(\iota(H)\vec{1}-\alpha_{H}\vec{y}_{1}\bigr)\\
\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\iota(G)+\iota(H)\\
\bigl(\iota(G)+\iota(H)\bigr)\vec{1}\;+\;\vec{x}_{1}\,(x_{w}+\vec{1}^{\top}\vec{y}_{H}-\alpha_{G})\\
\bigl(\iota(G)+\iota(H)\bigr)\vec{1}\;+\;\vec{y}_{1}\,(x_{w}+\vec{1}^{\top}\vec{y}_{G}-\alpha_{H})\\
\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=(\iota(G)+\iota(H))\vec{1},

as claimed, since

\displaystyle x_{w}+\vec{1}^{\top}\vec{y}_{H}-\alpha_{G}

\displaystyle=(\alpha_{G}+\alpha_{H}-1)+(1-\alpha_{H})-\alpha_{G}=0,

and similarly for the other term. This proves the first part of the theorem.

Next, we assume without loss of generality that
\iota(G)=\infty
. In this case, we claim that
\iota(G\,\circ\,H)=\infty
. By assumption there exists some
\vec{y}_{G}^{\top}=[\alpha\;\vec{y}_{G}^{\prime\top}]
such that

and
\vec{1}^{\top}\vec{y}_{G}=\alpha+\vec{1}^{\top}\vec{y}_{G}^{\prime}=0
. Define

\displaystyle\vec{x}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\alpha\\
\vec{y}_{G}^{\prime}\\
\vec{0}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{V(G\,\circ\,H)}.

Then, re-using the block decomposition

7
, we have

\displaystyle D\vec{x}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}0&\vec{x}_{1}^{\top}&\vec{y}_{1}^{\top}\\
\vec{x}_{1}&{D(G)}^{\prime}&\vec{x}_{1}\vec{1}^{\top}+\vec{1}\vec{y}_{1}^{\top}\\
\vec{y}_{1}&\vec{1}\vec{x}_{1}^{\top}+\vec{y}_{1}\vec{1}^{\top}&{D(H)}^{\prime}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\alpha\\
\vec{y}_{G}^{\prime}\\
\vec{0}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\vec{x}_{1}^{\top}\vec{y}_{G}^{\prime}\\
\vec{x}_{1}\alpha+{D(G)}^{\prime}\vec{y}_{G}^{\prime}\\
\vec{y}_{1}\alpha+\vec{1}\vec{x}_{1}^{\top}\vec{y}_{G}^{\prime}+\vec{y}_{1}\vec{1}^{\top}\vec{y}_{G}^{\prime}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}1\\
\vec{1}\\
\vec{1}\end{bmatrix},

since
\vec{x}_{1}\alpha+{D(G)}^{\prime}\vec{y}_{G}^{\prime}=\vec{1}
by construction, and since

\displaystyle\vec{y}_{1}\alpha+\vec{1}\vec{x}_{1}^{\top}\vec{y}_{G}^{\prime}+\vec{y}_{1}\vec{1}^{\top}\vec{y}_{G}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\vec{y}_{1}\alpha+\vec{1}+\vec{y}_{1}(-\alpha)=\vec{1}.

But then
\vec{1}^{\top}\vec{x}=\alpha+\vec{1}^{\top}\vec{y}_{G}^{\prime}+0=0
. Thus we have shown that there exists some
\vec{x}
with
D(G\,\circ\,H)\vec{x}=\vec{1}
and
\vec{1}^{\top}\vec{x}=0
. Let
\vec{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{V(G\,\circ\,H)}
be arbitrary such that

\displaystyle D(G\,\circ\,H)(\vec{y}-\vec{x})=\vec{0},

and thus
\vec{y}-\vec{x}\in\mathrm{ker}(D(G\,\circ\,H))
. Since

\displaystyle\vec{1}^{\top}\vec{y}

\displaystyle=\vec{1}^{\top}\vec{x}+\vec{1}^{\top}(\vec{y}-\vec{x})=0+0=0,

from which it follows that
\iota(G\,\circ\,H)=\infty
.
∎

3
]
, which considers the case

Theorem 3.7

(Graph joins)
.

Let
G,H
be graphs, connected or otherwise. Then

\displaystyle\iota(G+H)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\dfrac{\widetilde{\iota}(G)\widetilde{\iota}(H)-1}{\,\widetilde{\iota}(G)+\widetilde{\iota}(H)-2\,},&\widetilde{\iota}(G),\widetilde{\iota}(H)<\infty\ \text{and}\ \widetilde{\iota}(G)+\widetilde{\iota}(H)\neq 2,\\
1,&\widetilde{\iota}(G)=\widetilde{\iota}(H)=1,\\
\infty,&\widetilde{\iota}(G)+\widetilde{\iota}(H)=2,\ (\widetilde{\iota}(G),\widetilde{\iota}(H))\neq(1,1),\\
\widetilde{\iota}(H),&\widetilde{\iota}(G)=\infty,\ \widetilde{\iota}(H)<\infty,\\
\widetilde{\iota}(G),&\widetilde{\iota}(H)=\infty,\ \widetilde{\iota}(G)<\infty,\\
\infty,&\widetilde{\iota}(G)=\widetilde{\iota}(H)=\infty.\end{cases}

(9)

Proof.

For a fixed vector
\vec{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{|V(G)|+|V(H)|}
, partition
\vec{x}
and label its components in the following manner:

\displaystyle\vec{x}=\begin{bmatrix}\vec{x}_{G}\\
\vec{x}_{H}\end{bmatrix}

Write
s=\vec{1}^{\top}\vec{x}_{G}
and
t=\vec{1}^{\top}\vec{x}_{H}
. By properties of the distance matrix under graph joins (see, e.g.,
[
5
,
10
]
), it holds that

\displaystyle D(G+H)\vec{x}_{G+H}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\widetilde{D}(G)&J\\
J&\widetilde{D}(H)\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\vec{x}_{G}\\
\vec{x}_{H}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\widetilde{D}(G)\vec{x}_{G}+(\vec{1}^{\top}\vec{x}_{H})\vec{1}\\
(\vec{1}^{\top}\vec{x}_{G})\vec{1}+\widetilde{D}(H)\vec{x}_{H}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\widetilde{D}(G)\vec{x}_{G}+t\vec{1}\\
s\vec{1}+\widetilde{D}(H)\vec{x}_{H}\end{bmatrix}.

Now
\vec{1}^{\top}\vec{x}=1
if and only if

, and

if and only if

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\widetilde{D}(G)\vec{x}_{G}+t\vec{1}\\
s\vec{1}+\widetilde{D}(H)\vec{x}_{H}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\iota\vec{1}\\
\iota\vec{1}\end{bmatrix}.

The first row of this equation reads
\widetilde{D}(G)\vec{x}_{G}+t\vec{1}=\iota\vec{1}
, so that
\widetilde{D}(G)\vec{x}_{G}=(\iota-t)\vec{1}
. By definition of

, this is solvable with

only if

. Similarly, from the second row we have that

, so that

. Putting these equations together, we have

\displaystyle\begin{cases}s+t&=1,\\
\iota-t&=s\widetilde{\iota}(G),\\
\iota-s&=t\widetilde{\iota}(H).\end{cases}

(10)

If
\widetilde{\iota}(G),\widetilde{\iota}(H)<\infty
, this can be solved for

to recover the first three cases of

. Note the special case of

, which admits the solution

and

. If

and
\widetilde{\iota}(H)<\infty
, then we have the reduced system

\displaystyle\begin{cases}s+t&=1,\\
\iota-s&=t\widetilde{\iota}(H).\end{cases}

into which we can put

to recover the fourth case
Eq.

9
. The fifth case is similar, and the final case follows from the fact that if both modified indices are infinite, then we force

and no solution to
Eq.

exists.
∎

Remark 3.8
.

Although we have stated

in several cases, these can all be seen as limits of the first case if one is generous. For example, we have that

\displaystyle\lim_{\widetilde{\iota}(G)\to\infty}\dfrac{\widetilde{\iota}(G)\widetilde{\iota}(H)-1}{\,\widetilde{\iota}(G)+\widetilde{\iota}(H)-2\,}=\widetilde{\iota}(H),

4
Computing the curvature index for graph families

In this section we compute the curvature index for various graph families, which will be instructive for modeling the usage of the graph operations results in the previous section, and will be useful for constructing embeddings in the following section. Our first result concerns trees and symmetric graphs. Before stating it we recall the
Wiener index
of a graph, denoted
W(G)
, which is given by

\displaystyle W(G)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{u,v\in V}d(u,v).

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
G
be a connected graph on
n
vertices. Then the following hold:

(i)

is distance regular, then
\iota(G)=\frac{2W(G)}{n^{2}}

(ii)

\iota(G)=\frac{n-1}{2}

Proof.

For the first claim, since

is equal to
\frac{2W(G)}{n}
. Therefore, taking

, we have that

\displaystyle D\vec{x}=D\left(\tfrac{1}{n}\vec{1}\right)=\frac{2W(G)}{n^{2}}\vec{1},

and the claim follows by definition of
\iota(G)
. For the second claim, let

be a tree on
n
vertices. It is well known that the distance matrix of a tree is invertible (see
[
6
]
), so
\iota(G)\neq 0
. Let

[

]
(see also
[

\displaystyle\kappa_{u}

\displaystyle=\frac{n}{n-1}(2-d(u)),

so that by

\displaystyle\iota(G)

\displaystyle=\frac{n-1}{\sum_{u\in V(G)}(2-d(u))}=\frac{n-1}{2},

∎

5
Constructions of embeddings into distance exceptional graphs

In this section, we apply the results of the previous sections to construct embeddings of various graphs into distance exceptional graphs. We begin with a useful observation that the obstruction
\iota(G)=\infty
can be easily resolved at the expense of a nonisometric embedding.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be any graph on
n
vertices (connected or otherwise). Then there exists a connected graph
G^{\prime}
on at most
n+2
vertices containing an induced copy of
G
satisfying
\iota(G^{\prime})<\infty
.

Proof.

If
\iota(G)<\infty
take
G^{\prime}=G
. Otherwise if
\iota(G)=\infty
, let
G^{\prime}=G+2\mathsf{K}_{1}
be the join of
G
with two isolated vertices. As in the proof of
Theorem

4.3
, we have
\widetilde{\iota}(2\mathsf{K}_{1})=1
, so by
Theorem

3.7
,
\iota(G+2\mathsf{K}_{1})=1<\infty
.
∎

The next result states that
\iota(\cdot)
exhausts all rational numbers. In turn, for any
G
with
\iota(G)<\infty
, there exists a graph
G^{\prime}
with
\iota(G^{\prime})=-\iota(G)
so that both
G\,\Box\,G^{\prime}
and
G\circ_{u,v}G^{\prime}
(for any choice of

) are distance exceptional.

Theorem 5.2
.

Let
q\in\mathbb{Q}
. Then there exists a graph
G
such that
\iota(G)=q
.

Proof.

If
q=0
, supply any distance exceptional graph and the claim follows. Suppose
q<0
. Write
-q
as an Egyptian fraction:

\displaystyle-q=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\frac{1}{n_{i}},\quad n_{i}\in\mathbb{N},\,1\leq i\leq k.

Then we have that, by
Theorem

4.3
, it holds

\displaystyle\iota(\mathsf{K}_{1,1,2n_{i}+4})=-\frac{1}{n_{i}}.

Therefore

\displaystyle\iota\left(\Box_{i=1}^{k}\mathsf{K}_{1,1,2n_{i}+4}\right)=q.

\displaystyle r=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\frac{1}{n_{i}},\quad n_{i}\in\mathbb{N},\,1\leq i\leq k.

\displaystyle G=\left(\Box_{i=1}^{k}\mathsf{K}_{1,1,2n_{i}+4}\right)\;\;\square\;\;\mathsf{P}_{3}^{\Box m}.

\displaystyle\iota(G)

\displaystyle=m-\sum_{i=1}^{k}\frac{1}{n_{i}}=m-r=q.

∎
