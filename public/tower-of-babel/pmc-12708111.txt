Thigh-based Breast Reconstruction: A Decade of Experience With the Diagonal Upper Gracilis Flap
Authors: Oriana Haran, Amy Spencer, Babak J. Mehrara, Joseph H. Dayan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:The thigh is an alternative donor site for autologous breast reconstruction when abdominal flaps are unsuitable, though limited tissue and donor-site complications pose challenges. To improve volume and reduce morbidity, thigh flaps evolved from the transverse upper gracilis to the diagonal upper gracilis (DUG) designs. This study presented the largest DUG flap review, evaluating outcomes and donor-site morbidity.Methods:A retrospective review of patients undergoing DUG flap breast reconstruction was performed between 2012 and 2022. Demographics, surgical details, and complications were analyzed. Odds ratios assessed the association between unilateral versus bilateral donor-site harvest and revision surgery rates.Results:During a 10-year period, 109 flaps (70 patients, mean body mass index 24.3 kg/m2) were performed. Of these, 100 flaps were used for mastectomy reconstruction, 13 of which were stacked, and 9 were used for partial oncoplastic reconstruction. No flap failures occurred. The complication rate was 13.6%, of which 9.1% was related to the donor site, primarily cellulitis. No donor-site wound dehiscence, labial spreading, contour irregularity, or lymphedema was observed. Revisions occurred in 20% (breast) and 25.7% (donor site) of patients. Unilateral thigh flap harvest reduced the odds of donor-site revision and symmetrization procedures.Conclusions:The DUG flap’s diagonal design maximizes tissue harvest while minimizing donor-site morbidity. Unilateral harvest often achieves aesthetic results, curtailing the need for contralateral symmetrization. The DUG flap avoids concerns with thigh-based flaps, such as genital/gluteal distortion and lymphedema. Moreover, it offers a reliable learning curve, with bailout options via profunda artery perforator flaps.

Takeaways

Question: What are the outcomes and benefits of using the diagonal upper gracilis flap for autologous breast reconstruction?

Findings: The diagonal design enables greater tissue recruitment, and donor-site morbidity is low (9.1%), with no wound dehiscence, genital distortion, or lymphedema. Unilateral harvest often achieves good aesthetic outcomes, reducing the need for contralateral thigh symmetrization. Revisions were needed for 20% (breast) and 25.7% (donor site) of patients, mainly for cosmesis.

Meaning: The diagonal upper gracilis flap is a safe, effective option when abdominal flaps are contraindicated. Its design maximizes tissue harvest while minimizing donor-site morbidity, offering reliable and favorable outcomes.

INTRODUCTION

The thigh can be a challenging donor site for breast reconstruction, mostly because of the limited amount of available tissue.1–7 The transverse upper gracilis (TUG) flap has proven useful, and good results have been obtained4,8–10; however, its transverse design has some limitations, such as a more restricted flap width and higher tension along its donor site, thus increasing the risk of wound complications.9–13 A transverse incision inevitably transects more sensory nerves, leading to decreased sensation in more than 1 nerve distribution zone.10 Moreover, if tissue is harvested within the area above the femoral triangle, lower limb lymphedema could result as a potential complication.1,4,7,9,10,12–14

The diagonal upper gracilis (DUG) flap is a modification initially described by Dayan et al15 and Dayan.16 The purpose of this study was to introduce the DUG flap in detail, which compensates for some of the limitations inherent to a transverse design in this area. We present the largest review of patients who underwent DUG flap breast reconstruction, along with its associated possible complications, donor-site morbidity, and the need for donor-site revision or contralateral thigh symmetrization procedures when only 1 flap is harvested.

METHODS

We conducted a retrospective review of patients undergoing autologous breast reconstruction using the DUG flap at the Memorial Sloan Kettering Cancer Center between January 2012 and December 2022, following institutional review board approval. Patient demographic data, along with operative and perioperative details, were collected from the patient records. The primary endpoints were overall complications subdivided into breast flap complications (ischemia, congestion, hematoma, and infection) and donor-site complications, such as wound dehiscence, infection, hematoma, and seroma.

Demographic and clinical data were analyzed using IBM SPSS version 21 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY). The frequencies and proportions were obtained. The association between the number of donor sites harvested (1 versus 2) and revision surgery of the donor site was assessed using the χ2 test. The strength and direction of the correlation between the number of donor sites harvested (1 versus 2) and the need for revision surgery of the donor site or contralateral thigh symmetrization surgery were calculated using the odds ratio.

Preoperative Markings

To mark the flap, the patient is placed in a frog-legged position. First, the anterior incision is marked along the posterior border of the adductor longus, starting from the groin crease and extending 8–10 cm inferiorly. At this point, the line travels posteriorly in a curvilinear fashion along the relaxed skin tension lines, ending at the midpoint of the posteromedial thigh. A pinch test is performed to determine the appropriate flap width—this depends on skin laxity but is typically about 10 cm or more. The posterior markings complete the ellipse that marks the DUG flap (Fig. 1). This captures the angiosome of the flap, which is not purely horizontal, but oblique. Because the DUG flap is a musculocutaneous flap (not perforator-based) whose diagonal design inherently incorporates the medial circumflex femoral artery angiosome, we found preoperative Doppler examination unnecessary and therefore do not routinely perform it.

Surgical Technique

The dissection begins along the proximal anterior incision, which is based on the palpation of the adductor longus prominence, and then along the posterior border of the adductor longus muscle for about 10 cm, which keeps the dissection posterior and medial to the major lymphatic collectors and the greater saphenous vein. One critical point is to ensure that the assistant does not retract the thigh skin in 1 direction, as this can lead to transection of the perforators. The safest way to make the initial critical incision is for the surgeon to place his or her thumb and index finger on either side of the adductor longus and push straight down. The incision is then made directly down onto the adductor longus muscle. This ensures the perforators are preserved. There is only 1 posterior branch of the greater saphenous vein encountered in the deep subcutaneous plane that needs to be clipped. The greater saphenous vein itself is always preserved and remains lateral to the anterior incision. The dissection proceeds through the adductor longus fascia, which is incised and retracted laterally, exposing the gracilis pedicle in an avascular plane. The pedicle dissection is carried proximally until its origin (Fig. 2). Once isolated, the remaining skin incisions are made while typically beveling posteriorly to capture more of the subcutaneous volume. The gracilis muscle can be secured to the overlying skin with an interrupted 2-0 Vicryl to avoid undermining of the skin paddle by inadvertent shear forces. The gracilis muscle is then divided at its musculotendinous junction, and the flap pedicle can be divided for transfer to the chest. The donor site is closed primarily in a layered fashion over a closed suction drain. An ipsilateral flap is preferred for breast reconstruction because it allows the pedicle to be medially oriented and in proximity to the internal mammary vessels for anastomosis. In the case of stacked flaps, the ipsilateral flap is anastomosed anterograde to the internal mammary vessels and inset inferiorly, whereas the contralateral flap is anastomosed retrograde to the internal mammary vessels and inset superiorly (Fig. 3). This antegrade-retrograde anastomotic arrangement allows the flap pedicles to lie in an optimal orientation without kinking or tension.17

Stacked DUG flaps. The ipsilateral DUG flap is positioned at the inferior pole of the breast and anastomosed to the antegrade internal mammary vessels. The contralateral DUG flap is positioned in the superior pole of the breast with a retrograde anastomosis to the internal mammary vessels.

Postoperative Care

Postoperatively, there are no restrictions on the donor site other than standard incisional care. Postsurgical compression garments can be used in the first 3 weeks. A straight-line closure along relaxed skin tension lines allows for no postoperative mobility restrictions. Patients followed our institutional enhanced recovery after surgery protocol based on the general Enhanced Recovery after Surgery Society guidelines,18 incorporating early ambulation and multimodal analgesia, enabling patients to be discharged between 2 and 3 days postoperatively.

RESULTS

Seventy patients underwent 109 DUG flaps for breast reconstruction at our institution between 2012 and 2022. The average age of the patients was 51 years, and the mean body mass index (BMI) was 24.3 kg/m2. One hundred flaps were used for mastectomy reconstruction, 13 of which were stacked. Nine flaps were used for partial oncoplastic breast reconstruction (Table 1). For patients undergoing mastectomy reconstruction, the average flap width was 9.7 cm, and the average flap weight was 365 g (range, 96–830 g). The mean flap weight for patients undergoing lumpectomy reconstruction was 95 g (range, 53–173 g). The mean follow-up period in this cohort was 4.5 years. The overall incidence of complications was 13.6%, of which 9.1% were related to the donor site (Table 2). No instances of wound dehiscence were observed in this series. Six patients developed cellulitis of the donor site (5.5%), 2 had donor-site hematomas (1.8%), and 2 patients developed seromas (1.8%). One patient developed distal flap necrosis in an attempt to redesign the distal end of the flap more anteriorly to minimize the visibility of the posterior thigh scar, and 1 patient experienced fat necrosis, which was treated with a combination of HBOT and liposuction. Two patients returned to the operating room for revision of venous anastomosis.

Postoperative Complications and Revision Procedures

A total of 14 patients underwent revision surgery of the reconstructed breast (20%), and 18 patients underwent revision surgery of the donor site (25.7%). Revision surgery of the reconstructed breast was either performed through an augmentation procedure with an implant in 11 patients or mastopexy in 3 patients. A total of 20 (28.5%) patients underwent fat grafting of their flap(s), either during the primary procedure in 8 cases or subsequently in 12 other cases.

Revision surgery of the donor site was either soft-tissue excision with or without liposuction or scar revision. Contralateral symmetrization surgery implied liposuction of the contralateral thigh in cases involving the harvest of 1 DUG flap. Only 17% of the patients who underwent unilateral donor site (1 DUG flap) reconstruction required later donor-site revision and/or symmetrization procedures. However, in this series, there was no statistically significant association between the number of DUG flaps harvested in a single patient and the need for donor-site revision: χ2 value of 1.32, with a corresponding P value of 0.25 (Table 3). The calculated odds ratio was 2.31, but this was not statistically significant (Table 4).

DISCUSSION

Providing a reliable autologous option to patients beyond the DIEP flap is important but can be challenging because of the learning curve and complexity of alternative flaps. Additionally, the thigh donor site offers little room for error and exposes the patient to potential complications such as wound dehiscence, loss of sensation, and lymphedema. The thigh has become the donor site of choice for autologous breast reconstruction, especially in patients in whom the abdomen poses a certain contraindication or simply due to personal preference. Even thin patients may have a reservoir of fat along their inner thighs to support soft tissue–based breast reconstruction.

First introduced by Youssif et al19 in 1992, the transverse musculocutaneous gracilis flap exploits tissue excess of the medial thigh in a transverse or horizontal pattern below the gluteal crease and along lines of maximal tension in attempts to better camouflage the resulting scar. Despite this theoretical advantage, early series reported wound healing complications, such as dehiscence in up to 59% of cases,13 though subsequent modifications and surgical experience have reduced these rates to approximately 9.5%.9 Overall, some of these complications can be mitigated by avoiding overaggressive tissue harvesting; however, this invariably reduces the dimensions of the flap.13 It is the authors’ experience that in patients without much skin laxity, this can easily lead to a flap width of less than 6 cm.

Notably, transverse modifications have expanded flap volumes (ranging from 181 to 750 g in the series of Saint-Cyr4), challenging the assumption that diagonal designs inherently yield larger flaps. However, we contend that the DUG flap offers distinct advantages: its design aligns with the natural medial thigh tissue excess and lines of least tension, optimizing skin/tissue volume while minimizing donor-site risks. In our series, we observed no wound dehiscence, compared with the 8% rate reported by Saint-Cyr et al.4 This suggests that the DUG approach may better balance volume and safety in select patients. With a diagonal design along the lines of least tension, skin paddles with a width of 8–13 cm can safely be closed. This is particularly useful when replacement of mastectomy skin is required. Although the present study does not directly compare the diagonal design to the transverse one, it is the authors’ experience that a transverse design in this area leads to narrower flaps that are insufficient for mastectomy skin replacement if necessary, or to possibly larger flaps at the expense of donor-site morbidity.

The mean BMI of the patients in our study was 24 kg/m2. This can be explained by the fact that most patients with high BMI are good candidates for abdominal flaps. However, when tissue laxity predominates in the thigh area, flap volumes as high as 800 g can be recruited using a diagonal design. In our series, for total mastectomy reconstruction, flap volumes ranged between 200 and 800 g, depending on the patient’s needs, BMI, and body fat distribution. The largest flap volume in our series was that of a patient who underwent bilateral breast reconstruction with DUG flaps of 830 g each. She had a BMI of 36 kg/m2 following unsuccessful bariatric surgery. In our experience, as long as flaps are designed following the lines of least tension under an adequate pinch test, satisfactory wound healing should be achieved.

Thinner patients may benefit from the stacked approach, and certain patients may benefit from a hybrid approach. Thirteen patients benefited from a stacked approach to achieve the appropriate volume, and 11 patients chose consecutive augmentation with implants. The addition of an implant underlying the soft-tissue flap can often provide a very good shape and projection, whereas the tissue flap can camouflage the device and mitigate concerns such as rippling, which can be particularly problematic in very thin patients. Fat grafting is preferable to aggressive tissue recruitment, which certainly increases the risk of donor-site complications. Twenty patients underwent fat grafting, the majority of which occurred during a secondary procedure. Secondary procedures are common and are expected in these patients.

One-third of our patients underwent revision of either 1 or both donor sites. This is mainly for cosmetic purposes to achieve better contour and to improve symmetry. Although a higher percentage of patients who underwent reconstruction with 2 DUG flaps later underwent donor-site revision (32.5% versus 17.2%), this difference did not reach statistical significance in this sample. Although it seems intuitive that having 2 donor sites would lead to more contour irregularities, the current study may be underpowered to detect a statistically significant increase in donor-site revisions among these patients. We also observed that most patients who underwent unilateral flap harvest achieved an acceptable result at the level of their donor site, possibly curtailing the need for a revision and/or symmetrization procedure. Our data did not subcategorize revisions by specific indication (eg, symmetry versus general contour). Future studies may benefit from standardized subcategorization of revision indications.

Because donor- and recipient-site complications vary according to the number of flaps, their calculations rely on this rather than on the number of patients. The complication rate for the donor site was 9.1%, with no instances of dehiscence in this series. Distortion of the gluteal crease, as well as labial spreading, although uncommon, has also been reported as a direct consequence of the transverse design. A diagonal incision prevents genital or gluteal distortion, and patients may sit on their native skin rather than on a scar. A transverse pattern also affects the sensation in the distribution of more than 1 sensory nerve. Decreased sensation has been reported not only along the obturator nerve but also along the posterior and medial cutaneous nerves of the thighs as a consequence of the transverse incision.10 In our series, decreased sensation was reported only medially along the territory of the obturator nerve by 2 patients.

Lymphedema, or the suspicion it, has also been reported to be associated with the TUG or transverse musculocutaneous gracilis flap. Given that the gracilis pedicle lies in the septum between the adductor longus and gracilis muscle, the anterior extent of the TUG flap must cross the adductor longus. This traverses the greater saphenous vein along with the major lymphatic collectors of the lower extremity.20,21 For this reason, if a transverse flap orientation is most appropriate for the patient, a transverse profunda artery perforator flap (PAP), which lies more posteriorly and farther from the lymphatics, is preferred.22–24

As a musculocutaneous free flap—not a perforator-based flap—its design relies on the consistent axial blood supply from the medial circumflex femoral artery angiosome rather than individual perforators. As such, preoperative perforator mapping (eg, via Doppler) or detailed perforator anatomy is less relevant to its harvest or reliability. Although some anatomic variants have been identified, they are uncommon. It is the authors’ experience to bail out by performing a PAP flap in cases in which aberrant anatomy of the gracilis pedicle is encountered. In 2 cases, a double pedicle to the gracilis was encountered. In 1 case, the flap was turbocharged by mobilizing and anastomosing a single vein of the secondary pedicle to a side branch off the main pedicle vena comitante. In the other case, a diagonal PAP flap was performed instead.

Previous studies have proven that removing the gracilis muscle does not contribute to donor-site morbidity.25 Although there is 1 study that has shown an objective decrease in hip adductor strength of 11%, this was not noticeable to patients, and long-term weakness has not been reported.11 PAP dissection can result in some disruption of the adductor magnus muscle, depending on the degree of intramuscular dissection necessary. Although this also probably has minimal implications for donor-site morbidity, it should still be further evaluated because it is one of the thigh’s major adductors. Nevertheless, it is advantageous to master both options because many times the decision on whether to perform one or the other lies in patient anatomy, in terms of tissue laxity and perfusion. The diagonal PAP flap design, described by the senior author (J.H.D.), enables a larger skin paddle with reliable closure using a muscle-sparing approach.26

CONCLUSIONS

The DUG flap may help mitigate the challenges inherent to the transverse design of autologous thigh-based breast reconstruction. The diagonal pattern allows for more skin and soft-tissue recruitment while decreasing donor-site morbidity. Unilateral donor-site harvest can often yield an aesthetically acceptable result for most patients, possibly curtailing the need for a contralateral symmetrization procedure. Moreover, existing concerns with thigh-based flaps, such as genital or gluteal distortion and lower extremity lymphedema, are effectively avoided. Its consistent anatomy simplifies its learning curve, whereas variations can be safely bailed out through a profunda artery perforator flap.
