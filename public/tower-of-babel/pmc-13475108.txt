Rethinking Phase I units in the era of immuno-oncology: a three-layer framework
Authors: Liangjie Sun, Rongyang Xu, Shuhang Wang, Ning Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe rapid expansion of immuno-oncology (I-O) and other advanced therapies is reshaping the complexity of early-phase clinical development. While core Phase I principles remain fundamental across oncology, emerging modalities introduce additional requirements for translational integration, specialized safety management, and therapeutic-specific infrastructure. However, the organizational functions of Phase I units have not evolved at the same pace and remain largely centered on conventional operational responsibilities. Drawing on the practices of leading Phase I units across the USA, Europe, and China, and comparing evolving regulatory frameworks of the US Food and Drug Administration, European Medicines Agency/Medicines and Healthcare products Regulatory Agency, and China’s National Medical Products Administration, we propose a global perspective on the future development of Phase I units. We present a three-layer framework encompassing core Phase I foundations applicable across oncology, advanced translational capabilities, and ecosystem-level functions supporting emerging therapeutic development. Beyond ensuring patient safety and high-quality trial conduct, modern Phase I units should integrate translational research, artificial intelligence-enabled and model-informed drug development, regulatory science, and public engagement, while specialized centers may additionally support advanced therapy infrastructure, such as point-of-care manufacturing. Recognizing the diversity of institutional resources, we further propose a collaborative network model to facilitate scalable implementation and international harmonization. This framework positions Phase I units as integrated platforms connecting clinical investigation, translational science, regulatory innovation, and emerging therapy development, providing practical guidance for building future-ready early-phase clinical research infrastructure worldwide.

Layer 1: core Phase I unit foundations in oncology

Basic component

Modern Phase I units depend on an integrated operational infrastructure that supports five essential functions. First, patient safety remains the highest priority, requiring intensive screening, continuous monitoring, and prompt recognition and management of treatment-related toxicities. Second, robust protocol execution ensures accurate dose administration, cohort management, and consistent implementation of increasingly complex study designs. Third, dedicated Phase I teams provide real-time clinical judgment through continuous assessment of safety signals and rapid risk–benefit evaluation during dose escalation and expansion. Fourth, standardized collection of clinical, pharmacokinetic (PK), pharmacodynamic (PD), and biomarker data enables reliable evidence generation for regulatory decision-making. Finally, rigorous regulatory compliance, including ethical oversight, comprehensive documentation, and adherence to Good Clinical Practice (GCP), provides the governance framework necessary for high-quality first-in-human research.

Although the operational structures differ across healthcare systems, regulatory expectations for early-phase clinical trials have become increasingly convergent across major jurisdictions. The US Food and Drug Administration (FDA), European Medicines Agency (EMA), and China’s National Medical Products Administration (NMPA) all emphasize GCP, risk-based monitoring, investigator qualification, pharmacovigilance, and data integrity as prerequisites for first-in-human studies. Consequently, dedicated Phase I units are increasingly expected to provide standardized operational infrastructure capable of supporting global multicenter development rather than region-specific clinical research.

Clinical judgment beyond protocol execution

More importantly, Phase I investigators develop skills that are rarely acquired in standard clinical settings. These include attribution and grading of treatment-related toxicities, identification of dose-limiting toxicities and emerging safety signals, continuous risk–benefit reassessment, and first-in-human decision-making regarding dose escalation, cohort expansion, and trial adaptation. Beyond the classical maximum tolerated dose paradigm, modern early-phase oncology trials increasingly require dose optimization based on integrated PK/PD, biomarker, and clinical data. Immuno-oncology (I-O) further adds specific challenges, including the need for dedicated safety governance for immune-related toxicities and dynamic assessment of treatment response.

Such judgment cannot be acquired through protocol training alone. It depends on institutional structures that support continuous exposure to early-phase decision-making, including dedicated Phase I clinics, full-time investigators, multidisciplinary case review, and close interaction with translational scientists and clinical pharmacologists. Expertise is developed through mentorship-based training in which junior investigators participate in first-in-human studies under the supervision of experienced Phase I clinicians and is maintained through continuous professional development, including regular updates on evolving GCP requirements, international regulatory policies, innovative drug evaluation strategies, and advances in biomarker and translational technologies.

Layer 2: advanced translational capabilities

Integrated biospecimen and translational workflows

Unlike conventional oncology departments organized mainly by tumor type, dedicated Phase I units increasingly require cross-disciplinary platforms integrating clinical investigation with translational research. These may be organized around therapeutic modalities, biological mechanisms, or early-development programs, enabling collaboration among clinicians, translational scientists, pharmacologists, laboratory specialists, and regulatory experts. This integration is particularly important in I-O, where activity, immune modulation, toxicity, and benefit evolve dynamically across multiple data domains.

Translation is enabled through predefined workflows linking intensive clinical monitoring with protocol-specified PK/PD sampling, serial biospecimen collection, laboratory processing, and real-time data review. Clinical observations, safety events, PK profiles, immune biomarkers, and exploratory molecular analyses are assessed concurrently in multidisciplinary discussions. This allows emerging findings to guide patient care and refine translational hypotheses during the study, including the interpretation of treatment-mediated biological changes and the identification of mechanisms underlying efficacy, toxicity, and resistance.

Aggregating patients across tumor types also enables investigation of questions that transcend traditional disease boundaries. Rare cancers, uncommon molecular alterations, and atypical toxicity patterns can be studied systematically. Phase I units can therefore function as iterative learning systems that inform protocol refinement, patient selection, rational combination strategies, and the design of subsequent studies, rather than serving only as sites for protocol execution.

AI and model-informed drug development

FDA and EMA have published discussion papers on artificial intelligence (AI)-enabled drug development, while Chinese regulators have also encouraged digital technologies for clinical research, although governance remains evolving. Using high-quality clinical, laboratory, and biomarker data, AI may support prescreening, trial matching, and multimodal data integration. Model-informed drug development can further combine mechanistic PK/PD models with virtual patients, virtual cells, and human-relevant platforms such as patient-derived organoids and organ-on-chip systems.1 2 These approaches align with the regulatory shift toward new approach methodologies (NAMs), which use computational and human-relevant models to complement or reduce animal testing.3

Phase I units provide an early opportunity to evaluate these approaches against human outcomes through standardized collection of clinical, PK/PD, biomarker, and safety data. Implementation remains limited by insufficient standardized datasets, external model validation, platform interoperability, and regulatory governance. At present, these technologies should therefore remain complementary decision-support tools rather than replacements for investigator judgment, particularly when decisions involve incomplete data, rapidly evolving toxicity, or uncertain benefit-risk profiles.

Layer 3: ecosystem-level functions

Centralized standards in a decentralized manufacturing world

Embedding Phase I units within comprehensive hospitals creates an anchor for centralized standards in an increasingly decentralized development landscape. Under unified GCP governance, with coordinated institutional review boards and academic committees, protocol review, ethical evaluation, and scientific oversight can be anticipatory rather than fragmented. This is particularly important in first-in-human I-O trials, where uncertainty is intrinsic and safety signals may evolve rapidly. Addressing ethical and scientific risks at the design stage can protect participants while preserving methodological rigor and regulatory credibility.

For advanced therapies, especially individualized cellular products, integration also requires governance of manufacturing workflows, product quality, and traceability from collection to administration. Traditional boundaries between clinical care and manufacturing are therefore blurring. In the first confirmatory trial of tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes, an in-hospital production facility illustrated this convergence while creating substantial institutional and managerial responsibilities.4 As advanced modalities continue to evolve beyond conventional trial infrastructure, future-ready Phase I units may require additional physical space, specialized quality systems, and organizational flexibility to accommodate new manufacturing and clinical workflows. In 2025, the UK Medicines and Healthcare products Regulatory Agency advanced a decentralized manufacturing framework explicitly enabling point-of-care and hospital-based manufacture of personalized medicinal products.5

Regulatory science co-development

Phase I units increasingly participate in scientific advice meetings, regulatory consultations, and pilot programs organized by FDA, EMA, and NMPA. As NAMs, AI-assisted prediction, and decentralized manufacturing challenge traditional paradigms, regulators face questions about the evidence required for clinical translation. By generating early human data and comparing outcomes with predictions from novel platforms, Phase I units can help assess translational relevance and inform regulatory frameworks.

They are also among the first sites to implement new requirements in practice. Experience with informed consent, adaptive trial conduct, biospecimen governance, decentralized procedures, and cell therapy manufacturing can reveal implementation barriers and support refinement of guidance. Phase I units thus function not only as users of regulatory frameworks, but also as partners in their continued development and adaptation to emerging technologies and delivery models.

Public engagement and trial access

Leading Phase I units can improve public understanding of clinical research and access to innovative therapies. Dedicated clinical trial clinics with visibility comparable to routine outpatient services can integrate research participation into standard cancer care. Public outreach, including activities surrounding International Clinical Trials Day, may improve research literacy, trust, and awareness of trials as a treatment option. These efforts also support initiatives across jurisdictions to improve diversity, equity, and representativeness in enrollment, linking public engagement directly to trial access rather than treating it as a separate societal function.

Toward a collaborative network of Phase I units

This framework does not imply that every Phase I unit should develop all three layers. Rather, the layers represent complementary dimensions of functional maturity, and institutions may develop different capabilities according to clinical focus, scientific priorities, infrastructure, and resources (figure 1). Advanced translational laboratories, AI-enabled analytics, regulatory science programs, and manufacturing infrastructure require substantial investment and may therefore be concentrated in specialized centers.

A three-layer framework for high-level Phase I units in the era of immuno-oncology The framework illustrates the evolving functions of modern Phase I units across three interconnected layers. The three layers represent increasing functional breadth rather than a hierarchy of therapeutic modalities. Layer 1 comprises the core Phase I foundations applicable across oncology, including patient safety and toxicity management, protocol execution, real-time clinical judgment, reliable data generation, and regulatory compliance. Layer 2 encompasses advanced translational capabilities that integrate biospecimen collection, translational medicine, and AI-assisted clinical development to accelerate mechanistic understanding and optimize early drug development. Layer 3 highlights broader ecosystem functions through which leading Phase I units can inform regulatory decision-making, expand patient access to innovative therapies, and, for selected advanced therapies, support specialized infrastructure such as point-of-care manufacturing. Together, these three layers position Phase I units as integrated platforms that connect clinical investigation, translational science, regulatory innovation, and advanced therapy development while supporting collaborative networks for future early-phase clinical research. A collaborative network may improve scalability while preserving quality. Community hospitals and local oncology centers may focus on patient identification, education, referral, and selected early-phase studies. Regional cancer centers may provide broader trial portfolios, translational support, and multidisciplinary expertise. National cancer centers and highly specialized Phase I units may serve as hubs for first-in-human studies, advanced biomarker platforms, AI-enabled analytics, regulatory science innovation, and, where applicable, point-of-care manufacturing. Coordinated collaboration across these levels can share expertise and infrastructure more efficiently, expand patient access, and maintain scientific rigor, operational quality, and safety.

As early-phase development becomes increasingly multinational, harmonization of operational standards will be as important as scientific innovation. International alignment of regulatory expectations, investigator training, biospecimen governance, digital infrastructure, and quality systems can facilitate cross-regional trials, reduce duplication, and accelerate access to innovative therapies. Harmonization should prioritize shared standards for core Phase I operations while preserving flexibility for modality-specific capabilities in I-O and advanced therapies.
