Impact of mesenchymal stem cells on the coagulation mechanism in patients with liver cirrhosis: A review and prospects
Authors: Wanyuan Xiong, Zhongxia Yang, Zibing Qian, Xuebin Peng, Ying Li, Xiaorong Mao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCirrhosis, characterized by extensive liver fibrosis and architectural distortion, is commonly associated with coagulation disorders that increase the risk of both bleeding and thrombosis. Mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) have emerged as a promising therapeutic strategy for liver diseases, including cirrhosis, due to their regenerative and immunomodulatory properties. This review delves into the intricate relationship between MSCs and the coagulation system in cirrhotic patients, examining the impact on platelet dysfunction, coagulation factor synthesis, endothelial dysfunction, and fibrinolysis. The review highlights MSCs’ multilineage differentiation potential and their ability to secrete both procoagulant and anticoagulant factors, which are crucial in the context of liver disease. Clinical and preclinical findings on the effects of MSC therapy on coagulation are reviewed, along with safety and risk assessments. This review highlights the promising potential of MSCs in alleviating cirrhosis-associated coagulopathy, while identifying key challenges that must be addressed to advance clinical translation. Further research is therefore warranted to elucidate the underlying mechanisms and to optimize therapeutic strategies for clinical application.Keywords: Mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs), Liver cirrhosis, Coagulation mechanism, Blood coagulation, Liver function

Introduction

Liver cirrhosis is a disease caused by the progression of chronic liver inflammation. Persistent chronic inflammation results in the gradual replacement of healthy hepatic tissue with fibrous tissue and regenerative nodules, which impairs liver function, modifies the liver’s architecture, and eventually leads to cirrhosis.1 The main etiologies of cirrhosis include obesity, metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease,2 excessive alcohol consumption, hepatitis B or C infections,3 autoimmune diseases, cholestatic diseases, and iron or copper overload.4 Globally, cirrhosis is within the top 20 causes of disability-adjusted life years and years of life lost, accounting for 1.6% and 2.1% of the worldwide burden, and the associated societal and economic burden remains significant.5 A deeper understanding of the specific mechanisms underlying the occurrence and development of cirrhosis could further facilitate the development of targeted treatments. The development of cirrhosis is primarily related to changes caused by both parenchymal and non-parenchymal liver cells and the various cytokines they secrete.6

Moreover, accumulating evidence indicates that the progression of cirrhosis is related to changes in the hematological system. Patients with cirrhosis often experience complications, including coagulopathy, disseminated intravascular coagulation, accelerated fibrinolysis, abnormalities in fibrinogen (FIB) levels, and thrombocytopenia.7 Further clarification of these mechanisms could significantly contribute to early diagnosis and treatment.

Currently, numerous treatment methods are available for liver cirrhosis; however, their efficacy is often unsatisfactory. In recent years, research on the use of MSCs for the treatment of liver cirrhosis has gained increasing attention. MSCs possess several advantageous characteristics, such as the ability to expand ex vivo, low immunogenicity, immunomodulatory properties, abundant sources, and multipotential differentiation capacity. Therefore, MSCs may serve as an adjunctive therapy to liver transplantation or even as a potential alternative in certain cases. MSCs are a type of adult stem cell with the potential for self-renewal and multipotent differentiation, with remarkable therapeutic success in the field of regenerative medicine. To better understand their origins and development, it is important to revisit their initial discovery. MSCs were identified by Friedenstein in 1976 in the bone marrow, and subsequent research has shown their presence in various other tissues, including umbilical cord blood, adipose tissue, and placenta.8 MSCs can now be isolated from various tissues, including bone marrow MSCs (BMSCs), adipose-derived MSCs (AMSCs), umbilical cord-derived MSCs (UMSCs), and placenta-derived MSCs (PMSCs).9 As exogenous agents, MSCs induce a series of changes within the body after being injected, including alterations in coagulation function.10 This article aims to review the role of MSCs in the coagulation mechanisms of cirrhotic patients, analyze their potential therapeutic mechanisms, and explore clinical application prospects, thereby providing new insights for the use of MSCs in the treatment of liver cirrhosis.

Cirrhosis and blood coagulation

Liver cirrhosis, as the final stage of most liver diseases, has a very complex pathogenesis. The liver, being the largest parenchymal organ in the body, is the synthesis site for FIB and factors II, V, VII, IX, XI, XII, and XIII, playing a crucial role in the coagulation system.11 Coagulation and anticoagulation proteins are primarily produced in the liver; therefore, all liver diseases may disrupt the coagulation system. Clinically, changes in coagulation function are often due to alterations in the quantity of coagulation factors secreted by the liver. Alterations in various coagulation factors can influence the progression of liver cirrhosis to some extent. Furthermore, platelet (PLT) activation and FIB deposition also serve as significant contributors to the development of cirrhosis, as illustrated in Fig. 1. In patients with liver cirrhosis, hepatic dysfunction affects the synthesis of coagulation factors, PLT function, and the stability of vascular endothelium. These changes collectively lead to an imbalance in the coagulation system, increasing the risk of bleeding and thrombosis. Consequently, patients with cirrhosis often present with altered coagulation function, accelerated fibrinolysis, abnormalities in fibrinogen levels, and thrombocytopenia,7,12 as depicted in Fig. 2.

Mechanisms by which abnormal coagulation promotes liver fibrosis and cirrhosis. Abnormal coagulation function plays an important role in the occurrence and development of cirrhosis. Thrombin exerts multiple functions in the coagulation system. First, thrombin is regulated by negative feedback from the von Willebrand factor (vWF), which can promote the local generation of thrombin. Thrombin not only directly activates several coagulation factors, including factors V, XI, and VIII, but also promotes PLT activation. Activated PLTs aggregate to form clots and recruit immune cells (such as neutrophils and macrophages) to modulate inflammation, further promoting the generation and dissolution of fibrin, contributing to fibrosis. Additionally, activated PLTs release prothrombin, fibrinogen, as well as factors XI, V, and VIII, stimulating the synthesis of coagulation factors and fibrin, eventually resulting in hepatic fibrosis. Thrombin itself can convert fibrinogen in plasma into fibrin, further promoting the fibrotic process. Coagulation factor IIa can similarly facilitate this conversion. By activating protease-activated receptors 1 and 2, thrombin can also lead to the proliferation, migration, and differentiation of fibroblasts into myofibroblasts, thus promoting the development of cirrhosis. Similarly, coagulation factor Xa exerts a comparable effect through the same receptors. Furthermore, antithrombin can directly inhibit the activity of thrombin and factor Xa. Dysfunction of hepatic sinusoidal endothelial cells may lead to elevated levels of vWF, triggering hepatic fibrosis and even cirrhosis.

Changes in coagulation in patients with liver cirrhosis. Patients with cirrhosis often experience splenomegaly, which contributes to thrombocytopenia. Furthermore, cirrhosis itself induces PLT activation and the release of membrane particles and can cause decreased PLT counts or dysfunction. Moreover, cirrhosis lowers the levels of coagulation factors II, V, VII, and X, which is reflected in prolonged prothrombin time (PT) and activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT), as well as elevated international normalized ratio (INR) and D-dimer levels. Concurrently, cirrhosis also lowers the levels of antithrombin and leads to endothelial dysfunction, thereby increasing the concentration of vWF. Additionally, cirrhosis is often accompanied by abnormalities in fibrinogen, indicated by prolonged thrombin time, which also activates plasminogen, leading to hyperfibrinolysis.

The relationship between liver cirrhosis and coagulation factors

In patients with liver cirrhosis, coagulation factors play a crucial role in the regulation of hemostasis. Typically, the levels of coagulation proteins, including factors II, V, VII, and X, are decreased, resulting in prolonged prothrombin time (PT) and activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT), as well as elevated international normalized ratio (INR).7 While these changes in coagulation parameters are often viewed as clinical manifestations of liver cirrhosis, research indicates that coagulation factors themselves are significant contributors to the progression of liver fibrosis and cirrhosis.13

Coagulation factors Xa and thrombin are particularly important, as they drive profibrotic events that lead to the development of liver fibrosis.14 A potential link exists between coagulation factor-driven protease-activated receptor (PAR) activation and the coagulation cascade in the progression of fibrosis. Activated coagulation factor Xa contributes to this process by activating PAR-1 and PAR-2, promoting the proliferation and migration of fibroblasts and their differentiation into myofibroblasts, thus exacerbating liver fibrosis.15,16 Thrombin, a multifunctional enzyme central to the coagulation process, further influences this mechanism; it not only facilitates the formation of fibrin/PLT clots but also activates various coagulation proteins, such as factors XI, VIII, and V.17 The ability of PLTs from different individuals to support thrombin generation varies, which may be attributed to differences in PLT binding proteins.18

The coagulation cascade involves a series of proteolytic reactions, wherein factor Xa catalyzes the conversion of inactive plasma prothrombin into the active form, thrombin. Thrombin also activates PARs on the surface of PLTs, leading to PLT activation and aggregation.19 Importantly, clinical laboratory assessments reveal that PT and APTT are reflective of thrombin generation and serve as poor prognostic indicators for bleeding in patients with liver cirrhosis. For instance, research has demonstrated that patients with hepatitis C who have elevated plasma prothrombin levels resulting from single-nucleotide polymorphisms in the prothrombin gene, as well as those with heightened thrombin generation associated with the coagulation factor V Leiden polymorphism, exhibit accelerated fibrosis progression.20 In patients with hepatitis C virus (HCV), those with the factor V Leiden genotype tend to progress more rapidly toward liver cirrhosis, underscoring the role of the coagulation system in the disease’s pathogenesis.21

The coagulation disorders characteristic of liver cirrhosis present a complex interplay, marked by simultaneous reductions in procoagulant and anticoagulant factors due to liver synthetic defects. This results in an unstable hemostatic balance that predisposes patients to both thrombosis and bleeding. The bleeding tendencies in these patients can often be traced to reduced prothrombin synthesis, decreased PLT counts, and excessive fibrinolysis. However, due to other changes accompanying chronic liver disease that may restore the balance between anticoagulant and procoagulant effects, in some cases, the risk of thrombosis in chronic liver disease may even be greater than the risk of bleeding.22 The diminished levels of procoagulant factors may be partially offset by decreases in anticoagulants such as proteins C, S, and antithrombin.23, 24, 25

Antithrombin, produced by the liver, regulates the activity of intrinsic and extrinsic coagulation proteases.26 In the body, the primary targets of antithrombin are thrombin, factor Xa, and factor IXa. Protein C and protein S are vitamin K-dependent plasma glycoproteins that exert their anticoagulant effects by degrading activated factor VIIIa and activated factor Va.27 In patients with liver cirrhosis, systemic procoagulant changes commonly include decreased levels of antithrombin, protein C, and protein S, in addition to low levels of the metalloprotease ADAMTS-13, increased levels of von Willebrand factor (vWF), and elevated levels of procoagulant factors such as factor VIII.25

Fibrinogen, among the key coagulation factors, deserves particular attention. It is a glycoprotein synthesized and secreted by hepatocytes, serving a vital role in coagulation and hemostasis. Fibrinogen is also an acute-phase protein and is part of the coagulation cascade; thrombin or factor II, activated during the coagulation process, converts fibrinogen into fibrin in response to bleeding.28 As one of the earliest discovered coagulation factors with the highest concentration in plasma, fibrinogen acts as a substrate in thrombus formation and is involved in the clotting process. Structurally, fibrinogen is an elongated dimer formed by three pairs of polypeptide chains (α, β, and γ) linked by disulfide bonds.29

In liver cirrhosis patients, fibrinogen levels can be abnormally altered, characterized by an increase in certain carbohydrates on the fibrinogen structure, including multi-antennary glycans, GlcNAc, sialic acid, and mannose, coupled with additional protein carbonylation on the α chain. Notably, there is a reduction in the α-helix content of fibrinogen, leading to complex formation between fibrinogen and insulin-like growth factor-binding protein 1.30 In severe cases, serum fibrinogen levels may be slightly decreased or remain normal, but the functionally abnormal fibrinogen molecules can still be detected.24 The increased sialic acid content in fibrinogen among these patients may give rise to abnormal fibrinogen molecules, prolonging thrombin time, and impairing the conversion of fibrinogen to fibrin.31

Effect of liver cirrhosis on PLTs

PLTs are important anucleated blood components involved in the immune and hemostatic systems. They are formed and secreted by megakaryocytes and, under normal circumstances, are primarily involved in coagulation and fibrinolysis, while also participating in innate immune responses.32,33 In patients with liver cirrhosis, up to 78% experience thrombocytopenia, which is defined as a PLT count of fewer than 150 × 109/L.34 It is the most common hematological disorder in patients with chronic liver disease. The occurrence of thrombocytopenia is associated with various factors, including splenic hypersplenism, impaired PLT production, and thrombin-mediated PLT consumption.34,35

Previously, it was believed that splenic hypersplenism was the primary factor leading to decreased PLT counts in cirrhosis; however, subsequent studies have shown that impaired PLT production is also a very important factor. The production of PLTs is closely related to thrombopoietin, and the reduction in PLT production is primarily due to decreased levels of thrombopoietin.36 Thrombopoietin is mainly produced by the liver,37 and when the number of hepatocytes decreases, thrombopoietin levels significantly drop, which is one of the main reasons for thrombocytopenia in patients with liver cirrhosis.38 In the treatment of cirrhosis, restoring the levels of thrombopoietin can increase PLT counts and reduce the risk of bleeding.39,40 Thrombin, as one of the main PLT activators, can induce PLT activation, leading to time-dependent dysfunction and the breakdown of PLTs. After being stimulated by thrombin, PLTs break down into membrane particles, causing fragmentation of the PLTs and resulting in a decrease in their numbers.41

Liver cirrhosis causes changes in both the quantity and function of PLTs, and these changes can affect the progression of liver fibrosis. PLTs play a role in regulating the pathophysiological processes at various stages of liver fibrosis during liver disease. Abnormalities in coagulation factors such as fibrinogen and prothrombin in liver disease can activate PLTs.42,43 Activated PLTs undergo morphological changes and release various granules, which contain a range of bioactive lipids, small molecules, and other factors that promote PLT aggregation and thrombus formation while recruiting various immune cells. The recruitment of neutrophils and macrophages by PLTs is particularly important in liver disease, as they regulate inflammation, fibrin formation, and fibrinolysis, thereby contributing to fibrosis.44 The release of PLT granules can also influence the progression of liver fibrosis; for example, α-granules can stimulate and promote coagulation and fibrin formation by releasing prothrombin, FIB, factor V, and factor VIII, leading to liver fibrosis.33,45 Given the relationship between liver cirrhosis and PLTs, closely monitoring changes in PLT levels in patients with liver cirrhosis is essential.

Relationship between liver cirrhosis and vascular endothelial function

In addition to coagulation factors and PLTs, endothelial function and fibrin are closely related to liver cirrhosis. Endothelial cells form a thin layer that lines the interior of blood vessels, playing a crucial role in the barrier function between tissues and blood.46 Under normal conditions, vascular endothelial cells maintain blood fluidity, regulate blood flow, control vessel wall permeability, and maintain circulating leukocytes in a quiescent state.47 Moreover, endothelial cells perform important physiological functions in maintaining vascular integrity and regulating blood flow dynamics.48 Liver sinusoidal endothelial cells, as highly specialized endothelial cells, account for approximately 15%–20% of the total number of liver cells.49 Under physiological conditions, they maintain the quiescence of hepatic stellate cells through paracrine factors, thus inhibiting intrahepatic vascular contraction and the progression of fibrosis.50 In the context of liver cirrhosis, however, endothelial dysfunction occurs, leading to significant alterations in these functions.

In patients with liver cirrhosis, endothelial dysfunction is a cause of abnormal vascular responses. Endothelial dysfunction manifests as increased levels of vWF and elevated intrahepatic vascular resistance, which are key factors in the development of portal hypertension.51,52 The increase in vWF, a marker of endothelial dysfunction, is associated with the degree of liver fibrosis and the presence of esophageal gastric varices, and it is even correlated with the mortality rate of patients with liver cirrhosis.53,54 Additionally, the dysfunction of endothelial cells leads to a reduction in the production of nitric oxide, a vasodilator, which in turn contributes to increased vascular resistance and the progression of cirrhosis.49

The pathological role of endothelial dysfunction in liver cirrhosis is multifaceted. It not only participates in the development of portal hypertension by increasing intrahepatic vascular resistance and portal venous blood flow but also contributes to liver fibrosis.55 The activation of hepatic stellate cells by dysfunctional endothelium leads to the excessive deposition of extracellular matrix, a hallmark of liver fibrosis.56 Furthermore, the imbalance between procoagulant and anticoagulant factors in the endothelium of cirrhotic patients predisposes them to a prothrombotic state, which can exacerbate liver disease progression.57

These coagulation disorders play a key role in cirrhosis progression and directly impact clinical management. While liver transplantation remains the definitive treatment for end-stage cirrhosis, its application is severely limited by organ shortage and other constraints. MSCs, with their self-renewal, immunomodulatory, and antifibrotic properties, have emerged as a promising alternative strategy to repair liver injury, restore function, and improve cirrhosis-related outcomes.58,59

Biological properties of MSCs

MSCs are a type of multipotent stem cell originating from the mesoderm, possessing self-renewal and multipotent differentiation capabilities. They can differentiate into osteoblasts, chondrocytes, adipocytes, and other lineages, and they also play a role in maintaining tissue homeostasis, making them promising candidates for applications in regenerative medicine.60 MSCs can be obtained from various tissues, primarily including bone marrow, adipose tissue, umbilical cord, and placenta. In recent years, researchers have explored the isolation and biological characteristics of MSCs from different sources. For instance, BMSCs were the first type of MSCs discovered and are widely used in clinical applications, while UMSCs and PMSCs are gaining attention due to their superior proliferation capacity and differentiation potential.61

Regarding isolation methods, traditional bone marrow aspiration, although effective, can be traumatic and yields a limited number of cells. Recent advancements in microfluidic technology and cell separation equipment, such as Deterministic Lateral Displacement microfluidic sorting, have significantly improved the efficiency and purity of MSC isolation, providing higher-quality cell sources for clinical applications.62 Moreover, studies indicate that MSCs from different sources exhibit significant differences in biological characteristics and clinical application potential.63 This suggests that it is essential to consider the characteristics of MSCs and their compatibility with specific application scenarios when selecting the source.64

MSCs not only have the ability to differentiate into various cell types, such as osteoblasts, adipocytes, and chondrocytes, but they also exhibit significant immunoregulatory effects. Research has shown that MSCs can inhibit the proliferation and activation of T cells and modulate B cell function by secreting a variety of cytokines and growth factors, thereby affecting the body’s immune response.65 For example, the immunoregulatory function of MSCs derived from the oral cavity has been demonstrated to be one of their potential therapeutic advantages in tissue regeneration and disease treatment.66

In addition, MSCs display complex immunoregulatory roles within the tumor microenvironment, where they can both inhibit tumor cell growth and potentially promote tumor progression, making the study of MSCs significant in cancer therapy.67 In clinical research, these properties of MSCs are being explored for the treatment of a range of diseases, including graft-versus-host disease, diabetes, liver cirrhosis, and cardiovascular diseases. Many clinical trials are underway using MSCs either alone or in combination with other drugs for the treatment of severe degenerative and inflammatory diseases.68 The immunoregulatory and regenerative potential of MSCs offers new therapeutic strategies and hope in the medical field.

Impact of MSCs on coagulation mechanism

MSCs represent a rapidly developing therapeutic approach in regenerative medicine, and their safety has been validated in clinical applications. During the process of using MSCs to treat diseases, certain effects on the body occur, including changes in coagulation mechanisms, as illustrated in Fig. 3.

Specific mechanisms by which mesenchymal stem cells affectcoagulation function. The tissue factor (TF) expressed on the surface of MSCs activates the extrinsic coagulation cascade upon entering the bloodstream, increasing the generation of thrombin and activated factors such as FVIIa, FXIa, and FXIIa. This is reflected in elevated levels of PLTs (or PLT counts), plateletcrit (PCT), and fibrinogen (FIB), while PLT distribution width (PDW), mean PLT volume (MPV), PLT-large cell ratio (P-LCR), activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT), prothrombin time (PT), thrombin time (TT), and international normalized ratio (INR) all trend downwards. In addition, MSCs can secrete extracellular vesicles and exosomes. The TF released from extracellular vesicles can form a TF/FVIIa complex to initiate the coagulation cascade. Simultaneously, exosomes contain von Willebrand factor (vWF), coagulation factor X, and plasma kallikrein, further promoting coagulation. On the other hand, MSCs can also inhibit the activation and aggregation of PLTs in PLT-rich plasma (PRP) and whole blood induced by agonists, thereby exerting their anticoagulant function.

Effects of BMSCs on coagulation mechanisms

BMSCs are often thought to help improve coagulation. Studies have shown that BMSCs play an important role in coagulopathy induced by lipopolysaccharide (LPS).69,70 The infusion of BMSCs into a rat model with LPS-induced coagulopathy enhanced coagulation. This was demonstrated by an increase in PLT count, plateletcrit, and fibrinogen levels. Additionally, there was a reduction in PLT distribution width, mean PLT volume, proportion of large PLTs, APTT, PT, and INR.71 In addition, autologous BMSC treatment was confirmed by a meta-analysis to be beneficial for liver function and coagulation parameters in cirrhotic patients, such as alanine aminotransferase (ALT), total bilirubin (TBIL), albumin, PT, prothrombin activity (PTA), prothrombin concentration, and Child-Pugh score.72

Effect of UMSCs on coagulation mechanisms

UMSCs are thought to have endogenous procoagulant activity. Phosphatidylserine is exposed on exosomes released from UMSCs, providing a catalytic surface for the tenase complexes (factors VIIIa and IXa; factor X acts as substrate) and thrombin-generating complexes (factors Va and Xa, with factor II as substrate) in the coagulation cascade.73 Phosphatidylserine exposure supports coagulation by serving as an essential platform for the assembly of the intrinsic tenase complex (IXa, VIIIa, Ca2+) and the prothrombinase complex (Xa, Va, Ca2+). The interaction between phosphatidylserine and tissue factor may facilitate the correct localization of the tissue factor/FVIIa complex, which acts as a driving force for propagating coagulation.74 Phosphatidylserine may also help in the transformation of tissue factor from its inactive cryptic form to a bioactive state. Numerous studies have demonstrated that MSCs and their exosomes contain a variety of proteins associated with procoagulant activity, such as coagulation factor V, thrombospondin, myosin-9, histones, and CD9,75,76 all of which regulate coagulation through PLT-dependent mechanisms. 77, 78, 79

Effects of AMSCs on coagulation mechanisms

AMSCs produce extracellular vesicles with procoagulant activity, induced by a mechanism independent of the proinflammatory factors LPS and tumor necrosis factor. These exosomes induce coagulation via endogenous and exogenous coagulation pathways. Clot formation in plasma was inhibited in the absence of factor VII, suggesting that AMSCs primarily trigger the tissue factor-induced, factor VII-dependent extrinsic coagulation pathway. The intrinsic pathway of coagulation is mainly triggered on the surfaces of negatively charged objects, leading to the activation of factor XII. Since the coagulation time is prolonged in plasma deficient in factor XII when exposed to AMSC-derived extracellular vesicles, these vesicles can induce both the intrinsic and extrinsic pathways of coagulation.80 Furthermore, intravenous administration of allogeneic AMSCs demonstrated both proinflammatory and anti-inflammatory effects in septic patients, as well as enhanced coagulation activity and a reduced fibrinolytic response.81

Effects of PMSCs on coagulation mechanisms

Various parts of the placenta, such as the umbilical cord, amnion, chorion, and basal decidua, all contain MSCs, and these stem cells from different areas have varying impacts on the coagulation system.82 PMSCs can slightly activate the fibrinolytic system, but other blood activation markers remain stable, and expression profiling identified an increase in the expression of tissue factor in PMSCs.83 Research has found that the early administration of PMSCs in intracerebral hemorrhage can effectively reduce bleeding, significantly decrease the size of the hematoma, and increase vascular integrity, all of which reflect the procoagulant effects of PMSCs.84

In addition to procoagulant effects, placenta-derived mesenchymal stem cells (PMSCs) can also exert anticoagulant effects under specific conditions. As reported by Netsch et al.,85 PMSCs inhibit the activation and aggregation of stimulated platelets, thereby modulating primary hemostasis. Specifically, PMSCs suppress agonist-induced platelet activation and aggregation in both platelet-rich plasma and whole blood. The underlying mechanism involves the generation of adenosine via CD73 expressed on PMSCs during coculture with platelets. Adenosine elevates intracellular cyclic adenosine monophosphate levels and promotes the phosphorylation of vasodilator-stimulated phosphoprotein, further attenuating platelet activation and thus mediating the anticoagulant effects of PMSCs.85

Differences in coagulation regulation mechanisms of MSCs from different sources in the progressive stages of cirrhosis

Current research indicates that the coagulation-modulating effects of MSCs from different sources exhibit stage-dependent dynamic regulation during liver cirrhosis progression.86 In early fibrosis stages, MSCs primarily alleviate coagulation abnormalities by inhibiting hepatic stellate cell activation through mechanisms such as the secretion of HGF by BMSCs.87,88 In contrast, during advanced cirrhosis, MSCs appear to shift their therapeutic focus toward endothelial function repair, as demonstrated by UMSCs’ VEGF-mediated reduction of vWF levels to improve hypercoagulability.89,90 However, most existing studies concentrate on end-stage cirrhosis patients, creating a significant knowledge gap regarding MSC mechanisms during early and intermediate disease stages that warrants investigation through longitudinal studies. Furthermore, patient-specific factors including genetic polymorphisms (e.g., factor V Leiden mutation) and clinical variables (e.g., Child-Pugh classification) substantially influence MSC efficacy.91,92 Similarly, patients with baseline thrombocytopenia may demonstrate heightened sensitivity to MSC-induced PLT activation.93 These findings underscore the need for developing personalized treatment algorithms that integrate genomic profiling with clinical phenotyping to optimize MSC therapy outcomes across diverse patient populations and disease stages.

Role of MSCs in improving liver function

The treatment of liver cirrhosis patients with MSCs is not limited to improving coagulation function; MSCs also play an important role in reversing liver fibrosis and promoting liver regeneration.94 Studies have shown that MSCs can promote the proliferation and repair of hepatocytes through various mechanisms.95,96 Under the influence of the microenvironment of liver injury, MSCs can directly differentiate into hepatocytes.97 In the context of liver injury, MSCs can also secrete a variety of cytokines that exert anti-inflammatory effects and may activate hepatic stem cells in the liver, promoting hepatocyte regeneration and functional recovery.98 Specifically, MSCs can release hepatocyte growth factor, basic fibroblast growth factor, and other cytokines, which stimulate the proliferation and differentiation of hepatocytes.99,100 Furthermore, it has been found that the exosomes derived from MSCs also promote hepatocyte regeneration by modulating intercellular signaling and enhancing the self-repair capabilities of hepatocytes.101,102 In clinical research on acute liver failure and chronic liver disease, MSC treatment has demonstrated good safety and efficacy, significantly improving liver function indicators in patients.103

In addition to their role in hepatocyte regeneration, MSCs also possess immune regulatory functions that can alleviate liver inflammation and damage. MSCs can modulate the hepatic immune microenvironment, thereby promoting the recovery of liver function. Research has shown that MSCs can inhibit inflammatory responses in the liver and reduce liver damage through their immunomodulatory properties.104 MSCs can improve ischemia-reperfusion injury in the liver by limiting the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines from neutrophils and the formation of neutrophil extracellular traps through various factors and functional exosomes they carry. They can also regulate the activity of immune cells in the liver, suppress excessive inflammatory responses, and protect hepatocytes from damage.104,105 Additionally, MSCs can promote angiogenesis, improving blood supply to the liver and further facilitating liver regeneration.106 In studies of liver fibrosis, MSCs have been found to inhibit the progression of fibrosis by regulating the activity of hepatic stellate cells, thereby improving the microenvironment of the liver.107 Therefore, MSCs play not only an important role in hepatocyte regeneration but also in promoting the recovery of liver function by modulating the liver microenvironment and providing new strategies and methods for the treatment of liver cirrhosis.

Clinical efficacy of MSC therapy in liver cirrhosis

Improvements in liver function and coagulation parameters

MSC therapy for liver diseases can significantly improve liver function and coagulation function in patients with liver disease, and it can also reduce the degree of fibrosis to some extent. There are now many studies that elaborately discuss the changes in coagulation function after MSC treatment for cirrhosis.103,108 Collectively, these findings strongly suggest that MSCs hold considerable promise for the treatment of cirrhosis.

Research has found that autologous BMSCs transplanted into the hepatic artery can improve liver function and coagulation in patients with advanced cirrhosis within the first 90 days, resulting in increased albumin levels, decreased bilirubin levels, and reduced INR levels.109 In terms of combination therapy, a study found that combining BMSCs with silymarin effectively reduced PT and increased plasma fibrinogen concentration in carbon tetrachloride (CCl4)-induced cirrhotic rats.110 Suk et al.111 carried out a phase 2 clinical trial of autologous BMSC transplantation via the hepatic artery in patients with alcoholic cirrhosis, and the results showed that the treatment significantly reduced alkaline phosphatase levels, decreased the proportion of liver fibrosis, and improved the Child-Pugh score.111 In another clinical study, Zhang et al.112 applied intravenous infusion of human UMSCs to patients with decompensated liver cirrhosis, which not only improved coagulation indicators, including reduced INR, elevated prothrombin activity, and increased platelet count, but also increased albumin levels, decreased bilirubin levels, lowered MELD scores, improved liver function, and relieved ascites.112

UMSCs can also treat patients with decompensated cirrhosis. After treatment with UMSCs, patients with decompensated cirrhosis showed improvement in coagulation, clinical symptoms, hepatic synthetic function, and a reduction in the Child-Pugh score.113 Shi et al.59 conducted a follow-up on hepatitis B virus (HBV)-related decompensated cirrhosis patients for 48 weeks, measuring serum albumin, prothrombin activity, cholinesterase, and total bilirubin levels. They found that MSCs derived from umbilical cord blood significantly improved liver function and enhanced the long-term survival rate as well as liver function in patients with HBV-related decompensated cirrhosis.59 In a case report on the treatment of cirrhosis combined with other diseases using human UMSCs, UMSCs demonstrated good efficacy in treating a patient with cirrhosis combined with immune thrombocytopenia and refractory Henoch-Schönlein purpura. The patient received 8 weekly injections of UMSCs over 2 months, resulting in a gradual resolution of the rash and an increase in PLT count to >150 × 109/L. The levels of liver transaminases gradually decreased to normal, and abdominal ultrasound examinations showed that the round nodules in the liver had shrunk, and the thickness of the spleen had also reduced.114

In addition to directly improving liver function and coagulation function indicators, efficacy assessment after autologous MSC peripheral venous infusion in patients with HCV-related cirrhosis showed that MSCs had satisfactory tolerance and beneficial effects on liver synthetic function and liver fibrosis. Adverse reactions and countermeasures in MSC therapy

MSC therapy is an emerging therapeutic method for the treatment of liver cirrhosis, attracting significant attention for its potential efficacy. However, studies have indicated that the infusion of MSCs may also have adverse effects on the coagulation system, leading to thromboembolic events and instant blood-mediated inflammatory reaction (IBMIR).115, 116, 117 One reason for this effect is the inevitable blood compatibility issues arising as MSCs are increasingly sourced from non-hematopoietic tissues, such as adipose and placental tissues. Cells that do not frequently come into contact with blood tend to express varying levels of procoagulant tissue factor, which can result in mild to severe thromboembolic adverse events if appropriate anticoagulation measures are not implemented during intravenous infusion.118 Another major reason lies in the expression of tissue factor on the surface of MSCs; tissue factor not only has procoagulant properties but is also considered a crucial initiator of the extrinsic coagulation cascade.10,119

Upon contact with blood, MSCs initiate an innate immune response that transiently triggers blood-mediated inflammation. Specifically, MSCs activate the extrinsic coagulation cascade, prompting the activation of thrombin and various coagulation factors, such as factor VIIa, factor Xa, and factor IXa, which can lead to thrombus formation. Additionally, extracellular vesicles derived from MSCs can release tissue factor and form complexes with factor VIIa, further promoting the coagulation cascade.120,121 Research has also shown that their exosomes contain vWF, coagulation factor X, and plasma kallikrein, which may enhance their procoagulant activity.122,123

To address the potential risks posed by MSCs, several optimization measures have been taken in clinical practice. For example, a small dose of heparin can be administered concurrently with MSC infusion to reduce the risk of thrombosis. Heparin enhances the inhibitory effect of antithrombin on thrombin or factor Xa, thereby lowering the risk of excessive blood coagulation. However, heparin must be used cautiously, as it may interact with other acute-phase biomolecules released during inflammatory states, and dosage adjustments may be necessary to maintain its anticoagulant effect.124

Choosing healthy donors and conducting comprehensive screenings to ensure the quality and safety of stem cells are crucial methods to reduce potential risks. Furthermore, prioritizing the use of autologous MSCs can help lower the risk of immune rejection. Initiating treatment with small-dose infusions to assess patient responses is also a viable strategy. During the treatment process, close clinical monitoring, including assessments of liver function, immune responses, and potential complications, is necessary, alongside establishing follow-up mechanisms to promptly identify and address any issues that may arise.

To better mitigate adverse reactions during MSC therapy, clinical monitoring can be implemented with the following specific indicators: biomarker prediction involves the pretreatment assessment of plasma D-dimer, thrombin-antithrombin complex, and MSC levels to evaluate baseline hypercoagulability or hyperfibrinolysis. Dynamic imaging evaluation,such as ultrasound elastography (e.g., FibroScan), enables real-time monitoring of liver stiffness, indirectly reflecting fibrosis progression and the coagulation microenvironment. CT angiography aids in early detection of portal vein thrombosis. Real-time coagulation monitoring, such as thromboelastography or rotational thromboelastometry, can dynamically assess coagulation function during treatment, allowing timely adjustments to heparin dosage or temporary infusion suspension.

In summary, developing personalized treatment plans based on each patient’s specific circumstances, while considering the severity of the disease and the patient’s overall condition, can effectively reduce the risks associated with MSC therapy for liver cirrhosis, thereby improving the safety and efficacy of the treatment.

Conclusions

MSCs represent a promising therapeutic strategy for cirrhosis, as they can improve liver function and mitigate bleeding risk by regulating coagulation mechanisms. However, critical challenges remain, including post-transplantation immune responses, low cell survival, and variable treatment efficacy, with inconsistent findings across studies regarding long-term safety and effectiveness. Future research should prioritize large-scale randomized controlled trials to validate efficacy and safety, compare MSCs from different sources to identify optimal cell preparations, and clarify the underlying mechanisms. With advances in cell therapy, gene editing, and bioengineering, MSCs hold great potential for liver disease treatment, provided that ethical, regulatory, and safety issues are rigorously addressed. Further investigation is essential to resolve current controversies and establish a robust theoretical basis for clinical translation.
