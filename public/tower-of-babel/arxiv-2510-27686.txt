Quantitative Dependence of the Pierrehumbert Flow’s Mixing Rate on the Amplitude
Seungjae Son

Abstract
. We quantitatively study the mixing rate of randomly shifted alternating shears on the torus. This flow was introduced by Pierrehumbert ’94, and was recently shown to be exponentially mixing. In this work, we quantify the dependence of the exponential mixing rate on the flow amplitude. Our approach is based on constructing an explicit Lyapunov function and a coupling trajectory for the associated two-point Markov chain, together with an application of the quantitative Harris theorem.

1.
Introduction

1.1.
Main Results

We begin by stating the main result of this paper. Let
\mathbb{T}\cong\mathbb{R}/2\pi\mathbb{Z}
and
(\zeta_{n})_{n\geqslant 1}
be a sequence of i.i.d random variables that are uniformly distributed on
\mathbb{T}
. Define a random velocity field
u:[0,\infty)\times\mathbb{T}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
such that for each
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
,

(1.1)

u(t,x)=\begin{cases}A\sin(x_{2}-\zeta_{2n+1})e_{1}\,,&t\in[2n,2n+1)\\
A\sin(x_{1}-\zeta_{2n+2})e_{2}\,,&t\in[2n+1,2n+2)\end{cases}\,,

where
x=(x_{1},x_{2})\in\mathbb{T}^{2}
and
e_{i}
is the
i
-th standard basis vector.

Theorem 1.1
.

Fix
q<\infty
. For all sufficiently large
A
, there exists a random
D_{A}>0
such that every solution to the transport equation

(1.2)

\partial_{t}\phi+u\cdot\nabla\phi=0\,,

with mean-zero initial data
\phi_{0}\in H^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
satisfies

(1.3)

\lVert\phi_{n}\rVert_{H^{-1}}\leqslant D_{A}\exp(-\exp(-A^{96})n)\lVert\phi_{0}\rVert_{H^{1}}\,,\forall n\in 2\mathbb{N}\,,\quad\text{a.s}\,.

Moreover, there exists a finite deterministic constant
\bar{D}_{q}
, that is independent of
A
, such that

(1.4)

\bm{E}[D_{A}^{q}]\leqslant\bar{D}_{q}\,.

Here,
u
is defined as in (
1.1
) and
\bm{E}
is the expectation with respect to the probability measure where
(\zeta_{n})_{n\geqslant 1}
is defined.

Remark 1.2
.

Combining the argument in
[
CIRS25
, Theorem 1.1, Step 2]
with the
C^{1}
-regularity of the flow map, one can extend Theorem
1.1
to show that the mixing estimate (
1.3
) holds starting from any time
m\in 2\mathbb{N}
and for all flow times
t\geqslant 0
, at the expense of a time-dependent prefactor. More precisely, for all sufficiently large
A
, there exists a finite random variable
D_{A}^{\prime}>0
such that, almost surely, for all
m\in 2\mathbb{N}
and
t\geqslant 0
,

(1.5)

\|\phi_{m+t}\|_{H^{-1}}\leqslant A^{2}D_{A}^{\prime}\,(m^{2}+1)\,\exp\left(-\exp(-A^{96})\,\left\lfloor t/2\right\rfloor\right)\,\|\phi_{m}\|_{H^{1}}.

In addition,
D_{A}^{\prime}
has
A
-independent finite moments; that is,

(1.6)

\mathbb{E}[D_{A}^{\prime}]\leqslant\bar{D}<\infty\,,

for some constant
\bar{D}
independent of
A
.

We do not include this extension in the statement or proof of the theorem, as our main focus is on the mixing
rate
rather than on optimizing the prefactor in front of the decay estimate.

1.2.
Motivation and Background

We now motivate the problem and introduce the relevant background to place our result in the context of the existing literature.

The alternating sine–shear flow introduced by Pierrehumbert in
[
Pie94
]
has become a canonical model in the study of chaotic advection and tracer microstructure formation. Its appeal lies in its simplicity and its ability to generate stretching, folding, and small scales, making it an ideal setting to study mixing phenomena in geophysical and engineering contexts (see, e.g.,
[
BVO09
,
ABBea17
]
).

The chaotic dynamics of such flows can be rigorously analyzed through
mixing
. Broadly speaking, mixing describes the creation of finer scales and larger gradients in transported scalar fields, and its mathematical analysis has been the focus of extensive research
[
CKRZ08
,
LTD11
,
Sei13
,
MD18
]
. Explicit constructions of rapidly mixing velocity fields are available in works such as
[
YZ17
,
ACM19
,
EZ19
,
BBPS22
,
ELM25
]
. Moreover, mixing plays a central role in a variety of applications: it underlies enhanced dissipation
[
BBPS21
,
CZDE20
,
CIS25
,
TZ25
,
FI19
]
, the suppression of singularity formation in nonlinear PDEs
[
KX16
,
BH17
,
IXZ21
,
He23
]
, and rapid convergence of stochastic diffusions
[
CFIN23
]
.

In connection with the Pierrehumbert flow, numerical evidence in
[
CRWZ23
]
suggested that a closely related sawtooth shear exhibits fast (geometric) mixing. This was rigorously confirmed in
[
BCZG23
]
, which proved exponential mixing for the Pierrehumbert flow. Specifically,
[
BCZG23
]
shows that there exists
\gamma>0
and a finite random variable
D>0
such that, almost surely,

(1.7)

\|\phi_{t}\|_{H^{-1}}\leqslant D\,e^{-\gamma t}\,\|\phi_{0}\|_{H^{1}},\qquad\forall t\geqslant 0,

for every
\phi
that solves (
1.2
) with velocity field
u
defined in (
1.1
).
The mixing rate
\gamma
, however, is not explicit as the proof in
[
BCZG23
]
produces it through arguments of Furstenberg.

In this paper we are interested in quantifying the dependence of
\gamma
on the flow amplitude
A
.
Numerical simulations in
[
CRWZ23
]
indicate that the effective mixing rate increases with
A
, i.e. one expects
\gamma(A)\to\infty
as
A\to\infty
. Related results in
[
Coo23
, Theorem 2]
show that for deterministic phase shifts and random switching times, the logarithmic mixing scale increases at least linearly in
A
. However, rigorously capturing this dependence in the Pierrehumbert setting appears to be challenging.

The main contribution of this paper is to make one step in this direction.
Indeed, the quantitative estimate (
1.3
) we prove yields
\gamma(A)=\exp(-A^{96})\to 0
as
A\to\infty
.
This is weaker than the expected growth, and the reason for this is mainly
due to the difficulty in tracking how successive orthogonal shears interact at high amplitudes. For example, a thin vertical strip is stretched to scales of order
A^{-1}
after one horizontal shear, but following the vertical shear, the resulting structure becomes highly irregular and spatially inhomogeneous. It is unclear whether scales of order
A^{-2}
are produced uniformly, and this loss of coherence between shearing steps prevents a straightforward iteration of
A
-dependent mixing gains.

To overcome this challenge, we develop a new approach that makes the dependence on
A
explicit. Rather than tracing constants implicitly through the proof of
[
BCZG23
]
, which relies on qualitative tools such as equidistribution of irrational rotations, we construct explicit Lyapunov functions and coupling trajectories for the associated two-point Markov process. A key step is the identification of a family of “small sets” in phase space whose properties remain uniformly controlled in
A
, allowing for the application of a quantitative Harris theorem. To the best of our knowledge, this yields the first explicit quantitative upper bound on the mixing rate for the Pierrehumbert flow.

Finally, we note that the mixing rate
\gamma(A)
cannot grow faster than linearly in
A
(see, for example,
[
IKX14
]
). Taking this upper bound, the numerical evidence in
[
CRWZ23
]
, and heuristic insights from
[
Coo22
]
into account, it is natural to ask whether one can improve upon (
1.3
) to obtain sharper estimates or even saturate the optimal scaling
\gamma(A)\sim A
.

2.1.
Notation and Preliminaries

Random Dynamical System

Let
\xi^{m}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}(\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{2m})\in\mathbb{T}^{2m}
, and define the velocity field

u_{m}:[0,2m)\times\mathbb{T}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
by

(2.1)

u_{m}(t,x;\xi^{m})=\begin{cases}A\sin(x_{2}-\xi_{2n+1})e_{1},&t\in[2n,2n+1),\\[5.69054pt]
A\sin(x_{1}-\xi_{2n+2})e_{2},&t\in[2n+1,2n+2),\end{cases}

and consider the corresponding flow
\Phi:[0,2m)\times\mathbb{T}^{2}\to\mathbb{T}^{2}
defined by

(2.2)

\partial_{t}\Phi(t,x;\xi^{m})=u_{m}\big(t,\Phi(t,x;\xi^{m});\xi^{m}\big),\qquad\Phi(0,x;\xi^{m})=x.

Given a random sequence
(\zeta_{n})_{n\geqslant 1}
taking values in
\mathbb{T}
, define

\underline{\zeta}^{m}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}(\zeta_{1},\ldots,\zeta_{2m})
.
Then the sequence
(\Phi(2m,x;\underline{\zeta}^{m}))_{m\geqslant 1}
forms a random dynamical system satisfying

(2.3)

\Phi(2m+2,x;\underline{\zeta}^{m+1})=\Phi\big(2,\,\Phi(2m,x;\underline{\zeta}^{m});\,(\zeta_{2m+1},\zeta_{2m+2})\big)\,.

Moreover, the corresponding two-point process

(2.4)

\Phi^{(2)}(2m,(x,y);\underline{\zeta}^{m})=\big(\Phi(2m,x;\underline{\zeta}^{m}),\,\Phi(2m,y;\underline{\zeta}^{m})\big),\qquad(x,y)\in\mathbb{T}^{2,(2)},

defines a random dynamical system on the product space

(2.5)

\mathbb{T}^{2,(2)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}\mathbb{T}^{2}\times\mathbb{T}^{2}\setminus\Delta,\qquad\Delta\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{T}^{2}\times\mathbb{T}^{2}\mid x=y\}.

Here, the diagonal
\Delta
is removed to exclude the trivial stationary measure of the two-point chain.

Throughout the paper, we write
z=(x,y)
for points in
\mathbb{T}^{2,(2)}
with
x,y\in\mathbb{T}^{2}
, and we often suppress the explicit dependence of
\Phi
on deterministic
\xi^{m}
or random
\underline{\zeta}^{m}
when it is clear from context. For brevity, we also write

\Phi_{n}(x),\qquad\Phi_{2n}^{(2)}(z,\underline{\zeta}^{n}),\qquad\text{or}\qquad\Phi_{2n}^{(2)}(z,\xi^{n}).

Two-point Markov Process

From the independence of
(\zeta_{n})_{n\geqslant 1}
, we see that the process

(\Phi_{2m}^{(2)}(z))_{m\geqslant 1}
, defined for any
z\in\mathbb{T}^{2,(2)}
,
is a Markov chain taking values in
\mathbb{T}^{2,(2)}
.
In particular, we define the notation

(2.6)

P_{A}^{(2),n}(z,E)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}\bm{P}[\Phi_{2n}^{(2)}(z)\in E],\qquad\forall n\in\mathbb{N},

to denote the
n
-step transition kernel of the Markov process
\Phi^{(2)}
.

We also recall the notion of a
small set
, which will be used throughout the paper
in proving the ergodicity of the chain
P_{A}^{(2)}
.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
(E,\mathcal{E})
be a measurable space, and let

P:E\times\mathcal{E}\to[0,1]
be a Markov transition kernel.
A set
S\in\mathcal{E}
is called
small
if there exist
\alpha\in(0,1]

and a probability measure
\nu
on
(E,\mathcal{E})
such that

(2.7)

\inf_{z\in S}P(z,\cdot)\geqslant\alpha\,\nu(\cdot).

Here,
\nu
is referred to as a
minorizing measure
for
P
.

2.2.
Harris Conditions

In this subsection, we state the Harris conditions used to prove Theorem
1.1
.
These consist of a Lyapunov drift condition and a small set condition for the two-point Markov chain

, formulated respectively in Lemma
2.2
and Lemma

Once these are established, the quantitative Harris theorem
[

]
implies the geometric ergodicity of

The main contribution of this paper is the quantitative verification of the small set property in Lemma

A
.
Together with the Lyapunov drift condition, this provides a complete and quantitative proof of the Harris conditions required for Theorem

Lemma 2.2
.

There exist
A
-independent constants

,
K_{1}>0
, and a function

such that for all sufficiently large

,

(2.8)

P_{A}^{(2)}V\leqslant\gamma V+K_{1}A^{2p}\,.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let

be as in Lemma
2.2
. Then, for all sufficiently large
A
, there exist
A
-dependent constants
R_{2}=2CK_{1}A^{2p}/(1-\gamma)
,

, and a probability measure

on
\mathbb{T}^{2,(2)}
such that we have

(2.9)

\displaystyle P_{A}^{(2),M}V\leqslant\gamma^{M}V+K_{1}A^{2p}\frac{1-\gamma^{M}}{1-\gamma}\,,

(2.10)

\displaystyle\inf_{z\in\{V\leqslant R_{2}\}}P_{A}^{(2),M}(z,\cdot)\geqslant A^{-CA^{95}}\nu_{A}(\cdot)\,.

Here,

is an

-independent constant.

For continuity, we defer the proofs of Lemma
2.2
and Lemma
2.3
to Section
4
. In particular, to prove the small set condition (

)
in Lemma
2.3
, we require a key intermediate result—Lemma

—which establishes the uniform small set

Afterward, we proceed to prove the auxiliary lemmas needed for

2.2
and

,

3.2.
Proof of Lemma

Lemma

is a direct application of the quantitative inverse and implicit function theorems, stated below. In particular, if a family of functions

shares the same

-norm and Jacobian determinant at a point, these theorems provide estimates that are uniform across the family.

We first prove Lemma

Lemma 3.3

(Quantitative Inverse Function Theorem
[
Chr85
]
)
.

Let
F\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a
C^{2}
function. If we let

be two positive constants such that

(3.16)

D\geqslant\lVert F\rVert_{C^{2}}\quad\text{and}\quad r\leqslant\lvert\det DF(0)\rvert\,,

then there exists constants

(3.17)

C_{1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}C(d)D^{-d}\quad\text{and}\quad\quad C_{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}C(d)D^{-2d+1}\,,

such that the following holds.

(1)

F
is one-to-one on
B(0,C_{1}r)
.

(2)

F(B(0,C_{1}r))\supset B(F(0),C_{2}r^{2})
.

(3)

\frac{1}{2}r\leqslant\lvert\det DF(x)\rvert\leqslant\frac{3}{2}r
on
B(0,C_{1}r)
.

Lemma 3.4

(Quantitative Implicit Function Theorem)
.

Let
G:\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}+d_{2}}\to\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}

C_{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\textup{def}}}{{=}}C(d_{1}+d_{2})D^{-2(d_{1}+d_{2})+1}\,,

Here,
