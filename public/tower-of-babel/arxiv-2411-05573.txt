The second moment of the Riemann zeta function at its local extrema
Christopher Hughes, Solomon Lugmayer, Andrew Pearce-Crump

Abstract
. Conrey and Ghosh studied the second moment of the Riemann zeta function, evaluated at its local extrema along the critical line, finding the leading order behaviour to be e 2 − 5 2 ​ π ​ T ​ ( log ⁡ T ) 2 \frac{e^{2}-5}{2\pi}T(\log T)^{2} . This problem is closely related to a mixed moment of the Riemann zeta function and its derivative. We present a new approach which will uncover the lower order terms for the second moment as a descending chain of powers of logarithms in the asymptotic expansion.



1.
Background

Under the Riemann Hypothesis, Conrey and Ghosh
[
7
]
showed that

(1.1)

\sum_{0<\gamma\leq T}\max_{\gamma\leq t\leq\gamma^{+}}\left|\zeta\left(\frac{1}{2}+it\right)\right|^{2}\sim\frac{e^{2}-5}{4\pi}T(\log T)^{2}

as
T\rightarrow\infty
where
\gamma\leq\gamma^{+}
are successive ordinates of non-trivial zeros of
\zeta(s)
. In this paper, we will show how to obtain the lower order terms of this asymptotic, with the result given in Theorem
1
.

The Hardy
Z
-function
Z(t)
is the real function of a real variable which satisfies

|Z(t)|=|\zeta(1/2+it)|,

so Conrey and Ghosh’s result can be re-written in terms of the Hardy Z-function. It will be more convenient to work with the Hardy Z-function in various places throughout this paper, but we have endeavoured to link this back to the Riemann zeta function where applicable.

Since we are assuming the Riemann Hypothesis throughout this paper, we have that the zeros of
Z^{\prime}(t)
are interlaced with those of
\zeta(\frac{1}{2}+it)
, and hence with those of
Z(t)
.

Conrey and Ghosh further showed in
[
8
]
that this sum over the maxima can be rewritten in terms of a joint moment of the Z-function and its derivative, showing

(1.2)

\int_{1}^{T}|Z(t)||Z^{\prime}(t)|\ dt\sim\frac{e^{2}-5}{4\pi}T(\log T)^{2}.

We note that the corresponding value with the Hardy Z-function replaced by the Riemann zeta function is still unknown.

Conrey
[
6
]
established under the Riemann Hypothesis that

\frac{\sqrt{21}}{90\pi^{2}}T(\log T)^{5}\lesssim\sum_{0<\gamma\leq T}\max_{\gamma\leq t\leq\gamma^{+}}\left|\zeta\left(\frac{1}{2}+it\right)\right|^{4}\lesssim\frac{1}{2\sqrt{15}\pi^{2}}T(\log T)^{5},

where
A\lesssim B
means
A\leq B(1+o(1))
, with some improvements on the upper bound due to Hall
[
16
,
17
]
.

Although no other asymptotics for higher moments are known, Milinovich
[
25
]
established upper and lower bounds. He showed that for
k\in\mathbb{N}
and for
\varepsilon>0
,

T(\log T)^{k^{2}+1-\varepsilon}\ll\sum_{0<\gamma\leq T}\max_{\gamma<t\leq\gamma^{+}}|Z(t)|^{2k}\ll T(\log T)^{k^{2}+1+\varepsilon}

for sufficiently large
T
. He also gives an argument showing

\sum_{0<\gamma\leq T}\max_{\gamma<t\leq\gamma^{+}}|Z(t)|^{2k}=k\int_{1}^{T}|Z(t)|^{2k-1}|Z^{\prime}(t)|\ dt+O_{k,\varepsilon}(T^{\varepsilon}).

Finally, he conjectures for
k\in\mathbb{N}
that

\sum_{0<\gamma\leq T}\max_{\gamma<t\leq\gamma^{+}}|Z(t)|^{2k}\sim C_{k}T(\log T)^{k^{2}+1}

as
T\rightarrow\infty
, but did not conjecture the value of the implied constant.

The groundbreaking work of Keating and Snaith
[
21
,
20
]
changed all of this, when they used characteristic polynomials of random unitary matrices to conjecture moments of the Riemann zeta function of the form.

Following the groundbreaking work of Keating and Snaith
[
21
,
20
]
, the problem of calculating joint moments of the Riemann zeta function and its derivative was taken up by Hughes
[
18
]
in his thesis. Both the approaches involved calculating analogous moments of characteristic polynomials of random unitary matrices. Hughes conjectured the coefficient
F(s,h)
for integer
s,h
in terms of combinatorial sums, where

\int_{0}^{T}|Z(t)|^{2s-2h}|Z^{\prime}(t)|^{2h}\ dt\sim F(s,h)T(\log T)^{s^{2}+2h}

as

. The conjecture agreed with all values known at the time, namely

by Ingham
[
19
]

and

by Conrey
[

[

made sense for real

well, but not general real

, then we could infer Milinovich’s conjecture with the constant given explicitly.

Alternative approaches yielding different representations of the conjectured

with

were given by Conrey, Rubinstein and Snaith
[
5
]
(in the special case where

[

However, the problem of non-integer

was able to establish results for

Assiotis, Keating and Warren

In addition to the work on the typical joint moment, Keating and Wei

have considered the RMT analogue of joint moments for higher derivatives. The case of the joint moments of arbitrary numbers of higher

Curran

2.
Results

As mentioned previously, the purpose of this paper is to give a full asymptotic with lower-order terms for (
1.1
). Specifically, we prove the following theorem.

Theorem 1
.

Assume the Riemann Hypothesis. Given
\gamma
, an ordinate of a non-trivial zero of
\zeta(s)
, let
\gamma^{+}\geq\gamma
denote the next successive ordinate of a zeta zero. Let

L=\log\frac{T}{2\pi}
.
Then for any fixed
N\geq 0
, as
T\to\infty
we have

\sum_{0<\gamma\leq T}\max_{\gamma\leq t\leq\gamma^{+}}\left|\zeta\left(\frac{1}{2}+it\right)\right|^{2}=\frac{e^{2}-5}{2}\frac{T}{2\pi}L^{2}+\alpha_{-1}\frac{T}{2\pi}L+\frac{T}{2\pi}\sum_{n=0}^{N}\frac{\alpha_{n}}{L^{n}}+O_{N}\left(\frac{T}{L^{N+1}}\right)

where

\displaystyle\alpha_{-1}=5-e^{2}-10\gamma_{0}+2e^{2}\gamma_{0}

\displaystyle\alpha_{0}=12\gamma_{1}-4e^{2}\gamma_{1}-5+e^{2}+10\gamma_{0}-2e^{2}\gamma_{0}-4\gamma_{0}^{2}

and for
n\geq 1

\alpha_{n}=\sum_{k=n}^{\infty}\frac{2^{k+1}c_{k,n+2}}{(k-n)!}+(n-1)!\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{2^{k+1}}{(k-1)!}\sum_{j=0}^{\min\{k,n-1\}}\binom{k}{j}c_{k,j+2}

where the
c_{k,\ell}
are the Laurent series coefficients around
s=1
of

\left(\frac{\zeta^{\prime}}{\zeta}(s)\right)^{\prime}\left(-\frac{\zeta^{\prime}}{\zeta}(s)\right)^{k-1}\zeta^{2}(s)\frac{1}{s}=\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}c_{k,\ell}(s-1)^{-k-3+\ell}.

Remark
.

(1)

This recovers the leading-order asymptotic behaviour of Conrey and Ghosh.

(2)

This infinite descending chain of powers of
\log T
is due to a pole in the function that we consider near
1
, given by
\beta_{1}=1+2/L+O\left(L^{-2}\right)
. Were we able to find a closed form for the asymptotic as a function of
\beta_{1}
, we expect we would be able to give Theorem
1
with a power-saving error term of
O\left(T^{1/2+\varepsilon}\right)
for all
\varepsilon>0
. We have set aside the search for such a form for the time being.

3.
Overview of the paper

In Section
4
, we consider properties our auxiliary function

Z_{1}(s)=\zeta^{\prime}(s)-\frac{1}{2}\frac{\chi^{\prime}}{\chi}(s)\zeta(s)

used in the proof, where
\chi(s)
is the factor from the non-symmetrical form of the functional equation of the Riemann zeta function. This function is zero at the extrema of the Riemann zeta function.

We state and prove some preliminary lemmas required for the wider proof, including a twisted second moment of the Riemann zeta function, and some identities of arithmetic functions.

In Section
5
, we prove the theorem. This involves writing the sum over the extrema as the integral

\sum_{0<\gamma\leq T}\max_{\gamma\leq t\leq\gamma^{+}}\left|\zeta\left(\frac{1}{2}+it\right)\right|^{2}=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\mathcal{C}}\frac{Z_{1}^{\prime}}{Z_{1}}(s)\zeta(s)\zeta(1-s)\ ds

(up to a small error), where
\mathcal{C}
is a contour we specify later. We note at this point one of the main differences in our method with that of Conrey and Ghosh. Their method forces them to consider their integral over a short height (from
T
to
T+T^{3/4}
), whereas we are able to define our contour across a full height of length
T
, avoiding any complications arising from summing over dyadic intervals.

We begin by showing various parts of the respective contour integrals are small, and applying the twisted second moment result mentioned previously. The bulk of the argument then comes down to evaluating

\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{c+i}^{c+iT}\chi(1-s)\frac{Z_{1}^{\prime}}{Z_{1}}(s)\zeta(s)^{2}\ ds

for some
c>1
that we define later.

We apply various arithmetic identities to the logarithmic derivative of
Z_{1}(s)
in the integrand, giving a series of the form

\frac{Z_{1}^{\prime}}{Z_{1}}(s)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{a(n,s)}{n^{s}}

which converges for some
c>1
with coefficients
a(n,s)
that we specify later. Using this and the Dirichlet series for the square of the zeta function allows us to apply the method of stationary phase. That is, we can rewrite the integral as

-\sum_{nm\leq T/2\pi}\Lambda(n)d(m)+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}2^{k}\sum_{nm\leq T/2\pi}\frac{a_{k}(n)}{(\log nm)^{k}}d(m)

up to some small errors, where the
a_{k}(n)
come from the coefficients
a(n,s)
. We then apply Perron’s formula to turn the sums back into integrals, where the first summation is standard and the second summation over
k
is related to

\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{(c)}\left(\frac{\zeta^{\prime}}{\zeta}(s)\right)^{\prime}\left(-\frac{\zeta^{\prime}}{\zeta}(s)\right)^{k-1}\zeta^{2}(s)\frac{x^{s}}{s}\ ds,

and we complete the argument with partial summation. It is at this point that we are able to highlight the second main difference between our approach and that of Conrey and Ghosh, which leads to the correct lower-order term expansion, and explains why their approach must fail to give the correct expansion.

In Section
6
, we look at some numerical data related to our result, and describe how we were able to perform these numerical calculations. Finally, we plot some data to show how perfectly our results fit the truth, as well as how many terms in the asymptotic are required until it is clear that we are mostly just looking at error terms (even though there are infinitely many more terms left in the asymptotic expansion).

4.
Preliminary Lemmas

To motivate the initial steps of the proof, assuming the Riemann Hypothesis, the points that we wish to sum over are the real zeros of the derivative of Hardy’s function. In the complex plane, we can write Hardy’s function as

Z(s)=\chi(1-s)^{1/2}\zeta(s)

where
\chi(s)
is the term in the functional equation for zeta, which satisfies
\chi(s)\chi(1-s)=1
. Logarithmically differentiating we get

\displaystyle\frac{Z^{\prime}}{Z}(s)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\frac{\chi^{\prime}}{\chi}(1-s)+\frac{\zeta^{\prime}}{\zeta}(s)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\frac{\chi^{\prime}}{\chi}(s)+\frac{\zeta^{\prime}}{\zeta}(s)

This suggests an appropriate function to consider is

Z_{1}(s)=\zeta^{\prime}(s)-\frac{1}{2}\frac{\chi^{\prime}}{\chi}(s)\zeta(s).

We see that
Z_{1}(s)
is zero where
Z^{\prime}(t)=0
, that is, at an extrema of
|\zeta(s)|
on the critical line.

This function satisfies various properties, all of which are proved by Conrey and Ghosh in the Lemma in
[
7
]
. We refer the reader to their paper for the proof, and list only the properties that we need here.

Lemma 1
.

We have the following properties for
Z_{1}(s)
:

(1)

|Z_{1}(1/2+it)|=|Z^{\prime}(t)|
.

(2)

Z_{1}(s)
satisfies the functional equation

Z_{1}(s)=-\chi(s)Z_{1}(1-s)

for all
s
, where
\chi(s)
is the term in the functional equation for the Riemann zeta function,
\zeta(s)=\chi(s)\zeta(1-s)
.

(3)

The number of zeros of
Z_{1}(t)
up to a height
T
which lie off the critical line is bounded by
\log T
.

(4)

If
\ Z_{1}(\beta_{1}+i\gamma_{1})=0
then

\left|\beta_{1}-\frac{1}{2}\right|\leq\frac{1}{9}

for
\gamma_{1}
sufficiently large.

We will also need to calculate a twisted second moment of the Riemann zeta function.

Lemma 2
.

(4.1)

-\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{1}^{T}\frac{\chi^{\prime}}{\chi}\left(\frac{1}{2}+it\right)\left|\zeta\left(\frac{1}{2}+it\right)\right|^{2}\ dt=\\
\frac{T}{2\pi}\left(\log\frac{T}{2\pi}\right)^{2}+\frac{T}{2\pi}\log\frac{T}{2\pi}(-2+2\gamma_{0})+\frac{T}{2\pi}(2-2\gamma_{0})+O\left(T^{1/2+\varepsilon}\right),

where
\gamma_{0}
is Euler’s constant.

Proof.

We begin with the estimate, which can be found in
[
7
]
, for example,

(4.2)

\frac{\chi^{\prime}}{\chi}(s)=-\log\frac{|t|}{2\pi}+O\left(\frac{1}{1+|t|}\right),

where
s=\sigma+it
, with
|\sigma|\leq 2
.

Recall also the second moment of the Riemann zeta function, due to Ingham
[
19
]
, that

\int_{1}^{T}\left|\zeta\left(\frac{1}{2}+it\right)\right|^{2}\ dt=T\log\frac{T}{2\pi}+(2\gamma_{0}-1)T+O\left(T^{1/2+\varepsilon}\right).

Integration by parts then gives the result of the lemma.
∎

Lemma 3
.

We have the following two Möbius identities:

\sum_{d\mid n}\mu(d)\log(\tfrac{n}{d})=\Lambda(n)

and Selberg’s identity

\sum_{d\mid n}\mu(d)\log^{2}(\tfrac{n}{d})=\Lambda(n)\log(n)+(\Lambda\ast\Lambda)(n)

where
\mu(n)
is the Möbius function and
\Lambda(n)
is the von Mangoldt function.

We will also need the logarithmic derivative for
Z_{1}(s)
, which we note here for completeness as

(4.3)

\frac{Z_{1}^{\prime}}{Z_{1}}(s)=\frac{\chi^{\prime}}{\chi}(s)-\frac{Z_{1}^{\prime}}{Z_{1}}(1-s).

This follows from (2) in Lemma
1
.

Finally, we need a Dirichlet series for this logarithmic derivative of
Z_{1}(s)
, or at least an expansion that looks like a Dirichlet series.

Lemma 4
.

For
\mathfrak{Re}(s)>1
and
t\geq 100
, we have

\frac{Z_{1}^{\prime}}{Z_{1}}(s)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{a(n,s)}{n^{s}}+O\left(\frac{1}{t\log t}\right)

where

a(n,s)=-\Lambda(n)+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{f(s)^{k}}a_{k}(n)

with

(4.4)

f(s)=-\frac{1}{2}\frac{\chi^{\prime}}{\chi}(s)

and
a_{k}(n)
is of the form

(4.5)

a_{k}(n)=\left((\Lambda\log)\ast\Lambda_{k-1}\right)(n),

where we use the notation
\Lambda_{k}
to denote

\underbrace{\Lambda\ast\Lambda\ast\Lambda\ast\dots\ast\Lambda}_{k-1\text{ convolutions}}

with the convention that
\Lambda_{0}(n)
takes the value
1
if
n=1
and
0
otherwise, and where
\Lambda_{1}(n)=\Lambda(n)
is the usual von Mangoldt function, and for clarity
\Lambda_{2}(n)=(\Lambda\ast\Lambda)(n)
.

Remark
.

This way of writing the logarithmic derivative of
Z_{1}(s)
is another point of deviation from Conrey and Ghosh’s method. They set
f(s)=\frac{1}{2}\log T
and treat this as a constant. By keeping
f(s)
exact, we are able to retain more information for the asymptotic, as we will see later in the argument.

Proof.

We begin by writing

Z_{1}(s)=\zeta^{\prime}(s)+f(s)\zeta(s)

where
f(s)=-\frac{1}{2}\frac{\chi^{\prime}}{\chi}(s)
. Taking logarithmic derivatives we have

(4.6)

\frac{Z_{1}^{\prime}}{Z_{1}}(s)=\frac{\zeta^{\prime\prime}(s)+f(s)\zeta^{\prime}(s)}{\zeta^{\prime}(s)+f(s)\zeta(s)}+f^{\prime}(s)\frac{1}{\frac{\zeta^{\prime}}{\zeta}(s)+f(s)}

For
\mathfrak{Re}(s)>1
with
t\geq 1
, the last term can be bounded by
O(1/t\log t)
by noting that
f^{\prime}(s)=O(1/t)
(which follows from (
4.2
)),
f(s)=O(\log t)
and
\zeta^{\prime}/\zeta(s)=O(\log\log t)
(due to Littlewood
[
24
]
, under the Riemann Hypothesis) in the region under consideration.

For
s
with sufficiently large real part, it is sensible to factor the denominator as

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\zeta^{\prime}(s)+f(s)\zeta(s)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{f(s)}\frac{1}{\zeta(s)}\left(1+\frac{1}{f(s)}\frac{\zeta^{\prime}}{\zeta}(s)\right)^{-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{f(s)}\frac{1}{\zeta(s)}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{f(s)^{j}}\left(-\frac{\zeta^{\prime}}{\zeta}(s)\right)^{j}

where we have expanded the last term on the first line as a geometric series. This expansion will be valid so long as
\mathfrak{Re}(s)
is large enough so that
\left|\frac{1}{f(s)}\frac{\zeta^{\prime}}{\zeta}(s)\right|\leq 1
. That is, we can take
\mathfrak{Re}(s)>1
and
t\geq 100
.

Since
\mathfrak{Re}(s)>1
, we may substitute the following Dirichlet series into the first term on the right hand side of (
4.6
)

\displaystyle\zeta^{\prime\prime}(s)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\log^{2}(n)}{n^{s}}

\displaystyle\zeta^{\prime}(s)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\log(n)}{n^{s}}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\zeta(s)}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\mu(n)}{n^{s}}

\displaystyle-\frac{\zeta^{\prime}}{\zeta}(s)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\Lambda(n)}{n^{s}}

which gives

\frac{\zeta^{\prime\prime}(s)+f(s)\zeta^{\prime}(s)}{\zeta^{\prime}(s)+f(s)\zeta(s)}=\left(\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\log^{2}n}{n^{s}}-f(s)\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\log n}{n^{s}}\right)\frac{1}{f(s)}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\mu(n)}{n^{s}}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{f(s)^{j}}\left(\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\Lambda(n)}{n^{s}}\right)^{j}.

We now employ the two Möbius identities from Lemma
3
to give

(4.7)

\left(\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\log^{2}n}{n^{s}}-f(s)\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\log n}{n^{s}}\right)\frac{1}{f(s)}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\mu(n)}{n^{s}}\\
=\frac{1}{f(s)}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\Lambda(n)\log(n)}{n^{s}}+\frac{1}{f(s)}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\Lambda_{2}(n)}{n^{s}}-\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\Lambda(n)}{n^{s}}.

Finally, consider the Dirichlet series coming from the geometric expansion. They will consist of multiple convolutions, and recall we write
\Lambda_{k}
to denote
k-1
convolutions of
\Lambda
with itself. This means

\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{f(s)^{j}}\left(\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\Lambda(n)}{n^{s}}\right)^{j}=1+\frac{1}{f(s)}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\Lambda(n)}{n^{s}}+\frac{1}{f(s)^{2}}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\Lambda_{2}(n)}{n^{s}}\\
+\frac{1}{f(s)^{3}}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\Lambda_{3}(n)}{n^{s}}+\frac{1}{f(s)^{4}}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\Lambda_{4}(n)}{n^{s}}+\dots{}

Consider the last two Dirichlet sums in (
4.7
) multiplied with the above terms coming from the geometric expansion. Note they telescope nicely. Using the above, we have

\left(\frac{1}{f(s)}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\Lambda_{2}(n)}{n^{s}}-\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\Lambda(n)}{n^{s}}\right)\left(1+\frac{1}{f(s)}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\Lambda(n)}{n^{s}}+\frac{1}{f(s)^{2}}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\Lambda_{2}(n)}{n^{s}}+\dots\right)\\
=\frac{1}{f(s)}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\Lambda_{2}(n)}{n^{s}}+\frac{1}{f^{2}(s)}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\Lambda_{3}(n)}{n^{s}}+\frac{1}{f(s)^{3}}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\Lambda_{4}(n)}{n^{s}}+\dots{\\
}-\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\Lambda(n)}{n^{s}}-\frac{1}{f(s)}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\Lambda_{2}(n)}{n^{s}}-\frac{1}{f^{2}(s)}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\Lambda_{3}(n)}{n^{s}}-\dots=-\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\Lambda(n)}{n^{s}}

Returning to (

Putting everything together we have

\frac{Z_{1}^{\prime}}{Z_{1}}(s)=-\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\Lambda(n)}{n^{s}}+\frac{1}{f(s)}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\Lambda(n)\log(n)}{n^{s}}+\frac{1}{f(s)^{2}}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\left((\Lambda\cdot\log)\ast\Lambda\right)(n)}{n^{s}}\\
+\frac{1}{f(s)^{3}}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\left((\Lambda\cdot\log)\ast\Lambda_{2}\right)(n)}{n^{s}}+\frac{1}{f(s)^{4}}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\left((\Lambda\cdot\log)\ast\Lambda_{3}\right)(n)}{n^{s}}+\dots+O\left(\frac{1}{t\log t}\right).

∎

5.
Proof of Theorem
1

5.1.
Setting up the proof

The start of our proof follows that of Conrey and Ghosh
[
7
]
but we recreate it here for completeness sake. As we have already noted, under the Riemann Hypothesis the zeros of
Z^{\prime}(t)
are interlaced with those of
Z(t)
, and so the set of points where
|\zeta(1/2+it)|
achieves its maxima are exactly the points where
Z^{\prime}(t)=0
. We know that
Z_{1}(1/2+it)=0
if and only if
Z^{\prime}(t)=0
by part (1) of Lemma
1
.

Let
\rho_{1}=\beta_{1}+i\gamma_{1}
be the zeros of
Z_{1}(s)
. By part (3) of Lemma
1

Z_{1}(s)
has
O(\log T)
zeros off the critical line (that is,
\beta_{1}\neq 1/2
). At these zeros, using part (4) of Lemma
1
and the Lindelöf bounds on zeta, we have

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0<\gamma_{1}\leq T\\
\beta_{1}\neq 1/2\end{subarray}}\zeta(\rho_{1})\zeta(1-\rho_{1})\ll T^{1/9+\epsilon}T^{\epsilon}\log T\ll T^{1/8}.

Therefore if
\gamma
and
\gamma^{+}
denote consecutive zeros of the zeta function (which under the Riemann Hypothesis are real zeros of
Z(t)
) we have

\sum_{0<\gamma\leq T}\max_{\gamma\leq t\leq\gamma^{+}}\left|\zeta\left(\frac{1}{2}+it\right)\right|^{2}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0<\gamma_{1}\leq T\\
\beta_{1}=1/2\end{subarray}}\left|\zeta\left(\frac{1}{2}+i\gamma_{1}\right)\right|^{2}.

We can write this as an integral using Cauchy’s theorem,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\mathcal{C}}\frac{Z_{1}^{\prime}}{Z_{1}}(s)\zeta(s)\zeta(1-s)\ ds

\displaystyle=\sum_{0<\gamma_{1}\leq T}\zeta\left(\rho_{1}\right)\zeta\left(1-\rho_{1}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0<\gamma_{1}\leq T\\
\beta_{1}=1/2\end{subarray}}\left|\zeta\left(\frac{1}{2}+i\gamma_{1}\right)\right|^{2}+O\left(T^{1/8}\right)

where
\mathcal{C}
is a positively oriented contour with vertices
c+i
,
c+iT
,
1-c+iT
, and
1-c+i
, where
c=1+1/\log T
. We may assume, without loss of generality, that the distance from the contour to any zero
\rho_{1}
of
Z_{1}(s)
is uniformly
\gg 1/\log T
.

Remark
.

Note that this contour
\mathcal{C}
is the first time we diverge from the method of Conrey and Ghosh
[
7
]
, since their
c
is such that the contour lies inside the critical strip, and they go from
T
to
T+U
, where
U=T^{3/4}
. They do this because in their definition of
Z_{1}(s)
they use the approximation

-\frac{1}{2}\frac{\chi^{\prime}}{\chi}(s)\approx\frac{1}{2}\log\frac{T}{2\pi}

to obtain

Z_{1}(s)\approx\zeta^{\prime}(s)+\frac{1}{2}\log\frac{T}{2\pi}\zeta(s).

This approximation for
\chi^{\prime}/\chi(s)
relies on
T
staying roughly the same size. They then use dyadic sums to get their result for length
T
. Since we keep the factor of
\chi^{\prime}/\chi(s)
until late in our proof, we are able to take the height of our contour of length
T
and avoid any potential issues with dyadic summation.

5.2.
Initial manipulations of the Cauchy integral

By considering the Hadamard product for
Z_{1}(s)
(which has
N(T)
zeros on the critical line and
O(\log T)
zeros off the critical line), one can show that

\frac{Z_{1}^{\prime}}{Z_{1}}(s)=\sum_{|s-\rho_{1}|<1}\frac{1}{s-\rho}+O(\log T)

so if
|T-\rho_{1}|\gg 1/\log T
for all zeros
\rho_{1}
of
Z_{1}
, we have

\frac{Z_{1}^{\prime}}{Z_{1}}(\sigma+iT)\ll(\log T)^{2}

uniformly for
-1<\sigma\leq 2
.

Therefore, using the Lindelöf bounds, the integral along the horizontal sides of the contour are bounded by
O\left(T^{c-1/2+\epsilon}\right)
.

All that remains to calculate are the two vertical sides. That is,

\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\mathcal{C}}\frac{Z_{1}^{\prime}}{Z_{1}}(s)\zeta(s)\zeta(1-s)\ ds\\
=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{c+i}^{c+iT}\frac{Z_{1}^{\prime}}{Z_{1}}(s)\chi(1-s)\zeta^{2}(s)\ ds+\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{1-c+i}^{1-c+iT}\frac{Z_{1}^{\prime}}{Z_{1}}(s)\chi(s)\zeta^{2}(1-s)\ ds+O\left(T^{c-1/2+\epsilon}\right)\\
=I_{1}+I_{2}+O\left(T^{c-1/2+\epsilon}\right),

where we have used the functional equation for
\zeta(s)
in each integral.

Next, we use (
4.3
), that is, the logarithmic derivative of the functional equation for
Z_{1}(s)
, together with using the change of variables
s\mapsto 1-s
to write

I_{2}=\overline{I_{1}}-\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{1-c+it}^{1-c+iT}\frac{\chi^{\prime}}{\chi}(s)\zeta(s)\zeta(1-s)\ ds.

Therefore we have

\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\mathcal{C}}\frac{Z_{1}^{\prime}}{Z_{1}}(s)\zeta(s)\zeta(1-s)\ ds=2\mathfrak{Re}(I_{1})-\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{1-c+it}^{1-c+iT}\frac{\chi^{\prime}}{\chi}(s)\zeta(s)\zeta(1-s)\ ds+O\left(T^{c-1/2+\epsilon}\right).

We may now move the line of integration in the twisted integral to the half-line, obtaining

-\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{1}^{T}\frac{\chi^{\prime}}{\chi}\left(\frac{1}{2}+it\right)\left|\zeta\left(\frac{1}{2}+it\right)\right|^{2}\ dt+O\left(T^{c-1/2+\epsilon}\right).

This is exactly the second twisted moment integral evaluated in Lemma
2
.

Remark
.

Here is a trivial point of deviation from Conrey and Ghosh
[
7
]
. Since they are only after leading order behaviour, they ignore the lower-order terms from the twisted second moment, whereas since we are after a full asymptotic, we are obviously required to keep track of all lower-order terms throughout the argument.

Therefore, to complete the argument, we need to calculate
2\mathfrak{Re}(I_{1})
.

5.3.
Calculating the main terms

We begin this subsection by giving a rough overview of what we are aiming to do here as it is the most complicated part of the argument. Since we are to the right of the abscissa of absolute convergence for our
L
-functions, we can write them as their respective Dirichlet series. After this, we use the method of stationary phase to write each integral as a sum of length
T
. Finally, we use Perron’s formula to rewrite these sums as an integral, and after shifting the integral past the pole at
s=1
, we may collect residues to obtain a full asymptotic.

We already know that we can write

(5.1)

\zeta^{2}(s)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{d(n)}{n^{s}}

for
\mathfrak{Re}(s)>1
, and where
d(n)=\sum_{d|n}1
.

We have shown that in Lemma
4
for
\mathfrak{Re}(s)>1
and
t\geq 100
that

\frac{Z_{1}^{\prime}}{Z_{1}}(s)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{a(n,s)}{n^{s}}

where

a(n,s)=-\Lambda(n)+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{f(s)^{k}}a_{k}(n)

where
a_{k}(n)
is given by (
4.5
) and
f(s)
is given by (
4.4
).

While the integral
I_{1}
has been from
c+i
to
c+iT
, we now take it from
c+100i
to
c+iT
at the cost of an error of size
O(1)
.

By absolute convergence, we can swap the integral and the sum over
k
, obtaining

\displaystyle I_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{c+100i}^{c+iT}\chi(1-s)\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{-\Lambda(n)}{n^{s}}\zeta(s)^{2}\ ds

\displaystyle\qquad+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{c+100i}^{c+iT}\chi(1-s)\frac{1}{f(s)^{k}}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{a_{k}(n)}{n^{s}}\zeta(s)^{2}\ ds

\displaystyle=J_{0}+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}J_{k},

say.

Lemma 6
.

Under the Riemann Hypothesis we have

2J_{0}-\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{1}^{T}\frac{\chi^{\prime}}{\chi}\left(\frac{1}{2}+it\right)\left|\zeta\left(\frac{1}{2}+it\right)\right|^{2}\ dt=O\left(T^{1/2+\epsilon}\right).

Proof.

Extending
J_{0}
back to be over
1
to
T
, and remembering that
c=1+1/\log T
we may use a slight variation of Gonek’s Lemma 5
[
14
]
to write the integral
J_{0}
as

J_{0}=-\sum_{nm\leq T/2\pi}\Lambda(n)d(m)+O\left(T^{1/2+\epsilon}\right).

By Perron’s formula we have

-\sum_{nm\leq x}\Lambda(n)d(m)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{3/2-iT}^{3/2+iT}\frac{\zeta^{\prime}}{\zeta}(s)\zeta(s)^{2}\frac{x^{s}}{s}\ ds+O\left(T^{1/2+\epsilon}\right)

where
x=T/2\pi
.

By the standard methods of shifting the contour to the line with
\mathfrak{Re}(s)=1/2+1/\log T
, and evaluating the residue from the pole at
s=1
, we have

(5.2)

J_{0}=-\frac{T}{4\pi}\left(\log\frac{T}{2\pi}\right)^{2}+\frac{T}{2\pi}\log\frac{T}{2\pi}(1-\gamma_{0})+\frac{T}{2\pi}(-1+\gamma_{0})+O(T^{1/2+\varepsilon})

where
\gamma_{0}
is Euler’s constant.

By Lemma
2
,

\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{1}^{T}\frac{\chi^{\prime}}{\chi}\left(\frac{1}{2}+it\right)\left|\zeta\left(\frac{1}{2}+it\right)\right|^{2}\ dt\\
=\frac{T}{2\pi}\left(\log\frac{T}{2\pi}\right)^{2}+\frac{T}{2\pi}\log\frac{T}{2\pi}(-2+2\gamma_{0})+\frac{T}{2\pi}(2-2\gamma_{0})+O\left(T^{1/2+\varepsilon}\right).

This perfectly matches double the evaluation of
J_{0}
given in (
5.2
), thus proving the lemma.
∎

That is, we have shown that

(5.3)

\sum_{0<\gamma\leq T}\max_{\gamma\leq t\leq\gamma^{+}}\left|\zeta\left(\frac{1}{2}+it\right)\right|^{2}=2\mathfrak{Re}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}J_{k}+O\left(T^{1/2+\varepsilon}\right)

where

(5.4)

J_{k}=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{c+100i}^{c+iT}\chi(1-s)\frac{1}{f(s)^{k}}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{a_{k}(n)}{n^{s}}\zeta(s)^{2}\ ds

where the
a_{k}(n)
are given in (
4.5
) and
f(s)
is given in (
4.4
).

Lemma 7
.

We can write

J_{k}=2^{k}\sum_{nm\leq T/2\pi}\frac{a_{k}(n)}{(\log nm)^{k}}d(m)+O\left(T^{1/2+\varepsilon}\right).

where
a_{k}(n)
are given in (
4.5
) and
d(m)
is the Divisor function.

Proof.

First note that extending the integral to go from
c+i
rather than
c+100i
only introduces an error of size
O(1)
which we will ignore as it will be consumed by other larger error terms. We begin by rewriting (
5.4
) with
\zeta(s)^{2}
written as its Dirichlet series expansion. That is,

J_{k}=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{c+i}^{c+iT}\chi(1-s)\frac{1}{f(s)^{k}}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{a_{k}(n)}{n^{s}}\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\frac{d(m)}{m^{s}}\ ds,

where
d(m)
is the divisor counting function, with
d(m)=\sum_{d|m}1
. Also, by (
4.2
) and (
4.4
), we can write

f(s)=-\frac{1}{2}\frac{\chi^{\prime}}{\chi}(s)=\frac{1}{2}\log\frac{t}{2\pi}+O\left(\frac{1}{t}\right)

and so

\frac{1}{f(s)^{k}}=\frac{2^{k}}{(\log t/2\pi)^{k}}\left(1+O\left(\frac{1}{t\log t}\right)\right)^{-k}=\frac{2^{k}}{(\log t/2\pi)^{k}}\left(1+O\left(\frac{k}{t\log t}\right)\right).

Then

\displaystyle J_{k}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{c+i}^{c+iT}\chi(1-s)\frac{2^{k}}{(\log t/2\pi)^{k}}\left(1+O\left(\frac{k}{t\log t}\right)\right)\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{a_{k}(n)}{n^{s}}\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\frac{d(m)}{m^{s}}\ ds

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{c+i}^{c+iT}\chi(1-s)\frac{2^{k}}{(\log t/2\pi)^{k}}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{a_{k}(n)}{n^{s}}\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\frac{d(m)}{m^{s}}\ ds+O\left(T^{1/2+\varepsilon}\right).

By the method of stationary phase as given in Lemma
5
, the result follows.
∎

The issue is now clear - there is no obvious Dirichlet series for this sum for which we can apply Perron directly. Instead, we will use evaluate the partial sum of
a_{k}\ast d
using Peron’s formula, and then use partial summation to calculate
J_{k}
.

Remark
.

Note that since we have kept
f(s)
exact throughout this argument, we have genuine terms from the integral part of partial summation (which involes
f^{\prime}(s)
). This contributes some subleading terms to the asymptotic but since these are only subleading, it will be clear why Conrey and Ghosh are able to set it equal to a constant and obtain their leading order behaviour (which we reaffirm in our argument).

Lemma 8
.

Let

A_{k}(x)=\sum_{1\leq n_{1}n_{2}...n_{k}m\leq x}\log(n_{1})\Lambda(n_{1})\Lambda(n_{2})\dots\Lambda(n_{k})d(m).

which is the partial summation of
(a_{k}\ast d)(n)
for
n
up to
x
, where
a_{k}
is given by (
4.5
).

Then for large
x
,

A_{k}(x)=x\sum_{j=0}^{k+2}\frac{c_{k,j}}{(k+2-j)!}(\log x)^{k+2-j}+O\left(x^{1/2+\epsilon}\right)

where the
c_{k,\ell}
are the Laurent series coefficients around
s=1
of

\left(\frac{\zeta^{\prime}}{\zeta}(s)\right)^{\prime}\left(-\frac{\zeta^{\prime}}{\zeta}(s)\right)^{k-1}\zeta^{2}(s)\frac{1}{s}=\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}c_{k,\ell}(s-1)^{-k-3+\ell}.

Remark
.

A long but straightforward calculation shows that the first values of
c_{k,\ell}
are

\displaystyle c_{k,0}

\displaystyle=1

\displaystyle c_{k,1}

\displaystyle=-1-(k-3)\gamma_{0}

\displaystyle c_{k,2}

\displaystyle=1+(k-3)\gamma_{0}+\frac{1}{2}(k-1)(k-4)\gamma_{0}^{2}+2(k-3)\gamma_{1}

Here
\gamma_{0}
is the Euler–Mascheroni constant and
\gamma_{1}
is the first Steiltjes constant.

Proof.

By the method of Perron, we have

A_{k}(x)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{3/2-iR}^{3/2+iR}\left(\frac{\zeta^{\prime}}{\zeta}(s)\right)^{\prime}\left(-\frac{\zeta^{\prime}}{\zeta}(s)\right)^{k-1}\zeta^{2}(s)\frac{x^{s}}{s}\ ds+O\left(\frac{x^{3/2+\epsilon}}{R}\right).

We now calculate
A_{k}(x)
by shifting past the pole at
s=1
to the line
\mathfrak{Re}(s)=1/2+\epsilon
, and evaluating the residue there.

Note that the integrand has a pole of order
k+3
at
s=1
. Therefore, up to a power saving error term,
A_{k}(x)
will equal the residue of the integrand around that point, which means

A_{k}(x)=x\sum_{j=0}^{k+2}b_{k,j}(\log x)^{k+2-j}+O\left(R^{\epsilon}x^{1/2+\epsilon}\right)+O\left(\frac{x^{3/2+\epsilon}}{R}\right)

for some constants
b_{k,j}
. (Note we have chosen to start the sum at the largest power of
\log x
, as this simplifies some later calculations). Taking
R=x
optimises the error terms to be
O(x^{1/2+\epsilon})
.

To evaluate the residues, we employ the various Laurent expansions about
s=1
in the various terms in the integrand. We give the ones we need for ease of reference. First, define the Stieltjes gamma constants to come from the series expansion of zeta around
s=1
, namely

\zeta(s)=\frac{1}{s-1}+\gamma_{0}-\gamma_{1}(s-1)+\frac{1}{2!}\gamma_{2}(s-1)^{2}+\dots+(-1)^{j}\frac{1}{j!}\gamma_{j}(s-1)^{j}+\dots

Then the expansions we need are

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\zeta^{\prime}}{\zeta}(s)\right)^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\quad

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(s-1)^{2}}+\left(-2\gamma_{1}-\gamma_{0}^{2}\right)+\left(6\gamma_{0}\gamma_{1}+3\gamma_{2}+2\gamma_{0}^{3}\right)(s-1)+\dots

\displaystyle\left(-\frac{\zeta^{\prime}}{\zeta}(s)\right)^{k-1}

\displaystyle=\quad

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(s-1)^{k-1}}+\frac{(1-k)\gamma_{0}}{(s-1)^{k-2}}+\frac{\frac{1}{2}k(k-1)\gamma_{0}^{2}+2(k-1)\gamma_{1}}{(s-1)^{k-3}}+\dots

\displaystyle\zeta^{2}(s)

\displaystyle=\quad

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(s-1)^{2}}+\frac{2\gamma_{0}}{s-1}+\left(\gamma_{0}^{2}-2\gamma_{1}\right)+\dots

\displaystyle\frac{1}{s}

\displaystyle=\quad

\displaystyle 1-(s-1)+(s-1)^{2}+...

Combining these Laurent expansions allows us to write

\left(\frac{\zeta^{\prime}}{\zeta}(s)\right)^{\prime}\left(-\frac{\zeta^{\prime}}{\zeta}(s)\right)^{k-1}\zeta^{2}(s)\frac{1}{s}=\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}c_{k,\ell}(s-1)^{-k-3+\ell}

where we have explicitly given the first few values in the remark following the statement of the lemma.

The residue is the coefficient of
1/(s-1)
which will come from combining these terms with the
x^{s}
in the integrand (which is the only term that contains an
x
). Its expansion about
s-1
is

x^{s}=x\left(1+(s-1)\log x+\frac{(s-1)^{2}}{2!}(\log x)^{2}+...+\frac{(s-1)^{k}}{k!}(\log x)^{k}+...\right)

so the coefficient of
(\log x)^{k+2-j}
in the
(s-1)^{-1}
term equals

b_{k,j}=\frac{c_{k,j}}{(k+2-j)!}

and such a combination is possible for
j
between
0
and
k+2
.
∎

Recall by Lemma
7

\displaystyle J_{k}

\displaystyle=2^{k}\sum_{2\leq nm\leq T/2\pi}\frac{a_{k}(n)}{(\log nm)^{k}}d(m)+O\left(T^{1/2+\varepsilon}\right)

\displaystyle=2^{k}\sum_{2\leq n\leq T/2\pi}\frac{(a_{k}\ast d)(n)}{(\log n)^{k}}+O\left(T^{1/2+\varepsilon}\right)

so we can use partial summation together with with Lemma
8
to evaluate
J_{k}
.

Lemma 9
.

Writing
L=\log\frac{T}{2\pi}
, for any
N\geq 1
we have

J_{k}=\frac{T}{2\pi}\left(\frac{2^{k}}{(k+2)!}L^{2}+\beta_{k,-1}L+\beta_{k,0}+\sum_{n=1}^{N}\beta_{k,n}L^{-n}\right)+O\left(\frac{T}{L^{N+1}}\right)

where

\beta_{k,-1}=\frac{2^{k}}{(k+2)!}\left(-2-(k+2)(k-3)\gamma_{0}\right),

and

\beta_{k,0}=\frac{2^{k}\left((k+2)(k+1)c_{k,2}-kc_{k,0}+k(k+2)c_{k,1}\right)}{(k+2)!}

and for
1\leq n\leq k

\beta_{k,n}=\frac{2^{k}c_{k,n+2}}{(k-n)!}+\frac{2^{k}(n-1)!}{(k-1)!}\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\binom{k}{j}c_{k,j+2}

and for
n\geq k+1
,

\beta_{k,n}=\frac{2^{k}(n-1)!}{(k-1)!}\sum_{j=0}^{k}\binom{k}{j}c_{k,j+2}

where the
c_{k,\ell}
are given in Lemma
8

Proof.

Set
f(x)=1/(\log x)^{k}
, so
f^{\prime}(x)=-k/(x(\log x)^{k+1})
, and by partial summation we have

\displaystyle J_{k}

\displaystyle=2^{k}A_{k}\left(\frac{T}{2\pi}\right)f\left(\frac{T}{2\pi}\right)-2^{k}\int_{2}^{T/2\pi}A_{k}(x)f^{\prime}(x)\ dx+O\left(T^{1/2+\epsilon}\right)

\displaystyle=2^{k}\frac{T}{2\pi}\sum_{j=0}^{k+2}\frac{c_{k,j}}{(k+2-j)!}L^{2-j}+2^{k}k\sum_{j=0}^{k+2}\frac{c_{k,j}}{(k+2-j)!}\int_{2}^{T/2\pi}(\log x)^{1-j}dx+O\left(T^{1/2+\epsilon}\right)

where
L=\log\frac{T}{2\pi}
, and where

A_{k}(x)=\sum_{n\leq x}(a_{k}\ast d)(n),

which was evaluated in Lemma
8
.

Note the dominant term (that is,
L^{2}
) comes from the first sum only, whereas subleading and later terms also have contributions from the second sum. For
j=0
and
j=1
in the second sum, one can perform the integrals exactly, obtaining

(5.5)

J_{k}=\frac{T}{2\pi}\left(\frac{2^{k}c_{k,0}}{(k+2)!}L^{2}+\left[\frac{2^{k}c_{k,1}}{(k+1)!}+\frac{k2^{k}c_{k,0}}{(k+2)!}\right]L+\left[\frac{2^{k}c_{k,2}}{k!}-\frac{k2^{k}c_{k,0}}{(k+2)!}+\frac{k2^{k}c_{k,1}}{(k+1)!}\right]\right)\\
+\frac{T}{2\pi}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\frac{2^{k}c_{k,j+2}}{(k-j)!}\frac{1}{L^{j}}+\sum_{j=1}^{k+1}\frac{k2^{k}c_{k,j+1}}{(k+1-j)!}\int_{2}^{T/2\pi}\frac{1}{(\log x)^{j}}dx+O\left(T^{1/2+\epsilon}\right)

where we have relabeled the sums on the second line to start from
j=1
now. Note that the remaining integrals are all negative powers of
\log x
, so evaluate to an infinite chain of decreasing powers of
L
, for example for
1\leq j\leq N
, we have

\displaystyle\int_{2}^{T/2\pi}\frac{1}{(\log x)^{j}}\ dx

\displaystyle=\frac{T}{2\pi L^{j}}+j\int_{2}^{T/2\pi}\frac{1}{(\log x)^{j+1}}\ dx+O(1)

\displaystyle=\frac{T}{2\pi}\sum_{\ell=j}^{N}\frac{(\ell-1)!}{(j-1)!}\frac{1}{L^{\ell}}+O\left(\frac{T}{L^{N+1}}\right).

Finally,
\beta_{k,n}
collects all the coefficients of
L^{-n}
. For example

\displaystyle\beta_{k,-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{2^{k}c_{k,1}}{(k+1)!}+\frac{k2^{k}c_{k,0}}{(k+2)!}

\displaystyle=-\frac{2^{k}}{(k+2)!}\left(2+(k+2)(k-3)\gamma_{0})\right)

where we use the remark following the statement of Lemma
8
, to evaluate
c_{k,0}
and
c_{k,1}
.

If
n\leq k
then there will be a contribution from the second line of (
5.5
) coming from the first sum (with
j=n
) and from all the integrals in the second sum with
j
between
1
and
n
. We have (for
n\leq k
)

\displaystyle\beta_{k,n}

\displaystyle=\frac{2^{k}c_{k,n+2}}{(k-n)!}+\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{k2^{k}c_{k,j+1}}{(k+1-j)!}\frac{(n-1)!}{(j-1)!}

\displaystyle=\frac{2^{k}c_{k,n+2}}{(k-n)!}+\sum_{j=1}^{n}\binom{k}{j-1}\frac{2^{k}(n-1)!c_{k,j+1}}{(k-1)!}

whereas if
n\geq k+1
then there will only be contributions coming from the second sum of the second line of (
5.5
), but every term in that sum contributes. We find

\displaystyle\beta_{k,n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{k+1}\frac{k2^{k}c_{k,j+1}}{(k+1-j)!}\frac{(n-1)!}{(j-1)!}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{k+1}\binom{k}{j-1}\frac{2^{k}(n-1)!c_{k,j+1}}{(k-1)!}

These both give the same as in the statement of the lemma, after a trivial relabelling of the index
j
.
∎

Now we have evaluated
J_{k}
, in order to complete the proof of the Theorem, we can see from (
5.3
) that all we must do is sum the contribution from
2J_{k}
over all
k\geq 1
.

We have

(5.6)

\displaystyle\sum_{0<\gamma\leq T}\max_{\gamma\leq t\leq\gamma^{+}}\left|\zeta\left(\frac{1}{2}+it\right)\right|^{2}

\displaystyle=2\mathfrak{Re}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}J_{k}+O\left(T^{1/2+\varepsilon}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{T}{2\pi}\left(\alpha_{-2}L^{2}+\alpha_{-1}L+\alpha_{0}+\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{\alpha_{n}}{L^{N}}\right)+O_{N}\left(\frac{T}{L^{N+1}}\right)

where

\alpha_{-2}=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{2^{k+1}}{(k+2)!}=\frac{e^{2}-5}{2}

\displaystyle\alpha_{-1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{2^{k}}{(k+2)!}\left(-2-(k+2)(k-3)\gamma_{0}\right)

\displaystyle=5-e^{2}-10\gamma_{0}+2e^{2}\gamma_{0}

and

\displaystyle\alpha_{0}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{2^{k+1}\left((k+2)(k+1)c_{k,2}-kc_{k,0}+k(k+2)c_{k,1}\right)}{(k+2)!}

\displaystyle=12\gamma_{1}-4e^{2}\gamma_{1}-5+e^{2}+10\gamma_{0}-2e^{2}\gamma_{0}-4\gamma_{0}^{2}

after substituting the values for
c_{k,\ell}
found in the remark following the statement of Lemma
8
. Note that the
\alpha_{-2}
coefficient recovers the result of Conrey and Ghosh (

). Also,

\alpha_{n}=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}2\beta_{k,n}

with the
\beta_{k,n}
given in the previous lemma. This completes the proof of the Theorem.

Theorem
1
is stated for a fixed stopping point

. It is well known that a typical asymptotic expansion does not converge as an infinite series (hence why they should be treated as finite series), but one can nonetheless allow the truncation to grow with

. In this case we have carried through an additional error of size
O\left(T^{1/2+\epsilon}\right)

O\left(T^{1/2+\epsilon}\right)
.

Crudely estimating the size of
c_{k,\ell}
for large
k
and
\ell
, we find

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{2^{k+1}}{(k-1)!}\sum_{j=0}^{\min\{k,n-1\}}\binom{k}{j}c_{k,j+2}\right|

\displaystyle\ll\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{2^{k+1}}{(k-1)!}\sum_{j=0}^{k}\binom{k}{j}\left|c_{k,j+2}\right|

\displaystyle\ll 1

as
n\to\infty
and the rate of growth of

\sum_{k=n}^{\infty}\frac{2^{k+1}c_{k,n+2}}{(k-n)!}

is bounded by
C^{n}
for some
C>1
, so considerably smaller than
(n-1)!
.

Therefore
|\alpha_{n}|\ll(n-1)!
for large
n
, a result entirely consistent with asymptotic series looking like “factorial over power”.

So for large
T
, one expects to see
\alpha_{n}(\log\frac{t}{2\pi})^{-n}
initially decrease before increasing without bound. Normally, one finds an “optimal truncation point” but for our purposes equation (
5.6
) shows we simply need to find the smallest
N
such that

\frac{\alpha_{N}}{\left(\log\frac{T}{2\pi}\right)^{N}}\ll T^{-1/2+\epsilon}.

Replacing
\alpha_{N}
with
N!
and taking logarithms and using the crudest form of Stirling’s approximation, this will be the smallest
N
such that

N\log N-N-N\log\log T\ll(-1/2+\epsilon)\log T

for large
T
, and this has solution

N=\eta\log T

with
\eta
satisfying
\eta\log\eta-\eta=-1/2
which has a solution
\eta=0.186\dots
(with a larger, irrelevant, solution at
\eta=2.155\dots
).

That is, if we set
N\approx 0.2\log T
, then we expect

\sum_{0<\gamma\leq T}\max_{\gamma\leq t\leq\gamma^{+}}\left|\zeta\left(\frac{1}{2}+it\right)\right|^{2}=\frac{e^{2}-5}{2}\frac{T}{2\pi}L^{2}+\frac{T}{2\pi}\sum_{n=-1}^{N}\frac{\alpha_{n}}{L^{n}}+O\left(T^{1/2+\epsilon}\right).
