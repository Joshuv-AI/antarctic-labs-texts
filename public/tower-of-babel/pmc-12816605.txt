Dynamic graph neural network-based framework to increase detection accuracy in SDN under DDOS
Authors: Saad Ahmed Ali Kalafy, Saied Pashazadeh, Pedram Salehpour
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Software-Defined Networking (SDN) has changed the way networks are managed by providing a centralized and programmable framework. However, it also makes the SDN controller more vulnerable to Distributed Denial of Service (DDoS) attacks, which try to take it down. Current intrusion detection technologies, encompassing conventional machine learning and deep learning models, frequently encounter difficulties in adapting to evolving attack patterns or effectively capturing the intricate topological features inherent in SDN environments. To overcome these limitations, GCTNetwork is proposed as an innovative framework grounded in Dynamic Graph Neural Networks (DGNN) designed to identify DDoS attacks in Software-Defined Networks (SDNs) in real time. GCTNetwork utilizes Gated Convolutional Temporal (GCT) layers for integrated node–edge feature analysis, an Edge-Aware LSTM for temporal dependency modeling, and a Graph Attention Layer (GAT) to emphasize essential communication pathways. Utilizing the SDN Dataset, our evaluation indicates that GCTNetwork delivers reliable and efficient performance, achieving an accuracy of 94.08% and an F1-score of 93.27%, outperforming other advanced SDN-based models such as LR-STGCN, GRAN, and ST-GCN. The model also demonstrates a substantial reliability, as indicated by its low False Alarm Index (FAI) of 0.06, which signifies its effectiveness in reducing unnecessary alerts within dynamic controller environments. Training and validation trends exhibit stable convergence, characterized by progressively increasing accuracy and consistently declining loss across epochs, reflecting significant generalization without indications of overfitting. The results confirm the proposed framework’s capacity to deliver precise, efficient, and stable DDoS detection, underscoring the efficacy of dynamic, edge-aware graph learning in improving security within SDN-based infrastructures.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-32102-x.

Introduction

Software-Defined Networking (SDN) has become a groundbreaking networking architecture that separates the control and data planes. This configuration facilitates centralized traffic management and enables rapid modifications to the network1. SDN provides numerous benefits regarding flexibility and programmability; yet, its centralized architecture introduces certain security vulnerabilities2. One major concern is a Distributed Denial of Service (DDoS) attack, which can flood the SDN controller with harmful traffic, making network services unavailable and seriously affecting performance3. Detecting and stopping such attacks remains a significant challenge in SDN systems4.

Traditional Intrusion Detection Systems (IDS) and initial machine learning (ML) methodologies have demonstrated their capability to effectively detect DDoS attacks5. Nevertheless, these systems often rely on static traffic characteristics and require manual feature engineering. This limits their ability to adapt to changing network conditions and complex attack techniques. Deep learning models, such as Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs)3, Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs)6, and Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM)7 networks, have improved detection accuracy by effectively capturing complex temporal patterns in traffic flows. Nonetheless, these models remain inadequate in accurately representing the complex topological structure of SDN, which is essential for attack propagation.

Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) have lately garnered interest for their capacity to handle non-Euclidean data, rendering them particularly apt for network security applications8. In the domain of SDN, where interactions among network components may be efficiently represented as graphs, GNNs offer a powerful approach to learn from both spatial topology and flow-level characteristics9,10. Nonetheless, most existing GNN-based methods work on static graph configurations, which limits their ability to find quickly changing attack patterns that are typical of multi-vector DDoS attacks.

This study introduces GCTNetwork, an architecture based on a Dynamic Graph Neural Network (DGNN) aimed at resolving these challenges by facilitating Software-Defined Networks (SDNs) to detect DDoS attacks in real time. The proposed model incorporates spatial, temporal, and attentional dynamics into a unified architecture. It starts with Gated Convolutional Temporal (GCT) layers that work together to process information about nodes and edges. Then, an Edge-Aware LSTM module captures how network graphs change over time. To improve detection accuracy, a Graph Attention Layer (GAT) is added to focus on important communication pathways based on edge properties.

Related works

Recent studies have investigated the utilization of machine learning and deep learning methodologies for the detection of Distributed Denial of Service (DDoS) attacks in Software-Defined Networking (SDN). These initiatives mainly focus on addressing the limitations of traditional Intrusion Detection Systems (IDS) in adapting to the changing dynamics of contemporary network environments.

Conventional machine learning approaches, as demonstrated by Yang et al.10, utilized Support Vector Machines (SVM) and statistical features extracted from the KDD99 dataset for DDoS detection. The models achieved promising detection rates; nevertheless, they require human feature engineering and shown restricted adaptation to changing network conditions.

Tang et al.11 proposed a Deep Neural Network (DNN) for solving flow-based problems in Software-Defined Networks (SDNs), using the NSL-KDD dataset to improve detection accuracy and reduce false alarms. However, the model was trained on a limited set of features and shown restricted generalizability to real-world, high-dimensional SDN traffic.

Khekare et al.12 proposed a DDoS detection system utilizing Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) to identify evolving patterns in traffic. Their research efficiently modeled time-series data; however, it did not analyze the topological structure of the software-defined network (SDN). This omission is essential for understanding the cooperative behavior of network nodes during an attack.

Likewise, Priyadarshini et al.13 investigated the utilization of deep learning architectures, such as Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks and Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs), for threat detection within Software-Defined Networking (SDN) environments. While the methodology demonstrated promising results, its ability to generalize to previously unseen traffic patterns was limited by its reliance on predefined traffic characteristics.

Wan et al.14 acknowledged the significance of identifying sequential patterns in network data and employed a bidirectional Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) model for DDoS attack detection. Although the model achieved low false positive rates, its assessment on non-SDN datasets limits its practical applicability in SDN-based environments.

CNN-LSTM models15 focus on the SDN control plane by processing InSDN traffic using a convolutional front-end and an LSTM backend. They achieve approximately 99.2% accuracy, comparable to a Transformer baseline, in identifying different forms of assaults. They note that a previous NSL-KDD benchmark attained an approximate accuracy of 99.0%; however, they warn that legacy datasets such as NSL-KDD are outdated and do not accurately represent current SDN threats. These findings highlight the importance of capturing both spatial flow patterns (through CNN) and temporal sequences (via LSTM) in SDN intrusion data.

Recent methodologies have commenced the investigation of dynamic and graph-oriented models. In order to protect the southbound communication channels of SDN, Boukria et al. 16 implemented deep learning techniques, which resulted in a substantial degree of detection accuracy. However, their evaluation was limited to channel-level detection and utilized non-SDN datasets.

An approach that is explicitly designed for Software-Defined Networking (SDN) and is based on Graph Neural Networks (GNN) was introduced by Kalra et al. 17. This approach models flow interactions throughout the network topology. Despite their ability to capture geographical dependencies, their method relied on a static graph structure, which is insufficient for the rapid adaptation of DDoS attack patterns.

Saunders et al. 18 mitigated this limitation by providing a detection technique based on Graph Convolutional Networks (GCN) that accounts for the graph-structured characteristics of SDN traffic. Their methodology surpassed conventional models in encapsulating spatial dependencies. Nonetheless, static graph creation constrains the model’s adaptability to real-time fluctuations in traffic patterns.

FTG-Net-E 19 employs a hierarchical multi-level graph that includes a comprehensive traffic graph and individual flow-graphs (detailed packet interactions). The predictions are aggregated using bagging or boosting, and a collection of GNNs learns at various granularities. Utilizing the CICIDS2017 and CICIDS2018 datasets, FTG-Net-E achieved an accuracy of 99.67% (F1 score of 99.29%) in DDoS detection.

The paper20 presents an explainable, nature-inspired cyber-attack detection framework specifically designed for Software-Defined Internet of Things (SD-IoT) environments. The approach combines the Sheep Flock Optimization Algorithm (SFOA) with the Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) to develop a hybrid feature selection method that efficiently identifies a highly distinctive and minimal subset of network traffic features. This hybrid approach balances exploration and exploitation during the optimization process, reduces redundancy, and preserves the interpretability of the chosen attributes. Leveraging the enhanced feature set, machine learning classifiers are constructed to distinguish various attack types, while SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) are utilized to interpret and visualize the internal decision-making mechanisms of each model, thereby guaranteeing transparency and dependability. Experiments conducted on the benchmark datasets CICDDoS2019 and CIC-IoT-2023 demonstrate the proposed method’s superiority over traditional feature selection algorithms regarding accuracy, computational efficiency, and explainability. The system consequently provides a streamlined, effective, and comprehensible intrusion detection framework tailored for modern SD-IoT networks.

The paper21 presents a nature-inspired intrusion detection system (IDS) designed to improve the security of Software-Defined Network (SDN) controllers. The proposed framework employs a hybrid feature selection algorithm designated as WOA-F2I. This algorithm combines the Whale Optimization Algorithm (WOA) with Fisher Score and information-theoretic entropy ranking to automatically select the most pertinent traffic features for precise anomaly detection. Inspired by the echolocation and cooperative foraging behaviors of whales, WOA effectively balances exploration and exploitation to optimize feature subsets while minimizing redundancy. The optimized feature subset trains machine learning classifiers, thereby enhancing the precise differentiation between normal and attack traffic within the SDN control layer. Assessment of the model utilizing the UNSW-NB15 and UGR16 benchmark datasets indicates an approximate detection accuracy of 94%, along with notably reduced false alarm rates of around 5.2% compared to conventional approaches. The proposed WOA–FcI methodology thereby offers a computationally efficient, adaptive, and generalizable IDS solution for safeguarding SDN controllers against emerging network threats.

SD‑CED22 introduces an anomaly-driven detection framework, termed Software Defined Consumer Electronics Compromised Device Detection, developed to secure next-generation Software-Defined Consumer Electronics (SDN-CE) infrastructures against device compromise. The proposed method leverages intrusive alerts generated by Intrusion Detection Systems (IDS) to infer a device’s operational state normal or compromised. It models the transition dynamics of alerts using a Hidden Markov Model (HMM) framework, in which the Baum Welch algorithm performs the expectation–maximization (E and M-steps) to estimate transition probabilities, and the Viterbi algorithm determines the most likely hidden states representing device behavior. By analyzing state observation sequences and transition likelihoods under various attack types, the SD-CED demonstrates satisfactory discriminative performance detecting compromised conditions with 94% likelihood under Portscan and 82% under Slowloris scenarios, while maintaining 78% normal-state recognition in Denial-of-Service conditions. This work contributes an interpretable, probabilistic intrusion detection approach for adaptive security management in SDN-enabled consumer electronics. Its innovations lie on hierarchical graph representation and ensemble learning to mitigate overfitting.

The GraphSAGE-based SDN Intrusion Detection System23 utilizes GraphSAGE on SDN flow graphs for the detection of Denial of Service attacks. The authors contrast the GNN with unsupervised anomaly detection methods (HBOS, Isolation Forest, PCA, etc.) and ascertain that the GraphSAGE model markedly enhances detection accuracy. This study demonstrates that the integration of edge and flow features into GraphSAGE enhances Denial of Service detection in Software Defined Networking systems.

Lo et al.24 enhance GraphSAGE by using both node and edge information for intrusion detection in IoT networks. While not exclusive to SDN, it illustrates the advantages of edge-augmented GNNs: by embedding flow statistics on edges, E-GraphSAGE more effectively delineates communication patterns across devices. These concepts are directly relevant to SDN DDoS detection, in which graph edges signify flow traffic.

To address the constraints of static graphs, Wei et al.25 introduced a Dynamic Graph Neural Network (DGNN) model for real-time intrusion detection. Their approach constantly modifies the graph according to feature similarity and temporal intervals, enhancing adaptability and detection accuracy. Notwithstanding its benefits, their model was not explicitly designed for SDN architecture or multi-vector DDoS situations.

Materials and methods

The ongoing evolution and sophistication of Distributed Denial of Service (DDoS) attacks in Software-Defined Networking (SDN) environments necessitate detection mechanisms that can adapt dynamically to network changes and identify intricate behavioral patterns within traffic flows. The proposed GCTNetwork framework fulfills this necessity through a multi-stage dynamic graph neural network architecture specifically optimized for real-time intrusion detection in SDNs. By overcoming the inherent limitations of previous methods such as static graph modeling, limited temporal awareness, and inadequate edge semantics the system provides a unified spatial-temporal-attentional perspective on network traffic behavior.

The framework begins with the data collection stage, which in this study refers to the use of an already collected SDN dataset that contains flow-level records captured from controllers, switches, and hosts under various operational and attack conditions. Although no new traffic was detected in real-time, this phase continues to be a vital component of the framework, as it signifies the source of input data within an SDN environment. The dataset subsequently undergoes an extensive preprocessing phase involving data cleaning, normalization, label balancing, and feature extraction to guarantee uniformity and preparedness for subsequent analysis. The processed data are subsequently transformed during graph conversion, whereby each communication flow is represented as a directed edge linking nodes that denote network entities. Both node and edge representations are enriched with statistical and contextual attributes, forming the structured graph input required for downstream spatial-temporal learning.

Subsequently, the graph is processed through stacked Gated Convolutional Temporal (GCT) Layers, which concurrently learn node‑edge interactions and encode spatial relationships within the dynamic topology. To emphasize semantically important connections, a Graph Attention (GAT) Layer is integrated, enabling adaptive focus on critical communication pathways and improving discriminative precision. Temporal behavior of traffic evolution is then captured by the Edge‑Aware LSTM module, which aggregates node‑edge embeddings across sequential graph states to model time‑dependent dependencies and identify gradual or coordinated attack activities.

Finally, learned embeddings are propagated to the classification model, where edge‑level predictions determine whether specific interactions are benign or malicious. Performance evaluation is conducted using standard metrics Accuracy, ROC‑AUC, and F1‑score which collectively demonstrate the framework’s high detection accuracy and robustness in dynamic SDN environments. Through these integrated stages from data acquisition to graph‑based spatial‑temporal learning GCTNetwork achieves articulate, adaptive analysis of network behavior, ensuring effective identification of both overt and stealthy DDoS attacks across varying network conditions. Preprocessing of data

A method for systematic preprocessing was implemented to convert raw tabular network traffic records from the CICIDS2019 dataset into a graph structure that is suitable for Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) in order to optimize the use of graph-based learning for intrusion detection.

The preparation method comprises five primary stages: (i) data selection and cleansing, (ii) label balancing, (iii) feature modification and normalization, (iv) categorical encoding, and (v) graph structure creation with node-level feature aggregate.

Data selection and cleaning

The indications of Distributed Reflection Denial of Service (DDoS) attacks were identified through the use of attack-specific subsets extracted from the SDN Dataset. Each subset was individually examined, and extraneous or duplicate columns including SimillarHTTP and unnamed metadata were removed. Duplicates were eliminated using the Flow ID field to ensure distinct traffic flows. To mitigate data quality issues, infinite values were substituted with NaN, and all missing values were eliminated to preserve the integrity of the dataset. After completing this cleansing procedure, each purified subset was stored separately to enable subsequent normalization.

Label balancing

To address the class imbalance between benign and attack samples, a two-stage balancing approach was employed. Initially, benign samples were arbitrarily downsampled to correspond with the total number of DDoS attack samples. This approach guarantees that no individual class obtains an unjustified advantage throughout the training procedure. A fixed seed was utilized for the random selection to ensure the reproducibility of the results. Secondly, stratified sampling was utilized during dataset partitioning to ensure consistent class distributions across the training, validation, and testing subsets. This approach effectively prevents bias toward the majority class and guarantees equitable representation of all classes during the training process.

Feature transformation and normalization

For the purpose of standardization, we categorized the numerical properties into two normalizing procedures. Minimum-Maximum Normalization for range-sensitive variables (e.g., flow durations and packet sizes), and Z-score standardization Standardization for binary or count-based variables (e.g., flag counts, Inbound). A domain-informed feature selection approach revealed essential statistical properties for normalization, encompassing flow-level parameters such as packet statistics, inter-arrival periods, and segment sizes.

A subsequent normalization run was performed on properties with less common distributions, including Subflow Bytes, Init_Win_bytes, and TCP segment metrics. To alleviate heavy-tailed distributions, the data were scaled using log1p before normalization. All variables were normalized to the range [0, 1] using MinMaxScaler. Zero was substituted for any values that were either infinite or absent.

Categorical encoding and Port grouping

To ensure that the model accurately depicts the semantic meaning of network communication behavior, the Port Grouping strategy was implemented in accordance with the port classifications established by the Internet Assigned Numbers Authority (IANA). All port numbers were categorized into three groups: well-known ports (0–1023), linked to system-level services such as HTTP or DNS; registered ports (1024–49151), used by user-level applications; and dynamic/private ports (49152–65535), reserved for transient or client-assigned connections.

This classification serves two separate purposes. Initially, it mitigates the excessive sparsity and high cardinality of raw port numbers, which would otherwise introduce noise and hinder the generalization ability of the GNN. Second, it integrates domain knowledge related to traffic intent and communication hierarchy, allowing the model to recognize important interaction patterns between control-plane and data-plane entities. In our investigations, this abstraction improved model stability and reduced the occurrence of false alarms, demonstrating that port grouping enhances both interpretability and detection effectiveness within the graph-based representation.

Graph construction and node feature aggregation

The transformation of tabular SDN traffic records into a graph structure was carried out through a systematic process that ensures the absence of semantic information in node identifiers. Specifically, node IDs were constructed without including any attack-related labels or class indicators. Each network flow record was represented as a directed edge from a source node to a destination node, with node identifiers generated solely from communication metadata, as follows:

This method ensures that the model learns attack patterns from traffic behavior and relationships rather than relying on encoded labels. To ensure that the graph accurately reflects important communication dynamics, a careful feature selection process was applied before combining node-level statistics. All numerical flow attributes were examined using variance and correlation filtering, removing any variables that were constant or exhibited high correlation (|r| > 0.9). Next, mutual information analysis was conducted between each candidate feature and the class label to measure their discriminative contribution. Attributes with MI scores below the median threshold were omitted to prevent the introduction of noise.

In addition to statistical screening, domain knowledge from prior SDN-based intrusion detection research guided the retention of features known to capture key aspects of DDoS behavior, such as packet and byte counts, flow durations, inter-arrival times, and rate-based metrics. The final feature set comprised 132 aggregated statistics (revised from the original 156 after dataset refinement), which were then integrated across flows using six statistical operators mean, standard deviation, maximum, minimum, sum, and median to produce the node feature vectors. This hybrid feature selection method guarantees that the chosen features are both statistically significant and contextually pertinent to SDN traffic patterns, thereby improving model interpretability and decreasing computational complexity in dynamic graph learning.

The ultimate product of this pipeline is a heterogeneous, directed graph. Within this diagram, nodes represent network entities, and edges indicate the flows between them. Each node is defined by a high-dimensional feature vector that comprehensively encapsulates its attributes across diverse flow and attack contexts, thereby facilitating its utilization in GNN-based learning.

Model architecture

The GCTNetwork model is intended to identify DDoS attacks in Software-Defined Networks (SDNs) by analyzing dynamically evolving graph-structured traffic data. The architecture amalgamates spatial, temporal, and attention-based techniques within a cohesive framework to encapsulate both the structural patterns of communication and the temporal dynamics of network traffic. The model fundamentally adheres to a sequential processing pipeline, initiating with stacked spatial learning modules, succeeded by temporal modeling and attention refining, and culminating in a classification module. Initially, node and edge information are concurrently processed through various graph-based layers that map them into a common latent space and encapsulate interactions throughout the graph. This is succeeded by a recurrent layer that integrates temporal dependencies through an LSTM-based method, rendering the model responsive to fluctuations in traffic behavior over time, a crucial attribute for identifying emerging assault patterns. Edge-Aware LSTM graph layer

To capture the transitory dynamics of network traffic, the proposed model employs a custom Edge-Aware Graph Neural Network (GNN) Layer that is composed of Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) units. This module is explicitly designed to consider temporal dependencies by monitoring the evolution of node and edge features over time, in contrast to traditional GNNs, which emphasize spatial relationships. In the initial phase of the process, the local structure and interaction characteristics are integrated by aggregating messages from neighboring nodes and their associated edge features. The outcome of this process is a composite representation. The LSTM unit will process the aggregated vectors, resulting in the evolution of a hidden state with each time step. This enables the model to retain information from previous traffic states, effectively modeling the sequential nature of flow behaviors in Software-Defined Networks (SDNs). The inclusion of this edge-aware temporal mechanism serves several purposes. As shown in Fig. 4 first, it equips the model with the ability to retain memory of prior traffic patterns, which is crucial for detecting gradual deviations or escalation in malicious behavior.Secondly, the model is capable of detecting subtle temporal shifts that static approaches may neglect by processing sequential graph snapshots. Finally, this temporal sensitivity improves the model’s ability to respond to time-based anomalies, such as those induced by coordinated or slowly evolving Distributed Denial of Service (DDoS) attacks. The Edge-Aware LSTM layer makes a substantial contribution to the overall detection framework’s precision and robustness by capturing these dynamic patterns.

Graph attention layer (GAT)

The model employs a GAT layer to improve node embeddings, focusing on significant neighbors within the graph following temporal aggregation. This layer enhances the model’s ability to focus on critical communication pathways by utilizing edge properties in the computation of attention coefficients, thereby enabling edge-aware attention. Edge-aware attention methods dynamically evaluate the importance of each neighboring node by taking into account the properties of the connected nodes and the qualities of the edges that connect them. This ensures that the model prioritizes specific linkages within the graph structure while simultaneously reducing the importance of less significant connections. The attention coefficient for a node-edge interaction is determined by synthesizing node attributes and edge data, enabling the layer to assess the semantic relevance of edges and their influence on neighboring nodes. This method allows the model to concentrate on graph routes that yield the most significant signal for subsequent tasks, including edge classification. The GAT layer facilitates the acquisition of global context by dynamically emphasizing edges and nodes according to diverse graph topologies during training. This adaptability and edge-awareness allow the model to optimize its embeddings effectively and promote the transfer of essential information among interconnected nodes. The GAT layer serves as a conduit between topological graph characteristics and task-specific information extraction by highlighting significant neighbors and considering edge-level interactions.

The incorporation of the GAT layer into the model architecture is crucial for capturing intricate dependencies within the graph structure. Incorporating edge properties in the augmentation of node embeddings enhances expressiveness, proving particularly advantageous for edge classification applications. This edge-aware attention method enhances the model’s efficacy by focusing learning on essential communication pathways and improving the contextual representation of graph nodes.

Evaluation

The evaluation section thoroughly assesses the efficacy of the proposed GCTNetwork architecture for DDoS detection within software-defined networking (SDN) environments. Multiple comparable experiments were carried out against leading baseline models using the SDN Dataset to ensure a fair assessment. Standard evaluation criteria such as accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, detection rate, and false alarm index are presented to clarify different aspects of model performance. Comprehensive analyses are also presented via learning curves, interpretation of confusion matrices, and ablation studies. These analyses highlight the robustness, generalization capability, and efficacy of the proposed method in distinguishing between distinct traffic classes. Additionally, the code for the model has been made accessible at: https://github.com/saadhalafy/SDN-DDOS.

To meticulously evaluate the effectiveness of the proposed GCTNetwork framework for DDoS detection in software-defined networking (SDN), we compare its performance against three prominent baseline methodologies: Spatial-Temporal Graph Convolutional Network (ST-GCN)26, Graph Residual Attention Network (GRAN)27, and Low-Rate Spatial–Temporal Graph Convolutional Network (LR-STGCN)28. These methods were chosen for comparison because they exemplify cutting-edge graph-based and deep learning models utilized for DDoS detection and mitigation in contemporary network settings. All comparative experiments employ the SDN Dataset, following a uniform experimental design and evaluation metrics, which include accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, detection rate, and false alarm index. Furthermore, our evaluation offers insights into model efficacy through learning curves, confusion matrices, and ablation experiments.

ST-GCN is a deep learning model specifically designed for identifying DDoS attacks in programmable SDN, utilizing the intrinsic graph structure of the network. It represents switches and hosts as nodes and their interactions as edges, enabling the visualization of both geographical links within the network topology and the time progression of traffic. ST-GCN enables accurate detection of attack propagation and the identification of critical attack channels within the network by periodically constructing network state graphs using in-band network telemetry and utilizing specialized graph convolutional techniques.

GRAN model designed for intrusion detection in software-defined networking (SDN) environments, in which high‐dimensional SDN traffic features are first processed and then embedded within a graph representation of network flows. The model leverages both a graph attention mechanism and residual learning to effectively highlight salient edges and capture deeper flow‐level dependencies.

LR-STGCN is engineered to identify and counter low-rate DDoS (LDoS) attacks, characterized by nuanced and sporadic traffic patterns that can elude conventional detection techniques. This methodology integrates Graph Convolutional Networks (GCN) with Gated Recurrent Units (GRU) to concurrently extract spatial information from the Software-Defined Networking (SDN) topology and temporal features from traffic sequences. The system additionally integrates time-frequency analysis to differentiate LDoS attack flows according to behavioral signatures and Quality of Service (QoS) effects. LR-STGCN is directly integrated into the SDN controller, facilitating real-time detection and accurate localization of attack sources for swift mitigation.

Our study of GCTNetwork, in comparison to these advanced models, offers a thorough examination of its capabilities and contextualizes its performance within the realm of modern DDoS detection methods.

Dataset

To thoroughly assess the effectiveness and suitability of the proposed GCTNetwork framework for DDoS detection within Software-Defined Networking (SDN) environments, the SDN Dataset for Machine Learning-Based Network Intrusion Detection Systems available on Mendeley Data was employed11. This dataset was collected from a genuine SDN testbed consisting of Mininet emulation, the POX controller, and Open vSwitch (OVS) instances, offering an authentic environment for SDN control–data plane interactions. It contains both benign and attack traffic flows, making it highly suitable for validating intrusion detection methods in SDN-based infrastructures.

The dataset includes over 700,000 flow-level recordings derived from the SDN controller’s flow tables under various network conditions. Each entry pertains to a unique OpenFlow flow entry, distinguished by essential statistical parameters including packet count, byte count, flow duration, inter-arrival time, and protocol identifiers. The traffic scenarios include both normal operation and multiple attack conditions such as ICMP flood, TCP SYN flood, and UDP flood.

To make the dataset more suitable for SDN-oriented DDoS detection, we re-labeled the attack flows into four specific categories based on the dominant protocol and attack behavior:

BENIGN: Normal background traffic.DDoS_ICMP: Flooding via ICMP echo requests.DDoS_TCP: TCP SYN flood and connection exhaustion attacks.DDoS_UDP: High-rate UDP-based flooding attacks.

This enhanced labeling schema guarantees that the model acquires protocol-specific DDoS patterns while preserving balanced class representation.

Before constructing the graph, all flow features were meticulously cleansed and normalized. Continuous variables displaying heavy-tailed distributions (e.g., byte count, flow duration) were transformed using the log1p function and then normalized to the [0, 1] range with a MinMaxScaler. Missing or infinite values have been substituted with zero. Categorical variables, including protocol type and port range, were encoded numerically in accordance with the IANA standard port classifications (well-known, registered, and dynamic/private).

For the purpose of graph generation, each switch and host entity was represented as a node. Each flow interaction between source and destination pairings was depicted as a directed edge. Node identifiers were generated by integrating the IP address, port, and protocol to ensure distinctiveness across various flows. Node attributes were computed from aggregated statistical measures, encompassing the mean, standard deviation, maximum, minimum, sum, and median, which were derived from the associated flow records. This procedure generated comprehensive multidimensional embeddings suitable for dynamic graph analysis.

The dataset was divided into three subsets through a stratified sampling approach to preserve the proportional distribution of each traffic class. 70% for training, 10% for validation, and 20% for testing. To emulate real-time network activities, the data were partitioned sequentially in chronological order. This approach guaranteed that data from identical time segments was omitted from both the training and testing datasets, thus avoiding any potential information leakage.

Each statistic offers distinct insights into the model’s performance across different class distributions and detection contexts.

Model parameters and training configuration

The effectiveness of the proposed GCTNetwork framework significantly depends on its architectural design, the setting of its loss function, and the hyperparameters that control the training process. This section describes the key parameters used in the developed model and training procedure, which are selected to improve detection accuracy for DDoS attacks within the specified SDN context.

The GCTNetwork design, consisting of bespoke Gated Convolutional Temporal (GCT) layers, an Edge-Aware LSTM GNN layer, and a Graph Attention (GAT) layer, employs various critical factors to delineate its framework and capabilities. The node_dim option determines the input dimensionality for node features, whereas edge_dim defines the dimensionality of the initial edge features. The values of 80 and 37 are directly obtained from the processed feature set of the SDN Dataset dataset, indicating the richness of the normalized and aggregated node and edge attributes. A vital architectural parameter is hidden_dim, which dictates the magnitude of the feature representation space within the network’s hidden layers, encompassing the GCT layers, the Edge-Aware LSTM, and the GAT layer. Configured at 768, this dimension harmonizes model capacity with computational efficiency, enabling the model to acquire intricate patterns while reducing the likelihood of overfitting on potentially high-dimensional graph data. The edge_classes parameter, which is dynamically determined based on the unique labels within the training data, defines the output dimensionality of the network, corresponding to the total number of distinct classes for the edge classification task (such as different categories of DDoS attacks and benign traffic).

The training process is regulated by several hyperparameters in addition to the network architecture. The FocalLoss function serves as the training objective, especially effective in mitigating class imbalance commonly observed in intrusion detection datasets. The parameters, alpha and gamma, were established at 1.0 and 2.0, respectively. Alpha serves as a weighting factor for the overall loss (a value of 1.0 indicates no additional class weighting bias beyond that provided by gamma), whereas gamma (set to 2.0) emphasizes training on difficult, misclassified instances by reducing the weight of easily classified examples, which is essential for enhancing performance on minority attack classes. The selected optimization algorithm is Adam, a prevalent adaptive learning rate optimizer recognized for its efficacy in training deep neural networks. The learning rate (lr) for the Adam optimizer was established at a modest value of 0.0001 to guarantee sustained convergence and prevent overshooting the ideal parameter values. The model was ultimately trained for 10 epochs, a duration selected to provide adequate iterations for learning from the training data while assessing performance on the validation set to avert overfitting. Following the removal of the GAT module, the performance metrics declined to an accuracy of 92.03% and an F1-score of 92.83%, demonstrating that the attention mechanism is instrumental in enhancing node and edge embeddings by accentuating the most pertinent communication links. In the absence of GAT, the model treats all neighboring nodes uniformly, thereby reducing its ability to accentuate critical relational structures associated with DDoS propagation.

The elimination of the Edge-Aware LSTM led to an additional reduction in the model’s accuracy, which declined to 90.84%, along with a decrease in recall to 88.92%. This reduction highlights the significance of modeling temporal dependencies, as the LSTM component enables the framework to accurately capture sequential flow patterns and the dynamic progression of assault behaviors over time. The observed reduction in recall suggests that temporal reasoning is essential for the precise identification of attacks characterized by progressive evolution or intermittent traffic escalation, exemplified by low-rate distributed denial-of-service attacks.

Computational efficiency and deployment feasibility

To evaluate the feasibility of implementing GCTNetwork within operational SDN controllers, we examined the model’s computational performance concerning inference latency, memory utilization, and throughput. Experiments were performed on a workstation equipped with an Intel Core i7-12700 processor, 32 GB of RAM, and an NVIDIA RTX 3060 GPU with 12 GB of memory.

The model processes clusters of 128 flow edges within graphs during inference. The average forward-pass latency was approximately 6.8 ms per batch on the GPU and 13.6 ms on the CPU, resulting in a throughput of 41.2 thousand flows per second (GPU) and 18.8 thousand flows per second (CPU). The peak memory utilization was documented as 1.2 GB on the GPU and 760 MB on the CPU, indicating moderate resource usage.

When integrated with the Ryu SDN controller via RESTful APIs, the total detection-and-response cycle time averaged under 10 ms per flow batch, ensuring compatibility with the sub-second flow reconfiguration intervals typical of modern controllers. These findings demonstrate that GCTNetwork is computationally efficient for real-time SDN operation, and can be readily deployed on commodity controller hardware. Additional improvements, like reducing the dimensions of hidden layers or quantizing the model, can further improve scalability for large-scale enterprise or ISP-grade SDN deployments.

Conclusion

This paper investigates the persistent challenge of identifying Distributed Denial of Service (DDoS) attacks within Software-Defined Networking (SDN) architectures. It presents GCTNetwork, a dynamic graph neural network (DGNN) architecture designed for real-time, topology-aware intrusion detection. The proposed method utilizes the spatial and temporal characteristics of SDN traffic. This sets it apart from traditional machine learning and deep learning methods, which predominantly rely on static features, manually engineered features, or limited viewpoints within the network architecture.

GCTNetwork transforms raw flow data into directed graphs with associated node and edge attributes. This enables the model to recognize the evolving relationships and communication patterns that characterize both legitimate and malignant network activities.

The design integrates Gated Convolutional Temporal (GCT) layers, an Edge-Aware Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) network for sequential graph analysis, and a Graph Attention mechanism to emphasize key communication pathways. This integration allows the model to thoroughly encompass dependencies at both the node and edge levels, offering a detailed comprehension of network activity. Experiments performed on the SDN dataset reveal that GCTNetwork achieves superior multi-class classification performance, surpassing prominent models such as ST-GCN, GRAN, and LR-STGCN. The model attains an accuracy of 94.08%, an F1-score of 93.27%, and a ROC-AUC of 0.95, while sustaining a low False Alarm Index (FAI) of 0.06, thereby demonstrating its effectiveness in minimizing superfluous alerts. The training dynamics exhibit consistent convergence, thereby improving the model’s robustness and generalization performance across diverse network configurations. The findings affirm the advantages of the proposed contributions: (1) a systematic approach for constructing graphs that depict SDN traffic, (2) a hybrid spatial-temporal graph framework that integrates edge-awareness and attention-based learning, and (3) comprehensive experimental protocols that validate robustness across multiple performance metrics. GCTNetwork signifies a significant advancement in proactive SDN security protocols, owing to its demonstrated efficacy and scalability. Future research may focus on enhancing the model’s scalability for large-scale production environments, incorporating online learning to facilitate rapid adaptation to emerging threats, and expanding the dynamic graph architecture to encompass additional networked systems and threat vectors. Our study emphasizes the importance of graph-based, temporal deep learning techniques in improving security within programmable and adaptable network infrastructures.

GCTNetwork represents a notable advancement in proactive SDN security, offering a scalable and adaptable technology proficient in detecting complex infiltration behaviors within programmable networks. Future research may explore enhanced scalability for large-scale production deployments, expand the dynamic graph modeling pipeline to include other networked systems and threat categories, and utilize adaptive learning techniques to strengthen resilience against evolving attack scenarios. This study emphasizes the critical role of graph-based temporal deep learning in improving security in modern SDN architectures.

The proposed GCTNetwork demonstrates exceptional detection performance on the SDN dataset; however, the assessment employed preprocessed flow-level records rather than raw packet traces or temporally separate traffic streams. Consequently, its ability to generalize to completely new time periods or real-time SDN traffic necessitates empirical validation. Future initiatives will focus on improving the framework to operate directly with real-time streaming packet captures and controller logs. This would involve incorporating online graph updates and incremental learning techniques to maintain detection accuracy in the face of evolving traffic distributions. The practical application and continuous modification will provide more significant evidence of the model’s robustness in dynamic SDN environments.
