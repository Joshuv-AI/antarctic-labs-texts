First high-density linkage map based on GBS derived genome wide SNP markers in Labeo catla (Hamilton, 1822)
Authors: Bismay Sahoo, Priyanka Nandanpawar, Gargee Das, Nirjharini Priyadarshini, Lakshman Sahoo, Jitendra Kumar Sundaray, Paramananda Das
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLabeo catla is a commercially important Indian Major Carp, yet its genetic improvement has been hindered by a lack of high-resolution genomic resources. We report the construction of the first high-density SNP-based genetic linkage map for this species and present the associated genomic datasets deposited in public repositories. Mapping populations derived from five full-sib families (CF1, CF5, CF7, CF8, and CF11) comprising 196 individuals (10 parents and 190 offspring) were genotyped via Genotyping-by-Sequencing (GBS), generating 133.94 GB of raw sequence data. Using 782 informative SNP markers and Kosambi’s mapping function, we resolved 25 complete linkage groups (LGs), consistent with the haploid karyotype of L. catla (n = 25). The resulting map spans a total genetic distance of 1279.8 cM, achieving a high-resolution average marker interval of 1.63 cM. Beyond linkage mapping, these markers were utilized to anchor 668 previously unplaced scaffolds, effectively organizing 502.33 Mb (49.29%) of the L. catla draft genome into pseudo-chromosomes. LG2 was identified as the largest pseudo-chromosome (213.83 Mb), while LG4 was the smallest (1.8 Mb). The average chromosome size was approximately 36.39 Mb. Comparative genomic analysis confirmed extensive chromosomal colinearity and conserved synteny between L. catla and Labeo rohita, with significantly higher structural divergence observed against the Danio rerio model. This high-density map and the subsequent chromosome-level scaffold anchoring provide a critical genomic framework for positional cloning, Marker-Assisted Selection (MAS), and the identification of Quantitative Trait Loci (QTLs) for traits such as rapid growth and disease resistance, accelerating the genetic enhancement of this species in Asian aquaculture.

Background & Summary

Labeo catla (Hamilton, 1822), commonly known as “Catla”, ranks second in list of most favored Indian major carp1. This fish is endemic to the Indo-Gangetic riverine system in India and is also distributed across the rivers of Bangladesh, Nepal, Myanmar, and Pakistan2. Its widespread presence in these regions is attributed to its rapid growth, compatibility with other major carps, unique surface feeding behaviour, delectable taste, and high demand among consumers, leading to its frequent cultivation in polyculture systems across the Indian subcontinent3. Labeo catla currently represents about 3.4% of the global freshwater aquaculture production, equivalent to a value of 5 billion USD4. In the last twenty years, the culture of IMCs has gained prominence due to its substantial commercial worth, increasing market demand, and rapid expansion, yielding a production volume of 4,713,340 tons5.

Genomic tools and resources, particularly DNA markers, in aquaculture species are very much essential for a variety of purposes such as construction of genetic maps, identification of QTLs through linkage and genome-wide association studies, marker assisted selection, genomic selection, parentage analyses and many more. DNA markers linked to performance traits (QTLs) in conventional breeding programs can lead to marker assisted selection (MAS). Development of marker maps (genetic maps) in commercially important fish and shellfish species are useful genomic resources essential for QTL identification, genome annotations and others.

Developing a genetic linkage map is essential for marker-assisted selection (MAS), chromosome-level assembly and conducting comparative mapping studies with polymorphic markers6–8. Due to rapid advancement in molecular markers and linkage analysis technology, it is now feasible to build genetic linkage maps for a wide variety of species9. SNPs have been overtaking conventional molecular markers like AFLP and microsatellites because of its biallelic nature, uniform distribution, genetic stability and high abundance in molecular genetics studies10–12.

Among DNA markers, SNPs are the most abundantly prevalent molecular markers in the genomes of various vertebrate organisms and have emerged as the markers of choice for all the above applications, including linkage analysis. In the past two decades, the advancement of next-generation sequencing (NGS) technology has facilitated the rapid and cost-effective identification of genome-wide SNP markers, SNP arrays, high density linkage maps and QTLs for several fish species. Additionally, genotyping by sequencing (GBS) has emerged as a promising genome-wide genotyping tool, utilizing an enzyme-based complexity reduction method to simultaneously discover and genotype molecular markers across the entire genome, with or without a reference13. The genome-wide SNPs identified through the GBS technique have served as the foundation for various studies, including construction of linkage maps14–16, QTL analyses14, genome-wide association studies focusing on economically important traits such as disease resistance17–19, and the implementation of genomic selection20–24.

Despite the economic importance of L. catla, no high-density genetic linkage map existed for this species prior to this study. Here we present: (i) the first GBS-based high-density genetic linkage map of L. catla comprising 782 SNPs across 25 LGs spanning 1,279.8 cM; (ii) a scaffold-anchoring dataset and (iii) whole-genome synteny datasets comparing L. catla with L. rohita and D. rerio. These resources provide a critical genomic framework for positional cloning, MAS, and QTL mapping for traits including rapid growth and disease resistance directly enabling genetic improvement of catla in Asian aquaculture.

Methods

Ethics statement

All handling of fish was carried out following the guidelines for control and supervision of experiments on animals established by the Government of India and approved by the Institutional Animal Ethics Committee (AEC) of ICAR-CIFA.

Breeding and sample collection

For the purpose of generating mapping populations for the building of a linkage map, five full sib catla families (CF1, CF5, CF7, CF8, and CF11) were produced and grown in tanks till fingerling size in ICAR-CIFA, Bhubaneswar. Fin tissues were obtained from the parents and their offspring were reared in fish tanks until they attained the requisite size for subsequent tissue collection (Fig. 1). A total of 550 offsprings from the aforementioned 5 families were sampled and preserved in ethanol after achieving the required size. (fingerling stage; approximately 5–10 cm in total length, Approximate weight: 3–10 g).

Catla offspring rearing in aquarium and tissue collection. (Participants in the image have given consent to the publication of the image).

DNA isolation and quantification

High molecular weight genomic DNA was extracted from the reared offspring along with their parents using DNeasy Blood and Tissue Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) per the manufacturer’s instructions. The extracted DNA was subsequently stored at −20 °C until further utilization. The integrity of the isolated DNA was checked using 0.8% agarose gel electrophoresis and Nanodrop Biospectrometer (Eppendorf India Pvt. Ltd.). 200 individuals with a concentration ≥100 ng/μl were selected, including 190 offspring along with their parents (5 × 40) for subsequent processing.

GBS library construction and sequencing

GBS library was prepared following the Elshire et al.13 protocol with modifications. Briefly, 200 ng of genomic DNA from each sample was digested with ApeKI restriction enzyme (5 units, 75 °C, 4 hours). ApeKI was selected for its methylation sensitivity and preferential cutting in gene-rich genomic regions, making it suitable for genome-wide SNP discovery in teleost fishes. After cooling down to 4 °C, Illumina universal adapters comprising of unique barcodes and a “common” adapter were ligated to the DNA fragments. Post-ligation, pooled individuals were purified using AMpure DNA purification beads followed by amplification of genomic fragments in 50 µl volumes. Library quality was assessed using the 4150 TapeStation System (Agilent) and Agencourt AMPure XP SPRI magnetic beads (Beckman Coulter). The GBS libraries were sequenced using Illumina HiSeq 2000 sequencing platform.

Sequence data analysis and SNP discovery

The Illumina HiSeq sequenced data were quality checked using FastQC v0.11.825 and MultiQC v1.926 software, respectively. Low quality bases (Q < 30) and adapter sequences along with the duplicated sequences were removed from the raw reads using fastp v0.23.227 program. The processed quality reads of each individual were mapped to the catla genome28 using BWA-MEM v0.6.12 software29 with default parameters. SAMtools v0.1.1930 software was used for conversion of SAM files to BAM followed by sorting of BAM files. Following this, STACKS v2.5931 software was used to identify and genotype SNPs using GSTACKS program with the following settings: min-mapq 30; gt-alpha 0.05; var-alpha 0.05. The POPULATIONS program of STACKS software was used to output the data to a JoinMap file format with “CP” map type with the filtering parameter for minor allele frequency (MAF) of 0.05. SNP markers correctly genotyped in at least 75% of the offspring as well as exhibited polymorphisms in both parents of the L. catla mapping population were retained for further linkage map analysis.

Linkage map construction

Mendelian segregation at each locus was assessed by chi-square test. SNPs with P ≥ 0.05 were selected for linkage map construction. To fill missing genotype information and correct genotyping errors, we imputed segregating markers using the program Beagle v5.432 according to Ward et al.33. JoinMap v4.134 software was used to construct the linkage groups using the CP (cross-pollinator) algorithm. In CP genetic cross, three types of marker segregation were used for the genetic linkage mapping. In Type I (lm × ll), the female (lm) was heterozygous and the male (ll) was homozygous. In Type II (nn × np), the female (nn) was homozygous and the male (np) was heterozygous. In Type III (hk × hk), both parents were heterozygous. SNP Markers with distorted segregation (P < 0.05) were excluded from genetic map construction. Scaffold Anchoring

In order to integrate the physical map and the constructed high-density linkage map, all 782 mapped SNPs with 70-bp flanking sequences were aligned to the L. catla reference genome28 using BLAST (E-value ≤ 1 × 10−10). SNPs Alignments with ≥70 bp match length and ≤1 bp gap were accepted as precise mapping. Scaffolds carrying one or more precisely mapped SNPs were assigned to their corresponding LGs. Duplicate scaffolds assigned to multiple LGs were filtered out.

Comparative genomics

Syntenic relationships between the pseudo-chromosomes of L. catla28 and the reference genomes of rohu (Labeo rohita) and zebrafish (Danio rerio) were determined using Satsuma v2.035 (Satsuma synteny module). Syntenic blocks were visualized using Circos v0.6736 software.

Technical Validation

Sequencing output and read quality

A total of 892.90 million raw reads (133.94 Gb) were generated, averaging 58 million reads per parental sample and 835 million reads across offspring. To verify specificity, random read subsets were aligned against NCBI NT sequences, confirming predominant mapping to Cyprinus carpio and Labeo catla entries. After quality filtering with fastp, 96.19% of filtered reads successfully aligned to the L. catla reference genome indicating high sequencing specificity and efficient library preparation.

SNP discovery and genotyping

STACKS-based SNP calling identified between 12,701 (CF7) and 47,699 (CF11) raw GBS markers per family (Table 1). Of these, informative (polymorphic in both parents) marker counts ranged from 3,977 (CF7) to 16,495 (CF11). The CF11 family produced the highest marker yield, reflecting the greater heterozygosity and informativeness of its parental cross. SNP markers were verified for Mendelian segregation; loci with chi-square P < 0.05 and those with >10% missing data were removed prior to map construction to prevent distortion of recombination estimates (Table 2).

Linkage map completeness and marker distribution

The four smaller families (CF1, CF5, CF7, CF8) produced incomplete maps comprising 5, 8, 10, and 15 LGs respectively, attributable to insufficient marker coverage and lower parental heterozygosity in those crosses. The CF11 family alone yielded a complete map encompassing all 25 LGs, matching the haploid chromosome number of L. catla (n = 25)37 and confirming full genomic representation. The final map of 782 SNPs spans 1,279.8 cM, with individual LG lengths ranging from 27.0 cM (LG4) to 73.9 cM (LG2) and marker counts per LG ranging from 10 (LG22, LG23, LG25) to 90 (LG1) (Fig. 2, Table 3). The average marker interval of 1.63 cM is comparable to well-validated high-density maps in related cyprinids 1.3 cM in L. rohita38 and 0.33 cM in common carp39 and represents a substantial improvement over earlier SSR-based maps in this species group (34 cM average interval)40,41.

Linkage map quality validation

Map quality was assessed across four dimensions: marker distribution and gap analysis, detection of genotyping errors and double recombination events, karyotype concordance, and cross-family consistency of linkage relationships.

Marker distribution and gap analysis. Marker intervals across all 25 LGs ranged from 0.71 cM (LG1) to 3.98 cM (LG23), with a mean of 1.63 cM. No inter-marker gaps exceeding 10 cM were detected in any LG, indicating that the map is free of the large unresolved intervals that typically compromise QTL interval precision and scaffold anchoring reliability. The uniform marker distribution across LGs, with no clustering or void regions, further supports the suitability of ApeKI-based GBS for generating evenly spaced genome-wide markers in this species.

Detection of genotyping errors and double recombination events. Prior to map construction in each family, missing genotype data were imputed using Beagle v5.4, which simultaneously corrects likely erroneous genotype calls including those arising from double recombination events that manifest as isolated singleton genotypes flanked by opposing allele calls in a single individual. Following imputation, markers in each family were independently subjected to a stringent multi-step filtering pipeline: removal of loci with >10% missing data, exclusion of homozygous and non-segregating loci, elimination of identical duplicate loci, and removal of markers showing significant segregation distortion (chi-square test, P < 0.05). This sequential correction and filtering process, applied independently to each of the five families, directly minimized the influence of genotyping errors and spurious recombination signals on map distance estimation and marker ordering prior to final LG construction. The progressive reduction from 16,495 informative markers to 782 final mapped markers in CF11 (Table 2) reflects the cumulative stringency of this quality control pipeline.

Karyotype concordance. The number of resolved linkage groups (25) in the CF11 family precisely matched the known haploid chromosome number of L. catla (n = 25)37, providing independent confirmation that the map achieves complete genomic coverage without artificial LG splitting or merging — both of which are common indicators of poor map quality in linkage mapping studies.

Cross-family consistency of linkage relationships. Since genotype imputation and linkage map construction were performed independently and separately for each of the five mapping families (CF1, CF5, CF7, CF8, and CF11) in JoinMap v4.1, LG assignments for markers present in more than one family were derived from entirely independent analyses. Markers shared between the CF11 complete map and any of the four partial-coverage families were consistently assigned to the same LG across all independent family analyses, with no instances of conflicting LG assignment for any shared marker. This cross-family concordance achieved through fully independent imputation and mapping pipelines provides robust validation that the reported linkage group assignments are stable, reproducible, and not artefacts of a single family’s segregation pattern or dataset-specific genotyping noise

Scaffold anchoring

Of 5,435 unanchored scaffolds in the published L. catla draft genome28, 668 scaffolds totalling 502.33 Mb (49.29% of the assembled genome) were successfully anchored to 25 LGs using sequence information from the 782 mapped SNP markers (Table 4). The mean anchored pseudo-chromosome length was 36.39 Mb, though considerable size heterogeneity was observed across LGs. LG2 was the largest pseudo-chromosome (213.83 Mb; 303 scaffolds), accounting for approximately 42% of the total anchored sequence. LG4, by contrast, was both the shortest linkage group in genetic length (27.0 cM) and the smallest in anchored physical size (1.8 Mb; 2 scaffolds). The physical size of LG4 is substantially smaller than the average vertebrate chromosome and may reflect either a genuine biological characteristic such as a micro-chromosome, a feature documented in several teleost genomes following ancestral polyploidization or a technical limitation, whereby most LG4-associated scaffolds lacked sufficient SNP marker coverage to be assigned in the current mapping population. With only 16 mapped markers and 2 anchored scaffolds, the latter explanation cannot be excluded and should be investigated in future assemblies using cytogenetic or long-read approaches.

The remaining 4,767 scaffolds (50.71% of total scaffolds) could not be anchored to any linkage group. Several factors likely contribute to this. First, genomic regions enriched for repetitive elements or heterochromatin tend to suppress recombination, reducing the probability of informative SNP discovery and marker placement in those intervals. Second, regions with low heterozygosity in the CF11 mapping family would yield few or no polymorphic markers, precluding linkage assignment for the corresponding scaffolds. Third, the marker density achievable by ApeKI-based GBS, while substantially higher than earlier SSR-based maps, may be insufficient to cover all genomic regions uniformly particularly in gene-poor or repeat-rich intervals. These limitations are inherent to linkage-map-based anchoring strategies and are expected to be progressively resolved as higher-density genotyping resources, long-read sequencing, and Hi-C chromatin conformation data become available for L. catla.

Comparative genomics validation

Satsuma-based synteny analysis confirmed extensive chromosomal colinearity between L. catla and L. rohita (Fig. 3a), consistent with their close phylogenetic relationship as congeners within Cyprinidae. Large, continuous syntenic blocks spanned most of the 25 LGs, indicating that gross chromosomal architecture has remained highly conserved despite morphological and ecological divergence. In contrast, comparison with D. rerio revealed a more complex rearrangement pattern including inter-chromosomal translocations and inversions (Fig. 3b), reflecting the greater evolutionary distance between the Labeonini and Danioninae subfamilies. These conserved syntenic regions with the fully annotated zebrafish genome provide a functional bridge for candidate gene discovery within future QTL regions in catla.

This high-resolution framework provides a robust foundation for identifying the genetic architecture of commercially significant traits in catla.
