A histopathologically verified dataset of magnifying narrow-band imaging endoscopy for classifying the gastric precancerous cascade
Authors: Faguang Wang, Wanying Liao, Hengjie Su, Louzhe Xu, Huadan Xue, Linzhe Jiang, Yingyun Yang, Meihua Piao, Jing Sun, Ting Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Gastric cancer is a leading cause of mortality worldwide, yet the development of computer-aided diagnosis (CAD) systems for its early detection is hindered by the scarcity of high-quality datasets utilizing Magnifying Endoscopy with Narrow-Band Imaging (ME-NBI). Addressing this gap, we present EndoWLI-NBI, a large-scale dataset comprising 11,971 ME-NBI images from 868 patients, specifically curated to cover the full gastric precancerous cascade—including Intestinal Metaplasia & Chronic Gastritis, Low- and High-Grade Intraepithelial Neoplasia, and Early Gastric Cancer. Unlike existing public datasets, every image in EndoWLI-NBI is rigorously mapped to a biopsy-confirmed histopathological diagnosis, providing a definitive gold standard for label reliability. Technical validation using state-of-the-art deep learning benchmarks demonstrates the dataset’s quality and separability, while highlighting the intrinsic visual challenges in distinguishing high-grade neoplasia from early cancer. This dataset serves as a critical, medically verified resource for advancing fine-grained lesion classification, domain adaptation research, and endoscopic training in gastroenterology.

Background & Summary

Gastric cancer (GC) typically arises from a prolonged cascade of precancerous abnormalities, the Correa cascade, and is frequently detected only at advanced stages, leading to high mortality rates globally1–3. While the five-year survival rate for Early Gastric Cancer (EGC) exceeds 90%, it drops precipitously to below 20% for advanced cases, even in medically advanced regions like East Asia where diagnostic protocols are highly developed4–6. Therefore, interrupting this progression through the accurate classification of precancerous lesions (e.g., intestinal metaplasia, intraepithelial neoplasia) is critical for improving patient outcomes7–10.

Currently, magnifying endoscopy with narrow-band imaging (ME-NBI) enables detailed visualization of mucosal microsurface and microvascular patterns and is widely used for the assessment of early gastric lesions11. However, its diagnostic performance remains highly dependent on operator expertise, resulting in substantial inter-observer variability and limiting its utility in settings where experienced endoscopists are not available12,13. Computer-aided (CAD) systems have been proposed to address this limitation by standardizing image interpretation and reducing operator dependency. In clinical applications, these systems are typically categorized into computer-aided detection (CADe), which focuses on lesion identification during screening, and computer-aided diagnosis (CADx), which aims to characterize detected lesions to support clinical decision-making14–16. Despite their potential, the development of robust CAD systems remains constrained by the limited availability of high-quality, domain-specific datasets with reliable ground truth annotations.

In recent years, several AI-assisted systems for gastric neoplasia detection and characterization have been validated in large-scale clinical populations, demonstrating their feasibility for real-world deployment and improved diagnostic performance17. These advances underscore the critical role of high-quality datasets in enabling the development, validation, and generalization of such systems. Existing publicly available gastrointestinal endoscopy datasets (Table 1), although not specifically designed for gastric cancer, provide broad coverage of gastrointestinal findings but exhibit important limitations for gastric lesion analysis. While several datasets include gastric lesion images, such as18, most datasets either focus on limited lesion categories or lack sufficient representation of magnifying endoscopy with narrow-band imaging (ME-NBI), thereby restricting their utility for fine-grained lesion characterization. More recently, substantially larger-scale gastrointestinal endoscopy datasets have been introduced, further expanding data availability for AI development19. However, these datasets are typically general-purpose and are not specifically curated for gastric lesion analysis, often lacking detailed histopathological verification and structured representation of disease progression stages. In addition, many datasets include only a limited proportion of high-quality ME-NBI images20, further constraining their applicability for fine-grained lesion characterization. Moreover, existing datasets often do not capture the full spectrum of the gastric precancerous cascade, which is essential for studying disease progression21,22. More broadly, the advancement of artificial intelligence in gastric lesion analysis is constrained by multiple factors. To address these challenges, we present EndoWLI-NBI dataset, a large-scale endoscopic imaging dataset collected from Peking Union Medical College Hospital, comprising both white-light imaging (WLI) and narrow-band imaging (NBI) under magnifying and non-magnifying conditions. The diversity of imaging modalities and magnification levels enhances the dataset’s clinical representativeness and supports robust model development. The dataset comprises 11,971 images from 868 patients, each linked to biopsy-confirmed histopathological diagnoses, and is organized into four categories: early gastric cancer (EGC), high-grade intraepithelial neoplasia (HGIN), low-grade intraepithelial neoplasia (LGIN), and intestinal metaplasia with chronic gastritis (IM&CG)23. The dataset is specifically designed to support CADx tasks by enabling fine-grained characterization of gastric lesions based on histopathologically verified imaging data.

Methods

Data source and acquisition

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Peking Union Medical College Hospital (approval number: I-24PJ0272), and the requirement for informed consent was waived due to the retrospective design and the use of fully anonymized data. Endoscopic image data and corresponding histopathological records were retrospectively extracted from the institutional endoscopy and pathology information systems at Peking Union Medical College Hospital by authorized clinicians under IRB approval. The dataset comprises examinations performed between March 2014 and July 2021. All images were acquired during routine clinical gastroscopy using magnifying endoscopy with narrow-band imaging (ME-NBI). Only cases with corresponding histopathological biopsy results were considered for inclusion in the dataset.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

In addition to requiring histopathological confirmation, image quality was assessed using predefined and reproducible criteria. Images were excluded if they exhibited any of the following quality issues: (1) severe motion blur affecting lesion visualization, (2) out-of-focus regions covering >30% of the lesion area, (3) significant halation or overexposure obscuring mucosal patterns, (4) mucus or debris covering >50% of the lesion surface, or (5) significant occlusion by instruments or bubbles. Quality assessment was performed independently by two experienced endoscopists (with >5 years of experience in ME-NBI). Any disagreement was resolved by a third senior endoscopist (with >10 years of experience in ME-NBI). No automated quality assessment algorithm was used; all exclusions were based on human expert consensus.

Image-pathology matching and annotation

For each patient, lesion regions were first identified during endoscopic examination, and targeted biopsies were subsequently obtained from regions of interest. Endoscopic images captured at or around the time of biopsy were retrospectively reviewed and matched to the corresponding histopathological reports based on anatomical location and clinical records.

All histopathological diagnoses were performed by experienced gastrointestinal pathologists with more than 5–20 years of subspecialty clinical experience at a high-volume tertiary medical center. Diagnoses were initially evaluated by one pathologist and subsequently reviewed by a senior pathologist. In cases of diagnostic uncertainty, consensus discussion was conducted to improve diagnostic consistency. Based on the final pathology results, each image was assigned to one of four diagnostic categories: early gastric cancer (EGC), high-grade intraepithelial neoplasia (HGIN), low-grade intraepithelial neoplasia (LGIN), and intestinal metaplasia with chronic gastritis (IM&CG).

It should be noted that labeling is performed at the patient/procedure level, whereby all images from a given endoscopy report inherit the corresponding histopathological diagnosis. Although efforts were made during data curation to preferentially include images depicting the biopsied lesion based on anatomical location and visual assessment, this correspondence cannot be guaranteed for every image. As a result, some images may include adjacent mucosa or non-gastric regions (e.g., esophagus or duodenum), and explicit lesion-level annotations (e.g., bounding boxes or segmentation masks) are not provided. This constitutes a weakly labeled dataset, and caution should be exercised when interpreting model performance.

It should be noted that histopathological diagnoses were primarily based on biopsy specimens rather than resection specimens. As a result, sampling error may occur and could potentially lead to understaging, particularly for borderline lesions such as low-grade intraepithelial neoplasia. In addition, inter-pathologist variability is a recognized issue in gastrointestinal pathology, especially in distinguishing low-grade and high-grade intraepithelial neoplasia. Although formal inter-observer agreement metrics were not assessed due to the retrospective study design, the use of expert review and consensus discussion helps improve diagnostic consistency. Resection specimen data were not available for most cases due to the retrospective nature of the dataset. Therefore, the ground truth labels in this dataset should be interpreted as biopsy-based diagnoses rather than definitive post-resection pathology.

Data organization and metadata construction

The curated dataset consists of 11,971 images obtained from 868 patients. The number of images per patient varies due to differences in clinical imaging practices, including multiple views of the same lesion or the presence of multiple lesions in a single patient. Images were organized into four category-level directories corresponding to the diagnostic labels (EGC, HGIN, LGIN, and IM&CG), and further grouped by acquisition year. Each patient was assigned a unique anonymized identifier, which serves as the key linking image data to structured metadata.

A metadata file in CSV format (metadata.csv) was generated to accompany the dataset, providing essential contextual information for each patient. The metadata include diagnostic label, anonymized patient ID, sex, age group, endoscopic device model, and examination date. Age information was recorded in grouped intervals to minimize the risk of re-identification. Data preprocessing and de-identification

To ensure patient privacy and standardize the dataset, all images underwent a series of preprocessing procedures prior to release. Embedded metadata within the original image files were removed to eliminate hidden identifiers. Non-informative regions, including black borders and peripheral interface elements, were removed using a rule-based edge detection approach to retain only the endoscopic imaging area. Any on-screen textual information, such as timestamps or device overlays, was also cropped out. All image files were subsequently renamed using anonymized serial identifiers, and original file naming conventions and directory structures were discarded and replaced with standardized formats. Through these procedures, all direct and indirect identifiers were removed, and no information enabling patient re-identification is present in the released dataset.

All images in the dataset were acquired using a clinically deployed Olympus GIF-H260Z endoscope in conjunction with the EVIS LUCERA ELITE CV-290 processor. The original image resolutions were 1920 × 1080 pixels and 1440 × 1080 pixels, corresponding to the standard output configurations of the endoscopic system. Although the images were stored in compressed JPEG format and do not reach ultra-high-definition (e.g., 4 K) resolution, they faithfully reflect real-world clinical imaging conditions.

To ensure that the dataset focuses on diagnostically relevant content, all released images were generated by applying an edge-detection-based cropping algorithm to the original frames. This procedure removes peripheral black borders and textual overlays, retaining only the effective endoscopic field of view. As the region of interest (ROI) is automatically determined for each image, the resulting resolutions vary across samples.

Technical Validation

To assess the internal consistency, label reliability, and class separability of the EndoWLI-NBI dataset, we conducted a series of controlled classification experiments using convolutional neural networks (CNNs). These experiments were designed as a form of data-driven validation rather than algorithmic benchmarking.

Three representative CNN architectures, namely ResNet-15226, DenseNet-16127, and MobileNet28, pretrained on ImageNet, were employed to evaluate whether the dataset supports stable and reproducible learning patterns across different model structures. These architectures have been widely applied in medical image analysis, including gastrointestinal imaging tasks12,13,20,23,29.

All experiments were conducted using a nested cross-validation strategy30 to minimize overfitting and information leakage. Specifically, a five-fold outer loop was used for evaluation, while a five-fold inner loop was applied within the training data for model selection. To further ensure robustness, images from the same patient were restricted to a single partition within each fold, thereby preventing patient-level data leakage. Across all evaluated models, consistent performance was observed under the cross-validation framework, indicating that the dataset provides stable and reproducible signal patterns for supervised learning. The agreement of results across different architectures suggests that the dataset is internally coherent and not dependent on specific model assumptions.

Further insight into dataset structure is provided by the confusion matrices shown in Fig. 5. In the four-class evaluation (Fig. 5a,b), most categories demonstrate distinguishable patterns, indicating that the dataset supports meaningful class separation. However, a consistent pattern of misclassification is observed between early gastric cancer (EGC) and high-grade intraepithelial neoplasia (HGIN), with predictions frequently distributed across these two categories. This observation is reproducible across models and validation folds, suggesting that the overlap arises from intrinsic visual similarity between these pathological states rather than inconsistencies in annotation.Fig. 5Confusion matrices for averaged DenseNet-161, Resnet-152 and MobileNet in 4 Classes and 3 Classes evaluation.

In contrast, low-grade intraepithelial neoplasia (LGIN) and intestinal metaplasia with chronic gastritis (IM&CG) exhibit relatively concentrated prediction distributions, indicating clearer separability and supporting the reliability of their assigned labels. This difference in separability across categories reflects the heterogeneous difficulty levels inherent in the dataset.

To further examine this structure, a three-class evaluation was performed by merging EGC and HGIN into a single category (Fig. 5c,d). Under this configuration, prediction consistency improved across all models, with reduced misclassification rates and more concentrated confusion matrix distributions. This result supports the interpretation that the primary source of classification ambiguity lies in the boundary between high-grade lesions, rather than noise or labelling errors in the dataset.

The influence of dataset size on model stability was further evaluated by training models on progressively larger subsets of the data. Overall, these validation results indicate that the EndoWLI-NBI dataset exhibits coherent class structure and consistent annotation patterns. The observed separability and inter-class confusion align with known clinical challenges in distinguishing certain gastric lesion types, suggesting that the dataset reflects realistic diagnostic complexity. It should be noted that these validation experiments are intended as baseline evaluations using representative convolutional neural network architectures under controlled conditions, rather than as definitive performance benchmarks. The results are derived from lesion-level classification under controlled settings and do not directly reflect performance in real-world clinical environments. In addition, histopathological labels are based on biopsy specimens, which may introduce potential understaging and inter-observer variability. This should be considered when interpreting model performance. Future work may explore more advanced model architectures, such as transformer-based approaches (e.g., Vision Transformers), to further evaluate model performance on this dataset.

Usage Notes

In clinical endoscopic workflows, lesion detection (CADe) and lesion characterization (CADx) represent two sequential but distinct tasks, corresponding to lesion identification during screening and detailed characterization after detection. The EndoWLI-NBI dataset is specifically designed to support CADx applications, providing fine-grained classification of gastric lesions based on histopathological confirmation. This enables studies aimed at improving diagnostic consistency and interpretability in magnifying endoscopy with narrow-band imaging (ME-NBI).

Several important limitations should be considered when using this dataset. First, EndoWLI-NBI does not include negative samples of histologically confirmed normal gastric mucosa and is therefore not suitable for developing computer-aided detection (CADe) systems for screening or surveillance gastroscopy. In addition, all images are acquired as targeted, magnified views of lesions and do not capture the broader anatomical context encountered in routine endoscopic examinations.

All diagnostic labels in EndoWLI-NBI are derived from biopsy-confirmed histopathological diagnoses, enabling correlation between endoscopic imaging features and pathological outcomes. However, these labels correspond to histopathological categories (e.g., early gastric cancer, high-grade intraepithelial neoplasia, and low-grade intraepithelial neoplasia) rather than clinically actionable variables such as invasion depth or treatment strategy. As clinical decision-making is primarily based on factors including invasion depth and risk of lymph node metastasis8,31, results derived from this dataset should be interpreted with caution when extrapolated to treatment decision contexts32.

Due to the patient-level labeling strategy, the dataset contains weak labels. Images within the same patient or procedure are not guaranteed to exclusively depict the diagnosed lesion and may include adjacent mucosa or other anatomical regions. This should be considered when designing experiments and interpreting model performance. Additional annotation or filtering may be required for tasks that rely on precise lesion-level correspondence, such as detection or localization.

Users should also consider several technical aspects when working with the dataset. Due to variations in optical magnification and imaging distance inherent to clinical ME-NBI procedures, absolute spatial scaling is not standardized across images, and no fixed reference structure is provided. As a result, scale-dependent measurements may not be directly comparable, and normalization or scale-invariant approaches may be appropriate.

Because multiple images may originate from the same patient, data partitioning should be performed at the patient level to avoid information leakage between training and evaluation sets. The dataset also reflects the natural clinical distribution of gastric lesions, resulting in class imbalance across diagnostic categories. Analytical strategies that account for class imbalance may improve robustness in downstream applications.

Finally, the visual distinction between certain lesion types, particularly early gastric cancer and high-grade intraepithelial neoplasia, is inherently subtle and represents a known challenge in clinical gastroenterology. This characteristic should be considered when interpreting classification behaviour and may be relevant for studies focusing on fine-grained visual differentiation.
