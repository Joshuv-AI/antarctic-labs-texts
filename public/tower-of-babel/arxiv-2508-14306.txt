Machine-learning interatomic potentials achieving CCSD(T) accuracy for van-der-Waals-dominated systems via 𝚫 \mathbf{\Delta} -learning
Yuji Ikeda, Axel Forslund, Pranav Kumar, Yongliang Ou, Jong Hyun Jung, Andreas Köhn, Blazej Grabowski

Abstract
Machine-learning interatomic potentials (MLIPs) enable large-scale atomistic simulations at moderate computational cost while retaining ab initio accuracy. However, most MLIPs are trained on density-functional theory (DFT), which often falls short of chemical accuracy ( 1 kcal / mol 1\text{\,}\mathrm{k}\mathrm{c}\mathrm{a}\mathrm{l}\mathrm{/}\mathrm{m}\mathrm{o}\mathrm{l} ). Conversely, coupled-cluster methods—particularly CCSD(T), which includes single, double, and perturbative triple excitations—are considered the gold standard of computational chemistry but rarely applied to periodic systems due to their huge computational cost. Here we present a Δ \Delta -learning workflow to produce interatomic potentials with CCSD(T) accuracy for periodic systems including van der Waals (vdW) interactions. The procedure combines a dispersion-corrected tight-binding baseline with an MLIP trained on the differences of the target CCSD(T) energies from the baseline. This Δ \Delta -learning strategy enables training on compact molecular fragments while preserving transferability. The dispersion interactions are captured by including vdW-bound multimers in the training set; together with the vdW-aware tight-binding baseline, the formally local MLIP attains CCSD(T) accuracy for systems governed by long-range vdW forces. The resulting potential yields root - mean - square energy errors below 0.4 meV / atom 0.4\text{\,}\mathrm{m}\mathrm{e}\mathrm{V}\mathrm{/}\mathrm{a}\mathrm{t}\mathrm{o}\mathrm{m} on both training and test sets and reproduces electronic total atomization energies, bond lengths, harmonic vibrational frequencies, and inter-molecular interaction energies for benchmark molecular systems. We apply the method to a prototypical quasi-two-dimensional covalent organic framework (COF) composed of carbon and hydrogen. The COF structure, inter-layer binding energies, and hydrogen absorption are analyzed at CCSD(T) accuracy. Overall, the developed Δ \Delta -learning approach opens a practical route to large - scale atomistic simulations that include vdW interactions with chemical accuracy.



I
Introduction

Machine-learning interatomic potentials (MLIPs)
Behler 2016
are a new generation of interatomic potentials with flexible mathematics-oriented forms.
MLIPs can accurately reproduce complicated potential-energy surfaces (PESs) of
ab initio
calculations, typically with errors below
1\text{\,}\mathrm{m}\mathrm{e}\mathrm{V}\mathrm{/}\mathrm{a}\mathrm{t}\mathrm{o}\mathrm{m}

Grabowski et al. 2019
and at a fraction of the computational cost.

Most MLIPs to date are trained on density-functional theory (DFT) data.
However, DFT suffers from errors due to the intrinsic approximations in the exchange–correlation functionals and therefore often fails to reproduce experimental or higher-accuracy computational results
Erhard et al. 2016
.
Moreover, the local density approximation (LDA) and the generalized gradient approximation (GGA) themselves cannot capture long-range van der Waals (vdW) interactions even qualitatively due to their local or semi-local nature.
It is therefore common to add vdW interactions explicitly on top of these standard exchange–correlation functionals.
These vdW functionals can be divided into element-specific schemes (e.g., the D4 correction
Caldeweyher et al. 2019
;
Ehlert et al. 2021
) and element-independent ones (e.g., the rVV10 correction
Sabatini et al. 2013
;
Peng et al. 2016
;
Ning et al. 2022
). However, both categories remain essentially semi-empirical, as their parameters are tuned for limited benchmark sets, which can restrict their transferability.

Wavefunction-based post-Hartree–Fock (HF) methods
Szabo and Ostlund 1996
;
Helgaker et al. 2000
can overcome the issues associated with DFT, because their accuracy can be improved systematically in a non-empirical manner and vdW interactions are intrinsically included.
Particularly, the coupled-cluster method with single, double, and perturbative triple excitations (CCSD(T))
Raghavachari et al. 1989
can achieve the so-called chemical accuracy of
1\text{\,}\mathrm{k}\mathrm{c}\mathrm{a}\mathrm{l}\mathrm{/}\mathrm{m}\mathrm{o}\mathrm{l}
(
\approx

40\text{\,}\mathrm{m}\mathrm{e}\mathrm{V}\mathrm{/}\mathrm{s}\mathrm{y}\mathrm{s}\mathrm{t}\mathrm{e}\mathrm{m}
), even below typical experimental errors, and is thus regarded as the gold standard of computational chemistry.
However, the computational cost of canonical CCSD(T) scales steeply as
O(N^{7})
with the number of correlated orbitals
N
, restricting routine applications to systems containing only a few dozen atoms.
Furthermore, CCSD(T) has been implemented mainly for molecular (non-periodic) systems, and explicit periodic implementations
Booth et al. 2012
;
McClain et al. 2017
;
Gruber et al. 2018
;
Ye and Berkelbach 2023
;
Ye and Berkelbach 2024a
;
Ye and Berkelbach 2024b

are not yet in wide use.
While fragmentation strategies for molecular crystals
Hermann and Schwerdtfeger 2008
;
Yang et al. 2014
have alleviated this limitation, these are not immediately applicable for systems with periodically extended covalent bonding.

MLIPs offer a way to sidestep the drawbacks of both DFT and CCSD(T).
When trained on CCSD(T) reference data, MLIPs inherit its chemical accuracy and naturally incorporate vdW interactions while retaining the near-linear scaling of classical force fields.
Such MLIPs can be applied for both molecular and periodic systems on an equal footing.
These features make it possible to, e.g., access much longer length- and time-scales in molecular dynamics (MD) simulations (
\sim
1\,000\,000
atoms and
\sim
1\text{\,}\mathrm{n}\mathrm{s}
) with chemical accuracy.

Several CCSD(T)-quality MLIPs have already been reported.
One popular line of research trains MLIPs on CCSD(T) potentials for small molecules or molecular clusters such as protonated water clusters
Schran et al. 2017
;
Schran et al. 2018
;
Schran et al. 2019
;
Topolnicki et al. 2020
;
Schran et al. 2021
;
Beckmann et al. 2022
;
Chmiela et al. 2018
;
Sauceda et al. 2019
;
another targets liquid water
Yu et al. 2022
;
Qu et al. 2023
;
Zhu et al. 2023
;
Daru et al. 2022
;
Chen et al. 2023
.
Bogojeski
et al.

Bogojeski et al. 2020
developed CCSD(T)-based MLIPs employing the DFT electron density.
Herzog
et al.

Herzog et al. 2024
trained a CCSD(T)-based MLIP for a zeolite based on their own periodic CCSD(T) implementation.
Kim
et al.

Kim et al. 2024
developed multi-fidelity MLIPs based on one-hot encoding to reproduce the high-fidelity CCSD/CCSD(T)-level potential-energy surfaces of ethanol and aspirin molecules in combination with low-fidelity DFT data.
Tang
et al.

Tang et al. 2024
;
*Tang_NCS_5_184_2025
presented MEHnet, an E(3)
-
equivariant neural network that applies a learned CCSD(T)
-
level correction to a low
-
cost DFT Hamiltonian, enabling accurate prediction of both potential
-
energy surfaces and electronic properties for hydrocarbon molecules.
For wider chemical coverage, Smith
et al.

Smith et al. 2019
;
Smith et al. 2020
developed the ANI-1ccx potential trained on
500\,000
configurations of monomer molecules computed at the CCSD(T) level.
Despite these advances, dispersion
-
dominated interactions beyond hydrogen bonding have received far less attention in existing datasets.

In the present study, we develop a methodology that produces an interatomic potential with CCSD(T) accuracy for periodic systems including vdW interactions.
The potential combines a dispersion
-
corrected tight
-
binding baseline with an MLIP trained on the differences of the target CCSD(T) energies from the baseline.
This
\Delta
-learning strategy enables training on compact molecular fragments while preserving transferability to bulk systems.
We validate the potential by comparing electronic total atomization energies (eTAEs), bond lengths, vibrational frequencies, and intermolecular interaction energies with high
-
level reference data, and then apply it to a prototypical covalent organic framework (COF) to analyze its structure, inter
-
layer binding energies, and hydrogen absorption at CCSD(T) fidelity.

II
Methodology

II.1
Quantum-chemical calculations

Quantum-chemical calculations for the training dataset of the MLIP were performed with the MOLPRO 2024.1 program package
Werner et al. 2012
;
Werner et al. 2020
.
The energies were computed by the CCSD(T) method augmented with F12 explicit correlation
Ten-no 2004a
;
Ten-no 2004b
;
Tew et al. 2007
and employing the pair natural orbital (PNO) based local approximation, i.e., PNO-LCCSD(T)-F12
Ma and Werner 2018a
;
Ma and Werner 2021
.
The correlation-consistent polarized valence triple-
\zeta
basis sets of Dunning
Dunning 1989
augmented with diffuse functions
Kendall et al. 1992
for non-hydrogen atoms, i.e., heavy-aug-cc-pVTZ
ElSohly and Tschumper 2009
;
Papajak et al. 2011
, were considered in the present study.
Further details and the rationale behind the applied approaches are described in the following.

The computational cost of the CCSD(T) method in the traditional canonical form scales as
\mathcal{O}(N^{7})
, where
N
is the number of orbitals
Raghavachari et al. 1989
.
This steep scaling of the computational cost limits the application of CCSD(T) to only small molecules, particularly when a large basis set is used.
Local approximations, particularly those based on PNOs
Neese et al. 2009a
;
Neese et al. 2009b
;
Riplinger and Neese 2013
;
Riplinger et al. 2013
;
Pinski et al. 2015
;
Riplinger et al. 2016
;
Guo et al. 2016
;
Pavošević et al. 2016
;
Pavošević et al. 2017
;
Guo et al. 2018
;
Guo et al. 2020
;
Guo et al. 2023
;
Werner et al. 2015
;
Ma and Werner 2015
;
Schwilk et al. 2017
;
Ma et al. 2017
;
*Ma_JCTC_14_6750_2018_PNO4C
;
Ma and Werner 2018a
;
Krause and Werner 2018
;
Ma and Werner 2020
;
Ma and Werner 2021
,
dramatically reduce the computational cost and can achieve near-linear scaling.
This enables the computation for systems with a few hundreds of atoms at the CCSD(T) level.

For the F12 correction, which dramatically reduces the basis-set-incompleteness error of the correlation energy
Tew et al. 2007
, the F12b approximation
Adler et al. 2007
with the 3*A ansatz
Klopper and Samson 2002
;
Werner et al. 2007
and the diagonal fixed amplitude ansatz
Ten-no 2004a
;
Ten-no 2004b

were employed.

The many-electron integrals in the F12 method were approximated by introducing the resolution of the identity (RI) with utilizing the complementary auxiliary basis set (CABS)
Valeev 2004
.
The CABS singles correction
Adler et al. 2007
;
Knizia and Werner 2008
was considered to reduce the basis-set-incompleteness error of the Hartree–Fock energy.
All electrons including those in the core shells were considered in the correlation methods and the CABS singles correction unless otherwise noted.
The all-electron treatment substantially affects the eTAEs and the vibrational frequencies (
Section

III
), while the impact on dispersion interaction can be marginal (Sec.
S1
in the Supplemental Materials (SM)).

The basis-set-incompleteness error is typically mitigated by estimating the complete-basis-set (CBS) limit through an extrapolation combining two or more consecutive basis sets from a correlation-consistent family
Helgaker et al. 1997
;
Halkier et al. 1998
.
However, for local-correlation methods, Werner and Hansen
Werner and Hansen 2023
demonstrated that the basis-set convergence can become non-monotonic because the local approximations introduce basis-set-dependent errors, and they instead recommended using the F12 correction.
Guided by this finding, in the present study, we eschewed the basis-set extrapolation and employed the F12-corrected values obtained with the heavy-aug-cc-pVTZ basis set.

The basis-set-superposition error (BSSE), i.e., the error due to the basis-set-size difference among compared systems, is a particular type of the basis-set-incompleteness error.
The BSSE can be particularly substantial for intermolecular interaction energies.
Specifically, the total energy of a dimer system is not only modified by the interaction of the fragments but also by an improved description of the wavefunction of each fragment due to additional basis functions stemming from the other fragment.
In general, the BSSE may be addressed using, e.g., the counterpoise (CP) correction
Boys and Bernardi 1970
.
However, as written above, the F12 method dramatically reduces the basis-set-incompleteness error, which consequently also reduces the BSSE strongly
McMahon and Lane 2011
.
Combined with local-orbital methods, the BSSE is further suppressed
Saebø et al. 1993
;
Schütz et al. 1998
;
Grant Hill et al. 2006
;
Ma and Werner 2018b
.
These BSSE reductions due to the F12 and the local-orbital approaches are also confirmed in the present study for a benzene–benzene dimer with
\pi
–
\pi
stacking as detailed in Sec.
S1
in the SM.
Shortly, in the PNO-LCCSD(T)-F12 method without the CP correction, the errors from the reference data
Brauer et al. 2016
are less than
0.6\text{\,}\mathrm{k}\mathrm{c}\mathrm{a}\mathrm{l}\mathrm{/}\mathrm{m}\mathrm{o}\mathrm{l}
, which is smaller than the chemical-accuracy criterion of
1\text{\,}\mathrm{k}\mathrm{c}\mathrm{a}\mathrm{l}\mathrm{/}\mathrm{m}\mathrm{o}\mathrm{l}
.
Therefore, in the present study, we did not apply the CP correction and used the raw PNO-LCCSD(T)-F12 values for the sake of simplicity and reduction of computational cost.

The density-fitting approximation was applied to all the methods using the appropriate auxiliary basis sets for approximating Coulomb and exchange contributions to Fock matrix elements
Weigend 2002
and two-electron integrals required in the correlation methods
Weigend et al. 2002
.

For eTAEs, the spin-restricted open-shell variant of PNO-LCCSD(T)-F12, i.e., PNO-RCCSD(T)-F12
Ma and Werner 2021
was employed to compute the reference free-atom energies.
The
\rm{}^{2}S
state for H and the
\rm{}^{3}P
state for C were considered.
Note that the H atom is a single-electron system and the Hartree–Fock method already provides the corresponding energy.

For comparison, quantum-chemical calculations were also performed with other methods.
These include the the GGA in the Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof (PBE) form
Perdew et al. 1996
;
*Perdew_PRL_78_1396_1997
without and with the D4 vdW correction
Caldeweyher et al. 2017
;
Caldeweyher et al. 2019
;
Caldeweyher et al. 2020
, the second-order Møller–Plesset perturbation theory (MP2), CCSD, and CCSD(T) in the non-PNO form without and with the F12 correction, and the random-phase approximation (RPA) based on PBE Kohn–Sham orbitals.
The heavy-aug-cc-pVTZ basis set was used for these quantum-chemical calculations.

II.2
MLIP

II.2.1
Formalism

For the MLIP, we utilized the formalism of the moment-tensor potential (MTP)
Shapeev 2016
.
In the MTP formalism, the energy
E^{\mathrm{MTP}}
is the sum of contributions
V^{\mathrm{MTP}}(\mathfrak{n}_{i})
of atomic neighborhoods
\mathfrak{n}_{i}
for
N
atoms;

\displaystyle E^{\mathrm{MTP}}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}V^{\mathrm{MTP}}(\mathfrak{n}_{i}).

(1)

The neighborhood
\mathfrak{n}_{i}
is represented as a tuple;

\displaystyle\mathfrak{n}_{i}=(\{r_{i1},z_{i},z_{1}\},\dots,\{r_{ij},z_{i},z_{j}\},\dots,\{r_{iN_{\mathrm{nbh}}},z_{i},z_{N_{\mathrm{nbh}}}\}),

(2)

where
r_{ij}
are relative atomic positions,
z_{i}
and
z_{j}
are the types of central and neighboring atoms, and
N_{\mathrm{nbh}}
is the number of atoms in neighborhood.
Each contribution
V^{\mathrm{MTP}}(\mathfrak{n}_{i})
in the potential energy
E^{\mathrm{MTP}}
expands through a set of MTP basis functions
B_{\alpha}
as

\displaystyle V^{\mathrm{MTP}}(\mathfrak{n}_{i})=V_{0}(z_{i})+\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N_{\mathrm{lin}}}\xi_{\alpha}B_{\alpha}(\mathfrak{n}_{i}),

(3)

where
\xi_{\alpha}
are the linear parameters to be optimized, and
N_{\mathrm{lin}}
is the number of these parameters.

V_{0}
is the reference free-atom energy.
To define the functional form of the MTP basis functions and the number
N_{\mathrm{lin}}
, the so-called moment-tensor descriptors are introduced;

\displaystyle M_{\mu,\nu}(\mathfrak{n}_{i})=\sum_{j=1}^{N_{\mathrm{nbh}}}f_{\mu}(|r_{ij}|,z_{i},z_{j})\,r_{ij}^{\otimes\mu}.

(4)

The angular part is given as the outer product of
r_{ij}
as

\displaystyle r_{ij}^{\otimes\nu}\equiv\underbrace{r_{ij}\otimes\dots\otimes r_{ij}}_{\textrm{{$\nu$ times}}},

(5)

and thus a
\nu
-th-order tensor.
The radial part
f_{\mu}(|r_{ij}|,z_{i},z_{j})
is a polynomial function smoothly truncated at the cutoff radius
R_{\mathrm{cut}}
.
The MTP basis function
B_{\alpha}
is a scalar value obtained by tensor contraction of one or more moment-tensor descriptors
M_{\mu,\nu}
.
The number of MTP basis functions is limited by the so-called MTP level,
\mathrm{lev}_{\mathrm{max}}
.
Specifically, the level of an MTP basis function
B_{\alpha}
is defined as

\displaystyle\mathrm{lev}B_{\alpha}\equiv\sum_{p=1}^{P}\mathrm{lev}M_{\mu_{p},\nu_{p}},

(6)

where
M_{\mu_{p},\nu_{p}}
are the moment-tensor descriptors involved in
B_{\alpha}
, and

\displaystyle\mathrm{lev}M_{\mu,\nu}\equiv 2+4\mu+\nu.

(7)

For a given
\mathrm{lev}_{\mathrm{max}}
, only the MTP basis functions with
\mathrm{lev}B_{\alpha}\leq\mathrm{lev}_{\mathrm{max}}
are considered.
Further details of the MTP formalism can be found in the literature
Shapeev 2016
;
Novikov et al. 2021
;
Podryabinkin et al. 2023
.

The training and evaluation of MTPs were performed using our own implementation, which allows fixing the reference free-atom energies
V_{0}
to those obtained in the quantum-chemical calculations.
The MTP parameters were optimized in the procedure detailed in Sec.
S5
in the SM, where the main optimization method was the Broyden–Fletcher–Goldfarb–Shanno (BFGS) algorithm as implemented in SciPy
Virtanen et al. 2020
with a maximum number of 1000 iterations.
The loss function optimized was the energy per atom averaged over the configurations in the training dataset.
The cutoff radius
R_{\mathrm{cut}}
was set to
7\text{\,}\mathrm{\text{\AA}}
.

II.2.2
\Delta
-learning

In the present study, we aim to accelerate atomistic simulations for vdW-dominated periodic systems such as COFs with beyond-DFT coupled-cluster accuracy via an MTP.
The major challenge is that, while a COF has ideally a periodic framework, the highly accurate coupled-cluster methods are not routinely available with periodic boundary conditions, and therefore the training dataset must consist of molecular systems only.
Furthermore, although the forces on atoms are often included alongside the energy of each configuration to enrich the training dataset,
the analytical forces are not currently available for the PNO-LCCSD(T)-F12 method in MOLPRO, and therefore a significantly larger training dataset would be necessary to achieve comparable accuracy as the dataset with the forces.
Moreover, since vdW forces are long-range in nature, a larger cutoff radii would be required for the MTP, and thus significantly larger molecular systems in the training dataset.

To circumvent the issues above, we apply a
\Delta
-learning approach
Ramakrishnan et al. 2015
;
Zhu et al. 2019
;
Shuaibi et al. 2020
;
Meng et al. 2023
;
Huang et al. 2025
;
Hashim and Harvey 2025
with the baseline of the tight-binding method, specifically the GFN2-xTB method
Bannwarth et al. 2019
.
In this approach, the energies of the molecular configurations in the training dataset are decomposed as

\displaystyle E(\textrm{PNO-CCSD(T)-F12})=E(\textrm{GFN2-xTB})+\Delta E,

(8)

where
E(\textrm{PNO-CCSD(T)-F12})
and
E(\textrm{GFN2-xTB})
are the energies with the PNO-CCSD(T)-F12 and the GFN2-xTB methods, respectively, and
\Delta E
is the energy difference between the two methods.
An MTP is fitted to
\Delta E
based on the energies of the molecular systems in the training dataset.
By summing up the energies or the forces of GFN2-xTB and the trained MTP, we reproduce the potential energy surface (PES) of the PNO-CCSD(T)-F12 method.
We hereafter refer to the MTP trained on the
\Delta E
as the
\Delta
MTP and the sum of GFN2-xTB and
\Delta
MTP as TB+
\Delta
MTP.

In the above
\Delta
-learning approach, it is supposed that major features of the PES are already well reproduced in GFN2-xTB and that
\Delta E
provides corrections.
A smaller number of configurations are therefore required to train an MTP for
\Delta E
than for
E(\textrm{PNO-CCSD(T)-F12})
directly.
Also, since GFN2-xTB is several orders faster than DFT, and an MLIP is even faster than GFN2-xTB, the
\Delta
-learning approach makes it possible to evaluate the energies of the target system essentially only with the tight-binding cost.
Another advantage of taking GFN2-xTB as the reference is that
London dispersion interactions are already approximated with good accuracy by the D4 formalism
Caldeweyher et al. 2019
.
As demonstrated below for a benzene–benzene dimer with
\pi
–
\pi
stacking (
Section

III.5
), the London dispersion interaction in the distant region is well reproduced already in GFN2-xTB, and therefore only the region around the equilibrium distance needs to be corrected.
Therefore, the MTP needs to be trained only with a moderate cutoff radius, which can also reduce the sizes of the molecular systems in the training dataset.

The GFN2-xTB calculations were performed with the TBLITE code
1
1

1

https://tblite.readthedocs.io
in combination with the interface for the ASE code
Larsen et al. 2017
.
For the calculations of free atoms, an extension for open-shell high-spin states, i.e., spGFN2-xTB
Neugebauer et al. 2023
was employed.

The molecular systems for the training of the MTPs were selected from “components” of the target periodic COF, i.e., those that can be made by decomposing local regions of the COF with hydrogen termination.

Figure

1

In order to study also hydrogen absorption, dihydrogen (
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
) was also included.
To capture the inter-layer vdW interaction, several dimers, trimers, and tetramers composed of these monomers were also included in the training set, as exemplified in
Figure

1
(c).
In order to cover a wide range of inter-molecular distance on an equal footing, ten center-of-mass distances between each monomer were considered.
For each molecular system, MD simulations (detailed below) were conducted, and ten snapshots in the MD trajectory were selected.
Nine among them were cumulated into the master training dataset, while the last one was reserved for the validation of the MTPs after the training, which we hereafter refer to as the validation dataset.

Footnote

summarizes the considered molecular systems together with the numbers of configurations in both the datasets.

To assess the effect of the system size in the training dataset on MTP quality, four training sets were constructed from the master training dataset above based on the maximum number of benzene rings permitted in the systems. The first set included only molecules with up to two benzene rings, and the resulting
\Delta
MTP is labeled #2. Analogously, three more training sets allowed up to three, four, and five benzene rings, yielding models #3, #4, and #5, respectively.
Specifically,
\Delta
MTP#5 included 8 monomers, 12 dimers, 6 trimers, and 1 tetramer, containing at maximum 30 carbon atoms.

In addition to the molecular systems in the training datasets, we also evaluated larger molecular systems as test datasets to examine whether the trained MTPs retain predictive accuracy for systems beyond those used in training, thereby indirectly supporting their transferability to the periodic
\text{C}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{48}}}\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{24}}}
COF.
The systems for the test dataset consist of a 1,3,5-tri(4-biphenyl)benzene monomer (

\text{(}\text{C}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{6}}}\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{6}}}\text{)}\text{}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{5}}}

Further details of the geometry generation are as follows.
The geometries of the monomers were first obtained from the ChemSpider website
Pence and Williams 2010

They were then relaxed with the GFN2-xTB method using the BFGS algorithm implemented in ASE
Larsen et al. 2017
until all the forces on atoms became less than
0.001\text{\,}\mathrm{e}\mathrm{V}\mathrm{/}\mathrm{\text{\AA}}
.
The MD simulations were then performed starting from the relaxed geometries in the Langevin thermostat implemented in ASE at
300\text{\,}\mathrm{K}
with a friction parameter of
0.01\text{\,}\mathrm{f}\mathrm{s}\textsuperscript{$-$1}
for a simulation time of
1\text{\,}\mathrm{p}\mathrm{s}
with a time step of
1\text{\,}\mathrm{f}\mathrm{s}

For multimers, their constituent monomers were initially aligned along the axis corresponding to the largest principal moment of inertia, and the center-of-mass distances of the neighboring monomers were fixed to a given value during the MD simulations.
Ten values in
3.07.5\text{\,}\mathrm{\text{\AA}}

0.5\text{\,}\mathrm{\text{\AA}}

4.07.5\text{\,}\mathrm{\text{\AA}}
were considered for the test dataset.
(Shorter distances were excluded because quantum-chemical calculations often raised errors due to basis-set linear dependence.)
Thus, specifically for
\Delta
MTP#5, 1872 and 208, and 187 configurations were in the training, validation, and test test datasets.

II.2.4
Local extrapolation grade

The trained
\Delta
MTPs were also evaluated based on their uncertainties for given configurations.
Specifically, we employed the extrapolation grade
Podryabinkin and Shapeev 2017
;
Gubaev et al. 2019
defined based on D-optimality.
The extrapolation grade evaluates the similarity of the given configurations or the local atomic environments to those in the training dataset.
A value less than 1 indicates that the considered configuration of the local environment is interpolative and that the prediction should be reliable, while a higher extrapolation grade implies a larger uncertainty for properties predicted by the potential.
The extrapolation grade is advantageous because this can be evaluated only based on geometry and thus for periodic systems.
The extrapolation grade has often been employed as the uncertainty measure during active learning
Gubaev et al. 2021
;
Lysogorskiy et al. 2023
;
Ou et al. 2024
;
Kumar et al. 2025
.
As applied for high-entropy alloys in our previous study
Gubaev et al. 2021
, an extrapolation grade less than 2 should be safe for application.

The extrapolation grade can be defined both for a whole configuration and for each atom
Podryabinkin and Shapeev 2017
;
Gubaev et al. 2019
, and in the present study, we considered the latter one, which we hereafter refer to as the local extrapolation grade.
The MLIP3 package
Podryabinkin et al. 2023
was employed for the calculation of the local extrapolation grade.
The local extrapolation grades were evaluated for the molecular systems in the test dataset and for the periodic
\text{C}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{48}}}\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{30}}}
COF.

II.2.5
Comparison with ANI-1ccx

The performance of TB+
\Delta
MTP was compared with another MLIP, ANI-1ccx
Smith et al. 2019
;
Smith et al. 2020
.
The ANI-1ccx potential is an ANI-style neural network potential
Smith et al. 2017
trained on the CCSD(T)-level training dataset aiming for broad organic chemistry applications.
As many as
500\,000
configurations of molecules consisting of the elements in {H, C, N, O} were employed for the training, while
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
is absent in the dataset.
The CCSD(T)-level calculations were performed with the cc-pV
X
Z basis sets up to with
X
in {D, T}, without augmenting diffuse functions.
The cutoff radius was set to
5.2\text{\,}\mathrm{\text{\AA}}
.
Free-atom energies were obtained by linear fitting to the energy per atomic species in the training dataset.
The ANI-1ccx calculations were performed with the TorchANI code
Gao et al. 2020
with the interface for the ASE code
Larsen et al. 2017
.

For the training datasets, all the
\Delta

For the validation dataset, in contrast,
\Delta
MTP#2 shows about one order larger RMSEs than those for the training dataset.
This implies overfitting due to less configurations in the corresponding training dataset with respect to the number of MTP parameters as well as due to the incapability to predict larger molecules in the validation dataset not included in the training dataset.
With increasing the sizes of molecular systems in the training dataset, the difference between the RMSEs for the training and the validation datasets becomes smaller, demonstrating the suppression of the overfitting above.
Particularly for
\Delta
MTP#4 and
\Delta
MTP#5 at MTP levels of 16 and 20, the RMSEs for the validation dataset are about
0.4\text{\,}\mathrm{m}\mathrm{e}\mathrm{V}\mathrm{/}\mathrm{a}\mathrm{t}\mathrm{o}\mathrm{m}
or lower.
For these
\Delta
MTPs, however, while the RMSEs for the validation dataset decrease monotonously until an MTP level of 16, the RMSEs of the MTP levels of 16 and 20 are almost the same.
This implies that a further higher MTP level would not further improve the transferability of the MTP due to overfitting.
The test dataset also shows similar RMSEs as the validation datasets, as found in
Figure

2
(b), indicating that the predictive power is preserved even for the extended molecular systems not included in the training datasets.

Figure

\Delta

The extrapolation grades tend to be lower for the atoms in the exterior region of each system, and those for the atoms in the interior region tend to be higher.
Particularly for
\Delta
MTP#2, the extrapolation grades in the interior regions are higher than 2.5 and thus indicate large uncertainty for this
\Delta
MTP.
In contrast, for
\Delta
MTPs with larger training datasets, the extrapolation grades are much lower, supporting the transferability of these MTPs even for the extended systems not included in the training datasets.
The lowering of the extrapolation grades are found not only for the monomer molecule (
\text{C}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{42}}}\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{30}}}

This improvement demonstrates that the strategy to apply local MLIPs to extended systems should work in combination with the
\Delta

Below we first review the results of the quantum-chemical calculations and then discuss the quality of TB+

In
Figure

7
(a), PBE cannot predict the dimer attraction even qualitatively, as well known due to the absence of the vdW interaction in this exchange–correlation functional and consistent with a previous study
Barone et al. 2008

The agreement is largely improved in PBE-D4, as however the parameters in the D4 formalism are determined to reproduce the S66x8 values semi-empirically

The dimer attraction is qualitatively reproduced in the MP2 method.
However, the strength of the binding is largely overestimated, and the equilibrium dimer distance is substantially underestimated.
The direct RPA (dRPA) method shows a much better agreement with the reference values compared to DFT and MP2.
However, this agreement largely owes to the CP correction.
As detailed in Sec.
S3
in the SM, without the CP correction, the magnitude of the binding energy is overestimated, and particularly when an all-electron treatment is employed, the deviation is significant, meaning that the CP correction is essential if the RPA methods are used to generate a training dataset for an MLIP.
However, routine applications of the CP correction are highly cumbersome, particularly for systems with more than two molecules.
In this regard, the RPA methods are not suitable for making an MLIP applicable particularly for systems with inter-molecular interactions.

In
Figure

7
(b), PNO-LCCSD(T)-F12 agrees well with the reference values
Brauer et al. 2016

Further, unlike the RPA methods above, PNO-LCCSD(T)-F12 does not reveal a substantial BSSE (Sec.
S1

Hence, the calculations of multi-molecule systems for the training of an MLIP can be performed on an equal footing as for multimers.
Note that a recent study by Hansen
et al.

0.1\text{\,}\mathrm{k}\mathrm{c}\mathrm{a}\mathrm{l}\mathrm{/}\mathrm{m}\mathrm{o}\mathrm{l}

Since the computational cost of GFN2-xTB is much cheaper than DFT and post-HF, GFN2-xTB is a better baseline for the
\Delta

In

IV
Conclusions

We have developed a
\Delta
-learning strategy that couples a tight-binding baseline with a machine-learning interatomic potential (MLIP) to reach the gold-standard accuracy of quantum chemistry, CCSD(T), including faithful treatment of long
-
range van der Waals (vdW) interactions, for both molecular and periodic systems.
Trained solely on molecular reference data—including dispersion
-
dominated configurations—the model generalizes seamlessly to crystals and delivers root
-
mean
-
square errors (RMSEs) as low as
0.4\text{\,}\mathrm{m}\mathrm{e}\mathrm{V}\mathrm{/}\mathrm{a}\mathrm{t}\mathrm{o}\mathrm{m}
with respect to the reference PNO-CCSD(T)-F12 values.
We have showcased its capabilities by resolving the detailed structural symmetry, the harmonic vibrational frequencies, the inter-layer binding energy, and the hydrogen absorption energy of the prototypical covalent organic framework (COF),
\text{C}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{48}}}\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{30}}}
.

Although the present TB+
\Delta
MTP model is tailored to the
\text{C}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{48}}}\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{30}}}
COF, the workflow itself is transferable to other systems.
Expanding the training set to chemically diverse fragments will broaden the applicability of the potential.
The
\Delta
-learning strategy is also straightforward for porting to alternative MLIP architectures.
Such extensions will empower high
-
throughput, CCSD(T)
-
quality screening of large libraries of COFs and other vdW
-
dominated materials, thereby accelerating materials discovery.
