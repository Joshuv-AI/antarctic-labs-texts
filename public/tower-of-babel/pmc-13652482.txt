Reversible cerebral vasoconstriction syndrome as a rare postoperative complication after resection of a rosette-forming glioneuronal tumor: a case report and literature review
Authors: Ali Akil, Christin Berjaoui, Hussein Akil, Selim Nasser, Ibrahim Saikali
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction:Reversible cerebral vasoconstriction syndrome (RCVS) is an uncommon postoperative complication, rarely reported after posterior fossa tumor resections. Rosette-forming glioneuronal tumor (RGNT) is an infrequent, low-grade neoplasm of the central nervous system. To our knowledge, no prior cases of RCVS following RGNT resection have been documented.Case summary:We report a male patient in his 20s who underwent suboccipital craniotomy for resection of a cerebellar RGNT. Three weeks postoperatively, the patient presented with acute right hemiparesis and aphasia. Brain MRI demonstrated a left hemisphere watershed ischemic lesion. Magnetic resonance angiography revealed narrowing and “beading” of major intracranial arteries bilaterally, consistent with RCVS. Management included therapeutic anticoagulation, dual antiplatelet therapy, and intravenous hydration. The patient achieved complete neurological recovery.Clinical discussion:RCVS is characterized by transient, reversible multifocal cerebral arterial vasoconstriction and is associated with vasoactive triggers and surgical stress. Postoperative RCVS is rare after posterior fossa surgery, and differentiation from primary angiitis of the CNS and thromboembolic stroke is essential. Proposed mechanisms include endothelial dysfunction, surgical manipulation, and hemodynamic changes.Conclusion:RCVS can manifest as a delayed complication after posterior fossa tumor resection; Prompt diagnosis and supportive management are essential for recovery.

Introduction

First proposed by Komori in 2002, rosette-forming glioneuronal tumor (RGNT) is a very rare but benign WHO grade 1 mixed neuronal-glial tumor that most commonly arises in the posterior fossa and often presents with symptoms related to mass effect or hydrocephalus[1–3]. On the other hand, reversible cerebral vasoconstriction syndrome (RCVS) is characterized by reversible multifocal narrowing of cerebral arteries, often presenting with thunderclap headache and stroke-like symptoms. Postoperative RCVS is extremely rare and may be caused by eclampsia and several drugs, especially chemotherapeutic and vasoconstrictive agents[4]. Case presentation

A male patient in his 20s, previously healthy, a non-smoker, with no known allergies and no family history of cerebrovascular or autoimmune disease, presented with a 1-month history of progressive gait disturbance and headache. Neurological examination was notable for truncal ataxia and nystagmus, without motor, sensory, or cranial nerve deficits. MRI of the brain revealed a lesion in the cerebellar vermis with cystic and solid nodular components, heterogeneous enhancement, and mass effect on the brainstem, with superior displacement of the fourth ventricle (Fig. 1). The patient underwent resection of the tumor via a midline suboccipital craniotomy in the prone position. The procedure was performed at a well-equipped tertiary medical center by a neurosurgeon with fellowship training in vascular neurosurgery. Intraoperative findings revealed a firm, adherent, and highly vascularized lesion with no clear dissection plane, particularly at the interface with the brainstem. A thin residual layer of sticky tumor over the brainstem was intentionally left unresected to avoid neurological injury. Pathological examination confirmed the diagnosis of RGNT, WHO grade I (Fig. 1). Following surgery, the immediate postoperative course was uneventful, and the patient was discharged home on postoperative day 4. However, 10 days post-discharge, the patient presented to the emergency department (ED) for nausea and lethargy; no neurological deficits were reported at this stage, and he was discharged home. After a few days, the patient reported episodic right upper limb paresthesia and transient dysarthria, initially attributed to psychological stress. He later presented to the ED with acute right hemiparesis and aphasia. MRI showed restricted diffusion in the left centrum semi-ovale, consistent with an ischemic infarct (Fig. 2). Importantly, T2-weighted sequences revealed a narrowed appearance of both supraclinoid internal carotid arteries (ICAs) and middle cerebral arteries (MCAs), which were of normal caliber on preoperative imaging (Fig. 2). These findings supported a diagnosis of RCVS. The patient was admitted for close monitoring and appropriate treatment. Management included Lovenox 80 mg BID, dual antiplatelet therapy (Plavix BID and aspirin daily), and aggressive IV hydration (3 L/day). Neurological improvement was noted progressively, with restoration of ambulation by day 3 and gradual recovery of speech. By 2 weeks post-stroke, the patient had returned to his baseline neurological status, and repeat imaging confirmed resolution of the vasospasm (Fig. 3). Discussion

RCVS is a rare neurovascular disorder and an increasingly recognized cause of stroke, particularly in young individuals. It is characterized by transient, multifocal vasoconstriction of the cerebral arteries. It most commonly presents with sudden-onset, severe headaches (“thunderclap headaches”), but may also result in ischemic or hemorrhagic strokes, seizures, or encephalopathy[6]. Its hallmark radiologic feature is the presence of “beading” due to alternating segments of arterial narrowing and dilatation that are typically reversible within days to weeks. The pathophysiology remains incompletely understood, and RCVS can be precipitated by various triggers, including vasoactive substances, the postpartum state, and trauma[6].

RCVS is known to be associated with exposure to various vasoactive substances, particularly serotonergic agents. Among these, serotonin–norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors (SNRIs) are the most frequently reported pharmacological triggers[7]. Other reported triggers include antiemetics, such as ondansetron[7]. This association was further supported by a case report describing a patient who developed RCVS after receiving ondansetron, with radiological and clinical improvement following drug withdrawal[8]. In our case, the patient received ondansetron during the postoperative period, and while causality cannot be firmly established, its potential role as a triggering factor should be acknowledged, given the existing literature.

RCVS has previously been reported as a postoperative complication in multiple neurosurgical procedures, but it is extremely rare following posterior fossa tumor resection. Cerebral vasospasm typically manifests at approximately 6.5 to 7 days after surgery, with an observed range from 6 hours to 30 days[14].

Pituitary adenoma surgery through the transsphenoidal approach was reported to be the most common neurosurgical procedure associated with postoperative vasospasm, followed by meningioma surgery [14].

Vascular encasement of skull base meningiomas or narrowing identified in preoperative imaging seems to be a risk factor to consider[14]. Pituitary macroadenomas, which are in close proximity to the ICAs, can, in some cases and due to their large size, exert a mass effect on the carotids, so that sudden decompression or surgical manipulation can trigger endothelial dysfunction and initiate an inflammatory response that can lead to vasospasm[15].

Prolonged intraoperative hypotension and underlying atherosclerosis can elevate the risk of postoperative cerebral vasospasm, which was not encountered in our case[15].

RCVS has been described after skull base tumor resection, specifically in the anterior skull base or middle cranial fossa (after pituitary adenoma resection) and in the posterolateral skull base (after vestibular schwannoma resection)[16,17]. Some cases have suggested the presence of subarachnoid blood during the resection as a possible cause of post-resection vasospasm, while others have suggested vessel manipulation and dissection as a potential trigger for vasospasm[16,17].

In our case, no subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH) was documented on follow-up imaging, and the affected vessels were very distant from the surgical site.

Diagnostic considerations

The delayed postoperative presentation was most consistent with RCVS. Differential diagnoses included primary angiitis of the central nervous system (PACNS), thromboembolic stroke, and drug-induced vasculopathy. However, the delayed onset after surgery, normal preoperative vessel caliber, multifocal postoperative arterial narrowing, ischemic infarction, and subsequent radiographic resolution strongly favored RCVS. The complete neurological recovery and normalization of vessel caliber on follow-up imaging further supported this diagnosis. Comparative vascular imaging was particularly valuable in distinguishing RCVS from inflammatory or embolic etiologies[18,19].

Management

There is no standardized treatment algorithm for RCVS. Management focuses on trigger removal, avoidance of precipitating drugs, blood pressure optimization, and supportive care. In this patient, treatment included dual antiplatelet therapy, therapeutic anticoagulation, and aggressive intravenous hydration because severe vasoconstriction raised concern for cerebral hypoperfusion[20,21]. Calcium channel blockers (CCBs), such as nimodipine or verapamil, are sometimes used, although evidence remains limited[7,22–24]. However, CCBs were not initiated because maintaining adequate systemic blood pressure was prioritized. The patient demonstrated rapid neurological improvement, with complete clinical and radiographic recovery.

Novelty and clinical relevance

Postoperative vasospasm and RCVS have been reported after pituitary and skull-base tumor surgery, but remain uncommon following posterior fossa procedures[9,14]. Although perioperative hemorrhage and subarachnoid blood are recognized triggers, neither was identified in this case. The present report suggests that postoperative vasoconstriction may occur through alternative mechanisms, including endothelial dysfunction, inflammatory responses, or cerebrovascular autoregulatory disturbances. To our knowledge, this is the first reported case of delayed RCVS following resection of a RGNT. The case emphasizes the importance of vascular imaging in postoperative patients with delayed focal neurological deficits despite an initially uncomplicated recovery.

Strengths and limitations

Strengths of this report include serial vascular imaging demonstrating normal preoperative vessels, postoperative multifocal narrowing, and subsequent radiographic resolution. Limitations include the single-patient design, inability to establish causality, and uncertainty regarding the exact mechanism underlying vasoconstriction.

Patient perspective

A formal patient perspective was not documented for this case. The patient experienced delayed postoperative neurological deterioration but recovered completely approximately 2 weeks after the ischemic event, returning to baseline neurological function.

Conclusion

This case illustrates a rare occurrence of RCVS following resection of a RGNT from the posterior fossa. Early recognition and supportive treatment resulted in a favorable outcome. Surgeons and neurologists should consider RCVS in the differential diagnosis of delayed postoperative stroke, especially in young patients and after posterior fossa surgeries, even in the absence of typical risk factors. Thorough scrutiny of all perioperative medications is also essential to eliminate a potential offending agent.
