Differential dopaminergic modulation of the antennal lobe of Drosophila melanogaster
Authors: Gavriel Avishai, Moshe Parnas
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Olfaction plays a key role in the ability of organisms to monitor their ever-changing chemical environment. Dopamine is a key modulator of the mammalian and insect nervous systems, including the fruit fly Drosophila melanogaster. While there are few indications for dopamine signaling in the fly’s first-order olfactory processing center, the antennal lobe (AL), the role of dopamine in Drosophila early olfactory processing remains unknown. Here we reveal broad dopamine receptor expression in key AL cell types. Dopamine application differentially modulates AL output in an odor-dependent manner. Furthermore, we identify AL-innervating neurons expressing the marker for dopaminergic cells, tyrosine hydroxylase, and show that activation of these neurons leads to dopamine release in the AL. Finally, we find that endogenous dopamine release affects AL output in a similar manner to dopamine application.

Introduction

Olfaction plays a key role in the ability of organisms to monitor their ever-changing chemical environment, enabling the detection and identification of a vast array of molecules carried by the surrounding medium. These molecules, or odors, provide information which enables critical decisions to be made towards many goals, among them feeding1,2, mating3–5, and predator avoidance6,7.

Dopamine (DA) is one of the key modulators of the mammalian nervous system8. DA signaling is involved in many processes, such as learning, motor coordination, sleep, and reward perception8, and its misfunction is strongly associated with neurodegenerative and neuropsychiatric diseases, among them Parkinson’s disease (PD)9 and schizophrenia10. Furthermore, DA was shown to play a role in early olfactory processing across the animal kingdom, from nematodes to mammals, including insects11–16. In addition, impaired olfaction is a common symptom of PD, often manifesting at an early stage of the disease17.

There are ~130 known dopaminergic cells per hemisphere in the Drosophila brain, mainly found in 10 clusters18–20. These neurons innervate different areas of the brain, and are involved in multiple physiological processes, among them learning and memory21–24, sleep25, locomotion26, courtship27, and response to internal states, such as starvation28. The dopamine signal is transduced through four GPCRs: two D1-like receptors, the Gs coupled Dop1R129 and the Gq coupled Dop1R230,31, a Gi/o coupled D2-like receptor, Dop2R32, and a non-canonical receptor, DopEcR, which binds both dopamine and ecdysone, an insect hormone, and is coupled to Gs33.

In Drosophila, odors are detected by cholinergic olfactory receptor neurons (ORNs) residing in the antennae and maxillary palps34,35. ORNs project to the antennal lobe (AL) which serves as the first order olfactory processing center36. In general, each Drosophila ORN expresses only a single gene of a chemosensory receptor which binds odor molecules, alongside one or more broadly expressed co-receptors35,37–39. All ORNs expressing the same chemosensory receptor project to only one of the 51 AL glomeruli, with minor exceptions39–42. In the AL glomeruli, ORNs synapse with projection neurons (PNs) that convey olfactory signals to higher brain centers, mainly to the lateral horn (LH) and to the mushroom body (MB)43–47, which specialize in innate behaviors and in learning, respectively48–51. Most PNs are uniglomerular (i.e., their dendritic arborization is limited to a single glomerulus) excitatory cholinergic PNs (ePNs)43,47, but there is also a large population of GABAergic multiglomerular inhibitory PNs43,49,52. ORNs also project onto local interneurons (LNs), mostly inhibitory (iLNs)53,54. Most LNs are GABAergic, predominantly located at the dorsolateral AL cell cluster, providing inhibition to AL neurons through GABAA and GABAB receptors, in various forms and contexts53–57, mediating presynaptic gain control in ORN terminals57,58, postsynaptic gain control in PN dendrites56, and lateral inhibition between different AL glomeruli59,60, thus enhancing AL dynamic range and sharpening odor representations61–64. In addition, a large population of LNs, mostly located in the ventral AL cell cluster, are glutamatergic54,55,65, providing inhibition to different AL circuit components via the glutamate-gated Cl- channel GluClα55,66,67.

The AL shares many structural and functional characteristics with its vertebrate counterpart, the main olfactory bulb68–71. This, together with the lower numerical complexity of the Drosophila brain, the vast genetic toolkit available for Drosophila72–77, and the available connectomes78–81, provides a unique opportunity to study sensory processing, and olfaction in particular.

Although initially DA was not detected in the Drosophila AL19, recent reports had found multiple indications for the possible secretion of DA in the AL. Few LNs were found to be immunopositive for tyrosine hydroxylase (TH)54, and elevated activation of genetically encoded DA sensor (Dop1R1-Tango) was observed in the AL of flies fed with the DA precursor L-DOPA28. Furthermore, single-cell and single-nucleus RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq and snRNA-seq, respectively) studies detected expression of dopamine receptors (DopRs) in ORNs82,83 and in PNs84,85. Nevertheless, there is only very limited information on the effects DA may have on AL computation. DA was shown to induce cAMP elevation in PNs86. Dop2R in iLNs was shown to support anesthesia resistant memory87 while Dop1R1 in PNs supports olfactory learning88. Finally, Dop1R1 and Dop2R are required for PN dendritic targeting during development89.

However, systematic examination of the roles of DA signaling in AL circuitry was not performed. Here, using structural imaging, in vivo two-photon functional imaging, and genetic screens, we aim to shed light on the role of DA signaling modulation of early olfactory processing.

Results

DopRs are widely expressed in the AL

The expression of DopRs in AL neurons was identified to some extent in RNA-seq82–85 and was visible in gross anatomical mapping of receptor expression in the Drosophila brain90,91. However, mRNA transcripts, detected by RNA sequencing, are not necessarily translated, as was recently demonstrated in the Drosophila brain92. Thus, we aimed to validate the expression of each of the four DopRs in ORNs and ePNs, and to examine their expression in iLNs, for which no sequencing data is available. To this aim, we examined the expression of the receptors using recently developed fly lines, in which the stop codon of each DopR was replaced by T2A sequence fused to Gal490,91,93. Since T2A sequence induces ribosome skipping94, translation of the receptor-T2A-Gal4 results in the translation of the receptor and Gal4 as two separate proteins, creating Gal4 expression in a similar pattern to the receptor to which it is fused. Therefore, this method identifies translated proteins in contrast to RNA sequencing, which detects transcripts. To evaluate the expression of each receptor in ORNs, we expressed red fluorescent protein (RFP) under the Orco promoter (Orco > RFP), together with DopR-T2A-Gal4 driving UAS-mCD8::GFP reporter expression. Since separating between ORN axons and other neuronal processes in the AL is impractical, we stained and imaged antennae, in which ORN cell bodies are located. To quantify the abundance of each receptor in the ORN population, we counted RFP+GFP+ cells and divided them by the total number of RFP+ cells in each antenna, resulting in the ratio between ORNs expressing the receptor to total ORNs. As Dop1R2-T2A lines specific to two isoforms, termed RA and RC, were available90,91, both were used. In accordance with scRNA sequencing data of ORNs (Supplementary Fig. 1A), DopEcR is expressed in most ORNs, as is the case for Dop1R1, to a lower degree (Fig. 1B, C). As iLNs are key components in AL circuitry, we also examined which DopRs are expressed in iLNs. To this end we used the R29A12-LexA driver line that showed strong AL expression restricted to iLNs of the dorsolateral AL cell cluster, as well as ~90% overlap with GABAergic staining (29.83 ± 1.25 out of 32.83 ± 0.61 cells, Supplementary Fig. 2). In this iLN population we identified a high abundance of DopEcR expression, and low abundance of Dop1R1 and Dop2R (Fig. 1D, E). No expression of Dop1R2-RA or Dop1R2-RC was detected in these iLNs (Fig. 1D, E).

We used the same strategy to examine DopR expression in ePNs, using the GH146-QF driver, driving QUAS-tdTomato expression mostly (~90%) in ePNs of the anterodorsal and lateral AL cell clusters (adPNs and lPNs, respectively), and in a small subset of ventral PNs (vPNs)46,95 (collectively, will be termed ePNs). Similarly to scRNA-seq data of PNs (Supplementary Fig. 1B), we observed high expression of DopEcR and Dop1R1 in GH146 ePNs, as well as sparse and variable expression of Dop1R2-RC and moderate expression of Dop2R (Fig. 1F, G). Hence, ePNs express all four Drosophila DopRs.

Dopamine differentially affects AL neurons

The above results indicate that the AL is rich in DopRs, with broad expression of DopEcR and Dop1R1 (Fig. 1). Therefore, we examined how DA affects AL neurons’ activity. Given that both DopEcR and Dop1R1 are strongly expressed and are Gs coupled29,30,33, we expected DA application to increase cAMP levels in AL neurons. To examine this, we expressed the newly developed genetically encoded cAMP indicator G-Flamp196 in ORNs, ePNs or iLNs using Orco-Gal4, GH146-Gal4 or R29A12-LexA, respectively and recorded responses using two-photon functional imaging to local application of 1 mM DA. To prevent any circuit effects, the voltage-gated Na+ channel blocker tetrodotoxin (TTX) was used to block synaptic transmission. Neuromodulatory effects may differ greatly between glomeruli64,97–99. To examine whether DA neuromodulation also has differential effects we identified using the in vivo AL atlas100, five AL glomeruli located in the dorsal plane of the AL: D, DL3, DL1, DL5 and DM3. DA application caused a uniform cAMP elevations across glomeruli and cell types (Fig. 2B, C), in accordance with the high abundance of the Gs coupled Dop1R1 and DopEcR in ORNs and ePNs, as well as in iLNs (Fig. 1). To test whether DA also affects Ca2+ levels in AL neurons, we repeated the experiment with the genetically encoded Ca2+ indicator GCaMP7b101. DA application caused a significant elevation in the Ca2+ signal in ePNs of the five glomeruli but no increase in the Ca2+ signal was observed in ORNs or iLNs (Fig. 2D, E). Thus, although DA induces a uniform cAMP elevation, DA differentially modulates Ca2+ levels in the AL. The Gq coupled Dop1R2 is known to increase intracellular Ca2+ by triggering its release from internal stores30, and is expressed in ePNs but not in ORNs or iLNs. Alternatively, cAMP elevation can lead to increase in Ca2+ levels via direct activation of cyclic nucleotide-gated (CNG) channels and modulation of L-type Ca2+ channels102. Therefore, the ePN specific DA-induced Ca2+ elevation could stem from differential effector expression or activity downstream the cAMP pathway. To examine if the differential effect arises from differential receptor expression or from downstream processes, we elevated cAMP directly using forskolin which increases cAMP levels via adenylyl cyclase activation103. Bath application of 40 µM forskolin significantly increased Ca2+ level in all ePN glomeruli except DL3. Thus, cAMP elevation can induce Ca2+ elevation in ePNs (Supplementary Fig. 3B). Moreover, examination of RNA sequencing datasets from ORNs83 and PNs84 revealed broad expression of CNG channel subunits in PNs, but not in ORNs (Supplementary Fig. 3C). Dopamine differentially modulates the output of AL glomeruli

The above results indicate that DA induces broad cAMP elevation in AL neurons, as well as Ca2+ elevation specifically in ePNs (Fig. 2). Therefore, it is likely that DA application would also affect AL output. To test this, we used two-photon functional imaging to record the responses of ePNs to isoamyl-acetate (IAA) before and after bath application of 1 mM DA. To this end, we used the GH146-Gal4 driver line46 to drive UAS-GCaMP6f104. Glomeruli DL3, DL1, and DL5 showed enhanced odor responses following DA application that were not observed following application of only external solution (Fig. 3B, C), and DM3 showed a similar trend towards enhanced response, yet not significant (p = 0.08, Wilcoxon signed-rank test corrected for multiple comparisons). Glomerulus D showed strong oscillations, masking its odor responses, hence it was not analyzed. Previous studies have demonstrated that neuromodulation can affect the duration of odor responses thus affecting temporal sharpness58,61. To quantify a change in the temporal contrast of the responses following DA application, we calculated the sharpness coefficient ((ΔF/FPeak - ΔF/F2.5s post-pulse)/ΔF/FPeak)61 and found that DA application reduced the temporal sharpness of the response to IAA in glomeruli DL1, DL5, and DM3 (Fig. 3D). DL3 responses were excluded from this analysis due to high variability in temporal characteristics. As AL neuromodulation is often stimulus dependent64,99,105,106, we examined whether DA neuromodulation in the AL is odor dependent. To this end, 3-octanol (3-Oct) and 4-methylcyclohexanol (MCH) were used. In line with the DA-induced effect on odor response observed for IAA, the four glomeruli recorded for 3-Oct showed enhanced odor responses following DA application with significant enhancement of odor response was observed also for glomerulus DM3 (Fig. 3E–G). In the case of MCH, a significant increase in odor response was observed in only one glomerulus, DL1 (Fig. 3H–J), in contrast to the wide DA-induced effects observed for IAA and 3-Oct. Taken together, differential dopaminergic neuromodulation is also observed at the level of odor responses.

Dopamine can be released in the AL

Thus far we have demonstrated that exogenous application of DA differentially modulates AL output. We thus examined whether DA is endogenously released in the AL. Previously, it was reported that tyrosine hydroxylase (TH), a marker for dopaminergic neurons, is expressed in a number of LNs54. Given that many LNs respond to olfactory stimuli54,55,62,64, we examined whether odors can induce DA release in the AL. We used the newly developed G-protein-coupled receptor-activation-based DA2 (GRAB-DA2) indicator that increases its fluorescence upon DA binding107,108. While GRAB-DA2 was successfully expressed in ORNs and iLNs using the Orco-Gal4 and R29A12-LexA driver lines, respectively, its expression using the ePN driver lines GH146-Gal4 and GH146-LexA was developmentally lethal. Hence, To prevent GH146-Gal4>UAS-GRAB-DA2 lethal expression during development, we used tubP-Gal80[ts]109. Flies were raised until post-eclosion at 18 °C, a temperature in which Gal80[ts] efficiently repress Gal4 activity109. To allow UAS-GRAB-DA2 to express in ePNs, adult flies were transferred to 30 °C, a temperature in which Gal80[ts] does not repress Gal4 activity109, for 3–4 days prior to imaging experiments. GRAB-DA2 signal was measured using two-photon functional imaging during 5-second-long odor pulses of IAA, MCH or 3-Oct.

In ORNs and ePNs, no GRAB-DA2 responses were observed in glomeruli D, DL3, DL1, DL5, and DM3, indicating that, at least in these glomeruli, ORN axonal terminals and ePN dendrites do not receive substantial DAergic input in response to IAA, MCH or 3-Oct (Fig. 4B, C). Since odors did not cause a measurable DA release in the AL (Fig. 4B–D), we looked for an alternative strategy that could induce DA release in the AL. As it was reported that application of nicotine induces DA release in the Drosophila nervous system110, we reasoned that if DA can be released in the AL application of nicotine will result in DA release, measurable by GRAB-DA. Thus, we locally applied 100 µM nicotine in close proximity to the AL. Using this strategy, all ORN and ePN glomeruli recorded showed strong increase in GRAB-DA2 fluorescent signal following nicotine application, which was not apparent following control application of control external solution (Fig. 4F, G). A similar increase in GRAB-DA2 signal was observed in iLN processes (Fig. 4H). To validate that the increase in AL GRAB-DA2 signal was caused specifically by DA release, we bath-applied the TH inhibitor 3-Iodotyrosine (3-IY) at a 1 mg/ml concentration, and recorded GRAB-DA2 signal from iLNs in response to two consecutive nicotine puffs, separated by 5 min. A similar protocol was previously shown to significantly reduce DA release in the Drosophila nervous system due to DA depletion caused by the first stimulation and the inhibition of TH activity by 3-IY blocking DA recovery111. As expected, iLN GRAB-DA2 responses to the second nicotine stimulation in the presence of 3-IY were significantly lower compared to the control group (Supplementary Fig. 5B, C). In addition, the inhibition of DA synthesis using 3-IY also decreased GRAB-DA2 responses to the first nicotine stimulation (Supplementary Fig. 5B, C). Thus, increase in GRAB-DA2 signal in the AL is caused specifically by DA release.

Taken together, these results indicate that DA is released in the AL either by local neurons or neurons projecting to the AL. However, it appears that odor is not a main contributor to DA release in the AL.

Identification of TH+ neurons innervating the AL

The above results indicate that DA can be released in the AL (Fig. 4). However, dopaminergic neurons (DANs) innervating the AL are largely unstudied. To reveal which DANs innervate the AL to release DA we first used TH-Gal4, which is expressed in most DAN clusters in Drosophila18,20,112, to drive expression of UAS-mCD8::GFP transgene, and examined its AL innervations using confocal microscopy. To verify that TH-Gal4 labeled neurons indeed express TH we co-stained TH-Gal4 > mCD8::GFP brains with anti-TH antibody and looked for GFP+ AL processes and somata that are co-labeled by this antibody. Although most GFP+ AL processes in the AL were not stained by the anti-TH antibody, several puncti created by the TH staining co-localized to TH-Gal4 processes in the ventral region of the AL, indicating that at least some of the AL processes labeled by TH-Gal4 are of DAergic neurons (Fig. 5A). TH-Gal4 does not label all DANs18,20. Therefore, we also used R29A07-Gal4 and R29C06-Gal4, which are two driver lines generated using fragments from ple (the gene encoding TH), and appear to label AL neurons in FlyLight database74. While R29C06-Gal4 showed only faint, TH- signals in the AL (Supplementary Fig. 6A), R29A07-Gal4 clearly labeled TH+ processes in the ventral region of the AL (Supplementary Fig. 6B), similarly to TH-Gal4.

In a complementary approach, we searched the Hemibrain connectome through the neuprint server79 for known DANs innervating the AL. We found only two neurons, named PPM1201, that innervate the AL (Supplementary Fig. 6C). PPM1201 neurons were recently reported to be associated with locomotion initiation, but not with olfactory responses, although they are reciprocally connected to several LNs113. As the innervation pattern of PPM1201 neurons is similar in magnitude and location to the observed TH+ AL processes, and neurons of the PPM1/2 cluster are labeled by TH-Gal420 and R29A07-Gal4 (Supplementary Fig. 6B), it appears that the TH-Gal4 observed processes in the AL belong to PPM1201 neurons.

As TH-Gal4 labeling in the AL is dense (Fig. 5A), we could not trace the AL innervation of the TH+ soma adjacent to the AL. Hence, we attempted to achieve sparser labeling that would enable us to trace the AL processes of this soma, and to identify this cell using NeuronBridge, an online tool which enables the identification of cells from confocal stacks by matching them with neurons from the Hemibrain dataset and light microscopy datasets114. To this aim, we used the sparse predictive activity through recombinase competition (SPARC) method115, and expressed PhiC31 recombinase pan-neuronally (nSyb-PhiC31), alongside intermediate and the sparse variants of SPARC to stochastically label ~20% of TH-Gal4 cells with CsChrimson-tdTomato (SPARC2I-CsChrimson-tdTomato), or ~5% of them with mCD8::GFP (SPARC2S-mCD8::GFP). Using this approach, we achieved labeling of only the TH+ soma adjacent to the AL (mentioned above), yet its AL-innervating neurites were faintly labeled, preventing morphological analysis and identification (Fig. 5C). In addition, we detected two pairs of neurons with very large cell bodies that appear to innervate the AL and were positive to anti-TH (Fig. 5D, E). Analysis using NeuronBridge114 revealed that one neuron is a descending SIFamidergic (SIFa) neuron (Fig. 5D). According to the hemibrain79 and FlyWire80,81 connectomes SIFa has axonal terminals in the AL (Supplementary Fig. 7) and was shown to modulate ePN odor responses following starvation98. The second neuron is DNp32 (Fig. 5E). DNp32 is also a descending neuron that was shown to express the myoinhibitory peptide dromyosuppressin (DMS), which affects heart contractility116–118. Furthermore, DNp32 was reported to raise protein intake setpoint in mated females via DMS signaling119. Similar to SIFa, DNp32 has axonal terminals in the AL (Supplementary Fig. 8) but its role in the AL is not known. In line with the anti-TH signal we observed in SIFa and DNp32, neurotransmitter predictions in the Flywire dataset80,81,120 assign DA as the neurotransmitter used by both cell types.

Since vesicular monoamine transporter (Vmat) is expressed in monoaminergic neurons including DANs, we scanned the FlyLight database74 for driver lines related to the Vmat gene which innervate the AL, and found one line, R76F05-Gal4 (will be termed Vmat-Gal4). Following staining, anti-TH co-labeling with Vmat-Gal4 was observed in two AL-adjacent somata in most lobes examined (Fig. 6A). The first cell body, large in size and typically located posterior-ventral-laterally to the AL, appeared to project to higher brain regions and to the contralateral AL, similarly to the contralaterally projecting, serotonin-immunoreactive deuterocerebral (CSD) neuron, which is serotonergic121. The second cell body, located laterally to the AL, appeared to project in close proximity to the posterior plane of the AL. However, due to the dense AL labeling created by this line, we could not determine whether or not this cell innervates the AL (Fig. 6A). Hence, to allow sparse labeling which would facilitate identification of AL-innervating processes, we again used SPARC115 to label ~20% or ~5% of Vmat-Gal4 cells with mCD8::GFP. Endogenous DA release differentially enhances AL output

We have shown that exogenous DA increases AL output in response to odors, and that DA can be released to the AL endogenously by TH-Gal4 and OK371-Gal4 neurons, both of which label AL-innervating TH+ neurons. To test whether endogenous DA has a similar modulatory effect on AL output, we used a recently developed miRNA line18 to knock down TH expression in OK371-Gal4 neurons, hence diminishing the capability of the ~3 TH+ OK371-Gal4 vLNs to synthesize DA. Importantly, in contrast to the TH-Gal4 driver, OK371-Gal4 does not broadly label DANs outside the AL. Therefore, diminishing DA synthesis in OK371-Gal4 neurons should have little or no effect on DA synthesis outside the AL, minimizing the possibility of observing indirect effects of impaired DA signaling occurring outside the AL. Using 2-photon microscopy, we recorded AL responses of ePNs expressing LexAop-GCaMP7b to the odors IAA, 3-Oct and MCH. Significant reduction in odor response, accompanied by increased sharpness, was observed in glomerulus DL1 in response to IAA (Fig. 9D, G), mirroring the effects observed in the IAA response of this glomerulus following DA application (Fig. 3B–D). Discussion

Here, we presented an in-depth characterization of DA signaling in the Drosophila AL. We revealed broad expression of DopRs in ORNs, ePNs, and iLNs. DA increases cAMP levels in all these cell populations, yet it increases Ca2+ levels only in ePNs. Exogenous DA differentially enhances AL output in a glomerulus and stimulus-dependent manner. Moreover, utilizing the recently developed GRAB-DA sensors107,108, we present strong indications that DA can be released on ORNs, ePNs, and iLNs in the AL following broad nicotinic stimulation. Furthermore, we identified driver lines labeling TH+ cells innervating the AL, which their specific activation induced DA release in the AL. Finally, we revealed that endogenous DA released from these neurons differentially enhances AL output, in a glomerulus and stimulus-dependent manner.

What is the mechanism by which differential, odor-dependent effect of DA is achieved? Similar to our results with exogenous DA, application of 5-HT, another neurotransmitter of the monoamine family for which different receptor types are abundant in AL neurons106,125, was also shown to increase AL output in a differential and odor-dependent manner105. Somewhat counterintuitively, this previously reported 5-HT-induced increase in AL output is achieved together with enhancement of iLN responses, which inhibit ORN axon terminals via GABAB receptors105. As GABAergic iLNs were shown to differentially modulate AL output in a stimulus-dependent manner53,64, and express at least three of the four DopRs (Fig. 1), it is possible that the observed DA-induced enhancement in AL output is mediated by DopRs in iLNs, inhibiting iLN activity, hence reducing GABA release and differential, odor-dependent inhibition of the AL circuit. However, our results indicate that DA directly modulates Ca2+ levels only in ePNs, likely via the cAMP pathway. Furthermore, it was shown that GABAB receptor activity in the AL exerts stimulus-dependent inhibitory effects on the late phase of the odor response53, similar to the DA-induced effect described here, yet in the opposite direction. Another possible mechanism downstream the cAMP pathway might be downmodulation of GABAA activity by PKA, as was observed in rat olfactory bulb granule cells following D1 receptor activation126. In addition, DA was shown to modulate AL olfactory responses in the moth Manduca sexta, decreasing GABAA-dependent post-stimulus inhibitory period in a large population of neurons16. Therefore, it is possible that DA signaling in ePNs counteracts the activity of GABAA and GABAB receptors.

What might be the behavioral consequences of DA neuromodulation in the AL? Our results suggest that the olfactory responses of glomerulus DL1 are particularly affected by DA, exogenously applied (Fig. 3) or endogenously released (Fig. 9). DL1 glomerulus is known to strongly respond to repulsive odors such as benzaldehyde and methyl salicylate127–130, hence considered a glomerulus driving aversion. Thus, one might hypothesize that DA neuromodulation, by prolonging DL1 odor response, might be useful in certain natural settings in which robust aversion response is required. However, it was reported that even robust manipulation such as optogenetic activation of DL1 and its neighboring aversive glomerulus, DL5, reduced the valence of the attractive odor ethyl acetate (EA) at only one of two tested (EA) concentrations129. Furthermore, it was shown flies can retain normal olfactory behavior even when synaptic transmission is blocked by tetanus toxin in three glomeruli simultanously131. Hence, predicting the behavioral implication of a neuromodulatory effect in glomerulus DL1, much limited in nature than optogenetic activation or a complete silencing of synaptic transmission, could be challenging.

While strong nicotinic stimulation induced DA release in the AL, three odors which broadly activate AL glomeruli failed to do so. Which stimuli might modulate DA release in the AL? While this question was not in the scope of this study, our results demonstrate that odors do not affect DA release in the AL. It was previously shown that satiation states modulate the activity of multiple DANs28,132,133. In addition, we have shown that SIFa neurons, the descending SIFamidergic neurons, also innervate the AL and express TH (Fig. 5), thus, they potentially release DA in the AL. These neurons increase their activity upon starvation to modulate AL output via SIFamide98. Taken together, it is possible that DA release in the AL occurs following changes in satiety. However, it was shown that hunger does not affect AL DA levels28, indicating that satiation does not modulate DA release in the AL. We have also identified DNp32, another AL-innervating peptidergic neuron as expressing TH. DNp32 shows increased excitability in mated females compared to males and virgin females119. Thus, it is possible that DA is released following mating. Nevertheless, what physiological stimuli modulate DA release in the AL should be resolved in future studies.

In the vertebrate equivalent of the AL, the main olfactory bulb, most of the dopaminergic interneurons are also GABAergic134–136, capable of exerting complex combinations of neuromodulation and inhibition on different cellular targets and timescales134–136. Here we show that some of OK371-Gal4 vLNs of the glutamatergic ventro-lateral AL cluster express TH, and that activation of OK371-Gal4 neurons leads to an increase in AL GRAB-DA signal, indicating that DA is released from these cells. As glutamate is inhibitory in the AL55,67, further research on this population of vLNs may provide insights on co-transmission of fast acting inhibitory neurotransmitters and DA applicable to vertebrate olfactory systems.

Overall, we reveal that DA differentially modulates AL olfactory responses, and identify neuronal populations that release DA in the AL. Given the breadth of DopR expression in different AL cell types and the diversity of the identified AL DANs, it is highly likely that further research would shed light on other aspects of DA signaling in early olfactory processing in Drosophila.

Methods

Fly strains

Flies used in this work were raised on cornmeal agar food under a 12 h/12 h light/dark cycle at 25 °C. In case tubP-Gal80[ts] was used, flies were grown at 18 °C until post-eclosion and then transferred to 30 °C for 3–5 days. Female flies were used exclusively in all experiments with the exception of tubP-Gal80[ts], GH146-Gal4>UAS-GRAB-DA2 experiments, in which both sexes were used due to the scarcity of flies carrying the desired phenotype. The following transgenic lines were used: UAS-mCD8::GFP;Orco-RFP (BDSC 63045), Dop1R1-T2A-Gal490, Dop1R2-RA-T2A-Gal490, Dop1R2-RC-T2A-Gal491 (BDSC 84711), Dop2R-T2A-Gal490, DopEcR-T2A-Gal490, UAS-mCD8::GFP (BDSC 32185, 32186), GH146-QF,QUAS-mtdTomato (BDSC 30037), R29A12-LexA (BDSC 54127), LexAop-rCD2::RFP,UAS-mCD8::GFP (BDSC 67093), Orco-Gal4 (BDSC 26818), UAS-G-Flamp196, LexAop-G-Flamp196, GH146-Gal4 (BDSC 30026), UAS-GCaMP7b (BDSC 79029,80907), LexAop-GCaMP7b (BDSC 80915), UAS-GCaMP6f (BDSC 52869), UAS-GRAB-DA2 (BDSC 90878), tubP-Gal80[ts] (BDSC 7018), LexAop-GRAB-DA2 (BDSC 90879, 90880), TH-Gal4112, R29A07-Gal4 (BDSC 49476), R29C06-Gal4 (BDSC 49339), nSyb-phiC31115 (BDSC 84151), UAS-SPARC2-I-Syn21-CsChrimson::tdTomato115 (BDSC 84144), UAS-SPARC2-S-mCD8::GFP (BDSC 84148)115, Vmat-Gal4 (R76F05-Gal4, BDSC 41305), UAS-SPARC2-I-mCD8::GFP115 (BDSC 84147), R60F02-Gal4 (BDSC 48228), Ddc-Gal4123, R50E01-Gal4 (BDSC 38748), R51H05-Gal4 (BDSC 41275), R50G08-Gal4 (BDSC 38760), R51C07-Gal4 (BDSC 38773), R51G05-Gal4 (BDSC 38797), R27A05-Gal4 (BDSC 49208), R10H05-Gal4 (BDSC 48276), OK371-Gal4 (BDSC 26160), R49B11-Gal4 (BDSC 50411), R14B07-Gal4 (BDSC 48601), UAS-P2X2 (BDSC 91222, 91223), UAS-TH-G-miRNA18 (BDSC 93712), GH146-LexA137.

Structural imaging

For structural imaging, females 1–7 days post eclosion were dissected, fixed and stained according to a published protocol138. Briefly, brains were dissected in ice cold PBS, pH 7.4 (Hylabs), which for brains not requiring anti-TH or anti-GABA staining was supplemented with 0.3% Triton x100 (Sigma-Aldrich) (PBST). Following dissection, brains were fixed in 4% PFA in PBS for 20 min at room temperature, then quickly washed twice in PBST, followed by three additional washes in PBST, 20 min long each. Brains were then incubated for at least 40 min in blocking solution containing 5% normal goat serum (Vector Labs) in PBST. Following blocking, brains were incubated over 2–3 nights with primary antibodies in blocking solution, then washed in PBST as described after fixation (two quick washes and three 20-min washes) and incubated for additional 2–3 nights in blocking solution containing secondary antibodies. Following another similar washing procedure, brains were carefully mounted onto coverslips and embedded in Vectasheild (Vector Labs). Z-stacks were acquired at 1024x1024 pixels using Leica SP5 confocal microscope under a x20 HCX PL APO 0.70 NA or x63 HCX PL APO CS 1.40 NA oil immersion objectives. Images were processed using Fiji139. Some images were flipped horizontally/vertically for presentation purposes. Cell quantification was carried out manually, assisted by the CellCounter Fiji plugin. Primary antibodies used were Mouse anti-GFP (1:250, Abcam Ab1218), Rabbit anti-RFP (1:250, Abcam Ab62341), Rabbit anti-GABA (1:100, Sigma-Aldrich A2052), Rabbit anti-TH (1:250, Chemicon AB152), Mouse anti-Brp (nc82) (1:50, DSHB nc82), Chicken anti-GFP (1:2000, Abcam Ab13970), Rabbit anti-GFP (1:1000, Invitrogen A-11122). Secondary antibodies used were Goat Anti-Mouse IgG H&L-Alexa fluor488 (1:500, Abcam Ab150113), Goat Anti-Rabbit IgG H&L-Alexa fluor568 (1:500, Abcam Ab175471), Goat Anti-Mouse IgG H&L-Alexa fluor647 (1:500, Abcam Ab150115), Goat Anti-Chicken IgY H&L-Alexa fluor488 (1:500, Abcam Ab150173), Goat anti-Rabbit IgG (H + L)-Alexa fluor488 (1:500, Invitrogen A-11034).

Odors used

Isoamyl-acetate (IAA), 3-octanol (3-Oct) and 4-methylcylohexanol (MCH) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich at the highest purity level available. Odors were diluted in mineral oil (Sigma-Aldrich). For all odors, the final odor concentration reaching the flies was 5 × 10−2.

Functional imaging

All functional imaging experiments were performed as previously described51,64,140 using a two-photon scanning laser microscope (DF-scope installed on an Olympus BX51WI microscope, Sutter Instruments). Excitation of the fluorophores was done using a Ti-Sapphire laser (Mai-Tai HP DS, Spectra-Physics) at a 910 nm wavelength, attenuated by a Pockels cell (Conoptics) and coupled to a galvo-resonant scanner. Imaging and focusing of the laser beam onto the preparation were done using a x20, 1.0 NA XLUMPLFL20XW water immersion objective (Olympus). Detection of emitted photons was carried out by GaAsP photomultiplier tubes (Hamamatsu Photonics, H10770PA-40SEL). PMT signals were amplified (Hamamatsu Photonics HC-130-INV) and transferred to a computer running MScan 2.3.01 imaging software (Sutter Instruments). In all experiments, frames were collected at 30.96 Hz.

Sample preparation

For functional imaging, flies 2–10 days post-eclosion were briefly anesthetized on ice, and then a single fly was inserted into a small rectangular hole cut into an aluminum foil secured in a lab made recording chamber. Flies were carefully positioned in the hole to allow access to the AL area through the dorsal-anterior aspect of the head cuticle, while maintaining the antennae covered under the foil, hence dry and away from the external solution (ES). Flies were secured in the hole by warm wax, which was used also to carefully glue the proboscis to the ventral aspect of the thorax. Then, carbonated (95% O2, 5% CO2) ES, containing 103 mM NaCl, 3 mM KCl, 5 mM TES, 8 mM trehalose, 10 mM glucose, 26 mM NaHCO3, 1 mM NaH2PO4, 4 mM MgCl2, 1.5 mM CaCl2, pH 7.3 was applied to the imaging chamber, and using sharp forceps, part of the head cuticle between the ocelli and the antennae was removed to expose the dorsal-anterior aspect of the brain, including the AL. Air sacs and fat tissue were removed, and muscle 16 was cut to reduce brain movement. In experiments requiring application of pharmacological agents, the perineural sheath was gently removed from the AL.

Odor delivery

Air stream composed of carrier and compensation streams, both at a rate of 0.4 l/min controlled by mass-flow controllers (Sensiron), were delivered to the fly via a computer-controlled solenoid valves system (The Lee Company), terminating in a chemically inert 1/16’ Versilon tubing (Saint-Gobain) located ~5 mm from the antennae of the fly. Odors were delivered by replacing the compensation stream with a separate stream (also at 0.4 l/min) flowing through odor-containing 50 ml tubes. Hence, odor arriving the fly was diluted 1:1 by the carrier stream. In experiments in which multiple odors were presented to each fly, odors were presented in a random order.

Pharmacological experiments

Dopamine (Alfa Aesar A11136.5) was dissolved in DDW to make a 1 M stock solution which was stored at −20 °C in the dark. Nicotine was purchased as a 100 mM stock (in ethanol) from Sigma-Aldrich (N3876), Tetrodotoxin (Alomone Labs T-550) was dissolved in DDW to make a 2 mM stock solution which was stored at −20 °C. ATP (Sigma-Aldrich, A9187) was dissolved in DDW to make a 1 M stock solution stored in −20 °C. Forskolin (FERMENTEK FOR013B) was dissolved in DMSO to make a 40 mM stock solution stored in −20 °C. 3-iodotyrosine (Sigma-Aldrich I8250) was dissolved in ES at a concentration of 1 mg/ml (~3 mM) before each experiment. Thorough mixing was required to ensure full dissolvement of the 3-iodotyrosine.

In all experiments using pharmacology, the pharmacological agent was diluted to working concentration in ES before each experiment. In all experiments involving DA, 1 mM ascorbic acid was added to the DA working solution to prevent quick oxidation, as well as to the ES which was used as the negative control. DA solutions were kept in the dark.

In local application experiments, ES containing the pharmacological agent (or the appropriate control solution, ES and solvent) was ejected from a pulled borosilicate glass capillary located in close proximity to the AL, at 12 psi for duration of 3 s using a picoinjector (PLI-100, Medical Systems Corp.) Alexa 594 – hydrazide (Invitrogen A10438) was included in all ejected solution (10 µM) to allow monitoring of the ejections.

In experiments carried out in the presence of TTX, preparations were incubated in 1 µM TTX for 10 min prior to imaging to allow for maximal penetration of TTX to the AL, and TTX was also included in the ejected solution.

In experiments carried out in the presence of 3-IY, preparations were incubated in ES containing 1 mg/ml 3-IY for 20 min prior to imaging. Control preparations were incubated for 20 min in ES.

Analysis and quantification

For all functional imaging experiments, ROIs were manually drawn in Fiji139, and ΔF/F was calculated as previously described51,64,140. In some cases, brain movements on xy axes were corrected using TurboReg plugin141. Samples for which TurboReg could not compensate for brain movements during imaging were not analyzed. Baseline F was calculated by averaging 20 frames prior to stimulation. For experiments in which responses before and after application of pharmacology were compared within each fly, baseline fluorescence (F) before pharmacology was used to calculate ΔF/F after pharmacology. ΔF/F was calculated using Excel (Microsoft) or Python, and further analyses, statistics and plotting of the data were done in Python. Peak ΔF/F was calculated by averaging a total of 10 frames before and after the max value (including) following stimulation, and area under curve (AUC) was calculated by the trapezoid method. The frame range for which AUC was calculated was selected per experiment to match the typical response duration. Sharpness was calculated by subtracting the ΔF/F value 2.5 s (78 frames) after odor pulse termination from the peak ΔF/F value during the odor pulse, divided by peak ΔF/F value during the odor pulse. Both ΔF/F values were averaged from 10 frames.

Statistics and reproducibility

To determine the appropriate statistical method for each comparison, Shapiro-Wilk test was carried out to test for normality of each sample, followed by an appropriate test of equal variance (Bartlett’s for normally distributed data, Levene’s otherwise). For normal, independent samples, Student’s T test (equal variance) or Welch’s T-Test (non-equal variance) were used. For independent samples for which normality could not be determined, Mann–Whitney U-test (equal variance) or Brunner-Munzel test (non-equal variance) were used. For paired, normal samples with equal variances, paired T-test was used. In any other case involving paired samples, Wilcoxon sign-ranked test was used. For pair-wise comparison of multiple samples, Kruskal-Wallis test was used, followed by Dunn’s test post hoc. When correction for multiple comparisons was needed, p values were corrected using Benjamini-Hochberg False Discovery Rate procedure. For detailed statistical analysis, see Supplementary Data 1.

Connectome analysis

Neuronal skeletons from the Flywire80,81 (PPM1201, SIFa, DNp32) and hemibrain79 (SIFa, DNp32) connectomes were plotted using the navis142 and FAFBseg80,81 python libraries. Analysis of AL outputs of SIFa and DNp32 from the hemibrain79 and Flywire80,81 connectomes were carried out using neuprint-python143 and navis\FAFBseg80,81 python libraries, respectively.
