Choroidal Structural and Vascular Changes During Myopia Progression and in Response to Myopia Control Interventions: A Systematic Review
Authors: Claudio Iovino, Livio Vitiello, Alfonso Pellegrino, Dominique Bremond-Gignac, Andrzej Grzybowski, Yousif Subhi, Giuseppe Giannaccare, Aldo Vagge
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis systematic review evaluates current evidence on choroidal structural and vascular changes in myopia development and progression and assesses how these parameters are influenced by different myopia control interventions. A literature search was conducted in PubMed and Scopus for studies published between January 2000 and January 2026, following PRISMA 2020 guidelines. Eligible studies included human observational and interventional research investigating choroidal parameters, primarily choroidal thickness (CT) and choroidal vascularity index (CVI), in relation to myopia progression or treatment response, including pharmacological (atropine), optical (orthokeratology and defocus-incorporated lenses), and light-based interventions. Due to heterogeneity in study designs and outcomes, a qualitative synthesis was performed. Forty-one studies were included. Evidence from cross-sectional and longitudinal studies consistently showed reduced CT in myopic eyes, with choroidal thinning associated with axial elongation and refractive progression. Multiple interventions were associated with measurable choroidal changes, most commonly transient or sustained thickening. Optical and light-based therapies demonstrated more consistent and pronounced choroidal responses, often driven by vascular expansion, whereas atropine showed dose-dependent and variable effects. Early choroidal thickening correlated with slower axial elongation and reduced myopia progression, suggesting a predictive role. However, variability in imaging techniques, study populations, and follow-up durations limited comparability across studies. Overall, the choroid emerges as a dynamic, treatment-responsive tissue involved in myopia pathophysiology, and choroidal parameters (particularly early changes in CT and vascularity) show promise as functional and prognostic biomarkers for myopia control. Further standardized, longitudinal studies are needed to clarify underlying mechanisms and to establish the choroid’s role in personalized myopia management.Keywords: choroidal parameters, choroidal vascularity index, myopia, myopia control

Introduction

The increasing prevalence of myopia has become a public health concern. The global prevalence of myopia is predicted to increase from 27% of the world’s population in 2010 up to 52% by 2050.1 Myopia also raises the risk of threatening ocular disorders including cataract, glaucoma and myopic retinal alterations. The social and financial burden of both uncorrected myopia and the cost of myopic complications’ treatment is raising concerns around the world. Different biological mechanisms are involved in myopia development and progression.

One of the major structural determinants is in the scleral extracellular matrix resulting in scleral shape changes.2 Recent evidence suggests that the choroid may also contribute to myopic pathogenesis, in particular, its thinning precedes the development of myopia.3 Studies in animal models of induced hyperopia and myopia have further confirmed that choroidal vascular changes precede scleral remodeling and axial length changes.4,5 The choroid can directly influence the scleral biochemical properties by secreting various matrix metalloproteinases and growth factors.6

The choroid represents the main source of oxygen and nutrients to the outer retina and regulates the intraocular pressure via vasomotor control of blood flow7,8 and ocular temperature8,9 through the sympathetic and parasympathetic nervous systems.10

Nevertheless, the choroid also plays a critical role in the mechanisms underlying the development of refractive errors and the control of the eye growth.11,12 In fact, changes of choroidal thickness (CT) occur in several chorioretinal disorders, such as central serous chorioretinopathy, polypoidal choroidal vasculopathy and neovascular age-related macular degeneration,13,14 and in response to retinal defocus.15,16

While previous studies have examined choroidal changes in myopia, the role of the choroid as a unifying mechanism across different myopia control strategies remains unclear. In fact, the evidence remains fragmented across heterogeneous populations, imaging protocols, and intervention types, and no consensus exists on whether choroidal parameters can serve as reliable biomarkers of myopia progression or treatment response.

Clarifying how the choroid behaves during myopia progression and under treatment is clinically important: a reliable, non-invasive, OCT-derived biomarker of treatment response could enable earlier identification of responders and non-responders, support individualized treatment decisions, and shorten the time currently needed to judge efficacy through axial length changes alone.

A comprehensive synthesis integrating both structural and vascular parameters across the full spectrum of myopia control strategies is currently lacking. Therefore, the aim of this systematic review is to summarize current evidence on choroidal structural and vascular changes during myopia development and progression, and to evaluate how these parameters are modified by the main myopia control interventions, in order to clarify the potential role of the choroid as a biomarker and therapeutic mediator.

Materials and Methods

Protocol and Reporting Guidelines

This systematic review was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA 2020) guidelines.17 The review protocol was not registered in a public database. We acknowledge that the absence of prospective registration may reduce methodological transparency and increase the potential for selective reporting; to mitigate this, the eligibility criteria, search strategy, and outcomes of interest were defined a priority. The research question was formulated using the PICO framework:

Population (P): Children, adolescents and adults with myopia or pre-myopia.

Population (P): Children, adolescents and adults with myopia or pre-myopia.

Intervention (I): Pharmacological (atropine), optical (orthokeratology, defocus lenses), or light-based (repeated low-level red-light therapy) myopia control strategies.

Intervention (I): Pharmacological (atropine), optical (orthokeratology, defocus lenses), or light-based (repeated low-level red-light therapy) myopia control strategies.

Comparator (C): Untreated eyes, placebo, or alternative intervention.

Outcome (O): Choroidal structural parameters; CT, choroidal vascularity index (CVI) and their association with myopia development or progression.

Search Strategy

A comprehensive literature search was performed using two electronic databases: PubMed and Scopus, which jointly provide broad coverage of the biomedical and ophthalmic literature.

The search was last run on 1 February 2026 and covered the period from 1 January 2000 to 31 January 2026. The strategy combined Medical Subject Headings (MeSH, in PubMed) and free-text terms searched in title and abstract, organized in three concept blocks: myopia, choroidal parameters, and myopia-control interventions, combined with the Boolean operators AND/OR.

In PubMed, the following string was used: (“Myopia”[Mesh] OR myopia[tiab] OR “myopic progression”[tiab]) AND (“Choroid”[Mesh] OR choroid[tiab] OR “choroidal thickness”[tiab] OR “choroidal vascularity index”[tiab]) AND (“Atropine”[Mesh] OR atropine[tiab] OR “Orthokeratologic Procedures”[Mesh] OR orthokeratology[tiab] OR “defocus lenses”[tiab] OR “repeated low-level red-light therapy”[tiab]), with filters limiting results to English-language and human studies.

In Scopus, the equivalent string was: TITLE-ABS-KEY (myopia OR “myopic progression”) AND TITLE-ABS-KEY (choroid OR “choroidal thickness” OR “choroidal vascularity index”) AND TITLE-ABS-KEY (atropine OR orthokeratology OR “defocus lenses” OR “repeated low-level red-light therapy”), limited to English-language documents (LIMIT-TO (LANGUAGE,“English”)) and to the publication years 2000–2026.

Reference lists of retrieved articles and relevant reviews were additionally screened by hand to identify further eligible studies.

Eligibility Criteria

Only English original research articles (observational or interventional studies) published in peer-reviewed journals, involving human subjects (children, adolescents, or adults) and concerning the evaluation of choroidal parameters to investigate myopia development, progression, or myopia control interventions were considered eligible for inclusion in this systematic review. Conversely, reviews, case reports, conference abstracts, editorials, animal studies, studies not reporting choroidal outcomes and non-English articles were excluded for qualitative analysis of this review.

Study Selection

Titles and abstracts retrieved from the database searches were screened independently by two reviewers (C.I. and L.V.) to assess relevance to the topic of this review. Full-text articles of potentially eligible studies were subsequently assessed for inclusion. Studies that appeared potentially eligible based on title/abstract but were ultimately excluded after full-text review were rejected primarily due to absence of quantitative choroidal outcomes, mixed populations without stratification by refractive status, or insufficient reporting of intervention-specific data. Disagreements were resolved by discussion and consensus. If consensus could not be reached, the author group would be involved for final decision.

Among the 396 records excluded after title and abstract screening, the main reasons for exclusion were: studies not reporting choroidal outcomes (n = 212), reviews, editorials, or conference abstracts (n = 52), animal studies (n = 42), non-English articles (n = 18), and studies not involving myopia or myopia control interventions (n = 72). Of the 14 full-text articles that could not be retrieved, attempts to obtain them through institutional access and direct author contact were unsuccessful.

Data Extraction

Full texts of potentially eligible articles were then evaluated by two reviewers (C.I. and L.V.) for inclusion based on the predefined criteria. From each included study, relevant information was extracted using a standardized extraction form, including study design (cross-sectional, longitudinal, randomized controlled trial, cohort), population characteristics (sample size, age range), type of intervention (pharmacological, optical, light-based), duration of follow-up, choroidal parameters assessed, and main outcomes related to axial length and refractive progression. Disagreements were resolved by discussion and consensus. If consensus could not be reached, the author group would be involved for final decision.

Risk of Bias Assessment

The methodological quality of the included studies was assessed using validated tools: Cochrane Risk of Bias 218 for randomized controlled trials and Newcastle–Ottawa Scale19 for observational studies. Studies were categorized as low, moderate, or high risk of bias. The assessment was performed independently by two reviewers (C.I. and L.V.), with disagreements resolved by discussion. If consensus could not be reached, the author group would be involved for final decision. Formal inter-reviewer agreement statistics (eg, Cohen’s kappa) were not calculated; the independent double assessment with consensus-based resolution was nonetheless adopted to minimize selection and risk-of-bias assessment bias.

Data Synthesis

Given the heterogeneity of study designs, populations, interventions, and outcome measures, a quantitative meta-analysis was not feasible. Instead, qualitative synthesis was performed. Studies were grouped according to the type of myopia control intervention (pharmacological, optical, or light-based), and results were interpreted with a focus on common patterns, temporal relationships, and mechanistic implications of choroidal changes in relation to ocular growth.

Publication bias was not assessed through funnel plot analysis, as this method is applicable to meta-analyses and not to qualitative syntheses.

Results

Choroidal Vasculature Evaluation: Choroidal Thickness and Choroidal Vascularity Index

There are two main different ways to quantitatively measure and assess the choroid in research studies and clinical trials: CT and CVI.20–23 CT is usually measured with optical coherence tomography (OCT) as the distance between Bruch’s membrane and the sclera-choroidal interface under the fovea with enhanced depth imaging (EDI) mode.24 According to various clinical studies, CT has often been considered as a biomarker of both systemic,25–27 and ocular health.28–30 However, a noticeable discrepancy in CT across different clinical investigations has been identified,31–33 generating no shared consensus. This raises the question of whether there is a more reliable parameter to evaluate choroidal vasculature, and which specific choroidal layer is involved with increased or decreased CT.

For this reason, CVI has been proposed as a new OCT quantitative parameter measuring the vasculature status of the choroid, and it is calculated as the ratio of luminal choroidal area (LCA) over total choroidal area (TCA).20,21 Sonoda and colleagues initially presented a method for assessing subfoveal LCA and stromal choroidal area (SCA) using the free software ImageJ (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA) for image binarization of the EDI spectral domain OCT foveal scan.34,35 Briefly, the OCT image is loaded in ImageJ, and a 1500 µm wide region of interest centered on the fovea is selected using the polygon tool. To determine the TCA, the top boundary of the region of interest is traced along the choroidal–retinal pigment epithelium junction and the lower boundary along the sclerochoroidal junction. Then, the average value from the LCA of three choroidal vessels chosen with the oval selection tool is used to modify the brightness of the image, and Niblack’s autolocal threshold is utilized to binarize the picture and distinguish between the LCA and the SCA once it has been converted to an 8-bit image. After converting the picture to red, green, and blue, the dark pixels, which represent the LCA, are chosen using the color threshold tool. Finally, the TCA and LCA are measured. Subtracting LCA from TCA yields the SCA, while the LCA to TCA ratio is computed.

Several further changes to the first algorithm have been suggested. Specifically, the ratio between LCA and TCA was renamed as CVI by Agrawal and co-authors,20 along with certain methodological modifications. First, brightness is not changed in their method because doing this would lessen the contrast between LCA and SCA and might interfere with the autolocal threshold procedure. Second, the picture binarization is carried out before the region selection to improve the visibility of the sclerochoroidal interface.20 Moreover, an automated software able to significantly reduce the time of measurements and to compensate shadowing artifacts was introduced by Vupparaboina et al.36

Included Studies and Risk of Bias

The database search identified 534 records. After removing duplicates and screening titles and abstracts, 41 studies were included in the qualitative synthesis.

PRISMA flow diagram of study selection.

Risk of bias was formally assessed for all included studies. Among the 21 randomized trials evaluated with RoB 2, 7 were judged at low risk of bias and 14 as raising some concerns, with no trial at high risk. Concerns most frequently arose in the domain of outcome measurement (D4), as blinding of choroidal-image assessors was seldom reported, and, less often, in the randomization domain (D1), where allocation concealment was not always described. Among the 20 observational studies evaluated with the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale, 11 were rated as good and 9 as fair quality, with none rated poor; the most common limitations were in the comparability domain, reflecting incomplete adjustment for confounders such as axial length and diurnal choroidal variation, and, in cross-sectional or acute studies, the absence of longitudinal follow-up. These considerations were incorporated into the interpretation of the evidence: findings from studies at higher risk of bias or lower methodological quality were weighted more cautiously, and consistency of effects across studies of differing design and quality was used as an additional criterion when drawing conclusions.

Choroidal Changes and Refractive Status

Over the last decade, the choroid has emerged as a key structure in refractive development, particularly in the onset and progression of myopia. Advances in swept-source OCT (SS-OCT) and EDI have enabled increasingly precise in vivo characterization of CT and vascular parameters across childhood, adolescence, and early adulthood. Nowadays, the choroid is not considered merely as a passive vascular layer, but a dynamic and biologically responsive tissue involved in ocular growth regulation.

Early cross-sectional studies, notably those by Jin et al37 and later reinforced by Qi et al38 consistently demonstrated that CT is inversely related to myopic refractive error in children. In fact, hyperopic and emmetropic eyes exhibit a thicker subfoveal and parafoveal CT compared with myopic eyes, with axial length emerging as the strongest structural correlate. These findings align with adult data summarized by Read et al39 and suggest that choroidal thinning may accompany axial elongation during childhood. However, cross-sectional designs inherently limit causal inference. While thinner choroids are associated with myopia, these studies cannot determine whether choroidal thinning precedes axial elongation or it is a secondary consequence. This limitation prompted a shift toward longitudinal cohort studies.

Longitudinal investigations have substantially strengthened the hypothesis that choroidal changes are temporally linked to refractive shifts. Jin et al3 and Xu et al40 independently showed that children experiencing a myopic shift demonstrate significant choroidal thinning over time, whereas non-myopic or stable eyes show relative preservation or even thickening of the choroid. Importantly, these changes were often detectable concurrent with measurable refractive progression, supporting a role for the choroid as an early refractive biomarker.41 More recent cohort studies with longer follow-up, such as those by Shen et al42 and Lee et al,43 further refined this concept by showing that the magnitude and direction of choroidal change vary according to refractive trajectory. Children who developed or progressed in myopia exhibited sustained choroidal thinning, while non-myopic children showed stable or thickening patterns.42,43 Notably, several of these longitudinal studies also included hyperopic and emmetropic children as comparator groups,37,38,42,43 confirming that refractive trajectory (rather than age alone) is the primary determinant of the direction and magnitude of choroidal change over time. These findings argue against a uniform age-related decline in CT and instead highlight refractive status as a key determinant.

An important conceptual advance comes from studies investigating short-term choroidal changes. Wu et al44 demonstrated that subtle choroidal thinning over relatively brief periods may predict future myopic shift, even before clinically significant refractive change occurs. This aligns with experimental and clinical observations summarized by Read et al,39 where short-term choroidal thickening or thinning reflects retinal defocus signaling. Such findings reinforce the hypothesis that the choroid acts as a rapid-response tissue, potentially mediating biochemical or biomechanical signals between the retina and sclera.

More recent studies have expanded beyond simple CT measurements, assessing CVI and blood flow. Qi et al38 and Ho et al45 reported an altered choroidal vascular profile in myopic children, including reduced CVI, suggesting that vascular remodeling could accompany or precede structural thinning. These vascular changes may reflect reduced metabolic demand, compromised perfusion, or dysregulated angiogenic signaling during ocular elongation.45

In adults with pathological myopia, Wang et al46 demonstrated a decrease in all choroidal areas and a CT thinning correlated with visual acuity loss, indicating that choroidal compromise may have functional consequences beyond refractive error alone. While pediatric data are still limited, these findings raise concern that early choroidal changes could have long-term implications for retinal health. Collectively, these studies support a model in which the choroid plays a dual role: a biomechanical modulator, adjusting thickness in response to visual signals and axial growth demands; and a vascular-metabolic regulator, whose perfusion and structural integrity may influence scleral remodeling and retinal function (Table 1).

Choroidal Changes in Myopia: The Effects of Atropine

Pharmacological interventions, particularly atropine, have been extensively investigated for myopia control, yet their effects on the choroid appear heterogeneous and dose dependent.47 Understanding how atropine modulates CT and choroidal structures is essential to clarify whether the choroid acts primarily as a mediator or a secondary marker of treatment efficacy.

Chiang and Phillips48 demonstrated that the choroid behaves as a highly dynamic structure capable of rapidly responding to visual stimuli related to myopic signaling. In young adults, exposure to hyperopic defocus induced significant subfoveal and parafoveal choroidal thinning (approximately 7–8 µm within one hour). This response was completely suppressed after a single instillation of 0.5% atropine, without any increase in baseline CT, suggesting that atropine could primarily block the dynamic choroidal response to hyperopic defocus rather than inducing a static thickening.

In children, most clinical studies indicate that the effect of low-dose atropine on CT is modest and variable. In a randomized trial, Kong et al49 found no significant differences in CT between children treated with 0.01% atropine alone and those receiving atropine combined with auricular stimulation over six months, despite differences in refractive progression. Similarly, Wang et al50 reported a non-significant trend toward increased subfoveal CT after three months of 0.01% atropine, with no associated changes in choriocapillaris flow.

Several studies suggest that choroidal thickening occurs early during treatment and shows a dose-dependent pattern. Yam et al51 reported a significant increase in CT after two years with 0.05% atropine, a smaller increase with 0.025%, and no significant change with 0.01%. Choroidal thickening appeared within the first four months and then stabilized, and mediation analysis suggested that approximately 18.5% of the therapeutic effect of 0.05% atropine may be explained by changes in CT.51

Data from European cohorts further support a dose-dependent but relatively limited choroidal response to low-dose atropine. Hvid-Hansen et al52 showed greater preservation or increase of CT with a 0.1% loading dose compared to continuous 0.01% therapy or placebo, although axial length remained the strongest mediator of treatment efficacy. In contrast, Hansen et al53 found no significant atropine-related changes in macular CT over three years, but confirmed a strong association between thinner baseline choroid and greater axial elongation in untreated eyes.

More detailed structural analyses indicated that higher atropine concentrations may induce measurable choroidal remodeling. Xu et al54 showed that 1% atropine produced significant increases in TCA, LCA, and SCA, which regressed after dose reduction to 0.01%. These structural changes were associated with reduced axial elongation and may contribute to rebound phenomena. Consistent with this, short-term treatment with 0.1% atropine resulted in rapid and marked choroidal thickening (+22 µm in one month) and increased stromal volume, which correlated inversely with axial elongation.55

Long-term studies with 0.01% atropine showed mixed results. Lee et al56 reported physiological choroidal thickening during two years of treatment in both atropine and placebo groups, but noted that choroidal thickening continued during washout only in placebo-treated children, suggesting a persistent modulatory effect of atropine on choroidal adaptation. The MOSAIC trial57 similarly demonstrated stability of macular CT with 0.01% atropine over two years, in contrast to progressive thinning in placebo-treated children, and showed that preservation of CT was associated with slower myopic progression, in accordance with similar data by Parida and colleagues.58

Of note, intermittent dosing with 0.01% or 0.05% atropine administered every other day did not result in significant choroidal changes over one year,59 suggesting that dosing frequency, in addition to concentration, may influence the consistency of choroidal modulation.

Combination therapies appear to amplify the choroidal response. A Japanese prospective study60 reported greater increases in CT and blood flow with combined orthokeratology (Ortho-K) and 0.01% atropine compared with atropine alone, with both parameters inversely correlated with axial elongation.

Overall, the available evidence seems to indicate that atropine may influence the choroid in a dose-dependent and time-sensitive manner. In fact, high concentrations seem to induce rapid and substantial choroidal thickening, while low-dose atropine (0.01%) primarily appears to stabilize CT and suppress physiological or myopia-related thinning rather than consistently increasing it. Although changes in CT are not the sole mediator of atropine’s antimyopic effect, multiple studies support a contributory role of choroidal structural and vascular modulation in slowing axial elongation, particularly at higher doses or when combined with optical interventions (Table 2).

Choroidal Changes in Myopia: The Effects of Spectacle Lenses and Orthokeratology

Optical strategies designed to impose myopic defocus represent a cornerstone of contemporary myopia management and provide a valuable model for studying visually driven choroidal adaptation.61 Increasing evidence suggests that the choroid responds dynamically to these interventions, with changes in thickness and vascularity closely linked to axial growth modulation.

Breher et al62 investigated the short-term effect of different multifocal contact lens designs on CT in myopic adults. After 30 minutes of wear, only minimal and non-significant increases in macular CT were observed across all lens types, including center-distance, center-near, and full-defocus designs. These findings suggest that brief exposure to multifocal optics does not induce meaningful acute choroidal modulation in adults, indicating that any structural choroidal effects of multifocal lenses likely require longer or cumulative exposure.

In contrast, Ortho-K has been consistently associated with rapid and sustained choroidal thickening in children. Li et al63 showed that subfoveal CT increased by approximately 16 µm within one month of Ortho-K wear and remained stable over 12 months, while no comparable change was observed in spectacle-wearing controls. Importantly, CT returned to baseline after discontinuation of Ortho-K, accompanied by renewed axial elongation. The magnitude of early choroidal thickening was negatively associated with axial length elongation over one year, suggesting that the initial choroidal response may predict treatment efficacy.63

Experimental evidence further supports the dynamic nature of the choroidal response to optical stimuli. Delshad et al64 demonstrated that continuous hyperopic defocus induces axial length elongation and choroidal thinning in young adults, whereas brief interruptions of clear vision markedly attenuate both effects. These results indicate that not only the type but also the temporal pattern of defocus exposure can rapidly modulate choroidal physiology.

Longer-term Ortho-K studies confirm both structural and functional relevance of choroidal changes. Chen et al65 reported significant increases in subfoveal CT after one year of Ortho-K wear, with greater thickening associated with reduced axial length elongation. However, the absence of a control group in this study limits the interpretability of these findings.

Xu et al66 further showed that Ortho-K may induce increases in TCA and LCA, particularly within the first six months, and that a larger baseline LCA is associated with greater control of axial length elongation.

Hao and Zhao67 observed a rapid increase in subfoveal CT within one month in children treated with orthokeratology, either alone or combined with 0.01% atropine, with greater thickening consistently associated with less axial elongation. These findings further support the predominant role of orthokeratology in driving choroidal thickening compared to pharmacological treatment alone.

Spectacle lenses incorporating myopic defocus also produce measurable choroidal effects. In a two-year randomized trial, Chun et al68 found that defocus incorporating multiple segments lenses induced a rapid increase in subfoveal CT within one week, followed by sustained thickening over 24 months, whereas progressive thinning occurred with single-vision lenses. Greater choroidal thickening was associated with slower axial length elongation.68 Similarly, Huang et al69 reported that highly aspheric lenses attenuated or reversed the choroidal thinning typically observed with conventional spectacles, with changes in CT negatively correlated with axial length elongation.

Short-term experimental studies also suggest that peripheral myopic defocus alone is sufficient to induce acute choroidal thickening. Paulasto et al70 showed that exposure to highly aspheric lenslets or full-field myopic defocus for 90 minutes resulted in a modest but significant increase in subfoveal CT in young adults, comparable between the two defocus conditions. However, no concurrent changes in axial length were observed, indicating that acute choroidal thickening precedes measurable biometric effects.70

Finally, in a large, randomized trial, Han et al71 demonstrated that children wearing MYOGEN defocus-inducing lenses experienced less myopic progression and axial length elongation than those wearing single-vision lenses. Changes in CT were associated with refractive and axial outcomes in the defocus lens group, supporting the concept that choroidal modulation may contribute to the antimyopic effects of optical interventions.

Overall, these studies indicate that optical strategies incorporating myopic defocus can induce rapid and, in many cases, sustained choroidal thickening (Table 3). While short-term choroidal changes alone are not sufficient to explain myopia control, their consistent association with reduced axial elongation supports the choroid as a sensitive biomarker and a potential contributor to the mechanisms underlying optical myopia control.

Choroidal Changes in Myopia: The Effects of Repeated Low-Level Red-Light Therapy

More recently, light-based interventions such as repeated low-level red-light therapy (RLRL) have emerged as a novel and highly effective approach for myopia control.72 These therapies offer a unique opportunity to explore the choroid not only as a responsive tissue, but also as a potential primary biological target and predictor of treatment success.

A growing body of longitudinal evidence consistently demonstrates that RLRL therapy may induce marked, early, and sustained choroidal thickening in children, supporting a central role of the choroid in mediating the antimyopic effects of light-based interventions.

In the pivotal longitudinal study by Xiong et al,73 macular CT was comparable between treated and control groups at baseline, but increased rapidly in RLRL-treated children, peaking at approximately +14.8 µm (average macular CT) and +18.0 µm (central macular CT) at 1 month, followed by a gradual reduction at 3 and 6 months, and recovering to approximately +9.1 µm (average) and +7.3 µm (central) at 12 months, in contrast to the progressive thinning observed in controls. Importantly, greater early choroidal thickening at 3 months was strongly associated with reduced axial length elongation at one year, indicating that the initial choroidal response predicts treatment efficacy.73

A secondary structural analysis74 of the same randomized trial73 clarified that the observed choroidal thickening is driven by increases in both the LCA and SCA, with the proportional increase in LCA being greater than that of SCA, consistent with vasodilation and enhanced perfusion. These structural changes were associated with the slowing of axial length elongation in the RLRL group, reinforcing the hypothesis that choroidal vascular modulation represents a key mechanistic pathway.74

Evidence of a similar choroidal response has been reported not only in myopic children but also in those with pre-myopia. Liu et al75 demonstrated that RLRL therapy induces significant choroidal thickening in pre-myopic children, with a peak increase of approximately +19.6 µm at 3 months, followed by a gradual reduction to approximately +12.0 µm at 12 months. Significant increases in choroidal vessel volume and choriocapillaris perfusion area were also observed, particularly in the parafoveal and perifoveal regions, suggesting a predominantly vascular mechanism. These changes were associated with stabilization of refraction and reduction in axial length elongation, supporting a preventive role of choroidal modulation in the pre-myopic stage.75

Further longitudinal studies in myopic children76 confirmed that RLRL therapy could stimulate sustained choroidal thickening of approximately +25 µm over 12 months, with greater thickening consistently associated with less axial length elongation. Although detailed structural analyses were not always performed, the observed patterns align with a predominantly vascular mechanism, consistent with earlier findings.76

The prognostic value of choroidal response has been particularly emphasized in pre-myopic populations. Xiang et al77 showed that early choroidal thickening (approximately +10–15 µm within the first three months) independently predicted both reduced myopic shift and a lower risk of developing myopia over one year. Children with a stronger early choroidal response were significantly more likely to resist myopia onset, highlighting CT as a clinically relevant predictive biomarker.

The biological plausibility of these findings is further reinforced by combined preclinical and clinical evidence. In a randomized controlled trial, Wei et al78 demonstrated consistent choroidal thickening in RLRL-treated children (+12–18 µm at 3 months and up to +20–30 µm at 6–12 months), accompanied by predominantly vascular changes. Parallel experimental models showed concordant alterations in choroidal vascularization following red light exposure, strengthening the interpretation that RLRL could act directly on choroidal hemodynamics and perfusion.78

Comparative data further underscore the specificity of the choroidal response to RLRL therapy. Pang et al79 reported that RLRL therapy can induce earlier, larger, and predominantly vascular choroidal thickening than 0.01% atropine. While atropine treatment was associated mainly with stabilization or modest thickening (approximately +5–10 µm), RLRL therapy produced increases of +25–30 µm, with choroidal changes more strongly correlated with reduced axial length elongation. These findings suggest that RLRL therapy may induce a stronger and predominantly vascular choroidal response compared to low-dose atropine, though whether this reflects a more direct mediating role of the choroid remains to be established.

Finally, recent machine learning analyses have provided quantitative confirmation of the prognostic importance of choroidal parameters. In fact, Hu et al80 demonstrated that baseline CT and its early change were among the strongest predictors of RLRL efficacy, often matching or exceeding the predictive value of axial length. Children with a greater early choroidal response were more likely to achieve effective myopia control, suggesting that choroidal plasticity could reflect individual biological sensitivity to light stimulation.80

Collectively, all these studies provide significant and converging evidence that RLRL therapy may induce a rapid, sustained, and predominantly vascular thickening of the choroid, closely linked to reduced axial length elongation and myopia progression (Table 4). These findings position the choroid not merely as a passive marker, but as a dynamic and functionally involved tissue, in addition to a promising prognostic biomarker, in both therapeutic and preventive light-based strategies for myopia control.

Discussion

This systematic review provides converging evidence that the choroid represents a central and functionally relevant component in both the development and control of myopia. Because most of the included evidence is observational, the reported relationships between choroidal changes and myopia progression or treatment response should be interpreted as associations rather than proven causal mechanisms.

Beyond its well-described structural changes, the choroid seems to emerge from the present analysis as a dynamic, treatment-responsive tissue that may mediate the effects of diverse myopia control strategies through shared biological pathways. Across pharmacological, optical, and light-based interventions, effective treatments appear to be associated with early choroidal thickening or preservation, which frequently precedes and predicts reduced axial elongation. These findings may support a unifying model in which the choroid acts not only as a biomarker, but also as an active regulator of ocular growth.

One of the most compelling findings across studies is the temporal relationship between choroidal changes and axial elongation. In several interventions, particularly orthokeratology, defocus-incorporated lenses, and RLRL therapy, choroidal thickening occurs early, often within weeks or months, and precedes measurable differences in axial length. This temporal sequence could suggest that the choroidal response is not merely a secondary consequence of slowed eye growth but may play an active role in signaling or mediating growth regulation. The observation that early choroidal changes predict long-term biometric outcomes further strengthens this interpretation and positions CT as a potential functional biomarker of treatment responsiveness.

These observations are consistent with the concept of choroidal plasticity and resilience, the capacity of the choroid to undergo dynamic, reversible, and directionally appropriate changes in response to visual and therapeutic stimuli, operating within a framework of chorioretinal coupling, in which retinal signaling modulates choroidal perfusion and thickness and, in turn, scleral growth.

Beyond CT alone, recent studies emphasize the importance of choroidal structure, particularly its vascular component. Evidence from OCT-based structural analyses and choroidal vascularity indices consistently indicates that many antimyopic interventions, especially RLRL and orthokeratology, are primarily associated with expansion of the luminal area rather than stromal tissue. This vascular-dominant response supports mechanistic hypotheses involving choroidal perfusion, oxygenation, and metabolic signaling to the sclera and retina. Such mechanisms align well with experimental models in which choroidal blood flow and thickness are tightly coupled to visually guided eye growth.

At the same time, important differences between intervention categories should be acknowledged. Optical strategies based on myopic defocus tend to be associated with relatively modest but directionally consistent choroidal changes, which may fluctuate depending on exposure time, lens design, and visual behavior. Pharmacological approaches such as low-dose atropine appear to exert weaker and less consistent effects on the choroid, suggesting that their primary mechanisms may involve retinal or neural pathways rather than direct choroidal modulation. In contrast, RLRL therapy is consistently associated with large, rapid, and sustained choroidal thickening, with strong correlations to axial elongation control, supporting the idea that the choroid is a primary biological target of light-based therapies.

An additional perspective is that choroidal parameters may not only function as biomarkers of disease progression or treatment effect, but as indicators of an individual eye’s intrinsic responsiveness to myopia control. Across multiple studies, the magnitude of early choroidal change varies substantially between individuals exposed to the same intervention, and this variability consistently aligns with differences in subsequent axial elongation. This pattern suggests that choroidal responsiveness may reflect an underlying biological susceptibility of the eye, capturing how effectively visual or therapeutic signals are translated into growth modulation. Within this context, the choroid could be conceptualized as a biomarker of treatment responsiveness, rather than solely a downstream correlate of structural change. Such a role would extend its clinical relevance beyond monitoring. Early choroidal changes could be a potential tool for stratifying patients, identifying likely responders and non-responders, and informing individualized treatment selection in myopia management.

The strengths of this review include the systematic search strategy, the inclusion of multiple therapeutic approaches to myopia control, and the comprehensive synthesis of structural and vascular choroidal parameters across a wide range of clinical studies.

Despite these strengths, several limitations of the current literature and of this systematic review must be considered. First, substantial heterogeneity exists in study design, population age, refractive status, follow-up duration, and imaging protocols, which limits direct quantitative comparisons across studies. Moreover, the search strategy was intentionally focused exclusively on myopia control interventions; however, observational studies without intervention were also considered eligible and included when identified through manual screening of reference lists, partially addressing this limitation. Second, most available evidence is derived from pediatric populations, while adult data remain sparse and often limited to short-term experimental exposures. Third, CT measurements are sensitive to diurnal variation, imaging technique, and segmentation methodology, and not all studies adequately control for these factors. Fourth, although strong associations between choroidal changes and axial growth are repeatedly observed, definitive causal relationships cannot yet be established. Fifth, only English-language articles were included in this review, which may have introduced language bias and led to the exclusion of relevant studies published in other languages. Sixth, the review protocol was not prospectively registered, which may limit transparency and increase the risk of selective reporting. Seventh, the search was restricted to PubMed and Scopus; although these databases jointly offer extensive coverage, the exclusion of other sources such as Embase, Web of Science, and the Cochrane Library may have led to the omission of some eligible studies and cannot fully exclude the possibility of publication bias. Finally, it was not possible to perform a meta-analysis in this review. This decision was based on substantial clinical and methodological heterogeneity across studies. Sources of heterogeneity included: (i) participant characteristics (wide age range, baseline refractive error, and predominantly East Asian ethnicity); (ii) imaging devices and protocols, with choroidal parameters measured using different OCT platforms (spectral-domain vs swept-source) and enhancement modes, and differing segmentation and axial-length correction methods; (iii) outcome definitions, as CT was reported as subfoveal, macular, or regional values, and CVI was computed with non-standardized binarization algorithms; (iv) intervention type, dose, and duration; and (v) follow-up duration and timing of measurements, which is critical given the diurnal fluctuation of the choroid. This heterogeneity precluded meaningful statistical pooling and supported a structured narrative synthesis.

Future research should aim to address these gaps by adopting standardized imaging protocols, longitudinal designs with frequent early follow-up, and combined structural–functional analyses of the choroid. Integrating choroidal metrics with molecular, vascular, and biomechanical biomarkers may further clarify how choroidal modulation translates into long-term control of scleral remodeling and ocular growth. In addition, predictive modeling approaches, such as those using machine learning, could offer promising tools for identifying individual response patterns and moving toward personalized myopia management strategies based on early choroidal behavior.

Finally, little is still known about some new experimental approaches that may slow the progression of myopia through thickening of the choroid, such as the “red in focus” filter81 and reading text with inverted contrast,81,82 and the use of soft contact lenses to control myopia progression.83,84 Therefore, future studies should further clarify the effects of these therapeutic options on the choroid and, consequently, on myopia progression.

Conclusions

In conclusion, the scientific evidence seems to support a central role of the choroid in both the development and control of myopia. Across multiple therapeutic modalities, effective myopia control is consistently associated with choroidal thickening or preservation, often accompanied by vascular expansion . These changes are closely linked to reduced axial elongation and, in several contexts, predict long-term treatment efficacy.

We did not apply the GRADE framework to rate the overall certainty of evidence. GRADE is primarily designed to evaluate the certainty of effect estimates for predefined outcomes within quantitative synthesis and well-defined intervention–comparator questions. Given the descriptive nature of this review, the heterogeneity of imaging-based outcomes, and the absence of a meta-analysis, a formal GRADE assessment was considered not appropriate. Instead, the risk of bias of individual studies and the consistency of findings were used to inform the strength of our conclusions.

Furthermore, we specifically examined the RLRL studies for cohort overlap. Although several originated from active research groups in the same field, they reported distinct populations and enrollment periods; no duplicate cohorts were identified, and each study was treated as an independent data source.

Rather than acting as a passive bystander, the choroid seems to emerge as a dynamic, responsive, and potentially mediating tissue within the complex signaling cascade that regulates ocular growth. While further effort is needed to establish causality and standardize assessment methods, choroidal parameters (particularly early changes in CT and vascularity) hold strong promise as functional and prognostic biomarkers. Incorporating choroidal evaluation into future clinical trials and clinical practice may enhance both mechanistic understanding and individualized approaches to myopia management.
