Parents of youth and young adults with intellectual and developmental disabilities: Barriers experienced during transition
Authors: Christine Bottrell Mirzaian, Rowan Smith, Cecily L Betz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThe purpose of this study was to explore the experiences of parents of youth and young adults with intellectual and developmental disabilities who have undergone the transition from pediatric to adult healthcare as well as community-based transition and adult-focused services to better understand the barriers faced when individuals transition.MethodsEleven parents were recruited via email flyers and research information sheets, which were distributed to parent support/disability advocacy groups that are community partners of the Children’s Hospital Los Angeles University Center for Excellence in Developmental Disabilities. The study interview guide consisted of 11 open-ended questions. The questions were designed to explore experiences with their youth and young adults’ health care transition experience. Question items pertaining to barriers (five questions) with community-based services identified during the transition process are reported in this paper. All interviews were conducted via phone, audio-recorded, and transcribed.ResultsAnalysis of the data generated four major themes with subthemes. The day programs theme consisted of four subthemes: a) do not know what programs are available; b) lack of staffing; c) day program not appropriate; and d) waitlist. The education theme included four subthemes: a) no transition support provided; b) transition support not adequate; c) vocational training inadequate or not available; and d) need for adult supervision. The employment barrier theme contained three subthemes: a) loss of benefits; b) transition support not provided; and c) lack of accommodations or lack of options. The independent living barrier was composed of six subthemes: a) lack of resources; b) public benefits take a long time; c) self-determination services still inadequate; d) no transportation available; e) need to know to ask for services; and f) challenges pertaining to conservatorship.ConclusionThe findings revealed parents encountered numerous barriers to assisting their youth and young adults in assessing educational, employment, and community living services. The analysis supports the findings from previous studies and provides new insights and understanding.Keywords: intellectual and developmental disabilities, health care transition, employment, education, community living

Introduction

Health care transition (HCT) is now recognized as a necessary service component to facilitate the transfer of care from pediatric to adult-focused health care providers, as well as the transition to adulthood for youth and young adults with chronic conditions, including referrals to transition and adult-focused community-based services and supports.1,2 Nevertheless, evidence-based models of care to support HCT are lacking in this emerging field of practice, and what constitutes successful youth and young adult outcomes is not well defined.3–6 It is against this background of HCT practice and research that considerable and ongoing interdisciplinary and international efforts are underway to generate the evidence needed to implement models of care that will affect improved HCT outcomes for youth and young adults with chronic conditions, including those with intellectual and/or developmental disabilities (IDD).7–9

The concept of HCT was first introduced just over 30 years ago, when it was widely understood to be focused on the transfer of health care from pediatric to adult-focused care, as reflected in the following quotation: “…the purposeful, planned movement of adolescents and young adults with chronic physical and medical conditions from child-centered to adult-oriented health-care systems.”10, p.570 However, what has been missing in this early introduction of the HCT service model is the underlying assumption aligned with this statement: “Transition is a multifaceted, active process that attends to the medical, psychosocial, and educational/vocational needs of adolescents as they move from the child-focused to the adult-focused health-care system. Health care transition facilitates transition in other areas of life as well (e.g., work, community, and school).” 10 p.574 A comprehensive HCT model has subsequently been advocated for and supported.11–14 Importantly, the inclusive HCT model is in alignment with the World Health Organization definition of health as “….a state of complete physical, mental and social well-being and not merely the absence of disease or infirmity.” 15 This concept of health is especially relevant for the population of youth, young adults, and older adults with IDD.

A noticeable area of need is the development of HCT programs for the IDD population, as service disparities have been reported when compared to other groups of individuals with chronic conditions. 16 Additionally, youth and young adults with IDD can present with challenging developmental needs, unlike other populations of those with chronic conditions, as they have cognitive, sensory, and developmental challenges requiring ongoing and lifelong specialized services and support. 17 Regrettably, youth and young adults with IDD are faced with insufficient services and resources that can accommodate their significant needs, such as assistance with activities of daily living, augmentative communication devices, and access to American Sign Language interpretation, large print and illustrative educational materials, and transportation assistance. 18 As well, youth and young adults with IDD face other challenges pertaining to lifestyle outcomes, including postsecondary education and training19–21 and employment outcomes,22,23 as compared to other populations of individuals with chronic conditions. Postsecondary education,19–21 employment22,23 and community living24,25 outcomes are not typically identified in HCT literature. 26

In addition, HCT is a process that is not optimal for individuals with IDD. For example, in youth with autism spectrum disorder, fewer than 10% meet the national transition core outcome, and there is a greater disparity in HCT planning for non-Hispanic Black youth, youth with family income <400% of the federal poverty level, and youth with more severe activity limitation. 27

Learning from parents about their children’s perceived needs for HCT services provides insights into parental management and programmatic access and availability during this important period in their children’s lives. Given parents’ perceptions of their youth and young adults’ competency to achieve the developmental milestones of adulthood, their approach to transition to adulthood will vary from societal norms.16,28 Improved parental well-being has been associated with improved outcomes for their children 29 ; conversely, higher levels of parental stress are associated with adverse outcomes. 30

Parents/guardians are, and continue to be, an invaluable source of expertise that will assist with HCT service model development. Based on this research-affirming perspective, the study team sought to interview parents of individuals with IDD who had undergone the transition experience about the barriers they encountered, with a focus on employment, education, community living, and transportation services. The purpose of this study was to explore the experiences of parents of youth and young adults with IDD who have transitioned from child-focused services to adult-focused, community-based services and supports that comprise the HCT service model, to better understand the barriers faced during transition.

Methods

The study was approved by the institution’s IRB. Parents were recruited via email flyers and research information sheets, which were distributed to parent support/disability advocacy groups that are community partners of the Children’s Hospital Los Angeles University Center for Excellence in Developmental Disabilities (CHLA UCEDD). Interested parents contacted the research team; all details of the study, using language from the research information sheet, were provided, and any questions they had were answered. If parents indicated their willingness to participate, an interview was scheduled. Individuals were eligible for this study if they met the following inclusion criteria: a) ≥18 years old; b) parents/legal guardians of an individual with an IDD who had transitioned from pediatric to adult care; c) English-speaking, non-English-speaking, or with limited English proficiency, as an interpreter of the participant’s preferred language would be provided. Parents who were unable to consent were excluded.

Sixty participants expressed interest via email and were contacted by the study team; of those, 18 were determined to be eligible and scheduled for an interview, and 10 (55% of those originally scheduled) participated on the day of the interview. One additional participant from the CHLA UCEDD Community Advisory Committee reached out directly to request participation. Further details of the recruitment process are provided in a related publication. 31 A total of 11 parents were recruited into this study. Interviews were conducted from July to October 2023.

Study interview guide

The interview guide consisted of 11 open-ended questions. The interview guide was developed by reviewing the HCT literature1–6,32 and drawing on the clinical experience and expertise of the research team. Parents were asked questions about their experiences with their youth’s and young adults’ HCT, including referrals to transition and adult-focused community-based services and supports. These questions explored parents’ access to resources during HCT, advice parents would provide to other parents during the HCT period, service barriers to employment and educational resources encountered during the HCT process, plans for independent living, reflections about changes they would have made during HCT, and the feasibility of participating in a peer navigation program that was in development by the study team. A copy of the interview guide is presented in Box 1. All interviews were conducted via phone. Interviews were audio-recorded and transcribed. Question items pertaining to barriers (five questions) identified during the HCT process, which focused on referrals to transition and adult-focused community-based services and supports, are reported in this paper. Parental interviews lasted approximately 30 minutes. Participating parents received $50 gifts cards following the interview.

Data analysis

Data were analyzed using the Framework Method for Qualitative Analysis. 33 Three members of the research team (C.M., C.B., and R.S.) coded the first three parental interviews independently and then met to review areas of agreement and differences. The conflicts with codes for the initial interviews were resolved upon further discussion. Two research team members (C.M. and C.B) then separately coded the remaining parental interviews using this coding schema. The subsequent analyses were reviewed by the team members (C.M., C.B., and R.S.).

Barriers and parental quotations.

Day program barriers

Parents whose youth and young adults’ future planning was directed to enrollment in day programs identified four barriers to accessing or participating in these programs. These barriers, as described below, were a) do not know what programs are available; b) lack of staffing; c) day program not appropriate; and d) waitlist. The scope of their comments can be found in Table 2.

Do Not Know What Programs Are Available. Parents referred to the time-consuming effort of locating an appropriate program for their child. For example, two parents cited the intense extra efforts to ensure that their sons would be enrolled in a day program (Parents 1 and 4). Parent 4 remarked, “You have to go there and get it yourself, and it’s up to you if you can get a spot.”

Lack of Staffing. One parent (Parent 3) commented about the problems associated with widespread lack of appropriate staffing at day programs (Table 2).

Day Program Not Appropriate. Parents offered differing perspectives about the programs that were not appropriate placements. One parent noted that their son was exposed to challenging behaviors of consumers enrolled in the day program, making social interactions difficult (Parent 8). Two parents (Parents 2 and 9) shared concerns about the age differences among day program participants, some of whom were considerably older (Table 2).

Waitlist. Several parents expressed frustration with day programs’ waitlists, delaying their children’s access to day programs as the programs were at capacity. The comments of parents (Parents 1, 6, and 8) can be found in Table 2.

Education barriers

Parents identified barriers to accessing educational resources following their youth and young adults’ exit from high school. Their analyzed responses were categorized into four subthemes: a) no transition support provided; b) transition support not adequate; c) vocational training inadequate or not available; and d) need for adult supervision.

No Transition Support Provided. A parent (Parent 5) described the problems her son faced in high school, which prevented him from progressing forward with postsecondary options. She expressed her frustration with the lack of support she felt her son received, resulting in repeated grades and his dropping out of school (Table 2).

Education Transition Support Not Adequate. Another parent (Parent 10) felt the transition support for their children’s educational needs was inadequate. This parent noted that limited translation services were available for her son, whose preferred language was Spanish (Table 2).

Vocational Training Inadequate or Not Available. Several parents offered perspectives about their children’s access to vocational training services. One parent (Parent 5) expressed feelings of ambivalence about available services, whereas another parent (Parent 10) lacked confidence that her son would be successful in a program given his level of involvement. This parent shared, “So if you have a child with more profound needs, like my son, who’s minimally verbal, who has like other sensory issues, like gross motor, fine motor that prevent them from acquiring, you know, skills, that have memory problems, you know, so he has short-term, so learning is very difficult process, to them, nothing is offered. But to a child who they can showcase as a success, yes, things are offered to them.” Parent 1 expressed acknowledgment of the limitations of services available to her son. Their responses can be found in Table 2.

Need for Adult Supervision. A parent (Parent 2) shared the challenge that she encountered in finding adult-focused services after her daughter aged out of programs that would cover the time the parent was at work and unavailable (Table 2).

Loss of Benefits. Discussion

As the findings indicate, parents reported myriads of problems with the coordination and referral to transition and adult-focused services needed to support the acquisition of the benchmarks of adulthood. These issues were evident in the four thematic areas of barriers identified pertaining to day programs, transition/postsecondary education, employment, and independent living. Parents expressed a range of intense and draining emotions concerning the barriers they faced in attempting to obtain the services and supports that their youth and young adults needed. Several other studies have reported similar findings as to the complex feelings that parents experienced.16,33–37 The themes and subthemes presented in this paper reflect previous findings as well as provide new insights.

Service capacity was a barrier cited by parents, and they identified a number of contributing factors such as long waitlists and lack of appropriate staffing to address the needs of applicants.16,18,33,36 As parents discussed, the waitlists prolonged and even prevented access to programs that would be of benefit to their children’s lifestyle needs, an issue that has not been fully vetted in previous studies.

Another important issue that parents raised about service capacity is the staffing limitations associated with available programs. According to parental reports, the breadth of available services for individuals with complex IDD was limited, as has been reported in other studies.33–35 As evident in several study themes, personnel issues were identified by parents. Parents commented on the lack of programmatic staffing and skill deficits of staff, especially for those young adults with severe involvement, an issue cited in previous studies.33,35,36 Typically, most agencies are geared to individuals with mild/moderate disabilities. 33 It has been suggested the challenges associated with high levels of staff turnovers diminish the quality, type and scope of services available.18,36 A few parents suggested staff were unconcerned about being responsive to the needs of their children, a finding not widely reported. Other studies have noted attitudinal issues with staffing, referring to lower expectations of staff with provision of services to individuals with IDD as compared to other service recipients.28,35

Parents in this study identified problems with obtaining accommodations that would be expected in secondary settings. These parents reflected retrospectively on accommodations that could have facilitated improved circumstances/outcomes for their children in terms of future planning for post-secondary education/training and employment.30,35,37,38

Parents indicated their lack of acumen in knowing and understanding the type of programs available and, very importantly, how to effectively access and enroll in them. It was evident that parents and their youth and young adults were ill-prepared for transitioning to adulthood. In some instances, parents indicated they engaged in persistent efforts to locate needed information that was not previously available or shared with them, as has been reported previously.30,33

Navigating new systems of care was a formidable challenge, as illustrated in parental quotes. These families had long-standing experience in child-focused systems of care, which was not necessarily helpful in accessing new and unfamiliar systems of care.20,33 It was also evident that navigation support typically associated with pediatric services was not available with adult-focused services. Parents shared that it was a widespread experience for other parents they knew. Other studies have noted the drop-off associated with accessing adult-focused services as well.16,27,33

Implications for practice and research

As these findings indicate, there is a reduction in resources available for parents to support and assist their youth and young adults as they transfer their health care to adult-focused providers and community services, as reported in other studies.20,27,33,35,36 Addressing the needs of parents/guardians during this critical period of transition needs to be an essential component of learning that will assist with the facilitation of transition to adulthood for their youth and young adults. These needs could be integrated into transition individualized education programs (IEPs)/504 Plans or instituted as individualized instructional programs offered by the IDD service system. The Individuals with Disabilities Education Act (IDEA) mandates that the IEP includes measurable postsecondary goals based upon the individualized needs of the student. Agency representatives, such as rehabilitation specialists, are invited to attend the IEP meetings to coordinate services and goal planning. 39 The Rehabilitation Act of 1973 and the Workforce Innovation and Opportunity Act provide for vocational and pre-employment opportunities for young people that can be integrated into the student’s IEP.40,41 Section 504 of the Rehabilitation Act prohibits disability discrimination in educational programs that receive federal funding.

An interagency team focused on facilitating access to adult-focused services has been suggested as an intervention innovation for the IDD population.18,34 Encouraging parents to partner with other parents who are going through or have been through the transition experience could serve as a valuable resource option. In this study and others, parents expressed the need for emotional support.33,34,38 Through these support programs, which have been reported in other studies, parents could learn, acquire, and strengthen the skills necessary to assist with navigating this complex array of services/supports, including advocacy skills.33,38 It is evident that a need exists to upgrade the training of providers to address the service needs individuals with IDD who have a severe level of involvement, including those who provide transportation services, as has been reported in other studies as well.28,34

The findings of this study provide needed insights about the challenges facing the IDD community as they transfer their care to adult-focused providers and transition to adulthood. Future studies are needed to expand the understanding of comprehensive HCT services that include referrals to transition and adult-focused community-based services and supports to create evidence-based interventions to facilitate improvements in lifestyle outcomes.

Limitations

There are several study limitations to be acknowledged. The sample was recruited from Southern California and may not be representative of parents from other parts of the country. All parents who participated were mothers, and just two were parents of daughters, reflecting a gender bias. Limited demographic data were collected as interviews were conducted anonymously. Given the qualitative research design, the findings are not generalizable. 42

Conclusion

Eleven parents whose children completed the HCT process provided their insights as to the barriers that they encountered. As the findings reveal, parents encountered numerous barriers in assisting their youth and young adults with accessing educational, employment, and community living services. The analysis of findings supports previous studies as well as provides new insights and understanding. As the findings suggest, efforts are needed to create HCT service models that are tailored to the needs of the IDD community, as the preponderance of research has focused on other populations of youth and young adults. As the findings of this study and others suggest, particular attention needs to be directed to navigation services for parents. Furthermore, more rigorous, sufficiently powered research is needed to advance both the HCT science and practice for the IDD community.
