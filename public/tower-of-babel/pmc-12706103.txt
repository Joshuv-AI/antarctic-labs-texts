Impact of COVID-19 on the detection of tuberculosis in Guangdong, China based on the autoregressive integrated moving average model: a time-series study
Authors: Ruilong Wang, Fangjing Zhou, Guoqi Shi, Yuan Liu, Ying Bian, Huizhong Wu, Guanyang Zou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveChina has continued to improve tuberculosis (TB) control in the past decade; however, the sudden outbreak of COVID-19 hindered this progress. As a province with a large population and frequent international exchanges, Guangdong has been seriously affected by COVID-19. This study aimed to understand the effect of COVID-19 on TB detection in Guangdong based on the autoregressive integrated moving average (ARIMA) model.DesignTime-series study.SettingGuangdong, China.Outcome measuresWe used the ARIMA model to quantify the effect of COVID-19 by comparing reported cases during the COVID-19 pandemic with predicted cases under a counterfactual scenario of no COVID-19 pandemic. After model evaluation, we chose ARIMA (0,1,2)(0,1,1)12 as the prediction model. We also highlighted that there were three emergency response periods in which the responses and public responses to COVID-19 varied.ResultsDuring the pandemic period, the average annual TB notification rate was 57.95/100 000, which decreased by 27.97% compared with the pre-pandemic period. Although it decreased by 6.17% on average annually in the pre-pandemic period, it decreased by 14.92% in 2020 as compared with 2019, but only decreased by 0.34% in 2021 as compared with 2020. The results of the ARIMA model showed that the number of reported cases in 2020 decreased by 6.62% compared with that of the predicted cases, but this decreased by 0.42% only in 2021. The most seriously affected period was the second-level emergency response period in 2020, when the relative difference between reported and predicted cases reached the peak (−16.43%). The least affected period was the third-level emergency response period of 2021, the reported cases recovered and exceeded the predicted cases, with a gap of 0.77%.ConclusionsTB detection in Guangdong had generally declined during the COVID-19 pandemic, which might be related to the movement restrictions, diverted resources and patients’ concerns. This decline would lead to the delay or even interruption of diagnosis and treatment, which would cause the regression of TB control. To improve TB detection, it is important for stakeholders to take consorted effort during public health emergencies.

Introduction

Tuberculosis (TB) is one of the world’s leading infectious diseases,1 causing 1.3 million deaths in 2022. According to the global report, about 87% of cases came from 30 countries with a heavy TB burden. China accounted for 7.1% of the global TB burden, ranking third in the world.2 The United Nations Sustainable Development Goals proposed to end TB worldwide by 2030.3 Healthy China Initiative (2019–2030) also pointed out that by 2022, the prevalence of TB in China should drop to less than 55/100 000, and by 2030, it should still show a continuous downward trend.4 According to the global TB report and data released by the National Health Commission, the prevalence and notification rate of TB in China were 58/100 000 and 55.55/100 000 in 2019,5 whereas those were 55/100 000 and 45.37/100 000 in 2021.6 Although TB prevalence has continued to decline in the past decade, the sudden outbreak of the COVID-19 epidemic has significantly hindered this progress.7 The above data could show that the gap between TB prevalence and notification had become wider during the COVID-19 pandemic, which indicated a decline in TB detection. To prevent the continuous spread of COVID-19, many countries have successively introduced a series of prevention and control policies. Although these measures had improved people’s health awareness and reduced the social spread of TB, they also caused an increase in household spread and a significant increase in TB prevalence.8 Following the COVID-19 outbreak, the number of reported cases and the timeliness of diagnosis of TB have decreased.9 The WHO estimated that the number of reported cases of TB worldwide had decreased by 20%–30%.10 Studies in Nigeria,11 Sierra Leone,12 India13 and the Philippines14 have also shown that TB detection during the COVID-19 epidemic had decreased significantly. Some studies have pointed out that the global control of TB had regressed for about 10 years because of the effect of COVID-19.15

To fight against COVID-19, China has deployed various resources and manpower and implemented a series of public health interventions since the beginning of 2020, including restricting public transportation, prohibiting public gatherings, closing public spaces such as schools and libraries and isolation. While controlling the spread of COVID-19, these prevention and control policies have also significantly affected China’s medical and health system, including TB prevention and control.7 A relevant study conducted in China also showed that owing to the patient’s concern arising from the similar symptoms of COVID-19 and TB and the movement restrictions caused by the lockdown policy, the reported cases of TB during the epidemic decreased significantly.16 The manpower, materials and funds originally used for TB prevention and control were reallocated for the prevention and control of COVID-19, which might lead to a delay in the diagnosis of TB as elsewhere.17 If the diagnosis and treatment service of TB is seriously interrupted, the prevalence and death rate of TB will increase.18

As a coastal province with a large population and prosperous trade, Guangdong has also experienced different COVID-19 emergency response periods since the outbreak of COVID-19. At present, no study had examined the effect of COVID-19 on TB detection in Guangdong. Thus, this study aimed to understand the effect of COVID-19 on TB detection in Guangdong by comparing the detection before and after the COVID-19 pandemic. To further quantify such effect, the autoregressive integrated moving average (ARIMA) model was used to fit historical TB data, and cases reported during the COVID-19 pandemic were compared with predicted cases under a counterfactual scenario of no COVID-19 pandemic.

Methods

Study site

Guangdong Province is located in the southeast of China, at latitude 20°22ʹ–25°52ʹ north and longitude 109°66ʹ–117°30ʹ east. It has a resident population of 127.06 million, and its gross domestic product reached 135 67.32 billion yuan in 2023, which both ranked the first in China. According to the census data, there are 29.62 million migrants in Guangdong, which accounted for 23.5% of the total population. The international exchanges in Guangdong are also very frequent. In 2023, its total import and export trade was 830.07 billion yuan. The dense population and frequent movement of people led to a relatively high number of TB cases and the challenges of TB detection in Guangdong.

Data source

The monthly reported TB cases in Guangdong between 2015 and 2021 were drawn from the Health Commission of Guangdong Province (https://wsjkw.gd.gov.cn/zwyw_yqxx/).19 The population data of Guangdong over the years were extracted from the Guangdong Statistical Yearbook (http://stats.gd.gov.cn/gdtjnj/).20

Statistical analysis

In this study, monthly TB reported cases and the notification rate were used to evaluate the situation of TB detection in Guangdong.

First, the notification rate (/100,000) was calculated according to the number of annual reported cases and the population size. The plot of the monthly reported cases of TB in 2015–2021 was drawn to analyse the seasonality of TB detection. The differences between the pre-pandemic (2015–2019) and pandemic (2020–2021) periods were analysed so that we could tell whether TB detection declined during the COVID-19 pandemic.

Then, the ARIMA model was used to predict TB detection from January 2020 to December 2021 in the absence of COVID-19. The ARIMA model is widely used to predict the trend of infectious diseases. Recently, many studies have begun to use this model to analyse the effect of COVID-19 on other infectious diseases, such as influenza, hand, foot and mouth disease.21–23 Based on the data in the pre-pandemic period, these studies have used the ARIMA model to predict the trend of infectious diseases in the absence of COVID-19 and compare the predicted situation with the actual situation to determine the effect of COVID-19. The general form of the ARIMA model is ARIMA (p,d,q) (P,D,Q)s, where p, d and q stand for the autoregressive order, non-seasonal differencing degree and moving average order, and P, D and Q stand for the seasonal autoregressive order, seasonal differencing degree and seasonal moving average order, respectively. The expression ‘s’ represents the period of seasonality. The construction of the ARIMA model used in this study consisted of three steps. First, data preparation: a line chart was used to analyse seasonality, and the augmented Dickey–Fuller (ADF) test was used to verify the stationarity of the series. If the time series were not stable, data differencing should be carried out until stationarity was satisfied. The different times of the series were the values of d and D in the model. In this study, ADF tests were performed on the original data, indicating that the original series were not stationary (p>0.05). We made one non-seasonal difference (d=1) and one seasonal difference (D=1) to stabilise the incidence series. Second, p and q were determined. Parameters (p and q) were fixed to set up reasonable models by referring to the autocorrelation function (ACF) and partial autocorrelation function (PACF) plots of the stationary series. When the ACF plot was truncated and the PACF plot was dragged, the q-value corresponded to the truncated value in the ACF plot. When the ACF plot was tailed and the PACF plot truncated, the p value corresponded to the truncated value in the PACF plot. When the ACF plot and PACF plot were trailing, p was 0 and q was 2. Third, to choose the best model, P and Q were substituted into the model from low to high order (P and Q are usually <2). The Ljung–Box test was used to check the residuals of the optimal model. The residuals should be white noise. Besides, the model with the lowest corrected Akaike’s information criterion (AICc) and Bayesian information criterion (BIC) was taken for the optimal model. In this study, the ARIMA model (0,1,2) (0,1,1)12 was applied to forecast monthly reported TB cases in Guangdong province from January 2020 to December 2021.

Then, to quantify the effect of COVID-19 on TB detection, the number of reported cases was compared with the predicted cases between 2020 and 2021. The cumulative absolute difference and cumulative relative difference were calculated as follows:

Cumulative absolute difference = cumulative reported cases – cumulative predicted cases

Cumulative relative difference = cumulative absolute difference / cumulative predicted cases

Finally, the three different levels of emergency response periods were divided according to the policies. The policies in the first-level emergency response period (February 2020) included improving the prevention and control mechanism, strengthening the joint prevention and control system, stopping public gatherings, isolating close contacts and wearing masks in public places. The policies in the second-level emergency response period (March–April 2020 and June 2021) included gradually recovering the bus lines, inter-provincial long-distance transport and tourist business, strictly implementing measures such as frequent ventilation, temperature measurement, less gathering and ‘one-metre line’ (to keep social distance). The policies in the third-level emergency response period (May–December 2020 and January–May and July–December 2021) included implementing normalised prevention and control measures in all counties and cities, gradually restoring work and life to normal, and fully opening shopping malls, supermarkets, hotels and restaurants.

All statistical analyses and graphic drawings were carried out in the R Programming Language (version 4.1.3, R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Patient and public involvement

Patients and/or the public were not involved in the design, conduct or dissemination of this study.

Results

Epidemical trend of tuberculosis detection in Guangdong before and during the COVID-19 pandemic

During the period between 2015 and 2021, the Health Commission of Guangdong Province reported a total of 6 32 956 cases of TB, with the average annual notification rate of 74.02/100 000. The annual notification rate showed a decreasing trend in general except for 2017 between 2015 and 2021.

There were 4 86 307 cases of TB reported in the pre-pandemic period between 2015 and 2019. The average annual notification rate was 80.45/100 000. During the COVID-19 pandemic between 2020 and 2021, 1 46 649 cases of TB were reported, with the average annual notification rate of 57.95/100 000. Specifically, the notification rates were 58.04/100 000 and 57.85/100 000 in 2020 and 2021, respectively. The notification rate during the pandemic period decreased by 27.97% compared with the pre-pandemic period. Although it decreased by 6.17% on average annually in the pre-pandemic period, it decreased by 14.92% in 2020 as compared with that in 2019, but only decreased by 0.34% in 2021 as compared with that in 2020. Compared with the average notification in the pre-pandemic period, the notification rate decreased by 27.85% and 28.10% in 2020 and 2021, respectively. See table 1 for details.

Prediction of tuberculosis detection in the absence of COVID-19 and comparison between reality and prediction

Development of the autoregressive integrated moving average model

The ARIMA model was used to predict the theoretical number of reported cases from January 2020 to February 2022 in the absence of COVID-19 outbreak. Before applying the ARIMA model, the seasonality and stationarity of the reported cases of TB in Guangdong were analysed to ensure that the ARIMA model is applicable. As shown in figure 1, the reported cases of TB in Guangdong exhibited a seasonal pattern. The reported cases of TB changed regularly on a yearly basis (12 months), so s=12. April was the peak of TB notification, whereas February was at the bottom level across the years. The ADF test result remained significant (p<0.05), demonstrating that the time series were stable, which suggested the applicability of the ARIMA model. ACF and PACF function diagrams after the first-order difference are shown in figure 2. Through the ACF and PACF plots, the preliminary determination model was ARIMA (0,1,2)(P,1,Q)12. ARIMA (0,1,2)(0,1,1)12 was selected as the optimal model because it showed minimum AICc and BIC values.

Comparison between reality and prediction during the COVID-19 pandemic

In the scenario of no COVID-19, there were 78 470 predicted cases of TB in 2020. However, owing to the COVID-19 pandemic, only 73 275 cases were reported, 6.62% less than that of the predicted scenario. The cumulative absolute difference between the reported and predicted cases reached the maximum in August (−5427), whereas the cumulative relative difference between the reported and predicted cases was maximised in May (−13.23%). Discussion

Decline in tuberculosis detection during the COVID-19 pandemic compared with the pre-pandemic period in Guangdong

At the beginning of 2020, WHO declared COVID-19 as a global pandemic and a public health emergency of international concern. Countries around the world responded and took corresponding prevention and control measures. To diagnose and treat patients with infection in a timely manner and prevent COVID-19 spread, Guangdong also adopted a series of public health measures and deployed most of the medical resources. Due to various reasons, including the restrictions caused by policies, concern owing to the similar symptoms and insufficient health resources, the TB reporting system was significantly affected during the epidemic. Similar to the study in Shantou, which is a coastal city in Guangdong,24 the average number of reported TB cases in the whole province of Guangdong decreased by 27.97% during the epidemic as compared with that before the COVID-19 outbreak. In 2020, when the COVID-19 epidemic became worse, the reported cases of TB decreased by 14.92%, which was greater than before. The result of the Mann–Whitney test also showed that the notification rate of TB in the pandemic period significantly declined as compared with that in the pre-pandemic period. This result was consistent with the findings of studies conducted in Nigeria,11 Sierra Leone,12 India,13 the Philippines14 and South Korea.25 However, the decline in TB detection in Guangdong was smaller than that in Nigeria, India and the Philippines, which might be due to the relatively stronger economic conditions in Guangdong. Compared with other provinces in China, the decline in TB detection in Guangdong was similar to that in Tianjin26 and Ningxia Hui Autonomous Region,27 but smaller than that in Jiangsu28 and Shanghai.29 This might be because the studies conducted in Jiangsu and Shanghai only included the data before May 2020 when the prevention and control policies were still tough.

Decline in tuberculosis detection compared with prediction during the COVID-19 pandemic in Guangdong

Consistent with other studies in China,30 31 the present study finds that TB detection declined during the COVID-19 pandemic. The results of this study show that the number of reported cases of TB in 2020 decreased by 6.62% compared with that of predicted cases, indicating that TB detection in Guangdong has regressed due to the COVID-19 pandemic. However, inconsistent with the study based on the whole country,31 the decline in TB detection in Guangdong was smaller, which might be because the economic and health conditions of Guangdong were better than that of most parts of China. Furthermore, this study analysed the status of TB detection in 2021. When the effect of COVID-19 was relatively small, the reported cases of TB decreased by only 0.42% compared with that of the predicted scenario. This not only reflected that TB detection in Guangdong had gradually recovered after the prevention and control policies had normalised but also further proved that the sharp decline in TB detection in 2020 was mainly caused by COVID-19 rather than by continuous TB prevention and control policies.

Owing to a series of public health measures taken in Guangdong at the beginning, the local epidemic situation had been gradually controlled over time, which led the prevention and control policies to change. According to the degree of restriction caused by policies, 2020 was divided into the first-level to third-level emergency response periods, and the restriction would be weakened as the level escalated. The above results could show that the effect of COVID-19 reached its peak in the second period. As shown in figure 1, TB detection dropped in January and February each year when people would rather accompany their families and celebrate the Spring Festival than go to the health facilities.32 Therefore, although the restriction in the first-level emergency response period in 2020 was stricter, owing to its short duration and coinciding with the Spring Festival, the gap between the predicted and reported cases would not be maximised. During the second-level emergency response period, strict prevention and control policies were still maintained. Additionally, the continuous spread of COVID-19 sharpened the worries further. Both restriction and patients’ concerns would lead to the expansion of the effect of COVID-19 on TB detection.33 In the third period, the whole area of Guangdong was at low risk of contracting COVID-19 (the Chinese government had divided the risk of contracting COVID-19 in an area into high, medium and low levels). The restriction had been gradually removed, and people’s daily travel and medical activities had returned to normal. During this period, only one new confirmed domestic case of COVID-19 in Guangdong was reported, people’s worries about COVID-19 were alleviated, and the medical materials and manpower assigned to the prevention and control of COVID-19 also returned to TB prevention and control. Therefore, TB detection had gradually recovered, and the effect of COVID-19 had gradually declined in this period. The effect of COVID-19 on TB detection in 2021 was roughly the same as that in 2020.

At the beginning of 2021, there were more reported cases than predicted cases of TB. As mentioned above, the number of predicted cases would decline because of the Spring Festival. However, no new domestic COVID-19 cases were confirmed in Guangdong during this period, whereas the whole province continued the prevention and control policies of the third-level emergency response period. The negative effect of COVID-19 was further recovered, and patients would rather see a doctor at this time if COVID-19 were to spread again later. Therefore, the number of reported cases might become higher than the predicted scenario in this period. However, between March and June 2021, the number of reported cases fell below the predicted cases, and the difference between them became larger again. In fact, Guangdong was still at low risk of contracting COVID-19 and continued the prevention and control policies of the third-level emergency response period from March to May. The decline in TB detection during this period might be caused by the universal vaccination with the SARS-CoV-2 vaccine. Performing nucleic acid amplification tests was not recommended in a short time after vaccination, although multiple vaccinations were recommended. Previous studies have found that people might get false-positive results in a short time after vaccination because of the detection of inactive or residual SARS-CoV-2.34 However, the policy stipulated that people who entered medical institutions must hold a 48-hour negative result; as a result, patients were unable to see a doctor in time. COVID-19 spread again in June, and the prevention and control policies were changed to those of the second-level emergency response period. This may explain why TB detection declined during this period in 2021. At the beginning of July, the COVID-19 epidemic in Guangdong was controlled, and the prevention and control policies returned to the third level, which might lead to the recovery of TB detection.

Policy implications

In summary, TB detection declined during the COVID-19 pandemic, mainly due to the shortage of resources, movement restriction and patients’ concern caused by COVID-19 prevention and control. However, evidence has shown that strict prevention and control policies for COVID-19 are effective and necessary in dealing with emerging infectious diseases. Therefore, to respond to potential emerging infectious diseases in the future and ensure the process of TB control, we put forward several suggestions. First, the government should establish an emergency response mechanism to improve TB detection during the outbreak of emerging infectious diseases, including ensuring the support of manpower and medical resources and taking more active TB detection measures, such as integrating TB screening into large-scale testing of emerging infectious diseases. Second, infectious disease clinicians should strengthen daily health education to let people gain knowledge of TB and understand the difference between TB and other infectious diseases, especially respiratory infectious diseases. Furthermore, public health researchers should enhance the research on TB control during public health emergencies to provide scientific evidence for policymakers to improve the resilience of infectious disease control.

Limitations

This study also has some limitations. First, since the data analysed in this study came from the government’s open data, other indicators of TB detection cannot be obtained. Additionally, since the TB surveillance system involves passive monitoring, missed diagnoses and delayed reporting were inevitable, which might lead to bias. Moreover, some confounding factors, such as climatic conditions and positive TB treatment and control policies, would affect the epidemic and TB detection. However, since these factors are challenging to collect and quantify, this study has not yet included them in the analysis. Furthermore, since the ARIMA model was used for prediction, nonlinear effects could not be analysed, which was a problem. We will continue to explore this issue in combination with non-linear models in the future. Finally, the generalisation of the findings might be limited because we only report TB detection during the COVID-19 outbreak in one of the most populous and developed provinces in China. However, this study provides useful implications for the TB control under public health emergencies.

Conclusions

TB detection in Guangdong had generally declined during the COVID-19 outbreak, which might be related to movement restrictions, diverted resources and patients’ concerns. This would lead to the delay or even interruption of diagnosis and treatment, which would cause the relapse of TB control. Thus, concerted and consolidated efforts are necessary to improve TB detection and control during public health emergencies.
