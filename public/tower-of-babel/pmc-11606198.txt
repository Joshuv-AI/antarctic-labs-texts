Factors correlated to oral frailty and number of remaining teeth among 80-year-old population in Japan
Authors: Naofumi Tamaki, Takatoshi Hiroshimaya, Kazuto Fukuhara, Hiroshi Ijichi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOral frailty is proposed as a new concept defined as an age-related gradual loss of oral functions accompanied by a decline in cognitive and physical functions in older adults. Additionally, the participants with ≥ 20 remaining teeth are associated with a lower risk of cognitive frailty in older adults. Herein, we aimed to examine the correlations between the high risk of oral frailty and oral conditions or health behavior in community-dwelling 80-year-old adults in Japan. Additionally, our secondary aim was to investigate the factors associated with ≥ 20 remaining teeth.MethodsThe present investigation was designed as a cross-sectional study using data from an adult dental health field study. This study included participants only aged 80 years in Japan. Oral frailty was evaluated using the Oral Frailty Index-8, and ≥ 4 points were defined as high risk. Oral examination was performed, and data on oral health behavior and frailty were also collected by using a self-administered questionnaire. The logistic regression analysis was conducted, with the objective variable being the risk of oral frailty groups or remaining teeth.ResultsOverall, 3,222 participants ( 1,911 female and 1,311 men) were included. Among the participants, 1,217 (37.8%) had a high risk of oral frailty. The risk of oral frailty was significantly affected by smoking status, number of remaining teeth, dental plaque, oral malodor, family dental clinic, oral concerns, osteoporosis, and shrinking body weight. Logistic regression analysis revealed that a high risk of oral frailty was significantly associated with < 20 remaining teeth and poor oral behaviors. Additionally, 1,926(59.8%) participants had ≥ 20 remaining teeth. The participants with remaining teeth ≥ 20 were significantly affected by sex, smoking status, residential district, periodontal pocket, bleeding on probing, dental plaque, and interdental cleaning. The participants with ≥ 20 remaining teeth were significantly associated with the residential district, and interdental cleaning.ConclusionsThe main clinical implication of this study is that improving behaviors regarding oral healthcare among community-dwelling individuals may be an effective way to reduce oral frailty. Background

Recently, frailty has become one of the most important issues and has attracted much attention regarding the health of older adults. Frailty involves a multifaceted decline in physical, psychological, and social functions, and oral health influences each component [1]. Oral frailty is a new concept introduced in Japan and defined by the Japan Dental Association as a series of phenomena and processes that lead to changes in various oral conditions (number of teeth, oral hygiene, and oral functions) associated with aging, accompanied by decreased interest in oral health and reduced physical and mental reserve capacity [2]. An increase in oral frailty leads to eating dysfunction, and the overall effect is the deterioration of physical and mental function. A longitudinal study implied that oral frailty is associated with a higher risk of frailty, sarcopenia, the need for long-term care, and mortality [3]. However, the deterioration of oral function becomes irreversible over time and is difficult to detect and assess. To address this problem, the Oral Frailty Index-8 (OFI-8), which integrates oral health-related behaviors and associated indicators of oral frailty, was proposed to help screen older adults at risk of oral frailty in a community setting [3]. Estimating the risk of oral frailty in individuals with limited access to dental clinics could help increase oral health literacy and decrease oral concerns in the community. Differences in oral frailty or conditions between geographical regions may have an impact; that is, living in rural or remote areas may influence oral health. Thus, we also focused on the relationship between residential districts and the oral health of participants in Kagoshima Prefecture, Japan. Additionally, the significance association was found between oral frailty and eating behavior [4]. However, the relationships among oral frailty and health behaviors are unclear.

Tooth loss commonly occurs with aging in older individuals and has been associated with impaired chewing ability and changes in food preferences [5]. Decreased oral function is an important risk factor for sarcopenia and malnutrition. A positive relationship is indicated between physical activity and the number of remaining teeth or chewing ability in the 80-year-old Japanese population [6]. Additionally, the number of remaining teeth serves as a good marker of general health, reflecting the net accumulation of experiences from poor hygiene habits over time [7]. In 2022, Zhang et al. indicated that participants with ≥ 20 remaining teeth are associated with a lower risk of cognitive frailty in American older adults [8]. Furthermore, the participants with < 20 remaining teeth are more likely to have been diagnosed with sarcopenia or severe sarcopenia than those with ≥ 20 among older outpatients of community hospitals [9]. Additionally, Japan Dental Association is promoting “8020” movement, which means 20 remaining teeth at 80-year-old [10]. It is based on the research which reported that almost all kinds of food would be eaten with 20 teeth. However, further study is needed to clarify about 8020 in Japanese, one of the fastest aging populations in the world. Therefore, we hypothesized that oral frailty would be affected by the number of remaining teeth, which was divided into those with ≥ 20 or < 20 in 80-year-old Japanese participants.

This cross-sectional study aimed to verify whether a high risk of oral frailty is associated with the number of remaining teeth and oral health behaviors. Our secondary aim was to investigate the factors associated with ≥ 20 remaining teeth among community-dwelling 80-year-old adults in Japan.

Methods

Study population

The present investigation was designed as a cross-sectional study using data from an adult dental health field study conducted in the Kagoshima Prefecture. The corresponding author signed a memorandum of understanding with the Kagoshima Prefecture regarding the use of the data. All dental clinics were approved by the Kagoshima Dental Association, with manually trained dental clinicians. The participants were recruited at 80-year-olds, currently residing in Kagoshima prefecture, Japan. A written invitation in which the aim of the present study was briefly described was mailed to all 80-year-olds. The participants received the dental examination free of charge.

A total of 3,392 participants (2,010 females and 1,382 males; about 10% of the population) visited dental clinics between June 2021 and December 2022. 167 participants with dementia were excluded. Furthermore, 3 participants those with missing data on items representing the risk of oral frailty and other variables were also excluded. All participants provided signed informed consent before participating in the study. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Kagoshima University (No.230282) and performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The corresponding author signed a memorandum regarding the use of the survey data.

Oral examination

Periodontal examination was performed using a method modified from the Community Periodontal Index, based on the criteria given by the WHO [11], which includes bleeding on probing and pocket depth. Additionally, the number of remaining teeth, tooth decay and filling, dental plaque, tongue coating status and organoleptic malodors were also measured. The remaining teeth included mild to moderate caries and periodontal disease, and excluded dentures, pontics of bridge, and dental implants. The number of decayed teeth was calculated as D (Decayed), missing teeth as M (Missing), and filled teeth as F (Filled), and used to evaluate dental caries history [12]. Dental plaque was classified as good, normal (less than one-thirds) and poor (more than one-thirds) based on previous study [13]. Tongue coating status were visually examined as follows: 1 = a tongue coating affecting approximately one-third of the tongue, 2 = a tongue coating affecting approximately two-thirds of the tongue, 3 = a tongue coating affecting more than two-thirds of the tongue [14]. Poor tongue coating weas diagnosed when more than one-third of the area of the tongue dorsum surface was covered. The organoleptic malodor was estimated according to the procedures outlined in a previous report [15, 16] and “poor” was diagnosed when more than slight malodor was present.

Definition and assessment of oral frailty

The presence or absence of oral frailty risk was previously evaluated based on eight items to help screen older adults in the community setting [1, 3]. The OFI-8 is a screening questionnaire that integrates oral health-related behaviors and frailty concepts. The total OFI-8 score ranges from 0 to 11 points, with higher scores indicating poorer oral health. Each question was extracted from a questionnaire that is widely used in Japan. All items were scored as follows:

Do you have any difficulties eating tough foods compared to 6 months ago? (Yes, 2 points).

A score of ≥ 4 points indicated a high risk of oral frailty [1]. Therefore, a high risk of oral frailty was defined as ≥ 4 points of OFI-8 score in the present study.

Assessment of potential confounders

Participants completed a self-administered questionnaire. Information about each participant’s residential district, smoking status, current medication use, and medical history, including diabetes mellitus, hypertension, pneumonia, stroke, cardiovascular disease, osteoporosis, dyslipidemia, cancer, and dementia, was collected using standardized questionnaires by trained physicians and research assistants. Polypharmacy was defined as the concurrent use of five or more medications, with most studies using this definition [17]. Data on oral health behavior and frailty [18] were also obtained using a self-administered questionnaire based on the following criteria: (1) Family dental clinic: Do you have a family dental clinic? (2) Oral concerns: Do you have any concerns regarding your teeth or mouth? (3) Interdental cleaning: Do you use dental floss and/or an interdental brush?; and (4) Shrinking: Have you unintentionally lost 2 kg or more in the past 6 months?.

Statistical analysis

We divided the participants according to the risk of oral frailty (high and low) and the number of teeth (≥ 20 and < 20) [8]. Student t-test and Chi-square tests were used to compare continuous and categorical variables, respectively. Odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated using logistic regression models to explore the association between oral frailty risk and number of remaining teeth. The variables included sex, smoking status, residential district, remaining teeth, decayed teeth, pocket depth, bleeding on probing, dental plaque, tongue coating, oral malodor, oral health behavior, medical history, polypharmacy, and frailty-related questions. The goodness of fit of logistic regression models were assessed by the Hosmer-Lemeshow test, and fitted models (p > 0.1) were used. In addition, all variance inflation factors were less than five, and no multicollinaerlity was observed at all variables. The post hoc power calculations were performed, based on the results of remaining teeth between oral frailty groups. A sample size of 34 per group was required for detection of a significant difference (80% power, two-sided 5% significance level). All analyses were performed using the JMP software (version 12, SAS Institute), and statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

Participant characteristics

A total of 3,222 participants (1,911 female and 1,311 male) including edentulous were included in this study. Of the total participants, 1,217 (37.8%) and 2,005 (62.2%) had a high and low risk of oral frailty, respectively. Comparisons of participant characteristics according to the risk of oral frailty are presented in Table 1. The risk of oral frailty was significantly affected by smoking status, number of remaining teeth, decayed teeth, oral malodor, family dental clinic, oral concerns, osteoporosis, and shrinking body weight. However, no significant differences were observed in terms of sex, residential area, pocket depth, bleeding on probing, dental plaque, tongue coating, interdental cleaning, and excluded from explanatory variables.

Discussion

In the present study, we investigated whether oral frailty is related to the number of remaining teeth and oral health behaviors (family dental clinic and oral concerns) among community-dwelling 80-year-old adults in Japan. These results highlight the importance of disseminating information about family dental clinics and oral concerns to 80-year-old participants to prevent oral frailty. We also found that the risk of oral frailty was significantly increased among the components of dental plaque and oral malodor. These relationships persisted after adjusting for various confounding variables using a logistic regression model. Furthermore, the participants with ≥ 20 remaining teeth were significantly associated with sex, smoking status, residential district, pocket depth, bleeding on probing, dental plaque, and interdental cleaning. Especially, the importance of the residential district was informed to achieve ≥ 20 remaining teeth for 80-year-old participants. The participants living in Amami, an Island area, were negatively correlated to ≥ 20 remaining teeth.

This study indicated that 37.8% of the 80-year-old participants recognized oral frailty. A previous study indicated that the prevalence of oral frailty at baseline and 6 years was 16% and 24%, respectively [1]. However, all the participants investigated in this study were aged 80 years. The risk of oral frailty is associated with age [19]. Moreover, fewer than 20 remaining teeth were positively associated with oral frailty. The number of teeth is associated with mortality, physical frailty, functional disability, and quality of life [7]. In particular, a reduced number of teeth, which causes difficulties in chewing and lowers occlusal force/masticatory function, is the oral health indicator most frequently associated with an increased risk of oral frailty. Oral problems, such as pain and discomfort, lead to tooth loss and difficulty in eating; consequently, this affect esthetics, self-esteem, and quality of life. Additionally, a large community-based cohort study showed that number of teeth and dental plaque were associated with all-cause mortality [20]. The improving of oral health might work well in influencing oral frailty. A high risk of oral frailty was negatively associated with family dental clinics and positively associated with oral concerns. The reasons for oral pain, toothache and discomfort were indicated a positive association with dental visits. The complex interconnectedness of several factors and dental attendance in older adults were also revealed [21]. Participants with family dental clinics can easily visit a dental clinic for treatment. Barriers to dental treatment could be removed by motivating participants and making them aware of oral health problems to eliminate fear and anxiety and develop a positive attitude toward visiting dental clinics. Though oral malodor was positively associated with oral frailty, it was not corelated to all of each question of OFI-8 in this study. Tongue coating is the most important cause of intra-oral malodor [22], whereas was not significantly different between the high- and low-risk groups in this study. However, participants with a high risk of oral frailty had a slightly higher prevalence of tongue coating (p = 0.06). Further research might be needed to understand these relations. Dysbiosis of the tongue-coated microbiome is associated with the risk of aspiration pneumonia. Tongue cleaning in older adults may have a rehabilitation effect on respiratory and swallowing functions [22] and will hold an important place in the future.

In this study, remaining teeth included decayed teeth, and the pulpal decay teeth which are not useful for mastication, were consequently omitted from the definition. Additionally, functional teeth are usually defined as teeth that can carry out masticatory functions. These include natural sound teeth, filled teeth, or crowned teeth. Deterioration of masticatory function is associated with deterioration in general health, which is attributed to dietary selection and nutritional intake in older adults [23]. The mortality risk is significantly higher for the lowest masticatory function category than the participants with highest masticatory function [2]. Furthermore, the masticatory function was found to be most often associated with frailty [24], and used to assess oral frailty. Though the relations to functional teeth were not investigated in this study, oral frailty was affected by not only number of remaining teeth but also the presence of decayed teeth. Oral frailty would be influenced by deterioration of masticatory function, which was caused by decayed teeth.

Periodontal breakdown is a major cause of tooth loosening with age and can affect oral function. Epidemiological studies have shown that poor oral hygiene or high levels of dental plaque are associated with a high prevalence and severity of periodontal disease [25]. In this study, participants with < 20 remaining teeth were positively associated with poor oral hygiene and negatively associated with deep pocket depth and bleeding. The high frequency of severe tooth loss and edentulousness masks the potentially high prevalence of periodontitis among elderly individual [26]. The participants with ≥ 20 remaining teeth could assess gingival scores and pocket registration at most parts of the index teeth. However, participants with < 20 remaining teeth, including edentulous teeth, had fewer examined teeth and sextants qualifying. Missing index teeth were likely to have a negative relationship with deep pocket depth and the presence of bleeding. Additionally, interdental cleaning was positively related to ≥ 20 remaining teeth for 80-year-old participants. A previous study indicated that using interdental cleaning devices significantly affects periodontitis prevention and inequality alleviation effect in the population with ≥ 20 remaining teeth [27]. Additionally, a large-scale, nationally representative longitudinal study showed that frequent interdental cleaning was associated with better self-perceived oral health and less gingival bleeding [28]. Hence, interdental cleaning would be more important to achieve ≥ 20 remaining teeth for the 80-year-old population.

This study suggests that older adults with ≥ 20 remaining teeth exhibited a significant disparity between the residential districts, with a considerably lower prevalence observed in the Amami area compared to the Kagoshima, Aira/Isa, Soo, and Kimotsuki areas. Amami, an island located off the Asian mainland and between the main Japanese islands and Okinawa, has developed a distinct culture, a combination of different influences, and shared similarities. In contrast, Kagoshima is a large city with a higher budget than the other cities. Therefore, extensive oral health-related programs should be planned. The Aira/Isa and Soo areas are also populous and considered urban areas. A previous study indicated that urban-rural residential differences are significantly associated with social frailty [29]. Meanwhile, it is also revealed that residential disparities are identified in the linkage of oral health knowledge and practices with frailty [30]. Furthermore, a previous study indicated that older populations in rural areas generally have lower levels of oral health knowledge, poorer oral health, and reduced quality of life [31]. Consequently, older individuals in the Amami area, considered rural and remote areas, might lack the social resources necessary to meet their basic oral health needs, engage in oral health behaviors and activities, and possess self-management skills, resulting in a higher incidence of tooth loss than those in urban areas. This finding is in agreement with that of a previous study in Brazil, where older adults living in rural areas had a higher rate of tooth loss and poor oral health [31]. Further studies are required to confirm this information in the future.

The current study has a few limitations. First, it was a cross-sectional study with a large sample size. Although we verified the correlation between oral frailty and oral health, the causal relationships require further validation through longitudinal research. In the future, more longitudinal studies should focus on estimating the relationship between oral frailty and oral health behaviors and finding an effective way to reduce oral frailty. Second, this study confirmed the correlation between oral frailty and oral health status. However, how oral health causes oral frailty requires further investigation. Third, the study included only older Japanese adults from specific areas. Therefore, the results may not be generalizable to other populations. Finally, other possible confounders, such as education level, household income, and nutritional intake are widely accepted to affect oral conditions, and might have played an important role in this study. Further studies will be required to investigate these issues.

Conclusion

In summary, within the limitations of this study, our findings suggest that a high risk of oral frailty is significantly associated with the remaining teeth and oral health behaviors in community-dwelling participants in Japan. The main clinical implication of this study is that improving behaviors regarding oral healthcare among community-dwelling individuals may be an effective way to reduce oral frailty. Furthermore, ≥ 20 remaining teeth were impacted by factors such as sex, smoking status, periodontal conditions, and interdental cleaning, even after adjusting for possible confounders. Further, we found that the number of 80-year-old participants with ≥ 20 remaining teeth was notably higher in rural areas compared to urban areas in Japan.
