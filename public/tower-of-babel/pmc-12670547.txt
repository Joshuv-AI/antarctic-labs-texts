Rates of lumbar vertebral body compression fracture after basivertebral nerve ablation: A retrospective study utilizing a large national database
Authors: Justin Chau, Durga Ghosh, Sean Fox, Ayahiro D. Takashima, Andrew Stephens
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBasivertebral nerve ablation (BVNA) is an effective treatment for vertebrogenic chronic low back pain. However, data regarding rates of post-BVNA lumbar vertebral compression fracture (VCF), particularly among patients with osteoporosis or osteopenia, remains limited.Objective(1) Estimate the rate of lumbar VCF after BVNA. (2) Compare rates between patients with and without a pre-BVNA diagnosis of osteopenia/osteoporosis utilizing a large global database.MethodsA retrospective cohort study was performed using TriNetX, a national database of de-identified health records from over 130 million patients. Adults (≥18 years) who underwent BVNA between January 2022 and August 2025 were identified via CPT code. Patients were stratified by presence or absence of ICD-10 code for osteoporosis/osteopenia before undergoing BVNA. The primary outcome was incidence of ICD-10 code diagnosis of lumbar VCF after undergoing BVNA.ResultsA total of 2387 patients underwent BVNA (mean age 63.7 years; 48.0 % female). Of these, 669 had a pre-BVNA diagnosis of osteoporosis/osteopenia (mean age 70.2 ± 10.7 years; 64.4 % female). Lumbar VCF occurred in 37 patients (1.55 %; 95 % CI, 1.11–2.16 %) after BVNA. Patients with osteoporosis/osteopenia had higher rate of post-BVNA lumbar VCF compared to those without (4.64 % vs 0.37 %), though rates of post-BVNA lumbar VCF were overall low across all patients (1.55 %). Osteoporosis/osteopenia was independently associated with post-BVNA VCF (adjusted OR 6.5; 95 % CI, 3.7–12.3; p < 0.001).ConclusionWhile patients with pre-BVNA ICD-10 code diagnosis of osteoporosis/osteopenia had higher rates and were at higher risk of post-BVNA VCF than those without, overall rates were still low. Risk of VCF should be considered and risks/benefits should be discussed with patients, but a diagnosis of osteoporosis/osteopenia should not be considered an absolute contraindication to BVNA.

Introduction

Chronic low back pain (CLBP) is the leading cause of disability worldwide [1] and has been historically challenging to diagnose and treat effectively [2]. With advances in imaging, and improved understanding of spine biochemistry and pathophysiology, etiologies can now be more effectively diagnosed and treated with targeted interventions. This is true of vertebrogenic back pain, which is known to be secondary to damage to the vertebral endplates and identifiable on MRI [3]. The vertebral endplates are innervated by the basivertebral nerve [4], thus providing a clear interventional target. Radiofrequency ablation of the basivertebral nerve (BVNA) has been shown to have lasting positive effects in patients with vertebrogenic CLBP with rare complications [[5], [6], [7]].

In the BVNA procedure, the basivertebral nerve is accessed via an intraosseous approach by passing a trocar through the pedicle and advancing to the basivertebral nerve terminus within the vertebral body, theoretically putting the vertebra at risk of a compression fracture [8,9]. Patients with low bone mineral density are at increased risk of fractures [10]. The INTRACEPT and SMART trials demonstrated the safety of BVNA, with only 1 report of VCF in a patient on hormone therapy later diagnosed with osteopenia. These clinical trials excluded patients with osteoporosis [[5], [6], [7],11].

Fogel et al. reported 8 cases of VCFs in a cohort of 74 (11 %) patients after BVNA. All VCFs occurred in osteoporotic patients [9]. However, subsequent small real-world cohort studies by Schnapp et al. and McCormick et al. reported no VCFs [12,13], though these studies did not directly screen for osteoporosis/osteopenia. Additionally, Below et al. reported no VCFs in a real-world retrospective chart review on 134 patients that underwent BVNA, including those with diagnoses of osteoporosis/osteopenia [14].

Thus, the true incidence of post-procedure VCF is unknown. The purpose of this study was to (1) estimate the rate of lumbar VCF after BVNA and (2) compare rates between patients with and without a pre-BVNA diagnosis of osteopenia/osteoporosis utilizing a large global database.

Methods

Study design

This study was conducted using de-identified patient data obtained from the TriNetX Research Network, a global federated health research platform providing access to aggregated electronic health records. All data within TriNetX are de-identified in compliance with the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) and other applicable privacy regulations, and no protected health information is accessible to investigators. The use of de-identified data from TriNetX has been determined by the Stanford University Institutional Review Board to not constitute human subjects research and is therefore exempt from IRB review. All procedures were performed in accordance with institutional and national research ethics standards and the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent was waived as no identifiable data were used.

TriNetX is a large national database containing de-identified health records from more than 130 million patients across over 100 U S. healthcare systems. Data within TriNetX may be queried using International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10) codes and Current Procedural Terminology (CPT) codes. For this study, TriNetX was queried to identify all adult patients (≥18 years) who underwent BVNA (CPT 64628) in the United States between January 2022 and August 2025. No diagnostic or procedural codes (e.g., pre-procedure surgery) were used to exclude patients. However, patients lacking either pre-procedure data or any follow-up data after BVNA were excluded. As the database cannot restrict a minimum follow-up duration, patients were only included if their BVNA procedure occurred at least one year before the query period. Demographic variables including age, sex, race, and ethnicity were also collected. From this cohort, patients were then categorized into two distinct cohorts (1) patients with pre-BVNA ICD-10 codes for osteoporosis/osteopenia (ICD-10: 80, 81, 89, 85.8) and (2) patients without pre-BVNA ICD-10 codes for osteoporosis/osteopenia. The primary outcome was the incidence of lumbar vertebral compression fracture identified by ICD-10 code (ICD-10 S32.0) after undergoing BVNA.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were reported as means ± standard deviations (SD) for continuous variables and counts with percentages for categorical variables. Demographic differences were calculated using Welch's t-test for continuous variables and chi-square test for categorical variables. Wilson 95 % confidence interval was calculated for the number of patients with lumbar VCF after undergoing BVNA. Differences in post-BVNA lumbar VCF incidence was calculated using Fisher's exact test due to low event counts in one group. Odds ratio was calculated using the Woolf log method, and absolute risk difference (RD) was calculated using Newcombe-Wilson method. Penalized logistic regression (L2 regularization) adjusting for age and sex was used to assess whether osteoporosis/osteopenia remained an independent predictor of lumbar VCF. Bootstrap resampling (2000 iterations) was used to generate stable CIs due to sparse event counts. A two-sided p < 0.05 was considered significant. Analyses were performed in Python (SciPy, statsmodels, scikit-learn).

Results

Baseline characteristics

A total of 2387 patients underwent BVNA during this time frame (mean age 63.7 ± 14.1 years; 48.0 % female, 50.6 % male). Of the 2387 patients who underwent BVNA, 669 had a pre-BVNA ICD-10 code for osteoporosis/osteopenia (mean age 70.2 ± 10.7 years; 64.4 % female, 34.7 % male) and 1636 did not (mean age 61.0 ± 14.5 years; 41.4 % female, 57.1 % male). Compared with the non-osteoporosis/osteopenia cohort, patients with pre-BVNA osteoporosis/osteopenia were significantly older (70.2 vs 61.0 years, p < 0.001) and more likely to be female (64.4 % vs 41.4 %, p < 0.001). Patients with pre-BVNA osteoporosis/osteopenia were more likely to be White (89.7 % vs 85.4 %, p = 0.005) (Table 1).

Primary outcome

Of the 2387 patients who underwent BVNA, 37 patients had a post-BVNA ICD-10 code for lumbar VCF (37/2387 = 1.55 %; 95 % CI, 1.11–2.16 %). Among patients with pre-BVNA osteoporosis/osteopenia, 31 had a post-BVNA ICD-10 code for lumbar VCF (31/669 = 4.64 %; 95 % CI, 3.26–6.54 %), compared with 6 VCF without pre-BVNA osteoporosis/osteopenia codes (6/1636 = 0.37 %; 95 % CI, 0.17–0.80 %) (Table 1).

Patients with pre-BVNA osteoporosis/osteopenia had 12.6-fold higher risk of lumbar VCF after undergoing BVNA (OR 13.2; 95 % CI 5.5–31.8; p < 0.001). Discussion

This administrative database study evaluated rates of post-BVNA lumbar VCF in patients with and without a pre-BVNA diagnosis of osteoporosis/osteopenia.

Rates of lumbar VCF

We found that patients with osteoporosis/osteopenia had a statistically significantly higher rate (4.64 % vs 0.37 %) and were at higher risk of post-BVNA lumbar VCF compared to those without (OR 13.2). Even after adjusting for age and sex on multivariate analysis, osteoporosis/osteopenia remained an independent predictor of post-BVNA lumbar VCF (adjusted OR = 6.5). These rates are much higher than the 1 reported VCF in the INTRACEPT and SMART trials which excluded osteoporotic/osteopenic patients [[5], [6], [7],11]. But even in subsequent real-world studies conducted by McCormick et al., Schnapp et al., and Below et al., no VCFs were reported in patients who underwent BVNA [[12], [13], [14]]. The McCormick study prospectively followed 60 patients over 1 year [13]. The Schnapp study prospectively followed 31 patients over 12 months [12]. Neither study screened patients for osteoporosis/osteopenia, though the Schnapp study likely included some given the average age of patients (mean age 73.0) [12,13]. The Below study retrospectively reviewed 134 patients and included 23 patients with osteopenia and 9 with osteoporosis [14]. Our study comparatively had a much larger sample size (N = 2387). That, along with age and sex differences discussed below, may account for the higher rates and risk of post-BVNA lumbar VCF found in our study.

Fogel et al. study, raised attention when it reported 8 cases of post-BVNA VCFs in a cohort of 74 (11 %) patients. All VCFs in that study occurred in osteoporotic patients [9]. While our study does demonstrate a lower rate of VCF compared to Fogel et al., it should be noted that the thoracolumbar vertebrae are at high risk of VCF, and that 4 of the VCFs reported in the Fogel study occurred at the L2 region, a level that is not FDA cleared for BVNA [9,11,15]. Furthermore, 2 VCFs were at levels adjacent to prior fusion surgery, 1 was at a level adjacent to a prior kyphoplasty, and 1 occurred at an untreated level [9]. These factors may account for differences in post-BVNA VCF rates between our study and Fogel's study.

Demographic data

Patients in our study's osteoporosis/osteopenia cohort were significantly older (mean age 70.2 vs 61.0 years) and more likely to be female (64.4 % vs 41.4 %). When compared the original INTRACEPT (mean age 49.4) and SMART (mean age 46.9) clinical trials, our cohort was relatively older across all patients, especially in the osteoporosis/osteopenia group. While sex distribution in our full cohort and non-osteoporosis/osteopenia subcohort was comparable to the INTRACEPT (48.5 % female) and SMART (44.2 % female) trials, there was a much higher percentage of females in our osteoporosis/osteopenia group [[5], [6], [7],11]. The age of patients in all our cohorts were more comparable to the Schnapp study (mean age 73.0 years) and Below study (mean age 69.8 years all patients, 75.5 years reduced bone density group). However, our osteoporosis/osteopenia group had a much higher percentage of females (64.4 %) compared to either study (15.6 % Below, 29.0 % Schnapp) [12,14]. Older age and female sex are known risk factors for osteoporosis/osteopenia and fracture due to estrogen related changes [10]. Differences in age and sex distribution between our study and prior studies combined with a larger sample size, likely account for the higher rates and risk of post-BVNA lumbar VCF found in our study.

Safety and efficacy of BVNA

Although we found that patients with osteoporosis/osteopenia had significantly higher rates and were at higher risk of post-BVNA lumbar VCF compared to those without, rates of post-BVNA lumbar VCF were still overall low across all patients (1.55 %) and between cohorts (4.64 % vs 0.37 %). Numerous clinical trials, pooled cohort studies, systematic reviews, and prospective real-world studies continue to demonstrate BVNA to have significant and lasting positive effects in patients with vertebrogenic CLBP with rare complications [[5], [6], [7],11,13,14,[16], [17], [18], [19]]. This includes patients with osteoporosis/osteopenia. Below et al. demonstrated a statistically significant reduction in VAS scores in patients with reduced bone mineral density (BMD) after BVNA, with 78.1 % reporting improvement in their functional status [14].

Limitations

Our study has several limitations. Given that a database was utilized in this study, specific patient charts were unable to be assessed for accuracy of codes or completeness of data. Searching via ICD-10 code for osteoporosis/osteopenia also does not capture BMD data via DEXA scans and could under or overpredict the number of patients with a true low BMD diagnosis. Furthermore, the ICD-10 code used to assess VCF is technically limited to lumbar VCF only. BVNA is FDA cleared for intervention at the L3-S1 level, and our study may have missed any post-BVNA S1 fractures [11]. The observational retrospective design also limits our ability to establish causality and introduces potential for unmeasured potential confounders (steroid use, BMI, etc). Proper patient selection and procedural technique for BVNA were unable to be assessed.

Strengths of our study include that a large, well-defined cohort of 2387 patients was evaluated. Compared to most studies evaluating outcomes after BVNA, this work assessed outcomes of patients not enrolled in a randomized controlled trial. Despite being unable to control for indications for BVNA and assess adequate procedural technique, this work is the first study to estimate rates of post-BVNA lumbar VCF in patients with and without pre-BVNA osteoporosis/osteopenia in a large data set.

Conclusion

In this administrative database study, we found that while patients with pre-BVNA ICD-10 code diagnosis of osteoporosis/osteopenia had higher rates and were at higher risk of post-BVNA VCF than those without, overall rates were still low. While risk of VCF should be considered and risks/benefits should be discussed with patients, a diagnosis of osteoporosis/osteopenia should not preclude patients from receiving BVNA. BVNA continues to be a significantly effective treatment for patients with vertebrogenic CLBP with rare complications.
