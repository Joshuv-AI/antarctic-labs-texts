Look the Other Way: Designing ‘Positive’ Molecules with Negative Data via Task Arithmetic
Rıza ÖzçelikEindhoven University of TechnologyThe Netherlands r.ozcelik@tue.nl Sarah de RuiterEindhoven University of TechnologyThe Netherlands s.l.d.ruiter@student.tue.nl, Francesca GrisoniEindhoven University of TechnologyThe Netherlands f.grisoni@tue.nl

Abstract
The scarcity of molecules with desirable properties (i.e., ‘positive’ molecules) is an inherent bottleneck for generative molecule design. To sidestep such obstacle, here we propose molecular task arithmetic: training a model on diverse and abundant negative examples to learn ‘property directions’ – without accessing any positively labeled data – and moving models in the opposite property directions to generate positive molecules. When analyzed on 20 zero-shot design experiments, molecular task arithmetic generated more diverse and successful designs than models trained on positive molecules. Moreover, we employed molecular task arithmetic in dual-objective and few-shot design tasks. We find that molecular task arithmetic can consistently increase the diversity of designs while maintaining desirable design properties. With its simplicity, data efficiency, and performance, molecular task arithmetic bears the potential to become the de-facto transfer learning strategy for de novo molecule design.

Background

Task arithmetic

In the weight space of a trained model, certain directions correspond to improvements in performance on specific tasks
25
. These directions are known as “task vectors”
25
,
27
,
28
,
29
. In a transfer learning setting, where data for a task
t
are available, a task vector
\tau^{t}
can be computed by subtracting the pretrained model weights from the finetuned model weights
25
:

\tau^{t}=\theta^{t}_{\text{ft}}-\theta_{\text{pt}},

(1)

where
\theta^{t}_{\text{ft}}
and
\theta_{\text{pt}}
are the flattened weights of the finetuned and of the pretrained model, respectively. The task vector can then be used to modify the behavior of the pretrained model, e.g., subtracting the task vector from the pretrained model weights can yield a model that is worse at the finetuning task
t
:

\theta_{\text{new}}=\theta_{\text{pt}}-\lambda\tau^{t},

(2)

where
\lambda
is a scaling factor that determines the step size in the direction. Termed as “forgetting via negation”
25
, this approach can remove undesired behavior from deep models, such as toxic language generation
25
. Task vectors can also be added together to create models with multi-task capabilities (“learning via addition”
25
):

\theta_{\text{mobj}}=\theta_{\text{pt}}+\lambda(\tau^{t_{1}}+\tau^{t_{2}}+\tau^{t_{3}}+\dots+\tau^{t_{n}}),

(3)

where
\tau^{t_{i}}
is the task vector learned for the task
t^{i}
. Both in Eq. (
2
) and in Eq. (
3
),
\lambda
controls how far the final model lands from the pretrained one in the weight space. While too large
\lambda
values can have detrimental side effects on model performance, too low values fail to sufficiently edit the model behavior.

Molecular string generation for de novo design

Molecular strings are among the most popular molecular representations for de novo molecule design
30
,
31
,
32
,
33
. The most well-established representation is the Simplified Molecular Input Line Entry System (SMILES
31
) strings (Fig.
1
a). SMILES strings are created by traversing a molecular graph in any chosen direction, starting from any non-hydrogen atom, and annotating atomic symbols, plus branches and bond types. Multiple SMILES strings (often referred to as
randomized SMILES
) can be created by starting from different atoms (and/or with different traversals); this characteristic allows for data augmentation, where multiple instances referring to the same molecule are used to inflate the amount of training data
34
.
Canonicalization
algorithms have been proposed to obtain a single SMILES string from any molecule (i.e., by standardizing the starting atom and the graph traversal rules
35
,
36
). Canonicalization can aid in duplicate molecule detection and molecule ranking
37
,
38
,
39
. Both versions are commonly used for molecule design, with different advantages and drawbacks
40
.

For modeling purposes, the SMILES string represents a molecule
m
as
\{s_{1},s_{2},\dots,s_{n}\}
, where
s_{i}
corresponds to the
i^{th}
token in the molecular string. The next token prediction language modeling objective is then defined as:
\max\sum_{m}^{M}\sum_{i}\log P(s_{i}|s_{<i};\theta)
, where
\theta
is typically a long-short term memory network
41
,
42
,
14

or a transformer
43
,
44
,
15
(Fig.
1
b), and
M
is a dataset of SMILES strings.

In a transfer learning setting, two phases typically occur: (a)
pretraining
, where
M
is selected to contain hundreds of thousands of molecules to learn key elements, such as generating ‘chemically valid’ SMILES, and to learn basic molecular properties
17
; and (b)
(self-)supervised finetuning
, where a curated set of molecules with desired properties is used, with the same training objective to condition the model
45
,
46
,
17
. New molecules can then be designed by sampling the learned multinomial distribution autoregressively. Chemical language models have already demonstrated powerful design capabilities for drug discovery and are a well-established paradigm for molecule design
47
,
48
,

,
49
,

,

3
Molecular Task Arithmetic

Why task arithmetic?
Chemical space is vast but sparse: it is estimated to contain 10
60
molecules, but molecules of desirable properties are rare
3
. Current training strategies rely on these rare, ‘positive’ molecules (i.e., molecules possessing one or more desirable properties), and therefore, the limited data availability and diversity form a bottleneck. Here we ‘look the other way’ and propose a new transfer learning strategy that can leverage the abundant and diverse ‘negative’ molecules, that is, molecular task arithmetic.

Molecular task arithmetic views “forgetting via subtraction” (Eq.
2
) as an opportunity to learn
only
from negative data to design positive molecules. For the task
t
of designing molecules with a desirable property, molecular task arithmetic finetunes a pretrained model (
\theta_{pt}
) with molecules having non-desirable values of this property. This allows obtaining
\theta_{opp}
, a model that can generate negative molecules. Then the task vector
\tau^{t}
is computed by subtracting
\theta_{pt}
from
\theta_{opp}
. Finally, a model that creates the molecules of positive property,
\theta_{task}
(Fig.
1
c), can be constructed by subtracting
\tau^{t}
from
\theta_{pt}
with a scaling factor
\lambda
:

\displaystyle\tau^{t}

\displaystyle=\theta_{\text{opp}}-\theta_{\text{pt}},

\displaystyle\theta_{\text{task}}

\displaystyle=\theta_{\text{pt}}-\lambda\tau^{t}.

(4)

In other words, molecular task arithmetic learns the ‘direction’ of a molecular property using negative molecules and then ‘looks the other way’ – by moving the pretrained model in the opposite direction to design positive molecules. By not using any labeled positive molecules, this formulation opens the doors for unexplored tasks, e.g., zero-shot molecule design.

To test the impact of task arithmetic on steering de novo design of bespoke molecules, we have applied it to easily computable properties that are relevant for drug discovery, namely: (a) fraction of sp3-hybridized carbons (frac. sp
3
C), which reflects molecular complexity and 3D characteristics; (b) octanol-water partitioning coefficient (logP), influencing membrane permeability and solubility; (c) number of hydrogen bond donors, which affects a molecule’s ability to form key interactions with biological targets; (d) number of rings (No. Rings), linked to molecular rigidity and target binding specificity; and (e) topological surface area (TPSA).

We test molecular task arithmetic on chemical language models with an LSTM backbone. We pretrained the LSTM on 1.5M drug-like molecules and define conditional molecule design experiments as designing molecules with a higher or lower value than a predefined threshold of the five selected molecular properties (Fig.
A1
). By defining tasks for randomized and canonical SMILES and per property, we create 20 design tasks. Five splits of 1024 train, 256 validation, 256 test molecules, and 10240 negative molecules are curated per task for finetuning and molecular task arithmetic (
see
Appendix for more details).

4
Experiments

4.1
Can Task Arithmetic Even Generate Valid Molecules?

The language of SMILES strings possesses a strict grammar to represent molecules, where single-character changes might yield ‘chemically invalid’ strings (i.e., strings that cannot be converted back into molecules). Hence, our first question was whether the capacity to generate chemically valid strings could be retained under the model weight manipulation introduced by task arithmetic.

To answer this question, we applied molecular task arithmetic with
\lambda=0.50
(Eq.
3
) across the ten single-objective design tasks and the two SMILES formats (canonical and randomized). We then computed molecular validity (defined as the ratio of unique and chemically valid design strings among 100,000 generations), as a well-established control of the capacity of a model to learn the ‘chemical language’ of SMILES. Molecular task arithmetic obtains an average validity of 79.0% with randomized SMILES strings and 59.2% with canonical SMILES strings. While these values drop from the pretrained models (which were optimized for validity, obtaining values of 95.2% and 95.8% for randomized and canonical SMILES strings, respectively), molecular task arithmetic preserves its ability to generate chemically meaningful strings, especially with randomized SMILES strings. Remarkably, this finding indicates that the weight space of a chemical language model can be traversed with no loss guidance – opening new and unexplored avenues for de novo molecule design with generative deep learning.

In what follows, we explore the benefits and limitations of traversing the model weight space with molecular task arithmetic, with the following objectives:

1.

Exploring zero-shot de novo design
, where only molecules with ‘undesired’ properties are used to design ‘desirable’ molecules. In this context, we explore single-property molecule design (Fig.
1
c), and dual-objective molecule design (Fig.
1
d), where single and pairs of properties are targeted, respectively.

2.

Understanding the effect of task vector scaling
, where we analyze how the magnitude of the molecular task vector (
\lambda
) affects the capacity to explore the chemical space in search for molecules with desirable properties.

3.

Boosting few-shot molecule design
(Fig.
1
e), by augmenting traditional finetuning (which relies on positive molecules only) with negative molecules, via molecular task arithmetic.

Taken together, these findings display that molecular task arithmetic can preserve the diversity gained by pretraining during conditioning, while finetuning ‘forgets’. Side effects.

Another desirable trait of model conditioning is the possibility of having minimal side effects on the ‘off-target’ properties. Being able to minimize side effects would allow for a more precise steering of the model in the chemical space, by preserving desirable molecular properties (i.e., synthesizability, lack of toxicity, etc.). We quantify the side effects of both finetuning and molecular task arithmetic for all design tasks, on the remaining four descriptors. These descriptors have a low correlation with the ‘target’ properties (Table
A3
). For each task, we computed the Kolmogorov-Smirnov (KS) distance between the off-target molecular properties of the designs and the pretraining set (the lower the KS, the more similar the distributions). The usefulness of this analysis is corroborated by observing the KS distances of the designs obtained by pretraining (Fig.
A4
), which are all below 5%.

Across the ten design tasks for randomized SMILES, molecular task arithmetic showed a smaller mean distributional distance to the pretraining set in 32 of the 40 comparisons (Fig.
3
), of which 15 were confirmed by a statistical test (Mann-Whitney U test, p-value
<
0.01). On canonical SMILES strings, all models have a higher average distance (Fig.
A5
), possibly because canonicalization restricts the models to explore ‘side-effect-free’ portions of the chemical space. Overall, the analysis shows that molecular task arithmetic can combine its accuracy and diversity on the design task, with less side-effect than the traditional finetuning approach. This suggests that task arithmetic allows for a more controlled steering of the models in the desirable portions of the chemical space.

Dual-objective design.

We next explore the capabilities of molecular task arithmetic for dual-objective de novo design. This objective is central in drug discovery, for instance, for multi-target drug design, or selectivity optimization. We define three dual-objective tasks via the molecular properties above: (a) high fraction of sp
3
-hybridized carbons and high number of hydrogen bond donors, (b) high lipophilicity and low TPSA, and (c) high number of rings and low TPSA. These pairs of properties were selected to be minimally correlated (absolute correlation lower than 0.3; Table
A3
), so that the models neither face a conflicting nor a trivial task.

To apply molecular task arithmetic in a dual-objective setting (Fig.
1

3
) and scale the final vector in increasing
\lambda
values (0.05 to 1.00 with a step size of 0.05). For finetuning, we finetuned the models on the training set of the single tasks sequentially (using positive molecules), as in recent literature

,
57
, with both task permutations. We reported the number of (successful) design clusters, success rate, and validity for the
\lambda
and permutation yielding the highest number of successful design clusters for molecular task arithmetic and finetuning, respectively.

4.3
Effect of Task Vector Scaling

Here, we further dig into molecular task arithmetic, to study the impact of
\lambda
(Eq.
3
) on the performance. The parameter
\lambda
scales the task vector length and determines how far the task model ‘lands’ from the pretrained model after task negation. We compute validity and success rate across all 20 design tasks with 19 increasing
\lambda
values (from
0.05
to
1.00
with a step of
0.05
). We measured the validity and success rate in those increasing lengths of task vectors, to reveal how much the model can move in the weight space before its generation capabilities collapse.

Across experiments, the validity decreases with increasing
\lambda
values (Fig.
5
and Fig.
A7
). We explain this by the objective of the pretraining task: since maximizing next token prediction accuracy optimizes weights for generation validity, moving the model away causes a drop. The success rate also displays consistent patterns (Fig.
5
and Fig.
A7
): it first increases, then plateaus (typically for
0.30\leq\lambda\leq 0.50
), and monotonically decreases afterward. This shows that molecular task arithmetic gradually conditions the pretrained model as the model moves along the task direction, and then, generative capabilities start limiting the number of successful designs. The consistent behaviors offer a practical insight to tune
\lambda
for molecular task arithmetic models. Models can be edited by setting
\lambda=0.30
and gradually increasing the value until the success rate starts decreasing. Thanks to the peaking behavior, this heuristic can find the
\lambda
value optimal for the task.

We next delve into another interesting problem in drug discovery: few-shot molecule design. In few-shot molecule design, some positive labeled molecules are already available, which are used for molecule design. Additionally, negative molecules are usually available (and in general more abundant), but are not leveraged in traditional finetuning pipelines. Here we set out to investigate whether molecular task arithmetic can allow leveraging negative molecules alongside positive ones to boost the capacity of models to generate molecules with desirable properties.

We applied molecular task arithmetic as in the zero-shot design experiments (Eq.
3
) first, and then fine-tunex the obtained model with the known positive molecules (Fig.
1
e). In other words, this pipeline replaces the pretraining model used for supervised finetuning with the molecular task arithmetic model, to harness the benefits of abundant and structurally diverse negative molecules. We compare the proposed approach to traditional supervised finetuning by computing the number of design clusters when increasing the number of positive molecules.

Across all 20 tasks and the number of positive molecules studied, molecular task arithmetic surpassed the number of successful clusters obtained with finetuning, up to a 10K increase (Fig.
6
). A deeper look reveals that molecular task arithmetic can design at least as many successful designs as supervised finetuning (Fig.
A8
), and yet maintain the design diversity (Fig.
A9
). Overall, these experiments point to a synergy: whenever positive molecules are available, molecular task arithmetic can be combined with supervised finetuning to increase the number of diverse hits.

Molecular task arithmetic can reach maximum performance even with fewer than 50 positive molecules. The number of clusters gradually decreases as the model is finetuned with further molecules. Such performance fluctuations, however, have a smaller magnitude than vanilla finetuning, underlining the robustness of molecular task arithmetic. Taken together, the experiments highlight molecular task arithmetic as a data-efficient, robust, and well-performing transfer learning strategy for few-shot molecule design. Combined with its simplicity, molecular task arithmetic can potentially become the new standard for transfer learning in de novo design, by allowing one to leverage both positive and negative data simultaneously.

Related Work

Task arithmetic.

Discovering semantic directions in the model weight space has emerged as a new research direction that offers opportunities to edit model behavior with simple arithmetic operations
25
,
27
,
28
,
29
. Named as task arithmetic, this perspective has enabled higher performance with model merging
26
, in-context task learning
28
, and zero-shot information retrieval
27
. Its capability to recognize task directions from finetuning of opposite labels offers potential for molecular field, where negative samples are dominantly more available than positive ones. Here we explored this potential.

Molecule design.

Deep learning for de novo drug design has been an active field in the last decade

,
58
,
59
. Early approaches trained sequence models on string representations of molecules with a next-token prediction task, using language modeling
42
,
58
,
59
. Recent works used graph and 3D-based representations to represent molecules and graph neural networks and diffusion for learning
60
,
61
,
62
,
63
,
64
,
65
. While these approaches enable more fine-grained representation and high steerability in learning, decoder-only language models of molecular strings, termed chemical language models, remain popular to date due to their simplicity and performance
40
. Furthermore, one molecule can have multiple SMILES strings, which enables data augmentation for deep learning in low data
34
,

,

,

6
Limitations

Molecular task arithmetic relies on negative data. While negative molecules are abundant due to the characteristics of the chemical space, this still forms a limitation to applying molecular task arithmetic. Our experiments also revealed that the validity of generations drops with molecular task arithmetic. Smarter weight edits
29
and/or alternative architectures should be studied to minimize this side effect. Last, this study tested molecular task arithmetic only for de novo design using LSTMs on SMILES strings. Experiments on other molecular generation tasks with more architectures and representations are needed to determine the limits of molecular task arithmetic as a new transfer learning strategy for deep learning in chemistry.

In this study, molecular task arithmetic was tested on molecular properties that can be exactly calculated and that vary linearly. It is yet to be determined whether negating more complex and non necessarily linear properties (e.g., inactivity, in the case of ligand-protein interaction) would yield molecules with the desired properties (e.g., biological activity).

7
Conclusion

Drug discovery datasets are usually small and lack structural diversity. While deep generative modeling and transfer learning have already been transformative for drug design, they still must face this inherent challenge. This work proposed a remedy: molecular task arithmetic. Molecular task arithmetic is a new transfer learning strategy that leverages the diverse and abundant negative molecules. Our approach unlocks zero-shot ligand-based molecule design in single- and dual-objective settings – creating a new paradigm for drug discovery. Experiments across ten design tasks show that molecular task arithmetic – which uses
no positive examples
– can design more diverse hits that fulfill desirable properties than models trained solely on positive data. We show that molecular task arithmetic can also leverage the available positive molecules to further propel chemical space exploration – outperforming traditional transfer learning across all design tasks. Thanks to its simplicity and performance, molecular task arithmetic bears the potential to become a well-established transfer learning strategy for drug discovery, a field where negative data abound.

Appendix

Experimental settings

A chemical language model with an LSTM backbone is pretrained on a previously curated dataset of 1.5M drug-like molecules from ChEMBL
[
21
,
67
]
. 100 models were trained for either canonical or randomized SMILES strings, with random hyperparameters (Table
A1
). For each representation, the model that generates the highest number of unique and new designs was selected for the finetuning stage.

The finetuning sets were curated from a previous study
[
24
]
by ensuring that no finetuning molecule is present in the pretraining set. The thresholds for high and low values are computed by rounding the median of the molecular property values among the pretraining molecules (Table
A2
and Fig.
A1
). Five molecule sets are curated for each task (1024 train, 256 validation, 256 test molecules per split), where each molecule carries the task property. For models trained on randomized SMILES, 10-fold SMILES augmentation is used during finetuning. Early stopping on validation loss (cross-entropy) with a patience of five epochs and loss tolerance of
1\times 10^{-5}
is employed. 100,000 molecules were generated with each model.

Four metrics were computed to evaluate the quality of the de novo designs across the study:

•

Validity
: the percentage of ‘chemically valid’ molecules across designs. Chemical validity is checked by attempting to create a molecule object in
rdkit
, after filtering out empty string designs.

•

Success rate
: the frequency of distinct molecules outside the training sets that possess the task property. Molecules are deduplicated via canonicalizing the designs and keeping only one of the identical strings. Success rate evaluates the accuracy of the model (the higher, the better).

•

Number of clusters
: the number of clusters identified by the sphere exclusion algorithm. Tanimoto distance on extended connectivity fingerprints
[
68
]
is used with a threshold of 65% to identify the number of distinct clusters. The metric is linked to #Circles
[
69
]
, and the
rdkit
implementation is used. The higher the number of clusters, the more diverse the design library – a desired trait for drug discovery
[
70
]
.

•

Number of successful molecule clusters
: the number of cluster centers that possess the task property. This metric combines the evaluation of diversity and accuracy, and is used as the main evaluation metric across the study. Higher values are preferred.
