Graph Max Shift: A Hill-Climbing Method for Graph Clustering
Ery Arias-Castro, Elizabeth Coda, Wanli Qiao

Abstract
We present a method for graph clustering that is analogous with gradient ascent methods previously proposed for clustering points in space. We show that, when applied to a random geometric graph with data iid from some density with Morse regularity, the method is asymptotically consistent. Here, consistency is understood with respect to a density-level clustering defined by the partition of the support of the density induced by the basins of attraction of the density modes.



1
Introduction

A hill-climbing algorithm is typically understood as an algorithm that makes ‘local’ moves. In a sense, this class of procedures is the discrete analog of the class of gradient-based and higher-order methods in continuous optimization. Such algorithms have been proposed in the context of graph partitioning, sometimes as a refinement step, where the objective function is typically a notion of cut and local moves often take the form of swapping vertices in order to improve the value of the objective function.
More specifically, consider an undirected graph consisting of
n
nodes, which we take to be
[n]:=\{1,...,n\}
without loss of generality, and adjacency matrix
A=(a_{ij})
, so that
a_{ij}=1
when
i
and
j
are connected in the graph, and
a_{ij}=0
otherwise. Consider the simplest variant of graph partitioning, where assuming
n
is even, the goal is to bisect the node set into two subsets,
I\subset[n]
and
I^{\mathsf{c}}
, in order to minimize the cut:

{\rm cut}(I)=\sum_{i\in I}\sum_{j\in I^{\mathsf{c}}}a_{ij}.
Arguably the simplest hill-climbing procedure would start from some partition, perhaps a random one, and then iteratively swap the pair of vertices, one in each subset of the current partition, that makes the cut decrease the most, and stop when no swap makes the cut decrease.
Carson and
Impagliazzo 2001
propose such an algorithm, which although relatively simple, is shown to be optimal for partitioning a planted bisection up to a poly-logarithmic factor.
The procedure involves a careful initialization so that the hill-climbing part, meaning the swapping of pairs, may be seen as a refinement. This is also the case in other algorithms such as those of
[
17
,
9
,
23
]
. Sometimes the swapping is randomized
[
15
,
16
,
11
]
. Hill-climbing algorithms for graph partitioning date back at least to the work of
Kernighan and
Lin 1970
, who propose a more sophisticated swapping protocol than the one described above. The partition around medoids (PAM) algorithm of
Kaufman and
Rousseeuw 1987
includes a hill-climbing component.

In the present paper, we attach a totally different meaning to ‘hill-climbing’ that comes from the problem of clustering points in a Euclidean space. We adopt the definition of clustering proposed by
Fukunaga and
Hostetler 1975
. (We note that this definition has been shown in
[
3
,
4
]
to be intimately related to the level set definition of clustering proposed by
Hartigan 1975
.)
Assuming the points were generated iid from some density which is regular enough for the associated gradient ascent flow to be well-defined, the space, in fact the density support, is partitioned according to the basins of attraction of the density. The hill-climbing characterization comes from being a gradient method: we move each point in the density support upward, in the landscape given by the density, following the steepest ascent, and points that end at the same location are grouped together. Of course, in practice the density is unknown and one needs to find workarounds. The most famous one is the mean-shift algorithm
[
12
,
8
]
, although a number of other proposals have been suggested, including the most straightforward one which consists in first estimating the density, e.g., by kernel density estimation, and then computing the basin of attraction of the estimated density using a Euler scheme or similar — see
[
2
]
, where a number of these procedures are analyzed.

The bridge between the discrete setting of a graph and the continuous setting of a Euclidean space is the usual one: geometric graphs, meaning, graphs whose node set is embedded in a Euclidean space. Our focus will be random geometric graphs with nodes corresponding to points that are generated iid from a density with sufficient regularity, and edges connecting all pairs
\{y_{i},y_{j}\}
with
||y_{j}-y_{j}||\leq\epsilon
. The basic question that motivates the present work is the following:

Given a random geometric graph, is it possible to partition the graph in a way which is consistent with the clustering given by gradient ascent flow of the density?

We answer this question affirmatively in the following sense: We propose an algorithm for graph partitioning which, when applied to a random geometric graph with underlying points generated iid from a density with enough regularity, the output of the algorithm is essentially consistent with the partition given by the basins of attraction of the gradient ascent flow of the density in an asymptotic setting, driven by
n\to\infty
, where the connectivity radius of the graph tends to zero slowly enough. Importantly, the algorithm we propose does not attempt to embed the graph. Although a strategy that would consist in embedding the graph and then applying a method for gradient ascent flow clustering is tempting, the problem of embedding the graph is arguably harder than the partitioning problem that we consider here.

For more connections to the literature, we can single out the algorithm of
Koontz
et al. 1976
, which although proposed to cluster points in space, at its core is a hill-climbing graph partition algorithm close in intention to ours — but as pointed out in
[
2
]
, where it is called Max Slope Shift, the algorithm in its original form fails to achieve its purpose. Our algorithm can also be seen as a special case of a class of algorithms proposed by
Strazzeri and
Sánchez-García 2022
, although the intention there is very different. We elaborate on these works later on in Section
3.2
.

The rest of the article is organized as follows.
In Section
2
we provide some background on gradient ascent flow clustering.
In Section
3
we describe the algorithm we propose, which we call Graph Max Shift, make some connections to other algorithms, and establish its asymptotic consistency.
In Section
4
we present a series of numerical simulations demonstrating the use of the method. (Code is available to reproduce all experiments at
https://github.com/lizzycoda/GraphMaxShift
.)
We conclude with a brief discussion in Section
5
.

2
Preliminaries

In the gradient flow approach to clustering, which originated in the work of
Fukunaga and
Hostetler 1975
, each
x\in{\rm supp}(f)
is assigned to the limit point following along the direction of the gradient of
f
. Formally, assume that
\nabla f
is Lipschitz so that the gradient ascent flow exists. Then the gradient flow line originating at a point
x
is the curve
\gamma_{x}
defined by

\displaystyle\gamma_{x}(0)=x;\hskip 20.00003pt\dot{\gamma}_{x}(t)=\nabla f(\gamma_{x}(t)),\hskip 10.00002ptt\geq 0.

(2.1)

Then basin of attraction of a point
x^{*}
is
\{x:\gamma_{x}(\infty)=x^{*}\}
. Note that the basin of attraction of
x^{*}
is empty unless
x^{*}
is a critical point. It turns out that, if
f
is a Morse function
[
22
]
, meaning that it is twice differentiable and its Hessian is non-singular at every critical point, then the basins of attraction corresponding to local maxima, sometimes called modes, provide a partition of the support of
f
up to a set of zero measure.

A number of mode-seeking algorithms have been proposed to approximate the gradient flow lines
[
5
]
, some of them analyzed and proved to be consistent in
[
2
]
. In the same paper, another algorithm was proposed, called Max Shift, which consists in iteratively moving to the point with highest (estimated) density value.
With a search radius
r>0
, which is the only tuning parameter of the method, starting at a given point a sequence is constructed by iteratively including a point within distance
r
with highest density value: if the starting point is
x_{0}
, then Max Shift computes the sequence
(x_{k})
given by

\displaystyle x_{k+1}\in\argmax\limits_{x\in\bar{B}(x_{k},r)}f(x),\hskip 10.00002ptk\geq 0,

(2.2)

where
\bar{B}(y,r)
is the closed ball centered at
y
of radius
r
.
Ties are broken in some arbitrary but deterministic way. The algorithm is hill-climbing in the sense that at each step it ‘climbs’ the landscape given by the density. Max Shift clusters together points whose sequence have the same endpoint.

In practice, the density
f
is unknown and the straightforward strategy is to apply the procedure to an estimate of the density. In detail, given data points
\mathcal{Y}=\{y_{1},...,y_{n}\}
assumed to be iid from
f
, the Max Shift method utilizes a kernel density estimator
\hat{f}_{\epsilon}
with bandwidth
\epsilon
to estimate the density and proceeds as before but with
\hat{f}_{\epsilon}
in place of
f
. In the so-called medoid variant, only data points are considered, resulting in the following procedure: starting at some data point
y_{0}\in\mathcal{Y}
,

\displaystyle y_{k+1}\in\argmax\limits_{y\in\mathcal{Y}\cap\bar{B}(y_{k},r)}\hat{f}_{\epsilon}(y),\hskip 10.00002ptk\geq 0.

(2.3)

Max Shift and its medoid variant are shown in
[
2
]
to be asymptotically consistent — in the sense of
Fukunaga and
Hostetler 1975
— under some conditions on the underlying density
f
and on the kernel and bandwidth defining the density estimate. Specifically, it is assumed the density converges to zero at infinity; that it is twice differentiable with uniformly continuous zeroth, first, and second derivatives; and that it is of Morse regularity. And it is assumed that the kernel used is of second order and the bandwidth is chosen so that the density estimator is second order consistent, meaning that
\epsilon=\epsilon_{n}\to 0
in such a way that, as
n\to\infty
,

\begin{gathered}\sup_{x}|\hat{f}_{\epsilon}(x)-f(x)|\to 0,\qquad\sup_{x}\|\nabla\hat{f}_{\epsilon}(x)-\nabla f(x)\|\to 0,\qquad\sup_{x}\|\nabla^{2}\hat{f}_{\epsilon}(x)-\nabla^{2}f(x)\|\to 0.\end{gathered}

(2.4)

Our proposed algorithm for graph clustering, introduced later in Section
3
, coincides with Max Shift (based on a particular kernel density estimator) in the case of a geometric graph. However, the consistency of Max Shift established in
[
2
]
do not extend to the present setting, as the kernel function implicitly used does not satisfy the required conditions. We make this point again in Remark
3.2
.

3
Graph Max Shift

This is the section where we introduce our algorithm for graph partitioning that, when the graph under consideration happens to be a random geometric graph, implements a form of gradient ascent flow clustering of the underlying positions.

3.1
Description

We describe the graph clustering method for a general graph with
n
nodes, taken to be
[n]:=\{1,\dots,n\}
without loss of generality, and adjacency matrix
A=(a_{ij})
where, as usual,
a_{ij}=1
exactly when
i
and
j
are neighbors in the graph, and
=0
otherwise. We will use the convention that
a_{ii}=1
for all
i
, so that each node is a neighbor to itself. We will sometimes use the notation
i\leftrightarrow j
to indicate that
i
and
j
are neighbors, i.e.,
a_{ij}=1
.
The graph could be, but is not necessarily a geometric graph.
The degree function is defined as usual,
q_{i}=\#\{j:j\leftrightarrow i\}
.

Graph Max Shift, initialized at node
i
, computes the following sequence of nodes:

i_{0}=i;\hskip 20.00003pti_{t}\in\argmax\{q_{j}:j\leftrightarrow i_{t-1}\},\hskip 10.00002ptt\geq 1.

(3.1)

Ties are broken in some arbitrary, but deterministic way. We call the path
(i_{t})
the hill-climbing path originating from
i
where the landscape is given by the degree function
q
. In plain words, Graph Max Shift iteratively moves to neighbor with highest the degree.
We then cluster together nodes whose hill-climbing paths end at the same node. Finally, we merge together any two clusters whose associated nodes are within
\tau
hops, where
\tau\geq 1
is the only tuning parameter of the method.

3.2
Connections to other algorithms

3.2.1
Connection to Max Shift

Our claim is that Graph Max Shift implements gradient ascent flow clustering when applied to a geometric graph. Let
\mathcal{Y}:=\{y_{1},\dots,y_{n}\}
be a set of points in some Euclidean space, say,
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and consider the neighborhood graph with connectivity radius
\epsilon
, denoted
\mathcal{G}(\mathcal{Y};\epsilon)
, defined by the adjacency matrix
a_{ij}=1
if
\|y_{i}-y_{j}\|\leq\epsilon
, and
=0
otherwise.

It is straightforward to check that Graph Max Shift applied to
\mathcal{G}(\mathcal{Y};\epsilon)
will compute the same hill-climbing paths as Max Shift (
2.3
) applied to
\mathcal{Y}
when using the so-called flat kernel

K(x)=v_{d}^{-1}\mathbf{I}(\|x\|\leq 1),

(3.2)

where
v_{d}
is the volume of the unit ball in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and when the bandwidth and search radius are taken to be
\epsilon
.
Indeed, with that kernel function and bandwidth, the density estimator used by Max Shift is given by

\hat{f}_{\epsilon}(x)=\frac{1}{n\epsilon^{d}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}K\Big(\frac{x-y_{i}}{\epsilon}\Big)=\frac{\#\{i:\|x-y_{i}\|\leq\epsilon\}}{v_{d}n\epsilon^{d}},

(3.3)

so that, when applied to a data point,

\hat{f}_{\epsilon}(y_{i})=\frac{q_{i}}{v_{d}n\epsilon^{d}}\propto q_{i}.

Remark 3.1
.

We remark that, in the situation where the user is only provided with an adjacency matrix, which is the one we envision, when applying Graph Max Shift the user does not have a choice of kernel function or bandwidth.

3.2.2
Connection to Max Slope Shift

Koontz
et al. 1976
proposed an algorithm for clustering points in space, which is called Max Slope Shift in
[
2
]
for reasons that will become clear shortly. Given a point set
\mathcal{Y}:=\{y_{1},\dots,y_{n}\}
, it forms the weighted neighborhood graph with connectivity radius
\epsilon
, namely, the graph on
[n]
with dissimilarity between nodes
i,j\in[n]
given by
\delta_{ij}=\|y_{i}-y_{j}\|
if
\|y_{i}-y_{j}\|\leq\epsilon
, and
\delta_{ij}=\infty
otherwise.
Here
i
and
j
are considered neighbors if
\delta_{ij}<\infty
, and the degree function is defined as before,
q_{i}=\#\{j:j\leftrightarrow i\}
.
Then, starting at node
i
, Max Slope Shift computes the following sequence of nodes:

i_{0}=i;\hskip 20.00003pti_{t}\in\argmax\left\{\frac{q_{j}-q_{i}}{\|y_{i}-y_{j}\|}:j\leftrightarrow i_{t-1}\right\},\hskip 10.00002ptt\geq 1.

(3.4)

As argued in
[
2
]
, it is not hard to see that the end points will be local maxima not of the density itself, but of its gradient, meaning, density inflection points. The algorithm thus fails to output an accurate clustering as defined by
Fukunaga and
Hostetler 1975
. (A regularization is proposed in
[
2
]
to remedy this behavior.)

Max Slope Shift can be nonetheless seen to be closely related to ours. For one, it implies the use of the flat kernel for density estimation; and two, if one were to imagine how it would be adapted to clustering an unweighted graph, one would end up with Graph Max Shift, as the natural thing to do in that case is to replace the Euclidean distance in the denominator in the fraction appearing in (
3.4
) with 1.

3.2.3
Connection to Morse clustering

As we were preparing the manuscript, we realized that Graph Max Shift could be seen as a special case of a class of clustering algorithms called Morse Clustering recently proposed by
Strazzeri and
Sánchez-García 2022
. Although the class of algorithms bears the name of ‘Morse’, the motivation for their introduction is entirely different from ours.

A Morse Clustering algorithm is based on an edge preorder and a node preorder, which we denote by
\preceq_{E}
and
\preceq_{N}
, respectively. The choice of these preorders defines the algorithm. Indeed, starting from node
i
, if the path is at
i_{t-1}
at step
t-1
, the path ends there unless there is a node
j\leftrightarrow i
such that
(i,k)\preceq_{E}(i,j)
for all
k\leftrightarrow i
; and
i\preceq_{N}j
but
j\npreceq_{N}i
, in which case
i_{t}=j
and the path continues.
Nodes whose paths end at the same node are clustered together.

Graph Max Shift can be seen to belong in that class of algorithms. Assume that Graph Max Shift breaks ties between nodes by selecting the largest one when nodes are seen as an integers. Then define the node preorder
i\preceq_{N}j
if
i=j
, or
q_{i}<q_{j}
, or
q_{i}=q_{j}
and
i<j
; and define the edge preorder
(i,j)\preceq_{E}(i,k)
if
j\preceq_{N}k
. Then it is a matter of checking that the Morse Clustering algorithm with these two preorders coincides with Graph Max Shift.

Although our algorithm belongs to the class of Morse Clustering algorithms, our motivation and that of
Strazzeri and
Sánchez-García 2022
are very different. The impetus for proposing Morse Clustering algorithms is to address the impossibility result of
Kleinberg 2002
. In the context of weighted graphs, Kleinberg proposes three axioms that a clustering algorithm should satisfy,
scale-invariance
,
richness
, and
consistency
, and then proceeds to show that no algorithms satisfies all three.
Strazzeri and Sánchez-García offer some criticism of the consistency axiom, propose to replace it by a weaker axiom which they call
monotonic consistency
, and show that there is at least one Morse Clustering algorithm which satisfies this new axiom, and the other two axioms of scale-invariance and richness.

Due to the fact that the intention is very different to ours in the present work, it is not surprising that there is no analysis in
[
26
]
of asymptotic consistency in the sense considered here, i.e., the recovery of gradient ascent flow clustering.

3.3
Consistency

In this section, we establish the asymptotic (statistical) consistency of Graph Max Shift when applied to
\mathcal{G}(\mathcal{Y},\epsilon_{n})
, when
\mathcal{Y}:=\{y_{1},\dots,y_{n}\}
is generated iid from a density
f
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
which satisfies some regularity conditions, and when
\epsilon_{n}\to 0
slowly enough. In more detail, the reference partition is the one defined by
Fukunaga and
Hostetler 1975
, namely,
i
and
j
are in the same cluster if
y_{i}
and
y_{j}
belong to the basin of attraction of the same mode of
f
. We say that Graph Max Shift (or any other clustering algorithm) is consistent if the partition it outputs has Rand index
[
24
]
with respect to the reference partition tending to 1 in probability as
n\to\infty
, or more loosely, that the fraction of nodes that are incorrectly clustered converges to zero as the sample size increases without bound.

Remark 3.2
.

The consistency of Graph Max Shift is not obtained as a special case of the consistency results established in
[
2
]
, which crucially depend on the use of a second order kernel. The simplicity of Max Shift allows us to simplify the arguments in
[
2
]
(which were developed for generic hill-climbing methods) and, in particular, avoid the more complex aspects having to do with the stability of the basins of attraction to perturbation of the density.

Theorem 3.3
.

\mathcal{Y}:=\{y_{1},\dots,y_{n}\}
is generated iid from a density
f
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with compact support, twice continuously differentiable, and Morse. Then, with a large enough choice of tuning parameter
\tau
, Graph Max Shift applied to
\mathcal{G}(\mathcal{Y},\epsilon_{n})
is consistent when
\epsilon_{n}\to 0
, and
\epsilon_{n}^{\max\{d+4,2d\}}n/\log n\to\infty
.

The proof of Theorem
3.3
is in Section
6
. We note that the assumption of compact support is for simplicity, as the consistency result extends to densities that simply converge to zero at infinity as long as
f
and its first and second derivatives are bounded.

We note that in practice, the choice of tuning parameter
\tau=1
— meaning that clusters whose representative nodes are direct neighbors are merged — is often sufficient for good clustering performance, though without the merging post-processing step, it is possible that some of the population clusters defined by the density gradient ascent basins of attraction could be split into multiple clusters. However, without merging, Graph Max Shift applied to
\mathcal{G}(\mathcal{Y},\epsilon_{n})
is weakly consistent when
\epsilon_{n}\to 0
and
n\epsilon_{n}^{d+2}/\log n\to\infty
, in the sense that the fraction of pairs of points that belong to different population clusters but are clustered together by the algorithm tends to zero.

Numerical experiments

In this section, we demonstrate the behavior of Graph Max Shift on random geometric graphs with different underlying densities. The implementation is built upon the igraph package
[
10
]
. Python scripts to reproduce all figures in this section are available at
https://github.com/lizzycoda/GraphMaxShift
.

It should be noted that these numerical experiments are only meant to provide some confirmation for our theory. Graph Max Shift is not intended to improve the state-of-the-art, and in particular we did not compare with any other algorithm. (In fact, as we tried to make clear in the Introduction, we do not know of an algorithm that was proposed for the same purpose, so that there is no clear competitor.)

5
Discussion

5.1
Merging parameter

As we could not discard the presence of multiple points of local maximum degree in the vicinity of the same density mode (as specified by Lemma
6.8
), we opted for a post-processing step where clusters with nearby representative nodes are merged together. Even though less than elegant, this sort of step is not unusual in the literature. The choice of this tuning parameter is not straightforward, as is the case of most tuning parameters of clustering (and other unsupervised learning) methods, but is fairly intuitive here: set the parameter
\tau
to less than half the minimum distance between two cluster centers that the user wants to be able to distinguish. Side information specific to an application could provide some insight into that. (But, again, we do not want to emphasize the practical nature of the proposed method.)

5.2
Multi-hop variant

When Graph Max Shift as presented in Section
3
is applied to a geometric graph, the connectivity radius cannot be chosen — it is not a tuning parameter. One way to introduce some flexibility in the algorithm is to open the possibility of enlarging the search radius. In the context of an unweighted graph, the search radius is understood as the number of hops. With
m
denoting the number of hops defining the local search area, which is now a tuning parameter, Graph Max Shift, initialized at node
i
, computes the following sequence of nodes:

i_{0}=i;\hskip 20.00003pti_{t}\in\argmax\{q_{j}:j\leftrightarrow_{m}i_{t-1}\},\hskip 10.00002ptt\geq 1,

(5.1)

where
i\leftrightarrow_{m}j
if
i
and
j
are within
m
hops away, or said differently, if the graph distance separating them is bounded by
m
.

The consistency result established in Theorem
3.3
generalizes under some conditions on
\epsilon
and
m
in a straightforward manner. Choosing
m
is nontrivial, as is typically the case for the choice of tuning parameters in unsupervised learning. We also note that, in practice, unless
m
is quite small, the algorithm can be slow to run.

5.3
Weighted graphs

We have focused on unweighted graphs, but the algorithm can be easily adapted to work with weighted graphs. Consider a graph with
n
nodes, taken to be
[n]:=\{1,\dots,n\}
without loss of generality, and dissimilarity
\delta_{ij}
between nodes
i
and
j
, with
\delta_{ij}=\infty
if
i
and
j
are not neighbors. A straightforward way to apply Graph Max Shift to such a graph is to apply a threshold so that it becomes an unweighted graph. Specifically,
i
and
j
would be redefined as neighbors if
\delta_{ij}\leq h
, where
h
is now a tuning parameter. The multi-hop variant described in Section
5.2
is best implemented by defining the local search area around node
i
by
\{j:\delta_{ij}\leq r\}
, where
r\geq h
is another tuning parameter. The correspondence with the number of hops
m
introduced in Section
5.2
is roughly
r\approx mh
.

The situation most appropriate here would be a weighted geometric graph,
\mathcal{G}(\mathcal{Y},\epsilon)
, where the weights are given by
\delta_{ij}=\|y_{i}-y_{j}\|
if
\|y_{i}-y_{j}\|\leq\epsilon
and
=\infty
otherwise. The consistency result established in Theorem
3.3
generalizes in this setting under some conditions on
\epsilon
,
h
, and
r
, following the same line of arguments. Here too, choosing the tuning parameters
h
and
r
is challenging.

6
Proofs

As we noted earlier on the in paper, the arguments supporting Theorem
3.3
are similar to those establishing the consistency of Max Shift in
[
2
]
, but not exactly the same as the flat kernel (
3.2
) in use here is not twice differentiable or 2nd order, and the arguments in
[
2
]
rely crucially on these properties being satisfied by the kernel function used to produce a density estimate. The reason for that is that the proof structure is laid out in
[
2
]
to be applicable not just to Max Shift but to other methods such as Mean Shift. Because we focus on (Graph) Max Shift, we are able to tailor the arguments to the particular situation we are confronted with — and actually simplify them in the process.

6.1
Preliminaries

6.1.1
Density

Recall that the density
f
(on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
) is assumed to be compactly supported, twice continuously differentiable, and of Morse regularity.
Define

\kappa_{0}=\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(x),\hskip 20.00003pt\kappa_{1}=\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\|\nabla f(x)\|,\hskip 20.00003pt\kappa_{2}=\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\|\nabla^{2}f(x)\|,

(6.1)

which are well-defined and finite by the fact that
f
is assumed to be compactly supported and twice continuously differentiable.
Then, by Taylor expansion, the following inequalities hold for all
x,y
:

\displaystyle|f(x)-f(y)|\leq\kappa_{1}\|x-y\|;

(6.2)

\displaystyle\|\nabla f(x)-\nabla f(y)\|\leq\kappa_{2}\|x-y\|;

(6.3)

\displaystyle\big|f(y)-f(x)-\nabla f(x)^{\top}(y-x)\big|\leq\kappa_{2}\|x-y\|^{2}.

(6.4)

(In fact,
\kappa_{2}
above can be replaced by
\kappa_{2}/2
, but using this looser inequality avoids carrying the
1/2
factor around while it plays no essential role in our developments.)
Whenever
\nabla f(x)\neq 0
, we denote the normalized gradient by

N(x):=\frac{\nabla f(x)}{\|\nabla f(x)\|},

(6.5)

which is differentiable when well-defined, with derivative equal to

\displaystyle DN(x)=\frac{\nabla^{2}f(x)}{\|\nabla f(x)\|}-\frac{\nabla f(x)\nabla f(x)^{\top}\nabla^{2}f(x)}{\|\nabla f(x)\|^{2}}.

(6.6)

6.1.2
Upper level sets

The upper
s
-level set of
f
is defined as

\mathcal{U}_{s}=\{x:f(x)\geq s\}.

We let
\mathcal{C}_{s}(x)
refer to the connected component of
\mathcal{U}_{s}
containing
x
.

Lemma 6.1
.

Let
x^{*}
be a mode of
f
and let
s^{*}=f(x^{*})
. There exists
\lambda_{1}\geq\lambda_{0}>0
and
\delta>0
such that, for any
x\in\bar{B}(x^{*},\delta)
, the eigenvalues of
\nabla^{2}f(x)
belong to
[-\lambda_{1},-\lambda_{0}]
. Also, for any
s\in(s^{*}-\frac{1}{2}\lambda_{0}\delta^{2},s^{*})
,

\bar{B}\left(x^{*},\sqrt{2\lambda_{1}^{-1}(s^{*}-s)}\right)\subset\mathcal{C}_{s}(x^{*})\subset\bar{B}\left(x^{*},\sqrt{2\lambda_{0}^{-1}(s^{*}-s)}\right).

(6.7)

Proof.

The first part of the statement relies on the fact that
\nabla^{2}f(x^{*})
is negative definitive and that
\nabla^{2}f
is continuous. The second part is a restatement of Lemma 5.6 in
[
4
]
.
∎

For the kernel density estimator
\hat{f}=\hat{f}_{\epsilon}
, we will denote its upper
s
-level set and connected component containing
x
as
\hat{\mathcal{U}}_{s}
and
\hat{\mathcal{C}}_{s}(x)
, respectively, leaving
\epsilon
implicit whenever possible.

6.1.3
Approximations

Recalling that
\hat{f}
denotes the kernel density estimator that Graph Max Shift implicitly computes, which was defined in (
3.3
), set

\eta:=\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\big|\hat{f}(x)-f(x)\big|.

(6.8)

Lemma 6.2
.

For any mode
x^{*}
and any
s>0
,

\mathcal{C}_{s+\eta}(x^{*})\subset\hat{\mathcal{C}}_{s}(x^{*})\subset\mathcal{C}_{s-\eta}(x^{*}).

Proof.

For any
x
,
\hat{f}(x)-\eta\leq f(x)\leq\hat{f}(x)+\eta
, so that, for any
s>0
, if
f(x)\geq s+\eta
then
\hat{f}(x)\geq s
, and vice versa, if
\hat{f}(x)\geq s
then
f(x)\geq s-\eta
.
We deduce that
\mathcal{U}_{s+\eta}\subset\hat{\mathcal{U}}_{s}
and
\hat{\mathcal{U}}_{s}\subset\mathcal{U}_{s-\eta}
, and from that, we immediately get that any connected component of
\mathcal{U}_{s+\eta}
must be entirely within one of the connected components of
\hat{\mathcal{U}}_{s}
and that any connected component of
\hat{\mathcal{U}}_{s}
must be entirely within one of the connected components of
\mathcal{U}_{s-\eta}
. Therefore,
\mathcal{C}_{s+\eta}(x^{*})
is within a connected component of
\hat{\mathcal{U}}_{s}
, which must be
\hat{\mathcal{C}}_{s}(x^{*})
since this is the only one that contains
x^{*}
, and similarly,
\hat{\mathcal{C}}_{s}(x^{*})
is within a connected component of
\mathcal{U}_{s-\eta}
, which must be
\mathcal{C}_{s-\eta}(x^{*})
for the same reason.
∎

Lemma 6.3
.

In the present context, and with
\epsilon
as in Theorem
3.3
,

\eta=O_{P}\Big(\epsilon^{2}+\textstyle\sqrt{\log(n)/n\epsilon^{d}}\Big).

Throughout, we use the notation
x=y\pm\epsilon
when
\|x-y\|\leq\epsilon
.

Proof.

By the triangle inequality,

\eta\leq\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\big|\hat{f}(x)-\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}[\hat{f}(x)]\big|+\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\big|\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}[\hat{f}(x)]-f(x)\big|.

(6.9)

For the stochastic term on the right-hand side of (
6.9
), from the main result in
[
13
]
and how
\epsilon=\epsilon_{n}
is assumed to vary, we have that

\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\big|\hat{f}(x)-\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}[\hat{f}(x)]\big|=O_{P}\big(\log(1/\epsilon)/n\epsilon^{d}\big)^{1/2}.

(The big-O term depends on
f,d
, but not on
n
. Recall that
\epsilon
depends on
n
.)

For the deterministic (bias) term on the right-hand side of (
6.9
), take
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. By the definition of the kernel density estimator, and using (
6.4
) along the way,

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}[\hat{f}(x)]

\displaystyle=(v_{d}n\epsilon^{d})^{-1}n\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}(\|x-y_{1}\|\leq\epsilon)

(6.10)

\displaystyle=(v_{d}\epsilon^{d})^{-1}\int_{B(x,\epsilon)}f(y){\rm d}y

(6.11)

\displaystyle=(v_{d}\epsilon^{d})^{-1}\int_{B(x,\epsilon)}\Big(f(x)+\nabla f(x)^{\top}(y-x)\pm\kappa_{2}\|y-x\|^{2}\Big){\rm d}y

(6.12)

\displaystyle=(v_{d}\epsilon^{d})^{-1}\big(f(x)v_{d}\epsilon^{d}\pm\kappa_{2}\epsilon^{2}v_{d}\epsilon^{d}\big)

(6.13)

\displaystyle=f(x)+O(\epsilon^{2}).

(6.14)

We therefore have

\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\big|\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}[\hat{f}(x)]-f(x)\big|=O(\epsilon^{2}).

We conclude by combining the bounds on the stochastic and deterministic terms, together with the fact that
\log(1/\epsilon)=O(\log n)
when
\epsilon
is as in the statement of Theorem
3.3
.
∎

For
s>0
, set

\alpha_{s}:=\max_{x\in\mathcal{U}_{s}}\min_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}\|x-y\|=\max_{x\in\mathcal{U}_{s}}\min_{i\in[n]}\|x-y_{i}\|,

which quantifies how dense the sample
\mathcal{Y}
is in the set
\mathcal{U}_{s}
.
The following is standard. (See
[
2
, Lem 5.6]
for a detailed proof that follows the standard arguments and applies almost unchanged to the present context.)

Lemma 6.4
.

In the present context, for any
s>0
,

\alpha_{s}=O_{P}(\log(n)/n)^{1/d}.

We recall that the graph distance between two nodes is the minimum number of hops, or length of the shortest path, to travel between the two nodes.
Let
H_{ij}
denote the graph distance between nodes
i
and
j
. Under some circumstances, in the context of a geometric graph, the graph distance tracks the Euclidean distance. The following relies on a result established in
[
1
]
.

Lemma 6.5
.

For any
s>0
, there is a constant
C>0
such that, if
\alpha_{s/2}/\epsilon\leq 1/C
,

0\leq H_{ij}\epsilon-\|y_{i}-y_{j}\|\leq C(\alpha_{s/2}/\epsilon)\|y_{i}-y_{j}\|+\epsilon,

whenever
y_{i},y_{j}\in\mathcal{U}_{s}
.

Proof.

We note that the lower bound, which reads
\|y_{i}-y_{j}\|\leq H_{ij}\epsilon
is simply due to the fact that the straight line between
y_{i}
and
y_{j}
is the shortest curve between these points, and
H_{ij}\epsilon
is the length of the polygonal line embedding the shortest path in the graph between nodes
i
and
j
.

We therefore focus on establishing the upper bound.
Since
\mathcal{U}_{s}
is compact, it suffices to prove the result for
y_{i},y_{j}\in\mathcal{U}_{s}
that are close enough. Let
t>0
be the distance between
\mathcal{U}_{s}
and
\mathcal{U}_{s/2}^{\mathsf{c}}
, so that
\bar{B}(x,t)\subset\mathcal{U}_{s/2}
for any
x\in\mathcal{U}_{s}
. Now, consider
y_{i},y_{j}\in\mathcal{U}_{s}
such that
\|y_{i}-y_{j}\|\leq t
, so that
y_{j}\in\mathcal{S}:=\bar{B}(y_{i},t)
. The set
\mathcal{S}
is convex and, since
\mathcal{S}\subset\mathcal{U}_{s/2}
,

\max_{x\in\mathcal{S}}\min_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}\|x-y\|\leq\alpha_{s/2}.

Let
\tilde{H}_{ij}
denote the graph distance between nodes
i
and
j
within the subgraph defined by the nodes
k
such that
y_{k}\in\mathcal{S}
. In such circumstances, if
\alpha_{s/2}/\epsilon\leq 1/4
,
[
1
, Th 1]
yields

0\leq\tilde{H}_{ij}\epsilon-\|y_{i}-y_{j}\|\leq 4(\alpha_{s/2}/\epsilon)\|y_{i}-y_{j}\|+\epsilon.

And noting that
H_{ij}\leq\tilde{H}_{ij}
, we are able to conclude.
∎

6.2
Auxiliary results

The first few results are on the behavior of a typical hill-climbing sequence built by Graph Max Shift, namely, when started at
y_{0}\in\mathcal{Y}
,

\displaystyle y_{k+1}\in\argmax\limits_{y\in\mathcal{Y}\cap\bar{B}(y_{k},\epsilon)}\hat{f}_{\epsilon}(y),\hskip 10.00002ptk\geq 0.

(6.15)

Lemma 6.6
.

There is
C_{0}>0
such that, provided
\kappa_{1}\epsilon+2\eta\leq s/2
and
\alpha_{s/2}\leq\epsilon
, for the Graph Max Shift sequence
(y_{k})
initialized at any
y_{0}\in\mathcal{U}_{s}
,

\epsilon-C_{0}\frac{(\epsilon^{2}+\alpha_{s/2}+\eta)}{\|\nabla f(y_{k})\|}\leq\|y_{k+1}-y_{k}\|\leq\epsilon,\hskip 10.00002pt\forall k\geq 0.

(6.16)

Proof.

The upper bound is by construction of the sequence. The lower bound follows from applying the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality to the inner product in the left-hand side of (
6.28
) below. (Note that only the upper bound in (
6.16
) is used in the establishing of that inequality.)
∎

Lemma 6.7
.

Under the same setting as Lemma
6.6
,

y_{k+1}-y_{k}=\|y_{k+1}-y_{k}\|N(y_{k})\pm 2C_{0}\frac{\epsilon^{1/2}(\epsilon^{2}+\alpha_{s/2}+\eta)^{1/2}}{\|\nabla f(y_{k})\|^{1/2}},\hskip 10.00002pt\forall k\geq 0.

(6.17)

Proof.

Let
\alpha
be short for
\alpha_{s/2}
.
Let
y_{k+1}^{\prime}
be the closest point in
\mathcal{Y}
to
x_{k+1}^{\prime}:=y_{k}+(\epsilon-\alpha)N(y_{k})
and assume
\alpha\leq\epsilon
, so that
\|x_{k+1}^{\prime}-y_{k}\|=\epsilon-\alpha\leq\epsilon
.
We have

\displaystyle f(x_{k+1}^{\prime})

\displaystyle\geq f(y_{k})-\kappa_{1}\epsilon

(6.18)

\displaystyle\geq\hat{f}(y_{k})-\kappa_{1}\epsilon-\eta

(6.19)

\displaystyle\geq\hat{f}(y_{0})-\kappa_{1}\epsilon-\eta

(6.20)

\displaystyle\geq f(y_{0})-\kappa_{1}\epsilon-2\eta

(6.21)

\displaystyle\geq s-\kappa_{1}\epsilon-2\eta,

(6.22)

using (
6.2
), (
6.8
), the fact that
\hat{f}(y_{k})
is increasing by definition, and (
6.8
) again. Therefore, provided
\kappa_{1}\epsilon+2\eta\leq s/2
, we have
f(x_{k+1}^{\prime})\geq s/2
, so that
x_{k+1}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{U}_{s/2}
, and by definition of
\alpha
,
\|y_{k+1}^{\prime}-x_{k+1}^{\prime}\|\leq\alpha
. Therefore,

\|y_{k+1}^{\prime}-y_{k+1}\|\leq\|y_{k+1}^{\prime}-x_{k+1}^{\prime}\|+\|x_{k+1}^{\prime}-y_{k}\|\leq\epsilon-\alpha+\alpha\leq\epsilon,

by the triangle inequality, so that, by construction of
y_{k+1}
,

\hat{f}(y_{k+1})\geq\hat{f}(y_{k+1}^{\prime}).

Continuing, using (
6.8
), (
6.2
), and (
6.4
), we derive

\displaystyle\hat{f}(y_{k+1}^{\prime})

\displaystyle\geq f(y_{k+1}^{\prime})-\eta

(6.23)

\displaystyle\geq f(x_{k+1}^{\prime})-\kappa_{1}\alpha-\eta

(6.24)

\displaystyle\geq f(y_{k})+\nabla f(y_{k})^{\top}(x_{k+1}^{\prime}-y_{k})-\kappa_{2}\|x_{k+1}^{\prime}-y_{k}\|^{2}-\kappa_{1}\alpha-\eta

(6.25)

\displaystyle=f(y_{k})+\|\nabla f(y_{k})\|(\epsilon-\alpha)-\kappa_{2}(\epsilon-\alpha)^{2}-\kappa_{1}\alpha-\eta

(6.26)

\displaystyle\geq f(y_{k})+\|\nabla f(y_{k})\|\epsilon-\kappa_{2}\epsilon^{2}-2\kappa_{1}\alpha-\eta.

(6.27)

Similarly, in the other direction,

\hat{f}(y_{k+1})\leq f(y_{k+1})+\eta\leq f(y_{k})+\nabla f(y_{k})^{\top}(y_{k+1}-y_{k})+\kappa_{2}\epsilon^{2}+\eta.

Combining these gives

f(y_{k})+\|\nabla f(y_{k})\|\epsilon-\kappa_{2}\epsilon^{2}-2\kappa_{1}\alpha-\eta\leq f(y_{k})+\nabla f(y_{k})^{\top}(y_{k+1}-y_{k})+\kappa_{2}\epsilon^{2}+\eta,

which after some simplifications yields

N(y_{k})^{\top}(y_{k+1}-y_{k})\geq\epsilon-\frac{2\big(\kappa_{2}\epsilon^{2}+\kappa_{1}\alpha+\eta\big)}{\|\nabla f(y_{k})\|}.

(6.28)

Let
u_{k}:=y_{k+1}-y_{k}
and
\xi_{k}:=2(\kappa_{2}\epsilon^{2}+\kappa_{1}\alpha+\eta)/\|\nabla f(y_{k})\|
.
If
\epsilon-\xi_{k}<0
, we simply write

\displaystyle\big\|u_{k}-\|u_{k}\|N(y_{k})\big\|\leq 2\|u_{k}\|\leq 2\epsilon\leq 2(\epsilon\xi_{k})^{1/2}.

(6.29)

If
\epsilon-\xi_{k}\geq 0
, we may write

\displaystyle\big\|u_{k}-\|u_{k}\|N(y_{k})\big\|^{2}

\displaystyle=2\|u_{k}\|^{2}-2\|u_{k}\|N(y_{k})^{\top}u_{k}

(6.30)

\displaystyle\leq 2\|u_{k}\|^{2}-2\|u_{k}\|\left(\epsilon-\xi_{k}\right)

(6.31)

\displaystyle\leq 2\epsilon^{2}-2\epsilon\left(\epsilon-\xi_{k}\right)

(6.32)

\displaystyle=2\epsilon\xi_{k}.

(6.33)

Either way, (
6.17
) holds.
∎

Lemma 6.8
.

For any
s>0
, there is
\delta>0
and
C_{1}>0
such that, if
\epsilon,\eta\leq 1/C_{1}
, for any mode
x^{*}\in\mathcal{U}_{s}
, the Graph Max Shift sequence initialized at a point
y_{0}\in\mathcal{Y}\cap B(x^{*},\delta)
converges to a point in
\mathcal{Y}
within distance
C_{1}(\epsilon+(\alpha_{s/2}+\eta)/\epsilon)
of
x^{*}
.

Proof.

Because there are finitely many modes in
\mathcal{U}_{s}
, it is enough to consider a single mode
x^{*}\in\mathcal{U}_{s}
. For that mode, let
s^{*}:=f(x^{*})
, so that
s^{*}\geq s
, and let
\delta_{1},\lambda_{0},\lambda_{1}
be as defined in Lemma
6.1
. Then, by a Taylor development of
\nabla f
,

\displaystyle\|\nabla f(x)\|\geq\lambda_{0}\|x-x^{*}\|,\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for any $x\in B(x^{*},\delta_{1})$.}

(6.34)

Let
s_{1}:=s^{*}-\frac{1}{2}\lambda_{0}\delta_{1}^{2}
and fix any
s_{2}\in(s_{1},s^{*})
, for example,
s_{2}=\frac{1}{2}(s_{1}+s^{*})
. Provided
\eta<s_{2}-s_{1}
, invoking Lemma
6.1
, we have

\mathcal{C}_{s_{2}-\eta}(x^{*})\subset B(x^{*},\delta_{1}).

Similarly, by the same result, letting
\delta_{2}:=\sqrt{(s^{*}-s_{2})/\lambda_{1}}
, provided
\eta<\frac{1}{2}(s^{*}-s_{2})
, we also have

B(x^{*},\delta_{2})\subset\mathcal{C}_{s_{2}+\eta}(x^{*}).

Therefore, if
\eta<\min\big\{s_{2}-s_{1},\frac{1}{2}(s^{*}-s_{2})\big\}
, using Lemma
6.2
,

B(x^{*},\delta_{2})\subset\mathcal{C}_{s_{2}+\eta}(x^{*})\subset\hat{\mathcal{C}}_{s_{2}}(x^{*})\subset\mathcal{C}_{s_{2}-\eta}(x^{*})\subset B(x^{*},\delta_{1}).

(6.35)

Since we are assuming that
\eta<s_{2}-s_{1}
, it is the case that
s_{2}-\eta>s_{1}
, so that
\mathcal{C}_{s_{2}-\eta}(x^{*})\subset\mathcal{C}_{s_{1}}(x^{*})
. Assume that
\epsilon
is strictly smaller than the minimum separation between the connected components of
\mathcal{U}_{s_{1}}
and consider Graph Max Shift initialized at some
y_{0}\in B\left(x^{*},\delta_{2}\right)
. Note that
y_{0}\in\hat{\mathcal{C}}_{s_{2}}(x^{*})
, and by the fact that
(y_{k})
is hill-climbing with respect to
\hat{f}
, it must be the case that
(y_{k})\subset\hat{\mathcal{U}}_{s_{2}}
. In fact, since by Lemma
6.2
each connected component of
\hat{\mathcal{U}}_{s_{2}}
must be within a connected component of
\mathcal{U}_{s_{2}-\eta}
, and since
s_{2}-\eta>s_{1}
, each connected component of
\mathcal{U}_{s_{2}-\eta}
must be within a connected component of
\mathcal{U}_{s_{1}}
, and these are separated by a distance exceeding
\epsilon
, and since the increments in
(y_{k})
are of length bounded from above by
\epsilon
by Lemma
6.6
, it must be the case that
(y_{k})\subset\hat{\mathcal{C}}_{s_{2}}(x^{*})
, that is, the sequence must remain in the same connected component of
\hat{\mathcal{U}}_{s_{2}}
.
Continuing, since there are a finite number of points in
\mathcal{Y}
, the sequence
(y_{k})
must converge in a finite number of steps. Let
y_{\infty}
denote the limit, which by the fact that
(y_{k})\subset\hat{\mathcal{C}}_{s_{2}}(x^{*})
and (
6.35
) above must be in
B(x^{*},\delta_{1})
. Therefore, due to (
6.34
),

\displaystyle\|\nabla f(y_{\infty})\|\geq\lambda_{0}\|y_{\infty}-x^{*}\|.

(6.36)

We now take
s_{1}
large enough that
s_{1}\geq s^{*}/2
, and we let
\alpha
be short for
\alpha_{s/2}
.
Define
z=y_{\infty}+(\epsilon-\alpha)N(y_{\infty})
, and let
y
be the closest point in
\mathcal{Y}
to
z
.
By (
6.2
) and the fact that
y_{\infty}\in\mathcal{C}_{s_{2}-\eta}
,

\displaystyle f(z)\geq f(y_{\infty})-\kappa_{1}\epsilon\geq s_{2}-\eta-\kappa_{1}\epsilon,

(6.37)

so that
f(z)\geq s_{1}
if
\epsilon
and
\eta
are small enough that
\kappa_{1}\epsilon+\eta\leq s_{2}-s_{1}
, which we assumed henceforth. Because of that,
\|y-z\|\leq\alpha
, implying via the triangle inequality that
\|y-y_{\infty}\|\leq\epsilon
, making
y
a fair search point when the sequence is at
y_{\infty}
, and for that reason, it must be the case that
\hat{f}(y_{\infty})\geq\hat{f}(y)
.
Assuming that
\epsilon>\alpha
, we then derive

\displaystyle 0\geq\hat{f}(y)-\hat{f}(y_{\infty})

\displaystyle\geq f(y)-f(y_{\infty})-2\eta

(6.38)

\displaystyle\geq f(z)-f(y_{\infty})-\kappa_{1}\alpha-2\eta

(6.39)

\displaystyle\geq\nabla f(y_{\infty})^{\top}(z-y_{\infty})-\kappa_{2}\|z-y_{\infty}\|^{2}-\kappa_{1}\alpha-2\eta

(6.40)

\displaystyle=\|\nabla f(y_{\infty})\|(\epsilon-\alpha)-\kappa_{2}(\epsilon-\alpha)^{2}-\kappa_{1}\alpha-2\eta

(6.41)

\displaystyle\geq\|\nabla f(y_{\infty})\|\epsilon-\kappa_{2}\epsilon^{2}-2\alpha\kappa_{1}-2\eta.

(6.42)

Combining this with (
6.36
), we conclude that

\|y_{\infty}-x^{*}\|\leq\frac{\kappa_{2}\epsilon^{2}+2\alpha\kappa_{1}+2\eta}{\lambda_{0}\epsilon},

which establishes the claim.
∎

6.3
Proof of Theorem
3.3

It is enough to show that, for any
p>0
, there is
s>0
and a subset
\Omega\subset\mathcal{U}_{s}
with
\int_{\Omega}f\geq 1-p
such that, with probability at least
1-p
under the conditions of the theorem, any data point in
\Omega
that is in the basin of attraction of a mode is moved by Graph Max Shift to a point within distance
\frac{1}{3}\tau\epsilon
of that mode.
For that to work, we assume that
\alpha_{s/2}/\epsilon
is sufficiently small that
\alpha_{s/2}/\epsilon<1/2C
where
C
is the constant of Lemma
6.5
, which is implied by our assumptions (see further down).
In that case, with probability at least
1-p
, if we take two data points in
\Omega
(representing an expected proportion of
(1-p)^{2}
of all pairs of data points), if they are in the basin of attraction of the same mode, they will be moved by Graph Max Shift to points within distance
\frac{2}{3}\tau\epsilon
, so that the graph distance between the corresponding nodes is bounded by
(1+C\alpha_{s/2}/\epsilon)\frac{2}{3}\tau\epsilon+1<\tau+1
by Lemma
6.5
, implying a number of hops
\leq\tau
, and therefore clustered together; and if they are in different basins of attraction, assuming that
\tau\epsilon
is smaller than half the minimum separation between any two modes in
\mathcal{U}_{s}
, they will be moved by Graph Max Shift to points within distance
\frac{1}{3}\tau\epsilon
of their associated modes, so that their distance will exceed
2\tau\epsilon-\frac{2}{3}\tau\epsilon>\tau\epsilon
, implying that the corresponding clusters will not be merged. (Note that, regardless of
s>0
, there are only finitely many modes within
\mathcal{U}_{s}
.)

Therefore, fix
p>0
, let
s>0
and
\Omega\subset\mathcal{U}_{s}
be as in Lemma
6.11
. Let
\alpha
be short for
\alpha_{s/2}
.
For
\epsilon\leq q\leq 1
a constant to be chosen small later on, consider the event

q\epsilon\geq\max\big\{\alpha,\eta\big\}.

(6.46)

This event happens with probability tending to 1 because of the last condition on
\epsilon
in the statement of the theorem, as well as Lemma
6.3
and Lemma
6.4
, which give the order of magnitude of
\eta
and
\alpha
.
We assume that
\epsilon
is small enough that all the lemmas in Section
6.2
, called upon below, apply and that (
6.46
) occurs with probability at least
1-p
.

Note that we have already imposed a requirement on
q
above, that
q<1/2C
, where
C
is the constant of Lemma
6.5
, so that
\alpha/\epsilon<1/2C
. Below, each time there is a constraint on
q
, we assume it is small enough to satisfy the constraint. We just have to make sure that each constraint allows for
q>0
fixed and that there are only a finite (in fact, a few) constraints.

Taking any data point
y_{0}\in\mathcal{Y}\cap\Omega
as a starting point, let
(y_{k})
denote the sequence computed by Graph Max Shift based on
\hat{f}
as defined in (
6.15
); let
(x_{k})
denote the sequence computed by Max Shift based on
f
as defined in (
6.43
) (with

).
We note that, combining (
6.16
) and (
6.17
), and under (
6.46
), we have

\displaystyle y_{k+1}-y_{k}

\displaystyle=\epsilon N(y_{k})\pm C_{0}\frac{\epsilon^{2}+\alpha+\eta}{\|\nabla f(y_{k})\|}\pm 2C_{0}\frac{\epsilon^{1/2}(\epsilon^{2}+\alpha+\eta)^{1/2}}{\|\nabla f(y_{k})\|^{1/2}}

(6.47)

\displaystyle=\epsilon N(y_{k})\pm C^{\prime}_{0}\frac{q^{1/2}\epsilon}{\|\nabla f(y_{k})\|},

(6.48)

using the fact that
\|\nabla f(y_{k})\|\leq\kappa_{1}
.
Similarly, combining (
6.44
) and (
6.45
), we have

\displaystyle x_{k+1}-x_{k}

\displaystyle=\epsilon N(x_{k})\pm C^{\prime}_{0}\frac{q^{1/2}\epsilon}{\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|}.

(6.49)

The inequalities above are valid for all
k\geq 0
.

Let
x^{*}
be the mode to which
y_{0}
is attracted by gradient ascent. Let
\delta
be as in Lemma
6.8
, and suppose we are able to show that

y_{k}\in\bar{B}(x^{*},\delta)
for some
k
.

(6.50)

Then Lemma
6.8
tells us that
(y_{k})
will end at some point within distance
C_{1}(\epsilon+(\alpha+\eta)/\epsilon)
of
x^{*}
. Based on our assumptions on
\tau
and
\epsilon
in the statement of the theorem, this is bounded by
\frac{1}{3}\tau\epsilon
eventually in the limit
n\to\infty
. Therefore, it suffices to show that (
6.50
) happens under (
6.46
) when
q
is small enough.
Assuming that
x_{0}=y_{0}\notin B(x^{*},\delta/2)
, for otherwise our job is done, let
k_{\#}
be such that
x_{k_{\#}}\in B(x^{*},\delta/2)
while
x_{k}\notin B(x^{*},\delta/2)
for
k<k_{\#}
, which is well-defined by Lemma
6.11
, which also tells us that
k_{\#}\leq C_{2}/\epsilon
. By the triangle inequality, to establish (
6.50
) it is enough to show that

\|x_{k_{\#}}-y_{k_{\#}}\|\leq\delta/2.

(6.51)

Define
C^{\prime}_{1}=2C_{2}C^{\prime}_{0}
and
C^{\prime}_{2}=(2C_{2}+1)\kappa_{2}
, and assume
q
is small enough that

q^{1/2}\big[2C^{\prime}_{1}{C^{\prime}_{2}}^{-1}(\exp(C^{\prime}_{2}C_{2})-1)\big]\leq\frac{1}{2C_{2}\kappa_{2}}.

We show by recursion that

\|x_{k}-y_{k}\|\leq q^{1/2}[2C^{\prime}_{1}{C^{\prime}_{2}}^{-1}](\exp(C^{\prime}_{2}\epsilon k)-1),

(6.52)

for all
k\leq k_{\#}
.
By the fact that
k_{\#}\leq C_{2}/\epsilon
, when the inequality (
6.52
) is satisfied, we also have

\|x_{k}-y_{k}\|\leq\frac{1}{2C_{2}\kappa_{2}}.

(6.53)

We start by verifying that (
6.52
) is true at
k=0
since
x_{0}=y_{0}
.
Assume it is true at some
k<k_{\#}
, so that (
6.53
) is also true, and by Lemma
6.11

\|\nabla f(y_{k})\|\geq\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|-\kappa_{2}\|x_{k}-y_{k}\|\geq\frac{1}{C_{2}}-\kappa_{2}\frac{1}{2C_{2}\kappa_{2}}=\frac{1}{2C_{2}}.

Combining this with (
6.48

\displaystyle y_{k+1}-y_{k}

\displaystyle=\epsilon N(y_{k})\pm C^{\prime}_{1}q^{1/2}\epsilon;

(6.54)

\displaystyle x_{k+1}-x_{k}

\displaystyle=\epsilon N(x_{k})\pm C^{\prime}_{1}q^{1/2}\epsilon.

(6.55)

We then have, by the triangle inequality,

\displaystyle\|x_{k+1}-y_{k+1}\|

\displaystyle\leq\|x_{k}-y_{k}\|+\epsilon\|N(x_{k})-N(y_{k})\|+2C^{\prime}_{1}q^{1/2}\epsilon.

(6.56)

In view of (

for all

and

\|N(x_{k})-N(y_{k})\|\leq C^{\prime}_{2}\|x_{k}-y_{k}\|,

implying that

\displaystyle\|x_{k+1}-y_{k+1}\|

\displaystyle\leq(1+C^{\prime}_{2}\epsilon)\|x_{k}-y_{k}\|+2C^{\prime}_{1}q^{1/2}\epsilon.

(6.57)

Using the recursion hypothesis (
6.52

e^{a}-1-a\geq 0

\|x_{k+1}-y_{k+1}\|\leq q^{1/2}[2C^{\prime}_{1}{C^{\prime}_{2}}^{-1}](\exp(C^{\prime}_{2}\epsilon(k+1))-1),

(6.58)

so that the recursion carries on.

In particular, we have established that

\|x_{k_{\#}}-y_{k_{\#}}\|\leq q^{1/2}[2C^{\prime}_{1}{C^{\prime}_{2}}^{-1}](\exp(C^{\prime}_{2}C_{2})-1),

(6.59)

and taking

, which is what we stated in (
