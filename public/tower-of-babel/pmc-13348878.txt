Perceptions, challenges, and innovations in educational rehabilitation for students with intellectual disabilities in Saudi Arabia
Authors: Mubarak S Aldosari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study evaluated stakeholder perceptions, implementation barriers, and innovation readiness in educational rehabilitation programs for students with intellectual disabilities in Saudi Arabia. A quantitative cross-sectional design was employed using a structured 30-item questionnaire administered to 298 participants, including educators, administrators, students, and rehabilitation-related stakeholders. The instrument comprised three domains: Awareness and Perception, Barriers to Effective Educational Rehabilitation, and Innovations and Future Directions. Internal consistency was strong across all domains, with Cronbach’s alpha values of 0.856, 0.849, and 0.836, respectively. Descriptive statistics, independent-samples t-tests, and one-way ANOVA were used to examine response patterns and group differences. The findings revealed statistically significant variation among stakeholder groups, indicating differences in awareness, confidence, and perceived implementation capacity. Administrators reported higher program awareness, whereas educators identified practical constraints related to teacher preparation, resource availability, assistive technology access, and classroom-level implementation. Social stigma, financial limitations, weak school–rehabilitation collaboration, and limited family engagement emerged as major barriers. Although participants recognised the potential of personalised learning plans, assistive technologies, virtual learning environments, augmented reality, educational applications, job-readiness training, and artificial intelligence-supported learning, lower innovation-domain mean scores indicated limited implementation readiness. The findings suggest that effective rehabilitation requires integrated policy implementation, sustained professional development, equitable resource allocation, technical infrastructure, culturally appropriate Arabic-language digital resources, and stronger inter-sectoral collaboration. Introduction

Learning disability is part of intellectual disabilities (ID), characterised by impaired cognitive functioning, memory, and social skills, typically with an IQ below 70 and limitations in adaptive behaviour [5]. These conditions range from mild to profound, requiring varying levels of support. In Saudi Arabia, the prevalence is estimated at 1%–3%, though likely underestimated due to diagnostic challenges and limited data, especially in underserved areas,increasing awareness has led to improvements in diagnosis and healthcare services. Education is a fundamental right for individuals with intellectual disabilities (IDs) and is essential for achieving independence, social inclusion, and an improved quality of life. It goes beyond academic learning by fostering key life skills such as communication, social interaction, problem-solving, and vocational abilities needed for employment and daily functioning [41, 50]. Moreover, inclusive education helps reduce stigma and discrimination, promotes equal opportunities, and enables students to interact meaningfully with peers, teachers, and the wider community, highlighting the importance of identifying and supporting their individual needs [14, 53].

Educational rehabilitation for learners with IDs entails intervention services and support to ensure they are fully supported and optimised in their learning styles in class and other aspects of their lives [8, 61]. This rehabilitation process usually involves IEPs, teaching methods, technology aids, speech therapists, and psychological support [21, 22]. The main goal of educational rehabilitation is to bring the child to the level that society expects them to be in terms of learning capacity [42]. Since the educational process is individualised to reconstruct the skills and abilities of every learner, educational rehabilitation guarantees these students all the chances to succeed at school [40]. Besides, this process also becomes an academic skill, a social skill, an emotional regulation skill, and an independent living skill for those students, which is also essential for their future survival [20].

Educational rehabilitation for students with IDs in Saudi Arabia has not lain entirely without several challenges [3]. The lack of resources, specifically teacher training, is still one of the biggest impediments that learners face [11]. Due to the scarcity of expertise among teachers in teaching students with learning disabilities, there are ensuing deficits in the quality of education [31]. Moreover, the required specialized equipment, cf. occupational therapy equipment, specialized facilities, and assistive technologies for disabled students are scarce in schools and more or less inaccessible to rural disabled students, raising questions on quality education for intellectually disabled students [65]. Students with ID also face cultural stigma and societal misconceptions about ID, hence, limited educational chances for these students [12, 28, 32]. The failure of the U.S. schools compounds these problems, and the health care delivery system and social service agencies need to be a connected system of support and intervention [46]. Thus, most students with ID do not attend special schools to receive desirable individual focus, promoting learner-low productivity, social exclusion, and restricted life chances for students and families [23]. Since the above gaps are the primary considerations of this study, their adverse effects are severe, as they impede the advancement of a fair learning environment for all learners [71].

Recent studies in Saudi Arabia are preparing educational rehabilitation for students with IDs, and a few research articles focus on this issue [7, 9]. Much of the literature research concerns general provisions for special or inclusive education [26, 52]. It does not include a specific assessment of how to apply these provisions to students with IDs [44]. Furthermore, impact studies are scarce in investigating the effectiveness of education intervention over the best years, especially concerning academic and social outputs [49]. The current literature also has a bias towards studies that explore the experiences of students with IDs in the urban context, not the rural and remote zones [16]. Thus, increased research on the dissemination of EBPs and interventions for this population is necessary [66]. The research to be conducted includes identifying how technology affects instruction, teacher preparation, and the incorporation of culture into instruction.

Exposing the existing deficits in educational rehabilitation calls for ideas that are relevant and compatible with students with IDs learning abilities [48]. Due to technological developments, Augmented Reality (AR), Virtual Reality (VR), and specialized assistive devices are emerging as promising approaches for individualized learning [19]. These technologies can enhance the quality of learning experiences, making them better, more appealing to students, and more effective, while enabling students to understand them better [17]. Also, changes in how incentives for teachers and professionals are established through teacher training and professional development, as well as through online forums and inclusive Practices, are crucial to preparing teachers to serve students with disabilities. In addition, school, health service, and family involvement are brought into direct focus for community-based interventions as Part of educational rehabilitation solutions [43]. When applied in the educational system, these strategies help foster teaching and learning for students with IDs in a more inclusive environment [27].

This study aims to identify the roles of educational rehabilitation for learners with IDs in Saudi Arabia and the impact of interventions used for learning, such as technology, classroom inclusion, and community learning. This paper seeks to establish the challenges to learning faced by students with IDs and to explore ways to address them. This research is helpful, as the information gathered in this study will augment the existing knowledge base on educational rehabilitation, especially in Saudi Arabia, and educate educators, policymakers, and service providers about the most suitable strategies for supporting students with IDs. In addition, the study will focus on incorporating technological advancements that are likely to enhance these students’ learning experiences. The results will inform the development of policies and programs that should direct students toward integration in school and guarantee equal educational rights for learners with disabilities. The population of interest for this research includes the areas specific to Saudi Arabia, primary and post-secondary education, with an emphasis on urban and rural schools offering Special education services for students. A possible source of bias may be the nature, availability, and willingness of schools or families to participate, particularly in rural settings with low special education enrolment. However, limitations inherent in cross-cultural and cross-language research may hamper the study’s ability to obtain data and, even when collected, to interpret them adequately.

Materials and methods

Study design

The present research adopted a cross-sectional survey research design based on a quantitative research design to analyze the perceptions, challenges, and innovation-related attitudes towards educational rehabilitation of students with IDs in Saudi Arabia. This design was chosen due to the fact that it was possible to gather data at a single moment in time relating to the various stakeholder groups and make statistical comparisons on their opinions along important areas such as awareness, perceived barriers, and future directions of educational rehabilitation [30, 47].

Study setting and participants

The research was carried out in Saudi Arabia among the respondents who had a first-hand experience with educational rehabilitation and special education services to students with IDs. The ultimate Sample comprised 298 individuals that included educators, administrators, students, and other stakeholders related to rehabilitation. Teachers were involved due to their direct influence in terms of teaching and assisting intellectually challenged students, and administrators were involved due to their role in the planning, monitoring, and coordination of special education services. Student respondents were chosen because they were the group that had a direct impact on the educational rehabilitation programs, and other stakeholders were incorporated to ensure that broader views were captured in terms of family and rehabilitation provision.

Sampling strategy

A purposive convenience strategy was employed to recruit the participants. Purposive sampling was employed as the participants needed to possess pertinent knowledge or first-hand experience on educational rehabilitation among students with IDs, whereas convenience sampling was employed since the participants were identified as people working in or enrolled in available schools, special education, rehabilitation-related centres, and professional circles in Saudi Arabia [51, 57]. This method is ideal in situations where the research needs data from participants who are specifically related to the research topic [34, 56].

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The participants were selected based on the fact that they were directly linked to the educational rehabilitation, special education, or student support services of intellectually disabled students. Teachers had to have teaching or rehabilitation experience, administrators had to have supervisory or coordination experience, and students had to have an identified intellectual disability and be enrolled in a special education or rehabilitation-related program. The participants who lacked direct contact with the educational rehabilitation or special education were eliminated, and questionnaires with significant gaps in the information were not factored into the final analysis.

Study instrument

A structured 30-item questionnaire was used to gather the information, which was broken down into three domains: awareness and perception of educational rehabilitation programs, barriers to effective educational rehabilitation, and innovations and future directions in educational rehabilitation. The awareness, perceived effectiveness, teacher confidence, resource availability, family involvement, and inter-sectoral collaboration were measured in the first domain. The second area evaluated obstacles, including untrained teachers, financial issues, access, cultural beliefs, limited technology, social stigma, and policy loopholes. The third domain evaluated the practices associated with the future, such as customized learning plans, assistive technologies, virtual learning environments, the Universal Design of Learning, instructional applications, parent-support programs, job-readiness training, and artificial intelligence-aided rehabilitation. In educational research, Likert-type scales are popular in the measurement of perceptions and attitudes [2, 58].

Data collection procedure

The questionnaire was sent out electronically and in hardcopy forms, depending on the accessibility of the participant and institutional setting. Participants received information regarding the purpose of the study, voluntary participation, confidentiality, and the right to withdraw before filling out the questionnaire [67, 70]. Participation was done with informed consent. In the case of student participants who needed assistance, caregivers, teachers, or trained personnel helped them interpret the questions on the questionnaire, but these factors did not affect their answers. All the answers were coded and processed in the aggregate.

Scoring and interpretation

A five-point Likert scale was used to record the responses. The following thresholds were used to interpret the mean scores: 1.00–2.49 represented low perception or support, 2.50- 3.49 represented moderate perception or support, and 3.50–5.00 represented high perception or support. These were used throughout the analysis. The measurement of some items is based on barriers and others on support of programs or innovation, so each item was considered based on its wording and direction.

Instrument reliability

Cronbach’s alpha was applied to measure the internal consistency of the questionnaire, and it is extensively applied to assess whether the items in a scale are measuring the same underlying construct [35, 69]. The Awareness and Perception scale demonstrated high reliability with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.856, the Barriers scale demonstrated high reliability with a value of 0.849, and the innovations and Future Directions scale demonstrated acceptable to high reliability with a value of 0.836. The questionnaire was deemed reliable to the study since all of the values were greater than the generally accepted criterion of 0.70 [18, 33].

Statistical analysis

Descriptive and inferential statistics were used to analyse the data. Demographic characteristics and responses to questionnaires were summarised using frequencies, percentages, cumulative percentages, means, and standard deviations. Cronbach’s alpha was used to determine internal consistency. There were chi-square goodness-of-fit tests on whether the demographic category distributions were significantly different from equal distribution, and Cohen’s w was given as an effect-size estimate. One-sample t-tests were applied to determine whether the mean responses on an item level differed significantly from the test value, and one-way ANOVA was applied to determine differences between several groups of participants [29, 55]. The statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05, and p-values of 0.000 were considered as p < 0.001.

Ethical considerations

The Standing Committee of Bioethics Research at Prince Sattam bin Abdulaziz University granted the ethical approval. The final project title was: Perceptions, Challenges, and Innovations in Educational Rehabilitation of Intellectual Disability Students in Saudi Arabia, protocol number SCBR-619/2025. The approval date was 07.01.2026, and the approval remains valid until 06.01.2027. The method was voluntary participation, informed consent to participate, and all data were anonymized prior to the analysis. The manuscript did not contain any personal information, clinical data, or visual aids that could be identified.

Results

The results are presented according to the reliability of the survey instrument, demographic characteristics of the respondents, participant response patterns, and inferential statistical analysis. The questionnaire assessed three domains: awareness and perception of educational rehabilitation programs, barriers to effective educational rehabilitation, and innovations and future directions in educational rehabilitation. Findings showed strong internal consistency across the three scales, variation in participant perceptions across demographic and stakeholder groups, high perceived barriers in several areas, and low current readiness for innovation-based implementation, as detailed in supplementary file 1.

Study reliability and consistency of educational rehabilitation scales

Cronbach’s alpha was used to test the internal consistency of the survey instrument. The three domains of the questionnaires were analyzed independently and included 10 items each. The Awareness and Perception of Educational Rehabilitation Programs scale was found to be highly internally consistent, with a Cronbach alpha of 0.856. This means that the constructs used in this domain were always used to gauge the knowledge of rehabilitation programs, perceived effectiveness of programs, teacher confidence, availability of resources, family participation, and cooperation with other sectors among participants. The barriers to effective educational rehabilitation scale were also found to be highly reliable with Cronbach’s alpha of 0.849, indicating that the items were consistently able to measure perceived barriers like lack of trained educators, lack of finances, accessibility, cultural attitudes, poor parental awareness, limited access to special technologies, policy gaps, social stigma, and poor school-rehabilitation partnership. The scale, innovations, and future directions in educational rehabilitation demonstrated acceptable to strong reliability, with a Cronbach alpha value of 0.836, indicating that it is consistently measuring constructs related to innovation, including customized learning plans, assistive technology, online learning, universal design in learning, educational applications, parent-support programs, job-readiness training, and artificial intelligence-based rehabilitation. The item-level analysis indicated that the removal of each item did not significantly enhance the reliability of the scales; hence, all 30 items were used in the final analysis, and the results of the reliability are summarized in Table 1.

The respondent groups were evenly distributed across all three domains, with teachers, students, and other respondents each representing 33.3% of the Sample. The chi-square goodness-of-fit test confirmed that respondent-group distribution did not differ significantly from an equal distribution in any domain, χ2 = 0.000, df = 2, p = 1.000, Cohen’s w = 0.000. This balanced distribution indicates that the main respondent categories were equally represented across the survey domains, as shown in Table 2.

Demographic distribution and chi-square goodness-of-fit analysis of respondents across awareness, barriers, and innovation-readiness domains

Qualification patterns showed minor variation. In the Awareness and Perception domain, PhD respondents represented the largest group at 37.2%, followed by BS and MPhil respondents at 31.4% each; however, this difference was not statistically significant, χ2 = 1.744, df = 2, p = 0.418, Cohen’s w = 0.082. Similarly, in the Barriers domain, PhD respondents again formed the largest group at 37.2%, followed by BS respondents at 35.3% and MPhil respondents at 27.5%, but this distribution was also not statistically significant, χ2 = 4.070, df = 2, p = 0.131, Cohen’s w = 0.126. In the Innovations and Future Directions domain, qualification was equally distributed across BS, MPhil, and PhD categories, with each group representing 33.3% of respondents, χ2 = 0.000, df = 2, p = 1.000, Cohen’s w = 0.000. Gender distribution showed statistically significant differences across the domains. Female respondents were more represented than male respondents in all three domains, with 39.5% in ID1 and ID2, and 47.0% in ID3. The chi-square test showed significant gender differences in ID1 and ID2, χ2 = 7.432, df = 2, p = 0.024, Cohen’s w = 0.175, and a stronger significant difference in ID3, χ2 = 44.758, df = 2, p < 0.001, Cohen’s w = 0.475. These findings indicate that female respondents were consistently more represented, particularly in the innovation-readiness domain.

Age and professional experience distribution

Age distribution varied substantially across the three domains and showed statistically significant differences. In the Awareness and Perception domain, the 18–25 age group was the largest category, representing 35.4% of respondents, followed by the 26–35 group at 28.6%. This distribution was statistically significant, χ2 = 44.457, df = 4, p < 0.001, Cohen’s w = 0.504, indicating stronger representation of younger respondents in the awareness-related domain. In contrast, the Barriers and Innovations domains were dominated by older respondents. In the Barriers domain, the 46–60 age group represented 51.7% of participants, while in the Innovations and Future Directions domain, the same age group represented 56.9%. These distributions were statistically significant, with χ2 = 152.305, df = 4, p < 0.001, Cohen’s w = 0.803 for ID2 and χ2 = 185.657, df = 4, p < 0.001, Cohen’s w = 0.954 for ID3. These findings suggest that younger respondents were more represented in awareness-related responses, whereas older respondents were more represented in barrier and innovation-readiness responses. Professional experience also showed meaningful distributional differences. In the Awareness and Perception domain, respondents in the BPS-17/3–4 years category represented the largest group at 41.6%, followed by BPS-18/5–7 years and BPS-19/8–10 years at 29.2% each. This distribution was statistically significant, χ2 = 7.407, df = 2, p = 0.025, Cohen’s w = 0.175. In the Barriers domain, BPS-17/3–4 years respondents were also the largest group at 39.9%, followed by BPS-19/8–10 years at 30.9% and BPS-18/5–7 years at 29.2%, although this difference was not statistically significant, χ2 = 4.840, df = 2, p = 0.089, Cohen’s w = 0.141. In the Innovations and Future Directions domain, the BPS-17/3–4 years category again represented the largest group at 41.6%, followed by BPS-18/5–7 years at 30.5% and BPS-19/8–10 years at 28.0%. This distribution was statistically significant, χ2 = 7.630, df = 2, p = 0.022, Cohen’s w = 0.177. Overall, these findings show that respondent categories were balanced, but gender, age, and professional experience showed important distributional patterns that may influence how participants perceived awareness, barriers, and innovation readiness in educational rehabilitation.

Frequency distributions, percentage proportions, cumulative percentages, mean scores, and categorical decision thresholds are used to analyze the results on a five-point Likert scale. The indicators of central tendency are the mean values, and the distributions of responses depict the level of consensus and variability in the participants. The interpretation thresholds also categorize the responses into high, moderate, and low levels, which allows a subtle perception of the patterns of perception.

Awareness and perception of educational rehabilitation programs

The findings for awareness and perception (Q1–Q10) indicate a moderately high level of cognitive and perceptual engagement with educational rehabilitation programs among respondents. The distribution of responses is positively skewed toward agreement categories, with “Strongly Agree” responses ranging from 35.1% to 52.7%, demonstrating a relatively strong endorsement of awareness-related constructs. The mean scores, ranging from 3.16 to 3.71, suggest that the majority of items fall within the moderate to high perception categories, indicating that respondents possess a generally favourable understanding of rehabilitation initiatives. Specifically, items such as Q1 (M = 3.59), Q5 (M = 3.62), Q6 (M = 3.71), Q7 (M = 3.62), and Q10 (M = 3.60) reflect high perception levels, implying strong recognition of program relevance, importance, and implementation.

However, items such as Q2 (M = 3.40), Q3 (M = 3.16), Q4 (M = 3.47), Q8 (M = 3.49), and Q9 (M = 3.43) fall within the moderate perception range, suggesting variability in respondents’ awareness across specific dimensions. This variability is further supported by the presence of notable neutral response rates, particularly in Q6 (30.6%) and Q7 (23.7%), which may indicate informational gaps, ambiguity in understanding, or limited direct exposure to certain program components. From a technical perspective, the dispersion of responses across all five Likert categories suggests heterogeneity in respondent cognition, possibly influenced by demographic, professional, or experiential differences. Despite this variability, the overall pattern indicates that awareness is established but not uniformly deep, highlighting the need for more targeted dissemination strategies and stakeholder engagement mechanisms.

Perceived barriers to effective educational rehabilitation

The analysis of perceived barriers (Q11–Q20) reveals a strong and consistent recognition of systemic, institutional, and contextual constraints affecting the effectiveness of educational rehabilitation programs. The response distributions are heavily concentrated in the “Strongly Agree” category, with percentages reaching as high as 62.7% (Q11), indicating a high level of consensus regarding the presence of barriers. The mean scores, which range from 3.11 to 3.74, predominantly fall within the high perceived barrier category, with only a few items classified as moderate. Items such as Q11 (M = 3.74), Q15 (M = 3.61), Q16 (M = 3.68), Q17 (M = 3.65), Q18 (M = 3.56), and Q19 (M = 3.51) demonstrate particularly high values, suggesting that respondents strongly perceive these factors as critical impediments.

The comparatively lower average score of Q13 (M = 3.11) shows that, although this barrier is being recognized, it might not be as widely perceived as other ones. However, the general trend is that there is a strong level of consensus and low variance to the disagreement types, which strengthens the findings. Statistically, the high concentration of the responses in the categories of higher agreement and the high mean values of the responses implies low variability and high central tendencies, which implies that there is a common perception among the sample population. Moreover, the cumulative percent distributions indicate that a significant proportion of respondents are in categories that are in an agreement, which further indicates the structural nature of these barriers. These results suggest that issues like resource constraints, lack of professional training, poor coordination in an institution and socio-cultural restrictions are inherent in the system. These perceptions are powerful and consistent, motivating a comprehensive overhaul of the system, policy interventions, and strategic resource allocation, to overcome these obstacles.

Innovation readiness and future directions

Compared to the areas mentioned above, the scores of innovation preparedness (Q21–Q30) show a systematic low level of preparedness to embrace innovative and technology-based solutions in educational rehabilitation. All the items have an average score within a close range of 2.03 to 2.22, and they are categorized under low innovation readiness. Although the categories of Strongly Agree and Agree have relatively high frequencies in some of the items (e.g., Q21–Q26), the general scoring pattern indicates that respondents are conceptually supportive of innovation but are not practically ready, have no infrastructure or ability to implement it. This difference between attitudinal assent and operational preparedness is an expression of a failing deficiency in the system. The distribution of responses to Q27 is also interesting, with 63.8 percent of the respondents giving an answer of Agree, but the mean value is low (M = 2.03). This suggests that there might be an inconsistency in the response pattern, probably because of the interpretation of the scale or the understanding of the respondents on the related constructs of innovation. Also, the existence of similar proportions of disagreement and neutral answers on all items indicates the uncertainty and variability of perceptions and can be explained by the inadequate exposure to high-technological devices, the absence of training, or institutional support.

Technically, the low mean scores and the comparatively spread response patterns point to the lack of strong central tendency and increased variability, which are the characteristics of a fragmented perception of innovation readiness. This underscores the lack of a unified approach to the incorporation of contemporary teaching methodologies, including assistive technologies, learning systems, personalized, and digital platforms. Findings indicate that there is a multi-dimensional perception structure amongst respondents. Although educational rehabilitation program awareness is moderately to highly high and barriers also firmly identified, the innovation readiness is extremely low. The statistical trends, which are represented by the mean distributions, the concentrations of responses, and the thresholds of interpretation, point to the existence of an obvious disproportion between the identification of the problems and readiness to take future-oriented solutions. These results highlight the importance of combined policy frameworks, specific capacity-building strategies, and organized technological investment as a measure to overcome the gap between the current awareness of programs and the implementation of new rehabilitation practices.

Awareness, effectiveness, and structural barriers

The results on awareness and effectiveness indicate that the respondents have moderate awareness that is statistically significant on the topic of educational rehabilitation programs for students with IDs. The values of t-tests, greater than 27, and the difference in means, between 2.15 and 2.17, show that the participants are, on the whole, aware of the existence and the purpose of such programs, but the awareness is not comprehensive and deeply rooted. This implies that there might be gaps in the information dissemination processes, the communication policy, and the practice of engaging the stakeholders in the educational rehabilitation system. In the same vein, perceptions on the effectiveness of the current programs and their capability to meet the various educational needs of the students with IDs show medium levels of agreement, with the mean differences between 2.20 and 2.21. These findings suggest that although the programs are operational and functional, they might not be effective as required in terms of inclusivity, individualised learning support, and adaptive educational delivery.

One of the key aspects of this subdomain is a structural and systemic barriers analysis that achieved very high t-values (e.g., t = 56.925 and t = 55.626), meaning that there was a very large agreement between the respondents concerning the level of seriousness of these barriers. Such barriers are inadequate supplies of trained teachers, financial and institutional constraints, inaccessible assistive technology, and disjointed policy execution models. These results indicate serious flaws in the development of human capital, efficiency of resource allocation, and technological infrastructure in the sector of educational rehabilitation. In addition, socio-cultural barriers such as negative attitudes of society towards IDs, stigma, and physical inaccessibility in learning institutions were also statistically significant. The uniformity of the responses in these variables implies that these barriers are not solitary but are rather profoundly integrated into the system of more socio-educational factors. Moreover, institutional collaboration and family involvement were found to be very important elements of effective rehabilitation; however, moderate mean scores indicate that these practices are not fully implemented into routine educational systems yet. There is a discrepancy between the perceived importance and the actual implementation.

Innovation, technological integration, and future perspectives

The second dimension of analysis indicates a high and statistically significant inclination towards innovation and future-oriented educational changes in the rehabilitation services of the intellectually disabled students. Innovation-related items always had greater mean differences (M.D > 3.50) and t-values (approximately t ≈ 45–51), which signify that there is general agreement among the respondents on the need to incorporate modern pedagogical practices. The findings imply that there is a convincing understanding of the necessity to change the conventional rehabilitation systems with the incorporation of new methodologies and technologies.

The assistive technologies, such as speech-to-text systems, interactive digital learning platforms, and adaptive educational tools, were seen as very effective, and the mean differences were approximately 3.47. This is a good indication of belief in the power of technology as a means of inclusive learning and personalized learning support. Among the statistically significant results in this area is that of individualized learning plans, which had an enormously high ANOVA value (F = 495.25, ηp2 = 0.913), which means that the effect size is extremely large, and the variability among different groups of respondents is significant. It implies that although the overwhelming evidence supports the use of individualized educational strategies, the perceptions of the possibility of their implementation can differ based on the experience of working with children, professional position, or experience of being exposed to the practice of inclusive education.

Moreover, the respondents expressed a great deal of support for the integration of emerging technologies like artificial intelligence (AI), machine learning systems, and virtual learning environments, with the mean values of 3.40 and 3.63 showing optimism that these technologies will have positive impacts on learning outcomes, assessment procedures, and student engagement. Also noted during the analysis is the strong support of teacher training on new pedagogical frameworks, especially the Universal Design for Learning (UDL), which is critical towards meeting the diverse learning needs of students in inclusive classrooms. Mean scores (above 3.60) in this category are high, thus highlighting the perceived need for continuous professional development among educators.

Moreover, the respondents highly supported complementary interventions like parental support programs, job-readiness training, educational applications, and gamified learning tools, which are part of a more holistic rehabilitation model that does not exclusively focus on academic development but covers social integration and vocational preparedness. The high statistical significance and effect sizes of these variables show that there is a common understanding of the significance of multi-dimensional, collaborative, and technology-enhanced rehabilitation models. In general, this subdomain indicates a progressive and futuristic approach of the respondents, but at the same time, it shows the necessity of structural investment and capacity building to convert the readiness to innovate into real practice.

Discussion

The current study examined stakeholders’ perceptions, perceived barriers, and readiness to innovate in educational rehabilitation programs for students with IDs in Saudi Arabia. The findings reveal a clear gap: although stakeholders widely recognise the importance of educational rehabilitation, this awareness is not matched by adequate preparedness for implementation. This disconnect reflects a broader issue between conceptual support and practical capacity, as institutional readiness—such as infrastructure, professional training, and coordinated systems—remains insufficient. This pattern aligns with existing literature showing that inclusive education reforms often progress from policy recognition to slower, uneven implementation [24, 63]. Furthermore, responses indicate that perspectives vary by role: administrators tend to report higher awareness due to their involvement in policy and program coordination, while educators express more cautious views based on direct classroom challenges, including limited training, resource constraints, and insufficient assistive technologies. This disparity highlights that policy awareness alone does not ensure practical readiness, emphasising the need for stronger classroom-level support, continuous professional development, and effective implementation strategies.

The higher involvement among participants aged 36–45 may reflect their position at a midpoint between professional experience and openness to new practices. Individuals in this age group are likely to have accumulated sufficient experience to understand the needs of students with IDs, while also being more familiar with contemporary educational technologies and inclusive education reforms than older cohorts. By contrast, the lower engagement of the 26–35 age group may reflect limited decision-making authority, shorter professional experience, or less direct involvement in rehabilitation planning. This finding should be interpreted cautiously because small subgroup sizes may affect the stability of estimates. Nevertheless, it suggests that professional development should be differentiated by career stage: early-career educators may need mentoring and structured exposure to rehabilitation practices, while more experienced educators may need targeted training in assistive technologies, Universal Design for Learning, and collaborative service delivery.

These findings are enhanced by the reliability of the study instrument. The alpha of the Cronbach’s Awareness and Perception (0.856), Barriers (0.849), and Innovations and Future Directions (0.836) shows that the internal consistency in all the domains is high. These values exceed the generally accepted value of 0.70 in social science studies, which indicates that the tool was effective in measuring coherent constructs as opposed to fragmented opinions [36]. Moreover, the findings meet the suitability of Likert-scale methodologies in measuring the stakeholder attitudes in education settings, especially when items share similar concepts [2, 37]. Such a methodological strength is fundamental due to the complexity of stakeholder reactions, which can be both inclusive support and fear of impractical constraints.

The results in the Awareness and Perception domain show that there are moderate to high levels of stakeholder awareness about educational rehabilitation. There was a high level of consensus between respondents about the significance of rehabilitation services, family involvement, interdisciplinary collaboration, and long-term support. This is indicative of an increasing global consciousness of disability inclusion, which is led by policy changes and advocacy [13, 39]. In the Saudi context, no different tendencies have been identified, as the concept of inclusive education has become more prominent in discussions, yet it is still inconsistently applied [6, 10]. These findings indicate that the stakeholders are not opposed to the idea of rehabilitation,instead, they tend to be in more agreement with its principles.

Nonetheless, some awareness items did not have a strong reaction, which shows that there was an uneven awareness of how it is applied to practice. Stakeholders might value the relevance of educational rehabilitation, but without specific knowledge on how such services are provided, classroom practice, and coordination across agencies. This is a typical knowledge-to-practice gap, in which theoretical knowledge fails to be applied as practical competence. Additional evidence, as demonstrated by Al-Hasani et al. [1], indicates that training, experience, and institutional context contribute greatly to positive attitudes towards assistive technology among Saudi teachers. Thus, what stakeholders need is more than awareness,they need practical skills in assessment, individual planning, integration of assistive technology, and service delivery in collaboration.

The Barriers domain yielded some of the most substantial results and emphasised a systemic problem instead of a specific classroom problem. The respondents cited significant challenges such as a lack of teacher training, a lack of adequate finances, inadequate access to assistive technologies, weak implementation of policies, social stigma, and weak coordination between schools and rehabilitation centres. This is in line with the reports in the world that children with disabilities still experience structural imbalances in the education systems [15, 54]. It is important to note that the untrained teachers turned out to be a significant obstacle, which supports the fact that teacher competence is a crucial factor in successful rehabilitation. In the absence of proper preparation, any rehabilitation plans will just stay in theory and fail to be implemented in any meaningful classroom practice [64].

These challenges are further intensified by limited resources, as students with IDs require specialised tools, individualised materials, communication supports, and continuous assessment systems to effectively benefit from educational rehabilitation. Although teachers often demonstrate strong commitment, inadequate institutional support restricts effective implementation, highlighting that successful rehabilitation depends not only on individual effort but also on system-level capacity, including funding, infrastructure, and equitable resource allocation. In addition, social stigma remains a significant barrier, influencing family engagement, student participation, and broader community acceptance, and has been shown to limit the effectiveness of inclusion efforts by reinforcing gaps between policy and practice [25]. This stigma is experienced differently across stakeholder groups, with educators often witnessing its impact in classroom interactions and peer relationships, while administrators observe it through enrolment patterns, family communication, and institutional planning processes. Therefore, overcoming these barriers requires a multi-level approach involving public awareness initiatives, family education, inclusive school environments, continuous teacher training, and strong policy enforcement to safeguard the educational rights of students with IDs.

The lack of collaboration between schools and rehabilitation centres was also raised as a significant issue. Educational rehabilitation involves the collaboration of teachers, therapists, families, and administrators. In the absence of such coordination, students may have disjointed support, a lack of coherent goals, and less continuity of care. This is in line with the ecological systems theory, which places much focus on the interrelated environments in influencing the development of students. Lack of inter-system collaboration interferes with such links, ultimately impacting the quality and sustainability of rehabilitation results [60].

Demographic analysis gives further understanding of the views of stakeholders. Gender, age, qualification, and workplace experience differences indicate that there are individual and work-related factors affecting the perception. Female respondents showed more engagement, especially in areas of innovation, perhaps due to their leading role in the education and caregiving fields [38]. The differences in age indicated that younger stakeholders were more aware than older ones, with older participants having more awareness of barriers and innovation issues. This indicates that experience helps to increase the knowledge of systemic constraints, even as newcomers might be more affected by the modern discourse of inclusive education. On the same note, perceptions were influenced by professional experience with professionals in the middle career exhibiting a high level of interest and may need additional assistance in order to make knowledge implementable [68].

Results on the innovation preparedness show that there is a severe disjuncture between preparedness and acceptance. Even though stakeholders are aware of the potential of new technologies like assistive tools, virtual learning environments, and artificial intelligence, their willingness to adopt such innovations is low. This implies that innovation is not limited by resistance but rather by a lack of adequate infrastructure, training, and contextual adaptation. This opinion is supported by international studies that highlight that technology can be used to increase inclusion only when it is accessible, culturally relevant, and when trained educators [45, 59]. The significance of individualised learning plans, family engagement, and vocational training is also mentioned in the study, which supports the holistic approach to rehabilitation and emphasises not only academic performance but also life skills and social inclusion [4, 62]. In general, these results suggest that stakeholder support is not the main obstacle but the lack of systemic capabilities to translate the support into effective and sustainable practice.

Study strengths

This research has a number of strengths that contribute to the research on educational rehabilitation of students with IDs in Saudi Arabia. First, it is a significant field that is understudied as it deals with the awareness, barriers, and innovation-related perspectives in the Saudi special education setting. Second, the study design was a structured quantitative survey design with a relatively large Sample of 298 participants comprising educators, administrators, students, and other rehabilitation-related stakeholders, which ensured that the study captured role-based variation of the perceptions of educational rehabilitation. Third, the three survey scales were highly internally consistent in that Cronbach’s alpha was 0.856 with awareness and perception, 0.849 with barriers, and 0.836 with innovations and future directions. These values show high reliability and allow for the appropriateness of the tool in the measurement of stakeholder perceptions. Cronbach’s alpha is a commonly applied objective tool of scale reliability in educational and social studies. Fourth, descriptive statistics, t-test, and one-way ANOVA have reinforced the analysis value of the research in detecting significant variations between the groups of participants on variables like teacher confidence, resource availability, family involvement, social stigma, and implementation of technology. Research is also practically relevant as it indicates areas where the policy and practice can be improved, such as professional growth of teachers, improved resource allocation, enhanced family-school partnership, anti-stigma efforts, and cautious implementation of assistive technologies, virtual learning environment, augmented reality, and artificial intelligence.

Study limitations

In spite of these advantages, a number of limitations may be mentioned. First, the cross-sectional type of design involved the assessment of the perceptions of the stakeholders at one point in time; hence, the results will not indicate the causal relationships and how perceptions and program effectiveness can vary with time. Cross-sectional studies can be used to describe a population and establish associations, but do not track individuals over time. Second, the research was based on self-reported data in the form of a questionnaire, which is subject to social desirability bias, recall bias, or differences in the interpretation of the survey items. Third, despite the involvement of several stakeholder groups in the study, the direct voices of students with IDs were narrow since some student responses necessitated the assistance of caregivers, teachers or trained personnel. In future research, more direct methods of capturing the personal experiences of the students, such as available interviews, visual response tools, supported communication methods, or observational techniques, should be used. Fourth, the purposive convenience sampling method was appropriate in terms of accessing the participants who had the expertise pertinent to educational rehabilitation, but it could restrict the ability to generalise to all schools, regions or rehabilitation environments in Saudi Arabia. Fifth, the research failed to study the regional variation in a deep manner, especially the distinction between an urban and rural environment, where access to trained teachers, assistive technologies, and rehabilitation resources might differ. Lastly, the low average scores of items in the category of innovation in ID3 indicate that the participants might be willing to promote innovation in theory but are not sure about the present implementation preparedness, such as infrastructure, funding, technical support, digital resources in the Arabic language, and training of teachers. The effectiveness of educational rehabilitation programs and innovation-based interventions in the long term should be assessed in the future with larger, regionally balanced samples and longitudinal or mixed-method designs.

Conclusion

This research gives scientifically pertinent information regarding the current state, issues, and prospects of educational rehabilitation among Saudi Arabian students with IDs. A quantitative survey with valid scales of measurement demonstrates that the stakeholders mostly attribute the importance of educational rehabilitation, although there are some significant gaps in the implementation, especially in the areas of teacher preparation, availability of resources, access to assistive technology, family-school partnership, inter-sectoral coordination, and social acceptance. The pronounced disparity between educators, administrators, students, and other stakeholders implies that educational rehabilitation is perceived in varied ways by different stakeholders, which implies the necessity to correlate the planning process at the policy level with the one in the classroom. Despite the high potential of innovation-based strategies such as personalized learning plans, virtual world learning environments, augmented reality, specialized education apps, job-ready training, and AI-supported learning, their successful implementation presupposes institutional preparedness, long-term funding, technical infrastructure, culturally sensitive Arabic-language resources, and long-term professional growth. Thus, the next step of improvement should be not only the extension of the rehabilitation services but also the enhancement of the quality, accessibility, and practical implementation of the latter in different educational contexts in Saudi. The voices of students with IDs should be directly incorporated in future research, and longitudinal or mixed-method studies should be used to determine the long-term academic, social, behavioural, and transition consequences of educational rehabilitation programs.
