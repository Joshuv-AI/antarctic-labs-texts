DemoShapley: Valuation of Demonstrations for In-Context Learning
1 st Shan Xie, 2 nd Man Luo, 3 rd Chadly Daniel Stern, 4 th Mengnan Du, 5 th Cheng Lu

Abstract
Large language models (LLMs) using in-context learning (ICL) excel in many tasks without task-specific fine-tuning. However, demonstration selection and ordering greatly impact ICL effectiveness. To address this, we propose DemoShapley and Beta-DemoShapley, inspired by Data Shapley and Beta Shapley, to assess the influence of individual demonstrations. DemoShapley captures how each example influences performance in different contexts, unlike other influence-based methods that rely on a fixed number of demonstrations. Beta-DemoShapley further enhances this framework by incorporating the Beta distribution, allowing users to assign higher weights to smaller cardinalities, which aligns with ICL’s prompt length and computational constraints. Our findings show that the proposed algorithms improve model performance by selecting quality demonstrations, and enhancing generalization to out-of-distribution tasks. It also identifies noise-compromised data and promotes fairness in LLMs, protecting model performance and ensuring robustness across various scenarios.



I
Introduction

LLMs adapt to new tasks through in-context learning (ICL), leveraging a few input-label pairs (demonstrations) without fine-tuning
[
1
]
. Unlike traditional fine-tuning, ICL enables LLMs to handle diverse tasks with lower complexity and resource consumption. Its efficiency and flexibility have led to strong performance with few-shot settings in many area
[
2
,
3
]
.
Despite its potential, one significant challenge of ICL is its frequent unexpected behaviors
[
4
]
. Typically, prompts in ICL consist of demonstration examples randomly selected from the training dataset
[
1
]
. Numerous research has shown that ICL’s performance is highly sensitive to the choice, order, and format of demonstrations
[
5
,
6
,
7
,
8
,
9
]
. Selecting high-quality demonstrations improves predictions, while noisy ones can degrade performance
[
4
]
.

To address this challenge, we study the problem of estimating the influence of each demonstration example via data valuation
[
10
]
.
We introduce two Shapley value-based algorithms: DemoShapley, inspired by Data Shapley
[
10
]
, for fairly evaluating demonstration contributions, and Beta-DemoShapley, based on Beta Shapley
[
11
]
, which assigns higher weights to smaller subsets to address diminishing marginal contributions in larger datasets, both grounded in cooperative game theory
[
12
]
.
The Shapley value quantifies each participant’s average marginal contribution across all possible coalitions. Previous research
[
13
,
11
,
14
]
has successfully applied this concept to data evaluation in traditional machine learning. Our methods extend it to ICL, not only assessing data points’ marginal contributions but also accounting for sequence effects, which are critical in ICL. This refinement improves demonstration selection, enhancing model performance.

II
Related Work

II-A

Data Valuation

Data valuation in machine learning focuses on assessing the worth of data within machine learning frameworks. For a comprehensive review, we refer readers to
Sim et al. 2022
. Broadly, data valuation strategies fall into three categories: Leave-One-Out (LOO), Cooperative Game Theory (CGT)-based, and desiderata-based methods.
LOO-based methods, introduced by
Cook and Weisberg 1980
, evaluate a data point
D_{k}
by measuring changes in model performance when
D_{k}
is removed from the dataset.
CGT-based methods originate from game theory, with Shapley value
[
12
]
and Banzhaf index
[
17
]
being the most prominent. These methods assess
D_{k}
’s marginal contribution by measuring the performance change when adding
D_{k}
to different dataset coalitions. This contribution can be positive or negative.
Desiderata-based strategies include Shapley variants such as Data Shapley
[
10
]
, Distributional Shapley
[
18
]
, Beta Shapley
[
11
]
, and KNN-Shapley
[
14
]
. Other approaches include DVRL
[
19
]
, which uses reinforcement learning to optimize data valuation, and
Pruthi et al. 2020
, which estimates data influence by tracking gradient descent updates.

II-B

ICL Demonstrations Selection

Chang and Jia 2022a
found that selecting training samples based on their impact on model accuracy stabilizes ICL outcomes compared to random selection. Similarly,
Nguyen and Wong 2023
developed a method to assess and select impactful examples, boosting performance on SuperGLUE tasks. In retrieval-based ICL, demonstration selection often prioritizes similarity and diversity. Similarity-based methods choose examples aligned with the query using linguistic or structural features.
Liu et al. 2021
showed that semantically closer demonstrations improve GPT-3 ICL performance with kNN retrieval, while
Rubin et al. 2021
and
Cheng et al. 2023
proposed retrieval methods leveraging language model cues and cross-domain datasets, respectively.

Beyond similarity, diverse demonstrations help prevent redundancy, capture varied viewpoints, and improve test coverage
[
25
]
. Additionally, demonstrations with higher complexity, such as longer queries or more reasoning steps, can enhance ICL performance
[
26
]
.

III
Demonstration Valuation for ICL

III-A

Preliminaries

III-A
1
Data Shapley

As a method for equitable data valuation in machine learning, Data Shapley
[
10
]
assigns a quantitative value to each data point, reflecting its contribution to model performance. The Data Shapley value
\phi_{i}
provides a fair measure of the impact of a data point on the overall performance of a learning algorithm. For a dataset
D
with learning algorithm
A
and performance metric
V
, it is defined as:

\phi_{i}=\sum_{S\subseteq D\setminus\{i\}}\binom{n-1}{|S|}^{-1}[V(S\cup\{i\})-V(S)],

(1)

where
n
is the total number of data points,
S
is a subset of
D
excluding
i
-th data point, and
V(S)
represents the performance score of a predictor trained on
S
. The term
\binom{n-1}{|S|}
is the binomial coefficient accounting for subset selection.

III-A
2
Beta Shapley

Beta-Shapley

[
11
]
showed that when the cardinality
\lvert S\rvert
is large, the marginal contribution of individual data points becomes trivial. The uniform weighting in Data Shapley struggles to distinguish individual contributions, potentially leading to suboptimal decisions. Beta-Shapley enhances data-Shapley by incorporating
the Beta distribution, allowing users to assign higher weights
to smaller cardinalities.

III-B

From Data Shapley to (Beta-)DemoShapley

In ICL, demonstrations act similarly to training data in traditional machine learning. DemoShapley inherits the spirit of Data Shapley to estimate the marginal contribution of each demonstration by incrementally adding demonstrations to the prompt and measuring the resulting changes in the model’s performance. This performance difference reflects the importance of each demonstration. We further introduce Beta-DemoShapley, inspired by Beta Shapley, which improves upon Data Shapley by assigning higher weights to subsets with smaller cardinalities. This design is particularly suited to ICL, where the number of demonstrations is often limited due to context length constraints and computational costs. By emphasizing the impact of demonstrations in smaller subsets, Beta-DemoShapley better captures the characteristics of ICL, where each demonstration can have a significant influence on model performance.

While DemoShapley eliminates the need for repeated model fitting, relying solely on model inference, the exact computation of DemoShapley remains computationally intensive due to the exponential number of LLM inferences required. To address this, we adapt the
Truncated Monte Carlo (TMC) method

[
27
,
10
,
11
]
which uses Monte Carlo approximation to estimate marginal contributions through random sampling of subsets, reducing computational cost and speeding up the approximation of (Beta-)DemoShapley values.

III-B
1
Weight Computation for (Beta-)DemoShapley

To address the limitations of Data Shapley, we apply predetermined weights to smaller cardinalities. This approach is particularly suited to ICL, where context-length constraints and computational costs limit the number of demonstrations in prompts. By assigning larger weights to smaller cardinalities instead of using uniform weights as in DemoShapley, we achieve a more precise evaluation of each demonstration’s influence in ICL.

To achieve this, we apply the Beta distribution with positive hyperparameters
\alpha
and
\beta
to define the weight
w
given by:

\displaystyle w_{\alpha,\beta}^{(n)}(j)

\displaystyle:=n\int_{0}^{1}t^{j-1}(1-t)^{n-j}\frac{t^{\beta-1}(1-t)^{\alpha-1}}{\operatorname{Beta}(\alpha,\beta)}dt

\displaystyle=n\frac{\operatorname{Beta}(j+\beta-1,n-j+\alpha)}{\operatorname{Beta}(\alpha,\beta)},

where
\operatorname{Beta}(\alpha,\beta)=\frac{\Gamma(\alpha)\Gamma(\beta)}{\Gamma(\alpha+\beta)}
is the Beta function,
\Gamma(\cdot)
is the Gamma function, and
j
represents the cardinality of the current candidate set, corresponding to the testing prompt. This expression can be further simplified to:

w_{\alpha,\beta}^{(n)}(j)=n\frac{\prod_{k=1}^{j-1}(\beta+k-1)\prod_{k=1}^{n-j}(\alpha+k-1)}{\prod_{k=1}^{n-1}(\alpha+\beta+k-1)}.

When
\alpha\geq\beta=1
, the normalized weight prioritizes smaller cardinalities, reducing the influence of noise from larger ones. Conversely,
\operatorname{Beta}(1,\beta)
assigns greater weight to larger cardinalities. A special case occurs when
\alpha=\beta=1
, where the normalized weight
\tilde{w}^{(n)}_{\alpha,\beta}\left(j\right)=\left({}^{n-1}_{j-1}\right)w^{(n)}_{\alpha,\beta}\left(j\right)=1
for all
j\in[n]
which is the original DemoShapley algorithm.
In practice, this weight computation is performed only once per DemoShapley calculation, ensuring computational efficiency.

III-B
2
A Unified Framework

We propose a unified calculation framework that accommodates both the original DemoShapley and the Beta-DemoShapley algorithms.
We modify key steps to reflect ICL’s unique constraints, adapting TMC-Shapley for efficient calculation of DemoShapley values in LLMs.
Unlike traditional training, ICL selects a small set of demonstrations as prompts instead of the whole training dataset. DemoShapley can be expressed in the form of a weighted mean as follows:

\phi_{i}=\frac{1}{K}\sum^{K}_{j=1}\frac{1}{|D_{j}^{\backslash i}|}\sum_{S\in D_{j}^{\backslash i}}\tilde{w}_{\alpha,\beta}^{(n)}(j)[V(S\cup\{i\})-V(S)].

(2)

The process is described as follows:

1.

Weight Computation

We calculate the weight for DemoShapley based on the hyperparameters
\alpha
,
\beta
and
n
. For consistency and simplicity, original DemoShapley uses a uniform weight, which can be represented by setting
\alpha=\beta=1
, allowing original DemoShapley and Beta-DemoShapley to fit within the same framework.

2.

Random Sample and Permutation
:
Randomly select K demonstrations from the candidate set and permute them into a sequence
\Pi=\{\pi_{1},\pi_{2},\ldots,\pi_{K}\}
, forming the ordered prompt
P
. For zero-shot learning,
P
contains only task instructions.

3.

Performance Metric Calculation
: For prompt
P
, compute the performance metric
V(P,f)
for the LLM
f
. Initially, the zero-shot performance
V(\emptyset,f)
, where the prompt consists solely of the task instruction, needs to be calculated.

4.

Sequential Demonstration Addition
: Following
\Pi
, add demonstrations one by one to
P^{\prime}
, updating
V^{\prime}(P^{\prime},f)
with each addition while maintaining consistency with the development dataset. This is done using the same development dataset for consistency.

5.

Shapley Value Update
: If the performance change
v^{\prime}
with a newly added demonstration exceeds a certain threshold, update the DemoShapley value
\phi_{n}
for the newly added demonstration
\pi_{n}
using the equation:

\phi_{\pi^{t_{c}}[c]}\leftarrow\frac{t_{c}-1}{t_{c}}\phi_{\pi^{t_{c}-1}[c]}+\tilde{w}^{(n)}_{\alpha,\beta}\left(j\right)\frac{1}{t_{c}}\left(v^{{}^{\prime}}\right),

(3)

where
t_{c}
denotes the number of iterations for the
c
-th demonstration. For Beta-DemoShapley, a precomputed normalized weight
\tilde{w}^{(n)}_{\alpha,\beta}\left(j\right)
will be applied on
v^{\prime}
.

6.

Repetition for Convergence
: The process terminates under two conditions:

(1) Max Iterations Times
: Repeat
N
times to ensure all data points appear in various orders.

(2) MC Convergence Check
: our proposed algorithm is based on MC method which guarantees to converge to be true value if we repeat the sampling procedure. We use the Gelman-Rubin statistic
[
28
]
, a Monte Carlo (MC) convergence diagnostic, to stop sampling when the performance increment becomes negligible.

These steps ensure an efficient and stable estimation of DemoShapley values. Our adapted framework quantifies the contribution of each demonstration, optimizing selection for improved ICL performance. The full algorithm is provided in Algorithm
1
in the Appendix.

IV-B

The Prediction Efficacy of DemoShapley

We design two experiments to showcase the predictive performance of DemoShapley compared to other baselines.

Add In-context Learning Examples based on Demonstration Values.

We start with a zero-shot setting and apply two selection strategies. The first strategy incrementally adds examples with the highest Demonstration Shapley values, enriching the prompt with valuable demonstrations. The second strategy also begins from a zero-shot baseline but instead adds examples with the lowest values, introducing demonstrations that are theoretically less beneficial or even detrimental.

Remove In-context Learning Examples based on Demonstration Values.

We begin with 10 randomly selected demonstrations and conduct two removal experiments. The first removes the top five highest-valued examples to assess their impact on prediction performance. The second removes the five lowest-valued examples to evaluate how eliminating less valuable demonstrations affects performance.

DemoShapley Robust to Label Noise

In this section, we evaluate DemoShapley and Beta-DemoShapley for detecting label noise in datasets. Since crowdsourced annotations often introduce mislabeling that degrades model performance, we build on
[
10
]
, which used Data Shapley to identify mislabeled data, expecting (Beta-)DemoShapley to offer similar benefits. We randomly select 100 examples from each dataset as the demonstration pool, introducing label noise by modifying 10% of the labels with manual verification to ensure genuine mislabeling. We then compute evaluation values from different algorithms across datasets and models, analyzing the correlation between low (Beta-)DemoShapley values and mislabeled examples to assess its effectiveness in identifying noisy labels.

Results

Obvervation 1:
Our method demonstrates superior sensitivity in detecting label-flipped samples in toxic classification tasks across both ChatGPT-3.5-Turbo and GPT-J-6B, outperforming other approaches. IV-E

Evaluation for Algorithmic Fairness

Fairness in ICL is influenced by the selection of demonstrations, which may introduce biases based on the data distribution
[
42
]
. To investigate this, we examine whether ICL exhibits fairness when selecting demonstrations in ICL. Using the Adult dataset, where the task is to predict income levels, we consider gender as a sensitive attribute and analyze how different selection methods impact fairness.

Following
Wang et al. 2023
, we construct a test dataset with a fixed base rate parity (BPT) of 0.0, ensuring that any observed bias stems from the prompts rather than the dataset itself. We vary the base rate parity of demonstrations (BPC) among 0, 0.5, and 1 to assess how bias in the demonstrations influences fairness in predictions. We evaluate fairness using demographic parity difference (
M_{dpd}
) and equalized odds difference (
M_{eod}

Conclusion

We introduce DemoShapley and its extension, Beta-DemoShapley, as robust algorithms for selecting demonstrations in ICL. Inspired by Data Shapley and Beta Shapley, these methods fairly evaluate each demonstration’s contribution by considering its marginal impact across varying numbers of demonstrations, starting from zero. This dynamic approach captures how each example influences performance in different contexts, unlike other influence-based methods that rely on a fixed number of demonstrations. Beta-DemoShapley further enhances this framework by incorporating the Beta distribution, allowing users to assign higher weights to smaller cardinalities, which aligns with ICL’s prompt length and computational constraints. Together, these methods provide a comprehensive and efficient solution for demonstration selection, consistently improving performance, making it ideal for downstream AI applications.

Fairness related definitions

To assess demographic fairness using base rate parity (bP), where
Y
is the prediction label and
A
is the sensitive attribute.

\begin{split}b_{P}=\mathbb{P}_{(X,Y,A)\sim P_{XY}}[Y=1\mid A=1]\\
-\mathbb{P}_{(X,Y)\sim P_{XYA}}[Y=1\mid A=0]\end{split}

(4)

The demographic parity difference (
M_{dpd}
) measures the difference in positive prediction probabilities conditioned on the sensitive attribute
A
being 1 and 0. However, it does not consider ground-truth labels. The equalized odds difference (
M_{eod}
) addresses this by incorporating ground-truth labels when calculating the demographic gap. The equations are shown below.

\scriptstyle\begin{split}M_{dpd}=&\left|\mathbb{P}_{(X,Y,A)\sim P_{XY}}[f(X)=1\mid A=1]\right.\\
&\left.-\mathbb{P}_{(X,Y,A)\sim P_{XY}}[f(X)=1\mid A=0]\right|\end{split}

(5)

\scriptstyle\begin{split}M_{\mathrm{eod}}=\max\left\{M_{TP},M_{FP}\right\}\end{split}

(6)

The
M_{TP}
denotes the true positive equalized odds difference while the
M_{FP}
denotes the false positive equalized odds difference:

\scriptstyle\begin{split}M_{TP}=&\left|\mathbb{P}_{(X,Y,A)\sim P_{XY}}[f(X)=1\mid Y=1,A=0]\right.\\
&\left.-\mathbb{P}_{(X,Y,A)\sim P_{XY}}[f(X)=1\mid Y=1,A=1]\right|\end{split}

(7)

\small\begin{split}M_{FP}=\left|\mathbb{P}_{(X,Y,A)\sim P_{XY}}[f(X)=1\mid Y=0,A=0]\right.\\
\left.-\mathbb{P}_{(X,Y,A)\sim P_{XY}}[f(X)=1\mid Y=0,A=1]\right|\end{split}
