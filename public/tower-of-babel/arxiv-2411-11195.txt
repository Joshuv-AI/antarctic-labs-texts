SoK: Unifying Cybersecurity and Cybersafety of Multimodal Foundation Models with an Information Theory Approach
Ruoxi Sun 1 , Jiamin Chang 2 , Hammond Pearce 2 , Chaowei Xiao 3 , Bo Li 4 , Qi Wu 5 , Surya Nepal 1 , Minhui Xue 1

Abstract
Multimodal foundation models (MFMs) represent a significant advancement in artificial intelligence, combining diverse data modalities to enhance learning and understanding across a wide range of applications. However, this integration also brings unique safety and security challenges. In this paper, we conceptualize cybersafety and cybersecurity in the context of multimodal learning and present a comprehensive Systematization of Knowledge (SoK) to unify these concepts in MFMs, identifying key threats to these models. We propose a taxonomy framework grounded in information theory, evaluating and categorizing threats through the concepts of channel capacity, signal, noise, and bandwidth. This approach provides a novel framework that unifies model safety and system security in MFMs, offering a more comprehensive and actionable understanding of the risks involved. We used this to explore existing defense mechanisms, and identified gaps in current research – particularly, a lack of protection for alignment between modalities and a need for more systematic defense methods. Our work contributes to a deeper understanding of the security and safety landscape in MFMs, providing researchers and practitioners with valuable insights for improving the robustness and reliability of these models.



I
Introduction

Fig. 1:
An overview of our SoK on unifying cybersecurity and cybersafety of multimodal foundation models.

Multimodal foundation models (MFMs) are a form of artificial intelligence (AI) whichintegrate diverse data types, such as text, images, and audio, into unified frameworks capable of adapting to numerous downstream tasks
[
1
]
.
Notable examples of large-scale MFMs include the feature alignment model OpenAI’s CLIP
[
2
]
, image-generating diffusion models
[
3
]
, and multimodal large language models such as GPT-4
[
4
]
.
Deployed in industries such as healthcare, autonomous systems, content creation, MFMs can solve tasks beyond the reach of simpler unimodal models.
However, their rapid integration into high-stakes environments, including autonomous vehicles and medical diagnostics, amplifies the need for robust safety and security measures
[
5
,
6
]
.
Indeed, this growing use of MFMs in critical systems provides two main concerns: ensuring reliable, harm-free performance (safety) and protecting against malicious attacks (security).
These issues, traditionally treated separately, are increasingly intertwined
[
7
]
. Attacks on MFMs often undermine both safety and security, highlighting the need for unified analysis.
In addition, because of the richer data forms processed by MFMs, a wider range of more complex and covert attack vectors and scenarios have emerged as compared with simpler unimodal models.
This motivates our SoK paper.

Our approach.
To address the challenge of unifying safety and security in MFMs, we propose an innovative approach grounded in information theory. Information theory offers a powerful framework for understanding how information is transmitted, processed, and integrated across multiple modalities.
Specifically, we utilize concepts such as channel capacity, bandwidth, signal, and noise.
At the model level, our focus is on identifying safety threats that manipulate or exploit model vulnerabilities, either by diminishing the effective signal input to the system or by amplifying the disruptive impact of noise. At the system level, we extend this analysis by considering bandwidth constraints and exploring how information flows between agents and components across the broader system.
This integrated approach offers a comprehensive framework for understanding and mitigating threats in MFMs.
Additionally, we examine existing defense mechanisms, assess their effectiveness through experiments and analysis, and propose future research directions to further enhance the resilience of multimodal systems. An overview of our SoK is illustrated in Figure
1
.

However, their work remains high-level and does not address the specific technical challenges posed by MFMs. Other studies focus specifically on large language models (LLMs) and their vulnerabilities. For instance, Shayegani
et al.

and Das
et al.

extend this analysis to real-world LLM-based systems. Several surveys
[

12
]
highlight the limitations of existing defenses, though they primarily focus on a narrow subset of security threats, such as jailbreaking attacks, rather than addressing the broader threat landscape.
In the multimodal domain, Fan

While these studies focus on individual modalities or model-level threats, our work aims to provide a comprehensive review that unifies safety and security concerns at both the model and system levels for MFMs. We detail the literature collation method in Appendix

.

Contributions.
Our key contributions are fourfold:

•

Review of recent studies.
We present a comprehensive review of 229 recent studies on the safety and security of MFMs, exploring how different modalities and information flows introduce unique vulnerabilities and challenges in MFM systems.

•

Unified framework for threat analysis using information theory.
We propose a novel threat taxonomy framework grounded in information theory, particularly adapting the Shannon–Hartley theorem
[
15
,
16
]
to the context of multimodal learning. This framework unifies analysis of safety (focusing on the signal-to-noise balance) and security (emphasizing bandwidth constraints), and how these factors interact in MFMs. Furthermore, we identified 6 exploitable information flows encoded by the information theory: prediction, learning, and inference at the model level, as well as information flows between agents and applications, multi-agent interactions, and system memory at the system level. These let us produce a taxonomy to group attacks depending on their targeted information flows. We categorized 42 model-level safety attacks and 31 system-level security attacks based on this framework. Our findings reveal that attacks targeting multiple modalities simultaneously can be carried out more subtly and with greater ease.

•

An evaluation of defensive and mitigation strategies.
We examine existing MFM defenses and attack mitigation strategies, providing an experimental analysis over multiple methods. The results show that while defenses mitigate some of the impact of attacks, they remain inadequate and come with significant costs, achieving at most 51.8% accuracy on adversarial samples when against co-modality misleading attacks, and demonstrating insufficient performance against mislearning attacks. This suggests that relying solely on model-level protection is ineffective.

•

Future research directions.
We propose several directions for future research. We also emphasize the importance of understanding system-level interactions and propose new avenues for investigating cross-modal vulnerabilities in MFMs.

Key findings.

The unique architecture of multimodal models, especially their feature alignment and aggregation mechanisms, increases the potential for subtle, model-level attacks. This underscores the need for stronger protection measures and effective oversight. However, existing defenses often target specific vulnerable information flows or single modalities. We argue that applying bandwidth constraints at the system level offers considerable advantages and relying solely on model-level protection is often insufficient and sometimes ineffective.

From unimodal to multimodal learning.

Multimodal learning extends beyond traditional unimodal learning by integrating data from various modalities like text, images, and audio. In unimodal learning (Figure
2

In contrast, multimodal learning (Figure
2
(b)) involves mapping and aligning continuous feature spaces across different modalities, such as pairing images with text. Instead of directly predicting labels, multimodal models create unified representations by linking feature spaces from different data types. This allows for more complex tasks as the model can work with more diverse information.

III
Model Safety and System Security

In this section, we first outline the safety and security objectives in MFMs, then propose an information theory-based framework to analyze the interactions between these two concepts within MFMs.

III-A

Safety and Security Objectives

Across a broad range of sectors (e.g. aviation, nuclear energy, chemistry, power grids, and information systems), safety and security have conventionally been distinguished by
intentionality

[
25
,
26
,
27
,
28
,
29
]
.
Safety
focuses on preventing unintentional accidents, while
security
addresses protection from deliberate malicious actions
[
7
]
. In multimodal learning, safety refers to the system’s capacity to operate as intended without causing harm, even when faced with unpredictable conditions or inputs. For example, a vision-language model in an autonomous vehicle must not misinterpret shadows as obstacles. Security, on the other hand, involves defending against adversarial attacks and exploitation. Adversarial examples can subtly alter inputs, misleading models into incorrect predictions, such as small image perturbations generating misleading captions. Security also involves preventing data breaches that expose or manipulate sensitive information (
e.g.,
facial recognition).

Safety at model level.
This distinction between safety and security, however, can sometimes lead to confusion in the security domain. It is not entirely appropriate to differentiate between safety and security threats based solely on the presence of malicious intent, as all attack threat models inherently involve adversaries. For example, many attacks traditionally classified as safety attacks, such as jailbreak
[
30
,
31
]
or latency attacks
[
32
,
33
]
, involve adversarial intents and should be recognized as security-related according to this definition. Furthermore, the concept of model safety also encompasses objectives like adversarial robustness
[
34
,
35
]
, uncertainty estimation, trojan detection
[
36
]
, and alignment with human values
[
37
]
, which overlap with the root causes of security issues. At this level, we argue that security threats essentially represent attacks that target the model’s safety objectives. Thus,
these model-level threats should be seen as safety threats
. In practice, addressing safety issues at the model level involves tackling vulnerabilities throughout the model’s entire lifecycle, from training to deployment and inference.

Security at system level.

AI agents have been embedded in web platforms, email systems, file management services, and many other applications. At system levels such as these, security threats often arise and cannot be fully understood or mitigated by focusing solely on the model itself. For example, indirect prompt injection attacks
[
38
,
39
]
can place malicious prompts in external sources retrieved at inference time, such as in emails or web pages
[
40
,
39
]
. This enables attackers to manipulate an AI agent into unknowingly executing a malicious prompt, potentially exposing sensitive information to unauthorized third parties.

In such cases, even if each component in the system maintains good safety, without threats identification or applying operation constraints at the system level, serious security breaches can still occur within the broader system. Therefore, we emphasize that
security issues must be addressed at the system level
, where interactions between various components (agents, applications, and system memory) can provide for new risks and vulnerabilities.

III-B

An Information Processing Model to Unify Security and Safety in MFMs

Considering that machine learning models act as channels for information communication, where information flows from input samples through the model and eventually to the outputs, further spreading to other components in a system, we argue that information theory offers a suitable framework for analyzing the transmission, processing, and fusion of information across multimodal models.
Specifically, we adapt the Shannon–Hartley theorem
[
15
,
16
]
to multimodal safety and security analysis.
This has the formula:

C=B\log_{2}(1+\frac{S}{N}),

(1)

where
C
is the channel capacity (the measure of information transfer),
B
is the bandwidth of the channel (the availability for transmission),
S
is the signal power (the meaningful information being transmitted),
N
is the noise power (the unwanted interference affecting the signal).

For multimodal learning, we can adapt these definitions as follows:

•

Channel capacity
represents the model’s ability to effectively learn from and predict based on multimodal inputs, which is essential for assessing how well the model transmits meaningful information.

•

Signal
refers to the meaningful information that can be extracted from various modalities, such as textual features from documents, visual features from images, or auditory features from sound clips.

•

Noise
refers to any irrelevant or disruptive information that can distort the intended signal, which can stem from sources such as data collection errors, sensor inaccuracies, or deliberate perturbations injected into different modalities.

•

Bandwidth
refers to the capacity for transmitting information between agents or system components, determining how information can be exchanged, which directly impacts the performance and responsiveness of each agent within the system.

At the model level, improving performance and ensuring accurate predictions requires maximizing the effective signal. Robust models must accurately interpret and integrate data from various modalities to avoid errors or harmful outputs. Reducing noise, whether through data preprocessing, feature selection, or other methods, helps preserve relevant information while minimizing disruptive factors, leading to better model performance and safer operation. From an information theory perspective, this can be understood through Equation
1
, where safety-focused approaches aim to increase the signal-to-noise ratio (
S/N
). In contrast, safety attacks work to decrease this ratio and distort the information received by the model, either in training or prediction stages.
At the system level, information transmission considerations are critical in multimodal learning. Bandwidth
B
in Equation
1
influences the flow of information between different applications and agents, directly affecting their ability to process and combine diverse data types. Previous research
[
39
]
has shown that vulnerabilities may not be apparent when evaluating a single agent in isolation (
e.g.,
robust safety control
S/N
at model level); however, attacks can still exploit interactions across multiple agents, leading to significant security breaches within the system due to poor security management on
B
. To safeguard against security threats, it is essential to establish bandwidth constraints, including restrictions on agent behavior and filters that control the flow of information between agents. These measures are crucial for maintaining security at the system level.

Information theory provides the necessary tools to analyze and optimize the interactions among signals, bandwidth, and noise, guiding us in creating resilient mechanisms that bolster both safety and security.

Insight 1:

Using an information-theoretic framework, we can unify the concepts of model safety and system security for MFMs, enabling analysis and optimization of safety (signal-to-noise balance) and security (bandwidth constraints) in their interactions and fostering a more cohesive approach to robustness in multimodal models.

IV
Threat Models in MFMs

In this section, we introduce safety and security threats within a unified threat model, categorizing them based on the adversary’s goals, knowledge, and capabilities. We also outline the information flows in MFMs to highlight specific points of vulnerability that adversaries may target.

IV-A

Adversary’s Goals

At the model level, attackers aim to compromise the model by either forcing it to produce incorrect results or by extracting unauthorized information from its output:

•

Adversarial examples
(
e.g.,

[
41
,
42
]
). A key goal is to degrade model performance through adversarial examples, leading to unreliable predictions by manipulating the decision-making process across modalities.

•

Data poisoning
(
e.g.,

[
43
,
44
]
). Introducing malicious data into the training set to corrupt the learning process. This can degrade performance or alter behavior to serve the attacker’s interests, posing significant security risks.

•

Backdooring the model
(
e.g.,

[
45
,
46
]
). Hidden triggers are introduced during training, allowing attackers to activate malicious behaviors under specific conditions, compromising model integrity.

•

Jailbreaking
(
e.g.,

[
30
,
31
]
). Attackers may bypass safety mechanisms by exploiting model weaknesses, enabling the generation of unrestricted or harmful content.

•

Latency and energy consumption
(
e.g.,

[
32
,
33
]
). Attacks may increase latency or energy consumption, acting as denial-of-service tactics or exposing inefficiencies, disrupting service availability or inflating operational costs.

•

Prompt stealing
(
e.g.,

[
47
,
48
]
). Adversaries may seek to extract proprietary prompts by analyzing input-output pairs, potentially enabling unauthorized access to model capabilities.

•

Data extraction
(
e.g.,

[
49
]
). Using targeted techniques to infer training data, potentially exposing sensitive information or intellectual property.

•

Membership inference
(
e.g.,

[
50
,
51
]
). Determining whether specific data points were part of the model’s training set, compromising privacy and confidentiality.

At the system level, attackers aim to manipulate the system by misleading MFMs into performing unexpected behaviors, harmful interactions, or triggering malicious payloads:

•

Manipulated behavior
(
e.g.,

[
52
,
53
]
). Forcing agents to act contrary to their intended purpose, leading to dangerous actions or the spread of harmful content.

•

Goal hijacking
(
e.g.,

[
54
,
55
]
). Redirecting the agent’s objectives to serve the attacker’s intentions, hijacking the decision-making process for malicious activities.

•

Malicious payload
(
e.g.,

[
40
,
39
]
). Forcing agents to visit malicious or illegal web links or images, leading to security breaches or exploitation of vulnerabilities.

•

Malicious code
(
e.g.,

[
56
]
). Generating harmful code that compromises system integrity, spreads malware, or exploits weaknesses in connected systems.

•

Disinformation
(
e.g.,

[
57
,
58
]
). Spreading false or manipulated content that misguides other agents or users, impacting decision-making processes across systems.

•

Prompt leakage
(
e.g.,

[
55
]
). Extracting sensitive information by revealing hidden prompts or internal instructions, compromising user privacy and system security.

Some model-level goals, such as backdoor insertion, jailbreak, privacy leakage, data extraction, and membership inference, can also be achieved at the system level.

IV-B

Adversary’s Knowledge

Based on the level of accessibility, an attacker’s knowledge can be categorized into three main types: white-box, black-box, and grey-box attacks.

•

White-box attacks
(
e.g.,

[
42
,
30
]
) assume the adversary has comprehensive access to the MFM system’s data, covering both training and inference phases. In training, attackers can access the dataset, pre-trained models, MFM architecture, and hyperparameters. During inference, their knowledge extends to the trained model’s architecture and additional functions used in the MFM system, such as pre- or post-processing functions and defense mechanisms. While this level of access is less practical and typically applies only to open-source models, it serves as a foundation for advanced attacks.

•

Black-box attacks
(
e.g.,

[
41
,
59
]
) assume attackers have limited access to the MFM system, typically lacking insight into the training process, datasets, and hyperparameters. However, they can observe inputs and outputs during inference. Attackers may use auxiliary knowledge based on the MFM’s intended task to gather relevant data samples and construct shadow datasets that closely mimic training and inference data distributions.

•

Grey-box attacks
(
e.g.,

[
50
,
60
]
) fall between white-box and black-box scenarios, where attackers have partial knowledge of the MFM system. Some knowledge, such as the encoder used for specific modalities or general architectural components, may be readily accessible and common across different MFM systems. Grey-box attacks also consider the possibility that attackers may be aware of well-known defense mechanisms employed by the system, such as filters or detectors, which serve as additional functional blocks within the MFM architecture.

IV-C

Information Flows

Fig. 3:
An illustration of information flows in MFM system (represented by arrows).

Multimodal learning presents distinct challenges and vulnerabilities compared to unimodal systems, which manifest through various information flows within the model, as well as interactions between models (as shown in Figure
3
). Here, we categorize these flows and the associated adversary’s capabilities.

At the model level, the information flows include prediction, learning, and inferring.

•

Prediction information flow
(
e.g.,

[
61
,
41
]
) involves feeding multimodal inputs into a trained model, to generate predictions or outputs, where the inputs are digitized and encoded into latent space representations However, adversarial attacks can introduce malicious inputs that deceive the model, leading to incorrect or biased results.

•

Learning information flow
(
e.g.,

[
43
]
) refers to the process of training or fine-tuning a multimodal model using input from a training dataset. An attacker may inject incorrect or malicious samples into the training dataset, causing the model to mislearn and resulting in incorrect predictions or behavior.

•

Inferring information flow
(
e.g.,

[
51
,
49
]
) refers to the path of information in certain attacks where attackers can reverse-engineer model outputs to infer sensitive data or use malicious queries to extract confidential information.

From the system perspective, the information flows between various components, such as models, databases, and applications, can be categorized as:

•

Information flow between agents and applications
(
e.g.,

[
55
,
56
]
) refers to how agents within a system carry out actions based on the model’s outputs. Attackers may exploit this flow to misdirect the agent’s actions, causing unintended or harmful behaviors.

•

Information flow between multi-agents
(
e.g.,

[
62
,
63
]
) involves the communication and coordination between agents within a system. In multi-agent environments, adversaries can exploit this interaction by injecting false information or instructions that propagate throughout the system, disrupting coordination and enabling harmful behaviors to spread across otherwise secure agents.

•

Information flow between agent and system memory
(
e.g.,

[
58
,
64
]
) refers to how agents store, retrieve, and rely on historical data for decision-making. Attackers may target the integrity of this memory by manipulating stored information or introducing false data.

Taxonomy.

Traditionally, safety risks and security attacks have been categorized based on their observable effects rather than the underlying technical vulnerabilities. Terms like “adversarial attack”, “jailbreak attack”, and “membership inference attack” focus on the outcomes of these threats but do not directly express the root causes in the learning process.
We argue that categorizing these risks based on the underlying vulnerable points offers clearer insights into how the learning process is exploited. Specifically, we propose classifying threats in MFMs according to the information flows they target: prediction, learning, and inferring for model safety threats, and agent action, interaction, and memory flows for system security.
This unifies safety and security concerns under a single framework.

V
Attacks against Model Safety

In this section, we introduce a taxonomy of model-level safety threats in MFMs. We categorize safety attacks against MFMs into three types: input misleading, model mislearning, and output inferring attacks, each addressing specific vulnerable information flows within the multimodal models. We present the taxonomy of safety threats in Figure
4
and summarize related studies in Table
II
.

V-A

Misleading Attacks

{forest}

Fig. 4:
The taxonomy of safety threats at model level in MFMs.

Misleading attacks aim to intentionally deceive multimodal models during their inference processes, resulting in incorrect or manipulated predictions. These attacks exploit weaknesses in the model’s decision-making by providing carefully crafted inputs designed to confuse or misdirect the model. They can be executed through various methods, including adversarial examples, prompt manipulations, or subtle data alterations that influence how the model interprets multimodal inputs. A misleading attack exploits inherent errors in machine learning caused by discrepancies between the training samples and ground truth. By manipulating the inputs, the attackers push adversarial samples into this error zone. Figure
6
in the Appendix shows an example of misleading attacks.

Several recent studies have explored various types of misleading attacks, which can be categorized into two main approaches: perturbation-based and structure-based attacks. From an information theory perspective, perturbation-based attacks aim to increase the noise
N
in the system, effectively degrading the channel capacity
C
and reducing the model’s performance by distorting the input signal. This added perturbation leads to unreliable or erroneous predictions by disrupting the clarity of the input. Structure-based attacks, on the other hand, manipulate the signal
S
itself, such as embedding text signals into images, exploiting differences in how models process various modalities. For example, a model’s safety alignment mechanism may be sensitive to text inputs but less effective at detecting malicious content in images, even when both convey the same information. The actual
S
recognized by the image modality could be smaller than through text, further reducing the channel capacity
C
. Both types of attacks are covert, designed to mislead the model during inference while avoiding detection.

Perturbation-based attacks.
These attacks aim to induce incorrect predictions, which adversaries can exploit, raising significant security concerns. For instance, VLAttack
[
41
]
generates perturbations on images and, if ineffective, shifts to text. This method leverages pre-trained vision-language models to enhance adversarial transferability across modalities. Schlarmann
et al.

[
42
]
introduce a white-box attack on images to manipulate captions generated by multimodal models. Zhao
et al.

[
65
]
focus on the most vulnerable modality,
e.g.,
images, targeting pretrained models like CLIP and BLIP. These adversarial examples are transferable across VLMs. Dong
et al.

[
66
]
create adversarial examples that mislead popular models, such as Bard, by attacking white-box surrogate vision encoders. Cao
et al.

[
67
]
examine adversarial attacks on referring expression comprehension (REC) tasks, where perturbations mislead object identification. Dou
et al.

[
68
]
further refine this by transforming inputs to minimize embedding deviations, making adversarial attacks harder to detect. Other studies, like Zhang
et al.

[
69
]
, add noise to one modality to force its embedding to align with another, deceiving the model by exploiting cross-modal perturbations.

Some research shifts focus to adversarial attacks triggering toxic outputs,
i.e.,
jailbreaking. Carlini
et al.

[
30
]
show how continuous-domain adversarial images can prompt toxic content from language models, bypassing safety mechanisms. Qi
et al.

[
31
]
demonstrate how a single visual adversarial example combined with harmful instructions can jailbreak LLMs. Bagdasaryan
et al.

[
71
]
blend adversarial perturbations into images or audio, which steer the model towards attacker-chosen outputs. Niu
et al.

[
72
]
enhance attack transferability, generating adversarial images that consistently jailbreak MLLMs across different prompts and models, using a maximum likelihood-based approach.
Bailey
et al.

[
73
]
discuss “image hijacks”, where adversarial images manipulate model outputs, causing safety and security vulnerabilities like disinformation, context leakage, and jailbreaks. At a technical level, these attacks show how perturbations in the input space can mislead the model toward various vulnerabilities.
An additional focus is increasing prediction latency. Gao
et al.

[
32
]
introduce verbose images that slow down sequence generation, affecting model efficiency. Baras
et al.

[
33
]
propose QuantAttack, targeting quantized models by slowing their inference process and increasing memory usage and energy consumption.

Recent studies target both image and text modalities to enhance misleading effectiveness. Zhang
et al.

[
61
]
propose Co-Attack, jointly perturbing image and text modalities in vision-language pretrained models (VLPs). Yang
et al.

[
70
]
show that combined attacks on text and images can bypass safeguards, like prompt filters and NSFW detectors.
With increased effectiveness of multimodal perturbations, research further examines transferability across models. Wang
et al.

[
74
]
tailor modality consistency and discrepancy features, focusing perturbations on key image regions while altering relevant text. Luo
et al.

[
75
]
optimize cross-prompt adversarial transferability, adjusting perturbations in text and images to maximize impact. Lu
et al.

[
76
]
introduce Set-level Guidance Attack (SGA), which maintains adversarial effectiveness across black-box models through alignment-preserving augmentation.
Finally, AnyDoor
[
45
]
proposes a test-time backdoor attack that adds perturbations to both image and text. This backdoor effect activates only when perturbations in both modalities meet, creating a backdoor without altering training data.

Insight 2:

Perturbation-based attacks seek to introduce carefully crafted noise into model inputs. In unimodal perturbations, noise is often generated based on model gradients to create samples that cross decision boundaries. In contrast, multimodal attacks typically focus on disrupting the alignment between different modalities. Since this alignment is often achieved through contrastive learning, it makes the model’s decision boundaries less distinct and more susceptible to exploitation.

Structure-based attacks.

Structure-based attacks manipulate input format or structure to deceive models. For example, FigStep
[
59
]
embeds harmful textual content into images using typography to bypass textual safety mechanisms, avoiding direct harmful text input. Similarly, Zong
et al.

[
77
]
show that simple structural changes, like shuffling answer choices in multiple-choice prompts, mislead models, highlighting their vulnerability to adversarial permutations in answer sets.
Shayegani
et al.

[
78
]
introduce a gradient-based attack that updates images to match embeddings of malicious triggers in the joint embedding space. This method combines images with generic prompts to perform jailbreaks, using triggers like textual, OCR-based, visual, and combined textual-visual triggers. Ma
et al.

[
79
]
explore embedding harmful content in images and introduce “Role-play” into MLLM jailbreaks. Li
et al.

[
80
]
focus on amplifying malicious intent in text prompts (
e.g.,
“conduct the behavior in the image”) using typography and perturbations to enhance attack effectiveness.

Insight 3:

Structure-based attacks exploit vulnerabilities by manipulating the structure of input signals across different modalities. Recent research emphasizes how these attacks can evade safety-alignment mechanisms. Samples that appear harmless in a single modality may produce harmful outcomes when combined in a multimodal input, further complicating the development of effective defenses.

Insight 4:

Misleading attacks exploit vulnerabilities in probabilistic machine learning, particularly the error zone between training data and ground truth. Without a reasoning mechanism, models cannot perfectly learn modality mappings, so manipulating signals or noise will always reveal breakpoints.

V-B

Mislearning Attacks

Mislearning attacks target the training phase of multimodal models with the intention of corrupting the model’s learning process. Attackers inject fake, poisoned, or backdoor samples into the training data, causing the model to learn incorrect patterns or behaviors. This can lead to models that not only produce inaccurate predictions but also exhibit hidden vulnerabilities, such as triggering specific behaviors when exposed to particular inputs during inference (
e.g.,
backdoors). The attacker’s goal is to alter the model’s internal representations and understanding of the data, resulting in behavior that deviates from the designers’ intentions. Figure
7
in Appendix shows an example of mislearning attacks.

Mismatching-based attacks.

Mismatching-based attacks mismatch the modality pairings in the training samples, such as pairing the text “dog” with an image of a cat, to force the model to learn incorrect mappings. From an information theory perspective, this reduces the effective signal
S
to the model by introducing mismatches between modalities. For example, Shan
[
43
]
demonstrates a simple “dirty-label” poisoning attack against diffusion models by exploiting concept sparsity (only a few samples are associated with a specific concept or prompt). Similarly, Shadowcast
[
44
]
showcases a traditional label attack on VLM models, and both studies propose stronger attacks by adding image perturbations to the poisoned data for increased stealth.
ImgTrojan
[
81
]
differs from these approaches by focusing specifically on jailbreak attacks. It uses clean images as trojans, associating them with malicious jailbreak prompts via poisoned training, setting it apart from other backdoor attacks.

Optimized mismatching attacks.

These attacks introduce perturbations (
N
) to one or more modalities to push poisoned samples closer to the target concept in latent/alignment space. For instance, Shan
[
43
]
maximizes poison potency by introducing guided perturbations that push poisoned images toward “anchor images” in the feature space. Shadowcast
[
44
]
leverages VLMs’ text generation capabilities to craft persuasive, rational-sounding narratives, which skew the learned concepts in the model.

Trigger-injected attacks.

Trigger-injected attacks rely on specific patterns within images or text to activate an incorrect output, which inject created fake signal
S
into the model, thus compromising the model learning. For example, BadT2I
[
46
]
performs backdoor attacks at three levels,
i.e.,
pixel, object, and style, targeting vision semantics. BadDiffusion
[
82
]
engineers compromised diffusion processes to implant backdoors.
Chen
et al.

[
83
]
extend this further with TrojDiff, introducing a diffusion process that biases the generative model during training for effective backdoor implantation. For VLMs, VL-Trojan
[
84
]
uses an isolating and clustering strategy to enhance image trigger learning, while BadCLIP
[
85
]
aligns visual trigger patterns with textual semantics using a Bayesian-inspired framework, making it harder to unlearn backdoors.
Efficient backdoor attacks are also gaining focus. Han
et al.

[
86
]
propose the first data- and computation-efficient multimodal backdoor attacks, highlighting the growing sophistication of backdoor attack strategies.

Insight 5:

Mislearning attacks in multimodal scenarios exploit signal mismatches across modalities and use stealthy perturbations to push poisoned samples toward target concepts in the alignment space. More critically, trigger patterns in multimodal backdoors can be optimized based on multiple modalities simultaneously or focused solely on the most vulnerable modality, enabling stronger attacks with fewer manipulations.

V-C

Inferring Attacks

Inferring attacks focus on extracting private or sensitive information from the model by analyzing its outputs. These attacks exploit the model’s responses to uncover hidden patterns or sensitive data that were used during training or are embedded within the model’s architecture. In multimodal models, such attacks can be particularly harmful due to the diverse and rich nature of the data on which the models are trained.
In the context of information theory, the model can be viewed as a communication channel that transmits information learned from the training data, with its output serving as a signal that contains both valuable information and noise. The attacker seeks to maximize their understanding of the private or sensitive data based on the model’s outputs. Specifically, the attacker aims to filter out the noise to reverse-engineer the input signal,
i.e.,
increasing the signal-to-noise ratio (
S/N
), allowing them to extract more meaningful insights about the private data.

Prompt-based attacks.

One of the pioneering works in prompt-based data extraction in MFMs is by Carlini
et al.

[
49
]
, who use a generate-and-filter pipeline to extract over a thousand training examples from state-of-the-art models, particularly diffusion models.

Similarity-based inferring attacks.

Membership inference attacks (MIAs) have also been explored in MFMs. M4I
[
50
]
introduces two methods, metric-based and feature-based attacks. The feature-based M4I uses a pre-trained shadow multimodal feature extractor to compare input and output in feature space, allowing the attacker to infer whether data was part of the model’s training set. Similarly, Ko
[
51
]
assumes the attacker can query the model with pairs of images and captions and, based on the cosine similarity between image and text in alignment feature space, identifies membership status. EncoderMI
[
87
]
focuses on image encoders, analyzing similarities in feature vectors between augmented image samples.
CLiD
[
60
]
identifies a conditional overfitting phenomenon in text-to-image diffusion models, where models tend to overfit the conditional distribution of images based on corresponding text, rather than the marginal distribution of images alone, revealing a unique vulnerability in the context of membership inference.

Distribution-based attacks.

Prompt stealing is another form of inferring attack. Yang
[
47
]
proposes a two-phase process—prompt mutation and prompt pruning, where the attacker gradually infers target prompts by analyzing the critical features of input-output pairs. Shen
[
48
]
goes further by considering both the subject and modifiers in prompts to diffusion models. A subject generator infers the subject prompt, while a modifier detector deduces the modifier prompts within the generated image.

Insight 6:

Multimodal contrastive learning functions by pulling together features to create matching pairs within the alignment space. This introduces a new vulnerability for privacy exposure, as attackers can directly exploit the alignment between modalities, rather than relying solely on inferring model outputs.

Insight 7:

The unique architecture of multimodal models, especially their feature alignment and aggregation mechanisms, opens the door to a broader range of model-level attacks. These attacks can be executed more subtly and with greater ease, underscoring the need for stronger regulatory measures to ensure effective oversight and protection.

VI
Security Attacks at System Level

{forest}

Fig. 5:
The taxonomy of security threats at system level in MFMs.

MFMs are increasingly used to build autonomous multimodal agents for real-world environments. However, recent studies show that these agents introduce new security risks
[
105
,
5
,
106
,
53
]
.
In this section, we categorize system-level security attacks into three types based on targeted information flows. The first category, attacks on agent actions, involves manipulating agents’ behavior by exploiting limited bandwidth constraints and impairing agents’ information processing. The second category, attacks on agent interactions, involves compromising communication bandwidth, causing miscoordination between agents. The third category, attacks on system memory information flows, targets the storage, retrieval, and integrity of data.

VI-A

Attacks on Agent Actions

Several attacks on agent actions exploit vulnerabilities at the model level, such as misleading attacks, to misdirect agents’ behavior in multimodal systems. Wu
et al.

[
52
]
show how adversarial text strings combined with perturbations of trigger images can deceive multimodal agents. Similarly, Fu
et al.

[
88
]
reveal that obfuscated adversarial prompts can transfer to production-level agents, exposing vulnerabilities in real-world deployments. ROBOPAIR
[
89
]
presents an algorithm that jailbreaks LLM-controlled robots, triggering harmful actions by targeting their language models.

Prompt injection attacks represent another form of misdirection, where malicious data is injected into multimodal agents. Perez
et al.

[
55
]
explore goal hijacking and prompt leaking, where attackers alter model objectives and extract confidential information through manipulated prompts. Liu
et al.

[
54
]
formalize and benchmark prompt injection techniques, demonstrating their ability to compromise model security. Pedro
et al.

[
56
]
study prompt-to-SQL injections, which lead to malicious SQL code generation, a serious system-wide risk.

Indirect prompt injection attacks let adversaries exploit multimodal models remotely by embedding prompts in data retrieved at inference time. Greshake
et al.

[
38
]
show how maliciously embedded prompts can trigger arbitrary code execution, manipulating application functionality. Wu
et al.

[
39
]
and WIPI
[
40
]
explore indirect prompt injections that exploit web data to mislead web agents. Nakash
et al.

[
90
]
show that even benign requests can trigger indirect prompt injections, increasing the likelihood of subsequent malicious actions.
Dong
et al.

[
91
]
investigate exploiting low-rank adapters to control LLMs, compromising systems by executing malicious instructions.

Insight 8:

Attacks on agent actions exploit vulnerabilities at the model level to misdirect multimodal agents.
These attacks compromise or bypass the bandwidth constraints in information flow, leading to misinformation and breaking the integrity of the system, ultimately causing harmful outcomes.

VI-B

Attacks on Agent Interactions

In this subsection, we explore attacks on agent interactions, focusing on how adversaries can bypass bandwidth constraints to compromise multi-agent systems.

Tan
et al.

[
62
]
investigate infectious attacks, where jailbreaking a single agent spreads harmful behaviors to others in the system. They demonstrate that an MLLM agent, manipulated to generate specific prompts, can “infect” other agents in a society of MLLMs. Similarly, Gu
et al.

[
92
]
simulate multi-agent environments with up to one million LLaVA-1.5 agents, showing that feeding an adversarial image into any agent’s memory can trigger an infectious jailbreak, spreading harm across the system.

Weeks
et al.

[
63
]
explore toxicity injection targeting chatbots, focusing on Dialog-based Learning (DBL), where attackers inject toxic content into a chatbot’s training data, leading to harmful outputs in future interactions. Yu
et al.

[
94
]
and Huang
et al.

[
93
]
investigate topological properties that enhance the safety and resilience of multi-agent systems, showing that malicious agents can introduce subtle errors too stealthy for non-specialized agents to detect, posing significant security risks.

Prompt infection is another threat, where LLM-to-LLM prompt injection spreads malicious instructions across multi-agent systems, disrupting information flow. Lee
et al.

[
95
]
introduce privacy leaking attacks, where malicious prompts replicate across agents, exposing private information. This study highlights the vulnerability of multi-agent systems, even when agents do not publicly share all communications.

Insight 9:

In the absence of carefully designed bandwidth constraints on system-level information flow, threats and compromises can propagate throughout a system, amplifying their impact and compromising overall system integrity.

VI-C

Attacks on System Memory

In many commercial multi-agent systems, Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) serves as external memory for multimodal foundation models (MFMs), enhancing their ability to provide real-time, accurate information. RAG enables agents to retrieve relevant data from continuously updated databases, improving information flow between inputs, memory, and outputs. This ensures generative models access up-to-date content, crucial for practical applications. However, RAG introduces vulnerabilities in information flow, as adversaries may target memory to introduce disinformation or exploit bandwidth constraints. This section explores how attacks on RAG systems compromise the reliability and security of multimodal agents.

Attacks on model memory in RAG systems exploit vulnerabilities, introducing disinformation or bypassing constraints to leak sensitive data. Poisoning techniques, a common method, mislead RAG systems into providing incorrect outputs. Zhong
[
58
]
proposes an attack where adversarial passages are fine-tuned to resemble specific queries and injected into the retrieval corpus. PoisonedRAG
[
57
]
optimizes malicious text embeddings to guide the model into generating responses aligned with attacker goals. Even injecting a few malicious texts per question in a large database can yield high success. HijackRAG
[
97
]
uses a similar approach, while GARAG
[
98
]
introduces a genetic attack to simulate errors like typos to disrupt retrieval. Long
[
96
]
proposes a backdoor attack embedding hidden triggers in RAG, prompting false outputs like hate speech. Cho
[
98
]
introduces Morris II, an adversarial prompt that reproduces the input as output, delivering malicious payloads.

RAG systems also face privacy risks from leakage attacks bypassing bandwidth constraints. Membership inference attacks, such as Huang
[
103
]
, expose privacy risks, where the retrieval mechanism inadvertently leaks data. S2MIA
[
104
]
exploits semantic similarities in generated outputs to infer whether specific data were used in training. Successful extraction could lead to privacy breaches. Data extraction attacks further exploit RAG systems. Zeng
[
64
]
induces the model to generate private data using specific queries. Qi
[
100
]
exploits instruction-following to extract verbatim text through prompt injection. Liu
[
101
]
masks words in documents, prompting the RAG system to predict and reveal private content. Anderson
[
102
]
demonstrates how crafted queries can determine membership status, exposing privacy vulnerabilities.

Insight 10:

Attacks targeting system memory compromise the bandwidth constraints between the model and its external memory (RAG). Poisoning attacks inject disinformation, manipulating the data retrieved, while privacy attacks bypass constraints to leak sensitive information. However, current attacks on system memory focus primarily on the text modality.

Insight 11:

Multimodal agent-enabled systems create complex system-level security challenges, shifting from human-centered to agent-involved security and introducing new vulnerabilities due to loose control over interaction information flows.

VII
Defenses and Mitigation Strategies

In this section, we review defense methods and mitigation strategies against safety and security attacks in multimodal models.

VII-A

Defenses at Model Level

Defenses based on input purifying.

Purification methods are commonly used to filter out and counter perturbation-based attacks (
i.e.,
decreasing
N
). For image adversarial noise, techniques like bit reduction
[
34
]
lower pixel precision to remove subtle noise, while JPEG compression
[
35
]
can discard high-frequency perturbations.
Neural network-based approaches like Neural Representation Purifier (NRP)
[
107
]
restore perturbed images using an encoder-decoder framework. Diffusion models, including DensePure
[
108
]
and DiffPure
[
109
]
, enhance robustness with improved purification methods. For text perturbations, tools like LanguageTool
[
110
]
can be used to restore altered text, while GuardT2I
[
47
]
purifies text in Text-to-Image models using language models. Alternatively, AdvPT
[
111
]
boosts robustness by aligning adversarial image embeddings with learnable text prompts.
On another hand, several defenses focus on strengthen the input signal
S
. GuardT2I
[
47
]
purifies structure-based attacks in T2I models by converting text embeddings into natural language using large language models. Yang
et al.

[
112
]
introduced RoCLIP, which dynamically rematches images with the most similar text from a pool, breaking the association between poisoned image-caption pairs.

However, the model loss method showed poor performance, likely due to the dataset’s image complexity, where sample losses varied more than the differences between poisoned and clean samples.
Feature space similarity presented a different result: it achieved a perfect DR of 100% for the Nightshade attack but only 26% for Object-Backdoor. We hypothesize that this is because Nightshade perturbations push images toward the decision boundary, rather than the center of the target concept category.

VII-B

Safeguards at System Level in Theory and Practice

Existing safeguards applying bandwidth constraints.

Recent studies propose applying bandwidth constraints to either the inputs or outputs of MFMs. NotInject
[
131
]
introduces a prompt guard method to reduce bias in detecting malicious trigger words during prompt injection attacks. MLLM-Protector
[
132
]
combines image detection, adaptive alignment, and dynamic strategies to defend MFMs from visual input attacks. AdaShield
[
133
]
uses adaptive defense prompts to help models identify and resist malicious content in images. On the output side, JailGuard
[
111
]
applies divergence-based detection to filter malicious outputs. FATH
[
134
]
takes this further by requiring MFMs to label each response with a hash-based authentication tag to ensure output integrity.

Our perspective.

In addition to existing approaches, we propose that applying bandwidth constraints at the system level offers several advantages for addressing safety and security challenges in MFMs. One key advantage is that, thanks to the capabilities of powerful multimodal agents, enforcing bandwidth constraints system-wide with broader security objectives may be simpler and more effective than optimizing defenses for specific attacks. Furthermore, due to the probabilistic and data-driven nature of current machine learning models, rather than reasoning-based mechanisms, it is nearly impossible to eliminate vulnerabilities purely through model-level protections. Relying solely on such protections is often insufficient and sometimes ineffective. Our experimental results reported in Section
VII
show that while defenses mitigate some of the impact of attacks, they remain inadequate and come with significant costs. Furthermore, in practice, real-world commercial models also adopt similar strategies that resonate with our perspective. GitHub Copilot encourages developers to thoroughly review generated code and use GitGuardian Secrets Detection
[
135
,
136
]
, a system-level approach that provides automated security protection throughout the DevOps cycle. Meanwhile, OpenAI has identified potential risks at every stage of MLaaS (Machine Learning as a Service)
[
137
]
and is committed to building system-level safeguards, such as automation to restrict training data, safety evaluations, and red teaming during the development phase
[
138
]
.

System compartmentalization.

To address system-level security issues in practice, we believe that compartmentalization, a concept borrowed from the software domain, could be a potential solution for safeguarding MFMs. In software systems, compartmentalization involves dividing the system into isolated components or modules, each with its own distinct set of permissions and controls. This limits the potential damage from an attack, as the compromise of one compartment does not automatically expose others. Key techniques include restricting access to sensitive resources, enforcing boundaries between processes, and controlling communication pathways to prevent unintended interactions or information leaks.
In the context of MFMs, system compartmentalization starts with ensuring the safety of each isolated component at the model level. However, achieving flawless protection at this level is challenging, as shown in previous discussions and our experimental results. Therefore, the key to effective compartmentalization lies in managing the flow of information across the system as a whole. This includes controlling interactions between agents and applications, agents and system memory, and between different agents themselves. Compartmentalization at system level involves implementing access control mechanisms and information transmission constraints on the channel bandwidth that regulate how data moves through the system and who or what can access particular parts of it.

VIII
Promising Directions for Future Research

In this section, we propose several promising directions for future research on the safety and security of MFMs.

Attacks and defenses considering alignment space.
Recent studies, including our own experiments, show that attacks targeting multiple modalities simultaneously can be more potent than those focused on a single modality. This is because perturbations across modalities can amplify their effects in the alignment space, lowering the attack power required for each modality. This makes such attacks stealthier and more effective. From an information theory perspective, manipulation across multiple modalities attacks the system’s information flow, compromising the integrity and alignment of information between modalities. Future research should focus on understanding how adversarial perturbations propagate through these information flows and across the alignment space. Defenses should consider this space and how attacks spread through information channels, ensuring protection strategies address cross-modal interactions and their combined impact on the final output.

Emerging threats in agent-enabled systems.
The rise of agent-enabled multimodal systems, which integrate complex models like MFMs to perform various tasks, introduces new safety and security risks. The interaction between different types of information flows (input, model, output, agent action, agent interaction, memory), varying channel capacities, and attacker goals creates opportunities for novel attacks not yet fully explored. Adversaries may exploit bottlenecks or vulnerabilities in model alignment to introduce new forms of manipulation or data leakage. Our proposed framework can help identify these new threats by analyzing the interactions between flows and attack vectors, ensuring that vulnerabilities are systematically evaluated.

Holistic defense strategies.
Current defense strategies focus on protecting specific model-level vulnerabilities or single modalities, often overlooking broader system-level risks. Protecting multimodal systems effectively requires a comprehensive approach that considers all information flows. This means not only reinforcing model-level defenses but also strengthening the full spectrum of information flow to minimize noise and apply constraints on bandwidth between system components. Balancing these factors is crucial for preventing attacks while maintaining performance. Effective defenses should aim to reduce trade-offs between robustness and performance, ensuring protections in one flow do not weaken another part of the system. Future research should develop integrated defense strategies that optimize security across both model and system levels, ensuring multimodal systems are resilient to various threats.

IX
Conclusion

This paper presents our SoK on the cybersafety and cybersecurity of MFMs. By applying an information-theoretic framework, we provide a novel perspective on evaluating and classifying threats, allowing us to unify concepts of model safety and system security in MFMs.
This offers a deeper understanding of the risks that arise from integrating multiple modalities. We examined existing defense mechanisms and highlighted critical gaps, particularly the lack of protection for cross-modal alignment and the need for more comprehensive, system-level defenses. We believe that future research should focus on addressing these gaps to ensure the secure and reliable deployment of MFMs across various applications. Although uncertainties about the defense and mitigation strategies for potential threats in MFMs remain, this SoK serves as a proof of concept, aiming to foster structured discussions on unifying the cybersecurity and cybersafety of MFMs.
