Chromosomal-level genome assembly of minute pirate bug Orius nagaii Yasunaga, 1993 (Hemiptera: Anthocoridae)
Authors: Zhenjuan Yin, Chengxing Wang, Dingyin Li, Ruijuan Wang, Xiaoyan Dai, Li Zheng, Junrui Zhi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Species of the genus Orius, diminutive predatory insects that act as natural enemies of other arthropods, are frequently employed in agricultural pest management for controlling various pests, such as thrips, mites, aphids, whiteflies, etc. However, the scarcity of high-quality genomic resources for these predators hinders our comprehension of their population evolution and predation ecology. Consequently, we assembled and annotated a chromosomal-scale genome of Orius nagaii by collating PacBio and Illumina sequencing and Hi-C genomic analysis techniques. The final genome assembly size 152.62 Mb, with scaffold and contig N50 lengths of 11.53 and 2.39 Mb, respectively. It is organized into 12 pairs of autosomes and a pair of XY sex chromosomes. The quality assessment of the genomic data with BUSCO revealed a completeness of 98.5% (n = 1,367). Also, 11,917 protein-coding genes were discovered, with 94.28% of them having functional annotations. The high-quality genome of O. nagaii produced serves as a valuable resource for comprehending the interactions between predatory natural enemies and hosts, along with their evolutionary trajectories.

Background & Summary

Orius Wolff, the largest genus within the family Anthocoridae, consists of approximately 80 described species across the Oriental, Ethiopian, Palaearctic, Neotropical, and Nearctic regions, renowned for their well-established efficacy in agricultural pest management1,2. Certain species of Orius, such as Orius nagaii, are recognized as versatile predators whose nymphs and adults exhibit predatory behavior towards thrips, mites, aphids, whiteflies, and other minor pests3–7. Moreover, they demonstrate substantial control over the eggs and larvae of different lepidopteran pests8–10. As a result, O. nagaii serves as an essential biological asset for agricultural pest control, contributing significantly to pesticide reduction, ensuring agricultural product quality, and promoting environmental safety.

The rapid progress in genome sequencing technology and omics research has greatly expanded the genome resources accessible for natural enemy insects in the past few years11. This advancement represents a substantial milestone in the study of natural enemies, offering new opportunities to advance the concepts and methods of biocontrol12. However, the existing genetic resources for the predatory natural enemy Orius species are restricted, with only the genomes of three minute pirate bugs having been documented, named Orius laevigatus, Orius insidiosus, and Orius strigicollis12–14. Among these, only O. strigicollis has attained a chromosomal-level genome assembly, although the sex chromosomes remain unassembled14. The scarcity of high-quality genomes significantly hinders our exploration of “pest-control genes” during the interaction between natural enemy insects and hosts.

Here, we applied a combination of multiplatform sequencing approaches, specifically Illumina NovaSeq 6000 and PacBio Sequel IIe platform sequencing, and Hi-C genomic analysis technology to create a detailed genome map of O. nagaii at the chromosome level and the assembly of sex chromosomes was completed for the first time. Our purpose is to offer crucial reference information for discovering functional genes related to predator-host interactions and identify resistance genes useful for genetic improvement in insects.

Methods

Insect preparation and sequencing

The O. nagaii population was collected, originating in the rearing site of the Shandong Academy of Agricultural Sciences in Jinan, Shandong, China, in 2019. They were consistently cultivated at 26 ± 1 °C, 70 ± 5% relative humidity, under a 16/8 h (light/dark) cycle at the Key Laboratory of Natural Enemies Insects, Ministry of Agriculture and Rural Areas, China. Nymphs and adults were provided with Sitotroga cerealella eggs as food, while the females utilized fresh Phaseolus vulgaris as the substrate for oviposition.

Genomic DNA was extracted from 200 adult males using the FastPure Cell/Tissue DNA Isolation Mini Kit (Vazyme, Nanjing, China). The SMRTbell® Express Template Prep Kit 2.0 (Pacific Biosciences, PN 101-853-100) was used to prepare a 20 kb PacBio HiFi sequencing library. Sequencing was conducted on the PacBio Sequel IIe platform. We used the TruSeq DNA PCR-free kit (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) to prepare the Illumina whole genome sequencing (WGS) library, which contained 150 bp paired-end reads and a 350 bp insert element. The Hi-C process followed a widely recognized protocol, involving formaldehyde cross-linking (formaldehyde solution #F809702, Macklin, Shanghai, China), restriction enzyme MboI digestion (NEBNext Dpnll Restriction Endonuclease, #R0543L, Ipswich, MA, USA), end repair(NEBNext Ultra II End Repair/dA-Tailing, #E7546L, Ipswich, MA, USA), DNA cyclization, and DNA purification(AMPure XP Beads, Pacific Biosciences)15. Total RNA was isolated from 90 adult males using Trizol reagent (Vazyme, Nanjing, China) following the manufacturer’s protocol16. We used the TruSeq RNA v2 kit (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) to construct RNA sequencing libraries. Whole-genome, Hi-C, and transcriptome sequencing were subsequently performed on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform. All Illumina library preparation and sequencing were performed at Berry Genomics (Beijing, China). In the end, we generated 13.36 Gb (87.53×) of PacBio HiFi reads, 16.12 Gb (105.64×) of Illumina reads, 20.27 Gb (132.83×) of Hi-C data, and 8.86 Gb of transcriptome data for our genome assembly. Genome size assessment

To facilitate the subsequent selection of suitable assembly tools and parameter adjustment, genome survey was conducted to estimate genome size, heterozygosity, and the proportion of repetitive sequences. The Illumina genomic data was subjected to quality control using BBTools v38.8217. Identical sequences were removed with “clumpify.sh”, while “bbduk.sh” was employed to filter out low-quality sites (base quality scores < 20), sequences shorter than 15 bp, and poly-A/G/C tails longer than 10 bp, and to rectify bases using overlapping reads. Following this, k-mer analysis was conducted using GenomeScope v2.018 with parameters -k 21 -p 2 -m 1000. The predicted genome size was 151.67 Mb, heterozygosity was 1.04%, and repetitive sequences comprised approximately 29.13% (Fig. 1).Fig. 1The genome scope profile at 21-mer of O. nagaii.

Genome assembly

The initial assembly of high-quality HiFi reads was executed using Hifiasm v0.16.119. Contig sequences with a sequencing depth of over 10 × were preserved, while sequences with low sequencing depth that could be contaminated or erroneous were eliminated. Purge_Dups v1.2.520 was employed for alignment, utilizing the bam file sequences from Minimap2 v2.421 to eliminate heterozygous sequences and enhance assembly accuracy. The Hi-C data assembly commenced with quality control using Juicer v1.6.222 for Hi-C data evaluation, followed by scaffolding with default parameters using 3D-DNA v18092223. Manual refinement of the initial assembly was carried out with Juicebox v1.11.0822, and the final chromosome assembly results were generated with 3D-DNA. To detect potential contamination in the genome assembly, Blastn-like searches were performed using MMseq2 v1324 against NCBI Nucleotide25 and UniVec26 databases. Stringent filtering criteria automatically removed sequences showing >90% similarity to known contaminants, while sequences with 80–90% similarity were further verified by BLASTN against the NCBI nucleotide database. The final genome assembly passed NCBI’s automated contamination screening, revealing no significant contaminant sequences and thus reflecting high sample quality and sequencing accuracy.

Scaffolds longer than 10 kb from the O. nagaii genome were retained for downstream assembly evaluation and genome annotation. Following meticulous assembly and filtration, the assembly size was determined to be 152.62 Mb, comprising 67 scaffolds and 195 contigs (Table 2). The scaffold and contig N50 lengths stood at 11.53 Mb and 2.39 Mb, respectively, with a GC composition of 36.59% (Table 2). A sum of 150.91 Mb contigs were successfully mapped onto 14 chromosomes, reaching a robust loading efficiency of 98.88% (Fig. 2). Notably, the sequencing depth of the sex chromosomes ChrX and ChrY was significantly lower than that of the autosomes, a pattern supported by weaker interaction strength observed in the Hi-C heat map for ChrX and ChrY, and this observation is denoted by lighter colors in the corresponding regions (Fig. 2). Moreover, the X and Y chromosome were also detected by chromosome synteny between a hemipteran species, Anthocoris nemoralis. Syntenic blocks were identified and determined using D-Genies27. The results reveal strong homology between the X and Y chromosomes of O. nagaii and the X chromosome of A. nemoralis (Fig. 3). Moreover, the Hi-C contact heatmap clearly demonstrates that the X and Y chromosomes identified in this study represent two distinct chromosomes. In addition, previous karyotype studies have established that species of the genus Orius possess an XY sex determination system28. Genome annotation

The de novo repeat library for O. nagaii was generated using RepeatModeler v2.0.430 with an additional LTR search process (-LTRStruct) to identify specific repeat structures and predict new repeats. The Dfam 3.531 and RepBase-2018102632 databases were merged to form the final repeat sequence database. From this combined database, a subset of repeat sequences relevant to Hemiptera was extracted. Subsequently, RepeatMasker v4.1.433 was employed to predict repeat sequences based on the established repeat sequence database. The genome annotation statistics for repetitive elements revealed 282,741 repeat sequences, amounting to 47,952,711 base pairs, with repeated sequences making up 31.42% of the genome. The main repeat types included unclassified (25.13%), DNA transposon (1.86%), long-interspersed elements (LINEs, 1.69%), long-terminal repeats (LTRs, 1.14%), simple repeat (0.87%) and other elements (Fig. 5; Table 3).Fig. 5Visualization of genomic features of O. nagaii. Protein-coding gene structures were predicted using MAKER v3.01.0334, which integrates ab initio gene prediction, transcript evidence–based annotation, and homology-based prediction using protein sequences from related species. Ab initio gene prediction identifies putative genes solely from genomic sequence features without relying on external transcriptomic or proteomic evidence. In this study, gene-finding models were trained and optimized using combined transcriptomic and protein homology evidence. RNA-seq reads were aligned to the genome using HISAT2 v2.2.035, generating BAM alignment files that were subsequently assembled into reference-guided transcripts with StringTie v2.1.636. These transcript assemblies were used as evidence for both gene model training and annotation. BRAKER v2.1.637 was employed to autonomously train two de novo gene prediction tools, AUGUSTUS v3.3.438 and GeneMark-ES/ET/EP v4.68_3.60_lic39, incorporating arthropod protein sequences from OrthoDB v10.140 to improve prediction accuracy. In parallel, GeMoMa v1.8.341 was used for homology-based gene prediction by leveraging protein-coding gene models from six insect species downloaded from NCBI: Acyrthosiphon pisum (GCA_005508785.2)42, Apis mellifera (GCF_003254395.2)43, Bemisia tabaci (GCF_918797505.1)44, Drosophila melanogaster (GCF_000001215.4)45, Bombyx mori (GCF_014905235.1)46, and Tribolium castaneum (GCF_000002335.3)47. GeMoMa was run using the parameters gemoma.c = 0.4 and gemoma.p = 10. The prediction results from BRAKER and GeMoMa were incorporated into MAKER as ab initio training inputs. Following model optimization, ab initio predictions were performed and subsequently integrated with transcriptomic and protein homology evidence within the MAKER framework to generate the final gene annotation set. In total, 11,917 protein-coding genes were predicted, and BUSCO analysis indicated that the predicted protein sequences achieved a completeness of 97.8% (Table 3).

Gene functions were predicted by searching each gene using the sensitive mode (–very-sensitive-e 1e-5) in Diamond v2.0.1148. Then, the four databases—Pfam49, SMART50, Superfamily51, and CDD52—were referenced using InterProScan 5.53–87.053. Concurrently, the eggNOG v5.054 database was accessed via Eggnog-Mapper v2.1.555, jointly realizing the prediction of conserved protein sequences and domains, Gene Ontology (GO) terms, and pathways (KEGG, Reactome). The output data from the above predictions were consolidated to derive the final gene function annotation outcomes. Out of the total genome, 11,235 genes (94.28%) were aligned with UniProtKB entries. InterProScan delineated the protein domains of 9,975 protein-coding genes. The comprehensive genome structure (Fig. 5) was visualized using TBTools software56.

The annotation of non-coding RNAs (ncRNAs) was primarily conducted using Infernal v1.1.457 to align with the established ncRNA library (Rfam v14.10) and to achieve the annotation of ribosomal RNA (rRNA), small nuclear RNA (snRNA), microRNA (miRNA), and more. Additionally, tRNAscan-SE v2.0.958 was employed to predict transfer RNA (tRNA) sequences within the genome, with low-confidence tRNAs being filtered out by the ‘EukHighConfidenceFilter’ script. In total, 825 genomic ncRNAs were annotated, including 428 rRNAs, 51 miRNAs, 79 snRNAs, and 184 tRNAs.

Data Record

The raw reads and genome assembly have been archived in the NCBI database under accession PRJNA1243470. The identification numbers for PacBio, Illumina, Hi-C, and transcriptome data are SRX28754513-SRX2875451659. The assembled genome has been successfully submitted to the GenBank at NCBI and can be accessed through the accession number GCA_050947255.160. Data from the repeat analysis, along with annotated gene structures and functional predictions, are publicly accessible on Figshare61.

Technical Validation

The continuity and completeness of the genome assembly were assessed using BUSCO v5.5.062 with the Insecta gene set (n = 1,367) from OrthoDB v10, running in genome mode and employing MetaEuk as the gene predictor. The findings indicated an overall integrity of 98.5%, with 96.6% for single-copy genes and 1.9% for duplicate genes (Table 2). To further appraise assembly integrity, mapping rates were calculated by aligning Illumina and PacBio sequencing reads with the final assembly using Minimap2 and SAMtools. The mapping rates for Illumina and PacBio were determined to be 92.54% and 98.91%, respectively. Furthermore, BUSCO v5.5.0 assessment of the genome annotation revealed a high completeness of 97.8%, with 91.3% single-copy and 6.5% duplicate genes (Table 3). These assessments collectively affirmed the high quality of the genome assemblies generated in this research.
