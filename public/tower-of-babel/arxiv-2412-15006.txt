Towards plethystic 𝔰 ​ 𝔩 2 \mathfrak{sl}_{2} crystals Thanks: The author was funded by the University of Bristol Research Training Support Grant.
Álvaro Gutiérrez

Abstract
. To find crystals of 𝔰 ​ 𝔩 2 \mathfrak{sl}_{2} representations of the form Λ n ​ Sym r ​ ℂ 2 \Lambda^{n}\textup{Sym}^{r}\mathbb{C}^{2} it suffices to solve the combinatorial problem of decomposing the Young lattice into symmetric, saturated chains. We review the literature on this latter problem, and present a strategy to solve it. For n ≤ 4 n\leq 4 , the strategy recovers recently discovered solutions. We obtain (i) counting formulas for plethystic coefficients, (ii) new recursive formulas for plethysms of Schur functions, and (iii) formulas for the number of constituents of Λ n ​ Sym r ​ ℂ 2 \Lambda^{n}\textup{Sym}^{r}\mathbb{C}^{2} .



1.
Introduction

Consider the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}=\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(\mathbb{C})
with the natural action on
\mathbb{C}^{2}
.
Two classic facts are (i) the finite dimensional irreducible representations of
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}
are given by the symmetric powers
\Sym^{r}\mathbb{C}^{2}
for
r\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
, and (ii) if
V
is a representation of
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}
then so is the alternating power
\Alt^{n}V
for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
.

Problem A
.

Decompose
\Alt^{n}\Sym^{r}\mathbb{C}^{2}
into irreducible representations of
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}
.

This is one of the easiest cases of the problem of
plethysm
, and notoriously difficult to tackle
[
Sta00
,
COS+24
]
. Using classical notation, the problem asks to find the multiplicities
a_{1^{n}[r]}^{k}
fitting in

(1.1)

\Alt^{n}\Sym^{r}\mathbb{C}^{2}=\bigoplus_{k}(\Sym^{k}\mathbb{C}^{2})^{\oplus a_{1^{n}[r]}^{k}}.

As it is often in algebraic combinatorics, we ask for a solution that is
explicit and positive
, expressing
a_{1^{n}[r]}^{k}
as the cardinality of a set given by quasipolynomial equations and inequalities —this solves Problem
A
in the sense of
[
Sta00
,
Pak24
]
.

A solution to the deceptively similar problem of decomposing
\Sym^{a}\mathbb{C}^{2}\otimes\Sym^{b}\mathbb{C}^{2}
into irreducible representations of
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}
goes back to Clebsch and Gordan in the
xix
century
[
Gor87
]
. The tensor product problem is nowadays best understood through crystal theory, and Kashiwara’s tensor product rule
[
Kas90
]
.

For our purposes, an
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}
crystal is a directed graph attached to a representation of
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}
and whose vertices are weighted by elements of
\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{Z}
, see Figure
1(a)
. We follow
[
BS17
]
for the main concepts in crystals, but delay the details to §
3.2
.
If there is an arc
x\longrightarrow y
, then the weight of
y
satisfies
\wt(y)=\wt(x)-1
.
Decomposing a representation into irreducibles translates to decomposing its crystal into connected components.
For
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}
, each finite dimensional irreducible representation
\Sym^{k}\mathbb{C}^{2}
has a crystal which is a path graph in
k+1
vertices and weight-symmetric. For instance, the crystal in Figure
1(a)
decomposes as the crystal of
\Sym^{2}\mathbb{C}^{2}\oplus\Sym^{6}\mathbb{C}^{2}
.

\Alt^{2}\Sym^{4}\mathbb{C}^{2}
.

(b)

L(2,3)
.

Figure 1
.
Computing the crystal of
\Alt^{2}\Sym^{4}\mathbb{C}^{2}
shows
\Alt^{2}\Sym^{4}\mathbb{C}^{2}\cong\Sym^{2}\mathbb{C}^{2}\oplus\Sym^{6}\mathbb{C}^{2}

. The crystal can be computed by solving Problem

for
L(2,3)
.

Vertices of a crystal form a basis of the corresponding representation.
A canonical basis of

\Alt^{n}\Sym^{r}\mathbb{C}^{2}
is in bijection with the set
L(n,m)
of partitions whose Young diagram fits into an
n\times m
rectangle, where
r+1=m+n
. One such bijection is given in (

).
Endow
L(n,m)

with the partial order given by the
Young’s lattice (Figure
1(b)
); that is, with the covering relation
\lambda\lessdot\mu
if the Young diagram of
\lambda
is obtained from that of
\mu
by removing one box.

With these ingredients, a natural way of tackling Problem
A
is to solve the following:

Problem B
.

Decompose the poset
L(n,m)
into rank-symmetric, saturated chains.

In Figure
1
, we illustrate how to go from a solution to Problem
B
to a solution of Problem
A
.
We reiterate that, for these purposes,
we require an explicit solution to Problem
B
, in which the set of ‘highest weight elements’ of the decomposition (see §
3.2
) is fully described as a set given by some quasipolynomial equations and inequalities
[
OSSZ24
]
.

We begin by reviewing the literature for Problem
B
.
Our main contribution is to develop a crystal theoretic framework to tackle Problem
B
.
For
0\leq n\leq 4
, the framework produces explicit expressions for the crystal operators which are reminiscent of Kashiwara’s tensor product rule. In the language of posets, these are explicit matchings that produce symmetric chain decompositions of
L(n,m)
. We fully describe the highest weight elements, solving Problem
A
for
n\leq 4
.
We remark Problem
A
was already solved for
n\leq 4
(see
[
COS+24
]
for a short survey), and a solution for

Our constructions for Problem

2.
A literature review

2.1.
Symmetric chain decompositions of the Young lattice.

Stanley
[
Sta80
]
conjectures that a solution to Problem
B
exists, observing that it is true for
n\leq 2
. Unbeknownst to him at the time, solutions for all
n\leq 4
had been found by Rieß
[
Rie78
]
two years earlier.
This marks a precedent in the area that soon becomes a tradition.
Solutions for
n\!=\!3
by Lindström
[
Lin80
]
and
n\!=\!4
by West
[
Wes80
]
appeared shortly after, only acknowledging
[
Rie78
]
after the reviewing process. The constructions of Rieß do not coincide with those that came later; this too will be tradition.

Greene
[
Gre90
]
attributes to folklore that a greedy algorithm suffices to solve the problem, but shows that the approach is only successful for
n\leq 4
. Greene’s paper is significant, as it is the first attempt to solve the problem
with a single construction
, in which
n
is nothing more than a parameter. Again, the constructions are new and distinct.

Wen
[
Wen04
]
finds new computer-generated solutions to the problem for
n=3
and
4
based on a modified greedy algorithm.
Dhand
[
Dha14b
]
creates a framework in the language of tropical geometry that produces solutions for
0\leq n\leq 4
.

David, Spink, and Tiba
[
DST17
]
develop a geometric framework fitting the solutions of
[
Lin80
,
Wen04
,
Wes80
]
. Embed
L(n,m)
into
\mathbb{R}^{n}
by treating partitions as vectors. Then, dilate the embedding by
1/m
. For all
m
, the resulting set lies inside a fixed simplex
\Delta
of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, which is then divided into
regions
, each carrying a
direction
. A simultaneous solution for all
\{L(n,m):m\geq 0\}
is obtained from these regions, up to compatibility assumptions. Xin and Zhong
[
XZ21
]
study the applicability of a greedy algorithm to the problem, rediscovering the work of Greene.

In 2024 we see an explosion in interest for the problem. Most importantly, Wen
[
Wen24
]
manages to find computer-generated solutions to the
n\!=\!5
case: the first real progress after the conjecture was posed. However, their solution does not solve Problem
A
.
Coggins, Donley, Gondal, and Krishna
[
CDGK24
]
reframe Lindström’s construction in a diagrammatic way.

Orellana, Saliola, Schilling, and Zabrocki
[
OSSZ24
]
solve the problem for
n\!=\!3
and
4
. They attribute the abundance of different solutions to Problem
B
being too unrestricted, and impose further
desirable properties
to the decompositions.
They deduce counting formulas for
\smash{a_{1^{n}[r]}^{k}}
and recursive formulas for
\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{r+1}{n}
for
n\!=\!3
and
4
.

Our work maintains the tradition of rediscovery in the area. The bulk of the research was done independently of the authors above, and before some the mentioned works were released.
Our framework has characteristics in common with several of the papers in the literature:

(1)

The strategy is independent of
n
, as in
[
Dha14b
,
Gre90
,
XZ21
]
.

(2)

The framework is geometric in the sense of
[
DST17
]
.

(3)

The constructions obtained are explicit in the sense of
[
OSSZ24
]
and satisfy all of the additional desirable properties —some of which we do not impose a priori—, which allow us to show that the constructions are equivalent (Propositions
A.1
and
A.2
). This is the first instance that we know of two essentially different methods arriving to equivalent constructions.

The constructions presented here have only been shown to solve Problem
B
for
n\leq 4
.
The graphs have been implemented in SageMath
[
Sag22
]
and the decomposition of the character formulas into
q
-integers have been computed for
r\leq 100
. This highlights that the algorithms are computationally efficient.

2.2.
Gaussian coefficients and the Young lattice

A survey of Problems
A
and
B
would not be complete without a mention of some related problems. The remaining two parts of the survey are less exhaustive. We follow
[
Sta86
, Ch. 3]
for the background on posets.

A sequence
(a_{0},a_{1},\ldots,a_{k})
of integers is said to be
unimodal
if there is an element
a_{M}
such that
a_{0}\leq a_{1}\leq\cdots\leq a_{M}\geq\cdots\geq a_{k}
, and
symmetric
if
a_{i}=a_{k-i}
for all
i
.
A polynomial
f=\sum_{d=0}^{k}a_{d}x^{d}
is unimodal (resp. symmetric) if the sequence of its coefficients
(a_{0},a_{1},\ldots,a_{k})
is unimodal (resp. symmetric).
A poset
P
is
ranked
if it has a function
\textup{rk}:P\to\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
such that if
x\gtrdot y
then
\textup{rk}(y)=\textup{rk}(x)-1
.
A ranked poset is
rank-unimodal
(resp.
rank-symmetric
) if its rank-generating function
\sum_{x\in P}q^{\textup{rk}(x)}
is unimodal (resp. symmetric).
A ranked poset is
Sperner
if the largest incomparable subset is the size of the widest rank-level.

Fact

(
[
Sta80
]
)
.

A rank-symmetric poset admitting a decomposition into rank-symmetric saturated chains is rank-unimodal and Sperner.

The rank-generating function of
L(n,m)
is the polynomial in
q
obtained from the centred
q
-binomial as
q^{nm/2}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n+m}{n}
.
We refer to its coefficients as the
Gaussian coefficients
. That Gaussian coefficients form a unimodal sequence follows immediately from the bijection (
3.1
) between a basis of
\Alt^{n}\Sym^{r}\mathbb{C}^{2}
and
L(n,m)
—a homogeneous polynomial in
(x,y)
specialised to
(1,q)
is symmetric and unimodal if and only if it is the character of a representation of
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}

[
Sta85
]
—, but the result predates representation theory.
In the context of invariant theory and with different notation, the Gaussian coefficients were conjectured to form a unimodal sequence by Cayley in 1856, and proved for the first time by Sylvester in
[
Syl78
]
.
Gaussian coefficients are now known to be ‘strictly’ unimodal, see
[
PP13
]
.

The Sperner property of
L(n,m)
was established in the aforementioned
[
Sta80
]
using powerful tools from algebraic geometry. Stanley’s proof was then simplified by Proctor
[
Pro82
]
.

Another weakening of Problem
B
is to consider the reduced Young lattice
\tilde{L}(n,m)
, which is constructed from the Young lattice by adding more covering relations: all partitions of a given rank are covered by all partitions of the next rank. A decomposition of
\tilde{L}(n,m)
into rank-symmetric saturated chains was found by O’Hara
[
O’H90
,
Zei89a
]
, indirectly showing rank-unimodality of
L(n,m)
. In
[
Zei89b
]
and
[
Mac89
]
, the method was used to give an elementary algebraic proof of the rank-unimodality of
L(n,m)
which uses only Newton’s binomial theorem. O’Hara’s method is recursive: the reduced Young lattice is partitioned into smaller posets, which are defined using a non-rank-symmetric chain decomposition.
These posets belong to a family in which each one is a Cartesian product of smaller posets of the same family. One can construct a symmetric chain decomposition of a Cartesian product from symmetric chain decompositions of the factors. Solving a base case ends the proof.

We remark that the first non-rank-symmetric chains of O’Hara can be modified to decompose our original
L(n,m)

[
Dha13
]
, but the structure theorem no longer holds. This decomposition can still be used to establish the stronger strict unimodality of Gaussian coefficients purely combinatorially
[
Dha14a
]
,
but it does not solve Problem
B
.

2.3.
Plethysm and
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}
isomorphisms

The finite dimensional irreducible representations of
\mathfrak{sl}_{N}
are parametrised by partitions
\lambda
of length
\leq N
and constructed with Schur functors as
S^{\lambda}\mathbb{C}^{N}
. When
\lambda=(n)
then
S^{(n)}V=\Sym^{n}V
, and when
\lambda=(1^{n})
then
S^{(1^{n})}V=\Alt^{n}V
.
The most general problem of plethysm asks for the decomposition of
S^{\mu}S^{\nu}\mathbb{C}^{N}
into irreducible representations
S^{\lambda}\mathbb{C}^{N}
of
\mathfrak{sl}_{N}
.

To make the problem more approachable, some parameters are specialised. For instance,
\Sym^{2}S^{\lambda}\,\mathbb{C}^{N}
and
\Alt^{2}S^{\lambda}\,\mathbb{C}^{N}
were decomposed by Carré and Leclerc
[
CL95
]
.
We refer the reader
[
COS+24
]
for a brief survey of other known results.
In this document, we take
N=2
and
\mu=(1^{n})
to obtain Problem
A
. We solve it for
n\leq 4
. We remark that solutions for
n\leq 4
can be found in the literature
[
Thr42
,
Fou54
]
, and that the cases
n\geq 5
were announced by Pak and Panova.

There is a second way of simplifying the problem, by reducing the number of cases one needs to consider. The bijection we give in (
3.1
) from a basis of
\Alt^{n}\Sym^{r}\mathbb{C}^{2}
to
L(n,m)

lifts to an isomorphism of
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}
representations between
\Alt^{n}\Sym^{r}\mathbb{C}^{2}
and
\Sym^{n}\Sym^{m}\mathbb{C}^{2}
.
This isomorphism is sometimes attributed to Murnaghan
[
Mur51
]
, but it is known as the Wronskian isomorphism in invariant theory
[
AC07
]
. Since the word ‘isomorphism’ already refers to a modern interpretation of the result, the origins are hard to trace —Schmidt
[
Sch39
]
already considers the result to be classical in 1939.
The Wronskian isomorphism can be further lifted to an isomorphism of representations of
\SL_{2}(\mathbb{F})
over any characteristic
[
MW22
]
. The constructions we will present for
\Alt^{n}\Sym^{r}\mathbb{C}^{2}
can therefore be adapted to study
\Sym^{n}\Sym^{m}\mathbb{C}^{2}
.

Similarly, it is apparent from our combinatorial approach that
m
and
n
play symmetric roles. An isomorphism between
\Sym^{n}\Sym^{m}\mathbb{C}^{2}
and
\Sym^{m}\Sym^{n}\mathbb{C}^{2}
was discovered independently by Hermite
[
Her56
]
and later Cayley
[
Cay54
]
(with some special cases published simultaneously by Sylvester
[
Syl54
]
). It is now know as Hermite reciprocity or the Cayley–Sylvester formula, and was also lifted to an isomorphism of representations of
\SL_{2}(\mathbb{F})
over any characteristic in
[
MW22
]
.
See
[
PW21
,
MW25
,
GMSW25
]
for more reductions of this type, that allow one to understand more complicated
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}
plethysms in terms of
\Alt^{n}\Sym^{r}\mathbb{C}^{2}
.

Throughout the paper, we always assume
r+1=m+n

3.1.
Plethystic tableaux

We assume familiarity with the basic combinatorial objects of representation theory, and refer the reader to
[
Sta99
, §7.10]
for background on tableaux, and
[
Sta99
, §7.A2]
as a general reference for plethysm.

Let
\{x,y\}
be a basis of
\mathbb{C}^{2}
. A canonical basis of
\Sym^{r}\mathbb{C}^{2}
is given by

\{x^{r},~~x^{r-1}y,~~x^{r-2}y^{2},~~\ldots,~~y^{r}\}.

In particular, note that
\Sym^{r}\mathbb{C}^{2}
is
(r+1)
-dimensional. Given a basis
\{v_{0},\ldots,v_{r}\}
of a vector space
V
, a canonical basis of
\Alt^{n}V
is given by

\{v_{a_{n}}\wedge\ldots\wedge v_{a_{2}}\wedge v_{a_{1}}\ :\ r\geq a_{1}>a_{2}>\cdots>a_{n}\geq 0\}.

Combinatorially, a basis of
\Sym^{r}\mathbb{C}^{2}
is labeled by the set
\SSYT_{2}(r)
of semistandard Young tableaux of shape
(r)
in two letters. We identify tableaux in
\SSYT_{2}(r)
with bold integers
\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{r}
by sending the tableau
\begin{array}[]{|c|c|c|c|c|c|c|c|c|c|}\hline\cr 1&\cdots&1&2&\cdots&2\\
\hline\cr\end{array}
with
a
twos to the number
\boldsymbol{a}
. For instance, for
r\!=\!9
,

\boldsymbol{3}~~\text{ stands for }~~{\begin{array}[]{|c|c|c|c|c|c|c|c|c|c|}\hline\cr 1&1&1&1&1&1&2&2&2\\
\hline\cr\end{array}}~~\text{ which stands for }~~x^{6}y^{3}.

A canonical basis of
\Alt^{n}\Sym^{r}\mathbb{C}^{2}
is then labeled by the set
\SSYT_{2}(1^{n}[r])
of

\mathfrak{sl}_{2}

of outer shape
(1^{n})
and inner shape
(r)
, which are semistandard Young tableaux of shape
(1^{n})
with entries in
\SSYT_{2}(r)
. By the above identification,
\SSYT_{2}(1^{n}[r])
is identified with the set
\SSYT_{[\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{r}]}(1^{n})
of tableaux whose entries we write in bold. To save space, we usually write
\smash{{\begin{array}[]{|c|c|c|c|c|}\hline\cr\mathbf{a_{n}}&\mathbf{\cdots}&\mathbf{a_{1}}\\
\hline\cr\end{array}}^{\prime}}
for a column tableau. Summing up in an example: for
n\!=\!2
and
r\!=\!5
,

\smash{{\begin{array}[]{|c|c|c|c|c|}\hline\cr\mathbf{1}&\mathbf{3}\\
\hline\cr\end{array}}^{\prime}}~~\text{ stands for }~~{\begin{array}[]{|c|}\hline\cr\mathbf{1}\\
\hline\cr\mathbf{3}\\
\hline\cr\end{array}}~~\text{ which stands for }~~\begin{array}[m]{|l|}\hline\cr{\begin{array}[]{|c|c|c|c|c|c|c|c|c|c|}\hline\cr 1&1&1&1&2\\
\hline\cr\end{array}}\\
\hline\cr{\begin{array}[]{|c|c|c|c|c|c|c|c|c|c|}\hline\cr 1&1&2&2&2\\

\hline\cr\end{array}~~\text{ which stands for }~~x^{4}y\wedge x^{2}y^{3}\,.

Recall

and

\displaystyle\Psi:\SSYT_{2}(1^{n}[r])

3.2.
Crystals and characters

For background on crystals as combinatorial objects, we refer to
[
BS17
]
. In this paper a crystal is a seminormal
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}
crystal. The next definition is simplified accordingly.

Definition 3.2
.

A
crystal
is a set
\mathcal{B}
together with two maps
F:\mathcal{B}\to\mathcal{B}\cup\{0\}
and
\wt:\mathcal{B}\to\mathbb{Z}
such that:

(C0)

F
restricted to
F^{-1}(\mathcal{B})
is injective,

(C1)

\varphi(b)=\varepsilon(b)+2\wt(b)
for all
b\in\mathcal{B}
, and

(C2)

\wt(F.\,b)=\wt(b)-1
whenever
F.\,b\neq 0
,

where
\varphi(b)=\max\{k~:~F^{k}.\,b\neq 0\}
and
\varepsilon(b)=\max\{k~:~E^{k}.\,b\neq 0\}
, and
E:\mathcal{B}\to\mathcal{B}\cup\{0\}
is the inverse of
F
where the inverse is defined, and otherwise
0
.

We identify a crystal with the directed graph with vertex set
\mathcal{B}
and an arc
x\longrightarrow y
whenever
F.\,x=y
. Although not immediately clear, the weight
\wt
can be recovered from the graph
[
BS17
, Lemma 2.14]
. By
(C0)
the graph is a disjoint union of paths, by
(C1)
the graph is weight-symmetric.

Let
\alpha
be the simple root of
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}
, so that
\mathbb{Z}\frac{\alpha}{2}
is its weight lattice. A
crystal of a representation

V
is a crystal on the set of weights of
V
(with multiplicity) such that
\wt(k\frac{\alpha}{2})=k/2
.

A vertex
b
of a crystal is a
highest weight element
if
E\,.\,b=0
. There is exactly one highest weight element
b
for each connected component of a crystal of
V
, and the connected component of
b
is the crystal of
\Sym^{2\wt(b)}\mathbb{C}^{2}
. Therefore, given the set
\HW
of highest weight elements of the crystal of
V
, we can write

(3.2)

V\cong\bigoplus_{b\in\HW}\Sym^{2\wt(b)}\mathbb{C}^{2}.

Characters of
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}
representations are homogeneous polynomials in two variables
x,y
. Since the trace of an
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}
matrix is
0
, we may specialise these two variables to
\smash{q^{-{1}/{2}}}
and
\smash{q^{{1}/{2}}}
without loosing any information.
The character of the representation
is retrieved from its crystal via
\smash{\sum_{b\in\mathcal{B}}q^{\wt(b)}}

[
BS17
, §2.6 and Ch. 13]
.
The character of
\Sym^{r}\mathbb{C}^{2}
is the Schur polynomial

s_{(r)}(q^{-\frac{1}{2}},q^{\frac{1}{2}})={[r+1]=\frac{q^{\frac{r+1}{2}}-q^{-\frac{r+1}{2}}}{q^{\frac{1}{2}}-q^{-\frac{1}{2}}}}=q^{-\frac{r}{2}}+q^{1-\frac{r}{2}}+q^{2-\frac{r}{2}}+\cdots+q^{\frac{r}{2}},

and the character of
\Alt^{n}\Sym^{r}\mathbb{C}^{2}
is the plethysm of Schur polynomials

s_{(1^{n})}\circ s_{(r)}(q^{-\frac{1}{2}},q^{\frac{1}{2}})={\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{r+1}{n}=\frac{[r+1][r]\cdots[r-n+2]}{[n][n-1]\cdots[1]}}.

We will prefer the notation in terms of
q
-integers and
q
-binomials hereafter.

Note 3.3
.

Our definition of
q
-integers (and hence
q
-binomials) is non-standard. There are two objects typically called by this name: combinatorialists define the
q
-analogue of the integer

r+1
to be

\frac{1-q^{r+1}}{1-q}=1+q+\cdots+q^{r}.

With our notation, this is
q^{r/2}[r+1]
.
Algebraists define the
quantum integer

r+1
to be

\frac{q^{r+1}-q^{-r-1}}{q-q^{-1}}=q^{-r}+q^{-r+2}+\cdots+q^{r-2}+q^{r}.

With our notation, this is
[r+1]|_{q\mapsto q^{2}}
.
The
q
-integers we define arise most naturally in this paper.

3.3.
Posets

Many of the concepts that we use can be understood purely in the language of posets. We follow
[
Sta86
, Ch. 3]
for the terminology.

Let
P
be a ranked poset
P
and let
\textup{rk}:P\to\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
be its rank function.
A
chain
is a poset in which the order is total.
A
saturated chain
of a ranked poset is a subposet that is a chain and that ‘does not skip any rank’: if it has an element of rank
k
and an element of rank
k+N
, then it has an element of each rank
k+i
for
0<i<N
.
A
symmetric chain decomposition
of a ranked poset is a decomposition of the poset into rank-symmetric, saturated chains.
We describe symmetric chain decompositions in terms of a function matching each rank and the next one.

The bijection
\Psi
of (
3.1
) sends a tableau with entry-sum
k
to a partition of size
k-\frac{(n-1)(n-2)}{2}
. The weight function on
\SSYT_{2}(1^{n}[r])
will be a linear function on the entry-sum of the tableaux; the size of a partition is its rank on
L(n,m)
. Hence the weight function of our crystals will be the rank function on
L(n,m)
up to some shift.
The character of a crystal of
\Alt^{n}\Sym^{r}\mathbb{C}^{2}
is
\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{r+1}{n}=\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n+m}{n}
and the rank-generating function of
L(n,m)
is
q^{nm/2}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n+m}{n}
.

Embedding

\smash{{\begin{array}[]{|c|c|c|c|c|}\hline\cr\mathbf{a_{n}}&\mathbf{\cdots}&\mathbf{a_{1}}\\

Lemma 4.8

Note 4.14
.

The previous definition is reminiscent of Kashiwara’s tensor product rule
[
BS17
, §2.3]
, but we can make this more precise.
Let
\mathcal{B}_{1}=(\mathcal{B}_{1},F_{1},\wt_{1})
and
\mathcal{B}_{2}=(\mathcal{B}_{2},F_{2},\wt_{2})
be two
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}
crystals. Let
\varphi_{i}
and
\varepsilon_{i}
be defined in the usual manner, for
i=1,2
.

Our objective is to construct a crystal structure on
\mathcal{B}_{1}\times\mathcal{B}_{2}
with weight function
\wt(a,b)=\wt_{1}(a)+\wt_{2}(b)
.
As a first attempt, define an operator

(F_{1}\otimes 1).(a,b)=(F_{1}.a,b)
. This operator satisfies axioms
(C0)
and
(C2)
, but not necessarily
(C1)
. Similarly, we could attempt to define
1\otimes F_{2}
similarly, but we arrive at the same conclusion.

Kashiwara’s solution in

[
Kas90
, Prop. 6]

is to combine
F_{1}\otimes 1
and
1\otimes F_{2}
via

F.\,(a,b):=\begin{cases}(F_{1}\otimes 1).(a,b)&\text{if }\varphi_{2}(b)>\varepsilon_{1}(a),\\
(1\otimes F_{2}).(a,b)&\text{if }\varphi_{2}(b)\leq\varepsilon_{1}(a).\end{cases}

Working with
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}
crystals, this is the Clebsch–Gordan rule, but Kashiwara’s rule generalises it to any crystal.
Now note that applying axiom
(C1)
for
F_{1}
and
F_{2}
gives

\displaystyle\varphi_{2}(b)>\varepsilon_{1}(a)

\displaystyle\iff\varepsilon_{2}(b)+2\wt_{2}(b)>\varphi_{1}(a)-2\wt_{1}(a)

\displaystyle\iff\varepsilon_{2}(b)+2\wt(a,b)>\varphi_{1}(a)

\displaystyle\iff\tilde{A}(a,b)<0,

where
\tilde{A}:=\varphi_{1}-(\varepsilon_{2}+2\wt)
. We have recovered the same expression found in Definition
4.12
.

Define
E:\mathcal{B}(n)\to\mathcal{B}(n)\cup\{0\}
be the inverse of
F
if it exists, and
0
otherwise; define

\varepsilon(b)=\max\{k\ :\ E^{k}.\,b\neq 0\}
as before.
For each
r
, the operator
F
restricts to an operator
F_{r}:\mathcal{B}_{r}(n)\to\mathcal{B}_{r}(n)\cup\{0\}
by letting
F_{r}\,.\,t=0
if
F.\,t\not\in\mathcal{B}_{r}(n)
.
Define
\varphi_{r}(b)=\max\{k\ :\ (F_{r})^{k}.\,b\neq 0\}
. An tableau is
lowest weight
if
F_{r}\,.\,t=0
. We need a lemma relating lowest weight tableaux and the bottom
\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{bot}}(n)
.

Lemma 4.15

If

Proof.

Let
t=\smash{{\begin{array}[]{|c|c|c|c|c|}\hline\cr\mathbf{a_{n}}&\mathbf{\cdots}&\mathbf{a_{1}}\\
\hline\cr\end{array}}^{\prime}}\in\mathcal{B}(n)

\varphi_{a_{1}-a_{n}-1}(t^{\downarrow})=0

A(t)=\varphi^{\mathrm{bot}}_{a_{1}}(t)-\varphi_{a_{1}-a_{n}-1}(t^{\downarrow})+na_{n}=\varphi^{\mathrm{bot}}_{a_{1}}(t)+na_{n}\geq 0.\qed

For highest weight tableaux, we can similarly show that
