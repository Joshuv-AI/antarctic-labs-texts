Comparison of hip and knee range of motion measurements using Azure Kinect and a digital goniometer in elite female weightlifters
Authors: Bülent Işik, Kenan Erdaği, Serkan Örücü, Erkan Özbay, Usame Ömer Osmanoğlu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAccurate assessment of joint range of motion (ROM) is essential in both clinical and athletic contexts for monitoring mobility, guiding rehabilitation, and optimizing performance. Traditional tools such as goniometers are widely used but limited by operator dependency and static measurement constraints. Depth-sensing technologies offer a markerless alternative that may enhance practicality in field-based settings. This study examined the validity and reliability of the Microsoft Azure Kinect system compared with a digital goniometer for assessing hip and knee ROM in 30 elite female weightlifters from the Turkish National Team. Participants performed hip abduction, adduction, flexion, extension, and knee flexion, each measured using both tools. Agreement between systems was assessed using Pearson’s correlations, intraclass correlation coefficients [ICC (3,1)], and Bland-Altman analyses. No significant differences were found between methods (p > 0.05), with strong correlations (r = 0.974–0.997) and high intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC = 0.994–0.998), indicating excellent consistency. Bland-Altman analyses showed minimal bias and narrow limits of agreement. These findings support the Azure Kinect as a valid and reliable tool for lower-extremity ROM assessment and a viable alternative to traditional goniometers in field applications. Introduction

Limited joint range of motion (ROM) is associated with decreased movement efficiency, compromised athletic performance, and a heightened risk of musculoskeletal injuries (MSKI)1–4. Specifically, inadequate ROM is related to an increased risk of developing overuse injuries such as hamstring strains and lower back pain through altered movement patterns, reduced technical precision, and elevated mechanical stress on adjacent joints and soft tissues2. Such injuries can lead to considerable time loss from training and competition, increased medical and rehabilitation costs, and a greater likelihood of reinjury, ultimately hindering athletic development and performance continuity. Sports such as weightlifting require high levels of flexibility, balance, coordination, strength. Limited hip or knee mobility can impair performance and increase injury risk through improper execution of lifts and reduced force production5–7. Hip and knee mobility are particularly crucial in executing fundamental weightlifting movements such as the squat and clean and jerk, which require large joint excursions and precise coordination8,9. Limited ROM in these joints can restrict lifting depth, alter movement mechanics, and increase the risk of injury or performance decrements10. Given that restricted hip and knee ROM can adversely affect athletic performance, it is essential for practitioners to identify these limitations and implement corrective strategies to optimize function.

Understanding how limitations in hip and knee ROM develop requires a closer examination of the anatomical and biomechanical characteristics of these joints. The knee joint (articulatio genus) consists of the patellofemoral and tibiofemoral compartments and represents the largest synovial articulation in the human body, allowing a wide range of joint motion due to its complex anatomy11,12. Primarily responsible for flexion and extension, the convex configuration of the femoral condyles also enables limited rotational movement once approximately 30° of flexion is achieved13. Similarly, the hip joint is a multiaxial synovial articulation between the femoral head and the acetabulum of the pelvis14. It permits movement across three planes flexion and extension, abduction and adduction, internal and external rotation and is fundamental for maintaining stability and generating power during locomotion and weight-bearing activities15.

ROM assessment is a key clinical and performance metric for identifying movement restrictions, tracking rehabilitation progress, and optimizing athletic function1–4. The most common evaluation tools are goniometers, which quantify the angular displacement of a joint. Digital goniometers have become widely used in both clinical and sport settings because they provide fast, user-independent, and accurate measurements while minimizing observer bias16–21. These devices have demonstrated excellent reliability across joints, including both active and passive movements of the lower limbs (ICC ≥ 0.99)20. Their portability and ease of use make them ideal for on-field applications and continuous monitoring21. Despite their advantages, goniometric measurements remain partially dependent on examiner skill and consistency, which may introduce inter- and intra-rater variability, particularly in less standardized testing environments17,19,20. Furthermore, traditional goniometers are limited to single-plane static assessments, restricting their ability to evaluate dynamic, multi-planar motion22.

To overcome these limitations, marker-based motion-capture systems have been widely employed. These systems are regarded as the “gold standard” for kinematic analysis because they provide high spatial accuracy and enable comprehensive three-dimensional tracking of joint movements23,24. Despite their precision, marker-based systems are costly, require extensive setup, and are typically confined to laboratory environments, making them impractical for large-scale or field-based applications23,24. These constraints have led to increasing interest in portable, cost-effective alternatives that can deliver reliable biomechanical data outside controlled laboratory settings. Markerless motion-capture systems such as Microsoft’s Kinect offer a practical bridge between high-accuracy laboratory tools and accessible field assessments. Kinect sensors estimate joint positions in real time using infrared depth mapping and vector-based algorithms25–28. The second-generation Kinect (Kinect V2) has demonstrated strong validity and reliability in comparison with both marker-based systems and traditional goniometers, showing high correlations in the measurement of hip and knee joint angles (ICC ≥ 0.91)25,29–33. These findings establish a solid methodological foundation for further development of markerless technologies in biomechanics research.

The Azure Kinect, the latest model in this series, operates on the same depth-sensing, markerless principle but differs from Kinect V2 in hardware and algorithmic structure. It incorporates upgraded depth sensors, an expanded field of view, and artificial-intelligence-based skeletal-tracking algorithms. These advancements improve spatial resolution and robustness under varied lighting conditions, yet they also necessitate independent validation to confirm measurement reliability. Despite its technological upgrades, Azure Kinect’s accuracy has not yet been systematically compared with established reference methods in applied athletic settings25–33. Limited ROM can decrease athletic performance and increase the risk of injury1–4. Although marker-based motion capture remains the most accurate technique for motion analysis, its high cost, technical demands, and laboratory constraints make it inaccessible in many applied settings23,24. While these systems are considered the gold standard, their expense, complexity, and limited portability restrict their use in real-world environments23,24.

In contrast, digital goniometers are cost-effective, easy to use, and represent a common reference tool in both clinical and athletic practice16–21, although their accuracy can be partially dependent on the examiner. Reducing such human-dependent error has been a key motivation for the development of automated, markerless measurement systems such as depth-sensing cameras. Markerless motion-capture systems such as Kinect provide a promising alternative to marker-based motion capture, offering practicality and portability while maintaining good accuracy. However, their precision depends on proper joint and pelvic stabilization during measurement; insufficient stabilization, particularly during hip flexion, may lead to erroneous results17,19. Among these systems, the Azure Kinect represents an advanced, AI-driven platform. Despite its technological advantages, it has not yet been sufficiently validated in athletic populations. Accordingly, the present study addresses this gap by evaluating the validity and reliability of the Azure Kinect for measuring lower-limb ROM in elite female weightlifters, using a digital goniometer as the reference standard. It is hypothesized that the Azure Kinect will demonstrate high validity and reliability in quantifying hip and knee joint ROM when compared with digital goniometric measurements.

Materials and methods

Participants and sample size

This study included 30 elite female weightlifters from the Turkish National Women’s Team (age: 17.47 ± 2.46 years; height: 158.80 ± 7.63 cm; body mass: 58.07 ± 12.63 kg; BMI: 22.80 ± 3.33 kg/m²; training age: 5.63 ± 2.41 years), all of whom were preparing for the European Youth and U15 Weightlifting Championships (Madrid, July 2025). Anthropometric measurements were collected in the morning under fasting conditions using a bioelectrical impedance analyzer (Tanita MC-580, Japan)34, and height was measured with a portable stadiometer (Seca 213, Germany). BMI was calculated as weight (kg) divided by height squared (m²)35. Additionally, data on athletes’ maximum snatch and clean & jerk lifts, obtained from national team coaches, were included to contextualize the physical profile of the sample.

Participants had a minimum of three years of licensed training experience and followed a structured training program consisting of two sessions per day, six days per week. Elite female weightlifters were selected because their sport demands exceptional hip and knee mobility, postural control, and neuromuscular coordination under load. These characteristics make them an ideal model for assessing the performance of motion-tracking systems under realistic athletic conditions. Inclusion criteria included no hip or knee surgery or injury within the past six months, no structural limitations or rheumatic disease, active training status, and medical clearance by a sports medicine physician.

To control for hormonal influences on joint mobility, all assessments were conducted during the follicular phase of the menstrual cycle, when estrogen levels are relatively stable, thereby improving measurement consistency across participants and enhancing the generalizability of results36,37. The menstrual cycle phase was identified from the self-reported date of the last menstrual period36,38.

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki (2013) and approved by the Scientific Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Medicine at Karamanoğlu Mehmetbey University (Approval No: 08–2024/17). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants, and parental consent was secured for minors. Informed consent for the publication of identifiable images in an online open-access publication was obtained from all participants whose photographs appear in the manuscript. A priori power analysis (G*Power 3.1.9.734 indicated that a minimum of 27 participants were required to detect a medium effect size (Cohen’s d = 0.5) at α = 0.05 with 80% power. Thirty eligible athletes were included, yielding a post hoc statistical power of 0.92 for hip flexion measurements.

Experimental setup

All assessments were performed by the same investigator in a quiet, climate-controlled room to minimize environmental variability (Figs. 1 and 2). Ceiling-mounted LED lighting (≈ 220 lx, CEM DT-8820) provided uniform illumination, preventing infrared interference with the Azure Kinect depth sensors. Two Azure Kinect cameras (Microsoft Azure Kinect DK) were mounted on tripods positioned 3.0 m in front of the participants at a height of 1.1 m, forming a 60° arc with a 3.15 m separation (Fig. 2). The devices were aligned toward the center of the testing area to maintain full visibility of both lower limbs and minimize occlusion during capture. Participants performed each motion within a custom stabilization frame placed on a firm surface to ensure consistent posture and reduce measurement variability.

Prior to testing, participants completed a standardized five-minute dynamic warm-up including light mobility and neuromuscular activation drills (e.g., leg swings, hip rotations, bodyweight squats) and refrained from strenuous activity for at least six hours to ensure neuromuscular recovery. All measurements were conducted while participants maintained a stable, upright posture within a custom-built rope-guided stabilization frame to minimize trunk and pelvic sway, thereby improving consistency between trials (Fig. 1). Participants wore form-fitting athletic clothing to optimize body contour visibility and Kinect depth sensing. Anatomical alignment for each joint was performed according to standardized placements described in Fig. 1. All Kinect data were simultaneously collected during joint angle measurements using a dual-sensor configuration, as detailed in the section "Azure Kinect sensors" (Fig. 2). Although traditional goniometric assessments are typically performed in supine or prone positions39,40, the standing configuration used here allowed for data acquisition under conditions closely resembling real-life athletic posture (Fig. 3). This approach improved ecological validity and facilitated compatibility with the Azure Kinect sensor system.

Measurement of lower limb joint angles using the Azure Kinect sensor system in conjunction with a stabilization apparatus. a The athlete was positioned in a fixed stance within the stabilization frame to ensure proper alignment of the lower limbs, b During the hip abduction measurement, the athlete maintained a stable posture with the aid of guiding ropes, while the movement was simultaneously recorded by the Azure Kinect sensor.

Joint angle measurements

Lower-extremity ROM was assessed for hip abduction, adduction, flexion, extension, and knee flexion. Each movement was measured three times using both a digital goniometer (Baseline® Digital Goniometer, USA) and the Azure Kinect system, with the mean of the three trials used for analysis. Measurement procedures followed established protocols by Norkin and White (2016) and Luttgens and Hamilton (1997)39,40 to ensure consistent anatomical alignment. Specifically, hip abduction was measured in a standing position with the leg moved laterally, the goniometer centered on the anterior superior iliac spine (ASIS), and the arms aligned to the contralateral ASIS and anterior femur (Fig. 1c). Hip adduction was performed in the same posture, with the leg moved medially. Hip flexion was assessed with the leg raised forward and the goniometer pivot placed at the greater trochanter; the stationary arm aligned with the trunk and the movable arm with the lateral femur (Fig. 1a). Hip extension followed the same landmarks, with the leg extended backward (Fig. 1b). Knee flexion was measured while standing, with the pivot aligned to the lateral epicondyle of the femur, and the arms aligned with the femur and fibula (Fig. 1d).

Azure Kinect sensors

The Azure Kinect system combines a time-of-flight (ToF) depth sensor and a high-resolution RGB camera to capture synchronized color and depth images for real-time 3D motion tracking41–43. Data were collected using the Azure Kinect SDK v1.4.1 (Windows 10 Pro, 64-bit) with default factory calibration to align the RGB and depth streams via intrinsic and extrinsic parameters44,45. The device’s convolutional neural network (CNN)-based skeletal tracking algorithm identifies 32 anatomical joints in 3D space, enabling markerless biomechanical analysis46–48. Previous research has validated Azure Kinect’s accuracy and reliability for lower-extremity kinematics, clinical motion tracking, and sports performance assessment45,47,49–52. These features support its use as a valid alternative to traditional motion capture in applied biomechanics.

Sensor fusion and filtering techniques

To improve spatial tracking accuracy and reduce occlusion errors, a dual Azure Kinect configuration was used. One device was designated as the primary and the other as the secondary, with the secondary sensor’s coordinate data transformed into the primary reference frame using a predefined transformation matrix53.

Following spatial alignment, joint position data from both sensors were merged based on the confidence scores assigned by the Azure Kinect SDK. A weighted averaging approach was applied to generate the final 3D joint coordinates, ensuring that measurements with higher confidence contributed proportionally more to the fused output54.

Raw Kinect data were processed using the Azure Kinect SDK and the system’s built-in filtering algorithms. The data acquisition and processing workflow (Fig. 4) involved real-time RGB and depth capture, automatic joint extraction and confidence-weighted fusion of ten key joints (pelvis, hips, knees, and ankles), and double exponential smoothing (DES) filtering to minimize signal noise and outliers, as described by Piercy et al. (2024)55. Five key joint angles- hip abduction, adduction, flexion, extension, and knee flexion- were computed from three-dimensional joint vectors (pelvis-hip-knee and hip-knee-ankle triplets). The angular relationship between joint vectors was derived using the dot product, and a fourth-order low-pass Butterworth filter with a 5 Hz cutoff frequency was applied to the resulting time-series data to eliminate high-frequency noise while preserving movement integrity54,56. Post-processing was performed in MATLAB R2023a to improve temporal smoothness and reduce signal noise. A fourth-order low-pass Butterworth filter (cutoff frequency: 5 Hz) was applied to the joint angle time-series data, consistent with established biomechanical motion analysis standards57. The processed joint angle data were subsequently used for all statistical and validity analyses. The graphical interface developed for the system enabled real-time monitoring of both sensors and automatic visualization of computed joint angles (Fig. 5). This integrated approach provided synchronized and ecologically valid joint angle measurements between the Azure Kinect and digital goniometer systems under standardized experimental conditions.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and Python 3.7.9 (Python Software Foundation, DE, USA). Descriptive statistics, including the mean, standard deviation, median, and interquartile range (IQR), were calculated for all numerical variables to summarize central tendency and data dispersion58.

To evaluate the distributional properties of the data and assess normality, Levene’s test was applied to verify the homogeneity of variances59, and Q-Q plots together with histograms were visually inspected. Differences between the Azure Kinect and digital goniometer measurements were examined using the paired t-test for normally distributed variables or the Wilcoxon signed-rank test for non-normal data60,61. Concurrent validity between the two measurement methods was determined using Pearson’s correlation coefficient (r) for parametric data and Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient (ρ) for non-parametric data62. Agreement and potential systematic bias between measurement methods were further evaluated using Bland-Altman analysis63. Measurement reliability and inter-method consistency were quantified using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC (3,1)), calculated via a two-way mixed-effects model with absolute agreement, following the recommendations of Koo and Li (2016)64. Complementary reliability metrics, including the coefficient of variation (CV) and coefficient of repeatability (CR), were also reported to provide a broader assessment of measurement precision60,65. Interpretation of ICC values followed the classification proposed by Koo and Li (2016): ICC < 0.50 = poor reliability, 0.50 ≤ ICC < 0.75 = moderate reliability, 0.75 ≤ ICC < 0.90 = good reliability, ICC ≥ 0.90 = excellent reliability64. A significance level of p < 0.05 was adopted for all analyses.

In the hip adduction assessment, Azure Kinect and the digital goniometer showed a strong correlation (r = 0.989) and excellent agreement (ICC = 0.994). This was further supported by the Bland-Altman analysis, which revealed a small bias, narrow limits of agreement, and similar levels of variation and repeatability between the two devices (Table 3; Fig. 6b).

For hip flexion, Azure Kinect and the digital goniometer demonstrated a very high correlation (r = 0.995) and excellent reliability (ICC = 0.998). The Bland-Altman analysis supported these findings, showing minimal bias, narrow limits of agreement, and consistent variation and repeatability across both methods (Table 3; Fig. 6c).

In hip extension, Azure Kinect and the digital goniometer showed a strong correlation (r = 0.974) and excellent intraclass reliability (ICC = 0.995). These results were supported by the Bland-Altman analysis, which indicated a minimal mean difference, acceptable agreement, and consistent variation and repeatability between the two methods (Table 3; Fig. 6d).

In knee flexion, a very high correlation (r = 0.997) and excellent reliability (ICC = 0.998) were found between Azure Kinect and the digital goniometer. Consistent with these findings, the Bland-Altman analysis showed minimal bias, strong agreement, and similar variation and repeatability between the two methods (Table 3; Fig. 6e).

Although a few individual data points fell outside the limits of agreement in the Bland-Altman plots (1–2 participants), these instances represented less than 5% of the total sample and do not undermine the overall agreement between the two measurement methods.

Discussion

This study compared lower-extremity joint range-of-motion measurements obtained using the Azure Kinect and a digital goniometer across five parameters in an athletic sample. No statistically significant differences were identified between the two methods for any movement. High correlation coefficients, near-excellent between-method agreement, and narrow limits of agreement in Bland-Altman analyses were observed. In addition, the coefficient of variation (CV) and coefficient of repeatability (CR) values indicated comparable measurement stability across both systems. When considered together, these findings suggest that, under controlled assessment conditions, the Azure Kinect may provide a level of performance comparable to digital goniometry for evaluating lower-extremity joint ROM.

Hip joint: frontal plane movements

The Azure Kinect demonstrated a high level of agreement with the digital goniometer during hip abduction, supported by narrow limits of agreement and stable CV/CR values. Although small left-right deviations were present, these differences remained minor and consistent, suggesting that the system performed comparably under the controlled conditions used in this study. From a practical perspective, such slight asymmetries could influence the interpretation of frontal-plane imbalance in clinical or sport settings; however, the limited magnitude observed here indicates that Kinect-based measurements may still be suitable for basic screening when larger asymmetries are of primary concern.

These findings align with studies reporting strong reliability for frontal-plane hip motion under standardized conditions26,66. At the same time, previous research has documented more variable outcomes, including systematic over- or under-estimation when Kinect was compared with stereophotogrammetry or Vicon systems, particularly in dynamic or less-controlled environments32,67–70. Differences across studies may reflect variation in task complexity, population characteristics, and environmental factors, all of which are known to affect markerless tracking accuracy.

Consistent with the pattern observed for hip abduction, no statistically significant differences emerged between the Azure Kinect and the digital goniometer in hip adduction measurements. Although reliability values for adduction were slightly lower than those reported for sagittal-plane motions, strong correlations and comparable CV/CR values suggest that the Kinect was able to capture narrow-range frontal-plane motion with reasonable stability under the controlled conditions applied here. This interpretation aligns with literature reporting acceptable agreement for small-angle frontal-plane tasks in standardized settings, while also reflecting the greater variability documented in studies conducted under more dynamic or less-controlled conditions33,66–68. Overall, the findings indicate that Kinect-based assessments may be most appropriate for static or low-velocity frontal-plane evaluations where measurement consistency can be maintained.

Hip joint: sagittal plane movements

The Azure Kinect showed excellent agreement with the digital goniometer during hip flexion, reflected by the absence of significant between-method differences and the strong reliability indices observed in this study. Narrow limits of agreement and stable CV/CR values indicate that the system was able to capture large-range sagittal-plane displacement with a high degree of consistency under controlled testing conditions. From a practical standpoint, this level of concordance suggests that Kinect-based measurements may be suitable for basic mobility screening or longitudinal monitoring in sport and clinical settings, particularly when evaluating substantial flexion angles where larger asymmetries or changes over time are of interest.

These findings align with previous work demonstrating high reliability for hip flexion during slow or static tasks25,66,67. At the same time, other studies have reported systematic under- or over-estimation when Kinect was compared with gold-standard motion capture systems in more dynamic or complex movements, with errors increasing as joint excursion or task velocity increased33,68–70. Differences across studies likely reflect variation in movement speed, pelvic motion, task complexity, or environmental constraints, all of which are known to influence markerless tracking accuracy.

A similar pattern was observed for hip extension: although the coefficient of variation was slightly higher than for other sagittal-plane parameters, the Kinect and goniometer still demonstrated strong correlation and minimal mean difference. These results are broadly consistent with previous reports showing acceptable performance for basic extension tasks25,71, while also mirroring the reduced accuracy documented in more dynamic assessments68,70,72. Practically, while small-angle posterior movements may be more challenging for a depth camera due to reduced tracking sensitivity, the consistency observed under controlled conditions suggests that Kinect-based assessments may still hold value for static or low-velocity sagittal-plane evaluations in athletic or clinical populations.

Knee joint: flexion

The Azure Kinect demonstrated a high level of agreement with the digital goniometer during knee flexion, as reflected by the absence of statistically significant differences (p = 0.930), the very high correlation coefficient (r = 0.997), and the excellent between-method reliability (ICC = 0.998). Narrow confidence limits and a minimal mean difference further support the stability of the measurements, suggesting that the system performed comparably under the controlled conditions used in this study. From a practical standpoint, this level of consistency indicates that Kinect-based measurements may be suitable for basic sagittal-plane motion screening or longitudinal monitoring in settings where large-range knee flexion or substantial asymmetries are of primary interest.

These results are consistent with previous studies reporting strong agreement between Kinect-based systems and reference methods for knee flexion during slow, simple, or static tasks25,26,30,67,69. However, prior research has also shown that accuracy may decline under dynamic or complex movement conditions, with reduced correlations and systematic deviations documented during tasks such as drop vertical jumps or lunge-based assessments32,33,68. Differences across studies likely reflect variations in task velocity, plane of motion, and environmental constraints, all of which are known to influence the performance of markerless tracking systems.

Taken together, the findings suggest that while the Azure Kinect may provide highly consistent estimates of knee flexion in controlled, low-velocity conditions, its measurement precision may be more limited during high-speed or multi-planar movements. Accordingly, Kinect-based assessments may be most appropriate for static or slow sagittal-plane evaluations, particularly in clinical or athletic contexts where standardized conditions and repeatability are prioritized.

Limitations

A primary limitation of this study is that all assessments were performed in a highly controlled, static, and upright testing environment. Although such standardization is necessary for establishing baseline validity and reliability, it may not fully reflect the multi-joint, dynamic tasks (e.g., squats or functional lifting movements) that typically characterize clinical or sport-performance evaluations. Accordingly, the degree to which the present findings translate to more complex, real-world movement scenarios remains uncertain and should be addressed in future research. Additionally, although traditional goniometric assessments are typically performed in supine or prone positions, the standing configuration used in this study may represent a methodological limitation, as it differs from conventional assessment postures. However, this approach was chosen to improve ecological validity and better reflect real-life athletic conditions39,40.

A second limitation concerns the characteristics of the participant group, which consisted exclusively of healthy female athletes. While this homogeneity helped reduce between-subject variability, it may limit the generalizability of the findings to broader or more clinically diverse populations, including individuals with musculoskeletal impairments, older adults, or those exhibiting atypical movement patterns.

Finally, although no statistically significant differences emerged between measurement methods across the evaluated parameters, relatively greater variability was observed in hip adduction and hip extension compared with other movements. These subtle inconsistencies-despite not diminishing the overall agreement between systems-may relate to depth-camera sensitivity in smaller-range movements or planes that are less favorable for sensor visibility. Additional work involving dynamic tasks and multi-sensor configurations may help clarify the extent and practical implications of these variations.

Conclusions

This study represents an initial validation of the Azure Kinect as a reliable and valid tool for assessing lower-extremity joint ROM in elite female athletes. The findings demonstrate that Kinect-based measurements may provide accuracy comparable to digital goniometry under standardized conditions, while offering practical advantages such as reduced human error, shorter setup and assessment time, and lower operator training requirements. These features make the Azure Kinect a practical and accessible alternative for joint mobility assessment in sport and clinical environments, marking an important first step toward integrating markerless motion capture into athletic performance and rehabilitation analysis.

Practical applications

The Azure Kinect offers a fast, contact-free, and portable alternative to traditional goniometry for assessing lower-limb mobility. Its markerless design minimizes human measurement error, reduces setup and testing time, and requires less operator expertise, enabling efficient and repeatable assessments across large groups of athletes or patients. These characteristics support its integration into applied biomechanics, clinical screening, and sport-performance monitoring protocols, where reliability, efficiency, and accessibility are key priorities.
