A large-scale single-nucleus resource reveals a cardiomyocyte-like fibroblast and stage-specific remodeling across cardiomyopathies
Authors: Qianzi Tang, Xingyu Li, Xiongwei Fan, Chenqing Zheng, Huihui Ma, Rong Luo, Yuanling Tang, Shengzhong Liu, Chengming Fan, Yao Zhang, Wanfeng Zhang, Xiushan Wu, Xiaoping Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCardiomyopathies represent a heterogeneous group of myocardial disorders with distinct phenotypes and outcomes, yet their cell-type-specific mechanisms and differences remain incompletely defined.MethodsHere, we present the Cardiac Cell Atlas, a large-scale single-nucleus transcriptomic atlas of 525 cardiac tissue samples (20 newly generated and 505 publicly available) spanning dilated cardiomyopathy (DCM), hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM), arrhythmogenic right ventricular cardiomyopathy (ARVC), left ventricular non-compaction (LVNC), and non-failing controls, integrating > 2 million nuclei.ResultsThis resource delineates both shared remodeling signatures and divergent trajectories across cardiomyopathies. We identified a previously unrecognized fibroblast (FB) subpopulation expressing cardiomyopathy genes (TNNT2, RYR2, MYBPC3), positioned between fibroblasts and ventricular cardiomyocytes (VCM) and segregating into FB-like (SORBS2−) and ventricular VCM-like (SORBS2+) states, the latter with reparative potential. Notably, HCM was dominated by compensatory hypertrophic remodeling, whereas DCM showed progressive cardiomyocyte loss and maladaptive FB expansion, consistent with their distinct clinical outcomes. Left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF)-associated pseudotime staging revealed dynamic remodeling: early HCM showed enriched VCM-FB-endothelial(EC) signaling and effective compensation, while early DCM lacked EC involvement, predisposing to dysfunction. At mid-stage, signaling declined in HCM but intensified in DCM as ECs became active remodelers, whereas late-stage disease in both subtypes exhibited global signaling loss, with residual maladaptive extracellular matrix(ECM)-receptor activity persisting in DCM.ConclusionsTogether, the Cardiac Cell Atlas provides a comprehensive reference for human cardiac cell states and interactions, advancing mechanistic understanding and informing clinical strategies for risk stratification, stage-specific intervention, and improved outcomes in HCM and DCM. The web portal of Cardiac Cell Atlas together with all the datasets are publicly available at http://113.54.15.143:8000/app/index/.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12916-026-04954-1.

Background

Cardiomyopathies are a heterogeneous group of myocardial disorders with distinct phenotypes, prognoses, and molecular signatures. They are broadly classified into four types-DCM, HCM, ARVC, and LVNC. Among these, DCM and HCM are the most prevalent in clinical practice [1, 2].

Clinically and pathologically, both DCM and HCM share features of cardiomyocyte (CM) hypertrophy, apoptosis, and interstitial fibrosis, yet they diverge in structural remodeling molecular mechanisms, and prognosis. DCM is characterized by ventricular dilation, wall thinning, and progressive systolic dysfunction, frequently associated with cytoskeletal abnormalities, mitochondrial energetic failure, and impaired contractile reserve, and genetically linked to truncating variants in TTN, as well as mutations in LMNA, DSP, and other structural genes [1, 2]. In contrast, HCM typically presents with asymmetric ventricular hypertrophy, preserved or hyperdynamic systolic function but impaired diastolic relaxation, and is most often driven by sarcomeric mutations—particularly in MYH7 and MYBPC3—which confer hypercontractility and increased energetic demand [3–5]. Prognostically, DCM often progresses to end-stage heart failure with poor outcomes, whereas HCM generally shows more favorable survival despite increased risk of sudden cardiac death [1, 2]. Despite advances in diagnosis and therapy, the etiology and pathogenesis of cardiomyopathies remain poorly understood, underscoring the need for deeper molecular insights.

Recent single-cell and single-nucleus transcriptomic studies have revealed that, despite different genetic backgrounds, DCM and HCM display highly similar cellular remodeling programs. Both are characterized by CM loss, expansion of activated fibroblasts (FBs), and immune-endothelial(EC) reorganization, suggesting a convergent fibrotic-inflammatory axis [6, 7]. Direct comparisons between DCM and HCM showed only limited differential expression and few subtype-specific pathways, even when stratifying by genotype (e.g., TTN in DCM versus MYH7/MYBPC3 in HCM), indicating that transcriptional convergence dominates at advanced disease stages [6]. These findings challenge a purely gene-deterministic view and highlight that microenvironmental cues and intercellular interactions may be critical determinants of cardiomyopathy phenotypes. However, these studies were largely limited to end-stage explanted hearts and modest sample sizes, restricting their ability to resolve rare subpopulations or capture stage-specific remodeling dynamics [6, 7].

In this resource, we present the Cardiac Cell Atlas, a comprehensive single-cell atlas of the human heart comprising > 2 million cells or nuclei across in HCM and DCM (Additional file 1: Table S1-S4). This atlas enabled the discovery of a rare FB-CM transitional subpopulation, the delineation of transcriptomic differences between HCM and DCM, and the characterization of remodeling trajectories across disease stages. Using LVEF-associated pseudotime, we stratified patients into early, middle, and late stages and mapped remodeling between adjacent states, providing mechanistic insight into the divergent prognoses of DCM and HCM. Together, the Cardiac Cell Atlas serves as a valuable reference for cardiac cell states and functions, advancing understanding of disease progression and molecular diversity across cardiomyopathies (Additional file 2: Fig. S1).

Methods

Human samples

We analyzed 525 human cardiac samples [6–17] (20 newly generated and 505 publicly available), including DCM, HCM, ARVC, LVNC, and non-failing (NF) controls, spanning left ventricle (LV), right ventricle (RV), and atrial regions (For more details, please refer to our Additional file 3: Supplementary Methods) [18–39].

Diseased hearts were obtained during transplantation or surgery, and NF hearts from unused donors. The relevant clinical information of individuals could be found in Additional file 1: Table S5-S11. Of these 525 samples, 20 human myocardial tissue samples were collected from 13 patients with cardiomyopathy who underwent heart transplantation or left ventricular assist device implantation or surgical septal myectomy at Sichuan Provincial People’s Hospital between 2020 and 2023. All patients provided written informed consent prior to sample collection. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Sichuan Provincial People’s Hospital (Approval No. 2022 − 424). The collected samples were snap-frozen and stored at -80℃.

snRNA-seq

Nuclei were isolated from cryopreserved myocardium and processed using the 10x Genomics Chromium platform (v3/v3.1). Libraries were sequenced on Illumina NovaSeq 6000. Raw data were processed with Cell Ranger (v7.2.0) and aligned to GRCh38. After QC, > 2 million nuclei were retained.

Data integration and analysis

We integrated new and public datasets using scanpy (v1.9.6) [20], harmonypy (v0.0.9) [21] and scGen (v2.0.0) [22], followed by principal components analysis (PCA)/uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) for dimensionality reduction. Major cell types were annotated by canonical markers, and rare populations validated with spatial transcriptomics. Differential expression was assessed by DESeq2 (v1.38.3) [30] and a negative-binomial GLMM controlling for age, sex, race, and donor. Pseudotime trajectories (Monocle2) were aligned with LVEF to define disease stages. Cell-cell communication was inferred by CellChat (v2.1.2) [39], gene modules by weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) (v1.72.5) [32], and transcription factor (TF) regulons by pyscenic (v0.12.1) [34].

Validation

Spatial transcriptomic data and RNA in situ hybridization confirmed the novel CM-like FB subpopulation. Immunofluorescence used MYBPC3 (CM marker) and DCN (FB marker).

Statistics

Two-sided tests with false discovery rate (FDR) correction were applied; P < 0.05 was considered significant. Analyses were performed in R (v4.2) and Python (v3.9).

Results

Data summary

To begin with, we constructed the Cardiac Cell Atlas by performing snRNA-seq on LV, RV and interventricular septum (IVS) tissues from 20 new samples covering 13 individuals (7 DCM, 5 HCM, 1 ARVC), and by integrating 505 public datasets covering 196 individuals (81 DCM, 25 HCM, 8 ARVC, 1 LVNC, 81 NF) [6–11]. After stringent quality control (Additional file 2: Fig. S2), we retained ~ 2 million nuclei partitioned into 14 transcriptionally defined clusters (Fig. 1a-b). Based on marker genes and biological processes, we annotated CMs, FBs, ECs, pericytes, smooth muscle cells, and immune lineages (Fig. 1a, c), in line with prior reports [6].

Altered cell type composition in HCM and DCM

Next, we compared LV samples from NF, HCM and DCM hearts. In HCM, we observed increased lymphatic EC and smooth muscle cells, along with a trend toward fewer CMs (modest reduction) and more FBs. Moreover, HCM exhibited expansion of endothelial and immune compartments, including myeloid and NK/T cells. In DCM, CMs were markedly reduced, while FBs, smooth muscle cells, myeloid and NK/T cells significantly expanded (Fig. 1d-e). Together, these shifts highlight distinct remodeling of the cardiac cellular ecosystem in HCM versus DCM.

Sub-type remodeling across major lineages

Cardiomyocytes (CMs)

We identified nine ventricular cardiomyocyte (vCM) states (vCM1.0-5, vCM1.1-1.3, vCM3.1) [7] (Fig. 2a-b). In DCM, vCM1.1, vCM1.2 and vCM5 expanded, while vCM3.1 declined. Notably, vCM5 cells express DLC1 and EBF2, regulators of adipocyte differentiation [40], suggesting adipogenic infiltration as part of DCM remodeling. In HCM, the cardioprotective vCM3.1 was dramatically reduced, while stress-response vCM3 (expressing ANKRD1, FHL1, DUSP27) was increased, consistent with hypertrophic stress and arrhythmogenic remodeling. Both diseases also showed reduced vCM2 and increased vCM5 (Fig. 2c).

Fibroblasts (FBs)

Seven FB states were defined (FB1.0-4, FB1.1-1.2) (Fig. 2d-e). In both DCM and HCM, FB2 (pro-fibrotic) expanded, while FB3 (low ECM activity) decreased (markedly reduced in DCM and modestly in HCM), reflecting enhanced fibrotic remodeling [7]. Interestingly, FB1.1 was markedly reduced in HCM but only modestly in DCM (Fig. 2f), suggesting disease-specific regulation of FB subsets.

Endothelial cells (ECs)

We identified seven EC states (EC1-2, EC5-8, mesothelial) (Fig. 2g-h). Both DCM and HCM exhibited expansion of EC5 and EC6 (modest expansion), consistent with enhanced angiogenesis. Interestingly, capillary EC1 decreased in DCM (Fig. 2i), implying reduced capillary angiogenesis in DCM.

Identification and characterization of a novel transitional fibroblast sub-type

We identified a previously unrecognized FB sub-type overexpressing cardiomyopathy-related genes including TNNT2, MYBPC3 and RYR2 (Fig. 2d-e). Spatial transcriptomics confirmed its presence, with spots co-expressing C7, PDGFRA (FB markers) and FHL2 (VCM marker) (Additional file 2: Fig. S3-S4), and our dataset showed that this population accounts for less than 5% of total FBs (Fig. 2d). To validate the presence of this population, we further performed performed immunofluorescence staining and RNA in situ hybridization on a left ventricular sample from a DCM patient using the cardiomyopathy-associated marker MYBPC3 and the FB marker DCN. The results confirmed the existence of this novel CM-like FB subpopulation (Fig. 3a-b). Pseudotime analysis (Monocle2) positioned this population between FBs and VCMs (Fig. 3c), enriched for developmental programs such as ‘heart morphogenesis’ (P = 2.64 × 10− 13) and ‘ventricle development’ (P = 1.79 × 10− 9) (Additional file 1: Table S12). These features indicate a transitional FB-to-VCM state with iPS-like characteristics. The sub-type was reduced in HCM/DCM versus NF (P < 0.05), yet enriched for ‘Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy’ (P = 1.73 × 10− 4) in HCM and for ‘regulation of anatomical structure size’ (P = 2.29 × 10− 17) and ‘mitotic cell cycle process’ (P = 1.41 × 10− 14) in DCM (Additional file 1: Table S13-S14), suggesting disease-specific roles in counteracting hypertrophy and cell cycle arrest [41–45].

To further resolve its heterogeneity, we re-clustered this population into SORBS2− (FB-like) and SORBS2+ (VCM-like) clusters (Fig. 3d, Additional file 2: Fig. S5). Pseudotime placed SORBS2− closer to FB and SORBS2+ closer to VCM (Fig. 3e). Notably, iCM reprogramming genes (GATA4, TBX5, MEF2C, ESRRG, MYOCD, ZFPM2 [46]) were enriched in SORBS2− and decreased with pseudotime, consistent with an iPS-like early state (Fig. 3f). By contrast, SORBS2+ acquired VCM-like features and expanded in HCM/DCM (Fig. 3g), implying an activated reparative trajectory in disease. Functionally, SORBS2+ showed higher scores for ‘muscle structure development’ (Additional file 2: Fig. S6), enrichment of ‘cell division’ genes in HCM/DCM versus NF (P = 3.31 × 10− 8, 1.12 × 10− 5), and elevated expression of cardiomyopathy gene sets (Fig. 3h-i). It also upregulated ‘heart contraction’ genes in DCM (P = 3.4 × 10− 4), suggesting a role in preserving contractility and counteracting ventricular dilation.

Regulatory network analysis further highlighted protective features. FOXO3, PRRX1 and ATF3 were activated in HCM/DCM versus NF, factors known to inhibit fibrosis/hypertrophy and promote CM survival [47–50], whereas HOXB2, YY2, CREB1 and ETS2 were repressed, transcription factors implicated in fibrosis, hypertrophy, apoptosis and heart failure progression [51–54] (Fig. 3j). Importantly, the transcriptional profile of the SORBS2+ cluster suggests it may not only resemble VCMs but also re-engage developmental and survival programs that are silenced in terminal heart failure, thereby providing a potential endogenous source for myocardial repair.

Cell-cell communication analysis (CellChat) showed both clusters acted as major signal senders to FB, EC and VCM, comparable to FB and stronger than EC or VCM (Fig. 3k-l, Additional file 2: Fig. S7). Interaction number and strength increased significantly in HCM/DCM compared with NF (Additional file 2: Fig. S8-S9), underscoring a central hub role for this sub-type in disease remodeling. The fact that this transitional population not only transmits but also integrates signals from multiple cardiac cell types highlights its role as a communication nexus, orchestrating compensatory responses in failing hearts.

Together, these findings define a novel FB-derived transitional population, with SORBS2+ cells representing a VCM-like reparative state that compensates for myocyte loss, supports contractility, and counteracts remodeling through both intrinsic gene programs and intercellular communication. By uncovering an endogenous population with regenerative and protective properties, our work highlights a potential therapeutic target for precision strategies in cardiomyopathy.

Similarities and differences between HCM and DCM for gene expression changes compared to NF hearts

We compared DEGs between cardiomyopathy and NF hearts in VCMs, FBs and ECs using DESeq2 and a negative-binomial GLMM controlling for age, sex, race, and donor. Both HCM and DCM showed substantial remodeling, but with distinct patterns.

VCMs (Fig. 4a). ‘Mitotic metaphase and anaphase’ genes were downregulated in HCM (P = 2.51 × 10− 4) when compared to NF hearts but upregulated in DCM (P = 3.98 × 10− 4) when compared to HCM. DNA repair, replication and G2/M checkpoint genes were significantly reduced in DCM (P = 6.31 × 10− 5-2 × 10− 3) when compared to NF hearts, consistent with DNA damage-induced apoptosis [42], whereas HCM preserved G2/M entry and reactivated fetal programs‘tissue morphogenesis’ P = 2 × 10− 5) compared to NF hearts, supporting hypertrophic growth [44]. DCM also upregulated ‘cell morphogenesis’ (P = 1 × 10− 13) and ‘sensory organ development’ (P = 1.25 × 10− 7) when compared to NF hearts, consistent with senescence and loss of identity [41, 43, 45].

In addition, HCM exhibited specific downregulation of P53- and apoptosis-related pathways. ‘Apoptosis’ (P = 2 × 10− 3) and ‘Regulation of TP53 Degradation’ (P = 2.78 × 10− 3) were suppressed in HCM compared to DCM, while both ‘Apoptosis’ (P = 2.51 × 10− 3) and ‘Transcriptional Regulation by TP53’ (P = 5.01 × 10− 5) were reduced in HCM relative to NF (Fig. 4a). This suggests that HCM CMs suppress apoptotic signaling to maintain cell survival [55], whereas DCM CMs, with impaired DNA repair and checkpoint activity, are more vulnerable to apoptosis and progressive loss.

FBs (Additional file 2: Fig. S10a). Both subtypes exhibited strong enrichment of ECM organization (HCM P = 3.98 × 10− 10; DCM P = 7.94 × 10− 14) and blood vessel development (HCM P = 4.58 × 10− 11; DCM P = 1.27 × 10− 5) when compared to NF hearts. HCM uniquely upregulated ‘response to growth factor’ (P = 3.98 × 10− 7) [56], when compared to DCM. while DCM specifically increased ‘regulation of heart contraction’ (P = 5.15 × 10− 11) when compared to NF hearts, consistent with compensation for hypocontractility [57]. Subtype analysis showed FB2 expansion in both diseases, with stronger FB1.1 reduction in HCM (Fig. 2f).

ECs (Additional file 2: Fig. S10b). Both groups showed downregulation of ‘regulation of heart contraction’ (HCM stronger, consistent with hypercontractility [58, 59]) (HCM P = 9.2 × 10− 25; DCM P = 1.9 × 10− 3) and upregulation of vasculature development (HCM P = 2.84 × 10− 13; DCM P = 9.59 × 10− 5) when compared to NF hearts. HCM further reactivated PI3K-Akt (P = 3.2 × 10− 3) and fetal signaling, with stronger repression of FAO, OXPHOS, and mitochondrial genes (P = 10− 7-10− 16). EC subtype analysis revealed EC5/6 expansion in both diseases, while capillary EC1 decreased in DCM (Fig. 2i).

WGCNA confirmed coordinated modules: repression of sister chromatid segregation in HCM VCMs (Fig. 4b), OXPHOS in HCM FBs (Additional file 2: Fig. S11a), and contraction/energy metabolism in HCM ECs (Additional file 2: Fig. S11b). SCENIC analysis in VCM (Fig. 4c, Additional file 2: Fig. S12) showed HCM-specific repression of ATF3/FOS/JUNB regulons (apoptosis/P53), and activation of ZNF704 (growth/Wnt) and FLI1 (inflammation) [60, 61].

In summary, HCM is defined by preserved proliferative capacity, suppression of P53-mediated apoptosis, and FB/EC-driven hypertrophic remodeling, while DCM shows impaired DNA repair, enhanced apoptosis, FB contractile remodeling, and delayed endothelial activation. These divergent pathways are consistent with the preserved systolic function in HCM versus progressive failure in DCM.

Gene expression changes for ARVC and LVNC compared to NF hearts

Similarly, we analyzed DEGs for ARVC and LVNC compared to NF hearts in VCMs using DESeq2 (ARVC) or FindMarkers (LVNC). Both ARVC and LVNC showed substantial remodeling, but with distinct patterns.

‘FOXO PATHWAY’ (P = 2.02 × 10− 3), ‘PI3K-Akt signaling pathway’ (P = 3.48 × 10− 3), ‘cellular response to hormone stimulus’ (P = 3.33 × 10− 3), and ‘lipid biosynthetic process’ (P = 4.34 × 10− 3) were elevated in ARVC compared to NF hearts for VCMs (Additional file 2: Fig. S13a). Consistently, it is known that the up-regulation of FOXO and dysregulation of PI3K-Akt signaling pathways are known to promotes arrhythmias [62, 63], and evidence suggests that pharmacologically targeting the PI3K-Akt pathway exhibits significant anti-arrhythmic potential [62]. In addition, elevated serum testosterone levels in males and decreased estradiol levels in females are independently associated with major arrhythmic cardiovascular events in ARVC, and directly influence disease pathology [64], which supports our observation of elevated ‘cellular response to hormone stimulus’. Moreover, ARVC is characterized by right ventricular fibrofatty infiltration [65] which is in consistence with activation of ‘lipid biosynthetic process’ found in our study.

In contrast, ‘signaling by WNT’ (P = 0.047), ‘cardiac muscle cell development’ (P = 2.47 × 10− 4), ‘muscle contraction’ (P = 6.4 × 10− 3) and ‘cytoskeleton in muscle cells’ (P = 4.34 × 10− 3) were repressed in ARVC compared to NF hearts for VCMs (Additional file 2: Fig. S13a). Consistently, it is reported that partial nuclear displacement of plakoglobin from desmosomes leading to suppression of the canonical Wnt signaling in the cardiac progenitor cells is a putative mechanism for ARVC [66], and Wnt signaling is an important switch regulator of myogenesis versus adipogenesis in the differentiation of cardiac progenitor cells [67, 68]. Moreover, ARVC may develop isolated right heart failure, and the mechanism for the RV failure is dilation, thinning of the wall and progressive loss of contractile function [69], supporting our observation of repressed ‘muscle contraction’ in ARVC. In addition, both adherens junction and desmosome, which are components of the intercalated disk structure whose disruption is closely related to ARVC, also involve connections to cytoskeleton [65]. Therefore, it is not surprising to find ‘cytoskeleton in muscle cells’ repressed in ARVC.

‘trabecula morphogenesis’ (P = 0.037) was elevated in LVNC compared to NF hearts for VCMs, which is in accordance with hypertrabeculation in LVNC [70] (Additional file 2: Fig. S13b). ‘regulation of heart contraction’ (P = 1.15 × 10− 17), ‘cardiac conduction’ (P = 1.97 × 10− 11), ‘dilated cardiomyopathy’ (DCM) (P = 7.46 × 10− 10), ‘hypertrophic cardiomyopathy’ (HCM) (P = 3.07 × 10− 9), ‘heart development’ (P = 7.69 × 10− 13) and ‘cellular respiration’ (P = 2.9 × 10− 11) were repressed in LVNC compared to NF hearts for VCMs (Additional file 2: Fig. S13b). Consistently, it is reported that hemodynamic features of LVNC include decreased cardiac contractility, and conduction defects are frequently reported [70]. In addition, for 80 genes considered genetic causes of LVNC, 20 genes were shared between LVNC and DCM, and 18 genes were shared between LVNC and HCM [70]. Moreover, for LVNC, the process of compaction, a critical stage of normal cardiac development, is thought to be arrested, supporting our observation of repressed ‘heart development’ [70]. Last but not least, LVNC is found to be associated with mutations in the mitochondrial genome [70], therefore, repression of ‘cellular respiration’ is expected.

For FBs of ARVC, ‘Signaling by Rho GTPases’ (P = 7.76 × 10− 6), ‘regulation of actin cytoskeleton organization’ (P = 1.23 × 10− 3), and ‘PI3K Akt signaling’ (P = 2.09 × 10− 4 ), were elevated in ARVC compared to NF hearts (Additional file 2: Fig. S14a). Consistently, with increased fibrosis in ARVC [71], Rho GTPase signaling pathway is known to represent a convergent approach to targeting fibrosis, interacting with Rho-associated, coiled-coil containing protein kinase (ROCK) kinase to increase F-actin polymers and decrease G-actin monomers [72]. In addition, supporting our observation of elevated ‘PI3K Akt signaling’, PI3K Akt signaling has been reported to promote cardiac fibrosis [73, 74]. For FBs of LVNC, ‘Signaling by Rho GTPases’ (P = 5.5 × 10− 28), ‘regulation of actin filament polymerization’ (P = 1.26 × 10− 7), ‘cell division’ (P = 4.9 × 10− 19), ‘Signaling by Receptor Tyrosine Kinases’ (P = 1.32 × 10− 16) and ‘MAPK family signaling cascades’ (P = 6.31 × 10− 8) were elevated in LVNC compared to NF hearts (Additional file 2: Fig. S14b). Consistently, myocardial fibrosis is a common feature in LVNC [75], and Rho GTPase signaling pathway and F-actin polymer assembly are both closely related to tissue fibrosis [72]. In addition, PDGFs signaling through PDGFRs utilizes the general strategy for receptor tyrosine kinases (RTKs) to activate cardiac fibrosis [76, 77], and MAPK plays critical role in driving myocardial fibrosis [78].

For ECs of ARVC, ‘blood vessel morphogenesis’ (P = 8.32 × 10− 4) and ‘regulation of cell cycle G1/S phase transition’ (P = 1.17 × 10− 3) were elevated in ARVC compared to NF hearts (Additional file 2: Fig. S15a), in accordance with the report that tissue damage and hypoxia in the ARVC heart likely up-regulate VEGF, driving the formation of new vessels within the fibrofatty scar tissue to support tissue repair [79]. For ECs of LVNC, ‘PDGF pathway’ (P = 4.07 × 10− 3) and ‘MAPK family signaling cascades’ (P = 2.19 × 10− 9 ) elevated in LVNC compared to NF hearts (Additional file 2: Fig. S15b). These pathways were generally believed to be involved in blood vessel maturation [80, 81], and their up-regulation in LVNC adults might be relevant to increased angiogenesis induced by LVNC microvascular dysfunction related local hypoxia [82].

hdWGCNA confirmed coordinated modules: repression of ‘cardiocyte differentiation’ (P = 1.83 × 10− 4), ‘regulation of the force of heart contraction’ (P = 0.015), ‘muscle contraction’ (P = 1.11 × 10− 6), ‘protein localization to cell junction’ (P = 4.04 × 10− 3), and ‘actin filament-based process’ (P = 3.17 × 10− 3), as well as activation of ‘Transcription factor regulation in adipogenesis’ (P = 3.78 × 10− 4), ’response to steroid hormone’ (P = 3.61 × 10− 3), ‘regulation of intracellular steroid hormone receptor signaling pathway’ (P = 3.8 × 10− 3), and ‘Transcriptional Regulation by TP53’ (P = 1.6 × 10− 3) in ARVC VCMs (Additional file 2: Fig. S16).

SCENIC analysis in VCM showed ARVC-specific repression of STAT3 (promoting cardiomyocyte survival) [83], and activation of PPARG (adipogenesis) et al., while both diseases (ARVC and LVNC) shared elevated FOXO activity linked to arrhythmias in ARVC [63] and cardiomyocyte proliferation repression in LVNC [84] (Additional file 2: Fig. S17).

Dynamic cell-cell communications across distinct progression stages of DCM and HCM

To stratify clinical severity, we applied LVEF cutoffs of 50% for HCM and 25% for DCM, consistent with clinical practice where LVEF < 50% in HCM indicates transition to systolic dysfunction [85] and < 25% in DCM marks advanced disease with high mortality risk [86]. Notably, lower LVEF groups aligned with later pseudotime states across VCMs, FBs, and ECs (Additional file 2: Fig. S18), validating pseudotime stratification as a proxy for disease progression (Additional file 2: Fig. S19, Fig. 5a-b).

CellChat analysis revealed distinct stage-specific dynamics. In early HCM versus NF, ligand-receptor interactions were markedly increased, but declined progressively in middle and late stages (Fig. 5c-d). By contrast, early DCM showed weaker signaling, which was globally upregulated in the mid-stage before declining later (Fig. 5c and e). Thus, HCM achieves functional compensation early, whereas DCM exhibits delayed and maladaptive remodeling.

At the early stage, FB was the central organizer in HCM (Fig. 5d), acting on EC and VCM receptors (e.g., integrins) via ECM proteins (collagen, FN1), and releasing BMP and IGF to activate PI3K-AKT (growth) and TGFβ (DNA repair) in VCM (Fig. 5f). VCMs upregulated CADM, FGF1 and NRG, establishing a positive FB-VCM loop, while secreting VEGF to promote EC angiogenesis (Fig. 5f). ECs in turn provided TGFβ, reinforcing VCM repair (Fig. 5f). These interactions suggest VCMs in HCM preserve replication and DNA repair, avoiding early apoptosis. In early DCM, FB and VCM jointly dominated (Fig. 5g-h), but EC-FB and EC-VCM interactions were absent (Additional file 2: Fig. S20). Thus, VCMs did not receive EC-derived TGFβ or FB-derived BMP-critical for DNA repair and AKT-mediated growth-contributing to impaired compensation and dysfunction [42, 44].

Shared features included FB-VCM ECM-integrin signaling and VEGF-mediated VCM-EC cross-talk. Differences were: (1) FB alone dominated in HCM, but FB and VCM jointly in DCM; (2) ECs actively contributed in HCM but were “bystanders” in early DCM. The absence of EC-FB/VCM signaling in DCM likely impaired biochemical remodeling.

At the middle stage, HCM signaling declined (Fig. 5c), with weakened FB-VCM/EC ECM-integrin interactions and reduced BMP/TGFβ inputs (Additional file 2: Fig. S21). In contrast, DCM signaling was globally enhanced (Fig. 5c), with ECs now actively interacting with FBs and VCMs via collagen, laminin, JAM and FN1, and delivering BMP (Fig. 5i, Additional file 2: Fig. S22). These findings suggest that HCM completes early mechanical and chemical compensation, whereas DCM mounts delayed, less efficient remodeling.

At the late stage, both subtypes showed global attenuation (Fig. 5c). In HCM, only EC-VCM PTPRM persisted (Additional file 2: Fig. S23a). In DCM, ECM-receptor interactions decreased but information flow remained relatively high, indicating residual physical remodeling. However, ECs largely lost FB/VCM communication (Additional file 2: Fig. S23b, Fig. 5e), reflecting severely impaired chemical remodeling.

Pathway-level analyses confirmed these patterns. Early HCM activated DNA replication and repair while limiting apoptosis, consistent with effective endoreplication and DNA damage control. Early DCM suppressed replication/repair and increased apoptosis. Both subtypes upregulated DNA repair pathways, but HCM showed stronger activation, supported by cell cycle-dependent repair (Additional file 2: Fig. S24). Glutathione metabolism and autophagy—key defenses against reactive oxygen species (ROS)-induced DNA damage44,45—were elevated in HCM but reduced in DCM (Additional file 2: Fig. S24). Together, these findings suggest that HCM achieves early functional compensation via replication, robust DNA repair and ROS clearance, whereas inadequate repair in DCM leads to apoptotic loss and maladaptive remodeling.

Discussion

In this study, we constructed the Cardiac Cell Atlas, the largest single-nucleus transcriptomic resource of human cardiomyopathies (> 2 million nuclei from 525 cardiac samples: 20 newly generated and 505 publicly available). This dataset revealed a previously unrecognized FB-CM transitional subpopulation expressing cardiomyopathy genes (TNNT2, RYR2, MYBPC3), further stratified into SORBS2− (FB-like) and SORBS2+ (VCM-like) clusters. The SORBS2+ cluster emerged as a VCM-like state, associated with gene programs and intercellular cross-talk that may support reparative processes in cardiomyopathy. Comparative analyses delineated divergent remodeling trajectories: HCM preserved replication, DNA repair, and early intercellular compensation, while DCM exhibited defective repair, apoptosis, and delayed endothelial participation. Pseudotime stratification aligned with LVEF, linking molecular remodeling with clinical severity. Together, these findings provide a cellular framework for disease progression and suggest candidate biomarkers and therapeutic targets for precision cardiomyopathy management.

The origin and function of this transitional subpopulation remain incompletely understood. We found that iCM conversion genes (GATA4, TBX5, ESRRG, MEF2C, MYOCD, ZFPM2) correlated negatively with pseudotime and were enriched in SORBS2− cells, consistent with a FB-to-VCM trajectory. Prior studies have shown that FBs can be reprogrammed into iCMs with GMT (GATA4, MEF2C, and TBX5) factors in vitro and in vivo [87], suggesting that our observation may represent in vivo evidence of such spontaneous conversion. Functionally, SORBS2+ cells expressed higher levels of cardiomyopathy-related genes, with transcription factors such as FOXO3, PRRX1, and ATF3 -- known to suppress fibrosis and protect CMs [47–49]—activated in disease states. SORBS2+ cells also expanded in both DCM and HCM relative to NF, consistent with reports of SORBS2 upregulation in failing hearts [88], and Sorbs2 knockout in mice induces DCM [89]. These findings support the hypothesis that the SORBS2^+^ cluster represents a VCM-like state with potential reparative properties. Nevertheless, whether this population fully recapitulates iCM function in vivo requires further validation. Importantly, the plasticity of cardiac FBs may provide new conceptual frameworks and candidate therapeutic targets.

Approximately 40–50% of DCM and HCM cases are associated with pathogenic mutations [90, 91], yet mutations alone cannot explain disease onset. Our results indicate that different genetic backgrounds can converge on similar single-cell remodeling programs, underscoring that microenvironmental cues and intercellular regulation—rather than mutations alone—may be decisive. This view challenges genetic determinism and highlights opportunities for therapies that target common remodeling mechanisms beyond gene correction. Mechanistically, HCM displayed elevated DNA replication and repair, supporting hypertrophic growth, whereas DCM showed impaired repair, cell cycle arrest, and apoptosis, consistent with progressive myocyte loss and poorer prognosis (Additional file 1: Table S15).

Our atlas also reconciles prior inconsistencies between subtypes. Chaffin et al. [6]. profiled ~ 600,000 nuclei from advanced DCM and HCM and reported broad alterations compared with NF hearts but surprisingly few differences between subtypes, suggesting transcriptional convergence at end-stage disease. In contrast, by integrating > 2 million nuclei and stratifying samples by LVEF and pseudotime, we demonstrate that divergence becomes evident at earlier and intermediate phases, particularly in the timing of endothelial involvement (Fig. 6). This aligns with recent integrative analyses of multiple cardiomyopathies, which revealed a shared fibrotic-inflammatory axis but also subtype-specific remodeling biases—immune-driven in DCM and ECM/vascular remodeling in HCM [92]. Thus, our findings extend prior work by showing that early divergence is masked in late-stage datasets, highlighting the importance of stage-resolved analysis.

Methodologically, our study benefits from integrating > 2 million nuclei from diverse subtypes, ensuring statistical power to detect both common and rare states. By combining DESeq2 with a negative-binomial GLMM, we explicitly controlled for age, sex, race, and donor effects. Pseudotime analysis coupled with LVEF directly linked molecular remodeling to clinical severity, a rarely addressed dimension. Complementary approaches—including CellChat, WGCNA, and SCENIC—allowed cross-validation and revealed coordinated remodeling programs. Together, these innovations reinforce the robustness and translational value of the Cardiac Cell Atlas. Finally, we provide a freely accessible database of cardiomyopathy single-cell profiles as a community resource (available at [93]).

Limitations and outlook

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, most samples were obtained from end-stage surgical or transplant patients, which may bias findings toward advanced remodeling and limit extrapolation to earlier disease stages. Second, single-nucleus transcriptomics lacks spatial resolution and functional validation, so inferred cell-cell interactions and transitional states require confirmation with spatial and experimental approaches. Third, the cross-sectional design, based on single-time-point sampling rather than longitudinal follow-up, restricts temporal inference even with pseudotime analysis. Finally, our focus on DCM and HCM necessitates validation in larger, independent cohorts and across additional cardiomyopathy subtypes.

Conclusions

Despite these limitations, we present the largest single-nucleus atlas of human cardiomyopathies to date, identifying a previously unrecognized FB-CM transitional subpopulation and delineating stage-specific remodeling trajectories in DCM and HCM. These findings provide mechanistic insights into disease progression and suggest clinically relevant biomarkers and therapeutic targets to advance precision risk stratification and therapy.
