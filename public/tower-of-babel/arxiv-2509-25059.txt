Near-the-Axis Universality in Last Passage Percolation
Sam McKeown, Xinyi Zhang

Abstract
This note establishes a universal directed landscape limit for last passage percolation models in an intermediate scaling regime. We find as a quick consequence the transversal fluctuations for geodesics taken near the axis. We extend the technique of Bodineau and Martin, who in [ BM05 ] have already shown universal one-point fluctuations in this regime.

1
Introduction

The directed landscape
[
DOV22
]
is the conjectured universal scaling limit for a wide class of random planar metrics and other models belonging to the Kardar-Parisi-Zhang (KPZ) class in
1+1
dimensions. Last passage percolation (LPP) is one such model, wherein we consider i.i.d weights
\{\omega(a):a\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}\}
attached to the vertices of the integer lattice
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
. Between vertices
a,\,b\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
, with
a\leq b
componentwise, these weights define a
passage time

L_{\omega}(a;b)=\max_{\pi\in\Pi(a;b)}\sum_{r\in\pi}\omega(r).

(1)

Here
\Pi(a;b)
consists of all weakly increasing nearest-neighbour paths on the lattice
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
between
a
and
b
, namely those of the form
\pi=(\pi_{0},\pi_{1},\dots,\pi_{n})
with
\pi_{k+1}-\pi_{k}\in\{e_{1},e_{2}\}
and
\pi_{0}=a
,
\pi_{n}\in\{b-e_{1},b-e_{2}\}
. We use
e_{1}
and
e_{2}
to denote the horizontal and vertical unit vectors, respectively.

The conjectured joint convergence of the passage times
\{L_{\omega}(a;b):a,\,b\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}\}
to the directed landscape has only been rigorously established for geometric and exponential weights. A closely related model for which this limit is also known is Brownian last passage percolation (BLPP). Instead of the lattice
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
, our model now lives on the semi-continuous space
\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{Z}
, and the weights are replaced by a collection of independent two-sided Brownian motions
\{B_{k}(x):k\in\mathbb{Z},\,x\in\mathbb{R}\}
. Passage times in this setting are defined analogously: if
(x,m)\leq(y,n)
, then we define

L_{B}(x,m;y,n)=\max_{x=z_{m-1}\leq z_{m}\leq\cdots\leq z_{n}=y}\sum_{i=m}^{n}[B_{i}(z_{i})-B_{i}(z_{i-1})].

(2)

The desired directed landscape limit looks at rescalings of the variables
\{L_{\omega}(a;b)\}
when
a,\,b
are inside the box
[0,n]\times[0,n]
, then takes
n\to\infty
. Such limits remain out of reach for general weight distributions. However, Bodineau and Martin considered in
[
BM05
]
a different scaling using thin rectangles
[0,n]\times[0,n^{\alpha}]
, with
\alpha<3/7
, and showed that the Komlòs-Major-Tusnàdy (KMT) coupling of the weights to Brownian motion can be used to transfer knowledge of BLPP to general LPP in this regime. Specifically, they show that under the appropriate normalisation, the passage times
L_{\omega}\big\lparen 0,0;n,n^{\alpha}\big\rparen
converge to the Tracy-Widom GUE distribution. A result of this type was obtained independently by Baik and Suidan in
[
BS05
]
, using instead Skorokhod embedding to relate the environment to Brownian motion.

Neither pair of authors extracted richer information from this comparison, owing to the fact that much of the technology regarding the scaling limits of planar directed metrics did not yet exist. In this note we observe that modern techniques, namely the tools developed by Dauvergne and Virag in
[
DV22
]
and improvements on the KMT coupling due to Sakhanenko, can extend this idea beyond one-point convergence to a full directed landscape limit. The universal transversal fluctuations of geodesics conjectured in
[
BM05
]
fall out as a consequence.

2
Preliminaries

We briefly introduce the central objects in the study of such scaling limits. We do not attempt to precisely define these or consider any of the attendant technicalities, and refer the reader to
[
DOV22
,
DV22
]
for a more thorough treatment.

From here on, we normalise the weights so that
\mathbb{E}[\omega]=0
and
\mathbb{E}\lvert\omega\rvert^{2}=1
. Also assume that we have some
p>2
such that
\mathbb{E}\lvert\omega\rvert^{p}<\infty
. Notice that we do not assume the distribution to be bounded above or below. This means that, after reversing signs, everything we say for LPP will also hold in directed first passage percolation.

2.1
Melons and the Airy line ensemble

By a
line ensemble
we will mean a (possibly finite) sequence of functions
F=(F_{1},F_{2},\dots)
, with each
F_{k}:\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\to\mathbb{R}
continuous. We will also see line ensembles with domain
\mathbb{R}
.

An important transformation on such line ensembles is the
melon map
, a version of the Robinson–Schensted–Knuth correspondence with many pleasing properties
[
DNV22
]
. Given an integer
n\geq 1
and points
(x,m),\,(y,n)\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\times\mathbb{N}
, with
x\leq y
and
1\leq m\leq n-k+1
, we define
Q^{k}(x,m;y,n)
to be the set of
k
-tuples of cadlag step functions
\pi=(\pi^{1},\dots,\pi^{k})
such that:

•

Each
\pi^{i}:[x,y]\to\{m,\dots,n\}
is a non-decreasing cadlag function.

•

We have
\pi^{i}(y)=n
for all
i
.

•

If
i>j
, then
\pi^{i}(u)>\pi^{j}(u)
for all
u\in[x,y)
.

We may think of such a
\pi
as a collection of
directed
,
non-intersecting paths
on
\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{N}
with their endpoints at
(y,n)
.

Define integrals with respect to such paths by

\int dF\circ\pi^{i}=\sum_{j=m}^{n}F_{j}(x^{i}_{j+1})-F_{j}({x^{i}_{j}}),

where the times
x^{i}_{j}
are defined by
[x^{i}_{j},x^{i}_{j+1})=\{z:\pi^{i}(z)=j\}
, or
x^{i}_{j}=x^{i}_{j+1}
if this set is empty. All of this is just to say that the integral of an ensemble along a path
\pi^{i}
is the increment of the function
F_{\pi^{i}(z)}(z)
on the interval
[x,y]
, less the jumps. An integral with respect to a collection
\pi=(\pi^{1},\dots,\pi^{k})
is just

\int dF\circ\pi=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\int dF\circ\pi^{i}.

We now define the melon
W^{n}F=((W^{n}F)_{1},\dots,(W^{n}F)_{n})
. The components are determined by

\sum_{i=1}^{k}(W^{n}F)_{i}(y)=\sup_{\pi\in Q^{k}(0,1;y,n)}\int dF\circ\pi.

(3)

In particular,
(W^{n}F)_{1}(y)
has the form of a last passage time as defined in (
2
), from
(0,1)
to
(y,n)
. Observe that melons are non-intersecting: for all
y\geq 0
,

(W^{n}F)_{1}(y)\geq(W^{n}F)_{2}(y)\geq\cdots\geq(W^{n}F)_{n}(y).

The
Airy line ensemble
is an object conjectured to be a universal scaling limit for such melons. It is a random non-intersecting line ensemble
\mathfrak{A}=(\mathfrak{A}_{1},\mathfrak{A}_{2},\dots)
, where the
\mathfrak{A}_{k}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
are almost surely continuous and satisfy

\mathfrak{A}_{1}(y)>\mathfrak{A}_{2}(y)>\cdots

for all
y\in\mathbb{R}
. The next theorem will provide our working definition of the Airy line ensemble.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
B=(B_{1},B_{2},\dots)
consist of standard Brownian motions. The Airy line ensemble can be found as the limit

n^{1/6}\big\lparen(W^{n}B)_{k}(1+2yn^{-1/3})-2\sqrt{n}-2yn^{1/6}\big\rparen\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\to}}\mathfrak{A}_{k}(y),

the convergence here being in distribution, uniform on compact sets.

We take the above statement from Theorem 2.4 of
[
DOV22
]
, but this form of convergence was first proved by Johansson in
[
Joh03
]
. It follows from looking at the top lines of our ensembles that
\mathfrak{A}_{1}(y)
serves as the scaling limit for
L_{B}(0,1;1+2yn^{-1/3},n)
.

A more intrinsic characterisation of the Airy line ensemble was proven, with much effort, by the authors of
[
AH23
]
. It is the unique random line ensemble such that
\mathfrak{A}_{1}(y)-y^{2}
is stationary, and which satisfies the so-called Brownian Gibbs property. Essentially, the distribution of
\mathfrak{A}
restricted to a compact set, conditioned on the boundary values, is equal to that of non-intersecting Brownian bridges.

2.2
The Airy sheet and directed landscape

The Airy line ensemble provides the scaling limit for a one-parameter slice of BLPP, but of course we would like to take a limit in all four parameters jointly. The
Airy sheet
is the natural two-parameter extension, and the
directed landscape
is the result of the full, four-parameter limit. The directed landscape is a random function
\mathcal{L}:\mathbb{R}^{4}_{\uparrow}\to\mathbb{R}
, where
\mathbb{R}^{4}_{\uparrow}=\{(x,t;y,s)\in\mathbb{R}^{4}:t<s\text{ or }(x,t)=(y,s)\}
. The Airy sheet can be realised as the marginal
\mathcal{S}(x,y)=\mathcal{L}(x,0;y,1)
.

As we do not need the details of the constructions, we will content ourselves in recalling how they arise as limits of BLPP. Write
(x,t)_{n}=(t+2xn^{-1/3},\lfloor tn\rfloor)

Theorem 2.2

(Theorem 1.5 of
[
DOV22
]
)
.

Let
B=(B_{1},B_{2},\dots)
be a sequence of standard Brownian motions, as before. The directed landscape appears as the limit

n^{1/6}\big\lparen L_{B}\big\lparen(x,t)_{n};(y,s)_{n}\big\rparen-2(s-t)\sqrt{n}-2(y-x)n^{1/6}\big\rparen\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\to}}\mathcal{L}(x,t;y,s).

The convergence here is in distribution, uniform on compact sets.

Both objects may be defined directly in terms of the Airy line ensemble. The details can be found in
[
DV22
]
.

2.3
Geodesics in the directed landscape

Call the paths
\pi
which attain the maximum in (
1
) or (
2
) or (
3
) (with
k=1
)
geodesics
. When our variables are continuous these paths are unique almost surely, but to avoid ambiguity let us always take the right-most maximising path to be
the
geodesic.

We adopt a somewhat awkward convention for viewing these as non-decreasing cadlag functions, which will make our convergence result easier to state. Given points
a\leq b\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
, the geodesic
\pi
realising
L_{\omega}(a;b)
, and
v\in[a\cdot e_{2},b\cdot e_{2}]
, define

\gamma_{\omega}(a;b;v)=\min\{u\in\mathbb{Z}:(u,\lfloor v\rfloor)\in\pi\}.

Similarly, given
x\leq y\in\mathbb{R}
and
m\leq n\in\mathbb{Z}
, let
\pi:[x,y]\to\{m,\dots,n\}
be a path attaining the maximum in
(
2
)
(or with the collection of Brownian motions replaced by some line ensemble
F
), and take
v\in[m,n]
. Define

\gamma_{F}(x,m;y,n;v)=\min\{u\in[x,y]:\pi(u)=\lfloor v\rfloor\}.

Essentially, the functions
\gamma
encode the geodesics by taking a position on the vertical axis and giving the position on the horizontal axis at which the path reaches this level.

We can also define geodesics in the directed landscape. For
(x,t;y,s)\in\mathbb{R}^{4}_{\uparrow}
, define
\Gamma(x,t;y,s;v)
to be the almost surely unique path with the property that for each
v\in[t,s]
, one has

\mathcal{L}(x,t;y,s)=\mathcal{L}(x,t;\Gamma(x,t;y,s;v),v)+\mathcal{L}(\Gamma(x,t;y,s;v),v;y,s).

There exists a great literature on the properties of these geodesics, but we content ourselves with an elementary distributional equivalence.

Proposition 2.3
.

Take
x<y
and
t<s
. Then

\left\{\frac{\Gamma\big\lparen x,t;y,s;t+v(s-t)\big\rparen-x-v(y-x)}{(s-t)^{2/3}}:v\in[0,1]\right\}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\{\Gamma(0,0;0,1;v):v\in[0,1]\}.

This is an application of Lemma 7 of
[
Bha24
]
and the symmetries listed above it. We will simply write
\Gamma(v)
for
\Gamma(0,0;0,1;v)
.

2.5
Strong approximations by Brownian motion

Recall the environment of weights
\{\omega(a):a\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}\}
. Define a line ensemble
F=(F_{1},F_{2},\dots)
with domain
\mathbb{R}
as follows:

•

When
u\geq 1
is an integer, set
F_{i}(u)=\sum_{j=0}^{u-1}\omega(j,i)
.

•

When
u\leq 0
is an integer, set
F_{i}(u)=\sum_{j=u}^{-1}\omega(j,i)
.

•

For non-integer
u\in\mathbb{R}
, let
F_{i}(u)
be given by the linear interpolation between
F_{i}(\lfloor u\rfloor)
and
F_{i}(\lceil u\rceil)
.

When the
\omega
have finite variance, Donsker’s theorem tells us that the ensemble
F
converges, after centring and rescaling, to a collection of independent Brownian motions. When additional moments are assumed, this convergence can be made quantitative by way of strong couplings to Brownian motions.

The most famous result in this direction, and the one utilised in
[
BM05
]
, is due to Komlòs, Major and Tusnàdy
[
KMT76
,
Maj76
]
. While this would suffice for hypograph convergence, we are able to upgrade to compact convergence using the yet stronger coupling due to Sakhanenko
[
Sak85
]
. We use the translation found in
[
Zaĭ13
]
. Recall that we assume the
\omega
have finite
p
-th moment, for
p>2
.

Proposition 2.4

(
[
Zaĭ13
, Theorem 6]
)
.

Write
F=F_{i}
for one of the independent random walks in our ensemble. There is a constant
c
and a coupled Brownian motion
B
such that for all
n
,

\mathbb{E}[\max_{u\in[0,n]}\lvert F(u)-B(u)\rvert^{p}]\leq cn.

(4)

Strictly speaking, the original statement only covers integer times. We prove the (elementary) extension to all real times at the end of
Section

4
. Jensen’s inequality yields a useful corollary.

Proposition 2.5
.

In the same setting as
Proposition

2.4
, we have

\mathbb{E}[\max_{u\in[0,n]}\lvert F(u)-B(u)\rvert]\leq cn^{1/p}.

(5)

3
Results

We will establish versions of
Theorems

2.1
and
2.2
for general last passage times, but under a different scaling. Rather than boxes of dimension
n\times n^{\alpha}
with
0<\alpha<1
, as used in
[
BM05
]
, it will be notationally convenient to use dimensions
n^{\beta}\times n
, with
\beta>1
.

To allow the statements to fit horizontally on the page, let
(x,t)_{n,\beta}=(tn^{\beta}+2xn^{\beta-1/3},\lfloor tn\rfloor)
and define

W^{n,\beta}F(y)=n^{(1-3\beta)/6}W^{n}F\big\lparen 0,1;(y,1)_{n,\beta}\big\rparen-2n^{2/3}-2yn^{1/3}.

(6)

Also define

d^{n,\beta}_{\omega}(x,t;y,s)=n^{(1-3\beta)/6}L_{\omega}\big\lparen(x,t)_{n,\beta};(y,s)_{n,\beta}\big\rparen-2(s-t)n^{2/3}-2(y-x)n^{1/3},

(7)

and similarly for
d^{n,\beta}_{B}
.

Our first result is Airy line ensemble convergence for general random walks under this scaling.

Theorem 3.1
.

We have
W^{n,\beta}F\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\to}}\mathfrak{A}
compactly, whenever
\beta>\tfrac{7}{3}\cdot\tfrac{p}{p-2}
.

From the Airy line ensemble limit we can infer a directed landscape limit for the passage times. This is more or less immediate given the tools of
[
DV22
]
, except that there is an annoying technicality in the conversion between the fully discrete LPP and the semi-discrete BLPP.

Theorem 3.2
.

We have
d^{n,\beta}_{\omega}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\to}}\mathcal{L}
whenever
\beta>\tfrac{7}{3}\cdot\tfrac{p}{p-2}
, where this limit is in the sense of hypograph convergence. Moreover, this convergence is compact if
p>5
.

In particular, if
\omega
has finite moments of all orders, then these hold with
\beta>7/3
. The conjectured directed landscape limit for LPP corresponds to taking
\beta=1
.

That hypograph convergence implies uniform convergence of geodesics
[
DV22
, Theorem 8.7]
gives us the following distributional limits for geodesics near the axis.

Corollary 3.3
.

Take
(x,t;y,s)\in\mathbb{R}^{4}_{\uparrow}
. Let
\gamma_{n}=\gamma_{\omega}((x,t)_{n,\beta};(y,s)_{n,\beta})
be the cadlag function encoding a geodesic for
L_{\omega}
, taking the integer parts of the arguments if needed. For
\beta
as above,

\left\{\frac{\gamma_{n}\big\lparen\lfloor nt\rfloor+v(\lfloor ns\rfloor-\lfloor nt\rfloor)\big\rparen-n^{\beta}(t+v(s-t))-2n^{\beta-1/3}(x+v(y-x))}{2(s-t)^{2/3}n^{\beta-1/3}}:v\in[0,1]\right\}\\
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\to}}\{\Gamma(v):v\in[0,1]\},

where the distributional convergence is with respect to uniform convergence.

Remark 3.4
.

When we specialise to
(x,t;y,s)=(0,0;0,1)
, this turns into the much more palatable

\left\{\frac{\gamma_{\omega}\big\lparen 0,0;\lfloor n^{\beta}\rfloor,n;vn\big\rparen-vn^{\beta}}{2n^{\beta-1/3}}:v\in[0,1]\right\}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\to}}\{\Gamma(v):v\in[0,1]\}.

These limits are just a combination of geodesic convergence and the normalisation found in
Proposition

2.3
. From this we can identify the transversal fluctuation exponent in our stretched regime as
\beta-1/3
. Up to a difference in parameterisation, this is the exponent predicted in
[
BM05
]
.

4
Proofs

We will follow
[
BM05
]
and show that the strong coupling is strong enough to imply that the limits of
Theorems

3.1
and
3.2
hold in general once they hold for Brownian motion. The limits in the case of Brownian motion are a simple application of Gaussian scaling:
L_{B}(x,t;y,s)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}a^{-1/2}L_{B}(ax,t;ay,s)
. We arrive at the following.

Lemma 4.1
.

For all
0\leq\beta<\infty
, we have
W^{n,\beta}B\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\to}}\mathfrak{A}
and
d^{n,\beta}_{B}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\to}}\mathcal{L}
, both limits being in the sense of compact convergence.

With this, we proceed to prove the desired limit with general weights.

Proof of
Theorem

3.1
.

Couple
F
and
B
as in
Proposition

2.4
. Write

(T^{k}F)(y)=\sum_{i=1}^{k}(W^{n}F)_{i}(y)=\sup_{\pi\in Q^{k}((0,1)_{n,\beta};(y,n)_{n,\beta})}\int dF\circ\pi,

where for the second equality we recall the definition of the melon map.

Recall that we view
W^{n,\beta}F
and
\mathfrak{A}
as random functions from
\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{R}
to
\mathbb{R}
. Thus, to show that
W^{n,\beta}F\xrightarrow{d}\mathfrak{A}
uniformly over compact sets, it suffices to consider the compact sets of the form
\{1,2,\dots,\ell\}\times[w,z]
. Also, let us take
n
large enough to ensure

0<n^{\beta}+2wn^{(3\beta-1)/3}\leq n^{\beta}+2zn^{(3\beta-1)/3}<2n^{\beta}.

Then for
y\in[w,z]
and
k\in\{1,\dots,\ell\}
, we have

\displaystyle\lvert T^{k}F(y)-T^{k}B(y)\rvert

\displaystyle\leq T^{k}\lvert F-B\rvert(y)

\displaystyle=\sup_{\pi\in Q^{k}((0,1)_{n,\beta};(y,1)_{n,\beta})}\int d\lvert F-B\rvert\circ\pi

\displaystyle\leq k\sup_{\pi\in Q^{1}((0,1)_{n,\beta};(y,1)_{n,\beta})}\int d\lvert F-B\rvert\circ\pi

\displaystyle\leq k\sum_{i=1}^{n}\max_{u\in[0,n^{\beta}+2yn^{\beta-1/3}]}\lvert F_{i}(u)-B_{i}(u)\rvert

\displaystyle\leq k\sum_{i=1}^{n}\max_{u\in[0,2n^{\beta}]}\lvert F_{i}(u)-B_{i}(u)\rvert.

The last bound is uniform over all
y\in[w,z]
, so letting
\hat{T}^{k}[w,z]=\max_{y\in[w,z]}\lvert T^{k}F(y)-T^{k}B(y)\rvert
, we have

\hat{T}^{k}[w,z]\leq k\sum_{i=1}^{n}\max_{u\in[0,2n^{\beta}]}\lvert F_{i}(u)-B_{i}(u)\rvert.

Apply
Proposition

2.5
to this bound to find

\mathbb{E}[\hat{T}^{k}[w,z]]\leq ckn^{1+\beta/p}.

Now Markov’s inequality tells us that for any
\epsilon>0
,

\mathbb{P}\left(\max_{y\in[w,z]}\lvert T^{k}F(y)-T^{k}B(y)\rvert>n^{1+\beta/p+\epsilon}\right)\leq ckn^{1+\beta/p}n^{-({1+\beta/p+\epsilon})}\to 0.

Given our assumption on
\beta
, we can choose
\epsilon
such that
1+\beta/p+\epsilon<(3\beta-1)/6
. Then for all
k\in\{1,\dots,\ell\}
, we have

\displaystyle\max_{y\in[w,z]}n^{(1-3\beta)/6}\lvert T^{k}F(y)-T^{k}B(y)\rvert\rightarrow 0

in probability. This implies that

\displaystyle\max_{k\in\{1,\dots,\ell\}}\max_{y\in[w,z]}n^{(1-3\beta)/6}\lvert W^{n,\beta}_{k}F(y)-W^{n,\beta}_{k}B(y)\rvert\rightarrow 0,

(8)

also in probability.

To prove the claimed convergence to the Airy line ensemble, let
f:C(\{1,\dots,\ell\}\times[w,z])\to[0,1]
be a uniformly continuous function, with its argument in the space of continuous functions on our compact set equipped with the
L^{\infty}
norm. For any
\epsilon>0
, we may find
\delta>0
such that if
G,\,H\in C(\{1,\dots,\ell\}\times[w,z])
have
\lVert G-H\rVert_{\infty}<\delta
, then
\lvert f(G)-f(H)\rvert<\epsilon
. Thus, after restricting our melons to
\{1,\dots,\ell\}\times[w,z]
, we have the bound

\big\lvert\mathbb{E}[f(W^{n,\beta}F)]-\mathbb{E}[f(W^{n,\beta}B)]\big\rvert\leq\epsilon+\mathbb{P}(\lVert W^{n,\beta}F-W^{n,\beta}B\rVert_{\infty}>\delta)

(9)

Due to the convergence in probability established in (
8
), the right-hand side of (
9
) can be made arbitrarily small. Combined with
Lemma

4.1
, this gives the result.
∎

Having shown convergence of the melon, we would like to pass to a full directed landscape limit. In the semi-continuous setting of BLPP, the height of the top line of the melon is a last passage value, but this correspondence doesn’t hold up exactly for the lattice on which the passage times
L_{\omega}
live.

Consider
L_{F}
, defined analogously to
L_{B}
in (
2
). Observe that
(W^{n}F(y))_{1}=L_{F}(0,1;y,n)
, as with BLPP. The top line of
WF
also admits an interpretation as a lattice passage time.

Lemma 4.2
.

For
a\leq b\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
, we have the representation

L_{F}(a;b)=\max_{\pi\in\Pi(a;b)}\sum_{{\begin{subarray}{c}r\in\pi\\
r+e_{1}\in\pi\end{subarray}}}\omega(r).

(10)

The maximum is over weakly increasing nearest-neighbour paths in
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
connecting
a
to
b
, but now we only add weights from vertices from which we take a horizontal step.

Proof.

Recall that we define

L_{F}(x,m;y,n)=\max_{x=z_{m-1}\leq z_{m}\leq\cdots\leq z_{n}=y}\sum_{i=m}^{n}[F_{i}(z_{i})-F_{i}(z_{i-1})].

(11)

There will always be an extremising path with its jumps occurring at extreme points of the line ensemble. As the lines of
F
are linearly interpolated between integer times, these extreme points only exist at integer times. Thus when our end points are integers, we need only consider paths with integer jump points. Observe that a path in (
11
) collects weight only when it travels horizontally along a curve. That is, only when it moves horizontally on the lattice. This is equivalent to (
10
).
∎

This representation enables a simple comparison to
L_{\omega}
.

Lemma 4.3
.

When defined using the same weights, we have a deterministic inequality

\big\lvert L_{F}(a;b)-L_{\omega}(a;b)\big\rvert\leq\max_{\pi\in\Pi(a;b)}\sum_{{\begin{subarray}{c}r\in\pi\\
r+e_{2}\in\pi\end{subarray}}}\lvert\omega(r)\rvert.

That is, the effect of disregarding weights on vertical steps is bounded by the maximum possible contribution of vertical steps, after taking absolute values. This is useful because the paths we consider take proportionally few vertical steps.

Lemma 4.4
.

Given
t>0
, the following bound holds for the discrepancy between
L_{F}
and
L_{\omega}
on the box
[0,tn^{\beta}]\times[0,tn]
:

\mathbb{E}\left[\max_{a,\,b\in([0,tn^{\beta}]\times[0,tn])\cap\mathbb{Z}^{2}}\big\lvert L_{F}(a;b)-L_{\omega}(a;b)\big\rvert\right]\leq(tn)^{1+\beta/p}\big\lparen\mathbb{E}\lvert\omega\rvert^{p}\big\rparen^{1/p}.

(12)

In particular,

\mathbb{P}\left(\max_{a,\,b\in([0,tn^{\beta}]\times[0,tn])\cap\mathbb{Z}^{2}}\big\lvert L_{F}(a;b)-L_{\omega}(a;b)\big\rvert>n^{1+\beta/p+\epsilon}\right)\to 0.

(13)

Proof.

For simplicity take
t=1
. Set
M_{j}=\max_{0\leq i\leq n^{\beta}}\lvert\omega(i,j)\rvert
. Then clearly from
Lemma

4.3
, the discrepancy is bounded with

\big\lvert L_{F}(a;b)-L_{\omega}(a;b)\big\rvert\leq\sum_{j=a\cdot e_{2}}^{b\cdot e_{2}-1}M_{j}\leq\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}M_{j},

the latter bound uniform for all
a,\,b\in([0,n^{\beta}]\times[0,n])\cap\mathbb{Z}^{2}
. Call the sum on the right
M
.

The
p
-th moment of the
\omega
is finite, thus we have the bound
\mathbb{E}\lvert M_{j}\rvert^{p}\leq n^{\beta}\mathbb{E}\lvert\omega\rvert^{p}
. We then also get
\mathbb{E}\lvert M\rvert^{p}<n^{p+\beta}\mathbb{E}\lvert\omega\rvert^{p}
. Taking
p
-roots gives the desired bound. The claimed convergence of the probabilities is just Markov’s inequality.
∎

Lemma 4.5
.

When
\beta>\tfrac{7}{3}\cdot\tfrac{p}{p-2}
, we have
\lvert d^{n,\beta}_{\omega}-d^{n,\beta}_{F}\rvert\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\to}}0
compactly.

Proof.

Consider the difference

\lvert d^{n,\beta}_{F}(x,t;y,s)-d^{n,\beta}_{\omega}(x,t;y,s)\rvert=n^{(1-3\beta)/6}\lvert L_{F}((x,t)_{n,\beta};(y,s)_{n,\beta})-L_{\omega}((x,t)_{n,\beta};(y,s)_{n,\beta})\rvert.

By
Lemma

4.4
, the quantity in the absolute value is smaller than
n^{1+\beta/p+\epsilon}
with high probability, uniform for
(x,t;y,s)
in some compact set. Our condition on
\beta
is precisely enough for there to be
\epsilon>0
with
1+\beta/p+\epsilon+(1-3\beta)/6<0
. The convergence follows.
∎

Now we verify that the directed landscape limit holds for metrics
d^{n,\beta}_{F}
, defined analogously to (
7
).

Proposition 4.6
.

We have
d^{n,\beta}_{F}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\to}}\mathcal{L}
whenever
\beta>\tfrac{7}{3}\cdot\tfrac{p}{p-2}
, where this limit is in the sense of hypograph convergence. This convergence is compact if
p>5
.

Proof.

Theorems 1.14 and 1.16 of
[
DV22
]
together give criteria for hypograph convergence, telling us that we have convergence to the directed landscape if there is suitable convergence of the melon to the Airy line ensemble, and if the one-point distributions match those of
\mathcal{L}
. The former is exactly
Theorem

3.1
. For the latter, our argument for
Theorem

3.1
shows in particular that one-point distributions of the top lines of
W^{n,\beta}F
and
W^{n,\beta}B
agree in the limit. As recorded in
Lemma

4.1
, the latter is known to converge to
\mathcal{L}
.

There is a further criterion, Theorem 1.17 of
[
DV22
]
, telling us when we can conclude compact convergence from hypograph convergence. It is sufficient that

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\sup_{y\in[-3,3]}\mathbb{E}\lvert d^{n,\beta}_{F}(0,0;0,y)^{-}\rvert^{5+\delta}<\infty

for some
\delta>0
. Here
d^{-}
means the negative part of
d
.

Suppose
p>5
and let us choose
\delta>0
such that
q=5+\delta<p
(although large enough to ensure
\beta>\tfrac{7}{3}\cdot\tfrac{q}{q-2})
. Recall the proof of
Theorem

3.1
and specialise to
k=1
. Recall also that the top lines of our melons record the passage times. We get for
n
large enough that

\max_{u\in[-3,3]}\lvert L^{n,\beta}_{F}(0,0;0,u)-L^{n,\beta}_{B}(0,0;0,u)\rvert\leq\sum_{i=-3n}^{3n}\max_{u\in[-6n^{\beta},6n^{\beta}]}\lvert F_{i}(u)-B_{i}(u)\rvert.

Taking
q
-th moments and applying
Proposition

2.4
with Minkowski’s inequality,

\mathbb{E}[\max_{u\in[-3,3]}\lvert L^{n,\beta}_{F}(0,0;0,u)-L^{n,\beta}_{B}(0,0;0,u)\rvert^{q}]\leq c(6n)^{q}(6n^{\beta})=Cn^{q+\beta}.

Now the rescaled passage times have

\sup_{u\in[-3,3]}\mathbb{E}\lvert d^{n,\beta}_{F}(0,0;0,u)-d^{n,\beta}_{B}(0,0;0,u)\rvert^{q}\leq Cn^{q(1-3\beta)/6+q+\beta}.

Our assumption on
\beta
is such that this exponent is negative, and thus the quantity vanishes in the limit. We see that the condition of (
4.6
) holds for
d^{n,\beta}_{F}
if and only if it holds for
d^{n,\beta}_{B}
. But the condition is known to hold for
d^{n,\beta}_{B}
, owing to
Lemma

4.1
.
∎

All that’s left for
Theorem

3.2
is to observe that as
\lvert d^{n,\beta}_{\omega}-d^{n,\beta}_{F}\rvert\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\to}}0
compactly (by
Lemma

4.5
), and
d^{n,\beta}_{F}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\to}}\mathcal{L}
in either the hypograph sense or compactly (by
Proposition

4.6
), then — the latter being a possibly weaker form of convergence — we must have that
d^{n,\beta}_{\omega}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\to}}\mathcal{L}
in the same sense.

Remark 4.7
.

We address a technical point which appears in the proof of Theorem 1.17 of
[
DV22
]
, which we have used above. Strictly, the result is only proved for weights which are bounded below. To get around this, we define truncated weights
\tilde{\omega}_{n}=\omega\vee r_{n}
, where
r_{n}
is a sequence diverging to
-\infty
fast enough to ensure that
\mathbb{P}(\omega<r_{n})=o(n^{-(1+\beta)})
. This condition ensures that
\lvert d^{n,\beta}_{\omega}-d^{n,\beta}_{\tilde{\omega}_{n}}\rvert\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\to}}0
compactly. Thus
d^{n,\beta}_{\tilde{\omega}_{n}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\to}}\mathcal{L}
in the hypograph sense.

Also, it is easy to see that
(d^{n,\beta}_{\omega})^{-}
dominates
(d^{n,\beta}_{\tilde{\omega}_{n}})^{-}
. Thus the criterion in (
4.6
) holds for
(d^{n,\beta}_{\tilde{\omega}_{n}})^{-}
also. We then use Theorem 1.17 of
[
DV22
]
to upgrade the convergence
d^{n,\beta}_{\tilde{\omega}_{n}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\to}}\mathcal{L}
. Using once more that
\lvert d^{n,\beta}_{\omega}-d^{n,\beta}_{\tilde{\omega}_{n}}\rvert\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\to}}0
compactly, now in the opposite direction, gives the desired limit.

Proof of
Proposition

2.4
.

Let
B
be a coupled Brownian motion and write
\overline{B}
for the process with
\overline{B}(n)=B(n)
for integer
n
and linear interpolation in between. Write

\Delta[w,z]=\max_{u\in[w,z]}\lvert F(u)-B(u)\rvert,\quad\Delta_{1}[w,z]=\max_{u\in[w,z]\cap\mathbb{Z}}\lvert F(u)-B(u)\rvert.

We observe that
\Delta_{1}[w,z]
may equally be written as
\max_{u\in[w,z]}\lvert F(u)-\overline{B}(u)\rvert
, since this maximum must occur at an extreme point of the curves, namely an integer point.

In its original formulation, the approximation of Sakhanenko guarantees that we can find a coupled
B
such that
\mathbb{E}[\Delta_{1}[0,n]^{p}]\leq cn
. Now

\Delta[w,z]\leq\Delta_{1}[w,z]+\max_{u\in[w,z]}\lvert B(u)-\overline{B}(u)\rvert.

(14)

Write
\Delta_{2}[w,z]
for the latter term. Notice that
B(u)-\overline{B}(u)
is just a concatenation of independent Brownian bridges. Write
R_{m}(t)=B(m+t)-\overline{B}(m+t)
and
S_{m}=\max_{t\in[0,1]}\lvert R_{m}(t)\rvert
. In this notation,
\Delta_{2}[0,n]=\max_{0\leq m\leq n-1}S_{m}
. By the standard argument with the reflection principle, each
S_{m}
is stochastically dominated by
\lvert Z^{1}_{m}\rvert\vee\lvert Z^{2}_{m}\rvert
, where
Z^{1}_{m}
,
Z^{2}_{m}
are independent normals. So

\Delta_{2}[0,n]\leq\max_{0\leq m\leq n-1}(\lvert Z^{1}_{m}\rvert\vee\lvert Z^{2}_{m}\rvert).

Using the estimates for normal variables then, we can say
\mathbb{E}[\Delta_{2}[0,n]^{p}]\leq C\log^{p/2}n
. Returning to (
14
), the statement follows from this and the bound of
\Delta_{1}[0,n]
.

∎
