From nature to plate: harnessing naturally abundant scaffolds for sustainable production of protein-inspired cultivated snack
Authors: Neda Farhadi, Nafiseh Soltanizadeh, Elahe Masaeli, Mohsen Rabbani, Benjamin Zamanian
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The development of sustainable protein alternatives is crucial to addressing the limitations of conventional meat production, including energy consumption, food safety, and environmental impact. Simultaneously, protein deficiency in children remains a global issue, exacerbated by nutrient-poor snacks. To tackle these challenges, we propose a novel cultivated snack designed to provide a palatable and environmentally friendly source of meat protein. Central to this innovation is an edible porous scaffold (Carr:QSM) composed of kappa-carrageenan (Carr) and quince seed mucilage (QSM), naturally abundant polysaccharides. The newly developed scaffold exhibits a porous structure, a tenfold increase in swelling capacity, and superior mechanical performance compared to Carr. In vitro studies confirm its ability to support cell adhesion, proliferation, and differentiation, with enhanced expression of myogenic-related genes. The essential amino acids (EAAs) profile of Carr:QSM cultured with primary bovine myoblasts (PBM) under differentiation conditions reveals all nine EAAs, closely matching conventional meat. Sensory evaluation with 20 volunteers using a nine-point hedonic scale classified the snack as highly acceptable. This study highlights the potential of polysaccharide-based biomaterials to shift from therapeutic applications to early nutritional intervention. Techno-economic analysis (TEA) shows that industrial-scale production is achievable at < $0.065 per snack, with projected retail pricing below $1 by 2030.

Introduction

Protein deficiency remains a significant nutritional concern among children, with surveys indicating that one in seven school-aged children in the United States fails to consume sufficient protein on a daily basis1,2. Adequate intake of complete proteins, such as those derived from meat, is essential for healthy growth, cognitive development, and overall well-being3,4. However, conventional meat production faces critical challenges, including unsustainability and environmental limitations, which restrict its ability to meet growing global demand5–7. At the same time, children often obtain nearly one-third of their daily calories from low-nutrient snacks, further contributing to dietary imbalances2. These dual challenges highlight the need for sustainable and appealing strategies to deliver high-quality protein to children.

Cultured meat is a novel alternative protein source grown in laboratories7. This lab-grown technology offers a transformative solution to global crises such as food safety and environmental concerns8. As intensive livestock farming becomes increasingly unsustainable, the urgent need for clean, sustainable, and healthy protein sources drives scientific innovation9–11. Tissue engineering techniques, including culturing the cells in an environment that recapitulates in vivo conditions, are required to synthesize protein in vitro12,13.

Skeletal muscle progenitors, particularly myoblasts, are frequently employed in the production of cultivated meat. These cells serve as the primary building blocks to create protein-rich meat alternatives, mimicking the composition and structure of traditional animal-derived meat products5,6. As myoblasts undergo differentiation, they synthesize muscle-specific proteins and form a multinucleated structure called myotube7,14.

The cells’ natural environment comprises a honeycomb structure known as perimysium. In vitro, this perimysium honeycomb structure, as a porous scaffold, should be integrated externally, which can also provide mechanical stability7. Since the scaffold is a main ingredient of the final product, it and its by-products must be food-grade and edible15.

In the food industry, hydrocolloids, commonly referred to as gums, are extensively utilized as key food additives. These versatile substances serve various specific functions, enhancing the texture, stability, and overall quality of numerous food products16. Gums are a heterogeneous group of long-chain polysaccharides that function as thickeners, fat replacers, emulsion stabilizers, and gelling, clouding, and flocculating agents. They also inhibit the growth and expansion of ice crystals in frozen food and prevent the formation of sugar crystals in sugary products17. As low-calorie dietary fibers, hydrocolloids also have health-related properties, such as helping in weight management, supporting immune function, promoting colonic health, preventing cardiovascular diseases, and regulating glycemic and insulinemic levels in type 2 diabetes18.

Kappa-carrageenan (Carr), a marine-derived hydrocolloid, is a sulphated polysaccharide extracted from red seaweed. It forms firm, brittle gels when mixed with water19. Carr has unique characteristics, including biocompatibility, biodegradability, and non-toxicity, rendering it a significant component in developing food, pharmaceutics, and cosmetics20–22. Carr is also a promising alternative in tissue engineering and regenerative medicine because it resembles native sulphated glycosaminoglycans20. Furthermore, its biological properties, such as immunomodulatory, anticancer, antioxidant, and anticoagulant activity, have been broadly investigated19.

Quince seed mucilage (QSM), a seed-derived hydrocolloid, is isolated from the seeds of quince fruit23. In food production technology, it is considered a good substitute for some commercial gums24,25. QSM has excellent gelation characteristics as well as swelling properties, and it has been used for cell encapsulation, drug delivery, and tissue repair studies23,24,26–29. QSM is mainly composed of nanofibrillated cellulose and glucuronoxylan. Nanofibrillar cellulosic material provides good mechanical properties, while glucuronoxylan causes water adsorption30. QSM also contains minerals and a significant amount of bioactive polyphenolic compounds with excellent antioxidant activity, contributing to its potential health benefits31–33. Since Carr can interact with other hydrocolloids to modify gel strength and texture20,34, integrating Carr with QSM appears to form a stable hydrogel composed of cost-effective and naturally occurring dietary fibers. Carr has a pivotal role in gel formation by promoting a rigid, three-dimensional network that provides mechanical stability, which can support cell adhesion, while QSM imparts fruit aroma and swelling properties and enhances water retention, creating a hydrated environment essential for cell proliferation.

We hypothesize that the combination of Carr and QSM will form a structurally stable, biocompatible, and edible scaffold capable of supporting cell proliferation and differentiation, ultimately enabling the production of a sustainable, protein-enriched cultivated snack with improved sensory attributes for children.

This study aims to develop a sustainable meat protein alternative with enhanced structural and sensory appeal for children. To achieve this, the tailored scaffold of Carr and QSM was used, which can also be tailored to different sizes and shapes to fit bioreactor geometry and conditions for scale-up procedures. Carr:QSM was developed by combining various Carr and QSM ratios in the presence of physical crosslinkers. Carr:QSM characteristics were assessed and compared to Carr, a widely used scaffold in cell culture applications. The ability of cell proliferation and differentiation on the scaffolds was evaluated. The essential amino acids (EAAs) profile of Carr:QSM was assessed 7 days after PBM differentiation on the scaffold. Cultivated snack was produced in dried and jelly forms, and liking scores were captured using a nine-point hedonic scale.

To achieve the tailored scaffold, the suitable ratio of Carr and QSM was determined based on the water uptake and degradation properties of the final scaffolds. The blends were physically cross-linked with potassium chloride (KCl) or a combination of KCl and calcium chloride (CaCl2) solution as physical crosslinkers. The 1:1 ratio of Carr and QSM, which was cross-linked by 5% w/v KCl, showed the highest water uptake and the lowest degradation in Milli-Q water (Fig. 2a, b). As a result, it was selected as the tailored scaffold, Carr:QSM. The swelling kinetics of the scaffolds are shown in Fig. 2c. Carr:QSM indicated the swelling capacity tenfold higher than that of Carr over 24 h. The degradation behavior of the scaffolds after 21 days demonstrated that the dry mass of Carr and Carr:QSM was ~78% and 84%, respectively (Fig. 2d). Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images (Fig. 2e) revealed diverse-size porosity with a honeycomb structure for Carr:QSM. Analysis of pore-size distribution (Fig. 2f) using FIJI software35 indicated a broad range of pore sizes between 11.84 and 474.81 μm for Carr:QSM. The mechanical performance of Carr and Carr:QSM regarding compressive modulus is exhibited in Fig. 2g–j. A representative stress-strain curve is displayed in Fig. 2g. To assess the mechanical stability of the scaffolds, they were incubated in Milli-Q water at 37 °C for 24 h (Fig. 2h). Based on the observation, Carr:QSM reached a thickness of around 1.8 mm after 24 h in Milli-Q water, comparable to the scaffold’s thickness after 14 days in the culture medium. As expected, integrating Carr with QSM significantly improved its stiffness (Young’s modulus) at p ≤ 0.01 (Fig. 2i). Carr:QSM also showed an ultimate strength significantly greater than Carr at p ≤ 0.05 (Fig. 2j). Integrating Carr with QSM in the presence of KCl solution as a physical cross-linker formed an interlocking network without covalent bonds between them. This intertwined network provided additional reinforcement, making the structure more resistant to deformation under stress36,37.

The chemical structure of Carr and QSM powders, as well as Carr and Carr:QSM scaffolds, based on functional groups, was analyzed by Fourier-transformed infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR) (Fig. 3). As shown, four spectra showed all typical bands and peaks associated with the polysaccharides with small differences in wavenumbers17,38–40. The peak at around 1730 cm−1 observed in QSM powder and Carr:QSM scaffold was typically associated with the C=O stretching of ester, carbonyl, or carboxyl groups37. However, this peak was not observed in Carr powder and Carr scaffold. The presence of this peak with a small shift in the Carr:QSM scaffold suggests a potential change in the local environment of the carbonyl groups, and it may reflect physical interactions, such as ionic or hydrogen bonding, between the two biopolymers37. C2C12 proliferation on the scaffolds

To confirm the ability of the scaffolds to support cell activity and vitality during proliferation, the scaffolds were seeded with C2C12 cells. The optimum seeding density was determined by seeding various concentrations, including 2 × 106, 106, and 5 × 105 cells per scaffold. 5 × 105 cells were also seeded on tissue culture polystyrene (TCP). Metabolic activity was then measured on days 1, 3, 7, and 14 post-culture (Supplementary Fig. 1). The cells showed a slower rate of metabolic activity on the scaffolds during the initial 3 days compared to their performance on TCP. In contrast, by day 7, metabolic activity improved across all experimental sets except for TCP on account of its restricted surface area41. On day 7, metabolic activity also significantly increased at the concentration of 106 cells compared to other concentrations (p ≤ 0.0001) in both scaffolds. Accordingly, a concentration of 106 cells per scaffold was used for further analyses. The metabolic activity of 106 cells is indicated in Fig. 4a. In both scaffolds, metabolic activity was not significantly different on days 1, 3, and 14. However, on day 7, metabolic activity revealed a significant increase, as previously mentioned. The minimal metabolic activity during the first 3 days was due to the necessary adhesion and adaptation period23. On the contrary, the significant increase on day 7, followed by a decrease by day 14, could be associated with cell confluency during the first 7 days of culture. Metabolic activity in Carr:QSM was also significantly higher than that of Carr on each post-seeding day. C2C12 differentiation on the scaffolds

A conceptual illustration of myoblast differentiation into a porous matrix is depicted in Fig. 5a. As myoblasts differentiate, they undergo fusion with neighboring cells to form multinucleated structures, myotubes. The myotubes continue to mature, aligning themselves with the scaffold structure43,44. Differentiation assessment on the scaffolds indicated that, unlike Carr, a substantial proportion of the cells underwent differentiation on Carr:QSM and formed elongated, multinucleated myotubes (Fig. 5b, Supplementary Fig. 3). Analysis of myotube size distribution on Carr:QSM using FIJI software7 revealed that the myotube lengths ranged from 80 to 210 μm. Most (~20%) had a length of 90 μm, and around 15% showed a length of 200 μm (Supplementary Fig. 4). Quantitative PCR (qPCR) analysis of C2C12 differentiation after 3 and 7 days showed significantly improved expression of myogenic-related genes on Carr:QSM compared to Carr. PBM culture on Carr:QSM

Based on previous results and superior characteristics of Carr:QSM, Carr:QSM alone was utilized for further assessments. To evaluate the ability of Carr:QSM to support PBM proliferation, the scaffold was seeded with 106 PBM and observed for two weeks. As previously mentioned, Carr:QSM reached a thickness of approximately 1.8 mm after two weeks in a culture medium. In the first week, the cells could penetrate into Carr:QSM to a depth of 600 μm (Supplementary Fig. 5). By the end of the second week, the cells showed an infiltration ability to a depth of ~1.5 mm (Supplementary Fig. 6), suggesting that the cells adhered to the scaffold and proliferated on and throughout its thickness. As indicated in Supplementary Fig. 7, the cells covered the top and bottom of the scaffold within two weeks. Mechanical properties of the PBM-included Carr:QSM 7 days after cell differentiation, along with the empty scaffolds, are exhibited in Fig. 6a. Young’s modulus and ultimate strength of the cell-included scaffolds were found to be significantly greater than that of the scaffolds without the cells (p ≤ 0.001), thickness ≈1.8 mm. Cultivated snack production and sensorial characteristics

Figure 6b illustrates the production process for the cultivated snack in both jelly and dried forms, with detailed procedures provided in the Methods Section. Briefly, Carr:QSM scaffolds were prepared and subsequently dried in a vacuum oven at 50 °C for 24 hours. These scaffolds were then seeded with 10⁶ PBM and incubated at 37 °C with 5% moisture for two weeks, allowing for cell proliferation within the scaffold structure. After incubation, the scaffolds were transferred into silicon molds and covered with the gum solution. To have cultivated snacks in jelly form, the molds were left at RT for 15 min. Cultivated snacks in jelly form were then lyophilized for 8 to 12 hours to produce cultivated snacks in dried form. For sensory evaluation, a nine-hedonic scale from dislike extremely to like extremely was used (Fig. 6c). To capture the liking scores of our products, a panel group of twenty volunteers was considered. The results of sensorial characteristics are shown in Fig. 6d. Overall, the cultivated snack in dried form scored higher in terms of taste (p ≤ 0.0001), aroma (p ≤ 0.05), texture (p ≤ 0.01), and overall acceptability (p ≤ 0.001).

Discussion

Protein is essential for children’s physical growth and cognitive development, providing crucial EAAs that support both bodily functions and mental health4,46. It is considered a key macronutrient that has the most remarkable effect on children’s growth and development3,4. Protein deficiency in children includes physical symptoms such as edema, growth failure, weak muscles, and fragile hair. It also results in low levels of serum albumin and transferrin47. It has been reported that protein-calorie malnutrition has significantly affected the body mass index of children aged 6 to 7 years. Low body mass index has subsequently impacted the head and facial development of those children3. In addition, protein deficiency has been observed among 6-13-year-old children in the United States since a main part of their daily diet comprises nutrient-poor snacks1,2. Children’s snacking patterns are influenced by various factors, including social influences and emotional states. Socially, snacks are often consumed during activities with peers, while they can also serve as rewards or comfort48. Emotional context plays a role in snacking choices, with children in a sad mood tending to consume more energy-dense snacks like chocolate, while those in a happy or neutral mood prefer lighter options like crackers49. The consumption of healthy snacks such as fruit results in positive impacts, including lower anxiety, depression, and emotional distress in children. Unhealthy snacks such as chocolate and crisps, however, cause more cognitive difficulties and fatigue50. Therefore, including protein in children’s daily diet in the form of healthy snacks may have a dual effect. It accompanies a sense of pleasure while simultaneously providing them with the most important macronutrient3 for their growth and development.

Anabolic processes such as cellular growth and differentiation are regulated in the body with catalytic proteins. EAAs have a crucial role in activating these catalytic proteins. They cannot be synthesized within the body and must be obtained through the diet46,51. Plant-based proteins are limited in certain EAAs, including Lys and Leu. Limiting one EAA can hinder the utilization of other amino acids and subsequent protein synthesis in the body47. For instance, soybean protein is low in sulfur-containing amino acids such as Met and cysteine (Cys)52. Met serves as the initiating amino acid in protein synthesis, as it is the first amino acid integrated into the nascent polypeptide chain during the process of protein translation53,54. Even though the plant sources are considered complete in some respects55, they do not offer the same level of anabolic capacity as high-quality animal proteins such as meat and eggs46,55,56. The plant-based proteins also contain some antinutritional factors such as trypsin inhibitor, phytic acid, tannin, and cyanide47. Consequently, animal-sourced foods containing all EAAs in balanced proportions are considered the best and complete source of protein, and their incorporation into children’s diets is vital46,51,56.

In this study, biotechnological methods driven by the demand for sustainable meat production were employed to produce a cultivated snack that mimics the EAA profile of meat. Both Carr and QSM are naturally occurring dietary fibers. Their straightforward and efficient extraction process makes them the ideal scaffolding material for the large-scale production of food products. The stable hydrogel (Carr:QSM) from the combination of Carr and QSM is easily fabricated under 40 °C and in the presence of potassium ions (K+), which are highly applicable for mass production. Carr:QSM included a network of diverse-sized pores (Fig. 2e), with a majority of medium pore sizes providing a suitable structure for cell adhesion, growth, and spread57. Furthermore, Carr:QSM supported enhanced cell seeding efficiency (Fig. 4b) and adhesion (Supplementary video 1, 2, and Supplementary Fig. 2), which are critical for scaling up the production. The scaffold also showed gradual swelling ability and facilitated cell penetration throughout the structure within two weeks of culture (Supplementary Figs. 6 and 7). Cells exhibited differentiation potential on Carr:QSM and produced multinucleated long myotubes in starvation (low serum) conditions (Supplementary Figs. 3 and 4). Successful proliferation and differentiation of cells on Carr:QSM led to higher ultimate strength and Young’s modulus of the PBM-included scaffold compared to the scaffold without cells (Fig. 6a). All EAAs were found in Carr:QSM 4 days after PBM differentiation (Table 1). As previously mentioned, a complete protein must contain all 9 EAAS in an adequate proportion that the body requires for optimal health46,56. The ideal proportions of EAAs in a dietary protein are comparable to those of the tissue amino acid pattern and maintenance amino acid pattern58,59

Based on the World Health Organization (WHO)/Food and Agriculture Organization of the United Nations (FAO)/United Nations University (NUN) amended report in 2007, the ratio of Leu/Ilo and Lys/Trp in tissue amino acid pattern (whole-body protein) is around 2.2 and 6.1, respectively. These ratios in the maintenance amino acid pattern are around 2 and 7.5, respectively58. In addition, Lys/Trp ratio in different mammalian tissues, including muscle, viscera, and plasma, is 6.3, 5.3, and 6.2, respectively59. These ratios are also considered for the determination of amino acid requirements. As an example, in amino acid requirements (mg/kg/d) for children aged 3 to 14 years, the ratios of Leu/Ile and Lys/Trp are approximately 2 and 7.3, respectively58. Specifically, dietary protein utilization increases as its Lys/Trp ratio follows the muscle tissue value59. In Carr:QSM, Leu/Ile and Lys/Trp ratios were found to be 2.3 and 7.7, which are comparable to those mentioned above. Met/Trp ratio is also an important indicator for evaluating a product’s dietary value, with a normal value of 1; however, a higher value is more beneficial for protein synthesis in the body60. Met/Trp ratio in Carr:QSM was found to be 1.4. Carr:QSM biomass is estimated to contain 14.45 g of EAAs per 100 g, comparable to meat products such as beef protein powder available in the U.S. market61. A typical 50 g portion of biomass also provides approximately 7.3 g of EAAs, aligning with levels reported for various beef cuts (e.g., Droughtmaster) and specialized breeds like young Aberdeen-Angus and Hereford cattle60,62. The proportion of EAAs in our product, however, more closely resembles the tissue and maintenance amino acid patterns and the amino acid requirements (mg/kg/d) for 3-14-year-old children58. The amount of EAAs in 100 g of biomass also does not exceed the limited levels set by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration63.

To capture the liking scores of cultivated snacks in jelly and dried forms, a nine-point hedonic scale from extremely dislike to extremely like was used (Fig. 6c). Given the novelty of cultivated food products, recruiting large sensory panels remains challenging, as many individuals are hesitant to participate; therefore, a panel of 20 volunteers was considered adequate for this initial evaluation. On this scale, a mean liking score of 7 or above indicates a highly acceptable sensory profile of the food64,65. Both jelly and dried forms captured a score within a highly acceptable product range; on the contrary, dried cultivated snack gained higher scores than jelly form (Fig. 6d). Apart from the panel group whose scores determined the acceptability range of our product, informal feedback from children aged 3–13 years indicated a preference for the dried cultivated snack over the jelly form. For scaling up the production of dried cultivated snacks, it is important to consider the freeze-drier capacity and 8–12 h of storage at minus eighty degrees, which could potentially raise investment costs. In contrast, the maintenance and transportation costs of the dried form would be less than the jelly form, as it can be stored at RT. Dried cultivated snacks would also exhibit a prolonged shelf life compared to their jelly form.

TEA for cultivated snack production

This TEA assesses a novel cultivated snack’s commercial viability, cost-effectiveness, and scalability potential. The product embodies several key values that differentiate it from all market alternatives. This product is a natural, edible scaffold with cultured bovine muscle cells, which, based on the nature of the product, has an extended shelf life without requiring energy-consuming facilities such as refrigerators, etc. Its formulation offers a complete EAAs profile comparable to traditional meat, enabled by a modular and high-efficiency production process57–59.

Methods

Materials

Quince seeds were sourced from a local medicinal plant market in Iran. Carr powder was obtained from a commercial source and used without further purification. Based on publicly available product information from Gino Biotech (Qingdao, China), food-grade kappa-carrageenan typically contains no less than 85% polysaccharides, with quality parameters including moisture content ≤12%, total ash content 15–40%, pH between 8–11 (1% solution), and strict limits on heavy metal residues (arsenic ≤3 mg/kg, lead ≤5 mg/kg, cadmium ≤1 mg/kg, and mercury ≤1 mg/kg). These parameters ensure compliance with international food-grade standards and suitability for edible applications. The C2C12 cell line was purchased from ACCT, USA. High-glucose Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM), fetal bovine serum (FBS), penicillin-streptomycin, horse serum (HS), phosphate buffer saline (PBS), bovine serum albumin (BSA), and collagenase type I were acquired from Gibco, USA. PrestoBlue reagent, the live/dead kit, DAPI, and Alexa Fluor 594 were obtained from Invitrogen, USA. Paraformaldehyde, Triton X-100, hydrochloric acid (HCl), methanol, triethylamine (TEA), phenyl isothiocyanate (PITC), acetonitrile, and trifluoroacetic acid (TFA) were purchased from Thermo Scientific, USA. The primary antibody (rabbit polyclonal to desmin) and Alexa Fluor 647 goat anti-mouse IgG were sourced from Abcam, USA. The RNeasy Micro Kit was obtained from Qiagen, USA. The High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit was purchased from ABI, USA. Primers were acquired from Integrated DNA Technologies, Inc., USA. Standard EAAs, sodium hydroxide (NaOH), sucrose, thioglycolic acid, sulfuric acid (H₂SO₄), KCl, and CaCl₂ were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich, USA.

Gum extraction

The quince seeds were cleaned, and all impurities, such as dirt and dust, were removed. QSM was isolated using warm water extraction. In brief, the quince seeds (500 g) were dispersed in preheated Milli-Q water (1000 ml), and the temperature was adjusted to 60 °C. The mixture was stirred with a rod mixer during the entire extraction procedure (40 min). Separation of the mucilage from the swelled seeds was performed using cotton cloth. Crud gum was lyophilized (Millrock Technology, Inc., USA). The dried gum was then milled into powder by a laboratory milling machine (Retsch, Germany)78.

The resulting solution was stirred at 60 °C for 30 min, then poured into 16 mm diameter circular molds, and left at RT for 15 min. The scaffolds were then physically cross-linked in a 5% w/v KCl or a mixture of 1:1 KCl and CaCl2 solution (5% w/v) at RT for 1 h. Mechanical properties

Compressive stress-strain measurement of the scaffolds was carried out by a universal mechanical analyzer (Model 5943, MA, USA). Dried scaffolds were immersed in Milli-Q water at 37 °C for 24 h. The swelled scaffolds were then individually compressed at a rate of 2 mm min-1. The slope obtained from the linear regression of the stress-strain curve was determined as Young’s modulus (E*), and the maximum stress in the plot was determined as the ultimate strength7.

FT-IR analysis

The FT-IR spectra of Carr and Carr:QSM scaffolds, as well as Carr and QSM powders, were characterized using a Fourier transform infrared spectrometer (Bruker, Germany) with KBR pellets in a range of 4000-400 cm-117.

Cell culture and evaluation

C2C12 culture

C2C12 sub-cultured in regular growth medium, DMEM containing 10% (v/v) FBS and 1% (v/v) penicillin-streptomycin, and incubated in a 5% CO2 humidified incubator (Nick Booth, USA) at 37 °C. To induce myogenic differentiation, cells (at passage 8) were maintained in low serum medium, including DMED supplemented with 2% v/v HS and 1% v/v penicillin-streptomycin14.

C2C12 seeding on the scaffolds

Prior to cell seeding, the scaffolds (16 mm in diameter and 100 µm in thickness) were sterilized using UV radiation for 1 h and then transferred to 24-well plates. After cell seeding, the scaffolds were placed in the incubator under 5% CO2 at 37 °C for 30 min. Growth medium was then added to the scaffolds and replaced every 2 days. For the metabolic assay, different seeding concentrations, including 2 × 106, 106, and 5 ×105 cells per scaffold, were used. However, for subsequent evaluations, the scaffolds were seeded with 106 cells per scaffold. To evaluate C2C12 differentiation, the scaffolds were seeded in the growth medium for 7 days. The medium was then switched to the low serum medium and replaced every 2 days. The scaffolds were maintained in the differentiation medium for 4 days for desmin staining. To assess gene expression, the scaffolds were kept in the differentiation medium for 3 and 7 days.

Metabolic activity assay

Cell’s metabolic activity was examined at 1, 3, 7, and 14 days after culture using PrestoBlue reagent. PrestoBlue was mixed with the regular growth medium at a ratio of 1:9. Afterwards, 500 μl of the resulting solution was added to the scaffolds. They then incubated under 5% CO2 at 37 °C for 4 h. The fluorescence intensity caused by cell activity was obtained at excitation and emission wavelengths of 560 and 590 nm, respectively, using a fluorescence plate reader (BioTek, USA)42.

Cell seeding efficiency on Carr:QSM

The scaffolds were seeded in growth medium as previously described. The cells that attached to the well or remained suspended in the medium were collected and counted. The seeding efficiency was determined as a percentage of the total number of cells initially seeded7.

Cell viability

Cell viability was evaluated on days 1 and 7 of the culture. For this, a live/dead cell imaging kit was used. The cells were stained with 4 mM Calcein AM and 2 mM ethidium bromide mixed with the growth medium at a ratio of 1:1000 and 2:1000, respectively. They were then incubated under 5% CO2 at 37 °C for 15 min. The cells were visualized using a fluorescence microscope (Echo Revolution, USA). The FIJI software (Version 2.9.0) was used to quantify cell coverage and viability on days 1 and 7 of culture in a blind manner. Since Calcein AM fluorescence intensity is influenced by cell proliferation and live cell count, images were selected randomly, and a threshold was manually set for each scaffold. Cell viability was assessed by calculating the ratio of live cells (green count) to the total number of cells (live and dead). Cell coverage was measured by calculating the ratio of the thresholded area to the total scaffold area35.

DAPI and phalloidin staining

One day after culturing C2C12 on Carr:QSM, the scaffolds were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min. Then, they were washed three times with PBS. Cells were permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 for 15 min, and the scaffolds were washed three times with PBS. Filamentous actin (F-Actin) was then stained with the red-fluorescent Alexa Fluor 594 (1:100 diluted in PBS) for 2 h at RT. The scaffolds were washed three times with PBS, and then nuclei were stained with DAPI (1:1000 diluted in PBS) for 10 min at RT. Subsequently, the scaffolds were washed three times and kept at 4 °C until imaging and video recording, using the inverted confocal microscope (Zeiss, LSM 710, Germany) and the Zenn software (Carl Zeiss)14.

Immunofluorescent staining

After cell permeabilization, the scaffolds (Carr and Carr:QSM) were immersed in blocking serum (3% BSA in PBS) for 2 h at RT. They were then incubated with primary antibody (Rabbit polyclonal to desmin, 1:200 diluted with 0.5% BSA) overnight at 4 °C. Afterwards, the scaffolds were washed three times with BSA 0.5% and incubated with the secondary antibody, Alexa Fluor 647 goat anti-mouse IgG (1:500 diluted with 0.5% BSA), for 3 h at RT. The scaffolds were washed three times with BSA 0.5% and incubated with DAPI (at the concentration described above) for 10 min at RT. They were then washed three times with PBS and stored at 4 °C until the confocal imaging. Further image analysis, including myotube size distribution, was performed using FIJI software (Version 2.9.0)7.

RNA extraction

RNA was extracted using the RNeasy Micro Kit based on the manufacturer’s protocol. In brief, the scaffolds were washed with PBS and finely chopped with scissors. Subsequently, they were homogenized in a tube containing a mixture of buffer RLT and β-mercaptoethanol. The homogenized solution was then centrifuged (Thermo Scientific, LEGEND MICRO 21 R) at a speed of 14,100 × g for 3 min. The supernatant was carefully transferred into a new 2-ml tube containing 70% ethanol at a 1:1 ratio. The samples were then transferred into a column and subjected to centrifugation at 8000 × g for 15 s. The flow-through was discarded, and the remaining solution was reloaded onto the column and centrifuged again at the same speed and duration. RW1 buffer was added to the column and centrifuged at 8000 × g for 15 s. Then, the RPE buffer was added to the column and centrifuged at 8000 × g for 15 s. This process was repeated, and the columns were subsequently centrifuged at 8000 × g for 2 min and then at 14,100 × g for 1 min. DNase RNase-free double-distilled water (40 µl) was added to the column, which was then centrifuged at 8000 × g for 1 min. The concentration of RNA was determined using a NanoDrop (Thermo Scientific, 2000). Finally, the High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit was employed to prepare complementary DNA (cDNA) according to the instructions provided by the manufacturer81.

qPCR analysis

qPCR was carried out using RNA Taq Man Fast Universal PCR Master Mix (2X). The following primers were used: Myof5 (Forward: CAG CCC CAC CTC CAA CTG, Reverse: GCA GCA CAT GCA TTT GAT ACA TC), Myogenin (Forward: CCC ATG GTG CCC AGT GAA, Reverse: GCA GAT TGT GGG CGT CTG TA), MyHC (Forward: ACA GAG GAA GAC AGG AAG AAC CTA C, Reverse: GGG CTT CAC AGG CAT CCT TAG), MyoD (Forward: CGT GGC AGC GAG CAC TAC, Reverse: TGT AAT CCA TCA TGC CAT CAG A) and M-GAPDH as the reference gene. The reaction was performed in the BIORAD system (CFX384, USA). Data were shown 2−Δct.

PBM culture on Carr:QSM

PBM isolation

PBM was isolated from the resected animal muscle (semi-membranous muscle tissue of a 1-year-old animal or younger). The tissue was cut into pieces using scissors, transferred to a 1 L storage bottle containing 250 ml wash buffer (PBS, 6% penicillin-streptomycin, and 2% nystatin), and shaken vigorously. The small pieces were cut further down to 1 mm2 pieces to remove any visible fat (white tissue). The resulting product was minced into a paste-like consistency with a scalpel. A 5 g was loaded into a 50 ml conical tube (tube 1) containing 20 ml of digestion solution (DMEM and 3 mg ml-1 collagenase type 1). Tube 1 was placed on a tissue culture rotator in the incubator under 5% CO2 at 37 °C for 1.5 h. Collagenase was then neutralized with 20 ml of isolation medium (DMEM (38% v/v), DMEM-F12 (38% v/v,) FBS, (20% v/v), penicillin-streptomycin (3% v/v), and nystatin (1% v/v)). Tube 1 was centrifuged at 10 × g for 20 s under 4 °C. The supernatant was then transferred to a new 50 ml conical tube (tube 2). Tube 1 was added with 15 ml of 0.25% Trypsin-EDTA and incubated under 5% CO2 at 37 °C for 20 min. The trypsin was neutralized with 20 ml of isolation medium. Tube 1 was then centrifuged at 10 × g for 20 s under 4 °C. The resulting supernatant and tube 2 were centrifuged at 400 × g for 10 min under 4 °C. Supernatants were discarded, and the pellets were resuspended with 10 ml of isolation medium. The cells were then strained using a 100 μm strainer. All tubes were centrifuged at 400 × g for 5 min under 4 °C. Each pellet was resuspended with 3 ml of isolation medium. The pellets were then pooled together and strained through a 70 μm strainer. The resulting suspension was further centrifuged at 400 × g for 5 min under 4 °C. Supernatant was discarded, and the pellet was resuspended in isolation medium. To reduce fibroblast contamination, the cell suspension was cultured in a T-175 tissue culture flask with the isolation medium under 5% CO2 at 37 °C overnight to allow fibroblasts to attach. Then, the supernatant containing myoblasts was carefully transferred to a T-175 tissue culture flask and incubated under 5% CO2 at 37 °C. To assess the proliferation and differentiation of PBM, the same growth medium and low serum medium were used as those for C2C1282.

Tryptophan estimation

The tryptophan content was evaluated using the colorimetric method at 500 nm. For that, six scaffolds were seeded with PBM passages 2–4 for 2 weeks, then collected and lyophilized as described above. The dried sample was alkaline hydrolyzed with 10 ml of 5% NaOH under vacuum at 120 °C for 24 h. The content was then neutralized to pH 7.0 with 6 N HCl, and the final volume was adjusted to 100 ml using Milli-Q water. The solution was vacuum-filtered through a 0.45-mm pore-size membrane. To evaluate the tryptophan content of the sample, 100 μl of 2.5% sucrose and 100 μl of 0.6% thioglycolic acid were added to a glass test tube containing 4 ml of 50% H2SO4. The tube was then placed in a water bath at 50 °C for 10 min. Then, 100 μl of the hydrolyzed sample was added to the tube and vortexed. The volume was made to 5 ml with 0.1 N HCl and left at RT for 5 min to produce a pink color complex. The color intensity of the resulting solution was measured spectrophotometrically at 500 nm. To measure the tryptophan content of the empty scaffold, 6 scaffolds were milled and treated according to the above procedure. To generate the tryptophan standard curve, 0.5 g of standard tryptophan was dissolved in 1.0 N NaOH. The resulting solution was neutralized with 1.0 N HCl, and the total volume was made up to 100 ml with Milli-Q water. The solution was vacuum-filtered through a 0.45 mm pore-size membrane. A series of dilutions was then prepared and treated with sucrose, thioglycolic acid, and H2SO4, as described above. The color intensity of the pink complex was measured spectrophotometrically at 500 nm85.

Cultivated snack production and sensorial characteristics

Carr:QSM scaffolds were prepared and dried in a vacuum oven at 50 °C for 24 h. The scaffolds were then seeded with 106 PBM and incubated under 5% CO2 at 37 °C for 2 weeks. The growth medium without non-edible factors was used throughout the culture period. After the incubation period, the scaffolds were transferred to silicon molds and covered with the gum solution. The gum solution consisted of a 1:1 ratio of Carr and QSM at a total concentration of 1.5% w/v, making up 98% of the final formulation. Additional ingredients included rose water (5%), food colorant (5%), and stevia sweetener (1%). To produce a cultivated snack in jelly form, the molds were left at RT for 15 minutes. Cultivated snacks in jelly form were then lyophilized for 8–12 h to produce cultivated snacks in dried form.

The preference mapping study was conducted using a nine-point hedonic scale to assess the liking scores of twenty volunteers, including 14 women and 6 men, all between the ages of 32 and 45. (Due to ethical considerations and regulations surrounding research with vulnerable populations in the U.S., school-aged children could not be included in the formal sensory evaluation. Instead, adults participated in the panel, as they also could provide more detailed feedback on taste, aroma, texture, and overall acceptability. Both types of cultivated snacks were evaluated simultaneously across two sessions in natural (environmental) light. To neutralize lingering flavors and textures, panelists were provided with water and crackers between evaluations. The scale range was as follows: 0–1; extremely dislike, 1–2; very much dislike, 2–3; moderately dislike, 3–4; slightly dislike, 4–5; neither like nor dislike, 5–6; slightly like, 6–7; moderately like; 7–8; very much like, and 8-9; extremely like64.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using a statistical analysis system (version 9.4). According to the experiments, a student’s t test, one-way ANOVA with least significant difference mean comparison test, or factorial model based on CRD were used.

To analyze cell metabolic activity on the scaffolds with 106 cell seeding concentration, a full factorial model (2 × 4) of two independent factors and their interaction was fit to analyze the metabolic activity of the cells. Variance analysis of the main effects of two factors and their interaction is shown in Supplementary Table 1. The main factors were scaffold types with two levels (a1; Carr and a2; Carr:QSM) and post-seeding days with four levels (b1; day 1, b2; day 3, b3; day 7, and b4; day 14). Like the metabolic assay, a full factorial model (2 × 2) of two independent factors, including scaffold type and post-seeding days, and their interaction was used to analyze cell coverage on the scaffolds (Supplementary Table 2). The graphs were drawn by Prism 6 (GraphPad Software). The data were shown as bar graphs with error bars representing standard deviation (s.d.).
