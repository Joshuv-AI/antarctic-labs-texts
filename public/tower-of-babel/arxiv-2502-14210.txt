Sample Complexity of Linear Quadratic Regulator Without Initial Stability
Amirreza Neshaei Moghaddam, Alex Olshevsky, Bahman Gharesifard

Abstract
. Inspired by REINFORCE, we introduce a novel receding-horizon algorithm for the Linear Quadratic Regulator (LQR) problem with unknown dynamics. Unlike prior methods, our algorithm avoids reliance on two-point gradient estimates while maintaining the same order of sample complexity. Furthermore, it eliminates the restrictive requirement of starting with a stable initial policy, broadening its applicability. Beyond these improvements, we introduce a refined analysis of error propagation through the contraction of the Riccati operator under the Riemannian distance. This refinement leads to a better sample complexity and ensures improved convergence guarantees.



1.
Introduction

The Linear Quadratic Regulator (LQR) problem, a cornerstone of optimal control theory, offers an analytically tractable framework for optimal control of linear systems with quadratic costs. Traditional methods rely on complete knowledge of system dynamics, solving the Algebraic Riccati Equation
[
2
]
to determine optimal control policies. However, recent real-world scenarios often involve incomplete or inaccurate models. Classical methods in control theory, such as identification theory
[
8
]
and adaptive control
[
1
]
, were specifically designed to provide guarantees for decision-making in scenarios with unknown parameters. However, the problem of effectively approximating the optimal policy using these methods remains underexplored in the traditional literature. Recent efforts have sought to bridge this gap by analyzing the sample complexity of learning-based approaches to LQR
[
6
]
, providing bounds on control performance relative to the amount of data available.

In contrast, the model-free approach, rooted in reinforcement learning (RL), bypasses the need for explicit dynamics identification, instead focusing on direct policy optimization through cost evaluations. Recent advances leverage stochastic zero-order optimization techniques, including policy gradient methods, to achieve provable convergence to near-optimal solutions despite the inherent non-convexity of the LQR cost landscape. Foundational works, such as
[
7
]
, established the feasibility of such methods, demonstrating convergence to the globally optimal solution. Subsequent efforts, including
[
15
]
and
[
17
]
, have refined these techniques, achieving improved sample complexity bounds. Notably, all of these works assume that the initial policy is stabilizing.

A key limitation of these methods, including
[
15
,
17
]
, is the reliance on two-point gradient estimation, which requires evaluating costs for two different policies while maintaining identical initial states. In practice, this assumption is often infeasible, as the initial state is typically chosen randomly and cannot be controlled externally. Our earlier work
[
16
]
addressed this challenge, establishing the best-known result among methods that assume initial stability without having to rely on two-point estimates. Instead, we proposed a gradient estimation method inspired by REINFORCE
[
21
,
20
]
, that achieves the same convergence rate as the two-point method
[
15
]
while using only a single cost evaluation at each step. This approach enhances the practical applicability of model-free methods, setting a new benchmark under the initial stability assumption.

The requirement for an initial stabilizing policy significantly limits the utility of these methods in practice. Finding such a policy can be challenging or infeasible and often relies on identification techniques, which model-free methods are designed to avoid. Without getting technical at this point, it is worth pointing out that this initial stability assumption plays a major role in the construction of the mentioned model-free methods, and cannot be removed easily. For instance, this assumption ensures favorable optimization properties, like coercivity and gradient domination, that simplify convergence analysis. In this sense, removing this assumption while maintaining stability and convergence guarantees is essential to generalize policy gradient methods, a challenge that has remained an active research topic
[
22
,
18
,
13
,
23
]
.

As also pointed out in
[
22
]
, the
\gamma
-discounted LQR problems studied in
[
18
,
13
,
23
]
are equivalent to the standard undiscounted LQR with system matrices scaled by
\sqrt{\gamma}
. In
[
18
,
13
,
23
]
, this scaling results in an enlarged set of stabilizing policies when
\gamma
is sufficiently small, enabling policy gradient algorithms to start from an arbitrary policy by choosing
\gamma
appropriately.
However, in this setting, the discounting factor serves as an additional parameter that can be tuned (e.g., as in
[
18
]
).
In contrast, receding-horizon approaches
[
22
]
, and our work here, consider the undiscounted LQR setting, where no discount factor is introduced, and if one were present, it would be treated as a fixed problem parameter rather than a tunable one.

This paper builds on the receding-horizon policy gradient framework introduced in
[
22
]
, a significant step towards eliminating the need for a stabilizing initial policy by recursively updating finite-horizon costs. While the approach proposed in
[
22
]
marks an important step forward in model-free LQR, we address the reliance on the two-point gradient estimation, a known limitation discussed earlier.
Building on the gradient estimation approach from our earlier work
[
16
]
, we adapt the core idea to accommodate the new setup that eliminates the initial stability assumption. Specifically, our modified method retains the same convergence rate while overcoming the restrictive assumptions of two-point estimation. Beyond these modifications, we revisit the convergence analysis in
[
22
]
and identify a calculation error that obscured the fact that their error accumulation grows exponentially with the horizon length, affecting the claimed sample complexity guarantee. We also provide the corresponding corrected sample complexity rate. We then develop a refined argument based on the Riemannian distance
[
3
]
, under which the accumulated error grows only linearly with the horizon length rather than exponentially. This leads to a sample complexity bound of
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\epsilon^{-2})
, with no problem-specific constants appearing in the exponent of
(1/\epsilon)
.

In summary, the key contributions of this work are:

•

We develop a new error accumulation analysis under which the accumulated error grows linearly, rather than exponentially, with the horizon length, yielding an improved sample complexity bound of
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\epsilon^{-2})
.

•

We propose a more practical gradient estimate that avoids reliance on two-point estimation and its assumption of the ability to select the randomness of system.

1.1.
Algorithm and Paper Structure Overview

The paper is structured into three sections. Section II presents the necessary preliminaries and establishes the notation used throughout the paper. Section III introduces our proposed algorithm, which operates through a hierarchical double-loop structure, an outer loop which provides a surrogate cost function in a receding horizon manner, and an inner loop applying policy gradient method to obtain an estimate of its optimal policy. Section IV delves deeper into the policy gradient method employed in the inner loop, providing rigorous convergence results and theoretical guarantees for this critical component of the algorithm. Section V includes the sample complexity bounds, and comparisons with the results in the literature. Finally, we provide simulations studies verifying our findings in Section VI.

To be more specific, the core idea of the algorithm leverages the observation that, for any error tolerance
\epsilon
, there exists sufficiently large finite horizon
N
where the sequence of policies minimizing recursively updated finite-horizon costs can approximate the optimal policy for the infinite-horizon cost within
\epsilon
neighborhood. This receding-horizon design is key to eliminating the need for an initial stabilizing policy: because each horizon step solves a finite-horizon problem, all stage costs remain finite even when the initial policy is unstable. This insight motivates the algorithm’s design: a backward outer loop that iteratively constructs surrogate cost functions over a sequence of finite horizons, and a forward inner loop that optimizes these surrogate costs. At each stage
h
, the policies for later steps
h+1
to
N-1
are frozen, and the inner loop applies a policy gradient method to optimize only over the policy at stage
h
, yielding an approximately optimal policy
\widetilde{K}_{h}
for the finite-horizon cost for the interval
[h,N]
. The resulting policy
\widetilde{K}_{h}
is then incorporated into the cost function of the subsequent step in the outer loop, and the process repeats backward until
h=0
. Moreover, our inner loop uses a model-free, one-point gradient estimator, which relies solely on observed costs and does not require knowledge of the system dynamics. Finally, note that the terminology “receding-horizon” here refers to this backward policy-computation procedure, rather than an online receding-horizon implementation; in particular, the final output
\widetilde{K}_{0}
is a single time-invariant feedback gain that will be applied to the original infinite-horizon LQR problem.

The main difficulty in analyzing the proposed algorithm stems from the fact that the approximation errors from the policy gradient method in the inner loop propagate across all steps of the outer loop. To ensure overall convergence, the algorithm imposes a requirement on the accuracy of the policy gradient method in the inner loop. Each policy obtained must be sufficiently close to the optimal policy for the corresponding finite-horizon cost. This guarantees that the final policy at the last step of the outer loop converges to the true optimal policy for the infinite-horizon cost.

Preliminaries

In this section, we gather the required notation, closely following the ones in
[
22
]
which our work builds on. We use
\|X\|
,
\|X\|_{F}
,
\sigma_{\min}(X)
, and

to denote the 2-norm, Frobenius norm, minimum singular value, and the condition number of a matrix

\rho(X)
to denote the spectral radius of a square matrix
X
. Moreover, for a positive definite matrix

\|X\|_{W}^{2}:=\sup_{z\neq 0}\frac{z^{\top}X^{\top}WXz}{z^{\top}Wz}.

Furthermore, we denote the Riemannian distance
[
3
]
between two symmetric positive definite matrices
U,V\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times n}
by

\delta(U,V):=\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n}\log^{2}\lambda_{k}(UV^{-1})\right)^{1/2}.

Now consider the discrete-time linear system

(1)

x_{t+1}=Ax_{t}+Bu_{t},

where
x_{t}\in\real^{n}
is the system state at time
t\in\{0,1,2,\dotsc\}
,
u_{t}\in\real^{m}
is the control input at time
t
,
A\in\real^{n\times n}
and
B\in\real^{n\times m}
are the system matrices which are
unknown
to the control designer. In our setting, the initial state
x_{0}
is drawn randomly from a distribution
\mathcal{D}
and satisfies

(2)

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}[x_{0}]=0,\quad{\mathbb{E}}[x_{0}x_{0}^{\top}]=\Sigma_{0},\ \text{and}\ \|x_{0}\|^{2}\leq C_{m}\ \text{a.s.}

for some positive definite
\Sigma_{0}\in\real^{n\times n}
and scalar
C_{m}>0
. This assumption of a random initial state has been widely adopted in the recent LQR literature
[
7
,
15
,
17
,
11
,
9
,
14
,
5
,
12
]
.
The objective in the LQR problem is to find the optimal controller that minimizes the following cost

J_{\infty}={\mathbb{E}}\left[\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}x_{t}^{\top}Qx_{t}+u_{t}^{\top}Ru_{t}\right],

where
Q\in\real^{n\times n}
and
R\in\real^{m\times m}
are the symmetric positive definite matrices that parameterize the cost. We require the pair
(A,B)
to be stabilizable, and since
Q
is positive definite (which we also show by
Q\succ 0
), the pair
(A,Q^{1/2})
is observable.
As a result, the unique optimal controller is a linear state-feedback
u^{\ast}_{t}=-K^{*}x_{t}
where
K^{*}\in\real^{m\times n}
is derived as follows

(3)

K^{*}=(R+B^{\top}P^{*}B)^{-1}B^{\top}P^{*}A,

and
P^{*}\in\real^{n\times n}
denotes the unique positive definite solution to the undiscounted discrete-time algebraic Riccati equation (ARE)
[
2
]
:

(4)

P=A^{\top}PA-A^{\top}PB(R+B^{\top}PB)^{-1}B^{\top}PA+Q.

Moreover, following the notation in
[
22
]
, we denote the
P^{*}
-induced norm by
\|X\|_{*}
. Since the optimal controller is a linear state feedback, we formulate the costs directly in terms of the feedback gain
K
, taking
u_{t}=-Kx_{t}
for all
t\geq 0
.
Throughout the paper, we use the terms “policy” and “feedback gain” interchangeably to refer to such matrices
K
.

We now introduce important notation used in the algorithm description and the proof of the main result.
Let
N
be the horizon length and
h
the initial time step. The true finite-horizon cost
J_{h}(K_{h})
of a policy
K_{h}
is defined as

(5)

\displaystyle J_{h}(K_{h}):=

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}_{x_{0}\sim\mathcal{D}}\Bigg[\sum_{t=h+1}^{N-1}x_{t-h}^{\top}\left(Q+(K^{*}_{t})^{\top}RK^{*}_{t}\right)x_{t-h}+x_{0}^{\top}\left(Q+K_{h}^{\top}RK_{h}\right)x_{0}+x_{N-h}^{\top}Q_{N}x_{N-h}\Bigg],

where:

•

x_{0}
denotes the initial state
x_{0}
drawn from distribution
\mathcal{D}
,

•

Q_{N}
is the terminal cost matrix,

•

K_{h}
is the feedback gain applied at step
0
,

•

K^{*}_{t}
is the feedback gain at step
t-h
, to be formally defined via the Riccati difference equation in (
23
);

Finally, for all
t\in\{h+1,\dotsc,N-1\}
, the state evolves as

x_{t-h+1}=(A-BK^{*}_{t})x_{t-h},

with

x_{1}=(A-BK_{h})x_{0}.

While
J_{h}
is the true finite-horizon cost, its downstream policies
\{K^{*}_{h+1},\dotsc,K^{*}_{N-1}\}
are obtained from the RDE and cannot be computed directly when the system matrices are unknown. Therefore, we replace them with the policies generated by the algorithm and define the surrogate cost

(6)

\displaystyle\widetilde{J}_{h}(K_{h}):=

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}_{x_{0}\sim\mathcal{D}}\Bigg[\sum_{t=h+1}^{N-1}x_{t-h}^{\top}\left(Q+\widetilde{K}_{t}^{\top}R\widetilde{K}_{t}\right)x_{t-h}+x_{0}^{\top}\left(Q+K_{h}^{\top}RK_{h}\right)x_{0}+x_{N-h}^{\top}Q_{N}x_{N-h}\Bigg],

where
\widetilde{K}_{t}
is the feedback gain derived at step
t
of the outer loop of the algorithm, and for all
t\in\{h+1,\dotsc,N-1\}
, the state evolves as:

x_{t-h+1}=(A-B\widetilde{K}_{t})x_{t-h},

with

x_{1}=(A-BK_{h})x_{0}.

The key difference between
\widetilde{J}_{h}(K_{h})
and
J_{h}(K_{h})
lies in the use of
\widetilde{K}_{t}
versus
K^{*}_{t}
for
t\in\{h+1,\dotsc,N-1\}
. This distinction implies that
\widetilde{J}_{h}(K_{h})
incorporates all errors from earlier steps, precisely the ones at
\{N-1,\dotsc,h+1\}
, as the procedure is backward.

We now define several functions that facilitate the characterization of our gradient estimate, which uses ideas from our earlier work in
[
16
]
). To start, we let

(7)

\displaystyle\widetilde{J}_{h}(K_{h};x_{0})

\displaystyle:=x_{0}^{\top}(Q+K_{h}^{\top}RK_{h})x_{0}+\sum_{t=h+1}^{N-1}x_{t-h}^{\top}\left(Q+\widetilde{K}_{t}^{\top}R\widetilde{K}_{t}\right)x_{t-h}+x_{N-h}^{\top}Q_{N}x_{N-h}

(8)

\displaystyle=x_{0}^{\top}(Q+K_{h}^{\top}RK_{h})x_{0}+x_{0}^{\top}(A-BK_{h})^{\top}\widetilde{P}_{h+1}(A-BK_{h})x_{0},\qquad

so that

\widetilde{J}_{h}(K_{h})={\mathbb{E}}_{x_{0}\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[\widetilde{J}_{h}(K_{h};x_{0})\right].

Using (
8
), we can compute the gradient of
\widetilde{J}_{h}(K_{h};x_{0})
with respect to
K_{h}
as follows:

(9)

\displaystyle\nabla\widetilde{J}_{h}(K_{h};x_{0})

\displaystyle=\nabla\left(x_{0}^{\top}K_{h}^{\top}RK_{h}x_{0}+x_{0}^{\top}K_{h}^{\top}B^{\top}\widetilde{P}_{h+1}BK_{h}x_{0}-2x_{0}^{\top}A^{\top}\widetilde{P}_{h+1}BK_{h}x_{0}\right)

(10)

\displaystyle=2RK_{h}x_{0}x_{0}^{\top}+2B^{\top}\widetilde{P}_{h+1}BK_{h}x_{0}x_{0}^{\top}-2B^{\top}\widetilde{P}_{h+1}Ax_{0}x_{0}^{\top}

(11)

\displaystyle=2\left((R+B^{\top}\widetilde{P}_{h+1}B)K_{h}-B^{\top}\widetilde{P}_{h+1}A\right)x_{0}x_{0}^{\top},

and thus,

(12)

\displaystyle\nabla\widetilde{J}_{h}(K_{h})

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}_{x_{0}\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[\nabla\widetilde{J}_{h}(K_{h};x_{0})\right]

(13)

\displaystyle=2\left((R+B^{\top}\widetilde{P}_{h+1}B)K_{h}-B^{\top}\widetilde{P}_{h+1}A\right){\mathbb{E}}_{x_{0}\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[x_{0}x_{0}^{\top}\right]

(14)

\displaystyle=2\left((R+B^{\top}\widetilde{P}_{h+1}B)K_{h}-B^{\top}\widetilde{P}_{h+1}A\right)\Sigma_{0}.

Moreover, we define

(15)

\displaystyle Q_{h}(x_{0},u_{0})

\displaystyle:=x_{0}^{\top}Qx_{0}+u_{0}^{\top}Ru_{0}+\sum_{t=h+1}^{N-1}x_{t-h}^{\top}\left(Q+\widetilde{K}_{t}^{\top}R\widetilde{K}_{t}\right)x_{t-h}+x_{N-h}^{\top}Q_{N}x_{N-h}

(16)

\displaystyle=x_{0}^{\top}Qx_{0}+u_{0}^{\top}Ru_{0}+\widetilde{J}_{h+1}(\widetilde{K}_{h+1};Ax_{0}+Bu_{0})

(17)

\displaystyle=x_{0}^{\top}Qx_{0}+u_{0}^{\top}Ru_{0}+(Ax_{0}+Bu_{0})^{\top}\widetilde{P}_{h+1}(Ax_{0}+Bu_{0}),

so that

\widetilde{J}_{h}(K_{h};x_{0})=Q_{h}(x_{0},-K_{h}x_{0}),

and

\widetilde{J}_{h}(K_{h})={\mathbb{E}}_{x_{0}\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[Q_{h}(x_{0},-K_{h}x_{0})\right].

Having established the cost functions, we now introduce the notation used to describe the policies:

(18)

\displaystyle K^{*}_{h}

\displaystyle:=\operatorname{argmin}_{K_{h}}J_{h}(K_{h}),

(19)

\displaystyle\widetilde{K}^{*}_{h}

\displaystyle:=\operatorname{argmin}_{K_{h}}\widetilde{J}_{h}(K_{h}),

where
K^{*}_{h}
denotes the optimal policy for the true cost

, and
\widetilde{K}^{*}_{h}
denotes the optimal policy for the surrogate cost
\widetilde{J}_{h}(K_{h})
.
Additionally,
\widetilde{K}_{h}
represents an estimate of
\widetilde{K}^{*}_{h}
. It is obtained using a policy gradient method in the inner loop of the algorithm, which is applied at each step

of the outer loop to minimize the surrogate cost
\widetilde{J}_{h}(K_{h})
.

We now move on to the recursive equations. First, we have

(20)

\displaystyle\widetilde{P}_{h}

\displaystyle=(A-B\widetilde{K}_{h})^{\top}\widetilde{P}_{h+1}(A-B\widetilde{K}_{h})+\widetilde{K}_{h}^{\top}R\widetilde{K}_{h}+Q,

where
\widetilde{P}_{N}=Q_{N}
. In addition,

(21)

\displaystyle\widetilde{P}^{*}_{h}

\displaystyle=(A-B\widetilde{K}^{*}_{h})^{\top}\widetilde{P}_{h+1}(A-B\widetilde{K}^{*}_{h})+(\widetilde{K}^{*}_{h})^{\top}R\widetilde{K}^{*}_{h}+Q,

where
\widetilde{K}^{*}_{h}
from (
19
) can also be computed from

\widetilde{K}^{*}_{h}=(R+B^{\top}\widetilde{P}_{h+1}B)^{-1}B^{\top}\widetilde{P}_{h+1}A.

Finally, we have the Riccati difference equation (RDE):

(22)

\displaystyle P^{*}_{h}

\displaystyle=(A-BK^{*}_{h})^{\top}P^{*}_{h+1}(A-BK^{*}_{h})+(K^{*}_{h})^{\top}RK^{*}_{h}+Q,

where
P^{*}_{N}=Q_{N}
and
K^{*}_{h}
from (
19
) can also be computed from

(23)

K^{*}_{h}=(R+B^{\top}P^{*}_{h+1}B)^{-1}B^{\top}P^{*}_{h+1}A.

As a result, it is easy to follow that

(24)

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}_{x_{0}\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[x_{0}^{\top}\widetilde{P}_{h}x_{0}\right]

\displaystyle=\widetilde{J}_{h}(\widetilde{K}_{h}),

(25)

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}_{x_{0}\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[x_{0}^{\top}\widetilde{P}^{*}_{h}x_{0}\right]

\displaystyle=\widetilde{J}_{h}(\widetilde{K}^{*}_{h}),\quad\text{and}

(26)

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}_{x_{0}\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[x_{0}^{\top}P^{*}_{h}x_{0}\right]

We also define the Riccati operator

(27)

\mathcal{R}(P):=Q+A^{\top}(P-PB(R+B^{\top}PB)^{-1}B^{\top}P)A,

so that
\widetilde{P}^{*}_{h}
and

can also be shown as

(28)

\displaystyle\widetilde{P}^{*}_{h}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{R}(\widetilde{P}_{h+1})

(29)

\displaystyle P^{*}_{h}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{R}(P^{*}_{h+1}),

after replacing

and

We next record the nonexpansiveness of the Riccati operator
\mathcal{R}(\cdot)
under
\delta(\cdot,\cdot)
. Existing contraction results establish this property when
A
is nonsingular
[
4
]
.
The following lemma extends the result to arbitrary
A
.

Lemma 2.1
.

Consider the Riccati operator
\mathcal{R}(\cdot)
defined in (
27
). For any symmetric positive definite matrices
X,Y\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
, we have

\delta(\mathcal{R}(X),\mathcal{R}(Y))\leq\delta(X,Y).

Proof.

First, we make the dependence of the Riccati operator on
A
explicit and write

\mathcal{R}_{A}(P):=Q+A^{\top}\left(P-PB(R+B^{\top}PB)^{-1}B^{\top}P\right)A.

If
A
is nonsingular, the result follows directly from
[
4
, Theorem 1.7]
. Now let
A
be arbitrary. Since the set of nonsingular matrices is dense in
\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
, there exists a sequence of nonsingular matrices
\{A_{j}\}
such that
A_{j}\to A
. For each
j
, the result for the nonsingular case gives

(30)

\delta\big(\mathcal{R}_{A_{j}}(X),\mathcal{R}_{A_{j}}(Y)\big)\leq\delta(X,Y).

Fix
P\succ 0
, and define

S(P):=P-PB(R+B^{\top}PB)^{-1}B^{\top}P.

By the Woodbury identity,

S(P)=\left(P^{-1}+BR^{-1}B^{\top}\right)^{-1}\succ 0.

Hence

\mathcal{R}_{A_{j}}(P)=Q+A_{j}^{\top}S(P)A_{j}\succeq Q\succ 0,

and similarly
\mathcal{R}_{A}(P)\succ 0
. Moreover, for fixed
P
, the map

A\mapsto Q+A^{\top}S(P)A

is continuous. Thus, by continuity,

\lim_{j\to\infty}\mathcal{R}_{A_{j}}(P)=\mathcal{R}_{A}(P).

In particular, as
j\to\infty
,

\mathcal{R}_{A_{j}}(X)\to\mathcal{R}_{A}(X),\qquad\mathcal{R}_{A_{j}}(Y)\to\mathcal{R}_{A}(Y).

By the joint continuity of the Riemannian distance
\delta(\cdot,\cdot)
on the cone of positive definite matrices,

\delta\big(\mathcal{R}_{A}(X),\mathcal{R}_{A}(Y)\big)=\lim_{j\to\infty}\delta\big(\mathcal{R}_{A_{j}}(X),\mathcal{R}_{A_{j}}(Y)\big).

Taking
j\to\infty
in (
30
) yields

\delta\big(\mathcal{R}_{A}(X),\mathcal{R}_{A}(Y)\big)\leq\delta(X,Y),

which proves the result.
∎

3.
The Outer Loop (Receding-Horizon Policy Gradient)

It has been demonstrated that the solution to the RDE (

. As a result,

Theorem 3.1

Let

N_{0}=\frac{1}{2}\frac{\log{\left(\frac{\|Q_{N}-P^{*}\|_{*}\kappa_{P^{*}}\|A^{*}_{K}\|\|B\|}{\epsilon\lambda_{\min}(R)}\right)}}{\log{\left(\frac{1}{\|A^{*}_{K}\|_{*}}\right)}},

where

.
Then it holds that
\|A^{*}_{K}\|_{*}<1
and for all
N\geq N_{0}
, the control policy
K^{*}_{0}
computed by (
23
) is stabilizing and satisfies
\|K^{*}_{0}-K^{*}\|\leq\epsilon
for any
\epsilon>0
.

The proof of Theorem

is provided in Appendix
A

. A full discussion of this assumption is provided in Remark

Then, as later shown in (

for all

.

, the algorithm (i) freezes downstream policies, (ii) builds the surrogate cost
\widetilde{J}_{h}
, (iii) runs an inner loop of gradient-based updates with some step-size
\alpha_{h,i}
until approximate convergence, and (iv) outputs
\widetilde{K}_{h}
, which is incorporated into the surrogate cost for step
h-1
.

We formalize this procedure in Algorithm
1
and briefly state what information it uses in practice:

Information available to the algorithm.

At each stage

, the updates require neither knowledge of the system matrices
(A,B)
nor full trajectory information. Instead, the algorithm draws
\eta\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)
, applies a perturbed action
u_{0}=-K_{h,i}x_{0}+\sigma\eta

of length

is deferred to Remark
4.1

Note that in this section, we focus on the outer loop of Algorithm
1
, analyzing the requirements it imposes on the convergence of the policy gradient method employed in the inner loop. The details of the policy gradient method will be discussed in the next section.

Before we move on to the next result, we
define the following constants:

\displaystyle a:=\frac{\sigma_{\min}(Q)}{2},\quad\varphi:=\max_{t\in\{0,1,\dotsc,N-1\}}\|A-BK^{*}_{t}\|,

\displaystyle C_{1}:=\frac{\varphi\|B\|}{\lambda_{\min}(R)},\quad C_{2}:=2\varphi\|A\|\left(1+\frac{(P_{\max}+a)\|B\|^{2}}{\lambda_{\min}(R)}\right),\quad C_{3}:=2\left(\|R\|+(P_{\max}+a)\|B\|^{2}\right).

(32)

\sqrt{\frac{a}{C_{3}}\frac{\epsilon}{8eNC_{1}P_{\max}}},\sqrt{\frac{\epsilon}{4C_{1}C_{3}}}\end{array}\right\}&h\geq 1,\\[5.69054pt]
\displaystyle\frac{\epsilon}{4}&h=0.\end{cases}

(Main result: outer loop):

to

4.
The Inner Loop and Policy Gradient

In this section, we focus on the inner loop of Algorithm
1
, on which we will implement our proposed policy gradient method.

We seek a way to estimate the gradient of the cost function
\widetilde{J}_{h}(K)
with respect to
K
. To remedy, we propose:

(36)

\displaystyle\widehat{\nabla}\widetilde{J}_{h}(K):=-\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}Q_{h}(x_{0},u_{0})(u_{0}+Kx_{0})x_{0}^{\top},

where
x_{0}
is sampled from
\mathcal{D}
, and then
u_{0}
is chosen randomly from the Gaussian distribution
\mathcal{N}(-Kx_{0},\sigma^{2}I_{m})
for some
\sigma>0
. Moreover, we rewrite
u_{0}\sim\mathcal{N}(-Kx_{0},\sigma^{2}I_{m})
as

(37)

u_{0}=-Kx_{0}+\sigma\eta,

where
\eta\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{m})
. Substituting (
37
) in (
36
) yields

(38)

\displaystyle\widehat{\nabla}\widetilde{J}_{h}(K)=-\frac{1}{\sigma}Q_{h}(x_{0},-Kx_{0}+\sigma\eta)\eta x_{0}^{\top}.

This expression corresponds to the gradient estimate used in Algorithm
1
, as described in its formulation.

We now provide the following remark on the computation of
Q_{h}(x_{0},u_{0})
for this gradient estimate:

Remark 4.1
.

For any fixed
h
, the
Q
-function in (
38
) represents the finite-horizon cost-to-go of horizon length
N-h
, i.e.,
N-h
stage costs followed by a terminal cost (indices in the notation can be perceived as shifts). To clarify, let us use the following notation

\displaystyle u_{t}

\displaystyle:=\begin{cases}-Kx_{0}+\sigma\eta,&\text{: }t=0,\\
-\widetilde{K}_{h+t}\ x_{t},&\text{: }t\geq 1,\end{cases}

where
(x_{t},u_{t})
form the trajectory of the system, i.e., the dynamics follow

x_{t+1}=Ax_{t}+Bu_{t},

and
x_{0}\sim\mathcal{D}
. Now using the quadratic stage cost

c^{(h)}_{t}:=\begin{cases}x_{t}^{\top}Q_{N}x_{t}&\text{: }t=N-h,\\
x_{t}^{\top}Qx_{t}+u_{t}^{\top}Ru_{t}&\text{: otherwise},\end{cases}

we can write

Q_{h}(x_{0},u_{0})=\sum_{t=0}^{N-h}c^{(h)}_{t}.

This gives the rollout representation of
Q_{h}(x_{0},u_{0})
used in our gradient estimator.
\diamond

Thus, the expression in (
38
) is a one-point gradient estimator that requires only a single observation of the cost
Q_{h}(x_{0},u_{0})
at each iteration, as opposed to a two-point estimator, which requires two such observations for different policies under the exact same realization of the randomness
x_{0}
.

We start by showing that the estimator is unbiased with respect to true gradient:

Proposition 4.1
.

Suppose
x_{0}\sim\mathcal{D}
and
u_{0}
is drawn from
\mathcal{N}(-Kx_{0},\sigma^{2}I_{m})
as before. Then for any
K
, we have that

(39)

{\mathbb{E}}[\widehat{\nabla}\widetilde{J}_{h}(K)]=\nabla\widetilde{J}_{h}(K).

Proof.

Following (
38
),

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}[\widehat{\nabla}\widetilde{J}_{h}(K)]

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}_{x_{0}}\left[{\mathbb{E}}_{\eta}\left[\widehat{\nabla}\widetilde{J}_{h}(K)\big|x_{0}\right]\right]

(40)

\displaystyle\overset{\mathrm{(i)}}{=}{\mathbb{E}}_{x_{0}}\left[-\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}{\mathbb{E}}_{\eta}\left[Q_{h}(x_{0},-Kx_{0}+\sigma\eta)(\sigma\eta)\big|x_{0}\right]x_{0}^{\top}\right]

(41)

\displaystyle\overset{\mathrm{(ii)}}{=}{\mathbb{E}}_{x_{0}}\left[{\mathbb{E}}_{\eta}\left[-\nabla_{u}Q_{h}(x_{0},u)\bigg|_{u=-Kx_{0}+\sigma\eta}\big|x_{0}\right]x_{0}^{\top}\right],

where (i) follows from
x_{0}^{\top}
being determined when given
x_{0}
, and (ii) from Stein’s lemma
[
19
]
. Using (
17
), we compute

\displaystyle\nabla_{u}Q_{h}(x_{0},u)

\displaystyle=\nabla_{u}\left(x_{0}^{\top}Qx_{0}+u^{\top}Ru+(Ax_{0}+Bu)^{\top}\widetilde{P}_{h+1}(Ax_{0}+Bu)\right)

\displaystyle=2Ru+2B^{\top}\widetilde{P}_{h+1}Bu+2B^{\top}\widetilde{P}_{h+1}Ax_{0},

which evaluated at
u=-Kx_{0}+\sigma\eta
yields

\displaystyle\nabla_{u}Q_{h}(x_{0},u)\bigg|_{u=-Kx_{0}+\sigma\eta}=2\left((R+B^{\top}\widetilde{P}_{h+1}B)(-Kx_{0}+\sigma\eta)+B^{\top}\widetilde{P}_{h+1}Ax_{0}\right).

Substituting in (
41
), we obtain

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}[\widehat{\nabla}\widetilde{J}_{h}(K)]

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}_{x_{0}\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[2\left((R+B^{\top}\widetilde{P}_{h+1}B)K-B^{\top}\widetilde{P}_{h+1}A\right)x_{0}x_{0}^{\top}\right]

\displaystyle=2\left((R+B^{\top}\widetilde{P}_{h+1}B)K-B^{\top}\widetilde{P}_{h+1}A\right)\Sigma_{0}

\displaystyle\overset{\mathrm{(i)}}{=}\nabla\widetilde{J}_{h}(K),

where (i) follows from (
14
).
∎

Similar to
[
22
]
, we define the following sets regarding the inner loop of the algorithm for each
h\in\{0,1,\dotsc,N-1\}
:

(42)

\mathcal{G}_{h}:=\{K_{h}|\widetilde{J}_{h}(K_{h})-\widetilde{J}_{h}(\widetilde{K}^{*}_{h})\leq 10\zeta^{-1}\widetilde{J}_{h}(K_{h,0})\},

for some arbitrary
\zeta\in(0,1)
. We also define the following constant:

\displaystyle\widetilde{C}_{h}:=\frac{10\zeta^{-1}\widetilde{J}_{h}(K_{h,0})+\widetilde{J}_{h}(\widetilde{K}^{*}_{h})}{\sigma_{\min}(\Sigma_{0})\sigma_{\min}(R)}.

We next provide a second-moment bound on the size of the estimator:

Lemma 4.1
.

Suppose
\|\widetilde{P}_{h+1}-P^{*}_{h+1}\|\leq a
.
Then for any
K\in\mathcal{G}_{h}
, we have that

(43)

{\mathbb{E}}\left[\|\widehat{\nabla}\widetilde{J}_{h}(K)\|_{F}^{2}\right]\leq\xi_{h,3},

where
\xi_{h,1},\xi_{h,2},\xi_{h,3}\in\real
are given by

(44)

\displaystyle\xi_{h,1}

\displaystyle:=\Big(\|Q\|+2\|R\|\widetilde{C}_{h}^{2}+2(P_{\max}+a)(\|A\|^{2}+2\|B\|^{2}\widetilde{C}_{h})\Big)C_{m}^{3/2},

(45)

\displaystyle\xi_{h,2}

\displaystyle:=2\left(\|R\|+2(P_{\max}+a)\|B\|^{2}\right)C_{m}^{1/2},

(46)

\displaystyle\xi_{h,3}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\xi_{h,1}^{2}m+2\xi_{h,1}\xi_{h,2}m(m+2)+\sigma^{2}\xi_{h,2}^{2}m(m+2)(m+4).

Proof.

Using the Formulation of
\widehat{\nabla}\widetilde{J}_{h}(K)
derived in (
38
), we have

(47)

\displaystyle\|\widehat{\nabla}\widetilde{J}_{h}(K)\|_{F}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\sigma}Q_{h}(x_{0},-Kx_{0}+\sigma\eta)\|\eta\|\|x_{0}\|.

Before we continue, we provide the following bound:

Sublemma 4.1
.

Suppose
K\in\mathcal{G}_{h}
. Then it holds that

(48)

\|K\|^{2}_{F}\leq\widetilde{C}_{h}.

Proof of Sublemma
4.1
:

Using (
6
), we have

(49)

\displaystyle\widetilde{J}_{h}(K)

\displaystyle\geq{\mathbb{E}}_{x_{0}\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[x_{0}^{\top}(Q+K^{\top}RK)x_{0}\right]

(50)

\displaystyle=\tr\left((Q+K^{\top}RK)\Sigma_{0}\right)

(51)

\displaystyle\geq\sigma_{\min}(\Sigma_{0})\sigma_{\min}(R)\|K\|^{2}_{F}.

Rearranging (
51
) yields

\displaystyle\|K\|^{2}_{F}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\widetilde{J}_{h}(K)}{\sigma_{\min}(\Sigma_{0})\sigma_{\min}(R)}

\displaystyle\overset{\mathrm{(i)}}{\leq}\frac{10\zeta^{-1}\widetilde{J}_{h}(K_{h,0})+\widetilde{J}_{h}(\widetilde{K}^{*}_{h})}{\sigma_{\min}(\Sigma_{0})\sigma_{\min}(R)}=\widetilde{C}_{h},

where (i) follows from the definition of the set
\mathcal{G}_{h}
in (
42
).

\blacksquare

We now continue with the proof of Lemma
4.1
. Note that

\displaystyle Q_{h}(x_{0},-Kx_{0}+\sigma\eta)=

\displaystyle x_{0}^{\top}Qx_{0}+(-Kx_{0}+\sigma\eta)^{\top}R(-Kx_{0}+\sigma\eta)

\displaystyle+(Ax_{0}+B(-Kx_{0}+\sigma\eta))^{\top}\widetilde{P}_{h+1}(Ax_{0}+B(-Kx_{0}+\sigma\eta))

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\|Q\|C_{m}+\|R\|\|-Kx_{0}+\sigma\eta\|^{2}+\|\widetilde{P}_{h+1}\|\|Ax_{0}+B(-Kx_{0}+\sigma\eta)\|^{2}.

As a result,

(52)

\displaystyle Q_{h}(x_{0},-Kx_{0}+\sigma\eta)\leq

\displaystyle\|Q\|C_{m}+2\|R\|(\widetilde{C}_{h}C_{m}+\sigma^{2}\|\eta\|^{2})+2(P_{\max}+a)\|A\|^{2}C_{m}

(53)

\displaystyle+4(P_{\max}+a)\|B\|^{2}(\widetilde{C}_{h}C_{m}+\sigma^{2}\|\eta\|^{2}))

(54)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle C_{m}(\|Q\|+2\|R\|\widetilde{C}_{h})+2C_{m}(P_{\max}+a)(\|A\|^{2}+2\|B\|^{2}\widetilde{C}_{h})

(55)

\displaystyle+2\left(\|R\|+2(P_{\max}+a)\|B\|^{2}\right)\sigma^{2}\|\eta\|^{2},

where the inequality follows from Sublemma
4.1
along with the fact that by the assumption,

\displaystyle\|\widetilde{P}_{h+1}\|\leq\|P^{*}_{h+1}\|+\|\widetilde{P}_{h+1}-P^{*}_{h+1}\|\leq P_{\max}+a.

Combining (
47
) with (
55
) and (
2
), we obtain

(56)

\displaystyle\|\widehat{\nabla}\widetilde{J}_{h}(K)\|_{F}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\sigma}\Big(\|Q\|+2\|R\|\widetilde{C}_{h}+2(P_{\max}+a)(\|A\|^{2}+2\|B\|^{2}\widetilde{C}_{h})\Big)C_{m}^{3/2}\|\eta\|

(57)

\displaystyle\quad+2\left(\|R\|+2(P_{\max}+a)\|B\|^{2}\right)\sigma C_{m}^{1/2}\|\eta\|^{3}

(58)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sigma}\xi_{h,1}\|\eta\|+\sigma\xi_{h,2}\|\eta\|^{3}.

Now note that using (
58
), we have

\displaystyle\|\widehat{\nabla}\widetilde{J}_{h}(K)\|^{2}_{F}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\xi_{h,1}^{2}\|\eta\|^{2}+2\xi_{h,1}\xi_{h,2}\|\eta\|^{4}+\sigma^{2}\xi_{h,2}^{2}\|\eta\|^{6}.

Now since
\|\eta\|\sim\chi(m)
whose moments are known, taking an expectation results in

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[\|\widehat{\nabla}\widetilde{J}_{h}(K)\|^{2}_{F}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\xi_{h,1}^{2}{\mathbb{E}}[\|\eta\|^{2}]+2\xi_{h,1}\xi_{h,2}{\mathbb{E}}[\|\eta\|^{4}]+\sigma^{2}\xi_{h,2}^{2}{\mathbb{E}}[\|\eta\|^{6}]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\xi_{h,1}^{2}m+2\xi_{h,1}\xi_{h,2}m(m+2)+\sigma^{2}\xi_{h,2}^{2}m(m+2)(m+4)

\displaystyle=\xi_{h,3},

concluding the proof.
∎

We next establish some useful properties of the cost function
\widetilde{J}_{h}(K)
in the following lemma.

Lemma 4.2
.

For all
h\in\{0,1,\dotsc,N-1\}
, the function
\widetilde{J}_{h}
is
\frac{\mu}{2}
-strongly convex, where

\mu:=4\sigma_{\min}(\Sigma_{0})\sigma_{\min}(R),

and in particular, for all
K\in\real^{m\times n}
,

(59)

\|\nabla\widetilde{J}_{h}(K)\|_{F}^{2}\geq\mu(\widetilde{J}_{h}(K)-\widetilde{J}_{h}(\widetilde{K}^{*}_{h})),

where
\widetilde{K}^{*}_{h}
is the global minimizer of
\widetilde{J}_{h}
. Moreover, assuming that
\|\widetilde{P}_{h+1}-P^{*}_{h+1}\|\leq a
, we have that for all
K_{1},K_{2}\in\real^{m\times n}
,

(60)

\|\nabla\widetilde{J}_{h}(K_{2})-\nabla\widetilde{J}_{h}(K_{1})\|_{F}\leq L\|K_{2}-K_{1}\|_{F},

where

L:=C_{3}\|\Sigma_{0}\|.

Proof.

We first prove the strong convexity as follows:

\displaystyle\left\langle\nabla\widetilde{J}_{h}(K_{2})-\nabla\widetilde{J}_{h}(K_{1}),K_{2}-K_{1}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=2\tr\left(\Sigma_{0}(K_{2}-K_{1})^{\top}(R+B^{\top}\widetilde{P}_{h+1}B)(K_{2}-K_{1})\right)

\displaystyle\geq 2\sigma_{\min}(\Sigma_{0})\sigma_{\min}(R)\tr\left((K_{2}-K_{1})^{\top}(K_{2}-K_{1})\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\mu}{2}\|K_{2}-K_{1}\|_{F}^{2}.

Note the the next inequality is an immediate
consequence of the PL-inequality. Now we move on to the
L
-smoothness property:

\displaystyle\|\nabla\widetilde{J}_{h}(K_{2})-\nabla\widetilde{J}_{h}(K_{1})\|_{F}

\displaystyle=\|2(R+B^{\top}\widetilde{P}_{h+1}B)(K_{2}-K_{1})\Sigma_{0}\|_{F}

\displaystyle\leq\|\Sigma_{0}\|(2\|R+(P_{\max}+a)B^{\top}B\|)\|K_{2}-K_{1}\|_{F}

\displaystyle=C_{3}\|\Sigma_{0}\|\|K_{2}-K_{1}\|_{F}

\displaystyle=L\|K_{2}-K_{1}\|_{F},

concluding the proof.
∎

Before introducing the next result, let us denote the optimality gap of iterate
i
by

(61)

\Delta_{i}=\widetilde{J}_{h}(K_{h,i})-\widetilde{J}_{h}(\widetilde{K}^{*}_{h}).

Moreover, let
{\mathcal{F}}_{i}
denote the
\sigma
-algebra containing the randomness up to iteration
i
of the inner loop of the algorithm for each
h\in\{0,1,\dotsc,N-1\}
(including
K_{h,i}
but not
\widehat{\nabla}\widetilde{J}_{h}(K_{h,i})
). We then define

(62)

\tau:=\min\left\{i\ |\ \Delta_{i}>10\zeta^{-1}\widetilde{J}_{h}(K_{h,0})\right\},

which is a stopping time with respect to
{\mathcal{F}}_{i}
. Note that there is a slight abuse of notation, as
\Delta_{i}
,
{\mathcal{F}}_{i},
and
\tau
may differ for each
h\in\{0,1,\dotsc,N-1\}
. Since each outer-loop step
h
is analyzed independently, we use the same notation for simplicity.

Combining the unbiasedness of the estimate (Proposition
4.1
), the second-moment bound on its size (Lemma
4.1
), and the regularity properties of
\widetilde{J}_{h}
(Lemma
4.2
), we now show the following descent recursion for the optimality gap:

Lemma 4.3
.

Suppose
\|\widetilde{P}_{h+1}-P^{*}_{h+1}\|\leq a
, and the update rule follows

(63)

K_{h,i+1}=K_{h,i}-\alpha_{h,i}\widehat{\nabla}\widetilde{J}_{h}(K_{h,i}),

where
\alpha_{h,i}>0
is the step-size. Then for any
i\in\{0,1,2,\dotsc\}
,

(64)

{\mathbb{E}}[\Delta_{i+1}|{\mathcal{F}}_{i}]1_{\tau>i}\leq\left(\left(1-\mu\alpha_{h,i}\right)\Delta_{i}+\frac{L\alpha_{h,i}^{2}}{2}\xi_{h,3}\right)1_{\tau>i},

where
\Delta_{i}
is defined in (
61
).

Proof.

First, note that by
L
-smoothness, we have

\displaystyle\Delta_{i+1}-\Delta_{i}

\displaystyle=\widetilde{J}_{h}(K_{h,i+1})-\widetilde{J}_{h}(K_{h,i})

7.
Conclusion

In this paper, we introduced a novel approach to solving the model-free LQR problem, inspired by policy gradient methods, particularly REINFORCE. Our algorithm eliminates the restrictive requirement of starting with a stable initial policy, making it applicable in scenarios where obtaining such a policy is challenging. Furthermore, it removes the reliance on two-point gradient estimation, enhancing practical applicability while maintaining similar rates.

Beyond these improvements, we introduced a refined outer-loop analysis that significantly improves error propagation, leveraging the contraction of the Riccati operator under the Riemannian distance. This ensures that the accumulated error remains linear in the horizon length, leading to a sample complexity bound of
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\epsilon^{-2})
, independent of problem-specific constants, making the method more broadly applicable.

We provide a rigorous theoretical analysis establishing convergence to the optimal policy with competitive sample complexity. Our simulations also show favorable empirical performance without requiring two-point gradient estimates. Together, these results highlight the potential of the proposed method for model-free control. Future work includes extensions to nonlinear and partially observed systems, as well as robustness improvements.

Remark A.1

Since Theorem

requires

to be positive semidefinite for all

Lemma B.1

Appendix C
Proof of Theorem
3.3
: Sketch and Clarification

We follow
[
22
, App. C]
up to the recursion that controls the propagated error in the backward pass. First, select the horizon
N
exactly as in Theorem
3.1
, which ensures
\|K_{0}^{*}-K^{*}\|\leq\epsilon/2
. Then, repeating their bookkeeping for the outer-loop error terms yields the familiar requirement on the last-stage inner-loop error

(157)

\|e_{N-1}\|\;\lesssim\;\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{\frac{\epsilon}{\,C_{1}\,C_{2}^{\,N-2}\,C_{3}\,}}\right),

which is precisely the counterpart of eq. (C.19) in
[
22
]
.

Substituting the choice of
N
from (
34
) into (
157
) gives

C_{2}^{\,N-2}\approx C_{2}^{\,\frac{\log(1/\epsilon)}{2\log(1/\|A^{*}_{K}\|_{*})}}=(1/\epsilon)^{\frac{\log C_{2}}{2\log(1/\|A^{*}_{K}\|_{*})}},

and hence

\|e_{N-1}\|\sim\mathcal{O}\left(\epsilon^{\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\log C_{2}}{4\log(1/\|A^{*}_{K}\|_{*})}}\right).

In
[
22
]
, this exponent is (incorrectly) simplified to
3/4
, i.e., as if
\frac{\log C_{2}}{4\log(1/\|A^{*}_{K}\|_{*})}=\frac{1}{4}
held universally; however, this does not hold, since this ratio is system-dependent in general. The same substitution appears explicitly in
[
22
, (C.4) and (C.19)]
. Indeed, this error propagates to their final sample complexity. Since the inner-loop PG (two-point estimation) needs
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\|e_{h}\|^{-2})
steps to reach the required accuracy at stage
h
, the overall iteration budget must scale as

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(\max\big\{\|e_{0}\|^{-2},\|e_{N-1}\|^{-2}\big\}\right)=\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\!\left(\max\!\left\{\epsilon^{-2},\,\epsilon^{-1-\frac{\log C_{2}}{2\log(1/\|A^{*}_{K}\|_{*})}}\right\}\right),

rather than
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\epsilon^{-2})
obtained by substituting
\|e_{N-1}\|=\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{3/4})
.

Our fix.
Our outer-loop analysis avoids the exponential factor
C_{2}^{N-2}
entirely by exploiting that the Riccati operator
\mathcal{R}(\cdot)
is nonexpansive in the Riemannian distance
\delta(\cdot,\cdot)
(Lemma
2.1
). This yields stage-wise requirements that do not blow up with
N
, recovering the
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\epsilon^{-2})
total rate with a one-point model-free gradient estimator.
