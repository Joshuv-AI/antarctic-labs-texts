A Low-Cost Embedded System for Automated Patient Queue and Health Data Management in Private Medical Chambers
Kawshik Kumar Paul, Mahdi Hasnat Siyam, Khandokar Md. Rahat Hossain

Abstract
This paper presents the design and implementation of a low-cost microcontroller-based system for managing patient queues and preliminary health data collection in private medical chambers. Patient registration, queue management, and the collection of fundamental health metrics like heart rate and body temperature are all automated by the system. An ATmega32 microcontroller, an LM35 temperature sensor, an XD-58C pulse sensor, 4 × \times 4 matrix keypads, and 16 × \times 2 LCD displays are all integrated into the suggested system. The system separates patient-side input from doctor-side control, allowing doctors to call patients sequentially with a single button. The system reduces manual labor and contact-based data collection, making it feasible for small private practices in developing regions, according to experimental evaluation conducted under limited hardware conditions.



I
Introduction

Patient management in small private chambers is typically done by hand by an assistant who keeps track of patient information, keeps a queue, and sends each patient to the doctor individually. This manual method can be ineffective, time-consuming, and prone to errors, particularly when the patient’s basic health parameters (such as temperature and pulse) need to be taken before the doctor sees them.

Motivated by this, we suggest a microcontroller-based solution that enables patients to self-register using a keypad and built-in sensors, while the physician can use a button interface to call each patient in turn. The proposed approach focuses on low-cost implementations in developing-nation settings, where PC-based setups, Internet connectivity, and comprehensive hospital management systems might not be accessible.

The main contributions of this work are:

•

A full embedded design that integrates queue management, health data acquisition, and patient data entry on an ATmega32 MCU.

•

A dual-corner architecture: a doctor’s corner (display & control) and a patient booth (input & sensing).

•

Practical troubleshooting steps for UART/Bluetooth (HC-05) under internal oscillator constraints.

•

A low-cost path to later extend the system to wireless or mobile-based interfaces.

III-A

Patients’ Corner

The patient interface is implemented as a small self-service booth equipped with simple, low-cost input and sensing peripherals.

•

Two 4
\times

•

•

an XD-58C pulse sensor for heart rate measurement;

•

a 16
\times

The microcontroller assigns a unique serial number and adds the record to the internal queue once all necessary fields and sensor readings have been obtained.
Even non-technical users can operate the device without assistance because the entire workflow—from data entry to critical reading—is automated through sequential LCD prompts to reduce complexity.
By optimizing LCD control lines and using the same MCU I/O lines in matrix mode for keypad scanning, the design also reduces the amount of wiring and components.

IV
Hardware Implementation

IV-A

Microcontroller

The ATmega32 8-bit AVR microcontroller was selected because it provides:

•

built-in ADC channels for analog sensors (LM35);

•

UART for optional Bluetooth communication (HC-05);

•

sufficient I/O pins for keypad and LCD control;

•

support for internal 8 MHz oscillator, avoiding external crystals.

In order to lower UART baud-rate error, the internal oscillator was reconfigured during development from 1 MHz to 8 MHz via fuse-bit modification.

IV-B

Sensors

Firmware can easily convert the 10 mV/
∘
C output of the LM35A temperature sensor. Periodically, the XD-58C optical pulse sensor is read; heartbeats are identified by thresholding the sampled waveform, and the inter-beat interval is used to calculate beats per minute (BPM).

IV-D

Communication Module (Planned Feature)

In order to facilitate wireless communication between the doctor’s corner and the patient booth, an HC-05 Bluetooth module is being considered for future integration.
This improvement would increase the physical distance between the doctor’s display and the patient input unit and do away with the need for wired connections.
Although direct wiring is used for all communication in the current prototype, the firmware and UART configuration were made to be compatible with upcoming Bluetooth-based upgrades.

V
Software and Firmware Implementation

V-A

Queue Management

A simple circular buffer was implemented in C to hold patient records. Each record contains:

•

patient ID / serial number;

•

basic demographics (age, encoded name or short code, mobile number);

•

temperature reading;

•

pulse/BPM reading.

Following successful data entry and sensor acquisition, the enqueue operation is initiated. The doctor’s button initiates the dequeue process.

V-B

Sensor Acquisition

An ADC channel is linked to the LM35. The MCU uses the sensor scale to convert the ADC value to degrees Celsius after periodically sampling the sensor and averaging samples to reduce noise. BPM is calculated as follows:

\text{BPM}=\frac{60\times 1000}{T_{\text{beat}}}

where
T_{\text{beat}}
is the time in milliseconds between two consecutive detected peaks. The pulse sensor is sampled at predetermined intervals; peaks above a threshold are considered beats.

V-C

LCD Interface

Step-by-step instructions (“Enter Age’, “Place Finger on Sensor’, etc.) are displayed on the patient’s LCD, followed by the assigned serial. The dequeued patient’s data is displayed on the doctor’s LCD.

V-D

Programming and Configuration

USBasp was used to upload the firmware, which was written in embedded C (AVR-GCC/AVR Studio). To enable more precise UART, fuse bits were changed to choose the internal 8 MHz oscillator.

VI
System Calibration and Debugging

VI-A

Baud-Rate Error Mitigation

The UART baud generator generated an error that was too high for reliable HC-05 AT-mode communication at 1 MHz, especially at 38400 bps. Reliability was significantly increased by using the internal 8 MHz oscillator and double-speed UART.

Even under continuous enqueue–dequeue cycles, the queue management module—which was implemented as a circular buffer—proved to be resilient. The ATmega32’s available SRAM was the only constraint on the system’s ability to maintain correct order without data corruption for more than fifty simulated patients. Since all of the data is kept in volatile memory, a power outage clears the queue; this will be handled by EEPROM or SD-card logging in subsequent iterations.

From a usability standpoint, patients were successfully guided through the registration process by the patient’s step-by-step LCD prompts. Ten volunteers of various ages participated in field trials, which demonstrated the interface’s accessibility by demonstrating that participants could finish data entry and vital measurement without help after just one demonstration.

Overall, the experimental assessment confirms that the suggested embedded architecture satisfies its design objectives of minimal hardware dependency, low cost, and offline operation. The measured results show that an 8-bit microcontroller platform can fully accomplish accurate basic health data acquisition and dependable queue control. This makes it especially appropriate for small private chambers, remote clinics, or mobile medical units with limited computing resources or network connectivity.

VIII
Limitations and Future Work

The queue is currently stored in volatile memory; all entries would be erased in the event of a power outage.
For persistence, future iterations may include EEPROM or SD-card logging.
In order to facilitate mobile-based waiting list notifications and to physically divide patient and doctor corners, wireless connectivity via Bluetooth (HC-05) or Wi-Fi modules is planned.
The same embedded architecture can also be used to implement a multi-queue or multi-room variation.

IX
Conclusion

A useful and inexpensive microcontroller-based system for automating patient registration and queue management in a doctor’s private room was presented in this paper. The system operates completely independently of PCs and Internet connectivity by combining keypad-based data entry, temperature and pulse sensing, and embedded queue logic on an ATmega32 MCU. The experimental evaluation demonstrated dependable performance with sub-second response times from input to display, temperature accuracy within
\pm 1^{\circ}
C, and pulse accuracy within
\pm 3
BPM.

The suggested design preserves usability for non-technical users while successfully reducing manual labor and enforcing a strict first-come, first-served policy. Future upgrades like cloud-based synchronization, wireless Bluetooth communication, and persistent storage are made simple by its modular design. As a result, the system offers a useful basis for creating affordable, scalable, and Internet of Things-ready healthcare automation solutions appropriate for settings with limited resources.
