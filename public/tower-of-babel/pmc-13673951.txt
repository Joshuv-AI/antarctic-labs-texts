Optimizing Clinical Temporal Relation Extraction With Large Language Models: Comparative Analysis
Authors: Jianping He, Zenan Sun, Laila Rasmy, Haifang Li, Jianfu Li, Evan Yu, Degui Zhi, Cui Tao, Arriel Benis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundClinical temporal relation extraction (CTRE) is essential for reconstructing patient timelines from unstructured electronic health records (EHRs). However, the linguistic complexity of clinical notes and the high cost of expert annotation impede the development of large-scale training corpora. While large language models (LLMs) have transformed general natural language processing, their application to CTRE remains underexplored.ObjectiveThis study aimed to determine the optimal adaptation strategy for CTRE by conducting a controlled comparative benchmarking of LLM architectures and fine-tuning methodologies in both data-rich and limited-data regimes.MethodsWe evaluated 4 LLMs representing 2 distinct architectures—transformer encoders (GatorTron [University of Florida and NVIDIA]) and transformer decoders (LLaMA 3.1-8B [Meta AI] and MeLLaMA-13B [Yale University and University of Florida]). We compared four adaptation strategies: (1) standard fine-tuning, (2) hard-prompting, (3) soft-prompting, and (4) low-rank adaptation (LoRA). Experiments were conducted on the 2012 i2b2 CTRE benchmark in both full-supervision and 1-shot scenarios.ResultsRecent LLMs combined with appropriate adaptation strategies achieved strong performance for CTRE on the 2012 i2b2 benchmark. In the data-rich setting, the best-performing configuration was LLaMA3.1-8B with quantized low-rank adaptation (QLoRA) applied to the query and value projection layers, achieving a micro-F1-score of 89.15% (0.30). Comparative analyses showed that QLoRA applied only to the query and value projection layers achieved performance comparable to QLoRA applied to all linear layers, while requiring fewer trainable parameters. Hard-prompting showed particular effectiveness in the 1-shot scenario compared with standard fine-tuning for GatorTron-Base. Overall, Decoder-based models generally outperformed encoder-based models under QLoRA-based adaptation. However, under the most resource-constrained setting, where both training data and trainable parameters were highly limited, encoder-based models achieved better performance than decoder-based models.ConclusionsThis study provides a controlled benchmark for adapting LLMs to CTRE under different data and parameter constraints. Our findings suggest that QLoRA targeting only the query and value projection layers is a computationally efficient and competitive adaptation strategy. Decoder-based LLMs generally achieved stronger performance under QLoRA-based adaptation, whereas domain-specific encoder-based models showed advantages under the most resource-constrained soft-prompting setting. These results highlight the importance of selecting adaptation strategies according to both model architecture and available computational resources.

Introduction

Background and Significance

In recent years, temporal relation extraction (TRE) has attracted growing interest in clinical research due to its crucial role in understanding disease progression, drug efficacy, and patient outcomes [1]. Clinical temporal relation extraction (CTRE) aims to extract the temporal information about the patient’s past, current, and future status or conditions that are unavailable in structured electronic health records (EHRs) data [2,3]. This process is essential for reconstructing the chronological understanding of the events within clinical documents [1,4]. Accurate recognition and extraction of temporal relations between clinical events are crucial in improving diagnostic accuracy and informing treatment strategies [4].

However, extracting this information is uniquely challenging. Clinical notes have flexible formatting [5,6]. Therefore, medical providers prefer to use atypical grammatical constructions, including a lot of abbreviations and acronyms in clinical notes [7,8], which makes the annotation very troublesome in CTRE [9]. The CTRE annotation has a lower interannotator agreement (IAA) than other clinical annotation tasks, including clinical events and temporal mentions annotation tasks [3]. Consequently, constructing high-quality, large-scale datasets for supervision is difficult, creating an urgent need for systems that can achieve robust performance even in few-shot learning environments.

Evolution of Methods

Historically, the methodologies adopted in CTRE include rule-based methods, machine learning-based methods, deep learning-based methods, and hybrid methods [1]. The early rule-based systems for CTRE primarily relied on hand-crafted linguistic rules and domain-specific knowledge [10,11]. The machine learning methods used in CTRE consist of conditional random fields (CRF) [12,13], support vector machines (SVM) [14], random forests (RF) [15], and logistic regression (LR) [16]. Deep learning-based methods consist of recurrent neural networks (RNN) [17], convolutional neural networks (CNN) [18], long short-term memory (LSTM) [19], and BERT variants [20]. Deep learning systems or hybrid systems tend to outperform other methods [1]. Advances have been predominantly driven by Transformer-based architectures, particularly BERT variants, which excel at modeling long-range dependencies [20-24]. Despite these methodological improvements, the current state-of-the-art (SOTA) CTRE performance remains suboptimal and poses limitations for downstream clinical applications [1].

Furthermore, large language models (LLMs) have demonstrated exceptional efficacy across a diverse spectrum of natural language processing (NLP) tasks, driven by their advanced semantic understanding and generative capabilities [25-27]. In the biomedical domain, models such as GatorTron (University of Florida and NVIDIA), LLaMA 3.1 (Meta AI), and MeLLaMA (Yale University and University of Florida) have achieved notable advancements in information extraction, excelling at the identification, classification, and normalization of clinical entities within unstructured health care data [26,28,29]. However, despite their proven utility in general clinical NLP, to the best of our knowledge, the application of these advanced architectures to the specific complexities of the 2012 i2b2 CTRE task remains underexplored.

The Research Gap

While recent studies have begun to apply LLMs to CTRE, significant gaps remain. For instance, Peng et al [28] explored prompt tuning for GatorTron but did not evaluate parameter efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) methods like low-rank adaptation (LoRA) or benchmark performance on the i2b2 2012 dataset. Similarly, while decoder-based models have been tested in recent shared tasks [30,31], these studies often rely on zero-shot evaluations or fail to investigate the newest SOTA models, such as LLaMA 3.1. To our knowledge, no study has yet comprehensively compared the performance of medical-specific encoders (eg, GatorTron) against general and medical decoders (eg, LLaMA 3.1 and MeLLaMA) using a unified set of fine-tuning strategies.

The Proposed Study

To bridge these gaps, this study systematically benchmarks fine-tuning strategies for adapting LLMs to CTRE, evaluating performance across both data-rich (full supervision) and limited-data (few-shot) regimes on the 2012 i2b2 CTRE task [32]. Acknowledging the prohibitive memory costs associated with full-parameter optimization [33], we contrast standard fine-tuning and hard-prompting [34] with resource-efficient PEFT alternatives, specifically soft-prompting [35] and LoRA [36].

We benchmark following 4 distinct LLMs: GatorTron-Base (encoder-based, medical), GatorTron-Large [37] (encoder-based, medical), LLaMA 3.1 [38,39] (decoder-based, general), and MeLLaMA [26] (decoder-based, medical). By systematically applying standard fine-tuning, hard-prompting, soft-prompting, and LoRA, we aim to provide a roadmap for selecting the optimal architecture and tuning strategy for CTRE.

Methods

Dataset

In this study, we used the 2012 i2b2 Challenge dataset on temporal relations in clinical narratives [32] to assess the performance of our proposed fine-tuning strategies. Table 1 provides a summary of the i2b2 temporal relation corpus. The dataset comprises 310 anonymized patient discharge summaries, containing over 178,000 tokens, annotated for clinically relevant events (EVENT), temporal expressions (TIMEX3), and their relationships. Each discharge summary, on average, contains 86.6 annotated events, 12.4 temporal expressions, and 176 temporal relations.

The corpus categorizes 8 types of temporal relationships among events and temporal markers: BEFORE, AFTER, SIMULTANEOUS, OVERLAP, BEGUN_BY, ENDED_BY, DURING, and BEFORE_OVERLAP. Due to low IAA on certain temporal relationships, the challenge organizers established a consolidated taxonomy for evaluation purposes. In this official schema, the 8 relations were merged into three broad categories: (1) BEFORE (encompassing BEFORE, ENDED_BY, and BEFORE_OVERLAP); (2) AFTER (encompassing AFTER and BEGUN_BY); and (3) OVERLAP (encompassing SIMULTANEOUS, OVERLAP, and DURING). To ensure comparability with previous benchmarks, our study evaluates model performance exclusively on these 3 consolidated classes.

CTRE can be classified into 2 types; one is DocTimeRel, which refers to the temporal relations between the document creation time (DCT) and the events; the other one is temporal link (TLINK), which refers to the relations between the mentions (clinical events or temporal expressions) over text [1]. Among all temporal relations in the 2012 i2b2 CTRE test set, 48.01% (13,317/27,736) are EVENT-section time temporal relationships. The remaining ones are nonsection time TLINKs. Among all temporal relationships, 42.37% (11,752/27,736) are EVENT-EVENT temporal relationships, 7.34% (2035/27,736) are EVENT-TIMEX3 temporal relationships, 0.77% (214/27,736) are TIMEX3-TIMEX3 temporal relationships, 1.51% (418/27,736) are TIMEX3-EVENT temporal relationships [40]. Within the category of nonsection time TLINKs, OVERLAP relations constitute 63.48% (9153/14,419), BEFORE relations make up 21.93% (3162/14,419), and AFTER relations represent 14.59% (2104/14,419) of the total nonsection time TLINKs [40].

Data Preprocessing

The original training set was further split into training and validation subsets at a ratio of 9:1, while the original test set was retained as the final evaluation set.

We adopted the same preprocessing methodology as outlined in the previous work by Chen et al [21]. The process begins by segmenting the discharge summaries into individual sentences. Then, for each temporal relation instance, we constructed a localized contextual input based on the positions of the 2 relation arguments. Specifically, we identified the sentence containing the subject argument and the sentence containing the object argument. If both arguments appeared in the same sentence, that sentence was used as the input context. If the 2 arguments appeared in different sentences, the complete sentence span between the 2 argument-containing sentences was retained as the input context. No fixed maximum number of sentences was imposed; instead, the context window was determined by the minimal contiguous sentence span containing both arguments. This strategy preserved the local discourse necessary for both intrasentence and intersentence temporal relation classification while reducing unrelated document-level information.

To explicitly indicate the target argument pair, the subject and object mentions were enclosed with special markers, [@Subject$]...[@Subject$] and [@Object$]...[@Object$], respectively. These markers were retained in all input formats used for each fine-tuning strategy, including standard fine-tuning, hard-prompting, soft-prompting, and quantized low-rank adaptation (QLoRA), so that the target argument pair was explicitly indicated to the model. For the hard-prompting strategy, the prompt template used in this study was “Subject [MASK] [MASK] Object.” The marked contextual input was combined with this prompt template, where the subject and object markers explicitly indicated which entity mentions corresponded to the subject and object positions in the template.

For encoder-based models, the maximum sequence length was set to 512 tokens, whereas for decoder-based models, it was set to 4096 tokens. The final model input was tokenized and truncated to the corresponding maximum length if it exceeded the model-specific limit.

LLMs Used

This study uses 4 LLMs—GatorTron-Base (345M), GatorTron-Large (8.9B) [37], LLaMA3.1-8B [38,39], and MeLLaMA-13B [26].

GatorTron

GatorTron adopts a BERT-style architecture and was trained from scratch using 2 self-supervised tasks: masked language modeling (MLM) and sentence-order prediction (SOP). GatorTron was pretrained on over 90 billion words of text, including more than 82 billion words of deidentified clinical text. The GatorTron models were evaluated on 5 clinical NLP tasks spanning various linguistic levels—phrase, sentence, and document—using 6 publicly available benchmark datasets. Experimental results indicate that GatorTron outperformed existing biomedical and clinical transformers across all 5 tasks and 6 benchmark datasets. The model is available in three variants: GatorTron-Base (345M), GatorTron-Medium (3.9B), and GatorTron-Large (8.9B) [37].

LLaMA3.1

LLaMA3.1 was released by Meta AI in 2024. The pretraining process of LLaMA3.1 used more than 15 trillion tokens, all sourced from publicly available datasets [38,39]. One of the model’s most significant enhancements is the use of a tokenizer with a 128,256 token vocabulary, which enables more efficient language encoding. Additionally, LLaMA3.1 uses grouped query attention (GQA) to further optimize performance. LLaMA3.1 models, specifically the 8B, 70B, 405B, demonstrated superior performance across a wide range of tasks compared with other models of similar parameter scales. Notably, the LLaMA3.1-8B outperformed models of similar sizes, such as “Gemma2-9B” and “Mistral-7B.” The model was pretrained on sequences of 8192 tokens with a masking mechanism to ensure that self-attention does not cross document boundaries.

MeLLaMA

Xie et al [26] developed the MeLLaMA base models by continually pretraining LLaMA2 models using a mixed pretraining dataset comprising 129 billion tokens. The MeLLaMA-chat models were subsequently created by fine-tuning the MeLLaMA base models with an instruction tuning dataset containing 214,000 samples. Furthermore, leveraging a new medical evaluation benchmark (MIBE), which encompasses 6 critical medical tasks and 12 datasets, the authors fine-tuned the MeLLaMA base models with task-specific training sets from various evaluation datasets to assess their performance in a supervised learning setting. They also evaluated the Me-LLaMA-chat models in zero- and few-shot settings. MeLLaMA represents one of the most comprehensive open-source medical foundation LLMs, leveraging both biomedical and clinical data. Its evaluation demonstrates superior performance across a range of general and medical tasks when compared with other open-source medical LLMs, making it a highly promising option for applications in medical AI. MeLLaMA consists of 4 versions: MeLLaMA-13B, MeLLaMA-70B, MeLLaMA-13B-chat, and MeLLaMA-70B-chat.

To investigate the optimization of LLMs in resource-constrained scenarios—characterized by data scarcity and limited computational capacity—we focused on models with lower parameter counts, specifically, LLaMA3.1-8B and MeLLaMA-13B. To ensure a fair comparison across architectures of similar scale, GatorTron-Large (8.9B) was selected to represent the medical encoder-based architecture. Additionally, to thoroughly assess full-parameter-fine-tuning (FPFT) strategies, the smaller GatorTron-Base (345M) was included. Consequently, our comparative evaluation encompasses 4 models: GatorTron-Base (345M), GatorTron-Large (8.9B), LLaMA3.1-8B, and MeLLaMA-13B [26,37-39].

Table 2 illustrates the key architectural distinctions of various LLMs. GatorTron uses a transformer encoder-only design, processing raw text by tokenizing it into IDs using a 50,176-token vocabulary. These IDs pass through an embedding layer with a maximum sequence length of 512, producing embeddings with a 1024 feature dimension in the Base version and 3584 in the Large version. These embeddings then go through 24 (Base) or 56 (Large) transformer encoder layers, which generate contextualized representations by capturing intertoken relationships. Fine-Tuning Strategies for LLMs

Fine-Tuning Overview

Fine-tuning strategies include standard fine-tuning, hard-prompting, and PEFT. Standard fine-tuning is a supervised learning approach where the entire set of pretrained model parameters is refined. Hard-prompting appends fixed textual prompts to the input and reformulates the downstream task as a MLM problem, aligning it with the format of the pretraining task to improve the performance of language models [34]. Hard-prompting in transformer encoder-based models has demonstrated strong performance in few-shot scenarios across various NLP tasks [41-45]. Based on these findings, we hypothesize that applying this strategy to the CTRE task could significantly enhance performance, particularly in data-scarce settings. Both standard fine-tuning and hard-prompting necessitate full-parameter optimization, which results in prohibitive memory costs [33]. PEFT, also referred to as parameter efficient transfer learning or delta tuning, focuses on training only a small subset of parameters, either selected from the existing model or newly introduced [46-49]. This approach significantly reduces memory and storage demands since only the modified parameters are updated and saved. PEFT uses various techniques, including (1) adapter training integrating a bottleneck feed-forward network within each transformer block to enhance efficiency [50]; (2) LoRA identifying a low-rank delta within specific parameter matrices to optimize training [36]; and (3) soft-prompting appending a trainable matrix, known as a soft-prompt, to the input embeddings [35]. While soft-prompting may not be the most performant PEFT method, it is highly regarded for its exceptional parameter efficiency.

The fine-tuning strategies used in this study include (1) standard fine-tuning, (2) hard-prompting, (3) soft-prompting, and (4) LoRA. Additionally, quantized loading techniques are used to enhance computational efficiency. Standard fine-tuning and hard-prompting are full parameter fine-tuning strategies (Figure 1), whereas soft-prompting and LoRA fall under PEFT strategies (Figure 2).

Standard Fine-Tuning

Standard fine-tuning is the process of updating all parameters of a pretrained model on a specific task using labeled data, optimizing it for task-specific performance while retaining pretrained knowledge (Figure 1A). For encoder-based models, the original input is first processed by a tokenizer and then passed through the transformer embedding layer, producing initial token embeddings. These embeddings are static in the sense that they do not yet incorporate context. After passing through the transformer encoder layers, the model generates contextualized embeddings, where each token representation is influenced by its surrounding context. The hidden state of the [CLS] token is used as the sequence-level representation, and a classification layer is applied to this representation to predict the temporal relation class.

For decoder-based models, the input was formatted with a task-specific prompt to provide explicit instructions for temporal relation classification (Figure 3). The original input is structured as: “System Message + ‘Text: ’+ Original Input + ‘The temporal relationship between the subject and object is? <Answer>’.” The system message provides detailed instructions to guide the model’s response. For these models, the final hidden state of the last token was used as the sequence-level representation, and a classification layer was applied to this representation to predict the temporal relation class. Although the input was prompt-formatted, the decoder-based models were not trained as generative classifiers. Constrained decoding or free-text generation was not used to produce label tokens.

The prompt engineering for the transformer decoder-based models.

For both encoder-based and decoder-based models, the classification head produced logits over the three temporal relation classes, which were encoded as integer labels using the mapping BEFORE=0, AFTER=1, and OVERLAP=2. The models were trained using cross-entropy loss, and the predicted class was determined by the class with the highest softmax probability.

In standard fine-tuning, the model remains unfrozen, allowing all layers to be updated throughout the entire fine-tuning process. In this study, standard fine-tuning was applied exclusively to GatorTron-Base, as the other LLMs used—GatorTron-Large, LLaMA3.1-8B, and MeLLaMA-13B—were too large to fine-tune without freezing internal model parameters.

Hard-Prompting

Hard-prompting uses a structured prompt template, formatted as “Subject [MASK] [MASK] Object,” appended to the original input text (Figure 1B). By integrating this template, the CTRE task is reformulated into a MLM problem. The model is provided with predefined label words corresponding to each temporal relation class, such as “BEFORE” (labeled as “happened before”), “OVERLAP” (labeled as “happened overlap”), and “AFTER” (labeled as “happened after”). Rather than directly predicting the temporal relation, the model is used to predict the most probable label words to fill the [MASK] positions. The final predicted label words are then mapped to the corresponding temporal relation classification.

Specifically, the contextual vector is first derived by processing the original input text along with the appended hard-prompt template. This process yields the contextual representation of the [MASK] positions by selecting the corresponding slice from the overall contextual vector. Simultaneously, embedding vectors are derived for the predefined label words. The final classification is determined through a dot-product operation between the contextual vectors of the masked tokens and the embeddings of the predefined label words. This operation generates logits for each label word, allowing for the selection of the label word with the highest probability. The selected label word is then mapped to the corresponding temporal relation. Hard-prompting was applied exclusively to encoder-based models, as its underlying principle involves reformulating downstream tasks to align with the pretraining task format of encoder-based architectures, thereby enhancing the performance of pretrained language models (PLMs). Our previous study [51-53] demonstrated that reformatting downstream tasks to align with a model’s pretraining format significantly enhances performance. Additionally, for hard-prompting, we also kept the LLM weights unfrozen, allowing the model’s parameters to be adjusted during training to optimize task performance. Therefore, hard-prompting was implemented only for GatorTron-Base, as GatorTron-Large was too large to fine-tune without freezing internal model parameters.

Soft-Prompting

Soft-prompting is a technique that introduces learnable prompt embeddings, which are inserted into the input representation and processed alongside the original input by the frozen transformer encoder layers [54] (Figure 2A). This method facilitates task-specific adaptation without altering the pretrained model parameters, thereby substantially reducing computational overhead, as only the prompt embeddings are updated during fine-tuning.

LoRA

LoRA is a technique that freezes the pretrained model weights while introducing trainable rank decomposition matrices into each layer of the transformer architecture, significantly reducing the number of trainable parameters required for downstream tasks (Figure 2B). For instance, compared with fine-tuning GPT-3 175B using the Adam optimizer, LoRA can decrease the number of trainable parameters by 1000 times and reduce GPU memory requirements by threefold. Despite having fewer trainable parameters, LoRA achieves comparable or superior performance to standard fine-tuning [36]. In this study, we investigated the LoRA method to reduce trainable parameters, enabling the model to adapt to the CTRE task with minimal alteration to the PLMs.

Experimental Settings

This study examines fine-tuning approaches under computational resource constraints. Given that GatorTron-Base has 345M parameters, full parameter fine-tuning was feasible and applied to this model. However, for GatorTron-Large (8.9B parameters), LLaMA3.1-8B (8B parameters), and MeLLaMA-13B (13B parameters), full parameter fine-tuning was impractical due to their large parameter sizes and the limitations of available computational resources. Consequently, only PEFT strategies were used for these models. As summarized in Table 3, GatorTron-Base was fine-tuned using the following 4 strategies: (1) standard fine-tuning, (2) hard-prompting, (3) soft-prompting, and (4) LoRA. In contrast, GatorTron-Large, LLaMA3.1-8B, and MeLLaMA-13B were fine-tuned using only soft-prompting and LoRA to ensure computational efficiency while adapting the models effectively.

LoRA: low-rank adaptation.

In this paper, we used 4-bit floating-point (NF4) quantization to load LLMs for reducing memory and computational demands by representing model weights and activations with lower-precision data types, enabling efficient deployment of larger models and faster inference [55]. In this study, quantization was used together with PEFT strategies, including soft-prompting and LoRA, whereas full-parameter fine-tuning strategies, including standard fine-tuning and hard-prompting, were conducted without quantization. The LoRA strategy with quantization is referred to as QLoRA. In QLoRA, LoRA weights are incorporated into specific layers of the quantized model [56].

For soft-prompting with LLMs, we built upon insights from previous studies that demonstrated optimal performance for soft-prompt mechanisms with token lengths of 32 and 64 [28]. Accordingly, we evaluated soft-prompts of these 2 lengths in our experiments. Additionally, we applied LoRA in 2 distinct configurations: one targeting all linear layers and another restricted specifically to the query and value layers within the attention mechanism. For transformer decoder-based models, the “all linear layers” configuration targets the q_proj, k_proj, v_proj, o_proj, gate_proj, up_proj, and down_proj modules. In the restricted configuration, LoRA is applied solely to the q_proj and v_proj modules. For transformer encoder-based models, the “all linear layers” configuration encompasses the query, key, value, and dense modules, whereas the restricted configuration targets only the query and value modules. For QLoRA, we used the same core LoRA hyperparameters for both the QLoRA (qv) and QLoRA (all linear layers) configurations to ensure a controlled comparison. Specifically, the LoRA rank was set to 16, the LoRA α was set to 32, and the LoRA dropout rate was set to 0.1.

To ensure the validity of model comparisons, identical hardware configurations were used across all experimental settings. Specifically, all experiments were conducted using H100 GPUs.

In this study, we examine the performance of models for 1-shot learning settings in addition to a data-rich setting. In the K-shot scenarios, “K” instances were selected from each relation type in the original complete training dataset to construct the corresponding few-shot training set. Specifically, in the 1-shot scenario, one instance was sampled from each relation type and used as the training data. For validation, we used the same validation dataset as that in the data-rich setting. This design provided a sufficiently large validation set for model selection. To ensure a consistent benchmark, the evaluation was conducted on the original, unmodified test set, identical to that used in the standard fully supervised scenario.

In the data-rich setting, models were trained for up to 10 epochs with an early stopping patience of 3 epochs; if convergence was not achieved, training was extended to 20 epochs with the same early stopping patience. In the few-shot setting, training was initially performed for 20 epochs with an early stopping patience of 3 epochs, and if the model did not converge, training continued for up to 30 epochs. The learning rate was set according to model architecture. Encoder-based models were trained with a learning rate of 1e-4, whereas decoder-based models were trained with a learning rate of 2e-4. These architecture-specific learning rates were selected to support stable optimization across different backbone architectures.

For the data-rich regime, batch size and gradient accumulation steps were adjusted according to model architecture, adaptation strategy, and GPU memory requirements. Decoder-based QLoRA, decoder-based soft-prompting, and encoder-based standard fine-tuning used a training batch size of 4 with gradient accumulation steps of 4. Encoder-based QLoRA used a training batch size of 16 with gradient accumulation steps of 2. Encoder-based hard-prompting and encoder-based soft-prompting used a training batch size of 16 with gradient accumulation steps of 4. The settings were selected based on GPU memory requirements and training feasibility. Decoder-based models and full standard fine-tuning required substantially more GPU memory, so smaller per-device batch sizes were necessary. In contrast, encoder-based PEFT configurations were less memory intensive and allowed larger batch sizes. These settings were chosen to ensure feasible and stable training under GPU memory constraints. Because memory consumption differed substantially across architectures and adaptation strategies, identical batch settings were not always feasible. Therefore, we transparently report these settings and interpret cross-model comparisons cautiously, focusing on matched adaptation strategies, comparable trainable-parameter groups, and repeated-run mean (SD) performance. In addition, the approximate training time for each experiment has been provided in Multimedia Appendix 1.

To improve the robustness of the experimental results, each experimental setting was repeated using 3 random seeds: 1234, 42, and 47. For each setting, we reported the mean and SD of micro-F1, macro-F1, and weighted-F1 across the three runs. Micro-F1 was used as the primary evaluation metric, as it reflects the overall classification performance across all instances and provides an aggregate measure of model accuracy across relation types. Given that the dataset was imbalanced across relation classes, macro-F1 and weighted-F1 were also reported as supplementary metrics. Macro-F1 assigns equal importance to each class and is therefore useful for assessing model performance on minority classes, whereas weighted-F1 accounts for class support and reflects performance adjusted by the distribution of relation types. In addition, class-wise precision, recall, and F1-scores were calculated, and error analysis was conducted to further examine model behavior across relation types.

External Validation

This study was designed as a comparative analysis of multiple language models; we saved the best-performing fine-tuned checkpoint for each model under the data-rich regime on the 2012 i2b2 CTRE dataset and applied these checkpoints to the MACCROBAT [57,58] subset for external validation. This setting allowed us to examine whether the relative performance patterns observed on the i2b2 benchmark were maintained on an external dataset with a different source and annotation context.

MACCROBAT consists of 200 clinical case reports from PubMed Central and contains 19,089 annotated clinical events. Although the dataset was originally developed for broader clinical information extraction tasks, it also includes temporal relations annotated with three relation categories: BEFORE, AFTER, and OVERLAP. For external validation, we extracted a balanced subset of 1000 temporal relation instances from MACCROBAT, including 333 BEFORE instances, 334 AFTER instances, and 333 OVERLAP instances.

Ethical Considerations

This study used 2 previously released, deidentified benchmark datasets—the 2012 i2b2 temporal relations challenge corpus and MACCROBAT. The i2b2 dataset was accessed and used in accordance with its Data Use Agreement, and MACCROBAT is based on publicly available clinical case reports from PubMed Central. No new human participant data were collected, and no direct patient identifiers were accessed. Therefore, additional institutional review board approval and informed consent were not required.

The effect of prompt length was not consistent across models. For GatorTron-Base, increasing the prompt length from 32 to 64 improved micro-F1 from 71.07% (0.15) to 72.88% (0.60). Overall, soft-prompting achieved lower performance than the other fine-tuning strategies evaluated in this study, although it required substantially fewer trainable parameters. These results suggest that soft-prompting was less effective for CTRE in the current experimental setting, particularly for decoder-based models.

To further contextualize the results in the data-rich regime, we compared our findings with previously reported performance on the 2012 i2b2 CTRE benchmark. Knez and Žitnik [23] evaluated their model using the predefined test portion of the benchmark dataset, which is consistent with the evaluation protocol adopted in our study. Under this comparable setting, their highest reported micro-F1-score was 82.04%. In comparison, several configurations in our study achieved higher micro-F1-scores. The best-performing configuration was LLaMA3.1-8B with QLoRA (qv), which achieved 89.15% (0.30), followed by MeLLaMA-13B with QLoRA (all) at 88.94% (0.28). LLaMA3.1-8B with QLoRA (all), MeLLaMA-13B with QLoRA (qv), and GatorTron-Large with QLoRA (qv) also achieved strong results, with micro-F1-scores of 88.3% (1.18), 88.04% (2.15), and 87.33% (0.19), respectively. GatorTron-Base with standard fine-tuning and hard-prompting also performed strongly, achieving micro-F1-scores of 86.72% (0.19) and 86.1% (0.61), respectively.

Nevertheless, comparisons with previous studies should still be interpreted cautiously because evaluation settings are not always fully aligned across studies. For example, Uma et al [24] reported a micro-F1-score of 85.7%, but their study used a custom 80/10/10 data split and explicitly noted that their results were not directly comparable with earlier i2b2 studies because of differences in relation scope, data splitting, and evaluation settings. Although several configurations in our study achieved numerically higher micro-F1-scores than this previously reported result, these differences should be interpreted in light of the distinct experimental settings. Therefore, rather than claiming a definitive new SOTA result, we interpret our findings as providing strong contextualized evidence that recent LLMs combined with appropriate adaptation strategies can achieve competitive performance on the 2012 i2b2 CTRE benchmark.

Qualitative inspection of the misclassified cases suggested 2 major error characteristics. The dominant error pattern was the confusion between BEFORE and OVERLAP. Specifically, 904 BEFORE instances were incorrectly classified as OVERLAP, accounting for 30.75% (904/2940) of all misclassified cases, while 623 OVERLAP instances were incorrectly classified as BEFORE, accounting for 21.19% (623/2940) of all errors. These 2 directions together represented more than half (1527/2940, 51.94%) of all misclassifications (1527 of 2940 misclassified cases).

A representative example is “Workup on admission included [@Object$]a chest x-ray[@Object$] negative for [@Subject$]pneumonia[@Subject$], an MRI of the liver to investigate for focal lesion, with no evidence for abscess or focal lesion on report.” In this case, the gold label was BEFORE, but the model predicted OVERLAP. The diagnostic test and the clinical condition were semantically related and appeared in close textual proximity. The model may therefore have interpreted their clinical association as temporal overlap, even though the gold annotation indicated a sequential relation.

Additionally, the model showed greater difficulty in identifying AFTER relations. This is consistent with the per-class results, where AFTER had the lowest precision, recall, and F1-score among the 3 relation types. Among the true AFTER instances, 312 were predicted as BEFORE, and 528 were predicted as OVERLAP. A representative example is “She was [@Object$]discharged[@Object$] on [@Subject$]Coumadin[@Subject$] for a goal INR of 2.5 to 3,” where the gold label was AFTER, but the model predicted BEFORE. This example illustrates the difficulty of resolving temporal relations in compressed discharge statements. In clinical documentation, expressions such as “discharged on” may encode not only the discharge event itself but also an ongoing or postdischarge treatment plan. Therefore, correctly identifying the relation requires interpreting the implicit temporal scope of the clinical event, rather than relying only on the local word order of the sentence.

Overall, these findings suggest that the remaining errors were mainly concentrated in context-dependent temporal reasoning cases. In clinical narratives, annotated events may be semantically related and textually close, but their temporal relation may still depend on broader information from surrounding sentences, the hospital course, or the full discharge summary. Thus, although the best-performing QLoRA-based LLaMA3.1-8B model captured explicit temporal relations effectively, it was less robust when local textual evidence was insufficient. The main residual challenges involved identifying AFTER relations and distinguishing sequential relations from overlapping relations under limited local temporal cues.

Discussion

This study systematically evaluated the performance of different language models and adaptation strategies for CTRE. In the data-rich setting, multiple adaptation strategies achieved strong performance on the 2012 i2b2 CTRE benchmark. The best-performing configuration was LLaMA3.1-8B with QLoRA applied to the query and value projection layers, which achieved a micro-F1-score of 89.15% (0.30). In addition, other QLoRA-based configurations, standard fine-tuning, and hard-prompting also produced strong results. For example, MeLLaMA-13B with QLoRA (all) achieved 88.94% (0.28), while LLaMA3.1-8B with QLoRA (all) achieved 88.3% (1.18). Standard fine-tuning and hard-prompting on GatorTron-Base also achieved competitive micro-F1-scores of 86.86% (0.44) and 86.1% (0.61), respectively. Compared with previously reported results on the 2012 i2b2 CTRE dataset, these findings provide contextualized evidence that recent LLMs combined with appropriate adaptation strategies can achieve strong performance for CTRE. However, because previous studies differ in data splits, relation scope, and evaluation protocols, these results should not be interpreted as a definitive new SOTA claim. Instead, this study provides a controlled benchmark that demonstrates how different adaptation strategies and model architectures influence CTRE performance under varying data and parameter constraints.

The comparison between QLoRA (qv) and QLoRA (all) showed that increasing the number of trainable LoRA modules did not consistently improve performance. In the data-rich setting, QLoRA (qv) achieved the best overall result for LLaMA3.1-8B, whereas QLoRA (all) produced slightly higher micro-F1-scores for MeLLaMA-13B and GatorTron-Base. A similar pattern was observed in the 1-shot scenario and external validation, where QLoRA (qv) remained competitive with QLoRA (all). These results suggest that adapting only the query and value projection layers can provide a computationally efficient strategy without substantially compromising performance. This finding is practically important because QLoRA (qv) updates fewer trainable parameters and therefore reduces computational requirements compared with adapting all linear layers. Nevertheless, the relative advantage varied across models, indicating that the optimal QLoRA configuration is model-dependent rather than universal.

The comparison between decoder-based and encoder-based models further showed that architectural advantages depended on both the adaptation strategy and the resource setting. Under QLoRA-based adaptation, decoder-based models generally achieved stronger performance than encoder-based GatorTron models when the number of trainable parameters was broadly comparable. This trend was observed in both the data-rich and 1-shot settings, suggesting that recent decoder-based LLMs can be highly effective for CTRE when sufficient parameter-efficient adaptation is allowed. This finding contrasts with earlier assumptions that encoder-based models are generally preferable for relation extraction and suggests that the strong language modeling capacity of modern decoder-based LLMs can transfer effectively to clinical relation classification when adapted appropriately.

However, this advantage did not hold under the most parameter-efficient soft-prompting setting. When both the amount of training data and the number of trainable parameters were highly limited, encoder-based GatorTron models outperformed decoder-based LLaMA3.1-8B and MeLLaMA-13B. This pattern was especially evident in the 1-shot soft-prompting results, where GatorTron-Large achieved the strongest performance among the evaluated soft-prompting configurations. One possible explanation is that soft-prompting updates only a small number of continuous prompt parameters, making performance more dependent on the intrinsic representations learned during pretraining. Because GatorTron was pretrained on large-scale clinical corpora, its representations may be better aligned with the clinical language and temporal reasoning patterns in i2b2. In contrast, decoder-based models may require more extensive parameter adaptation to fully exploit their capacity for CTRE.

The comparison between standard fine-tuning and hard-prompting on GatorTron-Base also showed that the effectiveness of hard-prompting depends on the data regime. In the data-rich setting, standard fine-tuning and hard-prompting achieved broadly comparable performance on GatorTron-Base. In contrast, in the 1-shot scenario, hard-prompting achieved better performance than standard fine-tuning across micro-F1, macro-F1, and weighted-F1 on GatorTron-Base. This suggests that explicitly reformulating the task through a prompt template may be beneficial when very limited supervision is available. The advantage of hard-prompting in the 1-shot setting may be related to its closer alignment with the masked language modeling objective of encoder-based models. However, hard-prompting also has practical limitations, as its performance can be sensitive to prompt design and may require domain expertise to construct effective templates. Therefore, while hard-prompting may be useful in extremely low-resource scenarios, its generalizability and ease of implementation should be considered carefully.

Soft-prompting showed limited effectiveness for CTRE compared with other fine-tuning strategies evaluated in this study. Although soft-prompting required substantially fewer trainable parameters, its performance was considerably lower in the data-rich setting across the evaluated models. One possible explanation is that the limited representational capacity of short continuous prompts may create an information bottleneck for CTRE, which requires modeling complex temporal dependencies between clinical events. In the 1-shot setting, soft-prompting was more informative for comparing model architectures under strict parameter constraints, but its overall performance remained lower than strategies involving more extensive parameter updates. However, this interpretation should be considered in light of the specific soft-prompting configurations tested in this study. We evaluated prompt lengths of 32 and 64, and it is possible that longer prompt lengths, alternative prompt initialization strategies, or other prompt-based PEFT methods such as prefix tuning may yield different results. Therefore, our findings should not be interpreted as a general conclusion about all prompt-based PEFT approaches, but rather as evidence that the soft-prompting configurations evaluated here were less effective for high-precision CTRE than QLoRA-based adaptation or standard fine-tuning.

The external validation on MACCROBAT provided a preliminary assessment of cross-dataset transferability. As expected, the absolute performance was lower than that observed on the main 2012 i2b2 benchmark, likely reflecting differences in document source, annotation practice, relation distribution, and linguistic characteristics. Nevertheless, the external validation results partially preserved the main findings from the data-rich experiments. QLoRA (qv) remained competitive with QLoRA (all), supporting its role as an efficient and competitive adaptation strategy. In addition, LLaMA3.1-8B showed strong transfer performance under QLoRA-based adaptation, suggesting that decoder-based models can retain good cross-dataset generalizability when appropriately fine-tuned. At the same time, the performance drop highlights the difficulty of transferring CTRE models across datasets with different document types and annotation characteristics.

This study has several limitations. First, although the main experiments were conducted on the widely used 2012 i2b2 CTRE benchmark and supplemented with external validation on MACCROBAT, the evaluation still covered a limited range of CTRE datasets. Different datasets may vary in document type, event density, temporal expression patterns, and annotation guidelines, which may affect both absolute performance and model ranking. Second, this study used a 3-class relation setting consisting of BEFORE, AFTER, and OVERLAP to support controlled comparison and external validation after label harmonization. Other CTRE datasets may use more fine-grained temporal relation schemas, such as temporal containment, simultaneity, or boundary-specific relations. These finer distinctions may introduce additional ambiguity and may especially affect macro-F1 and minority-class performance. Third, the evaluated decoder-based models were limited to parameter-constrained variants. Larger models or more advanced instruction-tuned clinical LLMs may yield different performance patterns, particularly in low-resource settings. Future work should evaluate a broader range of CTRE datasets, relation schemas, and model scales to further assess the robustness and generalizability of these findings.

In summary, this study provides a controlled comparative benchmark of 4 LLMs and multiple adaptation strategies for CTRE. The results show that recent LLMs combined with appropriate adaptation strategies can achieve strong performance in the data-rich setting, with QLoRA (qv) offering a competitive and computationally efficient alternative to QLoRA (all). Decoder-based models generally performed better under QLoRA-based adaptation when sufficient trainable parameters were available, whereas encoder-based models showed advantages under the strictest soft-prompting constraints. Hard-prompting was particularly effective in the 1-shot setting compared with standard fine-tuning, but its advantage was not universal across settings. Overall, these findings highlight the importance of jointly considering model architecture, adaptation strategy, data availability, and computational constraints when designing CTRE systems for clinical narratives.
