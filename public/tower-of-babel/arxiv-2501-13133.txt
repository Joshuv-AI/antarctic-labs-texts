Graph Representation Learning with Diffusion Generative Models
Daniel Wesego

Abstract
Diffusion models have established themselves as state-of-the-art generative models across various data modalities, including images and videos, due to their ability to accurately approximate complex data distributions. Unlike traditional generative approaches such as VAEs and GANs, diffusion models employ a progressive denoising process that transforms noise into meaningful data over multiple iterative steps. This gradual approach enhances their expressiveness and generation quality. Not only that, diffusion models have also been shown to extract meaningful representations from data while learning to generate samples. Despite their success, the application of diffusion models to graph-structured data remains relatively unexplored, primarily due to the discrete nature of graphs, which necessitates discrete diffusion processes distinct from the continuous methods used in other domains. In this work, we leverage the representational capabilities of diffusion models to learn meaningful embeddings for graph data. By training a discrete diffusion model within an autoencoder framework, we enable both effective autoencoding and representation learning tailored to the unique characteristics of graph-structured data. We only need the encoder at the end to extract representations. Our approach demonstrates the potential of discrete diffusion models to be used for graph representation learning.

Introduction

Representation learning is a fundamental concept in machine learning, with the objective of learning useful and informative representations of data. The goal of representation learning is to transform raw data into a format that can be easily processed by machine learning models, often in the form of compressed low-dimensional embeddings or features
(
Bengio et al. 2013
)
. These representations are designed to capture the essential structure and patterns of the data, enabling downstream tasks such as classification, clustering, and generation. Representation learning has found applications in a wide range of domains, including natural language processing (NLP), computer vision, and graphs
(
Bengio 2009
;
Wesego and Rooshenas 2024b
;
Hinton and Salakhutdinov 2006
;
Hamilton et al. 2017
)
. In recent years, deep learning techniques, particularly neural networks, have significantly advanced the field of representation learning. These models are capable of learning highly expressive representations from raw data, often without the need for manual feature engineering. For example, in computer vision, a variational autoencoder (VAE) has been used to extract latent representations of images
(
Kingma and Welling 2014
;
Rezende et al. 2014
)
, while in NLP, transformer-based models like BERT learn contextual embeddings of text
(
Vaswani et al. 2017
)
. In the context of graph data, representation learning methods aim to learn embeddings that capture the structural and feature information of nodes, edges, or entire graphs, enabling the application of machine learning techniques to graph-structured data
(
Hamilton et al. 2017
)
.

Graphs are powerful structures that model relationships between entities, and they are widely used in various domains such as social networks, biological networks, transportation systems, and knowledge graphs
(
Hamilton et al. 2017
)
. In these domains, graphs represent complex systems, where nodes typically correspond to entities, and edges represent relationships or interactions between them. The ability to analyze and extract insights from graph-structured data is critical for applications such as recommendation systems, drug discovery, fraud detection, and social network analysis
(
Ying et al. 2018
;
Velickovic et al. 2018
)
. Graph representation learning has also become a very important component of modern graph machine learning, driven by the need to analyze and extract insights from graph-structured data. The primary goal of graph representation learning is to map graph-structured data into a low-dimensional vector embeddings that preserve the underlying structure and features.

Graph representation learning plays a crucial role in transforming graph data into low-dimensional vector embeddings, which preserve both local and global structural information, as well as node and edge features
(
Hamilton et al. 2017
)
. These embeddings facilitate efficient analysis and downstream tasks such as node classification, link prediction, and graph classification
Kipf and Welling 2017
. Despite the success of traditional graph representation learning methods, such as Graph Convolutional Networks (GCNs), several challenges remain
(
Zhang et al. 2019b
)
. Graphs often exhibit highly complex structures, including varying node degrees, long-range dependencies, and hierarchical relationships, which make it difficult to capture the full complexity of the graph in low-dimensional embeddings
(
Kipf and Welling 2017
)
. Additionally, graph data is often heterogeneous, with different types of nodes, edges, and features, requiring methods that can effectively handle this diversity in a unified framework
(
Zhang et al. 2019a
)
. Also the dynamic nature of many graphs, where new nodes and edges are continuously added or removed, introduces the challenge of learning representations that can capture temporal evolution and adapt to changes in the graph structure
(
Qin et al. 2023
)
.

On the other hand, recent advancements in deep generative models, particularly diffusion models, have opened new avenues for representation learning, offering a promising to learn meaningful representations
(
Preechakul et al. 2022
)
. This approach can be crucial to overcome the limitations of graph representation learning. Diffusion models,
Ho et al. 2020
, which have gained widespread attention for their remarkable ability to generate high-quality samples in domains like image synthesis and molecule generation, have recently been explored for graph data
(
Vignac et al. 2023
)
. Despite their potential, the application of diffusion models to graph representation learning remains an emerging area, with several challenges yet to be addressed. This study explores the use of discrete diffusion autoencoders for graph representation learning. Discrete diffusion models, which are designed to operate on discrete data, are particularly well-suited for graph data, where the features are often categorical or discrete in nature. By leveraging the generative capabilities of diffusion models, we aim to improve the quality of graph embeddings and enhance the expressiveness of learned representations. This approach also enables unsupervised learning of graph representations, eliminating the need for labeled data, which is often scarce and expensive to obtain in many real-world applications. We present a detailed examination of the discrete diffusion autoencoder framework and evaluate its effectiveness on a set of benchmark datasets, including the Protein dataset from TUDatasets
Morris et al. 2020
.

The application of diffusion models to graph representation learning is still in its early stages, but it holds significant potential
Yang et al. 2023
. Because of their ability to generate high-quality samples in various domains, they can offer several advantages for graph representation learning. The iterative denoising process in diffusion models enables the learning of hierarchical representations that capture intricate relationships within the graph structure, resulting in more informative embeddings. In addition, their generative capabilities allow them to generate new graph structures, which is valuable for applications such as drug discovery and molecular graph generation
Jin et al. 2018
. The main idea behind using these models for representation learning is that their strong generative capabilities suggest that they must have learned a valuable, compressed representation of the graph.

Diffusion Autoencoders transform the normal generative diffusion model into an autoencoder structure where an encoder learns a useful representation and the diffusion decoder generates data conditioned on the representation from the encoder
(
Preechakul et al. 2022
)
. Discrete diffusion autoencoders are a specialized class of diffusion models designed for discrete data, such as categorical features common in graph data
Austin et al. 2023
. This paper explores the use of discrete diffusion autoencoders for graph representation learning, with the following contributions:

•

We propose a discrete diffusion autoencoder learning setup for the structured graph data. This approach leverages the power of discrete diffusion models to progressively denoise graphs, capturing complex structural patterns and relationships within the graph.

•

We show how discrete diffusion models can improve the quality of graph representations by transforming the discrete nature of graph structures into a latent embedding. By integrating the generative capabilities of diffusion models with an autoencoder architecture, we enable the model to learn meaningful low-dimensional embeddings that effectively represent the inherent structure and features of the graph. Our model, which we call Discrete Diffusion Autoencoder (DDAE) shows promising result in downstream graph classification tasks.

2
Related Work

2.1
Diffusion Models

Diffusion models have emerged as powerful generative models, achieving a very good performance in various domains, including image synthesis, molecule generation, and representation learning
(
Ho et al. 2020
;
Vignac et al. 2023
;
Yang et al. 2023
;
Wesego and Rooshenas 2024a
)
. These models operate by progressively adding noise to data in the forward diffusion process and learning to reverse this noise. This iterative framework enables the generation of high-quality samples that closely resemble the data distribution. Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models (DDPM) introduced the foundational framework for diffusion-based generation, demonstrating their ability to produce realistic images through iterative denoising
(
Ho et al. 2020
)
. Improved DDPM further enhanced this framework by optimizing noise schedules, learning the variance, and architectural designs
(
Dhariwal and Nichol 2021
)
. Other notable advancements include Stable Diffusion, which integrates text conditions with diffusion models in the latent space to generate high-resolution images with that are guided by texts
(
Rombach et al. 2021
)
.

2.2
Discrete Diffusion Models

Discrete diffusion models extend the principles of diffusion to discrete data, making them suitable for domains like text, categorical data, and graph data. Unlike continuous diffusion models, which operate in a continuous state space, discrete diffusion models handle data in a manner that respects its inherent discrete nature
(
Austin et al. 2023
;
Hoogeboom et al. 2021
)
. For example, in natural language processing, discrete diffusion models can process sequences of tokens, preserving the integrity of the vocabulary during both the forward noise process and the reverse denoising process. Similarly, in graph generative models, discrete diffusion models can handle graphs with categorical node and edge attributes, ensuring that the generated or reconstructed data adheres to the original discrete graph structure.

2.3
Graph Diffusion Models

Graph diffusion models leverage the principles of diffusion processes to model graph-structured data. These models are particularly well-suited for capturing the complex relationships and hierarchical structures inherent in graphs. Since graphs has discrete data, most diffusion models fro graphs have focused on working on the discrete space using discrete diffusion models
(
Vignac et al. 2023
;
Chen et al. 2023
)
. DiGress, a discrete denoising diffusion model, focuses on generating graphs with categorical node and edge attributes, demonstrating its effectiveness in handling graph-structured data
(
Vignac et al. 2023
)
.
Wang et al. 2024
further expands the DiGress model by training it on a combination of different graphs from different domains to increase the generalization process. EDGE ,
Chen et al. 2023
, is another discrete diffusion model that generates the adjacency matrix of the graph structure conditioned on a degree. They also use absorbing distribution of empty graphs as their final distribution decreasing the diffusion time steps of their model because of the sparsity of graphs. These methods highlight the growing potential of discrete diffusion models for applications requiring structured data generation.

2.4
Graph Representation Learning

Graph representation learning has been an important area of research, focusing on transforming graph-structured data into low-dimensional embeddings that preserve structural and semantic information of the graph. There have been multiple previous approaches that are proposed to tackle the challenges associated with learning meaningful graph representations, ranging from contrastive learning techniques to generative and autoencoder-based methods. GraphCL (Graph Contrastive Learning) introduced a self-supervised contrastive learning framework that leverages data augmentations to maximize the agreement between representations of the same graph under different transformations
(
You et al. 2021
)
. GraphMAE (Graph Masked Autoencoder) adapts the masked autoencoding method, commonly used in natural language processing, to graphs. It involves masking portions of the graph and training the model to reconstruct the masked elements, thereby learning the inner structure of the data
(
Hou et al. 2022
)
. GraphVAE (Graph Variational Autoencoder) is a generative framework that models the probabilistic distribution of graph data. By learning latent variables that represent the graph structure and attributes, GraphVAE can learn to compress the graph into the latent space and reconstruct it again
(
Kipf and Welling 2016
)
.
Yang et al. 2023
used diffusion models for graph representation learning by extracting outputs from different parts of the model and adding directional noise.

In this study, we propose a Diffusion Autoencoder setup to learn and extract representations similar to
Preechakul et al. 2022
but adopted to the graph structured data. We use discrete diffusion models as the decoders over the adjacency matrix and a GCN encoder to extract the latent representation
z
. The model is trained is solely on discrete diffusion on the decoder where gradients propagate all the way to encoder.

Methodology

This section details the methodology of our discrete diffusion autoencoder model, which operates on graph-structured data. We begin by outlining the general discrete diffusion setup and then elaborate on its specific application within our autoencoder framework. Discrete diffusion models define a Markov chain of diffusion steps that gradually corrupt the input data into noise. This process is reversed by learning a generative model that denoises the data step-by-step, ultimately reconstructing the original input. A sequence of discrete random variables
\mathbf{x}_{0},\mathbf{x}_{1},...,\mathbf{x}_{T}
is defined in the diffusion process, where
\mathbf{x}_{0}
represents the original data and
\mathbf{x}_{T}
is pure noise. The transition probabilities
q(\mathbf{x}_{t}|\mathbf{x}_{t-1})
are defined such that the data distribution progressively approaches a tractable noise distribution. A model
p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t-1}|\mathbf{x}_{t})
is learned to reverse this diffusion process, starting from the noise distribution and iteratively refining the data until reaching the original data distribution.

Diffusion models maximize the ELBO of the data log-likelihood
(
Austin et al. 2023
)
where we define
p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{0}):=\int p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{0:T})d\mathbf{x}_{1:T}
as the probability of the original data, where
p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{0:T}):=p(\mathbf{x}_{T})\prod_{t=1}^{T}p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t-1}|\mathbf{x}_{t})
represents the joint probability of the entire diffusion process. The forward diffusion process is defined by the probability
q(\mathbf{x}_{1:T}|\mathbf{x}_{0}):=\prod_{t=1}^{T}q(\mathbf{x}_{t}|\mathbf{x}_{t-1})
. Maximizing the ELBO is equivalent to minimizing the following inequality:

Our approach utilizes the discrete diffusion framework within an autoencoder architecture to learn a latent representation of graph-structured data. The input data consists of a graph
\mathbf{G}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{A})
, where
\mathbf{X}
represents the node features and
\mathbf{A}
represents the adjacency matrix. An encoder network
E_{\phi}(\mathbf{G})
maps the input graph
\mathbf{G}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{A})
to a latent representation
\mathbf{z}
. This encoder can be any suitable graph neural network that effectively captures the graph’s structural and feature information but we used a GCN network. The decoder is a discrete diffusion model operating on the adjacency matrix
\mathbf{A}
. It is conditioned on the latent representation
\mathbf{z}
to generate a reconstruction of the original graph structure trained on equation
3
.

The input graph
\mathbf{G}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{A})
is passed through the encoder network
E_{\phi}(\mathbf{G})
to obtain the latent representation
\mathbf{z}
. The adjacency matrix
\mathbf{A}
undergoes a forward diffusion process, transforming it into a noisy matrix
\mathbf{A}_{t}
. This process is governed by a predefined sequence of transition probabilities that transforms the initial
\mathbf{A}
into a matrix of zeros. This means, all zeros is used as an absorbing distribution in our setup. The decoder, which is a conditional discrete diffusion model
p_{\theta}(\mathbf{A}_{t-1}|\mathbf{A}_{t},\mathbf{z})
, starts with the noisy matrix
\mathbf{A}_{T}
which in our case is a matrix of all zeros and iteratively refines it. At each step, it predicts the previous state
\mathbf{A}_{t-1}
based on the current noisy state
\mathbf{A}_{t}
and the latent representation
\mathbf{z}
. The final output of the reverse diffusion process is a reconstructed adjacency matrix
\mathbf{\hat{A}}
.

4
Experiments

This section outlines the experimental setup of our proposed model and the baselines. We compare our model against relevant baselines on a graph classification task using the PROTEINS dataset.

4.1
Dataset

The PROTEINS dataset comprises 1113 graphs representing proteins, each classified as either an enzyme (class 1) or non-enzyme (class 0). The graphs have an average of 39 nodes, with each node representing an amino acid and edges representing interactions between them. This dataset provides a suitable benchmark for evaluating our model’s ability to learn meaningful representations from graph-structured data
(
Morris et al. 2020
)
.

4.2
Baselines

We compare our discrete diffusion autoencoder (DDAE) against two baseline models:
Graph-VAE:
A variational autoencoder for graphs, utilizing the same GCN encoder architecture similar to that used in our model.
Graph-AE:
A standard autoencoder for graphs, also using the same GCN encoder architecture.

4.3
Model Architecture and Training

Our model utilizes a Graph Convolutional Network (GCN) encoder to extract the latent representation
\mathbf{z}
from the input graph’s features and adjacency matrix. The node embeddings from the GCN are aggregated using mean pooling to obtain a graph-level embedding. The decoder employs a UNET architecture, commonly used in diffusion models
(
Preechakul et al. 2022
)
, to reconstruct the adjacency matrix from the latent representation and noise. A diffusion timestep of 32 was used for the discrete diffusion process and a latent size of 64 dimensions is used across all models.

4.4
Evaluation

To evaluate the quality of the learned graph representations, we adopt the following procedure:

1.
Representation Extraction:
We remove the decoders from all models (DDAE, Graph-VAE, and Graph-AE) and extract the latent representation
\mathbf{z}
for each graph in the PROTEINS dataset. These models are all trained in an unsupervised manner at this stage.

2.
Logistic Regression Training:
We train a Logistic Regression model on the extracted representations
\mathbf{z}
and the labels using the training split of the PROTEINS dataset. We evaluate the trained Logistic Regression model on the test split of the PROTEINS dataset and report the classification accuracy.

This evaluation scheme allows us to directly assess the effectiveness of the learned representations in capturing discriminative information for graph classification from the graph properties.

5
Conclusion and Future Works

In this paper, we introduce a discrete diffusion autoencoder model for learning representations of graph-structured data. Our approach leverages the power of discrete diffusion models to capture the complex dependencies within the graph nodes and edges. By combining this generative framework with an encoder network, we learn a latent representation that effectively captures the underlying structure of the graph data. This representation can be used for various downstream tasks, such as graph generation, classification, and other use cases.

For future work, we plan to extend this model in several directions. First, we aim to explore the application of our model to a wider range of graph datasets. Then, we intend to incorporate multiple baselines for graph classification and node classification tasks using the learned representation. This will involve evaluating the effectiveness of our learned graph representations in comparison to existing methods and across diverse datasets. We believe that our discrete diffusion autoencoder model provides a promising new approach for learning representations of graph-structured data.
