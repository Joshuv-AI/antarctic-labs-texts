Predictors of post-stroke depression and anxiety at three months following acute ischemic stroke
Authors: Małgorzata Dec-Ćwiek, Joanna Słowik, Roman Pulyk, Ewa Pelc, Magdalena Bosak, Marcin Siwek, Marcin A Wnuk, Agnieszka Slowik
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPost-stroke depression (PSD) and post-stroke anxiety (PSA) are common emotional complications after acute ischemic stroke (AIS). We examined the incidence and risk factors for PSD alone, PSA alone, and their co-occurrence three months post-stroke.MethodsWe included 579 AIS patients who completed the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS) at discharge and 3 months later, using anxiety (HADS-A) and depression (HADS-D) subscales. Forty-one routinely collected parameters were analysed, including cognitive function (Montreal Cognitive Assessment) and oral health (Oral Health Impact Profile-14). Participants were grouped as: A – no PSA/PSD (n = 271), B – PSA only (n = 85), C – PSD only (n = 78), D – both PSA and PSD (n = 145).ResultsCompared with Group A, PSA was associated with female sex and anxiety at discharge; PSD with older age, ischemic heart disease, cognitive impairment, depression, and poorer functional outcomes; co-morbid PSA and PSD with female sex, infections, cognitive impairment, anxiety, depression, and worse discharge outcomes. Multivariate analysis showed PSA risk increased with anxiety at discharge (OR = 2.58; 98.3% CI:1.26–5.27), PSD risk with older age (OR = 1.05; 98.2% CI:1.01–1.09) and depression at discharge (OR = 3.99; 98.3% CI:1.61–9.80), and co-morbid risk with both anxiety (OR = 2.43; 98.3% CI:1.28–4.63) and depression at discharge (OR = 2.56; 98.3% CI:1.19–5.50).ConclusionsMood disorders affect up to 50% of stroke survivors, with co-morbid PSA and PSD most common. Early anxiety and depression at discharge strongly predict later emotional complications. Routine screening at discharge can identify high-risk patients and enable early interventions. Future studies should develop tailored psychological and rehabilitative strategies, that improve both mental health and functional recovery post-stroke.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Keywords: Ischemic stroke, Post-stroke depression, Post-stroke anxiety

Background

The global burden of stroke is an increasingly important public health concern [1]. Maximizing functional, cognitive, and emotional recovery after a stroke has become a primary goal of contemporary post-stroke care.

The most prevalent emotional consequences of stroke are depression and anxiety [2]. The incidence of post-stroke depression (PSD) has been widely studied. Three significant meta-analyses – comprising six studies (N = 4,648) [3], 43 studies (N = 20,293) [4], and 61 cohorts (N = 24,488) [5] – demonstrated that PSD affects up to one-third of stroke survivors, with rates of 23.8%, 29%, and 31%, respectively, in these analyses.

The incidence of post-stroke anxiety (PSA) has been studied less extensively, with most research indicating a similar or slightly lower incidence compared to PSD. A review of 97 studies (n = 22,262) found that anxiety disorders occurred in 15% of patients within 1 month after a stroke, 21.4% between 1 and 5 months, and 31.8% from 6 to 12 months after a stroke [6]. Notably, the incidence of both mood disorders may change over time following a stroke [7].

There remains confusion regarding the predisposing factors for mood disorders following a stroke, although some overlap between the factors is evident. Predisposing factors for PSD include female sex [8], marital status [9], longer education [9, 10], hypertension [8, 9], right-sided stroke location [11], bilateral stroke [11], strategic stroke [11], large vessel etiology of stroke [11], neurological deficit [12], anxiety and depression during hospitalization [13], cognitive impairment [14], stroke area [12], dysphagia [15], and blood inflammatory markers [16]. In contrast, factors predisposing to PSA include female sex [8], longer education [8], diabetes mellitus [8], hypertension [8], concomitant depression [17], dependency [8, 17], and dysphagia [15].

Additionally, both PSD and PSA are associated with poorer quality of life, delayed physical recovery, worsened long-term functional outcomes, and subsequent increases in morbidity and mortality [18].

There is limited data on the incidence of co-morbid PSD and PSA, as well as the factors that predispose individuals to this condition [19, 20].

In our study, we examined the incidence of PSD alone, PSA alone, and co-morbid of PSD and PSA 3 months after acute ischemic stroke (AIS). We compared these findings to patients who did not experience these mood disorders, using the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS) [21]. Additionally, we investigated factors influencing the risk of PSD alone, PSA alone, and the co-morbid presence of PSD and PSA after stroke. Our analysis considered both routinely collected acute stroke parameters and discharge evaluations that reflect cognitive status, depression, anxiety, and oral health-related quality of life.

Methods

Study design and participants

We enrolled patients with AIS who were admitted to the Comprehensive Stroke Center at the University Hospital in Krakow, Poland, from July 18, 2020, to December 14, 2022. Following the study protocol for “Identification and Clinical Validation of Biomarkers for Long-Term Outcome After Cerebral Ischemia (IBioStroke)” [22], we prospectively collected demographic, clinical, radiological, and laboratory data.

Ethics approval and consent to participate

All procedures adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki [23]. The Jagiellonian University Ethical Committee approved the study protocol (1072.6120.118.2020). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Measurements

For the current analysis, the following clinical data were collected: demographics, including age, sex, and years of schooling; stroke risk factors profile [24]. Stroke etiology categorized as small vessel disease stroke, large vessel disease stroke, cardio-embolic stroke, stroke of rare etiology, and unknown stroke etiology was determined based on the TOAST criteria (Trial of ORG 10172 in Acute Stroke Treatment) [25]. The treatment administered was either intravenous thrombolysis with alteplase (IVT), mechanical thrombectomy (MT), or both [26, 27]. The neurological deficit was assessed using the National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) upon admission and at 24 h post-admission [28]. The degree of disability, the modified Rankin Scale score (mRS) was noted before stroke and on discharge [29] Additionally, admission body temperature was measured.

Upon admission, patients followed a standardized neuroimaging study protocol described elsewhere [30]. For the current analysis, we recorded baseline ischemic core volume and penumbra volume using computed tomography perfusion. Post-processing analysis was conducted with RAPID software (iSchemaView, Menlo Park, CA).

Brain atrophy was assessed using the Global Cortical Atrophy (GCA) scale, as detailed in another study [30]. The history of silent stroke was defined as a covert stroke on CT scan. Hemorrhagic transformation, visible on the computed tomography scan taken 24 h post-stroke, was classified according to the ECASS-2 criteria [31]. The location of the ischemic lesion was used to categorize stroke topography into the right hemisphere, left hemisphere, and cerebellum/brain stem. Medical complications were diagnosed following the method described elsewhere [32].

Cognitive decline was evaluated at hospital discharge using the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) test [33]. The permission has been granted to use MoCA in the research (transaction ID: CBJCHBCAABAAlFj-P_lFSC6Hvu_WPB-FoUxbX5a3SoG0). The patient group received the Polish adaptation of the MoCA test at discharge [34]. Based on the MoCA test results, patients were divided into two groups: Group I scored 0–22 points, indicating cognitive impairment, while Group II scored 23–30 points, reflecting normal cognition.

During hospitalization, the Oral Health Impact Profile-14 (OHIP-14) questionnaire, which measures oral health-related quality of life related to the previous month [35], was administered.

On day 1, the following fasting blood tests were analyzed: C-reactive protein (CRP), white blood cell (WBC) count, platelet (PLT) count, fibrinogen levels, international normalized ratio (INR), creatinine levels, hemoglobin (Hb) levels, glucose levels, total cholesterol levels, low-density lipoprotein (LDL) levels, high-density lipoprotein (HDL) levels, and triglyceride levels.

The APOE genotype, consisting of the APOE ε2, APOE ε3, and APOE ε4 alleles, was determined by genotyping two single nucleotide polymorphisms: rs429358 and rs7412 (National Center for Biotechnology Information). This was accomplished using the TaqMan assay (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA).

The genotyping of the BDNF gene variant NM_001709.5:c.196G > A / p.(Val66Met) (rs6265) was performed through the melting-curve analysis of TaqMan probes (C__11592758_10 SNP assay; Thermo Fisher Scientific) validated with appropriate control samples.

Depression and anxiety were measured using the HADS at two time points: upon discharge and 3 months post-stroke. The HADS is a self-rated instrument that employs a 14-item, 4-point Likert scale (ranging from 0 to 3) to independently evaluate anxiety (HADS-A) and depression (HADS-D) symptoms, with seven items dedicated to each subscale. Scores ranging from 0 to 7 on each subscale are considered normal. This tool was specifically developed for medically ill populations and excludes items related to somatic symptoms, such as sleep, fatigue, and pain, which may result from co-morbid physical illnesses [21]. In the current study, participants were administered the Polish adaptation of the HADS questionnaire [36].

For the current study, the results of the HADS-A and HADS-D were dichotomized separately at two time points: upon discharge and 3 months post-stroke. Scores were categorized as indicating no mood disorder (0–7 points) versus the presence of a mood disorder (> 7 points).

We categorized our analysis into four outcome groups based on HADS-A and HADS-D scores, measured 3 months post-stroke. Group A comprised patients with no PSA (HADS-A: 0–7) and no PSD (HADS-D: 0–7). Group B included patients with PSA (HADS-A: >7) but no PSD (HADS-D: 0–7). Group C consisted of patients with no PSA (HADS-A: 0–7) but with PSD (HADS-D: >7). Finally, Group D encompassed patients with co-morbid PSA (HADS-A: >7) and PSD (HADS-D: >7).

Statistical analysis

We examined the distribution of demographic factors, stroke risk factors, received treatments, and clinical, radiological, biochemical, and genetic parameters across the four groups. Categorical variables were presented as numbers and percentages, both overall and within each group. For continuous variables, the mean and standard deviation were reported.

The statistical significance of differences between groups was evaluated through pairwise comparisons: A vs. B, A vs. C, and A vs. D. The selection of statistical tests depended on the distribution of the variables and included Pearson’s chi-squared test, Kruskal-Wallis rank sum test, or Fisher’s exact test. To adjust for multiple comparisons (n = 3), the Bonferroni correction was employed. Consequently, p-values were deemed statistically significant if they were less than 0.0167 (i.e., p < 0.05/3).

Univariate logistic regression analyses were subsequently conducted to examine factors associated with the risk of developing anxiety at 3 months (A vs. B), depression at 3 months (A vs. C), and co-morbid anxiety and depression at 3 months (A vs. D). To control for multiple comparisons, the Bonferroni correction was applied, setting statistical significance at p < 0.0167. Results were presented as odds ratios (ORs) with 98.3% confidence intervals.

Based on the univariate regression results, multivariate models were developed using a stepwise selection method with a backward elimination algorithm, maintaining the same significance threshold of p < 0.0167. All calculations were performed using R statistical software version 4.2.1 (Copyright (C) 2022 The R Foundation for Statistical Computing).

Results

During the study period, our Center admitted 1,809 patients diagnosed with ischemic stroke. Of these, 1,042 patients met the iBioStroke study’s inclusion and exclusion criteria. Furthermore, 579 patients, representing 55.6% of the cohort, completed the HADS-A and HADS-D questionnaires twice: once at discharge and again 3 months after the stroke. The causes of not performing the HADS test during hospitalization (n = 432) were as follows: deterioration of neurological and medical condition (n = 219), subsequent stroke (n = 29), death during hospitalization (n = 75) and refusal to perform the test (n = 109). The causes of not performing the HADS test 90 days later (n = 31) were: death (n = 3); lost to follow-up (n = 23) refusal to perform the test (n = 5).

Group A, which includes patients with neither PSA nor PSD, consists of 271 individuals (46.8%). Group B, comprising those with PSA but not PSD, includes 85 individuals (14.6%). Group C encompasses 78 patients (13.6%) with no PSA but with PSD. Finally, Group D, consisting of patients with both co-morbid PSA and PSD, includes 145 individuals (25%).

In the initial analysis comparing Group A with Group B, univariate regression testing indicated that female sex and the presence of anxiety at discharge were correlated with the presence of PSA 3 months post-stroke (Table 1).

A comparison between group A and group C using a univariate regression model revealed several correlations related to PSD 3 months after stroke. Older age, the presence of ischemic heart disease, cognitive impairment on discharge (MoCA score: 0–22), depression on discharge (HADS-D > 7), and a worse functional outcome on discharge (mRS ≥ 3) were all significantly correlated with PSD (Table 1).

In the univariate regression model, comparing Group A with Group D revealed several correlations. Female sex, cognitive impairment at discharge (MoCA score: 0–22), and the presence of anxiety (HADS-A > 7) and depression (HADS-D > 7) at discharge, poorer outcomes at discharge (mRS ≥ 3), stroke-associated infection that required antibiotic treatment during hospitalization correlated with the presence of co-morbid PSD and PSA 3 months after the stroke (Table 1).

We developed three logistic regression models. The first logistic regression model showed that among all studied parameters only the presence of anxiety on discharge (OR = 2.58; 98.3%CI: 1.26–5.27; p = 0.001) correlated with PSA.

The second logistic regression model demonstrated that among all studied parameters, older age (OR = 1.05; 98.2% CI: 1.01–1.09; p = 0.005) and depression at discharge (OR = 3.99; 98.3% CI: 1.61–9.80; p < 0.001) were associated with PSD.

The third logistic regression model indicated that, among all the parameters studied, anxiety at discharge (OR = 2.43; 98.3% CI: 1.28–4.63; p < 0.001) and depression at discharge (OR = 2.56; 98.3% CI: 1.19–5.50; p = 0.003) were associated with co-morbid PSD and PSA.

Discussion

We found that 50.2% of the study participants experienced various mood disorders 3 months after stroke, including PSD alone, PSA alone, and co-morbid PSD and PSA. Among those with mood disorders, approximately half exhibited co-morbid PSD and PSA, while about one-quarter suffered from PSD alone, and another quarter from PSA alone. Sufficient data exists on the incidence of PSD and PSA independently; however, information on co-morbid PSD and PSA remains limited, with significant variation in the available data. For instance, Ignatio KHD et al. [17] reported co-morbid PSD and PSA in 16.4% (67 participants), Bonner B et al. [37] in 18.7% (91 participants), Chun HY et al. [19] in 26.7% (15 participants), and Bromfield NM et al. [38] in 41.4% (456 participants). Our study’s most significant finding is that co-morbid PSD and PSA are the most prevalent mood disorders following AIS, compared to PSD or PSA alone. Our data suggest that in patients with PSD, clinicians should also assess for PSA, and vice versa.

In our study group, the incidence of PSD, irrespective of the presence of PSA (n = 223, 38.5%), is notably high. Interestingly, the incidence of PSD varies considerably across different populations: 20% in an Australian study [39], 31.1% in a German study [40], 33.9% in a Brazilian study [13], and 70.6% in a Saudi Arabian study [9]. These variations may be influenced by the timing of the assessment post-stroke [7] and the demographic and clinical characteristics of the studied populations [7, 9, 39, 40]. A previous multicenter study in the Polish population reported a slightly lower incidence of PSD (33.8%) when evaluated using the Geriatric Depression Scale (GDS) [41] 3 months after stroke, compared to our findings [42]. This discrepancy may be attributed to differences in study design (single-center in our study versus multicenter in the previous study [42]) or the assessment tool used to measure PSD: HADS in our study versus GDS in the previous study [42].

Similarly, our study found a high incidence of PSA, regardless of the presence of PSD, with 230 cases (39.7%). Previous research on different populations reported varying incidences of PSA, similar to PSD. For instance, a Brazilian study reported an incidence of 23.7% [13], a study on young Filipinos found a 34.7% incidence [17], and a Scottish study recorded an incidence of 46.9% [38].

We identified factors that increase the risk of the three studied mood disorders: solely PSD, solely PSA, and co-morbid PSD and PSA. Our study demonstrated that, among all parameters assessed, logistic regression analysis indicated depression at discharge increased the risk of PSD solely by nearly four times; anxiety at discharge increased the risk of PSA solely by 2.6 times; and both depression and anxiety at discharge independently elevated the risk of co-morbid PSD and PSA by 2.6 and 2.4 times, respectively.

We measured a total of 41 different parameters in AIS. While some parameters demonstrated significance in univariate regression analyses, their significance did not persist in multivariate logistic regression analyses. This finding is crucial for clinical practice, as it highlights the importance of assessing for mood disorders at hospital discharge. The presence of mood disorders significantly increases the risk of developing these disorders 3 months later. Our study suggests the necessity of monitoring mood disorders and considering both psychological and pharmacological interventions to potentially reduce the risk of PSD and PSA in the future. We also found that the risk of PSD is related to age. However, the nature of the relationship between age and PSD remains uncertain. While most studies have not confirmed such an association, some have reported findings to the contrary [43].

Previous studies examining the prognostic significance of mood disorders in the development of PSD and PSA have considered several factors: (1) past psychiatric history, (2) mood disorders diagnosed during the acute stroke phase (as in our study), and (3) co-morbid mood disorders identified during the post-stroke follow-up period.

The study by Chan LG et al. demonstrated that a prior psychiatric history increased the risk of PSD during hospitalization by more than threefold [11].

A study by Elias S et al. demonstrated that anxiety during an acute stroke is an independent risk factor for concurrent depression [12]. In a separate study, the same authors found that both depression and anxiety in acute stroke each represented a two-fold risk for PSD at follow-up [13].

Some studies indicate that anxiety during a follow-up period increases the risk of concurrent depression [13, 17].

The aforementioned studies underscore the importance of studying mood disorders in stroke patients. Several tools are available for screening depression, such as the Beck Depression Inventory [44], Hamilton Depression Rating Scale [45], Montgomery-Asberg Depression Rating Scale [46], and the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 [47], among others [48]. Additionally, anxiety in post-stroke patients can be assessed using instruments like the GAD-7 [49] and the Geriatric Anxiety Inventory [50].

We selected the HADS for our study primarily for its pragmatic advantages. HADS facilitates the diagnosis of both depression and anxiety, which are central to our research focus. In the iBioStroke study design, the control visit scheduled 3 months post-stroke was extensive and included multiple assessments. Therefore, we required a test that was concise, reliable, and validated. The HADS-D and HADS-A subscales have been validated for stroke patients without communication difficulties [51], mirroring the characteristics of our study participants. Nonetheless, we acknowledge that self-report measures can be influenced by several factors, such as participants’ comprehension of the questions, their introspective capability, and their image management.

Pathophysiological aspects of mood disorders following a stroke have been extensively analyzed. Evidence indicates that PSD is associated with specific locations of ischemic lesions, such as the right insular cortex, right putamen, and right amygdala. Additionally, structural disconnection in the right temporal lobe has been implicated [52].

In our study, we did not find any correlation between PSD and PSA and stroke location (right/left hemisphere or posterior fossa), nor with ischemic stroke volume or penumbra volume.

Some authors suggest that PSD is associated with reduced bioavailable concentrations of serotonin, norepinephrine, and dopamine. This reduction is attributed to disconnections in the ascending structures of the central nervous system caused by ischemic injury [53].

Some suggest that mood disorders may correlate with inflammatory markers [16]. However, our study did not confirm these findings.

Data indicate that lower BDNF levels correlate with PSD [54, 55]. BDNF levels are regulated genetically, with a functional single nucleotide polymorphism at nucleotide 196G/A, which causes a valine to methionine substitution at amino acid 66 (Val66Met) in the 5’ proBDNF molecule [56]. The Met allele is linked to reduced activity-dependent BDNF secretion compared to the Val allele [56]. This Met allele may negatively affect brain plasticity, potentially slowing stroke recovery. Some studies suggest that the BDNF Val66Met polymorphism increases PSD risk. However, a meta-analysis involving five studies with 272 stroke cases and 503 controls found no correlation between Val66Met polymorphism and PSD risk [57]. A separate study examining the relationship between PSA and BDNF Val66Met polymorphism also found no significant correlation [58]. Our study did not confirm a significant association between the rs6265 BDNF polymorphism and the risk of the studied mood disorders.

The present analysis possesses several notable strengths. It is a prospective study conducted on a clearly defined population, systematically evaluating numerous parameters in acute stroke, as well as mood disorders, cognition, and oral health related quality of life at discharge. It is important to note that we included only patients without a prior history of mood disorders or cognitive decline. We used standardized questions with participants or their caregivers to determine any pre-stroke cognitive decline or mood disorders and reviewed available medical records for this purpose. Additionally, it should be emphasized that, according to the standardized questionnaire administered during the follow-up visit, none of the study participants received antidepressants or anxiolytics during hospitalization or the follow-up period.

Our study does have certain limitations. We acknowledge that our findings apply to a specific group of stroke survivors: those who were able to communicate and were mostly independent. Patients who did not complete the HADS-A and HADS-D tests at discharge and 3 months later were likely more severely affected by their stroke. Consequently, it is possible that their results differed from those of our study participants. Therefore, caution is advised in generalizing our results.

Although the subgroup of patients aged ≥ 85 years in our study was small (n = 27), limiting detailed comparisons, our findings of no significant differences in PSD (25.9%), PSA (14.8%) and co-morbid PSD and PSA (25.9%) rates between patients aged ≥ 85 years vs. <85 years should be interpreted with caution, in light of evidence of Torres-Riera S et al. [59] indicating that very old stroke patients as compared to others may present distinct demographic and risk factor profiles as well as prognosis.

Another limitation of our study is that only CT imaging was available, which allowed the assessment of only major structural changes such as global cortical atrophy and the presence of silent strokes. In our study, the incidence of these abnormalities was similar across the study groups. The use of more advanced neuroimaging techniques, particularly MRI, would have provided a more comprehensive evaluation of cerebral pathology, including detailed white matter changes and other subtle brain abnormalities [60].

A limitation of our study is also that we did not assess sleep quality or sleep-related breathing disorders, which may influence ischemic stroke outcomes, such as PSD and PSA. Therefore, future studies incorporating these factors could provide a more comprehensive understanding of their role in post-stroke recovery [61].

We evaluated PSD and PSA twice after the stroke, once at discharge and again 3 months later. We acknowledge that the incidence of mood disorders following a stroke fluctuates over time [62]. Therefore, continuous monitoring during the follow-up period may be beneficial.

We should highlight that we did not consider the socioeconomic status, marital status, or income of the participants. We acknowledge that these factors might significantly impact the risk of mood disorders [7, 9]. Additionally, we did not collect information on alcohol consumption, as inquiries about drinking habits are regarded as embarrassing in Polish culture.

Conclusion

The results indicate the necessity of implementing screening tests for depression and anxiety in acute stroke patients. Early diagnosis of mood disorders shortly after a stroke permits timely monitoring, prevention, and treatment interventions, potentially mitigating the development of PSD and PSA in the future.

These findings underscore the importance of routine screening for anxiety and depression at hospital discharge, as early symptoms strongly predict subsequent mood disorders. Implementing structured assessments can guide early interventions—psychological support, counseling, and, when indicated, pharmacological treatment—to reduce the incidence and severity of PSD, PSA, and their co-occurrence. Future research should focus on developing individualized rehabilitation and psychological strategies, exploring underlying biological and psychosocial mechanisms, and evaluating interventions that simultaneously improve mental health and functional recovery post-stroke.
