Change in psychosocial target processes during prenatal depression intervention: A randomized clinical trial
Authors: Karl E. Conroy, Benjamin L. Hankin, Julianne M. Griffith, Catherine H. Demers, R. Chris Fraley, Mary Curran, Nancy Grote, Elysia Poggi Davis
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Prenatal depression is one of the most common obstetric complications. Psychotherapies alleviate depression during pregnancy, yet less is known about which psychosocial processes are changed during treatment and which psychotherapeutic targets associate with depression symptom improvement. This study addressed these questions using data from a prospective randomized clinical trial (RCT). Adult pregnant individuals, randomized to brief Interpersonal Therapy (IPT; n = 115; age M=29.7 [5.9] years) or active comparison condition of Enhanced Usual Care (EUC; n = 119; M=30.1 [5.9] years) which included integrated maternity support services plus one-on-one counseling session and written psychoeducation, completed measures assessing multiple forms of social support, perceived stress, rumination, attachment styles, and various coping skills at baseline (M=16.7 gestational weeks) and post-intervention at end of gestation (M=35.8 weeks; near term) alongside depression symptom scales across pregnancy. Results of linear mixed-effects models showed that most social support dimensions improved, perceived stress and rumination decreased, and multiple forms of coping changed for participants in both IPT and EUC across pregnancy. Perceived stress and emotionally-supportive coping changed more in the IPT group relative to EUC. Change in depression symptoms across pregnancy was predicted by change in social support dimensions of tangible and positive interactions, perceived stress, rumination, and less behavioral-disengagement coping. Causal mediation analyses showed rumination mediated the link between randomization to treatment group and reduced depression symptoms. Results identifying which psychosocial processes were changed during prenatal depression treatment and associate with symptom relief can inform future clinical research aiming at these target processes to enhance outcomes for distressed pregnant patients.

Depression is commonly experienced during pregnancy. The average global rate of prenatal depression is 17 % [1] with estimates as high as 37 % [2]. Prenatal maternal depression is associated with intergenerational risks such as preterm birth and elevated risk of psychopathology in offspring [3], [4], [5], [6], [7], [8], [9]. Many treatments, including psychotherapies, exist that reduce depression during pregnancy [10], [11], [12], [13], [14], [15], [16]. Yet little is known about the psychosocial processes that change due to intervention during pregnancy and the extent to which these processes relate to improvements in prenatal depression. Identifying which psychotherapeutic processes change from intervention, especially during pregnancy when individuals are more open to mental health services and are more connected to the healthcare system [17], has the potential to optimize treatment by bringing into focus psychological factors that can be changed by intervention and might lead to proximate symptom reductions [18]. Consistent with the goals of experimental therapeutics [19], improving understanding of behavioral and psychosocial processes that are changed during intervention and associate with symptom reduction holds promise for accelerating development and dissemination of more effective and personalized treatments for prenatal depression. By identifying which factors can be changed by intervention, clinicians can then target these processes to enhance outcomes for patients.

Consistent with experimental therapeutics, three steps need to be evaluated to make progress in understanding to achieve these goals. First is demonstrating which psychosocial processes change over time after initiation of intervention. Second is showing that changes in those processes are associated with depression symptom improvement throughout intervention. Each of these first two steps provides valuable information in their own right, including first on the malleability of change in psychosocial factors due to intervention and second regarding how depression symptoms can be reduced. Pending results from the first two steps, then the third step involves formal mediation testing to determine whether a specific intervention, compared to an alternative intervention, leads to improved symptom relief through particular psychosocial mediators. The current study investigated each of these three steps to advance knowledge on which psychosocial targets change during treatment provided during pregnancy and which processes improve prenatal depression. We evaluated various psychosocial change targets in a randomized clinical trial (RCT) that compared brief interpersonal psychotherapy (IPT) for depression to an active comparison condition of enhanced usual care (EUC) among pregnant individuals with elevated prenatal depression symptoms. We selected particular psychosocial processes that past theory and research suggest may change during intervention and predict depression improvement across pregnancy, including various forms of social support, perceived stress, rumination, attachment styles, and multiple facets of coping.

Psychosocial target processes

Our selection of psychosocial processes that may change during prenatal depression intervention was informed by the broad literature investigating depression risks and modifiable processes in depression treatments among adults generally [18], [20], [21], [22], [23], [24], [25], [26], [27]. First, social support represents one set of processes targeted in many psychological interventions around the world [28], [29], [30], [31]. A review of IPT interventions delivered across pregnancy and postpartum period finds change in broad social support within IPT (i.e., not compared to alternative treatment) [15]. A recent meta-analysis [10] of cognitive-behavioral (CBT) interventions with individuals during the perinatal period shows various CBT-based interventions target and significantly affected general aspects of social support. Second, many psychological interventions seek to diminish stress, and robust evidence shows that multiple psychotherapies reduce perceived stress during pregnancy [32] and the perinatal/postpartum period [10]. Third, rumination—a maladaptive and repetitive process of dwelling on negative thoughts and emotions without problem solving [33]—has been identified as risk for depression that can be altered via psychological intervention [24] among general adult depressed patients, although rumination has not been investigated specifically as a target during pregnancy. Last, a broad set of psychosocial processes involves coping, including multiple forms of maladaptive and adaptive coping strategies that are often targets for change across multiple interventions [34]. While past intervention research has investigated general coping skills among adults [35], less is known about changes in coping during treatment for prenatal depression. One RCT evaluated stress management intervention and found improved coping skills among healthy pregnant individuals [36].

In addition to these targets identified from the broad depression psychotherapy change literature, we also included targets deemed important for IPT according to processes central to IPT’s interpersonal model. IPT is conceptually grounded in relational and attachment theory [37], [38], which emphasize that a secure base of intimate connections with others, especially in times of stress, improves mental health. Consistent with these frameworks, Lipsitz and Markowitz [39] proposed four potential targets that may change in IPT, including (1) increased social support, (2) decreased interpersonal stress, (3) facilitating emotional processing, and (4) improving interpersonal skills. Guided by this theoretical model and interpersonal framework, IPT psychotherapy researchers have predominantly focused on stress, social support, and attachment styles as potential processes of change within IPT (not necessarily compared to alternative treatment) [40]. Consistent with the broad psychotherapy change literature in depression, a meta-analytic review [41] of IPT’s effects among general adult patients with depression found social functioning significantly changed. Regarding potential change processes in IPT among perinatal depression specifically, Sockol’s [15] review indicates that social adjustment and social support both showed significant within-IPT change, although relationship quality did not. Qualitative research [42] of brief IPT with perinatal depressed people found intervention reduced stress, improved emotion regulation and social skills.

While such evidence shows that some psychosocial processes change over time within IPT, less research has examined whether these processes are associated with depression symptom improvements over time (i.e., across more than two assessments). Little research has investigated whether these target processes are associated with prenatal depression in pregnant samples in particular. Indeed, only one RCT has evaluated change processes in IPT in the perinatal period [43]: Among mothers experiencing postpartum depression who are economically disadvantaged, patients’ perceived stress was reduced in IPT and correlated with symptom reduction. Further, family social support (measued with the Social Support Behaviors Scale) [44] predicted symptom improvement, although this social support component was not changed through IPT. In addition to this one IPT RCT addressing postpartum depression, two other studies with general adult depressed patients (i.e., not specifically pregnant participants) contribute information relevant for psychosocial targets of change for IPT depression by testing whether changes in processes correlated with depression improvement. The first was a non-randomized study which showed that anxious and avoidant attachment styles, general social support, relationship quality and interpersonal problems changed as a result of IPT, although none of these processes was associated with depression symptom improvement as hypothesized [45]. The second, a RCT with adult depressed patients comparing IPT with CBT, examined rumination, interpersonal functioning, self-esteem, dysfunctional cognitive beliefs, and therapeutic alliance. All targets changed over time in both IPT and CBT with the biggest effect found for rumination, and all mediators correlated concurrently with improvements in depression symptoms [24].

In summary, understanding of potential psychosocial processes for depression treatment among pregnant individuals is limited. First, while there exist some studies with individuals during the perinatal period, research is particularly sparse regarding which target processes change among distressed pregnant individuals over time. Second, little work has reported on the extent to which change in psychosocial processes correlates with changes in depression symptoms across pregnancy following treatment. Examining both steps is important to advance knowledge on experimental therapeutics and target processes of change specifically during pregnancy. Understanding which psychosocial targets can be changed via intervention during pregnancy and which of these changed processes associate with reduced prenatal depression matters for the mental and physical health and well-being of pregnant persons, as well as fetal development during pregnancy and offspring outcomes [3], [4], [5], [6], [7], [8], [9], [46].

Additionally, research into particular target processes has tended to focus on more global measures, such as general social support or coping, with less attention to more granular aspects of these multifaceted domains. Particular subcomponents of social support (e.g., tangible, affectionate, positive-social interactions, emotional/information support) can be measured and evaluated as specific targets of change. Furthermore, assessments can measure multiple fine-grained facets of coping, including adaptive (e.g., emotional-support, reframing, acceptance) and maladaptive (e.g., denial, behavioral-disengagement) processes, that could be changed via depression intervention during pregnancy, yet relatively less research has broken down and assessed these granular coping aspects in psychotherapy research. Based on IPT’s focus on targeting positive social interactions and emotional expression, for example, it may be that particular coping skills (e.g., emotionally-supportive coping) may be improved relatively more in IPT compared with EUC across pregnancy. Thus, by breaking down general psychosocial targets into more fine-grained component processes, additional new information can be learned about more granular and specific targets of change that can become the focus of clinical research for psychological interventions during pregnancy.

Study overview

The present study first sought to advance knowledge on target psychosocial processes that may change during prental treatment, including both IPT and EUC, among distressed pregnant individuals. Second, we aimed to examine which potential change processes were associated with decreased depression symptoms across pregnancy among participants from both IPT and EUC conditions using measures of psychosocial functioning that were not only general (e.g., total social support) but also fine-grained (e.g., facets of coping). For the first step we investigated the extent to which psychotherapeutic interventions predicted changes over time in more specific social support components, perceived stress, attachment styles, rumination, and multiple granular coping facets. In this first step we further evaluated the extent to which IPT specifically changes these targets above and beyond EUC, an active intervention comparison condition. For the second step we evaluated the degree to which change in these target processes correlates with improved depression symptoms over time. Last, we conducted causal mediation analyses among those target variables supported by the previous two steps to determine if any targets serve as mediators.

To address these questions, we used data from the Care Project, a RCT in which pregnant individuals with elevated levels of depression symptoms were randomized to IPT or EUC. Results from this published RCT [47] show that brief IPT led to greater decreases in depression symptom trajectories, measured repeatedly across pregnancy compared to EUC, an active comparison group consisting of enhanced standard of care obstetric maternity support services. As part of the RCT, participants completed psychosocial measures assessed at baseline (pre-randomization) and end of pregnancy (post intervention) along with depression symptoms throughout pregnancy.

Method

This study’s methods, measures, approaches, and hypotheses were pre-registered (https://osf.io/3zh4m/overview). Deviations are noted in on-line supplement.

Participants

We randomized 234 pregnant individuals recruited primarily from obstetrics clinics at two major medical centers in Denver, Colorado. Recruitment began July 2017 and ended August 2021 with a pause from mid-April through mid-June 2020 due to the COVID-19 pandemic. Initial screening was performed based on medical record review. Eligibility criteria included 18–45 years of age, English-speaking, 25 weeks gestational age (GA) or less, singleton pregnancy, and endorsing elevated depression symptoms when screened as part of standard of care (Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale score≥10). Exclusion criteria included current illicit drug or methadone use and major health conditions requiring invasive treatments. Additional assessment for eligibility was performed at baseline to exclude: 1) current or past psychosis or mania, and 2) currently receiving cognitive behavioral therapy or IPT.

119 individuals were allocated to EUC and 115 assigned to IPT; of those in the IPT group, 106 received intervention and 9 did not attend any sessions. All 234 participants (including 9 receiving no intervention) were considered part of the study once randomized (intent-to-treat design [IIT]). Procedures

Participants completed a baseline evaluation. Eligible individuals were randomly assigned to IPT or EUC using a computer-generated random numbers sequence. Participants were randomized in blocks of two and stratified on current MDD, GA (above or below 15 weeks), and Medicaid status for each study arm. Participants completed target process measures two times prenatally: 1) prior to intervention at baseline (M=16.7 gestational weeks) and 2) post intervention follow-up near end of gestation (M=35.8 weeks; near term). Consistent with main depression symptom outcomes reported in main RCT trial paper [47], measures were completed five times for EPDS and three times for SCL-20 over pregnancy.

Institutional Review Boards for the Protection of Human Subjects at the University of Denver and the University of Colorado Anschutz Medical Campus approved the study, which was registered on ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT03011801). Recruitment, outcomes, and adverse events were monitored by a data and safety monitoring board. Participants provided written and informed consent.

Intervention conditions

MomCare is a collaborative care intervention that delivers brief IPT [48]. Brief IPT consists of eight 50-minute individual sessions with the active phase of treatment during pregnancy; thereafter maintenance care is allowed with less frequent sessions. Intervention focuses on psychoeducation and interpersonal skill building to decrease interpersonal conflict and increase interpersonal support and competence. The intervention educates participants about linkages between feelings and interpersonal interactions and strategies to resolve interpersonal conflicts contributing to symptoms. As collaborative care model, participants could receive maternity support services (MSS) and be prescribed antidepressant medications, as needed in consultation with medical experts and patients’ preference. Adherence checklists ensured that IPT was delivered with fidelity, as shown by high fidelity ratings (M=1.90 on a 0–2 fidelity scale). Most participants attended nearly all 8 prenatal IPT sessions (M=7.27 [3.5]).

Enhanced usual care (EUC) augmented the usual standard-of-care pregnant individuals received within OB/GYN clinics through their primary care provider. EUC consisted of integrated maternity support services (MSS) and providing one-on-one session discussions with doctoral-level clinicians. During this one-on-one session the clinician reviewed written psychoeducational materials with participants to recognize their mood symptoms during pregnancy and discussed ways that participants could talk with partners about depression to elicit help. Additionally, based on patients’ preferences, pregnant individuals were connected with behavioral health services for MSS, including eclectic counseling [not IPT or CBT], psychiatric consultation, and other community services. To help overcome barriers to care, clinicians offered referral sources to participants and provided an extensive list of community resources, including information on additional alternative community mental health and counseling services, essential items and housing programs, and local resource centers for pregnancy and postpartum support.

Measures and outcomes

Proposed processes

Social support factors were assessed using the Medical Outcomes Study Social Support Survey (MOS) [49], which was administered twice during pregnancy, at baseline and follow-up. The MOS includes 19 items measuring the availability of social support in several domains. Items are scored on a 1–5 scale; higher ratings indicate greater social support availability. The MOS possesses an Overall Social Support score (all 19 items), as well as several subscales: Emotional/Informational Support (8 items), Tangible Support (4 items), Affectionate Support (3 items), and Positive Social Interaction (3 items). Scale scores were standardized to a 0–100 scale.

Perceived Stress Scale (PSS) [50] assesses subjective perceptions of recent stressful life events using 10-items, rated on 0 (never) to 4 (very often) scale. The PSS was assessed at baseline and post-treatemt/ end of pregnancy.

Attachment Styles were assessed via self-report using an abbreviated version (9 items) of the Experiences in Close Relationships-Relationships Structure scale (ECR-RS) [51] which assesses attachment-related anxiety and attachment-related avoidance related to their romantic partner. A prototypically secure person scores low on both dimensions. Each item is rated on a 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree) scale. The measure was given at baseline and follow-up.

Rumination was measured via five items from the original 22-item Ruminative Response Scale (RRS) [33], which measures tendency to ruminate. Participants rate how often they typically think or behave in particular ways when sad or down, from almost never (1) to almost always (4). RRS data from baseline and follow-up were used.

Multiple coping facets were assessed using the brief COPE [52], consisting of 28 items to assess coping via a Likert scale (0−3). The full brief COPE includes 14 different forms of coping, but we only included 10 facets, based on past intervention theory and research, that might be changed (see Table 2 for list of 10 coping aspects) with 2 items per coping facet. Outcome measures: depression symptoms

Symptom Checklist SCL-20 includes 20 items to measure depression symptoms from the full Symptom Checklist-90-R [53]. Items are rated on a 0–4 scale. Higher ratings indicate greater symptom severity. Item ratings were summed to generate a total score, ranging from 0 to 80. Prior work indicates its reliability and validity, including with pregnant participants [54]. SCL-20 was given at baseline and at two additional prenatal timepoints including post-treatment assessment.

Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale (EPDS) assesses maternal depression across the peripartum period as a reliable and valid measure of depression symptoms in pregnancy [55], [56]. Participants provided ratings on a 0–3 point scale; higher ratings signify greater symptom severity. Possible total scores range from 0 to 30. EPDS was assessed at five timepoints.

Sociodemographic and obstetric characteristics

Research staff blinded to condition collected these characteristics, including parity, via interview. Participants reported on psychotropic medication use during pregnancy (IPT (22.3 %) and EUC (28.2 %);[χ2= .88, p = .35]).

Data analytic plan

Step 1: Effects of treatment (IPT and EUC) on process variables

Linear mixed-effects models were used to estimate the effects of time and intervention (IPT and EUC) on process variables (e.g., social support). At Level 1, each variable was modeled as a function of time which references participants’ gestational age in weeks centered at randomization. At Level 2, the intercept was modeled as a function of treatment, parity, and additional fixed and random effects. The slope of time was modeled as a function of treatment and fixed main effect. Because process variables were measured twice (pre- and post-treatment) we did not include slope as random effect. We used false-discovery rate (FDR) correction [57] to adjust p-values given multiple predictors.

Step 2: Association between change in process variables and depression symptoms over time

Linear mixed-effects models were used to test whether difference scores (i.e., change in process variables pre- to post-treatment; represented as Δ) were associated with decreased depression symptoms over time.2 For Step 2, we focused on the process variables that exhibited significant change over time from Step 1. At Level 1 depression symptoms were modeled same as Step 1. At level 2, intercept and slope were modeled as a function of treatment, parity, difference score (e.g., social support), interaction of difference score with treatment, and additional fixed and random effects.3 FDR correction was used.

Previously published analyses [47] of some data from the project focused on reduced depression across pregnancy. Results showed that symptoms decreased over time in both IPT and EUC. Also treatment group (IPT vs EUC) interacted with time, such that IPT participants decreased in depression symptoms sooner compared to EUC participants. We build on these past depression findings with several new analyses, including models evaluating associations between depression symptoms and potential change processes. If change in a process variable (i.e., Δ) was associated with more rapid decrease in depression symptoms across both IPT and EUC, we would expect to find a significant estimate for the difference score on slope (i.e., time × Δ). A significant 3-way interaction of time × treatment × difference scores (Δ) would indicate that association between change in process variable and depression symptoms was stronger for a treatment group (e.g., IPT).

Step 3: Do psychosocial targets mediate change in symptoms of depression over time?

Causal mediation analyses, implemented using ‘mediation’ R package with 1000 simulations and robust standard errors [58], were used to investigate the extent to which pregnant individuals randomized to IPT (versus EUC) experienced greater change in a target process, which in turn, contributed to greater reduction in depression symptoms over pregnancy and explained why IPT participants exhibited less depression compared to EUC participants. Logically mediation depends on the prior two steps: 1) a process variable changes significantly over time after intervention delivery, and 2) change in that target process across pregnancy correlates with reductions in depression symptoms over time. Given these logical steps, we focused our causal mediation analyses on process variables that showed significant effects in these prior two steps. Because target process measures were not randomized (randomization protects again unmeasured confounding), we tested causal mediation analyses that relax the sequential ignorability assumption [59] as implemented in ‘mediation.’ Causal mediation analysis produces the multiplicative paths from steps 1 (path a) and 2 (path b) and the direct effect of intervention (IPT vs EUC) on depression symptoms as outcome after controlling for the mediator (path c). This analytic approach controls for intervention to test whether the mediator (indirect effect), called the natural indirect effect (NIE), is significantly different from 0. Observing a statistically significant NIE is considered evidence for causal mediation. Additionally, the proportion of the total effect that the process variable explains (called “proportion mediated”) provides descriptive effect size information indicating how much the association between randomization to intervention group and reduction in depression was accounted for by the mediator.

Participants in both IPT and EUC reported significantly greater social support in most domains over time across pregnancy: total social support (B= 0.146; p = .003), tangible support (B= 0.102; p = .010), positive-interaction social support (B= 0.085; p = .036), and emotional-informational support (B= 0.186; p = .006). There was no statistically significant interaction between time and treatment for any social support variable (e.g., B= −0.050; p = .109 for total social support). Perceived stress

Perceived stress decreased across pregnancy for participants in both IPT and EUC (B=-0.327; p < .001). Time interacted significantly with treatment to predict changes in perceived stress (B=-0.068; p = .026). Fig. 1 (bottom left) illustrates this interaction: Perceived stress improved over time for IPT participants (B=-0.364; p < .001) more so relative to EUC (B=-0.286; p < .001).

Attachment

Neither attachment avoidance nor anxiety changed across pregnancy. There was no time by treatment interaction.

Rumination

Fig. 1 (top left) shows that participants in both IPT and EUC decreased in rumination over time (B= -0.186; p = .003). The time × treatment interaction was not statistically significant (B= 0.052; p = .141).

Coping

For participants in both IPT and EUC, coping strategies changed in these domains: less substance use for coping (B=-0.130; p = .024), better emotionally-supportive coping (B=0.085; p = .007), less behavioral-disengagement coping (B=-0.157; p = .043), and less self-blame (B=-0.171; p = .008). Fig. 1 (bottom right) shows significant time × treatment interaction for emotionally-supportive coping (B=-.084; p = .032), which improved over time for IPT participants (B=.170; p = .005) but not EUC (B=.004; p = .937).

Discussion

Many effective treatments exist to alleviate depression. Nonetheless, empirical evidence revealing which psychosocial processes change from intervention delivery and which psychotherapeutic targets correlate with symptom improvement is limited [60], [61], [62]. This raises questions that are particularly salient for prenatal depression, a prevalent condition that affects both pregnant persons and their developing babies. To address these gaps, this study assessed a wide array of psychosocial process variables that the literature suggests may change as a result of depression intervention to examine which process variables actually changed over pregnancy within the context of an RCT that randomized pregnant individuals with elevated depression symptoms to either brief IPT or active intervention comparison (EUC).

Results showed several target processes changed significantly over time from baseline (pre-intervention) to follow-up (near term) for pregnant participants receiving either IPT or EUC as active treatments. Specifically, multiple dimensions of social support (total support, tangible, positive-social interaction, emotional/informational), perceived stress, rumination, and multiple forms of coping (less substance-use, less self-blame, more emotional support, less behavioral-disengagement) improved across pregnancy. Furthermore, perceived stress and emotionally-supportive coping changed more rapidly over time for pregnant individuals receiving IPT compared to EUC. Second, change in some of these process variables was associated with reduced prenatal depression symptoms over time. Specifically, depression symptom improvement among participants, regardless of assignment to IPT or EUC, was associated with increased tangible social support (SCL-20, EPDS), greater total social support (SCL-20), more positive-interactions social support (SCL-20), decreased perceived stress (SCL-20, EPDS), behavioral-disengagement coping (EPDS), and rumination (EPDS) across pregnancy. Finally, formal causal mediation analyses showed that ruminator served as a mediating mechanism as change in rumination over time explained why participants randomized to IPT compared to EUC exhibited less depression (EPDS) across pregnancy.

This research advances knowledge by adding empirical results to the field’s understanding of which psychosocial processes were changed among distressed pregnant individuals receiving active prenatal depression treatments, and which of these modified therapeutic targets were associated with reduced depression severity. We believe it is important to investigate both of these issues independently as empirical answers to each question yield relevant information that can be useful for future clinical research. Indeed our results highlight the importance of separately examining both steps, as prenatal depression interventions changed several target processes (step 1), but change in some of these target processes did not correlate with improved depression symptoms (step 2). Participants in both active treatment groups on average exhibited significant changes in several improved psychosocial process targets (e.g., multiple aspects of social support, stress, rumination, and various coping facets), and several of these targets (e.g., less perceived stress) predicted reduced depression symptoms across pregnancy. At the same time, our results also showed that IPT and EUC were both active depression treatments that could change a particular target process (e.g., self-blame coping), but these modifications were not necessarily related to reductions in prenatal depression. For example, participants in both IPT and EUC reported improvements in informational social support and less use of substances for coping, but neither of these processes was associated with reduced depression symptoms across pregnancy. Such findings highlight why it is important for research to explicitly and separately investigate both steps. Establishing that intervention changed some of these targets, even if some of these specific processes did not correlate with reduced depression, is important because change in such psychosocial targets might be important for understanding enhanced outcomes in other healthcare areas and outcomes. For example, the prenatal depression treatments in this study changed informational social support, which may be beneficial for pregnant people as they navigate and interact with medical health professionals (e.g., multiple obstetrician visits during pregnancy), and reduced use of substances to cope during pregnancy, which can be helpful for fetal development and offspring outcomes.

This research replicates and extends past findings in the relatively small literature examining psychosocial processes of change during interventions for perinatal depression as well as the processes of change for the broader global literature on IPT [63]. Our findings align with the one study to examine mediation in an RCT design comparing IPT to control focused on postpartum depression as the outcome [43], which reported that perceived stress decreased in IPT and that both lower stress and family social support correlated with reduced depression symptoms across the postpartum period. Also in line with two studies investigating mediating mechansisms and change targets in IPT among general adult depressed patients [24], [45], we found improved general social support and decreases in rumination across time [24] for both IPT and EUC, although attachment styles did not change over time in our study as found in this past study [45]. We found rumination mediated the link between randomization to treatment group and symptom improvement among distressed pregnant individuals, replicating work investigating mediating mechansisms in CBT and IPT among general adult depressed patients [24]. Our study’s results, in concert with past work, suggest that active psychological interventions change perceived stress, general social support, and rumination. Our work also uniquely builds on past work by investigating specific facets of social support and granular aspects of coping as target processes of change, as prior therapy process research focused on broad conceptualization and measurement of social support and coping. Multiple social support factors changed across pregnancy in both IPT and EUC. Moreover, several, albeit not all, aspects of coping changed in both intervention groups, including reductions in maladaptive forms of coping (self-blame, substance-use, and behavioral-disengagement) along with greater emotionally-supportive coping skills as adaptive coping. That these four coping skills, which are often targets in psychological interventions broadly, were changed, whereas six other measured coping facets were not, highlights the importance of measuring more fine-grained targets. Of those coping skills that did not show significant change over pregnancy, other evidence-based interventions, besides IPT and EUC, may more specifically target these and affect change. For example, CBT would be expected to change positive-reframing coping, and mindfulness interventions would likely affect coping via acceptance.

Study findings showed significant change in psychosocial targets and correlated reductions in depression symptoms for pregnant people in both IPT and EUC. IPT is an evidence-based brief intervention for prenatal depression. EUC in our study comprised an active control condition, as all EUC participants received psychoeducation about depression and information for how they could talk with partners to elicit help. Based on patient preference, EUC participants could receive counseling (but not IPT or CBT), psychiatric consultation, and other community and hospital services. Thus, study results showing that several psychosocial processes changed across pregnancy in both IPT and EUC likely reflects that all RCT participants had access to high-quality, evidence-based care for prenatal depression.

There were several limitations to the present research. First, participants self-reported on symptoms and psychosocial change processes. Future work could use other informants and methods (e.g., romantic partners, diagnostic interviews, observation) to examine change in psychosocial processes. Second, psychosocial targets were assessed twice during pregnancy, once at baseline before treatment delivery and then at follow-up near end of gestation. While this two-time point pre-post design enables evaluation of change over time and is the standard often used when investigating therapeutic change [18], this approach is limited as more complex forms of change and more precise temporal predictive associations cannot be examined. While we conducted caual mediation analyses, results should be cautiously interpreted because mediators were not assessed earlier and separately from depression as the main outcome so temporal precedence of change in a mediator affecting change in depression cannot be unambiguously demonstrated [18], [64] because mediators and symptoms were measured at the same time. Third, we included two active intervention conditions without a no-intervention comparison. Pregnancy is a dynamic period with normative changes, and psychological processes examined (e.g., stress, social support) change over time [65], [66]. Future work, building on study findings, could assess process targets and depression at multiple time points across time with a no-intervention normative comparison to provide enhanced understanding of temporal dynamics between change in depression and change in psychosocial processes.

In summary, this study investigated which psychosocial processes were changed via prenatal depression treatments among distressed pregnant women and which of these therapeutic targets correlated with reduced depression symptoms during pregnancy. Many forms of social support, perceived stress, rumination, and various coping aspects improved across pregnancy in both IPT and EUC treatment groups. Changes in tangible social support, positive-interaction social support, perceived stress, rumination, and behavioral-disengagement coping predicted improved depression over time. Rumination mediated the link between randomization to treatment group and reduced depression symptoms. Pending needed replication to ascertain robustness of these findings, information from this line of work can be used by clinicians and researchers to better target those psychosocial processes most amenable to change from prenatal depression treatment to enhance outcomes for distressed pregnant patients.
