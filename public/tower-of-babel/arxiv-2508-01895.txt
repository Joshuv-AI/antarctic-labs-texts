Abstract
. In this paper, we consider α ∈ ( 0 , 2 ) \alpha\in(0,2) and establish the strong well-posedness of McKean–Vlasov SDEs driven by an α \alpha -stable process with a Hölder (Besov) kernel K ∈ 𝐂 β K\in{\mathbf{C}}^{\beta} , where β > 1 − α \beta>1-\alpha . This condition coincides with the well-known threshold for the weak well-posedness.



1.
Introduction

Consider the following SDE driven by an
\alpha
-stable process:

\displaystyle{\mathord{{\rm d}}}X_{t}=b(X_{t}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}t+{\mathord{{\rm d}}}L_{t},

(1.1)

where
b:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
b\in{\mathbf{C}}^{\beta}
, where
{\mathbf{C}}^{\beta}
denotes the Besov space
{\mathbf{B}}^{\beta}_{\infty,\infty}
(see Definition
2.1
below). The process
(L_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
is a rotationally invariant
\alpha
-stable Lévy process with
\alpha\in(0,2)
, whose infinitesimal generator is the fractional Laplacian
\Delta^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}:=-(-\Delta)^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}
(see (
2.6
) below), defined on a complete filtered probability space
(\Omega,{\mathscr{F}},\mathbb{P},({\mathscr{F}}_{t})_{t\geqslant 0})
.
It is well known (see, e.g.,
[
8
]
) that:

(i)

If
\beta>1-\alpha
, there exists a unique weak solution.

(ii)

If
\beta>1-\frac{\alpha}{2}
, there exists a unique strong solution.

Remark 1.1
.

Let
\alpha\in(1,2)
. In case (i),
[
8
]
considers only the case
\beta>0
. For
\beta\in\left(\frac{1-\alpha}{2},0\right)
, the existence and uniqueness of weak and martingale solutions have been established in
[
15
,
5
]
, while the existence and uniqueness of strong solutions in the one-dimensional case have been shown in
[
1
]
. For
\beta\in\left(\frac{3(1-\alpha)}{2},\frac{1-\alpha}{2}\right]
and enhanced drifts
b
, a unique generalized martingale solution was shown in
[
14
]
via paracontrolled calculus. Furthermore, for
\beta\in(1-\alpha,\frac{1-\alpha}{2}]
and
\mathord{{\rm div}}b\in{\mathbf{C}}^{\beta}
, a unique weak solution was obtained in
[
12
]
.

The condition
\beta>1-\alpha
arises naturally from scaling arguments (see (
1.3
) below), whereas the stronger assumption
\beta>1-\frac{\alpha}{2}
is primarily technical conditions required for PDE estimates in Zvonkin-type transformations (cf.
[
16
,
7
,
23
,
8
,
21
]
). This discrepancy between the Hölder conditions for weak and strong solutions motivates the central question of this paper:

Can one establish the strong well-posedness under the assumption
\beta>1-\alpha
?

(Q)

When
\alpha=2
, this question has been resolved for the McKean–Vlasov SDE (MVSDE):

\displaystyle{\mathord{{\rm d}}}X_{t}=(K*\mu_{X_{t}})(t,X_{t}),{\mathord{{\rm d}}}t+{\mathord{{\rm d}}}W_{t},

where
K:\mathbb{R}_{+}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
is a measurable function,
\mu_{X_{t}}
is the time marginal law of the solution, and
W
is a standard
d
-dimensional Brownian motion. Specifically,
[
4
]
established pathwise uniqueness and strong well-posedness for
K\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{+};{\mathbf{C}}^{\beta})
with
\beta>-1
.

However, for
\alpha<2
and the MVSDE:

\displaystyle{\mathord{{\rm d}}}X_{t}=(K*\mu_{X_{t}})(t,X_{t}),{\mathord{{\rm d}}}t+{\mathord{{\rm d}}}L_{t},

(1.2)

[
4
]
only addresses the case
\alpha>1
and proves the strong well-posedness under the condition

\displaystyle\beta>2-\tfrac{3}{2}\alpha,

which is strictly stronger than
\beta>1-\alpha
.

In this paper, we aim to answer question (
Q
) for the MVSDE (
1.2
). Throughout, we assume
\alpha\in(0,2)
,
T>0
, and:

\displaystyle K\in L^{\infty}_{T}{\mathbf{C}}^{\beta}:=L^{\infty}([0,T];{\mathbf{C}}^{\beta}),\quad\text{for some }\beta\in{\mathbb{R}}.

(
H
β
)

Under the assumption
\beta>1-\alpha
, it is well-known that the MVSDE (
1.2
) admits a unique global weak solution (see
[
4
]
for the case
\alpha>1
and
[
10
]
for
\alpha\leqslant 1
).

Here is our main result.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
\alpha\in(0,2)
and assume condition (
Hβ
) holds with some
\beta>1-\alpha
. Then (
1.2
) admits a unique global strong solution for any initial date
X_{0}\in{\mathscr{F}}_{0}
.

Remark 1.3
.

This result fully answers question (
Q
) for the MVSDE (
1.2
) with
\alpha\in(0,2)
.

Remark 1.4
.

Let us now return to the original question (
Q
) for the SDE (
1.1
). This case is significantly more challenging than the McKean–Vlasov setting, as there is no mechanism to transfer regularity from
\mu_{X_{t}}
to the drift term
K*\mu_{X_{t}}
(see (
1.7
) below). In fact, even in the Brownian motion case (
\alpha=2
), question (
Q
) is only solved for
\beta\geqslant 0
(with
\beta=0
interpreted as
{\mathbf{C}}^{0}=L^{\infty}
). When
\beta<0
, strong well-posedness is only known in one dimension, and only for
\beta>-\tfrac{1}{2}
(see
[
3
]
). For
\beta>-1
, only weak well-posedness is known (see
[
13
,
9
]
).

Scaling analysis:

Let
X
be a solution to the SDE

{\mathord{{\rm d}}}X_{t}=B(t,X_{t}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}t+{\mathord{{\rm d}}}L_{t},

where
B\in L^{q}({\mathbb{R}}_{+};{\mathbf{C}}^{\beta})
for some
q\in[1,\infty]
and
\beta\in{\mathbb{R}}
.
For any
\lambda>0
, define the rescaled processes and drift by

X_{t}^{\lambda}:=\lambda^{-1}X_{\lambda^{\alpha}t},\ L_{t}^{\lambda}:=\lambda^{-1}L^{(\alpha)}_{\lambda^{\alpha}t},\ B_{\lambda}(x):=\lambda^{\alpha-1}B(\lambda^{\alpha}t,\lambda x).

Formally, the rescaled process
X^{\lambda}
satisfies the equation

\mathrm{d}X_{t}^{\lambda}=B_{\lambda}(t,X_{t}^{\lambda})\,\mathrm{d}t+\mathrm{d}L_{t}^{\lambda}.

Moreover, the scaling behavior of the drift in the
L^{q}({\mathbb{R}}_{+};{\mathbf{C}}^{-\beta})
norm satisfies

\|B_{\lambda}\|_{L^{q}({\mathbb{R}}_{+};{\mathbf{C}}^{\beta})}\approx\lambda^{\alpha-1-\frac{\alpha}{q}+\beta}\|B\|_{L^{q}({\mathbb{R}}_{+};{\mathbf{C}}^{\beta})}.

As
\lambda\to 0
, this leads to a classification of three scaling regimes based on the behavior of the norm:

\displaystyle\text{\it Scaling Subcritical: }\tfrac{\alpha}{q}+\beta<\alpha-1;

(1.3)

and

\text{\it Scaling Critical: }\tfrac{\alpha}{q}+\beta=\alpha-1;\quad\text{\it Scaling Supercritical: }\tfrac{\alpha}{q}+\beta>\alpha-1.

In the subcritical regime, we have
\lim_{\lambda\to 0}\|B_{\lambda}\|_{L^{q}({\mathbb{R}}_{+};{\mathbf{C}}^{\beta})}=0
, implying that the rescaled drift becomes negligible, and thus the solution behaves like the driving noise:
X\approx L
.

Notably, when
B(t,x)=B(x)
, there exists a well-known counterexample due to
[
18
]
, which shows that in one dimension, for any
\beta\in(0,1-\alpha)
(i.e., in the supercritical regime), there exists a drift
b\in{\mathbf{C}}^{-\beta}
, for which both pathwise uniqueness and uniqueness in law fail for SDE (
1.1
).

Sketch of the proof of Theorems
1.2
:

For two solutions
X^{1}
and
X^{2}
to (
1.2
) with the same
\alpha
-stable process
L
and initial data
X^{1}_{0}=X^{2}_{0}
,
{\mathbb{P}}
-a.s. By the well-known weak well-posedness, we have
\mu_{X_{t}^{1}}=\mu_{X_{t}^{2}}
. Hence we only need to construct a unique strong solution to the SDE:

\displaystyle{\mathord{{\rm d}}}X_{t}=B(t,X_{t}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}t+{\mathord{{\rm d}}}L_{t},

(1.4)

where
B(t,x):=(K*\mu_{X}^{1})(t,x)=(K*\mu_{X}^{2})(t,x)
.

Based on the Yamada-Watanabe theorem, to show the strong well-posedness, we only need to show the pathwise uniqueness of the SDE (
1.4
). We note that

\|B\|_{L^{\infty}_{T}{\mathbf{C}}^{\beta}}\lesssim\|K\|_{L^{\infty}_{T}{\mathbf{C}}^{\beta}}

with the same parameter
\beta>1-\alpha
, with which we cannot show the pathwise uniqueness directly for the SDE (
1.4
). However, the time marginal law
\mu_{t}:=\mu_{X^{1}_{t}}
satisfies the following nonlinear Fokker-Planck equation (FPE):

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mu_{t}=\Delta^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\mu_{t}-\mathord{{\rm div}}((K*\mu_{t})\mu_{t}).

(1.5)

It is well-known that under condition (
Hβ
),
\mu_{t}
admits a density
\rho_{t}
with respect to the Lebesgue measure (see
[
4
,
10
]
). By the scaling, it is expected that we can have for any
\varepsilon>0
,

\displaystyle\|\rho_{t}\|_{{\mathbf{B}}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}-\varepsilon}_{1,\infty}}\lesssim t^{-\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\varepsilon}{\alpha}}\in L^{2}[0,T],

(1.6)

for any
T>0
. Then the convolution inequality (see (
2.4
) below) yields that

\displaystyle\|B\|_{L^{2}_{T}{\mathbf{C}}^{\beta+\frac{\alpha}{2}-\varepsilon}}=\|K*\rho\|_{L^{2}([0,T];{\mathbf{C}}^{\beta+\frac{\alpha}{2}-\varepsilon})}\lesssim\left(\int_{0}^{T}\|\rho_{t}\|_{{\mathbf{B}}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}-\varepsilon}_{1,\infty}}^{2}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}t\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\|K\|_{L^{\infty}_{T}{\mathbf{C}}^{\beta}}<\infty.

(1.7)

For sufficiently small
\varepsilon>0
, we have
\gamma:=\beta+\frac{\alpha}{2}-\varepsilon>1-\frac{\alpha}{2}
under the assumption
\beta>1-\alpha
.
The condition
\gamma>1-\tfrac{\alpha}{2}
allows us to apply the classical approach based on Zvonkin’s transformation (see
[
8
,
17
,
10
]
). The key step is to derive suitable estimates for the solution
u
of the backward Kolmogorov equation

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u+\Delta^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}u+B\cdot\nabla u+B=0,\quad u(T)=0,

in particular to control

\|u\|_{L^{2}_{T}{\mathbf{C}}^{1+\frac{\alpha}{2}}}
. This quantity can be bounded by
\|u\|_{L^{2}_{T}{\mathbf{C}}^{\alpha+\gamma}}
, which follows from the Schauder theory.

Moreover, the assumption
B\in L^{2}_{T}{\mathbf{C}}^{\gamma}
remains in the scaling-subcritical regime (
1.3
), since

\tfrac{\alpha}{2}-\gamma=\tfrac{\alpha}{2}-(1-\tfrac{\alpha}{2}+\varepsilon)=\alpha-1-\varepsilon<\alpha-1.

Thus, it is natural that pathwise uniqueness for (
1.4
) can be obtained under such a condition on
B
. In fact, a rigorous proof of pathwise uniqueness for
B\in L^{2}_{T}{\mathbf{C}}^{\gamma}
with
\gamma>1-\tfrac{\alpha}{2}
has already been given in
[
19
]
. Therefore, the main task is to prove (
1.6
).

When
\alpha>1
, the estimates in (
1.6
) were obtained in
[
11
]
, which directly yields Theorem
1.2
.

For the case
\alpha<1
, which is called the supercritical case, it becomes a bit difficult since in this case the diffusion part
\Delta^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\mu_{t}
is weaker than the conservation term
\mathord{{\rm div}}((K*\mu_{t})\mu_{t})
. In the paper, we would like to use the energy method in
[
8
,
17
]
which was used to estimates the solution to corresponding PDE with the supercritical case
\alpha\in(0,1)
. However, we need the
L^{1}
-estimates for the density with block operator
{\mathcal{R}}_{j}\rho_{t}
, which can not be obtained with energy method, which is available for
L^{p}
-estimates with
p\geqslant 2
(see
[
8
, Theorem 3.3]
). To this end, we consider the following non-divergence form PDE with any fixed
t\geqslant 0
:

\displaystyle\partial_{s}u^{t}(s)=\Delta^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}u^{t}(s)+(K(t-s)*\rho_{t-s})\cdot\nabla u^{t}(s),\quad u^{t}(0)=\varphi\in{\mathbf{B}}^{-\delta}_{\infty,\infty}

with some
\delta>0
.
Applying Itô’s formula to
s\to u^{t}(t-s,X_{s})
, one sees that

\displaystyle\langle\rho_{t},\varphi\rangle={\mathbb{E}}\varphi(X_{t})={\mathbb{E}}u^{t}(t,X_{0}),

which implies that

\displaystyle\|\rho_{t}\|_{{\mathbf{B}}^{\delta}_{1,1}}\lesssim\sup_{\|\varphi\|_{{\mathbf{B}}^{-\delta}_{\infty,\infty}}=1}|\langle\rho_{t},\varphi\rangle|\leqslant\sup_{\|\varphi\|_{{\mathbf{B}}^{-\delta}_{\infty,\infty}}=1}\|u^{t}(t)\|_{\infty}.

Therefore, we only need to show the following estimate, which bypassed the
L^{1}
-estimates:

\displaystyle\|u^{t}(t)\|_{\infty}\lesssim t^{-\frac{\delta}{\alpha}}\|\varphi\|_{{\mathbf{B}}^{-\delta}_{\infty,\infty}}.

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Besov spaces

In this section, we introduce the definition and some basic properties of Besov spaces. Let
{\mathscr{S}}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
be the Schwartz space of all rapidly decreasing functions on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, and
{\mathscr{S}}^{\prime}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
be
the dual space of
{\mathscr{S}}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
called Schwartz generalized function (or tempered distribution) space. Given
f\in{\mathscr{S}}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
,
the Fourier transform
\hat{f}
and the inverse Fourier transform
\check{f}
are defined by

\hat{f}(\xi):=(2\pi)^{-d/2}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\mathrm{e}^{-i\xi\cdot x}f(x){\mathord{{\rm d}}}x,\quad\xi\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d},

\check{f}(x):=(2\pi)^{-d/2}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\mathrm{e}^{i\xi\cdot x}f(\xi){\mathord{{\rm d}}}\xi,\quad x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}.

For every
f\in{\mathscr{S}}^{\prime}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
, the Fourier and the inverse Fourier transform are defined in the following way respectively,

\displaystyle\langle\hat{f},\varphi\rangle:=\langle f,\hat{\varphi}\rangle,\ \ \langle\check{f},\varphi\rangle:=\langle f,\check{\varphi}\rangle,\ \ \forall\varphi\in{\mathscr{S}}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}).

Let
\chi:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to[0,1]
be a radial smooth function with

\displaystyle\chi(\xi)=\begin{cases}1,&\ \ |\xi|\leqslant 1,\\
0,&\ \ |\xi|>3/2.\end{cases}

For
\xi\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, define
\psi(\xi):=\chi(\xi)-\chi(2\xi)
and

\displaystyle\psi_{j}(\xi):=\begin{cases}\chi(2\xi),&\ \ \text{for}\ \ j=-1,\\
\psi(2^{-j}\xi),&\ \ \text{for}\ \ j\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}.\end{cases}

Denote
B_{r}:=\{\xi\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d};|\xi|\leqslant r\}
for
r>0
. It is easy to see that supp
\psi\subset B_{3/2}\backslash B_{1/2}
, and

\displaystyle\sum_{j=-1}^{k}\psi_{j}(\xi)=\chi(2^{-k}\xi)\to 1,\ \ \hbox{as}\ \ k\to\infty.

(2.1)

For
j\geqslant-1
, the block operator
{\mathcal{R}}_{j}
is defined on
{\mathscr{S}}^{\prime}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
by

\displaystyle{\mathcal{R}}_{j}f(x):=(\psi_{j}\hat{f})^{\check{\,}}(x)=\check{\psi}_{j}*f(x).

Now we state the definition of Besov spaces.

Definition 2.1

(Besov spaces)
.

For every
s\in{\mathbb{R}}
and
p,q\in[1,\infty]
, the Besov space
{\mathbf{B}}_{p,q}^{s}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
is defined by

{\mathbf{B}}_{p,q}^{s}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}):=\left\{f\in{\mathscr{S}}^{\prime}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})\,\big|\,\|f\|_{{\mathbf{B}}^{s}_{p,q}}:=\left[\sum_{j\geqslant-1}\left(2^{sj}\|{\mathcal{R}}_{j}f\|_{p}\right)^{q}\right]^{1/q}<\infty\right\}.

If
p=q=\infty
, we denote

{\mathbf{C}}^{s}:={\mathbf{B}}_{\infty,\infty}^{s}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}).

Remark 2.2
.

It is well known that when
s>0
and
s\notin{\mathbb{N}}
,

\displaystyle\|f\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{s}}\asymp\sum_{i=0}^{[s]}\|\nabla^{i}f\|_{\infty}+\sup_{x\neq y}\frac{|\nabla^{[s]}f(x)-\nabla^{[s]}f(y)|}{|x-y|^{s-[s]}},

which is the classical Hölder norm, where
[s]
is the integer part of
s
. We refer to
[
21
, Remark 3.4]
for more details.

Recall the following Bernstein’s inequality (cf.
[
2
, Lemma 2.1]
and
[
8
, Lemma 2.2]
).

Lemma 2.3

(Bernstein’s inequality)
.

For each
k\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
, there is a constant
c=c(d,k)>0
such that for all
j\geqslant-1
and
1\leqslant p_{1}\leqslant p_{2}\leqslant\infty
,

\displaystyle\|\nabla^{k}{\mathcal{R}}_{j}f\|_{p_{2}}\lesssim_{c}2^{(k+d(\frac{1}{p_{1}}-\frac{1}{p_{2}}))j}\|{\mathcal{R}}_{j}f\|_{p_{1}},

(2.2)

and for some
c=c(d,\alpha,p_{1})>0

\displaystyle\|\Delta^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}{\mathcal{R}}_{j}f\|_{p_{2}}\lesssim_{c}2^{(\alpha+d(\frac{1}{p_{1}}-\frac{1}{p_{2}}))j}\|{\mathcal{R}}_{j}f\|_{p_{1}}.

(2.3)

Apart from the classical Bernstein inequality, there are also more general versions for

\|\Delta^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}(|{\mathcal{R}}_{j}f|^{\frac{p}{2}})\|_{2},\quad\text{and}\quad\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}|{\mathcal{R}}_{j}f|^{p-2}{\mathcal{R}}_{j}f\Delta^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}{\mathcal{R}}_{j}f,\quad p\in[2,\infty),

as established in
[
6
]
and
[
15
, Lemma 3.2]
. Here, we introduce a frequency-localized maximum principle corresponding to the case
p=\infty
(see
[
17
, Lemma 4.7]
and
[
20
, Lemma 3.4]
).

Lemma 2.4
.

There is a constant
c_{0}>0
such that for any
j\geqslant 0
and
u\in L^{1}
,
{\mathcal{R}}_{j}u\in C_{0}
, and

\inf_{x\in J({\mathcal{R}}_{j}u)}\left\{\mbox{\rm sgn}({\mathcal{R}}_{j}u(x))\cdot\left(-\Delta^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}{\mathcal{R}}_{j}u(x)\right)\right\}\geqslant c_{0}2^{\alpha j}\|{\mathcal{R}}_{j}u\|_{\infty},

where
J({\mathcal{R}}_{j}u):=\{x:|{\mathcal{R}}_{j}u(x)|=\|{\mathcal{R}}_{j}u\|_{\infty}\}
.

Proof.

Since
u\in L^{1}
,
{\mathcal{R}}_{j}u
is a bounded continuous function. Its Fourier transform is supported in
\{(1/2)2^{j}<|\xi|<(3/2)2^{j}\}
and the Riemann-Lebesgue lemma implies
\lim_{x\to\infty}|{\mathcal{R}}_{j}u(x)|=0
. Thus, the proof follows directly from
[
20
, Lemma 3.4]
(see also
[
17
, Lemma 4.7]
).
∎

The following convolution inequality is well-known (see
[
11
, (2.13)]
for example).

Lemma 2.5
.

For any
\beta,\beta_{1},\beta_{2}\in{\mathbb{R}}
and
p,p_{1},p_{2}\in[1,\infty]
with

\displaystyle 1+\tfrac{1}{p}=\tfrac{1}{p_{1}}+\tfrac{1}{p_{2}},

it holds that

\displaystyle\|f*g\|_{{\mathbf{B}}^{\beta}_{p,\infty}}\leqslant 5\|f\|_{{\mathbf{B}}^{\beta_{1}}_{p_{1},\infty}}\|g\|_{{\mathbf{B}}^{\beta_{2}}_{p_{2},\infty}}.

(2.4)

We also introduce the following commutator estimate, which is proven in
[
8
, Lemma 2.3]
. Here for two operators
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
, we use
[T_{1},T_{2}]:=T_{1}T_{2}-T_{2}T_{1}
to denote the commutator.

Lemma 2.6
.

Let
p,p_{1},p_{2},q_{1},q_{2}\in[1,\infty]
satisfying
\frac{1}{p}=\frac{1}{p_{1}}+\frac{1}{p_{2}}

and
\frac{1}{q_{1}}+\frac{1}{q_{2}}=1
. Then for any
s_{1}\in(0,1)
and
s_{2}\in[-s_{1},0]

there exists a constant
C=C(d,p,p_{1},p_{2},s_{1},s_{2})>0
such that

\|[{\mathcal{R}}_{j},f]g\|_{p}\leqslant C2^{-j(s_{1}+s_{2})}\begin{cases}\|f\|_{B_{p_{1},\infty}^{s_{1}}}\|g\|_{p_{2}},&\kappa_{2}=0,\\
\|f\|_{B_{p_{1},\infty}^{s_{1}}}\|g\|_{B_{p_{2},\infty}^{s_{2}}},&s_{1}+s_{2}>0,\\
\|f\|_{B_{p_{1},q_{1}}^{s_{1}}}\|g\|_{B_{p_{2},q_{2}}^{s_{2}}},&s_{1}+s_{2}=0.\end{cases}

(2.5)

2.2.
\alpha
-stable processes

Fix
\alpha\in(0,2)
.
Let
(L_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
be a rotationally invariant and symmetric
\alpha
-stable process
whose generator is the fractional Laplacian

\Delta^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\phi(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(\phi(x+z)-\phi(x)-z^{(\alpha)}\cdot\nabla\phi(x)\right)\nu({\mathord{{\rm d}}}z),\quad\phi\in{\mathscr{S}}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),

(2.6)

where
z^{(\alpha)}:=z{\mathbbm{1}}_{\{\alpha\in[1,2)\}}{\mathbbm{1}}_{\{|z|\leqslant 1\}}
and the Lévy measure

\nu({\mathord{{\rm d}}}z)=\frac{c_{d,\alpha}}{|z|^{d+\alpha}}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}z

with some specific constant
c_{d,\alpha}>0
.
Let
N({\mathord{{\rm d}}}s,{\mathord{{\rm d}}}z)
be the associated Poisson random measure defined by

N((0,t]\times A):=\sum_{s\in[0,t]}{\mathbbm{1}}_{A}\left(L_{s}-L_{s-}\right)

for all
A\in{\mathscr{B}}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\{0\})
and
t>0
. Then it follows from Lévy–Itô’s
decomposition that

L_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{|z|\leqslant 1}z\widetilde{N}({\mathord{{\rm d}}}s,{\mathord{{\rm d}}}z)+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{|z|>1}zN({\mathord{{\rm d}}}s,{\mathord{{\rm d}}}z),

where
\widetilde{N}({\mathord{{\rm d}}}s,{\mathord{{\rm d}}}z):=N({\mathord{{\rm d}}}s,{\mathord{{\rm d}}}z)-\nu({\mathord{{\rm d}}}z){\mathord{{\rm d}}}s
is
the compensated Poisson random measure.

3.
Pathwise uniqueness for SDEs

In this section, we consider the following SDE:

\displaystyle{\mathord{{\rm d}}}X_{t}=B(t,X_{t}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}t+{\mathord{{\rm d}}}L_{t},

(3.1)

where
B\in L^{q}_{T}{\mathbf{C}}^{\gamma}
with some
\gamma>1-\frac{\alpha}{2}
and
q>1
.

The following result is from
[
19
, Theorem 1.1-(iii)]
.

Theorem 3.1
.

Assume
\alpha\in(0,2)
. Let
T>0
and
B\in L^{q}_{T}{\mathbf{C}}^{\gamma}
with some
\gamma>1-\frac{\alpha}{2}
and

\displaystyle\tfrac{\alpha}{q}-\gamma<\alpha-1.

(3.2)

Then for any initial data
X_{0}\in{\mathscr{F}}_{0}
, there is a unique strong solution to (
3.1
) on
[0,T]
. Moreover, the pathwise uniqueness holds.

For any

4.
Regularity of the time marginal law

In this section, we consider the nonlinear FPE (
1.5
). Under assumption (
Hβ
) with
\beta>1-\alpha
, it is well known (see, e.g.,
[
11
,
10
]
) that for any
t\geqslant 0
, the measure
\mu_{t}
admits a density with respect to the Lebesgue measure, denoted by
\rho_{t}
. The density
\rho_{t}
satisfies the nonlinear FPE:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho_{t}=\Delta^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\rho_{t}-\operatorname{div}\big((K*\rho_{t})\rho_{t}\big).

(4.1)

Our goal in this section is to establish the following regularity estimate for
\rho_{t}
under various assumptions and for different ranges of
\delta\geqslant 0
:

\displaystyle\|\rho_{t}\|_{{\mathbf{B}}^{\delta}_{1,\infty}}\leqslant Ct^{-\frac{\delta}{\alpha}}.

(4.2)

We now state the main result in this section:

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
\alpha\in(0,2)
and
T>0
. Assume that condition (
Hβ
) holds with
\beta>1-\alpha
. Then:

(i)

If
\alpha\in(1,2)
, then for any
\delta\geqslant 0
, there exists a constant
C>0
such that estimate (
4.2
) holds for all
t\in(0,T]
.

(ii)

If
\alpha\in(0,1]
, then for any
\delta\in[0,\alpha)
, there exists a constant
C>0
such that (
4.2
) holds for all
t\in(0,T]
.

Proof.

Since (
4.2
) is trivial when
\delta=0
, we consider only the case
\delta>0
in what follows.

(i) If
\alpha\in(1,2)
, then the result follows directly from
[
11
, Theorem 1.9, (B)-(i)]
.

(ii) Let
\alpha\in(0,1]
and
\beta>1-\alpha\geqslant 0
. For any
t\in(0,T]
and
\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{0}
, we consider the following PDE:

\displaystyle\partial_{s}u^{t}(s)=\Delta^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}u^{t}(s)+B(t-s)\cdot\nabla u^{t}(s),\quad u^{t}(0)=\varphi,

(4.3)

where
B(t):=K(t)*\rho_{t}
.
Taking
X
be the unique weak solution to (
1.2
), applying Itô’s formula to the process
s\to u(t-s,X_{s})
, we have

\displaystyle{\mathord{{\rm d}}}u^{t}(t-s,X_{s})=

\displaystyle\left(-\partial_{s}u^{t}(t-s)-\Delta^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}u^{t}(t-s)+B(s)\cdot\nabla u^{t}(t-s)\right)(X_{s}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}s

\displaystyle+\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}u(t-s,X_{s-}+z)-u(t-s,X_{s-})\tilde{N}({\mathord{{\rm d}}}s,{\mathord{{\rm d}}}z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}u(t-s,X_{s-}+z)-u(t-s,X_{s-})\tilde{N}({\mathord{{\rm d}}}s,{\mathord{{\rm d}}}z),

which implies that

\displaystyle\langle\varphi,\rho_{t}\rangle={\mathbb{E}}\varphi(X_{t})={\mathbb{E}}u^{t}(0,X_{t})={\mathbb{E}}u^{t}(t,X_{0}).

(4.4)

Then, based on
[
2
, Proposition 2.76]
,
we have

\displaystyle\|\rho_{t}\|_{{\mathbf{B}}^{\delta}_{1,\infty}}\leqslant\|\rho_{t}\|_{{\mathbf{B}}^{\delta}_{1,1}}\lesssim\sup_{\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{0}}\frac{|\langle\varphi,\rho_{t}\rangle|}{\|\varphi\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{-\delta}}}\leqslant\sup_{\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{0}}\frac{\|u^{t}(t)\|_{\infty}}{\|\varphi\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{-\delta}}}.

(4.5)

Thus, to show (
4.2
), we only need to give

\displaystyle\|u^{t}(t)\|_{\infty}\lesssim t^{-\frac{\delta}{\alpha}}\|\varphi\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{-\delta}},

(4.6)

for any
\delta\in(0,\alpha)
. To establish (
4.6
), by a standard argument, we may assume
K\in L^{\infty}_{T}C^{\infty}_{b}
and show that (
4.6
) holds with a constant depending only on
c_{K}:=\|K\|_{L^{\infty}_{T}{\mathbf{C}}^{\beta}}
.

Applying
{\mathcal{R}}_{j}
to act both sides of (
4.3
), we have

\displaystyle\partial_{s}{\mathcal{R}}_{j}u^{t}

\displaystyle=\Delta^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}{\mathcal{R}}_{j}u^{t}+{\mathcal{R}}_{j}(B(t-s)\cdot\nabla u^{t})

\displaystyle=\Delta^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}{\mathcal{R}}_{j}u^{t}+B(t-s)\cdot\nabla{\mathcal{R}}_{j}u^{t}+[{\mathcal{R}}_{j},B(t-s)]\cdot\nabla u^{t}.

Since
B\in L^{\infty}_{T}{\mathbf{C}}^{\infty}_{b}
and
\varphi\in L^{1}
, it is well-known that
u\in L^{\infty}_{T}L^{1}
(see
[
11
, Lemma C.1]
). Based on Lemma
2.4
,
{\mathcal{R}}_{j}u^{t}(s)\in C_{0}
, and we let
x_{s,j}
be the point such that
{\mathcal{R}}_{j}u^{t}(s)
reaches its
L^{\infty}
-norm. Without loss of generality, we may assume that
{\mathcal{R}}_{j}u^{t}(s,x_{s,j})>0
. Otherwise, we can consider the equation for
-u^{t}
.
Then Lemma
2.4
yields that for
j\geqslant 0
,

\displaystyle\Delta^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}{\mathcal{R}}_{j}u^{t}(s,x_{s,j})\leqslant-c_{0}2^{\alpha j}\|{\mathcal{R}}_{j}u^{t}(s)\|_{\infty}.

Moreover, for
j=-1

2.3

\displaystyle\Delta^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}{\mathcal{R}}_{-1}u^{t}(s,x_{s,j})\leqslant\|\Delta^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}{\mathcal{R}}_{-1}u^{t}(s)\|_{\infty}\leqslant c_{1}\|{\mathcal{R}}_{-1}u^{t}(s)\|_{\infty}

with some constant
c_{1}=c_{1}(d,\alpha)>0

Noting that
B(t-s,x_{s,j})\cdot\nabla{\mathcal{R}}_{j}u^{t}(s,x_{s,j})=0

\displaystyle\partial_{s}{\mathcal{R}}_{j}u^{t}(s,x_{s,j})\leqslant\left(-c_{0}2^{\alpha j}+c_{1}\right)\|{\mathcal{R}}_{j}u^{t}(s)\|_{\infty}+\|[{\mathcal{R}}_{j},B(t-s)]\cdot\nabla u^{t}(s)\|_{\infty}.

Thus, in view of
[
20

\displaystyle\partial_{s}\|{\mathcal{R}}_{j}u^{t}(s)\|_{\infty}=\partial_{s}{\mathcal{R}}_{j}u^{t}(s,x_{s,j})\lesssim(1-2^{\alpha j})\|{\mathcal{R}}_{j}u^{t}(s)\|_{\infty}+\|[B(t-s),{\mathcal{R}}_{j}]\nabla u^{t}(s)\|_{\infty}.

(4.7)

8

\displaystyle\|[B(t-s),{\mathcal{R}}_{j}]\nabla u^{t}(s)\|_{\infty}\lesssim\|B(t-s)\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{\beta+\vartheta}}\|\nabla u^{t}(s)\|_{{\mathbf{B}}^{-\beta-\vartheta}_{\infty,1}}\lesssim\|B(t-s)\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{\beta+\vartheta}}\|u^{t}(s)\|_{{\mathbf{B}}^{1-\beta-\vartheta}_{\infty,1}},

which by applying Gronwall’s inequality to (

\displaystyle\|{\mathcal{R}}_{j}u^{t}(s)\|_{\infty}\lesssim e^{-c2^{-\alpha j}s}\|{\mathcal{R}}_{j}\varphi\|_{\infty}+\int_{0}^{s}e^{-c2^{-\alpha j}(s-r)}\|B(t-r)\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{\beta+\vartheta}}\|u^{t}(r)\|_{{\mathbf{B}}^{1-\beta-\vartheta}_{\infty,1}}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}r.

(4.8)

Then, noting that

\displaystyle e^{-x}\lesssim_{\theta}x^{-\theta},\quad\text{for $x>0$,}

(4.9)

for any
\delta>0
and
\theta>1-\beta-\vartheta

\displaystyle 2^{j\theta}\|{\mathcal{R}}_{j}u^{t}(s)\|_{\infty}\lesssim s^{-\frac{\theta+\delta}{\alpha}}2^{-\delta j}\|{\mathcal{R}}_{j}\varphi\|_{\infty}+\int_{0}^{s}(s-r)^{-\frac{\theta}{\alpha}}\|B(t-r)\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{\beta+\vartheta}}\|u^{t}(r)\|_{{\mathbf{B}}^{1-\beta-\vartheta}_{\infty,1}}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}r,

(4.10)

which, after taking the supremum over
j

\displaystyle\|u^{t}(s)\|_{{\mathbf{B}}^{1-\beta-\vartheta}_{\infty,1}}\lesssim\|u^{t}(s)\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{\theta}}\lesssim s^{-\frac{\theta+\delta}{\alpha}}\|\varphi\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{-\delta}}+\int_{0}^{s}(s-r)^{-\frac{\theta}{\alpha}}\|B(t-r)\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{\beta+\vartheta}}\|u^{t}(r)\|_{{\mathbf{B}}^{1-\beta-\vartheta}_{\infty,1}}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}r.

Now, we prove (

(Step 1)

(Step 2)

(Step 3)

Finally, we conclude that (

(Step 1):
We let
\vartheta=0

\displaystyle\|B(t-r)\|_{{\mathbf{C}}^{\beta+\vartheta}}\lesssim\|K\|_{L^{\infty}_{T}{\mathbf{C}}^{\beta}}\|\rho_{t-r}\|_{{\mathbf{B}}^{0}_{1,\infty}}\lesssim c_{K},

\displaystyle\begin{split}\|{\mathcal{R}}_{j}u^{t}(t)\|_{\infty}&\lesssim\|\varphi\|_{{\mathbf{B}}^{-\delta}_{\infty,\infty}}\left(e^{-c2^{-\alpha j}t}2^{\delta j}+\int_{0}^{t}e^{-c2^{-\alpha j}(t-r)}r^{-\frac{\theta+\delta}{\alpha}}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}r\right)\\

(Step 2):

5.
Proof of Theorem
1.2

Since the weak uniqueness holds for

(see
[
4
]
for

[

]

) with

Based on (

, for any
