“Can Everyone Hear Me?” Lessons on Educating Post-2020
Authors: Kathryn Knoop
Published: 2026

The initial phases of the COVID-19 pandemic brought with it many changes to education and research. As most institutions switched to virtual classroom settings, faculty and students alike quickly realized how ill-equipped the standard classroom styles were for a virtual setting. Faculty and learners felt disconnected, isolated, and unheard. Learners had little connection with the material, whereas faculty could not understand why learners were unwilling to unmute and join in the discussion. Virtual options are now a part of our learning environments as an alternative attendance option; therefore, it is important to reflect on effective teaching strategies.

Beyond the initial difficulties in abruptly switching to virtual learning, the resulting shifts in education, particularly at the undergraduate level, have altered students’ expectations of learning environments. Generational differences in learning styles are becoming increasingly apparent as incoming graduate cohorts shift from Millennials to Gen Z, who increasingly prioritize flexibility and immediacy in their education outcomes. Research faculty are rarely taught pedagogy and are unlikely to keep up with educational trends. Whether you are new to lecturing or wish to refresh your teaching style, adapting standard teaching techniques can be applied to every classroom: virtual or in-person.

Set Learning Objectives

Every lecture should start and end with the learning objectives covered in the lesson. This is the most efficient way to increase engagement in lectures—make it clear what is being taught. Whether the point of the class is to “recognize how somatic hypermutations increase antibody diversity,” “understand how the epithelium regenerates in barrier function,” or “learn how to read and evaluate scientific papers,” learning objectives should be obvious and deliberate to both the instructor and the learners. To avoid overloading the lecture, consider 3 learning objectives for a standard class. The “rule of 3” is a classic structuring principle that proposes a trio of items creates a satisfying sense of completion with the audience. This constructs a simple pattern that supports memorization. Additionally, the short-term memory retains around 4 to 7 items, so 3 key learning objectives combined with a few extra details will allow for a balanced class.

When setting a learning objective, most educational learning modules will emphasize setting SMART learning objectives: Specific, Measurable, Attainable, Relevant, and Time-bound.1 Specificity and relevance are straightforward; stay on topic, teach the material, and only dive into the weeds when students ask for more detail. Attainable and time-bound require a bit of thought; consider what is an appropriate amount of material to cover during class and what are the 3 most important points on that topic. Setting a measurable objective is equally important. How will you evaluate what the students learned? Whether evaluating comprehension through an exam question, an open-ended writing prompt, or discussion participation, ensure that you are providing the students with the tools to display what they learned. Emphasizing what the learners are expected to know (and what they will be held accountable for) immediately engages more attention and builds trust with students.

Use New Tools

Lectures are the most straightforward way for an instructor to share information, but remember, the purpose of education is for the students to learn, not for a lecturer to talk. With every class, I have increasingly asked myself how I can move beyond a didactic format to encourage knowledge retention and learner engagement. The teaching methodology should reflect and support the learning objective. Although discussions promote learner interactions, we are all painfully aware of how quiet it can be when no one wants to unmute and ask a question during class. The expansion of virtual classrooms advanced the use of interactive tools, such as Slido or Kahoot, that offer live polls, quizzes, and open question-and-answer sessions. These tools or games may seem silly but consider that asking 1 student within a class to answer a question out loud only engages 1 student at a time. Posing a question to all students provides broad engagement and reinforces the learning objectives for everyone. I have received really positive feedback from incorporating quizzes, case studies, polls, and open question-and-answer sessions through Slido. This tool integrates seamlessly with my slides and allows students to join from their own devices, which is especially effective at bridging the in-person and virtual gap. To avoid any technical difficulties, be sure you test out the tools, platforms, and room technology that you intend to use. It can seem like technology is always advancing, but technical glitches can take away from an otherwise stellar classroom experience, reducing learner’s engagement.

Be Inclusive

The shift to online classrooms highlighted a variety of reasons why learners may need or prefer to attend class virtually. Inclusivity can acknowledge different physical locations, different learning styles, and different accommodations or abilities. Before a class session, check your slide presentation. Is the amount of text appropriate? Your class may include those who prefer text and those who learn through visual aids. Those who have difficulty hearing or following along to the spoken word may benefit from text that directs their attention. Is your font choice easy to read? Sans serif fonts are easier to read for those with dyslexia and can make a big difference to those who need it. Are your figures blurry or too busy? Are the graphics easy to discern for those who may be color-blind?

During the class, all learners should be treated equally and provided with the same learning experience. Hybrid teaching can be taxing on the lecturer’s attention span, jumping from those in person to those attending virtually to the chat and back again. Easy steps like pausing periodically, checking the chat, asking for questions, deliberately inviting those attending virtually to add to the discussion can help all learners feel included, which again helps build trust. Teaching in an inclusive environment requires adapting to each session and being prepared to pivot. Students may ask a question or raise a comment that feels wildly off-topic to what you want to discuss; however, respecting the students includes appreciating what they want to learn at that moment.

Prepare

For every hour of class, I tend to invest 2 to 3 hours of preparation, which is, admittedly, a significant cost not everyone can incur. Learning objectives are the first step in preparing any coursework, followed by making sure these objectives align with the content and teaching methodology. As higher-level learning moves beyond standard lectures, evaluate the content and ask if anything needs to be updated or reimagined. Are students only expected to recall information, or are you including enough tools for them to move beyond a fundamental understanding to higher-order thinking including applying and analyzing, or even evaluating and creating? The Bloom’s taxonomy of cognitive levels is especially important at the graduate level as we encourage our students to analyze data, evaluate research, and create new avenues of thought and study.2,3

Empowering learners to evaluate, apply, and analyze is typically done in discussion-based class or journal club–style courses. When preparing to teach a journal club and lead students through a discussion, ask yourself if the papers truly reflect the best way to discuss a concept. Once you are confident in your paper selection, create discussion points to lead the way. If the objective of the class is to get students to evaluate, apply, and analyze, they first must be taught to do so. It is up to the teacher to create the opportunity for students to analyze reported data, evaluate interpretations, and apply concepts they learned elsewhere to the experiments in a paper. Flag techniques used, summarize the interpretations of the figures, and craft prompts that ask the students if the findings are supported by the data. A good discussion-based class should allow the students to move beyond learning “what we know” to understand “how do we know what we know.” Guiding a thoughtful discussion will allow students to feel engaged and interact in the discussion by considering the prompts you have prepared.

Learning How to Teach

My route to teaching is familiar for academic researchers. I was a teaching assistant as a graduate student, but then largely forgot teaching existed until I became an Assistant Professor. However, being forced into a completely virtual platform compelled me to become a better teacher. I sought out courses, seminars, and workshops on how to effectively teach. If pedagogy interests you, consider looking into didactic training sessions on how to teach effectively. Your institution may offer them as part of career development or faculty engagement series. Alternatively, Coursera is a virtual platform that offers courses on a variety of topics including teaching. It can be difficult to carve out additional time from our schedules that are already stretched thin, but setting aside time to think about the topics discussed here can improve not only your teaching style in the classroom, but also your communication skills to broader audiences.

The scientist in me had no idea how important education would become to my self-identity as a research faculty. However, knowing the students recognize the contribution I provide to their education is incredibly fulfilling. There is not enough room in a lab to mentor all the students, but education in the classroom can have just as significant an impact on the developing scientists program-wide.
