Feasibility of optimizing muscle activation of individuals with spinal cord injuries to reduce caregiver effort in assisted transfers
Authors: Asif Arefeen, Kevin Foglyano, Lisa M Lombardo, Musa L Audu, Ronald J Triolo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSpinal cord injury (SCI) significantly affects the quality of life of affected individuals and their caregivers. Assisted transfers after SCI are physically demanding and one of the leading causes of musculoskeletal injuries among caregivers. While mechanical lift systems can reduce injury risk, their adoption is limited by high costs, large size, and portability. Functional neuromuscular stimulation (FNS) presents a promising alternative for facilitating transfers by activating the paralyzed muscles below the level of injury. However, determining the correct and optimal stimulation profiles during transfers is challenging with heuristic methods alone.MethodsTo address this, we utilized a three-dimensional musculoskeletal model in OpenSim to estimate muscle activation profiles for knee and hip extension during an assisted partial sit-to-stand (STS) maneuver of an individual with SCI by running predictive and inverse dynamics simulations with OpenSim Moco tool. To verify the simulation results, an open-loop controller delivered the predicted neural stimulation to two individuals with SCI (injury level, T10 and T8) during the STS portion of assisted transfer activities with a single physical therapist acting as a caregiver for consistency. The caregiver was able to complete the partial STS with stimulation, but could not complete the prescribed lifting maneuver without activation of the paralyzed muscles. The ground reaction forces and kinematic data from the experiment (with and without stimulation) were used in the OpenSim MocoInverse tool for inverse dynamics computation to estimate joint reaction forces of the caregiver model.ResultsOptimized stimulation assisted knee and hip extension of the participants with SCI reduced the predicted joint reaction forces experienced by the caregiver, significantly decreasing peak total shoulder joint reaction forces magnitudes by 23.8% (T10) and 33.2% (T8). For one participant (T8), predicted hip and lumbar spine joint reaction force magnitudes of the caregiver were reduced by 27.5%, and 35.3%, respectively.ConclusionsThe results indicate that optimizing neural stimulation profiles for individuals with SCI to facilitate assisted transfers is feasible and has the potential to reduce the physical effort and therefore the likelihood of injurious joint loads experienced by the caregiver.Keywords: Functional neuromuscular stimulation (FNS), Spinal cord injury (SCI), Musculoskeletal modeling, and Optimal control

Background

Spinal cord injury (SCI) affects approximately 18,000 new individuals annually in the U.S., with the majority occurring in young adults aged 16–30, who typically have a normal lifespan after injury [1]. Despite advances in acute care, individuals with SCI often live with permanent motor impairments, requiring lifelong assistance for daily activities such as transfers between the wheelchair and other surfaces. For individuals with cervical to mid-thoracic injuries, these dependent transfers are typically performed either entirely by or with the assistance of caregivers who are at significant risk for musculoskeletal injuries due to the physical demands of the maneuver [2, 3]. The highest rates of occupational injury occur during manual patient handling, contributing to over $20 billion in annual costs [4]. Current solutions such as mechanical lift systems can reduce injury risk, but carry significant disadvantages related to high cost, bulk and inconvenience, and spatial requirements [5, 6]. Therefore, they remain largely confined to hospitals, assisted living and long-term care facilities, and specific areas of a dwelling such as the bedroom and bathroom. This leaves a substantial portion of the SCI population, particularly those receiving care at home or non-institutional settings or who do not have strong and healthy personal assistants, without practical access to safe transfer assistance technologies.

Several studies suggest that functional neuromuscular stimulation (FNS) can assist with transfers by precisely activating paralyzed muscles of the user with SCI [7–11], while also providing additional benefits such as muscle conditioning, improved blood flow and relief from excessive seated pressures [12, 13]. While SCI interrupts the neural pathways between the brain and muscles, FNS works by delivering electrical pulses that activate peripheral nerves distal to the level of injury [8]. These peripheral nerves, although disconnected from voluntary control, then generate the action potentials that can elicit contractions in paralyzed muscles. This technology allows for restoration of basic functional movements such as standing, stepping, or trunk stabilization [14–16]. Although FNS has been successful in aiding movement and improving muscle health in individuals with SCI [17–20], its application to assist with dependent transfers to reduce caregiver biomechanical load remains unexplored. Previous studies have focused primarily on user-initiated motion or independent standing-pivot-transfers with little emphasis on real-time caregiver-SCI user interactions during dependent transfers.

Emerging evidence suggests that task-specific FNS may improve transfer performance and reduce the physical demand on caregivers [8–11]. However, no prior studies have developed an in-silico framework to simulate SCI-caregiver biomechanical interactions during assisted transfers, or validated such simulations against experimental measurements. Such a framework would enable the design of optimized FNS strategies that not only support the SCI individual but also allow for a full assessment of potential reduction in joint loading and injury risk in the caregiver.

This study addresses a pressing need for individuals with SCI and their caregivers by establishing the feasibility of an optimized, task-specific FNS system to assist with dependent transfers. The physical strain placed on caregivers during daily transfers remains a major, yet often overlooked, barrier to home-based care and long-term SCI management. This study bridges that gap by attempting to leverage the contractile properties of the paralyzed musculature through task-specific, optimized FNS to reduce caregiver injury risk while promoting safer and more effective transfers. These strategies may not only reduce caregiver burden but also promote the secondary health benefits of exercise and improved mobility that can lead to increased community and societal participation; all without the need for elaborate and bulky transfer devices.

Methods

Model

We developed a combined 9 degree of freedom (DOF) model in the sagittal plane consisting of a skeletal model of the caregiver (grey skeleton, 6 DOF, 66.13 Kg) and a musculoskeletal model of the SCI participant (green skeleton, 3 DOF, 57.65 Kg), modified from the model in [21], as shown in Fig. 1. Both the caregiver and SCI models are planar models. The caregiver model has free DOFs at the ankle, knee, hip, lumbar, shoulder, and elbow joints, whereas the SCI model has free DOFs at the ankle, knee, and hip joints. In the simulations, the maximum joint moment limits for the caregiver model were doubled to account for the contribution of two legs and arms. In both models, the head and neck were fixed with respect to the trunk. The positive directions for all local rotational joints in the caregiver model are clockwise in the plane, whereas in the SCI model they are counterclockwise in the plane. For simplicity, the feet of both the caregiver and SCI models were fixed to the ground using weld joints. Two muscle-tendon actuators typically activated by surface or implanted stimulation were included in the SCI participant model: vasti muscles, combined and treated as a single actuator for knee extension, and the gluteus maximus (GM), responsible for hip extension. Muscle strengths were doubled to represent the combined strength of both legs. A kinematic constraint (WeldConstraints) was required between the caregiver model’s hand and the SCI participant model’s hip joint to form a closed-chain system. Six coordinate (joint moment) actuators were attached to the hip, knee, ankle, lumbar, shoulder, and elbow joints of the caregiver model. No coordinate actuators were applied to the SCI participant model’s hip, knee, and ankle joints; instead, only musculotendon actuators were applied.

9 DOF skeletal model of the caregiver and musculoskeletal model of the SCI participant in OpenSim for predictive and inverse dynamic simulations

We estimated task-specific optimal muscle stimulation profiles for individuals with SCI to assist in the lifting required during dependent transfers using the model and biomechanical simulations in the open-source software OpenSim [22]. The simulations included both caregiver and SCI participant anthropometric data used in [21] and SCI-related muscle parameters to ensure accurate prediction of how muscle activation affects movement during transfers. A two-step simulation approach was used to estimate the optimal muscle activations of the SCI model to assist the model caregiver in generating a partial sit-to-stand (STS) component of the transfer maneuver to represent lifting to an intermediate position less than the user’s full height. In the first step, predictive simulation generated joint angle profiles for the SCI model, and the caregiver model during the partial STS task. In the second step, these predicted results were applied in an inverse dynamics simulation to estimate muscle activation profiles for the SCI model that would minimize caregiver joint loads during partial STS.

Predictive simulation

For predictive simulation (step-1), only the caregiver model joint actuators were used to complete the partial STS task with the SCI participant model remaining passive. The initial and the final poses of the models are shown in Fig. 1. OpenSim MocoStudy tool was utilized to solve this task, which was cast as an optimal control problem (OCP) [21]. Implementation of optimized activations

To verify if the simulation outputs (optimized activations) result in successful partial STS, we conducted a series of controlled experimental trials using two volunteers with SCI and a physical therapist with 28 years of experience who served as a caregiver. The experiments were conducted in the Motion Study Laboratory (MSL) at the Louis Stokes Cleveland Veterans Affairs Medical Center (LSCVAMC). All procedures were reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board at the LSCVAMC. Both SCI participants and the physical therapist provided written informed consent prior to any research related activities. The height and weight of the caregiver were 163 cm and 54.43 Kg, respectively. A summary of each SCI participant’s characteristics, and injury details are provided in Table 1. Stimulation for Subject 1 (S1) was delivered using a custom 8-channel surface stimulator with charge-balanced, biphasic constant-current pulses. For each channel, stimulation amplitude (100 mA) and maximum pulse durations (255 μs) were specified. Bipolar electrodes were placed on the motor points of the vasti and GM to assist with hip and knee extension. These electrodes could be moved over the skin to help locate the optimal position before adhering them to the skin surface. Two electrodes were used for each limb (right and left). For the GM, one electrode was placed on the superior aspect of the muscle, and the second electrode was placed just superior to the gluteal fold. For the vasti, one electrode was placed just above the patella, and the second electrode was placed near the proximal or middle third of the quadriceps muscle. Subject 2 (S2) had previously received an implanted 12-channel stimulator-telemeter [26] neural prosthesis used for standing and exercise [27]. The system received power and command signals via an external control unit and an inductive coil placed on the skin over the implanted stimulator. For this study, nerve cuff electrodes [28] around the femoral nerves () and intramuscular electrodes [29] innervating the posterior portion of the adductor magnus (AM) () were activated to produce knee and hip extension respectively to assist the transfer maneuver. Due to inadequate hip extension of the GM for S2, posterior portion of the AM was recruited as an alternative.

Motion capture data were collected at a sampling rate of 100 Hz, and the ground reaction forces under the feet of the caregiver were collected at a sampling rate of 2500 Hz. The feed-forward open-loop stimulation controller was run on a Speedgoat Real-Time Target computer running Matlab-Simulink. The caregiver was instructed to proceed with the partial STS transfer by lifting the subject as high as possible when the controller button was manually pushed after a count down from three to one from the operator of the Speedgoat Real-Time Controller. The caregiver and subject were instructed to complete the maneuver within two seconds, with stimulation applied for the first second and subsequently ramped down to zero over the next second. Data were also collected during trials without stimulation, in which the caregiver was allowed to decide when to initiate the partial STS transfer but was still asked to attempt to lift the subject as high as possible, even though they could not complete it fully. We conducted 4 trials with stimulation and 4 trials without stimulation in a random order. Caregiver perceived effort (PE) during transfers were evaluated using a seven-point Usability Rating Scale (URS) ranging from very difficult to very easy [30]. The motion capture and force plate data were processed in the Vicon Nexus 2.16 software and Matlab. The data were cropped and aligned with the lifting heights. The SCI participant’s sitting position corresponds to lifting time, and the standing height corresponds to lifting time. For trials in which the caregiver could not complete the partial STS, the maximum standing height achieved by the participant was defined as of the lifting time.

Caregiver model’s joint reaction forces computation

To quantify the mechanical load experienced during the actual transfer tasks and relate them to those imposed on caregiver joints while performing assisted transfers, we scaled only the caregiver OpenSim model based on their actual height and weight. The differences between vertical ground reaction forces (vGRF) and caregiver weight were calculated for both the stimulation and no-stimulation conditions, and these differences were represented computationally by an applied force on the caregiver model elbow joint during the total simulation time. Then, the GRF and applied force data from the experiment were used in the OpenSim MocoInverse tool (inverse dynamics simulation) to estimate caregiver model joint reaction forces at the hip, lumbar and shoulder joints.

Due to limitations in muscle strength of the contracting paralyzed muscles, both the vastus and GM muscles required high activation levels. The upper body (pelvis and proximal segments) of the musculoskeletal model was welded, resulting in a constant trunk flexion maintained throughout the entire lifting process (Fig. 1). Muscle activation sensitivity analysis

For the sensitivity analysis, six different weighting terms ( [0.25 0.5 0.75 1.0 1.25 1.5]) and muscle strength deficit conditions () used in the optimal control problem (Eq. 2) were evaluated. In addition, three subject-specific anthropometric conditions representing different body weights and heights were considered, including the model from [21] and two subjects (S1 and S2). To derive the optimized muscle activation profiles, a total of 108 combinations of these parameter values were generated for the inverse dynamics simulations. The estimated muscle activation profiles, including means and standard deviations are presented in the Fig. 6. The resulting mean activation patterns closely matched the activation patterns reported in Fig. 5, indicating that the overall temporal trends were preserved across parameter perturbations. The GM muscle demonstrated relatively low variability, and the vastus muscle exhibited moderately greater variability. Importantly, the variability observed across combinations remained within physiologically reasonable bounds, and no unstable or non-physiological activation patterns were observed. Collectively, these findings indicate that the muscle activation strategies reported in this study are robust to moderate parameter variations, thereby strengthening confidence in the reliability of the simulation results.

Discussion

The estimated stimulation profiles performed well during intermediate height lifting of the STS portion of the transfer maneuvers, and the in silico estimated muscle activations show similar profiles to those presented in [31] for the strength deficit model representing the actions of FNS on the contractions of the paralyzed muscles. Overall, the caregiver was able to complete the lifting task when the predicted stimulation was applied, and rated it as easier across all trials based on the URS, as shown in Fig. 13. In contrast, without stimulation, the caregiver was unable to complete the task and rated it as difficult on the URS scale. For S1, there was a 6-point change in the positive direction (from − 3 to + 3), while for S2, there was a 5-point change in the positive direction (from − 2 to + 3). Overall, PE ratings indicated that STS transfers were perceived as very in the stimulation conditions (median: 3) and difficult in the no stimulation conditions (medians: − 3 and − 2). Lifting heights showed significant differences between stimulation and no stimulation conditions (Fig. 10). These findings help support the feasibility optimizing and applying of subject-specific stimulation patterns to facilitate assisted transfers and their potential to reduce potentially injurious loads experience by caregivers.

We observed differences in the caregiver’s joint angle deviations between S1 and S2. S2 was taller than S1, which required an increase in seating height for S2 to allow the same caregiver to perform the STS task. The seating height for S2 was set approximately cm higher than that for S1, which may account for the observed joint angle deviations. While this is clearly an artifact of the specific experimental design we employed, it would suggest that the extent of the benefits to caregivers might be dependent on the body habitus of the transferees and the configuration of their specific seating systems. These results highlight the importance of developing subject-specific models so that each subject can serve as their own concurrent controls with and without stimulation. Also, these factors can be investigated further and the optimization procedures and resulting stimulation patterns further refined to account for such influences, as well as other environmental considerations like the seating surfaces and their relative orientation. Due to the small sample size and non-normality, a nonparametric Wilcoxon signed-rank test are applied to paired trial-level area under the curve of vGRF values for the with and without stimulation conditions. Effect size are quantified using the rank-biserial correlation (). For S1 and S2, a two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test on vGRF values did not indicate a statistically significant difference between the stim and no-stim conditions (). However, all four paired trials changed in the same direction, resulting in a large rank-biserial effect size (). These results suggest a potentially meaningful effect that warrants further investigation with a larger sample size.

For joint reaction forces, stimulation greatly affected the predicted total joint reaction forces experienced by the caregiver model. A previous study [32] reported high joint reaction loads at different spinal levels during flexion and extension from squat to upright positions, even without heavy lifting. In our partial STS, we similarly observed high spinal loads, which were reduced with stimulation. Although stimulation did not impact the hip and lumbar spine joint reaction forces for S1 due to the small differences in applied forces between the stimulation and no stimulation conditions, it significantly reduced the hip and lumbar spine joint reaction forces for S2. This effect may also result from the implanted system and recruitment of the AM muscle in S2, which likely generated larger extension moments at the knee and hip compared to the surface system used in S1. To the best of our knowledge, no previous studies have used optimal stimulation profiles to estimate joint reaction forces for caregiver models. For individuals with SCI, the timing of stimulation is critically important for maintaining stability during assisted movements. Although the resulting stimulation levels in the present study were relatively high, the optimized timings of hip and knee extensor muscle activations (Fig. 5) differed from the heuristic stimulation patterns used in earlier work [11]. In that earlier study, muscles were manually activated in the simulation environment at different phases of the STS maneuver based on activation patterns observed during able-bodied STS transitions (knee extensors: activation from s to s and from s to s; hip and trunk extensors: activation from s to s). Additionally, activating both the trunk and knee extensors at at the beginning of the lifting motion caused the SCI participant to pull posteriorly away from the caregiver, destabilizing the maneuver, thereby highlighting the importance of coordinated activation timing. In the earlier study, forward dynamics simulations (direct shooting method) were employed to estimate spinal compression, and the results showed that stimulation could reduce the intervertebral compressive forces. In addition, the caregiver model applied force was treated as a design variable in that study, whereas in the current study we used experimentally calculated applied force to estimate joint reaction loads. The previous study reported a reduction in caregiver work over the course of motion using heuristic stimulation patterns. In the present study (direct collocation method), caregiver work was reduced by and using optimized FNS for S1 and S2, respectively.

Limitations of this study that warrant further investigation include that the simulations were performed only in the sagittal plane, whereas dependent transfers involve transverse and coronal movements [33]. Since we focused primarily on the lifting portion of the task, rather than pivoting or lowering components which can also contribute to caregiver injuries during patient handling tasks, the simulation and optimization based approaches we employed would need to be extended to include the motions that are more representative of the entire dependent transfer maneuver. Another limitation is the small sample size, which prevents the results from being generalized. More subjects and trials are needed for a robust statistical analysis. To address this, future work should include recruitment of more subjects, particularly individuals with cervical to mid-thoracic injuries, and collecting of additional trials. Applying continuous forces at a specific point on the caregiver model’s elbow joint during the STS task to estimate joint reaction forces was also a simplifying assumption in this study. This was based on observing dependent transfers and the partial STS maneuvers during the experimental procedures experiment. However, other joints, particularly the interaction between the knees of the caregiver and SCI individual were also observed and may contribute to the overall joint loading outside the sagittal plane [11] but were not included in the current analysis.

Despite these limitations, our closed-chain musculoskeletal model was able to estimate optimal muscle activation profiles for dependent transfers. This overall framework may serve as a valid tool for estimating caregiver model joint reaction forces.

Conclusions

In this study, we developed and applied combined caregiver and SCI user musculoskeletal model to estimate optimal stimulation profiles for activating the paralyzed hip and knee extensor muscles to assist with a dependent transfer. Our results demonstrate that applying optimal neural stimulation profiles is feasible in real-time during the lifting portion of a caregiver-assisted transfer. Based on the estimated forces applied to the upper arm of a caregiver model by the weight of the individual with SCI, optimized activation of the hip and knee extensors significantly reduces the joint reaction forces at the caregiver shoulder, hip, and lumbar spine. In future work, we plan to extend our current 3D musculoskeletal model into a more complex representation with additional degrees of freedom and expanded muscle selections to simulate other more complex transfers such as those from wheelchair-to-bed. Additionally, we will verify predictions of the joint loads derived from this in-silico approach with experimental data from commercially available Biodex lifting system. Overall, this preliminary study demonstrates that the augmentation of assisted transfers with stimulation could be used to aid with the main objective of reducing caregiver effort and minimizing potential for shoulder and back injuries.
