Numerical simulation of the false vacuum decay at finite temperature
Haiyang Wang, Renhui Qin, Ligong Bian

Abstract
The false vacuum decay rate is of important meaning in understanding the Universe, such as the symmetry breaking process in the early universe and the age of our universe, which is conventionally calculated with the saddle-point approximation in the field theory. Utilizing the extension of the Wigner function in quantum field theory, we numerically calculate the decay rate of the false vacuum through path integral. We study the decay rate for the thermal fluctuation scenarios and its dependence on the potential shape, and found that the false vacuum decay occurs following an exponentially decay rate, and the speed of vacuum decay decreases when the initial energy of the system decreases and the potential height increase. The discrepancy between the simulation results and the theoretical prediction of finite temperature effective field theory is observed.



I
Introduction

The false vacuum decay (FVD) can occur through quantum tunneling at zero temperature
Coleman 1977
;
Callan and Coleman 1977
and/or thermal fluctuation in the early universe
Linde 1983
.
Quantum tunneling is crucial for understanding the decay rate of the electroweak vacuum or the age of our universe
Chigusa et al. 2017
;
Andreassen et al. 2018
. The foundational quantum field theory description of quantum tunneling was first established by Refs.
Coleman 1977
;
Callan and Coleman 1977
, and was later extended to finite-temperature scenarios to consider the thermal dynamical fluctuations
Linde 1983
;
Affleck 1981
based on the functional integration treatment of classically metastability problem
Langer 1967
;
Langer 1969
, which is of a lot similarity with the Kramer’s escape rate from the potential in the field theory
Berera et al. 2019
. Thermal fluctuations can result in spherically symmetric, localized configurations, oscillons, that seed the false vacuum decay through vacuum bubble nucleation
Copeland et al. 1995
;
Gleiser and Haas 1996
;
Gleiser 1994
;
Gleiser and Howell 2005
;
Gleiser et al. 2008a
;
Pîrvu et al. 2024a
. Vacuum bubble dynamics in the first-order cosmological thermal phase transition can produce detectable gravitational waves at space-based interferometers (such as LISA
Amaro-Seoane et al. 2017
, TianQin
Luo et al. 2025
and Taiji
Bian et al. 2025a
;
Ruan et al. 2020
), and further provides a chance to interpret the baryon asymmetry of the universe through electroweak baryogenesis
Cohen et al. 1993
;
Morrissey and Ramsey-Musolf 2012
;
Trodden 1999
.

It is known that the FVD rates (bubble nucleation rates) in both the quantum tunneling and thermal fluctuation scenarios can be calculated based on the saddle-point or the instanton approximation, live the prefactor of which hard to theoretically calculate and the real-time picture of the decay process unaccessible. Recent studies show that it is possible to perform computations of the FVD rate in the quantum tunneling case through real-time semiclassical approach simulation with the truncated Wigner function method with the quantum correction sitting in the vacuum fluctuation states and the classical evolution allows the appearance of bubble nucleation
Pirvu et al. 2022
;
Braden et al. 2019
, which was found to be largely different from the prediction by the instanton method
Hertzberg et al. 2020
. To reduce the discrepancy between the two methods, Ref.
Braden et al. 2023
suggests to include the renormalization effects on lattice into the instanton calculations. Refs.
Andreassen et al. 2016
;
Andreassen et al. 2017
propose a direct method for the calculation of the FVD at zero temperature analogous to quantum mechanics, which we extend to study the thermal fluctuation scenario.

In this Letter, we construct FVD probability based on the Wigner functional method, and perform the calculation of the FVD for the thermal fluctuation scenario through the path integral method on lattice.
Our study reveals that the FVD rate decreases exponentionally with the temperature decrease of the system. The method takes advantage of the instanton method for the large thermal fluctuation.

II
Direct method description

In the classical case of a metastable system with two ground states, a potential barrier prevents a particle from entering one ground state from another when its energy is lower than the barrier. However, when quantum effects are taken into account, the particle can still enter one ground state from another. The probability distribution of the system can be calculated once the wave function expression has been obtained. Refs.
Andreassen et al. 2016
;
Andreassen et al. 2017
combined probability descriptions to provide a precise expression for the decay rate:

\Gamma_{FV}=-\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}t/t_{\mathrm{NL}}\rightarrow 0\\
t/t_{\mathrm{slosh}}\rightarrow\infty\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{P_{\mathrm{FV}}(t)}\frac{d}{dt}P_{\mathrm{FV}}(t)\;,

(1)

where
P_{FV}
is the probability that the system is in a false vacuum, and the decay rate
\Gamma
always satisfies
P(t)\sim e^{-\Gamma t}
. The decay rate within the effective time limit can be obtained by simulating the evolution of the wave function through numerical solutions of the one-dimensional Schrödinger equation
Andreassen et al. 2017
, the key issue is to track the time evolution of the system’s probability distribution within a specific region. In quantum field theory, it is difficult to obtain an analytic solution for the wave function in the interacting case. Therefore, we consider the Wigner function extended to scalar field theory
Mrówczyński and Müller 1994
. Since the Wigner function serves as a quasi-probability distribution, the probability
P_{FV}(t)
can be expressed as:

P_{FV}(t)=\frac{1}{Z}\int_{FV}\mathcal{D}\phi\int\frac{\mathcal{D}\Pi}{2\pi}W[\phi,\Pi;t]\;

(2)

This is a functional integral expression, where
W[\phi,\Pi;t]
is the Wigner function extended to field theory,
Z
can be understood as a normalization constant. We can obtain it by using the functional integral restriction without the integral region restriction in the above formula. By analogy with the Wigner function in quantum mechanics, its definition is given by:

\displaystyle W[\phi(x),\Pi(x);t]=\int\mathcal{D}\varphi(x)\exp\left[-\frac{i}{\hbar}\int dx\Pi(x)\varphi(x)\right]\times\left\langle\phi(x)+\frac{\varphi(x)}{2}\right|\hat{\rho}(t)\left|\phi(x)-\frac{\varphi(x)}{2}\right\rangle\;,

(3)

where
\phi(x)
is the time-independent real scalar field in the Schrödinger picture.
\Pi(x)\equiv\frac{\delta\mathcal{L}}{\delta\dot{\phi}(x)}
is the momentum density conjugate to
\phi(x)
.
\hat{\rho}(t)=\left|\Psi(t)\right\rangle\left\langle\Psi(t)\right|
. We can use
\left\langle\phi(x)|\Psi(t)\right\rangle
to obtain the wave function
\Psi(\phi,t)
, which is a functional of the field configuration and evolves with time. In quantum mechanics, once we know the wave function, we can obtain the probability that the system is in a certain interval in coordinate space. Therefore, we extend this idea to field theory, drawing an analogy in field configuration space, and obtain the probability that the field takes values within a given region. Ref.
Mrówczyński and Müller 1994
shows that the Wigner function in QFT plays a role similar to that of a density distribution in the space spanned by
\phi
and
\Pi
. Although we know that Wigner Function is not always positive definite, it cannot be interpreted simply as a probability density. Fortunately,
W[\phi,\Pi;t]
in Eq.
2
remains positive throughout this study..

Now we consider the equation of motion of the Wigner function in field theory. In the Schrodinger picture, the density matrix operator
\hat{\rho}(t)
satisfies the von Neumann equation:

i\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\hat{\rho}(t)=[\hat{H},\hat{\rho}(t)]\;.

(4)

We consider only a real scalar field, for which the Hamiltonian operator can be written as:

\hat{H}=\int d^{3}x\hat{\mathcal{H}}=\int d^{3}x[\frac{\hat{\Pi}^{2}}{2}+\frac{(\nabla\hat{\phi})^{2}}{2}+V(\hat{\phi})]\;.

(5)

By substituting Eq.
5
into Eq.
4
, one obtains the equation of motion for the Wigner function
John and Wilson 1994
;
Braden et al. 2019
;
Mrówczyński and Müller 1994
:

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}W[\phi,\pi;t]=-2H\frac{1}{i\hbar}\sin{(\frac{i\hbar}{2}\Lambda)}W[\phi,\pi;t]\;,

(6)

Here,
\Lambda=\frac{\overleftarrow{\delta}}{\delta\Pi}\frac{\overrightarrow{\delta}}{\delta\phi}-\frac{\overleftarrow{\delta}}{\delta\Phi}\frac{\overrightarrow{\delta}}{\delta\Pi}
is the Poisson bracket operator. By expanding the sine function and neglecting terms of order
\hbar^{2}
and higher, we recover the classical Liouville equation:

\left[\frac{\partial}{\partial t}+\int d^{3}x(\frac{\delta H}{\delta\Pi}\frac{\delta}{\delta\phi}-\frac{\delta H}{\delta\phi}\frac{\delta}{\delta\Pi})\right]W[\phi,\Pi;t]=0\;.

(7)

The classical Liouville equation describes the evolution of the probability distribution function
W[\phi,\Pi;t]
over time in classical phase space. This equation can be solved using the method of characteristics, which allows us to determine the evolution of
W
by following classical trajectories. Under the classical approximation, Eq.
7
can be solved
John and Remler 1987
;
Mrówczyński and Müller 1994
:

W[\phi,\Pi;t]=W[\phi_{-t},\Pi_{-t};0]\;.

(8)

Here,
\phi_{-t}
and
\Pi_{-t}
on the right-hand side of the above equation are solutions to the classical equations of motion for the scalar field,

\left\{\begin{aligned} \frac{\delta H}{\delta\Pi}&=\frac{d\phi}{dt}=\Pi\;,\\
-\frac{\delta H}{\delta\phi}&=\frac{d\Pi}{dt}=\nabla^{2}\phi-\frac{\delta V(\phi)}{\delta\phi}\;,\end{aligned}\right.

(9)

corresponding to original field configurations that end in
\phi
and
\Pi
after time
t
. A schematic illustration of this dynamical process is shown in Fig.
1
.

Figure 1:
A schematic illustration of the solution of
W[\phi,\Pi;t]
, which describes the evolution of the system’s state via the classical equations of motion.

In our simulation, we adjust the sign of the
\Pi
field and then evolve the field equations of motion using the leapfrog algorithm to obtain
\phi_{-t}
and
\Pi_{-t}
. After obtaining the approximate solution by neglecting terms of order
\mathcal{O}(\hbar^{2})
, Eq.
2
can be written as:

\begin{split}P_{FV}(t)&=\frac{1}{Z}\int_{FV}\mathcal{D}\phi_{-t}\frac{\mathcal{D}\Pi_{-t}}{2\pi}W(\phi_{-t},\Pi_{-t};0)\;.\\
\end{split}

(10)

Here, we substitute the result of Eq.
8
and perform a transformation of the integral measure. The Jacobian determinant of the measure transformation is always unity in our case. This is because the phase space flow of a Hamiltonian system is a symplectic transformation, and Liouville’s theorem ensures the conservation of phase space volume. This can also be verified by evaluating the Jacobian matrix associated with the iterative transformation of the integral measure under the discrete equations of motion.

Figure 2:
The
V
–
\phi
diagram.
\phi_{m}
denotes the field value at the potential maximum;
V_{b}
is the barrier height;
\Delta V
is the potential energy difference between the two vacua.

Let us consider the potential of the form:
V(\phi)=A\phi^{2}+B\phi^{3}+C\phi^{4}
(see Fig.
2
). The scalar field is assumed to be approximately in thermal equilibrium at the initial time. The density operator is then given by:

\hat{\rho}=\frac{1}{Z}e^{-\beta\hat{H}}

(11)

where
\beta=(k_{B}T)^{-1}
and
T
is the temperature. In natural units, we set the Boltzmann constant
k_{B}=1
and
\hbar=1
. We obtain an approximate expression for the Wigner function at the initial time by neglecting terms of order
\mathcal{O}(\hbar^{2})
via perturbative expansion:

W[\phi,\Pi;0]=\frac{1}{Z}e^{-\beta H}\;,

(12)

see the
Supplementary Material
for more details, where
H=\int dx^{3}[\frac{1}{2}\Pi^{2}(x)+\frac{1}{2}(\nabla\phi(x))^{2}+V(\phi)]
is the classical Hamiltonian. Since both the initial distribution and the equation of motion of the Wigner function are computed neglecting
\mathcal{O}(\hbar^{2})
terms, quantum effects are retained only at order
\hbar
. This suggests that the Wigner function remains static over time when the
\mathcal{O}(\hbar^{2})
approximation is neglected. However, it should be noted that in the integral expression of Eq.
10
, the integration measure evolves with time, and performing the functional integral itself is nontrivial. To implement the functional integral with interval constraints, we sample field configurations in the discrete phase space and introduce a statistical factor
\chi
, defined as:

\chi_{FV}(\phi_{-t}^{(i,j)})=\frac{N(\phi_{-t}^{(i,j)}(x)<\phi_{m})}{N}\;.

(13)

Here,
(i,j)
is to mark different sample fields, and the denominator
N
represents the number of discrete coordinate space lattice points. In three-dimensional space,
N=N_{x}\cdot N_{y}\cdot N_{z}
. The numerator represents the number of grid points where the field values fall below
\phi_{m}
for a given sample field
(i,j)
, and
\phi_{m}
is the field value corresponding to the barrier (as shown in Fig.
2
). From the definition formula of
\chi
, it can be seen that it is used to measure how many ”quantities” of a certain field are within the target integral interval, and
\chi
is time-dependent. Therefore, the information of time evolution here is completely contained within
\chi
. Eventually, our probability expression can be written as:

\begin{split}P_{FV}(t)&=\frac{\int\mathcal{D}\phi_{-t}\frac{\mathcal{D}\Pi_{-t}}{2\pi}\chi_{FV}(\phi_{-t})W(\phi_{-t},\Pi_{-t};0)}{\int\mathcal{D}\phi_{-t}\frac{\mathcal{D}\Pi_{-t}}{2\pi}W(\phi_{-t},\Pi_{-t};0)}\\
&\approx\frac{\sum_{ij}\chi_{FV}(\phi_{-t}^{(i,j)})\cdot W(\phi_{-t}^{(i,j)},\Pi_{-t}^{(i,j)};0)}{\sum_{ij}W(\phi_{-t}^{(i,j)},\Pi_{-t}^{(i,j)};0)}\;.\end{split}

(14)

Where, we can see that the
\chi
factor comes from the classical field evolution part, while quantum effect at
\hbar
-order is contained in the Wigner function. Using the above expression, we can simulate the behavior of the decay rate at different initial temperatures.

III
Numerical simulation results

We conducted the simulation in the 1+1 dimensional case and adopted the natural unit system
\hbar=c=1
. The generalization from one to higher dimensions is straightforward. Unlike the analog potentials proposed to simulate vacuum decay in cold atom experiments
Braden et al. 2018
;
Billam et al. 2020
;
Billam et al. 2019
;
Billam et al. 2021
, here we consider a scalar potential that is conventionally used to study gravitational waves from vacuum first-order phase transitions in the early Universe
Cutting et al. 2018
;
Cutting et al. 2021
;
Bian et al. 2025b
. To simplify numerical computation, we perform a dimensionless rescaling:
\phi_{s}=\phi/f^{\star}
,
t_{s}=\omega^{\star}t
, and
x_{s}=\omega^{\star}x
, where the subscript “s” indicates simulation variables. This rescaling allows for more convenient numerical calculations. The Lagrangian density is

\begin{split}\mathcal{L}&=\frac{1}{2}\partial^{\mu}\phi\partial_{\mu}\phi-\Lambda[a(\frac{\phi}{f^{\star}})^{2}+b(\frac{\phi}{f^{\star}})^{3}+c(\frac{\phi}{f^{\star}})^{4}]\\
&=\Lambda[\frac{1}{2}\dot{\phi_{s}}^{2}-\frac{1}{2}(\nabla\phi_{s})^{2}-(a\phi_{s}^{2}+b\phi_{s}^{3}+c\phi_{s}^{4})]\;.\end{split}

(15)

In our simulation, we take
\Lambda=(f^{\star}\omega^{\star})^{2}
. We use a normalized form of the potential energy such that the true vacuum energy is fixed to
1
, with
a=0.8
. Different
\Delta V
and
V_{b}
are obtained by adjusting the parameters
b
and
c
. We set the integration time step to
\Delta t_{s}=0.01
, the lattice spacing to
\Delta x_{s}=0.25
, and the number of lattice points to
N_{x}=1024
. For each simulation, we generate
N_{\text{samples}}=1000
initial field configurations of
\phi
and
\Pi
. We give the initial condition using the following power spectrum in momentum space:

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{\phi}(k)=\frac{n_{k}}{w_{k}}=\frac{1}{w_{k}}\frac{1}{e^{w_{k}/T}-1}\;,

(16)

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{\Pi}(k)=n_{k}w_{k}=\frac{w_{k}}{e^{w_{k}/T}-1}\;,

where
w_{k}=\sqrt{k^{2}+m_{eff}^{2}}
,
m_{eff}^{2}=V^{\prime\prime}(\phi_{fv})
. Using the Bose–Einstein distribution and random field sampling, we obtain an initial vacuum configuration with thermal fluctuations. Specific details of the initial conditions and the corresponding power spectrum are provided in the
Supplemental Material
. By varying
\beta\equiv 1/T
, we obtain initial distributions corresponding to different temperatures. Higher temperatures correspond to larger initial fluctuations. Several representative initial distribution are shown in Fig.
5
of the
Supplemental Material
. We generate
N_{\text{samples}}\times N_{\text{samples}}
independent sample pairs to construct the discrete phase space
(\phi,\Pi)
. Each point in the phase space represents a sample
(\phi_{i},\Pi_{j})
and corresponds to a value of the Wigner function. Each sample pair evolves independently according to Eq.
9
. Finally, we compute the decay rate by performing numerical sampling over these sample pairs.

We simulate the time-dependent behavior of
P_{FV}
. We find that the decay processes on both sides of the critical point exhibit different characteristics: when
\beta\omega^{\star}<\beta_{c}\omega^{\star}
, i.e., the initial energy is greater than the potential barrier (the critical point of the initial energy),
the probability of false vacuum
P_{FV}
will have a continuous decline. When it drops to a sufficiently small threshold, we consider the system to have decayed. Therefore, the effective time range we take is before
P_{FV}
drops to a certain threshold.
Within the valid time, we use a sliding window to fit
P_{FV}\sim\exp(-\Gamma t)
, where the window length is
25\%
of the valid time and the goodness-of-fit threshold is
0.99
. Then, in the window that satisfies the goodness-of-fit, we identify the maximum value of
\Gamma
in the valid windows. The reason for taking the maximum value is that we believe that the maximum
\Gamma
here can be used as the characteristic value characterizing the decay process.
After
\beta\omega^{\star}\gtrsim\beta_{c}\omega^{\star}
, the energy is less than the barrier. The system needs some time to undergo thermalization to reach a stable decay process. By observing the
P_{FV}-t
graph, we find that the decay after thermalization behaves very stably. The decay during this period can well fit the exponential form
P_{FV}\sim exp(-\Gamma t)
. So our effective time range is set in the stable decay interval. Similarly, we are use the sliding window for fitting and extraction to obtain the final decay rate. The final result is shown in Fig.
4
. In
Supplemental Material
, we present the scenario of
V_{b}=0.035
for illustration. We obtain the variation of
\Gamma/T
with
\beta\omega^{\star}
. And we used the formula
\ln(\Gamma/T)=\mu(\beta-\beta_{0})V_{b}^{\frac{1}{2}}+\nu(\beta-\beta_{0})^{2}V_{b}+\lambda
to fit our results, the values we have fitted are respectively
\mu=12.29,~\nu=-54.27,~\lambda=-0.86
and
\beta_{0}\omega^{\star}=0.49
, goodness of fit is
0.987
.

To compare our simulation results with the finite-temperature theoretical predictions from thermal equilibrium field theory, we compute the predicted decay rate by obtaining the instanton solution following the method in
BubbleDet

Ekstedt et al. 2023
:
\Gamma\propto(S/2\pi T)^{1/2}e^{-S/T}\;
,
where
S
is the Euclidean spatial action, and is given by

S=\int dr\left[\frac{1}{2}(\frac{\partial\phi_{B}}{\partial r})^{2}+V_{eff}(\phi_{B})\right]\;,

(17)

where
\phi_{B}
is the bounce solution, which satisfies

\frac{\partial^{2}\phi_{B}}{\partial r^{2}}-\frac{\partial V_{eff}}{\partial\phi}=0\;.

(18)

The instanton prediction is qualitatively consistent with our simulation results. However, the decay process in our simulation is inherently probabilistic and statistical, and it incorporates non-equilibrium and nonlinear effects—such as those arising from bubble dynamics during the transition
Bian et al. 2025b
;
Pîrvu et al. 2024b
;
Pîrvu et al. 2024c
;
Pîrvu et al. 2024a
, whereas the instanton approach describes the tunneling process between two minima under thermal equilibrium conditions, the results of the two here do not exactly match.
It can be seen that the false vacuum decay rate
\Gamma
gradually decreases as the temperature drops, see the dashed lines in Fig.
4
. When
\beta\omega^{\star}<\beta_{c}\omega^{\star}
, the initial energy is greater than the barrier, the decay of the system is a classical decay, the influence of temperature on the decay rate is relatively small. When
\beta\omega^{\star}>\beta_{c}\omega^{\star}
,
\Gamma
will have a significant decrease. This is precisely because the initial energy is less than the barrier height, so the decay process is dominated by thermal fluctuations. When the temperature is lower, we conjecture that ensuring the reliability of our method at low temperatures may require a larger number of samples
N_{samples}
and an extended simulation scale. In our definition and description of the decay rate, the key quantity is the time-dependent probability distribution. In
Supplemental Material
, we also provide diagrams of the probability distribution at the initial moment and after evolution, clearly illustrating the vacuum-decay process.

IV
Conclusion and discussion

In this Letter, through (1+1)-dimensional lattice classical field simulation, we evaluated the false vacuum decay rate with the Wigner function method. We observe that: 1) the false vacuum probability follows an exponential time decay form; 2) the vacuum decay rate exponentially decreases as the thermal fluctuation magnitude decrease; and 3) the decay rate decreases as the potential barrier height increases. We demonstrate that the methodology employed in this study can yield results that deviate from those obtained via the instanton method grounded in the saddle-point approximation, particularly when thermal fluctuation effects are incorporated, which are argued to originate from the incomplete thermalization of the metastable phase
Pîrvu et al. 2024b
;
Pîrvu et al. 2024c
;
Hirvonen and Gould 2025
.

Our direct path integral approach circumvents limitations of saddle-point approximations, particularly in capturing non-perturbative thermal effects. This provides a framework for studying vacuum decay in scenarios when the thermal effective field perturbation theory fails. Future extensions to 3+1 dimensions could quantify gravitational wave signatures from cosmological phase transitions, leveraging the method’s capability to handle inhomogeneous field configurations
Bian et al. 2025b
. We expect that the method developed in this Letter can be directly applied to the calculation of baryon number violation processes—specifically, the sphaleron rate
Khlebnikov and Shaposhnikov 1988
—and may offer a promising tool for simulating baryon asymmetry generation in the early Universe.

In Eq.

&=\frac{1}{2}\int dx\int\mathcal{D}\Pi_{1}\Pi_{1}^{2}\delta(\Pi_{1}-\Pi)\\
&=\frac{1}{2}\int\Pi^{2}(x)dx\end{split}

(21)
