A remark on elephant random walks via the classical law of the iterated logarithm for self-similar Gaussian processes
Shuhei Shibata, Tomoyuki Shirai

Abstract
This paper investigates whether two independent Elephant Random Walks (ERWs) on ℤ \mathbb{Z} , each with a different memory parameter, can meet infinitely often, extending the work of Roy, Takei, and Tanemura. We also study the asymptotic behavior of their distance by providing an elementary and accessible proof of the classical Law of the Iterated Logarithm (LIL) for centered, continuous, self-similar Gaussian processes under a certain decay condition on the covariance kernel.

Introduction

The asymptotic behavior of random walks with long-range
memory has been extensively studied in recent years. Among
such models, the Elephant Random Walk (ERW), introduced by
Schütz and Trimper
[
37
]
, has attracted significant attention
as a non-Markovian stochastic process that
exhibits a phase transition from diffusive to superdiffusive behavior
(cf.
[
2
,
3
,
7
,
8
,
14
,
12
,
31
]
).
In this paper, we investigate the interaction between two
independent ERWs on the integer lattice

\mathbb{Z}
. Specifically, we focus on the phenomenon of
infinite collisions, inspired by the result of Roy, Takei,
and Tanemura
[
35
]
, who studied the case where both ERWs have
the same memory parameter. Our work extends their result by
considering different memory parameters for the two
walks. In addition, we obtain asymptotic results for the
distance between the two ERWs.

We recall the definition of the one-dimensional ERW.
The first step
X_{1}
of the elephant is
+1
with
probability
q
, and
-1
with probability
1-q
.
For each
n=1,2,\dots
, given the past steps
X_{1},X_{2},\dots,X_{n}
, we define the

(n+1)
-th step by

X_{n+1}=\begin{cases}+X_{U_{n}}&\text{with probability}\quad p,\\
-X_{U_{n}}&\text{with probability}\quad 1-p,\end{cases}

where
p\in[0,1]
is called the
memory parameter
, and
U_{n}
is uniformly
distributed on
\{1,2,\dots,n\}
and independent of

\{X_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
.
The sequence
\{X_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
generates the
one-dimensional ERW
\{{S_{n}}\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
defined by

S_{0}:=0,\quad\text{and}\quad S_{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}\quad\text{for}\quad n=1,2,\dots.

As is well known, the asymptotic behavior of the ERW mainly
depends on the memory parameter
p
which takes the values
from
0
to
1
. In the diffusive regime
p<3/4
as well as
in the critical regime
p=3/4
, the following asymptotic normality of
the ERW is established: as
n\to\infty
,

\frac{S_{n}}{\sqrt{n}}\xrightarrow{d}N\left(0,\frac{1}{3-4p}\right)\quad\text{for}\quad 0<p<3/4

and

\frac{S_{n}}{\sqrt{n\log n}}\xrightarrow{d}N\left(0,1\right)\quad\text{for}\quad p=3/4

(cf.
[
2
,
3
,
7
]
). On the other hand, in the
superdiffusive regime
p>3/4
, the limiting distribution of the ERW is not Gaussian:

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{S_{n}}{n^{2p-1}}=L_{p,q}\quad\textit{a.s.},

(1.1)

where
L_{p,q}
has a continuous distribution which is not Gaussian. (cf.
[
3
,
7
,
14
,
12
]
).

In the diffusive and critical regimes, Roy, Takei, and Tanemura
[
35
]
established the
asymptotic results for the distance between two independent
ERWs with the same memory parameter on
the integer lattice
\mathbb{Z}
.

Theorem 1.1

(
[
35
]
)
.

Let
\{S_{n}\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
and
\{S^{\prime}_{n}\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
be two independent ERWs on
\mathbb{Z}
with the same memory parameter
p
.

(a)

If
0<p<3/4

then

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\pm\frac{S_{n}-S^{\prime}_{n}}{\sqrt{2n\log\log n}}=\frac{\sqrt{2}}{\sqrt{3-4p}}\quad\text{a.s.}

(b)

If
p=3/4

then

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\pm\frac{S_{n}-S^{\prime}_{n}}{\sqrt{2n\log n\log\log\log n}}=\sqrt{2}\quad\text{a.s.}

In Theorem
1.1
, the memory parameters of the two elephants are
assumed to be identical. In this case, the LIL for the Brownian motion can be
applied to obtain their result, which leads to infinite collisions. However, when the memory
parameters are different, the LIL for self-similar
Gaussian processes is required instead.

Although the LIL has been extensively studied in a general
framework (cf.
[
38
,
41
,
5
,
19
]
), we do not aim to treat it
in full generality in this paper. Rather, we present a
self-contained and transparent exposition within a
restricted yet sufficiently meaningful framework, motivated
by the analysis of infinite collisions of two independent ERWs.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
\{X(s)\}_{s\geq 0}
be a centered continuous Gaussian
process with covariance kernel
R(s,t)
satisfying the
following.

(i)

There exists
\rho>0
such that for any

c>0
and
s,t>0
,

R(cs,ct)=c^{2\rho}R(s,t).

(1.2)

(ii)

Under (i), there exists
\eta>0
such that
the function
h(x)=x^{-\rho}R(1,x)\ (x\geq 1)
satisfies

h(x)=O((\log x)^{-\eta})\text{\hskip 10.22217ptas $x\to\infty$}.

(1.3)

Then, we have

\limsup_{s\to\infty}\pm\frac{X(s)}{\sqrt{2s^{2\rho}\log\log s}}=R(1,1)^{\frac{1}{2}}\quad a.s.

Remark 1.3
.

In
[
18
]
, Lamperti proved that if the
process
\{X(t)\}_{t\geq 0}
is nontrivial, stochastically continuous at

t=0
and there
exists a function
v(c)
such that

R(cs,ct)=v(c)R(s,t),

then it must be of the form
v(c)=c^{2\rho}
with
\rho\geq 0
.
Under the assumption (i), we have the

\rho
-self-similarity, i.e.,

\{X(ct)\}_{t\geq 0}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\{c^{\rho}X(t)\}_{t\geq 0}\quad(c>0)

(1.4)

and
X(0)=0
almost surely.
Moreover, if the process
\{X(t)\}_{t\geq 0}

is
\rho
-self-similar, then
\{Y(t)=e^{-\rho t}X(e^{t})\}_{t\in\mathbb{R}}
is strictly stationary.
Conversely, if the process
\{Y(t)\}_{t\in\mathbb{R}}

is strictly stationary, then
\{X(t)=t^{\rho}Y(\log t)\}_{t>0}
with
X(0)=0
is
\rho
-self-similar (cf.
[
36
,
9
]
).
From this correspondence, if we set
r_{Y}(t):=\mathbb{E}[Y(0)Y(t)]
, then
h(x)=r_{Y}(\log x)
, and the LIL for a self-similar Gaussian process

\{X(t)\}_{t\geq 0}
can be
rephrased as
\limsup_{t\to\infty}\pm Y(t)/\sqrt{2\log t}=r_{Y}(0)^{1/2}
for the associated stationary Gaussian process

\{Y(t)\}_{t\in\mathbb{R}}
.
Several related works on this form of the limit theorem and on
the version where the process
\pm Y(t)
is replaced by
its maximum process
\sup_{0\leq s\leq t}Y(s)

have been extensively studied in the continuous-parameter setting
[
22
,
21
,
16
,
17
,
20
,
6
,
1
]
, and also, in the discrete parameter setting

[
4
,
29
,
30
,
26
,
34
]
.

Remark 1.4
.

Our proof of Theorem
1.2
relies on a version of the (second) Borel–Cantelli lemma proved by
Erdős and Rényi
[
10
]
, along
with a decay condition (
1.3
).
As mentioned in Remark
1.3
,
the decay condition (or mixing condition) corresponding to
(
1.3
) on
the covariance function of
\{Y(t)\}_{t\in\mathbb{R}}

is
r_{Y}(t)=O(t^{-\eta})
as
t\to\infty
,
under which the Feller’s type LIL for stationary processes
was established in
[
33
]
.
Furthermore, this condition was relaxed to an even weaker mixing condition

r_{Y}(t)=O((\log t)^{-1})
in
[
28
]
by using the
zero-one law for the event
A=\{Y(t)>f(t)\ i.o.\}
,
where
\{Y(t)\}_{t\in\mathbb{R}}
is a stationary Gaussian process
and
f(t)
is any non-decreasing positive
function on some time interval
[a,\infty)

[
32
]
.
The functional LIL, as a natural extension, has also been thoroughly developed for self-similar Gaussian processes
(cf.
[
39
,
24
,
25
,
15
,
40
,
13
,
41
]
).

Here we give some examples of self-similar Gaussian
processes satisfying two assumptions (i) and (ii) in Theorem
1.2
.

Example 1.5
.

(a) Suppose that the process
X
is the fractional Brownian
motion (FBM)
\{B_{H}(t)\}_{t\geq 0}
with
the covariance kernel

R(s,t)=\frac{1}{2}(|t|^{2H}+|s|^{2H}-|t-s|^{2H})

for
0<H<1
. Then it satisfies (
1.2
) with
\rho=H
,
and

h(x)=O(x^{-(1-H)})\text{\quad as $x\to\infty$},

which implies (
1.3
).
We note that for
0<H<1
, if the process
X=\{X(t)\}_{t\geq 0}
is
an
H
-self-similar Gaussian process with
stationary
increments
, then it is necessarily the FBM up to a multiplicative constant
[
42
]
.

(b) Let
X(t)=\int_{0}^{t}(t-u)^{\beta}u^{-\gamma/2}dB(u)
for

\beta>-1/2
and
0\leq\gamma<1
.
This processes with
\gamma=0
appeared in

[
24
]
as typical examples in the context of the functional LIL,
while the version with general
\gamma
were introduced in

[
27
]

and is referred to as the generalized Riemann-Liouville
FBM. For example,
X(t)

reduces to the standard Brownian motion when
\beta=\gamma=0
, and to

X(t)=\int_{0}^{t}B(u)du
when
\beta=1,\gamma=0
.
In general, the covariance kernel is given by

R(s,t)=\int_{0}^{s\wedge t}(s-u)^{\beta}(t-u)^{\beta}u^{-\gamma}du,

which satisfies (
1.2
) with

\rho=\beta-\gamma/2+1/2
, and
h(x)
satisfies (
1.3
). Indeed,

h(x)=O(x^{-\frac{1}{2}(1-\gamma)})\text{\quad as $x\to\infty$}.

(c) As mentioned in Remark
1.3
,
if
\{X(t)\}_{t\geq 0}
is a
\rho
-self-similar Gaussian process, i.e., it
satisfies (
1.2
) with
\rho>0
,
then there exists a stationary Gaussian process
\{Y(t)\}_{t\in\mathbb{R}}
, and we have
\{X(t)=t^{\rho}Y(\log t)\}_{t>0}
with
X(0)=0
. If
\{Y(t)\}_{t\in\mathbb{R}}
is continuous in the mean, by Bochner’s theorem,
there exists a finite measure
\mu
on
\mathbb{R}

such that
r_{Y}(t):=E[Y(0)Y(t)]=R(1,1)\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{i\xi t}\mu(d\xi)
. Therefore, it is clear from the definition of

h(x)
that

h(x)=R(1,1)\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{i\xi\log x}\mu(d\xi).

For example, since the probability density
f_{\alpha}(x)

of the symmetric
\alpha
-stable distribution
corresponding to
\mu_{\alpha}(d\xi)=e^{-|\xi|^{\alpha}}d\xi
for

0<\alpha<2
is well known to satisfy
f_{\alpha}(x)\sim C_{\alpha}|x|^{-(\alpha+1)}
as
x\to\infty
(cf.
[
11
]
), we see that

h(x)=O((\log x)^{-(\alpha+1)})\text{\quad as
$x\to\infty$}.

For
\alpha=2
,
h(x)
decays even faster, i.e.,
h(x)=O(e^{-(\log x)^{2}/4})
as

x\to\infty
.

We obtain the asymptotic behavior of the distance between two
ERWs with different memory parameters by
applying Theorem
1.2
together with the
invariance principle for the ERW established by
Coletti, Gava, and Schütz
[
8
]
(see Lemma
2.1
below).

Theorem 1.6
.

Let
\{S_{n}\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
and
\{S^{\prime}_{n}\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
be two independent
ERWs on
\mathbb{Z}
with the memory parameters
p
and

p^{\prime}
, respectively.
If
0<p<3/4
and
0<p^{\prime}<3/4
, then we have

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\pm\frac{S_{n}-S^{\prime}_{n}}{\sqrt{2n\log\log n}}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{3-4p}+\frac{1}{3-4p^{\prime}}}\quad\text{a.s.}

(1.5)

The following result on infinite collisions of two ERWs with
distinct memory parameters follows from the difference in their speeds, which is
derived from
Theorems
1.1
and
1.6
.

Corollary 1.7
.

Two independent ERWs on
\mathbb{Z}
with memory parameters
p
and
p^{\prime}
meet each other
infinitely often almost surely if both parameters satisfy
0<p\leq 3/4
and
0<p^{\prime}\leq 3/4
; otherwise they meet each other only finitely often almost surely.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows.
In Section 2, we present a proof of Theorem
1.6
assuming
the LIL result stated in Theorem
1.2
. In Section 3, we establish upper
and lower bounds needed to prove the LIL result, while
leaving the main ingredient of the proof in Section 4.
The main arguments required to complete the proof of
Theorem
1.6
and Corollary
1.7

are carried out in Section 4.

2
Strong asymptotic distance between two

ERWs

First we give a proof of Theorem
1.6
assuming
Theorem
1.2
. To prove
Theorem
1.6
, we use the following strong asymptotic
relation between Brownian motion and ERW.

Lemma 2.1

(Coletti, Gava, and Schütz
[
8
]
)
.

Let
\{S_{n}\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
be the ERW on
\mathbb{Z}
with the memory parameter
p
, and
\{B(t)\}_{t\geq 0}
be the standard one-dimensional Brownian motion.

(a)

If
0<p<3/4

then

\ S_{n}-\frac{n^{2p-1}}{\sqrt{3-4p}}B(n^{3-4p})=o(\sqrt{n\log\log n})\quad\text{a.s.}

(b)

If
p=3/4

then

\ S_{n}-\sqrt{n}B(\log n)=o(\sqrt{n\log n\log\log\log n})\quad\text{a.s.}

Now we prove Theorem
1.6
by using Theorem
1.2
and Lemma
2.1
.

Proof of Theorem
1.6
.

Suppose that
0<p<3/4
and
0<p^{\prime}<3/4
. It follows form Lemma
2.1
that

\ S_{n}-\frac{n^{2p-1}}{\sqrt{3-4p}}B(n^{3-4p})=o(\sqrt{n\log\log n})\quad\textit{a.s.}

and

\ S^{\prime}_{n}-\frac{n^{2p^{\prime}-1}}{\sqrt{3-4p^{\prime}}}B^{\prime}(n^{3-4p^{\prime}})=o(\sqrt{n\log\log n})\quad\textit{a.s.,}

where
\{B(t)\}_{t\geq 0}
and
\{B^{\prime}(t)\}_{t\geq 0}
are
two independent copies of the standard one-dimensional Brownian motion.
Therefore, we have

S_{n}-S^{\prime}_{n}=\frac{n^{2p-1}}{\sqrt{3-4p}}B(n^{3-4p})-\frac{n^{2p^{\prime}-1}}{\sqrt{3-4p^{\prime}}}B^{\prime}(n^{3-4p^{\prime}})+o(\sqrt{n\log\log n})\hskip 5.0pt\textit{a.s.}

Now we consider the Gaussian process

X(t):=\frac{t^{2p-1}}{\sqrt{3-4p}}B(t^{3-4p})-\frac{t^{2p^{\prime}-1}}{\sqrt{3-4p^{\prime}}}B^{\prime}(t^{3-4p^{\prime}}).

Since its covariance kernel is given by

R(s,t)=\frac{(st)^{2p-1}}{3-4p}(s\wedge t)^{3-4p}+\frac{(st)^{2p^{\prime}-1}}{3-4p^{\prime}}(s\wedge t)^{3-4p^{\prime}},

we see that
R(s,t)
satisfies (
1.2
) with
\rho=1/2
, and

h(x)=\frac{x^{2p-\frac{3}{2}}}{3-4p}+\frac{x^{2p^{\prime}-\frac{3}{2}}}{3-4p^{\prime}}=O(x^{-(\frac{3}{2}-2(p\vee p^{\prime})})\quad\text{as $x\to\infty$},

which implies (
1.3
) for
0<p\vee p^{\prime}<3/4
. Therefore, we obtain (
1.5
) from Theorem
1.2
.
∎

3
Upper and lower bounds for the LIL

In this section, let
\{X(s)\}_{s\geq 0}
be a centered continuous Gaussian
process satisfying (
1.2
). Then, we begin by noting that Theorem
1.2
follows from Propositions
3.1
and

3.3
provided that condition
(
3.4
) below holds. This condition will be verified in the next section.

3.1
Upper bound for the LIL

First we show the upper bound in the LIL,
which is the simpler part of the proof.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
\{X(s)\}_{s\geq 0}
be a centered continuous Gaussian
process satisfying (
1.2
). Then, we have

\limsup_{s\to\infty}\frac{X(s)}{\sqrt{2s^{2\rho}\log\log s}}\leq\sigma\quad\text{a.s.},

where
\sigma:=R(1,1)^{1/2}
.

We recall below the well-known Borell–TIS inequality, which provides an estimate on the tail probability of the supremum of a Gaussian process
(cf.
[
23
, Theorem 4.2]
).

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
Y=\{Y(s)\}_{0\leq s\leq 1}
be a centered, continuous Gaussian process.
Set
v:=\sup_{0\leq s\leq 1}\var(Y(s))
.
Then,
m:=\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{0\leq s\leq 1}Y(s)\right]
is finite and we have, for all
x>m
,

\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{0\leq s\leq 1}Y(s)\geq x\right)\leq\exp\left(-\frac{(x-m)^{2}}{2v}\right).

Proof of Proposition
3.1
.

By (
1.2
) and
\rho
-self-similarity of
\{X(s)\}_{s\geq 0}
,
it follows from Theorem
3.2
that
for any
0<a<b
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\max_{a\leq s\leq b}X(s)>b^{\rho}M)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}(\max_{0\leq s\leq 1}b^{-\rho}X(bs)>M)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(\max_{0\leq s\leq 1}X(s)>M)

\displaystyle\leq\exp\Big(-\frac{(M-m)^{2}}{2v}\Big),

(3.1)

where
m=\mathbb{E}[\sup_{0\leq s\leq 1}X(s)]
and

v=\sup_{0\leq t\leq 1}\var(X(t))=\sup_{0\leq t\leq 1}(t^{2\rho}R(1,1))=\sigma^{2}.

(3.2)

Let
t_{n}=\alpha^{n}
for fixed
\alpha>1
and
set

M_{n}:=\sigma\sqrt{2\alpha\log\log t_{n}}=\sigma\sqrt{2\alpha(\log n+\log\log\alpha)}.

For any
\epsilon>0
, we have

\frac{(M_{n}-m)^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\geq(1-\epsilon)\alpha\log n

(3.3)

for any sufficiently large
n
.
Setting
b=t_{n+1}
and
M=M_{n}
in (
3.1
)
together with (
3.3
) and (
3.2
) yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\max_{t_{n}\leq s\leq t_{n+1}}X(s)>t_{n+1}^{\rho}M_{n}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\exp\Big(-\frac{(M_{n}-m)^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\Big)

\displaystyle\leq n^{-(1-\epsilon)\alpha}.

Hence, if we put
\alpha=(1-2\epsilon)^{-1}
, then

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(\max_{t_{n}\leq s\leq t_{n+1}}X(s)>t_{n+1}^{\rho}M_{n}\right)<\infty.

By the Borel-Cantelli lemma, we have, almost surely,

\max_{t_{n}\leq s\leq t_{n+1}}X(s)\leq t_{n+1}^{\rho}M_{n}\quad\text{for all $n\geq n_{0}$}.

Therefore, for
t_{n}\leq s\leq t_{n+1}
,

\frac{X(s)}{\sqrt{2s^{2\rho}\log\log s}}\leq\frac{t_{n+1}^{\rho}M_{n}}{\sqrt{2t_{n}^{2\rho}\log\log t_{n}}}=\alpha^{\rho+\frac{1}{2}}\sigma\quad a.s.

Since
\alpha>1
is arbitrary, it follows that

\limsup_{s\to\infty}\frac{X(s)}{\sqrt{2s^{2\rho}\log\log s}}\leq\sigma\quad a.s.

∎

3.2
Lower bound for the LIL

We now proceed to establish the lower bound in the LIL.

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
\{X(s)\}_{s\geq 0}
be a centered continuous Gaussian
process satisfying (
1.2
) and

\mathbb{P}\left(X(t_{n+1})-X(t_{n})>\left(2\gamma_{n}\log\log t_{n+1}\right)^{1/2}\ i.o.\right)=1,

(3.4)

where
t_{n}=\alpha^{n}
for any sufficiently large
\alpha
and
\gamma_{n}=\var(X(t_{n+1})-X(t_{n}))
.
Then, we have

\limsup_{s\to\infty}\frac{X(s)}{\sqrt{2s^{2\rho}\log\log s}}\geq\sigma\quad a.s.,

(3.5)

where
\sigma:=R(1,1)^{1/2}
.

Proof.

First we note that

\displaystyle\gamma_{n}

\displaystyle=R(t_{n+1},t_{n+1})-2R(t_{n+1},t_{n})+R(t_{n},t_{n}).

(3.6)

By the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality and (
1.2
), we see that

\displaystyle|R(t_{n+1},t_{n})|\leq R(t_{n+1},t_{n+1})^{1/2}R(t_{n},t_{n})^{1/2}=\sigma^{2}(t_{n+1}t_{n})^{\rho},

which, together with

t_{n}^{\rho}=\alpha^{-\rho}t_{n+1}^{\rho}=o_{\alpha}(t_{n+1}^{\rho})
,
implies

\gamma_{n}=\sigma^{2}t_{n+1}^{2\rho}(1+o_{\alpha}(1))\quad\text{as $n\to\infty$}.

(3.7)

Dividing both sides of the inequality in (
3.4
) by

\sqrt{2t_{n+1}^{2\rho}\log\log t_{n+1}}
yields

\frac{X(t_{n+1})}{\sqrt{2t_{n+1}^{2\rho}\log\log t_{n+1}}}>\left(\frac{\gamma_{n}}{t_{n+1}^{2\rho}}\right)^{1/2}+\frac{X(t_{n})}{\sqrt{2t_{n+1}^{2\rho}\log\log t_{n+1}}}.

Since

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{X(t_{n})}{\sqrt{2t_{n}^{2\rho}\log\log t_{n}}}\leq\sigma\quad a.s.

follows from Proposition
3.1
, by using (
3.7
),
we obtain

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{X(t_{n+1})}{\sqrt{2t_{n+1}^{2\rho}\log\log t_{n+1}}}\geq\sigma-\frac{\sigma}{\alpha^{\rho}}\quad a.s.

Taking the limit
\alpha\to\infty
yields (
3.5
).
∎

4
Proof of (
3.4
) via the Erdős-Rényi’s Borel-Cantelli lemma

To prove (
3.4
), we use the following version of
the Borel-Cantelli lemma, proved by Erdős and Rényi.

Theorem 4.1

(Erdős and Rényi
[
10
]
)
.

Let
A_{n}
be a sequence of events such that
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(A_{n}\right)=\infty
and

\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{n}\sum_{l=1}^{n}\left(\mathbb{P}\left(A_{k}\cap A_{l}\right)-\mathbb{P}\left(A_{k}\right)\mathbb{P}\left(A_{l}\right)\right)}{\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mathbb{P}\left(A_{k}\right)\right)^{2}}=0.

(4.1)

Then,
\mathbb{P}\left(A_{n}\ i.o.\right)=1
.

We denote
t_{n}=\alpha^{n}\ (\alpha>1)
as in
Proposition
3.3
,

a_{k}:=(2\log\log t_{k+1})^{1/2}=(2(\log(k+1)+\log\log\alpha))^{1/2}

(4.2)

and consider the event

A_{k}:=\{X(t_{k+1})-X(t_{k})>\gamma^{1/2}_{k}a_{k}\}.

(4.3)

First we note that since
\chi_{k}:=(X(t_{k+1})-X(t_{k}))/\gamma_{k}^{1/2}
is standard
normal, it follows from the bounds

\frac{1}{2}x^{-1}e^{-\frac{x^{2}}{2}}\leq\int_{x}^{\infty}e^{-\frac{y^{2}}{2}}dy\leq x^{-1}e^{-\frac{x^{2}}{2}}\quad(x\geq 1)

that

\mathbb{P}(A_{k})=\mathbb{P}(\chi_{k}>a_{k})=\Theta_{\alpha}\Big(\frac{1}{k\sqrt{\log k}}\Big).

(4.4)

Here we write
c_{k}=\Theta_{\alpha}(d_{k})
to mean that the ratio
c_{k}/d_{k}
is bounded above and below uniformly in
k=2,3,\dots
by positive constants
depending only on
\alpha
.

Now we estimate the numerator and denominator of
(
4.1
) separately.
The following estimate for the denominator follows immediately from
(
4.4
).

Lemma 4.2
.

Under the settings as above, we have

\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mathbb{P}\left(A_{k}\right)=\Theta_{\alpha}\big(\sqrt{\log n}\big).

(4.5)

Proof.

By summing both sides of (
4.4
), we obtain the
inequality (
4.5
).
∎

Next we estimate the numerator.
Let
R_{a,b}:=[a,\infty)\times[b,\infty)
and

Z_{\delta}\sim N(0,I_{\delta})
, where
I_{\delta}=\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}1&\delta\\
\delta&1\end{smallmatrix}\bigl)
is the covariance matrix of a bivariate normal distribution. We observe that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(A_{k}\cap A_{l})-\mathbb{P}(A_{k})\mathbb{P}(A_{l})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(\{\chi_{k}>a_{k}\}\cap\{\chi_{l}>a_{l}\})-\mathbb{P}(\chi_{k}>a_{k})\mathbb{P}(\chi_{l}>a_{l})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(Z_{\delta_{k,l}}\in R_{a_{k},a_{l}})-\mathbb{P}(Z_{0}\in R_{a_{k},a_{l}}),

where
\delta_{k,l}
denotes the covariance
between
\chi_{k}
and
\chi_{l}
, or equivalently, the
correlation coefficient between the increments

X(t_{k+1})-X(t_{k})
and
X(t_{l+1})-X(t_{l})
.
To proceed, we require an estimate for the tail probability of
the bivariate normal vector
Z_{\delta}
over the quadrant
R_{a,b}
.
In this context, we have the following remarkable formula for the tail distribution
function.

Proposition 4.3
.

Let us define

\psi(\delta,x,y):=\frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{1-\delta^{2}}}\exp\left(-\frac{x^{2}-2\delta xy+y^{2}}{2(1-\delta^{2})}\right),

which is the probability density function of the bivariate normal
random variable
Z_{\delta}\sim N(0,I_{\delta})
.
For
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
and
|\delta|<1
, we have

\displaystyle\phi(\delta,a,b):=\mathbb{P}(Z_{\delta}\in R_{a,b})-\mathbb{P}(Z_{0}\in R_{a,b})=\int_{0}^{\delta}\psi(t,a,b)dt.

Proof.

It is easy to verify that

\partial_{\delta}\psi(\delta,x,y)=\partial_{x}\partial_{y}\psi(\delta,x,y).

(4.6)

Differentiating
\phi(\delta,a,b)
with respect to
\delta

and using identity (
4.6
), we obtain

\displaystyle\partial_{\delta}\phi(\delta,a,b)

\displaystyle=\int_{a}^{\infty}dx\int_{b}^{\infty}dy\partial_{\delta}\psi(\delta,x,y)

\displaystyle=\int_{a}^{\infty}dx\int_{b}^{\infty}dy\partial_{x}\partial_{y}\psi(\delta,x,y)

\displaystyle=\psi(\delta,a,b).

Since
\phi(0,a,b)=0
, integrating both sides with respect
to
\delta
yields the desired formula.
∎

From this proposition, we have the following estimate for

\phi(\delta,a,b)
.

Lemma 4.4
.

For
a,b>0
and
|\delta|<1
,

\displaystyle|\phi(\delta,a,b)|\leq\frac{1}{2\pi}\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\delta^{2}}}\exp\left(-\frac{a^{2}+b^{2}}{2}\right)\exp\left(\frac{|\delta|ab}{1-\delta^{2}}\right)|\delta|.

Proof.

Now we estimate the following:

\phi(\delta,a,b)=\int_{0}^{\delta}\frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{1-t^{2}}}\exp\left(-\frac{a^{2}-2tab+b^{2}}{2(1-t^{2})}\right)dt.

For
|\delta|<1
, we obtain

\displaystyle|\phi(\delta,a,b)|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2\pi}\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\delta^{2}}}\exp\left(-\frac{a^{2}+b^{2}}{2}\right)\left|\int_{0}^{\delta}\exp\left(\frac{tab}{1-t^{2}}\right)dt\right|,

from which the desired inequality follows.
∎

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
\{X(s)\}_{s\geq 0}
be a centered continuous Gaussian
process satisfying (
1.2
), and set

\gamma_{k,l}=\cov(X(t_{k+1})-X(t_{k}),X(t_{l+1})-X(t_{l})),

and
\gamma_{k}=\gamma_{k,k}
, which is the same as in (
3.6
).
Then,

\delta_{k,l}:=\frac{\gamma_{k,l}}{\gamma_{k}^{\frac{1}{2}}\gamma_{l}^{\frac{1}{2}}}=\frac{L_{|k-l|}(\alpha)}{L_{0}(\alpha)},

(4.7)

where

L_{j}(\alpha):=h(\alpha^{j})-\alpha^{-\rho}\{h(\alpha^{j+1})+h(\alpha^{|j-1|})\}+\alpha^{-2\rho}h(\alpha^{j})
.

Proof.

Since
R(x,y)=(xy)^{\rho}h(xy^{-1})
when
x\geq y
,
we easily see from (
1.2
) that

\displaystyle\gamma_{k,l}

\displaystyle=R(t_{k+1},t_{l+1})-R(t_{k+1},t_{l})-R(t_{k},t_{l+1})+R(t_{k},t_{l})

\displaystyle=\alpha^{\rho(k+l+2)}L_{|k-l|}(\alpha),

from which (
4.7
) immediately follows.
∎

In the following lemma, let
a_{k}
be defined as in
(
4.2
), and for simplicity,
we write
\delta_{j}
to denote
\delta_{k,l}
when
|k-l|=j
.

Lemma 4.6
.

Suppose that (
1.2
) and (
1.3
)
hold.
Then, for
0<\epsilon<\frac{1}{2}(\eta\wedge 1)
and sufficiently large
\alpha
, there exist
C_{\eta,\alpha}>0
and

M_{\eta,\epsilon,\alpha}>0
such that

|\phi(\delta_{j},a_{k},a_{k+j})|\leq C_{\eta,\alpha}\frac{1}{k^{1+\epsilon}}\frac{1}{j^{1+\eta-2\epsilon}}

for any
j\geq M_{\eta,\epsilon,\alpha}
.

Proof.

From (
1.3
),
(
4.7
),
and the definition of
L_{j}(\alpha)
,
it follows that
L_{0}(\alpha)\to h(1)>0
as
\alpha\to\infty

and for any sufficiently large
\alpha
,

|\delta_{j}|=\left|\frac{L_{j}(\alpha)}{L_{0}(\alpha)}\right|\leq c_{\eta}(j\log\alpha)^{-\eta}\quad(j\geq 2).

(4.8)

It follows from Lemma
4.4
that

\displaystyle|\phi(\delta_{j},a_{k},a_{k+j})|

\displaystyle\leq C_{\eta,\alpha}\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\delta_{j}^{2}}}\frac{1}{k+1}\frac{1}{k+j+1}\exp\left(\frac{|\delta_{j}|a_{k}a_{k+j}}{1-\delta_{j}^{2}}\right)j^{-\eta}.

(4.9)

For any fixed
\epsilon
with

0<\epsilon<\frac{1}{2}(\eta\wedge 1)
, by (
4.8
), we
have
\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\delta_{j}^{2}}}\leq\frac{1}{2}
and
|\delta_{j}|\leq\epsilon

for sufficiently large
j
. Thus, we see that

\displaystyle\frac{|\delta_{j}|}{1-\delta_{j}^{2}}a^{2}_{k+j}

\displaystyle=\frac{|\delta_{j}|}{1-\delta_{j}^{2}}\times 2(\log(k+j+1)+\log\log\alpha)

\displaystyle\leq\epsilon\log(k+j+1)

holds
for any
j\geq M_{\eta,\epsilon,\alpha}
,
and hence,

\displaystyle\exp\left(\frac{|\delta_{j}|a_{k}a_{k+j}}{1-\delta_{j}^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(\frac{|\delta_{j}|a^{2}_{k+j}}{1-\delta_{j}^{2}}\right)\leq(k+j+1)^{\epsilon}.

It follows from (
4.9
) that

\displaystyle|\phi(\delta_{j},a_{k},a_{k+j})|

\displaystyle\leq C_{\eta,\alpha}\frac{1}{k+1}\frac{1}{k+j+1}(k+j+1)^{\epsilon}j^{-\eta}

\displaystyle\leq C_{\eta,\alpha}\frac{1}{k^{1+\epsilon}}\frac{1}{j^{1+\eta-2\epsilon}}.

This completes the proof.
∎

Lemma 4.7
.

Suppose that (
1.2
) and (
1.3
) hold. Then,

\sum_{k=1}^{n}\sum_{l=1}^{n}\left(\mathbb{P}\left(A_{k}\cap A_{l}\right)-\mathbb{P}\left(A_{k}\right)\mathbb{P}\left(A_{l}\right)\right)=O(\sqrt{\log n})\text{\hskip 10.22217ptas $n\to\infty$}.

(4.10)

Proof.

We divide the summation into

\displaystyle\sum_{k,l=1}^{n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k,l=1\atop{|k-l|<M_{\eta,\epsilon,\alpha}}}^{n}+\sum_{k,l=1\atop{|k-l|\geq M_{\eta,\epsilon,\alpha}}}^{n}=:I_{1}(n)+I_{2}(n).

As for
I_{1}(n)
, from (
4.4
), we see that

\displaystyle|I_{1}(n)|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k,l=1\atop{|k-l|<M_{\eta,\epsilon,\alpha}}}^{n}\mathbb{P}(A_{k})

\displaystyle\leq 2M_{\eta,\epsilon,\alpha}\left(1+c_{\alpha}\sum_{k=2}^{n}\frac{1}{k}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\log k}}\right)

\displaystyle=O(\sqrt{\log n})\quad\text{as $n\to\infty$}.

On the other hand, it follows from Lemma
4.6
that

\displaystyle|I_{2}(n)|

\displaystyle\leq 2\sum_{k=1}^{n}\sum_{{j\geq M_{\eta,\epsilon,\alpha}}}C_{\eta,\alpha}\frac{1}{k^{1+\epsilon}}\frac{1}{j^{1+\eta-2\epsilon}}=O(1)\text{\quad as $n\to\infty$}.

Therefore, we obtain (
4.10
).
∎

Finally, we turn to the proof of (
3.4
).

Proof of (
3.4
).

Erdős-Rényi’s Borel-Cantelli lemma (Theorem
4.1
), together with Lemmas
4.2
and
4.7
, yields (
3.4
).
∎

As a final remark, we provide the proof of Corollary
1.7
.

Proof of Corollary
1.7
.

In the cases
p=p^{\prime}
and
0<p\vee p^{\prime}<3/4
, the result
follows from Theorem
1.1
and Theorem
1.6
,
respectively. Therefore, it remains to verify the two
cases: (i)
p^{\prime}<p
and
p>3/4
, and (ii)
p^{\prime}<p=3/4
.

(i)

If
p^{\prime}<p
and
p>3/4
, then it follows from (
1.1
) that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{|S_{n}-S_{n}^{\prime}|}{n^{2p-1}}=|L_{p,q}|>0\quad\text{a.s.}

This means that the distance between two ERWs diverges to
infinity, i.e., they meet each other only finitely often almost surely.

(ii)

Similarly, if
p^{\prime}<p=3/4
, we obtain the
following result from the
LIL for ERWs (cf.
[
3
]
)

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\pm\frac{S_{n}-S_{n}^{\prime}}{\sqrt{2n\log n\log\log\log n}}=1\quad\text{a.s.}

This means that they meet each other infinitely often almost surely.

∎
