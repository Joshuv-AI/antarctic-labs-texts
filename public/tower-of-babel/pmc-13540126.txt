Smoldering multiple myeloma: a multi-country mixed-methods study on disease perceptions and patient treatment preferences
Authors: Matthew Quaife, Cecilia Jimenez-Moreno, Blanca Gros Otero, Aisha Asra, Niodita Gupta-Werner, Katharine S. Gries, Marie-Kristin Leisten, Kathryn Matt, Robin Carson, Robyn Dennis, Jianming He, Bennett Levitan, Sacheeta Bathija
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimsTo understand disease perceptions and treatment preferences of patients with high-risk smoldering multiple myeloma (HR-SMM) or multiple myeloma (MM) that recently progressed from HR-SMM, and to estimate the minimum progression-free survival (min-PFS) required to accept the treatment burden.MethodsThis mixed-methods study interviewed 50 patients (HR-SMM and MM 1:1) from the USA, France, Italy, and Spain. Qualitative semi-structured interviews explored symptoms, quality of life, and awareness and expectations of SMM treatments. A quantitative thresholding exercise elicited the min-PFS for patients to accept a hypothetical treatment. Results were reported for HR-SMM and MM.ResultsThe most common symptoms were fatigue (68%) and pain (56%); 20% were asymptomatic. Most treatment-naïve patients (n = 31/44) accepted physician recommendations that treatment was not needed, burdensome, or not available. Twenty-three patients (61%) were willing to start treatment while having HR-SMM. Patients expected treatments to delay disease progression (30%) or increase life expectancy (24%), whereas 22% expected to be “cured” if treated. The min-PFS for the given treatment was 60 months; 61 and 59 months for HR-SMM and MM, respectively.ConclusionsConsiderable psychological, physical, and functional burdens were imposed on HR-SMM patients by potential disease progression. Most patients were willing to receive treatment to delay progression and reduce symptomatic burden.

Introduction

Smoldering multiple myeloma (SMM) is an asymptomatic clonal plasma cell disorder associated with a high risk of progression to multiple myeloma (MM) [1,2]. The estimated incidence of SMM was 0.4 to 0.9 per 100 000 in 2016 [3,4]; the prevalence of SMM in people ≥40 years old was 0.53% in 2023 [5]. The incidence of clinically detected SMM was 1.4 per 100 000 in the general population and 2.8 per 100 000 for people ≥40 years old in 2021 [6]. Approximately one in seven patients with MM in the USA are smoldering at diagnosis [4]. The 2‑, 5‑, and 10-year risk of SMM progression to MM is 22%, 42%, and 64%, with the median time to progression of 6.4 years [7]; in addition, the overall risk of progression is 10% per year in the first 5 years, 3% per year for years 6 to 10, and 1% per year thereafter [8].

In clinical practice, risk stratification aims to identify patients with SMM and a 50% or greater risk of progression to MM within 2 years [1]. The current standard of care (SOC) for SMM is active monitoring until progression or until patients fulfill criteria for high-risk (HR)-SMM [2,9]. The National Comprehensive Cancer Network® Guidelines for Multiple Myeloma recommend participating in clinical trials or monitoring all patients with SMM or using daratumumab or lenalidomide for selected patients with HR-SMM [10]. Nonetheless, clinical trial data suggest that early treatment may delay disease progression to MM [11–13]. Follow-up should be tailored to individual needs and, in part, based on risk of progression [1,9,10]. In July 2025, daratumumab became the first approved treatment by the European Medicines Agency for HR-SMM [14].

Treatment of SMM is an active area of clinical research, with ≥40 interventional studies ongoing as of June 2025 [15]. Despite the high level of interest and patient-preference studies in MM [16–22], there are scarce data documenting the potential physical and psychological effects associated with a diagnosis of HR-SMM and patients’ treatment experiences [23–25]. Exploring patient experiences with or without treatment can inform unmet needs, perceptions of safety, and the benefit-risk trade-off a treatment must offer to be acceptable to patients [26]. We conducted this study aiming to understand patient experiences and perceptions after their HR-SMM diagnosis in those with HR-SMM or MM progressed from HR-SMM, including the impact and burden associated with the diagnosis as well as patients’ assessments of treatment attributes and the decision-making processes relating to treatment selection. We also estimated the minimum progression-free survival (min-PFS) required for patients to accept treatment risks and disease burden.

Methods

Study design

This was a non-interventional, observational, mixed-methods study in patients with HR-SMM or newly diagnosed MM, including those with a previous diagnosis of HR-SMM (referred to as MM throughout). We started the study with a targeted literature review to support hypothesis generation and then conducted semi-structured, qualitative interviews that included a quantitative thresholding task.

This study was conducted in accordance with International Council for Harmonization guidelines, preference-based methods from the International Society for Pharmacoeconomics and Outcomes Research, and other applicable requirements and guidelines [27–31]. The protocol was approved by a central institutional review board in the US (Salus; Ethics & Inclusion Study Number 22116-01, approved on 14 June 2022). This type of research, characterized by recruitment outside clinical settings, qualifies as low-risk market research and was determined eligible for ethics exemption for the multi-country submission, covering all four participating countries.

Targeted literature review

The literature review identified available information (2016 to early 2022) about patient experience and burden of being diagnosed with SMM or MM. It provided evidence for the key concepts to explore in the interview and the attributes to include in the thresholding exercise (for details, see Supplemental Materials, Supplemental Figure S1, Supplemental Table S1).

Findings from the literature review [17,32–34] and the knowledge from clinical studies led to three hypotheses: 1) patients with HR-SMM worry about disease progression; 2) patients with HR-SMM or MM prefer treatment at the SMM stage (as opposed to no treatment) and are willing to tolerate adverse effects if treatment delays disease progression to MM; and 3) patients with HR-SMM are less aware of treatment options compared with patients with MM. These hypotheses were used to structure the interview guides and refine the thresholding exercise.

Patient population

Patients with a diagnosis of HR-SMM or MM who recently progressed from HR-SMM were recruited in a 1:1 ratio in Spain, Italy, France, and the USA to participate in a one-on-one, semi-structured virtual interview. Patients were identified through physician referrals, recruiter databases, patient association referrals, and social media groups. Screening and recruiting were conducted by an independent specialist research recruitment company, who identified candidates via clinician referrals and patient panels, conducted screening by phone or online, and invited confirmed eligible candidates to participate.

Inclusion criteria were age ≥18 years, diagnosis of HR-SMM (≥30 days before screening) or MM (<1 year before screening) with a prior diagnosis of HR-SMM (<2 years before screening), and ability to read, speak, and understand their local language (i.e., Spanish, Italian, French, or English). HR-SMM was diagnosed per the International Myeloma Working Group (IMWG) 20/2/20 or Mayo Clinic 20/2/20 risk stratification criteria [35,36]. History of non-MM malignancy or amyloidosis <1 year of screening and current enrollment in a clinical trial of an SMM or MM treatment were key exclusion criteria (Supplemental Table S2).

Following confirmation of diagnosis, subjects were provided with the web link for an electronic consent form, and researchers and participants agreed on a date and time for the interview. The electronic consent form was approved by Salus IRB (No. 22116-01A to -02) and informed the participant about the requirements, risks, and expectations from their participation as well as the data collection, data privacy, and storage procedures to protect anonymity and ensure data protection following GDPR and HIPAA standards or legal data protection requirements. Voluntary consent from participants was documented and all legal data protection requirements were followed. Participants were able to print a PDF version of the consent, giving them enough time to read and consider the study specifics. Consent to take part in the audio-recorded interview was also confirmed verbally at the start of each interview.

Qualitative interviews and threshold exercise

Interviews were conducted by expert interviewers who followed a guide to ensure all concepts of interest were discussed in a standard sequence. Discussions focused on clinical history and disease awareness, signs and symptoms, impacts of the diagnosis on life, and perceptions of and experiences with treatments. Patients also completed a sociodemographic form and the functional scales of the European Organization for Research and Treatment of Cancer Quality of Life Core 30 (EORTC-QLQ-C30) questionnaire (not the complete questionnaire).

In the last part of the interview, patients were asked to complete a thresholding exercise in which they reviewed descriptions of a hypothetical treatment for HR-SMM and indicated their preference for treatment versus no treatment to elicit the min-PFS they would require to accept the hypothetical treatment profile (Supplemental Figure S2). The no-treatment option involved doctor appointments every 2 months (active monitoring, SOC) with a PFS of 38 months [11,37]. The hypothetical treatment included a detailed subcutaneous administration schedule and a 42% risk of any grade adverse events (AEs). The rate for AE risk was conservatively selected on the basis of clinical trial data (for example, the most common reported AE with daratumumab in a phase II trial was fatigue occurring at a frequency of 41.5% [11]). The min-PFS started at 70 months and varied systematically in subsequent treatment-choice questions according to patient choices to capture their willingness to take treatment for HR-SMM and tolerate related treatment burden (that is, treatment administration and risk of side effects) for a given increase in PFS. This design facilitated establishing the min-PFS (that is, the minimum number of months of PFS required to compensate for treatment administration and side effects) for the hypothetical treatment from 40 to 100 months with a 2-month precision (for example, 52–54 months) by asking each patient 5 treatment-choice questions.

Sample size determination

For most studies aiming to understand common perceptions and experiences, 12 interviews from a relatively homogeneous group of participants would provide sufficient information, and the basic meta theme elements were observed with 6 interviews [38]. Therefore, the current study enrolled 50 patients in total, with n = 12 each in France, Italy, and Spain, and n = 14 in the USA. This sample size was sufficient to capture all relevant concepts at the country level with data saturation (i.e., the point when no new information or themes are observed).

Because patients with HR-SMM or MM who progressed from HR-SMM were recruited at a 1:1 ratio, our sample (≥20 patients/group) sought to facilitate comparison between these two groups for quantitative data.

Data analysis

Voice recordings were fully transcribed in English for further analysis. Transcripts were anonymized and cleaned of personal identifiable information. A trained team coded each transcript using Atlas.ti version 22 (ATLAS.ti Scientific Software Development, GmbH) guided by a pre-defined coding dictionary based on expected themes from the interview guide. To establish inter-coder reliability, four coders independently coded the first five transcripts (10%), resolving discrepancies through discussion until >70% coder agreement was achieved. All coding was conducted blind to participants’ thresholding task responses. Code frequency counts and verbatim narrative data (i.e., patient quotes) associated with each code were quality-checked, critically analyzed, and summarized. All symptom data were derived from patient self-report and were not clinically verified; they therefore reflect patient perception and awareness of their condition rather than confirmed diagnoses.

Quantitative data from the sociodemographic form and the EORTC-QLQ-C30 were summarized using descriptive statistics overall and by subgroup (HR-SMM and MM). The findings were summarized in aspects (the same as in interview conduction) including impact on daily functioning, physical impact (ability to sleep, energy/tiredness/fatigue levels, motor coordination/sequential bodily movements, and pain), psychological impact (anxiety or worry/fear, depression or sadness, other emotions such as anger and hopelessness), cognitive impact, social impact, work (occupational) impact, financial impact, family impact, and impact on future perspectives/planning.

Results of the thresholding exercises were summarized using descriptive statistics for all patients, and by HR-SMM and MM groups. Missing data were coded as missing and not imputed.

Results

All 14 patients (100%) from the USA were diagnosed by chance and were asymptomatic when their blood was screened for other reasons, whereas 17 patients (47%) from Europe were diagnosed by chance, and the rest were diagnosed after reporting symptoms.

Six patients (6/50, 12%) took a medication during the SMM phase, four of whom had progressed to MM and two still had HR-SMM. Medications taken alone or in combination during the SMM phase were bortezomib (HR-SMM, n = 1; MM, n = 3), daratumumab (HR-SMM, n = 2; MM, n = 2), dexamethasone (HR-SMM, n = 1; MM, n = 3), and lenalidomide (MM, n = 4) (Table 1). One patient from France reported that SMM treatment was not yet available in their country at the time of their diagnosis, reflecting country-level variation in access to treatment options.

Disease awareness

Most patients understood SMM as a precursor to MM, but their understanding of SMM, HR-SMM, and MM was variable; 10 patients (20%) were unaware of a distinction between HR-SMM and SMM. All patients had a clinician-confirmed diagnosis of HR-SMM or MM; however, 10 patients (10/50, 20%) expressed that they were unaware of the term HR-SMM during the interview. Much of their understanding of the disease was based on the explanation provided by their clinician. Two patients mentioned other sources, such as a conference or independent research.

Symptoms associated with HR-SMM and MM

Ten patients (10/50, 20%) were asymptomatic when interviewed (HR-SMM, n = 8; MM, n = 2), meaning 80% of the combined HR-SMM and MM sample reported at least one symptom. Tiredness/fatigue (n = 34; 68% of total, 85% of symptomatic) and pain (n = 28; 56% of total, 70% of symptomatic) were the most frequently reported symptoms (Figure 1, patient quotes in Table 2). Tiredness/fatigue was also reported as the most bothersome symptom among 41% (14/34) of those affected by it; 36% (10/28) of those affected by pain and 28% (5/18) affected by weakness reported pain and weakness, respectively, as the most bothersome symptom. For tiredness/fatigue, patients also described the intensity (n = 9; e.g., strong, very much, overwhelming) or frequency (n = 3; e.g., chronic, a few days per week, during the day), or both (n = 8; e.g., too tired all the time, always very tired, persistent tiredness every day). Tiredness/fatigue (88% vs 48%), weakness (52% vs 20%), loss of appetite (24% vs 8%), loss of balance (20% vs 4%), and constipation (20% vs 0%) were more common in patients with MM than with HR-SMM (Supplemental Figure S4). Overall symptom burden rates were substantially lower in HR-SMM, with 32% (n = 8/25) of HR-SMM patients fully asymptomatic, which is consistent with published evidence of non-CRAB symptom burden in SMM [32,33]. For patients with MM, symptoms such as pain, fatigue, or weakness could have been present during the SMM phase before progressing to MM, but patients reported an increase in the severity of these symptoms that accompanied progression to MM.

Impact of HR-SMM and MM on health-related quality of life

Overall, the psychological impact (46/50, 92%) of the disease and the impact on perspectives for the future (41/50, 82%) were the most prevalent, even for asymptomatic patients (Figure 2). Forty‑two percent of patients (21/50) considered the psychological impact the most important, and anxiety, worry, and fear were the most common descriptors used. From a qualitative perspective, patients described their reactions to the initial diagnosis as feeling shocked, unsettled, confused, frightened, or in denial. Some patients felt calmer after understanding that a diagnosis of SMM did not necessarily mean progression to MM (patient quotes in Table 2). Some patients reported that the diagnosis increased bonding with family members or gave them a new appreciation for life. All patients in the MM group and 84% (21/25) in the HR-SMM group reported a psychological impact of their diagnosis. Self-reported impacts on day-to-day life were more frequently reported by patients with MM compared with HR-SMM; for instance, the increments in rates were 40% (96% vs 56%) for physical impact, 36% (84% vs 48%) for impact on work, 36% (100% vs 64%) for impact on future perspectives or planning, and 32% (76% vs 44%) for impact on social interactions, respectively (Supplemental Figure S5A).

Patients in the MM group were asked about health-related quality of life (HRQoL) impacts they experienced during the HR-SMM phase versus being diagnosed with MM. The self-reported psychological impact and impact on family, social interactions, or relationships were more prominent at the HR-SMM stage. Impacts on future planning and perspectives, physical function, and daily life activities were those most affected by disease progression (Supplemental Figure S5). All patients reported worsening physical and cognitive impacts with disease progression. Supplemental Table S3 shows EORTC-QLQ-C30 functional scales results.

Treatment experience and expectations

Patients preferred to know the treatment benefits and to be reassured of a low side-effect risk (Table 2). Most patients would rely on clinician advice to make treatment decisions. Of the treatment-naïve patients, 70% were not treated because the physician recommended that treatment was not needed, burdensome, or not available. Only three patients spontaneously mentioned an independent search for treatment information.

A list of potential treatment outcomes was presented to all patients to be ranked by importance. These attributes were presented as items for patients to prioritize according to their personal values, with no claims or implications that any treatment possessed these attributes. Forty-six patients completed the ranking, although 21 (21/50, 42%) felt it was complicated, and 4 (4/50, 8%) were unable to complete the task. Increasing life expectancy and symptom improvement were the 2 most often ranked attributes. “Delay disease progression to MM” was ranked as the most important (14/46, 30%), followed by “increase life expectancy” (11/46, 24%); whereas “improvement of symptoms” (12/46, 26%) and “reduce the impact of SMM in life” (9/46, 20%) were more commonly ranked in the third position or lower (Supplemental Table S4). Overall, 11 patients (11/50, 22%) mentioned spontaneously that they expected a treatment that would offer to “cure” the disease.

Treatment preferences – findings of the thresholding exercise

At the first-choice task to select treatment or no treatment (the treatment option included 3 years of varying frequency of subcutaneous injections with a 70-month PFS and a 42% risk of AEs; the no-treatment option involved a 38-month PFS with active monitoring), 31 patients (31/50, 62%) chose to have treatment. Of these, 23 patients (23/31, 74%) provided a clear reason for choosing to have treatment. The most common reason was trust in the medication (perceived as a “cure,” n = 10), followed by a clear description of benefits outweighing the risks (and administration requirements, n = 6), and third by the improvement in PFS (compared with active monitoring, n = 4). Three patients expressed hesitation when choosing to have treatment, and they would require additional information/evidence supporting the treatment or clinician endorsement. For those choosing not to have treatment, 17 patients (17/19, 90%) provided a clear reason, with “not enough benefit” being the most common (n = 7) followed by the fear of side effects (n = 6); other reasons included the treatment option being unrealistic, needing clinician endorsement, preference for just monitoring, or fear of injections (Table 2).

Overall, the median acceptable min-PFS benefit (minimum acceptable benefit [MAB]; range, 39–100 months) for treatment burden and adverse effects was 60 months (interquartile range [IQR]: 42–99 months). The MAB was similar for the HR-SMM group (n = 25; MAB [IQR]: 61 [45–100] months) and the MM group (n = 25; MAB [IQR]: 59 [41–87] months). Heterogeneity in MAB was found in both MM and HR-SMM groups as PFS >100 months (12/50, 24%), PFS 40 to 50 months (12/50, 24%), and PFS <40 months (8/50, 16%) were the 3 most common MAB ranges. Of the 12 patients who selected the highest PFS range (>100 months), 75% (n = 9) had HR-SMM. For those who selected the lowest PFS range (<40 months), 3 had HR-SMM and 5 had MM. The HR-SMM group had a higher proportion of patients selecting extreme MAB ranges, whereas the MM group had a more even distribution (Supplemental Figure S6).

Of the attributes presented in this exercise (PFS, side effects, mode of administration), the most important attribute influencing patient choice was PFS or side effects (17/41, 41%, equally important). Mode of administration was most important for five patients (owing to a fear of needles, perceiving struggles with the frequency, or taking medication as reminders of being unwell) and least important for eight patients. Some patients considered all attributes important.

Thirty-eight patients (HR-SMM, n = 18; MM, n = 20) were asked whether they would be, or would have been (in the case of patients with MM), willing to be treated during the HR-SMM stage or would prefer (or would have preferred) to wait until disease progression. Twenty-three patients (23/38, 61%) were willing to start treatment, 19 of whom (19/23, 83%) had an MAB ≤70 months (range: <40 to >100 months with a 2-month precision); 5 mentioned certain conditions, for example only with clinician endorsement or only with ensured treatment benefit. Twelve patients (12/38, 32%) would prefer to wait. For patients who could not decide (2/38, 5%) or would depend on clinician advice (1/38, 3%), the MAB was 50 to 70 months (Figure 3). Discussion

The findings of this mixed-methods study informed our understanding of disease awareness in patients with HR-SMM and MM that progressed from HR-SMM, as well as the psychological impacts the diagnosis had on their lives, their preferences for potential treatments, and the MAB required to accept treatment administration and associated adverse effects.

On the basis of the targeted literature review and our knowledge of the disease and treatment landscape of HR-SMM and MM, we derived three hypotheses focusing on patient views of HR-SMM disease progression, awareness of treatment options, and treatment preferences.

The study results showed that individuals diagnosed with HR-SMM are concerned about disease progression. Anxiety, worry, and fear were the most common terms patients used—also used by patients with SMM [25]—when describing the psychological impacts of HR-SMM, even among those who were asymptomatic. Psychological impacts were prevalent both before and after progression to MM.

The study also examined whether individuals with HR-SMM or MM that progressed from HR-SMM prefer treatment during the SMM stage of disease versus no treatment, and a willingness to tolerate adverse effects if the treatment delays disease progression to MM. There was strong evidence from the PFS thresholding task that patients in both groups were willing to tolerate adverse effects if the treatment delayed disease progression to MM. Patients required a median PFS of 60 months (IQR, 42–99 months) to accept a treatment with a 42% risk of adverse effects and requiring a subcutaneous injection. Although similar overall, preferences differed slightly between HR-SMM and MM groups; of those requiring the highest possible MAB (≥100 months), 75% of patients (9/12) were in the HR-SMM group. In ranking exercises, adverse effects were ranked as more important by patients with HR-SMM than those with MM, which may be due to the asymptomatic nature of HR-SMM. Patients with HR-SMM and MM desired a treatment to cure their disease regardless, in part owing to the psychological impact of the disease.

A priori, we hypothesized that individuals diagnosed with HR-SMM may be less aware of treatment options compared with individuals with MM. We found that levels of treatment awareness were low for the entire study population, and there was no evidence that this varied by HR-SMM or MM status. Of the total sample of 50 patients, 6 reported receiving treatment during the HR-SMM phase of their disease: 4 from the MM group and 2 from the HR-SMM group. However, details about how these patients received their treatment (for example, whether by participating in clinical trials or by off-label prescriptions) were not clear—being part of a clinical trial was an exclusion criterion, and participants were not asked about off-label use of treatment during interviews. Examination of the qualitative data provided no indication of a difference in awareness about treatment options; both groups reported being unaware of treatment options for HR-SMM and relying almost exclusively on clinician recommendations to monitor their health until indications of progression.

Patients included in the current study expressed their concerns and worries about disease progression. Evidence from real-world data demonstrated that most patients with HR-SMM progress to MM; however, a much smaller proportion of patients progressed to MM in those who received treatment shortly after HR-SMM diagnosis compared with those who were actively monitored (11% [2/19] vs 72% [51/71]) [39]. Most recently, the benefit of early intervention for HR-SMM, such as a significantly lower risk of disease progression compared with active monitoring, has been further demonstrated in the phase II I-PRISM trial [40] and the phase III AQUILA trial. These encouraging findings suggest an opportunity for patients to achieve their goals of delayed progression. The first treatment for HR-SMM, daratumumab, received approval by the European Commission in July 2025 [14].

Many patients expressed a willingness to accept treatment at the HR-SMM stage in the interviews. The thresholding exercise, which elicited individual-level trade-offs between risk, treatment benefit, and convenience attributes, showed that patients would accept the treatment burden (risk of adverse effects and repetitive treatment administration) with a minimal acceptable PFS of 60 months, which aligns with the findings of the AQUILA trial (ie, PFS was 63% with daratumumab and 41% with active monitoring at 60 months in patients with HR-SMM). In AQUILA, patients treated with daratumumab demonstrated a 51% reduction in risk of disease progression/death versus the active monitoring arm (hazard ratio [HR]: 0.49; 95% confidence interval [CI], 0.36–0.67) in the overall ITT population [41]. This risk reduction was even more pronounced in patients classified as being at high risk of disease progression according to Mayo 2018 risk criteria [36], with a 64% reduction in risk (HR: 0.36, 95% CI 0.23–0.58) compared with active monitoring [41]. In a UK survey of patients with SMM or monoclonal gammopathy of undetermined significance, patients desired treatments to prevent progression to MM rather than curative treatments after the disease had become active [42]. Furthermore, patients noted that treating SMM may contribute to reduced anxiety related to disease progression and may lead to improved quality of life and increased life expectancy. Given most patients with HR-SMM rely on their physicians to decide whether to initiate treatment, shared decision-making and patient education on available options in light of new advances (eg, using daratumumab for HR-SMM) are warranted.

Strengths and limitations

Findings from the current study supplement the data in the existing literature, which has not explored the perceptions and preferences of patients with SMM. We included patients with HR-SMM diagnosed per IMWG or Mayo Clinic risk stratification criteria [35,36], which were commonly used at study initiation prior to the newly developed Precursor Asymptomatic Neoplasms by Group Effort Analysis (PANGEA) criteria [43]. However, insights from the current study amplify the voice of patients with HR-SMM, a previously under-reached population, regarding potential SMM treatments. More broadly, the findings of this study align with those of existing studies, which found that patients hope for treatments that extend life with prolonged progression-free intervals, minimal cancer symptoms, and tolerable adverse effects [16–18,32–34].

The study has several limitations. First, although stated preference tools, including thresholding tasks, are a common method for eliciting stakeholder preferences for a wide range of products and services [44,45], the degree to which reported preferences reflect actual preferences remains uncertain [44,46,47]. Some stated preference tools may have utility in predicting real-world choices [44], but there is less evidence on thresholding tasks specifically. As with all stated preference tasks, the results must be interpreted within the context of the set of attributes and levels included in the study design. For example, the results of the current study can interpret the minimum acceptable benefit only for the 42% adverse effect risk presented in the task. It is worth noting that this risk was the most conservative estimate based on a specific symptom reported from a trial [11], whereas patients in real-world settings may have different considerations depending on the symptoms as well as physical and/or psychological experiences that are most meaningful to each person. In addition, adverse effect was a composite measure; different people weighed each component differently, which introduced noise to the results. A more flexible tool for quantitative-preference elicitation, such as a discrete-choice experiment or multidimensional thresholding tool, would facilitate estimation of trade-offs across attributes, though it would require a larger sample size than that obtainable in this study.

Moreover, qualitative methods have unique limitations. For example, acceptability biases may impact responses, a main idea may dominate the conversation, or information may be missed if not all questions are covered or given equal attention by interviews. Further, in mixed-methods studies such as this, concepts measured may be subtly different between methods used. The sampling frame sought to achieve heterogeneity in perspectives across disease stages and countries, and this intention for variety of perspective means generalizability may be difficult, especially given that a high proportion of patients were educated beyond high school. Furthermore, approximately 40% of participants were aged ≤50 years, which may not reflect the older age profile typical of real-world SMM populations [5,8], partly attributable to recruitment via social media and patient networks that inherently favor younger patients. The perceptions and treatment preferences of older patients with different comorbidity profiles and treatment priorities may therefore be underrepresented, and caution should be exercised when extrapolating findings to this group. The higher proportion of patients with HR-SMM with an ECOG score >0 versus patients with MM may also indicate a potential selection bias. Because only the functional scales of the EORTC-QLQ-C30 were administered, this precluded full standardized domain scoring and limits direct comparison with studies reporting complete instrument data. Finally, small sample sizes precluded country-level analysis of symptoms, disease impacts, and treatment preferences, and regional differences in patient perceptions may exist. Symptom data were collected via open-ended patient report and are not equivalent to clinician-assessed CRAB criteria; some symptom attribution by patients with HR-SMM may reflect comorbidities or general health rather than SMM itself. Notably, diagnostic pathways differed between the USA and Europe, where all US patients (100%) received their SMM diagnosis incidentally while asymptomatic compared with 47% of European patients, and treatment availability varied by country. Future studies with larger, country-stratified samples are needed to examine these differences systematically.

Conclusion

Patients highlighted the considerable psychological as well as physical and functional burden imposed by the potential for progression from HR-SMM to MM. Additionally, patients were willing to hear about potential treatment options from their clinicians and benefit from shared decision-making regarding their care. Furthermore, this study demonstrates a willingness among patients with HR-SMM and MM to tolerate the adverse effects and scheduled administration of SMM treatments in exchange for a delay in progression to MM.
