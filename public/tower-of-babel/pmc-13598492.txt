Retrospective Analysis of Imaging Influencing Factors and Early Stability of Cage Subsidence After Percutaneous Endoscopic Lumbar Interbody Fusion
Authors: Linsong Lu, Xinwei Liu, Kuo Xu, Hao Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo identify imaging predictors of cage subsidence following percutaneous endoscopic lumbar interbody fusion (PELIF) and assess their association with early postoperative clinical outcomes, and to conduct the analysis through a retrospective design.MethodsA retrospective analysis was conducted on 189 patients who underwent PELIF between January 2022 and June 2024. Patients were classified into subsidence (48 cases) and non-subsidence (141 cases) groups based on radiographic criteria. After screening the differential indicators through single-factor analysis, a multivariate logistic regression was used to identify independent imaging risk factors. Clinical outcomes, including lumbar range of motion (ROM), Visual Analog Scale (VAS), and Oswestry Disability Index (ODI), were compared between groups at baseline, 6 months, and final follow-up.ResultsCompared with the non-subsidence group, the endplate bone quality (EBQ) score and vertebral bone quality (VBQ) score of the subsidence group patients were both increased, while the average CT value of the vertebral body, the average CT value of the upper and lower vertebral bodies of the responsible segment, and the area of the multifidus muscle were significantly decreased (P < 0.05). Multivariate logistic regression analysis showed that EBQ score (OR=2.061, 95% CI: 1.458~2.915) and VBQ score (OR=1.854, 95% CI: 1.362~2.527) were independent risk factors for device subsidence, while average CT values of vertebral bodies (OR=0.240, 95% CI: 0.132~0.437), average CT values of upper and lower vertebral bodies of the responsible segment (OR=0.347, 95% CI: 0.228~0.528), and multifidus muscle area (OR=0.453, 95% CI: 0.291~0.705) were protective factors (P<0.05). Compared with the non-subsidence group, the ROM of the patients in the subsidence group was significantly lower at 6 months after surgery and at the last follow-up (P < 0.05), and the VAS score and ODI score were also significantly higher (P < 0.05).ConclusionImaging parameters, including vertebral CT values, EBQ/VBQ scores, and multifidus muscle area, are significant predictors of cage subsidence after PELIF. Subsidence is associated with poorer early postoperative outcomes. Based on the results of the above retrospective analysis, comprehensive preoperative imaging assessment is essential for risk stratification and optimizing surgical strategies.Keywords: endoscopic lumbar interbody fusion, fusion cage subsidence, imaging, early stability, correlation

Introduction

Spinal degenerative diseases are common causes of lower back pain and neurological dysfunction in clinical practice, mainly including lumbar disc herniation, lumbar spinal stenosis, and discogenic back pain, which seriously affect the quality of life of patients.1 Lumbar disc herniation is one of the most common types of degenerative diseases of the spine. Its pathological basis is the protrusion of the nucleus pulposus through the fibrous ring, which compresses the nerve roots or cauda equina, leading to lower back and leg pain and neurological dysfunction. The treatment strategies for lumbar disc herniation include conservative treatment, drug therapy, and surgical intervention, among which the selection of surgical plan needs to comprehensively consider factors such as the patient’s clinical symptoms, imaging findings, and degree of neurological damage.2 In addition to disc herniation, spontaneous degeneration of intervertebral discs is also an important pathological process leading to spinal segment instability. The loss of water, decrease in height, and decrease in elasticity associated with intervertebral disc degeneration can further exacerbate the imbalance of the spinal mechanical environment and promote the progression of degenerative diseases.3 To restore spinal stability, interbody fusion has become an important means of treating degenerative lumbar diseases. Percutaneous endoscopic lumbar interbody fusion (PELIF) is an important method for treating degenerative spinal diseases such as lumbar spinal stenosis and disc herniation. It can enhance spinal stability and minimize the impact of adjacent segment degeneration.4 However, implant related complications during spinal stabilization surgery remain important factors affecting surgical efficacy, including screw loosening, rod displacement, internal fixation failure, and cage subsidence.5 Among them, cage subsidence is one of the most common postoperative complications.6 Fusion cage subsidence refers to a decrease in intervertebral height caused by compression of the endplate or trabecular bone after implantation, which may lead to a reduction in the anterior convexity angle of the vertebral body, indirect decompression failure, and even non-fusion or vertebral deformity.7 Fusion cage subsidence not only affects immediate postoperative stability, but may also lead to long-term surgical failure and increased risk of revision surgery. Therefore, it is necessary to find reliable methods to early identify the occurrence of fusion cage subsidence after PELIF, intervene in treatment in a timely manner, and improve the postoperative quality of life of patients.

The etiology of cage subsidence following lumbar interbody fusion is multifactorial. Prior research has implicated various factors, such as fixation techniques and paraspinal muscle atrophy, in influencing cage loading and subsequently increasing the risk of subsidence.8 Moreover, biomechanical studies suggest that while larger fusion device heights can improve initial intervertebral stability, excessive distraction of the intervertebral space may paradoxically elevate endplate stress, thereby increasing the propensity for subsidence.9 Advanced imaging modalities, including X-ray, computed tomography (CT), and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), are routinely employed for both diagnostic purposes and prognostic assessment in spinal surgery.10,11 While these factors have been extensively investigated in the context of traditional open or transforaminal lumbar interbody fusion (TLIF) procedures, their specific relevance and manifestations in PELIF remain less thoroughly explored. The unique surgical characteristics of PELIF, such as its limited operative field and distinct endplate preparation techniques compared to conventional open approaches, may introduce novel mechanisms and influencing factors for cage subsidence.

Consequently, a systematic evaluation of the radiographic characteristics of cage subsidence following endoscopic fusion, utilizing high-resolution imaging (CT, MRI) in conjunction with clinical data, is urgently needed. Such an investigation should also explore its correlation with early postoperative stability, including parameters like vertebral displacement and intervertebral space maintenance. Building upon this recognized gap, the present study aims to comprehensively investigate the relationship between cage subsidence and early postoperative stability following PELIF. Furthermore, it seeks to identify and analyze the specific imaging indicators that influence the occurrence of cage subsidence. Ultimately, this research endeavors to provide robust, imaging-based evidence to facilitate the preoperative identification of high-risk patients and to inform the development of optimized, individualized surgical strategies for PELIF.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Patient Cohort

This study was a retrospective observational study. No prospective formal sample size estimation or power calculation was conducted. The sample consisted of consecutive cases that met the inclusion and exclusion criteria during the study period. Multivariate Logistic regression included a total of 5 independent variables. There were 48 cases in the subsidence group, and the ratio of the number of events to the number of independent variables (EPV) was 9.6. To control the risk of model overfitting caused by limited sample size, this study used Bootstrap method for internal validation in statistical analysis to evaluate model stability. The final sample size included was determined by the total number of cases that met the criteria.

A retrospective cohort study was conducted, including 189 consecutive patients who underwent single-level Percutaneous Endoscopic Lumbar Interbody Fusion (PELIF) at our institution between January 2022 and June 2024. Patients were stratified into subsidence (48 cases) and non-subsidence (141 cases) groups based on the presence or absence of postoperative implant subsidence, as determined by radiographic criteria. The diagnosis of cage subsidence was independently assessed by two senior spine surgeons who were not involved in the surgical procedures or data analysis and were blinded to other clinical data. Subsidence was defined according to established criteria.12 On lateral lumbar radiographs, the mean intervertebral height (calculated as the average of the anterior and posterior disc heights) at the surgical level was measured at 1 week postoperatively and at the final follow-up. A reduction of ≥2 mm in mean intervertebral height at the final follow-up, compared to the 1-week postoperative measurement, was classified as subsidence. Additionally, sagittal CT reconstructions were reviewed for evidence of fusion device protrusion into the superior or inferior vertebral cancellous bone exceeding 2 mm, or the presence of endplate fractures, as supplementary diagnostic criteria.

Surgical Procedure

All PELIF procedures were performed by the same team of experienced spine surgeons. Patients were placed in the prone position under general anesthesia, with abdominal suspension to reduce intra-abdominal pressure and minimize venous plexus engorgement. The target intervertebral level was localized using C-arm fluoroscopy, and the surgical incision was marked accordingly. A paramedian longitudinal skin incision, approximately 1.5 cm in length, was made. An endoscopic working channel was established under fluoroscopic guidance.

Under endoscopic visualization, the relevant anatomical structures at the target level were exposed. Endoscopic foraminoplasty was performed to enlarge the lateral recess and neural canal, ensuring thorough decompression and protection of both exiting and traversing nerve roots. The ligamentum flavum was meticulously excised to expose and protect the dural sac. Protruding nucleus pulposus material was completely removed to relieve neural compression, and the intervertebral disc space was thoroughly debrided. The cartilaginous endplates were carefully scraped, preserving the integrity of the bony endplates, until punctate bleeding was observed, indicating an optimal bone graft bed. Autologous or allogeneic bone graft material was implanted into the anterior and anteromedial regions of the intervertebral space.

An appropriately sized interbody fusion cage (PEEK or titanium alloy), pre-filled with autologous bone chips, was selected based on preoperative measurements and intraoperative trial fitting. Under fluoroscopic guidance, the cage was precisely positioned within the posterior aspect of the intervertebral space to restore intervertebral height and lumbar lordosis. All patients received adjunctive fixation with a percutaneous pedicle screw system. Following confirmation of optimal implant positioning, the surgical field was irrigated, hemostasis was achieved, and the incision was closed in layers.

Clinical Data Collection

Clinical data collected included demographic variables (gender, age), comorbidities (diabetes mellitus, hypertension), primary diagnosis (lumbar spinal stenosis, lumbar disc herniation), body mass index (BMI), surgical level (L2/3, L3/4, L4/5, L5/S1), operative time, estimated intraoperative blood loss, and length of hospital stay.

Lumbar range of motion (ROM) at the surgical level was assessed preoperatively, at 6 months postoperatively, and at the final follow-up. ROM was determined from lateral flexion-extension radiographs by measuring the angular difference between the superior endplate of the cranial vertebra and the inferior endplate of the caudal vertebra at the affected segment.

Computed Tomography (CT) Parameters

Lumbar spine CT scans were performed using a 64-slice spiral CT scanner (tube voltage: 120 kV; tube current: 300 mA; slice thickness: 0.625 mm; helical pitch: 1.0). The scan range included the affected segment and one adjacent vertebra above and below (eg, L3 to S1 for an L4/5 procedure).

All images were imported into a dedicated post-processing workstation (Advantage Workstation 4.6). Two radiologists, each with over five years of experience in spinal imaging, independently delineated regions of interest (ROIs) and measured CT values, blinded to clinical outcomes and grouping information. The inter-observer measurement consistency was evaluated using the Intraclass Correlation Coefficient (ICC). Among them, the inter-observer ICC of the average CT value of the vertebral bodies was 0.912, and the inter-observer ICC of the average CT values of the upper and lower vertebral bodies of the responsible segments was 0.897. For cases with ICC < 0.85, the measurement was re-conducted by the third senior physician with over 10 years of experience, and the result was arbitrated. The final arbitrated value was included in the statistical analysis.

The system software automatically calculated:

Mean CT value of the vertebrae directly involved in the surgical segment (eg, L4 and L5 for L4/5), reflecting local bone density.

Mean CT value of the vertebrae directly involved in the surgical segment (eg, L4 and L5 for L4/5), reflecting local bone density.

Mean CT values of the vertebrae immediately above and below the surgical segment (eg, L3 and S1 for L4/5), reflecting the bone density of adjacent load-bearing vertebrae.

Mean CT values of the vertebrae immediately above and below the surgical segment (eg, L3 and S1 for L4/5), reflecting the bone density of adjacent load-bearing vertebrae.

Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) Metrics

Preoperative MRI was used to assess vertebral bone quality (VBQ) and endplate bone quality (EBQ) scores:

VBQ Score

Calculated from T1-weighted midline sagittal images. ROIs were placed in the L1–L4 vertebral bodies and at the L3 cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) level. The VBQ score was defined as the ratio of the mean signal intensity of L1–L4 vertebral bodies to the mean signal intensity of the L3 CSF.

EBQ Score

Calculated from T1-weighted axial images. ROIs were placed 3 mm above and below the endplates of the surgical segment and at the L3 CSF level. The EBQ score was defined as the ratio of the average signal intensity of the upper/lower endplates to the average signal intensity of the L3 CSF.

Multifidus Muscle Area

On T2-weighted axial images at the affected intervertebral level, the multifidus muscles were manually contoured bilaterally. The system software automatically calculated the cross-sectional area within each ROI.

The measurement of the above MRI-related indicators was also independently completed by two radiologists with more than 5 years of experience in spinal imaging diagnosis under blind method. The inter-observer consistency was evaluated using ICC. The inter-observer ICC for EBQ scores was 0.885, for VBQ scores it was 0.876, and for the area of the multifidus muscle it was 0.903. For the measurement results with an ICC < 0.85, the final values were reviewed and finalized by the third senior physician.

Pain and Functional Outcome Assessment

Visual Analogue Scale (VAS)

Pain intensity was assessed using a 10-point Visual Analogue Scale (VAS),13 where 0 indicated “no pain” and 10 indicated “the most severe pain imaginable”. Patients marked their pain level on a 10-cm horizontal line. The VAS is a validated, widely used tool for quantifying subjective pain intensity in orthopedic postoperative assessment.

Oswestry Disability Index (ODI)

The Oswestry Disability Index (ODI), is a validated instrument for assessing functional disability due to lumbar spinal disorders.14 It comprises 10 sections, each scored from 0 (no disability) to 5 (maximal disability), with a total score ranging from 0 to 50. Higher scores indicate greater functional impairment. Patients completed the ODI questionnaire through face-to-face interviews conducted by trained research staff to ensure accuracy.

VAS and ODI scores were recorded at three times: preoperatively, 6 months postoperatively, and at the final follow-up.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 20.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables were assessed for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Normally distributed data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and compared using independent samples t-tests (between groups) or paired samples t-tests (within groups across time points). Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages, with intergroup comparisons performed using chi-square (χ2) tests or Fisher’s exact tests, as appropriate. A two-sided P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Before constructing the multivariate Logistic regression model, the collinearity of the independent variables was examined through the variance inflation factor (VIF) test. The VIF values of all variables were < 8, indicating that there was no significant collinearity and satisfying the independence assumption of the independent variables for the regression model. When conducting a two-factor repeated measures ANOVA, the Mauchly’s sphericity test was first performed. When the sphericity assumption was not met (P < 0.05), the Greenhouse-Geisser method was used to correct the degrees of freedom to ensure the robustness of the F-test results.

The inter-observer consistency assessment for the measurement of imaging parameters was as follows: The delineation of the regions of interest (ROIs) for CT and MRI indicators was independently completed by 2 physicians with more than 5 years of experience in spinal imaging diagnosis. The intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC) for each indicator between the two measurers ranged from 0.862 to 0.941, indicating excellent overall measurement consistency. For the individual indicators with significant measurement result differences (ICC < 0.85), the third senior physician would re-measure and arbitrate, and the final result would be included in the statistical analysis based on the arbitration outcome.

Multivariate Logistic regression model was used to analyze the influencing factors of device subsidence after PELIF. A difference was considered statistically significant when P < 0.05. To evaluate the effects of group (subsidence vs non-subsidence), time (preoperative, 6 months postoperative, final follow-up), and their interaction on ROM, VAS, and ODI scores, a two-way repeated measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) was employed. Post hoc pairwise comparisons were conducted using the Least Significant Difference (LSD) t-test where appropriate.

Results

Univariate Analysis of Baseline Characteristics and Imaging Parameters

Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics, including gender, diabetes mellitus, hypertension, lumbar spinal stenosis, lumbar disc herniation, surgical level distribution, age, body mass index (BMI), surgical duration, intraoperative blood loss, hospitalization duration, preoperative intervertebral disc height, lumbar lordosis angle, and pelvic tilt angle, demonstrated no statistically significant differences between the subsidence and non-subsidence groups. Conversely, significant differences were observed in several imaging parameters. In these cases, compared to the non-subsidence group, patients in the subsidence group exhibited significantly increased endplate bone quality (EBQ) scores and vertebral bone quality (VBQ) scores (P < 0.05). Concurrently, the subsidence group showed markedly reduced mean vertebral CT values, mean CT values of vertebrae above and below the affected segment, and multifidus muscle area (P < 0.05). Multivariate Logistic Regression Analysis of Factors Influencing Cage Subsidence

To identify independent predictors of post-PELIF cage subsidence, a multivariate logistic regression analysis was performed. Cage subsidence (coded as 1 for subsidence, 0 for no subsidence) served as the dependent variable. Independent variables included those identified as significant in the univariate analysis: mean vertebral CT value, mean CT values of vertebrae above and below the affected segment, multifidus muscle area, EBQ score, and VBQ score. Prior to regression, multiple collinearity testing was conducted, with all Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) values found to be less than 8, confirming the absence of significant collinearity among the independent variables.

In the sample of this study, the logistic regression analysis revealed that EBQ score and VBQ score were significant risk factors for post-PELIF cage subsidence. Conversely, higher vertebral mean CT value of vertebrae above and below the affected segment, and multifidus muscle area were identified as significant protective factors against subsidence (P < 0.05). Relationship Between Postoperative Cage Subsidence and Lumbar Range of Motion (ROM)

A two-way repeated measures ANOVA was conducted to analyze changes in lumbar ROM, with “group” (subsidence vs non-subsidence) as the between-subjects factor and “time” (preoperative, 6 months postoperative, final follow-up) as the within-subjects factor. The analysis revealed significant main effects for both group and time, as well as a significant interaction effect between group and time (P < 0.05).

Post-hoc LSD-t tests indicated no significant difference in preoperative ROM between the subsidence and non-subsidence groups (P > 0.05). However, during the follow-up period of this study, compared to the non-subsidence group, patients in the subsidence group exhibited significantly reduced lumbar ROM at both 6 months postoperatively and at the final follow-up (P < 0.05). Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) Scores

For VAS scores, ANOVA demonstrated statistically significant main effects for group, time, and a significant group time interaction effect (P < 0.05). Post-hoc analysis revealed no significant difference in preoperative VAS scores between the two groups (P > 0.05). During the follow-up of this study, it was observed that at both 6 months postoperatively and at the final follow-up, the subsidence group reported significantly higher VAS scores compared to the non-subsidence group (P < 0.05).

Discussion

The occurrence of lumbar intervertebral disc degenerative diseases is closely related to the spontaneous degeneration of the intervertebral disc. Pathological changes such as loss of nucleus pulposus water content, degradation of extracellular matrix, and endplate calcification can spontaneously initiate and accelerate the degeneration process, ultimately leading to loss of intervertebral space height, spinal instability, and compression of nerve roots, etc.2 The treatment for this type of disease needs to be stratified according to the severity of the condition, with conservative treatment as the initial plan. For patients who have failed standardized conservative treatment, have segmental instability or spinal stenosis, interbody fusion is the core surgical procedure for reconstructing spinal stability.3 This retrospective analysis systematically investigated the imaging factors associated with cage subsidence following percutaneous endoscopic lumbar interbody fusion (PELIF) in a cohort of 189 patients. Furthermore, it elucidated the correlation between subsidence and early postoperative stability and functional recovery. Our key findings indicate that higher mean vertebral CT values, elevated mean CT values of adjacent vertebrae at the affected level, and a larger multifidus muscle area were identified as independent protective factors against cage subsidence. Conversely, higher endplate bone quality (EBQ) and vertebral bone quality (VBQ) scores emerged as independent risk factors. Critically, cage subsidence was significantly associated with reduced postoperative lumbar range of motion (ROM), more pronounced postoperative pain (Visual Analogue Scale, VAS), and poorer functional recovery (Oswestry Disability Index, ODI). This study is a retrospective observational study. The conclusions drawn only indicate the correlations between the variables and cannot infer causal relationships.

Our results demonstrated that patients in the subsidence group exhibited significantly lower mean vertebral CT values and mean CT values of adjacent vertebrae at the surgical level compared to the non-subsidence group. Multivariate logistic regression analysis rigorously confirmed both measures as independent protective factors against cage subsidence after PELIF (P < 0.05). As a well-established radiographic indicator reflecting bone mineral density, CT values directly correlate with the weight-bearing capacity and structural integrity of vertebral cancellous bone.15 Low CT values signify reduced bone mass and compromised microarchitecture, leading to inadequate vertebral support for the implanted fusion device. Under the dynamic mechanical loading inherent in the postoperative period, this predisposition can result in endplate compression or device embedding, ultimately culminating in subsidence.16 This finding is consistent with existing literature. For instance, Xi et al17 observed a significantly higher incidence of fusion cage subsidence in patients with lower vertebral Hounsfield units (directly correlated with CT values) in a study of lateral lumbar interbody fusion, attributing this to insufficient support strength due to diminished bone density. Zhou et al18 further corroborated this in cervical fusion studies, demonstrating that reduced bone density increases the risk of fusion cage subsidence, while enhancing vertebral support strength effectively lowers this complication rate. The present study extends the applicability of these crucial findings to the PELIF procedure, underscoring the significant clinical value of preoperative CT assessment of vertebral and adjacent segment bone density for identifying patients at high risk of subsidence.

This study also revealed that patients in the subsidence group presented with significantly higher EBQ and VBQ scores than those in the non-subsidence group, with both scores independently identified as risk factors for cage subsidence (P < 0.05). EBQ and VBQ scores, derived from MRI signal intensity, serve as indirect indicators of the microstructural integrity and metabolic status of the endplates and vertebral bone tissue. Elevated values typically suggest abnormal bone metabolism, characterized by increased bone resorption and/or decreased bone formation, which consequently leads to reduced bone quality and compromised mechanical strength.19 Given that the endplate serves as the primary interface and direct support structure for fusion devices, abnormal endplate bone quality can lead to uneven stress distribution. Concurrently, reduced vertebral bone quality further weakens the overall supportive capacity, collectively increasing the susceptibility to subsidence.20 These findings align with previous research; Ai et al21 reported that EBQ and VBQ scores demonstrated high predictive value for cage subsidence (AUCs of 0.829 and 0.799, respectively) in their study of transforaminal lumbar interbody fusion (TLIF), thereby corroborating the present results. Yan et al22 additionally noted that elevated EBQ and VBQ scores frequently accompany adjacent vertebral degeneration, which can alter the spinal mechanical environment and indirectly affect the positional stability of fusion implants, offering a supplementary explanation for our observations. The application of these MRI-based metrics to the PELIF procedure further validates the universal applicability of bone quality scoring in predicting complications following minimally invasive spinal fusion surgery.

Our analysis indicated that patients in the subsidence group exhibited a significantly smaller multifidus muscle area compared to the non-subsidence group, with multifidus muscle area emerging as an independent protective factor against cage subsidence (P < 0.05). The multifidus muscle, a critical component of the core muscle group, plays a pivotal role in segmental spinal stability. Its contraction effectively distributes mechanical loads across the vertebrae and fusion devices, thereby mitigating local stress concentrations at the implant-endplate interface.23 A reduction in multifidus muscle area is typically associated with muscle fiber atrophy and fatty infiltration, leading to diminished contractile force and impaired dynamic spinal stability. This impairment results in an increased direct transmission of postoperative mechanical loads to the fusion device-endplate interface, consequently elevating the risk of subsidence.24 These findings are consistent with the systematic review by Tieppo-Francio et al,25 which concluded that multifidus dysfunction, both pre- and post-lumbar surgery, is closely associated with increased postoperative complication rates. Biomechanical investigations by Alkalay et al26 further confirm that the stabilizing effect of paraspinal muscles reduces stress loads on the fusion device, thereby decreasing complications such as subsidence and displacement. By focusing on the minimally invasive PELIF procedure, this study supplements existing evidence on the association between multifidus muscle function and fusion device stability in a minimally invasive context. It suggests that preoperative assessment of multifidus morphology and the implementation of early postoperative muscle function rehabilitation training may represent valuable strategies to reduce the risk of subsidence.

The present study employed a two-way repeated measures ANOVA, which revealed significant main effects of group (subsidence vs non-subsidence), time (preoperative, 6 months postoperative, final follow-up), and significant interaction effects (P < 0.05) for ROM, VAS scores, and ODI scores. Post-hoc analyses demonstrated that patients in the subsidence group exhibited significantly lower ROM at 6 months postoperatively and at the final follow-up compared to the non-subsidence group. Concurrently, VAS and ODI scores were significantly higher in the subsidence group at these time points. Notably, no significant differences existed between the two groups preoperatively. These findings unequivocally indicate that cage subsidence is not merely a radiographic observation but directly compromises early postoperative spinal stability and functional recovery. There are various types of implant related complications in spinal fusion surgery, including device subsidence, displacement, pseudarthrosis formation, and internal fixation loosening. Among them, device subsidence is a high-risk complication type in the early stage after interbody fusion surgery, which is related to multiple factors such as bone quality, endplate treatment, and fusion device design. It can directly affect the surgical efficacy and patient prognosis.5 The reduction in intervertebral disc height and the disruption of spinal biomechanical equilibrium caused by fusion cage subsidence constitute the core mechanisms underlying these functional abnormalities.27 Singhatanadgige et al28 reported that cage subsidence leads to a loss of anterior disc height, which can alter lumbar lordosis and potentially cause nerve root compression or traction, resulting in chronic pain and restricted mobility. Biomechanical studies by Walsh et al29 have also confirmed that cage subsidence disrupts the biomechanical equilibrium of the surgical segment, leading to increased local micro-movement or abnormal stiffness. This not only diminishes spinal dynamic stability but also adversely impacts patients’ motor function and overall quality of life. This study further demonstrated, through longitudinal follow-up data, that these adverse effects persist at 6 months postoperatively and at final follow-up, underscoring that early identification and timely intervention for cage subsidence are crucial for optimizing long-term patient outcomes.

The innovation of this study lies in its pioneering systematic integration of CT-based bone density measurement, MRI-based bone quality scoring (EBQ/VBQ), and multifidus morphology assessment within the specific context of the PELIF procedure. This comprehensive approach allowed for a thorough exploration of the predictive value of multimodal imaging indicators for cage subsidence and clarified their clinical correlation with early postoperative stability and functional recovery. Compared to previous studies predominantly focused on open or minimally invasive transforaminal approaches, this research specifically targets the endoscopic technique. Its findings provide more directly applicable guidance for preoperative planning, patient risk stratification, and postoperative management in PELIF procedures, thereby enhancing the precision of patient selection and surgical strategy.

This study still has some limitations. Firstly, this study was a single-center retrospective observational study. The design of the study determines that it can only identify the correlations between variables, but cannot establish causal relationships, and it cannot completely avoid selection bias. Secondly, bone density assessment relied on CT values, which are commonly used clinically but not as accurate as quantitative CT or dual energy X-ray absorptiometry. Moreover, the sample size of this study was limited, and the extrapolation of data from a single center has certain limitations. Finally, the imaging changes at different time points after the surgery were not dynamically monitored. Future studies can achieve a more precise depiction of the development trajectory of the subsidence by setting more frequent follow-up time points. The above conclusions still need to be further verified through larger-scale prospective multicenter cohort studies.

Conclusion

This retrospective single-center study suggested that the vertebral CT values, the EBQ/VBQ scores, and multifidus muscles area were significantly associated with the postoperative cage subsidence in PELIF. In this group of cases, there was a significant correlation between fusion device subsidence and postoperative decreased lumbar spine mobility, worsening pain, and poor functional recovery. Preoperative comprehensive evaluation of the above imaging features may help identify high-risk patients and provide reference for the development of individualized surgical strategies and rehabilitation plans. However, the above conclusion is based on a retrospective design and cannot infer a causal relationship. The relevant imaging indicators still need to be further verified fo their predictive efficacy and external applicability through well-designed prospective multicenter studies before they can be used as a definitive predictive tool for clinical decisions.
