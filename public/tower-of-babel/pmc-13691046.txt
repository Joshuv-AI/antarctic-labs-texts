The pivotal role of macrophages in tumorigenesis and pioneering cancer immunotherapy across pre-clinical and clinical frontiers
Authors: Mina Alimohammadi, Amir Mohammad Mirnejad, Najma Farahani, Neda Hedayati, Kiavash Hushmandi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) are among the most abundant cells in the tumor microenvironment (TME), with M1 and M2 polarization exhibiting anti-tumorigenic and tumorigenic functions, respectively. Originating from both embryonic precursors and circulating monocytes derived from hematopoietic stem cells, TAMs acquire distinct transcriptional, metabolic, and functional identities shaped by the TME. The interactions in the TME among various cells, including cancerous and non-cancerous cells, can determine the polarization and function of TAMs within the tumor. More recently, TAMs have garnered significant interest as a promising therapeutic target in oncology. The TAMs have been shown to enhance proliferation, metastasis, and angiogenesis in human cancers. Notably, cytokines and chemokines, among others, can regulate the infiltration of TAMs in TME. Given the crucial role of macrophages in regulating inflammation and immune responses, targeting these cells has become a significant focus in cancer immunotherapy. Currently, macrophages have been targeted through three strategies, including suppressing macrophage/monocyte recruitment, depletion of TAMs, and re-education of TAMs. Each of these approaches is being investigated in preclinical models and clinical trials, either as monotherapy or in combination with immune checkpoint inhibitors. TAMs can also be engineered to act as drug delivery tools, carrying nanoparticles directly into tumors. In the clinic, some types of TAMs, marked by proteins like CD163 and CD204, can help predict patient outcomes in several cancers. This review investigates the origins of TAMs and the mechanisms underlying their polarization and metabolism. Furthermore, we study macrophage-driven tumor progression and devising effective targeting strategies, as well as challenges that remain and where the field may go next in macrophage-based cancer treatment. Going forward, combining single-cell profiling with multi-target therapies may offer the best path toward more effective, TAM-based cancer treatments.

Introduction

The most fundamental phagocytes in living organisms, known as macrophages, are responsible for helping clear the body of harmful substances like bacteria, intracellular parasites, damaged or aberrant cells, cancerous cells, and cells that have undergone cell death (Li et al. 2021; Lemke 2019). Moreover, macrophages are present in almost every organ and tissue, playing as one of the body's initial lines of protection against both external and internal threats (Lech et al. 2012). In 1883, Elie Metchnikoff published a study describing phagocytic cells in frogs, which established the concept of cellular host defence. His reports explained both the host defence function of phagocytes and the metamorphosis process, in which these scavenger cells recognize and eliminate dead or dying cellular debris belonging to the host (Metchnikoff 1883). The idea of macrophage actuation was proposed in 1905 based on research by Elie Metchnikoff, which showed that macrophages from diseased animals can increase their capacity to fight bacteria (Metchnikoff 1905). Therefore, six decades of cumulative research have gradually shed light on the complex processes by which macrophages recognize and destroy bacteria (Gordon 2007). Ebert and Florey discovered in the 1930s that monocytes in the circulation may differentiate into macrophages by relocating to various organs and tissues (Ebert and Florey 1939). The existence of macrophage precursor cells in the bone marrow was first shown in 1968, a pivotal observation that helped to shape the mononuclear phagocyte system theory. By 1972, this theory had been formally acknowledged and established as the inaugural systematic model of macrophage ontogeny (Virolainen 1968). North and Mackaness later demonstrated that cytokines can initiate and regulate inflammatory responses (North and Mackaness 1973). Furthermore, Rosenstreich et al. demonstrated that the antimicrobial response of macrophages is fundamentally mediated by lymphocytes (Rosenstreich and Oppenheim 1976). Subsequently, it was found that lymphocyte-driven interferon-γ (IFN-γ) is a key mediator linking lymphocytes to macrophage activation (Zhang et al. 2023). It was also found that resting macrophages can be transformed into active macrophages with enhanced antibacterial and regulatory phagocytosis capabilities, as well as the ability to secrete proinflammatory compounds (Shapouri-Moghaddam et al. 2018). A key early step in studying macrophage polarization is recognizing macrophage subtypes. Macrophages having this activated phenotype were officially referred to as “classically activated” (later referred to as M1) (Mosser 2003). Research on macrophage polarization expanded remarkably during the next 30 years. In 1989, the identification of Th1 and Th2 subsets showed that Th2 cells may secrete interleukin-4 (IL-4), which can drive macrophage polarization towards a phenotype distinct from M1(Mosmann and Coffman 1989). Furthermore, later investigations demonstrated that IL-4 activates macrophages, suppresses their respiratory burst, and dramatically enhances the expression of Major Histocompatibility Complex (MHC) II. IL-4 stimulation also upregulates the mannose receptor (CD206), which is a marker linked to M2 macrophages (Abramson and Gallin 1990). These observations ultimately gave rise to the concept of 'alternatively activated macrophages,' which was first described in 1992 (Stein et al. 1992). Mosser and Edwards postulated that M1 and M2 represented the two ends of the spectrum in macrophage polarization by considering the flexibility and plasticity of macrophages in reaction to an altering environment (Mosser and Edwards 2008). The conception of macrophage polarization was amended again with the presentation of M2-like macrophages, and by 2010, the M2 category had been subdivided (e.g., M2a, M2b, M2c) to reflect different stimuli, such as immune complexes (M2b phenotype), IL-10 or transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β), or glucocorticoids (M2c phenotype) (Biswas and Mantovani 2010). The specific environmental elements can transform macrophage phenotype and function, enabling them to adapt to various stimulations and dynamically switch between the M1 and M2 phenotypes (DeNardo and Ruffell 2019).

Macrophage origin and metabolism

There are three separate developmental paths from which macrophages emerge (DeNardo and Ruffell 2019). Most of macrophages originate from embryonic precursors that germinate tissues during the prenatal and neonatal stages. Progenitors of macrophages from the foetal yolk sac or the foetal liver experience colonizing in developing tissues in at least two functional waves (Ginhoux and Guilliams 2016; Mass et al. 2016). These precursors migrate to peripheral tissues and establish long-lived, self-renewing populations of tissue-resident macrophages that remain throughout adulthood (Ginhoux and Guilliams 2016; Mass et al. 2016; Yona et al. 2013). After birth, monocytes formed from hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs) quickly replace these embryonic macrophages in particular tissues, like the colon. Nevertheless, it seems that microglia and other subsets of macrophages derive entirely from embryonic sources, with minimal input from HSCs when the body is homeostatic (Hoeffel et al. 2015; Ginhoux et al. 2010). Macrophages with mixed embryonic and hematopoietic origins are found in organs like the pancreas, breast, and lung (Calderon et al. 2015; Loyher et al. 2018). It is well established that macrophages in various tissues (such as the brain, liver, and lungs) acquire epigenetically-regulated programs reflecting their homeostasis and environmental characteristics in these organs. These phenotypes are primarily driven by metabolic factors and interferon responsiveness (Loyher et al. 2018; Gosselin et al. 2014). On the other hand, recent research suggests that cancers may use embryonic-derived macrophages differently from monocyte-derived ones regarding their phenotype and function (Loyher et al. 2018; Zhu et al. 2017; Bowman et al. 2016). Although it is believed that the majority of tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) originate from circulating monocytes (Movahedi et al. 2010; Strachan et al. 2013), animal models of brain, lung, and pancreatic cancers indicate that up to half of TAMs may arise from tissue-resident populations (Loyher et al. 2018; Zhu et al. 2017; Bowman et al. 2016). The TAMs derived from HSCs exhibit upregulated expression of genes that regulate immunosuppression and antigen presentation within tumors (Zhu et al. 2017; Bowman et al. 2016; Chen et al. 2017). Contrarily, TAMs arising from embryonic sources highly express genes involved in tissue remodelling and wound healing (Zhang et al. 2021). These findings suggest that tissue origin significantly influences macrophage immune function.

Macrophages are influenced by tissue hypoxia in two specific ways. Under hypoxia, tumor cells and stroma can upregulate key monocyte-recruiting agents like CCL2, CCL5, CXCL12, CSF-1, and VEGF. Once recruited to a hypoxic area, TAMs are retained there due to the downregulation of receptors that mediate macrophage regression (Henze and Mazzone 2016). Moreover, macrophages detect hypoxic conditions via hypoxia-inducible factors (HIFs). HIF signaling is a key adaptive response to low oxygen levels, regulating genes involved in angiogenesis, metabolism, and cell survival (Tirpe et al. 2019). In cancer, persistent HIF activation promotes tumor progression by enhancing immunosuppressive and pro-tumoral functions of TAMs (He and Zhang 2021). HIF-1α and HIF-2α are transcription factors that mediate the effects of HIF signaling. In the TAMs context, loss of HIF-1α reduces arginase-1 production and curtails immunosuppressive function in cultured cells (Doedens et al. 2010), while loss of HIF-2α decreases both the infiltration of macrophages and the secretion of inflammatory cytokines (Imtiyaz et al. 2010). Alteration of these HIFs in myeloid cells slows the growth of tumors in spontaneous tumor models, further highlighting their importance (Doedens et al. 2010; Imtiyaz et al. 2010). Deletion of neuropilin-1 in myeloid cells prevents macrophage accumulation in hypoxic tumor regions. This enhances anti-tumor immunity and reduces immunosuppressive polarization (Casazza et al. 2013). Tumor aerobic glycolysis reduces glucose availability and elevates organic acid accumulation regardless of tissue hypoxia. Macrophages activated by lipopolysaccharide (LPS) and/or IFNγ increase aerobic glycolysis and glucose uptake, reflecting the significance of this metabolic variation in inflammatory response. Moreover, the elevated transport of glucose induces the upregulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) (Freemerman et al. 2014; Geeraerts et al. 2017). IL-4-polarized macrophages, by contrast, are characterized by a metabolic preference for oxidative phosphorylation and fatty acid oxidation (Geeraerts et al. 2017). As a result, they are largely insensitive to disruptions in glycolytic flux (Wang et al. 2018a). Another point that has not been fully established is that TAMs will be more inclined to adopt a pro-tumor polarization state when glucose availability is low. Disrupting the genetic drivers of this pathway weakens the tumor-supporting functions of TAMs and suppresses neoplastic growth in preclinical models. This is because metabolic reprogramming influences macrophage function (Penny et al. 2016; Liu and Cao 2023).

Furthermore, lactate promotes macrophage expression of VEGF and Arg1 in a fundamentally HIF-1α-dependent manner. (Colegio et al. 2014). It appears that acidic pH, rather than lactate per se, directly participates in regulating macrophage polarization or enhancing the function of monocarboxylate transporters, (Colegio et al. 2014; Jones and Morris 2016). Supporting this recent research in this field, has been discovered that unstimulated macrophages at a pH of 6.1 can expand the production of Arg1, VEGFa, and HIF-1α in vitro (Bohn et al. 2018). Similarly, at a pH of 6.8, comparable outcomes have been observed following IL-4 stimulation. Conversely, raising pH within tumors results in downregulation of Arg1 expression by TAMs (El-Kenawi et al. 2019). The molecular basis for macrophage pH sensing is not completely understood. Still, activation likely involves G protein-coupled receptors and cyclic AMP generation (Radu et al. 2005), which in turn induces the transcription factor inducible cyclic AMP early repressor (ICER) (Bohn et al. 2018). It is noteworthy that mice that lack ICER in a myeloid-specific manner are unable to develop highly glycolytic tumors (Bohn et al. 2018).

Polarization of macrophages

During apoptosis and tissue remodelling, macrophages are crucial for clearing cellular remnants and maintaining homeostasis (Chen et al. 2023). Through pattern recognition receptors (PRRs), macrophages recognize pathogen-associated (PAMPs) and damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) to initiate immune responses, as the infection makes progress (Saeed et al. 2020; Hu and Spaink 2022). Primarily derived from bone marrow monocytes, macrophages are key components of innate immunity, which is a surveillance system, and the body's first line of defence against infectious diseases (Ruiz-Baca et al. 2021).

The pro-inflammatory M1 or anti-inflammatory/tissue-remodelling M2 polarizations are triggered by environmental factors, which ultimately determine the fate of macrophages. The process is tightly controlled by transcriptional and post-transcriptional networks, epigenetic mechanisms, and critical signaling pathways (Jia and Wei 2020; Lin et al. 2022). Moreover, Th2 cytokines like IL-4 and IL-13 promote M2 polarization, whereas pathogen-associated molecular markers, lipopolysaccharide, and IFN-γ enhance M1 response (Saeed et al. 2020; Padron et al. 2020; Keewan and Naser 2020). Various studies have shown that macrophage activation and polarization are influenced by multiple stimuli and have been described using diverse terminologies. For example, differences in M1 and M2 phenotypes have been observed between BALB/c and C57BL/6 mouse strains (Bleul et al. 2021). Abdelaziz and his team revealed that IL-4 and IL-13 enhance alternative macrophage polarization (Abdelaziz et al. 2020), while other research illustrated that regulatory macrophage (Mreg) activation (Suuring and Moreau 2021). The study demonstrates that classical IFN-α macrophage stimulation can induce a distinct macrophage phenotype, and similarly, oxidized lipids can also trigger the formation of antioxidant macrophages (Mox) with related features (Han et al. 2020). Gleissner and colleagues identified a distinct macrophage subset, termed M4, characterized by CXCL4-driven induction and loss of CD163 expression, while Based on the specific signals that drive their activation, Mantovani and his team classified alternatively activated macrophages into three subtypes—M2a, M2b, and M2c (Gleissner 2012; Mantovani et al. 2004). A recent work demonstrated that RNA-binding protein IGF2BP2 is a key factor driving macrophage activation (Schymik et al. 2022; Wang et al. 2021).

M1 polarization is induced by microbial products and proinflammatory cytokines, especially IFN-γ. IFN-γ binding to IFNGR activates Janus kinase adapters, resulting in STAT1 activation (JAK/STAT1 signaling) (Shaheryar et al. 2021; Moran et al. 2007). Different IFNs trigger different gene profiles. IFN-α enhances gene expression profiles, such as NOS2 and SOCS1, while in the classic M1/M2 polarization model, IFN-γ and LPS are the canonical inducers used to elicit the M1 state. LPS plus IFN-γ induces responses that differ from LPS or IFN-γ alone (Boutilier and Elsawa 2021).

PRRs, including Toll-like receptors (TLRs), are responsible for recognizing bacterial moieties and activating TLR4, which triggers the TIR-domain-containing adapter-inducing interferon-β (TRIF) and myeloid differentiation response 88 (MyD88). This induces kinase cascades and IRF3, which tracks IFN secretion. MyD88 activates the NF-kB signaling pathway and protein activator 1 (AP-1) via MAPK, inducing proinflammatory cytokines, chemokines, and antigen presentation molecules (Xia et al. 2021; Javaid and Choi 2020; Callahan et al. 2021).

M2 polarization and the IL-4Rα receptor are bound by the cytokines IL-4 and IL-13, and JAK1/JAK3 and STAT6 signaling. The STAT (Signal Transducer and Activator of Transcription) pathway is a key signaling cascade that transmits signals from cytokine or growth factor receptors at the cell surface to the nucleus, where it regulates gene expression. This pathway plays a crucial role in controlling immune responses, cell growth, differentiation, and survival (Hu et al. 2021). Once activated by STING (Stimulator of Interferon Genes), STAT6 translocates into the nucleus and transcriptionally regulates innate immune responses to viruses (Chen et al. 2011a). Additional transcription factors such as STAT6, IRF4, and PPARγ coordinate the expression of M2 macrophage-related genes such as Arg1, Ym1, Retnla, CCL17, and CD206. Also, fatty acid-sensing receptors can activate the PPARγ transcription factor, linking lipid metabolism to M2 polarization (Abdelaziz et al. 2020). Moreover, leukocyte-derived IL-10 engages IL-10R, which in turn triggers receptor autophosphorylation and subsequently activates the STAT3 signaling pathway. This binding leads to modulation of SOCS3 expression, blocking proinflammatory cytokine signaling pathways and preventing inflammation (Hutchins et al. 2013).

Another pathway involved in macrophage polarization is ERK5 (extracellular signal-regulated kinase 5). ERK5 is the largest MAPK family member and is activated by MEK5 in response to growth factors and stress signals (Paudel et al. 2021). Giurisato et al. showed that ERK5 controls macrophage polarity through STAT3. In mice lacking ERK5 in myeloid cells, STAT3 phosphorylation was blocked, macrophages shifted toward a pro-inflammatory state, and tumor growth was reduced in melanoma and carcinoma models. ERK5-deficient macrophages also produced less TGF-β, IL-10, and VEGF. These findings suggest ERK5 helps keeping TAMs in a tumor-supporting state by sustaining STAT3 activity, and that blocking ERK5 could push TAMs toward an anti-tumor phenotype (Giurisato et al. 2018). This makes the ERK5-STAT3 axis a possible target for combination immunotherapy.

Glucocorticoids are synthesized from cholesterol and act via the glucocorticoid receptor (GR) in macrophages. Because glucocorticoids are lipophilic, they cross membranes easily and, upon binding to their receptor, translocate into the nucleus to regulate gene expression (Meduri and Chrousos 2020). Through a direct DNA-complex interaction, anti-inflammatory genes such as IL-10 and IL-1R2 are activated for transcription. The GR complex can suppress proinflammatory factors via interacting alongside other transcription factors, including NF-kB and AP-1 (Summers et al. 2020; Chang et al. 2021).

The concept of M1/M2 macrophage polarization was introduced during the 2000s, defined primarily by distinct capacities for generating nitric oxide (NO) between C57BL/6 J and Balb/c mice (De Cunto et al. 2020; Cardoso et al. 2020). Differences in arginine metabolism contribute to macrophage phenotype differences between C57BL/6 J and Balb/c mice, with the former showing elevated inducible NOS activity, nitric oxide production, and Th1-type immune responses. In Balb/c mice, a Th2-skewed immunity and an ornithine-type arginase converge to produce M1 and M2 macrophage subsets that closely parallel the Th1/Th2 T-cell paradigm (Chen et al. 2023; Cardoso et al. 2020).

NO production is widely regarded as a hallmark of M1 macrophages, driven by the upregulated expression of inducible NOS2 (Palmieri et al. 2020). M1 macrophages secrete proinflammatory cytokines, such as TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-12, and IL-23, and ROS, which are responsible for removing pathogens and recruiting additional proinflammatory cell types (Shapouri-Moghaddam et al. 2018; Huang et al. 2021). Inflammation and inflammatory macrophage polarization can be mitigated by graphene oxide (GO), an antioxidant that helps reduce ROS in cells (Han et al. 2018). GO is also distinguished as a vital transporter for IL-4 plasmid DNA (IL-4 pDNA), promoting M2 macrophage responses (Mata et al. 2021). M2 macrophages participate in tumor progression, tissue repair, and wound healing by promoting matrix remodelling (Liu et al. 2021; Ricketts et al. 2021). Immunosuppressive macrophages include various functional subsets, including Mregs, myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), profibrotic macrophages (M2a), and TAMs due to their diverse roles (Salminen 2021). Though these subtypes exert various functions as activators and effectors, they generally share immunosuppressive features. Among these subtypes, Mregs are distinct as they are activated by IL-10 and lack the M1 enzyme NOS2. Despite this, Mregs suppress M1 macrophage and inflammatory response, thereby exerting immunosuppression (Ordikhani et al. 2020). Tumor-derived factors, such as hypoxia, polarize TAMs in tumor microenvironments (TME) towards immunosuppression and proangiogenic features, promoting tumor growth and progression (Poh and Ernst 2021). The tumor microenvironment provides a niche in which monocytic MDSCs can mature into TAMs. MDSCs express Ly6G/Ly6C (Gr1 in mice) and suppress innate and T-cell–mediated anti-tumor immune responses (Ostrand-Rosenberg and Sinha 2009). Macrophages represent plasticity post-activation, changing functional phenotypes based on conditions, though dysregulated responses can contribute to tumorigenesis, tissue necrosis, inflammation, and fibrosis (Zhao et al. 2021). Recent studies demonstrate that targeting PD-L1 in TAMs could represent an effective treatment approach. In colorectal cancer, tumor-derived extracellular vesicles (EV) transfer microRNAs to TAMs upregulating PDL-1 expression and thereby enhance tumor immune evasion. Yin et al. showed that targeting PDL-1 expression in TAMs or blocking siRNA-mediated PDL-1 upregulation by EV could substantially improve the therapeutic efficacy of PD-L1-targeted immunotherapies (Yin et al. 2022). In the breast cancer model, PYK2 regulates TAMs' function, and the downregulation of PYK2 expression in macrophages alone can reduce TAM activity, leading to reduced tumorigenesis and angiogenesis (Müller et al. 2022).

Owing to their remarkable plasticity, macrophages can readily respond and adapt to local microenvironmental signals, resulting in the adaptive switching between M1 and M2 macrophages (Gurvich et al. 2020). Notably, M2-polarized macrophages can re-polarize to M1 macrophages in response to stimulation by inflammatory factors such as IFN-γ (Ye et al. 2021). The native cytokine milieu plays a critical role in regulating macrophage polarization. IFN-γ inhibits IL-4 production and enhances M1 polarization (Keegan et al. 2021). M1 macrophage activation can be induced through Th1 T cells and NK cells, while IL-4 / IL-13-mediated STAT6 pathway triggers M2 macrophage activation (Medvedeva et al. 2021; Jurga et al. 2020; Duan and Luo 2021).

Besides, macrophage activation phenotypes can be identified by cytokines and chemokines released by activated macrophages. These markers can trigger the leukocyte infiltration and the infection clearance. IFN-γ derives from multiple immune sources, encompassing CD4⁺ Th1 cells, CD8⁺ T cells, and NK cells, upregulates several M1 markers. Macrophages respond to IFN-γ and LPS in humans, while in mice, this pathway is regulated by IL-12 and IL-18 (Labani-Motlagh et al. 2020).

It is important to emphasize that the M1/M2 classification framework, while it is useful for organizing macrophage biology, is fundamentally derived from in vitro stimulation conditions that do not fully representing complex microenvironment (Nahrendorf and Swirski 2016). However, within in vivo, macrophages are simultaneously exposed to a highly dynamic and heterogeneous mixture of cytokines, metabolites, hypoxic gradients, and extracellular matrix components, which collectively lead to various and heterogeneous polarization states (Huang et al. 2018a; Wang et al. 2014). Therefore, there is a spectrum of phenotypic states, many of which share features of both M1 and M2, while some can also express genes that belong to neither. This in vivo complexity has been investigated through several single-cell RNA sequencing studies, leading to the identification of subsets such as SPP1⁺ pro-angiogenic TAMs, FABP5⁺ lipid-associated immunoregulatory TAMs, TREM2⁺ checkpoint-resistant TAMs, and MMP12⁺ matrix-remodeling TAMs (Lao et al. 2025; Ma et al. 2022). Bill et al. analysed the tumor microenvironment across multiple human cancers and identified a fundamental axis of macrophage polarity defined by the ratio of CXCL9-expressing to SPP1-expressing TAMs. They found that CXCL9⁺ and SPP1⁺ TAMs did not fit the exact classical definition of M1 or M2, as they represent functionally distinct states, shaped by the broader cellular network of the TME rather than by a single polarizing stimulus (Bill et al. 2023). Translational research and clinical therapeutic design should ultimately move beyond binary polarization models toward frameworks that account for the heterogeneity of TAM states as they exist within human tumor tissues. Macrophages in tumor microenvironment

Macrophages are distinguished as ubiquitous innate immune cells that play a remarkable role in development, homeostasis, and tissue repair (Gordon et al. 2014). They develop from distinct embryonic sources and can expand in tissue both by monocyte infiltration or local proliferation of resident macrophage progenitors. Furthermore, studies revealed substantial transcriptomic diversity between macrophage populations even within the same tissue, despite the similar expression of canonical factors such as CSF1R and ADGRE in humans and mice (Gautier et al. 2012). CSF1R, a transmembrane tyrosine kinase class III receptor, is essential for many macrophages, indicating their varied origins, their ability to adapt to different tissue environments, and their wide-ranging involvement in disease (Chitu and Stanley 2006). By binding to its two ligands, CSF1 and IL-34, CSF1R orchestrates the differentiation, expansion, and survival of macrophages in both humans and mice (Stanley and Chitu 2014). Although IL-34 mimics CSF1 functionally, its amino acid sequence is unrelated, and it interacts with an alternative CSF1R binding site that exhibits higher affinity (Lin et al. 2008). IL-34 serves a vital function in the generation of particular macrophage subtypes, specifically microglia and Langerhans cells (Wang et al. 2012). Baghdadi and colleagues recently demonstrated that IL-34, when secreted by chemoresistant tumor cells, helps preserve the immunosuppressive activity of TAMs and supports cancer cell viability (Baghdadi et al. 2016).

ERK5 also participates in TAM proliferation, which affects tumor growth and metastasis. TAMs are not only recruited from circulating monocytes, they also proliferate locally within the tumor, which is driven by signals such as CSF1. This local proliferation significantly increases TAMs’ numbers, especially in advanced tumors (Stecca and Rovida 2019). ERK5 enhances this proliferation by suppressing p21, a protein that normally stops cell division. A high proportion of proliferating macrophages in human tumors express ERK5. When ERK5 was deleted in myeloid cells in mouse models, macrophage proliferation reduced, and tumor growth and metastases decreased. These results show that ERK5 affects TAMs in two ways: by keeping them in an immunosuppressive state and by helping them multiply within the tumor, which make ERK5 inhibition a promising strategy (Giurisato et al. 2020). Developing ERK5 inhibitors is therefore a worthwhile direction for TAM-targeted therapy.

In the late nineteenth century, tumors were considered a mix of malignant and normal cells. The foundational understanding of the TME was first established in the 1970s, suggesting that Tumor-infiltrating immune cells can actively drive cancer progression (Prehn 1972). Nonetheless, macrophages were identified as tumoricidal with the ability to eliminate tumor cells in vitro in early research (Fidler and Schroit 1988; Mantovani et al. 1986; Hibbs et al. 1987). By the early 1990s, studies found that macrophages are important in tumor progression, as elevated intratumoral macrophage density and increased CSF1 expression have been correlated with worse patient prognosis (Lewis and Pollard 2006). Moreover, A pivotal 2001 study demonstrated that knocking out CSF1 in the PyMT breast cancer model suppressed disease advancement and metastatic potential, providing evidence that macrophages possess tumor-promoting functions (Lin et al. 2001). Subsequent investigations further proved the pro-tumoral function of TAMs in various cancers, identifying subtypes in mice that promote primary tumor progression and metastasis (Hanahan and Coussens 2012; Quail and Joyce 2013; Kitamura et al. 2015). A meta-analysis of 55 studies found that high TAM infiltration associates with poor overall survival in breast, gastric, oral, ovarian, bladder, and thyroid cancers, but not colorectal cancer (Zhang et al. 2012). More recent meta-analyses verified an association between TAM infiltration and cancer progression in several malignancies, such as breast and gastric cancers, and NSCLC (Mei et al. 2016; Zhao et al. 2017; Yin et al. 2017). In CSF1R-expressing ovarian cancer cell lines, upregulation of CSF1 and urokinase plasminogen activator enhances invasive potential (Chambers et al. 1995). High CSF1 serum levels are linked to worse prognosis in ovarian and endometrial malignancies, and they are present in metastatic breast cancer and other cancers at the clinical level (Smith et al. 1995; Price et al. 1993; Scholl et al. 1994; Kacinski et al. 1991). The production of CSF1 by endometrial cancer cells is a distinct prognostic marker (Smith et al. 2013). A specific expression pattern of CSF1 in breast cancer was uncovered to be associated with poor prognosis (West et al. 2005). High CSF1R expression and CD68-positive macrophage infiltration independently predict lower PFS (progression-free survival) in Hodgkin lymphoma (Steidl et al. 2010). While normal breast and ovarian tissues typically lack CSF1R and CSF1 overexpression (Kacinski et al. 1991; Sapi 2004), some tumor cells can highly express this receptor-ligand pair (Tang et al. 1992; Scholl 1993; Chambers et al. 1997). CSF1R expression on cancer cells is rare and likely due to demethylation of a recently acquired retroviral element in humans (Lamprecht, et al. 2010). Over the past two decades, extensive studies across multiple tumor models have consistently demonstrated that TAMs promote malignancy via their interactions with neoplastic cells. Role of macrophages in tumorigenesis

Proliferation

Patients with solid tumors and hematologic malignancies have insignificant clinical outcomes due to the promotion of growth and metastasis by TAMs in the TME, which can happen through both immunological and non-immunological pathways (Petty and Yang 2017, 2019). TAMs secrete growth factors and structural proteins that support tumor proliferation and extracellular matrix remodelling (Pan et al. 2020). They can also downregulate ERα expression, resulting in endometrial cancer progression (Tong et al. 2016). In pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma, TAM-derived IL-1β suppresses 15-hydroxyprostaglandin dehydrogenase (15-PGDH), a change associated with advanced stage and lymph-node metastasis. Tumor progression and lymph node metastases are associated with lower 15-PGDH rates (Arima et al. 2018).

A subpopulation of tumor stromal cells known as cancer stem cells (CSCs) can proliferate indefinitely, allowing tumors to develop and become more resistant to cytotoxic treatment. TAMs both promote tumor cell proliferation and support the expansion of the CSC via secreting cytokines and regulating signaling pathways (Kreso and Dick 2014; Korkaya et al. 2011). TAMs release TNF-α, which induces the expansion of CSCs in colon cancer, and IL-6, derived from TAMs, promotes CSC growth in human hepatocellular carcinoma (Schwitalla et al. 2013; Wan et al. 2014). Signaling pathways such as Akt/mTOR, Src/NF-κB, Sonic hedgehog, and STAT3 play essential roles in increasing CSC populations by promoting self-renewal, survival, and resistance to therapy. TAMs can stimulate these signaling cascades through cytokine release and direct cell–cell interactions, enabling sustained tumor growth and recurrence. In renal cell carcinoma, TAMs expand the CSC population via enhancing Akt/mTOR signaling activation (Yang et al. 2016). Juxtacrine interactions between CSCs and TAMs via CD90 and EphA4 activate Src and NF-κB, reinforcing the CSC niche in breast cancer (Lu et al. 2014). Recent investigations illustrate that TAM-derived MFG-E8 (milk fat globule–epidermal growth factor 8) improves CSCs' survival during chemotherapy treatments by activating Sonic hedgehog and STAT3 pathways (Jinushi et al. 2011). TAMs can also regulate cancer stemness at the gene level. Yang et al. demonstrated that TAMs upregulate CSC-related gene programs, increasing drug efflux and chemoresistance in murine breast cancer cells (Yang et al. 2013). Furthermore, in pancreatic cancer, downregulation of CSF1R or CCR2 on TAMs reduces CSC proliferation and enhances responses to chemotherapy, indicating the role of CSF1R and CCR2 in CSC expansion via TAMs (Mitchem et al. 2013).

Metastasis

Metastasis is a complex multistep process, and TAMs contribute at several stages, including epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT) induction, local invasion, and preparation of distant metastatic niches. EMT is a key process that enhances epithelial tumor cells' ability to acquire migratory and invasive properties, facilitating metastasis. TAMs promote EMT by secreting cytokines and growth factors such as TGF-β and IL-6, thereby enhancing tumor cell invasion and metastatic potential (Hu et al. 2024). Previous studies demonstrate an association between TAM recruitment and elevated tumor cell invasiveness. In the PyMT breast cancer model, Vasiljeva et al. showed that TAMs produce cathepsin B, which increases tumor invasiveness and lung metastasis (Vasiljeva et al. 2006). TAMs promote invasion by secreting matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), serine proteases, and cathepsins that degrade extracellular matrix and disrupt cell junctions and basement membranes (Chanmee et al. 2014). MMPs play pivotal roles in tumor progression through various mechanisms. MMP-9 facilitates extracellular matrix remodelling, promoting tumor invasion and metastasis, while MMP-8 exhibits tumor-suppressive effects by inhibiting cell proliferation and inducing apoptosis. MMP-12 contributes to tumor progression by modulating inflammatory responses and extracellular matrix degradation. Understanding the differential roles of these MMPs is crucial for developing targeted therapies in cancer treatment (Mondal et al. 2020; Baidya et al. 2024; Amin et al. 2017; Dorjay Tamang et al. 2025). TAM-derived MIP-1β (macrophage inflammatory protein 1-β) and TGF-β enhance tumor cell invasion and matrix remodeling in breast and NSCLC models (Baghel et al. 2016; Zhang et al. 2017). TAMs also produce CCL18, which induces integrin clustering in breast cancer cells, promoting adhesion, intravasation, and metastasis (Chen et al. 2011b). In colon and Lewis lung carcinoma cells, enhanced production of S100A8 and S100A9 results in tumor invasion and migration; these are only two of the many chemicals produced by TAMs that improve cell invasion and metastasis (Lim et al. 2016). TAM-derived SPARC modulates tumor extracellular matrix deposition and tumor–stroma interactions by secreting an acidic, cysteine-rich protein (Sangaletti et al. 2008). High expression of mesenchymal markers reflects an invasive phenotype driven in part by macrophage-derived TGF-β, which enhances EMT pathways (Bonde et al. 2012; Cai et al. 2019; Fan et al. 2014). A specialized VEGFR1^high CX3CR1^high CCR2^high macrophage subset at pulmonary metastatic sites enhances metastatic efficiency, illustrating that macrophages not only expand metastatic efficiency but also foster cancer cell survival and growth (Qian et al. 2009).

Angiogenesis

Angiogenesis is critical for tumor growth and metastasis, and TAMs are major proangiogenic players that supply growth and chemotactic signals to endothelial cells. TAMs play a key role in the angiogenic process, from basement membrane degradation to MMPs production and cathepsin secretion. Furthermore, they promote the vascular system that cancer cells need to develop and spread, by secreting chemokines such as CCL2 and CXCL8, and pro-angiogenic factors such as VEGF and PDGF, and bFGF (Fig. 3) (Kessenbrock et al. 2010; Murdoch et al. 2008). VEGF and CCL2 recruit TAMs and drive proangiogenic polarization. Function of macrophages in immunosuppression and inflammation

The association between inflammation and cancer is well-established and considered a vital part of cancer progression (Christofides et al. 2022; Mantovani et al. 2008). Tumors are often described as wounds that do not heal, characterized by inflammatory cell infiltration, tissue remodelling, and enhanced coagulation within TME (Dvorak 1986). Unlike normal wound healing, however, the TME involves constant injury and healing (Mantovani et al. 2008). Cancer-related inflammation is initiated by soluble mediators, including growth factors, cytokines, and chemokines, produced downstream of oncogenic driver mutations such as KRAS^G12D, BRAFV600E, TP53, and BRCA1. These factors co-exist and persist in the TME, revealing the ongoing nature of cancer-related inflammation (Bayne et al. 2012; Ubertini et al. 2015; Mehta et al. 2021; Sumimoto et al. 2006). Moreover, cancer-related inflammation is established by the production of DAMPs, which are produced by cancer cells as they undergo rapid replication, apoptosis, nutrient starvation, and hypoxia. These DAMPs are recognized by PRRs in monocytes, macrophages, and other immune cells, initiating proinflammatory cascades. Cancer cells release exosomes that carry tumor DNA or RNA. These exosomes attract monocytes to the main tumor site and to areas where cancer may spread. (Keklikoglou et al. 2019; Dou et al. 2017; Liu et al. 2016; Su et al. 2018). The NLRP3 inflammasome detects ATP outside cells and triggers the release of inflammatory cytokines and chemokines. These signals recruit T cells during early anti-tumor immune responses (Di Virgilio et al. 2018). Cancer-produced cytokines and DAMPs induce emergency myelopoiesis in bone marrow progenitors, leading to the formation of PMN-MDSCs and M-MDSCs, which travel into tumors and turn into TAMs (Kumar et al. 2016).

Beyond soluble mediators, macrophage-driven immunosuppression is further reinforced by intracellular signaling pathways that regulate their recruitment, activation, and retention in the TME. Macrophages fight pathogens by launching pro-inflammatory responses. One example is the type I IFN response, which is triggered by viral infections. These responses serve dual functions as their proinflammatory pathways can enhance cancer-related inflammatory response and immunosuppression in TAMs (Hubel et al. 2019). On the other hand, mechanisms underlying CD8 + T cells recruitment and maintains in tumors can be altered through IFN I or DNA-damaging chemo- and radiotherapies (Mowat, et al. 2021), but can also be used by the tumor to induce cancer-related inflammation and immunosuppression (West et al. 2022). Conversion of tissue-resident macrophages (TRMs) into TAMs is regulated by Notch signaling, which is also involved in hematopoiesis and the generation of myeloid cells (Radtke et al. 2010; Franklin et al. 2014; Wang et al. 2010). Moreover, inflammation in cancer is mediated by PI3K pathway, which is considered an important regulator of effective immune responses (Kaneda et al. 2016a).

In addition to cytokine and signaling regulation, TAM-mediated immunosuppression also relies on the expression of inhibitory receptors and ligands that directly suppress T cell activation and macrophage cytotoxicity. Activation of TAM proinflammatory pathways may cause upregulation of inhibitory receptors and ligands, which enhances immunosuppression in TME. Notably, after cancer cell phagocytosis, the DNS activates AIM2, which increases expression of PD-L1 and IDO via cleaving cGAS, thereby suppressing macrophage anti-tumor function (Su et al. 2018). TAMs express multiple inhibitory receptors, including Tim3, Tim4, PD-1, and PD-L1, which play inhibitory role in macrophage functions and enhances SIRPα signaling to inhibit phagocytosis after CD47 engagement. When CD47 on tumor cells binds to SIRPα on macrophages, it triggers inhibitory signaling that suppresses cytoskeletal rearrangements needed for engulfment, thereby blocking macrophage phagocytosis of tumor cells. This whole process is inhibited by TAMs, improving immune suppression (Matlung et al. 2017; Chiba et al. 2012; Dixon et al. 2021). T cell activation is impaired by TAMs through a dual mechanism involving both co-inhibitory receptor signaling and the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS), while simultaneously maintaining a paradoxical proinflammatory state. Among all these inhibitory molecules, the PD-1/PD-L1 axis plays a particularly significant role in maintaining TAM-mediated immunosuppression. Blocking PD-L1 at the macrophage level can restore macrophage proliferation and activation, suggesting that TAM function can be modulated through this mechanism (Hartley et al. 2018).

Myeloid cell PD-1 expression is upregulated by Toll-like receptor signaling and is associated with decreased M1-like polarization (Chen et al. 2016). In infectious contexts, PD-1 expression on macrophages decreases innate inflammatory responses during sepsis and reduces Mycobacterium tuberculosis phagocytosis (Huang et al. 2009; Shen et al. 2016). Similarly, in the tumor context, PD-1 activation on TAMs also impairs their phagocytic capacity. A mouse model of colon cancer reveals that PD-1 activation in TAMs suppresses phagocytic efficacy, enhancing tumor progression (Gordon et al. 2017). In chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL), PD-L1 or antibody-mediated PD-1 inhibition in peripheral blood monocytes impedes BTK signaling, glycolysis, and phagocytosis (Qorraj et al. 2017). When PD-1 was removed or blocked specifically in macrophages, in both mouse tumor models and CLL patient monocytes, anti-tumor immune responses improved greatly (Gordon et al. 2017; Qorraj et al. 2017; Strauss, et al. 2020). Checkpoint inhibitors play a key role in the immunosuppressive properties of TAMs, but their expression and functions are poorly understood. Studying this could offer novel therapeutic strategies for cancer immunotherapy. Strategies in macrophage regulation for cancer immunotherapy

TAMs are promising therapeutic targets for cancer due to their protumoral characteristics that contribute to tumor growth (DeNardo and Ruffell 2019; Li et al. 2019a; Guerriero 2018; Noy and Pollard 2014). The present methods are focused on reducing this protumoral effects or directing TAMs into a tumoricidal state. These strategies can be divided into four main groups: inhibiting macrophage recruitment to the tumor, directly killing TAMs, converting TAMs from their M2-like protumor phenotype to an antitumor phenotype, and delivering therapeutic cargoes through TAMs (DeNardo and Ruffell 2019; Mantovani et al. 2017; Pathria et al. 2019). The following sections outline how each of these approaches are being investigated to enhance cancer immunotherapy efficacy.

Inhibition of monocyte/macrophage recruitment

One major strategy targets the early stages of TAM recruitment or infiltration into tumors. The CCL2-CCR2 axis is one of the main routes that recruits monocyte cells from the blood, thereby facilitating TAMs replenishment (Ren et al. 2012; Qian et al. 2011). Tumor cells, macrophages, and stromal cells release CCL-2, which can recruit monocytes and granulocytes to TME, driving tumor progression. The number of TAMs can be decreased by targeting the CCL2-CCR2 axis (Ren et al. 2012; Qian et al. 2011). In preclinical studies, the antitumor impacts can be increased by combining CCL-2 or CCR-2 inhibition with radiation, chemotherapy, or immunotherapy (Nywening et al. 2016; Fridlender et al. 2010). Clinical trials are ongoing for solid tumor treatment using various CCR-2 inhibitors (Pathria et al. 2019). Combination of conventional chemotherapies with CCL-2 inhibition declines cancer proliferation and invasion in experimental and animal models. However, discontinuing anti-CCL2 therapy may trigger a compensatory response, in which monocytes accumulate in excess within the bone marrow, which enhances angiogenesis and accelerates breast cancer metastasis (Bonapace et al. 2014). Carlumab (CNTO888) and PF-04136309 are the primary agents currently tested in clinical trials targeting the CCL2-CCR2 pathway. Both agents have shown promising clinical efficacies in pancreatic cancer patients when combined with FOLFIRINOX (Mantovani et al. 2017). Notably, combination of PF-04136309 and FOLFIRINOX did not lead to additional adverse effects compared with chemotherapy alone. In the combination group, nearly half (49%) achieved an objective response, with 97% achieving local tumor control (97%) (Nywening et al. 2016).

Beyond the CCL2-CCR2 pathway, several other signaling mechanisms contribute to TAM recruitment, offering alternative therapeutic targets for reducing TAM recruitment. TAM recruitment has multiple pathways, notably the CXCL12-CXCR4 chemokine axis and VEGF receptor signaling. CXCL-12 released by stromal cells, facilitates macrophage migration and TAM accumulation in tumor hypoxic sites (Hughes et al. 2015; Mota et al. 2016; Teicher and Fricker 2010). In addition, targeting CXCR-4 in ovarian, breast, and prostate xenografts, diminishes tumor burden and metastases (Teicher and Fricker 2010; Scala 2015). Disrupting the CXCL12-CXCR4 axis further impairs tumor progression by blocking TAM accumulation and their in-situ proliferation (Mota et al. 2016). Similarly, suppressing VEGFR2 signaling reduces number of macrophages and vessel growth in breast and pancreatic cancer models, blocking tumor advancement following sepsis while also curbing the local proliferation of TAMs within the tumor microenvironment (Dineen et al. 2008).

Besides the CXCL12-CXCR4 and CCL2-CCR2 axes, the CX3CL1/CX3CR1 signaling pathway is another important way through which TAMs are recruited into tumors. CX3CL1, also called fractalkine, exists as both a membrane-bound molecule and a soluble chemotactic factor (Szukiewicz 2024). Its receptor, CX3CR1, is found on circulating monocytes, tissue macrophages, NK cells, and some T cell populations. Inside the TME, CX3CL1 is upregulated, creating a gradient that pulls CX3CR1-expressing monocytes into the tumor and pushes them toward an immunosuppressive TAM phenotype (Conroy and Lysaght 2020). Xiang et al. showed that in hepatocellular carcinoma models, CX3CR1⁺ TAMs drive T cell exhaustion by secreting IL-27. They found that tumor-derived prostaglandin E2 (PGE2) acts as the key factor that converts TAMs to the CX3CR1⁺ state. Interestingly, blocking CX3CR1⁺ TAMs alongside anti-PD-1 therapy produced better anti-tumor response than either approach alone, suggesting that this axis actively limits the effectiveness of checkpoint inhibition (Xiang et al. 2024). These findings highlight the importance of CX3CL1/CX3CR1 in building an immunosuppressive TME and represent promising co-targets in combination immunotherapy strategies.

While chemokine and VEGF pathways regulate TAM recruitment into TME, other mechanisms, like the CSF1/CSF1R axis, regulate their differentiation, survival, and maintenance in tumor tissue. Colony-stimulating factor 1 (CSF1) is responsible for the proliferation, differentiation, recruitment, survival, and function of mononuclear phagocytes. The signaling pathway mediated by CSF1 receptor (CSF1R) in TAMs promotes M2-like phenotype with immunosuppressive and oncogenic features (Zhu et al. 2014; Cannarile et al. 2017). Therefore, the CSF1/CSF1R pathway represents a compelling therapeutic target due to its association with the tyrosine kinase transmembrane receptor family (Cannarile et al. 2017). Current research concentrates on blocking CSF1 binding and downstream signaling activation to limit TAM infiltration and enhance antitumor immune activity (Ries et al. 2014; Pyonteck et al. 2013). A recent review of previous studies confirmed that inhibiting the CSF1/CSF1R axis markedly reduces macrophage recruitment to tumor sites (Cannarile et al. 2017). There are multiple completed and ongoing trials on effects of CSF1/CSF1R inhibitors, with PLX3397 being the most extensively tested agent. PLX3397 suppressed tumor growth in patients and downregulated immunosuppressive TAMs, marking the therapeutic potential of targeting this pathway (Pyonteck et al. 2013; Tap et al. 2015). Moreover, additional small-molecules agents being investigated, including PLX7486, BLZ945, JNJ-40346527, and ARRY-382. Monoclonal antibodies targeting CSF1R or its ligand CSF1 are also being developed (Guerriero 2018). It has been uncovered in preclinical investigations that traditional treatment approaches could be improved by targeting the CSF1/CSF1R axis. While targeting monocyte/macrophage recruitment before tumor invasion is effective, there are ways to target TAMs during their activation and invasion. CSF1R blockade induces metabolic reprogramming that restores glycolysis, enhancing the maintenance of M1-like TAMs (Park et al. 2015). Thus, immunotherapies can target both TAMs and the broader immune-metabolic networks linking them to other stromal cells in the TME (Vitale et al. 2019).

Depletion of tumor-associated macrophages

The selective elimination of TAMs is a promising approach for cancer therapy. Initiating apoptosis allows for the restoration of local immune control and the inhibition of cancer progression. Compounds such as zoledronate, clodronate, and trabectedin can cause macrophage apoptosis and prevent the inhibitory effects of the CSF1/CSF1R axis on macrophage survival (Ries et al. 2014; Pyonteck et al. 2013). Among these agents, bisphosphonates represent one of the earliest and most studied classes for macrophage depletion. Bisphosphonates are a type of anti-resorptive drug that, once phagocytised, are cytotoxic to myeloid cells (Roelofs et al. 2006). Structurally, bisphosphonates are divided into two distinct classes: those that lack a nitrogen moiety and those that incorporate one (Roelofs et al. 2006). Bisphosphonates are commonly applied in clinics to hinder bone metastases and excessive bone resorption, and treat inflammatory diseases (Russell 2011). It has been reported that bisphosphonates may target TAMs (Rogers and Holen 2011). Bisphosphonates offer extensive of anti-cancer actions, such as reducing tumor cell growth, adhesion, invasion, anti-angiogenesis, immune surveillance, and synergy with anti-neoplastic medications (Acker et al. 2016). Clodronate, a bisphosphonate drug, is utilized in osteoporosis and bone metastasis treatment. Its encapsulated liposomes act as selective depletion agents for macrophages, causing their apoptosis due to their large size. This drug family is currently applied in osteoporosis and bone metastasis treatment (Rooijen and Kesteren-Hendrikx 2002). More importantly, clodronate-containing large multilamellar liposomes have been generated and effectively utilized in several cancer models, resulting in the regression of tumor growth, angiogenesis, and metastasis (Diel et al. 1998). Other bisphosphonates, such as zoledronate, which is a third-generation bisphosphonate containing nitrogen and share clodronate's privileges of macrophage depletion (Russell 2011). Zoledronate selectively affects TAMs, leading to altered MMP-9 expression, impeding myeloid cell differentiation, and raising the tumor-killing ability of macrophages (Giraudo et al. 2004).

The Trabectedin (tetrahydroisoquinoline alkylating agent) was formerly only derived from marine organisms, but is now synthetically produced (D'Incalci et al. 2014; Cuevas and Francesch 2009). Trabectedin is approved in some regions (such as Europe) as a second-line therapy for advanced soft tissue sarcoma and ovarian malignancies that have relapsed after being treated with platinum (D'Incalci and Galmarini 2010). This agent can remove cancer cells through binding to the minor groove of DNA, inducing transcriptional interference and DNA damage, leading to cell cycle arrest (Herrero et al. 2006; Germano et al. 2013). Trabectedin therapy lessens TAM density and angiogenesis in patients, and exploits TRAIL sensitivity in certain monocyte subsets. Therapeutic targeting of TAMs through apoptosis is an area of active investigation in both preclinical models and clinical trials for a range of malignancies (Germano et al. 2013; Liguori et al. 2016).

Macrophage reprogramming

Given macrophage plasticity, reprogramming TAMs toward antitumor states is an attractive alternative to depletion and is being focused on as a potential target (Noy and Pollard 2014). Tumor elimination can be achieved by manipulating environmental cues to change tumor-promoting M2-like TAMs to tumoricidal M1-like phenotypes. This approach is challenging as macrophages are crucial for chemotherapy and immunotherapy success. Alternatives to directly depleting TAMs for cancer therapy include targeting tumor cells and TAMs with methods like the CD47 antibody and other drugs (Guerriero 2018; Pathria et al. 2019; Guerriero et al. 2011; Gül et al. 2014).

Therapeutic approaches utilizing TAMs' effector function can target cells by binding to the surface of Fc segments on antibodies, enabling antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity/phagocytosis (ADCC/ADCP) (Mantovani et al. 2017). Myeloid cells, like macrophages, contain SIRPα, an inhibitory receptor that carries ITIM. It identifies CD47, a protein overexpressed in many cancers, as a "do not eat me" signal, allowing cancer cells to evade immune monitoring by preventing phagocytosis (Barclay and Berg 2014). TAMs can trigger antibody-dependent cellular phagocytosis (ADCP) of tumor cells by covering CD47 with a monoclonal antibody or soluble SIRPα-Fc construct, and an efficient immune response to cancer can be induced by allowing tumor-resident macrophages to engage in phagocytosis (Weiskopf et al. 2013; Tseng et al. 2013). On top of that, macrophage-dependent ADCP may increase through altering the CD47-SIRPα axis, which can push mice macrophages’ function towards an M1-like phenotype in vivo, enhancing antcancer immune responses (Willingham et al. 2012). Various CD47–SIRPα targeting agents (antibodies, recombinant proteins, bispecifics) are in preclinical and clinical development (Weiskopf 2017). Therapeutic antibodies enhance TAMs' immunosuppressive, proangiogenic, and protumoral activities by engaging macrophage-Fcγ receptors. Combining checkpoint blockade therapy with agents that boost TAM-mediated ADCP may induce efficient antitumor immunity (Su et al. 2018; Andreu et al. 2010).

Recent studies have found other phagocytosis-related targets on TAMs that can be used to reprogram them against tumors. Two of the most promising ones are MerTK and CLEVER. MerTK is a receptor tyrosine kinase that normally helps macrophages clear dead cells (Grabiec et al. 2018). A subset of TAMs expressing high levels of MerTK, exerts immunosuppressive effects leading to poor therapeutic outcomes. Wu et al. used single-cell RNA sequencing to show that MerTK⁺ macrophages are abundant in melanoma and enhance tumor growth and immunotherapy resistance. They found that the aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR) drives expression of ALKAL1, which activates MerTK, pushing macrophages toward an immunosuppressive state with high ARG1, CD206, and PD-L1 expression. Transferring MerTK⁺ macrophages into mice enhanced tumor growth even when other macrophages were depleted. Targeting AhR in TAMs with a mannose-modified nanoparticle carrying an AhR antagonist reduced MerTK activation and improved anti-tumor immunity (Wu, et al. 2024). On the other hand, CLEVER-1, transcribed from STAB1 gene, is a scavenger receptor found mainly on immunosuppressive TAMs and participates in immune evasion (Yu et al. 2025). In gastric cancer, CLEVER-1⁺ TAMs are highly expressed, suppressing T cell function, and are linked to unfavorable clinical prognoses and resistance to anti-PD-1 therapy. Blocking CLEVER-1 repolarized TAMs into a pro-inflammatory phenotype, boosted CD8⁺ T cell activity, and worked synergistically with PD-1 blockade to control tumor growth (Xiang et al. 2025).

CD40 belongs to the tumor necrosis factor receptor superfamily, and various immune cells express it such as APCs (antigen-presenting cells), DCs (dendritic cells), B cells, macrophages, monocytes, as well as, some tumor cells and non-hematopoietic cell types (Elgueta et al. 2009). CD40 binding to CD40L on APCs (primarily on activated CD4 + T cells) induces proinflammatory cytokines and upregulation of co-stimulatory molecules, such as peroxisome proliferators. (Elgueta et al. 2009). Furthermore, CD40 agonists combined with gemcitabine have shown tumor regression and restored immune surveillance in pancreatic cancer preclinical and early clinical studies (Beatty et al. 2011; Byrne and Vonderheide 2016). Ongoing clinical trials are focused on CD40 agonists therapies combination with various treatment strategies, such as chemotherapy, immunotherapy, vaccines, angiogenic inhibitors, and CD40 agonists with recombinant CD40Ls (Wiehagen et al. 2017). Preclinical combinations of CSF1R blockade with CD40 agonists resulted in improved tumor clearance and prolonged overall survival by reducing suppressive populations and activating antitumor immunity (Stromnes et al. 2019). Reprogramming TAMs to an anti-tumor phenotype may be a better therapeutic paradigm for cancer treatment than targeted ablation of TAMs.

The PI3K pathway regulates macrophage survival, metabolism, and polarization, promoting immunosuppressive and pro-tumor phenotypes and suppressing inflammatory activation. PI3Kγ is part of the Class I PI3K p110 family, regulates TAM function through immunosuppressive transcriptional programming, preventing adaptive immune response, and stimulating myeloid cell infiltration into TME. Targeting PI3Kγ in TAMs can also alter their responses (Kaneda et al. 2016a, b). Pharmacologic PI3Kγ inhibition (e.g., IPI-549) reduces protumor macrophages, enhances antitumor macrophages, and augments T-cell responses in mouse models. (Kaneda et al. 2016a). Epigenetic reprogramming of macrophages can instigate T cell responses through the suppression of histone deacetylases (HDACs). TMP195, is a drug that selectively inhibits HDAC, enhancing antitumor characteristics of TAMs, compatible with T cell reactions (Guerriero et al. 2017).

Macrophages, dendritic cells, and even certain endothelial cells express the nonopsonic phagocytic receptor known as the macrophage receptor with collagenous structure (MARCO). MARCO mediates recognition of pathogens and can influence macrophage polarization toward profibrotic phenotypes in some contexts. In the lung, MARCO contributes to asbestos sensing and can drive profibrotic M2-like polarization. (Arredouani 2014; Georgoudaki et al. 2016; Murthy et al. 2015). In the TME of mouse tumor models and human cancer, MARCO is expressed by a subset of TAMs that share an immunosuppressive gene signature with M2. Antibodies targeting MARCO hinder metastasis and decrease tumor growth. The MARCO gene is another potential target for the cancer immunotherapy tool known as chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T cells (Georgoudaki et al. 2016). Additionally, checkpoint blockade-based immunotherapy and CAR T cells targeted against MARCO may enhance the effectiveness of other immunotherapeutic methods (June et al. 2018; Dai, et al. 2016). More research is needed to determine if CAR T cell transfer therapy can be made more effective by co-expressing two distinct CARs, one for tumor cells and one for TAMs.

Tumor-associated macrophages and immune checkpoint immunotherapy

ICB (immune checkpoint blockades) immunotherapy is fundamentally driven by type 1 T cell-mediated immune responses, with myelomonocytic cells playing a complementary role in determining response and eligibility for this treatment (DeNardo and Ruffell 2019; Mantovani et al. 2017, 2022). The expression of immunosuppressive molecules by myelomonocytic cells underlies their role in driving both primary and acquired resistance to ICB, these molecules include checkpoint ligands PD-L1, PD-L2, CD80, CD86, and poliovirus receptor (PVR), which are an extremely important part of the tumor-extrinsic pathways (Sharma et al. 2017; Kryczek et al. 2006; Kuang et al. 2009; Braun et al. 2021). The predictive power of PD-L1 evaluation by immunohistochemistry may differ depending on the cell type, such as tumor or immune cells (Tang et al. 2018), though it is authorized as a companion diagnostic for anti-PD1 treatment in non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) and other tumors (Topalian et al. 2016; Havel et al. 2019). Preclinical data suggest PD-L1 expression on infiltrating immune cells correlates with ICB response more consistently than tumor-cell PD-L1 in some settings (Havel et al. 2019). Expression of PD-1 by macrophages, can inhibit their phagocytic function against cancer cells (Gordon et al. 2017; Laba et al. 2022). Expression of counter-receptors by myelomonocytic cells can enhance their interaction with negative regulators such as T cells and NK cells. Macrophages express VISTA, a ligand that inhibits T cell checkpoint activity, where it engages with PSGL1 as its binding partner (Wang et al. 2011; Johnston et al. 2019). mAbs against VISTA cause transcriptional and functional variations in human monocytes (Rogers et al. 2021).

The function of macrophages in ICB is massively tissue-dependent. For example, hepatic macrophages within liver metastases have been shown to undermine systemic immunotherapy efficacy through T cell elimination, while distinct myeloid compositions, including neutrophils and macrophages, contributed to ICB resistance in triple-negative breast cancer (Kim et al. 2019; Yu et al. 2021). Similarly, the pleural and peritoneal cavities serve as immunosuppressive niches that facilitate tumor advancement. Within these coelomic spaces, macrophage-derived TIM4 expression further suppresses CD8 + T cell activity, thereby altering the effectiveness of ICB (Chow et al. 2021), while downregulating TIM4 using antibodies increases ICB effectiveness. Vaccination (Fridlender et al. 2010), checkpoint inhibitors (Zhu et al. 2014; Neubert et al. 2018), and macrophage depletion can all work together to improve the efficacy of immunotherapy. Moreover, there are now active clinical trials that are focusing on TAM-centered treatment methods, such as combinations of checkpoint inhibitors and anti-CSF1R antibodies (DeNardo and Ruffell 2019; Mantovani et al. 2017).

Application of engineered macrophages in cancer drug delivery: Direct and indirect applications

Macrophages, particularly the proinflammatory M1 subtype, have recently gained attention as potential tools for targeted drug delivery due to their immune compatibility. Exosomes derived from macrophages contain surface proteins that can target tumor cells, making them suitable vectors for therapeutic delivery (Xia et al. 2020). To improve tumor specificity, nanoparticles are coated with macrophage membranes, which can suppress immune evasion and active tumorigenic properties. Macrophages are unique drug transporters compared to red blood cells, cancer cells, and neutrophils. Red blood cells (RBC) improve blood circulation but lack tumor-targeting capabilities, and neutrophils can only target inflammation. Macrophage-based drug carriers have a versatile range of functions and are superior to RBCs and neutrophils. They circulate efficiently and interact with tumor tissue by binding to vascular cell adhesion molecule-1(VCAM-1) on cancer cells using α4 and β1 integrins (Fang et al. 2018).

M1 macrophage subtypes, including bone marrow-derived macrophages and RAW264.7 cells, have been employed in tumor-targeting therapeutic applications. RAW264.7 macrophages can bind metastatic cells through α4/β1 integrin and VCAM-1 interactions (Cao et al. 2016). Similarly, monocytes exhibit intrinsic tumor-targeting capacity, though the mechanisms regulating their recruitment remain unclear (Wang et al. 2018b).

Several experimental studies have investigated the efficacy of engineered macrophages loaded with chemotherapeutic agents (Table 2). Fu et al. developed doxorubicin (DOX)-loaded RAW264.7 macrophages that improved viability and enhanced survival in mice with 4T1 tumors (Fu et al. 2015). Guo et al. revealed that DOX delivered via M1 macrophages, unlike liposomal DOX, effectively inhibited tumor invasion and growth (Guo et al. 2019a). Furthermore, DOX-loaded macrophages transfer drugs through tunnelling nanotubes, a rapid intercellular route surpassing conventional endocytosis, especially effective against ovarian carcinoma cells (Xia et al. 2020). However, certain chemotherapeutics such as 5-fluorouracil, irinotecan, cisplatin, and gemcitabine may cause toxic effects on M1 macrophages, impairing their viability. Cisplatin and gemcitabine suppress cell proliferation, while 5-fluorouracil and irinotecan have no effect on cell viability within a specific dose range (Sakai et al. 2013). Application of engineered macrophages in cancer drug delivery

To overcome these limitations, many studies use indirect delivery methods in which macrophages serve as carriers of therapeutic nanoparticles rather than free drugs. This improves the drug loading ratio and reduces drug toxicity to macrophages, improving their effectiveness. Li et al. demonstrated that macrophages loaded with paclitaxel (PTX)-based nanoparticles achieved significant tumor reduction greater than conventional formulations, showing enhanced efficacy and drug loading properties (Li et al. 2016).

Nanoparticles (NPs) can be loaded into monocytes or macrophages, which are effective in targeting tumors (Wang et al. 2018b; Fan et al. 2018; Lang et al. 2017). Macrophages, engineered with NPs, have been used in tumor therapy due to their superior targeting capabilities. Tao et al. utilized nano-PTX injected into bone marrow macrophages to suppress glioma progression. The nano-PTX-loaded macrophages showed better efficacy in suppressing tumor progression compared to nano-PTX alone (Tao et al. 2013). Other NPs, including iron oxide, SN38, MSN, DOX, rGO, and SN38, also showed remarkable anti-tumor activity in animal tumor models (Wang et al. 2018b; Huang et al. 2018b; Qiang et al. 2019; Dalzon et al. 2019).

Building on these findings, other studies engineered NP-loaded M1 macrophages to deliver drugs like 5-fluorouracil and magnetic nanoparticles. These macrophages actively migrate to hypoxic tumor regions in response to chemoattractant gradients from malignant cells (Ikehara et al. 2006; Choi et al. 2007). Magnetic nanoparticles (Fe3O4 NPs) have also been added to these macrophages, improving tumor burden and metastasis by increasing macrophage infiltration and directing them to TME using pulsed magnetic field gradients (Muthana et al. 2015). Likewise, peritoneal macrophages loaded with DOX-liposomes achieved greater suppression of tumor metastasis compared to DOX-liposomes alone (Choi et al. 2012).

Nanoparticle-based macrophage carriers have since evolved into diverse formulations with improved targeting, biocompatibility, and tumor penetration. Notably, M1 macrophages carrying PLGA-based nanoparticles demonstrate the capacity to penetrate through the blood–brain barrier, distributing drugs in brain tissue of glioma models evenly, and improve survival compared to nanoparticle treatment alone (Pang et al. 2018). Nanoparticles attached with macrophages reduce tumor metastasis, specifically to lung, in breast cancer, glioblastoma, and other malignancies by affecting tumor invasion through the blood–brain barrier (Wang et al. 2018b; He et al. 2017; Smith et al. 2014). Moreover, Zheng et al. injected CpG-modified gold-silver nanorods (AuNRs) onto apoptotic bodies, which were then engulfed by inflammatory Ly-6C + monocytes. These AuNR-loaded macrophages penetrated the tumor, raising the efficiency of photothermal therapy. This method significantly decreased metastasis and relapse in C57/BL6 mice with EL4 tumors (Zheng et al. 2020).

Phagosomes, a major barrier to drug release from macrophages, store medications and can be broken down when applied as drug carriers (Doshi et al. 2011). This makes macrophages limited in their utilization as drug carriers due to the presence of nanocarriers or medications in their phagosomes and the potential for bacteria to break down these medicines (Sakai et al. 2013; Doshi et al. 2011; Chang et al. 2013). To address these issues, researchers have managed to develop phagocytosis-resistant backpacks that allow microparticles to stay stuck to macrophage surfaces, preserving tumor-targeting ability and particle characteristics (Doshi et al. 2011). Furthermore, scientists can efficiently attach dendrimers of quantum dots (QDs) and polyamidoamine (PAMAM) on macrophage surfaces (Holden et al. 2010). Macrophage–nanoparticle interactions are also influenced by nanoparticle size, surface chemistry, and internalization dynamics. Studies suggest that nanoparticles around 100 nm achieve optimal uptake and migration. Miniature nanoparticles (between 30 and 50 nm) have the dual effect of reducing macrophage population in tumor tissue and limiting migration. Therefore, nanoparticles should be approximately 100 nm in size to allow NP-loaded macrophages to serve as efficient drug transporters (Chang et al. 2013). To further optimize macrophage-based therapy, researchers have turned to bioengineering and genetic modification strategies. Three methods exist to modify macrophages, with the most straightforward method being incubating NPs, lipid-ligand conjugates, or lipid-protein conjugates into the macrophage membrane, which enhances their targeting or provides additional activities (Cao et al. 2018; Li et al. 2019b). Chemical conjugation to macrophage surfaces, sodium periodate coupling with amine-containing ligands or nanoparticles, and genetic modification using CRISPR-Cas9 gene editing technology or transfecting plasmids encoding genes are three approaches to increase macrophage function (Holden et al. 2010; Aalipour et al. 2019; Ray et al. 2018).

Gambhir's team developed Arg1-responsive genetically modified macrophages, which can detect extremely tiny cancer cells using a highly sensitive blood test, demonstrating that enzyme-responsive peptides can expand the tumor targeting capabilities of macrophages (Aalipour et al. 2019; Ronald et al. 2015). Moreover, Cao et al. developed LD-MDS, a transformable legumain response nanovesicle, which can absorb metastatic 4T1 cancer cells after legumain protease treatment, preventing tumor metastasis, and can then be converted into soravtansine-loaded exosome-like nanovesicles (Cao et al. 2018). Furthermore, Ray and colleagues utilized CRISPR-Cas9 gene editing to establish macrophages lacking SIRPα. They used cationic arginine-coated gold nanoparticles as vehicles to deliver guide RNA alongside an engineered Cas9 protein. Despite a fourfold improvement in cancer cell phagocytosis, no further in vivo studies were conducted (Ray et al. 2018). Researchers have found that macrophages with chimeric antigen receptors can improve tumor cell phagocytosis (Morrissey et al. 2018). Modified macrophages with hyaluronic acid-decorated superparamagnetic iron oxide nanoparticles (HIONs) have been shown to improve tumor suppression in mice harbouring 4T1 tumors (Li et al. 2019b). Additionally, LM-DOXs, which are lipopolysaccharide-anchored macrophages, stimulate microtube formation and induce TAM to secrete TNF-α, enhancing anti-tumor effectiveness in a mouse orthotopic lung cancer model (Guo et al. 2019b).

Drug carriers based on macrophages can efficiently transport medications to primary and metastatic cancers, but interactions between macrophages and the payload can compromise delivery rate and therapeutic effectiveness. Modifying medication concentration, NP characteristics, and placement, as well as surface modification, genetic engineering, or well-designed loaded NPs, can improve macrophage targeting and phagocytic capacity.

Despite the significant preclinical progress achieved with CRISPR-Cas9-modified macrophages, nanoparticle-loaded carriers, phagocytosis-resistant backpacks, and membrane-coated constructs, the translation of these platforms into clinical practice is still unavailable. The first and most fundamental barrier is GMP (Good Manufacturing Practice) scalability (Iyer et al. 2018). The production of engineered macrophages for clinical use requires isolation of autologous or allogeneic monocytes from patient’s blood or bone marrow, ex vivo differentiation and activation under controlled conditions, genetic modification with therapeutic targets, and strict quality control (Poltavets et al. 2020). Each of these steps introduces significant variability, and the transition from small-scale laboratory protocols to large-scale GMP-compliant manufacturing remains technically demanding. Using a patient's own cells avoids immune rejection but yields very few monocytes, especially in patients weakened by prior chemotherapy. Using donor cells solves the yield problem but raises rejection risks (Haworth and Sharpe 2021; Sani 2025). No standard GMP protocol exists for this process yet, and regulatory classification as advanced therapy medicinal products adds another layer of complexity. Cost is another important barrier. For instance, CRISPR editing and nanoparticle surface engineering are expensive, making these treatments difficult to access widely (Kaupbayeva et al. 2024). Systemic toxicity is also another concern. Engineered macrophages can behave in unpredictable ways in the blood and may shift to unintended states. Nanoparticles can trigger immune responses or accumulate in other organs instead of the tumor. The long-term effects of CRISPR edits in humans, including off-target changes and immune responses to Cas9, are still not well understood (De Matteis 2017; Kalter et al. 2025). Solving these problems will need better manufacturing protocols, safer gene delivery methods, smarter nanoparticle designs, and clearer regulatory guidance for these complex therapies.

Targeting macrophages in cancer immunotherapy

Cancer immunotherapy uses the immune system to eliminate cancer cells, reducing cancer spread and progression. Key strategies include vaccines, immune checkpoint inhibitors, and adoptive cell therapies that can accelerate immunotherapy. However, efficacy is limited by poor drug accumulation, immune resistance, and TME suppression. Precision delivery systems, nanoparticles, and engineered exosomes provide more potent, safer, and longer-lasting immunity (Zhang et al. 2025; Lu et al. 2024). However, one of the main strategies can be the regulation or targeting of main factors regulating tumorigenesis and immune systems that TAMs are among them. TAMs, which are distinguished as a type of tumor stroma, have gained interest in cancer immunotherapy due to their presence in various cancers and their role in regulating immune cell functions (Petty and Yang 2017). Targeting TAMs provides new treatment options for cancers resistant to conventional chemotherapeutics and synergizes with existing immunotherapies. Furthermore, current therapeutic strategies concentrate on declining TAM abundance, preventing TAM accumulation, and inducing TAM reprogramming towards antitumoral functions. These strategies aim to conquer immunosuppressive and protumoral functions.

CSF1–CSF1R signaling blockers

Among the key molecular pathways regulating TAM recruitment and activation, the CSF1–CSF1R pathway is one of the most thoroughly studied therapeutic targets. The fact that small chemical antagonists or neutralizing antibodies can disrupt CSF1-CSF1R signaling encourages the development of drugs that target TAM recruitment and activation. Blocking CSF1-CSF1R signaling reduces cancer proliferation and invasion, and improves long-term survival in animal models (DeNardo et al. 2011). CSF-1R inhibitors, such as BLZ945, has been demonstrated to effectively suppress tumor growth in a murine K14-HPV-16 transgenic mouse model of cervical carcinogenesis (Strachan et al. 2013). This has been translated into a first-in-human Phase I/II investigation of BLZ945 alone or in combination with PDR001, a monoclonal antibody against PD-1 in advanced solid tumors. However, results of this study showed limited clinical efficacy despite good safety; further investigations is needed in this regard (Naing et al. 2020). PLX3397 blockage of CSF1R increases adoptive cell therapy effectiveness in murine melanoma by preventing immunosuppressive macrophage recruitment and activation. In addition, this agent enhances effectiveness of expands ionizing radiation therapy in xenograft glioblastoma from mice via hindering differentiation and oncogenic polarization (Mok et al. 2014; Stafford et al. 2016). In a human Phase I/II research that assessed the safety as well as effectiveness of PLX3397 in tenosynovial giant-cell tumors, 32% of patients attained disease stability, and 52% had a partial response after receiving the drug (Tap et al. 2015; Baldi et al. 2020). A neutralizing antibody against CSF1R, known as Emactuzumab, lessened macrophage accumulation within murine tumor models and illustrated similar therapeutic impacts against patients diagnosed with diffuse-type giant cell tumors. Two other neutralizing antibodies, AMG 820 and IMC-CS4, are under Phase I clinical trials for advanced solid tumor treatment (Ries et al. 2014). These findings acknowledge the clinical efficacy and therapeutic potential of CSF1-CSF1R signaling blockade as monotherapy or in combination with other therapies for human cancer treatments.

CCL2–CCR2 axis inhibitors

The migration of monocytes from the bone marrow to the tumor site is significantly dependent on the CCL2-CCR2 signaling axis (Qian et al. 2011; Chang et al. 2016; Sanford et al. 2013). High CCL2 levels in human neoplasia extend metastasis and decrease overall survival. In mouse models, knocking down gene expression or blocking CCR2 decreases both tumor growth and expansion (Sanford et al. 2013; Lu and Kang 2009). PF-04136309, a drug that inhibits CCR2 activation and downstream signaling, was discovered to be not only safe but also effective in a Phase-Ib clinical trial comparing FOLFIRINOX with PF-04136309 in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma patients. Combination treatment reduced TAM and Treg infiltration while enhancing CD4 + and CD8 + effector cell populations in TME. Combination of PF-04136309 plus FOLFIRINOX improved outcomes with better response to therapy compared with FOLFIRINOX alone (Nywening et al. 2016). Furthermore, carlumab, a human anti-CCL2 IgG1κ mAb, represented early anticancer effectiveness when tested on patients with solid tumors. Nevertheless, the clinical usefulness of carlumab is limited since it only temporarily suppresses free CCL2 in patients (Brana et al. 2015; Sandhu et al. 2013).

Trabectedin

One European-approved chemotherapeutic drug for soft tissue sarcoma, known as trabectedin, specifically binds to the minor groove of DNA leading to cell-cycle arrest and death. It selectively removes human monocytes and macrophages while preventing CCL2 and IL-6 production, representing therapeutic potentials in targeting TAMs in vivo (Germano et al. 2010). In preclinical and clinical investigations, trabectedin induces caspase-8-dependent apoptosis pathway selectively in mononuclear phagocytes of blood, spleen, and tumor microenvironment, reducing their expression, while preserving neutrophils and lymphocytes functions. Tumors in mice have downregulated CCL2 and VEGF, which declines angiogenic sprouting and monocyte recruitment. Patients with soft tissue sarcoma have shown reduced TAM density and tumor angiogenesis in biopsies (Germano et al. 2013). The agent trabectedin has been evaluated in multiple clinical trials for the treatment of soft tissue sarcoma. There was conclusive evidence of its therapeutic benefit from two multicenter Phase III trials, with one demonstrating it is superior to dacarbazine in treating advanced soft tissue sarcoma. Another research discovered that trabectedin plus pegylated liposomal doxorubicin improves progression-free survival and overall survival in recurrent ovarian cancer patients as a second-line treatment (Demetri et al. 2016; Monk et al. 2010).

Toll-like receptor agonists

Analyses of stromal macrophage function in early- and late-stage cancers exhibited that macrophages adopt an immunosuppressive phenotype with tumorigenic characteristics after increased contact with the TME (Wynn et al. 2013). There is ongoing research into the possibility of retraining Toll-like receptors to restore their tumoricidal capabilities in the context of immunotherapy. TLR agonists have been tested in mouse models to reprogram TAM functions, leading to the production of proinflammatory cytokines like IFN-γ, TNF-α, and IL-12 through NF-κB signaling (Wang et al. 2014). The FDA-approved TLR7 agonist imiquimod improved the impact of T-cell tumor infiltration and achieved complete tumor regression in breast cancer in mice, when combined with irradiation (Dewan et al. 2012). Similarly, the TLR7/8 agonist 3 M-052 reprogrammed TAMs towards M1-like cells and, when combined with CTLA-4 or PD-L1 antibodies, eradicated metastatic melanoma by restoring macrophage and CD8 + T cell cytotoxicity (Singh et al. 2014). Several early-phase clinical trials at Phase I and II levels are actively underway to explore TAM functional reprogramming through TLR-mediated pathways (Hanahan and Weinberg 2011; Zheng and Ma 2022).

Recent studies have shown positive effects of targeting TAMs in triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC). Niu et al. used DOX-containing nanoparticles to target TAMs through mannose-mannose receptor recognition in TNBC mouse models. They found that therapeutic efficacy of these nanoparticles is associated with targeting TAMs within TNBC stroma, and reducing their expression, leading to suppressed tumor growth (Niu et al. 2016). There are more recent approaches to target TAMs in vivo including DNA vaccines that target legumain, a TAM-associated protein, in murine cancer models. In addition, clodronate/liposome as an adjuvant therapy with TLR agonist-conjugated peptide Pam2IDG, improves antitumor immunity by reducing expression of TAMs (Shen et al. 2014; Luo et al. 2006). These evidences demonstrate that targeted therapeutic approaches can further exploit the considerable presence of TAMs within cancer stroma. TAM-targeted therapeutic strategies

Clinical importance of macrophages

High densities of macrophages expressing specific markers correlate with unfavorable clinical outcomes across multiple solid malignancy subtypes (Zhang et al. 2012; Ruffell and Coussens 2015; Komohara et al. 2014). However, in tumors such as lung, gastric, prostate, and bone cancers, the correlation remains inconsistent, (Zhang et al. 2012) likely due to the use of different macrophage markers applied for detection. CD68, which is a glycoprotein that can be seen in intracellular granules, is the most common marker for murine TAMs. CD68 is highly expressed in various cell types, including granulocytes, dendritic cells, fibroblasts, endothelial cells, and some lymphoid subsets (Gottfried et al. 2008; Hameed et al. 1994; Ruffell et al. 2012). While CD68 serves as a general macrophage marker identifying overall macrophage abundance, other receptors, such as CD163 and CD204, provide more specific insights. The presence of the macrophage scavenger receptors CD163 and CD204 is remarkably related to poor outcomes in NSCLC (Chung et al. 2012; Hirayama et al. 2012; Ohri et al. 2011; Quatromoni and Eruslanov 2012).

CD163 and CD204 are selective macrophage biomarkers that are responsible for activating macrophages towards tumor-promoting and immunosuppressive phenotypes. Notable associations have been found between CD163/CD204 and adverse results across multiple tumor types (Komohara et al. 2014). This reveals that macrophage polarization can direct clinical outcomes (Roxburgh and McMillan 2012). The presence of CD68 + cells is positively associated with survival rate in colorectal adenocarcinoma, where macrophages in human colorectal cancer are found to be both functionally and phenotypically anti-tumor (Biswas and Mantovani 2010; Zhang et al. 2015; Edin et al. 2012).

Evidence from both preclinical and early clinical studies suggests that repolarizing macrophages towards an anti-tumor phenotype can effectively suppress tumor growth and improve therapeutic response. Evidence proposes that a CD40 agonist monoclonal antibody, when administered in conjunction with gemcitabine, may be efficacious in treating pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) in both mice and humans (Beatty et al. 2011, 2013; Vonderheide et al. 2013). This is the first clinical investigation to represent the potential effectiveness of macrophage-targeted agents, alongside a CSF-1R-targeting depleting antibody administered in patients with diffuse-type giant cell tumors (Ries et al. 2014).

The use of agonists or antagonists, or the deliberate omission of specific states, should characterize treatments for malignancies at their advanced levels (Pittet et al. 2022). Early studies using pan-macrophage markers demonstrated that higher TAM abundance predicted poor clinical outcomes (Zhang et al. 2012). CD68, a pan-macrophage marker, is often applied for histological analyses but may not differentiate oncogenic TAMs from other macrophage populations. CD163, which is linked to M2-like activation, could be beneficial in recognizing TAMs that improve tumor growth. Transcriptomic analyses identified TAM-associated gene signatures, including TNF, SIGLEC1, CCL8, and CSF1, which are highly expressed in aggressive subtypes breast cancer and predict shorter disease-specific survival (Gentles et al. 2015; Cassetta et al. 2019). In a recent investigation, scientists used single-cell transcriptome data to reevaluate TAM characteristics. The outcomes demonstrated that revealing that distinct TAM subpopulations correlate with specific prognostic outcomes. The MMP12-expressing state, which resembles neither M1 nor M2 cells, is most strongly related to insignificant clinical consequences (Zhao et al. 2025). In ovarian, pancreatic, lung, and colorectal cancers, SPP1⁺ TAMs exhibiting pro-angiogenic M2-like features and elevated VCAN or FN1 expression correlate with poor clinical outcomes (Cheng et al. 2021).

A single-cell RNA-seq study discovered an enrichment of M2-like TAMs in the advanced disease stage (Braun et al. 2021). TAMs that lack clear M1 or M2 polarization markers may nevertheless carry significant functional relevance. In human breast cancers, for instance, single-cell RNA-seq analysis uncovered various TAM states, such as M1-like CXCL10-expressing TAMs, M2-like EGR1-and SIGLEC1-expressing TAMs, and lipid-associated macrophage-like FABP5- and APOE-expressing TAMs. FABP5-expressing TAMs were present at invasive tumor tissues, generated immunoregulatory molecules, and were correlated with unfavourable survival (Wu et al. 2021). Although FABP5⁺ macrophages do not fit traditional M2 definitions, this evidence reveals that they are potential therapeutic targets for breast cancer patients.

TAMs can modulate the outcomes of chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and immunotherapy by altering immune responses or drug bioavailability (Engblom et al. 2016). Specifically, TAMs can reduce immune checkpoint inhibitor (ICI) efficacy by eliminating anti-PD-1 antibodies from PD-1 + T cell surfaces. This process is associated with the crosstalk between glycans on antibody Fc-domain and FcγRs on TAMs. Recent studies are focusing clinical use of tislelizumab, an engineered anti-PD-1 antibody engineered to lack FcγR binding capacity (Arlauckas, et al. 2017). The combination of radiation and the CSF1R inhibitor sotuletinib is one example of TAMs reducing the effectiveness of radiation (Akkari et al. 2020). Conversely, certain TAM subsets can enhance radiotherapy outcomes when combined with nanoparticles by promoting vascular permeability and facilitating therapeutic nanoparticle penetration into tumors (Arlauckas, et al. 2017). Moreover, TAM-focused therapies can influence tumor metabolism, modulating glucose and lipid pathways that affect both antitumor and protumoral activities. The anti-SLAMF7 antibody elotuzumab is one example of a monoclonal antibody that may have its antitumor efficacy enhanced by TAMs (Kurdi et al. 2018; Xu et al. 2025).

Conclusion and remarks

The TME and the interactions occurring in this environment have been of importance for the researchers for a long time. Among the various kinds of cells present in the TME, the function of TAMs has been of importance. Despite decades of research, the diverse origins and various functions of TAMs remain incomplete for translational research. This review, therefore, aimed to (i) summarize current evidence linking TAM function, metabolism, and polarization to tumor progression and treatment resistance, and (ii) evaluate therapeutic strategies that target TAM recruitment, survival, and reprogramming. Macrophages are divided into embryo-derived vs monocyte-derived based on their origin, and their origin can affect function, gene expression, and response to therapy. The increasing evidence has shown that, in spite of the function of macrophages as immune cells, they play a significant role in the regulation of tumorigenesis. Microenvironmental factors such as hypoxia/HIFs, metabolic reprogramming, cytokines (CSF1, CCL2, IL-6, IL-1β), and ECM remodelling, drive TAM polarization toward tumor-promoting phenotypes that enhance proliferation, CSC maintenance, EMT, and metastasis. The TAMs have been shown to be promising targets for the treatment of cancer. The changes in the polarization of macrophages can affect the process of tumorigenesis. The increase in M1 polarization of macrophages can suppress tumorigenesis, while cancer cells enhance M2 polarization of macrophages to promote progression and obtain resistance to therapy. Currently, three important strategies have been developed to target TAMs, including suppressing the recruitment of monocytes/macrophages, depletion of macrophages, and re-polarization of macrophages from M2 into M1. The increased M2 polarization of macrophages has been shown to impair the process of immunotherapy, and therefore, targeting macrophages along with immune checkpoint inhibitors can improve the process of cancer suppression. Importantly, macrophages have been developed as nanocarriers for the delivery of drugs in cancer therapy. Moreover, the nanoparticles can be functionalized by macrophages to improve the nanoparticle-mediated delivery of drugs in tumor suppression. Recent advances in engineered delivery systems, including macrophage-targeted nanoparticles and injectable hydrogel platforms, provide improved spatial and temporal control over TAM polarization and drug release (Chao and Liu 2023; Zhang et al. 2024). These bioengineered strategies enhance therapeutic precision and represent a promising frontier in macrophage-centered immunotherapy. A number of modulators of macrophages have been developed for the process of cancer immunotherapy, including CSF1, CCL2, and TLR modulators. In addition to the pre-clinical studies, the clinical experiments have highlighted the importance of macrophages in the diagnosis and prognosis of patients. While several aspects of TAM function and role in cancer have been identified, major gaps remain that need to be filled. Previous studies lack consistent data about TAM markers, which can be very useful for developing novel prognostic tools. In addition, better-designed animal models and preclinical studies can help with a better understanding of macrophage subtypes. For identifying targetable TAM states, future studies can focus on combining single-cell analysis with functional assays. These plans and approaches will accelerate safe and effective TAM-directed therapies and improve the translational impact of TAM biology.
