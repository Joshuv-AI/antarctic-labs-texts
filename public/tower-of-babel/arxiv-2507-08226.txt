A Stokes-Brinkman-type formulation for the eigenvalue problem in porous media Thanks: Submitted to the editors DATE.
Felipe Lepe, Gonzalo Rivera, Jesus Vellojin

Abstract
In this paper we introduce and analyze, for two and three dimensions, a finite element method to approximate the natural frequencies of a flow system governed by the Stokes-Brinkman equations. Here, the fluid presents the capability of being within a porous media. Taking advantage of the Stokes regularity results for the solution, and considering inf-sup stable families of finite elements, we prove convergence together with a priori and a posteriori error estimates for the eigenvalues and eigenfunctions with the aid of the compact operators theory. We report a series of numerical tests in order to confirm the developed theory.

Introduction

Eigenvalue problems are attractive from the mathematical and computational point of view, where the applications of such problems
can be found in several fields, as structural engineering, electromagnetism, flow stability, among others. The analysis of eigenvalue problems is a well stated issue, where a survey of the developed theory on this matter can be found in, for instance, in
[
5
]
. Particularly, the analysis of numerical methods flow eigenvalue problems has an important amount of contributions
available on the literature, such as
[
11
,
18
,
19
,
20
,
21
,
23
,
24
]

The Brinkman system is relevant in continuum mechanics and fluid dynamics, particularly in the study of porous media flow. This problem addresses the flow behavior of a fluid in a medium where viscous and inertial effects are both relevant, extending Darcy’s law (which describes flow in porous media at low velocities) to regimes where these effects play a more significant role. The Brinkman problem applies to a range of physical scenarios, including fluid flow in biological tissues, industrial filtration processes, and environmental engineering. In porous media, high-velocity flows induce viscous and sometimes inertial forces, leading to deviations from simple Darcy flow. The Brinkman equation allows for modeling these effects with an added diffusion term. We refer to
[
1
,
10
,
13
,
15
,
17
,
25
]
and the references therein for a more deep analysis on different techniques and numerical schemes to study this problem.

As stated in
[
17
]
, the Brinkman equations are a parameter-dependent combination of the Darcy and Stokes equations. It acts as a coupling layer between a free-surface flow and a porous Darcy flow. One of the main difficulties arising from this coupling is that typical numerical schemes fails to predict the behavior when we move from the Stokes limit to Darcy. The standard approach to modeling such problems involves coupling Darcy and Stokes flows and enforcing the Beavers–Joseph–Saffman conditions along the interface
[
4
,
6
,
31
]
. However, with an appropriate scaling of the model, it is possible to study a complete system of equations that predicts fluid behavior in a domain with regions of varying permeability. The Stokes–Brinkman equations, which reduce to either Stokes or Darcy flow depending on the coefficients, were proposed in
[
27
]
as a replacement for coupled Darcy and Stokes models. A proper selection of this coefficients allows to represent both free-flow and porous regions, thus avoiding any further assumptions on the interface condition. An example of this approach is provided in
[
34
]
, where the authors present a residual-based a posteriori error analysis for the Stokes–Brinkman problem discretized with Taylor–Hood finite elements, using this analysis to guide an adaptive mesh refinement process.

The aforementioned study serves as the foundation for the present paper, whose primary goal is to propose an eigenvalue formulation for the Stokes-Brinkman problem. This formulation is based on defining a specific permeability parameter that characterizes both porous and fully permeable regions of some domain. Given the increased resistance to fluid flow within Darcy-type domains, the fundamental modes of the Stokes-Brinkman system can aid in studying energy minimization problems, as the fluid naturally favors regions with lower resistance. Furthermore, by selecting sufficiently low permeability values, certain zones can be treated as obstacles. This particular flexibility in the choice of permeability parameters introduces an additional tool for modeling eigenmodes in domains with multiple porous subdomains, thereby simulating behaviors analogous to domains with internal membranes. Potential applications include models for filtration, subsurface water treatment, and similar environmental and engineering processes.

The purpose of this paper is to analyze a primal velocity-pressure formulation for the Stokes-Brinkman eigenvalue model, which we analyze using the compact operators theory. For this type of formulation there exists well know inf-sup stable finite element families to approximate the velocity and the pressure.
We choose mini elements and Taylor-Hood to handle this Stokes-Brinkman eigenvalue problem because they are the most common alternatives to approximate numerically the spectrum of such system. For these numerical schemes, convergence and a priori error estimates are derived with the aid of the compact operators theory presented in
[
2
,
5
]
. A residual-based a posteriori error analysis is also provided, which under standard techniques, is proved to be reliable and efficient. Also, we perform a series of numerical experiments to illustrate the eigenvalue problem, where the intention is, as a first step, to conclude that the families of finite elements of our scheme does not allow to introduce spurious eigenvalues on the computational spectrum. In both two and three dimensions, we examine how varying the permeability parameter influences the spectrum and its regularity, particularly through the formation of regions with high pressure gradients. Furthermore, we provide a physical interpretation of the results, analyzing the shapes of the eigenfunctions for selected permeability values.

The paper is organized in the following manner: In Section
2
we propose the eigenvalue problem for the Stokes-Brinkman equations. The well-posedness and spectral characterization of the model is derived by means of a general inf-sup condition. Section
3
deals with the discrete problem, for which existence and uniqueness are established. In addition, the convergence of solution operators and the a priori error estimates for the eigenvalues and eigenfunctions are obtained. Section
4
provides the a posteriori analysis for the problem, which relies on the inf-sup condition and the Scott-Zhang interpolant. Finally, Section
5
is devoted to report several numerical examples to test the convergence and performance of the numerical scheme, and also to study behavior of the spectrum when different permeability scenarios are considered.

2
The model problem

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be an open bounded domain with Lipschitz boundary
\partial\Omega
. Let
\Gamma_{1}
and
\Gamma_{2}
be disjoint open subset of
\partial\Omega
such that
\partial\Omega=\overline{\Gamma}_{1}\cup\overline{\Gamma}_{2}
. Considering a steady-state of the balance laws for linear fluid equations, the problem to be studied is given as follows (see for example
[
34
]
)

\displaystyle\mathbb{K}^{-1}\boldsymbol{u}-\nu\Delta\boldsymbol{u}+\nabla p

\displaystyle=\lambda\boldsymbol{u}

\displaystyle\text{in $\Omega$},

(1)

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{u}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\text{in $\Omega$},

(2)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{u}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{0}

\displaystyle\text{on $\Gamma_{1}$},

(3)

\displaystyle(\nu\nabla\boldsymbol{u}-p\mathbb{I})\boldsymbol{n}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{0}

\displaystyle\text{on $\Gamma_{2}$}.

(4)

Here,
\boldsymbol{u}
is the fluid velocity,
p
is the pressure of the fluid,
\mathbb{I}
the identity matrix in
\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
, and the parameter
\nu
is the viscosity of the fluid. The vector
\boldsymbol{n}
represents the unit normal. We also consider
\mathbb{K}
as a bounded, symmetric, and positive definite tensor describing the permeability properties of the domain. If
\mathbb{K}^{-1}\rightarrow\boldsymbol{0}
, the equation is reduced to Stokes, while if
\nu=0
, we have Darcy’s law. In this study, we split the domain in two media
\overline{\Omega}:=\overline{\Omega}_{S}\cup\overline{\Omega}_{D}
, where
\Omega_{S}
and
\Omega_{D}
represent subdomains where there is free flow and porous media, respectively and assume
\nu>0
. For the free-flow domain, we take
\mathbb{K}^{-1}=\boldsymbol{0}
, while
\mathbb{K}^{-1}\gg\boldsymbol{0}
is considered in
\Omega_{D}
. This allows to study the eigenmodes on domains where we have membrane-like behavior or internal filters.

To derive the weak form of the governing equations, we first define the appropriate functional spaces for the velocity and pressure. The space for the velocity is given by

\boldsymbol{\mathrm{H}}_{\Gamma_{1}}(\Omega):=\{\boldsymbol{v}\in\boldsymbol{\mathrm{H}}^{1}(\Omega):\boldsymbol{v}=\boldsymbol{0}\text{ on }\Gamma_{1}\},

whereas
\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)
is the space for the pressure. We note that thanks to the natural boundary condition on
\Gamma_{2}
, the term
\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}
automatically adjusts itself on the outflow boundary to ensure that mass is conserved (the volume of flow entering is the same as the volume of flow exiting the domain), hence
p
is uniquely defined (see for instance
[
7
, Chapter 5]
). On the other hand, if
\Gamma_{2}=\emptyset
, then the pressure is defined up to a constant. For this case, we take
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{H}}_{\Gamma_{1}}(\Omega):=\boldsymbol{\mathrm{H}}_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
for the velocity space, and
\mathrm{L}_{0}^{2}(\Omega)
for the pressure.

Throughout this work, we assume that the permeability tensor is positive definite for all
\boldsymbol{v}\in\boldsymbol{\mathrm{H}}_{\Gamma_{1}}(\Omega)
. More precisely, there exist positive constants
\mathbb{K}_{*},\mathbb{K}^{*}>0
such that

0<\mathbb{K}_{*}\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{0,\Omega}^{2}\leq(\mathbb{K}^{-1}\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{v})_{0,\Omega}\leq\mathbb{K}^{*}\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{0,\Omega}^{2}.

By testing equations (
1
) with
\boldsymbol{v}\in\boldsymbol{\mathrm{H}}_{\Gamma_{1}}(\Omega)
and (
2
) with
q\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)
, integrating by parts, and using the boundary conditions (
3
)-(
4
) we obtain the following variational problem: Find
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
and
(\boldsymbol{0},0)\neq(\boldsymbol{u},p)\in\boldsymbol{\mathrm{H}}_{\Gamma_{1}}(\Omega)\times\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)
such that

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\mathbb{K}^{-1}\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}+\nu\int_{\Omega}\nabla\boldsymbol{u}:\nabla\boldsymbol{v}-\int_{\Omega}p\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{v}

\displaystyle=\lambda\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\boldsymbol{v},

\displaystyle\forall\boldsymbol{v}\in\boldsymbol{\mathrm{H}}_{\Gamma_{1}}(\Omega),

\displaystyle-\int_{\Omega}\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{u}\,q

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\forall q\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega).

Let us define the continuous bilinear forms
a:\boldsymbol{\mathrm{H}}_{\Gamma_{1}}(\Omega)\times\boldsymbol{\mathrm{H}}_{\Gamma_{1}}(\Omega)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
and
b:\boldsymbol{\mathrm{H}}_{\Gamma_{1}}(\Omega)\times\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
as follows:

\displaystyle a(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})

\displaystyle:=\int_{\Omega}\mathbb{K}^{-1}\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}+\nu\int_{\Omega}\nabla\boldsymbol{u}:\nabla\boldsymbol{v},\,\,\,\forall\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v}\in\boldsymbol{\mathrm{H}}_{\Gamma_{1}}(\Omega),

\displaystyle\quad\text{and}\quad b(\boldsymbol{v},q)

\displaystyle:=-\int_{\Omega}q\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{v}\quad\forall\boldsymbol{v}\in\boldsymbol{\mathrm{H}}_{\Gamma_{1}}(\Omega),\forall q\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega).

Thus, the weak formulation of system (
1
)–(
4
) is stated in the following manner:

Problem 1
.

Find
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
and
(\boldsymbol{0},0)\neq(\boldsymbol{u},p)\in\boldsymbol{\mathrm{H}}_{\Gamma_{1}}(\Omega)\times\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)
such that

\left\{\begin{aligned} a(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})+b(\boldsymbol{v},p)&=\lambda(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})_{0,\Omega},&\forall\boldsymbol{v}\in\boldsymbol{\mathrm{H}}_{\Gamma_{1}}(\Omega),\\
b(\boldsymbol{u},q)&=0,&\forall q\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega),\end{aligned}\right.

where
(\cdot,\cdot)_{0,\Omega}
denotes the usual
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{L}}^{2}
inner product.

In the forthcoming analysis, we need a suitable weighted norm which depends on the physical parameters. With this in mind, given
(\boldsymbol{v},q)\in\boldsymbol{\mathrm{H}}_{\Gamma_{1}}(\Omega)\times\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)
, we define the following norm:

{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|(\boldsymbol{v},q)\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}^{2}:=\|\mathbb{K}^{-1/2}\boldsymbol{v}\|_{0,\Omega}^{2}+\|\nu^{1/2}\nabla\boldsymbol{v}\|_{0,\Omega}^{2}+\|\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{v}\|_{0,\Omega}^{2}+\|q\|_{0,\Omega}^{2}.

Let us define the kernel
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{K}}
of

as follows

Let us remark that the bilinear form

satisfies the following properties

\displaystyle a(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})

\displaystyle\leq\max\{{\mathbb{K}^{*},\nu}\}\|\boldsymbol{u}\|_{1,\Omega}\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{1,\Omega}\quad\forall\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v}\in\boldsymbol{\mathrm{H}}_{\Gamma_{1}}(\Omega),

\displaystyle a(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{v})

\displaystyle\geq\min\{{\mathbb{K}_{*}},\nu\}\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{1,\Omega}^{2}\quad\forall\boldsymbol{v}\in\boldsymbol{\mathcal{K}},

whereas the form
b(\cdot,\cdot)
satisfies the following inf-sup condition

Lemma 2
.

There exists
\beta>0
such that

\sup_{\boldsymbol{0}\neq\boldsymbol{v}\in\boldsymbol{\mathrm{H}}_{\Gamma_{1}}(\Omega)}\frac{b(\boldsymbol{v},q)}{\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{1,\Omega}}\geq\beta\lVert q\rVert_{0,\Omega},\qquad\forall q\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega).

Since we have a free-flow boundary condition, the proofs follows by following the same arguments as in

.

Now we define the following bilinear form

\mathcal{A}((\boldsymbol{u},p),(\boldsymbol{v},q)):=a(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})+b(\boldsymbol{v},p)+b(\boldsymbol{u},q),\quad\forall\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v}\in\boldsymbol{\mathrm{H}}_{\Gamma_{1}}(\Omega),\,\,\,\,\,\forall p,q\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega),

which allows to rewrite Problem

Problem 3
.

Find
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
and
(\boldsymbol{0},0)\neq(\boldsymbol{u},p)\in\boldsymbol{\mathrm{H}}_{\Gamma_{1}}(\Omega)\times\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)

The following result establish a general inf-sup condition for the bilinear form

Lemma 4
.

For all
(\boldsymbol{0},0)\neq(\boldsymbol{u},p)\in\boldsymbol{\mathrm{H}}_{\Gamma_{1}}(\Omega)\times\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)
, there exists

with
{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|(\boldsymbol{v},q)\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}\leq C_{1}{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|(\boldsymbol{u},p)\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}

\mathcal{A}((\boldsymbol{u},p),(\boldsymbol{v},q))\geq C_{2}{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|(\boldsymbol{u},p)\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}^{2},

where
C_{1}
,
C_{2}
are constants depending on the physical parameters.

Proof.

The proof is followed by the same arguments as
[
26
, Lemma 4.3]

For the analysis of the eigenvalue problem, we introduce the so-called solution operators. These operators, which we denote by
\boldsymbol{T}
and
\boldsymbol{B}
, are defined by

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{T}

\displaystyle:\boldsymbol{\mathrm{L}}^{2}(\Omega)\rightarrow\boldsymbol{\mathrm{H}}_{\Gamma_{1}}(\Omega),\,\,\,\boldsymbol{f}\mapsto\boldsymbol{T}\boldsymbol{f}:=\widetilde{\boldsymbol{u}},

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{B}

\displaystyle:\boldsymbol{\mathrm{L}}^{2}(\Omega)\rightarrow\boldsymbol{\mathrm{L}}^{2}(\Omega),\,\,\,\boldsymbol{f}\mapsto\boldsymbol{B}\boldsymbol{f}:=\widetilde{p},

where the pair

is the unique solution of the following source problem

\mathcal{A}((\widetilde{\boldsymbol{u}},\widetilde{p}),(\boldsymbol{v},q))=(\boldsymbol{f},\boldsymbol{v})_{0,\Omega},\qquad\forall(\boldsymbol{v},q)\in\boldsymbol{\mathrm{H}}_{\Gamma_{1}}(\Omega)\times\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega),\\

(5)

which is well posed thanks to Lemma
4
. Moreover, Lemma
4
together with the Babǔska-Brezzi theory reveal that operators
\boldsymbol{T}
and
\boldsymbol{B}
are well defined and bounded.

Let
\mu
be a real number such that
\mu\neq 0
. Notice that
(\mu,\boldsymbol{u})\in\mathbb{R}\times\boldsymbol{\mathrm{H}}_{\Gamma_{1}}(\Omega)
is an eigenpair of
\boldsymbol{T}
if and only if there exists

such that,
(\lambda,(\boldsymbol{u},p))
solves Problem

with
\mu:=1/\lambda
.

Using the fact that the term
(\mathbb{K}^{-1}\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})_{0,\Omega}
is well defined, and taking advantage of the well known regularity results for the Stokes system (see

9
,
28
]
for instance), we have the following additional regularity result for the solution of the source problem (

) and consequently, for the generalized eigenfunctions of

.

Theorem 5
.

There exists
s>0
that for all
\boldsymbol{f}\in\boldsymbol{\mathrm{L}}^{2}(\Omega)
, the solution
(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{u}},\widetilde{p})\in\boldsymbol{\mathrm{H}}_{\Gamma_{1}}(\Omega)\times\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)
of problem (
5
), satisfies for the velocity
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{u}}\in\boldsymbol{\mathrm{H}}^{1+s}(\Omega)
, for the pressure
\widetilde{p}\in\mathrm{H}^{s}(\Omega)
, and

\|\widetilde{\boldsymbol{u}}\|_{1+s,\Omega}+\|\widetilde{p}\|_{s,\Omega}\leq C_{\mathbb{K},\nu}\|\boldsymbol{f}\|_{0,\Omega},

where
C_{\mathbb{K},\nu}>0
is a constant depending on the permeability and the viscosity.

It is important to note that the estimate on Theorem

holds for the source problem. On the other hand, for the eigenfunctions we have that there exists a positive constant
C
depending on the physical parameters and the eigenvalue
\lambda
such that

\|\boldsymbol{u}\|_{1+r,\Omega}+\|p\|_{r,\Omega}\leq C\|\boldsymbol{u}\|_{0,\Omega},

(6)

where

.
Hence, because of the compact inclusion

, we
conclude that
\boldsymbol{T}
is a compact operator and consequently, the following spectral characterization of

Lemma 6
.

(Spectral Characterization of
\boldsymbol{T}
).
The spectrum of
\boldsymbol{T}
is such that
\mathop{\mathrm{sp}}\nolimits(\boldsymbol{T})=\{0\}\cup\{\mu_{k}\}_{k\in{N}}
where
\{\mu_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbf{N}}
is a sequence of real eigenvalues that converge to zero, according to their respective multiplicities.

3
Finite element scheme

In this section our aim is to describe a finite element discretization of problem (
1
). To do this task, we will introduce two families of inf-sup stable finite elements for the Stokes problem which trivially are also valid for the Brinkman load problem. Clearly, the properties of these spaces also hold for the eigenvalue problem. We begin by introducing some preliminary definitions and notations to perform the analysis.

Let
\mathcal{T}_{h}=\{T\}
be a conforming partition of
\overline{\Omega}
into closed simplices
T
with size
h_{T}=\text{diam}(T)
. Define
h:=\max_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}h_{T}
. Let us denote by
\boldsymbol{V}_{h}
and
\mathcal{P}_{h}
the finite element spaces that approximate the velocity field and the pressure, respectively. In particular, the study is focused in the following two elections:

(a)

The mini element
[
8
, Section 4.2.4]
: Here,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{V}_{h}=\{\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\in\boldsymbol{C}(\overline{\Omega})\ :\ \boldsymbol{v}_{h}|_{T}\in[\mathbb{P}_{1}(T)\oplus\mathbb{B}(T)]^{d}\ \forall\ T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}\}\cap\boldsymbol{\mathrm{H}}_{\Gamma_{1}}(\Omega),

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{h}=\{q_{h}\in C(\overline{\Omega})\ :\ q_{h}|_{T}\in\mathbb{P}_{1}(T)\ \forall\ T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}\}\cap\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega),

where
\mathbb{B}(T)
denotes the space spanned by local bubble functions.

(b)

The lowest order Taylor–Hood element
[
8
, Section 4.2.5]
: In this case,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{V}_{h}=\{\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\in\boldsymbol{C}(\overline{\Omega})\ :\ \boldsymbol{v}_{h}|_{T}\in\mathbb{P}_{2}(T)^{d}\ \forall\ T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}\}\cap\boldsymbol{\mathrm{H}}_{\Gamma_{1}}(\Omega),

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{h}=\{q_{h}\in C(\overline{\Omega})\ :\ q_{h}|_{T}\in\mathbb{P}_{1}(T)\ \forall\ T\in\mathcal{T}\}\cap\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega).

Let us remark that from now and on, the discrete analysis will be performed in a general manner, in the sense that indistinctively we are considering both the mini element or the Taylor-Hood family. If some difference must be claimed, we will point it out when is necessary.

With the aforementioned discrete spaces at hand, the discretization of Problem
1
reads as follows:

Problem 7
.

Find
\lambda_{h}\in\mathbb{R}
and
(\boldsymbol{0},0)\neq(\boldsymbol{u}_{h},p_{h})\in\boldsymbol{V}_{h}\times\mathcal{P}_{h}
such that

\left\{\begin{aligned} a(\boldsymbol{u}_{h},\boldsymbol{v}_{h})+b(\boldsymbol{v}_{h},p_{h})&=\lambda_{h}(\boldsymbol{u}_{h},\boldsymbol{v}_{h})_{0,\Omega},&\forall\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\in\boldsymbol{V}_{h},\\
b(\boldsymbol{u}_{h},q_{h})&=0,&\forall q_{h}\in\mathcal{P}_{h}.\end{aligned}\right.

Let
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{K}}_{h}
be the discrete kernel of bilinear form
b(\cdot,\cdot)
defined by

\boldsymbol{\mathcal{K}}_{h}:=\{\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\in\boldsymbol{V}_{h}:b(\boldsymbol{v}_{h},q_{h})=0\quad\forall q_{h}\in\mathcal{P}_{h}\}.

For the elements on
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{K}}_{h}
, bilinear form
a(\cdot,\cdot)
satisfies

\displaystyle a(\boldsymbol{v}_{h},\boldsymbol{v}_{h})

\displaystyle\geq\min\{{\mathbb{K}_{*}},\nu\}\|\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\|_{1,\Omega}^{2}\quad\forall\boldsymbol{v}\in\boldsymbol{\mathcal{K}_{h}}.

On the other hand, since mini element and Taylor-Hood are inf-sup stable spaces, we have the there exists a constant
\widehat{\beta}>0
such that

\sup_{\boldsymbol{0}\neq\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\in\boldsymbol{V}_{h}}\frac{b(\boldsymbol{v}_{h},q_{h})}{\|\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\|_{1,\Omega}}\geq\widehat{\beta}\lVert q_{h}\rVert_{0,\Omega},\qquad\forall q\in\mathcal{P}_{h}.

Let us introduce the discrete solution operators
\boldsymbol{T}_{h}
and
\boldsymbol{B}_{h}
defined as follows

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{T}_{h}

\displaystyle:\boldsymbol{\mathrm{L}}^{2}(\Omega)\rightarrow\boldsymbol{V}_{h},\quad\boldsymbol{f}\mapsto\boldsymbol{T}_{h}\boldsymbol{f}:=\widetilde{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h},

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{B}_{h}

\displaystyle:\boldsymbol{\mathrm{L}}^{2}(\Omega)\rightarrow\mathcal{P}_{h},\,\,\,\boldsymbol{f}\mapsto\boldsymbol{B}_{h}\boldsymbol{f}:=\widetilde{p}_{h},

where the pair
(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h},\widetilde{p}_{h})\in\boldsymbol{V}_{h}\times\mathcal{P}_{h}
is the unique solution of the following discrete source problem

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcll}a(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h},\boldsymbol{v}_{h})+b(\boldsymbol{v}_{h},\widetilde{p}_{h})&=&(\boldsymbol{f},\boldsymbol{v}_{h})_{0,\Omega}&\forall\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\in\boldsymbol{V}_{h},\\
b(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h},q_{h})&=&0&\forall q_{h}\in\mathcal{P}_{h}.\end{array}\right.

Moreover, if
\mathcal{L}_{h}
represents the Lagrange interpolator operator and
\mathcal{P}_{h}
represents the
\mathrm{L}^{2}
-projection, the solutions of the continuous and discrete problems satisfy

{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{u}}-\widetilde{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h},\widetilde{p}-\widetilde{p}_{h})\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}^{2}\leq C\inf_{(\boldsymbol{v},q)\in\boldsymbol{V}_{h}\times\mathcal{P}_{h}}{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{u}}-\boldsymbol{v},\widetilde{p}-q)\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}^{2}\\
\leq C{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{u}}-\mathcal{L}_{h}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{u}},\widetilde{p}-\mathcal{P}_{h}\widetilde{p})\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}^{2}=\|\mathbb{K}^{1/2}(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{u}}-\mathcal{L}_{h}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{u}})\|_{0,\Omega}^{2}+\|\nu^{-1/2}\nabla(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{u}}-\mathcal{L}_{h}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{u}})\|_{0,\Omega}^{2}\\
+\|\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{u}}-\mathcal{L}_{h}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{u}})\|_{0,\Omega}^{2}+\|\widetilde{p}-\mathcal{P}_{h}\widetilde{p}\|_{0,\Omega}^{2}\\
\leq h^{2\min\{s,k\}}\max\{\mathbb{K}^{*},\nu^{*},1\}\left(\|\widetilde{\boldsymbol{u}}\|_{1+s,\Omega}^{2}+\|\widetilde{p}\|_{s,\Omega}^{2}\right).

Then, from Theorem
5
we have

{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{u}}-\widetilde{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h},\widetilde{p}-\widetilde{p}_{h})\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}\leq C_{\mathbb{K},\nu}h^{\min\{s,k\}}\left(\|\widetilde{\boldsymbol{u}}\|_{1+s,\Omega}+\|\widetilde{p}\|_{s,\Omega}\right)\leq C_{\mathbb{K},\nu}h^{\min\{s,k\}}\|\boldsymbol{f}\|_{0,\Omega},

(7)

where
k\in\{1,2\}
depending on whether mini-element or Taylor-Hood family is being used.

Now we have all the necessary ingredients to guarantee the convergence in norm of
\boldsymbol{T}_{h}

Theorem 8
.

Let
\boldsymbol{f}\in\boldsymbol{\mathrm{L}}^{2}(\Omega)

\widetilde{\boldsymbol{u}}:=\boldsymbol{T}\boldsymbol{f}
,
\widetilde{p}:=\boldsymbol{B}\boldsymbol{f}

\|(\boldsymbol{T}-\boldsymbol{T}_{h})\boldsymbol{f}\|_{1,\Omega}\leq C_{\mathbb{K},\nu}h^{\min\{s,k\}}\|\boldsymbol{f}\|_{0,\Omega},

(8)

and

\|(\boldsymbol{T}-\boldsymbol{T}_{h})\boldsymbol{f}\|_{0,\Omega}\leq C_{\mathbb{K},\nu}h^{2{\min\{s,k\}}}\|\boldsymbol{f}\|_{0,\Omega},

7
) and the regularity of Theorem

\left\{\begin{aligned} a(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{\varphi})+b(\boldsymbol{v},\psi)&=(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{u}}-\widetilde{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h},\boldsymbol{v})_{0,\Omega},&\qquad\forall\boldsymbol{v}\in\boldsymbol{\mathrm{H}}_{\Gamma_{1}}(\Omega),\\

Clearly (

\|\boldsymbol{\varphi}\|_{1+s,\Omega}+\|\psi\|_{s,\Omega}\leq C_{\mathbb{K},\nu}\|\widetilde{\boldsymbol{u}}-\widetilde{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h}\|_{0,\Omega}.

On the other hand, simple manipulations yield to the following identity

\|\widetilde{\boldsymbol{u}}-\widetilde{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h}\|_{0,\Omega}^{2}=a(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{u}}-\widetilde{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h},\boldsymbol{\varphi}-\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{h})+b(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{u}}-\widetilde{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h},\psi-\psi_{h})+a(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{u}}-\widetilde{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h},\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{h})+b(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{u}}-\widetilde{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h},\psi_{h})\\

where for the last equality we have used that

. Hence, taking
\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{h}
as the Scott-Zhang interpolant of

,
\psi_{h}
as the
\mathrm{L}^{2}
-orthogonal projection of
\psi
onto

\mathbb{P}_{1}
, applying Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, approximation properties of the interpolator and the projector, estimate (

\|(\boldsymbol{T}-\boldsymbol{T}_{h})\boldsymbol{f}\|_{0,\Omega}=\|\widetilde{\boldsymbol{u}}-\widetilde{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h}\|_{0,\Omega}\leq C_{\mathbb{K},\nu}h^{2\min\{s,k\}}\|\boldsymbol{f}\|_{0,\Omega},

where

, depending on whether mini-element or Taylor-Hood family is being used.
This concludes the proof

4
A posteriori analysis

In what follows, we focus our attention on an a posteriori error analysis. With this goal in mind, and by means of the finite elements previously introduced for the a priori analysis, we introduce a residual-base estimator which results to be fully computable. Let us remark that, for the forthcoming analysis, we consider eigenvalues with simple multiplicity, and their associated eigenfunctions. Lets us begin with some definitions. For any element
T\in{\mathcal{T}}_{h}
, we denote by
\mathcal{E}_{T}
the set of faces/edges of
T

and

\mathcal{E}_{h}:=\bigcup_{T\in{\mathcal{T}}_{h}}\mathcal{E}_{T}.

We consider the decomposition
\mathcal{E}_{h}:=\mathcal{E}_{\Omega}\cup\mathcal{E}_{\partial\Omega}
,
where
\mathcal{E}_{\partial\Omega}:=\{\ell\in\mathcal{E}_{h}:\ell\subset\partial\Omega\}

and
\mathcal{E}_{\Omega}:=\mathcal{E}\backslash\mathcal{E}_{\partial\Omega}
.
For each inner face/edge
\ell\in\mathcal{E}_{\Omega}
and for any sufficiently smooth tensor

\boldsymbol{\tau}
, we define the jump on
\ell
by
\llbracket\boldsymbol{\tau}\rrbracket:=\boldsymbol{\tau}|_{T}\boldsymbol{n}_{T}+\boldsymbol{\tau}|_{T^{\prime}}\boldsymbol{n}_{T^{\prime}},

where
T
and
T^{\prime}
are the two elements in
{\mathcal{T}}_{h}
sharing the
face/edge
\ell
and
\boldsymbol{n}_{T}
and
\boldsymbol{n}_{T^{\prime}}
are the respective outer unit normal vectors.

4.1
Local and global indicators

For an element
T\in{\mathcal{T}}_{h}
we introduce the following local error indicator:

\eta_{T}^{2}:=h_{T}^{2}\|\lambda_{h}\boldsymbol{u}_{h}+\nu\Delta\boldsymbol{u}_{h}-\mathbb{K}^{-1}\boldsymbol{u}_{h}-\nabla p_{h}\|_{0,T}^{2}\\
+\|\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{u}_{h}\|_{0,T}^{2}+\frac{h_{e}}{2}\sum_{e\in{\mathcal{T}}_{h}}\|\llbracket(\nu\nabla\boldsymbol{u}_{h}-p_{h}\mathbb{I})\boldsymbol{n}\rrbracket\|_{0,e}^{2},

and the corresponding global estimator

\eta:=\left(\sum_{T\in{\mathcal{T}}_{h}}\eta_{T}^{2}\right)^{1/2}.

(15)

We define the errors between the continuous and discrete eigenfunctions by

\texttt{e}_{\boldsymbol{u}}:=\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{h},\quad\texttt{e}_{p}:=p-p_{h}.

Now, our task is to demonstrate that our estimator is reliable and efficient. Let us begin with the reliability analysis.

4.2
Reliability

The goal of this section is to derive a global reliability bound for our proposed estimator defined in (
15
). This is contained in the following result.

Theorem 13
.

There exists a constant
C>0
independent of
h
such that

{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|({\boldsymbol{u}}-{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h},{p}-{p}_{h})\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}\leq C\left(\eta+\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{\nu}}|\lambda-\lambda_{h}|+\dfrac{|\lambda_{h}|}{\sqrt{\nu}}\|\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{h}\|_{0,\Omega}\right).

Proof.

Let
(\lambda,(\boldsymbol{u},p))
be the solution of Problem
1
and
(\lambda_{h},(\boldsymbol{u}_{h},p_{h}))
be its finite element approximation given as the solution of Problem
7
. Then we have:

\mathcal{A}((\texttt{e}_{\boldsymbol{u}},\texttt{e}_{p}),(\boldsymbol{v}_{h},0))=(\lambda\boldsymbol{u}-\lambda_{h}\boldsymbol{u}_{h},\boldsymbol{v}_{h})_{0,\Omega},

where
\boldsymbol{v}_{h}
is the Scott-Zhang interpolant of
\boldsymbol{v}
.
Let
{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|(\boldsymbol{v},q)\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}\leq C_{1}{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|(\texttt{e}_{\boldsymbol{u}},\texttt{e}_{p})\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}
where
C_{1}>0
is the constant involved in Lemma
4
. Then, from the previous estimate and using integration by parts, it follows that

C_{2}{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|(\texttt{e}_{\boldsymbol{u}},\texttt{e}_{p})\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}^{2}\leq\mathcal{A}((\texttt{e}_{\boldsymbol{u}},\texttt{e}_{p}),(\boldsymbol{v},q))=\mathcal{A}((\texttt{e}_{\boldsymbol{u}},\texttt{e}_{p}),(\boldsymbol{v}-\boldsymbol{v}_{h},q))+\mathcal{A}((\texttt{e}_{\boldsymbol{u}},\texttt{e}_{p}),(\boldsymbol{v}_{h},0))\\
=\lambda(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})_{0,\Omega}-\lambda_{h}(\boldsymbol{u}_{h},\boldsymbol{v}_{h})_{0,\Omega}-\mathcal{A}((\boldsymbol{u}_{h},p_{h}),(\boldsymbol{v}-\boldsymbol{v}_{h},q))\\
=\lambda_{h}(\boldsymbol{u}_{h},\boldsymbol{v}_{h}-\boldsymbol{v})_{0,\Omega}+(\lambda-\lambda_{h})(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})_{0,\Omega}+\lambda_{h}(\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{h},\boldsymbol{v})_{0,\Omega}-\mathcal{A}((\boldsymbol{u}_{h},p_{h}),(\boldsymbol{v}-\boldsymbol{v}_{h},q))\\
=(\lambda-\lambda_{h})(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})_{0,\Omega}+\lambda_{h}(\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{h},\boldsymbol{v})_{0,\Omega}+\int_{\Omega}\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{u}_{h}q+\lambda_{h}\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{u}_{h}\cdot(\boldsymbol{v}_{h}-\boldsymbol{v})\\
+\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\left(\int_{T}\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits(\boldsymbol{v}-\boldsymbol{v}_{h})p_{h}-\int_{T}\mathbb{K}^{-1}\boldsymbol{u}_{h}\cdot(\boldsymbol{v}-\boldsymbol{v}_{h})-\nu\int_{T}\nabla\boldsymbol{u}_{h}:\nabla(\boldsymbol{v}-\boldsymbol{v}_{h})\right)\\
=(\lambda-\lambda_{h})(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})_{0,\Omega}+\lambda_{h}(\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{h},\boldsymbol{v})_{0,\Omega}+\int_{\Omega}\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{u}_{h}q\\
+\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\left(\int_{T}\left(\lambda_{h}\boldsymbol{u}_{h}+\nu\Delta\boldsymbol{u}_{h}-\mathbb{K}^{-1}\boldsymbol{u}_{h}-\nabla p_{h}\right)\cdot(\boldsymbol{v}-\boldsymbol{v}_{h})\right)\\
-\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\int_{\partial T}\left(\nu\nabla\boldsymbol{u}_{h}-p_{h}\mathbb{I}\right)\boldsymbol{n}\cdot(\boldsymbol{v}-\boldsymbol{v}_{h})\\
\leq C\left(\eta+\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{\nu}}|\lambda-\lambda_{h}|+\dfrac{|\lambda_{h}|}{\sqrt{\nu}}\|\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}_{h}\|_{0,\Omega}\right){\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|({\boldsymbol{v}},{q})\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|},

where for the last estimate we have used the approximation properties of the Scott-Zhang interpolator.

{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|(\boldsymbol{v},q)\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}\leq C_{1}{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|(\texttt{e}_{\boldsymbol{u}},\texttt{e}_{p})\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}

5
Numerical experiments

This section is dedicated to conducting various numerical experiments to assess the performance of the scheme across different geometries and physical configurations. The implementations are using the DOLFINx software
[
3
,
29
]
, where the SLEPc eigensolver
[
14
]
and the MUMPS linear solver are employed to solve the resulting generalized eigenvalue problem. Meshes are generated using GMSH
[
12
]
and the built-in generic meshes provided by DOLFINx. The convergence rates for each eigenvalue are determined using least-square fitting and highly refined meshes.

An important fact of the proposed formulation is that it relies on the use of well-known inf-sup stable elements. Moreover, as the permeability parameter
\mathbb{K}
ranges in bounded parameters throughout the domain, the computational complexity remains the same as a usual Stokes eigenvalue problem with the same elements (see for example
[
20
, Section 5.4]
for a benchmark).

In what follows, we denote the mesh resolution by
N
, which is connected to the mesh-size
h
through the relation
h\sim N^{-1}
. We also denote the number of degrees of freedom by
dof
. The relation between
dof
and the mesh size is given by
h\sim\texttt{dof}^{-1/d}
, with
d\in\{2,3\}
.

Let us define
\texttt{err}(\lambda_{i})
as the error on the
i
-th eigenvalue, with

\texttt{err}(\lambda_{i}):={|\lambda_{h,i}-\lambda_{i}|},

where
\lambda_{i}
is the extrapolated value. Similarly, the effectivity indexes with respect to
\eta
and the eigenvalue
\lambda_{h,i}
is defined by

\texttt{eff}(\lambda_{i}):=\frac{\texttt{err}(\lambda_{i})}{\eta^{2}}.

In order to apply the adaptive finite element method, we shall generate a sequence of nested conforming triangulations using the loop

solve
\rightarrow
estimate
\rightarrow
mark
\rightarrow
refine,

based on
[
33
]
:

1.

Set an initial mesh
{\mathcal{T}}_{h}
.

2.

Solve Problem (
7
) in the actual mesh to obtain
\lambda_{h,i}
and
(\boldsymbol{u}_{h},p_{h})
.

3.

Compute
\eta_{T}
for each
T\in{\mathcal{T}}_{h}
using the eigenfunctions
(\boldsymbol{u}_{h},p_{h})
.

4.

Use the maximum marking strategy to refine each
T^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{T}}_{h}
whose indicator
\eta_{T^{\prime}}
satisfies

\eta_{T^{\prime}}\geq 0.5\max\{\eta_{T}\,:\,T\in{\mathcal{T}}_{h}\},

5.

Set
{\mathcal{T}}_{h}
as the actual mesh and go to step 2.

The refinement algorithm is the one implemented by DOLFINx through the command
refine
, which implements Plaza and Carey’s algorithms for 2D and 3D geometries. The algorithms use local refinement of simplicial grids based on the skeleton with local facet marking information.

It is important to note that although theoretically we have assumed that the permeability tensor is positive definite, in the numerical experiments we will assume that in some parts of the domain it is
\boldsymbol{0}
. Consequently, the behavior of the eigenfunctions in these regions aligns with that of the Stokes problem.

For simplicity, we have taken
\nu=1
in all the experiments. We will consider several definitions for
\mathbb{K}
in order to study the physical behavior of the eigenvalues, as well as their convergence rate. The division of
\Omega
into subdomains is such that there is mesh conformity between the regions, i.e, the subregions are delimited exactly by the facets of the domain. By the nature of the problem, the choice of a porous subdomain is known to be very general. Therefore, the experiments below will be focused on basic cases in order to provide a study on the behavior of eigenfunctions on porous media that can be compared in some sense with the existing literature. For full Dirichlet boundary conditions (
\Gamma_{2}=\emptyset
), we have that
p_{h}\in P_{h}^{0}
, with

\mathcal{P}_{h}^{0}=\{q_{h}\in C(\overline{\Omega})\ :\ q_{h}|_{T}\in\mathbb{P}_{1}(T)\ \forall\ T\in\mathcal{T}\}\cap\mathrm{L}_{0}^{2}(\Omega),

which means that the condition
\int_{\Omega}p_{h}=0
must be satisfied. The theory with the spaces
\mathrm{L}_{0}^{2}(\Omega)
and
\mathcal{P}_{h}^{0}
is exactly the same as in Sections
2
–
3
. The zero integral mean condition is implemented in Problem (
7
) by introducing a real Lagrange multiplier. This is achieved by using the
scifem
package along FEniCSx.

5.1
Square domain with internal porous region

In this experiment we consider the unit square with boundary conditions

\Omega:=(0,1)^{2}

\kappa=10^{j},j=-8,-7,...,-1,0,1,2,...,7,8

5.2
2D Lshaped porous domain with mixed boundary conditions

In this experiment we aim to test the scheme when solving the eigenvalue problem on a two-dimensional domain with a singularity and with different permeability zones. To this end we consider the Lshape domain given by
\Omega:=(0,1)^{2}\backslash\left((0.5,0)\times(1,0.5)\right)
with chessboard-like configuration for
\Omega_{S}
and
\Omega_{D}

do-nothing
boundary conditions are assumed on
\Gamma_{1}
and
\Gamma_{2}
, respectively. We take
\mathbb{K}
such that
\mathbb{K}^{-1}=\boldsymbol{0}
on
\Omega_{S}

\mathbb{K}^{-1}=10^{3}\mathbb{I}
on
\Omega_{D}
.

Convergence results for uniform refinements in the first four eigenvalues are given in Table
4
. Because of the singularity, we expect that the first eigenvalue converge with suboptimal rate. However, we also note that the fourth eigenvalue is singular. The second and third eigenvalues are regular up to
\mathcal{O}(h^{3.5})
approximately. Streamlines of the velocity and surface plots of pressure for the computed eigenfunctions are portrayed in Figure
7

Focusing our attention on the two singular eigenmodes. Note that high pressure gradients are present on Figure
7
. When performing adaptive refinements, we present the error and efficiency results, for mini and Taylor-Hood elements, on Figure
8
. For mini elements we have performed 15 adaptive iterations, while 20 iterations were considered for Taylor-Hood. Here we observe that optimal rates are attained in comparison with uniform refinements. Also, the estimator stays properly bounded. Some oscilation in the first iterations were observed with Taylor-Hood elements, but keeping the error curves always below of those of mini elements. When observing the adaptive meshes, depicted in Figures
9
-
10
, we note that the estimator detects the singularity, as well as the pressure gradients between Stokes and Darcy subdomains, and refines accordingly. This is clearly observable in the first eigenmode. Also, we note that a considerable lower of elements are marked and refined to achieve the optimal rate
\mathcal{O}(h^{4})
with Taylor-Hood elements in the same number of iterations.

5.3
The 3D Lshaped domain

For this final experiment test we have considered a 3D geometry with heterogeneous media with singularities. The domain is a 3D Lshaped domain defined by

\Omega:=(-1,1)\times(-1,1)\times(-1,0)\backslash\bigg((-1,0)\times(-1,0)\times(-1,0)\bigg).

The domain contains a dihedral singularity along the line
(0,0,z)
, for
z\in[-1,0]
. The subdomain division as well as the initial mesh for uniform and adaptive refinement is depicted in Figure
11
. For simplicity, homogeneous boundary conditions are considered on the whole boundary, thus
\Gamma_{2}=\emptyset
. Because of the nature of the geometry, we focus our attention on the behavior of the first eigenvalue.

Convergence results for almost permeable (
\mathbb{K}^{-1}|_{\Omega_{D}}=10^{-8}\mathbb{I}
) up to mild porous region (
\mathbb{K}^{-1}|_{\Omega_{D}}=10^{2}\mathbb{I}
) are presented in Table
5
. It is observed that there is a suboptimal convergence rate ranging between
\mathcal{O}(h^{1.4})
and
\mathcal{O}(h^{1.6})
for the first eigenvalue, independent of the element used. This behavior is expected because of the dihedral singularity. A visual description of the eigenfunctions is depicted in Figure
12
. Here we note that for
\mathbb{K}^{-1}|_{\Omega_{D}}=10^{2}\mathbb{I}
the eigenmode start to get confined in the cube-shaped Stokes region of the domain, however, the pressure gradients are still high across the singularity. Also, we note that the pressure gradients are more pronounced when we increase the permeability of
\Omega_{D}
, which is expected since we are approaching to the first eigenvalue in a Stokes eigenvalue problem with similar geometry (see for example
[
22
, Section 5]
).

When studying the a posteriori estimator on the three singular modes from Table
5
, we observed that the estimator detects the geometrical singularity, which also contains high pressure gradients, and refines accordingly. With mini elements, an optimal rate
\mathcal{O}(h^{2})\approx\mathcal{O}(\texttt{dof}^{-0.66})
is attained, while
\mathcal{O}^{(}h^{4})\approx\mathcal{O}(\texttt{dof}^{-1.33}
) is obtained with Taylor-Hood elements. By observing Figures
13
–
14
we note that for
\mathbb{K}^{-1}|_{\Omega_{D}}=10^{2}\mathbb{I}
there are less refinements across the domain and more on the singularity. Subtle differences between choosing
10^{-8}\mathbb{I}
and
10^{1}\mathbb{I}
for
\mathbb{K}^{-1}
are observed since the estimator still gets most of the error values from the singularity. Also, similar to the 2D case, Taylor-Hood adaptive refinements mark less elements outside the singularity. The associated error curves and efficiency for the aforementioned results are presented on Figure
15
. It is clear that optimal rates are attained for the two families of elements and the three cases under study. The efficiency of the estimator is also evidenced, with more oscillations when Taylor-Hood elements are used.

6
Conclusions

In this work, we have formulated an eigenvalue problem for fluid flow over a domain with potentially heterogeneous permeability. The model formulation emphasizes on the variability of the permeability parameter
\mathbb{K}
across the domain. This approach has allowed us to show that an eigenvalue problem based on the Brinkman equations can yield valuable insights into the behavior of confined or free fluids within heterogeneous media. While this study does not address all possible configurations and limitations related to the choice of permeability in the spectral analysis, the numerical experiments consistently indicate that fluid flow tends to concentrate within Stokes regions when permeability-limited zones are present, suggesting a natural energy minimization tendency within the system. This observation invites further investigation, as this work represents the first step in that direction. Future studies will expand on this framework by considering aspects such as variable viscosity and the analysis of the coupled Stokes-Darcy eigenvalue problem.
