Mechanism of ECM Stiffness-Integrin-Hippo-ABCG1 axis in regulating ferroptosis and targeted nanodrug intervention in LUAD
Authors: Tingting Liu, Shuo Yu, Lu Zhang, Guangdong Wang, Wenwen Ji, Tingting Li, Tinghua Hu, Zhihong Shi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRecent advances in mechanobiology have established extracellular matrix (ECM) stiffness as a critical biomechanical cue promoting lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) metastasis and therapy resistance through mechano-transduction pathways. Ferroptosis is vital in health and disease, regulating LUAD cell death through iron and lipid peroxidation. How mechanical signals are transduced into the electrochemical determinants that govern ferroptosis remains fundamentally unexplored.MethodsLUAD cells were cultured on polyacrylamide (PA) hydrogel of varying stiffness to mimic physiological and pathological ECM conditions. Transcriptome sequencing (RNA-seq) was employed to identify differential expressed genes (DEGs) across varying ECM stiffness conditions, followed by molecular validation of potential regulatory mechanisms through in vitro experiments, in vivo studies, and clinical samples. Based on this mechanistic insight, we aimed to develop nanocarriers for drug encapsulation and assess their therapeutic efficacy in vitro.ResultsOur study have demonstrated that elevated ECM stiffness suppressed ferroptosis in LUAD cells. Mechanistically, high ECM stiffness elevated integrin levels, suppressed the Hippo pathway, and increased YAP levels, which upregulated the downstream molecule ATP-Binding Cassette G1 (ABCG1), thereby reducing ferroptosis sensitivity. This mechanistic model was rigorously validated through a combination of stepwise molecular knockdown experiments in vitro, supporting animal studies in vivo, and analyses of clinical patient samples. Notably, dihydromyricetin (DMY) downregulated ABCG1 expression and sensitized cells to ferroptosis, which was further enhanced by nanocarrier-mediated drug delivery for improved therapeutic outcome.ConclusionOur findings revealed a novel mechanism by which ECM stiffness regulated ferroptosis via the Integrin-Hippo-ABCG1 pathway in LUAD. Nanodrugs designed to target ABCG1 demonstrate significant potential for future LUAD therapy by resensitizing tumors to ferroptosis (Fig. 1).Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12931-025-03403-6.

Introduction

LUAD is the most frequent subtype of non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), remains a significant challenge in cancer treatment due to its aggressive nature and high mortality rate [1–4]. Among the various therapeutic strategies, chemotherapy has long been a cornerstone in the treatment of LUAD patients [5]. However, the emergence of chemotherapy resistance in cancer cells often limits the efficacy, leading to disease recurrence and poor prognosis [6–8].

In recent years, increasing evidences have highlighted the crucial role of the tumor microenvironment (TME), particularly the ECM in tumor progression and drug resistance [9]. TME facilitates the physical translocation of cancer cells and reprograms their phenotypic traits, thereby enabling them to survive, invade, and ultimately colonize distant tissues [10]. Upon sensing mechanical signals via surface receptors, cells initiate intracellular signaling cascades that critically modulate malignant behaviors in cancer, including proliferation, invasion, and metastatic dissemination [11]. In conclusion, understanding how mechanobiological principles interact with various cancer characteristics may result in the finding and development of new strategies to improve cancer treatment [12].

The ECM, a complex network of proteins and polysaccharides [13], provides structural support to cells and regulates various cellular processes, including proliferation, migration, and differentiation [14, 15]. A key physical property of the ECM, its stiffness, has been shown to influence tumor behavior and treatment response by regulating cellular mechano-transduction [16]. ECM stiffness can regulate cellular mechano-transduction, which involves converting mechanical stimuli into biochemical signals [17, 18]. For example, increased ECM stiffness has been linked to increased activation of signaling pathways like focal adhesion kinase (FAK) and mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK), promoting cell migration and proliferation [19].

Recent studies have revealed that ECM stiffness can modulate the activity of the Hippo pathway, leading to changes in cell behavior [20]. The Hippo pathway, an evolutionarily conserved signaling cascade, plays a pivotal role in regulating cell growth, proliferation, and apoptosis [21, 22]. Hippo pathway is composed of a series of kinases that form a cascade, including mammalian sterile 20-like kinase 1/2 (MST1/2), large tumor suppressor kinase 1/2 (LATS1/2), yes-associated protein (YAP) and transcriptional co-activator with PDZ-binding motif (TAZ) [23]. When Hippo signaling is active, YAP/TAZ are phosphorylated and sequestered in the cytoplasm, preventing their entry into the nucleus and transcription of genes that promote cell proliferation. Conversely, when Hippo signaling is inactive or downregulated, YAP/TAZ accumulate in the nucleus and activate the expression of genes that drive cell growth and proliferation [24].

ABCG1, a member of the ATP-binding cassette transporter family, is known to be involved in multidrug resistance [25]. ABCG1 pumps drugs out of cells, reducing intracellular drug accumulation and thus contributing to drug resistance [26, 27]. Recent study has showed that oxaliplatin resistance is enhanced by saracatinib via upregulation Wnt-ABCG1 signaling in hepatocellular carcinoma [28]. Abnormal expression of ABCG1 affects lipid metabolism and involves in the progression of various diseases, such as atherosclerosis, Alzheimer’s disease and cancers [29–31]. However, the role of the ABCG1-ECM stiffness axis in driving malignant phenotypes remains uninvestigated, despite emerging evidence on mechano-transduction in cancer. Recent advances in oncology research have prioritized the investigation of non-apoptotic cell death modalities, particularly ferroptosis, as a promising strategy to overcome treatment resistance and achieve durable responses in refractory malignancies [32]. Ferroptosis is mechanistically defined as a lytic form of regulated cell death driven by iron-dependent phospholipid peroxidation, culminating in catastrophic plasma membrane permeabilization and subsequent redox imbalance [33]. The intersection of mechanobiology and ferroptosis unveils a dynamic regulatory network where physical forces modulate iron metabolism, lipid peroxidation, and antioxidant defenses. Deciphering these mechanisms holds promise for innovative therapies in oncology, cardiology, and beyond [34].

Nanoparticle-based drug delivery systems (NDDS) have emerged as a revolutionary approach to enhance therapeutic efficacy while minimizing systemic toxicity [35]. By encapsulating drugs within nanoscale carriers, these systems improve drug solubility, prolong circulation time, and enable targeted delivery to diseased tissues. Mesoporous Polydopamine (MPDA) is a versatile nanomaterial synthesized via the oxidative self-polymerization of dopamine under alkaline conditions, forming a biocompatible polydopamine (PDA) matrix with tunable mesopores (pore size: 2–50 nm). MPDA-based systems are widely explored for targeted cancer therapy, leveraging its synergistic drug delivery and photothermal ablation [36, 37].

Based on these findings, we hypothesized that ECM stiffness might regulate ferroptosis in LUAD cells via modulation of the Integrin-Hippo-ABCG1 pathway. In this study, we aimed to elucidate the underlying mechanism of this process and investigate the potential therapeutic implications. DMY@MPDA, designed to target ABCG1, effectively suppresses tumor progression in our models, suggesting its significant potential as a future therapeutic strategy for LUAD.

Materials and methods

Cell culture

LUAD cell lines (A549, H1299, H1975 and LA795) were purchased from Procell Life Science Technology Co, Ltd (Wuhan, China), including cell STR identification reports. All cell lines were cultured in complete medium (RPMI 1640 or DMEM plus 10% serum). Cell lines were cultured in the incubator with 37℃, 5% CO2 environment.

Fabrication of PA hydrogel[38, 39]

Firstly, glass slides were subjected to hydrophilic and hydrophobic treatments. A mixture of 40% acrylamide and 2% bisacrylamide was prepared in a specific ratio. After the mixture was cured through the combined action of ammonium persulfate (APS) and N, N,N’,N’-tetramethylethylenediamine (TEMED), the coupling of collagen to the surface of the PA hydrogel for cell attachment was typically achieved using bifunctional crosslinkers, such as sulfosuccinimidyl-6-[4-(p-azidophenamido) butylamido] hexanoate (sulfo-SANPAH). This process resulted in the preparation of PA hydrogels with varying stiffness. Subsequently, a cell suspension was seeded onto the surface of the hydrogel for stiffness interventions.

Quantitative Real-Time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR)[40]

LUAD cells were obtained from the PA hydrogel by using collagenase according to the manufacturer’s instructions. we first isolated total RNA from the LUAD cell samples using the provided RNA extraction protocol. Then, we synthesized cDNA from the RNA using the reverse transcription reagents as outlined in the manual. For the PCR amplification step, we mixed the cDNA with the PCR reagents according to the recommended ratios and performed the reaction on a thermal cycler with the cycling conditions specified by the manufacturer. Finally, for the qRT-PCR step, we loaded the PCR products into the instrument and monitored the fluorescence signal according to the kit’s protocol. Cycle settings: 1 cycle at 95 ℃ for 3 min, 40 cycles at 95 ℃ for 15 s and 60 ℃ for 1 min. The data analysis was performed using the software provided by the manufacturer to determine the expression levels of our target genes. The primers used in this study include:

Immunofluorescence assay and phalloidin staining[42–44]

Cultured cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) in PBS for 30 min at room temperature, permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100 in PBS for 20 min, blocked with 5% Bovine Serum Albumin (BSA) for 30 min. Apply the diluted primary antibody to the cells and incubate for overnight at 4 °C in a humidified chamber. Dilute the secondary antibody in blocking solution according to the manufacturer’s recommendations. Apply DAPI to the cells and incubate for 5–10 min at room temperature in the dark. Observe the slides under a fluorescence microscope equipped with appropriate filters for the fluorescent dyes used.

Perform Phalloidin staining of cells according to the instructions in the manual. Later, observe the cytoskeletal morphology of the cells under different magnifications of a fluorescence microscope.

Si-RNAs were transfected into LUAD cells by jetPRIME transfection reagent (Polyplus). qRT-PCR and western blotting were used to validate the efficacy of a specific knockdown sequence targeting the gene after 24–48 h. Cancer cells with high transfection efficiency were further collected for further functional experiments.

Inhibitor transfection[47–49]

After LUAD cells were cultured on PA hydrogel with a stiffness of 100 kPa for 24 h, 0.5µM YAP1 inhibitor CA3 (CL56) and Cilengitide were added. qRT-PCR experiments were performed 24 h post-treatment, and protein-level analyses were conducted 48 h after treatment.

CCK8 and EDU assays[50]

Successfully transfected cells were seeded on a 96 well plate (1500 LUAD cells in 100 µl cell suspension). Cell viability was measured using CCK8 reagent according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The proliferation ratios (OD 450) at Day 0, 1, 2, 3 were detected using micro-plate reader.

Seed cells into 6-well plates at a suitable density to allow for cell proliferation during the experimental period. Prepare EdU solution by diluting the EdU reagent in complete cell culture medium according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Stain the nuclei of the cells with a DNA-specific fluorophore (Hoechst 33342) to facilitate the identification of individual cells and to provide a reference for quantifying EdU-positive cells.

Wound healing assay[51]

Select LUAD cell lines and culture them under optimal conditions until a confluent monolayer was formed on a 6 well plate. Using a 200ul pipette tip to create a straight or patterned scratch across the cell monolayer. The width and depth of the scratch could be adjusted based on the experimental design. At designated time points (0 h, 24 h, 48 h), we observed the scratch area under a microscope equipped with a camera to capture images of the migrating cells. The cell migration rate was determined by comparing the migration areas between groups.

ROS detection[52]

Seed LUAD cells at an appropriate density in 6-well plates and culture until they reach the logarithmic growth phase. Aspirate the culture medium and discard the supernatant. Add an appropriate concentration of a ROS-sensitive fluorescent probe diluted in serum-free medium to the cells. Incubate the cells for 1 h under standard culture conditions (37 °C, 5%CO₂, protected from light). After incubation, immediately observe the cells under a fluorescence microscope using the appropriate excitation wavelengths.

MDA detection[53]

Log-phase cells were lysed using an appropriate cell lysis buffer. Protein concentration in the lysate was determined using a BCA protein assay kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Prepare the working solution as specified by the MDA assay kit protocol. Mix the working solution with the cell lysate and incubate under designated conditions. Measure the absorbance of the reaction mixture at 532 nm using a microplate reader or spectrophotometer.

GSH measurement[54]

For cellular GSH assay, LUAD cells were seeded on 6 well plates for 24 h. Then, the cells were lysed by Triton-X-100 (200µL, 4%) to collect the lysates and centrifuge. Finally, we collected 50ul of lysate and 100ul of Ellman’s reagent and measured them at 405 nm on an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) reader.

Protocol for lentiviral Transduction[55]

Designed 3 sets of knockdown ABCG1 lentivirus sequences LV-ABCG1-RNAi (P24K-0755, P24K-0756, P24K-0757) and 1 set of control sequences (CON313). Took tumor cells with good growth status from a 24 well plate, transfected the above virus sequences separately, and added virus transfection reagents. After 48 h of intervention, the knockdown efficiency would be verified, followed by amplification of cells with high transfection efficiency.

Subcutaneous tumor formation experiment[56]

Slow virus tumorigenic experiment: Collected stable viral cell suspensions with high knockdown efficiency and control group cells, carefully injected them subcutaneously into the backs of 8 mice, and observed the tumorigenic situation carefully for 1 month. Measured the longest diameter a (mm) and shortest diameter b (mm) of the tumor on days 7, 9, 11, 14, 21, and 28 respectively, calculated the volume(1/2ⅹaⅹb2), and drew the growth curve. After 28 days, dissected the tumor and measured its volume and weight.

GelMA subcutaneous tumor formation: Prepared 5% and 15% GelMA gel, UV cured GelMA microspheres, buried them in the back of mice, carefully sutured the skin tissue, and observed the tumor formation for one month.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

The paraffin section shall be placed at 65℃ for an hour, and then did the gradient alcohol dewaxing treatment. 5 ml EDTA was used to keep the antigen repair. Dripped goat serum blocking solution, incubated at 37℃ for 1 h. Added a drop of freshly prepared color developing solution to each slice to avoid too deep coloring. After the tissue sections were fully dried overnight, used the gun head to carry out neutral resin sealing film to completely cover the lung tissue as far as possible. Observed the staining difference between the experimental group and the control group under the microscope, and saved the results [57].

Transcriptome sequencing

We collected tumor cells cultured in environments with different stiffness levels and performed transcriptome sequencing to identify potential stiffness-related differential genes. Subsequently, we conducted functional enrichment analysis to gain insights into the roles and pathways of these genes [58].

Clinical patients and animals

Cancerous and paired normal lung tissues were obtained from 20 LUAD patients with written informed consent. Animal experiments used 4–6 weeks C57BL/6 mice under protocols approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC).

Synthesis of MPDA nanoparticles

MPDA nanoparticles were synthesized via a template-directed method. Briefly, the block copolymer F127 was dissolved in an aqueous Tris buffer (pH ~ 8.5) under mild stirring. Dopamine hydrochloride was then introduced into the solution, initiating its polymerization at room temperature. The resulting brown-to-black solution was allowed to react for several hours. Finally, the MPDA nanoparticles were collected by centrifugation and washed, followed by template removal through dialysis or ethanol extraction to obtain the mesoporous product [59].

Statistical analysis

GraphPad Prism7.0, SPSS22.0 and EXCEL tables were used for statistical analysis in this study. In order to quantify the results of Western Blotting experiment, Image J semi quantitative software was used for gray value analysis, and the target gene was compared with the internal reference ratio. If the data between the two groups conformed to the normal distribution, we used the t-test statistics of independent samples. Non parametric test was used to calculate non normal distribution data and single factor analysis of variance was used to compare data of multiple experimental groups. The statistical test used a two-sided test. If the P value was less than 0.05, it meant there was a systematic difference (*P < 0.05; **P < 0.01; ***P < 0.001; ****P < 0.0001).

Similarly, Western blotting also demonstrated higher levels of collagen expression in tumor cells cultured in high-stiffness environment, and after statistical analysis of multiple data sets, significant differences were observed (Fig. 2P-Q). High mechanical stiffness conferred decreased sensitivity to ferroptosis in tumor cells

Fluorescence microscopy revealed lower ROS levels in tumor cells cultured under high-stiffness conditions. Compared with low-stiffness conditions, tumor cells in high-stiffness environments exhibited significantly lower MDA levels (P < 0.05). Western blotting analysis demonstrated that tumor cells in high-stiffness conditions exhibited upregulated expression of ferroptosis-related molecules (Nrf2, SLC7A11, and GPX4), correlating with reduced ferroptosis levels. In summary, our results demonstrate that high ECM stiffness confers reduced susceptibility to ferroptosis, establishing a direct mechanistic link between biomechanics and this cell death pathway (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Subcutaneous tumor formation assay using GelMA hydrogel implantation

Supplementary Fig. 2 illustrated the GelMA hydrogel curing process and subcutaneous implantation procedure. Comparative tumor growth in 5% versus 15% GelMA hydrogels after 30 days in vivo Histological analysis demonstrated characteristic carcinoma nest formation and prominent vascularization in tumors grown within 15% GelMA hydrogels. Western blotting analysis of tumor tissues revealed upregulated expression of ferroptosis-related proteins (SLC7A11 and GPX4) in 15% GelMA-grown tumors, correlating with reduced ferroptosis levels. IHC analysis demonstrated that high stiffness upregulated YAP1 and ABCG1 expression levels. IHC analysis comparison between LA795 subcutaneous tumors and normal lung tissue revealed significant upregulation of YAP1, ABCG1, Nrf2, SLC7A11, and GPX4 in tumor tissues (Supplementary Fig. 2). These in vivo results were consistent with our in vitro findings, demonstrating that high matrix stiffness reduces cellular susceptibility to ferroptosis.

Discussion

The interplay between ECM stiffness, integrin signaling, and the Hippo-ABCG1 axis represented a novel biomechanical-electrochemical coupling mechanism that governed ferroptosis susceptibility in LUAD. Our findings demonstrated that ECM stiffening, a hallmark of fibrotic and advanced tumors, activated integrin-FAK mechano-transduction, leading to YAP/TAZ nuclear translocation and subsequent upregulation of ABCG1, a key ferroptosis suppressor. This mechanosensitive pathway established a feedforward loop where ECM stiffness not only sustained tumor cell survival but also reprogramed redox homeostasis by modulating lipid peroxidation resistance. Critically, we identified ABCG1 as a druggable node within this pathway. The nano-agent DMY@MPDA, designed to target ABCG1, effectively disrupted this protective mechanism, thereby sensitizing LUAD cells to ferroptosis and achieving significant therapeutic efficacy.

ECM stiffness and cancer progression

ECM stiffness has emerged as a critical factor in cancer progression, influencing cell proliferation, migration, and invasion [60–63]. This alteration in ECM mechanics can be attributed to various factors, including the activation of myofibroblasts, which deposit excessive amounts of ECM proteins such as collagen and fibronectin. The increased ECM stiffness creates a physical barrier that can impede the infiltration of immune cells into the tumor core, thereby promoting immune evasion and tumor growth. Our results were consistent with previous studies demonstrating that ECM stiffness can activate signaling pathways that promote cancer cell survival and proliferation. To investigate the role of matrix stiffness, we cultured LUAD cells on PA hydrogels with varying stiffness. Our results demonstrated that high stiffness promoted cell growth, as evidenced by enhanced proliferative capacity and reduced apoptosis at the molecular level. These findings were consistently validated across multiple cell lines. Furthermore, in vivo experiments corroborated these results, showing that tumors embedded in 15% GelMA hydrogels exhibited significantly faster growth rates compared to those in 5% GelMA. Our above experimental results were consistent with current researches.

Ferroptosis: A novel therapeutic target in lung cancer

Ferroptosis is a unique form of regulated cell death triggered by iron-dependent lipid peroxide accumulation, distinct from caspase-mediated apoptosis. Its role in cancer biology has spurred interest in its therapeutic targeting. Our study significantly extended this paradigm by revealing that ECM stiffness was a critical determinant of ferroptotic susceptibility in LUAD. Specifically, we linked mechanical cues to the core ferroptosis machinery by showing that stiffness-activated YAP/TAZ signaling upregulates the ferroptosis suppressor ABCG1. This mechanism not only promoted tumor survival but also established a form of “mechanical immunity” against this cell death pathway, highlighting the need for co-targeting the biomechanical niche to unlock the full potential of ferroptosis-based therapies. By targeting the biomechanical regulation of cell death, our findings on mechano-mediated ferroptosis establish a novel and promising therapeutic modality for LUAD.

Integrin-Hippo-ABCG1 axis: A key regulatory pathway

The Integrin-Hippo [64–66]-ABCG1 axis represents a critical signaling pathway that links ECM stiffness to ferroptosis regulation. Integrins are transmembrane receptors that mediate cell-ECM interactions and transmit mechanical signals from the ECM to the cell interior. Upon activation by ECM stiffness, integrins recruit and activate a series of intracellular signaling molecules, including FAK and Rho GTPases. These signaling molecules then influence the Hippo pathway, a conserved signaling cascade that regulates cell growth, organ size, and tissue homeostasis.

ABCG1, a member of the ATP-binding cassette (ABC) transporter family, is one of the target genes regulated by the YAP/TAZ-TEAD complex [67, 68]. ABCG1 plays a crucial role in cellular cholesterol homeostasis and has been implicated in cancer progression and drug resistance. To this end, we employed a multi-tiered inhibitory approach to delineate the causal relationships within this pathway. By systematically inhibiting integrin, YAP1, and ABCG1, we demonstrated that the suppression of any upstream component (integrin or YAP1) effectively phenocopied the ferroptosis-sensitizing effect of direct ABCG1 inhibition, thereby confirming their positions in a linear signaling axis that governs ferroptosis susceptibility. This molecular mechanism was validated in both mouse models and clinical patient samples. Furthermore, the identification of the ECM stiffness-Integrin-Hippo-ABCG1 axis unveils a novel regulatory mechanism for ferroptosis in LUAD, positioning it as an attractive therapeutic target. This is particularly relevant for fibrotic, stiff tumors that rely on this pathway for survival. By pharmacologically targeting this signaling cascade, it becomes possible to devise innovative treatment strategies aimed at enhancing ferroptosis sensitivity. Such approaches, potentially used in combination with conventional therapies, hold significant promise for improving treatment outcomes for LUAD patients, especially those with advanced, therapy-resistant disease.

Discussion on DMY@MPDA as a targeted nanotherapeutic

Our study not only delineated the pivotal role of the ECM stiffness-Integrin-Hippo-ABCG1 axis in conferring ferroptosis resistance but also provided a compelling therapeutic solution by developing the nano-agent DMY@MPDA. The rational design of DMY@MPDA to specifically target ABCG1 represented a strategic intervention at a critical node within this mechano-signaling pathway. Our in vitro and in vivo data consistently demonstrated that DMY@MPDA effectively suppressed ABCG1 expression, thereby dismantling the protective shield that shielded LUAD cells from ferroptotic death. The subsequent cascade, including the accumulation of lipid peroxides and the sensitization of tumor cells to ferroptosis, validates ABCG1 as a druggable target and confirms the nano-formulation’s potent on-target efficacy. The significant tumor suppression observed in our mouse model underscored the high translational potential of this approach. Importantly, by directly targeting a downstream effector of biomechanical signaling, DMY@MPDA offers a promising strategy to counteract the pro-tumorigenic and therapy-resistant environment created by a stiff ECM. This positions DMY@MPDA not merely as a cytotoxic agent, but as a “mechano-modulating” therapy that can selectively eradicate tumors thriving in stiff, fibrotic niches. Future efforts should focus on optimizing its delivery and evaluating its efficacy in combination with standard-of-care treatments to fully realize its clinical potential for LUAD patients.

Implications for future research and clinical applications

The findings of this study have several important implications for future research and clinical applications in the field of LUAD. The Integrin-Hippo-ABCG1 axis provides a potential therapeutic target for modulating ferroptosis in LUAD. By developing small-molecule inhibitors or agonists that can specifically target this signaling pathway, it may be possible to selectively induce ferroptosis in LUAD cells while sparing normal cells. In our study, the nano-formulation DMY@MPDA demonstrated significant efficacy in vitro. Subsequent in vivo validation confirmed its potent antitumor effects, laying a foundation for clinical translation in LUAD patients.

Despite the compelling evidence presented, this study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, while we have established a coherent signaling cascade from ECM stiffness to ABCG1-mediated ferroptosis resistance, the precise molecular intermediaries linking Integrin/FAK activation to the core Hippo kinase cascade (LATS1/2) remain to be fully elucidated. A more detailed mechanistic dissection is required to map this connection unequivocally. Second, the translational relevance of our findings is constrained by the inherent limitations of the animal models used. Although our in vivo data are robust, subcutaneous xenograft models may not fully recapitulate the complex tumor microenvironment and the spatial heterogeneity of ECM stiffness found in human LUAD. Future investigations employing genetically engineered mouse models or patient-derived organoids would be valuable to validate this axis in a more clinically relevant context. Finally, regarding our therapeutic candidate DMY@MPDA, its precise molecular target and binding affinity, while evidently impacting ABCG1, were not exhaustively characterized. A comprehensive understanding of its pharmacokinetics, biodistribution, and potential long-term off-target effects is essential before clinical translation can be pursued.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our study elucidates a novel biomechanical pathway in which ECM stiffness orchestrates ferroptosis resistance in LUAD via the Integrin-Hippo-ABCG1 axis. This discovery not only provides a mechanistic link between the tumor’s physical microenvironment and its electrochemical resilience but also opens promising therapeutic avenues. Our findings posit that targeting key nodes along this axis ECM remodeling, Integrin-Hippo signaling, or ABCG1 expression, represents a viable strategy to re-sensitize tumors to ferroptosis. Furthermore, the efficacy of our engineered nanodrug, DMY@MPDA, serves as a proof-of-concept, highlighting its significant potential for clinical translation and underscoring the promise of targeting this pathway to improve LUAD treatment outcomes.
