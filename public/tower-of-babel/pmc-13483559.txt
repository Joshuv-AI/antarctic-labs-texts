Single-cell RNA sequencing reveals chemotherapy-induced apoptosis in acute myeloid leukemia via B cell depletion and M2 to M1 macrophage repolarization
Authors: Di Zhou, Xue Bai, Chenchen Wang, Lintao Bi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeThis study aimed to investigate the dynamic changes of immune microenvironment in acute myeloid leukemia (AML) during chemotherapy, thereby, exploring the mechanism of chemotherapy resistance of AML.MethodsSingle cell RNA sequencing (scRNAseq) was performed on 6 bone marrow samples from AML before or after chemotherapy (BC, AC) to obtain the fastq files. Bioinformatics analysis including GSEA, GSVA, pseudotime trajectory and cell chat analysis was used for constructing single-cell transcriptome and observing the dynamic changes of immune microenvironment. Western blot was carried out for validation.ResultsA total of 11 cell subpopulations were obtained, among which B cells, HSCs, and TAMs were the significant changed between BC and AC groups. MCM7 gene was highly expressed in malignant cells, co-expressed with CD34 + pre B cells in BC groups, and decreased in AC group. The SPINK2 + B cells activated JAK-STAT pathway. Furthermore, we found that chemotherapy driven the transition for M2 to M1 in TAMs polarization.ConclusionsMCM7 and SPINK2 were the targeted genes in AML during chemotherapy, which help for providing a theoretical basis for combined immunotherapy/targeted-therapy strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40246-026-01001-1.

Introduction

Acute myeloid leukemia (AML) is a malignant hematologic disorder characterized by clonal proliferation and impaired differentiation of myeloid progenitor cells in the bone marrow [1]. In the US, AML has an age-adjusted incidence of 4.3 cases per 100,000 individuals, which demonstrated a strong age-dependent pattern [2]. Besides, AML carries a particularly poor prognosis, with a 5-year survival rate of only 24% [3].

Despite the emergence of novel therapeutic agents, conventional intensive chemotherapy remains the cornerstone of AML treatment [4]. Widely used regimens included Venetoclax + Azacitidine/Decitabine, Gilteritinib (Xospata) + Venetoclax, and Gemtuzumab Ozogamicin [5–8]. However, chemotherapy in AML is usually associated with severe complications, including life-threatening infections, anemia, and thrombocytopenia, primarily due to bone marrow suppression. Growing evidence suggested that alterations in the immune microenvironment play a pivotal role in determining chemotherapy efficacy [9, 10]. The tumor microenvironment not only influences leukemic cell proliferation and differentiation but also contributes to immune evasion and chemotherapy resistance [11]. Given these insights, a deeper understanding of the dynamic remodeling of the immune microenvironment during chemotherapy is essential for unraveling the mechanisms underlying treatment resistance and disease relapse.

Single cell RNA sequencing (scRNAseq) analysis revealed the cell types, signaling pathways, and cell-cell interactions that drive malignant cell progression and therapeutic response, thereby fundamentally altering the cancer process [12]. The dynamic changes in characterizing cellular and molecular features are crucial for identifying new targets in cancer diagnosis, risk stratification, and clinical outcome prediction. Granja et al. [13] indicated that despite widespread epigenetic heterogeneity within the patient cohort, common malignant signatures across patients were observed. Besides, previous researches revealed significant differences have been demonstrated between adult and pediatric AML microenvironments [14, 15]. However, the above studies only explored the cellular heterogeneity and immune microenvironment changes in the progression of AML, which did not focus on dynamic changes in the immune microenvironment before and after chemotherapy. Notably, exploration of immune microenvironment and potential therapeutic targets among AML patients before and after chemotherapy helps investigate the application of combination therapy with chemotherapy in the treatment of AML.

Therefore, this study performed scRNAseq on bone marrow samples of AML before or after chemotherapy to systematically characterize the dynamic remodeling of the immune microenvironment in AML. Our study aimed to provide fundamental insights into the tumor-immune ecosystem dynamics during AML treatment through comprehensive single-cell analysis of immune cell states, intercellular communication networks, and pathway activities.

Materials and methods

Collection of bone marrow samples of AML patients

A total of twelve bone marrow samples were obtained from AML patients at China-Japan Union Hospital of Jilin University and divided into two cohorts based on experimental purposes. Six of these samples served as the sequencing cohort for single-cell sequencing and included three patients in the post-chemotherapy recovery phase (after chemotherapy, AC) as well as three newly diagnosed AML patients who had not yet received chemotherapy (before chemotherapy, BC). The other six samples formed the validation cohort for experimental validation and consisted of another three BC-phase AML patients and three AC-phase AML patients. A schematic overview of this sample grouping and the experimental workflow was presented in Figure S1. All patients provided the written informed consent that was approved by Institutional Review Board (IRB) and certified that the study was performed in accordance with the ethical standards as laid down in the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki. This study obtained the approval from the Ethics Committee of China-Japan Union Hospital of Jilin University (approval number: 2025052914).

Single-cell RNA sequencing

The scRNAseq libraries were constructed from bone marrow samples that had been successfully revived. This was achieved by employing anti-human antibodies from Biolegend, along with Chromium single-cell 3’v3 reagent kits provided by 10x Genomics. Sequencing operations were carried out using NextSeq 500 high output in combination with Novaseq S4 kits from Illumina. Subsequently, the obtained fastq files were processed with Cell Ranger version 7.0.0 [16]. This software was utilized for demultiplexing, aligning the sequences to the human genome (hg38), and generating gene-count matrices, which were then used for subsequent bioinformatics analysis.

Data pre-processing

The scRNAseq data was processed using Seurat package (version 4.4.0) [17] in R software to obtained single cell gene expression profile. Using all cells obtained from scRNAseq, low quality cells (the minimum expressed cells number ≥ 10, and genes number ≥ 300) we standardization of scRNAseq data. Besides, ‘FindVariableFeatures’ function was used to identify the 5000 most variable genes. PCA analysis was performed on data using ‘RunPCA’function. Furthermore, harmony package (version 1.2.3) [16] was used for removing batch effects among different bone marrow samples with AML.

Clustering and annotation of subpopulation of cells

‘FindCluster’ function in Seurat package (version 4.4.0) was applied for clustering of cells and subpopulation [17]. Then, uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) was used to identify and exhibit cells subpopulation. Subsequently, cell subpopulation was named with high-expressed genes and cellmarkers (http://117.50.127.228/CellMarker/index.html).

Calculation on differentially expressed genes

‘FindAllMarkers’ function was carried out to identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs) of each cell subpopulation with threshold of min.pct = 0.25 and logfc.threshold = 0.25.

Gene function enrichment analysis

The Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) analysis were performed on DEGs using Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) website (https://www.gsea-msigdb.org/gsea/index.jsp) to reveal the related signaling pathway in the development of AML. The cutoff was set to P < 0.05.

PROGENy pathway activity analysis

The pathway activity of 14 types of signaling pathways (including Androgen, Estrogen, Epidermal Growth Factor Receptor (EGFR), Hypoxia, Janus Kinase-Signal Transducer and Activator of Transcription (JAK-STAT), Mitogen-Activated Protein Kinase (MAPK), Nuclear Factor Kappa-B (NF-κB), Phosphatidylinositol 3-Kinase (PI3K)-Akt, p53, Transforming Growth Factor Beta (TGF-β), Tumor Necrosis Factor Alpha (TNF-α), TNF-Related Apoptosis-Inducing Ligand (TRAIL), Vascular Endothelial Growth Factor (VEGF) and WNT signaling pathways) in cells was analyzed using progeny [18] in R package.

Gene set variation analysis

The gene enrichment was performed using msigdbr (version 7.5.1) in R package and Gene Set Variation Analysis (GSVA, version 2.0.4), respectively.

Pseudotime trajectory analysis

Ppseudotime trajectory analysis was performed on each cell subpopulation using Monocle2 (version 2.24.0) [19] in R language. The gene-cell matrix of raw UMI counts is derived from Seurat-processed data. An object for trajectory analysis was generated using ‘newCellDataSet’ function with threshold that an average gene expression value was above 0.1 and expressed in at least 10 cells. The “differentialGeneTest” function was used to identify DEGs across cell subpopulations, retaining only those with q-value < 0.01 for downstream dimensionality reduction. Dimension reduction was performed using the “reduceDimension” function with parameters reduction_method="DDRTree” and max_components = 2. Cell trajectories were then ordered and visualized using “plot_cell_trajectory”. After that, ‘differentialGeneTest’ function with the model formula fullModelFormulaStr=‘sm.ns(Pseudotime)’ to determine genes dynamically regulated along pseudotime (q-value <1e-4). Subsequently, we employed the branch expression analysis model (BEAM) to investigate gene expression patterns associated with bifurcation events in cell fate decisions.

Cell-cell communication analysis

Cell-cell communication analysis was performed using CellChat (version 1.1.3) [20]. A CellChat object was generated using the createCellChat function. And CellChatDB.mouse was used as ligand-receptor database, from which the ‘Secreted signaling’ ligand-receptor interactions were selected. Next, highly expressed ligand-receptor pairs were determined using the ‘identifyOverExpressedGenes’ and ‘identifyOverExpressedInteractions’, which were then mapped onto a protein-protein interaction network (PPI). Cell-cell communication probabilities were calculated using computeCommunProb, with excluding interactions that were fewer than 10 cells. Pathway-level communication networks were subsequently derived using computeCommunProbPathway. For comprehensive visualization of intercellular communication patterns, we employed netVisual_bubble to generate an integrated view of ligand-receptor interactions across all cell types.

Intercellular crosstalk analysis among cell subpopulations

Cell-cell communication analysis was performed using NicheNet [21] to identify key molecular mediators of fibroblast-macrophage interactions.

Immune infiltration analysis

The relative proportions of 22 immune cells among samples were calculated using CIBERSORT [22]. ‘Estimate’ function (version 1.0.13, https://R-Forge.R-project.org/projects/estimate/) was applied for calculating the ImmuneScore, ESTIMATEScore, StromalScore and TumorPurity of samples.

Western blot

Western blot was carried out as previous description [23]. In brief, cell lysates from every bone marrow sample in BC and AC group (n = 3) were separated via 10% sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and then transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes. The entire PVDF membranes were blocked using a solution of 10% milk in Tris-buffered saline with Tween 20 and incubated with primary antibodies (1:1000 dilution) at 4℃ overnight. Subsequently, the nitrocellulose-bound primary antibodies were incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:5000 dilution). Chemiluminescent signals were detected using the Amersham Imager 680 (GE Healthcare, UK). Protein expression levels were quantified by gray values and analyzed using Image J software (National Institutes of Health, USA). The primary antibodies used were listed in Table S1.

Statistical analysis

The source of single-cell data and sequencing methods were statistically analyzed using R software, and the ggplot package was used to generate stacked plots of immune cell distribution and differential box plots. All experiments in this study were independently repeated at least three times to ensure reproducibility. Unpaired Student’s t-tests were applied for statistical comparisons between the two groups. A P-value < 0.05 was regarded as statistically significant, and calculations were performed using GraphPad Prism 9.

Results

Construction of single-cell transcriptome in AML

All cells in the 6 bone marrow samples of AML were clustered into 11 cell subpopulations after removing batch effects using Harmony method (Figure S2A, 1B). Then, the cell types in all samples were determined using marker genes that were shown in Fig. 1A. Then, the cell type between BC and AC were represented in Fig. 1B, C, which revealed that that the ratio of B cell, hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs), tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), and mast cells in AC samples were significantly decreased when compared with BC samples (Fig. 1D). The proportion of cells in each sample is shown in Figure S3A.

Specifically, the cell populations were identified using marker genes. A total of 8,332 B cells were marked with NEGR1 and SPINK2, 7,522 neutrophils characterized by the high expression of DEFA3 and DEFA4, and 6,016 hematopoietic stem cells positive for MKI67 and TOP2A. Besides, 5,226 TAMs had high expression of LGALS1 and SAMHD1, along with 4,845 mast cells expressing TPSB2 and TPSAB1. Additional populations included 3,401 myeloid cells was highly expressed by S100A12 and CAMP, 1,886 T/NK cells positive for CCL5 and TRBC2, and 975 fibroblasts exhibiting high expression of CXCL12 and COL1A1. Furthermore, we identified 907 macrophages expressing C1QA and C1QB, 886 plasma cells marked by IGHG1 and IGHA1, and endothelial cells were characterized by high expression of FABP4 and DNASE1L3 (Figure S3B, Table S2). In addition, the UMAP reflected the marker expression and distribution of these sub-clusters in the cells atlas (Fig. 1E, F). Furthermore, GSVA analysis was performed on the marker genes to explore the function of cell subpopulations, revealing that hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs) exhibited significant enrichment in glycolysis-related pathways, mast cells showed prominent activation of MYC-targets pathways, B cells were significantly enriched in MYC-targets-V1, MYC-targets-V2, DNA-repair, E2F-targets and G2M-checkpoint pathways. These pathways were closely associated with cell cycle progression, DNA replication and damage repair, as well as cell proliferation and activation, suggesting that B cells may be in a state of high proliferation and metabolic activation before chemotherapy, while TAMs were markedly enriched in PI3K-AKT-mTOR signaling pathways (Fig. 1G). Additionally, PROGENy pathway activity analysis pointed out the differential activation patterns of 14 signaling pathways between the AC and BC groups (Figure S3C, D).

Pseudotime trajectory analysis revealing MCM7 participated in malignant cell differentiation

The pathogenesis of AML is associated with developmental arrest and abnormal proliferation of malignant cell, therefore, the pseudotime trajectory analysis was performed on scRNAseq data to investigate the cells with a decreased proportion after chemotherapy. The results indicated that compared with cells in AC group, the cells in BC group was characterized by unclear differentiation boundary (Fig. 2A). In particular, cells in BC group were predominantly distributed in earlier differentiation stages compared to that in AC group, suggesting that malignant bone marrow cells were emerged during initial differentiation phases (Fig. 2B). Next, we further investigated the genes related to cell differentiation trajectory between BC and AC group using BEAM method. As shown in Fig. 2C, MCM7 was co-expressed with STMN1 related to CD34 + pre B cell, which was highly expressed during the pre-branching developmental phase as visualized in the BEAM heatmap (Fig. 2C). Notably, HSCs and TAMs in the BC group tended to be in early stages of cell differentiation (Fig. 2D), suggesting their potential pathogenic role in progression of AML. Moreover, we focused on investigating the expression of MCM7 within the pseudotime trajectory between the BC and AC groups, revealing that MCM7 expression in BC group was higher than AC group (Fig. 2E). Finally, the western blot was used for validating the expression of MCM7 in both BC and AC AML patients. The results were similar with the above description that MCM7 was highly expressed in BC (p < 0.05, Fig. 2F, G).

Cellchat revealing the changes of B cell communication networks before/after chemotherapy

Cellchat was performed to explore the changes of B cell communications, the results indicated that the interaction strength of B cells only with TAMs, HSC and Mast cell was strong in BC group, with other cells was weak (Fig. 3A), while the interaction strength of B cells with other cells (such as myeloid cells, fibroblast and marophage) was enhanced in AC group (Fig. 3B). Notably, we found that AC group showed more interactions number but lower average interaction weights compared to BC group (Fig. 3C). Conservative and specific signaling pathway analysis demonstrated that MPZ and CEACAM signaling pathway exclusively activated in the BC group, while CLEC, IL1, and SPP1 pathways were uniquely demonstrated activation in AC group (Fig. 3D). Besides, we systematically analyzed aggregated signaling patterns by comparing outgoing, incoming, and overall communication strengths across cell populations. The results represented that B cells was provided with the strongest activity of overall signaling in the BC group, and TAMs had the most robust communication networks in the AC group (Fig. 3E). Figure S4A showed the communication frequency of TAMs and B cells, and Figure S4B exhibited the heatmap of complementary interaction.

After that, to determine vital regulatory factors involved in the interaction between B cells and other cell types in AML patients, we used the NicheNet package to examine the mechanisms of interactions. The results indicated that high levels of ligand activity for JAM2, TNF, COL18A8, and S100B was observed in TAMs, meanwhile, the expression of S100B and JAML was relatively elevated (Fig. 3F–H). In addition, F11R, ITGA4, and ITGB1 receptors that bind to the protein encoded by JAML are highly expressed (Figure S4C), notably, JAML was highly expressed in TAMs of the BC group (Figure S4D). Above all, these results suggested that B cells and TAMs may regulate the formation of an immunosuppressive environment through the JAML axis.

The SPINK2-positive B cell subpopulation exhibited significant reduction in AC group

As above results have revealed the important role in cell communication of B cells before/after chemotherapy, we further explore the function of B cell in AML treated by chemotherapy. That is to say, by re-clustering B cells, six B cell subpopulations (SPINK2 + B cell, GADD45B + B cell, S100A8 + B cell, TRH + B cell, FTH1 + B cell and IGHG1 + B cell) were obtained according to gene expression profile (Fig. 4A). All the genes expressed significantly in B cell subpopulations were shown in Fig. 4B. Besides, we observed that SPINK2 + B cell ratio was decreased in the AC group after comparing the ratio of B cell subpopulations (Fig. 4C). Furthermore, the western blot results verified that SPINK2 expression was reduced in the AC group when compared with BC group (p < 0.01, Fig. 4D).

The results indicated that SPINK2 was the therapeutic target gene in the development of AML, suggesting that AML patients with high expression of SPINK2 were more suitable for receiving chemotherapy. The UMAP distribution of different subtypes of cells before or after chemotherapy was shown in Fig. 4E. Moreover, KEGG analysis was performed to investigate the function of B cell subpopulations in AML, indicating that SPINK2 + B cell was involved in WNT, JAK-STAT signaling pathway, etc. (Fig. 4F, Table S3). Meanwhile, PROGENy analysis further emphasized that SPINK2 + B cell subpopulation promoted the process of AML through JAK-STAT signaling pathway (Fig. 4G). Furthermore, the validation using western blot suggested that the activity of JAK-STAT signaling pathway was reduced in the AC group (Fig. 4H, I). Additionally, we found that S100A8 + B cell subpopulation was associated with cell apoptosis, also, which was up-regulated in the AC group (Table S4). The above results demonstrated that chemotherapy blocked the signal transduction of the JAK-STAT pathway, inhibited the proliferation of leukemia cells, and induced their apoptosis, thereby exerting therapeutic effects.

In addition, we have already found that MCM7 participated in the malignant cell differentiation process. Here, we further explore the expression of MCM7 in B cell (Fig. 4J), the results indicated that MCM7 was distributed in SPINK2 + B cells, GADD455 + B cells, TRH + B cells, and FTH1 + B cells in the BC group, while which were down-regulated in the AC group, further indicating the role of MCM7 in malignant cells.

Abnormal differentiation of B cells involved in the process of AML through pseudotime trajectory analysis

The results of pseudotime trajectory analysis for B cells indicated that SPINK2 + B cell mainly distributed in the early differentiation trajectory, while S100A8 was in the end of differentiation trajectory (Fig. 5A–C). Then, the marker gene of the six B cell types and the top five genes with the most significant differences from start to finish in a pseudotime trajectory were shown in Fig. 5D, E. Also, we observed that the early stage of differentiation was mainly associated with B cells differentiation by the BEAM analysis, suggesting that abnormal differentiation of B cells leads to the accumulation of immature or abnormally differentiated B cells in the malignant stage of AML (Fig. 5F), which may have characteristics such as uncontrolled proliferation and inhibited apoptosis. Thus, we inferred that B-cell differentiation related genes may become new targets for AML treatment.

Patients with AML may have immune dysfunction. The cell proportion plot showed that MRC1 + TAMs were significantly down-regulated in AC group (Fig. 6B). The significant gene expression of each subtype was shown in Fig. 6C.

TAMs differentiate into M1 and M2 types, which play a crucial role in the occurrence, development, and treatment resistance of leukemia. M1 has anti-tumor properties and can secrete pro-inflammatory factors such as interleukin-12 (IL-12), interferon (IFN)-γ, and tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-α; M2 has immunosuppressive properties and secretes anti-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-10 and transforming growth factor (TGF)-β. Therefore, we further re-clustered TAMs based on expression of marker genes, the results showed that MRC1 + TAMs were classified as M2 type, while the other four subtypes were classified as M1 type (Fig. 6D). Meanwhile, the expression of MRC1 in AML samples was validated using western blot, suggesting that chemotherapy significantly inhibited the expression of MRC1 (Fig. 6E). Moreover, the UMAP of TAMs distribution was shown in Fig. 6F. The UMAP between AC and BC groups represented that most TAMs in the BC samples was distributed in the M2 type, while which in AC was in the M1 type (Fig. 6G). This result suggested that chemotherapy promoted transformation of cells from M2 to M1 type, relieved immune suppression, and alleviated the patient’s condition through secreting anti-tumor factors.

Moreover, The GSVA analysis results showed that the M2 subtype is mainly enriched in cell cycle and PI3K-AKT-mTOR, promoting the proliferation of tumor cells related to AML (Fig. 6H). At the same time, we found pathways related to cell apoptosis in the M1 subtype, which is consistent with the findings as above results (Fig. 4). All these results indicated that chemotherapy drugs promoted tumor cell apoptosis and inhibited the process of AML. Meanwhile, western blot was performed for validating the expression of PI3K-AKT-mTOR in both BC and AC groups, the results indicated that PI3K-AKT-mTOR signaling pathway was decreased in the AC group (Fig. 6I, p < 0.05, p < 0.01). Figure 6J showed the proportion of each subtype before and after chemotherapy.

Furthermore, we have found that MCM7 gene is involved in the differentiation process of malignant cells. Therefore, further observation of the distribution of MCM7 in TAMs showed that MCM7 is distributed in the M1 subtype of the BC group, and these subtypes are down-regulated in the AC group, which further illustrated the role of MCM7 in malignant cells (Fig. 6K).

Pseudotime trajectory analysis of TAMs subpopulation representing the pro-apoptotic effect of chemotherapy

The results of the pseudotime trajectory analysis showed that MRC1 + TAMs (M2) were at the end of differentiation, while other TAMs subtypes (M1) were at the initial stage of differentiation (Figs. 7A, B). The density plot showed that the end of cell differentiation in the BC group corresponded to the MRC1 + TAMs (M2) subtype when compared to the AC group (Fig. 7C, D). The results further verified that TAMs in the BC group tended to be M2 subtype, while chemotherapy promoted transformation of TAMs from M2 to M1 type. Furthermore, the BEAM analysis results found that TAMs in the early differentiation stage (Cluster2/M1), which mainly played a role in pathway related to apoptosis (Fig. 7E). Additionally, the western blot results probed that cell apoptosis was promoted in the AC group (Fig. 7F, p < 0.01). These results further confirmed the role of chemotherapy in driving cell apoptosis.

Discussion

Chemotherapy is a key therapeutic modality for AML, as it can eliminate leukemia cells via pharmacologic agents and induce disease remission [24]. However, in cases of relapsed AML, patients treated by chemotherapy are often associated with high rates of subsequent relapse, severe toxicities, and mortality [25]. Previous studies have demonstrated that the immune microenvironment plays a critical role in the chemotherapeutic response of AML. It influences treatment efficacy and patient prognosis by regulating immune cell functions, cytokine networks, and interactions between leukemia cells and the microenvironment [26]. Therefore, this study performed scRNA-seq on bone marrow samples from AML patients to investigate the single-cell transcriptomic landscape before and after chemotherapy. We focused on characterizing the cellular dynamics and molecular mechanisms underlying treatment response and resistance, aiming to enhance the current understanding of AML pathogenesis and identify potential therapeutic targets.

In this study, we described the single-cell transcriptomic atlas of AML using scRNAseq and obtained 11 cell subpopulations. Among these cell subpopulations, B cells, HSCs, and TAMs were the significant cell subsets between BC and AC groups. Notably, the distribution of cell subpopulations among different samples exhibited a certain degree of heterogeneity rather than a completely homogeneous pattern. This heterogeneity mainly originated from the individual biological differences among different patients in terms of bone marrow microenvironment, immune background, disease baseline, and chemotherapy response. Accumulating studies indicated that B cells [27], HSCs [28] and TAMs [29] played an important role in promoting immune evasion and disease progression in AML. Guo et al. [30] suggested that impaired B cell activation and loss of plasticity may be important features of AML relapse. HSCs determined the occurrence and recurrence of the disease, while TAMs promoted tumor progression by shaping the immune microenvironment [31]. Thus, exploration for the function of these cells would help strengthen the understanding the mechanism of AML. Ulteriorly, this study observed that B cells were significantly enriched in MYC-targets-V1, MYC-targets-V2, DNA repair, E2F targets and G2M checkpoint pathways. These pathways were closely involved in cell cycle progression, DNA replication and damage repair, as well as cell proliferation and activation, strongly suggesting that B cells in pre‑chemotherapy AML patients were in a highly proliferative and metabolically active state, which may contribute to the distinct immune status and disease phenotypes between BC and AC groups [30]. Meanwhile, GSVA analysis revealed that TAMs regulated AML progression via the PI3K-AKT-mTOR signaling pathway. Also, BEAM analysis results confirmed that TAMs regulated cell apoptosis in AML post-chemotherapy through PI3K-AKT-mTOR signaling pathway. Recent evidence has indicated that the constitutive activation of the PI3K-Akt-mTOR pathway exhibits interpatient variability, and heightened activity within this pathway serves as an adverse prognostic indicator in AML [32, 33]. has indicated that the constitutive activation of the PI3K-Akt-mTOR pathway exhibits interpatient variability, and heightened activity within this pathway serves as an adverse prognostic indicator in AML [34]. Our study further highlighted that the importance of PI3K-Akt-mTOR pathway in TAMs, thereby providing the theoretical basis for the application of targeted drugs combined with chemotherapy.

Next, we found that MCM7 gene was highly expressed in malignant cells in the BC group, co-expressed with CD34 + pre B cells in BC groups, which decreased in AC group. MCM7 is one of the subunits of MCM2-7 complex, which is frequently dysregulated in hematological malignancies. Tian et al. [35] revealed that MCM7 was highly expressed in AML cells, subsequently knockdown of MCM7 was performed for inhibiting its endogenous expression in AML cells and created a new therapeutic strategy. The results in our study were consistent with those reported in the literature regarding as a risk factor of MCM7 in leukemia. Besides, our study further performed pseudotime analysis to reveal MCM7 enrichment in early differentiation trajectories suggests its potential role in maintaining leukemic stem cell properties, consistent with reports of MCM family proteins in preserving progenitor states [36]. The therapeutic implications are particularly compelling, as preclinical models show that MCM7 inhibition sensitizes AML cells to chemotherapy [36], supporting its exploration as a therapeutic target.

Moreover, the dramatic reduction of SPINK2 + B cells after chemotherapy echoed current conclusions that SPINK2 was regarded as an important marker for chemotherapy-resistant subpopulation [37]. Also, the observation in this study indicated that surviving SPINK2 + B cells activated JAK-STAT pathways, which provides mechanistic insight into their persistence, suggesting potential synergy with JAK inhibitors. Conversely, the expansion of S100A8 + apoptotic-prone B cells mirrors reports of S100A8/9 as mediators of chemotherapy-induced cell death [38]. Furthermore, we found that chemotherapy driven the transition for M2 to M1 in TAMs polarization, which was a significant application for immunotherapy approaches. The result verified the recent literature that M2-like TAMs protect AML cells through PD-L1 and IL-10 secretion [39]. The temporal pattern of polarization suggests a therapeutic window for combining chemotherapy with TAM-targeting agents or immune therapy, to sustain M1-like phenotypes. Notably, our cell communication analysis revealed persistent JAML-mediated interactions between residual B cells and TAMs, identifying a potential resistance mechanism that could be disrupted with F11R/ITGB1-targeting strategies.

Conclusions

In conclusion, our study establishes a high-resolution framework for understanding AML’s cellular ecosystem during chemotherapy treatment. Besides, MCM7 was highly expressed in malignant cells and early differentiation before chemotherapy, which was a potential target for AML. Furthermore, we found that chemotherapy improved AML might regulated SPINK2 + B cells differentiation and cell apoptosis. Finally, TAMs subpopulation analysis revealed that chemotherapy Chemotherapy promotes the transformation of TAMs from M2 type (immunosuppressive type) to M1 type (anti-tumor type), thereby alleviating the immunosuppressive state and enhancing the anti-tumor immune response. These findings not only revealed the impact of chemotherapy on immune cell function, but also provided a theoretical basis for combined immunotherapy/targeted-therapy strategies.
