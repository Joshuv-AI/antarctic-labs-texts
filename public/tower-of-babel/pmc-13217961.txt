Multi-enzymatic catalysis copper ceria-polyphenol nanozyme for balancing peroxidase catalysis and reactive oxygen scavenging: a synergistic strategy for enhancing drug-resistant bacteria eradication
Authors: Caizhen Liang, Yingsi Wang, Yanling Xu, Gang Zhou, Qingshan Shi, Xiaobao Xie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractStrong catalysis and novel copper-induced cell death enable copper-based nanozymes to present good bactericidal capacity against antibiotic-resistant pathogens, but their aspect of the balance between low ROS and low-dose antibacterial performance remains a significant challenge. Herein, an innovative copper/cerium (Cu/Ce)-doped metal-polyphenol network nanozyme (CCP) was synthesized for synergistically modulating the multi-enzymatic activities and enhancing methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) eradication, where Cu and Ce individually decorated on carbon nanosheet skeleton. Benefited from the Cu/Ce dual-site structure, CCP exhibited remarkable multi-enzymatic (superoxide dismutase-, peroxidase-, glutathione peroxidase-, and nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide peroxidase-like) and •OH scavenging activities. While possessing negative surface charge and good biocompatibility, CCP displayed excellent antibacterial capability. With the addition of H2O2, CCP achieved complete killing against MRSA at 30 µg·mL− 1, whose process exhibited rather low ROS content, suggesting the balanced ability of CCP between peroxidase catalysis and ROS scavenging. Surprisingly, CCP could eradicate ~80% MRSA biofilms at 60 µg·mL− 1 without H2O2, probably attributed to the weak acidic, H2O2- and GSH-rich biofilm microenvironment for enhancing the peroxidase and glutathione peroxidase catalyses. Mechanism explorations revealed that the prominent antibacterial behavior was associated with the Cu/Ce synergistic effect on blocking bacterial cell membrane construction and cell wall biosynthesis, breaking ATP and protein synthesis, inducing protein toxicity stress and promoting TCA cycle to react with toxic Cu+/Ce3+ for cuproptosis-like death. Furthermore, shrimp preservation experiment demonstrated the great potential of CCP in food preservation field. Introduction

Bacteria, ubiquitous in human habitat including body, food, water and interfaces, have been one of the top 10 threats to human health and security [1]. Although the widespread application of antibiotics has controlled numerous bacterial infections, their unreasonable use and the rapid replication of bacteria simultaneously accelerated the evolution of multidrug-resistant strains, becoming a global crisis [2, 3]. Methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA), a superbug with high virulence, is resistant to most β-lactam antibiotics due to its acquired mecA on a mobile genetic element, which has been one of the top death-induced bacteria, causing over 120,000 deaths per annum [4]. Even worse, its tendency to form biofilms with complex structure exacerbates the multidrug resistance, resulting in the difficulty in therapy and expensive financial burden, while the development of new antibiotics remains in the doldrums because of the costs and technical challenge [5]. Therefore, it is urgent to develop a more efficient and antibiotic-free strategy to eradicate MRSA in microbial safety investigation.

Copper (Cu), an indispensable trace element for living organism but toxicity at excess level, has been reported as an antibacterial agent with a long history [6]. The US Environmental Protection Agency also has approved its antimicrobial activity as the only hard surface material [7]. To date, the exploration of Cu-based antibacterial materials encompass nanomaterial, coatings, complexes, and hydrogels [8, 9]. Among the nanomaterials, Cu-based nanozymes with intrinsic enzyme-like activities are popular in killing drug-resistant bacteria due to their low biological toxicity, high ROS production, non-drug-resistance and facile synthesis [10, 11]. The prevailing view is that peroxidase (POD)-like nanozymes can convert the cuprous ions (Cu+) into cupric ions (Cu2+) using H2O2 as substrate, along with the generation of •OH, while the oxidase (OXD)-like nanozymes can catalyze O2 into H2O2, O2•− and •OH, leading to the damage of DNA, protein or cell membrane, thus killing bacteria [12]. Since aerobic organisms are more sensitive to Cu-induced death, the researchers recently put forward a specific cell death mechanism that the overload Cu2+ into bacterial cells released from Cu-based nanozymes can cause the aggregation of lipoylated proteins and loss of ion-sulfur (Fe-S) cluster protein in tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, which was recognized as a promising strategy for combating drug-resistant bacteria [13]. Nevertheless, there is a challenge related with the Cu-induced bacterial death, as the Cu ions released from Cu-based nanozymes can be depleted by the abundant glutathione from cells or biofilms. To figure out this issue, considerable studies exploited nanozymes with glutathione peroxidase (GPx)-like activity to deplete the high level of GSH, while the reduction of Cu2+ to Cu+ could enhance the Cu-induced death process [14]. Therefore, integrating biomimetic multi-catalytic properties into Cu-based nanozymes will be more effective for multimodal antibacterial application.

Currently, a number of Cu-based nanozymes equipped with multi-enzymatic activities, such as SOD-POD [15], SOD-catalase (CAT) [16, 17], glucose oxidase (GOx)-POD [18, 19], and lactate oxidase (LOD)-POD [20], have been designed logically for improving the antibacterial performance. Despite of the great progress, excess ROS (e.g. H2O2 and •OH) with non-targeted ability generated from the catalytic processes would cause the indiscriminate attack between harmful and harmless targets. Besides, the low penetrability of ROS was not valid for eliminating complicate biofilms with high resistance [21]. These negative feedbacks will push Cu-based nanozymes into an embarrass situation with high-dose bactericidal efficacy (> 100 µg·mL− 1) and antimicrobial resistance, thus introducing a ROS generation-scavenging system for balancing ROS in antibacterial process is challenging but necessary. According to this issue, some strategies were put forward by researchers: (1) Incorporating Cu2+ (antimicrobial component) and antioxidant ligands (such as polyphenol) into metal-organic networks, which not only provided high antioxidant activity to the antibacterial system but also improved the biosafety, and exhibited facile synthesis feature [8, 22]. (2) Introducing metal atoms of multi-valence states into the antibacterial system, which could intensify Cu+/Cu2+ redox cycle and stabilize the catalytic active sites, hence improving the balanced ability between ROS generation and scavenging during antibacterial process [23, 24]. For instance, Jiang et al. [24]. enhanced the antibacterial capacity of nanoceria nanozyme system by regulating POD-like activity and hydroxyl radical antioxidant capacity (HORAC) of Cu single-site modified on CeO2. Inspired by these reports, it can be hypothesized that the introduction of Cu and Ce in one nanozyme system could achieve the synergetic catalytic activities, thereby obtaining a valuable “1 + 1>2” antibacterial efficacy. However, there is still lack of Cu/Ce-based nanozymes with both high antibacterial ability and appropriate antioxidant activity.

Here, a multifunctional metal-phenolic network nanozyme (CCP) was synthesized through a one-step facile approach (Scheme 1) for synergistic multidrug-resistant bacteria eradication. By introducing Cu and Ce ions in aqueous gallic acid system, ultrathin nanosheet structure with high bactericidal activity was achieved. Extended X-ray absorption fine structure (EXAFS) indicated that Cu and Ce coordinated with oxygen at defect-rich sites, without metallic bond. The superior SOD-, POD- GPx- and nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide peroxidase (NPx)-like activities as well as •OH scavenging activity were detailedly explored by experimental and theoretical methods. Despite of its drug-free and negative charge, the Cu/Ce codoping amplified the complete bactericidal efficacy of CCP against MRSA at a low concentration of 30 µg·mL− 1 with the aid of H2O2, accompanied by a high scavenging function toward cellular ROS, indicating the valid balanced ability of CCP between POD catalysis and ROS scavenging. Interestingly, CCP displayed exceptional eradication capability against MRSA biofilms by eliminating EPS and interior bacteria without H2O2. Systematic in vitro mechanism studies revealed that Cu/Ce ions released from CCP could enter MRSA cells to block cell membrane construction and cell wall biosynthesis, disrupt ATP and protein syntheses, induce protein toxicity stress and promote TCA cycle to react with toxic Cu+/Ce3+ for cuproptosis-like death. Further, CCP film was fabricated using polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) and nanocellulose as matrix for shrimp preservation, which presented feasible antioxidant and antibacterial abilities, suggesting the great potential application of CCP in food preservation. Therefore, the controllable synthesis and prominent antibacterial capability of the unique Cu/Ce nanozyme could conduce to a significant understanding of the formation and antibacterial mechanisms of multi-enzymatic nanomaterials, providing a new direction in investigating multifunctional Cu-based nanozymes.

Materials and methods

Synthesis of Cu-based nanozymes

Typically, CCP was synthesized by a one-step heating reflux process. 24.81 mM Cu(NO3)2·3H2O and 12.35 mM CeCl3·7H2O were added into 500 mL pure water (18.2 MΩ·cm), and then was soluble under stirring. Concurrently, 35.27 mM gallic acid was slowly added into the mixture. Subsequently, the pH of this aqueous system was adjusted to pH 7.0–8.0 with 5 mM NaOH solution, which was transfer to 1000 mL three-necked flask and refluxed at 90 °C in oil bath. After overnight, the resulting product was collected by centrifugation at 10,000 rpm·min− 1, washing of 4–6 times, and followed by freeze-drying. CuP and CeP were also prepared in the same procedure, where the addition amount of Cu(NO3)2·3H2O and CeCl3·7H2O was 35. 36 mM and 36.26 mM, respectively.

Characterization

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) imaging and elemental mapping were carried out by a FEI Talos F200x electron microscope (Thermo Scientific, USA). Zeta potentials and hydrodynamic diameters were detected by a Malvern Zetasizer Pro Nano ZS90 dynamic light scattering (DLS) spectrometer (Malvern, UK). The chemical structure and functional groups were characterized by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, Thermo Scientific Nexsa, USA) and Brucker VERTEX 70 Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectrometer. The chemical structure and coordination environment were analyzed by X-ray absorption near-edge structure (XANES) and extended X-ray absorption fine structure (EXAFS) spectra. The Cu content was obtained by an Agilent 7800 inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS). UV-vis absorbance spectra were conducted by a PerkinElmer UV/VIS/NIR spectrometer (Lambda 750, USA). The reactive oxygen species (ROS) were confirmed by electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) spectrometer (Brucker EMXplus-6/1, Germany), where the generation of superoxide radical (O2•−) and hydroxyl radical (•OH) used DMPO as spin-trapping adduct, while the production of singlet oxygen (1O2) used TEPO as spin-trapping adduct.

SOD-like activity assay

The SOD-like activities of CuP, CeP, and CCP were measured based on the protocols of the SOD assay kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China). In brief, SOD detection buffer, WST-8/xanthine oxidase (XO) working solution, and various concentrations (10, 30, 60, 90, 120 µg·mL− 1) of CuP, CeP, and CCP were added into 96-well plates, followed by adding xanthine reaction-initiating working solution. After incubation at room temperature for 30 min, the absorbance at 450 nm was recorded using a microplate reader. Inhibition rate of each sample toward O2•− was then calculated.

POD-like activity assay

The POD-like activities of CuP and CCP were investigated using TMB as probe. CuP or CCP solution (120 µg·mL− 1), TMB (1 mM), and Tris-HCl buffer (100 mM, pH 6.0) were added into 96-well plate. Then different concentrations (0, 0.5, 1, 2, 5, and 10 mM) of H2O2 solutions were employed to initiate the kinetic reaction. The absorbance of oxTMB (ε = 39000 M− 1·cm− 1) at 652 nm was monitored every min in 30 min by a microplate reader. The Michaelis-Menten constant (Km) was calculated by the Lineveaver-Burk Plot [25, 26]: 1/V = Km/Vmax (1/[S] + 1/Km). The turnover number (TON) was calculated according to the Equation [27]: TON = Vmax/[E0]. Vmax is the maximum reaction velocity, [S] is the concentration of H2O2, and [E0] is the molar concentration of metal in enzyme mimics.

•OH free radical scavenging activity assay

Briefly, Na2EDTA (80 µM, 100 µL), FeCl3 (25 µM, 50 µL), H2O2 (2.8 mM, 50 µL), 2-deoxyl-D-ribose (2.8 mM, 50 µL), and ascorbic acid (0.1 mM, 50 µL) were added into KH2PO4/Na2HPO4 buffer (0.2 M, pH7.4, 300 µL) containing different amounts of CuP, CeP or CCP (0, 2, 4, 6, 8, 10 and 15 µg·mL− 1), which were incubated in 50 °C water-bath for 20 min. Subsequently, trichloroacetic acid (50 µg·mL− 1, 0.5 mL) and 2-thiobarbituric acid (TBA, 10 µg·mL− 1, 0.5 mL) were added into the mixtures, and then placed in 105 °C oven for 20 min, followed by cooling to room temperature. The absorbance at 532 nm was measured by a microplate reader.

GPx-like activity assay

The kinetic assays of Cu-based nanozymes eliminating GSH were performed using 5,5’-dithiobis(2-nitrobenzoic acid) (DNTB) as a indicator. Specifically, 300 µM DNTB and 90 µg·mL− 1 CuP or CCP were added to PBS (10 mM, pH7.4), mixing with various concentrations of GSH (0, 0.2, 0.4, 0.6, 0.8, and 1.0 mM). The absorbance change of the mixtures was immediately recorded by a microplate reader at 412 nm every 5 min for 3 h (ε = 13600 M− 1·cm− 1 for TNB).

NADH-peroxidase (NPx)-like activity assay

This test was carried out by using NADH (ε = 6220 M− 1·cm− 1) as substrate. Briefly, various concentrations of NADH (0, 0.1, 0.2, 0.4, 0.6, 0.8, and 1.0 mM) were added into CuP or CCP aqueous system, which were then detected with a microplate reader at an absorbance of 340 nm.

Anti-planktonic bacteria experiments

Methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA, ATCC33591) was employed as the representative drug-resistant pathogenic bacterium to explore the antibacterial abilities of CuP, CeP and CCP. The antibacterial ability was evaluated by minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC), the absorbance at 600 nm, and minimum bactericidal concentration (MBC) experiments. A series of final concentrations of CuP, CeP or CCP (0, 10, 30, 50, 70, and 90 µg·mL− 1) were separately blended with 2 × 105 CFU·mL− 1 of MRSA in nutrient broth (NB, 18 g·L− 1), which were cultured in a 37 °C incubator with a shaking rate of 80 rpm. After 24 h, the absorbance of the bacterial suspensions at 600 nm (OD600) was recorded to calculated the MIC value. MRSA suspensions treated with both H2O2 (100 µM) and CuP, CeP or CCP were also implemented in the same procedure, each concentration repeated triple. Then, each bacterial suspension was diluted 101−107 times and incubated on NB agar plate for 18 h at 37 °C for colony counting, subsequent calculation for confirming the MBC value.

DNA damage assay

DLS experiment: Genomic DNA was extracted from MRSA using a HiPure Bacterial DNA Kit (Magen, Guangzhou, China), while the plasmid (PUC19) from E. coli was harvested with SanPrep Column Plasmid Mini-Preps Kit (Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China), and their concentrations were determined by NanoDrop 2000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Shanghai). Subsequently, genomic DNA (0.0015 pmol·mL− 1) and plasmid (0.5 pmol·ml− 1) were incubated with PBS, H2O2, CuP, CeP, CCP, CuP + H2O2, CeP + H2O2, and CCP + H2O2, respectively, for 2 h at 37 °C. The diameter changes of these samples were evaluated on a Malvern Zetasizer Pro Nano ZS90 dynamic light scattering (DLS) spectrometer. For gel retardation assay, the treated genomic DNA (63.5 µg·mL− 1) and plasmid (32.6 µg·mL− 1) were run on a 1% agarose gel with Gel-Red for 25 min, and the DNA band shift was captured by gel imaging system (Bio-Rad ChemiDoc MP, USA).

Bacterial transcriptomic and untargeted metabolomic analyses

To evaluate the gene expression and metabolite changes of CCP treated bacteria, MRSA suspensions with a OD600 of 0.6 were co-cultured with NB, CCP + H2O2 at 37 °C for 3 h, respectively, NB treated MRSA suspension was utilized as the control group. After 3 h incubation, MRSA cells were then harvested and snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen for further analysis. For RNA-seq analysis, the collected bacteria were submitted to Shanghai Applied Protein Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China) for RNA extraction, sequencing, and initial analysis. Gene expression analysis was implemented using RSEM (http://deweylab.github.io/RSEM/). Genes with p < 0.05 and |log2 Fold change| ≥ 0.5 were considered as DEGs. For metabolomic analysis, the collected MRSA cells were submitted to Beijing Biomarker Technologies Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China) for metabolite detection using liquid chromatography-mass spectrometer (LC-MS, Waters Acquity I-Class PLUS, Waters Xevo G2-XS QTOF, USA). Progenesis QI (Waters Corporation, USA) was used for statistical analysis and metabolic database (https://metlin.scripps.edu/) was applied for identified metabolites.

Biofilm eradication experiments

To investigate the eradication capacity of CuP, CeP, and CCP against MRSA biofilms, the MRSA suspensions with a concentration of 1 × 107 CFU·mL− 1 (500 µL) were added into 48-well plate for 48 h at 37 °C to form mature biofilms. Subsequently, the media were discarded and the attached biofilms was gently washed with PBS, followed by adding diluted CuP, CeP, CCP, CuP + H2O2, CeP + H2O2, or CCP + H2O2 at a concentration range of 0–120 µg·mL− 1 (H2O2: 100 µM). After 24 h incubation, the residual biofilms were rinsed with PBS for three times, stained with 1% crystal violet for 30 min, washed, and dissolved with 95% ethanol. The solutions were recorded at the absorbance of 595 nm by microplate reader. Additionally, the residual biofilms subjected to the same treatment were stained with WGA-AF488 to evaluate the eradication of the extracellular polymeric substances (EPS) in MRSA biofilms. Besides, the viabilities and morphologies of the biofilm samples were observed by CLSM and SEM, respectively.

Preparation of CCP films

The CCP films were fabricated through the solution casting strategy. First, α-cellulose was used as the raw material to synthesized nanocellulose by tempo-oxidization [28] and ultrasonical homogenization. For the matrix, 6.25 g 1% aqueous nanocellulose (NCS) was mixed with 2.5 g 10% aqueous polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) and 0.1 g glycerol under magnetic stirring. Different volumes of CCP (30–60 µL, 10 mg·mL− 1) were added into the matrix and water was replenished to 10 g. After 30 min stirring, the mixture was ultrasonicated for 30 min to remove the bubbles, which was subsequently transferred to Petri dishes and dried at 40 °C for 16–24 h to obtain CCP films (denoted as CP-0.073% CCP and CP-0.145% CCP). The film without CCP was also prepared for comparison.

Statistical analysis

Statistical data were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) using the t-test. The p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Result and discussion

Synthesis and characterization of CCP nanozyme

In this work, a facile heating-reflux procedure was proposed to prepare Cu/Ce dual-atom nanozyme (CCP) for efficiently combating multidrug-resistant bacteria with low concentration, as illustrated in Scheme 1. For comparison, Cu single-atom nanozyme (CuP) and Ce nanozyme (CeP) were also fabricated using the same method, respectively. Transmission electron transmission microscopy (TEM) images (Fig. 1A) indicated that CCP had a shape of nanosheet, while CuP and CeP were irregular nano-agglomerates, which was probably attributed to the synergistic effect of the different orbital electrons of Cu and Ce interacting with gallic acid ligand. The high-resolution TEM (HRTEM, Fig. 1B) and XRD spectra (Fig. S1) demonstrated that all of them were amorphous structures. The dynamic light scattering (DLS) measurement result revealed that CuP, CeP, and CCP had a hydrodynamic diameter of about 36 nm, 334 nm, and 534 nm (Fig. 1C), respectively, indicating the aggregation of CeP. The element mapping analysis presented that C, O, Cu or Ce were uniformly distributed in the three materials, demonstrating the successful doping of Cu and Ce. The zeta potential result (Fig. 1D) indicated the negative charges on the surfaces of CuP, CeP, and CCP due to the functionalization of gallic acid ligand with abundant carboxyl and phenolic hydroxyl groups. Fourier Transform Infrared (FT-IR) spectra (Fig. 1E) showed that a broad characteristic peak of C–OH at 3668–2510 cm− 1 presented in the three samples, while the stretching vibration peaks of benzene ring located at 1427–1178 cm− 1 basically disappeared, suggesting the coordination of gallic acid with Cu or Ce. Besides, CCP exhibited two new peaks with two shoulder peaks at 1400–1648 cm− 1, probably attributed to the Cu-O-C and Ce-O-C bonds. These results indicated the successful synthesis of CuP, CeP, and CCP.

The chemical compositions and element binding states of CuP, CeP and CCP were investigated by the X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS). Figure 1F and Fig. S2A showed that CCP were composed of C, O, Cu and Ce elements, while CuP and CeP lacked of Ce and Cu, respectively. The Cu 2p XPS spectra (Fig. 1G) exhibited two featured peaks around 934.68 eV and 954.48 eV for CuP and CCP, which corresponded to the Cu 2p3/2 and Cu 2p1/2 spin-orbital splitting, respectively, and accompanied by two satellite peaks. This indicated the presence of Cu+ and Cu2+ for potential multi-enzymatic catalysis activities, where the Cu+/Cu2+ ratio of about 1:4 for CuP increased to about 1:1.69 for CCP due to the introduction of Ce element. The Ce 3 d XPS spectra (Fig. 1H) were deconvoluted into 10 peaks, belonging to the Ce3+ species at v0, v’, u0, u’ and Ce4+ species at v, v’’, v’’’, u, u’’, u’’’, respectively. Interestingly, Ce4+ content for CCP (53.06%) was comparable to that for CeP (56.09%), suggesting that the introduction of Cu played a minor effect on Ce valence state, but the reverse effect was prominent. Meanwhile, the XPS analysis (Fig. S2A) and inductively coupled plasma-mass spectrometry (ICP-MS) results (Fig. S2B) revealed that the Cu content in CuP and Ce content in CeP were more than twice to that in CCP, respectively. The C 1 s and O 1 s XPS spectra (Figure S2C-D) revealed the presence of C = C/C-C, C-O and O-Cu/Ce bonds in CCP. These results suggested that the dual-valence structure of Cu+/Cu2+ and Ce3+/Ce4+ in CCP could improve its multi-enzymatic catalysis properties.

Subsequently, X-ray absorption fine-structure (XAFS) spectroscopy was employed to explore the chemical state and coordination environment of Cu/Ce dual-atom sites in CCP nanozyme. The Cu K-edge X-ray absorption near-edge structure (XANES) spectra (Fig. 2A) indicated that the average valence state of Cu in CCP was higher than that in CuP and CuO standard sample, and no Cu2+ was reduced into metal copper. In contrast, the XANES spectra (Fig. 2B) of the Ce L3-edge demonstrated that the valence state of Ce in CCP was higher than +4 but lower than that in CeP. The extended X-ray absorption fine structure (EXAFS) was applied for uncovering the structural information of CCP in K space (Fig. 2C-D). The Fourier-transformed (FT) k2-weight (FT-EXAFS) spectra of CuP and CCP (Fig. 3E) exhibited a prominent Cu-O coordination peak at ~1.94 Å, and no Cu-Cu coordination peak, signifying that Cu atoms were atomically dispersed in CuP and CCP. The minor peak at about 2.73 Å might associate with Cu-C backscattering in the second shell [29]. The Ce L3-edge FT-EXAFS spectrum (Fig. 2F) analysis revealed that CCP contained Ce-O bonds with a second shell of Ce-C, and no Ce-Ce bond. Besides, neither of Cu K-edge and Ce L3-edge FT-EXAFS spectra confirmed the existence of Cu-Ce bond. Further, least-squares EXAFS fitting in R and K space (Fig. 2G-I, Fig. S3 and Table S1-2) suggested that the Cu atoms in CuP were coordinated by 4.5 ± 0.9 O atoms, while coordinated by 4.2 ± 0.8 O atoms and 3.3 ± 0.7 C atoms in CCP, confirming the asymmetric environment of Cu atoms and higher electron density in CCP. Whereas, Ce atoms were coordinated with 11.0 ± 2.0 O atoms in CCP (Table S2). This high coordination phenomenon was probably because Ce and Cu situated at the defect-rich sites, leading to the lattice distortion and forming the unsaturated coordination. In addition, wavelet transform (WT)-EXAFS analysis (Fig. 2J-K) intuitively revealed the consistent results that Cu and Ce in CCP mainly bonded with O atoms. The possible structures of CCP and CuP were constructed by first-principle calculations (Fig. 2G-H). Without direct bonding between Cu and Ce, the coordination of three O atoms for Cu center was consistent with that for Ce center in CCP, while Cu in CuP was coordinated with four O atoms, echoing with the EXAFS analysis.

Multi-enzymatic activities of CCP nanozyme

Ensuring the potential catalytic properties of CCP was vital for its role in ROS generation and scavenging. Thus, the multi-enzymatic activities including SOD, POD, GPx and nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide peroxidase (NPx) catalyses and antioxidant properties were studies in this work. Since superoxide anion (O2•−) is not only one of the dominant ROS, but an important source for producing H2O2 and O2 by SOD catalysis, the SOD-like activity of CCP was first evaluated by exploring the ability of inhibiting O2•− generation using 2-(2-methoxy-4-nitrophenyl)−3-(4-nitrophenyl)−5-(2,4-disulfophenyl)−2H-tetrazolium sodium salt (WST-8) method. As seen in Fig. 3A-B, CCP exhibited dose-dependence in O2•− scavenging, and could achieve about 63% inhibition efficiency at the concentration of 90 µg·mL− 1, while CuP and CeP showed dose-independence and lower inhibited rate (< 44%), demonstrating the higher SOD-like activity of CCP. POD is a universal enzyme that could catalyze H2O2 into •OH, which plays an important role in chemodynamic catalytic reaction. Hence, the POD-like activity of CCP was then measured using the 3,3’,5,5’-tetramethylbenzidine (TMB) colorimetric assay with H2O2 as substrate (Fig. 3C). Figure S4 showed that the absorbance at 652 nm of CCP system increased more remarkable than that of CuP system, indicating the superior POD-like activity of CCP. However, no signal change was observed for CeP aqueous system, suggesting that the POD-like activity of CeP could not be activated in this condition. Further, the catalytic efficiencies of CuP and CCP toward various H2O2 concentrations were performed to achieve the Michaelis-Menten fitting curves and kinetic parameters (Table S3). The analysis results in Fig. 3D-E elucidated that CCP presented a higher maximal reaction velocity (Vmax, 7.43 µM·s− 1), but comparable Michaelis-Menten constant (Km, 0.751 mM), compared with CuP (5.15 µM·s− 1, 0.748 mM), suggesting their similar affinity for H2O2 substrate. Nevertheless, the turnover number/Km (TON/Km, 20.98 mM− 1 s− 1) of CCP was higher than that of CuP (14.77 mM− 1 s− 1), indicative of the superior POD-like activity of CCP due to the synergistic effect of Cu/Ce active sites. As reported, NADH/NAD+ plays a crucial role in bacterial electron transport system and ATP synthesis [30, 31], thus it is essential to evaluate whether CCP possessed NPx-like activity of catalyzing NADH to NAD+ (Fig. 3F) for disrupting the NADH/NAD+ cycle. Figure S5 showed that the absorbance of NADH at 340 nm rapidly fell to approximate zero in 60 min after incubation with CCP, which was more prominent compared to that treated with CuP or CeP, proving that the NPx-like activity of Cu/Ce dual-atom nanozyme was superior to that of Cu or Ce single-atom nanozyme. The Michaelis-Menten curves originated from the oxidation rates of different NADH concentrations by CCP exhibited a 4.05-fold higher Vmax (15.29 µM·s− 1) and 1.52-fold lower Km (0.066 mM) than that by CuP (3.77 µM·s− 1, 0.10 mM), accounting for the more efficient catalytic activity and stronger affinity of CCP toward NADH (Fig. 3G-H). Moreover, compared with CuP (TON = 8.20 × 10− 3 s− 1; TON/Km = 81.96 mM− 1·s− 1), CCP displayed higher TON (4.60 × 10− 2 s− 1) and TON/Km (697.79 mM− 1·s− 1) for oxidizing NADH. Furthermore, NAD+/NADH Assay Kit was used to verify the NAD+ product in the catalytic process. As displayed in Fig. S6, the NADH concentrations of 0.6, 0.8 and 1 mM were nearly oxidized into NAD+ in CCP system, while these conversion rates were slightly lower in CuP system. This result confirmed the oxidation of NADH into NAD+ catalyzed by CuP or CCP, and the synergistic effect of Cu/Ce on enhancing the NPx-like activity. Besides, Hydrogen Peroxide Assay Kit was also applied to detect if there was H2O2 product in the oxidation process of NADH by the three materials. Figure S7 illustrated that negligible change was observed for the absorbance at 560 nm in CuP and CCP treated groups, whereas the CeP groups presented similarly incremental values to the non-treated group. It indicated that H2O2 was not the product in the pathway of CuP and CCP catalyzing NADH, which was conducive to disrupt the bacterial metabolism without the participation of excess ROS, different from the reported NADH-oxidase-like nanozymes that could produce H2O2.

Depleting the bacterial GSH could destroy the redox balance of bacteria and biofilm, hence the GPx-like activity of CCP was evaluated in this work. In this context, Glutathione Peroxidase Assay Kit was used to verify the GPx-like activity of CCP for converting GSH to oxidized glutathione (GSSG). Figure S8 demonstrated that the GPx-like activity of CCP increased progressively with the increase of its concentration, and could reach ~5.5 mU·mL− 1 at 90 µg·mL− 1, indicating the favorable GPx-like activity of CCP. Based on this result, the scavenging amount of GSH by CuP and CCP was also assessed by 5,5′-dithiobis-(2-nitrobenzoic acid) (DTNB) assay (Fig. 3J-K and Fig. S9). Notably, the initial oxidation rates of various GSH concentrations by CuP and CCP obeyed to Michaelis-Menten fitting. Interestingly, CCP showed a higher Vmax (2.92 µM·s− 1) and a 5.33-fold higher Km (0.64 mM) in comparison to CuP (1.92 µM·s− 1, 0.12 mM), indicating that the affinity toward GSH was mainly attributed to the Cu2+ sites which could oxidize GSH into GSSG. Simultaneously, CCP exhibited a higher TON (8.79 × 10− 3 s− 1) but 1.63-fold lower catalytic efficiency (TON/Km, 13.74 mM− 1·s− 1) than CuP (2.70 × 10− 3 s− 1, 22.46 mM− 1·s− 1) for oxidizing GSH. In biological system, the endogenous GSSG oxidized by GPx could be reduced into GSH with the aid of the cofactor nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate (NADPH) [32, 33]. Therefore, UV-vis spectroscopy was carried out to monitor the change of NADPH in the presence of CuP or CCP (Fig. S10). Upon the addition of CCP, the typical absorption band at 337 nm decreased distinctly along with the time going, while subtle changes were recorded following the attendance of both CuP and CeP, highlighting the synergistic effect of Cu/Ce dual-atom sites on improving the GPx-like activity of CCP. Moreover, this result suggested the potential irreversible oxidized process of GSH by GPx-like activity from CCP, which may be beneficial for breaking the bacterial redox equilibrium. The above superior multi-enzymatic activities (Fig. 3L) were compared with the reported Cu/Ce-based nanozymes in Table S3, which demonstrated the higher catalysis activities of CCP nanozyme.

Based on the above excellent multi-enzymatic activities of CCP, the comparisons of POD-, SOD-, and GPx-like activities of CCP in pH 6.0 (weak acidic environment) and pH 7.4 (neutral environment) were performed. As illustrated in Fig. S11A-B, in pH 6.0, the growth trend of the absorbance at 652 in 30 min became more obvious with the increase of H2O2 concentration, while that in pH 7.4 showed negligible change, indicating that the POD-like activity of CCP in pH 7.4 was difficult to be activated. On the contrary, the GPx-like activity of CCP in pH 7.4 was higher than that in pH 6.0 (Fig. S11D-E). Interestingly, compared with in pH 7.4, CCP in pH 6.0 displayed higher SOD-like activity for inhibiting O2•− generation in the concentration of 10–60 µg·mL− 1 but similar activity in the concentration of 90–120 µg·mL− 1, which was advantageous for CCP to eliminate O2•− in weak acidic and neutral environment. These findings demonstrated that the POD-like activity of CCP dominated in weak acidic environment while GPx-like activity was more prevalent in neutral environment, but the SOD-like activity was remarkable in both weak acidic and neutral environments.

Based on the broad-spectrum multi-enzymatic catalysis properties, electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) spectroscopy was carried out to verify the ROS species produced by CuP, CeP and CCP with H2O2 treatment. The analyses utilized 2,2,6,6-tetramethylpiperidine (TEMP) as a trapping agent for singlet oxygen (1O2) and 5,5’-dimethyl-1-pyrroline-N-oxide (DMPO) as both O2•− and •OH trapping agent, respectively. As shown in Fig. 3M-N, typical three-line signals (1:1:1) assigned to 1O2 peaks and six-peaks vested in O2•− were effectively captured for CCP, and their corresponding contents were much higher than that generated by CuP, especially for the O2•− (Table S4). However, CeP exhibited negligible or no 1O2 and O2•− signals, which was well in accordance with its considerably weak POD-like activity. Additionally, similar characteristic peaks in a ratio of 1:2:2:1 were detected for CuP and CCP, demonstrating the generation of •OH. It was worth noting that the yield of 1O2 (< 4.5 µM) produced by CuP and CCP was much lower than the corresponding O2•− and •OH products. These phenomena confirmed the ability of producing ROS by CuP and CCP in the presence of H2O2, which may be advantageous for regulating the balance between POD catalysis and ROS scavenging.

•OH is one of the dominant ROS for inducing oxidative damage, thus it is essential to determine the scavenging capability of CCP toward •OH. Surprisingly, the result (Fig. 3P) revealed that CuP and CeP displayed negative scavenging efficiency toward •OH against the concentration of 2–15 µg·mL− 1, whereas CCP sustained a lower scavenging efficiency of ~40%, substantiating that CCP possessed a moderate scavenging capability against •OH, which was favorable for balancing catalysis and •OH scavenging. Besides, 2,2’-Azino-bis(3-ethylbenzothiazoline-6-sulfonic acid) (ABTS•+), a nitrogen centered radical (RNS), was employed for examining the total antioxidant capacities of CuP, CeP, and CCP. As shown in Fig. 3Q, the three materials exhibited over 90% scavenging rate against ABTS•+, confirming their superior antioxidant behaviors. These findings unveiled the potential self-cascading catalytic system constructed by CCP, involving SOD-like activity of catalyzing O2•− into H2O2 and O2, followed by POD-/GPx-like activities eliminating H2O2 and GSH, respectively, accompanied with the remarkable NPx-like activity for disrupting the NADH/NAD+ homeostasis and the antioxidant activity for scavenging excess •OH (Fig. 3R). More importantly, the cooperative effect of Cu/Ce active sites rendered CCP more conspicuous multi-enzymatic activities but comparable antioxidant ability compared to CuP with single Cu active site.

Theoretical calculation simulations of catalytic mechanisms of CCP nanozyme

First, first-principle calculation was carried out for uncovering the synergistic regulation mechanism of Cu and Ce codoping on CCP carbon-based nanozyme. As shown in Fig. S12A-C, the density of states (DOS) of pure C framework was low (~1.08 states/eV) at the Fermi level, exhibiting semiconductor property. After Cu doping, the DOS at the Femi level increased sharply to ~5.03 states/eV, which was attributed to the strong hybridization of Cu 3 d electrons with C, achieving metallization. Further Ce doping introduced strongly correlated 4f electrons, and a spin polarization peak appears at +4 eV (spin up 14.6 states/eV, spin down 8.74 states/eV), forming d-f hybridization, which endowed the system with magnetism and retaining metallicity. The crystal orbital Hamilton population (COHP) and charge density analyses (Fig. S12D-J) indicated that the change of electronic structure led to charge rearrangement: Ce was preferentially oxidized (effective charge: + 0.974 eV), which kept Cu at a higher electron density (from +0.616 eV to +0.301 eV), thereby increasing the covalent property of Cu-O bond by 57% (-ICOHP increased from 0.137 eV to 0.214 eV). This complete image from electronic structure to chemical bonds provided a unified microscopic mechanism explanation for the synergistic enhancement of catalytic performance by Cu and Ce.

Next, in order to clarify the mechanism by which Ce doping enhanced the catalysis activities of Cu site at atomic level, density functional theory (DFT) was carried out to calculate the complete catalytic free energy diagrams (Fig. 4B-G) of the SOD and POD catalysis reactions (Fig. 4A). The DFT calculation system revealed the synergistic enhancement mechanism of Cu/Ce codoped catalyst (CCP) in multiple catalytic tasks compared with the single Cu-doped catalyst (CuP), covering ROS scavenging and generation kinetics, and substrate selectivity. In the POD and SOD reactions, the introduction of Ce significantly optimized the reaction pathways through its synergistic effect with the Cu site. For the SOD reaction (2O2•− + 2 H+ → O2 + H2O2), the electronic modulation of Ce4+ enhanced the adsorption energy of O2•− from −0.45 eV to −50 eV, and its variable 4f electrons stabilized the electron transfer intermediate, reducing the rate-determining step energy barrier from 0.15 eV to 0.11 eV. In the second half of the cycle, Ce4+/Ce3+ redox pair assisted the protonation reduction of the second O2•− (∆G optimized from −0.25 eV to 0.30 eV) and completed self-regeneration. For the POD reaction (H2O2 + H+ +e− → H2O + •OH), Ce4+, as an electron transfer medium, formed an electron transfer relay with Cu2+/Cu+ in the rate-determining step (H2O2 single electron reduction), which reduced the energy barrier from 0.70 eV to 0.59 eV, and enhanced the initial adsorption of H2O2 (∆G decreased from −0.21 eV to −0.32 eV). The total driving forces of the two systems remained unchanged, confirming that Ce played a kinetic promoting role rather than a thermodynamic driving role.

At the substrate selectivity level, the Ce doping modulated the electronic structure and substrate affinity of the catalyst, resulting in opposite catalytic preference of CuP and CCP for GSH and NADH. The adsorption energy calculation (Fig. 4H, Tabe S5) indicated that CuP had a stronger binding to GSH (−27.56 kcal/mol vs. CCP’s −27.14 kcal/mol), while CCP had a better binding to NADH (−25.31 kcal/mol vs. CuP’s −24.26 kcal/mol), which was consistent with the experimental TON/Km trend. The frontier orbital analysis (Fig. 4I) further revealed that the HOMO (−0.197 eV) electron density of GSH was concentrated in the sulfhydryl group, and the orbital overlap with CuP was better. The HOMO (−0.151 eV) electron density of NADH was distributed in the nicotinamide ring, and it had a stronger electronic coupling with CCP and a lower transfer energy barrier. The adsorption energy and orbital matching jointly determine the substrate specificity. In summary, the Ce doping, on the one hand, reduced the kinetic energy barriers of POD and SOD catalyses through electron modulation and redox synergy, and on the other hand, modulates the electronic structure of the catalyst, resulting in higher affinity and electron transfer efficiency for NADH. This mechanism made CCP a biofunctional catalyst with both efficient nanozyme catalysis ability and specific substrate selectivity.

Antibacterial performance of CCP nanozyme

The outstanding POD-, GPx- and NPx-like activities of CCP enabled the synergistic effect of Cu/Ce on disrupting bacterial metabolic environment and substance synthesis, providing the potential for killing bacteria. Therefore, the antibacterial ability of CCP against methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) was investigated. First, the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) was confirmed by incubating MRSA with different concentrations of CCP with and without 100 µM H2O2 in broth at 37 °C for 24 h, respectively, followed by recording the absorption values at 600 nm (OD600). For comparison, CuP and CeP were also measured. As illustrated in Fig. 5B, D and F, the bacteriostatic ability of the three materials with H2O2 against MRSA was better than that without H2O2. Upon the treatment of H2O2, the MIC value of CCP against MRSA decreased from 50 µg·mL− 1 to 30 µg·mL− 1, while CuP and CeP had a higher MIC value of 70 µg·mL− 1. The difference could be ascribed to synergistic effect of Cu/Ce dual-site catalysis. Further, the minimum bactericidal concentration (MBC) values of CuP, CeP and CCP were verified using the plate colony counting method (Fig. 5A, C, E, and G). Obviously, nearly no MRSA colony was observed after incubation with 30 µg·mL− 1 of CCP + H2O2, demonstrating the outstanding bactericidal ability of CCP toward MRSA. However, MRSA regrew in all CuP, CuP + H2O2, CeP, CeP + H2O2 and CCP treated groups, revealing their efficient bacteriostatic activity but poor bactericidal ability. In brief, the Cu/Ce codoped structure played a significant role in killing MRSA, while H2O2 facilitated the synergistic bactericidal capability. Then the survival status of MRSA after treatment was evaluated by a confocal laser scanning microscope (CLSM) with SYTO9 and propidium iodide (PI) staining, in which SYTO9 emits green fluorescence by combined with nucleic acid in living cells while PI reports the damaged or dead cells with red fluorescence. As shown in Fig. 5H, despite of the aggregation, MRSA in CeP and CeP + H2O2 treated groups presented green fluorescence, similar to the control, CuP and CuP + H2O2 treated groups, manifesting their sufficiently high survival rate. Conversely, almost all the MRSA in CCP + H2O2 treated group exhibited intense red fluorescence in aggregation morphology, and dim red fluorescence emerged in the CCP treated group, proving the superior bactericidal ability of CCP with low H2O2 concentration.

Bacterial biofilms were densely three-dimension (3D) structure formed by the bacteria ensconced within extracellular polymeric substance (EPS), which leads to a more drug-resistant bacterial community for combating antibiotics, immune system or mechanical attacking. Thus, it was essential and urgent to evaluate the eradication capability of CCP against MRSA biofilms. The mature MRSA biofilms cultured for 48 h were used as the model for the anti-biofilm experiments. The biofilms were treated with CuP, CeP, CCP in the presence and absence of H2O2 for 24 h, respectively, and the NB and H2O2 treated groups as the control, which were then collected and quantified utilizing crystal violet staining. As shown in Fig. 6A-C, no significant difference was detected among the CuP and CeP treated groups regardless of the H2O2 assistance compared to the control groups, indicating a negligible effect in removing MRSA biofilms. Conversely, with the increase of concentrations, the dark violet in both CCP and CCP + H2O2 treated groups progressively became nearly colorless. Correspondingly, the biofilm eradication efficacy reached about 80% after treated by 60 µg·mL− 1 (2×MIC) CCP with and without H2O2. This considerable biofilm eradication efficacy could be attributed to the weak acidic, H2O2-and GSH-rich biofilm microenvironment [34] for enhancing the POD- and GPx-like activities of CCP, in turn defeating the stubborn biofilms. Following that, the MRSA biofilms upon different treatments were stained with SYTO9 and then visualized by 3D CLSM (Fig. 6E). Obviously, the biofilms exposed to NB, H2O2, CuP, CuP + H2O2, CeP and CeP + H2O2 remained dense and intact 3D structure with strong green fluorescence, verifying that CuP and CeP possessed no anti-biofilm activity. In contrast, after treatment with 15 µg·mL− 1 CCP, the biofilm collapse occurred, which was exacerbated in the presence of H2O2. When the treated concentration increased to 30 µg·mL− 1, the 3D matrix of biofilms became sparse with a substantial reduction in thickness, and they were almost wiped out at 60 µg·mL− 1 CCP with and without H2O2. These results demonstrated the superior synergistic effect of Cu/Ce active sites for improving the biofilm eradication ability of CCP.

The wheat germ agglutinin (WGA) staining assay [35] was applied to evaluate the impact of CCP on the EPS of MRSA biofilms. Unexpectedly, CuP and CuP + H2O2 significantly promoted growth of EPS (~307% at 120 µg·mL− 1 without H2O2, Fig. 6F), suggesting that single Cu ion system was more prone to serve as nutrient substance for EPS growth; while CeP and CeP + H2O2 hardly showed disruption capability against EPS (Fig. 6G). Whereas, the EPS content decreased to about 63% with the treatment of 30–90 µg·mL− 1 CCP, and reduced much more obviously upon cooperation with H2O2, suggesting that the addition of H2O2 could raised CCP’s POD catalysis for destroying the EPS structure. This finding confirmed the higher scavenging capacity of Cu/Ce codoped CCP against EPS. Further, the SEM characterization (Fig. 6I and Fig. S13) revealed that the biofilms in NB, H2O2, CuP, CuP + H2O2, CeP and CeP + H2O2 treated groups showed dense structure and healthy MRSA with smooth surfaces, where CuP and CuP + H2O2 treated groups exhibited substantially visible EPS. However, the nanosheet CCP with and without H2O2 could efficiently blow up MRSA biofilms, and most of the EPS and interior bacteria were damaged and eliminated, resulting in the residual bacteria with cotton-like shape and holes, which could be ascribed to the synergistic effect of multi-enzymatic activities, released Cu/Ce ions, and unique topology structure of CCP. These outcomes confirmed the valid effect of CCP in multidrug-resistant biofilm eradication by destroying EPS and killing interior bacteria.

The above findings demonstrated an interesting fact that single Cu-doped CuP and Ce-doped CeP nanoparticles displayed minor or negative effect on planktonic MRSA and biofilm eradication, but the Cu/Ce codoped CCP with nanoflake structure was able to completely kill planktonic MRSA at low concentration in the presence of H2O2 and exhibited high eradication efficiency toward MRSA biofilms. More importantly, the comparisons between CCP and reported Cu/Ce based nanozymes in Table S3 proved that CCP showed higher antibacterial performance at lower concentration, indicative of the innovative CCP for MRSA eradication.

The bactericidal mechanism of CCP nanozyme

According to the evidence of the pronounced eradication efficiency of CCP against MRSA, its bactericidal mechanism study was needed. At this context, SEM was further employed to inspect the morphological changes of MRSA cells following different treatments. As shown in Fig. 7A, MRSA maintained the spherical morphology characteristic with intact and smooth cell walls before or after treated with H2O2, CeP and CeP + H2O2, and retained their undamaged shape in the state of being wrapped by CuP upon exposure to CuP and CuP + H2O2, indicating the limit bactericidal capabilities of CeP and CuP. By contrast, MRSA displayed morphological deformation with slight wrinkling and variable level of sunken under the treatment of CCP, which suffered seriously damage involving cellular skeleton collapse, rupture and fragment with the assistance of H2O2. This demonstrated that H2O2 promoted CCP to kill MRSA by breaking the cell wall and membrane integrity. On account of the case that the surface zeta potential variation could cause the increased bacterial membrane permeability or destabilization [33], the zeta potential was detected after MRSA incubated with the three materials (Fig. 7C). Compared with the untreated MRSA (~ −17 mV), the surface zeta potentials of CuP and CCP treated MRSA became smaller (~ −23mV), but not occurred in CeP treated MRSA (~ −15 mV). Combine with the SEM result, this finding suggesting that CuP and CCP increased the membrane permeability of MRSA. Moreover, the Cu and Ce contents in MRSA cells quantified by ICP-MS were implemented to evaluate if Cu or Ce ions released from CuP, CeP and CCP could enter MRSA cells. As shown in Fig. 7B, despite of the lower Cu and Ce contents of CCP, MRSA cells treated with CCP and CCP + H2O2 contained more Cu than CuP, CuP + H2O2 treated groups, and comparable Ce relative to CeP and CeP + H2O2 treated groups, in line with the zeta potential analysis. This finding indicated that the presence of Ce promoted the internalization of Cu in MRSA cells, assisting in amplifying the remarkable bactericidal capability of CCP + H2O2 against MRSA. Besides, the assistance of H2O2 for CCP to kill MRSA indicated the synergistic effects of Cu/Ce and multi-enzymatic activities from CCP on enhancing the bactericidal capability. Additionally, a ROS assay was conducted to evaluate the impact of ROS on killing MRSA, using a 2’,7’-dichlorodihydrofluorescein (DCFH-DA) probe (Fig. 7D-E). Surprisingly, regardless of the addition of H2O2, MRSA cells treated with the three samples at the concentration of 30 µg·mL− 1 for 1 h showed much weaker fluorescence and kept little change even after 4 h, compared to the PBS and H2O2 treated groups. Similar phenomenon was detected after incubation with 60 µg·mL− 1 of the samples. This result suggested that the SOD-like and •OH scavenging activities of CCP played an important role in scavenging the excess ROS produced by MRSA and CCP’s POD catalysis in the bactericidal process, revealing the excellent balanced capacity of CCP for POD catalysis and ROS scavenging. It was different from the reported Cu- or Ce-based nanozymes with both oxidase and antioxidase activities that produce sufficient or excessive ROS for killing bacteria.

The superior NPx-like activity of CCP provided potential for destroying bacterial NADH/NAD+ cycle that affected ATP synthesis and electron transport chain, hence the ATP level in MRSA cells after CCP treatment was investigated (Fig. 7F). It was found that the intracellular ATP level was significantly reduced with the treatment of CuP, CuP + H2O2, CCP, and CCP + H2O2, whereas that treated with CeP was nearly unchanged and even increased in the presence of H2O2, reflecting the ATP synthesis blockage because of the NPx-like activity of CuP and CCP. Previous research proved that Cu2+ was able to destroy the respiratory chain complex activity [36]. As shown in Fig. 7G-H, the activity of bacterial respiratory chain complex I in CCP and CCP + H2O2 treated groups was dramatically impaired but did not change or slightly decreased in CuP, CuP + H2O2, CeP and CeP + H2O2 treated groups, suggesting the synergistic effect of Cu/Ce on reducing the complex I activity in electron transport chain. Whereas, the activity of bacterial respiratory chain complex II exhibited subtle variation in CCP treated group, and even increased in CCP + H2O2 and CeP + H2O2 treated group, which was possibly attributed to the synergistic contribution of Ce and H2O2 to relieving the effect of NPx-like activity on the complex II activity. Furthermore, the impact of CCP on DNA damage was explored by gel retardation assay. The result in Fig. 7I illustrated that the plasmid migration could be retarded by CuP + H2O2, CCP, and CCP + H2O2, implying the interaction between plasmid and Cu/Ce ions. Notably, upon incubation with CCP and CCP + H2O2, motion trail but no striped signal of genomic DNA (gDNA) was observed in gel, while almost no migration change happened after treated with CuP, CuP + H2O2, CeP and CeP + H2O2. This finding demonstrated the synergistic attack of Cu/Ce from CCP for degrading the DNA of MRSA, and the DLS experiments in Fig. S14 showed the consistent results.

In brief, with the Cu/Ce synergistic effect on SOD-like, POD-like, NP-like and •OH scavenging activities, the as-prepared CCP exerted excellent bactericidal activity with aid of H2O2. Upon exposure to CCP + H2O2, the POD catalysis might help to destroy MRSA’s cell wall/membrane, while CCP’s NPx-like activity could break the equilibrium of NADH/NAD+ cycle, resulting in the ATP synthesis blockage and respiratory chain disorder, contributing to MRSA's metabolic disturbance. Further, excess Cu/Ce accumulation in MRSA cells could disrupt the Cu metabolic homeostasis and intensify the toxicity to DNA, thus exacerbating the DNA degradation, metabolism dysfunction and difficulty in cell wall/membrane restoration, ultimately leading to the cell death. Importantly, in this bactericidal process, the excess ROS produced by POD catalysis and MRSA could be responded and eliminated by CCP’s SOD-like and •OH scavenging activities, thus leading to the low cellular ROS level. This function could reduce the non-targeting effect of ROS, contributing to the small MBC value against MRSA. Moreover, combined with the POD-like and •OH scavenging activity analyses, it further explained that CCP could not scavenge the •OH generated from POD catalysis immediately and completely, suggesting the potential cascade reaction of POD catalysis and •OH scavenging. Therefore, H2O2 promoted the Cu/Ce synergistic effect for killing MRSA, demonstrating the valid balanced ability of CCP for POD catalysis and ROS scavenging in the bactericidal process. These analyses confirmed the feasibility of the innovative CCP for MRSA eradication.

To get insight into the bactericidal mechanism of CCP + H2O2 against MRSA in molecular level, the RNA sequencing transcriptomic analysis was explored. As displayed in the volcano plots of Fig. 8A, CCP + H2O2 treated MRSA presented 811 differentially expressed genes (GEGs) with 476 genes up-regulated and 335 genes down-regulated, compared to the untreated MRSA (control group). The result of Gene Ontology (GO) annotation analysis exhibited that these DEGs were relevant to biological processes, cellular components and molecular functions (Fig. 8B), which were primarily enriched in carboxylic acid metabolism, amino acid metabolism, protein metabolism and transmembrane transporter activity. Under CCP + H2O2 treatment, the molecular functions and biological processes, such as carboxylic acid transmembrane transporter activity (e.g., brnQ), endonuclease activity (e.g., rnhC), transcription regulator activity (e.g., nrdR) and signal peptide processing (e.g., lepB) were significantly inhibited (Fig. 8C and Fig. S15), suggesting the substantial damage to the MRSA cell membrane, nucleic acid, and protein processing and metabolism, thus breaking the proliferation capability of MRSA.

The DEGs enriched in Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway revealed that CCP + H2O2 prominently affect the substance and energy metabolism of MRSA (Fig. 8D and Fig. S16), such as nucleotide metabolism (pyrimidine and purine), lipid metabolism (Glycerophospholipid and Glycerolipid), protein export, and citrate cycle (TCA cycle). The related gene expressions presented in the heatmap (Fig. 8E) showed that the ABC transporter (e.g. tagH, tagG, and ugpC, closely related to ATP hydrolysis process and transport on cell membrane) was inhibited, implying the negative effect of CCP + H2O2 on the cell membrane and growth of MRSA [37, 38]. Further, the cell wall and peptidoglycan biosynthesis (e.g. clpP, cwrA, mraZ, isaA, and sgtB), and the cell membrane construction and mobility regulatory (glycerophospholipid/glycerolipid metabolism) were obviously disrupted, demonstrating the damage of cell wall/membrane of MRSA, matched with the SEM analysis. Especially, CCP + H2O2 treated MRSA showed a considerable enrichment index but a smaller FDR in TCA cycle pathway. The corresponding genes, including pckA, sucB, sdhA, sucD, sdhB, icd and acnA, were significantly up-regulated, probably associated with the increase of NAD+ regulated by NPx-like activity. Conversely, the genes associated with glycolysis, such as gap, tpiA, pfkA, fdaB and RS06775, were distinctly down-regulated, suggesting the enhanced aerobic respiration in MRSA metabolic pattern, which was beneficial for MRSA to respond to the change of Cu ions. As reported, cuproptosis occurs due to the abnormal accumulation of Cu ions that bind to the lipoylated components in TCA cycle, resulting in the aggregation of lipoic acid acylated enzymes and the damage of iron-sulfur (Fe-S) cluster protein functionality, accompanied by a protein toxicity stress response and consequent cell death [39, 40]. Heatmap analysis (Fig. 8E) revealed an up-regulation for the genes (e.g., copZ) of Cu ion binding, which was corresponding to the Cu ion overloading in MRSA cells, along with the upregulation or downregulation of the genes associated with iron-sulfur (Fe-S) cluster protein (e.g., sufD, sufB, atpE, cysK, lip1 and lip2), indicating the damage of Fe-S cluster protein. Besides, the up-regulation of protein/DNA toxicity stress (e.g., recO, dnaJ and dnaK) indicated that the POD catalysis and excess Cu/Ce ions still caused oxidative damage to the protein/DNA, although CCP possessed pronounced scavenging ability against ROS. These findings suggested that CCP + H2O2 could induced MRSA’s cuproptosis-like death. In addition, the ribosome blockage further indicated the interrupting of protein biosynthesis, failing in replenishing the protein damaged by toxicity stress.

RNA sequencing transcriptomic analysis demonstrated that the metabolic pathway disorder in MRSA was a primary cause for CCP + H2O2 against MRSA, which was further investigated by untargeted metabolomic analysis. As shown in Fig. 9A and 536 metabolite expressions were down-regulated and 319 metabolite expressions were up-regulated in MRSA after treated with CCP + H2O2. In addition to the undefined differential metabolites, another top 3 super classes of differential metabolites were organic acids and derivatives, lipids and lipid-like molecules, and organoheterocyclic compounds (Fig. 9B), indicating the capability of CCP + H2O2 to regulate the amino acid- and cell wall-associated biological processes. The functions of differential metabolites were conditioned to KEGG enrichment annotation analysis. The generated lollipop plot (Fig. 9C) showed the top 20 metabolic pathway, involving ABC transporters, mannose type O-glycan biosynthesis, pyrimidine and purine metabolism, demonstrating the effect of CCP + H2O2 on disturbing the cell wall/membrane and nucleic acid metabolism, in accord with the transcriptomic analysis. Besides, the up-regulation of NADP+ and NAD (Fig. 9D) elucidated the depletion of cellular GSH and NADH by CCP + H2O2, suggesting the damage to redox system and ATP synthesis. Further, the down-regulation of UDPMurNAc (oyl-L-Ala-D-γ-Glu-L-Lys-D-Ala-D-Ala), ADP, ATP, (R)-lipoate and lipoyl-AMP substantiated the interference of CCP + H2O2 to peptidoglycan biosynthesis (related to cell wall), ATP synthesis and lipoyl-protein metabolism (related to TCA cycle). The above results demonstrated the uniformity of transcriptomic, metabolomic, and morphology/ATP/complex activity/DNA damage analyses.

(C) KEGG enrichment of the metabolites in top 20 relevant pathways. (D) Heatmap plot of the annotated differential metabolites (n = 6). (E) Schematic diagram of the antibacterial mechanism of CCP against MRSA

Based on the transcriptomic and metabolomic analysis results, CCP + H2O2 killed MRSA mainly by blocking MRSA membrane construction and cell wall biosynthesis, disrupting TCA cycle and inducing protein toxicity stress for cuproptosis-like death (Fig. 9E). First, gallic acid residual carboxyl groups could interact with the amino groups from MRSA cell wall, which might disturb the cell wall metabolic order. This situation could be deteriorated by CCP’s POD catalysis, increasing cell membrane permeability and arousing copper ion transport pump, thus leading to the excess Cu+/2+ and Ce3/4+ internalization for breaking cellular metabolic homeostasis. The inhibition of peptidoglycan biosynthesis caused the deficiency and accumulation of cell wall associated raw materials, resulting in the failure of cell wall restoring, while the down-regulation of ABC transporter related genes and metabolites brought about the cell membrane damage. Excess cellular Cu2+/Ce4+ promoted the GPx-like activity for oxidizing GSH into GSSG that could increase the bactericidal capability of CCP against MRSA. Simultaneously, the enhancement of TCA cycle for aerobic metabolism could improve the susceptibility of MRSA to copper ions, so that the toxic Cu+/Ce3+ could quickly enter TCA cycle to bind to the protein, leading to the protein aggregation and iron-sulfur (Fe-S) cluster protein loss, followed by the protein toxicity stress. Furthermore, the imbalanced NADH/NAD+ cycle caused by NPx-like activity, insufficient lipoate related components and reduced ribosome activity made MRSA lose the ability to provide energy and channel to supplement the damaged proteins, intensifying MRSA’s cuproptosis-like death. These phenomena jointly triggered the death of MRSA. It was worth mentioning that the favorable SOD-like and •OH scavenging activities of CCP was not able to timely cope with the effect of POD catalysis and Cu/Ce ions on protein toxicity stress, demonstrating the excellent balanced capability of CCP between POD catalysis and ROS scavenging for improving its bactericidal activity.

Biocompatibility

Biosafety is requisite for CCP in antibacterial or antioxidant applications, thus its cytotoxicity to rabbit red blood cells and human immortalized keratinocyte (HaCat) cells were performed by hemolysis experiment and Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay kits. The hemolysis assay (Fig. S17A-B) showed that almost all the red blood cells were settled at the bottom of centrifuge tube and negligible hemolysis (< 1.3%) was detected after treatment with CCP, CuP and CeP in the range of 10–90 µg·mL− 1 for 7.5 h, indicating their superior blood biocompatibility. Additionally, the CCK-8 assay (Fig. S17C) illustrated that the three materials exhibited good cell biocompatibility. For instance, approximately 95% HaCat cells were alive upon incubation with 70 µg·mL− 1 CCP for 24 h, while most of the MRSA biofilms were eradicated at 60 µg·mL− 1 (Fig. 6) and the planktonic bacteria could hardly survive at 30 µg·mL− 1 with the aid of H2O2 (Fig. 5). These results affirmed the good biosecurity of CCP.

Potential application

With the synergistic effect of Cu/Ce active sites, CCP exhibited a high level of killing multidrug-resistant bacteria and their biofilms, as well as a remarkable antioxidant capability for scavenging ROS and RNS. This nanozyme is biocompatible, facile synthesized and scalable. With these attractive characteristics, CCP could find potentials in antimicrobial and antioxidant applications, such as food preservative film. In this study, a feasible and simple strategy was demonstrated for making antibacterial and antioxidant films by integrating CCP into nanocellulose (NCS) and polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) matrix (CP) to produce CCP films. These films could inhibit bacteria soaring and retard food spoilage, showing great potential in food preservation.

The plump body, high protein and moderate moisture of shrimps make them as ideal targets for microbial growth and spoilage, thereby, the preservation performance of CCP films was evaluated through comparing the food preservation indexes of weightlessness loss rate, pH, TVB-N, TVC and the sensory quality, where the shrimps packed without CP film as the control group. As displayed in Fig. 10, all the shrimp samples showed a downward trend in freshness indicator as the storage time went on at 20 °C. Figure 10A depicted that at onset time (0 h) of the experiment, the fresh shrimps were caesious, glossy, plump and flexible, with intact tissue. However, by 24 h, stains occurred at the shell along abdomen, cephalothorax and claws of shrimps, and even turn reddish due to the action of polyphenol oxidase in control (CK) group [41], followed by an unpleasant fishy odor. When the storage time arrived at 36 h, the shrimps in both CK and CP groups turned reddish brown and darkened across the head and spine, with a stronger putrid odor, which was a significant indicator for shrimp spoilage evaluation. Meanwhile, their bodies lose luster, being hardened and contracted. By contrast, the shrimps packed with CP-0.073% CCP film and CP-0.145% CCP film maintained in a better state. By 48 h, all groups except the CP-0.145% CCP film group continued exhibiting visibly spoilage, including reddish brown body with uneven colors, no elasticity, and unbearable odor, while the symptoms of shrimps in CP-0.145% CCP film group were slightly better, confirming the vital role of CCP in prolonging the shelf-life of shrimp preservation.

Application of CCP film for shrimp storage in 48 h at 20 °C. (A) Photographs of shrimp package changes during the storage time in different films. (B) Weight loss, (C) pH, (D) TVB-N, and (E) TBC changes during the storage time in different films

The ability of blocking water was a critical criterion for assessing the capacity of food preservation [42]. Generally, bacterial spoilage would accelerate the nutrient content loss, such as water, leading to the weightlessness loss and body stiff for shrimp. As shown in Fig. 10B, the weight loss of shrimps in CK and CP groups increased from ~13% to ~25%, while those in CCP film groups kept nearly a constant of ~13% during the whole storage process, demonstrating that CCP could efficiently slow down the erosion of bacteria to shrimps and their moisture loss. As reported, the pH value was an important indicator of meat quality and freshness in the storage time [43]. However, the pH values in all the experiment groups fluctuated around 7.0 (Fig. 10C), signifying the weightlessness loss and bacterial spoilage were not able to obviously alter the pH value of shell-based shrimp during the storage time. This phenomenon was probably attributed to the protection of “double shell” (shrimp shell and film) against environment effect on shrimp meat.

Seafood spoilage was often accompanied by the production of TVB-N (such as amines and ammonia) from protein decomposed by microorganisms [44]. As illustrated in Fig. 10D, the TVB-N contents in CK and CP groups increased after 24 h storage and reached approximately 19 mg·100− 1g− 1 at 48 h; whereas, that in CP-0.073% CCP film group grew after 36 h, lower than 12 mg·100− 1g− 1 by 48 h, which was in accordance with the sensory and weightlessness loss results. Specially, CP-0.145% CCP film group showed abnormal change toward TVB-N content. Its content was similar to that in CK group at 36 h, but remained below 12 mg·100− 1g− 1 by extended 12 h, which may be ascribed to catalytic effect from CCP nanozyme. These results indicated that CCP film was capable of prolonging the shelf-life of shrimp by 12–24 h.

Food, such as shrimp, was a favorite nutrient source for microorganism growth due to its abundant proteins, saccharides and moisture. Besides, food spoilage was always associated with the fast growth of bacteria [45], thus, the total viable counts (TVC) of bacteria in shrimp during storage needed to be measured. As shown in Fig. 10E, the TVC of fresh shrimp was 4.02-lg CFU·g− 1. With the increment of storage time, TVC in all groups increased, where CK (6.67-lg CFU·g− 1) and CP (6.47-lg CFU·g− 1) groups reach over 6.0-lg CFU·g− 1 (spoilage threshold) by 36 h, while CCP film groups exhibited about 5.4-lg CFU·g− 1, indicating that the shrimps in CK and CP group had started decaying in 36 h. By 48 h, the TVC in CP-0.073% CCP film group increased to 5.87 (approximately 6.0)-lg CFU·g− 1, suggesting the shrimps was about to get into the rotting stage. Nevertheless, TVC in CP-0.145% CCP film group was 5.39-lg CFU·g− 1, proving that the increase of CCP amount in film could improve the antibacterial ability of CCP film. Hence, CCP film was able to prolong the shelf-life of shrimp.

In brief, the fresh shrimp packaged in CCP film could prevent it from spoilage for over 48 h at 20 °C, exceeding the shelf-life of CP film group of below 36 h. In addition, the experimental result of ion release (Table S6) of CCP film revealed that the release rates of Cu (< 5.6%) and Ce (< 11.3%) in water were considerably low, and the cytotoxicity result of CCP film indicated its high biocompatibility. These findings demonstrated the great potential of CCP in food preservation application.

Conclusion

In summary, a dual-metal doped nanozyme CCP with multi-enzymatic activities, favorable biocompatibility and outstanding antibacterial performance was successfully prepared by a simple one-pot self-assembly strategy of Cu2+, Ce3+ and gallic acid. As-prepared CCP nanosystem featured with SOD-like and •OH scavenging activities for ROS elimination while with POD-like activity for ROS production, exhibiting the balanced ability between POD catalysis and ROS scavenging. According to the experimental investigations and DFT calculations, it demonstrated that Cu/Ce codoping endowed CCP with improved SOD-, POD- and NPx-like activities, in comparison with single Cu-doped CuP and Ce-doped CeP. Systematic in vitro experiments indicated that the drug-free and negatively charged CCP not only achieved an impressively low bactericidal concentration of about 30 µg·mL− 1 for completely killing MRSA in combination with H2O2, but also presented about 80% eradication rate against MRSA biofilms through destroying EPS and interior bacteria at 60 µg·mL− 1. Transcriptomics and metabolomics revealed that the high bactericidal activity of CCP was mainly caused by the synergistic effect of Cu/Ce active sites on impeding cell membrane construction and cell wall biosynthesis, blocking ATP and protein syntheses, promoting TCA cycle to react with the excess toxic Cu+/Ce3+ for inducing cuproptosis-like death. Further food preservation study revealed that CCP acting as film also exhibited superior antibacterial and antioxidant capability at a rather low content. Overall, this study provided helpful insights to rationally design multi-enzymatic Cu-based nanoplatform with pronounce eradication capability against multi-drug resistant bacteria. Although CCP exhibited great potential in food preservation application, but the safety effect and mechanism of its film still needed to be further explored.
