ABSTRACT
Background and Purpose: Independent dose calculation (IDC) and log file-based patient-specific quality assurance (PSQA) are increasingly used in scanned particle therapy (PT) to address pitfalls of phantom-based verifications. Highly modulated treatment fields, steep dose gradients, and growing interest for adaptive treatments require quality assurance (QA) approaches that are cost effective, patient-specific, and closely linked to the delivered treatment. Practical guidance is therefore needed for the safe clinical implementation of IDC- and log file-based PSQA.Materials and Methods: The ESTRO-EPTN (European Particle Therapy Network) developed consensus-based recommendations on IDC- and log file-based PSQA in PT. Current clinical practice, commissioning strategies, risk-management approaches, and available technical solutions were evaluated. Following a review of the literature, expert-based guidelines were developed covering clinical scopes, acceptance, commissioning and validation, integration with machine QA, data management, governance, and staged implementation.Results: IDC- and log file-based PSQA were identified as complementary elements within a quality-management framework. Log file-based approaches enable evaluation of the delivered treatment fraction(s), while IDC provides computational verification of the planned dose. When appropriately validated, these methods increase sensitivity to potentially clinically relevant deviations and can substantially reduce reliance on phantom-based measurements. Safe application requires robust machine QA, characterization of model limitations, risk analysis, and data traceability. Recommendations applicable to all centers were developed for IDC. Log file-based PSQA recommendations include minimum requirements for the vendors.Conclusions: These recommendations aim to promote consistent and safe adoption across PT centers, while preserving the need for machine QA.

Introduction

Patient-specific quality assurance (PSQA) in radiotherapy is a workflow-integrated, risk-informed process encompassing technical verification, data integrity, human procedures, and continuous improvement [1]. Traditionally, phantom-based PSQA verifies radiation treatment fields to assess beam delivery, transfer from the treatment planning system (TPS) to the machine, and TPS dose calculation [2]. However, there is evidence [3] that phantom-based PSQA may not adequately detect treatment errors for advanced and modulated delivery methods such as intensity modulated radiotherapy (IMRT, [4]) and intensity modulated particle therapy (IMPT, [5]). In addition, efficiency-driven shortcuts in measurement-based PSQA, such as the use of fixed gantry angles, plastic phantoms, low resolution detectors and permissive gamma criteria [6] undermine the value of physical measurements. Phantom-based PSQA is also a resource intensive component of the workflow [2] and is not applicable in online-adaptive PT (OAPT) workflows. In this setting, pre-delivery QA must rely on fast, measurement-less approaches, primarily independent dose calculation (IDC). A further specificity of OAPT is that a pre-treatment dummy irradiation cannot usually be performed; therefore, log-file-based verification is generally shifted to a post-delivery offline step, where the dose implied by the recorded delivery can be reconstructed on suitable daily images [7], [8]. Therefore, phantom-less PSQA methods with complementary failure-mode-dependent sensitivities, such as IDC and log file-based PSQA, have been proposed. These two methods are discussed in more detail in Section 3 and 4, but can be briefly defined as follows: IDC consists in forward dose re-computation of a treatment plan with an independent algorithm, with the purpose to check the clinically approved dose distribution from the TPS; log file-based QA evaluates recorded delivery parameters against the intended treatment, optionally including dose reconstruction (log file-based IDC). These methods are not exclusive and may be used in addition to existing phantom-based QA methods. The specific workflows and recommendations for PSQA in OAPT are beyond the scope of the present guideline. State of the art

Independent dose calculation

Varieties of IDC systems have been developed, sometimes using analytical dose algorithms [9], [10], but mostly Monte Carlo (MC)-based: either using general-purpose MC codes such as Geant4 and FLUKA [11], [12], [13], [14] or fast dedicated MC codes [15], [16], [17], [18], [19]. MC-based IDC was found to illuminate dose errors from analytical and MC TPS algorithms that traditional phantom-based PSQA would not detect [14], [19], [20]. IDC provides information on the full 3D dose distribution at the voxel level. To supplement the usage of gamma index (similar to photons), review of Dose-Volume Histograms (DVH) and 3D dose distributions has been suggested [21], [22].

Log files

The poor sensitivity of traditional phantom-based PSQA for PT was highlighted in several studies [5], [23]. Log file-based IDC was used to evaluate beam delivery uncertainties on a fraction basis [21], [24] and identified ‘skipped spots’ due to technical limitations in the delivery of low-weighted spots [21]. In [25], the log file records of plan-relevant spot parameters were found to be well reproducible over multiple fractions. The information available in log files and their uncertainties should also be considered. In [26], a model accounting for log file uncertainties for a discrete spot-scanning system was implemented: while the initial log file-based IDC showed significant deviations from the reference planned dose, the corrected log file-based IDC was in much closer agreement, thus demonstrating the limitations of raw logs. For quasi-discrete spot scanning (sometimes called raster scanning), log file uncertainties have been reported to limit the determination of beam delivery parameters [10], [27]. Some discrepancies were due to the transient dose delivered in-between spots, while others were simply due to the approximate algorithm used to determine spot positions. Finally, the time-resolved capabilities of log files may be one of the most valuable inputs for evaluating the interplay effect between tumor motion and scanned ion beam delivery [28], [29], [30].

There is currently one medical product, namely MyQAiON (IBA Dosimetry, Schwarzenbruck, Germany), supporting scanned proton and carbon ion beam IDC, as well as log file data analysis for certain types of machines. The IDC software part relies on open-source MC systems: MCsquare [31] and IDEAL [14] (based on GATE-RTion/Geant4 [13]).

Independent dose calculation

Level of independence

Following [32], the terminologies “independent” and “secondary” are considered equivalent. In this report for the purpose of PSQA, the terminology “Independent Dose Calculation (IDC)” is used and means a stand-alone secondary dose calculation system (independent from the TPS). As proposed in [9], the level of independence of secondary dose calculation can vary. The highest degree of independence is recommended and achieved when a separate software platform is employed, implementing alternative physics models and calculation methods, and operating entirely independently from the TPS. In addition, different input base data can be used: beam modeling data (beam optics, range, dose) and CT calibration curves (CT-number-to-material properties calibration). However, this is not necessarily recommended for clinical implementation, if the input data has already been validated independently, e.g., via QA procedures. We recommend that dose evaluation tools are also independent from the TPS.

Numerous additional factors can impact the calculated dose, e.g., beam model tuning, dose algorithm, calculation grid, relative biological effectiveness (RBE) models and input tables, etc. For MC algorithms, further settings could vary, e.g., statistical uncertainty, type of dose scoring (to medium or water), physics settings and computation parameters (cut-off energy threshold, step-size, etc.). Hence, the IDC system must undergo full dosimetric commissioning (Supplementary Material B).

Dose computation algorithm

The MC method is considered to be the gold standard calculation and therefore recommended over analytical algorithms such as pencil beam (PB) algorithms in patient anatomy [33], [34], [35]. Commissioning work of the IDC must be performed to understand the accuracy and limitations of the algorithm against reference measurements [34], [36], [37], [38]. The dose output may be specified as an absorbed dose or an RBE-weighted dose according to ICRU (corresponding to physical and effective dose in the Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine (DICOM) standard). For MC algorithms, dose scoring may be dose-to-medium or dose-to-water [3], [19], [39]. For IDC-based PSQA, it is recommended to use the same type of dose output as clinically implemented in the TPS, to ease the comparison with TPS. For light ion beams (including protons), the same RBE models are also required to allow for RBE-weighted dose comparisons. Fast, dedicated MC codes are preferred, such that IDC results are available within a few minutes or even within a few seconds when ultra-fast solutions (e.g., using Graphics Processing Unit (GPU)) are used [16], [19]. Such fast (or ultra-fast codes) are also suitable for on-line treatment adaptation.

Phantom and patient geometry

IDC in water (or homogeneous geometry) allows direct comparison with measurements on a beam per beam basis [40], and can serve as a first step away from phantom-based PSQA [41]. Compared to phantom-based PSQA, it allows evaluation of the full 3D dose distribution of each beam and is not limited to a subset of measurement points. However, it remains difficult to relate dose deviations to changes in target and organ doses.

Using patient CT geometry allows the TPS algorithm to be evaluated not only in water, but also including patient and support geometry modeling, dealing with the effect of biological tissue heterogeneities and interfaces. IDC in patient geometry is therefore recommended, but careful commissioning must be considered (Supplementary Material B). Considerations for the evaluation of IDC against TPS doses are discussed in Section 3.5.

TPS treatment plan and machine log files

To evaluate the TPS dose calculation alone, the treatment plan should be exported from the TPS and used as the input to the IDC. Alternatively, machine log files can be used as the input, but in that case, the evaluation will also include the effects of beam delivery and log file uncertainties [15], [21], [26] (Section 4). Unfortunately, access to log files requires either a dry run or waiting for the patient's first treatment fraction. In addition, log files are not accessible to the user for some systems. We therefore recommend the DICOM treatment plan-based IDC workflow as a first QA step.

IDC-to-TPS comparison metrics

Dose difference metrics and gamma analysis methodology: For the comparison of dose distributions, a straightforward first step is to evaluate the dose difference. However, in high gradient dose regions or in low dose regions, large relative dose differences may occur without being clinically meaningful. Gamma analysis [42] can be used to address dose differences in regions with a high dose gradient. These dose differences can be caused by discrepancies between the IDC and the TPS in the modeling of the beam, scattering, stopping powers, the external volume, regions of interest (ROI), structures, etc. Normalizing the dose difference to the maximum or prescribed dose rather than the local dose enables large relative dose differences in low dose regions to be dealt with. However, there is a lack of correlation between gamma index passing rate in phantom geometry (for phantom-based PSQA) and dose differences in anatomic ROIs [3], [43]. Similar results are to be expected for IDC in phantom geometry, which is why IDC in patient geometry is recommended [15], [19]. Evaluation of dose distributions by 3D gamma analysis offers advantages over 2D gamma analysis, as it directly compares dose points in three-dimensional space and is therefore recommended.

Selection of gamma criteria and statistical considerations: The gamma index calculation is sensitive to noise, resolution, the choice of the reference dose, interpolation methods, etc. [44], [45]. Particular attention should be paid to the choice of distance-to-agreement (DTA) criterion and the dose spatial resolution used in the gamma analysis. According to AAPM-TG218 report: “the resolution of the evaluated dose should be not greater than one-third of the DTA criterion. Interpolation can be used to satisfy this rule.” [46]. Regarding the dose-difference (DD) criterion, it should be related to the statistical uncertainty of the MC simulation. Larger statistical uncertainty may lead to higher or lower passing rates, depending on whether the MC algorithm is used as comparison or reference dose map, respectively [45]. We recommend a DD equal to or larger than 3 standard deviations: e.g., if DD of 3% is requested, combined statistical uncertainty from TPS and IDC algorithms should be lower than about 1%. Statistical uncertainties may be reported differently for different TPS and IDC tools. Overall, recommendations about gamma index computation as provided in [46] report apply. For proton PBS, these recommendations are consistent with the framework used in AAPM TG-350 (under review), which employs similar gamma analysis criteria (e.g., 3%/3 mm with a 10% low-dose threshold) in multi-institution benchmarking exercises. In practice, the DTA criterion can be defined as treatment site-dependent, e.g., with ~2 mm for small targets with steep gradients (e.g., peri-ocular, stereotactic) and ~ 3 mm for larger or more complex volumes (e.g., pelvis, thorax, head-and-neck). The conventional 3%/3 mm (global, 10% threshold) remains the initial reference, while 3%/2 mm or 2%/3 mm may be applied depending on whether spatial or dosimetric accuracy is prioritized [46]. Stricter thresholds, such as 2%/2 mm, could be applied to high-precision applications. The use of non-zero DTA parameter is justified in high gradient dose regions and related to the implementation of different beam models (incl. Inherent beam modeling uncertainties) and different algorithms between the TPS and IDC. For example: 1) a systematic 1% difference in stopping power between IDC and TPS for a 30 cm range beam would lead to a 3 mm range difference; 2) difference in multiple Coulomb scattering algorithms will lead to different transverse dose profiles; 3) different scoring resolution will inevitably impact dose gradient and volume averaging effects longitudinally and transversally. Additionally, if one compares a PB algorithm-based TPS against a MC simulation-based IDC, substantial variations can be expected in terms of transverse and longitudinal dose profiles (in particular in the dose gradient regions), in the vicinity of heterogeneities, as well as in the presence of range shifter in the beam path. The DTA and DD parameters should be defined during the commissioning process, considering the TPS and IDC implementation at the user facility (beam modeling, algorithms used, treatment sites, scoring resolution, etc.). Global normalization should be used as it is more clinically relevant than local normalization: local normalization is more stringent and can be used during commissioning and for troubleshooting. The dose threshold should be set to exclude low-dose areas that have no or little clinical relevance (e.g. 10%) and the gamma distribution should be carefully reviewed. The review of gamma results should not be limited to only the percentage of points that fail, but should include other relevant gamma values (maximum, mean, minimum, median). The gamma analysis should be reviewed on a structure-by-structure basis if the software allows for it. Examples of gamma index sensitivity analysis using different parameters for two algorithms are presented in Supplementary Material C. In addition, the importance of a gamma analysis review in the PSQA process is highlighted for selected cases.

Clinical interpretation of IDC: Treatment plan-based IDC is recommended over field-by-field analysis, as it is more clinically relevant. Field-by-field analysis can be used to reveal more subtle field specific differences during commissioning or for troubleshooting. Eventually, we recommend that gamma analysis should not be used as a stand-alone evaluation. Indeed, for small tumor volumes, dose differences outside relevant ROIs may impact the overall passing rate significantly, while on the other hand for large tumor volumes, clinically relevant hot/cold spots within a ROI may not [24]. Therefore, IDC evaluation should always be complemented by a review of the 3D dose distributions, and by clinically relevant parameters such as DVHs and clinical goals [21], [47].

IDC results in patient geometry should be reviewed first by the medical physicist and differences should be interpreted in the context of commissioning results, especially since small numerical discrepancies (arising from different algorithms, grid interpolation, or ROI boundaries handling) may cause clinical goals that were within tolerance in the TPS to appear out of tolerance in the IDC. For this reason, it is advisable to establish a statistical tolerance margin in advance for clinical goals when evaluating IDC results (Supplementary Material B). This avoids re-optimizing the plan based on IDC only and maintains the role of IDC as a verification tool. Nevertheless, in some specific cases, if dose differences are too large (despite being understood) and potentially clinically relevant, it may be advisable to involve the responsible physician in the decision-making process (e.g., when a TPS with an analytical dose engine with limited accuracy is used and evaluated against a MC-based IDC of higher accuracy [20]).

Log file-based PSQA

Introduction of log files

With a robust PSQA program in place, log file-based PSQA has been shown to detect errors that conventional phantom-based PSQA could not [5]. In addition to supporting pre-treatment plan verification, log file-based methods enable fraction-resolved QA, trend monitoring, and, in systems providing temporal information, assessment of interplay effects in moving targets [28], [29]. Patient Positioning System (PPS) log data may further enhance motion management and geometric QA [48], [49]. As mentioned earlier (Section 1 and 2), log file-based monitoring has potential to detect anomalies that arise post-PSQA, such as data corruption, control system faults, or unintended software behavior, particularly in cases where machine safety interlocks are not triggered [21], [50].

Treatment delivery log files are generated and stored by the beam delivery system after each irradiation, providing time-resolved records of key delivery parameters such as spot position and monitor units (MU). The structure, availability, and reliability of this data, however, vary significantly depending on the vendor, beam monitoring system, machine configuration, and export format. A rigorous understanding of which parameters are directly measured, algorithmically derived, or merely reported from the plan is essential before usage for QA.

Comparing log file data sources in PSQA

Log file data can be sourced from the DICOM RT Ion Beams Treatment Record, typically exported by the record and verify (R&V) system, which offers a standardized summary of delivered MU per spot. It sometimes lacks actual measured quantities such as spot position and/or size, in which case planned values are reported. In contrast, raw log file data from vendor-specific treatment delivery systems may include high-frequency, time-stamped measurements of spot MU and position, typically sampled at sub-millisecond intervals. These raw log files, when accessible, may offer superior granularity and completeness. In some cases, when raw log file data are not made available to the user and planned values are reported to the OIS, then the user cannot effectively access log file data for QA purposes.

Considerations before using log file data for PSQA

Log files require dedicated commissioning, as their quality depends on beam monitor resolution, calibration protocols, network communication and reconstruction algorithms [10], [18]. Due to the machine-specific nature of log file data formats and the inherent limitations of log files for each system, a dedicated QA program must be established. For example, some beam-monitoring systems self-regulate the beam delivery. In this case, log files confirm functional behavior rather than providing independent verification, limiting their ability to detect latent errors [10]. Additionally, critical delivery parameters such as beam energy, gantry angle, snout position, and passive-element settings are often absent from log files or assumed from the treatment plan. Even when some log files provide information on spot size, it often suffers substantial uncertainties [10], [18]. Therefore, when using log file data for PSQA, thorough commissioning is a pre-requisite, followed by complementary machine QA checks for these parameters before usage, which could be incorporated into routine daily or weekly QA. Log file data can be used either to verify delivery consistency and machine performance or for dose reconstruction (Section 3.4). If a 3D dose reconstruction workflow is chosen, it requires more comprehensive datasets and validation protocols, including not only commissioning of log files, but also of IDC system (Supplementary Material B). Furthermore, the 3D dose reconstruction may require specific correction methodologies to mitigate systematic uncertainties of spot position and MU in log files [26].

While reliable log file usage was demonstrated for some machines, the variations in the treatment log file data availability and quality across different particle therapy systems are still very large, thus preventing the development of generic recommendations applicable to all centers for log-based PSQA. Indeed, some users have access to high-quality log files, but a significant number either lack access to log files entirely or only have access to low-quality data. Although the guideline provides guidance for implementing log file-based PSQA, it is not possible to recommend this approach universally for all users. Therefore, requirements for the vendors are proposed in Supplementary Material H.

Clinical implementation of phantom-less PSQA

Pre-requisites

Clinical integration of phantom-less PSQA systems requires acceptance testing and commissioning (Supplementary Materials A and B). Supportive practical examples are provided in Supplementary Materials C and D. Before reducing or replacing a PSQA procedure, centers should document a prospective multidisciplinary workflow risk assessment using process-based FMEA or an equivalent method [1], [51] (Supplementary Material E). In addition to safety and risk management, external audit and regulatory aspects must be considered (Supplementary Materials F and G).

Examples and recommendations

This subsection first reviews existing phantom-less PSQA methods and then develops the recommendations presented in this guideline.

A combination of phantom-based PSQA and fluence review based on log files was proposed in [52]. In [53], part of the phantom-based PSQA measurements was replaced by a PB algorithm-based IDC and treatment delivery log file was evaluated. The authors highlighted the need to include additional QA of the log files, due to their dependence on the machine. A combination of patient-specific and patient-independent QA was proposed in [20], leading to a reduction of phantom-based PSQA, but including IDC in patient geometry and log files evaluation. In [54], log file-based QA of the MLC has been added as an additional sanity check to the phantom-based PSQA. A workflow including IDC in water and log file review at first fraction was proposed in [27]. Based on the outcome of the previous two tests, additional phantom-based PSQA and log file-based IDC could be requested. The importance of phantom-based PSQA experience was highlighted in [18]: it advocates the substitution of phantom-based PSQA with IDC for treatment sites which were sufficiently and successfully passing the QA in the past. Similarly, in [22], IDC was introduced to substitute phantom-based PSQA based on the phantom-based PSQA experience of more than 2700 plans and in [19] for more than 6000 plans. A strong argument from [22] supporting the transition from phantom-based PSQA to IDC was to rely on a comprehensive beam delivery QA program and the establishment of additional monthly QA procedures to compensate for the lack of PSQA-like measurements. IDC was introduced stepwise over one year and used to replace phantom-based PSQA in 90% of the cases.

Based on current experience [2], beam delivery QA and phantom-based PSQA should be established as the first QA pillars for any new facility and/or treatment localization. Measurements are very useful in the initial phase of a new facility, as they provide insight into system performance. Therefore, it is common to have an intensive QA program at the beginning and reduce QA test frequencies based on experience over time. As every patient treatment is unique, neither commissioning nor a machine-specific QA program can establish coverage of all clinically relevant failures. Therefore, it is recommended that phantom-based PSQA is used as a starting point. However, due to the limited sensitivity of phantom-based PSQA methods, the implementation of a seamless treatment plan–based IDC workflow is expected to further increase the level of safety. A MC-based IDC solution in patient geometry is recommended against IDC in phantom. We encourage users to implement phantom-less PSQA in parallel to gain experience, while the facility is not yet operating at full capacity. This will support staff training and interpretation of discrepancies. As discussed in Supplementary Material E, different types of QA allow for catching different types of errors. Over time, as experience increases, the workload of phantom-based PSQA should reduce and transition to more phantom-less PSQA methods as treatment capacity increases. Substitution of phantom-based PSQA with IDC can be established progressively, but should be based on experience at the user facility and risk analyses (Supplementary Material E). Measurement frequency should therefore be reduced only within a documented, locally validated clinical scope, following the prospective risk assessment specified in Section 5.1. The replacement pathway must retain effective control of calculation, data-transfer, and delivery failures, including those not observable by IDC or machine log files. Beam delivery QA and phantom-based PSQA must demonstrate stability. We recommend a pragmatic threshold for implementation of minimum of 6 months and 100 patients of phantom-based PSQA experience and the implementation of extra beam delivery QA tests, such as monthly PSQA-like consistency tests, as well as representative random or triggered patient-specific measurements, covering the range of clinical indication at the facility. Replacing phantom-based PSQA with IDC facilitates treatment adaptation workflows. Due to the vendor-dependent availability and quality of log files, it is not possible to provide generic recommendations for the usage of log files. Nevertheless, a log file-based IDC can supplement plan-based IDC, provided that the completeness, accuracy, origin, and uncertainty of the logs have been validated. As stated in Section 4, log files enable daily treatment fraction monitoring and time-dependent QA capabilities, serving an important element of PSQA, that is beyond one-time pre-treatment PSQA (Table 1). Obviously, any method implemented requires resources for commissioning, QA, and further maintenance. Safety and risk management as presented in Supplementary Material E must be considered and guide the clinical implementation. The overall list of recommendations is summarized in Supplementary Material I.

Summary and recommendations

Phantom-less PSQA methods, consisting of IDC and log file-based PSQA, have emerged as key components of a modern dose verification strategy in PT. Their development enables a substantial reduction in PSQA workload while addressing well-recognized limitations of traditional phantom-based measurements, particularly in the presence of steep dose gradients, highly modulated fields, and adaptive workflows. These approaches enable verification strategies that are more closely linked to the delivered treatment, more scalable in routine practice, and better aligned with contemporary clinical and technical capabilities.

Due to the vendor- and system-specific nature of treatment log file data, it is essential to evaluate data completeness, accuracy and uncertainty locally. The validity of log file-based QA depends directly on availability, accurate monitoring and reliable recording of machine parameters and output. Generic log file-based QA strategies may not be applicable universally but strongly depend on the characteristics of the specific PT delivery system. As a result, this report led to the generation of minimum log file requirements for the vendors. Nevertheless, log file-based PSQA has the potential for treatment errors to be caught, that phantom-based PSQA and IDC, cannot detect (file corruption, file transfer or control system bugs). In several European countries, IDC alone is not sufficient to meet local regulations, and an additional PSQA element addressing delivery and data consistency is mandatory. Using independently commissioned and validated dose calculation algorithms, IDC offers external verification of the clinical TPS and provides a complementary assessment of the planned dose. MC-based IDC in patient geometry is preferred, as it provides the highest accuracy in clinically relevant conditions. In suitable workflows, the recalculation of the dose implied by the recorded delivery (log file) in patient geometry can be provided by the IDC.

A structured, staged, and carefully governed implementation is required for the clinical adoption of IDC and log file-based PSQA. Site-specific commissioning is essential, involving representative treatment sites, realistic plan complexity, and intentional error scenarios. Software restrictions/limitations, sensitivity to different error classes, and relevant sources of uncertainty should be explicitly characterized. Clear criteria for pass–fail decisions and escalation pathways must be defined, documented, and periodically reviewed. The transition from phantom-based PSQA towards phantom-less approaches should be progressive, based on experience, supported by parallel use, outcome monitoring, and staff training.

For new centers or newly introduced treatment indications, the dose verification strategy should initially be based on phantom-based PSQA, with subsequent reductions in measurements being experience-driven and locally justified. A comprehensive machine QA program is therefore a prerequisite for the safe use of phantom-less PSQA methods.

Proactive quality and risk management should guide the selection of methods, clinical approval, and subsequent changes. Core elements of a mature PSQA system include documentation, version control and traceability. Reassessments should result from the analysis of incidents as well as from technological or clinical changes. Independent external audits and consideration of applicable regulatory frameworks, particularly for in-house developed IDC tools, remain complementary safeguards. User expertise is crucial. Automated workflows and advanced algorithms cannot replace professional judgment: medical physicists must interpret deviations within the clinical context and determine when additional verification, including measurements, is required.

The recommendations presented here support the pragmatic, harmonized, and safe implementation of PSQA based on IDC and log files across particle therapy centers within the European Particle Therapy Network and beyond.
