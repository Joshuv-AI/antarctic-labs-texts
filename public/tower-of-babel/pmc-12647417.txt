African swine fever virus L11L interferes with antiviral responses by targeting the IRF3 and PKR
Authors: D K Haluwana, Ji-Won Cha, Asela Weerawardhana, Nuwan Gamage, Ashan Subasinghe, WeonHwa Jheong, Jong-Soo Lee
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe African Swine Fever Virus (ASFV) is a large, complex DNA virus that infects wild boars and domestic pigs. It causes African Swine Fever (ASF), an intense and often fatal disease with 100% fatality rate. After infection, ASFV employs specific viral proteins to enhance pathogenicity. However, the functions of many of these proteins remain unknown. This study investigated the molecular mechanism by which ASFV L11L evades type I interferon (IFN) pathway and protein kinase R (PKR)-mediated antiviral responses. Interestingly, we found that L11L interacts with interferon regulatory factor 3 (IRF3), specifically with the S396 and S398 amino acids, leading to the inhibition of the phosphorylation of IRF3 by Tank-binding kinase 1 (TBK1). Consequently, this interaction impedes IRF3 dimerization, nuclear translocation and subsequent IFN-β production. Besides, L11L inhibits the dimerization of PKR by targeting its K196 amino acid, preventing the phosphorylation of its substrate, eukaryotic initiation factor-2 (eIF2), to promote viral replication. Altogether, these novel findings conclude that L11L suppresses antiviral immunity and promotes viral replication, implying the identification of a novel target for the advancement of live-attenuated vaccines against ASF.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00018-025-05956-7.Keywords: African swine fever virus, L11L, IRF3, PKR, Type I interferon

Importance

The African Swine Fever Virus is a large, complex DNA virus that infects domestic pigs and wild boars, leading to the development of a severe and frequently fatal condition referred to as African swine fever. This disease is notorious for its extremely high mortality rate, often resulting in the death of 100% of infected animals. Since no effective vaccine is available for ASF, it has become a substantial threat to the pig industry in recent years. The absence of an effective vaccine against ASF has posed a substantial and growing threat to the global pig industry in recent years. L11L is an ASFV protein, and its exact function is unknown. Here, for the first time, we identified that this uncharacterized protein counteracts dual host immune factors to hijack the type I IFN pathway and global translation shutoff in host cells. In accordance with our novel findings, this protein directly targets IRF3 and PKR. These significant discoveries will play a crucial role in advancing our comprehension of ASFV pathogenesis and the development of live attenuated ASFV vaccines.

Introduction

African swine fever (ASF), first identified in Kenya in 1921, is a highly contagious and infectious disease that affects domestic pigs and wild boars. It causes extensive tissue hemorrhagic necrosis and has a nearly 100% mortality rate. African Swine Fever Virus (ASFV; genus Asfavirus), the etiological agent responsible for ASF disease, is the sole member of the Asfaviridae family [1]. ASFV is a large enveloped virus with a double-stranded DNA (dsDNA) genome of 170 to 194 kb that replicates predominantly in the cytoplasm of infected monocytes and macrophages [2]. ASFV produces around 150 to 167 virion proteins during replication, the functions of which include virus replication, virus-host interactions, and immune evasion [3]. Despite extensive research on ASFV, many gaps in our knowledge exist concerning disease pathogenesis and immune evasion, posing a critical challenge to the development of effective vaccines [4, 5].

Type I IFNs exert pleiotropic biological effects during pathogen infection, primarily triggered by the recognition of dsDNA released by invading pathogens via cyclic GMP-AMP synthase (cGAS), a key pattern recognition receptor (PRRs) present in the cytoplasm of cells [6]. After recognition of dsDNA, cGAS synthesizes 2′,3′ cyclic GMP-AMP (2′,3′-cGAMP) and the synthesized 2′,3′-cGAMP then binds to stimulator of interferon genes (STING) on the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) membrane [7, 8]. STING then oligomerizes and traffics to the Golgi apparatus via the ER-Golgi intermediate compartment, where it recruits TANK-binding kinase 1 (TBK1). TBK1 phosphorylates STING, then STING recruits interferon regulatory factor 3 (IRF3) for phosphorylation by TBK1. Phosphorylated IRF3 dimerizes and translocates to the nucleus to trigger the production of type I interferons (IFNs) and subsequent transcription of interferon-stimulatory genes (ISGs), playing a vital role in inducing IFN-mediated antiviral immunity and limiting viral spread [9, 10]. In contrast, activation of the IFN cascade also induces the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines, highlighting the complex nature of antiviral responses to pathogen infection [11].

In addition to type I IFNs, double-stranded RNA-dependent protein kinase R (PKR) is a well-known protein that plays a critical role in antiviral innate immune response. PKR recognizes dsRNA in virus-infected cells and becomes activated through dimerization and subsequent autophosphorylation [12]. This active form of PKR phosphorylates its key substrate, eukaryotic initiation factor-2 (eIF2α), and blocks the translation of viral proteins to limit viral replication. Thus, PKR also plays an important role in restricting DNA and RNA viral replication and spread [13–15].

African Swine Fever Virus is sensitive to Interferons and antiviral proteins [16]. Therefore, it has developed immune evasion mechanisms to suppress antiviral responses, similar to many other viruses [17]. For instance, ASFV proteins DP71L, A137R, and E120R counteract the functions of STING, TBK1, and IRF3, respectively, to inhibit type I IFNs [18–20].

L11L is a late protein produced during ASFV replication that probably encodes an integral membrane protein [21]. Although the exact function and mechanisms of L11L in evading antiviral immune responses are not fully understood, it plays an important role in ASFV virulence [22, 23]. In this study, we investigated the molecular mechanism underlying antiviral response evasion by ASFV L11L and found that L11L inhibits the phosphorylation of IRF3 and dimerization of PKR, thereby impeding the antiviral immune response.

Materials and methods

Cells and antibodies

HEK293T cells (ATCC® CRL11268™), 293-Dual™ hSTING-A162 cells (Invivogen, 293d-a162), PK-15 cells (ATCC® CCL-33), A549 cells (ATCC CCL-185), Vero cells (ATCC® CCL-81™), and MA104 (CRL-2378.1) cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM) (Cytiva), and 3D4/21 cells (ATCC® CRL¬2843™) were cultured in Roswell Park Memorial Institute medium (RPMI) (Cytiva). The medium was supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco) and 1% antibiotic/antimycotic (Gibco). Porcine alveolar macrophages (PAMs) (Optipharm Inc., Cheongju, Republic of Korea) were cultured in RPMI medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% Penicillin-Streptomycin (Gibco™). The cells were maintained in a humidified 5% CO2 incubator at 37 °C. The following antibodies were used for immunoblot and immunoprecipitation analysis: anti-Flag (Cell Signaling Technology, 8146), anti-GST (Santa Cruz, sc-138), anti-IRF3 (Cell Signaling Technology, 4302 S), anti-phospho IRF3 (Ser396; Cell Signaling Technology, 4947), anti-phospho IRF3 (Ser386; Cell Signaling Technology, 37829 S), anti-p65 (Cell Signaling Technology, 4764 S), anti-phospho p65 (Cell Signaling Technology, 3031 S), anti-TBK1 (Cell Signaling Technology, 3504 S), anti-phospho-TBK1 (Cell Signaling Technology, 5483 S), anti-phospho IҡBα (Cell Signaling Technology, 2859 S), anti-IҡBα (Cell Signaling Technology, 9242 S) anti-STING (Cell Signaling Technology, 3337 S), anti-PKR (Cell Signaling Technology, 3072 S), anti-phospho PKR (T446; Abcam, ab32036), anti-eIF2α (Cell Signaling Technology, 9722 S), anti-phospho-eIF2α (Cell Signaling Technology, 9721 S), anti-puromycin (EMD Milipore, MABE343), anti-adenovirus Type 5 antibody (Abcam ab6982), anti-β-actin (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, SC 47778), anti-Lamin B1(Santa Cruz Biotechnology, sc-20682), and anti-α tubulin (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, sc-53029).

Plasmids

A plasmid expressing the full-length ASFV L11L gene was cloned into a Flag-tagged pIRES vector and a pEBG vector with a GST tag. A mutation cloning kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, F530S) was used to generate S396D, S398D, S402D, T404D, and S405D point mutants in IRF3, and the K296R point mutant of PKR. The construction of IFN-β and NF-κB promoter-luciferase plasmids has been detailed in a previous study [24]. Plasmids containing Flag-tagged TBK1 and IKKε were produced by amplifying template DNA by PCR and inserting them into the pIRES vector. Strep- and GST-tagged IRF3 and PKR plasmid constructs were obtained by PCR of template DNA and cloned into pEXPR and pEBG vectors, respectively. The integrity of all sequences was verified by sequencing analysis.

Virus infection and plasmid transfection

GFP-expressing adenovirus (ADV-GFP), herpes simplex virus (HSV-GFP), and vaccinia virus (VACV-GFP) were propagated in PK-15 cells and Ceropithecus aethiops epithelial kidney (Vero) cells, respectively, and then titrated by plaque assay. Prior to virus infection, the culture medium was replaced with DMEM or RPMI containing 1% FBS. The virus was then introduced into the target cells at a specific multiplicity of infection (MOI). After a 2-h incubation at 37 °C, the viruses remaining outside the cells were removed and the culture medium was replaced with DMEM or RPMI containing 10% FBS. At specified time intervals, the cells were detached and the supernatants were collected and centrifuged at 3000 RPM for 3 min. The supernatant from each sample was separated from the cell pellet and used for ELISA. The cell pellet was resuspended in 300 µL of phosphate-buffered saline, and the fluorescence of each sample was measured using a fluorometer (Glomax detection system; Promega). Plasmids were transfected into PK-15, 3D4/21, and MA-104 cells using Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen), and into HEK293T and 293-Dual™ hSTING-A162 cells using polyethylenimine (Polyscience Inc.23966). For the ASFV infection experiments, PAM cells were infected with ASFV (Korea/wildboar/Hwacheon/2020–2287) (MOI = 0.5). Cell pellets were harvested at 0, 3, 6, 9, 12, 15, and 18 h post-infection. Subsequently, RNA was extracted and qRT-PCR was performed using primers specific for L11L (Table S2). ASFV I73R (an early gene) and B646L (a late gene) were used as controls.

Measurement of the virus titer

Virus-infected cell pellets and cell culture supernatants were collected at the designated intervals. Virus titers were measured by plaque assay using A549 cells (for ADV-GFP and VACV-GFP) or Vero cells (for HSV-GFP and NDV-GFP). Briefly, a monolayer of A549 or Vero cells was cultured in 12-well plates for 12 h, after which the cells were exposed for 2 h to supernatants containing the virus, which were serially diluted in 1% DMEM. Next, the inoculum was removed and replaced with DMEM containing 0.1% agarose (Sigma-Aldrich). Subsequently, the plates were incubated at 37 °C for an additional 36 h, and then checked for plaque formation under a magnification of 200×. Virus titers were determined by calculating the number of plaque-forming units, taking into account the dilution factor.

ELISA

The amounts of IFNs and pro-inflammatory cytokines secreted into the cell culture supernatants were measured by ELISAs designed to detect human IL-6 (BD OptEIA, 5552220), human interferon-β (CUSABIO, CSB-E09889h), porcine IL-6 (R&D Systems, p6000B), and porcine IFN-β (CUSABIO, CSB-E09890p).

Quantitative real-time PCR

PK-15 or 3D4/21 cells expressing L11L-Flag or Flag-vector (control) were grown at 37 °C in 6-well tissue culture plates (1 × 106 cells/well). The cells were infected with ADV-GFP (MOI = 1.0) and harvested at 0, 12, and 24 h post-infection. Total RNA was extracted from the cells using the Machery Nagel Nucleospin RNA kit (790955.250), and cDNA was synthesized using reverse transcriptase (Toyobo) and then measured by quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) using the Smart Gene SYBR Green Q-PCR master mix (SG.SYBR.500) kit. The sequences of the primers used for qRT-PCR are listed in Table S1.

Immunoprecipitation of proteins

HEK293T cells were transfected with plasmids and harvested at 36 h post-transfection. Whole-cell lysates (WCL) were acquired by lysing the cells using lysis buffer containing radio-immunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) buffer, protease inhibitor cocktail (PI), and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (Sigma). The RIPA lysis buffer comprised 50 mM Tris-HCl, 150 mM NaCl, 0.5% sodium deoxycholate, 1% IGEPAL, 1 mM NaF, and 1 mM Na3VO4. The lysate was then sonicated using a sonicator (Sonics), and centrifuged. The resulting whole cell lysate (WCL) was precleared with Sepharose 6B (GE Healthcare Life Science) at 4 °C in a rotor for 2 h. Flag-tagged proteins were precipitated with an anti-Flag (M2) antibody for 12 h at 4 °C, followed by incubation with A/G PLUS-Agarose (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, sc-2003) for 4 h at 4 °C. For GST and Strep pull-down assays, WCL was incubated with glutathione sepharose 4B (Cytiva Sweden AB, 17075601) and Strep-Tactin Sepharose resin (IBA Lifesciences; 2–1201-002) for overnight at 4 °C. The beads were collected by centrifugation and washed with NP40 lysis buffer under relevant washing conditions. The beads were mixed with the sample buffer and heated at 100 °C for 10 min. The prepared samples were subjected to immunoblot analysis.

Mass spectrometry

HEK293T cells transfected with GST-vector or GST-L11L were harvested at 36 h post-transfection. Cell pellets were collected using lysis buffer containing RIPA buffer, protease inhibitor cocktail, and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail. The WCL was collected after sonication and centrifugation, followed by incubation with Sepharose 6B beads for 2 h at 4 °C. Pre-cleared WCL was subjected to pull-down with glutathione Sepharose 4B for overnight at 4 °C. The Unbound proteins were washed away with lysis buffer four times and phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) twice. The proteins bound to the beads were eluted using a sample buffer and the eluted fractions were filtered by centrifuge filter tube (Costar). The samples were then separated by 4–15% NuPAGE gels (Invitrogen; NP0323PK2), followed by silver staining. Protein bands present exclusively in the gel were subjected to mass spectrometry.

Immunoblot analysis

Cell lysates or samples prepared with immunoprecipitated beads were separated via SDS-PAGE and then transferred onto a PVDF membrane using a Trans-Blot® semi-dry transfer cell from Bio-Rad in Seoul, Korea. Following this, the membrane was obstructed for an hour with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) and incubated overnight at 4 °C with the primary antibody. On the following day, the membranes were rinsed with either tris-buffered saline with tween-20 (TBST) or phosphate-buffered saline with tween-20 (PBST). Subsequently, the membrane was exposed to horseradish peroxidase-conjugated (HRP) secondary antibody for 2 h at room temperature. The membrane was washed three times with TBST or PBST, the reaction was visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence detection system (ECL-GE Healthcare, Little Chalfont, United Kingdom) using a Las-3000 mini Lumino Image Analyzer.

Luciferase reporter assay

HEK293T and 293-Dual™ hSTING-A162 cells were seeded in 12-well tissue culture plates at a density of 3.5 × 105 cells per well. The cells were then incubated overnight at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere. 293-Dual™ hSTING-A162 cells, which overexpress human STING, were used to investigate IFN-β luciferase activity induced by poly(dA: dT), cGAS and 2’,3’-cGAMP. HEK293T cells were employed to assess IFN-β luciferase activity induced by STING, TBK1, IKKε, IRF3, and IRF3-5D. HEK293T cells were transfected with IFN-β expressing luminescence, TK renilla-luciferase reporter plasmid (an internal control for the normalization of the transfection efficiency), with Flag-tagged L11L plasmid dose-dependently. Poly(dA: dT) (InvivoGen) and 2’,3’-cGAMP ligands (InvivoGen) were transfected with Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen) and Lipofectamine RNAimax (Invitrogen), respectively into 293-Dual™ hSTING-A162 cells. At 36 h post-transfection, luciferase activity in HEK293T cells was measured using the Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay System (Promega; E1980) and luciferase activity in 293-Dual™ hSTING-A162 cells was measured by QUANTI-Luc (InvivoGen) following the manufacturer’s protocol.

Immunofluorescence assay

PK-15 cells were placed in an eight-well chamber slide (ibidi; 80826) and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 min at room temperature. After washing with PBS, the cells were methanol-permeabilized (−20 °C, 20 min), blocked with 2% BSA (1 h, room temperature), and incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. The following day, the cells were washed three times with PBST and incubated with the appropriate secondary antibody. The cells were washed with PBST three times and stained with DAPI (Invitrogen; D1306) at room temperature for 10 min. Images were acquired under Leica DMi8 microscope and then analyzed with LAS-X software (version 3.7.1.21655). The Pearson correlation coefficient of the confocal images was calculated using Fiji Image J (version 1.54c), an open-source platform for biological image analysis.

IRF3 dimerization assay

Cells were lysed using RIPA lysis buffer, supplemented with a protease inhibitor cocktail and a phosphatase inhibitor cocktail. The resulting lysates were subjected to electrophoresis on an 8% Tris-glycine gel (Invitrogen) in Tris-glycine native running buffer (25 mM Tris, pH 8.3, 192 mM glycine). Monomeric and dimerized IRF3 proteins were subsequently detected through immunoblot analysis.

Puromycin incorporation assay

3D4/21 or PK-15 cells were transfected for 24 h and incubated with 10 µg/mL puromycin (Invivogen) in 10% FBS/DMEM for 15 min. Cells were harvested in Laemmli sample buffer supplemented with 2-mercaptoethanol and analyzed by immunoblotting. For cycloheximide (CHX) treatment, 50 µg/mL CHX was added 15 min before puromycin incorporation.

RNA interference experiments

Two siRNAs targeting L11L transcripts (Table S3) were designed and synthesized by Bioneer (Daejeon, Republic of Korea). A nontargeting siRNA was used as a negative control (UUCUCCGAACGUGUCACGU). PAMs (1 × 106 cells in 24-well plates) were transfected with negative control (siControl) or L11L (siL11L) siRNA using RNAiMax (Invivogen). At 6 hpt, the cells were either left uninfected or infected with ASFV (MOI = 0.5). The mRNA expression levels of the target genes, IFNs, and ISG mRNA were determined by qRT-PCR. All experiments involving ASFV were conducted following the Standard Operating Procedure (SOP) in the biosafety level 3 (BSL-3) laboratory of the NIWDC in the Republic of Korea.

Statistical analysis

Graphs and all Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software version 6 for Windows. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (S.D.) and represent at least two independent experiments. An unpaired t-test was performed to compare the control and treatment groups at each time point. *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, *** p < 0.001 or ****, P < 0.0001 was regarded as significant.

Results

ASFV L11L inhibits IRF3-mediated IFN-β promoter activity

In our previous study, Dodantenna et al. explored the potential ASFV genes linked to virus replication in PK-15 cells, followed by infection with a green fluorescent protein-expressing adenovirus (ADV-GFP) [25]. Among these ASFV genes, L11L (corresponding to gene 59) was identified as one of the most promising candidates, exhibiting enhanced viral replication. Since type I IFNs serve as a first line of defense against DNA virus infections, we investigated the potential target of L11L in the cGAS-STING pathway. Therefore, we conducted a luciferase promoter assay in 293-Dual™ hSTING-A162 and HEK293T cells co-transfected with L11L and cGAS-STING pathway molecules. We found that dose-dependent transfection of L11L led to a significant reduction in poly(dA-dT)-, cGAS-, 2’,3’-cGAMP-, STING-, TBK1-, IKKε- and IRF3-mediated IFN-β promoter activity; however, there was no detectable change in IRF3-5D (an active form of IRF3) -mediated IFN-β promoter activity (Fig. 1A). As IRF3 is a downstream molecule in the type I IFN induction pathway, these results suggest that IRF3 is a potential target of L11L. To confirm this, we conducted a pull-down assay after transfecting HEK293T cells with IRF3 and L11L. The result demonstrated a direct interaction between L11L and IRF3 (Fig. 1B). To further validate the L11L-IRF3 interaction, we performed immunoprecipitation assays in HEK293T cells and L11L stably expressing 3D4/21 cells and obtained consistent results in terms of the endogenous L11L-IRF3 interaction (Fig. 1C and Fig. S1A). Moreover, we conducted an immunofluorescence assay in PK-15 cells, which confirmed the co-localization of L11L and IRF3 (Fig. 1D).

L11L interferes with IRF3 phosphorylation

Next, to examine the detailed mechanism, we truncated IRF3 into three domains: the DNA binding domain (DBD), IRF association domain (IAD), and auto-inhibition element (AIE) (Fig. 2A). We then conducted a pull-down assay after co-expressing L11L along with the IRF3 constructs in HEK293T cells. The results showed that L11L bound strongly to IRF3-WT and the AIE domain (380-427aa) (Fig. 2B). AIE domain is crucial for IRF3 phosphorylation and subsequent activation [26, 27]. Therefore, we hypothesized that L11L may affect IRF3 phosphorylation. To check this, we examined the phosphorylation of IRF3 in HEK293T and 3D4/21 cells transfected with different doses of L11L along with IRF3 and TBK1. Cells were harvested at 36 h post-transfection, and phosphorylation was analyzed by western blotting. Because S396 and S386 are critical for TBK1-mediated phosphorylation of IRF3 [28–30], we used both S396 and S386 phospho-IRF3 antibodies to check IRF3 phosphorylation. As shown in the results, L11L attenuated IRF3 phosphorylation in both cell lines in a dose-dependent manner, supporting the notion that L11L interferes with IRF3 phosphorylation (Fig. 2C, D and Fig. S1B, C).

Next, we examined the mechanism by which L11L inhibited IRF3 phosphorylation. As shown in Fig. 2E, L11L interacted directly with IRF3 but not with IRF3-5D. IRF3-5D harbors sequential changes in phosphor-acceptor sites to phosphor-mimetic aspartic acid, starting with serine 396, 398, 402, 405, and threonine 404, which activate interferon-stimulated response elements (ISRE) [29]. Hence, we assumed that these five amino acids were responsible for the L11L-IRF3 interaction. To examine this, we constructed five IRF3 serine/threonine mutants, including in IRF3-5D. We then co-transfected each of these IRF3 mutants, along with L11L, into HEK293T cells and performed a pull-down assay. Interestingly, the S396D and S398D mutations completely attenuated the interaction between L11L and IRF3 (Fig. 2F). The IFN-β promoter activity of each single mutation further validated these results (Fig. 2G). Indeed, these data suggest that L11L interferes with the phosphorylation of serine residues at amino acid positions 396 and 398, thereby inhibiting downstream signaling mediated by phosphorylated IRF3.

Next, we examined the inhibitory effect of L11L on IRF3 dimerization under virus-infected conditions. For that, we conducted a competition assay by co-transfecting GST-IRF3 and Strep-IRF3 into HEK293T cells, along with increasing amounts of L11L, followed by ADV-GFP infection (MOI = 1.0). The cells were then harvested to perform a pull-down assay. The results illustrated that the levels of GST-IRF3 and Strep-IRF3 homodimers fell as L11L expression increased, whereas the interaction between IRF3 and L11L increased (Fig. S1D). Moreover, we confirmed this result under endogenous conditions by transfecting L11L into 3D4/21 cells in a dose-dependent manner. After ADV-GFP-mediated stimulation, samples were harvested and subjected to native PAGE. Interestingly, we observed inhibition of endogenous IRF3 dimerization as the levels of L11L increased (Fig. 3A).

Furthermore, we investigated whether L11L inhibits nuclear translocation of IRF3 by inhibiting IRF3 phosphorylation. To examine this, we conducted a fractionation assay using the 3D4/21 cells. Nuclear and cytoplasmic fractions of 3D4/21 cells were prepared at the indicated times post-ADV-GFP infection (MOI = 1.0). Fractionation efficiency was confirmed by the complete localization of α-tubulin within the cytoplasmic fraction and Lamin B1 in the nuclear fraction. According to the results, we found that the nuclear translocation of pIRF3 and IRF3 declined in the presence of L11L in a time-dependent manner (Fig. 3B, C). To confirm this result, we conducted an immunofluorescence assay by transfecting PK-15 cells with L11L (Fig. 3D, E). These results were consistent with those obtained from the fractionation assay. Taken together, these results confirm that L11L prevents the nuclear translocation of IRF3 by inhibiting IRF3 phosphorylation.

L11L downregulates transcription of IFN-stimulated genes (ISGs)

To determine the antagonistic effect of L11L on the type I IFN signaling cascade, we examined DNA virus-mediated phosphorylation of TBK1, IRF3, IκBα, p65 (RelA), and STAT1 in L11L stably expressing 3D4/21 cells and L11L-overexpressing PK-15 cells by conducting immunoblot analysis at different time points after ADV-GFP (MOI = 1.0) infection. As shown in Fig. 4A and C, the phosphorylation levels of TBK1, IRF3, IκBα, p65, and STAT1 were significantly lower in both L11L-expressed 3D4/21 and PK-15 cells than in the control at the indicated times post-infection. Next, to further assess the effect of L11L on the transcription of IFN-β and IFN-γ, pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-6, IL-1β, and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), and ISGs IFIT1, MCP-1, IFITM3, ISG15, MX-1, and OASL, we infected L11L stably expressing 3D4/21 cells and L11L-overexpressing PK-15 cells with ADV-GFP (MOI = 1.0) and then subjected them to qRT-PCR using specific primers (Table S1). The results revealed a significant reduction in the expression of mRNA encoding IFNs, pro-inflammatory cytokines, and ISGs in both L11L-expressed 3D4/21 cells (Fig. 4B and S2A) and PK-15 cells (Fig. 4D and S2B). These results indicate that L11L downregulates DNA virus-mediated type I IFN signaling and the subsequent transcription of antiviral genes.

L11L negatively regulates immune responses

Since L11L inhibits IRF3 phosphorylation, we explored whether L11L affects the antiviral immune response. Therefore, we transiently transfected PK-15 cells with L11L and Vector plasmids, followed by infection with ADV-GFP (MOI = 1.0), GFP-expressing HSV (HSV-GFP) (MOI = 1.0), or GFP-expressing VACV (VACV-GFP) (MOI = 1.0), as these DNA viruses are sensitive to type I IFNs. As shown in Figure S3A-F, overexpression of L11L induced replication of ADV-GFP, HSV-GFP, and VACV-GFP to a greater extent than vector-transfected cells; however, both secreted IFN-β and IL-6 levels were markedly lower in L11L-transfected cells than in vector-transfected cells. To further validate these results, we generated 3D4/21 and monkey kidney epithelial (MA-104) cells stably expressing L11L and infected them with ADV-GFP, HSV-GFP, or VACV-GFP. As in PK-15 cells, stable expression of L11L in 3D4/21 and MA-104 cells increased virus replication and the virus titer while reducing IFN-β and IL-6 cytokine levels (Fig. 5, S4). Taken together, these results indicate that inhibition of IRF3 phosphorylation promotes replication of DNA viruses in PK-15, 3D4/21, and MA-104 cells while abrogating the production of type I IFNs and pro-inflammatory cytokines. To validate transfection efficiency, we confirmed the expression of L11L in 3D4/21, PK-15, and MA-104 cells by western blotting (Fig. S5A - C) and quantitative reverse transcription PCR (qRT-PCR) (Fig. S5D - F).

L11L interacts directly with PKR

To further identify a specific target molecule for L11L involved in the innate immune response, we conducted a large-scale pull-down assay and mass spectrometry in HEK293T cells transfected L11L. Interestingly, mass spectrometry analysis of L11L identified PKR as a likely candidate target of L11L, leading us to hypothesize that L11L also interacts with PKR (Fig. 6A). To explore whether L11L also counteracts PKR, we infected L11L and vector stably expressing 3D4/21 cells with the green fluorescent protein-expressing Newcastle disease virus (NDV-GFP) (MOI = 1.0) and obtained results similar to those obtained after DNA virus infection. Stable expression of L11L resulted in increased NDV-GFP replication while reducing cytokine secretion (Fig. S3G, H). To validate the mass spectrometry data, we checked the interaction between L11L and PKR by conducting an immunoprecipitation assay after transfecting PKR and L11L into HEK293T cells. The results confirmed direct interaction between L11L and PKR (Fig. 6B). Next, we investigated the interaction between endogenous PKR and L11L under poly(I: C) stimulation in L11L stably expressed 3D4/21 cells. The results confirmed the interaction between L11L and endogenous PKR (Fig. 6C). This interaction was further validated by immunofluorescence assays in PK-15 cells under overexpression and endogenous conditions (Fig. 6D, E).

To understand the mechanism underlying this interaction, we conducted a PKR domain study. As shown in Fig. 6F, we truncated PKR into three major domains and then conducted a pull-down assay after transfecting the PKR constructs, along with L11L, into HEK293T cells. The results revealed that the N-lobe (270–380 aa) of PKR is indispensable for its interaction with L11L (Fig. 6H). Moreover, we further narrowed down the N-lobe domain into two domains: the N terminal 270–330 aa and the N terminal 320–380 aa (Fig. 6G). Next, we conducted a pull-down assay after transfecting the narrowed-down constructs along with L11L into HEK293T cells. The data showed that L11L interacted with the 270–320 aa region of the N-lobe, which is important for PKR dimerization (Fig. 6I). These results suggest that L11L may interfere with PKR dimerization.

L11L counteracts dimerization of PKR to inhibit autophosphorylation and phosphorylation of eIF2α

To examine the effect of L11L on PKR dimerization, we performed a dimerization inhibition assay, as described previously [31]. Briefly, we co-transfected HEK293T cells with Strep-PKR and GST-PKR, along with increasing doses of L11L, and conducted a pull-down assay. The results revealed a dose-dependent inhibition of PKR dimerization by L11L (Fig. 7A). PKR-K296R is a well-known kinase mutant that inhibits PKR dimerization and consequently inhibits PKR kinase activity [32]. Since this residue is located within the 270–330 amino acid region, we checked whether it is important for the PKR-L11L interaction. To verify this, we generated a PKR-K296R mutant and compared the interactions between L11L and PKR-WT or PKR-K296R. As shown in Fig. 7B, we found that PKR-K296R completely abolished the interaction between L11L and PKR. Taken together, these data confirm that L11L targets the dimerization of PKR to interrupt host innate immune responses. Next, to check whether L11L inhibits PKR phosphorylation, we transfected the L11L into 3D4/21 and HEK293T cells in a dose-dependent manner. Cells were harvested after poly(I: C) stimulation and subjected to western blotting analysis. The results showed that L11L downregulated PKR phosphorylation in a dose-dependent manner, thereby inhibiting PKR-mediated phosphorylation of eIF2α (Fig. 7C, D). Because eIF2α is a major initiator of translation in host cells, we wondered how L11L affects the host translation machinery. Therefore, we performed a puromycin labelling assay to check host protein translation in 3D4/21 cells. We found no detectable changes in host protein synthesis with increasing amounts of L11L (Fig. 7E). Moreover, we compared the effect of L11L on host protein translation with that of PKR-K296R under poly(I: C) stimulation. The results demonstrated that activation of PKR inhibited host protein translation, whereas L11L rescued PKR-mediated host protein translation inhibition in a manner similar to PKR-K296R (Fig. 7F), suggesting that L11L can counteract the PKR-mediated host protein shut-off by acting as a PKR inhibitor. Furthermore, these data were validated in an immunofluorescence assay in PK-15 cells (Fig. 7G). Finally, we checked whether L11L affects viral protein synthesis by transfecting 3D4/21 cells with increasing amounts of L11L. The cells were then infected with ADV-GFP (MOI = 1.0) at 12 h post-transfection. Western blot analysis detected increasing amounts of adenoviral capsid proteins as the amount of L11L increased (Fig. 7H), suggesting that inhibition of phosphorylation of eIF2α facilitates continued host protein translation, thereby promoting viral replication. Collectively, these results show that L11L maintains continuous host translation, thereby boosting viral protein synthesis in virus-infected cells. Thus, L11L targets both IRF3 and PKR to escape the host’s innate immune system.

L11L is a late-transcribed protein involved in the antagonism of IFNs and ISGs transcription

To determine the stage at which L11L transcription occurs, we infected PAMs with ASFV and analyzed the gene transcription levels by qRT-PCR as described by Ranathunga et al. [33]. As shown in Fig. 8A, L11L transcribed late stages following ASFV infection, with a similar pattern to the late-transcribed gene B646L. These results suggest that L11L may play a role in the late stages of ASFV infection. To explore the immunomodulatory role of L11L during ASFV infection, we employed L11L-specific small interfering RNA (siRNA) to knock down the expression of L11L mRNA induced by ASFV infection in PAMs. Afterwards, these cells were infected for 24 h with ASFV (MOI = 0.5). The knockdown efficiency of L11L mRNA was approximately 90% at 24 h post-infection (Fig. 8B). According to the qRT-PCR analysis results of antiviral gene expression, transcription of IFNs and ISGs was upregulated upon L11L knockdown in ASFV-infected PAMs, as expected (Fig. 8C). These results indicate that L11L inhibits the host immune response during ASFV infection to induce viral replication.

Discussion

The data presented in this study demonstrate that ASFV L11L employs different molecular mechanisms to evade antiviral immunity and maintain viral replication. Accordingly, ASFV L11L directly targets both IRF3 and PKR to inhibit type I interferon secretion and suppress viral protein translation shut-off.

The cGAS-STING pathway detects intracellular viral DNA and triggers type I IFN secretion. cGAS produces 2’, 3’-cGAMP, activating the STING-TBK1-IRF3 axis [34–36]. IRF3, a key transcription factor in innate immunity, contains three functional domains (AIE, IAD, and DBD), each with unique functions responsible for IRF3 phosphorylation, dimerization, and interaction with IFN-stimulated response elements (ISRE), respectively [37]. IRF3 activation involves interaction with STING [38, 39] and phosphorylation by TBK1, primarily at serine residues 386 and 396 [40, 41]. Additionally, serine/threonine residues 398, 402, 404, and 405 are also involved in IRF3 activation [42]. After phosphorylation, IRF3 undergoes dimerization and nuclear translocation to transactivate the expression of type I IFN genes [43, 44].

In compliance with published knowledge, ASFV counteracts IRF3 to escape the host antiviral immune system. For instance, ASFV E120R disrupts the interaction between TBK1 and IRF3, thereby preventing IRF3 phosphorylation [20]. ASFV M1249L degrades IRF3 via the lysosomal pathway and ASFV MGF360-14 L promotes the ubiquitination and degradation of IRF3 by recruiting E3 ligase TRIM21 [45, 46]. Moreover, two other ASFV proteins, MGF505-7R and E301R, interfere with the type I IFN cascade by preventing the nuclear translocation of IRF3 [47, 48].

Here, we demonstrated a novel molecular mechanism by which ASFV L11L suppresses cellular IRF3-mediated antiviral immune responses. First, we confirmed that ASFV L11L interacts with IRF3 by an IFN-β luciferase reporter assay and immunoprecipitation assays under overexpression and endogenous conditions. Second, ASFV L11L interacts specifically with the AIE domain (380–427 aa), and sequentially inhibits IRF3 phosphorylation, dimerization, and nuclear translocation after interacting with IRF3. Interestingly, serine 396 and serine 398 of IRF3 were critical for this interaction. Third, based on this mechanism, we found that overexpression of ASFV L11L in porcine cell lines reduced DNA virus-mediated production of IFNs and proinflammatory cytokine signaling cascades and promoted DNA virus replication. Taken together, these findings indicate that L11L inhibits host type I IFN signaling by preventing IRF3 phosphorylation.

Protein kinase R (PKR), an intracellular serine-threonine kinase encoded by the EIF2AK2 gene, plays a pivotal role in innate immunity, as well as numerous critical cellular functions, such as transcriptional regulation, apoptosis, and cell proliferation [49]. PKR molecules contain a regulatory domain comprising two double-standard RNA-binding domains (dsRBD1 and dsRBD2) at the N-terminus and a kinase domain at the C-terminus, comprising an N-lobe and a C-lobe. The N-terminal and C-terminal domains are connected by an interdomain linker region [50]. The dsRBD motif regulates PKR activity in response to dsRNA recognition and PKR activation is primarily triggered by viral dsRNA [51]. At the native stage, PKR exists as a monomer, but upon detection of dsRNA, a conformational change occurs that dissociates the dsRBD from the kinase domain. This results in the dimerization of PKR, where N-lobe is important. Dimerized PKR undergoes autophosphorylation, for which the T446 and T451 residues in C-lobe are critical for PKR activation [52]. Auto-phosphorylated PKR is considered to be the active form of PKR, which then phosphorylates its substrate eIF2α at serine 51 to inhibit host protein and viral protein translation in virus-infected cells [51, 52].

PKR exhibits broad antiviral activity by inhibiting the replication of RNA and DNA viruses and many viruses have developed strategies to block PKR activation at various stages of the process. For instance, the Middle East respiratory syndrome coronavirus and influenza viruses sequester and bind to dsRNA, thereby inhibiting dsRNA recognition by PKR, whereas the NSP15 protein of Infectious bronchitis virus limits the amount of available dsRNA for PKR activation by degrading it [53, 54]. NS proteins of Toscana virus and Rift Valley fever virus mediate the proteasomal degradation of PKR to inhibit host protein shut-off [55, 56]. Moreover, the Andes and Hepatitis C viruses inhibit PKR dimerization to regulate PKR activity [31, 57]. In addition to RNA viruses, short RNAs and VAI RNA produced by adenovirus, vaccinia virus K3L, and herpes simplex virus US11 inhibit PKR autophosphorylation, while E3L of vaccinia virus and Epstein bar virus interact with both PKR and dsRNA, preventing dsRNA from binding to PKR [58–63].

To date, no ASFV proteins have been reported to counteract PKR activity. In this study, we demonstrated a novel mechanism by which L11L negatively regulates PKR-mediated protein translation for protein synthesis. First, mass spectrometry analysis and immunoprecipitation assays confirmed a direct interaction between L11L and PKR. Second, domain mapping analysis showed a specific interaction between L11L and the N-lobe domain of PKR and we confirmed that L11L interferes with PKR dimerization via the K296 residue of the N-lobe domain. Third, because L11L blocks PKR dimerization, it also inhibits the downstream phosphorylation and activation of PKR. Finally, we demonstrated that L11L prevents the phosphorylation of eIF2α and inhibits the shutdown of the protein transcription machinery, thereby facilitating the synthesis of viral proteins.

Previous studies have shown that the levels of PKR mRNA increase at an early time point after ASFV infection, whereas the phosphorylation of PKR decreases at a later time point after infection [64, 65]. Castello et al. also reported similar results, noting that eIF2α remains unphosphorylated during ASFV infection [66]. Given that L11L is also a late protein produced after ASFV infection (Fig. 8A) [21], these reports further supports our findings.

Previous research has shown that ASFV L11L is involved in ASFV virulence. Kleiboeker et al. reported that the non-pathogenic, cell culture-adapted European isolate BA71V does not have the L11L protein and a recombinant ASFV-Malawi Lil-20-ΔL11L virus showed attenuated virulence in pigs [21]. Additionally, Zhang et al. and Fan et al. showed that recombinant ASFV-SY18-ΔL7L-ΔL11L and ASFV-SY18-ΔL11L had significantly reduced virulence and protected against infection by the parental strain [22, 23]. These studies suggest that ASFV L11L is closely related to ASFV virulence and may be a target for developing safer vaccine candidates in vivo. Despite that, there is a lack of comprehensive understanding of the specific mechanisms by which L11L contributes to ASFV pathogenesis and virulence through immune evasion. This knowledge gap hinders the development of targeted interventions and effective countermeasures against ASFV infection.

In this study, we describe a novel molecular mechanism by which ASFV L11L acts as a negative modulator of type I IFNs signaling and antiviral responses by impairing the phosphorylation of IRF3 and dimerization of PKR. Even though, this study was conducted within the constraints of in vitro cell culture models, which lack the complexity of a living host, our findings support the notion that L11L is important for ASFV virulence. This enhances our understanding that a recombinant ASFV strain with a deleted L11L gene could potentially exhibit reduced virulence, making it a promising candidate for a live-attenuated vaccine.
