Efficient Penalty-Based Bilevel Methods: Improved Analysis, Novel Updates, and Flatness Condition
Liuyuan Jiang, Quan Xiao, Lisha Chen, Tianyi Chen

Abstract
Penalty-based methods have become popular for solving bilevel optimization (BLO) problems with or without constraints, thanks to their effective first-order nature. However, they often require inner-loop iterations to solve the lower-level problem and small step sizes to handle the increased smoothness induced by large penalty terms, leading to suboptimal complexity. This work considers the general BLO problem with coupled constraints (CCs) and leverages a novel penalty reformulation that decouples the upper- and lower-level variables. This reformulation yields an improved analysis of the smoothness constant, enabling larger step sizes and reduced iteration complexity for a Penalty-Based Gradient Descent algorithm in ALTernating fashion (ALT-PBGD). Building on the insight of reduced smoothness, we further propose PBGD-Free, an efficient algorithm that avoids solving the lower-level problem iteratively to its optimality. It is a fully single-loop algorithm for the uncoupled constraint case, requiring only one update of the lower-level variable per iteration. For the coupled constraint case, it still employs inner-loop updates but with substantially reduced iteration complexity. Furthermore, we propose a curvature condition, which describes the "flatness" of the upper-level objective with respect to the lower-level optimal variable without relying on the small Lipschitz modulus assumption. This condition relaxes the traditional upper-level Lipschitz requirement, enables smaller penalty constant choices, and results in a negligible penalty gradient term during upper-level variable updates. We provide rigorous convergence analysis and validate the method’s efficacy through hyperparameter optimization for support vector machines and fine-tuning of large language models (LLMs).

These applications involve both constrained and unconstrained settings, motivating us to study the BLO problem with coupled constraints (CCs):

\displaystyle\min_{x\in\mathcal{X}}~

\displaystyle\phi(x):=\min_{y\in S_{g}(x)}f(x,y)

(1a)

where

\displaystyle\quad S_{g}(x):=\arg\min_{y\in\mathcal{Y}(x)}g(x,y)\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{Y}(x)=\{y\in\mathcal{Y}:c(x,y)\leq 0\}.

(1b)

Here,
f:\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}\times\mathbb{R}^{d_{y}}\to\mathbb{R}
and

are the UL and LL objectives, respectively. The UL variable
x
is restricted to the feasible set

y
is constrained to
\mathcal{Y}(x)=\{y\in\mathcal{Y}:c(x,y)\leq 0\}

\mathcal{Y}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d_{y}}
is an easy-to-project set, such as an Euclidean ball, and
c:\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}\times\mathbb{R}^{d_{y}}\to\mathbb{R}^{d_{c}}
encodes coupled inequality constraints.

The goal of the BLO problem (
1
) is to find an optimal

\phi(x)
and the corresponding LL optimal solution

. In the unconstrained case, i.e.,
\mathcal{Y}=\mathbb{R}^{d_{y}},~c(x,y)=0

\displaystyle\nabla\phi(x)=\nabla_{x}f(x,y_{g}^{*}(x))+\nabla_{y}f(x,y_{g}^{*}(x))\nabla_{yy}g(x,y_{g}^{*}(x))^{-1}\nabla_{yx}g(x,y_{g}^{*}(x))

(2)

\displaystyle\min_{x\in\mathcal{X},y\in\mathcal{Y}(x)}f(x,y)\quad\text{s.t.}\quad d_{S_{g}(x)}(y)^{2}\leq\epsilon.

(4)

This enables the convergence analysis of BLO through the penalized objective
\tilde{F}_{\gamma}
in (
4
). Additionally,

;

;

;

, which are more efficient than IGD methods.

However, for large-scale problems, the joint projection onto
\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}(x)
required by projected gradient-based updates can be costly per iteration

. In addition, PBGD requires an inner loop to find
y_{g}^{*}(x)\in S_{g}(x)
for estimating
\nabla v(x)
. Although this inner loop converges linearly under lower-level strong convexity, it still imposes a non-negligible computational burden in large-scale settings. Moreover, the outer-loop convergence is slowed by the need for a small step size,
\eta=\mathcal{O}(\gamma^{-1})
, to satisfy
\eta\leq l_{\tilde{F}_{\gamma},1}^{-1}
as required by gradient descent, since the smoothness constant
l_{\tilde{F}_{\gamma},1}
scales with
\gamma=\Theta(\epsilon^{-0.5})
. Taken together, these factors significantly increase the overall complexity, limiting the practical efficiency of standard penalty-based methods.

Consequently, this paper contributes by presenting an improved theoretical analysis of the smoothness of a variant of value-function-based penalty reformulations, developing an efficient algorithm for BLO with CCs in (
1
), and establishing convergence guarantees under new, mild conditions.

1.1
Main contributions

We highlight key contributions in developing efficient BLO algorithms as follows.

C1)
Improved outer-loop iteration complexity for solving BLO with CCs that matches gradient descent.
This is enabled by writing
\tilde{F}_{\gamma}(x,y)
into a decoupled form:

\displaystyle F_{\gamma}(x):=\min_{y\in\mathcal{Y}(x)}\tilde{F}_{\gamma}(x,y)=\gamma\min_{y_{\gamma}\in\mathcal{Y}(x)}\left(\gamma^{-1}f(x,y_{\gamma})+g(x,y_{\gamma})\right)-\gamma\min_{y_{g}\in\mathcal{Y}(x)}g(x,y_{g})

(5)

which leads to an
alternating PBGD scheme
that first optimizes
y
, then updates
x
. While the scheme avoids the expensive joint projection onto
\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}(x)
, its iteration complexity under CCs is suboptimal
Jiang et al. 2024
.
Inspired by the enhanced analysis for the unconstrained BLO problem
Chen et al. 2024
, we show that
F_{\gamma}(x)
is smooth with modulus
l_{F_{\gamma},1}=\mathcal{O}(1)
independent of
\gamma
for both non-coupled constrained setting in Sec.
3
and coupled constrained setting in Sec.
5.1
, which is the key to obtain the improved convergence rate.

C2)
We propose PBGD-Free, a value-function-free algorithm.
Building on the improved outer-loop complexity of the alternating scheme, PBGD-Free can avoid the inner loop for computing
y_{g}^{*}(x)\in S_{g}(x)
by estimating
\nabla F_{\gamma}(x)
using
\nabla_{x}f(x,y^{\gamma})
, with
y^{\gamma}\approx\arg\min_{y\in\mathcal{Y}(x)}F_{\gamma}(x,y)
(see Fig.
2
). In Sec.
4
, we start by demonstrating its empirical effectiveness on a LLM parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) problem. However, we also show that without additional assumptions, PBGD-Free does not necessarily converge for the worst-case performance (cf. Sec.
4.1
).

C3)
We introduce a mild sufficient condition for PBGD-Free to converge.

In Sec.
4
, we propose the
(\delta,\alpha)
-flatness
condition (Def.
11
), which relaxes
\alpha
-Hölder continuity and characterizes the flatness of
f(x,y)
at
y=y_{g}^{*}(x)
. This condition serves as an alternative to the classical Lipschitz continuity of
f(x,\cdot)
and is validated on the PEFT problem in Sec.
4.3
.
In Sec.
4.4
, we show that under flatness, the update bias of PBGD-Free becomes negligible. For uncoupled problems, the fully
single-loop version
of PBGD-Free converges to a stationary point of BLO, achieving the same rate as gradient descent. For BLO with coupled constraints, Sec.
5.2
shows that PBGD-Free with a
\mathcal{O}(\ln(\epsilon^{-1}))
-inner loop is similarly effective and requires one fewer inner loop compared with the existing alternating-update method
Jiang et al. 2024
. A detailed comparison with other algorithms is given in Table

.

C4)
We empirically validate our theoretical results.

We first confirm the
\mathcal{O}(1)
-smoothness of
F_{\gamma}(x)
on toy examples (cf. Sec.
6.1
). We then evaluate PBGD-Free on real-world tasks, including LLM PEFT in Sec.
6.2
and SVM hyperparameter optimization in Sec.
6.3
, which showcase the computational efficiency of our algorithm.

1.2
Prior art

BLO without coupled constraint.
Unconstrained BLO has a long history, starting with
Bracken and McGill 1973
. Recent advances focus on efficient gradient-based methods with finite-time guarantees. Implicit gradient descent (IGD), introduced by
Pedregosa 2016
, approximates the hypergradient "
\frac{\partial}{\partial x}y_{g}^{*}(x)
" using the implicit function theorem including Hessian inverse. Several studies have explored finite-time convergence for unconstrained BLO
(
Hong et al. 2023
;
Ji et al. 2021b
;
Chen et al. 2021
;
Khanduri et al. 2021
;
Ji et al. 2021a
;
Shen and Chen 2022
;
Li et al. 2022
;
Sow et al. 2022
;
Chen et al. 2022
;
Yang et al. 2023
;
Ghadimi and Wang 2018
)
,
mostly focus on the strongly convex lower-level problem.
Another line of work, penalty-based methods, reformulate BLO as a single-level problem with penalty terms, which avoids using Hessian, and are able to tackle non-strongly-convex lower-level problem and are fully first-order. This approach can be dated back to
Ye et al. 1997
and has been gaining popularity recently
(
Liu et al. 2021
;
Mehra and Hamm 2021
;
Ye et al. 2022
;
Kwon et al. 2023
;
Kwon et al. 2024
;
Shen et al. 2023
;
Gao et al. 2022
;
Lu 2023
)
due to its efficiency. This approach can handle the feasible-domain constraint
\mathcal{Y}
, but it cannot be applied directly to coupled constraints.

BLO with coupled constraint
. However, BLO with lower-level constraints has been less explored until recent years. IGD methods have been developed to address lower-level constraints that can be characterized by
c(x,y)
, progressing from linear constraints
(
Khanduri et al. 2023
;
Xiao et al. 2023b
)
to coupled inequality constraints
c(x,y)\leq 0

(
Xu and Zhu 2023
)
. In comparison, penalty-based methods can effectively handle the nonsmoothness introduced by the general uncoupled feasible constraints
\mathcal{Y}

(
Shen et al. 2023
)
. Built upon this, recent works, such as LV-HBA
(
Yao et al. 2024
)
, BLOCC
(
Jiang et al. 2024
)
, and BiC-CAFFA
(
Yao et al. 2025
)
consider both feasible constraint
\mathcal{Y}
and coupled inequality
c(x,y)\leq 0
. However, none of them match the convergence rate of gradient descent in the unconstrained setting.

BLO algorithms with high complexity.
Since most BLO algorithms rely on inner loops for hyper-gradient estimation and lower-level optimization, another line of research focuses on improving the per-iteration complexity of BLO algorithms, specifically by reducing the inner loop.
Li et al. 2022
proposed an IGD-based fully single-loop method for unconstrained BLO. For stochastic BLO with uncoupled lower-level inequality constraints,
Kwon et al. 2023
introduced a penalty-based fully single-loop method with momentum, achieving
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{-1.5})
complexity for non-stochastic problems.
For coupled constraints, BiC-CAFFA
(
Yao et al. 2025
)
and BLOCC
(
Jiang et al. 2024
)
achieve
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{-3})
and
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{-2.5})
iteration complexity. However, these methods are still of high complexity and may be less feasible for large-scale problems.

Landscape-aware optimization.
Landscape-aware optimization leverages structural properties of objective functions into algorithm design to accelerate the convergence or improve the generalization. Newton-type methods, which use second-order curvature information to rescale gradients, have been utilized in BLO
(
Fang et al. 2025
;
Ramzi et al. 2022
;
Dong et al. 2025
)
for efficient Hessian-vector calculation in IGD-based BLO methods. Sharpness-aware minimization
(
Foret et al. 2021
)
, which seeks solutions robust to local perturbations and promotes convergence to flat minima, has also been incorporated into BLO
(
Abbas et al. 2022
)
for improved generalization. Other landscape conditions in single-level optimization, such as relaxed smoothness
(
Zhang et al. 2020
;
Li et al. 2023
)
and Hessian spectrum
(
Zhang et al. 2024
;
Ghorbani et al. 2019
)
, are key to explaining the theoretical benefits of empirically effective algorithms like gradient clipping and Adam
(
Kingma and Ba 2014
)
. However, most existing works focus on second-order BLO algorithms, and none have explored BLO tailored landscape conditions.

1.3
Notations and general preliminaries

We use
\|\cdot\|
to denote the
\ell_{2}
-norm. For a set
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
\text{int}(\mathcal{S})
and
\operatorname{bd}(\mathcal{S})
denote the interior and boundary of
\mathcal{S}
, respectively, and

denotes the distance of
q\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
to
\mathcal{S}
. For a square matrix
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
,
A^{-1}
denotes its inverse and
A^{\dagger}
its Moore–Penrose pseudoinverse. Standard asymptotic notations are used:
\mathcal{O}(\cdot)
,
\Omega(\cdot)
, and
\Theta(\cdot)
denote upper, lower, and tight bounds up to constant factors, and
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(\cdot)
suppresses logarithmic factors.

In the following, we provide some critical definitions useful in this paper.

Definition 1
(Lipschitz Continuity and Smoothness)

Let
h:\mathcal{Q}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{m}
. We say
h
is
l_{h,0}
-Lipschitz continuous on
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathcal{Q}
if

\displaystyle\|h(q_{1})-h(q_{2})\|\leq l_{h,0}\|q_{1}-q_{2}\|,\quad\forall q_{1},q_{2}\in\mathcal{S}.

If
h
is differentiable on
\mathcal{S}
, we say
h
is
l_{h,1}
-smooth on
\mathcal{S}
if its gradient
\nabla h
is
l_{h,1}
-Lipschitz continuous on
\mathcal{S}
, or equivalently, if

\displaystyle h(q_{2})\leq h(q_{1})+\nabla h(q_{1})^{\top}(q_{2}-q_{1})+\frac{l_{h,1}}{2}\|q_{2}-q_{1}\|^{2},\quad\forall q_{1},q_{2}\in\mathcal{S}.

Moreover, we say
h
is
locally Lipschitz
on
\mathcal{S}
if for all
q_{1}\in\mathcal{S}
, there exist constants
l_{h,1}>0
and
\delta>0
such that

\displaystyle\|h(q_{1})-h(q_{2})\|\leq M\|q_{1}-q_{2}\|,\quad\forall q_{2}\in\mathcal{S}\text{ with }\|q_{1}-q_{2}\|<\delta.

Definition 2
(Convexity and Strong Convexity)

Let
h:\mathcal{Q}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a function and
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathcal{Q}
be a convex set.
We say
h
is
convex
on
\mathcal{S}
if for all
q_{1},q_{2}\in\mathcal{S}
and all
\lambda\in[0,1]
,

\displaystyle h(\lambda q_{1}+(1-\lambda)q_{2})\leq\lambda h(q_{1})+(1-\lambda)h(q_{2}).

We say
h
is
\mu_{g}
-strongly convex
on
\mathcal{S}
for some constant
\mu_{g}>0
if for all
q_{1},q_{2}\in\mathcal{S}
and all
\lambda\in[0,1]
,

\displaystyle h(\lambda q_{1}+(1-\lambda)q_{2})\leq\lambda h(q_{1})+(1-\lambda)h(q_{2})-\frac{\mu_{g}}{2}\lambda(1-\lambda)\|q_{1}-q_{2}\|^{2}.

If
h
is differentiable on
\mathcal{S}
, then
h
is
\mu_{g}
-strongly convex on
\mathcal{S}
if and only if

\displaystyle h(q_{2})\geq h(q_{1})+\nabla h(q_{1})^{\top}(q_{2}-q_{1})+\frac{\mu_{g}}{2}\|q_{2}-q_{1}\|^{2},\quad\forall q_{1},q_{2}\in\mathcal{S}.

Definition 3
(Projection)

For any convex set
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the projection of
q\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
onto
\mathcal{S}
under a well-defined
A
-norm is defined by

\displaystyle\Proj_{\mathcal{S}}^{A}(q):=\arg\min_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\|s-q\|_{A}.

When
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is a positive definite matrix, the
A
-norm of a vector
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is defined as
\|x\|_{A}:=\sqrt{x^{\top}Ax}
.
When
A=I
, the
A
-norm reduces to the standard
\ell_{2}
-norm, and we write
\Proj_{\mathcal{S}}(q):=\Proj_{\mathcal{S}}^{I}(q)
.

Definition 4
(Directional Derivative
(
Bonnans and Shapiro 2013
)
)

Let
h:\mathcal{Q}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
and let
d\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. The first-order
directional derivative
of
h
at
q\in\mathcal{Q}
along
d
is

\displaystyle D_{d}h(q)=\lim_{r\downarrow 0}\frac{h(q+rd)-h(q)}{r}.

If
D_{d}h(q)
exists and is finite for all

, we say
h
is
directional differential
in direction
d
at
q
. Additionally, the
second-order directional derivative
of
h
at
q\in\mathcal{Q}
along
d
is

\displaystyle D_{dd}^{2}h(q)=\lim_{r\downarrow 0}\frac{D_{d}(h(q+rd))-D_{d}(h(q))}{r}.

Definition 5
(Tangent Cone & Critical Cone)

Let
q\in\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
. The
tangent cone
at
q
with respect to

is

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{S}}(q):=\big\{d\in\mathbb{R}^{n}|\exists\,t_{k}\downarrow 0,\,\exists\,d_{k}\to d\text{ such that }q+t_{k}d_{k}\in\mathcal{S},\forall k\big\}.

Let

be differentiable on
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathcal{Q}
, the
critical cone
of

at

with respect to

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{S}}(q):=\big\{d\in\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{S}}(q)|\langle\nabla h(q),d\rangle=0\big\}.

Remark 1

If

, then
\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{S}}(q)=\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Definition 6
(Generalized (Proximal) Gradient)

Let
h:\mathcal{Q}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be differentiable on
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathcal{Q}
, and
\eta>0
be some small scalar. We say the proximal gradient of
h(q)
on
\mathcal{S}
is

\displaystyle G_{h,\mathcal{S}}(q)=\frac{1}{\eta}\Big(q-\Proj_{\mathcal{S}}(q-\eta\nabla h(q))\Big),\quad\forall q\in\mathcal{S}.

Definition 7
(Proximal PL, EB, and QG)

Let
h:\mathcal{Q}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be differentiable on
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathcal{Q}
. We say
h(q)
satisfies proximal
\mu_{g}
-Polyak-Łojasiewicz (PL) condition on
\mathcal{S}
if

\displaystyle h(q)-\min_{q\in\mathcal{S}}h(q)\leq\frac{1}{2\mu_{g}}G_{h,\mathcal{S}}^{2}(q),\quad\forall q\in\mathcal{S}.

Denote
S_{h}^{*}=\arg\min_{q\in\mathcal{S}}h(q)
as the solution set. We say
h(q)
satisfies proximal
\mu_{g}
-Error Bound (EB) condition on
\mathcal{S}
if

\displaystyle d_{S_{h}^{*}}(q)\leq\frac{1}{\mu_{g}}G_{h,\mathcal{S}}(q),\quad\forall q\in\mathcal{S}.

We say
h(q)
satisfies
\mu_{g}
-Quadratic-Growth (QG) condition on
\mathcal{S}
if

\displaystyle d_{S_{h}^{*}}(q)^{2}\leq\frac{2}{\mu_{g}}(h(q)-\min_{q\in\mathcal{S}}h(q)),\quad\forall q\in\mathcal{S}.

Lemma 1
(Strongly Convexity and Proximal PL
(
Karimi et al. 2016
, Appendix E)
)

If
h:\mathcal{Q}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is
\mu_{g}
-strongly convex on
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathcal{Q}
and
\mathcal{S}
is a convex set, then

satisfies proximal
\mu_{g}
-PL condition on
\mathcal{S}
.

Lemma 2
(Equivalence of PL, EB, and QG condition
(
Liao et al. 2024
, Theorem 3.1)
)

Suppose

is differentiable and

-smooth on convex domain

. Then if

satisfies proximal

-PL condition on

satisfies proximal

-EB and

Definition 8
(LICQ)

For any fixed
x
, consider the lower-level feasible set
\mathcal{Y}(x)=\{y\in\mathcal{Y}:c(x,y)\leq 0\}
where
\mathcal{Y}
can be locally characterized by
\phi(y)\leq 0
near
y_{g}^{*}(x)
and
c
is differentiable.
We say that
\mathcal{Y}(x)
satisfies the
Linear Independence Constraint Qualification (LICQ)
at
y_{g}^{*}(x)
if the set of gradients of all active constraints

\{\nabla_{y}\phi_{j}(y_{g}^{*}(x)):j\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{Y}}^{0}(x)\}\cup\{\nabla_{y}c_{i}(x,y_{g}^{*}(x)):i\in\mathcal{I}_{c}^{0}(x)\}

is linearly independent, where

\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{Y}}^{0}(x):=\{j:\phi_{j}(y_{g}^{*}(x))=0\}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{I}_{c}^{0}(x):=\{i:c_{i}(x,y_{g}^{*}(x))=0\}

denote the active constraints.

Lemma 3
(First-Order Variational Inequality
(
Rockafellar and Wets 1998
)
)

Consider the constrained optimization problem
\min_{q\in\mathcal{S}}h(q)
,
where
h:\mathcal{Q}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is differentiable on
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathcal{Q}
.
A point
q^{*}\in\mathcal{S}
is a first-order stationary point of this problem if and only if it satisfies the first-order variational inequality condition

\displaystyle\langle\nabla h(q^{*}),q-q^{*}\rangle\geq 0,\quad\forall q\in\mathcal{S},

or equivalently,

\displaystyle\langle\nabla h(q^{*}),d\rangle\geq 0,\quad\forall d\in\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{S}}(q^{*}).

Remark 2

If
q^{*}\in\mathrm{int}(\mathcal{S})
, then Lemma
3
reduces to the unconstrained first-order condition
\nabla h(q^{*})=0
. Additionally, there is
\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{S}}(q^{*})=\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{S}}(q^{*})=\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Definition 9
(Hausdorff-Lipschitz Continuity)

Let
K:\mathcal{Q}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a set-valued mapping with nonempty images. We say that
K
is
Hausdorff-Lipschitz continuous
on
\mathcal{Q}
if there exists a constant
L\geq 0
such that for any
q_{1},q_{2}\in\mathcal{X}
,

\displaystyle d_{H}\big(S(q_{1}),S(q_{2})\big)\leq L\|q_{1}-q_{2}\|,

where
d_{H}(\cdot,\cdot)
denotes the Hausdorff distance:

\displaystyle d_{H}(A,B):=\max\Bigg\{\sup_{a\in A}\inf_{b\in B}\|a-b\|,\sup_{b\in B}\inf_{a\in A}\|a-b\|\Bigg\},\quad A,B\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}.

Definition 10
(Smooth Boundary
(
Lee 2003
)
)

Let
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a closed set. We say that
\mathcal{S}
has a smooth boundary at
q\in\operatorname{bd}(\mathcal{S})
if there exists a neighborhood
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
of
q
and a continuously differentiable function

\varphi:U\to\mathbb{R}
such that

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}\cap U

\displaystyle=\{z\in U:\varphi(z)\leq 0\},\quad\operatorname{bd}(\mathcal{S})\cap U=\{z\in U:\varphi(z)=0\},\quad\text{and}\quad\nabla\varphi(q)\neq 0.

In this case, the tangent space with respect to
\text{bd}(\mathcal{S})
at
q\in\text{bd}(\mathcal{S})
is

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{\text{bd}(\mathcal{S})}(q):=\{d\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\nabla\varphi(q)^{\top}d=0\}.

Lemma 4
(
(
Bubeck 2015
, Lemma 3.1)
)

Suppose
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
is convex, closed, and nonempty. For any
q_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and any
q_{2}\in\mathcal{S}
, it follows that

\displaystyle\langle\Proj_{\mathcal{S}}(q_{1})-q_{2},\Proj_{\mathcal{S}}(q_{1})-q_{1}\rangle\leq 0.

In this way, take

\displaystyle\langle g,q_{3}^{\eta,g}-q_{2}\rangle\leq-\frac{1}{\eta}\langle q_{3}^{\eta,g}-q_{2},q_{3}^{\eta,g}-q_{3}\rangle,\quad\forall q_{2},q_{3}\in\mathcal{S}.

Technical challenges for constrained BLO.

For unconstrained BLO, a key step in establishing the tight

is deriving

\displaystyle\langle\nabla v(x),d\rangle=\langle\nabla_{x}g(x,y_{g}^{*}(x)),d\rangle

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(b)}}{{=}}

\displaystyle\lim_{r\downarrow 0}\frac{1}{r}(g(x+rd,y_{g}^{*}(x+rd))-g(x,y_{g}^{*}(x)))

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(c)}}{{=}}

\displaystyle\langle\nabla_{x}g(x,y_{g}^{*}(x)),d\rangle+\langle\nabla_{y}g(x,y_{g}^{*}(x)),\lim_{r\downarrow 0}\frac{y_{g}^{*}(x+rd)-y_{g}^{*}(x)}{r}\rangle

where (a) follows the
\nabla v(x)=\nabla_{x}g(x,y_{g}^{*}(x))
; (b) is the definition of directional derivative; and (c) applies Taylor expansion and
\|y_{g}^{*}(x+rd)-y_{g}^{*}(x)\|=\mathcal{O}(rd)
:

\displaystyle g(x+rd,y_{g}^{*}(x+rd))=

\displaystyle g(x,y_{g}^{*}(x))+\langle\nabla_{x}g(x,y_{g}^{*}(x)),rd\rangle

\displaystyle+\langle\nabla_{y}g(x,y_{g}^{*}(x)),\,y_{g}^{*}(x+rd)-y_{g}^{*}(x)\rangle+\mathcal{O}(r^{2}).

In this way, we can conclude the proof.

Negative theoretical observations of PBGD-Free

\displaystyle|f(x,y_{1})-f(x,y_{2})|\leq l_{f,0}\|y_{1}-y_{2}\|,\quad\forall y_{1},y_{2}\in\mathcal{Y}.

For differentiable

4.2
A relaxed condition: UL
(\delta,\alpha)
-flatness

In this section, We introduce a relaxed condition that is less restrictive, and therefore more general, than the uniform small-Lipschitz-constant assumption on
f(x,\cdot)
.

Definition 11
(
(\delta,\alpha)
-flatness)

Function
f(x,\cdot):\mathcal{Y}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is called
(\delta,\alpha)
-flat with modulus
c\geq 0
at
y_{g}^{*}(x)\in S_{g}(x)
with
\delta\geq 0,\alpha\geq 1
if

|f(x,y_{g}^{*}(x))-f(x,y)|\leq c\|y_{g}^{*}(x)-y\|^{\alpha}+\delta,~~~\forall y\in\mathcal{Y}.

(32)

When
\delta=0
and
y_{g}^{*}(x)
is replaced by an arbitrary
y^{\prime}
, Definition
11
reduces to the standard Hölder condition. Under the Lipschitzness condition in
Assumption
1
.
1
, the function
f(x,\cdot)
satisfies
(0,1)
-flatness with modulus
c=l_{f,0}
. However, setting
\delta=0
naturally imposes the constraint
\alpha\leq 1
whenever
\nabla_{y}f(x,y_{g}^{*}(x))\neq 0
. Unless otherwise specified, we assume
\delta>0
and
\alpha>1
when referring to flatness afterwards.

In the following, we discuss the relations of Lipschitz condition in Assumption
1
.
1
and the new flatness condition in Definition
11
through several observations.

Observation 1

Under the
l_{F_{\gamma},1}
-smoothness condition of
f(x,\cdot)
, if
\|\nabla_{y}f(x,y_{g}^{*}(x))\|=\delta^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}
, then
f(x,\cdot)
is
(\delta,\alpha)
-flat with some modulus
0\leq c\leq\mathcal{O}(l_{F_{\gamma},1})
.

Proof

For
\|y-y_{g}^{*}(x)\|\geq 1
, by the
l_{f,0}=\delta^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}
-Lipschitzness of
f(x,y)
in
y
, there is

\displaystyle\|f(x,y)-f(x,y_{g}^{*}(x))\|\leq l_{f,0}\|y-y_{g}^{*}(x)\|\leq\delta^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}\|y-y_{g}^{*}(x)\|^{\alpha}.

For small
\|y-y_{g}^{*}(x)\|<1
, Taylor’s expansion gives

\displaystyle f(x,y)-f(x,y_{g}^{*}(x))=\langle\nabla_{y}f(x,y_{g}^{*}(x)),y-y_{g}^{*}(x)\rangle+R(x,y).

Here,
R(x,y)
is a remainder. By Hölder-Continuous Gradient Condition
(
Berger et al. 2020
)
,
(
Nesterov 2013
, Section 2)
, which is implied by the smoothness, there exists some
0\leq c\leq l_{F_{\gamma},1}/2
,
1<\alpha<2
such that
\|R(x,y)\|\leq c\|y-y_{g}^{*}(x)\|^{\alpha}
. By Cauchy-Schwarz’s inequality, there is

\displaystyle\|\langle\nabla_{y}f(x,y_{g}^{*}(x)),y-y_{g}^{*}(x)\rangle\|\leq

\displaystyle\|\nabla_{y}f(x,y_{g}^{*}(x))\|\|y-y_{g}^{*}(x)\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\delta^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}\|y-y_{g}^{*}(x)\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\delta+\|y-y_{g}^{*}(x)\|^{\alpha}

where the last inequality holds as for
a,b\in(0,1)
and
\alpha\in(1,2)
, there is
ab\leq\max\{a,b\}^{2}\leq\max\{a,b\}^{\alpha}\leq a^{\alpha}+b^{\alpha}
and here
a=\delta^{1/\alpha}
and
b=\|y-y_{g}^{*}(x)\|
.

Observation
1
demonstrates that assuming small
\|\nabla_{y}f(x,y_{g}^{*}(x))\|
is stronger than assuming flatness since
\ell_{f,0}=\delta^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}>\delta
when
\alpha>1
. Below, we will show that the flatness condition automatically holds near the LL optimal solution
y_{g}^{*}(x)
.

Observation 2

Under the
l_{F_{\gamma},1}
-smoothness condition of
f
, the
(\delta,\alpha)
-flatness condition holds automatically for all
y\in\{y:|f(x,y)-f(x,y_{g}^{*}(x))|\leq\delta\}
.

Since
f
is continuous and smooth, this observation implies that the flatness condition permits abrupt, unstable changes in the
\mathcal{O}(\delta)
-neighborhood of
y_{g}^{*}(x)
. This demonstrates that the flatness condition is relatively mild and further confirms that it is strictly weaker than the small Lipschitz condition, which explicitly requires
\|\nabla_{y}f(x,y_{g}^{*}(x))\|
to be small.

visualizes Observation

Observation 3

Under
(\delta,\alpha)
-flatness, the growth rate of
f(x,\cdot)
outside the
\mathcal{O}(\delta)
neighborhood is

\displaystyle\frac{|f(x,y)-f(x,y_{g}^{*}(x))|}{\|y-y_{g}^{*}(x)\|}\leq\begin{cases}\mathcal{O}(1),&\text{if }\mathcal{O}(\delta)\leq\|y-y_{g}^{*}(x)\|\leq\mathcal{O}(1),\\
\mathcal{O}\left(\|y-y_{g}^{*}(x)\|^{\alpha-1}\right),&\text{if }\|y-y_{g}^{*}(x)\|>\mathcal{O}(1).\end{cases}

(33)

This is obtained by dividing both sides of the flatness inequality by
\|y_{g}^{*}(x)-y\|
. For small
\|y_{g}^{*}(x)-y\|
, the second term dominates and leads to a
{\cal O}(1)
bound, which is the same as the Lipschitz condition. However, for

\|y_{g}^{*}(x)-y\|
, since
\alpha>1
, the bound
\mathcal{O}(\|y_{g}^{*}(x)-y\|^{\alpha-1})
can be larger than
{\cal O}(1)
.
This observation further demonstrates that the flatness condition relaxes the Lipschitzness of
f(x,\cdot)
in Assumption
1
.
1
. Specifically, while Lipschitz continuity would require a uniform bound on the gradient, flatness allows for a higher growth rate of
\mathcal{O}(\|y-y_{g}^{*}(x)\|^{\alpha-1})
.
For UL objective
f(x,\cdot)
with fixed

, given a

\alpha
and modulus

, the

\displaystyle\delta(x):=\max\{0,|f(x,y_{g}^{*}(x))-f(x,y_{\gamma}^{*}(x))|-c\|y_{g}^{*}(x)-y_{\gamma}^{*}(x)\|^{\alpha}\}.

(34)

\delta(x)
can effectively be

. Therefore, together with Observation

, we can write

f(x,y^{\prime})\approx f(x,y_{g}^{*}(x))+c\|y^{\prime}-y_{g}^{*}(x)\|^{\alpha}
for some
\alpha>1
and constant

.
In such cases, the additive term in (

) vanishes and
\delta(x)
is effectively zero.
This implies that the flatness condition can hold even when no Lipschitz bound on
f(x,\cdot)
is available, particularly for locally curved objectives.

\displaystyle\pi_{x,y}(r|z):=\text{softmax}(\text{model}_{x,y}(z))_{r}.

\displaystyle q_{\beta}(x,y;z,r_{w},r_{\ell}):=\beta\log\frac{\pi_{x,y}(r_{w}\mid z)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(r_{w}\mid z)}-\beta\log\frac{\pi_{x,y}(r_{\ell}\mid z)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(r_{\ell}\mid z)},

The SFT loss operates on supervised dataset

6.2.3
LLM PEFT problem

In this section, we consider the PEFT problem in (
28
) experimented on open-source large language models.

Problem setting and its flatness

SFT for pre-trained LLMs enhances their adaptation to downstream tasks, while DPO is commonly used to align the model with human preferences. To achieve both goals, a straightforward approach is to sequentially optimize both objectives. However, this often leads to a forgetting issue
(
Fernando et al. 2024
)
. In practical applications, it is common to fine-tune only a lightweight head on SFT while keeping the backbone fixed or lightly tuned
(
Pfeiffer et al. 2020
;
Zaken et al. 2021
;
Ren et al. 2023
)
. This naturally leads to a decomposition of the model into a backbone
x
(e.g., attention weights) and an output head
y
.

In this way, the bilevel PEFT formulation (
28
) addresses the catastrophic forgetting issue by prioritizing SFT at the LL to create a more reliable base model and use DPO at the UL to guide the human preference alignment. This hierarchical structure preserves task-specific knowledge in
y
, while guiding
x
updates toward better preference alignment.

This formulation operates in a standard post-SFT setting, where DPO fine-tunes a pretrained backbone after SFT has adapted the model to downstream tasks. Moreover, given that user preferences are typically consistent across tasks, this approach trains a representation model that captures these preferences, allowing only the head to be fine-tuned for future tasks and achieving parameter-efficient adaptation. In this way, the head can specialize for SFT tasks, while the backbone is optimized via DPO for both alignment and generalization.

As preliminarily demonstrated in Fig.
8
, this
BLO PEFT problem
in (
28
) features flatness (small
\delta
), which is further corroborated by the observation in experiment that
the LL solution
y_{g}^{*}(x)
and
y_{\gamma}^{*}(x)
have
\ell_{2}
-distance significantly greater than target
\epsilon
, suggesting a negligible flatness constant
\delta(x)
by (
34
).

In our experiments, we adopt the low rank adaptation (LoRA)
(
Hu et al. 2022
)

x

Llama-3-3b

(
Grattafiori et al. 2024

and

(

)

(

7
Conclusions

In summary, we address the challenges of BLO with coupled constraints by providing a tighter smoothness anlysis of value-function-based penalty reformulations. Building upon this, we propose the alternating PBGD scheme and develop an efficient PBGD-Free algorithm, which eliminates the costly value-function computation while maintaining practical efficiency. To establish the theoretical guarantees of the PBGD-Free algorithm, we introduce a novel flatness condition that relaxes the traditional Lipschitz continuity assumption, particularly when the Lipschitz constant is small. We provide both theoretical and empirical insights into this flatness condition and demonstrate its validity. Under this condition, we further prove that PBGD-Free achieves a convergence rate comparable to that of standard gradient descent. Our theoretical insights, complemented by empirical validation on both toy and large-scale tasks, demonstrate that efficient and scalable BLO methods are achievable even in the presence of complex constraint structures.
