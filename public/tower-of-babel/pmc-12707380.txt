Correlation of change in V4c implantable Collamer lens vaulting and pupil diameter in different light conditions
Authors: Rajesh Sinha, Reena Singh, Manasi Tripathi, M. Sravanthi, Jeewan S. Titiyal
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Purpose:To study the change in the vault of phakic Intraocular Lens (IOL) (pIOL) with RT view anterior segment optical coherence tomography (ASOCT) in different light conditions and define its relationship with the change in pupillary diameter.Design:Prospective Observational Study.Methods:Thirty eyes of 15 myopic subjects were enrolled. The implantable Collamer lens (ICL) vault was measured in three light conditions: room light, scotopic, and photopic conditions. Pupillary diameter was recorded in three light conditions using ASOCT and ImageJ software. The primary outcome measure was the change in ICL vault from baseline (room light) to scotopic and photopic conditions, respectively.Results:The mean age of participants was 22.33 ± 3.1 years. V4c ICL implantation was done for a mean refractive error of −10.08 ± 4.0D. The mean ICL vault in room light, scotopic, and photopic conditions were 568.57 ± 215.83 µm, 567.47 ± 225.11 µm, and 418.4 ± 210.04 µm, respectively. As compared to room light, there was no significant change in the ICL vault in scotopic condition (P = 0.89), but it was significantly decreased in photopic (P < 0.0001) condition. On regression analysis of change in vault in relation to the change in pupillary diameter, it was found that in photopic condition with every 1 mm decrease in pupil diameter as measured with ImageJ and ASOCT, there was a decrease in vault of 59.51 µm and 56.19 µm, respectively.Conclusion:ICL vault decreases consistently and significantly in photopic conditions; however, scotopic condition has an inconsistent and lesser effect.

Implantable Collamer lens (ICL) has now become an established modality for refractive correction of ametropia, especially for moderate and high myopia. The sulcus-supported ICL (Visian ICL; STAAR Surgical, Nidau, Switzerland) is now the most commonly used phakic IOL (pIOL) for correction of refractive error.

In addition to precise ICL power calculation, it is equally important to select an appropriate size or overall diameter of ICL to achieve optimal results. The vaulting of ICL depends upon the overall diameter of ICL, which in turn is calculated based on white-to-white (WTW) or sulcus-to-sulcus (STS) measurement and anterior chamber depth.[12] Improper selection of ICL size can result in a higher or lower vault, and in both instances, there are increased chances of complications. Despite the presence of ICL vault within the safe lower and upper limit ranging between 250 and 750 µm,[34] adverse effects in the form of anterior subcapsular cataract, pupillary block glaucoma, pigment dispersion, and endothelial compromise are possible.[5]

Accommodation and pupillary diameter changes with iris movements do have some effect on ICL vaulting, which may have some bearing on the occurrence of side effects due to ICL. Lee H, et al.[67] have shown that accommodation has a lesser effect on ICL vaulting than changing light conditions. But Chixin Du et al.[8] found that a significant change in vaulting occurs with pharmacological accommodation. Petternel V et al.,[9] however, have shown that subjective and pharmacological accommodation does not affect vaulting significantly, but photopic stimulation can cause a significant change in vaulting.

An individual with an implanted ICL may be exposed to various light conditions in their day-to-day activities. We have tried to study the intermittent changes in ICL vaulting under three different light conditions: room light, dark adaptation of 10 min with the head covered with a dark black cloth, and direct stimulation of the pupil with intense light without changing the room light.

To the best of our knowledge and literature search, this is the first study evaluating changes in an ICL vault in different light conditions and by creating a situation of direct exposure to light by directly stimulating the pupil for photopic recording. This study will further add to the understanding of the quantitative correlation of change in V4c ICL vaulting and pupil diameter.

Methods

A prospective, observational study was conducted in the Indian population, implanted with ICL, from April 2021 to June 2022. Ethical clearance was obtained from the Institute’s Ethics Committee. The tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki were adhered to. All the patients were high myopes implanted with ICL. The preoperative investigations included distance uncorrected and corrected visual acuity, refraction, intraocular pressure (IOP) on a non-contact tonometer (Non-Contact Tono/Pachymeter TONOPACHY™ NT-530P, NIDEK Co. Ltd. Japan), Specular count (Topcon SP-1P, Topcon Medical Systems, Oakland, NJ), and PentacamTM (OCULUS, Wetzlar, Germany). The IOL Master 500 (Carl Zeiss Meditec, Inc. 5160 Hacienda Drive, Dublin, CA 94568 USA) was used for measuring anterior chamber depth (ACD), WTW, and mesopic pupil diameter.

ICL vault was measured with anterior segment optical coherence tomography (ASOCT, Fourier domain RT Vue, Optovue, Fremont, CA, USA) with a scan diameter of 6 mm. All the scans were captured under three light conditions: room light, after 10 minutes of dark adaptation with the head covered with a nontransparent black cloth, and with an intense light directly thrown upon the pupil. The first scan in the room light was taken with the patient fixating with the eye under study on the given target on ASOCT, and the other eye relaxed by asking him to look at a distant target. The scotopic scan was taken with the head covered with a black cloth after dark adaptation for at least 10 minutes. The third and final scan was taken under photopic conditions with a light-emitting diode (LED) torch light directly focused on the pupil of the eye under study. Patrick Giudicelli in the apple store (version 1.1.0) measured lighting levels using the Newtaste studios Lux meter. In all three lighting conditions, the vaulting of the ICL and the horizontal pupillary diameter were measured. The vaulting for each eye in different light conditions was measured at the center of the pupil [Fig. 1]. All the patients were divided into three groups based on vault in photopic condition; group 1 included vaulting less than or equal to 250 µm, group 2 vaulting 250 to 750 µm, and group 3 of vaulting more than 750 µm.

ASOCT has an inherent limitation of 6 mm light entry, and hence, it was difficult to measure the pupillary diameter in scotopic conditions when the pupil dilated more than 6 mm, and in photopic conditions, the details were sometimes blurred at the iris ends. To overcome these difficulties ImageJ program was used, which is a free Java image processing program (developed National Institute of Health (NIH), version 1.4, home page: rsbweb. NIH. Gov). With this software, fixed magnification-controlled photographs were taken during measurement of pupillary diameter and ICL vault. It can calculate area and pixel value statistics of user-defined selections. It can also measure distances and angles. Pupil diameter was standardized with a 6 mm arrow available on the screen control of ASOCT, and accordingly, the horizontal pupil diameter was calculated. In the software, the 6 mm arrow was measured, a value was standardized, and others were measured and calculated against this value.

For measurements of intensity of light, a pocket light meter was used (Nuwaste studios pocket light meter app and also by Lux meter by Patrick Giudicelli in Apple store version 1.1.0). It is a camera-based app wherein the camera is placed at the level of a patient’s eye from behind, with the camera facing toward the light source. The light intensity readings were noted in different lighting conditions. The intensity estimated in room light, scotopic, and photopic conditions was 74–78 Lux, 0 Lux, and 6200–6238 Lux, respectively.

The data obtained was analyzed using STATA Statistical Software. Paired t-test and the Wilcoxon signed-rank test were used for comparing the variables. The limits of agreement have been calculated for the two methods of pupil measurements, and Bland-Altman plot analysis was done. Spearman’s correlation was seen for pupil diameter and vaulting in three light conditions. Spearman’s correlation and regression analysis were done for the change in pupil diameter and the change in vaulting.

Results

A total of 30 eyes of 15 myopic subjects of mean age 22.33 ± 3.1 years, including 10 males and five females, were enrolled. The mean refractive error in spherical equivalent was −10.08 ± 4.0 diopters. Their mean follow-up at the time of measurement of vault was 8.33 ± 3.84 (range of 2 to 18) months. The preoperative uncorrected visual acuity (UCVA) was 1.32 ± 0.37 log MAR, and postoperative mean UCVA was 0.187 ± 0.167 log MAR (P = 0.001). The mean ACD and WTW were 3.12 ± 0.31 mm and 11.83 ± 0. 54 mm, respectively.

On comparing preoperative (14.3 ± 1.7 mm Hg) and postoperative (14.73 ± 1.8 mm Hg) values, there was no significant change seen in IOP (P = 0.32) at 8-week postoperative. No significant change in IOP was seen in all the groups, irrespective of the change in the vault (group 1; P = 0.14, group 2; P = 0.39, group 3; P = 0.74).

At 8-week postoperative follow-up, there was a significant decrease seen in specular count (preoperative specular count was 3229.43 ± 256.29 cells/mm2 and postoperative, it was 3024 ± 271.54 cells/mm2, mean difference 205 ± 197 cells/mm2, P = 0.001).

The ICL size selected on WTW diameter and ACD on PentacamTM. The average size of implanted ICL was 12.83 ± 0.35 mm. The mean ICL vault of 30 eyes in room light, scotopic, and photopic conditions was 568.57 ± 215.83 µm, 567.47 ± 225.11 µm, and 418.4 ± 210.04 µm, respectively. Normal range (250–750 µm) of vaulting was seen in 25 (83.33%) eyes in room light, 23 (76.66%) in photopic, and 20 (66.67%) in scotopic conditions. While the mean difference in vaulting between room light and scotopic conditions was not statistically significant (mean difference 1.1 ± 42.17, P = 0.8874), individual variability resulted in some eyes falling outside the normal range. Conversely, there was a significant decrease in vaulting in photopic conditions as compared to room light (mean difference 150.17 ± 53.14, P = 0.001). Further, it was seen that in photopic conditions, all 30 eyes showed a significant decrease in vaulting in comparison with the room light (P = 0.001). In scotopic condition, 16 (53.33%) patient had increased in vaulting (31.81 ± 22.65 µm, median 25, range of 3 to 83 mm), and 14 (46.67%) eyes had a decrease in vaulting (34 ± 29.79 µm, median 27, range of 3 to 81 mm), but in photopic condition, all the patients had a decrease in vaulting. Less than 250 µm vaulting was seen in 7 (23.33%) eyes in photopic conditions and 1 (3%) in scotopic conditions. All seven eyes with a lower than 250 µm vault in photopic condition also had lower ACD. These seven eyes had a mean initial vaulting of 350 ± 79.47 µm in room light that decreased to 198.28 ± 43.92 µm in photopic condition. None of the eyes showed lower than 250 µm vaults in the room light. Higher vault of more than 750 µm was seen in five (16.67%) eyes in room light, six (20%) eyes in scotopic, and three (10%) eyes in photopic conditions [Table 1].

In the room light, average pupil diameter on ImageJ and ASOCT was 5.2 ± 0.71 (n = 30) and 4.7 ± 0.89 (n = 22), respectively. In scotopic condition, average pupil diameter on ImageJ and ASOCT was 6.12 ± 0.61 (n = 30) and 5.2 ± 0.41 (n = 10), respectively. In photopic conditions, average pupil diameter on ImageJ and ASOCT was 2.8 ± 0.33 (n = 30) and 2.7 ± 0.52 (n = 24), respectively. Bland-Altman plot analysis was done to evaluate the agreement between describing methods of pupil measurement on ImageJ and ASOCT, and it was found that the two methods have good limits of agreement [Fig. 2].

A Bland-Altman plot showing the difference between mean pupil diameters measured by ImageJ software and ASOCT on the y-axis plotted against average values of two on the x-axis right picture in room light, scotopic, and photopic conditions. ASOCT = Anterior segment optical coherence tomography

There was a significant decrease in the diameter of the pupil in photopic conditions in comparison with room light, on both ASOCT and ImageJ software measurements (both methods n = 24 and P = 0.001). Also, there was a consistent decrease in vaulting in photopic conditions as compared to room light in all 30 eyes. Therefore, we tried to see the correlation between change in pupil diameter and change in vaulting on changing light conditions from room light to photopic condition, but widely scattered values were seen on a scatter plot [Fig. 3a] due to weak correlation between the two (Spearman’s correlation, r = 0.18). Even though the relation of decrease in pupil diameter and decrease in vaulting was found weakly correlated, but on regression analysis of pupil diameter and vaulting, it was observed that on ImageJ, a 1 mm decrease in pupil diameter from room light to photopic condition caused a 59.51 µm (95% confidence interval 51.13–67.89) decrease in vaulting. Likewise, on ASOCT, a 1 mm decrease of pupil diameter caused a 56.19 µm (95% confidence interval 43.99–68.39) decrease in vaulting.

There was an increase in pupil diameter in all the eyes by changing from room light to scotopic condition on ImageJ measurement (mean increase 0.87 ± 0.38 mm; P = 0.001). In the scotopic condition, 16 patients had increased vaulting, and 14 had decreased vault in comparison with the room light. Both these groups had an increase in pupil diameters in scotopic condition as compared to the room light, and the difference in the mean increase was not significant (Mann–Whitney test, z = 0.77). The correlation of an increase in pupil diameter and change in vaulting was weak [r = 0.0199, Fig. 3b].

Discussion

The ICL vaulting is not constant inside the eye, and it keeps on fluctuating with the light condition and accommodation.[7891011] In the current study, the dynamic nature of change in ICL vaulting has been evaluated in different light conditions. Pharmacological drops have not been used, as natural dynamic pupillary change cannot be simulated with drugs.

Although previous studies have shown the dynamic nature of vault changes under different light conditions,[612] the amount of variation and its relationship with variation in pupil diameter, ACD, WTW, and the diameter of ICL used is not well defined.

Safe vaulting of 250 µm to 750 µm was defined in earlier models before V4c ICL. With the introduction of the V4c model with central aquaport, there is a growing consensus that vaults lesser than 250 µm may also be safe. Various authors have reported different cut-offs for suspected lower vaulting causing anterior subcapsular cataract: Gonvers[13] reported 90 µm, Schmidinger[14] reported 98 ± 100 µm, Rayner[15] noted 50 µm, and Maeng et al.[16] have suggested a range of 50 to 250 as minimal limits of vaulting. In all the above studies, it was observed that few eyes developed complications, and a few of them remained normal even with similar kind of lower vaulting. Hence, Maeng et al.[16] has suggested that prior screening for eyes at risk should be done. They observed that higher age, lower initial vault, and higher power of implantations are at greater risk for complications related to low vaulting. We are also in consensus with Maeng et al.[16] that the initial safe, vault should be higher than 250 µm as there can be further compromise in vaulting with photopic stimulation. This compromise in vaulting can lead to crystalline lens pIOL touch in the periphery, increased pigment dispersion and inflammation due to the movement of pIOL, and compromised aqueous circulation leading to cataract formation.

In their study, Kato et al.[17] found that light-induced pupillary constriction significantly lowered ICL vault. Song et al.[18] also found a significant correlation between change in vault and PD change under dark-to-light conditions for all patients as well as the high-vault subgroup. During our investigation, we observed that in photopic condition, there was a significant decrease in vault in all the eyes in comparison with room light, but in scotopic conditions, there was no significant change in vault as compared to room light. In fact, in scotopic condition, there was almost equal, although insignificant chance of increase or decrease in vaulting (53.33% vs. 46.67%) as compared to room light. This could be because of the fact that ICL always stays beneath the iris and is in contact with it which causes more of posterior pushing effect on ICL during constriction of the pupil. But at the same time, the dilatation of the pupil causes less effective relaxation over ICL to increase the vault of ICL. We tried to focus on those eyes, whose initial room light vault was within normal range, but decreased to lower than 250 µm in photopic conditions. Xiong et al.[19] found that the rate of change of vault according to light conditions had a significant decrease with increased mesopic vault. In our study, we found that eyes with low photopic vault (<250 microns) were not significantly different from other eyes on the basis of age. While refractive power of the pIOL did not significantly correlate with vault change in our sample, smaller ICL diameters and ACD values were consistently associated with lower photopic vaults [Table 1]. With the current model of ICL with aquaport, it was thought that lower or borderline vault would be safe as aqueous is allowed to flow through the port. But in our study, we have seen that with ICLs having lower or border line vault, there could be a further decrease in vault, vault fluctuations can occur in daily life situations with changes in light conditions, and these eyes can have lower than safe limit vaulting in photopic conditions. This can lead to complications in the form of iris pigment dispersion, intermittent peripheral ICL and lens touch and secondary glaucoma in the long run.

Our findings suggest that it is advisable to ensure a comfortable safety margin above 250 µm vault in room light, as vault may significantly decrease in photopic conditions. Such reductions could increase the risk of anterior subcapsular cataract or pigment dispersion, especially in borderline cases.

There could be some fallacies in our study, especially during acquisition of vaulting on ASOCT. Some accommodative component could not have been avoided even though subjects were instructed to focus on the target for fixation with the eye under study and other eye focused at a distance to avoid physiological accommodation. Additionally, it is fair to acknowledge that vaulting can vary across different makes and models of pIOLs. However, our study was limited to the V4c model with a central aquaport. Further comparative research is necessary to generalize our findings across other pIOL designs under varying light conditions for a more comprehensive understanding. Further, our study has another limitation of having a lower number of eyes recruited.

Despite the limitations, this study further adds to our knowledge that position of ICL in the eyes does not remain constant, and hence, vaulting is dynamic in nature that changes with light conditions. This intermittent movement can lead to increased iris rubbing and pigment dispersion, intermittent angle compromise, intermittent natural lens touch, and compromise in the metabolism of the lens due to off and on compromise in aqueous flow. Borderline lower vaulting can further get compromised in photopic conditions. Further, it is a proven fact that the vault reduces per se over a period of time in the late postoperative period due to various factors.[20] Hence, it may not be considered appropriate to have vaults lower than 250 µm even with the current generation of ICL.

The present study adds to the considerations of dynamic pupil measurement before final calculations of pIOLs diameter for any type and designs of pIOLs. This can help us understand the dynamic relationship of ICL with anterior segment structures of the eye and to predict safe vaulting and long-term safety.

Conclusion

Vaulting of V4c ICLs is not static and decreases consistently under photopic conditions, while changes in scotopic conditions are variable and less predictable. A reduction in vault below 250 μm may occur in borderline cases, highlighting the need to maintain a safe margin above this threshold at the time of implantation. These findings emphasize the importance of considering dynamic pupil changes during ICL sizing to ensure long-term safety and minimize risks such as cataract formation or pigment dispersion.
