Peripheral blood GATA2 expression impacts RNF213 mutation penetrance and clinical severity in moyamoya disease
Authors: Yohei Mineharu, Takahiko Kamata, Mei Tomoto, Noriaki Sato, Yoshinori Tamada, Takeshi Funaki, Yuki Oichi, Kouji H Harada, Akio Koizumi, Tetsuaki Kimura, Ituro Inoue, Yasushi Okuno, Susumu Miyamoto, Yoshiki Arakawa
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe p.R4810K founder mutation in the RNF213 gene confers susceptibility to moyamoya disease (MMD) and non-MMD intracranial artery disease. However, penetrance is incomplete, and the underlying molecular mechanism remains unknown.Methods and resultsTranscriptome analysis of peripheral blood was conducted with nine MMD patients and five unaffected mutation carriers from four familial MMD pedigrees. Bayesian network analysis identified upregulated gene modules associated with lipid metabolism and leucocyte development (including GATA2 and SLC45A3), and epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) signalling (UBTD1). It also identified downregulated gene modules related to mitochondrial ribosomal proteins (RPS3A and RPL26), and cytotoxic T cell immunity (GZMA and TRGC1). The GATA2 network was replicated through weighted gene co-expression network analysis and further examined in a case–control study, comprising 43 MMD patients, 16 non-MMD patients, 19 unaffected carriers and 35 healthy controls. GATA2 exhibited a significant linear correlation with SLC45A3 and was significantly higher in MMD patients compared with age-matched and sex-matched unaffected carriers or wild-type controls. Among patients with the p.R4810K mutation, higher GATA2 expression was associated with an earlier age of onset, bilateral involvement and symptomatic disease onset.ConclusionsPeripheral blood GATA2 expression was associated with increased penetrance of the RNF213 mutation and more severe clinical manifestations in MMD.Keywords: Cerebrovascular Disorders, Genetics, Risk Factors, Stroke

Introduction

Moyamoya disease (MMD) is a progressive cerebrovascular condition characterised by steno-occlusive lesions at the terminal portion of the internal carotid arteries, with the development of multiple collateral networks, including basal moyamoya vessels and periventricular anastomoses.1 2 While the exact pathophysiology of MMD is not yet fully understood, various related molecules have been gradually identified.2 3 The RNF213 gene is a major susceptibility gene for MMD, with the founder mutation p.R4810K occurring in approximately 90% of Japanese patients, 70% of Korean patients and 40% of Chinese patients.4 Other mutations were found in around 10% in European populations,4 and these findings were replicated in multiple studies.5,7 Even though the p.R4810K founder mutation in the RNF213 gene increases the risk of MMD by 300 times, its penetrance is incomplete. Given that the p.R4810K mutation is found in 1–2% of the general Japanese population, the penetrance of the heterozygous mutation is estimated to be around 0.5–1.0%. This has prompted a search for factors that could modify penetrance, but none have been definitively identified. Furthermore, the p.R4810K mutation is associated not only with MMD but also with non-moyamoya intracranial large artery disease (ICAD).8 9 Identifying modifier genes for MMD could provide insights into the differences between MMD and ICAD.

Recent studies have indicated that the immune system is involved in the pathogenesis of MMD,10,12 and RNF213 plays a role in regulating immune responses to intracellular pathogens.13 RNF213 is highly expressed in human immune cells, and a mouse model showed that RNF213 mutations affect dendritic cell function,14 and its deletion disturbs the function of regulatory T cells.15 16 The association of certain microbiota, such as Ruminococcus gnavus (R. gnavus) and Campylobacter, with MMD also suggests that systemic factors may play a role in the pathogenesis of MMD.17,19 Based on this, we hypothesised that transcriptional changes in peripheral blood might influence the penetrance of the p.R4810K mutation. In the present study, we identified differentially expressed gene networks between patients with MMD and unaffected mutation carriers in familial MMD. Furthermore, we demonstrated a linear relationship with clinical severity, such as disease onset patterns and bilaterality, and suggested its potential to explain the differences between MMD and ICAD.

Patients and methods

Study population and sample collection

Four families with incomplete penetrance of the p.R4810K mutation were used for transcriptome analysis (figure 1). This group comprised nine patients with MMD (#3, 4, 6, 7, 10, 12, 13, 15, 16) and five carriers (#2, 5, 9, 11, 14). Patient #2 later developed MMD, and patient #5 had middle cerebral artery stenosis, but at the time of participation, neither met the criteria for MMD, so they were classified as unaffected. Patients #1 and #8 provided blood samples, but they were excluded because the RNA extraction from these samples did not meet the quality standards. Gene expression of candidate genes identified by the transcriptome analysis was measured in a prospectively recruited replication cohort consisting of 43 patients with MMD, 16 patients with ICAD, 19 unaffected family members of MMD patients including 10 mutation carriers (8 cases were family members of sporadic MMD patients, while 2 were family members of familial MMD patients), and 35 controls with other comorbidities but no history of cerebrovascular diseases. Because one-fourth of mutation carriers show cerebrovascular disease in familial MMD, we estimated that 25% of patients have abnormal gene expression, while 3% of unaffected carriers have it. Power calculation suggested that 41 cases and 34 controls are required for a two-sided α of 0.05 and a power of 80%. For age-matched and sex-matched analysis, participants were selected such that the age range was within 10 years and gender was matched. Peripheral blood samples (4–9 mL) were collected from the participants, and whole blood samples for transcriptome analysis were collected into PAXgene Blood RNA tubes (BD Bioscience, #762165, Franklin Lakes, New Jersey, USA).

Diagnosis was made based on the official diagnostic criteria established by the Research Committee on the Spontaneous Occlusion of the Circle of Willis, under the Ministry of Health and Welfare, Japan.20 ICAD was defined as stenotic lesions in the internal carotid artery (ICA), middle cerebral artery or anterior cerebral artery (ACA), without stenosis at the terminal portion of the ICA and without the presence of moyamoya vessels. Cases with isolated ACA stenosis were excluded due to the difficulty in distinguishing them from hypoplasia. Specifically, the majority of lesions involved M1 occlusion, which sometimes resembles MMD but does not meet the diagnostic criteria, and includes cases classified as ‘Q cases’.21 22

Transcriptome analysis

RNA was extracted according to the manufacturer’s instructions and kept frozen until use. The NEBNext Poly(A) mRNA Magnetic Isolation Module was used to isolate mRNA from the total RNA. The library was created using the NEBNext Ultra II RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina. Paired-end sequencing was performed on the NovaSeq 6000 (Illumina, #20028401, San Diego, California, USA). RNA libraries were prepared for sequencing using standard Illumina protocols. Read counts were calculated by featureCounts and normalised by the trimmed mean of M-values using edgeR. Details of RNA sequencing are described in the online supplemental methods.

Principal component analysis

To compare gene expression profiles between case and control groups, we performed Principal component analysis (PCA) on RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) data. Gene expression values were standardised using z-score transformation, and PCA was conducted using the scikit-learn library, retaining the first two principal components (PC1 and PC2). Group differences in PC1 and PC2 were assessed using Welch’s t-test, assuming unequal variance. To visualise group variability, we plotted 95% confidence ellipses based on the covariance matrix of each group. All analyses were performed in Python V.7.2.2.

Bayesian network analysis

INGOR.0.14.0 (a newer version of SiGN-BN) was used for Bayesian network (BN) estimation.23 The Basal network was estimated by a BN using the B-spline nonparametric regression model, a graphical model that represents conditional independence among variables as a directed acyclic graph. We included all genes without prioritising specific candidates based on differential expression analysis. Then, we constructed a basal network of these genes that represents common gene interactions in the entire cohort (cases and controls combined) and then extracted subnetworks that exhibited differences between cases and controls. Specifically, subnetworks using Edge Contribution value (ECv) were extracted, and ECvs were compared to identify differences in the networks of two samples between different conditions. The difference between ECvs, Delta Edge Contribution value (ΔECv), in sample groups is calculated. By extracting edges with ΔECv above a threshold value and the nodes connected to them, we can extract subnetworks that move characteristically under certain conditions. In past analyses, a threshold value of 1.0 for ΔECv has been used.24 Visualisation of the estimated network was performed using Cytoscape. Details of these analyses are described in the supplemental methods.

WGCNA

We applied weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) to construct gene co-expression networks and identify consensus modules and hub genes that play significant roles in enhancing the penetrance of the p.R4810K mutation, using RNA-Seq datasets. The relationships among the module eigengenes were visualised using the R library pvclust. Details of the WGCNA are described in the supplemental methods.

RT-qPCR

The extracted total RNA PAXgene Blood RNA kit was reverse transcribed into complementary DNA using Superscript III (Invitrogen, Waltham, Massachusetts, USA). The expression of target genes was normalised using RPS18 as the reference gene. Quantitative PCR (qPCR) was performed with KOD SYBR qPCR Mix (TOYOBO, Osaka, Japan) on a StepOnePlus system (Thermo Fisher Scientific), following the Delta Delta Ct method. The PCR cycling conditions adhered to the recommendations provided by TOYOBO. The forward and reverse primer sequences used for amplification are summarised in online supplemental table 1. Relative expression was calculated using the value from the patient #CVD004 as reference.

Genotyping

DNA was extracted from peripheral blood samples using the QIAamp DNA Blood Mini Kit (Qiagen, Germantown, Maryland, USA), following the manufacturer’s instructions. Genotyping for the p.R4810K mutation was performed for all participants using a TaqMan probe (Custom TaqMan SNP Genotyping Assays; Applied Biosystems, Foster City, California, USA) and a 7300/7500 Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems) in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions.

16S rRNA metagenomic analysis

Faecal samples were obtained from 48 individuals (26 MMD patients, 5 family members, 9 ICAD patients and 16 controls). The samples were collected in stool sample containers (FS-0014, TechnoSuruga Laboratory Co., Ltd., Shizuoka, Japan) and stored at −80°C. Details regarding the 16S rRNA metagenomic analysis have been described elsewhere,17 and in the supplemental methods.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were analysed using either the χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test, depending on the context. Continuous variables were compared using the Student’s t-test with Welch’s correction. The Cochran–Armitage test was applied to detect linear trends in categorical variables based on GATA2 expression levels, while Welch’s analysis of variance was used for continuous variables, with GATA2 expression levels categorised into low (0–8), medium (8–16) and high (>16). Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis was used to determine the optimal cut-off value for GATA2 expression in distinguishing patients from unaffected mutation carriers. The optimal cut-off on the ROC curve was identified as the point that maximises the vertical distance from the diagonal line (where Sensitivity = 1 − Specificity). Pearson’s correlation coefficient (r) was used to assess linear correlations between continuous variables. A p value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were conducted using R V.4.0.5 and GraphPad Prism V.9.3.1.

Results

Bayesian network analysis identified gene networks that affect the p.R4810K penetrance

RNA-Seq was conducted with 10 patients with MMD and 5 unaffected mutation carriers (figure 1). The clinical characteristics of the study population are shown in table 1. The unaffected carriers were significantly younger than the patients with MMD. First, as part of quality control, we performed PCA to examine whether there were distribution differences between cases and controls. The results showed that cases and controls exhibited distinct distributions, with a significant difference observed in PC2 (p=0.037, online supplemental figure 1). Next, we used Bayesian network analysis to identify gene networks associated with the increased penetrance of the p.R4810K mutation. The complete network was successfully visualised using the INGOR program (figure 2a). The ΔECv represents the difference in ECv between patients and unaffected mutation carriers, allowing for the identification of patient-specific networks that differ from those of unaffected carriers. The top 1% of the network by ΔECv was depicted (figure 2b). Networks with ΔECv >1.0 were considered significant, leading to the identification of four key networks (figure 2c–f). These included a downregulated network of mitochondrial ribosomal proteins (RPS3A, RPL23 and RPL26) and a downregulated T cell immunity network (GZMA, APOBEC3H and TRGC1). Additionally, two upregulated networks were identified: an epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) signalling network (UBTD1) and a lipid-leucocyte module (GATA2, SLC45A3, HDC and AKAP12) which is involved in both lipid metabolism and leucocyte development. Although it did not reach the significance threshold, another interesting network emerged (figure 2b, dotted circle; details in online supplemental figure 2), which included the upregulation of IL-1β and SOCS3.

Association of GATA2 with MMD and increased penetrance of the RNF213 mutation

The significant association of the lipid-leucocyte module with increased penetrance of the p.R4810K mutation was confirmed through WGCNA (figure 3a). RT-qPCR analysis also showed a strong correlation between GATA2 and SLC45A3 expression levels (figure 3b). We then explored the association of GATA2 expression with MMD, including patients with ICAD, a type of RNF213-related vasculopathy. GATA2 expression was measured using RT-qPCR, and the levels were compared among MMD patients with the mutation, age-matched and sex-matched unaffected mutation carriers, and wild-type controls without cerebrovascular diseases. As shown in figure 3c, GATA2 expression was similar between mutation carriers and wild-type controls (p=0.896), but significantly higher in patients with MMD compared with mutation carriers (p=0.0285). ROC analysis revealed an area under the curve of 0.74, with an optimal cut-off for relative GATA2 expression of 8.06 to distinguish MMD patients from unaffected mutation carriers (figure 3d). This cut-off value was applicable to the original familial populations (figure 1), where all individuals with GATA2 expression greater than 8.06 had MMD except for one patient who had middle cerebral artery stenosis (#5), and all individuals with GATA2 expression less than 8.06 did not have MMD except for one patient with MMD (#7).

We further examined GATA2 expression among individuals with the heterozygous p.R4810K mutation. GATA2 expression in patients with bilateral MMD was higher than in unaffected carriers, and it was also higher than in those with bilateral ICAD (figure 3e). This significance persisted when the entire study population was considered, with patients with MMD showing higher GATA2 expression than both controls and patients with ICAD (figure 3f). The difference between MMD patients and their family members might be due to the higher expression level in family members with the wild-type allele, suggesting that elevated GATA2 expression appears to increase the risk of MMD in the presence of the p.R4810K mutation.

Clinical significance of GATA2 expression in RNF213-related vasculopathy

We then assessed the clinical significance of GATA2 expression in RNF213-related vasculopathy, including MMD and ICAD. When we divided the patients into three groups based on relative GATA2 expression (0 to 8, 8 to 16, and ≥16), higher expression of GATA2 was significantly associated with younger age at onset (p=0.0353, figure 4a). To determine whether elevated GATA2 expression in early-onset patients might be influenced by the age at blood collection rather than its association with the disease, we examined GATA2 expression levels in a control group. The results indicated that GATA2 expression was higher in older individuals (figure 4b), suggesting that higher GATA2 levels in early-onset cases are linked to the disease and not confounded by the age at blood collection. We also investigated the relationship between GATA2 expression and disease duration (the time between disease onset and blood collection) and found no significant correlation (figure 4c), confirming that variability in this interval does not strongly affect GATA2 expression. When stratifying GATA2 expression by gender and age (children and adults), expression levels were higher in boys during childhood and slightly higher in women during adulthood, with a significant interaction effect. In an analysis limited to patients, the same trend was observed, although the difference was not statistically significant (online supplemental figure 3). Higher GATA2 expression was also associated with a higher proportion of bilateral involvement and symptomatic onset (figure 4d and e), although the linear trend was not statistically significant. The association between GATA2 expression and clinical features became less apparent when patients with ICAD were excluded (online supplemental figure 4).

Relationship between peripheral GATA2 levels and Ruminococcus gnavus in the gut

In our previous studies, we reported that the relative abundance of R. gnavus is associated with MMD.17 It has also been reported that R. gnavus promotes the secretion of type 2 cytokines, IL-5 and IL-13, from CD4+T cells.25 Given that GATA2 is involved in type 2 inflammation, which encompasses eosinophils and mast cells, we hypothesised a potential connection between R. gnavus and GATA2. Upon analysing the correlation between the relative abundance of R. gnavus and GATA2 expression, a weak but significant correlation was observed (figure 5a). However, when the analysis was restricted to patients only, the statistical significance disappeared (figure 5b).

Discussion

By comparing the transcriptome profiles of patients with MMD and unaffected mutation carriers in highly aggregated familial MMD families, we identified differentially expressed gene networks that could potentially influence the penetrance of the RNF213 founder mutation. The networks identified through Bayesian network analysis included (1) the lipid-leucocyte module, which regulates lipid metabolism and the development of leucocyte including eosinophils and mast cells (GATA2, SLC45A3, HDC and AKAP12); (2) mitochondrial ribosomal proteins and apoptosis (RPL23, RPS3A, RPL26 and others); (3) cytotoxic T cell immunity (GZMA, APOBEC3H and TRGC1); and (4) EGFR signalling (UBTD1). Of these networks, we focused on the lipid-leucocyte module because it showed the strongest signal in Bayesian network analysis and was replicated in WGCNA. Among individuals with the p.R4810K mutation, GATA2 expression was significantly higher in MMD patients compared with age-matched and sex-matched unaffected carriers. ROC analysis indicated that a GATA2 expression level above 8.06 could distinguish MMD patients from unaffected carriers. In familial MMD with the p.R4810K mutation, all individuals with GATA2 expression levels above 8.06 developed MMD, while only one individual with low GATA2 expression did so. Notably, higher GATA2 expression was significantly associated with an earlier age at onset. This observation is unlikely to be confounded by the age at which blood was collected, as GATA2 expression showed positive correlations with age in control individuals. Higher GATA2 expression was also associated with a greater proportion of bilateral disease distribution and a higher proportion of symptomatic onset, although significant linear correlation was not confirmed. Interestingly, GATA2 expression was higher in patients with MMD compared with those with ICAD, and the association between GATA2 and clinical features was more apparent when MMD and ICAD were analysed together, suggesting that RNF213-related vasculopathy represents a disease spectrum. It is believed that factors beyond RNF213, including GATA2, contribute to variations in phenotype across this spectrum.

GATA2 is a transcription factor that regulates the maintenance and expansion of haematopoietic stem cells and progenitor cells and is essential for mast cell and megakaryocyte development.26 27 It is also expressed on endothelial cells and haemogenic endothelium during the developmental stage, playing a role in endothelial-to-hematopoietic transition and endothelial-to-mesenchymal transition.28 A recent study showed that GATA2 is a member of the lipid-leucocyte module, along with SLC45A3, HDC, AKAP12, CPA3 and MS4A2.29 Indeed, we confirmed that GATA2 expression exhibited a significant linear correlation with SLC45A3. The lipid-leucocyte module is positively associated with triglycerides and negatively associated with high-density lipoprotein (HDL). This aligns with the finding that low HDL is a risk factor for MMD. Genes in the lipid-leucocyte module play a role in the development of basophils and mast cells.30 31 In an autopsy case of a patient who died from MMD, electron microscopy detected basophils and neutrophils, but not lipid-laden macrophages, in the endothelial layer at the stenotic lesions of the internal carotid arteries.32 33 Recent transcriptome analysis of the intracranial arteries of MMD patients suggested an upregulation of eosinophils and natural killer (NK) cells.12 These lines of evidence suggest that GATA2 and its associated gene networks play a role in the pathophysiology of MMD.

Zhao and colleagues conducted a co-expression analysis of lncRNA and mRNA in Chinese patients with MMD without the RNF213 mutation, reporting increased expression of ANXA3 among lncRNAs and IL-13 among mRNAs.34 Although they did not examine GATA2 expression, nor is it mentioned in the main text, the abstract suggests that GATA2 might be involved in the disease pathology. We speculate that this inference is likely due to GATA2 being a transcription factor for IL-13 and the fact that both ANXA3 and the lncRNA GATA2-AS1 are involved in monocyte differentiation,35 36 presumably resulting in identification of GATA2 as common partner in their bioinformatic analysis. Since IL-13 is linked to Th2 cells and ANXA3 is associated with Th17 cell infiltration,37 their findings support our hypothesis, suggesting that elevated GATA2 expression may be a shared factor across ethnicities. The originality of our study lies in identifying related molecules within the lipid-leucocyte module as a network unit and in demonstrating that GATA2 expression correlates with clinical severity. No biomarkers have been reported to be associated with the clinical severity of MMD except homozygous p.R4810K mutation, which is a strength of this study. It is worth exploring the relationship between clinical severity and indicators related to GATA2, such as eosinophil count, in future research.

It has previously been reported that a high abundance of R. gnavus is associated with MMD.17 Additionally, patients with MMD have a lower frequency of human herpesvirus 6 (HHV6) infection.38 Interestingly, R. gnavus has been shown to stimulate T cells to secrete IL-5 and IL-13,25 whereas HHV6 suppresses the levels of these cytokines.39 Given GATA2’s role in type 2 inflammation, including the development of eosinophils, basophils and mast cells, we were interested in analysing the relationship between GATA2 expression and the relative abundance of R. gnavus in the gut, but only a weak correlation was found among patients with MMD and ICAD, suggesting that R. gnavus and GATA2 are not in a direct one-to-one relationship but could be involved in disease progression through multiple distinct pathways.

Type 2 inflammation is particularly intriguing when considering the age of onset and gender differences in MMD. Of note, the age-of-onset patterns for MMD resemble those of asthma. Both exhibit a biphasic pattern, with the gender ratio shifting towards females in adulthood compared with childhood.40 41 It is presumed that androgen receptor signalling suppresses the expression of GATA2, leading to suppression of its target IL1RL1 (ST2, an IL-13 receptor) on ST2+ Treg, which may decrease the prevalence of the disease.42 In fact, in our study population, there was a trend of higher GATA2 expression in boys during childhood and higher expression in women during adulthood, although this observation could be subject to selection bias.

Our study indicates that reduced mitochondrial ribosomal proteins may be associated with MMD. RPS3A positively regulates the mitochondrial function of human periaortic adipose tissue and is linked to coronary artery diseases.43 Cells expressing GATA2 show reduced mitochondrial cristae density when observed under an electron microscope.44 These findings suggest that the lipid-leucocyte module and mitochondrial dysfunction could be related, as indicated by our Bayesian network analysis. Of note, mitochondrial destruction has been noted in circulating endothelial colony-forming cells from MMD patients,45 and loss of mitochondrial matrix factors induces RNF213 expression.46

GZMA and TRGC1 are expressed in cytotoxic T cells, effector memory T cells and NK cells.47 GZMA is crucial for immune tolerance, and its deficiency leads to autoimmune conditions. TRGC1 encodes the T cell receptor gamma, and reduced copy number has been associated with type 2 inflammation, including asthma.48 UBTD1 regulates cellular senescence through a UBTD1-Mdm2/p53 positive feedback loop,49 and is also known as a gene related to asthma exacerbation. Thus, not only the lipid-leucocyte module but also the effector T cell module and the UBTD1 module are associated with type 2 inflammation.

We also found that IL-1β and SOCS3 are connected in the network and that both are elevated. IL-1β has previously been reported to be elevated in the blood of patients with MMD. SOCS3 is known to promote the differentiation into GATA3+Th2 cells,50 aligning with the result. On the other hand, SOCS3 can suppress STAT3, leading to a reduction in Th17 differentiation. Because IL-1β can either activate or inhibit SOCS3 depending on the context, the balance between Th2 and Th17 cells may vary with the situation. In patients with MMD, both type two cytokines (IL-4 and IL-5) and type 17 cytokines (IL-17 and IL-22) have been reported to be elevated.51 This implies that further research is needed to understand not only type 2 inflammation but also the regulation of the immunological microenvironment, including Th17 cells, in RNF213-related vasculopathy.

The p.R4810K mutation is the most common mutation observed in East Asia. In familial cases, it shows an OR of 1000 in Japan, Korea, and China, while in sporadic cases, the OR is 134 in Japan, 100 in Korea, and 31 in China.6 The same meta-analysis reported that p.E4950D and p.A5021V mutations have ORs ranging from 5 to 9 in East Asia.6 The p.R4810K mutation is not observed in Western populations, where extremely rare variants, such as C3997Y and D4013N, are reported in about 8% of cases.4 6 7 Interestingly, the founder mutation p.R4810K has a lower penetrance than other rare variants. This lower penetrance, paradoxically, has led to research aiming to identify factors that increase penetrance, contributing to our understanding of the disease pathology. Recently, a variant in HAPLN3 has been reported to be associated with increased penetrance of p.R4810K,52 similar to how an MRVI1 variant increases the penetrance of MMD in individuals with an NF1 mutation.53 A reduction in HAPLN3 disrupts its role as a linker between hyaluronan and proteoglycans, regulating interactions between endothelial and immune cells.

The functional relationship between RNF213 and GATA2 remains unclear. However, considering that RNF213 is involved in Treg differentiation through FOXO1,16 it is conceivable that RNF213 mutation may impair Treg function, which is further shifted towards Th2 inflammation via GATA2, such as through increased IL1RL1 expression on Treg (ST2+ Treg),42 thereby prolonging chronic vascular inflammation. Importantly, the RNF213 p.R4810K mutation is associated not only with intracranial vascular disease but also with coronary artery disease or pulmonary arterial hypertension,54,57 and vascular disorders associated with various types of autoimmune diseases or Down syndrome exhibit a moyamoya-like phenotype. Therefore, elucidating the relationship between RNF213 and GATA2 could provide insights into the pathophysiology of various vascular disorders, highlighting the need for functional studies to further investigate this mechanism. By using a patient-derived iPS cell line in which GATA2 can be inducibly expressed, it may be possible to elucidate the relationship between RNF213 and GATA2 by evaluating its effects on vascular organoid formation and the differentiation and function of haematopoietic cells. Additionally, in animal models, forced expression of GATA2 or the induction of type 2 inflammation may enable the creation of relevant models, providing further insights into its functional mechanisms.

There are several limitations to this study. First, the study population is relatively small, indicating a need for further replication studies with larger sample sizes and multivariable analyses. Second, we analysed peripheral blood mononuclear cells but not the blood vessel walls where the stenosis occurs. Although it is challenging to obtain specimens from the vessel walls at the terminal portion of the internal carotid artery except in autopsy cases, vessel walls from peripheral middle cerebral arteries could be analysed in future studies. Nonetheless, it is significant that our study demonstrated that peripheral blood can serve as a biomarker and that systemic circulation may be involved in the pathogenesis of MMD and the broader moyamoya spectrum, including RNF213-related vasculopathy. Third, this study did not directly establish a causal relationship between peripheral gene expression and MMD. Functional studies are needed to assess whether overexpression of GATA2 induces MMD phenotypes. Fourth, we did not validate our findings at the protein level using methods such as immunocytochemistry or western blotting. A previous study reported a correlation between GATA2 immunoreactivity in peripheral blood and eosinophil count,58 highlighting the potential relevance of this topic in the context of MMD. Further studies are required to explore this aspect. Finally, we did not test whether high GATA2 expression is associated with an increased incidence of MMD among individuals with wild-type RNF213. Given that many family members with wild-type RNF213 who exhibited high GATA2 expression did not develop MMD, the impact of GATA2 expression may be less evident in patients with wild-type RNF213. Further research is needed to explore these aspects.

Conclusions

Our data suggest that higher GATA2 expression is associated not only with increased penetrance of the p.R4810K mutation but also with a more severe form of MMD, characterised by symptomatic onset, an earlier age at onset and a higher likelihood of bilateral involvement. The functionality of the gene network points to type 2 inflammation and mitochondrial dysfunction potentially playing a role in the pathogenesis of MMD spectrum and RNF213-related vasculopathy.
