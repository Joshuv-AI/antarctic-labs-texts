Juvenile Parkinson's disease: Symptom burden and treatment challenges
Authors: Lorenzo Malfer, Capucine Piat, Aidan F. Mullan, Ryan J. Uitti, Leonardo G. Fugoso, Elizabeth A. Coon, James H. Bower, Keith A. Coffman, Rodolfo Savica
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Juvenile Parkinson's disease (JPD) is a rare condition characterized by the onset of parkinsonian motor symptoms before the age of 21. Sixteen patients with JPD who presented to the Mayo Clinic between 1990-2020 were included. The median age of symptoms onset was 18.5 years, and six cases had pathogenic PRKN mutations. Resting tremor was the most frequent initial symptom (81.3%), and non-motor symptoms were notably common, including mild cognitive impairment (56.3%), anxiety (62.5%), and depression (56.3%). Levodopa responsiveness was suboptimal in 9 patients and 14 exhibited motor fluctuations. JPD demonstrates distinct clinical characteristics compared with early-onset and late-onset PD.

Early-onset Parkinson's disease (EOPD) is defined as motor symptom onset before age 50, whereas juvenile Parkinson's disease (JPD) is defined by onset before age 21 and both conditions are considerably less frequent compared to late-onset Parkinson's disease (LOPD).1,2 JPD diagnosis is challenging, as this condition is clinically heterogeneous and carries a broad differential that includes autoimmune, infectious, and metabolic disorders.1,2 Despite these complexities, JPD remains poorly characterized in the literature and case series are scarce. We report 16 patients with JPD evaluated at a single tertiary center over three decades. Patients with a clinical diagnosis of PD who presented to the Mayo Clinic between 1990–2020 were identified, and all cases with motor symptom onset before age 21 were included in the JPD cohort. Demographic, clinical, imaging and therapeutic data were collected retrospectively.

A total of 16 patients were identified, 6 males (37.5%) and 10 females (62.5%), with a median age at onset of 18.5 years. A family history of PD or other movement disorders was present in 10 patients (62.5%). Genetic testing was performed in 11 cases (68.8%): four carried homozygous or compound heterozygous pathogenic PRKN variants, two carried heterozygous pathogenic PRKN variants, one a heterozygous pathogenic POLG variant, and one a heterozygous pathogenic SPG7 variant together with trisomy 21 (Down syndrome). The initial motor symptom was resting tremor in 13 patients (81.3%), and atypical subtle pyramidal signs were present in 5 (31.3%). The most common non-motor symptoms were orthostatic hypotension (50.0%), followed by bladder urgency (43.8%), constipation (37.5%), insomnia (37.5%), sialorrhea (25.0%), and REM sleep behavior disorder (18.8%). Mild cognitive impairment (MCI) was diagnosed in 9 cases (56.3%) and dementia in one. Psychiatric and behavioral features were common, with depression in 56.3% and anxiety in 62.5% of the subjects. Additionally, 6 patients (37.5%) demonstrated symptoms of impulse control disorder (ICD), which were clearly related to dopamine agonists (DAs) use in 3 (Table 1).

DaT-Scan was performed in 6 patients (37.5%), showing presynaptic dopaminergic deficit consistent with a PD diagnosis in 4 of them. Brain PET/CT was performed in 4 patients: 3 of them demonstrated alterations, particularly showing frontoparietal and posterior cortical hypometabolism. All patients were treated with levodopa with a good-to-moderate response in 7 cases (43.8%), moderate-to-poor in 6 (37.5%), and initially good with a rapidly reducing benefit over the following months in 3 (18.8%). Levodopa-induced motor fluctuations were observed in 87.5% of patients: 11 with dyskinesias and 10 with wearing-off phenomena. Dystonia was observed in 10 cases and 5 PRKN-mutated patients, though only 2 of them were clearly related to levodopa administration. DBS was performed on 4 patients (25.0%): three with bilateral subthalamic placement, and one with internal globus pallidus and ventralis intermedius nucleus placement. All reported a good response.

Genetic testing in this JPD cohort identified pathogenic variants in the majority of cases tested, whereas previous studies reported an incidence of PD-related mutations of approximately 11.6% in EOPD patients and 37.7% in PD patients with onset before the age of 30 years. 7 These findings support the hypothesis that JPD is mainly driven by genetic factors, consistent with previous studies that reported PRKN variants in up to 77% of patients.1,2,8 Clinically, JPD patients exhibited a higher frequency of subtle pyramidal signs, which are rarely observed in EOPD/LOPD. 3 Furthermore, non-levodopa-induced dystonia was notably prevalent both in the whole cohort and in patients with PRKN mutations. 9 Non-motor symptoms such as cognitive impairment and autonomic symptoms, including orthostatic hypotension and bladder urgency, were also more prevalent in JPD than comparable EOPD cohorts described in the literature.5,6 Cognitive dysfunction and non-motor features have been associated with early-stage and widespread involvement of cortical brain regions. 10 The presence of a potential cortical involvement in our cohort is partially supported by the presence of frontoparietal and posterior hypometabolism in most cases who underwent brain PET. Behavioral symptoms, including depression and anxiety, were also moderately increased in JPD patients, as marked limbic network dysfunction and striatal atrophy have been consistently associated with depression in early and juvenile parkinsonism cases. 11 DaT-Scan results were consistent with a PD diagnosis in most cases and the negative results are likely due to a lack of nigrostriatal pathway involvement in the early stages of disease, considering the imaging was performed a short time after symptoms onset. 12 The initial response to levodopa was reported to be good-to-moderate in most cases. However, the responsiveness declined rapidly in a subset of patients with frequent early development of motor fluctuations.1,4 The early-stage wearing-off phenomena suggest a potential faster degeneration of presynaptic dopaminergic neurons, resulting in an impaired levodopa storage. 13 The exceptionally long disease duration in these patients may also contribute to the suboptimal long-term treatment response and to the motor fluctuations, making medication management considerably more complex. 13 Additionally, these findings are consistent with previous studies that reported levodopa-induced dyskinesia in 30% of PD patients of any age, with an increased frequency especially in younger-onset subjects. 14 To better address these motor complications, some patients underwent DBS implantation, which demonstrated superiority in several domains compared with medical therapy. Other possible treatment options may be considered for these patients, including foscarbidopa/foslevodopa subcutaneous infusion, which has been shown to significantly improve motor complications. 15

Overall, these findings highlight the distinct genetic, clinical, and therapeutic characteristics of JPD, distinguishing it from both EOPD and LOPD. The presence of significant motor fluctuations and the necessity for advanced options such as DBS underscore the importance of personalized therapeutic approaches in these patients. This study was conducted over a 30-year enrollment period (1990–2020), with a long observation period, and follow-up extended until January 2025, resulting in a stable and well-characterized study cohort. However, the sample size of the cohort was relatively small, largely due to the extreme rarity of JPD. The limited existing literature on JPD poses significant challenges and often leads to diagnostic delays. Further research is needed to better understand the pathophysiology of this condition and define its diagnostic criteria.
