Collective Turns in Spinless Flocks
Joao Lizárraga, Marcus de Aguiar

Abstract
Using a minimal aggregation-based model, we address the efficient information transfer observed in natural flocks during collective turns. Specifically, we demonstrate that this feature can arise solely from the non-reciprocal nature of local interactions. Through a perturbative analysis, moreover, we find that velocity fluctuations (in the continuum) can be described by a Born approximation. We then show that a wave propagating across the flock undergoes scattering. Our model provides testable predictions and can be extended to study other physical contexts exhibiting polar order.



Birds gather and fly together, forming a flock. The early observation of this phenomenon led to the study of such collectives using frameworks based on a velocity alignment mechanism. Indeed, for a long time, the Vicsek model
23
;
9
;
18
was considered a pillar in the study of related problems
3
;
15
;
10
;
13
. This status, however, faced challenges as subsequent field observations were carried out. Particularly, studies on starling flocks revealed hallmarks of their collective behavior that were not contemplated by Vicsek’s formulation: strong alignment and cohesion are maintained during both straight flight and turns
2
;
5
. Moreover, despite the absence of leaders to reach collective coherence, the flocks’ turning events were found to be triggered by single individuals
1
.

After recognizing the limitations of the Vicsek model in reproducing the phenomena exhibited by starlings, Cavagna
et al.
introduced the Inertial Spin Model (ISM)
8
;
7
. At its core, the ISM stems from the incompleteness of the Vicsek model. It does rely on the velocity-alignment framework; yet, rather than directly promoting the alignment of individual velocities, it does so through internal rotation generators: the
spins
. As argued by Cavagna
et al.
, the Vicsek model characterizes an overdamped system: if waves emerge, the system’s nature causes them to dissipate rather than propagate. In contrast, the ISM allows the system to operate in one of two regimes: overdamped or underdamped. Therefore, since real starling flocks allow for the propagation of waves during collective turns, they were hypothesized to match the ISM underdamped scenario.

Although the ISM effectively reproduces the turning phenomenon of starling flocks, recent experiments have given rise to an apparent
paradox

6
. Under the ISM formulation, if the flock were underdamped, two peaks, representing the spin-waves, should emerge in the frequency domain. However, in real starling flocks, these peaks are not observed. Thus, to reconcile the experimental evidence with the theoretical framework, Cavagna
et al.
proposed that starlings may operate adaptively depending on the amplitude of fluctuations. That is, for small fluctuations, the system is overdamped, and for larger ones (
turning signals
), the system becomes underdamped.

In this letter, we address the propagation phenomenon experienced by turning flocks without explicitly relying on the
standard
alignment-based framework. Instead, we extend a model of aggregation introduced in
14
. In its simplest form, this model describes a collective of individuals who align their velocities as a by-product of achieving cohesion. Each individual’s self-propelling velocity is constant.

Here, in contrast to the form presented in
14
, we consider that individuals are affected by their intention to match neighboring velocities. That is, self-propulsion is
adaptive
. Thus, a system of
N
individuals is governed by

\displaystyle\begin{split}\dot{\vec{r}}_{i}&=\vec{f}_{i}(t)+\alpha\left(\langle\vec{r}\rangle-\vec{r}_{i}\right),\\
\dot{\vec{f}}_{i}&=\gamma\sum_{j=1}^{N}A_{ij}(\dot{\vec{r}}_{j}-\dot{\vec{r}}_{i}),\end{split}

(1)

where
\vec{r}_{i}
and
\vec{f}_{i}(t)
respectively represent the
i
-th individual’s position and self-propelling velocity, and
\alpha
and
\gamma
are constant coupling strengths. Topological velocity interactions are defined by the adjacency matrix
A
: the self-propulsion of the
i
-th individual is affected by those of its
\eta
closest neighbors in space. Note that this restriction does not imply reciprocity; that is,
A
may not be symmetric. Indeed, hereafter we consider the general case where
A_{ij}\neq A_{ji}
.

Fig.

illustrates the behavior of the system described by Eqs. (
1
) during both straight and turning motion. Regardless of its trajectory, the non-reciprocity of interactions is present throughout the entire event [Fig.

(b)]. During the straight flight, when the flock’s velocity is roughly constant (fluctuations vanish), the interaction asymmetry index,
\psi_{A}
, reaches a plateau. Nevertheless, during the collective turn, small variations in
\psi_{A}
reveal that the network of interactions is also time-varying:
A=A(t)
. In this state, the acceleration differences among individuals are small [Fig.
1
(c)], thus evidencing that all individuals have effectively received the
turning signal
(see the Supplemental Material (SM) for details on the turning hierarchy).

Our main interest lies in how information is transferred among individuals. Specifically, we focus on the propagation of velocity fluctuations,
\vec{\varphi}_{i}=\dot{\vec{r}}_{i}-\langle\dot{\vec{r}}\rangle
, across the flock (which acts as a medium). To this end, we start by rearranging Eqs. (
1
) into the fluctuation dynamics:

\dot{\vec{\varphi}}_{i}+\alpha\vec{\varphi}_{i}+\gamma\sum_{j=1}^{N}L_{ij}\vec{\varphi}_{j}-\vec{\xi}_{i}=0,

(2)

where
L
is the in-degree Laplacian associated with
A
, and
\vec{\xi}_{i}
represents a stimulus that remains constant for all the nodes. Since this global term arises from the non-reciprocity of interactions, it can be written in terms of the nodes’ in- and out-degrees as
\vec{\xi}_{i}=\gamma\sum_{j}\vec{\varphi}_{j}(d_{j}^{out}-d_{j}^{in})
.

To explore the system’s spatiotemporal behavior, we extend Eq. (
2
) to the continuum limit:

\dot{\vec{\mu}}+\alpha\vec{\mu}+\gamma\mathcal{L}\vec{\mu}-\vec{\xi}=0,

(3)

where
\vec{\mu}(\vec{r},t)
is the field of velocity fluctuations and
\mathcal{L}
is the Laplace operator. In the frequency domain, where our analysis lies, handling a time-dependent operator such as
\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}(t)
may be challenging, complicating the drawing of conclusions. To bypass this issue without compromising much of the system’s nature, we decompose the Laplacian as

\mathcal{L}\approx\mathcal{L}_{0}+\sigma\mathcal{L}_{1}(t).

Here,
\mathcal{L}_{0}
and
\sigma
are constant, and a small
\sigma
allows us to treat the time-varying component of the interaction operator as a perturbation. The variations exhibited by
\psi_{A}
[Fig.
1
(b)] provide a qualitative indication on the small time-varying effects of the interactions. However, the validity of the approximation is better justified by the relative magnitudes between the constant and time-varying components (see SM).

In the zeroth order (
\vec{\mu}_{0}
), the constant Laplacian is defined as
\gamma\mathcal{L}_{0}=\vec{C}_{1}\nabla+\nabla\cdot(\mathbf{C}_{2}\nabla)
, where the coefficients
\vec{C}_{1}
and
\mathbf{C}_{2}
are constant, and
\vec{C}_{1}\nabla
and
\nabla\cdot(\mathbf{C}_{2}\nabla)
respectively represent advection and diffusion terms (see SM for details). Then, by plugging
\mathcal{L}_{0}
into Eq. (
3
), and performing the Fourier transform, we obtain
\hat{G}_{0}^{-1}\hat{\mu}_{0}=0
, with
\hat{.}
denoting that the variable is in the frequency domain, and

\hat{G}_{0}^{-1}=\alpha-\vec{k}\cdot\mathbf{C}_{2}\vec{k}+i(\vec{C}_{1}\cdot\vec{k}-\omega).

(4)

Notice that transforming
\vec{\xi}
leads to a term proportional to
\delta(\vec{k})
, which vanishes as we restrict our study to cases where
\vec{k}\neq\vec{0}
. Lastly, by solving for the temporal frequency, we determine that the system obeys the dispersion relation

\omega(\vec{k})=\vec{C}_{1}\cdot\vec{k}+i\left(\vec{k}\cdot\mathbf{C}_{2}\vec{k}-\alpha\right),

(5)

which holds as long as
\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}_{0}
. Remarkably, a non-zero real part of
\omega(\vec{k})
indicates a wave-propagating behavior. However, as the single root of
\omega(\vec{k})
introduces a dependence on the direction of
\vec{k}
, the propagation is chiral.

The time-varying component,
\mathcal{L}_{1}(t)
, allows us to find that the first-order correction,
\vec{\mu}_{1}
, is characterized by (see SM for details)

\hat{G}_{0}^{-1}\hat{\mu}_{1}+\gamma\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{1}*\hat{\mu}_{0}=0.

Unlike the unperturbed case, which allowed us to find the dispersion relation [Eq. (
5
)] by removing
\hat{\mu}_{0}
, we are now constrained by the convolution term
\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{1}*\hat{\mu}_{0}
. Despite this drawback, the effects generated by the time-varying component are still noticeable in
\vec{\mu}\approx\vec{\mu}_{0}+\sigma\vec{\mu}_{1}
. Indeed, this approximation reveals that

\hat{\mu}\approx\hat{\mu}_{0}-\hat{G}_{0}(\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{1}*\hat{\mu}_{0}),

(6)

which corresponds to a Born approximation with propagator
\hat{G}_{0}
. That is, if (
\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{1}*\hat{\mu}_{0}
) is non-zero, the system undergoes scattering. For notational simplicity, the local coupling strength (
\gamma
) was absorbed by the propagator as it does not hinder its interpretation.

To illustrate the phenomena described above, we use data from simulations of the system with a finite number of individuals [Eqs. (
1
)]. Our analysis, then, focuses on the spatiotemporal correlations among velocity fluctuations. The correlation function we use is defined as

C(\vec{k},t)=\frac{1}{N}\left\langle\sum_{i,j}^{N}\vec{\varphi}_{i}(t_{0})\cdot\vec{\varphi}_{j}(t_{0}+t)e^{-i\vec{k}\cdot\vec{r}_{ij}}\right\rangle_{t_{0}},

(7)

where
\vec{r}_{ij}=\vec{r}_{i}(t_{0})-\vec{r}_{j}(t_{0}+t)
, with positions measured relative to the flock’s instantaneous center of mass. Notice that analyzing the correlations during the flock’s straight flight is meaningless, as velocity fluctuations are, by definition, zero (the effects of noise in this state are discussed in SM). In light of this, we focus on the velocity fluctuations that emerge during collective turns.

Since the system is anisotropic, the velocity correlations can be evaluated in any spatial direction; this is, indeed, captured by the dependence of
C(\vec{k},t)
on the wave vector. Therefore, the significant spatial frequencies are bounded by
2\pi/L_{q}
and
\pi/\ell_{q}
, where
L_{q}
and
\ell_{q}
are respectively the maximum and mean interparticle distances along the
q=(x,y,z)
axis. Finally, for a full view of the system’s frequency behavior, we focus on
|\hat{C}(\vec{k},\omega)|
.

Figs.
2
(a),
2
(b), and
2
(c) display the spectra obtained by analyzing the turning event described in Fig.
1
. Notably, the system’s anisotropy is evidenced by probing the velocity correlations with wave vectors of different orientations. Each panel, associated with individual spatial axes, shows that not a single curve is symmetric. The spectra exhibiting greater distortions [Figs.

(a) and

(b)] are consistent with the flock’s behavior: the collective turn is predominantly performed in the
x-y
plane. In contrast, the smoother curves [Fig.

(c)] characterize the lack of collective
effort
along the
z
-axis.

It is worth recalling that the dispersion relation [Eq. (
5
)] was obtained for a constant interaction network. Therefore, as the spectra displayed in Fig.
2
were calculated for a time-varying case, one should not necessarily expect the emergence of a single peak at
\mathrm{Re}[\omega(\vec{k})]
. Remarkably, the spectra showing major consistency with this prediction are the ones where the time-varying effects impact the system the least [Fig.
2
(c)].

Before delving deeper into the effects of a time-varying interaction network, we find it important to show the effects of performing an
isotropic averaging
. In a recent work on real flocks
6
, the spatiotemporal correlation function is reformulated by neglecting the wave vector’s orientation (
k=|\vec{k}|
). Thus, Eq. (
7
) becomes

C(k,t)=\frac{1}{N}\left\langle\sum_{i,j}^{N}\vec{\varphi}_{i}(t_{0})\cdot\vec{\varphi}_{j}(t_{0}+t)\text{sinc}(kr_{ij})\right\rangle_{t_{0}},

where
r_{ij}=|\vec{r}_{ij}|
and sinc(.) is the cardinal sine. By evaluating data averaged over different orientations of
\vec{k}
, the expectation is to mitigate undesired noise. In our system, however, performing the
isotropic averaging
masks the effects associated with the nature of interactions [Fig.
2
(d)]. Notice that, even though the interaction network changes over time, the corresponding spectra lead us to believe that the dispersion relation [Eq. (
5
)] is fulfilled (for
\vec{k}\rightarrow 0
). On top of that, the spectra follow a Lorentzian-like shape centered at
\omega=0
, which closely resembles the spectral profile of an overdamped system. That is, the
isotropically averaged
spectra suggest that the system is unable to sustain the propagation of waves. Remarkably, given the non-reciprocity in interactions, the correlation functions should be complex; in turn, spectral symmetry should not be achieved.

Fig.
2
highlights the importance of treating the system as anisotropic. However, the simple superimposition of spectra corresponding to different wave vectors tells very little about the system’s actual propagation features. To better understand the system’s nature, we exploit the lack of motion constraints in our simulations. Since we do not rely on periodic boundary conditions, the components of
\vec{k}
can vary continuously within their respective boundaries. That is, we can evaluate the system’s frequency response to waves exhibiting a wide range of spatiotemporal characteristics. Specifically, to obtain a description of the system’s
preferred modes
, we focus on the velocity correlations at specific (
\vec{k},\omega
) pairs. On this basis, Fig.
3
displays the dispersion maps obtained for different turning events (for details on calculating the power spectral density, see SM).

At a glance, all panels of Fig.
3
share a prominent triangular structure. This preliminary observation provides a clear insight into the system’s behavior. As the wave number approaches zero, the system’s preferred temporal frequency is also found near zero. That is, at large spatial scales, wave propagation is extremely slow, or entirely absent. In contrast, as the wave number increases, the preferred temporal frequencies spread. Since brightness reflects the energy concentration, the brightest lines (framing the triangular structures) represent the dominant frequency components of the velocity fluctuations. The additional bright blurred structures, centered around
\omega=0
and spanning the entire spatial domain, represent waves that, rather than propagating, dissipate or diffuse locally.

The behavior described above may seem contradictory as the system is found to operate at both propagating and non-propagating regimes simultaneously. However, this is simply a hallmark of the system’s complexity. More thoroughly, a closer look at Fig.
3
reveals the presence of clouds and ripples filling up spaces between the diffusive and propagating modes. These patterns are evidence of the scattering phenomenon predicted by Eq. (
6
). A single individual initiates the turning event, yet the message is not transmitted cleanly. Along its way, the wave carrying the turning signal faces obstacles inherent to the medium: non-reciprocities and evolving interactions. As a result, its energy spreads into intermediate modes.

The scattering experienced by the system does not undermine the propagation of waves. Indeed, the linear relationship between the preferred temporal and spatial modes is preserved. This, characterized by the brightest lines in Fig.
3
, suggests that the system’s dispersion relation, during the collective turn, is predominantly governed by
\omega_{t}(\vec{k})\approx c_{s}k
, where the velocity of propagation,
c_{s}
, is constant. Evidencing this argument, turning events with a longer duration [Figs.
3
(a) and
3
(b)] show a slower propagation than turning events with a shorter duration [Figs.
3
(c) and
3
(d)].

The model we presented in this Letter reproduces wave-propagating features exhibited by natural flocks during collective turns. The system’s dynamics are based on a simpler aggregation model
14
, modified to allow the dynamic adjustment of individual self-propelling velocities. This
self-regulation
occurs locally among individuals interacting in a topological fashion; specifically, they
adapt
to maintain local coherence.

Although the number of interacting neighbors is fixed, non-reciprocity arises naturally: the set of neighbors influencing an individual differs from the set it influences. The characteristic network of interactions, then, asymmetric and time-varying, plays a major role in the emergence of the flock’s propagating regime. Remarkably, even in the scenario where interactions are time-invariant, their non-reciprocal nature sets the system up for allowing the chiral propagation of waves.

A perturbative treatment of the Laplacian’s time-dependent component allows for describing the field of velocity fluctuations using a Born approximation [Eq. (
6
)]. This similarity suggests that the propagating wave experiences a scattering phenomenon, a prediction that was also confirmed through numerical simulations (Fig.
3
). The argument we used to perform a perturbation analysis was based on the small relative magnitude between the constant and time-varying components of the Laplacian (see SM). It is worth remarking that previous work on real flocks
6
;
16
has relied in a similar assumption: quasi-static interactions.

In our system, the non-reciprocal interactions are highlighted as a source of chirality for propagating waves. However, performing an
isotropic averaging
results in the apparent absence of anisotropic effects. As a consequence, employing this technique may obscure the interpretation of the system’s spectral features. Although the numerical results presented here correspond solely to turning events, the same remarks on anisotropy can be made by analyzing the system during straight flights (see SM). This scenario is important as it enables us to make a connection with results presented in
6
. In such work, based on information obtained from real starling flocks, the authors show that these collectives exhibit spectral signatures of an overdamped behavior. Notably, as suggested by our results [Fig.
2
], a characteristic Lorentzian-like peak may also arise from performing an
isotropic averaging
in non-reciprocal systems, as it collapses the propagation directional features.

The
damping paradox
, also described in
6
, arises based on discrepancies between the behavior of real flocks and the ISM nature. Accordingly, the authors showed that adding a quartic term to the ISM alignment dynamics may solve the inconsistencies. While this modification is theoretically sound, it increases the model’s complexity (and so its functional interpretation). Our system, in a complementary manner, shows that a fundamental behavioral feature is sufficient to untangle the apparent contradictions. Remarkably, supporting our argument, non-reciprocity has been shown to play an important role in the behavior of other active matter systems
24
;
12
;
4
;
11
;
22
.

Finally, although further research is needed, we believe our model can be tailored to characterize other highly polarized collectives, such as fish or insects
17
;
19
;
20
;
21
, in a physically consistent manner.

Appendix S1
The continuum limit

The global stimulus, denoted in Eq. (2) of the main text by
\vec{\xi}_{i}
, becomes:

\xi({\vec{r},t})=\gamma\int_{V}\vec{\mu}(r^{\prime},t)D(r^{\prime})d^{3}r^{\prime},

where the field
D(r^{\prime})
represents the local asymmetry of interactions, and
V
is the flock’s volume. Since the integral does not depend on
\vec{r}
but only on
t
, the spatially uniform field can be written as:
\xi(\vec{r},t)=\xi(t)
. Applying the spatial Fourier transform leads to:

\hat{\xi}(\vec{k},t)=\xi(t)\int e^{-i\vec{k}\cdot\vec{r}}d^{3}r,

which is the same as
\hat{\xi}(\vec{k},t)\propto\xi(t)\delta(\vec{k})
. Therefore, if we study cases where
\vec{k}\neq 0
, as we did in the main text,
\hat{\xi}(\vec{k},t)
becomes zero.

The Laplacian, introduced in Eq. (2) of the main text, is another term that relates to individual interactions. However, in contrast to the global stimulus, finding a specific representation for our system’s discrete Laplacian, in the continuum limit, is challenging. Although the interaction network is not fully regular, we can make a rough approximation by assuming that neighbors are separated by the mean interparticle distance
\ell
.

In the symmetric case, the discrete Laplacian becomes a single diffusion term, whereas in the asymmetric case, an additional advection term is present. For the form presented in the main text, each element of
\vec{C}_{1}
is expected to be proportional to
\ell
. Under the same assumption, we can expect each term of
\mathbf{C}_{2}
to be proportional to
\ell^{2}
. This consideration does not undermine our analysis as our results do not rely on estimating
\vec{C}_{1}
or
\mathbf{C}_{2}
.

Appendix S2
The magnitude of “small”

The perturbative analysis begins with the assumption that the evolution of the interaction network is characterized by only small variations. Accordingly, we express the Laplacian as the sum of a constant component and a small time-varying perturbation. Before delving into the analysis details, it is worth justifying the validity of this approximation.

In Fig 1(b) of the main text, we showed that, during a collective turn, the asymmetry index fluctuates around a fixed value. This observation is complemented by Figs.
S1
(a) and
S1
(b), where the number of reconnections experienced by the interaction network is displayed. The evaluated events correspond to those presented in Fig. 3 of the main text for different turning durations. Notice that, for
N=100
individuals, with each one allowed to make
\eta=6
connections, the number of entries in the Laplacian matrix would be
600
. However, the number of reconnections over time is much smaller.

Figure S1:
Time-varying Laplacian as a small perturbation. The evaluated turning events lasted
1000
(left panels) and
100
(right panels) time steps. The number of entry changes (top row) is computed as the Frobenius norm of the difference between consecutive Laplacian matrices. The relative magnitude of the perturbation (bottom row) is given as
\epsilon_{F}=\|L_{t}-L_{0}\|_{F}/\|L_{0}\|_{F}
, where
L_{0}
is the temporal average of Laplacian matrices during the collective turn:
\langle L_{t}\rangle
.

The small variations in the asymmetry index and the low number of reconnections only partially characterize the “small” perturbation. For a more quantitative description, we compute the relative magnitude between the time-varying and constant components of the Laplacian. From Figs.
S1
(c) and
S1
(d) combined, we spot a maximum relative magnitude of
\epsilon_{F}\approx 0.14
positioned at the very end of the collective turn. Although this value cannot be neglected, it is small enough for the first-order approximation to capture a major part of the phenomenon experienced by the system (
\epsilon_{F}^{2}\approx 0.019
). Properly, if rather than analyzing a single peak, we average the relative magnitudes obtained along the whole turning event, we obtain:

\langle\epsilon_{F}\rangle_{t}\approx 0.1.

On this basis, we consider the Born approximation to be qualitatively robust.

Appendix S3
Perturbation Analysis

Although we discussed our treatment of the time-varying interaction network in the main text, this section provides details omitted for brevity.

In the continuum, the Laplacian operator (
\mathcal{L}
) is defined as the sum of a constant component (
\mathcal{L}_{0}
), and a time-varying one (
\sigma\mathcal{L}_{1}
). In turn, the system’s solution is approximated as
\vec{\mu}\approx\vec{\mu}_{0}+\sigma\vec{\mu}_{1}
. In the zeroth-order, the system is found to be governed by

\dot{\vec{\mu}}_{0}+\alpha\vec{\mu}_{0}+\mathcal{L}_{0}\vec{\mu}_{0}+\vec{\xi}=0,

(S1)

which depends only on the Laplacian’s constant component. Then, by transforming this equation to the frequency domain, we obtain

\left[\alpha-\vec{k}\cdot\mathbf{C}_{2}\vec{k}+i(\vec{C}_{1}\cdot\vec{k}-\omega)\right]\vec{\mu}_{0}=0,

from which we define the propagator
\hat{G}_{0}
[Eq. (5) of the main text], and calculate the dispersion relation
\omega(\vec{k})
[Eq. (6) of the main text].

Beyond the trivial case, in the first-order approximation, we plug the approximate
\mathcal{L}
and
\vec{\mu}
into the continuum dynamics [Eq. (3) of the main text]. Thus, we obtain:

(\dot{\vec{\mu}}_{0}+\sigma\dot{\vec{\mu}}_{1})+\alpha(\vec{\mu}_{0}+\sigma\vec{\mu}_{1})+\gamma(\mathcal{L}_{0}+\sigma\mathcal{L}_{1})(\vec{\mu}_{0}+\sigma\vec{\mu}_{1})=\vec{\xi},

(S2)

which, by grouping in powers of
\sigma
, leads to:

\dot{\vec{\mu}}_{1}+\alpha\vec{\mu}_{1}+\gamma\mathcal{L}_{0}\vec{\mu}_{1}+\gamma\mathcal{L}_{1}\vec{\mu}_{0}=0.

(S3)

Note that we neglected the higher order term multiplying
\sigma^{2}
. Finally, in the frequency domain, the equation becomes

\left[\alpha-\vec{k}\cdot\mathbf{C}_{2}\vec{k}+i(\vec{C}_{1}\cdot\vec{k}-\omega)\right]\hat{\mu}_{1}+\gamma\mathcal{L}_{1}*\hat{\mu}_{0}=0,

(S4)

which can be represented as
\hat{G}_{0}\hat{\mu}_{1}+\gamma\mathcal{L}_{1}*\hat{\mu}_{0}=0
. Then, after solving for
\hat{\mu}_{1}
, we obtain the form presented in Eq. (7) of the main text.

Notice that the local coupling strength (
\gamma
) affects both
\mathcal{L}_{0}
and
\mathcal{L}_{1}
. In the main text, we define
\gamma\mathcal{L}_{0}=\vec{C}_{1}\nabla+\nabla\cdot(\mathbf{C}_{2}\nabla)
; yet, since
\mathcal{L}_{1}
is not presented in the same fashion,
\gamma
does not cancel out. That is, without loss of generality, we could define
\gamma\mathcal{L}_{1}=\vec{C}_{1}^{(1)}(t)\nabla+\nabla\cdot(\mathbf{C}_{2}^{(2)}(t)\nabla)
, and then get rid of
\gamma
in the subsequent steps. That is why, in Eq. (7) of the main text,
\gamma
is said to be absorbed by the propagator. On the other hand,
\sigma
is simply an indicator of the perturbation magnitude.

Appendix S4
Numerics

In all simulations, individuals were initially distributed uniformly in a cubic box with a side length of
100
. Initial velocities were drawn from uniform distributions on the intervals
[0,13]
(
x
-axis),
[0,10]
(
y
-axis), and
[-12,12]
(

S4.1
Turning delays

The radial accelerations,
a_{i}
, displayed in Fig. 1(c) of the main text, demonstrated the system’s underdamped behavior while turning. To complement that observation, Fig.
S5
(b) shows that the differences in maximum accelerations, reached by each individual, are almost negligible. The order in which each
p
individual turns is calculated by estimating the pairwise delay between accelerations, based on the cross-correlation of their time series. An illustration of how the wave propagation affects individuals in a hierarchical order is shown in Fig.
S5
(a).

Figure S5:
Hierarchy of individuals during a turning event. (a) Snapshot of mid-turn individual positions and orientations (blue arrows). The initiator (red) and turning individuals (yellow) are marked, while those not yet affected are unmarked. (b) Maximum radial acceleration of each
p
individual over time. (c) Distance traveled by the propagated wave over time; the gray line represents a linear fit.

Given that individuals are reached by the propagating wave at different times, the wave’s velocity can be estimated by measuring the distance traveled between hits. Fig.
S5
(c) shows that the relationship between distance traveled by the propagating wave and the elapsed time is linear. That is, the wave propagates with a constant velocity. It is important to recognize, however, that this estimate relies on the assumption of isotropic propagation, which limits its interpretation to a qualitative level.

S4.2
Noise

In the main text, we focused on turning events given that velocity fluctuations were absent during the flock’s straight flight. To address this shortcoming, we introduce noise. Then, the system is governed by:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\dot{\vec{r}}_{i}&=\vec{f}_{i}(t)+\alpha\left(\langle\vec{r}\rangle-\vec{r}_{i}\right),\\
\dot{\vec{f}}_{i}&=\vec{\zeta}_{i}(t)+\gamma\sum_{j=1}^{N}A_{ij}(\dot{\vec{r}}_{j}-\dot{\vec{r}}_{i}),\end{split}

(S5)

where
\zeta_{i}(t)
is a random variable, white in nature, with an intensity of
D_{\zeta}
.

The correlation function for the flock governed by Eq. (
S5
), during a straight flight, is shown in Fig.
S6
. As argued in the main text, performing an
isotropic averaging
results in a Lorentzian-shaped spectrum. When the wave number,
k
, increases, the spectrum widens, as is expected for a system in the underdamped regime.

Remarkably, when the velocity correlations are evaluated during a turn (as done in the main text), their behavior is largely dominated by the turning effects. Consequently, if noise were added to the system’s dynamics, its effects would appear negligible compared to those of the turn. When the system is evaluated during a straight flight, however, the effects of noise are much more noticeable. A consequence is that numerical artifacts may become more noticeable. Fig.
