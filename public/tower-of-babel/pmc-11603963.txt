Seasonal variation in suicide: age group and summer effects in the United States (2015–2020)
Authors: Samantha To, Erick Messias, Laura Burch, John Chibnall
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT

Background

Suicide is a global public health concern that affects more than 700,000 people globally every year [23]. In the United States (U.S.) in 2022, it was the second leading cause of death among Americans ages 10–14 and 20–34. Seasonal variation in suicide rates is a phenomenon that has been well-documented: worldwide, most studies describe a peak in completed suicides during the springtime, mainly for men and older individuals, and a secondary peak during autumn [4, 7, 25]. In Mexico, from 2000–2013, researchers identified a May peak in suicides, mostly for men in urban areas as well as a secondary peak in August [9]. In South Korea, from 1991–2015, there was a general spring peak in April/May with a trough in winter [24]. In Sweden and Finland, suicide peaks occurred in May and again in October [12], while in the Netherlands, suicide peaks occurred over winter but not spring months in older adults [22]. Other studies looking at the seasonality of youth suicide describe a decrease during the summer months. In 2011, Hansen and Lang examined suicide rates from 1980–2004 in the U.S. by age groups and observed that there was a stark decrease in average suicides for the 14–18 age group during the summer months, with a trough in June and July. This protective “summer effect” on youth, however, disappeared once they turned 18, coinciding with when most youth conclude secondary education. In Japan, from 1974–2014, researchers concluded that there was an increase in the frequency of suicides by middle school students (ages 12–15 years) and high school students (15–18 years) in the months of April and September, both of which coincided with the start of the school session [15]. The researchers noted that youth suicide rates were lowest during school breaks and increased by 40% for high school students towards the end of the summer break, suggesting that youth suicide may be related to the academic school year calendar [15]. Ajdacic-Gross and colleagues [1] investigated seasonal patterns of suicidality over a span of 125 years and found that seasonal suicide patterns decreased as the agricultural work force declined, suggesting that a seasonal suicide pattern may be related to type of work in adults. More recent data on the seasonality of suicide within specific age groups and sex in the United States remains less well understood. This study aimed to characterize the seasonality of suicide by sex (Male vs. Female) and age (15–24, 25–34, 35–44, 45–54, 55–64, 65–74, 75 + years) in the United States from 2015–2020.

Methods

The aim of this study was to better understand the seasonality of suicide by sex and age in recent years in the United States from 2015–2020. The years 2015–2020 were focused on specifically for the feasibility of the study. Publicly available data on monthly suicides was obtained from the Underlying C3 CDC WONDER, 1999–2020 for six full calendar years (January 1 through December 31 of each year) from 2015–2020 [5]. Suicides were classified using the International Statistical Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD–10), underlying cause of death codes X60–X84 for intentional self-harm. The incidence of summer suicides, defined as occurring in June, July and August, for two groups, 15–24 and ages 25 and older, was calculated and a two-sided test was performed. All summer incidences are presented per 100,000 individuals. As the purpose of the study does not involve creating forecasting models to predict future trends in death by suicide (models that would require adjusting out any seasonal component), an ARIMA model was not applicable. The results presented here were obtained in SPSS version 28.0 [14] using the Seasonal Decomposition analysis, as has been demonstrated elsewhere [9, 19, 20]. Each time series created from 2015–2020 for every age group was converted into Trend, Seasonality, and Residual, respectively. The Y axis on the Seasonality figures represents the difference between the original (raw) value for that month and what the value would be for that month if all seasonality was adjusted out of the model. Therefore, the larger numbers have more influence on the seasonal component of the data,that is, movement away from 0 indicates the ebb and flow of the seasonal component of the time series. The seasonality analyses are based on suicide counts over time and therefore assume relatively stable population sizes over the six study years.

Results

In the United States from 2015–2020, there were a total of 277,858 suicides. For the group ages 15–24, there were 35,662 total suicides. For all ages 25 and older, there were 239,049 total suicides. In 2020, the incidence of summer suicide (ISS) per 100,000 in the 15–24 group was 3.62 and 4.63 in the 25 + group; incidence rate ratio (IRR) was 0.782 (p-value < 0.00001). In 2019, the ISS in the 15–24 group was 3.4, and in the 25 + group was 4.8; IRR was 0.70 (p-value < 0.00001). In 2018, the ISS for the 15–24 group was 3.54, and in the 25 + group was 5.0; IRR was 0.693 (p-value < 0.00001). In 2017, the ISS of the 15–24 group was 3.68, and in the 25 + group was 4.88; IRR was 0.754 (p-value < 0.00001). In 2016, the ISS for the 15–24 group was 3.17, and in the 25 + group was 4.72; IRR was 0.673 (p-value < 0.00001). In 2015, the ISS for the 15–24 group was 3.04, and in the 25 + group was 4.7; the IRR was 0.646 (p-value < 0.00001).

Over the six years from 2015–2020, female suicide rates remained mostly stable until 2020. By contrast, male suicide rates trended upward with the sharpest rise in April 2016 (Fig. 11A). With the exception of age group 45–54 (Fig. 15A), all other age groups also demonstrated an uptrend in suicides over time, as seen in Figs. 12A, 13A, 14A, 16A,17A, and 18A. Across both sexes and the majority of the age groups, there was a stark decrease in suicides in early 2020. Figure 12B depicts the seasonality of suicides for ages 15–24, and it differs from the rest of the age groups' seasonality. There was no difference found in the seasonality of suicide between sexes (Figs. 11C, 11D).

Discussion

This study examined seasonality of suicide by sex and age groups using a seasonal decomposition analysis. The peak for all male and female suicides in the U.S. was in July. Like other studies on seasonality performed in Hong Kong and Taiwan [11] and the Netherlands [22], seasonality of suicides did not differ between males and females. Americans between ages 15–24 experienced the highest suicide incidence in October and the lowest in June. These findings are similar to older data examining youth suicide in the U.S. and Japan [15], though these findings concluded that the summer effect was lost when individuals turned 18. Given that our data combined youth and young adults ages 15–24, it is possible that the summer effect may indeed be lost at age 18 but not captured in the present study, or that some individuals up to age 24 also experience the summer effect. Indeed, it is possible that youth and young adults may face increased stress and experience worse mental health during the academic calendar when school is in session, and the “summer effect” may be protective.

In the present study, suicide rates across most age groups decreased in early 2020. This is similar to other studies (Curtin et al., [8]) and may be due to an increase in deaths secondary to the SARS-CoV pandemic. Notably, previous research has demonstrated an increase in suicides and suicide attempts throughout the pandemic resulting from a variety of factors such as social distancing and loss of employment (Pathirathna et al., [18]), indicating that the decrease in suicides in the present study may have been a temporary early effect of the SARS-CoV pandemic.

Limitations

The etiologies of suicide are multifactorial and complex. As such, the exact explanation for the seasonality of suicide remains less clear. The literature suggests multiple theories, such as effects from temperature and/or weather on human neuro-physiological changes, including seasonal affective disorder patterns [6, 7]. Bioclimatic factors, such as weather changes and reduced daylight hours in most U.S. states over the fall and winter months, are associated with increased depression [21, 25], which may in turn lead to increased suicide. Other studies cite a “holiday effect” where suicide rates tend to increase around major national holidays like New Year's, Independence Day, or Christmas, perhaps due to social isolation or anniversary reactions [3].

The present study investigated seasonality of suicide but not of suicide attempts. Previous research has demonstrated mixed findings on suicide completion vs. attempt, such that some found that both suicides and suicide attempts increased during spring months [2], while others found suicide attempts decreased over spring months but for females only [16]. Future research should examine if suicide attempts differ from the completed suicide trends found in the current study.

The data in the current study were collected from a public database from the years 2015–2020. While the large, ecological nature of the study allowed for population analysis across time, individual factors could not be captured. It is also possible that some deaths were inaccurately recorded, such that some deaths may have been miscoded as suicides or some suicides attributed to another cause of death. Further, the years 1999–2014 were excluded in the present study. As the aim of the current study was to investigate sex and age differences for each year in recent data, limiting the timeframe allowed for the study’s feasibility. Incorporating data from 1999–2014 is a target for future directions on this project.

Finally, the present study examined suicidality in males and females from youth through adulthood. Given the increased understanding of gender expansiveness and thus the rising number of individuals who report their gender as neither male nor female [13], future studies should elucidate if and how seasonal variation in suicide presents among transgender and gender nonconforming individuals.

Conclusions

While seasonal variation in suicide rates continue to exist, individual experiences of suicide are influenced by many factors, including modifiable and nonmodifiable risk factors such as pre-existing mental health conditions, coexisting substance use disorders, socioeconomic status, age, gender, sexual orientation, access to healthcare, and social support [10, 16, 17, 23]. Increased awareness of these seasonal patterns can help inform suicide prevention efforts and support systems, and they should be considered within the broader context of mental health throughout the year. Further investigation into the seasonal differences in suicidality among young people under the age of 25 is needed.
