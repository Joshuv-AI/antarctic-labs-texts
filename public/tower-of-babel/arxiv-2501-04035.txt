Knudsen boundary layer equations with incoming boundary condition: full range of cutoff collision kernels and Mach numbers of the far field
Ning Jiang, Yi-Long Luo, Yulong Wu, Tong Yang

Abstract
. This paper establishes the existence and uniqueness of the nonlinear Knudsen layer equation with incoming boundary conditions. It is well-known that the solvability conditions of the problem vary with the Mach number of the far Maxwellian ℳ ∞ \mathcal{M}^{\infty} . We consider full ranges of cutoff collision kernels (i.e., − 3 < γ ≤ 1 -3<\gamma\leq 1 ) and all the Mach numbers of the far field in the L x , v ∞ L^{\infty}_{x,v} framework. Additionally, the solution exhibits exponential decay exp ⁡ { − c ​ x 2 3 − γ − c ​ | v | 2 } \mathrm{exp}\{-cx^{\frac{2}{3-\gamma}}-c|v|^{2}\} for some c > 0 c>0 . To address the general angular cutoff collision kernel, we introduce a ( x , v ) (x,v) -mixed weight σ \sigma . The proof is essentially based on adding an artificial damping term. Keywords. Introduction and main results

1.1.
The description of the problem

In the studies of the hydrodynamic limits of the scaled Boltzmann equation in the domains with boundary, a (linear or nonlinear) kinetic boundary layer equation, known as the Knudsen layer equation usually will be generated, see
[
1
,
9
,
15
,
16
,
22
,
23
]
for instance. In this paper, we consider the nonlinear Knudsen layer equation over
(x,v)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}
with the incoming (sometimes called absorbing) boundary condition at
x=0
:

(1.1)

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{aligned} &v_{3}\partial_{x}F=\mathcal{B}(F,F)+H\,,\ x>0,v\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\,,\\
&F(0,v)|_{v_{3}>0}=F_{b}(v)\,,\\
&\lim_{x\to+\infty}F(x,v)=\mathfrak{M}(v)\,,\end{aligned}\right.

where
H=H(x,v)\in\mathrm{Null}^{\perp}(\mathcal{L})
, with the linearized Boltzmann collision operator
\mathcal{L}
defined in (
1.7
), and
F_{b}(v)=0
if
v_{3}<0
, and
\mathfrak{M}
denotes the global Maxwellian in the far field, defined as

\displaystyle\mathfrak{M}(v)=\frac{\rho}{(2\pi T)^{3/2}}\mathrm{exp}\big[-\tfrac{|v-\mathfrak{u}|^{2}}{2T}\big]\,.

Here
\rho,T>0
and
\mathfrak{u}=(\mathfrak{u}_{1},\mathfrak{u}_{2},\mathfrak{u}_{3})\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
are given constants representing the macroscopic density, temperature, and velocity, respectively. By a shift of the variables
v_{2},v_{3}
, we can assume without loss of generality that
\mathfrak{u}_{1}=\mathfrak{u}_{2}=0
. Then, the sound speed and Mach number of this equilibrium state are given by

(1.2)

c=\sqrt{\frac{5}{3}T}\,,\quad\mathcal{M}^{\infty}=\frac{\mathfrak{u}_{3}}{c}\,.

In this paper, we consider all the ranges of the Mach number of the far Maxwellian, i.e.
\mathcal{M}^{\infty}\in\mathbb{R}
.

The Boltzmann collision operator
\mathcal{B}(F,F)
is defined as

(1.3)

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}(F,F)=\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\mathbb{S}^{2}}(F^{\prime}F_{*}^{\prime}-FF_{*})b(\omega,v_{*}-v)\mathrm{d}\omega\mathrm{d}v_{*}\,.

Here
\omega\in\mathbb{S}^{2}
is a unit vector, and
\mathrm{d}\omega
represents the rotationally invariant surface integral on
\mathbb{S}^{2}
. The terms
F_{*}^{\prime}
,
F^{\prime}
,
F_{*}
and
F
denote the number density
F(\cdot)
evaluated at the velocities
v_{*}^{\prime}
,
v^{\prime}
,
v_{*}
and
v
respectively, i.e.,

\displaystyle F_{*}^{\prime}=F(v_{*}^{\prime})\,,\ F^{\prime}=F(v^{\prime})\,,\ F_{*}=F(v_{*})\,,\ F=F(v)\,.

Here
(v_{*}^{\prime},v^{\prime})
are the velocities after an elastic binary collision between two molecules with initial velocities
(v_{*},v)
, and vice versa. Since both momentum and energy are conserved during the elastic collision,
v_{*}^{\prime}
and
v^{\prime}
can be expressed in terms of
v_{*}
and
v
as

(1.4)

\displaystyle v_{*}^{\prime}=v_{*}-[(v_{*}-v)\cdot\omega]\omega\,,\ v^{\prime}=v+[(v_{*}-v)\cdot\omega]\omega\,,

where the unit vector
\omega\in\mathbb{S}^{2}
is parallel to the deflections
v_{*}^{\prime}-v_{*}
and
v^{\prime}-v
, and is therefore perpendicular to the plane of reflection. In the collision term
\mathcal{B}(F,F)
,
F_{*}^{\prime}F^{\prime}
is the gain term, while
F_{*}F
is referred to the loss term.

According to Grad’s work
[
10
]
, the collision kernel
b(\omega,v_{*}-v)
can be expressed in the factored form

(1.5)

\displaystyle b(\omega,v_{*}-v)=\tilde{b}(\theta)|v_{*}-v|^{\gamma}\,,\ \cos\theta=\frac{(v_{*}-v)\cdot\omega}{|v_{*}-v|}\,,\ -3<\gamma\leq 1\,,

where
\tilde{b}(\theta)
satisfies the small deflection cutoff condition

(1.6)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}\tilde{b}(\theta)\mathrm{d}\omega=1\,,\ 0\leq\tilde{b}(\theta)\leq\tilde{b}_{0}|\cos\theta|

for some constant
\tilde{b}_{0}>0
. The cases
-3<\gamma<0
and
0\leq\gamma\leq 1
are respectively referred to as the “soft” and “hard” potential cases. In particular,
\gamma=0
is the Maxwell potential case, and
\gamma=1
is the hard sphere case, in which
\tilde{b}(\theta)=\tilde{b}_{0}|\cos\theta|
.

Considering the perturbation
F=\mathfrak{M}+\sqrt{\mathfrak{M}}f
around the far Maxwellian
\mathfrak{M}
, and neglecting the nonlinear term, we obtain the linearized Knudsen layer problem with a source term:

(KL)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &v_{3}\partial_{x}f+\mathcal{L}f=S\,,\\
&f(0,v)|_{v_{3}>0}=f_{b}(v)\,,\\
&\lim_{x\to+\infty}f(x,v)=0\,,\end{aligned}\right.

where
S=S(x,v)\in\mathrm{Null}^{\perp}(\mathcal{L})
and
f_{b}(v)=0
if
v_{3}<0
. The symbol
\mathcal{L}
denotes the linearized Boltzmann collision operator
\mathcal{B}(F,F)
around the far Maxwellian
\mathfrak{M}
, defined as

(1.7)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}f=

\displaystyle-\mathfrak{M}^{-\frac{1}{2}}(v)\big[\mathcal{B}(\mathfrak{M},\sqrt{\mathfrak{M}}f)+\mathcal{B}(\sqrt{\mathfrak{M}}f,\mathfrak{M})\big]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\nu(v)f(v)-Kf(v)\,,

where
\nu(v)
denotes the collision frequency, given by

(1.8)

\displaystyle\nu(v)=\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\mathbb{S}^{2}}\mathfrak{M}(v_{*})b(\omega,v_{*}-v)\mathrm{d}\omega\mathrm{d}v_{*}\overset{\eqref{Cutoff}}{=}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}|v_{*}-v|^{\gamma}\mathfrak{M}(v_{*})\mathrm{d}v_{*}\,,

and the operator
Kf(v)
can be decomposed into two parts:

(1.9)

\displaystyle Kf(v)=-K_{1}f(v)+K_{2}f(v)\,.

Here the loss term
K_{1}f(v)
is

(1.10)

\displaystyle K_{1}f(v)=\mathfrak{M}^{\frac{1}{2}}(v)\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\mathbb{S}^{2}}f(v_{*})\mathfrak{M}^{\frac{1}{2}}(v_{*})b(\omega,v_{*}-v)\mathrm{d}\omega\mathrm{d}v_{*}\,,

and the gain term
K_{2}f(v)
is

(1.11)

\displaystyle K_{2}f(v)=

\displaystyle\mathfrak{M}^{\frac{1}{2}}(v)\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\mathbb{S}^{2}}\big[\mathfrak{M}^{-\frac{1}{2}}(v^{\prime})f(v^{\prime})+\mathfrak{M}^{-\frac{1}{2}}(v_{*}^{\prime})f(v_{*}^{\prime})\big]\mathfrak{M}(v_{*})b(\omega,v_{*}-v)\mathrm{d}\omega\mathrm{d}v_{*}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\mathbb{S}^{2}}\big[\mathfrak{M}^{\frac{1}{2}}(v^{\prime}_{*})f(v^{\prime})+\mathfrak{M}^{\frac{1}{2}}(v^{\prime})f(v_{*}^{\prime})\big]\mathfrak{M}^{\frac{1}{2}}(v_{*})b(\omega,v_{*}-v)\mathrm{d}\omega\mathrm{d}v_{*}\,,

where the last equality is derived from the collisional invariant
\mathfrak{M}(v)\mathfrak{M}(v_{*})=\mathfrak{M}(v^{\prime})\mathfrak{M}(v_{*}^{\prime})
.

To deal with the general Mach number, we define the following linearized entropy flux as in
[
5
]
:

P(f,g)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}v_{3}fg\mathrm{d}v\,.

The null space
\mathrm{Null}(\mathcal{L})
of the operator
\mathcal{L}
is spanned by the basis (Coron-Golse-Sulem
[
5
]
)

(1.12)

\displaystyle\psi_{0}=\xi_{1}\sqrt{\mathfrak{M}}\,,\ \psi_{1}=\xi_{2}\sqrt{\mathfrak{M}}\,,\ \psi_{2}=\Big(\sqrt{\tfrac{5}{2}}-\tfrac{|\xi|^{2}}{\sqrt{10}}\Big)\sqrt{\mathfrak{M}}\,,

\displaystyle\psi_{3}=\Big(\tfrac{\xi_{3}}{\sqrt{2}}+\tfrac{|\xi|^{2}}{\sqrt{30}}\Big)\sqrt{\mathfrak{M}}\,,\ \psi_{4}=\Big(-\tfrac{\xi_{3}}{\sqrt{2}}+\tfrac{|\xi|^{2}}{\sqrt{30}}\Big)\sqrt{\mathfrak{M}}\,.

where
\xi=\frac{v-\mathfrak{u}}{\sqrt{T}}
. In fact, the basis is chosen to be orthogonal in the Hilbert space
L^{2}:=L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
and the entropy flux
P
. Specifically,

(1.13)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\psi_{i}\psi_{j}\mathrm{d}v=P(\psi_{i},\psi_{j})=0\,,\quad i\neq j\,.

It is well-known (see
[
5
,
25
,
4
,
29
]
) that the solvability of the Knudsen layer problem varies with the Mach number of the far Maxwellian, which is related to the signature of the entropy flux. It is important to note that

(1.14)

\displaystyle P(\psi_{i},\psi_{i})

\displaystyle=\rho c\mathcal{M}^{\infty}\,,\quad i=0,1,2\,,

\displaystyle P(\psi_{3},\psi_{3})

\displaystyle=\rho c\Big(\mathcal{M}^{\infty}+1\Big)\,,

\displaystyle P(\psi_{4},\psi_{4})

\displaystyle=\rho c\Big(\mathcal{M}^{\infty}-1\Big)\,.

As we will see below, the number of solvability conditions
n^{+}
of the Knudsen layer problem (both linear and nonlinear) changes with the Mach number
\mathcal{M}^{\infty}
as follows:

(1.15)

n^{+}=\#\{I^{+}\cup I^{0}\}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}0\,,\quad\mathcal{M}^{\infty}<-1\,,\\
1\,,\quad-1\leq\mathcal{M}^{\infty}<0\,,\\
4\,,\quad 0\leq\mathcal{M}^{\infty}<1\,,\\
5\,,\quad 1\leq\mathcal{M}^{\infty}\,,\end{array}\right.

where the sets
I^{+}
and
I^{0}
are defined in (
1.17
). Let
\mathrm{Null}^{\perp}(\mathcal{L})
be the orthogonal space of
\mathrm{Null}(\mathcal{L})
in
L^{2}
, namely,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Null}(\mathcal{L})\oplus\mathrm{Null}^{\perp}(\mathcal{L})=L^{2}\,.

We define the projection
\mathcal{P}:L^{2}\to\mathrm{Null}(\mathcal{L})
by

(1.16)

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}f=\sum_{i=0}^{4}a_{i}(f)\psi_{i}\,,\quad a_{i}(f)=\frac{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}f\psi_{i}\mathrm{d}v}{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\psi^{2}_{i}\mathrm{d}v}\,,\quad i=0,1,2,3,4\,.

To construct the damping term, we define

(1.17)

I^{+}=\{j\mid P(\psi_{j},\psi_{j})>0\}\,,\quad I^{0}=\{j\mid P(\psi_{j},\psi_{j})=0\},\quad I^{-}=\{j\mid P(\psi_{j},\psi_{j})<0\}\,.

For
j\in I^{0}
, it follows that

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}v_{3}\psi_{j}^{2}\mathrm{d}v=0\,,

which, together with (
1.13
), implies that
v_{3}\psi_{j}\in\mathrm{Null}^{\perp}(\mathcal{L})
. The Fredholm alternative for the linearized Boltzmann operator implies that there exists a unique
X_{j}
such that

(1.18)

X_{j}\in\mathrm{Null}^{\perp}(\mathcal{L})\quad\text{and}\quad\!\mathcal{L}X_{j}=v_{3}\psi_{j}\,.

We now define the operator

(1.19)

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}^{+}f

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\in I^{+}}\frac{(\psi_{j},f)}{(\psi_{j},\psi_{j})}\psi_{j}\,,

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}^{0}f

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\in I^{0}}\frac{(X_{j},v_{3}f)}{(X_{j},\mathcal{L}X_{j})}\psi_{j}\,,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}f

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\in I^{0}}\frac{(X_{j},f)}{(X_{j},\mathcal{L}X_{j})}\mathcal{L}X_{j}\,.

One can easily check that

\mathcal{P}^{+}\mathcal{P}^{+}=\mathcal{P}^{+}\,,\quad\mathbb{P}\mathbb{P}=\mathbb{P}\,,\quad\mathcal{P}^{0}\mathcal{P}^{0}=\mathcal{P}^{0}\,.

Therefore, the operators defined above are indeed projections. Additionally, we present the following lemma:

Lemma 1.1
.

(Lemma 2.3 of
[
6
]
)
The projections
\mathbb{P}
and
\mathcal{P}^{0}
satisfy the relations

\mathbb{P}v_{3}f=v_{3}\mathcal{P}^{0}f;\quad\mathbb{P}\mathcal{L}f=0\,,\quad\text{if}\quad\!\ v_{3}f\in\mathrm{Null}\mathcal{L}\,.

Moreover,
(\mathcal{P}^{0}f,f)\geq 0.

We emphasize that the above operators will be utilized in designing the artificial damping term.

1.2.
Overdetermined far-field condition
\lim_{x\to+\infty}f(x,v)=0

Inspired by Theorem 3.3 of
[
3
]
, the function

solving the problem

(1.20)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &v_{3}\partial_{x}f+\mathcal{L}f=S\,,\ x>0\,,\\
&f(0,v)|_{v_{3}>0}=f_{b}(v)\end{aligned}\right.

will exhibit the asymptotic behavior

(1.21)

\displaystyle\lim_{x\to+\infty}f(x,v)=f_{\infty}=a_{\infty}\psi_{0}(v)+b_{1\infty}\psi_{1}(v)+b_{2\infty}\psi_{2}(v)+b_{3\infty}\psi_{3}(v)+c_{\infty}\psi_{4}(v)

for some constants

,
b_{1\infty}
,
b_{2\infty}
,
b_{3\infty}
and
c_{\infty}
, with the functions
\psi_{i}(v)
are given in (
1.12
). In other words, the far-field condition
\lim_{x\to+\infty}f(x,v)=0
in (
KL
) is overdetermined for general source terms
S\in\mathrm{Null}^{\perp}(\mathcal{L})
and
f_{b}
.

However, the far-field condition
\lim_{x\to+\infty}f(x,v)=0
is necessary in proving the hydrodynamic limits of the Boltzmann equation with Maxwell reflection or incoming boundary conditions. In order to overcome the overdetermination of zero far-field condition, some further assumptions on the source terms

and
f_{b}
are required. Similar to
[
14
]
, we introduce the so-called

(
\mathbb{VSS}
) defined by

(1.22)

\displaystyle\mathbb{VSS}=\big\{(S,f_{b});S(x,v)\in\mathrm{Null}^{\perp}(\mathcal{L})\textrm{ and }f_{b}(v)\textrm{ in \eqref{KL-d} such that }\lim_{x\to+\infty}f(x,v)=0\big\}\,.

Since (
1.20
) is linear and
f_{\infty}\in\mathrm{Null}(\mathcal{L})
, we observe that
\tilde{f}=f-f_{\infty}
solves (
KL
) with sources
(S,f_{b}-f_{\infty})
. Therefore, we know that

(1.23)

\displaystyle\mathbb{VSS}\neq\varnothing\,.

1.6.
Methodology and novelties

In this subsection, we methodologically sketch the proof of the main results and illustrate the novelties of this paper. As shown in the previous subsection, the core of current work is to derive the uniform estimates of the approximate problem (
Ap-eq
) associated with the parameter
A>1
. The main ideas are displayed as follows.

(I) Choice of the mixed weight
\sigma(x,v)
.
The major part of the equation (
KL
) reads

(1.56)

\displaystyle v_{3}\partial_{x}f+\nu(v)f=s.o.t.\textrm{ (some other terms)}\,.

For the hard sphere model
\gamma=1
,
v_{3}
and
\nu(v)
have the same order as
|v|\gg 1
, i.e.
|v_{3}|\lesssim\nu(v)
. Roughly speaking, the
x
-decay can be expected from

\displaystyle\partial_{x}f+cf=s.o.t.\,,

which means that the expected
x
-decay is
e^{-cx}
.

For the cases
-3<\gamma<1
,
|v_{3}|\lesssim\nu(v)|v_{3}|^{1-\gamma}
. The Case is much more complicated. Note that (
1.56
) implies

\displaystyle\partial_{x}(e^{\frac{\nu(v)}{v_{3}}x}f)=s.o.t.\,.

This inspires us to introduce an
(x,v)
-mixed weight
\sigma(x,v)
to deal with the power
\frac{\nu(v)}{v_{3}}x
. More precisely, (
1.56
) reduces to

\displaystyle v_{3}\partial_{x}(e^{\hbar\sigma}f)+(\nu(v)-\hbar v_{3}\sigma_{x})e^{\hbar\sigma}f=s.o.t.\,,

which inspires us to find a weight
\sigma(x,v)
such that
v_{3}\sigma_{x}(x,v)\thicksim\nu(v)
. Chen-Liu-Yang’s work
[
4
]
designed the weight
\sigma(x,v)
, which satisfies

|v_{3}|\sigma_{x}(x,v)\lesssim\nu(v)\,,\sigma(x,v)\geq c(\delta x+l)^{\frac{2}{3-\gamma}}\,,\sigma_{x}(x,v)\lesssim(\delta x+l)^{-\frac{1-\gamma}{3-\gamma}}\,.

The derivative
\sigma_{x}
of
\sigma
actually balances the disparity of
|v_{3}|
and
\nu(v)
. Chen-Liu-Yang investigated the Knudsen layer equation with the nondegenerate moving boundary condition with hard potential collision kernel
0<\gamma\leq 1
.

(II) Artificial damping mechanism.
It is well-known that the linearized Boltzmann operator
\mathcal{L}
does not provide the coercivity structure in the null space
\mathrm{Null}(\mathcal{L})
, i.e., macroscopic damping effect. Previous studies introduced various artificial damping terms to obtain the macroscopic coercivity, and then remove these terms using appropriate techniques. For instance, in
[
5
,
7
,
11
,
12
,
13
]
, the damping
\varepsilon f
was introduced to deal with the linear Knudsen layer equation for hard sphere collision case with various boundary conditions, and later removed by taking
\varepsilon\to 0
using compact arguments. In
[
4
,
27
,
28
,
29
]
, the artificial damping
-\gamma P_{0}^{+}\xi_{1}f
(
\gamma>0
) coming from the eigenspace corresponding to positive eigenvalues of the linear operator
P_{0}\xi_{1}P_{0}
are applied to deal with the incoming boundary conditions for the nondegenerate case, i.e. the Mach number of the far Maxwellian
\mathcal{M}^{\infty}\neq 0\,,\pm 1
. In order to return the original equation, a further assumption on the incoming data
a(\xi)
(the data
a(\xi)
vanishes in the eigenspace corresponding to positive eigenvalues of the linear operator
P_{0}\xi_{1}P_{0}
, i.e.,
P_{0}^{+}\xi_{1}P_{0}a=0
) will be imposed such that the artificial damping
-\gamma P_{0}^{+}\xi_{1}f
vanishes by the Grönwall inequality argument. For the degenerate case, Golse
[
6
]
introduced the artificial damping term
-\alpha\Pi_{+}(v_{1}\phi)-\mathbf{p}(\phi)
, later removing it through similar arguments.

In this paper, to deal with the full ranges of the Mach number of the far Maxwellian with all the cutoff collision kernels, we introduce the artificial damping term:

-\bar{\alpha}(\delta x+l)^{-\frac{\Theta}{2}}\mathcal{P}^{+}v_{3}g-\bar{\beta}(\delta x+l)^{-\frac{\Theta}{2}}\mathcal{P}^{0}g.

Finally, we impose additional assumptions on the source terms to remove the damping term, establishing the solvability conditions of the Knudsen layer problem.

(V) Existence of the equations (

Based on the uniform a priori estimates on the approximate problem (

The first step is to establish the existence and uniqueness of the approximate problem (

The second step is to justify the existence of (

and assuming

constructed in Lemma

obeys the uniform-in-

is defined on

to

is the unique solution to (

).

The third step establishes the existence and uniqueness of the linear Knudsen layer equation (

). We begin by decomposing (

(1.57)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &v_{3}\partial_{x}(I-\mathcal{P}^{0})f+\mathcal{L}(I-\mathcal{P}^{0})f=(I-\mathbb{P})S\,,\\
&\lim_{x\to+\infty}(I-\mathcal{P}^{0})f(x,v)=0\,,\end{aligned}\right.

and

\left\{\begin{aligned} &v_{3}\mathcal{P}^{0}f=\mathbb{P}S\,,\\
&\lim_{x\to+\infty}\mathcal{P}^{0}f(x,v)=0\,.\end{aligned}\right.

Note that for the nondegenerate case
\mathcal{M}^{\infty}\neq 0\,,\pm 1

\mathcal{P}^{0}f=\mathbb{P}S\equiv 0
for any

1.8.
Incoming and Maxwell reflection boundary conditions

In the final part of this Introduction, we make some comments on the two widely-used types of the boundary conditions of the Knudsen boundary layer equations: incoming and Maxwell reflection conditions. Physically, these two types of boundary conditions are quite different. A typical model for the incoming boundary condition is the evaporation and condensation of the liquid-gas interface (See Sone’s books
[
22
,
23
]
). Simply saying, the boundary conditions of the fluid equations derived from Boltzmann equations with these two conditions are completely different. For example, in the derivation of acoustic system, the boundary condition from Maxwell reflection condition is the well-known
impermeable
condition:

\mathrm{u}(t,x)\cdot\mathrm{n}(x)=0\,,\quad\mbox{on}\quad\!\partial\Omega\,.

where
\mathrm{n}(x)
is the outer normal vector at
x\in\partial\Omega
on the boundary. However, from the Boltzmann equation with incoming condition, the boundary of the acoustic system is the following
pressure jump boundary condition
(
[
2
,
18
]
)

\rho(t,x)+\theta(t,x)=\Lambda(\mathrm{u}(t,x)\cdot\mathrm{n}(t,x))\quad\text{ on }\quad\!\partial\Omega.

Here the constant
\Lambda>0
is named as
pressure jump coefficient
. It comes from solving the Knudsen boundary layer equation. More interestingly, a classic result in the constant coefficient symmetric hyperbolic system tells us that there are only types of boundary conditions such that the acoustic system is well-posed: impermeable and pressure jump boundary conditions. In this sense, incoming and Maxwell reflection conditions at kinetic level together provide the complete and complementary boundary conditions at the fluid levels. This example illustrates that the the Boltzmann equation and its corresponding Knudsen layer boundary layer equations with incoming and Maxwell reflection boundaries deserve studies separately. Both of them have the interests of their own to be investigated. For other more complicated nonlinear fluid equations, such as compressible Euler and Navier-Stokes equations, the boundary conditions from Boltzmann equations with incoming and Maxwell reflection conditions are also completely different and complementary
[
19
]
.
