Changes in EEG and ECG signal connectivity during tilt table testing in healthy young adults without syncope
Authors: Anastasiya Runnova, Maxim Zhuravlev, Mikhail Novikov, Aina Borodusova, Anton R Kiselev
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionTilt table testing (TTT) has been used for over fifty years in clinical and basic medicine to study the adaptation of heart rate and blood pressure to changes in body position. In the area of ​​studying local features of electroencephalogram (EEG) and electrocardiogram (ECG) signal synchronization, there is a lack of research devoted to the precise quantitative mathematical analysis of electrophysiological signals recorded during TTT. Furthermore, most studies related to brain activity analysis focus on the development of syncope.MethodsThis study analyzed electrophysiological signals (EEG and ECG) in 19 healthy men. The TTT procedure was performed in a gentle mode with a slight elevation (45°) of the volunteers after 15 min of horizontal positioning. This procedure eliminated the risk of syncope. Time-frequency analysis of the EEG was performed using a continuous wavelet transform. Synchronization of electrophysiological signals in the brain and cardiac activity was assessed using wavelet bicoherence, assessed pairwise between ECG and EEG signals.ResultsEEG activity in the brain remained unchanged before and after verticalization of the volunteers. Heart rate increased, on average, from 1.0 to 1.05 Hz in the horizontal position to 1.3–1.4 Hz in the vertical position (p < 0.005). Significant changes in synchronization were demonstrated before and after verticalization of the volunteers. Most interesting is the increase in ECG and EEG synchronization in the low-frequency range [0.25; 0.75] Hz in response to the tilt table test. Such changes in synchronization can be interpreted in the context of the activation of the human body’s adaptive responses during this test.ConclusionThis study results provide a basis for understanding the physiological relationship between the cardiovascular system and brain activity during the standing position test (SPT) without syncope. These results suggest that low-frequency (below the baseline heart rate) components of P-QRS-T complex variability may be a promising approach for studying neurocardiac interactions.Keywords: Electroencephalography (EEG), Electrocardiography (ECG), Tilt table test, Wavelet bicoherence, Neurocardiac interaction

Introduction

Tilt table testing (TTT) has been used in clinical and basic medicine for over fifty years to study the adaptation of heart rate and blood pressure to changes in body position [1]. Furtemore, the use of tilt testing allows modeling of reactions to bleeding, assessment of orthostatic hypotension characteristics, and study hemodynamic and neuroendocrine responses in congestive heart failure, autonomic dysfunction, and hypertension [1–3].

The essence of this test is a sharp vertical rise of the body after 15–20 min of the patient lying in a supine position [4, 5]. A sudden transition of a person to a vertical position often causes a state of vasovagal (neurocardiogenic) syncope or presyncope with hypotension even in healthy volunteers – from 8 to 20% of cases depending on the characteristics of the test protocol [6–10]. During a state of syncope, significant changes occur on the electroencephalogram (EEG) [11–13]. The combined use of EEG and TTT allows for a more accurate description of the clinical etiology of loss of consciousness in this case [5, 14]. At the same time, studies of the brain EEG activity are focused, firstly, on solving the problem to detect the patient’s condition during syncope or presyncope. Secondly, the conducted analysis of the electrical activity of the cerebral cortex is often reduced to visual and amplitude or simple spectral analysis [11, 14–16]. In our opinion, there is a certain lack of works with precise quantitative mathematical analysis of electrophysiological signals recorded during TTT. In particular, there is an obvious lack of research activity in the field of studying local features of synchronization of EEG and electrocardiogram (ECG) signals. In the presented work, we carry out a sequential analysis based on the apparatus of continuous wavelet transform (CWT) and wavelet bicoherence (WB) of objective quantitative changes in electrophysiological signals recorded in healthy volunteers during TTT without achieving positive extreme states of syncope and presyncope. Such work, in our opinion, can create some understanding of the basis - the physiological norm during the verticalization test - TTT. Starting from this conditional norm, we can then consider the entire array of different possible pathological body reactions to TTT.

The TTT protocol used in our work included raising the volunteers to a fundamentally low degree (45°) in order to artificially exclude the positive situation of syncope and presyncope, and, therefore, provide an exceptional opportunity to examine the situation of changes in electrophysiological signals during normally tolerated verticalization. Based on the general premises, the presence of connectivity between ECG and EEG signals seems to be indirectly proven [17, 18]. In addition, studies show that the central nervous system’s cardiac and respiratory rhythm generators are located anatomically close to each other in the brainstem [19, 20]. Interactions between cardiovascular and brain electrophysiological signals have been demonstrated in animals under different states of anesthesia [21]. The presented work demonstrates how the quantitative measure of synchronization between electrophysiological signals of the cardiovascular system and the brain in healthy volunteers changes paradoxically during abrupt TTT verticalization – decreasing in the high-frequency region (0.75–1 Hz) and increasing in the low-frequency region (0.25–0.75 Hz).

Results

ECG analysis

The dynamics of the cardiovascular system rhythm underwent clear significant changes during the tilt test. Figure 1, a shows the results of statistical evaluation for a group of healthy volunteers. On average, the heart rate increased from 1.0 to 1.05 Hz in the horizontal state to 1.3–1.4 Hz in the vertical state. The differences in heart rate values before and after verticalization are statistically significant according to the Mann-Whitney U-test (p < 0.005). Figure 1, b shows the calculation of the difference in values between the average heart rate of the patient before verticalization during the TTT and after it. In addition, the differences in heart rate before and after verticalization are within the Bland–Altman limits [22, 23] with a negative mean value (Fig. 1, c), which emphasizes the presence of significant, but at the same time not catastrophic changes in heart rate during tilt testing. Figure 1, d shows the dependence of the heart rate for one of the patients with an initially low heart rate, about 0.8 Hz. It is clearly seen that for this patient, verticalization also leads to an increase in the heart rate, but to slightly lower values, not exceeding 1.25 Hz.

Discussion

The main peak of the ECG spectrum in our study coincide with the frequency of the main heart rhythm and does not exceed 1.6 Hz at different stages of the tilt test, which is typical for relatively healthy individuals [24].

We estimate the connection between cardiovascular and cerebral electrophysiological signals, previously identified by various methods of nonlinear dynamics, for example, in [17, 25]. In this paper, changes in phase connectivity are studied in EEG and ECG signals, rather than the time series of heart rate variability (HRV) obtained after identifying sequences between RR intervals. As shown in Fig. 6a, most of the HR curve consists of approximating fragments located between directly determined points of R peaks. In this regard, the frequency content of the final HRV signal changes significantly when using different approximation functions in the analysis based on the Fourier transform, i.e. even without taking into account its time dynamics (Fig. 6, b). In addition, the HRV assessment is always complicated under conditions of arrhythmia and other disturbances of normal heart rhythm. Note that, obviously, such a choice of methodology leads to a significant change in the spectral composition of the analyzed activity of the cardiovascular system. At the same time, as shown in Fig. 7, the spectral composition of the ECG and HRV signals demonstrates a significant difference. The spectral composition of the original ECG signal is characterized by the presence of two oscillatory components of maximum intensity, namely the heart rate itself (0.8–0.9 Hz in Fig. 7, c) and respiratory activity (≈ 0.35 Hz). Thus, the observed significant differences in WB synchronization between EEG and ECG signals before and after verticalization in the bands ∆f1 [0.25; 0.75] Hz and ∆f2 [0.75; 1.5] Hz can be associated precisely with the influence of these intense oscillatory processes – respiratory activity and HRV.

The frequency bands used in our study to assess EEG and ECG connectivity are significantly lower than the characteristic frequency components of the ECG spectrum associated with the QRS complex and other components of the typical waveforms of the cardiac cycle on the ECG [26–28]. Therefore, we believe that the obtained estimates are associated primarily with low-frequency influences (< 1 Hz) on the periodicity of the P-QRS-T waveform as a whole, possibly (our hypothesis) having a similar nature to the generally accepted frequency estimates used in the classical analysis of heart rate variability.

The obtained results indicate a statistically significant change in the structure of the WB interaction of the electrophysiological activity system of the EEG and ECG signals during verticalization of healthy participants in the tilt-table testing procedure. At the same time, the structure of the EEG activity itself remains unchanged, without demonstrating statistically significant differences in any of the standard neurophysiological ranges of rhythmic activity. Thus, the ongoing metamorphosis in synchronization between the EEG and ECG signals may be due to an increase in heart rate caused by abrupt verticalization during TTT. At the same time, we note that despite the increase in heart rate, an increase in synchronization between the ECG and EEG leads is observed in the low-frequency band [0.25; 0.75] Hz, while in the band of relatively high frequencies [0.75; 1.5] Hz WB synchronization, on the contrary, is significantly reduced. Such dynamics may be associated with a complex structure of the low-frequency components of the ECG spectrum, which has various superimposed oscillations nonlinearly related to each other [29]. In particular, it is known that respiratory sinus arrhythmia may depend on oscillations of the respiratory pacemaker in the central nervous system. The pacemaker hypothesis states that a core of neurons with nonlinear intrinsic properties is essential for generating synchronized rhythmic activity that spreads to subsequent respiratory neurons [30, 31]. Both the pacemaker and actual breathing can cause fluctuations in heart rate [32]. Central modulation of sympathetic nerve activity by respiration occurs due to the connection of presympathetic centers in the medulla oblongata with central generators of breathing patterns [33, 34]. Thus, the change in synchronization in the recorded surface electrophysiological signals may reflect deep processes of adjustment of heart rate patterns to maintain consciousness and normal respiratory rhythm of a healthy volunteer during cardiovascular stress from verticalization on tilt-table testing.

The obtained results in terms of describing the dynamics of the cardiovascular system do not contradict classical descriptions [10]. The absence of direct changes in the electroencephalographic activity of the brain is due to the complete preservation of consciousness and the absence of sincope and presincope states in the volunteers [5, 14]. However, the proposed approach to studying synchronization based on a comprehensive assessment of the dynamics of electrophysiological signals of the cardiovascular system and the brain as a single interconnected system is a very innovative approach for analyzing tilt-table testing procedures. At the same time, this approach is used in other areas of neuroscience and fundamental research. In particular, for example, when studying polysomnographic records, it was shown that analyzing the synchronization measure between the ECG and EEG of patients allows us to identify a certain correlation with their pulse pressure [17].

The question remains as to how well the frequency components in P-QRS-T waveform variabilty with a frequency lower than the basic heart rate can be compared with the known frequency measures used in the classical analysis of heart rate variability. Our hypothesis is that regulatory processes (sympathetic, parasympathetic, baroreflex and respiratory), similar to those studied based on the spectral analysis of heart rate variability, exert their modulating effect on the components of the P-QRS-T waveform, forming frequency responses in the ECG spectrum. At the same time, due to the high resolution of the ECG signal (in our case 1000 sps), there is a greater opportunity to study their relationship with the frequency components of the EEG, relative to the less discrete classical series of R-R intervals allocated for HRV analysis (usually 4 sps). Perhaps, the frequency components of P-QRS-T waveform variabilty can carry additional information on the regulatory processes that ensure the adaptation of the body during the tilt test.

Conclusion

The most interesting finding of our study is the increase in WB synchronization between ECG and EEG in the low-frequency band [0.25; 0.75] Hz in response to the tilt-table test, which generally coincides with the known data on the activation of adaptive reactions in the human body when performing this test. We believe that the obtained results allow us to consider the low-frequency (below the frequency of the basic heart rhythm) components of P-QRS-T waveform variabilty as a promising approach for studying neurocardiac interactions.

Limitations

The main limitation of our study is a limited number of subjects in groups. We present pilot results that will allow planning future studies.

In this study, we did not include women, because we did not have the opportunity to take into account the characteristics of the reproductive system (e.g. serum sex hormone levels and the onset of menopause). This gender exclusion criterion also limits the results of our study. Gender-related differences become less relevant with age and diminish around the time of menopause, which may suggest a potential hormonal influence on the autonomic nervous system [35].

Materials and methods

Subject selection

Nineteen healthy subjects, men, participated in our work. Subjects were recruited through advertisement and received no payment for their participation. Potential subjects were interviewed before the tilt test. They were eligible for inclusion if they were aged 18 years or older, and had no past history of fainting or unconsciousness, cardiovascular or neurological disease, diabetes, or any serious current illness. Subjects were excluded if they were taking any regular drugs, or showed any abnormality on examination or on a 12 lead ECG. None of the subjects had previously undergone tilt testing.

All subjects had their blood pressure measured in a sitting position using the oscillometric method upon inclusion in the study to exclude undiagnosed hypertension.

Tilt test protocol

For all subjects who were examined in the afternoon hours on an empty stomach, in conditions of spontaneous breathing, we recorded blood pressure (BP), ECG and EEG for 30 min during the passive head-up tilt test. A continuous ECG and 19-channel EEG were simultaneously recorded while non-invasive BP was recorded by oscillometric method every fifteen minutes. The international 10–20 system was performed utilizing the NVX-52 by Medical Computer Systems (RF, Zelenograd). All signals were sampled at 1000 sps.

The room temperature was within a comfortable range (22–23 °C).

The protocol of the passive head-up tilt test used in our study included the following steps:

(iii)The subject was passively placed in an upright position with a tilt angle of 45°. The motorised tilt table Vario-Line Tilt Table (RF, Beka RUS) achieved 45° of tilt over 10 s. During the test, subjects were restrained by two Velcro straps placed around the legs and waist, and conversation, other than reporting symptoms, was discouraged.

Data processing

ECG analysis

Based on the ECG signal, the continuous heart rate (in Hz) was identified in the time intervals before and after raising the tilt-table, and then its variability was assessed. Spectral analysis of the ECG signal was also performed.

EEG analysis

To assess the oscillatory characteristics of the EEG, the calculation of the continuous wavelet transform (CWT) was used in the classical neurophysiological frequency bands: delta, theta, alpha, beta1, beta2, gamma. For each band, the instantaneous average energy was calculated, which was averaged over 30-second time intervals. Then, the data were averaged over 15 min - the periods of a supine rest and the tilt-test.
