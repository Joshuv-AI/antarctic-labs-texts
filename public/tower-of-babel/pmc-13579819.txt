Vasoactive-inotropic score and major cardiovascular events in patients undergoing esophagectomy: a retrospective analysis of intravenous lidocaine and thoracic epidural anesthesia
Authors: Syed Hasan Metwaly, Anne Kamphausen, Wolfgang Hitzl, Luca Giulini, Patrick Heger, Markus K. Diener, Jens Christian Kubitz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEsophagectomy is associated with clinically relevant perioperative morbidity and mortality. Analgesia may be provided using opioids in conjunction with thoracic epidural anesthesia (TEA) or lidocaine infusion. This study evaluates whether perioperative vasoactive requirements and cardiopulmonary outcomes differ between patients receiving these two analgesic regimens.MethodsThis retrospective single-center cohort study included 441 patients undergoing esophagectomy from 2013 to 2023. The primary endpoint was the intraoperative maximum vasoactive-inotropic score (VISmax OP). Propensity-score full matching used age, sex, body mass index, Lee Revised Cardiac Risk Index, operative time, surgical approach, and year of surgery, followed by covariate adjustment. Secondary endpoints included postoperative VISmax (VISmax ICU), major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE), pulmonary complications, postoperative arrhythmias (atrial fibrillation), and 30-day mortality.ResultsOne hundred sixty-nine patients received lidocaine, and 272 received TEA. The lidocaine group demonstrated a significantly higher VISmax OP than the TEA group after propensity-score full matching and multivariable covariate adjustment (adjusted mean: 14.6 [95% CI: 13.0–16.3] vs. 12.5 [95% CI: 11.3–13.8]; adjusted mean difference: 2.14, 95% CI 0.22–4.10; p = 0.029). VISmax ICU was also significantly higher in the lidocaine group than in the TEA group (adjusted mean, 37.1 [95% CI, 26.1–48.1] vs. 22.7 [95% CI, 17.1–28.3]; adjusted mean difference, 14.4 [95% CI, 2.48–26.3]; p = 0.019). Postoperative atrial fibrillation occurred more frequently in the lidocaine group (40.4% vs. 21.4%; adjusted ratio 0.55, 95% CI 0.38–0.79; p = 0.001), whereas no significant differences were observed in pulmonary complications, 30-day mortality, or cardiopulmonary arrest after matching and adjustment.ConclusionThe lidocaine group demonstrated a significantly higher VISmax OP (intraoperative) and VISmax ICU (postoperative) compared to the TEA group after adjustment for BMI, age, sex, Lee RCI, operative time, surgical approach, and year of surgery. Higher VISmax was associated with significantly increased odds of postoperative arrhythmias. However, these findings should be interpreted cautiously and externally validated in future prospective studies on optimal perioperative analgesia strategies in esophagectomy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12871-026-04198-9.

Introduction

Patients undergoing major noncardiac surgeries are at risk of experiencing major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE). Esophagectomy is considered a high-risk thoracoabdominal procedure that is associated with high postoperative morbidity and mortality, with an overall complication rate of 59% [1]. The most common MACE include atrial arrhythmias (11.5% to 22%) and myocardial infarctions (1.1% to 3.8%) [2].

For perioperative analgesia, thoracic epidural analgesia (TEA) has been considered the gold standard procedure in esophagectomy [3, 4]. The benefits of TEA include a reduction in postoperative pulmonary complications, faster recovery, better analgesia, and shorter hospital stays compared to parenteral opioids in these patients [5]. Further blockade of thoracic sympathetic innervation may reduce heart rate and lead to cardiac protection [6]. However, this reduction in sympathetic tone can lead to lower systemic vascular resistance and may necessitate vasopressor therapy [7]. Hemodynamic instabilities have been reported, particularly in patients with ischemic heart disease [8]. On the other hand, vasopressor treatment can increase cardiac mortality and morbidity [9].

An alternative perioperative analgesia regimen involves the use of intravenous lidocaine. Lidocaine has been shown to reduce acute pain in the early postoperative period, 1 h, 4, 24, and 48 h after surgery [10]. Moreover, evidence suggests that lidocaine can reduce the postoperative development of chronic pain [11], therefore reducing the rate of opioid consumption and its related complications [12]. Additionally, lidocaine may lower perioperative morbidity by protecting against bronchial hyperreactivity and by reducing the rate of ileus [13]. However, little is known about the cardiovascular effects of intravenous lidocaine in patients undergoing esophagectomy. At the same time, studies have shown that TEA can reduce morbidity and mortality post-esophagectomy compared to intravenous opioids [3, 14]. To our knowledge, some investigations have compared the analgesic effects of TEA and intravenous lidocaine, without assessing cardiovascular stability [10, 15].

The primary objective of this retrospective analysis was to compare VISmax OP values to evaluate vasoactive support between patients receiving intravenous lidocaine and those receiving thoracic epidural analgesia (TEA) during esophagectomy performed between 2013 and 2023. Secondary outcomes included VISmax during ICU stay, major adverse cardiovascular events, pulmonary complications, postoperative atrial arrhythmias, cardiac troponin T elevation (cTnT), and 30-day mortality.

The maximum vasoactive-inotropic score (VISmax) has previously been used as a predictor of major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE), particularly in cardiac surgery and sepsis [16–18]. Previous studies have suggested that VISmax OP values above 30 points are associated with increased perioperative morbidity and cardiovascular complications [19–20]. Secondary exploratory analysis evaluated the association between VISmax and postoperative cardiopulmonary complications.

The study aimed to evaluate whether the perioperative analgesic procedures (TEA and lidocaine) were associated with differences in VISmax OP and VISmax ICU.

Materials and methods

Study design and patient selection

This monocentric retrospective cohort study was conducted at the Nuremberg Hospital in Germany. All patients aged 18 or older who underwent open, hybrid (abdominal minimally invasive approach and thoracotomy), or robotic-assisted minimally invasive esophagectomy for cancer, either as an Ivor Lewis (i.e., with intrathoracic anastomosis) or as a McKeown (i.e., with cervical anastomosis) procedure, between January 2013 and December 2023 were considered eligible. Patients with neither TEA nor lidocaine, patients undergoing esophagectomy for benign disease or in an emergency setting, ASA IV patients, and patients with incomplete medical records were excluded.

A thorough chart review of all anesthesia, surgery, and ICU reports, as well as laboratory findings, was conducted. Data collection began on 9 February 2024 and ended on 9 June 2024.

We finalized the protocol and uploaded it into a registry to maintain transparency and ensure the study was conducted according to our planned methods. The registration for this study can be found on Zenodo under the registration DOI: https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.15480201 and on Open Science Framework (OSF) under the registration DOI: https://osf.io/j4ap5.

Ethics

This retrospective study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The Ethical Committee of Paracelsus Medical University, Nuremberg, Germany (protocol number: IRB-2023-21), provided ethical approval for this study on 9 February 2024, with Prof. Dr. Univ. Med. Markus Gosch, Chairman of the Institutional Review Board, serving as the approving authority. Informed consent was not required.

Variables and quantitative data

The primary endpoint was intraoperative VISmax (VISmax OP). Secondary endpoints were VISmax during ICU stay (VISmax ICU), the postoperative incidence of MACE, overall and 30-day mortality rates, pulmonary complications according to The National Surgical Quality Improvement Project (NSQIP) criteria, postoperative arrhythmias (atrial fibrillation), length of hospital and ICU stays (days), extubation time (minutes), intraoperative blood loss (milliliters), number of PRBCs transfused, surgery duration time (incision-to-closure time in minutes), and postoperative cardiac troponin T (cTnT) elevation.

Anesthesia procedure

Patients received general anesthesia (GA) either with TEA or lidocaine infusion. Thoracic epidural anesthesia was placed before the induction of GA according to the institutional standard operating procedure. TEA was placed between Th5 and Th9. After excluding spinal position, 10 mL Ropivacaine 0.2% and 0.2–0.3 µg/kg Sufentanil Epidural® were administered over 10 min. A continuous infusion of Ropivacaine 0.2% and epidural Sufentanil (0.5 µg/kg) was given at 5–8 mL/h until the sixth postoperative day. Anesthesia was induced with Sufentanil or Fentanil, Propofol, and Rocuronium or Cisatracurium as muscle relaxants. A double-lumen tube (35–39 Ch/French) was then inserted into the trachea for lung separation, and fibreoptic bronchoscopy was employed to verify the correct placement of the tube. Anesthesia was maintained with Propofol infusion (6–9 mg/kg/h) alongside Sevoflurane or Desflurane to reach MAC = 1, and EEG monitoring was conducted using Narcotrend® with a target value of 20–40. All patients received a three-lumen central venous catheter and an arterial line to the right side. Muscular relaxation was monitored using the train-of-four stimulation.

In accordance with our institution’s surgical standards, a 42 French Ryle tube was introduced orally. Mechanical ventilation was performed using lung-protective settings with tidal volumes between 5 and 7 mL/kg.

In cases of TEA refusal or contraindications to TEA, the patients received an intravenous lidocaine infusion perioperatively. A dose of 1.5 mg/kg was injected over 10 min, followed by a maintenance rate of 1.5 mg/kg/h throughout the surgery. The infusion ended automatically after 6 h. Intravenous lidocaine was used throughout the study period (2013–2023).

All patients who underwent esophagectomy between 2013 and 2023 received fluid therapy and hemodynamic support according to institutional standard operating procedures (SOPs), which follow a restrictive fluid management approach. Hemodynamic management aimed to maintain a mean arterial pressure of approximately 60–65 mmHg and a urine output of about 0.5–1.5 mL/kg/h. Balanced crystalloids, primarily Ringer’s acetate, were used as the preferred intraoperative fluid throughout the study period.

Data sources and measurement

The study analyzed a comprehensive clinical database of anesthesiologic and surgical electronic medical records using a universal unit of measurement for each variable.

The intraoperative and ICU VISmax were calculated by summing the doses of the following medications using the following formula:

VIS = dopamine (µg/kg/min) + dobutamine (µg/kg/min) + 100 × epinephrine (µg/kg/min) + 10 × milrinone (µg/kg/min) + 10,000 × vasopressin (U/kg/min) + 100 × norepinephrine (µg/kg/min) [16–18].

The VISmax OP was defined as the highest recorded VIS value during surgery. VISmax during ICU stay was defined as the highest VIS value recorded during postoperative ICU treatment.

Data on major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE), including postoperative cardiopulmonary arrest, angina pectoris, acute heart failure, cerebral stroke, and shock, were extracted according to the registered ICD-10 codes [21, 22].

Postoperative pulmonary complications, including reintubation and noninvasive ventilation for unexpected medical events like respiratory insufficiency caused by hypoxemia or hypercapnia, were also documented [23].

Mortality was assessed at 30 days. Postoperative arrhythmias (atrial fibrillation) were recorded using electrocardiography (EKG). CTnT elevation exceeding 0.04 ng/mL indicated postoperative cardiac ischemia [24].

Additionally, the duration of surgery, extubation time, transfusion requirements, and blood loss were recorded. The patients’ digital surgery records were utilized to record the surgical technique and the operating surgeon’s name. The anesthesiologist assigned the ASA classification for each patient. To protect their privacy, all identities and personal data were removed from the records. Data storage adhered to the European Union General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) 2019 and was encoded for future use.

Statistical analysis

Data consistency was verified. Before matching, continuous variables were analyzed using independent and dependent bootstrap t-tests based on 5,000 Monte Carlo simulations, with and without the assumption of homogeneity of variance. Cross-tabulation tables based on Monte Carlo simulations were analyzed using Pearson’s χ²-test, along with Fisher’s exact and marginal homogeneity tests.

A full matching based on propensity scores using sex, BMI, age, Lee RCI score, operative time, year of surgery, and procedure type (open, laparoscopic, hybrid, robotic) as matching criteria was done using the R package ‘matchit’ [25] based on propensity score matching via generalized linear models. All 441 patients included in the final analytic cohort had complete data for the variables used in the propensity-score model, the primary endpoint, and the analyzed secondary outcomes. Therefore, no observations were excluded from the matching or adjusted regression analyses because of missing data, and no data imputation was performed. The operating surgeon was not included as a matching criteria, because all esophagectomies were performed or directly supervised by the same senior esophageal surgeon.

Matching reporting followed published recommendations for propensity-score analyses in observational studies [26].

The primary endpoint (VISmax OP) and the secondary endpoint (VISmax ICU) were analyzed using generalized linear models based on the Gamma distribution, with additional multivariable covariate adjustment using the above matching criteria. Results present adjusted marginal means for both VISmax OP and VISmax ICU as well as all other secondary endpoints. To estimate the effects between both groups (TEA and Lidocaine), differences of VISmax OP and VISmax ICU with 95% CI were computed using the R packages ‘marginaleffects’ and ‘emmeans’ (version 1.11.2-8) [27, 28]. Secondary endpoints were analyzed in the same way as primary endpoints; however, binomial distributions were used instead of gamma distributions in the generalized linear models. Secondary endpoints were chosen to provide supportive evidence for the primary objective and to extend the understanding of the treatment effects.

Furthermore, logistic regression models were used to analyze the associations between increased ΔVISmax (VISmax ICU – VISmax OP), VISmax OP, and VISmax ICU with postoperative cardiovascular events, including arrhythmias, 30-day mortality, shock, and acute heart failure. To address model calibration, optimism correction based on 300 bootstrap repetitions was performed, and corresponding mean absolute errors (MAE) between observed and predicted probabilities were reported using the R package rms (version 8.1-1) [29]. A MAE < 2% corresponds to excellent calibration. The predicted risks are highly reliable for individual clinical decision-making, and an MAE of 2% to 5% corresponds to good to moderate calibration, which is highly acceptable for most clinical risk prediction models. To assess model discrimination, areas under the receiver operating characteristic curves (ROC AUC) were computed and reported.

All tests reported were two-sided, and p-values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant. In accordance with the ICH-E9 guidelines, secondary endpoints were not adjusted for multiple testing. All statistical analyses in this report were performed using NCSS (NCSS 2022, NCSS, LLC, Kaysville, UT) and STATISTICA (version 13, TIBCO Software Inc., 2015/2016). Results

Participants

In total, 477 adult patients who underwent esophagectomy were assessed for eligibility. Among these, 36 patients were excluded: 30 had neither TEA nor lidocaine, 4 had no records, 1 had esophageal metastatic malignant melanoma, and 1 underwent emergency esophagectomy. The final cohort consisted of 441 patients; 169 had intravenous lidocaine, and 272 had TEA (Fig. 1).

Baseline demographic characteristics and preoperative risk measures were comparable between the groups. No statistically significant differences were observed in age, body mass index, sex distribution, ASA Classification, or Lee Revised Cardiac Risk Index. The surgical time was significantly shorter in the lidocaine group (274 min ± 50) compared to TEA (288 min ± 59) (P = 0.005). A statistically significant difference in the distribution of year of surgery was found between the two groups (P < 0.001). Additionally, TEA was more commonly performed in open and hybrid esophagectomies, while lidocaine was infused more frequently in minimally invasive robotic surgeries (P = 0.0007).

The lidocaine group had a mean intraoperative blood loss of 311 milliliters ± 496 compared to 241.6 milliliters ± 326 in the TEA group (P = 0.108). Lidocaine patients required a mean of 2.4 ± 4.3 PRBC transfusion units, while the TEA group had 1.6 units ± 3.3 (P = 0.053), which was not statistically significant. Lidocaine patients were extubated in a mean time of 1,052 min ± 1645 vs. 1,018 min ± 2,739 in the TEA group (P = 0.826). They had a mean ICU stay of 11 days ± 12.6 days compared to 9.6 days ± 11 days in the TEA group (P = 0.12) and a general hospital stay of 23.5 days ± 15.9 days vs. 22.3 days ± 15.8 days in the TEA group (P = 0.446), with no statistical significance (Table 1).

Primary endpoint

The VISmax OP was significantly higher in the lidocaine group compared to the TEA group after using a generalized linear model and propensity score–based full matching. Operative time (p = 0.014), year of surgery (p < 0.0001), and type of surgery (p = 0.0015) differed significantly between the lidocaine and TEA groups, whereas BMI, age, sex, and Lee RCI score were not. The adjusted mean VISmax OP was 14.6 (95% CI, 13.0–16.3) in the lidocaine group and 12.5 (95% CI, 11.3–13.8) in the TEA group. The adjusted mean difference, calculated as lidocaine minus TEA, was 2.14 points (95% CI, 0.22–4.10; p = 0.029) (Fig. 2).

Secondary endpoint

After propensity-score full matching and multivariable adjustment, the adjusted mean VISmax during ICU treatment was higher in the lidocaine group than in the TEA group. The adjusted mean VISmax ICU was 37.1 (95% CI, 26.1–48.1) in the lidocaine group and 22.7 (95% CI, 17.1–28.3) in the TEA group, corresponding to an adjusted mean difference of 14.4 points (95% CI, 2.48–26.3; p = 0.019). The adjusted probability ratio of postoperative atrial fibrillation for TEA versus lidocaine was 0.55 (95% CI, 0.38–0.79; p = 0.001). Conversely, postoperative cardiac troponin T elevation was less frequent in the lidocaine group. The adjusted probabilities were 47.2% (95% CI, 38.4–56.0%) in the lidocaine group and 63.6% (95% CI, 56.8–70.4%) in the TEA group. The adjusted probability ratio for TEA versus lidocaine was 1.29 (95% CI, 1.01–1.64; p = 0.039).

No statistically significant adjusted between-group differences were observed for pulmonary complications, 30-day mortality, or cardiopulmonary arrest. The adjusted probabilities of 30-day mortality were 8.4% in the lidocaine group and 7.6% in the TEA group (adjusted probability ratio for TEA versus lidocaine, 0.91; 95% CI, 0.43–1.91; p = 0.803). Cardiopulmonary arrest also did not differ significantly between groups (adjusted probability ratio for TEA versus lidocaine, 0.39; 95% CI, 0.10–1.61; p = 0.194) (Table 2; Fig. 3).

Similarly, VISmax during ICU stay (VISmax ICU) showed positive strong correlations with extubation time (ρ = 0.37, p < 0.001), intraoperative blood loss (ρ = 0.21, p < 0.001), transfusion requirements (ρ = 0.45, p < 0.001), ICU length of stay (ρ = 0.54, p < 0.001), and overall hospital length of stay (ρ = 0.35, p < 0.001).

Discussion

This retrospective study compared TEA to intravenous lidocaine infusion for perioperative analgesia, using VISmax OP in patients undergoing transthoracic esophagectomy for cancer, with a focus on major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE). The primary finding was that patients who received lidocaine had a significantly higher VISmax OP compared to those who received intravenous TEA, after propensity-score full matching and multivariable covariate adjustment. The adjusted mean difference in VISmax OP was 2.14 points, whereas the adjusted difference in VISmax ICU was larger and more pronounced at 14.4 points.

However, VISmax is a surrogate marker of maximal inotropic treatment intensity rather than a direct measure of cardiovascular injury. Its value may be influenced by analgesia technique, intraoperative blood pressure targets, fluid management strategies, and clinical preferences. Therefore, a higher VISmax does not necessarily indicate worse cardiovascular function or explain postoperative morbidity. The above-mentioned primary and secondary outcome findings do not indicate the superiority of either analgesic strategy and should be interpreted in the context of an observational study design and not as a Minimal Clinically Important Difference (MCID).

Regarding the secondary outcomes, postoperative atrial fibrillation was more frequent in the lidocaine group, while postoperative cTnT elevation was less frequent. No statistically significant adjusted between-group differences were observed in pulmonary complications, 30-day mortality, or cardiopulmonary arrest, with only 10 events in the entire cohort. Because our study relies on a single, clearly defined primary endpoint to establish efficacy, the secondary endpoints serve to provide a complete, contextual picture of clinical safety and efficacy rather than independent claims.

Beyond the direct comparison between the two analgesic groups, logistic regression analysis revealed an increase of 14.4 VISmax ICU units, which corresponds to a 1.79-fold higher odds of postoperative atrial fibrillation, and demonstrated that higher VISmax was significantly associated with an increased incidence of postoperative cardiopulmonary complications (Fig. 4).

According to Asfar et al., in septic ICU patients, augmenting blood pressure with catecholamines did not influence mortality but increased the rate of atrial fibrillation [30]. To reduce catecholamine requirements and VISmax, strategies such as goal-directed fluid therapy [31], optimal perioperative nutrition [32], enhanced recovery protocols [33], Levosimendan as a non-adrenergic inotrope agent [34], and non-catecholamine vasopressors such as Vasopressin [35] could be considered if indicated.

Comparing lidocaine infusion with TEA has revealed a relevant aspect of perioperative hemodynamic management and supports the concept that perioperative vasopressor requirements may reflect the severity of underlying physiologic stress during major thoracoabdominal surgery such as esophagectomy.

It is well known that TEA reduces sympathetic tone, thereby decreasing the stress response to major upper abdominal surgeries. In this study, vasopressor requirements were lower in the TEA group. TEA may be associated with hypotension that is more profound than that observed with postoperative paravertebral block [36], although the present findings demonstrated lower vasoactive requirements with TEA than with lidocaine infusion (Fig. 2).

Conversely, the lower probability of postoperative cTnT elevation observed with lidocaine may be compatible with previously described anti-inflammatory effects of intravenous lidocaine. Kuo et al. noted that patients receiving lidocaine during major upper abdominal surgery exhibited reduced production of pro-inflammatory cytokines, which have been linked to an increased risk of myocardial injury [37]. Nevertheless, a previous meta-analysis by Kranke et al. found no overall superiority of lidocaine compared with other analgesic regimens in a general surgical population and therefore did not recommend its routine use [38].

The exploratory subgroup analysis suggested that patient-specific factors such as BMI, sex, and Age may influence perioperative outcomes. Underweight patients receiving lidocaine showed higher mean VISmax OP and VISmax ICU as well as higher rates of acute heart failure, postoperative cTnT elevation, and postoperative arrhythmias than other BMI groups. This could be explained by variations in lidocaine metabolism, as underweight patients tend to have a slower hepatic metabolism than those with normal body weights [39].

Female patients in the lidocaine group had a higher incidence of pulmonary complications and postoperative arrhythmias compared to male patients. This observation may be attributed to slower hepatic metabolism mediated by the enzymes CYP1A2 and CYP3A4 [40, 41]. Furthermore, it has been reported that female patients undergoing cardiac surgery often experience increased postoperative morbidity [42].

Elderly patients over 75 on either lidocaine or TEA had higher serum concentrations of cTnT than middle-aged adults. This suggests that age is a risk factor for myocardial infarction, as reported by Lowry MTH et al. [43]. Furthermore, higher cTnT concentrations, indicating myocardial injury, whether with or without a definitive diagnosis of myocardial infarction, are related to an increase in 30-day and 1-year mortality [44]. However, these observations should be interpreted with caution due to the small sample size and the limited number of events within the subgroups.

This study has several limitations. First, its retrospective design, which precludes causal inference despite adjustment for multiple covariates. Out of 477 patients initially eligible for inclusion, only 441 were ultimately included; the sample size was determined by the number of eligible patients who underwent esophagectomy during the study period and met the inclusion criteria. The sample sizes for certain subgroup analyses were small and may be insufficient to demonstrate statistical significance, especially given the wide 95% CI.

Second, residual confounding cannot be excluded despite careful matching and adjustment for BMI, age, sex, Lee RCI, operative time, surgical approach, and year of surgery using full matching and a generalized linear model. Important variables that might have influenced both the selection of the analgesic technique and perioperative vasoactive requirements were unavailable. These variables include baseline mean arterial pressure, arterial hypertension, chronic beta-blocker therapy, angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitor or angiotensin receptor blocker use, preoperative left ventricular ejection fraction, pulmonary hypertension, and neoadjuvant chemotherapy or radiotherapy. Confounding by indication is an important limitation because treatment allocation was not random. Further prospective and multicentric investigations might be conclusive to validate our findings externally. Because TEA was avoided in patients with contraindications or refusal, confounding by indication remains possible.

Third, temporal changes represent an additional limitation. Although year of surgery was included in the propensity-score matching and adjusted models, the 10-year span of data collection is pertinent since application of enhanced recovery protocols, increasing use of minimally invasive and robot-assisted surgery, changes in fluid and vasopressor management, modifications to anesthetic protocols, and accumulated experience of the surgical and anesthesiology teams could have occurred over this time, potentially impacting the outcome. Nevertheless, it is worth noting that the anesthesiologic and surgical teams remained largely consistent throughout this period; both thoracic epidural anesthesia (TEA) and intravenous lidocaine were used throughout the study period and the core principles of hemodynamic management remained relatively consistent; all esophagectomy procedures were performed or supervised by a single surgeon, which ensured high surgical consistency; both anesthesiological and surgical procedures adhered to institutional standard operating procedures (SOPs).

Fourth, data collection relied solely on the documentation provided by anesthesiologists, surgeons, and ICU personnel; however, inconsistencies or missing data could lead to information bias. Case-selection bias may be reflected in patient characteristics such as BMI, sex, comorbidity, and operative complexity. While conducting this study as a single-center investigation provided standardized anesthesiologic and surgical care, it may also have limited the generalizability of the results. However, the study was conducted at one German tertiary-care institution using local anesthetic, hemodynamic, surgical, and postoperative care protocols.

Conclusion

After propensity-score full matching and multivariable covariate adjustment, intravenous lidocaine was associated with higher VISmax OP and VISmax ICU than TEA in patients undergoing esophagectomy. Lidocaine was also associated with a significantly higher adjusted probability of postoperative atrial fibrillation but a lower probability of cTnT elevation, while pulmonary complications, 30-day mortality, and cardiopulmonary arrest did not differ significantly between groups. Higher VISmax values were associated with increased odds of postoperative atrial fibrillation. However, these findings should be interpreted cautiously and externally validated in future prospective studies on optimal perioperative analgesia strategies in esophagectomy.
