Management of cerebrospinal fluid leak after a minimally invasive lumbar decompression procedure: illustrative case
Authors: Mark A Pacult, S Harrison Farber, Luis M Tumialán, Mark E Oppenlander
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBACKGROUNDNeurogenic claudication caused by lumbar stenosis is a prevalent disorder of the spinal canal for which many treatment options exist. The mild (minimally invasive lumbar decompression) procedure has recently been introduced as a safe and effective alternative to medical management in the treatment of lumbar stenosis. However, data on complications are rarely reported, and the incidence of complications, when reported, is frequently assessed at 0% in the literature.OBSERVATIONSThe case of a patient with a cerebrospinal fluid leak following a mild procedure performed at an outpatient facility who presented to an inpatient emergency department is discussed. After lumbar drainage failed, the patient required 2 operative procedures to rectify the leak.LESSONSA seemingly innocuous procedure may have highly morbid complications requiring a lengthy inpatient stay and return trips to the operating room. The physicians and surgeons who manage these complications are frequently not those who performed the index procedure; they should be educated on the risks of the procedure and best practices for definitive management. https://thejns.org/doi/10.3171/CASE24497 Keywords: case report, cerebrospinal fluid leak, complications, lumbar stenosis, mild procedureABBREVIATIONS: CSF = cerebrospinal fluid, EVD = external ventricular drain, MRI = magnetic resonance imaging.

Symptomatic lumbar stenosis is a pathological state of narrowing of the spinal canal with resultant compression of the neural elements, which results in a clinical constellation of symptoms that can include neurogenic claudication and axial back pain. The most commonly accepted theory regarding its pathogenesis posits that degeneration of the intervertebral disc results in increased load-bearing and subsequent hypertrophy of posterior elements, such as the ligamentum flavum and articular facets, resulting in narrowing of the spinal canal and ischemia of the enclosed nerve roots with activity.1, 2

Evidence-based treatment guidelines for lumbar stenosis recommend conservative measures such as physical therapy, multimodal medications, and epidural corticosteroid injections. Surgical decompression is recommended for definitive management in cases that are refractory to nonoperative measures.3 The surgical procedure for lumbar decompression involves the removal of the lamina to access and resect the thickened ligamentum flavum, which is routinely performed by neurosurgeons and orthopedic spine surgeons.4 Because the degenerative processes responsible for lumbar stenosis frequently lead to mechanical instability and structural changes of the spinal column, treatment may also involve fixation and fusion, with the potential placement of intervertebral devices aimed at restoring disc height and achieving and maintaining optimal spinal alignment.5, 6

In recent years, a variety of minimally invasive techniques have been marketed as treatments for spinal stenosis.7–9 The mild (minimally invasive lumbar decompression) procedure refers to a proprietary intervention marketed by Vertos Medical that involves percutaneous port access to pass instruments to focally remove the lamina and ligamentum flavum.10 The mild procedure is performed by anesthesiologists or physical medicine and rehabilitation physicians who specialize in pain management procedures but are not surgeons trained in spinal surgery or the biomechanics of the spinal column. The viability of the mild procedure was established via a prospective, randomized clinical trial that compared the intervention to an epidural spinal injection and was sponsored by the device manufacturer.11

Although the initial viability study reported a low rate of adverse events (1.3%), data on specific complications following the procedure are sparse.11 Indeed, promotional materials tout an “extremely low” complication rate compared to a “moderate/high” rate for open surgery.12 We report the case of a symptomatic cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) leak following a mild procedure performed by an interventionalist at an outside facility, requiring transfer and extensive hospitalization. The patient underwent numerous neurosurgical interventions for definitive management. This case illustrates the potential risks of the mild procedure and the multidisciplinary care required to manage them.

Illustrative Case

A man in his late 60s presented to the emergency department with headache, dizziness, tinnitus, and serosanguineous drainage from a surgical site 1 day after undergoing the L4–5 mild procedure at an outpatient surgery center with a pain specialist. The patient’s past medical history was significant for prior strokes, deep venous thrombosis, atrial fibrillation requiring anticoagulation, congestive heart failure, and diabetes mellitus. The patient reported that he had sought care at his pain specialist’s office on the day of presentation and had been sent home with a dressing change after his drainage had reportedly slowed.

On presentation, physical examination revealed a small, left paramedian, port-sized incision in the lumbar spine with active extravasation of clear fluid. Computed tomography of the head and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the lumbar spine were performed; MRI revealed moderate stenosis at L4–5, severe stenosis at L5–S1, no fluid collection to suggest a CSF leak, and edema in the paraspinous tissues (Fig. 1).

Sagittal (A) and axial (B) noncontrast T2-weighted MRI sequences obtained in the patient upon presentation to the emergency department, showing persistent lumbar stenosis.

The patient was admitted to the intensive care unit, where his incision was oversewn in a figure-eight fashion. A lumbar drain was placed, and the patient was maintained with head-of-bed flat precautions for 72 hours. The patient was then allowed to mobilize with the lumbar drain present and draining for 48 hours, after which the lumbar drain was clamped. The patient’s wound began to drain after clamping the drain; thus, the lumbar drain was reopened for an additional 48 hours, during which time there was no wound drainage. Therefore, the drain was clamped a second time, and the wound began to drain clear fluid again. Due to persistent leakage after a second clamp trial, the patient was offered surgical exploration for CSF leak repair.

Nine days after having undergone initial lumbar drain placement, the patient underwent open surgical exploration of his wound. Opening and extension of the patient’s original incision revealed an epithelialized pocket filled with clear fluid with a tract through the subcutaneous tissue and muscle. With the assistance of plastic surgeons, the pocket was debrided, the tract was obliterated, and the fascia was closed in a watertight fashion utilizing interrupted braided sutures. A fat and superficial fascial flap was raised and oversewn, and the skin was closed with a running nylon stitch after a Jackson-Pratt drain had been left in the subcutaneous space. The patient’s lumbar drain was maintained for a 48-hour period postoperatively and discontinued after a 24-hour clamp trial was tolerated without drainage from the surgical incision.

Forty-eight hours after discontinuation of the lumbar drain and 24 hours after discontinuation of the Jackson-Pratt drain, the patient had limited serosanguineous drainage from the Jackson-Pratt drain exit site along with positional headache, and his drain exit site was oversewn. He then tolerated mobilization without drainage from his wound. One week later, while the patient was undergoing physical therapy in a downgraded hospital room, he experienced worsening positional headaches along with a palpable subcutaneous fluid collection. His headache was debilitating when upright and better when supine. The lumbar subcutaneous fluid collection created a wave with palpation. Repeat surgical exploration was thus offered to the patient in combination with postoperative CSF drainage via an external ventricular drain (EVD) due to the previous failure of lumbar drainage.

Twenty-four days after his initial mild procedure, the patient underwent repeat surgical exploration. As the previous surgical incision had healed, a new midline incision was created in combination with plastic surgery to afford better visualization of midline structures. CSF was encountered in both the subcutaneous and subfascial spaces, and the tissue was exposed down to the level of the L4 lamina, at which point microscopic investigation revealed leakage inferior to the right (contralateral) L4 lamina. We noted a lack of meaningful laminar or ligamentum removal from the mild procedure. A bilateral laminectomy at L4–5 was thus performed, revealing a right-sided dural defect. This defect was closed with a 6-0 suture (Gore-Tex) with an overlying autologous muscle patch. Valsalva maneuvers revealed no active CSF leakage, and fibrin glue was placed over the surgical site. The wound was irrigated and closed in conjunction with plastic surgeons, who performed a muscle flap closure (Fig. 2).

Intraoperative photographs demonstrating the dural defect with a CSF leak after a mild procedure (A), primary closure and autologous muscle plug (B), and application of fibrin glue over the primary repair site in the epidural space (C).

An EVD was placed in the intensive care unit on the same day postoperatively and maintained for 6 days while draining a minimum of 10 mL of CSF per hour. The potential for permanent CSF diversion via a ventriculoperitoneal or lumboperitoneal shunt was discussed with the patient and family; the patient wished to avoid this if possible. After tolerating a 24-hour clamp trial, the EVD was removed. The patient was discharged 24 hours after EVD removal and 31 days after the initial presentation.

At the last follow-up, 4 months after the index procedure, the patient’s incision had healed without further drainage. He was no longer experiencing positional headaches. He reported persistent radicular pain that had been present preoperatively.

Discussion

Observations

The prevalence of a CSF leak following traditional open laminectomy for degenerative spine conditions has been widely reported; in one systematic review spanning 10 years, the pooled incidence was reported as 8%.13 However, despite the growing body of literature describing the mild procedure as a treatment for lumbar stenosis, data on the procedure’s complication rates, specifically CSF leak, are sparse or nonexistent.

Furthermore, interventional techniques such as the mild procedure are not routinely included in interventional pain fellowship programs, and recent commentary has focused on the fact that many practitioners learn these skills from industry-led efforts, where the incentive is not necessarily aligned with patient safety and outcomes. The central issue is the capacity to manage complications, which is outside the realm of interventional pain management.14 One potential explanation for the extremely low complication rates reported in the mild literature is that patients with complications, notably CSF leakage, may be managed by physicians who did not perform the index procedure and who may have a different specialty. The burden of managing complications, which are potentially underreported and seldom discussed, may fall on neurosurgeons. The discussion and reporting of these complications become paramount.

In one recent randomized controlled trial examining the mild procedure compared to standard medical management (including epidural steroid injections) of lumbar stenosis, the authors followed 64 patients who had undergone the mild procedure over a period of 2 years and reported “no device- or procedure-related adverse events” in either treatment group, with 14 patients who had undergone the mild procedure experiencing “unrelated” adverse events, which were undefined in the study.15

In another study, the authors retrospectively examined 147 patients who had undergone the mild procedure at a single institution and reported a 0% complication rate at the 3-month follow-up.16 Another study documented an experience with 75 patients who had undergone the mild procedure with a 5-year follow-up and reported 0 of 75 (0%) patients with major complications and 13 of 75 (17%) patients with minor complications; minor complications included postprocedural pain in 11 patients and ecchymosis and allergic dermatitis in 1 patient each.17 Indeed, the only study to report any device-related complications of the procedure involved 143 patients treated with the mild procedure prospectively compared to 131 patients undergoing epidural steroid injection; 2 patients experienced complications following mild, with 1 patient experiencing “intraoperative oozing” and another reporting “postoperative pain possibly related to mild.”18

Remarkably, none of the trials concerning mild has reported any instance of CSF leak following the procedure, although the complication has been detailed in case reports. Tumialán and colleagues identified 10 patients who had presented to a single institution with complications following the mild procedure, 8 with refractory neurogenic claudication and 2 with CSF leaks, one of whom had injured nerve roots identified on corrective surgery (Video 1).19 Although decompression performed by neurosurgeons and orthopedic surgeons offers direct visualization and resection of the ligamentum flavum as well as correction of any induced complications such as a CSF leak, the mild procedure involves the injection of a contrast agent into the epidural space and uses that as a frame of reference to blindly pluck the ligamentum flavum with the intent of debulking the mass effect of the ligamentum flavum hypertrophy. The blind nature of this maneuver can lead to a catastrophic CSF leak, as seen in this case.

Our report illustrates the extensive management required to treat a potentially underreported and morbid complication following the mild procedure. Our experience reveals not only that such a complication is possible following this procedure but also that its management requires a multidisciplinary team that may involve neurosurgeons, critical care physicians, and plastic surgeons. Furthermore, the American Association of Neurological Surgeons and Congress of Neurological Surgeons Section on the Disorders of the Spine and Peripheral Nerves has issued a position statement expressing patient safety concerns for interventionalists performing procedures outside of their capacity to manage the complications.20 The requirement of a 31-day hospitalization and 3 corrective procedures to manage a condition that is routinely managed by spine surgeons in the outpatient setting underscores this concern.

Lessons

Complications following the mild procedure are rarely reported, according to both the peer-reviewed and industry-provided literature concerning its implementation and benefits. Herein, we detail the case of a CSF leak that required 2 multidisciplinary surgical explorations and a 31-day stay in the hospital in a patient who had presented to the emergency department 1 day after undergoing the mild procedure at an outpatient surgery center. This case highlights the need for comprehensive reporting on complications following this procedure to both educate the physicians responsible for managing them on best practices and provide an inclusive picture of the risks of this procedure.
