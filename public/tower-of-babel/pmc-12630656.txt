Design and characterization of anti-microbial novel herbal nanofiber scaffolds for the management of periodontal diseases
Authors: Pragati Dubey, Neelam Mittal, Brahmeshwar Mishra, S Manjit, Pooja Goswami, Biplob Koch, Anju Gautam, Pradeep Kumar, Kavindra Nath Tiwari
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractDesign and evaluation of PCL and gelatin loaded nanofiber containing herbal formulation named Ashvakatri in the treatment of chronic periodontitis. The electro-spinning method was used to create the composite nano-fibers containing herbal formulation, which were then evaluated for various parameters including in-vitro antimicrobial study and in-vitro drug release. An interconnected, continuous and bead free nanofiber with an average diameter of 150–250 nm was exhibited by the optimized nanofiber. The addition of gelatin and PCL to the optimized nanofiber improved its biocompatibility and bioadhesive characteristics. According to anti-microbial investigation and CLSM study indicated the remarkable inhibition of periodontal pathogens such as Aggregabacter actinomycetemcomitans (AI), Porphyromonas Gingivilis (PG), Fusobacterium spp (FB), and Porphyromonas intermedia (PI) respectively by optimized nanofiber and in-vitro drug release study also demonstrated the controlled release of composite nanofiber for over 220 h. Additionally, the MTT assay and in-vitro scratch assay indicated the composite NF had no cytotoxicity to the fibroblast cell line. The fabricated NF is a potential option for chronic periodontitis treatment since it expedited anti-bacterial, cytotoxic and sustained release medications.Keywords: Ashvakatri, Nanofiber scaffolds, Herbal formulation, Anti-bacterialSubject terms: Drug discovery, Health care

Introduction

India has traditionally employed herbal remedies in established alternative medical systems like Ayurveda, Naturopathy, Homeopathy, Siddha, and Unani. There are currently around 2500 plant species used as herbal medicines in India. Herbal remedies have been utilized for over 3000 years as traditional medicines or indirectly in modern medication manufacturing1. Approximately 80% of people still get their primary medical care from medicinal plants. Given that India is known for its herbal plants, any particular information about these plants may be significant for therapeutic purposes2. The use of plants in ethnomedicine is expanding globally, and eco-friendly and bio-friendly plant-based commodities have lately been considered for the prevention and treatment of many ailments, including microbial disorders. Therefore, one may find novel, pocket-friendly, and more effective medications by using knowledge of traditional botanicals.

Conventional periodontal treatment involves systemic antibiotic delivery and mechanical cleaning, which is not promising. Scaling and root planning, as well as surgical intervention, are examples of potential therapeutic strategies. Systemic antibiotics and mouthwashes containing chlorohexidine are also recommended. Deep pockets are frequently difficult to debride with scaling and root planing, and recovery following surgical intervention is time-consuming1,3.

Over the past ten to fifteen years, there has been much focus on the local intra-pocket drug delivery system. Several articles addressed local drug delivery systems like carbon mediated responsive nanomaterials applied in probe, tumour therapy4, smart textile with dual characteristic features like sensing pH and tempreture5, specific nanogel for food application6, bacterial Cellulose/Gelatin Composite Scaffolds for culturing of hepatocyte7 have been developed. It has been shown that local medication delivery devices, used alone or in conjugation with other dental treatments, can provide more effective treatment than systemic antimicrobial drug administration4,5. Thus, a possible method of treating periodontitis is to use local delivery systems that include antibiotics.

To tackle more serious ailments, it is crucial to combine Indian Ayurvedic remedies with innovative drug delivery systems. Herbal treatments were long disregarded for developing innovative formulations due to a lack of scientific support and challenges with processing, including the extraction, identification, and standardization of individual medicinal components in intricate polyherbal systems. Modern phytopharmaceutical research, however, can address the scientific requirements for herbal medicines to be included in novel drug delivery systems, such as liposomes, solid dispersions, nanoparticles, microemulsions, matrix systems, solid lipid nanoparticles, and so forth6,7. These requirements include pharmacokinetics, mechanism of action, site of action, and precise dosage.

The HR-MS studies reveals that 29 phyocompounds like Protocatechuic acid, L-(+)-Citrulline, Pipecolic acid, Gericudranin C, Silydianin, Kojic acid, Coumarin, Myrmicacin, Ambrosic acid, Lupinisoflavone D.

A range of PCL and Gelatin proportions were utilized in the fabrication of composite nanofibers. The gelatin content varied from 1 to 5% w/v while the total polymer concentration was maintained in the solvent of trifluoroethanol 10% v/v. The nanofibers fabricated with 15% PCL showed uneven nodes and the development of beads in the nanofiber matrix (Fig. 1A). The “Tylor cone” became stable and the spinnability was much enhanced in the PCL-GLA (14% w/v Polycaprolactone and 1% w/v Gelatin) formulation. Even with a further increase in gelatin concentration to 12% w/v PCL and 3% w/v gelatin (PCL-GC) showed continuous nanofiber with coarse nanofiber texture but fewer beads and interconnected nodes were observed in Fig. 1B. Additionally, an increase in gelatin concentration to 11% PCL and 4% w/v G resulted in continuous nanofiber with good mechanical strength and smooth texture but the only problem was the low yield obtained. Likewise, a further increase in gelatin concentration of 10% PCL and 5% G produced nanofiber with a smooth texture and higher comparable yield post-electrospinning. As a result, it was discovered that the concentration of G directly affected the shape, hydrophobicity, diameter, and uniformity of the nanofiber scaffold.

Morphological characterization of nanofiber scaffolds

Scanning electron microscopy was used to evaluate the morphology of PCL-GE (Fig. 1C) and PCL-GE-A2 (Fig. 1D) nanofiber scaffolds. The diameter of all nanofibers was evaluated by using Image J software. The investigation revealed that the herbal formulation in PCL-GE nanofiber was uniformly distributed with an average diameter of less than 150 nm and a median diameter of 87.35 nm. Further, the PCL nanofiber diameter was increased from 64.67 to 110.22 for PCL-GE (Fig. 1E) and 128.7 nm for PCL-GE-A2 (Fig. 1F).

The Atomic force microscopy also suggested the 2D and 3D structure morphology of nanofibers similar to the SEM result obtained. AFM was utilized to investigate the roughness and topology of nanofiber with average roughness and root square mean values of 0.872 ± 0.28 μm and 0.549 ± 0.12 μm respectively. The PCL-GE and PCL-GE-A2 nanofiber scaffold represented a smoother morphology as compared to PCL nanofiber, which showed higher as evidenced value of roughness and root mean square values (1.956 ± 0.49 μm). The AFM micrograph in 2-D and 3-D structures (Fig. 2) of PCL-GE and PCL-GE-A2 resulted in smooth, equally dispersed, randomly coupled, arbitrarily interconnected nanofibers with rare or no bead defects.

Solid-state characterization

Differential scanning calorimetry is primarily used to investigate the melting point (enthalpy difference) and crystallinity of different samples concerning the temperature over time. Figure 3A shows the DSC thermogram for different formulations such as polymers PCL and G, Blank nanofiber, drug extract, and drug-loaded nanofiber. The drug extract showed an endothermic peak at 88.6 ˚C, which correspondence to the drug melting point, and a larger peak at 92.21 ˚C, which correspondence to the loss of water molecules from the hydrate. The amorphous form of gelatin resulted in a broad peak at 200–290 ˚C in DSC spectra, whereas the PCL thermogram had an endothermic peak at 62.04 ˚C. The polymeric mixture may resemble the peak obtained as PCL and G suggested the endothermic. Hence, the Drug nanofiber thermogram demonstrated the proper distribution of the medication Ashvakatri in composite nanofiber.

2-D and 3-D AFM structure of (A) PCL-GE, (B) PCL-GE-A2.

Figure 3B illustrated thermogram of polymers PCL and G, Blank nanofiber, drug extract, and drug-loaded nanofiber. The Gelatin undergoes one thermal breakdown stage at 200–250 ˚C and a second thermal breakdown undergoes at 350–400 ˚C, resulting in 10% and 88% weight loss respectively. Likewise, the PCL under heat breakdown at 280–340˚C resulted in a weight loss of 86%. The findings are similar to the recent studies on the heat breakdown of PCL and Gelatin. The polymeric mixture was thermally degraded similar to individual nanofiber at 250–350 ˚ C, losing 96.4% of its weight. Furthermore, in comparison with pure PCL and gelatin, polymeric mixture nanofiber exhibits worse thermal stability. This is because nanofibers have a larger surface area and are more reactive than pure-form polymers, allowing heat breakdown to occur more quickly. Also, the thermal breakdown of extract occurred at 280–330 ˚C, and for drug-loaded nanofiber was occurred at 340–380 ˚C.

Surface characterization of nanofiber scaffolds

Drug, polymers, and nanofibrous formulations were all characterized to access the drug-polymer interaction, measure changes in crystallinity, and verify herbal formulation encapsulation into nanofiber.

Figure 3C determines the FTIR spectra for herbal drug extract, polymers (PCL and G), blank nanofiber (mixture of PCL-G nanofiber), and Drug-loaded nanofiber. The IR bands of the extract fraction of Asvakatri were observed at 2917 cm-1 (C-N stretching), 2360 cm-1 (asymmetric stretching of C = C), 2125 cm-1 (C = O stretching in COOH), 1722 cm-1 (symmetric C-C stretching), 1626 cm-1 (C-O for OH stretching), 1447 cm-1 for (C-H alkane stretching), and 815 cm-1 for (asymmetric C-H akene) respectively. Also, PCL showed similar peaks as per the literature at 2949 cm-1 (asymmetric C-H stretching), 2868 cm−1 (symmetric C-H stretching), 1726 cm-1 (C = O stretching), and 1239 cm-1 (C-O-C stretching). The peak values indicated in PCL nanofiber were similar to the reported peaks as per the previously reported literature. Similarly, the gelatin nanofiber absorption band was observed at 1658 cm-1 (C-N stretching), 1532 cm-1 (N-H stretching), and 1328 cm-1 (C-O stretching). For gelatin, the absorption band at 1658 and 1532 indicated the amide I and amide II bands respectively. The FTIR spectra of drug-loaded nanofiber showed characteristic peaks of polymer and drug extract. Good compatibility was demonstrated by the lack of any additional bands or peaks and the absence of any typical peak shifting, however, the intensity of the extracted peak was reduced indicating that the drug had been successfully loaded into the nanofibrous scaffolds.

The purpose of the XRD investigation was to determine if the drug was polymorphic after it was encapsulated in composite nanofiber scaffolds. Figure 3D displays the XRD pattern of PCL, GEL, blank nanofiber, the polymeric mixture of PCL-G, and drug-loaded nanofiber. Ashvakatri isolated fraction drug showed peaks at 23.17˚, 26.62˚, 31.03˚, and 34.43˚. The semi-crystalline structure of Ashvakatri was shown by the XRD pattern. Similarly, the PCL crystal sharp peak was shown at a 2Ɵ value of 22.49˚, and 24.81˚. The band observed, resembled the peak values as per the literature. Gelatin was amorphous in form and lacked the typical sharp peak. The blank nanofiber spectra resembled those of PCL and gelatin peaks. Likewise, the drug nanofiber suggested the presence of two polymers and the drug itself through an XRD pattern. However, the low-intensity peak of the drug and two polymer mixture peaks in drug nanofiber spectra indicated that the drug is encapsulated in composite nanofibrous scaffolds.

In-vitro drug release study

The drug release was conducted with a pH close to 6.9 in an artificial saliva model and the percentage herbal formulation release vs. time profile was obtained by examination of the drug release mechanism and cumulative percentage drug release (CPDR) was plotted in two different graphs. The release profile showed a biphasic drug release mechanism. The drug-loaded nanofiber showed an initial burst release in the first two hours as shown in Fig. 4B. Thereafter, the drug was released in a sustained manner for up to 220 h. Almost 50% of the drug from the nanofiber was released within 24 h followed by gradual release over 200 h. Drug-loaded nanofiber (PCL-GE-A2) released 46.27 ± 2.38% of the drug in the first 24 h and almost 80% (75.28 ± 1.39%) of the drug was released in a controlled manner within 150 h. The more gradual release of PCL-GE-A2 formulation had been showing up to 200 h due to its higher hydrophobicity as shown in Fig. 4C. As a result, this increased or enhanced hydrophobicity in the formulation may have decreased the degree of swelling, and therefore, the result confirmed a controlled release profile of PCL-GE-A2 nanofiber scaffold was obtained.

Water contact angle

The water contact angles of PCL NF, GE NF, PCL-GE NF, and PCL-GE-A2 NF are demonstrated in Fig. 4D–G. The water contact angle for only PCL and only G NF was found to be 103 ± 1.5˚ and 40 ± 1.0˚ respectively. Additionally, the PCL NF had the highest contact angle which was decreased after the incorporation of G in PCL-GE NF as indicated in Fig. 4H. Furthermore, the PCL-GE-A2 NF showed a slight increase in the water contact angle of about 89 ± 1.0˚.

Drug release kinetic

Table 3 displays the parameters for the various kinetic models used to fit the release data. The Higuchi model provided the greatest match for drug release form for PCL-GE-A2 formulation. The results indicated that diffusion was the primary factor influencing the release mechanism. In other words, the high hydrophobicity of the PCL-GE-A2 nanofiber release profile resulted in a two-step (bi-phasic) release mechanism which therefore, follows the Fickian diffusion rather than the degradation process.

Plate counting assay (CFU)

The anti-bacterial efficacy of PCL-GE-A2 was also determined by plate count assay, which revealed a significant decrease in the number of bacterial colonies of periodontal pathogens as shown in Fig. 7A, indicating the anti-bacterial assessment. For AA, the bacterial colony-forming number in the control group was 120 × 10−7 CFU/ml, and in PCL-GE-A2 NF the CFU was found to be 44 × 10−7 CFU/ml after 24 h of incubation, followed by 16 × 10−7 CFU/ml after 48 h respectively. Likewise, for the PG control group, the CFU was 232 × 10−7 CFU/ml and 36 × 10−7 CFU/ml in 24 h of plate which was reduced to 13 × 10−7 CFU/ml in 48 h. Additionally, the plate-forming bacteria for FB was 186 × 10−7 CFU/ml in the control group, and 23 × 10−7 CFU/ml in the nanofiber injected plate after 24 h moreover decreased to 11 × 10−7 CFU/ml after 48 h. Also, after 24 h, the PI plate bacterial count was 284 × 10−7 CFU/ml and 47 × 10−7 CFU/ml for PCL-GE-A2 loaded plate, but after 48 h it was reduced to 16 × 10−7 CFU/ml bacterial count. Therefore, the result revealed that the presence of the PCL-GE-A2 NF (experimental group) hindered the growth of bacterial colonies as compared to the control group as shown in Fig. 7B–C.

Bacterial viability test

The live-dead assay was used to determine the viability of the bacteria. Figure 8 represents the percentage of viable cells of periodontal pathogens after treatment with PCL-GE-A2 NF at 0 h, 6 h, and 12 h respectively. Initially after treatment at 0 h, the % of viable cells of AA was 68.71 ± 1.24%. Nevertheless, after 6 and 12 h of treatment, these percentages significantly reduced to 13.56 ± 0.10 and 2.18 ± 0.11% respectively. Likewise, viable cells of PG were 88.93 ± 2.493% at the beginning of 0 h but were found to be 19.25 ± 0.24% and 0.74 ± 0.02% respectively after 6 and 12 h of treatment. Also, at the beginning of 0 h, the visible cells for FB were 76.20 ± 2.85%; however, after 6 and 12 h of treatment, they were reduced to 20.18 ± 1.56% and 3.72 ± 0.91% respectively. Consequently, the viable cell count for PI was 49.56 ± 1.68% at the beginning of 0 h, but it was determined to be 27.39 ± 1.20% and 7.66 ± 0.38% after 6 and 12 h respectively. Image J software was used to quantify the number the living and dead cells in each Confocal Laser Scanning Microscopy (CLSM) image, as shown in Fig. 8A–D. The results represented that increasing the concentration of PCL-GE-A2 NF, the decrease in a significant number of living cells that have been green in colour while increment of the red colour cell occurred. Red colour denoted that dead bacterial cell due the effects of the PCL-GE-A2 NF doses that was indicating a strong antibacterial synergy. The results of acridine orange-ethidium bromide dual staining showed distinct fluorescence patterns as demonstrated in Fig. 9B. In vitro scratch assay

The in vitro scratch assay is a simple and cost-effective method employed in laboratories to investigate cell migration Fig. 9C. This assay allows for controlled analysis of the mechanisms involved in cell movement and tissue repair. The wound healing assay results indicated that nanofibers enhanced the migration ability of treated fibroblast cells compared to control cells. After 24 h of treatment, L929 cells showed minimal migration, but treatment for 48 and 72 h resulted in significant wound closure, evident from images showing in Fig. 9D increased wound width reduction. The results indicated that treating L929 cells with 300 µg of nanofibers significantly enhanced their migration compared to untreated cells and blank nanofibers. This was observed using phase-contrast microscopy at 100X magnification. This enhancement was reflected in the accelerated closure of wounds, driven by the movement of cells from the inner endothelial lining towards the wound site.

Conclusion

Periodontal disease contains various pathogenic microorganisms that, in vulnerable patients, cause an inflammatory immune response that is not functioning properly and leads to the destruction of supporting tissue7,8. The current work goal is fabricating an herbal formulation loaded with composite nanofiber scaffolds to treat periodontitis. By using the electrospinning method, a PCL-GE-based composite nanofiber scaffold co-loaded with Ashvakatri an herbal formulation was developed. PCL nanofiber offers excellent mechanical strength, poor aqueous solubility, and no bio-adhesiveness due to its hydrophobic characteristics. Also, gelatin was added to produce sufficient mechanical strength, optimal hydrophobicity, and high bio-adhesiveness followed by prolonged drug release action. The best yield value and bead-free linked nanofibers were found in the optimized PCL-GE-A2 nanofiber scaffolds. The absence of incompatibility was verified by FTIR, DSC, TGA, and XRD which also showed the effectiveness of encapsulated nanofiber. Additionally, the contact angle was used to determine the wettability of composite NF by adding the gelatin in PCL, reducing the average water contact angle from 103˚ to 89˚ for PCL-GE-A2 NF. Moreover, the effectiveness of herbal formulation was examined across the batches of nanofibers. Therefore, the outcomes revealed that the percentage of gelatin is directly proportional to the percentage of entrapment efficiency. Also, the result concluded for all composite NFs illustrated by minimal variation with a standard deviation of ˂ 5% which suggested a uniform distribution of herbal formulation in tan he nanofiber matrix. Furthermore, the release kinetic model concluded that the drug release mechanism for the PCL-GE-A2 NF scaffolds followed the Higuchi model since the correlation coefficient was higher than in the other models, suggesting Fickian diffusion regulated drug release. Consequently, the CLSM analysis concluded a decrease in the significant number of living cells corresponded to an increase in the number of dead cells, indicating a strong antibacterial synergy. Also, the MTT assay and in-vitro scratch assay concluded the composite NF had no cytotoxicity to the fibroblast cell line. The antibacterial activity also demonstrated that the PCL-GE-A2 nanofiber scaffold showed effective inhibition against isolated periodontal pathogens such as AA, PG, FB, and PI for ˃ 96 h. Therefore, PCL-GE-A2 NF scaffolds possessed good anti-bacterial activity indicating the great potential in the treatment of periodontitis. Therefore, based on the results of our investigation, it is possible to conclude that the developed PCL-GE-A2 hybrid NF scaffold, has a promising performance for treating periodontal infections by extending the duration of periodontal residence, which improves therapeutic efficacy. Furthermore, it facilitates intimate contact between dosage and periodontal pocket, which may lead to a high concentration of drugs in the local area. Utilizing herbal nanofiber scaffolds in conjunction with other therapeutic approaches, such as growth factors and stem cells, is anticipated to boost the healing capacities of these scaffolds. Especially in situations involving intricate tissue regeneration, these combinations have the potential to overcome some of the limits that nanofiber scaffolds alone have encountered. Creating treatments for regeneration and repair of tissues that are more functional, effective, and therapeutically feasible is the future of this technology.

Methods

Plant material collection and extract preparation

The fresh rhizome of Ashvakatri was collected from Yercaud, Tamil Nadu, India, in June 2022 and authenticated by institutional coordinator Dr. Jasmeet Singh. Also, specimen voucher (DG/22–23/563) was deposited in the Department of Dravyaguna, Institute of Medical Sciences, Banaras Hindu University. Afterward, the rhizome was cut, shade-dried, and powdered by an electrical mixer grinder, followed by storage in an airtight container until further studies.

Extraction of the drug and its high-resolution mass spectrometry (HR-MS) studies

The powder (2 kg) of Ashvakatri dried rhizome was extracted with 3 L of ethanol: water (6:4) by using a maceration process for 72 hours8. After this, the mixture was filtered and evaporated using a water bath to afford a brownish sticky mass (256.78 gm) and then freeze-dried using a lyophilizer. Fabrication of nanofiber scaffold

Selection of polymeric materials

Many trials were taken to develop the compatibility of the polymer with our extract for the electrospinning process. The polymers were selected based on literature reviews. After examining considerable literature, we decided on Chitosan as our initial selected polymer but failed. Afterward, we briefly experimented with PEG but also struggled to establish a suitable electrospinning process to produce results with desired material properties. Many attempts were made to develop a suitable process and finally, we shifted our focus to the electrospinning PCL (poly-caprolactone)- gelatin hybrid process9. In this research, we combined PCL with gelatin to improve the final composition’s physical, chemical, and exploitative properties and control the drug release rate. To find the optical parameters for our specific electrospinning setup, we conducted multiple trials with varied PCL-G-Ashvakatri loaded concentrations, solvents, and electrospinning settings. We demonstrated our ability to construct a homogeneous, repeatable scaffold with the appropriate attributes.

Preparation of PCL-G and PCL-G-Ashvakatri polymeric solutions

Poly (e-caprolactone) (PCL) is a biocompatible, biodegradable, semi-crystalline, and hydrophobic polymer that might be employed as a drug delivery method with an extended effect due to its slow disintegration property. It is classified based on its molecular weight (MW). Gelatin (G) is a remarkable biopolymer that is easy to use, economical, and biocompatible10,11. It also has a wide surface area and increased porosity and biodegradability.

The polymeric solutions were prepared by mixing two polymers such as polycaprolactone and gelatin in a solvent trifluoroethanol (10 ml). Then after, the polymeric solution was Electrospun at a flow rate of 0.2–0.6 ml/hr., voltage of 1.5 KV/cm, temperature of 22 ˚C, and relative humidity of 60%. Firstly, three distinct formulations of polymeric solution were developed for the electrospinning process: PCL alone (15%), Gelatin alone (15%), and hybrid nanofiber comprised of PCL-G blend with varied Gelatin concentrations ranging from 1 to 5% w/v.

Furthermore, after the selection of a suitable PCL-G polymeric solution, the addition of the herbal formulation Ashvakatri (5–10%) was done in the same manner.

Electrospinning process

The electoral spinning process was followed exactly as reported by Manjit et al. In brief, PCL-G and PCL-G-laden Ashvakatri extract solutions were loaded individually into a 10 ml plastic syringe equipped with a 21G needle. The Electrospun nanofibers were created using commercially available apparatus (KD Scientific) with 18 kV voltage. The needle tip was 12 centimeters away from the aluminum foil-wrapped collector plate as described in Fig. 10. The screw and washer were used in the plate collar to secure the charge distribution plate to the positive lid from the power source12,13.

Characterization of nanofiber

Morphological characterization of nanofiber

The morphological characterization of nanofiber was examined by using high-resolution scanning electron microscopy (HR-SEM) and atomic force microscopy (AFM) or scanning probe microscopy (SPM). For surface morphology, the nanofiber mat was cut into pieces and fixed over a glass slide. Further, the glass slide was coated with a thin layer of gold (Au) using a sputter coater (Q150 RES QUORUM, Germany). Finally, the sample pieces were deposited on a sample holder for HR-SEM (FEI Nova Nano SEM 450) and examined at different magnifications. The software called Image J was used to analyze images of nanofibers at various magnifications to determine their diameter.

Likewise, atomic force imaging was used to determine nanofiber’s 3-D surface morphology and grain distribution using an atomic force microscope or scanning probe microscopy (NTEGRA Prima NT-MDT, Moscow, Russia)14.

Solid state characterization of nanofiber

Drug entrapment in nanofibrous scaffolds and potential drug-polymer interaction were investigated using solid-state characterization. To do this investigation differential scanning calorimetry (DSC), X-ray diffraction (XRD), Thermogravimetry analysis (TGA), and Fourier Transform Infrared Analysis (FTIR) of extract, gelatin, PCL, blank polymer nanofiber, and drug-loaded nanofiber were carried out15,16.

Using 64 scans at ambient temperature, the FTIR spectra on SHIMADZU 8400 S, Tokyo, Japan were recorded between 4000 cm−1 and 400 cm-1. The samples were compressed into pellets using a hydraulic press after being triturated with KBr (potassium bromide). However, for nanofiber, samples were analyzed by spreading on a KBr pellet.

The X-ray diffractometer (Rigaku MiniflexTM 600) was equipped with a D/tex Ultra detector used to study the change in physical state and crystallinity of samples. A scan rate of 2˚ min-1 was used to record the diffraction pattern throughout a 2Ɵ range of 5˚ to 65˚. The DSC and TGA studies were used to analyze the stability of samples such as extract, gelatin, and PCL, a physical combination of both polymer (blank nanofiber) and drug-loaded nanofiber. The DSC and TGA plots were recorded by Shimadzu DSC-60 Plus of the same samples between a temperature range of 25˚C to 400˚C at a heating range of 10˚C/min and nitrogen purging at 40 ml/min17,18.

To verify the drug or isolated extract loading within the nanofiber matrix, the entrapment effectiveness of the nanofibrous membrane was conducted from seven samples of 2 mg of nanofiber such as PCL, GEL, PCL-GA-A2, PCL-GB-A2, PCL-GC-A2, PCL-GD-A2, and PCL-GE-A2 were cut from different regions and dissolving into 1 ml Trifluoroethanol. Water contact angle

The water contact angle of nanofibrous scaffolds was determined using the sessile drop method with a static contact angle measuring device (KRUSS GmbH, Germany, DSA 10). For this, a drop of water (about 4 µl) was placed on the nanofiber’s surface, and the water droplet’s form change over time was observed. The surface contact angle between the water droplet and the nanofiber was observed. Each nanofiber was examined at six locations for consistent results21,22.

In-vitro drug release

Based on the United States Pharmacopoeia (USP), an in-vitro Ashvakatri-loaded nanofiber release profile was conducted in a tank containing artificial saliva at 37 ˚C and 100 rpm. The release studies were thermally controlled and done for 75 min at pH 6.9. The nanofiber was cut into 1 cm2 area and weighed accurately. Then the nanofiber was placed in the bottom of a beaker with 5 ml of artificial saliva in it. At pre-determined time points, one ml of solution was taken out of the dissolving tank for UV-visible spectrophotometry at 295 nm of λ max. It was also replaced with 1 ml of synthetic saliva to maintain the tank condition. The investigation was carried out in triplicate for phenolic compounds, and the release value was plotted against predefined time intervals of 0 to 220 h22.

Patient sampling for isolation of periodontal pathogens

Freshly prepared RC medium in McCartney bottles was used for sampling the GCF from the periodontal pocket of 5 patients from the OPD of the Faculty of Dental Sciences, Institute of Medical Sciences, Banaras Hindu University, Varanasi, Uttar Pradesh. All the experimental procedures were approved by ethics committee of Institute of Medical sciences, Banaras Hindu University (Approval number Dean/2024/FC/6970). And Written informed consent was also obtained from all participants before collection of GCF. The GCF samples were collected with the help of sterile absorbent paper points from the subgingival from the base of the periodontal pocket site, as shown in Fig. 6A. The McCartney sampled bottles were incubated for seven to fifteen days for further investigation. The changes in the RCM media were examined daily.

Isolation of bacterial colonies

A subculture of aerobic bacteria was made on TSA media and McConkey agar media from the top of RCM media and incubated for 24 h at 37˚C. For anaerobic isolation, the sub-culturing was done from near meat particles of RCM media on 5% sheep blood agar media and brain heart infusion agar media enriched with haemin (1 µg/ml) and vitamin K (0.1 µg/ml). An anaerobic jar was used to cultivate anaerobes with the combination of 90% hydrogen and 10% CO2, and incubated at 37 ˚C for 72 h. Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Pseudomonas melalinogenica (biological indicators), and methylene blue (chemical indicator) were used to control the anaerobiosis inside the jar. Additionally, isolation physical and biochemical characterization was done to identify the isolate obtained23,24.

Plate counting assay (CFU)

This method was carried out to determine the survival rate of PCL-GE-A2 nanofiber scaffolds against isolated periodontal pathogens. The small piece of nanofiber mat was dissolved in 1% trifluoroethanol.

Media preparation: First, TSA medium was prepared into the bottle and boiled in the water bath for 5 min. Then, it was autoclaved at 121 ˚C for 15 min. Furthermore, it was cooled at 48 ˚C using a water bath.

Bacterial suspension: Prepared 10−7 times fold serial dilution of all individual bacterial samples (0.1 ml from 6th folds dilution in 10 ml distilled water). The prepared individual bacterial suspension was separated into two groups: Control (untreated bacteria) and PCL-GE-A2 NF (dissolved mat), then cultured at 25˚C for 24 h.

Pour plate technique for plate counting: In each labeled sterile petri plate, 1 ml of control suspension and 1 ml of PCL-GE-A2-NF cultured suspension were added. Afterward, each plate was poured with media and incubated at 25˚C for 48 hours25.

Minimum Inhibitory concentration and Minimum Bactericidal concentration determination

A previously reported methodology was used to calculate the MIC and MBC. 80 µL of samples (serially diluted with Tryptic Soy Broth medium) were added to a 96-well plate25. Afterward, it was inoculated with 120 µL of bacterial cultures (1 × 105 CFUs/ml) individually and incubated for a full night at 37 ˚C. The MIC was determined by the highest dilution and did not exhibit any bacterial growth. The MBC was determined by placing 100 µL of culture broth from wells onto a TSA plate and incubating it overnight at 37 ˚C and, the highest dilution that yielded inhibition in bacterial cell number was reported as MBC. Doxycycline was used as a standard drug for comparison or positive control. The sample % was used to express the MIC and MBC26.

Bacterial susceptibility study via ditch plate method

The anti-bacterial activity of Asvakatri-laden PCL-G nanofiber scaffolds was evaluated using the Tryptic Soy Agar (TSA) plate technique against isolated periodontal pathogens by CLSI guidelines 2022 and with minor modifications. With the use of a cotton swab, 0.5 McFarland matched suspensions of isolated Periodontal Pathogen (every 1.5 × 108 cells/ml) were prepared in normal saline and dispersed over the cation-adjusted Tryptic Soy agar plate. A 5 mm standard-size punch was used to drill each petri plate to make wells of uniform 2 mm depth.

Afterward, 4 mg of freshly cut nanofibers scaffold (blank, drug-loaded, and standard) were transferred into a cultured plate followed by incubation for 24 h at 37 ˚C in a CO2 incubator. The zone of inhibition was evaluated after 24 24-hour incubation period and subsequently, nanofiber mats were transferred to freshly inoculated petri plates and incubated again to determine the continued antimicrobial activity of the same nanofibers, this was repeated for 3–4 days with 24-hour incubation in a CO2 incubator. Finally, the zone of inhibition was measured for each plate by using a Vernier caliper and images were taken with a high-resolution camera27,28.

Bacterial viability study

The microbial viability test was examined using confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM). Using the LIVE-DEAD™ Bac-light™ bacterial viability kit (by Thermofisher Scientific), the goal is to determine the percentage of viable and non-viable bacterial cells. As per the instructions provided in the user manual of the Bac-light microbial viability kit consisting of two separate solutions of SYTO 9 (3.3 mM) and propidium iodide (20 mM) dyes in 300 µL of DMSO. The stain mixtures were applied in three different ways:

Directly to biofilm-covered surfaces, where it was pipetted out on the surface after being diluted with Phosphate buffer solution (PBS) and covered with a coverslip;

Applied to microbial cells removed from the surface using ultrasonography

Applied to the microbes extracted from the top layer of the microbial sample.

The samples that were stained were incubated for 15 min, at ambient temperature under dark conditions and then after the images were captured by using CLSM21,23.

In-vitro cytocompatibility study

Product sources

The L929 mouse fibroblast cell lines were procured from the National Centre for Cell Science in Pune, India. Phosphate Buffer Saline (PBS) was made using premium analytical chemicals, and the laboratory supplies were supplied by Gibco, Eppendorf, Genetics Biotech Asia Pvt. Ltd., and Gibco. The dyes, like acridine orange and ethidium bromide, were provided by Real Gene.

Cell line maintenance

The L929 cell lines were cultured in DMEM with FBS and penicillin-streptomycin antibiotic solution, maintained in a 5% humidified CO2 incubator. Once the cells reached 70–80% confluency, they were detached using a 1X trypsin EDTA solution, allowing gentle detachment from the surface for further experiments while minimizing cell stress and damage.

Cytocompatibility assessment

In the cytocompatibility study, L929 cell lines were cultured under specific conditions to evaluate their interaction with nanofiber samples. The cells were maintained in DMEM with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and Pen-strep™ antibiotic solution in a CO2 incubator at 37 °C with a 5% CO2 concentration. This ensured optimal growth and provided essential nutrients and protection against contamination. Nanofiber samples weighing 300 µg were prepared and placed in 6-well cell culture plates before cell seeding. L929 cells were seeded at a density of 3 × 104 cells/mL onto the nanofiber samples and incubated for 24, 48, and 72 h. After adding an MTT-containing medium, the cells were incubated for 2 h at 37 °C to allow MTT to be metabolized by viable cells and form formazan crystals. The absorbance of the formazan product was measured at 570 nm, allowing quantification of cell viability. Phase contrast imaging was conducted at various time points (24, 48, and 72 h) using a fluorescence inverted phase contrast microscope (EVOS FL) at a magnification of 100X, with a scale bar set at 400 μm. This visual evaluation was supported by quantitative data obtained from MTT assays. The experiments were repeated three times to ensure the reliability and reproducibility of the results. These thorough evaluations yielded insights into the cytocompatibility and potential biological impacts of the nanofiber samples on L929 cells across the designated observation periods29.

The morphological study by AO/EtBr dual staining

AO/EB staining was used to assess apoptosis and cell viability in L929 cells (mouse fibroblast cell line). Briefly, 3 × 104 cells per well in 6-well plates, and cells were maintained at 5% CO2 incubator. Following treatment with 300 µg of nanofiber, the cells were incubated in a CO2 incubator for 24, 48, and 72 h. 200L of the AO/EtBr dye mixture was appended after the spent medium was removed. Using an inverted fluorescent phase contrast microscope at 200X magnification, apoptotic, viable, and nonviable cells were identified to determine the level of apoptosis30.

In vitro scratch assay

To assess the cytocompatibility of nanofibers, an in vitro scratch assay was conducted as follows: L929 cells were seeded at a density of 3 × 104 cells per well in a 6-well culture plate and cultured overnight to form a confluent monolayer. A sterile 200-µl pipette tip was then used to create a controlled scratch in the cell monolayer, followed by the removal of the medium to clear away cell debris. The cells were subsequently treated with nanofibers. Images of the scratch were captured immediately after scratching (0 h) and at 24, 48, and 72 h by a phase contrast microscope at 100X magnification scale bar at 400 μm. These images allowed for monitoring and analysis of cell migration and wound closure dynamics in response to the nanofiber treatment over time30.
