Normal tissue complication probability models of late cardiopulmonary toxicity following deep inspiration breath hold for breast cancer adjuvant radiotherapy
Authors: Lin Lin, Liang Liu, Zhengxin Gao, Yaling Hong, Qing Gong, Zhan Yu, Xue Liu, Qiaoying Hu, Yongqiang Li, Shen Fu, Qi Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and purposeDeep inspiration breath hold (DIBH) is a respiratory control technique designed to minimize cardiopulmonary toxicity induced by adjuvant radiotherapy for left‐sided breast cancer. This study aimed to evaluate the dosimetric effects of DIBH compared with those of free breathing (FB) in patients undergoing postoperative radiotherapy for left‐sided breast cancer in order to assess differences in potential cardiopulmonary complications between the two approaches.MethodsForty patients with left‐sided breast cancer undergoing postoperative adjuvant radiotherapy were included. Of these, 20 patients received conventional radiotherapy (5000 cGy in 25 fractions), and the other 20 patients received hypofractionated radiotherapy (4005 cGy in 15 fractions, with a sequential boost to the tumor bed of 1000 cGy in 5 fractions). Treatment plans were performed on both FB and DIBH computed tomography images for each patient. Mean heart dose (MHD), mean left lung dose, and other cardiopulmonary parameters were recorded. The normal tissue complication probability (NTCP) model was applied to predict complications, including pericarditis/pericardial effusion, heart valvular dysfunction (RVD), coronary artery disease (CAD), and Radiation Therapy Oncology Group (RTOG) grade ≥ 2 radiation pneumonitis (RP). Clinical anatomical features, including the number of contact layers between the heart surface and the chest wall (defined as Contact_Heart) and the Haller index (HI), were also documented, followed by correlation analysis.ResultsThe DIBH technique significantly reduced MHD by 22% in both conventional radiotherapy (p = 0.004, 95% confidence interval [CI][14, 31]) and hypofractionated radiotherapy (p = 0.003, 95% CI[14, 30]). DIBH also reduced the mean left lung dose by 10% in conventional radiotherapy (p = 0.010, 95% CI[2, 15]) and by 7% in hypofractionated radiotherapy (p = 0.008, 95% CI[0, 14]). Using the NTCP model to predict the risk of toxicities, DIBH reduced the predicted occurrence rates of pericarditis/pericardial effusion, RVD, CAD, and RTOG grade ≥ 2 RP by 35–39%, 46–51%, 4–7%, and 4–6%, respectively, across both treatment modalities. Contact_Heart was positively correlated with MHD but only weakly correlated with HI.ConclusionDIBH significantly reduces radiation exposure to the heart and lungs in patients with left‐sided breast cancer undergoing radiotherapy, potentially decreasing the risk of cardiopulmonary toxicity.Keywords: adjuvant radiotherapy, breast cancer, cardiopulmonary toxicity, deep inspiration breath hold, normal tissue complication probability

INTRODUCTION

Breast cancer has become increasingly prevalent among Chinese women and is affecting an increasing number of younger individuals. 1 Although postoperative radiotherapy for breast cancer has reduced the local recurrence rate by 70%–88% and overall mortality by 5.3% over a 15‐year follow‐up period, 2 , 3 the associated radiation‐induced late adverse effects require careful attention.

One of the most significant adverse effects is heart disease. Research has indicated that the risk of coronary artery disease (CAD) increases by 4%–16.5% per Gy increase in the mean heart dose (MHD), with virtually no minimum dose threshold. 4 − 6 The odds of heart valvular dysfunction (RVD) increase 14‐fold for each 10‐year period following radiotherapy. 7 Multiple long‐term studies have demonstrated a significant correlation between dose–volume parameters in modern radiotherapy and the risk of pericarditis/pericardial effusion. 8 In addition, specific cardiac substructures, including the left anterior descending artery (LAD) and left ventricle (LV), have received considerable attention. Based on the available evidence, the German Society for Radiation Oncology breast cancer expert panel recommends adherence to dose constraints for MHD, LV mean dose, LV volume receiving 500 cGy (LV V500 cGy), LV V2300 cGy, LAD mean dose, LAD V3000 cGy, LAD V1000 cGy, and LAD V500 cGy. 9

Another significant adverse effect is radiation pneumonitis (RP). Current studies indicate that when the total dose is ≥ 4000 cGy and the daily dose is ≥ 267 cGy, the likelihood of pulmonary‐related events increases significantly. 10 Furthermore, lung V2000 cGy and V3000 cGy are considered independent risk factors for severe RP. 11 Although respiratory toxicity in breast cancer patients has historically been relatively limited, RP has received increasing attention because of the growing adoption of hypofractionated radiotherapy (≥ 267 cGy/fraction) in current clinical practice.

Numerous factors influence radiation exposure to the heart and lungs, including tumor location, irradiated lymphatic drainage areas, patient positioning, radiation dose, and treatment technique. When these factors cannot be modified clinically, deep inspiration breath hold (DIBH), an active breathing control technique, can be utilized. During computed tomography (CT) simulation and treatment, patients are instructed to take a deep breath and hold it, thereby expanding lung volume and displacing the heart away from the left chest wall. Consequently, this technique can minimize cardiopulmonary radiation exposure. Existing studies have demonstrated that DIBH can reduce the radiation dose to the heart and the LAD by 25%–67% and 20%–73%, respectively. 12 , 13

In clinical practice, monitoring the occurrence of late toxicity requires long‐term follow‐up, particularly for heart disease, which may develop from 9 months to 30 years after treatment. The normal tissue complication probability (NTCP) model is a theoretical model developed for different organs at risk and toxicity endpoints. Although it cannot precisely predict toxicity in individual patients, it provides a theoretical framework for comparing outcomes across different techniques. 14 The Lyman–Kutcher–Burman (LKB) model is among the most widely used models and is based on the dose–volume histogram (DVH) of normal tissues. 15 , 16 Moreover, empirical models derived from clinical data, such as the Darby NTCP linear model for major CAD, are also widely used. 6

This study compared radiotherapy plans for adjuvant treatment of left‐sided breast cancer under DIBH and free breathing (FB) conditions in the same patients and clarified the differences in radiation doses delivered to the heart, its substructures, and the lungs. Based on the NTCP model, the probabilities of late cardiopulmonary toxicities associated with the two techniques were compared. In addition, descriptive statistical analyses were performed on relevant clinical anatomical indices obtained from CT simulation images, and Pearson correlation analysis was conducted between anatomical and cardiac dose parameters to identify the anatomical characteristics of patients who would benefit most from this technique.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Patient selection and radiotherapy plan

This retrospective study was approved by the institutional ethics review board (2025‐KY‐001‐01). Female patients with left‐sided breast cancer who underwent postoperative adjuvant radiotherapy between November 2019 and October 2024 were included according to the following inclusion criteria: (1) tumor stage consistent with pT1–4N1–3M0 based on the 8th edition of the American Joint Committee on Cancer Breast Cancer Staging Manual; and (2) completion of postoperative adjuvant chemotherapy or fulfillment of the criteria for chemotherapy omission. A total of 40 patients were selected using a random sampling method and divided into two groups according to the radiotherapy regimen, with 20 patients in each group. The conventional radiotherapy group received irradiation to the left chest wall combined with the regional lymphatic drainage areas, whereas the hypofractionated radiotherapy group received irradiation to the whole left breast and tumor bed. All enrolled patients provided written informed consent.

Patients were positioned supine and immobilized using a breast board. Two sets of CT scans were acquired sequentially under FB and DIBH conditions with a slice thickness of 2.5 mm. The respiratory gating system used was the Varian Real‐time Position Management System, with the marker placed centrally below the xiphoid process. Prior to CT simulation, each patient underwent breathing training using abdominal breathing techniques and was required to be able to hold their breath for at least 18 seconds and achieve a respiratory amplitude of at least 1.2 cm.

Target volumes were delineated on FB and DIBH CT images by a radiation oncologist with 5 years of experience in target volume delineation. Target volume delineation was performed according to the Radiation Therapy Oncology Group (RTOG) guidelines. 17 CTV_ChestWall was defined as the entire left chest wall. CTV_SCV included the supraclavicular region, axillary level III, and part of level II. CTV_IMN included the internal mammary lymph nodes. CTV_Breast was defined as the entire left breast. CTV_TumorBed was defined as the surgical tumor bed identified by surgical clips. Planning target volumes (PTVs) were generated by adding a 5‐mm margin to the corresponding CTVs. Among these, PTV_Breast was retracted 5 mm inward from the skin surface. For PTV_ChestWall extending to the skin surface, a 3‐mm bolus was applied to ensure adequate dose coverage of the skin.

Similarly, normal tissues were delineated on both sets of CT images by the same physician. The contours of the lungs, heart, and LV were delineated according to the RTOG atlases. The delineation of cardiac substructures was based on the cardiac substructure atlas described by Feng et al., 18 and the LAD and LV were contoured on all axial slices. When the LAD was not clearly visible on CT images, it was defined as the anterior portion of the interventricular sulcus. 19

The prescribed dose for conventional radiotherapy was 5000 cGy delivered in 25 fractions. The prescribed dose for whole‐breast hypofractionated radiotherapy was 4005 cGy delivered in 15 fractions, with a sequential boost to the tumor bed of 1000 cGy delivered in 5 fractions.

The OAR dose constraints were as follows: for conventional radiotherapy, heart V2000 cGy < 10%, V1000 cGy < 25%, V500 cGy < 50%, MHD < 800 cGy, left lung mean dose < 1500 cGy, left lung V2000 cGy < 30%, and V1000 cGy < 68%; for hypofractionated radiotherapy, heart V2000 cGy < 3%, V1000 cGy < 12%, V500 cGy < 45%, MHD < 400 cGy, left lung mean dose < 1600 cGy, V2000 cGy < 26%, and V1000 cGy < 54%. Currently, there is no unified consensus regarding dose constraints for the LAD and LV. In this study, because radiation dose to critical cardiac substructures is considered important in determining the probability of cardiac toxicity, we also evaluated radiation doses to substructures such as the LAD and LV.

All patients were treated with 6‐MV X‐ray beams using a Varian TrueBeam linear accelerator (Varian Medical Systems, Palo Alto, CA, USA). Radiotherapy planning was stratified according to the different PTVs as follows:

Volumetric modulated arc therapy (VMAT) planning for PTV_ChestWall, PTV_SCV, PTV_IMN (conventional radiotherapy group), and PTV_Breast (hypofractionated radiotherapy group): VMAT was adopted for the above‐mentioned PTVs because of its superior dose conformity and delivery efficiency. Faster dose delivery reduces the duration of DIBH, allowing patients to complete daily treatment using only 3–4 breath‐holds. The VMAT plan consisted of two coplanar arcs with a gantry rotation range of 300°–170°. The radiation field was expanded as much as possible to ensure complete coverage of all target volumes, while the field size in the X‐axis direction was restricted to 15 cm. To improve dose conformity to the target shape, the collimator was rotated by 5° at the arc start angle. For patients receiving chest wall radiotherapy, a 3‐mm bolus was applied to the skin surface to compensate for inadequate superficial dose coverage. Target coverage was typically evaluated using 95% of the prescribed dose for PTV_ChestWall and PTV_Breast and 90% of the prescribed dose for PTV_SCV and PTV_IMN.

The LKB model 15 was used to predict the occurrence of cardiac and pulmonary complications:

The model includes three key parameters: D50, m, and n. D50 represents the dose at which uniform irradiation of the entire organ results in a 50% probability of complications; m represents the slope of the dose–response curve; and n describes the volume dependence of normal tissue response. Different toxicity endpoints are predicted using specific OAR parameters and DVH data.

All model parameters were obtained from the literature, and equivalent doses were calculated using a fraction size of 200 cGy. The parameters used to predict the probability of pericarditis/pericardial effusion were TD50 = 4800 cGy, m = 0.10, n = 0.35, and a cardiac α/β ratio of 2.5. 8 The parameters used to predict the incidence of RVD were D50 = 2900 cGy, n = 0.16, m = 0.41, and a cardiac α/β ratio of 3. 20 For predicting the incidence of RP (RTOG grade ≥ 2), the parameters were D50 = 2990 cGy, n = 1, m = 0.41, and a lung α/β ratio of 3. 21

The Darby NTCP linear model 6 was employed to predict the incidence of CAD.

Parameters: Bs = 1.9%, K = 7.4%, and X = MHD.

Haller index (HI): the ratio of the maximum transverse diameter to the minimum anteroposterior diameter measured at the same level on axial CT images (see Figure 1B). The standardized measurement protocol for HI was as follows: at approximately the T9 vertebral level on axial CT images, the deepest point of sternal depression was identified. All Contact_Heart and HI measurements were performed by one physician and independently verified by a second physician. Both operators received standardized measurement training and followed the same measurement protocol. As shown in Figure 1C–F, representative measurements of Contact_Heart and HI on FB and DIBH CT simulation images from the same patient are presented. All measurements were performed on mediastinal‐window CT simulation images using the Eclipse treatment planning system.

Numerical variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation values. The paired t‐test was used for data that conformed to a normal distribution, with a confidence level of 95%. For data that did not conform to a normal distribution, the Wilcoxon signed‐rank test was used. Multiple comparisons between the FB and DIBH groups were adjusted using the Holm–Bonferroni method. All original p‐values were corrected using this method; a corrected p‐value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant, and 95% confidence intervals were also reported.

Pearson correlation analysis was conducted to evaluate the relationships between anatomical parameters and cardiac dose parameters, whereas simple linear regression was performed for variables showing statistically significant associations. A positive Pearson correlation coefficient (r > 0) indicates a positive correlation between two variables, whereas a negative coefficient (r < 0) indicates a negative correlation. In general, 0.4 ≤ |r| < 0.6 is considered indicative of a moderate correlation, 0.6 ≤ |r| < 0.8 of a strong correlation, and |r| ≥ 0.8 of a very strong correlation. All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 10.4.0.

RESULTS

The target volume parameters for the two groups are presented in Table 2. The volumes of the chest wall, whole‐breast, supraclavicular, and internal mammary nodal PTVs were similar between the FB and DIBH CT datasets. The dose distributions in both the FB and DIBH plans met the prescribed planning objectives. PTV_ChestWall and PTV_Breast received more than 95% of the prescribed dose, whereas PTV_SCV and PTV_IMN received more than 90% of the prescribed dose. Furthermore, the dose distributions were comparable between the two groups. Heart and lung volumes were measured on CT simulation images. In both the conventional and hypofractionated radiotherapy groups, heart volumes were comparable between the FB and DIBH datasets, whereas lung volumes differed significantly. In the conventional radiotherapy group, the lung volume in the DIBH dataset was 54% ± 18% greater than that in the FB dataset (p < 0.001). Similarly, in the hypofractionated radiotherapy group, the lung volume in the DIBH dataset was 57% ± 26% greater than that in the FB dataset (p < 0.001).

Table 3 summarizes the dose distributions to the heart and lungs in the FB and DIBH plans. The MHD in the conventional radiotherapy group was < 500 cGy, whereas the MHD in the hypofractionated radiotherapy group was < 400 cGy; both values complied with the OAR dose constraints. DIBH improved the dose distribution to the heart in both conventional and hypofractionated radiotherapy plans following surgery for left‐sided breast cancer, reducing the MHD by 22% (p = 0.004, 95% confidence interval [CI][14, 31] in the conventional radiotherapy group; p = 0.003, 95% CI[14, 30] in the hypofractionated radiotherapy group). The doses to the cardiac substructures LAD and LV were also significantly reduced. DIBH also tended to reduce radiation exposure to the left lung. Specifically, DIBH reduced the mean left lung dose by 10% in the conventional radiotherapy group (p = 0.010, 95% CI[2, 15]) and by 7% in the hypofractionated radiotherapy group (p = 0.053, 95% CI[0, 14]). Regarding the DVH parameters of normal lung tissue, DIBH resulted in a significant reduction in left lung V2000 cGy compared with FB (12%, p = 0.017 in the conventional radiotherapy group; 14%, p = 0.007 in the hypofractionated radiotherapy group). For left lung V1000 cGy, however, DIBH resulted in only a 9%–10% reduction, which did not reach statistical significance. Additionally, in the conventional radiotherapy group, DIBH reduced left lung V500 cGy by 6% (95% CI[1, 13]; p = 0.025), whereas no statistically significant difference was observed in the hypofractionated radiotherapy group (p = 0.877).

The NTCP model was used to predict the incidence of pericarditis/pericardial effusion, RVD, CAD, and RTOG grade ≥ 2 RP, and the results are presented in Figure 2 and Table 4. The results for conventional fractionated radiotherapy are shown in Figure 2A. The predicted incidence of pericarditis/pericardial effusion was reduced by 39% with DIBH (p = 0.013, 95% CI[24, 59]). Similarly, the predicted incidence of RVD decreased by 51% (p = 0.017, 95% CI[33, 67]), the predicted incidence of CAD decreased by 7% (p = 0.025, 95% CI[3, 9]), and the predicted incidence of RTOG grade ≥ 2 RP decreased by 6% (p = 0.050, 95% CI[2, 18]). Similar findings were observed in the hypofractionated radiotherapy cohort, as shown in Figure 2B. Overall, DIBH resulted in a significant reduction in the predicted risk of these cardiopulmonary complications across both treatment regimens. However, for the prediction of RTOG grade ≥ 2 RP, the p‐value was 0.050, indicating borderline statistical significance and warranting further investigation and validation.

Contact_Heart represents the number of consecutive axial CT slices in which any part of the heart surface is in contact with the chest wall. During FB, the mean number of contact slices between the heart and the chest wall was 26–28, which decreased to 22–24 following the application of the DIBH technique (Figure 3A and 3C). The HI is defined as the ratio of the maximum transverse diameter of the inner thorax to the minimum anteroposterior diameter measured at the most concave level of the sternum. This ratio was lower during DIBH than during FB (Figure 3B and 3D).

The associations between clinical anatomical parameters and MHD are illustrated in Figure 4. Contact_Heart was positively correlated with MHD in both the conventional and hypofractionated radiotherapy groups (Figure 4). Specifically, an increase in the number of contact slices between the heart surface and the chest wall was associated with a higher MHD. Conversely, the DIBH technique reduces cardiac radiation exposure by increasing the distance between the heart and the chest wall. As a result, a decrease in the number of heart–chest wall contact slices was associated with a lower MHD. Furthermore, correlation analysis between HI and MHD did not demonstrate a statistically significant relationship (p > 0.05). Pearson correlation analysis between HI and mean left lung dose showed that, in the hypofractionated radiotherapy group, the correlation coefficient (r) was 0.47 with a p‐value of 0.002, indicating a moderate positive correlation. In contrast, in the conventional radiotherapy group, the correlation coefficient (r) was −0.07 with a p‐value of 0.674, indicating no meaningful correlation. Therefore, the relationship between HI and mean left lung dose requires further validation using additional data.

DISCUSSION

This study was a self‐controlled dosimetric analysis. Target delineation and treatment planning were performed on FB and DIBH CT simulation images from the same patients, thereby minimizing potential confounding effects related to tumor location, surgical procedure, patient positioning, target volume, dose distribution, and other factors. There were no significant differences in PTV volumes or dose distributions between the two imaging conditions, which enhanced the reliability of the subsequent dosimetric analyses.

Consistent with the organ volume measurements obtained from the CT images, no significant difference in heart volume was observed between the FB and DIBH datasets, whereas a statistically significant difference was found in total lung volume. Specifically, the DIBH technique increased lung volume by approximately 52%–54%. We further quantified Contact_Heart and found that the number of contact slices on DIBH CT images was 2–6 slices fewer than that on FB CT images. Additionally, the HI was approximately 2.6–2.7 on FB images, whereas DIBH expanded the thoracic cavity, resulting in a lower HI value of approximately 2.4. These findings are highly consistent with the underlying mechanism of DIBH, which promotes lung inflation and expansion, thereby displacing the heart away from the chest wall.

Currently, two primary dose‐fractionation regimens are used for adjuvant radiotherapy in breast cancer. The first is the conventional regimen, typically consisting of 5000 cGy delivered in 25 fractions. The second approach is hypofractionated radiotherapy. Clinical studies have demonstrated that hypofractionated radiotherapy achieves outcomes comparable to those of conventional radiotherapy in terms of local control, survival, and adverse effects. Commonly prescribed hypofractionated regimens include 4005 cGy in 15 fractions with a sequential boost to the tumor bed, as used in the START B trial; 23 4256 cGy in 16 fractions in the Canadian trial; 24 and 2600 cGy in 5 fractions in the FAST‐Forward trial. 25 In this study, conventional radiotherapy is used for chest wall irradiation with regional lymphatic drainage irradiation, whereas the START B regimen is applied for whole‐breast radiotherapy.

In this study, a comprehensive dosimetric evaluation was conducted to compare these two fractionation regimens, with particular emphasis on dose variations in the cardiac substructures, including the LAD and LV. This investigation addresses a limitation of previous studies, which have often provided only limited evaluation of cardiac substructures. As described above, compared with FB, DIBH reduced the MHD by approximately 22% in both the conventional radiotherapy group (p = 0.004) and the hypofractionated radiotherapy group (p = 0.003). This finding is consistent with previous reports showing that DIBH can reduce MHD by 15%–60%. 26 − 28 Moreover, significant reductions were observed in heart V500 cGy, V1000 cGy, and V2000 cGy. Regarding cardiac substructures, the mean LAD dose was reduced by 43% in the conventional radiotherapy group (p = 0.006, 95% CI[9, 52]), whereas the mean LV dose was reduced by 27% (p = 0.003, 95% CI[17, 36]). The corresponding reductions in the hypofractionated radiotherapy group were 28% for the LAD (p = 0.004, 95% CI[9, 46]) and 25% for the LV (p = 0.003, 95% CI[17, 34]). Notably, this study extended the comparative analysis to include additional dose–volume parameters, all of which demonstrated that DIBH effectively reduced cardiac irradiation volumes. Furthermore, we observed that DIBH produced a more pronounced reduction in radiation dose to cardiac substructures in the conventional radiotherapy group. This finding may be attributable to the inclusion of the internal mammary nodal region within the irradiation field in these patients.

With respect to pulmonary dosimetry, DIBH resulted in a 7%–10% reduction in the mean left lung dose. Specifically, DIBH reduced left lung V2000 cGy by 12% in the conventional radiotherapy group (p = 0.017) and by 14% in the hypofractionated radiotherapy group (p = 0.007). However, the effect of DIBH on reducing left lung V1000 cGy did not reach statistical significance. These findings are consistent with those reported in previous studies. 27 , 29 Larger studies will be required to further clarify the effect of DIBH on lung dosimetric parameters.

The NTCP model is based on the dose–volume effect relationship. By analyzing large datasets of clinical outcomes, it converts physical radiation doses into quantifiable probabilities of biological effects, thereby enabling prediction of the risk of normal tissue toxicity under specific treatment conditions. Among the various NTCP models, the Lyman model is one of the most widely used. Its fundamental principle involves defining normal tissue dose‐threshold parameters (D 50) and the slope parameter of the dose–response curve (m). Combined with the dose–volume effects of tissues, the Lyman model uses mathematical formulations to estimate the probability of specific radiation‐induced complications.

In clinical practice, the NTCP model provides substantial value because it can translate the abstract concept of “dose‐related risk” into a quantitative probability estimate. This is particularly useful for severe or late toxicities that can only be assessed through long‐term follow‐up, as the model provides objective risk estimates to support radiation oncologists in treatment decision‐making. According to the current literature, the predictive accuracy of the Lyman model for specific complications is approximately 75%, whereas that of the Darby NTCP linear model is approximately 86%. 6 , 30 , 31 In the present study, NTCP models were used to compare radiotherapy plans under DIBH and FB conditions, with the aim of theoretically identifying the optimal treatment approach that balances tumor control with the risk of normal tissue complications.

A comparison of predicted cardiopulmonary toxicity risks between conventional and hypofractionated radiotherapy was also performed. Radiation‐induced cardiac toxicities include pericardial disease, myocardial injury, CAD, valvular heart disease, arrhythmias, microvascular injury, and mixed cardiac injuries. 32 NTCP models for pericarditis/pericardial effusion, RVD, and CAD have been extensively validated. Although arrhythmias are common radiation‐induced cardiac adverse events, there is currently no consensus regarding a standardized NTCP model for their prediction because of inconsistent findings across studies. Therefore, arrhythmia risk was not evaluated in the present study.

Compared with the findings reported by Bruzzaniti et al., 27 who observed statistically significant differences in the overall incidence of cardiac adverse events only for the conventional fractionation regimen, our analysis demonstrated that DIBH may reduce the predicted incidence of pericarditis/pericardial effusion, RVD, and CAD. In Bruzzaniti et al.’s study, no significant differences were observed for pericarditis or RP. This discrepancy may be related to differences in sample size and irradiation fields. Specifically, Bruzzaniti et al. enrolled eight patients who primarily received whole‐breast irradiation, whereas our cohort comprised 40 patients, including 20 who underwent chest wall and regional lymphatic drainage irradiation and 20 who received whole‐breast irradiation with a tumor bed boost. Our results also indicated that hypofractionated radiotherapy was associated with a lower predicted incidence of toxicities than conventional radiotherapy. The underlying reason remains unclear and warrants further investigation, particularly to determine whether this finding is attributable to improved normal tissue sparing with hypofractionated regimens or to the inclusion of internal mammary nodal irradiation in the conventional radiotherapy group.

Finally, we analyzed clinical anatomical factors that may contribute to the significant reduction in MHD. Various methods have been used in previous studies to quantify the distance between the heart and the chest wall, including measuring the contact length between the heart and the chest wall on corresponding axial or sagittal planes 33 or counting the number of contact slices between the heart (or LAD) and the chest wall on axial CT images. 22 In this study, Contact_Heart was defined as the number of contact slices between the heart and the chest wall measured on axial CT images. This method is simple and rapid to perform and may reduce inter‐observer variability.

Our findings were consistent with those of Lee et al., 22 demonstrating that the DIBH technique reduced Contact_Heart and that MHD was significantly correlated with Contact_Heart (conventional radiotherapy group: r = 0.49, p = 0.001; hypofractionated radiotherapy group: r = 0.42, p = 0.008). Furthermore, Lee et al. identified a subgroup of patients likely to benefit from DIBH using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis and proposed that Contact_Heart ≥ 25 slices (2‐mm axial slice thickness) could predict MHD ≥ 170 cGy (sensitivity, 68%; specificity, 82%). However, no valid ROC curve analysis results were obtained in our study.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, as a retrospective study, the sample size was relatively small (n = 40), which may limit the generalizability of the findings, and prospective validation in larger cohorts is still lacking. Second, no definitive conclusion could be drawn regarding the relationship between the HI and lung dose, and further investigation in larger patient cohorts is warranted. In addition, the risk of cardiac events after radiotherapy is strongly influenced by patients' baseline risk factors, such as comorbidities and lifestyle habits, which may contribute to discrepancies between NTCP‐based predictions and long‐term clinical outcomes. Our observation that younger patients with higher educational levels demonstrated better compliance with DIBH suggests that they may also be more likely to engage in healthy lifestyle behaviors, including dietary management and regular exercise. These factors may independently reduce baseline cardiovascular risk and contribute to lower rates of adverse cardiac events. Finally, because long‐term clinical follow‐up data were not available, the toxicity risks predicted by the NTCP models could not be validated against real‐world clinical outcomes.

In future work, we plan to expand the sample size and use Contact_Heart as a key parameter for constructing a database that integrates patients' baseline characteristics and treatment‐related factors. This database will be used to further establish a predictive model linking Contact_Heart with actual cardiopulmonary toxicity outcomes. We are currently conducting regular follow‐up assessments to monitor the development of cardiac toxicity through long‐term observation. Following CT simulation, the potential benefit of DIBH may be rapidly evaluated through quantitative assessment of Contact_Heart. For patients with a high predicted benefit, implementation of DIBH during radiotherapy may effectively reduce the risk of cardiopulmonary toxicity, whereas for patients with a low predicted benefit, routine use of this technique may not be necessary. Such an approach could not only improve treatment efficiency but also optimize the use of healthcare resources, including clinical workload and treatment time. More importantly, long‐term clinical follow‐up will enable validation of the predictive model and further improve its accuracy.

In summary, based on dosimetric and NTCP‐model analyses, DIBH significantly reduced radiation doses to the lungs, heart, and cardiac substructures in patients undergoing adjuvant radiotherapy following surgery for left‐sided breast cancer. NTCP‐based predictions suggest that these dose reductions may translate into a lower predicted incidence of cardiopulmonary toxicities. Although the present NTCP analysis provides preliminary evidence, prospective randomized clinical trials are required to further evaluate both short‐term and long‐term cardiopulmonary complication rates.
