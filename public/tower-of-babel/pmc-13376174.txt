Jets from a stellar-mass black hole are as relativistic as those from supermassive black holes
Authors: X Zhang, W Yu, F Carotenuto, R Fender, S Motta, A Bahramian, J C A Miller-Jones, T D Russell, S Corbel, P A Woudt, P Atri, C Knigge, G R Sivakoff, A K Hughes, J van den Eijnden, J H Matthews, M C Baglio, P Saikia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRelativistic jets from supermassive black holes in active galactic nuclei are amongst the most powerful phenomena in the universe. Similar jets from stellar-mass black holes offer a chance to study the phenomena on accessible observation time scales. However, such comparative studies across black hole masses and time scales remain hampered by the long-standing perception that stellar-mass black hole jets are in a less relativistic regime. Here, we show the detection of two distinct, relativistic jet ejections from the Galactic black hole X-ray binary 4U 1543−47 during a single outburst, with radio interferometry monitoring observations. Our measurements reveal a likely Lorentz factor of approximately 8 and a minimum of 4.6 at launch with 95% confidence, demonstrating that stellar-mass black holes in X-ray binaries can launch jets as relativistic as those seen in active galactic nuclei.Subject terms: Transient astrophysical phenomena, High-energy astrophysics

Introduction

The first superluminal jets discovered from the Galactic X-ray binary GRS 1915+105, some 30 years ago1 prove the existence of microquasars2 in our Universe. This opened up the window to study relativistic jets launched from nearby (<10 kpc) accreting black holes in the Galaxy. Past observations have shown that the most relativistic jets measured in Galactic black hole X-ray binaries are those episodic jets launched when X-ray spectral transitions between the hard state (or intermediate state) and the soft state3, especially in black hole X-ray binary transients during the rising phase of an accretion outburst. The highest minimum Lorentz factor of relativistic jets constrained in these black hole X-ray binaries from those radio interferometric observations in the past few decades has been around 2.44,5. Since on average only 2–3 galactic black hole X-ray binaries turn into bright outbursts yearly and some sources have turned into an outburst multiple times in the past6, the sample of stellar-mass black holes observed with superluminal, relativistic jets is still no more than a dozen to date7.

Relativistic jets in active galactic nuclei (AGN) are amongst the most powerful and extreme phenomena in the universe that play a significant feedback role in regulating the evolution of massive galaxies8. These jets from supermassive black holes are much more relativistic (with Lorentz factors typically 10)9–11 than those from their stellar-mass counterparts (with Lorentz factors assumed widely to be 2)3. The Lorentz factor of relativistic jets from AGN, based on a sample size more than an order of magnitude larger than that of the stellar-mass black holes, is found distributed in a broader range but more or less peaked slightly below about 109–11. Observational studies of supermassive black holes tend to select face-on systems called blazars in a flux-limited manner10, thus it has been widely believed that jets in stellar-mass black holes are not as relativistic, and they are laboratories of jet physics in a less relativistic regime3. The actual Lorentz factor distribution of the jets in AGN is probably of a power-law form12, suggesting that there are more sources towards lower Lorentz factors than what has been observed13. On the other hand, the distribution corresponding to those in stellar-mass black holes is observed rather flat7. Furthermore, stellar-mass black holes in X-ray binaries offer a chance to study relativistic jets on timescales a million or more times shorter. Detection of similarly relativistic jets in stellar-mass black hole X-ray binaries and tracing them in full cycles of accretion events can therefore shed light on our understanding of relativistic ejections in the high-Γ regime, as well as the entire process of how the relativistic jet ejections are built up, which is otherwise inaccessible on current human-observable time scales.

4U 1543−47 (IL Lup) is a black hole X-ray binary discovered in 1971 by the Uhuru satellite14, with subsequent X-ray outbursts occurring nearly every decade15,16. The system has a reported orbital inclination angle θorb of 20.7∘ ± 1.5∘16,17 with distance estimates of 7.5 ± 0.5 kpc from optical photometry18 and 5.0−1.2+2.0 kpc (see Methods, subsection “Distance estimation”) from combining the parallax listed in Gaia Data Release 3 (Gaia DR3) and a Milky Way density prior for low-mass X-ray binaries19. With a companion star of spectral type A2V and mass of 2.45 ± 0.15 M⊙, the system is thought to host a black hole with a mass MBH of 9.4 ± 1.0 M⊙20, with the black hole spin a* estimated in the range of 0.67–0.9821–23.

Here, we report the detection of two sets of highly relativistic, superluminal jet ejections in the black hole X-ray binary 4U 1543−47 during its 2021–2023 outburst with radio interferometry monitoring observations. We are able to trace the motion of the ejected blobs of these jet ejections over 15 months. The proper motions of the blobs are the highest ever detected in microquasars, implying a most likely Lorentz factor of 8 (with a 90% confidence interval of 4.6–20.4) at launch, comparable to those typically seen in face-on AGN.

Results

Radio and X-ray monitoring observations

On June 11, 2021 (Modified Julian Date (MJD) 59,376), 4U 1543−47 was detected in a new X-ray outburst by the Monitor of All-sky X-ray Image (hereafter MAXI24). On June 19 (MJD 59,384), we started weekly radio observations of the source of 15 min each in the 1.28 GHz band using the Meer Karoo Array Telescope (MeerKAT25) under large survey project (LSP) ThunderKAT26 (see Methods, subsection “Radio observations and data reduction”). These observations typically reached a sensitivity level of about 20 μJy/beam. X-ray monitoring observations with MAXI and the Burst Alert Telescope (BAT) on board the Neil Gehrels Swift Observatory27 (hereafter, Swift) indicate that the black hole X-ray binary quickly transited from the X-ray hard state to the soft state on the same day as the outburst discovery. Quenching of the compact jet emission and the ejection of radio blobs have typically been associated with such X-ray spectral and timing state transitions in X-ray binaries3. Coordinated quasi-simultaneous observations by the X-ray telescope (XRT) on board Swift and X-ray monitoring observations by MAXI, Swift/BAT, and the Neutron Star Interior Composition Explorer (NICER) helped us discriminate X-ray states throughout the major outburst and subsequent mini-outbursts (see Fig. 1 and see Methods, “Subsection X-ray observations and data reduction”).

The first MeerKAT radio detection occurred on June 19, 2021 (MJD 59,384), spatially coincident with the known source position, which is the Gaia Data Release 2 (Gaia DR2) optical position (see Methods, subsection “Radio astrometry of the ejected blobs”). The radio emission remained consistent with the source position for the next 3 weeks. A new radio component about 9″ away from the source was detected on September 5, 2021 (MJD 59,462), 84 days after the discovery of the X-ray outburst, indicating a transient jet had been launched at an earlier time.

We continued our radio monitoring observations to track the downstream jet. The overall radio emission from the system is consistent with the conventional picture of jet emission from microquasars28, which is composed of both compact and transient jet components. The investigations of the compact jet at the core position and its radio vs. X-ray correlation have been presented in a separate paper29. In this paper, we focus on the transient jets. The downstream ejecta of the transient jets had been detected in our MeerKAT observations of the source in the flux density range from 65 ± 19 to 365 ± 23 μJy over a total of 24 epochs from September 5, 2021 (MJD 59,462) to January 7, 2023 (MJD 59,951). All the detected ejecta were consistent with two ejections approaching the observer towards the South-East (SE) with consistent position angles within about 1 ∘ (see Methods, subsection “Position angle of the ejecta”).

Astrometric measurements of discrete blobs

Astrometric measurements up to November 8, 2021 (MJD 59,526) show that the first ejection (hereafter Ejection 1 or E1) underwent a clear deceleration when it reached an angular distance of 9–12″ away from the source, corresponding to a projected physical separation of about 0.2–0.3 pc at a source distance of 5.0 kpc. According to the established picture of microquasar jets28, E1 was likely launched after the rapid state transition during the early rise of the outburst, as there is no evidence of X-ray activity above its quiescent level up to more than 2 months prior to the discovery of the outburst. E1 eventually reached an angular distance of about 12 ″ from the central binary, suggesting that the ejecta may have gone through a relatively low-density region before hitting a higher-density region—at least about 3 ″ (or 0.1 pc) thick along the direction of the proper motion (see Fig. 2). We also identified a second ejection, hereafter ejection 2 or E2, in observations from MJD 59,559 (see Methods, subsection “Proper motion of the ejecta and the modelings”). E2 followed the same path as E1 as suggested by their consistent position angles; however, either due to E2 being more energetic or E1 having cleared a path, E2 propagated with a higher apparent speed to a much larger projected distance, reaching nearly 50″ (about 1.2 pc) away from the central binary about 15 months later (see Fig. 3). It is worth noting that this is the largest projected distance ever detected in the jet ejecta from galactic black hole X-ray binaries.

Proper motions and constraints on Lorentz factors

We modeled E1 as having a constant deceleration starting from a launch date that we conservatively set to June 11, 2021 (MJD 59,376), when MAXI and Swift/BAT observed that the source swiftly transitioned from the X-ray hard state to the soft state; by applying a constant-deceleration model defined as Eq. 1, we obtained that E1 had an initial proper motion of 152.8 ± 8.8 mas/day. For a source distance of 5 kpc, the Lorentz factor Γ is constrained to > 4.5 ± 0.2 while the jet inclination angle θjet should lie in the range 0∘–(25.6∘ ± 1.4∘). We found the proper motion of E2 is more extreme. By applying a constant-deceleration model to E2, which follows Eq. 1, we constrained its launch time as MJD 59,506.9 ± 5.2 with an initial proper motion of 186.7 ± 5.3 mas/day. The corresponding Lorentz factor is constrained to > (5.5 ± 0.2), while θjet is constrained to lie in the range 0∘–(21.0∘ ± 0.6∘). Both jet ejections are consistent with high Lorentz factors and a small jet inclination angle.

The first detections of E1 and E2 in our MeerKAT monitoring campaign correspond to a projected angular separation about 9″ away from the central binary, likely suggesting there is an approximately 0.1 pc, or slightly larger, inner cavity of interstellar media (ISM) surrounding the black hole X-ray binary. Before hitting the higher-density ISM further out, E2 was likely moving close to ballistic motion, since the previous E1 would have carved out a channel several months earlier. Thus, we applied a ballistic motion for E2 before its first detection on MJD 59,559. A constant-deceleration model (Eq. 1) of E2 is applied to ejecta beyond the likely cavity. This provides a more conservative estimate of the launching date than our previous estimate using the constant-deceleration model. The launch date of E2 is then obtained as MJD 59,502.7 ± 5.0, with an initial proper motion of 166.8 ± 2.7 mas/day. The launch date is well after the transition to the soft X-ray state, about 20 days after a few hard X-ray flares decayed, as seen with Swift/BAT, suggesting an important role of the approaching disk flow in the ballistic jet launch (see Fig. 1). The corresponding Lorentz factor is then conservatively constrained to > (4.9 ± 0.1). This is the absolute minimum of the Lorentz factor at launch, dependent only on the assumed distance estimate of 5 kpc. Similarly, θjet is also constrained in the range 0∘–(23.5∘ ± 0.4∘). The highest minimum Γ at launch previously determined in all black hole X-ray binaries has been approximately 2.4, seen in the stellar-mass black hole transients GX 339−4 and GRO J1655−404,5. Our constraint on the minimum Γ at launch in 4U 1543−47 (see Methods, subsection “Constraints on the relativistic ejections”) is a factor of two larger; the relativistic jets we detected have the most extreme minimum Lorentz factor observed to date in any microquasar. Furthermore, the proper motion is also significantly larger than the largest observed in microquasars in the past30.

Since the uncertainties in the estimates of the source distance would affect the best constraint we can put on the Lorentz factor Γ, we made use of the distance, 5.0−1.2+2.0 kpc, obtained by combining Gaia DR3 parallax of the source and a Milky Way density prior (see Methods, subsection “Distance estimation”), as the prior distribution of source distance. We then perform Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) analysis to obtain the posterior distribution (see Methods, subsection “Distribution of the Lorentz factor with MCMC”) of the Lorentz factor of both jet ejections. The inferred probability distribution of the Lorentz factor of E2 is shown in Fig. 4, which shows that the median is 7.8−2.3+5.0, with a minimum of 4.6 at launch with 95% confidence. This implies that the relativistic ejection E2 has a probability distribution of the Lorentz factor similar to the observed distribution of the Lorentz factors of the highly relativistic jets in more than a hundred radio-loud blazars10 and a few tens of gamma-ray blazars9,31.

The constraint on the minimum Lorentz factor in 4U 1543−47 was derived from the astrometric measurements of the ejecta more than 50 days after launch, when scaled by the black hole mass, the accretion timescale corresponds to the order of 104–8 years for black holes with a mass of about 106−10M⊙. If we were able to detect the ejecta soon enough and trace the ejecta at much earlier times, a higher Lorentz factor may have been detected, since the ISM in the vicinity of 4U 1543−47, although could be thin, should still slow down the ejecta. The usual monthly to yearly monitoring time scales of very long baseline interferometry (VLBI) observations of AGN jets roughly correspond to accretion time scales of seconds in a microquasar. This implies that the relativistic jets in microquasars could be more extreme at early times than what we report here. Future sensitive, high-cadence (hourly or less) VLBI monitoring observations may be able to trace jet motion in a more extreme regime with the help of techniques, such as dynamic phase center tracking32.

Discussion

To date, superluminal, relativistic jet ejections have been observed from no more than a dozen stellar-mass black holes. Past radio interferometric observations have constrained the highest minimum Lorentz factor of these relativistic jets to around 2.4, which has been obtained with the best measurements of proper motions4,5 of the ejected blobs. Thus, the jets we detected in 4U 1543−47, with the Lorentz factor constrained to around 8, represent the top 10% most relativistic jets in the stellar mass black hole sample. The proper motions of the corresponding ejected blobs constrain the inclinations to one of the lowest jet inclinations (<21.0∘) among the sample. Since all these black hole X-ray binaries have been monitored by X-ray all-sky monitors and radio interferometric monitoring observations usually follow the detection of X-ray outbursts, the sample of the stellar-mass black hole jets therefore does not have a significant bias towards or against low-inclination angle systems. On the other hand, there are significant selection effects towards low-inclination angle systems for AGN jets. Specifically, an investigation of 232 AGNs from the “1.5 JyQC” sample10 indicates that roughly 70% of their jets achieve a Lorentz factor greater than 8. But the simulation suggests that nearly all these jets within the “1.5 JyQC” quasars are observed at angles less than approximately 10∘ from the line of sight, with the distribution peaked at 2∘10. If we take the jet inclination of 4U 1543−47 as 2∘ at a source distance of 5 kpc, then the Lorentz factor would be 9, comparable to the typical Lorentz factor in the “1.5 JyQC” sample, corresponding to the distribution peak around 1010. It is worth noting that the measured proper motions combined with the distance uncertainties do not allow us to quantitatively measure the exact Lorentz factors of the relativistic jets, but give only lower limits33. Therefore, we conclude that 4U 1543−47 can launch jets with a Lorentz factor comparable to those typical in face-on AGN.

Both compact and transient jets have been observed in AGN11, but the correspondences of AGN jets to what we observed in 4U 1543−47 are unclear. In stellar-mass black hole X-ray binaries, the ejected blobs of transient jets are usually found associated with state transitions or spectral variations. Typical accretion-ejection evolution timescales, represented by these state transitions in stellar-mass black hole X-ray binaries, range from days to weeks34. Since accretion timescales in accreting black holes scale with the black hole mass, corresponding time scales should be millions of times longer in those potential counterparts in AGN jets, which cannot be traced by observations performed in the past few decades. We have noticed that in recent studies, changing-look AGN, showing transitions between different types on typical timescales of years or longer, might display similarities to accretion state transitions in X-ray binary outbursts35. These transitions may not correspond to the state transitions seen in accreting stellar-mass black holes based on the accretion timescale argument.

We have detected large-scale, transient jets following either state transitions or hard X-ray flares in 4U 1543−47, as well as compact jets in the X-ray hard state29. The maximum projected distance of the transient jet reached beyond 1.2 pc, the largest ever detected in the current stellar-mass black hole sample. Such a long propagation distance of the ejected blobs seems to favor the nature of the ejected blobs being electron-ion plasma (see ref. 36 and references therein). In the case of 4U 1543−47, we observed two distinct jet ejections, manifested by 24 detected blobs with consistent position angles and occurred at different X-ray luminosities. It is still difficult for us to assure if these blobs are dynamically consistent with electromagnetically launching along the spin axis and by the spinning black hole37, since consistent jet position angles may not be the result of fixed direction of the angular momentum of the black hole or the binary orbit, but that of the inner accretion flow. Bardeen–Petterson effect38 might have been at work to align the inclination of the inner accretion flow with that of the black hole spin, thus, in principle, can produce consistent position angles for distinct ejections.

In summary, since the discovery of superluminal, relativistic jets from Galactic stellar-mass black holes1, it has been accepted that jets from stellar-mass black holes are less relativistic than those from AGN3. However, our observations of 4U 1543−47 show jets from stellar-mass black holes can cover nearly the same range of the Lorentz factors observed in AGN jets, allowing us to probe a similarly extreme regime of relativistic jet ejections in accreting black holes of stellar mass scales as well. The small inferred inclination angle of the jets leads to a very high minimum Lorentz factor that would have been impossible to constrain and measure at larger inclination angles, given our astrometric precision limits. Considering that less than a dozen Galactic stellar-mass black holes have been observed superluminal, relativistic jets in the past few decades, this offers a natural explanation to why this is the first measurement of a Lorentz factor comparable to those seen in AGN that occurred for this source. The fact that these jets were observed with a small inclination angle would likely be compatible with a scenario in which a substantial fraction of the microquasar jets are this relativistic. Furthermore, 4U 1543−47, as an example, showed two full cycles of accretion-ejection processes during a single X-ray outburst; consistent jet position angles on different occasions indicate that those discrete jets may follow a direction fixed in time, for instance, the rotation axis of the black hole and/or the inner disk. Galactic stellar-mass black holes, therefore, allow us to observe full cycles of distinct accretion and jet ejections on human observing time scales to determine what properties persist and what properties vary, which is hardly achievable in observations of AGN.

Methods

X-ray observations and data reduction

MAXI and Swift/BAT monitoring observations

The daily and orbital MAXI and Swift/BAT X-ray monitoring light curves are publicly available. We obtained the MAXI light curves in the 2–4, 4–10, and 10–20 keV bands (http://maxi.riken.jp/top/slist.html), respectively. We also obtained the Swift/BAT 15–50 keV light curves (https://swift.gsfc.nasa.gov/results/transients/BlackHoles.html). We use these X-ray light curves (see Fig. 1) to identify source states and soft and hard X-ray flares. The decreases in MAXI flux from MJD 59,454 to 59,463, and from 59,381 to 59,390, were not used due to a MAXI detector structure problem and shadows by the MAXI detector structure, respectively.

Swift/XRT and NICER pointed observations

There are 30 successful Swift/XRT observations of 4U 1543−47 during the 2021–2023 outburst. 4U 1543−47 was also monitored intensely by NICER during its 2021–2023 outburst since June 12, 2021 (MJD 59,376), only one day after the MAXI discovery of the X-ray outburst. The data reduction is the same as those in the report29 on the radio vs. X-ray correlation of the compact jet. These X-ray measurements are used to identify X-ray states. Specifically, we found the source made a hard-to-soft state transition on MJD 59,376 when it was discovered entering the 2021–2023 outburst and entered and stayed in the X-ray intermediate state on MJD 59,453.

Radio observations and data reduction

MeerKAT observations and data reduction

As part of the MeerKAT/ThunderKAT project, which targets X-ray binaries, cataclysmic variables, supernovae, and gamma-ray bursts, we monitored the stellar-mass black hole transient 4U 1543−47 under the X-ray binary monitoring program with MeerKAT on about a weekly cadence between June 19, 2021, and March 9, 2023. During the MeerKAT campaign on both compact jets and transient jets in 4U 1543−47, data were taken in the 856–1712 MHz band (L-band) with a central frequency of 1.28 GHz. J1939−6342 was used to calibrate the absolute flux and bandpass, and J1501−3918 was used as the phase calibrator29. The detailed MeerKAT measurements of the compact jet emission at the core and the investigation of the compact jet physics are presented in ref. 29. In this study, we focus on the MeerKAT measurements of the ejected blobs of transient jets in 4U 1543−47. Standard data reduction procedures (i.e., flagging, calibration, applying solutions to data, and splitting target data) were done through the Common Astronomy Software Application package (CASA, version 5.6.2, hereafter CASA39). Then we flagged additional calibrated target data in CASA to exclude more bad data. Finally, we produced images in total intensity (Stokes I) using TCLEAN in CASA after performing phase self-calibration (calmode set to “p”) of the data. A multi-scale multi-frequency synthesis algorithm was carried out in the image deconvolution, while multi-frequency synthesis with nterms of 2 and a central frequency of 1.28 GHz were set. We used a Briggs weighting scheme with a robust parameter of 0 as a compromise between angular resolution and sensitivity. The size of the pixel of all images was set to 1.5 arcsec (cell = “1.5arcsec”) in TCLEAN. Flux densities and positions for the ejections at 1.28 GHz of 4U 1543−47 were measured through the CASA task IMFIT. Errors on flux densities were measured by taking the root-mean-square (RMS) noise of a close-by source-free region in each observation. Since the detection confidence of the ejected blobs at the central frequency (1.28 GHz) is not high (around 5σ), we simultaneously performed imaging and flux density detection on the source in two sub-bands in the 856–1284 MHz band and the 1284–1712 MHz band, respectively, to cross-check the authenticity of the source detection. The source detections and corresponding flux densities of the source were also cross-checked by both the dedicated oxkat semi-automated pipeline40 (step-by-step processing steps were executed sequentially) and the official pipeline images, all of which show consistency with our measurements as introduced above.

ATCA observations and data reduction

4U 1543−47 was observed by the Australia Telescope Compact Array (ATCA) on three dates during its 2021–2023 outburst, under project code CX501 (https://atoa.atnf.csiro.au/query.jsp; PI: T.D. Russell). Observations were performed on April 27, 2022, 17:37:00–22:03:40 UT (MJD 59,696.83 ± 0.09), May 28, 2022, 11:24:50–13:33:30 UT (MJD 59,727.52 ± 0.04), and June 12, 2022, 09:16:10–18:03:30 UT (MJD 59,742.57 ± 0.18). The data reduction is the same as reported in ref. 29. These results were used to search for potential ejected blobs and to monitor core jet emission.

Radio astrometry of the ejected blobs

We first detected a discrete blob that we associated with E1 at an angular separation of about 9″ from the core (at the source position) on September 5, 2021, with the 1.28 GHz image, and then clearly traced subsequent ejected blobs for E1 with monitoring observations to an angular separation of about 12″ from 4U 1543−47 over 60 days. The ejection displayed evidence of significant deceleration in the end. The latter angular separation translates to a projected separation of about 0.3 pc at the source distance of 5 kpc. Then, a month later, we discovered additional discrete blobs at a position closer to the core, which showed apparently higher proper motion than those of E1. This indicates an additional ejection (E2) happened. The ejection had remained detected until January 7, 2023, reaching an angular distance about 50″ away from 4U 1543−47, corresponding to about 1.2 pc at the source distance of 5 kpc. It is interesting to show the distance scale in gravitational radii. Considering a black hole mass of 10 M⊙, the projected separations of E1 and E2 reached about 3 × 1011 Rg (Rg, Schwarzchild radius) and about 1.2 × 1012 Rg, respectively. This indicates that both deceleration and collimation of the ejections extend to these radii.

To obtain accurate angular separations from the core for all radio-emitting blobs, we made use of their measured radio positions and the Gaia DR2 optical position of 4U 1543−47 (ICRF: [J2000] = 15h47m08.27667s, −47∘40′10.285″) as the position where the blobs originated. Absolute positions of the blobs in the radio band were measured by the CASA IMFIT task. We evaluated the systematic offset of each position of the discrete blobs in the framework of Gaia DR241, which provides the reference astrometry. The offset was obtained by subtracting the radio position of each epoch from the Gaia DR2 optical position (The International Celestial Reference Frame (ICRF) of (R.A., Dec.) [J2000] = (15h47m18.8419s, −47∘37′40.306″)) of a bright check source (hereafter BCS), which is about 3′ away from 4U 1543−47 with a stable radio flux density of about 1 mJy.

Instead of recording the statistical errors on R.A. and Dec. of the discrete blobs reported from the CASA IMFIT task, which are thought to underestimate the statistical errors, we estimated the statistical errors of R.A. and Dec. of the discrete blobs by calculating the corresponding size of synthesized beam in the direction of R.A. and Dec. divided by twice the signal-to-noise ratio in each observation. Similarly, we obtained the statistical errors on R.A. and Dec. for each observation for the BCS. Then we added in quadrature the statistical errors on R.A. and Dec. of both sources to get the positional errors for all the discrete blobs. All of the positional measurements are shown in Supplementary Information (Supplementary Table 1). Figure 2 shows the angular separation vs. time of all the discrete blobs we detected.

Position angle of the ejecta

Based on the errors on the R.A. and Dec. estimated above, we can also calculate the position angles for all the radio-emitting discrete blobs of the ejections, which are shown in Fig. 6. The fitted position angle of the blobs is 139.9∘ ± 0.3∘ East of North. Following the classification of ejected blobs as belonging to two ejections, E1 and E2 (see below), we obtained the position angles 141.1∘ ± 0.9∘ and 139.7∘ ± 0.3∘ East of North, for E1 and E2, respectively, suggesting that the two ejections were launched with consistent position angles. If E1 and E2 have identical paths, E1 would have swept the way for E2, allowing E2 to go further and faster at the same angular separation from its origin; it is also possible that E2 might have hit and passed through E1. Our measurements of the position angles of the ejections also indicate that episodic jets from the stellar-mass black hole had a surprisingly stable inclination angle during the outburst—a specific jet inclination angle is preferred by the two ejections from 4U 1543−47.

Distance estimation

Previous optical observations have resulted in a best estimate of the distance of 7.5 ± 0.5 kpc18 to the black hole X-ray binary through modeling optical light curves and spectra of the system, which improved from a previous estimate of 9.1 ± 1.1 kpc16. An independent estimate of the distance of the black hole X-ray binary can be derived from the parallax with a low significance of around 3.5σ (0.191 ± 0.055 mas) measured with Gaia, as listed in DR3. Assuming Bailer-Jones’ prior that relies on the Galactic stellar distribution, which black hole low mass X-ray binaries do not follow, a distance of 5 ± 2 kpc is derived42. While combining with the assumption of the prior that is derived based on the distribution of black hole low mass X-ray binaries19, a distance of 5−1.2+2.0 kpc (where a zero point correction has also been applied) is estimated (Fig. 7). The latter distance is consistent with the previous best-estimate, and can be used to infer conservatively how relativistic the jet ejections would be.

Proper motion of the ejecta and the modelings

Physical considerations

Proper motions of the ejecta can tell us about important properties of jets and the source environment. Modeling of the proper motion will tell us about how relativistic the jet is, as well as the jet’s inclination angle and launch time. In episodic jets of microquasars, each discrete blob corresponds to a forward-moving plasma clump that is launched from the source. A single ejection can interact with the ISM on its way forward, allowing us to detect them multiple times in both time and space sequences. It is worth noting that the following physical considerations are necessary to model the proper motion of the ejecta and helpful for understanding.

Firstly, an ejected blob will move further and further from the source position before it terminates. We shall call it the “going forward” rule. This means that the radio position of the same ejection at a later time is more distant from the source than its previous position. This helped us identify the occurrence of a new ejection E2 and distinguish E2 from E1 during the outburst of 4U 1543−47.

Secondly, any ejected blob can only move at most with a ballistic motion or decelerate due to its interactions with the ISM. We shall call this the “no acceleration” rule. No acceleration of any episodic jet ejecta has been observed before in microquasars. In the angular separation vs. time plot of the ejected blobs, acceleration or deceleration will be shown as a steepening or a flattening of the slope. This is the second reason why E2 must have occurred.

Thirdly, we should consider modeling the proper motions of the blobs with the least number of ejections possible. We shall call this the “least ejection” rule. This helps us get clues of jet physics with the most simplest interpretation but with the strongest constraints from the data. We started from single ejection models to fit the data, and found they are not possible to model the angular separation vs. time relation. It helps us to model the ejecta in the simplest way to reveal the primary physics from the ejecta’s proper motion. Notice that one can model every detection of ejecta as a single isolated ejection event, but clearly, it will not be physical.

Classification of ejections

With the above considerations in mind, we performed model fits to the angular separation vs. time relation shown in Figs. 2 and 8. At first, we tried to model the motion of the discrete blobs with a single ejection. Both “going forward” rule and “no acceleration” rule provide strong evidence against the single ejection scenario: (1) the blob detected on MJD 59,559, marked as blob A (see Fig. 8), is at an angular distance 9.48 ± 0.82 “from our source, significantly lower by 2.4σ than the averaged angular separations in the range 11.44–12.09 ” the ejection reached more than two months ago during MJD 59,510–59,526. In addition, alternative model fits suggest blob A significant deviate (3.7σ or more) from the trend of the previous blob movement (see below); (2) the blobs during MJD 59,559–59,782 show proper motion with a higher apparent speed than that formed by the discrete blobs detected before MJD 59,526 in the angular separation vs. time plot shown in Fig. 8, indicating the later discrete blobs, attributing to a single ejection, had a higher proper motion. Thus, those blobs we detected should come from more than one ejection.

The blobs corresponding to E1 and E2 are shown in red and blue filled circles, respectively. We investigated the detected discrete blobs to identify those associated with E1. By default, all discrete blobs in the group before MJD 59,526 are considered to be members of the same ejection (E1). The second discrete blob detected on MJD 59,470 seems to deviate from the adjacent detections by showing a larger angular separation. Our investigation shows that the second discrete blob and the core emission are not fully distinguished in the image, bringing complexity in the determination of source components and positions (Fig. 5). The position and flux density of the second blob were obtained by fixing a Gaussian at the Gaia position. Therefore, although the second discrete blob shows a deviation, it still likely belongs to E1.

Since blob A detected on MJD 59,559 is found at a smaller angular separation than the previous blobs, it should correspond to a new ejection from 4U 1543−47. Furthermore, blob A, B, and others detected in the period MJD 59,559–59,782 establish a steeper slope in the angular separation vs. time plot shown in Fig. 8. This demonstrates that these blobs should correspond to an additional ejection, namely E2, instead of E1. We noticed that there is a statistical possibility that blob B belongs to E1, but this would require: (1) E1 remained at a slow proper motion for nearly two months and (2) E2 did not hit the relatively slowly moving E1 from behind so that E2 could maintain its motion and (3) E2 could not be detected when blob B (the E1 blob) was detected. Therefore, blob B belonging to E2 is more reasonable. In summary, considering the rules for the ejection classification and various investigations of model fits (Supplementary Fig. 2 and Fig. 5), we associated all blobs detected before MJD 59,526 as due to E1 and those detected since MJD 59,559 as due to E2. Detailed model fits and additional MCMC tests suggest that the angular separation vs. time relation of all discrete blobs is very well explained with only two ejections—E1 and E2 from 4U 1543−47 (see below).

Distribution of the Lorentz factor with MCMC

We further investigated the jet motion for the ejections E1 and E2 with a simple constant-deceleration model (Eq. 1), where the parameters are the initial proper motion μ in mas/day and the deceleration (μ°) in (mas/day)2, as well as the ejection date tej. We used a Bayesian approach with MCMC (with the emcee44 Python library), adopting a flat prior on tej, μ and μ°. For E2, we left all the parameters free to vary, while for E1, we fixed the ejection date to MJD 59,376. In the MCMC, we used 100 walkers that sampled the parameter space for a total of 105 steps. The posterior corner plots for μ, μ° and tej are shown in Fig. 9.
