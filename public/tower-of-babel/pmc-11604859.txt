Distribution and association of road traffic accident with depression among Indian population aged 45 years and above: nested multilevel modelling analysis of nationally representative cross-sectional survey
Authors: Pritam Halder, Sayan Saha, Anshul Mamgai, Abhinav Chandra Sekhar Kolachala, Ankita Chattopadhyay, Shivani Rathor, Manish Chandra Prabhakar
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
IntroductionThe prevalence of important public health problems like road traffic accidents (RTA) and depression are surging. This study was aimed to estimate distribution and determine the association between RTA and depression among Indian population aged 45 years and above: overall and stratified into age group, gender and across states/union territories as aspirants, achievers, and front runners.MethodsUsing Longitudinal Aging Study in India (LASI) dataset (April 2017–December 2018), we have conducted this study among middle aged (45–59 years) and older adults and elderly (≥ 60 years) Indians. Bivariate analysis was conducted to estimate the prevalence of RTA and depression nationally and across aspirants, achievers, and front runner states. States and union territories were categorised as low, medium, and high as per RTA and depression prevalence, which were further cross tabulated. Spatial distribution maps were created using Microsoft Excel. We have documented the association of RTA with depression. To reduce the confounding effects of demographic and socioeconomic; health related and behavioural covariates; propensity score matching (PSM) was conducted. Nested multilevel regression modelling was analysed using STATA version 17.ResultsPrevalence of RTA was 1.84% (1.74–1.94) nationally, highest among achiever states [2.04% (1.82–2.30)]. Prevalence of depression was 6.08% (5.90–6.26) nationally, highest among aspirant states [7.02% (6.74–7.30)]. The adjusted odds of having RTA was significantly among depressed [aOR (95% CI) 1.76 (1.45–2.15)] than non-depressed participants; which was much higher among females [aOR (95% CI) 1.93 (1.43–2.62)] than in males [aOR (95%CI) 1.67 (1.29–2.16)] and much higher among middle aged [aOR (95%CI) 2.08 (1.63–2.65)]. Odds of RTA was highest across front runners [aOR (95%CI) 1.86 (1.26–2.72)] followed by aspirant states [aOR (95%CI) 1.79 (1.37–2.33)].ConclusionThis study established the positive association between depression and road traffic accidents among middle aged, older adults and elderly. Therefore, efforts must be taken to address mental health issues in them with proper policy implication more focused on females and middle aged. Front runner’s states should get the limelight followed by aspirant states.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s44192-024-00118-w.

Introduction

In India, older adults and elderly persons (60 years and above) constitute 8.6% of the total population (India Census 2011), which is projected to reach 19% by 2050 [1]. Depression is one of the most common illnesses in the older adults and elderly population [2]. Over the years several Indian studies on older adults and elderly population showed prevalence of depression is up to 37.8% [3]. The National Mental Health Survey reports that nearly 1 in 40 and 1 in 20 suffer from past and current depression, respectively and has higher rates of disability compared to other psychiatric illness. The 40–49 years age group had higher rates of depression of up to 3.6% [4]. Middle aged (> 60 years) had a higher lifetime (6.93%) and current (3.53%) prevalence of depressive disorders compared to younger population [5]. The prevalence of suicidal risk was also more in 40–49 age group posing a major public health concern. The NCRB 2022 data on suicide shows up to 4880 (2.9%) victims died due to road traffic accidents [6]. A study involving 16 States of India estimated that the prevalence of depression among Indian older adults and elderly population was 34.4% (95% CI 29.3–39.7) [7]. Among older adults and elderly people, chronic diseases, restricted mobility, bereavement, older adults and elderly abuse, isolation, and loss of income are major risk factors for depression, in addition to common risk factors in all age groups [8].

The annual statement released by the Ministry of Road transport and highways in 2022 reported a total number of 4,61,312 road accidents resulting in loss of 1,68,491 lives and causing injuries to 4,43,366 persons increasing by 11.9% from previous year. It also cites driver's lack of attention or distraction and panic stops being one of the causes. Age profile of the victims showed up to 25% of the fatalities occurred in ages 45 years and above. Uttar Pradesh has highest RTA fatality rate with 13.9% followed by Tamil Nadu (9.8%), Maharashtra (8.1%), Karnataka (6.82%) and Rajasthan (6.81%) [9].

Secondary to change in demography more older drivers are emerging and age related diseases and treatment negatively affect driving fitness [10]. Although scarce, there are enough case–control, prospective and retrospective studies to demonstrate the impact of depression on driving performance and risk of road traffic accidents [11]. The vehicular collision risk increases by four to five-fold in severely depressed patients [12]. They also demonstrate more driving violations and perform poorly in driving stimulators [13–15]. Positive association was also found between depression and aggressive and risky driving behaviour such as drunk driving especially in teens [16, 17]. Experimental studies correlated depression with cognitive and psychomotor functioning essential for driving. Contributing factors include slower steering rection time, reaction to steering speed, poor selective attention, poor stress tolerance, and reduced arousal. Increased substance use like reboxetine, mirtazapine and lond term usage of anti-depressant etc. and poor sleep in depressed patient also lead to road traffic accidents [18–20]. Risk of car crash injury was reported higher in untreated drivers having suicidal ideation. Hence road traffic injuries in depressed patient can be both intentional and unintentional [21]. However, all these studies are limited by low response rate and sample sizes, neglecting less serious collisions, underreporting and gaps in methodology. Age and severity are major factors moderation the relation between depression and road traffic accidents [11]. Also, in patients taking treatment, the unwanted effects of anti-depressants may adversely affect cognitive and psychomotor function, Hilton et al. [12] and Drummer and Yap, 2016 reported detection of antidepressants in 7.9% and benzodiazepines 7.0% in fatally injured Australian drivers [22]. Benzodiazepine and sedating antidepressant have been implicated in increasing the risk for car crashes. A 2015 review by Hill et al., reported 40% elevated risk of car accidents with antidepressants (OR = 1.40; 95%CI 1.18 to 1.66) [23].

Research on important public health topics, such as RTIs and mental health, is still lacking in India, as it is in the majority of LMICs. Thus, this study was conducted to unveil the curtain from this important public health problem aiming at further health policy implementation. Therefore, this study was conducted to estimate the prevalence of depression and road traffic accident (RTA) among Indian population aged 45 years and above; overall and stratified into age group, gender and across states/union territories as aspirants, achievers, and front runners. At the same time, this study also determines the association of road traffic accident with depression among Indian population aged 45 years and above; overall and stratified into age group, gender and across states/union territories as aspirants, achievers, and front runners.

Methods

Longitudinal aging study in India (LASI)

LASI-1st wave data from 35 Indian states and union territories (UTs) were used in the current research. In order to gather comprehensive data on the psychological, social, economic, and health components of ageing in India, this nationwide longitudinal study is being conducted. It was developed to fill the information vacuum about thorough survey data on ageing in India that is similar to data from other countries. The study was funded by the United Nations Population Fund, the Nation Institute on Ageing, and the Ministry of Health and Family Welfare of the Government of India. The Harvard T.H. Chan School of Public Health, the International Institute for Population Sciences, and the University of Southern California are collaborating on it. The demography, health, economy, and social factors are just a few of the important topics it focuses on. There were 73,000 adult Indians in LASI [24].

NITI Aayog calculates the Annual Health Index with the objective of tracking progress on health outcomes and health systems performance, developing healthy competition and encouraging cross learning among states and UTs. Based on their annual Health Index score, the States and UTs are classified into front-runner (top one-third), achiever (middle one-third) and aspirant (lowest one-third) [25].

Eligibility criteria

Participants more than equal to 45 years were included. Participants not able to provide answers of the questionnaire themselves and did not provide consent were excluded. Thus, the final sample size was 66,606 (Supplementary Figure S1).

The largest study of its kind in the world and the first in India, it assesses the scientific evidence from a variety of factors, including demographics, household economic status, mental health (depression and cognitive function), chronic and symptom-based health conditions, functional health, biomarkers, healthcare utilisation, family, social networks, social welfare programs, employment, satisfaction, and life standards. The survey aims to follow a representative sample of older adults every 2 years over the next 25 years. The sample size will be adjusted to account for attrition from migration, death, non-response, and non-reachable reasons [24].

Study instruments

Outcome variable

Road traffic accident was taken as outcome variable. “What is the cause of injury in the last 2 years”-was asked during interview. Participants answering “traffic accident” were included for analysis.

Explanatory variable

The explanatory variable of interest was depression. The Composite International Diagnostic Interview—Short Form (CIDISF) scale was used to assess individuals with diagnosable major depressive episodes (MDEs) [26]. It is a reliable, cost-effective, non-clinical survey instrument for diagnostic purposes that is comparable to the original CIDI scale. It accurately diagnose depression and a trustworthy structured instrument for use with experienced interviewers [27–29]. The Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Third Edition (DSM-III), has accepted it [30]. It was created as a non-clinical diagnostic tool for circumstances in which a clinician's clinical examination and assessment of a patient's mental health is impractical. Validation studies have shown that the CIDI-SF scale may accurately produce diagnostic categories for depressive episodes [31]. Comparability with other ageing research is improved by using CIDI-SF in LASI [32]. It is often utilised in comprehensive national surveys, like the World Mental Health Survey [29], the US National Health Interview Survey [33] and the Canadian National Population Health Survey [27].

In this study, CIDI-SF was translated into sixteen significant locoregional languages in accordance with the approved translation standards provided by the WHO [34] After survey and health investigators received training (over the course of five weeks), the translated LASI tool. As part of the fieldwork, it was pretested in each one of the regional languages. However, as they went outside the scope of this big research, the psychometric capabilities of the CIDI-SF were not extensively examined as part of this inquiry. The LASI national report and other sources give a more thorough explanation of the field survey implementation methods. In the LASI, people were classified as having probable depression (MDEs) if they checked yes to three or more of the CIDI-SF scale's seven symptoms of depression [24, 35].

Covariates

These variables were categorised into age-group in years (45–59, ≥ 60), gender (female, male), demographic and socio-economic, health related and behavioural factors. Under demographic and socioeconomic factors, the included factors were- education status (illiterate, less than primary. primary completed, middle completed, secondary school, higher secondary, and Diploma/graduate), current residence (urban, rural), current marital status (unmarried, in live-in/married, divorced/Widow/separated), monthly per-capita expenditure/MPCE quintile (poorest, poorer, middle, richer, richest), current health insurance (no, yes), current occupation (unemployed, professional and semi-professional- ‘legislators and senior officials, professionals, technicians and associate professionals’, clerical and skilled- ‘clerks, service workers and shopkeepers, skilled agriculture and fishery workers, craft and related trade worker, plant and machine operator’, unskilled). Under health-related and behavioural factors, the included factors were- current physical activity (everyday, once per week, 1–3 times per week, once per month, never), current self rated health (excellent, very good, good, fair, poor), tobacco consumption (no, yes) and alcohol consumption (no, yes) [35].

Statistical analysis

Data was analysed in Stata version 17 (StataCorp. 2017. Stata Statistical Software: Release 17. College Station, T X: Stata Corp LP.). For categorical data, the participant characteristics were expressed as frequencies and percentages. During the analysis, individual sample weights were taken into account. We have evaluated the chi-squared p-value. RTA and sleep disturbance were used to classify Indian states and union territories into three groups: low (0–33rd percentile), intermediate (34–66th percentile), and high (67–100th percentile). We have also documented the distribution across aspirants, achievers, and front runner states. We have used Microsoft Excel to create an Indian map that documents these categories. Additionally, we cross-tabulated them to create common categories. Spatial distribution maps were created using Microsoft Excel.

We have employed propensity score matching (PSM) to mitigate the confounding effects of variables. In order to demonstrate the connection between RTA and sleep disturbance, we used nested multilevel regression modelling. We have used total 4 models. In the Model-1, we have included depression. In the Model-2, Model-3, and Model-4; we have subsequently added age group and gender; demographic and socioeconomic factors; health related and behavioural factors, respectively after assessing the multicollinearity among explanatory variables using the VIF (Variance inflation factor). VIF > 5 indicates a high correlation and were omitted. (Self-related health and marital status had VIF > 5). Hence, all the covariates except these two were included in the final association. (Supplementary Table S1) We documented likelihood ratio, log-likelihood, pseudo R2, AIC: Akaike Information Criterion and BIC: Bayesian Information Criterion to estimate the best fit model [36]. We documented the association as per age group, gender and across aspirants, achievers, and front runner states. p < 0.05 were considered as statistically significant.

Results

The prevalence of road traffic accident (RTA) in overall population was 1.84%. More than half of population was female (53.40%). Prevalence of RTA was higher in male (2.67%) than female (1.12%). Almost half of the population were older adults and elderly (≥ 60 years). Road traffic accidents were seen more in participants aged 45–59 years (2.20%). Almost half (47.07%) of the participants were illiterate. Accidents were most prevalent among participants educated at least higher secondary (3.23%). Almost 3/4th participants were married or in live-in relationships with 2.02% accident prevalence. Among MPCE quintiles, prevalence of accident was highest (2.24%) in richest and lowest (1.26%) in poorest population. Only 2.33% population has health insurance; among them 2.69% had accident. More than half of the population was unemployed (51.32%) having the least prevalence of accident (1.38%). Professional and semi-professional participants had the highest prevalence of accident (3.07%). Majority of the participants never performed any physical activity. Among them, accident prevalence was least (1.64%). Participants with poor self-rated health had highest accident prevalence (2.37%). Tobacco abusing participants (2.25%) had more accident prevalence than the non-abusers (1.61%). Participants with history of alcohol consumption had higher (1.84%) accident prevalence than the non-consumers (1.66%). The prevalence of depression was 6.08% in overall population; higher in females (6.86%) than males (5.18%); higher in participants aged ≥ 60 years (6.41%). Discussion-

The prevalence of depression was 6.08% in overall population comparable to previous studies. The prevalence of accident was 30% lower among older adults and elderly (≥ 60 years) than middle aged (45–59 years). This may be because of the tendency of the older adults and elderly (≥ 60 years) to stay indoor when compared to middle aged (45–59 years) who still constitute the working population and are exposed to the risk of road traffic accidents. The prevalence of road traffic accident in overall population was 1.84%. Accident rates were lowest (1.26%) in the poorest and highest (2.24%) in the richest. On the other hand, impoverished persons are more likely to have bad road traffic health outcomes and are exposed to greater risk factors due to a variety of circumstances, including living in high-risk areas and situations. Furthermore, dangerous activities, such as those involving driving, are more prevalent in this population [37].

The odds of accident were significantly 76% higher among depressed participants than non-depressed, which was even higher in females (93%) and participants aged 45–59 years (1.08% or 2.08 times). Pictorial representation has been documented in Fig. 5. This may be because they are more likely than others to have accidents because of things like decreased focus and attention (often brought on by obsession with depressing or unsettling thoughts); anxiety, which frequently coexists with depression; irritability, agitation, or retardation; exhaustion from restless nights; and weakness from inadequate food intake. Furthermore, depressed individuals may take chances without thinking through the repercussions, attempt or make suicide gestures, and use alcohol or other substances that impair functioning as a coping mechanism for their suffering [38]. Females may have more depressed episodes because to hormonal disturbances. Neurotransmitter linked to mood disorders are impacted by the effects of estrogen and progesterone. The connection between female sex hormones and mood is further highlighted by disorders such as premenstrual dysphoric disorder (PMDD). Furthermore, there is a higher risk for perimenopausal and postmenopausal women who have experienced significant depression in the past [39]. Women are more likely to be diagnosed with depression due to gender inequality and increasing health-seeking behaviour [40]. Due to work-related stress, exhaustion, family obligations, unhealthy lifestyle, multitasking, middle-aged drivers may display hazardous behaviour when driving [41, 42].This was well documented in this study since participants abusing tobacco (2.25%) had prevalence of accidents higher than the non-abusers (1.61%), and those participants with history of alcohol consumption had higher (1.84%) accident prevalence than the non-consumers (1.66%).Fig. 5Pictorial abstract showing nested multilevel regression modelling: Model-3 (Adjusted odds ratio with 95% confidence interval) of road traffic accident and depression

Pictorial abstract showing nested multilevel regression modelling: Model-3 (Adjusted odds ratio with 95% confidence interval) of road traffic accident and depression

Madhya Pradesh, Uttar Pradesh, Punjab, West Bengal, Uttarakhand, Goa documented both high prevalence of depression and RTA. Empowered Action Group states like Madhya Pradesh and Uttar Pradesh have higher rates of depression prevalence due to socioeconomic inequality, urban rural disparity and a lack of adequate mental health infrastructure [43]. The higher rate of depression may be impacted by urbanisation, changes in lifestyle, and social stressors in Uttar Pradesh and West Bengal. Environmental challenges, migration, and isolation due to geography might be relevant in Uttarakhand [44]. Increasing population density, crowding of roads, improper management at traffic signals due to lack of trained manpower and infrastructure, over speeding, drink and drive, lack of safety awareness, rural kaccha/unpaved roads with overloaded vehicle might be some of the important cause of RTA in Punjab, Uttar Pradesh, West Bengal and Madhya Pradesh. Hilly narrow roads, tourism traffic in Uttarakhand might contribute to high prevalence of RTA [43, 44].

The odds of accidents among depressed participants were highest (86% more) in front runner states than non-depressed ones followed by aspirant states (79% more) with statistically significant difference. This could be due to the lack of social and mental support in front runner states owing to lack of joint families when compared to aspirant states. Professional and semi-professional participants had the highest prevalence of accident (3.07%). This in turn may be the result of work-related factors that contribute to depression, such as the use of skills, decision-making authority, psychological and physical demands, hours worked, irregular or regular work schedules, daytime or nighttime work schedules, social support from coworkers, supervisor, and family, job insecurity, recognition, promotion, and bullying [45–47].

Our study had significant strengths. It is based on the data sourced from the first largest, and most thorough survey in India on older adults and the present study attempts to provide countrywide distribution and association of depression and RTA. It is based innovative large-scale survey methodology, internationally comparable survey instruments, and standardised research and quality control standards that are not usually found in other studies. To identify depression as a serious mental health issue with substantial state and socio-economic patterns and state-specific policy implications, this study relies heavily on the representativeness of data at the national, state, and socio-economic spectrum, which in term leads to higher generalisability. Additionally, the present study is based on robust econometric technique of nested multilevel modelling which attempts to identify the most significant predictors of the association between depression and RTA. Coming to limitations, due to the cross-sectional study design, causality could not be established. Recall bias and social desirability bias were not completely excluded due to self-reporting style of questionnaire. We recommend evidence-based community level large scale trials with regular follow-ups to strengthen our findings.

Policy implication and recommendation

The state-funded government psychiatric hospitals and nursing homes; private psychiatric hospitals and nursing homes; non-government organisations; and the most significant, unofficial source—family members serving as caregivers—are the four categories of resources available in India to treat senior mental health difficulties. However, rather than focusing on the development of basic care or rehabilitative programmes, the focus of mental health treatment in India remains on tertiary care and acute management. Additionally, the scientific community has not given much attention to RTA and mental health research because to a lack of focus on national policies and financing possibilities, particularly in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) [48].

Only selected groups, such as the Association of Gerontology, the Geriatric Society of India, the Indian Academy of Geriatrics, and the Indian Association for Geriatric Mental Health, engage in advocacy and research. To address this issue, government programmes should teach peripheral health professionals, include medical schools and regular camps in identifying mental health difficulties in older individuals and the older adults and elderly, and facilitate successful referrals between them and psychiatrists. To protect the mental well-being of middle aged and the older adults and elderly, certain new projects, including day care centres, old age residential homes, counselling, memory clinics, helplines, and recreational facilities, may be implemented in both rural and urban settings [49].

Conclusion

Available evidence established the positive association between depression and road traffic accidents among middle aged and older adults and elderly. Therefore, efforts must be taken to address mental health issues more road safety specially for females and participants aged 45–59 years (middle aged). Policy implications specially catered to this age group should be implemented to bridge this gap in public health more focused on front runners followed by aspirant states.
