Abstract
The P 3 \text{P}{{}^{3}} T scheme is a hybrid method for simulating gravitational N N -body systems. It combines a fast particle-tree (PT) algorithm for long-range forces with a high-accuracy particle-particle (PP, direct N N -body) solver for short-range interactions. Preserving both PT efficiency and PP accuracy requires a robust PT-PP switching criterion. We introduce a simple free-fall-based switching criterion for general stellar systems, alongside the commonly used velocity-dispersion-based ( σ \sigma -based) criterion. Using the petar code with the P 3 \text{P}{{}^{3}} T scheme and slow-down algorithmic regularization for binaries and higher-order multiples, we perform extensive simulations of star clusters to evaluate how each criterion affects energy conservation and binary evolution. For systems in virial equilibrium, we find that the free-fall-based criterion is generally more accurate for low- σ \sigma or loose clusters containing binaries, whereas the σ \sigma -based criterion is better suited for high- σ \sigma systems. Under subvirial or fractal initial conditions, both criteria struggle to maintain high energy conservation; however, the free-fall-based criterion improves as the tree timestep is reduced, whereas the σ \sigma -based degrades due to its low-accuracy treatment of two-body encounters.

I
Introduction

Star-by-star
N
-body simulations of collisional stellar systems, such as star clusters, are challenging due to their multiple timescales. “Collisional” refers to systems where close encounters between stars significantly influence their dynamical evolution
(
6
, e.g.,)
.
Two timescales control the long-term dynamical evolution of such systems.
The crossing timescale
t_{\mathrm{c}}
is the time required for an object to cross the system.
For star clusters, the half-mass crossing time
t_{\mathrm{ch}}
is typically
1-10
Myr
(
11
;
29
)
.
For accurate simulations, the integration time step must be much shorter than
t_{\mathrm{c}}
.
The two-body relaxation timescale
t_{\mathrm{r}}
is the time interval over which significant orbital changes for an object result from interactions with surrounding objects.
For star clusters, the half-mass relaxation time
t_{\mathrm{rh}}
is about
10^{2}-10^{4}
Myr
(
11
;
29
)
; and old clusters, such as globular clusters (GCs) in the Milky Way, have undergone at least one
t_{\mathrm{rh}}
.
Simulating a cluster’s full lifetime thus requires numerous integration steps to cover several
t_{\mathrm{rh}}
and resolve individual stellar motions within one
t_{\mathrm{ch}}
.
Additionally, star formation often produces many binaries, triples, and higher-order multiples
(
27
)
, where orbital periods range from days to Myrs.
Resolving the evolution of the shorest-period binaries demands extremely small integration steps
(
12
)
, making global simulations computationally intensive.

To address extreme multi-timescale challenges, several schemes have been developed.
In direct
N
-body (particle-particle; PP) methods such as the state-of-the-art code
nbody6

(
1
)
, block time steps are used to bridge the gap between
t_{\mathrm{c}}
and
t_{\mathrm{r}}
.
Objects in dense regions with frequent short-range interactions use smaller time steps for accuracy, while those in sparse regions use larger steps to save computation.
With block time steps and without considering multiples, the computational cost is about
O(N^{3})
per
t_{\mathrm{rh}}

(
26
)
.

For massive stellar systems like GCs, even block-time steps remain computationally intensive. To improve efficiency, the Ahmad-Cohen neighbor scheme
(
2
)
and hybrid methods have been developed.
Both approaches divide interactions into long-range and short-range forces using a neighbor radius.
Since long-range gravitational forces are much weaker than short-range ones, they can be approximated with a minor impact on overall accuracy.
The AC neighbor scheme uses larger time steps for long-range forces while retaining direct
N
-body algorithms.
This method is implemented in
nbody6

(
1
)
.

In contrast, hybrid methods employ faster approximate algorithms with a fixed time step
\Delta t_{\mathrm{soft}}
, such as Barnes-Hut particle-tree (PT)
(
3
;
28
;
20
;
18
)
, particle-mesh
(
16
)
and the fast multiple method
(
9
)
, for long-range interactions. This work focuses on the hybrid
\text{P}{{}^{3}}
T method that combines the PP and PT approaches.
Unlike the
O(N^{2})
scaling of the full force calculations in the PP method, the PT method achieves greater efficiency with an
O(N\log N)
scaling.

21
introduced a parallelization framework for developing particle simulation codes (
fdps
) based on the
\text{P}{{}^{3}}
T scheme, and
31
developed the
petar
code based on
fdps
.

petar
also incorporates the slow-down algorithm regularization (
sdar
) package to address timescale issues in multiple systems
(
33
)
.
These codes (
nbody6
series and
petar
) have been widely used to study the dynamical evolution of star clusters.

The
\text{P}{{}^{3}}
T method introduces a changeover region with inner and outer radii (
r_{\mathrm{in}}
and
r_{\mathrm{out}}
) to smoothly transition between PP and PT treatments as objects approach each other
(
28
)
.
The original method used a universal changeover region for all objects.
Subsequently,
31
proposed mass-dependent changeover radii for stellar systems, and
18
applied a variant to planetesimal systems in which the radii depend on mass, distance to the central star, and local velocity dispersion.

Properly choosing the changeover radii
r_{\mathrm{in}}
and
r_{\mathrm{out}}
, and the tree timestep
\Delta t_{\mathrm{soft}}
, is essential for a smooth transition and physically accurate results.
Previous studies have examined how these parameters affect energy errors in star clusters
(
20
;
31
)
and planetesimal systems
(
28
;
19
;
18
)
.

For spherically symmetric clusters,
20
found that energy errors depend on
\theta
and
\Delta t_{\mathrm{soft}}/r_{\mathrm{out}}\sigma
, where
\sigma
is the global 3D velocity dispersion.
petar
adopts this
\sigma

r_{\mathrm{out}}
and
\Delta t_{\mathrm{soft}}

(
31
)
.
However, it remains unclear whether this criterion applies to more complex, non-spherical stellar systems or star clusters with binaries, as this has not been thoroughly investigated.
Such complex systems are crucial for understanding early star cluster evolution and binary orbital dynamics.

We present a new, simple free-fall-based criterion that can be applied for general stellar systems and compare it to existing
\sigma
-based criteria. Section
II
describes the
\text{P}{{}^{3}}
T method and the
N
-body code
petar
. Section
III
introduces the free fall-based criterion, while Section
IV
compares both criteria across isolated binaries; spherical star clusters with equal/variable masses; clusters with primordial binaries and different virial ratios; and non-spherical clumpy clusters. Section
V
discusses limitations and potential improvements. Finally, Section
VI
summarizes our findings.

II
\text{P}{{}^{3}}
T method and
N
-body code

In this work, we use the
petar
code which combines the
\text{P}{{}^{3}}
T approach with the
sdar
method for numerical simulations
(
31
)
. Specifically,
petar
combines three integration methods:

•

The PT method computes long-range forces using a second-order symplectic leapfrog integrator.

•

The PP method uses a fourth-order Hermite integrator with block time steps for short-range forces on stars and centers-of-mass of multiples.

•

The
sdar
method
(
31
)
integrates multiple systems, including hyperbolic encounters, binaries, and hierarchical few-body systems.

As our focus is the criterion for switching between the PT and PP methods, we begin by introducing the underlying mathematics and defining the relevant parameters.

The
\text{P}{{}^{3}}
T method is done via the Hamiltonian splitting for a system with
N
particles
(
28
, e.g.,)
:

\displaystyle H_{\mathrm{hard}}=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(\frac{p_{i}^{2}}{2m_{i}}-\sum_{i<j}^{N}\frac{Gm_{i}m_{j}}{r_{ij}}W(r_{ij})\right)

(1)

\displaystyle H_{\mathrm{soft}}=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1,i<j}^{N}\frac{Gm_{i}m_{j}}{r_{ij}}\left[W(r_{ij})-1\right].

where
H_{\mathrm{hard}}
indicates the short-range interactions and kinetic energies and
H_{\mathrm{soft}}
indicates the long-range interaction;
p_{i}
and
m_{i}
are, respectively, the momenta and mass of the particle
i
;
r_{ij}
is the separation between the
i
and
j
particles;
G
is the gravitational constant;
W(r_{ij})
is a changeover function to smoothly transfer pieces between
H_{\mathrm{hard}}
and
H_{\mathrm{soft}}
.

petar
uses a mass-dependent eighth-order polynomial changeover function for each particle.
The polynomial order significantly influences the accuracy of chaotic solutions
(
14
;
13
)
. An eighth-order choice guarantees smoothness through the third derivatives of the forces in the fourth-order Hermite method.
The corresponding potential changeover function can be described as

W(x)=\begin{cases}\beta(1-2x)&(x\leq 0)\\
\beta(1-2x)-1+f(x)&(0<x<1)\\
0&(x\geq 1)\\
\end{cases}

(2)

where

\displaystyle f(x)

\displaystyle=1+\beta x^{5}\left(14-28x+20x^{2}-5x^{3}\right)

(3)

\displaystyle x

\displaystyle=\frac{r_{ij}-r_{\mathrm{in},ij}}{r_{\mathrm{out},ij}-r_{\mathrm{in},ij}}

\displaystyle\beta

\displaystyle=\frac{r_{\mathrm{out},ij}-r_{\mathrm{in},ij}}{r_{\mathrm{out},ij}+r_{\mathrm{in},ij}}.

Here
r_{\mathrm{in},ij}
and
r_{\mathrm{out},ij}
denote the inner and outer boundaries of the changeover function for each object pair
i
and
j
.
To ensure that the force changeover function ranges from 1 to 0,
W(x)\neq 1
for
x\leq 0
, see
31
for details.

With a reference value
r_{\mathrm{in}}
set as a simulation parameter, each object has

r_{\mathrm{in},i}=\left(\frac{m_{i}}{\langle m\rangle}\right)^{1/3}r_{\mathrm{in}}

(4)

where
m_{i}
is the mass of object
i
and
\langle m\rangle
is the average mass. The
1/3
exponent reflects the scaling from tidal forces. The pairwise radius is then defined as the maximum of the two radii:

r_{\mathrm{in},ij}=\max\left(r_{\mathrm{in},i},r_{\mathrm{in},j}\right).

(5)

The ratio
r_{\mathrm{in}}/r_{\mathrm{out}}
(
r_{\mathrm{in},ij}/r_{\mathrm{out},ij}
) is fixed for all objects. In our simulations, we set
r_{\mathrm{in}}/r_{\mathrm{out}}=0.1
, leaving only
r_{\mathrm{in}}
to be determined for the switching criterion.

For time steps in the
\text{P}{{}^{3}}
T method, the PT part uses a shared time step
\Delta t_{\mathrm{soft}}
, while the PP part assigns each particle an individual time step based on
(
1
;
28
)
:

\displaystyle\Delta t_{\mathrm{hard},i}=

\displaystyle\min\left(\eta\sqrt{\frac{\sqrt{|\bm{A}^{(0)}_{i}|^{2}+A^{2}_{0}}\,|\bm{A}^{(2)}_{i}|+|\bm{A}^{(1)}_{i}|^{2}}{|\bm{A}^{(1)}_{i}|\,|\bm{A}^{(3)}_{i}|+|\bm{A}^{(2)}_{i}|^{2}}},\right.

(6)

\displaystyle\left.\Delta t_{\mathrm{hard,max}}\right),

where
\bm{A}^{(0)}_{i}
is the acceleration of particle
i
;
\bm{A}^{(j)}_{i}
is its
j
-th time derivative;
A_{0}
is a scalar smoothing parameter (with units of acceleration) to prevent unnecessarily small steps; and
\Delta t_{\mathrm{hard,max}}
is the maximum time step.
In our simulations, we set
\Delta t_{\mathrm{hard,max}}=\Delta t_{\mathrm{soft}}
to smoothly couple the PT and PP methods, adopt
\eta=0.1
as a standard value
(
1
, e.g.,)
, and define
A_{0}=G\langle m\rangle/r_{\mathrm{out}}^{2}
as the scalar acceleration from a mass
\langle m\rangle
at distance
r_{\mathrm{out}}
.

To switch on the
sdar
method for a multiple system, we select members using a mass-dependent radial criterion
r_{\mathrm{g}}
.
In this work,
r_{\mathrm{g}}
is uniform for all particles and satisfies
r_{\mathrm{g}}<r_{\mathrm{in}}
.
The switching rule also considers velocities and external perturbations. For a two-body
sdar
system on a hyperbolic orbit, if the radial relative velocity is high enough that the predicted next-step separation exceeds
r_{\mathrm{g}}
while the current separation is
>0.2~r_{\mathrm{g}}
, the system is terminated. An
sdar

sdar
system.

III
Free-fall-based switching criterion

III.1
Criterion for two-body system

Because both
r_{\mathrm{in}}
and
\Delta t_{\mathrm{soft}}
are free parameters, a linking criterion is needed to preserve efficiency and accuracy in the
\text{P}{{}^{3}}
T scheme.
Based on numerical experience,
20
proposed the
\sigma
-based criterion:

\Delta t_{\mathrm{soft}}=\alpha\frac{r_{\mathrm{in}}}{\sigma},

(7)

where
\alpha
is a user-defined coefficient.
It is intended for spherically symmetric stellar systems.
A limitation is that
\sigma
must be measured for the specified initial condition. For systems with few objects or irregular shapes,
\sigma
is difficult to determine.

To develop a more general switching criterion, we begin by integrating a simple binary system using the
\text{P}{{}^{3}}
T method.
In this case, the PT part functions as a lower-accuracy (second-order leapfrog) PP method, unaffected by the tree-force approximation.
To simplify, we used only the Hermite integrator in the PP part to model the binary orbit, thus avoiding the complexity of including the
sdar
method.
With only two objects, we denote the changeover radii as
r_{\mathrm{in}}
and
r_{\mathrm{out}}
without “
i
” and “
j
”.

Figure 1:
Free-fall-based switching criterion: set
\Delta t_{\mathrm{soft}}
to
1/n_{\mathrm{s}}
of the circular binary period with semi-major axis
r_{\mathrm{in}}
.

As demonstrated in Figure
1
, if the binary orbit is within their changeover region, both the PT and PP methods are used to integrate the orbit.
As the separation between the two components passes through the changeover region, a reasonable criterion should ensure that the PT part with a given
\Delta t_{\mathrm{soft}}
matches the accuracy of the PP part.
For the PT leapfrog integrator, it is necessary to set
\Delta t_{\mathrm{soft}}
much less than the binary period to ensure accuracy.
Thus, the maximum time step
\Delta t_{\mathrm{soft,max}}
can be described as
P/n_{\mathrm{s}}
, where
n_{\mathrm{s}}
and
P
are the minimum number of time steps per orbit and the binary period, respectively.
In the changeover region, a circular orbit in the inner boundary with the semi-major axis
a
equal to
r_{\mathrm{in}}
has the shortest period
P_{\mathrm{in}}
that requires the leapfrog method to handle.
Thus,
P_{\mathrm{in}}/n_{\mathrm{s}}
gives
\Delta t_{\mathrm{soft,max}}
for all binary orbits crossing changeover region. The resulting switching criterion of the
\text{P}{{}^{3}}
T method is

\Delta t_{\mathrm{soft}}=\frac{P_{\mathrm{in}}}{n_{\mathrm{s}}}=\frac{2\pi}{n_{\mathrm{s}}}\sqrt{\frac{r_{\mathrm{in}}^{3}}{G\left(m_{1}+m_{2}\right)}},

(8)

where
m_{1}
and
m_{2}
are masses of binary components.
Using this criterion, either
\Delta t_{\mathrm{soft}}
or
r_{\mathrm{in}}
can be calculated if the other is known.
Since the criterion formula resembles the free-fall time at scale
r_{\mathrm{in}}
, we call it the ”free-fall-based” criterion.

The above analysis considers only the circular binary case with
a=r_{\mathrm{in}}
. For eccentric binaries or hyperbolic encounters with pericenter separation
r_{\mathrm{peri}}=r_{\mathrm{in}}
, the period or encounter timescale is longer than
P_{\mathrm{in}}
, but the velocity at
r_{\mathrm{peri}}
exceeds the circular velocity.
Thus, achieving an integration accuracy comparable to the circular case requires a larger
n_{\mathrm{s}}
.

For eccentric orbits or hyperbolic encounters, we can use the pericenter-passage timescale
t_{\mathrm{peri}}
instead of
P_{\mathrm{in}}
to derive a new criterion.
The timescale
t_{\mathrm{peri}}
is defined as

t_{\mathrm{peri}}=2\pi\frac{r_{\mathrm{peri}}}{v_{\mathrm{peri}}}=2\pi\sqrt{\frac{r_{\mathrm{peri}}^{3}}{G(m_{1}+m_{2})(1+e)}}.

(9)

With
r_{\mathrm{in}}=r_{\mathrm{peri}}
, we obtain
t_{\mathrm{peri}}=P_{\mathrm{in}}/\sqrt{1+e}
.
Therefore, replacing
P_{\mathrm{in}}
with
t_{\mathrm{peri}}
in Equation
8
introduces only a factor of
1/\sqrt{1+e}
:

\Delta t_{\mathrm{soft}}=\frac{t_{\mathrm{peri}}}{n_{\mathrm{s}}}=\frac{2\pi}{n_{\mathrm{s}}}\sqrt{\frac{r_{\mathrm{in}}^{3}}{G\left(m_{1}+m_{2}\right)(1+e)}}.

(10)

Since
v_{\mathrm{peri}}\propto\sqrt{(1+e)/|1-e|}
, the largest energy error occurs near
e\sim 1
. In this limit, Equation
10
and
8
differ by only a factor of
1/\sqrt{2}
, so Equation
8
is a reasonable criterion for all cases, and we adopt it for the free-fall-based criterion. However, the numerical tests in the following section show that maintaining high energy conservation at
e\sim 1
remains challenging.

III.3
Multi-mass criterion for
N
-body systems

Ignoring the environment perturbations, a pair force between nearby objects
i
and
j
in an
N
-body system like star clusters can be approximated as a Keplerian orbit.
Thus, the free-fall-based switching criterion for
\text{P}{{}^{3}}
T method can be extended to
N
-body systems.

In an
N
-body system, each object may have a different mass and changeover radii.
For the free-fall-based criterion in Equation
8
, we consider a pair of objects with masses
m_{i}
and
m_{j}
and replace
r_{\mathrm{in}}
with
r_{\mathrm{in},ij}
(Equation
4
and
5
), yielding

\displaystyle\Delta t_{\mathrm{soft}}=\frac{P_{\mathrm{in}}}{n_{\mathrm{s}}}=

\displaystyle\frac{2\pi}{n_{\mathrm{s}}}\sqrt{\frac{\max{(m_{i},m_{j})}}{\langle m\rangle}\frac{r_{\mathrm{in}}^{3}}{G\left(m_{i}+m_{j}\right)}}

(11)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{2\pi}{n_{\mathrm{s}}}\sqrt{\frac{r_{\mathrm{in}}^{3}}{2G\langle m\rangle}}

In particular,
\Delta t_{\mathrm{soft}}
reaches its maximum value when
m_{i}=m_{j}
, becoming independent of the individual masses. We therefore adopt this maximum value as the universal criterion for the entire stellar system.

IV.5
Spherical star clusters with different virial ratios

During the early star-forming phase, star clusters are often not in virial equilibrium. The virial ratio
Q=E_{\mathrm{k}}/\left|E_{\mathrm{V}}\right|
, with
E_{\mathrm{k}}
and
E_{\mathrm{V}}
as the total kinetic and potential energies, indicates the system’s virial state.
Clusters collapse when
Q<0.5
(subvirial) and expand when
Q>0.5
(supervirial).
In collapsing systems, the initial velocity dispersion
\sigma
is low and quickly rises as the cluster re-equilibrates. If the
\sigma
-based criterion uses the initial
\sigma
to set
r_{\mathrm{in}}
, it may underestimate
r_{\mathrm{in}}
in these cases, whereas the free-fall-based criterion, being independent of
\sigma
, is less affected.

V
Discussion

We introduce the free-fall-based criterion for the
\text{P}{{}^{3}}
T scheme, but this approach can be extended to other hybrid methods that combine accurate short-range and fast approximate long-range integrators. The
n_{\mathrm{s}}
value may vary depending on the choice of integrator. For instance, using higher-order symplectic methods for the PT part or alternative Hamiltonian splittings may reduce the required
n_{\mathrm{s}}
when
\theta
is small.

For the
\text{P}{{}^{3}}
T method, neither the free-fall-based nor the
\sigma
-based criterion matches the accuracy of Hermite with adaptive time steps at optimized
\Delta t_{\mathrm{soft}}
, as shown in Figures
10
and
13
. This indicates that increasing the step control parameter
\eta
can enhance computing performance for Hermite (PP part) at the cost of some accuracy when strict energy conservation is not essential.

In addition, both PT and PP integrations use double-precision (64-bit) in our simulations, while the default in
petar
assigns single precision (32-bit) to PT and double precision to PP for greater efficiency. Using single precision for PT limits
\epsilon
to about
10^{-6}
due to roundoff errors, making Hermite’s higher accuracy of
10^{-8}
to
10^{-7}
unattainable for the PT part. Thus, increasing
\eta
to align Hermite’s accuracy with the
10^{-6}
threshold has minimal impact on global
\epsilon
. As shown by
7
, this accuracy is sufficient for statistical equivalence with more precise simulations for triple systems, and most studies of collisional star clusters adopt an energy error limit of
10^{-6}
.

Alternatively, when high accuracy is required for simulations, the PT component’s precision can be enhanced by using a more accurate integrator in place of leapfrog rather than reducing
\Delta t_{\mathrm{soft}}
. This approach can boost performance and may resolve accuracy issues in modeling fractal and subvirial stellar systems.

We have examined both criteria for various stellar systems. For star clusters with a Plummer model and equal-mass distribution, we investigate the different
N
up to
10^{5}
. In fact, the
\text{P}{{}^{3}}
T method offers greater benefits for larger
N
, improving both performance—thanks to
O(N\log N)
scaling—and accuracy. As
N
increases, the two-body relaxation timescale increases and the PT method’s force accuracy improves because the long-range gravitational potential becomes smoother, resulting in more accurate long-range forces
(
15
)
. Therefore, we have explored more challenging scenarios for the
\text{P}{{}^{3}}
T method.

The two criteria are compared with fixed parameters in most simulations:
n_{\mathrm{s}}=64
for the free-fall-based criterion and
\alpha=0.2
for the
\sigma
-based criterion. While increasing these parameters can improve accuracy in challenging cases, as seen in the subvirial simulations (Figure
18
), we did not explore this further, focusing instead on how each criterion performs across different stellar systems with a fixed parameter set.

VI
Conclusion

We propose a free-fall-based criterion (Equation
8
and
11
) to set the changeover radii and the global tree time step
\Delta t_{\mathrm{soft}}
for the
\text{P}{{}^{3}}
T
N
-body algorithm, ensuring sufficient accuracy when switching between PT and PP methods.
Unlike the
\sigma
-based criterion (Equation
7
), this criterion is easier to evaluate, requires no measurement of
\sigma
, and thus is more versatile, particularly for systems with binaries or for low-
\sigma
, subvirial, and irregular systems where
\sigma
is difficult to accurately determine.

We test both criteria across a range of astrophysically relevant stellar systems, including spherical star clusters with equal-mass stars, the Kroupa IMF, primordial binaries, various virial ratios, and fractal star clusters. Overall, we find that the free-fall-based criterion with
n_{\mathrm{s}}=64
yields better accuracy and error scaling with respect to
\Delta t_{\mathrm{soft}}
than the
\sigma
-based criterion with
\alpha=0.2
for
N=1000
.
For high-
\sigma
systems, such as spherical star clusters with
N=10000
and
r_{\mathrm{h}}=1
pc, or more massive systems like GCs and NSCs, the
\sigma
-based criterion is more accurate due to larger
r_{\mathrm{in}}
for a given
\Delta t_{\mathrm{soft}}
. In a virial equilibrium system, the transition timestep at which the two criteria yield the same
r_{\mathrm{in}}
satisfies the simple relation
\Delta t_{\mathrm{soft}}/t_{\mathrm{ch}}\propto 1/N
. In practice, the two criteria can be combined to cover the full range of densities.

For spherical star clusters with equal-mass stars, both criteria yield similar
\epsilon
when
\Delta t_{\mathrm{soft}}
exceeds the optimal value (Figure
10
and
13
). However, at smaller
\Delta t_{\mathrm{soft}}
, especially for low-
\sigma
system like
N=1000
, the free-fall-based criterion achieves higher accuracy, while the
\sigma
-based criterion worsens, as its changeover radii are too small to capture close encounters (Figure
11
). For
N\geq 10^{5}
with
r_{\mathrm{h}}=1
pc, the
\sigma
-based criterion has better accuracy for most acceptable
\Delta t_{\mathrm{soft}}
.

For opening angles
\theta\leq 0.1
, accuracy is nearly independent of
\theta
(Figure
13
), making the PT method indistinguishable from the direct
N
-body approach (
\theta=0
) in this range, consistent with
15
. However, this may change when
N
or the PT group size differs.

For spherical star clusters with the Kroupa IMF, both criteria yield higher
\epsilon
at the same
\Delta t_{\mathrm{soft}}
(Figure
15
). The wider stellar mass range makes the accuracy harder to achieve and increases the stochastic variations in
\epsilon
, though the overall trend remains similar to the case of equal mass.

For spherical clusters with the Kroupa IMF and primordial binary model, the free-fall-based criterion shows a significant advantage in ensuring the integration accuracy of wide binaries in a low-density clusters (Figure
16
). In this case,
\sigma
can have a large variation depending on whether binaries are resolved. In the unresolved case, the
r_{\mathrm{in}}
is too small so that wide binaries with
a>10^{-3}
AU are not included in the accurate
sdar
method, thus having large errors after only 1.2 Myr of simulations.

For spherical clusters with subvirial initial conditions, the collapse poses challenges for energy conservation with both criteria. Reducing
\Delta t_{\mathrm{soft}}
improves errors for the free-fall-based criterion, but not for the
\sigma
-based criterion, as the initially low
\sigma
leads to an underestimated
r_{\mathrm{in}}
.

Fractal initial conditions are the most challenging for both criteria. Reducing
\Delta t_{\mathrm{soft}}
can improve the free-fall-based criterion, but gains are stochastic, while the
\sigma
-based criterion benefits little. This reflects the complex evolution of clumpy structures toward virial equilibrium.

Future work should examine whether replacing leapfrog in the PT part with a more accurate integrator improves accuracy under subvirial and fractal conditions.
