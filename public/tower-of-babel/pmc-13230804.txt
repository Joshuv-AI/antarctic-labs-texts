Forecasting toxic metal concentrations in an inland sea ecosystem with machine learning algorithms
Authors: Aylin Ucan, Nihat Tak, Asli Hocaoglu-Ozyigit, Ibrahim Ilker Ozyigit
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In recent years, statistical and data-driven modeling approaches have been increasingly employed to predict element concentrations and to examine relationships among environmental features. In this context, the integration of feature selection techniques with machine learning models enhances model generalization and reduces model complexity by enabling the identification of key elements that are strongly associated with the target feature. This study applies machine learning models to investigate the relationships between Aluminum (Al) and other elements and to predict Al concentration levels in an inland marine ecosystem. Specifically, the study evaluates whether accurate predictions can be achieved using a reduced subset of informative elements rather than the full feature set. The findings demonstrate that machine learning methods, when combined with feature selection, can successfully predict Al concentrations while yielding more interpretable models based on a limited number of significant elements.

Introduction

Nowadays, the environment is confronting many significant pollution challenges, particularly due to the escalating prevalence of human-induced activities. Rapid industrialization, population growth, urbanization, and irregular use of natural resources have contaminated essential environmental components, including water, air, and soil. This has led to the disruption of ecological balances1,2. One of the most notable forms of pollution is the accumulation of toxic metals/metalloids in the environment, which results in irreversible damage. Toxic metals are defined as elements that are toxic to living organisms at concentrations above a certain threshold, and which pose a permanent health risk due to their accumulation in biological systems. Toxic metals/metalloids that cause permanent effects, especially in aquatic environments, include arsenic (As), aluminum (Al), cadmium (Cd), chromium (Cr), lead (Pb), and mercury (Hg), The accumulation and distribution of toxic metals in saltwater ecosystems is a complex phenomenon3. Human activities, including industrial operations, agriculture, urbanization, and mining, contribute to the increased accumulation of heavy metals in these environments4. Through their participation in chemical reactions, these metals are activated by oxidation and dissolution, entering the food chain and causing irreversible damage to human health and water quality. A more profound comprehension of the accumulation and consequences of toxic metals in saltwater ecosystems is imperative for the sustainable management of marine environments5,6.

Among these toxic metals, Al is the third most abundant element in the Earth’s crust, after silicon and oxygen, and is transported to aquatic environments through both natural processes and anthropogenic activities7,8. Geological processes, especially weathering, and erosion of rocks, transport Al through soils and sediments into aquatic ecosystems such as oceans, seas, lakes, and rivers. In addition, volcanic activities and wind-borne soil particles also play important roles in the transfer of Al to water through natural sources. Anthropogenic sources refer to Al released into nature as a result of human activities. Al is transported to the marine ecosystem, especially as a result of mining and agricultural activities, industrial waste, burning of fossil fuels, and maritime transportation. Chemical fertilizers and pesticides from agricultural fields are also important sources of pollution. The solubility and chemical form of Al in aqueous environments may vary depending on factors such as the pH level of the water, temperature, the amount of dissolved organic carbon (DOC), and the hardness of the water14–16. Therefore, the toxicity and bioavailability of Al produce varying effects on aquatic organisms as a result of environmental factors.

High concentrations of Al can cause serious toxic effects on marine organisms. In saline environments where acidification increases, Al solubility rises, and consequently, the toxic potential of Al increases9,17. These impacts disrupt the vital functions of organisms such as growth, reproduction and respiration, leading to negative impacts on biodiversity and ecosystem balance3,12. Studies on water and sediment samples have also shown that Al is generally found in higher concentrations than other toxic metals, and therefore there is increasing awareness of the toxic effects of Al. In addition, Al is present in high concentrations, especially in coastal waters; this is largely due to anthropogenic activities18–21. Like many other toxic metals, Al tends to bioaccumulate in both terrestrial and marine organisms. Bioaccumulation in marine organisms is usually more pronounced under low pH conditions, where water-soluble forms of Al become more toxic. Bioaccumulated Al can be carried up the food chain via marine organisms, posing greater risks to both predators that consume these organisms and humans22–24. The adverse effects of Al on human health include neurodegenerative diseases (especially Alzheimer’s and epilepsy), nervous system toxicity, lung diseases (fibrosis and decreased lung function), and bone diseases. In addition, long-term exposure to Al can lead to serious health problems such as hormonal imbalances, kidney damage, and cancer25–27.

Therefore, continuous monitoring of the values of Al and/or other toxic metals in water and sediment samples in marine ecosystems is crucial for both the protection of the ecosystem itself and the assurance of human health. Knowledge of these values is also critical for understanding bioaccumulation processes in marine ecosystems. In addition, information about the levels of toxic metals in different localities provides a significant contribution to understanding regional differences and developing pollution control strategies28,29. In this context, marine elemental monitoring studies are indispensable for sustainable marine management and environmental protection strategies.

Determining elemental concentrations in marine water and sediments is fundamental for environmental monitoring and pollution assessment; however, large-scale and long-term monitoring programs often generate complex datasets that are difficult to interpret through direct measurements alone. In this context, statistical and Machine Learning (ML) based approaches are increasingly used as complementary tools in environmental analysis to examine multivariate relationships and support environmental interpretation within monitored systems. Several recent studies have demonstrated the effectiveness of ML approaches in environmental prediction tasks. Yaseen et al. focused on predicting heavy metal concentrations in marine sediments using ML and metaheuristic optimization techniques and Deng et al. evaluated water and stream sediment quality in a coal mining area through chemical and statistical analyses30,31. Bhagat et al. and Mert & Kasapoğulları applied artificial intelligence (AI)-based models to predict heavy metal pollution in aquatic environments32,33. Yolcu et al. modeled the effects of essential heavy metals on environmental pollution using linear and nonlinear ANN-based approaches34. Chen et al. and Cui et al. applied ML approaches for analyzing and assessing environmental pollution and related risks35,36. Gryech et al. developed ML- and IoT-based frameworks for air pollution prediction and environmental health analysis, including the MOREAIR dataset37,38. Xu et al. proposed an ML-based approach for predicting water quality under incomplete datasets, achieving up to 75% accuracy despite 57% missing data39. Nguyen et al. applied multiple regression-based ML models to predict rare earth element concentrations, identifying Random Forest (RF) as the most effective model40. Han et al. compared LSTM, RF, Decision Tree, and SVM models for Water Quality Index prediction, demonstrating that LSTM achieved the highest accuracy due to its ability to capture temporal dependencies41. Chen et al. further proposed an interpretable ensemble ML framework for predicting dissolved oxygen levels in estuarine systems42. These studies collectively highlight the increasing importance and reliability of ML approaches in environmental prediction tasks.

In parallel, feature selection has become an essential component of machine learning applications, as it enhances model accuracy while reducing computational complexity in environmental prediction tasks. Dai et al. developed a hybrid model that uses XGBoost-based feature selection to eliminate redundant features, followed by MSCNN and GA-optimized LSTM for PM2.5 prediction43. Zhang et al. proposed a Bayesian optimization-based XGBoost-RFE feature selection method to determine the optimal feature subset, significantly improving ozone prediction performance44. In another study, Demir and Sahin applied GA-based feature selection and hyperparameter optimization to RF, SVM, and XGBoost models, demonstrating that XGBoost achieved the highest accuracy, especially when trained with balanced data45. Garabaghi et al. employed a wrapper-based feature selection approach and showed that dimensionality reduction improves prediction accuracy, with Random Forest emerging as the most robust model for dissolved oxygen prediction46. Ahajjam et al. used GA based feature selection to identify the most relevant features, then highlighted the key factors influencing wildfire occurrence, and observed significant improvements in prediction accuracy47.

In this context, the study evaluates whether ML and feature selection methods can identify statistically informative element combinations to predict Al concentrations in water and sediment samples from the Marmara Sea. Unlike original dataset with all features, this study specifically investigates whether Al concentrations can be accurately predicted using a reduced subset of elements. The underlying assumption is that Al can be predicted more effectively with fewer but more informative element predictors, thus reducing the number of features while maintaining or even improving prediction performance. The goal is to evaluate the predictive utility of multivariate element relationships and demonstrate how feature selection can support more efficient, interpretable, and data-driven environmental monitoring strategies.

In accordance with these objectives, in this study, 6 water and sediment samples from 17 sites of the Sea of Marmara were collected and digested using a microwave oven, then the concentrations of Al (Al), Boron (B), Calcium (Ca), Cadmium (Cd), Chromium (Cr), Cobalt (Co), Copper (Cu), Iron (Fe), Potassium (K), Magnesium (Mg), Manganese (Mn), Sodium (Na), Nickel (Ni), Lead (Pb), and Zinc (Zn) were determined by ICP-OES (Inductively Coupled Plasma Optical Emission Spectroscopy) device. As part of this study, the collected data from water and sediment samples, observed at different times and locations,were combined to form two datasets with a total of 102 observations each. Feature selection methods were then used to select important features, and the prediction models were evaluated, with all results carefully documented for further analysis.

Feature selection

Feature Selection (FS) plays a critical role in reducing model training time, enhancing the training process, and improving prediction accuracy. As noted by48 FS effectively reduces the dimensionality of the feature space by eliminating irrelevant and redundant features, while maintaining the model’s decision-making quality. The success of predictive analysis largely depends on the appropriate selection of features included in the model. In this study, the FS process is optimized using wrapper methods, which assess the relevance of features to select an optimal subset tailored to the specific mining algorithm49. This research employs two robust wrapper methods RFE and GA each using distinct strategies to identify the most effective subset of features for the same target features.

Recursive feature elimination (RFE)

Recursive Feature Elimination (RFE) systematically evaluates the importance of features by evaluating the performance of the model and gradually removing the least important ones50. Throughout this process, the model is retrained at each iteration to identify the most optimal subset of features. Each feature is evaluated on an individual basis.

A key parameter of RFE is the type of regression or classification model used in its configuration (e.g., linear regression, random forest). Additionally, parameters such as the number of features to be selected or the number of features to be eliminated at each iteration can also be adjusted to fine-tune the selection process. In this study, the implementation of the RFE algorithm is focused specifically on identifying the model type and the number of iterations. By allowing the model to leverage its inherent decision-making capabilities, the optimal features are identified without pre-specifying the number of features to be selected. Algorithm 1 (provided in Supplementary Information) presents pseudocode designed to fit this approach, utilizing the “rfe” function from the “caret” package for efficient and customizable feature elimination51.

Genetic algorithm (GA)

Genetic Algorithm (GA), introduced by John Holland in the 1970s52, is an optimization technique inspired by the principles of biological evolution. Rather than assessing the importance of individual features, GA evaluates different combinations of features and employs genetic operations, such as crossover and mutation, to identify the optimal subset. As a population-based method, GA evaluates the fitness of entire populations and operates within a broad search space, striving to find the global optimum. This approach is particularly effective in uncovering nonlinear relationships within the data.

The GA is inherently more complex, and dynamic compared to RFE, necessitating the careful tuning of additional hyperparameters. Key parameters such as mutation rate, crossover, selection mechanisms, and population size are critical in identifying the optimal subset of features. The mutation probability introduces diversity within the population by randomly altering individuals, while the selection process determines which individuals are chosen for reproduction based on their fitness levels. The number of generations influencesthe duration of the evolutionary process, and elitism ensures that the best-performing individuals are preserved in subsequent generations. The fitness function is crucial as it evaluates the quality of solutions in relation to the optimization objective, guiding the algorithm toward the best results. Given the complexity and interdependence of these parameters, the design and tuning of the GA require careful attention to achieve optimal feature selection53

Due to its inherent nature, the GA requires more computational time compared to other methods. When dealing with smaller datasets, using wide ranges in the search for optimal hyperparameters may increase the risk of overfitting. Therefore, in this study, only specific hyperparameters such as the number of iterations, population size (popsize), mutation rate (pmutation), elitism (elite), and crossover (pcrossover) probability were considered to optimize the hyperparameter search, with the aim of speeding up the process and minimizing the risk of overfitting. Algorithm 2 (provided in Supplementary Information) presents the pseudocode for this approach, utilizing the “gafs” function from the “caret” package to achieve efficient feature selection by minimizing Mean Squared Residuals (MSR) with the fewest features51.

Prediction models

In this study, we focus on these methods: Multiple Linear Regression, Elastic Net Regression, Type 1 Fuzzy Function, XGBoost, RF, Simple Average and Stacking54 from Ensemble Learning Methods. Each method offers a unique approach to modeling and was chosen based on its ability to address different aspects of the prediction problem.

Multiple linear regression (MLR)

Multiple Linear Regression is one of the most used predictive models for examining the relationships among features55. This approach is particularly prevalent in determining the relationship between a dependent feature and multiple independent features. MLR, a statistical method, seeks to predict a dependent feature (or target feature) by utilizing more than one independent (or explanatory) feature. The goal of this model is to establish a linear relationship between the dependent features and multiple independent features. For the model to yield accurate results, certain assumptions must be satisfied, including the independence of independent features, normality of the error terms, and homoscedasticity. Failure to meet these assumptions can undermine the model’s reliability and complicate the interpretation of its results. Nonetheless, multiple linear regression remains a fundamental tool for modeling and predicti relationships between features. Type-1 fuzzy functions (T1FFs)

Type-1 Fuzzy Functions (T1FFs) were developed by Turksen56 as an alternative to rule-based fuzzy inference systems, eliminating the need for expert knowledge. In rule-based fuzzy inference systems, rules are required to establish a relationship between input and output. However, the proposed T1FFs approach represents the relationships between input and output through functions derived from clustering methods, without relying on such rules. This approach offers a significant advantage in modeling uncertainties and is commonly used in classification and regression problems56–58.

The structure of T1FFs is based on clustering the input matrix using the FCM algorithm “ppclust” package59.The algorithm results in grouping similar objects with higher membership degrees, where each cluster has a membership matrix containing the membership degrees of the observations. After this process, as many input matrices as the number of clusters are generated by adding the membership degrees to the input matrix separately. Finally, the predicts obtained from these input matrices are combined. Elastic net (ENET)

The Elastic Net (ENET) method is a type of regularized optimization applied to linear regression that compromises ridge regression and the lasso60. This approach is utilized to mitigate overfitting in regression models, whilesimultaneously ensuring both sparsity and the selection of groups of correlated features. ENET can handle cases where the linear regression model assumptions are unmet. For example, when there are cases of multiple collinearities within the dataset, it can handle this problem by co-selecting features with such dependencies.

The goal of ENET is to generate a sparse feature space by applying the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$l_1$$\end{document} penalty, while also enhancing stability and retaining correlated features through the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$l_2$$\end{document} penalty. Like the lasso, solving this optimization problem is computationally challenging; however, efficient algorithms have been developed to address it. Additionally, a free parameter, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha$$\end{document}, allows for the adjustment of the balance between the two penalties61.

The ENET uses a mixture of the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$l_1$$\end{document} (lasso) and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$l_2$$\end{document} (ridge regression) penalties and can be formulated as in Equation (7):7\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \hat{\beta } = \arg \min _{\beta } \left( \Vert Y - X\beta \Vert _2^2 + \lambda (\alpha \Vert \beta \Vert _1 + (1-\alpha ) \Vert \beta \Vert _2 )\right) \end{aligned}$$\end{document}As evident from the Equation (7), the performance of the ENET model is largely dependent on two critical hyperparameters: \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\lambda$$\end{document}. Extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost)

XGBoost (Extreme Gradient Boosting) is an advanced machine learning method based on the gradient boosting algorithm developed63 in 2001. This method, known as MART (Multiple Additive Regression Trees), is a successful technique for making predictions using decision trees. Built on this foundation, XGBoost was introduced by64 and quickly gained popularity. XGBoost focuses on minimizing error by generating sequential decision trees and is known for its exceptional speed, efficiency, and performance. It is widely applied in both classification and regression tasks. Unlike traditional models, XGBoost has less stringent assumptions, though having well-prepared and organized data is crucial for maximizing model accuracy. Since the algorithm is robust enough to handle missing data and multiple linear dependencies, it can be preferred over other methods in practice.

The objective function in XGBoost consists of two primary components: the loss function, which quantifies the discrepancy between the actual and predicted values, and a regularization term designed to penalize model complexity, thereby reducing the risk of overfitting. The model optimizes this objective function through the application of gradient descent.

Random forest (RF)

The Random Forest algorithm (RF), introduced by Breiman (2001)67, has become a widely used non-parametric method for both classification and regression. It builds predictive models using different types of predictor features without requiring any predefined assumptions about how these features relate to the response feature. The method is based on combining a large number of decision trees and producing predictions through aggregation. In regression problems, the final prediction is obtained by averaging the outputs of individual trees, whereas in classification problems, the final class is determined by majority voting.

Hyperparameter tuning plays an important role in improving the performance of the RF model. To achieve better prediction accuracy, hyperparameters can be optimized using a grid search method. Al concentration prediction in water samples

The water dataset, collected from various time periods and locations, provides valuable insights into environmental fluctuations and serves as the foundation for developing predictive models to predict Al concentration using feature selection techniques.

Figure 3 presents a scatter plot matrix, which visually summarizes the relationships between Al concentration and other elements in the dataset. In the upper triangle, Pearson correlation coefficients reveal strong positive correlations between Al and several elements, including Cd (0.783), Co (0.808), and Fe (0.844), suggesting the presence of multicollinearity. Notably, Al also shows moderate negative correlations with elements such as K (-0.240) and Mn (-0.293). The scatter plots in the lower triangle display both linear and non-linear relationships between pairs of features, while the diagonal contains density plots, offering a clear view of each feature’s distribution.This graphical analysis helps identify potential predictors for Al concentration while simultaneously revealing the presence of non-linear interactions and outliers in the data. These challenges, along with the high degree of multicollinearity, indicate that traditional linear methods may struggle to provide accurate predictions. Consequently, advanced machine learning models such as T1FFs, XGBoost, and RF which are capable of handling complex interactions and multicollinearity, will be explored as alternatives to linear regression. Tree-based models (e.g., XGBoost and RF) are generally scale-invariant; however, models such as MLR and ENET are highly sensitive to the magnitude of input features. In the water dataset, substantial differences exist in concentration ranges between major elements (e.g., Fe, Na, K) and trace elements (e.g., Cd, Pb). Discussion

Experimental evidence from water and sediment data shows that feature selection approaches applied to selected datasets can provide superior prediction performance compared to the original dataset. Several observations further clarify the method’s behavior.

The results obtained from the water data show that the best prediction performance in terms of RMSE is achieved by modeling the selected features by GA using XGBoost. In the original dataset, the XGBoost model was trained using 14 features and obtained RMSE value of 0.0637 on the test set. After feature selection, the number of features was reduced to 6, obtained lower RMSE of 0.0354. The feature selection process improved the RMSE of the XGBoost model by 44.4% compared to the original dataset. Furthermore, when compared to the MLR (RMSE = 0.0455), the best individual method demonstrates an improvement of approximately 22.2%. As illustrated in Figure 7, the Shapley Additive Explanations (SHAP) based feature importance analysis of the best-performing individual model reveals that Cu and Cr are the most influential features. Mn and Na exhibit moderate contributions, whereas Ni and Pb have a lower impact on the model output. In addition, the ensemble learning results demonstrate that stacking outperforms simple averaging. This can be attributed to the ability of stacking to learn an optimal combination of base learners through a meta-model. Moreover, as presented in Table 4, the RF model showed a performance increase of 2% with RFE and 17% with GA in terms of RMSE after feature selection.

The findings from the sediment dataset indicate that the best prediction, in terms of RMSE, is achieved when features selected by GA are using XGBoost. In the original dataset, the XGBoost was trained with 14 features and produced an RMSE of 447.0389 on the test set. Following feature selection, the feature count was reduced to 6, resulting in a lower RMSE of 356.5694. This reduction corresponds to a 20% improvement in RMSE compared to the No FS. Additionally, when compared with the MLR (RMSE = 655.5621), the best model showed an approximate improvement of 45%. As shown in Figure 8, SHAP-based feature importance analysis for the model highlights B and Cd as the most significant faetures. Furthermore, as presented in Table 6, the RF model demonstrated a 7% and 11% improvement in RMSE with RFE and GA, respectively.

Feature selection improved the prediction performance of XGBoost and RF models, while no improvement was observed in MLR, T1FF, and ENET methods. This difference may stem from the structural characteristics of the methods. XGBoost and RF models are tree-based models. They have flexible structures based on a splitting mechanism, which can better model nonlinear relationships and intervariable interactions than classical methods. Therefore, feature selection and elimination of noisy features can improve model performance by providing more optimal splits. However, other methods, such as MLR, rely on the contribution of all features under a linear assumption. Therefore, removing some features from the model as a result of feature selection analysis can lead to a decrease in explained variance and negatively impact prediction performance.

While the predictive performance of the models provides quantitative evidence of the effectiveness of feature selection, it is also important to interpret the selected predictors within a geochemical context in order to better understand the underlying environmental processes that may be represented by the models. In the water dataset, the GA based selected features (Cr, Cu, Mn, Na, Ni, and Pb) may represent overlapping environmental signals related to anthropogenic inputs, particulate transport, and adsorption-scavenging processes. Transition metals such as Cr, Cu, Ni, and Pb are commonly reported to originate from similar sources and may exhibit co-variation due to shared transport pathways. Mn may influence trace metal behavior through redox-driven scavenging processes, while Na can reflect salinity-driven variability and hydrographic mixing. These factors may collectively suggest that Al distribution in the water dataset is governed by broader physicochemical conditions rather than a single controlling feature.

In the sediment dataset, the selected features (B, Cd, K, Ni, Pb, and Zn) may indicate that Al prediction is associated with a combination of lithogenic background and anthropogenic influences. K is generally linked to the mineral structure of sediments, whereas Cd, Ni, Pb, and Zn are often associated with anthropogenic inputs and their accumulation in reactive sediment phases. B may reflect both natural processes and marine geochemical conditions. These features can be interpreted as indicators of sediment composition and binding processes that may influence elemental distributions. Overall, the selected features may be interpreted as co-varying indicators of the same geochemical environment, rather than direct causal determinants of Al concentration.

To further contextualize these relationships, it is important to consider the environmental behavior of Al. In marine systems, Al distribution is primarily governed by physicochemical processes such as particle interactions, hydrographic variability, and source inputs, rather than a single controlling factor. In sediments, Al is typically associated with lithogenic background and mineral composition, while anthropogenic influences may contribute to localized variability. In this regard, factors such as sediment texture, organic matter, and reactive mineral phases play a key role in regulating metal binding and distribution. Accordingly, Al concentrations should not be interpreted as an isolated indicator of contamination, but rather as the result of the combined influence of natural geochemical processes and human activities.

Within this framework, the Al concentrations observed in the present dataset gain environmental context from both recent marine guideline values and regional sediment studies. For marine waters, the 2025 ANZG guideline defines aluminium thresholds of 9, 37, 72, and 160 µg/L corresponding to 99%, 95%, 90%, and 80% species protection levels, respectively, and identifies 37 µg/L as indicative of slightly to moderately disturbed ecosystems. The guideline further specifies that dissolved aluminium measurements, operationally defined as 0.45 µm-filtered samples, are appropriate for such comparisons (ANZG, 2025). In sediments, however, Al is more meaningfully interpreted within a regional geochemical context rather than against a universal threshold. Evidence from the Sea of Marmara indicates that Al and Fe are the most abundant sedimentary elements, with localized enrichment patterns linked to industrialization, maritime activity, and urbanization69. Source-apportionment studies further classify Al and Fe within lithogenic components, whereas Cd, Pb, and Zn are more closely associated with anthropogenic inputs, with sediment texture and organic matter influencing metal binding70. Similarly, studies from the East China Sea highlight grain size and organic matter as primary controls on heavy metal distribution, with fine-grained terrestrial inputs playing a significant role in metal transport and accumulation71. Additionally, manganese oxides act as efficient natural scavengers through adsorption and oxidation processes, supporting the role of Mn-bearing phases in trace metal retention72. Conclusion

In this study, water and sediment samples collected from 17 sites in theSea of Marmara were analyzed, and the concentrations of 15 elements were determined using ICP-OES. The resulting datasets enabled the investigation of relationships among elements for predicting Al concentrations. In this context, the study evaluates whether machine learning and feature selection methods can identify statistically informative combinations of elements for Al prediction. Unlike the use of the full feature set, this study specifically examines whether Al concentrations can be accurately predicted using a reduced subset of elements.

In this context, MLR, T1FFs, ENET, XGBoost, RF, and Ensemble Learning methods were applied to predict the concentration levels of the Al element. To enhance model performance, achieve similar or better predictive performance with fewer features, and gain a better understanding of the relationships between features, feature selection methods RFE and GA were employed in addition to the prediction techniques. During the model evaluation phase, the datasets were divided into training, validation and testing , and hyperparameter optimization was carried out. The findings indicated that the features selected through GA (Cr, Cu, Mn, Na, Ni, Pb) achieved the highest performance for the XGBoost model in the water dataset. In the sediment dataset, the features determined by GA (B, Cd, K, Ni, Pb, Zn) yielded the best results for the XGBoost model on the test dataset.

At the conclusion of the study, it is observed that the number of important features for predicting Al concentration can be reduced from 14 to 6 when comparing the original dataset to the dataset created using the selected features for the both water and sediment dataset. This demonstrates that similar or even superior results can be obtained using only 6 features instead of 14, and that analysis processes can be effectively conducted with data optimized through feature selection. Furthermore, the findings highlight the importance of element interactions in predicting Al concentrations and demonstrate that feature selection improves model performance while maintaining prediction accuracy. Overall, the study shows that integrating feature selection with machine learning offers an effective approach to more accurately predicting Al concentrations using a reduced set of informative elements, while also contributing to a clearer and more interpretable presentation of relationships between elements.

In future studies, the application of additional machine learning models, such as deep learning algorithms, may further enhance the prediction accuracy of element concentrations in environmental datasets. In this context, deep learning approaches based on neural networks, which have been reported to demonstrate superior performance compared to conventional machine learning models in similar prediction tasks, may also be considered73. Furthermore, expanding the dataset by incorporating seasonal and spatial variations across a broader sampling area in the Sea of Marmara and other similar inland seas would provide more comprehensive insights.
