Mesoscale Simulations of Blood Coagulation in Flow and Quiescent Domains Using SDPD
Authors: Marina Echeverría Ferrero, Nicolas Moreno, Marco Ellero
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBlood coagulation is governed by tightly regulated reaction networks whose activation and early evolution are influenced by transport processes. While reduced kinetic models of the intrinsic and extrinsic pathways have been validated in well‐mixed experimental settings, their behavior in spatially resolved domains remains insufficiently characterized. In this work, two established reduced coagulation networks are embedded within a thermodynamically consistent mesoscale particle‐based framework that resolves fluid momentum transport together with multispecies advection–diffusion–reaction dynamics. Unlike conventional continuum coagulation solvers, the proposed formulation captures coupled transport and biochemical interactions through interacting particles within a unified hydrodynamic description. The framework is used to investigate the initiation phase of coagulation under controlled microvascular‐like flow conditions in simplified channel geometries. The simulations reproduce characteristic thrombin generation curves (TGCs) across physiologically relevant parameter ranges while additionally revealing spatial transport effects that are not captured by outlet‐averaged measures alone. In particular, the results show that transport–reaction coupling induces pronounced spatial heterogeneities in thrombin concentration, with transitions between localized activation, wall‐aligned accumulation, and advective washout depending on Reynolds and Péclet regimes. Injury geometry is further shown to modulate coagulation amplification, with capped configurations producing enhanced thrombin accumulation due to localized surface‐mediated activation. Complementary quiescent‐domain simulations additionally reproduce qualitative trends observed in thrombodynamics assays, including fibrinogen‐dependent variations in fibrin formation. Overall, the study introduces a unified mesoscale computational framework for analyzing how flow, transport, and biochemical kinetics jointly regulate early coagulation dynamics in spatially resolved domains.Keywords: blood coagulation, smoothed dissipative particle dynamics (SDPD), thrombin generation, transport–reaction coupling

Introduction

Blood clotting, or coagulation, is a complex process driven by a cascade of chemical reactions and cellular interactions within the bloodstream. It is broadly categorized into three stages: initiation, amplification, and propagation [1]. A critical outcome of this process is the formation of a fibrin network, which stabilizes the clot and prevents excessive bleeding. This involves the enzymatic conversion of soluble fibrinogen into insoluble fibrin by thrombin, a key protease generated through a sequence of proteolytic reactions involving clotting factors such as prothrombin and Factor X. These reactions occur primarily on the surface of activated platelets, which provide a localized environment for the clotting factors to interact.

While the initiation phase of coagulation is relevant across vessels of all sizes, its dynamics take on particular significance in the microvasculature [2, 3]. Here, smaller vessel dimensions, slower flow rates, and higher surface‐to‐volume ratios amplify the localized effects of tissue factor (TF) exposure and platelet activation. These features create conditions where clotting must be tightly regulated to balance hemostasis and thrombosis. During the initial stages of clotting (Figure 1), TF, exposed due to vessel injury or inflammation, triggers the extrinsic pathway, leading to Factor VIIa activation and subsequent Factor X activation. Similarly, the intrinsic pathway, initiated by Factor XII activation on collagen or negatively charged surfaces, also converges at Factor X activation [1, 4]. Factor Xa cleaves prothrombin (II) to thrombin (IIa), which not only catalyzes fibrin (Ia) formation but also amplifies the cascade through feedback loops.

Mathematical and computational models of coagulation have expanded greatly, with a large number of publications indexed in PubMed since the 1970s [5]. These models aim to capture the interplay of coagulation factors, platelet activity, fluid flow, and vessel wall interactions [6, 7, 8, 9]. Yet, reproducing the full multiscale physiology remains challenging [10, 11], as it requires coupling reaction kinetics with diffusion, advection, and cellular processes. Early ODE‐based kinetic models described thrombin generation but neglected spatial transport [12, 13]. Reaction–diffusion (RD) models later addressed this by adding diffusion, though at the cost of greater computational load, reduced reproducibility, and difficulty in validation [14, 15]. Numerical stability can also be compromised by stiff reaction terms [16]. Reduced models based on physiologically motivated simplifications have been proposed, retaining essential dynamics while limiting parameter complexity [15, 17]. Sensitivity analyses have also been used to identify the influence of reaction rates and initial concentrations on thrombin generation metrics [18]. To better capture in vivo conditions, RD models were further extended to include flow, resulting in advection–diffusion–reaction (ADR) frameworks [11, 19]. These models enable the simulation of clot formation under physiologically relevant flow regimes by accounting for the influence of transport [14, 20] and shear forces [14, 21, 22].

The large biochemical scope of the coagulation cascade has motivated substantial efforts toward subsystem or reduced‐order formulations that isolate the key drivers of thrombin generation. Early kinetic models, including those by Hockin et al. [12], Anand et al. [8], and Chatterjee et al. [9], provided important insights while keeping parameter counts tractable. Building on these foundations, Fogelson and colleagues incorporated flow and surface effects [6, 7, 14], highlighting the importance of spatial and hemodynamic regulation in clotting. More recent reduction strategies include multistep model‐compression approaches [23], thrombin‐focused schemes by Chen and Diamond [24], patient‐specific calibrations proposed by Ratto et al. [25], and automated or genetic algorithm‐based reduction methods [26, 27]. These efforts collectively highlight the continuing need for models that capture essential biology while remaining computationally tractable and interpretable.

In parallel, particle‐based methods have played an important role in advancing spatially resolved models of thrombosis, complementing continuum and hybrid approaches. Reactive DPD frameworks have been used to explore fibrin polymerization dynamics [28], while hybrid particle–continuum models have captured flow–thrombus interactions at mesoscale resolution [29, 30]. Related approaches include SPH formulations in which particles transition from fluid to solid phases based on coagulation species and mechanical triggers [31]. Smoothed dissipative particle dynamics (SDPD) provides a particle discretization of fluctuating hydrodynamics consistent with the Navier–Stokes equations, in contrast to conventional DPD formulations based on a soft equation of state, making it particularly suitable for studying the coupling between fluid transport and biochemical reaction networks in coagulation systems. Furthermore, increasingly realistic multiphysics frameworks have integrated biochemical kinetics with image‐based platelet aggregation, RD descriptions of fibrin polymerization, and fluid‐mechanical characterizations of shear‐dependent clot deformation. These multiscale approaches [32, 33, 34, 35] provide a comprehensive view of thrombosis mechanisms, but often rely on extensive reaction networks or tightly coupled modules whose calibration remains challenging. Even focused sensitivity analyses, such as the 88‐equation system examined by Stobb et al. [36], illustrate the inherent trade‐off between biological realism and computational tractability. Despite this progress, important questions remain regarding how flow, geometry, and spatially evolving biochemical fields jointly regulate the initiation phase of clot formation; existing ADR models typically rely on continuum formulations that do not capture mesoscale hydrodynamics, while particle‐ or agent‐based approaches often incorporate only limited reaction networks [37, 38].

Compared with conventional continuum ADR formulations based on finite‐volume, finite‐element, or finite‐difference discretizations [39, 40], the SDPD framework adopted here represents the fluid and transported biochemical species through interacting particles. This allows transport, diffusion, and hydrodynamic fluctuations to be captured through local interparticle interactions [41, 42]. Continuum coagulation models have successfully described reaction–transport coupling in blood clotting and have been extended through adaptive‐mesh techniques to resolve steep gradients and moving activation fronts [40]. In contrast, the SDPD formulation does not rely on a fixed computational mesh and naturally accommodates evolving spatial heterogeneities, complex geometries, and coupled transport–reaction effects within a thermodynamically consistent hydrodynamic framework. This makes it particularly suitable for microscale coagulation problems where multiple physical processes interact across scales. Importantly, this mesoscale description also provides a natural bridge toward future extensions involving clot microstructure and mechanics. In particular, recent fibrin gel models that resolve fibrin network formation and rheology explicitly [43, 44] can be directly coupled to the present framework, enabling a unified description of biochemical activation and fibrin structural evolution. Further extensions may also incorporate thixotropic multiphase formulations and multiscale blood rheology models including red blood cell dynamics [45].

The primary aim of this work is to assess how established reduced kinetic models of the intrinsic [25] and extrinsic [24] pathways behave when embedded in a spatially resolved mesoscale flow framework capable of resolving coupled transport and reaction dynamics. To this end, these validated reaction networks are integrated within a thermodynamically consistent SDPD particle‐based formulation [46], which captures fluid momentum transport, multispecies advection–diffusion, and their nonlinear biochemical interactions. Accordingly, the present SDPD formulation is intended as a mesoscale Lagrangian fluctuating extension of continuum Navier–Stokes and ADR approaches rather than a direct replacement. Its motivation lies in representing hydrodynamics, species transport, and future discrete clot constituents within a unified particle‐based framework, which is particularly relevant for problems involving spatial heterogeneity, evolving interfaces, and transport–reaction coupling at the microscale. The associated increase in computational cost and timestep restrictions relative to continuum formulations is discussed in Section 2.

The resulting framework is used to analyze thrombin and fibrin formation in both channel‐flow configurations (including capped and uncapped geometries) and quiescent domains. Thrombin generation curves (TGCs) [47, 48] are used to characterize thrombin dynamics, while selected biochemical perturbations, such as variations in fibrinogen concentration, are used to probe model response. The focus is on evaluating how established coagulation models interact with transport under controlled conditions, with emphasis on transport–reaction coupling and its impact on spatial activation dynamics.

Computational Model

Compositional SDPD

We model blood flow and species transport using a compositional SDPD framework for mesoscale ADR processes [46]. In this approach, the fluid is represented by interacting particles carrying mass, momentum, and biochemical species, and the governing dynamics are formulated directly at the particle level within a thermodynamically consistent fluctuating‐hydrodynamics framework [41, 49].

The SDPD formulation is constructed such that, in the hydrodynamic limit, it is consistent with the weakly compressible Navier–Stokes equations, dρ/dt=−ρ∇⋅v and ρdv/dt=−∇p+η∇2v+(ζ+η/D)∇(∇⋅v), 1 together with advection–diffusion–reaction balances for transported species. In the numerical implementation, these continuum equations are directly discretised by means of pairwise interacting particles. The fluid is represented by particles with position ri, velocity vi, and volume Vi, such that the particle number density is defined as di=1/Vi=∑jWrij,h, where Wrij,h is a normalized smoothing kernel with compact support h, and rij=|ri−rj|. Pairwise interactions are constructed from the kernel gradient Fij=−∇Wrij,h/rij. In this work, we adopt the Lucy quartic kernel [50]. The system evolution is therefore described in terms of particle velocities (vi) and concentrations of the species α (Ciα), governed by conservative, dissipative, and stochastic interactions. At the particle level, the momentum equation includes reversible pressure forces, viscous dissipation, and thermal fluctuations consistent with the fluctuation–dissipation theorem (FDT) [51]:

This approximation is consistent with the mesoscopic concentration regimes considered here; further details and discussion can be found in [46]. Under the assumptions of negligible compositional fluctuations (C˜iα≈0), absent cross‐species diffusive coupling, and local reaction kinetics, this discrete formulation preserves species mass for the diffusive transport contribution, remains thermodynamically consistent, and reproduces Fickian diffusion.

We adopt a system of dimensionless (reduced) units by defining characteristic scales for length (l), mass (m), and energy (ε). These scales yield a natural unit of time, τ=lm/ε, and all quantities are expressed relative to them. For example, a domain length Lx=50 corresponds to 50l, and an interparticle spacing dx=0.2 provides a resolution of 0.2l. Transport and reaction parameters are likewise nondimensionalized: diffusion coefficients in l2/τ, reaction rates in 1/τ, and velocities in l/τ. This consistent framework facilitates comparison across all test cases.

To characterize transport regimes, dimensionless numbers are defined using the channel height H as the characteristic length scale. The Reynolds number, Re=ρuH/η=uH/ν, measures the ratio of inertial to viscous forces, where ρ is the fluid density, u a characteristic velocity, η the dynamic viscosity, and ν the kinematic viscosity. The Péclet number, Pe=uH/D, quantifies the relative importance of advection and diffusion, while the Schmidt number, Sc=ν/D, compares momentum and mass transport. Finally, the Damköhler number, Da=H2/Dτr, relates diffusion and reaction timescales, with τr denoting a characteristic reaction time. Additional methodological details, including stability criteria, are summarized in Appendix A.

In this work, we focus on the microvascular regime, characterized by vessel diameters of 5–100 μm, low Reynolds numbers (Re ≲1), and moderate‐to‐high Peclet numbers (Pe ∼101−104). Such conditions typify arterioles and venules and are often reproduced in flow‐based in vitro assays of coagulation and thrombus formation [53, 54, 55]. This regime is physiologically and experimentally relevant and well suited for mesoscale modeling, where transport and reaction dynamics are strongly coupled. The corresponding parameter estimates for our simulations and detailed description range of Pe and Re simulated are provided in Estimates for Microvascular Regimes. The choice of characteristic scales and non‐dimensional parameters ensures that the simulated flow remains within the physiological range of arteriolar conditions. The ADR–SDPD scheme is implemented in the open‐source Large‐scale Atomic/Molecular Massively Parallel Simulator (LAMMPS) [56] (stable release: October 29, 2020), which provides a robust parallel framework for particle‐based simulations and compatibility with existing molecular and rigid‐body models. The computational cost of the SDPD formulation is primarily governed by short‐range interparticle interactions, leading to an approximately linear scaling with the number of particles. The simulations presented here involve approximately 1.5×104 fluid particles, providing sufficient spatial resolution to resolve the benchmark transport–reaction mechanisms studied [46]. Compared with standard SDPD formulations, the present model incurs additional computational cost due to the simultaneous evolution of multiple reacting species, reflecting the increased physical complexity of the coagulation problem. For larger or fully three‐dimensional domains, this motivates the use of multiscale strategies, in which SDPD resolves local clot microstructure and biochemical activation while continuum models capture transport at larger scales [57].

Computational Domain and Scaling Considerations

Simulations were first performed in homogeneous domains without flow or geometric features, providing a minimal setting to test the reaction kinetics in (3) and (4) against reference thrombin generation assays. We then extended the framework to heterogeneous domains including localized injury sites, wall‐bound particles, and capped regions, representing simplified spatial configurations in which coagulation is locally initiated. In this way, homogeneous cases serve as a baseline for model validation, while heterogeneous domains enable analysis of the interplay between flow, transport, and localized biochemical activation. Geometric ratios and flow conditions are selected following established dimensional scaling guidelines for coagulation flow models. In particular, the choice of channel aspect ratio (H/Lx<0.2) and relative injury size (Linj/H=1) is consistent with recommendations from the ISTH Scientific and Standardization Committee [58], which define parameter ranges for transport–reaction systems under shear flow. These choices promote well‐developed laminar flow and ensure that coagulation dynamics are governed by surface‐mediated activation within physiologically relevant transport regimes.

Within the heterogeneous group, two main configurations were distinguished. The first configuration consists of rectangular channel domains (Figure 2a) in which transport is governed by convection and diffusion, characterized by the Re and Pe ranges introduced in Section 2.1. Boundary and Source Term Conditions

Different boundary conditions are applied depending on the configuration (channel‐flow or cuvette‐like), as detailed below. For particle motion, no‐slip conditions were imposed at solid walls, while periodic boundaries were applied along the streamwise direction [60] in the channel‐flow configurations. This choice leads to statistically stationary flow conditions and removes inlet–outlet and entrance‐length effects, which allows a controlled study of transport–reaction coupling. Clot initiation is represented through localized reactive surfaces and static geometric features, rather than dynamically evolving occlusive growth. This keeps the focus on biochemical activation, advection, and diffusion, without the additional complexity associated with imposed inlet velocity profiles or developing flow conditions.

In the channel‐flow configurations, prothrombin (II) was continuously supplied at the inlet at reference physiological concentrations (see Table A3), whereas homogeneous Neumann conditions, n⋅∇Cα=0, were applied at the outlet for all species. Coagulation was locally triggered through a prescribed Factor Xa flux applied to particles above the injury region, defined as flux=γCX0−CXa,avg where γ and CX0 are given in Table A3, and CXa,avg denotes the spatial average Xa concentration over the fluid particles in the injury region. For semicircular cap geometries, the total Xa supply increased naturally with the size of the cap region, as more particles were subjected to the prescribed flux. To achieve target Re and Pe numbers representative of microscale flows, a body force was applied to drive the flow, with the fluid viscosity η and characteristic velocity u adjusted to systematically vary Re and Pe (see Estimates for Microvascular Regimes). In quiescent, cuvette‐like configurations, coagulation was initiated by TF immobilized at a localized wall region under zero‐flow conditions. The red region in Figure 2b indicates the activator surface, where a prescribed nonzero TF concentration locally triggered the reaction cascade. No‐flux boundary conditions were imposed at all remaining domain boundaries.

Results

In the following, we present the results in increasing order of complexity. We begin with a consistency check of the reduced coagulation models in homogeneous domains, where the SDPD formulation is compared against the corresponding ODE‐based reference solutions. In this context, system (3) is used for the intrinsic pathway and system (4) for the extrinsic pathway, serving as independent benchmark reaction networks rather than a coupled model.

Building on this verification step, we extend the simulations to channel‐flow configurations, where the intrinsic pathway is used to examine the influence of transport parameters, injury size, and variations in fibrinogen concentration on thrombin generation and fibrin formation. Finally, we perform in vitro‐inspired simulations motivated by experimental assays such as thrombodynamics [59], highlighting the role of diffusion in reaction kinetics under controlled conditions.

Time is nondimensionalized as t*=t/tref, with tref=1s, and concentrations as C*=Cα/Cref, with Cref=1μM representing a typical inlet fibrinogen concentration. The SDPD solver operates in its own mesoscopic units, while tref provides a consistent reference to express reaction rates in dimensionless form. This normalization is applied throughout all figures unless otherwise noted.

The SDPD simulations successfully reproduce the trends predicted by the ODE systems (3) and (4), supporting the robustness of our framework for modeling both coagulation pathways. Flow‐Driven Simulation Results

We analyzed blood coagulation in platelet‐free plasma under flow conditions using system (3) describing the reduced intrinsic pathway. The results are reported as peak thrombin values and local outlet‐averaged thrombin concentrations. In the spatially resolved channel configuration, thrombin concentrations vary along the flow direction due to advection and local reaction dynamics. To obtain a representative global signal comparable to TGCs, concentrations are averaged over fluid particles crossing the outlet section of the channel. These quantities can be directly compared to TGCs as standard benchmarks for the temporal evolution of thrombin. From this point forward, prothrombin and thrombin will be referred to as II and IIa, respectively.

Capped configurations are used as idealized representations of injury or partially occlusive geometries in microvascular flow, allowing systematic control of the procoagulant surface area and local flow constriction (see Section 3.2.2). In these simulations, the imposed obstacles perturb the local hydrodynamic field and thereby affect advection and transport of reacting species. However, the evolving coagulation state is not coupled back to the flow and does not alter either the geometry or the hydrodynamic field. This configuration isolates transport–reaction coupling during clot initiation, without accounting for feedback between coagulation dynamics, flow modification, and structural evolution.

To assess the role of transport in coagulation dynamics, we examined channel domains at low Re (0.02) while varying Pe (10, 70), consistent with the microvascular regimes introduced in Section 1. The transport–reaction system comprised Factor Xa, II, and IIa, coupled through the kinetic scheme in ((3a), (3b), (3c)) and subject to advection–diffusion.

Overall, these results indicate that higher Pe not only enhances advective transport but also alters the spatial distribution of coagulation species, increasing heterogeneity in local IIa concentrations. Such heterogeneity cannot be inferred from TGCs, which provide only global outlet‐averaged kinetics. Complementary spatially resolved descriptors are therefore required to fully characterize coagulation in the microvasculature.

The maps reveal that IIa remains concentrated near the bottom‐wall injury at intermediate Re (0.08 and 0.1, circles and diamonds), consistent with a stronger convective washout with larger Re, and reflected at the larger magnitude for mean profiles in the plateaus of Figure 5a, while at Re =0.5, stronger advection enhances downstream transport but limits local accumulation intensity near the injury. Across all cases, however, the concentration front extends downstream and arches upward, reaching the upper wall despite the absence of any source there. This apparent symmetry results from the left‐to‐right inflow of reactive species and their rapid vertical homogenization, driven by the combined effects of diffusion and flow. The observed arch‐like pattern indicates a regime with high Da ≫ 1. Computed timescales (Table A2) give DaIIa≈2.3, confirming a reaction‐dominated regime in our parameter window, where fast reaction kinetics further promote homogenization across the channel height. These findings are consistent with previous microfluidic studies under Re <1, which also reported laminar, reaction‐dominated conditions [61, 62, 63]. Likewise, confinement effects have been shown to modulate activation thresholds in coagulation networks [64], supporting our interpretation that in microvascular‐like channels, subtle variations in Re, Pe, or aspect ratio can shift the balance between localized activation and homogenized downstream propagation. While geometric confinement is not varied explicitly in the present simulations, prior microfluidic work has demonstrated that confinement can modulate activation thresholds by altering the balance between advection, diffusion, and reaction [64]. The present results isolate transport–reaction effects under controlled conditions, without addressing confinement effects explicitly.

Impact of Injury Size

We investigated how the size of the procoagulant region affects thrombin generation and flow patterns. Three cap sizes were simulated in a channel with radii r=1.5, 4.5, and 8.5, corresponding to r/H=0.15, 0.45, and 0.85 (denoted S, M, and L). Figure 6a shows particle velocity fields at t*=5000, illustrating that cap size strongly influences local hydrodynamics. Small caps allow faster flow, whereas the largest cap (L) substantially slows the fluid, consistent with previous studies treating growing clots as partially occluding porous regions [65]. Conclusions

Using a particle‐based fluctuating‐hydrodynamics framework [46], we investigated how transport processes interact with biochemical kinetics during the initiation of thrombin generation under controlled microvascular‐like flow conditions. Validated reduced coagulation networks were embedded within the SDPD method to systematically examine the influence of flow intensity, dispersion, and injury geometry on the spatial organization and temporal evolution of clotting reactions.

The results indicate that transport–reaction coupling plays a dominant role in shaping coagulation dynamics. Variations in the Péclet number influence not only transport rates but also the development of pronounced spatial heterogeneities in thrombin concentration, which are not captured by outlet‐averaged measures such as TGCs. Changes in Reynolds number (Re = 0.02–0.5) further modify dispersion regimes, from localized activation near the injury at low Re, to wall‐aligned accumulation at intermediate Re, and reduced thrombin levels under stronger advective washout. Injury geometry introduces an additional regulatory mechanism: capped configurations with concentrated surface‐mediated Xa fluxes significantly enhance thrombin production compared with diffuse injuries, highlighting the impact of geometric confinement on reaction amplification. Complementary cuvette‐like simulations reproduced qualitative features observed in in vitro thrombodynamics assays [59], including the sensitivity of clot propagation to fibrinogen concentration [67, 68], illustrating the flexibility of the computational framework.

These findings demonstrate that the behavior of reduced coagulation networks can differ markedly once embedded in spatially resolved flow fields, emphasizing the importance of resolving transitions between diffusion‐dominated and advection‐enhanced regimes. While the present study focuses on plasma‐based flow and idealized geometries to isolate transport–reaction mechanisms, the framework provides a solid numerical foundation for further developments. At present, the framework resolves the influence of flow on coagulation dynamics but does not yet account for feedback of the developing clot on the flow field, making it most appropriate for studying coagulation initiation and early transport–reaction coupling. Future extensions incorporating thrombus permeability, fibrin mechanics, and cellular interactions will enable tighter coupling between biochemical reaction networks and emergent clot structure [44, 69]. Within the present SDPD framework, these extensions could be incorporated through additional particle populations and modified transport interactions. For example, thrombus permeability could be represented through locally varying drag or resistance effects that alter momentum and species transport within clot regions, while platelets or red blood cells could be introduced as discrete mesoscale entities coupled to the surrounding fluid particles [45]. In addition, recent mesoscale fibrin‐clot models [43, 44] already provide explicit descriptions of fibrin gel mechanics and microstructure, offering a natural route for coupling the present biochemical transport framework with emergent clot rheology. At larger scales, these developments could further be integrated into heterogeneous multiscale or thixotropic multiphase formulations [70], extending the applicability of the model across multiple spatial scales. With targeted validation in microfluidic settings, this mesoscale approach offers a robust building block for multiscale numerical models of coagulation.

Smoothed Dissipative Particle Dynamics Formulation

This appendix summarizes the particle‐level formulation of the compositional Smoothed Dissipative Particle Dynamics (SDPD) model used in Section 2. This appendix summarizes the particle‐level formulation of the compositional Smoothed Dissipative Particle Dynamics (SDPD) model used in Section 2. SDPD is a mesoscale particle‐based method that combines the Lagrangian discretization of Smoothed Particle Hydrodynamics (SPH) with thermodynamically consistent thermal fluctuations as in Dissipative Particle Dynamics (DPD), providing a discrete representation of the fluctuating Navier–Stokes equations [41, 71]. Its main advantages are the direct specification of transport properties (e.g., speed of sound, viscosity, and diffusivity) and consistency with the fluctuation–dissipation theorem and the GENERIC framework for nonequilibrium thermodynamics [72]. In the macroscopic limit, SDPD recovers the continuum hydrodynamic behavior of SPH and deterministic Navier–Stokes flow.

Numerical Implementation

The SDPD equations are integrated in time using a stochastic velocity–Verlet scheme [73], which preserves weak second‐order accuracy for the deterministic dynamics while consistently incorporating stochastic forcing. The fluid is discretized using particles with equilibrium spacing dx=0.2 and kernel cutoff h=4dx. The timestep Δt=10−3τ is selected to ensure numerical stability of the coupled hydrodynamic and diffusive dynamics, satisfying the characteristic viscous and acoustic stability constraints of the SDPD discretization [74].

At each timestep, the coupled transport–reaction problem is advanced sequentially. First, particle positions and velocities are updated from the momentum balance. Second, diffusive transport is evaluated using the updated particle configuration. Third, local reaction source terms are integrated according to the reduced coagulation networks given in Tables A3 and A4. This operator‐splitting scheme provides a consistent coupling between hydrodynamics, diffusion, and reaction kinetics.
