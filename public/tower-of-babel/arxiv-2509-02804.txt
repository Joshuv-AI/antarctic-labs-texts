Abstract
We study the problem of minimizing a m m -weakly convex and possibly nonsmooth function. Weak convexity provides a broad framework that subsumes convex, smooth, and many composite nonconvex functions. In this work, we propose a proximal descent method , a simple and efficient first-order algorithm that combines the inexact proximal point method with classical convex bundle techniques. Our analysis establishes explicit non-asymptotic convergence rates in terms of ( η , ϵ ) (\eta,\epsilon) -inexact stationarity. In particular, the method finds an ( η , ϵ ) (\eta,\epsilon) -inexact stationary point using at most 𝒪 ⁡ ( ( 1 η 2 + 1 ϵ ) ​ max ⁡ { 1 η 2 , 1 ϵ } ) \mathcal{O}\!\left(\Big(\tfrac{1}{\eta^{2}}+\tfrac{1}{\epsilon}\Big)\max\!\left\{\tfrac{1}{\eta^{2}},\tfrac{1}{\epsilon}\right\}\right) function value and subgradient evaluations. Consequently, the algorithm also achieves the best-known complexity of 𝒪 ⁡ ( 1 / δ 4 ) \mathcal{O}(1/\delta^{4}) for finding an approximate Moreau stationary point with ‖ ∇ f 2 ​ m ​ ( x ) ‖ ≤ δ \|\nabla f_{2m}(x)\|\leq\delta . A distinctive feature of our method is its automatic adaptivity : with no parameter tuning or algorithmic modification, it accelerates to 𝒪 ⁡ ( 1 / δ 2 ) \mathcal{O}(1/\delta^{2}) complexity under smoothness and further achieves linear convergence under quadratic growth. Overall, this work bridges convex bundle methods and weakly convex optimization, while providing accelerated guarantees under structural assumptions.

1.1
Search for optimality or stationary points

(Sub)gradient methods.
For convex functions, the most common performance measure is the rate at which the function values decrease, that is, how quickly
f(x_{k})-\min_{x}f(x)
converges to zero.
In particular, when
f
is differentiable and its gradient map
x\mapsto\nabla f(x)
is
M
-Lipschitz continuous, the negative gradient direction always provides a descent direction. In this case, the basic gradient descent method

x_{k+1}=x_{k}-\alpha_{k}\nabla f(x_{k}),\,k=0,1,2,\ldots,

with stepsize
\alpha_{k}=1/M
, achieves an iteration complexity of
\mathcal{O}(1/\epsilon)
to find a point satisfying
f(x_{k})-\min_{x}f(x)\leq\epsilon

[
10
, Section 1.2.3]
. Moreover, Nesterov’s accelerated method improves this rate to the optimal
\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{1/\epsilon})

[
10
, Section 2.2]
. When
f
is convex but nonsmooth, the situation is markedly different. In this case, the standard algorithm is the subgradient method
x_{k+1}=x_{k}-\alpha_{k}g_{k},\,k=0,1,2,\ldots,

where
g_{k}\in\partial f(x_{k})
is a subgradient. Unlike the smooth case, the negative subgradient direction may no longer guarantee descent in function value, and constant stepsize schemes typically fail to ensure convergence. Instead, a diminishing stepsize schedule is usually required to establish convergence of the function values, which achieves an iteration complexity of
\mathcal{O}(1/\epsilon^{2})

[
10
, Section 3.2.3]
.

For nonconvex functions, it is generally unrealistic to expect the function values to converge to a global minimum. Instead, the standard objective is to identify a stationary point. In the case of nonconvex but smooth functions, a common measure is the gradient norm along the iterates,
\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|
. In particular, the basic gradient descent method with a constant stepsize achieves an iteration complexity of
\mathcal{O}(1/\epsilon^{2})
for finding an
\epsilon
-stationary point, i.e., a point satisfying
\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|\leq\epsilon

[
10
, Equation 1.2.22]
.
For nonconvex and nonsmooth problems, one may instead consider the minimal norm subgradient,
\mathrm{dist}(0,\partial f(x_{k}))
, as a natural stationary measure. However, this quantity can be uninformative for subgradient methods, since
\mathrm{dist}(0,\partial f(x_{k}))
may remain bounded away from zero along all iterates, for example, when
f(x)=|x|
. This difficulty arises from the discontinuity of
x\mapsto\mathrm{dist}(0,\partial f(x))
, which complicates the analysis of nonsmooth nonconvex problems.

Weak convexity provides a useful setting for analyzing nonsmooth and nonconvex problems. As highlighted in
[
2
]
, a key tool in this setting is the
Moreau envelope
, defined for any
\rho>m
as

\displaystyle f_{\rho}(x):=\inf_{y}\Big\{f(y)+\tfrac{\rho}{2}\|y-x\|^{2}\Big\}.

The Moreau envelope smooths the
m
-weakly convex function
f
and admits a Lipschitz continuous gradient, even when
f
itself is nonsmooth. Consequently, the gradient norm
\|\nabla f_{\rho}(x)\|
serves as an informative and continuous stationary measure. We defer a more detailed review of the Moreau envelope to
Section

2.1
. In particular, the work
[
2
]
established a non-asymptotic convergence rate of
\mathcal{O}(1/\delta^{4})
for the subgradient method in terms of the Moreau envelope: after
J
iterations, the method ensures

\min_{k=1,\ldots,J}\|\nabla f_{2m}(x_{k})\|\leq\delta

with a complexity of order
\mathcal{O}(1/\delta^{4})
. While this result provides the first rigorous rate guarantee in the weakly convex setting, the stopping criterion requires evaluating
\|\nabla f_{2m}(x_{k})\|
, which may be prohibitively expensive in practice.

Proximal point methods.

In contrast to the subgradient method, the natural stationary measure
\mathrm{dist}(0,\partial f(x_{k}))
works well for the proximal point method (PPM)
[
11
]
, which follows the recursion

\displaystyle x_{k+1}=\argmin_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\;f(x)+\frac{\alpha}{2}\|x-x_{k}\|^{2},\;\forall k=1,2,\ldots,

(2)

with
\alpha>m
.
A simple telescoping argument shows that PPM attains the rate
\mathcal{O}(1/\delta^{2})
for finding a point with
\mathrm{dist}(0,\partial f(x_{k}))\leq\delta
(see
proposition

2.3
). The main drawback of PPM, however, is that each iteration requires solving problem
eq.

2
which itself can be non-trivial.

In the convex but nonsmooth setting (i.e.,
m=0
), it is common to consider inexact variants of PPM, where the subproblem (
2
) is solved only approximately
[
12
,
11
]
. The proximal bundle method (
PBM
) is a particularly efficient implementation of this idea
[
7
]
. Roughly speaking,
PBM
addresses the difficulty of solving (
2
) by constructing a sequence of simple minorants
\{\tilde{f}_{j}\}
of
f
(i.e.,
\tilde{f}_{j}\leq f
, which is easy to construct since
f
is convex), and then generating candidate points
\{w_{j}\}
via

\displaystyle w_{j}=\argmin_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\;f_{j}(x)+\frac{\alpha}{2}\|x-x_{k}\|^{2},\;\forall j=1,2,\ldots,

which admits an efficient solution by the construction of
f_{j}
.
Through a systematic procedure for building these minorants and a specialized descent test,
PBM
ensures that the resulting sequence
\{w_{j}\}
yields a valid inexact solution of (
2
). Compared with the subgradient method,
PBM
does not require delicate stepsize tuning to guarantee convergence of the function values, and it further achieves the optimal convergence rates for convex problems
[
13
,
14
,
15
]
.

Classical results on
PBM
have primarily focused on the convex setting
[
13
,
14
,
15
,
7
,
16
,
17
]
. More recently, several works have extended
PBM
to weakly convex problems, including
[
8
,
9
,
18
,
19
,
20
,
21
]
. Among these, the work
[
19
]
introduced a novel scheme that combines PPM with the subgradient method, called the
proximally guided stochastic subgradient method
(PGSG). This algorithm achieves a non-asymptotic complexity guarantee of
\mathcal{O}(1/\epsilon^{2})
finding a point
x_{k}
such that

\mathrm{dist}\!\left(x_{k},\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\,\middle|\,\mathrm{dist}(0,\partial f(x))^{2}\leq\varepsilon\right\}\right)^{2}\leq\varepsilon.

However, PGSG requires a
predefined
number of inner subgradient iterations for approximately solving the subproblem
eq.

2
, which reduces its flexibility in practice.
Another line of work
[
8
,
9
]
explored constructing convex bundle models to lower approximate the local convexification
f(\cdot)+\tfrac{m}{2}\|\cdot-x_{k}\|^{2}
. This idea is conceptually very appealing and indeed serves as one of the motivations for our approach; but no iteration complexity guarantees were established in
[
8
,
9
]
.
More recently,
[
20
]
also exploited the idea of local convexification and proposed a variant of the classical
PBM
for weakly convex functions, and proved that it can find a point
x_{k}
satisfying
\|\nabla f_{2m}(x_{k})\|\leq\delta
at a rate of
\mathcal{O}(1/\delta^{4})
. Nonetheless, the algorithmic framework of
[
20
]
appears considerably more complicated compared to the classical
PBM
for convex functions or the subgradient method.

1.2
Our contributions

In this work, we develop a
simple
and
efficient
first-order algorithm, called the
proximal descent method
, for computing stationary points of the weakly convex optimization problem
eq.

1
. Our method is based on the framework of the inexact proximal point method
[
11
]
, with each subproblem solved efficiently via the classical convex bundle method
[
7
]
.
A central concept in our algorithm is the
\epsilon
-inexact subdifferential
\partial_{\epsilon}f(x)
(see
eq.

7
for a precise definition). At each iterate
x_{k}
, the proximal descent method aims to generate a new point
x_{k+1}
satisfying the decrease condition

f(x_{k+1})\leq f(x_{k})-\frac{\tilde{\beta}}{2\alpha}\|\tilde{g}_{k+1}\|^{2},

(3)

where
\tilde{\beta}\in(m/\alpha,1]
and
\tilde{g}_{k+1}\in\partial_{\epsilon_{k+1}}f(x_{k+1})
is an inexact subgradient with accuracy
\epsilon_{k+1}\geq 0
. For the exact PPM update
eq.

2
, this inequality holds with
\tilde{\beta}=1
and
\epsilon_{k+1}=0
, i.e.,
\tilde{g}_{k+1}\in\partial f(x_{k+1})
. Since solving
eq.

2
exactly is generally intractable, we relax the condition to allow both a smaller decrease and inexact subgradients as in
eq.

3
. If
eq.

3
holds, a simple telescoping argument then guarantees that the method finds a point satisfying
\min_{k=1,\ldots,T}\|\tilde{g}_{k+1}\|\leq\eta
within
T=\mathcal{O}(1/\eta^{2})
iterations. Additional analysis shows that the inexactness terms
\epsilon_{k+1}
also converge to zero, ensuring true stationarity (
Theorem

1
).

Our key observation is that a point
x_{k+1}
satisfying
eq.

3
can be computed efficiently using classical convex bundle methods. Following the idea of
[
8
,
9
]
, we decompose the PPM parameter
\alpha
as
\alpha=m+\rho
, leading to the subproblem

x_{k+1}=\argmin_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\;f(x)+\frac{m}{2}\|x-x_{k}\|^{2}+\frac{\rho}{2}\|x-x_{k}\|^{2},

where
m
is the
convexification parameter
and
\rho
the
proximal parameter
. The function
x\mapsto f(x)+\tfrac{m}{2}\|x-x_{k}\|^{2}
is convex for any
x_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, which enables us to adapt convex bundle methods to efficiently construct lower approximations of this local convex model and obtain iterates satisfying
eq.

3
. Unlike the classical convex bundle setting, however, the local convexified model
f(\cdot)+\tfrac{m}{2}\|\cdot-x_{k}\|^{2}
evolves with the iterate
x_{k}
, necessitating a different analysis for the proposed algorithm.

The idea of combining inexact PPM with convex bundle methods provides a natural extension of the classical
PBM
from the convex to the weakly convex setting. Our main technical contribution is to establish explicit convergence rates for the resulting proximal descent method. We measure progress using the notion of
(\eta,\epsilon)
-inexact stationarity
, defined as
\mathrm{dist}(0,\partial_{\epsilon}f(x))\leq\eta
. For weakly convex functions, we show that the proximal descent method finds an
(\eta,\epsilon)
-inexact stationary point within at most

\mathcal{O}\!\left(\Big(\tfrac{1}{\eta^{2}}+\tfrac{1}{\epsilon}\Big)\max\!\left\{\tfrac{1}{\eta^{2}},\tfrac{1}{\epsilon}\right\}\right)

function and subgradient evaluations (
theorem

2
). Moreover, since
(\eta,\epsilon)
-inexact stationarity implies
(\alpha,\delta)
-Moreau stationarity, defined as
\|\nabla f_{\alpha}(x)\|\leq\delta
, our analysis further guarantees that the method produces an iterate
x_{k}
with
\|\nabla f_{2m}(x_{k})\|\leq\delta
in at most
\mathcal{O}(1/\delta^{4})
function and subgradient evaluations (
corollary

4.2
), matching the best known rates in
[
2
,
22
]
.

Finally, we show that the proximal descent method
automatically accelerates
in the presence of additional structural assumptions, such as smoothness or quadratic growth, without requiring any algorithmic modification. These accelerated guarantees apply for nonconvex functions, thereby extending the convex-only results of
[
15
]
to the nonconvex setting. In particular, when
f
is
M
-smooth, the method adapts to find a point
x_{k}
with
\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|\leq\delta
in
\mathcal{O}(1/\delta^{2})
iterations (
theorem

3
). If
f
further satisfies a quadratic growth condition, the proximal descent method achieves a linear convergence rate (
theorem

6
). Unlike existing methods for weakly convex optimization (e.g., subgradient or PGSG), which require explicit tuning or modification to exploit smoothness, our approach adapts naturally. A summary of our convergence results across different settings is provided in
table

1
. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first analysis that unifies best-known rates in the
weakly convex
setting with accelerated guarantees under additional structural assumptions. A more detailed comparison with existing algorithms is given in
Section

4.4
.

2.1
Subdifferential and the Moreau envelope

We focus on algorithms to minimize weakly convex functions. Let us begin with the definition.

Definition 2.1
.

A function
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
is called
m
-weakly convex if the function
x\mapsto f(x)+\frac{m}{2}\|x\|^{2}
is a convex function.

The class of weakly convex functions is quite broad, including any smooth functions, convex functions, and the composition of a convex Lipschitz function and a smooth function
[
2
]
. Given an
m
-weakly convex function
f
, it is clear that
f(\cdot)+\frac{m}{2}\|\cdot-z\|^{2}
is also convex for any fixed
z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. This means that the function
f
is convex up to a quadratic perturbation with curvature
m
. This simple fact is important since it allows us to construct a global affine under-estimator for the perturbed function
f(\cdot)+\frac{m}{2}\|\cdot-z\|^{2}
.

Consider a proper closed function
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
and a point
x
with
f(x)
finite. The Fréchet subdifferential of
f
at
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is defined as
[
23
, Definition 1.10]

\partial f(x)=\left\{v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid\liminf_{y\to x}\frac{f(y)-f(x)-\left\langle v,y-x\right\rangle}{\|y-x\|}\geq 0\right\}.

Informally, the Fréchet subdifferential
\partial f(x)
consists of all vectors
v
such that the function
f
admits an affine under-estimator
l(y)=f(x)-\left\langle v,y-x\right\rangle
locally at the point
x
. If
f
continuously differentiable, the Fréchet subdifferential
\partial f(x)
only consists of the gradient
\{\nabla f(x)\}

[
24
, Exercise 8.8]
. For convex functions, it becomes the same as the usual convex subdifferential
[
24
, Proposition 8.12]

\partial f(x)=\{v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid f(y)\geq f(x)+\left\langle v,y-x\right\rangle,\forall y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\}.

The following result characterizes the Fréchet subdifferential of a weakly convex function.

Lemma 1
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
be a closed
m
-weakly convex function. We have

\displaystyle\partial f(x)=\left\{v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid f(y)\geq f(x)+\left\langle v,y-x\right\rangle-\frac{m}{2}\|y-x\|^{2},\;\forall y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\right\}.

(4)

This result is standard; see, for example,
[
2
, Lemma 2.1]
.
Lemma

1
means that a weakly convex function admits global
concave quadratic
under-estimators from any subgradient. This generalizes the case of convex functions, where any subgradient yields a global affine under-estimator.

Given a function
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
and
\rho>0
, the Moreau envelope is defined by

f_{\rho}(x):=\inf_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left\{f(y)+\frac{\rho}{2}\|y-x\|^{2}\right\}.

(5)

The Moreau envelope
f_{\rho}
is a natural minorant of
f
by definition since
\inf_{y}\{f(y)+\frac{\rho}{2}\|y-x\|^{2}\}\leq f(x)
. When
f
is convex, the Moreau envelope is continuously differentiable for any
\rho>0
; When
f
is
m
-weakly convex, with
\rho>m
, the function
y\mapsto f(y)+\frac{\rho}{2}\|y-x\|^{2}
becomes strongly convex, and the minimizer
\hat{x}:=\argmin_{y}\{f(y)+\frac{\rho}{2}\|y-x\|^{2}\}
is unique. In this case, the Moreau envelope also becomes continuously differentiable and the gradient is given by the following lemma.

Lemma 2
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
be a closed
m
-weakly convex function. Then, for any
\rho>m
, the Moreau envelope
f_{\rho}
is continuously differentiable with gradient given by

\displaystyle\nabla f_{\rho}(x)=\rho(x-\hat{x}),

(6)

where
\hat{x}:=\argmin_{y}\{f(y)+\frac{\rho}{2}\|y-x\|^{2}\}
is unique.

A simple but fundamental fact is that for any
\rho>m
, we have
\nabla f_{\rho}(x)=\{0\}
if and only if
0\in\partial f(x)
, i.e., the set of stationary points of a weakly convex function coincides with those of its Moreau envelope (see
proposition

A.1
). Moreover, at these stationary points, the function values also agree, i.e.,
f(x)=f_{\rho}(x)
whenever
0\in\partial f(x)
.
Figure

1
illustrates this fact using a
2
-weakly convex function
x\mapsto|x^{2}-1|
.

:

-weakly convex function

with
\rho=4
and

. Their stationary points are the same at
-1,0,
and

, and their function values also agree at these stationary points.

2.2
Approximate stationary points

When minimizing a weakly convex function, we cannot guarantee the discovery of its global minimum due to the potential nonconvexity. The practical goal is to find a first-order stationary point, i.e., a point
x
satisfying
0\in\partial f(x)
. This motivates the need for a notion of approximate stationarity.

For smooth functions, the gradient norm
\|\nabla f(x)\|
is a natural measure of stationarity since
\nabla f
is continuous: small gradient norms indicate proximity to a stationary point.
For nonsmooth functions, one might consider
\mathrm{dist}(0,\partial f(x))
, which is the minimum distance from the origin to the subdifferential. However, this quantity may be discontinuous: even when
x
is close to a stationary point,
\mathrm{dist}(0,\partial f(x))
might be bounded away from zero. For example, in the scalar function
f(x)=|x|
, whose subdifferential jumps discontinuously at
x=0
.

For an
m
-weakly convex function, we can consider an inexact Fréchet subdifferential, defined as
1

\partial_{\epsilon}f
in
eq.

7

\epsilon
-subdifferential defined in
[

is more aligned with the
\epsilon

\displaystyle\partial_{\epsilon}f(x)

\displaystyle=\left\{v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid f(y)\geq f(x)+\left\langle v,y-x\right\rangle-\frac{m}{2}\|y-x\|^{2}-\epsilon,\forall y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\right\},

(7)

\epsilon\geq 0

\epsilon=0
,
\partial_{\epsilon}f(x)
recovers the original subdifferential
\partial f(x)
(see

(\eta,\epsilon)

Definition 2.2

(
(\eta,\epsilon)
-inexact stationarity)
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
be an
m
-weakly convex function, and
\eta\geq 0,\epsilon\geq 0
. A point
x\in\mathrm{dom}\,f
is called an
(\eta,\epsilon)
-inexact stationary point if

\displaystyle\mathrm{dist}(0,\partial f_{\epsilon}(x))\leq\eta.

This
(\eta,\epsilon)
-inexact stationarity is also called the
regularized stationarity
in
[
20
, Definition 2.5]
.
It is clear that the above notion recovers the exact stationary if
\eta=0
and
\epsilon=0
. For a fixed
\epsilon>0
,
\mathrm{dist}(0,\partial_{\epsilon}f(x))
is indeed a continuous function in
x
when
f
is convex; see
lemma

A.6
for details.

As mentioned above, the
Moreau envelope

f_{\rho}
of an
m
-weakly convex function is
continuously differentiable
when
\rho>m
. The magnitude
\|\nabla f_{\rho}(x)\|
naturally provides a quantification in terms of near-stationarity. We define another type of approximate stationary point.

Definition 2.3

(
(\delta,\alpha)
-Moreau stationarity)
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
be an
m
-weakly convex function, and
\delta>0,\alpha>m
. A point
x\in\mathrm{dom}\,f
is called an
(\delta,\alpha)
-Moreau stationary point if

\displaystyle\|\nabla f_{\alpha}(x)\|\leq\delta.

The optimality condition of the minimization problem
eq.

5
directly implies that for any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\alpha>m
, the point
\hat{x}:=\argmin_{y}\{f(y)+\frac{\alpha}{2}\|y-x\|^{2}\}
satisfies

\displaystyle\|\hat{x}-x\|

\displaystyle=\|\nabla f_{\alpha}(x)\|/\alpha,

(8a)

\displaystyle f(\hat{x})

\displaystyle\leq f(x),

(8b)

\displaystyle\mathrm{dist}(0,\partial f(\hat{x}))

\displaystyle\leq\|\nabla f_{\alpha}(x)\|.

(8c)

We now see from
eq.

8a
that that the distance between
x
and
\hat{x}
is
\|\nabla f_{\alpha}(x)\|/\alpha
. Also,
eq.

8b
means the point
\hat{x}
achieves a lower function value; and
Equation

8c
says that the magnitude of the minimal norm element in the subdifferential at
\hat{x}
is at most
\|\nabla f_{\alpha}(x)\|
. Thus, a small
\|\nabla f_{\alpha}(x)\|
indicates that there is a nearby point
\hat{x}
that is close to stationary, measured by
\mathrm{dist}(0,\partial f(\hat{x}))
. If
\|\nabla f_{\alpha}(x)\|=0
, we must have
\mathrm{dist}(0,\partial f(x))=0
, i.e.,
x
is an exact stationary point.

These two notions,
(\eta,\epsilon)
-inexact stationarity and
(\delta,\alpha)
-Moreau stationarity, are closely related, as shown below.

Proposition 2.1

(
(\eta,\epsilon)
-inexact stationarity to
(\delta,\alpha)
-Moreau stationarity)
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
be an
m
-weakly convex function. Suppose
x
is a
(\eta,\epsilon)
-inexact stationary point. For any
\lambda>0
, the point
x
satisfies

\displaystyle\|\nabla f_{m+\lambda}(x)\|\leq(m+\lambda)\left(\frac{2}{\lambda}\eta+\sqrt{\frac{2}{\lambda}\epsilon}\right).

If
\lambda=m
, it simplifies to
\|\nabla f_{2m}(x)\|\leq 4\eta+2\sqrt{2m\epsilon}
.

A similar result appears in
[
20
, Proposition 2.10]
where
\lambda=m
. Our result holds for any
\lambda>0
.
Proposition

2.1
implies that an
(\eta,\epsilon)
-inexact stationary point must also be an
(\delta,\alpha)
-Moreau stationary point, where
\delta
depends on
\epsilon
and
\eta
. A natural question is then whether
(\delta,\alpha)
-Moreau stationarity implies
(\eta,\epsilon)
-inexact stationarity. This is the case when the function is Lipschitz continuous.

Proposition 2.2

(
(\delta,\alpha)
-Moreau stationarity to
(\eta,\epsilon)
-inexact stationarity)
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
be an
m
-weakly convex function and
L
-Lipschitz continuous. If
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is an
(\delta,\alpha)
-Moreau stationary point, then
x
is an
(L\delta/\alpha,\sqrt{2(\alpha-m)L{\delta}/{\alpha}})
-inexact stationary point.

We provide the proofs of
propositions

2.1
and
2.2
, as well as further discussions on different stationarities, in
Appendix

A
.

2.3
Subgradient methods and proximal point methods

We next review two basic algorithms to minimize a weakly convex function
f
. The simplest algorithm is arguably the
subgradient method
, which iteratively updates

x_{k+1}=x_{k}-\alpha_{k}g_{k},\qquad k=0,1,2,\ldots,

(9)

where
g_{k}\in\partial f(x_{k})
, and
\alpha_{k}>0
is a step size. If
f
is
M
-smooth, then
eq.

9
becomes the gradient descent algorithm. In this case, a proper choice of step sizes can ensure the best gradient norm
\min_{k\in\{1,2,\ldots,T\}}\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|
converges to zero with rate
\mathcal{O}(1/\sqrt{T})

[
10
, Section 1.2.3]
. If
f
is convex and
M
-smooth, then we can ensure that the function value gap
f(x_{k})-\min_{x}f(x)
converges to zero with rate
\mathcal{O}(1/T)

[
10
, Corollary 2.1.2]
. If the function
f
is only convex but nonsmooth, the best function value gap
\min_{k\in\{1,2,\ldots,T\}}f(x_{k})-\min_{x}f(x)
converges to zero with rate
\mathcal{O}(1/\sqrt{T})

[
10
, Section 3.2.3]
. However, it was an open question to determine the rate of convergence for
eq.

9
when applied to the class of
m
-weakly convex functions. One difficulty was that neither the function value gap
f(x_{k})-\min_{x}f(x)
nor the stationary measure
\mathrm{dist}(0,\partial f(x_{k}))
goes to zero.

Algorithm 1

Subgradient method

x_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, a sequence of step sizes
\alpha_{k}>0
, and maximum iteration
T

for

k=1,2,\ldots,T

do

set
x_{k+1}=x_{k}-\alpha_{k}g_{k},
where
g_{k}\in\partial f(x_{k})
;

end

for

The seminal work
[
2
]
established the first convergence rate using the notion of Moreau stationarity. We here state the deterministic version of
[
2
, Theorem 3.1]
.

Lemma 3
.

Consider the
m
-weakly convex problem
eq.

1
. Let
\alpha=\rho+m
with
\rho>0
. The subgradient method in
Algorithm

1
generates a sequence of iterates, satisfying

\displaystyle\min_{k\in\{1,\ldots,T\}}\|\nabla f_{\alpha}(x_{k})\|^{2}\leq\frac{\alpha}{\rho}\frac{(f_{\alpha}(x_{1})-f^{\star})+\frac{\alpha L^{2}}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{T}\alpha_{k}^{2}}{\sum_{k=1}^{T}\alpha_{k}},

where
L\geq\max_{k\in\{1,\ldots,T\}}\|g_{k}\|
. If we choose a constant step size
\alpha_{k}=\sqrt{\frac{\Delta}{mL^{2}(T+1)}}
with
\Delta\geq f_{2m}(x_{1})-\min_{x}\,f(x)
, then it holds that

\displaystyle\min_{k\in\{1,\ldots,T\}}\|\nabla f_{2m}(x_{k})\|^{2}\leq\sqrt{\frac{2m\Delta L^{2}}{T+1}}.

Lemma

3
shows that the subgradient method ensures the Moreau stationary measure goes to zero with a rate
\mathcal{O}(T^{-1/4})
. In other words, the subgradient method finds an iterate
x_{k}
with
\|\nabla f_{2m}(x_{k})\|\leq\delta
within at most
\mathcal{O}(1/\delta^{4})
iterations. However, we should carefully interpret this complexity result. First, as in the standard subgradient method, the stepsize
\alpha_{k}
depends on the iteration horizon
T
. Moreover, the measure
\|\nabla f_{2m}(x_{k})\|
is implicit and, in general, not computable, and thus it is difficult to implement a stopping criterion for the subgradient method.

Another conceptually simple algorithm is the PPM
[
11
]
, which is closely linked with the Moreau envelope. For an
m
-weakly convex function
f
, PPM generates a sequence of iterates by

\displaystyle x_{k+1}=\argmin_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\;f(x)+\frac{\alpha}{2}\|x-x_{k}\|^{2},\;\forall k=1,2,\ldots

(10)

where
\alpha>m
. Since we choose
\alpha>m
, the minimization problem in
eq.

10
becomes strongly convex, and thus the minimizer is unique. Accordingly, the iterates in
eq.

10
are well-defined. The convergence of PPM is very well-studied for convex functions
[
11
,
25
]
. Even for
m
-weakly convex functions, it is relatively straightforward to derive the following convergence guarantee.

Algorithm 2

Proximal point method (PPM)

x_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
\alpha>m
, the maximum number of iterations
T
.

for

k=1,2,\ldots,T

do

x_{k+1}=\argmin_{y}f(y)+\frac{\alpha}{2}\|y-x_{k}\|^{2}
.

end

for

Proposition 2.3
.

Consider the
m
-weakly convex problem
eq.

1
. Let
\alpha>m
. The proximal point method in
Algorithm

2
generates a sequence of iterates, satisfying

\min_{k\in\{1,2,\ldots,T+1\}}\mathrm{dist}^{2}(0,\partial f(x_{k}))\leq\frac{2\alpha(f(x_{1})-f^{\star})}{T}.

(11)

Proof.

Since
x_{k+1}
is the minimizer in
eq.

10
, we have

f(x_{k+1})+\frac{\alpha}{2}\|x_{k+1}-x_{k}\|^{2}\leq f(x_{k}).

(12)

By the optimality condition, we have
\nabla f_{\alpha}(x_{k})=\alpha(x_{k+1}-x_{k})
in
eq.

8a
, we have

\|\nabla f_{\alpha}(x_{k})\|^{2}\leq 2\alpha(f(x_{k})-f(x_{k+1})).

By a telescope sum, we obtain

\sum_{k=1}^{T}\|\nabla f_{\alpha}(x_{k})\|^{2}\leq 2\alpha(f(x_{1})-f(x_{T+1}))\leq 2\alpha(f(x_{1})-f^{\star}).

Considering the property in
eq.

8c
, we have
\mathrm{dist}(0,\partial f(x_{k+1}))\leq\|\nabla f_{\alpha}(x_{k})\|
, leading to

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{T}\mathrm{dist}^{2}(0,\partial f(x_{k+1}))\leq 2\alpha(f(x_{1})-f^{\star})\quad

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\quad T\min_{k\in\{2,\ldots,T+1\}}\mathrm{dist}^{2}(0,\partial f(x_{k}))\leq 2\alpha(f(x_{1})-f^{\star})

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\quad T\min_{k\in\{1,\ldots,T+1\}}\mathrm{dist}^{2}(0,\partial f(x_{k}))\leq 2\alpha(f(x_{1})-f^{\star}).

This completes the proof.
∎

Note that the convergence guarantee of the PPM is much stronger than that of the subgradient method. In particular,
Proposition

2.3
provides a direct convergence rate for the stationary measure
\mathrm{dist}(0,\partial f(x_{k}))
, which decays at a rate
\mathcal{O}(1/\sqrt{T})
for any parameter
\alpha>m
.
This convergence rate holds uniformly for both convex and
m
-weakly convex functions. The PPM not only ensures convergence of
\mathrm{dist}(0,\partial f(x_{k}))
, but also achieves a faster rate of
\mathcal{O}({T}^{-1/2})
than the subgradient method, which converges at
\mathcal{O}({T}^{-1/4})
in terms of the Moreau stationary measure.

One significant drawback in PPM is its computational complexity in each iteration
eq.

10
. Updating the iterates via
eq.

10
is typically much more expensive than the simple subgradient update in
eq.

9
. As a result, PPM is often employed as a conceptual algorithm that guides the design and analysis of more practical methods
[
5
,
26
]
.

3
Implicit subgradient and proximal descent methods

In this section, we first introduce the idea of implicit subgradient updates. The discussion applies to both convex and
m
-weakly convex functions, with the convex case corresponding to
m=0
. We then introduce a conceptual yet implementable proximal descent method.

3.1
Motivation: inexact subgradient method

It is known that the PPM can be viewed as an implicit subgradient method
[
6
]
, and this perspective remains valid when minimizing a weakly convex function. In particular, the optimality condition in PPM update
eq.

10
ensures that
0\in\partial f(x_{k+1})+\alpha(x_{k+1}-x_{k}),
and thus, we can rewrite it as

x_{k+1}=x_{k}-\frac{1}{\alpha}g_{k+1},

(13)

where
g_{k+1}\in\partial f(x_{k+1})
and
\alpha>m
. Unlike the standard (explicit) subgradient method
eq.

9
, this iteration is called an implicit subgradient update, as the subgradient
g_{k+1}
is evaluated as the next point
x_{k+1}
rather than the current one.

Meanwhile, the cost drop for PPM
eq.

12
can be expressed in terms of the implicit subgradient as

f(x_{k+1})\leq f(x_{k})-\frac{1}{2\alpha}\|g_{k+1}\|^{2}.

(14)

A telescope sum from
eq.

14
directly leads to the convergence rate in
Proposition

2.3
. The implicit subgradient method
eq.

13
is conceptual, but it provides a guiding principle for implementable algorithms. In particular, many implementable methods can be interpreted as approximately achieving the cost decrease in
eq.

14
. We discuss here a general idea of inexact subgradient methods.

In particular, we aim to find a point
x_{k+1}
satisfying

f(x_{k+1})\leq f(x_{k})-\frac{\beta}{2\alpha}\|\tilde{g}_{k+1}\|^{2},

(15)

where
\beta\in(0,1)
relaxes the cost value drop and
\tilde{g}_{k+1}\in\partial f_{\epsilon}(x_{k+1})
is an inexact subgradient at the next point with inexactness
\epsilon>0
(recall the inexact subdifferential in
eq.

7
).
It is now clear that the inexact subgradient update
eq.

15
guarantees that

\min_{k\in\{1,\ldots,T+1\}}\mathrm{dist}^{2}(0,\partial_{\epsilon}f(x_{k}))\leq\frac{2\alpha(f(x_{1})-\min_{x}f(x))}{\beta T}.

In other words, the update
eq.

15
finds an
(\eta,\epsilon)
-inexact stationary point for weakly convex functions with a rate
\mathcal{O}(T^{-1/2})
. We emphasize that
eq.

15
is a relaxed version of
eq.

14
from the PPM. Still, we need to establish an implementable method to achieve
Equation

15
efficiently.

3.2
An inexact subgradient method from proximal approximations

The inexact update
eq.

15
is a good guiding principle, but it does not guarantee the convergence of stationarity as the inexactness
\epsilon
is fixed across iterations. Based on
Equation

15
, we here consider the following modified requirement

f(x_{k+1})\leq f(x_{k})-\frac{m+\beta\rho}{\alpha}\times\frac{1}{2\alpha}\|\tilde{g}_{k+1}\|^{2},

(16)

where
\rho>0
,
\alpha=m+\rho
,
\beta\in(0,1)
and
\tilde{g}_{k+1}\in\partial f_{\epsilon_{k+1}}(x_{k+1})
. Compared with
eq.

15
, the cost reduction factor is relaxed to be

\frac{m+\beta\rho}{\alpha}\in\left(\frac{m}{\alpha},1\right),

which allows the inexactness
\epsilon_{k}
to be time varying. We will properly control
\epsilon_{k}
such that they are summable, thus approaching zero.

A general idea is that if a trial point
z_{k+1}
does not satisfy
eq.

16
, we will use it to find a better point. With some iterations, it guarantees that we can find a point satisfying
eq.

16
efficiently. We will detail this strategy as
proximal bundle updates
later, which builds on the idea of
[
8
,
9
]
. Throughout this section, we let
\rho=\alpha-m>0
. Then, the exact proximal update
eq.

10
can be written as

\displaystyle x_{k+1}=\argmin_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\;f(x)+\frac{m}{2}\|x-x_{k}\|^{2}+\frac{\rho}{2}\|x-x_{k}\|^{2}.

(17)

Since
f
is
m
-weakly convex, the function
f(\cdot)+\frac{m}{2}\|\cdot-x_{k}\|^{2}
is naturally convex. Thus,
eq.

17
can also be viewed as a proximal mapping on the convex function
f(\cdot)+\frac{m}{2}\|\cdot-x_{k}\|^{2}
with the parameter
\rho>0
, centered at
x_{k}
. Here, we call
m
the
convexification
parameter and
\rho
the
proximal
parameter.

One key idea is to use a
simple
convex function
\tilde{f}
to lower approximate the convex part
f(\cdot)+\frac{m}{2}\|\cdot-x_{k}\|^{2}
, such that the following proximal update

\displaystyle z_{k+1}:=\argmin_{x}\;\tilde{f}(x)+\frac{\rho}{2}\|x-x_{k}\|^{2}

(18)

can be solved efficiently. Thanks to the convexity, a globally lower and convex approximation

\tilde{f}(x)\leq f(x)+\frac{m}{2}\|x-x_{k}\|^{2},\qquad\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

(Minr.)

is easy to construct, e.g., using a first-order approximation. Then, the optimality condition for
eq.

18
naturally gives

{w}_{k+1}:=\rho(x_{k}-z_{k+1})\in\partial\tilde{f}(z_{k+1}).

Furthermore, with some arguments, we see that
\tilde{g}_{k+1}:={w}_{k+1}+m(x_{k}-z_{k+1})=\alpha(x_{k}-z_{k+1})
is an inexact subgradient of the original function
f
at the trial point
z_{k+1}
.

Lemma 4
.

Consider the
m
-weakly convex problem
eq.

1
. Let
x_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\tilde{f}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
be a convex function that satisfies the lower approximation
eq.

Minr.
. Denote
z_{k+1}
as the proximal update in
eq.

18
. Then, we have

\tilde{g}_{k+1}:=\alpha(x_{k}-z_{k+1})\in\partial f_{\epsilon_{k+1}}(z_{k+1}),

(19a)

where
\alpha=m+\rho
and the inexactness is given by

\epsilon_{k+1}=f(z_{k+1})+\frac{m}{2}\|z_{k+1}-x_{k}\|^{2}-\tilde{f}(z_{k+1})\geq 0.

(19b)

Proof.

We observe the following property: for all
y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, it holds that

\displaystyle f(y)+\frac{m}{2}\|y-x_{k}\|^{2}\geq\tilde{f}(y)\geq\tilde{f}(z_{k+1})+\left\langle w_{k+1},y-z_{k+1}\right\rangle,

(20)

where the first inequality is by the lower approximation
eq.

Minr.
and the second inequality is due to the subgradient
{w}_{k+1}:=\rho(x_{k}-z_{k+1})\in\partial\tilde{f}(z_{k+1})
for the convex function
\tilde{f}
. Then, by adding
\frac{m}{2}\|y-z_{k+1}\|^{2}
on both sides of
eq.

20
and a simple rearrangement of terms, we obtain

f(y)+\frac{m}{2}\|y-z_{k+1}\|^{2}\geq\tilde{f}(z_{k+1})+\left\langle{w}_{k+1},y-z_{k+1}\right\rangle+\frac{m}{2}(\|y-z_{k+1}\|^{2}-\|y-x_{k}\|^{2}).

(21)

Also, we have the identity

\displaystyle\|y-x_{k}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\|y-z_{k+1}\|^{2}-2\langle x_{k}-z_{k+1},y-z_{k+1}\rangle+\|z_{k+1}-x_{k}\|^{2}.

(22)

Substituting
eq.

22
into
eq.

21
, we obtain

\displaystyle f(y)+\frac{m}{2}\|y-z_{k+1}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\tilde{f}(z_{k+1})+\left\langle{w}_{k+1},y-z_{k+1}\right\rangle+m\langle x_{k}-z_{k+1},y-z_{k+1}\rangle-\frac{m}{2}\|z_{k+1}-x_{k}\|^{2}.

\displaystyle=\tilde{f}(z_{k+1})+\left\langle\tilde{g}_{k+1},y-z_{k+1}\right\rangle-\frac{m}{2}\|z_{k+1}-x_{k}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=f(z_{k+1})+\left\langle\tilde{g}_{k+1},y-z_{k+1}\right\rangle-\left(f(z_{k+1})+\frac{m}{2}\|z_{k+1}-x_{k}\|^{2}-\tilde{f}(z_{k+1})\right),

for any
y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. This confirms that
\tilde{g}_{k+1}=(\rho+m)(x_{k}-z_{k+1})=\alpha(x_{k}-z_{k+1})\in\partial f_{\epsilon_{k+1}}(z_{k+1})
with
\epsilon_{k+1}
given in
eq.

19b
.
∎

As shown in
eq.

19b
, the inexactness
\epsilon_{k+1}
is the same as the approximation gap at the next trial point
z_{k+1}
. Furthermore, since

eq.

18
is an exact proximal update for the approximated function
\tilde{f}
, so we must have

\tilde{f}(z_{k+1})\leq\tilde{f}(x_{k})-\frac{1}{2\rho}\|w_{k+1}\|^{2}\leq f(x_{k})-\frac{1}{2\rho}\|w_{k+1}\|^{2}

(23)

where the first inequality comes from
eq.

14
, and the second inequality is due to
eq.

Minr.
. We also note the following relationship

\|w_{k+1}\|=\frac{\rho}{\alpha}\|\tilde{g}_{k+1}\|.

We thus observe that this cost value drop
eq.

23
is bounded by a fraction of
\|\tilde{g}_{k+1}\|^{2}
, similar to the requirement
eq.

16
, with the exception that
eq.

23
measures the approximated function value
\tilde{f}(z_{k+1})
.

The value
f(x_{k})-\tilde{f}(z_{k+1})
can be viewed as the approximated cost value drop that is predicted by
\tilde{f}
. If
\tilde{f}(z_{k+1})
approximates the true value
f(z_{k+1})+\frac{m}{2}\|z_{k+1}-x_{k}\|^{2}
good enough in terms of

f(x_{k})-\left(f(z_{k+1})+\frac{m}{2}\|z_{k+1}-x_{k}\|^{2}\right)\geq\beta\left(f(x_{k})-\tilde{f}(z_{k+1})\right),

(Approx.)

where
\beta\in(0,1)
is a relaxation parameter, we will obtain the desired descent criterion
eq.

16
. In particular, we have the following result.

Lemma 5
.

Consider the
m
-weakly problem
eq.

1
. Let
x_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\tilde{f}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
be a convex function that satisfies the lower approximation
eq.

Minr.
. Denote
z_{k+1}
as the proximal update in
eq.

18
. If this point
z_{k+1}
satisfies
eq.

Approx.
, then we have

\displaystyle f(z_{k+1})

\displaystyle\leq f(x_{k})-\frac{m+\beta\rho}{\alpha}\times\frac{1}{2\alpha}\|\tilde{g}_{k+1}\|^{2},

(24a)

\displaystyle\epsilon_{k+1}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1-\beta}{\beta}\left(f(x_{k})-f(z_{k+1})-\frac{m}{2\alpha^{2}}\|\tilde{g}_{k+1}\|^{2}\right),

(24b)

with
\tilde{g}_{k+1}\in\partial f_{\epsilon_{k+1}}(z_{k+1})
.

Proof.

We observe the following property.

\displaystyle f(z_{k+1})

\displaystyle\leq f(x_{k})-\frac{m}{2}\|z_{k+1}-x_{k}\|^{2}-\beta\left(f(x_{k})-\tilde{f}(z_{k+1})\right)

\displaystyle\leq f(x_{k})-\frac{m}{2}\|z_{k+1}-x_{k}\|^{2}-\frac{\beta}{2\rho}\|w_{k+1}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=f(x_{k})-\frac{m+\beta\rho}{\alpha}\times\frac{1}{2\alpha}\|\tilde{g}_{k+1}\|^{2},

where the first inequality is a rearrangement of
eq.

Approx.
, the second inequality applies
eq.

23
, and the last inequality uses
w_{k+1}=\rho(x_{k}-z_{k+1})
and the relationship
\|w_{k+1}\|=\frac{\rho}{\alpha}\|\tilde{g}_{k+1}\|
. When
eq.

Approx.
holds, the inexactness parameter
\epsilon_{k+1}
also satisfies

\displaystyle\epsilon_{k+1}

\displaystyle\leq(1-\beta)(f(x_{k})-\tilde{f}(z_{k+1}))

(25)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1-\beta}{\beta}\left(f(x_{k})-\left(f(z_{k+1})+\frac{m}{2}\|z_{k+1}-x_{k}\|^{2}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1-\beta}{\beta}\left(f(x_{k})-f(z_{k+1})-\frac{m}{2\alpha^{2}}\|\tilde{g}_{k+1}\|^{2}\right).

where
\tilde{g}_{k+1}\in\partial f_{\epsilon_{k+1}}(z_{k+1})
is defined in
eq.

19a
.
∎

If a trial point
z_{k+1}
satisfies
eq.

Approx.
, we denote this step as

z_{k+1}=\texttt{ProxDescent}(x_{k},\beta,\rho),

(26)

which means that
z_{k+1}

Remark 3.1

(Approximation criteria)
.

The approximation criterion

is very commonly used in bundle-type methods
[

,

]
.
Another potential approximation criterion might be

4.1
Proximal bundle updates for
ProxDescent

We here detail a classical bundle idea to achieve the proximal descent step
eq.

26
. This step has two criteria: 1) a global convex lower approximation in
eq.

Minr.
; and 2) an appropriate approximation quality for the next proximal point in
eq.

Approx.
. In particular, any convex lower approximation
\tilde{f}
in
eq.

Minr.
with a proximal mapping guarantees an inexact subgradient for the original function, as shown in
eq.

19a
; and the approximation quality
eq.

Approx.
guarantees the inexact update
eq.

24
.

We now consider a fixed proximal center
x_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. For any convex lower approximation
\tilde{f}
satisfying
eq.

Minr.
, we have

\displaystyle\min_{x}\left\{\tilde{f}(x)+\frac{\rho}{2}\|x-x_{k}\|^{2}\right\}\leq f_{\alpha}(x_{k}):=\min_{x}\left\{f(x)+\frac{\alpha}{2}\|x-x_{k}\|^{2}\right\},

(30)

where we recall
\alpha=m+\rho
.
If
eq.

Approx.
is not satisfied, we will update
\tilde{f}
such that the value
\min_{x}\{\tilde{f}(x)+\frac{\rho}{2}\|x-x_{k}\|^{2}\}
gets closer to the true Moreau envelope value
f_{\alpha}(x_{k})
. With these iterations, the criterion
eq.

Approx.
will be satisfied eventually.

For notational convenience, let an index
j\geq 1
and
\tilde{f}_{j}
be a convex lower approximation satisfying
eq.

Minr.
, and we denote

\displaystyle z_{j+1}=\argmin_{x}\left\{\tilde{f}_{j}(x)+\frac{\rho}{2}\|x-x_{k}\|^{2}\right\},\quad\eta_{j}:=\min_{x}\left\{\tilde{f}_{j}(x)+\frac{\rho}{2}\|x-x_{k}\|^{2}\right\}.

(31)

From the optimality condition
s_{j+1}:=\rho(x_{k}-z_{j+1})\in\partial\tilde{f}_{j}(z_{j+1})
, we observe that

\displaystyle\eta_{j}=\min_{x}\left\{\tilde{f}_{j}(z_{j+1})+\left\langle s_{j+1},x-z_{j+1}\right\rangle+\frac{\rho}{2}\|x-x_{k}\|^{2}\right\}.

(32)

This means that the minimization problems in
eq.

31
and
eq.

32
have the same optimal value and the same minimizer.
Thus, if the next lower approximation
\tilde{f}_{j+1}
satisfies
eq.

Minr.
, as well as

\displaystyle\tilde{f}_{j+1}(\cdot)

\displaystyle\geq\tilde{f}_{j}(z_{j+1})+\left\langle s_{j+1},\cdot-z_{j+1}\right\rangle,

(Aggr.)

then the next optimal value
\eta_{j+1}:=\min_{x}\{\tilde{f}_{j+1}(x)+\frac{\rho}{2}\|x-x_{k}\|^{2}\}
is guaranteed to satisfy

\displaystyle\eta_{j}\leq\eta_{j+1}\leq f_{\alpha}(x_{k})

where the first inequality is due to
eq.

Aggr.
and the second inequality is due to
eq.

Minr.
.

To ensure a strict improvement, we further require
\tilde{f}_{j+1}
incorporates a subgradient of
f(\cdot)+\frac{m}{2}\|\cdot-x_{k}\|^{2}
at the trial point
z_{j+1}
, i.e., there is a
g_{j+1}\in\partial\left(f(\cdot)+\frac{m}{2}\|\cdot-x_{k}\|^{2}\right)(z_{j+1})
such that

\displaystyle\tilde{f}_{j+1}(\cdot)\geq f(z_{j+1})+\frac{m}{2}\|z_{j+1}-x_{k}\|^{2}+\left\langle g_{j+1},\cdot-z_{j+1}\right\rangle.

We have a key technical result that quantifies the improvement in terms of the proximal value

Lemma 6

Consider an

-weakly convex function

4.3

In this section, we re-interpret

in

f_{k}\leq f+\frac{m}{2}\|\cdot-x_{k}\|^{2}

\displaystyle z_{k+1}=\argmin_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\;f_{k}(x)+\frac{\rho}{2}\|x-x_{k}\|^{2}.

(38)

To decide if
z_{k+1}
is a good solution to the original problem

\displaystyle\beta(f(x_{k})-f_{k}(z_{k+1}))\leq f(x_{k})-\left(f(z_{k+1})+\frac{m}{2}\|z_{k+1}-x_{k}\|^{2}\right),

(39)

, which is the same as

. If
eq.

(

(

Regardless of a descent or null step, we construct a new function

satisfying the conditions below.

Assumption 4.1
.

The convex function
f_{k+1}
satisfies three conditions:

1.

Lower approximation:
Global convex lower approximation,

f_{k+1}(x)\leq f(x)+\frac{m}{2}\|x-x_{k+1}\|^{2},\quad\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

2.

4.4
Connections and complexity comparison

The use of proximal bundle updates for solving weakly convex optimization problems is not new. This idea can be traced back to
[
9
,
8
]
, and a recent work
[
20
]
also explored a similar idea. In particular, the works
[
9
,
8
]
focus on minimizing a broader class of functions, namely lower-
C^{2}
functions, and therefore do not provide iteration complexity guarantees. In this work, we establish iteration complexity results in
Theorems

2
and
4.2
finding an inexact stationary point. The recent work
[
20
]
modifies the classical proximal bundle update to obtain an inexact solution to the proximal subproblem
eq.

10
. In contrast, as established in
Section

4.3
, our
Algorithm

3
retains the classical proximal bundle update and can be viewed as a natural generalization of the proximal bundle method
[
7
,
14
,
15
]
from convex optimization to nonconvex optimization.

We here compare our main complexity results in
Theorems

2
and
4.2
with several closely related works
[
2
,
19
,
29
,
30
,
31
,
20
]
. First, in terms of finding an
(\delta,\alpha)
-Moreau stationary point, our result in
corollary

4.2
is comparable to the subgradient method in
[
2
, Theorem 3.1]
(see
lemma

3
), both of which require
\mathcal{O}(1/\delta^{4})
iteration complexity. However, the subgradient method is less practical as 1) its stepsize depends on the total number of iterations and 2) the stationarity measure
\min_{k=1,\ldots,T}\|\nabla f_{\alpha}(x_{k})\|
is implicit and not easily computable.
In contrast,
Algorithm

3
is guaranteed to converge for any parameter
\alpha>m
(or
\rho>0
), and relies on two explicit and easily computable quantities
\tilde{g}_{k+1}
and
\epsilon_{k+1}
in
eq.

19
to certify whether an iterate is
(\eta,\epsilon)
-stationary.

Second, in terms of finding an
(\eta,\epsilon)
-inexaxt stationary point, our
theorem

2
matches the state-of-the-art complexity result in
[
20
, Theorem 3.3]
, both of which require
\mathcal{O}\left(\left(\frac{1}{\eta^{2}}+\frac{1}{\epsilon}\right)\max\left\{\frac{1}{\eta^{2}},\frac{1}{\epsilon}\right\}\right)
function value and subgradient evaluations. However, as we have clarified above, our
Algorithm

3
is more aligned with the classical proximal bundle method
[
14
,
15
]
that uses sufficient objective descent in
Equation

Approx.
. In contrast, the algorithm in
[
20
]
uses a different and potentially more complicated algorithm structure.

Third, our
Algorithm

3
is developed within the framework of the inexact proximal point method. A related algorithm in this framework is the proximally guided stochastic subgradient method (PGSG) in
[
19
]
. Although PGSG was originally designed for stochastic optimization, its deterministic variant can be interpreted as applying the subgradient method as a subroutine to inexactly solve the proximal subproblem
eq.

10
. In contrast, our
Algorithm

3
adapts the proximal bundle idea to inexactly solve the proximal subproblem.
While both algorithms share the same algorithmic framework, the criteria used by their respective subroutines to certify an inexact solution to
eq.

differ. Specifically, PGSG uses a
predetermined
number of subgradient iterations, determined by the target accuracy, whereas our
Algorithm

3
applies the dynamic test in
eq.

Approx.
to adaptively determine termination. This makes
Algorithm

more flexible and potentially more efficient in practice. Our numerical experiments in
Section

6
further confirm this advantage.

Finally, two other classes of algorithms that can minimize
m
-weakly convex functions are the Interpolated Normalized Gradient Descent (INGD) method in
[
30
,
29
]
and the gradient sampling method in
[
31
]
.
One key difference is that we use the notion of inexact subdifferential in
eq.

7
, whereas the INGD and the gradient sampling approximate with probability the notion of
Goldstein subdifferential

\hat{\partial}_{\delta}f(x):=\mathrm{conv}\left(\bigcup_{y\in\mathbb{B}_{\delta}(x)}\partial f(x)\right)

where
\delta>0
and
\mathbb{B}_{\delta}(x):=\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid\|y-x\|\leq\delta\}
. As a result, our
Algorithm

3
is deterministic, while the INGD and the gradient sampling are probabilistic. Moreover, while the INGD and the gradient sampling algorithms work for a more general class of nonconvex and nonsmooth functions, their convergence guarantees are much weaker than
Theorems

2
and
4.2
.

5.2
Nonconvex functions with quadratic growth

In convex nonsmooth optimization, algorithms generally have an improved convergence rate under the quadratic growth property, i.e., there exists
\mu_{\mathrm{q}}>0

\displaystyle f(x)-\min_{x}f(x)\geq\frac{\mu_{\mathrm{q}}}{2}\cdot\mathrm{dist}^{2}(x,S),\;\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{n},

[

and the proximal bundle method
[

[

\displaystyle 2\mu_{\mathrm{p}}(f(x)-\min_{x}f(x))\leq\mathrm{dist}^{2}(0,\partial f(x)),\;\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

An immediate implication of the PL inequality is that every stationary point is a global minimizer. It is also known that, under these assumptions (i.e., weakly convex functions satisfying the PL inequality), the proximal point method in

Remark 5.1

(Approximate stationarity versus cost value gap)
.

Throughout this paper, we have focused on finding an (approximate) stationary point (i.e.,
\mathrm{dist}(0,\partial f_{\epsilon}(x))\leq\eta
and
\|\nabla f_{\alpha}(x)\|\leq\delta
) for an
m
-weakly convex function (
theorems

2
,
3
,
5
and
6
). Our results hold for both the convex case (in which

) and the nonconvex case (in which

). In the convex case, our complexity bounds in
theorems

and
5
may appear worse than those of the classical proximal bundle method in
[
15
]
, which are
\mathcal{O}(1/\epsilon^{2})
for convex Lipschitz functions and

under additional quadratic growth. However, we emphasize that these discrepancies are due to the change of measure from finding an approximate stationary point to an

-optimal solution (i.e.,

). Our results are indeed comparable to the results in
[

]
when switching to the measure

. With
m
-weak convexity, quadratic growth
eq.

41
, and
\mu_{\mathrm{q}}>m
, it is possible to establish an iteration complexity of
\mathcal{O}(1/\epsilon)
for the cost value gap
f(x)-\min_{x}f(x)
, even for the nonconvex case.

where

. This means that
\alpha\|x_{k}-x_{k+1}\|
serves as a proxy of the near-stationary up to the error
\alpha\|x_{k+1}-\hat{x}_{k}\|
. When

and
\hat{x}_{k}
are close, the measure
\alpha\|x_{k}-x_{k+1}\|
approximately indicates the stationarity (see
[
19
, Remark 1]
for related discussion).

7
Conclusion

In this paper, we have introduced a proximal descent algorithm for solving weakly convex optimization problems.
The algorithm is built upon the framework of the inexact proximal point method, with each subproblem efficiently addressed via a proximal bundle update. From a methodological perspective, our work extends the well-studied proximal bundle method from the convex setting to the broader class of weakly convex functions. From a theoretical perspective, we established explicit non-asymptotic convergence guarantees: the algorithm finds near-stationary points at the best-known rates for weakly convex optimization, and it automatically accelerates under additional structural conditions such as smoothness or quadratic growth. Finally, our numerical experiments validate the theoretical findings, illustrating the effectiveness and robustness of the proposed method for solving practical non-convex problems.

We conclude by highlighting a few directions for future research:

•

Weakly convex parameter and growth condition:

Algorithm

3
requires the knowledge of the weakly convex parameter
m
. In practice, however,
m
may be unknown even though the objective is weakly convex. An interesting direction is to design adaptive schemes that estimate or learn the weak convexity parameter on the fly, thereby broadening the applicability of the method in real-world settings. In addition,

Section

5.2
focused on the quadratic growth condition
eq.

41
. A natural extension is to consider the more general growth condition

\displaystyle f(x)-\min_{x}f(x)\geq\frac{\mu}{2}\cdot\mathrm{dist}^{\alpha}(x,S),\;\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{n},

where
\mu>0
,
\alpha\geq 1
, and
S=\argmin_{x}f(x)
. Investigating this condition could yield sharper convergence guarantees, paralleling the recent advances for convex proximal bundle methods
[
15
]
.

•

Convergence to local minima:
Examining the proof for
theorem

4
, we find that the assumption can be relaxed while still guaranteeing the same improved iteration complexity.
The linear convergence result in
theorem

4
relies on the linear decay of the function value gap
f(x)-f^{\star}
, which follows from the quadratic growth condition
eq.

41
together with the assumption
\mu_{\mathrm{q}}>m
(in which case no local minima exist). When the function admits local minima, if it satisfies a quadratic growth-type condition in a neighborhood of a local minimum, and assuming the algorithm converges to that local minimum, then the same complexity bound can still be established. This raises a natural open question: Does the proximal descent method always converge to a local minimum?

•

Constrained weakly convex optimization and applications to control-theoretic problems.
Robust control was one of the earliest motivations and applications for nonsmooth optimization
[
34
]
. However, control problems naturally involve nonconvex domains, since the set of stabilizing policies is nonconvex
[
35
]
. Recent advances on benign nonconvexity
[
36
,
37
,
38
]
suggest that many control problems may in fact be weakly convex over compact regions. This makes it particularly interesting to extend the proximal descent method to constrained weakly convex optimization, thereby opening new opportunities for applications in control-theoretic settings.

Appendix A
Different notions of stationarity and their relationship

In this appendix, we review different notions of stationarity, including gradient norm,
(\eta,\epsilon)
-inexact stationarity, and
(\delta,\alpha)
-Moreau stationarity. We also summarize their relationship.

A.1
Different notions of stationarity

For an
M
-smooth function
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
, a point
x
is stationary if
\nabla f(x)=0
, and a natural measure of near-stationarity is the norm of its gradient
\|\nabla f(x)\|
. If
\|\nabla f(x)\|
is small, we say
x
is close to a stationary point. For a closed function
f
, its Fréchet subdifferential at
x
with
f(x)
finite is defined as

\partial f(x)=\left\{v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid\liminf_{y\to x}\frac{f(y)-f(x)-\left\langle v,y-x\right\rangle}{\|y-x\|}\geq 0\right\}.

If
f
is further
m
-weakly convex, the subdifferential
\partial f
can be equivalently characterized as

\displaystyle\partial f(x)=\left\{v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid f(y)\geq f(x)+\left\langle v,y-x\right\rangle-\frac{m}{2}\|y-x\|^{2},\;\forall y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\right\}.

We say
x
is a stationary point if
0\in\partial f(x)
. However, the quantity
\mathrm{dist}(0,\partial f(x))
may not be a good measure of near stationarity, since
\mathrm{dist}(0,\partial f(x))
may be a discontinuous function. For example, consider
x\mapsto|x|
, we have
\mathrm{dist}(0,\partial f(x))=1,\forall x\neq 0
, and
\mathrm{dist}(0,\partial f(x))=1,\text{if}\,x=0
.

To quantify approximated stationarity for
m
-weakly convex functions, we have considered two notions in the main text:
(\eta,\epsilon)
-inexact stationarity, and
(\delta,\alpha)
-Moreau stationarity.
An inexact version of the subdiffernetial
\partial f
introduces an inexactness
\epsilon>0
and defines

\displaystyle\partial_{\epsilon}f(x)=\left\{v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid f(y)\geq f(x)+\left\langle v,y-x\right\rangle-\frac{m}{2}\|y-x\|^{2}-\epsilon,\;\forall y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\right\}.

Ideally, we aim to find a point
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that
\mathrm{dist}(0,\partial_{\epsilon}f(x))\leq\eta
with both
\epsilon
and
\eta
being small. Thus, we consider the notion of
(\eta,\epsilon)
-inexact stationarity.

Definition A.1

(
(\eta,\epsilon)
-inexact stationarity)
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
be an
m
-weakly convex function, and
\eta>0,\epsilon>0
. A point
x\in\mathrm{dom}\,f
is called an
(\eta,\epsilon)
-stationary point if

\displaystyle\mathrm{dist}(0,\partial f_{\epsilon}(x))\leq\eta.

When
\epsilon=0
and
\eta=0
, an
(\eta,\epsilon)
-inexact stationary point becomes a true stationary point, i.e.,
0\in\partial f(x)
. Another stationary measure uses the notion of the Moreau envelope, defined as

f_{\rho}(x):=\inf_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left\{f(y)+\frac{\rho}{2}\|y-x\|^{2}\right\},

where
\rho>0
is fixed. For a
m
-weakly convex function and any
\rho>m
, the function
f_{\rho}
becomes continuous differentiable (see
lemma

2
), and the gradient can be computed as

\nabla f_{\rho}(x)=\rho(x-\hat{x}),

where
\hat{x}:=\argmin_{y}\{f(y)+\frac{\rho}{2}\|y-x\|^{2}\}
is unique. Moreover, we have the following result.

Proposition A.1
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
be an
m
-weakly convex function,
\rho>m
and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Then
\nabla f_{\rho}(x)=\{0\}
if and only if
0\in\partial f(x)
.

Proof.

The proof follows easily from
lemma

2
. Suppose
\nabla f_{\rho}(x)=\{0\}
. From
eq.

6
, we know that
x=\argmin_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\{f(y)+\frac{\rho}{2}\|y-x\|^{2}\}
and therefore
0\in\partial f(x)+\rho(x-x)=\partial f(x)
.

Conversely, suppose
0\in\partial f(x)
. Then
0\in\partial f(x)+\rho(x-x)
, showing that
x\in\argmin_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\{f(y)+\frac{\rho}{2}\|y-x\|^{2}\}
. By
eq.

6
, we know
x
is the unique minimizer and
\nabla f_{\rho}(x)=\rho(x-x)=0
.
∎

This motivates the following approximate stationary notion.

Definition A.2

(
(\delta,\alpha)
-Moreau stationarity)
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
be an
m
-weakly convex function,
\delta>0,
and
\alpha>m
. A point
x\in\mathrm{dom}\,f
is called an
(\delta,\alpha)
-Moreau stationary point if

\displaystyle\|\nabla f_{\alpha}(x)\|\leq\delta.

Clearly, if
\delta=0
,
(\delta,\alpha)
-Moreau stationarity recovers
\|\nabla f_{\alpha}(x)\|=0
, i.e.,
x
is a stationary point.

A.2
Properties and their relationship

In this subsection, we establish the relationship between different stationary measures.

Lemma A.1

(
(\eta,\epsilon)
-inexact stationarity to
(\delta,\alpha)
-Moreau stationarity)
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
be an
m
-weakly convex function. Suppose
x
is a
(\eta,\epsilon)
-inexact stationary point. Then we have

\displaystyle\|\nabla f_{m+\lambda}(x)\|\leq(m+\lambda)\left(\frac{2}{\lambda}\eta+\sqrt{\frac{2}{\lambda}\epsilon}\right),\quad\forall\lambda>0.

If
\lambda=m
, it simplifies to

\|\nabla f_{2m}(x)\|\leq 4\eta+2\sqrt{2m\epsilon}.

Proof.

Since
x
is a
(\eta,\epsilon)
-inexact stationary point and
\partial_{\epsilon}f(x)
is a closed set, there exists a
w\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that

\displaystyle\|w\|\leq\eta,\quad f(y)+\frac{m}{2}\|y-x\|^{2}\geq f(x)+\left\langle w,y-x\right\rangle-\epsilon,\;\forall y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

(A.1)

Let
\lambda>0
and
\hat{x}=\argmin_{y}\{f(y)+\frac{m+\lambda}{2}\|y-x\|^{2}\}
. Since
f(\cdot)+\frac{m+\lambda}{2}\|\cdot-x\|^{2}
is
\lambda
-strongly convex, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\lambda}{2}\|\hat{x}-x\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq f(x)+\frac{m+\lambda}{2}\|x-x\|^{2}-\left(f(\hat{x})+\frac{m+\lambda}{2}\|\hat{x}^{\lambda}-x\|^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\overset{(a)}{\leq}f(x)-\left(f(\hat{x})+\frac{m}{2}\|\hat{x}-x\|^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\overset{(b)}{\leq}\left\langle w,x-\hat{x}\right\rangle+\epsilon

\displaystyle\overset{(c)}{\leq}\eta\|\hat{x}-x\|+\epsilon

where
(a)
drops a nonpositive term
\frac{\lambda}{2}\|\hat{x}-x\|^{2}
,
(b)
uses
eq.

A.1
with
y=\hat{x}
, and
(c)
uses Cauchy-inequality with the bound
\|w\|\leq\eta
in
eq.

A.1
.
Therefore, we obtain

\displaystyle\|\hat{x}-x\|^{2}-\frac{2}{\lambda}\eta\|\hat{x}-x\|-\frac{2}{\lambda}\epsilon\leq 0.

Since the above is a quadratic function in terms of
\|\hat{x}-x\|
, the quantity
\|\hat{x}-x\|
can not be larger than its right root. We thus have

\displaystyle\|\hat{x}-x\|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{2}{\lambda}\eta+\sqrt{\frac{4}{\lambda^{2}}\eta^{2}+\frac{8}{\lambda}\epsilon}\right)\leq\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{4}{\lambda}\eta+\sqrt{\frac{8}{\lambda}\epsilon}\right)=\frac{2}{\lambda}\eta+\sqrt{\frac{2}{\lambda}\epsilon}.

Lastly, using the relationship
\nabla f_{m+\lambda}(x)=(m+\lambda)(\hat{x}-x)
from
eq.

6
, we obtain

\displaystyle\|\nabla f_{m+\lambda}(x)\|\leq(m+\lambda)\left(\frac{2}{\lambda}\eta+\sqrt{\frac{2}{\lambda}\epsilon}\right).

This completes the proof.
∎

Lemma A.2

8

where the first inequality uses the

Lemma A.4

(Consequences of the proximal gap)
.

Assume

is
m
-weakly convex. Fix

,

, and
\alpha=m+\rho

•

\Delta_{x}
-inexact subgradient of

•

(\Delta_{x},\sqrt{2\rho\Delta_{x}})

(\sqrt{\frac{2\Delta_{x}\alpha^{2}}{\rho}},\alpha)

Let
\hat{x}^{\rho}=\argmin_{y}\{f(y)+\frac{m+\rho}{2}\|y-x\|^{2}\}

\displaystyle(m+\rho)(x-\hat{x}^{\rho})\in\partial f(\hat{x}^{\rho}).

. As

-weakly convex, for all

4

\displaystyle f(y)+\frac{m}{2}\|y-\hat{x}^{\rho}\|^{2}\geq f(\hat{x}^{\rho})+\left\langle v,y-\hat{x}^{\rho}\right\rangle,

\displaystyle f(y)+\frac{m}{2}\|y-\hat{x}^{\rho}\|^{2}+\frac{m}{2}\|y-x\|^{2}\geq\frac{m}{2}\|y-x\|^{2}+f(\hat{x}^{\rho})+\left\langle v,y-\hat{x}^{\rho}\right\rangle.

\displaystyle f(y)+\frac{m}{2}\|y-x\|^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{m}{2}\|y-x\|^{2}-\frac{m}{2}\|y-\hat{x}^{\rho}\|^{2}+f(x)-f(x)+f(\hat{x}^{\rho})+\left\langle v,y-\hat{x}^{\rho}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\overset{(a)}{=}f(x)+\frac{m}{2}\|x-\hat{x}^{\rho}\|^{2}+m\left\langle\hat{x}^{\rho}-x,y-\hat{x}^{\rho}\right\rangle-f(x)+f(\hat{x}^{\rho})+\left\langle v,y-\hat{x}^{\rho}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=f(x)+\left\langle w,y-x\right\rangle-\left(f(x)-f(\hat{x}^{\rho})-\frac{m+\rho}{2}\|x-\hat{x}^{\rho}\|^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=f(x)+\left\langle w,y-x\right\rangle-\Delta_{x},

where
(a)

with

and

(b)
uses

, and

adds and subtracts the term

.

On the other hand, due to the

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\rho}\|w\|^{2}=\frac{\rho}{2}\|x-\hat{x}^{\rho}\|^{2}\leq\Delta_{x},

Lemma A.6
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
be a convex function and
\epsilon>0
. The function
x\mapsto\min_{v\in\partial_{\epsilon}f(x)}\|v\|
is continuous for any given
\epsilon>0
.

Proof.

The proof can be established through the following steps:

•

Since
f
is locally bound, for any
\epsilon>0
, the set value map
\partial_{\epsilon}f
is also locally bounded
[
40
, Corollary 1]
;

•

Fix any
\bar{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Since
f
is convex, it holds that
\lim_{x\to\bar{x}}\mathbb{H}(\partial_{\epsilon}f(x),\partial_{\epsilon}f(\bar{x}))=0

[
40
, Proposition 5]
, where
\mathbb{H}
is the Hausdorff distance (i.e., Pompeiu-Hausdorff distance) defined as

\displaystyle\mathbb{H}(A,B):=\max\left\{\sup_{x\in A}\;\mathrm{dist}(x,B),\sup_{y\in B}\;\mathrm{dist}(y,A)\right\}

for two closed sets
A,B\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

•

Since
\partial_{\epsilon}f
is locally bounded, it holds that
\lim_{x\to\bar{x}}\mathbb{H}(\partial_{\epsilon}f(x),\partial_{\epsilon}f(\bar{x}))=0
if and only if the set value map
\partial_{\epsilon}f
is continuous at
\bar{x}

[
24
, Corollary 5.21]
;

•

Since
\partial_{\epsilon}f
is continuous at
\bar{x}
, for any sequence
\{x_{k}\}
converges to
\bar{x}
, we have
\partial_{\epsilon}f(x_{k})\to\partial_{\epsilon}f(x)
. The minimal norm element also converges as
\lim_{x\to\bar{x}}\argmin_{v\in\partial_{\epsilon}}f(x)=\argmin_{v\in\partial_{\epsilon}}f(\bar{x})

[
24
, Proposition 4.9]
. This completes the proof.

∎

Lemma C.1
.

Suppose

is an
m
-weakly convex function. At every iteration

\displaystyle f(x_{k+1})\leq f(x_{k})-\beta\Delta_{k}.

Proof.

By the update rule of

, we know that there is a lower approximation function
\tilde{f}\leq f(\cdot)+\frac{m}{2}\|\cdot-x_{k}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\Delta_{k}

\displaystyle=f(x_{k})-\inf_{x}\left\{f(x)+\frac{\alpha}{2}\|x-x_{k}\|^{2}\right\}

\displaystyle\leq f(x_{k})-\inf_{x}\left\{\tilde{f}(x)+\frac{\rho}{2}\|x-x_{k}\|^{2}\right\}

where the first equality is due to, the second equality is from

Note that the cost value improvement in

Lemma C.2

Let

m
-weakly convex function and satisfies

C.3

\mu_{\mathrm{q}}>m

and
\alpha=m+\rho

\rho>0

and

Then the Moreau envelope can be estimated as

\displaystyle f_{\alpha}(w)=\inf_{x}\left\{f(x)+\frac{\alpha}{2}\|x-w\|^{2}\right\}\leq\begin{cases}f(w)-\Lambda+\frac{\rho}{2}\|w-x^{\star}\|^{2},&\text{ if }\Lambda>\rho\|w-x^{\star}\|^{2}\\

(C.5)

Proof.

The proof is inspired by
[

and follows from a few simple steps. To begin, we first recall a property for weakly convex functions
[
2
, Lemma 2.1]
. That is,

-weakly convex if and only if

\displaystyle f(\gamma x+(1-\gamma)y)\leq\gamma f(x)+(1-\gamma)f(y)+\frac{\gamma(1-\gamma)m}{2}\|x-y\|^{2},\;\forall x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\forall\gamma\in[0,1].

(C.6)

Therefore, it holds that

\displaystyle f_{\alpha}(w)=

\displaystyle\inf_{x}f(x)+\frac{\alpha}{2}\|x-w\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\inf_{t\in[0,1]}f((1-t)w+tx^{\star})+\frac{\alpha}{2}\|(1-t)w+tx^{\star}-w\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\inf_{t\in[0,1]}(1-t)f(w)+tf(x^{\star})+\frac{t(1-t)m}{2}\|w-x^{\star}\|^{2}+\frac{\alpha t^{2}}{2}\|w-x^{\star}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f(w)+\inf_{t\in[0,1]}-t\Lambda+\frac{\rho}{2}t^{2}\|x^{\star}-w\|^{2},

where the first inequality restricts the searching space to the line segment between

-weakly convex function

\displaystyle t^{\star}=\min\left\{1,\frac{\Lambda}{\rho\|x^{\star}-w\|^{2}}\right\}.

Plugging in the minimizer

This leads to the bound on the total function value and subgradient evaluation

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{T}T_{k}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{8(1+m/\rho)^{2}L^{2}}{(1-\beta)^{2}\rho}\frac{1}{\kappa(1-m/\mu_{\mathrm{q}})\sigma}\sum_{k=1}^{T}\gamma^{T-k}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{8(1+m/\rho)^{2}L^{2}}{(1-\beta)^{2}\rho}\frac{1}{\kappa(1-m/\mu_{\mathrm{q}})\sigma(1-\gamma)}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{8(1+m/\rho)^{2}L^{2}}{(1-\beta)^{2}\rho}\frac{1}{\kappa(1-m/\mu_{\mathrm{q}})(1-\gamma)\min\left\{\frac{m+\beta\rho}{2\alpha^{2}},\frac{\beta}{1-\beta}\right\}}\max\left\{\frac{1}{\eta^{2}},\frac{1}{\epsilon}\right\}.

This completes the proof.
