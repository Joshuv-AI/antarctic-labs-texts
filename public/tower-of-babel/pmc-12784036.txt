Comparison of Cosmos caudatus extract and vitamin C effects on antioxidant response and bone metabolism in rats under high-intensity exercise-induced oxidative stress
Authors: Gadis Meinar Sari, Idha Kusumawati, Siti Khaerunnisa, Yoga Akbar Arifandi, Julian Benedict Swannjo, Zahras Azimuth Doman, Zulhabri Othman
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract[Purpose]High-intensity exercise increases oxidative stress, disrupts bone remodeling and accelerate bone loss. While vitamin C is commonly used to counteract these effects, high doses may act as pro-oxidants. Cosmos caudatus, a traditional herb rich in antioxidants, may serve as a natural alternative. In this study compared the effects of C. caudatus extract and vitamin C on oxidative stress and bone health in a rat model of exercise-induced oxidative stress.[Methods]Fifty-four male Sprague-Dawley rats (3–4 months old, 200–300 g) were divided into five groups (10–11 rats each). Group P1 received distilled water, whereas groups P2–P4 received C. caudatus extract at 0.91, 1.82, and 3.64 mg/0.5 mL/200 g BW. Group P5 was administered vitamin C at 9 mg/0.5 mL/200 g BW. Treatments were administered orally for 28 days. Rats underwent high-intensity swimming with an 18% BW load at 90% maximal swim time (T90), twice daily, three times weekly. Femoral and blood samples were collected for malondialdehyde (MDA), osteocalcin (OC), C-telopeptide (CTX), trabecular bone ratio, and bone cell analyses.[Results]MDA levels were significantly elevated across all groups (p = 0.007), particularly in the high-dose C. caudatus and vitamin C. However, trabecular bone ratio improved significantly in medium-dose Cosmos caudatus and vitamin C groups (p = 0.016).[Conclusion]These findings suggested that medium-dose C. caudatus supports bone integrity comparably to vitamin C and may exert osteoprotective effects beyond direct reactive oxygen species (ROS) scavenging, offering a potentially safer alternative for bone health under oxidative stress.Keywords: Sports nutrition, Cosmos caudatus, oxidative stress, high-intensity interval exercise, bone microarchitecture, sports health, antioxidant

INTRODUCTION

Indonesia’s demographic profile is dominated by a productive-age population, with over 69% falling within the 15–64 age bracket [1]. This group regularly engages in physical activity, either occupationally or recreationally. Such activity can significantly increase the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS), especially during intense or prolonged exercise. The body’s natural antioxidants may become overwhelmed by excess ROS, leading to oxidative stress and potential cell damage, particularly in tissues such as blood and skeletal muscles, which are major sources of ROS during physical activity [2,3]. Over time, chronic oxidative stress contributes to inflammation, reduced bone density, and muscle fatigue, ultimately impairing performance and increasing the risk of injury [4,5]. Antioxidants, found in food, supplements, and traditional Indonesian herbal medicines, counteract ROS by neutralizing free radicals, thereby supporting musculoskeletal health and enhancing post-exercise recovery [6,7].

Vitamin C (ascorbic acid) is a well-known antioxidant that scavenges ROS, enhances immune function and supports collagen synthesis. It also serves as a cofactor in enzymatic reactions essential for tissue healing and energy metabolism [8]. However, at high concentrations and in the presence of metals such as Fe³⁺ and Cu²⁺, vitamin C may act as a pro-oxidant by triggering the Fenton reaction, thereby increasing ROS production and cellular damage [9,10]. These paradoxical effects raise concerns regarding the safety of high-dose vitamin C supplementation for maintaining bone health under oxidative stress [11].

As an alternative, Cosmos caudatus, a traditional medicinal plant widely used in Indonesia and Southeast Asia, stands out as a promising candidate due to its long history of application in treating various ailments [12]. For instance, 100 g of fresh C. caudatus contains approximately 64.6 mg of vitamin C, a moderate amount considered safer for regular use compared with megavitamin supplementation [13]. In addition to vitamin C, C. caudatus is rich in flavonoids and phenolic compounds such as quercetin, kaempferol, myricetin, luteolin, and catechins, which have been shown to exert antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and immunomodulatory effects [14-16]. Studies in ovariectomized rat models demonstrated that supplementation with C. caudatus extract improved bone histomorphometric parameters, including trabecular volume, trabecular number, and trabecular separation, indicating a bone-protective role against estrogen deficiency and oxidative stress [17]. More recent findings revealed that a 28-day administration of C. caudatus extract enhanced cellular antioxidant activity (e.g., superoxide dismutase), reduced bone resorption markers such as C-telopeptide, and promoted osteoblast activity as well as trabecular bone formation [18]. Compared with vitamin C alone, the diverse phytochemical profile of C. caudatus may provide broader protection against oxidative stress and facilitate tissue recovery, including direct benefits for bone remodeling.

Therefore, in this study, we aimed to compare the effects of Kenikir (C. caudatus) extract and vitamin C on oxidative stress, osteoblast activity, and trabecular bone microarchitecture in a rat’s model of exercise-induced oxidative stress.

METHODS

Study Design and Participants

This study involved fifty-four (n = 54) adult male rats, aged 3–4 months and; weighing 200–300 g, obtained from the Experimental Animal Laboratory, Faculty of Medicine, Universitas Airlangga. Animals were acclimatized for seven days under controlled laboratory conditions (temperature 22–24°C, 12 h light/dark cycle) and were provided with a diet of rodent chow and distilled water. Following acclimatization, the rats were randomly allocated into five groups (n = 13 per group) using a computerized random number generator.

The sample size was estimated using G*Power version3.1.9 software. A medium effect size, as defined by Cohen (1988), was used in the calculations. The experimental design consisted of five distinct groups, with an alpha level (α) set at 0.05 and an expected statistical power of 0.8. The analysis yielded a minimum sample size of 55 rats to achieve the desired power and significance levels. To account for potential attrition, the initial sample size was adjusted based on a dropout rate of 14.5%, derived from previous research involving similar experimental conditions [19]. Incorporating this dropout rate resulted in a final calculated sample size of approximately 63 (62.98, rounded to 63 for practical purposes) [20].

Procedures

All rats underwent a high-intensity swimming protocol adapted from Sari et al. (2020). Each rat was loaded with 18% of its body weight (BW) using a tail-mounted load positioned 1 cm (approximately 0.39 in) from the base of the tail. The protocol consisted of two swim intervals at 90% of each rat’s maximum swim time (T₉₀), measured from the time the rats were introduced into the pool until exhaustion (inability to swim and visible air bubbling). Rest periods between intervals were twice the T₉₀. Exercise was conducted three times per week over a 28-day period [21].

During the exercise period, treatments were administered orally once daily using a gastric gavage in a total volume of 0.5 mL per 200 g BW. The dosing regimen followed Kusumawati and Prajarto (2016) [22] as summarized in Table 1.

Sample Collection

At the end of the 28-day intervention, the rats were euthanized, and the right femoral bones and blood samples were harvested by trained personnel. Femoral metaphysis sections were fixed, decalcified, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) for histological evaluation. Digital micrographs were captured using a Nikon H600L light microscope equipped with a DS-Fi2 300-megapixel camera. Quantitative histomorphometry of osteoblasts, osteoclasts, osteocytes, and trabecular bone ratios was performed using ImageJ software.

The collected blood samples were centrifuged to isolate the serum, which was then analyzed using an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) to quantify oxidative stress biomarkers and bone metabolism indicators, such as malondialdehyde (MDA), osteocalcin (OC), and C-terminal telopeptide (CTX), following the manufacturer’s instructions.

Statistical Analysis

Data are presented as medians with interquartile ranges (Q1–Q3). The normality of the data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk and Kolmogorov–Smirnov tests. Based on these tests, several variables—specifically MDA levels, trabecular ratio, osteoblast count, and osteoclast count—did not meet the assumption of normality (p < 0.05), wheres OC, CTX, and osteocyte count were normally distributed (p > 0.05). Given the non-parametric distribution of key variables, the Kruskal-Wallis test was applied to evaluate overall differences among groups. Post-hoc analysis was performed using Dunn’s test with Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons. All statistical analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.4.3)

Ethical Clearance

This study was approved by the Animal Ethics Committee Unit, Faculty of Veterinary Medicine, Airlangga University, under the approval code 2.KEH.116.07.2023, ensuring that all procedures were conducted ethically and in accordance with applicable norms.

RESULTS

At the end of the 28-day experimental period, 54 of the 65 rats survived, yielding an overall survival rate of approximately 83%. The attrition, which occurred across different groups, was likely attributable to the physiological strain induced by the high-intensity exercise protocol, compounded by the effects of oxidative stress.

A progressive increase in MDA, a marker of oxidative stress, was observed from the control group (P1, 2.48 Umol/L) to the positive control group receiving vitamin C (P5, 3.14 Umol/L), indicating an overall rise in oxidative load. OC levels remained relatively stable, with the lowest median observed in the low-dose C. caudatus group (P2, 12.1 ng/mL) and the highest in the vitamin C group (P5, 16.5 ng/mL). CTX a bone resorption marker, showed greater variability, peaking in the high-dose C. caudatus group (P4, 7.47 ng/mL) and reaching the lowest value in the low-dose C. caudatus group (P2, 5.99 ng/mL).

Trabecular bone ratios were elevated at P3 (28%), P4 (27%), and P5 (29%), relative to P1 (22%) and P2 (21%), suggesting improvements in bone microarchitecture among the higher-dose intervention groups. Osteoblast counts ranged from 54 cells/FOV (P3) to 76 cells/FOV (P5), with wider variability in P1 and P5. Osteoclast counts were highest in P5 (7.7 cells/FOV), more than double those observed in P1, P2, and P4 (3.1–3.4 cells/FOV). Osteocyte numbers were elevated in P3 (102 cells/FOV) and P5 (101 cells/FOV), with the lowest median found in P2 (71 cells/FOV). Overall, P5 consistently demonstrated higher medians for oxidative, structural, and cellular markers, indicating a robust biological response to vitamin C supplementation.

Figure 1 presents boxplots illustrating biomarker levels, trabecular ratios, and bone cell counts for the five groups (P1–P5), expressed as medians with IQR. As shown in Figure 1A, OC levels were highest in P5 (vitamin C group) and lowest in the low-dose C. caudatus group (P2), indicating enhanced bone formation with greater antioxidant support. Notably, P3 (medium dose) and P4 (high dose) showed intermediate values, suggesting a dose-dependent effect. MDA levels progressively increased from P1 to P5, reflecting heightened oxidative stress, particularly at higher doses, possibly due to metabolic activation or systemic compensatory mechanisms. CTX, a marker of bone resorption, was most elevated in P4 and P5, while P2 exhibited the lowest levels, indicating potential suppression of bone turnover at lower doses.

As shown in Figure 1B, the trabecular bone ratio was markedly elevated in the medium- and high-dose C. caudatus groups (P3 and P4) as well as in the vitamin C group (P5), suggesting improved bone microarchitecture. The greatest variability was observed in P3, indicating heterogeneous structural remodeling in response to the medium dose.

As shown in Figure 1C, osteoblast counts peaked in P5 and were relatively higher in P2, while P3 and P4 showed modest declines—suggesting optimal stimulation at low doses and in the vitamin C group. Osteoclast counts remained low and stable in P1, P2, and P4, but increased in P5, indicating active remodeling. Osteocyte counts were highest in P3 and P5; and lowest in P2, suggesting that medium-dose C. caudatus and vitamin C more effectively promote osteocyte viability.

DISCUSSION

In this study, we evaluated the effects of C. caudatus (Kenikir) extract on oxidative stress and bone remodeling in a rat model of high-intensity exercise-induced oxidative stress. We hypothesized that C. caudatus, with its antioxidant and osteoprotective phytochemicals, would reduce oxidative damage and improve trabecular bone integrity. The findings indicate that medium-dose C. caudatus (P3, 1.82 mg/0.5 mL/200 g BW) effectively preserved bone microarchitecture and enhanced osteocyte viability under oxidative stress, showing results comparable to those observed with vitamin C (P5, 9 mg/0.5 mL/200 g BW).

Groups P3 and P5 exhibited significantly elevated trabecular bone ratios (P3: 28; P5: 29) compared with the control group P1 (22) (p = 0.001), as well as higher osteocyte counts (P3: 102; P5: 101). These improvements were not present in the low-dose group (P2) or the control group (P1), suggesting a dose-responsive effect. Interestingly, the high dose of C. caudatus group(P4) did not exceed the performance of P3, indicating that increasing the dosage may not yield additional benefits and could potentially result in harmful oxidative effects.

MDA, a reliable indicator of lipid peroxidation, was identified as a sensitive predictor of the systemic oxidative state in this model. MDA levels showed a consistent rise in all treatment groups, peaking in P5 (3.14 μmol/L) and P4 (3.12 μmol/L), both of which were significantly higher than the control group P1 (2.48 μmol/L) (p < 0.05). Although these groups exhibited improved bone metrics, the concurrent increase in oxidative indicators suggests that high-dose antioxidant supplementations may paradoxically exacerbate the oxidative burden.

Previous studies have demonstrated that high levels of vitamin C, when combined with transition metals such as Fe³⁺ and Cu²⁺, can enhance the Fenton reaction. This process generates hydroxyl radicals that have the potential to inflict DNA damage and lead to cellular toxicity [9,10]. A similar process might be associated with the heightened polyphenol levels in C. caudatus, which, like other plants rich in flavonoids, can participate in redox cycling and exhibit pro-oxidant effects at higher concentrations [23,24].

The recorded mild MDA level in P3 (2.93 μmol/L), combined with improved trabecular architecture and cellular survival, suggesteds that an appropriate antioxidant dosage may provide structural protection while avoiding significant oxidative reactions. These findings support a growing body of research indicating that antioxidant therapies exhibit a biphasic or hormetic response, in which low to moderate doses offer benefits, whereas excessive amounts may be ineffective or harmful.

Although there were no statistically significant differences in the bone turnover markers OC and CTX among the groups, the observed patterns were biologically meaningful. OC levels, indicative of bone formation, were elevated at P5 and P3, corresponding with their enhanced trabecular structure. CTX levels varied across groups and lacked a clear dose-dependent trend, suggesting a dynamic balance between bone formation and resorption under oxidative stress. Among the bone cell parameters, osteoblast numbers peaked in P5, whereas osteocyte counts were highest in both P3 and P5. These finding support the hypothesis that both treatments enhanced bone cell viability and turnover.

These findings are consistent with earlier studies conducted on ovariectomized rats, which demonstrated that supplementation with C. caudatus has a positive effect on trabecular quantity and bone volume [25]. Moreover, the similar effects observed between C. caudatus and vitamin C in our study align with the findings of Mohamed et al. (2012), who reported analogous improvements in bone calcium content and microarchitecture in estrogen-deficient rats treated with either substance [25]. These data highlight the potential of C. caudatus as a viable phytotherapeutic option for bone protection.

These effects may be attributed to the phytochemical content present in C. caudatus. The leaves are notably rich in phenolic compounds and vitamin C, providing approximately 64.6 mg of vitamin C per 100 g, along with an antioxidant capacity equivalent to 2500 mg of ascorbic acid. These compounds not only demonstrate antioxidant properties but may also play a role in regulating signaling pathways associated with bone health.

Vitamin C is known for its capacity to enhance the expression of specific osteogenic and regulatory genes, such as TGF-β, ER-α, OPN, Runx2, Osterix, and BMP-2, all of which contribute to osteoblast development and bone matrix formation. Given its composition, C. caudatus may activate similar pathways, offering both a buffering effect against ROS and promoting anabolic processes in bone.

Oxidative stress plays a crucial role in the regulation of Nrf2, a key transcription factor that controls antioxidant response elements and maintains cellular redox balance. Although this study did not directly assess Nrf2 activity, current models suggest that oxidative stress impedes Nrf2 signaling, leading to increased osteoclastogenesis via the MAPK pathway and upregulation of MYC expression [11,26,27]. This pathway may help explain the elevated osteoclast counts observed in P5, where the combination of a high antioxidant dosage and increased ROS might impede the normal feedback inhibition of osteoclast activity. These interactions highlight the complexity of redox regulation in bone tissue and the importance of maintaining balance during antioxidant therapy.

Despite these positive results, this study has several limitations. First, although critical oxidative and structural biomarkers were assessed, molecular data concerning downstream signaling pathways, such as Nrf2, MAPK, and the expression of osteogenic genes, were lacking. As a result, the underlying mechanisms remain speculative and require further investigation. Second, the 28-day research period may not be sufficient to capture long-term structural changes or potential cumulative toxicity associated with prolonged high-dose supplementation. Third, the C. caudatus extract used in this study was not chemically standardized or profiled, meaning that the concentrations of specific bioactive components, such as flavonoids and ascorbic acid, remain unspecified and may vary across different preparations. Finally, although the rat model offers valuable insights into bone response to oxidative stress, its applicability to human physiology is limited. Further clinical trials are necessary to assess the safety, bioavailability, and efficacy of C. caudatus in humans, particularly in populations vulnerable to bone loss due to oxidative stress.

In conclusion, medium-dose C. caudatus extract (1.82 mg/0.5 mL/200 g BW) signficantly improved trabecular bone microarchitecture, showing effect comparable to those of vitamin C, despite elevated systemic oxidative stress. These benefits may arise from direct modulation of osteogenic signaling rather than classical antioxidant effects. Future studies are warranted to explore the underlying molecular pathways and to evaluate long-term outcomes in oxidative bone loss models.
