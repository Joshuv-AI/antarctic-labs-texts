The homogeneous and inhomogeneous Dirichlet problem Dedicated to Marius Mitrea on his 60 th birthday, with friendship and appreciation
David S. Jerison, Carlos E. Kenig

Abstract
We revisit the homogeneous and inhomogeneous Dirichlet problem for the Laplacian on Lipschitz domains. This is motivated by the recent postings by Amrouche and Moussaoui which purport to contradict known area integral estimates of Dahlberg and known Sobolev space estimates of Jerison and Kenig. We explain the fatal gap in the reasoning in these postings and give a self-contained proof of a special case of the results of Dahlberg. We then show that this is sufficient to disprove the central conclusions of these postings. We also provide two further proofs of the results of Dahlberg, adapted from work of Kenig and of Dahlberg-Kenig-Pipher-Verchota. Other proofs are in the original paper by Dahlberg and in work by Fabes-Mendez-Mitrea.



The classical theorem concerning the inhomogeneous Dirichlet problem (or Poisson problem) says that for every bounded, open set
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
and every
f\in H^{-1}(\Omega)
, there is a unique
u\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
that solves

\displaystyle\Delta u\

\displaystyle=\ f.

(1)

(The spaces
H^{-1}
,
H_{0}^{1}
,
H^{2}
,
H^{s}
are the standard
L^{2}
-based Sobolev spaces.) Furthermore,
u
satisfies

\displaystyle\|\nabla u\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\

\displaystyle=\ \|f\|_{H^{-1}(\Omega)}.

(2)

In other words,
\Delta^{-1}
is an isometry from
H^{-1}(\Omega)
to
H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
, provided
H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
is equipped with the norm
\|u\|_{H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)}=\|\nabla u\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}
and
H^{-1}(\Omega)
with the dual norm.

We can only expect higher regularity of

under further assumptions on
f
and the domain
\Omega
.
For a bounded Lipschitz domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, one can choose the norm
\|\cdot\|_{\ast}
on the space of
u\in H^{2}(\Omega)\cap H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
to be

\displaystyle\|u\|_{\ast}^{2}\

\displaystyle=\ \int\limits_{\Omega}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\left(\partial_{i}\partial_{j}u\right)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\quad\mbox{with}\ \ x=(x_{1},\,x_{2},\,\dots\,,\,x_{n})\ \ \mbox{and}\ \ \partial_{j}=\partial/\partial x_{j}.

In 1963, J. Kadlec proved the optimal second-order regularity for the inhomogeneous Dirichlet problem in convex domains.
A special case of this theorem is as follows
[
21
]
.

Theorem 0.1
.

If
\Omega
is a bounded, open, convex subset of

, then for every

the unique function

such that
\Delta u=f
satisfies

\displaystyle\|u\|_{\ast}\,

\displaystyle\leq\,\|f\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}.

In other words, the inverse mapping
\Delta^{-1}\colon L^{2}(\Omega)\to H^{2}(\Omega)\cap H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
is well-defined and a contraction under the

norm.
(For optimal regularity up to third order, see
[
14

).

In essence, Kadlec’s proof follows from an integration by parts formula in the plane.
Suppose that
\Omega
is a smooth, bounded, planar domain,
u\in C^{2}(\overline{\Omega})
and
u=0
on
\partial\Omega

Then

\displaystyle\int\limits_{\Omega}\left[(\partial_{xx}u)(\partial_{yy}u)-(\partial_{xy}u)^{2}\right]\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y\

\displaystyle=\ \int\limits_{\partial\Omega}\kappa\left(\frac{\partial u}{\partial\nu}\right)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\sigma

(3)

with

the normal derivative,

the arc length measure of

In particular, if

\displaystyle\int\limits_{\Omega}\left(\partial_{xy}u\right)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y\

\displaystyle\leq\ \int\limits_{\Omega}\left(\partial_{xx}u\right)\left(\partial_{yy}u\right)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y.

It follows from Fubini’s theorem and by summing over all pairs

that for smooth, bounded, convex domains
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
and functions

satisfying

, we have

\displaystyle\int\limits_{\Omega}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\left(\partial_{i}\partial_{j}u\right)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\

\displaystyle\leq\ \int\limits_{\Omega}\left(\Delta u\right)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x,\quad x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}).

From this inequality, Kadlec deduced Theorem
0.1

[
16

In the case of polygons

\displaystyle\int\limits_{\Omega}\left[\left(\partial_{xx}u\right)\left(\partial_{yy}u\right)-\left(\partial_{xy}u\right)^{2}\right]\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y\

\displaystyle=\ 0

The proof in

It follows easily from Fubini’s theorem that the analogous statement holds true for polyhedra in all dimensions.
Moreover, the identity remains valid for functions in the closure in

of this class of functions

.

Remark 2.2

We will give a second proof of Theorem

Proof.

We begin with an explanation of the idea of the proof in the special case in which

is smooth and

Then we will use an approximation argument to give a complete proof.

For
z=x+{\mathrm{i}}y\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2}

\displaystyle H(z)\

\displaystyle=\ F\circ\Phi(z)(\Phi^{\prime}(z))^{1/2}.

\displaystyle\int\limits_{\partial\Omega}|F(\zeta)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\sigma(\zeta)\

) applied to

) gives

\displaystyle\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}|H(x)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\

\displaystyle=\ 4\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2}}y|H^{\prime}(z)|\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y.

Next, we write

\displaystyle H^{\prime}(z)\

\displaystyle=\ \left(F^{\prime}\circ\Phi(z)\right)\left(\Phi^{\prime}(z)\right)^{3/2}+\frac{1}{2}\left(F\circ\Phi(z)\right)\left(\Phi^{\prime}(z)\right)^{-1/2}\Phi^{\prime\prime}(z)

\displaystyle=\ M(z)+E(z).

The first term,

.
Indeed, using Proposition

\displaystyle A\

\displaystyle=\ \int\limits_{\Omega}\delta(\zeta)|F^{\prime}(\zeta)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\xi\,\mathrm{d}\eta\ =\ \int\limits_{\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2}}\delta(\Phi(z))|F^{\prime}\circ\Phi(z)|^{2}|\Phi^{\prime}(z)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y

In other words,

\displaystyle\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2}}y|M(z)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y\

To estimate the error term

\displaystyle\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2}}y|E(z)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y\

\displaystyle=\ \frac{1}{4}\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2}}y|F\circ\Phi(z)|^{2}\frac{|\Phi^{\prime\prime}(z)|^{2}}{|\Phi^{\prime}(z)|}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y\ =\ \int\limits_{\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2}}|H(z)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mu(z)

\displaystyle=\ \sqrt{1+|\nabla_{z}F(z,t)|^{2}}\left(\nu_{n+1}\partial_{j}-\nu_{j}\partial_{y}\right)f

\displaystyle=\ \sqrt{1+|\nabla_{z}F(z,t)|^{2}}T_{j}f.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\int\limits_{\mathcal{S}_{t}}\theta^{2}\left(T_{j}f_{1}\right)f_{2}\,\mathrm{d}\sigma_{t}\

\displaystyle=\ \int\limits_{|z|<2}\theta(z)^{2}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial z_{j}}f_{1}\left(z,F(z,t)\right)\right)f_{2}\left(z,F(z,t)\right)\,\mathrm{d}z

\displaystyle=\ -\int\limits_{|z|<2}f_{1}\left(z,F(z,t)\right)\frac{\partial}{\partial z_{j}}\left(\theta(z)^{2}f_{2}(z,F(z,t))\right)\,\mathrm{d}z

\displaystyle=\ -\int\limits_{\mathcal{S}_{t}}f_{1}T_{j}\left(\theta^{2}f_{2}\right)\,\mathrm{d}\sigma_{t}.

Next, since
\nu_{n+1}^{2}+\sum_{j=1}^{n}\nu_{j}^{2}=1
and
\Delta u=0
, we have

\displaystyle\partial_{y}^{2}u\

\displaystyle=\ \sum_{j=1}^{n}\nu_{j}T_{j}\partial_{y}u+\nu_{n+1}\sum_{j=1}^{n}T_{j}\partial_{j}u.

(15)

Finally, using the co-area formula
[
12
]
, (
15
), (
14
), and the property that
T_{j}\rho=0
, we find that

\displaystyle\left|\int_{\{0<\rho<2\}}II\,\mathrm{d}X\right|

\displaystyle\qquad=\ \left|\sum_{j}\int_{0}^{2}\int_{\mathcal{S}_{t}}\rho(\partial_{y}u)T_{j}\left(\frac{\theta^{2}u\partial_{j}\rho}{|\nabla\rho|^{2}\partial_{y}\rho}\right)+\rho(\partial_{j}u)T_{j}\left(\frac{\theta^{2}u}{|\nabla\rho|^{2}}\right)\,\mathrm{d}\sigma_{t}\,\mathrm{d}t\right|

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\ C\int\limits_{\Omega_{2}}\rho|\nabla u|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}X+C\int\limits_{\Omega_{2}}|u|\,|\nabla u|(\rho|\nabla^{2}\rho|)\,\mathrm{d}X+C\int\limits_{\Omega}\rho|u|\,|\nabla u|\,|\nabla\theta^{2}|\,\mathrm{d}X

\displaystyle\leq\ CA_{\Omega_{3}}(u)+CA_{\Omega_{3}}(u)^{1/2}\left(\int_{\mathcal{B}_{2}}N(u1_{\Omega_{2}})\,\mathrm{d}\sigma\right)^{1/2}.

The first inequality above follows by grouping terms of three types.
The first type is the ones in which
T_{j}
acts on
u
.
The second type is the ones for which
T_{j}
acts on
\partial_{j}\rho
,
\partial_{y}\rho
, and
|\nabla\rho|^{2}
.
The last type arises when
T_{j}
acts on
\theta^{2}
.
For the second inequality, the first term is dominated by
CA_{\Omega_{3}}
simply because
\Omega_{2}\subset\Omega_{3}
.
The second term is controlled in exactly the same way as term
III
above.
The final term is estimated using only elementary inequalities.
The Cauchy-Schwarz inequality implies

\displaystyle\int\limits_{\Omega_{2}}\rho|u|\,|\nabla u|\,|\nabla\theta^{2}|\,\mathrm{d}X\

\displaystyle\leq\ \left(\int_{\Omega_{2}}\rho|\nabla u|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}X\right)^{1/2}\left(\int_{\Omega_{2}}\rho u^{2}\,\mathrm{d}X\right)^{1/2}.

Moreover, since
|u(z,F(z,t))|\leq N(u)(z,\varphi(z))
and
\sqrt{1+|\nabla_{z}F(z,t)|^{2}}\leq C
,

\displaystyle\int\limits_{\Omega_{2}}\rho u^{2}\,\mathrm{d}X\

\displaystyle=\ \int\limits_{0}^{2}t\int\limits_{\mathcal{S}_{t}}u^{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma_{t}}{|\nabla\rho|}\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\leq\ C\int\limits_{0}^{2}t\,\mathrm{d}t\int\limits_{\mathcal{B}_{2}}N(u1_{\Omega_{2}})^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\sigma

\displaystyle=\ C\int\limits_{\mathcal{B}_{2}}N(u1_{\Omega_{2}})^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\sigma.

Thus, the third term is dominated by the same expression as the second term.
This concludes the proof of Lemma
4.4

Recall that on a Lipschitz domain

\displaystyle\delta(X)\

\displaystyle=\ \min_{Q\in\partial\Omega}|X-Q|,\quad\Gamma(Q)\ =\ \left\{X\in\Omega:|X-Q|\leq C\delta(X)\right\}

for

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
\Omega
be a bounded Lipschitz domain in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
,
n\geq 1
.
There exist constants
C_{1},C_{2}>0
depending only on
n
and the Lipschitz character of
\Omega
, and a compact set

, such that

(a)

\dist(X,\partial\Omega)\geq C_{2}
for all
X\in K
.

(b)

If

is harmonic in
\Omega
and

,
\nabla u
, and

belong to
C^{\gamma}(\overline{\Omega})
for some

\displaystyle\int\limits_{\partial\Omega}u^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\sigma\

\displaystyle\leq\ C_{1}\left[A_{\Omega}(u)+A_{\Omega}(u)^{1/2}\left(\int_{\partial\Omega}N_{\Omega}(u)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\sigma\right)^{1/2}+\int_{K}u^{2}\,\mathrm{d}X\right].

Lemma
4.5
follows from Lemma
4.4
by an easy covering argument.

Lemma 4.6
.

Under the assumptions of Lemma
4.5
, there exist constants
C_{3},C_{4}>0
depending only on
n
and the Lipschitz character of
\Omega
, and a compact set
K\subset\Omega
, such that
\dist(X,\partial\Omega)\geq C_{3}
for all
X\in K
, and

\displaystyle\int\limits_{\partial\Omega}N(u)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\sigma\

\displaystyle\leq\ C_{4}\left[A_{\Omega}(u)+\int_{K}u^{2}\,\mathrm{d}X\right].

Proof.

By work of Dahlberg
[
9
]
, every harmonic function
u
on a bounded Lipschitz domain that is continuous up to the boundary satisfies

\displaystyle\int\limits_{\partial\Omega}N_{\Omega}(u)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\sigma\

\displaystyle\leq\ C\int\limits_{\partial\Omega}u^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\sigma

with
C
a constant depending only on the dimension and the Lipschitz character of
\Omega
.
This combined with Lemma
4.5
implies

\displaystyle\int\limits_{\partial\Omega}N_{\Omega}(u)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\sigma\

\displaystyle\leq\ CC_{1}\left[A_{\Omega}(u)+A_{\Omega}(u)^{1/2}\left(\int_{\partial\Omega}N(u)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\sigma\right)^{1/2}+\int_{K}u^{2}\,\mathrm{d}X\right]

\displaystyle\leq\ CC_{1}\left[A_{\Omega}(u)+\frac{1}{\epsilon}A_{\Omega}(u)+\epsilon\int_{\partial\Omega}N_{\Omega}(u)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\sigma+\int_{K}u^{2}\,\mathrm{d}X\right].

Since
\int_{\partial\Omega}N_{\Omega}(u)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\sigma<\infty
, we can choose
\epsilon=2/CC_{1}
, and subtract, to show that

\displaystyle\int\limits_{\partial\Omega}N_{\Omega}(u)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\sigma\

\displaystyle\leq\ 2CC_{1}\left[(1+1/\epsilon)A_{\Omega}(u)+\int_{K}u^{2}\,\mathrm{d}X\right],

which proves Lemma
4.6
.
∎

Conclusion of the proof of Theorem
4.1
.

We now deduce our theorem using approximation.
Following Nec̆as
[
25
]
and Verchota
[
29
]
, there exist
C^{\infty}
domains
U_{j}
such that
\overline{U}_{j}\subset\Omega
and
U_{j}
have uniformly bounded Lipschitz character.
Furthermore, there are Lipschitz homeomorphisms
\Lambda_{j}\colon\partial\Omega\to\partial U_{j}
with
|Q-\Lambda_{j}(Q)|\to 0
uniformly for
Q\in\partial\Omega
as
j\to\infty
.

Consider a harmonic function
u
on
\Omega
such that
A_{\Omega}(u)<\infty
.
Since
u\in C^{\infty}(\overline{U}_{j})
for all
j
, we can apply Lemma
4.6
to obtain

\displaystyle\int\limits_{\partial U_{j}}N_{U_{j}}(u)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\sigma_{j}\

\displaystyle\leq\ C_{3}\left(A_{U_{j}}(u)+\int_{K}u^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\right)\ \leq\ C_{3}\left(A_{\Omega}(u)+\int_{K}u^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\right)

for some compact
K\subset\Omega
bounded away from
\partial\Omega
.
For each fixed
\epsilon>0
, the truncated maximal function

\displaystyle N_{\epsilon}(u)(Q)\

\displaystyle\coloneqq\ \sup\left\{|u(X)|:X\in\Gamma_{\Omega}(Q),|X-Q|>\epsilon\right\}

satisfies

\displaystyle N_{\epsilon}(u)(Q)\

\displaystyle\leq\ N_{U_{j}}(u)(\Lambda_{j}(Q))

for sufficiently large
j
depending on
\epsilon
.
Since
N_{\epsilon}(u)(Q)
is increasing in
\epsilon
and tends to
N_{\Omega}(Q)
for each

, the monotone convergence theorem implies

\displaystyle\int\limits_{\partial\Omega}N_{\Omega}(u)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\sigma\

\displaystyle\leq\ C_{3}\left(A_{\Omega}(u)+\int_{K}u^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\right).

A theorem of Hunt and Wheeden
[
18
]
says that the non-tangential limit

\displaystyle\lim_{X\in\Gamma_{\Omega}(Q)}u(X)\

\displaystyle=\ f(Q)

exists almost everywhere with respect to harmonic measure on
\partial\Omega
.
A theorem of Dahlberg
[
8
]
says that harmonic measure and surface measure

are mutually absolutely continuous.
Thus, by the dominated convergence theorem,
|f(Q)|\leq N_{\Omega}(u)(Q)
almost everywhere
\mathrm{d}\sigma
,

, and

\displaystyle\int\limits_{\partial\Omega}f^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\sigma\

\displaystyle\leq\ C_{3}\left(\int_{\Omega}\delta_{\Omega}(X)|\nabla u(X)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}X+\int_{K}u^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\right)

as desired.

Corollary 4.7
.

Let
\Omega
be a bounded Lipschitz domain in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
,
n\geq 1
.
Assume that
u
is harmonic in
\Omega
and
u\in L_{3/2}^{2}(\Omega)
.
Denote by
f
the non-tangential pointwise limit of
u
, almost everywhere
\mathrm{d}\sigma
.
Then
f\in L_{1}^{2}(\partial\Omega)
and
f=\Tr(u)
, where
\Tr(u)
is the Sobolev trace of
u
on
\partial\Omega
.

Proof.

Let
W_{j}=\frac{\partial u}{\partial x_{j}}
for
j=1,2,\ldots,n+1
.
Then
W_{j}\in L_{1/2}^{2}(\Omega)
, and by
[
20
, Theorem 4.2]
, the area integral square norm is equivalent modulo constants to the square of the
L_{1/2}^{2}(\Omega)
norm, and consequently,

\displaystyle\int\limits_{\Omega}\delta_{\Omega}(X)\left|\nabla W_{j}\right|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}X\

\displaystyle<\ \infty.

Thus, as in the proof of Theorem
3.1
,

\displaystyle\int\limits_{\partial\Omega}N_{\Omega}(W_{j})^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\sigma\

\displaystyle<\ \infty.

Moreover, for almost every
Q\in\partial\Omega
with respect to
\mathrm{d}\sigma
,
\nabla u
has a non-tangential limit
\vec{W}(Q)=(W_{1},W_{2},\ldots,W_{n+1})
with
W_{j}\in L^{2}(\partial\Omega,\mathrm{d}\sigma)
.

To show that
\Tr(u)\in L_{1}^{2}(\partial\Omega)
, it will suffice to prove it for
u
defined in a coordinate chart.

Lemma 4.8
.

Let
U_{1}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
be the unit cylinder and
\mathcal{D}_{1}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
the unit disk, defined by

\displaystyle U_{1}\

\displaystyle=\ \left\{(z,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}:|z|<a,0<t<1\right\};\quad\mathcal{D}_{1}\ =\ \left\{z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:|z|<1\right\}.

Suppose
v
is a locally Lipschitz continuous function on
U_{1}
belonging to the Sobolev space
L_{1}^{2}(U_{1})
.
Suppose, in addition, that there exists an
\mathbb{R}^{n}
-valued function
\vec{V}\in L^{2}(\mathcal{D}_{1},\mathrm{d}z)
such that

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}\int\limits_{\mathcal{D}_{1}}\left|\nabla_{z}v(z,t)-\vec{V}(z)\right|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}z\

\displaystyle=\ 0.

Then

(a)

There exists
v_{0}\in L^{2}(\mathcal{D}_{1},\mathrm{d}z)
such that
v_{0}(z)=\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}v(z,t)
for almost every
z\in\mathcal{D}_{1}
with respect to
\mathrm{d}z
.

(b)

\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}\int_{\mathcal{D}_{1}}|v(z,t)-v_{0}(z)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}z=0
.

(c)

v_{0}(z)=\Tr(v)(z,0)
for almost every
z\in\mathcal{D}_{1}
, where
\Tr(v)
is the Sobolev trace of
v
on the bottom boundary
\{t=0\}\cap\partial U_{1}
.

(d)

\nabla_{z}v_{0}(z)=\vec{V}(z)
.
In particular,
v_{0}\in L_{1}^{2}(\mathcal{D}_{1})
.

Proof.

Since
v
is absolutely continuous in
t
for
t>0
, we have

\displaystyle v_{0}(z)\

\displaystyle=\ -v(z,1/2)-\int\limits_{0}^{1/2}\partial_{t}v(z,t)\,\mathrm{d}t\text{ exists and }\ v_{0}(z)=\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}v(z,t),

provided we can show that the integral is convergent.
In fact, the bound

\int_{U_{1}}|\nabla v|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}z\,\mathrm{d}t<\infty

and Fubini’s theorem imply that the integral is absolutely convergent for almost every
z
with respect to
\mathrm{d}z
.
Furthermore, for all
0<t_{1}<t_{2}<1
,

\displaystyle\left|v(x,t_{2})-v(x,t_{1})\right|^{2}\

\displaystyle=\ \left|\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\partial_{t}v(x,t)\,\mathrm{d}t\right|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\ \left(t_{2}-t_{1}\right)\int\limits_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\left(\partial_{t}v(x,t)\right)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\leq\ \left(t_{2}-t_{1}\right)\int\limits_{0}^{1}\left(\partial_{t}v(x,t)\right)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t.

Integrating in
z
, we have

\displaystyle\int\limits_{\mathcal{D}_{1}}\left|v(z,t_{2})-v(z,t_{1})\right|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}z\

\displaystyle\leq\ \left(t_{2}-t_{1}\right)\int\limits_{U_{1}}\left|\partial_{t}v(z,t)\right|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}z\,\mathrm{d}t.

Using a telescoping sum, it follows that

\displaystyle\lim_{j\to\infty}\int\limits_{\mathcal{D}_{1}}\left|v(z,2^{-j})-v_{0}(z)\right|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}z\

\displaystyle=\ 0,

and from this, one easily deduces not only that
v_{0}\in L^{2}(\mathcal{D}_{1},\mathrm{d}z)
, but also part (b).

To prove (c), note that the subspace of Lipschitz continuous functions on
\overline{U}_{1}
is dense in
L_{1}^{2}(U_{1})
.
The reasoning above shows that for
v
in this subspace,

\displaystyle\int\limits_{\mathcal{D}_{1}}\left|v(z,s)-v(z,0)\right|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}z\

\displaystyle\leq\ s\int\limits_{U_{1}}\left|\nabla v(z,t)\right|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}z\,\mathrm{d}t.

The ordinary restriction of such continuous functions coincides with the Sobolev trace on every set
\{t=s\}
.
Then by continuity of the Sobolev trace operation from
L_{1}^{2}(U_{1})
to
L^{2}(\{t=s\})
, we can pass from the dense class to all of
L_{1}^{2}(U_{1})
, establishing (c).

Finally, we prove part (d) by a limiting argument.
Since as
t\to 0^{+}
,

\displaystyle v(\cdot,t)\to v_{0}(\cdot)\text{ and }\nabla_{z}v(\cdot,t)\to\vec{V}(\cdot)\text{ in }L^{2}(\mathrm{d}z),

the distribution
\nabla_{z}v_{0}
is defined for test functions
g\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathcal{D}_{1})
by

\displaystyle\left(\nabla_{z}v_{0}\right)(g)\

\displaystyle=\ -\int\limits_{\mathcal{D}_{1}}v_{0}(z)\nabla_{z}g(z)\,\mathrm{d}z\ =\ -\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}\int\limits_{\mathcal{D}_{1}}v(z,t)\nabla_{z}g(z)\,\mathrm{d}z

\displaystyle=\ \lim_{t\to 0^{+}}\nabla_{z}v(z,t)g(z)\,\mathrm{d}z\ =\ \int\limits_{\mathcal{D}_{1}}\vec{V}_{j}(z)g(z)\,\mathrm{d}z

as desired.
∎

We can now deduce Corollary
4.7
.
For any Lipschitz function
\varphi
, consider the mapping

\displaystyle\Psi\colon U_{1}\to\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\text{ given by }\Psi(z,t)\

\displaystyle=\ (z,t+\varphi(z))

and define

\displaystyle\Omega_{1}\

\displaystyle=\ \Psi\left(U_{1}\right),\quad\mathcal{B}_{1}\ =\ \left\{\Psi(z,0):z\in\mathcal{D}_{1}\right\}.

Every coordinate chart of
\Omega
in the neighborhood of
\partial\Omega
can be written in this form up to rigid motion and dilation.
If
\Omega_{1}
is a coordinate chart of
\Omega
with
\mathcal{B}_{1}\subset\partial\Omega
, and
u
satisfies the hypotheses of Corollary
4.7
, then
v(z,t)=u(\Psi(z,t))
satisfies the hypotheses of Lemma
4.8
.
Indeed,

\displaystyle\nabla_{z}v(z,t)\

\displaystyle=\ \nabla_{x}u(x,y)+\nabla_{z}\varphi(z)\partial_{y}u(x,y)\text{ for }(x,y)=\Psi(z,t),\quad(z,t)\in U_{1},

so that
\nabla v\in L_{loc}^{\infty}(U_{1})
and
v\in H^{1}(U_{1})
.
Recall that
\vec{W}=\nabla_{xy}u
.
The estimate above for
N_{\Omega}(W_{j})
,
1\leq j\leq n+1
, and the dominated convergence theorem imply

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to 0}\int\limits_{\mathcal{D}_{1}}\left|\frac{\partial v}{\partial z_{j}}(z,t)-\left[W_{j}(\Psi(z,0))+\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial z_{j}}W_{n+1}(\Psi(z,0))\right]\right|^{2}\sqrt{1+|\nabla_{z}\varphi(z)|^{2}}=0.

Thus, the final hypothesis of Lemma

is satisfied with

\displaystyle V_{j}(z)\

\displaystyle=\ W_{j}(\Psi(z,0))+\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial z_{j}}(z)W_{n+1}(\Psi(z,0)),\quad j=1,\ldots,n.

Applying the lemma, we have, in particular,
v_{0}\in L_{1}^{2}(\mathcal{D}_{1})
.
Finally, the bi-Lipschitz change of variables
\Psi(\cdot,0)
preserves non-tangential limits and sends
L_{1}^{2}(\mathrm{d}z)
to
L_{1}^{2}(\mathcal{B}_{1})
.
This concludes the proof of Corollary

.
∎

Lemma 4.10
.

Suppose that
1/2<\gamma<3/2
and
v\in L_{\gamma}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n+1})
.
Then

\displaystyle\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left|v(x,t)-v(x,0)\right|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\

\displaystyle\leq\ \int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left|\hat{v}(\xi,\tau)\right|^{2}|(\xi,\tau)|^{2\gamma}\,\mathrm{d}\xi\,\mathrm{d}\tau

\displaystyle=\ Cy^{2\gamma-1}\|v\|_{H^{\gamma}(\mathbb{R}^{n+1})}^{2}.

Proof.

By Plancherel’s theorem and the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality,

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left|v(x,t)-v(x,0)\right|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\ \int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}}(e^{{\mathrm{i}}tr}-1)\hat{v}(\xi,\tau)\,\mathrm{d}\tau\right|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\xi

\displaystyle\leq\ \int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}|e^{{\mathrm{i}}t\tau}-1|^{2}(|\xi|+|\tau|)^{-2\gamma}\,\mathrm{d}\tau\right)\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\hat{v}(\xi,\tau)|^{2}(|\xi|+|\tau|)^{2\gamma}\,\mathrm{d}\tau\right)\,\mathrm{d}\xi

\displaystyle\leq\ C_{\gamma}t^{2\gamma-1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\hat{u}(\xi,\tau)|^{2}(|\xi|+|\tau|)^{2\gamma}\,\mathrm{d}\tau\,\mathrm{d}\xi.

The second inequality follows from the computation

\displaystyle\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}\left|e^{{\mathrm{i}}t\tau}-1\right|^{2}(|\xi|+|\tau|)^{-2\gamma}\,\mathrm{d}\tau\

\displaystyle\leq\ \int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}|e^{{\mathrm{i}}t\tau}-1|^{2}|\tau|^{-2\gamma}\,\mathrm{d}\tau

\displaystyle=\ t^{2\gamma-1}\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}\left|e^{{\mathrm{i}}k}-1\right|^{2}|k^{-2\gamma}\,\mathrm{d}k

\displaystyle=\ C_{\gamma}t^{2\gamma-1}.

The integral over
k\in\mathbb{R}
is finite because
1/2<\gamma<3/2
.
∎

The lemma says, in particular, that the Sobolev trace
v(\cdot,t)
is continuous in the variable

in the
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
norm. (In fact, in our case,

is continuous for

norm to the trace boundary values.)

Since

By the dominated convergence theorem, and the fact that

is comparable to

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}\int\limits_{\{|x|<1\}}\left|v(x,t)-f(x)\right|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}\int\limits_{\{|x|<1\}}\left|u(x,\varphi(x)+t)-f(x)\right|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x=0.

Thus,
f(x)=v(x,0)
is the Sobolev trace.
∎

, the Lusin area function is defined by

\displaystyle S(u)(x)^{2}\

\displaystyle=\ \int\limits_{\Gamma((x,0))}\left|\nabla u(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\right|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x^{\prime}\,\mathrm{d}y^{\prime}.

Recall that the non-tangential maximal function is defined by

\displaystyle N(u)(x)\

\displaystyle=\ \sup_{Y\in\Gamma((x,0))}\left|u(Y)\right|.

The special case of the Gundy-Wheeden theorem that we need is as follows.

Theorem A.1
.

Suppose that
w
is an
A_{\infty}
weight on
\mathbb{R}
, and
u
is a harmonic function in the upper half-plane
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2}
.
If
S(u)\in L^{2}(w\,\mathrm{d}x)
, then there is a unique constant
c
such that
u(x,y)-c
tends to zero as
y\to\infty
, the non-tangential maximal function of
u-c
belongs to
L^{2}(w\,\mathrm{d}x)
, and

\displaystyle\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}N(u-c)^{2}w(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\

\displaystyle\leq\ C\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}S(u)(x)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x,

with a constant depending only on the
A_{\infty}
constant of the weight
w
.

Proof.

The weight
w
to which we apply this theorem is
w(x)=|\Phi^{\prime}(x)|
, which we showed in Theorem
1.9
(d) belongs to the Muckenhoupt class
A_{2}
.
As is well-known, the class
A_{2}
is a subset of the class
A_{\infty}
(see
[
28
, p. 196, 1.7]
).
In fact, the class
A_{\infty}
is sometimes defined as the union of the classes
A_{p}
for
1<p<\infty

[
28
, p. 196, 1.7]
.

Recall in the notation of Theorem
2.1
,
\zeta=\xi+{\mathrm{i}}\eta
and

\displaystyle A\

\displaystyle=\ \int\limits_{\Omega}\delta(\zeta)\left|F^{\prime}(\zeta)\right|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\xi\,\mathrm{d}\eta.

Let
\tilde{F}(z)=F\circ\Phi(z)
,
z\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2}
.
Changing variables and using Proposition
1.11
,

\displaystyle A\

\displaystyle=\ \int\limits_{\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2}}\delta(\Phi(z))\left|\tilde{F}^{\prime}(z)\right|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}x\ \simeq\ \int\limits_{\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2}}y\left|\Phi^{\prime}(z)\right|\left|\tilde{F}^{\prime}(z)\right|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y,

with
z=x+{\mathrm{i}}y
.
Next note that if
I=\{s\in\mathbb{R}:|s-x|<y\}
, then the lower bound on the Poisson kernel
y/\pi(t^{2}+y^{2})\geq 1/2\pi y
for
|t|\leq y
yields, for
w(s)=|\Phi^{\prime}(s)|
,

\displaystyle\int\limits_{I}w(s)\,\mathrm{d}s\

\displaystyle\lesssim\ \int\limits_{I}\re(\Phi^{\prime}(s))\,\mathrm{d}s\ \leq\ 2\pi y\re(\Phi^{\prime}(x+{\mathrm{i}}y))\ \lesssim\ y\left|\Phi^{\prime}(x+{\mathrm{i}}y)\right|.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}S(\tilde{F})^{2}(s)w(s)\,\mathrm{d}s\

\displaystyle=\ \int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(\int_{|s-x|<y}\left|\tilde{F}(x+{\mathrm{i}}y)\right|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y\right)w(s)\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=\ \int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2}}\left|\tilde{F}^{\prime}(x+{\mathrm{i}}y)\right|^{2}\left(\int_{|s-x|<y}w(s)\,\mathrm{d}s\right)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle\lesssim\ \int\limits_{\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2}}\left|\tilde{F}^{\prime}(x+{\mathrm{i}}y)\right|^{2}y\left|\Phi^{\prime}(x+{\mathrm{i}}y)\right|\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle\simeq\ A.

Since
F(\zeta)\to 0
as
|\zeta|\to\infty
, provided
\delta(\zeta)\geq\delta_{0}>0
, the same holds for
\tilde{F}(x+{\mathrm{i}}y)
in the region
y\geq y_{0}>0
.
Hence, Theorem
A.1
shows that

\displaystyle\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}N(\tilde{F})^{2}w(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\

\displaystyle\lesssim\ A.

In particular,
\tilde{F}
is bounded on almost every cone
\Gamma((x,0))
with respect to
w(x)\,\mathrm{d}x
.
By Calderón’s theorem (see
[
27
, Theorem 3, p.201]
), the non-tangential limit
\tilde{F}(x)
exists almost everywhere and

\displaystyle\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}\left|\tilde{F}(x)\right|^{2}w(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\

\displaystyle\lesssim\ A.

By Remark
1.12
,
F
also has non-tangential limits at almost every
\zeta\in\partial\Omega
with respect to
\mathrm{d}\sigma
, and
F(\zeta)=\tilde{F}(x)
for
x=\Phi^{-1}(\zeta)
.
In all,

\displaystyle\int\limits_{\partial\Omega}\left|F(\zeta)\right|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\sigma(\zeta)\

\displaystyle=\ \int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}\left|\tilde{F}(x)\right|^{2}w(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\ \lesssim\ A

as desired.
∎

As explained in Section 2, we also deferred to this appendix the one remaining step to prove Corollary
2.3
. Recall that it suffices to prove
the following proposition.

Proposition A.2
.

Let
\Omega
and
\epsilon_{0}>0
be as in Theorem
2.1
.
Suppose that
F
is analytic in
\Omega
, and

\int_{\Omega}\delta(\zeta)|F^{\prime}(\zeta)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\xi\,\mathrm{d}\eta=A<\infty,\quad(\zeta=\xi+{\mathrm{i}}\eta).

Then there exists
c\in\mathbb{C}
such that for any
\delta_{0}>0
,

\displaystyle F(\zeta)\to c\ \text{ as }|\zeta|\to\infty\quad{for}\ \ \zeta\in\Omega\ \ \mbox{satisfying}\ \ \delta(\zeta)\geq\delta_{0}.

Proof.

The main step of the argument is the same as the one above
between (
11
) and (
12
), localized to cones
\Gamma_{\alpha}(\zeta)
defined below. For each
\zeta\in\partial\Omega
, denote by

A(\zeta)=\int_{\Gamma_{2}(\zeta)}\delta(z)|F^{\prime}(z)|^{2}\,dxdy\quad(z=x+iy),

where
\Gamma_{\alpha}(\zeta)
is the cone with vertex
\zeta
given by

\Gamma_{\alpha}(\zeta)=\{\zeta+x+iy:|x|<(1+\alpha)y,\ \ (x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\}.

Let
z\in\Gamma_{0}(\zeta)
. We claim that

|F^{\prime}(z)|\lesssim A(\zeta)^{1/2}\delta(z)^{-3/2}.

(16)

To see this, consider
D
, the disk of radius
\delta(z)/100
around
z
. Then
since
|F^{\prime}|^{2}
is subharmonic, and
D\subset\Gamma_{2}(\zeta)
, we have

|F^{\prime}(z)|^{2}\leq\frac{1}{|D|}\int_{D}|F^{\prime}(\zeta)|^{2}\,d\xi d\eta\lesssim\delta(z)^{-3}\int_{D}\delta(\zeta)|F^{\prime}(\zeta)|^{2}\,d\xi d\eta\leq\delta(z)^{-3}A(\zeta).

Next, consider any
z
,
w\in\Gamma_{0}(\zeta)

such that
\delta(z)/10\leq\delta(w)\leq 10\delta(z)
. Then,
integrating along the line
z_{t}=tz+(1-t)w
, and applying (
16
),
we have

|F(z)-F(w)|=\left|\int_{0}^{1}(z-w)F^{\prime}(z_{t})\,dt\right|\lesssim A(\zeta)^{1/2}\delta(z)^{-1/2}.

Take any sequence
w_{k}\in\Gamma_{0}(\zeta)
such that

2^{k}\delta(z)\leq\delta(w_{k})\leq 2^{k+1}\delta(z)
,
k=1,\,2,\dots
.
Then the preceding estimate yields

|F(w_{k+1})-F(w_{k})|\lesssim A(\zeta)^{1/2}2^{-k/2}\delta(z)^{-1/2},

and hence the limit

c(\zeta)=\lim_{k\to\infty}F(w_{k})

exists. Since this is valid for any such sequence, we have

\lim_{|w|\to\infty}(F(w)-c(\zeta))=0\quad\mbox{for}\ \ w\in\Gamma_{0}(\zeta).

Furthermore, the cones
\Gamma_{0}(\zeta)
and
\Gamma_{0}(\zeta^{\prime})
overlap for sufficiently large
|w|
, so
c(\zeta)
is a constant, independent of
\zeta\in\partial\Omega
.

We also have for all
z\in\Gamma_{0}(\zeta)
, the estimate

\displaystyle|F(z)-c|

\displaystyle\leq|F(z)-F(w_{1})|+\sum_{k=}^{\infty}|F(w_{k+1}-F(w_{k})|

\displaystyle\lesssim\ A(\zeta)^{1/2}\delta(z)^{-1/2}\,\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}2^{-k/2}\ \lesssim\ A(\zeta)^{1/2}\delta(z)^{-1/2}.

Lastly, note that the monotone convergence theorem, and the fact that the full area integral,

\int_{\Omega}\delta(\zeta)|F^{\prime}(\zeta)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\xi\,\mathrm{d}\eta=A,

is finite imply
A(\zeta)\to 0
as
|\zeta|\to\infty
,
\zeta\in\partial\Omega
.
In all, if
w\in\Omega
,
\delta(w)\geq\delta_{0}>0
, and
|w|\to\infty
, then either

\delta(w)\to\infty
in which case the estimate

|F(w)-c|\lesssim A(\zeta)^{1//2}\delta(w)^{-1/2}\leq A^{1/2}\delta(w)^{-1/2}\to 0,

or else
w\in\Gamma_{0}(\zeta)
with
|\zeta|\to\infty
, and we have

|F(w)-c|\lesssim A(\zeta)^{1//2}\delta(w)^{-1/2}\lesssim A(\zeta)^{1//2}\delta_{0}^{-1/2}\to 0.

This concludes the proof of the proposition, and hence also Corollary
2.3
.
∎
