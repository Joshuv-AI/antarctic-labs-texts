The impact of natural and engineered extracellular vesicles on post-myocardial infarction angiogenesis
Authors: Hongzhou Guo, Xiangpeng Kong, Dan Jiang, Jintong Jiang, Yingzhen Bu, Daopeng Wang, Chuan Lu, Xin Zhao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractMyocardial infarction (MI) causes significant disintegration to the coronary microcirculation. Reperfusion following MI is essential to ensure the viability of cardiac tissue. Angiogenesis after MI involves interactions among various cell types, especially stem cells, which can be exploited as a promising therapeutic approach. Extracellular vesicles (EVs) transfer signaling molecules to adjacent cells in a paracrine manner, thereby playing a vital role in intercellular communication. This review summarizes the angiogenic regulating effects of EVs involved in the MI microenvironment and the therapeutic benefits of exogenous stem cell-derived EVs, and further clarifies the underlying mechanisms. We also discuss advanced methodologies to increase the yield and enhance the biological activities of engineered EVs to address major limitations preventing clinical applications to improve cardiac repair. Finally, highly efficient delivery systems are proposed to improve the efficacy of EVs for treatment of MI.Keywords: Extracellular vesicles, Exosomes, Angiogenesis, Myocardial infarction

Introduction

Myocardial infarction (MI) is often triggered by erosion or rupture of atherosclerotic plaque, leading to ischemic and hypoxic injuries [1]. Despite the efficacy of interventional and pharmacological strategies to reduce cell death and improve survival, many MI patients experience heart failure and sudden death due to irreversible loss of cardiomyocytes (CMs) [2, 3]. Accumulating evidence suggests that timely promotion of angiogenesis may prevent cell death and improve cardiac remodeling around the border zone [4]. Consequently, therapeutic angiogenesis has emerged as a significant cardiac repair strategy to avoid heart failure following MI.

Precise regulation of intercellular communication in the MI microenvironment is essential to ensure adequate neovascularization. Extracellular vesicles (EVs), including exosomes, are loaded with various bioactive proteins, mRNAs, and noncoding RNAs that can be transferred to target cells and regulate downstream signaling pathways [5–9]. EVs can significantly influence angiogenesis in various tissues, including the heart (Fig. 1). Furthermore, stem cell-derived EVs, which exhibit effects similar to donor cells without the complexities of cell transplantation, have received attention for treatment of cardiovascular diseases [10]. EV-based therapy can mitigate the effects of MI, but is limited by certain disadvantages, such as achieving an adequate supply of EVs to ensure biological efficacy, while amplified cells in vitro are susceptible to senescence, thereby diminishing therapeutic efficacy [11]. In addition, effective delivery strategies are needed to address the limited retention and invasive injection to ensure the complete regenerative benefits of EV-based therapy [12]. Moreover, engineered EVs can act as biocompatible carriers of functional molecules, such as microRNAs (miRNAs) and proteins [13]. The benefits of EV-based therapy include the administration of protective biomolecules via biocompatible nanoparticles that can be modified to specific particular cell types [14].

In this review article, we discuss the biological functions and the underlying mechanisms of natural EVs for regulation of intercellular communication to promote angiogenesis after MI. We further describe advanced engineering methodologies to improve the protective effects of EVs in order to enhance existing therapeutic strategies and develop innovative treatments for MI patients.

The angiogenic effects of EVs involved in the MI microenvironment

EVs released from CMs, immune cells (macrophages, dendritic cells [DCs], neutrophils), endothelial cells (ECs), and circulating plasma act as paracrine messengers that transfer angiogenic cues through miRNAs, proteins, and signaling molecules. These EVs collectively modulate endothelial proliferation, migration, and vessel formation within the ischemic myocardium, thereby orchestrating the repair process [15]. The following subsections detail the angiogenic mechanisms and specific molecular cargo of EVs derived from each cell source, highlighting both common pathways and distinctive regulatory features in post-MI neovascularization.

Cardiomyocyte-derived extracellular vesicles

CMs are central to myocardial contractility and metabolic homeostasis, yet al.so participate in paracrine communication to guide the behavior of ECs after ischemic injury [16]. Understanding these interactions helps clarify how cardiac muscle cells indirectly govern vascular regeneration.

Occlusion of the coronary artery causes severe hypoxia of the infarct region and subsequent changes to the cargo and biological effects of exosomes [17]. Ribeiro-Rodrigues et al. reported that CM-derived exosomes (CM-exos) stimulate ECs to release matrix metalloproteases under ischemia-mimetic conditions [18]. Subsequent in vitro and in vivo studies demonstrated that under ischemia-mimetic conditions, exosomes promote neovascularization after MI, including proliferation of ECs, and that miR-222 and miR-143, which are abundant in ischemic exosomes, promote the angiogenic capacities of ECs. Notably, mir-143 was found to significantly promote migration and proliferation of mouse cardiac ECs with subsequent formation of capillary-like structures, but had no obvious effect on human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs), whereas miR-222 enhanced angiogenesis of HUVECs, while exerting no substantial regulatory influence on mouse cardiac ECs. These different effects of miR-222 and miR-143 on ECs have been attributed to specific roles in large blood vessels as well as microvessels. Therefore, the functions of biomolecules must be meticulously evaluated across distinct ECs to prevent misinterpretation under specific conditions.

In patients with myocardial ischemia, CM-exos also exhibit pro-angiogenic effects. Li et al. reported that co-culture of exosomes isolated from the coronary serum of patients with myocardial ischemia can enhance the proliferation, migration, and angiogenic capacities of ECs. A mouse model of hind limb ischemia confirmed that these exosomes can markedly enhance blood flow recovery and neovascularization in vivo [19]. Microarray analysis revealed that exosomal expression of miR-939-5p was markedly downregulated following myocardial ischemia. Notably, miR-939-5p reduces endothelial nitric oxide production and inhibits angiogenesis by suppressing the expression and activity of inducible nitric oxide synthase. Meanwhile, Geng et al. found that exosomes in the coronary serum of MI patients facilitate angiogenesis via the miR-143/insulin-like growth factor 1 receptor pathway and that CMs may be the primary source of exosomes, but did not further verify the potential benefits with an MI model [20]. Collectively, these findings clarify that CM-exos act as messengers that coordinate endothelial responses and vascular remodeling within the infarct border zone.

Circulating extracellular vesicles

Circulating EVs can directly reach distant locations through the blood circulation, allowing for instant interactions with target cells and regulation of intercellular pathways. This section introduces the influence of circulating EVs on neovascularization following MI and potential for non-cell-based therapy.

Plasma exosomes have demonstrated protective benefits in a rat model of ischemia-reperfusion through the transmission of signaling molecules [21]. Duan et al. found that peripheral serum exosomes isolated from patients with acute MI within 12 h of symptom onset promoted angiogenesis both in vivo and in vitro through the miR-126-3p/TSC1/mTORC1/HIF-1α signaling pathway [22].

Remote ischemic conditioning is a cell-free therapeutic approach to ameliorate ischemic injury by stimulating peripheral tissues to secrete protective molecules, which act on target organs through exosomes [23]. Chen et al. pointed out that remote ischemic conditioned exosomes markedly promoted angiogenesis and improved cardiac remodeling and cardiac function after MI via targeting Hsp70 [24, 25]. Although this discovery offers novel insights for the treatment of MI, further studies are needed to elucidate the proangiogenic effects of the molecules loaded in the vesicles. Neonatal plasma exosomes (NP-exos) also exhibit therapeutic potential to repair the heart structure and enhance cardiac function after MI. Subsequent in-depth proteome analysis indicated that the proteins carried by NP-exos primarily exert angiogenic effects by modulating the activities of ECs [26].

Exosomes can also indirectly influence angiogenesis by interacting with other cells. Gao et al. demonstrated that serum exosomes extracted from MI mice promoted proliferation of adipose-derived mesenchymal stem cells (ADMSCs) via activation of ERK1/2, while miR-1956 carried by exosomes was found to enhance ADMSC-mediated angiogenesis via down-regulation of Notch-1 expression, and confirmed the ischemic myocardium and kidney may be the primary sources of serum exosomes after MI [27]. Therefore, future studies should focus on the functions of exosomes in intercellular interactions among various tissues and organs, as well as modulation of other cells under pathological conditions. Circulating EVs hold substantial therapeutic potential by mediating protective cross-organ signaling.

Immune cell-derived extracellular vesicles

Immune cells are pivotal regulators of post-infarction healing by orchestrating inflammation, debris clearance, and angiogenesis. Immune cell–derived EVs carry cytokines, miRNAs, and regulatory proteins that modulate endothelial and stromal responses. By examining macrophages, DCs, and neutrophils, this section explores how immune cell-derived EVs fine-tune the balance between inflammation and angiogenesis during cardiac repair.

Angiogenesis after MI involves intricate intercellular communication, with macrophages serving as a crucial driving force [28]. A network of intercellular interactions in infarcted mouse hearts, established using single-cell RNA sequencing and mapping of ligand-receptor pairings, indicated that macrophages exhibited the maximum number of outgoing connections, whereas ECs had the maximum number of inbound connections [29]. However, due to the phenotypic plasticity of macrophages, different subsets play varying roles in myocardial repair [30]. M1 macrophages promote the inflammatory response and clear debris produced by CMs, whereas M2 macrophages facilitate myocardial repair under pathological conditions through the generation of anti-inflammatory cytokines [31, 32]. Recent findings suggest that the timely shift of M1 to M2 macrophages is important to alleviate cardiac dysfunction [33–35]. Similarly, M1 and M2 macrophages exhibit distinct angiogenic capabilities. M2 macrophages generate elevated quantities of angiogenic growth factors and cytokines, and facilitate tube formation, while M1 macrophages exhibited an inhibitory impact in vitro [36]. Liu et al. reported that M1 macrophage-derived exosomes secrete an abundance of pro-inflammatory factors with antiangiogenic effects on human coronary artery ECs and exacerbate cardiac dysfunction after MI [37], including MiR-155, which targets Rac family small GTPase 1, p21-activated kinase 2, Sirtuin 1, and protein kinase AMP-activated catalytic subunit alpha 2 in ECs. M2 macrophage-derived exosomes contain an abundance of miR-132, which enhances angiogenesis and promotes cardiac repair after MI, and modulates THBS1 expression by directly interacting with the 3’ untranslated region of miR-132-3p, thereby down-regulating the angiogenic capacity of ECs [10].

DCs are recruited to the infarcted zone and ablation of DCs suppressed the angiogenic response and restrained myocardial repair following MI [38, 39], indicating that DCs may exert a pro-angiogenic role in post-MI angiogenesis. Moreover, Liu et al. found that exosomes isolated from DCs increased expression of VEGF in ECs and promoted angiogenesis in a mouse model of MI, while the cargo miR-494-3p facilitated tube formation both in vivo and in vitro [40].

Neutrophils, the first cellular responders of the innate immune system, rapidly accumulate in the infarcted region after MI and initiate the inflammatory response and removal of cell debris [41]. However, neutrophils exhibit anti-inflammatory and proangiogenic properties, thereby aiding in the resolution of inflammation and myocardial repair [42], while facilitating angiogenesis directly through the secretion of cytokines or indirectly by inducing the polarization of macrophages toward proangiogenic subsets [43, 44]. Furthermore, neutrophil depletion impedes angiogenesis. However, the functions of neutrophil-derived exosomes in MI have yet to be elucidated.

Immune-cell-derived EVs exhibit dual roles—either stimulating or suppressing angiogenesis—depending on the cellular phenotype and timing. Defining the temporal dynamics and molecular determinants of these effects remains essential for targeted immunomodulatory therapy after MI.

Endothelial cell-derived extracellular vesicles

ECs, which serve as the connection between the blood and tissues, are essential for anti-inflammatory, anti-thrombotic, and vascular barrier functions. ECs are also crucial for intercellular communication during vascular damage and are regarded as possible mediators under physiological and pathological conditions [45]. EC-derived exosomes (EC-exos) have been implicated in ischemia [46–48]. Shen et al. found that HUVEC-derived-exosomes enhanced the proangiogenic effects of ADMSCs in a limb ischemia model, and miR-486-5p targeted phosphatase and tensin homolog deleted on chromosome 10 (PTEN) and subsequently regulated the AKT/MTOR/HIF-1α pathway [6, 47]. However, most previous studies have concentrated on the biological activities of ECs as recipient cells in post-MI angiogenesis. Thus, further studies are needed to clarify the biological functions of EC-exos in the MI microenvironment.

Other sources

Beyond classical cardiac and immune origins, several unconventional EV sources have been implicated in angiogenesis.

Telocytes are newly identified interstitial cells in several organs and tissues, including the heart [48–50]. Cardiac telocytes may serve as niche-supporting cells facilitating intercellular interactions of cardiac cells to modulate angiogenesis and inflammation [51]. Transplanted cardiac telocytes effectively reduced infarct size and enhanced cardiac function at 14 weeks post-MI. This protective effect was partially ascribed to enhanced angiogenesis and reconstitution of cardiac telocytes [52]. Yang et al. reported that the angiogenic capacity of ECs was improved after culturing in conditioned medium, but diminished in the absence of exosomes. Injection of telocyte-derived exosomes (CT-exos) contributed to improved cardiac function and enhanced angiogenesis in a mouse model of MI [53]. Liao et al. found that CT-exos suppress apoptosis, enhance the viability of ECs, promote angiogenesis, and provide cardioprotective benefits following MI, while the abundance of miR-21-5p in CT-exos was responsible for these therapeutic effects via target cell death inducing p53 target 1 [54].

Salvia miltiorrhiza, a traditional Chinese medicine, is used for the prevention and treatment of cardiovascular diseases [55]. Based on the beneficial effects on angiogenesis, Zhang et al. reported that exosome-like nanoparticles extracted from S. miltiorrhiza improved the viability, migration, and angiogenic capabilities of ECs after myocardial ischemia–reperfusion injury [56].

Overall, these alternative EV sources reveal novel therapeutic possibilities and emphasize the evolutionary diversity of vesicle communication. Nevertheless, limited mechanistic data and translational evidence necessitate deeper experimental validation.

Stem cells

Stem cell transplantation, which promotes angiogenesis and facilitates cardiac repair following myocardial injury, is regarded as a highly attractive method for treatment of MI. However, emerging research indicates that multilineage differentiation was not the principal mechanism underlying the capacity of stem cells to improve angiogenesis in the infarcted zone [57]. Instead, the protective effects of stem cells mainly involved paracrine factors carried by exosomes as a promising cell-free option [58]. In this section, we focus on the practical roles and underlying mechanisms of stem cells in post-MI angiogenesis.

Mesenchymal stem/stromal cells (MSCs)

MSCs are multipotent stromal cells characterized by self-renewal and differentiation capabilities, as well as immunomodulatory features, which are extensively utilized for treatment of cardiovascular diseases [59]. MSCs have been isolated from the bone marrow (BM), umbilical cord, and adipose tissue. The proangiogenic effects of MSCs are mainly derived in a paracrine manner, and MSC-derived exosomes (MSC-exos) provide similar therapeutic advantages. Due to availability and low immunogenicity, MSC-exos hold promise for treatment of MI.

Preclinical studies have found that MSC-EVs promote the angiogenic capacity of ECs and alleviate cardiac injury after MI [60–77] (Table 1). Huang et al. demonstrated that combination therapy with MSC-exos and MSCs reduced scar size, improved cardiac repair, and enhanced angiogenesis post MI as compared to MSC-exos or MSCs alone, and that pre-treatment with MSC-exos improved survival of MSCs both in vivo and in vitro. Furthermore, delivery of MSC-exos within 30 min after MI and transplantation of MSCs on day 3 following MI achieved the most significant enhancement of cardiac function [62]. As compared to exosomes from aged BM-derived MSCs, those from young BM-derived MSCs more effectively promoted tube formation, prevented apoptosis of CMs in vitro, and enhanced cardiac function in vivo. Notably, miR-221-3p was enriched in the exosomes of young BM-derived MSCs and responsible for the protective effects through promoting Akt kinase activity by suppressing phosphatase and tensin homolog [67]. Meanwhile, Ratushnyy et al. reported that replicative senescence of ADMSCs inhibits angiogenesis and substantially alters the profiles of factors associated with angiogenesis [78]. Therefore, the exosomes obtained from donor cells should be carefully screened to avoid compromising the therapeutic effects of exosome-mediated repair.

Induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs)

Accumulating evidence suggests that iPSCs are a promising resource for regenerative therapy. Enhanced cardiac function has been achieved in animal models of MI following administration of iPSC-derived EVs (iPSC-EVs) [79, 80]. Besides cellular replacement, paracrine factors released by iPSCs play a significant role in cell-based cardiac repair. iPSC-EVs contain numerous miRNAs, proteins, and iPSC-specific bioactive molecules. As compared to iPSCs, iPSC-EVs improved cardiac function and perfusion in a mouse model of MI. Intramyocardial injection of iPSCs led to teratoma formation, whereas injection of iPSC-derived EVs was safe [81]. Therefore, owing to easy acquisition, low immunogenicity, and ethical issues, iPSC-EVs have emerged as effective and safe materials for cardiac repair following MI.

The application of iPSC-derived CMs (iPSC-CMs) is a promising approach to repair the injured heart, alleviate the infarcted zone, and improve cardiac function [82]. Several experimental studies have demonstrated the therapeutic efficiency of iPSC-derived EVs (iPSC-CM-EVs) for treatment of MI, which were shown to increase expression of the proangiogenic factors of ECs and promote angiogenesis in vitro [83]. Gao et al. discovered that iPSC-CM-exos facilitated EC tube formation and sprouting from aortic rings, and protected iPSC-CMs by inhibiting apoptosis, preserving intracellular calcium homeostasis, and elevating adenosine 5′-triphosphate levels in vivo. In a swine model of MI, iPSC-CM-exos promoted angiogenesis and myocardial recovery without increasing the incidence of arrhythmia [84]. Tominaga et al. revealed the pleiotropic effects of iPSC-CM-EVs on ischemic cardiomyopathy. The miRNAs and proteins loaded in the EVs facilitated angiogenesis, regulated macrophage polarization, and attenuated apoptosis. Intramyocardial injections of iPSC-CM-EVs enhanced cardiac function and reduced mortality after MI by promoting neovascularization and inhibiting fibrosis and inflammation in a mouse model [85].

Embryonic stem cells (ESCs)

ESCs are pluripotent stem cells with self-renewal capabilities, demonstrating significant potential for the treatment of various diseases [57]. EVs generated by ESC-derived cardiac progenitor cells (CPCs) (ESC-CPC-EVs) have been shown to considerably enhance cardiac function, reduce fibrosis after MI, and improve neovascularization and CM survival in the border zone. ESC-CPC-EVs consistently promoted tube formation of ECs, improved cell viability, and reduced lactate dehydrogenase release by CMs subjected to oxygen-glucose deprivation [86]. A comprehensive comparison of EVs derived from ESC (ECS-EVs), BM-derived MSCs, human immortalized MSCs, ESC-derived CPCs, ESC-derived CMs, and human primary ventricular cardiac fibroblasts demonstrated that ESC-EVs exerted the greatest cardioprotective effect and improved adverse left ventricular remodeling after MI by reducing fibrosis and enhancing angiogenesis, while facilitating heart repair indirectly by shifting M1 macrophages to M2 macrophages via biomolecules associated with various pathways involved in angiogenesis, particularly fibroblast growth factor-2 [87].

CPCs and endothelial progenitor cells (EPCs)

CPCs are a specific type of stem cell located in the adult heart that can differentiate into multiple cell types and become activated after myocardial injury [88]. The protective effects of CPCs are exerted by directly differentiating into various cardiac cells and activating local stem cells, but mainly through paracrine release of biological factors, such as EVs [89]. CPCs of patients undergoing cardiac surgery co-cultured with CMs and ECs in conditioned medium can prevent apoptosis of CMs and promote the angiogenic capabilities of ECs. However, these protective functions were attenuated in the absence of EVs. The abundances of miR-210 and miR-132 are elevated in CPC-derived EVs, as compared to fibroblasts. Notably, miR-210 can mitigate apoptosis of CMs, while miR-132 is reported to enhance the angiogenic capabilities of ECs [90].

EPCs, acknowledged as precursors of ECs, exhibit potential for treatment of cardiovascular diseases owing to robust proliferation capacity in response to tissue ischemia or hypoxia [91]. Circulating EPCs, comprising early and late EPCs, can facilitate regeneration of the vascular intima [92]. Early release of angiogenic cytokines, chemokines, and growth factors from EPCs can promote recruitment and regulate proliferation of mature ECs during vascular damage [93, 94]. Late EPCs demonstrate an elevated proliferation rate and robust capacity to facilitate regeneration [95]. EPCs have attracted significant interest because of the possible therapeutic benefits of derived exosomes (EPC-exos) for treatment of cardiovascular diseases. EPC-exos have anti-inflammatory, angiogenic, and regeneration properties, which play critical roles in cardiac repair following MI [96, 97]. Chen et al. revealed that EPC-EVs enhance angiogenesis and promote cardiac repair after MI [96]. The proteomic similarities between EVs and EPCs have firmly established that exosomes can yield therapeutic advantages comparable to EPCs. Furthermore, a study conducted by Huang et al. reported that EPC-exos containing miR-1246 and miR-1290 modulate the phenotypic transformation of fibroblasts into ECs, thereby providing proangiogenic effects following MI through the regulation of ELF5 and SP1 [98].

Although the existence of resident cardiac progenitor or stem cells in the adult mammalian heart remains controversial, accumulating evidence suggests that small populations of tissue-resident multipotent cells contribute to endogenous repair after myocardial injury. EVs derived from stem cells or endothelial sources can modulate the activation, survival, and paracrine signaling of these local progenitor-like cells, thereby indirectly enhancing neovascularization and myocardial remodeling. Nonetheless, further lineage-tracing and functional validation studies are required to definitively confirm the regenerative potential of these cells and their precise interactions with therapeutic EVs.

Cumulatively, stem-cell-derived EVs reproduce many benefits of parent cells without the ethical and immunologic complications of transplantation. Yet, large-scale manufacturing, potency consistency, and potential off-target effects remain challenges before clinical adoption.

Improved protective effects of EVs after MI

To overcome intrinsic limitations, EVs have been engineered through culture optimization, hypoxic preconditioning, genetic modification, and biomaterial-based delivery [9]. These innovations aim to enhance yield, angiogenic potency, and tissue targeting. This section reviews current engineering strategies to amplify EV-mediated cardiac protection and discusses their translational potential (Fig. 2).

Improved protective effects of EVs for treatment of MI. Overview of major strategies used to improve the therapeutic efficacy of EVs for cardiac repair after MI. These approaches include: (1) Enhanced yield through 3D cultures, extrusion, or stimulation; (2) Hypoxic preconditioning, which enriches EVs with cardioprotective molecules; (3) Genetic modification or transfection, introducing miRNAs or proteins, such as GDF15, miR-214-3p, HIF-1α, and miR-223, to enhance reparative signaling; (4) Pretreatment of donor cells with bioactive substances, cytokines, or drugs to modify EV cargo; and (5) Advanced delivery systems, such as hydrogels, membrane fusion, and cardiac-homing peptide modification, to improve EV stability and myocardial targeting

Enhanced yield of EVs

The effective generation of substantial amounts of active EVs is a significant limitation to clinical applications. For this reason, Sun et al. cultured umbilical cord-derived MSCs (ucMSCs) with a hollow fiber bioreactor-based three-dimensional (3D) system to mimic a natural in vivo environment. This culture system supports intercellular communication to elicit biochemical and physiological responses. The 3D culture system enhanced the biological functions and concentration of ucMSC-derived EVs (ucMSC-EVs). As compared to conventional culture systems, 3D cultures were shown to improve the cardioprotective effects by promoting angiogenesis and inhibiting inflammation after MI [11]. Beneficial effects of the 3D culture system for other diseases have also been demonstrated [99, 100]. These results indicate that the 3D culture system can enhance the yield and therapeutic application of EVs. Yu et al. investigated the angiogenic effects of EVs isolated from pretreated EPCs using silicate ion solutions obtained from calcium silicate and found that the increased yield of these pretreated EPCs enriched the biological activities of miR-126a-3p, VEGF, SDF-1, CXCR4, and eNOS. Moreover, hydrogel microspheres were used to effectively improve cardiac function by promoting angiogenesis following MI [101]. Extrusion has recently emerged as an efficient method to enhance the generation of nanovesicles (NVs) by more than 11 fold [102]. Comparisons of extruded NVs and natural secretion of EVs from MSCs found that the molecules encapsulated in NVs more closely resembled those of MSCs than those in EVs, and the extruded NVs demonstrated a significant protective effect both in vitro and in vivo. The ability to increase production and therapeutic effectiveness render NVs an optimal candidate for cell-free therapy [103].

Hypoxia

Hypoxia serves as a significant angiogenic stimulant during development, maintenance of adult tissue homeostasis, and responses to pathological conditions [104]. Maintaining mice in a hypoxic environment post-MI enhanced proliferation of ECs and facilitated neovascularization in the infarcted zone [105]. Hypoxia is reported to enhance the amount, cargo, and biological effects of EVs [17, 106]. Hence, hypoxia has emerged as an efficient strategy to increase the angiogenic capabilities of EVs for treatment of MI. As mentioned above, miR-486-5p was upregulated in hypoxic EC-exos and enhanced the proangiogenic effects of ADMSCs in a limb ischemia model [47]. Interestingly, hypoxia was also found to increase the level of miR-486-5p in MSC-EVs and promote angiogenesis following MI in both mice and nonhuman primates by targeting matrix metalloproteinase 19 [63]. Furthermore, miR-486-5p carried by the exosomes of BM-derived MSCs promoted proliferation and inhibited apoptosis of H9C2 cells in a hypoxia/reoxygenation model. Moreover, miR-486-5p provides a protective effect against myocardial ischemic injury by negatively regulating expression of PTEN and activation of the PI3K/AKT pathway [107]. These findings highlight the potential of miR-486-5p-engineered EVs to improve angiogenesis and cardiac function after MI. Sánchez-Sánchez et al. reported that miR-4732-3p is modulated by hypoxia and has cardioprotective effects against ischemic injury. Overexpression of miR-4732-3p in MSC-EVs was found to promote angiogenesis both in vitro and in vivo, and improve fibroblast differentiation [66]. Shao et al. reported that hypoxia enhanced the proangiogenic effects of ucMSC-EVs. The protective properties of hypoxic ucMSC-EVs are partly mediated by miR-214, which also downregulated Sufu expression and activated hedgehog signaling in ECs following MI [108]. Additionally, hypoxic ESC-CPC-EVs enhanced angiogenesis, as compared to ESC-CPC-EVs, in the heart post MI. Mechanistically, enrichment of the long non-coding RNA MALAT1 was responsible for the functions of hypoxic ESC-CPC-EVs through the target miR-497 [86]. Moreover, several investigations have demonstrated that the functional molecules carried by hypoxic EVs convey protective benefits against ischemic injury in various organs, including the kidneys and brain [106, 109]. These findings suggest that hypoxia plays a vital role in the proangiogenic effects of EVs.

Transfection and pretreatment

EVs, as vesicles for delivery of biological contents, can enhance proangiogenic properties. The overexpression of proangiogenic molecules has been confirmed as an efficient therapeutic strategy for treatment of MI [110–124] (Table 2). For instance, EVs obtained from H9C2 cells transfected with a lentivirus coding for growth differentiation factor-15 (GDF-15) significantly reduced the infarct zone and improved cardiac function in a mouse model of MI, which was mainly due to enhanced angiogenesis, reduced infiltration of inflammatory cells, and suppression of apoptosis [124]. Similarly, overexpression of GDF-15 in macrophages also promoted cardiac repair by reprogramming macrophages after MI through regulated fatty acid-binding protein 4 [118]. HIF-1α mediates cellular adaptation to hypoxia by activating several angiogenic factors, subsequently restoring the injured angiogenic capacity of EVs and facilitating cardiovascular protection through enhanced neovascularization [125]. Sun et al. found that exosomes overexpressing HIF-1α derived from MSCs exerted superior cardioprotective effects by improving impaired migration, proliferation, and angiogenesis of hypoxia-preconditioned ECs [116]. HIF-1α engineered EVs have also been utilized in other research under similar circumstances [117]. Macrophage migration inhibitory factor (MIF) is an inflammatory cytokine extensively expressed in various cell types that is crucial for cell survival and proliferation, and exosomes from MSCs with MIF overexpression enhanced cardiac protection in MI [126, 127]. Zhu et al. reported that exosomes generated from MIF-engineered ucMSCs confer protective benefits by facilitating angiogenesis, while upregulation of miR-133a-3p exhibited biological functions through the AKT signaling pathway [122]. Transfection of the proangiogenic molecules miR-126, miR-322, and miR-132 also exerted cardioprotective effects [112, 114, 121]. Furthermore, inhibition of antiangiogenic genes loaded in EVs was also found to convey cardioprotective effects. Gou et al. demonstrated that suppression of miR-19a-3p loaded in CM-exos enhanced angiogenesis and improved cardiac function in a mouse model of MI by targeting HIF-1α [128]. Pretreatment of cells with drugs, cytokines, and other bioactive substances may enhance the capability of EVs to improve angiogenesis following MI [25, 129–134]. For example, nicotinamide mononucleotide (NMN) is crucial for the regulation of various physiological and metabolic processes. Previous research has indicated that administration of NMN may effectively protect the heart against ageing, doxorubicin treatment, and ischemic injury. NMN-preconditioned MSC-EVs enhanced proliferation, migration, and angiogenesis of ECs, while significantly decreasing ventricular remodeling, and promoted heart function following MI [131]. Pretreatment with atorvastatin enhanced the proangiogenic effects of MSC-EVs and exerted cardioprotective effects against MI. The long non-coding RNA H19 restored the functions of atorvastatin-pretreated MSC-EVs by negatively regulating expression of miR-675 and activating VEGF and intercellular adhesion molecule-1 [25].

New delivery systems

While transplantation of EVs serves as an effective therapeutic strategy to promote angiogenesis post MI, the delivery of exosomes to the heart is complicated by low stability, invasive procedures, and off-target effects, which hinder achieving protective functions [135]. Several effective strategies have been developed to optimize the bioavailability of EVs, including the use of hydrogels and targeting technologies.

Hydrogels are composed of highly hydrophilic, 3D networks characterized by exceptional biocompatibility and biodegradability that can rapidly transition from a solution by altering temperature, pH, or mechanical pressure, thereby facilitating the release of encapsulated targeted therapeutics [136]. EV-containing hydrogels have yielded promising results for treatment of MI [96, 117, 137–147] (Table 3). Yan et al. invented a bio-conductive polymer, poly-pyrrole-chitosan (PPY-CHI), which is a semiconductive and biocompatible hydrogel capable of electrically connecting contracting cells over a distance. As the PPY-CHI hydrogel degrades, incorporated MSC-exos were gradually released to the infarcted hearts of mice and mitigated cardiac dysfunction by stimulating the EGF/PI3K/AKT pathway, thereby promoting angiogenesis and inhibiting apoptosis. Moreover, PPY-CHI/hEMSC-Exo preserved conductive qualities, which alleviated arrhythmia by resynchronizing cardiac electrical transmission [145]. Similarly, Wang et al. developed an innovative exo-loaded conductive hydrogel to augment cardiac healing through simultaneous enhancement of biological and electrical signals. Conductive hydrogels based on black phosphorus loaded with ADMSC-exos demonstrated superior biocompatibility, biodegradability, and sustained exosome release, guaranteeing extended therapeutic efficacy. The exo-encapsulated conductive hydrogel markedly improved recovery of heart function by promoting angiogenesis, suppressing inflammation, and augmenting electrical activity of myocardial cells. Concurrently, black phosphate enhanced the electrical conductivity of the hydrogel and intensified the electrical signal, thereby producing a reparative effect after MI [146]. To improve the retention rate of hydrogels, Han et al. incorporated cardiac protective peptides and a matrix metalloprotease-2 degradable sequence into a peptide amphiphile, which was combined with naphthalene–diphenylalanine dipeptide to produce a peptidoglycan hydrogel with self-assembly capabilities that can release MSC-exos at a stable and sustained rate to improve cardiac repair by reducing inflammation and apoptosis, and promoting angiogenesis [147].

To enhance organ-targeting capability, EVs can be engineered by incorporating surface ligands specific to the target tissue. Utilizing a cardiac homing peptide to target the infarcted region can increase the efficacy and decrease the effective dose of exosomes [148, 149]. Wang et al. reported that a peptide targeting the ischemic myocardium improved the efficacy and specificity of MSC-exos for treatment of MI [150]. Platelets can also be attached to the damaged vascular wall following MI. Platelet membranes can be utilized to functionalize nanoparticles, as several specialized proteins on the platelet membrane identify the injury location and facilitate targeted medication delivery [151]. Li et al. engineered MSC-EVs with fused platelet membranes, which had accumulated in the damaged endothelium of the ischemic heart area and promoted the angiogenic potential of EVs [152]. Furthermore, Zhang et al. engineered MSC-EVs with monocyte mimics using monocyte membranes to promote targeting of EVs to infarcted hearts [153].

Translational challenges and clinical prospects of EV-based therapies

Although EV-based therapeutics have demonstrated remarkable cardioprotective and pro-angiogenic effects in preclinical studies, translating these findings into clinical benefit remains an unmet challenge [154]. Multiple biological and technical barriers limit the efficiency and reproducibility of EV therapy for MI. Within the ischemic myocardium, the acidic and oxidative microenvironment can destabilize EV membranes and degrade their RNA or protein cargo, resulting in reduced bioactivity [155]. Moreover, the dense fibrotic matrix and limited perfusion impede EV penetration and homogeneous distribution across the infarct zone, creating difficulties in achieving effective local concentrations. Consequently, optimization of the dose, route, and timing of administration is crucial. Intramyocardial injection achieves high local retention but is invasive, whereas intravenous delivery is safer yet limited by rapid systemic clearance and off-target accumulation in the liver or spleen [155].

To overcome these limitations, biomaterial-assisted delivery platforms, including hydrogels, conductive scaffolds, and peptide-based nanomatrices, have been developed to provide sustained release, mechanical support, and improved myocardial retention. Comparative investigations have revealed that encapsulated or surface-engineered EVs maintain cargo integrity under low-pH conditions and extend therapeutic half-life as compared to naked EVs. However, large-scale reproducibility and long-term biocompatibility of these constructs still require validation in clinically relevant models.

In parallel, potential adverse and off-target effects must be rigorously assessed. EVs may carry unwanted pro-thrombotic, pro-fibrotic, or inflammatory molecules depending on the donor cell state and culture conditions. Immunogenicity is another concern, particularly for EVs derived from non-autologous sources, such as embryonic, tumor, or xenogeneic cells. Therefore, the establishment of standardized protocols for isolation, purification, and potency assays is critical to minimize heterogeneity and ensure product safety. Emerging purification technologies, such as tangential flow filtration, size-exclusion chromatography, and microfluidic isolation, hold promise for clinical-grade EV preparation.

Furthermore, engineering strategies have provided tangible functional improvements by enhancing EV targeting, stability, and angiogenic potency. Genetic modification to overexpress pro-angiogenic miRNAs or transcription factors augments reparative signaling and endothelial activation. Pretreatment with small molecules or hypoxia further improves cargo composition and promotes cardioprotection through the PI3K/AKT and HIF-1α pathways. These approaches provide concrete mechanistic evidence that engineered EVs outperform natural vesicles in preclinical models.

In terms of clinical translation, several early-phase trials have been initiated to evaluate the safety and feasibility of EV-based therapeutics. While clinical evidence directly targeting MI remains limited, several early-phase trials are evaluating the feasibility and safety of EV-based interventions in related ischemic and inflammatory diseases (e.g., NCT04327635, NCT05437671, NCT04664738). Preliminary results indicate favorable safety profiles and immunologic tolerance in non-cardiac settings, providing a foundation for future cardiovascular applications. Importantly, regulatory frameworks for EV-based products are still evolving: the U.S. Food and Drug Administration and the European Medicines Agency currently classify therapeutic EVs under the category of biological products, requiring compliance with Good Manufacturing Practice and detailed characterization of identity, potency, and purity. Continued alignment between scientific development and regulatory oversight will be essential to transition EVs from proof-of-concept to approved therapeutics in cardiovascular medicine.

Finally, EV-based strategies should be compared systematically with existing regenerative approaches, including stem cell transplantation, gene therapy, and cytokine delivery. While EVs offer advantages, such as lower immunogenicity and ease of storage, their therapeutic window and cost-effectiveness require further clinical evaluation. Looking forward, interdisciplinary collaboration integrating bioengineering, clinical pharmacology, and regulatory science will be essential to accelerate the transition from experimental proof-of-concept to standardized, patient-ready EV therapeutics for cardiac repair.

Conclusions and perspectives

Delivery of therapeutics to promote angiogenesis can enhance recovery from MI by improving cardiac function, reducing infarcted regions, and preventing remodeling, suggesting that the endogenous angiogenic response is inherently constrained and can be augmented to achieve a therapeutic impact [3, 156]. EVs play an essential role in angiogenesis following MI by precisely regulating the interactions among various cell types involved in heart repair. Here, we summarized the intercellular communication mediated by EVs in the MI microenvironment, including CMs, immune cells, ECs, and circulating EVs. We further reviewed stem cell-derived EVs as cell-free therapeutics to promote angiogenesis post MI. EVs have similar biological functions to their donor cells and provide cardioprotective effects without the complexity associated with cell transplantation. However, most research has focused the biological activities of miRNAs encapsulated in EVs, rather than long noncoding RNAs and proteins, which may also act as crucial effectors driving the protective effects of EVs. Furthermore, stem cell-derived EVs may exert antiangiogenic effects under specific conditions [78]. Nonetheless, EVs must be rigorously assessed to prevent compromised protective efficacy in cell-free therapy.

Several bioengineering techniques have been employed to enhance the protective properties of natural EVs. Donor cells pretreated with hypoxia or drugs, cytokines, and other bioactive substances may augment the effectiveness of derived EVs to promote angiogenesis after MI. Moreover, engineered EVs loaded with proangiogenic molecules or pharmaceuticals are potential therapeutics for vascular regeneration. However, efficient production of sufficient quantities is a major constraint for preclinical and clinical applications of active EVs. Hence, several groups have attempted to enhance the yield of EVs to achieve a significant cardioprotective effect. In addition, the integration of delivery systems can enhance the stability, regulate release, and facilitate targeting of EVs. Recently, various delivery techniques have been developed to optimize the efficacy of EVs, with notable success at the preclinical stage, including hydrogels and targeting technologies. Collectively, EVs are essential for intercellular communication and provide new insights for the treatment for MI. Furthermore, advanced engineering methodologies offer an effective approach for therapeutic interventions. Hence, EVs are emerging as potential therapeutics post MI.

However, despite significant advances in understanding the reparative mechanisms of EVs in MI, translating these preclinical successes into effective clinical therapies remains challenging. The main obstacles include the heterogeneity of EV preparations, low yield and scalability of production, and limited targeting efficiency in the ischemic myocardium. In addition, batch variability, cargo instability under acidic and oxidative conditions, and uncertain in vivo biodistribution hinder reproducibility and dose standardization. Also, consistent large-scale efficacy and safety across diverse patient populations have yet to be demonstrated.

From a clinical standpoint, large-scale Good Manufacturing Practice–compliant production, establishment of potency and safety assays, and clear regulatory classification are urgently needed to bridge experimental progress with therapeutic application. Therefore, multidisciplinary collaboration integrating bioengineering, pharmacology, and regulatory science will be crucial to optimize EV delivery, ensure biosafety, and accelerate clinical translation. Continued efforts toward standardization, controlled release systems, and patient-specific EV formulations may ultimately enable EV-based therapies to move from proof-of-concept to clinical reality.
