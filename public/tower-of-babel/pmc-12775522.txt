Maple-seed-inspired asymmetric microdisk resonators
Authors: Inah Yeo, Je Hwang Lee
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Microdisk resonators are a cornerstone of integrated photonics, enabling advances in on-chip optical information processing, yet the field is primarily based on conventional symmetric architectures. The potential of biomimetic asymmetric designs remains largely unexplored. Here, we present asymmetric geometries for microdisk resonators inspired by maple seed profiles. These structures provide additional operational capabilities beyond those of conventional symmetric designs. Our approach demonstrates theoretically how such asymmetric disk geometries enable coupling strength control in nanoscale monolithic pillar structures. The resulting resonators offer versatile platforms for scalable mechanical networks through strain-mediated coupling. Their inherent directional anisotropy facilitates selective coupling across diverse geometric scales. This approach demonstrates the potential of nature-inspired asymmetric designs in advancing mechanical network technologies for integrated photonic systems.

Introduction

Maple seeds are a remarkable example of natural engineering, exhibiting a distinctive asymmetric disk-like architecture that has evolved for efficient dispersal1,2. Their structure features an integrated wing–seed system, where the asymmetry arises from the precise arrangement of the wing relative to the central pod3,4. These natural systems transcend their biological function by providing fundamental insights into solid and elastic mechanical dynamics, with their wing mechanics serving as foundational principles for gliders and rotorcraft while catalyzing advances in bio-inspired structural engineering1–5.

In parallel to this natural architecture, disk-shaped resonators are fundamental building blocks in integrated photonics, enabling accurate optical manipulation at the microscale through effective light confinement in miniaturized structures while maintaining high-Q resonances. These structures utilize total internal reflection to establish whispering-gallery modes at the micro/nanoscale6,7. Recent advancements in materials science have significantly enhanced the performance of disk-shaped resonators8,9, with gallium arsenide (GaAs) platforms demonstrating exceptional versatility for nonlinear optics and photonic integration10–12. These resonant systems are essential tools for integrated photonics across diverse technological domains, bridging applications from classical signal processing to quantum single-photon generation and control13.

Traditional microdisk resonator designs rely on circular or elliptical configurations, offering well-characterized modal behaviors and deterministic coupling dynamics. Previous investigations of conventional and truncated pillar resonators have established systematic frameworks for characterizing resonator interactions in these conventional architectures14–17. In this work, we introduce a microdisk resonator distinguished by a characteristic indentation that breaks its rotational symmetry–a design inspired by the distinctive asymmetric morphology of maple seeds. Drawing from the natural asymmetric design principles, we hypothesize that this biologically inspired approach can introduce multifunctional capabilities by engineering mechanical coupling properties through strategically designed indentations that also consider optical resonance compatibility. We theoretically investigate the vibrational characteristics of individual biomimetic disk resonators and demonstrate directional coupling control in paired resonator configurations through systematic geometric tailoring. Such asymmetric configurations are anticipated to provide versatile coupling control mechanisms and an extended evanescent field interaction. They can enhance mechanical coupling capabilities while potentially opening pathways toward integrated optical resonances and emission pattern engineering beyond those enabled by conventional symmetric designs. This approach holds promise for diverse applications, ranging from precision mechanical sensing to advanced optomechanical resonator network architectures.

The incorporation of biomimetic indentations creates localized perturbations in the strain field that selectively couple with specific vibrational modes, thereby enhancing modal selectivity and enabling systematic frequency control. Discussion

We investigated biomimetic microdisk resonators inspired by maple seed morphology, establishing their potential as promising platforms for advanced mechanical coupling networks. In contrast to earlier strain-mediated coupling studies in vertical-cavity surface-emitting laser pillar structures16, we introduced an alternative approach focusing on different applications through biomimetic maple-seed-inspired microdisk geometries that leverage elliptical geometry and offer distinctive mechanical coupling properties via minimal geometric modifications of widely-used disk resonator architectures. These asymmetric structures are a distinctive alternative to conventional symmetric resonator designs: they offer enhanced geometric flexibility and directional coupling control, addressing fundamental limitations in scalable mechanical systems.

The key advantage lies in enabling ultra-compact spacing down to a single radius through simple asymmetric indentations, in contrast to traditional disk geometries that require diameter-scale spacing, while achieving directional coupling anisotropy with order-of-magnitude selectivity. This biomimetic approach enables dense packing while maintaining the mechanical coupling strength within tens of kilohertz, comparable to that of conventional GaAs coupled pillar resonators14,15. The structural anisotropy manifests as highly directional coupling behavior, with the strength of longitudinal coupling (parallel to the coupler) exceeding that of transverse coupling by an order of magnitude. This directional preference arises from indentation-induced compliance changes, which enhance longitudinal strain-mediated coupling through the direct modulation of critical interatomic distances; by contrast, transverse strain predominantly induces inefficient shear deformation. Consequently, the indentation depth becomes a critical design parameter, improving the coupling strength by nearly tenfold for coupler-aligned modes while negligibly affecting perpendicular modes.

The proposed maple-seed-like microdisk geometry builds upon established GaAs nanofabrication techniques and leverages the widespread adoption of disk-based architectures in commercial photonics platforms32–34. GaAs nanodisk resonators have been successfully fabricated with high precision using electron-beam lithography (EBL) and reactive ion etching (RIE), demonstrating sub-10 nm resolution9,10,35. The fabrication of elliptical nanopillars with precise dimensional control (e.g., 75\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\times$$\end{document}120 nm\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$^2$$\end{document} elliptical cross-sections) in GaAs-based structures has been achieved, confirming the capability to produce asymmetric geometries required for our design36. The nanoscale indentation depths and asymmetric features are well within the demonstrated dimensional control capabilities of current GaAs processing techniques.

In realistic fabrication, a GaAs/Al\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_{x}$$\end{document}Ga\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_{1-x}$$\end{document}As/GaAs heterostructure with sacrificial layer etching can be employed, where the chiral disk pattern and support pillar are formed through independent processes: EBL patterning of the disk layer and selective wet etching of the sacrificial layer, respectively. This decoupled approach ensures that the critical indentation parameters (e.g., \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta b_\textrm{p}$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$a_\textrm{p}$$\end{document}) are controlled exclusively by the EBL mask and anisotropic RIE with vertical pattern transfer at sub-10 nm precision, independent of the isotropic support pillar formation process. For the simulations in this work, we use a simplified uniform GaAs structure. We verified that incorporating an Al\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_{0.3}$$\end{document}Ga\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_{0.7}$$\end{document}As support affects only the pillar flexural modes (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sim$$\end{document}0.3% frequency shifts) while preserving all disk-based chiral trends. Critically, the strain-mediated coupling mechanism through bottom couplers, which forms the basis of our theoretical analysis, has been experimentally validated in nanomechanical pillar resonator arrays with good agreement between finite element simulations and experimental measurements14,15. These existing fabrication and coupling demonstrations provide a realistic pathway for experimental implementation of these biomimetic structures with direct compatibility for integration into existing disk-based photonic platforms.

The biomimetic approach addresses specific challenges in mechanical resonator networks. While conventional designs face scalability limitations due to diameter-scale spacing requirements and limited directional control, our structures overcome these constraints through compact geometries and inherent anisotropy. This approach aligns with the growing demand for compact, directionally-selective coupling in commercial photonic platforms. The resulting capabilities enable applications ranging from multiparameter sensing arrays to mechanical computing and reconfigurable photonic systems.

Conclusion

This work establishes maple-seed-inspired microdisk resonators as a viable platform for alternative mechanical coupling networks. The demonstrated combination of ultra-compact spacing equal to a single radius, directional coupling anisotropy with order-of-magnitude selectivity, and tunable coupling strengths from tens of kHz down to sub-Hz creates new design possibilities that were previously inaccessible with symmetric resonator geometries. The compatibility with established GaAs nanofabrication techniques offers a direct route for experimental realization. These findings enable developing sophisticated mechanical networks that exploit directional coupling control and compact geometries. Future efforts should prioritize experimental validation through fabrication of prototype biomimetic microdisk arrays and systematic characterization of their coupling properties, followed by exploration of multi-resonator network dynamics.

Numerical simulation

Modal analysis was conducted using finite element modeling in COMSOL Multiphysics 6.2. The three-dimensional geometry incorporated both the active resonator elements and the underlying substrate to capture substrate-mediated elastic interactions and energy dissipation mechanisms. GaAs disk resonators were modeled with Young’s modulus and mass density of Y = 85.3 GPa and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\rho$$\end{document} = 5320 kg/m\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$^3$$\end{document}, respectively, at room temperature37.

The computational domain was discretized using second-order tetrahedral elements with adaptive mesh sizing. For structural analysis of the GaAs disk resonators with thickness t = 100 nm, the smallest element size was set to 20 nm in critical regions including disk edges, indentation features, and the central anchor to accurately capture high-stress gradients and mode confinement. For the substrate region, a graded mesh was employed with element sizes ranging from 20 nm at the disk-substrate interface to 500 nm in the bulk substrate, optimizing computational efficiency while maintaining interface accuracy. Mesh convergence was verified by ensuring eigenfrequency variations remained below 0.1% when refining the mesh by a factor of 1.5.

Mechanical constraints were applied at the peripheral and basal boundaries of the substrate. The coupling regions were modeled as thin ridge couplers with rounded geometry and finite curvature, representing the actual etched pillar base configuration. These couplers had width equal to the pillar bottom diameter and thickness matching the disk thickness. The computational framework accounts for strain propagation extending into the underlying substrate, where resonator oscillations generate mechanical disturbances within the anchoring region, creating elastic waves that traverse into the substrate material, resulting in energy leakage. From a theoretical perspective, the substrate-mediated elastic interactions persist between individual disk resonators regardless of intermediate coupler dimensions, including the limiting case where the coupler thickness approaches zero. When substrate dissipation mechanisms dominate over intrinsic resonator losses, this coupling pathway significantly degrades system performance. The substrate and coupler dimensional parameters constitute auxiliary design variables for modulating inter-resonator coupling dynamics17,38. To avoid artificial boundary effects on resonator dynamics, the GaAs substrate thickness was modeled to be two orders of magnitude larger than the pillar dimensions.
