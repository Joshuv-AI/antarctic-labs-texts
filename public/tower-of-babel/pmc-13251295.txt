CDK4 inhibition by luteolin enhances lenvatinib sensitivity in HCC via Wnt/β-catenin modulation
Authors: Baohong Yuan, Ruotian Wang, Ruhong Li, Songlin Yang, Dong Zhan, Jing Tan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) frequently develops resistance to lenvatinib. We investigated CDK4’s role in this resistance and the potential of Danshen/luteolin to overcome it.MethodsUsing bioinformatics, in vitro models, and in vivo xenografts, we modulated CDK4 expression (overexpression/shRNA) in HCC cells. Danshen extract and luteolin’s effects on lenvatinib sensitivity were assessed via viability/apoptosis assays and molecular analyses. Wnt/β-catenin pathway involvement was tested with an inhibitor and luciferase reporter.ResultsNetwork pharmacology identified CDK4 as a Danshen target in resistant HCC. High CDK4 correlated with poor prognosis and increased resistance. Danshen/luteolin reduced CDK4 protein, enhanced lenvatinib sensitivity, and suppressed tumor growth in vitro and in vivo. Luteolin promoted CDK4 degradation via the ubiquitin-proteasome pathway and modulated Wnt/β-catenin signaling, crucial for resistance.ConclusionsCDK4 is a key mediator of lenvatinib resistance in HCC. Danshen-derived luteolin acts as a CDK4 inhibitor, enhancing lenvatinib sensitivity by degrading CDK4 and targeting Wnt/β-catenin, supporting combination therapies to overcome resistance.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12935-026-04274-7.

Introduction

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is one of the leading causes of cancer-related deaths worldwide, accounting for approximately 90% of primary liver cancers [1]. The incidence of HCC has been steadily increasing, particularly in Western countries, due to the rising prevalence of risk factors such as chronic hepatitis B and C infections, alcohol abuse, and non-alcoholic fatty liver disease [2]. Treatment options for HCC have evolved over the years, ranging from surgical resection and liver transplantation for early-stage disease to locoregional therapies and systemic treatments for advanced cases [3]. However, the management of HCC remains challenging due to the heterogeneity of the disease, underlying liver dysfunction, and the development of drug resistance [4].

Lenvatinib, a multi-kinase inhibitor, has emerged as a first-line treatment option for advanced HCC [5]. It targets multiple receptor tyrosine kinases involved in tumor angiogenesis and oncogenesis, including vascular endothelial growth factor receptors (VEGFRs), fibroblast growth factor receptors (FGFRs), platelet-derived growth factor receptor α (PDGFRα), KIT, and RET [6]. By inhibiting these pathways, lenvatinib suppresses tumor growth and metastasis. Despite initial efficacy, the development of drug resistance remains a significant challenge in lenvatinib therapy [7]. Resistance mechanisms may involve the activation of alternative signaling pathways, the upregulation of drug efflux pumps, the expansion of cancer stem cell populations, and alterations in the tumor microenvironment [8]. Understanding and overcoming these resistance mechanisms is crucial for improving the long-term efficacy of lenvatinib and overall outcomes for HCC patients [9].

The exploration of natural products and Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM) in cancer treatment has gained significant attention in recent years, offering potential complementary approaches to conventional therapies [10, 11]. Among these, Danshen (Salvia miltiorrhiza Bge.), a widely used herb in TCM, has shown promising therapeutic effects in various cancer types, including HCC [12]. Danshen contains numerous bioactive compounds, such as tanshinones and phenolic acids, which exhibit anti-tumor, anti-angiogenic, and anti-inflammatory properties [13, 14]. Recent studies have demonstrated that Danshen and its active components can modulate multiple signaling pathways involved in cancer progression, including PI3K/AKT, MAPK, and NF-κB pathways [15]. Furthermore, Danshen has been shown to enhance the efficacy of chemotherapeutic agents and potentially reverse drug resistance in cancer cells [16]. In HCC specifically, Danshen extracts have demonstrated the ability to inhibit tumor growth, induce apoptosis, and suppress metastasis both in vitro and in vivo [17]. Moreover, recent research has highlighted the potential of Danshen in targeting cancer stem cells and modulating the tumor microenvironment [18–20], which are crucial factors in HCC progression and drug resistance. The multi-targeted nature of Danshen’s effects, combined with its relatively low toxicity profile, makes it an attractive candidate for combination therapies in HCC treatment.

The primary objective of this study was to identify candidate targets of Danshen’s active components in lenvatinib-resistant HCC and examine their potential to overcome this resistance. Through network pharmacology analysis and experimental validation, we identified CDK4 as a critical target. Our findings revealed that CDK4 overexpression correlated with poor prognosis and increased lenvatinib resistance in HCC. Danshen extract and its active component luteolin effectively reduced CDK4 protein levels, enhanced lenvatinib sensitivity, and suppressed tumor growth both in vitro and in vivo. Mechanistically, luteolin promoted CDK4 protein degradation and modulated Wnt/β-catenin signaling. These results highlight the potential of Danshen-derived luteolin as a CDK4 inhibitor to enhance lenvatinib sensitivity, providing a rationale for combination therapy strategies targeting CDK4 and Wnt/β-catenin signaling to overcome lenvatinib resistance in HCC treatment.

Methods

Danshen extract preparation

Danshen (Salvia miltiorrhiza Bge.) raw material, originating from Mengyin County, Linyi City, Shandong Province, China, was sourced from the Department of Pharmacy at Kunming Municipal Hospital of Traditional Chinese Medicine (Kunming, China). The plant material was authenticated by the hospital’s pharmacognosy experts according to the Chinese Pharmacopoeia standards, with morphological and microscopic identification confirming the species identity. To ensure consistency and eliminate batch-to-batch variability, a single large-scale extraction was performed to provide sufficient material for all experiments in this study. Ground Danshen powder was extracted using 75% EtOH/water. The ethanol was then removed under reduced pressure at 45–55 °C. The concentrated extract was partitioned between dichloromethane and water to separate a 15% triterpene glycoside (TG) fraction from the dichloromethane layer. Subsequently, dichloromethane was removed under reduced pressure at 45–55 °C. The concentrated fraction was further partitioned between n-butanol and water, yielding a 27% TG fraction from the n-butanol phase. After weighing, the extract was dissolved in DMSO, aliquoted, and stored at -20 °C for later use throughout the study.

Bioinformatics

Active components of Danshen were screened using TCMSP (http://tcmspw.com/), BATMAN-TCM (http://bionet.ncpsb.org/batman-tcm/), and TCMID (http://119.3.41.228:8000/tcmid/) databases. Criteria of Drug-likeness ≥ 0.18 and Oral Bioavailability ≥ 30% were applied. Transcriptome data (GSE211850) from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database was analyzed to identify differentially expressed genes in lenvatinib-resistant HCC cells using R software (version 4.0.3) with the limma package. Protein-protein interaction (PPI) network analysis was performed using STRING database (https://string-db.org/). Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis was conducted using the clusterProfiler R package.

Kaplan-Meier (KM) survival analysis was performed using data from The Cancer Genome Atlas Hepatocellular Carcinoma (TCGA-HCC) cohort, accessed through the UCSC Xena browser (https://xenabrowser.net/). Gene expression data and corresponding clinical information were downloaded for subsequent analysis. Patients were stratified into high- and low-expression groups based on the median expression level of each candidate gene. KM survival curves were generated using the survival and survminer R packages. Log-rank tests were performed to assess the statistical significance of differences in overall survival between high- and low-expression groups. Hazard ratios (HR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) were calculated using Cox proportional hazards regression models for both univariate and multivariate analyses.

Molecular docking of luteolin with CDK4 was performed using AutoDock Vina (version 1.1.2). The three-dimensional structure of CDK4 (PDB ID: 2W96) was obtained from the Protein Data Bank (https://www.rcsb.org/), and the structure of luteolin was retrieved from the PubChem database (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/). AutoDockTools (version 1.5.6) was used for preparing the receptor and ligand files. The docking grid box was centered on the ATP-binding site of CDK4, and the exhaustiveness was set to 8. The docking results were visualized using PyMOL (version 2.3.0).

Clinical sample collection

Forty pairs of HCC tumor tissues and adjacent non-tumor tissues were collected from patients undergoing surgical resection at Yan’An Hospital Affiliated to Kunming Medical University. Samples were immediately snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at -80 °C until further use. This study was approved by the Medical Research Ethics Committee of Yan’An Hospital Affiliated to Kunming Medical University (approval number: 2024-277-01), and written informed consent was obtained from all patients.

Cell culture and treatment

HepG2 and Huh7 cell lines were obtained from Procell (Wuhan, China). Cells were cultured in DMEM (Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% FBS (Gibco, USA) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Invitrogen, USA) at 37 °C with 5% CO2. MG132, Bafilomycin A1, luteolin, lenvatinib, and ICG-001 were purchased from MedChemExpress (Shanghai, China). For cell viability assays, cells were treated with indicated concentrations of lenvatinib, luteolin, ICG-001, or a combination for 48 h.

CDK4 overexpression and shRNA-mediated silencing

For CDK4 overexpression, the full-length human CDK4 cDNA was cloned into the pLVX-Puro vector (Clontech, USA). HCC cells were transfected with 2 µg of pLVX-Puro-CDK4 or empty pLVX-Puro vector using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Cells were incubated for a total of 48 h post-transfection before being used for subsequent experiments. For CDK4 silencing, shRNA targeting CDK4 was cloned into the pLKO.1 vector (Addgene, USA). Lentiviral particles were produced in HEK293T cells using the psPAX2 and pMD2.G packaging plasmids (Addgene, USA). HCC cells were transduced with lentiviral particles and selected with puromycin (2 µg/ml, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) for 7 days.

Establishment of drug-resistant cell lines

Lenvatinib-resistant HepG2 and Huh7 cell lines were established by exposing cells to gradually increasing concentrations of lenvatinib (2, 5, 10, 15, 20, 30 µM) for at least 2 weeks at each concentration, over a total period of 6 months. Control cells were treated with an equivalent volume of DMSO. Established resistant clones were maintained in 10 µM lenvatinib.

CCK-8 viability assay

Cells (5 × 10³ per well) were seeded in 96-well plates. After overnight attachment, cells were treated with drugs or vehicle for 48 h. CCK-8 solution (10 µL; Dojindo, Japan) was added to each well and incubated for 2 h at 37 °C. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a Synergy H1 microplate reader (BioTek, USA). Cell viability was calculated as a percentage relative to the control group. Experiments were performed in triplicate and repeated three times.

TUNEL fluorescence staining

For cell samples: Cells (2 × 10⁴ per well) were seeded on glass coverslips in 24-well plates and treated for 48 h. For xenograft tumors: Fresh tumor tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned (5 μm thickness). Both cell and tissue samples were processed using the In Situ Cell Death Detection Kit, Fluorescein (Roche, Switzerland) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (1 µg/mL, Sigma-Aldrich). Images were captured using a Zeiss LSM 800 confocal microscope (40× objective). The percentage of TUNEL-positive cells was calculated from at least five random fields per sample. Experiments were repeated three times independently.

Western blot

Proteins were extracted using RIPA buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) containing protease inhibitors. Protein concentration was determined using the BCA Protein Assay Kit (Pierce, USA). Equal amounts of protein were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes (Millipore, USA). Membranes were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. The following antibodies were used: β-catenin (1:1000, #8480, Cell Signaling Technology), CDK4 (1:1000, #12790, Cell Signaling Technology), GSK3β (1:1000, #12456, Cell Signaling Technology), p-GSK3β (Ser9) (1:1000, #5558, Cell Signaling Technology), Cleaved caspase-3 (1:1000, #9664, Cell Signaling Technology), caspase-3 (1:1000, #9662, Cell Signaling Technology), Bcl-2 (1:1000, #15071, Cell Signaling Technology), Bax (1:1000, #5023, Cell Signaling Technology), and β-actin (1:5000, #4970, Cell Signaling Technology). After washing, membranes were incubated with either horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (1:5000, #7074, Cell Signaling Technology) or horse anti-mouse IgG (1:5000, #7076, Cell Signaling Technology) secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature, depending on the species of the primary antibody. Signals were visualized using ECL substrate (Pierce, USA) and the ChemiDoc Imaging System (Bio-Rad, USA). Band intensities were quantified using Image Lab software (Bio-Rad, USA), and protein levels were normalized to β-actin.

CDK4 immunoprecipitation and ubiquitination detection

Cells were lysed in IP lysis buffer (Pierce, USA) containing protease inhibitors. CDK4 was immunoprecipitated using anti-CDK4 antibody (1:50, #12790, Cell Signaling Technology) and Protein A/G PLUS-Agarose beads (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, USA). Ubiquitination was detected by western blot using anti-ubiquitin antibody (1:1000, #3936, Cell Signaling Technology).

TCF4 luciferase reporter assay

β-catenin transcription activity was assessed using the TCF/LEF Reporter Kit (Wnt Signaling Pathway) from BPS Bioscience. This kit includes a TCF/LEF luciferase reporter vector containing firefly luciferase under the control of TCF/LEF responsive elements, premixed with a constitutively-expressing Renilla luciferase vector for internal control. Cells (2 × 104 per well) were seeded in 96-well plates and transfected with the TCF/LEF reporter mixture using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen, USA). A non-inducible luciferase vector served as a negative control. After 24 h, cells were treated with indicated compounds for an additional 24 h. Luciferase activity was measured using the Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay System (Promega, USA) on a GloMax Navigator Microplate Luminometer (Promega, USA). Firefly luciferase activity was normalized to Renilla luciferase activity, and results were expressed as fold change relative to the control group.

RT-qPCR

Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, USA). cDNA was synthesized using the PrimeScript RT Reagent Kit (Takara, Japan). qPCR was performed using SYBR Premix Ex Taq (Takara, Japan) on a LightCycler 480 II Real-Time PCR System (Roche, Switzerland). Relative quantification of gene expression was performed using 2−∆∆Cᴛ with β-actin gene as an internal control.

Xenograft mouse model

All animal experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Laboratory Animal Ethics Committee of Yan’An Hospital Affiliated to Kunming Medical University, China (approval number: 2025002). Six-week-old male BALB/c nude mice were obtained from Shanghai SLAC Laboratory Animal Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Mice were housed in a specific pathogen-free facility with controlled temperature (22 ± 2°C), humidity (50 ± 10%), and a 12-hour light/dark cycle. Animals had free access to standard chow and water. To examine the effect of Danshen extract on lenvatinib sensitivity, mice were subcutaneously injected with 5 × 106 parental or lenvatinib-resistant Huh7 cells. When tumors reached ~ 100 mm3, mice were randomly divided into four groups (n = 6 per group): [1] control [2], lenvatinib (10 mg/kg/day) [3], Danshen extract (50 mg/kg/day), and [4] lenvatinib + Danshen extract. To investigate the effect of luteolin on lenvatinib sensitivity, a separate set of experiments was conducted. Mice were subcutaneously injected with 5 × 106 parental or lenvatinib-resistant Huh7 cells and randomly divided into four groups (n = 6 per group) when tumors reached ~ 100 mm3: [1] control [2], lenvatinib (10 mg/kg/day) [3], luteolin (10 mg/kg/day), and [4] lenvatinib + luteolin. In both sets of experiments, treatments were administered by oral gavage for 30 days. Tumor volumes were measured every 5 days and calculated as (length × width2)/2. At the end of the experiment, mice were euthanized by CO2 asphyxiation and cervical dislocation, and tumors were harvested from terminally dead mice for further analysis.

Statistics

Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) from at least 3 independent experiments in cell samples. Statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism 8.0 software (GraphPad Software, Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). Comparisons between two groups were performed using Student’s t-test. One-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test was used for multiple group comparisons. Kaplan-Meier survival analysis was performed using the log-rank test. The Cox proportional hazards model was used for univariate and multivariate analyses. P-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Identification of CDK4 as a potential target of Salvia miltiorrhiza Bge. (Danshen) in lenvatinib-resistant hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC)

In our effort to identify potential protein targets of Danshen in lenvatinib-resistant HCC, we employed a two-step approach. We initially screened the active components of Danshen using TCMSP, BATMAN-TCM, and TCMID databases, applying criteria of Drug-likeness ≥ 0.18 and Oral Bioavailability ≥ 30%, which yielded 58 active components corresponding to 119 potential targets (Fig. 1A). Next, we analyzed transcriptome data (GSE211850) to identify differentially expressed genes in lenvatinib-resistant HCC cells. By intersecting these differentially expressed genes with the potential targets of Danshen, we identified 53 overlapping targets which are potentially relevant to lenvatinib resistance in HCC (Fig. 1B). The top 30 targets, ranked according to their association strength with Danshen’s active components (Fig. 1C), underwent protein-protein interaction (PPI) network analysis. From this analysis, the top ten nodes were identified as key protein targets (Fig. 1D). Furthermore, gene ontology (GO) enrichment analysis revealed that the 53 overlapping targets were mainly related to inflammatory responses (Figure S1). The top 10 genes associated with enriched GO terms were identified (Figure S2). Finally, the subsequent intersection of the top ten nodes from the PPI analysis and the top 10 candidates from the GO analysis resulted in 7 common candidates (CDKN1A, RELA, CDK4, MYC, STAT3, EGFR and JUN) (Fig. 1E).

Next, we analyzed data from the TCGA-HCC (The Cancer Genome Atlas Hepatocellular Carcinoma) cohort. We performed Kaplan-Meier (KM) survival analysis after stratifying patients into high- and low-expression groups based on the expression levels of each candidate gene. Our analysis revealed that high-level expression of RELA, JUN, or CDK4 was significantly associated with reduced overall survival in HCC patients. Notably, the CDK4 high-expression group demonstrated the poorest survival outcome (Fig. 1F and Figure S3). Moreover, analysis of the HCC cohort revealed that CDK4 has a Hazard Ratio significantly above 1 in both univariate and multivariate analyses (Table 1). This indicates that higher CDK4 expression is strongly associated with increased mortality risk in HCC patients, independent of other factors. The consistency across both analyses underscores CDK4’s potential as a robust prognostic marker in HCC. The heightened expression levels of CDK4 were further verified in the clinical samples of HCC tumors by RT-qPCR (Fig. 1G) and Western blot (Fig. 1H), compared to the adjacent normal tissues. Collectively, these findings identify CDK4 as a dismal progression marker of HCC and highlight CDK4 as a promising target of Danshen in lenvatinib-resistant HCC, warranting future investigation of Danshen’s effect on CDK4 and its potential to modulate lenvatinib drug response.

Lenvatinib drug resistance development is associated with CDK4 overexpression in HCC cells

To investigate the implication of CDK4 in lenvatinib drug resistance, we established drug-resistant HepG2 and Huh7 cell lines by exposing the cells to escalating doses of lenvatinib. A CCK-8 cell viability assay confirmed the increased resistance to lenvatinib in the resistant cell lines (Fig. 2A). Drug resistant cells could also proliferate in 20 µM lenvatinib, while the parental cell lines failed to proliferate (Fig. 2B). Moreover, drug resistant cells demonstrated reduced apoptotic cell death upon lenvatinib treatment, as revealed by a lower percentage of TUNEL staining positive cells (Fig. 2C), reduced levels of pro-apoptotic proteins (cleaved caspase-3 and Bax) and an increased level of anti-apoptotic protein (Bcl-2) (Fig. 2D). Furthermore, drug-resistant cells exhibited upregulated levels of drug efflux genes (MRP1, P-Gp, and BRCP) (Fig. 2E) and cancer stem cell marker genes (EpCAM, CD90, and CD24) (Fig. 2F). Additionally, cell cycle-related genes (CDK4, CDK6, Cyclin B, and Cyclin E) were also significantly upregulated in drug-resistant cells (Fig. 2G), with CDK4 showing the strongest overexpression at the protein level (Fig. 2H).

Silencing CDK4 sensitizes drug-resistant HCC cells to lenvatinib treatment

We next applied lentivirus carrying a control shRNA (sh-NC) or a CDK4-targeting shRNA (sh-CDK4) to transduce drug-resistant HepG2 and Huh7 cell lines. CDK4-targeting shRNA effectively reduced the protein levels of CDK4 in drug-resistant HCC cells (Fig. 3A). CDK4 knockdown sensitized drug-resistant cells to 20 µM lenvatinib treatment, as revealed by reduced cell viability (CCK-8 assay, Fig. 3B) and an increased percentage of TUNEL-staining positive cells (Fig. 3C) in the cells with CDK4 silencing. The levels of pro-apoptotic proteins (cleaved caspase-3 and Bax) were increased after CDK4 knockdown, while the level of anti-apoptotic protein (Bcl-2) was reduced (Fig. 3D). Furthermore, silencing CDK4 also suppressed the expression of drug efflux genes (Fig. 3E) and cancer stem cell marker genes (Fig. 3F). These results demonstrate that CDK4 knockdown can effectively reverse lenvatinib resistance in HCC cells, highlighting its potential as a therapeutic target for overcoming drug resistance in liver cancer treatment.

Danshen extract increases the response of HCC cells to lenvatinib treatment in vitro and in vivo

Since we discovered that the active components of Danshen may target genes (including CDK4) involved in lenvatinib resistance in HCC cells, we next sought to examine whether Danshen extract could affect lenvatinib sensitivity. First, we applied increasing doses of Danshen extract (0 to 200 µg/ml) to HepG2 and Huh7 cells, and CCK-8 cell viability assay demonstrated that concentrations at or above 40 µg/ml induced cytotoxicity (Figure S4). Consequently, we adopted a 20 µg/ml Danshen extract concentration in the following experiments to minimize its toxic effect.

At the mRNA level, Danshen extract did not affect CDK4 expression (Fig. 4A); however, the protein levels of CDK4 were significantly reduced by the treatment with Danshen extract in both parental and drug-resistant HepG2 and Huh7 cell lines (Fig. 4B). Danshen extract treatment also repressed the expression of drug efflux genes (Fig. 4C) and cancer stem cell marker genes (Fig. 4D) in drug-resistant cells. Importantly, the co-treatment with Danshen extract increased cellular sensitivity to lenvatinib in both parental and resistant cells, as evidenced by reduced cell viability (Fig. 4E) and increased levels of TUNEL staining-positive cells (Fig. 4F).

Luteolin is an active component which promotes CDK4 protein degradation

Luteolin has been reported as a promising active component of Danshen in multiple studies [21–24]. Our molecular docking analysis revealed that luteolin shows strong potential interaction with the CDK4 protein (Free Energy = -9.0 kcal/mol, Fig. 5A). In Huh7 cells, luteolin demonstrated a half maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) of approximately 50 µM (Fig. 5B). At a concentration of 25 µM, luteolin did not significantly affect CDK4 mRNA levels (Fig. 5C), but significantly reduced its protein levels (Fig. 5D). These findings suggest that luteolin may exert its effects on CDK4 primarily through post-transcriptional mechanisms. After inhibiting de novo protein synthesis with cycloheximide, we demonstrated that the presence of luteolin reduced CDK4 protein stability (Fig. 5E). The decreased protein stability was associated with increased CDK4 protein ubiquitination (Fig. 5F), and the application of MG132 (a proteasome inhibitor) rescued CDK4 protein levels in the presence of luteolin treatment (Fig. 5G). Since MG132 did not completely abrogate CDK4 degradation, we further examined the potential involvement of the autophagy-lysosome pathway by treating cells with Bafilomycin A1, an inhibitor that blocks autophagosome-lysosome fusion. Bafilomycin A1 treatment resulted in a modest increase in CDK4 protein levels compared to luteolin treatment alone (Fig. 5H), suggesting that the autophagy-lysosome pathway also contributes to CDK4 degradation, albeit to a lesser extent. These results indicate that luteolin promotes CDK4 protein degradation primarily through the ubiquitin-proteasome pathway, with a complementary contribution from the autophagy-lysosome pathway.

Luteolin enhances lenvatinib drug sensitivity via targeting CDK4 in HCC cells

To evaluate whether luteolin affects lenvatinib drug sensitivity through targeting CDK4, we overexpressed CDK4 in Huh7 cells and examined their response to lenvatinib treatment. Luteolin effectively reduced CDK4 protein levels in both control and CDK4-overexpressing cells (Fig. 6A). Notably, overexpression of CDK4 decreased cellular sensitivity to lenvatinib treatment, as evidenced by CCK-8 cell viability assay and TUNEL staining (Fig. 6B and C, respectively). However, luteolin treatment significantly attenuated this CDK4-mediated drug resistance effect. Similarly, CDK4 overexpression enhanced the expression of drug efflux genes (Fig. 6D) and cancer stem cell marker genes (Fig. 6E), which was mitigated by luteolin treatment.

Wnt/β-catenin signaling is implicated in CDK4- and luteolin-regulated lenvatinib sensitivity

Given that Wnt/β-Catenin signaling pathway is implicated in lenvatinib drug resistance [25, 26], we next investigated whether this signaling pathway is involved in regulatory effect of luteolin. The Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway was found to be activated in drug-resistant Huh7 cells, as revealed by the increased levels of β-catenin and p-GSK3β (Ser9) phosphorylation (Fig. 7A). Consistently, the TCF4 luciferase reporter assay showed that drug-resistant Huh7 cells displayed a heightened level of β-catenin-dependent transcription activity (Fig. 7B), and increased mRNA expression levels of β-catenin target genes (Axin2, c-MYC and Cyclin D1) (Fig. 7C). These effects were suppressed by the β-catenin signaling inhibitor ICG-001, while the expression level of CDK4 was not affected (Fig. 7B and C). Inhibiting β-catenin activity also significantly sensitized both parental and drug-resistant cells to lenvatinib treatment (Fig. 7D, CCK-8 assay; and Fig. 7E, TUNEL staining). These findings indicate that Wnt/β-catenin signaling activation is indispensable for lenvatinib drug-resistance, but CDK4 expression is not dependent on this signaling pathway.

Discussion

In this study, we identified CDK4 as a critical target of Danshen in lenvatinib-resistant hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) through network pharmacology analysis and experimental validation. Our findings revealed that CDK4 overexpression correlates with poor prognosis in HCC patients and contributes to lenvatinib resistance in HCC cells. Importantly, we demonstrated that Danshen extract and its active component luteolin effectively reduce CDK4 protein levels, leading to enhanced lenvatinib sensitivity and suppressed tumor growth both in vitro and in vivo. Mechanistically, we found that luteolin promotes CDK4 protein degradation via the ubiquitin-proteasome pathway and modulates Wnt/β-catenin signaling, a pathway crucial for lenvatinib resistance. These results highlight the potential of Danshen-derived luteolin as a CDK4 inhibitor to overcome lenvatinib resistance in HCC, providing a novel approach to enhance the efficacy of existing targeted therapies.

The identification of CDK4 as a key player in lenvatinib resistance underscores its significance in HCC progression and treatment outcomes. Our findings align with previous studies that have implicated CDK4 in various aspects of HCC pathogenesis [27]. CDK4 overexpression has been associated with increased cell proliferation, enhanced survival, and metastatic potential in HCC [28]. Moreover, the correlation between high CDK4 expression and poor prognosis observed in our study is consistent with earlier reports in HCC and other cancer types [29–31]. In the context of drug resistance, our results expand upon previous work demonstrating CDK4’s role in chemoresistance [32]. For instance, CDK4 activation has been linked to resistance to PI3K inhibitors in breast cancer and EGFR inhibitors in non-small cell lung cancer [33, 34]. Our study now extends this concept to targeted therapy resistance in HCC, specifically to lenvatinib. The potential of CDK4 as a therapeutic target in HCC is further supported by a preclinical study showing that CDK4/6 inhibitors improve the anti-tumor efficacy of lenvatinib in HCC cells [35]. Collectively, these findings highlight CDK4 as a promising target for overcoming lenvatinib resistance and improving treatment outcomes in HCC.

Our study reveals a dual mechanism of action for luteolin in overcoming lenvatinib resistance in HCC. Firstly, luteolin promotes CDK4 protein degradation via the ubiquitin-proteasome pathway, effectively reducing CDK4 levels and its downstream oncogenic effects. This mechanism differs from traditional CDK4 inhibitors, which typically block kinase activity without affecting protein levels [36, 37]. The protein degradation approach may offer advantages in terms of more complete pathway inhibition and potentially reduced compensatory mechanisms [38, 39]. Secondly, luteolin modulates Wnt/β-catenin signaling, a pathway implicated in cancer stem cell maintenance and drug resistance [40, 41]. By targeting both CDK4 and Wnt/β-catenin pathways, luteolin addresses multiple aspects of lenvatinib resistance, including cell cycle dysregulation, stemness, and survival signaling [42, 43]. This dual action distinguishes luteolin from other CDK4 inhibitors like palbociclib or abemaciclib, which primarily target the cell cycle [44]. Moreover, compared to specific Wnt/β-catenin inhibitors such as PRI-724 [45], luteolin’s broader mechanism may provide additional benefits in overcoming resistance. The ability of luteolin to simultaneously target these interconnected pathways likely contributes to its efficacy in sensitizing HCC cells to lenvatinib. This multi-targeted approach aligns with emerging strategies in cancer therapy that aim to combat resistance by addressing multiple resistance mechanisms concurrently [46–48].

The findings of this study have significant clinical implications for HCC treatment. The potential of combining luteolin or Danshen extracts with lenvatinib presents a promising strategy to enhance treatment efficacy and overcome drug resistance in HCC. This approach aligns with the growing interest in combining targeted therapies with natural compounds to improve outcomes and reduce toxicity [49]. However, it is important to acknowledge the limitations of our study. While we demonstrated the efficacy of luteolin in vitro and in xenograft models, further investigations in more complex preclinical models and ultimately clinical trials are necessary to validate these findings. Future research should focus on optimizing the dosing and scheduling of luteolin or Danshen extracts in combination with lenvatinib, as well as exploring potential biomarkers to identify patients most likely to benefit from this approach. Additionally, it is worth noting that Danshen extract may contain other active components beyond luteolin that could contribute to its anti-cancer effects [50]. For instance, our network pharmacology analysis identified multiple potential targets, including EGFR and CDK6, both of which have been reported as critical drivers of lenvatinib resistance in HCC [51, 52]. This suggests that Danshen’s therapeutic efficacy may result from multiple bioactive components acting synergistically on complementary resistance pathways. Further characterization of these components and their potential synergistic effects with luteolin and lenvatinib is warranted. By leveraging the multi-targeted nature of natural compounds, we may be able to address the complex mechanisms of drug resistance more effectively, paving the way for more durable treatment responses in cancer patients.

Conclusion

In summary, our study identifies CDK4 as a critical target of Danshen in lenvatinib-resistant HCC and demonstrates the potential of its active component, luteolin, in overcoming this resistance. The dual mechanism of luteolin, involving CDK4 protein degradation and Wnt/β-catenin signaling modulation, offers a novel approach to enhance the efficacy of lenvatinib in HCC treatment. These findings provide a strong rationale for further investigation of combination therapies incorporating Danshen extracts or luteolin with lenvatinib in HCC. Future research should focus on optimizing this combination strategy, exploring potential biomarkers for patient selection, and evaluating its efficacy in clinical trials. Additionally, further characterization of other active components in Danshen extract may uncover additional synergistic effects.
