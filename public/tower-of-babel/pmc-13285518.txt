Hormone therapy preserves ocular surface integrity following circadian alignment disturbance in menopausal rats
Authors: Semra Acer, Mehmet Argun, Özlem Özmen, Cafer Açıkgöz, Mustafa Yavuz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study examines the impact of a light-cycle shift regimen on corneal and conjunctival tissues in menopausal rats and evaluates the protective role of combined hormone therapy.MethodsTwenty-four menopausal female albino rats were randomly assigned to three groups (n = 8) following a 10-day acclimatization period. Group 1 (Control+Saline) was maintained under a 12:12 light/dark cycle. Light-cycle shift regimen was induced in Groups 2 and 3 using a rotating 7-day light-exposure sequence repeated over 21 days; this protocol consisted of 24 h of continuous light, 72 h of inverted dark–light timing, and 72 h of standard light–dark conditions. Groups 1 and 2 received saline, while Group 3 received 17β-Estradiol and drospirenone daily via oral gavage. After 31 days, eyes were enucleated for histological and immunohistochemical analyses of corneal, conjunctival, and palpebral tissues, including caspase-3 (Cas-3), tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), and PERIOD-2 (PER2) expression.ResultsLight-cycle shift regimen (Group 2) significantly increased corneal thickness (p < 0.001), conjunctival inflammation, and vascular congestion, with marked upregulation of Cas-3 and TNF-α and downregulation of PER2. Hormone therapy (Group 3) attenuated these effects, showing reduced corneal edema, diminished inflammatory infiltration, and partial normalization of molecular markers.ConclusionsShifting light-dark cycles may aggravate inflammatory and apoptotic changes in the ocular surface during menopause. Estrogen–progestin therapy attenuates these alterations by modulating the expression of the circadian-associated protein PER2 and maintaining structural integrity. These findings suggest that hormone therapy may offer potential benefits for preserving ocular surface homeostasis in menopausal women experiencing sleep or circadian rhythm disturbances.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12886-026-04877-8.Keywords: Circadian rhythm, Ocular surface, Menopause, Hormone therapy, PER2

Introduction

An optimal night sleep is essential for maintaining physical and mental health. Unfortunately, sleep disorders are common in aging women especially at menopausal period. Hormonal changes, vasomotor symptoms, depressive mood changes, aging and comorbid conditions are the main reasons of poor sleep quality in menopausal women [1, 2]. Sleep deprivation can disrupt circadian rhythms and has been increasingly associated with ocular disorders, most notably dry eye disease (DED) [3].

The menopausal period is associated with significant physiological changes that may alter the resilience of the ocular surface. Recent research has demonstrated the presence of androgen, estrogen, and progesterone receptors in the lacrimal and meibomian glands, as well as on the ocular surface [4]. The higher incidence of DED in menopausal women suggests a strong relationship between sex hormones and tear film and ocular surface homeostasis [5]. These findings support the hypothesis that hormonal fluctuations during menopause may directly impair the cellular stability of the ocular surface, potentially contributing to the pathogenesis of ocular surface disorders.

Hormone therapy (HT) is commonly used to relieve menopausal symptoms and may provide protective benefits. Restoring hormone levels through HT can reduce the risk of osteoporosis, cardiovascular disease, stroke, and possibly dementia, though risks and benefits must be considered individually [6–7]. While HT effectively improves many vasomotor and psychological symptoms, its effects on ocular health are controversial, with studies reporting both protective and detrimental impacts on tear parameters and ocular surface characteristics, highlighting the need for further research [8–14].

The frequent co-occurrence of menopause and sleep disorders may contribute to greater disruption of ocular surface homeostasis compared to either condition alone. In this study, we evaluated the impact of a light-cycle shift regimen—a model for circadian alignment disturbance—on the ocular surface, as well as to assess the potential protective effects of HT in a rat model of menopause. In addition to histological analysis, immunohistochemical analysis was performed to assess the expression of key molecular markers, including caspase-3 (Cas-3), a critical executioner enzyme in the apoptotic cascade; tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), a pro-inflammatory cytokine involved in immune regulation and inflammatory responses; and Period Circadian Regulator 2 (PER2), a component of the Period gene family that regulates circadian rhythms in locomotor activity, metabolism, and behavior [15–17].

Materials and methods

Study design

Twenty-four female Wistar albino rats (12–15 months old; 350–400 g) were selected for the study. All animals were obtained from Burdur Mehmet Akif Ersoy University Experimental Animal Research Center. The animals were institutionally owned by the university, and all experiments were performed at the same facility. With an effect size of 1.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a power of 0.80, it was calculated that eight animals per group were sufficient to detect statistically significant differences while adhering to the ethical principle of Reduction in animal research.

Menopausal status was assessed by daily vaginal cytology for seven consecutive days [18]. Vaginal lavage was performed by gently inserting a dropper containing 0.9% saline 1–2 cm into the vagina, flushing and aspirating 2–3 times, and transferring the fluid onto glass slides. Vaginal smears were collected from each rat every morning between 08:00 and 10:00 and stained with alkaline methylene blue and examined microscopically.

The rat estrous cycle normally lasts 4–5 days and includes four stages: proestrus, estrus, metestrus, and diestrus. Smears dominated by leukocytes and containing few epithelial cells were classified as diestrus. Rats that consistently exhibited diestrus-like cytology with no evidence of cyclicity for one week were considered menopausal and assigned to the experimental groups.

The experimental rats were provided with a standardized commercial pelleted diet and water ad libitum throughout the study. All animals were maintained under controlled environmental conditions (21–23 °C with 55–60% relative humidity) and housed in Euro-type IV cages. Experimental timeline and light-exposure schedule

Acclimatization phase (days 1–10)

All rats were maintained under a standard 12:12 light/dark (LD) cycle for an initial 10-day period to allow for physiological stabilization.

Experimental phase (days 11–31)

On Day 11, the rats were randomly assigned to three groups (n = 8 per group) by an investigator who was unaware of the specific characteristics of each animal at the time of allocation to ensure unbiased distribution for a 21-day treatment period:

Group 1 (Control+Saline): Maintained under a standard 12:12 LD cycle and received a daily oral gavage of 0.9% saline to control for handling stress.

Environmental circadian stress was induced in Groups 2 and 3 was induced using a rotating 7-day light-exposure sequence, repeated three times for a total of 21 days [19]. Each 7-day cycle consisted of:

Hormone administration

Group 3 received a daily oral gavage of a combined estrogen–progestin regimen consisting of 0.0875 mg/kg/day of 17β-Estradiol and 0.175 mg/kg/day of drospirenone. These doses were extrapolated from the human maintenance dose (Angeliq®; 1 mg estradiol hemihydrate + 2 mg drospirenone) based on a standard human weight of 70 kg, using the body surface area (BSA) scaling method, where the Km factor is 37 for humans and 6 for rats [20]. The calculation followed the standard formula;

The hormones were finely crushed and suspended in 0.9% saline, administered in a constant volume of 1.0 mL per animal. To control for handling-induced stress, Groups 1 and 2 received an equivalent volume of the saline vehicle via gavage at the same daily frequency.

No adverse events, including significant weight loss, behavioral abnormalities, or mortality, were observed in any of the experimental groups during the 21-day light-cycle rotation and daily gavage periods. All 24 rats successfully completed the study protocol.

Euthanasia and tissue collection (day 32)

On Day 32, all rats were euthanized between 10:00 and 12:00 AM (corresponding to ZT1–ZT3), immediately following the conclusion of the final disruption cycle. This timing was strictly synchronized across all experimental groups to control for the phase-dependent expression of PER2 and inflammatory cytokines, thereby eliminating time-of-day effects as a confounding variable. Euthanasia was performed under deep anesthesia induced by intraperitoneal administration of 80 mg/kg ketamine and 10 mg/kg xylazine. The depth of anesthesia was confirmed by the absence of pedal withdrawal and corneal reflexes. Following confirmation of adequate anesthesia, surgical exsanguination was performed. Both eyes were then enucleated, and corneal, eyelid conjunctival, and palpebral tissues were carefully dissected and collected for histological and immunohistochemical analyses.

Histopathological analyses

Following enucleation, the eyeballs were fixed in 10% buffered formalin during necropsy. To insert the tissue processing cassette, the middle two parts were removed. Samples were routinely processed and embedded in paraffin wax. Paraffin blocks were sectioned at a thickness of 5 μm, and the sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) for histological evaluation. Morphometric analysis of the corneal tissue was conducted using an Olympus CX41 light microscope equipped with the CellSens Life Science Imaging Software System (Olympus Corporation, Tokyo, Japan). Measurements of corneal epithelial thickness and total corneal thickness were obtained from the central region of the cornea in each specimen.

Immunohistochemical examination

Three consecutive 4-µm sections obtained from formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded tissue blocks were mounted on poly-L-lysine–coated slides. Immunohistochemical staining was performed using a streptavidin–biotin–peroxidase method with a horseradish peroxidase (HRP) detection system and diaminobenzidine (DAB) as chromogen (EXPOSE Mouse and Rabbit Specific HRP/DAB Detection Kit, ab80436, Abcam, Cambridge, UK), strictly following the manufacturer’s protocol. After deparaffinization and rehydration, antigen retrieval was performed using citrate buffer (pH 6.0). Endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked with 3% hydrogen peroxide. Sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies diluted 1:100: tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α; ab307165, Abcam), caspase-3 (ab208161, Abcam), and period circadian protein 2 (PER2; A88334, Antibodies.com, Cambridge, UK). After incubation with biotinylated secondary antibodies, HRP-conjugated streptavidin was applied, and immunoreactivity was visualized using DAB as the chromogen. Slides were counterstained with hematoxylin, dehydrated, and mounted. Negative controls were processed identically, with the primary antibody replaced by antibody diluent.

Evaluation and scoring

Immunoreactivity was evaluated semi-quantitatively by two independent blind observers, including an experienced pathologist from an external institution. Discrepancies were resolved by joint review to achieve consensus. Staining intensity and distribution were assessed using a predefined ordinal scoring system: (0) no staining; (1) focal, weak staining (< 25% of cells); (2) diffuse, weak-to-moderate staining (25–50% of cells); (3) diffuse, strong staining (> 50% of cells). For each specimen, ten non-overlapping high-power fields (40× objective) were randomly selected from standardized anatomical regions of interest (ROIs) to minimize selection bias. Care was taken to avoid necrotic or artifact-prone areas.

Image acquisition was performed using the Database Manual Cell Sens Life Science Imaging Software System (Olympus Co., Tokyo, Japan). ImageJ software (version 1.48, National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA) was used for image documentation and archiving purposes only; no automated optical density or positive area quantification was performed. The final immunohistochemical score for each sample was calculated as the median of the evaluated fields and used for statistical analysis.

In the present study, the experimental unit was defined as the individual animal. To avoid pseudo-replication, measurements obtained from both eyes of the same animal were not treated as independent observations. For analyses in which bilateral samples were available, values from both eyes were averaged to generate a single representative value per animal prior to statistical evaluation. Similarly, for corneal thickness and other morphometric assessments, multiple measurements were obtained from predefined non-overlapping regions within each section; however, these within-eye measurements were first averaged to yield a single value per eye, and subsequently averaged per animal. Thus, all statistical analyses were conducted using one aggregated value per animal as the unit of inference.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (version 22.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The Shapiro-Wilk method was employed to assess the normality of the data distribution. Immunohistochemical staining scores were considered ordinal (categorical) data rather than continuous variables. Therefore, the results were expressed as median (minimum–maximum) values. Comparisons among groups were performed using the Kruskal–Wallis test. When a statistically significant difference was detected, pairwise comparisons were conducted using Dunn’s post hoc test with Bonferroni correction. A p value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Histopathological findings

The stratified squamous epithelium, stroma, and endothelium in the corneal sections obtained from the Group 1 (Control+Saline) displayed normal histological characteristics of cornea and eyelid conjunctiva. The corneal epithelium in Group 2 exhibited a marked increase in thickness, along with nuclear alterations in epithelial cells—some appearing pale and others displaying peripheral chromatin margination. Additionally, stromal edema and overall corneal thickening were observed. The conjunctiva in this group also exhibited hyperemia, edema, and epithelial sloughing. The pathological findings in the cornea and conjunctiva were reduced by hormone therapy in Group 3 (Fig. 2).

Immunohistochemical analysis of corneal and conjunctival sections revealed distinct expression patterns for Cas-3, TNF-α, and PER2 across the experimental groups. In Group 1 (Control+Saline), Cas-3 and TNF-α expressions were either negative or characterized by only mild, baseline staining, whereas PER2 exhibited strong, marked expression in the nuclei and cytoplasm of the epithelial layers. In contrast, Group 2 (Light-cycle shift+Saline) demonstrated a significant shift in molecular markers, characterized by higher expression levels of the apoptotic marker Cas-3 and the pro-inflammatory cytokine TNF-α. This was accompanied by a notable downregulation (lower expression) of PER2, indicating a breakdown of the local circadian clock. Discussion

The results of this study indicate that a light-cycle shift regimen designed to disturb circadian rhythm contributes to significant histopathological alterations of the ocular surface in a rat model of menopause. Our findings demonstrate that when hormonal depletion is compounded by irregular light–dark cycles, the cornea and conjunctiva undergo marked structural and molecular shifts, characterized by epithelial injury, stromal edema, and a pro-inflammatory environment. While rats do not undergo a ‘menopause’ identical to humans, 12-to-15-month-old female rats enter a state of reproductive senescence often termed ‘estropause.’ This phase is biologically characterized by the same depletion of ovarian follicles and a sharp drop in circulating estradiol and progestin seen in clinical menopause [18]. Our results show that this hormonal decline, much like in humans, leaves ocular tissues more vulnerable to environmental stress.

Menopause is the natural end of women’s reproductive years with depletion of ovarian follicles, which occurs approximately at the age of 51 years. The levels of estrogen and progesterone gradually decline with aging and drop sharply to near-undetectable levels during menopause [1]. The decrease in estrogen concentration causes most of the menopausal symptoms including hot flushes, night sweats, anxiety and depression, sexual dysfunction and insomnia [1–2].

Menopause is frequently associated with sleep disturbances, affecting up to 60% of women during the menopausal transition [1]. These disturbances result from complex interactions among hormonal fluctuations, mood changes, vasomotor symptoms (e.g., hot flashes), and aging-related comorbidities. The decline in estrogen and progesterone is particularly significant, as these hormones regulate sleep architecture and neurotransmitters such as serotonin and gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA). Estrogen deficiency is linked to increased sleep latency and reduced REM sleep, while progesterone’s sedative effects often protect against sleep apnea [1–2]. Consequently, the chronic sleep deprivation common in menopause serves as a primary driver of circadian rhythm disruption [21–22].

Beyond sleep fragmentation, the loss of estrogen directly impairs the master circadian pacemaker. Circadian rhythms are endogenous 24-hour oscillations that allow organisms to maintain homeostasis and anticipate environmental changes. Peripheral tissues relay signals to the suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN) of the anterior hypothalamus, which coordinates these systemic rhythms [22]. Estrogen exerts a significant regulatory influence on the SCN; its withdrawal dampens the amplitude of circadian oscillations, leading to a state of “internal desynchrony” [23]. The influence of estrogens on these rhythms is well-documented; for instance, locomotor activity increases when serum estrogens are elevated during estrus or following estrogen supplementation in ovariectomized models [24–25]. These suggest that estrogens have an impact on the circadian system. It is important to note that our model utilized drospirenone, a synthetic progestin with unique anti-mineralocorticoid properties. While environmental studies in zebrafish have suggested that progestins may interfere with circadian networks [26], the mammalian response appears more complex. In humans, progestins are known to influence sleep architecture and modulate neurotransmitters such as GABA, which may indirectly support circadian stability [1–2]. While our study focuses on how HT improves ocular surface injury caused by irregular light exposure, this therapy also appears to have broader effects on the overall circadian system.

The ocular surface is uniquely vulnerable to this internal desynchrony. Ocular tissues not only express circadian genes but also synthesize melatonin, a key regulator of local rhythms [3]. When the master clock and peripheral oscillators are unable to synchronize, tissues can no longer effectively coordinate daily repair processes and antioxidant defences. This disruption is a known contributor to various ocular disorders, including glaucoma and retinal degeneration, but most frequently manifests as DED [3, 27, 28]. Our findings demonstrate that histopathological damage on ocular surface may occur by these irregular light-dark patterns, likely by disrupting the eye’s internal clock.

In the cornea, animals subjected to the light-cycle shift exhibited marked thickening and stromal edema. These features are consistent with a loss of corneal homeostatic control and barrier instability. The nuclear changes observed, such as chromatin margination, suggest a state of heightened cellular stress and early apoptotic activity. Similarly, the lid conjunctiva displayed parallel pathological changes, including hyperemia and epithelial shedding. These findings are consistent with previous reports demonstrating that constant light exposure or disturbances in the light–dark cycle impair ocular surface physiology by altering tear film composition and delaying epithelial repair. A recent experimental study using a chronic jet-lag mouse model demonstrated that circadian disruption impairs ocular surface homeostasis by increasing corneal epithelial defects and inflammatory cytokine expression, primarily through BMAL1-dependent downregulation of the transmembrane mucin MUC4 [29]. Importantly, the restoration of circadian signaling with melatonin protected the corneal epithelium, highlighting the critical role of the circadian clock in maintaining ocular surface integrity. Notably, while these mice exhibited significant molecular and structural injury, they did not show significant changes in tear volume or goblet cell density, further highlighting the importance of characterizing tissue-level integrity. Our findings align with this paradigm; although we did not evaluate clinical functional parameters such as tear secretion, we observed significant histopathological and inflammatory shifts following a light-cycle shift regimen. This underscores the importance of characterizing cellular and molecular changes in tissue-level integrity as a critical component of ocular surface health, which may occur independently of observable changes in tear volume.

A significant challenge in circadian research is the objective confirmation of misalignment. While systemic behavioral proxies, such as locomotor activity monitoring, were not recorded, the efficacy of our regimen was validated through the immunohistochemical analysis of PER2. The profound downregulation of PER2 expression in Group 2 serves as a validated local molecular indicator of circadian misalignment [17]. This molecular disturbance was directly accompanied by elevated expression of Caspase-3 (Cas-3) and TNF-α. The increase in Cas-3 indicates enhanced apoptotic activity, reflecting a heightened rate of programmed cell death in response to circadian stress. Similarly, the elevated expression of TNF-α signifies an upregulated inflammatory response. These molecular shifts document the pathogenesis of ocular surface injury and suggest that circadian alignment disturbance affects multiple components of the anterior ocular surface by amplifying inflammatory signaling and reducing cellular survival.

This stabilization was evident at the molecular level, as HT successfully reduced corneal edema and conjunctival inflammation while restoring PER2 expression toward control levels. These results suggest that estrogen–progestin therapy provides a protective shield against the cellular stress caused by light-cycle shifts. While some clinical studies have reported a negative correlation between HT and ocular comfort [9–11], others, such as Altıntaş et al., found significant improvements in objective tear parameters [12–13]. These discrepancies in the literature likely reflect differences in therapy timing, hormone formulations, and patient age [14]. Ultimately, our study provides evidence at the tissue level that, in the context of menopausal hormonal decline, HT helps maintain the structural integrity and homeostatic balance of the ocular surface.

Our findings are intended to reflect the real-world situation of menopausal women with disrupted sleep patterns. However, our study has several limitations. First, we report our histological data and did not include functional assays—such as tear break-up time (TBUT), or corneal fluorescein staining—which could have provided additional insights into tear film stability and the extent of ocular surface damage. Instead, our study documents the tissue changes and molecular precursors that likely contribute to the development of ocular surface disorders in clinical settings. Second, this study did not include an estrogen-only group to isolate the specific effects of the hormones. Additionally, we did not monitor systemic adaptation over a longer duration. Despite these limitations, to the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to demonstrate how hormone therapy preserves ocular surface integrity under the combined stress of menopause and a light-dark shift regimen.

In conclusion, a light-cycle shift regimen expected to disturb circadian alignment exacerbates pathological tissue changes and inflammatory signaling on the ocular surface during menopause. Combined estrogen–progestin therapy effectively attenuated these apoptotic and inflammatory shifts, likely by supporting local molecular clock stability and epithelial resilience. While these findings require clinical validation through evaluations of ocular surface homeostasis, they suggest that HT may offer therapeutic benefits for maintaining ocular surface integrity in women with menopause-related rhythm irregularities.
