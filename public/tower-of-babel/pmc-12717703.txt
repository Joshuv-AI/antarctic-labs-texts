Vitamin C enhances cisplatin sensitivity in bladder cancer via 5hmC-mediated epigenetic modulation of ATF4
Authors: Chunru Xu, Wenwei Ying, Yuhui He, Yucai Wu, Tai Tian, Jilong Zhang, Shiming He, Cuijian Zhang, Xuesong Li, Yanqing Gong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Cisplatin resistance remains a major challenge in the clinical treatment of bladder cancer (BC), and the epigenetic regulation of this resistance, particularly involving 5-hydroxymethylcytosine (5hmC), has not been fully elucidated. Here, we investigated the role of 5hmC and vitamin C (VC) in modulating cisplatin sensitivity in BC. Clinical analyses of 36 BC patients receiving cisplatin-based neoadjuvant chemotherapy showed that reduced 5hmC levels in pre-chemotherapy tumor tissues were significantly associated with cisplatin resistance (CR-BC) and poor prognosis, with low 5hmC correlating with shorter progression-free survival (PFS). In vitro, we established two cisplatin-resistant cell lines (T24-CR, UMUC-3-CR) that exhibited reduced 5hmC compared to parental cells. Treatment with 100 μM VC significantly restored 5hmC levels in CR-BC cells by activating TET enzymes, inhibited cell proliferation, and enhanced cisplatin sensitivity; these effects were abrogated by the TET inhibitor Bobcat339, confirming VC acts in a TET-dependent manner. Mechanistically, genome-wide 850 K methylation array and RNA-seq analyses revealed that VC upregulated methylation specifically at the promoter of ATF4, a downstream effector of the MAPK pathway, thereby downregulating ATF4 expression. ATF4 knockdown in CR-BC cells increasing cisplatin sensitivity, while Bobcat339 reversed VC-induced ATF4 downregulation. In vivo, VC combined with cisplatin significantly inhibited tumor growth in T24-CR xenografts, and co-treatment with ATF4 knockdown further enhanced this effect, accompanied by elevated 5hmC and reduced Ki67 in tumors. Collectively, our findings identify reduced 5hmC as a hallmark of cisplatin-resistant BC and reveal a novel mechanism by which VC enhances cisplatin sensitivity. VC activates TET enzymes to increase 5mC at the ATF4 promoter, downregulating ATF4 and modulating the MAPK pathway. This highlights VC as a potential epigenetic adjuvant to overcome cisplatin resistance in BC.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13148-025-02011-x.

Introduction

Bladder cancer, predominantly urothelial carcinoma, has emerged as the second most common urological malignancy worldwide, imposing significant economic and public health burdens annually [1]. The development of muscle-invasive bladder cancer (MIBC) typically indicates a higher risk of recurrence and metastasis [2, 3]. Consequently, current clinical guidelines recommend neoadjuvant cisplatin-based chemotherapy in combination with radical cystectomy as the standard treatment regimen for MIBC to achieve improved tumor control [4]. However, the high prevalence of cisplatin resistance, whether innate or acquired, largely precludes many patients from reaping the benefits of chemotherapy, resulting in accelerated disease progression [5].

As an intermediate product of the DNA demethylation process, 5hmC shows relative stability in certain genomic contexts but remains dynamically regulated by active demethylation [6]. Low levels of 5hmC are significantly associated with the development and unfavorable prognosis of a variety of malignant tumors [7, 8]. Moreover, 5hmC levels have been reported to correlate with chemoresistance in numerous neoplasms, as decreased levels of 5hmC at specific genomic loci can activate or silence the transcriptional start regions of genes related to drug resistance [9–11].

Previously, our research has demonstrated that vitamin C elevates genomic 5hmC levels and reverses the malignant phenotype in BC and kidney tumors, suggesting the potential therapeutic value of vitamin C [12–14]. However, the efficacy of vitamin C in combating cisplatin resistance in BC remains unclear. Therefore, this study aims to investigate the ability of vitamin C to enhance chemosensitivity to cisplatin in BC and to elucidate its underlying regulatory mechanisms.

Reduced 5hmC significantly associates with poor prognosis in cisplatin-resistant bladder cancer

Our previous research has shown that loss of 5hmC is an epigenetic hallmark of BC and that the genomic level of 5hmC plays a crucial role in tumor initiation and progression [12]. However, to further investigate whether 5hmC levels influence cisplatin resistance of BC, we conducted immunohistochemical (IHC) staining and prognostic analyses on pre-chemotherapy tumor specimens from 36 BC patients undergoing cisplatin-based neoadjuvant chemotherapy, along with 9 unpaired normal urothelium tissues as a control group. Clinical baseline characteristics of included patients

After histochemical scoring of each sample, we observed a significant decrease in overall 5hmC modification levels in BC tissues compared to normal urothelium (t = 4.596, p < 0.001, Fig. 1a-b). Using the median score as the cutoff (H score = 100), we performed correlation survival analyses of BC tissues with low-5hmC levels (5hmC-low) and high-5hmC levels (5hmC-high), respectively. Notably, 5hmC levels showed no significant association with age, sex, Tumor stage, or tumor grade (p > 0.05 for all). However, low 5hmC expression was strongly correlated with chemosensitivity in the neoadjuvant therapy cohort (p < 0.01, Table 1). The results indicate that patients with BC presenting high-5hmC levels have significantly better overall survival (OS) and progression-free survival (PFS) compared to those with low-5hmC levels (Fig. 1c and d). Multifactorial regression analysis revealed that high-5hmC levels are an independent predictor of better PFS (Fig. 1 e and f), although they are not significantly associated with OS (Supplementary Fig. 1a-b). To validate these findings, we analyzed TET family gene expression (TET1/2/3) in the TCGA-BLCA cohort, as TET enzymes catalyze 5mC-to-5hmC conversion and their expression correlates with 5hmC abundance. Consistent with our clinical data, TET gene expression progressively decreased with advanced TNM stage and higher tumor grade (Supplementary Fig. 1c), further supporting the link between 5hmC loss and BC aggressiveness.

We further classified the patients into two groups—cisplatin-resistant BC (CR-BC) and cisplatin-sensitive BC (CS-BC)—based on imaging results, where radiological tumor shrinkage after neoadjuvant therapy was defined as cisplatin sensitivity. The results demonstrated a significantly decreased 5hmC levels in the CR-BC group compared to the CS-BC group (t = 6.119, p < 0.001, Fig. 1g and h). Using a concentration-escalation induction method, we successfully established two CR-BC cell lines, T24-CR (IC50: 4.1 μg/mL vs. parental 3.3 μg/mL) and UMUC-3-CR (IC50: 3.7 μg/mL vs. parental 2.9 μg/mL), which exhibited stable survival in cisplatin -containing environments (T24-CR: 1.5 mg/mL; UMUC-3-CR: 0.5 mg/mL, Fig. 2a-c). These CR-BC cell lines showed significant up-regulation of drug resistance-related gene expression (ERCC1, MRP1, P-gp, STAT3) compared to the parent BC (P-BC) cells (Fig. 2d). DNA Dot blot showed that CR-BC cells had significantly lower levels of 5hmC modification compared to its parent cells (Fig. 2e-f). Furthermore, notable differences in the expression of known 5hmC-regulated genes (TET2/3, IDH1/2, L2HGDH) and vitamin C channel-associated genes (SVCT1/2) were observed between the CR-BC and their parent cell lines (Fig. 2g–i). Specifically, the significant downregulation of TET2 and TET3 directly reduced 5hmC levels; IDH2 inhibited TET activity by decreasing α-ketoglutarate (α-KG) production; L2HGDH significantly upregulated to indirectly inhibit TET activity; and SVCT1 and SVCT2 (sodium-dependent vitamin C transporters) showed a compensatory increase to promote vitamin C uptake. Our preliminary results suggest that BC cells that have acquired cisplatin resistance exhibit significantly reduced levels of 5hmC modification. Vitamin C enhances 5hmC and promotes sensitivity to cisplatin in bladder cancer cells

Vitamin C (ascorbate, VC), though not a direct cofactor, plays a crucial role in the activation of Fe(II)-2-oxoglutarate dioxygenase enzymes, including the TET family of enzymes, can significantly enhance TET enzyme activity, thereby promoting the rapid synthesis and accumulation of intracellular global 5hmC [15, 16]. Cell viability assay assay identified 100 µM vitamin C as the optimal concentration for subsequent experiments, as it maintained > 90% cell viability (Fig. 3a). Upon the application of vitamin C to cisplatin-resistant cells, we observed a significant elevation of 5hmC levels in CR-BC cells (Fig. 3b). Additionally, vitamin C induced a substantial increase in the expression of 5hmC-related enzymes (including TET enzymes and IDH2) and a further increase in the expression of the SVCT1 transporter in CR-BC cells, though it had no regulatory effect on L2HGDH (Fig. 3c-d). Furthermore, the proliferation capacity of cisplatin-resistant bladder cancer cells was significantly inhibited in the presence of vitamin C in T24-CR (IC50: 4.0 μg/mL vs. control 3.4 μg/mL) and UMUC-3-CR (IC50: 3.9 μg/mL vs. control 3.2 μg/mL) cells (Fig. 3e-g).Fig. 3Vitamin C enhances 5hmC and promotes sensitivity to cisplatin in bladder cancer cells. a Optimal concentration of vitamin C for T24-CR and UMUC-3-CR cells treated. b DNA Dot blot analysis of 5hmC modification levels in T24-CR and UMUC-3-CR cells after vitamin C treatment. c Western blot analysis of 5hmC-regulated genes (TET2/3, IDH1/2, L2HGDH) and vitamin C channel-associated genes (SVCT1/2) expression levels in T24-CR and UMUC-3-CR cells following vitamin C treatment. d Quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) analysis of 5hmC-regulated genes (TET2/3, IDH1/2, L2HGDH) and vitamin C channel-associated genes (SVCT1/2) expression levels in T24-CR and UMUC-3-CR cells after vitamin C treatment. e and f IC50 values (e) and growth curves (f) of cisplatin in T24-CR and UMUC-3-CR cells after vitamin C treatment (100 uM). g Clonogenic formation assays to assess the impact of vitamin C (100uM) on clonogenic potential in T24-CR and UMUC-3-CR cells. Tumor specimens were collected for measurement and IHC analysis after the model construction and eight weeks of drug treatments (Fig. 5a). The results indicated that neither cisplatin nor vitamin C alone had a significantly impact on tumor proliferation, while the combination treatment of vitamin C and cisplatin led to a substantial reduction in tumor size and weight (Fig. 5b). Furthermore, the expression of the cell proliferation marker Ki67 was markedly reduced under the combined treatment of vitamin C and cisplatin (Fig. 5d). Vitamin C significantly up-regulates 5hmC modifications in the MAPK signaling pathway

To investigate the potential mechanisms by which vitamin C regulates cisplatin sensitivity in bladder cancer cells, we analyzed the genomic methylation data of two groups of CR-BC cells, with and without vitamin C intervention, using the Infinium MethylationEPIC Beadchip (850 K DNA Methylation Microarrays). The screening criteria for differential methylation positions (DMPs) were set as |Δβ|> 0.1 and adjusted P-value (adj.P) < 0.05. Compared with the control group, the transcription start sites (TSS) in the vitamin C treatment group exhibited hypermethylation (Fig. 6a), with this hypermethylation pattern being more pronounced in T24-CR cells. Meanwhile, we employed RNA-seq to analyze gene expressions profiles of T24-CR cells (with 3 independent biological replicates per group) treated with or without 100 μM vitamin C. A total of 489 differentially expressed genes were identified after vitamin C intervention (fold change > 2, adjusted p-value < 0.05; see Supplementary material for full list) (Fig. 6c). Among these, 157 genes were upregulated, and 332 genes were downregulated (Fig. 6c). Subsequently, we performed a correlation analysis based on the 850 K microarray data and RNA-seq gene expression data, and generated a scatter plot showing the overall differential correlation distribution between methylation sites and genes, with a Pearson correlation coefficient of 0.01074 (Fig. 6d). Furthermore, we conducted gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) combined with Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis to identify signaling pathways enriched with the differential expressed genes (Fig. 6e). The results showed that the MAPK signaling pathway, focal adhesion and proteoglycan in cancer were significantly altered in T24-CR cells after vitamin C treatment (Fig. 6e). The MAPK signaling pathway, a well-known regulator of cellular stress and proliferation, has been extensively reported to be significantly associated with cisplatin resistance in various tumors [17, 18].

Following vitamin C treatment, the promoter region of ATF4, a downstream regulatory factor of the MAPK pathway, showed a significant increase in methylation levels at specific CpG sites (Fig. 6f). Further analysis revealed that the protein expression level of ATF4 was significantly downregulated in CR-BC cells after vitamin C treatment (Fig. 6g). Notably, we also observed a reduction in ATF4 mRNA expression (Fig. 6h), which indicates that DNA methylation in the promoter region can decrease ATF4 expression. Meanwhile, our results showed that Vitamin C significantly downregulated the expression of MAPK pathway associated proteins, such as P38 and ERK (p42/44), while upregulating JNK in T24-CR cells (Fig. 6i). These findings suggest that vitamin C may regulate cisplatin sensitivity in bladder cancer cells by modulating the expression and activity of ATF4 within the MAPK signaling pathway (Fig. 6j).

We then performed methylated DNA immunoprecipitation-qPCR (MeDIP-qPCR) to assess the alterations in 5mC and 5hmC levels within the ATF4 gene following the addition of vitamin C to CR-BC cells. The results indicated that the 5mC modification level of the ATF4 gene in CR-BC cells was significantly increased post vitamin C treatment (Fig. 6k), while the 5hmC modification level was significantly decreased (Fig. 6l). The above findings suggest that vitamin C can significantly enhance the 5mC level of ATF4, a representative gene in the MAPK signaling pathway, while concurrently reducing ATF4 expression in CR-BC cells (Fig. 6j).

Vitamin C reduces ATF4 expression and increases the sensitivity of cisplatin-resistant bladder cancer cells to cisplatin

ATF4 is a stress-regulated gene involved in numerous biological functions, including antioxidant response, apoptosis and autophagy [19]. Elevated expression of ATF4 has been associated with cisplatin resistance in previous studies [20, 21]. Consequently, our findings reveal a significant elevation of ATF4 levels in CR-BC cells compared to the P-BC cells (Fig. 7a-b). Vitamin C treatment effectively reduced ATF4 expression in CR-BC cells, while subsequent treatment with Bobcat339 markedly reversed this vitamin C-induced downregulation of ATF4 (Fig. 7c-d). These suggest that high expression of the ATF4 gene may be closely correlated with the development of cisplatin resistance in bladder cancer. Furthermore, IC50 values and growth curve revealed that the sensitivity of CR-BC cells to cisplatin was significantly increased following ATF4 knockdown in T24-CR (IC50: 4.5 μg/mL vs. shSCR 3.3 μg/mL) and UMUC-3-CR (IC50: 3.8 μg/mL vs. shSCR 2.9 μg/mL) cells (Fig. 7e-g, Supplementary Fig. 3a-b). Discussion

Cisplatin resistance remains a significant challenge in the comprehensive treatment of bladder cancer, contributing to the poor prognosis of patients. In this study, we reported for the first time that vitamin C (L-ascorbate) may serve as a potentially viable agent to ameliorate cisplatin resistance in bladder cancer. Vitamin C can broadly affect 5hmC and 5mC levels and gene expression across the genome, our data specifically implicate ATF4 as a key player in cisplatin resistance, as evidenced by the significant increase in methylation at its promoter region and the corresponding reduction in its expression and protein levels. However, recent studies suggest that 5hmC may exert context-dependent effects on transcription, particularly when stabilized at promoter regions through TET enzyme activity or interactions with transcriptional repressors [22, 23]. We acknowledge that other genes may also contribute to this phenomenon, and further genome-wide studies are needed to identify additional targets.

As a well-known oxidation, vitamin C has been demonstrated to elevate 5hmC modifications within the cellular genome, a feature closely linked to the suppression of tumor stemness and malignant phenotypes in urological tumors [24]. This effect is likely mediated through its role as a cofactor for TET enzymes, which catalyze 5mC-to-5hmC conversion. Vitamin C enhances TET activity by maintaining iron (Fe2+) in its reduced state, a critical cofactor for TET dioxygenase function [25]. Our data extend this understanding by showing that vitamin C’s chemosensitizing effects in bladder cancer correlate with TET2 upregulation, suggesting a TET-dependent mechanism for ATF4 repression. Future studies should directly interrogate TET2’s involvement via knockdown/rescue experiments. However, our study is the first to confirm the role of vitamin C in enhancing cisplatin sensitivity in bladder cancer. It is important to note that 5hmC is an intermediate in active DNA demethylation, and in promoter and enhancer regions, this process could activate gene expression for multiple genes. This mechanism may explain the broader effects of vitamin C on gene regulation beyond ATF4, and future studies should explore this aspect in greater detail.

In the follow-up of a clinical cohort of bladder cancer patients, we found that individuals with decreased levels of 5hmC in their tumor tissues exhibited a significantly worse prognosis, which is consistent with previous reports [26, 27]. Additionally, we observed that tissues from patients showing a significant cisplatin-resistant phenotype had markedly lower levels of 5hmC. This finding suggests that bladder cancer may possess a chemoresistance regulatory mechanism similar to those observed in hepatocellular carcinoma and promyelocytic lymphoma, and elevated 5hmC modification levels could enhance cellular sensitivity to cisplatin [10, 28]. However, 5hmC modifications are widespread across various genomic structures, and further investigation is required to identify the specific genes and regulatory regions influenced by these modifications, particularly those with differential expression patterns associated with cisplatin resistance.

To address the above questions regarding the role of 5hmC in cisplatin resistance, we established two cisplatin-resistant BC (CR-BC) cell lines, T24-CR and UMUC-3-CR, with stable cisplatin resistance phenotype through induced resistance methods. The elevated expression levels of genes notably associated with cisplatin resistance, such as ERCC1, MRP1 and P-gp, confirmed that the cisplatin resistance in the selected cell lines was representative [29–31]. Comparative analyses between cisplatin-resistant and parent cells lines revealed significant alterations in enzymes and genes involved in 5hmC regulation, accompanied by a notable decrease in the level of 5hmC modification [32–34].

Activating Transcription Factor 4 (ATF4), a member of the CREB/ATF family of transcription factors, plays a crucial role in the cellular stress response [35]. ATF4 regulates the expression of numerous genes involved in the unfolded protein response (UPR), amino acid metabolism, and apoptosis [36]. In the context of cancer, ATF4 has been implicated in the development of chemoresistance, particularly in bladder cancer. Dysregulation of ATF4 can enhance cancer cells’ resistance to chemotherapeutic agents by promoting cell survival and suppressing apoptosis [35]. Our findings suggest that the 5hmC modification of the ATF4 gene is a critical regulatory mechanism in the development of cisplatin resistance in BC, providing a novel therapeutic target for improving treatment outcomes.

However, our study does have some limitations. While we have demonstrated that vitamin C, in combination with cisplatin, enhances chemosensitivity in CR-BC cells, this does not rule out the involvement of molecular pathways beyond epigenetics. The observed effects could potentially be mediated by other mechanisms, such as oxidative stress modulation, DNA repair pathways, or metabolic reprogramming, which warrant further investigation. Additionally, the in vivo efficacy of vitamin C in reversing cisplatin resistance needs to be confirmed through animal models. Future studies will focus on these aspects to better understand the molecular mechanisms and to validate the potential clinical applications of vitamin C in enhancing cisplatin sensitivity in bladder cancer.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that vitamin C can significantly enhance chemosensitivity of bladder cancer cells to cisplatin in by increasing 5hmC levels, including the modulation of 5hmC on ATF4, thereby indicating its potential as an adjuvant therapy to overcome cisplatin resistance.

Materials and methods

Tissue samples and cell lines

Bladder tumor tissue specimens were obtained from 36 muscle-invasive bladder cancer (MIBC) patients who underwent transurethral resection of bladder tumor (TURBT) at Peking University First Hospital, along with 9 normal urothelial tissue samples for comparative analysis. All enrolled patients with muscle-invasive bladder cancer (MIBC) received neoadjuvant chemotherapy consisting of 1–4 cycles of cisplatin combined with gemcitabine and underwent regular imaging assessments (CT/MRI) to evaluate the effectiveness of their treatment. Based on the imaging results, patients were stratified into two groups: cisplatin-resistant group defined as radiographic disease progression (≥ 20% increase in target lesions) or new lesions during/after chemotherapy (CR-BC, n = 15); and a cisplatin-sensitive group defined as stable disease, partial response (≥ 30% reduction in lesions), or complete response on imaging (CS-BC, n = 21). The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of Peking University First Hospital (Approval No. 2023Y407-003). Human bladder cancer cell lines (T24, UMUC-3, supplement Table 3) were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, USA). The cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium. Cisplatin-resistant cell lines were maintained in culture medium containing cisplatin at concentration of 1.5 mg/mL (T24-CR) and 0.5 mg/mL (UMUC-3-CR). All culture media were supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) and H-score assessment

IHC was performed over two days according to the immunohistochemistry kit protocol (PV-9000, ZSGB-Bio, China). For antigen retrieval, tissue sections were heated in citrate buffer (pH 9.0) at 95 °C for 20 min. The 5hmC antibody (1:200, 39769, Active motif, USA) was used as the primary antibody. The level of 5hmC was quantified using IHC. At least three selected areas were evaluated by two independent pathologists using ImageJ software (NIH, USA) to measure staining intensity and positive cell density. The final H-score was calculated based on the intensity and density of staining (staining intensity × percentage of positive cells), and the average H-score was determined.

In vitro cell proliferation and cisplatin toxicity assay

Bladder cancer cell lines, cultured to > 90% viability, were inoculated into 96-well plates at a density of 5000 cells per well. The serial cisplatin dilutions (0.1–100 μg/mL) and gradient vitamin C concentrations (0.01–10 mM) were added to the specified wells and cultured for 3 days. 10 µL of CCK-8 (HY-K0301, MCE, USA) solution was added to each well and incubated at 37 °C for 2 h. The absorbance of each sample was then measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader (Biotek, USA). The IC50 values were calculated using GraphPad Prism 9.0 (GraphPad Software, USA).

Colony formation assay

Colony formation assays were conducted as described in our previous study. Briefly, bladder cancer cells were seeded into cell culture plates at a density of 1,000 cells per plate. After a 7 days incubation period, the cells were washed with phosphate buffer solution (PBS) and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde. Subsequently, the cells were stained with crystal violet and the number of colonies were quantified.

RNA Sequencing and 850 K DNA methylation microarray

For the Illumina Infinium MethylationEPIC BeadChip (850 K DNA methylation microarray) analysis, T24-CR and UMUC3-CR cells (with 3 biological replicates per group) were treated with or without 100 μM Vitamin C for 48 h; genomic DNA from these cell samples was extracted and purified using the Qiagen DNeasy Blood and Tissue Kit (Cat. No.: 69504), followed by DNA quantification and purity assessment via the Qubit®3.0 Fluorometer (Qubit dsDNA HS Assay Kit, Cat. No.: Q32854) and NanoDrop One spectrophotometer, respectively, while DNA integrity (to exclude degradation, protein, and RNA contamination) was verified by gel electrophoresis; subsequently, bisulfite conversion was performed using the EZ DNA Methylation Kits (Cat. No.: D5001/D5003) with an overnight incubation for 16 h, which converts unmethylated cytosines (C) to uracils (U) while retaining methylated cytosines (5mC) to lay the foundation for subsequent microarray detection; after conversion, the DNA was subjected to amplification, fragmentation, precipitation, and resuspension, then hybridized with the 850 K microarray, followed by extension, washing, and staining procedures, and finally, the microarray was scanned using the Illumina iScan System (Model: SY-101-1001) to obtain raw idat files; for data normalization and batch correction, the Beta-Mixture Quantile Normalization (BMIQ) algorithm was applied to standardize the raw β values (an indicator of methylation levels); batch effects were identified through Singular Value Decomposition (SVD) analysis, and the Combat algorithm was used for batch correction, with SVD re-performed after correction to verify the elimination of batch effects; for the analysis of differentially methylated positions (DMPs), the screening thresholds for differences were set as |Δβ|> 0.1 and adjusted P-value (adj.P) < 0.05, where the Benjamini & Hochberg method was used for P-value correction to control the false discovery rate (FDR). For RNA sequencing (RNA-Seq) analysis, T24-CR cells (with 3 biological replicates per group) were treated with or without 100 μM Vitamin C for 48 h; total RNA was extracted from the treated cells, and then messenger RNA (mRNA) was enriched using oligo(dT) magnetic beads, followed by chemical fragmentation, complementary DNA (cDNA) synthesis, and modification for library construction; sequencing was conducted on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform in the paired-end 150 (PE150) mode; for bioinformatics analysis, FastQC was first used to evaluate the quality of raw fastq files, and then fastp was employed to filter low-quality reads to retain clean reads; subsequently, Hisat2 software was used to align the clean reads to the human GRCh38.102 genome, and the alignment rate, functional region distribution, and sequencing saturation were evaluated; next, Stringtie software was applied to count the fragments of genes/transcripts, which were normalized by the Trimmed Mean of M-values (TMM) method and quantified as Fragments Per Kilobase of transcript per Million mapped reads (FPKM), with functional annotation performed using the Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) databases; thereafter, edgeR software based on the negative binomial distribution model was used to calculate differential expression, and differentially expressed genes/transcripts were screened using two criteria: the primary criterion of Q-value < 0.05 and fold change (FC) ≥ 2 or ≤ 0.5, and the supplementary criterion of FC ≥ 1.5 and P-value < 0.05, with volcano plots generated using ggplot2 for visualizing the results. For the integrated analysis, a scatter plot was constructed to show the overall differential correlation distribution between methylation sites and genes, where the X-axis represents the mean difference (Δβ) of β values of methylation sites between the two groups of samples, the Y-axis represents the log2-transformed fold change (log2FC) of gene expression levels between the two groups of samples, and the color of each scatter is distinguished by the default thresholds of |log2FC|> 1 and |Δβ|> 0.2; additionally, the Pearson correlation coefficient and its significance P-value were calculated and reported.

shRNA, PCR, and quantitative real-time PCR

The procedure for this part was adapted from previous reports [12]. Specific genes in resistant cells were knocked down using lentiviruses harboring the appropriate shRNA. Cells were transduced with lentiviral particles (MOI = 10) followed by puromycin selection (2 μg/mL, 72 h) to establish stable knockdown cell lines. The shRNA sequence of scramble is as follows:

CCGGTTCTCCGAACGTGTCACGTTTCAAGAGAACGTGACACGTTCGGAGAATTTTTG (Forward)

and

AATTCAAAAATTCTCCGAACGTGTCACGTTCTCTTGAAACGTGACACGTTCGGAGAA (Reverse)

The shRNA sequence of ATF4 is as follows:

CCGGCCACTCCAGATCATTCCTTTACTCGAGTAAAGGAATGATCTGGAGTGGTTTTTG (Forward)

and

AATTCAAAAACCACTCCAGATCATTCCTTTCTCGAGTAAAGGAATGATCTGGAGTGG (Reverse). To validate ATF4 knockdown efficiency, total RNA was extracted from cells using the RNA Easy Fast Kit (DP451, TIANGEN, China). The concentration of the extracted RNA was quantified using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer. Reverse transcription was then performed using the FastKing gDNA Dispelling RT SuperMix (KR118, TIANGEN,China) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Quantitative PCR (qPCR) was conducted using SYBR® FAST qPCR Kits (KK4600, KAPA Biosystems, USA) in a final reaction volume of 20 μL on the 7500 Fast Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, USA). Primer information is provided in Supplementary Table 1. Glyceraldehyde-3-phosphatedehydrogenase (GAPDH) was selected as the endogenous reference control, and and the expression levels of target mRNAs were standardized using the ΔΔCq method.

MeDIP-qPCR

For each immunoprecipitation (IP) reaction, 2 μg of fragmented DNA (200–1000 bp) was used. The DNA was then fragmented into 200-1000 bp segments using sonication. The enriched DNA fragments were subjected to qPCR analysis. MeDIP-qPCR for 5mC and 5hmC quantification was performed using the EpiQuik Hydroxymethylated DNA Immunoprecipitation (hMeDIP) Kit (P-1038, Epigentek, USA). Quantitative analyses were conducted after sample preparation, following the qPCR procedure as specified by the manufacturer. Enrichment of the target DNA fragment was calculated as %input.

Western blotting

Cells were first lysed thoroughly using NP-40 and ultrasonic shaking. The protein concentration was quantified to 1ug/uL using the Enhanced BCA Protein Assay Kit (P0009, Beyotime, China). A total of 40 μg protein per sample was loaded into each well for SDS-PAGE. Following electrophoresis, the sample proteins were transferred to PVDF membranes. TET, IDH and SVCT related antibodies (Supplementary Table 2) were used as primary antibodies and Rabbit IgG HRP or DyLight™ 680 -conjugated secondary antibodies were employed for detection (Supplementary Table 2). Protein bands were visualized using the G:BOX Chemi XRQ Imaging System (Syngene, UK) with the ECL chemiluminescence kit (WBKLS0100, Merck Millipore, Germany) or Odyssey System (LI-COR, USA) was used for exposure. Quantitative measurements were further processed and validated with Fiji (ImageJ 2.0, NIH, USA).

DNA dot blot

Genomic DNA was extracted using the TIANamp Genomic DNA Kit (CDP304, TIANGEN, China). DNA samples were denatured at 95 °C for 10 min, then immediately chilled on ice for 5 min before loading onto a pre-wetted nylon membrane (Amersham Hybond-N + , GE Healthcare) using a dot blot apparatus. After sample application, DNA was cross-linked to the membrane by UV irradiation (254 nm, 120 mJ/cm2) using a Stratalinker 2400 (Stratagene). The membrane was then blocked with 5% non-fat milk and incubated sequentially with 5- Hydroxymethylcytosine primary antibody (1:1000, Active Motif, Cat# 39,769) and HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (1:5000, Santa Cruz, Cat# Sc-2030). For detection, the membrane was developed with ECL chemiluminescence reagent (WBKLS0100, Merck Millipore) and imaged using the G:BOX Chemi XRQ system (Syngene, UK). Membrane staining with 0.02% methylene blue was performed to verify equal DNA loading and transfer efficiency.

Tumor xenograft model

Cells (1 × 10^7 cells) suspended in PBS were mixed 1:1 with Matrigel (356,234, Corning, German) and then injected subcutaneously into the left flank of 4–6 weeks old male BALB/c nude mice obtained from Beijing Vitalriver Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). Tumor volume was measured every 4 days using calipers. Drugs administration was performed via intraperitoneal injection, cisplatin was administered at a dose of 5 mg/kg once a week, and vitamin C was administered at a dose of 4 g/kg daily. After 6–8 weeks, mice were euthanized using CO2, and tumor tissues were collected and measured. All animal experiments were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of the Peking University First Hospital (Approval No. J2024002).

Statistics analysis

Unless otherwise specified, quantitative data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) from technical repeated-well measurements, and comparison between groups were performed using unpaired t tests. Categorical variables were analyzed using chi-square tests. Kaplan–Meier curves, constructed using the log-rank test, and Cox proportional hazards regression models were employed for survival analyses to determine hazard ratios (HR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI). For all statistical results, a two-sided p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Data analysis and visualization were conducted using the R software (version 4.1.2; R Core Team, Austria, RRID: SCR_001905) and Graphpad Prism 9.0 (RRID: SCR_002798).
