Dual Activity Microbial Peptides Catalog
Authors: Özlem Sağıroğlu, Muzaffer Arıkan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Antibiotic resistance and cancer represent two of the most pressing global health challenges. In recent years, peptides have emerged as a promising molecular class to address these issues, with antimicrobial peptides (AMPs) in particular attracting attention as alternatives to conventional antibiotics. Notably, some AMPs have also been found to exhibit anticancer peptide (ACP) properties, thus possessing dual activity. Despite this potential, ACPs remain underrepresented in current databases compared to AMPs, largely due to the under-exploration of microbial sources. In this study, we adopted a consensus-based strategy using three ACP prediction tools and two AMP prediction tools to identify candidate peptides with dual activity. Using these tools, we screened a total of 124,051,427 microbial peptides from publicly available small open reading frame (smORF) catalogues and identified 40,251 non-toxic peptides that display dual activity. These were compiled into Dual Activity Microbial Peptides Catalog (DAMPC), a curated and searchable resource of dual-activity, non-toxic microbial peptide sequences.

Background & Summary

Cancer and antibiotic resistance are among the most urgent global health challenges. Cancer is still a leading cause of mortality, with cancer-related deaths projected to reach 11.5 million by 20301,2. At the same time, antibiotic resistance poses a serious worldwide threat to human health3. Conventional drugs are increasingly ineffective against the ESKAPE pathogens (Enterococcus faecium, Staphylococcus aureus, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Acinetobacter baumannii, Pseudomonas aeruginosa and Enterobacter spp.) which are highly prone to developing resistance. Recognizing this threat, the World Health Organization has emphasized the urgent need for novel antimicrobial molecules4,5.

Despite its prevalence and lethality, no completely effective treatment for cancer has yet been established6. Conventional approaches such as chemotherapy and radiotherapy can be ineffective in certain cases and frequently harm healthy cells alongside cancer cells, resulting in severe toxic side effects7,8. Therefore, there is a pressing need for innovative treatment strategies that selectively target tumor cells, minimize toxicity in healthy tissues, and reduce the likelihood of resistance development1,7.

Therapeutic peptides have emerged as promising candidates in the search for novel therapies against both antibiotic resistance and cancer. Their high selectivity, tunable physicochemical properties, and low toxicity make them attractive alternatives9. Among these, antimicrobial peptides (AMPs) are short sequences (typically 5–50 residues) produced by all living organisms as part of their innate defense against bacteria, fungi, and viruses5,10. AMPs are generally cationic, amphipathic, and hydrophobic, properties that enable them to interact with and disrupt microbial membranes5,10–12.

Similarly, anticancer peptides (ACPs) are short peptides that selectively induce cytotoxicity in cancer cells13. Like AMPs, ACPs are typically cationic and amphipathic, allowing preferential interaction with negatively charged cancer cell membranes. While not all AMPs possess anticancer properties, a subset displays dual antimicrobial-anticancer activity, exerting potent effects on both bacteria and tumor cells while sparing normal mammalian cells9,11,14. This dual activity arises from shared structural and physicochemical determinants that govern interactions with distinct cell types5. Initially studied for their antimicrobial effects, AMPs are now recognized as multifunctional bioactive molecules with antimicrobial, anticancer, and antiviral potential8.

Structurally, AMPs are extremely diverse. Commonly, they can be divided into two classes: α-helical peptides such as cecropins and magainins, and cysteine-rich β-sheet peptides such as defensins10,11. Magainin-like AMPs primarily disrupt bacterial membranes through toroidal pores or carpet-like mechanisms. Defensin-like AMPs, on the other hand, exhibit antimicrobial activity by increasing membrane permeability following a different strategy11.

Similarly, ACPs utilize similar membrane targeting principles but achieve selectivity by recognizing the distinct characteristics of cancer cell membranes11. One of the most well-known ACPs is lactoferricin. It is a peptide derived from the proteolytic cleavage of lactoferrin, and it adopts amphipathic β-sheet or cyclic structures and induces selective cancer cell lysis. Another important ACP example is human cathelicidin LL-37. It destabilizes cancer cell membranes via toroidal pore formation while exhibiting limited toxicity toward normal cells10.

These well-characterized examples provide biological context and mechanistic rationale for their therapeutic potential in both antimicrobial and anticancer applications.

Several specialized databases have been developed to compile peptides with experimentally validated antimicrobial or anticancer activity. AMP databases include dbAMP315, DRAMP416, CAMPR417, DBAASP v318, APD619, whereas DCTPep20 and CancerPPD221 serve as key ACP resources. These resources provide structured collections of experimentally validated peptides with curated annotations and activity data. However, they remain unevenly populated, as microbial-source ACPs are markedly underrepresented, and microbiomes remain insufficiently mined for both AMP and ACP discovery. Recent computational frameworks such as Macrel22 and MetaPepticon23, together with microbiome-oriented AMP databases like AMPSphere24,25, have advanced large-scale peptide discovery from microbial datasets. Nevertheless, no dedicated resource currently focuses on microbial peptides with dual antimicrobial-anticancer activity. Experimental identification of such peptides remains slow and resource-intensive; therefore, computational prediction frameworks are essential for large-scale screening and prioritization26.

In this study, we developed DAMPC (Dual Activity Microbial Peptides Catalog) using a consensus-based computational strategy integrating five prediction tools: Amp-Scanner-v227 and Macrel22 for antimicrobial peptides, and AntiCP228, ACPred-BMF29, and Con_ACP30 for anticancer peptides. Following initial filtering, 4,072,884 peptides from DBsmORF31,32 and 119,978,543 peptides from the Global Microbial smORFs Catalogue v1.033,34 were screened. Peptides consistently predicted as both AMPs and ACPs across all five tools were retained, and subsequent toxicity analysis identified 40,251 non-toxic peptides that constitute the DAMPC catalog. Many of these peptides are absent from current ACP databases, highlighting the novelty of the dataset. DAMPC provides a curated and reproducible resource of microbial peptides with computationally predicted dual antimicrobial-anticancer potential. Therefore, it serves as a hypothesis-generating resource, designed to narrow the candidate search space and produce a prioritized set of peptides to guide and support future experimental validation.

Selection of prediction tools

In order to identify prediction tools for both AMPs and ACPs, an extensive literature review was performed on PubMed. For ACPs, the search term “anticancer peptide AND prediction AND (model OR tool OR method)” was used, covering the period 2015–2025 and yielding 758 articles. For AMPs, the search term “antimicrobial peptide AND prediction AND (model OR tool OR method)” was applied with the same time range, resulting in 1227 articles. From the pool of retrieved articles, studies were evaluated according to six predefined criteria: (i) availability of accessible source code, (ii) provision of a standalone version, (iii) clear installation and usage instructions, (iv) applicability beyond tissue- or source-specific contexts, (v) ability to handle large-scale datasets, and (vi) usability as a prediction tool that can run on standard CPU infrastructure. Only studies that fulfilled these requirements were retained for further consideration. Following the elimination process, three ACP prediction tools were retained: AntiCP228, ACPred-BMF29, and Con_ACP30 along with two AMP prediction tools: Amp-Scanner-v227 and Macrel22.

The DBsmORF catalog31,32 and the Global Microbial smORFs Catalogue v1.033,34 were selected for analysis due to their large data content and public availability. The peptide sequences used in this study were obtained from these publicly accessible resources and processed through the filtering and prediction pipeline described below. From the GMSC catalog, we downloaded and utilized the 100AA list. Since the initial methionine residue is generally not retained at the N-terminus of mature proteins, the Macrel tool automatically removes this residue from sequences during the prediction phase. To ensure consistency, the N-terminal methionine was also removed from sequences using SeqKit so that the cataloged peptides could be analyzed consistently across all prediction tools.

ACP and AMP Predictions

With Amp-Scanner-v2, the original paper model was employed. For ACPred-BMF, the alternative mode was selected, as it utilizes ACPs and random peptides as positive and negative samples, respectively. In the case of AntiCP2, default parameters were applied, except for the display setting, where the option to report all peptides in the result file was enabled. For Con_ACP, the ACP-Mixed-80 dataset was used, as recommended by the developers.

Following the filtration of peptide catalogs, all five tools were employed for screening, and peptides that were consistently predicted as both AMPs and ACPs across all tools were selected for the subsequent step. ToxinPred336 was employed as a sequence-based toxicity screening tool to exclude potentially harmful peptides, following common practice in in silico peptide discovery pipelines. Similar usage has been reported in previous computational studies focused on antimicrobial and anticancer peptide prioritization37–39. ToxinPred3 was applied with default model (Hybrid approach, which is the ensemble of Extra tree + MERCI) and a threshold value of 0.5 to perform toxicity analysis. Following this evaluation, the non-toxic peptides were compiled into the Dual Activity Microbial Peptides Catalog (DAMPC).

Statistical analysis

The chi-square test (prop.test) was applied to assess amino acid frequency differences among the three data sets (DAMPC, dbAMP3, and CancerPPD2). For each amino acid, the ratios between the two groups were compared, and false discovery rate (FDR) correction was applied for multiple testing using the Benjamini–Hochberg (BH) method. The significance threshold was set at p_adj_BH < 0.05. Furthermore, a frequency difference (diff_hat) of at least 2–3% (0.02–0.03) was considered biologically significant.

To evaluate the performance of the prediction tools and to assess whether the consensus approach reduces the false positive rate, model outputs for each prediction tool and for the consensus approach were binarized using tool-specific decision thresholds and compared against ground-truth class labels (positive vs. negative). To assess the effect of combining multiple predictors, a consensus prediction was defined using a stringent logical AND criterion, whereby a peptide was classified as positive only if all evaluated tools simultaneously produced a positive prediction. All performance metrics were computed using the same framework for individual tools and the consensus approach to ensure direct comparability across tools and negative dataset constructions.

The complete DAMPC datasets are also publicly available on Zenodo40. The datasets include two files: DAMPC.fasta, containing the peptide sequences described in this study in FASTA format, and DAMPC_metadata.csv, which provides the corresponding metadata in CSV format. The FASTA file contains peptide identifiers and their associated sequences. All sequences consist exclusively of natural amino acids and range from 10 to 50 amino acids in length. The metadata file lists, for each peptide, the following information: Peptide_Id, Peptide_Sequence, Habitat, Taxonomic_Assignment, ConACP_Score, AntiCP2_Score, AMPScanner_Score, Macrel_Score, ToxinPred3_Score, Experimental_Validation, and Source. The metadata further includes information on the peptide’s habitat, taxonomic assignment, prediction scores obtained from prediction tools, whether experimental validation is available, and the source publication from which the peptide was derived.

Technical Validation

To assess the novelty and biophysical characteristics of the predicted dual-activity peptides, we compared the DAMPC catalog with existing antimicrobial and anticancer peptide databases. Duplicate entries were removed from all databases prior to comparison. None of the DAMPC peptides were found in the dbAMP3 or CancerPPD2. Allowing a single amino acid mismatch, no matches were found in CancerPPD2, while only 10 sequences matched in dbAMP3, indicating that DAMPC represents a largely novel set of dual-activity candidates.

As shown in Fig. 3b, dbAMP3 and CancerPPD2 peptides are generally 10–20 amino acids long, whereas DAMPC contains a higher proportion of peptides between 20 and 40 residues, highlighting a distinct size profile compared with known AMP and ACP databases. This difference likely reflects the limited inclusion of microbial peptides in existing ACP resources, a trend also reported for candidate AMPs identified across global metagenomes24.

Figure 3c presents normalized amino acid frequencies across the three datasets. Here, the reported adjusted p values (p_adj_BH) correspond to p values corrected for multiple testing using the Benjamini–Hochberg method. Compared with dbAMP3, the DAMPC dataset shows a statistically significant enrichment of Glycine (G) (adjusted p = 4.65 × 10−170). In the same comparison, Lysine (K) is significantly more abundant in CancerPPD2 than in dbAMP3 (adjusted p = 2.50 × 10−130), while Cysteine (C) is enriched in dbAMP3 relative to CancerPPD2 (adjusted p = 9.04 × 10−48). When DAMPC is compared with CancerPPD2, Lysine (K) occurs at a significantly higher frequency in DAMPC (adjusted p = 1.57 × 10−30). The enrichment of Lysine (K) supports an overall increase in positive charge, a physicochemical feature commonly linked to membrane interaction. The elevated Glycine (G) frequency suggests increased structural flexibility, while the comparatively reduced Cysteine (C) content indicates fewer disulfide-constrained motifs relative to certain antimicrobial peptide classes. In addition to residue-level differences, DAMPC peptides predominantly cluster within moderate sequence lengths and exhibit positively charged profiles relative to control peptide sets.

It should be noted that these observations reflect statistical associations between sequence features and predicted dual activity and do not demonstrate antimicrobial or anticancer function. Experimental validation remains necessary, and the reported physicochemical patterns should be interpreted as properties of computationally predicted candidates rather than confirmed biological traits. Given the large sample sizes, very small p-values likely reflect high statistical power rather than large biological effect sizes. Thus, statistical significance here indicates compositional association, not functional causation.

Figure 3d shows the overlap between DAMPC and the AMPSphere24,25 database. Since the DAMPC catalog includes only peptides from the 100AA list of the GMSC catalog, methodological differences explain much of the discrepancy. Singleton rescue in GMSC, absent in AMPSphere, and the selection of representative sequences during 90% amino acid clustering contribute to differences: 6,401 sequences matching AMPSphere are not in the 90AA GMSC catalog, while sequences unique to DAMPC were retained only in the 100AA list. These observations are consistent with dataset construction strategies and do not undermine the reliability of DAMPC peptides.

Usage Notes

For users wishing to download the complete dataset, the catalog and metadata are deposited in Zenodo40. The DAMPC catalog is also available through our web page (https://arikanlab.com/Home/DAMPC), which offers a searchable interface for peptide sequences. Users can input a sequence to determine whether it is included in the catalog and, if so, view associated source habitat, taxonomy, and prediction scores.

The primary objective of this study is not to assert biological activity or therapeutic efficacy, but to reduce the vast smORF-derived peptide search space and generate a prioritized set of candidates for downstream experimental investigation. We designed DAMPC as a hypothesis-generating and prioritization resource to support experimental validation efforts. Experimental validation, including confirmation of expression and functional assays, will be essential to determine the biological relevance of individual candidates identified in this resource.

This study has several limitations inherent to large-scale, sequence-based analyses. The peptide candidates analyzed here are derived from predicted microbial smORFs obtained from metagenomic and genomic assemblies. Although the source catalogs apply multiple in silico quality controls, these smORFs lack direct experimental evidence of transcription or translation. Consequently, it remains uncertain whether many of the predicted peptides are expressed or biologically functional under natural conditions. The predicted antimicrobial and anticancer activities should therefore be interpreted as properties of hypothetical peptides rather than confirmed cellular products. All ACP, AMP, and non-toxic annotations in this work are exclusively based on computational prediction tools and do not rely on experimental validation.
