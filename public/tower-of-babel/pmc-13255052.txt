Successful Management of Achromobacter-Associated Periprosthetic Joint Infections After Total Knee Arthroplasty: A Case Series of Ten Knees in Six Patients
Authors: Rahul Singla, Pulak Vatsya, Vrishank Malhotra, Ashish Chauhan, Gaurav Garg, Rajesh Malhotra
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPeriprosthetic joint infections (PJIs) following total knee arthroplasty (TKA) are often caused by common pathogens; however, rare organisms like Achromobacter species pose unique diagnostic and therapeutic challenges. This case series evaluates 10 Achromobacter-related PJIs among 6 patients.MethodsA prospective single-center case series of patients aged greater than 18 years with culture-confirmed Achromobacter PJI was conducted at a New Delhi tertiary care center beginning July 2023. Diagnosis was established using European Bone and Joint Infection Society criteria. Data collection included demographic details, comorbidities, diagnostics, microbiology, surgical interventions, and outcomes. Management was individualized based on infection severity and patient factors.ResultsFive female and 1 male patient [mean age: 61.83 years] presented with ten infected TKAs. Four developed bilateral infections. Most patients presented with sinus tracts; 2 immunocompetent patients had an indolent course. Diagnosis was confirmed using automated continuously monitored blood culture system (n = 3), conventional culture (n = 2), and matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight (n = 1). Four patients (6 knees) had Achromobacter xylosoxidans, 2 patients (4 knees) had Achromobacter denitrificans. One patient with initial bilateral A xylosoxidans infection developed reinfection with the A denitrificans strain in the left knee. Five patient had significant comorbidities. Initial debridement, antibiotics, and implant retention (performed prior to referral) failed in 3 cases. Five patients (9 knees) underwent two-stage revision. One patient (1 knee) underwent single-stage revision and One patient required resection arthroplasty.Following prolonged targeted antibiotic therapy, none of the patients had signs of infection at final follow-up.ConclusionsAchromobacter-related PJIs are rare but aggressive. Successful management requires accurate diagnosis, tailored surgical, and prolonged targeted antimicrobial therapy.Keywords: Periprosthetic joint infection, Achromobacter, Total knee arthroplasty, Two-stage revision, DAIR, Prosthetic infection

Introduction

The increasing prevalence of primary joint arthroplasties has been accompanied by a proportional rise in complications, including periprosthetic joint infection (PJI)—one of the most challenging and dreaded outcomes [1]. Over the past few decades, significant advancements in arthroplasty techniques and microbiological diagnostics have enhanced our understanding of PJI, particularly regarding the isolation, characterization, and management of causative organisms [2]. A thorough understanding of microbiology, including identification of pathogens and their antibiotic susceptibility and resistance profiles, is critical for optimizing infection eradication and achieving successful secondary reimplantation, ultimately improving patients’ quality of life [3].

While coagulase-negative staphylococci are recognized as the most common causative agents of PJI [4], the isolation of rare and atypical pathogens from PJI cultures is not uncommon. Numerous case reports have highlighted these rare organisms, including Achromobacter species. First identified in 1971 from human ear discharge [5], Achromobacter has been predominantly associated with respiratory infections in patients with cystic fibrosis [6]. However, it is also a nosocomial opportunistic pathogen implicated in surgical site infections across multiple specialties, including cardiac, ophthalmic, otorhinolaryngology, and orthopaedic prosthetic procedures [7]. Its role in PJI, particularly in immunocompromised patients, has garnered increasing attention. Notably, Lee et al. [8] reported a case of Achromobacter PJI in an immunocompetent host, challenging the conventional association of this pathogen with immunosuppression. Reports of Achromobacter causing PJI have emerged from various regions worldwide, signaling the need for vigilance, particularly in high-volume arthroplasty centers [9].

As far as the microbiological characteristics are concerned, Achromobacter is a nonfermenting, motile, gram-negative, peritrichous, nonspore-forming, aerobic bacillus microorganism [10]. It is known to cause infections in hot and humid parts of the world, mostly nosocomial and in immunocompromised hosts. Achromobacter spp. are frequently misidentified as other common [ie, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Stenotrophomonas maltophilia, Burkholderia cepacia complex, Acinetobacter spp.] and rare [ie, Pandoraea spp. and Ralstonia spp.] nonfermenting gram-negative bacilli with conventional methods due to biochemical similarities [[11], [12], [13]]. Furthermore, most species are referred to as Achromobacter xylosoxidans due to diagnosis with conventional methods, although there are 19 total species of this bacterium, most of which have been reported in the last decade. Fifteen of these species have been clinically isolated: A xylosoxidans, Achromobacter denitrificans, Achromobacter ruhlandii, Achromobacter piechaudii [14], Achromobacter animicus, Achromobacter mucicolens, Achromobacter pulmonis [15], Achromobacter insolitus, Achromobacter spanius [16], Achromobacter deleyi [17], Achromobacter aegreficans, Achromobacter insuavis, Achromobacter anxifer, Achromobacter dolens [18], and Achromobacter marplatensis [19].

Worldwide, A xylosoxidans is the most commonly isolated species. A ruhlandii is the second most common species in the United States, whereas A dolens and A insuavis are more prevalent in Europe [20]. Although the clinical significance of species variation is not well characterized.

Achromobacter is considered an opportunistic infection mostly spread as a nosocomial infection. Immunocompromised hosts are considered at higher risk, although these claims have been challenged by multiple reports showing that this bacterium can cause infection even in immunocompetent hosts, including PJI [8,21]. Comorbidities such as diabetes mellitus and hypertension, followed by chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, chronic renal failure, cirrhosis, treatment with high-dose corticosteroids, and use of immunosuppressive drugs have been considered as risk factors for infection in immunocompetent hosts [22].

Alarmingly, this organism can form biofilms, thus allowing it to cause deep infections, especially around prostheses, and has already shown resistance to several antibiotics [23]. Also, multiple genes and antibiotic resistance mechanisms have been discovered in this bacterium. This bacterium has also shown the ability to grow in common disinfectants like chlorhexidine, distilled water, as well as vacuum-assisted closure dressings [24]. Such characteristics make this a difficult-to-treat infection and give it the potential to cause outbreaks in operation theaters [25].

Considering the rising trend of this atypical bacterium, we present a series of 6 patients (10 joints) of PJI caused by Achromobacter species. We outline the protocols used for diagnosis, surgical and medical management, and outcomes for these patients.

Material and methods

Study design

This prospective single-center case series was conducted at a tertiary care hospital in New Delhi, India, beginning in July 2023. All cases met the European Bone and Joint Infection Society criteria for PJI diagnosis [26]. Infection due to Achromobacter species was confirmed through either preoperative or intraoperative cultures. Management strategies were individualized based on the clinical aggressiveness of the infection and the patient's immune status, as decided by the senior surgeon. This study is a descriptive case series reporting clinical observations from routine care. No experimental intervention was performed, and no patient identifiers are disclosed. Therefore, approval from the Institutional Review Board was not required. Furthermore, written informed consent was obtained from all patients prior to their inclusion in the study and the documentation of their clinical outcomes.

Study population

The inclusion criteria comprised all patients aged over 18 years with culture-confirmed Achromobacter species PJI. Data were collected prospectively and included demographic details, laboratory results, microbiological and radiological findings, intraoperative notes, wound healing progression, and reimplantation surgery details. Patients were followed up until clinical resolution, confirmed eradication, or treatment failure.

Definitions and criteria

Achromobacter prosthetic joint infections (PJI) were diagnosed based on the European Bone and Joint Infection Society criteria. Treatment failure was defined as any of the following: recurrence with the same or a new organism, death attributable to PJI, or indeterminate clinical failure supported by suggestive laboratory or radiological findings after treatment [26]. A good outcome was defined as the absence of any treatment failure criteria.

Results

This case series included 5 female and 1 male patients with a mean age of 61.83 years, involving a total of 10 infected knee prostheses. Four patients presented with bilateral involvement, while 2 had a unilateral infection. Five patients had previously undergone simultaneous bilateral total knee arthroplasty (TKA), while one patient had undergone left TKA elsewhere. Clinically, 4 of the 6 patients presented with well-established sinus tracts. The 2 immunocompetent patients showed a relatively subtle presentation characterized by localized pain, warmth, and joint effusion, but without sinus formation. Five patients developed PJI within 1 year of their index surgery. Notably, 3 of these cases were operated on at the same center within a window of 3 months, raising suspicion of a localized outbreak. The inflammatory profile of Achromobacter-associated PJI in this series demonstrated a consistent systemic response. All patients presented with elevated serum markers; specifically, C-reactive protein levels exceeded 1 mg/dL in every case, and the erythrocyte sedimentation rate was universally raised, with a cohort average of 55 mm/h.

Local joint evaluation was guided by clinical presentation. For the 4 patients who presented with a chronic draining sinus tract, preoperative aspiration was deferred as the sinus tract provided definitive clinical evidence of infection and a clear pathway for intraoperative sampling. In the remaining 2 patients without a sinus tract, synovial fluid aspiration was performed. These cases yielded synovial white blood cell counts of 2100/μL and 2500/μL, respectively.

Microbiological diagnosis employed a combination of techniques: automated continuously monitored blood culture vial system in 3 cases, conventional culture in 2, and matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight (MALDI-TOF) mass spectrometry (MS) in another. Due to the absence of nrdA gene sequencing, subspecies classification remained limited. Among the isolates, 4 patients were infected with A xylosoxidans and 2 with A denitrificans. Interestingly, one patient initially infected with A xylosoxidans later developed reinfection with A denitrificans.

Regarding surgical history, the current procedure was the index revision for 4 patients. The remaining 2 patients had more complex histories of surgical failure, having undergone 1 and 2 prior revision attempts, respectively, before presenting to our center. The surgical approaches varied: 3 patients underwent debridement, antibiotics, and implant retention (DAIR) prior to referral and presented subsequently to our institution with a sinus tract; however, DAIR was unsuccessful in all 3 cases, necessitating further intervention. The most common definitive intervention was a two-stage revision, with the first stage involving thorough debridement and insertion of a nonarticulating antibiotic-loaded cement spacer containing vancomycin and tobramycin, followed by reimplantation using rotating hinge revision knee system. A single-stage revision using a mediolateral constrained condylar knee prosthesis was performed in the immunocompetent patient. In one case, a resection arthroplasty was required after the failure of a two-stage revision twice. Regarding soft tissue management in the 4 patients presenting with a draining sinus, none required specialized plastic surgery interventions or soft tissue flaps. In all cases, the soft tissue defects were managed successfully with standard debridement and primary closure.

In terms of immunological and comorbid status, only 2 patients were immunocompetent and experienced a relatively indolent, unilateral infection. The other 4 patients had significant comorbidities, including diabetes, hypertension, and rheumatoid arthritis. All 4 also had a history of recurrent urinary tract infections, though no single pathogen was consistently isolated from urine cultures. None had a history of malignancy or long-term immunosuppressive therapy. Postoperatively, all patients received prolonged courses of targeted antibiotic therapy tailored to their culture sensitivities and guided by infectious disease specialists. Due to the complex resistance profiles of Achromobacter species, antimicrobial regimens had to be individualized. Most patients achieved infection resolution with combined surgical and medical management, though one patient experienced reinfection necessitating resection arthroplasty.

Discussion

The increasing incidence of primary arthroplasties has led to a concomitant rise in periprosthetic joint infections (PJI), which now represent a significant burden both economically and in terms of patient quality of life. While Achromobacter-related PJIs remain rare, the frequency of these infections has been steadily increasing in recent years. Many reports focus on immunocompetent hosts, underscoring the importance of awareness of this pathogen in the orthopaedic setting.

Health care-associated outbreaks of Achromobacter

Several outbreaks related to Achromobacter species have been documented. Arjun et al. [27] reported a series of 12 cases of bacteremia in their ICU (intensive care unit) within 2 months, with an investigation revealing contaminated furosemide ampules as the source of the outbreak. Similar outbreaks have been reported following prostate biopsies and in neonatal ICUs [28].

Chandrashekar et al. [28] emphasized the pathogenic potential of Achromobacter species, especially in immunocompromised hosts, and cautioned against dismissing it as a contaminant. In addition, aerosolization of Achromobacter has been implicated in cases of pneumonia, meningitis, and secondary bacteremia in neonatal units [7]. Gómez-Cerezo et al. [7] described 54 cases of Achromobacter bacteremia, of which 52 were nosocomial and linked to contaminated tap water and transmission via health-care workers' hands. In our series, 3 out of 6 patients had undergone surgery at the same center within 3 months, suggesting a possible localized outbreak. However, we were unable to confirm additional infections or containment measures, highlighting the importance of infection control practices in orthopaedic settings.

Diagnostic challenges and subspecies identification

Traditionally, Achromobacter isolates are classified as A xylosoxidans using conventional microbiological methods. However, more accurate subspecies identification has been made possible by advanced techniques such as nrdA gene sequencing and multilocus sequence typing [29]. Coward et al. [29] highlighted that nrdA gene sequencing is the most reliable method for differentiating Achromobacter subspecies. Despite this, routine microbiological testing often does not include genetic sequencing due to limitations in its availability and feasibility.

MALDI-TOF MS has proven effective in identifying Achromobacter at the genus level, but species-level identification remains challenging due to the limited number of species included in MALDI-TOF databases [30]. Studies have demonstrated >90% accuracy in species identification when the database is expanded to include multiple Achromobacter species [31]. In our series, 4 out of 6 cases were diagnosed using advanced tools such as automated continuously monitored blood culture system and MALDI-TOF MS. However, none of the patients underwent nrdA gene sequencing, likely due to limited awareness and availability of subspecies identification methods. In one case, Achromobacter was initially identified in a discharging sinus, but the first culture was deemed a contaminant, emphasizing the potential for underdiagnosis in orthopaedic infections.

First report of A denitrificans in PJI

Three cases (5 knees [2 primary TKA, 1 revision TKA]) in our series were identified as A denitrificans, marking the first report of this subspecies causing PJI in knee arthroplasty. Notably, these infections exhibited an aggressive course and posed significant challenges for infection control, with all the patients requiring reoperations before the infection was successfully managed. In one case, resection arthroplasty was necessary to eradicate the infection. Neither of these patients were severely immunocompromised—one had diabetes mellitus (type-II), and 2 had a history of recurrent urinary tract infections. The only other reported outbreak of A denitrificans was described by Yoon et al. [32], who documented hospital-acquired cases traced to contaminated chlorhexidine and water solutions. Additionally, A denitrificans has been reported as a common cause of chronic rhinosinusitis following the COVID-19 pandemic [33].

Antibiotic resistance and treatment challenges

Achromobacter species exhibit intrinsic resistance to several classes of antibiotics, including narrow-spectrum penicillin, first-generation and second-generation cephalosporins, ceftriaxone, cefotaxime, aztreonam, tetracyclines, and aminoglycosides. However, they often remain susceptible to ceftazidime, cefepime, piperacillin, carbapenems, sulphonamides, fluoroquinolones, doxycycline, tigecycline, and colistin. Notably, contemporary clinical isolates, particularly from patients with chronic cystic fibrosis, frequently develop multidrug resistance, especially to fluoroquinolones [34]. In our series, all isolates were sensitive to piperacillin-tazobactam but were resistant to fluoroquinolones. Additionally, 3 out of 6 cases were resistant to imipenem and trimethoprim. In one patient, A xylosoxidans was initially isolated, but a subsequent reinfection with A denitrificans showed an altered antibiotic susceptibility profile. This phenomenon of rapid resistance evolution has been previously reported, particularly in respiratory infections among immunocompromised hosts [34]. Achromobacter species harbor at least 50 genes encoding antibiotic resistance, including multiple β-lactamases and efflux pumps, further complicating therapy [35]. Genetic modifications, such as biofilm formation and altered motility, enable these bacteria to thrive in host environments, thereby reducing the efficacy of antibiotics [22]. These genetic factors may also contribute to the increased occurrence of Achromobacter infections in immunocompetent hosts, as shown by Coward et al. [29].

Clinical implications for PJI management

Given the biofilm-forming ability and multidrug resistance of Achromobacter species, aggressive treatment strategies are crucial. In our series, only 1 immunocompetent patient with an indolent infection was successfully managed with a single-stage revision. However, this patient developed ototoxicity following prolonged antibiotic therapy, possibly due to minocycline or levofloxacin.

Three out of 6 patients failed DAIR procedures, necessitating 4–5 reoperations for infection control. Given the high failure rate of DAIR, we recommend that two-stage revision be considered the preferred approach for Achromobacter PJI. The combination of biofilm formation and antibiotic resistance suggests that a prolonged implant-free interval may be necessary for effective infection eradication [[36], [37], [38], [39]]. Their inherent antimicrobial resistance, biofilm-forming ability, and tendency for recurrence make management particularly challenging. Orthopaedic surgeons must remain vigilant when encountering atypical organisms in culture and be prepared for the possibility of prolonged therapy and multiple surgical interventions. Nevertheless, the interpretation of our findings should be tempered by the inherent limitations of this series, including the small sample size, retrospective design, and lack of standardized treatment protocols across patients. These limitations restrict broad generalization but do not diminish the clinical importance of recognizing this pathogen. Future multicenter studies with larger cohorts and standardized management strategies are essential to better define the epidemiology, therapeutic options, and long-term outcomes of Achromobacter-related PJI.

Conclusions

Achromobacter-related PJIs are uncommon but clinically significant due to their aggressive nature and multidrug resistance. Accurate identification, often requiring advanced diagnostics, is essential. DAIR procedures show limited success, whereas two-stage revision with targeted antibiotic therapy offers more reliable outcomes. Early diagnosis, individualized management, and strict infection control are crucial for effective treatment.
