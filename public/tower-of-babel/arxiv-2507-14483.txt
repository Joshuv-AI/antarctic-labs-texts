Abrupt transitions in the optimization of diffusion with distributed resetting
Pedro Julián-Salgado, Leonardo Dagdug, Denis Boyer

Abstract
Brownian diffusion subject to stochastic resetting to a fixed position has been widely studied for applications to random search processes. In an unbounded domain, the mean first passage time at a target site can be minimized for a convenient choice of the resetting rate. Here we study this optimization problem in one dimension when resetting occurs to random positions, chosen from a distribution with compact support that does not include the target. Depending on the shape of this distribution, the optimal resetting rate either varies smoothly with the mean distance to the target, as in single-site resetting, or exhibits a discontinuity caused by the presence of a second local minimum in the mean first passage time. These two regimes are separated by a critical line containing a singular point that we characterize through a Ginzburg-Landau theory. To quantify how useful a given resetting position is for a search process, we calculate the distribution of the last resetting position before absorption. The discontinuous transition above separates two markedly different optimal strategies: one with a small resetting rate where the last path before absorption starts from a rather distant but likely position, while the other strategy has a large resetting rate, favoring last paths starting from not-so-likely points but which are closer to the target.



I
Introduction

Diffusive processes subject to stochastic resetting to a given position represent an efficient way of carrying out a random search task. After some time of unfruitful search to locate a specific target region, resetting gives the opportunity to the searcher to come back to its starting point for exploring new pathways. Brownian motion under resetting achieves its first passage at a target site in an unbounded medium within a finite time, contrary to ordinary diffusion where such a time diverges on average. In this situation, which is quite generic, the mean first passage time (MFPT) is a non-monotonous function of the resetting rate, with a unique minimum at a certain optimal value
Evans and Majumdar 2011a
;
Evans and Majumdar 2011b
;
Evans and Majumdar 2014
;
Pal et al. 2016
;
Chechkin and Sokolov 2018
;
Evans et al. 2020
. This property makes resetting appealing as a mechanism to expedite the completion of a task, and applications have been discussed in a variety of biological contexts, for instance, enzymatic reaction kinetics
Reuveni et al. 2014
;
Rotbart et al. 2015
;
Pal and Prasad 2019a
, RNA recovery
Roldán et al. 2016
, active cellular transport
Bressloff 2020
, animal foraging
Mercado-Vásquez and Boyer 2018
;
Giuggioli et al. 2019
;
Pal et al. 2020
;
Altshuler et al. 2024
or population dynamics
Kang and Park 2022
;
Plata et al. 2020
;
Ali et al. 2022
. The optimization of first passage times through resetting has also been generalized to a variety of other processes such as anomalous diffusion models
Kusmierz et al. 2014
;
Kuśmierz and Gudowska-Nowak 2015
;
Kuśmierz and Gudowska-Nowak 2019
;
Méndez et al. 2021
;
Pal et al. 2024
, and tested on Brownian motion in controlled experiments using colloidal particles
Besga et al. 2020
;
Tal-Friedman et al. 2020
.

Nevertheless, the effect of resetting on first passage times can be diverse. Resetting can also hinder the completion of a task instead of expediting it, as in some cases the MFPT turns out to be a monotonously increasing function of the resetting rate
r
. A simple universal criterion actually predicts the effect of a small resetting rate on the MFPT of an arbitrary process
Reuveni 2016
;
Pal and Reuveni 2017
. An example is provided by a Brownian particle in an interval: depending on the resetting/starting position, the mean exit time from the interval is either a non-monotonous function of
r
with a minimum at
r^{*}>0
, or a monotonously increasing function of
r
whose optimum is therefore
r^{*}=0
(the absence of resetting favors the exit)
Pal and Prasad 2019b
;
Durang et al. 2019
;
Singh et al. 2020
. The transition between these two optimal regimes occurs at a certain resetting position and it is of “second order”, i.e.,
r^{*}
tends to
0
continuously. A similar transition occurs in other setups, e.g., in drift-diffusion processes with an absorbing boundary
Ray et al. 2019
.

In some systems, the variations of the MFPT with the resetting rate
r
are more complex and exhibit two local minima, one at a value
r_{1}>0
and the other at
r_{2}=0
. Examples range from the kinetics of enzymatic catalysis
Rotbart et al. 2015
;
Pal and Prasad 2019a
to Brownian motion under resetting in certain external potentials
Ahmad et al. 2022
or diffusion in narrow channels of varying width
Jain et al. 2023
. In these cases, discontinuous (or “first order”) transitions from a vanishing to a non-zero optimal resetting rate can be observed as the resetting position (or some other parameter) is varied. Another example where the MFPT at a target site can exhibit more than one minimum is provided by Brownian diffusion in unbounded space with resetting to a random position taken from a Gaussian distribution
Besga et al. 2020
. Since the particle has a non-zero probability density to be reset at the target position itself, the MFPT is usually a decreasing function of
r
in that case, and the globally optimal rate is always
r_{1}=\infty
. However, if the variance of the Gaussian is sufficiently small, a secondary “metastable” minimum at a finite
r_{2}
appears.

A situation of particular interest in the present study and which has been little discussed previously, is the case of a MFPT having multiple minima at some finite rates, e.g.,
0<r_{2}<r_{1}<\infty
, meaning that both a fast and a slow resetting strategy are locally optimal, while neither
r=0
nor
r=\infty
are advantageous. The mean search time of symmetric Lévy flights of index
\mu
under resetting exhibits such a behavior, with two local minima in the
(r,\mu)
plane
Kuśmierz and Gudowska-Nowak 2015
. A more recent example is Brownian search under a time constraint and where each resetting event comes with a cost, such that the searcher seeks to minimize the total cost while maintaining a high probability of locating the target within the allotted time
Sunil et al. 2024
.
In the context of quantum walks, mean detection times with multiple minima have been found in evolutions subject to periodic resetting
Yin and Barkai 2023
;
Yin et al. 2024
. A fourth example is diffusion with two resetting points
Julián-Salgado et al. 2024
, a simple extension of the paradigmatic single-site resetting search problem of

Evans and Majumdar 2011a
. All these examples exhibit rich behaviors for the optimal strategy
r^{*}
, in particular discontinuous transitions between finite values.

In this paper, we revisit the first passage properties of diffusive processes under resetting to randomly distributed positions and show that they naturally exhibit discontinuous transitions when the resetting distribution has a compact support, thus generalizing our previous finding on two resetting points
Julián-Salgado et al. 2024
. Resetting to multiple sites finds applications in many fields such as foraging ecology
Lambertucci 2010
;
Lambertucci and Ruggiero 2013
, human mobility
Song et al. 2010
, internet search
Brin and Page 1998
;
Ermann et al. 2015
, target search along the DNA
Coppey et al. 2004
, and can be also implemented in optical tweezers experiments
Besga et al. 2020
.
A handful of works have examined the effects of stochastic resetting to a distribution of positions, since the initial work of Evans and Majumdar
Evans and Majumdar 2011b
. For example
González et al. 2021
investigated the impact of resetting to multiple nodes on first passage times for random walks on networks. Depending on the number of resetting nodes, the mean first passage time may or may not be optimizable by a finite resetting probability.
Ref.
Olsen 2023
developed a method for calculating the moments of the steady-state distribution of a process with resetting, expressed in terms of averages computed from the resetting distribution. This approach is particularly useful when the full steady-state distribution is inaccessible. Distributed resetting was also studied from the point of view of information theory
Toledo-Marín and Boyer 2023
.
In
Méndez et al. 2024
, the mean time to absorption was analyzed under different resetting scenarios where the optimal resetting rate varied smoothly.

The paper is organized as follows. We first recall the theory of distributed resetting and show the interest of using truncated densities, i.e., restricted to a compact support (Section
II
). We apply these results to truncated Gaussian and exponential distributions, and unveil abrupt transitions in the optimal rate as the mean or variance are varied (Section
III
). In Section
IV
, we develop a Ginzburg-Landau theory that accounts phenomenologically for these first-order transitions and derive a general condition for the existence of an associated critical point, in analogy with the liquid-gas critical point. This singularity with infinite derivative is located on a critical line where the variations of
r^{*}
are still continuous. To obtain further insights on the mechanisms that lead to a discontinuous transition, we study in Section
V
the
last resetting point density
, i.e., the probability density of the last resetting position used by the particle before finding the target.
The analysis of this density allows us to show how two equivalent, “coexisting” optimal strategies can be markedly different. We also revisit the two-resetting point problem under this scope and see how it differs in some aspects from the other distributions studied. We conclude in Section
VI
.

II
Setup

Let us consider Brownian diffusion subject to stochastic resetting with rate
r
to random positions
z
distributed with density
\rho(z)

Evans and Majumdar 2011b
. More specifically, during the time interval
[t,t+dt]
, with probability
rdt
, a position
z
is drawn from the distribution
\rho(z)
and the particle is set to position
z
, while with complementary probability
1-rdt
, the particle continues to diffuse. The successive resetting positions are assumed to be independent.

Here, the motion takes place in a one-dimensional, semi-infinite channel with a single absorbing target at the origin (
x=0
), see Fig.
1
a.
We recall the general renewal equation for the survival probability
Evans and Majumdar 2011b
,

S_{r}(t|x_{0})=e^{-rt}S_{0}(t|x_{0})\\
+\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\rho(z)\left[r\int_{0}^{t}\differential\tau e^{-r\tau}S_{r}(t-\tau|x_{0})S_{0}(\tau|z)\right]\differential z,

(1)

where
S_{r}(t|x_{0})
stands for the probability that the particle subject to resetting and with initial position
x_{0}
at
t=0
has not crossed the origin until time
t
, while
S_{0}(t|x_{0})
is the survival probability in the absence of resetting (
r=0
). For the free Brownian particle,
S_{0}(t|x_{0})={\rm erf}(\frac{|x_{0}|}{\sqrt{4Dt}})
and
D
is the diffusion coefficient.

The first term of the right-hand side (rhs) of Eq. (
1
) comes from the surviving trajectories that have not been reset until time
t
, which occurs with probability
e^{-rt}
. The second term in the rhs is an average over the resetting position
z
where the particle had its last reset before the current time
t
. This average applies to trajectories that had their last reset at some time
t-\tau
and then continued to evolve during the interval
[t-\tau,t]
with a simple Brownian motion.
We proceed further by applying the Laplace transform
{\cal L}(\cdot)=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-st}(\cdot)dt
to both sides of Eq. (
1
), which reduces the problem to an algebraic equation whose solution is

q_{r}(s|x_{0})=\frac{q_{0}(r+s|x_{0})}{1-r\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\rho(z)q_{0}(r+s|z)\differential z},

(2)

where
q_{r,0}(s|x_{i})={\cal L}[S_{r,0}(t|x_{i})]
, respectively. For free Brownian motion, one can rely on a specific closed form given by
q_{0}(s|x_{i})=\frac{1-e^{-\sqrt{s/D}|x_{i}|}}{s}
, see, e.g.
Evans and Majumdar 2011a
. From Eq. (
2
) the MFPT for a particle starting from
x_{0}
is readily identified with
q_{r}(s=0|x_{0})=\int_{0}^{\infty}dt\ S_{r}(t,x_{0})

Dagdug et al. 2024
and given by

T(x_{0})=\frac{1-e^{-\alpha_{0}|x_{0}|}}{r\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\rho\left(z\right)e^{-\alpha_{0}|z|}\differential z},

(3)

where

\alpha_{0}=\sqrt{r/D}

(4)

is proportional to the inverse typical distance diffused by the particle between two consecutive resets.

In the following, we assume that the initial position
x_{0}
is also distributed according to
\rho(z)
. The main quantity of interest is therefore the averaged mean first passage time, defined by
\mathcal{T}_{r}=\int\rho(x_{0})T(x_{0})\differential x_{0}
and whose expression is

\mathcal{T}_{r}=\frac{1}{r}\left(\frac{1}{\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\rho\left(z\right)e^{-\alpha_{0}|z|}\differential z}-1\right).

(5)

The purpose of the present study is to determine the rate
r^{*}
where Eq. (
5
) reaches a global minimum.
At small
r
, it is easy to see that Eq. (
5
) yields

{\cal T}_{r}\simeq\frac{\langle|z|\rangle}{\sqrt{Dr}}\to\infty,

(6)

similarly to single-site resetting, where the average
\langle|z|\rangle
with weight
\rho(z)
is replaced by
x_{0}

Evans and Majumdar 2011a
.

Let us now point out two rather different situations related to the
large

r
behavior of
{\cal T}_{r}
:

\bullet
Case
\rho(0)\neq 0
, i.e., the probability density at the target position is finite. In the large
r
limit (taking
\alpha_{0}\to\infty
),
\rho(z)
can be replaced by
\rho(0)
in Eq. (
5
) and integration over
z
gives

\mathcal{T}_{r}\simeq\frac{1}{2\rho(0)\sqrt{Dr}}\to 0\,.

(7)

Since
\lim_{r\to\infty}{\cal T}_{r}=0
, the optimal resetting rate
r^{*}
is infinite. Qualitatively, when the distribution
\rho(z)
is sampled infinitely fast, the particle can reset very near the target site and the latter is quickly found after very little diffusion. Although
{\cal T}_{r}
behaves as
1/\sqrt{r}
for both large and small values of
r
, it can exhibit non-monotonous variations for intermediate values of

, depending on the variance of
\rho(z)
. This scenario was studied in
Besga et al. 2020
.

\bullet
Case
\rho(0)=0
. It is not possible to reset very close to the target site and Eq. (
7
) is not valid. An example is when
\rho(z)
vanishes everywhere except in a compact support, i.e.,
\rho(z)\neq 0
only in some interval
[z_{min},z_{max}]
with
0<z_{min}<z_{max}<\infty
. At large
r
, Eq. (
5
) now reads

\mathcal{T}_{r}\simeq\frac{e^{\alpha_{0}z_{min}}}{\rho(z_{min})\sqrt{Dr}}\to\infty.

(8)

From Eqs. (
6
) and (
8
), one expects that
\mathcal{T}_{r}
has at least one local minimum and that
r^{*}
is nontrivial.

In the following, we analyze Eq. (
5
) for choices of
\rho(z)
belonging to the second case
\rho(0)=0
above. In Ref.
Julián-Salgado et al. 2024
we considered a particular example exhibiting a rich phenomenology, that is, two resetting points at positions

and
z_{2}
. In that case,
\rho(z)=a_{1}\delta(z-z_{1})+a_{2}\delta(z-z_{2})
with
0<z_{1}<z_{2}
and

{\cal T}_{r}
of

could exhibit two local minima (instead of one) while the optimal rate

corresponding to the global minimum underwent a discontinuous “first-order” transition as

was varied (fixing

). This discontinuous jump existed provided that

was large enough and exceeded a non-trivial critical value of
6.600817\ldots
Below this threshold, the variations of

with

were smooth, although not necessarily monotonously decreasing like in single-site resetting

III
First-order transitions in the optimal resetting rate

We consider several resetting point distributions with compact support and that are symmetric around their mean
\mu
. We use dimensionless space and time units.
By definition, the left edge of the support is located at a unit distance from the target at the origin, and unless indicated, the diffusion coefficient is set to

[1,2\mu-1]
and vanishes everywhere else, as depicted in Fig.
1

Two natural choices of
\rho(z)
are the truncated Gaussian and truncated exponential (Laplace) distributions.

IV
Phenomenological Ginzburg-Landau theory

The purpose of this Section is to write a Taylor expansion of
{\cal T}_{r}
for
r
close to
r_{c}
that captures the different shapes observed in the panels and insets of Fig.
2
, when the parameters
\mu
and
\sigma
are varied around
\mu_{c}
and
\sigma_{c}
, respectively.

We first need to determine the values of the critical parameters, if they exist. This critical point is characterized by a change in the concavity of
{\cal T}_{r}
at
r=r_{c}
, as
r_{c}
is a unique minimum of
{\cal T}_{r}
for
\sigma\leq\sigma_{c}
and a local maximum for
\sigma>\sigma_{c}
(in any case, it is assumed that
\sigma
is close to
\sigma_{c}
). This implies vanishing first and second derivatives at this point. For
\sigma>\sigma_{c}
but close to
\sigma_{c}
, if one sets
\mu=\mu_{t}(\sigma)\simeq\mu_{c}
, one finds two symmetric, equivalent minima at
r_{1}^{*}(\sigma)
and
r_{2}^{*}(\sigma)
on each side of the local maximum
r_{c}
, with
r_{2}^{*}(\sigma)<r_{c}<r_{1}^{*}(\sigma)
. Hence, the Taylor expansion of
{\cal T}_{r}
should not include a cubic term
(r-r_{c})^{3}
(although asymmetry can become important further away from the critical point; see, e.g., Fig.

{\cal T}_{r}
with respect to

must vanish at the critical point,

\left.\frac{\partial^{n}\mathcal{T}(r,\mu,\sigma)}{\partial r^{n}}\right|_{\left(r,\mu,\sigma\right)=\left(r_{c},\mu_{c},\sigma_{c}\right)}=0\quad{\rm for}\ n=1,2,3.

(14)

The equation with

follows from the fact that

is a minimum by definition (optimal rate) at
\sigma=\sigma_{c}
and
\mu=\mu_{c}
, as illustrated by the insets of Figs.
2
b and
2
e. Relations analogous to Eqs. (
14
) were used in
Julián-Salgado et al. 2024
for the two-point problem.
Hence the critical parameters for the TGD and TED cases can be calculated by numerically solving the system of equations (
14
) with the help of Mathematica, using Eqs. (

) and (

\displaystyle\mu_{c}=19.0105...

\displaystyle\sigma_{c}=8.7610...

(15)

\displaystyle r_{c}=0.1382...,

while for the TED,

\displaystyle\mu_{c}=12.9376...

\displaystyle\sigma_{c}=8.2355...

(16)

\displaystyle r_{c}=0.2028...\ .

These are the values reported previously in Section
III
.

\displaystyle{\cal T}_{r}

\displaystyle\simeq

\displaystyle T_{c}+\frac{a_{2}}{2}(r-r_{c})^{2}+\frac{a_{4}}{4}(r-r_{c})^{4}\,,

\displaystyle\quad\mu=\mu_{t}(\sigma),\ \sigma\geq\sigma_{c},

(19)

V
Last resetting position before absorption

In the presence of multiple resetting points, we can explore a question specific to distributed resetting: in what position the particle was reset just before being absorbed by the target? Let us introduce the last resetting point density
\rho_{l}(z)
, defined as the probability that the particle was reset near
z
at some time, given that it subsequently diffused from there and was absorbed without further resetting. Since the last resetting position
z
is conditioned to further absorption,
\rho_{l}(z)\neq\rho(z)
.
This quantity gives information on how useful is a given position
z
for the search process. In particular, the region of space where
\rho_{l}(z)
is larger indicates those resetting positions that are more efficient for a given choice of
\rho(z)
and
r
.

Let us consider an arbitrary underlying process (with
r=0
) for which the density of the first passage time
t
at the origin is known and denoted as
{\cal P}_{0}(t|z)
, where
z
is the initial position. For the process subject to resetting, let us introduce the joint probability density
{\cal P}_{r}(t,z)
of the first passage time
t
at the origin and of the position
z
where the underlying process was reset for the last time. We have the general relation,

\mathcal{P}_{r}(t,z)=\rho(z)e^{-rt}\mathcal{P}_{0}(t|z)\\
+r\rho(z)\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\rho(x_{0})\differential x_{0}\int_{0}^{t}e^{-r\tau}S_{r}(t-\tau|x_{0})\mathcal{P}_{0}(\tau|z)\differential\tau.

(25)

The construction of
\mathcal{P}_{r}
is similar to Eq. (
1
). The first term of the rhs of Eq. (
25
) takes into account the contribution from the trajectories that started near
z
at
t=0
and found the target at time
t
, without any resetting. The second term represents those trajectories that started from some position
x_{0}
, underwent at least one reset and had their last resetting at time
t-\tau
near
z
before being absorbed
\tau
time units later.
The last resetting point density follows from the marginal
\rho_{l}(z)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathcal{P}_{r}(t,z)\differential t
. Integrating Eq. (
25
) over
t
gives after straightforward algebra

\rho_{l}(z)=(r\mathcal{T}_{r}+1)\rho(z)\widetilde{{\cal P}}_{0}(r|z),

(26)

where
\widetilde{{\cal P}}_{0}(s|z)
is the Laplace transform of
{\cal P}_{0}(t|z)
and
where we have used the definition
\mathcal{T}_{r}=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\differential x_{0}\rho(x_{0})\int_{0}^{\infty}\differential tS_{r}(t|x_{0})
.
For a Brownian particle,
{\cal P}_{0}(t|z)
is given by the Lévy-Smirnov distribution
Dagdug et al. 2024

\mathcal{P}_{0}(t|z)=\frac{|z|}{\sqrt{4\pi D}}\frac{e^{-z^{2}/4Dt}}{t^{3/2}},

(27)

whose Laplace transform is
\widetilde{{\cal P}}_{0}(s|z)=e^{-\sqrt{s/D}|z|}
. One can check using Eq. (
5
) that
\rho_{l}(z)
in Eq. (
26
) is normalized. The sought distribution thus reads

\rho_{l}(z)=\mathcal{C}\rho(z)e^{-\alpha_{0}|z|},\quad\alpha_{0}=\sqrt{\frac{r}{D}},

(28)

with
\mathcal{C}
the normalization constant.

The expression (
28
), or more generally (
26
), is one of our main result and reveal an important aspect of distributed resetting:
\rho_{l}(z)
is a product of two terms that can have antagonistic effects. For a given
z
, the density
\rho_{l}(z)
can be high for essentially two reasons:

\bullet

\rho(z)
is large, i.e.,
z
is frequently used as a resetting position and therefore likely to be the last one;

\bullet

z
is close to the target (
|z|
small), making the exponential factor in Eq. (
28
) not small. Hence
z
may not be a frequently used position, but from this resetting point the capture probability is high.

Loosely speaking, in the first case “entropic” effects dominate (frequent positions are useful), whereas in the second one “energetic” effects dominate (positions close to the target are useful). The competition between these two trends gives rise to rich behaviors, and the discontinuous transitions can be re-analyzed under this scope.

V.1
Truncated distributions

With the truncated Gaussian in Eq. (
9
), the last resetting point distribution
\rho_{l}(z)
is also a truncated Gaussian. Its explicit expression for
1\leq z\leq 2\mu-1
is

\rho_{l}(z)=\frac{2e^{-\frac{\alpha_{0}^{2}\sigma^{2}}{2}}e^{\alpha_{0}\mu}e^{-\left(z-\mu\right)^{2}/2\sigma^{2}}e^{-\alpha_{0}z}}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^{2}}\left[\erf\left(\frac{\mu-1+\alpha_{0}\sigma^{2}}{\sqrt{2}\sigma}\right)-\erf\left(\frac{1-\mu+\alpha_{0}\sigma^{2}}{\sqrt{2}\sigma}\right)\right]},

(29)

and its most probable position
\hat{z}_{l}\equiv{\arg\max}_{z}\,\rho_{l}(z)
is shifted to the left of
\mu
and given by
\hat{z}_{l}={\rm max[1,\mu-\alpha_{0}\sigma^{2}]}
. In other words,

\hat{z}_{l}=\begin{cases}\mu-\sqrt{\frac{r}{D}}\sigma^{2}\quad{\rm if}\ r<D\left(\frac{\mu-1}{\sigma^{2}}\right)^{2}\\
\\
1\quad{\rm if}\ r>D\left(\frac{\mu-1}{\sigma^{2}}\right)^{2}.\end{cases}

(30)

Given a distribution
\rho(z)
with parameters
(\mu,\sigma)
, Eq. (
30

r
large enough,
\rho_{l}(z)
is peaked at
z=1
, the left edge of the support. Hence, successful diffusive bouts are those that tend to start close to the target (“energetic” regime). Conversely, for very small

,
\rho_{l}(z)\simeq\rho(z)
hence the most successful bouts are those that start where
\rho(z)
is maximal (“entropic” regime).

The optimal rate can belong to either qualitative regime, depending on
(\mu,\sigma)
. The large discontinuities in

in Fig.

3

\mu>\mu_{t}(\sigma)
), the rate can be significantly smaller, so that the searcher is optimal thanks to the central part of
\rho(z)
, which is farther away but where resetting predominantly occurs. This regime change can be understood by noticing that when
\mu
increases at fixed
\sigma
, it becomes very unlikely to reset near the left edge, since
\rho(z=1)\sim e^{-\mu^{2}/2\sigma^{2}}\to 0
. Therefore, the optimal resetting rate must be small in order to allow the bouts starting from the central part of the distribution to reach the target.

In Fig.

, we verify these predictions by showing the distributions
\rho_{l}(z)
corresponding to the two coexisting optimal rates
r_{1}^{*}(\sigma)
and
r_{2}^{*}(\sigma)
.
Clearly, near the critical point (
\sigma\approx\sigma_{c}
) the two curves in Fig.
4

z=1
and rapidly vanish in the bulk. They thus belong to the “energetic” regime and are essentially superposed because the two optimal rates are very close to each other. However, as
\sigma-\sigma_{c}
grows, the difference between
r_{1}^{*}(\sigma)
and
r_{2}^{*}(\sigma)
becomes more evident. While
\rho_{l}(z)
for

remains peaked at
z=1

widens and can even exhibits a maximum inside the support at large
\sigma
(Figs.
4

4

In summary, the two equivalent optimal strategies are very different.

The truncated exponential distribution leads to qualitatively similar results. In this case the most probable position of
\rho_{l}(z)
is given by

\hat{z}_{l}=\begin{cases}\mu\quad{\rm if}\ r<\frac{2D}{\sigma^{2}}\\
\\
1\quad{\rm if}\ r>\frac{2D}{\sigma^{2}}.\end{cases}

(31)

Once again, a large drop of the optimal rate from

VI
Conclusions

We have studied first passage observables of one-dimensional diffusion subject to resetting to random positions distributed on compact supports. Notably, the optimal resetting rate
r^{\ast}
that minimizes the mean first passage time at a target located outside the support can exhibit a singularity and discontinuous transitions in parameter space. This phenomenology differs from standard single-site resetting and can be described generically by a Ginzburg-Landau approach, where the existence of the critical point (similar to a “liquid-gas” point) obeys a simple general criterion. We have illustrated these results by considering truncated Gaussians and truncated exponential distributions, adding to results previously obtained with two resetting points
Julián-Salgado et al. 2024
.

Distributed resetting exhibits rich behaviors when the most probable resetting positions
z
are in the middle zone of the support, while the positions closer to the target (at the edge) are less likely. The optimal rate
r^{\ast}
can exhibit a variety of features, depending on the shape of the distribution
\rho(z)
of the resetting points. For example,
r^{\ast}

We further interpreted our results by analyzing the probability density
\rho_{l}(z)
of the last resetting position visited by the particle before absorption. Upon a normalization constant,

\rho(z)
weighted by an attenuation factor
e^{-\sqrt{r/d}|z|}
that decays with the distance
|z|
to the target. The distribution
\rho_{l}(z)
identifies the resetting zone that is actually efficient for finding the target, and two qualitative regimes can be observed: the efficient zone can be the one nearest to the target (“energetic” regime), or where the most probable resetting positions are (“entropic” regime). The optimal rate
r^{*}

\rho(z)

r_{1}^{*}
and
r_{2}^{*}
are equally efficient for the target search, but they can be based on very different resetting zones of the support.

In summary, search processes based on resetting to random positions that are distributed on a compact support can exhibit co-existence between two optimal resetting rates, corresponding to two markedly different strategies.
