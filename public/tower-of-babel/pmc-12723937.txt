Moving from recognising to responding to oral health needs in eating disorders: matters arising from Gidlund et al., 2025
Authors: James Downs
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractGidlund and colleagues’ recent study highlights the profound unmet dental care needs of people with eating disorders, extending earlier work that showed oral health can remain a visible scar long after illness. Their findings reveal how shame, stigma, and systemic barriers prevent timely care, while oral rehabilitation can play a vital role in restoring dignity, hope, and identity. The study strengthens the case for viewing dental complications not as secondary impacts of eating disorders, but as core symptoms that demand timely, evidence-based, and compassionate care. Meeting this challenge requires a shift from recognition to response. This includes changes to clinical practice, where harm-reduction approaches and sensitive communication are essential; to health systems and policy, where equitable access and interdisciplinary pathways must be established; and within research, where coproduced interventions and longitudinal studies can provide the evidence base that is currently lacking. Oral health should no longer be treated as an afterthought in eating disorder care but recognised as an integral part of recovery.Keywords: Eating disorders, Dentistry, Oral health, Lived experience, Recovery

Introduction

Gidlund and colleagues’ recent qualitative study makes an important contribution by showing how “unmet dental care needs marked the journey to recovery” for individuals with eating disorders (EDs) [1]. It builds on their earlier work, which drew attention to how oral health problems often remain a persistent and visible marker of EDs [2], illustrating in greater depth how dental experiences intersect with shame, capability and hope.

For example, participants described dental services as “navigating uncharted territory”, characterised by professional uncertainty and a lack of tailored guidance. Others recalled that repeated consultations were “unhelpful and characterised by feelings of shame, condemnation, and blame”, creating a cycle of avoidance. Conversely, oral rehabilitation was seen as “part of becoming whole in some way, to put an end to a chapter”.

Although these studies are modest in scale and acknowledge the limited diversity in their samples, together they establish oral health not as a peripheral concern but as a central component of ED recovery and identity. This is something I have argued myself from the perspective of lived experience of severe dental impacts from a longstanding ED [3].

From recognition to response

Considering the ethical imperative of meeting clinical need with evidence-based care, the task is now to move from recognition to response. If oral health is integral to recovery, it must be integral to care. That responsibility spans multiple arenas: the delivery of individual treatment, the systems and policies that shape access and continuity, and the research base that informs practice.

This also requires a key shift in how oral complications are conceptualised in EDs. In my personal experience, dental erosion, caries, and other oral sequelae are too often regarded as distant consequences, treatable only once remission has been achieved [3]. A more appropriate understanding is to recognise them as symptoms of EDs themselves, carrying clinical, psychosocial and ethical significance. Persisting in treating these problems as separate reflects a reductive dualism between mind and body, sustaining fragmented, siloed care and constraining interdisciplinary knowledge-generation [4–7]. Reframing oral health in this way legitimises timely intervention and establishes dentistry as an integral dimension of ED care.

The following sections outline three domains in which this reframing can be operationalised: (1) clinical practice, where harm-reduction and sensitive communication can be embedded; (2) systems and policy, where structural barriers must be addressed; and (3) research, where new evidence and greater coproduction with people with lived experience are required.

1) Clinical translation: embedding oral health into care

In clinical practice, Gidlund and colleagues’ study postponing dental treatment until patients achieve remission from EDs results in preventable physical and psychosocial suffering, including irreversible damage to teeth, heightened shame, and lost opportunities to support recovery. They argue that a harm-reduction approach offers a more constructive alternative, enabling oral health interventions to take place during illness. In my own experience, this approach not only helps to prevent long-term complications, but can also strengthen possibilities for recovery by addressing shame, preserving dignity, and fostering interdisciplinary care.

The following suggestions illustrate how oral health could be more effectively embedded within ED care:

1A Staged interventions

Simple measures such as protective coatings, fluoride applications and temporary restorations can be offered while illness is ongoing [8]. These interventions do not depend on full remission but can reduce disease progression, relieve pain, and may preserve hope by signalling that recovery is possible and worth investing in.

1B Early screening and referral

Timely identification of oral health problems can also provide an entry point into ED care. As one participant recalled, she contacted her dentist immediately after her first episode of vomiting, yet “the only thing she did was prescribe mouthwash” [1]. That moment could have been transformative had there been clear protocols for sensitive enquiry and referral into specialist services. It is well-recognised that dentists occupy a crucial position in the early detection of EDs [3, 8, 9]. However, to realise this potential, dentists need guidance, clear referral pathways to ED services, and confidence in how to respond - something which is crucially lacking [10].

1C Communication training

Participants repeatedly emphasised the importance of empathy. One noted: “It would be important to also have some education in the emotional part… because it’s not just soft drinks and poor dental hygiene that cause dental erosion” [1]. Training for oral health professionals must therefore extend beyond technical competence to include awareness of stigma, skills to engage patients in compassionate dialogue despite distress or ambivalence, and the ability to set collaborative goals. This aligns with wider evidence that respectful and non-judgemental communication fosters trust and reduces stigma and the avoidance of care amongst those with EDs [11–13].

2) Systems and policy: addressing structural barriers

Clinical care does not occur in isolation but within systems that shape access, continuity, and equity. Even where treatment was technically available, participants in Gidlund and colleagues’ study described systemic obstacles that undermined its reach. Services were characterised as “incredibly bureaucratic and complicated”, with the financial cost of treatment emerging as a recurrent source of anxiety. Such barriers are structurally determined and therefore require structural solutions, for example:

2A Equity in access

Barriers to dental care are well documented and vary across healthcare systems, often becoming most acute for socially or economically marginalised groups [14, 15]. Gidlund et al. make clear how these challenges may be compounded by the psychological burden of stigma and shame for those with EDs, making it harder to seek and sustain care [1, 2]. Addressing these inequities requires structural solutions, such as commissioning arrangements to reduce financial barriers to dental care for those with EDs, ensuring that oral rehabilitation is treated as an essential component of health rather than an optional add-on.

2B Integrated care and interdisciplinary collaboration

As Gidlund and colleagues report, patients described being left to “navigate uncharted territory” when trying to access dental support [1]. Multidisciplinary protocols linking ED and dental services would provide clarity about roles, responsibilities and referral routes, reducing uncertainty for professionals and breaking cycles of avoidance for patients. Beyond individual pathways, system-level integration is also required: training curricula and commissioning frameworks must reflect the profound oral health implications of EDs, with shared learning, case discussions and aligned structures essential for genuinely integrated care [3].

2C Outcome benchmarking

Oral health outcomes are rarely included in ED service audits, meaning that complications remain invisible in performance metrics [16]. Incorporating oral health into quality frameworks would make this dimension of illness and recovery visible, while supporting accountability for its improvement.

3) Research: addressing a thin evidence base with coproduced approaches

The clinical and structural changes outlined above must be grounded in evidence. As Gidlund and colleagues acknowledge, the research base on oral health in EDs remains thin - both limited in scope and scarce in quantity. Strengthening this evidence base requires not only broader and more inclusive study designs but also coproduction, ensuring that people with lived experience help to shape research questions, interventions and outcomes. Coproduction is essential to achieving epistemic justice - that is, redressing the marginalisation of patient knowledge within research and practice, generating evidence that is both clinically relevant and socially legitimate [17–20].

Future priorities include:

3A Broader inclusion

Studies should extend beyond women in remission to include adolescents, men and those experiencing active illness, capturing the diversity of needs and trajectories which are too often underrepresented in ED research [21, 22].

3B Longitudinal designs

Prospective studies are required to examine how oral health needs change over time and how timely intervention influences recovery. Such work could also investigate the role of oral health and dental treatment within recovery trajectories, including their potential as predictors of recovery outcomes.

3C Intervention testing

Evidence for oral health interventions in EDs, particularly during active illness, remains scarce [10]. To address this gap, harm-reduction measures such as protective coatings, fluoride treatments and early restorative work should be trialled, with careful assessment of their effectiveness and cost.

3d Routine data and quality improvement

Incorporating oral health metrics into routine ED service evaluation would both strengthen the evidence base and improve care in real time. Research and service improvement are mutually-reinforcing processes that should not be treated as a trade-off (i.e. where a lack of comprehensive evidence precludes making improvements to care in the here-and-now) [23].

3E Greater coproduction

Lived experience must be embedded not only as testimony but as an active partnership in shaping research questions, co-designing interventions, informing training, and guiding policy. Developing this evidence base through coproduction with people with lived experience of EDs is not only a methodological necessity but an ethical one [22, 24]: it ensures that future care is shaped by those most affected, and that oral health is recognised as integral to both the science and practice of ED recovery. Without this, research risks reinforcing the very gaps in recognition, relevance and accessibility that patients have described [25, 26].

Conclusion: moving from insight to implementation

Taken together with their earlier work, Gidlund and colleagues have demonstrated how oral health is not peripheral but central to the recovery journey in EDs [1, 2]. Their findings show how dental needs intersect with shame, dignity and hope, and how systemic neglect compounds suffering. The task now is to ensure that this recognition translates into meaningful responses across the domains of clinical practice, systems and policy, and research.

Delays in treatment, structural barriers and professional uncertainty must give way to harm-reduction, greater accessibility, shared learning, and coproduction. This requires embedding oral health into therapeutic processes, ensuring system-level integration between dentistry and ED services, and building a stronger, coproduced evidence base. Only then can oral health be treated not as an optional afterthought in EDs, but as a vital component of care and recovery.
