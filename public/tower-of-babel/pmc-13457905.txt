Postmortem Evidence of Lymphatic Vessels Located at the Boundary of Central and Peripheral Nervous Systems in the Cervical Spine
Authors: Shalin S Bhatt, Caitlin R Coker, Lauren K Bagian, Carlo S Tornatore, Jayanti A Kulkarni, Jennifer H Um, Ethan T Weatherdon, Caroline R Hallowell, Alexandra M Akhtarzandi-Das, Marissa N Dreger-Tretheway, Alec J Casely, Emily V Deale, Sabrina F DeLeonibus, Henry Li, Julia S Hakeem, Shreya V Bhatt, Divpriya Talwar, Jeremiah O Olabosipo, Jacqueline M Caputo, Brent T Harris

ABSTRACT
AbstractRecent modern characterizations of human meningeal lymphatic vessels have reframed long-standing views of central nervous system neurofluid drainage. However, gross anatomical data on lymphatic vessels remains sparse. In this cadaveric study, we combined dissections and immunohistochemical analysis in formalin-fixed human body donors to characterize lymphatic vessels associated with cervical spinal nerves. Using manual, low-pressure peroxide–dye injections via the thoracic duct and left jugular lymphatic trunk, we effervesced and identified candidate lymphatic pathways extending toward the head and neck, highlighting vessels accompanying cervical spinal nerves at the interface between the vertebral canal and surrounding soft tissues. Dye-identified vessels and adjacent dural and perineural tissues were harvested and processed for D2-40 immunohistochemistry, targeting the lymphatic endothelial marker podoplanin. Across four of nine donors, we observed D2-40–positive, thin-walled luminal structures within epineurial and peridural compartments at the craniovertebral junction, along cervical intervertebral foramina, and near brachial plexus roots, consistent with lymphatic vessels. These findings identify D2-40–positive vessel profiles consistent with lymphatic vasculature extending along spinal nerves without directly demonstrating intradural channels or cerebrospinal fluid outflow. Introduction

The lymphatic system has been recognized for millennia as a crucial component of bodily fluid regulation. Hippocrates (460 − 375 B.C.) described the milky fluid, ichor, traveling in various vessel lumina, while Aristotle (384–322 B.C.) observed distinct fiber-like, fluid-containing structures between blood vessels and nerves (Kanter 1987; Hu et al. 2024). Jean Pecquet (1624–1674) identified the thoracic duct, detailing how lymph flows through it and empties into the left subclavian vein rather than into the liver (Pecquet 1654; Hu et al. 2024). Niels Stensen (1638–1686) not only described the thoracic duct and left jugular lymphatic vessel that drain between the left subclavian and left internal jugular veins, but also discovered cervical lymphatic vessels (Irschick et al. 2019; Hu et al. 2024). Perhaps most interestingly, by the late 18th century, Paolo Mascagni (1755–1815) observed lymphatic vessels within the human dura mater using mercury injections into lymphatic vessels, thereby identifying what we now know as meningeal lymphatic vessels (mLVs) (Mascagni 1787; Di Matteo et al. 2015; Hu et al. 2024). Although his interpretations of intracranial lymphatic vessels were later questioned, his anatomical wax models of dural lymphatics remain preserved in the Josephinum of Vienna, Austria, and twentieth-century reports similarly described lymphatic-like structures in human dura and potential connections between the central nervous system (CNS) and peripheral lymphatic pathways (Lukić et al. 2003; Scatena and Naccarato 2020). Such skepticism has persisted in the scientific community throughout recent decades, despite additional studies in the 20th century describing lymphatic vessels in human dura and proposing connections between CNS and peripheral lymphatic drainage (Lecco 1953; Földi et al. 1966).

In current literature, lymphatic vessels are recognized as a circulatory network essential for fluid homeostasis and immune surveillance. Their responsibilities include draining interstitial fluid from the periphery that is not drained in venous circulation (Hu et al. 2024). Furthermore, lymphatic vessel disruption is linked to disease processes such as cancer metastasis, immune dysfunction, and chronic inflammatory disorders, including lymphedema and autoimmune diseases (Hu et al. 2024).

Despite these milestones, the CNS was long thought to lack conventional lymphatic drainage because traditional anatomical studies, hindered by the limitations of early dissection and staining techniques, rarely revealed lymphatic vessels traveling into the brain or spinal cord (Proulx 2021). Furthermore, gross anatomical mapping of the lymphatic vasculature in humans declined for much of the twentieth century (Louveau et al. 2015; Suami 2017). The apparent absence of lymphatic structures in the CNS led researchers to propose that cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) was the primary medium for waste clearance and immune regulation within the CNS, circulating through the ventricles and around the spinal cord and brain in a closed loop that returned to venous circulation via arachnoid granulations (Proulx 2021). This CSF flow model suggested an isolated system wherein immune responses would occur largely independently of peripheral lymphatic pathways.

In 2015, this perspective changed with the landmark findings of lymphatic vessels within the meninges, challenging the century-old assumption of CNS immune isolation (Aspelund et al. 2015; Loveau et al. 2015). Jacob et al. identified lymphatic vasculature from the vertebral column in murine models, while Yoon et al. identified lymphatic vasculature exiting the cranial cavity into a nasopharyngeal lymphatic plexus (NPLP) in murine models, a major contributor for cerebrospinal fluid drainage (Jacob et al. 2019; Yoon et al. 2024). Albayram et al. used noninvasive MRI techniques to characterize CSF outflow along meningeal and perineural pathways passing through cranial foramina toward cervical lymph nodes. Most recently, Rzepliński et al. mapped CSF outflow pathways from the spinal canal, including contrast-based tracing along thoracolumbar spinal nerves. However, direct immunohistochemical confirmation of lymphatic vessel identity at CNS-adjacent regions, particularly at cervical spinal levels, remains limited (Rzepliński et al. 2025). These findings are especially interesting considering the discovery of the glymphatic system, a perivascular drainage system walled by parenchymal blood vessels interiorly and glial cells exteriorly (Iliff et al. 2012; Kipnis 2024). Cervical lymphatic drainage of brain interstitial solutes depends partly on upstream glymphatic CSF–interstitial fluid exchange, which is strongly modulated by arterial blood gases such as hypercapnia (Goodman and Iliff 2020). Together, these observations support a model in which glymphatic CSF–interstitial fluid exchange interfaces with meningeal and extracranial lymphatic pathways, yet detailed human anatomical data on lymphatic vessels in and around the cervical spinal canal remain sparse.

Building on this foundation, the present cadaveric study investigates lymphatic vessels associated with the inferior cranial base and cervical spinal canal in humans. We used retrograde dye injections via the thoracic duct and left jugular lymphatic trunk in formalin-fixed donors to highlight patent lymphatic pathways extending toward the head and neck, then sampled dye-identified vessels accompanying cervical spinal nerves and brachial plexus elements for histological and D2-40 immunohistochemical analysis. Our goal was to characterize CNS-adjacent lymphatic vessels in peridural and paravertebral compartments at cervical levels and to refine the anatomical context for discussions of spinal lymphatic drainage, rather than to directly map CSF outflow routes or intradural lymphatic channels.​

Materials and Methods

This cadaveric study was conducted in the Georgetown University School of Medicine (GUSOM) Anatomy Lab, using nine formalin-fixed human body donors from the Georgetown University Anatomical Donor Program. The thoracic duct was traced to its venous drainage site in order to examine lymphatic structures in the cervical and cranial regions. To begin, the thoracic cavity was opened by retracting the anterior thoracic wall. To expose the posterior mediastinum, the heart and lungs were removed. The thoracic duct was identified as an anatomic landmark between the esophagus and the azygos vein. The thoracic duct was carefully traced as it ascended the vertebral column within the posterior mediastinum. It was followed leftward to its drainage point at the left venous angle. The left jugular trunk was also identified. To allow controlled dye filling of the left jugular trunk and prevent retrograde venous flow, the left brachiocephalic vein, left subclavian vein, and left internal jugular vein were clamped using hemostats immediately distal to the point where the thoracic duct drains into the venous circulation (Fig. 1a). This clamping step was essential to prevent retrograde flow and allow for controlled filling of the left jugular trunk.

The thorax of the supine human body donor was elevated by placing a wooden block beneath the superior thorax to hyperextend the cervical spine and tilt the head posteriorly, facilitating cranial migration of the dye mixture. The thoracic duct was cut proximal to where it traverses leftward (Point 1 on Fig. 1a), and an initial injection of 20 mL of 5% hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) was administered using a 30-gauge, ½-inch needle. In formalin-fixed tissue, this low-concentration H₂O₂ ‘primer’ was intended to gently expand the lymphatic lumen and clear intraluminal clots or precipitates that could impede subsequent dye flow. Injections were delivered manually using gentle, low-pressure strokes, just sufficient to advance the fluid column without visible over-distension or rupture. The needle was inserted into the cut lumen of the thoracic duct held by flat head forceps. Once inserted, pressure was applied using forceps to create a seal and prevent retrograde overflow (Fig. 1b). This initial primer injection facilitated vessel expansion, providing a clearer pathway for subsequent dye application into the left jugular trunk. Given the fragility of the thoracic duct, a second cut was made at the left jugular trunk, distal to its junction with the thoracic duct (Point 2 on Fig. 1a). This was followed by a secondary primer injection of 20 mL of 5% hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) and subsequent 3:1 mixture of 5% H₂O₂ and green Rit dye (50 mL) injection into the left jugular trunk lumen. Green dye was utilized upon the realization of the lack of efficacy of blue and black dyes. Blue dye provided difficulty in distinguishing venous vasculature from lymphatic vasculature, while black dye and peroxide mixture resulted in small amounts of precipitated solute. If lymphatic vessel rupture occurred, resulting in the migration of dye outside of lymphatic circulation, cyanoacrylate adhesive (super glue) was applied to seal the site of leakage. Injection would proceed after the super glue dried. In the event of super glue causing lumen obstruction, the lymphatic vessel was cut just proximal to the rupture site, and injection continued at the new lumen site. The human body donor was left overnight with the thorax in an elevated position, allowing the dye to flow towards the head (Fig. 1C). Because formalin fixation stiffens lymphatic walls and valves and the cervical network contains multiple nodal and valvular stations, retrograde filling was not expected to be complete; in this study, the peroxide-dye served as an opportunistic tracer to highlight a subset of patent lymphatic pathways that could then be sampled for histology, rather than as comprehensive lymphangiographic mapping.

Two primary observation methods were employed to assess dye migration and lymphatic structures. In one approach, the calvaria was removed to visualize the skull base from a superior view (Fig. 2a). In the second approach, tissue containing dyed vessels was retained and reflected, and the neck was transected (Fig. 2b). The head and neck were bisected along the midline to observe dye distribution in midline structures. Tissue containing the thoracic duct and any dyed vessels was visually inspected and dissected using a “follow the vessel” technique, allowing for careful retention of any vessel segments that had taken up the dye. Any dyed vessels that appeared to grossly exit the cranial or spinal cavities were excised at their most proximal entry point, along with surrounding tissue, for histological analysis. The head and neck were also investigated for any dye migration.

The harvested tissue samples were fixed in 70% ethanol, and decalcification using formic acid was performed for bone-containing sections. Standard Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) staining was applied to visualize tissue morphology. For dyed vessels located in or traveling into the cervical vertebral canal, immunostaining with D2-40, a monoclonal antibody that targets transmembrane protein podoplanin of lymphatic endothelial cells, was used to confirm lymphatic identity.

Formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded tissue blocks containing dyed vessels were sectioned at 4 μm and mounted on charged glass slides. Deparaffinization, rehydration, and heat-induced epitope retrieval (HIER) were performed in a Dako PT Link instrument (PT100/PT101) using diluted EnVision FLEX Target Retrieval Solution, High pH (9.0, 50×, K8004). Slides were pre-heated to 85 °C, subjected to epitope retrieval at 97 °C for 20 min, cooled to 65 °C, and then placed in diluted EnVision FLEX Wash Buffer (20×, K8007) for 5 min.

Immunostaining was carried out on a Dako Autostainer Link 48 using a ready-to-use mouse monoclonal anti-podoplanin antibody (D2-40, clone D2-40, Dako IR072). Sections were incubated with FLEX Peroxidase Blocking solution (SM801) for 5 min, followed by D2-40 for 20 min, FLEX Mouse Linker (SM804) for 20 min, FLEX HRP (SM802) for 20 min, FLEX DAB Substrate-Chromogen (SM803) for 10 min, and FLEX Hematoxylin (SM806) for 5 min. Between each step, slides were washed in EnVision FLEX Wash Buffer for 5 min to remove residual reagent. Expected staining for D2-40 is membranous in lymphatic endothelium, with possible cytoplasmic/perinuclear staining in other cell types such as myoepithelial cells.

Discussion

In this study, lymphatic vessels were identified along cervical spinal nerves and related perineural tissues at the level of intervertebral foramina and adjacent cervical soft tissues. Our retrograde cadaveric dye-tracing and D2-40 immunohistochemistry identify podoplanin-positive lymphatic vessels in perineural and dural compartments at cervical levels, particularly along spinal nerves at the C2–C3 intervertebral foramen and in the lateral neck near the brachial plexus. Dyed, IHC-unconfirmed retro-nasopharyngeal regions, including tissue inferior to the sphenoid sinus and inferior clivus, suggest additional potential cranial base drainage routes in humans, but these sites remain inferential in this cohort and require future histologic confirmation.​

Rzepliński et al. schematically summarized their findings as two spinal CSF outflow pathways: a perineural route along spinal nerves, with tracer reaching the endoneurial and perineurial spaces and then epidural vessels, and a meningeal route involving arachnoid granulations, intradural cuffs, and vessels interpreted as venous and/or lymphatic outflow channels. Our findings are conceptually aligned with this framework but with retrograde injection into the cervical region and emphasize the presence of D2-40–positive lymphatic vessels distal to dorsal root ganglia.​

An important interpretive caveat is that D2-40 can also label perineurium, and some of the podoplanin-positive profiles in our sections lie immediately adjacent to perineurial sheaths (Jokinen et al. 2008). In these cases, the D2-40–positive structures typically form discrete, thin-walled lumina within the connective tissue sleeve surrounding the nerve, rather than solid, layered bands following the full circumference of the fascicles, suggesting that they reside within the epineurium in close apposition to the perineurium. We therefore interpret many of these profiles as intra-epineural lymphatic vessels closely bordering the perineurium, while acknowledging that some degree of perineurial staining cannot be excluded without additional markers.​ Additionally, because the dye was delivered retrogradely through the thoracic duct–jugular lymphatic trunk rather than anterogradely from the subarachnoid space, only lymphatic channels that remain anatomically continuous with peripheral lymphatics filled, implying that the dyed cervical dural and epineurial vessels we identified are parts of a patent peripheral lymphatic network rather than isolated, blind-ending channels.

The glymphatic system, comprised of perivascular channels supported by glial cells, has been proposed to clear interstitial solutes from brain parenchyma and deliver them to CSF and meningeal lymphatics (Pinho-Correia et al. 2025; Smyth et al. 2025). In humans, MRI and tracer studies indicate that CSF can exit the cranial vault along cranial nerves and dural lymphatic pathways toward deep cervical lymph nodes (dcLNs) (Albayram et al. 2022; Zhang et al. 2025). Our cervical findings are anatomically consistent with these models, in that lymphatic vessels accompany spinal nerves as they exit the vertebral canal and traverse intervertebral foramina. However, our study did not directly visualize CSF or intradural lymphatic channels. As such, any implications for CSF outflow or glymphatic clearance should be regarded as hypotheses that require dedicated functional and imaging studies.

Building on this anatomical positioning and integrating prior evidence across the neurofluid literature, we propose the Cerebrolymph Hypothesis, a unified anatomical framework describing the complete circuit of neurofluid homeostasis encompassing CSF production, parenchymal exchange, and extracranial clearance (Fig. 11). In this model, oxygenated blood enters the cranial cavity through the internal carotid and vertebral arteries and perfuses the choroid plexus through respective choroidal branches, producing CSF and create a functional Blood-CSF barrier (BCSFB) (Bitanihirwe et al. 2022).

CSF circulates through the ventricular system, where circumventricular organs (CVOs), specialized structures lacking a blood–brain barrier, line the ventricular walls and regulate CSF composition through sensory and secretory functions (Kaur and Ling 2017). Between the CVOs and the CSF of the ventricles is a layer of specialized ependymal cells called tanycytes that form tight junctions, thereby creating a BCSF barrier, similar to the choroid plexus (Langlet et al. 2013). Ventricular CSF then flows into the subarachnoid space via the median and lateral apertures of the fourth ventricle (Ducros and Biousse 2015). Within the subarachnoid compartment, CSF is positioned to participate in glymphatic exchange: arterial perivascular inflow, driven by cardiac pulsations and vasomotion, delivers CSF into the parenchyma across astrocytic endfeet at the glia limitans, promoting interstitial solute clearance (Kipnis et al. 2025). Venous perivascular outflow, modulated by respiratory rhythms, returns solute-rich fluid to the subarachnoid space (Kipnis et al. 2025).

At the subarachnoid–dural interface of the arachnoid mater, cleared fluid exits via two coordinated pathways: (1) reabsorption into the dural venous sinuses through arachnoid granulations, and (2) meningeal lymphatic uptake through both arachnoid granulations and arachnoid cuff exit (ACE) points (Zhang et al. 2025). We introduce the term “cerebro-lymphatic barrier” (CLB) to denote the CSF fraction that not only drains into meningeal lymphatic vessels, but lymphatic fraction that may also dynamically communicate with the subarachnoid pool. Evidence for retrograde or countercurrent meningeal lymphatic flow is emerging but limited, and most data are indirect or limited. A 2024 in vivo mouse study injected nanoparticles into the deep cervical lymph nodes and tracked their movement back toward the meninges and brain, explicitly defining this as “retrograde directional flow” along the cervical–meningeal lymphatic network (Ramos-Zaldívar et al. 2024). Human MRI work has shown that flow in dorsal mLVs adjacent to superior sagittal sinus runs posterior-to-anterior, opposite and countercurrent to venous blood flow (Kuo et al. 2018). Most notably, ACE points have been characterized as bidirectional interfaces along bridging veins where discontinuities in the arachnoid barrier permit both cerebrospinal fluid drainage into the dura and selective molecular or cellular entry from the dura into the subarachnoid space, thereby linking CNS clearance and immune communication pathway (Smyth et al. 2024).

Our human postmortem immunohistochemical analysis in the present study demonstrates that D2‑40–positive, nerve‑adjacent lymphatic vessels (naLVs) located at the craniovertebral junction and cervical intervertebral foramina are anatomically positioned as the extracranial continuation of meningeal lymphatic outflow, supporting a putative linkage between dural drainage and deep cervical lymph nodes. Each upstream component of this circuit aligns with established literature, while the naLV step represents the specific anatomical contribution defined by our observations.

The Cerebrolymph Hypothesis is a synthesizing framework directly addressing the anatomical circuit by which CSF is produced, circulates through the CNS, and exits into peripheral lymphatic circulation. CSF is produced by the choroid plexus (1), with circumventricular organs modulating CSF composition, and circulates through the ventricular system (2). Via median and lateral apertures, CSF exits into the subarachnoid space (3). CSF enters perivascular spaces along cerebral arteries, crossing astrocyte end-feet into brain parenchyma to facilitate glymphatic waste clearance (4); solute-laden fluid returns via venous perivascular spaces, modulated by respiration. Cleared fluid drains through two parallel exit routes: dural venous sinuses and meningeal lymphatic vessels (5). The present study positions cervical nerve-adjacent lymphatic vessels (naLVs) as the extracranial continuation of meningeal lymphatic outflow, draining into deep cervical lymph nodes (6), the specific anatomical step supported by the D2-40 immunohistochemical findings reported here, and should be interpreted as a conceptual synthesis rather than a proven flow pathway. This study has important limitations that constrain how the findings should be interpreted. First, the use of formalin-fixed, embalmed donors and retrograde dye injection into the thoracic duct and jugular lymphatic trunk provides only a partial and non-physiologic view of patent channels; we could not assess dynamic flow, valve function, or in vivo filling patterns. Second, we did not directly trace continuity between subarachnoid CSF spaces and the D2-40–positive vessels described, so our observations should be viewed as an anatomical description of lymphatic-like vessels at cervical CNS exit sites rather than as a functional demonstration of CSF outflow. Future studies in fresh or lightly fixed tissue, incorporating systematic H&E correlation and multiplex lymphatic and neural markers (e.g., LYVE-1, PROX1, SMA), will be essential to definitively resolve vessel identity at these boundary sites.

Resource constraints, including limited dissection time, a small number of cadaveric donors, and restricted funds for comprehensive histological and immunohistochemical processing, prevented analysis of all potentially relevant samples. The novelty and evolving nature of the injection protocol meant that some preparations were less informative as dye techniques were optimized by trial and error, and anatomical variation among donors further limits generalizability. Accessing lymphatic vessels via dissection often required removing surrounding tissues, which may have disrupted native relationships or damaged delicate vessels and thereby influenced dye migration. Dye migration failures could also reflect undetected intraluminal blockages or suboptimal primer/dye volumes, which we were unable to systematically investigate. Because specimens were formalin-fixed and processed after dye injection, some intraluminal dye was likely lost or redistributed during fixation, decalcification, and sectioning, which may have reduced our ability to visualize dye microscopically. Finally, grossly dyed cranial base and nasopharyngeal regions in Donor 4 were not subjected to IHC, and any inferences about cranial drainage from these sites must therefore be regarded as preliminary.

Although all D2-40–positive structures were evaluated morphologically on routine sections, we did not systematically present paired H&E images for each immunolabeled vessel in this report. As such, while wall architecture and luminal profiles are consistent with lymphatic identity, additional markers and comprehensive H&E–IHC paneling will be important in future work to definitively exclude perineurial or other non-lymphatic structures. Despite these limitations, the present work offers a novel anatomical perspective on lymphatic vessels associated with cervical spinal nerves and highlights concrete directions for refining techniques and testing mechanistic hypotheses in future studies.

Conclusion

This cadaveric study identifies D2-40–positive vessels, morphologically consistent with lymphatic channels, closely associated with cervical spinal nerves and brachial plexus roots in human donors at the craniovertebral junction and along the lateral neck. These findings provide anatomical evidence that lymphatic vasculature extends along spinal nerves toward the interior of the cervical vertebral canal and approaches CNS boundary structures, supporting the concept that lymphatic networks are positioned to interface with, but remain distinct from, central nervous system compartments. While our observations are consistent with proposed models in which glymphatic and meningeal lymphatic pathways cooperate to clear interstitial solutes and support immune surveillance, they do not directly demonstrate cerebrospinal fluid outflow or intradural lymphatic channels.

To build upon these initial findings, future studies should refine and standardize methodologies for tracing CNS-adjacent lymphatic pathways across cervical, thoracic, lumbar, and sacral segments; integrate high-resolution imaging with systematic H&E and multiplex immunohistochemistry to better define vessel identity and smooth muscle investment; and compare subcranial and spinal lymphatic anatomy across health and disease states. Such work will be essential to clarify how lymphatic vessels at CNS boundary sites contribute to fluid clearance, immune cell trafficking, and the pathophysiology of neurological and autoimmune disorders, and may ultimately inform therapeutic strategies that leverage lymphatic flow to improve CNS health. The Cerebrolymph hypothesis, encompassing glymphatic exchange, parallel meningeal lymphatic and venous CNS exit, and extracranial cervical lymphatic drainage, provides a conceptual anatomical scaffold for that future work, rather than a demonstration of any specific fluid pathway.
