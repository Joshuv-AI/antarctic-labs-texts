Clinical efficacy and safety of modified tracer fixation technique in robot-assisted percutaneous pedicle screw fixation for thoracolumbar fractures
Authors: Gen Li, Junwei Zhang, Zhongfeng Zhou, Kai Huang, Chunjiu Gao, Li Zheng, Hua Li, Yunqing Wang, Li Jing, Feng Yuan, Xuebin Tang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThis study aims to compare the clinical efficacy and safety of the modified skin-fixed tracer with spinous process-fixed tracer in robot-assisted percutaneous pedicle screw (PPS) fixation for thoracolumbar fractures.MethodsA total of 98 patients with thoracolumbar fractures undergoing robot-assisted PPS fixation were retrospectively recruited from April 2023 to June 2024 in the Second Affiliated Hospital of Xuzhou Medical University, including 46 in skin-fixed tracer group and 52 in spinous process-fixed tracer group. In skin-fixed group, there were 20 males and 26 females, with an average age of 44.0 ± 8.7 years (range 19–59 years); In spinous process-fixed group, there were 30 males and 22 females, with an average age of 42.7 ± 8.9 years (range 19–58 years). Clinical outcomes were assessed by preoperative and postoperative (day 7, 3-month and 6-month) Visual Analogue Scale (VAS), anterior vertebral height ratio (AVHR), Cobb angle, and Oswestry Disability Index (ODI). Surgical indicators included tracer fixation time, total duration of surgery, intraoperative blood loss, number of intraoperative fluoroscopy, and length of incision. Besides, Gertzbein-Robbins classification was used to evaluate the accuracy of PPS fixation.ResultsAll surgeries were successfully completed under intraoperative neurophysiologic monitoring (IONM) without complications of spinal cord injuries. Patients were followed up for 6 to 14 months, with an average follow-up time of 10.2 ± 2.8 months. Two groups showed markedly improvements (P < 0.05) in postoperative VAS, AVHR, Cobb angle, and ODI, but no statistical intergroup differences were found (P > 0.05). Compared with spinous process-fixed group, skin-fixed group had significantly shorter tracer fixation and surgical duration, less intraoperative blood loss and lower length of incision, with all P values less than 0.05. However, the two groups had similar numbers of intraoperative fluoroscopy and accuracy rates of PPS fixation, with both P values greater than 0.05.ConclusionIn robot-assisted PPS fixation for thoracolumbar fractures, the modified skin-fixed tracer has the same safety and accuracy as spinous process-fixed tracer, but can shorten tracer fixation and surgical duration, reduce length of incision, decrease intraoperative blood loss, as well as promote pain relief and functional recovery. Thus, skin-fixed tracer is a safe and feasible method that is worthy of promotion and application.

Introduction

Due to the increasing frequency of motor accidents and falls from heights recently, incidence of thoracolumbar fractures is growing year by year, which not only seriously endangers both physical and mental health of patients, but aggravates their economic burden. Surgical treatment for thoracolumbar fractures relies on strong internal fixation to provide a good long-term effect on fracture healing. With the advantages of minimally invasive, quick recovery and low complication rate, percutaneous pedicle screw (PPS) fixation has become the mainstream of spinal surgery for thoracolumbar fractures [1]. However, repeated fluoroscopy is required to determine screw position in PPS, which increases the overall operation time, and seriously affects the physical health of patients and surgeons as well [2, 3]. Therefore, it is urgent to seek a treatment modality with more efficiency, safety and precision.

With the continuous development of robot technology, robot-assisted orthopedic surgery is widely acknowledged as its superior clinical efficacy and safety in terms of high accuracy, less radiation exposure and trauma, and it has been commonly applied in treating spinal compression fractures [4, 5]. During robot-assisted PPS fixation surgery, three-dimensional fluoroscopy and spine navigation provide surgeons with real-time coronal, axial and sagittal reconstructions to further improve the accuracy of pedicle screw placement. However, the limitations of traditional tracer fixation method cannot be ignored.

Currently, The tracer is usually fixed on the spinous processes of the vertebrae adjacent to the injured vertebrae, which not only increases the number of surgical incisions, but also carries the potential risk of additional tissue damage, resulting in aggravated postoperative pain and prolonged recovery [6]. In order to solve this problem, research team proposed a modified tracer fixation in clinical practice and achieved preliminary results [7]. This latest fixation method can reduce both incidence of unnecessary surgical trauma and surgical complications.

By retrospectively recruiting and analyzing patients with thoracolumbar fractures after robot-assisted PPS fixation, this study aims to compare the feasibility and safety of the modified skin-fixed tracer with spinous process-fixed tracer in treating thoracolumbar fractures, thus to promote the development of robot-assisted orthopedic surgery.

Materials and methods

General data

Patients with thoracolumbar fractures undergoing robot-assisted PPS fixation were retrospectively recruited from April 2023 to June 2024 in the Second Affiliated Hospital of Xuzhou Medical University. All patients underwent only percutaneous pedicle screw (PPS) internal fixation for thoracolumbar fractures, without any decompression procedures. Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) single vertebra fracture, either thoracic or lumbar spine; (2) new fracture within 2 weeks confirmed by MRI; (3) Thoracolumbar Injury Classification and Severity Score (TLICS) 4 points and above, with surgical indications; (4) aged 18–60 years; (5) follow-up time 6 months and longer; (6) complete data including imaging results, clinical information and follow-up data. Patients were excluded if (1) they had spinal cord nerve compression accompanied by neurological symptoms; (2) they had comorbid local spinal infection or systemic active infection; (3) they were combined with spinal tumor and spinal tuberculosis; (4) they had osteoporosis; (5) they had coagulation dysfunction or could not cooperate; (6) they were complicated with severe underlying or autoimmune diseases that could not meet the anesthesia conditions or tolerate the surgery; (7) they had missing data that could not support the analysis.

A total of 98 thoracolumbar fractures patients were eligible, and were then divided into skin-fixed group and spinous process-fixed group according to the method of tracer fixation. Among the 46 cases in skin-fixed group, 20 were males and 26 were females, with an average age of 44.0 ± 8.7 years (range 19–59 years) and body mass index (BMI) of 22.86 ± 4.47. In terms of fracture segments, 6 cases were at T10, 10 at T11, 12 at T12, 14 at L1, and 4 at L2. All injuries were high-energy trauma, including 30 falls and 16 motor accidents. Among the 52 cases in spinous process-fixed group, 30 were males and 22 were females, with an average age of 42.7 ± 8.9 years (range 19–58 years) and BMI of 22.56 ± 5.55. In terms of fracture segments, 10 cases were at T10, 12 at T11, 14 at T12, 8 at L1, and 8 at L2. All injuries were high-energy trauma, including 32 falls and 20 motor accidents. As shown in Table 1, no significant differences were found in general characteristics between the two groups, with all P values greater than 0.05.

Surgical process

The surgeries of both groups of patients were performed by an experienced associate chief physician under intraoperative neurophysiologic monitoring (IONM). Screw and titanium rod were provided by Weigao Group Co.,Ltd. Orthopedic robotic system was TiRobot II developed by Beijing Jishuitan Hospital and Tinavi Medical Technologies Co., Ltd.

Skin-fixed group: After general anesthesia with intubation, catheterization was performed and IONM was connected. Patients were in prone position, with protection of forehead, face, eyes, knees and ankles, and a soft pillow was placed under abdomen to suspend it from squeezing. The fractured vertebra and the adjacent vertebral bodies were located and marked through C-arm fluoroscopy. Routine disinfection and draping were performed and aseptic adhesive film was used to secure the tracer to the skin (Fig. 1), the specific method for attaching the tracer to the skin is as follows: Prior to disinfection, perform fluoroscopic localization and mark the fracture site. Then disinfect the area with iodophor, deiodize using alcohol, apply an antibacterial drape, and finally fix the tracer at a relatively flat area located 2–3 vertebral segments adjacent to the fractured vertebra. TiRobot II was then activated and calibrated, a reference ruler was installed. Centered on the injured vertebra, 3D scan was conducted using C-arm to collect the data, followed by information upload to robotic workstation. Puncture path and nail placement angle were planned, after which, the robotic arm was automatically directed to a the designated location in sequence. Guide pin was inserted using electric drill, once the correct position was identified by fluoroscopy, gradual cannula expansion and a guidewire insertion were conducted. Cannulated screws were placed (universal screws for injured vertebra and fixing screws for the adjacent vertebra) in the direction of the guide pin. A appropriate length of pre-bent titanium rod was then placed, fixed the distal end and distracted the proximal end percutaneously, tightened the proximal end cap, and finally tightened the end cap of the injured vertebra. Suture incision and dressing compression bandaging were carried out after vertebral height restoration and good position of pedicle screw confirmed by fluoroscopy. Surgery completed.

Spinous process-fixed group: The surgical steps were basically the same as those of the skin-fixed group, with the main difference lying in the tracer fixation procedure. First, a small vertical incision was made and the fascia was cut by electrotome. Second, paraspinal muscles were separated until the spinous process was exposed. Last, spinous process clamp was used to secure the tracer to the spinous process. See Fig. 2.

Postoperative management

Patients were admitted to the ward after operation and received oxygen inhalation, finger pulse oxygen monitoring, ECG monitoring, air pressure pump to prevent thrombosis, routine use of flurbiprofen injection for analgesic treatment, and preventive use of antibiotics. Patients were allowed to roll over in bed and were instructed to ankle pump exercise, straight leg raising and other functional exercises. Observed the dressing on incision every day (2–3 days dressing change). Appropriate out-of-bed activities were allowed with waist circumference one week after operation, and thoracolumbar DR and CT were performed. About 7–10 days after operation, patients with good incision healing and basically normal reexamination indicators could be discharged for recuperation. Regular outpatient follow-up visits were conducted at 3, 6 and 12 months after discharge to determine if the internal fixation could be removed.

Observation indicators

General information: demographics were recorded, including gender, age, BMI, cause of injury, and fracture segments.

Perioperative data: (1) total duration of surgery: the time from completion of disinfection draping to complete closure of incision; (2) tracer fixation time: (a) for skin-fixed group, this was defined as the time to fix tracer on skin near surgical field with adhesive film (around 2 min); (b) for spinous process-fixed group, this was defined as the time to touch the spinous process, incise the skin and subcutaneous fascia, separate the paravertebral muscles along the side facing the spinous process bone by electrotome and bone stripping, expose and install the clamp at the root of spinous process, and assemble and fix the tracer on the clamp (around 8 min); (3) intraoperative blood loss; (4) length of incision: measured the incision with sterile ruler after closing the surgical incision; (5) number of intraoperative fluoroscopy.

Clinical indicators: Preoperative and postoperative (day 7, 3-month and 6-month) Visual Analogue Scale (VAS), anterior vertebral height ratio (AVHR), Cobb angle, and Oswestry Disability Index (ODI). Besides, Gertzbein-Robbins classification was used to evaluate the accuracy of PPS fixation: (1) Grade A: no cortical breach; (2) Grade B: the screw breaches the pedicle cortex < 2 mm; (3) Grade C: 2 mm ≤ the screw breaches the pedicle cortex < 4 mm; (4) Grade D: 4 mm ≤ the screw breaches the pedicle cortex < 6 mm; (5) Grade E: the screw breaches the pedicle cortex ≥ 6 mm. Accuracy rate of PPS fixation = number of Grade A screws / total screws.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (x ± s) for normally distributed ones, while intergroup comparison were conducted using t test. Categorical variables were described as frequencies and percentages, while intergroup comparison were conducted using chi-square test. The threshold for significance was set at P < 0.05. All statistical analyses were processed by SPSS, Version 27.0.

Results

General information

All patients were followed up for at least 6 months. No significant baseline differences were found between two groups in terms of gender, age, BMI, cause of injury, and fracture segment, with all P values greater than 0.05. See Table 1 for details.

Intraoperative results

All surgeries in 98 patients were successfully completed without serious complications. Compared with spinous process-fixed group, skin-fixed group had significantly shorter tracer fixation and surgical duration, less intraoperative blood loss and lower length of incision, with all P values less than 0.05 (Table 2, Fig. 3). However, the two groups had similar numbers of intraoperative fluoroscopy and accuracy rates of PPS fixation, with both P values greater than 0.05 (Table 3).

Discussion

As one of the vertebral fractures, thoracolumbar fracture is commonly seen in skeletally fragile population caused by trauma and osteoporosis. It is mainly manifested as collapse, rupture and instability of the spinal vertebral body, and may cause irreversible damage to neurological function in severe cases, thus affecting quality of life [8]. According to traumatic mechanism, thoracolumbar fracture can be categorized into compression fractures and burst fractures, of which the latter remains more difficult to treat because it will potentially lead to spinal canal compromise due to the vertebra is crushed in all directions [9]. Treatments of thoracolumbar fractures mainly include conservative and surgical treatments. The former method primarily relies on bed rest, medication, wearable braces and other methods to relieve symptoms, but may have limited effect for some patients, especially those with nerve compression. Therefore, surgical treatment may become the first choice for such patients [10].

As a classical approach in PPS fixation, posterior median approach can reconstruct the three-column stability of the spine early and promote fracture healing. However, on the other hand, as it requires dissection of massive muscles on both sides of the paravertebral region, intraoperative trauma and large bleeding volume, as well as postoperative scar adhesions and chronic low back pain may occur [11]. On the basis of the classical approach, Wiltse paraspinal approach has been widely used in thoracolumbar fracture surgery because of its advantages of protecting joint capsule and muscles, reducing low back pain, and rapid recovery from trauma [12, 13]. With the development of medical technology, through location by C-arm machine, PPS fixation makes a small incision and places screws into the vertebral body by guide pin, with superiorities of less trauma and rapid recovery, so it has been widely applied in clinical practice. However, such technique has certain drawbacks, especially the de-visualization of screw placement process compared with open surgery, so for some patients with atypical or pedicle [14], repeated fluoroscopy may be required to adjust the screw placement angle. Even senior chief physicians with rich experience cannot completely overcome the physiological limitations, thereby there exists a possibility of nerve and blood vessel injury caused by perforating the inner wall of pedicle. Therefore, under the concept of minimally invasive, precise, rapid and less traumatic PPS remains the future development direction.

In recent years, with the progress of science and technology, orthopedic surgical robots have shown their unique advantages in PPS fixation for thoracolumbar fractures in terms of improving the accuracy of surgery while reducing the number of intraoperative fluoroscopy and the incidence of complications, which further facilitating the postoperative recovery. In line with the minimally invasive concept, robot-assisted surgery has become the mainstream treatment of thoracolumbar fractures [15, 16]. Kuntelhardt et al. [17] reported that accuracy rate of robot-assisted PPS and conventional freehand screw placement was 94.5% and 91.4%, separately. Compared with the traditional freehand method, robot-assisted PPS fixation had significant reductions in analgesic medication use, mean postoperative hospital stay and incidence of adverse events. In this study, when planning the pedicle screw path, the planned screw tail position could be visualized on the main console to make sure a straight line in the coronal position to facilitate the penetration of the titanium rod. This is also named as three-screw first-line rod penetration method, which can decrease surgical trauma and surgical duration compared with the traditional C-arm fluoroscopy rod penetration.

In robot-assisted orthopedic surgery, location and placement of tracer play an important role. Traditional iliac spine or spinous process tracer fixation has certain degree of trauma because it requires an additional incision to fix the tracer on patient’s body surface, which will not only increase the difficulty and time of surgery, but trigger some postoperative complications and is not conducive to rapid postoperative rehabilitation [6]. In addition, bone-fixed tracer may be mistouched during the operation, resulting in the change or even detachment of the position, thus affecting the accuracy of screw placement [18]. Previous studies indicated that tracers might receive the effect of deep muscle tissue traction when fixed to bone by spinous process clamp, ultimately reducing the precision of screw placement [19].

To address issues mentioned above, our team proposed a modified skin-fixed tracer method, that is, the tracer was directly attached to the skin with an adhesive film, which could reduce the effect of skin deformation on tracker position while avoiding additional trauma. One of the most important advantages of this modified fixation lies in its easy operation in placing tracer under guidance of robotic arms, especially benefits patients with obesity or difficult exposure of bony structures [20].

Previous research has confirmed that the accuracy rate of robot-assisted PPS fixation by spinous process-fixed tracer can raise to 95.3%. Our study reported high accuracy rates of screw placement in both skin-fixed tracer group (97.83%) and spinous process-fixed tracer group (98.08%), demonstrating safety and reliability of the modified method. Furthermore, compared with spinous process-fixed group, skin-fixed group had significantly shorter tracer fixation and surgical duration, less intraoperative blood loss and lower length of incision, suggesting that skin-fixed tracer might be conducive to postoperative recovery.

Both groups showed significant improvements in postoperative VAS and ODI at day 7, 3-month and 6-month, but no statistical intergroup differences were found at these time points. The above results pointed out the satisfied clinical efficacy of both groups in pain relief and functional recovery status. Our findings showed that postoperative VAS in skin-fixed group were consistently lower than those in the spinous process-fixed group at any time point, revealing that the invasive spinous process-fixed method might aggravate the pain in the early postoperative period and even lead to chronic low back pain [21], which could be related to the damage and dissection of circumflex muscle and multifidus tendon attached to both sides of the spinous process during spinous process clamp fixation. Klingler [22] found that a skin noninvasive tracker (SpineMask) contributed to intraoperative 3D navigation without the need for additional skin incisions for muscle separation on both sides of the spinous process, however this required a larger intraoperative 3D image dataset for accurate registration, resulting in 2.25-fold higher radiation exposure to the patient. The two groups in our study had statistically similar numbers of intraoperative fluoroscopy, indicating no significant radiation exposure to patients and surgeons during skin-fixed operation.

There have some limitations in this study. First, despite the comparative analysis, the small sample size limited the generalization of our results to different regions or types of patients, studies with larger sample size are still required. Second, our study population focused on those who recently received treatment, so the short study period limited the observation of long-term efficacy and complications. For this medical intervention with long-term follow-up needs, the short-term observation results might not completely reflect the actual effect, and longer follow-up time is needed in the future. In summary, subsequent studies should consider increasing the sample size and extending the follow-up time in order to obtain more accurate and comprehensive scientific evidence.

In conclusion, without additional trauma, skin-fixed method can ensure the accurate tracer placement at the predetermined position, thereby guiding the robot to perform precise internal fixation operations. The modified skin-fixed tracer has the same safety and accuracy as the traditional spinous process-fixed tracer, but can shorten tracer fixation and surgical duration, reduce length of incision, decrease intraoperative blood loss, as well as promote pain relief and functional recovery. Thus, skin-fixed tracer is a safe and feasible method that is worthy of promotion and application.
