Recovering Communities in Structured Random Graphs
Michael Kapralov, Luca Trevisan, Weronika Wrzos-Kaminska

Abstract
The problem of recovering planted community structure in random graphs has received a lot of attention in the literature on the stochastic block model, where the input is a random graph in which edges crossing between different communities appear with smaller probability than edges induced by communities. The communities themselves form a collection of vertex-disjoint sparse cuts in the expected graph, and can be recovered, often exactly, from a sample as long as a separation condition on the intra- and inter-community edge probabilities is satisfied. In this paper, we ask whether the presence of a large number of overlapping sparsest cuts in the expected graph still allows recovery. For example, the d d -dimensional hypercube graph admits d d distinct (balanced) sparsest cuts, one for every coordinate. Can these cuts be identified given a random sample of the edges of the hypercube where each edge is present independently with some probability p ∈ ( 0 , 1 ) p\in(0,1) ? We show that this is the case, in a very strong sense: the sparsest balanced cut in a sample of the hypercube at rate p = C ​ log ⁡ d / d p=C\log d/d for a sufficiently large constant C C is 1 / poly ​ ( d ) 1/\text{poly}(d) -close to a coordinate cut with high probability. This is asymptotically optimal and allows approximate recovery of all d d cuts simultaneously. Furthermore, for an appropriate sample of hypercube-like graphs recovery can be made exact . The proof is essentially a strong hypercube cut sparsification bound that combines a theorem of Friedgut, Kalai and Naor on boolean functions whose Fourier transform concentrates on the first level of the Fourier spectrum with Karger’s cut counting argument.

Introduction

Graph clustering, or community detection, is a fundamental problem in data analysis. The input is a graph
G=(V,E)
, where a subset
C\subseteq V
of vertices is considered a ‘community’ if it is sparsely connected to the rest of the graph and is reasonably well-connected as an induced subgraph. The task is to recover the communities.

Graph clustering with a planted solution has received a lot of attention in the literature. In this setting the vertex set
V
of the graph
G
is assumed to be partitioned into vertex disjoint clusters
C_{1},C_{2},\ldots,C_{k}
such that the clusters induce well-connected subgraphs and are sparsely connected to the rest of the graph
[
26
,
18
,
31
,
33
,
34
,
12
,
2
,
17
,
15
,
25
]
. For example, in the stochastic block model (SBM;
[
5
]
) edges of
G
are generated independently, where an edge
\{u,v\}
is included in the graph with higher probability if
u
and
v
belong to the same cluster, and lower probability otherwise. A large body of work on the stochastic block model shows that, if the edge probabilities satisfy a separation condition, the communities
C_{1},C_{2},\ldots,C_{k}
can be recovered from a sample graph with high probability. Determining the exact recovery threshold is a fascinating information theoretic problem for which tight bounds have been obtained over the past two decades
[
16
,
32
,
37
,
14
,
34
,
2
,
4
]
. Most of the work on SBM has focused on the case of non-overlapping communities, with only a few works allowing for some overlap. At the same time, in the practice of graph clustering one typically does not expects to have very pronounced cluster. Instead, several clusterings of the vertex set may be consistent with the edge set of the graph. Our central question in this paper is:

Can highly overlapping clusterings be recovered from a sample of the underlying graph?

Perhaps the most basic example of a graph with a large number of overlapping communities is the hypercube graph on
n=2^{d}
vertices, where each of
d
coordinate cuts is a sparsest cut, and defines a partition of the vertex set into two ‘communities’, namely the two corresponding coordinate halfspaces. This setting is very different from the SBM with two communities, where the expected graph is a union of two cliques on the two clusters and a clique on the entire vertex set, and therefore the two communities are uniquely defined. Formally, the main question we ask in this paper is a structured version of the stochastic block model that allows for many communities with large overlaps:

Can coordinate cuts be recovered from the edge set of a subsampled hypercube?

A priori it would seem plausible that cuts of sparsity comparable to the coordinate cuts may emerge in a subsampled hypercube. Intuitively, this could be a mixture of several coordinate cuts in the original cube (a similar effect is seen in rounding the SDP solution to the sparsest cut problem on the hypercube). However, we show that this is not the case:

Theorem 1
.

Let
Q_{d}
be the
d

Q^{\prime}_{d}
be obtained by including each edge with probability
p\geq C\cdot\log d/d
, where
C
is a sufficiently large constant. There exists an algorithm with running time
2^{O(n\log n)}

that, given
Q^{\prime}_{d}
, recovers
d
orthogonal balanced cuts, each with Hamming distance
O(2^{d}/\mathrm{poly}(d))
to a coordinate cut, with probability at least
1-d^{-100}
over the subsampling.

We say that a cut
A\subseteq V
is

|A|=|V|/2
. The Hamming distance between two sets

is given by
|A\triangle B|
, and we say

Definition 2

(Hypercube)

Define the
d

to be the graph

with vertex set
V=\{0,1\}^{d}
, and any two vertices are connected by an edge if their Hamming distance is exactly

.
We let

We note that the subsampling rate in Theorem

C\log d=C\log\log n

factors, since the expected number of isolated vertices in

is at least
2^{d}/\mathrm{poly}\left(d\right)

Q^{\prime}_{d}
is distributed as
\mathrm{Bin}(d,p)

, the probability that a given vertex is isolated is

(1-p)^{d}=\left(1-\frac{C\log d}{d}\right)^{d}\;\geq\;e^{-C\log d}\left(1-\tfrac{(C\log d)^{2}}{d}\right)=\frac{1}{\mathrm{poly}\left(d\right)}.

So in expectation, at least

Exact recovery.

Furthermore, we show that, similarly to SBM, exact recovery is possible for a sufficiently high degree sample, specifically, a sample where every vertex has expected degree at least

Let

Open problem.

Our work leaves open a very exciting open problem of recovering coordinate cuts with the same precision as our results, but in polynomial time. The SoS hierarchy seems to be a promising direction.

Proof of Theorem
1

In this section we prove Theorem
1
. The proof is algorithmic. We state the (simple) algorithm below, and then proceed to analyze it.

The algorithm.

Our algorithm finds
d
orthogonal sparse cuts in the subsampled hypercube by solving the optimization problem (solved by direct enumeration in time
2^{O(n\log n)}
):

\displaystyle\min

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{d}|E^{\prime}(A_{i},V\setminus A_{i})|\qquad\text{subject to}

(1)

\displaystyle|A_{i}|=2^{d-1}

\displaystyle\forall i,

\displaystyle|A_{i}\triangle A_{j}|=2^{d-1}

\displaystyle\forall i\neq j.

Known results from Fourier analysis show that
before subsampling
, every sparse cut in the hypercube is close (in Hamming distance) to a coordinate cut. Using this, we will prove that
after subsampling
, all cuts that are far from coordinate cuts remain large, and will therefore not be a part of the optimal solution to (
1
).

We will need tools from Fourier analysis of boolean functions, and start by setting up the necessary preliminaries.

Preliminaries.

Let
Q_{d}=(V,E)
denote the
d
-dimensional hypercube with vertex set
V=\{0,1\}^{d}
and edges connecting pairs of vertices that differ in exactly one coordinate. We write
E^{\prime}
for the set of edges obtained from
E
after subsampling. For a subset
A\subseteq V
, we sometimes write
\mathbbm{1}_{A}\in\{0,1\}^{V}
for its indicator function, and
\partial(A)=E(A,V\setminus A)
for its edge boundary. We say that a cut
A\subseteq V
is
balanced
if
|A|=\frac{|V|}{2}
, and we say that cuts
A,B\subseteq V
are
orthogonal
if
|A\triangle B|=\frac{|V|}{2}
.
For
j\in[d]
,
b\in\{0,1\}
, we use the notation
S_{j,b}
for the coordinate cut
\{x\in\{0,1\}^{d}:x_{j}=b\}
.

For a function
f\colon\{0,1\}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
, we define its Fourier transform
\widehat{f}:2^{[d]}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
by

\widehat{f}(S)\coloneqq\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x\in\{0,1\}^{d}}\left[f(x)\chi_{S}(x)\right]=2^{-d}\langle f,\chi_{S}\rangle,

where
\chi_{S}
is the the
Fourier character

\chi_{S}(x)=(-1)^{\sum_{i\in S}x_{i}}
. We call
\widehat{f}(S)
the
Fourier coefficient
of
f
at
S
.

The Fourier characters form an orthogonal basis for functions on
\{0,1\}^{d}
, which gives the inverse formula

f(x)=\sum_{S\subseteq[d]}\widehat{f}(S)\chi_{S}

and Parseval’s identity

\sum_{S\subseteq[d]}\widehat{f}(S)^{2}=2^{-d}\sum_{x\in\{0,1\}^{d}}f(x)^{2}.

Furthermore, for every
S\subseteq[d]
, the Fourier character
\chi_{S}
is an eigenvector with eigenvalue
2|S|
of the unnormalized Laplacian matrix
\mathcal{L}=dI-A
of the hypercube.

Finally, we need the fact that the singleton cuts are the minimum cuts in a hypercube.

Lemma 5

(Folklore, see e.g., Example 4.1.3 in
[
38
]
)
.

The min cut of the
d
-dimensional hypercube
Q_{d}=(V,E)
has size
d
, that is

\min_{A\subseteq V:1\leq|A|\leq|V|/2}|E(A,V\setminus A)|=d.

Every sparse cut is close to a coordinate cut.

We begin by showing that every cut that is sparse in the original hypercube is indeed close to a coordinate cut. For this, we use the following standard Fourier–analytic identity, which expresses the size of a cut in terms of the Fourier coefficients of its indicator function (see e.g. Theorem 2.38 in
[
35
]
). We include a proof for completeness.

Lemma 6
.

Let
Q_{d}=(V,E)
be the
d
-dimensional hypercube, and let
A\subseteq V
. Let
f\colon V\rightarrow\{0,1\}
denote the indicator function on
A
. Then

|E(A,V\setminus A)|=2^{d+1}\sum_{S\subseteq[d]}|S|\cdot\widehat{f}(S)^{2}.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{L}
be the unnormalized Laplacian of
Q_{d}
. The cut size of
A
is given by

|E(A,V\setminus A)|=\sum_{\{x,y\}\in E}(f(x)-f(y))^{2}=f^{\top}\mathcal{L}f.

(2)

Expanding
f
in the Fourier basis gives

f=\sum_{S\subseteq[d]}\widehat{f}(S)\chi_{S}.
Since the Fourier characters are eigenvectors of
\mathcal{L}
with eigenvalues
2|S|
, we obtain

f^{\top}\mathcal{L}f=\left(\sum_{S\subseteq d}\widehat{f}(S)\chi_{S}\right)^{\top}\mathcal{L}\left(\sum_{S\subseteq d}\widehat{f}(S)\chi_{S}\right)=\sum_{S\subseteq[d]}2|S|\widehat{f}(S)^{2}\|\chi_{S}\|^{2}_{2}=2^{d+1}\sum_{S\subseteq[d]}|S|\cdot\widehat{f}(S)^{2}.

(3)

Combining Equations (
2
) and (
3
) gives the lemma.
∎

From the above lemma, we will show that every sparse cut must place most of its Fourier mass on the first two levels. This is because the contribution of each Fourier coefficient to the cut size is weighted by
|S|
, so the mass on higher levels contributes to the cut size proportionally.

This will allow us to apply the Friedgut–Kalai–Naor (FKN) theorem, which states that any boolean function with nearly all of its Fourier mass on the first two levels, has to be close to the indicator function of a coordinate cut.

Theorem 7

(FKN theorem, Theorem 1.1 in
[
20
]
)
.

If
f\colon\{0,1\}^{d}\rightarrow\{0,1\}
is a boolean function,
\|f\|_{2}^{2}=p
and if
\sum_{|S|>1}\widehat{f}(S)^{2}\leq\delta
then either
p<K^{\prime}\delta
or
p>1-K^{\prime}\delta
or
\|f(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{d})-x_{i}\|\leq K\delta
for some
i
or
\|f(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{d})-(1-x_{i})\|\leq K\delta
for some
i
. Here,
K^{\prime}
and
K
are absolute constants.

We remark that the conclusion of the FKN theorem is far from obvious. In particular, the assumption that
f
is a boolean function is essential. For example, consider a convex combination of coordinate cuts
f(x)=\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}\chi_{i}(x)
. This places all of its Fourier mass on the first two levels, but is in general not close to any coordinate cut.

A corollary of the FKN theorem (Theorem
7
) is that every sparse cut in the hypercube must be close to a coordinate cut. For completeness, we include a proof of this known fact.

Lemma 8

(Sparse cuts are close to coordinate cuts, Corollary 1.2 in
[
20
]
)
.

Suppose
A\subseteq Q_{d}
with
|A|\leq 2^{d-1}
. If
|E(A,V\setminus A)|\leq(1+\epsilon)|A|
, then there exists a coordinate cut
S_{j,b}
such that

|A\triangle S_{j,b}|\leq 2^{d}K\cdot\epsilon.

Here
K
is an absolute constant.

Proof.

We start by bounding the Fourier mass above the first two levels in order to apply the FKN theorem (Theorem
7
).
Suppose
|E(A,V\setminus A)|\leq(1+\epsilon)|A|
. By Lemma
6
,

(1+\epsilon)|A|\geq|E(A,V\setminus A)|=2^{d+1}\sum_{S\subseteq[d]}|S|\cdot\widehat{f}(S)^{2}.

(4)

On the other hand,

|A|=\sum_{x\in V}f(x)^{2}=2^{d}\sum_{S\subseteq[d]}\widehat{f}(S)^{2}

(5)

and, since
\widehat{f}(\emptyset)=2^{-d}\sum_{x\in V}f(x)=2^{-d}|A|
, we have

\widehat{f}(\emptyset)^{2}=2^{-2d}|A|.

(6)

Combining Equations (
4
), (
5
) and (
6
) gives

\displaystyle\epsilon|A|

\displaystyle\geq 2^{d+1}\sum_{S\subseteq[d]}|S|\cdot\widehat{f}(S)^{2}-2^{d}\sum_{S\subseteq[d]}\widehat{f}(S)^{2}

by (
4
) and (
5
)

\displaystyle=2^{d+1}\sum_{|S|\geq 1}(|S|-1)\widehat{f}(S)^{2}-2^{d+1}\widehat{f}(\emptyset)^{2}+2^{d}\sum_{S\subseteq[d]}\widehat{f}(S)^{2}

\displaystyle\geq 2^{d+1}\sum_{|S|\geq 2}\widehat{f}(S)^{2}+2|A|\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{|A|}{2^{d}}\right)

\displaystyle\text{by \eqref{eq:A_bound} and \eqref{eq:f_emptyset_bound}}.

The second summand is non-negative by the assumption that
|A|\leq 2^{d-1}
, which gives
\sum_{|S|\geq 2}\widehat{f}(S)^{2}\leq\epsilon 2^{-(d+1)}|A|\leq\epsilon/4
. Therefore, by
7
we have
|A\triangle S_{j,b}|\leq 2^{d}K\cdot\epsilon
or
|A|\leq K^{\prime}\epsilon
. Finally, to rule out the second possibility, note that the above equation gives
\epsilon|A|\geq 2|A|(1/2-|A|/2^{d})
, which rearranges to
|A|\geq 2^{d-1}(1-\epsilon)
.
∎

Cut counting on the difference from a coordinate cut.

Next, we want to show that a cut that is far (in Hamming distance) from every coordinate cut, cannot become the sparsest cut after subsampling.
Let
A
be a cut with
E(A,V\setminus A)=(1+\epsilon)2^{d-1}
and
\epsilon>1/\mathrm{poly}\left(d\right)
. Then
|A\triangle S|\leq O(\epsilon)2^{d-1}
for some coordinate cut
S
. We want to show that with a high probability,
|E^{\prime}(A,V\setminus A)|>E^{\prime}|(S,V\setminus S)|
, i.e. that the coordinate cut
S
is still sparser than
A
after subsampling. To show this, we want to apply the Chernoff bound to show concentration for each cut, and Karger’s cut-counting theorem to union bound over all possible choices for the cut
A
.

Theorem 9

(Karger’s cut counting theorem
[
27
]
)
.

Let
\alpha\geq 1
. Then for all graphs
G
, the number of
\alpha
-approximate minimum cuts in
G
is at most
2^{\lceil 2\alpha\rceil}{n\choose\lceil 2\alpha\rceil}
.

We start by noting that a direct cut counting plus Chernoff bound argument does not work. Indeed, a direct Chernoff bound applied to
E(A,V\setminus A)
and
E(S,V\setminus S)
would require concentration within a
(1\pm\epsilon)
factor
for all
\epsilon>1/\mathrm{poly}\left(d\right)
simultaneously
, which is too strong. Instead, we use the fact that
A
is close to
S
, and show that the differences
E^{\prime}(A,V\setminus A)\setminus E^{\prime}(S,V\setminus S)
and
E^{\prime}(S,V\setminus S)\setminus E^{\prime}(A,V\setminus A)
concentrate well. In essence, we apply a Karger-style cut counting argument
on the difference between
A\triangle S
, thereby only requiring the Chernoff bound to handle a constant factor deviation.

Applying a Chernoff bound, using the trivial upper-bound

|E(A,V\setminus A)\triangle E(S,V\setminus S)|\leq d|A\triangle S|\leq d\cdot O(\epsilon)2^{d-1},

we can show that
E^{\prime}(A,V\setminus A)
and
E^{\prime}(S,V\setminus S)
concentrate within a
O(1/d)
-factor with probability at least
1-e^{-\Omega(p\epsilon 2^{d-1}/d)}.

To union bound, we must enumerate over all cuts
A
with
|E(A,V\setminus A)|=(1+\epsilon)2^{d-1}
. A naive application of Karger’s theorem (using
\mathrm{mincut}(Q_{d})=d
, by Lemma
5
) shows that there are at most
2^{O(2^{d-1}/d)}{2^{d}\choose O(2^{d-1}/d)}
such cuts, which is too weak of a bound.

Instead, we observe that for a fixed coordinate cut
S
, the set
A
is uniquely determined by
A\triangle S
, so it suffices to enumerate the possible choices for
A\triangle S
. Applying Karger’s cut-counting theorem with the trivial bound

|\partial(A\triangle S)|\leq d|A\triangle S|\leq d\cdot O(\epsilon)2^{d-1}

gives that there are at most
2^{O(\epsilon)2^{d-1}}{2^{d}\choose O(\epsilon)2^{d-1}}\approx 2^{O(\epsilon)\log 1/\epsilon 2^{d}}
possible choices for the set
A\triangle S
. However, this bound is still too weak. We will therefore derive a stronger bound on
\partial(A)\triangle\partial(S)
and
\partial(A\triangle S)
.

Lemma 10
.

Let
A\subseteq V
be a set with
|A|\leq 2^{d-1}
and
|\partial(A)|\leq(1+\epsilon)|A|
and let
S
be the coordinate cut such that
|A\triangle S|\leq K\cdot\epsilon 2^{d}
(exists by Lemma
8
). Then

|\partial(A)\triangle\partial(S)|=|\partial(A\triangle S)|\leq C\cdot\epsilon 2^{d-1}.

Here
C
is an absolute constant.

Proof.

It is straightforward to verify that for every pair of sets
T_{1},T_{2}
, it holds that
\partial(T_{1})\triangle\partial(T_{2})=\partial(T_{1}\triangle T_{2})
, which gives the equality
|\partial(A)\triangle\partial(S)|=|\partial(A\triangle S)|
. We now prove the inequality
|\partial(A\triangle S)|\leq C\cdot\epsilon 2^{d-1}
.

Let
A^{+}\coloneqq A\setminus S
and
A^{-}\coloneqq S\setminus A
. Furthermore, write
\overline{S}=V\setminus S
. Then
V
is partitioned into the four sets
A\cap S
,
A^{-}
,
A^{+}
and
\overline{S}\setminus A
(see Figure
1
).

Figure 1
:
Illustration of the sets
A
(red),
S
,
A^{+}
and
A^{-}
, and the edges incident on
A^{+}
and
A^{-}

The high-level idea is that the edge boundaries of
A^{+}
and
A^{-}
consist of
E(A^{-},\overline{S})
and
E(A^{+},S)
, which cross the cut
S
, and
E(A^{-},A\cap S)
and
E(A^{+},\overline{S}\setminus A)
, which cross the cut
A
. Since the former two sets cross the coordinate cut, they have size at most
|A^{+}|+|A^{-}|=O(\epsilon)2^{d-1}
. Since the latter two sets contribute to the cut
A
, they cannot be too large, as otherwise the edge-boundary of
A
would have size significantly larger than
(1+\epsilon)2^{d-1}.
We now prove this more formally.

Claim 11
.

|\partial(A^{+})|+|\partial(A^{-})|\leq|\partial(A)|-|\partial(S)|+2|E(A^{+},S)|+2|E(A^{-},\overline{S})|.

Proof.

Since
A
is partitioned into
A\cap S
and
A^{+}
, and
V\setminus A
is partitioned into
A^{-}
and
\overline{S}\setminus A
, we have

|\partial(A)|=|E(A\cap S,\overline{S}\setminus A)|+|E(A^{+},A^{-})|+|E(A\cap S,A^{-})|+|E(A^{+},\overline{S}\setminus A)|.

Similarly, since
S
is partitioned into
A\cap S
and
A^{-}
, and
\overline{S}
is partitioned into
A^{+}
and
\overline{S}\setminus A
, we have

|\partial(S)|=|E(A\cap S,\overline{S}\setminus A)|+|E(A^{-},A^{+})|+|E(A\cap S,A^{+})|+|E(A^{-},\overline{S}\setminus A)|.

Combining, we get

\displaystyle|\partial(A)|-|\partial(S)|

\displaystyle=|E(A^{-},A\cap S)|+|E(A^{+},\overline{S}\setminus A)|-|E(A\cap S,A^{+})|-|E(A^{-},\overline{S}\setminus A)|

\displaystyle\geq|E(A^{-},A\cap S)|+|E(A^{+},\overline{S}\setminus A)|-|E(A^{+},S)|-|E(A^{-},\overline{S})|.

On the other hand, we have

|\partial(A^{-})|+|\partial(A^{+})|=|E(A^{-},S\cap A)|+|E(A^{-},\overline{S})|+|E(A^{+},S)|+|E(A^{+},\overline{S}\setminus A)|.

Combining the above two equations yields the claim.
∎

To continue, note that the edges in
E(A^{+},S)

\displaystyle\min

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{d}|E^{\prime}(A_{i},V\setminus A_{i})|\qquad\text{subject to}

We want to use the FKN theorem (Theorem

It is important to note that when

Definition 18

(Component of

Let

Later, in Remark

We start by analyzing the spectrum of the

\mu_{S}=\mathcal{K}_{k}(|S|;d)\coloneqq\sum_{j=0}^{k}(-1)^{j}{|S|\choose j}{d-|S|\choose k-j}.

Lemma 19

(Eigenvalues of
Q_{d,k}
)

Let
k

\mathcal{L}
be the unnormalized Laplacian of

\chi_{S}

\lambda_{S}=2\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\in[k]:\\
j\text{ odd}\end{subarray}}\binom{|S|}{j}\binom{d-|S|}{k-j}.

Proof.

For every vertex
x\in\{0,1\}^{d}

\{x\oplus\mathbbm{1}_{T}:T\in[d]^{(k)}\}

\mathcal{L}v(x)=\sum_{T\in[d]^{(k)}}\left(v(x)-v(x\oplus\mathbbm{1}_{T})\right).

We now show that every Fourier character
\chi_{S}
is an eigenvector. For every

and every

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}\chi_{S}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{T\in[d]^{(k)}}\left(\chi_{S}(x)-\chi_{S}(x\oplus\mathbbm{1}_{T})\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{T\in[d]^{(k)}}\left((-1)^{\langle x,\mathbbm{1}_{S}\rangle}-(-1)^{\langle x\oplus\mathbbm{1}_{T},\mathbbm{1}_{S}\rangle}\right)

\displaystyle=(-1)^{\langle x,\mathbbm{1}_{S}\rangle}\sum_{T\in[d]^{(k)}}\left(1-(-1)^{\langle\mathbbm{1}_{T},\mathbbm{1}_{S}\rangle}\right)

\displaystyle=\chi_{S}(x)\sum_{T\in[d]^{(k)}}\left(1-(-1)^{\langle\mathbbm{1}_{T},\mathbbm{1}_{S}\rangle}\right).

Therefore,
\chi_{S}
is an eigenvector of
\mathcal{L}
with eigenvalue
\sum_{T\in[d]^{(k)}}\left(1-(-1)^{\langle\mathbbm{1}_{T},\mathbbm{1}_{S}\rangle}\right).

To simplify the expression, note that

\left(1-(-1)^{\langle\mathbbm{1}_{T},\mathbbm{1}_{S}\rangle}\right)=\begin{cases}0,&\text{if $|T\cap S|$ is even}\\
2,&\text{if $|T\cap S|$ is odd,}\end{cases}

For every
j\in[k]
, the number of sets
T\in[d]^{(k)}
such that
|S\cap T|=j
is
{|S|\choose j}{d-|S|\choose k-j}
, so

\sum_{T\in[d]^{(k)}}\left(1-(-1)^{\langle\mathbbm{1}_{T},\mathbbm{1}_{S}\rangle}\right)=2|\{T\in[d]^{(k)}:|T\cap S|\text{ is odd}\}|=2\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\in[k]:\\
j\text{ odd}\end{subarray}}\binom{|S|}{j}\binom{d-|S|}{k-j}=\lambda_{S},

which completes the proof.
∎

Using the above lemma, we can write the size of any cut in
Q_{d,k}
in terms of the Fourier coefficients of its indicator function.

Lemma 20
.

Let
Q_{d,k}
be the
d
-dimensional
k
-distance hypercube, and let
Q=(V,E)
be component of
Q
(as per Definition
18
). Let
A\subseteq V
, and let
f\colon V\rightarrow\{0,1\}
denote the indicator function on
A
. Then

|E(A,V\setminus A)|=2^{d}\sum_{S\subseteq[d]}\lambda_{S}\widehat{f}(S)^{2}.

Proof.

Since there are no edges between
Q_{d,k}^{E}
and
Q_{d,k}^{O}
, we can use the unnormalized Lapacian
\mathcal{L}
of the
entire
graph
Q_{d,k}
to express the cut size of

as

|E(A,V\setminus A)|=\sum_{\{x,y\}\in E}(f(x)-f(y))^{2}=f^{\top}\mathcal{L}f.

(12)

On the other hand, expanding

in the Fourier basis gives

f=\sum_{S\subseteq[d]}\widehat{f}(S)\chi_{S}.
By Lemma
19
, every Fourier character
\chi_{S}
is an eigenvector of
\mathcal{L}
with eigenvalue
\lambda_{S}
, which gives

f^{\top}\mathcal{L}f=\left(\sum_{S\subseteq d}\widehat{f}(S)\chi_{S}\right)^{\top}\mathcal{L}\left(\sum_{S\subseteq d}\widehat{f}(S)\chi_{S}\right)=\sum_{S\subseteq[d]}\lambda_{S}\widehat{f}(S)^{2}\|\chi_{S}\|^{2}_{2}=2^{d}\sum_{S\subseteq[d]}\lambda_{S}\cdot\widehat{f}(S)^{2}.

(13)

Combining Equations (
12
) and (
13
) gives the lemma.
∎

To argue that every sparse cut places most of its Fourier mass on the first two levels, we first need to argue that the eigenvalues
\lambda_{S}
with
|S|>1
are large compared to those with
|S|=1
.

Lemma 21
.

Let
k
be a positive integer, and let
d=d(k)
be sufficiently large. Denote by
\lambda_{1}
the eigenvalue corresponding to sets
S\subseteq[d]
of size
|S|=1
. Then:

•

If
k
is odd, then
\lambda_{S}\geq\tfrac{3}{2}\,\lambda_{1}
for every
S\subseteq[d]
with
|S|\geq 2
.

•

If
k
is even, then
\lambda_{S}\geq\tfrac{3}{2}\,\lambda_{1}
for every
S\subseteq[d]
with
2\leq|S|\leq d-2
, and
\lambda_{S}=\lambda_{1}
for
every
S\subseteq[d]
with
|S|=d-1
.

Proof.

Let
S\subseteq[d]
. Recall from (
11
) that
\lambda_{S}=2\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\in[k]:\\
j\text{ odd}\end{subarray}}\binom{|S|}{j}\binom{d-|S|}{k-j}

and
\lambda_{1}=2\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\in[k]:\\
j\text{ odd}\end{subarray}}\binom{1}{j}\binom{d-1}{k-j}=2{d-1\choose k-1}
.
If
k
is even and
|S|=d-1
, then

\lambda_{S}=2\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\in[k]:\\
j\text{ odd}\end{subarray}}\binom{d-1}{j}\binom{1}{k-j}={d-1\choose k-1}=\lambda_{1},

since the
j=d-1
term is the only non-zero term in the sum.

We now show that
\lambda_{S}\geq 3/2\lambda_{1}
in the remaining cases. We have

\displaystyle\frac{3}{2}\lambda_{1}=\frac{3}{2}\cdot 2{d-1\choose k-1}=\frac{2}{(k-1)!}(d-1)(d-2)\cdots(d-k+1)\leq\frac{2}{(k-1)!}d^{k-1}

so it suffices to show that

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\in[k]:\\
j\text{ odd}\end{subarray}}{|S|\choose j}{d-|S|\choose k-j}\geq\frac{1}{(k-1)!}d^{k-1}.

(14)

Write
s\coloneqq|S|
, and consider four cases: The case
s\leq 2^{k}
, the case
2^{k}\leq s\leq d/2
, the case
d/2<s\leq d
and
k
is odd, and the case
d/2<s\leq d-2
and
k
is even.

Case 1:
2\leq s\leq 2^{k}
.

Then we can view
s
as a constant which is independent of
d
. By only considering the
j=1
term in the sum in (
14
), we get

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\in[k]:\\
j\text{ odd}\end{subarray}}{s\choose j}{d-s\choose k-j}

\displaystyle\geq s{d-s\choose k-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{s}{(k-1)!}(d-s)(d-(s+1))\cdots(d-(s+k-2))

\displaystyle\geq\frac{2}{(k-1)!}d^{k-1}-\Omega(d^{k-2})

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{(k-1)!}d^{k-1},

where the third transition uses that
s
is a constant independent of
d
, and the last transition holds for
d
sufficiently large.

Case 2:
2^{k}\leq s\leq d/2
.

By only considering the
j=1
term of the sum in (
14
), we get

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\in[k]:\\
j\text{ odd}\end{subarray}}{s\choose j}{d-s\choose k-j}

\displaystyle\geq s{d-s\choose k-1}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{2^{k}}{(k-1)!}\left(d-\frac{d}{2}\right)\left(d-\left(\frac{d}{2}+1\right)\right)\cdots\left(d-\left(\frac{d}{2}+k-2\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{(k-1)!}d(d-2)\cdots(d-2k+4)

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{(k-1)!}d^{k-1}-\Omega(d^{k-2})

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{(k-1)!}d^{k-1},

where the second transition uses that
s\geq d/2
, and the last inequality holds for
d
is sufficiently large.

Case 3:
d/2\leq s\leq d
and
k
is odd.

By only considering the
j=k
term of the sum in (
14
), we get

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\in[k]:\\
j\text{ odd}\end{subarray}}{s\choose j}{d-s\choose k-j}\geq{s\choose k}\geq{d/2\choose k}=\frac{1}{2^{k}(k-1)!}d^{k}-\Omega(d^{k-1})\geq\frac{1}{(k-1)!}d^{k-1},

where the last inequality holds for
d
sufficiently large.

Case 4:
d/2\leq s\leq d-2
and
k
is even.

Let
g(s)
denote the sum
g(s)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\in[k]:\\
j\text{ odd}\end{subarray}}{s\choose j}{d-s\choose k-j}
. Note that if
j
is odd and
k
is even, then
k-j
is odd, so we can relabel the sum to obtain

g(s)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\in[k]:\\
j\text{ odd}\end{subarray}}{s\choose j}{d-s\choose k-j}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\in[k]:\\
j\text{ odd}\end{subarray}}{s\choose k-j}{d-s\choose k-(k-j)}=g(d-s)\geq\frac{3}{2}{d-1\choose k-1},

where the last inequality follows by applying Case 1 and Case 2 to
d-s
.
∎

Using the spectral gap established in the previous lemma, we will show that every sparse cut places most of its Fourier mass on the first two levels. As a first step, we derive a lower bound on the expansion of a cut in terms of the higher-level Fourier coefficients.

Lemma 22
.

Let
k
be an integer, let

be sufficiently large, and let

be a component of

(as per Definition

). Let

(Coordinate cuts are sparsest cuts)

Proof.

We have

|A|=\sum_{x\in V}f(x)^{2}=2^{d}\sum_{S\subseteq[d]}\widehat{f}(S)^{2}

and, since

(16)

We now consider the two cases

even separately.

of size

. Note from (

\lambda_{\emptyset}=0
.
Combining

) and (

\displaystyle=2^{d}\sum_{S\subseteq[d]}\lambda_{S}\widehat{f}(S)^{2}

by

\displaystyle=2^{d}\sum_{|S|\geq 2}\left(\lambda_{S}-\lambda_{1}\right)\widehat{f}(S)^{2}+2^{d}\lambda_{1}\sum_{S\subseteq[d]}\widehat{f}(S)^{2}-2^{d}\lambda_{1}\widehat{f}(\emptyset)^{2}

\displaystyle\geq 2^{d}\frac{\lambda_{1}}{2}\sum_{|S|\geq 2}\widehat{f}(S)^{2}+2^{d}\lambda_{1}\sum_{S\subseteq[d]}\widehat{f}(S)^{2}-2^{d}\lambda_{1}\widehat{f}(\emptyset)^{2}

.

Case 2:

.

(as per Definition

Next, we want to apply Karger’s cut-counting theorem (Theorem

) and a Chernoff bound (Lemma

).
Similarly to the proof of Theorem

, we need to establish a strong bound on the size of

Lemma 26
.

Let
Q=(V,E)
be component of
Q_{d,k}
(as per Definition
18
) and let

be a set with

and

be a coordinate cut in

Here

Proof.

The proof is almost identical to the proof of Lemma
10
.
It is straightforward to verify that for every pair of sets

, it holds that
\partial(T_{1})\triangle\partial(T_{2})=\partial(T_{1}\triangle T_{2})
, which gives the first inequality. We now prove the equality.
Let

and

.

|\partial(A^{+})|+|\partial(A^{-})|\leq|\partial(A)|-|\partial(S)|+2|E(A^{+},S)|+2|E(A^{-},\overline{S})|.

Identical to the proof Claim
11
.

To continue, note that the edges in

are crossing the coordinate cut

where the last inequality follows from the lemma assumption.

\displaystyle\leq|\partial(A)|-|\partial(S)|+2K\cdot\epsilon{d-1\choose k-1}2^{d-1}

\displaystyle\leq(1+\epsilon){d-1\choose k-1}|A|-{d-1\choose k-1}|S|+2K\cdot\epsilon{d-1\choose k-1}2^{d-1}

To apply Karger’s cut-counting theorem, we also need to establish the size of the minimum cuts.

Lemma 28

(The singleton cuts are min-cuts)
.

Let
k
be an integer, and let

be sufficiently large. Let

be a component of

(as per Definition

). Then

\min_{A\subseteq V:1\leq|A|\leq|V|/2}|E(A,V\setminus A)|={d\choose k}.

If
|A|=1
, then
|E(A,V\setminus A)|={d\choose k}.

For the rest of the proof, we consider sets
A
of size

.

|A|\geq{d\choose k}/{d-1\choose k-1}

,

|E(A,V\setminus A)|\geq{d-1\choose k-1}|A|\geq{d\choose k}.

2\leq|A|<{d\choose k}/{d-1\choose k-1}
.

Each vertex in
Q
has degree
d\choose k
. Every vertex in
A
can have at most
|A|-1

{d\choose k}-|A|+1

where the last inequality follows from

.

-approximate minimum cut with

is at most

Here

∎

Lemma 30
.

Let
Q=(V,E)
be a component of
Q_{d,k}
(as per Definition
