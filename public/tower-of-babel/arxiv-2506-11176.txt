Model Discovery and Graph Simulation: A Lightweight Alternative to Chaos Engineering
Anatoly A. Krasnovsky, Alexander Zorkin

Abstract
Microservice applications are prone to cascading failures because of dense inter-service dependencies. Ensuring resilience usually demands fault-injection experiments in production-like setups. We propose model discovery —an automated CI/CD step that extracts a live dependency graph from trace data—and show that this lightweight representation is sufficient for accurate resilience prediction. Using the DeathStarBench Social Network, we build the graph, simulate failures via Monte-Carlo, and run matching chaos experiments on the real system. The graph model closely matches reality: with no replication, 16 trials yield an observed resilience of 0.186 versus a predicted 0.161; with replication, both observed and predicted values converge to 0.305 (mean absolute error ≤ \leq 0.0004). These results indicate that even a simple, automatically discovered graph can estimate microservice availability with high fidelity, offering rapid design-time insight without full-scale failure testing.



I
Introduction

Modern cloud applications increasingly adopt microservices architectures, breaking the system into many decoupled services that communicate over a network
[
1
,
2
]
. While this design brings advantages in development and scalability
[
3
]
, it also introduces reliability challenges. Each user request may invoke a chain of microservices, so the failure of even a single service can cascade and cause the entire request to fail
[
4
,
5
]
. Ensuring resilience—i.e., the system’s ability to remain operational despite failures—has become a critical concern
[
6
]
. Industry practitioners have pioneered chaos engineering techniques (e.g., Netflix’s Chaos Monkey) that randomly disable components in production to test system robustness
[
7
]
. Academic efforts like Gremlin
[
8
]
have provided systematic frameworks for resilience testing of microservices by injecting faults to observe system behavior. These methods, however, require running the full system and can be time-consuming and risky to execute in production
[
9
]
.

In this paper, we propose a novel offline estimation technique based solely on system topology, offering a safe and lightweight alternative. We hypothesize that by representing the microservice application as a directed graph of service dependencies, we can predict the system’s resilience to service failures without needing to physically disrupt the live system. Our hypothesis posits that Monte-Carlo fault-injection simulation applied to a static service-dependency graph can predict the system’s success rate under 30% component-failure stress with at most 15% relative error (measured against the prediction). If true, this would enable engineers to evaluate “what-if” failure scenarios quickly and safely during design or testing
[
10
]
, and to identify critical weak points in the architecture
[
11
]
.

To evaluate this hypothesis, we selected a realistic, complex microservice application: the DeathStarBench Social Network
[
12
]
. This benchmark resembles a scaled-down Twitter or Facebook, consisting of many interconnected microservices for social networking functionality
[
13
]
. We construct a dependency graph of these microservices from tracing data collected with Jaeger, where edges represent observed service-to-service dependencies, and then perform a Monte-Carlo simulation of random service outages on the graph. We also set up the actual social-network application (with dozens of Docker containers) and conduct chaos experiments by killing services and measuring the resulting successful request rate.

Our contributions are as follows:

1.

We present a detailed graph-based modeling technique to estimate microservice resilience, which eliminates the need for intrusive fault injection.

2.

We describe an open experimental framework combining simulation and live fault injection for empirical validation.

3.

We provide an empirical validation showing that the graph model’s resilience estimates closely match the actual system’s resilience, under both non-replicated and replicated deployment scenarios.

4.

We discuss the implications of these results, highlighting how such modeling can inform architecture design and guide fault tolerance decisions
[
14
]
.

II
Background and Related Work

II-A

Microservice Benchmark - Social Network

We use the Social Network application from the DeathStarBench suite
[
12
]
as a representative microservices system. This application implements a social media platform with unidirectional follow relationships (similar to Twitter)
[
13
]
. Users can compose posts containing text, media, links, and mentions of other users; these posts are then broadcast to all of the author’s followers. Other users can read their personal home timeline (an aggregated feed of posts from people they follow) or visit another user’s profile to read that user’s user timeline (posts by a specific user). Under the hood, the Social Network is composed of about a dozen microservices, each responsible for a specific piece of functionality. Requests like post composition or feed reading traverse multiple services, forming dynamic invocation chains with varying degrees of parallelism and optionality. These characteristics make the system a realistic and valuable benchmark for studying the impact of individual service failures and evaluating overall resilience.

Fig.
1
illustrates the architecture of the social-network microservices system. The entry point is an NGINX-based API Gateway that receives HTTP requests from clients
[
12
]
. Depending on the request (e.g., reading a timeline or composing a post), the gateway forwards it to the appropriate service. For instance, a compose post request goes to the ComposeService, which coordinates several downstream services to handle different aspects of posting. These include a UserService (for user profile lookups), MediaService (to handle image or video attachments), TextService (for processing text content, e.g., filtering or formatting), URLShortenService (to shorten URLs in the post), and UserMentionService (to record user mentions) – some of which may be invoked in parallel or conditionally
[
13
]
. The compose workflow also involves generating a new post ID via a UniqueIDService, storing the post in the PostStorageService (backed by a database), updating the author’s own timeline via a UserTimelineService, and pushing the new post to all followers’ feeds via a HomeTimelineService. The SocialGraphService maintains the follower graph and is consulted to find all followers of a user for feed fan-out. Meanwhile, read requests for timelines are handled by the timeline services: a home-timeline read goes directly to HomeTimelineService, and a user profile read goes to UserTimelineService. These services fetch the requested posts (from caches or storage) and return them to the gateway, which responds to the client. The Social Network uses a combination of storage systems: Redis and Memcached for caching, and MongoDB for persistent storage of posts, user profiles, and social graphs
[
12
]
.

Fig. 1:
Microservice architecture of the Social Network benchmark. Each node is a service or component, and directed edges represent calls (dependencies) between services. The system includes a front-end (NGINX gateway), logic tier services (ComposeService, timeline services), numerous specialized microservices, and data stores (dependencies on data stores are often implicit in the services that use them). Source: https://github.com/delimitrou/DeathStarBench, Apache-2.0 license.

This Social Network application is non-trivial in scale: it consists of 12 stateless microservices (each potentially running in its own container) and several stateful data stores. This architecture involves deep request chains (up to 8 services in a compose workflow), centralized ID generation, and fan-out patterns during feed updates — all of which contribute to potential fragility and make it a good candidate for resilience analysis
[
15
]
.
A failure in any one of the critical services could disrupt certain user-facing functionalities. For example, if the TextService crashes, posts cannot be composed properly; if the HomeTimelineService is down, users cannot load their feed, etc. The presence of caching layers and data replication in databases can mitigate some failures
[
16
]
. However, in our setup no automatic failover between microservices is implemented (aside from an experiment scenario with replicated instances, discussed later). Thus, the resilience of the application primarily depends on the redundancy (or lack thereof) of each microservice and the structure of the call graph
[
17
]
.

II-B

Microservice Resilience and Fault Tolerance

The challenge of achieving resilience in microservice systems has spawned considerable interest in both industry and academia
[
18
,
19
]
. Reliability engineering principles from large-scale systems (e.g., Google’s Site Reliability Engineering handbook
[
6
]
) emphasize designing for failure, and microservices demand applying these at a fine granularity. Common resiliency patterns have been adapted to microservices, such as circuit breakers and retries, to prevent cascading failures when a service becomes unresponsive
[
20
]
. Tools like Netflix Hystrix implement these patterns in practice, and are widely used to make microservices more resilient by isolating failures
[
21
]
. While Hystrix popularized resilience patterns, it is no longer maintained and has been replaced by more modern tools (e.g., Resilience4j).

In terms of systematic resilience testing, Heorhiadi et al. (2016) introduced Gremlin, a framework for systematic resilience testing of microservices
[
8
]
. Gremlin can inject failures by manipulating network traffic between services, enabling controlled experiments on how failures propagate. Such fault injection experimentation (often termed chaos engineering) has been embraced as a way to reveal hidden failure modes in complex microservice deployments
[
7
,
22
,
23
,
24
]
. However, testing all combinations of failures is combinatorially expensive for systems with many services due to high computational complexity, which motivates the need for smart modeling
[
9
]
. Observability plays a crucial role in understanding and mitigating failures in such complex systems
[
25
]
.

Recent research has also looked at model-driven resilience analysis
[
26
]
. Mendonça et al. (2020) explored model-based analysis of microservice resiliency patterns
[
27
]
. Their framework encodes common resiliency patterns—specifically Retry and Circuit Breaker—as continuous-time Markov chains and analyses them with PRISM
[
28
]
, obtaining metrics such as success probability, mean time-to-repair, and latency across a range of threshold configurations. Similarly, other works have applied probabilistic model checking to evaluate different resiliency pattern configurations under varying component availability assumptions. These works highlight that abstract modeling can be a valuable complement to empirical testing for microservices resilience.

II-C

Discovered Gap and Proposed Approach

Existing model-based approaches often require formal system specifications or focus narrowly on resiliency patterns. They rarely use real runtime dependency data, and do not quantitatively validate model predictions against live systems. This creates a gap between theoretical modeling and practical resilience analysis in real-world microservice architectures. Meanwhile, our approach aims to be more direct and broadly applicable: we leverage the actual dependency graph of the application (obtained via tracing) and perform a straightforward Monte-Carlo simulation on that graph. This is akin to classic network reliability analysis, where the probability of successful communication is computed by simulating random failures over a network connectivity graph
[
29
]
, but applied to a microservice call graph. Such an approach can naturally incorporate the presence of service replicas, which we include in our study — we model replication by assigning a service node a failure probability that reflects the aggregate availability of all its replicas, assuming independent failures.

Proposed approach can be driven by real observed dependencies rather than idealized models. To our knowledge, this work is the first to demonstrate quantitatively that a simple graph failure model can predict the resilience of a complex microservice benchmark with high accuracy. Our results strengthen the argument that model-based analysis can guide resilience improvement
[
14
]
, potentially reducing the need for exhaustive fault injection testing in every scenario
[
9
]
. The security aspects of microservices, while not the direct focus here, also benefit from understanding dependencies and potential failure points
[
30
,
31
]
.

III
Experimental Setup

To test our hypothesis, we set up an environment where we can measure the social-network application’s resilience under controlled failure scenarios. Our experimental setup involves two key aspects: (a) deploying the Social Network microservices on a containerized platform with configurable replication, and (b) running a load generator and failure injection mechanism (a ”chaos” script) to observe the system’s behavior when certain services crash.

III-A

System Infrastructure

We deployed the social-network microservices using Docker Compose, which allowed us to easily run each service in a separate Docker container on a single host. The system comprises the following core services (each corresponding to one container in the no-replication case): NGINX Gateway, ComposeService, UserService, MediaService, TextService, URLShortenService, UserMentionService, UniqueIDService, SocialGraphService, PostStorageService, UserTimelineService, and HomeTimelineService
[
12
]
. These services communicate via Thrift RPC or HTTP calls. Additionally, the application uses Redis and Memcached as in-memory caches and MongoDB as the primary database. We ran single-instance Redis, Memcached, and MongoDB containers.

In our no-replication scenario, each microservice above is a single point of failure (one container instance). In the with-replication scenario, specific stateless services were configured with multiple instances. Based on the resilience.py script, the replication counts were:

•

compose-post-service: 3 replicas

•

home-timeline-service: 3 replicas

•

user-timeline-service: 3 replicas

•

text-service: 3 replicas

•

media-service: 3 replicas

The replication factor of 3 was chosen to allow one or two replicas to fail while still retaining service availability, in line with common redundancy practices. Other services not in this list (such as user-service, unique-id-service, url-shorten-service, user-mention-service, social-graph-service, post-storage-service), and the databases, remained single instances. The Docker Compose setup was adjusted for the replication scenario by launching the specified number of containers for these services. By default, NGINX was configured with round-robin upstream balancing to distribute requests among the available replicas. Each service group had a separate upstream definition.

III-B

Workload Generation

To measure the system’s resilience, we used a workload generator (wrk2 HTTP benchmarking tool with scripting) to continuously issue a mix of read and write requests against the social network’s API. We simulated users performing actions such as loading their home timeline, viewing another user’s profile timeline, and composing new posts. Although the load is moderate (300 RPS, 30 seconds of workload, 10 concurrent user threads), it was verified to fully utilize basic request paths without saturating system resources. The ratio of reads to writes was approximately 4:1. Each request was checked for success (HTTP 200 OK).

III-C

Failure Injection

We developed a bash script (chaos.sh) to terminate a fraction of running containers. We parameterized it with a failure probability
P_{\text{fail}}=0.30
(30% of total containers to kill). The script uses Docker commands to randomly select 30% of the service containers (excluding databases from this count but they could be part of the overall container fleet subject to failures if not explicitly excluded by the script’s container selection logic, which typically targets application service containers) and kill them. The NGINX gateway was generally kept alive to ensure requests could reach the backend. Containers remained down for the experiment duration. By default, wrk2 runs a warmup period before injecting failures (about 10 seconds) and continues the workload for a fixed duration post-failure (e.g., 1 minute).
During the chaos experiment, we measured the success request rate. The live resilience metric (
R_{\text{live}}
) is calculated as:

R_{\text{live}}=1-\frac{\text{Number of failed responses}}{\text{Total requests issued}}

(1)

In a fully healthy run (no failures),
R_{\text{live}}=1.00
. Under failures,
R_{\text{live}}
drops.

III-D

Data Collection

We performed the failure injection test for each scenario (no-replication and with-replication), using fixed random seed for reproducibility. After the test, we collected
R_{\text{live}}
. In parallel, we ran our simulation model to produce a predicted
R_{\text{model}}
for the same scenario. After that, we calculated their means and standard deviations. The load generator logged each HTTP request, including timestamp and status code. The failure rate was calculated from the log as the proportion of 5xx server errors + socket errors to the total number of requests. We excluded 4xx errors because they are considered relatively successful in delivering the intended service outcomes.

IV
Methodology

Our methodology consists of constructing a graph-based model of the microservice system and performing a Monte-Carlo resilience simulation on this model, then comparing it to the empirical results.

IV-A

Model Discovery or Dependency Graph Extraction

Model discovery is a proposed new family of CI/CD-friendly tools that automatically mine runtime telemetry—distributed traces, logs, metrics—to synthesize an explicit, analyzable system model (e.g., a service-dependency graph, queueing network, or CTMC). Positioned right after the build stage, a model-discovery step keeps an up-to-date architectural model on every commit, bridging observability and formal verification. Industrial surveys show that trace-driven graph extraction is already routine, while in-house platforms such as Uber’s CRISP and IBM’s Istio/Jaeger graph generator demonstrate the feasibility of fully automated pipelines
[
23
]
; our own extraction of a dependency graph from Jaeger traces therefore acts as a first concrete prototype of this tool class.

We obtained a directed graph
G=(V,E)
representing service call dependencies in the Social Network application. Each node
v\in V
corresponds to a microservice. A directed edge
(u\rightarrow v)\in E
indicates that service
u
directly calls service
v
. Edges are unweighted in this study; all calls are treated as equally critical. We built this graph by using distributed tracing data (Jaeger) from the system’s execution, written to a deps.json file and loaded into a NetworkX directed graph in Python
[
32
]
. The ‘nginx-web-server‘ is included as a node in the graph model, serving as the entry point whose aliveness is checked as part of request success, consistent with the resilience.py script’s logic. Database systems were not explicitly modeled as separate nodes; their failure is implicitly captured by the failure of services relying on them.

Fig. 2:
Dependency graph extracted from Jaeger traces for the DeathStarBench Social-Network. Rectangles are micro-services; the ‘nginx-web-server‘ node is the primary external entry point within the model. Directed edges denote observed, blocking Thrift-RPC/HTTP calls. The graph structure is a directed acyclic graph (DAG) representing the call hierarchy for the main user operations.

IV-B

Monte-Carlo Failure Simulation

Given the dependency graph
G
, we want to estimate the probability that a user request succeeds given a random pattern of service failures. We identified three representative endpoint operations. Their required services (targets) and weights are defined in resilience.py as follows:

•

home-timeline: Targets: home-timeline-service, post-storage-service, social-graph-service. Weight: 0.6.

•

user-timeline: Targets: user-timeline-service, post-storage-service. Weight: 0.3.

•

compose-post: Base Targets: compose-post-service, user-service, unique-id-service, post-storage-service, user-timeline-service, home-timeline-service, text-service, social-graph-service. Additionally, media-service, url-shorten-service, and user-mention-service are added to the targets conditionally based on random choices during each simulation sample for this endpoint (specifically, media-service is added with probability
p=4/5
, url-shorten-service with
p=5/6
, and user-mention-service with
p=5/6
). Weight: 0.1.

The simulation is implemented in a Python script resilience.py. The input is as follows: dependency graph
G
, failure probability
P_{\text{fail}}=0.30
, number of samples
N=4,500,000
, and a flag indicating whether replication is enabled.
For each sample
1\dots N
:

1.

Determine Failed Containers/Services: A global list of all container instances (across all services and their respective replicas as defined in Section 3) is created. The total number of containers to kill,
K_{\text{killed\_containers}}
, is calculated as
P_{\text{fail}}
multiplied by the total number of container instances, rounded to the nearest integer.
K_{\text{killed\_containers}}
container instances are randomly sampled without replacement from the global list (victims = random.sample(container_list, K_killed_containers)). A microservice is marked as ”alive” in a simulation sample if at least one of its container instances remains running after failure sampling (otherwise, it is considered ”failed”). It models basic availability under replication, assuming client-side load balancing can access any healthy replica. Described process yields a set
F
of failed services for that sample.

2.

Determine Endpoint Success: For each endpoint type, the request is considered successful if the nginx-web-server (client) is alive, all its necessary target services (as defined above, including conditionally added ones for compose-post) are alive (i.e.,
\notin F
), and there is a path in the graph of currently alive services (
G_{\text{alive}}
) from the nginx-web-server to each of these necessary target services.

3.

Record a success (1) or failure (0) for each endpoint.

4.

After sampling, compute the estimated availability for each endpoint (
R_{\text{endpoint}}
) as its average success rate (number of successes / N).

5.

Compute an overall resilience metric
R_{\text{model}}
as a weighted average of the endpoint availabilities:

R_{\text{model}}=\sum(R_{\text{endpoint}}\times\text{weight}_{\text{endpoint}})

(2)

using the weights 0.6 for home-timeline, 0.3 for user-timeline, and 0.1 for compose-post.

6.

Output the individual endpoint availabilities and
R_{\text{model}}
.

Our simulations used
N=4,500,000
Monte-Carlo samples per run, repeated 16 times for each scenario (32 runs in total). This sampling method, which selects a fixed number of containers to fail globally from all available instances, differs from a model where each service or each replica fails independently with probability
p
. The conditional target selection in compose-post introduces probabilistic variance into the endpoint availability estimates. This variance was empirically observed to be low due to the large number of samples (
N=4.5
million), stabilizing estimates to three decimal places.

IV-C

Combining Model and Experimental Results

After running the simulation, we obtain
R^{\text{norepl}}_{\text{model}}
and
R^{\text{repl}}_{\text{model}}
. From the chaos experiments, we have measured
R^{\text{norepl}}_{\text{live}}
and
R^{\text{repl}}_{\text{live}}
(averaged over trials). We collate these into a comparative view.
The resilience metrics we focus on in results are:

•

No Replication Scenario:
R_{\text{model\_norepl}}
vs
R_{\text{live\_norepl}}
.

•

With Replication Scenario:
R_{\text{model\_repl}}
vs
R_{\text{live\_repl}}
.

A perfect model would have
R_{\text{model}}=R_{\text{live}}
. We analyze any deviations to understand their source. It’s worth noting that our model assumes independence of failures across services and no shared fate or cascading internal logic (e.g., retries, backpressure). Also, it does not incorporate network latency or partial degradation — it is a pure availability model (service either works or not). These limitations are discussed in Section
VI
.

; a GitHub Actions workflow re-executes the entire pipeline on every fork, so any researcher can reproduce every figure and table “as is” with a single click.

V
Results

We now present the results of our resilience estimation, comparing the graph-based model predictions with the observed empirical outcomes. We evaluate both the no-replication deployment (each service single-instance) and the with-replication deployment (where key services had 3 replicas each, and others one). Fig.
3
visualizes the comparison.

V-A

No Replication (Single Instance per Service)

In this scenario, the system has no redundancy – each microservice is a single point of failure. Across 16 independent chaos experiments at a 30% random container failure rate, our model predicted an overall resilience
R_{\text{model\_norepl}}=0.161
(i.e.,
\sim
16% of requests would succeed). The empirically measured resilience was
R_{\text{live\_norepl}}=0.186
(
\sim
18% success rate). These values are quite low, which is expected: with nearly one-third of services down, most multi-service request flows are broken. The key observation is that the model’s prediction was very close to the actual result — the absolute error therefore averaged
0.025\pm 0.001
(range 0.023–0.028), corresponding to a 13.5% mean relative error and 14.6% worst-case when normalised by the model value.

V-B

With Replication (Specified Instances per Service)

In this scenario, key services (compose-post-service, home-timeline-service, user-timeline-service, text-service, media-service) had three replicas each, while other services and data stores remained single instances. Our model predicted
R_{\text{model\_repl}}=0.305
(
\sim
30% success rate). Remarkably, the observed resilience was
R_{\text{live\_repl}}=0.305
as well — an exact match to three decimal places. 15 of the 16 trials were within 0.003 of the prediction, and the largest deviation (0.009) corresponds to a 2.9% relative error. This almost identical outcome provides strong evidence in support of our hypothesis: the graph model accurately captured the effect of replication on resilience as implemented in the code. With this specific replication strategy, the system’s resilience roughly doubled compared to no-replication. While the exact match in the replicated scenario is encouraging, further testing across broader replication strategies and workloads is required to generalize this finding.

V-C

Why the Model Slightly Underestimated No-Replication Resilience

The small difference (0.161 vs 0.186) in the no-replication case could be due to several factors:

1.

Sampling Methodology Nuances: The model simulates failures by choosing a fixed number of containers globally to fail. The live experiment also aims for a percentage of container failures. Subtle differences in how ”30% failure” is instantiated across a relatively small number of services/containers can lead to minor variations. Discrete, container-level implementation means the exact mix of failed services can differ slightly between repetitions, shifting aggregate success by up to
\pm 0.002
.

2.

Endpoint Weighting and Actual Workload: The model uses fixed weights (0.6, 0.3, 0.1). The actual wrk2 script’s request mix might not perfectly align with these weights in every short experimental window, which led to shifts in
R_{\text{model}}
of up to
\pm 0.007
, consistent with the observed 0.025 gap. This supports the idea that minor workload skew can affect aggregate resilience.

3.

Dynamic Targets in compose-post: The compose-post operation in the model has conditionally included services. This stochastic nature, averaged over many simulations, is compared against live experiments which also face this inherent variability per request.

V-D

Summary of Key Result

The graph-based model reproduced system resilience closely in both scenarios, supporting our central hypothesis that simulated service failures on the dependency graph mirror real-world behaviour. The model slightly under-predicted success in the no-replica configuration but tracked the replicated configuration almost exactly, showing it captures the benefit of redundancy. VI
Discussion

The experimental results show that a graph-based failure simulation can be an effective proxy for real-world resilience testing
[
34
]
.
Key moments:

•

Accuracy and Usefulness: The close match, particularly with replication, suggests that for this benchmark, failure behavior is largely determined by service dependencies, their connectivity, and the number of available container instances. This supports using static/model-based analysis of architecture for resilience audits
[
10
,
14
]
. The Monte-Carlo simulation approach is scalable for larger graphs
[
35
]
.

•

Identifying Critical Services: The model can pinpoint critical services by observing which failures most impact the weighted
R_{\text{model}}
. This is valuable for prioritizing redundancy or mitigation efforts
[
11
,
36
]
. The ”what-if” analysis capability (e.g., simulating different replica counts) is a strong advantage. PRISM 4.0 couples symbolic (parametric) model checking with a discrete-event simulation engine, so key configuration parameters—such as the number of replicas per service—can be left as variables and later instantiated without rebuilding the model, yielding availability or latency predictions in seconds
[
28
]
.

This ability to sweep parameters and obtain closed-form or rapidly re-evaluated quantitative results turns PRISM into an automated “what-if” oracle, enabling sensitivity studies that would be prohibitively expensive to conduct through repeated fault-injection experiments.

•

Single Points of Failure (SPOFs): The database (MongoDB) remained a single instance in both scenarios. Its failure would severely impact most operations. This was reflected in both model and live experiments, capping overall resilience. The model could easily be extended to simulate DB replication.

VI-A

Limitations

Despite the success, several limitations must be acknowledged:

1.

Fail-Stop Assumption: We assumed services fail completely (stop serving) and otherwise behave normally. Real failures can be more complex: partial outages, slow responses (latency spikes), or gray failures where a service is degraded but not totally down
[
37
]
. These are not captured in our binary model. Performance degradation modeling would require more complex simulations
[
38
,
39
]
.

2.

No Dynamic Request Routing or Asynchronous Communications: In our system, if a service is down, requests that need it just fail. In more advanced systems, there may be fallbacks or async calls between services
[
20
]
. Our model would need a way to capture such conditional dependencies.

3.

Perfect Knowledge of Dependencies: We built our graph from exhaustive tracing. We assume we know all service dependencies
[
25
,
27
]

VII
Conclusion

This paper investigated a graph-based approach to estimating the resilience of a microservice application and evaluates its effectiveness on a complex social-network benchmark. We formulated the hypothesis that a simple dependency graph, subjected to simulated failures, can accurately predict the real system’s behavior under similar failures. Through a combination of Monte-Carlo modeling and controlled chaos experiments, we found strong support for the hypothesis: across 32 trials under 30% outages the model’s predictions were within 2.5 percentage points of the observed value in the no-replica setup (14% relative error) and within 0.9 percentage points in the replicated setup (
\leq
3% relative error), with an overall mean relative error of 8%.

Our work contributes a validated methodology that allows engineers to assess and enhance microservice resilience
[

,
41
]
, without always resorting to expensive live experiments. This complements traditional testing by providing a theoretical baseline. In environments where running chaos experiments is difficult (e.g., in production with real users), this approach offers a low-risk alternative to glean resilience insights. Moreover, the model can systematically evaluate many combinations of failures (via simulation) far faster than one could trial them on a real deployment.
