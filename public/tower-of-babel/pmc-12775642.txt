Ultrasonic aspirators in minimally invasive laminectomies: a pilot study and technical note on reduced Kerrison rongeur usage without increased blood loss or operative time
Authors: Pravarakhya Puppalla, Chase McKevitt, Diogo P Moniz Garcia, Alaa Montaser, Rodrigo Navarro-Ramirez, Stephen M Pirris
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundKerrison rongeurs are frequently used during decompressive procedures but carry risks such as incidental dural tears and occupational strain for spine surgeons. High-speed drills are often used for bony decompression. In other surgical specialties, ultrasonic aspirators have been shown to safely remove bone near critical structures like the dura and nerves. However, their application in spine surgery remains underexplored. The aim of this pilot study was to evaluate the safety and efficacy of ultrasonic aspirators as an alternative to high-speed drills and Kerrison rongeurs in minimally invasive laminectomies.MethodsTwenty-seven patients underwent 40 level of lumbar laminectomies for degenerative spinal stenosis using minimally invasive tubular approaches. Patients were grouped by decompression tool used: high-speed drill only (19 patients, 24 levels), ultrasonic aspirator only (12 patients, 16 levels), or both tools (4 patients). The number of Kerrison bites per level were recorded. Estimated blood loss (EBL) was compared in single-level cases between the ultrasonic aspirator (n=6) and drill only groups (n=10).ResultsThe mean number of Kerrison bites was significantly lower in the ultrasonic group (8.06) compared to the drill group (38.25; P<0.001). Among single-level cases, mean EBL was reduced in the ultrasonic group (21 mL) versus the drill group (52 mL; P=0.02). No adverse events were observed with ultrasonic use.ConclusionsThis pilot study suggests ultrasonic aspirators may reduce the need for Kerrison rongeur use and intraoperative blood loss in minimally invasive laminectomies, without increasing operative time. These findings support further prospective evaluation.Keywords: Laminectomy, spine surgery, bone scalpel, piezoelectric scalpel, pilot study

Highlight box

Key findings

• This pilot study suggests that using an ultrasonic aspirator during minimally invasive tubular lumbar laminectomies is safe and effective. It may reduce the need for Kerrison rongeur bone bites and decrease intraoperative blood loss without increasing operative time. In single-level cases, the mean number of Kerrison bites was 8.06 for the ultrasonic group vs. 38.25 for the drill group (P<0.001). Estimated blood loss was also lower: 21 vs. 52 mL (P=0.02). No complications were attributed to ultrasonic aspirator use.

What is known and what is new?

• Ultrasonic bone aspirators are established tools in skull base and dental surgery for safe bony removal near critical structures, and prior open spine studies suggest reduced blood loss and complications. However, their role in minimally invasive spine surgery remains underexplored.

• This manuscript introduces the first quantitative evaluation of ultrasonic aspiration in minimally invasive tubular spine surgery, potentially showing effectiveness and safety.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• These early findings suggest that ultrasonic aspirators can improve efficiency and safety in minimally invasive decompressions by potentially reducing reliance on Kerrison rongeurs and possibly minimizing blood loss. Given these benefits and the favorable ergonomic and safety profile, spine surgeons may consider incorporating ultrasonic technology into their workflows when performing lumbar spine decompressions. Larger, multicenter prospective studies are warranted to further validate these findings and establish best practices for ultrasonic aspirator use in minimally invasive spine surgery.

Introduction

Ultrasonic bone aspirators, also known as piezoelectric scalpels or bone scalpels, have been utilized for the bone removal near delicate neurovascular structures by dentists, otolaryngologists and skull-base neurosurgeons (1-4). In contrast, their use in spine surgery is less common, particularly in minimally invasive spine surgery, where estimated utilization is under 5% of spine surgeries. This is likely the first study to quantitatively evaluate ultrasonic aspiration for minimally invasive spine surgery using a tubular retractor approach. Prior studies have suggested that the use of ultrasonic aspirators in spine surgery may reduce blood loss, operative time and complications. However, most data originate from open-conventional procedures (5-8). Reports of their use in minimally invasive spine procedures remain scarce (2). Bone scalpels are considered safe due to their vibratory rather than rotatory bone eroding capacity, and their operation below the threshold for thermal injury (9). With recent technological advances, ultrasonic aspirators (SONOPET Stryker®, Kalamazoo, MI, USA) can be used through smaller tubular retractors and have been incorporated into minimally invasive lumbar decompression procedures. A key intraoperative observation is that ultrasonic aspiration may allow for more bone removal before Kerrison rongeurs are required, compared to the traditional use high-speed drills. This facilitates removal of the hypertrophied ligamentum flavum in a near en-bloc fashion after releasing its attachments with a blunt nerve hook. Ultrasonic aspirators may enhance both safety near the dura and surgical efficiency, as demonstrated in Figure 1 (10,11). In this pilot study, Kerrison rongeur use for bone removal was quantitatively assessed. Despite growing evidence supporting their use in open spine procedures, no studies have quantitatively examined their benefits in minimally invasive tubular spine surgery. This pilot study addresses this gap by directly comparing intraoperative metrics and surgical efficiency associated with ultrasonic aspiration. This pilot study was motivated by the immediate and noticeable changes in surgical workflow observed after adopting the technology, even in early cases with a small sample size. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://jss.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jss-25-92/rc).

Methods

The team retrospectively collected data on 27 consecutive patients who underwent primary decompressive lumbar laminectomies using a minimally invasive tubular retractor technique. All patients were recruited from the clinical practice of the senior author at the Mayo Clinic in Florida. All procedures were performed by the senior authors (S.M.P./R.N.R.) who have extensive experience with ultrasonic aspirators from various manufacturers. Recent improvements have helped create piezoelectric tools with a smaller profile that allow for their use in smaller working corridors such as those afforded by minimally invasive tubular retractors. It is important for the operating room staff to become familiar with these tools as the set up may involve several different steps and nuances when compared with the more commonly utilized high speed drills. Seven of these cases involved a concomitant instrumented fusion via lateral interbody technique with percutaneous pedicle screw fixation. Sixteen of these cases were single-level laminectomies, 10 of which utilized a high-speed drill and 6 which used an ultrasonic aspirator. The diameter of the tubular retractor used in these cases was either 18 or 20 mm. Visualization through the tubular retractor around the operating tools was deemed to be adequate in all cases. This data point was determined by the senior surgeon or trainee performing the operation based on their preferences at the time of surgery and was not prospectively collected. A total of 43 laminectomy levels were performed, 26 were performed with a drill and 17 were performed with the ultrasonic aspirator. The decision on whether to use a high-speed drill or ultrasonic aspirator was determined at the time of surgery and was mostly based on the comfort of the assisting staff. The first assistant in each of these cases was tasked with counting the Kerrison bites on bone and confirming with the senior authors throughout and at the end of each case. The primary endpoint of the pilot study was comparing the number of Kerrison bone bites in laminectomies with the use of a high-speed drill versus an ultrasonic aspirator. Secondary endpoints included surgical time as well as estimated blood loss (EBL). The EBL recorded was based on the estimated amount of output through the suctions as registered in the machines minus the estimated amount of saline irrigation used during the operation. The surgical time was determined from the “Procedure Start” and “Procedure Finish” times as documented in the electronic medical record. Dural tears were noted by direct observation of clear fluid egress and dural injury noted under the microscope in the operating room. Anesthesia held a Valsalva maneuver in all patients to help confirm that there were not occult dural injuries or spinal fluid leakage prior to removing the microscope and starting the closure. There were no new neurologic deficits noted in any patients. All cases were performed by the same surgical team to reduce procedural variation. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study was approved by the institutional review board of Mayo Clinic Florida IRB# 18-003951 (short- and long-term outcomes after surgical treatment of spinal pathologies). The patients all provided informed consent for the surgeries but were not specifically consented for this study.

Statistical analysis

There were no missing data for primary or secondary endpoints in this dataset. Basic comparison methods were used for statistical analysis. This pilot study included all eligible cases without a formal sample size calculation as it was designed to evaluate feasibility.

Results

A total of 27 cases involving epidural decompression were included in this pilot study. An overview of surgical procedures is shown in Table 1. Of these, eight cases (29.7%) were performed using only SONOPET ultrasonic aspiration, 16 (59.2%) were performed using only traditional drill instrumentation, and three (11.1%) were performed using a combination of both. Epidural decompression encompassing one, two, and three vertebral levels were included in this study. Single vertebral level epidural decompression were the most common and accounted for 6 of the 8 SONOPET ultrasonic aspiration only cases (75%) and 10 of the 16 drill-only cases (62.5%). One case performed on three vertebral levels used a combination of SONOPET ultrasonic aspiration and traditional drill instrumentation. In total, 14 vertebral levels were performed using SONOPET ultrasonic aspiration and 25 using traditional drill instrumentation. Following SONOPET ultrasonic aspiration, the mean Kerrison rongeur bites per level was 8 [standard deviation (SD): ±3.47]. The mean Kerrison rongeur bites per level after traditional drill instrumentation was 38 (SD: ±10.47). Use of ultrasonic aspirators led to a statistically significantly lower number of Kerrison bites per level (P<0.001). EBL was recorded in all surgical cases. For standardization, only cases involving a single vertebral level were used to investigate differences in EBL between cases using SONOPET ultrasonic aspiration and traditional drill instrumentation. The lateral interbody fusion procedure and percutaneous pedicle screw fixation did not add substantial blood loss to the procedures. The mean EBL for SONOPET ultrasonic aspiration single vertebral cases (21 mL, SD: ±7.35) was less than the mean EBL for traditional drill instrumentation single vertebral cases (52 mL, SD: ±33.25). EBL was also significantly reduced in the ultrasonic aspirator group for a single vertebral level epidural decompression (P=0.02). In a similar manner, total procedural length for epidural decompression of a single vertebral level was performed (Table 2). The mean procedural length for SONOPET ultrasonic aspiration was 98.33 minutes (SD: ±34.44) and 97 minutes (SD: ±43.27) in the traditional drill instrumentation group. Operative time was statistically equivalent between groups (P=0.48), indicating no loss in efficiency despite the change in technique.

There was one dural tear on a multilevel laminectomy in the traditional drill group. The dural tear occurred with use of a Kerrison rongeur and the ultrasonic aspirator was then used to more safely remove bone around the opening for primary suture repair of the dural defect. There was also one dural tear in the combined group (SONOPET) that occurred during an awake operation while the patient moved during one of the Kerrison bites. Neither patient in the Drill or Sonopet group had any sequelae or persistent cerebrospinal fluid leakage. No other complications were encountered in this series. All analyses were unadjusted due to the pilot nature of this study.

Discussion

Recent advances have allowed for ultrasonic bone aspiration to be used through smaller tubular retractors. As such, these tools have started to become implemented when performing minimally invasive lumbar decompression surgery. In this pilot study, the reduction in Kerrison usage was immediate and substantial, with statistically significant differences despite the small scale. Notably, this occurred without increasing blood loss or operative time, justifying future prospective evaluation for the improvement of surgical workflow. Compared to the standard high-speed drill, the ultrasonic aspirator permitted more effective laminar and lateral recess bone removal prior to the use of Kerrison rongeurs. This is clearly delineated in the results of this early pilot study.

Current ultrasonic bone aspirators have the potential to be used in minimally invasive procedures and could beneficially disrupt the state of neurosurgical procedures. Multiple studies have demonstrated the ability of ultrasonic devices to improve metrics such as intraoperative blood loss, laminectomy time, and rates of complications such as durotomies. These devices may prove to be useful in a variety of neurosurgical procedures. For instance, one prospective randomized control trial analyzed 31 patients who underwent open decompressive laminectomy and pedicular screw fusion with the use of an ultrasonic bone scalpel (n=15) or conventional technique (n=16) (10). The study authors found that the ultrasonic bone scalpel group had significantly lower intraoperative blood loss and laminectomy time (656.0±167.6 mL, 54.5±27.4 minutes) than in the conventional group (936.9±413.2 mL, 73.4±28.1 minutes) (10). Overall operation time, the length of hospital stay, and complication rates were similar between the groups (10). Another randomized control trial of 190 patients who underwent cervical open-door laminoplasty found that the 95 patients who underwent the operation with the ultrasonic bone scalpel had significantly less intraoperative bleeding (P<0.01) and dural injuries only occurred in the 95 patient high speed drill group (2.1%) (12).

Aside from reduced amounts of intraoperative blood loss, several studies have found that ultrasonic bone devices can result in lower complication rates in neurosurgical operations. In a prospective review of 231 patients who underwent a laminectomy (64 patients in traditional group, 32 patients in ultrasonic bone scalpel group), the rates of durotomies in the ultrasonic bone scalpel group was 0% compared to 12.5% in the traditional group respectively (P=0.05) (13). Another comparative study of 46 patients who underwent a cervical laminectomy for cervical spondylotic myelopathy divided the patients into 23 who underwent the procedure with a ultrasonic bone scalpel (Group I) and 23 patients where a high speed drill was used (Group II) (14). The study authors found that in Group I, the mean laminectomy time was 2.2±0.4 min/level, mean blood loss was 180 cc, hospitalization was 3.0±0.0 days, with C5 radiculopathy seen in 1 patient (14). In Group II, the mean laminectomy time was 7.4±2.6 min/level, mean blood loss was 380 cc, hospitalization was 3.7±1.3 days, and C5 radiculopathy was seen in 1 patient and dura injuries in 3 patients (14). The recovery rate was observed to be 47.6% in Group I and 48.8% in Group II (14). Other retrospective studies have also found reduced rates of surgical complications and postoperative morbidity with the use of an ultrasonic device (10,12,15). This has also been illustrated in procedures such as anterior cervical discectomy and fusion (ACDF) for retro vertebral osteophytes and in unilateral biportal endoscopic spine surgery (2,16). Verification of nerve decompression may also provide potential guidance in quantifying motor action potentials (17,18).

In a literature review of 10 studies, only two found no statistically significant difference in unintentional injuries such as durotomies (19,20). In addition, one study found that the ultrasonic bone scalpel did not outperform a high-speed drill (21). Ultimately, ultrasound devices can improve a neurosurgeon’s ability to perform a variety of operations. These devices can reduce complications such as durotomies and intraoperative blood loss (22). Recent cohort data corroborate prior findings that ultrasonic bone scalpels are associated with significantly less intraoperative blood loss and improved functional scores compared to traditional high-speed drills (23). In matched comparisons of high-frequency ultrasonic osteotomy versus burr-based laminectomy for spinal intradural extramedullary tumors, reduced operating time and length of stay were observed without increased complications with the ultrasound group (24). However, one single-surgeon retrospective study of 193 patients undergoing spine surgery found no significant differences in durotomy, length of stay, and blood loss between ultrasonic bone scalpels and high-speed drills (21). A trending lower incidence of neurological injury was observed in the ultrasound group. A meta-analysis performed evaluating the use of ultrasonic osteotomes in spine surgery found evidence that using ultrasound in spine surgery is safe and effective and may minimize intraoperative bleeding and operative time (19). No significant differences in symptom improvement rate, hospital stay, or postoperative complications were observed compared to standard equipment. Further prospective research with larger sample sizes should be done across a variety of populations to corroborate this evidence. Moreover, neurosurgeons may want to consider training and using these ultrasound devices when applicable to potentially improve the efficiency and safety of their operations.

The pilot study did have two unintentional durotomies, one in each group, which were related to the use of Kerrison rongeurs. In the ultrasonic aspirator group, the dural tear occurred when the patient was under a spinal (awake) rather than general anesthetic, who became nauseated and moved their trunk just as the Kerrison rongeur was being applied deep in the lateral recess. The ultrasonic aspirator was then utilized again to safely clear more of the local bone in preparation for primary suture repair of the defect. The technique has since been adjusted to release the attachments of the ligamentum flavum with a nerve hook rather than Kerrison rongeurs to limit the potential risk of dural tear during these steps. Due to the lower number of patients in the ultrasonic aspirator group, the rate can be interpreted as being higher in that group. The 2 dural tears in this pilot study both occurred with the use of Kerrison rongeurs, which further illustrates the importance of potentially limiting the number of Kerrison bites necessary to perform spinal decompression surgery.

This pilot study demonstrates that the ultrasonic bone aspirator may reduce the number of Kerrison bites required for bone removal compared to the high-speed drill in minimally invasive decompressive laminectomies. The authors recognize that this is not a well reported data point, but it was a notable finding that was readily apparent when using the ultrasonic aspirating device and may be of significant interest to the readership. Appropriate neural decompression was accomplished utilizing this technique with an acceptable complication rate (Figure 2). The use of Kerrison rongeurs may be associated with higher risk of dural tears. The literature has plenty of references regarding dural tears associated with spine surgery, however it is more difficult to ascertain which part of the operation leads to dural tears (25-27). This preliminary work establishes a framework that may guide future prospective or multicenter trials exploring the broader role of ultrasonic aspiration in spine surgery.

Kerrison rongeurs could also be considered as a potential contributor to the increased rate of carpal tunnel syndrome in neurosurgeons. A recent survey showed that neurosurgeons have a significantly increased rate of carpal tunnel symptoms compared to the general population (43.6% vs. 1–5%) (28). Spine surgeons in non-teaching settings were significantly more affected compared to spine surgeons who work in teaching institutions (50% vs. 45%, P=0.01) (28). The authors postulate that this could be due to protective effects of the residents utilizing the Kerrison rongeurs and drills. This publication was not powered to determine the actual cause of the increased rate of carpal tunnel symptoms, but it is well known that spine surgeons repetitively utilize Kerrison rongeurs to complete their operations.

There is a learning curve for usage of tubular retractors in challenging decompressions and for the ultrasonic bone aspirator (29). It is imperative that the surgeon continues to move the tip to prevent overheating and potential thermal injury at the working site. Video 1 shows the workflow in detail. The learning curve also involves more gentle use of force and allowing the tool to work its way through the bone rather than the typical tactile feel of utilizing the high-speed drill. As surgeons progress through the learning curve, the power and irrigation flow on the ultrasonic settings can be increased or decreased per surgeon preference.

Working tips on the ultrasonic bone aspirator have been developed to allow for directional usage and a higher level of safety when working over exposed dura. This incremental technology has significantly improved the ability to utilize ultrasonic bone aspirators through a minimally invasive tubular retractor. The authors have noted an improved ability to decompress the contralateral lateral recess (superior articulating process plus ligamentum flavum compression over the traversing nerve root) utilizing the ultrasonic aspirator rather than the more standard techniques of high-speed drill and Kerrison rongeurs. Figure 1C shows this concept and why it theoretically would decrease the risk of dural tear or nerve root injury. The authors believe that this also adds extra safety when performing decompressions on awake patients since possible patient movement may have less likelihood of injury to the dura compared to the use of a high-speed rotating drill. Ultrasonic technology is expected to continually progress with further ergonomic improvements to allow for greater surgeon comfort as well as visualization of the working space. Exploring the cost-effectiveness, ergonomics, and long-term outcomes associated with ultrasonic aspiration in spine surgery could further support its widespread adoption. While the pilot study reflects a single-center experience, the results may inform future multicenter investigations into the broader applicability of ultrasonic aspiration in spine surgery. This technical note was submitted based on the magnitude of difference observed early on and while not powered for outcomes shows compelling preliminary workflow advantages through the observed magnitude of difference in Kerrison usage and reduced blood loss.

Limitations

The authors recognize several weaknesses within this study. The amount of bone removal from the drill prior to utilizing Kerrison rongeurs is a very individual decision by each surgeon with some surgeons preferring to drill more aggressively and extensively than other surgeons. Potential sources of bias include operator preference in tool selection and variability in Kerrison bite counting, which were not standardized across all cases. Counting the number of Kerrison bites on bone is a potential source for inappropriate data collection as different individuals were responsible for counting the bites in different cases. There was no randomization between which levels were performed with the drill versus which levels were performed with the ultrasonic aspirator. The fact that the same senior surgeons perform the procedures and the relatively small sample help to reduce this the effect of this variable. Making any conclusive statements regarding blood loss is not possible. The blood loss in these operations is at best an estimate based on the estimated amount of output through the suctions as registered in the machines minus the estimated amount of saline irrigation used. The authors included this data point only to help conclude that this technique does not significantly increase the blood loss of these operations. Despite these weaknesses and possible bias, the authors still believe that this early study and original workflow description warrants publication because of the unprecedented use of such tool (Sonopet) in tubular Minimally Invasive Approaches and the comparative use of Kerrison’s for bone removal among drill vs bone scalpel groups. While not powered for outcomes, the large and statistically significant reductions in Kerrison usage underscore the potential utility of this technique and justify future and larger prospective studies.

Conclusions

The use of an ultrasonic aspirator, also known as bone scalpels, in minimally invasive tubular laminectomies is safe, effective and may decrease the need for bone bites with a Kerrison rongeur. Future large scale prospective studies are needed to corroborate the senior authors observations reported in this original work with regards to minimally invasive spine surgery. The early findings presented in this pilot study justify broader investigation and illustrate how even a limited pilot cohort can reveal meaningful technical refinements in minimally invasive spine surgery.
