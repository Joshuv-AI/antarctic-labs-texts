Trace Al/Rb regulation stabilizes Cu-Fe5C2 active sites for ambient-pressure photothermal CO2 hydrogenation to C2-C4 olefin
Authors: Ling Li, Shangbo Ning, Fangqi Cheng, Mingjun Chen, Xingyuan San, Zhuofeng Hu, Linjie Gao, Yuan Tang, Yuchen Hao, Cuncai Lv, Yaguang Li, Jinhua Ye
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Photothermal hydrogenation of CO2 offers a solar-driven route to multicarbon hydrocarbons, but controlling carbide formation and maintaining reconstructed active sites remain challenging for iron-based catalysts. Here, we develop a trace Al/Rb co-promoted CuFe2O4 catalyst that enables efficient and stable photothermal CO2 hydrogenation to light olefin under ambient pressure. The optimized catalyst achieves high C2-C4 olefin selectivity of approximately 63%, suppressing CH4 selectivity and sustaining stable photothermal-catalytic performance for 200 h. Integrated experimental characterizations and theoretical calculations reveal that the CuFe2O4 precursor undergoes reaction-induced reconstruction to form Cu-Fe5C2 interfacial structures. Trace Al/Rb promotion facilitates the formation and stabilization of reconstructed metal–carbide interfaces, with Al regulating the Fe-centered coordination environment and carbide stability, while Rb tunes the interfacial electronic structure and adsorption–hydrogenation balance. This cooperative regulation stabilizes carbide-based interfacial active sites and promotes CO2-derived C1 intermediates toward C–C coupling and C2-intermediate stabilization, thereby favoring C2-C4 olefin formation.

Introduction

The direct conversion of carbon dioxide (CO2) into high-value chemicals such as light (C2-C4) olefin, utilizing abundant and clean solar energy, represents a promising route toward sustainable chemical production1–4. Globally, the annual demand for olefins, particularly ethylene and propylene, surpasses 300 million tons, with these C2+ olefins predominantly sourced from energy-intensive steam cracking of naphtha and light oils under high temperature and pressure conditions5. Recently, solar-driven catalytic hydrogenation of CO2 effectively harnesses the full spectrum of sunlight, facilitating the synergistic integration of photoexcitation and photothermal heating to enhance catalytic performance6–8. However, the direct CO2 Fischer-Tropsch synthesis (CO2-FTS) of C2-C4 olefin via photothermal catalysis involves a complex multi-electron transfer and hydrogenation process, resulting in a low selectivity for C2+ olefins compared to C1 products9–11. Achieving selective C–C coupling to light olefin under photothermal CO2 hydrogenation remains challenging because the active carbide phases are dynamically reconstructed and oxidized.

Fe-based catalysts exhibit promising potential for CO2-FTS, attributed to its cost-effectiveness, moderate activity in the reverse water-gas shift reaction, and the tunability of olefin product distributions in FTS. Nevertheless, single-component Fe catalysts often suffer from low catalytic activity and poor stability under photothermal low-temperature conditions. Similar electronic environments at adjacent Fe sites may induce dipole–dipole repulsion between neighboring C1 intermediates (*CO and *CHO), thereby impeding C–C coupling and favoring methanation pathways12. The selectivity of CH4 in hydrocarbon products derived from single Fe-based catalysts, such as 10% Fe/C and Fe3C, can remain above 60%13. Furthermore, the byproduct H2O, generated during CO2 hydrogenation, oxidizes iron carbide of active phase into Fe3O4, thereby severely degrading the activity and stability14,15. By constructing heteroatom-modified multiple active sites, the surface active structure and electronic properties of Fe-based catalysts are regulated, effectively promoting the activation of CO2 and optimizing the migration and coupling reaction of C1 intermediates.

Promoter engineering offers a versatile strategy for tuning catalytic performance by regulating local coordination environments, surface electronic structures, and adsorption properties. Spinel CuFe2O4, as a narrow-bandgap semiconductor with strong broadband light absorption, provides a suitable photothermal platform for CO2 hydrogenation16,17 .Al incorporation has been reported to enhance the morphological integrity and redox cyclability of ferrite oxygen carriers, whereas Al additives can promote substrate activation in Fe-catalyzed hydrogenation through Lewis-acid interactions18,19. In parallel, Rb substitution can tune the electronic structure of semiconductor photocatalysts, while Rb promotion in Fe3O4 catalysts can alter surface carbon species and the oxide–carbide distribution during thermocatalytic CO2 hydrogenation to light olefin20,21. These studies establish system-dependent promotional effects of Al and Rb, yet it remains unclear whether their distinct functions can be integrated within a single catalyst to jointly regulate active-site evolution, interfacial electronic structure, adsorption–hydrogenation behavior, and C–C coupling under photothermal CO2 hydrogenation. Establishing the mechanistic connection between trace-promoter regulation, active carbide-interface reconstruction, and selective C1-to-C2+ coupling remains a key challenge in designing catalysts22–25.

Here, we report a trace Al/Rb co-promoted CuFe2O4 catalyst for selective photothermal CO2 hydrogenation to light olefin. Under reaction conditions, the CuFe2O4 precursor undergoes in situ reconstruction to generate Cu-Fe5C2 interfacial sites, while the introduction of trace promoters regulates the local electronic and structural environment of the catalyst. Combined experimental characterization and density functional theory (DFT) calculations indicate that this regulation stabilizes metallic Cu and Fe carbide species, promotes electron redistribution at Fe-centered active sites, and reduces the energetic requirement for C–C coupling of C1 intermediates. As a result, the catalyst exhibits enhanced selectivity toward C2–C4 olefin together with improved catalytic stability under photothermal conditions. This work establishes a strategy for coupling electronic and structural regulation to stabilize dynamic active sites and promote selective CO2 conversion to multi-carbon hydrocarbons.

Results

To systematically elucidate the regulatory roles of Al and Rb in CuFe2O4-based catalysts for CO2 hydrogenation, a series of Al/Rb-modified CuFe2O4 catalysts was prepared by a sol-gel method (Supplementary Fig. 1). The Rb content was fixed while the Al loading was varied from 0.05% to 0.6%, yielding xAl-RCF catalysts, denoted as 0.05Al-RCF, 0.1Al-RCF, 0.3Al-RCF, 0.6Al-RCF. Subsequently, the Al content was fixed at 0.1% while the Rb loading was varied to obtain 0.1Al-yRb/CF catalysts (y = 0.1%, 0.25%, 0.4%, correspondingly named 0.1Al-R1CF, 0.1Al-RCF, 0.1Al-R2CF). Through the design of Al- and Rb- variable sample combinations, the independent effects of Al and Rb modifying amounts on the catalyst structure and performance can be investigated respectively, providing a comparative basis for revealing the Al/Rb synergistic regulatory mechanism. XRD results indicate that the fresh Al/Rb-modified catalysts are CuFe2O4-based precursors, although most samples show weak or broad diffraction features due to low crystallinity or partially amorphous structures. After activation, the diffraction peaks become more pronounced, indicating activation-induced structural reconstruction involving reduction and phase reorganization (Supplementary Fig. 2). SEM images reveal a smooth sheet-like morphology for fresh 0.1Al-RCF, with nanoparticles appearing on the nanosheet surface after activation (Supplementary Fig. 3).

Thermocatalytic performance of CO2 hydrogenation

The fresh catalysts were activated in situ under a mixed gas atmosphere (H2: CO2: Ar = 3: 1: 1) at 320 °C for 6 h. After activation, the thermocatalytic hydrogenation performance of CO2 was systematically evaluated. Among all catalysts, the 0.1Al-RCF catalyst exhibits the best overall performance. Under ambient pressure reaction conditions, it afforded a light olefin (C2-4 = ) yield of approximately 399 μmol g−1 h−1 and a C5+ hydrocarbon yield of about 101 μmol g−1 h−1 (Fig. 1a). To clarify the promotional effects of Al and Rb, the catalytic performance of 0.1Al-RCF was compared with that of CF and RCF activated under identical conditions. The unpromoted CF catalyst displayed a CH4 selectivity of ~57%, indicative of a strong tendency towards methanation (Fig. 1b). Upon introduction of Rb, the CH4 selectivity decreased markedly to ~42%, while the selectivity to C2-4= increased to ~46% (Fig. 1b). These results suggest that Rb plays a regulatory role in the reaction, effectively suppressing methanation while promoting C–C coupling toward C2-C4 hydrocarbons. Further incorporation of Al into the Rb-modified catalyst led to an additional and substantial enhancement in catalytic performance. Compared with RCF, 0.1Al-RCF delivered a 2.4-fold higher C2-4= yield and an approximately 1.3-fold higher selectivity (Fig. 1a, b), while also exhibiting the highest CO2 conversion among the catalysts examined. To further clarify the effect of Rb loading, a series of 0.1Al-Rb/CuFe2O4 catalysts was prepared with a fixed Al content and varied Rb contents. Supplementary Fig. 4 shows that 0.1Al-RCF gives the optimal C2-4= selectivity with suppressed CH4 formation. These results highlight the importance of an appropriate Al/Rb balance for efficient C–C coupling.

The catalytic stability of the 0.1Al-RCF catalyst was evaluated through long-term CO2 hydrogenation tests. After 27 h of reaction, the 0.1Al-RCF catalyst exhibited better stability and a higher yield of C2-C4 olefin compared with the RCF catalyst (Supplementary Fig. 5). Extended testing over 100 h revealed a gradual increase in CH4 selectivity for the 0.1Al-RCF catalyst, reaching ~24%. Correspondingly, the selectivity toward C2-4= slightly decreased to ~59%, yet remained at a relatively high level (Supplementary Fig. 6). These results indicate that the activated 0.1Al-RCF catalyst maintains excellent hydrogenation stability while preserving favorable C2+ hydrocarbon selectivity during prolonged operation. Furthermore, the influence of gas hourly space velocity on the catalytic performance of 0.1Al-RCF was systematically investigated. Increasing the space velocity from 2400 mL g−1 h−1 to 7200 mL g−1 h−1 resulted in a slight decrease in CH4 selectivity from ~19% to ~11%, accompanied by an increase in C5+ selectivity from ~10% to ~21%. Meanwhile, the CO2 conversion rate declined from ~36% to ~18% (Supplementary Fig. 7), indicating a typical trade-off between reactant residence time and catalytic CO2 conversion.

Photothermal-enhanced performance of CO2 hydrogenation

The photothermal CO2 hydrogenation performance of the activated catalysts was evaluated at ambient pressure with a space velocity of 3600 mL g−1 h−1. The time-dependent evolution of product selectivity reveals catalytic performance for the 0.1Al-RCF catalysts (Fig. 1c). The 0.1Al-RCF catalyst exhibits markedly different performance under photothermal conditions. During the initial 40 h, the selectivity towards C2-4 paraffins (C2-40) gradually decreases, while the selectivity towards C2-4= increases to ~62%. CH4 selectivity remains at a low level, while C2-4= selectivity gradually increases during the first 40 h. Upon prolonged operation (40–200 h), both CO2 conversion (~30%) and C2-4= selectivity (~63%) remain nearly constant (Fig. 1c), demonstrating that the active phase formed under photothermal conditions is highly stable.

The improved stability and sustained olefin selectivity over 0.1Al-RCF suggest that Al incorporation effectively stabilizes the active sites responsible for chain growth, while Rb suppresses excessive hydrogenation pathways. As a result, after 40 h of reaction, the 0.1Al-RCF catalyst achieves a C2-4 hydrocarbon selectivity of up to ~68% and an olefin-to-paraffin ratio of ~12. The high C2-C4 olefin selectivity together with low CH4 selectivity highlights the selective light-olefin formation enabled by 0.1Al-RCF under photothermal CO2 hydrogenation reaction (Supplementary Tables 1–2).

Disentangling photothermal and thermal contributions in CO2 hydrogenation

To elucidate the synergistic roles of photo and light-to-heat in the catalytic process, a series of control experiments was designed to distinguish the respective contributions of photocatalysis and thermocatalysis. The CO2 hydrogenation performance of the activated 0.1Al-RCF catalyst was therefore systematically investigated under photo-assisted thermocatalytic reaction.

Under purely thermocatalytic conditions, increasing the reaction temperature to 260 °C results in a CH4 yield of ~33 μmol g−1 h−1 and a C2-4= yield of ~42 μmol g−1 h−1 (Fig. 2a). The corresponding selectivity distribution shows that CH4 accounts for ~31% of total hydrocarbons, while C2-4= accounts for ~39% (Fig. 2b), indicating that thermocatalysis alone leads to a substantial extent of over-hydrogenation and limited olefin selectivity. In contrast, under light-assisted photothermal conditions, the catalytic performance is markedly enhanced at lower external heating temperatures. The C2-4= yield reaches a maximum of ~433 μmol g−1 h−1 at an external heating setpoint of 220 °C, corresponding to an actual catalyst-bed temperature of ~321 °C (Fig. 2c), while the highest C2-4= selectivity of ~56% is achieved at an external heating setpoint of 180 °C (Fig. 2d), accompanied by suppressed CH4 formation. Time-resolved measurements further confirm the role of light irradiation in promoting catalytic activity (Fig. 2e). Upon switching on the light, the hydrocarbon formation rate increases immediately, with a particularly strong response in the production of C2-C4 hydrocarbons. When the light is turned off, the activity rapidly decreases to the thermocatalytic baseline. This reversible response indicates that light irradiation provides an additional photothermal contribution to the surface reaction kinetics rather than merely causing irreversible catalyst reconstruction. Collectively, these results indicate that the enhanced performance originates from a photothermal synergistic effect, in which light-induced local heating and energy input facilitate C–C coupling while suppressing excessive hydrogenation pathways. This effect leads to both higher olefin formation rates and improved selectivity compared with thermo-driven catalysis.

To further exclude the possibility of purely photocatalytic contributions, additional experiments were conducted under light irradiation while suppressing temperature rise through active cooling. Under photocatalytic conditions, the detected hydrocarbons are dominated by CH4, indicating that light alone is insufficient to promote efficient C2-C4 olefin formation (Supplementary Fig. 8). Additionally, the influence of reaction pressure on catalytic performance was also investigated. Under purely thermocatalytic conditions, increasing the pressure results in only limited improvement in both yield and selectivity of C2-4= olefin (Supplementary Figs. 9–10). In contrast, the introduction of light significantly promoted the C–C coupling and hydrogenation reaction and improved the formation of olefin. At an optimal pressure of 5 bar, the C2-4= selectivity reaches ~52%, corresponding to a 1.7-fold increase compared with thermocatalysis, while the olefin yield increases by 9.3-fold to 3748 μmol g−1 h−1. These results further demonstrate that photo- and thermal co-activation effectively promote catalytic efficiency under reaction conditions.

Trace Al/Rb-regulated reconstruction of Cu/Fe5C2 interfacial active sites

The catalytic performance of the Al/Rb-promoted CuFe2O4 system is closely associated with its structural evolution under reaction conditions. To elucidate the nature of the active phase, the morphology and structure of the 0.1Al-RCF catalyst were systematically characterized before and after activation. The Brunauer-Emmett-Teller (BET) surface areas decrease from 143.7 to 82.3 m2 g−1 after activation (Supplementary Figs. 11–12), suggesting partial restructuring of the nanosheet morphology rather than extensive structural collapse. To further confirm the structural changes induced by photothermal CO2 hydrogenation, X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis was performed. Supplementary Fig. 13 shows the XRD results of the activated Al-RCF catalyst after CO2 hydrogenation for different durations (6 h, 60 h, 100 h, and 200 h). The XRD patterns exhibit more pronounced Fe5C2 peaks in the 40–50° region, while the diffraction peaks of CuFe2O4 and metallic Cu are still clearly observed. The absence of crystalline Al- or Rb-containing phases indicates that the trace promoters are highly dispersed and not to form detectable crystalline phases (Supplementary Fig. 14).

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) provides nanoscale structural evidence for the nanoscale structure of the fresh and activated catalyst. The high-resolution TEM (HR-TEM) images of fresh 0.1Al-RCF show lattice spacings of 0.29, 0.47 and 0.25 nm, indexed to the CuFe2O4 (220), (111), and (311) planes, respectively, confirming the preserved spinel precursor structure before activation (Supplementary Fig. 15a–c). Figure 3b, c and Supplementary Fig. 15d–f show the TEM and HR-TEM images of the activated 0.1Al-RCF catalyst. The lattice spacings are 0.205, 0.21, and 0.25 nm, corresponding to the (510) plane of Fe5C2, (111) plane of Cu, and (311) plane of CuFe2O4, respectively, which are in good agreement with the crystal phases identified in the XRD patterns26–29. Comparing the TEM images of the catalyst after 6 h, 100 h, and 200 h of the test (Supplementary Fig. 16–18), no substantial carbonaceous deposits were observed, and the particle size does not significantly increase. Thermogravimetric analysis was performed to evaluate the thermal stability of the catalyst before and after reaction. The activated 0.1Al-RCF catalyst after CO2 hydrogenation exhibited a slightly lower weight loss than the fresh catalyst (Supplementary Fig. 19). To further understand the spatial distribution and elemental dispersion in the activated catalyst, elemental mapping by HAADF-STEM (Fig. 3d–g) reveals that Cu and Fe species are locally enriched but remain well distributed within the nanosheet framework, indicating incomplete but controlled phase segregation during activation. In contrast, Al and Rb species are highly dispersed (Supplementary Fig. 20), supporting their role as structural or electronic promoters rather than as independent active phases. Such dispersion is beneficial for modulating the local chemical environment without disrupting the primary active phase. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) provides further insight into the surface chemical states. The fresh 0.1Al-RCF catalyst (Supplementary Fig. 21) is dominated by Cu2+ and Fe3+ species. After activation, the catalyst exhibits a Cu 2p3/2 component at 932.5 eV, indicating the co-existence of oxidized Cu2+ and reduced Cu0/Cu+ species (Supplementary Fig. 22)30. The Fe 2p spectrum also shows Fe0 component with remaining Fe3+ species, suggesting partial Fe reduction during activation. The characteristic peaks in the Al 2p and Rb 3d spectra belong to Al3+ and Rb+, respectively31,32, indicating that these promoters remain in oxidized states.

Based on the combined structural characterizations, the 0.1Al-RCF catalyst undergoes a pronounced transformation during CO2 hydrogenation, evolving from a single-phase CuFe2O4 precursor into a multi-phase system composed of metallic Cu, Fe5C2 and CuFe2O4 (Fig. 3h). In this reconstructed architecture, Cu-Fe5C2 clusters are anchored on partially transformed CuFe2O4 nanosheets, giving rise to abundant interfacial sites. These interfaces are expected to play a central role in catalysis, enabling CO2 activation, CO-derived intermediate formation, hydrogenation, and C–C coupling through cooperative interactions among oxide, carbide, and metallic domains. Meanwhile, the highly dispersed Al and Rb species likely contribute to stabilizing this multi-phase configuration and modulating the local electronic environment, thereby enhancing catalytic activity and selectivity.

Electronic regulation of reconstructed Cu/Fe5C2 interfaces by trace Al/Rb promoters

After confirming CuFe2O4 reconstruction and the high dispersion of Al and Rb, we compared the activated catalysts by XPS. The lower-binding-energy shifts of Al 2p and Rb 3d, together with variations in Cu+/Cu0 and low-valence/carbidic Fe species, suggest cooperative Al/Rb regulation of the reconstructed Cu-Fe5C2 interface (Supplementary Fig. 23–24). To further elucidate the electronic structure evolution and identify the active sites under photothermal CO2 hydrogenation, XPS and X-ray absorption spectroscopy (XAS) were performed on the activated 0.1Al-RCF catalyst after different reaction times. The Cu LMM Auger spectra show two characteristic features at ~916.8 and ~919.5 eV, corresponding to Cu+ and Cu030, respectively, indicating that Cu species exist in a partially reduced state under reaction conditions (Fig. 4a). The Fe 2p spectra (Fig. 4b) reveal oxidized Fe species together with low-binding-energy Fe0/Fe–C-related components29, indicating partial reduction and transformation of Fe during activation. A slight shift towards higher binding energy is observed with increasing reaction time, suggesting a gradual modulation of the Fe electronic environment, which is consistent with the formation of iron carbide phases accompanied by residual oxidic species. In contrast, the nearly unchanged Al 2p spectra indicate that Al remains in a stable oxidized state during reaction, supporting its role in stabilizing the local coordination environment (Fig. 4c)33. However, as shown in Fig. 4d, the Rb 3 d peak shifts toward higher binding energy. The positive shift of the Rb 3 d peak suggests a more electron-deficient local environment around Rb under reaction conditions, consistent with electronic interaction between Rb species and the reconstructed Cu-Fe interfacial domains. With prolonged reaction time, the peak intensities of Al 2p and Rb 3d orbitals in the 0.1Al-RCF catalyst gradually decrease (Fig. 4c, d). This is likely due to the in-situ surface redistribution of Al and Rb species under photothermal CO2 hydrogenation conditions. Despite their low contents, the highly dispersed Al and Rb species can stabilize the valence states of Cu and Fe through interfacial electronic interactions34.

To further determine the electronic structure and coordination state in fresh and activated 0.1Al-RCF, X-ray absorption near-edge structure (XANES) and extended X-ray absorption fine structure (EXAFS) tests were performed. The Cu K-edge XANES spectrum of fresh 0.1Al-RCF was located close to that of CuO, whereas that of activated 0.1Al-RCF was located between Cu foil and CuO (Fig. 4e), indicating the partial reduction of Cu during activation35. As shown in Fig. 4f, the Cu K-edge EXAFS spectrum of fresh 0.1Al-RCF exhibits a characteristic feature at ~1.5 Å, which can be assigned to first-shell Cu–O coordination. In contrast, the activated 0.1Al-RCF exhibits characteristic features at ~1.5 Å and ~2.1 Å, which can be assigned to Cu–O coordination in the first shell and Cu–Cu coordination associated with metallic Cu, respectively (Fig. 4f, Supplementary Fig. 25 and Table 3). The Cu K-edge WT-EXAFS contour plot further reveals a low-R feature attributable to Cu–O scattering together with a dominant Cu–Cu scattering feature at higher k values, closely resembling the WT signature of Cu foil (Fig. 4i, j and Supplementary Fig. 26)30. These results support the co-existence of oxide-derived Cu coordination and metallic Cu domains in the activated catalyst.

The Fe K-edge absorption of fresh 0.1Al-RCF was located close to that of Fe2O3, whereas that of activated 0.1Al-RCF was located between Fe foil and Fe2O3 (Fig. 4g), indicating that the valence state of Fe species was between 0 and +336. The Fourier-transformed EXAFS spectrum at the Fe K-edge of fresh 0.1Al-RCF exhibits a characteristic feature at ~1.5 Å, whereas the activated 0.1Al-RCF exhibits characteristic features at ~1.6 Å and ~2.2 Å (Fig. 4h). Considering the co-existence of oxide species evidenced by XPS and XRD, the short-range feature centered at 1.5–1.6 Å in the Fe K-edge EXAFS spectrum is assigned to overlapping Fe–O and Fe–C scattering contributions. The FT-EXAFS spectra show Fe–C/O- and Fe–Fe-related scattering features in the phase-uncorrected R space, while EXAFS fitting gives corresponding phase-corrected coordination distances of ~2.01 and ~2.58 Å, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 25 and Table 4), supporting the co-existence of oxide/carbon-coordinated Fe species and Fe5C2-derived local coordination37. Furthermore, the results of the wavelet transform contour plots of Fe K-edge weighted EXAFS were also consistent with the above analysis on activated 0.1Al-RCF (Fig. 4k–l and Supplementary Fig. 26). This result supports the co-existence of Fe5C2-derived carbide domains and residual oxide-derived Fe coordination in the activated catalyst. To independently evaluate the evolution of the Fe-containing phases during activation, Mössbauer spectra were collected after 12 and 20 h of activation. After 12 h of activation, Fe5C2 and ferric oxide accounted for ~15% and ~85% of the total iron phases, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 27 and Table 5). Extending the activation time to 20 h further increases the Fe5C2 spectral contribution, suggesting continued carburization and growth of Fe5C2-containing domains (Supplementary Fig. 28 and Table 6). Therefore, above analyses confirm that the catalyst contains partially reduced Cu species and Fe carbide/oxide domains, forming a mixed metal–carbide–oxide interface stabilized under photothermal reaction conditions.

Reaction pathway and C–C coupling during photothermal CO2 hydrogenation

To investigate the origin of selective C–C coupling during photothermal CO₂ hydrogenation, a combination of in situ spectroscopy, isotopic labeling, adsorption measurements, and theoretical calculations was employed. In situ diffuse reflectance Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (DRIFTS) provided direct insight into the evolution of surface intermediates during reaction38. Under a mixed H2/CO2 atmosphere, the peak intensity of adsorbed CO2 changed with the increase in photothermal catalytic reaction (Supplementary Fig. 29a). With the extension of reaction time, the enhanced band observed at 1302 cm−1 was assigned to *HCOO species39. The band intensity of CO species (at 2174 and 2113 cm−1) gradually increased30,40,41. Furthermore, the absorption peaks at 2930–2970 cm−1, attributed to *CH2 intermediates, indicate that the CO-derived intermediates further participate in C–C coupling processes leading to C2-C4 olefin (Supplementary Fig. 29b). To further determine the carbon source of the reaction products, we collected in situ DRIFTS to study the reaction trajectory of 0.1Al-RCF during the hydrogenation process of carbon-13 labeled 13CO2 (Fig. 5a). The positions of the 13CO peaks observed at 2126 and 2068 cm−1 showed significant differences from the positions of the 12CO peaks (2174 and 2113 cm−1)17. The promoter effect on the reaction pathway was further examined by comparative in situ DRIFTS under CO2/H2 and CO/H2 atmospheres. For CF, CO2/H2 produces CO-related bands at 2175, 2107 and 2052 cm−1, together with a dominant *CH feature at 3015 cm−1 and weak *CH2 and *HCOO signals, indicating that although CO2 can be activated to CO derived species, these intermediates are preferentially hydrogenated toward CH4 (Supplementary Fig. 30). After Al promotion, 0.1Al-CF shows stronger CO adsorption and more evident *CH features, especially under CO/H2, indicating that Al facilitates CO adsorption and hydrogenation; however, these intermediates are still mainly directed toward methanation (Supplementary Fig. 31).

Upon further co-promotion with Rb, the 0.1Al-RCF catalyst exhibited a much more pronounced dynamic evolution of *HCOO, *CO, and *CH2/*CH3 species (Supplementary Fig. 29a), suggesting that Al and Rb act cooperatively to promote CO2 activation, stabilize key C1 intermediates, and suppress excessive hydrogenation. In addition, in situ DRIFTS comparisons under CO2/H2 show distinct intermediate evolution over 0.1Al/Rb-Fe, 0.1Al/Rb-Cu, and 0.1Al-RCF (Supplementary Fig. 32). While the single-component samples show limited CO-related or *CH/*CH2 features, 0.1Al-RCF exhibits more pronounced CO-related, *CH2, and carbonaceous-species signals, suggesting cooperative Cu-Fe5C2 promotion of CO2 hydrogenation and C–C coupling to olefin.

Adsorption measurements provide further insight into how Al/Rb regulation tunes the surface availability of CO2/CO-derived species and reactive hydrogen, which is critical for subsequent carbon-species transformation. CO2-TPD shows that, compared with the fresh catalyst, the activated 0.1Al-RCF exhibits additional desorption peak at 388 °C and a markedly reduced high-temperature CO2-desorption signal, indicating that activation converts excessively strong CO2 binding into a more moderate adsorption–desorption regime (Supplementary Fig. 33)42. H2-TPD further shows that CF has concentrated high-temperature H2 desorption in the range of 500–650 °C, consistent with strong hydrogen retention and its high CH4 selectivity, whereas RCF gives weaker and broader H2 desorption. In contrast, 0.1Al-RCF displays a broad H2-desorption profile over 300–700 °C, suggesting a more distributed hydrogen environment that can supply reactive hydrogen while limiting excessive hydrogenation (Supplementary Fig. 34)43.

The promoter-dependent CO2-TPD coupled with online mass spectrometry (MS) profiles further reveal distinct but complementary effects of Al and Rb. 0.1Al-CF exhibits strong high-temperature CO2 desorption, indicating strong CO2 retention, whereas RCF shows weaker and broader CO2 desorption. 0.1Al-RCF displays multiple moderate CO2-desorption features, together with CO2-derived MS signals, suggesting that Al/Rb co-promotion does not simply maximize CO2 adsorption but facilitates the adsorption and subsequent evolution of CO2-derived species (Supplementary Fig. 35). CO-TPD-MS further shows promoter-dependent CO adsorption. RCF exhibits weak and broad CO desorption, while 0.1Al-CF shows a sharp high-temperature peak, indicating strong CO retention. In contrast, 0.1Al-RCF presents moderate CO desorption with correlated CO-, CH4-, and C2/C3-related fragments, suggesting that Al/Rb co-promotion tunes CO binding to a more suitable regime and facilitates the thermal evolution of CO-derived surface species (Supplementary Fig. 36).

Composition-controlled TPD-MS further clarifies the role of the reconstructed Cu/Fe structure. In CO2-TPD-MS, the 0.1Al/Rb-Cu component shows stronger CO2 adsorption than the 0.1Al/Rb-Fe component, whereas the complete 0.1Al-RCF catalyst exhibits more pronounced multi-peak desorption and stronger MS signals associated with CO2 and CH4 (Supplementary Fig. 37). In CO-TPD, both 0.1Al/Rb-Cu and 0.1Al/Rb-Fe components show only weak CO desorption and limited fragment evolution, while 0.1Al-RCF gives clear multi-peak CO desorption at 421.4, 534.5, and 613.7 °C (Supplementary Fig. 38). These comparisons indicate that the key function of the reconstructed Cu-Fe5C2 interface is to integrate CO2 adsorption-activation and reactive-hydrogen supply on Cu-related sites with the transformation of CO-derived species on Fe5C2 related domains. Al/Rb co-promotion further regulates this interfacial adsorption–hydrogenation balance, enabling C1 intermediates to undergo C–C coupling rather than excessive hydrogenation to CH4. This supports the trace-promoter strategy for stabilizing Cu-Fe5C2 interfacial sites and promoting selective C2–C4 olefin formation.

CO probe DRIFTS was performed to identify the CO adsorption sites in the reconstructed system. Distinct Cu+–CO, Cu0–CO, and clustered Cu0–CO bands are observed over 0.1Al/Rb-Cu, whereas 0.1Al/Rb-Fe shows only weak CO signals, indicating that reduced Cu, especially metallic Cu, promotes CO adsorption. In 0.1Al-RCF, the Cu–CO bands remain but display different relative intensities and profiles, suggesting that adjacent Fe5C2 modulates the electronic environment of Cu and the Cu–CO interaction (Supplementary Fig. 39). The transformation of CO-derived intermediates toward C2-C4 olefin over 0.1Al-RCF was further elucidated by in situ DRIFTS (Supplementary Fig. 40). Under photothermal conditions, a pronounced enhancement of hydroxyl-related bands (3600–3900 cm−1) was observed, which is indicative of an increased population of surface hydrogen species associated with the dynamically reconstructed Cu-Fe5C2 interfacial phase. When correlated with the evolution of *CO species identified in the DRIFTS and isotopic-labeling experiments, these results suggest that CO-derived intermediates undergo hydrogen-assisted transformation into *CHO/*CHx species, followed by C–C coupling to form C2 intermediates that further evolve into olefin.

To further elucidate the intrinsic origin of the enhanced C–C coupling over 0.1Al-RCF, DFT calculations were performed to support the experimental result. Considering that the CuFe2O4 precursor undergoes in situ reconstruction into a multiphase Cu/Fe5C2/CuFe2O4 structure under reaction conditions, separate theoretical models were constructed on the base of experiment data to describe the promoter effects in the oxide precursor/residual oxide domains and in the reconstructed carbide active phase.

Projected density of states (PDOS) calculations was carried out for CuFe2O4 and Al-doped CuFe2O4 models to clarify the electronic effect of Al incorporation (Fig. 5b). The raw data of the optimized computational models are provided in Supplementary Data 1. Compared with CuFe2O4, Al incorporation induces electronic redistribution around Fe sites, with the Fe 3d states shifting closer to the Fermi level. This modulation suggests possible enhanced interaction between Fe-centered sites and adsorbed reaction intermediates, which is favorable for CO2 activation and stabilization of CO-derived C1 species38–40. In contrast, the Cu-derived states show a less pronounced variation, indicating that Fe sites are more affected by Al incorporation and likely play a role in activating and transforming C1 intermediates.

The structural role of Rb was evaluated by placing Rb at different positions within the CuFe2O4 slab, followed by structural relaxation (Fig. 5c). The relaxed configurations show that Rb is more stable when located in subsurface or lattice-adjacent positions than when directly exposed on the top surface. This result suggests that Rb is unlikely to act as a direct catalytic center; instead, it may contribute to local structural stabilization and surface reconstruction. Such a stabilizing effect may help preserve the reconstructed interfacial environment during photothermal CO2 hydrogenation, thereby it is likely to maintain active configurations favorable for intermediate transformation and C–C coupling.

DFT calculations are performed to compare the CO2-to-*CHO conversion process on Cu, Fe5C2, and Cu-Fe5C2 substrate (Supplementary Fig. 41). The Cu-Fe5C2 shows the strong adsorption energy of activated *CO2 (−3.88 eV). In the next step, the energy only increases by about 0.21 eV, which is smaller than that for Fe5C2 (0.42 eV). This suggests the Cu-Fe5C2 provides a more favorable energetic profile than pure Fe5C2. With regard to pure Cu, it strongly stabilizes *HCOO (−4.61 eV) but makes the subsequent *HCOO-to-*CO and *CO-to-*CHO steps energetically uphill, indicating unfavorable *CHO formation. The Cu-Fe5C2 interface provides a balanced energy profile and makes *CHO formation from *CO exergonic. Because Fe5C2 is experimentally identified as a key reconstructed carbide phase, Al/Rb-modified Fe5C2 models were further constructed to evaluate CO2 activation and C–C coupling behavior (Supplementary Fig. 42). Structural analysis of CO2 adsorption shows that, compared with Fe5C2, Al/Rb-modified Fe5C2 induces stronger CO2 distortion and more pronounced C–O bond elongation, indicating facilitated CO2 activation on the promoted carbide surface. This result could be a possible support for the experimentally observed formation of CO-derived intermediates in the DRIFTS measurements.

The energetics of C–C coupling were then compared on Fe5C2 and Al/Rb-modified Fe5C2 (Fig. 5d). On Fe5C2, the formation of co-adsorbed *CHO and *CO from CO2 is thermodynamically favorable, with the relative energy decreasing to −0.97 eV. However, the subsequent formation of the *CHO–*CO intermediate is energetically uphill (0.92 eV), increasing the relative energy to −0.05 eV. This indicates that C–C coupling is relatively unfavorable on pristine Fe5C237,41. Al and Rb modification change this energy profile: the energy increase for *CHO–*CO formation decreases to 0.61 eV on Al-Fe5C2 and 0.88 eV on Rb-Fe5C2, indicating a stronger promotional effect of Al and Rb in this step (Supplementary Fig. 43). For Al/Rb-modified Fe5C2, the co-adsorbed *CHO + *CO state lies at −0.79 eV, while the uphill energy change for forming *CHO–CO is reduced to 0.57 eV, giving a lower relative energy of −0.22 eV for the coupled intermediate. The final hydrogenated *CHCH2 intermediate is further stabilized to −1.48 eV, which is much lower than that on Fe5C2. These results indicate that Al/Rb modification facilitates *CHO–CO coupling and stabilizes the subsequent C2 intermediate, thereby promoting C–C bond formation toward C2+ olefins production.

Overall, the theoretical results are consistent with the experimentally proposed cooperative roles of Al and Rb. Al and Rb are beneficial to the activity enhancement. After in situ reconstruction, the combined Al/Rb modification of Fe5C2 lowers the energetic penalty for *CHO–CO coupling and stabilizes C2 intermediates, providing a possible energetic basis for the enhanced C2–C4 olefins selectivity. These conclusions are based on the computational models employed here. Given the difficulty of reproducing the exact catalyst structure under operating conditions, the calculations should be regarded as supportive rather than definitive mechanistic evidence. In the future, a series of more models is necessary to understand the mechanism more deeply.

Discussion

In summary, we developed a trace of Al/Rb co-promoted CuFe2O4 catalyst for efficient photothermal CO2 hydrogenation to light olefins. Under ambient-pressure photothermal conditions, the optimized catalyst maintained high C2-C4 olefins selectivity and durability over 200 h. When the pressure was increased to 5 bar, the light olefins yield reached 3748 μmol g−1 h−1. Combined structural characterization, in situ spectroscopy, and DFT calculations reveal that Al/Rb promotion facilitates in situ reconstruction of CuFe2O4 into Cu-Fe5C2 containing interfacial structures, which favor CO-derived intermediate transformation and C–C coupling. By regulating the electronic structure and local coordination environment, trace Al/Rb co-promotion stabilizes reconstructed Cu-Fe5C2 interfacial sites and couples CO2 activation hydrogenation and CO-derived intermediate conversion, thereby promoting C–C coupling toward C2-C4 olefins. These findings demonstrate that trace promoter engineering is an effective strategy for stabilizing dynamic interfacial active sites and promoting selective CO2 conversion to C2+ hydrocarbons.

Methods

Chemicals

Copper nitrate hydrate (Cu(NO3)2·xH2O), Iron nitrate nonahydrate (Fe(NO3)3·9H2O), and Rubidium carbonate (Rb2CO3) were purchased from Shanghai Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. Aluminum nitrate nonahydrate (Al(NO3)3·9H2O) was purchased from Shanghai Macklin Biochemical Co., Ltd. The citric acid (C6H8O7·H2O) was purchased from Tianjin Bohuatong Chemical Products Sales Co., Ltd. Nitric acid (HNO3, AR), ammonia solution (NH3·H2O, AR), and ethylenediamine (C2H8N2, AR) were purchased from Damao Chemical Reagents Factory. All chemicals were used directly without further treatment.

Synthesis of fresh catalysts

Five Gram C6H8O7·H2O, 2 mmol Cu(NO3)2·xH2O, 3.95 mmol (Fe(NO3)3·9H2O), 0.025 mmol Rb2CO3 and 0.05, 0.1, 0.3 and 0.6 mmol Al(NO3)3·9H2O were mixed in the beaker, respectively. Then, 2.5 ml of HNO3 and 1 ml of C2H8N2 were added to the beaker with magnetic stirring. To adjust the pH = 6, a gel was calcined in a tubular furnace under an Ar atmosphere at 400 °C for 2 h, and annealed in air at 400 °C for 2 h to obtain fresh Al-Rb/CuFe2O4: 0.05Al-RCF, 0.1Al-RCF, 0.3Al-RCF, and 0.6Al-RCF. Rb/CuFe2O4, Al/CuFe2O4, Rb/Fe2O3 and CuFe2O4 were prepared following the same procedure described above, with the Al component, the Rb component, and both the Al and Rb components omitted, respectively.

Thermocatalytic hydrogenation of CO2

The thermocatalytic activity of catalysts for hydrogenation of CO2 to produce C2+ hydrocarbons was tested by the flow bed reactor (XM190708-007) in continuous flow form. Typically, 50 mg of catalyst was placed in a quartz flow reactor. The mass flow controller regulated the feed gas of CO2/H2/Ar = 1/3/1 with a 3–10 sccm flow rate. After activation at 320 °C for over 6 h, the reaction products were tested by Gas Chromatography (GC, PANNA A91 Plus), equipped with FID (PLOT Q chromatographic column) and Thermal Conductivity Detector (TCD, column model TDX-01).

Photothermal-catalytic hydrogenation of CO2

Photothermal-catalytic reaction evaluation setup (PLR RVTF-PM, Beijing Perfectlight) was used to test the activity of photothermocatalysis CO2 hydrogenation for C2+ hydrocarbons production in a continuous flow manner. Typically, 50 mg of a fresh Al-Rb/CuFe2O4 catalyst was placed in a quartz flow reactor. The feed gas of CO2/H2/Ar = 1/3/1 was regulated at a flow rate of 3–10 sccm using a mass flow controller. After activation at 320 °C for over 6 h, the reaction products under light irradiation were detected using GC PANNA A91 Plus, equipped with FID (PLOT Q chromatographic column) and TCD (TDX-01 chromatographic column).

Characterization

Powder X-ray diffraction measurement (Bruker AXS D8 ADVANCE) was performed using a diffractometer equipped with 9 kW rotating-anode X-ray source operated at 3 kW. Scanning electron microscopy (FEI Nova Nano SEM450) was used to identify the overall and porous morphology of the sample. The spherical aberration-corrected transmission electron microscope (JEOL ARM200F) was utilized to determine the morphology and crystal structure of the nanostructure and EDS. The specific surface areas of the catalysts before and after reaction were determined from N2 adsorption-desorption isotherms using the BET method (BELSORP MAX Microtrac MRB). The valence states of various metal elements were studied using the XPS (Thermo ESCALAB 250Xi), with monochromatic Al Ka (hν = 1486.6 eV), 150 W power, 500μm beam spot, and an energy analyzer with a fixed transmission energy of 30 eV. The data were corrected with the binding energy of the sample C 1s main peak set at 284.8 eV. The 57Fe Mössbauer Spectrometer (MFD-500AV) was used for room temperature testing to characterize the iron phases, valence states, and coordination structures in the sample. The sample prepared with 57Fe significantly improved the Mössbauer absorption efficiency and spectral quality. The Shanghai Synchrotron Radiation Facility (SSRF) was used to collect beamline data to obtain Fe/Cu K-edge X-ray absorption structure data. The IFEFFIT software was used for energy scale calibration, background signal correction, and intensity normalization. Five backgrounds were removed in K-space using a five-domain cubic spline to extract EXAFS oscillations. The obtained K-space data were further transformed using Fourier transformation.

Computation

Density functional theory calculations were performed using the projector-augmented-wave method with the Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof generalized gradient approximation functional. Periodic boundary conditions were applied in all three spatial directions. The computational model was constructed according to the experimentally determined or geometry-optimized crystal structure. For surface calculations, a periodic slab model was employed, and the exposed surface was generated from the corresponding bulk structure. A vacuum region of approximately 15 Å was introduced along the direction perpendicular to the surface to avoid artificial interactions between adjacent periodic images. A plane-wave basis set was employed to expand the smooth part of the wavefunctions, with a kinetic-energy cutoff of 450 eV. The electronic self-consistent-field convergence criterion was set to 1 × 10−5 eV. All atomic coordinates were optimized according to the constraints defined in the structural model, while the lattice parameters were kept fixed during slab calculations. Structural optimization was considered converged when the Hellmann–Feynman force acting on each unconstrained atom was smaller than 1 × 10−2 eV Å−1. No symmetry constraint was imposed during structural relaxation, allowing the atoms to fully respond to local bonding and surface reconstruction effects. The Brillouin-zone integrations were performed using a Γ-centered 2 × 2 × 2 k-point mesh. The same computational parameters were consistently applied to all investigated models to ensure a reliable comparison of their relative energies and electronic properties.

In situ diffuse reflectance Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy

In situ DRIFTS measurements were performed using a Fourier-transform infrared spectrometer (INVENIO S, Bruker) equipped with a liquid-nitrogen-cooled detector. Spectral acquisition, processing, and analysis were carried out using OPUS software. Prior to measurement, the sample was placed in a reaction cell and pretreated under an Ar flow at 180 °C for 60 min to remove adsorbed water and surface impurities. After cooling to room temperature, a background spectrum was collected under an inert atmosphere. The gas feed was then switched to an Ar/H2/CO2 mixture with a volume ratio of 1/3/1 for in situ DRIFTS measurements of CO2 hydrogenation, or to an Ar/CO mixture with a volume ratio of 95/5 for CO-probe experiments. Spectra were continuously collected during the reaction to monitor the dynamic evolution of adsorbed surface species and reaction intermediates.

Temperature-programmed desorption coupled with online mass spectrometry

CO-, CO2-, and H2-temperature-programmed desorption experiments were performed using a fully automated chemisorption analyzer (ChemBET Pulsar) coupled with an online mass spectrometer. Fresh and activated samples were pretreated in a He flow at 200 °C for 60 min to remove physically adsorbed impurities and surface contaminants. After cooling to 40 °C, the samples were exposed to CO, CO2, or H2 for 30 min to allow gas adsorption. The system was then purged with He at 30 mL min−1 for 1 h to remove weakly adsorbed or gas-phase species. Subsequently, the samples were heated from 40 to 800 °C at a ramping rate of 10 °C min−1 under He flow, and the desorbed species were continuously monitored by online mass spectrometry.
