The quadro-iliac plane block: a systematic review of a promising novel technique
Authors: Alireza Shakeri, Mohammadreza Alinejadfard
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe Quadroiliac Plane Block (QIPB) is an emerging fascial plane block designed to provide extensive analgesia for various surgical procedures. While its use has been described in preliminary reports, a comprehensive evaluation of the existing evidence is needed.ObjectiveThis systematic review aimed to evaluate the clinical applications, efficacy, and safety of the QIPB by analyzing all available published literature.MethodsA comprehensive search of PubMed, Scopus, Embase, and Web of Science was conducted up to September 4, 2025. Inclusion criteria encompassed all study designs, including randomized controlled trials (RCTs), case series, and case reports. Data on surgical context, pain scores, rescue analgesia, and adverse events were extracted. The quality of RCTs was assessed using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 tool, while the JBI Critical Appraisal Checklist was used for observational studies.ResultsNineteen studies, including two RCTs and 17 observational studies comprising a total of 164 patients, met the inclusion criteria. The RCTs provided the primary efficacy data: one demonstrated that the QIPB was non-inferior to the Erector Spinae Plane (ESP) block for lumbar discectomy, while the other suggested superiority over standard analgesia for hip arthroplasty. Observational studies described the block’s use in a wide range of orthopedic, spinal, and abdominal surgeries, with reported outcomes appearing to be context-dependent.ConclusionThe QIPB is an emerging regional anesthesia technique with preliminary evidence suggesting non-inferiority to the ESP block in some contexts. However, the current evidence is limited by the variable quality of the included studies and inconsistent safety reporting. The definitive role of the QIPB will be clarified by several ongoing RCTs that are expected to provide higher-quality evidence on its comparative effectiveness and safety.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12893-025-03320-x.

Introduction

Regional anesthesia has undergone significant advancements in recent years, with fascial plane blocks emerging as a cornerstone in managing acute and chronic pain [1]. These blocks offer targeted analgesia by depositing local anesthetics into specific fascial planes, thereby blocking the transmission of pain signals from the surgical site or affected region [2]. Compared to systemic opioids, fascial plane blocks provide several advantages, including superior pain control, reduced opioid consumption, fewer systemic side effects (e.g., nausea, vomiting, respiratory depression), and enhanced recovery after surgery [3]. As a result, they have become an integral part of multimodal analgesia strategies in various surgical and non-surgical settings.

These benefits have solidified their role in multimodal analgesia strategies across a range of surgical and non-surgical settings. The success of established fascial plane blocks—such as the Transversus Abdominis Plane (TAP) block, Quadratus Lumborum Block (QLB), and Erector Spinae Plane Block (ESPB)—has fueled innovation in regional anesthesia [4]. Widely used for abdominal, thoracic, and lumbar procedures, these blocks provide effective somatic and, in some cases, visceral pain relief, demonstrating their versatility and clinical value [4–7].

Inspired by these advantages, anesthesiologists have sought to expand the scope of fascial plane blocks by identifying new anatomical targets to address unmet needs in pain management. One such innovation is the Quadro-Iliac Plane Block (QIPB), which targets the fascial plane between the inner iliac crest and the posterior surface of the quadratus lumborum muscle (QL) [8]. This novel approach achieves extensive nerve coverage, including the ilioinguinal, iliohypogastric, subcostal, genitofemoral, and lumbar plexus, making it a promising technique for pain control in the lumbosacral, abdominal, and hip regions [8].

QIPB is an emerging technique with the potential to extend the benefits of fascial plane blocks to a broader range of clinical scenarios, including complex surgical cases and possibly chronic pain conditions. However, despite its theoretical promise, clinical evidence for QIPB remains limited, primarily consisting of small randomized controlled trials (RCTs), case reports, and small case series. This early stage of research underscores the need for further investigation to validate its efficacy and safety across diverse applications.

Motivated by the potential of QIPB as an evolution of fascial plane block technology, this review evaluated the current evidence to assess its clinical applications, efficacy, and safety. By exploring its role across diverse surgical and pain management contexts, this review aimed to elucidate QIPB’s advantages and identify evidence gaps requiring further study. Ultimately, this study aimed to provide clinicians with a clearer understanding of QIPB’s potential as a valuable addition to regional anesthesia techniques.

Methods

The protocol for this systematic review was registered prospectively with the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO), registration number: CRD420251016848. This study was conducted in accordance with the PRISMA guideline checklist to ensure methodological rigor and transparency [9].

Search strategy

A systematic search was conducted across PubMed, Scopus, Embase, and Web of Science. The initial search was conducted up to March 16, 2025, and was updated on September 4, 2025, during the manuscript revision process to capture the most current literature. The search query used for this study was: “Quadro-iliac plane block” OR “Quadro iliac plane block” OR “Quadroiliac plane block” OR “QIPB” OR “Quadro iliac plane” OR “Quadro iliac block “OR “QIP block” to identify all relevant articles on this topic. The full search strings adapted for each database are provided in Supplementary Table S1.

To supplement the database search, a manual search was also performed. This involved screening the reference lists of all included articles and conducting targeted searches on Google Scholar and searching the ClinicalTrials.gov registry. This supplemental search was performed to identify additional literature, including conference abstracts and ongoing trials. No restrictions on publication date or language were applied to either search strategy.

Screening process & selection criteria

All retrieved records from the databases were exported to EndNote 21 software (Thomson Reuters, Toronto, ON, Canada). After removing duplicates, two independent reviewers (A.S. and M.A.) screened titles and abstracts for relevance. Potentially eligible studies then underwent a full-text assessment by both reviewers. Any disagreements regarding study inclusion at this stage were resolved through discussion until consensus was reached.

Our inclusion criteria consisted of any studies, including RCTs, cohorts, case controls, cross-sectional studies, case reports, and case series that evaluated the use of the quadro-iliac plane block in patients, regardless of the clinical context. Studies were excluded if they did not report original clinical outcomes. This included review articles, letters to the editor without original patient data, and purely anatomical or cadaveric studies. However, while these non-clinical studies were excluded from the formal results synthesis, relevant anatomical reports were referenced to provide context for the block’s hypothesized mechanism in the Discussion section.

Data extraction

Using a standardized form, two reviewers (M.A. and A.S.) independently extracted data from included studies. The extracted data from both reviewers were then compared, and any discrepancies were resolved through discussion and consensus. these data were then organized into two summary tables. Key elements included first author, year of publication, study design, type of surgery, drug and dose used for the QIPB, side of block (unilateral or bilateral), number of patients, pain scale (numerical rating scale (NRS) or visual analogue scale (VAS)), details of rescue analgesia, key findings, and reported adverse events. Postoperative pain scores were recorded at intervals such as 0, 1, 2, 4, 6, 8, 12, 16, and 24 h, categorized as at rest or during movement. Rescue analgesia details, including medications like tramadol, meperidine, dexketoprofen, or fentanyl, were also documented.

Quality assessment

The methodological quality and risk of bias of each included study were critically appraised using a tool appropriate for its design. For case reports and case series, we used the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Critical Appraisal Checklist for Case Reports [10].

The JBI checklist was used to evaluate the methodological rigor and risk of bias across the following eight domains:

Instead, the results of the critical appraisal were used to inform the interpretation of the findings and are discussed in detail in the Limitations section of the Discussion.

Data synthesis

Due to the significant clinical and methodological heterogeneity of the included studies, a statistical meta-analysis was not considered appropriate. Therefore, a narrative synthesis was performed to summarize the findings. Data were categorized by type of surgery to structure the summary of reported clinical outcomes.

Results

Literature search

From the database search, 71 records were initially identified. After removing duplicates, 30 articles were screened by title and abstract, resulting in 24 articles being selected for full-text review. After assessing the full-text articles for eligibility, a final 19 studies were included in this systematic review.

Separately, a search of clinical trial registries was conducted to identify relevant ongoing research. This search identified four additional registered trials that are not included in the results of this review but are discussed in the “Future Directions” section. Study characteristics

The body of evidence on the QIPB is recent, with all 19 included studies published between 2024 and 2025. This literature consists of two RCTs and 17 case reports or case series. The two RCTs, both conducted in Turkey, enrolled a combined total of 124 patients. One trial investigated the QIPB for hip arthroplasty, with a control group receiving standard intravenous analgesia [12], while the other compared the QIPB to the ESP block for single-level lumbar discectomy [13] (Table 1. study characteristics of randomized controlled trials.).

The remaining 17 studies were case reports and series, documenting an additional 40 individual cases where the block was used. These case studies, with sample sizes ranging from one to seven patients, cover a broad spectrum of applications, including orthopedic [14–18], general [19–23], neurosurgical [24, 25], urological [26, 27], obstetric [28], and endocrine surgeries [29], as well as chronic pain management [30]. Pain and recovery were assessed using various tools, including the NRS, the VAS, the Face, Legs, Activity, Cry, and Consolability (FLACC) scale for pediatric patients, and the Quality of Recovery-15 (QoR-15) score. These procedures were performed under either general or spinal anesthesia. The block characteristics varied, but most commonly involved bupivacaine at concentrations of 0.125% or 0.25%, with volumes generally ranging from 20 mL to 50 mL per side. Ropivacaine and levobupivacaine were also used, and one study reported adding dexamethasone as an adjuvant. Bilateral blocks were typically applied for spinal surgeries, Cesarian delivery, and some abdominal procedures, whereas unilateral blocks were common for surgeries like hip fracture and inguinal hernia repair (Table 2. study characteristics of case reports and case series based on the type of surgery.).

Efficacy of QIPB

The efficacy of the Quadroiliac Plane Block has been evaluated in 19 studies comprising 164 patients, with evidence drawn from two RCTs and 17 case reports or case series.

Findings from RCTs

The most compelling evidence comes from the two RCTs. One trial involving 64 patients undergoing hip arthroplasty found that the QIPB group experienced significant postoperative pain relief and a better quality of recovery compared to the control group [12]. The other RCT, a head-to-head comparison with the ESP block in 60 patients for single-level lumbar discectomy, established that QIPB was not inferior in its analgesic effect, with comparable 12-hour NRS scores and 24-hour tramadol consumption [13].

Observational findings

The 17 case reports and series describe the block’s use and reported outcomes across 40 individual cases in various clinical settings, with detailed data provided in Supplementary Tables S2 and S3.

Orthopedic and spinal surgeries

In the case series for major orthopedic and spinal procedures, the need for supplemental analgesia was frequently reported. For hip fracture surgery, where NRS scores on movement reached 4, all four patients required rescue tramadol at 24 h [14]. Similarly, for proximal femoral nail surgery, where VAS scores on movement also reached 4, two of three patients used their tramadol patient-controlled analgesia (PCA) multiple times [18]. Outcomes were variable in spinal procedures. For lumbar stabilization, where VAS scores on movement were reported with a median of 3 at 24 h, three of five patients required rescue tramadol [16]. In a scoliosis report, where NRS scores on movement peaked at 4, both patients also required rescue analgesia [15]. In contrast, for lumbar multi-level instrumentation, postoperative pain scores were reported as not exceeding an NRS of 4, with no rescue fentanyl administered via PCA [17].

General surgeries

Following laparoscopic cholecystectomy, one patient required a single dose of rescue analgesia when their NRS score peaked at 5 [23]. In several inguinal hernia repair cases, reported pain scores were low, with NRS scores on movement not exceeding 3, and no postoperative rescue analgesia was required [19–21]. After an appendectomy, one patient needed a single dose of dexketoprofen when their VAS score reached 4 at the 16-hour mark [22].

Neurological surgeries

For neurosurgical procedures, including tethered cord release in a pediatric patient and single-level lumbar discectomies, reported pain scores were low [24, 25]. In the case of the tethered cord release, no rescue analgesia was required, and in the lumbar discectomy series, only one of five patients required it [24, 25].

Urological surgeries

For laparoscopic renal transplantation, one patient required rescue meperidine when their NRS score reached 5 in the first postoperative hour [27]; however, pain scores for most patients in this series were 0 or 1 by 24 h. A patient undergoing retrograde intrarenal surgery was managed with no postoperative opioids, with NRS scores on movement remaining at or below 4 [26].

Other surgical and Non-Surgical contexts

The case reports cover several other applications. In obstetrics, two of three patients who had a cesarean delivery required rescue paracetamol when their NRS scores on movement reached 5 [28]. For an endocrine surgery case, the patient’s pain scores reportedly did not exceed an NRS of 4, with no rescue analgesia needed [29]. Finally, a report on a non-surgical application for chronic low back pain described a rapid reduction in the patient’s NRS score from 9 to 1 within ten minutes of the block [30].

Safety of the QIPB

The systematic reporting of adverse events was inconsistent across the included observational studies. While many studies did not list safety as a primary outcome, those that did report on safety noted a favorable profile. The strongest evidence comes from the RCT comparing the QIPB to the ESP block, which formally assessed safety and found no difference between the two techniques [13]. Furthermore, the consistently reported absence of postoperative nausea and vomiting (PONV) is likely a secondary benefit of the block’s opioid-sparing effect, characterized by reduced opioid use, contributing to a better quality of recovery as noted in the hip arthroplasty RCT [12].

Quality assessment

The methodological quality of the two included RCTs was assessed using the RoB 2 tool. The study by Turan et al. [13] was judged to have an overall low risk of bias, with a low-risk rating across all five assessment domains. In contrast, the study by Satici et al. [12] was judged to have an overall high risk of bias. This determination was based on a high risk of bias identified in the domains of “Deviations from intended interventions,” due to issues with the blinding of participants and personnel, and “Selection of the reported result,” due to discrepancies between the registered trial protocol and the final published article.

Case reports were assessed across multiple domains of the JBI checklist to evaluate their quality and risk of bias. In terms of patient demographics, thirteen studies were rated at low risk of bias and four at high, showing that most authors included sufficient details about age, sex, or background, though a small number omitted this important information. The description of patient history and clinical timeline was also satisfactory, with twelve studies rated low risk and five high, indicating that the majority offered a clear chronological account of disease progression, though gaps remained in some reports.

The clinical condition of patients at presentation was among the best-reported domains, with sixteen studies judged low risk and only one at high, reflecting consistent and detailed documentation of baseline status. Reporting of diagnostic tests and results was similarly strong, with fifteen studies at low risk and two at high, suggesting that most case reports adequately outlined both the methods used and the findings obtained.

The most robust domains were the descriptions of interventions, post-intervention conditions, and takeaway lessons. All seventeen studies were rated low risk in these categories, demonstrating that treatments, patient outcomes, and broader clinical insights were consistently well communicated across the reports.

In contrast, adverse or unanticipated event reporting was weaker. Nine studies were at low risk, while eight were at high, highlighting that safety outcomes were inconsistently documented. While some reports provided transparent accounts of complications, others neglected to mention adverse events, reducing the completeness of their clinical contribution. Taken together, these findings indicate that most case reports demonstrated strong methodological reporting across nearly all domains, with adverse event documentation standing out as the primary area of concern.

Discussion

This is the first systematic review to gather the published literature on the QIPB. Our findings reveal an emerging but methodologically limited evidence base, comprising two RCTs and 17 observational studies. The RCTs provide the most significant insights. The low-risk trial demonstrated that the QIPB was non-inferior to the established ESP block for lumbar discectomy, suggesting it is a viable alternative in this context. The second RCT suggested the QIPB was superior to standard analgesia for total hip arthroplasty; however, this conclusion must be interpreted with caution, as the study was judged to have a high risk of bias.

The observational studies show that the block has been applied in a wide variety of surgical procedures, though the outcomes appear to be context-dependent. For instance, in procedures such as inguinal hernia repair and lumbar multi-level instrumentation, the reported need for postoperative rescue analgesia was low. In contrast, for high-pain orthopedic surgeries like hip fracture and proximal femoral nail surgery, supplemental analgesia was consistently required, suggesting the QIPB may be insufficient as a sole analgesic in these settings. This variability is likely related to the block’s hypothesized mechanism of an extensive fascial plane spread to the lumbar plexus. While this provides broad sensory coverage, the resulting analgesic density may not be sufficient for procedures with intense pain pathways without a multimodal approach.

The potential of the QIPB as part of an opioid-sparing strategy, aimed at reducing perioperative opioid requirements, has been highlighted in a case report of a high-risk patient undergoing retrograde intrarenal surgery. In this instance, the block was associated with postoperative NRS scores remaining at or below 4, and the patient had an opioid-free postoperative course. This case suggests the block may have utility in complex patients where minimizing opioid-related adverse events is a priority [26].

The application of the QIPB has also been reported for chronic pain management. In a case of chronic low back pain, a rapid and significant reduction in pain was described, with an NRS score decreasing from 9 to 1 within 10 min of block administration [30]. While the mechanism for such a rapid effect in a chronic pain state is speculative, it is hypothesized to be related to the block’s ability to target sensitized nerve endings within fascial planes. These preliminary findings suggest a potential role for the QIPB beyond acute postoperative pain, though substantial further investigation is required to explore its use in chronic pain conditions.

A key consideration when interpreting the findings of this review is the substantial clinical heterogeneity across the included studies. The evidence base encompasses a wide spectrum of procedures, from minimally invasive laparoscopic surgery to complex spinal and orthopedic operations, as well as varying patient populations and local anesthetic regimens. This makes it challenging to synthesize the findings into a single conclusion.

The pain pathways and degree of surgical trauma in a laparoscopic cholecystectomy, for example, are vastly different from those in major spinal surgery. Consequently, a reported outcome in one context cannot be readily extrapolated to another. Therefore, the findings for the QIPB should be considered context-specific, and its utility likely varies significantly depending on the surgical procedure and patient population.

A definitive assessment of the QIPB’s safety profile is challenging due to inconsistent reporting of adverse events across the included observational studies. Many case reports did not systematically document complications. However, several of the reports that did address safety explicitly noted an absence of adverse events such as nausea, vomiting, or motor blockade.

More robust evidence comes from the RCT comparing the QIPB to the ESP block, which formally assessed safety as an outcome and found no significant difference between the two techniques. Furthermore, the frequently reported absence of postoperative nausea and vomiting is likely a secondary benefit of the block’s opioid-sparing effect, reflected in a reduction of opioid consumption, which contributes to an improved quality of recovery, as was measured by the Quality of Recovery-15 score in the RCT on hip arthroplasty.

Anatomical basis and mechanism of action

The anatomical and clinical evidence suggest that QIPB is effective in addressing both somatic and visceral pain, making it a versatile tool in regional anesthesia. The block’s ability to target the fascial plane between the QL muscle and the iliac crest allows for widespread distribution of local anesthetic, reaching key nerves such as the lumbar plexus, ilioinguinal, iliohypogastric, subcostal, and genitofemoral nerves [8] (Figs. 3 and 4). This extensive spread enables QIPB to provide analgesia for somatic pain as well as visceral pain.

Another fascial plane block targeting nerves near the QL muscle is the QLB. The nerve targets typically include consistent blockade of the subcostal, iliohypogastric, and ilioinguinal nerves. In some cases, the genitofemoral and lateral femoral cutaneous nerves may also be blocked. Caudally, the spread of local anesthetic can sometimes reach the lumbar nerve roots, though this outcome varies depending on the technique and injectate volume used [31].However, as a deep block, it requires precise needle placement and a detailed understanding of anatomy, making it more challenging than superficial blocks like the TAP or ESPB, and the depth increases the risk of potential complications, though the QIPB can be used as an alternative to improve safety and simplicity [30].Clinically, QIPB has been used in procedures where both somatic and visceral pain are relevant, such as lumbar spine surgery, proximal femoral surgery, and renal transplantation [16, 18, 27]. In these cases, the block has demonstrated pain-relieving properties, supporting its dual efficacy. For example, in abdominal surgeries, QIPB can alleviate somatic pain from the abdominal wall while also addressing visceral pain from internal organs, which is often inadequately managed by other regional techniques.

Theoretically, the mechanism of QIPB further supports its dual efficacy. The spread of local anesthetic due to fluid pressure in the fascial planes can disrupt fascial adhesions and target free nerve endings, which are involved in both somatic and visceral pain pathways [32]. This mechanism not only enhances the spread of the anesthetic but also provides broader analgesic coverage, making QIPB a promising option for complex pain management scenarios [8].

Clinical implementation

Implementing the QIPB requires practical considerations regarding training, equipment, patient selection, and protocol integration.

Recommended training requirements

Clinicians should have prior experience with ultrasound-guided truncal blocks like QLB and TAP, as familiarity with the sonoanatomy is essential [33]. Standard equipment includes an ultrasound with a low-frequency curvilinear probe (2–5 MHz), an 80–100 mm echogenic needle, and sterile supplies. Full resuscitation equipment for local anesthetic systemic toxicity must be readily available [33].

Patient selection criteria

The QIPB is suitable for patients undergoing procedures where somatic and visceral analgesia of the lower torso, hip, or groin is desired (e.G., hip, inguinal, or renal surgeries) [14–29]. It should be avoided in patients with standard contraindications, such as local infection, uncorrected coagulopathy, or LA allergy. Relative contraindications include severe spinal deformity or prior retroperitoneal surgery. Common regimens involve 20–40 mL per side of low-concentration, long-acting local anesthetic like bupivacaine 0.125–0.25% or ropivacaine 0.2–0.25%), with careful dose calculation in pediatric or small adult patients [16, 33].

Protocols integration

The QIPB can be performed pre-, intra-, or postoperatively. It is not a standalone technique and should be integrated into a multimodal analgesia regimen, alongside other non-opioid analgesics. Meticulous aseptic technique and real-time needle visualization are critical to minimize complications [16, 33].

Comparative analysis of the QIPB

The QIPB is an emerging interfascial plane technique for truncal anesthesia. Its value is best understood when compared against other methods, including the various QLBs, the ESPB, and the TAP block. This comparison focuses on analgesic efficacy, safety, and technical considerations.

QIPB vs. QLB variants

The QIPB is conceptually related to the QLB as both target fascial planes around the QLM. However, the QIPB is distinguished by its specific, more superficial, and caudal injection point at the QLM’s attachment to the iliac crest [8]. This anatomical distinction leads to key comparative points. The posterior (QLB2) and anterior/transmuscular (QLB3) approaches provide broad somatic and visceral analgesia, making them effective for major abdominal surgeries [34]. The QIPB is hypothesized to achieve similar coverage with a single injection. Cadaveric studies show that QIPB injectate spreads to the anterior and posterior surfaces of the QLM, staining the lumbar plexus and key nerves (ilioinguinal, iliohypogastric) [8]. This suggests a single QIPB may replicate the effects of more complex QLB approaches. Deeper QLB approaches, particularly the QLB3, are advanced techniques carrying risks like kidney injury, peritoneal puncture, or unintentional femoral nerve block, causing quadriceps weakness [34]. The QIPB was designed to mitigate these risks. Its target uses the iliac crest as a bony backstop, preventing deep needle advancement [8]. This feature may reduce procedural difficulty and prevent complications associated with deeper blocks.

QIPB vs. ESPB

The ESPB is another versatile interfascial plane block noted for its safety and technical simplicity.

A recent RCT directly compared the QIPB and ESPB for lumbar discectomies. The study concluded that the QIPB was not inferior in analgesic effect and safety outcomes to the ESPB. Both groups had similar 12-hour pain scores, total tramadol consumption, and side effect incidence (nausea, vomiting) [13]. Crucially, no motor block occurred in either group, highlighting a key safety advantage.

QIPB vs. TAP block

The TAP block is a common truncal block, but its utility is limited to somatic pain of the anterolateral abdominal wall.

The TAP block’s primary limitation is its lack of visceral analgesia. This is why QLBs consistently provide superior, longer-lasting analgesia and reduced opioid consumption for intra-abdominal surgeries [35]. The QIPB, by achieving extensive retroperitoneal spread, is designed to provide the robust visceral analgesia the TAP block lacks. Therefore, the QIPB is a more comprehensive technique, comparable to the QLB and superior to the TAP block for procedures requiring visceral pain control (Table 3. Limitations

This systematic review has several important limitations that should be acknowledged when interpreting its findings. The primary limitation is the nature of the included evidence. The majority of the studies were case reports or small case series, which are susceptible to publication bias and represent a low level of evidence, limiting the generalizability of the findings. In addition to publication bias, the non-randomized nature of these reports introduces a high risk of selection bias. While this review includes two RCTs, their quality was variable. One trial was judged to have a low risk of bias, whereas the other had a high risk of bias due to issues with deviations from the intended intervention and selection of the reported result. Furthermore, the methodological quality of the observational studies was inconsistent. A significant weakness across nearly all case reports was the failure to systematically report on adverse events, which prevents a credible assessment of the block’s overall safety profile. Second, there was substantial clinical heterogeneity across the included studies. The reports cover a wide spectrum of surgical procedures, from minimally invasive operations to major orthopedic and spinal surgeries, as well as varying patient populations and anesthetic techniques. This extends to the block technique itself, with a lack of standardized protocols for anesthetic volume and concentration. This high degree of variability prevents a quantitative synthesis of the data (such as a meta-analysis) and makes it difficult to draw broad conclusions. The effectiveness of the QIPB is likely highly dependent on the specific clinical context. Finally, the included literature focused exclusively on acute, short-term outcomes, with a lack of long-term follow-up data across all studies. Furthermore, other unaddressed factors limit the current evidence, including the potential impact of operator experience, the geographic clustering of the published studies, and the absence of any economic analysis.

Future directions

To establish the clinical utility of the QIPB, future research must address the significant gaps identified in this review. There is an urgent need for well-designed RCTs to compare the efficacy and safety of the QIPB against both placebo controls and other established regional anesthesia techniques. Such studies should employ standardized protocols and use validated outcome measures that assess analgesia, functional recovery, and adverse events.

Encouragingly, several registered RCTs are now underway that have begun to address these fundamental questions. The need for placebo-controlled data is being addressed by trials investigating the QIPB against standard analgesia (no block) in the context of femoral fracture (NCT07009782) and inguinal hernia (NCT06862609) surgery. Furthermore, crucial head-to-head comparisons are being performed against other modern blocks, such as the pericapsular nerve group (PENG) block for femoral fractures (NCT06604806) and the thoracolumbar interfacial plane (TLIP) block for lumbar microsurgery (NCT06851533). These trials are also incorporating validated and patient-centered outcome measures, including 24-hour opioid consumption and comprehensive recovery assessments like the QoR-15 score (Table 4. Despite this progress, further investigation will still be necessary. Long-term follow-up is needed to determine the duration of the block’s effect and its potential role in preventing chronic pain. Additionally, systematic research into technical variables, such as the optimal local anesthetic volume or the use of adjuvants, remains an important area for future study to refine the technique.

Conclusion

The QIPB is an emerging regional anesthesia technique with an emerging evidence base consisting of two small RCTs and a larger body of observational studies. This review found that the QIPB has been applied across a wide range of surgical contexts. The strongest available evidence suggests the block is non-inferior to the ESP block for lumbar discectomy and may be superior to standard analgesia for hip arthroplasty, though this finding is from an RCT with a high risk of bias.

However, the current literature is defined by significant limitations, including the variable methodological quality of the RCTs and the inconsistent reporting and high potential for publication bias within the observational studies. Therefore, while the QIPB shows potential, its definitive efficacy, safety, and precise role in the clinical armamentarium remain to be established. Encouragingly, several high-quality RCTs are currently underway to address these gaps, and their results will be essential for determining the ultimate value of the QIPB in modern anesthetic practice.
