Astragaloside IV attenuates podocyte apoptosis via regulating TXNIP/NLRP3/GSDMD signaling pathway in diabetic nephropathy
Authors: Zhibo Hu, Yu Zhou, Cailing Gao, Junfen Liu, Congqing Pan, Jun Guo
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesAmong all the diabetes complications brought on by persistent inflammation is diabetic kidney disease (DKD). One essential method of the inflammatory response's programmed cell death is anthrax. One of the main causes of diabetic renal disease progression in a high-glycemic environment is the lysis of renal resident cells.MethodThis investigation sought to determine whether Astragaloside IV (AS-IV)'s anti-pyroptosis action provides a protective function for the kidneys. For 12 weeks, db/db mice received 40 mg/kg of AS-IV by transgastric gavage. To validate the possible in vitro mechanism, mouse podocytes were cultivated for additional experiments.ResultsIn vitro, AS-IV led to a significant reduction in blood urea nitrogen (BUN), urine albumen-to-creatinine ratio (UACR), serum creatinine (CREA), and hyperglycemia in db/db mice and lessen the pathological alterations in the kidney. Moreover, pyrin structural domain of the NLR family pyrin domain containing 3 (NLRP3), cleaved-caspase-1, gasdermin D (GSDMD), IL-18, and IL-1β were down-expressed and podocyte markers podocin and nphs1 were up-regulated following AS-IV intervention. By silencing GSDMD, we demonstrated in vitro that HG-stimulated podocytes undergo pyroptosis. We also discovered that AS-IV can mitigate this pyroptosis. To confirm that AS-IV prevented the NLRP3 inflammasome from activating, the NLRP3 inhibitor CY-09 was employed. It was also discovered that AS-IV prevents the expression of TXNIP and NLRP3 as well as their interaction. GSDMD expression was significantly downregulated following TXNIP-siRNA treatment, whereas GSDMD expression was upregulated in TXNIP overexpression cells; this upregulation could be undone with AS-IV.ConclusionsThe anti-pyroptosis effect of AS-IV via the TXNIP-NLRP3-GSDMD axis improves the renal function and podocyte damage of db/db mice and delays the onset of DKD, according to in vivo and in vitro experimental data.

Introduction

DKD is one of the diabetes longer-term comorbidities that has a strong correlation with patient mortality [1]. About 40% of diabetic patients have diabetic kidney disease (DKD), which is the primary cause of chronic kidney disease (CKD) globally. The main clinical manifestations are glomerular hyperfiltration, progressive proteinuria, and decreased glomerular filtration rate (GFR). These conditions eventually lead to end stage renal disease (ESRD). The advancement of the disease is further aided by oxidative stress, cytokines, inflammatory factors, and disorders of glucose metabolism [2, 3].

Long thought to be a microvascular consequence of diabetes, DKD is now known to be significantly influenced by podocyte injury and dysfunction [4]. Podocyte damage and loss are important factors in their continued development, based on both oxidative stress and inflammatory factor mechanisms [5]. A diabetic environment causes podocytes to adapt pathologically. Involving dedifferentiation, autophagy, apoptosis, and podocellular skeletal rearrangement. Showed flattening and contraction (referred elimination), a widened shape, decreased mobility, a rise in the creation of tight junctions between cells, a reduction in the diaphragm's length, glomerular hypertrophy, detachment, and swelling [6–8]. Because of this podocytes—particularly their abnormalities of growth and differentiation—become the central players in the pathogenesis of DKD [9].

Pyroptosis, a recently discovered process of programmed cell death linked to inflammatory reactions, is triggered by different stimuli and occurs in a variety of organs, including the kidney and liver [10]. In addition to causing excessive inflammatory damage, pyroptosis results in cell death. Activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome cleaves Pro-caspase-1 into cleaved caspase-1, which in turn cleaves the proteins in the gaspimin (GSDM) family, ultimately causing pyroptosis [11]. A related study indicates that kidney disease, particularly contrast-induced acute kidney injury, may be significantly influenced by NLRP3-associated scorch death [12]. Still, not many research has addressed the critical role that pyroptosis plays in DKD.

One major factor thought to play a role in the development of DKD is oxidative stress [13]. Thioredoxin interaction protein (TXNIP) is negatively regulated by thioredoxin (TRX), which preserves the intracellular redox balance [14]. It was demonstrated that TXNIP is essential during oxidative stress and that it also increases the inflammatory response through NLRP3 inflammasome-dependent mechanisms [15]. TXNIP might serve as a link between the hyperglycemic environment and the inflammatory process of DKD [16].

Over 2,000 years have passed since astragalus was first used as a traditional herbal medicine to treat illnesses. With the chemical structure 3-O-β-D-xylopyranosyl-6-O-β-D-glucopyranosyl cycloastragenol, astragaloside IV is a naturally occurring saponin. Its anti-inflammatory, anti-cancer, anti-oxidant, and immunomodulatory properties have all been demonstrated [17–19]. Astragalus has also been reported to have therapeutic effects among other complications of diabetes, such as peripheral neuropathy, diabetic retinopathy, and cardiovascular disease [20–22]. By lowering the expression of TXNIP, astragaloside IV has been shown in a prior study to improve endothelial cells' oxidative stress and inflammatory response in relation to endoplasmic reticulum stress [23]. Given that Astragaloside IV lowers TXNIP levels to function as an antioxidant and anti-inflammatory, we propose that Astragaloside IV can also be used to lower TXNIP in order to enhance oxidative stress and the inflammatory response in DKD.

The present investigation aimed to demonstrate the beneficial effects of Astragaloside IV on diabetic kidney disease (DKD) using a genetic mouse model of type 2 diabetes. Additionally, we investigated the potential involvement of the TXNIP/NLRP3/GSDMD pathway in the pyroptosis mechanism.

Materials and methods

Animals and treatment

A total of twelve six-week-old male db/db(C57BLKS/J-leprdb/leprdb) mice were randomly assigned to receive AS-IV treatment (db/db + AS-IV) and a group of twelve db/db mice. The control group consisted of twelve six-week-old db/m mice. Sodium carboxymethylcellulose (40 mg/kg body weight/day) was administered intragastrically to mice in the db/db + AS-IV group once a day for a duration of 12 weeks, while mice in the db/db group were given the same amount by gavage. The mice were weighed every 2 weeks and were put to death after 12 weeks. Before being sacrificed, the mice were moved into metabolic cages 2 days beforehand.

Each mouse's 24-h urine samples were taken in a different metabolic cage on the final 2 days of the experiment. To fix the tissue for use in histological, immunohistochemical, and immunofluorescence assays, the renal cortex was submerged in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h at 4°. For use in upcoming research, the remaining tissues were kept in storage at −80 °C. The National Institutes of Health's Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals was followed, and the Animal Ethics Committee of Tianjin Medical University approved this study.

Six-week-old db/db mice were used for this study. These mice were subjected to treatments for a duration of 12 weeks, from the age of 6 weeks to 18 weeks. At 18 weeks of age, the mice received either the experimental treatment (AS-IV) or vehicle (control) for an additional 2 weeks until they reached 20 weeks of age.

From 18 to 20 weeks, the mice continued to receive the assigned treatments, during which time renal function was closely monitored. At 20 weeks, the mice exhibited clear signs of renal insufficiency. The interventions during this period were consistent with those administered from 6 to 18 weeks, with AS-IV or vehicle continuing based on the group assignment.

Cell culture

The ATCC provided the conditionally immortalized mouse podocyte cell line that was purchased. To culture mouse podocytes, RPMI-1640 (Gibco) medium was supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco), 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 U/mL streptomycin. To stimulate proliferation, podocytes were grown at 33 °C in a medium containing 100 U/ml IFN-γ. For 7 days, podocytes were cultivated in medium devoid of IFN-γ in order to induce differentiation. The next study was conducted after a 24-h treatment of the cultured podocyte with normal rat serum. Following that, the cells were split into four groups: (1) normal contrast group (cells treated with 5 points5 mM glucose); (2) high glucose group (cells treated with 33 points3 mM glucose); (3) high glucose + 40 µM AS-IV group (cells treated with 33 points3 mM glucose and 40 µM AS-IV); and (4) MA group (cells treated with 5 points5 mM glucose and 27.5 mM mannitol). The following protocol will be followed for the cellular transfer of TXNIP and GSDMD siRNA. The NLRP3 Inflammasome was inhibited by adding CY-09 (Santa Cruz, CA, USA) to the medium containing podocytes. Podocytes were taken for study after a 72-h course of treatment.

Biochemical examination

Mice were given 24-h urine collections to measure urinary microglobulin (UMA). Each animal was given a 12-h fast, weighed, and then given an intraperitoneal injection of chloral hydrate to induce anesthesia. The blood samples underwent biochemical analysis. An automatic biochemical analyzer assessed the following parameters: blood glucose level, serum creatinine (Scr), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), total cholesterol (TC), total triglyceride (TG), and blood urea nitrogen (TC). The kidneys that were harvested were promptly weighed, preserved at −80 °C, or fixed in 10% neutral formalin.

Histology

Sections of kidney tissue embedded in 4 micron paraffin were subjected to histologic examination. The sections were stained using Hematoxylin and Eosin (HE), Periodic Acid-Schiff (PAS), Periodic Acid-Silver Metheramine (PASM), and Masson's trichrome (Leagene Biotechnology, China) staining kit (Leagene Biotechnology, China) in accordance with the manufacturer's instructions to assess glomerular alteration.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

For ten minutes, 3 percent (v/v) hydrogen peroxide was applied to the deparaffinized and rehydrated kidney tissues. The antigen repair solution-treated sections were subsequently cleaned, penetrated, and sealed. The following primary antibodies were then incubated on the sections for 12 h at 4 °C: NLRP3, TXNIP, IL-1β, IL-18, and caspase-1. After one hour of incubation at 37 °C with either mouse or rabbit secondary antibody, ten cortical sections (× 400) were chosen at random, and the stained sections were examined under a light microscope (Olympus BX-50, Olympus Optical, Tokyo, Japan) without being able to see the staining.

Detection of oxidative stress

The amount of oxidative stress was assessed using kidney cortex tissue. Commercial biochemical kits (Solarbio, China) were used to measure the glutathione (GSH) activities, malondialdehyde (MDA) levels, and superoxide dismutase (SOD) levels in the kidney cortex. To measure the amount of reactive oxygen species (ROS), mouse podocytes treated with HG and AS-IV were submerged in 3 μM dihydroethingot (DHE) dye (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) for half an hour. Using a fluorescence microscope (Olympus BX-50, Olympus Optical, Tokyo, Japan), the data were examined, and representative fields were selected for use.

Western blotting

In the RIPA lysis buffer that also contains loading buffer, phosphatase inhibitor, and PMSF, extract the proteins from the mouse podocyte and the renal cortex. Following the manufacturer's instructions, use the BCA Protein Detection Kit to measure the protein concentration. Protein samples should be loaded onto SDS-PAGE gels and then transferred to PVDF (polyvinylidene fluoride; Millipore, MA, USA) membranes. The membrane was incubated with anti-podocin antibody overnight at 4 °C after being blocked for an hour at 25 °C using 5% nonfat dry milk in TBS containing 0.1 percent Tween-20. The second anti-rabbit or mouse antibody was then applied to the membrane, and it was left at 37 °C for an hour. The protein was then detected using an ECL system. Using GAPDH as a reference, protein expression was normalized.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

IL-1β and IL-18 (Ray Biotech, USA) expression and activity levels in cell culture supernatants and mouse serum were assayed using the appropriate ELISA kits following the manufacturer's directions.

Transfection of siRNA and pReceiver-M90-TXNIP

Using the Lipomycin 3000 reagent (Genechem, China) and the TXNIP or GSDMD targeted siRNA (Shanghai GenePharma, China) according to the manufacturer's protocol, MPC-5 was transfected. The podocyte was harvested for research after a 48-h transfection. Next, podocytes were infected with the receiverm90-TXNIP (geneecopoeia, Guangzhou, China) vector in accordance with the manufacturer's instructions in order to investigate the role of AS-IV on TXNIP in hg-induced podocyte injury. The forward sequence for the pReceiver-M90-TXNIP was 5'-CAGCCTCCGGACTCTAGC-3', and the reverse sequence was 5'-CTCTACAAATGTGGTATGGC-3'. Following transfection, podocytes were gathered for use and new culture medium was added.

Immunofluorescence (IF)

TXNIP and NLRP3 within podocytes in kidney tissues were double-stained using immunofluorescence, as was the case with the MPC-5 mouse podocyte cell line. One percent Triton X-100 was infused into paraformaldehyde-fixed cells. The cells were subsequently sealed using 5% BSA and incubated for 12 h at 4° with primary antibodies against TXNIP (1:100) and NLRP3 (1:100). The cells were rinsed with PBS and incubated for 1 h at 37° with the AlexaFluor-488-conjugated secondary antibody. A fluorescence microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) was used to detect the fluorescence signal after the nucleus had been labeled with DAPI.

Immunoprecipitation (IP)/WB analysis

RIPA lysis buffer containing PMSF is used to extract cell proteins, and their concentration is adjusted to 200 μg/ml. For IP, two hundred μg of protein were extracted from each sample using two distinct antibodies. Initially, protein samples were incubated with various antibodies in the IP buffer (25 mM Tris–HCl buffer, which contains 0.5 percent Triton-X 100 [pH 7.5] and 25 mM NaCl) for an overnight period at 4 °C with gentle rotation. After that, add the appropriate amount of protein A/G beads to the sample, resuspend it in the SDS sample buffer, boil it for ten minutes, and then add the whole thing to a 15 percent SDS-PAGE before Western blotting. The experiment was run multiple times, averaging the density of each strip.

Computational binding simulation

TXNIP's structure was obtained using the Protein Data Bank (PDB ID: 4LL4). As a control, we used the TXNIP inhibitor TXNIP-IN-1 to investigate the connection between ligand and protein. AutoDock 4.2 and AutoDockTools 1.5 were used to carry out and analyze the molecular docking simulation. 6. A grid diagram showing the different protein residues and the active sites of TXNIP was produced by executing the AutoGrid file. With each ligand, we were able to determine which docking structure had the lowest docking free energy, and we used that information to determine which docking pose was optimal. Using Pymol and LigPlot + , we were able to render the AutoDock output and compute the hydrophobic interaction and hydrogen bond sum distance.

Statistical analysis

GraphPad Prism were conducted to analyze all data. Data are shown as the mean ± standard deviation (x ± SD). One-way ANOVA analysis was used to evaluate the significance of the results. P < 0.05 was defined as statistically significance.

According to Western blot results, podocytes treated with HG had significantly higher levels of GSDMD, IL-1β, and IL-18, while their levels of the podocyte marker NPHS1 had decreased. While the podocyte marker NPHS1 levels rise following HG treatment, the increase in IL-1β and IL-18 levels can be substantially attenuated by knockdown of GSDMD expression (Fig. 5A, B). In order to corroborate that AS-IV modulates the NLRP3 inflammasome to cause pyroptosis, the expression levels of NLRP3, caspase-1, IL-1β, and IL-18 in HG-induced MPC-5 cells were evaluated (Fig. 5D, E). The findings demonstrated that under high sugar conditions, the aforementioned protein expression increased in comparison to normal sugar concentrations. Furthermore, there was an increase in the expression of IL 1β and IL 18 in the MPC-5 cell supernatant, suggesting that the NLRP3 inflammasome had been activated. Next, it was investigated if AS-IV prevents pyroptosis caused by NLRP3 in order to have a protective effect. After administering AS-IV (40 μM) to the model group and using CY-09 (5 μM), an NLRP3 inhibitor, as a positive control, we identified factors associated with inflammation in both groups. The findings indicated that following treatment with either AS-IV or CY-09, there was a decrease in caspase-1, IL-1β, and IL-18. Next, we examined the NLRP3 inflammasome's activity. Western blot analysis revealed that following AS-IV and CY-09 treatment, the NLRP3 inflammasome's constituents, including NLRP3 and caspase-1, were inhibited. Moreover, following AS-IV administration, there was a decrease in the inflammatory products linked to pyroptosis, IL-1β and IL-18. In the AS-IV treatment group and the CY-09 treatment group, GSDMD, the primary effector of pyroptosis, was significantly reduced (Fig. 5F, G). These findings demonstrate that AS-IV primarily lowers pyroptosis by preventing the NLRP3 inflammasome from activating.

AS-IV attenuates HG-induced oxidative stress and reduces the co-localization and interaction of TXNIP and NLRP3

The development of diabetic kidney disease (DKD) is significantly influenced by oxidative stress. First, oxidative stress was found in MPC-5 cells. Following AS-IV treatment, the amount of MDA in podocytes was reduced. Conversely, oxidative stress-protective elements like GSH and SOD were elevated (Fig. 6A–C). Next, we administered 40 μM of AS-IV to MPC-5 cells that had been stimulated by HG. HG-stimulated MPC-5 cells produced more ROS, according to DHE probe analysis (Fig. 6D, E). When comparing the cells stimulated by high glucose to the control group, TXNIP, a regulator of cellular oxidative stress, was found to be significantly up-regulated. When comparing the high glucose-stimulated cells to the control group, there was a significant down-regulation of TRX, an endogenous antioxidant (Fig. 6F, G). Furthermore, we evaluated the changes in TXNIP and NLRP3 co-localization with IF staining. TXNIP and NLRP3 co-localization was enhanced in MPC-5 cells subjected to an HG environment. After receiving AS-IV therapy, these alterations were reversed (Fig. 6H, I). Discussion

Highly differentiated podocytes are typically referred to as the weakest link in the kidney cell population due to the intricate anatomical and physiological structure of the kidney, which is one of the many kidney resident cells involved in the pathological features of diabetic kidney disease [24]. Changes in podocyte "pathological adaptation" brought on by a diabetic environment lead to glomerular dysfunction and structure, which in turn lead to proteinuria and a reduction in renal function [25]. A common animal model used to study diabetic kidney disease is the db/db mouse. According to studies, db/db mice eliminate albumin at a rate that is 8–62 times higher than that of control mice at 8 weeks of age [26]. At 12 weeks, the albumin-to-creatinine ratio (ACR), total urine protein, and 24-h urine volume were all higher in the db / db mice than in the control mice. The significant reduction in 24-h urine volume observed in the AS-IV treatment group may suggest an improvement in renal function, likely through enhanced renal water and sodium retention. AS-IV has been shown to have anti-inflammatory and anti-fibrotic effects, which could help alleviate the pathological changes associated with diabetic nephropathy, such as glomerular hyperfiltration and tubulointerstitial fibrosis. This may result in improved renal concentrating ability, leading to reduced urine output. Additionally, AS-IV might have a protective effect on renal tubular function, preventing excessive diuresis commonly seen in diabetic mice. The DKD in db / db mice is confirmed by these results.

In comparison to control mice, 18-week db/db mice showed significantly higher levels of 24 h urine albumin, ACR, glomerular hypertrophy, GBM thickening, ECM deposition, and glomerulosclerosis. After 12 weeks of AS-IV treatment, however, renal hypertrophy and glomerular hyperfiltration decreased while 24-h urinary albumin levels and ACR decreased. Various staining techniques were employed to detect pathological changes in glomeruli. The observation of a lower kidney coefficient in the AS-IV-treated db/db group compared to the untreated db/db group may initially seem counterintuitive. However, we believe this difference may be due to the improved kidney function and reduced kidney damage in the AS-IV-treated group. Kidney atrophy is often associated with chronic kidney disease and fibrosis, but it is possible that AS-IV treatment reduced edema or interstitial inflammation, leading to a smaller overall kidney mass despite improved renal function. The kidney coefficient (kidney weight/body weight ratio) may be influenced by both structural changes and functional improvements, and further histological studies, such as assessing kidney fibrosis or glomerular damage, could help clarify the underlying mechanisms. The findings demonstrated that renal fibrosis, ECM deposition, and glomerular mesangial cell proliferation were all decreased in db/db mice with AS-IV.

The discovery that pyroptosis is present in diabetic kidney injury and that AS-IV has a potent anti-pyroptotic effect is a noteworthy finding of this investigation. A type of planned cell death associated with the production of inflammatory products is called pyroptosis. Activated caspase-1, which hydrolyzes the N- and C-terminal membrane proteins GSDMD, is a prerequisite for the typical pyroptotic pathway. The mature IL-1β and mature IL-18 released by the pore-forming, plasma membrane-permeable N-terminal domain of GSDMD simultaneously cause cell swelling and eventually rupture the cell membrane [27]. Recent research has discovered pyroptosis in diabetes complications [28], particularly in diabetic cardiomyopathy [29]. Nonetheless, it has been noted that there is little proof of pyroptosis in diabetic kidney disease [30]. In this investigation, kidney damage was noted in db/db mice, which was correlated with elevated expression of proteins related to pyroptosis (DSDMD, NLRP3, caspase-1, IL-1β, and IL-18). Podocytes contained these proteins. However, following AS-IV treatment, the db/db mice's kidney damage was lessened, and the pyroptotic related protein was down-regulated. These findings imply that AS-IV has an anti-pyroptosis effect and that podocyte pyroptosis occurs in diabetic kidney disease.

The process of scorched earth is significantly influenced by the accumulation of inflammatory vesicles [31]. Caspases are cleaved by mature NLRP3 inflammatory vesicles, which in turn causes the cleavage of GSDMD family proteins [32]. We observed that kidney tissue and highly glucose-stimulated MPC-5 cells exhibited increased release of cleaved caspase-1, IL-1β, and IL-18, as well as the activation of NLRP3 inflammasomes. Nevertheless, AS-IV prevented the NLRP3 inflammasome from activating and the release of cleaved caspase-1, IL-1β, and IL-18. More importantly, we discovered that high glucose stimulation causes the N-terminus of GSDMD to increase, indicating the presence of pyroptosis. We showed that the primary mechanism of cell death in a high-glucose environment is pyroptosis by knocking down GSDMD. Pyroptosis is a common method of inducing cell death, but it also exacerbates diabetic kidney damage due to the release of inflammatory factors like IL-1β and IL-18.

TXNIP is a crucial component of cellular redox homeostasis that is implicated in insurance resistance and diabetes [33]. In physiologic conditions, TRX attaches to TXNIP and prevents its function. The development of diabetic nephropathy is largely attributed to excess ROS production. Excess ROS facilitate TRX and TXNIP detachment in hyperglycemic environments. Interestingly, multiple investigations have demonstrated that TXNIP, also referred to as the TXNIP-NLRP3 axis, not only preserves redox homeostasis but also interacts with NLRP3 to start the inflammatory process [34]. According to our recent research, the AS-IV protective function in diabetic kidneys is associated with the TXNIP-NLRP3 axis. Of greater significance, we discovered that TXNIP-NLRP3 axis activity is the cause of GSDMD-induced pyroptosis and may also be the source of excessive inflammatory damage to the diabetic kidney. This study also demonstrated that AS-IV inhibited the production of ROS and TXNIP expressed in MPC-5 cells that were highly stimulated with glucose. AS-IV treatment resulted in a decrease in TXNIP-NLRP3 co-localization, indicating that AS-IV has an impact on TXNIP-NLRP3 interaction. These findings suggest that ROS-TXNIP could be an AS-IV target.

In conclusion, by acting on the TXNIP-NLRP3-GSDMD axis, AS-IV can lessen the focal death of glomerular podocytes. For the treatment of diabetic nephropathy, this could be a crucial AS-IV mechanism. Additionally, the current work needs to address a few constraints. Since our results are based solely on experiments, numerous promising clinical trials are needed to confirm the effects of AS-IV in humans. We are certain that AS-IV offers a wide range of therapeutic applications in the management of diabetic nephropathy.
