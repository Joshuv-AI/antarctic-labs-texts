Mechanoadaptive nanowire viscoplastic composites with strain-enhanced conductivity for reprocessable bioelectronic interfaces
Authors: Ran Huo, Shengyun Huang, Dixiang Gong, Xin Zhou, Heng Zhu, Pengyun Zhang, Hideaki Okuyama, Guangyu Bao, Xuan Li, Aram Bahmani, Lisa Martignetti, Zixin Zhang, Hongyu Hou, Changhong Cao, Yixun Cheng, Wei Gao, Dongling Ma, Nicole Y. K. Li-Jessen, Jianyu Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Bioelectronic interfaces must mechanically conform and adapt to biological tissues while maintaining stable electrical performance during motion and deformation. Existing soft conductors, however, often couple mechanical strain to electrical resistance, accumulate interfacial stress, and hinder reprocessing. Here, we report nanowire viscoplastic composites that combine silver nanowires with a dynamically crosslinked, short-chain polyborosiloxane matrix to produce emergent electromechanical behavior. The viscoplastic matrix permits deformation-induced nanowire reorganization, yielding high conductivity enhanced with strains. It also imparts fast stress relaxation, controllable yielding, and plastic conformability, enabling stress-free, mechanoadaptive contact with biological tissues. The material is self-healing, reconfigurable, and solvent-processable for recycling. It forms stable adhesion across porcine, rat, and human skin; accommodates hair regrowth; and exhibits excellent cytocompatibility and antibacterial function. As dry electrodes, these composites exhibit low impedance and resolve perilaryngeal muscle fatigue through high-resolution surface electromyography in humans. This work expands the materials design space for bioelectronic interfaces.

INTRODUCTION

Bioelectronic interfaces are transforming human-machine interactions across wearable technologies, biosensors, neural prosthetics, and therapeutic treatments (1–3). Their performance depends on forming intimate, stable contact with biological systems (4, 5), which are soft, constantly moving, and continuously remodeling (6, 7). This requires not only mechanical compliance but also the ability to minimize interfacial stresses that otherwise constrain tissue motion, growth, or cause device detachment (8, 9). Soft conductors, as key components of bioelectronics, must therefore combine high electrical conductivity with mechanical conformability and adaptability (10, 11). In parallel, sustainable device design increasingly demands reprocessable and reusable materials (12, 13).

Existing strategies for making soft conductors fall into two categories. Structural engineering approaches use metallic films in serpentine or fractal layouts on deformable substrates, but they require complex and often expensive microfabrication (14, 15). Intrinsically soft conductors embed conductive fillers or ions into elastomeric or gel matrices but often suffer from limited conductivity, strain-dependent electrical resistance, solvent evaporation, or permanent crosslinks that hinder recycling (16, 17). Recent progress highlights liquid metal (LM)–based conductors, such as gallium alloys patterned in microchannels or dispersed as droplets in elastomers (18, 19). While these systems offer low resistance insensitive to strains, they depend on encapsulation or microchannel fabrication and are prone to leakage and surface oxidation, complicating interfacial contact and long-term stability (20, 21). In all cases, nearly all existing designs rely on elastic or viscoelastic matrices that store mechanical energy when adhered to tissues, thereby building interfacial stresses and constraining tissue motion (22, 23). The accumulated interfacial stress could further destabilize tissue contact and cause mechanical failure (24). These challenges persist in the design and performance of bioelectronic interfaces on or inside the human body (25).

To address these limitations, we propose a design strategy of nanowire viscoplastic composites for creating mechanoadaptive, reprocessable bioelectronic interfaces. Our strategy integrates high-aspect-ratio metallic nanowires with a viscoplastic polymer matrix. Unlike elastic or viscoelastic matrices, the viscoplastic matrix deforms elastically at small strains but yields and flows at larger strains. Although viscoplastic materials such as polyborosiloxane (PBS) are well known (26, 27), their potential in bioelectronic interfaces has not been explored because they typically lack electronic functionality and afford limited control over their mechanical behavior. We hypothesize that nanowire viscoplastic composites could yield electromechanical behaviors fundamentally distinct from elastic or viscoelastic composites (28–31). Specifically, the dynamic polymer matrix could provide fast stress relaxation, predictable yielding, and the capacity for mechanical reshaping and solvent-assisted reprocessing, while plastic flow would allow nanowires to reorganize, align, and reconnect under strain, enabling strain-insensitive resistance.

In this study, we materialize this design with viscoplastic conductors (VPCs), formed by embedding silver nanowires (AgNWs) within a rationally engineered PBS matrix. The PBS is designed with short polymer chains and weak dynamic boronic crosslinks that break and reform under load, enabling rapid stress relaxation, plastic flow, and intimate, stress-free conformability to irregular tissue surfaces. Coupling this matrix with AgNWs leads to deformation-induced nanowire alignment and reconnection, yielding high electrical conductivity (∼4 × 105 S/m) and nearly strain-insensitive resistance under >100% strain. The dynamic network also imparts reconfigurability and both mechanical and solvent-assisted reprocessability while maintaining performance. These combined properties allow VPCs to form stable mechanoadaptive interfaces that accommodate biological processes such as hair growth, remain adhered for more than 7 days in vivo, and function as high-resolution, low-impedance dry electrodes capable of detecting subtle perilaryngeal surface electromyography (sEMG) fatigue signatures in humans. Together, these emergent mechanical, electrical, and reprocessing behaviors establish nanowire viscoplastic composites as a materials platform for wearable technologies, implantable devices, and human-machine interactions.

RESULTS

Viscoplastic matrix synthesis

VPC is composed of two key components: a viscoplastic matrix and conductive fillers. In this study, we use a model system comprising a PBS putty and AgNWs (Fig. 1A). AgNWs are chosen for their high conductivity (∼107 S m−1), tunable geometry, biocompatibility, and intrinsic antibacterial functionality, making them particularly attractive for biomedical applications (32). Our synthesized AgNWs feature a high aspect ratio and, importantly, retain their structural and chemical integrity after long-term storage, as confirmed by x-ray diffraction (XRD) and x-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analyses even after 3 months, mitigating concerns about nanowire degradation (fig. S1). The PBS putty was rationally designed to impart viscoplasticity. Whereas conventional PBS contains strong interchain borosiloxane crosslinks, our strategy uses short-chain polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) functionalized with boroxane and boronic ester groups, thereby suppressing permanent network connectivity while introducing highly dynamic covalent bonds (33) (Fig. 1B).

To synthesize the PBS putty, we reacted hydroxyl-terminated PDMS with excess boronic acid at 200°C. The excess boronic acid shifted the equilibrium away from interchain borosiloxane crosslinks, which would otherwise limit viscoplasticity and reprocessability (34). Elevated temperature can partially reduce the molecular weight of PDMS through thermally activated siloxane redistribution or limited chain scission, thereby suppressing chain entanglements and facilitating viscoplastic behavior (35). Meanwhile, it simultaneously drives the condensation of boronic acid into boroxane motifs tethered to PDMS chains. To probe the temperature effect, gel permeation chromatography (GPC) measurements were performed on PDMS/boric acid mixtures after heat treatment at different temperatures (fig. S2). The averaged molecular weight decreased from room temperature to 200°C (table S1), supporting moderate thermally induced molecular weight reduction. The resulting polymers after 200°C treatment can dissolve readily in polar solvents such as isopropanol (IPA), enabling uniform nanowire dispersion and chemical reprocessing. By contrast, an unheated PDMS–boric acid system remains insoluble in IPA due to its permanent crosslinked network.

To further enhance viscoplasticity, we intentionally introduced more dynamic bonds by adding ethylene glycol, which reacts with boroxane groups to form highly labile boronic esters. These transesterification-like exchanges can continuously reshuffle under stress, thereby accelerating stress relaxation and enhancing viscoplastic flow (36, 37). The addition of ethylene glycol softens the matrix while enhancing its deformability under rheological tests; however, when added in excess, it disrupts the network, causing the material to lose its solid-like character and transition into a liquid (fig. S3). To this end, XPS analysis confirmed the coexistence of borosiloxane, boroxane, and boronic ester linkages within the PBS (fig. S4), and this dynamic equilibrium underlines the distinctive viscoplastic behavior of VPC compared with classical PBS or silicone elastomers. Notably, given its low vapor pressure, ethylene glycol remains in the PBS matrix after processing, preserving dynamic chemistry and ensuring stability under ambient conditions (38). As a result, VPCs are easily reconfigured and reprocessed and can be readily reshaped into diverse geometries (Fig. 1C).

Nanowire dispersion and conductivity

Uniform dispersion and effective percolation of conductive nanowires within the matrix are critical for electrical conductivity (39). To ensure homogeneous mixing at a molecular level, we leveraged the IPA-soluble property of PBS and the high dispersibility of AgNWs in IPA (fig. S5). Upon solvent removal, the VPC recovered as a putty, where AgNWs were uniformly embedded within the PBS matrix, forming continuous, percolating conductive pathways, as shown by scanning electron microscopy (Fig. 1D). Thermogravimetric analysis further showed consistent thermal degradation profile across different AgNWs contents (fig. S6).

The high aspect ratio of AgNWs enables efficient charge transport at relatively low filler content (40). We systematically varied the AgNW concentration within the PBS to assess its impact on electrical conductivity. We found that conductivity rose sharply with AgNWs content, exceeding 104 S m−1 at concentrations below 5 vol % and plateauing above ∼10 vol % (Fig. 1E). Analyzing the data with statistical percolation theory, σ∝(ϕ−ϕc)t, yielded a percolation threshold ϕc ≈ 3.74 vol % and a critical exponent t ≈ 0.64 (41). While the threshold lies within the typical range reported for similar composites, the low exponent highlights a sharp onset of conductivity. This behavior can be attributed to the uniform dispersion of AgNWs in the PBS matrix, as a result of our synthesis strategy. Combined with the high aspect ratio of the nanowires, this strategy enabled the formation of highly efficient percolating networks.

To benchmark performance of VPCs, we compared the conductivity–filler loading relationship with several previously reported soft conductive composites, including graphene putty (G Putty), Ag-LM PVA hydrogels, Ag-Au nanowire styrene-butadiene-styrene (SBS) elastomers, Ag flake tough hydrogels, and Ag flake self-healing polymers. Across these comparisons, VPCs achieved high conductivity at relatively low conductive filler volume fractions while simultaneously exhibiting the viscoplastic electromechanical behavior described below (Fig. 1F). To further investigate the role of conductive filler morphology, we additionally compared AgNWs with silver fillers of lower aspect ratio, including Ag nanoparticles and microparticles (fig. S7). Among the silver fillers tested, long AgNWs led to very low electrical resistance and efficient percolation at substantially lower filler loading compared with particulate fillers (table S2). Note that the observed differences in electrical behavior cannot be attributed solely to morphology; synthesis-dependent variations in surface chemistry, dispersibility, and aggregation may also influence filler distribution and the formation of a continuous conductive network within the VPC.

Electromechanical response

It is important for soft conductors to maintain stable performance under deformation (42). Most conductive elastomers and gels exhibit strain-dependent resistance, typically increasing with tensile strain due to geometric effects or network disruption (43, 44). The synergy between the viscoplastic PBS matrix and AgNWs endows VPC with an electromechanical behavior not attainable in elastic or viscoelastic composites. We first demonstrate this capability with a simple proof-of-concept device: VPCs were stencil-printed into serpentine conductive patterns on a stretchable vVHB (3M) substrate. Here, the serpentine geometry was used to demonstrate the compatibility of VPCs with stencil printing and their ability to be fabricated into diverse deformable device layouts. Under repeated folding and stretching, the circuit continued to operate, as indicated by constant LED brightness (Fig. 2A). The unchanged light intensity suggests stable conductivity and current flow independent of applied deformation.

We next characterize the strain dependence of electrical performance using VPC samples with a simple rectangular geometry to ensure a uniform strain field during testing. VPCs exhibit electrical resistance nearly unchanged up to ∼100% elongation (Fig. 2B). This strain-insensitive resistance contrasts sharply with AgNWs-loaded PDMS elastomers, whose resistance increases substantially, even exceeding the theoretical geometric scaling prediction by R/R0 = λ2. It is likely due to the contact loss between nanowires under large deformation. In VPCs, the viscoplastic matrix minimizes internal stress buildup and preserves conductive pathways. Consistently, cyclic compression load-unload tests revealed pronounced mechanical hysteresis and large plastic deformation, while the electrical resistance remained very low throughout the loading cycles (fig. S8). This strain-insensitive resistance was further confirmed with VPCs with varying AgNWs concentrations (Fig. 2C). When geometric thinning during stretching is accounted for, the result suggests that the intrinsic conductivity of the composite actually increases with strain.

To investigate the structural basis for this strain-enhanced conductivity, we imaged VPCs under scanning electron microscopy (SEM). In the undeformed state, AgNWs are randomly oriented within the matrix. At 100% strain, the nanowires reorient inside the PBS matrix and align along the loading direction (Fig. 2D). The quantitative analysis of the two-dimensional (2D) orientation distribution demonstrated the projected angles of the nanowires in the VPC after stretching deviate from the random distribution, confirming strain-induced reorganization (Fig. 2E). This alignment enhances percolation pathways and explains the strain-insensitive resistance. While SEM does not directly resolve junctional reconnection, the observed increase in conductivity implies that re-establishment or strengthening of conductive contacts likely accompanies alignment during plastic flow. This behavior is inaccessible to viscoelastic matrices, which store elastic energy and pull nanowires apart, whereas the flowing viscoplastic matrix allows the network to reorganize and maintain electrical continuity under large deformation. While these results support a viscoplasticity-enabled mechanism for conductive network stabilization, directly resolving the 3D evolution of conductive pathways during large deformation remains challenging and warrants future in situ characterization and multiscale electromechanical modeling.

The viscoplastic property of VPC enables exceptional tolerance to both mechanical deformation and repeated reshaping. To evaluate this point, we screen-printed the VPC on flexible substrates and subjected them to cyclic mechanical folding. Specifically, we measured the electrical resistance of the printed patterns during 1000 cycles of repeated folding and unfolding. As a benchmark, we compared the performance of our VPC with a commercial silver paste printed on the same substrate. As shown in Fig. 2F, the VPC maintained stable electrical resistance throughout the entire cyclic test, whereas the commercial silver paste showed early signs of degradation—with a sharp increase in resistance occurring within the first 100 cycles, consistent with prior reports of poor mechanical stability under strain (45).

To further examine reusability, we examined the electrical and electromechanical performance of the VPC after repeated reshaping. We first repeatedly measured the electrical resistance of the VPC and reformed it by molding it into the same rectangular shape. The electrical resistance remained constant across all 100 cycles of reforming (Fig. 2G). Further, we examined the electromechanical response of the VPC under the same reshaping process by measuring its electrical resistance while subjecting it to uniaxially tensile loading of up to 60%. As a result, both the absolute resistance and the strain-independent electromechanical behavior of the VPC remained consistent across all reshaping events (Fig. 2H). This strain-invariant and reshaping-tolerant behavior is attributed to the synergy between matrix viscoplasticity and nanowire percolation.

Rate-dependent viscoplastic response under tension

Building upon the demonstrated electrical performance of the VPC, we next characterized its mechanical behavior and developed an analytical model that not only validates our physical understanding but also provides a framework for rational design. In essence, the mechanics of the VPC is governed by the interplay between the high–aspect ratio AgNWs and the viscoplastic PBS matrix. This combination not only imparts exceptional electrical properties but also enables tunable mechanical responses, including stiffness, yielding, stretchability, fracture, and adhesion. We found that the incorporation of AgNWs simultaneously increases stiffness, stretchability, and toughness of VPC while maintaining the overall viscoplastic character.

We first benchmarked the tensile behavior of the VPC as a function of AgNWs content and loading rate (fig. S9). Uniaxial tensile tests were performed at a constant strain rate of 2.5% s−1, varying the AgNWs content from 0 to 8.4% by volume. Engineered stress was calculated as the force divided by the cross-sectional area of undeformed sample, and the stretch ratio was defined as the ratio of deformed to undeformed length. The stress-stretch curves manifest characteristic viscoplastic responses (Fig. 3A): Stress initially increases linearly up to a yield point, followed by strain softening. We found that the critical stretch, where the stress peaks, remained nearly constant, ∼1.3, across all AgNWs concentrations, suggesting that yielding is governed by a matrix-controlled strain threshold dictated by the dynamic covalent bonds of the matrix.

The elastic modulus, determined from the initial linear region of the stress-stretch curve, increases from ∼25 kPa (0% AgNWs) to ∼150 kPa at 8.4% AgNWs (Fig. 3B). This trend suggests that the AgNWs stiffen the polymer matrix. Motivated by the nearly linear dependence of elastic modulus on AgNW content, we applied the rule of mixtures and analyzed the VPC’s small-strain response as a composite (see next section). With the critical stretch for yielding remained nearly constant, the yield stress scaled directly with the modulus, highlighting that the yielding strength of VPC can be predictably tuned through AgNW content.

Beyond yielding, the VPC exhibits pronounced strain softening, where stress gradually decreases to zero without material rupture (Fig. 3A). This softening behavior also depends on the AgNWs content, implying the role of the nanowire network on post-yield mechanics. Despite the pronounced stiffening and strengthening effects of AgNWs, the stretchability of VPC improves notably—from a stretch ratio of ∼2 (100% strain) for pristine PBS to ∼4 (300% strain) at 8.4% AgNWs. Whereas many elastomers exhibit a trade-off between stiffness and stretchability, the VPC defies this trend. We speculate that this counterintuitive behavior arises from the AgNWs’ ability to redistribute local strain and stabilize the matrix under large deformation, thereby delaying failure and enhancing overall stretchability.

The tensile behavior is also highly sensitive to loading rate (Fig. 3C). At a slow loading rate of 0.2% s−1, the conductor flows spontaneously as a liquid, without notable resistant force. The phenomenon implies that the VPC lacks permanent covalent crosslinks. When the loading rate increases, VPC becomes stiffer and stronger. When the loading rate is low, despite the presence of 8.4% AgNWs, the material exerted no resistance against deformation, suggesting that AgNWs are physically embedded without bonding between them. At a higher loading rate of 20% s−1, the elastic modulus increases significantly, reaching up to 300 kPa (Fig. 3D). This pronounced rate dependence suggests that the material exhibits viscoplastic behavior, attributed to the dynamic bonds within the PBS matrix, such as transesterification exchange in boronic esters and ethylene glycol. If deformed slowly, then they can readily adapt to the applied deformation (allowing for high conformability), and when deformed fast, the bonds could resist and exhibit linear elastic response up to critical stretches before softening.

Theoretical modeling of tensile behavior

To enable rational design of the mechanical behavior of VPC, we developed an analytical model for its tensile responses that accounts for both the stiffening effect of AgNWs and the rate-dependent behavior of the PBS matrix. Experimentally, the tensile responses of VPC exhibit three characteristic stages: an initial linear region, yielding at a critical stretch, and softening postyielding. Notably, under constant strain rate loading, the stress gradually decays to zero—a feature that cannot be captured by conventional viscoelastic or hyperelastic models. Motivated by this response and the underlying physical picture, our model combines a shear-lag framework to describe stress transfer between the soft PBS matrix and the rigid AgNWs with a power-law viscoplastic response for the matrix (fig. S10). Interfacial friction between the matrix and AgNWs was also incorporated. The effective elastic modulus of the composite Eeff follows a rule-of-mixtures–like expression dependent on the strain rate ε˙, the matrix modulus Eo, the filler modulus Ef, the volume fraction of the inclusion c, and an effective transfer factor ϕeff (see Materials and Methods)Eeff(ε˙,c)=(1−c)Eo·ε˙n+cEfϕeff(1)

This model very well predicts the modulus of VPC with different AgNWs concentrations and loading rates (Fig. 3, B and D) (29). To capture post-yield softening, we extended the Neo-Hookean model by introducing a smooth, strain-controlled damage evolution, where damage initiates once the stretch λ exceeds a critical threshold. Together, this framework reproduces the full mechanical behavior of VPC, from the initial linear regime through yielding and softening across a wide range of loading rates (Fig. 3C) and provides a foundation for rational tuning of properties and future model development.

Compressive and shear responses

Bioelectronic interfaces are subjected to complex mechanical loadings in real-life applications (5). To study the behavior of the VPC under these conditions, we investigated the mechanical responses of the VPC under compression and shear loading. Unconfined compression tests revealed consistent rate-dependent responses: the compressive modulus increased with both AgNW content and loading rate, mirroring trends observed in tensile tests (fig. S11). Differences emerged when comparing VPC with conventional elastic elastomers. When compared with PDMS (a crosslinked elastomer), VPC exhibited rapid stress relaxation with a characteristic relaxation time of ∼0.33 s, decaying to nearly zero residual stress (Fig. 3E). By contrast, PDMS relaxed slowly (≈100 s) and retained more than 80% of its peak stress, characteristic of crosslinked elastomers. The rapid relaxation of VPC enables fast conformal adaptation and adhesion formation on dynamically deforming skin surfaces with minimal pressing time (fig. S12).

To probe plasticity, we performed creep-recovery tests. VPC showed pronounced time-dependent creep, accumulating strain gradually under constant stress (Fig. 3F). In contrast, PDMS displayed a step-like elastic response with negligible creep. Upon unloading, PDMS instantly recovered to its original shape, whereas VPC preserved nearly 100% of the plastic strain, confirming its viscoplastic character (Fig. 3G). To examine potential recovery over long term, we stretched VPC and found no recovery of plastic strains even after 7 days (fig. S13). This behavior enables VPC to conform seamlessly to irregular tissue surfaces and be reshaped postsynthesis on demand.

The shear response is critical for device-skin interfaces, where shear loading dominates. Rheological measurements showed stable storage and loss moduli at small strains and shear thinning behavior at large strains and strain rates (fig. S14). Consistent with other tests, increasing AgNW content enhanced stiffness and viscosity but did not alter the overall shear-thinning response. The combination of shear thinning and stress relaxation suggests that the dynamic boronic bonds, instead of entanglements, govern the viscoplastic behavior of VPC. To probe bond dynamics directly, we conducted temperature-controlled rheological test. Plotting tan(δ) (G″/G′) as a function of temperature revealed a crossover near 50°C, indicating a transition from elastic- to viscous-dominated behavior due to initiation of bond exchange within the boronic ester network (Fig. 3H). At ∼120°C, a pronounced tan(δ) peak appeared, likely corresponding to B─O bond dissociation. Arrhenius analysis of this transition yielded activation energy of ∼98 kJ/mol, respectively, in agreement with literature values (46). These results suggest that dynamic boronic bonding is the molecular origin of the viscoplasticity of VPC.

Renewable adhesion, self-healing, and reprocessability

As the dynamic boronic bonds are highly reversible, we hypothesize that VPC has renewable adhesion and reprocessability. Unlike conventional elastomeric conductors with permanently crosslinked networks, VPC flows plastically under stress and can reform its bonds and matrix (47). This dynamic behavior allows it to refresh its adhesive surface after detachment and to be reprocessed postsynthesis both mechanically and chemically, which are properties rarely integrated into soft conductors.

We first characterized the adhesion of VPC to porcine skin using modified lap-shear tests. Although VPC lacks chemical moieties for covalent bonding with biological tissues, its viscoplastic flow enables intimate conformity to the irregular skin surface and maximizes weak physical interactions, including hydrogen bonding and van der Waals forces (48). For comparison, we tested a permanently crosslinked PDMS (Ecoflex) that shares a similar base chemistry as the VPC. While crosslinked PDMS failed through brittle interfacial fracture, VPC achieved significantly higher adhesion energy and strength (Fig. 3I). Adhesion energy measurements confirmed that VPC substantially outperformed PDMS (Fig. 3J), attributable to its ability to plastically conform and sustain abundant reversible interfacial interactions.

The combination of physical interactions with skin and matrix viscoplasticity also makes the adhesion of VPC renewable. After detachment, VPC can be remolded to absorb contaminants into the bulk without washing while simultaneously refreshing functional groups at the surface (Fig. 3K). In contrast, conventional adhesives such as pressure-sensitive adhesives lose functional groups after the first use due to reaction with tissue surfaces and are easily contaminated by dust or oils, rendering them nonrenewable (49). In our tests, we applied and detached VPC in a lap-shear test and then remolded the same specimen to renew its surface before reapplying it for the next test cycle. Repeated lap-shear tests confirmed stable adhesion performance over at least 20 consecutive cycles using the same specimen (Fig. 3l). This renewable adhesion arises from the liquid-like reconfigurability of the matrix, which refreshes interfacial contact through deformation.

The dynamic nature of VPC also enables self-healing and solvent-assisted recyclability. To assess self-healing, we prepared samples with both low (0.5 vol %) and high (14.5 vol %) AgNWs loading, cut them cleanly in half, and monitored healing of the fractured interface. Visually, the cut edges became increasingly indistinct after ∼5 hours and were nearly indistinguishable after 24 hours, particularly in low-filler samples where higher matrix mobility accelerated bond reformation (fig. S15). Electrical resistance measurements and LED circuit tests confirmed that conductivity was rapidly restored, especially under mild heating (fig. S15). For recyclability, whereas conventional PBS is difficult to recycle due to its limited solubility in common solvents, used VPC can be readily dissolved in IPA, redispersed by vortex mixing and sonication, and reformed by solvent evaporation (fig. S16). After five recycling cycles, ∼70% of the material was recovered with comparable conductivity and mechanical performance. The observed mass loss was largely attributable to suspension-transfer losses during the multistep handling process, rather than degradation of the network. This loss could likely be further reduced by implementing collection steps such as filtration to capture residual material remaining in the solvent phase.

Biocompatibility and antibacterial function

For applications interfacing with the human body, it is important to evaluate the biocompatibility of the VPC. All components of the VPC are biocompatible and commonly used in clinical settings, while the incorporation of AgNWs imparts intrinsic antibacterial function (32). To test this, we conducted a series of in vitro and in vivo experiments, including cell culture, bacterial culture, and rodent model tests.

To assess cytocompatibility, we first cultured human vocal fold fibroblast cells in VPC-conditioned medium. After 24 hours, the cells maintained ∼95% viability, indicating minimal cytotoxicity (Fig. 4A). Concentration-dependent testing revealed no significant differences in viability for 20 and 40 mg/ml compared to control, and although viability decreased slightly at 100 mg/ml, it remained high overall (fig. S17).

We next evaluated the in vivo skin interfacing performance of VPCs using a freely moving rat dorsal skin model, in comparison with the PBS matrix (without AgNWs), a commercial gel electrode (Kendall), and untreated skin control (Fig. 4B). This experiment was designed to assess the ability of VPCs to maintain stable and conformal skin interfacing during prolonged wear under animal motion and ongoing tissue renewal, such as hair regrowth. All materials were applied to shaved dorsal skin of Sprague-Dawley rats and mechanically secured using a silicone ring and sutured Tegaderm dressing to maintain stable attachment during free movement (fig. S18). VPC conformed intimately to the skin of freely moving animals and remained adherent for 7 days. Notably, hair regrowth occurred directly through the VPC without compromising adhesion, whereas conventional hydrogel-based interfaces typically obstruct or constrain hair penetration (50). These observations demonstrate that VPCs can accommodate dynamic tissue remodeling and hair penetration while maintaining stable interfacial contact during prolonged wear. Hair penetration may additionally contribute to mechanical interlocking, further stabilizing the interface. To evaluate tissue responses, skin tissues beneath the materials were excised after 7 days and subjected to histological analysis (Fig. 4C). Comparable tissue morphology was observed across all groups. Quantitatively, epidermal thickness was slightly higher in the commercial electrode group, although differences were not statistically significant (Fig. 4D). Inflammation scores were minimal, and no evident immune response was observed (Fig. 4E), confirming the excellent biocompatibility of VPC. These results suggest the mechanoadaptive and biocompatible nature of VPC, which accommodates dynamic biological processes such as hair penetration while maintaining a seamless tissue interface.

To examine antibacterial function, we cultured Gram-negative Escherichia coli and the Gram-positive bacteria Enterococcus faecalis on VPC surfaces. The two representative strains were selected because they belong to distinct bacterial classes that differ in cell wall architecture, allowing us to assess whether the intrinsic antibacterial activity of AgNWs is broad spectrum; in addition, both species are clinically relevant to skin-contacting devices, as they are common opportunistic pathogens associated with skin and soft tissue infections. We found that, although some bacteria adhered to VPC, most were dead, consistent with the bactericidal effect of AgNWs and their gradual release of silver ions (Fig. 4F). Quantification confirmed significantly fewer live bacteria on VPC compared with PBS matrix controls (Fig. 4G), validating the contribution of AgNWs to antibacterial activity. The combination of demonstrated biocompatibility, mechanical adaptability on living tissue, and inherent antibacterial activity underscores the potential of VPC as a high-performance, reconfigurable skin electrode.

Skin electrode performance

The combination of electrical, mechanical, and biological properties makes VPC appealing for skin electrode applications. Its viscoplastic nature allows it to be easily reshaped to any desired geometry and pressed into full conformal contact with skin, and its high conductivity enables accurate electrical measurements with a high signal-to-noise ratio. The moderate and reversible adhesion ensures secure attachment during use while still enabling easy, nondamaging removal.

We first assessed VPC electrodes in a general forearm test by characterizing their impedance-frequency response in comparison with commercial Ag/AgCl metal and 3M Ag/AgCl gel electrodes (Fig. 5A). We further analyzed the impedance spectra with an equivalent circuit (fitted parameters listed in table S3). The VPC electrodes exhibited low overall impedance, with an Rs of 162 Ω and relatively small R1 (38,778 Ω) and R2 (80,082 Ω), reflecting efficient charge transfer and reduced interfacial resistance. Compared to commercial Ag/AgCl metal dry electrodes, which showed much larger R1 (55,832 Ω) and R2 (123,032 Ω) values, the VPC achieved markedly better interfacial performance, attributable to its stress-free, conformal skin contact. While 3M Ag/AgCl gel electrodes displayed the lowest impedance across frequencies, likely due to the presence of an ionic gel medium, the performance of VPC as a dry electrode closely approached that of the gel electrodes. To further evaluate interfacial stability under dynamic deformation, we compared the electrochemical impedance spectra (EIS) of VPCs and 3M Ag/AgCl gel electrodes on both relatively flat forearm skin and highly curved elbow skin before and after joint movement (fig. S19). VPCs exhibited highly consistent impedance responses across different skin geometries and motion states, while the commercial gel electrodes showed substantially larger impedance variations depending on skin curvature and movement history. Together, these results establish VPCs as a new class of dry electrodes that combine low interfacial impedance with stable conformal skin coupling under dynamic deformation.

Human sEMG for vocal fatigue assessment

Motivated by the performance of VPC-based mechanoadaptive skin electrodes, we next conducted human studies using VPC electrodes to characterize perilaryngeal muscle activity during phonation and to assess vocal muscle fatigue following experimental loading. The human larynx presents a particularly challenging site for sEMG mounting due to its curvilinear geometry and mechanically dynamic surface. Quantifying changes in activity of the small and deeply positioned laryngeal muscles as well as accessory respiratory muscles is both technically demanding and clinically important for understanding fatigue-related voice and upper-airway dysfunctions (51). Conventional gel-based wet electrodes are prone to drying over extended use, limiting their suitability for continuous monitoring. Their comparatively large size also makes it difficult to isolate signals from small laryngeal muscle groups, and their rigid interfaces conform poorly to the curved neck surface, compromising signal fidelity. These limitations underscore the need for soft, conformal, and stable electrodes such as VPC electrodes for high-quality perilaryngeal sEMG acquisition.

To capture localized sEMG activity from multiple muscle groups involved in phonation, we used a multielectrode configuration. Six small VPC electrodes were placed on the dominant side of the neck over the suprahyoid, infrahyoid, and sternocleidomastoid (SCM) muscles, with a reference electrode on the clavicle (Fig. 5B). Electrode pairs at each recording site were spaced 1.5 cm apart following established configurations. The mechanical adaptability of the VPC allowed us to fabricate small, customizable electrodes—offering a clear advantage over the bulkier, fixed-size commercial counterparts (Fig. 5C). This feature is particularly valuable for high-density surface recordings, where fine spatial resolution is required to minimize muscle cross-talks. Analyzing the time-domain data, we observed that the signal quality from the VPC was comparable to that of the commercial electrode (Kendall), exhibiting low noise and stable recordings (Fig. 5D). Participants then produced sustained /a/ phonation under typical (modal) and atypical (pressed) conditions (Fig. 5E). Despite the anatomical complexity, VPC electrodes reliably captured condition-dependent differences. Root mean square (RMS) analysis of the recorded signals revealed clear increases in amplitude during pressed phonation compared to modal ones, demonstrating the feasibility of using VPC electrodes to detect muscle activation differences associated with vocal strain (Fig. 5F). Among the three muscle groups, the suprahyoid region exhibited slightly higher RMS values, indicating its potentially greater involvement or activation intensity during a typical pressed phonation.

To assess perilaryngeal muscle fatigue, participants completed an institutionally approved protocol with evaluations before, during, and after an intensive vocal loading task (VLT). Fatigue was induced by having participants read passages continuously and loudly (>75 dB for 15 min) (Fig. 5G). Participants’ perceived fatigue scores at both localized vocal [self-administered vocal rating (SAVRa)] and general physical levels (Borg CR-10) increased progressively across phases (Fig. 5H). Concurrently, the VPC electrodes consistently recorded high-quality sEMG signals, demonstrating stable performance under repeated use and prolonged phonation tasks. The quantitative analysis of the signals showed a consistent decline in median frequency (MDF) with increasing vocal load, reflecting fatigue-related spectral shifts (Fig. 5I). This trend was observed across all muscle groups, with the infrahyoid region consistently exhibiting lower MDF values compared to the suprahyoid and SCM. Despite occasional motion artifacts arising from weak integration between the VPC material and metal wires, the electrodes provided stable, high-quality signals over repeated phonation tasks. Collectively, these results demonstrate the feasibility of VPC-based electrodes for noninvasive sEMG monitoring in humans and highlight their potential for clinical and research applications in voice and upper airway health assessment and treatment monitoring.

DISCUSSION

In summary, we introduce VPCs as a new class of soft electronic materials that overcome key trade-offs between mechanics, electronics, and sustainability. By integrating a dynamic viscoplastic matrix with AgNWs, VPCs exhibit emergent electromechanical responses. The viscoplastic matrix imparts rate- and strain-dependent plasticity, enabling conformal, stress-free integration with biological tissues while allowing reshaping, reprocessing, and recycling without loss of performance. Here, we focus on skin-interfacing conductive electrodes rather than fully integrated electronic devices. In this context, optimized viscoplasticity enables adaptive conformal contact, interfacial stress relaxation, reshaping, and stable electrical performance under deformation, providing a design strategy complementary to conventional elastic or hyperelastic systems. Comprehensive characterization and theoretical modeling establish the underlying design principles that govern these behaviors, and demonstrations in biocompatibility, skin-mounted bioelectronics, and high-fidelity sEMG validate the material’s potential for bioelectronic interfaces. Compared with commercial electrodes, VPCs provide superior sensitivity to subtle biosignals, exemplified by real-time detection of perilaryngeal muscle fatigue in human subjects, underscoring their potential for next-generation clinical monitoring and human-machine interfaces. More broadly, this work illustrates how integrating dynamic material mechanics with electronic functionality can yield reconfigurable, sustainable bioelectronic systems. Future directions include incorporating bioadhesive chemistries for enhanced tissue integration, scaling toward high-density sensor arrays, and deploying VPC-based devices in clinical diagnostics and rehabilitation. These advances open new possibilities in wearable electronics, mobile health monitoring, and soft human-machine interaction.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Materials

All chemicals were used as purchased without further purification. Materials involved in the synthesis of AgNWs include silver nitrite (AgNO3, Thermo Fisher Scientific, AA1141414), iron (III) nitrite (Fe(NO3)3 • 9H2O; Alfa Aesar, 12226), sodium chloride (NaCl; Sigma-Aldrich, S9888), ethylene glycol (EG; Sigma-Aldrich, 324558), silicone oil (Thermo Fisher Scientific, AAA1272822), and IPA (Sigma-Aldrich, 190764). Materials used in the PBS synthesis include PDMS, hydroxy terminated (viscosity: 2550 to 3570 cSt and 18,000 to 22,000 cSt, Sigma-Aldrich, 432989 and 432997, respectively), boric acid (BA; Sigma-Aldrich, B0394), and lithium chloride (LiCl; purity ≥99%; Sigma-Aldrich, 310468).

Nanowire synthesis

AgNWs were synthesized according to a seed-based growth strategy (32). 30 mM Fe(NO3)3 • 9H2O and 40 mM NaCl were dissolved in EG for 24 hours to obtain a homogeneous solution. The EG solution of 315 ml was heated to 110°C in a three-neck flask (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 10-163-3A) submerged in silicone oil bath. When the temperature was reached, 7.5 ml of 30 mM Fe(NO3)3 • 9H2O solution and 1.5 ml of 40 mM NaCl solution were added. Meanwhile, 75 ml of 102.04 mM AgNO3 was dissolved in EG in a dark environment. Immediately after full dissolution, the AgNO3 solution was added to the reaction mixture drop-by-drop via a dropping funnel (StonyLab Scientific) while maintaining the temperature at 110°C. The reaction lasted for 15 hours and quenched sin ice bath until reaching room temperature. AgNWs in EG suspension was then collected. To wash the product, the AgNWs-in-EG suspension was centrifuged at 3600 rpm for 4 min, and the supernatants were discarded. The AgNWs was then washed with IPA thrice and resuspended. To calibrate the yield of AgNWs, six batches of AgNWs-in-EG suspension were thoroughly mixed, and 50 ml of the mixed suspension was washed with the above procedure. The AgNWs-in-IPA suspension was then transferred into a container with known weight for complete solvent evaporation and the weight of the AgNWs per 50 ml of original suspension was determined to be the weight gain of the container.

VPC synthesis

To synthesize the reprocessable PBS, 40 ml of PDMS were mixed with 12 g of BA at room temperature for 12 hours. The mixture was subsequently heated to 200°C in an oven for 1 hour and then cooled to room temperature. The resulting product, containing excess BA, was washed three times with IPA to obtain transparent PBS. Specifically, the PBS was fully dissolved in IPA at 3.75 g ml−1 for 24 hours, vacuum-filtered (pluriSelect; 1 μm), and transferred into a petri dish, and the solvent was evaporated in a fume hood overnight. The final PBS product was stored in a closed container at 53% relative humidity before use. To control humidity, 400 ml of 8 M LiCl solution was placed in the container, and the relative humidity was monitored using a hygrometer (Fisherbrand, 15-079-679).

To prepare AgNWs-laden PBS or VPC, the PBS after the above procedure was added into the AgNWs-in-IPA suspension at AgNWs-to-PBS weight ratio of 1:1 and thoroughly stirred for 24 hours. The resulting mixture was then cast into a petri dish under ambience conditions, and VPC was collected after complete solvent evaporation. To prepare VPCs with varying AgNWs contents (10, 15, and 30% w/w), the same procedure was used with adjusted AgNWs-to-PBS weight ratios of 1:9, 3:17, and 3:7, respectively.

Epidermal patch preparation

Adhesive electrode patches incorporating AgNWs VPC disk electrodes were fabricated for monitoring neck muscle activities associated with vocal fatigue, specifically targeting the suprahyoid, infrahyoid, and SCM muscles. VPC disks (10-mm diameter) with embedded wires were placed onto the back of a polyurethane adhesive film (Tegaderm, 3M), aligned with prepunched holes of 8-mm diameter and 1.5-cm center-to-center distance. A second layer of polyurethane adhesive film was then applied on top to encapsulate the VPC and electrical cables. The aligned holes allowed direct skin contact with the electrodes, enabling efficient biosignal acquisition. The horizontal and vertical center-to-center spacing between electrodes was 2 and 1.5 cm, respectively.

Microstructure characterization

The structure and composition of AgNWs were characterized using XPS and XRD. SEM (LYRA3, Tescan, operated at 10 kV) was used to characterize the nanowire morphology and orientation under strains. Surface element composition was analyzed with XPS (VG Esclab220i XL), operated with an Al Kα excitation source. The crystal structure of AgNWs was confirmed with XRD (D8 Advance, Bruker) using a Cu Kα radiation source (λ = 0.15406 nm) at 40 kV and 40 mA. PBS XPS characterization was performed using a Thermo Fisher Scientific Nexsa G2 XPS system equipped with a monochromatic Al Kα x-ray source (hν = 1486.6 eV). Survey and high-resolution spectra were collected at pass energies of 200 eV with a step size of 1.0 and 50 eV with a step size of 0.1 eV, respectively. For the binding energy of spectra, the charge is referenced to adventitious C1s, C-C peak at 284.8 eV. Data analysis was carried out using “Avantage” Surface Chemical Analysis software (version 6.7.0), with peak fitting performed using a Smart background and mixed Gaussian-Lorentzian to fit the peak shapes.

Tensile tests

To assess the tensile properties, VPCs were fabricated into strips with a stretching length of 40 mm, width of 8 mm, and thickness of 1 mm using screen printing with a polylactic acid (ERYONE) mask produced via 3D printing (Creality, Ender 3 V2). The specimens were stretched unidirectionally by a mechanical testing machine (Instron, Model 5965, 10-N load cell, 2530-10N) at displacement rates of 6, 60, and 600 mm min−1 to investigate strain-rate dependence. Force and displacement were recorded continuously. Nominal stress was calculated by dividing the measured force by the original cross-sectional area, while engineering strain was defined by dividing the change in length by the original length, and stretch ratio (λ) was calculated as the ratio of deformed length to initial length.

Mechanics of VPC

Based on the shear-lag model and the stretch-triggered evolution of material damage, we establish an analytical model to capture the mechanical behavior of VPC (see more details in Supplement Text). The model effectively captures the modulus enhancement governed by stress transfer through frictional sliding at the fiber-matrix interface, as well as the softening response induced by stretch-triggered material damage under large deformations (52–54).

Note that the parameter k0 quantifies the intrinsic interfacial shear transfer capability between the nanowires and the surrounding matrix; a larger k0 indicates stronger interfacial coupling and more efficient load transfer, whereas a smaller k0 corresponds to weaker interfacial interactions and increased interfacial slip.

Compression tests

Compression tests were performed on AgNW-VPC nanocomposites to determine the compressive stress-strain behavior and elastic modulus. Samples were pressed into a 3D-printed mold and carefully extracted. Cylindrical shaped samples with a diameter of 10 mm and height of 5 mm were compressed by mechanical testing machine (Instron, 2-kN load cell), while force and displacement were measured. To study the effect of loading rate, loading rates of 0.01, 0.02, 0.1, 0.5, and 1 mm s−1 were applied. The nominal stress was obtained by dividing the force by the original cross-sectional area. The nominal strain was obtained by dividing the change in height by the original height.

To characterize the time-dependent behavior, stress relaxation and creep tests were conducted under compression. For stress relaxation, a step compressive strain of 20% was applied, and the force-time response was recorded. Stress relaxation time constant (τ) was extracted by fitting the decay curve using nonlinear least squares method in MATLAB (MathWorks), solved with the Levenberg-Marquardt algorithm, based on the equation belowσ(t)=σre(−tτ)+σ∞(7)where σ∞ is the equilibrium stress and σr the relaxed stress.

The creep-recovery test was performed by applying a step strain of 50% and holding force (creep) for 5 min and ramping the force down to 0 and holding the force (recovery) for 5 min, while recording the strain-time profile. Maximum strain (εmax) is expected to be reached at the end of the creep period while the irreversible strain (εir) is recorded as the final strain at the end of the recovery period. The plasticity of the material is calculated by the ratio of irreversible and maximum strain εir/εmax.

Rheology tests

Rheological properties of VPC were characterized using a Discovery HR-2 hybrid rheometer (TA Instruments, USA) equipped with a 20-mm parallel plate geometry and a fixed gap of 1 mm. For self-healing mechanism study of VPC, temperature-dependent moduli were measured with temperature sweep (25° to 140°C) with 5°C min−1 ramp speed at 0.5% shear strain and 1-Hz shear frequency. The critical temperature at which the solid-to-liquid transition happened was determined to be the point where storage and loss moduli equal.

Adhesion tests

The modified lap shear test was implemented to determine the adhesion energy of the AgNW-PBS nanocomposites to different adherends. AgNW-PBS nanocomposites were shaped into strips of length L = 20 mm, W = 10 mm, and thickness T = 1 mm by screen printing with 3D-printed PLA masks. Rigid polymethyl methacrylate (PMMA) sheets (thickness of 1.5 mm) were glued to the back of the samples by applying a thin layer of adhesive primer (Loctite SF 7701, Henkel) and gluing (Krazy Glue) after primer evaporation. The adherends including VHB (4905, 3M) and porcine skin were prepared by cutting into strips of 20 mm by 10 mm and gluing to PMMA sheets, while clean glass substrates were used as purchased. To form adhesion, AgNW-PBS nanocomposites were brought into contact with various adherends with an edge initial crack C = 5 mm. Free ends were pulled unidirectionally with an Instron machine (model 5965, 10-N load cell, 2530-10N) at a constant rate of 0.5 mm s−1, while force F and displacement δ were recorded. Recyclability test

The recycling process of VPC was performed through a solvent-based reprocessing method. As shown in the schematic (fig. S16A), used VPC (400 mg) was first put in IPA solution (15 ml) and then subjected to 10-min vortex mixing and rotary mixing (20 rpm) overnight to form homogenous AgNWs/PBS-IPA suspension. Next, the solvent was evaporated in a fume hood for 48 hours to be reformed into VPC, marking the completion of one recycling cycle. The mass retention of recycled VPCs over five cycles was quantified (fig. S16B).

GPC measurement

PDMS/boric acid mixtures were heat-treated at different temperatures for 1 hour and then dissolved in chromatographic-grade tetrahydrofuran (THF) for gel permeation chromatography (GPC) analysis. When necessary, the samples were dissolved under heating and magnetic stirring to ensure sufficient dissolution. Insoluble residues, if present, were removed by filtration before injection. GPC measurements were performed on an Agilent 1260 Infinity II GPC/SEC system equipped with a differential refractive index detector. Two PL MIXED-C columns were connected in series, including a PL MIXED-C column (7.5 mm by 50 mm, 5 μm) and a PL MIXED-C column (7.5 mm by 300 mm, 5 μm). THF was used as the mobile phase at a flow rate of 1.0 ml min−1, and the column temperature was maintained at 40°C. The injection volume was 50 μL. Molecular weight calibration was carried out using polystyrene standards. The number-average molecular weight (Mn), weight-average molecular weight (Mw), peak molecular weight (Mp), z-average molecular weight (Mz), and dispersity (Mw/Mn) were calculated from the resulting chromatograms. These measurements were used to evaluate temperature-dependent changes in the apparent molecular weight distribution of PDMS after heat treatment with boric acid.

Impedance measurement

Skin-electrode impedance was measured using an electrochemical workstation (VersaSTAT 3, AMETEK, USA) over a frequency range of 1 Hz to 100 kHz at 10 mV in a three-electrode configuration. AgNW-VPC electrodes of 1-cm diameter were used as the working electrode, while two commercial adhesive Ag/AgCl gel electrodes (3M Red Dot 2360, area 2 cm by 2 cm) served as the reference and counter electrodes. All electrodes were placed on the left lower forearm of a volunteer, with an 8-cm center-to-center spacing between adjacent electrodes. The reference electrode was positioned between the working and counter electrodes. Commercial Ag/AgCl gel electrodes were also tested under identical conditions for comparison. An equivalent circuit model shown in Fig. 5A was fitted to characterize interfacial impedance behavior.

Human VLTs

Human studies were conducted in compliance with the guidelines of the Ethical Conduct for Research Involving Humans and were approved by McGill University’s Research Ethnics Board (protocol number: A11-B62-19A). Before participation, all participants received an information letter describing the study procedures, potential risks and benefits, and their rights as research participants. Informed consent was obtained from all participants before the study was initiated. sEMG signals from perilaryngeal muscles were obtained during sustained vowel phonation (SVP) to quantitatively evaluate vocal fatigue. Participants, fitted with either VPC or commercial electrodes (Kendall EMG/ECG Foam Solid Gel Electrodes), sat comfortably, and were instructed to sustain the vowel /a/ using two voice types, namely modal voice (representing healthy phonation) and pressed voice (representing stressed phonation). Each SVP session included five consecutive SVPs of 3-s duration, with 7-s rest intervals between them. To experimentally induce vocal fatigue, participants performed two VLT sessions (55). Specifically, they were instructed to read excerpts from The Big Book of Canadian Trivia continuously at a loud voice (>75 dB) for 15 min. In addition, the SAVRa (56) and Borg Rating of Perceived Exertion (Borg CR-10) (57) were deployed to track participants’ perception of vocal and general physical fatigue and exertion, respectively. Vocal evaluations were implemented at three time points: before the first VLT (prefatigue), between the two VLTs (mid-fatigue), and after the second VLT (postfatigue).

sEMG measurements

sEMG signals were recorded from the neck muscles using a biosensing board (Cyton, OpenBCI, USA) with a sampling rate of 250 Hz and amplifier gain of 24. Custom adhesive patches incorporating pairs of VPC electrodes were placed over the suprahyoid, infrahyoid, and SCM muscles on the dominant side of the neck, with a reference electrode on the clavicle. sEMG signals were acquired in a bipolar configuration and processed offline. Preprocessing included removal of the initial 2 s, linear detrending, application of a fourth-order Butterworth band-stop filter at 60 and 120 Hz (2-Hz bandwidth) to suppress powerline interference, and a fourth-order Butterworth bandpass filter (15 to 124 Hz) to isolate sEMG activity. For each muscle group, five repetitions of the middle 2 s of SVP were analyzed. RMS amplitude and MDF were calculated. To enable cross-trial comparison, sEMG amplitudes were normalized to the maximum voluntary contraction (MVC), defined as the peak rectified amplitude observed across the central 2 s of the five phonation trials (excluding noisy trials). Normalized values were expressed as a percentage of MVC for each muscle.

In vitro cell culture

Cytocompatibility was assessed using human epithelial cells following ISO 10993-5 guidelines. Extracts were prepared by incubating 0, 20, 40, and 100 mg of VPC in 1 ml of minimum essential medium at 37°C for 24 hours. The extract (0 mg/ml; vehicle control) served as the negative control, representing baseline cellular response to the extraction procedure. After incubation, extracts were supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum, 1% l-glutamine, and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. Human epithelial cells (passage 17) were seeded in eight-well microchambers and cultured with the extract media for 24 hours at 37°C in a humidified 5% CO2 atmosphere. A positive control was included by treating cells with 70% methanol for 30 min before staining. Cell viability was assessed using a Live/Dead assay, and samples were imaged using an inverted confocal laser scanning microscope (Zeiss LSM 800).

Bacterial culture

Antibacterial performance was evaluated against Gram-negative E. coli and Gram-positive E. faecalis. Strains were recovered from −80°C glycerol stocks and cultured on nutrient agar before experiments. Bacteria were grown in Luria-Bertani broth at 37°C and 200 rpm until exponential phase, centrifuged at 4000g for 10 min (Heraeus Multifuge X3R, Thermo Fisher Scientific), and resuspended in PBS. The optical density at 600 (OD600) was adjusted to 0.2 using a microplate reader (Synergy HTX, Agilent). Samples were incubated in 1-ml bacterial suspension in a 24-well microplate for 30 min to allow bacterial attachment, rinsed gently with PBS to remove loosely bound cells, and stained with the Live/Dead BacLight Kit (Molecular Probes) for 15 min. SYTO 9 stained bacteria with intact membranes (live; green, excitation of 483 nm/emission of 503 nm), while propidium iodide stained bacteria with compromised membranes (dead; red, excitation of 535 nm/emission of 617 nm). Samples were imaged using a confocal laser scanning microscope (Zeiss LSM 710), with ≥4 fields acquired per substrate. The percentage of live bacteria was calculated as the ratio of intact cells to total cells.

Animal experiments

Animal experiments were conducted in compliance with the guidelines of the Canadian Council on Animal Care and were approved by McGill University’s Facility Animal Care Committees (protocol number: MCGL-8275). Male Sprague–Dawley (SD) rats aged 8 to 16 weeks (250 to 450 g) were used in this study. The in vivo skin attachment experiment was designed to evaluate the conformability, adhesion stability, and skin compatibility of VPCs during prolonged attachment to the skin of freely moving animals.

For skin preparation, rats were anesthetized using 5% isoflurane in an induction chamber and maintained under 2% isoflurane during the procedure. The dorsal region was shaved using electric clippers and further depilated with depilatory cream to obtain a clean skin surface for sample attachment. Four test sites were marked on the upper dorsal skin of each animal. At each site, ∼50 mg of material was applied, including PBS matrix without AgNWs (0% AgNW), VPC containing 50 weight % AgNWs, a commercial ECG gel electrode (Kendall, MA, USA), and an untreated blank control (fig. S18).

To secure samples and prevent displacement during free movement, each material was confined within a silicone ring and covered with a protective Tegaderm film, which was sutured to the surrounding skin using 5-0 nylon sutures. These sutures intentionally penetrated the superficial skin tissue to mechanically secure the dressing throughout the study and may account for the localized skin marks visible near the interface region in Fig. 4B. Each rat was additionally fitted with a protective jacket (RJ03) and dermal insert (RJ IND) from LOMIR Biomedical Inc. (Québec, Canada) to maintain sample positioning and minimize interference while allowing daily activities.

The material attachment behavior and animal skin condition were monitored daily for 7 days by visual inspection and photography. Attention was paid to material retention, conformal contact with the skin surface, signs of erythema or irritation, and hair regrowth through the materials. The experiment continued for either 3 or 7 days before tissue harvest (n = 3 per time point). After euthanasia by CO2 inhalation, skin tissues from the application sites were harvested and fixed overnight in 4% paraformaldehyde at 4°C, cryoprotected in 20% sucrose in PBS, embedded in OCT compound, and frozen. Cryosections with a thickness of 10 μm were prepared using a cryostat (CM1850, Leica) and stored at −80°C until further histological analysis.

Histological processing and analysis

Skin tissues were processed for histological evaluation using hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining (Hematoxylin and Eosin Staining Kit, #ab245880, Abcam, Cambridge, UK). Cryosectioned samples were stained with H&E to observe inflammatory responses. Sections (10 μm) were examined under bright-field illumination with an inverted microscope (Axiovert, Carl Zeiss, Germany). Inflammatory cell infiltration (neutrophils, lymphocytes, monocytes, and macrophages) was assessed by a board-certified pathologist. Inflammatory severity was graded on a four-point semi-quantitative scale: 0 = none, 1 = mild, 2 = moderate, 3 = marked. Epidermal thickness, an indicator of local inflammatory response, was quantified from H&E-stained sections by determining the vertical distance from the basal layer to the stratum corneum at four interpapillary regions per sample using ImageJ software (NIH, USA). The average value for each sample was calculated, and group comparisons were performed with GraphPad Prism version 10 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA). Because of limited sample size per time point, data from days 3 and 7 were pooled for analysis.

Statistical analysis

All quantitative data are presented as means ± SD unless otherwise noted. Group comparisons were performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post hoc test for multiple comparisons or two-tailed unpaired Student’s t tests when comparing two groups. Significance thresholds were set at ns (not significant), *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, and ****P < 0.0001, as indicated in the figure legends. All statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism version 10 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA).
