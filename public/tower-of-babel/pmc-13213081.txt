Menarche in the third decade: congenital adrenal hyperplasia masquerading as Mayer–Rokitansky–Küster–Hauser syndrome
Authors: Lalitha A. V. Alekya, Sravanthi Mashetty, Arun Guddeti, C V S Manasa, Aishwarya Bora, K V S Hari’Kumar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPrimary amenorrhea (PA) in a young girl can result from structural, functional, or hormonal disorders. Accurate diagnosis is essential for counseling on hormone replacement and fertility. Mayer–Rokitansky–Küster–Hauser (MRKH) syndrome is characterized by an absent or hypoplastic uterus and presents with PA. Congenital adrenal hyperplasia (CAH) is a steroidogenic defect that may present at any age but rarely as PA. We report a 33-year-old woman who initially presented at age 17 with PA and was labeled as having MRKH syndrome due to a hypoplastic uterus. She was treated by multiple dermatologists for resistant acne between ages 20 and 32. During evaluation of hyperandrogenism, her computed tomography scan of the abdomen revealed a right adrenal mass, prompting an endocrine referral. Our evaluation showed virilization, clitoromegaly, and a hormonal profile suggestive of simple virilizing CAH. Gene sequencing showed a homozygous missense variation in exon 4 of the CYP21A2 gene (chr6:g.32007203T>A) (c.518T>A) (p.Ile173Asn), confirming CAH. She was managed with glucocorticoids, antiandrogens, and estrogen supplements. She had menarche 6 months after therapy at age 33. We report this case to highlight the importance of detailed endocrine evaluation in patients with PA.

Introduction

Primary amenorrhea (PA) is defined as the absence of menarche by age 15 years. Common etiologies of PA include genital outflow tract obstruction, gonadal dysgenesis, hypothalamic-pituitary abnormalities, chronic systemic illness, psychiatric disorders, and endocrine diseases, such as hyperprolactinemia, hypogonadism, and hypothyroidism, as well as constitutional delay [1]. The diverse hormonal and fertility implications of the differential diagnoses make accurate identification of the underlying disorder imperative. Misdiagnosis can lead to adverse outcomes and add to the psychological burden of a fertility-impacting condition. Mayer–Rokitansky–Küster–Hauser (MRKH) syndrome is characterized by aplasia of the Müllerian structures in an individual with a 46XX karyotype [2]. The incidence is about 1 in 5000, and patients have an absent uterus and the upper portion of the vagina. MRKH syndrome is divided into type 1 and type 2 based on the absence or presence of renal, skeletal, and multisystemic features, respectively [2]. Uterine transplantation and gestational surrogacy are the only options for fertility in patients with MRKH syndrome [3]. Congenital adrenal hyperplasia (CAH) is a steroidogenic defect resulting in disorders of sexual development and has a varied presentation from infancy to adulthood [4]. 21-hydroxylase deficiency is the most common type of CAH and may present with salt-losing crisis in neonates and with infertility or hirsutism in adults. Ambiguous genitalia may be seen in 46XX infants but not in those with a 46XY karyotype. Patients with CAH are treated with glucocorticoids and antiandrogens and may have subnormal fertility [5]. We present a 33-year-old female who was initially diagnosed with MRKH syndrome at age 17 years due to PA and a hypoplastic uterus. Resistant acne for over a decade, extending into the third decade, along with a chance detection of a right adrenal mass, led to an endocrine referral at age 33 years. Her clinical, biochemical, and hormonal profile was suggestive of simple virilizing CAH, and whole-exome sequencing revealed a homozygous missense variant in exon 4 of the CYP21A2 gene.

Case presentation

A 33-year-old woman was referred to us for evaluation of resistant acne and an adrenal mass. She was the second child in a nonconsanguineous marriage, with a birth weight of 2.5 kg. Her parents did not report a history of genital ambiguity or salt-losing crisis. She met normal developmental milestones and identified as female. She had been the shortest in her class since childhood and noticed pubarche at 13 years of age, along with hirsutism. Her hirsutism was treated with mechanical methods for 6-8 sessions, with no significant improvement. She consulted a gynecologist for suboptimal breast development and PA. There was no withdrawal bleed after a progesterone trial, and ultrasonography (USG) of the abdomen showed a hypoplastic uterus with normal ovaries. The gynecologist considered the external genitalia to be normal, and the patient did not consent to genital examination under anesthesia. In view of breast development, absent progesterone withdrawal, and a hypoplastic uterus on USG, she was labeled as MRKH syndrome and counseled accordingly. The parents did not seek a repeat consultation or a second opinion for the amenorrhea after the initial diagnosis. However, her hirsutism persisted into adulthood, and she subsequently developed nodular acne, which was resistant to local and systemic therapies, including oral doxycycline, isotretinoin, and topical tretinoin and clindamycin. She received various therapies for resistant acne between 20 and 32 years of age, and the dermatologist who attended to her ordered abdominal imaging to look for a mass lesion. She underwent a computed tomography (CT) scan of the abdomen for evaluation of hyperandrogenism, which showed a hypoplastic uterus and a right adrenal mass, leading to an endocrine referral for further evaluation.

Diagnostic assessment

On examination, she had short stature (140.5 cm; mid-parental height of 151 cm, −3.1 SDS), a body weight of 42 kg, a body mass index of 21 kg/m2, normal blood pressure and body proportions, and was at Tanner stage A4B3P4. She had no skeletal malformations or hyperpigmentation. Her modified Ferriman–Gallaway score was 5/36 (a laser removal session had been performed 2 weeks prior to presentation). External genitalia were female-appearing, with normal genital openings and clitoromegaly (3 cm length, 1 cm breadth), with a clitoral index of 300 m2, as shown in Fig. 1. She had a 46XX karyotype, and we performed a dexamethasone-androgen suppression test (DAST) to identify the probable source of excess androgens. She was given a 0.5 mg dexamethasone tablet every 6 hours starting at 8 Am on day 1 (a total of 8 tablets), and blood samples were collected for cortisol, total testosterone, and DHEAS at baseline (8AM, before the first tablet of dexamethasone) and on the third day at 8 Am. Her hormonal and imaging profiles suggested CAH, most likely the simple virilizing type. We performed next-generation sequencing and multiplex ligation-dependent probe amplification of the CYP21A2 gene. We detected a homozygous missense variant in exon 4 of the CYP21A2 gene (chr6:g.32007203T>A) that results in substitution of asparagine for isoleucine at codon 173 (c.518T>A; p.Ile173Asn; ENST00000418967.2), commonly known as I172N. This variant has residual enzyme activity of 2-11% and may result in simple virilizing CAH in the homozygous form, as seen in our patient [7].

Treatment

She was treated with oral prednisolone 5 mg and spironolactone 100 mg daily. After 3 months of therapy, her hirsutism improved, and ET increased to 7 mm. A 5-day progesterone-withdrawal trial did not induce bleeding. Subsequently, we increased prednisolone to 7.5 mg and initiated estradiol valerate 0.5 mg daily with spironolactone. Outcome and follow-up

She had spontaneous menarche after 6 months of therapy at age 33 and is currently taking prednisolone 5 mg daily and spironolactone 50 mg. Her hirsutism improved, requiring fewer laser sessions, and she continues to have regular menses. Her family members have been counseled regarding genetic screening and the change in diagnosis from MRKH to CAH. She remained unmarried at the last review, and we explained the subfertility associated with CAH. We consider that the right adrenal mass in our patient could be due to adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH)-driven adrenal nodular hyperplasia in long-standing, uncontrolled CAH, and we plan to repeat adrenal imaging after 1 year to assess changes in the size of the adrenal mass.

Discussion

We present a case of simple virilizing CAH, misdiagnosed as MRKH syndrome for more than a decade, with menarche at age 33 years. Androgen excess in CAH causes hirsutism, genital ambiguity, acne, and accelerated epiphyseal fusion, leading to short stature in adulthood [8]. It also interferes with endometrial maturation and breast development. Hyperandrogenemia is associated with a thin endometrium irrespective of the underlying etiology [9]. Patients with polycystic ovarian syndrome may have a thin endometrium due to hyperandrogenemia, contrary to the expectation of a thickened endometrium from unopposed estrogen [10]. In our patient, androgen excess could account for the hypoplastic uterus and partially developed breasts, thereby misleading the diagnosis of MRKH syndrome. Johannsen et al have shown reduced uterine volume in almost all patients with CAH compared with controls [11]. Another finding from the same study is the lack of change in uterine size, even after pregnancy. Uterine hypoplasia and inadequate endometrial development are proposed as anatomical features that impair fertility in patients with CAH [12]. Girls with CAH have normally developed vaginas, whereas patients with MRKH syndrome have a blind vaginal pouch and uterovaginal abnormalities. A thorough genital examination of our patient could have identified CAH earlier, given the significant clitoromegaly. Our patient was initially evaluated by a gynecologist who missed the clitoromegaly, leading to a delayed diagnosis of CAH.

CAH is a group of disorders with autosomal recessive inheritance, and the most common enzyme deficiency is 21-hydroxylase deficiency caused by mutations in CYP21A2 [13]. The disease is classified into 3 types (salt-wasting, simple virilizing, and non-classical) based on residual enzyme activity (below 1%, 1-5%, and 20-25%), respectively [4]. In the absence of 21-hydroxylase, precursor steroids are diverted from the deficient synthesis of cortisol and aldosterone into the unaffected androgen pathways, leading to excess androgen production. Menstrual abnormalities are common in CAH, but PA is rarely observed [14]. Classic CAH is diagnosed in infancy, and well-controlled CAH has a normal menarche. Simple virilizing CAH may present with early pubarche and delayed menarche. Patients with poorly controlled CAH have acne, hirsutism, irregular menses, and infertility. PA in our patient could be due to untreated CAH and excess androgen levels, with virtually no endometrial development.

Long-standing, uncontrolled CAH patients develop adrenal hyperplasia and mass lesions [15]. The lack of glucocorticoids from the adrenal cortex stimulates ACTH secretion, which may lead to adrenal cortical hyperplasia. This hyperplasia may progress to an adrenal adenoma and, rarely, to carcinoma [16]. It is important to evaluate all adrenal masses when making the diagnosis of CAH, as management differs markedly between a non-functional adrenal adenoma and one resulting from CAH. Adrenal mass lesions have been reported mostly in 21-hydroxylase deficiency and, rarely, in other types, such as 17-hydroxylase deficiency [17].

PA has various etiologies, with MRKH being one, especially in a normally estrogenized female with normal stature. A hypoplastic uterus is the most common finding in MRKH syndrome, though other etiologies associated with a hypoestrogenic state also contribute to this finding. Our report highlights CAH as a cause of a hypoplastic uterus, leading to misdiagnosis as MRKH syndrome. Most patients with CAH are fertile and require lifelong glucocorticoid replacement. Our report emphasizes that a thorough clinical examination is essential in patients resistant to standard management to identify the correct diagnosis.
