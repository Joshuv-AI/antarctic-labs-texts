Quantitative strong approximation for ternary quadratic forms I
Zhizhong Huang

Abstract
. We derive asymptotic formulas with a secondary term for the (smoothly weighted) count of number of integer solutions of height ⩽ B \leqslant B with local conditions to the equation F ⁡ ( x 1 , x 2 , x 3 ) = m F(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})=m , where F F is a non-degenerate indefinite ternary integral quadratic form, and m m is a non-zero integer satisfying − m ​ Δ F = □ -m\Delta_{F}=\square which can grow like O ⁡ ( B 2 − θ ) O(B^{2-\theta}) for some fixed θ > 0 \theta>0 . Our approach is based on the δ \delta -variant of the Hardy–Littlewood circle method developed by Heath-Brown.



1.
Introduction

Let
F(\boldsymbol{x})\in{\mathbb{Z}}[x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}]
be a ternary integral quadratic form and let
m
be a non-zero integer, such that
(F=m)
defines a smooth affine quadratic surface
W_{m}
in
{\mathbb{A}}^{3}
. We assume that
F
is indefinite.
The fact that the Brauer–Manin obstruction to strong approximation of rational points is the only one is well-understood, thanks to work
[
3
,
4
]
.
The problem of counting integral points on such varieties (more generally on symmetric varieties) has been addressed in many works. See notably
[
8
,
9
]
.

Let
\Delta_{F}
denote the discriminant of
F
. Depending on whether
-m\Delta_{F}
is a perfect square or not, the order of growth of integral points features very different behaviour.
The case where
-m\Delta_{F}\neq\square
is well-studied, and it is shown that the number of integral points of bounded height
B
say, grows like
cB
, where the constant
c
involves the Tamagawa measure of the corresponding adelic open set defined by a prescribed local condition (strictly speaking, intersecting the loci where orbits contain rational points). See
[
1
]
.

The current article focuses on the case
-m\Delta_{F}=\square
. While it is known that there is no Brauer–Manin obstruction, the infinite product of non-archimedean local densities (the so-called “singular series”) diverges to
\infty
. We next describe our result concerning counting integral points with arbitrary local conditions, followed by a historical account.

Principal results

Let
w:{\mathbb{R}}^{3}\to{\mathbb{R}}
be an infinitely differentiable weight function, and we define the
weighted singular integral
(with respect to the quadratic form
F
) to be

(1.1)

{\mathscr{I}}(w):=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{3}\times{\mathbb{R}}}w(\boldsymbol{t})\textup{e}(\theta F(\boldsymbol{t}))\operatorname{d}\boldsymbol{t}\operatorname{d}\theta.

If
w
is positive, our assumption that
F
is indefinite (i.e. isotropic over the real numbers) guarantees that
{\mathscr{I}}(w)>0
.

Let
{\mathscr{W}}_{m}
be the
{\mathbb{Z}}
-scheme defined by
(F=m)\subset{\mathbb{A}}^{3}_{{\mathbb{Z}}}
. We assume that the adelic space

{\mathscr{W}}_{m}(\widehat{{\mathbb{Z}}}):=\prod_{p<\infty}{\mathscr{W}}_{m}({\mathbb{Z}}_{p})

(with respect to the integral model
{\mathscr{W}}_{m}
) is non-empty. Let
L\in{\mathbb{N}}
an and let
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{m}\in{\mathscr{W}}_{m}({\mathbb{Z}}/L{\mathbb{Z}})
.
For every prime
p
, we define the
p
-adic local density
(with respect to the congruence condition
(L,\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{m})
) to be

\sigma_{p}({\mathscr{W}}_{m};L,\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{m}):=\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{\#\{\boldsymbol{v}\in{\mathscr{W}}_{m}({\mathbb{Z}}/p^{k}{\mathbb{Z}}):\boldsymbol{v}\equiv\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{m}\bmod p^{\operatorname{ord}_{p}(L)}\}}{p^{2k}},

and we define
the “modified” singular series
(with the convergence factors
\left(1-p^{-1}\right)_{p}
) to be

(1.2)

\widehat{\mathfrak{G}}({\mathscr{W}}_{m};L,\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{m}):=\prod_{p<\infty}\left(1-\frac{1}{p}\right)\sigma_{p}({\mathscr{W}}_{m};L,\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{m}).

We define the counting function

(1.3)

{\mathscr{N}}_{m}(w;L,\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{m};B):=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{x}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{3},F(\boldsymbol{x})=m\\
\boldsymbol{x}\equiv\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{m}\bmod L\end{subarray}}w\left(\frac{\boldsymbol{x}}{B}\right).

Throughout this article, the weight function
w
, the form
F
as well as the integer
L
are fixed.

Theorem 1.1
.

As
B\to\infty
, assume that
-m\Delta_{F}=\square
and

(1.4)

m=O(B^{2-\theta})\text{ for a certain fixed }\theta>0.

Then

{\mathscr{N}}_{m}(w;L,\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{m};B)={\mathscr{I}}(w)\widehat{\mathfrak{G}}({\mathscr{W}}_{m};L,\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{m})B\log B+O\left(B(\log B)^{\frac{1983}{1984}}\right).

Moreover,

\frac{1}{\log\log B}\ll\widehat{\mathfrak{G}}({\mathscr{W}}_{m};L,\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{m})\ll 1.

Theorem
1.1
is a quantitative form of the strong approximation property for
{\mathscr{W}}_{m}
, which gives a leading constant differing slightly from those obtained in
[
14
,
15
]
.
Firstly, the constant
m
is absent from the singular integral
{\mathscr{I}}(w)
. After
[
14
]
,
{\mathscr{I}}(w)
features the (weighted) real volume of the projective curve defined by the homogeneous equation
(F=0)
in
{\mathbb{P}}^{3}
. Secondly, the order of growth is
B\log B
, and the appearance of
\log B
coincides with the speed of divergency of the usual product of local densities, so that inserting the convergence factors
(1-\frac{1}{p})_{p}
ensures the absolute convergence of
\widehat{\mathfrak{G}}({\mathscr{W}}_{m};L,\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{m})
.
In the Appendix, we show that such phenomena
are in accordance with the framework of Chambert-Loir and Tschinkel
[
2
]
on the distribution of integral points. From this perspective, Theorem
1.1
shows that integral points are equidistributed in
{\mathscr{W}}_{m}(\widehat{{\mathbb{Z}}})
.

Our next theorem shows the existence of a secondary term of order
B
, which confirms a suggestion of Rudnick (as pointed out in
[
24
, p. 918]
). Let
\omega
be the function which counts the number of prime divisors.

Theorem 1.2
.

Under the assumption of Theorem
1.1
, assume moreover that

(1.5)

\omega(m)=O(1).

Then there exists
a=a(w;m;L,\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{m})\in{\mathbb{C}}
such that,

{\mathscr{N}}_{m}(w;L,\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{m};B)={\mathscr{I}}(w)\widehat{\mathfrak{G}}({\mathscr{W}}_{m};L,\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{m})B\log B+aB+O\left(\frac{B}{(\log B)^{\frac{1}{4}(1-\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2})}}\right).

Moreover, we have
a=O(1)
and
\widehat{\mathfrak{G}}({\mathscr{W}}_{m};L,\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{m})\asymp 1
.

If we view
m
as fixed, such asymptotic formulas can be proven using different methods (see below). On the other hand, it is evident that there can be no points if
m\gg B^{2}
. The significant feature of our result is that we can allow
m
to grow “as fast as possible” like (
1.4
) in the
B
-aspect. We refer to
[
22
, Fig. 1]
for numerics supporting the condition (
1.4
).
In both theorems our method actually provides an error term which saves a small power of
\log B
(compared to
B\log B
and
B
respectively).

Methods and further comments

Traditionally, the circle method has been applied with success when the quadratic form has more than four variables. It is Heath-Brown who develops his
\delta
-version of the circle method in
[
11
]
(the predecessor goes back to work of Duke–Friedlander–Iwaniec
[
6
]
), and first applies to varieties defined by
homogeneous
ternary forms towards quantitative Hasse principle. His arguments rely crucially on homogeneity, which guarantees, amongst other things, the multiplicativity of the quadratic exponential sums arising naturally after the execution of Poisson summation.

In this article and the subsequent ones
[
12
,
13
]
we undertake systematic study of quantitative strong approximation results for ternary quadratic forms. This appears to be the first time (to our knowledge) that circle method is employed to inhomogeneous equations of the form defining
W_{m}
. Our approach is based on
[
11
]
, and is inspired by our past investigation
[
14
,
15
]
. The main technical challenge lies in dealing with the arithmetic part involving exponential sums, as when averaging over non-zero Poisson variables
\boldsymbol{c}
, the associated quadratic exponential sums
\widehat{S}_{q}(\boldsymbol{c})
are not multiplicative. In this ternary case one is led to estimating average of certain Salié sums, for which the Weil bound is insufficient.
Depending on whether
-F^{*}(\boldsymbol{c})
is a perfect square or not, eventually one is faced with the distribution of congruent residues of certain quadratic polynomials, and for which we draw ingredients from the classical work of Hooley
[
17
]
and the recent one due to Dartyge–Martin
[
5
]
. While adapting these ingredients, more demanding control of quadratic exponential sums over “bad moduli” is also needed, and one has to go beyond the relatively easy square-root cancellation established by e.g.
[
11
, Lemma 25]
.
For Theorem
1.2
, it might be possible to adapt some ideas from e.g.
[
16
,
7
]
to achieve a power-saving error term, with some extra work.

Despite the treatment of the oscillatory integrals in the current article being relatively standard, in our subsequent investigation
[
12
]
towards Linnik-type problem this becomes an issue, for which we work out refined arguments there.

Our result adds to the (rare) literature that the circle method is capable of producing asymptotic formulas whose order of growth is not merely a power of
B
. Also the real part (i.e. real density) and the arithmetic part (i.e. product of local densities) both arise very naturally.
Moreover, the flexibility of allowing
m
to grow like (
1.4
) does not seem easily accessible via previously mentioned work, even assuming standard conjectures. See e.g.
[
22
, Remark 1.7]
. Our approach applies without difficulty to the case
-m\Delta_{F}\neq\square
, subject to appropriate growth condition on its square-free part.

The constant
a
of the secondary term encodes the contribution from a mixture of certain oscillatory integrals and “twisted quadratic exponential sums” regarding the Poisson variables
\boldsymbol{c}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{3}\setminus\boldsymbol{0}
satisfying
-F^{*}(\boldsymbol{c})=\square
in a somewhat complicated way, but it is nevertheless totally explicit.
2
2

2

In
[
11
, §13]
similar contribution from
\boldsymbol{c}\neq\boldsymbol{0}
also appears in the secondary term in the case where
m=0
, and either
F
is quaternary and
\Delta_{F}
is a perfect square, or
F
is ternary. However no explanation is provided up to now.
In
[
21
]
it is interpreted as a certain “regularised Eisenstein distribution” (cf.
[
21
, Remark 1.4]
).
It would be interesting to compare the constants obtained via all these different approaches.

2.
Recap on Heath-Brown’s
\delta
-method

We start by recalling several basic manipulations from
[
14
,
15
]
to deal with our inhomogeneous problem.
In contrast to
[
14
]
, in this paper we shall use the letter
d
to denote the number of variables, which is the dimension of the ambient affine space.

Let
F(\boldsymbol{x})\in{\mathbb{Z}}[\boldsymbol{x}]
be a non-degenerate integral quadratic form in
d\geqslant 3
variables, and let
m
be a non-zero integer. Let
{\mathscr{W}}_{m}
be the affine
{\mathbb{Z}}
-scheme defined by
(F=m)\subset{\mathbb{A}}^{d}_{{\mathbb{Z}}}
. Let
L\in{\mathbb{N}}
and let
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{m}\in{\mathscr{W}}_{m}({\mathbb{Z}}/L{\mathbb{Z}})
. We choose
\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{m}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
a “lift of
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{m}
”, i.e. such that
F(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{m})\equiv m\bmod L
and its coordinates have absolute value bounded by
L
. Fix
w:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}
a compactly supported weight function. We associate the counting function weighted by
w
:

N_{{\mathscr{W}}_{m}}(B):=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{x}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d},F(\boldsymbol{x})=m\\
\boldsymbol{x}\equiv\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{m}\bmod L\end{subarray}}w\left(\frac{\boldsymbol{x}}{B}\right).

Write
\boldsymbol{x}=L\boldsymbol{y}+\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{m}
. Since

L^{2}\mid F(L\boldsymbol{y}+\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{m})-m\Leftrightarrow L\mid\widehat{H}(\boldsymbol{y}),

where we define

(2.1)

\widehat{H}(\boldsymbol{y}):=\frac{F(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{m})-m}{L}+\nabla F(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{m})\cdot\boldsymbol{y},

we therefore have

N_{{\mathscr{W}}_{m}}(B)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{y}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}\\
L\mid\widehat{H}(\boldsymbol{y})\end{subarray}}w\left(\frac{L\boldsymbol{y}+\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{m}}{B}\right)\delta\left(\frac{F(L\boldsymbol{y}+\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{m})-m}{L^{2}}\right).

Applying
[
11
, Theorem 1]
and Poisson summation, we get

(2.2)

N_{{\mathscr{W}}_{m}}(B)=\frac{C_{Q}}{Q^{2}}\sum_{q=1}^{\infty}\sum_{\boldsymbol{c}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}\frac{\widehat{S}_{q}(\boldsymbol{c})\widehat{I}_{q}(w;\boldsymbol{c})}{(qL)^{d}},

where
C_{Q}=1+O_{N}(Q^{-N})
,

(2.3)

\widehat{S}_{q}(\boldsymbol{c}):=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a\bmod q\\
(a,q)=1\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{\sigma}\in({\mathbb{Z}}/qL{\mathbb{Z}})^{d}\\
\widehat{H}(\boldsymbol{\sigma})\equiv 0\bmod L\end{subarray}}\textup{e}_{qL}\left(a\left(\widehat{H}(\boldsymbol{\sigma})+LF(\boldsymbol{\sigma})\right)+\boldsymbol{c}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}\right)

is a twisted quadratic exponential sum, and

(2.4)

\widehat{I}_{q}(w;\boldsymbol{c}):=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}w\left(\frac{L\boldsymbol{y}+\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{m}}{B}\right)h\left(\frac{q}{Q},\frac{F(L\boldsymbol{y}+\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{m})-m}{L^{2}Q^{2}}\right)\textup{e}_{qL}\left(-\boldsymbol{c}\cdot\boldsymbol{y}\right)\operatorname{d}\boldsymbol{y}

is the “oscillatory integral”. We emphasize that
\widehat{I}_{q}(w;\boldsymbol{c})
also depends on
B,Q
, but to keep the notation lighter we omit them. Here
h(x,y):(0,\infty)\times{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
is a “nice” infinitely differentiable function satisfying the properties in
[
11
, §4]
.

2.1.
Quadratic exponential sums

Along the same lines as in
[
14
, §2.4]
,
for
q\in{\mathbb{N}}
, let

q=q_{1}q_{2}

be a decomposition such that

\gcd(q_{1},q_{2}\Omega)=1.

Let

\widehat{k}:=\frac{F(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{m})-m}{L},

and write

\widehat{k}=\widehat{k}_{2}q_{1}+\widehat{k}_{1}q_{2}L,

with
\widehat{k}_{1}\bmod q_{1}
and
\widehat{k}_{2}\bmod q_{2}L
. Then using the Chinese remainder theorem, we can decompose
\widehat{S}_{q}(\boldsymbol{c})
into

\widehat{S}_{q}(\boldsymbol{c})=\widehat{S}_{q}^{(1)}(\boldsymbol{c})\widehat{S}_{q}^{(2)}(\boldsymbol{c}),

where

(2.5)

\widehat{S}_{q}^{(1)}(\boldsymbol{c}):=\sum_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{1}\bmod q_{1}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a_{1}\bmod q_{1}\\
(a_{1},q_{1})=1\end{subarray}}\textup{e}_{q_{1}}\left(a_{1}\left((q_{2}L)^{2}F(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{1})+q_{2}(\nabla F(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{m})\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{1}+\widehat{k}_{1})\right)+\boldsymbol{c}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{1}\right),

(2.6)

\widehat{S}_{q}^{(2)}(\boldsymbol{c}):=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{2}\bmod q_{2}L\\
L\mid\widehat{H}(q_{1}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{2})\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a_{2}\bmod q_{2}\\
(a_{2},q_{2})=1\end{subarray}}\textup{e}_{q_{2}L}\left(a_{2}\left(q_{1}^{2}LF(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{2})+q_{1}(\nabla F(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{m})\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{2}+\widehat{k}_{2})\right)+\boldsymbol{c}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{2}\right).

Indeed,
\widehat{S}_{q}^{(1)}(\boldsymbol{c})
and
\widehat{S}_{q}^{(2)}(\boldsymbol{c})
are defined in exactly the same way as in
[
14
, Lemma 4.2]
, except that we replace all
k_{i}
by
\widehat{k}_{i}
for
i=1,2
and in
\widehat{S}_{q}^{(2)}(\boldsymbol{c})
we replace
H_{\boldsymbol{\lambda},L}
by
\widehat{H}
.

The term
\widehat{S}_{q}^{(1)}(\boldsymbol{c})
(
2.5
) is an exponential sum over “good moduli” and can be estimated explicitly.

Proposition 2.1
.

Assuming
\gcd(q_{1},\Omega)=1
, we have

\widehat{S}_{q}^{(1)}(\boldsymbol{c})=\textup{e}_{q_{1}}\left(-\overline{q_{2}L^{2}}\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{m}\cdot\boldsymbol{c}\right)\\
\times\iota_{q_{1}}^{d}\left(\frac{\Delta_{F}}{q_{1}}\right)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a\bmod q_{1}\\
(a,q_{1})=1\end{subarray}}\left(\frac{a}{q_{1}}\right)^{d}\textup{e}_{q_{1}}\left(-am-\overline{4\Delta_{F}a}(q_{2}L^{2})^{2}F^{*}(\boldsymbol{c})\right).

Sketch of proof.

The change of variables

a_{1}\mapsto a_{1}^{\prime}:=a_{1}\overline{L^{2}},\quad\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{1}\mapsto{\mathbf{b}}:=q_{2}L^{2}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{1}+\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{m}

gives

(2.7)

\widehat{S}_{q}^{(1)}(\boldsymbol{c})=\textup{e}_{q_{1}}\left(-\overline{q_{2}L^{2}}\boldsymbol{c}\cdot\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{m}\right)T_{q_{1}}(F,m;\overline{q_{2}L^{2}}\boldsymbol{c}),

where we let

(2.8)

T_{q}(F,m;\boldsymbol{c}):=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a\bmod q\\
(a,q)=1\end{subarray}}\sum_{{\mathbf{b}}\in({\mathbb{Z}}/q{\mathbb{Z}})^{d}}\textup{e}_{q}\left(a(F({\mathbf{b}})-m)+\boldsymbol{c}\cdot{\mathbf{b}}\right).

Applying
[
14
, Lemma 4.3]
gives that, when
\gcd(q,2\Delta_{F})=1
,

T_{q}(F,m;\boldsymbol{c})=\iota_{q}^{d}\left(\frac{\Delta_{F}}{q}\right)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a\bmod q\\
(a,q)=1\end{subarray}}\left(\frac{a}{q}\right)^{d}\textup{e}_{q}\left(-am-\overline{4\Delta_{F}a}F^{*}(\boldsymbol{c})\right).

We thus obtain the desired formula.
∎

Proposition 2.3
.

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}q\leqslant X\\

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}q\leqslant X\\
q_{m\Omega}>\psi(X)\end{subarray}}\frac{\left|\widehat{S}_{q}(\boldsymbol{c})\right|}{q^{\frac{d+1}{2}}}\ll_{\varepsilon}\Upsilon_{\frac{1}{4}}(m)|\boldsymbol{c}|^{2+\varepsilon}X\psi(X)^{-\frac{1}{4}}.

.

Under the decomposition

\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0}
,

(2.31)

{\mathscr{J}}_{1}\ll_{\varepsilon}d_{x_{0,1}}\left(\frac{b_{1}}{d_{x_{0,1}}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}+\varepsilon}\ll b_{1}^{\frac{1}{2}+\varepsilon}\gcd(x_{0},b_{1})^{\frac{1}{2}}.

To control
{\mathscr{J}}_{2}
, we may assume that

b_{2}=p^{\alpha}

with
\alpha\geqslant 2
. We apply

, Lemmas 12.2 & 12.3]
which yields

{\mathscr{J}}_{2}\ll p^{\lceil\frac{\alpha}{2}\rceil}\#\{y\bmod p^{\lfloor\frac{\alpha}{2}\rfloor}:p\nmid b_{1}x_{0,2}y+y^{2},bb_{1}x_{0,2}\overline{(b_{1}x_{0,2}y+y^{2})}\equiv\overline{b_{1}}L\boldsymbol{c}\cdot\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0}\bmod p^{\lfloor\frac{\alpha}{2}\rfloor}\}

The integer
b=b(\chi_{b_{2}})
is uniquely determined modulo
p^{\lceil\frac{\alpha}{2}\rceil}
in
[
20

. More precisely, write

\beta_{1}:=\operatorname{ord}_{p}(b),\quad\alpha_{1}:=\operatorname{ord}_{p}(|(\chi_{b_{2}})_{*}|).

Then

\beta_{1}=\begin{cases}\lceil\frac{\alpha}{2}\rceil&\text{ if }\alpha_{1}\leqslant\lfloor\frac{\alpha}{2}\rfloor;\\
\alpha-\alpha_{1}&\text{ if }\alpha_{1}>\lfloor\frac{\alpha}{2}\rfloor.\end{cases}

In all cases we have

\beta_{1}\leqslant\alpha-\alpha_{1}.

Let

\beta_{0}:=\operatorname{ord}_{p}(x_{0,2}),\quad\beta:=\beta_{0}+\beta_{1}.

We first assume
\beta<\lfloor\frac{\alpha}{2}\rfloor
.
Then we must have
p^{\beta}\mid\overline{b_{1}}L\boldsymbol{c}\cdot\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0}
. On writing
y^{\prime}\equiv\overline{y}\bmod p^{\lfloor\frac{\alpha}{2}\rfloor-\beta},t_{1}:=\frac{bb_{1}x_{0,2}}{p^{\beta}},t_{2}:=\frac{\overline{b_{1}}L\boldsymbol{c}\cdot\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0}}{p^{\beta}}
, we are led to

(2.32)

\begin{split}{\mathscr{J}}_{2}&\ll p^{\lceil\frac{\alpha}{2}\rceil+\beta}\#\{y^{\prime}\bmod p^{\lfloor\frac{\alpha}{2}\rfloor-\beta}:y^{\prime 2}-\overline{t_{1}}t_{2}b_{1}x_{0,2}y^{\prime}-\overline{t_{1}}t_{2}\equiv 0\bmod p^{\lfloor\frac{\alpha}{2}\rfloor-\beta}\}\\
&\ll p^{\lceil\frac{\alpha}{2}\rceil+\frac{1}{2}\lfloor\frac{\alpha}{2}\rfloor+\frac{1}{2}\beta}\leqslant p^{\frac{3}{4}\alpha+\frac{1}{2}\beta+\frac{1}{4}}\leqslant p^{\frac{5}{4}\alpha+\frac{1}{4}+\frac{1}{2}\min(\beta_{0},\alpha)-\frac{1}{2}\alpha_{1}}.\end{split}

Here we use the estimate

(2.33)

\#\{x\bmod p^{\gamma}:x^{2}\equiv a\bmod p^{\gamma}\}\ll p^{\frac{1}{2}\gamma}

for any
a\bmod p^{\gamma}
. (Indeed, the bound

is attained precisely when
a=0

a\neq 0

O(1)

If
\beta\geqslant\lfloor\frac{\alpha}{2}\rfloor

(2.34)

{\mathscr{J}}_{2}\ll p^{\alpha}\leqslant p^{\frac{5}{4}\alpha+\frac{1}{4}+\frac{1}{2}\min(\beta_{0},\alpha)-\frac{1}{2}\alpha_{1}}.

Recall that
b_{2}
is square-full. Gathering together (
2.31
) (
2.32
) (
2.34
), we thus obtain

\displaystyle{\mathscr{J}}_{\chi}(x_{0};\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0};\boldsymbol{c})

\displaystyle\ll_{\varepsilon}b_{1}^{\frac{1}{2}+\varepsilon}\gcd(x_{0},b_{1})^{\frac{1}{2}}b_{2}^{\frac{5}{4}+\frac{1}{8}+\varepsilon}\gcd(x_{0},b_{2})^{\frac{1}{2}}|(\chi_{b_{2}})_{*}|^{-\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\ll b_{1}^{1+\varepsilon}b_{2}^{\frac{5}{4}+\frac{1}{8}+\varepsilon}\gcd(x_{0},b_{1}b_{2})^{\frac{1}{2}}|(\chi_{b_{1}})_{*}|^{-\frac{1}{2}}|(\chi_{b_{2}})_{*}|^{-\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\ll q_{2}^{\frac{11}{8}+\varepsilon}\gcd(x_{0},q_{2}L^{2})^{\frac{1}{2}}|\chi_{*}|^{-\frac{1}{2}}.

Going back to (

\displaystyle{\mathscr{R}}_{q_{2}}(a_{2};\boldsymbol{c})\ll_{\varepsilon}\frac{1}{L}\sum_{l\bmod L}\frac{q_{2}^{\frac{11}{8}+\varepsilon}}{|\chi_{*}|^{\frac{1}{2}}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x_{0}\bmod q_{2}L^{2}\end{subarray}}\gcd(x_{0},q_{2}L^{2})^{\frac{1}{2}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0}\bmod q_{2}L\\
\eqref{eq:divcond}\text{ holds}\end{subarray}}q_{2}^{d}\ll_{\varepsilon}\frac{q_{2}^{\frac{11}{8}+1+d+\varepsilon}}{|\chi_{*}|^{\frac{1}{2}}}.

The proof of the claim (

2.2.
The oscillatory integrals

We choose

(2.35)

Q=\frac{B}{L}.

The change of variables
\boldsymbol{t}=\frac{L\boldsymbol{y}+\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{m}}{B}
in the integral (
2.4
) gives

(2.36)

\widehat{I}_{q}(w;\boldsymbol{c})=\left(\frac{B}{L}\right)^{d}\textup{e}_{qL^{2}}(\boldsymbol{c}\cdot\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{m})\widehat{{\mathscr{I}}}_{\frac{q}{Q}}^{*}\left(w;\frac{\boldsymbol{c}}{L}\right),

where for any
{\mathbf{b}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and
r\in{\mathbb{R}}_{>0}
we define

(2.37)

\widehat{{\mathscr{I}}}_{r}^{*}(w;{\mathbf{b}}):=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}w(\boldsymbol{t})h\left(r,\widehat{F}_{B}(\boldsymbol{t})\right)\textup{e}_{r}\left(-{\mathbf{b}}\cdot\boldsymbol{t}\right)\operatorname{d}\boldsymbol{t},

and we write

(2.38)

\widehat{F}_{B}(\boldsymbol{t}):=F(\boldsymbol{t})-\frac{m}{B^{2}}.

Let us also recall

(2.39)

\widehat{{\mathscr{I}}}_{r}(w;{\mathbf{b}}):=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}w(\boldsymbol{t})h\left(r,F(\boldsymbol{t})\right)\textup{e}_{r}\left(-{\mathbf{b}}\cdot\boldsymbol{t}\right)\operatorname{d}\boldsymbol{t},

which is the “usual” one that we have seen in
[
11
, §7]
(or in a slightly different form in
[
14
, §3.1]
).

Heath-Brown proves the “simple estimates” and “harder estimates”
[
11
, §7 & §8]
for
\widehat{{\mathscr{I}}}_{r}(w;{\mathbf{b}})
and its derivative with respect to
r
. (See also
[
14
, Corollary 3.3]
.) As long as
m=O(B^{2-\theta})
and
B
is large enough, all the arguments in
[
11
, §7, §8]
regarding
\widehat{{\mathscr{I}}}_{r}^{*}(w;{\mathbf{b}})
work similarly. We summarise these estimates as follows as they will be used repeatedly.

Lemma 2.5
.

Assume (
1.4
).
Then uniformly for all
{\mathbf{b}}
and
r
,

\max\left(\widehat{{\mathscr{I}}}_{r}(w;{\mathbf{b}}),\widehat{{\mathscr{I}}}_{r}^{*}(w;{\mathbf{b}})\right)\ll 1;\quad\text{``Trivial estimate''}.

If
{\mathbf{b}}=\boldsymbol{0}
we have

\max\left(\widehat{{\mathscr{I}}}_{r}(w;\boldsymbol{0}),r\frac{\partial\widehat{{\mathscr{I}}}_{r}(w;\boldsymbol{0})}{\partial r},\widehat{{\mathscr{I}}}_{r}^{*}(w;\boldsymbol{0}),r\frac{\partial\widehat{{\mathscr{I}}}_{r}^{*}(w;\boldsymbol{0})}{\partial r}\right)\ll 1.

If
{\mathbf{b}}\neq\boldsymbol{0}
, we have

\max\left(\widehat{{\mathscr{I}}}^{*}_{r}(w;{\mathbf{b}}),\widehat{{\mathscr{I}}}_{r}(w;{\mathbf{b}}),r\frac{\partial\widehat{{\mathscr{I}}}_{r}(w;{\mathbf{b}})}{\partial r}\right)\begin{cases}\ll_{N}r^{-1}|{\mathbf{b}}|^{-N};&\quad\text{``Simple estimates''},\\
\ll_{\varepsilon}\left(\frac{r}{|{\mathbf{b}}|}\right)^{\frac{d-2}{2}-\varepsilon};&\quad\text{``Harder estimates''}.\end{cases}

The following lemma measures how close is

\widehat{{\mathscr{I}}}_{r}^{*}(w;{\mathbf{b}})
to
\widehat{{\mathscr{I}}}_{r}(w;{\mathbf{b}})
as
B\to\infty
.

Lemma 2.6
.

Assume (
1.4
). We have, uniformly for any
0<r\ll 1
and
{\mathbf{b}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
,

\widehat{{\mathscr{I}}}_{r}^{*}(w;{\mathbf{b}})=\widehat{{\mathscr{I}}}_{r}(w;{\mathbf{b}})+O(r^{-2}B^{-\theta}).

Concerning
{\mathbf{b}}=\boldsymbol{0}
we have

\widehat{{\mathscr{I}}}_{r}^{*}(w;\boldsymbol{0})=\widehat{{\mathscr{I}}}_{r}(w;\boldsymbol{0})+O(B^{-\theta})+O_{N}(r^{N}).

Proof.

We have

\displaystyle\widehat{{\mathscr{I}}}_{r}^{*}(w;{\mathbf{b}})-\widehat{{\mathscr{I}}}_{r}(w;{\mathbf{b}})

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{3}}w(\boldsymbol{t})\left(h(r,F(\boldsymbol{t}))-h(r,\widehat{F}_{B}(\boldsymbol{t}))\right)\textup{e}_{r}\left(-{\mathbf{b}}\cdot\boldsymbol{t}\right)\operatorname{d}\boldsymbol{t}

\displaystyle\ll\sup_{\boldsymbol{t}\in\operatorname{Supp}(w)}\left|\frac{\partial h}{\partial y}(r,F(\boldsymbol{t}))\right|\frac{|m|}{B^{2}}.

By
[
11
, Lemma 5]
, the partial derivative above is
\ll r^{-2}
, so the difference above is
\ll r^{-2}B^{-\theta}
.

We now deal with the case
{\mathbf{b}}=\boldsymbol{0}
.
According to
[
11
, Lemma 13]
, we have

\widehat{{\mathscr{I}}}_{r}(w;\boldsymbol{0})={\mathscr{I}}(w)+O_{N}(r^{N}),

\widehat{{\mathscr{I}}}_{r}^{*}(w;\boldsymbol{0})={\mathscr{I}}^{*}(w)+O_{N}(r^{N}),

where
{\mathscr{I}}(w)
is defined by (
1.1
) and
{\mathscr{I}}^{*}(w)
is defined similarly.
Without loss of generality we may assume
\frac{\partial F}{\partial x_{1}}\gg 1
in
\operatorname{Supp}(w)
, and we may write

{\mathscr{I}}(w)=\int_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{x}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{3}\\
x_{1}\text{ solved by }F(\boldsymbol{x})=0\end{subarray}}w(\boldsymbol{x})\frac{\operatorname{d}\boldsymbol{x}}{\frac{\partial F}{\partial x_{1}}(\boldsymbol{x})},\quad{\mathscr{I}}^{*}(w)=\int_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{x}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{3}\\
x_{1}\text{ solved by }F(\boldsymbol{x})=\frac{m}{B^{2}}\end{subarray}}w(\boldsymbol{x})\frac{\operatorname{d}\boldsymbol{x}}{\frac{\partial F}{\partial x_{1}}(\boldsymbol{x})}.

Then

{\mathscr{I}}(w)-{\mathscr{I}}^{*}(w)\ll\sup_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\operatorname{Supp}(w)}\left|\frac{\partial w}{\partial x_{1}}\left(\frac{\partial F}{\partial x_{1}}\right)^{-1}(\boldsymbol{x})\right|\frac{|m|}{B}\ll B^{-\theta}.

This finishes the proof.
∎

3.
Equidistribution of certain quadratic congruences

In this section we assume
-m\Delta_{F}=\square
and let

(3.1)

d_{0}:=\sqrt{-m\Delta_{F}}.

Recall the polynomial
G_{l_{1},l_{2},\boldsymbol{c}}(T)
defined in (
2.19
). Then

G_{l_{1},l_{2},\boldsymbol{c}}(T)=(\Delta_{F}l_{1}T)^{2}-(d_{0}l_{2}L^{2})^{2}(-F^{*}(\boldsymbol{c})).

So
G_{l_{1},l_{2},\boldsymbol{c}}(T)
is irreducible if and only if
-F^{*}(\boldsymbol{c})\neq\square
.

Let
\chi
be a Dirichlet character. For
X\gg 1
, our main focus is the following average of certain exponential sums against quadratic congruences (recall (
1.6
)):

(3.2)

{\mathscr{U}}_{l_{1},l_{2},\boldsymbol{c}}(\chi;X):=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leqslant X\\
\gcd(n,m\Omega)=1\end{subarray}}\chi(n)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}v\bmod n\\
G_{l_{1},l_{2},\boldsymbol{c}}(v)\equiv 0\bmod n\end{subarray}}\textup{e}_{n}(v).

The crucial technical core of the proof of Theorem
is the following two results, treated separately depending on whether or not
-F^{*}(\boldsymbol{c})=\square
.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
\chi
be a Dirichlet character. Let
l_{1},l_{2}\in{\mathbb{N}}
be such that
l_{i}\mid(m\Omega)^{\infty},i=1,2
.

(1)

Let
\boldsymbol{c}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{3}\setminus\boldsymbol{0}
be such that
-F^{*}(\boldsymbol{c})\neq\square
. Then uniformly for such
\chi,l_{1},l_{2},\boldsymbol{c}
, we have

{\mathscr{U}}_{l_{1},l_{2},\boldsymbol{c}}(\chi;X)=O_{\varepsilon}\left(|\boldsymbol{c}|^{2+\varepsilon}\frac{X(\log\log X)^{\frac{5}{2}}}{(\log X)^{1-\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}}}\right).

(2)

Let
\boldsymbol{c}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{3}\setminus\boldsymbol{0}
be such that
-F^{*}(\boldsymbol{c})
is a non-zero square. Then there exists
\Gamma(\chi;l_{1},l_{2};\boldsymbol{c})\in{\mathbb{C}}
such that

{\mathscr{U}}_{l_{1},l_{2},\boldsymbol{c}}(\chi;X)=\Gamma(\chi;l_{1},l_{2};\boldsymbol{c})X+O_{\varepsilon}\left((l_{1}l_{2}m|\boldsymbol{c}|X)^{\varepsilon}(|\chi|^{\frac{11}{8}}l_{1}^{\frac{7}{8}}X^{\frac{63}{64}}+l_{2}|\boldsymbol{c}|m^{\frac{1}{2}})\right).

The constant
\Gamma(\chi;l_{1},l_{2};\boldsymbol{c})
satisfies

\Gamma(\chi;l_{1},l_{2};\boldsymbol{c})\ll_{\varepsilon}\frac{(l_{2}|\boldsymbol{c}||\chi|)^{\varepsilon}}{|\chi_{*}|^{\frac{15}{32}}}\Upsilon_{1}(m)

where
\chi_{*}
is the primitive character of modulus
|\chi_{*}|
inducing
\chi
.

3.1.
Proof of Theorem
3.1
(1)

Our proof is an adaptation of Hooley’s work
[
17
]
on equidistribution modulo one of irreducible polynomial congruences.

Following
[
17
, p. 40]
, for every
n\geqslant 2
and for every
h\bmod n
, let us introduce

S_{\boldsymbol{c}}(h,n):=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}v\bmod n\\
v^{2}\equiv-F^{*}(\boldsymbol{c})\bmod n\end{subarray}}\textup{e}_{n}(hv).

By
[
17
, Lemma 2]
,
S_{\boldsymbol{c}}(h,n)
is “almost” multiplicative, in the sense that if
\gcd(n_{1},n_{2})=1
,

(3.3)

S_{\boldsymbol{c}}(h,n_{1}n_{2})=S_{\boldsymbol{c}}(h\overline{n_{2}},n_{1})S_{\boldsymbol{c}}(h\overline{n_{1}},n_{2}),

where
\overline{n_{1}}n_{1}\equiv 1\bmod n_{2}
and
\overline{n_{2}}n_{2}\equiv 1\bmod n_{1}
.

We recall (
2.13
).
Clearly for every
h\bmod n
,

\left|S_{\boldsymbol{c}}(h,n)\right|\leqslant\varrho_{\boldsymbol{c}}(n),

and
[
17
, Lemma 1]
provides

(3.4)

\sum_{h\bmod n}\left|S_{\boldsymbol{c}}(h,n)\right|^{2}\leqslant n\varrho_{\boldsymbol{c}}(n),

for any
n\geqslant 2
.

We now have

{\mathscr{U}}_{l_{1},l_{2},\boldsymbol{c}}(\chi;X)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leqslant X\\
\gcd(n,m\Omega)=1\end{subarray}}\chi(n)S_{\boldsymbol{c}}(\overline{\Delta_{F}l_{1}}d_{0}l_{2}L^{2},n).

As in
[
17
, p. 43]
, we define

{\mathscr{X}}={\mathscr{X}}(X):=X^{(48\textup{e}\log\log X)^{-1}}.

And for every
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, we write

n_{{\mathscr{X}}}:=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\mid n\\
p\leqslant{\mathscr{X}}\end{subarray}}p^{\operatorname{ord}_{p}(n)}.

Let us split the sum
{\mathscr{U}}_{l_{1},l_{2},\boldsymbol{c}}(\chi;X)
into two parts according to the range of
n_{{\mathscr{X}}}
:

\Sigma_{1}:=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leqslant X,n_{{\mathscr{X}}}>X^{\frac{1}{3}}\\
\gcd(n,m\Omega)=1\end{subarray}}\chi(n)S_{\boldsymbol{c}}(\overline{\Delta_{F}l_{1}}d_{0}l_{2}L^{2},n),~\Sigma_{2}:=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leqslant X,n_{{\mathscr{X}}}\leqslant X^{\frac{1}{3}}\\
\gcd(n,m\Omega)=1\end{subarray}}\chi(n)S_{\boldsymbol{c}}(\overline{\Delta_{F}l_{1}}d_{0}l_{2}L^{2},n).

We first consider
\Sigma_{1}
. Using (
2.15
), the arguments in
[
17
, pp. 44–45]
(based on the Hardy–Ramanujan inequality) offer

(3.5)

\left|\Sigma_{1}\right|\leqslant\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leqslant X\\
n_{{\mathscr{X}}}>X^{\frac{1}{3}}\end{subarray}}\varrho_{\boldsymbol{c}}(n)\ll_{\varepsilon}|\boldsymbol{c}|^{1+\varepsilon}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leqslant X\\
n_{{\mathscr{X}}}>X^{\frac{1}{3}}\end{subarray}}2^{\omega(n)}=O_{\varepsilon}\left(|\boldsymbol{c}|^{1+\varepsilon}\frac{X}{\log X}\right).

To deal with
\Sigma_{2}
, we make use of the multiplicativity (
3.3
) to get

\displaystyle\Sigma_{2}

\displaystyle=\underset{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leqslant X,(n,m\Omega)=1\\
n_{1}=n_{{\mathscr{X}}}\leqslant X^{\frac{1}{3}},n_{2}=n/n_{{\mathscr{X}}}\end{subarray}}{\sum\sum}\chi(n)S_{\boldsymbol{c}}(\overline{\Delta_{F}l_{1}n_{2}}d_{0}l_{2}L^{2},n_{1})S_{\boldsymbol{c}}(\overline{\Delta_{F}l_{1}n_{1}}d_{0}l_{2}L^{2},n_{2})

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n_{1}\leqslant X^{\frac{1}{3}},\gcd(n_{1},m\Omega)=1\\
p\mid n_{1}\Rightarrow p\leqslant{\mathscr{X}}\end{subarray}}T_{n_{1},\boldsymbol{c}}(X),

where

T_{n_{1},\boldsymbol{c}}(X):=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n_{2}\leqslant\frac{X}{n_{1}}\\
p\mid n_{2}\Rightarrow p>{\mathscr{X}}\end{subarray}}\varrho_{\boldsymbol{c}}(n_{2})\left|S_{\boldsymbol{c}}(\overline{\Delta_{F}l_{1}n_{2}}d_{0}l_{2}L^{2},n_{1})\right|.

We pause to analyse the second moment of the summands in
T_{n_{1},\boldsymbol{c}}(X)
for fixed
n_{1}
. Let
y
be such that
y\geqslant X^{\frac{2}{3}}
.
The arguments in
[
17
, p. 46]
(using sieve methods
[
17
, Lemma 7]
) plus (
2.15
) offer

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n_{2}\leqslant y\\
p\mid n_{2}\Rightarrow p>{\mathscr{X}}\end{subarray}}\varrho_{\boldsymbol{c}}(n_{2})^{2}\ll_{\varepsilon}|\boldsymbol{c}|^{2+\varepsilon}y\frac{(\log\log X)^{4}}{\log X}.

Now for every fixed
n_{1}
, using
[
17
, Lemma 8]
and (
3.4
),

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n_{2}\leqslant y\\
p\mid n_{2}\Rightarrow p>{\mathscr{X}}\end{subarray}}\left|S_{\boldsymbol{c}}(\overline{\Delta_{F}l_{1}n_{2}}d_{0}l_{2}L^{2},n_{1})\right|^{2}=

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}h\bmod n_{1}\\
(h,n_{1})=1\end{subarray}}\left|S_{\boldsymbol{c}}(h,n_{1})\right|^{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n_{2}\leqslant y,p\mid n_{2}\Rightarrow p>{\mathscr{X}}\\
n_{2}\equiv\overline{h\Delta_{F}l_{1}}d_{0}l_{2}L^{2}\bmod n_{1}\end{subarray}}1

\displaystyle\ll

\displaystyle n_{1}\varrho_{\boldsymbol{c}}(n_{1})\frac{y}{\varphi(n_{1})}\frac{\log\log X}{\log X}.

Therefore, on letting
y=\frac{X}{n_{1}}
, by Cauchy-Schwarz lemma we have

\displaystyle T_{n_{1},\boldsymbol{c}}(X)

\displaystyle\ll_{\varepsilon}|\boldsymbol{c}|^{1+\varepsilon}\frac{X(\log\log X)^{\frac{5}{2}}}{\log X}\left(\frac{\varrho_{\boldsymbol{c}}(n_{1})}{n_{1}\varphi(n_{1})}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

Hence as in
[
17
, p. 47]
,

\displaystyle\Sigma_{2}

\displaystyle\ll|\boldsymbol{c}|^{1+\varepsilon}\frac{X(\log\log X)^{\frac{5}{2}}}{\log X}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n_{1}\leqslant X^{\frac{1}{3}}\\
p\mid n_{1}\Rightarrow p\leqslant{\mathscr{X}}\end{subarray}}\left(\frac{\varrho_{\boldsymbol{c}}(n_{1})}{n_{1}\varphi(n_{1})}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

\displaystyle\ll_{\varepsilon}|\boldsymbol{c}|^{1+\varepsilon}\frac{X(\log\log X)^{\frac{5}{2}}}{\log X}\sum_{l\leqslant X}\left(\frac{\varrho_{\boldsymbol{c}}(l)}{l\varphi(l)}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\ll_{\varepsilon}|\boldsymbol{c}|^{1+\varepsilon}2^{\omega(F^{*}(\boldsymbol{c}))}\frac{X(\log\log X)^{\frac{5}{2}}}{\log X}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\leqslant X\\
p\nmid 2F^{*}(\boldsymbol{c})\end{subarray}}\left(1+\frac{\varrho_{\boldsymbol{c}}(p)^{\frac{1}{2}}}{p}\right).

Our focus in the remaining of the proof is the product over
p
, denoted by
\Pi_{\boldsymbol{c}}(X)
say. Note that for every
p\nmid 2F^{*}(\boldsymbol{c})
, by (
2.14
),

\varrho_{\boldsymbol{c}}(p)=\begin{cases}2&\text{ if }\left(\frac{-F^{*}(\boldsymbol{c})}{p}\right)=1;\\
0&\text{ otherwise}.\end{cases}

Let
K_{\boldsymbol{c}}:={\mathbb{Q}}(\sqrt{-F^{*}(\boldsymbol{c})})
be the quadratic field extension generated by the roots of
G_{l_{1},l_{2},\boldsymbol{c}}(T)
, and let

{\mathscr{P}}_{\boldsymbol{c}}(X):=\{p\leqslant X:p\text{ is unramified and splits in }K_{\boldsymbol{c}}\}.

In particular

\Pi_{\boldsymbol{c}}(X)=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\in{\mathscr{P}}_{\boldsymbol{c}}(X)\end{subarray}}\left(1+\frac{\sqrt{2}}{p}\right).

Our analysis diverges into the relative size of
|\boldsymbol{c}|
and
\log X
.

Case I.
Assume
|\boldsymbol{c}|\leqslant\log X
.
Applying the Siegel–Walfisz Theorem (see e.g.
[
20
, Corollary 5.29]
) regarding all quadratic residues modulo
-4F^{*}(\boldsymbol{c})
, for any
A>2
, we have

\displaystyle\#{\mathscr{P}}_{\boldsymbol{c}}(X)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\frac{X}{\log X}+O_{A}\left(|F^{*}(\boldsymbol{c})|\frac{X}{(\log X)^{A}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\frac{X}{\log X}+O_{A}\left(\frac{X}{(\log X)^{A-2}}\right),

by our assumption on the size of
|\boldsymbol{c}|
. We may now fix
A=4
.
By partial summation we have

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\in{\mathscr{P}}_{\boldsymbol{c}}(X)\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{p}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=2}^{X}\frac{\#{\mathscr{P}}_{\boldsymbol{c}}(k)-\#{\mathscr{P}}_{\boldsymbol{c}}(k-1)}{k}+O(1)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{X}\frac{\#{\mathscr{P}}_{\boldsymbol{c}}(k)}{k^{2}}+O(1)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{X}\left(\frac{1}{2k\log k}+O\left(\frac{1}{k(\log k)^{2}}\right)\right)+O(1)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\log\log X+O(1).

We therefore conclude, uniformly for such
\boldsymbol{c}
,

\displaystyle\Pi_{\boldsymbol{c}}(X)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(\sum_{p\in{\mathscr{P}}_{\boldsymbol{c}}(X)}\log\left(1+\frac{\sqrt{2}}{p}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=O\left(\operatorname{exp}\left(\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\log\log X\right)\right)=O\left((\log X)^{\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}}\right).

Case II.
Assume that
|\boldsymbol{c}|>\log X
. We estimate trivially

\Pi_{\boldsymbol{c}}(X)\leqslant\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\leqslant X\end{subarray}}\left(1+\frac{\sqrt{2}}{p}\right)=O\left((\log X)^{\sqrt{2}}\right)=O\left(|\boldsymbol{c}|(\log X)^{\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}}\right),

thanks to our assumption on the size of
|\boldsymbol{c}|
.

So we conclude that in any case

\Pi_{\boldsymbol{c}}(X)=O\left(|\boldsymbol{c}|(\log X)^{\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}}\right),

and hence

(3.6)

\Sigma_{2}\ll_{\varepsilon}|\boldsymbol{c}|^{1+\varepsilon}\frac{X(\log\log X)^{\frac{5}{2}}}{\log X}\Pi_{\boldsymbol{c}}(X)\ll_{\varepsilon}|\boldsymbol{c}|^{2+\varepsilon}\frac{X(\log\log X)^{\frac{5}{2}}}{(\log X)^{1-\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}}}.

We finally combine the estimates (
3.5
) (
3.6
) to yield the desired bound.
∎

Proof of Theorem

(2)

Our proof is inspired by the work of Dartyge–Martin
[

, Theorem 1]
. In addition to the character sums, the key variant is that we need to control the dependency on

and
\boldsymbol{c}
in the error term, while dealing with the extra gcd condition on
n
.

We begin with the following slight generalisation of
[
5
, Lemma 1]
concerning mixed exponential sums involving a multiplicative inverse.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
q,s\in{\mathbb{N}}
be such that
\gcd(s,q)=1
and let
\chi
be a Dirichlet character such that
|\chi|
divides
q
. Then there exists
\Theta(q;s,t,\chi)\in{\mathbb{C}}
such that, uniformly for any
t\in{\mathbb{Z}}
,

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leqslant X\\
\gcd(n,sq)=1\end{subarray}}\chi(n)\textup{e}_{q}\left(t\overline{n}\right)=\Theta(q;s,t,\chi)X+O_{\varepsilon}\left(s^{\varepsilon}q^{\frac{7}{8}+\varepsilon}\gcd(q,t)^{\frac{1}{2}}\right).

The constant
\Theta(q;s,t,\chi)
satisfies

\Theta(q;s,t,\chi)\begin{cases}\leqslant\frac{1}{\varphi(q/(t,q))}&\text{ if }\chi\text{ is principal};\\
\ll_{\varepsilon}\frac{q^{\varepsilon}\gcd\left(t,\frac{q}{|\chi_{*}|}\right)}{q}|\chi_{*}|^{\frac{1}{2}}&\text{ otherwise}.\end{cases}

Proof.

We proceed via a standard completing process, which is an adaptation of
[
5
, Lemma 1]
. We have

(3.7)

\begin{split}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leqslant X\\
\gcd(n,sq)=1\end{subarray}}\chi(n)\textup{e}_{q}\left(t\overline{n}\right)&=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\bmod q\\
\gcd(x,q)=1\end{subarray}}\chi(x)\textup{e}_{q}(t\overline{x})\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leqslant X,n\equiv x\bmod q\\
\gcd(n,s)=1\end{subarray}}1\\
&=\frac{1}{q}\sum_{a\bmod q}S_{q}(t;a)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leqslant X\\
\gcd(n,s)=1\end{subarray}}\textup{e}_{q}(an),\end{split}

where for fixed
a\bmod q
, we let

S_{q}(t;a):=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\bmod q\\
\gcd(x,q)=1\end{subarray}}\chi(x)\textup{e}_{q}(t\overline{x}-ax).

We first analyse the main term
a=0
in (
3.7
).
It is elementary to see that

(3.8)

\#\{n\leqslant X:\gcd(n,s)=1\}=\left(\sum_{d\mid s}\frac{\mu(d)}{d}\right)X+O_{\varepsilon}(s^{\varepsilon}).

We first assume that
\chi
is principal. Then
S_{q}(t;0)
is a Ramanujan sum, and can be evaluated as

(3.9)

S_{q}(t;0)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\bmod q\\
(x,q)=1\end{subarray}}\textup{e}_{q}\left(tx\right)=\mu\left(\frac{q}{(q,t)}\right)\frac{\varphi(q)}{\varphi(q/(t,q))}.

We now assume that
\chi
is not principal.
Then
S_{q}(t;0)
is a Gauss sum, and by
[
20
, Lemma 3.2]
,

\displaystyle S_{q}(t;0)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\bmod q}\chi(x)^{c}\textup{e}_{q}\left(tx\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{x\bmod|\chi_{*}|}\chi_{*}(x)^{c}\textup{e}_{|\chi_{*}|}(x)\right)\left(\sum_{d\mid\gcd\left(t,\frac{q}{|\chi_{*}|}\right)}d\chi_{*}\left(\frac{t}{d}\right)\mu\left(\frac{q}{d|\chi_{*}|}\right)\right).

It satisfies

(3.10)

S_{q}(t;0)\ll|\chi_{*}|^{\frac{1}{2}}\sum_{d\mid\gcd\left(t,\frac{q}{|\chi_{*}|}\right)}d\ll_{\varepsilon}q^{\varepsilon}\gcd\left(t,\frac{q}{|\chi_{*}|}\right)|\chi_{*}|^{\frac{1}{2}}.

For the terms with
a\neq 0
in (
3.7
), we proceed as the proof of Proposition
2.4
. We factorize

q=q_{1}^{\prime}q_{2}^{\prime}

with
q_{1}^{\prime}
square-free,
q_{2}^{\prime}
square-full and
\gcd(q_{1}^{\prime},q_{2}^{\prime})=1
, so that we decompose
S_{q}(t;a)
into a mod
q_{1}^{\prime}
-sum
{\mathscr{J}}_{1}^{\prime}
and a mod
q_{2}^{\prime}
-sum
{\mathscr{J}}_{2}^{\prime}
. For
{\mathscr{J}}_{1}^{\prime}
, regardless of
\chi
being principal or not, by the Hasse–Weil estimate
[
27
]
, we have

{\mathscr{J}}_{1}^{\prime}\ll\tau(q_{1}^{\prime})\gcd(a,t,q_{1}^{\prime})^{\frac{1}{2}}q_{1}^{\prime\frac{1}{2}}.

Now for
{\mathscr{J}}_{2}^{\prime}
, we may again assume that
q_{2}^{\prime}=p^{\alpha}
with
\alpha\geqslant 2
. By
[
20
, Lemmas 12.2 & 12.3]
, we are led to

{\mathscr{J}}_{2}^{\prime}\leqslant p^{\lceil\frac{\alpha}{2}\rceil}\#\{x\bmod p^{\lfloor\frac{1}{2}\alpha\rfloor}:p\nmid x,ax^{2}-bx+t\equiv 0\bmod p^{\lfloor\frac{1}{2}\alpha\rfloor}\},

where
b=b(\chi)\bmod p^{\lceil\frac{\alpha}{2}\rceil}
depends on
\chi
. Let
\beta:=\operatorname{ord}_{p}(\gcd(a,b,t))
.
If
\beta<\lfloor\frac{1}{2}\alpha\rfloor
, on writing
a^{\prime}:=\frac{a}{p^{\beta}},b^{\prime}:=\frac{b}{p^{\beta}},t^{\prime}:=\frac{t}{p^{\beta}}
, we have

{\mathscr{J}}_{2}^{\prime}\leqslant p^{\lceil\frac{1}{2}\alpha\rceil+\beta}\#\{x^{\prime}\bmod p^{\lfloor\frac{1}{2}\alpha\rfloor-\beta}:p\nmid x^{\prime},a^{\prime}x^{2}-b^{\prime}x+t^{\prime}\equiv 0\bmod p^{\lfloor\frac{1}{2}\alpha\rfloor-\beta}\}.

If moreover
p\mid a
, then
p\nmid b^{\prime 2}-4a^{\prime}t^{\prime}
(otherwise
p\mid\gcd(a^{\prime},b^{\prime},t^{\prime})
) and the number of such
x^{\prime}
is
O(1)
. Hence in this case
{\mathscr{J}}_{2}^{\prime}\ll p^{\lceil\frac{1}{2}\alpha\rceil+\beta}
.
Otherwise if
p\nmid a^{\prime}
, then using (
2.33
), we have

{\mathscr{J}}_{2}^{\prime}\ll p^{\lceil\frac{1}{2}\alpha\rceil+\frac{1}{2}\lfloor\frac{\alpha}{2}\rfloor+\frac{1}{2}\beta}\leqslant p^{\frac{3}{4}\alpha+\frac{1}{2}\beta+\frac{1}{4}}\leqslant p^{\frac{3}{4}\alpha+\frac{1}{4}}\gcd(p^{\alpha},a,t)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

If
\beta\geqslant\lfloor\frac{1}{2}\alpha\rfloor
we estimate trivially

{\mathscr{J}}_{2}^{\prime}\ll p^{\alpha}\leqslant p^{\frac{3}{4}\alpha+\frac{1}{4}}\gcd(p^{\alpha},a,t)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

Therefore we conclude that

(3.11)

S_{q}(t;a)\ll_{\varepsilon}q^{\frac{7}{8}+\varepsilon}\gcd(q,a,t)^{\frac{1}{2}}\leqslant q^{\frac{7}{8}+\varepsilon}\gcd(q,t)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

Using (
3.11
), the contribution from the terms
a\neq 0
is,

\displaystyle\ll_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle q^{\frac{7}{8}+\varepsilon}\gcd(q,t)^{\frac{1}{2}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a\bmod q\\
a\neq 0\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{q}\left|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leqslant X\\
\gcd(n,s)=1\end{subarray}}\textup{e}_{q}(an)\right|

\displaystyle\ll

\displaystyle q^{\frac{7}{8}+\varepsilon}\gcd(q,t)^{\frac{1}{2}}\sum_{d\mid s}\mu^{2}(d)\times\frac{1}{q}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a\bmod q\\
a\neq 0\end{subarray}}\left|\sum_{n^{\prime}\leqslant X/d}\textup{e}_{q}(adn^{\prime})\right|.

We recall that
\gcd(s,q)=1
, and hence
\gcd(d,q)=1
, so the change of variable
a\mapsto ad
yields that the inner
a
-sum and
n^{\prime}
-sum is, uniformly for
d\mid s
,

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}b\bmod q\\
b\neq 0\end{subarray}}\left|\sum_{n^{\prime}\leqslant X/d}\textup{e}_{q}(bn^{\prime})\right|\ll\sum_{c=1}^{\frac{q}{2}}\frac{q}{c}\ll q\log q,

cf. the argument in
[
5
, p. 5]
.
So the overall contribution is

\ll_{\varepsilon}s^{\varepsilon}q^{\frac{7}{8}+\varepsilon}\gcd(q,t)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

We now finish the proof by taking

\Theta(q;s,t,\chi):=\frac{S_{q}(t;0)}{q}\sum_{d\mid s}\frac{\mu(d)}{d},

and the bound results from (
3.9
) and (
3.10
). (Note that
\sum_{d\mid s}\frac{\mu(d)}{d}=\prod_{p\mid s}\left(1-\frac{1}{p}\right)\leqslant 1
.)
∎

We now enter the proof of Theorem
3.1
(2) by setting some notational preliminaries.
We write

-F^{*}(\boldsymbol{c})={\mathscr{N}}(\boldsymbol{c})^{2}

for
{\mathscr{N}}(\boldsymbol{c})\in{\mathbb{N}}
, and

(3.12)

a_{0}:=\frac{l_{1}\Delta_{F}}{\gcd(\Delta_{F}l_{1},d_{0}l_{2}L^{2}{\mathscr{N}}(\boldsymbol{c}))},\quad b_{0}:=\frac{d_{0}l_{2}L^{2}{\mathscr{N}}(\boldsymbol{c})}{\gcd(\Delta_{F}l_{1},d_{0}l_{2}L^{2}{\mathscr{N}}(\boldsymbol{c}))}.

We shall consider the auxiliary polynomial

\widehat{G}_{l_{1},l_{2},\boldsymbol{c}}(T):=(a_{0}T+b_{0})(a_{0}T-b_{0}).

The discriminant is

{\mathscr{D}}:=2|a_{0}b_{0}|\asymp l_{1}l_{2}|\boldsymbol{c}||m|^{\frac{1}{2}}.

Note that

G_{l_{1},l_{2},\boldsymbol{c}}(T)=\gcd(\Delta_{F}l_{1},d_{0}l_{2}L^{2}{\mathscr{N}}(\boldsymbol{c}))^{2}\widehat{G}_{l_{1},l_{2},\boldsymbol{c}}(T)

and
\gcd(a_{0},b_{0})=1
.
Thanks to the condition
\gcd(n,m\Omega)=1
, we have

G_{l_{1},l_{2},\boldsymbol{c}}(u)\equiv 0\bmod n\Leftrightarrow\widehat{G}_{l_{1},l_{2},\boldsymbol{c}}(u)\equiv 0\bmod n.

(So that the coefficients have gcd one.)
In view of
[
5
, Lemmas 2&3]
, upon prescribing
\gcd(n,{\mathscr{D}})
then separating the congruence conditions of the two factors of
\widehat{G}_{l_{1},l_{2}\boldsymbol{c}}
, we have

\displaystyle{\mathscr{U}}_{l_{1},l_{2},\boldsymbol{c}}(\chi;X)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}g\mid{\mathscr{D}}\\
(g,a_{0})=1\end{subarray}}\mu^{2}(g)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leqslant\frac{X}{g}\\
(n,{\mathscr{D}}m\Omega)=1\end{subarray}}\chi(n)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r\bmod ng\\
\widehat{G}_{l,\boldsymbol{c}}(r)\equiv 0\bmod ng\end{subarray}}\textup{e}_{ng}\left(r\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}g\mid{\mathscr{D}}\\
(g,a_{0})=1\end{subarray}}\mu^{2}(g)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leqslant\frac{X}{g}\\
(n,{\mathscr{D}}m\Omega)=1\end{subarray}}\chi(n)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j,k:n=jk\\
(j,k)=(j,a_{0})=(k,a_{0})=1\end{subarray}}\textup{e}_{ng}\left(r_{j,k,g}\right),

where
r_{j,k,g}\bmod ng
is uniquely determined by the congruence conditions

a_{0}r_{j,k,g}+b_{0}\equiv 0\bmod k,\quad a_{0}r_{j,k,g}-b_{0}\equiv 0\bmod j,\quad r_{j,k,g}\equiv b_{0}\overline{a_{0}}\bmod g.

If
X\leqslant b_{0}
, then we sum everything trivially and obtain

{\mathscr{U}}_{l_{1},l_{2},\boldsymbol{c}}(\chi;X)\ll_{\varepsilon}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}g\mid{\mathscr{D}}\\
(g,a_{0})=1\end{subarray}}\mu^{2}(g)\sum_{n\leqslant b_{0}}n^{\varepsilon}\ll b_{0}^{1+\varepsilon}.

We next assume
\frac{X}{g}>b_{0}
for certain
g\mid{\mathscr{D}}
and we separate the sum over
j
and
k
respectively. Let
\mathfrak{S}_{1}(\chi;X)
be the sum with the restriction
k\leqslant\sqrt{\frac{X}{g}}
and let
\mathfrak{S}_{2}(\chi;X)
denote the sum with the remaining range. Proceeding as in
[
5
, p. 7, the equation above (7)]
we obtain

\mathfrak{S}_{1}(\chi;X)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}g\mid{\mathscr{D}}\\
(g,a_{0})=1\end{subarray}}\mu^{2}(g)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\leqslant\sqrt{\frac{X}{g}}\\
(k,{\mathscr{D}}m\Omega)=1\end{subarray}}\chi(k)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\leqslant\frac{X}{gk}\\
(j,k{\mathscr{D}}m\Omega)=1\end{subarray}}\chi(j)\textup{e}_{a_{0}k}\left(\widehat{t}\cdot\overline{j}\right)\textup{e}_{a_{0}gjk}(b_{0}).

Here
\widehat{t}=\widehat{t}(g,k,a_{0},b_{0};\boldsymbol{c})\bmod a_{0}k
which is independent of
j
and satisfies
\gcd(\widehat{t},a_{0}k)=1
. Thanks to
\textup{e}_{a_{0}gjk}(b_{0})=1+O\left(\frac{b_{0}}{a_{0}gjk}\right)
, we obtain further

(3.13)

\mathfrak{S}_{1}(\chi;X)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}g\mid{\mathscr{D}}\\
(g,a_{0})=1\end{subarray}}\mu^{2}(g)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\leqslant\sqrt{\frac{X}{g}}\\
(k,{\mathscr{D}}m\Omega)=1\end{subarray}}\chi(k)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\leqslant\frac{X}{gk}\\
(j,k{\mathscr{D}}m\Omega)=1\end{subarray}}\chi(j)\textup{e}_{a_{0}k}\left(\widehat{t}\cdot\overline{j}\right)+O_{\varepsilon}(b_{0}^{1+\varepsilon}(\log X)^{2}).

We set

q:=\lcm(|\chi|,a_{0}k),\quad s:=\prod_{p\mid m{\mathscr{D}}\Omega,p\nmid q}p,\quad t:=\frac{q}{a_{0}k}\widehat{t}.

Observe that

\gcd(q,t)=\frac{q}{a_{0}k}\leqslant|\chi|.

On applying Lemma
3.2
, there exists
\Theta(k;\chi)
depending also on
a_{0},b_{0}
such that the inner
j
-sum in (
3.13
) equals

\frac{X}{gk}\Theta(k;\chi)+O_{\varepsilon}\left((m{\mathscr{D}})^{\varepsilon}|\chi|^{\frac{11}{8}+\varepsilon}(a_{0}k)^{\frac{7}{8}+\varepsilon}\right),

whence

\mathfrak{S}_{1}(\chi;X)=X\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}g\mid{\mathscr{D}}\\
(g,a_{0})=1\end{subarray}}\frac{\mu^{2}(g)}{g}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\leqslant\sqrt{\frac{X}{g}}\\
(k,{\mathscr{D}}m\Omega)=1\end{subarray}}\lambda_{1}(k)+O_{\varepsilon}\left((m{\mathscr{D}})^{\varepsilon}|\chi|^{\frac{11}{8}+\varepsilon}a_{0}^{\frac{7}{8}+\varepsilon}X^{\frac{15}{16}+\varepsilon}+b_{0}^{1+\varepsilon}X^{\varepsilon}\right).

where

\lambda_{1}(k):=\frac{\chi(k)\Theta(k;\chi)}{k}.

Moreover, on observing

\gcd\left(t,\frac{q}{|\chi_{*}|}\right)\leqslant\min\left(\frac{q}{a_{0}k},\frac{q}{|\chi_{*}|}\right)\leqslant\frac{q}{|\chi_{*}|^{\frac{31}{32}}(a_{0}k)^{\frac{1}{32}}},

Lemma
3.2
also gives,

(3.14)

\Theta(k;\chi)\begin{cases}\leqslant\frac{1}{\varphi(k)}\ll_{\varepsilon}\frac{1}{k^{1-\varepsilon}}&\text{ if }\chi\text{ is principal};\\
\ll_{\varepsilon}\frac{|\chi|^{\varepsilon}}{|\chi_{*}|^{\frac{15}{32}}(a_{0}k)^{\frac{1}{32}-\varepsilon}}&\text{ if }\chi\text{ is not principal}.\end{cases}

Therefore

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\leqslant\sqrt{\frac{X}{g}}\\
(k,{\mathscr{D}}m\Omega)=1\end{subarray}}\lambda_{1}(k)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\in{\mathbb{N}}\\
(k,{\mathscr{D}}m\Omega)=1\end{subarray}}\lambda_{1}(k)+O_{\varepsilon}(|\chi|^{\varepsilon}g^{\frac{1}{64}}X^{-\frac{1}{64}+\varepsilon}).

So extending the
k
-sum to infinity results in
O_{\varepsilon}(({\mathscr{D}}|\chi|)^{\varepsilon}X^{\frac{63}{64}+\varepsilon})
. So, on inserting the error term in (
3.13
), we henceforth conclude that

(3.15)

\mathfrak{S}_{1}(\chi;X)=X\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}g\mid{\mathscr{D}}\\
(g,a_{0})=1\end{subarray}}\frac{\mu^{2}(g)}{g}\right)\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\in{\mathbb{N}}\\
(k,{\mathscr{D}}m\Omega)=1\end{subarray}}\lambda_{1}(k)\right)\\
+O_{\varepsilon}\left((m{\mathscr{D}})^{\varepsilon}|\chi|^{\frac{11}{8}+\varepsilon}a_{0}^{\frac{7}{8}+\varepsilon}X^{\frac{63}{64}+\varepsilon}+b_{0}^{1+\varepsilon}(\log X)^{2}\right).

As for
\mathfrak{S}_{2}(\chi;X)
, we just need to reverse the role of
j
and
k
, and we deduce that
\mathfrak{S}_{2}(\chi;X)
has the same asymptotic formula as (
3.15
).

Therefore we conclude the proof of Theorem
3.1
(2) on letting

(3.16)

\Gamma(\chi;l_{1},l_{2};\boldsymbol{c}):=2\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}g\mid{\mathscr{D}}\\
(g,a_{0})=1\end{subarray}}\frac{\mu^{2}(g)}{g}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\in{\mathbb{N}}\\
(k,{\mathscr{D}}m\Omega)=1\end{subarray}}\lambda_{1}(k).

Then by (
3.14
),

\Gamma(\chi;l_{1},l_{2};\boldsymbol{c})\ll_{\varepsilon}\frac{|\chi|^{\varepsilon}}{|\chi_{*}|^{\frac{15}{32}}}\Upsilon_{1}(2b_{0})\ll_{\varepsilon}\frac{(l_{2}|\boldsymbol{c}||\chi|)^{\varepsilon}}{|\chi_{*}|^{\frac{15}{32}}}\Upsilon_{1}(m)

as desired.
∎

4.
Sums of Salié sums

We specialise from now on to the case where
d=3
and
-m\Delta_{F}=\square
.

Recall (
2.3
). For
\boldsymbol{c}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{3}
and

, let us define

{\mathscr{F}}_{\boldsymbol{c}}(X):=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}q\leqslant X\end{subarray}}\frac{\textup{e}_{qL^{2}}(\boldsymbol{c}\cdot\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{m})\widehat{S}_{q}(\boldsymbol{c})}{q^{2}}.

Based on results established in §

, our goal is prove the following evaluation of “sums of Salié sums”.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
\boldsymbol{c}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{3}\setminus\boldsymbol{0}

(1)

Assume that

is not a perfect square. Then

{\mathscr{F}}_{\boldsymbol{c}}(X)\ll_{\varepsilon}|\boldsymbol{c}|^{3+\varepsilon}X\min\left(\Upsilon_{\frac{1}{4}}(m)(\log X)^{-\frac{1}{4}(1-\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2})+\varepsilon},(\log X)^{\frac{1}{4}(3+\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2})+\varepsilon}\right).

(2)

Assume that
-F^{*}(\boldsymbol{c})
is a perfect square (including

). Then

{\mathscr{F}}_{\boldsymbol{c}}(X)\begin{cases}&=\eta(\boldsymbol{c})X+O_{\varepsilon,\beta}\left((mX)^{\varepsilon}|\boldsymbol{c}|^{1+\varepsilon}(X^{\frac{43}{8}\beta}(X^{\frac{63}{64}}+m^{\frac{1}{2}})+X^{1-\frac{1}{32}\beta})\right);\\
&\ll_{\varepsilon}|\boldsymbol{c}|^{1+\varepsilon}X\left(\Upsilon_{\frac{31}{64}}(m)(\log X)\exp\left(-\frac{1}{64}(\log X)^{\frac{33}{64}}\right)+(\log X)^{\frac{1983}{1984}}\right).\end{cases}

for any fixed
0<\kappa_{1}<1
and
\beta>0
, where
\eta(\boldsymbol{c})\in{\mathbb{R}}
is defined in (
4.6
) (
4.11
) and satisfies
\eta(\boldsymbol{c})\ll\Upsilon_{\kappa_{0}}(m)

.

Remark
.

The exponents of these bounds, though being sufficient for the goal of the current article, are by no means optimal.

Proof of Theorem
4.1
.

4.1.

By Proposition
2.1

4.1.2.
Case where

Proposition 5.9

\displaystyle=-\int_{Q^{1-\delta}}^{cQ}A_{t}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left(\frac{\widehat{{\mathscr{I}}}^{*}_{\frac{t}{Q}}(w;\boldsymbol{0})}{t^{3}}\right)\operatorname{d}t+\left[A_{t}\frac{\widehat{{\mathscr{I}}}^{*}_{\frac{t}{Q}}(w;\boldsymbol{0})}{t^{3}}\right]_{Q^{1-\delta}}^{cQ}.

By Proposition
5.9
and the “trivial estimate” in Lemma
2.5
, the variation term equals

A_{Q^{1-\delta}}\frac{\widehat{{\mathscr{I}}}^{*}_{Q^{-\delta}}(w;\boldsymbol{0})}{Q^{3(1-\delta)}}=\frac{1}{3}L^{4}\widehat{\mathfrak{S}}({\mathscr{W}}_{m};L,\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{m})\widehat{{\mathscr{I}}}^{*}_{Q^{-\delta}}(w;\boldsymbol{0})+O_{\varepsilon}(Q^{-\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\delta+\varepsilon}).

Moreover, the term with integral equals

(5.10)

-\frac{1}{3}L^{4}\widehat{\mathfrak{S}}({\mathscr{W}}_{m};L,\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{m})\int_{Q^{1-\delta}}^{cQ}t^{3}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left(\frac{\widehat{{\mathscr{I}}}^{*}_{\frac{t}{Q}}(w;\boldsymbol{0})}{t^{3}}\right)\operatorname{d}t,

with error

\displaystyle\ll_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\int_{Q^{1-\delta}}^{cQ}t^{\frac{5}{2}+\varepsilon}\left|\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left(\frac{\widehat{{\mathscr{I}}}^{*}_{\frac{t}{Q}}(w;\boldsymbol{0})}{t^{3}}\right)\right|\operatorname{d}t\ll_{\varepsilon}Q^{-\frac{1}{2}+\frac{3}{2}\delta+\varepsilon},

by Lemma
2.5
.
Integration by parts yields that the integral in (
5.10
) equals

-\widehat{{\mathscr{I}}}^{*}_{Q^{-\delta}}(w;\boldsymbol{0})-3\int_{Q^{1-\delta}}^{cQ}\frac{\widehat{{\mathscr{I}}}^{*}_{\frac{t}{Q}}(w;\boldsymbol{0})}{t}\operatorname{d}t.

(Here we use the fact that
\widehat{{\mathscr{I}}}_{r}^{*}(w;\boldsymbol{c})
vanishes around
r=c
.)
We conclude that

\sum_{Q^{1-\delta}<q\leqslant cQ}{\mathscr{G}}^{*}(q;\boldsymbol{0})=L^{4}\widehat{\mathfrak{S}}({\mathscr{W}}_{m};L,\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{m})\int_{Q^{-\delta}}^{c}\frac{\widehat{{\mathscr{I}}}^{*}_{r}(w;\boldsymbol{0})}{r}\operatorname{d}r+O_{\varepsilon}(Q^{-\frac{1}{2}+\frac{3}{2}\delta+\varepsilon}),

once the usual change of variable
t\mapsto r:=\frac{t}{Q}
is executed. We now use (the first equality of) Lemma
2.6
to compute

\int_{Q^{-\delta}}^{c}\frac{\widehat{{\mathscr{I}}}^{*}_{r}(w;\boldsymbol{0})}{r}\operatorname{d}r=\int_{Q^{-\delta}}^{c}\frac{\widehat{{\mathscr{I}}}_{r}(w;\boldsymbol{0})}{r}\operatorname{d}r+O(B^{-\theta+2\delta}).

Let us consider the function
K:{\mathbb{R}}_{>0}\to{\mathbb{C}}
by

K(v):={\mathscr{I}}(w)\log v+\int_{v}^{c}\frac{\widehat{{\mathscr{I}}}_{r}(w;\boldsymbol{0})}{r}\operatorname{d}r.

Then the argument in
[
11
, p. 202–203]
(an application of
[
11
, Lemma 13]
) shows that

K(0):=\lim_{v\to 0^{+}}K(v)

exists and as
v\to 0^{+}
,

K(v)=K(0)+O_{N}(v^{N}).

Consequently,

\int_{Q^{-\delta}}^{c}\frac{\widehat{{\mathscr{I}}}_{r}(w;\boldsymbol{0})}{r}\operatorname{d}r=\delta{\mathscr{I}}(w)\log Q+K(0)+O_{N}(Q^{-N\delta}).

So gathering together all the estimates we obtained so far gives

\displaystyle\sum_{q\ll Q}{\mathscr{G}}(q;\boldsymbol{0})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathfrak{S}}({\mathscr{W}}_{m};L,\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{m})\left({\mathscr{I}}(w)\left(L^{4}\log Q+\gamma\right)+L^{4}K(0)\right)+O_{\varepsilon}(B^{-\frac{1}{2}+\frac{3}{2}\delta+\varepsilon})+O_{N}(B^{-N\delta})

\displaystyle L^{4}\left(\widehat{\mathfrak{S}}({\mathscr{W}}_{m};L,\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{m})\left({\mathscr{I}}(w)\log B+{\mathscr{I}}(w)\left(L^{-4}\gamma-\log L\right)+K(0)\right)\right)+O_{\varepsilon}(B^{-\frac{1}{2}+\varepsilon}),

on first taking
N

\delta
, then on taking
\delta
arbitrarily small. We conclude the proof of Theorem
5.8
with

(5.11)

which is clearly
\ll\widehat{\mathfrak{S}}({\mathscr{W}}_{m};L,\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{m})
.
∎

Appendix A
Interpretation via Chambert-Loir–Tschinkel

In
[
2
]
, Chambert-Loir and Tschinkel initiate a programme on counting integral points on projective varieties along with an interpretation of the asymptotic formulas.

Let us place ourselves in the simplest setting which covers the affine surface that we study before.
Let
X
be a smooth projective split variety over
{\mathbb{Q}}
. Let
D\subset X
be an effective, geometrically integral divisor such that the log-anticanonical line bundle
\omega_{X}(D)^{-1}
is ample, and let
H
be a height function associated to
\omega_{X}(D)^{-1}
. Let
{\mathscr{U}}
be a flat integral model of the open subset
U:=X\setminus D
. After
[
2
]
, we expect that, for
{\mathscr{A}}\subset{\mathscr{U}}(\widehat{{\mathbb{Z}}})
a non-empty adelic open subset,

\#\{P\in{\mathscr{A}}\cap X({\mathbb{Q}}):H(P)\leqslant B\}\sim\alpha(D)\tau_{\infty}(D)\tau_{f}({\mathscr{A}})B(\log B)^{\operatorname{rank}\operatorname{Pic}(X)-1},

where

\alpha(D):=\frac{1}{(\operatorname{rank}\operatorname{Pic}(X)-1)!}\int_{\mathscr{C}(X)^{\vee}}\exp\left(-\langle\omega_{X}(D)^{-1},\boldsymbol{y}\rangle\right)\operatorname{d}\boldsymbol{y},

is analogous to Peyre’s
\alpha
-constant (which roughly speaking computes a certain weighted volume of the dual of the pseudo-effective cone
\mathscr{C}(X)
),

\tau_{\infty}(D)
is certain real Tamagawa volume of
D({\mathbb{R}})
defined in terms of the “residue measure” induced by
\omega_{X}(D)^{-1}
, and

\tau_{f}:=\prod_{p<\infty}\left(1-\frac{1}{p}\right)^{\operatorname{rank}\operatorname{Pic}(X)-1}\tau_{(X,D),p},

is a finite Tamagawa measure on
{\mathscr{U}}(\widehat{{\mathbb{Z}}})
. The key reason why we should only see
D({\mathbb{R}})
in the real part is that integral points of
{\mathscr{U}}
, or rational points of
X
that are integral with respect to
D
, accumulate arbitrarily close to the boundary
D
as the height grows. So the appearance of certain volume of
D({\mathbb{R}})
is the “limit” of such a behaviour.

In our case, the height is associated to the naive metric, and the surface
W_{m}
can be compactified into the split quadric surface
X_{m}:={\mathbb{P}}^{1}\times{\mathbb{P}}^{1}\subset{\mathbb{P}}^{3}
by adding the boundary divisor
D:(F=0)
, a conic isomorphic to
{\mathbb{P}}^{1}
as a
{\mathscr{O}}(1,1)
-section of
{\mathbb{P}}^{1}\times{\mathbb{P}}^{1}
. We have
\operatorname{rank}\operatorname{Pic}(X_{m})=2
,
\omega_{X_{m}}(D)^{-1}\simeq{\mathscr{O}}(1,1)
,
\alpha(D)=1
, and
\tau_{(X_{m},D),p}
coincides with the induced Tamagawa measure on
W_{m}({\mathbb{Q}}_{p})
. Finally,
\tau_{\infty}=2\times\tau_{D,\infty}
on
D({\mathbb{R}})
, where the real measure
\tau_{D,\infty}
is induced by
{\mathscr{O}}_{{\mathbb{P}}^{1}}(2)
. The discussion in
[
14
, Remark 6.3]
shows that the singular integral
{\mathscr{I}}(w)
(
1.1
) is the “weighted” version of the real volume
\tau_{\infty}(D({\mathbb{R}}))
.
