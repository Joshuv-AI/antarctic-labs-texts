Matrix completion via residual spectral matching
Ziyuan Chen, Fang Yao

Abstract
Noisy matrix completion has attracted significant attention due to its applications in recommendation systems, signal processing and image restoration. Most existing works rely on (weighted) least squares methods under various low-rank constraints. However, minimizing the sum of squared residuals is not always efficient, as it may ignore the potential structural information in the residuals. In this study, we propose a novel residual spectral matching criterion that incorporates not only the numerical but also locational information of residuals. This criterion is the first in noisy matrix completion to adopt the perspective of low-rank perturbation of random matrices and exploit the spectral properties of sparse random matrices. We derive optimal statistical properties by analyzing the spectral properties of sparse random matrices and bounding the effects of low-rank perturbations and partial observations. Additionally, we propose algorithms that efficiently approximate solutions by constructing easily computable pseudo-gradients. The iterative process of the proposed algorithms ensures convergence at a rate consistent with the optimal statistical error bound. Our method and algorithms demonstrate improved numerical performance in both simulated and real data examples, particularly in environments with high noise levels.

1
Introduction

1.1
Literature review

Matrix completion aims to reconstruct the target matrix
\bm{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
from partial observations of its entries that are corrupted by random noise, which has applications across different fields.
For example, it has been utilized in recommendation systems
(
Jannach et al., 2016
;
Ramlatchan et al., 2018
)
, signal processing
(
Weng and Wang, 2012
;
Li et al., 2019
)
, and image restoration
(
Ji et al., 2011
;
Jia et al., 2022
)
.
Recently, matrix completion based statistical models have also been applied to various problems, including wireless sensor networks
(
Xie et al., 2016
;
Kortas et al., 2021
)
, causal inference
(
Kallus, Mao and Udell, 2018
;
Athey et al., 2021
)
, and policy evaluation
(
Iweze, 2020
;
Duan, Li and Xia, 2024
)
.

In the matrix completion model, the sample size is often significantly smaller than the matrix dimensions
m\times n
. Completing the target matrix
\bm{M}
is generally impossible without specific structures and properties. The mostly considered property for the underlying matrix
\bm{M}
is a low-rank structure or related characteristics, such as the rapid decay of singular values. A common approach to addressing the low-rank matrix completion problem is to impose strict low-rank constraints through specific matrix factorization techniques. For instance,
Keshavan, Montanari and Oh (2009)
introduced a method that employs thin singular value decomposition. The typical method for constraining the rank of matrix
\bm{M}
to be less than
s
represents it as
\bm{M}=\bm{L}\bm{R}^{\prime}
, where
\bm{L}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times s}
and
\bm{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times s}
. Although this low-rank representation lacks identifiability, extensive research has examined the properties of least squares methods utilizing this matrix factorization with alternative minimizing schemes. This includes the incorporation of regularization terms
(
Sun and Luo, 2016
;
Ge, Lee and Ma, 2016
)
and algorithmic analyses
(
Jain, Netrapalli and Sanghavi, 2013
;
Hardt, 2014
;
Zheng and Lafferty, 2016
;
Ma et al., 2018
;
Chen, Liu and Li, 2020
)
. Following the findings of
Candès and Recht (2009)
, another prevalent method has emerged. They demonstrated that exact matrix completion is achievable in the absence of random noise, by relaxing the strict rank constraint to a nuclear norm constraint. Consequently, the nuclear norm constraint is widely used in both practical applications and theoretical analyses due to its convex properties. Various least squares estimators based on the nuclear norm
(
Candes and Plan, 2010
;
Rohde and Tsybakov, 2011
;
Negahban and Wainwright, 2011
)
and their variants
(
Koltchinskii, Lounici and Tsybakov, 2011
;
Cai and Zhang, 2015
;
Cai and Zhou, 2016
)
have been thoroughly investigated.

Recent studies have also explored various extensions to traditional noisy matrix completion settings. Works such as
Klopp (2014)
and
Bi et al. (2017)
extended the missing data mechanism from “missing completely at random” to “missing at random”.
Later, the missing mechanism was further extended to “missing not at random”
(
Bhattacharya and Chatterjee, 2022
;
Choi and Yuan, 2023
)
.
Chen et al. (2019)
and
Xia and Yuan (2021)
investigated both completion and statistical inference problems.
Gui, Barber and Ma (2023)
later combined conformal inference techniques with matrix completion. Using additional covariate information,
Mao, Chen and Wong (2019)
and
Jin, Ma and Jiang (2022)
proposed a column space decomposition model.
Ma et al. (2024)
also studied the inference problem related to this model.
Xu and Davenport (2016)
and
Chen, Yang and Yao (2024)
introduced dynamic matrix estimators designed for time-varying target matrices. In the absence of low-rank structure, high rank matrix completion
(
Eriksson, Balzano and Nowak, 2012
;
Elhamifar, 2016
;
Ongie et al., 2017
)
and nonlinear variants
(
Fan and Chow, 2017
;
Fan and Cheng, 2018
)
have also been extensively studied. Additionally, tensor completion has gained popularity due to the development of its efficient algorithms
(
Zhang et al., 2020
;
Luo and Zhang, 2023
)
.

1.2
Different perspective from random matrix theory

For the noisy matrix completion problem and its variants, previous works typically treats the noisy matrix completion model as data sampled from the target matrix
\bm{M}
with random noise. The commonly used criterion for evaluating performance in noisy matrix completion is the (weighted) squared error. Least squares methods are often employed in statistical models involving noise, and the resulting estimators generally exhibit good performance. However, the rank structure of matrices relates to both the values and locations of entries, indicating that random noises possesses both numerical and locational characteristics in the noisy matrix completion problem. Applying least squares methods directly overlooks the locational properties of residuals and may not be efficient, especially in high-noise scenarios.

In contrast, we approach the noisy matrix completion problem from a different perspective.
We model the data as samples from a low-rank perturbation
\bm{M}
of a random matrix
\bm{H}
consisting of random noise. This perspective motivates us to propose a criterion based on the similarity between the residual matrix and sparse random matrices. Inspired by the fact that singular values capture both numerical and positional information about entries in matrices, we measure the similarity by comparing the distribution of singular values between the residual matrix and sparse random matrices.
The properties of singular values of mean zero rectangular random matrices
\bm{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
with
\mathbb{E}[\bm{X}^{2}_{i,j}]=1
, are directly linked to the empirical spectral distribution (ESD) of sample covariance matrices
\bm{S}=\bm{X}\bm{X}^{\prime}/m
. This ESD converges to the Marchenko-Pastur (M-P) distribution as
m\rightarrow\infty
with the ratio
n/m\rightarrow\rho>0

(
Yin and Krishnaiah, 1986
;
Bai and Yin, 1988
)
.
Extensive research has examined the convergence rate to the M-P distribution
(
Götze and Tikhomirov, 2004
;
Tikhomirov, 2011
;
Bai, Hu and Zhou, 2012
)
, local convergence in various settings
(
Bourgade, Yau and Yin, 2014
;
Alt, Erdos and Krüger, 2017
;
Bao, Erdős and Schnelli, 2017
)
and the universality property
(
Erdos, Yau and Yin, 2010
;
Erdős, Yau and Yin, 2012
;
Tao and Vu, 2012
;
Pillai and Yin, 2014
)
.

Benaych-Georges and Nadakuditi (2012)
provided the limit values of leading singular values and vectors in low-rank perturbations of random matrices. Subsequent studies have explored additional properties, such as the limit distribution of leading singular values
(
Veraart et al., 2016
;
Bao, Ding and Wang, 2021
;
Bao et al., 2022
)
. The tail distributions of entries in classical random matrix theories are often constrained by bounded high-order moment conditions.
Sparse random matrices, where each entry is multiplied by a random variable following a binomial distribution with probability
p_{i,j}\rightarrow 0
, do not satisfy these conditions.
For example, the entries in Gaussian sparse random matrices
\bm{X}
with
\mathbb{E}[\bm{X}^{2}_{i,j}]=1
have a divergent fourth moment
\mathbb{E}[\bm{X}^{4}_{i,j}]=3p_{i,j}^{-1}
.
Recently, the spectral properties of sparse random matrix have attracted much attention. For example, the local law for sparse sample covariance matrices has been established
(
Lee, 2021
;
Tikhomirov and Timushev, 2022
;
Kafetzopoulos and Maltsev, 2023
)
. The universality property of these matrices is also discussed in several studies
(
Ding and Yang, 2018
;
He, 2023
;
Brailovskaya and van Handel, 2024
)
.

1.3
Our contributions and paper organization

In this paper, we propose a novel residual spectral matching criterion
for noisy matrix completion. We adopt the perspective of low-rank perturbation of random matrices and leverage the properties of sparse random noise matrices to design this effective criterion. The criterion incorporates both the locational and numerical information of residuals, unlike the Frobenius norm loss which considers only the numerical aspect. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first approach that accounts for the location of residuals in noisy matrix completion.
The proposed criterion is formulated as the weighted
L_{2}
distance between the ordered singular values of the residual matrix and the expected singular values of variance-adjusted sparse Gaussian random matrices.
Adjusting the spectral distribution of the residual matrix to resemble that of sparse random matrices provides more structural information than merely minimizing the Frobenius norm of the residual matrix.
Our research demonstrates both optimal theoretical properties and improved numerical performance for this criterion, especially at a high noise level.

The main contributions of our study are four-fold. First, a new residual spectral matching criterion is proposed for noisy matrix completion. This criterion is user-friendly and adaptable to various low-rank constraints. Second, the statistical properties of estimators under both strict low-rank and relaxed nuclear norm constraints are provided. We develop an analytical framework that leverages techniques from low-rank perturbation, random missing data, and sparse random matrices. Oracle bounds are presented, and optimal upper bounds are achieved by selecting the appropriate rank constraint and tuning hyper-parameter for the nuclear norm constraint. Third, a general iterative algorithm for minimizing the proposed criterion is presented. An alternating descent scheme and a pseudo-gradient formula are introduced to handle the non-convexity of the criterion and the challenge of formulating explicit gradients. Finally, the properties of algorithm iterations are carefully analysed. We demonstrate that our algorithm achieves the optimal error bound within a finite number of iterations by monitoring both the incoherence condition and the distance between iterative series and the target matrix.

The structure of the paper is as follows. Section
2
provides a brief introduction of the noisy matrix completion problem, and discusses the success of the low-rank perturbation viewpoint and the random matrix theory in the matrix denoising model, a special case of noisy matrix completion. In Section
3
, we present the spectral matching criterion, estimation methods and the corresponding algorithm. Section
4
establishes the optimal statistical properties of the proposed estimators and the error analysis for the iterative series in the proposed algorithm. In Section
5
, both simulation and real data experiments demonstrate the favorable performance of the proposed methods over commonly used Frobenius norm methods, particularly when dealing with high noise levels. Conclusions and additional issues are discussed in Section
6
. Proof sketches and algorithms are presented in the Appendix, while additional technical lemmas and detailed proofs are provided to an online Supplementary Material for space economy.

2
Preliminary and Motivation

2.1
Settings for noisy matrix completion and notations

We first introduce some notations to be used throughout the paper. For any matrix
\bm{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
, the following norms are defined:
\|\bm{M}\|
for the spectral norm (the largest singular value),
\|\bm{M}\|_{F}
for the Frobenius norm (the square root of the sum of squared entries),
\|\bm{M}\|_{*}
for the nuclear norm (the sum of singular values),
\|\bm{M}\|_{\infty}
for the maximum norm (the largest absolute value of entries), and
\|\bm{M}\|_{2,\infty}
for the largest
L_{2}
norm of the rows (sometimes named as the
L_{2}/L_{\infty}
norm
(
Ma et al., 2018
)
). The transpose of
\bm{M}
is denoted by
\bm{M}^{\prime}
, and
\operatorname{Proj}_{s}(\bm{M})
indicates the projection of
\bm{M}
onto the space of matrices with rank no more than
s
. We use the notation
\bm{M}_{[1:s]}
to denote the matrix formed by the first
s
columns of
\bm{M}
, and
(a)_{+}
to represent the positive part of
a
. Additionally, the notations
a(m)\lesssim b(m)
means that there exists a constant
c>0
such that
|a(m)|\leq c|b(m)|
and similarly
a(m)\gtrsim b(m)
means that
|a(m)|\geq c|b(m)|
.

Noisy matrix completion involves recovering the target matrix
\bm{M}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n},n/m=\rho\in(0,1]
from partial observations of its noisy perturbations. Formally speaking, it aims to estimate
\bm{M}_{0}
from the observation
Y\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}

\displaystyle\bm{Y}=P_{\bm{\Omega}}(\bm{M}_{0}+\bm{H}),

where
\bm{\Omega}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
is a binary matrix with ones indexing the locations of observed entries, and
\bm{H}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
is an unknown noise matrix that is independent of
\bm{M}_{0}
. The projection
P_{\bm{\Omega}}(\cdot)
preserves the observed entries in
\bm{\Omega}
and assigns zeros to the unobserved entries.

In previous related works,
\bm{\Omega}_{i,j},1\leq i\leq m,1\leq j\leq n
are often assumed to follow independent binomial distributions with probability
p
and
H_{i,j},1\leq i\leq m,1\leq j\leq n
are independent mean zero random variables with variance
\sigma^{2}
. To separate the target matrix
\bm{M}_{0}
from the random noise
\bm{H}
, a key assumption is that
\bm{M}_{0}
has a low rank
r
which is far less than
n
. The incoherent condition defined in Definition
2.1
is also necessary for
\bm{M}_{0}
, to ensure that the missing pattern
\bm{\Omega}
does not completely disrupt the singular subspace information of
\bm{M}_{0}
.

Definition 2.1
.

A rank-
r
matrix
\bm{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
is said to be
\mu
-incoherent if

\displaystyle\|\bm{U}\|_{2,\infty}\leq\sqrt{\frac{\mu r}{m}},\quad\|\bm{V}\|_{2,\infty}\leq\sqrt{\frac{\mu r}{n}},

where
\bm{M}=\bm{U}\bm{D}\bm{V}^{\prime}
is the singular value decomposition.

This incoherent condition of a low-rank matrix
\bm{M}
implies the delocalization of its singular vectors, ensuring that each entry of
\bm{M}
contributes similarly important information to the matrix as a whole. This condition, in turn, provides favorable properties for projection operator
P_{\bm{\Omega}}(\cdot)
onto sparse observations.
Specifically, the restricted isometry property (RIP)
(1-\delta)\|\bm{M}\|_{F}^{2}\leq\|p^{-1/2}P_{\bm{\Omega}}(\bm{M})\|_{F}^{2}\leq(1+\delta)\|\bm{M}\|_{F}^{2}
holds for
P_{\bm{\Omega}}(\cdot)
with high probability, where
\delta\geq 0
is sufficiently small.

Two mainstream methods and their variations have been proposed and extensively studied for noisy matrix completion. The first type is mostly based on the low-rank factorization
\bm{M}=\bm{L}\bm{R}^{\prime},\bm{L}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times s},\bm{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times s}
of a rank
s
matrix
\bm{M}
. The matrix
\bm{M}_{0}
is then estimated by

\displaystyle\widehat{\bm{M}}_{\text{fac}}=\underset{\bm{M}=\bm{L}\bm{R}^{\prime},\bm{L}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times s},\bm{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times s}}{\arg\min}\|\bm{Y}-P_{\bm{\Omega}}(\bm{L}\bm{R}^{\prime})\|_{F}^{2}.

(1)

Several variations add different regularization terms, such as the Frobenius norm penalty
\lambda(\|\bm{L}\|_{F}^{2}+\|\bm{R}\|_{F}^{2})
or the balanced penalty
\lambda\|\bm{L}^{\prime}\bm{L}-\bm{R}^{\prime}\bm{R}\|_{F}^{2}
. The second type constrains the rank of the estimator by controlling its nuclear norm, i.e.,

\displaystyle\widehat{\bm{M}}_{\text{nuc}}=\underset{\bm{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}}{\arg\min}\|\bm{Y}-P_{\bm{\Omega}}(\bm{M})\|_{F}^{2}+\lambda\|\bm{M}\|_{*}.

(2)

In the absence of noise matrix
\bm{H}
, Theorem 1.7 in
Candès and Tao (2010)
shows that the lower bound for
p
to achieve exact matrix completion with high probability
1-\delta
is
p\gtrsim\mu_{0}rm^{-1}\log(m/2\delta)
. For sufficiently large
p
, the lower bound for the Frobenius norm distance between
\bm{M}_{0}
and its estimator
\widehat{\bm{M}}
is
\|\widehat{\bm{M}}-\bm{M}_{0}\|_{F}\gtrsim\sigma\sqrt{mr/p}
. Both estimators mentioned above have been proven to achieve the optimal or near-optimal (up to a logarithmic factor
\log m
) upper bounds. These results can be found in methods based on matrix factorization
(
Keshavan, Montanari and Oh, 2009
;
Ma et al., 2018
;
Chen et al., 2020
, e.g.,)
and for convex relaxed methods
(
Koltchinskii, Lounici and Tsybakov, 2011
;
Klopp, 2014
, e.g.,)
.

2.2
A motivating example: matrix denoising

The matrix denoising problem aims to separate the underlying signal matrix
\bm{M}_{0}
from its noisy version
\bm{M}_{0}+\bm{H}
. In fact, the model can be viewed as a special case of matrix completion with
p=1
, where
\bm{Y}=\bm{M}_{0}+\bm{H}
is fully observed. When we apply the estimation methods (
1
) and (
2
) in parallel to the matrix denoising problem, two estimators for
\bm{M}_{0}
are obtained respectively:

\displaystyle\widecheck{\bm{M}}_{\text{fac}}=\sum_{i=1}^{s}\sigma_{i}\bm{u}_{i}\bm{v}_{i}^{\prime},\quad\widecheck{\bm{M}}_{\text{nuc}}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\sigma_{i}-\lambda/2)_{+}\bm{u}_{i}\bm{v}_{i}^{\prime},

where
\bm{Y}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sigma_{i}\bm{u}_{i}\bm{v}_{i}^{\prime}
is the singular value decomposition of
\bm{Y}
.
While both estimators achieve optimal upper bounds that match the problem’s lower bound
\sigma\sqrt{rm}
, we shall demonstrate that neither performs the best in matrix denoising compared to the estimator based on random matrix theory. And the performance gap widens as the noise level rises.

Rather than viewing the matrix denoising model as a target matrix contaminated by random noise, we consider the model as a random matrix
\bm{H}
perturbed by a low-rank matrix
\bm{M}_{0}
. Classical random matrix theory
(
Bai and Silverstein, 2010
)
shows that the empirical spectral distribution of
\bm{H}^{\prime}\bm{H}/m
converges to the Marchenko-Pastur distribution. Consequently, the density of the singular values of
\bm{H}/\sqrt{m}
converges to

\displaystyle\nu(x)=\frac{1}{\pi\sigma^{2}}\frac{\sqrt{(\lambda^{2}_{+}-x^{2})(x^{2}-\lambda^{2}_{-})}}{\rho x}\mathbf{1}_{x\in[\lambda_{-},\lambda_{+}]},\quad\lambda_{\pm}=\sigma(1\pm\sqrt{\rho}).

The smallest
s
singular values of
\bm{H}
are found to be larger than
\sqrt{m}\lambda_{-}
with high probability. Taking
\widecheck{\bm{M}}_{\text{fac}}
as an example,
\widecheck{\bm{H}}=\bm{Y}-\widecheck{\bm{M}}_{\text{fac}}
can intuitively be viewed as an estimator of the random noise matrix
\bm{H}
. However, the
s
smallest singular values of
\widecheck{\bm{H}}
are zero, which differs from those of the random noise matrix
\bm{H}
. This discrepancy indicates that
\widecheck{\bm{M}}_{\text{fac}}
may not be adequate.
Benaych-Georges and Nadakuditi (2012)
derived the limit distribution of singular values of random matrices with low-rank perturbations. It helps us to construct the estimator
\widecheck{\bm{M}}_{\text{rmt}}=\sum_{i=1}^{s}\widetilde{\sigma}_{i}\bm{u}_{i}\bm{v}_{i}^{\prime}
, where

\displaystyle\widetilde{\sigma}_{i}=\left(\frac{1}{2}\left(\left(\sigma_{i}^{2}-\widehat{\sigma}^{2}(1+\rho)m\right)+\sqrt{\left(\widehat{\sigma}^{2}(1+\rho)m-\sigma_{i}^{2}\right)^{2}-4\widehat{\sigma}^{4}\rho m^{2}}\right)\right)^{1/2},

and
\widehat{\sigma}
is a
\sqrt{m}
-consistency estimator of
\sigma
. To verify our motivation, We conduct a numerical study to compare the performance of the estimator
\widecheck{\bm{M}}_{\text{rmt}}
with
\widecheck{\bm{M}}_{\text{fac}}
and
\widecheck{\bm{M}}_{\text{nuc}}
in Table
1
, which shows the outstanding performance of the estimator
\widecheck{\bm{M}}_{\text{rmt}}
based on random matrix theory, and the increasing performance gap for larger
\sigma
.

While random matrix theory has achieved significant success in matrix denoising models, its application to noisy matrix completion remains underexplored.
The strong performance of the estimator
\widecheck{\bm{M}}_{\text{rmt}}
in matrix denoising prompted us to adopt the perspective of low-rank perturbations of random matrices. In the noisy matrix completion problem, we treat the observation
\bm{Y}=P_{\bm{\Omega}}(\bm{M}_{0}+\bm{H})
as a perturbation of the sparse random matrix
P_{\bm{\Omega}}(\bm{H})
. Thus, finding an optimal estimator
\hat{\bm{M}}
of
\bm{M}_{0}
corresponds to approximating
P_{\bm{\Omega}}(\bm{H})
through the residual matrix
\bm{Y}-P_{\bm{\Omega}}(\hat{M})
. Unlike matrix denoising, there is no closed-form solution for
\hat{M}
in noisy matrix completion. Due to limitation in sample size, approximating each entry of
P_{\bm{\Omega}}(\bm{H})
is challenging. We leverage the spectral distributions of sparse random matrices, which are stable and converge rapidly to their limit distributions. By using the distance between spectral distributions as a criterion, we can assess whether the residual matrix belongs to the high probability set of sparse random matrices. This approach offers a novel perspective on the noisy matrix completion problem.

3
Methodology and algorithm

3.1
Proposed method

A sparse Gaussian random matrix of size
m\times n
and missing probability
1-p_{i,j}\geq 0,1\leq i\leq m,1\leq j\leq n
can be formulated as

\displaystyle\bm{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n},\quad\bm{M}_{i,j}=\bm{\Phi}_{i,j}\bm{\Omega}_{i,j},1\leq i\leq m,1\leq j\leq n,

where
\bm{\Phi}_{i,j}
are independent Gaussian random variables with mean zero and variance
1/m
, and
\bm{\Omega}_{i,j}
follow independent binomial distributions with success probability
p_{i,j}
.
Define the probability space
\mathcal{S}_{m,n,p}=\left(\mathbb{R}^{m\times n},\mathcal{B}_{m,n},\mathcal{P}_{m,n,p}\right)
, where
\mathcal{B}_{m,n}
is the Borel
\sigma
-algebra of
\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
, and the probability measure
\mathcal{P}_{m,n,p}
has the density function

\displaystyle f_{m,n,p}(\bm{M})=\prod_{i=1}^{m}\prod_{j=1}^{n}\left[p_{i,j}\phi\left(\sqrt{m}\bm{M}_{i,j}\right)+(1-p_{i,j})\delta(\bm{M}_{i,j})\right],

where
\phi(x)
is the density function of standard Gaussian distribution, and
\delta(x)
is the Dirac delta function.
We denote the singular values of
\bm{M}
as
\lambda_{1}(\bm{M})\geq\lambda_{2}(\bm{M})\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_{n}(\bm{M})
and combine them into a vector
\bm{\lambda}(\bm{M})
.
In the probability space
\mathcal{S}_{m,n,p}
, we define the expectation of the random vector
\bm{\lambda}(\bm{M})
as

\bm{\lambda}=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{M}}\bm{\lambda}(\bm{M})
. Although deriving the explicit formulation of
\bm{\lambda}
is challenging, it can be estimated using a simple Monte Carlo procedure
\widehat{\bm{\lambda}}=\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\bm{\lambda}(\bm{M}_{i})/\ell
where
\bm{M}_{i},1\leq i\leq\ell
are independent sparse Gaussian random matrices. The entries of
\widehat{\bm{\lambda}}
are denoted as
\widehat{\lambda}_{i},1\leq i\leq n
.

We then construct a criterion
l(\bm{N};\bm{\omega})
to evaluate whether a matrix
\bm{N}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
belongs to a high probability set
in
\mathcal{S}_{m,n,p}
. Let the ordered singular values of
\bm{N}
be denoted as
\sigma_{1}(\bm{N})\geq\sigma_{2}(\bm{N})\geq\cdots\geq\sigma_{n}(\bm{N})
, and define the vector
\bm{\sigma}(\bm{N})=(\sigma_{1}(\bm{N}),\cdots,\sigma_{n}(\bm{N}))
. The criterion calculates the weighted
L_{2}
distance between
\bm{\sigma}(\bm{N})
and
\widehat{\bm{\lambda}}
, i.e.,

l(\bm{N};\bm{\omega})=\left\|\bm{\sigma}(\bm{N})-\widehat{\bm{\lambda}}\right\|_{\bm{\omega}}^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\omega_{i}\left(\sigma_{i}(\bm{N})-\widehat{\lambda}_{i}\right)^{2},

where
\bm{\omega}=(\omega_{1},\cdots,\omega_{n})
is the weight satisfying
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\omega_{i}=1
.

For noisy matrix completion, any matrix
\bm{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
close to
\bm{M}_{0}
will result in the residual matrix

\bm{Y}-P_{\bm{\Omega}}(\bm{M})=P_{\bm{\Omega}}(\bm{M}_{0}-\bm{M})+P_{\bm{\Omega}}(\bm{H})

being close to
P_{\bm{\Omega}}(\bm{H})
. The latter is located in a high probability set of
\mathcal{S}_{m,n,p}
after variance adjustment.
Therefore, we propose the following method to find an estimator
\widetilde{\bm{M}}\in\mathbb{M}
for
\bm{M}_{0}
as

\displaystyle\widetilde{\bm{M}}=\underset{\bm{M}\in\mathbb{M}}{\arg\min}\ l\left(\frac{\bm{Y}-P_{\bm{\Omega}}(\bm{M})}{\sqrt{m}\widehat{\sigma}(\bm{M})};\bm{\omega}\right).

(3)

Here
\widehat{\sigma}(\bm{M})
is the estimated working standard deviation, defined as

\displaystyle\widehat{\sigma}(\bm{M})=\frac{\sum_{i=n/3}^{i=2n/3}\sigma_{i}\left(\bm{Y}-P_{\bm{\Omega}}(\bm{M})\right)}{\sqrt{m}\sum_{i=n/3}^{2n/3}\lambda_{i}},

(4)

where
\sigma_{i}\left(\bm{Y}-P_{\bm{\Omega}}(\bm{M})\right),1\leq i\leq n
are the ordered singular values of
\bm{Y}-P_{\bm{\Omega}}(\bm{M})
. The working estimator
\widehat{\sigma}(\bm{M})
approximates the standard deviation
\sigma
of the noise by calculating the proportion of the bulk singular values’ sum from the residual matrix and sparse random matrices. This approach is efficient because the perturbation
P_{\bm{\Omega}}(\bm{M}_{0}-\bm{M})
has less impart on the bulk singular values than on the edge singular values of
P_{\bm{\Omega}}(\bm{H})
.

When the estimation space
\mathbb{M}
is chosen as
\mathbb{M}_{s}=\left\{\bm{M}\ |\ \bm{M}=\bm{L}\bm{R}^{\prime},\bm{L}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times s},\bm{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times s}\right\}
, it results in the matrix factorization based estimator
\widetilde{\bm{M}}_{\text{fac}}

\displaystyle\widetilde{\bm{M}}_{\text{fac}}=\underset{\bm{M}=\bm{L}\bm{R}^{\prime},\bm{L}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times s},\bm{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times s}}{\arg\min}l\left(\frac{\bm{Y}-P_{\bm{\Omega}}(\bm{L}\bm{R}^{\prime})}{\sqrt{m}\widehat{\sigma}(\bm{L}\bm{R}^{\prime})};\bm{\omega}\right).

(5)

If the space
\mathbb{M}
is defined as
\mathbb{M}_{C}=\left\{\bm{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}\ |\ \|\bm{M}\|_{*}\leq C\right\}
, we equivalently obtain the convex relaxed nuclear norm estimator
\widetilde{\bm{M}}_{\text{nuc}}

\displaystyle\widetilde{\bm{M}}_{\text{nuc}}=\underset{M\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}}{\arg\min}\ l\left(\frac{\bm{Y}-P_{\bm{\Omega}}(\bm{M})}{\sqrt{m}\widehat{\sigma}(\bm{M})};\bm{\omega}\right)+\lambda\|\bm{M}\|_{*}.

(6)

It is notable that our proposal, unlike the Frobenius norm based methods, incorporates the spectral properties of the noise matrix rather than solely minimizing the Frobenius norm of the residual matrix. In many statistical problems, random noise lacks meaningful structural information, and minimizing the sum of squared errors often yields optimal results. However, in models involving a fixed low-rank matrix plus a random noise matrix, the low-rank matrix perturbs only a few dominant singular values of the random matrix significantly. Thus, the spectral distribution of the residual matrix in noisy matrix completion can provide additional insights. Particularly, in scenarios where the Signal-to-Noise Ratio (SNR)
\|\bm{M}_{0}\|_{F}/\|\bm{H}\|_{F}
is low, disregarding the information encoded in
\bm{H}
and merely reducing the norm of the residual matrix may result in a loss of efficiency.

3.2
Optimal algorithm

Minimizing the term
\|\bm{Y}-P_{\bm{\Omega}}(\bm{M})\|_{F}^{2}
in equations (
1
) and (
2
) is straightforward due to its strong convexity. However, in our proposed method, minimizing
l((\bm{Y}-P_{\bm{\Omega}}(\bm{M}))/\sqrt{m}\widehat{\sigma}(\bm{M});\bm{\omega})
is more challenging because it is non-convex and lacks of a computable gradient. To address this, we have developed efficient algorithms and their convergence properties will be demonstrated in the next section.

In our algorithms,
\bm{M}
and
\widehat{\sigma}(\bm{M})
are updated alternatively in each iteration step.
When
p_{i,j}=1
,
P_{\bm{\Omega}}(\bm{M})=\bm{M}
and the gradient for
\bm{M}
of can be directly calculated as

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{M}}l\left(\frac{\bm{Y}-\bm{M}}{\sqrt{m}\widehat{\sigma}};\bm{\omega}\right)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}\widehat{\sigma}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\omega_{i}\left(\sigma_{i}(\bm{M})-\sqrt{m}\widehat{\sigma}\widehat{\lambda}_{i}\right)\bm{u}_{i}(\bm{M})\bm{v}^{\prime}_{i}(\bm{M}),

(7)

where
\bm{u}_{i}(\bm{M}),\bm{v}_{i}(\bm{M})
are the corresponding left and right singular vectors of
\bm{Y}-P_{\bm{\Omega}}(\bm{M})
. In the context of matrix completion where
p_{i,j}<1
, computing the gradient becomes non-trivial. A pivotal step in our algorithm is finding a direction that closely approximates the true gradient for iterative gradient descent. Inspired by the form of gradient in (
7
), we define the pseudo-gradient as

\displaystyle\widehat{\nabla}_{\bm{M}}l\left(\frac{\bm{Y}-P_{\bm{\Omega}}(\bm{M})}{\sqrt{m}\widehat{\sigma}};\bm{\omega}\right)=P_{\bm{\Omega}}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}\widehat{\sigma}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\omega_{i}\left(\sigma_{i}(\bm{M})-\sqrt{m}\widehat{\sigma}\widehat{\lambda}_{i}\right)\bm{u}_{i}(\bm{M})\bm{v}^{\prime}_{i}(\bm{M})\right).

(8)

It is straightforward to verify that the pseudo-gradient coincides with the gradient when
p=1
, and we will later demonstrate its adequacy in our algorithms for
p<1
. We provide the algorithm for the estimator
\widetilde{\bm{M}}_{\text{fac}}
in Algorithm
3.2
and defer the algorithm for the estimator
\widetilde{\bm{M}}_{\text{nuc}}
to the Appendix.

Algorithm 1
Iteration Algorithm for
\widetilde{\bm{M}}_{\text{fac}}

0:

Observed data
\bm{Y}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
, missing index matrix
\bm{\Omega}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
and used rank
s
.

0:

Completion matrix
\bm{M}
.

1:

\bm{U}^{(0)},\bm{D}^{(0)},\bm{V}^{(0)}\leftarrow\operatorname{svd}(p^{-1}Y)
;

2:

\bm{R}^{(0)}\leftarrow\bm{U}^{(0)}_{[1:s]}(\bm{D}^{(0)}_{[1:s]})^{1/2}
and
\bm{L}^{(0)}\leftarrow\bm{V}^{(0)}_{[1:s]}(\bm{D}^{(0)}_{[1:s]})^{1/2}
;

3:

Sample
l
standard Gaussian random matrix
\bm{M}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
with singular value vector
\bm{\lambda}(\bm{M}_{i}),1\leq i\leq l
;

4:

\widehat{\bm{\lambda}}\leftarrow\sum_{i=1}^{l}\bm{\lambda}(M_{i})/l
,
\operatorname{part\_sum}\leftarrow\sum_{i=n/3}^{2n/3}\widehat{\lambda}_{i}
;

5:

while

k\leq K

do

6:

\bm{U}^{(k+1)},\bm{D}^{(k+1)},\bm{V}^{(k+1)}\leftarrow\operatorname{svd}(\bm{Y}-P_{\bm{\Omega}}(\bm{R}^{(k)}\bm{L}^{(k)^{\prime}})
;

7:

\widehat{\sigma}^{(k+1)}\leftarrow\sum_{i=n/3}^{2n/3}\bm{D}^{(k+1)}_{i,i}/\operatorname{part\_sum}
;

8:

\widehat{\nabla}^{(k+1)}_{\bm{M}}\leftarrow\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}(\bm{D}^{(k+1)}_{i,i}-\widehat{\sigma}^{(k+1)}\widehat{\lambda}_{i})\bm{U}^{(k+1)}_{[i]}\bm{V}^{(k+1)^{\prime}}_{[i]}
;

9:

\bm{R}^{(k+1)}\leftarrow\bm{R}^{(k)}-\eta_{k+1}P_{\bm{\Omega}}(\widehat{\nabla}_{\bm{M}}^{(k+1)})\bm{L}^{(k)}
;

10:

\bm{L}^{(k+1)}\leftarrow\bm{L}^{(k)}-\eta_{k+1}P_{\bm{\Omega}}(\widehat{\nabla}_{\bm{M}}^{(k+1)})\bm{R}^{(k)}
;

11:

end

while

12:

return

\bm{M}=\bm{R}^{(K+1)}\bm{L}^{(K+1)^{\prime}}
.

4
Theoretical results

Several common assumptions prevalent in noisy matrix completion researches are outlined here.

Assumption 1
.

The random noise matrix
\bm{H}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
satisfies that

(a)

\bm{H}_{i,j},\ 1\leq i\leq m,1\leq j\leq n
are independent random variables with mean zero and variance
\sigma^{2}
.

(b)

\bm{H}_{i,j},\ 1\leq i\leq m,1\leq j\leq n
have sub-exponential tailed distribution, i.e., there exist constant
c>0
such that

\displaystyle P\left(|\bm{H}_{i,j}|\geq t\right)\leq\exp\left\{-\frac{t}{c\sigma}\right\},\quad\forall t>T>0.

(c)

The standard deviation
\sigma
is large enough such that

\displaystyle\sigma\gtrsim\left((mn)^{-1/2}\|\bm{M}_{0}\|_{F}\right)r^{1/2}m^{-1/2}\log^{1/2}n.

Assumption 2
.

The underlying fixed matrix
\bm{M}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n},n/m=\rho\leq 1
, with a rank
r\ll n
and singular value decomposition
\bm{M}_{0}=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\sigma_{i}\bm{u}_{i}\bm{v}_{i}^{\prime}
, satisfies that

(a)

The condition number
\kappa=\sigma_{1}/\sigma_{r}
is bounded by a constant,

(b)

The rank
r
matrix
\bm{M}_{0}
is
\mu_{0}
-incoherent.

Assumption 3
.

Data are missing completely at random with observed probability
p_{i,j}=p,1\leq i\leq m,1\leq j\leq n
and
1\geq p\gtrsim\mu_{0}rm^{-1}\log^{\alpha}m
for some large enough constant
\alpha>0
.

Assumptions
1
,
2
, and
3
are standard for noisy matrix completion, except for (
c
) in Assumption
1
. Assumption
1
.(
c
) ensures the noise level is sufficiently high to make the structural information of the noise matrix
\bm{H}
useful. This mild assumption applies to a wide range of noise levels, approximately when
\sigma\geq m^{-1}n^{-1/2}\|\bm{M}\|_{F}
. Assumptions
3
can be relaxed to include a missing at random mechanism, where
p_{i,j}
are not all equivalent and may depend on
i
. We assume Assumptions
1
,
2
, and
3
hold throughout this section.

4.1
Statistical properties of matrix factorization estimation

We consider the oracle properties of
\widetilde{\bm{M}}_{\text{fac}}
. This involves finding a matrix
\bm{M}
with rank at most
s
that minimizes the loss function

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\bm{M};\bm{Y},\bm{\Omega})=p^{-1}l\left(\frac{\bm{Y}-P_{\bm{\Omega}}(\bm{M})}{\sqrt{m}\widehat{\sigma}(\bm{M})};\bm{\omega}\right).

We verify that the loss function
\mathcal{L}(\bm{M};\bm{Y},\bm{\Omega})
is reasonable by showing that
\mathcal{L}(\bm{M}_{0};\bm{Y},\bm{\Omega})
is sufficiently small. Lemma
4.1
asserts that at the point
\bm{M}_{0}
, the value of this loss function rapidly converges to zero with high probability.

Lemma 4.1
.

With probability larger than
1-cn^{-3}
, it holds that

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\bm{M}_{0};\bm{Y},\bm{\Omega})\leq C_{0}\log^{-2}m,

with some constant
C_{0}\geq 0
independent of
m,n,p,\sigma
and
\bm{M}_{0}
.

In Assumption
1
, we do not assume a specific distribution for the entries of the noise matrix
\bm{H}
. Classical random matrix theory suggests that the spectrum of
\bm{H}
can be approximated by that of Gaussian random matrices. This property, known as universality, helps in determining suitable criteria for the spectrum of
\bm{H}
. However, a different scenario arises when considering the sparse random matrix
P_{\bm{\Omega}}(\bm{H})
. A key technique for proving Lemma
4.1
is to establish a similar universality property of sparse random matrices.

Define the estimation space

\displaystyle\mathbb{M}_{s,\mu_{1}}=\left\{\bm{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}\ |\ \operatorname{rank}(\bm{M})\leq s,\text{$\bm{M}$ is $\mu_{1}$-incoherent}\right\}.

Utilizing the matrix factorization
\bm{M}=\bm{L}\bm{R}^{\prime}
, we can only partially fulfill the conditions such that
\operatorname{rank}(\bm{M})\leq s
constrained in
\mathbb{M}_{s,\mu_{1}}
. Although no constraint is explicitly imposed to ensure that
\bm{M}
adheres to any incoherent condition, we will demonstrate in Section
4.3
that the incoherent property is maintained implicitly throughout the iteration process. Therefore, we consider the properties of estimators constrained in the estimation space
\mathbb{M}_{s,\mu_{1}}
.

Firstly, we focus on the scenario where the rank
s
of the estimation space
\mathbb{M}_{s,\mu_{1}}
is properly selected, i,e.,
s=r
. Consider the estimator

\displaystyle\widetilde{\bm{M}}=\underset{\bm{M}\in\mathbb{M}_{r,\mu_{1}}}{\arg\min}\ \mathcal{L}(\bm{M};\bm{Y},\bm{\Omega}).

We provide a lemma demonstrating that any sub-optimal estimator
\bm{M}
within the space
\mathbb{M}_{r,\mu_{1}}
will result in a sufficiently high value for the loss function
\mathcal{L}(\bm{M};\bm{Y},\bm{\Omega})
.

Lemma 4.2
.

For
\bm{M}\in\mathbb{M}_{r,\mu_{1}}
, if

\displaystyle\|\bm{M}_{0}-\bm{M}\|_{F}-(3+5\kappa)(2+2\sqrt{\rho}+\mathcal{C}^{1/2})\sigma\sqrt{mr/p}\gtrsim(\log^{-1}m)\sigma\sqrt{mr/p}.

then with probability larger than
1-cn^{-3}
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\bm{M};\bm{Y},\bm{\Omega})>\mathcal{C}\omega_{1}.

Lemma
4.1
demonstrates that at the point
\bm{M}_{0}
, the loss function yields a sufficiently small value. In contrast, Lemma
4.2
illustrates that an inadequate estimator
\bm{M}
results in a significantly large value of the loss function. Integrating these two findings, it is evident that there exists an
\bm{M}\in\mathbb{M}_{r,\mu_{1}}
for which the loss
\mathcal{L}(\bm{M};\bm{Y},\bm{\Omega})
is small. This minimized loss ensures a small estimation error
\|\bm{M}_{0}-\bm{M}\|_{F}
, leading to Theorem
4.1
that gives the upper bound of the Frobenius norm error
\|\widetilde{\bm{M}}-\bm{M}_{0}\|_{F}
with high probability.

Theorem 4.1
.

When the weight
\bm{\omega}
satisfies that
\omega_{1}\gg\log^{-2}m
, with probability larger than
1-cn^{-3}
,

\displaystyle\|\widetilde{\bm{M}}-\bm{M}_{0}\|_{F}\leq(3+5\kappa)(2+2\sqrt{\rho}+o(1))\sigma\sqrt{mr/p}.

Remark 4.1
.

The upper bound presented in Theorem
4.1
coincides with the minmax lower bound as shown in Theorem 3 in
Negahban and Wainwright (2012)
and Theorem 5 in
Koltchinskii, Lounici and Tsybakov (2011)
, with an order of
\sigma\sqrt{mr/p}
. This is owing to the ratio
\rho=n/m\leq 1
, and we assume that the condition number
\kappa
is bounded by a constant. It has been demonstrated that methods minimizing the squared Frobenius norm of the residual matrix
\bm{Y}-P_{\bm{\Omega}}(\bm{M})
also achieve optimal bounds. Consequently, our bound aligns with results of those methods. Additionally, we provide an explicit term in the upper bound of Theorem
4.1
, elucidating the influence of
\kappa
and
\rho
especially when they are no longer constant and increase as
m\rightarrow\infty
.

Our method primarily relies on the spectral properties of the random noise matrix, which implies that the noise level cannot be too low. In Assumption
1
.(
c
), we impose a constraint on the lower bound of
\sigma
. This can be also viewed as constraining the Signal-to-Noise Ratio (SNR)
\|\bm{M}_{0}\|_{F}/\|\bm{H}\|_{F}
, ensuring it is not too large. As previously discussed, this assumption holds for a wide range of noise levels, requiring only that the standard deviation
\sigma
of the noise exceeds approximately
m^{-1}n^{-1/2}\|\bm{M}_{0}\|_{F}
. Even if this mild assumption is not satisfied, we can still bound the estimation error. The error bound will be affected by the Frobenius norm of
\bm{M}_{0}
if (
c
) in Assumption
1
is not satisfied, as demonstrated in Corollary
4.1
.

Corollary 4.1
.

If Assumption
1
.(
c
) does not hold, when the weight
\bm{\omega}
satisfies that
\omega_{1}\gg\log^{-2}m
, with probability larger than
1-cn^{-3}
,

\displaystyle\|\widetilde{\bm{M}}-\bm{M}_{0}\|_{F}\leq(3+5\kappa)(2+2\sqrt{\rho}+o(1))\sigma\sqrt{\frac{mr}{p}}+8(3+5\kappa)(\mu_{0}+\mu_{1})\sqrt{\frac{r^{3}\log n}{mnp}}\|\bm{M}_{0}\|_{F}.

Remark 4.2
.

The additional term in Corollary
4.1
becomes dominant when the standard deviation
\sigma
of the noise is very small. In the extreme case of matrix completion without noise, i.e.,
\sigma=0
, Theorem 1.2 in
Candès and Tao (2010)
demonstrates that exact completion can be achieved with high probability using (
2
). In scenarios with minimal noise, uncertainty in the spectral properties of the noise matrix
\bm{H}
can obscure its structural information, reducing the effectiveness of our method. Directly minimizing the Frobenius norm of the residual matrix
\bm{Y}-P_{\bm{\Omega}}(\bm{M})
is more effective in this case. However, when the noise level is high, which is the more challenging scenario, the advantage of our method becomes evident.

Several studies have investigated the estimation and testing issues concerning the underlying rank
r
of
\bm{M}_{0}
, e.g.,
Zhang, Shapiro and Xie (2019)
and
Li et al. (2023)
.
When the noise level is significant, precisely estimating the true rank of
M_{0}
may not always be feasible. Specifically, there is uncertainty about whether the selected low-rank space
\mathbb{M}_{s,\mu_{1}}
matches
\mathbb{M}_{r,\mu_{1}}
. Misjudging the underlying rank of
\bm{M}_{0}
results in increased estimation errors. Nevertheless, for
s\geq r
that is not excessively large, we establish that the Frobenius norm error of the estimator
\widetilde{\bm{M}}_{s}
, defined as

\displaystyle\widetilde{\bm{M}}_{s}=\underset{\bm{M}\in\mathbb{\bm{M}}_{s,\mu_{1}}}{\arg\min}\mathcal{L}(\bm{M};\bm{Y},\bm{\Omega})

can still be controlled at a low level.

Corollary 4.2
.

When
r\leq s\leq mp\log^{-2}m
, the weight
\bm{\omega}
satisfies that
\omega_{1}\gg\log^{-2}m
and the noise standard deviation
\sigma\geq s^{3/2}r^{-1}m^{-1}n^{-1/2}\log^{1/2}n\|\bm{M}_{0}\|_{F}
, with probability larger than
1-cn^{-3}
,

\displaystyle\|\widetilde{\bm{M}}_{s}-\bm{M}_{0}\|_{F}\leq\left((2+4\kappa)r^{1/2}+(1+\kappa)s^{1/2}\right)(2+2\sqrt{\rho}+o(1))\sigma\sqrt{m/p}.

When
s
is selected as
s=r
, the upper bound stated in Corollary
4.2
aligns with the results of Theorem
4.1
. If
s>r
, the estimation error
\|\widetilde{\bm{M}}_{s}-\bm{M}\|_{F}
is bounded by the order
\sigma\sqrt{sm/p}
when both
\kappa
and
\rho
are considered constants. A higher value of
s
corresponds to a larger estimation space
\mathbb{M}_{s,\mu_{1}}
, leading to increased errors. An additional source of error arises from including the top singular subspace of the noise matrix in the estimator
\widetilde{\bm{M}}_{s}
, due to the excess rank
s>r
. Frobenius norm based methods tend to capture the entire top subspace of noise when minimizing the Frobenius norm of the residual matrix. In contrast, our proposed method is less affected because it also considers the spectral distribution of the random noise matrix.

4.2
Statistical properties of convex relaxation estimation

In addition to the strictly low-rank constraint
\bm{M}=\bm{L}\bm{R}^{\prime}
, several studies such as
Candès and Recht (2009)
suggest that the nuclear norm
\|\bm{M}\|_{*}
serves as an effective convex relaxation alternative for the exact rank. Our method, which aims to minimize the spectral distance between the residual matrix and sparse random matrices, can employ a similar convex relaxation procedure for low-rank constraints. Formally, we investigate the properties of the estimator
\widetilde{\bm{M}}_{\lambda}
, defined as

\displaystyle\widetilde{\bm{M}}_{\lambda}=\underset{\bm{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}}{\arg\min}\mathcal{L}(\bm{M};\bm{Y},\bm{\Omega})+\lambda\|\bm{M}\|_{*}.

Methods that minimizing the Frobenius norm benefit from the convex relaxation, as the overall estimation problem is strongly convex. This property enhances both theoretical analysis and computational feasibility.
Previous techniques demonstrating the performance guarantee of
\widehat{\bm{M}}_{\text{nuc}}
often relies on the convexity of the squared Frobenius norm and the nuclear norm. In contrast, analyzing our method is more challenging due to the non-convexity of
\mathcal{L}(\bm{M};\bm{Y},\bm{\Omega})
. When focusing on estimation properties, the estimation space
\mathbb{M}_{C}=\left\{\bm{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}\ |\ \|\bm{M}\|_{*}\leq C\right\}
is more difficult to control compared to
\mathbb{M}_{s,\mu_{1}}
. This difficulty arises from the ambiguity surrounding the rank of matrices in
\mathbb{M}_{C}
. The following theorem provides an error bound for the estimator
\widetilde{\bm{M}}_{\lambda}
with high probability for any
\lambda\geq 0
.

Theorem 4.2
.

For some
\bar{\omega}>0
, define that

\displaystyle R_{1}=2(1+\sqrt{\rho})\sigma\sqrt{m/p},\quad R_{2}=R_{1}+\frac{\lambda\sigma^{2}m}{\bar{\omega}p}+\left(\frac{\lambda\sigma^{2}m}{\bar{\omega}p}\right)^{1/2}R_{1}^{1/2}.

If
\min_{1\leq i\leq r(R_{1}+R_{2})/R_{1}}\omega_{i}\geq\bar{\omega}
,
with probability larger than
1-cn^{-3}
,

\displaystyle\|\widetilde{\bm{M}}_{\lambda}-\bm{M}_{0}\|_{F}

\displaystyle\leq(4+5\kappa)\left(\left(\frac{\lambda\sigma^{2}m}{\bar{\omega}p}\right)^{1/2}\left(rR_{2}-R_{1}\right)^{1/2}+R_{1}+R_{2}\right)

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad+\left((C_{0}+1)\lambda^{-1}\log^{-2}m+2rR_{2}\right)^{1/2}R_{1}^{1/2}.

The upper bound in Theorem
4.2
illustrates two different sources of error related to the choice of the tuning parameter
\lambda
. One source is the term
(C_{0}+1)\lambda^{-1}\log^{-2}m
, which arises from the flexibility of the estimation space. A higher
\lambda
reduces this flexibility, thereby decreasing the generalization error. The other source is the term
\lambda\sigma^{2}m/\bar{\omega}p
, which results from the shrinkage of singular values due to nuclear norm regularization. A smaller
\lambda
leads to decreased approximation error. These two distinct sources of error create a trade-off when tuning
\lambda
to minimize the bound, as illustrated in Corollary
4.3
.

Corollary 4.3
.

When the weight
\bm{\omega}
satisfies that
\min_{1\leq i\leq 4r}\omega_{i}\gg\log^{-2}m
, choosing
\lambda=2(1+\sqrt{\rho})\sigma^{-1}p^{1/2}m^{-1/2}\log^{-2}m
, with probability larger than
1-cn^{-3}
,

\displaystyle\|\widetilde{\bm{M}}_{\lambda}-\bm{M}_{0}\|_{F}\leq 2(1+\sqrt{\rho})\left((\sqrt{6}+4\sqrt{3}+5\sqrt{3}\kappa)r^{1/2}+4(4+5\kappa)+o(1)\right)\sigma\sqrt{m/p}.

By adjusting the tuning parameter
\lambda
, our results in Corollary
4.3
match the minmax lower bound for noisy matrix completion. Estimation properties for methods based on minimizing the squared Frobenius norm of the residual matrix with nuclear norm regularization, have been well understood. For example, assuming
\bm{M}_{0}
is a
\mu
-incoherent matrix, Corollary 2 in
Koltchinskii, Lounici and Tsybakov (2011)
provides a nearly optimal upper bound of the order
\sigma\sqrt{mr\log m/p}
. Compared to results from classical convex relaxation methods, our upper bound no longer contains logarithmic factors.

4.3
Algorithmic properties of iterative procedure

Iterative gradient descent algorithms are proposed to find approximate solutions, as closed-form solutions for the proposed methods are not feasible. Due to the non-convex nature of the loss function
\mathcal{L}(\bm{M};\bm{Y},\bm{\Omega})
and the non-convex constraint
\operatorname{rank}(\bm{M})\leq s
, general optimization theory does not guarantee global convergence. It is essential to accurately measure the performance gap between estimators (often the global minimizer) and the results obtained through iterative algorithms. For the constraint
\operatorname{rank}(\bm{M})\leq s
, methodologies such as matrix factorization
\bm{M}=\bm{L}\bm{R}^{\prime}
with alternating descent schemes, have been comprehensively researched. Therefore, our primary focus is on addressing the challenges arising from the proposed loss function
\mathcal{L}(\bm{M};\bm{Y},\bm{\Omega})
.

We formally define the iterative procedure for proposed method
\min_{\bm{M}\in\mathbb{M}_{s,\mu_{1}}}\mathcal{L}(\bm{M};\bm{Y},\bm{\Omega})
. With a feasible initial value
\bm{M}^{(0)}
, we define the iterative series
\left\{\bm{M}^{(k)}\right\}_{k=0,1,\cdots}
and the nuisance series
\left\{\bm{N}^{(k)}\right\}_{k=1,2,\cdots}
as

\displaystyle\bm{N}^{(k+1)}

\displaystyle=\bm{M}^{(k)}-\eta_{k+1}\widehat{\nabla}\mathcal{L}(\bm{M}^{(k)};\bm{Y},\bm{\Omega}),

\displaystyle\bm{M}^{(k+1)}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Proj}_{s}(\bm{N}^{(k+1)}),\quad k=0,1,\cdots,

where

\widehat{\nabla}\mathcal{L}(\bm{M};\bm{Y},\bm{\Omega})=p^{-1}\widehat{\nabla}_{\bm{M}}l\left(\frac{\bm{Y}-P_{\bm{\Omega}}(\bm{M})}{\sqrt{m}\widehat{\sigma}};\bm{\omega}\right)

is the pseudo-gradient given by (
8
). We also define the singular value decomposition for
\bm{M}^{(k)}
as
\bm{M}^{(k)}=\bm{U}^{(k)}\bm{D}^{(k)}\bm{V}^{(k)}
.

There are three primary challenges. First, control the influence by using the pseudo-gradient
\widehat{\nabla}\mathcal{L}(\bm{M};\bm{Y},\bm{\Omega})
instead of the true gradient. Second, bound the impact of projecting
\bm{N}^{(k+1)}
onto the rank
s
matrix
\bm{M}^{(k+1)}
. Finally, maintain the incoherent condition throughout the iterative process. We examine the scenario where
s=r
and establish bounds for the iterative series in the proposed algorithms.

We begin our algorithm analysis with the selection of the initial value
\bm{M}^{(0)}
. Since
p^{-1}P_{\bm{\Omega}}(\bm{Y})
is an unbiased estimator of
\bm{M}_{0}
, a commonly used initial value for matrix completion problems is the rank-
r
projection of this estimator, i.e.,

\bm{M}^{(0)}=\operatorname{Proj}_{r}(p^{-1}P_{\bm{\Omega}}(\bm{Y})).

Error bounds for the Frobenius and infinity norms, as well as an incoherent bound for
\bm{M}^{(0)}
, are provided in Lemma
4.3
.

Lemma 4.3
.

With probability larger than
1-cn^{-3}
, it holds that

\displaystyle\|\bm{M}^{(0)}-\bm{M}_{0}\|_{F}\leq C(\rho^{-1/4}\|\bm{M}_{0}\|_{\infty}+\sigma)\kappa\sqrt{\frac{mr}{p}}

\displaystyle\|\bm{M}^{(0)}-\bm{M}_{0}\|_{\infty}\leq\|\bm{U}\|_{2,\infty}\|\bm{V}\|_{2,\infty}(\|\bm{M}^{(0)}-\bm{M}_{0}\|_{F}+\sqrt{r}\|\bm{H}\|)(1+f^{(0)})^{2}

\displaystyle\|\bm{U}^{(0)}\|_{2,\infty}\leq\|\bm{U}\|_{2,\infty}(1+f^{(0)}),\quad\|\bm{V}^{(0)}\|_{2,\infty}\leq\|\bm{V}\|_{2,\infty}(1+f^{(0)}),

where

\displaystyle f^{(0)}=C_{1}\sqrt{r}(\kappa-1)+C_{2}(\kappa+\sqrt{r})(\|\bm{M}_{0}\|_{\infty}+6\sigma c\log m)^{1/2}\left(\frac{m}{p\sigma_{r}^{2}}\right)^{1/2}\log m.

In Lemma
4.3
, we demonstrate that
\bm{M}^{(0)}
serves as a suitable initial matrix. It is sufficiently close to
\bm{M}_{0}
and preserves the crucial incoherence condition without excessively increasing the incoherence coefficient. Using mathematical induction, we establish the main result below that tracks both the error
\bm{M}^{(k)}-\bm{M}_{0}
and the incoherence condition for
\bm{M}^{(k)},\ k=1,2,\cdots
.

Theorem 4.3
.

With probability larger than
1-cn^{-3}
, for each
M^{(k)}
in the iterative series
\left\{\bm{M}^{(k)}\right\}_{k=1,2,\cdots}
with
\|\bm{M}^{(k)}-\bm{M}_{0}\|\geq C_{3}\kappa\sigma\sqrt{m/p}
, it holds that

\displaystyle\|\bm{M}^{(k)}-\bm{M}_{0}\|_{F}\leq\|\bm{M}^{(0)}-\bm{M}_{0}\|_{F}\prod_{i=0}^{k-1}\left(1-\gamma_{i}(1-\alpha_{i})\right),

\displaystyle\|\bm{U}^{(k)}\|_{2,\infty}\leq\|\bm{U}^{(0)}\|_{2,\infty}\prod_{i=0}^{k-1}\left(1+C\mu_{0}r^{4}\frac{\|\bm{M}^{(i)}-\bm{M}_{0}\|_{F}}{\sigma_{r}^{(k)}}\gamma_{i}\alpha_{i}\log^{3/2}m\right),

\displaystyle\|\bm{V}^{(k)}\|_{2,\infty}\leq\|\bm{V}^{(0)}\|_{2,\infty}\prod_{i=0}^{k-1}\left(1+C\mu_{0}r^{4}\frac{\|\bm{M}^{(i)}-\bm{M}_{0}\|_{F}}{\sigma_{r}^{(k)}}\gamma_{i}\alpha_{i}\log^{3/2}m\right),

\displaystyle\|\bm{M}^{(k)}-\bm{M}_{0}\|_{\infty}\leq 2\|\bm{M}^{(k)}-\bm{M}_{0}\|_{F}\|\bm{U}^{(k)}\|_{2,\infty}\|\bm{V}^{(k)}\|_{2,\infty},

where we define that
\sigma_{r}^{(k)}
is the
r
th singular value of
\bm{M}^{(k)}
,
\kappa_{r}^{(k)}
is the condition number,
C^{(k)}_{2}<1
is a sufficiently small constant and

\displaystyle\gamma_{k}

\displaystyle=\frac{2\eta_{k+1}}{\widehat{\sigma}(\bm{M}^{(k)})^{2}}\frac{(\|P_{\bm{\Omega}}(\bm{M}^{(k)}-\bm{Y})\|-\widehat{\sigma}(\bm{M}^{(k)})\lambda_{1})}{\|\bm{M}^{(k)}-\bm{M}_{0}\|_{F}},

\displaystyle\alpha_{k}

\displaystyle=\left(C^{(k)}_{2}+\left(\frac{(\sigma^{(k)}_{r}\kappa^{(k)}_{r})^{2}(2-2r(\|\bm{M}^{(k)}-\bm{M}_{0}\|_{F}/\sigma^{(k)}_{r})^{-2})^{2}}{\|\bm{M}^{(k)}-\bm{M}_{0}\|_{F}^{2}}+1\right)^{-1/2}\right).

Remark 4.3
.

When treating
\rho=1-\gamma_{i}(1-\alpha_{i})<1
as a constant, we obtain an exponentially decaying sequence such that
\|\bm{M}^{(k)}-\bm{M}_{0}\|_{F}\leq\rho^{k}\|\bm{M}^{(0)}-\bm{M}_{0}\|_{F}
holds if
\|\bm{M}^{(k)}-\bm{M}_{0}\|\geq C_{3}\kappa\sigma\sqrt{m/p}
. Theorem 2 in
Ma et al. (2018)
provides a similar decay for the gradient descent of the problem (
1
). A primary challenge it addresses is the ill-posed representation
\bm{M}=\bm{L}\bm{R}^{\prime}
which lacks identifiability. In our theorem, we omit the matrix factorization and the alternating optimal step. We mainly focus on the issues related to the non-convex loss
\mathcal{L}(\bm{M};\bm{Y},\bm{\Omega})
in our proposed method. Expanding our results to the alternating gradient descent algorithm with the representation
\bm{M}=\bm{L}\bm{R}^{\prime}
is straightforward by integrating their techniques.

In Theorem
4.3
, we demonstrate that the Frobenius and infinity norms of
\bm{M}^{(k)}-\bm{M}_{0}
decrease successively while controlling the growth rates of
\|\bm{U}^{(k)}\|_{2,\infty}
and
\|\bm{V}^{(k)}\|_{2,\infty}
. This ensures that with a finite step
k
, we can obtain an
\bm{M}^{(k)}
with small errors in both the Frobenius and infinity norms while maintaining the incoherence condition with a small incoherence coefficient.

Corollary 4.4
.

If
p\gtrsim\mu_{0}^{4}\kappa^{4}r^{11}m^{-1}\log^{\alpha}m
, for sufficiently small step sizes
\eta_{k},k=1,2,\cdots
, there exist
K>0
with probability larger than
1-cn^{-3}
that

\displaystyle\|\bm{M}^{(K)}-\bm{M}_{0}\|_{F}\lesssim\kappa\sigma\sqrt{mr/p},

\displaystyle\|\bm{M}^{(K)}-\bm{M}_{0}\|_{\infty}\lesssim\kappa\sigma\mu_{0}\sqrt{r^{3}\log^{3}m/np}.

Remark 4.4
.

Corollary
4.4
demonstrates that
\bm{M}^{(K)}
is a reliable approximation for the estimator
\widetilde{\bm{M}}
. It has the same order in Frobenius error bounds as
\widetilde{\bm{M}}
, as established in Theorem
4.1
. The additional assumption in Corollary
4.4
is slightly more stringent than Assumption
3
. Treating
\mu_{0}
,
\kappa
, and
r
as constants, the lower bound of observation probability
p\gtrsim m^{-1}\log^{\alpha}m
still aligns with the conditions specified in Assumption
3
.

Corollary
4.4
confirms that the algorithmic error does not dominate the statistical error. Our proposed algorithm achieves this by ensuring a sufficiently small step size in each update and an adequate number of iterations. As a result, there is no gap in the error bound rates between the oracle estimators and the results obtained through numerical methods. This is important because it guarantees the numerical performance of the proposed algorithm for our method.

Discussion

This paper introduces a novel spectral matching criterion for noisy matrix completion. We demonstrate that estimators based on this criterion perform well both theoretically and numerically. Efficient algorithms are devised for the proposed criterion, ensuring small Frobenius and maximum norms. Our methods provide advantages over commonly used least squared estimators by utilizing more information from noise, especially in high noise level situations.

Our method can be readily generalized to encompass broader missing mechanisms and more complex noise matrices if the variance structure of the noise is understood. However, expanding our method becomes challenging in scenarios with heterogeneous noise matrices, e.g., the entries of noise matrices have different and unknown variances. If a uniform upper bound for the variances of noise exists, a comparable error bound can still be derived, similar to the results in least squares estimators. However, this bound might be inadequate, especially when the upper bounds for variances are significantly larger than the normalized spectral norm of the noise matrix. Therefore, it is meaningful to develop methods that adapt the proposed approach to more complex noise scenarios in the future.
