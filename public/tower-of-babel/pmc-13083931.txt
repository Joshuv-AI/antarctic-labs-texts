Changes in antimicrobial resistance profiles of Escherichia coli and the metagenome on Dutch pig farms after antimicrobial usage interventions
Authors: REC Luiken, H Prinsen, S N Dasari, H Zweerus, A J Timmerman, D C Speksnijder, W Dohmen, J A Wagenaar, D J J Heederik, A L Zomer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe use of antimicrobials in livestock farming drives selection and dissemination of antimicrobial resistance (AMR), prompting implementation of veterinary stewardship programs to reduce antimicrobial usage (AMU). We evaluated changes in AMR on 45 Dutch pig farms before and after tailored, coaching-based interventions using phenotypic testing of Escherichia coli and metagenomic profiling of pooled faeces. Post-weaning pig farms, including nursery and fattening units, entered the intervention in a stepped-wedge design, with intervention periods ranging from 10 to 27 months. Across farms, AMU and abundances of several antimicrobial resistance gene classes declined over time, alongside reductions in overall resistome levels. Proportions of phenotypic AMR in E. coli were more variable, although decreased AMU was associated with lower resistance for specific antimicrobial classes, such as tetracyclines and beta-lactams. While longer follow-up is required to fully assess long-term impacts, these findings indicate that veterinary antimicrobial stewardship programs can yield measurable short-term reductions in AMR at farm level.Subject terms: Biotechnology, Microbiology

Introduction

The main goal of antimicrobial therapy is to reduce or eliminate infection-causing pathogens. However, antimicrobial therapy also leads to antimicrobial resistance (AMR)1. In livestock farming, the selection for resistant bacteria is exacerbated by the practice of group treatments, preventive use and use of antimicrobials as antimicrobial growth promotor. The World Health Organization recognized AMR as one of the top ten global health threats facing humanity2. Both human and animal health sectors called for action and many countries have embraced the One Health approach3–5.

Veterinary antimicrobial stewardship is a key strategy to limit the development and proliferation of AMR across animal and human health, as antimicrobial use (AMU) and infection prevention and control are the main drivers of AMR selection and dissemination6. Observations of transmission of resistant strains from animals to humans have led to the initiation of specific actions in the Netherlands by farmers, veterinary stakeholders and the Dutch government, resulting in a 77% reduction in herd-level AMU since 20097–9. During the same period, AMR measured in indicator bacteria among slaughter pigs decreased, e.g. tetracycline resistance in Escherichia coli (E. coli) dropped from ~60% to 30%8. While correlations between usage and resistance in livestock have been demonstrated over time10,11, longitudinal research studying the quantitative effect of farm interventions aimed at AMU reduction and epidemiologically linked AMR data remain limited, especially at farm level.

Measuring AMR generally involves determining AMR levels based on phenotypic resistance of isolated E. coli because of its ease in culturing and its ubiquitous presence in livestock and the human gut12,13. Using this indicator organism enables comparisons between studies and longitudinal trend analysis, which is essential in shaping policy against AMR and determining the effectiveness of interventions. However, E. coli only forms a small part of the bacterial community present in livestock that may harbor resistance genes. AMU also results in resistance gene acquisition by many organisms in the microbiome that are either pathogenic themselves or which form a reservoir for resistance genes, thereby facilitating further spread14,15. Metagenomic approaches offer a broader view on all the resistance genes present in the microbiome, although which species contains the resistance genes cannot be established adequately from short-read sequencing data16,17.

Previous work in Dutch high-usage pig farms demonstrated that a structured coaching approach targeting farm management teams can contribute to meaningful reductions in AMU. In a 2-year intervention study involving 45 farms, coaching combined with tailored management measures resulted in significant decreases in AMU in both weaned piglets (post-weaning nursery pigs) and fattening pigs18. The reduction appeared to be primarily driven by the implementation of specific farm interventions facilitated by coaching, confirming the potential of behavior-oriented stewardship strategies. However, the impact of such reductions in AMU on antimicrobial resistance (AMR) dynamics at farm level remains insufficiently understood.

The aim of this study is to describe changes in pig farms’ AMR profiles before and after implementing tailor-made, coaching-based interventions described in detail in the study by Prinsen et al.18, which aimed to reduce AMU. We determined AMR profiles of weaned piglet and fattener pig farms by means of phenotypic resistance of E. coli and resistome of the metagenome of pooled fresh faeces, compared these before and after interventions took place and analysed the association with AMU.

Results

Forty-five post-weaning Dutch pig farms were included in this study, consisting of 27 weaned piglet farms (of which 67% farrow-to-finish farms) and 18 fattener farms (of which 44% farrow-to-finish farms). Pre-weaned piglets were not included because no farms with high AMU in sows with pre-weaned piglets enrolled in the project. Fattening pig farms that keep only finishing pigs apply an all-in, all-out system at the barn level. Breeding farms use different production systems; most operate on a weekly system, while a few use a two- or three-week system. As a result, different age groups are present on the farms. Table 1 gives an overview of the five main characteristics of these farms. The duration of the interventions and thus time between sampling moments ranged from ten to 27 months. Descriptive AMU levels declined over time (Table 1), the formal mixed-effects analysis demonstrated a statistically significant negative association between coaching and AMU.

Phenotypic resistance

E. coli isolates collected at weaned piglet farms showed higher resistance levels to several classes of antimicrobials compared to those collected at fattener farms. Some weaned piglet farms showed 100% phenotypic resistance for ampicillin, sulfamethoxazole, tetracycline and trimethoprim, meaning all isolates were above ECOFF breakpoint (i.e. non-wild type), at both the start and the end of the study. Low resistance values for cefotaxime and ceftazidime (potentially Extended Spectrum Beta-Lactamase (ESBL) producing) were detected (Fig. 1). In fatteners differences in phenotypic resistance were statistically significant for ciprofloxacin (higher after intervention), and sulfamethoxazole (lower after intervention). Tigecycline resistance was significantly higher after intervention in weaned piglets. After multiple testing adjustment, only tigecycline resistance in weaned piglets remained statistically significant. Similar results are obtained if all farms were tested together; but with tigecycline and ciprofloxacin resistance in E. coli both still significantly higher after multiple testing adjustment after the intervention. All test results can be found in Supplemental Fig. 1 and Table 1.

Resistome

The total gene abundance significantly decreased over time in both weaned piglet and fattener pig farms (Fig. 2a). Tetracycline, MLS and beta-lactam resistance genes were most abundant in both age groups (Fig. 2b). In some classes of antimicrobials, like polymyxin (i.e., colistin), resistance genes were not detected at all in any of the samples. A statistically significant decrease was observed in abundance of aminoglycoside and tetracycline resistance genes in fatteners over the intervention period. Similarly, a statistically significant decrease was found in these antimicrobial classes in weaned piglets, as well as in beta lactam resistance genes. After applying multiple testing adjustments, only tetracycline resistance genes in weaned piglets remained statistically significant; that is, associated resistance was significantly lower at the end of the intervention. Similar results were obtained when testing for all farms together, with tetracycline and aminoglycoside resistance gene abundance being statistically significantly lower over the intervention period. All test results can be found in Supplemental Fig. 2 and Table 2.

The effect of AMU on AMR profiles

The most frequently used antimicrobials were beta-lactams, MLS and tetracyclines (expressed in DDDAf) (Supplemental Figs. 3 and 4). AMR level for several antimicrobial classes was statistically significantly associated with all potential confounding variables in univariable analyses (age group, sampling time and season of sampling) (Supplemental table 3) and were therefore included in the final model associating AMU and AMR (Table 2). Tetracycline use was significantly and positively associated with tetracycline resistance for both phenotypic and metagenomic determined resistance, while adjusted for potential time and seasonal effects. Higher polymyxin use was associated with significantly higher phenotypic colistin resistance, and MLS use was associated with significantly more MLS resistance genes in the resistome. No multicollinearity between variables was observed (VIF < 5) and six out of seven statistically significant associations between AMU and AMR were also significantly associated in univariable analysis of AMU and AMR (Supplemental table 3).

Discussion

Our study assessing the impact of a coaching intervention in weaned-piglet and fattener farms on AMU and AMR found that the total abundance of resistance genes was significantly lower at the end of the study than at the beginning. Metagenomic analysis of read counts coding for resistance genes indicated that the reduction in AMR occurred mainly due to a decrease in aminoglycoside and tetracycline genes. In a comparison of the phenotypic AMR profile between the start and end of the intervention, tigecycline and ciprofloxacin resistance was statistically significantly higher after the intervention. Other differences were not detectable. AMU, while adjusted for time effects, had a significant effect on some of observed phenotypic and metagenomic AMR prevalences, suggesting that next to general time trends, interventions aimed at AMU reduction had an effect on AMR levels.

The class level analyses revealed significant positive associations between tetracycline use and resistance for both phenotypic and metagenomic AMR results. Positive associations were also shown for MLS use on MLS gene abundance, beta lactam use and ceftazidime resistance prevalence in E. coli and several other corresponding AMR classes. These associations were adjusted for time and season of sampling indicating an additional effect of lower AMU on AMR on these farms in addition to existing nationwide trends in AMU reduction, and time of year. Total resistance gene abundance in the metagenome was significantly associated with season of sampling and significantly decreased over the course of the study. Our study showed in this case no significant effect of AMU on total AMR levels. We also saw a significant increase in phenotypic quinolone resistance in E. coli and a decrease in aminoglycoside resistance genes in the metagenome although little to no use of respective drugs is reported on these farms at any time during the study. Our results demonstrate that current (decreasing) AMU on a farm explains part of the resistance seen, but clearly not all. Additionally we show that age category, season of sampling and time (i.e., date) of sampling also have effects on certain AMR levels, next to usage.

The Dutch Nationwide AMR monitoring program uses the same phenotypic susceptibility testing protocol as was used in the present study. The nationwide monitoring program showed overall lower resistance levels in E. coli in slaughter pigs than findings in our study in fattener pigs during the relevant study period8. Higher resistance levels can probably be explained by the selection criterion for farms in our study of above average AMU, which is congruent with our finding of positive associations between AMU and AMR levels in this study and also reported in other studies19. The strength of our intervention study is that farm AMU levels have been associated to farm AMR levels where in nationwide monitoring programs only analyses on aggregated (country level) AMU and AMR data can be performed.

Several unexpected findings were observed in the phenotypic resistance results. Phenotypic tigecycline resistance level was slightly but significantly increased after intervention, however tigecycline is not used in food producing animals and no association with tetracycline use on the farms was found. Resistance to tigecycline in E. coli can be explained by the presence of tetracycline resistance gene tetX20, however no increase was observed in the resistome for specifically this gene. It is possible that other tetracycline resistance genes contribute to resistance development (e.g. tetL, tetM), as we did observe an increase in overall tetracycline resistance in the resistome. For phenotypic ciprofloxacin resistance a similar increase was observed. Associations with (fluor)quinolones use could not be tested as usage was limited to only one treatment on these farms. The nationwide monitoring program showed no increasing trends for both resistance classes in E. coli in fattener pigs21–23.

Despite the ban on chloramphenicol use in food animals24, we found a relatively high level of phenotypic chloramphenicol resistance in both age groups, higher in fatteners than in weaned piglets. The Dutch nationwide monitoring program has shown a stable level of chloramphenicol resistance in E. coli for many years8,25. Earlier studies have demonstrated that the usage of other antimicrobials co-select E. coli strains that carry chloramphenicol-resistance genes26. Furthermore, a positive association between amphenicol (specifically, florfenicol) use and amphenicol gene abundance was found in our study but not to chloramphenicol resistance in E. coli. The latter is in concordance with findings in Callens et al.11; however, Ceccarelli et al.27 have found a positive correlation between amphenicol use and chloramphenicol resistance in E. coli in fattener pigs. Targeted research on this topic is needed to come to better evidence.

The phenotypic and metagenomic analyses sometimes resulted into comparable results (e.g. for tetracycline resistance) but also provided complementary results (e.g. for polymyxin resistance). Phenotypic methods provide direct information on the commensal susceptibility, specific pathogens and risk assessment, while metagenomic methods provide a broader view on the presence of resistance genes in microbial communities, which could aid in the surveillance and understanding of AMR spread. Integrating data from both approaches contributes to a comprehensive understanding of AMR16,28. Phenotypic resistance in E. coli represents a small fraction of the overall resistance in the microbiota. Whilst resistome data gives a broader view, ARGs may be missed when they are carried by organisms that have a lower abundance in the microbiome29,30. Moreover, relatively low risk resistance genes integrated into stable genomic islands carried by commensals may conceal other relevant resistance gene counts. Furthermore, current metagenomic analytical tools are largely limited to identification of acquired ARGs. They are not able to identify target gene mutations as in the case of AMR conferred by single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) nor new resistance conferring genes which are not already included in libraries31 which is possible using phenotypic approaches. All in all, for both approaches we observed for several antimicrobial classes significant positive associations with antimicrobial use and for several classes we did not.

In this study we demonstrate that reducing current AMU, results in reduced AMR on the farm for some specific AMU – AMR combinations. However historic use on the farm32 (and in the country) but also determinants such as hygiene (including disinfectants), biosecurity, environmental conditions and housing, have an effect on resistance levels at the farm33–35. Others, for example Storey et al., showed a stable level of phenotypic resistance in E. coli throughout a twelve-month study on a low-use pig farm, containing multiple age groups, including both weaners and fatteners35. Pollock et al. published similar findings where AMR gene prevalence and abundance was not affected by AMU on a high using pig farm over the course of one production cycle, probably because of a stable integration of these genes in the intestinal microbiome33. Therefore, an important factor to consider when studying the relationship between reduced AMU and AMR is the expected time between these changes in these variables. A delayed response between decreased AMU and AMR levels, in this case measured as a phenotypic resistance in E. coli, was also described in a study on the withdrawal of antimicrobial group treatments in pigs36. A significant increase in E. coli susceptibility was observed after 11 months but not earlier, suggesting that several production cycles are required before an effect on AMR becomes evident following a change in AMU36,37. Limited negative fitness effects from carrying resistance genes may play a role here, as has already been described for the determination of some E. coli resistance on plasmids38.

Despite the longitudinal nature of this study, it was difficult to explore this delayed effect because of the relatively short period of follow-up and varying periods between sampling moments inherent to the chosen stepped-wedge design. The sensitivity analysis of farms with over 500 intervention days compared to the full analysis, including farms with shorter intervention periods, also did not show major differences between farms with long or shorter intervals between implementing farm management practices and sampling.

Although farm management interventions were known on these farms, it was unfortunately not possible to disentangle individual attributions of coaching and technical farm interventions on AMU because interventions were tailored for each farm and multiple technical and management interventions took place per farm. The study did not have sufficient power to perform refined analyses focusing on the relative contributions of intervention techniques to the observed changes in AMU and AMR. Another limitation of this study was the fact that previous antimicrobial treatments in earlier age groups were not known.

While most studies examining AMR on pig farms focus on fattener pigs, which are closest to the human food supply chain, it is known that the total birth-to-slaughter AMU influences the level of ARGs at slaughter36,39. In this study we focused on AMU and AMR in both weaned and fattener pigs. Pre-weaned piglets could not be included because no participating farms with high AMU in sows with pre-weaned piglets enrolled in the project. We chose to include weaned piglets in our analysis because AMU (including the use of group treatments) is highest in this group9,40, making this age group particularly relevant for studying the relationship between AMU and AMR. That said, as mentioned before, we did not study the effects of lower AMU throughout the whole life span of a pig, which could have resulted in even larger differences in AMR if pre-weaners were included in both coaching, AMU and AMR analysis. More research is needed on how early life exposure to ARGs and AMU in pigs affects AMR levels at slaughter and to also examine how this may contribute to human exposure to AMR.

In conclusion the total amount of ARGs and some specific clusters of resistance genes reduced over the course of this intervention study, but this was not seen in the phenotypic AMR profile of E. coli isolates. Our study reveals that lowering usage is associated with lower resistance for some classes of antimicrobials, after adjusting for potential time and seasonal effects, in addition to the national trend of gradually reducing AMU. These results were shown in both phenotypic and metagenome resistance indicators. Although future studies with a longer follow-up time may shed more light on long-term effects of lowering usage, this study shows positive effects of increased veterinary antimicrobial stewardship on weaned piglet and fattener pig farms over a relatively short intervention period.

Methods

Study design and farm selection

The work described in this manuscript was part of a larger study18. The larger study aimed to assess the effect of implementing coaching and farm interventions, with the objective of improving animal health and reducing AMU at the farm. In our step-wedge design, clusters of farms were included in the intervention group at different moments between March 2019 and December 2021. At the end of the study, all farms were included in the intervention. Both fattener pig and weaned piglet farms were studied as antimicrobial treatment practices differ between these animal categories. Interventions were tailored per farm in order to achieve the highest possible AMU reduction. The intervention effect was studied by comparing farms in the different intervention stages (no-intervention versus intervention) and over time. On the first and last farm visits within the study (t = 1 and t = 2), fresh faecal samples were collected to test for AMR. In total, 57 Dutch post-weaning pig farms were included at the start of the intervention: 37 farms with weaned piglets (approximately 7–25 kg body weight) and 20 fattening pig farms (approximately 25 kg to slaughter weight). All pig production farms were eligible to be included in the study if their AMU was above national benchmark values (>5 Defined Daily Doses Animal farm (DDDAf) for fattener pigs and >20 DDDAf for weaned piglets) there were no other selection criteria. Farmers were approached through communication platforms of livestock, pig and veterinary organizations, and farmers participated voluntarily. Further details on farm selection and study design can be found in Prinsen et al.18. Twelve farms were excluded because farmers stopped farming, farms did not meet aforementioned inclusion criteria or because of incomplete data. In total 45 farms were included in the analyses.

Sample collection

Sampled fattener pigs were as close to slaughter as possible, with a maximum of two weeks before slaughter. Sampled weaned piglets were between eight and ten weeks old. A total of 25 fresh, still warm, undisturbed faecal droppings were taken from pen floors in one barn per farm. The number of pens per barn ranged from 1 to 30 for fattening pigs, with 10–50 pigs per pen, depending on pen size. For weaned piglets, barns contained between 10 and 30 pens, with 10–30 piglets per pen. We aimed to collect at least one sample per pen. When fewer than 25 pens were present, multiple samples were collected per pen. When more than 25 pens were present, samples were randomly taken across pens throughout the barn. A minimum of 10 g faeces per sample was taken. Faeces were sampled wearing gloves and using sterile spoons. After placing a faecal dropping in a separate jar new gloves and new sterile spoons were used to gather the next faecal sample. All samples were directly stored at 4 °C and cooled transported within a few hours to the lab immediately after sampling.

Sample processing

Upon laboratory arrival, equal aliquots of 25 fresh faeces samples per farm were mixed into one sample. Part of the sample was homogenized with glycerol (30%) and stored at -80 °C for E. coli isolation. Another part was used for DNA isolation.

E. coli isolation and susceptibility testing

To isolate E. coli, faecal samples were thawed and inoculated onto five MacConkey agar plates (Thermo Fisher®) per sample to increase the chance to have separate colonies and incubated overnight at 37 °C. Thirty suspected E. coli colonies were subcultured individually on blood agar (BA) plates (Thermo Fisher®) and incubated overnight at 37 °C. Bacterial isolates were identified using MALDI-TOF (Bruker®) to confirm E. coli. The first 25 isolates per sample that tested positive for E. coli were subcultured onto a BA plate and incubated overnight at 37 °C. Subsequently, a 0.5 McFarland suspension was prepared for each isolate. 10 µl of the bacterial suspension was transferred into 11 ml Mueller-Hinton Broth (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and mixed homogeneously. Finally, the MHB suspension was transferred into Sensititre plates (50 µl/well), sealed and incubated at 37 °C for 18-24 hours.

Minimum inhibitory concentrations (MIC) were determined for each isolate for a selection of 14 antimicrobials (Ampicillin, Azithromycin, Cefotaxime, Ceftazidime, Chloramphenicol, Ciprofloxacin, Colistin, Gentamicin, Meropenem, Nalidixan, Sulphamethoxazol, Tetracycline, Tigecycline, Trimetoprim) using EUVSEC plates (EUVSEC MIC plate, Thermo Fisher Scientific ®). An automatic photometer (MERLIN Diagnostika GmbH, Germany) was used to read the results. EUCAST ECOFF breakpoints were used (version 13) valid in 2023 to assess wildtype and non-wildtype populations. (https://www.eucast.org/).

Metagenomics processing

DNA extraction was performed using the QIAamp Fast DNA Stool Mini Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) following a previously published protocol41, with a minor modification. Treatment of samples in the TissueLyser at 30 Hz for 3 × 30 s with cooling on ice in between treatments was replaced with bead-beating samples for 5 min on a Vortex-Genie 2 (Scientific Industries, Bohemia, New York, USA). Shotgun metagenomic sequencing libraries were prepared with the Illumina Nextera XT kit. Multiplexing and sequencing were performed using the Illumina NextSeq platform (150 bp paired-end sequencing) targeted at 60 million reads per sample.

DNA reads were processed using FastDeME with default settings (https://github.com/aldertzomer/FastDeMe). Briefly, reads were processed using FastP to remove poor-quality reads and sequencing adapters and barcodes42. The tool KMA43 was used to detect hits to known antimicrobial resistance genes with the default settings in the Resfinder database31 to investigate the resistome. Finally, the bacterial load was determined by counting matching shotgun metagenomic reads to the 16S SILVA database v.132 using Kraken244. Information on resistance classes of genes was obtained from the Resfinder database.

AMU data collection

Farm-level AMU data was derived from two national databases for the pig sector from 2018 until 2021 (see Prinsen et al.18 for details). AMU was expressed as DDDAf per farm per antimicrobial class and for the total of antimicrobials used45. DDDAf was calculated by dividing the total number of treated kg of animal on a farm for a specific time period by the average number of kg of animals present on that farm for each animal category (i.e., weaned piglets and fattener pigs).

Data analyses

AMR was assessed using two approaches. In the phenotypic approach, the percentage of non-wildtype isolates (i.e. above the epidemiological cutoff value [ECOFF]) of E. coli per farm was calculated. Throughout this manuscript, isolates above the ECOFF are referred to as ‘resistant’, acknowledging that this reflects deviation from the wild-type population rather than clinical resistance. The percentage resistant E. coli per farm was calculated as the number of resistant E. coli isolates for a given antimicrobial class divided by the total number of E. coli isolates from that farm. For the metagenomic approach, the relative abundance of antimicrobial resistance genes (ARGs) was assessed per farm by counting ARGs in the metagenome (adjusted for gene length), dividing by the total number of 16S reads as an indicator of bacterial count, and multiplying by 10^6 for scaling purposes. Subsequently gene abundances were summed by antimicrobial class and in total, assigning genes conferring resistance to multiple classes to each relevant class.

Boxplots were generated by age group and time and the total of samples to explore and visualize the AMR and AMU distributions. AMR levels were analyzed per antimicrobial class as well as for total metagenomic-determined levels. Wilcoxon signed rank (paired) test was used to determine statistical significance (p-value < 0.05) between abundances or resistance percentages at both the start and end of the intervention. Multiple testing adjustment was performed using Benjamini-Hochberg (FDR).

To test associations between AMU and AMR, a mixed linear modelling approach was employed to account for potential correlations between results from samples from the same farm. In this model, the dependent variable was farm AMR level categorized by antimicrobial class. The independent variable was AMU per antimicrobial class, expressed as DDDAf. Additionally, the association between total AMU versus total AMR was calculated for metagenomic AMR levels as gene abundance can be summed. Confounding was tested for by age group (weaned piglets and fatteners), time of sampling, and sampling season. Time of sampling was calculated as the number of days after baseline sampling to potentially allow for sampling period effect on AMR and AMU levels. To enable model comparisons across different antimicrobial classes, the final set of variables was similar for each model. Multicollinearity was checked by exploring the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF). Unique Farm ID was included as random variable, allowing a random intercept. The study included two sampling points, before and after intervention. AMU represents the sum of AMU in the relevant age group, six months prior to sampling (in DDDAf). To enable meaningful analysis of the association between our AMR outcomes and AMU on the farm, we calculated the DDDAf for the six months preceding each sampling event. This period was chosen as it provides a representative estimate of AMU for the respective farm and age group. Using a longer period was not feasible due to the interval between sampling events varying from 10 to 27 months, while a shorter period would make the data more susceptible to random fluctuations.

To assure meaningful associations, antimicrobial classes were considered only when more than 10% of total number of datapoints showed use. This resulted in modeling for the following classes: tetracycline, beta-lactam, macrolide-lincosamide-streptograminB (MLS), polymyxin, amphenicol and trimethoprim-sulfonamides. As EUSVAC plates provide separate results for phenotypic resistance to trimethoprim and sulphamethoxazole, these could only be shown and analyzed individually.

Additionally, a sensitivity analysis was performed with 2/3 (30/45) of the farms having the longest time between sampling i.e. intervention period, to determine whether effects were different for farms which had received more coaching and had more time to implement farm interventions.

All data analyses were performed using R Studio version 4.2.346.
