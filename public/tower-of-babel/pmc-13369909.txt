Three parsimonious spatiotemporal patterns in cerebellum reveal individual traits in function and behavior
Authors: Shuo Lv, Jinlong Li, Ruoqi Yang, Xinyu Wu, Zhiming Wang, Wenjing Zhu, Tan Gao, Jia-Hong Gao, Guoyuan Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe cerebellum plays a critical role in large-scale brain functional connectivity structure and synchronizing global waves propagation. Despite extensive studies on cortical spatiotemporal architecture, particularly in zero-lag and time-lag synchrony, leaving the parsimonious cerebellar representational structure largely overlooked. Using complex principal component analysis (CPCA) on resting-state fMRI data from the Human Connectome Project, we delineate three dominant low-dimensional spatiotemporal patterns within the cerebellum, manifesting as unimodal-to-transmodal progression, functional antagonism, and hemispheric asymmetry. These patterns correlate with various empirical functional connectivity topographies, including functional connectome network structure, quasiperiodic pattern, functional connectivity gradients and brain network dynamics hierarchy. Importantly, functional connectivity reconstructed from these patterns preserves the intrinsic structural modularity, proving the robustness of the sparse cerebellar dynamics. Moreover, personalized CPCA-derived features enable accurate sex classification and prediction of cognitive, emotional, alertness and personality traits. Our findings position the cerebellum as an active, trait-sensitive integrator of brain-wide spatiotemporal organization, suggesting propagation-based motifs constitute a foundational mode of individual brain intrinsic function.Subject terms: Dynamical systems, Cognitive neuroscience

Introduction

Understanding the spatiotemporal dynamics of spontaneous brain activity presents a foundational challenge in neuroscience. Intrinsic blood oxygenation level dependent (BOLD) fluctuations measured by resting-state functional MRI (rs-fMRI) reflect organized spatial and temporal patterns, and have been widely used to establish fundamental principles of neural integration and propagation, revolutionizing the study of neural architecture1,2. Numerous studies have demonstrated that low-frequency (<0.1 Hz) neural activity manifests as both synchronous co-activations across the brain and propagating dynamics over time, supporting the capacity for distributed processing in the absence of external stimuli3–6. To date, zero-lag and time-lag approaches have offered powerful and complementary insights into brain organization. Zero-lag methods, such as independent component analysis (ICA) and functional connection gradients7,8, delineate the spatial arrangement of synchronous co-activation patterns. Time-lag methods, including quasi-periodic patterns (QPPs)9–11 and lag projection12–14, reveal dynamic wave-like patterns of neural propagation across regions6,15–20. Extensive prior work has demonstrated that these lag structures are robust and reproducible under specific low-frequency BOLD regimes, and are systematically associated with behavior, arousal, and disease states, reflecting temporally structured propagation and hierarchical information flow rather than static synchrony21,22. Although most existing studies adopt either zero-lag or time-lag approaches, there may be an underlying integrated characterization that encompasses both structural aspects to delineate latent spatiotemporal patterns. Recently, complex principal component analysis (CPCA) offers a unique opportunity to capture both zero-lag and time-lag features as mathematically coupled aspects of the same latent component within a single representation2,23. By jointly encoding standing-wave-like spatial structure and traveling-wave-like temporal propagation, CPCA offers an advantage over alternative approaches for characterizing global functional brain organization that would otherwise require combining multiple zero-lag or lag-based methods, while explaining a larger proportion of variance with fewer latent components. By leveraging CPCA, the cerebral cortex can be represented as a parsimonious taxonomy of prominent global spatiotemporal patterns in spontaneous BOLD activity, providing insights into the functional architecture of the human brain2. However, these investigations have primarily focused on the cerebral cortex, leaving the cerebellum relatively underexplored in terms of its intrinsic spatiotemporal dynamics24.

The human cerebellum, comprising over 80% of the brain’s neurons, has long been recognized for its role in motor coordination, but increasing evidence suggests that it contributes substantially to higher-order cognitions, including emotion regulation, language and fear in brain function25–27. Given its distinct anatomy and long-range cerebro-cerebellar loops, the cerebellum represents a compelling but relatively underexplored substrate for studying spatiotemporal dynamics in the brain24,28. In recent years, studies have revealed that the cerebellum shows both zero-lag functional gradients and time-lag propagation patterns analogous to those observed in cortical regions25,29–31. First, zero-lag studies have revealed spatial gradients and modular organization consistent within the cortex and cerebellum. Specifically, researchers have identified a unimodal-to-transmodal hierarchy within the cerebellum that mirrors cortical organization30,32. Cerebellar regions involved in abstract cognition (e.g., Crus I/II) are spatially distinct from those supporting sensorimotor processing, and they exhibit both higher spontaneous activity amplitudes and hemispheric asymmetries—features that reflect competitive or lateralized mechanisms suggested by prior anatomical and functional mapping27,30. Second, time-lag approaches have revealed the propagation dynamics of spontaneous activity within the cerebellum and between the cerebellum and cerebral cortex through two modeling strategies: one that captures recurrent spatiotemporal patterns (e.g., QPP)33,34, and another that maps instantaneous signal delays using lag-based or phase-based measures (e.g., lag projection)31. Wave-like propagation through cerebellar territories or intrinsic timescale estimation from autocorrelation decay often reveals the consistent spatiotemporal dynamics across cortical and subcortical structures6, thereby supporting the possibility that the cerebellum may also be involved in similar cognitive and emotional regulation work35. Recent studies indicate that cortical dynamics in the ultra-slow frequency range require intact cortical-subcortical communication, including the cerebellum, and are critically shaped by large-scale coordination across distributed brain systems, supporting stable integration of intrinsic brain activity13,36. These insights indicate that the cerebellum participates actively in temporally structured brain-wide communication. Nevertheless, a comprehensive, unified understanding of how these structured patterns emerge, interact, and represent meaningful biological and behavioral features is still lacking37–39.

Growing interest in modeling individual differences in brain function has spurred the development of analytic frameworks linking intrinsic dynamics to stable biological and behavioral traits40,41. In the cortex, low-dimensional spatiotemporal features ranging from static gradients to dynamic propagation modes have been used to predict sex42, cognitive ability43, emotion regulation44, and psychopathology risk45. These increasingly emphasize low-dimensional representations that capture meaningful variance across individuals, moving beyond group-average descriptions. Recent evidence suggests that cerebellar imaging markers—including lobule-specific structural variability, functional gradients, and large-scale connectivity profiles—are sensitive to individual behavioral characteristics such as memory, motor coordination, and social cognition32,46,47. This cerebellum-behavior association has become an increasingly important focus in neuroscience, providing critical insights into how cerebellar dysfunction contributes to cognitive diversity, emotion regulation, and vulnerability to neuropsychiatric conditions such as depression, autism spectrum disorder, and attention deficit hyperactivity disorder48,49. However, given that low-dimensional spatiotemporal representations within the cerebellum exhibit marked spatial heterogeneity across lobules and networks—reflecting its diverse connectivity and functional specialization—it remains an open question whether such representations, particularly their dynamic propagation patterns, can serve as effective neural phenotypes capable of reliably capturing and predicting individual behavioral and biological traits.

Here, we applied CPCA to rs-fMRI data from the Human Connectome Project (HCP) to synchronously decompose complex-valued intrinsic BOLD fluctuations into complex maps reflecting zero-lag structure and phase delay maps capturing cycling propagation in the cerebellum2,50. We first characterized the group-level spatiotemporal patterns and validated their correspondence with topologies extracted from empirical functional connectivity methods. Then, we tested the utility of personalized features for predicting both sex and behavioral phenotypes43. These findings reveal that cerebellar activity represents trait-like information through temporally organized propagation motifs. By positioning the cerebellum as a low-dimensional biomarker of individual differences, our study underscores its underappreciated role in intrinsic brain organization and demonstrates that cerebellum-derived spatiotemporal patterns capture individual-level variability that is not fully redundant with cortical representations, particularly when contrasted with cortex alone and whole-brain decompositions. Together, these results may ultimately contribute to redefining how we model whole-brain functional integration, where the cerebellum exhibits rich spatiotemporal patterns identified in cortex24.

Results

To systematically characterize cerebellar spatiotemporal dynamics and their biological relevance, we performed analyses using four explicitly defined subsets of the HCP Young Adult (HCP-YA) cohort, each constructed for distinct analytic purposes, as described in the “Methods”. These subsets include a discovery sample (subset 1), a matched 3T-7T validation sample for test-retest replication (subset 2), and unrelated samples for sex classification and behavioral prediction analyses (subsets 3 and 4), enabling a structured assessment of spatiotemporal pattern identification, robustness across scanner field strengths, and individual-level relevance. To evaluate the generalizability of our findings, we replicated the entire pipeline in an independent developmental cohort, the subset of the HCP Development (HCP-D) dataset, confirming the reproducibility of both spatial and temporal features across datasets. We applied the Hilbert transform to cerebellar rs-fMRI data to construct complex-valued signals and extracted low-dimensional spatiotemporal representations using CPCA (Fig. 1a). Each principal component (PC) was further converted into a complex pattern capturing the static spatial structure, and a phase pattern reflecting directed propagation dynamics. To validate the complex PCs, we compared these real-valued patterns to canonical zero-lag patterns derived from empirical functional connectivity methods. We further reconstructed BOLD signals through bin averaging of complex PCs and phase patterns, and revealed that the reconstructed signals preserved key features of resting-state functional connectivity (rsFC) and modular organization (Fig. 1b). Then, to explore whether personalized variations of CPCA-derived patterns could effectively reflect biological information and individual behavioral measures, we computed sine relative phase (SRP) from individual-level CPCA results, yielding biologically interpretable representations of propagation timing51,52. First, we selected a sex-balanced subsample and trained machine learning models with individualized complex and SRP PCs to decode biological sex (Fig. 1c)53. Second, we predicted behavioral phenotypes with individual-level CPCA-derived patterns by using sparse canonical correlation analysis (sCCA) (Fig. 1d)54,55.

Additionally, to explicitly test whether cerebellar spatiotemporal patterns reflect information that is non-redundant with, rather than assumed to be distinct from, cortical and whole-brain dynamics, we applied the identical CPCA pipeline at multiple levels. Specifically, parallel analyses were performed on rs-fMRI data from the cerebellum alone, the cortex alone, and the whole-brain, enabling direct, quantitative comparisons of how spatiotemporal structure is expressed under cerebellum-specific, cortex-dominated, and global decompositions2,56. These multi-level decompositions enabled systematic comparisons of group-level spatiotemporal patterns, as well as downstream sex classification and behavioral prediction analyses across cerebellar, cortical, and whole-brain representations.

Three prominent spatiotemporal patterns of the cerebellum reflect FC topographies

We applied CPCA to cerebellar resting-state BOLD signals in the group-level dataset of the HCP-YA subset 1 (n = 100) and decomposed the signals into a set of complex-valued principal components (Fig. 2). The real part of each PC (complex PC) reflected the dominant spatial organization of intrinsic cerebellar activity, highlighting how functional territories were spatially arranged and co-fluctuate in amplitude. The phase angle (i.e., the angle between the real and imaginary parts of each PC) of the same PC represented the relative phase delay, indicating the sequential ordering of signal propagation through the cerebellum. These two representations were complementary aspects of the same underlying PC: the complex PC captured the spatial architecture of intrinsic activity, while the phase delay PC depicted its temporal structure over time, together providing a unified description of intrinsic cerebellar spatiotemporal dynamics.

The first complex PC accounted for 47.68% of the explainable variance in cerebellar BOLD signals, capturing the dominant axis of spontaneous amplitude fluctuations (Fig. 2a). It highlighted regions with the strongest spontaneous amplitude fluctuations, most prominently in Crus I/II, lobule VII B, and adjacent parts of lobule VI. These areas are key regions that are mapped to cerebral transmodal association networks, and suggest high endogenous activity in cognitive-emotional subsystems57,58. By contrast, the anterior lobe and vermis exhibit much lower amplitudes, consistent with lower spontaneous engagement of the sensorimotor region during rest58.

The second complex PC accounted for 16.62% of the explainable variance in cerebellar BOLD signals, capturing a spatially anti-correlated pattern between distinct functional territories (Fig. 2b). The real-valued map reveals lower amplitudes primarily in bilateral Crus I/II regions, which are consistently implicated in cerebral transmodal association networks supporting abstract reasoning and internal state representation. In contrast, higher amplitudes were expressed in lobules VI, VII B, VIII A, VIII B, IX, and parts of the vermis. Specifically, the observed distribution suggests temporally reciprocal fluctuations between cerebellar regions linked to transmodal association networks (e.g., Crus I/II) and those associated with sensorimotor processing (e.g., lobules VI–VIII, vermis). This antagonistic interplay may reflect a cerebellar instantiation of the large-scale coordination between internally and externally directed processes, akin to the canonical dynamics observed in the cortex6,33.

The third complex PC accounted for 8.37% of the explainable variance in cerebellar BOLD signals, capturing a spatially asymmetric activation feature (Fig. 2c). The real-valued map reveals higher amplitudes in the partial right Crus I/II and a small region of left Crus II, while lower amplitudes dominate the symmetrical position of left Crus I/II. This hemispheric dissociation suggested lateralized fluctuations within regions that are mapped to cerebral transmodal association circuits, potentially reflecting functional asymmetries linked to cognitive modulation59,60.

Together, these three components captured 72.67% of the explainable variance in cerebellar BOLD signals (Fig. 2d), far exceeding the variance explained by subsequent components. The sharp drop in variance beyond PC3, combined with the spatial interpretability of the first three PCs, supports focusing on the first three PCs as a parsimonious basis for subsequent analyses. These components thus provide a compact yet comprehensive representation of the major spatiotemporal patterns in intrinsic cerebellar dynamics.

As demonstrated by Bolt et al.2, the real values of three spatiotemporal patterns in the cortex primarily reflect standing-wave-like spatial structure, capturing zero-lag functional connectivity topographies rather than time-lagged propagation patterns. Then, we investigated the fundamental properties of CPCA-derived patterns, and compare the spatial similarity between the first three complex PCs with a range of empirical functional connectivity topographies. These included spatial ICA (SICA)61, temporal ICA (TICA)62, hidden Markov models (HMM)63, eigenmaps64, functional connectivity gradients8. We constructed similarity matrices based on spatial correlation between maps of complex PCs and maps from each empirical functional connectivity topography. We found that the vast majority of functional connectivity topography were strongly matched by one or more of the first three complex PCs (|r| > 0.5, pmoran < 0.05) (Fig. 2e). Statistical significance of the spatial correlation between each empirical FC topography and its correlated complex PC was computed using Moran permutation tests (see the Methods for more details)64. In addition, comparisons with the time-shuffled null model (Supplementary Fig. 1a) further supported the interpretation of the complex PCs as reflecting biologically meaningful spatial organization rather than temporal structure, since their spatial patterns remained largely preserved under temporal randomization. Overall, these results revealed a considerable consistency in empirical FC topographies with maps of complex PCs.

To directly assess whether cerebellar complex PCs reflect information that is unique to the cerebellum or merely a redundant representation of global cortical dynamics, we performed a systematic comparison across CPCA decompositions constructed in the cortex alone, the cerebellum alone, and the whole-brain (Supplementary Fig. 2). When CPCA was applied to the whole-brain, cortical patterns closely resembled those obtained from cortex alone decompositions, whereas the corresponding cerebellar patterns appeared spatially smoother and less well differentiated than those derived from the cerebellum alone. This suggests that variance dominated by large-scale cortical dynamics may obscure the finer-grained, cerebellum-specific spatial structure. These findings indicate that performing dimensionality reduction on the cerebellum alone can more effectively preserve its intrinsic spatiotemporal dynamics, providing a principled basis for subsequent comparisons of group-level patterns and individual-level analyses.

Phase delay components capture temporal propagation patterns

The first three phase delay PCs revealed coherent temporal propagation across cerebellar territories, capturing how intrinsic activity unfolds in an orderly cyclic sequence through functional networks, consistent with the cerebellum’s role in coordinating distributed sensorimotor and cognitive processes. To further characterize these temporal motifs, we mapped phase values onto 7 canonical network parcellations and visualized their distribution65,66 and revealed two characteristic temporal organizations: one represented by PC1 which showed a continuous gradient of propagation across the cerebellum, and another represented by phase delay PC2 and PC3, which exhibited two-phase opposition patterns broadly separating functional ensembles (Fig. 3). Together, these three phase patterns delineate a low-dimensional yet functionally rich basis for cerebellar temporal dynamics, capturing complementary propagation motifs that structure intrinsic activity into cyclic, behaviorally relevant patterns.

The first phase delay PC revealed a temporal gradient across cerebellar lobules (Fig. 3a). Lower phase delay appeared in posterior-inferior regions such as lobule IX, VIII A, VIII B, and parts of the vermis, whereas higher phase delays were observed in anterior-lateral lobules, particularly Crus I and Crus II. When aggregated by large-scale functional networks, the phase delay reflected a progression from unimodal (visual, VS; somatomotor, SM) toward transmodal (frontoparietal, FP; default mode, DM; dorsal attention, DA; ventral attention, VA; limbic, LM) territories58,67. This directionality aligned with established cortical hierarchies6,15 and suggested a temporal sequencing mechanism in the cerebellum whereby neural activity propagates from early sensory-motor regions to higher-order association regions, supporting cerebellar role in predictive integration across internal models68.

The second phase delay PC did not exhibit a spatial gradient but instead showed a sharp transition between two spatially distinct phases (Fig. 3b). Lobules VI, VII B, VIII A, and VIII B exhibited the lower phase, while Crus I/II and lobule IX exhibited the higher phase. The discontinuity suggested a phase-locked anti-synchrony between two functional ensembles: one corresponding to sensorimotor-attention systems and the other to transmodal association networks. Rather than reflecting traveling dynamics, this phase structure revealed an opposition in bistable or reciprocal modes of spontaneous cerebellar activity, supporting competitive resource allocation or alternating internal-external attentional states16,19.

The third phase delay PC exhibited a clear cross-hemispheric asymmetric phase delay structure divided into two parts (Fig. 3c). The higher phase mainly appeared in the left Crus I/II and left lobule VII B, while the lower phase was found in right Crus I, lobule IX, and posterior regions including lobule X. This asymmetric temporal structure indicated that some spontaneous activity may originate in the left cerebellar hemisphere and propagate toward the right, suggesting interhemispheric coordination across distributed transmodal circuits6,30. Rather than a strictly hierarchical unimodal-transmodal progression, this pattern reflected a laterality-sensitive temporal architecture, possibly shaped by affective-cognitive lateralization69.

We further compared the phase pattern to two widely used representations of intrinsic brain dynamics: lag projection and the QPP18,33. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 3, the phase delay map of the first complex PC exhibited a striking spatial similarity with both the lag projection (r = 0.971, pmoran < 0.001) and QPP (r = 0.773, pmoran < 0.001), two independent methods for estimating of large-scale time-lag structure. The lag projection reflected voxel-wise delays relative to the global BOLD signal, while QPP identified recurring spatiotemporal motifs in resting-state spontaneous BOLD activity. The marked correspondence between these patterns indicated that the first phase PC extracted by CPCA captures a canonical, temporally coherent propagation mode, consistent across model-free and data-driven approaches. This cross-method convergence provided compelling evidence that CPCA effectively isolated meaningful temporal flow in the cerebellum and established a valid low-dimensional basis for analyzing dynamic brain organization. Importantly, considering that brain BOLD signals contain both 1/f aperiodic component as well as physiological signals that introduce more periodic structure—such as systemic low-frequency hemodynamic oscillations (~0.01–0.1 Hz) and other physiological rhythms—we further evaluated whether CPCA could effectively capture concise patterns of synchrony. Simulations using synthetic signals with matched 1/f spectral properties showed that CPCA did not generically recover stable phase delay structure in aperiodic signals, supporting the interpretation that the phase delay patterns observed here reflect structured spatiotemporal periodic organization in the cerebellum (Supplementary Fig. 4)22,70.

Phase delay maps of null models by temporally shuffling the timepoints were markedly altered (Supplementary Fig. 1b), particularly for PC1, highlighting their dependence on the temporal sequence of the BOLD signals. This disruption confirmed that PC1 captured a wave-like propagation structure that was highly sensitive to fine-grained temporal organization, consistent with the interpretation of traveling dynamics2,71. The shuffling phase delay maps of PC2 and PC3 remained visually similar to those derived from the original data. This apparent stability suggested that PC2 and PC3 reflected both time-lagged and spatially defined zero-lag correlation structure, with random shuffling largely eliminating the time-lagged correlations while preserving much of the zero-lag structure. For instance, the anti-synchronous mode in PC2 and the hemispheric asymmetry in PC3 were plausibly anchored in a zero-lag correlation structure that remains largely intact under random shuffling. This dissociation indicated that, although all phase delay PCs encoded temporally structured patterns, their differential susceptibility to time shuffling delineated distinct temporal motifs, spanning fine-grained propagation gradients to coarse-grained zero-lag correlation structure2.

We also conducted analogous whole-brain comparisons for phase delay CPCA components to determine whether cerebellar temporal propagation patterns were uniquely expressed under cerebellum alone dimensionality reduction (Supplementary Fig. 5). Whole-brain phase delay PCs showed that cortical patterns closely resembled those observed in cortex alone analyses, whereas the corresponding cerebellar patterns exhibited greater spatial heterogeneity and reduced phase coherence compared with cerebellum alone results. Although computed from the same cerebellar voxels, these differences likely reflected the dominance of global cortical variance in the whole-brain decomposition, leading to partial averaging and attenuation of cerebellar-specific temporal organization. Collectively, these findings indicated that cerebellar phase delay structure was most clearly expressed when CPCA was performed within the cerebellum itself, rather than inferred from whole-brain decompositions.

CPCA-based reconstruction preserves functional connectivity modularity

In addition to the above interpretation of the spatial and temporal dynamic patterns of CPCA-derived PCs, we further revealed that the real-valued and phase-valued patterns jointly allow for reconstruction of the original BOLD signals (Fig. 4). By limiting the component dimensions in the singular value decomposition (SVD) results, CPCA preserved the major spatial and temporal patterns of cerebellar activity while emphasizing low-dimensional organizational structure rather than approximating the full functional connectivity matrix. As a result, the reconstructed signals captured the core modular and antagonistic organization observed in the original data, while individual connection strengths may differ, yielding moderate correspondence at the edge-wise level despite preservation of network-level structure. This compact representation, therefore, provided a physiologically interpretable description of the dominant organizational scaffold underlying cerebellar functional interactions.

The reconstructed rsFC matrix not only retained the core modular structure observed in the original data (Pearson’s r = 0.474, p < 0.05) but also exhibited higher overall connectivity values. b Modularity partition maps derived from the original (left) and reconstructed (right) rsFC matrices. Despite dimensionality reduction, the reconstructed signals faithfully reproduced the major functional subdivisions of the cerebellum (NMI = 0.94). Source data are provided as a Source Data file.

The reconstructed time series yielded a rsFC matrix that closely mirrored the original correlation structure (Fig. 4a). Although the absolute correlation between original and reconstructed rsFC was moderate (Pearson’s r = 0.474, p < 0.05), the major patterns of positive coupling were preserved, indicating that CPCA retains the topological layout of cerebellar interactions even after dimensionality reduction. Beyond pairwise correlations, the reconstructed rsFC maintained the modular organization of the cerebellum (Fig. 4b). Louvain community detection revealed a nearly identical module partition to the original data (NMI = 0.939)72, suggesting that the low-dimensional reconstruction preserves the mesoscale network structure across cerebellar territories. Given that the within-mode rsFC reconstruction showed limited similarity to the original rsFC, we repeated the analysis in a larger, higher-quality HCP-YA 7 T cohort comprising 170 participants (HCP-YA subset 2). The same three dominant CPCA patterns re-emerged, modular organization was preserved, and the correspondence between the original rsFC and the reconstructed rsFC improved relative to the 3 T data (Supplementary Fig. 6). Together, these results suggested that the moderate correspondence was partly attributable to SNR limitations in cerebellar signals; however, even with enhanced SNR and a larger sample, gained in functional connectivity reconstruction remain limited. In summary, the reconstruction demonstrated that CPCA yields a decomposable and interpretable architecture for cerebellar BOLD fluctuations, in which spatial and temporal structure were orthogonally decomposed and sparsely recomposed.

Cross-dataset validation confirms the stability of spatiotemporal dynamics

To determine whether the cerebellar spatiotemporal patterns identified from resting-state data generalized to task-evoked brain activity, we examined CPCA-derived PCs using task fMRI data within the HCP-YA subset 1 (Supplementary Figs. 7 and 8)73. Across multiple tasks, both complex PC1 and phase delay PC1 showed highly preserved spatial organization and temporal ordering, closely resembling the patterns observed during rest2,18. This consistency suggests that PC1 reflected a core cerebellar propagation mode that was largely invariant to cognitive context. In contrast, PC2 and PC3 exhibited increasing sensitivity to task demands. PC2 demonstrated moderate task-related modulation, characterized by task-dependent changes in relative network contributions and temporal coordination, while retaining its overall antagonistic structure17,74,75. Notably, PC3 showed the strongest task dependence, with pronounced alterations in both spatial amplitude distribution and phase delay organization across tasks. These task-specific deviations indicated that PC3 captured context-dependent cerebellar dynamics that were more tightly coupled to ongoing cognitive processes27. Together, these results revealed a hierarchical organization of cerebellar spatiotemporal patterns, in which PC1 represented stable, state-invariant dynamics, whereas PC2 and PC3 encoded flexible, task-sensitive modes of cerebellar processing.

To assess the generalizability of the identified cerebellar spatiotemporal patterns, we further performed two complementary validation analyses: (1) a test-retest validation across 7T scanner field strengths within the HCP-YA subset 2, and (2) an independent replication with the HCP-D subset. These two subsets were processed using an identical pipeline from the discovery subset (HCP-YA subset 1), including rs-fMRI data preprocessing and CPCA decomposition of rs-fMRI signals into complex and phase delay PCs. First, we evaluated reproducibility across scanner field strengths using a validation cohort of 170 subjects in the HCP-YA subset 2 with matched-duration rs-fMRI acquisitions at both 3T and 7T. Applying identical CPCA pipelines separately to the two datasets yielded highly consistent group-level complex and phase delay components, indicating strong robustness of the dominant cerebellar spatiotemporal patterns across field strengths (Supplementary Fig. 9). Second, we assessed generalizability using an independent cohort from the HCP-D dataset. Despite differences in age range and acquisition parameters, both complex and phase delay PCs closely resembled those identified in the discovery dataset, preserving large-scale spatial gradients, temporal organization, and modular structure (Supplementary Figs. 10–12).

To further evaluate the developmental influence, we explored age effects within the HCP-D subset using a linear mixed-effects model (LMM) controlling for sex, head motion, and education background76. Before performing LMM, we first transformed each individualized phase delay PC into a sine relative phase (SRP) representation to enable cross-subject comparisons (Supplementary Fig. 13)52,71,77. SRP mapped the phase angle onto a continuous linear domain while preserving the relative ordering of activity across cerebellar territories, overcoming the difficulty in interpreting and applying it in regression. This transformation avoided discontinuities at ±π boundaries and facilitates consistent group-level averaging and statistical testing. Importantly, SRP retained the sequence information encoded in the original phase delay, thereby providing a robust basis for subsequent validation and individual-level analyses. We found that both complex PCs and SRP PCs showed age-related trends that were consistent with those observed in the group-level patterns (Supplementary Fig. 14). As age increases, complex PC1 showed a posterior-to-anterior shift characterized by increased age effect in the SM, VA, DA networks and decreased age effect in the FP and DM networks, but no obvious significant trend was observed in SRP PC1. Complex PC2 showed a concentrated decrease trend except for the SM and LM networks, while SRP PC2 showed a contrary changing trend of age effect pointed in complex PC1. Both complex and SRP PC3 showed largely lateralization with positive changes in the left FP and DM networks, and negative changes in the right hemisphere. These findings indicated that while the dominant spatiotemporal patterns of the cerebellum were robust across different development stages, gradual refinement in both spatial and temporal components may reflect ongoing maturation of cerebro-cerebellar circuits during adolescence.

Sex differences are detected by machine learning methods

To explore whether the spatiotemporal dynamics of the cerebellum carry stable biological information, we performed sex classification analyses based on CPCA-derived patterns. Initially, we selected a subset of subjects from the HCP-YA subset 3 meeting strict quality control: unrelated individuals with balanced sex and age distribution (140 males, 140 females) and no significant group differences in head motion (t = 0.042, p = 0.967). We selected the first three complex PCs as well as SRP PCs as features to train machine learning classifiers, including linear support vector machines (LSVM) and artificial neural networks (ANNs). All models were optimized via grid search and evaluated using 5-fold cross-validation78,79. To validate the significance of models, we achieved the χ2 test across sexes and null model shuffling the labels of subjects for 1000 iterations.

Specifically, SRP PC1 yielded a relatively low classification accuracy, with an area under the ROC curve (AUC) of 0.71 (Fig. 5a, left). The confusion matrix indicated balanced classification performance between sexes, with the χ² test confirming statistical significance beyond chance (p < 0.001), and the permutation-based null model distribution (p < 0.001) further ruled out random classification (Fig. 5a, middle). To further understand which cerebellar networks contributed most to sex classification, we analyzed the average LSVM weights across the 7 network parcellations for sex predictions separately (Fig. 5a, right). Across both sexes, the VS and SM networks exhibited the largest contributions, indicating that sensorimotor systems were the most informative regions for sex differentiation based on SRP PC1. In contrast, the FP and DM networks contributed the least, suggesting that higher-order transmodal regions affected relatively less for sex classification. When disaggregated by sex, female-predictive weights (striped bars) were strongest in the VS and SM networks. In contrast, male-predictive weights (solid bars) were most prominent in the LM, VA, and DA networks. This spatial divergence of the weight map corresponded to the pattern of phase delay PC1, which exhibited a coherent posterior-to-anterior propagation gradient across the cerebellum. This classification performance was also confirmed by the ANN model, which achieved comparable accuracy and statistical significance (Supplementary Fig. 15a).

SRP PC2 achieved moderate classification performance (AUC = 0.77) (Fig. 5b, left). The confusion matrix, χ² test (p < 0.001), and permutation null model (p < 0.001) further demonstrated balanced classification performance and confirmed statistical significance (Fig. 5b, middle). SRP PC3 yielded the highest classification accuracy (AUC = 0.87) (Fig. 5c, left). The confusion matrix revealed strong diagonal dominance with minimal misclassification, and statistical significance was confirmed by both χ² testing (p < 0.001) and permutation null model (p < 0.001) (Fig. 5c, middle). As for network-level, the weight map of SRP PC3 exhibited a relatively uniform profile across cerebellum expect the VS network (Fig. 5c, right). On the male prediction side (solid bars), classifier weights were sharply concentrated in the VS network, with all other networks contributing at relatively similar and lower levels. This suggested that VS network, possibly linked to internal-perceptual loops, played an important role in identifying males, consistent with a focused and efficient classifier representation. In contrast, female-predictive weights (striped bars) were more broadly distributed and uniform across networks, with the LM and SM networks contributing most, followed by the DA and VA networks. This relatively uniform distribution of classifier weights across networks may reflect the lateralized nature of the phase delay PC3, where activity propagated asymmetrically across hemispheres. Such asymmetry likely weakened the influence of traditional network boundaries, causing the classifier to draw on information dispersed across multiple systems rather than concentrated within specific modules. These high-accuracy results were further validated by the ANN classifier, which yielded similar performance and statistical reliability (Supplementary Fig. 15c).

The classification performance of the first three complex PCs was shown in Supplementary Figs. 16 and 17, classification accuracies of each complex PC based on LSVM and ANN were moderate, with AUC values ranging from 0.63 to 0.73 and their statistically significant above chance as confirmed by χ² testing and permutation-based null models. This likely reflected the fact that complex PCs mainly capturing zero-lag spatial structures were effective but less sensitive to sex differentiation than phase delay maps.

Given the typical sex differences in cerebellar volume80, we further examined whether volumetric effects could account for the observed classification performance80. First, we demonstrated that males exhibited significantly larger total cerebellar volume than females (p < 0.001; Supplementary Fig. 18a). Then, we divided males and females into three groups based on cerebellar volume: larger, medium, and smaller total cerebellar volume groups. We trained machine learning models within each sex group to classify the larger and smaller total cerebellar volume groups. We found that the classification performance was near chance for both male and female groups (accuracy range: 0.443–0.586; Supplementary Fig. 18b). These results indicated that cerebellar volume did not provide sufficient information for reliable CPCA-based classification within sex.

To further assess whether the observed sex classification performance reflects cerebellar unique information, we compared classification accuracy obtained from CPCA-derived patterns in cerebellum alone, cortex alone, and whole-brain decompositions, as well as cerebellar patterns extracted from the whole-brain results (Supplementary Fig. 19). Across most of the patterns, whole-brain CPCA achieved the highest accuracy, consistent with its access to the most comprehensive signal variance. Importantly, cerebellum alone CPCA features generally outperformed cortex alone features and cerebellar features extracted from the whole brain in sex classification, particularly for SRP PC2 and PC3. These results indicated that cerebellar spatiotemporal patterns contained discriminative information that was not redundant relative to cortical signals, and that this cerebellar specificity could be attenuated by whole-brain-level decompositions.

To examine whether cerebellar spatiotemporal dynamics represent behaviorally relevant individual differences, we used sCCA to link CPCA-derived features to a broad range of behavioral phenotypes81,82. Specifically, for complex PCs and SRP PCs, we selected the top 10% most variable voxels across n = 335 individuals (HCP-YA subset 4) as predictors. For behavioral data, we focused on 35 measures spanning 4 behavioral categories, including alertness, cognition, emotion, and personality from the HCP-YA behavioral data (Supplementary Table 1). Then, we applied PCA to 35 behavior measures to reduce dimensionality and finally retained eight orthogonal behavioral components representing distinct phenotypic axes based on component weights (Fig. 6a and Supplementary Fig. 20).

Across both complex and SRP PCs, cerebellum alone patterns consistently achieved robust behavioral prediction performance that was comparable to or exceeded that of cortex alone and whole-brain features, and exhibited prediction profiles distinct from those obtained using the others.

Discussion

While most prior work has focused on cortical dynamics and has revealed structured propagation modes, the extent to which similar principles govern cerebellar dynamics, despite its increasingly recognized role in large-scale brain networks, remained unclear84. In particular, previous studies have rarely integrated spatial and temporal dimensions of intrinsic activity within a unified analytic framework, nor have they been extended to characterize individual-level variability in cerebellar dynamics. Here, we addressed these gaps by applying CPCA to rs-fMRI data, yielding three orthogonal real and imaginary components, low-dimensional PCs that jointly capture the spatial and temporal structure of intrinsic cerebellar activity15,84. These components recapitulated known FC approaches and modular partitions, while also revealing temporally structured propagation motifs along sensorimotor-to-transmodal and lateralized axes. Notably, individual-level expression of these PCs predicted sex and behavior traits, suggesting that cerebellar dynamics derived from CPCA provide a compact, biologically meaningful embedding of trait variability6. Together, these findings positioned the cerebellum as an active system in spontaneous brain activity, whose low-dimensional dynamics captured by CPCA reflected structured spatiotemporal patterns, thereby providing a compact and biologically meaningful embedding of individual-level trait variability.

Integrated spatial and temporal PCs reveal cerebellar functional specialization

Each of the three CPCA components uncovered distinct facets of cerebellar function, identifying regionally heterogeneous spatial functional structure and temporal propagation mode85. Importantly, the extracted components captured canonical dynamic forms found in distributed systems through generalizable dynamical principles86. Organizing cerebellar territories along distincted functional gradients and temporal propagation axes, three components form a set of orthogonal, low-dimensional, and sparsely distributed dynamic modes25. These patterns collectively revealed how cerebellar dynamics contributed to large-scale neural coordination. Importantly, this framework offered a unified spatiotemporal perspective for understanding how the cerebellum integrated domain-specific computations while embedding itself within the broader architecture of brain-wide propagation and behavioral individuation66. It was important to note that the advantage of CPCA overcame traditional dimensionality reduction methods lies in its ability to capture both zero-lag and time-lag synchrony patterns within a single framework, while offering a more parsimonious description of global functional brain organization2. This enabled the explanation of more variance with fewer latent components and allowed for more efficient associations with behavior and neurophysiology.

We found that the three spatiotemporal patterns demonstrated prominent features and collectively represented the spatial attributes of cerebellar functional fluctuations. Specifically, complex PC1 mapped a hierarchical gradient extending from posterior-inferior lobules (e.g., lobule IX) toward anterior-transmodal zones (e.g., Crus I/II), supporting widespread behavioral predictions and mirroring the known unimodal-to-transmodal organizational axis85. Phase delay PC1 appeared to reflect a propagation mode that originated in unimodal networks and flowed toward transmodal networks, potentially enabling sequential information integration akin to predictive processing hierarchies across the cerebellum6,87. Complex and phase delay PC2 highlighted a bistable-state pattern involving sharp anti-phase relationships between anterior sensorimotor and posterior transmodal regions—possibly reflecting competition between internally and externally oriented brain states, which in turn may underlie preparatory or attentional functions88. This functional interpretation aligned with the view that anti-synchronous organization embodied a dynamic switching mechanism orchestrating transitions between task-negative and task-positive brain states89. Both complex and phase delay PC3 revealed pronounced hemispheric asymmetry, particularly in Crus I/II and lobule IX. This asymmetry aligned closely with task-based functional lateralization observed in recent evidence. Specifically, regions involved in emotion, social cognition, and autobiographical memory were predominantly engaged in the left Crus I/II and lobule IX of the cerebellum90. These areas were found to correspond with right-hemisphere medial prefrontal and lateral temporal regions, which were known to play critical roles in emotional process and self-referential thinking46,91. Notably, the reciprocal connectivity between these cerebellar and cerebral regions underscored the integration of social and emotional learning, suggesting that the cerebellum was integral not only to motor control but also to higher-order cognitive functions related to emotional and social processing. This finding reinforced the framework of crossed cerebro-cerebellar specialization84,92 and supported the view that cerebellar asymmetry was not just anatomical but dynamically expressed in low-dimensional spatiotemporal patterns, tightly linked to behavioral phenotypes. Moreover, we found that CPCA-derived patterns accounted for the vast majority of prior observations in spontaneous BOLD signal fluctuations (Fig. 2e), suggesting that these three spatiotemporal patterns, captured within a unified framework, may reconcile models for both standing and traveling BOLD oscillations.

Temporal antagonism performs as a driver of intrinsic brain functional modularity

We further showed that the reconstruction based on PCs from CPCA not only preserved the modular structure of the original BOLD signals but also enhanced core features of rsFC, providing a high-fidelity, physiologically plausible representation of spontaneous activity69,72. Notably, PC2’s structure revealed reciprocal, anti-synchronous dynamics between transmodal and sensorimotor systems that are highly resemble of the modularity division. This temporal opposition suggested that some modular boundaries may arise not solely from correlated amplitude fluctuations but from phase-based bistability, hinting at a rhythmically toggled segregation of internal versus external processing states34,93. These findings provided converging evidence for the view that resting-state modularity could be shaped by temporally structured antagonistic propagation modes34,94. The alignment between PC2 and modular connectivity supported the idea that cerebellar co-activation was tightly coupled with the intrinsic organization95. Moreover, the spatiotemporal pattern observed in PC2, consistently presented in both the HCP-YA and HCP-D cohorts, closely parallels the coarse anterior-posterior functional parcellations in the cerebellum identified in children96, implying that the bistability reflected in PC2 may be rooted in early-established large-scale functional architecture. This convergence suggested that the cerebellar modular architecture was not only spatially embedded but also temporally orchestrated, and that stable spatial patterns were already established during adolescence.

Substrate for sex differentiation concentrates on CPCA-derived features

Using complex PCs and SRP PCs, we achieved robust sex classification performance in which SRP PC3, characterized by hemispheric asymmetry, showed the highest discriminability, likely stemming from its alignment with cerebellar circuits that were both anatomically and functionally sensitive to sex differences97. The classification outcomes of all PCs obtained through LSVM can be consistently reproduced in the ANN model and interpreted by accounting for the distinct spatiotemporal features captured by each PC. Temporal asymmetry revealed in SRP PC3 mirrored structural findings of sex differences in gray matter volume (GMV) and cortical thickness within lateralized cerebellar regions such as Crus I/II47. This functional lateralization likely reflected more than transient activation asymmetries, for example, fetal single-cell epigenomic and spatial transcriptomic data indicated that the cerebellum’s hemispheric specialization was scaffolded by early lineage allocation and regional specialization98,99. These embryonic organizational gradients plausibly set the stage for hemisphere-specific propagation trajectories (e.g., SRP PC3) expressed in adult cerebellar dynamics. Longitudinal MRI of early postnatal development further revealed persistent sex differences in lobular GMV growth rates, particularly in right Crus II/VIIA, VIII, and anterior lobules, with volumetric trajectories diverging before 2 years of age100,101. This pattern resonated with population-level evidence that cerebellar-cortical networks showed sex-specific lateralization102. Importantly, supplementary analyses controlling for total cerebellar volume indicated that the observed sex-classification performance cannot be explained by gross volumetric differences alone. This suggested that sex-related differences in cerebellar organization were reflected more strongly in spatiotemporal dynamics than in anatomical size. Together, these findings suggested that the asymmetric propagation captured by complex and SRP PC3 arised from foundational developmental architectures, reinforced by sex-linked volumetric and network-level divergence, providing a robust substrate for the adult cerebellar lateralization observed in functional imaging, further supporting the notion that cerebellar spatiotemporal organizational coordination contributed to understanding large-scale functional connectivity.

Low-dimensional cerebellar dynamics reflect multivariate behavioral architecture

Different CPCA components predicted distinct behavioral dimensions, including emotion regulation, cognition function, personality, and alertness, which supported its role in coordinating cognitive and emotional processes86,103. Notably, these behavioral associations were captured by both SRP PCs and complex PCs. Leveraging both representations offered a more comprehensive characterization of trait information, as they captured complementary aspects of cerebellar dynamics. These supported the notion that cerebellar dynamics appeared to contribute complementary information beyond cortical features, aligning with recent work showing additive predictive power of subcortical dynamics in behavioral prediction48,49. It was important to note that these spatiotemporal dynamics and their association with cognition were preserved across task-evoked fMRI conditions (Supplementary Figs. 7 and 8).

The behavioral dimensions associated with cerebellar spatiotemporal patterns spanned four broad functional domains, which differed in their degree of sensitivity to cognitive state as revealed by task fMRI analyses. First, emotion-related traits (e.g., emotion regulation and fear) emerged as a particularly complex dimension, requiring the joint contribution of multiple cerebellar spatiotemporal patterns that included both state-invariant and state-sensitive components. SRP PC1 was associated with general affect regulation and alertness, suggesting that global propagation gradients supported sustained emotional tone and state monitoring78,93. Consistent with this interpretation, SRP PC1 exhibited high stability across task conditions. While SRP PC3 predicted fear-related traits and highlighted a role for hemispheric asymmetry in threat processing59. In parallel, complex PC2 was linked to aggressive behavior, possibly reflecting bistable dynamics between externally triggered action and internal emotion control104, with moderate task-related modulation consistent with its intermediate hierarchical position. Second, cognitive functions such as working memory and language comprehension were primarily associated with SRP PC1 and PC3, emphasizing the cerebellum’s integrative role in coordinating domain-general cognitive performance, as well as the underlying basis for cognitive lateralization105,106. Notably, PC3 predicting cognition regulation also showed the strongest task-dependent modulation, indicating sensitivity to cognitive demands. Third, alertness regulation showed specific associations with the unimodal-to-transmodal axis of SRP PC1, reinforcing the cerebellum’s involvement in sustaining arousal and global awareness15,107. Finally, personality traits were linked to SRP PC1, suggesting that stable personality configurations may be reflected by global cerebellar propagation gradients108,109. These associations supported a model in which the cerebellum contributed complementary but non-redundant behavioral signatures, rooted in distinct spatiotemporal dynamics that spanned both stable trait representations and flexible state-dependent modulations93. Crucially, the analytic framework that linked CPCA-derived features with behavioral components via sCCA could offer an interpretable and scalable approach to uncover how low-dimensional neural motifs underpinned multi-domain behavioral phenotypes110. Unlike traditional connectivity-based models, our framework mapped abundant spatiotemporal patterns onto different behavioral axes, respectively. Meanwhile, task-evoked activation data further indicated that CPCA-derived components reconfigure—either in their spatial topographies or in the variance they accounted for—as a function of cognitive-state loading. Integrating evidence from behavioral prediction and sex classification, our findings supported a unified interpretation in which CPCA-derived components encoded both trait and state via complementary mechanisms: spatial patterns primarily reflected trait-like constraints imposed by individual-specific anatomy and physiology, whereas temporal dynamics indexed state-dependent modulation driven by arousal, cognition, and emotion.

Limitations and future directions

This work has several limitations that point to promising directions for future research. First, although CPCA combined with Hilbert-based phase estimation can recover coherent spatiotemporal propagation patterns in empirical cerebellar BOLD data, this approach was not expected to detect phase delays in aperiodic signals. To clarify the methodological limits, we performed simulations using synthetic signals with 1/f spectral characteristics matched to fMRI BOLD signal, in which controlled phase delays were imposed. These simulations showed that CPCA did not reliably recover the imposed phase structure in generic 1/f signals, indicating that the method is not a general-purpose phase detector for aperiodic data. Instead, CPCA may preferentially capture dominant physiological signals that introduce more periodic structure, such as systemic low-frequency hemodynamic oscillations and other physiological rhythms. This constraint strengthened the interpretation that the phase delay patterns observed in the cerebellum reflected structured neural dynamics rather than trivial consequences of broadband spectral properties. Second, although the top three CPCA-derived PCs captured over 70% of the explainable variance, they inevitably discarded part of the original signal. While low-dimensional representation enhanced interpretability and generalization, it came at the cost of reduced completeness. Higher-order or residual components may encode finer-grained temporal dynamics or subject-specific idiosyncrasies. Third, CPCA was an inherently linear decomposition technique, optimized for capturing global oscillatory modes under stationary assumptions. While it offered computational efficiency and theoretical clarity, linearity may obscure nonlinear or nonstationary features such as rapid state switching or phase resetting23. Future work could concentrate on recurrent neural architectures, high-order connections, or time-varying extensions to better model transient and nonlinear patterns embedded in cerebellar signals111,112.

Collectively, our study proposed that the cerebellum functions as parsimonious spatiotemporal patterns, expressing low-dimensional propagation modes that jointly depict network structural, biological and behavioral individuality. These patterns, characterized by hierarchical gradients, reciprocal modules, and hemispheric asymmetries, not only reflected internal cerebellar architecture but also supported trait-like features predictions. This work motivated new brain-wide models in which the cerebellum was not a passive relay, but an active, dynamic organizer of integrative and individualized brain states.

Methods

Experimental design

Resting-state fMRI data from the Human Connectome Project (HCP) in Young Adult (HCP-YA) and in Development (HCP-D) datasets were included in this study to be analyzed with complex principal component analysis (CPCA), focusing exclusively on cerebellar spatiotemporal dynamics. We further specified three aims: (1) to extract and validate dominant spatiotemporal components against known functional connectivity topographies; (2) to test the reproducibility and modularity-preserving capacity of these components; and (3) to assess their biological and behavioral relevance through sex classification and multivariate prediction of behavioral traits.

Participants and data acquisitions

Resting-state fMRI (rs-fMRI) data were obtained from the HCP-YA and HCP-D datasets113,114. All participants were healthy, unrelated individuals with no history of neurological or psychiatric disorders. Informed consent was obtained from all participants, and all procedures adhered to institutional and ethical guidelines.

For HCP-YA, we explicitly defined four HCP-YA subsets, each serving a distinct analytic purpose. Importantly, no arbitrary or random sampling was applied; all subsets were derived using explicit, reproducible criteria based on data completeness and quality control. The complete subject lists corresponding to each subset are provided in the released source code, enabling full reproducibility of all analyses.

HCP-YA subset 1 for group-level CPCA analysis

Group-level cerebellar CPCA PCs were derived from a subset of 100 unrelated subjects from the HCP-YA dataset (45 males; age range: 22–36 years). This subset was selected based on complete resting-state fMRI, task fMRI, and standard quality-control criteria, and was used exclusively to estimate group-level complex and phase delay PCs and associated functional topographies derived from traditional methods.

HCP-YA subset 2 for group-level CPCA replication in both 3T and 7T

To further assess the reproducibility of the identified cerebellar spatiotemporal patterns across scanner field strengths, we analyzed a test-retest validation cohort from the HCP-YA dataset. This cohort consisted of 170 subjects (65 males; age range: 22–36 years) who had matched-duration resting-state fMRI acquisitions at both 3T and 7T field strengths. Identical preprocessing and CPCA decomposition pipelines were applied separately to the 3T and 7T datasets, enabling direct comparison of group-level complex and phase delay PCs across field strengths.

HCP-YA subset 3 for subject-level sex classification

Sex classification analyses were conducted using a sex-balanced subset of 280 subjects (140 males; age range: 22–36 years), with no significant group differences in either age and head motion. Individualized CPCA-derived features from this subset were used for machine learning.

HCP-YA subset 4 for subject-level behavioral prediction

Behavioral prediction analyses were performed in a larger unrelated sample of 335 HCP-YA subjects (165 males; age range: 22–36 years) with complete behavioral assessments and resting-state imaging data115. Individualized CPCA-derived patterns extracted from this subset were used for multivariate brain-behavior association analyses.

All participants were scanned using a 3T MRI scanner (Siemens Prisma) while instructed to remain still with eyes open and fixate on a cross. Echo-planar imaging (EPI) was used for rs-fMRI acquisition with parameters including repetition time (TR) = 720 ms, echo time (TE) = 33.1 ms, flip angle = 52°, voxel size = 2 mm isotropic, and 72 slices. Each session included 1200 time points (14.4 min total), with two sessions in total for each subject. For the T1-weighted (T1w) images, acquisition parameters including TR = 2400 ms, inversion time (TI) = 1000 ms, TE = 2.14 ms, flip angle = 8°, 0.7-mm isotropic resolution with fat suppression were achieved using a non-selective inversion recovery pulse. Motion correction was not prospectively applied, but participants were carefully instructed to remain still during the scan. For the T2-weighted (T2w) images, parameters include TR = 3200 ms, TE = 565 ms, and 0.7-mm isotropic resolution with a variable flip angle turbo spin-echo sequence. Detailed 7T acquisition parameters can be found in Vu et al.116. As with T1w scans, no prospective motion correction was used in T2w. Both structural images were acquired at the beginning of each scanning session and were used for subsequent surface reconstruction and alignment steps in data preprocessing.

HCP-D subset for group-level CPCA replication

An independent sample of 100 subjects (43 males; age range: 8–21 years) from the HCP-D dataset was selected to validate the generalizability of the identified spatiotemporal patterns. All subjects were scanned using a 3T MRI scanner (Siemens Prisma) while instructed to remain still with eyes open and fixate on a cross. EPI parameters were used for rs-fMRI data acquisition: TR = 800 ms, TE = 37 ms, flip angle = 52°, voxel size = 2 mm isotropic, and 72 slices. Each scan included 478 time points (6.37 min total), with two sessions in total for each subject. For the T1w images, acquisition parameters including TR = 2500 ms, TI = 1000 ms, TE = 1.8, 3.6, 5.4, and 7.2 ms, 0.8-mm isotropic resolution, and flip angle = 8° with water excitation. Motion robustness was enhanced with prospective motion correction (vNavs), allowing up to 30 TRs for reacquisition in cases of head movement. For the T2w images, parameters include TR = 3200 ms, TE = 564 ms, 0.8-mm isotropic resolution with a turbo spin echo factor of 314. Up to 25 TRs were allowed for motion-induced reacquisition. As with T1w scans, vNavs were employed to improve image quality in the presence of motion.

MRI data preprocessing

For the HCP-YA dataset, we analyzed data which had undergone the minimal preprocessing pipeline. Importantly, our analyses focused exclusively on the cerebellum, and this was achieved by isolating cerebellar regions using the brain region labels available in the CIFTI-format data header. All rs-fMRI data underwent an additional four-step preprocessing pipeline to enhance signal quality and comparability across individuals. The preprocessing was implemented primarily on CIFTI-format data using a combination of Connectome Workbench tools and Python scripts. In line with the preprocessing strategy adopted by Bolt et al.2, global signal regression was not performed at any stage of the preprocessing.

Step 1. Each rs-fMRI run (REST2_LR and REST2_RL) was normalized to MNI space and smoothed using a Gaussian kernel with 2.12 mm full-width at half maximum (FWHM), via the Connectome Workbench instruction.

Step 2. To reduce inter-subject variability, voxel-wise cerebellar time series were standardized (z-score) across subjects for each time point.

Step 3. To increase temporal resolution and maximize usable data, voxel-wise cerebellar time series collected from two directions were merged into one session.

Step 4. To isolate low-frequency fluctuations, the time series were band-pass filtered between 0.01–0.1 Hz using a fifth-order zero-phase Butterworth filter. This step reduces scanner drift and high-frequency noise while preserving the temporal phase relationships critical for later analyses.

All additional preprocessing steps for the HCP-D were identical to those applied to the HCP-YA dataset to ensure methodological consistency and allow direct cross-dataset comparison.

Simulation of phase delay structure in BOLD-like 1/f signals

To examine the ability and limitations of Hilbert-based phase estimation combined with CPCA in aperiodic signals, we conducted simulations using synthetic time series with 1/f spectral characteristics matched to fMRI BOLD data. The simulation pipeline consisted of the following conceptual stages:

Step 1. Neural field generation with broadband temporal structure. We first simulated spatially extended neural activity using a two-dimensional Wilson-Cowan neural field model. To approximate the scale-free temporal structure of resting-state neural fluctuations, we injected stochastic inputs composed of a weighted mixture of Ornstein-Uhlenbeck (OU) processes spanning multiple time constants (1–100 s). The superposition of these processes yielded neural time series with stable 1/f-like spectral properties over the frequency range typically analyzed in resting-state neural activity without physiological periodic oscillations.

Step 2. Hemodynamic forward modeling to generate BOLD-like signals. The simulated neural activity was passed through an established Balloon-Windkessel hemodynamic model without modification117. This step introduced realistic hemodynamic smoothing, nonlinearity, and temporal filtering, resulting in continuous BOLD-like signals that closely matched empirical fMRI power spectra after downsampling to the fMRI repetition time.

Step 3. Spatial embedding and voxel-level signal construction. Neural field activity defined on a regular grid was interpolated onto cerebellar voxel coordinates derived from a real CIFTI template. This ensured that the spatial sampling, geometry, and voxel count of the simulated data were comparable to those used in the empirical analyses.

Step 4. Controlled introduction of spatially organized phase delays. To introduce a ground-truth propagation structure, voxel-wise time series were shifted according to a spatially smooth delay map defined along one anatomical axis. This procedure imposed a systematic wave-like delay pattern across voxels, while preserving the underlying broadband temporal statistics of the signals.

Time-shuffling null model

To assess whether CPCA components reflect meaningful spatiotemporal structure rather than artifacts of static correlation and hemodynamic convolution, we implemented a time-shuffling null model that selectively destroys temporal relationships while preserving convolution-induced temporal autocorrelation. Specifically, fMRI time series were first deconvolved using a canonical hemodynamic response function (HRF) to estimate latent neural signals. These deconvolved signals were then randomly shuffled 1000 times along the time dimension, thereby removing temporal continuity and time-lag structure at the neural level. The shuffled signals were subsequently reconvolved with the same HRF to restore the temporal autocorrelation structure introduced by hemodynamic filtering. After shuffling, the analytic signal was constructed as in the original pipeline, and CPCA was applied identically to extract complex components.

Comparison with traditional spatiotemporal models

To validate the biological plausibility of CPCA-derived spatiotemporal patterns, we compared real-valued and phase-valued patterns as vectors with several established methods of functional topography and dynamics. For complex PCs, we assessed spatial similarity with maps derived from spatial ICA (SICA)61, temporal ICA (TICA)62, Hidden Markov Models (HMM)63, diffusion-based gradients8, and eigenmaps64. For phase delay PCs, we compared against spatiotemporal templates extracted from quasi-periodic patterns (QPP)34 and lag projection18, both of which capture propagation-like features in resting-state BOLD activity. Correlation was computed using voxel-wise spatial Pearson’s correlation, and all methods were applied to the same preprocessed rs-fMRI data to ensure methodological consistency and fair comparison. To assess statistical significance of spatial correlations, permutation tests based on Moran randomization in BrainSpace Python package64, preserving spatial contiguity, were applied 1000 times on cerebellar maps. First, voxel coordinates for the cerebellar mask were extracted in MNI space, and a spatial weight matrix W was constructed based on k-nearest neighbors (k = 7 through optimization) in Euclidean space. Distances were converted to weights by inverse distance scaling and symmetrized, and the matrix was row-normalized to preserve relative connection strengths. Connectivity of the voxel graph was verified using connected component analysis to ensure a single connected component. For each randomization, the correlation between the permuted and empirical maps was recalculated to obtain a null distribution. Below are brief introductions to classic methods:

Spatial independent component analysis (SICA)

SICA decomposes the BOLD fMRI data into a set of spatially independent components, each associated with a unique time course. The method assumes that underlying sources (e.g., functional networks) are statistically independent in space. The decomposition is typically achieved using the FastICA algorithm from scikit-learn 1.6.1 applied to voxel-wise time series. The output is a set of spatial maps and associated time courses, where each spatial map reflects the contribution of a functional process across voxels.

Temporal independent component analysis (TICA)

TICA takes the transpose of the data matrix and assumes statistical independence across time instead of space. This method separates temporally distinct sources, i.e., patterns that recur at different time scales or phase relationships, even if their spatial maps overlap. TICA is useful for identifying temporally fluctuating sources like noise, oscillations, or transient functional modes. We achieve the decomposition through scikit-learn 1.6.1.

Hidden Markov models (HMM)

HMM models brain activity as a probabilistic sequence of hidden functional “states”, each defined by a unique multivariate covariance structure across voxels or regions. At each time point, the brain is assumed to be in one latent state, and transitions between states follow a Markov process. This model is typically fitted using the Expectation-Maximization (EM) algorithm from hmmlearn 0.2.7 on the time series data.

Diffusion-based gradients

This approach computes continuous spatial gradients of functional connectivity by applying diffusion map embedding to the functional connectivity matrix, achieving through BrainSpace Toolbox (v0.1.4) with diffusion map embedding parameters α = 0.5 and diffusion_time = 0 for gradients. The idea is to model how connectivity similarity “diffuses” across the space of voxels, revealing axes of functional variation. The first few gradients explain major axes of functional organization.

Eigenmaps

Eigenmaps are derived by performing spectral decomposition (e.g., PCA or Laplacian eigenmaps) through scikit-learn 1.6.1 on the functional connectivity matrix. The resulting eigenvectors capture the principal modes of spatial variation in functional connectivity, often interpreted as large-scale network organization axes.

Quasi-periodic patterns (QPPs)

QPPs are spatiotemporal motifs that recur intermittently in resting-state BOLD signals. They are extracted using a template-matching procedure (https://github.com/FCP-INDI/C-PAC/blob/master/CPAC/qpp/qpp.py) with a 20-TR window length, 50 iterations, and a correlation threshold of 0.3: a short segment of the time series is iteratively matched to similar segments throughout the scan, and the resulting matched segments are averaged to form a recurring pattern. The first QPP often resembles a large-scale propagating wave across the brain.

Lag projection

Lag projection estimates the average temporal delay of the BOLD signal arrival at each voxel relative to the rest of the brain (https://github.com/RaichleLab/lag-code). For each voxel, its BOLD time series is cross-correlated with all other voxels, and the lag corresponding to the maximum correlation is recorded. The voxel’s lag value is then computed as the mean of these pairwise peak-lag times. The resulting lag map reflects a unidirectional spatiotemporal propagation structure, where negative lag values indicate earlier activation and positive values indicate delayed response.

Cerebellar visualization

To enable standardized visualization, the group-level or individual-level cerebellar maps represented in CIFTI format were transformed into MNI standard space and subsequently projected onto a flat representation of the cerebellar surface using the SUIT toolbox119. The SUIT flatmap is based on volume-averaged anatomical data and constructed such that the surface area of each region is approximately proportional to the corresponding cerebellar gray-matter volume. This design ensures that closed clusters in the volume remain closed on the surface and that the entire cerebellar gray matter is represented in a single, interpretable 2D display.

The projection was achieved by sampling along lines connecting corresponding vertices on the inner white-matter and outer gray-matter surfaces, thereby assigning each surface vertex values from the underlying gray-matter voxels. For continuous functional maps, values were averaged across the sampled voxels, whereas for parcellation labels a winner-take-all (mode) assignment was used. This procedure enabled faithful visualization of both statistical maps and categorical parcellations at the group level on a standardized cerebellar flat surface.

Signal reconstruction and functional connectivity

The complex-valued principal components derived from CPCA can be used to reconstruct the original BOLD signal by multiplying the corresponding left singular vector U, singular value s, and conjugate transposed right singular vector V in singular value decomposition2,23. Afterwards, the neural propagation trajectory is described by dividing the phase into equidistant interval boxes, converting continuous oscillations into discrete time points, and then describing the spatial distribution and temporal dynamics of cerebellar activity. We visualized propagation as phase-coded trajectories by segmenting the continuous phase values into equidistant bins (8 intervals per cycle), thereby converting oscillatory activity into discretized temporal frames that reflect spatial propagation.

To test the generative capacity of CPCA, we reconstructed fMRI time series using the top 3 complex and phase delay components and computed rsFC matrices from the reconstructed signals. Pearson’s correlation coefficients were computed between voxel time courses and transformed using Fisher’s z. Community structure was estimated using modularity maximization (Louvain method), and overlap with original FC was assessed using normalized mutual information (NMI)72. Both of these calculations were implemented based on bctpy 0.6.1.

Sex classification

We conducted a classification analysis using LSVM and ANN from scikit-learn 1.6.1 to assess whether individual CPCA-derived patterns represent sex-specific information. A total of 280 subjects (HCP-YA subset 4) were selected, matched on age and head motion between sexes. Each subject’s complex or SRP PCs were vectorized and used as input to the classifier.

To reduce the feature dimensionality and focus on the most discriminative voxels, we performed univariate feature selection using the ANOVA F-statistic. The number of selected voxels was optimized through a grid search over proportional thresholds, ranging from all voxels to 1/6 of the voxels with larger F-values. Features were z-scored before classification.

For model performance evaluation, we used the average accuracy of 100 5-fold cross-validation to ensure robustness on the modest dataset. In each fold, the left-out subject was predicted after training the model on the remaining samples. We computed classification accuracy, confusion matrices, and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves with area under the curve (AUC) scores using the scikit-learn 1.6.1 package. To optimize hyperparameters, we conducted an inner grid search within each training fold. For LSVM, we varied the penalty parameter C from 10−4 to 10−3 in steps and retained the best-performing configuration per component. For ANN, hidden layer sizes from 50 to 200 units and dropout rates from 0 to 0.5. The voxel selection threshold was also co-optimized. The grid search containing 50 combinations was repeated for each fold, and the best configuration was selected based on classification performance on the validation set within each fold.

Cerebellar volume analysis

To assess whether sex classification performance was spuriously driven by cerebellar volume differences rather than sex-specific spatiotemporal dynamics, we performed complementary volume-based control analyses. Specifically, total cerebellar volume was computed for each participant by summing left and right cerebellar gray matter and white matter volumes provided by the HCP-YA dataset. Group-level sex differences in total cerebellar volume were evaluated using two-sample t-tests. To further examine whether cerebellar volume could account for classification performance, we conducted within-sex stratified analyses. For males and females separately, participants were rank-ordered by total cerebellar volume and divided into three equal-sized groups (larger, medium, and smaller). Classification analyses were performed between the larger and smaller groups within each sex.

Behavior data

We collected 35 behavioral scales covering four broad domains: emotion (e.g., fear, aggressive behavior), recognition (e.g., language comprehension, working memory), personality, and alertness (Supplementary Table 1). Each scale was standardized across subjects. To reduce dimensionality and account for collinearity, we performed principal component analysis (PCA) on the full behavioral matrix and retained the top 8 components, which collectively explained 67.64% of the total variance. Each subject’s behavioral profile was thus represented by an eight-dimensional latent vector.

Sparse canonical correlation analysis

To relate cerebellar phase patterns to individual behavioral traits, we employed sparse canonical correlation analysis (sCCA)120. Before sCCA, we applied a feature selection step on the cerebellar features derived from complex and phase delay PCs: for each voxel, we computed its median absolute deviation (MAD) across subjects and retained the top 10% most variable voxels55. MAD is a robust, order-based measure of dispersion that remains stable in the presence of outliers and under heavy-tailed or skewed distributions and heteroscedasticity—conditions commonly encountered in voxelwise fMRI feature selection. This ensured that only voxels exhibiting substantial inter-individual variability were included in subsequent analysis. For each subject, the CPCA-derived maps of top 3 components (including complex PCs and SRP PCs) were vectorized and concatenated into a feature matrix. sCCA was then used to identify pairs of canonical variates, including linear combinations of brain and behavior dimensions, which were maximally correlated under sparsity constraints. Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted in Python (version 3.9) using standard scientific computing libraries. Unless otherwise stated, two-sided tests were employed with a significance threshold of p < 0.05, corrected for multiple comparisons where applicable. Permutation tests (1000 iterations) were used for spatial correlation analyses between complex principal components (PCs) and empirical connectivity maps, with spatial autocorrelation preserved using Moran spectral randomization64. Functional connectivity reconstruction was evaluated by normalized mutual information (NMI) between original and reconstructed modular structures. For sex classification, accuracy and area under the ROC curve (AUC) were computed under 5-fold cross-validation; statistical significance was assessed by label permutation tests (1000 iterations). For behavioral prediction, sparse canonical correlation analysis (sCCA) was applied with grid-searched sparsity parameters, and significance was tested using permutation (1000 iterations) with false discovery rate (FDR) control at q < 0.05. The values of sample size, significance values, and the specific statistical test used for each analysis are reported in the main text or figure legends.
