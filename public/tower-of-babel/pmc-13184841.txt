Reply
Authors: Mao-Shin Lin, Hao-Yun Liu, Kuan-Yu Lin, Chiueng-Fang Wu, Wen-Jeng Lee
Published: 2026

We sincerely thank Dr Boukheloua and colleagues for their thoughtful comments on our recently published case report and for sharing their experience with delayed diagnosis of congenital aortopathies. Their observations further underscore the heterogeneous clinical presentation of congenital aortic anomalies and the important role of multimodal imaging in unveiling these complex conditions.

We fully agree that a single imaging modality may be insufficient to characterize intricate aortic arch variants. In our reported case, however, the right-side dominant double aortic arch with marked angulation and extreme vascular tortuosity not only represented a diagnostic finding but also directly influenced the technical feasibility and procedural risk stratification of transcatheter aortic valve replacement.1 In this setting, preprocedural computed tomography with three-dimensional reconstruction was critical, not merely to confirm the anatomical diagnosis, but to evaluate vascular access, arch curvature, device delivery trajectory, and potential procedural hazards.

Unlike the young adults described by the authors, our patient was elderly and had concomitant degenerative severe aortic stenosis. This intersection between congenital vascular anatomy and age-related valvular degeneration introduces an additional layer of complexity in structural heart intervention. As transcatheter aortic valve replacement is increasingly performed in older populations, previously unrecognized congenital aortic arch anomalies may be encountered more frequently during preprocedural planning. In such scenarios, extreme tortuosity and calcific burden may collectively affect device manipulation and embolic risk.

We therefore propose that multimodal imaging in these patients should be viewed not only as a diagnostic tool but also as an integral component of procedural planning and risk assessment. Detailed computed tomography–based analysis and 3-dimensional reconstruction may assist in optimizing device selection, access strategy, and anticipation of technical challenges, ultimately improving procedural safety in complex anatomical settings.2

We appreciate the authors for expanding the discussion on delayed diagnosis of congenital aortopathies. Continued dialog across cases will help refine our understanding of complex aortic anatomy in the evolving era of structural heart interventions.
