Generalizations of Frobenius-Schur indicators from Kuperberg invariants
Liang Chang, Siu-Hung Ng, Yilong Wang

Abstract
. We introduce an approach to produce gauge invariants of any finite-dimensional Hopf algebras from the Kuperberg invariants of framed 3-manifolds. These invariants are generalizations of Frobenius-Schur indicators of Hopf algebras. The computation of Kuperberg invariants is based on a presentation of the framed 3-manifold in terms of Heegaard diagram with combings satisfying certain admissibility conditions. We provide framed Heegaard diagrams for two infinite families of small genus 3-manifolds, which include all the lens spaces, and some homology spheres. In particular, the invariants of the lens spaces L ⁡ ( n , 1 ) L(n,1) coincide with the higher Frobenius-Schur indicators of Hopf algebras. We compute the Kuperberg invariants of all these framed 3-manifolds, and prove that they are invariants of the tensor category of representations of the underlying Hopf algebra, or simply gauge invariants.



1.
Introduction

The 2nd Frobenius-Schur (FS-) indicators
\nu_{2}(V)
for complex irreducible representations
V
of a finite group
G
were introduced more than a century ago
[
9
]
for studying the fundamental group of closed non-orientable surfaces
\Sigma
, namely

|{\operatorname{Hom}}(\pi_{1}(\Sigma),G)|=\frac{1}{|G|^{\mathcal{X}(\Sigma)-1}}\sum_{V\in{\operatorname{Irr}}(G)}(\nu_{2}(V)\dim(V))^{\mathcal{X}(\Sigma)}

where
{\operatorname{Irr}}(G)
denotes the set of complex irreducible representations of
G
, and
\mathcal{X}(\Sigma)
is the Euler characteristic of
\Sigma
. The Frobenius-Schur Theorem asserts that
\nu_{2}(V)\in\{0,1,-1\}
for any irreducible representation
V
and that
\nu_{2}(V)=1
(resp.
-1
) if
V
admits a nondegenerate
G
-invariant symmetric (resp. skew symmetric) bilinear form on
V
, and 0 otherwise. The notion of Frobenius-Schur (FS-)indicators was first generalized to any finite-dimensional representation of semisimple Hopf algebras
[
24
]
, and the corresponding Frobenius-Schur theorem was established therein. Around the same time, some generalizations of the 2nd FS-indicator were introduced for
C^{*}
-fusion categories
[
10
,
11
]
, semisimple quasi-Hopf algebras
[
27
]
, and pivotal tensor categories
[
32
]
.

Higher FS-indicators
\nu_{n}(V)
for any finite-dimensional representation
V
of a semisimple Hopf algebra
H
and positive integer
n
were also introduced in
[
24
]
, and were studied extensively in
[
15
]
. It was discovered in
[
27
,
33
]
that the
n
-th indicators, for any positive integer
n
, are invariants of the tensor category of representations of semisimple (quasi-)Hopf algebras. More precisely, if
H
and
H^{\prime}
are semisimple (quasi-)Hopf algebras over
{\mathbb{C}}
such that there exists an equivalence of tensor categories
{\mathcal{F}}:{\operatorname{Rep}}(H)\rightarrow{\operatorname{Rep}}(H^{\prime})
, then
\nu_{n}(V)=\nu_{n}({\mathcal{F}}(V))
for all
V\in{\operatorname{Rep}}(H)
. In particular, if the tensor categories of complex representations of finite groups
G_{1}
,
G_{2}
are equivalent, then any representation of
G_{1}
has the same indicators as its image in
{\operatorname{Rep}}(G_{2})
under the equivalence. The same statement holds for semisimple Hopf algebras.

The FS-indicator
\nu_{n}(H)
of the regular representation of
H
admits many interesting interpretations in various contexts. For example, if
H={\mathbb{C}}G
for a finite group
G
, then

\nu_{n}(H)=\#\{x\in G\mid x^{n}=1\}\,.

In particular,
\nu_{2}({\mathbb{C}}G)
is the number of involutions in
G
. In general, for any semisimple Hopf algebra
H
over
{\mathbb{C}}
,

\nu_{2}(H)={\operatorname{Tr}}(S)

where
S
is the antipode of
H
(cf.
[
15
, p.19]
). Moreover,
\nu_{n}(H)/\dim(H)
is the Reshetikhin-Turaev invariant of the lens space
L(n,1)
associated to the modular fusion category
{\operatorname{Rep}}(D(H))
, where
D(H)
is the Drinfeld double of
H
. If
{\operatorname{Rep}}(H)
and
{\operatorname{Rep}}(H^{\prime})
are equivalent fusion categories, then
{\operatorname{Rep}}(D(H))
and
{\operatorname{Rep}}(D(H^{\prime}))
are equivalent modular fusion categories (equipped with the canonical pseudounitary pivotal structure). Since the Reshetikhin-Turaev invariants of 3-manifolds are constructed from categorical data, it is immediate to see that
\nu_{n}(H)=\nu_{n}(H^{\prime})
. Thus, one can conclude that the FS-indicator
\nu_{n}(H)
is an invariant of the tensor categories
{\operatorname{Rep}}(H)
.

A formula for
\nu_{n}(H)
was obtained in terms of the integral and antipode of a semisimple Hopf algebra
H
for each positive integer
n
in
[
15
, p.19]
. However, the value of this formula, again denoted by
\nu_{n}(H)
, can be computed for any
nonsemisimple
finite-dimensional Hopf algebra
H
over any base field and each positive integer
n
. It was proved algebraically in
[
14
]
that
\nu_{n}(H)=\nu_{n}(H^{\prime})
if
{\operatorname{Rep}}(H)
and
{\operatorname{Rep}}(H^{\prime})
are equivalent as tensor categories which are not necessarily semisimple. Throughout this paper, we will simply call a quantity
f(H)
obtained from a finite-dimensional Hopf algebra
H
a
gauge invariant
if
f(H)=f(H^{\prime})
for any Hopf algebra
H^{\prime}
such that
{\operatorname{Rep}}(H)
and
{\operatorname{Rep}}(H^{\prime})
are equivalent tensor categories.

Analogous to the gauge invariance of
\nu_{n}(H)
for any semisimple Hopf algebras
H
over
{\mathbb{C}}
viewed as the Reshetikhin-Turaev invariant of lens space
L(n,1)
, one can consider the Hennings-Kauffman-Radford (HKR) invariants of closed oriented 3-manifolds
[
13
,
17
]
. The HKR invariant
Z_{\operatorname{HKR}}(M,H)
of a 3-manifold
M
is defined for any finite-dimensional unimodular ribbon factorizable Hopf algebra over
{\mathbb{C}}
(not necessarily semisimple), and is shown to be a gauge invariant of
H

[
3
, Thm. 2]
. In this paper, we explore the gauge invariance of another type of 3-manifold invariants, called the
Kuperberg invariants
, for any finite-dimensional Hopf algebra.

Given a finite-dimensional Hopf algebra
H
, Kuperberg introduced in
[
20
]
a topological invariant
K(M,\mathfrak{f},H)
for any closed oriented 3-manifold
M
equipped with a framing
\mathfrak{f}
. The 3-manifold
M
can be considered as a mapping class of the boundary surface
\Sigma
of some handlebody. The corresponding Heegaard diagram is a planar presentation of
\Sigma
decorated with the so-called upper and lower gluing curves determined by the mapping class corresponding to
M
(see Section
3
for details). The framing
\mathfrak{f}
is depicted in terms of a 2-dimensional vector field, called a
combing
, on the Heegaard diagram with a
twist front
satisfying the
admissible condition
(
3.1
), which is crucial to guarantee the correctness of the combinatorial data needed for computing
K(M,\mathfrak{f},H)
. One can construct from these combinatorial data an element
w\in H^{\otimes g}
and
w^{*}\in{H^{*}}^{\otimes g}
in terms of the integrals, the antipode and the distinguished grouplike elements of
H
and
H^{*}
, where
g
is the genus of
\Sigma
. The invariant
K(M,\mathfrak{f},H)
is a scalar in the base field of
H
obtained by evaluating
w
against
w^{*}
, see (
3.4
).
By
[
20
]
, the scalar
K(M,\mathfrak{f},H)
is independent of the choice of framed Heegaard diagrams of
(M,\mathfrak{f})
.
For example, it is shown in Corollary
4.14
that the lens space
L(n,1)
with a specific framing
\mathfrak{f}_{n}
has the Kuperberg invariant given by

K(L(n,1),\mathfrak{f}_{n},H)={\lambda}S\left(\sum_{({\Lambda})}\Lambda_{(1)}\Lambda_{(2)}\cdots\Lambda_{(n)}\right)

for any finite-dimensional Hopf algebra
H
with the antipode
S
, where
{\lambda}\in H^{*}
is a right cointegral and
{\Lambda}\in H
is a left integral such that
{\lambda}({\Lambda})=1
.
Here
\sum_{({\Lambda})}\Lambda_{(1)}\Lambda_{(2)}\cdots\Lambda_{(n)}
is the product of the
n
-folded comultiplication of
{\Lambda}
, or simply the
n
-th Sweedler power
{\Lambda}^{[n]}
of
{\Lambda}
. When
H
is ribbon factorizable over
{\mathbb{C}}
, the Kuperberg invariant for some lens spaces were computed in
[
2
]
.
This invariant of the framed 3-manifold
(L(n,1),\mathfrak{f}_{n})
for each positive integer
n
coincides with the aforementioned Frobenius-indicator
\nu_{n}(H)
for any finite-dimensional Hopf algebra
H
.
In particular, the Kuperberg invariant
K(L(n,1),\mathfrak{f}_{n},H)
is a gauge invariant of
H
, and this alludes to the following question:

Question 1.1
.

For any given framed 3-manifold
(M,\mathfrak{f})
, must the Kuperberg invariant
K(M,\mathfrak{f},H)
be gauge invariant for any finite-dimensional Hopf algebra
H
?

Inspired by the categorical invariance of the Frobenius-Schur indicator
\nu_{n,k}(V)
for any positive integers
n,k
and object
V
in a spherical fusion category
\mathcal{C}
, one can consider
\nu_{n,k}(\mathcal{C})=\sum_{V\in{\operatorname{Irr}}(\mathcal{C})}\dim(V)\nu_{n,k}(V)
, which is an invariant of spherical fusion categories
[
32
]
. When
\mathcal{C}={\operatorname{Rep}}(H)
for any semisimple Hopf algebra
H
over
{\mathbb{C}}
,
\nu_{n,k}(\mathcal{C})=\nu_{n,k}(H)
, which is a gauge invariant for semisimple Hopf algebras over
{\mathbb{C}}
. This observation motivates our investigation of Question
1.1
for the lens spaces
L(n,k)
for any coprime positive integers
n,k
, which are 3-manifolds of genus
1
. We have investigated Question
1.1
for framed 3-manifolds of genus
\leq 2
and provide an affirmative answer for all framed 3-manifolds of genus 1.

Theorem 1.2

(Theorem
4.16
)
.

Let
M
be a 3-manifold of genus 1, and
\mathfrak{f}
an arbitrary framing on
M
. For any finite-dimensional Hopf algebra
H
,
K(M,\mathfrak{f},H)
is a gauge invariant of
H
.

We also consider a family of genus 2 framed large Seifert manifolds
\mathbb{M}_{m,n}
with a fixed choice of framing
\mathfrak{f}_{m,n}
parametrized by
(m,n)\in{\mathbb{N}}^{2}
which includes some classical 3-manifolds such as the Poincaré homology sphere, and we have proved

Theorem 1.3

(Theorem
7.3
)
.

For any
(m,n)\in{\mathbb{N}}^{2}
and finite-dimensional Hopf algebra
H
, the Kuperberg invariant
K(\mathbb{M}_{m,n},\mathfrak{f}_{m,n},H)
is a gauge invariant of
H
.

Since the sequence
\{\mathbb{M}_{m,n}\}_{m,n}
contains lens spaces of the form
L(k,1)
for
k\geq 3
(see Remark
7.1
), the theorem justifies that
K(\mathbb{M}_{m,n},\mathfrak{f}_{m,n},H)
is a genus 2 generalization of Frobenius-Schur indicators of
H
. In particular, when
H
is semisimple,
K(\mathbb{M}_{m,n},\mathfrak{f}_{m,n},H)
coincides with a special case of the genus 2 generalization of the FS-indicator called the topological indicator defined in
[
25
]
.

In the proof of both Theorems
1.2
and
1.3
, we first obtain a framed Heegaard diagram of the framed 3-manifold under study. If the 3-manifold is of genus
g
, then the framed Heegaard diagram consists of
g
upper curves,
g
lower curves, a combing, and
g
twist fronts satisfying the admissible condition (see Section
3
). From the diagram, we tabulate the combinatorial data of the angles made at the intersections among the upper curves, lower curves and twist fronts, which enables us to compute the Kuperberg invariant. Then using Radford’s formula for traces
[
36
]
, we rewrite the Kuperberg invariant as the trace of a linear operator
P_{H}:H^{{\,\otimes\,}g}\rightarrow H^{{\,\otimes\,}g}
. This operator
P_{H}
, which depends on
(M,\mathfrak{f})
, can be defined for any finite-dimensional Hopf algebra
H
.

The last step above plays a key role for establishing our major results. Let
H
and
K
be finite-dimensional Hopf algebras. Recall from
[
32
]
that
{\operatorname{Rep}}(H)
and
{\operatorname{Rep}}(K)
are equivalent tensor categories if and only if
K\cong H_{F}
as Hopf algebras, where
H_{F}
is the Drinfeld twist of
H
by a
2-cocycle

F\in H^{{\,\otimes\,}2}
(cf. (
2.6
)). More precisely,
H_{F}=H
as an algebra, with the comultiplication

\Delta_{F}(h)=F\Delta(h)F^{-1}\,.

To prove that
K(M,\mathfrak{f},H)
is a gauge invariant of
H
, it suffices to show that

K(M,\mathfrak{f},H)=K(M,\mathfrak{f},H_{F})

for any 2-cocycle
F\in H^{{\,\otimes\,}2}
. Since
H=H_{F}
as vector spaces, we need to prove

(1.1)

{\operatorname{Tr}}(P_{H})={\operatorname{Tr}}(P_{H_{F}}).

for any 2-cocycle
F\in H^{{\,\otimes\,}2}
.

In order to establish the equality (
1.1
), one needs to understand the properties of the sequence
\{F_{n}\}_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
, associated to any 2-cocycle
F
, which is defined in the following way:
F_{1}:=1
,
F_{2}:=F
and
F_{n+1}=(1{\,\otimes\,}F_{n})({\operatorname{id}}{\,\otimes\,}\Delta^{n})(F)
where
\Delta^{n}
is the
n
-folded comultiplication. An elaborated list of properties of the sequence
\{F_{n}\}_{n}
are proved in Section
5
, and these technical results are essential to the proofs of our main theorems.

Throughout this paper, Hopf algebras are always assumed to be finite-dimensional over an arbitrary field unless otherwise stated. The rest of this paper is organized as follows. In Section 2, we briefly review Hopf algebras, 2-cocycles and their Drinfeld twists. Some basic results on general Hopf algebras will be established in this section. In Section 3, we explain how the Kuperberg invariant of a framed 3-manifold
(M,\mathfrak{f})
of genus
g
is defined. This encompasses how the combinatorial data is derived from a framed Heegaard diagram of
(M,\mathfrak{f})
, and how this data is applied to construct the elements
w\in H^{\otimes g}
and
w^{*}\in H^{*{\,\otimes\,}g}
so that
K(M,\mathfrak{f},H)=w^{*}(w)
. The examples of computations for the 3-manifolds
{\mathbb{S}}^{3}
,
{\mathbb{S}}^{1}\times{\mathbb{S}}^{2}
and the quaternion orbifold
{\mathbb{S}}^{3}/Q_{8}
of
{\mathbb{S}}^{3}
are discussed in detail. In Section 4, we focus on the Kuperberg invariants of lens spaces
L(n,k)
. We start with a certain choice of framed Heegaard diagrams of
L(n,k)
representing a framing
\mathfrak{f}_{R}
to obtain an expression of the corresponding Kuperberg invariant
K(L(n,k),\mathfrak{f}_{R},H)
in terms of the integrals
{\lambda}\in H^{*}
,
{\Lambda}\in H
and its antipode
S
of a finite-dimensional Hopf algebra
H
. This expression is shown to be the trace of a linear operator
P_{H}:H\rightarrow H
, whose gauge invariance is established in Theorem
4.10
by using the properties of
F_{n}
for any 2-cocycle
F
of
H
. By investigating the Kuperberg invariant for different spin classes and Hopf degrees of framings, we prove the gauge invariance of
K(L(n,k),\mathfrak{f},H)
for arbitrary framing
\mathfrak{f}
in Theorem
4.16
.
We dedicate Section 5 to the proofs of all technical results on the sequence
\{F_{n}\}_{n}
for any 2-cocycle
F
of
H
. These results are used throughout this paper. Inspired by the gauge invariance of
K(L(n,k),\mathfrak{f},H)
for any Hopf algebra
H
, we define a family of gauge invariants
\tilde{\nu}_{n,k}(H)
for any positive coprime integers
n,k
. These gauge invariants of
H
are generally different, but
\tilde{\nu}_{n,k}(H)=K(L(n,k),\mathfrak{f},H)
when
H
is semisimple over
{\mathbb{C}}
. Finally, in Section 7, we study a family of framed 3-manifolds
(\mathbb{M}_{m,n},\mathfrak{f}_{m,n})
of genus 2, and prove the gauge invariance of the Kuperberg invariant
K(\mathbb{M}_{m,n},\mathfrak{f}_{m,n},H)
for any Hopf algebra
H
.

2.
Hopf algebras and their Drinfeld twists

In this paper, we consider finite-dimensional Hopf algebras over an arbitrary base field
{\Bbbk}
unless stated otherwise. We briefly recall some basic notions on general finite-dimensional Hopf algebras in this section. The readers are referred to
[
30
]
,
[
46
]
or
[
38
]
for more details. For the purpose of defining and computing the Kuperberg invariants in the later sections, we will point out the differences between the conventions in this paper and that in Kuperberg’s paper
[
20
]
.

2.1.
Integrals of Hopf algebras

Let
H
be a finite-dimensional Hopf algebra over a field
{\Bbbk}
with multiplication
m:H{\,\otimes\,}H\rightarrow H
, comultiplication
{\Delta}:H\rightarrow H{\,\otimes\,}H
, counit
{\varepsilon}:H\rightarrow{\Bbbk}
and antipode
S\in{\operatorname{End}}_{\Bbbk}(H)
. We will use the Sweedler notation
{\Delta}(h)=\sum_{(h)}h_{(1)}{\,\otimes\,}h_{(2)}
for any
h\in H
or even
{\Delta}(h)=h_{(1)}{\,\otimes\,}h_{(2)}
with the summation suppressed.

Since
H
is finite-dimensional, its dual space
H^{*}
is also a Hopf algebra with multiplication
{\Delta}^{*}
, identity
{\varepsilon}
, comultiplication
m^{*}
, counit given by evaluation at
1_{H}
and antipode
S^{*}
. An element
x\in H
is called
grouplike
if
x\neq 0
and
\Delta(x)=x{\,\otimes\,}x
. An element
{\beta}\in H^{*}
is grouplike if and only
m^{*}({\beta})(h{\,\otimes\,}k)={\beta}(h){\beta}(k)
for
h,k\in H
, or equivalently,
{\beta}
is an algebra homomorphism. For any grouplike element
{\beta}\in H^{*}
, we will simply denote its
convolution
inverse in
H^{*}
by
{\beta}^{-1}
.

There are natural left and right
H^{*}
-actions
\rightharpoonup
and
\leftharpoonup
on
H
respectively given by
f\rightharpoonup h:=\sum_{(h)}h_{(1)}f(h_{(2)})
and
h\leftharpoonup f:=\sum_{(h)}f(h_{(1)})h_{(2)}
for
f\in H^{*}
and
h\in H
. Since
H\cong H^{**}
as Hopf algebras via the canonical isomorphism of vector spaces
j:H\rightarrow H^{**}
, there are
H
-actions
\rightharpoonup
and
\leftharpoonup
on
H^{*}
, namely

h\rightharpoonup f=\sum_{(f)}f_{(1)}f_{(2)}(h)
and
f\leftharpoonup h=\sum_{(f)}f_{(1)}(h)f_{(2)}
.

The iterated comultiplications on
H
are defined inductively by

(2.1)

{\Delta}^{(0)}={\varepsilon}\cdot 1_{H}\,,\ {\Delta}^{(1)}={\operatorname{id}}_{H}\,,\ {\Delta}^{(2)}={\Delta}\,,\ {\Delta}^{(n+1)}=({\operatorname{id}}{\,\otimes\,}{\Delta}^{(n)})\circ{\Delta}\text{ for $n\geq 2$}\,.

For simplicity, we will use the abused notation
{\Delta}^{n}
for
{\Delta}^{(n)}
, and the suppressed Sweedler notation of
{\Delta}^{n}(h)=h_{(1)}{\,\otimes\,}\cdots{\,\otimes\,}h_{(n)}
for any
h\in H
.

A right (resp. left)
integral
in
H
is an element
{\Lambda}^{R}
(resp.
{\Lambda}^{L}
) in
H
such that for all
h\in H
,
{\Lambda}^{R}h={\varepsilon}(h){\Lambda}^{R}
(resp.
h{\Lambda}^{L}={\varepsilon}(h){\Lambda}^{L}
).
A right (resp. left)
cointegral
of
H
is an integral
{\lambda}^{R}
(resp.
{\lambda}^{L}
) in
H^{*}
, i.e.,
h\leftharpoonup{\lambda}^{R}={\lambda}^{R}(h)\cdot 1
(resp.
({\lambda}^{L}\rightharpoonup h)={\lambda}^{L}(h)\cdot 1
) for all
h\in H
.

Since the space of left (resp. right) integrals in
H
, denoted by
\int^{l}_{H}
(resp.
\int^{r}_{H}
), is 1-dimensional, any
{\Lambda}^{L}\in\int^{l}_{H}\setminus\{0\}
determines a
distinguished grouplike element

{\alpha}\in H^{*}
by
{\Lambda}^{L}h=\alpha(h){\Lambda}^{L}
for all
h\in H
. We say that
H
is
unimodular
if
\alpha={\varepsilon}
. Similarly, the distinguished grouplike element
g\in H
is determined by

(2.2)

h\rightharpoonup{\lambda}^{R}={\lambda}^{R}(h)g\quad\text{for all }h\in H\,.

The celebrated Radford formula
[
36
]
remarkably relates the antipode and these distinguished grouplike elements:

Theorem 2.1

(Radford
[
36
]
)
.

Let
H
be a finite-dimensional Hopf algebra over any field
{\Bbbk}
with antipode
S
. Then

S^{4}(h)={\alpha}\rightharpoonup ghg^{-1}\leftharpoonup{\alpha}^{-1}\quad\text{ for all }h\in H,

where
g\in H
and
{\alpha}\in H^{*}
are the distinguished grouplike elements.

Since the cyclic group algebra of any grouplike element is a Hopf subalgebra of
H
, by the Nichols–Zoeller freeness theorem
[
34
]
, we have the orders
\alpha
and
g
are divisors of
\dim(H)
, and
\alpha(g)\in{\Bbbk}
is a
\dim(H)
-th root of unity.

It was proved in
[
37
]
that
(H,\leftharpoonup)
is free right
H^{*}
-module generated by
{\Lambda}^{L}
and hence
{\lambda}^{R}({\Lambda}^{L})\neq 0
. From now on, we choose
{\lambda}^{R}\in\int^{r}_{H^{*}}
and
{\Lambda}^{L}\in\int^{l}_{H}
such that

{\lambda}^{R}({\Lambda}^{L})=1\,,

and call them a pair of
normalized integrals for
H
. The following lemma is well-known, and the statement will serve as a reference of our notation for the remainder of this paper. The proofs are provided for the sake of completeness.

Lemma 2.2
.

Suppose the integrals
{\lambda}^{R}\in H^{*}
and
{\Lambda}^{L}\in H
are normalized, and that
g\in H
,
{\alpha}\in H^{*}
are the distinguished grouplike elements.
Define

{\Lambda}^{R}:=S({\Lambda}^{L})\in H\quad\text{and}\quad{\lambda}^{L}:={\lambda}^{R}\circ S^{-1}\in H^{*}\,.

Then:

(i)

{\Lambda}^{R}
is a right integral of
H
, and
{\lambda}^{L}
is a left integral of
H^{*}
;

(ii)

{\lambda}^{R}({\Lambda}^{R})={\lambda}^{L}({\Lambda}^{R})=1
;

(iii)

{\lambda}^{L}({\Lambda}^{L})={\alpha}(g)
.

Proof.

The first statement follows immediately from the fact that
S
is an algebra and coalgebra anti-automorphism of
H
. By
[
38
, Prop. 3]
, we have

{\lambda}^{R}({\Lambda}^{R})={\lambda}^{R}(S({\Lambda}^{L}))={\lambda}^{R}(g{\Lambda}^{L})={\varepsilon}(g){\lambda}^{R}({\Lambda}^{L})=1\,.

The equality
{\lambda}^{L}({\Lambda}^{R})=1
is a direct consequence of the definition of
{\Lambda}^{R}
and
{\lambda}^{L}
. Finally, we have

{\lambda}^{L}({\Lambda}^{L})={\lambda}^{R}(S^{-1}({\Lambda}^{L}))={\alpha}({\Lambda}_{1}^{L}){\lambda}^{R}({\Lambda}_{2}^{L})=\alpha(g)\,.\qed

From (
2.2
) and Lemma
2.2
, we have
{\lambda}^{R}\rightharpoonup{\Lambda}^{R}={\lambda}^{R}({\Lambda}^{R})g=g
,
which is consistent with the definition of the distinguished grouplike element of
H
in
[
20
, p. 113]
. Moreover, by definition, for any
h\in H
, we have

(2.3)

h{\Lambda}^{R}=h\cdot S({\Lambda}^{L})=S({\Lambda}^{L}\cdot S^{-1}(h))=\alpha(S^{-1}(h))S({\Lambda}^{L})=\alpha^{-1}(h){\Lambda}^{R}\,.

Therefore, our
\alpha
coincides with the inverse of Kuperberg’s convention
[
20
, p. 113]
for the distinguished grouplike element in
H^{*}
.

For any integer
n
, define

(2.4)

{\Lambda}_{n-\frac{1}{2}}:=\alpha^{-n}\rightharpoonup S({\Lambda}^{L})\quad\text{and}\quad{\lambda}_{n-\frac{1}{2}}:=g^{n}\rightharpoonup{\lambda}^{R}\,.

Then it is easy to derive the following equations from definition and
[
38
, Prop. 3]
:

(2.5)

\begin{split}&{\Lambda}_{-\frac{1}{2}}={\alpha}^{0}\rightharpoonup{\Lambda}^{R}={\Lambda}^{R}=S({\Lambda}^{L})\,,\quad{\Lambda}_{\frac{1}{2}}={\alpha}^{-1}\rightharpoonup S({\Lambda}^{L})={\alpha}^{-1}\rightharpoonup({\alpha}\rightharpoonup{\Lambda}^{L})={\Lambda}^{L}\,,\\
&{\lambda}_{-\frac{1}{2}}=g^{0}\rightharpoonup{\lambda}^{R}={\lambda}^{R}\,,\quad{\lambda}_{\frac{1}{2}}=g\rightharpoonup{\lambda}^{R}={\lambda}^{R}\circ S^{-1}={\lambda}^{L}\,.\end{split}

We will simply write
{\lambda}\in\int_{H^{*}}^{r}
and
{\Lambda}\in\int^{l}_{H}
instead of
{\lambda}^{R}
and
{\Lambda}^{L}
respectively. However, the conventions stated in Lemma
2.2
remain. The following theorem on integrals, essentially proved in
[
38
]
, will be repeatedly used in many of our proofs in this paper. In particular, statement (v) of the following theorem is often called the
Radford trace formula
.

Theorem 2.3
.

Let
H
be a finite-dimensional Hopf algebra over
{\Bbbk}
,
{\lambda}\in\int^{r}_{H^{*}}
and
{\Lambda}\in\int_{H}^{l}
be a pair of normalized integrals, i.e.,
{\lambda}({\Lambda})=1
. For any
a,b\in H
and
{\Bbbk}
-linear map
X\in{\operatorname{End}}_{\Bbbk}(H)
, we have

(i)

{\lambda}(ab)={\lambda}(S^{2}(b\leftharpoonup\alpha)a)
,

(ii)

{\lambda}S(ab)={\lambda}S(bS(S(a)\leftharpoonup{\alpha}))
,

(iii)

\Lambda_{(1)}{\,\otimes\,}a\Lambda_{(2)}=S(a)\Lambda_{(1)}{\,\otimes\,}\Lambda_{(2)}
,

(iv)

\Lambda_{(1)}a{\,\otimes\,}\Lambda_{(2)}=\Lambda_{(1)}{\,\otimes\,}\Lambda_{(2)}S(a\leftharpoonup{\alpha})
,

(v)

{\operatorname{Tr}}(X)={\lambda}\left(S(\Lambda_{(2)})X(\Lambda_{(1)})\right)={\lambda}\left(S(X(\Lambda_{(2)}))\Lambda_{(1)}\right)
,

(vi)

{\lambda}S\left(aX\left({\Lambda}_{(1)}\right){\Lambda}_{(2)}\right)={\lambda}S\left(X\left({\Lambda}_{(1)}S(a)\right){\Lambda}_{(2)}\right)
.

Proof.

Statements (i), (iii), (iv) and (v) are proved in
[
37
,
38
]
. Statement (ii) follows immediately from (i), and statement (vi) is a consequence of (ii) and (iv).
∎

The
n
-th
Sweedler power

P^{(n)}(x)
of an element
x\in H
is defined by
P^{(n)}(x)=x_{(1)}\cdots x_{(n)}
with
P^{(0)}=1\cdot{\varepsilon}
. The statement (vi) of the preceding theorem can be generalized as follows, and will be used in Section
7
.

Corollary 2.4
.

Assuming the conditions of Theorem
2.3
. For any positive integer
n
,
x\in H
, and linear operator
Y:H\rightarrow H
, we have

(i)

{\lambda}S\Big(xY(\Lambda_{(1)})P^{(n)}(\Lambda_{(2)})\Big)=\lambda S\left(Y\left(S^{2}(x_{(1)})\Lambda_{(1)}S(x_{(3)})\right)S^{2}(x_{(2)})P^{(n)}\left(\Lambda_{(2)}\right)\right)
,

(ii)

{\lambda}S\Big(P^{(n-1)}(\Lambda_{(1)})Y(\Lambda_{(2)})x\Lambda_{(3)}\Big)={\lambda}S\Big(P^{(n-1)}(\Lambda_{(1)})S^{2}(x_{(2)})Y(S(x_{(1)})\Lambda_{(2)}S^{2}(x_{(3)}))\Lambda_{(3)}\Big)
.

Proof.

(i) For
n=1
,

\begin{split}&\lambda S\left(xY\left(\Lambda_{(1)}\right)\Lambda_{(2)}\right)=\lambda S\left(xY\left(\Lambda_{(1)}\right)S(\Lambda_{(2)})\Lambda_{(3)}\Lambda_{(4)}\right)\\
=\,&\lambda S\left(Y\left(\Lambda_{(1)}S(x_{(3)})\right)S\left(\Lambda_{(2)}S(x_{(2)})\right)\Lambda_{(3)}S(x_{(1)})\Lambda_{(4)}\right)\text{ by Theorem \ref{t:t2} (vi)}\\
=\,&\lambda S\left(Y\left(S^{2}(x_{(1)})\Lambda_{(1)}S(x_{(5)})\right)S(S^{2}(x_{(2)})\Lambda_{(2)}S(x_{(4)}))S^{2}(x_{(3)})\Lambda_{(3)}\Lambda_{(4)}\right)\text{ by Theorem \ref{t:t2} (iii)}\\
=\,&\lambda S\left(Y\left(S^{2}(x_{(1)})\Lambda_{(1)}S(x_{(3)})\right)S^{2}(x_{(2)})\Lambda_{(2)}\right)\text{ by the properties of the antipode}.\end{split}

Assume the statement holds from some positive integer
n
. Note that

{\lambda}S\Big(xY(\Lambda_{(1)})P^{(n+1)}(\Lambda_{(2)})\Big)={\lambda}S\Big(x(Y*{\operatorname{id}})(\Lambda_{(1)})P^{(n)}(\Lambda_{(2)})\Big)

where
Y*{\operatorname{id}}
is the convolution product of

and
{\operatorname{id}}_{H}
. By the induction assumption, we find

\begin{split}&{\lambda}S\Big(xY(\Lambda_{(1)})P^{(n+1)}(\Lambda_{(2)})\Big)={\lambda}S\Big((Y*{\operatorname{id}})(S^{2}(x_{(1)})\Lambda_{(1)}S(x_{(3)}))S^{2}(x_{(2)})P^{(n)}(\Lambda_{(2)})\Big)\\
=\,&{\lambda}S\Big((Y(S^{2}(x_{(1)})\Lambda_{(1)}S(x_{(5)}))S^{2}(x_{(2)})\Lambda_{(2)}S(x_{(4)})S^{2}(x_{(3)})P^{(n)}(\Lambda_{(3)})\Big)\\
=\,&{\lambda}S\Big((Y(S^{2}(x_{(1)})\Lambda_{(1)}S(x_{(3)}))S^{2}(x_{(2)})P^{(n+1)}(\Lambda_{(2)})\Big).\end{split}

Statement (ii) follows similarly by induction on
n
where the case
n=1
can be proved as follows:

\begin{split}&\lambda S\left(Y\left(\Lambda_{(1)}\right)x\Lambda_{(2)}\right)=\lambda S\left(\Lambda_{(1)}S(\Lambda_{(2)})Y\left(\Lambda_{(3)}\right)x\Lambda_{(4)}\right)\\
=\,&\lambda S\left(S(x_{(3)})\Lambda_{(1)}S(S(x_{(2)})\Lambda_{(2)})Y\left(S(x_{(1)})\Lambda_{(3)}\right)\Lambda_{(4)}\right)\text{ by Theorem \ref{t:t2} (iii)}\\
=\,&\lambda S\left(\Lambda_{(1)}S^{2}(x_{(3)})S(\Lambda_{(2)}S^{2}(x_{(4)}))S^{2}(x_{(2)})Y\left(S(x_{(1)})\Lambda_{(3)}S^{2}(x_{(5)})\right)\Lambda_{(4)}\right)\text{ by Theorem \ref{t:t2} (vi)}\\
=\,&\lambda S\left(S^{2}(x_{(2)})Y\left(S(x_{(1)})\Lambda_{(1)}S^{2}(x_{(3)})\right)\Lambda_{(2)}\right)\text{ by the properties of the antipode}.\qed\end{split}

2.2.
Gauge transformation

Before proceeding, we introduce some notations involving tensors. Let
V
be any finite-dimensional
{\Bbbk}
-linear space. For any
n\geq 1
, we write an
n
-fold tensor
v\in V^{\otimes n}
as

v=\sum_{i}v^{[1]}_{i}\otimes v_{i}^{[2]}\otimes\cdots\otimes v^{[n]}_{i}

or simply
v=v^{[1]}_{i}\otimes\cdots\otimes v^{[n]}_{i}
with the summation notion suppressed.
We may further abbreviate
v=v^{[1]}_{i}\otimes\cdots\otimes v^{[n]}_{i}
as
v=\bigotimes_{t=1}^{n}v^{[t]}_{i}
. For instance, we write
v=v^{[1]}_{i}\otimes\bigotimes_{t=2}^{n-1}v^{[t]}_{i}\otimes v_{i}^{[n]}
to separate the first and last components from the others. Moreover, a permutation
{\sigma}
on
\{1,\dots,n\}
, denoted by
\sigma\in\operatorname{Sym}_{n}
, can be considered as a linear automorphism on
V^{\otimes n}
given by

\sigma v=v^{[{\sigma}1]}_{i}\otimes v_{i}^{[{\sigma}2]}\otimes\cdots\otimes v^{[{\sigma}n]}_{i}=\bigotimes_{t=1}^{n}v^{[\sigma t]}_{i}\,.

Now let
H
be a finite-dimensional Hopf algebra over
{\Bbbk}
. The iterative product of
H^{\otimes{n}}
will be simply denoted by
m
, i.e.,
m:H^{\otimes{n}}\rightarrow H
,
m(v)=\prod_{t=1}^{n}v_{i}^{[t]}
for any
v\in H^{\otimes{n}}
(where the sum over
i
will always be suppressed as before). We will use the convention
m(v)=v
for any
v\in H
. For
h\in H
, we denote
{\Delta}^{n}(h)=\bigotimes_{i=1}^{n}h_{(i)}
by the Sweedler notation. In particular,
{\Delta}^{n+l}(h)=\bigotimes_{i=1}^{n}h_{(i)}{\,\otimes\,}\bigotimes_{i=n+1}^{n+l}h_{(i)}
for any positive integers
n,l
.

A
gauge transformation
of
H
is an invertible element
F\in H^{\otimes 2}
such that
({\varepsilon}\otimes{\operatorname{id}})F=({\operatorname{id}}\otimes{\varepsilon})F=1
. By changing the comultiplication of
H
into

{\Delta}_{F}(h)=F{\Delta}(h)F^{-1}\quad\text{for all $h\in H$}\,,

one obtains a quasi-Hopf algebra
H_{F}
which is identical to
H
as an algebra with the same counit
{\varepsilon}
(see, for example,
[
16
, Chap. XV]
). This quasi-Hopf algebra
H_{F}
is a Hopf algebra if and only if
F
is a
2-cocycle
(see
[
14
]
), which means
F
is a gauge transformation such that the following equality holds

(2.6)

1\otimes 1=(1\otimes F)\cdot({\operatorname{id}}\otimes{\Delta})(F)\cdot({\Delta}\otimes{\operatorname{id}})(F^{-1})\cdot(F^{-1}\otimes 1)\,.

Let
F\in H\otimes H
be a 2-cocycle, and write
F=f_{i}^{[1]}\otimes f_{i}^{[2]}
with inverse
F^{-1}=d_{i}^{[1]}\otimes d_{i}^{[2]}
. It can be easily derived from (
2.6
) that
u:=f_{i}^{[1]}S(f_{i}^{[2]})
is invertible with inverse
u^{-1}=S(d_{i}^{[1]})d_{i}^{[2]}
. As mentioned above, in this case,
H_{F}
is a Hopf algebra with antipode

(2.7)

S_{F}(h):=uS(h)u^{-1}\,,\quad\text{for all $h\in H$}\,.

We call
H_{F}
the (Drinfeld) twist of
H
by the 2-cocycle
F
. The iterative comultiplication
{\Delta}_{F}^{n}
of
H_{F}
is the conjugation of
{\Delta}^{n}
by
F_{n}\in H^{\otimes{n}}
, which is defined inductively as follows (cf.
[
14
]
):
F_{1}=1_{H}
and

(2.8)

F_{n+1}=(F_{n}{\,\otimes\,}1)({\operatorname{id}}{\,\otimes\,}{\Delta}^{n})(F)\quad\text{for any integer }n\geq 1\,.

The following lemma on
F_{n}
was proved in
[
14
, Lem. 2.3]
.

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
F
be a 2-cocycle of a finite-dimensional Hopf algebra
H
. Then we have
F_{n+1}=(F_{n}{\,\otimes\,}1)({\Delta}^{n}{\,\otimes\,}{\operatorname{id}})(F)
and
{\Delta}_{F}^{n}=F_{n}{\Delta}^{n}F_{n}^{-1}
for any integer
n\geq 1
.

Extending the notations of
F
and
F^{-1}
, we will write
F_{n}=f_{i}^{[1]}\otimes\cdots\otimes f_{i}^{[n]}
with inverse
F_{n}^{-1}=d_{i}^{[1]}\otimes\cdots\otimes d_{i}^{[n]}
. Together with the Sweedler notation, we find

(2.9)

{\Delta}^{n}_{F}(h)=f_{i}^{[1]}h_{(1)}d_{j}^{[1]}\otimes\cdots\otimes f_{i}^{[n]}h_{(n)}d_{j}^{[n]}\quad\text{ for }h\in H.

Let
{\operatorname{Rep}}(H)
be the category of finite-dimensional
H
-modules over
{\Bbbk}
. It is a finite tensor category in the sense of
[
7
]
. Following
[
14
]
, we give the definition of gauge invariants as follows.

Definition 2.6
.

Two finite-dimensional Hopf algebras
H
and
H^{\prime}
over a base field
{\Bbbk}
are called
gauge equivalent
if
{\operatorname{Rep}}(H)
is equivalent to
{\operatorname{Rep}}(H^{\prime})
as tensor categories. A quantity
f(H)
assigned to each finite-dimensional Hopf algebra
H
over
{\Bbbk}
is called a
gauge invariant
if
f(H)=f(H^{\prime})
whenever
H
is gauge equivalent to
H^{\prime}
, i.e.,
f(H)
is an invariant of the tensor category
{\operatorname{Rep}}(H)
.

It is shown in
[
33
]
(see also
[
43
,
6
]
) that two finite-dimensional Hopf algebras
H
and
H^{\prime}
over
{\Bbbk}
are gauge equivalent if and only if there exists a 2-cocycle
F\in H\otimes H
such that
H^{\prime}\cong H_{F}
as bialgebras. Hence, gauge invariants of Hopf algebras are those remain unchanged under Drinfeld twists. The goal of this paper is to prove that the Kuperberg invariants of certain framed 3-manifolds are gauge invariants of Hopf algebras.

3.
Framed 3-manifolds and their Kuperberg invariants

In this section, we review the construction of the Kuperberg invariant for closed oriented framed 3-manifolds. Following
[
20
]
, the invariant is defined by a diagrammatic presentation of any framed 3-manifold, called a
framed Heegaard diagram
.

3.1.
Framed 3-manifolds

Let
M
be a closed oriented 3-manifold. It is well-known (see, for example,
[
41
, Chap. 9]
) that
M
admits at least one
Heegaard splitting
, which is a decomposition
M=B_{1}\cup_{f}B_{2}
into a union of two handlebodies
B_{1}
and
B_{2}
of the same genus, say
g
, glued along their common boundary surface, say
\Sigma_{g}
, via a homeomorphism
f
. Such a splitting can be encoded by a Heegaard diagram
(\Sigma_{g},\mu,\eta)
of
M
, where
\mu=\{\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{g}\}
and
\eta=\{\eta_{1},\ldots,\eta_{g}\}
are two collections of disjoint non-separating simple closed curves (or circles) on
\Sigma_{g}
, and
g
is called the genus of the Heegaard diagram. Here, the curves are used to indicate how the handles of
B_{1}
and
B_{2}
are attached which equivalently describe the homeomorphism
f
.

To obtain
M
from a Heegaard diagram
(\Sigma_{g},\mu,\eta)
, first note that
{\mathbb{S}}^{3}\setminus\Sigma_{g}
has two connected components whose closures are handlebodies. We arbitrarily call one of them the
upper
handlebody and the other one
lower
, and glue these two handlebodies along
\Sigma_{g}
. The gluing is made so that the meridians of upper handlebody are attached to the curves
\mu_{i}
and the meridians of lower handlebody are attached to the curves
\eta_{j}
. These curves are called the upper curves and the lower curves correspondingly.
It is well-known that any closed oriented 3-manifold can be constructed in this way
[
41
]
. Conversely, according to the Reidemeister-Singer Theorem
[
40
,
45
]
(see also
[
19
, Thm. 4.1]
for a simple proof), any two Heegaard diagrams of a 3-manifold are equivalent by a sequence of
stabilization moves
. A stabilization move is a modification of a Heegaard diagram
(\Sigma_{g},\mu,\eta)
by the following procedure: Take the connected sum of
\Sigma_{g}
with a torus at a disk in
\Sigma_{g}
that is disjoint from all of its upper and lower curves, then add a pair of upper and lower curves at the attached torus along its meridian and longitude (see Figure
1
).

Figure 1.
The stabilization move.

We will use the following planar presentation of Heegaard diagrams (see, for example,
[
42
, Lec. 1]
). Note that it suffices to indicate where the upper and lower curves are located on the surface
\Sigma_{g}
. We will draw
\Sigma_{g}
minus a point as a plane with
2g
open disks removed, whose boundaries are depicted by black circles in our presentation. It is understood, although not shown in the picture, that there are
g
handles (above the plane) attached to the
g
pairs of circles, which are called the
attaching circles
. We arrange the diagram so that a pair of attaching circles joined by a handle is far away from the other pairs. The orientation of
M
is induced by the standard orientation of
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
where the Heegaard diagram sits as the
xy
-plane.
Figure
2
is a Heegaard diagram of the lens space
L(2,1)
(see Section
4
for definition), which is homeomorphic to
\mathbb{RP}^{3}
, where the two black circles represent the attaching circles of the handle, the upper curve is colored in blue, and the lower curve is colored in green.

L(2,1)
.

Equip
M
with a Riemannian metric. A
combing
on
M
is a unit-length tangent vector field up to homotopy, and a
framing

\mathfrak{f}
on
M
consists of three orthonormal combings. Combings and framings can be drawn on Heegaard diagrams of
M
. Given a Heegaard diagram
(\Sigma_{g},\mu,\eta)
, a combing of
\Sigma_{g}
is a vector field on
\Sigma_{g}
with
2g
singularities of index
-1
, one on each curve, and one singularity of index
+2
disjoint from all curves. The singularity of index
-1
on a curve is called the
base point
of the curve and the two outward-pointing vectors are tangent to the curve. It is shown by Kuperberg
[
20
]
that a combing on
\Sigma_{g}
can be extended to a combing of
M
, and any combing of
M
is homotopic to an extension of some combing on
\Sigma_{g}
.
In this paper, we use gray dashed lines with arrows in a Heegaard diagram to depict the flows of a combing on
\Sigma_{g}
which is demonstrated as an example on the left part of Figure
3
.

Figure 3.
The gray dashed lines with arrows represent the combing
b_{1}
, and the gray bullet on each curve stands for the base point. Left:
b_{1}
points outwards from the base point along an upper or a lower curve. Right:
\alpha
is a lower curve,
\beta
is an upper curve. The black arc with small triangles represents the twist front.

To illustrate a framing
\mathfrak{f}
on a Heegaard diagram
(\Sigma_{g},\mu,\eta)
of
M
, we start with a combing
b_{1}
on
\Sigma_{g}
, which extends to a combing on
M
. Then, we describe a combing
b_{2}
on
\Sigma_{g}
, which is orthogonal to
b_{1}
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
(not necessarily tangent to
\Sigma_{g}
), using
twist fronts
, as instructed in
[
20
]
.
Twist fronts are arcs in the Heegaard diagram on which
b_{2}
is normal to
\Sigma_{g}
while pointing from the lower to the upper handlebody. A twist front is transversely oriented in the direction that
b_{2}
rotates by the right-hand rule relative to
b_{1}
, indicated by small triangles based on the arc, for any local segment of the arc homotopic to an integral curve of
b_{1}
. When viewing from the upper handlebody, the triangles point counterclockwise near the base point of the upper curve, and they point clockwise near that of the lower curve, see Figure
3
. We then define a
2-combed Heegaard diagram
of a 3-manifold
M
to be a Heegaard diagram
(\Sigma_{g},\mu,\eta)
of
M
that is decorated with a combing
b_{1}
on
\Sigma_{g}
and a twist front, see Figure
4
for example.

As is mentioned above, the combing
b_{1}
on
\Sigma_{g}
extends to a combing on
M
. However, unlike
b_{1}
, the combing
b_{2}
on the surface
\Sigma_{g}
described by twist fronts may not be extended to a combing of 3-manifold
M
orthogonal to
b_{1}
.
The
admissibility condition
is described as follows. Orient all the lower and upper curves arbitrarily.
For each of these curves
c:[0,1]\rightarrow\Sigma_{g}
with
c(0)=c(1)
, and any
t\in(0,1]
, let
\theta_{c}(p)
denote the aggregated counterclockwise rotation of the tangent of
c
relative to
b_{1}
from the base point
c(0)
to
p=c(t)
in units of
1=360^{\circ}
. We denote
\theta_{c}(c(1))
by
\theta_{c}
. Note that
\theta_{c}
is always a half integer. Similarly, let
\phi_{c}(p)
denote the aggregated right-handed rotation of
b_{2}
around
b_{1}
from the base point to
p
, and
\phi_{c}
denote the total rotation when it is back to the base point. Alternatively,
\phi_{c}(p)
is the aggregated number of signed crossings of
c
with twist fronts: It is positive if the triangle on the twist front and the orientation of
c
coincide at the intersection, and negative otherwise.
The base point of
c
contributes only half of the sign to
\phi_{c}
when
c
returns. By
[
20
]
,
b_{2}
can be extended to a combing on
M
if and only if it satisfies the following admissibility conditions:

(3.1)

\theta_{\mu}=-\phi_{\mu}
for each upper curve
\mu
, and
\theta_{\eta}=\phi_{\eta}
for each lower curve
\eta
.

A 2-combed Heegaard diagram of
M
satisfying the above admissibility condition is called a
framed Heegaard diagram
of
M
. A framed Heegaard diagram
D
affords a pair of orthogonal combings
(b_{1},b_{2})
on
\Sigma_{g}
that can be extended to
M
, and consequently yields a framing
\mathfrak{f}:=(b_{1},b_{2},b_{1}\times b_{2})
on
M
. We call
\mathfrak{f}
the
diagram framing
associated to the framed Heegaard diagram
D
. Conversely, any framing on
M
admits a (not necessarily unique) framed Heegaard diagram by restriction to a Heegaard surface.

3.2.
The Kuperberg invariant

In
[
20
]
, for a fixed choice of Hopf algebra, Kuperberg defined a numerical invariant for framed 3-manifolds using framed Heegaard diagrams, and showed that this invariant only depends on the homeomorphism class of the framed 3-manifolds. In this section, we briefly recall the definition of this invariant.

Let
D
be a framed Heegaard diagram of a 3-manifold
M
consisting of upper and lower curves
\{\mu_{1},...,\mu_{g}\}
and
\{\eta_{1},...,\eta_{g}\}
respectively. Applying the stabilization move (Figure
1
) if necessary, we may assume that
g\geq 1
. Let
I
be the set of all intersection points between the lower and upper curves, and
n:=|I|
. For any
p\in I
, there exist unique
i,j
such that
p\in\eta_{i}\cap\mu_{j}
. If
p
is the
x
-th intersection point starting from the base point of
\eta_{i}
, and the
y
-th point from the base point of
\mu_{j}
, then we write
l(p)=(i,x)
and
u(p)=(j,y)
. It is immediate to see that
l,u:I\rightarrow{\mathbb{N}}^{2}
are injective maps. We order
I
in two different ways via the lexicographical orderings of
l(I)
and
u(I)
, and denote the corresponding ordered lists of intersection points by
I_{*}=\{p_{1},\dots,p_{n}\}
and
I^{*}=\{p^{1},\dots,p^{n}\}
. Then we can define a permutation
{\sigma}_{D}\in\operatorname{Sym}_{n}
on
\{1,\ldots,n\}
via
p_{\sigma_{D}(r)}=p^{r}
for all
1\leq r\leq n
. This in turn determines a linear isomorphism, again denoted by
{\sigma}_{D}:H^{\otimes n}\rightarrow H^{\otimes n}
, that permutes tensor components.

Let
T:H\rightarrow H
be the linear map
T(x):=\alpha^{-1}(x_{(1)})\cdot S^{-2}(x_{(2)})\cdot\alpha(x_{(3)})
, where we use the suppressed Sweedler notation (see Section
2
),
S
is the antipode of
H
, and
\alpha\in H^{*}
is the distinguished grouplike element (cf.
[
20
, p. 118]
). Note that
T
and
S
commute.
The framed Heegaard diagram
D
of
M
gives rise to a generalization of the Sweedler power operator

(3.2)

P(M,D,H):=\left(\bigotimes_{j=1}^{g}m_{j}\right)\circ{\sigma}_{D}\circ\left(\bigotimes_{r=1}^{n}S^{s_{r}}T^{t_{r}}\right)\circ\left(\bigotimes_{i=1}^{g}{\Delta}^{|\eta_{i}\cap I|}\right):H^{\otimes g}\rightarrow H^{\otimes g}\,,

where for any
p_{r}\in I_{*}
, we define
s(p_{r})
and
t(p_{r})
by

(3.3)

s(p_{r}):=2(\theta_{\eta_{i}}(p_{r})-\theta_{\mu_{j}}(p_{r}))+\dfrac{1}{2}\,,\quad t(p_{r}):=\phi_{\eta_{i}}(p_{r})-\phi_{\mu_{j}}(p_{r})\,,

and we may simply write
s_{r}
and
t_{r}
for
s(p_{r})
and
t(p_{r})
respectively. For
1\leq j\leq g
,
m_{j}:H^{\otimes|\mu_{j}\cap I|}\rightarrow H
is the multiplication (corresponding to the partition of
I^{*}
by the upper curve
\mu_{j}
) . If a lower curve
\eta_{i}
has no intersection with any upper curve, it is understood that
{\Delta}^{|\eta_{i}\cap I|}({\Lambda}_{\theta(\eta_{i})})={\varepsilon}({\Lambda}_{\theta(\eta_{i})})1_{H}
, see (
2.1
).

Recall the conventions of
{\Lambda}_{n-\frac{1}{2}}
and
{\lambda}_{n-\frac{1}{2}}
defined in (
2.4
) for any integer
n
.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
H
be a finite-dimensional Hopf algebra over a field
{\Bbbk}
,
M
a 3-manifold with framing
\mathfrak{f}
and
D
a framed Heegaard diagram of
M
representing
\mathfrak{f}
. The Kuperberg invariant of the framed manifold
(M,\mathfrak{f})
based on
H
is defined to be the scalar

(3.4)

K(M,\mathfrak{f},H):=\left\langle\bigotimes\limits_{j=1}^{g}{\lambda}_{-\theta(\mu_{j})},\,P(M,D,H)\left(\bigotimes\limits_{i=1}^{g}{\Lambda}_{\theta(\eta_{i})}\right)\right\rangle\in{\Bbbk}\,,

where
g
is the genus of
D
,
\{\mu_{i}\}_{i=1}^{g}
and
\{\eta_{i}\}_{i=1}^{g}
are upper and lower curves of
D
respectively,
{\lambda}\in\int^{r}_{H^{*}}
and
{\Lambda}\in\int^{l}_{H}
are a pair of normalized integrals, and the bracket represents the evaluation of an element in
H^{\otimes g}
against an element in
(H^{*})^{\otimes g}
.

By the above definition, an easy way to get the formal expressions of the Kuperberg invariant can be described as follows. Using the notations above, for each lower curve
\eta_{i}
, we write
|\eta_{i}\cap I|=n_{i}
,
L^{i}:={\Lambda}_{\theta(\eta_{i})}
, and
{\Delta}^{n_{i}}(L^{i})=L^{i}_{(1)}\otimes\cdots\otimes L^{i}_{(n_{i})}
, and write

L=L^{1}_{(1)}{\,\otimes\,}\cdots{\,\otimes\,}L^{1}_{(n_{1})}{\,\otimes\,}L^{2}_{(1)}{\,\otimes\,}\cdots{\,\otimes\,}L^{1}_{(n_{2})}{\,\otimes\,}\cdots{\,\otimes\,}L^{g}_{(1)}{\,\otimes\,}\cdots{\,\otimes\,}L^{g}_{(n_{g})}=L^{[1]}{\,\otimes\,}\cdots{\,\otimes\,}L^{[n]}\,.

Now we go through the following process to write down the Kuperberg invariant:

(1)

Label the intersection points by the coproduct factors of the
L^{i}
’s according to the order of the point on the lower curves: If an intersection point
p
satisfies
l(p)=(i,x)
, then we label
p
on the diagram by
S^{s(p)}T^{t(p)}(L^{i}_{(x)})
, where the powers of
S
and
T
are given by (
3.3
). For example, the first intersection point
p
on
\eta_{i}
is labeled by
S^{s_{p}}T^{t_{p}}(L^{i}_{(1)})
.

(2)

The permutation
\sigma_{D}
and
\bigotimes_{j=1}^{g}m_{j}
is implemented as follows. For each upper curve
\mu_{j}
, starting from the base point, concatenate the labels on the intersection points on it according to its orientation, we will get a word of symbols
S^{s(p)}T^{t(p)}(L^{i}_{(x)})
denoted by
W_{j}
. For example, suppose
|\mu_{1}\cap I|=k_{1}
and the intersection points on
\mu_{1}
are
p^{1},...,p^{k_{1}}
, then
W_{1}
is
S^{s(p^{1})}T^{t(p^{1})}(L^{i_{1}}_{(x_{1})})\cdots S^{s(p^{k_{1}})}T^{t(p^{k_{1}})}(L^{i_{k_{1}}}_{(x_{k_{1}})})
, where for
j=1,...,k_{1}
,
(i_{j},x_{j})=l(p_{\sigma_{D}(j)})=l(p^{j})
.

(3)

Evaluate
\langle{\lambda}_{-\theta(\mu_{j})},W_{j}\rangle
for each
\mu_{j}
and compute their products. Since
L
is a sum of simple tensors, the Kuperberg invariant should be a summation of these products for each summand of
L
.

Convention 3.2
.

In the remainder of this paper, we will always work with a pair of normalized integrals, so we will drop the superscripts for left/right (co)integrals of Hopf algebras. More precisely, for any finite-dimensional Hopf algebra
H
, we will always denote a pair of normalized integrals for
H
by
{\lambda}\in\int^{r}_{H^{*}}
and
{\Lambda}\in\int^{l}_{H}
such that
{\lambda}({\Lambda})=1
.

The minimal genus of Heegaard diagrams of
M
is called the genus of
M
. In the following, we give several examples of Kuperberg invariants of 3-manifolds of small genera.

.

. In principle, we should be able to compute the Kuperberg invariant using a framed Heegaard diagram with neither handles nor upper/lower curves. [

Proposition 3.4
.

Let
H
be a finite-dimensional Hopf algebra over an arbitrary base field,

be distinguished grouplike elements. Then for any framing
\mathfrak{f}
of

such that

In particular,

We consider the genus one 3-manifold

4.
Gauge invariance of the Kuperberg invariant of lens spaces

In this section, we study the gauge invariance of the Kuperberg invariants of framed 3-manifolds admitting Heegaard splittings of genus 1, which are called lens spaces. In a genus 1 Heegaard diagram of a lens space, there is only 1 lower curve and 1 upper curve. One can always fix the lower curve so that the lens space is determined (up to homeomorphism) by the homotopy class of the upper curve, which is parameterized by a pair of coprime integers
n,k\in{\mathbb{Z}}
. Such a lens space is said to have type
(n,k)
and is denoted by
L(n,k)
(see
[
41
, Chap. 9]
). In particular, we have homeomorphisms
L(1,k)\cong{\mathbb{S}}^{3}
for all
k\in{\mathbb{Z}}
,
L(0,1)\cong{\mathbb{S}}^{2}\times{\mathbb{S}}^{1}
, and

(4.1)

L(n,k)\cong L(n,-k)\cong L(-n,-k)\cong L(n,k+mn)\quad(\forall m\in{\mathbb{Z}})

for all coprime integers
n,k
.

By Propositions
3.4
and
3.6
, the Kuperberg invariants of the lens spaces
{\mathbb{S}}^{3}
and
{\mathbb{S}}^{2}\times{\mathbb{S}}^{1}
are gauge invariants of the underlying Hopf algebras for any choice of framings. The main theorem of this section is an extension of these results to all lens spaces, which is stated as follows.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
n
,
k
be coprime integers, and
H
a finite-dimensional Hopf algebra over a field
{\Bbbk}
. For any framing
\mathfrak{f}
on the lens space
L(n,k)
, the Kuperberg invariant
K(L(n,k),\mathfrak{f},H)
is a gauge invariant of
H
.

In light of (
4.1
) and Propositions
3.4
and
3.6
, we only have to prove the theorem for
0<k<n
, which is what we assume in the following.

We start with our presentation of a Heegaard diagram of
L(n,k)
without combing information, which is shown in Figure
7
.
Let
r
be the remainder upon the division of
n
by
k
. In Figure
7
, the blue lines represent the upper curve and the the horizontal green line is the lower curve.
A strand labeled by
k
,

or

is understood as the corresponding number of parallel strands of the upper curve, and the box is used to denote the place where we separate the strands. The two black circles are the attaching circles of one handle. In particular, there are exactly
n
strands of the upper curve between the attaching circles and passing through the lower curve, i.e., the upper curve and the lower curve have
n
intersection points. As before, we denote the set of intersection points between the lower and upper curve by
I
, and an element in

is called an
I
-point. We orient the lower and upper curves so that the lower curves goes from left to right, and the upper curve go through the lower curve from top to bottom.

.

Proposition 4.6
.

Let
n>k>0
be a pair of coprime integers such that
(n-k)
is odd, and denote
k_{0}=\tfrac{n-k-1}{2}
. Then the 2-combed Heegaard diagram
D
in Figure
13
is a framed Heegaard diagram of
L(n,k)
with
\theta_{\mu}=\theta_{\eta}=\phi_{\eta}=\tfrac{1}{2}
and
\phi_{\mu}=-\tfrac{1}{2}
. The permutation
{\sigma}_{D}
is given by
{\sigma}_{D}(i)=\overline{k\cdot(i-1)}
for
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
, where
\overline{a}
is the positive residue of
a
modulo
n
. Moreover, the sequence
\{s_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
satisfies the following recursive relations with
s_{n}=1
:

(i)

If
1\leq i\leq k_{0}
, then
s_{\overline{i+k}}=s_{i}+2
;

(ii)

If
k_{0}+1\leq i\leq n-k-1
, then
s_{\overline{i+k}}=s_{i}-2
;

(iii)

If
n-k\leq i\leq n
, then
s_{\overline{i+k}}=s_{i}
.

In particular,
s_{n}=s_{n-k}=s_{k}=1
,
s_{i}
is an odd integer for all
1\leq i\leq n
, and these recursive relations completely determine
\{s_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
.

Proof.

By definition, to show that
D
is a framed Heegaard diagram, it suffices to count the rotation numbers in Figure
13
and show that
\theta_{\eta}=\phi_{\eta}
for the lower curve
\eta
(green), and
\theta_{\mu}=-\phi_{\mu}
for the upper curve
\mu
(blue).

We first analyze the rotation numbers of the lower curve
\eta
. Start from
\xi_{1}
and go along
\eta
to the right, it is easy to see that for
1\leq i\leq n-1
, we have
\theta_{\eta}(p_{i})=0
, and
\theta_{\eta}(p_{n})=\tfrac{1}{4}
.
As we go through the handle and come back to
\xi_{1}
, the combing
b_{1}
and
\eta
are in opposite direction, so the total rotation number
\theta_{\eta}
is equal to
\tfrac{1}{2}
.
By design, there is no intersection between the twist front and
\eta
, and the direction of the twist front aligns with that of
\eta
near
\xi_{1}
. Thus, we have
\phi_{\eta}=\tfrac{1}{2}=\theta_{\eta}
.

Now we study the rotation numbers of the upper curve
\mu
. We will compute
\theta_{\mu}(p_{i})
at the
I
-points, while finding patterns for
{\sigma}_{D}
and
s_{i}=2(\theta_{\eta}(p_{i})-\theta_{\mu}(p_{i}))+\tfrac{1}{2}
. Reading directly from
D
, we find
p^{1}=p_{n}
or
{\sigma}_{D}(1)=n
. Moreover, Observation
4.2
implies that when traveling along
\mu
, we always go from
p_{i}
to
p_{\overline{i+k}}
. Thus,
{\sigma}_{D}(i)=\overline{k\cdot(i-1)}
. At
p_{n}
, we have
\theta_{\mu}(p_{n})=0
. So we have

s_{n}=2(\theta_{\eta}(p_{n})-\theta_{\mu}(p_{n}))+\frac{1}{2}=2\cdot(\frac{1}{4}-0)+\frac{1}{2}=1\,.

We have the following cases when computing
\theta_{\mu}(p_{i})
for
1\leq i\leq n-1
.

Starting from
p_{n}
, the next
I
-point we encounter is
p_{\overline{n+k}}=p_{k}
. The local picture is illustrated in Figure
14
where only the relevant part of
\mu
is drawn.
We go downwards from
p_{n}
and enter the handle at the attaching circle on the right, during which
\theta_{\mu}
increases by
\tfrac{1}{2}
. Then we exit from the left attaching circle to reach
p_{k}
, and
\theta_{\mu}
decreases by
\tfrac{3}{4}
. Therefore,

, and we have

Definition 4.7
.

Let
n
be a positive integer and
l
an integer coprime to
n
.

(a)

We define
{\sigma}_{(n,l)}\in{\operatorname{Sym}}_{n}
by
{\sigma}_{(n,l)}(i)=\overline{il}
for
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
. Since
{\sigma}_{(n,l)}(n)=n
, we also consider
{\sigma}_{(n,l)}\in{\operatorname{Sym}}_{n-1}
by restriction.

(b)

Let
H
be a finite-dimensional Hopf algebra, and
n>k>0
an integer with
(n-k)
odd. We define the
{\Bbbk}
-linear operator
P^{(n,-k)}:H\rightarrow H
as

P^{(n,-k)}:=m\circ{\sigma}_{(n,-k)}\circ\left(\bigotimes_{i=1}^{n-1}S^{2c_{i}}\right)\circ\Delta^{n-1}

where the sequence
\{c_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
is determined by the following recursive relations with
c_{n}=0
:

(i)

If
1\leq i\leq k_{0}
, then
c_{i+k}=c_{i}+1
where
k_{0}=\frac{n-k-1}{2}
;

(ii)

If
k_{0}+1\leq i\leq 2k_{0}
, then
c_{i+k}=c_{i}-1
;

(iii)

If
2k_{0}+1\leq i\leq n
, then
c_{\overline{i+k}}=c_{i}
.

(c)

Finally, we define
\delta(i):=c_{\overline{i+k}}-c_{i}
for
i=1,\dots,n
.

Remark 4.8
.

For any coprime integers
n,k
with
(n-k)
odd, it is obvious that the sequences
\{c_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
above and
\{s_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
in Proposition
4.6
are related by
s_{i}=2c_{i}+1
for
i=1,\dots,n
. In particular,
c_{n}=c_{n-k}=0
.

Theorem 4.9
.

Let
H
be any finite-dimensional Hopf algebra. For any coprime integers
n>k>0
such that
(n-k)
is odd, we have

\displaystyle K(L(n,k),\mathfrak{f}_{R},H)={\operatorname{Tr}}(S\circ P^{(n,-k)})\,.

Proof.

By (
3.4
), the Kuperberg invariant is given by

K(L(n,k),\mathfrak{f}_{R},H)={\lambda}_{-\theta(\mu)}\left(m\circ{\sigma}_{D}\circ\left(\bigotimes_{i=1}^{n}S^{s_{i}}\right)\circ\Delta^{n}({\Lambda}_{\theta(\eta)})\right)

where
{\lambda}\in\int^{r}_{H^{*}}
and
{\Lambda}\in\int^{l}_{H}
are a pair of normalized integrals. Recall (
2.5
) that
{\lambda}_{-\frac{1}{2}}={\lambda}
and
{\Lambda}_{\frac{1}{2}}={\Lambda}
, by Proposition
4.6
, we have

(4.3)

\displaystyle K(L(n,k),\mathfrak{f}_{R},H)={\lambda}_{-\frac{1}{2}}\left(m\circ{\sigma}_{D}\circ\left(\bigotimes_{i=1}^{n}S^{s_{i}}\right)\circ\Delta^{n}({\Lambda}_{\frac{1}{2}})\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\displaystyle\lambda}\left(m\circ{\sigma}_{D}\circ\left(\bigotimes_{i=1}^{n}S^{s_{i}}\right)\circ\Delta^{n}({\Lambda})\right)={\lambda}\left(S^{s_{n}}({\Lambda}_{(n)})S^{s_{k}}({\Lambda}_{(k)})\cdots S^{s_{n-k}}({\Lambda}_{(n-k)})\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\displaystyle\lambda}\left(S({\Lambda}_{(n)})S\left(S^{2c_{n-k}}({\Lambda}_{(n-k)})S^{2c_{\overline{n-2k}}}({\Lambda}_{(\overline{n-2k})})\cdots S^{2c_{k}}({\Lambda}_{(k)})\right)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\displaystyle\lambda}\left(S({\Lambda}_{(2)})\left(S\circ P^{(n,-k)}\right)({\Lambda}_{(1)})\right)={\operatorname{Tr}}(S\circ P^{(n,-k)})\,,

where the last equality follows from Theorem
2.3
(v).
∎

Now we are ready to prove the gauge invariance of
K(L(n,k),\mathfrak{f}_{\operatorname{R}},H)
.

.

is odd, denote by

\displaystyle K(L(n,k),\mathfrak{f}_{L},H^{{\operatorname{op}}})=

\displaystyle{\lambda}S(m^{{\operatorname{op}}}\circ\tau_{n,k}\circ(\otimes_{i=1}^{n}S^{-s_{i}})\circ\Delta^{(n)}(S({\Lambda})))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\lambda}S(m^{{\operatorname{op}}}\circ\tau_{n,k}\circ(\otimes_{i=1}^{n}S^{-s_{i}+1}({\Lambda}_{(n+1-i)})))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\lambda}S(S^{-s_{n+1-k}+1}({\Lambda}_{(k)})\cdots S^{-s_{1+k}+1}({\Lambda}_{(n-k)})S^{-s_{1}+1}({\Lambda}_{(n)}))

Let

,

, and

\displaystyle K(L(n,k),\mathfrak{f}_{L},H^{{\operatorname{op}}})=

\displaystyle{\lambda}S(S^{\tilde{c}_{n-\tilde{k}}}({\Lambda}_{(n-\tilde{k})})\cdots S^{\tilde{c}_{\tilde{k}}}({\Lambda}_{(\tilde{k})})S^{\tilde{c}_{n}}({\Lambda}_{(n)}))

4.3.
Gauge invariance of
K(L(n,k),\mathfrak{f},H)
with arbitrary framing
\mathfrak{f}

In this subsection, we study the Kuperberg invariants of lens spaces with general framings and prove their gauge invariance.
In order to do it, we start by giving a brief description of the homotopy classification of framings on a general 3-manifold
M
.
Once a framing on
M
is fixed, the set of framings up to homotopy can be identified with
[M,{\operatorname{SO}}(3)]
, the homotopy set of maps from
M
to
{\operatorname{SO}}(3)
. Any map from
M
to
{\operatorname{SO}}(3)
can be viewed as an extension from the 0-skeleton
M^{0}
of
M
to
M=M^{3}
. By obstruction theory, up to homotopy, maps from
M^{1}
to
{\operatorname{SO}}(3)
which can be extended to
M^{2}
are classified by
H^{1}(M,\pi_{1}{\operatorname{SO}}(3))=H^{1}(M,\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})
. Since
\pi_{2}{\operatorname{SO}}(3)=0
, any of the extendable map above can be further extended to
M=M^{3}
, and up to homotopy, the extensions of such a map to the whole manifold
M
are classified by
H^{3}(M,\pi_{3}{\operatorname{SO}}(3))=H^{3}(M,\mathbb{Z})
. Therefore, each map from
M^{2}
to
{\operatorname{SO}}(3)
has
|H^{3}(M,\mathbb{Z})|
extensions to
M
up to homotopy, i.e.,
[M,{\operatorname{SO}}(3)]=H^{1}(M,\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})\times H^{3}(M,\mathbb{Z})
.
Relative to the fixed choice of framing, we call the
H^{1}(M,\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})
-component of any framing its
spin class
, and the
H^{3}(M,\mathbb{Z})
-component its
degree
, see
[
20
, Sec. 2]
for details.

Let
H
be a finite-dimensional Hopf algebra. By
[
20
]
, when the degree of a framing
\mathfrak{f}
of
M
changes by +1, the corresponding Kuperberg invariant
K(M,\mathfrak{f},H)
gets a factor of
\alpha(g)
, which is a gauge invariant. Therefore, if
K(M,\mathfrak{f},H)
is a gauge invariant for some framing
\mathfrak{f}
, then
K(M,\mathfrak{f}^{\prime},H)
is also a gauge invariant for any framing
\mathfrak{f}^{\prime}
that has the same spin class as
\mathfrak{f}
. Consequently, in order to prove the gauge invariance of the Kuperberg invariant associated to an arbitrary framing on
M
, we only need to show that for any spin class, there is a framing
\mathfrak{f}
on
M
in that spin class such that
K(M,\mathfrak{f},H)
is a gauge invariant.

The spin class of the diagram framing
\mathfrak{f}
of
M
associated to a framed Heegaard diagram with combings
b_{1}
and
b_{2}
can be read from
b_{1}
as follows. By the universal coefficient theorem and the Poincaré duality, we have the following split short exact sequence

0\rightarrow H^{1}(M,{\mathbb{Z}})\otimes{\mathbb{Z}}/2{\mathbb{Z}}\rightarrow H^{1}(M,{\mathbb{Z}}/2{\mathbb{Z}})\rightarrow{\operatorname{Tor}}(H^{2}(M,{\mathbb{Z}}),{\mathbb{Z}}/2{\mathbb{Z}})\rightarrow 0\,,

and the spin class of
\mathfrak{f}
is determined by its components in
H^{1}(M,{\mathbb{Z}})\otimes{\mathbb{Z}}/2{\mathbb{Z}}
and
{\operatorname{Tor}}(H^{2}(M,{\mathbb{Z}}),{\mathbb{Z}}/2{\mathbb{Z}})
(“the torsion part”). In particular, if
M
satisfies
H^{1}(M,{\mathbb{Z}})\cong H_{2}(M,{\mathbb{Z}})=0
, then the spin class of
\mathfrak{f}
is completely determined by its torsion part. In
[
20
]
, it is argued that the torsion part of
\mathfrak{f}
is given by the characteristic class
c\in H^{2}(M,{\mathbb{Z}})
of
b_{1}
(by viewing
b_{1}
as a map from
M
to
S^{2}
). Moreover, the reason for
c\in{\operatorname{Tor}}(H^{2}(M,{\mathbb{Z}}),{\mathbb{Z}}/2{\mathbb{Z}})
is that
2c
is the obstruction of the extension of
b_{1}
to
\mathfrak{f}
, which has to vanish.

In this way, we are able to compare the spin classes of two diagram framings of a 3-manifold with vanishing second integral cohomology, and lens spaces can be easily shown to satisfy this condition (for example by the Mayer-Vietoris sequence associated to a genus 1 Heegaard diagram such as the one in Figure
7
).
This enables us to obtain our main theorem on the gauge invariance of the Kuperberg invariants for lens spaces.

Theorem 4.16
.

Let
n
,
k
be coprime integers. For any framing
\mathfrak{f}
of lens space
L(n,k)
, the Kuperberg invariant
K(L(n,k),\mathfrak{f},H)
is a gauge invariant of finite-dimensional Hopf algebras.

Proof.

Denote
L(n,k)
by
{\mathbb{L}}
for short. Since
H^{1}({\mathbb{L}},{\mathbb{Z}})=0
,
H^{1}({\mathbb{L}},{\mathbb{Z}}/2{\mathbb{Z}})\cong{\operatorname{Tor}}(H^{2}({\mathbb{L}},{\mathbb{Z}}),{\mathbb{Z}}/2{\mathbb{Z}})\cong{\operatorname{Tor}}({\mathbb{Z}}/n{\mathbb{Z}},{\mathbb{Z}}/2{\mathbb{Z}})
by Poincaré duality. We have the following two cases.

When
n
is odd,
H^{1}({\mathbb{L}},{\mathbb{Z}}/2{\mathbb{Z}})=0
. Therefore, up to degree, any framing
\mathfrak{f}
on
{\mathbb{L}}
is homotopic to either
\mathfrak{f}_{L}
(when
k
is odd) or
\mathfrak{f}_{R}
(when
k
is even). Thus, up to a power of
\alpha(g)
,
K({\mathbb{L}},\mathfrak{f},H)
is equal to
K({\mathbb{L}},\mathfrak{f}_{L},H)
or
K({\mathbb{L}},\mathfrak{f}_{R},H)
, and we are done by Theorems
4.10
and
4.13
.

When
n
is even, then

has to be odd, and

. In light of the above discussions, by Theorems

and

, it suffices to show that

and
\mathfrak{f}_{R}
have different spin classes. Denote the 2 combings in the framed Heegaard diagram for

by

and

\mathfrak{f}_{\operatorname{R}}
by

and

respectively. To compare the spin classes of

and
\mathfrak{f}_{R}
, we only need to compare the characteristic classes
c
,

of
b_{1}
and
b_{1}^{\prime}
respectively. The relative characteristic class

can be presented by its Poincare dual

. According to
[

, this homology class is represented geometrically by a closed curve

on which

. Apparently, if

in

, then
\mathfrak{f}_{\operatorname{L}}
and
\mathfrak{f}_{\operatorname{R}}
have different spin classes in
H^{1}({\mathbb{L}},{\mathbb{Z}}/2{\mathbb{Z}})
.

To find the

, we compare the framed Heegaard diagrams

(resp.

Corollary 4.17
.

Let
H
be a finite-dimensional Hopf algebra over a field
{\Bbbk}
, and let
D(H)
be the Drinfeld double of
H
. Let
n,k\in{\mathbb{Z}}
be a pair of coprime integers, and denote
L(n,k)
by
{\mathbb{L}}
.

(i)

For any framing
\mathfrak{f}
of
{\mathbb{L}}
,
K({\mathbb{L}},\mathfrak{f},D(H))=K({\mathbb{L}},\mathfrak{f},H)\cdot K({\mathbb{L}},\mathfrak{f},H^{{\operatorname{op}}})
.

(ii)

The value
K({\mathbb{L}},\mathfrak{f},D(H))
is independent of the framing
\mathfrak{f}
of
{\mathbb{L}}
.

Proof.

(i) By
[
4
]
,
D(H)
is gauge equivalent to
H\otimes(H^{*})^{{\operatorname{cop}}}\cong H\otimes(H^{{\operatorname{op}}})^{*}
, so by Theorem
4.16
and the multiplicativity of the Kuperberg invariant
[
20
, Sec. 5]
, we have

K({\mathbb{L}},\mathfrak{f},D(H))=K({\mathbb{L}},\mathfrak{f},H)\cdot K({\mathbb{L}},\mathfrak{f},(H^{{\operatorname{op}}})^{*})=K({\mathbb{L}},\mathfrak{f},H)\cdot K({\mathbb{L}},\mathfrak{f},H^{{\operatorname{op}}})\,,

where the second equality follows from the invariance of the Kuperberg invariant under taking duals of Hopf algebras, which is argued in
[
20
, Sec. 5]
.

(ii) As is argued in
[
20
]
, when we fix the spin class of
\mathfrak{f}
and change its Hopf degree, the resulting Kuperberg invariant differs only by a power of
\alpha(g)
of
H
. Since
D(H)
is unimodular, the Kuperberg invariant is independent of the degree of the framing
\mathfrak{f}
. It remains to show that it is also independent of the spin class of
\mathfrak{f}
. Since
H^{1}({\mathbb{L}},{\mathbb{Z}}/2{\mathbb{Z}})=0
for odd
n
, the framings
\mathfrak{f}
,
\mathfrak{f}_{\operatorname{L}}
,
\mathfrak{f}_{\operatorname{R}}
belong to the same spin class of
{\mathbb{L}}
whenever they are well-defined. In particular, we have

K({\mathbb{L}},\mathfrak{f},D(H))=\begin{cases}K(L(|n|,\overline{k}),\mathfrak{f}_{\operatorname{L}},D(H))&\text{if $\overline{k}$ is odd};\\
K(L(|n|,\overline{k}),\mathfrak{f}_{\operatorname{R}},D(H))&\text{if $\overline{-k}$ is odd},\end{cases}

where
\overline{k}
again denotes the least positive residue of
k
modulo
n
.

Now, we assume
n
is even. Then both
\overline{k}
and
\overline{-k}
are odd. If
\mathfrak{f}
has the same spin class as
\mathfrak{f}_{\operatorname{L}}
, then

K({\mathbb{L}},\mathfrak{f},D(H))=K(L(|n|,\overline{k}),\mathfrak{f}_{\operatorname{L}},D(H))=K(L(|n|,\overline{k}),\mathfrak{f}_{\operatorname{L}},H)K(L(|n|,\overline{-k}),\mathfrak{f}_{\operatorname{R}},H)

by Proposition
4.12
. On the other hand, if
\mathfrak{f}
and
\mathfrak{f}_{\operatorname{R}}
are in the same spin class, then

K({\mathbb{L}},\mathfrak{f},D(H))=K(L(|n|,\overline{-k}),\mathfrak{f}_{\operatorname{R}},D(H))=K(L(|n|,\overline{-k}),\mathfrak{f}_{\operatorname{R}},H)K(L(|n|,\overline{k}),\mathfrak{f}_{\operatorname{L}},H)\,.

Therefore,
K({\mathbb{L}},\mathfrak{f},D(H))
is also independent of the spin class of
\mathfrak{f}
.

∎

Example 4.18
.

Let
n\geq 2
be an integer. For any primitive
n
-th root of unity
\zeta\in{\mathbb{C}}
, let
T(\zeta)
be the Hopf algebra over
{\mathbb{C}}
with the algebra generated by
x,g
subject to the relations:

x^{n}=0,\,g^{n}=1,\,gx=\zeta xg\,.

The coalgebra structure and antipode of
T(\zeta)
are given by

\begin{split}&{\Delta}(g)=g\otimes g,\,\quad{\Delta}(x)=x\otimes g+1\otimes x,\,\\
&{\varepsilon}(g)=1,\,\,{\varepsilon}(x)=0,\,\,S(g)=g^{-1},\,\,S(x)=-xg^{-1}.\end{split}

This Hopf algebra, known as the Taft algebra
[
47
]
, has dimension
n^{2}
and
\{x^{i}g^{j}\mid 0\leq i,j\leq n-1\}
forms a basis for
T(\zeta)
. Moreover,

{\Lambda}=(\sum_{i=1}^{n}g^{i})x^{n-1}\in\int^{l}_{T(\zeta)}
and
{\lambda}=\delta_{x^{n-1}}\in\int^{r}_{T(\zeta)^{*}}
form a pair of normalized integral for
T(\zeta)
, i.e.,
{\lambda}({\Lambda})=1
.

(i)

Consider the lens spaces
L(7,1)
and
L(7,2)
. As is explained in the proof of Theorem
4.16
, since
H^{1}(L(7,1),{\mathbb{Z}}/2{\mathbb{Z}})=H^{1}(L(7,2),{\mathbb{Z}}/2{\mathbb{Z}})=0
, the spin classes of all framings of
L(7,1)
are equal, which means any two framings of
L(7,1)
are different only by their Hopf degrees. The same is true for
L(7,2)
. In particular, for any finite-dimensional Hopf algebra
H
, the Kuperberg invariant for
L(7,1)
with any framing is equal to
K(L(7,1),\mathfrak{f}_{L},H)
up to a root of unity, and the invariant for
L(7,2)
with any framing equals
K(L(7,2),\mathfrak{f}_{R},H)
up to some root of unity. By Theorem
4.9
and Proposition
4.12
, we have

\displaystyle K(L(7,1),\mathfrak{f}_{L},H)=

\displaystyle\lambda(\Lambda_{(7)}\Lambda_{(6)}\Lambda_{(5)}\Lambda_{4}\Lambda_{(3)}\Lambda_{(2)}\Lambda_{(1)})\,,

\displaystyle K(L(7,2),\mathfrak{f}_{R},H)=

\displaystyle\lambda S(\Lambda_{(5)}S^{2}(\Lambda_{(3)})\Lambda_{(1)}\Lambda_{(6)}S^{2}(\Lambda_{(4)})\Lambda_{(2)}\Lambda_{(7)})\,,

where
{\lambda}\in H^{*}
,
{\Lambda}\in H
is a pair of normalized integrals. With the help of the
GAP
algebra system, we obtain that
K(L(7,1),\mathfrak{f}_{L},T(\zeta_{7}))=-42\zeta_{7}-35\zeta_{7}^{2}-28\zeta_{7}^{3}-21\zeta_{7}^{4}-14\zeta_{7}^{5}-7\zeta_{7}^{6}
, while
K(L(7,2),\mathfrak{f}_{R},T(\zeta_{7}))=0
, where
\zeta_{7}
is a primitive 7-th root of unity. Consequently, the Kuperberg invariants of
L(7,1)
and
L(7,2)
for any choices of framings cannot be equal to each other. Therefore, the Kuperberg invariant can distinguish
L(7,1)
and
L(7,2)
up to homeomorphism, although they are homotopic (see, for example,
[
41
]
).

(ii)

Moreover, the Kuperberg invariant can also distinguish spin classes of framings of a manifold. For example, for any finite-dimensional Hopf algebra
H
, we have

6.
Gauge invariance of
\tilde{\nu}_{n,k}(H)

In this section, we introduce another gauge invariant
\tilde{\nu}_{n,k}(H)
for finite-dimensional Hopf algebras
H
, which are similar to
K(L(n,k),\mathfrak{f},H)
, but not directly obtained from the Kuperberg invariants of
3
-manifolds. It is unclear whether or how
\tilde{\nu}_{n,k}(H)
and
\nu_{n,k}(H)
are related.

Definition 6.1
.

Let
H
be a finite-dimensional Hopf algebra
H
. For any a coprime integers
n,k
with
n>1
, we define the “shuffled” Sweedler power

{\tilde{P}}^{(n,k)}=m\circ{\sigma}_{(n,k)}\circ{\Delta}^{n-1}\,,

and

\tilde{\nu}_{n,k}(H):={\operatorname{Tr}}(S\circ{\tilde{P}}^{(n,k)})\,.

Note that

\displaystyle{\tilde{P}}^{(n,k)}(x)=\sum\limits_{(x)}x_{(\overline{k})}x_{(\overline{2k})}\cdots x_{(\overline{(n-1)k})}\quad\text{ for }x\in H\,.

In particular,
{\tilde{P}}^{(n,1)}=P^{(n,1)}
, and so
\tilde{\nu}_{n,1}(H)=\nu_{n,1}(H)
.

It follows from Radford’s trace formula (Theorem
2.3
(v)) that

\tilde{\nu}_{n,k}(H)=\sum{\lambda}(S({\Lambda}_{(2)})S({\tilde{P}}^{(n,k)}({\Lambda}_{(1)})))=\sum{\lambda}S({\Lambda}_{(\overline{k})}{\Lambda}_{(\overline{2k})}\cdots{\Lambda}_{(\overline{(n-1)k})}{\Lambda}_{(n)})

for any normalized integrals
{\lambda}\in\int_{H^{*}}^{r}
and
{\Lambda}\in\int_{H}^{l}
for
H
. Thus,
\nu_{n,1}(H)=\tilde{\nu}_{n,1}(H)={\operatorname{Tr}}(S\circ P^{(n)})
, which is equal to the higher indicators
\nu^{\rm KMN}_{n}(H)
defined in
[
14
]
. Therefore, both
\nu_{n,k}(H)
and
\tilde{\nu}_{n,k}(H)
are generalizations of
\nu^{\rm KMN}_{n}(H)
. We will show that
\tilde{\nu}_{n,k}(H)
is also a gauge invariant of
H
as follows.

Theorem 6.2
.

Let
H
be a finite-dimensional Hopf algebra over any field, and
F\in H\otimes H
a 2-cocycle of
H
. Then for any pair of coprime integers
n,k
with
n>1
, we have

\tilde{\nu}_{n,k}(H_{F})=\tilde{\nu}_{n,k}(H)\,.

Proof.

By definition,
\tilde{\nu}_{n,k}(H)=\tilde{\nu}_{n,\overline{k}}(H)
for all
k
coprime to
n
, and the gauge invariance of
\tilde{\nu}_{n,1}
is proved in
[
14
]
. Therefore, we assume without loss of generality that
2\leq k<n
.
It follows from Radford’s trace formula (Theorem
2.3
(v)) that

\tilde{\nu}_{n,k}(H_{F})={\operatorname{Tr}}(S_{F}\circ{\tilde{P}}^{(n,k)}_{F})={\lambda}(S({\Lambda}_{(2)})(S_{F}\circ{\tilde{P}}^{(n,k)}_{F}({\Lambda}_{(1)})))={\lambda}(S({\Lambda}_{(2)})(S_{F}\circ Y(\Delta_{F}^{n-1}{\Lambda}_{(1)})))

for any normalized integrals
{\lambda}\in\int^{r}_{H^{*}}
and
{\Lambda}\in\int^{l}_{H}
for
H
, where
Y=m\circ{\sigma}_{(n,k)}
. Let
F_{n}=f^{[1]}_{j}\otimes\cdots\otimes f^{[n]}_{j}
and
F_{n}^{-1}=d_{i}^{[1]}{\,\otimes\,}\cdots{\,\otimes\,}d^{[n]}_{i}

\displaystyle\ \tilde{\nu}_{n,k}(H_{F})\ =\ {{\lambda}S}\Bigl(d_{i}^{[1]}Y\left(f_{j}^{[1]}{\Lambda}_{(1)}d_{i}^{[2]}{\,\otimes\,}\cdots{\,\otimes\,}f_{j}^{[n-1]}{\Lambda}_{(n-1)}d_{i}^{[n]}\right)f_{j}^{[n]}{\Lambda}_{(n)}\Bigl)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ {{\lambda}S}\biggl(d_{i}^{[1]}f_{j}^{[k]}\Lambda_{(k)}\prod_{p=2}^{n}d^{[\overline{1+(p-1)k}]}_{i}f^{[\overline{pk}]}_{j}{\Lambda}_{\left(\overline{pk}\right)}\biggl)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\dagger)}}{{=}}\ {{\lambda}S}\biggl({\Lambda}_{(k)}\,\prod_{p=2}^{n}S(d^{[1]}_{i}f^{[k]}_{j})_{(\overline{(p-1)k})}d^{[\overline{1+(p-1)k}]}_{i}f^{[\overline{pk}]}_{j}{\Lambda}_{(\overline{pk})}\biggl)

\displaystyle\ {{\lambda}S}\circ Y\biggl(\bigg(\bigotimes_{p=1}^{k-1}S(d^{[1]}_{i}f^{[k]}_{j})_{(\overline{p-k})}d_{i}^{[\overline{p-k}+1]}f_{j}^{[p]}\Lambda_{(p)}\bigg){\,\otimes\,}\Lambda_{(k)}{\,\otimes\,}\bigotimes_{p=k+1}^{n}S(d^{[1]}_{i}f^{[k]}_{j})_{(p-k)}d_{i}^{[p-k+1]}f_{j}^{[p]}\Lambda_{(p)}\biggl)

7.
Kuperberg invariants of genus 2 framed 3-manifolds

So far we have been working with 3-manifolds of genus 1. It is then natural to ask whether there exist framed 3-manifolds of higher genera whose Kuperberg invariants are gauge invariant. In this section, we answer these questions by studying a family of 3-manifolds of genus 2, whose fundamental groups are certain central extensions of triangle groups studied by Milnor in his investigation of the Brieskorn manifolds
[
29

.

We start with a brief review on the triangle groups. Let
p
,
q
,

be integers. The

\Theta^{*}(p,q,r)
admits the following presentation
[

\Theta^{*}(p,q,r)\cong\langle a,b,c\mid a^{2}=b^{2}=c^{2}=(ab)^{p}=(bc)^{q}=(ca)^{r}=1\rangle\,.

Geometrically,

is the group generated by reflections along the edges of the triangle with interior angles

\pi/q
and
\pi/r
in
\mathbb{P}(p,q,r)

\mathbb{P}(p,q,r)=\begin{cases}{\mathbb{S}}^{2}&\text{ if }\ p^{-1}+q^{-1}+r^{-1}>1\,,\\
{\mathbb{H}}^{2}&\text{ if }\ p^{-1}+q^{-1}+r^{-1}<1\,,\\
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}&\text{ if }\ p^{-1}+q^{-1}+r^{-1}=1\,,\end{cases}

where
{\mathbb{S}}^{2}
,
{\mathbb{H}}^{2}
and
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
stand for the unit sphere, the hyperbolic plane and the Euclidean plane respectively. For simplicity, we write
\mathbb{P}
for

[

, Cor. 2.5]
, the index 2 subgroup

\Theta(p,q,r)=\langle u,v,w\mid u^{p}=v^{q}=w^{r}=uvw=1\rangle\,.

Denote the connected Lie group of orientation preserving isometries of

by

\operatorname{I}^{+}(\mathbb{P})=\begin{cases}{\operatorname{SO}}(3)&\text{if }\ \mathbb{P}={\mathbb{S}}^{2}\,,\\
\operatorname{PSL}(2,{\mathbb{R}})&\text{if }\ \mathbb{P}={\mathbb{H}}^{2}\,,\\

As is listed in

If we denote the universal covering group of

and
