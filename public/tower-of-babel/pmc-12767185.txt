Heterozygous nonsense FLI1 p.Met100∗ variant results in thrombocytopenia and increased erythroid gene expression

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMegakaryocytes and platelets arise from hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells through a tightly regulated process involving various transcription factors, including erythroblast transformation–specific family member FLI1. Pathogenic variants in FLI1, particularly those affecting the erythroblast transformation–specific DNA-binding domain, have been implicated in inherited platelet disorder and are associated with abnormal giant α granules, as seen in Paris-Trousseau Syndrome. However, the precise pathophysiological mechanisms remain unclear.ObjectivesIn this study, we describe a patient with a de novo heterozygous nonsense mutation, FLI1 p.Met100∗ (c.297del) and investigate its effects on megakaryopoiesis. We hypothesized that this variant generates a truncated protein that exerts a dominant-negative effect on megakaryocyte maturation.MethodsWild-type (WT) FLI1 and FLI1 p.Met100∗ were expressed in Meg01 cells. Bulk RNA sequencing, proteomics, and flow cytometry were performed to assess their functional consequences.ResultsWestern blot analysis confirmed truncated FLI1 p.Met100∗ protein expression in Meg01 cells. Gene set enrichment analysis revealed that WT FLI1 enhanced megakaryocytic phenotypes, while suppressing erythroid phenotypes, whereas FLI1 p.Met100∗ upregulated both. Flow cytometry confirmed that erythroid markers CD235a and KLF1 were downregulated by WT FLI1 but elevated by FLI1 p.Met100∗. Additionally, WT FLI1 promoted megakaryocyte maturation and adhesion, as evidenced by increased expression of the megakaryocyte marker CD61 and adhesion markers CD34 and CD44. Moreover, we demonstrated through immunoprecipitation that both WT FLI1 and FLI1 p.Met100∗ interact with shared cofactors.ConclusionCollectively, our findings suggest that FLI1 p.Met100∗ impairs megakaryocyte maturation through a dominant-negative manner, potentially contributing to thrombocytopenia by promoting erythroid features at the expense of proper megakaryocytic development.

Introduction

Inherited platelet disorders (IPDs) are characterized by a reduced platelet count (<150 × 109/L) and/or abnormalities in platelet size and function. Advances in clinical diagnostics and next-generation sequencing have led to the identification of ∼70 genes associated with different forms of IPDs, including transcription factors such as FLI1, ETV6, GFI1B, GATA1, RUNX1, IKZF5, EVI1, NFE2, and HOXA11 [1]. However, the precise molecular mechanisms by which these genetic variants impair megakaryopoiesis are still poorly understood.

The ETS family transcription factors play an important role in hematopoiesis. These factors share a conserved C-terminal DNA-binding domain, while some, including FLI1, ERG, and ETV6, also possess an N-terminal pointed domain mediating protein-protein interactions. FLI1 was discovered alongside SPI1/PU.1 as one of the two integration sites for Friend-virus–induced erythroleukemia, leading to their abnormal expression in erythroid cells and subsequent transformation [[2], [3], [4]]. One of our recent studies demonstrated that FLI1 is highly expressed in the hemogenic endothelium and early hematopoietic progenitors derived from induced pluripotent stem cells [5]. Furthermore, it has been demonstrated by others that FLI1 is essential for hematopoietic stem cell (HSC) maintenance and differentiation into downstream hematopoietic lineages [6,7].

Most of the transcription factors that regulate early hematopoietic stem and progenitors, such as FLI1, are also critical for megakaryocyte development. The direct differentiation of megakaryocytes from HSCs may explain the close regulatory relationship between the two[[8], [9], [10]]. In addition, megakaryocytes arise from megakaryocyte-erythrocyte progenitors (MEPs), a process that is thought to be controlled by the balance of FLI1 and KLF1 [11]. Overexpression of FLI1 in cell lines and primary erythroblasts suppresses erythroid development [4,12,13], while combined ectopic expression of GATA1, FLI1, and TAL1 in hematopoietic progenitors promotes megakaryocyte expansion [14].

The involvement of FLI1 in platelet disorder was first noted in Jacobsen syndrome, which is caused by a terminal 11q deletion (∼3-20 Mb) that includes FLI1 and its close family member ETS1, both located at 11q24.3. Some of these patients present with thrombocytopenia and/or platelet dysfunction, sometimes combined with lymphopenia and/or lymphocyte dysfunction [15]. However, in Jacobsen syndrome, it remains unclear whether the observed phenotypes result from FLI1 haploinsufficiency alone or from the concurrent deletion of ETS1. Pathogenic variants in FLI1 have since been identified in individuals with IPDs, typically characterized by normal or mildly reduced platelet counts, enlarged a-granules, and impaired dense granule secretion, occasionally accompanied by alopecia, eczema, psoriasis, and recurrent viral infections [[16], [17], [18], [19]]. Most mutations occur within the ETS DNA-binding domain and follow an autosomal dominance inheritance pattern, although homozygous cases have been reported [[16], [17], [18],20]. While the exact mechanisms underlying FLI1-related platelet disorder remain unclear, certain mutations have been shown to disrupt DNA binding and cause cytoplasmic retention of FLI1 [[15], [16], [17]]. Multiple studies have suggested MYH10 as a potential marker for FLI1-associated platelet disorder [[16], [17], [18], [19],21,22]. MYH10 must be downregulated during proplatelet formation but remains elevated in patients with FLI1, RUNX1, and GATA1 mutations [23]. In this study, we describe a patient with a novel pathogenic FLI1 variant in the N-terminal region, presenting clinically with neonatal thrombocytopenia. The mutation introduces a premature stop codon at amino acid 100 (p.Met100∗), suggesting a potential role for the FLI1 N-terminal domain in disease progression. We hypothesized that this variant, resulting in a putative truncated protein, has a dominant-negative effect on megakaryocyte maturation. To investigate this, we expressed FLI1 p.Met100∗ in megakaryocytic cells and examined its molecular impact on megakaryopoiesis.

Methods

Patient data and study design

This study was conducted in accordance with the Medical Research Involving Human Subjects Act and was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Erasmus University Medical Center (MEC-2016-218). The patient was included after providing written informed consent, in compliance with national laws and regulations. Clinical data were derived from electronic patient files and included a medical history, bleeding symptoms, and laboratory results for blood counts and hemostatic tests performed prior to inclusion. Bleeding symptoms were scored on the day of inclusion using the Bleeding Assessment Tool of the International Society on Thrombosis and Haemostasis, which has been validated for platelet disorders [24]. The tool evaluates 13 predefined bleeding items, each scored from 0 (absent) to 1 to 4 according to severity. The total score is the sum of all items, with values ≥3 considered abnormal in children under 18 years [25]. Laboratory tests for hemostatic function, molecular diagnostics, and electron microscopy of patient platelets are detailed in the Supplementary Methods and Supplementary Table 1.

Cell culture

Meg01 cells were cultured in Roswell Park Memorial Institute 1640 medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific) supplemented with 10% fetal calf serum (Bodinco) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Sigma). For differentiation experiments, Meg01 cells were treated with 10 nM phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA; Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 3 days. HEK293T cells were maintained in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific) with 10% fetal calf serum and 1% penicillin/streptomycin. Cells were incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO2. For virus production, HEK293T cells were cultured in Meg01 culturing medium. Images of Meg01 cells were captured using a Bresser LCD Evos microscope. Additional experimental procedures, including the generation of in vitro–transcribed single guide RNAs (gRNAs), nucleofection, polymerase chain reaction (PCR), and Sanger sequencing, are described in the Supplementary Methods and Supplementary Table 1.

Transduction

Lentivirus was produced by transfecting HEK293T cells with either the empty vector (EV) or the FLI1 lentiviral construct (Supplementary Methods), along with the psPAX2 (Addgene #12260) and VSV.G (Addgene #14888) packaging plasmids, using polyethylenimine (Sigma). Viral supernatant was collected at 48 and 72 hours posttransfection and directly applied to Meg01 cells.

Flow cytometry

Meg01 cells were stained in FACS buffer (phosphate-buffered saline [PBS] + 0.1% BSA + 0.5mM EDTA) at 4 °C for 30 minutes using the following antibodies: CD31 (1:800; APC-eF780; eBioscience; clone WM-59), CD34 (1:300; BV-510; Biolegend; clone 581), CD41a (1:100; Pacific Blue; BioLegend; clone HIP8), CD42b (1:50; APC; eBioscience; clone p3.6.2.8.1), CD44 (1:2000; APC; eBioscience; clone IM7), CD117 (1:50; FITC; Life Technologies; clone 104D2), CD235a (1:2500; PE; Origene; clone JC159), CD36 (1:100; APC; BD Biosciences; clone CB38 [NL07]), CD61 (1:50; PerCp-Cy5.5; BD Biosciences; clone VI-PL2). After staining, cells were washed with FACS buffer and analyzed on a FACSymphony A5 (BD Bioscience). LentiV2GFP-EV–transduced Meg01 cells were single-cell sorted into a 96-well plate using a BD FACSAria III (BD Biosciences). Flow cytometry data were analyzed using FlowJo software, version 10 (BD Biosciences).

Western blot and immunoprecipitation

Meg01 cells were washed with PBS and lysed in CARIN buffer [26] supplemented with orthovanadate (BioLabs), phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (Sigma-Aldrich), protease inhibitor (Merck), okadaic acid (Calbiochem), and benzonase (Sigma-Aldrich). For western blotting, lysates were heated in sample buffer (Invitrogen) at 95 °C for 5 minutes and loaded onto NuPAGE 4-12% Bis-Tris gels (Invitrogen). Proteins were transferred using the iBlot-NC system (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and probed with the following primary antibodies: rabbit anti–FLI1-N-terminus (Invitrogen; PA5-96374), rabbit anti–FLI1-C-terminus (Abcam; AB180902), mouse anti–V5-HRP (Novex; R96125), mouse anti-GAPDH (Merck; MAB374), and mouse anti-RhoGDI (Thermo Fisher Scientific; MA5-17032). Goat antimouse Ig HRP (Dako; p0447) and goat antirabbit Ig HRP (Dako; p0448) were used as secondary antibodies.

For immunoprecipitation (IP), lysates were first incubated with the indicated antibodies, and the antigen/antibody complexes were precipitated using Dynabeads Protein G (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Beads were washed 5 times with PBS before being processed for mass spectrometry (MS) analysis (Supplementary Methods). For western blotting, beads were dried and heated in sample buffer at 95 °C for 5 minutes. TidyBlot-HRP (Bio-Rad) was used to reduce the inference of denatured IgG.

Bulk RNA sequencing analysis

Parental Meg01 cells were transduced with EV, wild-type (WT) FLI1, or FLI1 p.Met100∗. RNA library was prepared as previously described [27]. Sequencing was conducted on the Illumina HiSeq 2500 system. RNA reads were mapped to the GRCh38 (hg38) reference genome using STAR. Low-quality reads were filtered, and differential gene expression analysis was performed using the glmLRT function in edgeR, applying a fold-change threshold at 1.5 and a false discovery rate at 0.01.

Gene enrichment analysis

Differentially expressed genes between EV and WT FLI1/FLI1 p.Met100∗ transduced Meg01 cells were ranked based on log2 fold change. This ranked gene list was used as input for gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) using the clusterProfiler R package with the C5: ontology gene sets and C8: cell type signature gene sets from the Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB). For overlapping genes identified in RNA sequencing and MS, clustering and gene ontology analysis were performed using the ClusterGVis R package.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10 (GraphPad Software). Group comparisons were conducted using either the Mann-Whitney test or 1-way/2-way analysis of variance (anova), followed by Tukey or Šídák multiple comparisons test where applicable. A P value of <.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient characteristics

We present a 17-year-old girl with congenital thrombocytopenia. She is the first child of nonconsanguineous parents. The pregnancy was uneventful after 6 previous miscarriages. She was born at 39 weeks of gestation with a normal birth weight. At birth, she was diagnosed with bilateral clubfeet, which required plaster casting and correction during the first year of life. Additionally, she presented with generalized alopecia from an early age and was diagnosed with hypermetropia at the age of 4, which was corrected with glasses. At the same age, a progressive swelling in the neck was observed. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) showed a large, right-sided thoracic benign lipoma measuring 13 × 3 × 10 cm in the mediastinal space, extending from the cranial level of C5 to the caudal level at the origin of the superior vena cava. The lipoma encircled the brachiocephalic trunk and innominate vein, displacing the right carotid artery, internal jugular vein, and right subclavian artery. Histopathological analysis of a biopsy confirmed the diagnosis of benign lipoma. Follow-up MRIs at ages 10 and 17 years showed only minimal progression of the lesion. MRI showed a structurally normal heart.

Reduced FLI1 expression in Meg01 cells does not manifest altered phenotype and growth disadvantage

First, we aimed to generate FLI1 knockout clones using Meg01 cells to establish a cellular model for studying the role of FLI1 p.Met100∗ in the presence or absence of WT FLI1 (parental lines). This cell line carries 3 copies of chromosome 11, where the FLI1 gene is located [29]. Ribonucleoproteins (RNPs) composed of in-house-produced Cas9 and gRNAs targeting either exon 3 (g-exon3) or exon 4 (g-exon4#1, g-exon4#2, and g-exon4#3) of FLI1 were generated and delivered in a double RNP approach [30] (Supplementary Figure 2A, B). PCR using primers flanking the RNP target sites confirmed successful gene editing. Expected deletion bands of 573 bp, 581 bp, and 699 bp were observed when g-exon3 was combined with g-exon4#1, g-exon4#2, or g-exon4#3, respectively (Supplementary Figure 2B; lanes 1-3). The WT band (5363 bp) was not detected in control samples, presumably due to its large size (Supplementary Figure 2B; lane 6). When using the more closely spaced gRNA pairs (g-exon4#3/g-exon4#1 or g-exon4#3/g-exon4#2), smaller PCR products of 814 and 817 bp were observed, migrating just below the nonedited WT PCR product of 935 bp (Supplementary Figure 2B; lanes 4, 5, and 7, where lane 7 is a non-CRISPR–edited control). We expanded single-cell clones from 3 RNP conditions: g-exon3+g-exon4#2, g-exon3+g-exon4#3, and g-exon4#2+g-exon4#3. Four clones (HET1-4) derived from the g-exon3/g-exon4#2 condition showed a 581-bp deletion (Supplementary Figure 2C). Interestingly, PCR analysis using primer pairs spanning exon 3 or exon 4 also detected WT bands (973 bp for exon 3 and 891 bp for exon 4) (Supplementary Figure 2C). These 4 clones (HET1-4) were confirmed to be FLI1-disrupted by Sanger sequencing and western blot, where 2 of the 3 FLI1 alleles were mutated and FLI1 protein expression was reduced (Figure 2A; Supplementary Figures 2C, D and 3). For comparison, we selected 4 unmodified clones for further study (Figure 2A, B; Supplementary Figures 2C, D and 3). To assess whether reduced FLI1 expression affected cell proliferation, we conducted a competition assay. A Meg01 clone in which a constitutive GFP reporter was lentivirally introduced, was mixed in equal proportions with either unmodified or FLI1-disrupted clones. The percentage of green fluorescent protein–positive cells was recorded over the course of 2 weeks, revealing no significant differences in proliferation between unmodified and FLI1-disrupted clones (Figure 2C; Supplementary Figure 4). Proteomic analysis indicated that partial FLI1 deletion did not result in statistically significant changes in protein expression compared with unmodified clones (Figure 2D; Supplementary Table 2). Flow cytometry further confirmed that FLI1 disruption had no significant impact on the expression of megakaryocytic markers (CD41a and CD42b), the erythroid marker (CD235a), or hematopoietic progenitor markers (CD31, CD44, and CD117) (Figure 2E; Supplementary Figure 5). Overall, our results suggest that a complete FLI1 knockout in Meg01 cells could not be achieved using these gRNAs, possibly due to FLI1 being indispensable. Ectopic expression of WT FLI1 in Meg01 cells downregulates erythroid features, while FLI1 p.Met100∗ upregulates these

To investigate how FLI1 p.Met100∗ impairs megakaryopoiesis, we generated lentiviral constructs expressing either WT FLI1 (lenti_FLI1-WT) or FLI1 p.Met100∗ (lenti_FLI1-Met100∗) with a green fluorescent protein reporter. These constructs were transduced into parental Meg01 cells (Figure 3A; Supplementary Figure 6A). Western blot confirmed the presence of lenti_FLI1-WT or lenti_FLI1-Met100∗ (Figure 3A). RNA sequencing followed by multidimensional scaling analysis revealed that 61% of the variance was explained by dim1 and 9% by dim2, effectively clustering the different conditions. Notably, lenti_FLI1-WT transduced Meg01 cells separated distinctly from lenti_FLI1-Met100∗-expressing and control (lenti_EV) cells (Supplementary Figure 6B). Compared with control Meg01 cells, lenti_FLI1-WT overexpression resulted in 693 upregulated and 801 downregulated genes, whereas expression of lenti_FLI1-Met100∗ led to 167 upregulated and 226 downregulated genes (Supplementary Table 3A, B). As expected, lentivirally expressed FLI1 was among the top differentially expressed genes (Supplementary Figure 6C; Supplementary Table 3A, B). GSEA revealed an upregulation of megakaryocytic signatures in both lenti_FLI1-WT– and lenti_FLI1-Met100∗–transduced Meg01 cells (Supplementary Table 3C, D). However, key erythroid genes, including KLF1 and CD235a, were repressed by lenti_FLI1-WT yet activated by lenti_FLI1-Met100∗ (Figure 3B; Supplementary Figure 6D; Supplementary Table 3C, D). CD36, which is shared by both megakaryocytic and erythroid programs, was similarly activated in the lenti_FLI1-Met100∗ condition (Supplementary Figure 6D; Supplementary Table 3C, D). GSEA also revealed enrichment of cell division–related processes in lenti_FLI1-Met100∗–transduced Meg01 cells compared with lenti_FLI1-WT (Supplementary Figure 6C, E; Supplementary Table 3E). PCA analysis of MS data from the same samples showed a clustering pattern similar to that observed in the RNA sequencing results (Supplementary Figure 7A). The presence of ectopically expressed FLI1 was confirmed by MS (Supplementary Figure 7B, C; Supplementary Table 3F). When we compared differentially expressed genes with corresponding proteins detected by MS, we found that gene expression patterns (Figure 3C) closely mirrored proteomics data (Figure 3D). Notably, CD36 and KLF1 followed the same trends (Supplementary Figure 7D; Supplementary Table 3F). Unsupervised clustering of differentially expressed genes resulted in 5 distinct clusters (Figure 3C; Supplementary Table 3G). Among them, clusters C3 and C5 were downregulated by lenti_FLI1-WT and upregulated by lenti_FLI1-Met100∗, while cluster C2 showed the opposite trend. Gene ontology (GO) analysis of C3 and C5 highlighted enrichment in erythroid cell types (Supplementary Figure 8; Supplementary Table 3H), reinforcing the GSEA findings (Figure 3B). Genes in C2 were associated with cellular components such as the mitochondrial matrix and secretary granules (Supplementary Figure 9; Supplementary Table 3I), as well as sulfur and glutathione metabolism pathways (Supplementary Figure 10; Supplementary Table 3J). In conclusion, we identified a subset of genes dysregulated by lenti_FLI1-Met100∗, particularly those involved in erythroid differentiation, which were suppressed by lenti_FLI1-WT but upregulated by lenti_FLI1-Met100∗. Ectopic expression of WT FLI1 in Meg01 cells increases cellular adhesion and megakaryocyte differentiation

By overlaying RNA sequencing and MS data, we identified a distinct gene cluster (C1) that was exclusively upregulated by lenti_FLI1-WT (Figure 3C, D) and significantly enriched in endothelial and megakaryocytic cell types (Supplementary Figure 8; Supplementary Table 3G, H). This cluster included genes such as ETV6, IL1R1, IL6R, ITGB3 (CD61), SELP, and CD44. Flow cytometry confirmed these findings, showing increased fold changes in CD61+ and CD34+ cell percentages and CD44 mean fluorescence intensity in lenti_FLI1-WT expressing cells, but not in lenti_FLI1-Met100∗–transduced cells (Figure 4A; Supplementary Figure 11). Additionally, a cell adhesion strength assay performed 3 days posttransduction confirmed enhanced cellular attachment upon lenti_FLI1-WT overexpression, whereas no such effect was observed in untransduced or lenti_FLI1-Met100∗ conditions (Figure 4B). FLI1 p.Met100∗ does not interact with endogenous or overexpressed FLI1

Next, we aimed to provide mechanistic insights into how the mutant interferes with the function of FLI1 by investigating whether FLI1 p.Met100∗ interacts with WT FLI1 or other cofactors. To test this, we cotransfected lenti_FLI1-WT and lenti_FLI1-Met100∗ into HEK293T cells, which do not express FLI1 endogenously. IP of WT FLI1 using a C-terminal antibody did not coimmunoprecipitate FLI1 p.Met100∗ (Supplementary Figure 13, of note this C-terminal epitope is not present in FLI1 p.Met100∗). In addition, we lentivirally transduced Meg01 cells with lenti_V5-FLI1-WT or lenti_V5-FLI1-Met100∗ and successfully immunoprecipitated the V5-tagged FLI1 proteins using an anti-V5 antibody (Figure 5A). To examine a potential interaction with endogenous FLI1, we stained the blot with an antibody targeting the N-terminus of FLI1. However, no interaction between endogenous FLI1 and either V5-FLI1-WT or V5-FLI1-Met100∗ was observed (Figure 5A). Furthermore, when we immunoprecipitated endogenous FLI1 using a C-terminal antibody in Meg01 cells expressing V5-FLI1-Met100∗, no co-IP of FLI1 p.Met100∗ was observed (Figure 5B). MS analysis revealed that the transcription factor ETV6, E3 ubiquitin ligases UBR4, USP9X, and UCHL5, as well as the histone acetyltransferase (HAT)1, specifically coimmunoprecipitated with V5-FLI1-WT (Figure 5C; Supplementary Table 4). Interestingly, several proteins were found to interact with both V5-FLI1-WT and V5-FLI1-Met100∗, including the transcription factor GTF2I, previously linked to bleeding and platelet dysfunction [31], although this interaction was not statistically significant for the mutant form (Figure 5C; Supplementary Table 4). Discussion

ETS family transcription factors, such as FLI1, ERG, and ETV6, play crucial roles at various stages of hematopoiesis, including the development of hematopoietic stem cells from hemogenic endothelium and differentiation into functional blood lineages.

Complete deletion of Fli1 in mice results in embryonic lethality (E11.5-12.5) due to severe thrombocytopenia and defective vasculature development [32]. Additionally, hematopoietic specific deletion of Fli1 in mice leads to reduced peripheral blood counts and an inability of HSCs to engraft [33]. Beyond hematopoiesis, FLI1 is implicated in cellular proliferation across various cancers, and its overexpression in cell lines inhibits apoptosis [34]. Consequently, FLI1 inhibition has emerged as a therapeutic strategy, as its suppression can induce cell death in cancer cells [35]. Given FLI1’s essential role in cell survival and megakaryopoiesis, we speculate that we did not observe any Meg01 clones (of 64 screened) with a homozygous deletion of FLI1, as this would be lethal. Western blot and MS demonstrated half-normal FLI1 levels in FLI1-disrupted clones, with no significant change in overall protein expression. FLI1 expression dynamics vary during megakaryocyte differentiation, with monoallelic expression in early megakaryocyte progenitors and biallelic expression in late-stage megakaryocytes [36]. Although Meg01 cells harbor 3 copies of FLI1 due to +1 Chr11, they represent early megakaryocytes and thus possibly require less FLI1 expression [29]. This may explain the phenotypic similarities between the parental Meg01s (3 copies of FLI1) and the FLI1-disrupted Meg01s.

FLI1 functions both as a transcription activator and repressor, regulating both megakaryocyte and erythrocyte differentiation [35]. Overexpression of FLI1 in human pluripotent stem cells enhances megakaryopoiesis [14,37]. It activates megakaryocytic genes, including MPL, ITGA2B, GPIBA, GP6, GP9, and CXCL4 [[38], [39], [40], [41]]. Our findings provide more resolution on the time point where FLI1 may be operational during megakaryopoiesis, showing and confirming that FLI1 overexpression enhances megakaryocytic features. Additionally, we observed increased cellular adhesion, which is similar to the effects seen when inducing Meg01 differentiation with PMA [42]. FLI1 and KLF1 have been shown to antagonize each other during megakaryocyte-erythroid differentiation [11]. In mice, Klf1 deletion increases platelet production and retains platelet markers on committed erythroid cells [43], whereas Fli1 deletion enhances erythroid differentiation [33]. Overexpression of FLI1 and KLF1 in cell lines suppresses erythroid and megakaryocytic development, respectively [44]. Single-cell proteomics revealed coexpression of FLI1 and KLF1 in human erythroid–biased MEPs, followed by a decrease in FLI1 expression during erythroid differentiation [45]. The same study demonstrated that FLI1 overexpression in erythroid–biased MEPs shifts differentiation toward the megakaryocytic trajectory [45]. Consistently, we found that ectopic FLI1 expression in Meg01 cells not only upregulates megakaryocytic features but also suppresses erythroid genes, including KLF1, at both the mRNA and protein levels.

So far, almost all documented FLI1 mutations occur within the ETS DNA-binding domain. We have reported the de novo variant FLI1 p.Met100∗, which introduces a premature stop codon upstream of the pointed domain. When ectopically expressed in Meg01 cells, the truncated peptide was detected by western blot and MS. MYH10, previously described as a marker of FLI1-related platelet disorders, was not upregulated in Meg01 cells carrying the variant, likely because this cell line corresponds to an early stage of megakaryocyte differentiation. In contrast, the patient’s platelets showed elevated MYH10, consistent with a defect in its normal downregulation during megakaryocyte differentiation [1]. Transcriptomic and proteomic analyses revealed that FLI1 p.Met100∗ overexpression led to an upregulation of erythroid signatures, contrasting with WT FLI1. Furthermore, we identified a subset of genes upregulated by WT FLI1 but downregulated by FLI1 p.Met100∗. GO analysis of this subset indicated enrichment in mitochondrial matrix and sulphur/glutathione metabolism, processes linked to reactive oxygen species production. Given that reactive oxygen species play crucial roles in megakaryopoiesis, further investigation is required to establish this connection [46]. Physiologically, megakaryocytes undergo endomitosis to become polyploid, which is reflected in the reduced cell division signatures observed in WT FLI1-transduced cells. In contrast, the upregulation of cell division programs in FLI1 p.Met100∗ may point to a disruption of this process, potentially contributing to impaired megakaryocyte maturation. Overall, the gene dysregulation caused by FLI1 p.Met100∗ supports a dominant-negative function, disrupting FLI1’s regulatory role in megakaryopoiesis. While Meg01 cells provide a convenient and reproducible tool to study megakaryocytic differentiation and gene regulation, they do not fully recapitulate primary megakaryocyte maturation and platelet formation. The present study provides the basis for further analysis in primary cells, for example, CD34+ cells.

FLI1 p.Met100∗ lacks the DNA-binding domain but may still interact with and sequester cofactors from WT FLI1, potentially disrupting its function. Structural analyses have predicted and identified a potential dimerization region within the DNA-binding domain [47]. Although dimerization of the EWS-FLI1 fusion protein via the FLI1 C-terminal domain has been reported in Ewing sarcoma, germline FLI1 homotypic interaction was not observed using fluorescence resonance energy transfer microscopy [48]. In line with this, we did not observe FLI1 dimerization or interaction between FLI1 p.Met100∗ and WT FLI1 using Meg01 and HEK293T cells. It remains to be determined whether FLI1 dimerization is stage dependent during megakaryopoiesis. The hematopoietic transcription factor ETV6 was coimmunoprecipitated exclusively by WT FLI1, consistent with previously reported interactions between these 2 proteins [49]. FLI1 has been shown to contain a sumoylation site at Lys67 and to interact with PIASxα/ARIP3 for repression [50]. Although we did not observe this, IP-MS revealed that the E3 ubiquitin ligases UBR4, USP9X, and UCHL5 co-immunoprecipitated with WT FLI1 but not with the truncated FLI1 p.Met100∗. Notably, USP9X has been shown to bind the ETS domain of EWS-FLI1, which may explain its absence in the FLI1Met100 complex [51]. Given their role in protein degradation, loss of E3 ligase interaction could affect the stability of the mutant protein. FLI1 is acetylated at multiple sites within its ETS domain by histone acetyltransferases such as p300 and PCAF, which modulate its DNA binding and transcriptional activity [52,53]. Although p300 and PCAF were not detected by IP-MS, the specific co-IP of HAT1 with WT FLI1 suggests it may similarly contribute to FLI1 regulation. The data here suggest that the first 100 amino acids of FLI1 are not sufficient for these interactions. However, several proteins were coimmunoprecipitated with both WT FLI1 and FLI1 p.Met100∗, including the transcription factor GTF2I, which is hemizygously deleted in Williams-Beuren Syndrome and associated with bleeding [[54], [55], [56]]. Gtf2i deletion in mice leads to vascular defects and megakaryocyte reduction [31,56]. Additionally, GTF2I represses β-globin gene expression in K562 and MEL cells [31]. Given FLI1’s role in vascular and megakaryocyte development, as well as its involvement in β-globin transcription repression in MEL cells [11,31], competitive sequestering of GTF2I may contribute to the upregulation of erythroid gene expression in FLI1 p.Met100∗-expressing Meg01 cells and the thrombocytopenia observed in the patient with FLI1 p.Met100∗ mutation.

In summary, we identified a novel FLI1 variant (FLI1 p.Met100∗), without the ETS DNA-binding domain, which is associated with a congenital platelet disorder leading to thrombocytopenia and increased bleeding. Overexpression of FLI1 p.Met100∗ in Meg01 cells produced a truncated peptide. While WT FLI1 enhanced megakaryocytic gene expression and suppressed erythroid genes, FLI1 p.Met100∗ upregulated erythroid markers, potentially via a dominant-negative mechanism. The molecular interactions underlying this effect remain to be elucidated.
