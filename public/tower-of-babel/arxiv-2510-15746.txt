LLMs Judge Themselves: A Game-Theoretic Framework for Human-Aligned Evaluation
Yang Gao, Yuhang Liu, Siyu Miao, Xinyue Liang, Zhengyang Liu, Heyan Huang

Abstract
Ideal or real—that is the question. In this work, we explore whether principles from game theory can be effectively applied to the evaluation of large language models (LLMs). This inquiry is motivated by the growing inadequacy of conventional evaluation practices, which often rely on fixed-format tasks with reference answers and struggle to capture the nuanced, subjective, and open-ended nature of modern LLM behavior. To address these challenges, we propose a novel alternative: automatic mutual evaluation, where LLMs assess each other’s output through self-play and peer review. These peer assessments are then systematically compared with human voting behavior to evaluate their alignment with human judgment. Our framework incorporates game-theoretic voting algorithms to aggregate peer reviews, enabling a principled investigation into whether model-generated rankings reflect human preferences. Empirical results reveal both convergences and divergences between theoretical predictions and human evaluations, offering valuable insights into the promises and limitations of mutual evaluation. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first work to jointly integrate mutual evaluation, game-theoretic aggregation, and human-grounded validation for evaluating the capabilities of LLMs.

Measuring the performance of large language models (LLMs) remains a challenging task due to their broad and diverse capabilities

;

. To complement traditional evaluation methods, we focus on a game-theoretic perspective.

. These properties naturally align with the inherent capability differences, judgment conflicts, and task adaptability among LLMs, enabling game-theoretic approaches to effectively simulate complex human evaluation processes

;

. Conversely, LLMs offer an ideal testbed for empirically validating theoretical insights from game theory. Motivated by these observations, we ask:
Can game-theoretic methodologies yield model rankings that align with human judgments in evaluating the capabilities of LLMs?

To validate the aforementioned hypothesis, one key challenge lies in the presence of self-preference bias

, where models tend to favor their own outputs when serving as evaluators. Such bias can compromise the fairness and objectivity of peer assessment. To address this, we adopt an automated peer evaluation mechanism utilizing large models

Chu et al. 2024b

decentralized peer ranking

It generates pairwise preferences over the outputs of its peers, which are then aggregated into a global preference matrix that captures collective judgments across models. Based on this matrix, we apply a suite of aggregation algorithms

;
Charikar et al. 2024b
;

;

to derive model rankings and systematically compare voting rules in terms of their alignment with human evaluations.

This decentralized peer ranking framework offers a novel approach to LLM evaluation by leveraging model-to-model interaction for collaborative assessment. By treating each model as both an evaluatee and an evaluator, the framework enables more informative pairwise comparisons and captures nuanced judgments that may be overlooked in traditional evaluation setups. However, the effectiveness and fairness of such a system depend on several critical factors. Before presenting the experimental design, it is important to clarify the core research objectives our study seeks to address:

During the peer evaluation, how well do different game-theoretic peer evaluation algorithms align with human judgments in ranking LLMs?
This question probes the core hypothesis of our work. We aim to determine if the collective intelligence derived from LLM peer evaluation can reflect human preference of performance, and to identify which aggregation algorithms most effectively translate pairwise comparisons into reliable global rankings.

Can game-theoretic ranking algorithms mitigate such self-bias and produce more reliable evaluation rankings?
Prior studies have demonstrated that LLMs often exhibit self-preference bias
Liu et al. 2024
;
Dietz et al. 2025
, where models tend to assign favorable scores to their own outputs when serving as evaluators. This phenomenon poses a challenge to the fairness and objectivity of model evaluation. We investigate whether game-theoretic methods, which aggregate rankings from multiple evaluators based on collective decision-making ranking, can effectively reduce this bias and yield more trustworthy assessment results.

Are there specific LLM capabilities where game-theoretic evaluation exhibits varying alignment with human judgment?
LLMs possess diverse capabilities across a wide range of tasks, from logical reasoning to creative generation. Analyzing alignment across different task categories helps identify where this evaluation method performs reliably, and where it may fall short in capturing human preferences.

Our empirical analysis is organized into three components to systematically evaluate the effectiveness and fairness of game-theoretic peer evaluation for LLMs. First, we compare model rankings produced by different aggregation algorithms against human-annotated ground truth to assess alignment with human judgment. Second, we quantify self-preference bias by measuring the extent to which LLMs favor their own outputs, and examine whether rank aggregation mitigates this bias through collective decision-making. Third, we analyze alignment scores across different task types (e.g., mathematic, chinese, instruction-following, open-ended generation and so on) to investigate whether evaluation consistency varies by capability domain. Together, these experiments provide a comprehensive assessment of our framework and highlight its potential for reliable, and fair model evaluation. In summary, this paper makes the following key contributions:

•

We formulate peer evaluation among LLMs as a game-theoretic voting problem, establishing a principled framework for systematically comparing model capabilities.

•

We demonstrate that robust aggregation algorithms can yield model rankings that closely align with human judgments, enabling fair, scalable, and trustworthy evaluation of LLMs.

•

We empirically identify systematic self-assessment biases in LLMs and propose practical strategies to mitigate them within evaluation protocols.

2
Related Work

2.1
Evaluation Methods for LLMs

Evaluating LLMs remains a central yet challenging problem due to their rapidly expanding capabilities. Existing methods can be broadly categorized into two paradigms:
human-annotated evaluation
and
LLM-as-a-judge evaluation
. Traditional benchmarks such as MMLU
Hendrycks et al. 2021
and GSM8K
Chiang et al. 2024
rely on curated datasets with reference answers and human or rule-based grading schemes. These approaches offer consistency and reproducibility, enabling fair model comparisons. However, they face increasing concerns about overfitting to static test sets, which may compromise the generalizability of evaluation results
Zhou et al. 2023b
;
Xu et al. 2024
.

An increasingly popular alternative is to use LLMs themselves as evaluators. This
LLM-as-a-judge
paradigm enables scalable and flexible evaluation, particularly suited to open-ended or subjective tasks where reference answers are lacking. For example,
Chu et al. 2024a
propose selecting a single LLM as the evaluator through a qualification stage, after which it is used to evaluate all candidate responses. This approach can introduce evaluator bias and limited perspective, especially if the selected model shares architectural or behavioral similarities with the models being judged. In contrast, our method adopts a fully decentralized evaluation paradigm: rather than relying on a single judge, we incorporate feedback from all participating models and apply game-theoretic aggregation to derive a consensus ranking. This approach mitigates individual bias and provides a more diverse and representative assessment of model performance.

2.2
Game-Theoretic Approaches to Evaluation

Game-theoretic methods provide a general framework for evaluation based on relative comparisons and strategic interactions, without relying on fixed ground-truth labels. Prior work in this area has explored two main directions. The first focuses on constructing game-inspired environments to examine decision-making and strategic behavior, particularly in the context of language models. For example,
Akata et al. 2023
;
Chen et al. 2023
develop benchmarks based on matrix games and auctions to study how models behave under varying payoff structures. While such setups are valuable for behavioral analysis, they are primarily used for probing rather than for evaluating performance or generating rankings.

The second direction leverages preference aggregation techniques from social choice theory to derive consensus rankings from pairwise comparisons. These methods help reduce evaluator bias and increase robustness when scoring outputs that may be noisy or subjective. For instance,
Zhang et al. 2024
propose an enhanced Borda count (EBC) algorithm to re-rank math reasoning responses based on comparative judgments. Such aggregation strategies have been applied across domains including LLM evaluation, recommender systems, and tournament design, where they facilitate consistent evaluation in the absence of absolute ground truth.

3
Methodology

3.1
Overview of Our Evaluation Framework

Conventional evaluation methods for LLMs primarily rely on comparisons against predefined reference answers. However, this approach risks overfitting, where models exploit surface patterns in benchmarks rather than demonstrating robust reasoning or generalization capabilities. Such behavior undermines both the fairness and validity of evaluation. Furthermore, human-annotated evaluations remain costly, subjective, and inherently limited in scalability.

As shown in Figure
2
, we propose an automated peer evaluation framework in which LLMs assess the outputs of all models, including their own. Each model serves as both an evaluatee and an evaluator, producing rankings over peer responses. These decentralized judgments are aggregated via game-theoretic voting algorithms to produce a coherent global model ranking, enabling analysis of alignment with human preferences. The overall framework consists of three core components: peer evaluation protocol, aggregation over per-question rankings, and alignment with human judgment.

3.2
Peer Evaluation Protocol

Let
\mathcal{M}=\{LLM_{1},\dots,LLM_{m}\}
denote a set of
m
language models, and let
\mathcal{Q}=\{q_{1},\dots,q_{n}\}
represent a shared evaluation question set. Each model
LLM_{i}\in\mathcal{M}
generates a bundled response
A_{i}^{\mathcal{Q}}=\{a_{i1},\dots,a_{in}\}
, where
a_{ij}
denotes the
{i}
-th model’s answer to question
q_{j}
.

As illustrated in the upper part of Figure
2
, the evaluation process comprises two stages:

•

Response Generation.
Each model independently answers every question
q_{j}\in\mathcal{Q}
, producing a response
a_{ij}
for each
LLM_{i}\in\mathcal{M}
.

•

Decentralized Peer Evaluation.
For each question
q_{j}
, every model
LLM_{i}
serves as an evaluator and is presented with the anonymized responses
\{a_{1j},a_{2j},\dots,a_{mj}\}
, including its own. The evaluator provides a complete ranking over the responses based on their quality with respect to the given question.

Each of these rankings is converted into a set of pairwise preferences, such as
a_{xj}\succ a_{yj}
, indicating that the evaluator prefers model
x
’s response over that of model
y
for question
q_{j}
. Aggregating these preferences across evaluators and questions results in a preference structure that forms the basis for downstream model ranking. This evaluation framework facilitates automated, model-driven assessment and also enables the study of phenomena such as self-preference bias, which we analyze in subsequent sections.

3.3
Aggregation over Per-Question Rankings

Given the collection of pairwise preferences obtained from peer evaluations, we aim to derive a global ranking over models that reflects their overall performance. This is formulated as a rank aggregation problem, where the goal is to combine individual rankings, each derived from a specific evaluation question, into a consensus ranking that best captures the overall preference structure. Since input rankings may vary due to evaluator differences or local noise, the aggregation method must be both robust and consistent.

As shown in our experiments, the Kemeny-Young algorithm consistently achieves the highest alignment with human preferences, and is therefore adopted as the default aggregation strategy. This method identifies the ranking that maximizes overall alignment with the input rankings by minimizing pairwise discordance. Its objective is to find the ranking that has the smallest total disagreement, measured by Kendall-Tau distance, with all input rankings. Alternative aggregation strategies, including Borda count and Copeland voting, are discussed in Appendix
E
, along with implementation details and comparative analysis.

Experimental Setups

Datasets.

We evaluate model reasoning across a diverse set of benchmarks, including GSM8K

Models.

Our experiments include six recent LLMs from OpenAI (GPT-4o variants) and Anthropic (Claude-3 and 3.5 series), chosen for their top-tier performance in recent benchmarks.

Human Preference Reference.

To evaluate how well our framework aligns with human preferences, we compare the resulting model rankings against those from Chatbot Arena
Chiang et al. 2024
. Since Chatbot Arena provides system-level rankings across multiple domains, we match each benchmark to the most relevant leaderboard subset. Specifically, we use the GSM8K for
math-specific
ranking, the MMLU and GPQA for
overall
ranking, the CEval for
Chinese
ranking, the IFEval for
instruction-following
ranking, and the MBPP for
code-related
rankings. This task-to-domain mapping ensures that our comparisons reflect the most appropriate human preference signals. Alignment is quantified using both Pearson and Kendall
Kendall 1948
correlation coefficients between our aggregated rankings and those from Chatbot Arena.

Evaluation Levels.

To assess how well model-generated rankings align with human preferences, we evaluate correlation at two complementary levels:
micro-level
and
macro-level
.

•

Micro-level correlation
evaluates alignment at the level of individual questions. For each question in a dataset, we compute the correlation between the rankings from a specific aggregation method and the corresponding human reference (e.g., from Chatbot Arena), yielding a sequence of correlation scores. To assess both the consistency and quality of this alignment, we analyze their distribution using descriptive statistics such as the mean, standard deviation, and key percentiles. For instance, a higher 25th percentile indicates that at least three-quarters of the evaluation questions yield rankings with strong alignment to human judgments.

•

Macro-level correlation
computes the average of per-question rankings to obtain a single overall ranking of models for the entire dataset, and then computes a single correlation score with the human-provided global ranking. This captures overall alignment at the dataset level but does not reflect per-question variability.

Ranking Algorithms.

To produce a final ranking over models from decentralized peer evaluations, we implement nine classical aggregation methods from social choice theory and voting literature: Average, Borda Count
Saari 1985
, Copeland
Saari and Merlin 1996
, Dodgson
Brandt 2009
, Instant-Runoff Voting (IRV)
Brandt 2009
, Kemeny-Young
Kemeny 1959
, Kendall
Kendall 1948
, and Spearman. These algorithms take as input either ranking lists or pairwise preferences collected across evaluation questions and produce an overall consensus ranking of models. They cover a broad range of principles, including positional scoring methods such as Borda, pairwise comparison approaches such as Copeland, and consistency-based objectives such as Kemeny. Further implementation details are provided in Appendix
E
.

4.3
Q2: Can Game-Theoretic Re-ranking Mitigate Self-Preference Bias in LLMs?

Recent research has demonstrated that LLMs commonly exhibit
self-preference bias
when evaluating their own outputs
Liu et al. 2024
;
Dietz et al. 2025
. In such cases, models tend to assign favorable scores to their own generations, compromising the objectivity of evaluation outcomes. This phenomenon raises concerns about the reliability of LLMs as autonomous evaluators, particularly in decentralized or multi-agent settings where self-assessment can distort collective judgment.

To investigate this issue, we examine whether game-theoretic re-ranking, specifically consensus aggregation using the
Kemeny-Young
method, can mitigate self-preference bias and lead to more objective model evaluations. We define four evaluation protocols to isolate the effects of self-assessment and peer assessment. Self Evaluation
(SE)
collects the rankings generated by each model when serving as an evaluator, including the position it assigns to itself. These self-generated rankings are then used to construct an overall model ranking, allowing us to observe the extent of self-preference bias. Peer Evaluation
(PE)
considers only the rankings provided by other models, excluding all self-evaluations, and averages the rankings each model receives from its peers. Self-Inclusive Evaluation
(SIE)
aggregates all rankings, including both peer and self-evaluations, using the
Kemeny-Young
method to compute a consensus ranking. In contrast, Self-Free Evaluation
(SFE)
applies the same aggregation procedure but excludes all self-generated rankings, enabling a clearer view of peer-only assessments and providing a cleaner estimate of bias introduced by self-evaluation.

Game-Theoretic Aggregation Mitigates Self-Preference Bias.

As shown in Table
3
, LLMs exhibit a clear
self-preference bias
. Across all benchmarks, SE consistently yields more favorable self-assigned rankings compared to peer evaluations in PE. For instance, on GSM8K,
4o-1120
improves by +0.44 positions when evaluated by others (from 2.00 in SE to 2.44 in PE), and
Hk-1022
shows an even greater shift of +0.49 (3.86 to 4.35). These results confirm that models tend to overrate their own performance. Notably, on GSM8K, MMLU, and GPQA, more than three models demonstrate inflated self-rankings under SE compared to PE. Even more strikingly, as shown in Table
13
, five models exhibit self-preference in the instruction-following (IFEval) benchmark. To assess whether game-theoretic aggregation can mitigate this bias, we compare rankings from SIE and SFE using the Kemeny-Young method. While SE–PE differences are often substantial, SIE-SFE differences are generally modest. For example, on GSM8K,
4o-0513
shifts by only -0.02 (from 3.08 in SIE to 3.06 in SFE), and
4o-1120
shifts by +0.18. These small deltas indicate that aggregation substantially reduces the influence of self-preference. Overall, the SIE and SFE rankings are much closer than those from SE and PE, suggesting that the aggregation procedure helps recover more balanced and objective rankings.

To evaluate whether these rankings align with human judgment, Figure
3
presents the distribution of Pearson correlations between model-generated rankings and human preferences across evaluation questions. The results show that rankings from SE yield the weakest and most variable alignment with human judgments, indicating that models’ self-assigned rankings are often unreliable. In contrast, SIE achieves the strongest alignment overall, suggesting that incorporating all available rankings, including self-evaluations, can produce results that are most consistent with human preferences. Furthermore, the correlation distributions of SIE and SFE are close, indicating that game-theoretic aggregation is effective in mitigating self-preference bias, even when self-evaluations are included. Due to space constraints, we report results on three representative benchmarks in Table
3
and Figure
3
, with full results provided in Appendix
G
.

4.4
Q3: Which Abilities of LLMs Can Be Reliably Evaluated Through Game Theory?

Game-Theoretic Evaluation Yields the Most Human-Aligned Rankings in Math Tasks.

Figure
4
shows how well game-theoretic aggregation using the Kemeny-Young algorithm aligns with human preferences across different types of LLM capabilities. For each benchmark, we report both micro-level and macro-level Pearson correlations with human rankings from the Chatbot Arena. The box plots display the distribution of micro-level correlations across individual questions, while the purple dots represent macro-level correlations aggregated over the dataset. Among all tasks, the GSM8K benchmark shows the strongest alignment with human judgment. Game-theoretic aggregation achieves both the highest median micro-level correlation and the smallest interquartile range, indicating stable and consistent alignment across questions. At the macro level, it reaches a Pearson correlation of 0.941, outperforming all other datasets. In contrast, benchmarks involving CEval or Creative Writing exhibit lower and more variable correlations, suggesting that capturing human preferences in linguistically nuanced tasks remains more challenging. These findings suggest that game-theoretic aggregation is particularly effective in tasks with clearer evaluation criteria, such as math and code generation. While it also shows promise in more subjective tasks, additional factors such as task context or stylistic variation may need to be considered to better align with human judgment. Notably, despite high micro-level variance, the
Creative Writing
benchmark achieves remarkably high macro-level alignment with human preferences (0.914), indicating that aggregate judgments can still reliably reflect human consensus. Overall, the results highlight the applicability of peer-based evaluation across diverse capabilities while also revealing its current limitations.

5
Conclusion

We explore the potential of game-theoretic methodologies for evaluating the performance of artificial intelligence agents in the context of LLMs. By incorporating peer assessments, our proposed framework demonstrates close alignment with human judgments. Notably, game-theoretic aggregation also mitigates self-preference bias, leading to fairer and more objective evaluations. These findings underscore the promise of game-theoretic evaluation as a scalable alternative to traditional manual benchmarks, while also revealing key limitations and directions for future research. For instance, the effectiveness of our approach varies across task types, achieving stronger alignment in objective domains than in creative writing. We anticipate broader applications and empirical validations at the intersection of game theory and LLMs.

Appendix C
Ranking Metrics and Aggregation Algorithms

C.1
Kendall-Tau Distance and Coefficient

The Kendall-Tau distance between two permutations
\pi
and
\sigma
is defined as the number of discordant pairs:

D(\pi,\sigma)=\sum_{i<j}\mathbf{1}\left[\text{sign}(\pi(i)-\pi(j))\neq\text{sign}(\sigma(i)-\sigma(j))\right],

(1)

where
\text{sign}(\cdot)
returns
+1
,
-1
, or
0
, and
\mathbf{1}[\cdot]
is the indicator function.

The Kendall-Tau
coefficient
is the normalized difference between concordant and discordant pairs:

\tau(\pi,\sigma)=\frac{C-D}{\binom{n}{2}},

where
C
and
D
are the number of concordant and discordant pairs, respectively.

Example:

For
\pi=[A,B,C]
and
\sigma=[B,A,C]
, the only discordant pair is
(A,B)
, so
D=1
and
\tau=\frac{2-1}{3}=\frac{1}{3}
.

C.2
Kemeny-Young Rank Aggregation

The Kemeny-Young method finds a consensus ranking
\sigma^{*}
minimizing the total Kendall-Tau distance to all
m
input rankings:

\sigma^{*}=\mathop{\text{argmin}}_{\sigma}\sum_{k=1}^{m}D(\sigma,\pi_{k})

This problem is NP-hard, but efficient heuristics exist.

Procedure:

1.

Pairwise Comparison:
Count how many times
i
ranks above
j
across all rankings.

2.

Preference Graph:
Construct a directed graph with weighted edges representing preference conflicts.

3.

Minimum Feedback Arc Set:
Remove minimal-weight edges to make the graph acyclic; the topological sort gives the consensus.

Example:

Given input rankings
[A,B,C],[B,A,C],[C,A,B]
:

•

B\to A
(1 disagreement),
A\to C
,
B\to C
.

•

The consensus ranking minimizing total discordance is
[B,A,C]
.

In our implementation, we adapt this procedure by replacing
D(\sigma,\pi_{k})
with
\tau(\sigma,\pi_{k})
in the objective, enabling coefficient-based consensus aggregation.

Appendix D
Dataset Statistics and Selection Criteria

To comprehensively evaluate the reasoning capabilities of large language models (LLMs), we adopt a multi-level, multi-dimensional evaluation framework. We carefully select three representative benchmark datasets—GSM8K, MMLU, and GPQA—that span tasks of varying difficulty levels and reasoning demands, as summarized in Table
6
.

These benchmarks are widely recognized for their effectiveness in probing high-level cognitive capabilities in LLMs, each emphasizing distinct dimensions of reasoning:

•

GSM8K

Chiang et al. 2024
focuses on basic mathematical reasoning. We sample 100 elementary-level arithmetic word problems to assess the model’s capabilities in computation, basic logic, and semantic understanding.

•

MMLU

Hendrycks et al. 2021
extends the evaluation to interdisciplinary knowledge integration. We select 114 multiple-choice questions across 57 subjects, spanning STEM fields (e.g., mathematics, physics) as well as humanities and social sciences (e.g., history, law), to evaluate the model’s cross-domain reasoning and conceptual abstraction skills.

•

GPQA

Rein et al. 2023
is a high-level benchmark specifically introduced in this study. It comprises 50 open-ended generative questions that require comprehensive problem solving, often involving multimodal information fusion, multi-constraint optimization, and counterfactual reasoning. Models are required to produce full natural language solutions, posing greater demands on deep reasoning, creative thinking, and knowledge transfer.

•

CEval

Huang et al. 2023
is a Chinese-language exam-style benchmark that evaluates model performance on national-level standardized test questions across subjects like law, medicine, and finance. We use a 100-example subset drawn from its development set, covering both humanities and STEM.

•

IFEval

Zhou et al. 2023a
is designed to evaluate instruction-following capabilities of LLMs. It includes a diverse mix of tasks involving goal specification, constraint following, and multi-step procedural reasoning. We sample 100 instances from various task types to cover general instruction comprehension.

•

MBPP
dataset
Austin et al. 2021
targets program synthesis. Each item includes a short programming task with an English description and a set of unit tests. We sample 100 problems from the dataset to evaluate coding and functional reasoning abilities.

•

Creative Writing
. The Creative Writing benchmark
Wu et al. 2025
consists of open-ended generation tasks such as story continuation, character development, and stylistic rewriting. We sample 100 prompts to assess models’ creativity, coherence, and narrative fluency under subjective evaluation.

Appendix E
Rerank Methods

•

Average
: Computes the average ranking position of each model across all questions. Models with lower average ranks are placed higher.

•

Borda Count

Saari 1985
: Assigns each model a score based on its position in each ranking (with higher-ranked positions receiving more points). The final ranking is obtained by sorting models by total Borda score in descending order.

•

Copeland Method

Saari and Merlin 1996
: For each pair of models, assigns
+1
for a win,
-1
for a loss, and
0
for a tie. Each model’s net score is computed by summing across all pairwise comparisons. Higher net scores lead to higher ranks.

•

Dodgson Method

Brandt 2009
: Measures how many adjacent swaps in the rankings are needed to make each model a Condorcet winner. The fewer swaps required, the better the rank.

•

IRV
(Instant-Runoff Voting)
Brandt 2009
: Iteratively eliminates the model with the fewest first-choice votes and redistributes the votes to remaining candidates, until one model remains. Final ranking is based on the order of elimination.

•

Kemeny-Young Method

Kemeny 1959
: Computes the total pairwise disagreement (Kemeny distance) between each possible ranking and all input rankings. The ranking that minimizes total disagreement is selected as the consensus.

•

Kendall Aggregation

Kendall 1948
: Enumerates all possible rankings and selects the one that maximizes the total Kendall tau correlation with the input rankings.

•

Spearman Aggregation
: Searches all permutations and selects the one that maximizes the sum of absolute Spearman correlations with the input rankings.
