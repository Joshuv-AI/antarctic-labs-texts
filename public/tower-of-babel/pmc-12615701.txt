Development of a machine learning model for automatic data extraction from breast cancer pathology reports
Authors: Christy Oi Ting Kwok, Gregory Arbour, Annah Zhang, Jenna Hiemstra, Alan M. Nichol, Raymond Ng, Kathryn V. Isaac
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Data extraction from medical records is crucial for clinical research, with current methods relying on human annotation. Natural Language Processing (NLP) and Machine Learning-based approaches show promise. We develop and evaluate an NLP pipeline constructed by selecting among four candidate models; ClinicalBERT, PubMedBERT, BioMedRoBERTa and Mistral-Nemo LLM to automate data extraction of 1,795 breast cancer pathology reports obtained from the Providence Health Services Authority in British Columbia. We also explore the effect of further pre-training the BERT-based models using the SQuAD question-answering dataset. Accuracy was evaluated by comparing model output and human annotation. PubMedBERT pre-trained on SQuAD proved to be the best performing model, achieving an overall accuracy of 97.4%. 30 of the 32 FOIs had an accuracy greater than 95.0%. Our model outperformed a previous rule-based algorithm (95.6%). Our findings demonstrate how a high-performing question-answering NLP pipeline for breast cancer pathology can provide a scalable approach to high-fidelity extraction of clinicopathologic features, thereby enhancing research efficiency and improving clinical outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-23461-6.

Introduction

Breast cancer is one of the most prevalent cancers in the world, with incidence rates as high as 91.6 cases per 100,000 in certain countries1. In Canada, 1 in 8 women are expected to develop breast cancer in their lifetime, with a corresponding mortality rate of 1 in 312. The high incidence rate of breast cancer generates a vast amount of data that is primarily captured in pathology reports. This data is a valuable resource for breast cancer research as it has a high predictive value for treatment strategies and prognosis3,4.

Electronic formats of medical records have become ubiquitous in health care systems. British Columbia has adopted a synoptic reporting approach from a modified Delphi process5, which standardizes reporting practices to reduce variability and support efficient communication amongst healthcare providers. Multiple studies have found that synoptic reports have higher overall operative report completeness than non-synoptic, or narrative, alternatives5,6.

Despite the vast quantity of available data, there is a huge bottleneck in the data extraction process as much of the desired information in unstructured text reports can only be extracted via expensive human review. The scalability, speed and accuracy of the extraction is limited by human ability, and the volume of work available for data extraction greatly exceeds manual annotation capacity. Due to the finite resources available for data extraction, a significant quantity of information remains inaccessible. This inefficiency hinders innovative exploratory clinical research and efforts to evaluate treatment strategies in a real-world setting outside of clinical trials.

A promising solution is to use computational methods like Natural Language Processing (NLP) and Machine Learning (ML)7. While NLP methods are widely utilized and integrated in industry and business8, implementation of NLP models in breast cancer care and research is limited by the performance of models relative to human expertise7,10,11. This gap in breast cancer research hinders the realization of its full potential in parsing and mining pathology reports, which are critical datasets in advancing clinical understanding and treatment strategies. We have previously used regular expressions (regex) for automated data extraction from 100 synoptic operative and 100 pathology reports, and achieved comparable success rates to human annotation (95.4% & 96.0% accuracy, respectively)11. Although regex performs well with these semi-structured reports, generalizability is restricted. Transformer models have recently emerged as a dominant paradigm in NLP, achieving top scores in many NLP benchmarks12. Notably, Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers (BERT) models are widely used due to their performance and robust development ecosystem13,14. These models have a deep sense of language context and flow and are capable of complicated tasks, such as question answering and named entity recognition, without needing substantial task-specific architecture modifications. Compared with popular and more powerful models such as GPT-315, BERT models have the advantage of being small enough to be locally hosted and trained. This is advantageous when dealing with clinical data and the concomitant constraints of handling private and sensitive data.

Zhou et al., (2022) created three different versions of CancerBERT by further pre-training BlueBERT – a model that is pre-trained on PubMed abstracts and MIMIC-III clinical notes – and by modifying the vocabulary to incorporate more cancer-specific terms in order to extract eight cancer fields of interest (FOIs) from clinical text16,17. Sushil et al. (2024) investigated the use of zero-shot inference of breast cancer pathology reports to extract information related to thirteen key tasks18. The authors found that a zero-shot implementation of GPT-4 outperforms more traditional supervised methods, including Random Forest and BERT models.

Hussain et al. (2024) evaluated machine learning, deep learning, and large language model baselines, including BioGPT, for the automated extraction of BI-RADS classification from radiological reports. Kefeli et al. uses a BERT-based model to extract or infer staging information from cancer pathology reports19.

A variety of open-source transformers models pre-trained on clinical text are available. We explore the use of PubMedBERT21, BioMedRoBERTa22, ClinicalBERT23 in a supervised learning setting. We also explore the use of further pre-training these models on SQuAD24, an out-of-domain question-answering dataset, Lastly, we compare the results to a Mistral LLM used in a zero-shot capacity and compare and contrast its strengths and weaknesses to that of the BERT models. Specifically, we selected Mistral-Nemo-Instruct-2407, a 12.2B parameter model. We selected this model as it is one of the largest LLM’s that is still able to be fit in memory of a moderate sized GPU. In this work, our primary contribution is the design, implementation, and evaluation of a complete, optimized, domain-specific extractive QA pipeline that integrates pre-processing, model fine-tuning, and post-processing to enable automated extraction of population-scale structured breast cancer pathology data.

Overview

We developed a domain-specific extractive QA pipeline to automate the extraction of 32 FOIs from synoptic breast cancer pathology reports. A high-level summary of the design is displayed in Fig. 1.

Study population and datasets

Digital medical pathology reports from a diverse cohort of patients diagnosed with breast cancer were obtained from the Provincial Health Services Authority, a provincially maintained population database. Inclusion criteria were a diagnosis of invasive carcinoma or ductal carcinoma in situ from January 1st, 2018 to December 31st, 2021. Reports were excluded if they were archived in error, incomplete, or did not contain a synoptic section.

Data collection and annotation

A codebook created by oncologists defined the 32 fields of interest (FOI) corresponding to important clinicopathologic features of breast cancer phenotypes (Supplemental Table 1). Trained annotators reviewed and labelled 1,795 reports according to the codebook, generating a total of 33,746 annotations. Several rounds of double annotation, where each annotator labelled the same reports, were performed and discrepancies between annotators were reviewed. This continued until inter-annotator agreement exceeded 99% after which reports were only labeled by a single annotator. Annotation was completed under the direct supervision of a plastic and reconstructive surgeon specializing in breast reconstruction for cancer care. Doccano, an open-source text annotation software that allows for sequence labelling with overlapping entities (Supplementary Fig. 1), was used to annotate each report. These annotations served as gold-standard labels for training and evaluation purposes. Example FOIs included Nottingham score, distance from closest invasive margin, histologic type, tumour focality, distance from closest DCIS margin and tumour size.

We defined three types of FOI’s: free text, categorical (single) and categorical (multiple). The goal of free text FOIs is to simply extract the annotated text from the report and not further attempt to classify the output. Categorical FOIs are ones in which the extracted text can be mapped to a fixed set of categories. Categorical (single) FOIs are mapped to a single category whereas categorical (multiple) are mapped to one or more categories (detailed in Supplementary Table 2).

NLP pipeline development

NLP pipeline development consisted of three steps: (1) data preprocessing (2) model development (pre-training & fine-tuning) (3) post-processing model predictions (Fig. 1). Data preprocessing consisted of isolating the synoptic section of the report using regex, removing leading or trailing whitespace from the annotations, and creating “unanswerable” training examples. Isolating the synoptic section was done because the focus of this project was to extract data elements specifically from the synoptic section of the report, which is the highest-fidelity source of information for our FOIs.

To train a BERT Question-Answering (QA) model, we required both answerable and unanswerable training examples. Each report contains, on average, 18.8 gold standard annotations. This implies that there is an average of 13.2 unanswerable FOIs per report. For example, if the human coders annotated spans of text for 30/32 FOIs but did not label any text corresponded to “Mitotic Rate” or “Tumour Size”, this implies that the pathology report does not contain the necessary information to specify answers for those two FOIs. These cases are termed “unanswerable” or “negative examples”24. The final step in preprocessing was therefore to create unanswerable entries for each report, bringing the total number of training examples per report to exactly 32.

Isolating the synoptic section

In pre-processing, the dataset was optimized for BERT performance by reducing the token length. After isolating the synoptic section, 78.9% of input sequences were less than 512 tokens in length, compared with only 3.9% when using the entire document as input (Fig. 2). Although the input sequence limit of BERT is 512 tokens, all our candidate QA models can accommodate sequences longer than 512 tokens via a moving window approach. However, it is our experience that QA accuracy is improved if irrelevant text is removed prior to modeling.

Model pre-training and fine-tuning

We conducted a series of experiments using the “pre-train, fine-tune” strategy that is common with BERT models25,26. We used training and validation sets to explore the effect of base model choice (ClinicalBERT23, BioMedRoBERTa22, PubMedBERT21 and further pre-training of the base model on 10k SQuAD2.0 training examples24. SQuAD2.0 is a question-answering dataset, which, importantly, also contains unanswerable training examples. We performed hyperparameter tuning on each candidate model (both with and without additional pre-training on SQuAD) using random grid search with eight repetitions. See Supplemental Table 3 for a list of candidate hyperparameters. All models were trained on an Nvidia RTX A5000 with 24 GB of memory.

Use of training, validation and test datasets

The dataset was split into training (80%, n = 1,432 reports, 45.8k FOIs), validation (10%, n = 181 reports, 5.8k FOIs) and test (10%, n = 181 reports, 5.8k FOIs ) sets. Hyperparameter tuning was done by fine-tuning each model on the training set and evaluating on the validation set. The best performing hyperparameter set was selected and then used to retrain the model on the combined train and validation set. Finally, this model was evaluated on the test set in order to obtain an unbiased estimate of model performance on unseen data. The Mistal model, used in a zero-shot capacity, was prompt-tuned manually on the combined train-validation set until no further improvements could be made, and then evaluated on the test set.

Post-processing model predictions

BERT QA models return both an answer from the context as well as a confidence score for that answer. Answers that had a confidence score below the default threshold of 0.5 were classified as unanswerable. Both categorical (single) and categorical (multiple) FOIs require that the extracted text be mapped to one or more of a set of fixed categories, as outlined in our code book. Post-processing is achieved using a regex keyword search, which achieves near-perfect results. We also attempted a sentence-similarity approach using pre-trained word embeddings in the Python spaCy library although the results were substantially worse. Incorrect predictions resulting from the pipeline were nearly always a result of the QA extraction as opposed to the regex matching function.

Evaluation metrics

To evaluate the performance of our pipeline, we use the Exact Match (EM) and F1 score metrics. For free text FOIs, EM = 1 if every character in the extraction matches the gold standard annotation exactly; otherwise, EM = 0. For categorical FOIs (both single and multiple), EM = 1 if all the gold standard categories are predicted correctly and EM = 0 otherwise. For example, if the QA extraction + regex matching function correctly predicts only ¾ categories correctly, EM = 0 (Fig. 3). For unanswerable questions where the correct answer is that the FOI is missing from the report, if the QA extraction model returns any answer, then EM = 0. EM is the most restrictive QA metric; other metrics such as F1, recall or precision reward partially correct answers, EM does not.

Error analysis

We manually reviewed each of the 196 test set errors from PubMedBERT-SQuAD. 166/196 errors were answerable, compared to only 30/196 unanswerable. 138/166 of these answerable errors were due to model not returning any span of text at all, as opposed to an incorrect span of text. Usually this was due to the correct answer appearing in a non-standard format.

Typical case: “DCIS Estimated Size: 5.0 mm”.

Error case: “DCIS involves only 2 of 11 blocks containing invasive tumour from the larger tumour”.

The Mistral LLM errors, on the other hand, tended to be due to the model returning a far longer span of text than required, resulting in an EM = 0 and a low F1 score. While these answers were still useful, they required a much higher degree of post-processing in order to coerce the answer into a useful format.

Importantly, none of the errors resulted from long-range dependencies in the text that exceeded the 512 token limit of BERT. In certain scenarios this can pose a challenge and would motivate the use of either a different pre-processing strategy or the use of a model capable of longer input sequences.

Discussion

In this study, we developed and evaluated a customizable NLP extraction pipeline using PubMedBERT for breast cancer pathology reports using a relatively large cohort size. Our model is robust, adaptable and efficient. It outperforms our previous rule-set based model11 and achieved high accuracy across most of the FOIs. 30/32 FOIs achieved an overall accuracy above 95.0%, and the mean EM for answerable and unanswerable FOIs was 95.8% and 99.2%, respectively. The model accuracy for all FOIs was 97.4%, exceeding the accuracy of a regular expression (regex)-based model from our preliminary studies (95.6%). The few poorly performing FOIs (Tumour Site, Extent (ID), Extent (IC)) are also those with few training examples, suggesting that a remedy to this situation is to gather more data. Mistral LLM performed well on these fields, which is unsurprising given that LLM’s are well suited for zero-shot tasks. Therefore, a possible solution to improving accuracy on these four FOIs may be an ensemble of PubMedBERT-SQuAD and Mistral.

This demonstrates that a highly accurate NLP pipeline can be constructed using a modestly sized language model and without large computational resources. This is particularly advantageous for clinical and research settings with limited access to high-performance computing infrastructure, and enables broader adoption and scalability.

The implementation of a PubMedBERT model offers substantial benefits for breast cancer research and may also prove useful in clinical settings. The primary advantage lies in this model’s ability to automate the extraction of clinically important variables from synoptic breast cancer pathology reports, significantly reducing the reliance on manual review. This not only expedites data analysis but increases consistency in extraction and minimizes the risk of human error.

The Canadian Cancer Statistics published have historically relied on data extraction and collection from previous years. Data for the published 2021 statistics were from 1984 to 2017 (1984 to 2010 for Quebec)27. Deployment of this model could accelerate the data extraction and compilation process, thereby facilitating more timely statistical analyses and reducing the burden involved with manual data extraction.

Pipeline development

The “pre-train, fine-tune” paradigm has become popular in NLP research with large language models such as BERT, T5, and others leveraging this strategy25. Pre-training allows the model to “develop general-purpose abilities and knowledge that can then be transferred to downstream tasks” usually by training it in an unsupervised manner on a large quantity of unlabeled data26. The type of data and pre-training task have been shown to have significant implications for the model’s performance on the downstream task. Pre-training data that is from a similar domain to the downstream task, which was clinical text or biomedical text for our study is desirable. Our experiments show that pre-training on SQuAD2.0 in this manner increases the performance of the extractive QA portion of our pipeline.

Limitations of this study are inherent in the study design based on a dataset of synoptic breast cancer pathology reports. Generalization of the model to different geographic regions or healthcare institutions may be limited depending on variations in reporting practices, report structure and template, terminology or healthcare infrastructure. Lastly, since the model’s performance is inherently tied to the quality and representativeness of the training data, there may be inherent biases.

Future directions include using this model to automatically extract data for other types of cancers with structured pathology reports, like prostate, pancreatic and colorectal cancers28, and for unstructured breast cancer reports. The accuracy of the current model’s FOIs with sub-90% accuracy may be improved by adding additional rules, at the expense of further impairing generalizability. Paired with a model for non-synoptic (unstructured) breast cancer pathology reports, we plan to integrate this NLP model into clinical settings and research environments to enhance the scalability and speed of data extraction of population-based datasets. Preliminary work applying a similar methodology to unstructured breast cancer pathology reports, with slight modifications to the preprocessing and QA-pair generation steps, suggest that a similar pipeline can extract key FOIs with reasonable accuracy, indicating that the approach is adaptable beyond fully structured synoptic formats. Additionally, the incorporation of pathology reports from other provinces into this model can help assess the model’s adaptability across different reporting standards and healthcare infrastructures, and serve as an external validation. We also aim to explore transfer learning strategies, like using newer LLM or other strategies to reduce the burden of annotations; thus, increasing diversity and size of dataset for generalizability.

Conclusion

Our data and findings illustrate the superiority of using a pre-trained transformer model to automatically extract FOIs from breast cancer pathology reports and suggest that the PubMedBERT NLP pipeline is suitable for automatic data extraction of breast cancer pathology reports. It achieved high accuracy on many FOIs and outperformed human annotation, algorithm-based models and all other candidate models; thus, demonstrating its feasibility for clinical and research settings. Automating data extraction for breast cancer pathology reports will reduce the labor and resources needed and holds promise for revolutionizing breast cancer research.
