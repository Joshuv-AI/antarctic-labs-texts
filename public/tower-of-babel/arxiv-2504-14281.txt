Exploiting Symmetric Non-Convexity for Multi-Objective Symbol-Level DFRC Signal Design
Ly V. Nguyen, Rang Liu, Nhan Thanh Nguyen, Markku Juntti, Björn Ottersten, A. Lee Swindlehurst

Abstract
Symbol-level precoding (SLP) is a promising solution for addressing the inherent interference problem in dual-functional radar-communication (DFRC) signal designs. This paper considers an SLP-DFRC signal design problem which optimizes the radar performance under communication performance constraints. We show that a common phase modulation applied to the transmit signals from an antenna array does not affect the performance of different radar sensing metrics, including beampattern similarity, signal-to-interference-plus-noise ratio (SINR), and Cramér-Rao lower bound (CRLB). We refer to this as symmetric-rotation invariance, upon which we develop low-complexity yet efficient DFRC signal design algorithms. More specifically, we propose a symmetric non-convexity (SNC)-based DFRC algorithm that relies on the non-convexity of the radar sensing metrics to identify a set of radar-only solutions. Based on these solutions, we further exploit the symmetry property of the radar sensing metrics to efficiently design the DFRC signal. We show that the proposed SNC-based algorithm is versatile in the sense that it can be applied to the DFRC signal optimization of all three sensing metrics mentioned above (beampattern, SINR, and CRLB). In addition, since the radar sensing metrics are independent of the communication channel and data symbols, the set of radar-only solutions can be constructed offline, thereby reducing the computational complexity. We also develop an accelerated SNC-based algorithm that further reduces the complexity. Finally, we numerically demonstrate the superiority of the proposed algorithms compared to existing methods in terms of sensing and communication performance as well as computational requirements.

This paradigm shift is particularly beneficial for DFRC, where radar-induced interference, rather than being suppressed, can be exploited to improve communication performance. Through joint optimization of radar sensing and communication functionalities, SLP-based DFRC designs harness both spatial and temporal degrees of freedom (DoFs), leading to superior system performance. Despite these advancements, the high computational complexity of existing SLP-DFRC optimization methods remains a fundamental challenge for practical deployment. The joint symbol-level optimization of radar and communication functionalities inherently leads to large-scale, non-convex, and highly nonlinear problems, posing significant difficulties for real-time implementation. Most existing approaches rely on iterative algorithmic frameworks such as alternating direction method of multipliers (ADMM), majorization maximization (MM), successive convex approximation (SCA), or the Lagrangian method with block successive upper-bound minimization (ALM-BSUM). These approaches, while effective, often suffer from slow convergence rates and substantial per-iteration computational overhead.
Moreover, many of these methods lack closed-form solutions for some variables, necessitating the use of convex optimization solvers within the iterative procedure, which further exacerbates computational complexity. Even in cases where closed-form solutions have been derived for certain subproblems

29

Motivated by these challenges, in this paper we propose a computationally efficient SLP-DFRC waveform design framework that exploits the symmetric non-convexity (SNC) property of radar sensing metrics.
While preliminary results in
[
1
]
demonstrated the potential of this approach for beampattern similarity optimization, this work extends the SNC-based framework to radar SINR and CRLB optimization, providing a generalized, low-complexity solution for SLP-DFRC waveform design.
Our main contributions are summarized as follows:

•

We first propose a novel SLP-DFRC waveform design method that maximizes the radar beampattern similarity under symbol-level communication constraints. We observe that a common phase modulation applied to the transmit signals from an antenna array does not affect the beampattern similarity metric, a property we hereafter refer to as “
symmetric-rotation invariance
”. Motivated by this observation, we propose an SNC-based algorithm that first identifies a set of radar-only solutions leveraging the non-convexity of the radar beampattern metric. The radar-only solutions can be precomputed offline since the sensing metric is independent of the communication channel and data symbols, therefore significantly reducing computational complexity. The phase of the elements of each locally optimal radar-only waveform is then perturbed in order to (1) meet safety margin constraints for the communication users, and (2) yield a DFRC waveform that is as close as possible to the radar-only solution multiplied by a common phase modulation across all antennas.

•

Next, we derive two other SLP-DFRC waveform designs that optimize the radar SINR and CRLB. We show that the symmetric-rotation invariance property also applies to the radar SINR and CRLB metrics, enabling the previously proposed SNC-based algorithm to be effectively used for DFRC signal optimization involving the SINR and CRLB. We derive closed-form expressions for the gradients of the complex SINR and CRLB metrics involved, facilitating the optimization.

•

We then develop an accelerated SNC-based algorithm, hereafter referred to as the aSNC approach, that further reduces the complexity. We show analytically that the computational complexity of the proposed SNC and aSNC algorithms is significantly lower than that of other existing approaches.

•

Finally, we provide extensive numerical results to validate the effectiveness of the proposed algorithms, demonstrating their superior radar sensing and communication performance compared to existing approaches. Furthermore, we show significantly reduced computational complexity in multi-objective optimization scenarios.

\displaystyle\minimize_{\{\mathbf{x}\}}

\displaystyle\st

\displaystyle\eqref{eq:beam_constrain2},

{x}^{\mathrm{RC}}_{n}

{x}^{\mathrm{R}}_{n}
both have the same constant modulus, we can write

\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{RC}}=\operatorname{diag}{(}\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{R}})\boldsymbol{{\phi}},

(10)

where
\phi_{n}=e^{\mathtt{j}\varphi_{n}},\varphi_{n}\in[0,2\pi],\forall n
. Thus, given a radar solution
\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{R}}
, the design of
\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{RC}}
is equivalent to the design of the vector of rotations
\boldsymbol{{\phi}}
, which are found such that
\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{RC}}
satisfies the communication constraints (
3a
) and (
3b
). To avoid adversely affecting the radar performance, we decompose
\boldsymbol{{\phi}}
into two components as follows:

\boldsymbol{{\phi}}=e^{\mathtt{j}\varphi}\boldsymbol{{\tilde{\phi}}},

(11)

where
\boldsymbol{{\tilde{\phi}}}
is desired to be as close as possible to
\mathbf{1}_{N}
, the
N
-dimensional vector whose elements all equal one. If
\boldsymbol{{\tilde{\phi}}}=\mathbf{1}_{N}
, then the DFRC signal in (
10
) will have the same radar performance as
\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{R}}
due to Remark 3. Thus, the basic idea of our algorithm presented next is to design
\varphi
and a vector
\boldsymbol{{\tilde{\phi}}}
close to
\mathbf{1}_{N}
to ensure the communication constraints. The steps of the algorithm are outlined below.

III-B
1
Optimize
\varphi
for a given
\boldsymbol{\tilde{\phi}}

Using the decomposition in (
11
), the DFRC signal
\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{RC}}
can be written as

\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{RC}}=e^{\mathtt{j}\varphi}\operatorname{diag}{(}\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{R}})\boldsymbol{{\tilde{\phi}}}.

The rotated noiseless received signal is then given as

\mathbf{z}=\operatorname{diag}{(}\mathbf{s}^{*})\mathbf{H}\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{RC}}=e^{\mathtt{j}\varphi}\mathbf{Q}\boldsymbol{{\tilde{\phi}}}=e^{\mathtt{j}{\varphi}}\mathbf{\tilde{s}},

where
\mathbf{Q}=\operatorname{diag}{(}\mathbf{s}^{*})\mathbf{H}\operatorname{diag}{(}\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{R}})
and
\mathbf{\tilde{s}}=\mathbf{Q}\boldsymbol{\tilde{\phi}}
.

Let
\mathbf{\tilde{s}}=[\tilde{s}_{1},\,\ldots,\,\tilde{s}_{U}]^{\mathrm{T}}
. The safety margin of user
u
can be written as a function of
{\varphi}
as follows:

\displaystyle\delta_{u}(\varphi)

\displaystyle=\Re\{e^{\mathtt{j}\varphi}\tilde{s}_{u}\}\sin(\pi/M)-|\Im\{e^{\mathtt{j}\varphi}\tilde{s}_{u}\}|\cos(\pi/M).

(12)

To ensure the constraints in (
3a
), our objective will be to find an angle
\varphi
such that
\min_{u}\,\big(\delta_{u}(\varphi)-\gamma_{u}\big)
is maximized. If
\varphi
can make
\min_{u}\,\big(\delta_{u}(\varphi)-\gamma_{u}\big)\geq 0
, then the communication constraints are satisfied. Hence, we need to solve the following optimization problem:

\displaystyle\maximize_{0\leq\varphi\leq 2\pi}\;\;\min_{u}\,\big(\delta_{u}(\varphi)-\gamma_{u}\big).

(13)

This is a non-convex problem but it can be solved easily since it involves only the single variable
\varphi
which is constrained to lie in
[0,2\pi]
. We propose an efficient algorithm to solve (
13
) as follows.

First, we uniformly sample the range
[0,2\pi]
to obtain
C
angle samples
\{\check{\varphi}_{1},\,\ldots,\,\check{\varphi}_{C}\}
, i.e.,
\check{\varphi}_{c}=2\pi(c-1)/(C-1)
. Then, a coarse solution for
\varphi
can be found as the value in
\{\check{\varphi}_{1},\,\ldots,\,\check{\varphi}_{C}\}
that yields the largest
\min_{u}\,\big(\delta_{u}(\check{\varphi_{c}})-\gamma_{u}\big)
, i.e.,

\varphi^{\mathrm{coarse}}=\argmax_{\{\check{\varphi}_{c}\}}\;\min_{u}\,\big(\delta_{u}(\check{\varphi_{c}})-\gamma_{u}\big).

(14)

Next, starting from the coarse solution
\varphi^{\mathrm{coarse}}
, a refined solution
\varphi^{\mathrm{fine}}
can be obtained by moving along the gradient direction until convergence:

\varphi^{\mathrm{fine}}\leftarrow\varphi^{\mathrm{fine}}+\eta_{2}\frac{\partial\delta_{\bar{u}}(\varphi^{\mathrm{fine}})}{\partial\varphi},

(15)

where
\eta_{2}
is the step size,
\bar{u}
is the user index for which
\bar{u}=\argmin_{u}\big(\delta_{u}(\varphi^{\mathrm{fine}})-\gamma_{u}\big)
, and the gradient is given as

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\delta_{\bar{u}}(\varphi)}{\partial\varphi}

\displaystyle=(-\sin(\varphi)\Re\{\tilde{s}_{\bar{u}}\}-\cos(\varphi)\Im\{\tilde{s}_{\bar{u}}\})\sin(\pi/M)\;-

\displaystyle\qquad\frac{\cos(\varphi)\Im\{\tilde{s}_{\bar{u}}\}+\sin(\varphi)\Re\{\tilde{s}_{\bar{u}}\}}{|\cos(\varphi)\Im\{\tilde{s}_{\bar{u}}\}+\sin(\varphi)\Re\{\tilde{s}_{\bar{u}}\}|}\;\times

\displaystyle\qquad(-\sin(\varphi)\Im\{\tilde{s}_{\bar{u}}\}+\cos(\varphi)\Re\{\tilde{s}_{\bar{u}}\})\cos(\pi/M).

We choose to find
\varphi_{\mathrm{fine}}
from the coarse solution in (
14

[0,2\pi]
because the objective function in (
13
) is non-convex. Directly moving along the gradient direction from a random sample in
[0,2\pi]
will likely lead to a local solution.

III-B
2

\boldsymbol{\tilde{\phi}}
for a given
\varphi

\mathbf{Q}=[\mathbf{q}_{1},\,\ldots,\,\mathbf{q}_{U}]^{\mathrm{H}}

\displaystyle\mathbf{\tilde{q}}_{2u}

\displaystyle=e^{-\mathtt{j}{\varphi}}\mathbf{q}_{u}\big[\sin(\pi/M)+e^{\mathtt{j}\pi/2}\cos(\pi/M)\big],

\displaystyle\mathbf{\tilde{q}}_{2u-1}

\displaystyle=e^{-\mathtt{j}{\varphi}}\mathbf{q}_{u}\big[\sin(\pi/M)-e^{\mathtt{j}\pi/2}\cos(\pi/M)\big].

Then the constraint
\delta_{u}\geq\gamma_{u}
is equivalent to the following two conditions:

\displaystyle\Re\big\{\mathbf{\tilde{q}}_{2u}^{\mathrm{H}}\boldsymbol{\tilde{\phi}}\big\}\geq\gamma_{u}

\displaystyle\Re\big\{\mathbf{\tilde{q}}_{2u-1}^{\mathrm{H}}\boldsymbol{\tilde{\phi}}\big\}\geq\gamma_{u}.

(17)

\Re\big\{\mathbf{\tilde{q}}_{2u}^{\mathrm{H}}\boldsymbol{\tilde{\phi}}\big\}

Consider a function of
\boldsymbol{\tilde{\phi}}

\xi_{u^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{\tilde{\phi}})=\Re\big\{\mathbf{\tilde{q}}_{u^{\prime}}^{\mathrm{H}}\boldsymbol{\tilde{\phi}}\big\}-\tilde{\gamma}_{u^{\prime}},

(18)

where

and
\tilde{\gamma}_{u^{\prime}}=\gamma_{u}
if

or

, respectively. We need to achieve
\min_{u^{\prime}}\xi_{u^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{\tilde{\phi}})\geq 0
because the two conditions (

when

. Therefore, we update
\boldsymbol{\tilde{\phi}}
by moving along the Riemannian gradient of the function

, where

\bar{u}=\argmin_{u^{\prime}=1,\,\ldots,\,2U}\xi_{{u^{\prime}}}(\boldsymbol{\tilde{\phi}}).

The Riemannian gradient of

\nabla_{\mathrm{Rie}}\;\xi_{\bar{u}}(\boldsymbol{\tilde{\phi}})=\mathbf{\tilde{q}}_{\bar{u}}-\Re\left\{\mathbf{\tilde{q}}_{\bar{u}}\odot\boldsymbol{\tilde{\phi}}^{*}\right\}\odot\boldsymbol{\tilde{\phi}},

and thus an update of
\boldsymbol{\tilde{\phi}}

\boldsymbol{\tilde{\phi}}\leftarrow\mathrm{Proj}\left(\boldsymbol{\tilde{\phi}}+\eta_{3}\nabla_{\mathrm{Rie}}\;\xi_{\bar{k}}\big(\boldsymbol{\tilde{\phi}}\big)\right),

(19)

where
\eta_{3}
is a given step size.

\varphi

IV
Radar SINR-Based Design

In the previous section, we showed that Remarks 1-3 hold for the beampattern metric, based on which we developed an efficient SNC-based transmit DFRC signal design. In this section, we show that Remarks 1-3 also hold for the radar SINR metric. Thus, the SNC-based method proposed in the previous section can be adapted to solve the problem of optimizing the radar SINR under communication constraints.

To derive the SINR for target
k
, we vectorize the received signal matrix
\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{r}}
to obtain

\displaystyle\mathbf{y}_{\mathrm{r}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\alpha_{k}\big(\mathbf{I}_{\tau}\otimes(\mathbf{a}_{k}\mathbf{a}_{k}^{\mathrm{H}})\big)\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{n}_{\mathrm{r}}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\alpha_{k}\mathbf{B}_{k}\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{n}_{\mathrm{r}},

(20)

where
\mathbf{y}_{\mathrm{r}}=\mathrm{vec}(\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{r}})
,
\mathbf{B}_{k}=\mathbf{I}_{\tau}\otimes(\mathbf{a}_{k}\mathbf{a}_{k}^{\mathrm{H}})
,
\mathbf{x}=\mathrm{vec}(\mathbf{X})
, and
\mathbf{n}_{\mathrm{r}}=\mathrm{vec}(\mathbf{N}_{\mathrm{r}})
. The SINR for target
k
is thus

f_{\mathrm{SINR}_{k}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{w}_{k})=\displaystyle\frac{\sigma_{k}^{2}|\mathbf{w}_{k}^{\mathrm{H}}\mathbf{B}_{k}\mathbf{x}|^{2}}{\mathbf{w}_{k}^{\mathrm{H}}\left(\sum_{i\neq\ell_{k}}\sigma^{2}_{i}\mathbf{B}_{i}\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{H}}\mathbf{B}_{i}^{\mathrm{H}}+\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{r}}\mathbf{I}\right)\mathbf{w}_{k}},

(21)

where
\ell_{k}\in\mathcal{L}
is the angle index of target
k
in
\mathcal{L}
and
\mathbf{w}_{k}
is the radar receive filter for target
k
. The location indices corresponding to other targets and clutter are in the set
\mathcal{L}\setminus\{\ell_{k}\}
.

We want to design the transmit signal
\mathbf{x}
and the receive radar filters
[\mathbf{w}_{1},\,\ldots,\,\mathbf{w}_{K}]
so that the minimum radar SINR is maximized. Hence, the problem of interest is given as

\displaystyle\maximize_{\{\mathbf{x},\,\mathbf{w}_{1},\,\ldots,\,\mathbf{w}_{K}\}}

\displaystyle\min_{k}\,f_{\mathrm{SINR}_{k}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{w}_{k})

(22)

\displaystyle\st

\displaystyle\eqref{eq:beam_constrain1}\;\text{and}\;\eqref{eq:beam_constrain2}.

For a given transmit signal vector
\mathbf{x}
, problem (
22
) reduces to a minimum variance distortionless response optimization problem
[
20
,
19
]

\displaystyle\minimize_{\{\mathbf{w}_{k}\}}

\displaystyle\mathbf{w}_{k}^{\mathrm{H}}\bigg(\sum_{i\neq\ell_{k}}\sigma^{2}_{i}\mathbf{B}_{i}\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{H}}\mathbf{B}_{i}^{\mathrm{H}}+\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{r}}\mathbf{I}\bigg)\mathbf{w}_{k}

(23)

\displaystyle\st

\displaystyle\mathbf{w}_{k}^{\mathrm{H}}\mathbf{B}_{k}\mathbf{x}=1,

whose optimal solution
\mathbf{w}_{k}
is given as

\mathbf{w}_{k}=\frac{\big(\sum_{i\neq\ell_{k}}\sigma^{2}_{i}\mathbf{B}_{i}\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{H}}\mathbf{B}_{i}^{\mathrm{H}}+\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{r}}\mathbf{I}\big)^{-1}\mathbf{B}_{k}\mathbf{x}}{\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{H}}\mathbf{B}_{k}^{\mathrm{H}}\big(\sum_{i\neq\ell_{k}}\sigma^{2}_{i}\mathbf{B}_{i}\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{H}}\mathbf{B}_{i}^{\mathrm{H}}+\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{r}}\mathbf{I}\big)^{-1}\mathbf{B}_{k}\mathbf{x}}.

(24)

Substituting (
24
) into (
21
) yields the SINR for target
k
that only depends on the transmit signal
\mathbf{x}
as follows:

f_{\mathrm{SINR}_{k}}(\mathbf{x})=\sigma^{2}_{k}\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{H}}\mathbf{B}_{k}^{\mathrm{H}}\mathbf{C}_{k}^{-1}\mathbf{B}_{k}\mathbf{x},

(25)

where

\mathbf{C}_{k}=\sum_{i\neq\ell_{k}}\sigma^{2}_{i}\mathbf{B}_{i}\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{H}}\mathbf{B}_{i}^{\mathrm{H}}+\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{r}}\mathbf{I}.

(26)

Thus, problem (
22
) can be written in the following form:

\displaystyle\maximize_{\{\mathbf{x}\}}

\displaystyle\min_{k}\,f_{\mathrm{SINR}_{k}}(\mathbf{x})

(27)

\displaystyle\st

\displaystyle\eqref{eq:beam_constrain1}\;\text{and}\;\eqref{eq:beam_constrain2},

which now only depends on the transmit signal
\mathbf{x}
.

The function
f_{\mathrm{SINR}_{k}}(\mathbf{x})
in (
25
) has a complicated form that involves a matrix inversion, making it challenging to solve problem (
27
). However, we will exploit the fact that Remarks 1-3 continue to hold for the SINR function
f_{\mathrm{SINR}_{k}}(\mathbf{x})
, as described below:

•

f_{\mathrm{SINR}_{k}}(\mathbf{x})
is non-concave w.r.t.
\mathbf{x}
and therefore has many local maxima.

•

f_{\mathrm{SINR}_{k}}(\mathbf{x})
does not depend on the communication channel
\mathbf{H}
and the users’ data symbols
\mathbf{s}
.

•

If each signal vector
\mathbf{x}_{t}
is scaled by
e^{\mathtt{j}\varphi_{t}}
for any
\varphi_{t}
and we let
\boldsymbol{\Omega}=\operatorname{diag}{(}e^{\mathtt{j}\varphi_{1}},\,\ldots,\,e^{\mathtt{j}\varphi_{\tau}})
, then the rotated transmit signal
\mathbf{\tilde{x}}=\big(\boldsymbol{\Omega}\otimes\mathbf{I}_{N}\big)\mathbf{{x}}
does not change the radar SINR, i.e.,

f_{\mathrm{SINR}_{k}}(\mathbf{\tilde{x}})=f_{\mathrm{SINR}_{k}}(\mathbf{x}),\ \forall\varphi_{1},\,\ldots,\,\varphi_{\tau}.

(28)

The result in (
28
) can be easily obtained by calculating the SINR after right-multiplying the received signal
\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{r}}
in (
2
) with the rotation matrix
\boldsymbol{\Omega}
.

Hence, we can directly adopt the SNC-based method proposed in the previous section to solve problem (
27
). The main difference is in how the set of prior radar solutions is generated, as this requires calculation of the gradient of
f_{\mathrm{SINR}_{k}}(\mathbf{x})
w.r.t.
\mathbf{x}
, as derived in the following lemma.

Lemma 1
.

The Euclidean gradient of
f_{\mathrm{SINR}_{k}}(\mathbf{x})
w.r.t.
\mathbf{x}
is given as

\displaystyle\nabla f_{\mathrm{SINR}_{k}}(\mathbf{x})=

\displaystyle\quad\sigma^{2}_{k}\Big(\mathbf{B}_{k}^{\mathrm{H}}-\sum_{i\neq\ell_{k}}\sigma^{2}_{i}(\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{H}}\mathbf{B}_{k}^{\mathrm{H}}\mathbf{C}_{k}^{-1}\mathbf{B}_{i}\mathbf{x})\mathbf{B}_{i}^{\mathrm{H}}\Big)\mathbf{C}_{k}^{-1}\mathbf{B}_{k}\mathbf{x}.

(29)

Proof.

See Appendix
A
.
∎

Since SINR-based objective is
\min_{k}\,f_{\mathrm{SINR}_{k}}(\mathbf{x})
, the gradient for a given
\mathbf{x}
has to be taken for the target with the lowest SINR. To design a transmit DFRC signal vector
\mathbf{x}_{t}
, we can use Algorithm
1
since, as noted in (
28
), an arbitrary rotation
e^{\mathtt{j}\varphi_{t}}
to the transmit signal vector
\mathbf{x}_{t}
does not change the radar SINR.

V
Radar CRLB-Based Design

Thus far, we have exploited the SNC structure of both the radar beampattern and SINR mectrics to develop efficient DFRC signal designs. In this section, we demonstrate that the SNC property extends to the radar CRLB metric, enabling the proposed SNC-based method to be directly applied for CRLB-based designs.

First, let us denote
\boldsymbol{\psi}=[\alpha_{1},\,\ldots,\,\alpha_{K},\,\vartheta_{1},\,\ldots,\,\vartheta_{K}]^{\mathrm{T}}
as the vector of target parameters and let
\mathbf{G}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\alpha_{k}\mathbf{a}_{k}\mathbf{a}_{k}^{\mathrm{H}}
, then we can write the received signal
\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{r}}
as follows:

\displaystyle\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{r}}=\mathbf{G}\mathbf{X}+\mathbf{N}_{\mathrm{r}}.

(30)

We also vectorize the received signal
\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{r}}
to obtain

\displaystyle\mathbf{y}_{\mathrm{r}}=(\mathbf{X}^{\mathrm{T}}\otimes\mathbf{I}_{N})\mathbf{g}+\mathbf{n}_{\mathrm{r}},

(31)

where
\mathbf{g}=\mathrm{vec}(\mathbf{G})
.

Given
\mathbf{X}
and
\boldsymbol{\psi}
, the received signal
\mathbf{y}_{\mathrm{r}}
is distributed as
\mathcal{CN}(\boldsymbol{\mu},\sigma_{\mathrm{r}}^{2}\mathbf{I}_{N})
where
\boldsymbol{\mu}=(\mathbf{X}^{\mathrm{T}}\otimes\mathbf{I}_{N})\mathbf{g}
. The Fisher information matrix w.r.t.
\boldsymbol{\psi}
is given by
[
41
]

\displaystyle\mathbf{F}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{\mu}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\psi}}\right)^{\mathrm{H}}(\sigma_{\mathrm{r}}^{2}\mathbf{I}_{N})^{-1}\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{\mu}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\psi}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{r}}}\left(\frac{\partial\mathbf{g}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\psi}}\right)^{\mathrm{H}}\big((\mathbf{X}^{*}\mathbf{X}^{\mathrm{T}})\otimes\mathbf{I}_{N}\big)\frac{\partial\mathbf{g}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\psi}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{r}}}\mathbf{T}^{\mathrm{H}}\big((\mathbf{X}^{*}\mathbf{X}^{\mathrm{T}})\otimes\mathbf{I}_{N}\big)\mathbf{T}=\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{r}}}\mathbf{T}^{\mathrm{H}}\mathbf{\tilde{X}}\mathbf{T},

(32)

where we have defined
\mathbf{T}=\frac{\partial\mathbf{g}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\psi}}
and
\mathbf{\tilde{X}}=(\mathbf{X}^{*}\mathbf{X}^{\mathrm{T}})\otimes\mathbf{I}_{N}
.

While the result we describe holds for any array geometry, assume as an example that a uniform linear array (ULA) with half-wavelength antenna spacing is employed, so that the steering vector is expressed as

\mathbf{a}_{k}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}[1,\,e^{\mathtt{j}\pi\sin(\vartheta_{k})},\,\ldots,\,e^{\mathtt{j}(N-1)\pi\sin(\vartheta_{k})}]^{\mathrm{H}}.

Define

\displaystyle\mathbf{\dot{a}}_{k}=\frac{\partial\mathbf{a}_{k}}{\partial\vartheta_{k}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}[0,\,\mathtt{j}\pi\cos(\vartheta_{k})e^{\mathtt{j}\pi\sin(\vartheta_{k})},\,\ldots,\,

\displaystyle\qquad\mathtt{j}\pi(N-1)\cos(\vartheta_{k})e^{\mathtt{j}\pi(N-1)\sin(\vartheta_{k})}]^{\mathrm{H}},

and note that
\mathbf{T}
can then be written in the following form

\mathbf{T}=[\mathbf{T}_{\alpha}\;\,\mathbf{T}_{\vartheta}]

(33)

where
\mathbf{T}_{\alpha}
and
\mathbf{T}_{\vartheta}
are given in (
35
) and (
36
), respectively. The Fisher information matrix
\mathbf{F}
can now be expressed as

\mathbf{F}=\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{r}}}\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{T}_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{H}}\mathbf{\tilde{X}}\mathbf{T}_{\alpha}&\mathbf{T}_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{H}}\mathbf{\tilde{X}}\mathbf{T}_{\vartheta}\\
\mathbf{T}_{\vartheta}^{\mathrm{H}}\mathbf{\tilde{X}}\mathbf{T}_{\alpha}&\mathbf{T}_{\vartheta}^{\mathrm{H}}\mathbf{\tilde{X}}\mathbf{T}_{\vartheta}\end{bmatrix}.

(34)

\mathbf{T}_{\alpha}=\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc}\mathbf{a}_{1}&&\mathbf{a}_{K}\\
e^{-\mathtt{j}\pi\sin(\vartheta_{1})}\mathbf{a}_{1}&&e^{-\mathtt{j}\pi\sin(\vartheta_{K})}\mathbf{a}_{K}\\
\vdots&\cdots&\vdots\\
e^{-\mathtt{j}\pi(N-1)\sin(\vartheta_{1})}\mathbf{a}_{1}&&e^{-\mathtt{j}\pi(N-1)\sin(\vartheta_{K})}\mathbf{a}_{K}\end{array}\right]

(35)

\mathbf{T}_{\vartheta}=\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc}\alpha_{1}\mathbf{\dot{a}}_{1}&&\alpha_{K}\mathbf{\dot{a}}_{K}\\
\alpha_{1}e^{-\mathtt{j}\pi\sin(\vartheta_{1})}(\mathbf{\dot{a}}_{1}-\mathtt{j}\pi\cos(\vartheta_{1})\mathbf{a}_{1})&&\alpha_{K}e^{-\mathtt{j}\pi\sin(\vartheta_{K})}(\mathbf{\dot{a}}_{K}-\mathtt{j}\pi\cos(\vartheta_{K})\mathbf{a}_{K})\\
\vdots&\cdots&\vdots\\
\alpha_{1}e^{-\mathtt{j}\pi(N-1)\sin(\vartheta_{1})}(\mathbf{\dot{a}}_{1}-\mathtt{j}\pi(N-1)\cos(\vartheta_{1})\mathbf{a}_{1})&&\alpha_{K}e^{-\mathtt{j}\pi(N-1)\sin(\vartheta_{K})}(\mathbf{\dot{a}}_{K}-\mathtt{j}\pi(N-1)\cos(\vartheta_{K})\mathbf{a}_{K})\end{array}\right].

(36)

Since we are primarily interested in the target angles
\vartheta_{1},\,\ldots,\,\vartheta_{K}
, the CRLB-based objective function will be
\operatorname{tr}((\mathbf{F}^{-1})_{K+1:2K,K+1:2K})
. By the Schur complement, we have

(\mathbf{F}^{-1})_{K+1:2K,K+1:2K}=\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{r}}\mathbf{F}_{\vartheta}^{-1}

(37)

where

\mathbf{F}_{\vartheta}=\mathbf{T}_{\vartheta}^{\mathrm{H}}\mathbf{\tilde{X}}\mathbf{T}_{\vartheta}-\mathbf{T}_{\vartheta}^{\mathrm{H}}\mathbf{\tilde{X}}\mathbf{T}_{\alpha}(\mathbf{T}_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{H}}\mathbf{\tilde{X}}\mathbf{T}_{\alpha})^{-1}\mathbf{T}_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{H}}\mathbf{\tilde{X}}\mathbf{T}_{\vartheta}.

(38)

Hence, the CRLB-based optimization metric is

f_{\mathrm{CRLB}}(\mathbf{X})=\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{r}}\operatorname{tr}\big(\mathbf{F}_{\vartheta}^{-1}\big)

(39)

and the CRLB-based DFRC signal design problem is

\displaystyle\minimize_{\{\mathbf{X}\}}

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{r}}\operatorname{tr}\big(\mathbf{F}_{\vartheta}^{-1}\big)

(40)

\displaystyle\st

\displaystyle\eqref{eq:beam_constrain1}\;\text{and}\;\eqref{eq:beam_constrain2}.

Again, we can see that the optimization problem (
40
) is challenging to solve due to the complicated form of the objective function in (
39
), which involves nested matrix inversions. However, the SNC-based method proposed above can be adopted in this case to efficiently solve the signal design problem (
40
). This is because the objective function
f_{\mathrm{CRLB}}(\mathbf{X})
is (
i
) non-convex w.r.t.
\mathbf{X}
with many local minima, (
ii
) it is independent of the channel
\mathbf{H}
and the users’ data
\mathbf{s}
, and (
iii
) it is invariant to any rotation
\boldsymbol{\Omega}
applied to the transmit signal
\mathbf{X}
, i.e.,
f_{\mathrm{CRLB}}(\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\Omega})=f_{\mathrm{CRLB}}(\mathbf{X})
, which can be easily verified by replacing
\mathbf{X}
by
\mathbf{\tilde{X}}=\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\Omega}
in the objective function in (
39
).

To construct the set of radar solutions for the CRLB-based metric, the challenge is in the derivation of the gradient of
f_{\mathrm{CRLB}}(\mathbf{X})
w.r.t.
\mathbf{X}
, which is addressed in Lemma
2
below.

Lemma 2

IX
Conclusion

In this paper we have developed two low-complexity yet highly effective algorithms for DFRC signal design using symbol level precoding. The proposed algorithms are based on the property of symmetric rotational invariance, which refers to the fact that a common symbol-rate phase modulation applied to the transmit signals across an antenna array does not affect the value of various radar sensing metrics, including beampattern similarity, SINR, and CRLB. We exploited this observation by perturbing the phase of the elements of a locally optimal radar-only waveform in order to (1) meet safety margin constraints for the communication users, and (2) yield a DFRC waveform that is as close as possible to the radar-only solution multiplied by a common phase modulation across all antennas. The proposed SNC- and aSNC-based algorithms were shown to be versatile and outperform existing methods in terms of radar sensing and communication performance as well as computational complexity.

\displaystyle\;=\operatorname{tr}\big(\mathbf{X}^{\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{\tilde{V}}d(\mathbf{X}^{*})\big)+\operatorname{tr}\big(d(\mathbf{X})\mathbf{X}^{\mathrm{H}}\mathbf{\tilde{V}}^{\mathrm{T}}\big).

Therefore, the total derivative of
f_{\mathrm{CRLB}}
w.r.t.
\mathbf{X}
and
\mathbf{X}^{*}
is

\displaystyle df_{\mathrm{CRLB}}=\operatorname{tr}\big(\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{r}}\mathbf{X}^{\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{\tilde{V}}d(\mathbf{X}^{*})\big)+\operatorname{tr}\big(\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{r}}d(\mathbf{X})\mathbf{X}^{\mathrm{H}}\mathbf{\tilde{V}}^{\mathrm{T}}\big).

(52)

Comparing the total derivative in (
52
) with the fact that

df_{\mathrm{CRLB}}=\operatorname{tr}\left(\frac{\partial f_{\mathrm{CRLB}}}{\partial\mathbf{X}^{*}}d\mathbf{X}^{*}\right)+\operatorname{tr}\left(d\mathbf{X}\frac{\partial f_{\mathrm{CRLB}}}{\partial\mathbf{X}}\right)

(53)

implies

\frac{\partial f_{\mathrm{CRLB}}}{\partial\mathbf{X}^{*}}=\sigma_{\mathrm{r}}^{2}\mathbf{X}^{\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{\tilde{V}}.

(54)

Therefore, the Euclidean gradient of
f_{\mathrm{CRLB}}(\mathbf{X})
w.r.t.
\mathbf{X}
is

\nabla_{\mathbf{X}}f_{\mathrm{CRLB}}=\frac{\partial f_{\mathrm{CRLB}}}{\partial\mathbf{X}^{\mathrm{H}}}=\sigma_{\mathrm{r}}^{2}\mathbf{\tilde{V}}^{\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{X},

(55)

which is the result in (
41
).
