Epidemiological investigation of a clinical case of Q fever in the Brazilian Amazon
Authors: Igor Silva Silito, Matheus Pasini-Martins, Salatiel Ribeiro Dias, Antonio Humberto Hamad Minervino, Marcelo B Labruna
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe bacterium Coxiella burnetii, responsible for causing Q fever in humans and coxiellosis in animals, is globally distributed, with the exception of New Zealand. In Brazil, is a neglected zoonosis, with scarce information, and with the number of reported cases in humans and animals is certainly underestimated, being considered an important neglected zoonosis. The present study aimed to evaluate, through an epidemiological investigation, a farm located in Belterra, Pará, Brazil, where a recent human case of Q fever occurred, considered the first recognized in the Amazon region of Brazil. After the clinical confirmation of persistent Q fever in the patient, blood samples were collected from 2 dogs, 5 cattle, 2 horses, and 22 goats living on the farm, as well as ticks found on cattle and horses (46 in total). Indirect Immunofluorescence Assay (IFA) show two dogs and three goats seropositive for C. burnetii, demonstrating their roles as sentinels and potential reservoirs and environmental spreaders. One goat had high endpoint titer (1024) to C. burnetii phase II antigen, indicating recent infection. Although no C. burnetii DNA was detected in ticks from livestock, we amplified DNA of Coxiella-like endosymbiont from the cattle tick Rhipicephalus microplus. The results of this study contribute to a better understanding of the ecology of hosts, ticks, and C. burnetii under natural and anthropogenic environmental conditions and how human activities can affect the distribution of this important pathogen.Keywords: Coxiella burnetii, Goats, Ticks, Q fever, Coxiellosis, Amazon region

Introduction

Q fever (Query fever) is a zoonotic disease caused by Coxiella burnetii, a pleomorphic, obligate intracellular, non-motile bacterium that has been classified in the class Gammaproteobacteria, order Legionellales, family Coxiellaceae (Brenner et al., 2005). In animals, it is associated with late-term abortions and reproductive disorders, with domestic ruminants (cattle, sheep, and goats) being the main reservoirs (Mioni et al., 2022). In humans, infection generally presents as a mild, self-limiting febrile illness, though in rare cases it may progress to more severe clinical manifestations, most commonly endocarditis (Damasceno and Guerra, 2018).

The primary route of transmission is through the inhalation of airborne bacterial cells or contaminated aerosols. However, more than 40 tick species belonging to 12 genera, including Ixodes, Rhipicephalus, Amblyomma, and Dermacentor, have been found naturally infected, raising the hypothesis that these arthropods may act as vectors (Kazar, 2005; Duron et al., 2015; Damasceno and Guerra, 2018). Overall, Q fever is considered an occupational disease, posing a greater risk to individuals in close contact with animals, animal products, and biological samples (Santos et al., 2007).

In Brazil, human cases have been reported in several states outside the Amazonian region, including São Paulo (Brandão et al., 1953; do Valle et al., 1955; Siciliano et al., 2015; França et al., 2022), Minas Gerais (Riemann et al., 1974; Costa et al., 2005, Costa et al., 2006), Rio de Janeiro (Lamas et al., 2009; Lamas et al., 2013; Lemos et al., 2011, Lemos et al., 2018; Rozental et al., 2012; Mares-Guia et al., 2016), Bahia (Siciliano et al., 2008) and Paraná (França et al., 2024a, França et al., 2024b, França et al., 2024c; França et al., 2024b; França et al., 2024c). These reports were based on molecular and/or serological testing.

In 2013, serological testing for Rickettsia rickettsii and C. burnetii was conducted in sheep and goats near the Serra das Confusões National Park in southeastern Piauí state, with a reported seropositivity rate of 2% (Guimarães et al., 2017). In 2017, the first study documenting natural C. burnetii infections in wild rodents from Brazil's Atlantic Forest was published (Rozental et al., 2017), followed in 2020 by serological evidence of exposure in dogs, rodents, and marsupials in the semi-arid northeast (Oliveira et al., 2020). In 2024, a study confirmed the presence of anti-C. burnetii antibodies in goats, sheep, and humans in 60% of the properties surveyed in the same region (Souza et al., 2024). However, to date, no similar studies have been conducted in the North Region of Brazil, particularly the Amazon. The role of ruminants in the epidemiological cycle of Q fever is well understood. However, little is known about the involvement of other domestic animals that maintain close contact with humans, such as dogs and horses, or about the role of ticks as vectors.

In March 2022, a human case of C. burnetii-caused endocarditis was diagnosed in a patient (farmer) that was attended in the Albert Eistein Hospital in São Paulo City. The patient used to live in a farm in the Brazilian Amazon region. Laboratory confirmation of the case relied on serological methods (indirect immunofluorescence assay) performed by a private laboratory, which resulted in IgG endpoint titers of 512 and 2048 for antigens of C. burnetii phase I and II, respectively (Dr Rinaldo Focaccia Siciliano, personal communication). Following confirmation of the diagnosis, Dr. Siciliano contacted our laboratory in the University of São Paulo (São Paulo City), in order to initiate an epidemiological study on the aforementioned farm. Hence, the present study aimed to conduct a cross-sectional investigation involving molecular analyses of ticks and both serological and molecular testing of animals (dogs, horses, cattle, and goats) on the farm where the afore-mentioned Q fever patient lived in the Amazon region.

Materials and methods

Study site

The farm of the Q fever patient is located in the Brazilian municipality of Belterra, in the state of Pará, which is part of the Lower Amazon Mesoregion. It is situated in the northern region of Brazil, at a latitude of 02° 38′ 11″ south and a longitude of 54° 56′ 14″ west, approximately 45 km from the city of Santarém, of which it was a part until December 28, 1995, when it became an independent municipality. Belterra belongs to the metropolitan region of Santarém, has an equatorial climate, an altitude of 152 m, and a population density of 4.1 inhabitants per square kilometer. Field sampling

Ticks and animal sampling were performed on site in September of 2022. A total of 31 domestic animals present on the farm were sampled for blood and ticks, comprising 2 dogs, 5 cattle, 2 horses, and 22 goats (Fig. 2). These animals were examined for the presence of ticks, which were collected and stored in plastic vials containing absolute ethanol. Whole blood was drawn into EDTA tubes and plain tubes (no anticoagulant). EDTA blood was transferred into plastic microtubes, while tubes with no anticoagulant were centrifuged to obtain serum. All samples were frozen immediately thereafter at −20 °C. Whole blood, serum and ticks were shipped to FMVZ/USP for analyses. Morphological identification of the collected ticks was carried out according to Barros-Battesti et al. (2006). Tick and blood collection were performed by a private veterinarian in charge of the farm, upon request of the farm owner, to send to our laboratory for analysis.

View of goats foraging the farm sampled in the present study.

Indirect Immunofluorescence Assay (IFA)

Serum samples from all animals were thawed at room temperature and tested against crude antigens of two Coxiella burnetii strains: the At12 strain, originally isolated from the tick Amblyomma tigrinum in Argentina (Pacheco et al., 2013), and the Nine Mile strain, isolated from the tick Dermacentor andersoni in the United States (Davis and Cox, 1938). For antigen preparation, each strain was cultured in VERO cells and maintained at 28 °C, as previously described (Pacheco et al., 2013), until a 100% infection rate of the monolayer cells was reached, assessed by Giménez staining (Gimenez, 1964). At this point, antigens were prepared and fixed onto slides, following protocols previously described (Horta et al., 2004), until their use in the indirect immunofluorescence assay (IFA) (Souza et al., 2018).

All serum samples were initially screened at a 1:64 dilution. Reactive samples were subsequently titrated in serial twofold dilutions to determine the endpoint titers, as described (Horta et al., 2004). Each slide included a known positive control serum sample reactive at 1:64 dilution, and ultrapure autoclaved water was used as a negative control. For each animal species, we used the specific fluorescein isothiocyanate-labelled anti-IgG conjugate (Sigma, St Louis, MO, USA) in an appropriate dilution previously standardized in our laboratory (IgG anti-cattle at a 1:2000 dilution, IgG anti-horse at a 1:1500 dilution, IgG anti-sheep at a 1:1500 dilution, and IgG anti-goat at a 1:1500 dilution).

Previous tests with human reference sera, carried out at the Instituto Adolfo Lutz (São Paulo, SP), indicated that C. burnetii strain At12 was reactive to anti-phase I serum, whereas C. burnetii strain Nine Mile was reactive to anti-phase II serum. The sera used to validate the strains employed in this study were previously tested using a commercial kit: I-QFB01X 10 × 12 wells, Scimedx, whose slides contained two different antigens (Phase I and Phase II) in the same well. The reaction was performed in-house using Invitrogen's anti-human IgG conjugate (A18830), at a dilution of 1:100. The reference serum for the acute phase was collected from a patient with clinical symptoms of acute Q fever, who reacted only to phase II with a titer greater than 1024, not reacting to phase I. The reference serum for the chronic phase was collected from a patient with clinical symptoms of infectious endocarditis with a negative blood culture, who reacted only to phase I, with a titer greater than 1024, not reacting to phase II. Based on this statement and following the protocol described for IFA targeting bacteria of the genus Rickettsia, serum samples showing a titer at least fourfold higher to strain At12 than to strain Nine Mile were considered reactive for anti-phase I antibodies, whereas the opposite was considered reactive for anti-phase II antibodies (Horta et al., 2004).

Molecular analyses

DNA extraction from the blood samples collected from animals on the farm was performed using the kit “Qiagen DNeasy Blood & Tissue Kit” (QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany) following the manufacturer's instructions. Initially, a PCR targeting the mitochondrial cytochrome b gene (cytb) of mammals was conducted as described by Steuber et al. (2005), in order to validate the success of the extraction process and prevent false negatives. For tick samples, DNA was extracted individually using the guanidine isothiocyanate method, as described by Sangioni et al. (2005), followed by conventional PCR targeting the mitochondrial 16S rRNA gene of ticks to ensure the reliability of the extraction (Mangold et al., 1998).

Both blood and tick samples were individually tested for Coxiella spp. Initially, the DNA samples were subjected to real-time PCR (7500 Real Time PCR Systems apparatus - Applied Bio Systems, Foster City, CA, USA) to amplify a 70 bp fragment of the transposase elements gene (IS1111), using the primers: IS1111F and IS1111R and an internal fluorogenic probe, as described (Schneeberger et al., 2010). A Cycle threshold (CT) value of <40.0 was considered positive and samples testing positive by real-time PCR were further analyzed using four conventional polymerase chain reaction (PCR) assays. The first one used the primer pair Trans1 and Trans2, targeting a 687 bp fragment of the transposase elements gene (IS1111), as described by Willems et al. (1994). The second PCR assay used the primer pair CAPI844F and CAPI844R, targeting a 557 bp fragment of the capsular polysaccharide biosynthesis protein gene (CAP) according to Reeves et al. (2006). The third assay used primers COX-CbF and COX-CbR, targeting a 246 bp fragment of the isocitrate dehydrogenase gene (icd), adapted from the qPCR protocol by Fernández-Carrillo et al. (2023). The fourth assay used primers 16S rDNAF and 16S rDNAR, targeting a 325 bp fragment of the 16S rDNA gene of an Amblyomma americanum-Coxiella endosymbiont (AY939824) (Karounos et al., 2025). All PCR reactions included positive controls (DNA from C. burnetii strain At12 cultured in cell culture) and ultrapure water type I was used as the negative control. Amplified products by conventional PCR were analyzed by electrophoresis on 1.5% agarose gels stained with SYBR Safe DNA Gel Stain (Life Technologies, Grand Island, NY, USA) and visualized under UV transillumination. All PCR products of the expected size were purified with ExoSap (USB, Cleveland, OH, USA) and sequenced (bidirectional Sanger sequencing) on an automated ABI sequencer (Applied Biosystems/Thermo Fisher Scientific, ABI 3500 Genetic Analyzer, Foster City, CA, USA) using the same primers as in the PCR. The resulting sequences were compared to one another and submitted to BLAST analysis (www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/blast) to determine the closest matches available in GenBank.

Results

Blood samples were collected from a total of 31 domestic animals on the farm, being 2 dogs, 2 horses, 5 cattle, and 22 goats. IFA analysis of the sera samples from these animals revealed that 15.5% (5/31) showed reactivity (titer ≥64) to at least one of the two strains of C. burnetii; 84.5% (26/31) of the animals showed no reactivity to C. burnetii (Fig. 3).

One serum sample from an adult female goat presented an endpoint titer of 1024 against the Nine Mile strain and 256 against the At12 strain, indicating a phase II reaction. Both dog samples showed similar endpoint titers for both strains, making it impossible to determine the most likely infection phase. Through molecular analyses, all blood samples collected from the animals yielded amplicon sizes within the expected range for the endogenous cytb gene, thereby confirming successful DNA extractions. Subsequently, the samples were subjected to PCRs, all of which returned negative results.

A total of 46 ticks were collected on the farm, being 34 Rhipicephalus microplus (2 males, 17 females, 15 nymphs) from cattle, 1 R. microplus female from horse, and 11 Dermacentor nitens (1 male, 10 females) from horses. All these 46 ticks were submitted to DNA extraction, and when their DNA samples were tested by PCR targeting the tick mitochondrial 16S rRNA gene; two D. nitens females were negative and were excluded from further analysis. Among the 35 DNA samples from R. microplus ticks tested by real-time PCR, 24 (68.6%) were positive, with CT values ranging from 33.26 to 37.38. These 24 positive samples (1 male, 12 females, 11 nymphs) were subjected to a panel of conventional PCRs, and amplification was observed exclusively in the PCR targeting the Coxiella 16S rRNA gene, in which 100% of the 24 samples yielded positive results. The partial 16S rRNA gene sequences obtained from these samples were identical to each other and 100% identical to a sequence of Coxiella-like endosymbiont of R. microplus from GenBank (LC635184). All tested D. nitens samples were negative.

Discussion

Based on a case of C. burnetii-caused endocarditis recently diagnosed in a farmer of the Amazon region of Brazil, we performed a field epidemiological study in the farm where the patient used to live in close contact with domestic animals. Serological analyses indicated 15.5% seroreactive animals to C. burnetii, with endpoint titers ranging from 64 to 1024. Low titers obtained in the IFA, such as those observed in two goats (64 and 128), should be interpreted with caution, as they may represent cross-reactions with other intracellular pathogens. Studies in humans have shown that low-titer cross-reactivity in patients with Q fever endocarditis ranged from 41% to 50% with Bartonella henselae antigens (La Scola and Raoult, 1996; Shapira et al., 2021). There are also studies showing cross-reactions with Chlamydia pneumoniae (Lukácová et al., 1999) and Legionella pneumophila (Edouard et al., 2017). Therefore, for greater specificity in the serological diagnosis of C. burnetii it is recommended to consider high titers (generally ≥256). In our study, only one adult goat showed a titer of 1024 for the Nine Mile strain (Phase II), suggesting recent exposure or active infection in the farm. Although there are no studies demonstrating the circulation of C. burnetii in the Amazon region of Brazil, Guimarães et al. (2017) and Souza et al. (2018) reported serological evidence for the circulation of the bacterium among livestock (goats and sheep) in two areas of the Caatinga biome in the northeastern region of Brazil.

Dogs, due to their close contact with humans and other domestic animals, as well as their high mobility, have been identified as important sentinels for the circulation of C. burnetii in rural and peri-urban environments. Studies using IFA in countries such as Chile, Portugal, and Australia showed quite different seroprevalence: 0% positivity in Chile (Di Cataldo et al., 2022), 12.6% to 1.7% over time in Portugal (Anastácio et al., 2022), and 6.5% in dogs from Aboriginal communities in Australia (Shapiro et al., 2016). In Brazil, although serological surveys in dogs are scarce, there is growing interest in including dogs in multi-species serosurveys. Although there are no studies in the northern region of the country, in the state of Rio de Janeiro (southeast region) 14.3% of dogs showed seropositivity at the cutoff point (1:64) (Mares-Guia et al., 2014). In the southern region, seropositivity to C. burnetii ranged from 30% in working dogs to 0.69% in dogs from Indigenous communities (França et al., 2024a, França et al., 2024b, França et al., 2024c; Farinhas et al., 2024). In the present study, both dogs were seropositive with similar endpoint titers (less than four-fold different) for both strains of C. burnetii, precluding determination of the infection stage of these animals. All cattle analyzed were seronegative. Studies in other regions of Brazil have detected cattle seroprevalence ranging from 13% to 23.8% (Mioni et al., 2020; Ramos et al., 2020). In this study, the sample size was small, with only six subsistence cattle.

Both tested horses were also seronegative. A systematic review estimated a global average seroprevalence of 15.8% in horses, comparable to cattle (Marenzoni et al., 2013), and ranging from 4% to 12% in a hyperendemic Q fever area in southern France (Desjardins et al., 2018). As with the cattle, our small sample is not representative of the equine population in the region, but it suggests that on this farm, neither the cattle nor the horses were exposed to the bacterium. However, it is worth noting that although a positive antibody titer is evidence of current or past infection with C. burnetii, it is not necessarily indicative of current or past shedding or disease. It is important to highlight that seronegative animals (regardless of the test used) may actively shed the pathogen (Rodolakis et al., 2007).

Although the molecular analyses (PCR) performed on blood were negative for C. burnetii, the absence of detection may be related to intermittent bacteremia and low bacterial loads in asymptomatic populations, requiring multiple samplings and highly sensitive methods, since serology alone is sensitive for detecting exposure but does not discriminate between bacterial load/bacteremia. A German study observed that in cattle, there are variable shedding patterns, including persistent, intermittent, and sporadic C. burnetii shedding profiles, even in seropositive animals (Böttcher et al., 2024).

Regarding goats, kidding or abortion is the main time of transmission, with high zoonotic risk due to the high bacterial load facilitating spread to the environment and to other animals or humans. One study showed that bacteria were shed by some goats for nearly four months following an outbreak and also during the two successive parturitions (Berri et al., 2007). In addition to the problem of long-term shedders, there is the issue of persistent environmental contamination. Our results suggest that although no animals were detected as positive by PCR, we cannot rule out the possibility of animals carrying circulating bacteria below the detection threshold, indicating that animals on the farm, like humans, are exposed to infection.

Regarding the collected ticks, 36 were identified as belonging to the species R. microplus, commonly known as the cattle tick, an ectoparasite widely distributed in tropical and subtropical regions and closely associated with cattle, its preferred hosts (Barros-Battesti et al., 2006). Previous studies have detected the presence of Coxiella-like endosymbionts (CLE) at variable infection levels among R. microplus populations through the Word (Jiao et al., 2021; Machado-Ferreira et al., 2016; Guizzo et al., 2017). Interestingly, it has been shown that CLE is a mutualistic endosymbiont maternally transmitted and present in all life stages of R. microplus, to whom the bacteria provide vitamins and enzyme cofactors essential for the tick metabolism (Guizzo et al., 2017).

In this study, 9 D. nitens ticks were also tested and shown to be free of Coxiella spp. or CLE. This result is in accordance with a previous study in Brazil, in which no Coxiella DNA was detected D. nitens from several other regions of Brazil (Machado-Ferreira et al., 2016). On the other hand, one study in the Colombian Caribbean reported CLE in four D. nitens tick samples (Cotes-Perdomo et al., 2020). Regarding other species of the genus Dermacentor, there are reports of either C. burnetii or CLE in Dermacentor spp. ticks from Europe and Asia (Spitalská and Kocianová, 2003; Špitalská et al., 2018; Jiao et al., 2021).

Although the present study did not identify positive specimens for C. burnetii in ticks, our molecular analyses targeting the IS1111 insertion element indicated the presence of Coxiella sp., confirmed to be CLE by a conventional PCR that generated partial sequences of 16S rRNA gene. Although PCR targeting the IS1111 insertion element is known to be non-specific, capable of amplifying both the pathogenic bacterium and CLE (Duron et al., 2015), there are no studies demonstrating the specificity levels of the other target genes used in this study. Moreover, Angelakis et al. (2016) reported a Coxiella-like organism similar to Rhipicephalus spp. endosymbionts in eschars of patients parasitized by ticks. Therefore, its role as a possible pathogen should not be ruled out.

Although studies in the Amazon Biome are scarce, the presence of at least one goat with high endpoint titers to C. burnetii supports the circulation of the bacterium in the farm where a human case of C. burnetii-caused endocarditis was diagnosed in northern Brazil. Due to the high diversity of wildlife in the Amazon biome, further studies should evaluate their infection by C. burnetii the Brazilian Amazon. For instance, a Q fever outbreak in 2014 in French Guiana implicated capybaras (Hydrochoerus hydrochaeris) as reservoirs active in the wild cycle, shedding bacteria in their feces (Christen et al., 2020). Also, in the Amazon of French Guiana, the three-toed sloth (Bradypus tridactylus) was reported as a potential reservoir of C. burnetii (Gardon et al., 2001).

Although scarce, scattered, and highly heterogeneous, studies published in other Latin American countries have found molecular evidence of C. burnetii in wild animals from the orders Pilosa, Cingulata, Artiodactyla, Perissodactyla, and Rodentia (Epelboin et al., 2023). Recent studies in Brazil present serological evidence in rodents and marsupials collected in areas where dogs were also seropositive, suggesting that wild and peridomestic cycles may be connected by rodents and other wild mammals (Oliveira et al., 2020). This scenario has also been observed in other regions where peridomestic cycles have significant impacts on Q fever cases rather than wild cycles (Bolaños-Rivero et al., 2017).

Bats have also been investigated as potential wildlife hosts of C. burnetii. In northern Brazil, Amazonas, Pará, Roraima, and Amapá states, molecular screening of spleen samples from hematophagous bats did not detect C. burnetii DNA (De Mello et al., 2024). In contrast, 6.3% of insectivorous and frugivorous bats captured in a cave in Colombia tested positive, representing the first molecular evidence of the bacterium in the Colombian Amazon (Silva-Ramos et al., 2022). In Queensland, Australia, in 2014, C. burnetii DNA was found in bat urine pools from a Pteropus frugivorous bat (Pteropodidae) (Tozer et al., 2014). These findings in non-hematophagous bats suggest that bats may act as reservoirs or spreaders of the bacterium in wild environments, potentially infecting sympatric animals.

This study presents the epidemiological investigation associated with the first known human clinical case of Q fever in the Amazon region of Brazil. It is noteworthy that during tick collection, only one-host tick species was obtained from cattle and horses, with no samples from dogs, goats, or the environment. In addition, no prior information was available regarding the animals present on the farm, such as their origin, length of residence, records of deaths or births, or the presence of clinical signs, such as abortions. One of the main limitations of this study is that only blood samples were available for molecular analyses. Indeed, additional samples, such as milk and vaginal swabs, could have contributed to a more comprehensive investigation of potential environmental sources of infection. Previous studies have demonstrated that bacteremia is transient and associated with the early phase of infection; following the acute phase, there is a significant decline in the detection of C. burnetii in blood by PCR (Schneeberger et al., 2010; Bae et al., 2019). In contrast, infected animals may intermittently shed the bacterium in milk when it persists in specific tissues, such as the mammary gland (Bauer et al., 2024). Furthermore, Joulié et al. (2017) demonstrated that seropositivity and vaginal shedding are not clearly associated, and seronegative animals may continue to excrete C. burnetii in vaginal mucus at levels detectable by PCR from vaginal swabs.

Conclusion

Based on the first known human case of Q fever in the Brazilian Amazon region, an epidemiological investigation in the farm where the Q fever patient used to live revealed seroreactivity of dogs and goats to C. burnetii, with the indication of recent exposure of at least one goat to C. burnetii. These results suggest that small ruminants could be maintained the enzootic cycle of C. burnetii on the farm. This study was the first to conduct serological and molecular analyses targeting C. burnetii in dogs, cattle, horses, goats, and their ticks in the Brazilian Amazon region. Further studies are needed to better understand the role of wild animals and their ticks in the transmission of the bacterium to peri-urban cycles, as well as to clarify the true distribution of this important zoonosis in northern Brazil, with direct implications for public health.
