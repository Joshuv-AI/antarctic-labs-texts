HPV vaccine uptake in Pakistan: A qualitative study to identify barriers and facilitators before the first HPV national rollout
Authors: Khola Noreen, Samina Naeem Khalid, Mehreen Noor, Saba Maryam, Abdul Momin Rizwan Ahmad, Nilab Mohammadi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAlthough vaccines remain a mainstay tool for the prevention of infectious diseases, their efficacy, safety, and trust among the population remain controversial. For an effective rollout of the HPV vaccine in Pakistan, financial constraints, health system fragility, and complex cultural values should be kept in mind, and these should be apprehended as both facilitators and barriers, since these findings will help policymakers to design effective interventions and improve public trust. To explore barriers and motivators to HPV vaccine uptake in Pakistan, the study used a descriptive qualitative study design. It was conducted from February 2025 to August 2025 across districts of Northern Punjab and Southern Punjab. Inclusion of diverse stakeholders such as community workers/religious scholars, healthcare providers, teachers/school administrators, adolescent girls, and their parents/guardians allowed us a better understanding of multilevel influencers on HPV vaccine acceptance. Barriers to HPV vaccination were summarized under BeSD framework, including fear of side effects, vaccine skepticism, misinformation via social media, religious perception, restricted social and peer norms, fear-based motivation, long waiting time, service dissatisfaction, patriarchal decision-making, and community resistance. Conversely, facilitators included recognition of vaccine need and benefits, supportive family and community norms, health worker counseling, public health advocacy, trust in school authorities, religious belief, self-determination, effective outreach and door-to-door services, and gender-neutral vaccine acceptance. The findings underscore the need for culturally sensitive, trust-building strategies that go beyond a clinical approach to effectively strengthen equitable HPV vaccine uptake, improved vaccine confidence, and ultimately reduced HPV-related diseases.KEYWORDS: Papillomavirus vaccine, qualitative research, cervical cancer prevention, HPV vaccines, Pakistan

Introduction

Vaccines are one of the most practical strategies in the prevention of infectious diseases, but their use has frequently been affected by safety issues, efficacy, and mistrust.1 Pakistan is not an exception, as it encountered severe problems with vaccine hesitancy during polio and COVID-19 vaccination campaigns. The WHO’s Strategic Advisory Group of Experts (SAGE) established a Working Group on Vaccine Hesitancy in 2012, defining it as the “delay in acceptance or refusal of vaccines despite availability of vaccination services.”2 and recognizing it as a major driver of under-vaccination, especially in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs).3 Vaccine refusal is shaped by a complex interplay of social, cultural, religious, and epidemiological factors.4 In Pakistan, the problem of vaccine hesitancy has been a constant liability to immunization efforts because it is a multifaceted challenge of social, cultural, religious, and epidemiological nature, not to mention the presence of vaccine refusal. In high-risk communities, refusal rates to have routine childhood vaccines have reached 41%, driven by myths related to vaccines, mistrust in government, logistical challenges, and widespread fears of side effects.4 Culturally embedded myths asserting that vaccines contain haram ingredients, cause infertility, or are part of a foreign plot frequently originate from misinformation circulated via religious networks and social media.5 Similarly, during the COVID-19 pandemic, 52% of adults expressed an immediate intention to have a vaccine, but misinformation shared through religious networks and social media is often the cause of their reluctance.6,7 Lack of trust in the healthcare system, which was seen as corrupt or unconcerned, undermined acceptance further. Regarding Pakistan, these barriers cannot be overcome by supply alone and need to be mandated; they should be overcome by reestablishing community trust through culturally attentive dialogue and using avenues of influence that are trusted.8

Another vaccine-preventable pressing public health issue is cervical cancer, which is an increasing health problem in society. It is estimated to be the fourth most prevalent cancer in women worldwide, with 662,301 new cases and 348,874 deaths reported in 2022.9 More than 90% of these cases occur in LMICs, especially in South and Southeast Asia, where organized screening and HPV vaccination programs are absent, contributing to relatively high incidence and mortality.10–12 Another contributing factor is socioeconomic disparities; women living in countries with high HDI have almost 50% higher 5-y survival than in low‑HDI conditions.13,14 In Asia alone, 397,082 new cases were reported in 2022, and the number of new cases is projected to increase to over half a million by 2045.15 Even though it is one of the most preventable cancers, the survival outcomes are still poor in resource-limited settings, therefore demonstrating the urgency of effective prevention strategies.16

Organized prevention programs, including cytology-based screening and, more recently, HPV vaccination, have resulted in significant incidence and mortality reductions in countries.17 Bhutan, Maldives, and Sri Lanka have integrated HPV vaccination into national immunization schedules with encouraging uptake rates of 74%, 41%, and 50%, respectively.18–20 India has been undergoing phased demonstration projects since 2009,17,21,22 while Bangladesh and Nepal are at the national rollout stage.23–25 Pakistan and Afghanistan have fallen behind because of barriers in their sociocultural, political, and health systems.26–28

The paradigm shift is now underway, and Pakistan has entered a new era of cervical cancer prevention with the recent launch of HPV vaccination. A major milestone has been achieved with a country-wide National HPV vaccination campaign from 15–27 September 2025, with first-round activities in Punjab, Sindh, Islamabad, and Azad Jammu and Kashmir. Supported by the WHO and Gavi, the program targeted 13 million girls aged 9–14 y for a single-dose HPV vaccination, making Pakistan the 150th country to introduce the HPV vaccine into its national immunization program.29 Over 49,000 vaccinators, social mobilizers, and data staff are being trained for rollout, with school- and community-based delivery strategies. Future expansion to Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, Baluchistan, and Gilgit-Baltistan is planned by 2027,30 with the eventual goal of being included in the regular National Immunization Program to eventually achieve 90% coverage by the age of 15 by 2030. WHO31

The new evidence highlights the existence of multifactorial barriers to HPV vaccination in South Asia. The usual barriers seen are a lack of awareness of HPV and cervical cancer, the perceived cost of vaccines, distrust in new vaccines, parental fear, and socioeconomic status (especially the role of fathers and extended family in health-related choices).24 Facilitators include government-led vaccination programs, school-based campaigns, healthcare professional recommendations, and increased community awareness.32 The same patterns have been seen with other vaccines like COVID-19, with parental education, socioeconomic status, and trust in government-led programs playing significant roles in uptake.33

The combination of economic inequalities, poor health infrastructure, and ingrained cultural values makes HPV vaccine uptake especially difficult in Pakistan, so the identification of barriers and facilitators of HPV vaccine uptake is of special significance for designing effective interventions to better uptake of HPV vaccines and help to achieve global elimination objectives. It is in this background that the current qualitative research examines the obstacles and enablers of HPV vaccine uptake in Pakistan. This research will develop insights that will help in policy formulation, enhance implementation plans, and eventually alleviate the cervical cancer burden in Pakistan by getting perspectives based on sociocultural dynamics, limitations of the health system, and community beliefs.

Methodology

Study context and time frame relative to nationwide HPV vaccine introduction

National introduction of HPV vaccine involved a series of preparatory phases. Following the approval of the proposal in June 2024, Gavi’s Independent Review Committee delegation visited on 26 August 2024 to assess the preparation, and final approval for vaccine rollout was on 26 November 2024. During the third National Immunization Technical Advisory Group (NITAG) meeting on 8 August 2025, national readiness was assessed, followed by programmatic and operational preparedness assessment during the second National Interagency Coordination Committee (NICC) meeting on 15 August 2025. The nationwide HPV vaccination campaign started on 15 September 2025 in Punjab, Sindh, and Azad Jammu and Kashmir. The HPV vaccination campaign was planned to end on 27 September 2025. However, it was extended by 3 d, from 29 September to 1 October 2025, and implemented across all targeted provinces.

The current study was conducted during pre-implementation phases, and all qualitative interviews were completed before the launch of the nationwide HPV vaccination campaign on 15 September. Accordingly, participants’ perspectives purely represent baseline behavior and social drivers of vaccination, as at that time they were not influenced by public awareness campaigns, implementation experiences, and vaccine delivery initiatives. This time frame offered a unique opportunity to explore behavior and social drivers of vaccination shaping HPV vaccine uptake before the launch of the national vaccination program.

Conceptual framework

Since there is no universal solution to vaccine hesitancy, it is crucial to employ context-specific strategies. Hence, the study employed a qualitative research approach based on the WHO Behavioral and Social Drivers (BeSD) framework, integrating domains, namely thinking and feeling, social processes, and practical issues influencing vaccine uptake as shown in Figure 1. It provides in-depth analysis of various behavioral and social factors influencing vaccine uptake by broadly classifying them into internal factors influencing motivation to take vaccination, namely thinking and feeling, and social processes, while practical issues encompass the domain influenced by external factors, including logistical and affordability issues.

Behavior and social driver of vaccination framework (BeSD).

Study setting and design

The current study was part of a broader research initiative focused on contextualizing the WHO BeSD Framework to develop a preliminary version of a culturally adapted BeSD-HPV tool to enhance HPV vaccine uptake in Pakistan, to generate context-specific evidence to inform the design of culturally relevant strategies for improving HPV vaccine uptake among adolescents.

The current study adopted a descriptive qualitative design and was conducted between February 2025 and August 2025 across District Rawalpindi and Islamabad Capital Territory in Northern Punjab, and District Bahawalpur in Southern Punjab. The detailed steps of the methodology are shown in Figure 2.

Sample size and sampling strategy

As the study was conducted in the pre-implementation phase before the launch of HPV vaccination in Pakistan, a purposive maximum variation sampling strategy was employed to recruit the participant that can provide rich information regarding barriers and facilitators related to behavior and social drivers of vaccination uptake. A maximum variation purposive sampling strategy was used when the researcher wants to ensure that the recruited sample includes a range of different stakeholders that potentially differ across various dimensions influencing vaccine uptake.

Additionally, locations and participants were purposively selected to ensure that they had firsthand experience with the program’s execution and could offer in-depth insights into the social, behavioral, contextual, and operational factors influencing HPV vaccine uptake. This process also ensured the capture of a wide range of implementation experiences across various administrative, health system, geographic, and sociocultural contexts. Efforts were made to recruit participants of different genders, age groups, sociocultural contexts, educational backgrounds, and urban and rural settings to capture diverse viewpoints and experiences. Participants were sampled to reflect variation in socioeconomic and cultural settings in District Rawalpindi and Islamabad Capital Territory in Northern Punjab, and District Bahawalpur in Southern Punjab. The regions were chosen to indicate disparities in the infrastructure of the health system, community practices, and vaccine hesitancy.

This process also enhanced the findings’ credibility, transferability, and contextual richness. The sample was recruited until theoretical saturation was reached.

The sample was formulated in such a way that it has a broad representation of the stakeholders: the adolescent girls (aged 9–17 y), parents/guardians of adolescent girls, health care providers (vaccination staff, gynecologists, pediatricians, nurses, and physicians), community health workers (Lady Health Workers, Lady Health Supervisors, Lady Health Visitors), teachers and school administrators, and community leaders and religious scholars belonging to the Ministry of Religious Affairs. This heterogeneous recruitment provided an all-inclusive examination of the impediments as well as promoters at an individual, household, neighborhood, and system level.

Before participation, all individuals were informed about the study objectives, and rapport was established to foster trust and encourage candid responses. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants, and for adolescent participants, consent was obtained from parents/guardians in addition to assent from the adolescents themselves. Recruitment and data collection continued until thematic saturation was reached, defined as the point when no new information emerged to refine or expand the analytical categories.36

Data collection tool

Data were collected through semi-structured interview guides based on WHO BeSD qualitative interview guides.37 To find potential obstacles and enablers of the HPV vaccine in the setting of complex hierarchical dynamics, gendered, religious, and societal factors, a desk review of the literature was conducted. Interview guides were then adapted to the local context.

To ensure contextual relevance, the study guides were adapted through a broader doctoral research project aimed at adapting the WHO BeSD framework in the sociocultural context of Pakistan. Special attention was paid to using culturally appropriate language while obtaining data regarding sensitive issues involving sexual and reproductive health, cervical cancer, HPV, and its mode of transmission. This was done to ensure participants’ comfort and understanding. The interview guides were pilot-tested with a representative sample, and minor changes were made to improve clarity and reduce ambiguity.38

Data collection and measurements

Data collection took place between 03 March and 31 August 2025. A trained research assistant coordinated scheduling and was physically present during all interviews and focus group discussions. At the start of each session, the assistant briefed participants on the study’s purpose, provided written consent forms, and managed the process of obtaining informed consent. In addition, the assistant collected demographic information such as age, gender, educational status, occupation, and district of residence, managed the audio-recording of each interview, and documented detailed observational field notes in English and Urdu. All in-depth interviews and focus group discussions with participants were conducted personally by the principal investigator (KN) and the research team members (MN, SM, SNK), which guaranteed the lack of inconsistency and cultural sensitivity during the process of data collection. Forty-five in-depth interviews and four focus groups, which lasted between 37 min and 50 min, were used to gather data until data saturation was reached. No dropouts or refusals occurred. Confidentiality and anonymity were maintained.

Analysis plan

Braun and Clarke’s six-step thematic analysis39 using a combination of inductive and deductive coding approaches in NVivo qualitative analysis software was utilized in this study as shown in Figure 3. Deductive codes were aligned with the BeSD (Behavioral and Social Drivers) framework WHO BeSD.37

Six-step Braun and Clarke thematic analysis.

All interviews and focus group discussions were audio-recorded, transcribed in full, and reviewed for accuracy. A professional transcriptionist prepared the transcripts, which were subsequently verified by the research team. The transcripts were uploaded into NVivo to facilitate coding and qualitative data management. The members of the research group participated in the analysis (KN, MN, SM, SNK). To begin with, a subset of the transcripts was coded by two researchers independently to generate a preliminary codebook that represented deductive codes (BeSD domains: thinking, feeling, social processes, motivation, and practical issues) and emergent inductive themes. The coder and the principal investigator (KN) regularly meet to discuss how the coding process will proceed, differences, and the limits of the definition of codes and themes to agree on the definition limits.

Thematic saturation

Data analysis was conducted in parallel with the data collection process using an iterative process to facilitate continuous refinement of the coding framework and enhancement of thematic saturation. Two researchers, KN and SN, independently coded transcripts using NVivo software. Braun and Clarke’s six step process was used for the thematic analysis approach. A preliminary code book was developed inductively based on initial interviews and constantly refined throughout the process using constant comparison. Regular meetings were done through put the process to discuss discrepancies until consensus was achieved. The senior qualitative researcher, AMRA, discussed discrepancies and resolved them after discussion during regular review meetings. The record of the entire process was maintained as code book revisions, reflexive memos, and analytic decisions. All emerging conflicting viewpoints were documented in the audit trail. After every focus group discussion and in-depth interview, a saturation log was maintained and considered to be achieved when no new codes emerge, and emerging themes and subthemes sufficiently represent research objectives. Saturation was assessed within each stakeholder category separately and also across all groups to avoid duplication and ensure comprehensive representation of diverse perspectives of all stakeholders. The rigor and trustworthiness of the process were maintained throughout. During the recruitment phase, it was maintained through enrollment of a diverse group of participants and community representatives, and during the analysis phase, it was maintained through analytical meetings, audit trails, reflexive memos, investigator triangulation, and iterative team discussions.

To achieve methodological rigor, the team continued to revise and refine the coding framework as new data were being analyzed. The barriers and facilitators to HPV vaccine uptake were identified through thematic clustering, and the findings interpreted through the available literature on the local cultural and contextual dynamics. The point of data saturation was reached, at which point no new themes or information were obtained in subsequent transcripts. All subjects in the direct quotes reporting were given pseudonyms to maintain confidentiality.

All these measures led to the rigor of the methodology of the research and the strength of the authenticity, reliability, and relevance of the results to HPV vaccine hesitancy and acceptance in the Pakistani context. The research followed the Consolidated Criteria of Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) checklist to ensure that the study methodology was rigorous. The 32-item COREQ checklist was created to enhance the comprehensive reporting of qualitative inquiries, covering such areas as study design (theoretical framework, selection of participants, setting, and data collection), the reflexivity of the research team, analysis, and findings.40

Researcher reflexivity and positionality

This research study was conducted by a multidisciplinary research team involving researchers from diverse backgrounds, including Public Health (SNK), (SM), (MN), Medical Education (KN), Implementation Science (AMRA), and Behavior Scientist (NM). All interviewers of team posses experience in conducting qualitative research interviews and focus group discussion sessions, specifically in the field of HPV research. Before the start of this project, researchers were already part of a broader team working on a doctoral research project investigating the social drivers of HPV vaccination in the sociocultural context of Pakistan using a grounded theory-based approach, which has been submitted at Dove press Journal of Taylor & Francis Group and is currently under the process of review. As part of a broader research project, the research team had already received training in qualitative research methods and had substantial experience in coding, transcript review, and thematic analysis. This experience significantly enhanced the consistency in data collection and ensured adherence to principles of qualitative research while maintaining cultural sensitivity in the diverse sociocultural context of Pakistan. At the same time, recognizing the fact that prior experience and professional background as a Public Health expert, Physician, Implementation Science expert, Behavior Scientist, and Medical Educationist could influence their analytical decision-making. Hence, a reflexive approach was adopted throughout the study. Pre-data collection meetings and debriefing sessions were scheduled before every session to critically reflect on prior knowledge, HPV vaccine experience, assumptions about vaccine hesitancy, communication perceptions, and cultural expectations. These meetings aimed to minimize the influence on participants’ interaction and data interpretation. These deliberations helped researchers to bracket preconceived ideas and remain open to unexpected findings.

Results

Barriers and facilitators to HPV vaccination were categorized under the BeSD framework as shown in Figure 4. Within Thinking and Feeling, participants reported fear of side effects, religious perceptions, skepticism toward vaccines, speculation over vaccine components, and negative social media influence. In Social Processes, barriers included restrictive norms set by religious leaders, peer influences discouraging uptake, and an anti-government stance. Motivation was affected by basing the message on fear and the view of vaccination as a governmental imposition. Practical Issues also had a role to play, as there was restricted access to vaccination facilities, travel-related difficulties, service dissatisfaction, and long waiting times were identified. Finally, Cultural Integration barriers included a patriarchal decision-making process that restricted women’s independence and resistance to vaccination programs within a community.

Qualitative systems mapping-linkages between behavior and social barriers and facilitators to HPV vaccine uptake.

On the other hand, participants reported perceived benefits of vaccination, reassurance from doctors and nurses, consistent vaccine availability, paternal support as decision-makers, and inspirational stories from other people as the major facilitators for the vaccine uptake.

Discussion

The basis of analysis was the WHO Behavioral and Social Drivers (BeSD) framework that has four domains, i.e. Thinking and Feeling, Social Pressure, Motivation and Practical Issues.37 After applying this comprehensive framework, the discussion moves beyond just numbering of findings to deep insights into the interconnected ecosystem that governs human behavior and health. Our main objective was to look deep inside how a barrier in one domain can influence or create a hindrance in another and a facilitator in one domain can cause a ripple effect in other domains. This report’s findings express lived experiences of vast groups of people such as teachers, healthcare workers, parents and adolescents.

The emotional and cognitive perspective circling acceptance of the vaccine is the center of qualitative analysis, marked by a mixture of fear and trust. A prominent barrier was fear of side effects of the vaccine, such as infertility, early puberty, hormonal imbalance, or obesity, opinions that echo past bad experiences such as polio vaccination.41 This mirrors HPV vaccine hesitancy observed in other LMICs, such as India and Kenya, where rumors of sterilization severely derailed early demonstration projects.42,43 However, the Pakistani context is uniquely complicated by the historical baggage of the polio vaccination legacy, transforming this misunderstanding from a clinical knowledge deficit into a deeply ingrained emotional barrier.

On the other hand, we explored a deeply strong belief that vaccination is necessary for protection against diseases. Many respondents expressed and recognized that vaccination is crucial for their daughter’s health. Both parents and teachers recognized the protective effects of vaccines against diseases such as cervical cancer. This demonstrates a complex interplay of emotions where people fear the side effects of vaccines while acknowledging their benefits.

Hesitancy to vaccination is merely an isolated phenomenon but deeply rooted in complex behaviors transformed through past experiences and geographical backgrounds.44 Past negative experiences, real or perceived, from pandemic COVID−19 effects to acceptance of HPV vaccination, shape this hesitancy. The observation that those who received the COVID-19 vaccine still experienced severe illness or death during the pandemic ignited public skepticism and questioned vaccine efficacy in general. This shows that a new program cannot be started with a clean slate but has to address historical backgrounds and the emotional residue of previous campaigns. This is further aggravated by mistrust toward developed countries; people from developing countries thought of themselves as “testing grounds.” This is exacerbated by mistrust of the quality of vaccines or drugs available in the market, prompting some to wish for verification of ingredients and manufacturing details. This suspicion of being treated as a “testing ground” by developed countries aligns with post-colonial medical mistrust frequently documented across Sub-Saharan African LMICs,45 though in Pakistan, it is distinctly amplified by recent geopolitical events and post-pandemic era skepticism.

The spread of misinformation and mythology in the modern world creates a powerful emotional and psychological barrier. Social media is thought of as a channel for “propaganda, fear, rumor & fearmongering” rather than a correct source of information.46 To be mentioned here, rumors such as the presence of culturally or religiously inappropriate substances, such as pig-gelatin or donkey-derived ingredients, are widespread, further causing suspicion. While conspiracy theories regarding population control are a ubiquitous barrier in global HPV literature, concerns regarding halal compliance are uniquely potent in Islamic contexts like Pakistan. These misconceptions, along with conspiracy theories like “vaccination is to control population,” cause further mistrust toward vaccination and are closely linked to anxiety that cannot be controlled with information alone.

The fear of infertility is a major barrier toward vaccination against HPV. In many cultures, a woman’s value and identity are closely linked to her ability to bear children and maintain family lineage.47 A threat to this value, whether a drug or a vaccine, is a direct threat to her social standing, marriage prospects, and future security. Similar to findings in conservative LMICs across South Asia,48,49 this issue necessitates campaigns specifically designed to decouple HPV vaccination from fertility concerns.

The decision of vaccination is not merely a personal choice but a complex interplay of emotions, family members, and community gatekeepers. Our interpretation highlights the role of trustworthy messengers in the acceptance or rejection of vaccination. Lady health workers can play a pivotal role in the acceptance of the vaccine as they are seen as a trusted messengers through their rapport-building with the community. Teachers and religious scholars, through their educational and religious platforms, respectively, can educate many people in the community. They can influence caregivers to vaccinate daughters by addressing misconceptions and endorsing vaccination. In Pakistan’s context, bypassing these community gatekeepers can derail implementation. However, if religious scholars have ambiguity about vaccination, it causes hindrance toward acceptations because many people believe on scholar’s more than medical advice.

Vaccine influence can be positively influenced by family and community stakeholders. The data show that culture, family values, and positive decision-making can strengthen vaccine acceptance. However, patriarchal structures where male in-laws make decisions about health act as a major barrier, frequently limiting mothers’ willingness to vaccinate their daughters. This contrasts with some LMICs where maternal autonomy in pediatric healthcare is higher.32,50 Peer norms can also play a major role in settings with low vaccine uptake; reluctance can increase hesitancy, normalizing non-vaccination. When trusted messengers do not endorse vaccination, it can create a vacuum that is duly filled with misinformation. So, social processes should be properly addressed to combat these challenges.

Social proof and lived experience can be powerful facilitators. When a hesitant person sees an educated person receiving the vaccine, it causes a desire in him to get vaccinated as well. The ripple effect of community awareness is also a strong motivator. Lived experience and stories of cancer survivors act as powerful motivators that mere statistics can’t. This indicates that trust is not a monolithic concept. These trust in doctors’ capabilities, relational trust in community health workers, and institutional trust in schools as an authentic source of information are all important. A successful strategy is leveraging these trusts in relevant contexts and making use of most of it.

The motivation for vaccine uptake is a nuanced and often paradoxical domain. The analysis shows a paradox between short-term fear-based compliance and long‑term self-determined seeking behavior. The COVID-19 pandemic serves as a case study where people take vaccination in fear of illness or death, which leads to a surge in vaccination.51 This kind of emotional urgency is usually short-lived and may lead to future noncompliance or regret. This highlights a public health dilemma: fear-based messaging that leads to short-term compliance but in the long run, a lack of trust in medical advice.

The role of government mandates also emerged as a significant driver of action. Many individuals took vaccine of COVID-19, when government made it compulsory for travel or entering an examination hall through law enforcement. This clearly indicates that when compulsion is made through law, it increased the vaccination surge. This reliance on external enforcement through laws reflects the need to shift strategy toward making internal motivation and understanding individual behavior.

On the other hand, a sense of responsibility toward family is also a strong motivator. The head of the family takes responsibility for the health and wellness of all individuals. He also wants to protect his family from diseases. In addition, it is also a religious obligation to protect family from diseases. Illness is not a divine decree.

Logistic and systemic challenges often cause hindrance to compliance with vaccination. Hidden charges from transportation, lack of sufficient facilities in rural areas, inadequate vaccination and time off work are major obstacles which represent systemic barriers typical of fragmented LMIC health systems.

The quality of service and timeliness at vaccination centers is also crucial for an effective campaign. Unsupportive staff, inadequate vaccination, and poor service at vaccination cause a lack of trust in individuals. These untrusted individuals can share their bad experiences with other peers, which causes a sense of mistrust in vaccination campaigns across various groups of people. This means that a successful vaccine rollout must be highlighted as a holistic service where the quality of patient experience is as important as vaccination itself.

On the other hand, innovative and accessible delivery models can increase vaccine uptake. For the ongoing national HPV vaccination rollout in Pakistan, leveraging these models is paramount. Door-to-door campaigns for vaccination build trust in the health system through building rapport and interaction. Similarly, campaigns in schools are also visualized positively because these initiatives use institutional trust placed in schools and ease of reaching many adolescents in a similar setting. These successes demonstrate that overcoming logistical challenges during the national rollout will require a transition from passive clinical delivery to active, community-centered solutions.

One cultural barrier is the silence and stigma associated with the female reproductive system. It is considered taboo to even talk about the health of the female reproductive system, especially with male family members, and this silence creates a lack of awareness and informed decision-making. Our analysis interprets this barrier as fundamentally rooted in cultural rather than religious norms; therefore, reframing the discussion within a culturally appropriate context can be a positive step forward.

The nature of HPV vaccination also causes questions and suspicions, with some explaining it as gender bias.52 Many individuals raised questions about the nature of this vaccine due to a lack of trust, misinformation, and propaganda associated with female reproductive health. People often perceive male and female health as separate entities, and female intervention as thought with suspicion and doubt. Therefore, efforts should be made to address these misconceptions and add this vaccination as a part of preventive efforts in childhood immunization.

Based on this critical interpretation, explicitly informing Pakistan’s upcoming national HPV rollout requires a multi-pronged implementation strategy. Campaigns must be conducted to effectively address misconceptions such as infertility or fear of side effects. The Expanded Program on Immunization (EPI) should hold training sessions to educate teachers, religious scholars, and health workers to deliver accurate information. Door-to-door campaigns or school-based campaigns should be used to communicate and educate people through rapport building, overcoming logistical barriers. Motivation should be made in such a way as to make people’s innate desire to vaccinate by pointing out the long-term health benefits of it. Campaigns must include male family members, acknowledging their role in decision-making and empowering them to make correct and positive decisions about their family.

A program for sensitive intervention like HPV requires a holistic and nuanced approach. The BeSD framework guided qualitative analysis and reveals that vaccine acceptance is not merely shaped by a single factor but by a complex interplay of different factors such as emotional beliefs, social dynamics, practical dynamics, and cultural norms. By prioritizing culturally sensitive communication, engaging trusted messengers, and implementing innovative delivery models, public health programs can effectively build trust, leading to more robust and equitable vaccine uptake.

Conclusion

A program for a sensitive intervention like the HPV vaccine requires a holistic and nuanced approach. This qualitative analysis, guided by the BeSD framework, reveals that vaccine acceptance is not shaped by a single factor but by the complex interplay of emotional beliefs, social dynamics, practical logistics, and deeply embedded cultural norms. The findings highlight the critical importance of moving beyond a purely clinical or informational approach to one that actively engages with and addresses the lived realities, fears, and social structures of communities. By prioritizing culturally sensitive communication, engaging trusted messengers, and implementing innovative, accessible delivery models, public health programs can effectively build sustainable trust, which is crucial for supporting a more robust and equitable vaccine uptake.
