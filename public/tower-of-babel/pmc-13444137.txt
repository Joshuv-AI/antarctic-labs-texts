Role of woody plants in carbon sequestration: evidence from Sulula Mofa Forest, Northern Ethiopia
Authors: Hussen Yimer, Gonfa Kewessa, Siraj Mammo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Forests are vital for maintaining the global carbon balance and mitigating climate change by sequestering CO2 and storing organic carbon, contributing to sustainable development. However, Ethiopia lacks national-level carbon inventories, monitoring systems, and databanks to enhance carbon sequestration. This study assessed the carbon stock potential along altitudinal, slope, and aspect gradients in the Sulula Mofa Dry Afro-Montane Forest. Using stratified random sampling, 42 plots (400 m² each) were established. A total of 210 subplots (1 m × 1 m; 1 m²) were established within the main plots for soil and litter sampling. Equal amounts of soil and litter materials were collected from the subplots and separately homogenized to form composite samples of approximately 100 g each. Tree biomass was estimated using nondestructive allometric models, while organic carbon in litter and soil was analyzed in the laboratory. The results indicate that the Sulula Mofa Forest has substantial potential for carbon storage and climate change mitigation. The total carbon stock of the forest was estimated at 933.9 t ha⁻¹, corresponding to a CO₂ sequestration potential of 3,427.43 t ha⁻¹. Among the carbon pools, the tree biomass pool contributed the largest share (658.55 t ha⁻¹), followed by the soil pool (188.02 t ha⁻¹) and litter pool (87.33 t ha⁻¹), demonstrating the dominant role of woody vegetation in carbon accumulation. A small number of species contributed disproportionately to carbon storage, with 91.15% of the total carbon stock concentrated in ten woody species, particularly Ficus sur, Juniperus procera, Cupressus lusitanica, Acacia abyssinica, and Cordia africana. Spatial variation in carbon stocks was also observed, with higher values recorded in the western (246.09 t ha⁻¹) and southern (195.9 t ha⁻¹) sections, as well as at middle and upper altitudinal ranges. These findings highlight the ecological importance of Sulula Mofa Forest as a significant carbon sink and emphasize the need for effective conservation and sustainable management strategies to maintain and enhance its role in climate change mitigation.

Introduction

Forests are playing a great role in mitigating climate change1,2, serving in soil and water conservation3, providing habitat for terrestrial biological diversity4 and enhancing the livelihood of poor people5–6. Forests contain up to 80% of the total above-ground terrestrial C and 40% of the below-ground C, hence having a critical role in the global Carbon cycle7. Particularly, tropical forests are playing a key role in climate change mitigation by sequestering and storing carbon and soils are potential sinks for elevated CO2 emissions8, accounting for half of the aboveground carbon in the vegetation9,10. Hence, forest ecosystems are playing a key role in both climate change mitigation and adaptation11–12.

Forest carbon stocks are influenced by a variety of biophysical and environmental factors that determine vegetation growth, biomass accumulation, and soil organic carbon dynamics. Among these factors, topographic variables such as slope and aspect play a significant role in shaping the spatial distribution of carbon within forest ecosystems. Slope affects soil depth, erosion rates, water runoff, and nutrient retention, which in turn influence vegetation productivity and soil carbon storage. Similarly, aspect determines the amount of solar radiation received by a site, thereby influencing temperature, moisture availability, and microclimatic conditions that regulate plant growth and organic matter decomposition13–14. In addition to topographic controls, forest carbon stocks are also affected by other ecological and environmental factors such as tree species composition, soil nutrient availability, altitude, climate, disturbance history, and forest management practices, all of which influence biomass production and carbon sequestration potential15. Therefore, understanding the combined effects of these factors is essential for accurately assessing carbon storage and evaluating the role of forest ecosystems in climate change mitigation.

Globally, climate change is one of the greatest problems for human being, causing an ever increasing global warming, which in turn resulted in the loss of biodiversity16. Today, the mitigation of climate change has received the serious attention of resource managers, scientists, NGOs and policymakers1,17.

Ethiopia recognized as is one of the world’s most important biodiversity hotspot areas due to the diverse ecological conditions, along with its diverse flora and fauna18, but the country is suffering from rapid and extensive loss of biodiversity19,20. In Ethiopia, there is a significant loss of forest biodiversity and ecosystem services due to agricultural land expansion; urbanization and overgrazing21. During the last decades, in Ethiopia, forest land has dramatically declined due to population growth and investment followed by deforestation and land use change14,22,23.

Different scholars estimated Africa’s and Ethiopia’s carbon stocks. African forests constitute 21% of the global total carbon stock of forest biomass and can sequester up to 630 kg of carbon per hectare per year24. The estimated forest cover of Ethiopia was 11.40% in 2015 (FRA,25. Ethiopia has one of the largest forest resources in the Horn of Africa and has a good potential in mitigating climate change26, 27. The forest resources of Ethiopia is estimated to store 2.76 billion metric tons of carbon, which is equivalent to 10 billion metric tons of CO2 in the AGB, and the SOC pool in Ethiopia was 14 billion metric tons of C/year, which is about an investment of $150 million per year28. The contribution of Ethiopia to global emissions is estimated to be 0.27% and ranked 163 out of 181 countries, the 22nd most vulnerable country and the 31st least ready country29. Compared to others terrestrial ecosystems a huge amount of aboveground carbon are stored Dry Afromontane forests30.

There is great interest in assessing forest carbon stock31, since it is considered as a global issue and large-scale CO2 emission reductions are required with strict limits on each country to what level carbon is absorbed to mitigate climate change32. Forests are a current focus for action, since they play an important role in mitigating climate change by naturally taking carbon out of the atmosphere through the photosynthesis process33.

Despite Ethiopia’s rich natural vegetation and diverse forest ecosystems, comprehensive data on forest carbon stocks remain limited. Most previous studies on tree biomass and carbon sequestration in Ethiopia have focused on a few species and relatively small diameter ranges, particularly plantation species such as Eucalyptus and Acacia34,35. As a result, there is a significant lack of detailed information on carbon storage potential across natural forests and their ecological gradients. Furthermore, Ethiopia lacks a well-established national carbon inventory system and comprehensive databanks that document carbon accumulation across different forest ecosystems1. This data gap limits the country’s ability to accurately estimate forest carbon stocks and to develop effective climate change mitigation strategies. In particular, the carbon storage potential of many natural forests, including the Sulula Mofa Dry Afromontane Forest, has not yet been systematically studied.

Assessing slope and aspect is essential for understanding spatial variation in carbon stocks within forest ecosystems. Including these topographic variables helps to identify areas with higher carbon sequestration potential and improves the accuracy of forest carbon stock estimation and management planning. Therefore, assessing the carbon stock potential of Sulula Mofa Forest is essential for understanding its contribution to carbon sequestration and climate change mitigation. The present study aims to quantify the total carbon stock of the forest by estimating carbon stored in major ecosystem pools, including aboveground biomass (AGB), belowground biomass (BGB), litter biomass (LB), and soil organic carbon (SOC). In addition, the study examines how environmental variables such as altitude, slope, and aspect influence carbon storage patterns in the forest ecosystem.

Materials and methods

Description of study area

The study was conducted at Dessie-Haik District, South Wollo Zone, North-East part of the Amhara region, Ethiopia (Fig. 1). The District Haik city is found around Lake Haik, 30km away from zone administration, Dessie City, 454km away from the regional capital, Bahir Dar and 431km away from the capital city of Ethiopia, Addis Ababa. It shares immediate borders in the North with Ambassel woreda, in the South with Dessie zuria woreda, in the East with Worebabo Woreda and in the West with Kutaber and Ambassel Woreda (Fig. 1). The Woreda Hayik district has a total area of 44,030 hectares and is subdivided into nineteen rural and five small urban kebeles. The Altitude of the Woreda ranges from 1488 to 2900 m.a.s.l. Seid,36. The woody species of Sulula-Mofa forest is a mixed type of natural and plantation forest starting from the Derg Regime, is found in Kebele 01 and 08 and is approximately positioned at latitude 110 23’07”-110 23’ 53"N and 39067’10” -39 068 ‘80” E longitude.

The study region is characterized by a semi-arid climate with warm-humid summers and cold-dry winters having the minimum temperature in January and the maximum temperature in June37. It has ‘Dega’ (13%); ‘Weina Dega’ (72%) and ‘Kolla’ (15%) agroecological zones. It has ‘Dega’ (13%); ‘Weina Dega’ (72%) and ‘Kolla’ (15%) agroecological zones. It has an average annual rainfall of 1030 mm and an average temperature of 21°c per annum36.

The soils in the Wollo plateau including the Sulula Forest area are reddish brown, gravelly and shallow at higher altitudes, while at lower sites they tend to become dark grey and deep; whereas, soils in the surrounding low plains are vertisol, black soils with characteristic high clay content38.

The study forest is a type of Dry Evergreen Afromontane Montane Forest type which is grouped under a Dry single-dominant Afromontane forest with a canopy of several strata occurring between (2200–2800 m) ranges of altitude usually dominated by Tid/Gatira (Juniperus procera) followed by Weira/Ejersa (Olea europaea subsp. cuspidata), Grar (Acacia abyssinica) and in some parts dominated by Asta (Erica arboreal)39.

The total forest cover of the region is estimated as 12,884 km2, that is, about 8.2% of the total land area. Including bush lands, it is about 21,783km2, which is about 13.85% and the Percentage of forest cover in the South Wollo zone was estimated as Natural forest 0.13%, Woodlands 0.25%, Plantation 0.7%, Riverine 0.02% and the total of zonal forest cover were 1.1%40.

It is the most populous area in the Amhara region with a total population of 152,891. Out of the total land cover, 15,937 ha are used for crop production, 736 ha for grazing, 14,308 ha is forest and bush land, 3800 ha water body and 1000 ha are wasteland. The average land holding per household is estimated at 0.5 ha36.

Data collection and sample size determination

Reconnaissance survey

A reconnaissance survey was conducted to obtain an overview of the study forest and determine the number, position, and length of transects. Sample plot locations were mapped using GIS and AutoCAD, and field navigation was carried out using the WGS84 Mobile Topographer application to record geographic coordinates, altitude (elevation), slope, and aspect of each plot. The center points of all plots were permanently marked, and their GPS coordinates were recorded and later mapped in Google Earth for spatial visualization of the study area. Reference sketches showing distances and bearings from permanent landmarks to the plot centers were also documented. In addition, the Topographer application was used to measure slope degree (azimuth), horizontal distances, and tree heights, while slope correction tables were applied where necessary to ensure accurate measurements.

Sample size determination

The sample size was chosen based on how diverse the tree species were in the forest stand—more diversity means we need to sample more trees. So, figuring out the right sample size took into account the type of vegetation, logistics, available manpower, and costs41. To ensure we had enough data, we first did a reconnaissance survey and collected 93 Universal Transverse Mercator (UTM) points from various spots in the forest using the WGS 84 Mobile Topographer app. We then uploaded this data to Google Earth for spatial planning. The study area was outlined and mapped as a polygon, covering an estimated 3,000,000 m² (or 300 hectares) based on those UTM coordinates.

Next, we divided the forest into 209 potential plots, each measuring 20 m × 20 m (400 m²), with 120 m between the centers of each plot. From these, we randomly picked 42 plots for sampling, which totaled a sampled area of 16,800 m² (1.68 hectares). In each plot, we counted and recorded all the tree and shrub species in a spreadsheet. We also measured environmental factors like altitude, geographic coordinates (latitude and longitude), slope, and aspect using the WGS 84 Mobile Topographer app (GPS) on a Samsung Galaxy A10, which had an accuracy of ≤ 5 m. The study area was classified by slope into three categories: lower (1–1000), middle (101–2500), and higher (251–3430), following the method outlined by Gadow et al.42. We categorized the aspect into eight directions—North, Northeast, Northwest, South, Southeast, Southwest, East, and West—with plot proportions of 11.9%, 4.76%, 4.76%, 19.1%, 4.76%, 11.9%, 19.1%, and 23.82%, respectively.

Vegetation data collection

The major activities of carbon measurement during the field data collection was collecting data for above ground tree biomass, leaf litter biomass, and soil organic carbon measurements. The AGB consists of all living woody plant species above the soil including stems, stumps, branches, bark, seeds and foliage. The DBH at ≥ 1.3 m & the diameter at DBH ≥5 cm were measured in each sampling plot.

All living woody plant species above the soil DBH at ≥ 1.3 m & the diameter at DBH ≥5 cm were measured in each sampling plots. The DBH of trees and shrubs was measured with Tape meter and GPS Garmin (72) was used to navigate and record elevation of the sample plots. The topographic aspects of the sample plots were identified using GPS Garmin (72). Along with these measurements litter biomass samples were collected using plastic bags.

Litter biomass and soil data collection

Litter Biomass (LB) is defined as all dead organic surface material on top of the mineral soil43. A total of 210 small plots with a size of 1 m×1 m (1m2) were established and litter samples were collected from 42 plots. The litter samples were taken from the sub plots of (1m2) along a diagonal from one corner to the other and then the leaf litters within the 1m2 sub plots were collected. 100 g of Litter Biomass (LB) sub sample of fresh weights were sampled from the five sub samples from each quadrant and were mixed homogeneously.

In this study, soil organic carbon (SOC) samples were collected from the field with five sub-plots within each major plot7. The Samples were dug using a core sampler with a diameter of 5 cm and up to a depth 0–30 cm. Mixing of soil samples was done properly by taking an equal amount of soil from each sub plots to make about 100 g of composite sample to make homogeneity.

All the collected litter and soil samples were labeled and taken to Wollo University Chemistry laboratory for analysis. In the laboratory, soils were prepared and oven-dried at 105°c for 24 h to remove the soil moisture to determine the percentage of organic carbon. Finally, the bulk density and soil organic carbon were determined.

Data analysis

Estimation of above and below ground biomass carbon stock

The estimation of biomass carbon stocks in Sulula Mofa Forest was conducted using a nondestructive method based on allometric equations, which establish statistical relationships between easily measurable tree parameters and biomass. Equation (1) was specifically developed and validated for dry evergreen Afromontane forest types, which are ecologically comparable to the study area1,44. The model uses diameter at breast height (DBH), a commonly measured and reliable variable in forest inventories, making it practical and appropriate for field-based biomass estimation without destructive sampling. Moreover, the equation incorporates both linear and quadratic terms of DBH, improving the accuracy of biomass prediction across different tree size classes.

Compared to other general tropical biomass models, Eq. (1) was preferred because it was calibrated for similar vegetation structure and environmental conditions, thereby reducing estimation bias. Many global equations are derived from mixed tropical forests and may overestimate or underestimate biomass when applied to specific forest types such as dry evergreen Afromontane forests. Therefore, the use of this locally adapted allometric equation improves the reliability of above-ground biomass estimates in the study forest. Correlation of the various carbon pools with one another was tested using Pearson’s product-moment correlation coefficient and P value between Carbon pools and environmental gradients of slope and altitude to observe the correlations between AGBC, BGBC, LBC, DWBC and SOC using SPSS23 software48. Differences at the 95% (α = 0.05) confidence interval was used to see the significant differences.

Results and discussion

Estimations of biomass and carbon stock pools of SF

Estimations of AGB, AGC and CO2e

The result of the study revealed that the total above-ground biomass, carbon stored and CO2 equivalents in the study was 1033.02, 516.51 and 1895.6 t/ha respectively. The maximum total biomass analyzed, carbon stored and CO2 captured within in their order of AGB, AGC and CO2 eq estimated by five most important woody species such as Ficus sur (240.13, 120.07 & 440.64), Cupressus lusitanica (96.79, 48.39 & 77.61), Eucalyptus globulus (74.95, 37.48 & 137.54), Juniperus procera (67.04, 33.52 & 123.01) and Acacia abyssinica (47., 23.7 39 & 86.97). Accordingly, the result indicates that 91.15% of AGB, AGC and CO2 equivalents contributed by the following ten woody tree species of the study forest; Ficus sur, Cupressus lusitanica, Eucalyptus globulus, Juniperus procera, Cordia africana, Acacia abyssinica, Erythrina abyssinica, Pterolobium stellatum, Croton macrostachys and Olea europaea subsp cuspidata with their descending order.

The results are similar to the previous research of Afromontane Forests; like the study in Siemen Mountain National Forest51, the average biomass stock recorded in AGB was 576.36 t/ha. The study at Chilimo forest52, reported the AGB for the sampled woody species Juniperus procera contributed 184.62t/ha carbon stock. In comparison, Ficus sur was the top contributor of carbon stock estimated to be 240.13 t/ha in the current study.

As the current study revealed that the maximum carbon stock of above ground biomass, carbon stored and CO2 sequestered were observed with values of AGB, AGC and CO2 equivalents respectively in the five plots 7 (1901.51, 950.75 & 3489.27 t/ha) Plot 42 (1684.86, 3091.73 & 842.43 t/ha), plot 20 (1672.16, 836.08 & 3068.41 t/ha) plot 6 (1598.98, 799.49 & 2934.13 t/ha) and plot 35 (2911.41, 793.3 & 1586.6 t/ha) (Fig. 2).

Estimations of LB, LC and CO2e

The results revealed that the Litter, Herb, and Grass (LHG) carbon pool contributed considerable biomass and carbon storage within the study forest. The mean values of litter biomass (LB), litter carbon (LC), and CO₂ equivalent (CO₂eq) were 3.98 t/ha, 87.33 t/ha, and 320.5 t/ha, respectively. The highest carbon density in the LHG pool was recorded in plot 38, where LB, LC, and CO₂eq were 5.26 t/ha, 122.23 t/ha, and 448.57 t/ha, respectively. In contrast, the lowest values were observed in plot 5, with 1.11 t/ha LB, 25.57 t/ha LC, and 93.83 t/ha CO₂eq. The variation in litter biomass and carbon stock among plots could be attributed to differences in vegetation density, canopy cover, litter production, and decomposition rates. Forest areas with dense canopy cover and high species diversity tend to accumulate more litter biomass due to higher leaf fall and organic matter deposition, which subsequently increases carbon storage in the forest floor.

Similar patterns have been reported in other Afromontane forests of Ethiopia, where litter and herbaceous carbon pools show spatial variability across sampling plots due to environmental and vegetation differences. For example, a study conducted in the Gerba-Dima moist Afromontane forest by reported that litter carbon accounted for a measurable portion of the total forest carbon stock, demonstrating the importance of forest floor biomass in overall ecosystem carbon storage53,54. Likewise, research in the Choke Mountain dry evergreen Afromontane forests found that litter carbon contributed a smaller but significant fraction of total carbon pools, with mean values around 2.69 t/ha, highlighting the role of litter in nutrient cycling and carbon sequestration processes55. Additionally, Studies conducted in dry Afromontane forests of northwestern Ethiopia also reported spatial variation in litter biomass and carbon stock across forest plots due to differences in disturbance level, vegetation density, and decomposition processes56.

The observed differences among plots in the current study may also be influenced by micro-environmental factors such as canopy cover, altitude, and disturbance level, which significantly affect litter accumulation and decomposition. Studies in Ethiopian forests indicate that variations in canopy structure and environmental gradients can lead to significant differences in carbon storage among forest plots57. Therefore, the higher LHG carbon stock observed in some plots of the present study suggests relatively greater litter deposition and lower decomposition rates, while the lower values recorded in other plots may reflect reduced vegetation cover or faster organic matter decomposition. Overall, although the LHG carbon pool generally contributes a smaller proportion compared to aboveground biomass and soil organic carbon, it plays a crucial role in nutrient cycling, soil fertility maintenance, and long-term carbon sequestration in forest ecosystems.

Estimations of total carbon stock in SF

The mean total biomass (TB) stored in the study forest was estimated to be 1329.19 ± 353.64 t/ha (1033.02 ± 277.37, 284.08 ± 76.28, 3.98 & 8.1t/ha) in the order of above ground, below ground, litter and soil carbon pools. Whereas, the total Carbon stored (TC) in the study forest were estimated to be 933.9 ± 176.82t/ha (516.51 ± 138.68, 142.04 ± 38.14, 87.33 & 188.02t/ha) in the order of above ground, below ground, litter and soil carbon pools respectively where the Litters had the lowest.

Accordingly, the current study forest’s CO2 equivalents (TCO2) from the atmosphere for its purpose of making organic carbon by sequestration in the study forest were estimated to 3427.42 ± 648.94 t/ha (1895.6 ± 508.98, 521.29 ± 139.97, 320.5 & 690.04) in the order of Carbon pools of above ground, below ground, litter and soil Carbon pools per 1.68 ha of plot areas.

The total carbon stock estimated for the Sulula Mofa Forest was higher than that reported for Banja Forest by Fentahun et al.58, who recorded a total carbon stock of 639.87 t/ha, with 406.47 t/ha stored in tree biomass, 2.58 t/ha in litter, and 230.82 t/ha in soil pools. The relatively higher carbon stock in Sulula Mofa Forest may be attributed to differences in forest structure, tree density, species composition, and management practices. Similarly, Ayalew,59 reported that in Wof Washa Forest approximately 51% of the carbon stock was stored in above-ground vegetation, 34% in soil, and 15% in litter and below-ground pools. This distribution pattern is generally consistent with the findings of the present study, where the largest proportion of carbon was also stored in the tree biomass pool.

Comparisons with other forest ecosystems in Ethiopia indicate that the carbon stock of Sulula Mofa Forest falls within the range reported for several dry evergreen Afromontane forests. The carbon density and CO2 sequestration potential are comparable to those reported for Simien Mountains National Park51 in terms of tree and litter carbon pools, and are also similar to the total carbon density reported for Humbo Forest60. However, the present findings differ from those reported in woody plant communities of Mount Zequalla Monastery, where the total carbon stock and CO2 sequestration were estimated at 348.91 t/ha and 1280.50 t/ha, respectively61. The variation among these forests may be related to differences in altitude, vegetation composition, forest age, disturbance history, and management regimes. In contrast, the carbon stock contribution and CO 2equivalents in Sulula Mofa Forest differ markedly from those reported for Ethiopian church forests62, likely due to differences in forest size, level of protection, and ecological conditions.

Carbon stock along slope gradient

Topographic features like slope gradient and aspect are crucial in shaping forest structure, biomass accumulation, and carbon storage. In this study, we found that plots on steeper slopes (≥ 25°) had notably higher total biomass, carbon density, and CO2 equivalents compared to those on gentler slopes. Plots in the south and west were more diversified and had greater carbon stocks and CO2 equivalents (Table 6). This trend likely stems from the reduced human disturbance on steep slopes, which are often harder to access for activities like grazing, collecting firewood, and expanding agriculture. Consequently, vegetation in these areas can thrive with less interference, allowing for greater biomass and carbon stock accumulation over time. Similar patterns have been observed in the dry Afromontane forests of northwestern Ethiopia, where steeper slopes correlated with higher carbon stocks due to less human impact and grazing pressure53,54,65.

On the flip side, the lowest tree diversity, carbon stocks, and CO₂ equivalents in our study were found on moderate slopes (15–20°). These regions are typically more accessible for human activities and livestock grazing, which can lead to a decrease in tree density and species richness, ultimately resulting in lower biomass and carbon storage. Other studies in Ethiopian forest ecosystems have reported similar findings, where moderate slopes faced greater disturbance intensity compared to their steeper counterparts, leading to diminished carbon stocks66.

Slope aspect plays a crucial role in determining how tree diversity and carbon stocks are distributed within the study forest. The increased species diversity and carbon storage found on the south- and west-facing slopes likely stem from differences in solar radiation, soil moisture, and microclimatic conditions that promote plant growth. The way the landscape is shaped affects how productive the vegetation can be by altering sunlight exposure, evapotranspiration rates, and the availability of soil moisture, all of which are key factors in biomass production and carbon accumulation. Numerous studies have shown that carbon stocks can vary widely across different slope aspects due to the unique microclimatic and soil conditions67.

In a similar vein, research in Ethiopian montane forests has revealed that the distribution of carbon stocks is heavily influenced by environmental factors like slope, altitude, and aspect, which in turn affect soil characteristics, plant composition, and the intensity of disturbances68. The variations seen in Sulula Mofa Forest underscore the significance of topographic diversity in shaping both forest structure and the potential for carbon sequestration. Grasping these connections is vital for enhancing forest management practices and boosting the role of forests in combating climate change.

Carbon stock along aspect gradient

It was estimated that plots in the south and west had greater carbon stocks and CO2 sequstration. Northeastern parts of the sample plots observed with lowest in species diversity, higher DBH, shorter height, lower carbon stock density and smallest CO2 equivalents (Table 7).

The results of the present study indicated that plots located on the south and west aspects had relatively higher carbon stocks and CO₂ sequestration compared with other slope aspects. This pattern may be associated with favorable environmental conditions such as better soil moisture availability, moderate solar radiation, and suitable microclimatic conditions that enhance vegetation growth and biomass accumulation. Aspect influences the amount of solar radiation received by a site, which in turn affects soil temperature, evapotranspiration rates, and soil moisture availability, ultimately influencing plant productivity and carbon storage (Geiger et al. 2009). Studies conducted in Ethiopian Afromontane forests have also reported significant variation in carbon stock distribution across slope aspects due to differences in microclimate and soil characteristics67.

In contrast, the northeastern plots in the present study exhibited the lowest species diversity, relatively larger diameter at breast height (DBH), shorter tree height, and lower carbon stock density as well as smaller CO₂ equivalents. The lower diversity in these plots may indicate the dominance of a few mature tree species with relatively larger diameters but limited vertical growth and regeneration, which could reduce overall biomass accumulation and carbon storage. Environmental factors such as higher exposure to wind, lower soil moisture retention, or higher disturbance levels may also contribute to these patterns. Similar findings have been reported in several tropical forest ecosystems where aspect and associated environmental gradients significantly influence species composition, structural characteristics, and carbon storage capacity53,54,65.

Additionally, the differences in carbon stock across various slope aspects are often tied to factors like soil depth, nutrient availability, and the history of disturbances. Slopes that face south and west typically see greater vegetation productivity because they enjoy more balanced sunlight and better moisture conditions, which in turn promote tree growth and biomass accumulation68. This variation observed in the Sulula Mofa Forest underscores how important topographic diversity is for shaping forest structure, species diversity, and the potential for carbon sequestration. Grasping these connections is vital for enhancing forest management strategies and maximizing the role of forests in combating climate change.

Comparison between different carbon pools in SF

At the global level, 19% of C in the earth’s biosphere is stored in plants and 81% in the soil and about 31% of C is stored in the biomass and 69% in the soil69. Organic C stored in the world’s soils is about 1100-1600Pg, more than twice the C in living vegetation (560Pg) or in the atmosphere (750Pg)7. Soil is a good source of C pool and, thus, a C sinks70. SOC sequestration is a major sink for atmospheric CO2, with the global soil C pool (1550Pg) being three times greater than the biotic pools (560Pg) and twice the atmospheric C pool (760Pg)71. Soils play a key role in the global C pool, resulting in the storage of ~ 75% of terrestrial C26, 27.

In the study forest, the total carbon stocks were 933.9t/ha and the CO2 sequestration potentials of the study forest were estimated at 3427.43t/ha. Tree pools above and below ground were the largest, soil pools were the second whereas, litter pools was the least contributors for carbon stock (658.55, 87.33 and 188.02t/ha respectively) and total CO2 equivalents of 3427.43t/ha having values of, (2416.89, 320.5 and 690.04t/ha) CO2 equivalent values of tree, soil and litter pool respectively (Fig. 3 and Appendix 1).

The role of forests in carbon stock and climate change mitigation

GHG emissions could rise by 25–90% by 2030 relative to 2000 and the Earth could warm by 3 °C72. Emissions estimated a median global temperature that will increase from post-2030 of 2.7 °C to 3.5 °C by 210073.

Forests are thought to offer a mitigation strategy to reduce global warming and store over half of the carbon residing in terrestrial ecosystems74. Forests sequester and store more carbon than any terrestrial ecosystem by storing more than 80% of all terrestrial AGC and more than 70% of all soil organic carbon75. It was pointed out that, forest ecosystems are regarded as the largest terrestrial carbon pool that has an average biophysical mitigation potential of 5,380Mt CO2/year until 205072.

Trading carbon credits and its potential to improve livelihoods

Forests and soils are potential sinks for elevated CO2 emissions and are being considered in the list of acceptable offsets and they are environmentally acceptable and cost-effective ways to capture and store large amounts of atmospheric carbon8.

Forest carbon activities have further potential for a triple benefit: climate change mitigation, community development and biodiversity conservation; therefore, Forest and Soil management efforts help to capture all three benefits82. Developing countries are more vulnerable to climate change because a large proportion of their population directly depends on agricultural and natural ecosystems for their livelihoods83. Forest and Soil management strategies therefore become an important C mitigation approach through mitigation measures involving both CO2e reduction and increasing C sinks72.

A tradable carbon credit offers financial incentives for considering carbon storage in forest management decisions84. Carbon sequestration by Ethiopian forests has the potential to contribute to Ethiopian well-being in two ways. First, sequestration of carbon in forests, thereby, slowing the rate of climate change and second, the potential to gain foreign income85,34. Ethiopia is one of the world’s lowest emitters of GHG emissions; however, most vulnerable country and the least ready country86. Developing countries will require $28-67B in funds to enable adaptation to climate change in 2030 as estimated by (UNFCCC, 2007).

Bottom-up regional studies show that forestry mitigation options have the economic potential at costs up to 100 US$/tCO2eq to contribute 1.3-4.2Gt CO2eq/year (average 2.7Gt CO2eq/year) in 2030. About 50% can be achieved at a cost under 20 US$/tCO2-eq (around 1.6Gt CO2/year) with large differences between regions. Global top-down models predict far higher mitigation potentials of 13.8Gt CO2eq/year in 2030 at carbon prices less than or equal to 100 US$/tCO2eq69.

The current study, in Sulula Mofa Forest, estimated and predicted the mitigation potentials to 3427.43tCO2 equivalents per hectare of the forest. Based on the global top-down and bottom-up regional model of 2030, Sulula Mofa Forest could contribute forestry mitigation options by having justified and predicted the economic potential of the study forest at costs from ≥ 82,292 US$ up to ≤ 342,743 US$ per year carbon prices and it would be financial incentives to improve Livelihoods for the surrounding community. It is proved that there is interaction between the local community and a state forest87.

Conclusion and recommendations

This study quantified the carbon stock density and carbon sequestration potential of Sulula Mofa Forest by analyzing three major carbon pools: tree biomass, soil organic carbon, and litter biomass. The findings revealed that the total carbon stock density of the forest was estimated at 933.9 t/ha, with the largest contribution coming from the tree carbon pool (658.55 t/ha), followed by the soil carbon pool (188.02 t/ha) and the litter carbon pool (87.33 t/ha). Similarly, the forest demonstrated a considerable CO₂ sequestration potential of 3427.43 t/ha, of which 2416.89 t/ha was stored in tree biomass, 690.04 t/ha in soil organic carbon, and 320.5 t/ha in litter biomass.

Additionally, the study found that environmental factors like altitude, slope, and slope aspect have a notable impact on the diversity of woody species, carbon stock density, and CO₂ sequestration potential within the forest ecosystem. Differences in carbon storage were noted across various sampling plots along these environmental gradients. Notably, the southern and southwestern areas of the forest showed higher carbon stocks in tree, litter, and soil pools, likely due to more favorable ecological conditions, such as better vegetation cover and reduced disturbance levels. In summary, the findings highlight that the Sulula Mofa Forest is a crucial carbon sink, playing a significant role in carbon storage and the sequestration of atmospheric CO₂. This forest is essential for mitigating climate change, conserving biodiversity, and ensuring ecosystem sustainability, while also connecting local forest management practices to the broader global carbon cycle and carbon budget.

Based on the findings of this study, it is recommended that Sulula Mofa Forest should be given strong conservation priority due to its high carbon stock density and significant CO₂ sequestration potential .Hence, Strengthening sustainable forest management and community-based conservation practices is essential to reduce anthropogenic disturbances and maintain the forest’s carbon sequestration capacity. In addition, restoration and enrichment planting in relatively low-carbon areas should be implemented to enhance biomass accumulation and improve overall carbon storage. Integrating the forest into national and international climate change mitigation initiatives, including carbon financing mechanisms such as the BioCarbon Fund and the Kyoto Protocol Clean Development Mechanism (CDM), could provide financial incentives that support forest conservation while improving the livelihoods of local communities.
