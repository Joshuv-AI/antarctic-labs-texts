APOE-Glia Crosstalk in Epilepsy: Mechanisms, Controversies, and Therapeutic Perspectives
Authors: Meng-Yao Zhang, Jia-Hui Song, Xiao-Man Li, Meng Wang, Yan-Feng Zhang, Yan-Chao Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTemporal lobe epilepsy (TLE) is a common type of focal epilepsy that is prone to developing into drug-resistant epilepsy (DRE). It is often accompanied by neuroinflammation, lipid metabolism disorders, and cognitive impairment. However, its underlying pathological mechanisms remain incompletely understood. As a pivotal lipid transporter in the central nervous system (CNS), apolipoprotein E (APOE) exists as three isoforms: APOE ε2, ε3 and ε4. The APOE ε4 allele represents a high-risk genetic variant for neurological disorders. More and more evidences suggest that APOE gene polymorphisms may modulate seizure susceptibility, severity, and clinical outcomes, indicating that APOE plays an important role in the pathogenesis of epilepsy. Glial homeostasis disruption acts as a core driver of epilepsy. Notably, following epileptic injury, APOE expression significantly increases, with a dynamic shift in its cellular source. Activated microglia replace astrocytes as the main source of APOE. APOE may mediate interactions between microglia and astrocytes, promoting the formation of lipid-accumulating reactive astrocytes (LARAs), and thereby driving a vicious cycle involving inflammation, lipid metabolism disruption, and neuronal excitotoxicity, contributing to hippocampal sclerosis (HS) and worsening of DRE. Although significant progress has been made, the mechanistic understanding underlying APOE-glia crosstalk in epileptogenesis remains incomplete, and the clinical translation potential of targeting APOE requires further validation. This review summarizes current knowledge on the role of APOE in epilepsy, with a focus on its regulatory effects on glial cell function, providing insights into the pathogenesis of refractory epilepsy and informing potential therapeutic strategies.

Introduction

Epilepsy is a chronic brain disorder characterized by recurrent spontaneous epileptic seizures, with a global prevalence of approximately 0.5% to 1%. Among these patients, about 30% develop drug-resistant epilepsy (DRE).1 Temporal lobe epilepsy (TLE) is the most common type of focal epilepsy, often accompanied by hippocampal sclerosis (HS) and progressive cognitive impairment. Its pathogenic mechanisms involve multiple factors such as neuronal injury, abnormal synaptic remodeling, neuroinflammation, and metabolic disorders.2,3

During epileptic seizures, abnormal ion channel function or disrupted neurotransmitter release can trigger synchronized neuronal firing,4 which is often accompanied by excessive release of the excitatory neurotransmitter glutamate. This glutamate surge disrupts lipid homeostasis and subsequently induces lipid peroxidation, which in turn further affects neuronal ion permeability and neurotransmission.5 Glial activation has also been observed during epileptic episodes.6 In the early stages, activated glial cells may exert a protective effect on neurons.7 However, as epilepsy progresses and glial activation intensifies, the inflammatory factors released by glial cells can enhance neuronal excitability.8 Consequently, the initially protective role of glial cells gradually shifts toward a detrimental one as the disease advances.9

Apolipoprotein E (APOE) is a main lipid carrier in the central nervous system (CNS).10 There are three common alleles of the APOE, among which the APOE ε4 allele is the strongest genetic risk factor for Alzheimer’s disease (AD), while APOE ε2 has a protective effect.3 Notably, AD and epilepsy, especially TLE, share significant pathological overlaps.3,11 In recent years, an increasing amount of evidence has indicated that the APOE ε4 allele also affects the susceptibility, age of onset, severity, and cognitive prognosis of epilepsy, suggesting that APOE may be a key molecular linking neurodegeneration and epilepsy.3,12,13

This article aims to summarize the research progress on the interaction between APOE and glial cells in the pathogenesis of epilepsy. Starting from the structural and functional basis of APOE, we review the experimental phenomena and clinical evidence in epilepsy, elaborate in depth on the interaction mechanisms between APOE and glia after epilepsy, then explores therapeutic strategies targeting this interaction axis to provide new ideas for the precise prevention and treatment of epilepsy.

The Structure, Function and Isoform of APOE

APOE is a 34–37 kDa glycoprotein composed of 299 amino acids, containing two main domains. The N-terminal receptor-binding domain and the C-terminal lipid-binding domain, which are connected by a hinge region.10 In the CNS, the main function of APOE is to transport cholesterol and phospholipids between astrocytes and neurons.14 APOE is also involved in synaptic plasticity,15 cell membrane stability, nerve injury repair,16,17 debris clearance,18 immune regulation, anti-inflammatory responses,19,20 and energy metabolism.19,21

There are three main isoforms of APOE protein, APOE2, APOE3, and APOE4. Although these amino acid substitutions are located close to each other, they significantly change the stability and lipid-binding properties of the protein.10,22 (Figure 1) Structural differences among APOE isoforms, particularly at the C-terminus, determine their isomer-specific lipid-binding efficiency,21 which directly affect lipid transport efficiency.23 Among them, APOE4 exhibits the lowest lipid-binding and transport capacity. Beyond structural differences, at both expression and functional levels, cerebrospinal fluid APOE levels in APOE ε4 allele carriers are lower than those in APOE ε3 allele carriers.24 This is because APOE4 enters lysosomes via the endosomal sorting pathway for degradation and cannot be recycled,23 resulting in APOE2 and APOE3 outperforming APOE4 in lipid binding ability21 and in transporting and releasing phospholipids and cholesterol under equivalent protein concentrations.25 Consequently, APOE4 struggles to bind lipids effectively, leading to impaired lipid transport and clearance of lipid byproducts.25,26 APOE4 fails to efficiently transport intracellular abnormal lipids to the extracellular space, causing lipid accumulation and increased lipid peroxidation, which makes astrocytes expressing APOE4 more susceptible to lipid peroxidative damage.27 In summary, individuals carrying APOE ε4 allele are more prone to inflammatory injury when suffering from traumatic brain injury or neurodegenerative diseases.28,29 Figure 1The APOE gene is located in region 19q13.2 on chromosome 19. The Role of APOE in Epilepsy

The role of APOE in epilepsy is complex. It has a potential protective effect on epileptic neurons, but the related research still remains controversial. The reason is that differences in APOE genotypes affect the age of onset, severity of seizures, neuronal damage, and prognosis of epilepsy. In recent years, the association between APOE ε4 and epilepsy has gradually attracted the attention of researchers. It has now been confirmed to be a genetic risk factor for epilepsy, especially closely related to temporal lobe epilepsy, late-onset epilepsy, and drug-resistant epilepsy.3

APOE is Significantly Upregulated in the Brain After Epilepsy

Kainic acid (KA)-induced animal epilepsy model demonstrates that the hippocampus shows an increase in APOE after epilepsy. However, the regulatory effect of the elevated APOE on hippocampal damage remains controversial. Some rodent experiments have shown that APOE has neuroprotective functions, and APOE knock-out mice have more severe epileptic seizures and more significant hippocampal neuron damage.30 In rat epilepsy model, it has also been confirmed APOE in the hippocampus increased after epilepsy, participating in brain injury repair and remodeling, and is a key molecule regulating epilepsy-induced brain damage.31,32 However, another study found that APOE knock-out can alleviate seizure and neuronal loss, suggesting that the upregulation of APOE can also aggravate epilepsy-induced brain damage.33 Further studies have shown that the upregulation of APOE in the hippocampus of the epilepsy model can promote the activation of microglia and astrocytes, intensify neuroinflammation and neuronal damage, while APOE knock-out can effectively alleviate secondary hippocampal pathological damage after epilepsy.34

The Spatiotemporal Expression Characteristics of APOE in the Brain After Epilepsy

Under normal conditions, APOE in the CNS is mainly synthesized by astrocytes, with very low expression in microglia, neurons, oligodendrocytes, and ependymal cells.17,34 After epilepsy, the expression of APOE in the brain increases. Meanwhile, the main source of APOE shifts with the progress of epilepsy.

In the KA-induced model, the APOE mRNA and immunoreactivity of APOE in hippocampal neurons significantly increased after 48–72 hours of seizure.35 APOE in neurons increases with the aggravation of neural injury.36 During culturing neurons with APOE-specific knockout astrocytes, Chen et al found that after adding KA, fatty acids were not transferred from neurons to astrocytes.37 This conclusion suggests that astrocytes remain an important source of APOE after epilepsy, and the APOE secreted by them mediates lipid transport between astrocytes and neurons, participating in neural injury.33 Xu et al introduced an enhanced green fluorescent protein cDNA containing a termination codon downstream of the translation initiation site of the APOE gene locus in mice, and conducted co-localization analysis of hippocampal immunofluorescence images in mice 1 day after KA-induced epilepsy. They found that approximately 3/4 of the astrocytes expressed APOE, while only 3–9% microglia expressed APOE.31 Therefore, these researchers believed that APOE in the brain after epilepsy was mainly synthesized by astrocytes, and the contribution of microglia was relatively small. This conclusion changed after long-term monitoring of APOE expression during seizure and the application of transcriptomics. Shi et al found that in the acute phase, 3 days after epilepsy onset, astrocytes were still the main source of APOE. But in the chronic phase, 14 days after epilepsy onset, the expression of APOE in astrocytes in the mouse brain decreased, and activated microglia significantly upregulated APOE. Therefore, microglia gradually replaced astrocytes as the main expressing cells of APOE.2 The transformation of APOE cellular source was further verified in the brain tissue of patients with TLE. TLE patients with HS had elevated hippocampal APOE protein. Microglial APOE was identified as a core differentially expressed gene, confirming that microglia dominated the abnormal increase of APOE in epilepsy pathology.2

The regional distribution of APOE in the brain is closely associated with its function in epilepsy. Spatial transcriptomics studies have revealed that under physiological conditions, APOE concentration is highest in the cerebellum, followed by the frontal cortex and the hippocampus38 which are brain regions that are vulnerable to damage following epileptic seizures. After epilepsy, APOE-expressing microglia in mice tend to cluster in the CA1 and CA3 regions of the hippocampus, where neuronal loss and glial hyperplasia are most pronounced. The microglia interacts closely with neurons, further promoting epileptic focus progression.2 It is notable that lipid accumulation in mice after epilepsy is also concentrated in the CA1 and CA3 regions of the hippocampus.39 This might suggest that the distribution of APOE is related to lipid metabolism in the brain.

The expression of APOE after epilepsy undergoes changes in both cell origin and spatial distribution over time, suggesting that at different stages of epilepsy, APOE derived from distinct cell sources may exert different functions. It is believed that in the acute phase, astrocyte-derived APOE is mainly involved in lipid clearance and neuroprotection,39 while in the chronic phase, microglia-derived APOE is associated with pro-inflammatory activation, lipid metabolism disorders, and persistent neuronal hyperexcitability.2 This spatiotemporal distribution pattern indicates that the functional impact of APOE on epilepsy pathology is critically dependent on the disease stage and the specific cell types, implying that therapeutic strategies targeting APOE may need to take both the stage of epilepsy and the differences in glial cell types into consideration. However, limitations of the current studies include its heavy reliance on single-cell sequencing and transcriptomics approaches, as well as insufficient systematic validation through in vivo experiments and disease models.

Clinical Research on APOE Gene Subtype in Epilepsy

Currently, the clinical research on APOE and epilepsy mainly focuses on the impact of APOE isoform differences on epilepsy. The prevailing view is that, similar to Alzheimer’s disease, APOE ε4 is a genetic risk factor for various types of epilepsy,3 whereas APOE ε2 may confer potential neuroprotective effects.3,7 APOE ε3, on the other hand, is not associated with epilepsy onset, but carriers of this isoform tend to exhibit less neuronal damage and better structural stability.7,40

It should be noted, however, that not all clinical studies consistently support the association between APOE subtypes and epilepsy susceptibility. Early studies failed to detect significant correlations between APOE genotypes and epilepsy episodes, seizure severity, or hippocampal pathology.41–43 A clinical cohort study in Turkey suggested that there was no significant difference was observed in the frequency of the APOE ε4 allele between 47 patients with TLE and 62 controls.44 Moreover, within these patients, no significant associations were found between APOE genotypes and electroencephalogram manifestations, memory disorders, or hippocampal atrophy.44 Similarly, studies on pediatric epilepsy did not confirm a correlation between APOE polymorphisms and epilepsy types.45 And no significant role of APOE ε4 was identified in simple febrile seizures46 or post-traumatic epilepsy.47 Furthermore, some studies have suggested that APOE polymorphisms may not constitute the primary genetic risk factor for non-dysplastic TLE.48

In contrast, more and more clinical evidence has confirmed a significant association between APOE ε4 and epilepsy risk, particularly in TLE. APOE ε4 carriers exhibit an earlier age of seizure onset by approximately 4–10 years,49 more severe cognitive impairment,11 and a higher risk of drug resistance.50 Long-term follow-up of patients with childhood-onset epilepsy further revealed that APOE ε4 carriers developed significant cerebral Aβ protein accumulation after 50 years, whereas APOE ε2 homozygotes did not show this phenomenon,34 suggesting that APOE genotype influences the long-term prognosis of epilepsy patients. In terms of neurological function and neuronal injury, TLE patients with the APOE ε4 genotype exhibit impairments in verbal learning ability.51

The reasons for these discrepancies may include multiple factors such as differences in study design, population heterogeneity, sample size limitations, and variations in the definition of epilepsy subtypes. This conflict in conclusions highlights the need for large-scale, prospective cohort studies with high-quality phenotyping to ultimately clarify the clinical significance of APOE gene polymorphisms in epilepsy.

The Different Roles of APOE Subtypes in the Pathogenesis of Epilepsy

Studies using humanized APOE knock-in mouse models have clearly demonstrated the functional differences among these APOE isoforms.52 Different subtypes have distinct regulatory effects on epilepsy susceptibility, onset age, neuronal damage, cognitive function, and drug response.3,12,13 The different effects observed following knock-in of different APOE genotypes may also help explain the conflicting findings regarding the role of APOE upregulation in neuronal injury in the aforementioned studies. The mechanistic differences are closely related to the varying efficiency of each isoform in lipid transport, glial–neuronal crosstalk, and neuroinflammatory regulation. Further investigation of APOE isoforms may provide a basis for developing personalized diagnostic and therapeutic strategies tailored to different genotype carriers in the future. The Interaction Between APOE and Glial Cells and Its Regulation of Epilepsy

Glial Cells are Involved in the Pathogenesis of Epilepsy

Glial cells in the CNS are the main components that maintain the homeostasis of the brain and mediate the pathological damage of epilepsy.57 The interaction between neurons and glial cells is a key mechanism of abnormal neuronal discharge, disease progression, and chronicity of epilepsy.2,57 Under physiological conditions, astrocytes preserve blood-brain barrier integrity, stabilize the homeostasis of ions and neurotransmitters, and provide metabolic energy for neurons.57 Microglia are primarily responsible for central immune surveillance, synaptic pruning, and tissue homeostasis repair.1 Oligodendrocytes and Nerve-Glial antigen 2 (NG2) glia modulate neural network activities by mediating myelin formation and axonal signal transduction.57,58

Different glial cells play different roles in epilepsy pathology. Activated astrocytes can promote the development of epilepsy through regulating neurotransmission, exacerbating ion disorders, promoting neuroinflammation,1,57 and damaging the blood-brain barrier.59 The activation of neurotoxic astrocytes after epilepsy can aggravate the pathological damage after epilepsy.60 Microglia have different roles at different stages of epilepsy. In the acute stage, they can inhibit abnormal neuronal discharge and reduce cell apoptosis to exert protective effects.1 In the chronic stage, they continue to activate and release a large amount of inflammatory mediators, exacerbating neuronal damage.1 Microglia in TLE can differentiate into pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory subtypes to regulate the progression of epilepsy.61,62 Oligodendrocytes and NG2 glial cells undergo demyelination damage under the stimulation of epilepsy inflammation and oxidative stress, changing the excitability of axons, disrupting the stability of neural circuits, and amplifying abnormal neuronal discharge.57

The Interaction Between APOE and Glial Cells

APOE is an important factor for lipid metabolism and immune regulation in the brain, and is mainly secreted by astrocytes and microglia. By regulating the functions and interactions of these two types of glial cells, APOE mediates neuroinflammation and lipid metabolism disorders, promoting the occurrence and development of epilepsy as well as long-term neurodegenerative lesions. Previous studies have confirmed that gliosis is widely observed in epileptic brain tissues,63 but it is not clear whether it is an inducer of epileptic seizures or a secondary injury.64

The Interaction Between APOE and Microglia in Epilepsy

In epilepsy, APOE is a key molecule that regulates the activation, differentiation, and dysfunction of microglia.2 Following epileptic seizures, key pro-inflammatory cytokines such as tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), interleukin-1β (IL-1β), and interleukin-6 (IL-6) are mainly secreted by microglia and colocalize with APOE. Notably, inhibition of APOE expression can downregulate the secretion of these inflammatory cytokines, confirming that APOE markedly promotes the secretion of inflammatory factors from microglia.2 Single-cell sequencing has revealed that microglia after epilepsy exhibit markedly increased complement activation, oxidative stress, and activation of the interleukin-6-Janus kinase-signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (IL6-JAK-STAT3) inflammatory pathway, accompanied by abnormal activation of metabolic pathways including glycolysis and oxidative phosphorylation.2 This result suggests an important role for microglia in inflammatory responses and metabolic disturbances after epilepsy. Metabolomics evidence validates that APOE overexpression disturbs cerebral lipid homeostasis, inflammatory signaling and metabolic profiles in the brain. Lipid metabolism disorder is the core pathological change in this process.2 As epilepsy progresses, microglia begin to express APOE at progressively higher levels.2 These observations suggest that microglia-derived APOE may influence the onset and progression of epilepsy through its involvement in lipid metabolism and neuroinflammatory responses.

The abnormal activation of microglia mediated by APOE can regulate the excitability of neurons and promote the progression of epilepsy. Adeno-associated virus (AAV)-mediated targeted overexpression models have demonstrated that microglial APOE overexpression markedly elevates neuronal excitability, while APOE knockout mitigates excessive neuronal excitation and epileptic brain injury.2 In the state of epilepsy, the interaction between microglia and neurons is significantly activated. Microglia with high APOE expression gather in the epileptic injury area and induce neuronal apoptosis and abnormal discharges through inflammation and metabolic disorders.2 At the same time, activated microglia can mediate the secondary neuronal loss in epilepsy through phagocytosis, aggravating brain damage.7

APOE exhibits subtype differences in relation to microglia and the course of epilepsy. In human epileptic brain tissue, it has been observed that patients with APOE ε4/ε4 genotype exhibit elevated tissue levels of interleukin-1α (IL-1α) and more pronounced microglial proliferation compared to those with APOE ε3/ε3 genotype,41,65 which may contribute to the sustained amplification of the inflammatory response. In addition, APOE also has potential protective effects. In epilepsy models, APOE knockout mice have excessive activation of microglia and increased expression of chemokines,2 suggesting that physiological levels of APOE can inhibit excessive microglial stress and maintain cerebral homeostasis. After seizures, APOE4 can further increase the secretion level of pro-inflammatory factors by microglia, induce microglia to differentiate into an inflammatory phenotype,61 weaken their phagocytic clearance ability,66 and exacerbate neurological pathological changes.67

Microglia are highly heterogeneous, and the conventional M1/M2 dichotomous classification is insufficient to fully define their diverse pathological phenotypes during epilepsy progression.68 Based upon multi-omics approaches such as single-cell sequencing,69 the activation subtypes of microglia can be precisely distinguished. He et al identified a novel microglial subset named MG3 in the brain tissue of TLE patients through single-cell sequencing, which highly expresses APOE.70 MG3 shows disrupted iron metabolism and undergoes lipid peroxidation through the Fenton reaction, with activation of pro-inflammatory pathways such as TNF and interleukin-17 (IL-17). The expression level of MG3 is positively correlated with seizure frequency, a finding further validated by multiplex immunofluorescence experiments. This study provides evidence at the single-cell level in humans that APOE mediates oxidative inflammation in microglia following TLE.70 However, the identification of this cell subpopulation based solely on single-cell sequencing and immunofluorescence remains relatively limited, and further in vivo and in vitro experiments are needed to verify the existence and function of MG3 in the future.

Meanwhile, APOE can widely regulate Aβ clearance,71 synaptic integrity,71 blood-brain barrier homeostasis,33 and mitochondrial function, leading to abnormal interactions between microglia and neurons and participating in the chronic process of epilepsy.30

The Interaction Between APOE and Astrocytes in Epilepsy

APOE can cause phenotypic and functional changes in astrocytes in epilepsy models. Brain-specific Bassoon (BSN) is a presynaptic scaffold protein that regulates the transport of synaptic vesicles and the release of neurotransmitters in neurons. The BSN gene mutations can disrupt the lipid homeostasis of astrocytes, further exacerbating excessive neuronal excitation, confirming that lipid metabolism disorders in astrocytes is an important trigger for epilepsy.72 A distinct reactive astrocyte subtype termed lipid-accumulating reactive astrocytes (LARAs) has been identified by Chen et al in brain tissues from TLE patients and epileptic mice.39 This cell subtype is characterized by high expression of APOE, abnormal accumulation of lipid droplets, and accumulation of neutral lipids such as triglycerides. Moreover, APOE knockout can effectively inhibit the generation of LARAs and alleviate epileptic seizures.39 Epileptic seizures can activate phospholipase A2 (PLA2) in brain tissue.73 Activated PLA2 hydrolyzes neuronal membrane phospholipids and facilitates arachidonic acid accumulation in the brain.74,75 At the same time, mitochondrial secretory phospholipase A2 (sPLA2) disrupts the mitochondrial respiratory chain, jointly promoting the accumulation of ROS and lipid peroxides, and aggravating neuronal oxidative damage.75 Under the epileptic state, APOE mediates the transfer of excess fatty acids from damaged neurons into astrocytes,39 which may induce lipid metabolism disorders in astrocytes and driving the formation of LARAs.39

APOE mediates the formation of LARAs to cause abnormal release of neurotransmitters and exacerbate neuroinflammation, thereby promoting the progression of epilepsy. In LARAs formed after TLE, lipid accumulation upregulates adenosine A2A receptors, leading to downregulation of amino acid transporter (EAAT) transporter expression. This leads to a marked increase in extracellular glutamate levels, enhances neuronal excitability,39 and induces sustained seizure activity. Following epileptic seizures, large amounts of lipid peroxidation products70 accompany neuroinflammatory responses,76 and these inflammatory factors can activate the Toll-like receptor 4-myeloid differentiation primary response 88-extracellular signal-regulated kinase (TLR4-MyD88-ERK) inflammatory signaling axis within astrocytes.77 As a specific astrocyte subpopulation characterized by abundant lipid droplet accumulation in epileptic brain,39 LARAs may potentially serve as the key cells mediating the overactivation of this pathway and initiating downstream pro-inflammatory cascades. However, direct experimental evidence confirming the specific activation of this pathway in LARAs is currently lacking and awaits further investigation. Reactive astrocytes after epilepsy can also sustain inflammatory responses through nuclear factor-kappa B (NF-κB) and JAK-STAT3-related inflammatory pathways, and interact with microglia and peripheral immune cells to exacerbate neuroinflammatory damage.40 LARAs, as a specific subtype of reactive astrocytes following epilepsy, may also participate in this process. In addition, APOE4 encapsulated lipoproteins derived from astrocytes are rich in saturated long-chain fatty acids and can induce neuronal death through the fat apoptosis mechanism,10 aggravating the neurological damage of epilepsy. Therefore, targeting the lipid transport and metabolic disorders mediated by astrocyte APOE and intervening in the formation of LARAs may become a potential new therapeutic74 strategy for refractory TLE.39

Lipid composition alterations and astrocyte interactions with APOE following epilepsy contribute to disease progression, with APOE also participating in this process. Epileptic seizures activate PLA2 in brain tissue.78,79 Activated PLA2 hydrolyzes neuronal membrane phospholipids, leading to the accumulation of arachidonic acid in the brain.26,74 Saturated fatty acids and n-6 polyunsaturated fatty acids can induce pro-inflammatory activation of glial cells, further lowering the seizure threshold.80 Meanwhile, mitochondrial secretory phospholipase A2 (sPLA2) disrupts the mitochondrial respiratory chain, collectively resulting in the accumulation of ROS and lipid peroxidation products, thereby exacerbating oxidative neuronal damage.81 Astrocytes participate in the regulation of acute inflammation by releasing various fatty acids,82 while APOE influences post-epileptic neuroinflammatory responses by modulating the transport and metabolism of fatty acids released from astrocytes. Compared to APOE ε3-type astrocytes, lipid droplets in APOE ε4-type astrocyte are enriched in unsaturated fatty acids, damaging the lysosomal fat phagocytic function, causing continuous lipid accumulation, thereby further exacerbating the neuroinflammation caused by glial cells. Glia-mediated inflammation can also interact with leukocytes and cerebrovascular endothelial cells to disrupt the blood-brain barrier,83 thereby promoting the sustained progression of epileptic pathology.

APOE Interacts with Microglia and Astrocytes Together to Promote the Progression of Epilepsy

In the normal brain, astrocyte-derived APOE can attenuate inflammatory signaling in both astrocytes and microglia.20 However, following epilepsy, the cellular source of APOE shifts, accompanied by a functional switch from its anti-inflammatory role under physiological conditions to a pro-inflammatory role in the epileptic state.

Bioinformatics analysis and cellular experiments have validated that there are several functional sites involved in inflammatory responses. TLR4, cyclic GMP-AMP synthase (cGAS), and stimulator of interferon genes (STING) are upregulated in microglia with high APOE expression following epilepsy.2 This suggests that the microglia with high expression of APOE activate the TLR4 and cGAS-STING signaling cascades, releasing a large number of pro-inflammatory cytokines, which can directly aggravate neuroinflammatory damage2 and influence the metabolism of glial cells.2 For example, IL-1 has been shown to alter the rate of neuronal signal transmission in brain and stimulate lipid peroxidation and reactive oxygen species production in astrocytes and microglia.20 These oxidative stress products can activate the TLR4-MyD88-ERK inflammatory pathway in astrocytes,77 further stimulating the release of inflammatory factors. This process may also occur following epilepsy. As an essential upstream regulatory molecule, APOE also facilitates astrocytic transformation into the LARAs phenotype and mediates excessive lipid translocation from damaged neurons to astrocytes, thereby inducing aberrant intracellular lipid droplet deposition and disrupting astrocytic lipid metabolism.39 Increased APOE may be accompanied by abnormal lipid accumulation and oxidative stress during the process of mediating LARA formation. These metabolic products can activate microglia reversibly and maintain the pro-inflammatory phenotype,84 further upregulating the expression of APOE in microglia.39 In this process, the APOE genotype has an important influence. Among them, the APOE ε4 allele can exacerbate the pro-inflammatory polarization of microglia and the lipid toxicity of astrocytes,41 accelerate neuronal damage, hippocampal sclerosis44 and the progression of epilepsy.3,10 The lipid accumulation mediated by APOE can also damage the Kir4.1 potassium channels and glutamate transporter EAAT functions of astrocytes,85,86 weaken the extracellular potassium ion buffering and glutamate clearance ability, and increase neuronal excitability.87 These pathological alterations ultimately facilitate epileptic network formation and recurrent seizure episodes (Figure 2). Research Progress on APOE in Other Glial Cells in Epilepsy

Both oligodendrocytes and oligodendrocyte precursor cells (OPCs) express APOE to varying degrees.88 In response to aging, injury, or certain diseases, oligodendrocytes and OPCs have been shown to strongly regulate APOE expression, suggesting a key role of APOE for these cells in lipid transport for myelin synthesis, membrane maintenance, and metabolism.89–91

In addition to astrocytes and microglia, oligodendrocytes may also participate in APOE-related pathological processes in epilepsy. To date, many studies have investigated the involvement of oligodendrocyte-derived and OPCs-derived APOE in the pathogenesis of neurological disorders, particularly APOE4, although the underlying mechanisms remain incompletely understood.88 In Alzheimer’s disease, APOE4 oligodendrocytes exhibit increased cholesterol synthesis and intracellular cholesterol storage, along with enhanced endoplasmic reticulum stress, compared to APOE3 oligodendrocytes.92 This finding suggests that oligodendrocyte-derived APOE4 may contribute to neurological disease pathogenesis through its effects on lipid metabolism. In the brain of TLE patients, lipid accumulation was also observed in OPCs. However, following induction of epilepsy in APOE knockout mice, no significant changes in lipid accumulation were detected in OPCs.39 This observation suggests that lipid metabolism abnormalities occur in oligodendrocytes after epilepsy, but these changes may not be associated with APOE.

Although APOE expression in oligodendrocytes is relatively low under physiological conditions,88 whether epileptic activity induces APOE upregulation in these cells and whether this contributes to the pathogenesis of epilepsy remain open questions.

Future Precision Medical Approaches for Treating Epilepsy

Based on the roles of APOE and glial cells in the pathogenesis of TLE, we propose several promising therapeutic and preventive strategies targeting this axis. Direct pharmacological targeting of APOE represents one of the most straightforward approaches.22,93 Modulating post-epileptic metabolic pathways, particularly lipid metabolism, may intervene in the disease process at the upstream level of inflammatory responses.94–96 In addition, direct inhibition of post-epileptic neuroinflammation is also an important therapeutic direction.93,96 Beyond therapeutic value, APOE in combination with inflammatory cytokines may serve as important biomarkers for epilepsy, facilitating the development of individualized treatment strategies and early intervention in high-risk populations.1,13 The therapeutic and screening strategies based on the underlying mechanisms are summarized in Table 2.1,13,22,72,87,93–99 However, these promising applications have not yet been validated, and we hope that there will be more experimental studies and better clinical translation in the future. Conclusion and Perspectives

As a pivotal regulator of cerebral lipid metabolism, APOE exhibits elevated expression and altered cellular origins following epileptic seizures. Current evidence suggests that abnormal APOE expression is associated with microglial pro-inflammatory activation, astrocytic transformation into LARAs with subsequent activation of neuroinflammatory pathways, and impaired cerebral ion homeostasis and neurotransmitter clearance. These pathological changes may constitute a vicious cycle involving glial inflammatory activation, abnormal lipid accumulation, and neuronal excitotoxicity, which may in turn sustain HS, disease chronicity, and epileptic progression. Different APOE isoforms play different regulatory roles in epileptic pathogenesis: the APOE ε4 is associated with aggravated pathological cascades, whereas APOE ε2 and APOE ε3 are linked to more stable glial function and attenuated post-seizure secondary brain injury. Increasing evidence has confirmed that the interaction between APOE and glial cells represents an important pathological hallmark of TLE, providing theoretical support for exploring the pathogenesis of refractory TLE and advancing the development of novel targeted therapeutic agents.

However, several critical knowledge gaps remain. The upstream regulatory mechanisms that govern APOE expression and function in glial cells following epileptic injury remain unclear. The specific pathways through which APOE modulates glial inflammatory responses also lack systematic experimental validation. Moreover, given that most current evidence is derived from animal models and single-cell sequencing, the causal relationship between APOE-mediated glial dysfunction and epileptogenesis remains unclear. In addition, considering the inherent limitations of rodent models in recapitulating human diseases and the complexity of epilepsy as a neurological disorder, the translation of existing findings into clinical applications remains largely insufficient. Whether APOE-targeted interventions can modify the disease course rather than merely alleviate symptoms still requires further confirmation in clinical studies. Therefore, beyond elucidating the deeper mechanisms underlying the interaction between APOE and glia in epileptogenesis, it is even more important to achieve therapeutic translation by targeting the APOE–glia interaction axis in future research.
