Scaling and Population Loss in Mexican Urban Centres
Gonzalo G. Peraza-Mues, Eugen Resendiz, Rodolfo Figueroa-Soriano, Rafael Prieto-Curiel, Roberto Ponce-Lopez

Abstract
Despite its pervasive implications, many cities around the world continue to expand in a fragmented and horizontal manner. Here, we analyse the complex dynamics of urban growth across 69 metropolitan areas in Mexico, using census data from 1990 to 2020, and develop a model of urban form change that considers changes in population size, density, and spatial configuration. We employ a radial probability density function and the urban expansion factor to create a framework for comparing urban expansion over time and across different regions. Over the past three decades, Mexico’s urban population has nearly doubled; however, populations have shifted outward, resulting in a decline of 2.5 million residents in central areas. Our analysis shows that distances from the city centre have increased by 28% on average, driven by population losses in central zones combined with growth in peripheral regions.



1
Introduction

The global population is increasingly urban, with the population in cities nearly doubling between 1990 and 2020, and projected to grow by another 60% by 2050, while the rural population has already peaked and is expected to decline
[
1
]
.
This demographic shift suggests that future population growth will occur primarily in cities.
In this context, cities respond to population growth in two main ways: by increasing density within their existing built-up areas and by expanding outward into surrounding areas. These processes are intertwined.
In recent decades, most urban population growth has been absorbed through outward expansion, often occurring in a fragmented and unplanned fashion
[
2
]
.
This kind of fragmented growth has long-term implications for cities’ resilience, sustainability, and spatial equity
[
3
,
4
,
5
,
6
]
.

Urban sprawl places a significant burden on cities
[
7
,
8
]
. It increases travel distances and creates a fragmented labour market, particularly in areas with inadequate transit infrastructure
[
9
]
. Additionally, sprawl diminishes access to essential services such as water and sewage
[
10
]
, and increases the presence of informal settlements
[
11
]
. Despite extensive research on the characteristics and measurement of urban sprawl, a notable knowledge gap persists regarding the population dynamics that drive urban expansion.
Traditionally, urban economics suggests that population densities should be highest in central areas where jobs and services are concentrated
[
12
]
.
However, a counter-trend is emerging in many cities where central regions experience population decline.
In contrast, peripheral areas are growing at an even faster rate, resulting in higher population densities in the fringe compared to the core.
This reversal of the expected patterns is not just an anomaly; it is a key driver of urban fragmentation.
The population’s retreat from the centre to the peripheries gives rise to numerous urban issues commonly attributed to sprawl in fast-growing urban areas.
In multiple cities, the impact of this sprawl is exacerbated by weak institutional capacity, unplanned growth in peripheral areas, and the neglect of cities’ cores, leading to the proliferation of informal settlements, fragmented labour markets, insufficient and inadequate infrastructure, services, and longer travel distances
[
13
,
9
,
11
]
.
The city’s core abandonment, rather than sprawl itself, is a critical yet under-examined force in urban dynamics.
This phenomenon exacerbates spatial and social inequalities, ultimately reducing urban efficiency.

These demographic shifts have been associated with distinct spatial patterns, often described under the traditional framework of urban sprawl.
While
expansion
refers to the physical growth of cities,
sprawl
captures the low-density, car-dependent, and fragmented development that typically accompanies unplanned growth on cities’ peripheries
[
2
]
.
Research has widely examined and measured sprawl through various methods, including geographic analysis
[
14
]
, cellular automata
[
15
]
, land-use models
[
16
]
, and spatial indicators of density and land-use mix
[
17
]
.
Factors contributing to sprawl include the relocation of jobs and industries to the urban periphery
[
18
,
19
]
and its consequence, such as the spatial mismatch between housing and employment
[
20
]
, as well as the development of car-oriented urban systems
[
21
]
.
Additionally, although much research on changes in city size focuses on the impact of population growth, demographic trends in many countries (such as Hungary, Bulgaria, Japan, and Italy) suggest that both national and urban populations are likely to shrink. It remains unclear, however, whether cities experiencing population decline tend to sprawl or become more compact in their urban form. In terms of urban form, is population decline simply a reverse process of population increase?

The causes and consequences of a city’s core abandonment, although recognised as a significant issue, have been poorly captured through data and models.
Several functions have been proposed to capture the radial distribution of cities, including a negative exponential function
[
22
]
, a shifted normal
[
23
]
, an inverse power law
[
24
]
, among others.
For example, a density gradient based on a negative exponential function was used to quantify the movement of population towards urban peripheries
[
22
,
25
,
26
]
. However, these functions fit poorly near the centre, where we aim to model the population decline
[
22
]
. Additionally, some decay parameters often yield conflicting results and a poor fit
[
27
]
. Thus, no model yet exists to effectively capture, forecast, and quantify one of the most harmful urban dynamics: the abandonment of a city centre.

In this study, we examine the pathways of urban expansion in Mexico across 69 metropolitan areas
[
28
]
.
Between 1990 and 2020, Mexico’s population increased from 81.7 million to 126 million, marking a 54% increase over 30 years.
In parallel, the country’s urbanisation rate increased from 71% to 81%, resulting in the urban population nearly doubling within three decades
[
28
]
.
This accelerated population growth raises a critical question regarding the spatial distribution of this population within urban areas: Has growth in Mexican metropolitan areas occurred evenly, remained concentrated in traditionally dense central zones, shifted toward peripheral regions, or significantly altered overall urban density patterns?

We analyse the radial patterns of urban growth based on varying distances from the city centre, defined as the historical point where each city initially developed.
Our findings indicate that Mexican cities have experienced a decline in population at their core, a dynamic we illustrate through the evolution of their population density profiles, or radial population densities.
The observed decrease in residential density in central urban areas, alongside rising peripheral densities, suggests a shift towards suburbanization.
This pattern is similar to urban development in car-oriented cities in high-income countries and cities in some low- to middle-income countries, like the US, Canada, and India
[
22
,
29
]
.
However, this pattern of dispersion is not universal since cases of reoccupation of the city’s core have also been documented
[
30
,
25
]
.
We propose a model that splits population diffusion into two components:
extensive
, which depends on population size, and
intensive
, which captures processes independent of size (such as housing policies and other elements which are not a direct consequence of population size).
This model provides a good fit for to quantify the shift towards the urban periphery and allows us to estimate the extent to which that process is due to urban agglomeration effects (the extensive component) and how much is due to other potential processes (the intensive component) that are in effect throughout the entire system (e.g., the federal housing and infrastructure policies).

2
Results

Data from four census waves, spanning 1990 to 2020 at 10-year intervals, were used to measure changes in population density distribution and the effects on distances to the urban centre in 69 metropolitan areas of Mexico
[
28
]
.
We used the 2020 definitions of Mexican metropolitan areas
[
31
]
and considered the urban population within the limits of the central urban cluster in 2020.
The urban population within the geographical limits was disaggregated onto a regular grid of
470\times 470
m resolution, allowing us to track population change within each cell over time (details in the Methods Section).
Distance to the city centre is one of the most critical urban form features related to many urban elements, such as transportation, car ownership
[
32
]
, air pollution
[
33
]
, and even patterns of victimisation
[
34
]
.
Through analysing any location in a city based on concentric rings around the centre, we aim to capture the spatial dynamics of dispersion and expansion as these cities grow
[
35
,
36
,
37
]
.
We take the historical point of origin, typically marked by the location where the city was first established, as the city centre (details in Supplementary Note 1). Although there are other candidates to be considered as the city centre, it has little impact for a radial analysis of a city (Supplementary Note 1).
Results show that despite experiencing population growth, all cities lost population near the city centre.
For example, in 1990, approximately 485,000 people lived within 5 km of Monterrey’s centre, but by 2020, this number had decreased to 377,000 (Figure
1
).
A similar phenomenon occurred in Guadalajara, where the population within 5 km of the centre dropped from 866,000 to 626,000 in 30 years.
The central part of Mexico City lost 166,000 people within the same radius during the same period.
Yet, those metropolitan areas –the three largest in Mexico– increased their population during that period by 99%, 71%, and 38%, respectively.

2.1
Population loss in urban centres

Comparing distances between cities of different sizes is problematic, given that more populous cities naturally occupy a larger surface.
For example, the metropolitan area of Mexico City (with 21 million people) has nearly 100 times the population of other cities (such as Chetumal, Guanajuato, and others).
Thus, we scaled distances in cities by considering the
remoteness
, a metric that brings all cities to the same comparable scale, without distorting the differences in compactness and distribution inherent to each city
[
10
]
.

Remoteness scales all distances in
i
by

{r}=1000\frac{s}{\sqrt{P_{i}}},

(1)

where
s
is the radial distance to the centre of
i
and
P_{i}
is the population of the city. The remoteness scales distances in a city, so that it occupies the area of an equivalent city (same internal population distribution, same average density) with a reference population of a million inhabitants.
Remoteness is directly comparable to the distance to the centre in a city of one million people, for which the scaling factor is one.

Remoteness has been classified according to the constructed surface in a city into four intervals, creating four concentric rings
[
10
]
.
Locations with
{r}<3
are considered part of the city’s central zone; those with
{r}\in[3,5)
are the intermediate zone;
{r}\in[5,9.3)
corresponds to distant areas, and locations with
{r}>9.3
are classified as the peri-urban zone.
For each city, we classified locations into these four regions based on their remoteness
{r}
in 2020.
Our results demonstrate that this classification effectively identifies the central areas of cities experiencing population decline.
All cities in Mexico lost population in the central region (
{r}<3
), while all other areas gained population between 1990 and 2020 (Figure
1
f, Figure
2
).
Nationwide, central regions lost 2.5 million people between 1990 and 2020. However, intermediate, distant, and peri-urban areas gained 5.8, 16.1, and 11.7 million, respectively. The drop in terms of the share of the population is even more pronounced. More than 40% of the urban population in 1990 lived in a central area, but it dropped to 22% by 2020 (Figure
1
g). Between 1990 and 2020, the share of the urban population living in a central area decreased by nearly half.

The distribution of people in cities by 1990 is described by a peak in the central region and a decrease further from the city centre.
Yet, by 2020, this distribution changed (Figure

First, population density increases with distance from the centre, peaks in the distant regions, and finally decreases again in the peri-urban areas.

Punctual densities

The difference of these densities between different years,

Despite a rapid population growth between 1990 and 2020, all cities in Mexico became less dense around their centre and more dense away from it.

2.2
Scaling of radial densities

As a city grows, the radial distribution
\rho(s,t)
of its population changes.
These changes can occur due to four factors: births, deaths, migration, and housing relocation.
These changes can lead to a new population distribution that depends on individual decisions and displays self-organising characteristics
[
38
]
, so even if each household chooses its residential location individually in an open market, the overall redistribution of locations shows a general organised pattern observed across many cities (see Supplementary Note 2).
The function
\rho(s,t)
specifies the distribution of radial distances
s
in the city at time
t
, so
\rho(s,t)\,\mathrm{d}s
is the probability of finding a randomly chosen person between
s
and
s+\mathrm{d}s
.
In all cities considered, the updated distribution of the population can be obtained from the old one by stretching along the radial distance (while keeping
\rho
normalised to one).
By characterising this scaling, we can reevaluate the magnitude of population loss in urban central regions and its dynamics (Figure
4
).

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\Phi_{ij}}\rho\left(\frac{s}{\Phi_{ij}},t_{i}\right),

t_{i}

Equation (

\rho=2\pi s\sigma/P
(see Methods Section

\displaystyle\sigma(s,t_{j})

\displaystyle=\frac{P(t_{j})}{P(t_{i})}\frac{1}{\Phi_{ij}^{2}}\sigma\left(\frac{s}{\Phi_{ij}},t_{i}\right),

(3)

where
P(t)
is the population of the city at time

We estimated the scaling factors
\Phi_{ij}

Q(p,t_{j})=\Phi_{ij}Q(p,t_{i})
induced by (

Q(p,t)

When distribution functions for

Whenever

, the distribution

For
\Phi_{ij}<1

This expansion/contraction is proportional to the magnitude of

.

The average radial population density
\bar{\sigma}
follows the same scaling relation (
3

and the

P(t_{j})/P(t_{i})

Consider a disk of radius

with radius

According to equation (

\Phi_{ij}>\sqrt{\frac{P(t_{j})}{P(t_{i})}}

(4)

This result is independent of the city radius, so it applies to disks at different scales, e.g., a disk covering the city centre (

When equation (

In such cases, the density function
\sigma

This boundary divides the regions of density loss and gain in the space of variables

What would a city look like when it grows and preserves the average density?
Given the empirical evidence for scaling and density loss in Mexican cities, it is relevant to compare the observed density with the
counterfactual
case where city’s average population density is preserved.
The scaling factors for Mexican cities are usually larger than those of density-preserving scaling. Still, the magnitude of the loss against the counterfactual informs how many more people have been displaced than needed if residential densities had remained constant on average.
To compute this comparison, we took the observed density
\sigma(s,1990)
as the base density, and scaled it up to
t_{j}=2000,2010,2020
using the observed population counts with
\Phi_{ij}=\sqrt{P(t_{j})/P(t_{i}})
.
We then used these scaled versions to compare the corresponding observed and counterfactual population counts and fractions at each year (Figure
6
).
Between 1990 and 2020, 4.7 million people were displaced from the city centre, nearly double the number in a counterfactual scenario that preserved density.
These people have relocated towards the periphery of the city (Figure
6
).

2.3
Modelling the urban expansion factor

The urban expansion factors
\Phi_{ij}
have similar distributions for the periods of 1990-2000 and 2000-2010, while the period of 2010-2020 is characterised by smaller
\Phi_{ij}
values (Figure
5
).
These smaller
\Phi_{ij}
values correlate with a slower population growth in the period.
The trend in Mexico is for
\Phi_{ij}
to increase with the population growth factor
P(t_{j})/P(t_{i})
, attributing the slowing expansion rates of recent periods mainly to a decrease in population growth.
Further including data for the 20-year intervals 1990-2010 and 2000-2010, and the 30-year interval 1990-2020, shows that
\Phi_{ij}
is log-linearly related to the growth factor
P(t_{j})/P(t_{i})
(Figure
7
), so we fit the following model:

\Phi_{ij}=\left(\frac{P(t_{j})}{P(t_{i})}\right)^{\beta}e^{\alpha(t_{j}-t_{i})},

(5)

which is linear in logarithmic variables

\ln\Phi_{ij}=\beta\ln\left(\frac{P(t_{j})}{P(t_{i})}\right)+\alpha(t_{j}-t_{i}),

(6)

where
\alpha
and
\beta
are parameters, and
\Delta t=t_{j}-t_{i}
is the length of the interval over which
\Phi_{ij}
is evaluated, with observed values of 10, 20 and 30 years.
We obtain (using ordinary least squares) that
\beta=0.60\ (0.54,0.67)
and
\alpha=0.0057\ (0.0043,0.0071)
, with 95% bootstrapped confidence intervals.
The coefficient of determination is
R^{2}=0.82
.
The statistically-significant time-dependent
\alpha\Delta t
term allows for the different intercepts observed for
\Phi_{ij}
corresponding to different
\Delta t
(Figure
7
).
When
\Delta t=0
(
t_{j}=t_{i}
), equation (
6
) correctly predicts
\Phi_{ii}=1
(density distributions should remain invariant). A model without intercept (
\alpha=0
, no time dependence) could also be considered. However, this is rejected (using an F-test), with a reduced
R^{2}=0.78
.

Equation (
5
) contributes in two ways to understanding expansion, an exponential term that depends only on the elapsed time
e^{\alpha\Delta t}
, and a power law term that depends on population growth
(P(t_{j})/P(t_{i}))^{\beta}
, but not on time.
The exponential term captures the city’s expansion due to
intensive
factors, i.e., factors that are not related to population growth, e.g., housing relocation choices responding to job location dynamics.
The power law term captures expansion driven by population growth, i.e.,
extensive
processes.
A positive and statistically significant
\alpha
exponent means that distances in a city have increased over time, even in the absence of population growth.
The estimated value of
\alpha
corresponds to a displacement of 6 meters outside the city centre per year and per kilometre. That means that a location in the population distribution in some city that is 10 kilometres away from the centre in 1990 will be within 11.8 kilometres by 2020.

2.3.1
Scaling of radial distances

Increased distances result in longer commuting times, increased vehicle emissions, and negatively impact overall physical and mental health. To estimate the burden of density loss in the city centre, consider how the mean and total commuting distances change under the model (
15
) and take
\gamma
as the fraction of commuting trips to work in the city centre.
If the city is monocentric, then
\gamma\approx 1
; otherwise, it is generally less than one.
We express the total commuting distance travelled to the city centre as a function of the mean distance
D\approx\gamma P\left<s\right>
.
Under the scaling assumption, the ratio of the actual observed total distance
D
to the distance expected if the city scaled at constant average density from some time
t_{i}
is just the ratio of the observed expected distance at
t_{j}
,
\left<s\right>(t_{j})
, to the counterfactual expected distance
\left<s_{f}\right>(t_{j})
(Scaling of
\left<s\right>(t)
is derived from the general scaling of the moments of
s
in Methods Section
5.6
).
The ratio is given by

\frac{\left<s\right>(t_{j})}{\left<s_{f}\right>(t_{j})}=\left(\frac{P(t_{j})}{P(t_{i})}\right)^{\beta-0.5}e^{\alpha(t_{j}-t_{i})},

(7)

The further
\alpha
and
\beta
are from their density-preserving values,
\beta=0.5
and
\alpha=0
, the faster the distances grow as the average density decreases.
The estimated values for the Mexican cities over the 30 years from
t_{i}=1990
to
t_{j}=2020
, for which the average growth factor is 2.16, give a ratio of 1.28. Thus, distances are 28% larger on average due to density losses in Mexican cities.

2.3.2
Dynamical regimes of the model

Model (
5
) captures different dynamics with varying values of
\alpha
and
\beta
.
Given that a different set of cities (e.g., a different country) may possess a different set of exponents, a discussion of these dynamics is warranted.
To simplify the discussion, we assume positive
\beta
values, i.e., population growth, drives expansion.
There are three different cases to consider depending on the value of
\beta
(Figure
8
).

Figure 8
:

Possible model behaviour for
\Phi_{ij}
(equation (
5
)) for different values of exponent
\beta
.
Different cases enter and leave the density gain region at various points.
The boundary between regions of density loss and gain is given by
\Phi_{ij}=\sqrt{P(t_{j})/P(t_{i})}
.
Full coloured lines refer to models with
\alpha>0
.
Dashed lines refer to models with
\alpha<0

1.

For
\beta=0.5
, population growth does not affect the scaling of
\sigma
, and the model remains parallel to the boundary between density gain and loss regions.
If
\alpha>0
, it is impossible to increase density for any value of population growth.
If
\alpha<0
(so that the contribution of the temporal term corresponds to a compression, opposite to a stretching), density always increases, but we enter the region of urban expansion (
\Phi_{ij}>1
) when growth is large enough (point A), resulting in a combination of expansion and densification.
If
\alpha=0
and
\beta=0.5
, the model coincides with the boundary, and we recover remoteness scaling, so density is preserved for any value of population growth.

2.

If
\beta<0.5
, the power law term favours concentration of population into the existing footprint, and density increases if population growth is large enough for this effect to dominate over the temporal expansion associated with
\alpha
(point C).
Despite the growth in central density, it is also true that
\Phi_{ij}>1
, resulting again in expansion with densification beyond point C.

3.

Finally, if
\beta>0.5
, the density only increases if the city loses population below point B, resulting in densification and compression.
For any value of population growth, the outcome is expansion with density loss.

Note that in all cases, for
\beta>0
, population growth always leads to expansion since in all three cases we end up in the first quadrant with density loss and population loss (Figure
8
). However, moderate population loss may also result in urban expansion (second quadrant) if
\alpha>0
.

The regimes discussed resemble those of sub-diffusion and super-diffusion in physical systems
[
39
]
, with
\beta<0.5
corresponding to sub-diffusion of the population from the centre of the city, so that density is allowed to increase, while
\beta>0.5
corresponds to super-diffusion, where density at the centre is depleted.
Mexican cities have followed super-diffusive dynamics.
Even in the sub-diffusive regime of expansion with densification, existing density valleys at
t_{i}
would grow at
t_{j}
, as the density function stretches away from the centre and the valley’s edge position is displaced. Still, the average density within the valley will increase.
The special case of remoteness,
\beta=0.5,\alpha=0
, corresponds to standard diffusion and preserves average density in the city.

3
Discussion

Over the past three decades, the urban population of Mexico nearly doubled, but the central areas of its cities experienced a rapid population decline. Despite overall demographic growth and urbanisation, these areas have lost approximately 2.5 million residents and nearly 5 million if compared to the density-preserving counterfactual scenario. Historically, population density peaked in the urban cores, but now it peaks several kilometres away from them. This shift places a significant burden on the population, reducing accessibility and provision of services and increasing commuting distances, among other consequences. Here, we developed a model to quantify population loss in urban centres by examining population dynamics, demonstrating that population distribution scales with time and population growth. This characterisation allowed us to compare observed distributions with the counterfactual scenario that maintains scaling at constant density.

The scaling of the radial probability density functions enabled us to identify six distinct population dynamics regarding density loss and gain in relation to urban expansion. Our analysis revealed that the majority of urban centres experienced patterns of density loss, urban expansion and population growth throughout the 30-year study period. By focusing on the probability density, we were able to identify and quantify the diffusive characteristics of population dynamics. Our analyses revealed that in cities, distances are, on average, 28% larger due to density losses.
Urban expansion is an inevitable process that presents challenges related to population health, sustainability, equity, and the economy
[
40
]
. However, with adequate planning, it can create opportunities to manage population growth by fostering compact and diverse urban areas, allowing us to address population and economic needs without encouraging uncontrolled urban sprawl or compromising sustainability
[
41
,
42
]
.

The abandonment of the centre of all cities in the country suggests a self-organising dynamic in growing cities where housing redistribution has pushed people to more remote areas
[
38
]
. While some basic diffusion models have been shown to lead to scaling, these are limited to reproducing exponential density functions
[
26
]
. Yet, we observe here density shifts that such basic models cannot capture. Thus, there is a need for a comprehensive theory that incorporates factors such as housing, transportation, and employment location, along with principles of urban economics and urban scaling, to explain how cities evolve and how their shapes change
[
12
,
43
]
.

In cities across Latin America, factors such as migration, urban expansion, and land use changes influence population change and contribute to varying population distributions
[
40
]
. Our results suggest there is a combination of household relocation from the city centre, with the creation of new residential areas and consolidated urban centres in the city’s periphery
[
44
]
. Results for both processes can be modelled by analysing the distribution of distances from the city centre, where contributions from extensive and intensive urban processes have been linked to the magnitude of the exponents
\beta
and
\alpha
.
Given that the extensive and intensive growth of a city are coupled, the abandonment of central urban areas is the result of various economic, territorial, and demographic factors, along with the suburbanization process influenced by dysfunctional markets.
The observed decline in population in central areas can also be attributed to a lack of affordable housing in these regions, coupled with a shift in consumer preferences toward the periphery, where affordable housing options are more readily available
[
45
,
46
]
.

The magnitude of the
\beta
exponent captures abandonment driven by population growth, including increased demand for non-residential land uses at the centre, disruptions to the housing market due to an increased demand for limited available housing, and the establishment of consolidated urban centres in peripheral regions contributing to the ongoing suburbanization process
[
45
]
. These new centres often emerge around significant employment, shopping, or business hubs where modern infrastructure, services, and facilities have been developed, rendering city centres less appealing to new families and existing residents
[
47
]
.
The magnitude of the
\alpha
exponent captures the centre decline and the suburbanization process due to non-growth-related factors.
These processes are fuelled by the need for more space for urban development, the emergence of new urban centres in peripheral locations, real estate market speculation, the financialisation of housing, changes in housing regulations
[
45
]
, and shifts in consumer preferences. Additionally, these trends were exacerbated by reforms to housing finance agencies in Mexico during the early 1990s
[
48
]
. These reforms enabled private companies to build affordable housing in peripheral areas where land was less expensive, mainly driven by market speculation and a departure from the previously government-led incremental urbanisation approach
[
48
]
.
The effects of these urban processes can be addressed through thoughtful urban planning that strives to contain growth-related scaling through comprehensive housing, transportation, and economic policy strategies that aim to maintain existing population densities.

The functional form for
S\propto P^{\beta}e^{\alpha(t-t_{0})}
is similar to those found in previous urban scaling studies
[
49
,
50
]
, where the scaling exponents
\alpha
and
\beta
can be related to intensive and extensive growth processes.
Extensive processes depend on the population size
P
(agglomeration effects), while intensive processes are independent of
P
.
It has been previously noted that scaling exponents for extensive urban properties
Y
(e.g., GDP or innovation) estimated transversally for a system of cities follow the relation
Y\propto P^{\beta}
, which differs from the scaling exponents of a single city
i
over time,
Y_{i}\propto P^{\alpha_{i}}
; these exponents are also different among individual cities
[
51
,
52
]
.
Typically, the temporal exponent includes contributions from both extensive and intensive processes, which cannot be separated using data from a single city.
By explicitly introducing scaling for both extensive (with
P
) and intensive (with
t
) processes into the definition of
S
, we can simultaneously fit data for the system of cities in both cross-sectional and longitudinal analyses. This approach helps to avoid instabilities in the estimation of temporal exponents.

Our study illustrates that population redistribution in Mexican metropolitan areas follows a systematic diffusion pattern influenced by both extensive and intensive dynamics. By using a radial probability density function and the urban expansion factor, we provide a framework for understanding and comparing urban expansion across space and time in growing metropolitan areas, as well as for exploring urban form in cities that are experiencing population loss and shrinkage. Future research can expand on this framework to examine the causes and impacts of urban scaling on accessibility, sustainability, and population health. The use of the provided framework in urban and transport modelling, planning practices, and policies can help researchers and policymakers anticipate the spatial consequences of population growth and urban sprawl and design evidence-based strategies and interventions that promote more equitable and sustainable urban development.

Methods

5.1
Urban population mesh

Population data for this study are processed from census data by Mexico’s National Institute of Statistics and Geography (INEGI) for 1990, 2000, 2010, and 2020
[
28
]
.
There are other datasets (such as the Global Human Settlement Population (GHS-POP)
[
53
]
and WorldPop
[
54
]
).
However, they are generated by extrapolating census data from a single point in time, which does not allow them to capture demographic redistribution events not considered in the extrapolation model.
Additionally, there are limitations associated with these and similar models, including their methodologies, data sources, and time frames, which result in differences between population estimates and official censuses.
[
55
,
56
,
57
,
58
]
.
Furthermore, the count errors for GHS-POP and WorldPop in Mexican metropolitan areas are significant in some areas and display spatial patterns that disagree with the assumption of random error
[
59
]
.
These systematic variations may introduce bias when analysing intra-urban population dynamics in Mexican metropolises.
To better capture these dynamics, we built a grid of urban population for Mexico, using official population data for 1990, 2000, 2010, and 2020.

Mexico is divided by INEGI using a hierarchical partition of administrative boundaries composed of states, municipalities, localities (urban and rural) and
basic geostatistical areas
AGEBs (equivalent of US census tracts).
Localities are classified as rural or urban according to a population threshold of 2500 inhabitants.
Urban localities are subdivided into urban AGEB, a collection of between 1 and 50 city blocks.
Only the urban population is considered for disaggregation onto the grid.
Population counts for each AGEB are obtained from the Population and Housing Censuses
[
28
]
. AGEBs’ geometries were obtained from the National Geostatistical Framework
[
60
]
(years 2000, 2010, 2020) and the System for Census Information Consultation (SCINCE) (year 1990) provided directly by INEGI upon request.

Geographic grids break down areas into uniform cells.
They enable urban planning, epidemiology, and climate analysis at varying levels of detail, as they provide more granular insights than traditional administrative boundaries
[
61
,
62
,
63
]
.
For this study, the urban grid required the alignment of AGEBs for the years 1990 and 2000.
The official census reference geometries for such years displayed gross translational and deformation errors when compared to the georeferenced geometries of the most current year, 2020.
This misalignment has been noted before, and a correction attempted, with limited success
[
64
]
.
The alignment is not straightforward, given that AGEBs’ codes, which would allow for matching across two censal years, are preserved only for a fraction of the AGEBs.
Codes change for several reasons, including the splitting of the original geometry into new and smaller geometries, the merging of geometries, reclassification into a higher-level administrative unit, and the addition of new AGEBs to the urban class, among others.
As a solution, we developed a custom alignment algorithm that automatically identifies control points between the geometries of consecutive census years.
First, the algorithm identifies intersection points between three or more AGEBs whose code numbers remain constant and designates them as control points.
These control points are generally street intersections unlikely to change from one census year to the next.
Then, the control points are manually inspected, and any erroneous ones are removed.
Finally, geometries are aligned using a thin plate spline transformation
[
65
]
.

In some cases, we perform a manual alignment by building a replacement list, where the AGEB codes from the year being aligned match a set of codes from the following year.
This mapping can be one-to-one, one-to-many, or many-to-one.
Once the list is built, we replace the offending geometries with the new, correct ones.
Once all AGEBs are correctly aligned, their population is disaggregated into INEGI’s National Geostatistical Grid
[
66
]
level 9, at a resolution of approximately 470 m
\times
470 m.
The population corresponding to each cell is the sum of all the AGEBs intersecting it, weighted by the area fraction of each AGEB within the cell.

For each metropolitan area, we restrict the analysis to the circumference that contains the main urban cluster in 2020, defined so that radial density functions in 2020 are non-zero within the circumference and become zero at the boundary.
These core regions contain
\approx 98\%
of the population of all metropolitan zones.

5.2
Defining radial distributions

The distribution of residential population in a city is specified by the collection of position vectors
\{\vec{r}_{i}\mid i=1,\ldots,P\}
where
P
is the population, and
i
indexes every person in the city.
In practice, it is often not possible to perfectly define the vectors
\vec{r}_{i}
, thus a probability density function is assigned to each person
\rho_{i}(\vec{r})
, so that
\rho_{i}(\vec{r})dA
, is the probability of finding person
i
within the element area
dA
.
Probability densities
\rho_{i}
are expected to be peaked around each person’s home location.
They are often defined as constant within some area element (such as a census block or a grid cell) and zero everywhere else.
The expected number of people within element area
dA
around the position
\vec{r}
is then

\sum_{i=1}^{P}\rho_{i}(\vec{r})dA=\sigma(\vec{r})dA,

(8)

where we have defined the two-dimensional population density

\sigma(\vec{r})=\sum_{i=1}^{P}\rho_{i}(\vec{r}),

(9)

as the number of people within a unit element of area (taken here as 1 km
2
).
This population density possesses the expected property that its integral over the city is equal to the total population,
\int_{city}\sigma(\vec{r})dA=P
.
By normalising
\sigma(\vec{r})
by the total population of the city, one obtains the probability density function describing the distribution of all positions within the city,

\rho(\vec{r})dA=\frac{1}{P}\sigma({\vec{r}})dA.

(10)

The probability density
\rho(\vec{r})
can also be interpreted as the probability of finding a randomly chosen person within the element area
dA
.
As a probability density,
\rho(\vec{r})
is independent of the city’s population, but not of its physical extent, so it’s not directly comparable across cities of different sizes without rescaling.

We pick the city centre as a reference point and focus on the radial distribution of people (details in Supplementary Note 1). We express
\sigma(\vec{r})=\sigma(s,\theta)
in polar coordinates, where
s
is used for the radial distance to avoid confusion with remoteness
{r}
.
Let
R
be the maximum distance from the centre, and
\sigma(s)
is the mean population density at
s
(people per km
2
), obtained by integration over
\theta
and the mean value theorem.
A (one-dimensional) radial probability density is

\rho(s)=\frac{2\pi}{P}s\sigma(s).

(11)

This represents the distribution of radial distances in the city, also known as the probability that a randomly selected person is found within a distance
s
and
s+ds
from the city centre.

5.3
Estimating radial distributions

To estimate the radial population density
\sigma(s)
and the radial probability density
\rho(s)
, we built a set of concentric rings of width 100 m around each city’s centre.
We calculated the population within each ring as the weighted sum of the population of all grid cells that intersect the ring, with the weights given by each cell’s area fraction within the ring.

\sigma(s)
is estimated as the population of the ring whose midpoint is located at
s
, divided by the area of the ring.
The probability density
\rho(s)
is obtained from equation (
11
).
It is numerically verified that
\sum_{i=1}^{M}\Delta s\rho(s_{i})\approx 1
, where the sum is over the
M
rings that cover the urban area and
\Delta s=100\ m
is the wing width.

5.4
Delineating the urban area

Mexican metropolises correspond to the latest official definition from 2023
[
31
]
, which considers metropolitan areas, metropolitan municipalities, and conurbated areas. For this analysis, we selected 47 metropolises (out of 48) and all 22 metropolitan municipalities (see Supplementary Note 1 for the complete list).
The metropolitan area of Playa del Carmen was excluded as it did not exist in 1990.
The 2023 definitions are valid for 2020; for years prior to 2020, we use the same total bounding area as defined by the 2020 definitions.
The urban areas included in years before 2020 may consist of areas not integrated into the metropolises at the time, but they allow us to study the temporal evolution of individual metropolises using the same unit of analysis.

The 2023 definition of Mexican metropolises considers the metropolis as a functional urban unit by integrating commuting zones.
The whole area of the municipalities that contain urban populations is considered part of the metropolis, even if any such municipality is urbanised only over a small fraction of its area.
This leads to large areas of effectively empty rural space being considered part of the metropolis, over which radial functions are zero.
Since scaling is observed near the urban core and always within the main urban cluster, we truncate all radial functions at the first zero occurrence and renormalise all probability densities to one.
This truncation makes the comparison of radial functions easier and removes the influence of remote commuting zones.
Remote locations are not expected to influence the diffusion dynamics at the urban core, since their dynamics are expected to be more heavily constrained by their historical development and the area’s natural geography.
After truncation, we retain approximately 98% of the 2020 population.

5.5
Empirical scaling of
\rho

The radial density function at year
t_{i}
, expressed as
\rho(s,t_{i})
, changes by year
t_{j}
, according to the equation

\rho(s,t_{j})=\frac{1}{\Phi_{ij}}\rho\left(\frac{s}{\Phi_{ij}},t_{i}\right),

(12)

where
\Delta t=t_{j}-t_{i}
can be one of
\{10,20,30\}
, corresponds to one tuple in the set
(t_{i},t_{j})\in
{(1990, 2000), (2000, 2010), (2010, 2020), (1990, 2010), (1990, 2020), (2000, 2020)}, and where
\Phi_{ij}
is a scaling factor that stretches or compresses
\rho
along the horizontal axis by scaling the radial distances
s
.
Equation (
12
) is the only valid scaling transformation for
\rho
, since it preserves the normalisation of the probability density.
An equivalent expression for
\sigma(s,t)
results from equation (
11
),

\sigma(s,t_{j})=\frac{P(t_{j})}{P(t_{i})}\frac{1}{\Phi_{ij}^{2}}\sigma\left(\frac{s}{\Phi_{ij}},t_{i}\right).

(13)

Scaling according to equation (
12
) would appear as a perfect line in a quantile-quantile plot of
\rho(s,t_{i})
and
\rho(s,t_{j})
, with
\Phi_{ij}
being the slope of such a line, so that the following relation holds,

Q_{j}(p)=\Phi_{ij}Q_{i}(p),

(14)

where
Q_{i}(p)
is the quantile function of
\rho(s,t)
, defined from

\int_{0}^{Q(p)}\rho(s)ds=p
.
In practice, scaling is observed in a region near
s=0
, where a line fits the data well, using quantiles calculated at
\Delta p=0.01
intervals from
p=0.01
to
p=1.00
.

To estimate the scaling factors
\Phi_{ij}
, we performed Theil-Sen regression with no intercept on the quantiles from
\rho(s,t_{i})
and
\rho(s,t_{j})
up to the quantile that covers the regions of density loss for all cities between 1990 and 2020 (the value of
p=0.38
was obtained as the maximum quantile at which density loss is observed, which occurs in Coatzacoalcos).
Theil-Sen slopes are the slopes between all points considered in the regression and the origin.
The Theil-Sen estimate for
\Phi_{ij}
is the median slope.
Theil-Sen regression is chosen for its robustness against deviations from scaling at quantiles observed far from the origin.
These deviations cause standard regression to fail to fit the scaling relation where it is stronger (more linear), i.e., around the centre.
The mean
R^{2}
value for the regressions is 0.99, signalling an excellent linear fit.
Q-Q plots for every city, along with the regression estimates, can be found in Supplementary Note 3.

5.6
Scaling of radial moments

We redefine the relation (
2
) to a base distribution at
t=t_{0}
(e.g. 1990), so that the density at time
t
is given by

\rho\left(s,t\right)=\frac{1}{S(t)}\rho\left(\frac{s}{S(t)},t_{0}\right),

(15)

where
S(t)=\Phi_{0t}
depends on time and
S(t_{0})=1
.
From the expression (
15
), the original period-specific scaling factors can be written as

\Phi_{ij}=\frac{S(t_{j})}{S(t_{i})}.

(16)

Using the model equation (
5
), equation (
15
) is a diffusion-like relation, with

S(t)=\frac{1}{P(t_{0})^{\beta}e^{\alpha t_{0}}}P^{\beta}(t)e^{\alpha t}=K_{0}P^{\beta}(t)e^{\alpha t},

(17)

with
K_{0}^{-1}=P(t_{0})^{\beta}e^{\alpha t_{0}}
and moments for
s
are given by

\left<s^{n}\right>(t)=S^{n}(t)\left<s^{n}\right>_{0}=\left<s^{n}\right>_{0}K_{0}^{n}P^{n\beta}(t)e^{n\alpha t},

(18)

where
\left<s^{n}\right>_{0}
is the
n
-th moment at
t_{0}
.

For
n=2
, the term
\left<s^{2}\right>_{0}K_{0}^{2}
takes a similar role to that of an anomalous diffusion coefficient, specifying the base rate at which population diffuses with
P
and
t
.

Supplementary Information 1
Identifying the city centre and the central parts

We identify the city centre as the historical point where each city initially developed. While multiple locations could serve as the city’s centre, the main objective is to identify remote areas, for which the exact position of the centre is of lesser importance
[
67
]
. Studies have shown that defining the city centre as the city hall, for example, and then shifting this point to other locations, has a minor effect on radial analyses of the city
[
68
]
. Thus, while the exact definition of the city centre may be ambiguous, it has little influence on which areas are classified as remote or periurban.
Centres identified for the 69 metropolitan areas in Mexico are listed in Supplementary Table
1
.

A technique to evaluate the relevance of the definition of a city centre is to quantify the overlap between different central areas
[
10
]
. That means, to measure the extent to which the rings with
R_{i,j}\leq 3
intersect. A high degree of overlap indicates that, regardless of the definition used, the delineation of central, inter-urban, distant, or peri-urban areas remains broadly consistent. Across many cities, it was observed that the overlap of the central region is roughly 90 to 95%
[
10
]
, meaning that although the precise location of the centre of a city might be ambiguous, it has a small impact on the overall radial analysis of a city.

Supplementary Information 2
Cities as a self-organising object

A self-organising object is an object or system that can autonomously arrange its internal structure or behaviour without external control. It uses local interactions among its parts to create an overall organised pattern or function, often adapting dynamically to changing conditions. Cities can also be observed as a self-organising system
[
38
]
. Through countless decisions (for example, where people build, work, trade, and gather), they develop complex patterns, infrastructure, and social networks without a central planner controlling every detail.

For example, segregation has been observed to emerge as a complex process, driven often by housing relocation within a city
[
69
]
. Although segregation is frequently perceived as an attribute related to poverty, in some cities, the rich voluntarily segregate themselves to avoid interactions with the rest of the population
[
70
]
.
