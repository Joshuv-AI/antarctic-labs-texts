Valorization of kiwifruit branches as plant-derived aroma materials: HS-GC-IMS-guided volatile profiling and aroma reconstruction
Authors: Lili Zhao, Na Li, Yixin Zhang, Zhenbin Liu, Lishan Yao, Lu Tian, Yangyang Jia, Haizhen Mo, Hongbo Li, Liangbin Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractKiwifruit branches are abundant pruning residues with potential as plant-derived aroma materials. This study evaluated their feasibility for reconstructing the volatile profile of a commercial silver vine-associated aroma reference. Six kiwifruit branch samples and one commercial reference sample (G7) were analyzed using HS-GC-IMS. A total of 64 volatile compounds were tentatively annotated, and multivariate analysis showed that G7 had a distinct volatile profile compared with the individual branch samples. Twenty-six differential volatiles selected based on VIP > 1 and P < 0.05 were grouped into four odor-based aroma modules for blending optimization. A binary blend of Jintao and Jinyan branches at a ratio of 0.69:0.31 showed the highest module-level similarity to G7. ROAV analysis further identified eight key odor-active differential compounds associated with the characteristic aroma profile of G7. These results indicate that selected kiwifruit branches have potential as aroma materials for the reference-guided reconstruction of commercial silver vine-associated volatile profiles.Keywords: Kiwifruit branches, Pruning residues, Valorization, Aroma, Blending, Volatiles

Introduction

With growing demand for environmental enrichment and functional pet products, plant-derived aroma materials associated with feline behavioral responses have attracted increasing commercial interest. Materials such as catnip and silver vine can elicit characteristic behavioral responses in cats, including approaching, sniffing, rubbing, licking, biting, and rolling (Bol et al., 2022; Uenoyama et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2019). Previous studies have shown that these responses are closely associated with specific bioactive plant-derived volatiles, including nepetalactol, actinidine, isodihydronepetalactone, dihydronepetalactone, and related iridoids (Obara et al., 2024; Uenoyama et al., 2022). However, commercially available feline-attractant products often show considerable variation in quality, unstable bioactive volatile profiles, and limited standardization. Kiwifruit branches are abundant pruning residues generated during orchard management and are available from diverse sources. At present, however, they are generally treated as agricultural waste (Chamorro et al., 2022). Branches from different kiwifruit cultivars contain diverse volatile compounds (Garcia et al., 2012). Because kiwifruit and silver vine both belong to the family Actinidiaceae, kiwifruit branches may represent a promising plant-derived aroma resource for reconstructing silver vine-associated volatile profiles (Jia et al., 2024; Lan et al., 2021). Systematic characterization of their volatile composition and potential applications may therefore provide a new route for expanding the range of plant-derived aroma materials while promoting the valorization of pruning residues.

Gas chromatography–ion mobility spectrometry (GC–MS), which provides rapid analysis, high sensitivity, and intuitive fingerprint visualization, has been widely applied to characterizing food and plant aromas, monitor processing-related changes, and screening differential volatiles (Bai et al., 2023; Jia et al., 2024; Li et al., 2024). However, the development of target aroma products should not be limited to the identification of differential compounds (Cao et al., 2022). In recent years, flavor blending models based on similarity evaluation and linear assumptions have been used to optimize complex food flavor profiles. For instance, Chen et al. combined FA-TOPSIS with a linear assumption-based algorithm to screen candidate samples and determine blending proportions to reproduce a target green tea taste profile, thereby transforming a complex sensory system into a quantifiable optimization problem (Chen et al., 2026). Nevertheless, previous studies on plant-derived materials associated with feline behavioral responses have mainly focused on identifying bioactive components and evaluating behavioral effects. Less attention has been given to whether fruit-tree pruning residues, such as kiwifruit branches, can be developed as aroma materials through reference-guided volatile profiling and blending optimization.

This study aimed to evaluate the feasibility of valorizing kiwifruit branches as plant-derived aroma materials for reconstructing the volatile profile of a commercial silver vine-associated aroma reference sample (G7). HS-GC-IMS was used to characterize the volatile fingerprints of six kiwifruit branch samples and G7. Differential aroma-associated volatiles were screened by integrating orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) and significance testing, while relative odor activity value (ROAV) analysis was used to evaluate their potential odor contributions. Odor-based aroma modules were then constructed to describe the target volatile profile, and a linear blending model was used to optimize the proportions of candidate branch samples. The optimized blend was further assessed in an exploratory cat behavioral assay to compare its response pattern with that of G7. These results provide a basis for the valorization of kiwifruit pruning residues as plant derived aroma resources and for the reference-guided reconstruction of complex volatile profiles.

Materials and methods

Sample collection and preparation

Branches from six major kiwifruit cultivars grown in Shaanxi Province, China, were selected as candidate plant-derived aroma materials, including Cui Xiang (G1), Jin Tao (G2), Jin Yan (G3), Qin Mei (G4), Xu Xiang (G5), and Zhonghua (G6). Fresh and healthy branches without visible mechanical damage, decay, or disease symptoms were collected during the pruning period. After collection, the samples were immediately transported to the laboratory, where surface debris was removed. The branches were then cut into small pieces, ground, sealed, and stored at −20 °C until analysis. G7 was a commercial silver vine-based cat chew stick purchased under the Miaoxuan brand. According to the available product information, the sample was produced in China, listed silver vine as the raw material, and was sold in stick form. Before analysis, G7 was cut into small pieces, ground, homogenized, sealed and stored at low temperature under the same conditions as the kiwifruit branch samples. All samples were subsequently subjected to HS-GC-IMS analysis.

Analysis of volatile compounds using HS-GC-IMS

Volatile compounds in all samples were analyzed using the HS-GC-IMS technique (Ren et al., 2025). A 1.0 g portion of each ground sample was placed in a 20 mL headspace vial, with three replicates prepared for each sample, and 10 μL of 8.16 mg/L 2-methyl-3-heptanone solution was added as an internal standard. The samples were incubated at 60 °C and 500 rpm for 15 min, and then 500 μL of the headspace gas was withdrawn using the autosampler at 85 °C and introduced in non-flow injection mode. The separation and detection of volatile components were performed using a FlavourSpec® gas chromatography-ion mobility spectrometer. The instrument was operated according to the method of Denawaka et al. (2014). An MXT-5 capillary column (15 m × 0.53 mm × 1.0 μm) was used for separation, with the column temperature maintained at 60 °C. High-purity nitrogen (≥ 99.999%) was used as the carrier gas, with the following flow rate program: 2.0 mL/min for the initial 2 min, linearly increased to 10.0 mL/min over 8 min, then to 100.0 mL/min over 10 min, and finally to 150.0 mL/min over 5 min. The total run time was 25 min. The injection port temperature was set at 80 °C. Volatile compounds were tentatively annotated using VOCal software by comparing their retention indices and drift times with the built-in GC retention-index and IMS drift time databases. Retention indices were calculated using a retention time–retention index calibration curve generated from six n-ketone standards (C4–C9) analyzed under the same chromatographic conditions. The available annotation parameters, including compound name, CAS number, retention index, retention time, drift time, and peak area, are provided in Table S1. When the same compound appeared as monomer, dimer, or other cluster-ion signals, the corresponding peak areas were summed and treated as a single parent compound in subsequent statistical analyses.

Multivariate analysis and ROAV calculation

To evaluate the odor contribution of volatile compounds, the ROAV was calculated. The odor threshold values used for ROAV calculation were obtained from Compilations of Odor Threshold Values in Air, Water and Other Media (van Gemert, 2011). The odor activity value (OAV) of each compound was first calculated as OAVi = Ci/OTi, where Ci is the relative content of compound i, and OTi is its odor threshold in water. The identities of the compounds were verified using CAS numbers and synonyms. FlavorDB2 provides integrated information on flavor-related compounds, including sensory attributes and chemical properties, and was used to assist in the systematic characterization of the odor profiles of the volatile compounds (Goel et al., 2024). Following the principle of grouping odor attributes according to semantic similarity in sensory descriptive analysis, the differential volatile compounds were classified into four odor-based aroma modules by integrating established odor lexicons with the predominant odor descriptors of each compound. Compounds associated with descriptors such as green, grass-like, leafy, vegetative, fresh, cucumber-like, and green bean-like were assigned to the Green leafy-twig module (Hongsoongnern & Chambers IV, 2008). Compounds characterized by floral, herbal, fruity, sweet, citrus-like, pineapple-like, and ester-like descriptors were categorized into the Herbal-floral module. Compounds with woody, earthy, mushroom-like, balsamic, and almond-like odor characteristics were classified into the Woody-earthy module (Dudareva et al., 2013). Compounds exhibiting roasted, nutty, sulfurous, acidic, fermented, buttery, caramel-like, and pungent descriptors were grouped into the Characteristic-supporting module (Drake, 2007).

Behavioral response comparison

The behavioral response comparison was conducted according to previously reported methods for evaluating feline responses to plant-derived attractant materials, such as catnip and silver vine (Uenoyama et al., 2021). Six healthy indoor domestic cats aged 1–2 years were included in the assay, including two 2-year-old neutered male domestic shorthair cats with orange tabby coats and four 1-year-old Ragdoll cats, consisting of two neutered males and two spayed females. None of the cats had previous exposure to plant-derived feline-responsive materials, such as catnip or silver vine, before the experiment.

A randomized crossover design was used to compare three treatments: blank control (CK), commercial target sample (G7), and the predicted blend. Each cat was exposed to all three treatments in a randomized order. All samples were placed in identical breathable sachets with the same mass. In each test, one sachet was placed in the test area, and the cat was allowed to move freely for 10 min. The test area was cleaned between trials to reduce potential carry-over effects. Two behavioral variables were recorded: total response duration and latency to first contact. Total response duration was defined as the cumulative time spent showing typical positive responses, including continuous sniffing, facial rubbing, rolling, licking, or biting the sachet. Latency to first contact was defined as the time from sample presentation to the first active approach and physical contact with the sachet. All animal procedures were reviewed and approved by the Experimental Animal Welfare and Ethics Committee of Henan Institute of Science and Technology (No. LLSC2026101).

Statistical analysis

The raw HS-GC-IMS data were processed using VOCal software, and three-dimensional spectra, two-dimensional spectra, difference spectra and fingerprint spectra were generated using the Reporter and Gallery Plot plugins. Experimental data were analyzed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) in SPSS 26.0, and PCA and OPLS-DA were performed using SIMCA 14.1. Figures were generated using TBtools, Origin 2021 and Python. Behavioral data, including total response duration and latency to first contact, were analyzed separately using nonparametric tests for repeated measures. Differences among CK, G7, and the predicted blend were assessed using the Friedman test, followed by pairwise Wilcoxon signed-rank tests with Bonferroni adjustment. Results and discussion

GC-IMS spectral characteristics of volatile compounds in kiwifruit branches and G7

As shown in Fig. 1A, the GC–IMS 3D topographic plots revealed clear differences among G1–G7 in signal number, distribution, and intensity, reflecting marked variations in volatile compound composition and signal abundance. Most signals were distributed within retention times (Rt) of 100–900 s and drift times (Dt) of 1.0–1.8 ms. The two-dimensional (2D) top-view plots (Fig. 1B) further confirmed that, despite a generally similar overall fingerprint among samples, pronounced regional differences were evident in signal intensity and spot distribution. Several compounds were detected in both monomeric and dimeric forms, which is typical in GC–IMS spectra (Song et al., 2024). Notably, G7 showed distinct signal patterns relative to G1–G6 in several regions, indicating a more differentiated volatile fingerprint. The difference plots generated using G1 as the reference (Fig. 1C) provided a clearer visualization of between-group differences. Red, blue, and white regions indicated higher, lower, and comparable signal intensities relative to G1, respectively. G2–G5 exhibited highly similar differential patterns, whereas G6 and especially G7 showed more obvious deviations, particularly within Dt values of 1.5–2.0 ms. These results confirm that the volatile profile of G7 differed more markedly from those of the kiwifruit branch samples.

Comparison of volatile fingerprints and analysis of characteristic regions

To characterize the distribution of volatile compounds across the samples, volatile compounds were further tentatively annotated by comparing their retention indices, retention times, and drift times with the VOCal GC-IMS library. As shown in Fig. 1D, each signal point represents a detected volatile feature, and the annotated signals were assigned to corresponding volatile compounds when matching library entries were available. In total, 94 signal peaks were detected, of which 64 were tentatively annotated as volatile compounds, while the remaining peaks were retained as unidentified signals. Among the volatiles tentatively annotated in G7, actinidine and feline-responsive iridoid compounds reported in previous GC–MS-based studies of silver vine, such as nepetalactol, nepetalactone, isodihydronepetalactone, and dihydronepetalactone, were not annotated in the present HS-GC-IMS dataset (Uenoyama et al., 2023). HS-GC-IMS is suitable for rapid volatile fingerprinting and tentative annotation through comparison of retention indices and drift times with GC-IMS library data, but it does not provide mass-spectral confirmation of compound identity. Therefore, the present method was not intended as a targeted approach for detecting these feline-responsive compounds. Accordingly, the subsequent analysis focused on the major annotated volatile compounds of G7 and their odor-based aroma modules, rather than on reconstructing the actinidine- or iridoid-associated bioactive compound profile.

A gallery plot was then constructed to visualize the volatile fingerprints of the different samples (Fig. 1E), in which darker colors indicate higher relative signal intensities (M. Li et al., 2019). According to the distribution patterns of the volatile signals, five characteristic regions (A–E) were visually delineated. Region A contained compounds such as linalool, 2-pentylfuran, 1-pentanol, 2-methylbutanal, and 3-methylbutanal, primarily comprising alcohols, aldehydes, and a furan, and represented the main differential region among the samples. Region B was mainly associated with compounds including 2-pentanone, methylbutyl propanoate, and 2-methylbutyl butanoate. Although this region covered a relatively narrow range, the differences were highly concentrated, suggesting that a small number of compounds contributed strongly to sample differentiation. Region C mainly reflected variations in medium-retention volatiles, involving compounds such as 3-methyl-2-butenal, 2-methylbutanol, phenylacetaldehyde, and (Z)-3-hexenol, and several kiwifruit branch samples showed localized signal patterns similar to those of G7 in this region. Region D primarily comprised aldehydes and ketones, including (E)-2-heptenal, (Z)-4-heptenal, 1-penten-3-one, and 1-octen-3-one. Region E mainly corresponded to several low- to medium-abundance volatiles, such as linalool oxide, dimethyl disulfide, and furfural.

Taken together, G7 did not completely overlap with any single kiwifruit branch sample, but instead exhibited a distinctive volatile fingerprint across multiple characteristic regions. These results indicate that the target volatile profile could not be adequately represented by a single branch sample and may instead require complementary contributions from multiple kiwifruit branch sources.

Composition and distribution characteristics of volatile compounds

HS-GC-IMS tentatively annotated 64 volatile compounds, including 1 acid, 10 alcohols, 18 aldehydes, 9 esters, 4 furans/lactones/O-heterocycles, 14 ketones, 3 N/S-containing compounds, and 5 terpenes/terpenoids (Fig. 2). Overall, aldehydes, ketones, and alcohols dominated the volatile profiles of all groups, together accounting for more than 85% of the total annotated peak area, which is broadly consistent with the general compositional pattern reported for plant-derived materials (L.-N. Sun et al., 2025). Among G1–G6, aldehydes remained the predominant class, ranging from 39.22% to 45.93%, with the highest proportions observed in G2 and G5 at 45.93% and 44.01%, respectively (Fig. 2A–G). This pattern is typical of plant tissues, in which C6–C9 aldehydes derived from lipid oxidation are major contributors to green and fresh odor notes (Loreto & Schnitzler, 2010; ul Hassan et al., 2015). In contrast, ketones and alcohols showed greater variation among samples. G7 exhibited a more distinct class-level profile than G1–G6. Its ketone proportion reached 40.45%, the highest among all samples, whereas the proportion of alcohols decreased to 11.34%, the lowest among all groups. In addition, the proportions of esters and furans/lactones/O-heterocycles reached 6.28% and 5.53%, respectively, both of which were generally higher than those in most candidate groups. Accordingly, the characteristic profile of G7 was not defined by the enrichment of a single chemical class, but rather by a redistribution centered on elevated ketones and reduced alcohols, together with the participation of esters and minor heterocyclic compounds. Among the candidate branch samples, G3 showed a relatively similar class-level pattern, with a high ketone proportion (34.38%), a high aldehyde contribution (41.49%), and a low alcohol proportion (15.51%). Although G2 remained aldehyde-dominant, its overall volatile-class composition was also relatively close to that of G7. These results suggest that the G7-associated volatile profile cannot be adequately matched by any single kiwifruit branch sample, but may be more effectively reconstructed through complementary blending of different candidate samples at the volatile class level (Pan et al., 2014).

In addition to class-level composition, comparison of the total peak areas revealed differences in the overall volatile signal abundance among samples (Fig. 2H). G3 exhibited the highest total peak area among G1–G6, followed by G2 and G1, whereas G6 showed the lowest overall signal abundance. The G7 reference sample displayed an intermediate total peak area, indicating that similarity to the target volatile profile depends not only on overall signal abundance but also on the relative contributions of different volatile classes and odor-active compounds. Although HS-GC-IMS provided rapid volatile fingerprinting and library-assisted annotation, compound identification based on retention indices, retention times, drift times, and database matching remained tentative. Further confirmation with authentic standards or an orthogonal method such as GC–MS is still needed, especially for key differential and odor-active compounds.

Multivariate statistical analysis and screening of differential volatiles

PCA, HCA, and OPLS-DA were performed to further evaluate differences in the volatile profiles of G7 and G1–G6 (Fig. 3). The PCA and HCA results indicated that G7 was clearly separated from G1–G6 in terms of volatile characteristics (Fig. 3A and B). The first two principal components explained 43.8% and 22.3% of the total variance, respectively. The replicates within each group were well clustered, and G7 exhibited a relatively distinct volatile profile in the principal component space. The HCA results were consistent with the PCA pattern and further supported the differentiation of G7 from the candidate branch samples. This finding is consistent with previous reports showing that GC-IMS combined with PCA and HCA can effectively distinguish volatile profiles among different food samples (Duan et al., 2023; Zhu et al., 2023).

Based on these results, an OPLS-DA model was constructed to distinguish G7 from G1–G6 and to screen differential volatiles (Fig. 3C). The results showed that the two types of samples were clearly distinguished in the supervised discrimination model. The intercepts of the 200-permutation test were R2 = 0.153 and Q2 = −0.651, respectively, indicating that the model was not obviously overfitted (Fig. 3D). Thirty volatile compounds had VIP > 1. Variables with VIP > 1 are generally considered important contributors to class discrimination in OPLS-DA models (Mahieu et al., 2023). After combining this criterion with significance testing (P < 0.05), 26 differential volatile compounds were retained (Fig. 3E and F).

ROAV analysis of major odor-active compounds

ROAV analysis was further applied to the 26 differential volatiles previously screened by VIP > 1 and P < 0.05 to identify the major odor-active compounds associated with G7 (Ye, Gui, et al., 2025). Compounds with ROAV ≥1 are generally considered key aroma contributors (Yang et al., 2024). In G7, eight compounds showed ROAV ≥1, namely (Z)-4-heptenal, (E,E)-2,4-heptadienal, 1-penten-3-one, 1-hexanol, 2-ethyl-3,5-dimethylpyrazine, (Z)-2-pentenol, 1-octen-3-ol, and linalool oxide. Among them, (Z)-4-heptenal showed the highest ROAV value (100.00), indicating the greatest relative odor contribution among the compounds evaluated (Table 2). This agrees with previous reports that low-threshold unsaturated aldehydes often play a central role in green, fatty, and pungent odor perception (Ye, Huang, et al., 2025). (E,E)-2,4-heptadienal also maintained a relatively high ROAV, further indicating the importance of unsaturated aldehydes in the characteristic volatile profile of G7.

Model-based blending optimization using odor-based aroma modules

To balance the discriminatory capacity of the selected variables with the completeness of the subsequent blending model, 26 differential volatile compounds that simultaneously met VIP > 1 and P < 0.05 were selected as the basis for odor-based aroma module construction. Unlike the approach of screening only a few major odor-active compounds based on ROAV ≥1, these 26 compounds not only effectively characterized the differences between G7 and candidate samples but also retained less dominant components that may contribute to the overall aroma profile, making them more suitable for the structured reconstruction of the target volatile profile. On this basis, according to the reported odor descriptors and the module assignment criteria described in Section 2.4.1 and Table 1, the compounds in Table 2 were classified into four odor-based aroma modules: Characteristic-supporting, Herbal-floral, Green leafy-twig, and Woody-earthy. Accordingly, the blending model in this study was established to approximate the major annotated volatile profile of G7 at the aroma-module level, whereas the possible contribution of actinidine- or iridoid-related feline-responsive compounds, if present, remain to be clarified through targeted chemical analysis and behavioral evaluation. This strategy integrated discrete volatiles into more interpretable structural aroma units, reducing the impact of fluctuations in individual compounds on the model and improving the practicality of reference-guided blending optimization (Shui et al., 2019). Nevertheless, these modules should be regarded as literature-assisted odor-based groupings rather than categories validated by sensory panels, and further validation using trained sensory panels or aroma recombination experiments is still needed.

As shown in Fig. 4A, all samples (G1–G7) contained the same four odor-based aroma modules, but their relative module proportions varied markedly. Based on aroma-module similarity to G7, G2 and G3 showed the highest composite scores among the individual candidate branch samples, with values of 0.998 and 0.986, respectively, whereas G6 showed the lowest similarity score (Fig. 4B). This result indicated that G2 and G3 were the closest individual branch samples to the commercial aroma reference at the aroma-module level. On this basis, all 20 possible ternary combinations of G1–G6 were subjected to constrained optimization and ranked using SSE, Euclidean distance, cosine similarity, and mean aroma-module deviation. Under the original weighting scheme, the top five optimized blending schemes are shown in Fig. 4C. The highest composite score was obtained for the G2 + G3 + G5 combination, followed by G2 + G3 + G6, G2 + G3 + G4, G1 + G2 + G3, and G1 + G2 + G5. Notably, G2 and G3 were consistently present in the top-ranked schemes, indicating their central role in approximating the aroma-module profile of G7 (Table 3).

To determine whether the ranking was dependent on the selected weighting scheme, a sensitivity analysis was performed by recalculating the composite scores using alternative weight combinations. As shown in Table S2 the top-five optimized blending schemes remained unchanged across the original, equal-weight, lower-cosine-weight, and higher-cosine-weight schemes. This result indicates that the optimized blend selection was robust to changes in the weighting strategy and was not driven by a single arbitrary weight setting. Although the top-ranked scheme was generated from a ternary combination, the optimized coefficient of the third component converged to zero under the non-negative constraint, resulting in a boundary solution corresponding to a binary blend. Therefore, the final predicted blend was composed of G2 and G3 at an optimized ratio of approximately 0.69:0.31. The aroma-module proportion curve of this predicted blend was highly similar to that of G7, with only slight deviations in the Herbal-floral and Green leafy-twig modules (Fig. 4D). The Euclidean distance between the predicted blend and G7 was 0.0439, which was lower than that of any individual candidate sample. These results indicate that the G2:G3 blend provided the closest module-level approximation of the volatile profile of the commercial reference among the tested branch samples and blending schemes.

Behavioral response comparison

Behavioral responses to CK, G7, and the predicted blend were compared using total response duration within 10 min and latency to first contact (Fig. 4E and F). For total response duration, both G7 and the predicted blend produced significantly longer responses than CK, whereas no significant difference was observed between G7 and the predicted blend (Fig. 4E). For latency to first contact, CK showed a significantly longer latency than both G7 and the predicted blend, while G7 and the predicted blend did not differ significantly from each other (Fig. 4F). These results indicate that, under the present experimental conditions, the predicted blend produced a behavioral response pattern similar to that observed for G7, although the absence of a significant difference should not be interpreted as evidence of behavioral equivalence. This pattern is generally consistent with previous research on catnip- and silver vine-related materials, where cats typically exhibit active approach, rubbing, rolling, licking, and chewing behaviors in response to plant-derived olfactory stimuli, whereas control materials elicit minimal to no such responses (Uenoyama et al., 2021). Feline responses to silver vine and catnip are closely associated with typical iridoid compounds, including nepetalactol, nepetalactone, isodihydronepetalactone, and dihydronepetalactone. Beyond these canonical iridoids, structurally distinct volatile compounds such as actinidine and dihydroactinidiolide have also been reported to induce catnip-like responses in some cats (Bol et al., 2022; Tay et al., 2026), indicating that feline behavioral reactions may not be restricted to specific iridoid profiles. Therefore, the responses observed in this study may reflect the integrated olfactory effects of the reconstructed volatile profile and/or contributions from feline-responsive compounds that were not annotated by HS-GC-IMS. These observations provide preliminary behavioral support for the relevance of the optimized blend under the tested conditions. However, the specific chemical basis underlying the observed responses remains unclear. Future studies with larger sample sizes, appropriate positive controls containing established feline-responsive compounds, and targeted GC–MS analysis are needed to clarify the bioactive components of the reconstructed aroma and their relationship to feline behavior.

Conclusion

This study established an HS-GC-IMS-guided approach for evaluating kiwifruit branches as plant-derived aroma resources for reconstructing the volatile profile of a commercial silver vine-associated aroma reference sample (G7). Among the tested branch samples, G2 and G3 exhibited the highest aroma-module similarity to G7. Based on 26 differential volatiles identified using VIP > 1 and P < 0.05, four odor-based aroma modules were constructed and used to compare aroma structures and optimize blending proportions. The optimized binary blend of G2 and G3 at a ratio of 0.69:0.31 showed the greatest module-level similarity to G7 and a lower Euclidean distance than any individual branch sample, supporting the complementary contributions of G2 and G3 to the target volatile profile. ROAV analysis further identified eight major odor-active differential compounds in G7, providing a more specific basis for interpreting the characteristic aroma of the commercial reference. In the preliminary behavioral evaluation, the optimized blend produced an aroma-associated response pattern broadly similar to that observed for G7 under the tested conditions. Taken together, these results indicate that selected kiwifruit pruning residues have potential as aroma materials for reference-guided volatile profile reconstruction. Nevertheless, the present analysis was limited to the major volatile compounds tentatively annotated by HS-GC-IMS. The identities and contributions of key odor-active compounds should therefore be confirmed using authentic standards and orthogonal GC–MS analysis. In particular, the possible roles of known feline-responsive compounds, including actinidine and iridoid derivatives, remain to be determined. Further studies should also evaluate volatile yield, extraction efficiency, raw-material variability, process scalability, and economic feasibility before practical application.
