Efficient Catalytic Ozonation of Dimethyl Carbonate on M‑Mn (M = Cr, Cu, Co)/ZSM‑5 Zeolites: Further Analysis of Reaction Mechanism
Authors: Yulin Sun, Yiwei Zhang, Yong He, Wubin Weng, Yanqun Zhu, Peixi Liu, Zhihua Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Mn-based catalysts usually exhibit considerable potential for enhancing the catalytic ozonation of volatile organic compounds (VOCs) at a lower temperature. In this study, bimetallic oxides M-Mn (M = Cr, Cu, Co) were supported on HZSM-5 zeolites to degrade dimethyl carbonate (DMC, chemical formula C3H6O3), a typical VOC emitted during the productive process of lithium batteries, with Mn/ZSM-5 for comparison. The Cr-doped catalyst CrMn/ZSM-5 could achieve 92% DMC conversion with 91% CO2 selectivity (O3/DMC = 6, T = 120 °C), showing an excellent catalytic performance among the prepared catalysts. The exceptionally low-temperature activity of CrMn/ZSM-5 was attributed to the synergistic effect of its large surface area, sufficient oxygen vacancies, and suitable acidity and reducibility, which collectively enhanced the level of O3 activation and accelerated DMC degradation. Furthermore, the catalyst demonstrated desirable stability and resistance to varying concentrations of water vapor. To elucidate the reaction pathway, in situ DRIFTS was employed to track the evolution of intermediate species during the adsorption and catalytic ozonation of DMC. With a further study of DFT calculation, the reaction mechanism of DMC catalytic ozonation on CrMn/ZSM-5 catalysts was put forward.

Introduction

The rising release of volatile organic compounds (VOCs) due to industrial growth has led to severe air pollution issues. Additionally, VOCs are not only hazardous to the human body and ecological environment but also related to the generation of photochemical smog, which is a key precursor to ozone and particulate matter formation. , Therefore, the development of new types of innovation techniques to reduce VOC emissions economically and effectively has become essential and urgent.

Many treatment methods have been adopted for VOC degradation, including adsorption, membrane separation, photocatalytic oxidation, biocatalytic oxidation, catalytic oxidation, and so on. − Compared to these techniques, catalytic ozonation is regarded as a potential way to eliminate VOCs because of its high efficiency, low reaction temperature, no secondary pollution, and low energy consumption. Due to its strong oxidizing property, O3 molecules proceed to produce active species (free radicals •O2 –, •O2–, •OH) during the catalytic reaction, which can effectively transform VOCs to CO2, H2O, and other harmless products at a lower temperature. However, the elimination of VOCs via catalytic ozonation highly depends on the selection of high-efficiency catalysts.

Supported metal catalysts always play important roles in catalysis fields, and their performance is determined by various factors, such as the properties of loaded metals, the function of supports, and the interaction between metals and supports. , The noble metals are widely researched for catalytic oxidation; however, the high price, instability, and easily poisoning properties hinder their large-scale industrial application. , To address these limitations, transition metals with low cost and high activity, such as Mn, , Cu, and Co, , have been extensively investigated as promising candidates for VOC removal. Furthermore, Mn-based catalysts have proven to be effective in the catalytic degradation of VOCs owing to the advantage of diverse valence states and excellent redox ability. For instance, Shao et al. demonstrated that Mn3+ and oxygen species of supported Mn-based catalysts positively influence catalytic performance during toluene catalytic ozonation. Among the tested supports (TiO2, SiO2, and γ-Al2O3), the 5%Mn/γ-Al2O3 catalyst achieved nearly 100% toluene conversion at 120 °C, with great stability and poison tolerance.

As for the supports, HZSM-5 zeolites are considered effective support materials in promoting catalytic processes because of their high adsorption capacity, unique mesoporous structure, and abundant acid–base sites. Tang et al. explored the efficiency of Mn/HZSM-5 samples in the dichloromethane catalytic ozonation and found that the overall acidity and the properties of acid sites (type, intensity, etc.) significantly influenced the degradation performance of catalysts.

Additionally, the mixed-metal oxides demonstrate greater catalytic performance than single-metal oxides owing to the synergistic effect between the multiple metal oxides. Liu et al. prepared bimetallic M-Mn/ZSM-5 (M = Co, Cu, Sm, and Ce) catalysts for toluene degradation and found that the addition of an auxiliary metal greatly improved the degradation performance. Among them, the Ce-doped catalyst presented the best toluene conversion efficiency because of its lower average oxidation state (AOS) and increased oxygen vacancies. Soltan et al. synthesized serial Fe–Mn/HZSM-5 samples, which achieved great results in degrading toluene due to the uniform distribution and synergistic effect of active metals, sufficient oxygen vacancies, favorable acidity, and adequate reactive oxygen species. Chang et al. used the Mn–Co/HZSM-5 catalyst to enhance acetaldehyde degradation, which showed higher catalytic activity compared to Mn/HZSM-5 and Co/HZSM-5 owing to its satisfactory surface oxygen species, redox ability, and crystalline structure.

Nowadays, lithium batteries have become popular in electronic equipment and electric vehicles. As a typical VOC in exhaust gas emitted from the production process of lithium batteries, dimethyl carbonate (DMC) was selected as a target VOC for this investigation. Wu et al. explored the DMC oxidation efficiency over Pt/Al2O3 and Pt/CeO2 catalysts, while the Pt/CeO2 catalyst showed better catalytic performance, achieving total oxidation at about 350 °C. Until now, however, the research about DMC removal is still limited.

Hence, a series of M-Mn (M = Cr, Cu, Co) metal oxides was doped on ZSM-5 supports via a wet impregnation method in this work. Also, the catalytic activity of these catalysts in DMC catalytic ozonation was assessed by DMC conversion efficiency and CO x (CO and CO2) selectivity. Analysis methods such as XRD, SEM, BET, XPS, and TPD/TPR were conducted to study the properties of catalysts. Also, a possible reaction pathway was presented through both in situ DRIFT research and density functional theory (DFT) computation.

Experimental and Methodology

Preparation of Catalysts

Commercial HZSM-5 zeolite (SiO2/Al2O3 = 21) was supplied by Tianjin Nankai University Catalyst Company and calcined at 500 °C for 4 h to remove impurities. The MnO x was introduced to HZSM-5 zeolites by an equivalent-volume impregnation method using Mn­(CH3COO)2·4H2O (Aladdin, China) as a precursor. After thorough impregnation, the mixture was treated by ultrasonic waves for 1 h and aged for 23 h. Next, the samples were fully dried at 100 °C and subsequently calcined at 500 °C for 4 h. The Mn loading amount of the obtained catalyst was 5 wt % and denoted as Mn/ZSM-5. The synthesis of CuMn/ZSM-5, CoMn/ZSM-5, and CrMn/ZSM-5 catalysts was similar to that of Mn/ZSM-5, with Cu­(NO3)2·3H2O (Sinopharm, China), Co­(NO3)2·6H2O (Sinopharm, China), and Cr­(NO3)3·9H2O (Sinopharm, China) as precursors of auxiliary metals. Mn was the first metal with a 5 wt % loading amount, and Cu, Co, and Cr were the second metals with a content of 1 wt %. All of the catalysts prepared for experiments were 40∼60 mesh, and the loading amounts of metal elements were measured by ICP-OES, which were in good agreement with the theoretical values.

Catalytic Activity

The catalytic activities of the prepared catalysts were evaluated in an experimental setup, as shown in Figure S1, with a total flow rate of 200 mL/min and a DMC concentration of 50 ppm. In every test, about 150 mg of catalyst (40–60 mesh) was mixed evenly with SiO2 (Sinopharm, 40–60 mesh) to achieve a gas hour space velocity (GHSV) of 30,000 h–1. Ozone was produced by a dielectric barrier discharge reactor and measured by an ozone analyzer. A gas chromatograph was used to measure the concentrations of DMC and CO/CO2. Also, the specific experimental processes are described in the Supporting Information (Text 1).

Characterization of Catalysts

The prepared catalysts were characterized by ICP-OES for element content confirmation, XRD for structural analysis, SEM for morphology images, N2 adsorption–desorption for pore structure evaluation, H2-TPR to assess redox ability, NH3-TPD to study acidity, XPS to analyze surface properties, and in situ DRIFTS to record intermediate products during reaction. The detailed characterization information is included in the Supporting Information (Text 2).

DFT Computation

Density functional theory (DFT) computations were conducted using the CASTEP package of Materials Studio software, using the generalized gradient approximation (GGA) with the Perdew–Burke–Enzerhof (PBE) functional to research the electron-exchange correlation. A 600 eV energy cutoff was set, and the energy convergence criteria and self-consistent field (SCF) were fixed as 10–5 and 10–6 eV/atom, respectively. The ZSM-5 unit cell was modeled with a Si96O192 structure, and the lattice parameters after optimization were a = 20.503, b = 20.288, and c = 13.623 Å, which were close to theoretical values (a = 20.090, b = 19.738, c = 13.142 Å). Moreover, a cluster was built with Al-substituted Si, leading to a similar Si/Al ratio to that of the prepared catalysts, as depicted in Figure S3. Results and Discussion

DMC Catalytic Ozonation

According to Figure (a), the DMC conversion of the prepared catalysts increased evidently when the reaction temperature increased from 60 to 160 °C. A theoretical equivalent of the O3/DMC molar ratio was set as 6, as presented in eq . For the most active catalyst, CrMn/ZSM-5, a 92% DMC conversion could be achieved at 120 °C. Also, the catalytic efficiency of the prepared catalysts (T = 120 °C) decreased in the order of CrMn/ZSM-5 > CuMn/ZSM-5 > Mn/ZSM-5 > CoMn/ZSM-5. The Mn/ZSM-5 and CoMn/ZSM-5 catalysts exhibited relatively worse performance, attaining less than 80% DMC conversion at 120 °C. As demonstrated in Figure (b), the CrMn/ZSM-5 catalyst, which achieved the best performance in activity tests, also exhibited the highest CO2 selectivity (more than 90% at 120 °C) and lowest CO selectivity simultaneously, suggesting less by-product formation. For CoMn/ZSM-5, which performed badly in catalytic activity, its CO2 selectivity was less than 80% in the whole range, indicating the generation of more CO and intermediates (aldehydes, alcohols, etc.) due to the partial oxidation. Moreover, CrMn/ZSM-5 also achieved above 80% mineralization rate at 120 °C, meaning that the harmless CO2 was the main product (Figure (c)).

The XRD patterns of the prepared samples are presented in Figure . After active metal loading, the crystalline texture of ZSM-5 zeolites basically remained. Figure S2 shows the N2 adsorption–desorption isotherms and corresponding pore size distribution curves of the prepared catalysts in order to study their textural properties. The curves exhibited the characteristics of type IV isotherms and H4 hysteresis rings, which were typically associated with the micropore structure formation. CrMn/ZSM-5 showed evidently bigger surface area and pore volume than the other catalysts, which likely contributed to its superior catalytic activity. The increased surface area could greatly promote the formation of exposed active sites and enhance reactants’ adsorption capacity, contributing to higher reaction efficiency and deeper oxidation.

Acidity and Redox Ability of Catalysts

NH3-TPD profiles were employed to assess the acidic properties of the prepared catalyst, as presented in Figure (a). The peaks were classified as weak acidity (<280 °C), moderate acidity (280∼450 °C), and strong acidity (>450 °C), according to the desorption temperature [17]. Also, the weak and moderate acidity were correlated with Lewis acid sites (NH3 and NH4 + physical adsorption), while the strong acidity was ascribed to Brönsted acid sites (NH4 + desorption). Apparently, the synthesized catalysts showed a sharp peak with high intensity centered at ∼90 °C, and the corresponding peak of the CrMn/ZSM-5 catalyst shifted to a lower temperature (81 °C) compared to other catalysts. Weak acidity always plays a critical role in VOC deep oxidation, for its facilitation of reactive oxygen species formation and C–C bond breaking, and a lower desorption temperature is conducive to accelerating catalytic reaction at a lower reaction temperature. , For the peak at ∼90 °C, the number of acid sites decreased in the sequence of CrMn/ZSM-5 (10.33 au·gcat –1) > CuMn/ZSM-5 (10.17 au·gcat –1) > CoMn/ZSM-5 (9.96 au·gcat –1) > Mn/ZSM-5 (6.78 au·gcat –1), showing that CrMn/ZSM-5 had higher acid sites at a lower desorption temperature.

(a) NH3-TPD and (b) H2-TPR profiles of the synthesized catalysts.

As shown in Figure (b), the redox ability of the synthesized catalysts was estimated by H2-TPR experiments. Typically, the peak observed around 192 °C was identified as the reduction of Cu2+ to Cu+ on the CuMn/ZSM-5 catalyst. No obvious H2 reduction peaks for CoO x and CrO x were discovered, which might be overlapped by the reduction peaks of MnO x , like the successive reduction of MnO2 → Mn2O3 → Mn3O4 → MnO. , The H2 consumption amount was figured out and listed as the following order: CrMn/ZSM-5 (18.8 au·gcat –1) < Mn/ZSM-5 (20.5 au·gcat –1) < CuMn/ZSM-5 (22.3 au·gcat –1) < CoMn/ZSM-5 (23.1 au·gcat –1). As recorded by literature, a lower H2 consumption amount could promote the decomposition of O3 and VOCs. In addition, the reduction temperatures of CuMn/ZSM-5 (192 °C) and CrMn/ZSM-5 (290 °C) were relatively lower among the four catalysts studied, leading to better reducibility at a lower temperature. There may be an appropriate redox ability of CrMn/ZSM-5, neither too high to intensify excessive oxidation nor too low to slow down the catalytic reaction.

Effect of H2O

Long-time stability and H2O resistance tests were conducted on the CrMn/ZSM-5 catalyst for its excellent performance in activity tests, as shown in Figure . Also, the reactions proceeded at 120 °C, with an O3/DMC ratio set as 6. With excellent stability, CrMn/ZSM-5 kept ∼92% DMC conversion for 7 h continuous operation, showing no observable downward trend. Upon the introduction of 2 vol % H2O to the flue gas, the DMC conversion slightly decreased, dropping from ∼91% to ∼89%, and gradually recovered to ∼90% after stopping H2O. Next, when 5 vol % H2O was added, the DMC conversion decreased to ∼87% and then recovered to ∼90% after the H2O was stopped, revealing that the H2O-induced deactivation was reversible. In addition, H2O addition enhanced the generation of CO2 owing to the generated OH active radicals applied for DMC deep oxidation.

In Situ DRIFTS Studies

To probe the mechanism of DMC catalytic ozonation over the CrMn/ZSM-5 catalyst surface, in situ DRIFTS analysis was employed to record the generated intermediates during the reaction process. As shown in Figure (a), the adsorption bands of DMC were presented in the spectra, with the balance gas of nitrogen. The bands around 1760, 1456, and 1280 cm–1 were ascribed to the carbonyl group ν­(CO) stretching vibration, the C–H bond bending vibration, and the C–O bond stretching vibration in DMC, respectively. Also, the bands near 2856 and 2956 cm–1 were related to the C–H bond stretching vibration of DMC. Additionally, the observed rise in band intensity suggested the continuous accumulation of DMC on the surface of the sample over time.

In situ DRIFTS spectra of DMC ozonation over the CrMn/ZSM-5 catalyst, with experimental conditions: (a) 50 ppm DMC + N2 and (b) 50 ppm DMC+300 ppm of O3 + N2.

When adsorption was completed, a stream of ozone was fed into the reaction system to investigate the catalytic ozonation process of DMC, as depicted in Figure (b). Clearly, the peaks corresponding to DMC gradually disappeared, suggesting the oxidation reaction between DMC and O3. It has been illustrated that the C–O bond is more likely to be first broken due to its low dissociation energies. , Typically, the peaks around 1720 and 1700 cm–1 were associated with CO stretching vibrations of carboxylic acid or aldehyde species, while the peak around 1210 cm–1 was assigned to C–O stretching vibrations (group of OC–O) of carboxylic acid species. Also, the peak around 1316 cm–1 was classified as carbonates, demonstrating the formation of CO2. Moreover, the negative band at 3636 cm–1 could be identified as hydroxyl groups, possibly ascribed to the utilization of OH species under the catalytic ozonation process.

DFT Computation

As a heterogeneous catalytic reaction, the DMC catalytic ozonation process over the CrMn/ZSM-5 catalyst started from the reactant’s adsorption on the surface of the catalysts. Therefore, the adsorption energy and bond length of DMC and O3 on the Cr/ZSM-5 and Mn/ZSM-5 clusters were calculated with the optimized model configurations, as presented in Figure . For the adsorption of ozone, the adsorption energy of the Mn/ZSM-5 cluster was calculated to be −1.59 eV, with the Mn–O bond length of 1.788 Å, while on the Cr/ZSM-5 cluster, the adsorption energy of the O3 was −1.70 eV and the Cr–O bond distance was 1.601 Å. As for DMC adsorption, the adsorption energy (E ads = −1.78 eV) and Mn–O bond length (1.918 Å) on the Mn/ZSM-5 cluster were lower than those on the Cr/ZSM-5 cluster (E ads = −1.62 eV, L Cr–O = 1.988 Å). In general, lower adsorption energy, often coupled with shorter bond length, usually reflects an enhanced interaction between adsorbed species and adsorption surface. Consequently, adsorption preferences revealed that O3 adsorption occurred primarily on the Cr/ZSM-5 cluster, while DMC tended to adsorb on the Mn/ZSM-5 cluster. It suggested that the doping of Cr facilitated the activation and decomposition of O3, while Mn sites were vital for DMC oxidation.

Reaction Mechanism

As illustrated in Figure , a possible reaction route for DMC catalytic ozonation over the CrMn/ZSM-5 catalyst was proposed. To begin with, DMC primarily adsorbed on the Mn sites, while the aqueous O3 was activated on the Cr sites and decomposed to generate reactive oxygen radicals (O*) with the promotion of oxygen vacancies. Then, these O* radicals migrated to the Mn sites to oxidize DMC. The C–O bond of DMC was preferentially cleaved, yielding acetic acid and aldehyde as intermediate products, and the aldehyde was then further converted to acetic acid. Ultimately, acetic acid was mineralized to form CO2 and H2O.

Conclusions

In this work, ZSM-5 zeolites were loaded with various metal oxides (CrMnO x , CuMnO x , CoMnO x , and MnO x ) via the impregnation method, and the obtained catalysts were applied to low-temperature catalytic ozonation of DMC. The CrMn/ZSM-5 catalyst demonstrated superior catalytic activity, achieving 92% DMC degradation efficiency, as well as 91% CO2 selectivity at 120 °C when the O3/DMC ratio was 6. As for CoMn/ZSM-5, its catalytic efficiency was poor, attaining only 80% DMC conversion under the same conditions. With more ozone input, CrMn/ZSM-5 could achieve above 95% DMC conversion and CO2 selectivity at an O3/DMC ratio of 8, with almost no residual O3. The physical and chemical properties of catalysts were also characterized via various methods, such as BET, XRD, XPS, SEM, NH3-TPD, and H2-TPR. According to the characterization results, CrMn/ZSM-5 had well-dispersed metal oxide particles, more surface defects, and a larger surface area, contributing to abundant active sites as well as reactant adsorption. Furthermore, its higher concentration of Mn3+ and Oad, favorable acidity, and appropriate low-temperature redox ability enhanced the formation of oxygen vacancies and the mobility of reactive oxygen, which thereby accelerated both O3 decomposition and DMC ozonation. CrMn/ZSM-5 also performed good stability and H2O resistance, keeping above 85% DMC conversion with 5 vol % H2O. DFT calculations indicated that the adsorption of O3 was favored on the Cr sites, while the adsorption of DMC occurred primarily on the Mn sites. Also, in situ DRIFTS measurement simultaneously identified key intermediates (acetic acid and aldehyde). Integrating these findings, a potential reaction pathway for DMC degradation was proposed.
