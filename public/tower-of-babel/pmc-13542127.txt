Physics-informed neural Volterra compensation enabling over 2600× efficiency improvement in 12,057-km ultra-long-haul coherent transmission
Authors: Xingchen He, Lianshan Yan, Lin Jiang, Anlin Yi, Wei Pan, Bin Luo, Zhengyu Pu, Alan Pak Tao Lau, Changyuan Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFiber nonlinearity limits reach and capacity in long-haul coherent systems. Here we present a single-step, physics-informed neural approach to nonlinear compensation that preserves the parallel structure of the Volterra-series transfer function while learning data-driven corrections, unlike widely used digital back-propagation, which reconstructs propagation with numerous cascaded steps and therefore incurs high computational complexity and latency at comparable performance. The method integrates a trainable linear kernel with a compact nonlinear mapping and provides a hardware-suitable architecture. In a 1125-km WDM link, NN-VS matches the maximum performance achieved by leading digital back-propagation variants at 19% computational complexity and 1/25 latency; in a 12,057-km WDM system, the reductions increase to ~6.6% and ~1/176, yielding an efficiency improvement exceeding 2,600×. These results demonstrate that single-step, parallel compensation—augmented by learned corrections—helps mitigate performance–complexity–latency trade-offs, suggesting a pathway for the efficient deployment of nonlinear compensation in next-generation coherent optical networks.Subject terms: Fibre optics and optical communications, Computational science, Nonlinear optics

Introduction

As a cornerstone of modern information infrastructure, long-haul coherent optical communication systems confront escalating bandwidth demands and increasingly stringent latency budgets. As capacity in the linear operating regime approaches the Shannon limit1–3, nonlinear compensation (NLC)4–7 becomes indispensable for further throughput gains. Against this backdrop, modern digital signal processing (DSP) underpins coherent transceivers; to unlock the system’s practical potential, DSP-based NLC must be simultaneously effective, deployable, and real-time—delivering high performance with modest computational and memory footprints under tight latency constraints. Accordingly, advancing NLC approaches that co-optimize the performance–complexity–latency trade-offs is of both scientific and practical significance.

Although DSP-based NLC has delivered substantial gains over the past decade, it still cannot resolve the performance–latency–complexity trade-offs. Representative approaches—exemplified by digital backpropagation (DBP)8,9 and its variants (e.g., weighted or correlation-aided DBP)10–12—approximate the inverse of the Manakov/NLSE through multi-step cascades, delivering appreciable gains. However, in long-haul transmission, achieving high compensation performance typically necessitates a large number of steps. This requirement drives up computational and memory demands, increases hardware power consumption and cost, and causes processing latency to grow approximately linearly with the step count. Driven by the imperative to reduce cascade depth while enabling low latency and practical deployment, an important alternative approach centers on the first-order perturbation theory-based (PB) methods13–15. As single-step approximations, these methods offer low complexity and simple implementation under low to medium nonlinear intensity. However, their performance is usually lower than that of the DBP family under strong nonlinearity and long-distance conditions. Another line of research also employs a single-step, inherently parallel architecture, the inverse Volterra-series transfer function (VSTF) equalizer. This approach has been widely adopted in short-reach Intensity-modulation direct-detection systems16–18, where a second-order structure compensates for square-law–induced ISI/SSBI, resulting in short memory, manageable coefficient counts, and predominantly real-valued multiply–accumulate operations that map efficiently onto FPGA/ASIC parallelization and pipelining. In contrast, long-haul coherent transmission demands first- and third-order kernels derived from the NLSE/Manakov equation to jointly capture dispersion and Kerr nonlinearity, including phase information. Under these conditions, the full third-order VSTF exhibits per-block complexity that scales cubically with the FFT block length and increases rapidly with kernel order. Although various simplified approximations19–23 can partially alleviate this burden, their performance typically falls short of that achieved by multi-step DBP variants.

Recently, deep learning (DL) has attracted considerable attention within the research community of optical fiber communications24–29. In terms of NLC, learned digital backpropagation (LDBP)30–32 “neuralizes” the linear and nonlinear operators—unrolling them into trainable layers and optimizing filter coefficients in a data-driven manner—thereby achieving enhanced performance with comparatively few steps. However, LDBP remains fundamentally a multi-step cascade, and in long-haul links, attaining high accuracy still necessitates numerous iterations, rendering LDBP effectively an ultra-deep neural network (NN). This introduces training challenges such as vanishing gradients and unstable convergence, while activation storage and backpropagation cost scale linearly with the number of steps. NNs based on perturbation features (NN-PB)33,34 retain a single-step structure and lower complexity, yet under diverse link conditions and strong nonlinearity their overall accuracy generally fails to surpass the DBP family. Consequently, neither classical methods nor their learned variants have resolved the performance–complexity–latency trade-offs in a unified manner.

Building on these considerations, we introduce a physics-informed neural network-based Volterra-series (NN-VS) framework that aims to directly improve the trade-offs. The approach is structured upon the interpretable architecture of the VSTF: the first-order Volterra kernel, which encapsulates physical priors, is parameterized as trainable coefficients, while a sliding-convolution-window mechanism is embedded into a simplified third-order kernel23 to explicitly capture nonlinear interactions across local symbol neighborhoods. The first-order kernel and window parameters are then optimized on experimental data using stochastic gradient descent or adaptive variants (e.g., Adam), thereby correcting biases induced by model simplifications and device nonidealities. From an implementation perspective, NN-VS preserves a single-step and hardware-efficient design; it markedly reduces multiplications and memory footprint, effectively decoupling latency from the computational complexity at the architectural level. A related NN-VS from our group was previously used for simulation-based WDM channel modeling35, whereas here we focus on experimental receiver-side NLC.

Experimental results demonstrate that NN-VS enhances compensation performance while simultaneously reducing complexity and latency, offering strong deployment potential on resource-constrained platforms. Its advantages over existing algorithms become increasingly pronounced with longer transmission distances. In 1125 km Wavelength Division Multiplexing (WDM) transmission, NN-VS achieves the maximum attainable performance of LDBP with only ~19% of the computational cost, while reducing latency to ~1/25. For ultra-long-haul transmission over 12,057 km, the computational cost is further reduced to ~6.6% and latency to 1/176. Moreover, within the constrained complexity regime, NN-VS achieves up to 0.26 dB Q-factor improvement over DBP variants. These findings indicate that NN-VS mitigates the long-standing performance–complexity–latency trade-offs in NLC, providing a practical pathway for cost-efficient deployment of ultra-long-haul coherent optical networks.

The optimization of the simVSTF kernel with NN

We utilize NNs to enhance performance, wherein the optimization of the simVSTF kernel is directly inspired by the architectural principles of NNs. It should be noted that the number of parallel branches NPB in the nonlinear part of simVSTF is strictly aligned with the number of spans Nspan. Here, we generalize this concept by introducing the step size Δz, such that NPB=z/Δz, rather than being constrained by Nspan. This formulation is analogous to the DBP algorithm, with the key distinction that the steps in DBP are arranged in series, whereas in this case they are executed in parallel. Specifically, the first-order kernel K1(ω,mΔz) and K1(ω,z−mΔz) in Eqs. (10) and (8) resemble the linear transformations in NNs, and can be represented by two weight matrices W1∈CNPB×N andW2∈CNPB×N, while Eq. (9) corresponds to the nonlinear activation function σ(⋅) in NNs. Thus, the Eqs. (8) to (10) can be rewritten as

In conventional NNs training, the weight parameters W1,W2 are randomly initialized, and such a simple network structure struggles to capture the evolutionary characteristics of signals in optical fibers in a purely data-driven scheme. Inspired by physics-informed neural networks like LDBP, here we initialize W1,W2 using the simVSTF kernels K1(ω,mΔz) and K1(ω,z−mΔz), respectively. Unlike commonly used activation functions in NNs such as ReLU41 and GeLU42, the σ(⋅) in (12) mainly continues to adopt the nonlinear term from simVSTF. Considering that the nonlinear contribution within a sample is influenced by the power of adjacent samples, similar to weighted DBP11 (W-DBP), we further introduce a sliding convolution window of length Nf before the power summation in the time domain to account for the impact of neighboring samples on the nonlinearity of the target sample. This window is initialized with uniform weights ξT=[1/Nf,1/Nf...,1/Nf] and is further optimized during training. Consequently, the formulation of Eq. In practical implementation, we adopted the method described in ref. 23 to prune the complexity of the NLC part. For the initialization of the linear part, the frequency-domain signal E~x/y(ω,z) retains the original baud rate as the processing rate. In contrast, for the initialization of W1,W2 in the nonlinear part, the processing rate of E~x/y(ω,z) is reduced to half of its original value. Specifically, down-sampling is achieved by removing outer frequency components before inputting the frequency signal E~x/y(ω,z) into W1, while upsampling is performed by zero-padding after the signal passes through the accumulation of Wones. Consequently, the block length of all FFT and IFFT operations in the parallel branches of the NLC part is halved, resulting in W1,W2 having a shape of (NPB,N/2), without compromising performance. In this manner, the convolution involved in computing the nonlinear phase shift is transformed into applying a convolution kernel of shape (1,1,Nf) to an image of shape (1,NPB,N/2) via two-dimensional convolution.

Training stage

The training is conducted using PyTorch 1.8, a version that supports complex-valued gradients. Similar to classical algorithms like DBP and VSTF, the input for NN-VS consists of the time-domain complex signal E(t,z)=[Ex(t,z),Ey(t,z)] for x and y polarization states. The minimum penalty-free FFT size is determined by the duration of the pulse response associated with the optical fiber link, which is primarily governed by the accumulated dispersion. In transmission links of 1125 km and 12,057 km, for single-step compensation techniques like NN-VS, the minimum penalty-free FFT block lengths required for the linear step implementation without inter-block interference are 1024 and 16384, respectively. The overlap-save algorithm is employed in this study, where each FFT sample block overlaps with N/2 samples of adjacent blocks, resulting in the discarding of the first and last N/4 samples in each block after NLC compensation.

We adopt a “physics-distillation then task-level fine-tuning” training strategy, denoted as Training Stage-1: waveform fitting and Training Stage-2: symbol fitting.

In Training Stage-1, the maximum achievable performance of the DBP variant (DBP-MAP) output serves as a proxy for the “numerical ground truth,” guiding NN-VS with waveform-level loss to learn a mapping that approximates the numerical solution, thereby reducing the search space and enhancing convergence stability, as illustrated in Fig. 2a. At this stage, the sliding convolution window weights of NN-VS participate in training together with other parameters, starting from an initial state of[1/Nf,1/Nf...,1/Nf]. During training, the selection of the sliding window length Nf in the 1D convolution has an influence on the performance of NN-VS, with the optimal value varying across different system configurations. To avoid performance degradation caused by suboptimal choices of Nf, we adopted an adaptive strategy in Training Stage-1. In practice, training begins with a small window length Nfmin (here we choose 1) and an upper bound Nfmax (here we choose 101) to ensure sufficient coverage of potential neighborhood effects. The window length is incrementally enlarged during training, implemented in code via binary 0/1 masks—for instance, by increasing it by six every five epochs—while the average Q-factor(where Q=20log10(2erfc−1(2BER))) performance is evaluated on the test sets for each candidate Nf. By tracking the Q-factor improvements across successive expansions, if the most recent result fails to surpass its predecessor, the performance is regarded as saturated and will subsequently fluctuate within this plateau. Once this saturation condition is first satisfied, the latter of the two consecutive Nf values is selected as the optimal window length, thereby terminating the search.

NN-VS NLC for 1125 km WDM transmissions

We evaluate NN-VS using a publicly available 7-channel PDM-16QAM WDM dataset transmitted over 1125 km of single-mode fiber43. For decorrelation, two 36-GBaud 16-QAM sequences are generated from independent random bit sources and assigned to the even and odd channels. The coherent detection traces and all acquisition parameters are consistent with the dataset specifications and are described in detail in the Methods section.

As the objective of NN-VS in Training Stage-1 is to approximate the solution space of the DBP-MAP, a widely accepted method to evaluate its effectiveness is to compare the consistency of the waveforms generated by their respective outputs. The NMSE is utilized as a metric to measure the disparity between these two waveforms. In this transmission experiment, the 45-step W-DBP is considered as the DBP-MAP, as additional steps show negligible improvement. As a result of brute-force grid search, the optimal nonlinear phase de-rotation factor and Gaussian-shaped window length to be considered for the power-weighted are 1.5 and 5 for 45 steps W-DBP. Therefore, its output waveform is adopted as the fitting target in Training Stage-1. Figure 3a depicts the convergence curve of NN-VS with a launch power of 0 dBm in Training Stage-1. It can be observed that increasing NPB, i.e., decreasing the Δz, effectively enhances the non-linear expressive capability of NN-VS upon reaching convergence, thereby reducing the NMSE between benchmark. Despite the fact that more NPB imply more iterations required to reach convergence, the 45 NPB NN-VS, achieving optimal performance, only needs approximately 20 iterations. This is attributed to the initialization of the weight matrix using physical knowledge, enabling a more explicit directionality in gradient backpropagation. By disregarding physical information and employing random initializations for W1,W2 in 45 NPB NN-VS, along with substituting the non-linear term σ with the Gelu function, it is observed that NMSE value consistently fails to achieve effective convergence, as denoted by the dashed black line.

Given that computational complexity remains a primary challenge in implementing NLC within practical DSP-based coherent optical communication systems, NN-VS offers a favorable balance between performance and implementation feasibility. Compared to simVSTF, the performance enhancement of NN-VS primarily stems from its ability to implicitly learn the optimal DSP parameters for a given link. Such deviations arise from practical deployment factors, including device impairments and discrepancies from theoretical models that are difficult to quantify. Through data-driven learning, NN-VS captures these differences and correspondingly optimizes the parameters of W1 and W2 to compensate for these deviations.

This study divides the training process of NN-VS into two steps. To emphasize its effectiveness, Fig. 6 compares the variations of the Q-factor with epochs under different training schemes. Here, 15 NPB NN-VS at 0 dBm launch power is taken as an example, with similar outcomes observed for other configurations. The blue solid line denotes the convergence trajectory employing direct single-step training, i.e., only utilizing Lsym with ρ=0 exclusively throughout the entire training process. Such an end-to-end training approach is commonly used in other NN-based NLC studies, but it appears suboptimal for architectures like NN-VS, where the Q-factor increases slowly with epochs and starts oscillating around the 30th epoch after reaching a maximum value of 8.25 dB, which remains lower than that achieved by traditional DBP/W-DBP. To address these limitations, we propose a two-stage training strategy that integrates both waveform- and symbol-level objectives into a unified process. In Stage 1, waveform-level loss is employed to train a teacher model, offering NN-VS a physically meaningful initialization that ensures smooth convergence while maintaining interpretability; however, because this teacher model essentially approximates DBP-MAP, it remains restricted to reproducing numerical solutions and cannot compensate for hardware-induced distortions, thus its performance ceiling is bounded by DBP-MAP. In Stage 2, symbol-level loss is introduced to achieve task alignment and end-to-end optimization by directly minimizing symbol errors, enabling the model to match system-level metrics such as Q-factor and BER; through backpropagation, NN-VS learns signal statistics that support the convergence of DSP modules, thereby implicitly correcting hardware impairments and extending performance beyond inverse channel modeling. Nonetheless, when fiber impairments couple with device imperfections, the resulting characteristics become highly complex, and for lightweight networks, relying solely on symbol-level loss risks unstable convergence due to noise-sensitive gradients and potential overfitting of local patterns in the absence of waveform constraints. To mitigate these issues, Training Stage-2 incorporates the correction term Lwave in Eq. (13)—a lightly weighted waveform regularization—which stabilizes optimization and prevents noise-driven overfitting; as demonstrated by the green dashed line in Fig. 6, omitting this term degrades test performance. Overall, the two-stage strategy first leverages waveform-level loss to approximate the optimal behavior of traditional algorithms and then fine-tunes the model with symbol-level loss under waveform regularization, effectively avoiding instability and overfitting while achieving higher-level task alignment that allows NN-VS to surpass the performance of its teacher model.

The analysis is then extended for more parallel branchs NPB and DBP steps Nstep from 6 up to 480, aiming to determine the best trade-off between performance and computational complexity for each NLC method. The obtained results show the Q-factor as a function of the NPB or Nstep are presented in Fig. 8c. The results show that the simVSTF only achieves slightly better performance than CDC, and the gap between it and DBP-MAP (320 steps W-DBP) performance is further widened compared to 1125 km transmission. The standard DBP algorithm is not adequate for low-complexity DBP, since its performance tends to quickly degrade for steps smaller than the fiber span. This phenomenon arises from the initial development aimed at achieving precise modeling of signal propagation within fiber. On the contrary, it is shown that the NN-VS, LDBP, W-DBP algorithms are able to tolerate much smaller NPB or Nstep without severely compromising the system performance. Following the second stage of training, NN-VS demonstrates performance gains over both LDBP and W-DBP algorithms in the low-complexity regime. For example, across all three entropy conditions, NN-VS with only 30 NPB achieves a Q-factor comparable to the maximum values obtained by LDBP and W-DBP, and on average, it exceeds the Q-factor of 30 Nstep LDBP and W-DBP by 0.13 dB and 0.34 dB, respectively. Moreover, NN-VS demonstrates the most pronounced Q-factor advantage over LDBP at NPBorNstep=6, with an average improvement of 0.26 dB. It is important to note that LDBP can be regarded as a deep neural network. Consequently, in architectures comprising tens or even hundreds of cascaded stages, the gradient of the loss function must be backpropagated layer by layer. The repeated multiplication of Jacobian matrices inherent to the chain rule can lead to exponential attenuation or amplification of the gradient, thereby posing a risk ofvanishing or exploding gradients. A detailed theoretical proof is provided in Supplementary Note 2. On the other hand, the computational cost of backpropagation increases linearly or even super-linearly with the number of layers, imposing demands on hardware resources and prolonging training time. During training of the 12,057 -km system, we observed that LDBP began to exhibit pronounced instability once the number of layers exceeded 60. To ensure stable training of deep LDBP models, a learning rate decay strategy was employed in this study, wherein the learning rate was progressively reduced as training proceeded. However, this approach unavoidably slows parameter updates, potentially causing the network to converge to suboptimal solutions and limiting further performance improvements. Indeed, we have not yet identified a more effective method to mitigate this issue.

The computational complexity was estimated by calculating the number of real multiplications (RMpts) per transmitted symbol, while addition operations were disregarded. Latency was estimated based on the ideal serial multiplication depth. The formal definitions of the relevant algorithms, along with a detailed, comprehensive analysis of computational complexity and latency, are provided in Supplementary Note 3.

Figure 9c, d depict the Q-factor evolution of various NLC algorithms as a function of latency. NN-VS, simVSTF, and NN-PB are single-step algorithms that rely heavily on parallel computation, resulting in latency that remains constant regardless of the number of RMs. In contrast, LDBP, W-DBP, and DBP are inherently serial algorithms, exhibiting latency that scales proportionally with computational complexity. Consequently, as transmission distance increases, the latency advantage of parallel algorithms becomes increasingly pronounced. Historically, however, simVSTF and NN-PB have demonstrated lower performance compared to serial DBP-based algorithms, forcing practitioners to navigate trade-offs between latency, complexity, and performance without a clear optimal choice. The proposed NN-VS in this work achieves consistent advantages across the three key dimensions of computational complexity, latency, and performance. At a transmission distance of 1125 km, NN-VS reduces latency by up to 25× compared to the DBP family while maintaining a 0.08 dB Q-factor advantage. Compared with NN-PB, NN-VS provides a higher Q-factor improvement (by ~0.35 dB in Fig. 9a) at the expense of a higher latency (~1.7× in Fig. 9c), highlighting a performance–latency trade-off between the two single-step approaches. At 12,057 km, NN-VS delivers an even greater latency reduction—up to 176× compared to DBP-based algorithms—while its latency remains ~1.7× that of NN-PB.

While Fig. 9a–d independently compareΔQ against RMs and latency, such isolated analyses may overlook the combined impact of these parameters in DSP implementations, particularly on hardware platforms with varying degrees of parallelism and pipelining capabilities. To assess each algorithm’s ability to deliver Q-factor improvements per unit computational burden, ΔQ is normalized by the product of RMs and latency τ, enabling a fairer comparison between serial and parallel architectures. Figure 9e, f illustrate the resulting normalized ΔQ boxplots for the 1125-km and 12,057 -km systems, respectively. In the 1125-km transmission scenario, the DBP family shows similar distributions of normalized ΔQ in the box plots, while the three single-step algorithms display narrower distribution ranges due to their fixed latency. Notably, NN-VS achieves a maximum normalized ΔQ that is at least ~3.25 times higher than that of the other algorithms. In contrast, the maximum normalized ΔQ values of the remaining five algorithms differ only marginally. This suggests that at medium-to-long transmission distances, ML-enhanced methods such as LDBP and NN-PB primarily improve absolute performance but offer limited gains in computational resource utilization, whereas NN-VS achieves a balanced improvement in both aspects. In the 12,057 -km ultra-long-haul scenario, the AI-assisted algorithms LDBP, NN-PB, and NN-VS show higher normalized ΔQ per RMs×latency than conventional methods in computational resource efficiency. Among them, NN-VS further amplifies this advantage, achieving a maximum normalized ΔQ that is at least ~6 times greater than those of the other algorithms.

Discussion

The proposed NN-VS framework offers a practical set of advantages for nonlinear fiber impairment compensation. Its core features of low latency and reduced computational complexity effectively alleviate the key bottlenecks that constrain the application of the DBP family. By executing compensation in a single parallelized stage, NN-VS reduces dependence between computational complexity and latency observed in the multi-step DBP family, thereby enabling low-latency processing. Its architecture is readily parallelizable and hardware-efficient, relying on matrix operations and FFTs that map efficiently onto parallel processing units or dedicated DSP chips. Unlike conventional deep NN with many sequential layers—where end-to-end latency tends to increase with depth due to inter-layer dependencies—the parallel branches of NN-VS are inherently independent and can be readily mapped onto hardware platforms, facilitating pipelining and utilization of hardware accelerators. A further hardware-oriented advantage of NN-VS is that it does not require exponential functions or nonlinear activations such as sigmoid, tanh, or GELU. Consequently, its fixed-point implementation and pipelined execution are more straightforward, eliminating the need for specialized nonlinear function units. It should be emphasized that the present work does not implement NN-VS on FPGA/ASIC hardware. All results are obtained under floating-point numerical simulations. Therefore, practical deployment issues—such as fixed-point quantization noise, coefficient memory bandwidth, hardware pipeline scheduling, and DSP/BRAM resource consumption—remain open and will be investigated in future studies. The contribution of this paper is to establish a hardware-suitable architecture, rather than to provide a complete hardware implementation.

Another key advantage lies in its performance: NN-VS matches or exceeds the Q-factor improvements of optimized DBP variants, effectively bridging the gap between single-step methods and the multi-step DBP family while delivering comparable performance in a fraction of the time. This is achieved through the intelligent integration of fiber physics into NN architecture, ensuring the capacity to learn complex nonlinear interactions intrinsic to real-world systems. Thirdly, relative to cascaded approaches such as LDBP, the single-step, parallel structure avoids sequential dependencies during optimization. As transmission distance increases, the number of steps in LDBP grows with the link length, increasing model depth and training cost. This scaling exacerbates challenges like slow convergence, high computational demands during training, and susceptibility to vanishing gradients and overfitting. By contrast, NN-VS’s highly parallelized single-step design mitigates these issues, resulting in faster, more stable training and reduced sensitivity to network depth-related limitations.

A practical next step for future development involves extending the NN-VS framework from frequency-domain compensation to a fully time-domain implementation, similar to that demonstrated by LDBP. Currently, NN-VS employs FFT-based operations within each parallel branch to handle CD, leveraging frequency-domain computational efficiency. Replacing these with FIR filters for CD compensation could simplify hardware realization further and potentially enhance processing speed. A time-domain implementation would eliminate dependence on repeated FFT/IFFT computations, reduce latency, and allow for more flexible, deeply pipelined parallel architectures. Additionally, model-compression and pruning strategies developed for Volterra-type equalizers can provide useful guidance for further reducing the complexity and memory footprint of NN-VS in a time-domain realization45, making NN-VS even more attractive for deployment in resource-constrained optical transceivers.

Methods

1125 km WDM experiments setup

We utilize an openly available experimental dataset created by the Hong Kong Polytechnic University43. The dataset demonstrates a 1125 km 7-channel PDM-16QAM WDM transmission using single-mode fiber. To achieve decorrelation between odd and even channels, two 36 GBaud 16-QAM sequences are generated at the transmitter side, each derived from independent random bit sources. After raised-cosine pulse shaping with a 0.1 roll-off factor, the signal is loaded into 112 GSa/s arbitrary waveform generator (AWG, Keysight 8194 A). The channel spacing is set to 50 GHz. The transmission link comprises 15 heterogeneous spans, each followed by an EDFA-VOA-optical filter triplet. After transmission, the test channel is filtered out at the receiving end through a wavelength-selective switch (WSS), detected by a coherent receiver sampled by a real-time digital oscilloscope with 80 GSa/s sampling rate. Two unique sequences of 170,624 symbols are obtained by a live quantum random numbers generator46 and each sequence is repeated multiple times to consist of two sequences of 1 and 1.5 million symbols for training and testing datasets, respectively. In this study, the experimental results of the middle channel (channel 4) are chosen as a representative example to illustrate the performance of NN-VS. As a physics-informed scheme, NN-VS NLC can achieve convergence within 30 epochs in Training Stage-1, starting with a learning rate of 0.005 that is progressively halved every 10 epochs, and each training and testing batch consists of 50 FFT blocks. On the other hand, Stage-2 achieves convergence in 15 epochs with a learning rate of 0.0001, and each training and testing batch comprises 200 FFT blocks.

12057 km WDM experiments setup

We adopted the experimental setup described in ref. 44 to verify the effectiveness of NN-VS. As shown in Fig. 7, the C + L band transoceanic transmission system consists of 257 optical channels. The test channel is generated using a tunable external-cavity laser with a linewidth of less than 10 kHz, Concurrently, the supplementary channels are constituted by spurious optical channels, delineated at 0.3 nm intervals, derived from an ASE noise source and partitioned by WSS. The modulation process generates PDM 32 GBaud probabilistically shaped (PS) 16QAM optical signals with an oversample factor of 2 and shapes them using a square root raised cosine (SRRC) with a roll-off factor of 0.01 and converted to analog signals through a DAC device (Keysight, M8195A) at 64 GSa/s and 25 GHz analog bandwidth. The circulating loop test-bed consists of 10 spans with ~75 km single-mode fiber and an erbium-doped fiber amplifier (EDFA) each. Total C + L band output power launched into the 12,057 km transmission link, which totally consists of 160 spans is ~18 dBm. We deploy C- and L-band WSSs (II–VI, 1000 A) to equalize the gain spectrum and filter out ASE noise in the circulating loop test-bed. Moreover, we implement a loop synchronous polarization controller to emulate the polarization effects typically seen in straight-line systems. In the receiver terminal (Rx), the test channel is filtered by C- or L-band WSS, detected by an integrated coherent receiver, and sampled by a real-time digital oscilloscope (Keysight, DSOZ634A) with 33 GHz electrical bandwidth and 80 GSa/s sampling rate. Training and testing datasets both are comprised of four million sampling points collected from the oscilloscope, with each dataset corresponding to one unique sequence of 131,072 transmitted symbols generated by a pseudo-random bit sequence, and each training and testing batch consists of 10 FFT blocks. On the other hand, Stage-2 achieves convergence in 15 epochs with a learning rate of 0.0001, and each training and testing batch comprises 26 FFT blocks.
