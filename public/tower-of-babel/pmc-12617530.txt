Donor Activation–focused Rehabilitation Approach: Maximizing Outcomes After Lower Extremity Nerve Transfers
Authors: Luke T. Juckett, Breanne L. Bowers, Lorna C. Kahn, Julie M. West, Amy M. Moore
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:Nerve transfers have become a mainstay in the surgical management of peripheral nerve injuries. Following surgical repair, a donor activation–focused rehabilitation approach (DAFRA) has been shown to significantly improve patient outcomes compared with nonspecific physical therapy approaches. Focused rehabilitation is critical following nerve transfers in the lower extremity due to the significant demands of weight-bearing and ambulation, both of which greatly impact a patient’s quality of life.Methods:DAFRA is formatted to strengthen the altered neural pathways created by nerve transfers by respecting the patient’s new anatomy, encouraging cortical plasticity, and thoroughly educating the patient. This article outlined the early, middle, and late phases of therapy for patients with lower extremity nerve injuries who undergo nerve transfers to restore function.Results:There is no predetermined timeline of progression through the DAFRA program. The 3 phases of recovery are adapted to the individual and their personal needs and recovery.Conclusions:The purpose of this article was to present the optimal DAFRA protocols for common nerve transfers in the lower extremity to reestablish hip stability, knee extension, and ankle mobility.

Takeaways

Question: Is physical therapy important to function after a lower extremity nerve transfer?

Findings: This article outlined specific rehabilitation protocols for lower extremity nerve transfers.

Meaning: Lower extremity nerve transfers restore function in patients but require focused rehabilitation per transfer done regularly over time to achieve function.

INTRODUCTION

Peripheral nerve injuries (PNIs) are debilitating injuries that can result in lifelong functional deficits and pain. Surgical management of nerve injuries includes primary repair, nerve transfers, nerve decompression, or tendon transfers to aid in the restoration of function. Nerve transfers are now considered a fundamental part of PNI management.1,2 Nerve transfers use a healthy, expendable donor nerve to create novel neural pathways that restore movement and sensation to the affected area.

Functional reinnervation of the affected musculature is complex, and outcomes vary depending, in part, on rehabilitation. Targeted rehabilitation has been shown to improve outcomes after nerve transfers in the upper extremity.3,4 The donor activation–focused rehabilitation approach (DAFRA) is a motor relearning program that strengthens the relationship between donor and recipient nerves in the motor and somatosensory cortices and has been shown to improve functional recovery.5,6 In 2016, Kahn and Moore7 first detailed the elements of DAFRA and outlined preferred rehabilitation protocols for upper extremity nerve transfers. Lower extremity nerve transfers have gained popularity; thus, focused rehabilitation protocols are needed to optimize patient recovery. In this article, DAFRA principles are briefly reviewed, followed by a series of specific protocols designed to optimize recovery after lower extremity nerve transfers.

FACTORS INFLUENCING RETURN OF FUNCTION

The following is a summary of DAFRA principles adapted from the article titled Donor Activation Focused Rehabilitation Approach: Maximizing Outcomes After Nerve Transfers by Kahn and Moore.7

Cortical Plasticity

Nerve transfers create new neural outputs from the cortex. Cortical imaging has shown that after nerve transfers, the cortical territory representing the recipient nerve is the most active when donor nerve musculature is activated.8,9 Therefore, nerve transfers rely on neuroanatomical connections between recipient and donor territories in the cortex to power functional reinnervation.5,6 DAFRA is designed to strengthen cortical connections between donor and recipient nerves via the following:

Exercise

Postoperative exercise is essential to maximize functional outcomes; however, the intensity should be monitored closely.10,11 Low-intensity training (ie, high repetitions with low weight) promotes axon growth, but high-intensity exercise (ie, few repetitions with high weight) can have a negative impact on axonal regeneration.12,13 Given the slow rate of regeneration (1 mm/d), patients should be counseled to remain patient throughout recovery.

Positioning

Following denervation injuries, sensory and proprioception afferents from musculature are impaired, which can lead to improper muscle length of affected and unaffected muscles around a joint.5,6,14,15 Patients should be encouraged to use slings or splints to support proper muscle length and full range of motion (ROM) during recovery.16

PREOPERATIVE THERAPY

A preoperative visit with a physical and/or occupational therapist who understands the surgical plan is recommended. Baseline measures of strength and ROM should be obtained to monitor postoperative recovery. Teach the patient exercises that will activate donor muscle groups. Additionally, educate the patient on limb positioning, splinting, and edema management to facilitate postoperative care.

POSTOPERATIVE THERAPY

DAFRA can begin 3 weeks after surgery and progresses through 3 phases: early, middle, and late.

Early Phase

Patient Education

Patient education is essential. At the first postoperative visit, all members of the care team should understand how the patient’s anatomy has been changed. Patients who know how to activate their nerve transfer are more likely to participate in their own recovery. Finally, efforts should be made by the care team to emphasize the expected timeline and duration of recovery. The manual muscle testing (MMT) model is a good tool to measure “success” for patients.

Home Exercise Program

Three weeks after surgery, encourage patients to begin with high-repetition, low-resistance movements (10–20 full ROM movements each hour) to maximize traffic through their new neural pathway—conceptualized as “flooding the donor.” Initially, weekly visits are encouraged to ensure compliance and accuracy of movements.

Monitoring for Return of Function

Return of function is tracked using the MMT at regular postoperative visits. Once a muscle twitch or contraction is noted, the patient can progress in their rehabilitation pathway.

Middle Phase

Advancing the HEP

Once a twitch is perceived in the recipient muscle, the authors suggest advancing the HEP based on MMT levels:

When the recipient muscle progresses to MMT 3, full ROM against gravity and resistance exercises can be slowly introduced. It may take some patients 2–3 years to reach this MMT level.

The Human Gait Cycle

There are 2 phases of the gait cycle: stance and swing. The stance phase is the period from heel strike to toe-off, and the swing phase is from toe-off to heel strike. Throughout the cycle, muscle groups are required to work cooperatively and contract concentrically, eccentrically, and isometrically for proper ambulation (Table 2). It is important for patients and providers to understand how to activate the new neural network during ambulation.

Nerve Transfers to Restore Hip Function

The superior and inferior gluteal nerves control the muscles that extend and stabilize the hip and support the pelvis during the stance phase of the gait cycle (Table 2). Injuries to either can lead to significantly impaired ambulation. Loss of the superior gluteal nerve will lead to an inability to support the pelvis during the stance phase of the gait cycle (Trendelenburg gait). Loss of the inferior gluteal nerve results in weak hip extension, causing a gluteus maximus gait. Nerve transfers that capitalize on the redundancy of the sciatic fascicles supplying toe movement can be used to help stabilize the pelvis.

Sciatic Fascicular Transfers to the Superior and/or Inferior Gluteal Nerves

Considerations

Redundant sciatic fascicles for toe movement can be used to reinnervate the superior and inferior gluteal nerves. During intraneural dissection, the strongest sciatic fascicles for toe flexion and toe extension are identified. With severe injuries, the sciatic nerve can also be injured; thus, it is important to determine fascicle viability and redundancy intraoperatively. If the sciatic nerve is uninjured, fascicles for toe flexion are the preferred donor. If only 1 transfer can be performed, the superior gluteal nerve is prioritized due to the critical role the gluteus medius plays in hip stabilization.

Early Phase

Flood the sciatic nerve by performing active dorsiflexion or plantar flexion, toe flexion, or toe extension, depending on the fascicles used for surgery. These exercises are best done from a seated or supine position, if able. Encourage regular stretching of the hip flexors and adductors.

Middle Phase

During the middle phase, introduce gravity-eliminated exercises. These will be individualized depending on the donor (ie, toe flexion or extension) and the recipient (ie, superior or inferior gluteal nerve) used. After repair of the inferior gluteal nerve, the patient should take a side-lying position for gravity-eliminated hip extension. Instruct the patient to plantar flex (or dorsiflex) their ankle while attempting to extend the hip (Fig. 1). After repair of the superior gluteal nerve, the patient can use a supine position for gravity-eliminated hip abduction. A therapist or caregiver may be required to aid the recipient muscle movement and control early in the middle phase of recovery. If available, a slide board can be used to reduce friction. Aquatic therapy or harness-supported techniques can be helpful to mimic the functional demands on hip musculature.

The side-lying position is recommended for gravity-eliminating exercises. While actively engaging their donor motion (yellow arrow—dorsiflexion), the patient attempts to extend their hip, driving their knee backward.

Late Phase

At this stage, donor movement is combined with active side-lying hip abduction, supine isometric hip extensions, and if able, standing hip extension and abduction (Fig. 2). If strength continues to improve, weights or bands can be introduced. The gluteal nerves are most active during the stance phase of the gait cycle, and therefore, patients can learn to pair donor activation with gluteal use. Strengthening the relationship between donor activation and the gait cycle may improve outcomes.

During the late phase of recovery, patients can begin to challenge their recipient strength by moving against gravity—here, by extending the hip (black arrow) from a standing position. At this stage, patients should begin to isolate the recipient movement from the donor movement (yellow arrow).

Nerve Transfers to Restore Knee Extension

The femoral nerve passes under the inguinal ligament and provides motor branches to the sartorius muscle and the quadriceps muscle group. Injuries to the femoral nerve will result in quadriceps wasting and weakened knee extension. The weak quadriceps can lead to knee buckling and a shortened stride length. Nerve transfers borrow from redundant action in the obturator, sciatic, and femoral nerves to help restore ambulation. Patients should maintain hamstring flexibility and knee ROM due to muscle imbalance around the knee.

Considerations

Patients with femoral nerve injuries may require knee bracing to protect against joint buckling and falls. Surgical restoration may reduce the need for bracing and improve independent mobility. The decision to continue brace use should be based on patient preference and therapist guidance.

Obturator Nerve Transfer to Femoral Nerve

Early Phase

Patients can easily activate the obturator network with hip adductor squeezes from a side-lying or seated position. Instruct the patient to place an object (ie, a fist, ball, towel, or pillow) between their knees and squeeze to flood the donor muscles (Fig. 3).

Early phase obturator nerve activation is achieved by squeezing an object (yellow arrows) between the patient’s legs.

Middle Phase

We recommend starting with gravity-assisted knee extension and progressing to gravity-eliminated exercises. A simple gravity-assisted exercise is heel slides from a hook-lying position. While the patient is squeezing an object between their knees, ask them to extend their legs fully during each repetition. A smooth surface beneath the heels is recommended. Patients may also move to gravity-eliminating exercises, performed from a side-lying position (Fig. 4). Knee extension repetitions in a pool can encourage motor recovery.

From the side-lying position, while the patient adducts their hips (yellow arrows), the patient should attempt knee extension (black arrow). The patient should try to extend both knees for improved visual reinforcement; however, extending only the subject knee is also acceptable. A low-friction surface is advisable.

Late Phase

We recommend starting with an exercise from a long-sitting position. Instruct the patient to place a towel beneath their knee and then to extend the knee against gravity by applying downward pressure on the towel with concomitant hip adduction. Patients can advance to seated knee extensions against gravity and then to static holds at full extension (Fig. 5). As strength and endurance improve, weights and/or bands may be introduced to further recovery. Hip adduction and knee extension are both active during the stance phase, so gait relearning may be less challenging than other transfers.

Knee extensions (black arrow) against gravity. Concomitant hip adduction is only used if necessary.

Sciatic Fascicular Transfer to Femoral Nerve Branches

Early Phase

The sciatic nerve network can be flooded depending on the fascicles used during surgery. The long-sitting position allows the patient to visualize their quadriceps during donor activation, which can provide positive feedback during recovery. Encourage patients to maintain hamstring flexibility.

Middle Phase

Introduce knee extension activities such as those described earlier for obturator transfers. However, toe extension or toe flexion is paired with each knee extension movement. Instruct the patient to dorsiflex their ankle and toes before initiating heel slide repetitions.

Late Phase

We encourage the long-sitting position before progressing to extension from sitting or standing (Fig. 6). For this nerve transfer, swimming is a great exercise to build endurance in both the recipient and donor muscle groups. Plantarflexion is synergistic with knee extension during kicking in the pool. Gait relearning will involve pairing toe movements with the late swing and stance phase of the gait cycle.

Using a soft object (ie, a ball or towel), the patient activates knee extension by applying a downward force on the ball (black arrow). Meanwhile, donor activation against resistance (yellow arrow) can improve nerve conduction down the new neural network.

Sartorius Nerve Branches to Branches of the Femoral Nerve

Considerations

The sartorius contributes weakly to several movements of the hip and knee—adding to hip flexion, knee flexion, and internal rotation of the leg. The motor branch to the sartorius is an ideal donor to the quadriceps because it lies close to the femoral nerve recipients, and the function of the muscle is expendable.17

Early Phase

Start with hip flexion repetitions—bring knees to chest, from either a seated or hook-lying position. From a long-sitting position, internal leg rotation repetitions can also activate the sartorius (Fig. 7). Alternatively, supine hip abduction repetitions can be used.

Internal rotation of the surgically repaired leg can activate the nerve network supplying the sartorius muscle (yellow arrow).

Middle Phase

When a motor twitch occurs in the quadriceps, sartorius movements need to be combined with knee extension. Knee extension with hip flexion can be combined with a gravity-eliminating side-lying position; instruct the patient to bring their knee to their chest while attempting to extend the knee. Alternatively, instruct the patient to lie prone and abduct their hips while letting gravity assist with their knee extension.

Late Phase

Start in the long-sitting position (internal rotation of the leg for knee extension against gravity) before progressing to active knee extension and static holds from a sitting or standing position. Aquatic therapy may be beneficial because hip flexion and knee extension are used during swimming.

Nerve Transfers to Restore Ankle Function

The ankle is a fundamental piece of the kinematic chain of the lower leg.18 Loss of ankle plantar flexion via the tibial nerve affects the stance phase and strength of propulsion during toe-off of the gait cycle. Peroneal nerve injuries cause impaired ankle dorsiflexion resulting in foot drop and a steppage gait pattern, increasing the risk of falls. In addition to motor deficits, loss of ankle stability impairs balance and may result in knee and hip injuries.18

Terminal Femoral Nerve Branches to the Tibial Nerve

Considerations

Femoral branches to the vastus medialis and vastus lateralis are used to innervate the medial and lateral gastrocnemius branches of the tibial nerve, respectively. If the nerve transfer is under tension or if nerve grafting was required, patients may need a hinged knee brace in the immediate postoperative period. At the 3-week mark, the knee brace may be removed. In the setting of a high sciatic nerve injury, gastrocnemius nerve reinnervation is the priority for restoration of gait.19 Ankle-foot orthotics (AFOs) are used perioperatively for lower leg stability.

Early Phase

Patients can perform hourly knee extension repetitions from a long-sitting, seated, or standing position. Due to muscle changes around the knee joint, patients should maintain passive ROM of the knee. Additionally, patients should stretch the quadriceps, gastrocnemius, and anterior tibialis. An AFO should be used during recovery.

Middle Phase

Introduce active knee extension with plantarflexion of the ankle and toes. Isometric plantarflexion exercises at full knee extension also combine donor and recipient movements. Alternatively, patients can combine plantarflexion to their “early phase” routine; however, they will likely require active assistance to achieve plantarflexion. A side-lying position may be used to reduce the effects of gravity. Additionally, applying resistance to knee extension may encourage a stronger plantar contraction. Aquatic therapy is excellent for these patients because water promotes plantarflexion during underwater knee extensions.

Later Phase

The patient may initiate light resistance band plantarflexion repetitions (Fig. 8). Patients may progress to shuttle heel raises when they reach full ROM against the resistance band without significant fatigue. If the patient continues to progress, they can attempt standing heel raises. Swimming is an excellent option to improve strength and endurance after this transfer.

Resistance bands can be used during the late phase of recovery. Using a light band, apply a gentle dorsiflexion force and perform plantarflexion repetitions (black arrow) to strengthen the gastrocnemius.

Partial Tibial Nerve Transfers to Peroneal Nerve

Considerations

Most patients who have experienced a peroneal nerve injury will require an AFO to prevent tripping and falls. Once a muscle twitch is observed on examination, patients can begin testing activity without the AFO. However, to avoid fatigue, an AFO is encouraged throughout recovery until 4/5 strength or sufficient endurance is achieved. The primary goal of these nerve transfers is to improve mobility; however, many patients will always require some degree of AFO use.

Early Phase

The most common donor fascicles are those that control toe flexion; therefore, donor flooding is achieved with toe flexion repetitions. A long-sitting position can be used to provide visual feedback during donor activation. Another option is using a band or belt at the forefoot to passively hold the foot in dorsiflexion while completing toe flexion repetitions. This position combines donor and recipient movements without taxing the recipient nerve network. Patients should also maintain full ankle ROM and lower leg flexibility. Nighttime splinting of the ankle in dorsiflexion is often needed to maintain full ROM.

Middle Phase

Synergistic activation of the donor and recipient muscle groups is challenging after this transfer. Patients can use a belt or band to support dorsiflexion repetitions. Instruct the patient to flex their toes and use the band to initiate dorsiflexion repetitions, resetting to a plantarflexed position after each repetition (Fig. 9). Additionally, incorporating functional activity may improve outcomes.20 For example, place 2 shallow hoops on the ground and place marbles within 1. Instruct patients to move the marbles from 1 hoop to the other using just their toes. Patients will require toe flexion and ankle dorsiflexion to complete this task. The visual feedback and active participation may facilitate motor recovery.

Synergistic activation of donor and recipient muscle groups is challenging following a tibial-to-peroneal nerve transfer. To concomitantly fire the involved neural networks, the toes are flexed (yellow arrow), and the patient engages in dorsiflexion repetitions (black arrow). The band is useful in supporting the foot and helping achieve full ROM during the earlier stages of recovery.

Late Phase

If a patient progresses to 3/5 strength, resisted dorsiflexion exercises are recommended. Patients may also benefit from further strengthening of plantarflexion and toe flexion, encouraging strong signals through the tibial nerve network. Assisted or unassisted walking will continue to normalize the natural movements associated with ambulation; however, due to the risk of excessive fatigue and falling, continued AFO use is strongly encouraged.

CONCLUSIONS

Nerve transfers are mainstream treatment options for PNIs. Surgical techniques for lower extremity nerve transfers continue to evolve; however, nerve transfers are known to be an effective solution for restoring function. Targeted rehabilitation protocols after nerve transfers are imperative to their success. DAFRA has been shown to improve patient outcomes, and this article provided detailed rehabilitation protocols for use after lower extremity nerve transfers. This is not an exhaustive list of lower extremity nerve transfers or available exercises, but by understanding the anatomy of nerve transfers and the principles of DAFRA, a targeted rehabilitation protocol can be designed to maximize outcomes for any nerve transfer.
