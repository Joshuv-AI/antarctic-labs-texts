Lineage-specific decoupling of arginine vasotocin-evoked aggression in male darters (Percidae: Etheostomatinae)
Authors: Taylor N. Black, Kyle Grundy, Isabelle Mendoza, Rachel L. Moran
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The repeated evolution of parental care is often accompanied by shifts in the neural circuits and hormonal pathways that regulate motivation and aggression. Nonapeptides of the oxytocin/vasopressin family, including arginine vasotocin (AVT) in teleost fishes, modulate diverse social behaviors across vertebrates such as courtship, pair bonding, parental care, and territoriality. The mechanism behind conservation of these neuromodulators during behavioral diversification remains unclear. We used a comparative pharmacological approach across six darter species (Percidae: Etheostomatinae) by injecting either AVT, an AVT receptor antagonist (Manning’s compound), or a vehicle control (saline), followed by physiological and behavioral monitoring in standardized intruder assays. AVT administration significantly increased respiration rate, quantifiable as opercular beats per minute (OBPM) across species, indicating conserved AVT-responsive physiology. In contrast, AVT-evoked aggression varied sharply among lineages: AVT robustly elevated fin flare displays in caregiving species (Etheostoma podostemone, E. nigrum, E. flabellare, and Nothonotus starnesi) and in one non-care species with intense male-male competition (N. rufilineatus), but had no effect in E. vitreum, which has secondarily lost paternal care and spawns communally. This implies that AVT signaling remains physiologically intact, but its behavioral coupling has been selectively strengthened or lost across evolutionary transitions in parental care. These results identify lineage-specific rewiring of nonapeptide control of aggression and establish a framework for cellular and molecular dissection of AVT receptor expression and circuit remodeling in the vertebrate social brain.

Introduction

Parental care behavior plays a fundamental role in offspring health and has evolved multiple times across vertebrates (Alonso-Alvarez and Velando, 2012; Barbasch et al., 2022; Maciejewski and Bell, 2022). Broadly defined, parental care includes any behavior that increases the survival and fitness of an individual’s offspring. Caregivers incur fitness costs such as increased energy expenditure and predation risk, reduced foraging time, and limitations on lifetime reproductive output. These direct and indirect costs and benefits have likely driven the astonishing diversity of strategies we see in parental care across the animal kingdom, with variation present at the species, sex, and individual level (Alonso-Alvarez and Velando, 2012; Smith and Wootton, 1995; Goldberg et al., 2020). For example, care is predominantly provided by mothers (i.e., maternal care) in mammals, predominantly biparental in birds, and predominantly provided by fathers (i.e., paternal care) in fishes (Maciejewski and Bell, 2022). The type of care provided is also highly variable, ranging from protecting embryos against predation to extensive, long-term provisioning for offspring (Amundsen, 2003; Barbasch et al., 2022; Furness and Capellini, 2019; Mock, 2022; Wesolowski, 1994).

Despite this striking variation, the evolution of caregiving has been repeatedly linked to modulation by the oxytocin/vasopressin family of nonapeptides acting in hypothalamic–limbic circuits to organize social behavior in vertebrates (Balment et al., 2006; Wilczynski et al., 2017). In mammals, vasopressin (AVP) regulates paternal responsiveness, territorial aggression, pair bonding, and social recognition (Balment et al., 2006; Lonstein & Vries, 1999; Bayerl and Bosch, 2019; O’Connell and Hofmann, 2012). Variation in AVPR1A expression and receptor distribution predicts species differences in social organization, and experimental manipulation of receptor signaling alters both affiliative and parental behaviors (Lonstein & Vries, 1999; Bosch and Neumann, 2012; Nephew and Bridges, 2008a; Nephew and Bridges, 2008b). In birds and amphibians, vasotocin and mesotocin (homologs to mammalian vasopressin and oxytocin, respectively) influence care-related behaviors such as pair bonding, mate choice, egg attendance, and offspring transport (Goodson and Bass, 2001; Goodson et al., 2012; Guoynes and Marler, 2025; Lema et al., 2015; Schulte and Summers, 2017; O’Bryant and Wilczynski, 2010). Perturbations of nonapeptide pathways can suppress or alter parental behavior (Bendesky et al., 2017; DeAngelis et al., 2020; Dan et al., 2024). Teleost fishes, which display exceptional diversity in reproductive strategies, possess homologous peptides (arginine vasotocin [AVT] and isotocin) that modulate aggression, dominance rank, courtship, and parental effort (Balment et al., 2006; Backström and Winberg, 2009; Baylis, 1981; Culbert et al., 2024; Kagawa et al., 2013). In cichlids, sticklebacks, and gobies, AVT neuron activity in the preoptic area predicts variation in parental care, and pharmacological manipulations robustly shift territoriality and nest defense (Almeida and Oliveira, 2015; Almeida et al., 2012; Balshine and Abate, 2021; Huffman et al., 2015; Oldfield et al., 2013; Dan et al., 2024; Kulczykowska and Kleszczyńska, 2014; Sokołowska et al., 2020; Sokołowska et al., 2015; Maruska et al., 2007).

Despite this trait conservation, the evolutionary lability of parental care raises a fundamental question in neuroendocrinology: How are ancient neuromodulatory systems repurposed as social behaviors diverge? Model systems permit cell-level mechanistic resolution; but provide limited behavioral diversity. Conversely, comparative field systems reveal rich diversity; but rarely allow direct experimental leverage on neuropeptide function. Systems that bridge these scales, combining natural evolutionary variation with manipulable neuroendocrine pathways, are rare and powerful.

A lineage of freshwater fishes, commonly referred to as darters (Percidae: Etheostomatinae) provide an excellent opportunity to study the neural and hormonal changes associated with gain and loss of parental care. This North American radiation of over 250 known species includes multiple independent gains of male-only parental care and at least two well-supported secondary losses, representing natural evolutionary replicates for investigating the mechanism of neuromodulatory circuit evolution (MacGuigan and Near, 2019; Near et al., 2011; Carlson and Wainwright, 2010; Page and Swofford, 1984). Caregiving males select nest cavities, fan and clean eggs, and vigorously defend territories; whereas closely related species spawn communally and show little aggression or parental investment (Kelly et al., 2012; Bandoli, 2016; Bandoli, 2006; Constantz, 1985; Grant and Colgan, 1984; Knapp and Sargent, 1989; Knouft et al., 2003; Knouft and Page, 2004; Winn and Picciolo, 1960). Within Etheostoma, care has been gained once in the clade Goneaperca, where caregiving species exhibit robust male-only nest defense and egg care (Fig. 1) (Kelly et al., 2012; Harrington and Near, 2015). Two clades within Goneaperca exhibit secondary loss of paternal care: only one of these loss-of-care lineages (the glassy darter, E. vitreum), is unique in that it a) does not appear to exhibit any male-male aggression and b) spawns communally (i.e., multiple males and females release sperm and eggs simultaneously, and fertilized eggs are attached to substrate and abandoned) (Kelly et al., 2012; Harrington and Near, 2015; Heckman et al., 2009; Winn and Picciolo, 1960). Independent contrasts also arise in multiple Nothonotus darter lineages that differ in presence or absence of nest site territoriality and male-only paternal care (Keck and Near, 2008; Near and Keck, 2005).

Here, we use a natural radiation with replicated gains and losses of male-only parental care to understand how a conserved nonapeptide system, arginine vasotocin (AVT), is wired into the vertebrate social decision-making network to modulate territorial aggression. Territorial aggression becomes pronounced in several paternal caregiving species of darter during the spawning season, when males establish and defend nesting substrates such as rocks, logs, or vegetation. Females deposit eggs on these guarded substrates, after which males fertilize and subsequently guard, clean, and aerate the developing eggs until hatching. In this context, male–male aggression functions as a key component of paternal care, specifically through nest defense. To investigate the neuroendocrine mechanisms underlying this behavior, we combine comparative natural history with experimental manipulation of AVT signaling. Using a standardized male–male intruder assay and pharmacological manipulation of AVT signaling across six species, we ask: (1) Is the physiological sensitivity to AVT conserved across lineages, as would be expected if core AVT signaling pathways remain intact?, and (2) Has the behavioral output of AVT, specifically territorial aggression, diverged across species that differ in the presence or absence of male–male aggression?

Because darters represent a natural evolutionary gradient in parental behavior, this system allows us to assess how neuromodulator-sensitive circuits are evolutionarily rewired. By embedding pharmacological experiments within a phylogenetically structured behavioral radiation, we begin to identify general principles governing how conserved neuropeptide systems generate species-specific social phenotypes.

Methods

Animal collection and husbandry

Adult males of Etheostoma vitreum, E. podostemone, E. flabellare, and E. nigrum were collected from streams in Virginia during the breeding season (March–May) and transported to the Mountain Lake Biological Station (MLBS), Virginia (Table S1). Adult males of Nothonotus rufilineatus and N. starnesi were collected from streams in Tennessee during the spawning season (May–July) and transported to the lab at Texas A&M University (Table S1). Species were chosen based on paternal care state and phylogenetic relationship (Fig. 1). Males and females were housed under ambient temperature and photoperiod in aquaria supplied with natural pond water and substrate. Females were maintained in 38-L group aquaria, while stimulus males were held individually in 11.4-L tanks to facilitate identification and record keeping. All focal and stimulus males were size-matched within 5 mm standard length to control for size-dependent dominance. All trials were conducted within the week of collection.

Pharmacological assays

For each species, focal males were randomly assigned to one of three treatments: exogenous AVT (4 μL g−1, at a concentration of 0.4 μg/g), Manning’s compound (4 μL g−1, at a concentration of 0.4 μg/g; a vasotocin/vasopressin V1a receptor antagonist with minimal antidiuretic effects), or a saline vehicle control (0.9% saline, 10 μL g−1) (Lema et al., 2019; Shaw et al., 2023). Intraperitoneal injections were performed using 29-gauge syringes. Immediately after injection, each male was placed into an individual 11.4-L observation tank provided with a ceramic tile nest site. Focal males were allowed a standardized 5-minute recovery period to ensure systemic distribution of the hormone before the introduction of a conspecific, size-matched stimulus male. This standardized recovery period was determined from preliminary observations conducted for three days prior to experimental trials. During this time, injected males consistently resumed normal posture and began moving around the tank within several minutes of injection and began exhibiting physiological responses to treatment including changes in respiration rate and coloration. The observation trial began upon introduction of the stimulus male and lasted 10 min total.

We scored directed fin flares, chases, bites, and attacks by the focal male toward the stimulus male. Following Moran et al. (2017) and Moran and Fuller (2018), fin flares were analyzed as the primary aggression measure because they were the most frequent and reliably identified across species (Moran et al., 2017; Moran and Fuller, 2018). Respiration rate was measured as opercular beats per minute (OBPM), recorded by counting opercular beats for 15 s (and then multiplying by 4 to convert to beats per minute) at the beginning, middle and end of each assay as an index of physiological response to AVT (Proczka et al., 2021).

At the end of each trial, focal males were euthanized by rapid decapitation, and whole brains were dissected within 120 s. Brains were flash-frozen for later molecular analyses by immersion in chilled methanol placed inside a dry-ice bath. All procedures were conducted in accordance with the NIH Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and were approved by the Texas A&M University Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC protocol number 2023–0007). Our methods conform to ARRIVE guidelines.

Statistical analyses

Fin flare counts were analyzed with negative-binomial GLMMs (glmmTMB R package) including Treatment, Species, and their interaction (Treatment x Species) as fixed effects, with weight (g) of the focal male as a covariate (Bolker, 2016). The year was tested as a random intercept and dropped when non-informative. Model diagnostics using DHARMa indicated no evidence of overdispersion (dispersion = 1.01, p = 0.83) or zero-inflation (observed/predicted zero ratio = 0.95, p = 0.57), confirming that the negative-binomial GLMM provided an adequate fit for the data (Florian, 2016). Type-III Wald χ2 tests (car package) evaluated fixed effects, and estimated marginal means (EMMs; emmeans R package) were reported on the response scale (Gallant and Tauchen, 1993).

OBPM was analyzed with Gaussian GLMMs including Treatment, Species, and interaction. Dunnett tests (dunnettx R package) contrasted AVT and Manning’s to Saline. All plots were generated in R with ggplot2 (Wickham, 2011). For the Gaussian OBPM model, we quantified effect sizes for main effects and interactions as partial η2 from Type III sums of squares. For the negative binomial fin flare model, we quantified effect size at the model level as a deviance-based pseudo-R2 (1 − deviance_full / deviance_null) and for planned pairwise comparisons as incidence rate ratios (IRRs) and their 95% confidence intervals, obtained from the exponentiated treatment contrasts of the glmmTMB model. We follow recommendations in Laken’s (2013) for reporting effect sizes alongside test statistics (Lakens, 2013).

Results

AVT increases respiration rate across species

AVT treatment significantly elevated OBPM relative to both treatment with Manning’s compound and the vehicle control (χ2 = 40.49, df = 2, p < 0.001). Baseline OBPM differed among species (χ2 = 221.62, df = 5, p < 0.001), but Treatment x Species was non-significant (χ2 = 5.49, df = 10, p = 0.86), indicating a conserved physiological response (Fig. 2). Dunnett contrasts on EMMs (species-averaged) confirmed AVT treatment resulted in greater OMPB compared to the saline vehicle control (Δ = 30.53 ± 5.47 SE, t90 = 5.59, p < 0.0001) and no Manning’s vs. saline difference (Δ = 1.05 ± 5.41 SE, p = 0.96). For OBPM, partial η2 indicated medium-to-large effects of Treatment (η2 = 0.29), strong species differences (η2 = 0.67), and only a small interaction effect (η2 = 0.05), following Lakens (2013).

Lineage-specific divergence in AVT-evoked aggression

Fin flare counts significantly differed among species (χ2 = 24.60, df = 5, p < 0.001); overall treatment main effect was not significant (χ2 = 0.00, df = 2, p = 0.999). Although the Treatment x Species interaction was not significant (χ2 = 15.09, df = 10, p = 0.13), EMMs revealed pronounced differences among lineages (Figs. 3–4): AVT elevated aggression in E. podostemone, N. starnesi, and N. rufilineatus, and modestly in E. flabellare and E. nigrum, but produced no increase in E. vitreum (AVT mean ± SE = 0.33 ± 0.20 vs. saline 0.00 ± 0.00). Zero fractions (no flares) were lowest in E. flabellare (0.00) and highest in E. vitreum (0.78), indicating strong baseline differences in aggressive propensity. Stimulus males mirrored these trends; intruders paired with aggressive AVT-treated conspecific focal males often flared more, and those paired with saline controls remained largely passive.

Within species, AVT tended to elevate aggressive displays relative to saline in caregiving lineages, with IRRs ranging from 1.6 to 3.8 in E. flabellare, E. nigrum, and E. podostemone and 8.3 in N. starnesi (Table S1). Confidence intervals were wide, especially for E. podostemone, reflecting modest sample sizes, but the direction of effect was consistently positive. In E. vitreum and N. rufilineatus, IRRs were extremely large with confidence intervals extending from near zero to infinity, reflecting sparse flare counts and providing no evidence for a reliable AVT-induced increase in aggression despite the formal ratios. The negative binomial model explained ~27% of the deviance in flare counts relative to an intercept-only model (pseudo-R2 deviance = 0.27), indicating modest but non-trivial explanatory power of Treatment and Species for aggression.

Discussion

Our comparative pharmacological experiments reveal that conserved nonapeptide pathways can produce strikingly different behavioral outputs across closely related species. Across all six darter species tested, AVT consistently increased opercular beat rate, confirming that the core physiological machinery of vasotocin signaling remains intact even in the lineage that has lost territoriality and paternal care. By contrast, AVT-induced aggression varied dramatically among species: aggressive behavior (fin flares) markedly increased in caregiving lineages and in a non-care lineage with intense male–male competition, modestly increased in a subset of caregiving Etheostoma, and remained entirely absent in E. vitreum, the only darter species that is known to have secondarily lost nest territoriality and paternal care. These results demonstrate that the behavioral coupling as opposed to the underlying physiology of AVT signaling has been selectively rewired across evolutionary time.

The result of strong, species-invariant increase in opercular beats per minute following AVT treatment confirms that AVT retains its conserved cardiovascular and osmoregulatory roles across darters, consistent with findings from other teleosts and vertebrates (Sangiao-Alvarellos et al., 2006; Balment et al., 2006). This conserved physiological response is important because it shows that the absence of AVT-evoked aggression in E. vitreum does not reflect a global disruption of AVT sensitivity or receptor function. Instead, it indicates that downstream integration points linking AVT signaling to aggressive motor patterns have been modified after the evolutionary loss of paternal care. This pattern aligns with a central theme in neuroendocrinology: neuromodulators often act through distributed circuit nodes, and evolutionary changes can selectively alter specific nodes without disrupting the entire system (Roberts et al., 2022; Shine, 2023; Silva et al., 2013). The maintenance of robust physiological responses under AVT in E. vitreum suggests that AVT facilitates the activation of aggression circuits in the presence of appropriate social stimuli. In species where intrasexual aggression has been reduced or lost, such as E. vitreum, there may be limited scope for AVT to potentiate these circuits.

Teleost aggression and parental care are regulated by the social decision-making network (SDMN), a conserved suite of hypothalamic–limbic nuclei whose connectivity and neuromodulator receptor expression shape social behavior (O’Connell et al., 2012). Our results are consistent with the hypothesis that divergence in AVT-evoked aggression reflects evolutionary changes in how AVT signaling influences components of this network, rather than changes at the peptide or receptor level alone. In caregiving species such as E. podostemone and N. starnesi, AVT strongly facilitates territorial aggression. Strong behavioral activation by AVT in these lineages mirrors findings in biparental cichlids and sticklebacks, where heightened nonapeptide signaling promotes nest defense and offspring care (Balshine and Abate, 2021; Barbasch et al., 2022). In contrast, the communally spawning E. vitreum shows no behavioral response to AVT despite preserved physiological response, suggesting that the coupling between AVT signaling and aggression has been reduced or lost in this lineage. One possible explanation is that evolutionary changes in neural sensitivity to AVT within the SDMN have altered the extent to which AVT modulates aggression-related circuits. However, our data do not directly resolve the neural mechanisms underlying these differences, and future work examining AVT receptor expression and neural activity across species will be necessary to test these hypotheses.

The two Nothonotus species provide a complementary evolutionary contrast. Both show strong AVT-induced aggression even though one (N. rufilineatus) lacks parental care. This outcome indicates that AVT–aggression coupling can evolve independently of care and suggests that male–male competition alone can maintain or amplify AVT sensitivity in aggression circuits. Similar patterns are documented in wrasses, damselfishes, and cichlids, where intensity of territorial competition predicts AVT neuron number and receptor expression independent of reproductive strategy (Huffman et al., 2015; Santangelo and Bass, 2010; Warner et al., 1995). Darters therefore recapitulate a broader vertebrate theme: nonapeptide systems are evolvable substrates for shaping aggression, affiliative behavior, and parental responses, and selection can act on different behavioral components in parallel.

The mechanistic implications of these findings are considerable. Species differences in AVT-evoked aggression likely map onto spatial and quantitative variation in AVPR1A expression across SDMN nuclei, with caregiving and highly aggressive non-care species predicted to show enriched receptor expression in the preoptic area, ventral telencephalon, or amygdala regions. In E. vitreum, the near absence of AVT-induced aggression suggests either reduced receptor expression in these regions or altered spatial patterning that shifts AVT influence away from aggression circuits. Differences may also arise at the level of cell-type specificity, with certain AVT-responsive neuronal populations diminished, reorganized, or functionally disconnected in E. vitreum. Comparative genomic work further suggests that receptor function itself has evolved: positive selection on avpr1a and galr2 (Galanin-receptor 2) in caregiving lineages (Moran, unpubl. data) implies potential modifications of binding affinity or downstream signaling pathways, changes that could combine with circuit-level rewiring to produce lineage-specific behavioral outputs. Whether E. vitreum has experienced synaptic loss between AVT neurons and aggression circuits, or whether downstream nodes are inhibited or repurposed, remains an open question that forthcoming snRNAseq maps can help resolve.

An important consideration in interpreting these results is the mechanistic pathway through which peripheral AVT administration influences behavior. The increase in opercular beat rate observed across species likely reflects peripheral physiological effects of AVT, as nonapeptides are known to act on cardiovascular and respiratory systems in teleost fishes (Balment et al., 2006). In contrast, the behavioral effects on aggression imply modulation of neural circuits regulating social behavior. Although the degree to which peripherally administered AVT crosses the blood–brain barrier remains uncertain, several mechanisms could link peripheral peptide signaling to central behavioral responses. First, circulating nonapeptides may influence neural activity indirectly through peripheral sensory or autonomic pathways that project to brain regions within the social decision-making network (Godwin and Thompson, 2012; Sokołowska et al., 2020). Second, in teleosts the blood–brain barrier can be more permissive in certain hypothalamic and preoptic regions, potentially allowing limited peptide access to central targets (Abbott et al., 2010; Bernstein and Streicher, 1965; Jeong et al., 2008). Finally, peripheral endocrine signals may alter endogenous neuropeptide release within the brain, thereby modulating central AVT signaling pathways that regulate social behavior (McKinley and Oldfield, 1998; Rose and Moore, 2002; Zhang and Eiden, 2019). Thus, while the present experiments cannot distinguish among these possibilities, the results demonstrate that systemic AVT manipulations are sufficient to modulate both physiological state and aggression in most species examined (Maciejewski and Bell, 2022; DeAngelis et al., 2020). The lineage-specific absence of behavioral responses to AVT in E. vitreum therefore most likely reflects evolutionary changes in the sensitivity of aggression circuits to AVT signaling, rather than differences in peripheral physiological responsiveness.

Finally, this study positions the darter radiation as a uniquely powerful system for neuroendocrine evolution. Neuroethology often relies either on experimentally tractable laboratory species or on natural systems with limited neural tools. Darters offer the rare combination of a replicated evolutionary radiation, fine-scale behavioral diversity, and expanding molecular and circuit-level datasets. Their repeated gains, losses, and modifications of paternal care provide natural experiments for dissecting how neuromodulatory circuits evolve, how the SDMN is developmentally and genomically remodeled, and how neuromodulator–behavior coupling can be selectively loosened, strengthened, or rewired. By demonstrating lineage-specific decoupling of AVT signaling from aggressive behavior, our work underscores the flexibility of vertebrate social circuits and provides a foundation for integrating pharmacology, cell-type–specific expression, neural activity mapping, and evolutionary genomics in a unified framework.
