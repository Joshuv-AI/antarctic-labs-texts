Chronic intussusception revealing primary diffuse large B-cell lymphoma of the small intestine: A case report
Authors: Sarah Lembarki, Fatima Ez-Zahrae El Malki, Fatima Zahra Laamrani, Youssef Omor, Rachida Latib, Sanae Amalik
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Adult intussusception is an uncommon clinical entity, representing approximately 5% of all intussusception cases and 1%-5% of intestinal obstructions in adults. (1) In contrast to the pediatric population, adult intussusception is most often secondary to an underlying organic lesion, frequently a neoplasm. Malignant lesions account for nearly 30%-50% of small bowel cases. (2) Among these, primary small intestinal lymphoma is a rare cause, with diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL) being particularly uncommon. We herein report a case of chronic intermittent intussusception revealing primary DLBCL of the small intestine, emphasizing the diagnostic challenges encountered in this rare presentation.

Introduction

Adult intussusception accounts for less than 1%-5% of all bowel obstructions and differs significantly from the pediatric form, which is predominantly idiopathic [1]. In adults, more than 70%-90% of cases are associated with an identifiable organic lesion acting as a pathological lead point [2], and malignant tumors account for nearly 30%-50% of small bowel cases specifically [3]. The etiological spectrum ranges from benign lesions, such as polyps and lipomas, to malignant tumors, with colonic involvement being more frequently associated with malignancy than small bowel involvement [4].

Primary malignant tumors of the small bowel are rare, representing less than 2%-5% of all gastrointestinal malignancies [5]. Among these, lymphomas constitute an uncommon subset, with diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL) being the most frequent histological subtype of primary gastrointestinal non-Hodgkin lymphoma [6]. The ileum is a well-recognized site of extranodal lymphoma because of its abundant lymphoid tissue, particularly Peyer's patches [5]. However, presentation as intussusception remains an exceptional and diagnostically challenging occurrence.

Although primary gastrointestinal DLBCL is increasingly recognized, its presentation as intussusception remains exceedingly rare, with only isolated case reports published to date, most involving the ileocolic region and, less commonly, isolated small bowel segments. Reported clinical presentations are typically nonspecific and subacute, including abdominal pain, altered bowel habits, gastrointestinal bleeding, weight loss, and anemia, which frequently leads to diagnostic delay [[7], [8], [9]].

We report a case of chronic intussusception secondary to primary small intestinal DLBCL in a young adult, notable for its dual ileocolic and ileo-ileal involvement and for its management with upfront systemic immunochemotherapy without surgical resection, and discuss its diagnostic and therapeutic implications.

Case presentation

A 33-year-old male patient, with a history of chronic tobacco use and occasional alcohol consumption, presented with progressive diffuse abdominal pain. The pain was associated with altered bowel habits, characterized by alternating episodes of diarrhea and constipation, as well as episodes of rectal bleeding. These symptoms had developed gradually in a context of declining general health, including asthenia, weight loss, and loss of appetite.

The patient was referred to our department for an abdominal ultrasound, which revealed an ileocolic digestive mass measuring approximately 90 mm in length. The lesion exhibited a concentric “target” or “bull's-eye” appearance, highly suggestive of intussusception. A contrast-enhanced abdominal computed tomography (CT) was then performed, confirming the presence of 2 sites of intussusception: 1 ileocolic and 1 ileo-ileal, both occurring on a segment of small bowel with demonstrating circumferential, symmetric mural thickening with homogeneous enhancement after contrast administration. Colonoscopy with biopsy was subsequently performed, demonstrating viable tumor foci within large areas of necrosis, consistent with a malignant large-cell tumor proliferation. Histopathological examination, supplemented by immunohistochemical analysis, revealed a proliferation of medium- to large-sized tumor cells positive for CD20 and BCL6, positive for MUM1, and negative for CD10, with a Ki-67 proliferation index of 70%. This immunophenotypic profile was compatible with diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL), nongerminal center B-cell-like (non-GCB) subtype according to the Hans algorithm.

Staging work-up, including contrast-enhanced CT of the chest, abdomen, and pelvis, showed no evidence of extra-abdominal disease, corresponding to Ann Arbor stage II. Serum lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) was within normal limits (98 U/L).

Given the diagnosis of DLBCL, the therapeutic approach consisted of systemic immunochemotherapy. The patient was scheduled to receive the R-CHOP regimen, combining rituximab, cyclophosphamide, doxorubicin, vincristine, and prednisone. This regimen represents the current standard of care for DLBCL and has been shown to significantly improve overall survival and progression-free survival when compared to R-CHOP alone, particularly in young patients. The treatment plan included 6-8 cycles of R-CHOP, with close clinical, biological, and radiological monitoring. Surgical intervention was not indicated in this case, as there were no signs of intestinal perforation or uncontrolled bleeding, and the patient was hemodynamically stable. After 4 cycles of R-CHOP, the patient showed favorable clinical and radiological evolution, with no complications reported.

Discussion

Adult intussusception is an uncommon clinical entity with a presentation that differs markedly from the pediatric form. While the classic triad of abdominal pain, palpable abdominal mass, and currant jelly stool is well recognized in children [10], adult cases usually present with nonspecific and often chronic symptoms, which may delay diagnosis [2,4]. Abdominal pain is the most frequent manifestation and may be associated with nausea, vomiting, altered bowel habits, or gastrointestinal bleeding [11]. Because of this nonspecific presentation, adult intussusception may initially be misdiagnosed as other gastrointestinal disorders such as chronic enteritis, acute appendicitis, irritable bowel syndrome, or intestinal obstruction.

Unlike pediatric cases, which are predominantly idiopathic, 75%-90% of adult intussusceptions are associated with an identifiable pathological lead point, which may be benign or malignant [12]. The mechanism involves telescoping of a proximal bowel segment into the adjacent distal segment under the effect of peristalsis, usually triggered by a fixed structural lesion acting as a lead point [13].

Primary gastrointestinal lymphoma is the most common form of extranodal lymphoma, accounting for approximately 30%-40% of extranodal lymphomas but only a small proportion of all gastrointestinal malignancies [14,15]. Among histological subtypes, diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL) is the most frequent. Although the ileum is a known site of involvement because of its abundant lymphoid tissue, presentation as intussusception remains rare. Compared with the 8 previously reported adult cases of DLBCL-associated intussusception [[16], [17], [18], [19], [20], [21], [22], [23]], our patient is notably younger—only 1 previously published case involved a patient under 40 years of age—and demonstrates a rarer pattern of combined ileocolic and ileo-ileal involvement rather than isolated ileocolic disease. This observation suggests that lymphoma should not be excluded from the differential diagnosis of intussusception in younger adults on the basis of age alone.

Imaging plays a pivotal role in the diagnosis of intussusception and in identifying its underlying lead point. Contrast-enhanced CT is considered the gold standard, owing to its high sensitivity and its ability to demonstrate the characteristic “target” or “donut” sign, while also identifying the lead point and assessing bowel wall abnormalities [1,24].

Beyond confirming intussusception itself, certain CT features suggest an underlying lymphomatous lead point rather than another neoplasm. Typical signs of small bowel lymphoma include circumferential, homogeneous mural thickening with relative preservation of wall enhancement, and—characteristically—aneurysmal luminal dilatation of the involved segment due to destruction of the myenteric plexus and muscularis propria, sometimes containing an air–fluid level. These signs are often accompanied by bulky mesenteric and retroperitoneal lymphadenopathy closely associated with the involved segment, contrasting with adenocarcinoma, which more typically causes short-segment stricture, luminal narrowing, and mesenteric fat infiltration rather than aneurysmal widening [25,26]. In our case, the marked circumferential mural thickening involving both the ileocolic and ileo-ileal segments was consistent with this pattern and raised early suspicion of an underlying lymphoproliferative process. Ultrasonography remains a useful first-line modality because of its accessibility and noninvasive nature, often revealing the classic “target” or “bull’s-eye” sign, as observed in our patient.

Therapeutic management in adults depends on the underlying etiology. Surgical resection is traditionally considered the standard treatment, particularly in cases with suspected malignancy, bowel ischemia, or perforation. However, in hemodynamically stable patients without acute complications, management may be guided by histological diagnosis and oncological considerations. Unlike most previously reported cases [[16], [17], [18], [19], [20], [21], [22]], in which surgical resection was performed either to establish the diagnosis or because of acute complications, our patient's diagnosis was secured endoscopically prior to any surgical intervention. In the absence of perforation, uncontrolled bleeding, or bowel ischemia, and given localized stage II disease with a normal LDH, upfront systemic immunochemotherapy without resection was considered an appropriate individualized approach, with a favorable clinical and radiological response observed after 4 cycles of R-CHOP. In our case, the diagnosis of DLBCL allowed prompt initiation of R-CHOP immunochemotherapy, which remains the current standard treatment [23].

This case highlights the importance of considering lymphoma among the differential diagnoses of chronic adult intussusception and underlines the central role of imaging and histopathological confirmation in guiding management.

Conclusion

Adult intussusception is an uncommon clinical problem, almost always related to an underlying lesion. Primary small intestinal lymphoma is a rare but important cause. Our case illustrates the diagnostic difficulty of chronic intussusception and emphasizes the role of surgery in establishing the diagnosis. Once DLBCL is confirmed, systemic therapy is required, and outcomes in localized disease can be excellent with modern immunochemotherapy. Clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion when evaluating adults with prolonged or recurrent abdominal complaints. In our patient, upfront immunochemotherapy without surgery achieved a favorable early response, supporting a selective, nonoperative approach in stable patients with a secured histological diagnosis and no acute surgical complications.
