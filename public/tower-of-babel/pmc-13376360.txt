Naïve CD4+ T-cells and disease status at CART infusion correlate with clinical outcomes in real-world large B-cell lymphoma patients receiving second-line CAR T therapy

ABSTRACT
Axicabtagene ciloleucel (axi-cel) and lisocabtagene maraleucel (liso-cel) are standard-of-care second-line therapies for relapsed or refractory large B-cell lymphomas, yet clinical and immunological predictors of durable benefit remain poorly defined. Here, we retrospectively analyze 64 patients treated with second-line commercial axi-cel (n = 35) or liso-cel (n = 29). Overall (66%) and complete (55%) response rates are similar between CART products. The 24-month progression-free survival (PFS) and overall survival rates are 46% and 76%, respectively. Primary refractory disease and progressive disease at infusion are associated with inferior response rates on multivariate analysis. Importantly, toxicity rate is relatively low, with 3% of patients experiencing Grade 3-4 cytokine release syndrome (CRS) and immune effector cell-associated neurotoxicity syndrome (ICANS). Furthermore, lower CD4+ T-cell counts at apheresis correlate with inferior PFS. Immunophenotyping reveals that higher naïve CD4+ T-cell counts at apheresis predict durable response in axi-cel-treated patients. Greater CART expansion, enrichment for naïve CD8+ CAR+ T-cells, and lower PD1 expression at day 7 post-infusion are associated with response. Thus, these findings highlight the efficacy of second-line CART and identify disease and immune-related predictors of long-term response.

Introduction

Large B-cell lymphomas (LBCL) are the most prevalent subtype of non-Hodgkin lymphoma, with approximately 150,000 new cases diagnosed annually worldwide1. Although many patients achieve long-term remission with frontline immunochemotherapy2, approximately 40% of them experience primary refractory disease or relapse3.

CD19-targeted chimeric antigen receptor T-cell immunotherapy (CART) has revolutionized the treatment landscape for patients with relapsed/refractory (R/R) LBCL. Building on early findings4–10, CART was investigated as a second-line treatment in patients with primary refractory disease or relapse within 12 months of initial immunochemotherapy11,12. In randomized trials, axicabtagene-ciloleucel (axi-cel)13,14 and lisocabtagene maraleucel (liso-cel)15,16 outperformed standard-of-care therapy, establishing second-line CART as the preferred treatment for this high-risk population17.

Clinical characteristics and T-cell phenotypes in both apheresis samples and CART products have been linked to treatment efficacy and toxicity. High disease burden and elevated lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) have been associated with poor outcomes18,19. Additionally, correlative data from the ZUMA-714 trial, along with other preclinical20 and clinical18,21–23 studies, suggest that CART products enriched for less differentiated T-cells are associated with improved survival. Furthermore, features such as CD4:CD8 ratio, differentiation status, and exhaustion during CART manufacturing have been associated with in vivo T-cell expansion, persistence, and clinical outcomes24–27. However, to date, there are limited data published on the efficacy of second-line CART for R/R LBCL in real-world settings, and a comprehensive analysis of clinical, disease, and T-cell-related features as predictors of efficacy has yet to be explored.

To address these knowledge gaps, here we conduct a retrospective analysis of all consecutive patients treated with second-line commercial CART at our academic center and perform comprehensive immunophenotypic analyses on apheresis samples and in vivo CART collected on days 7 and 28 post-infusion. We identify primary refractory disease and progressive disease at the time of infusion as key clinical predictors of poor disease response and survival outcomes. Our correlative analyses demonstrate that higher naïve CD4⁺ T-cell counts at apheresis, greater CART expansion, a higher proportion of naïve CD8⁺ CART cells, and a lower proportion of PD-1⁺ CART cells at peak expansion are associated with improved clinical response and longer progression-free survival (PFS). These results have implications for prognosticating which patients are most likely to benefit from CART therapy and for the development of future generations of CART products.

Results

Efficacy of second-line CART for LBCL in the real-world

Sixty-four consecutive patients with R/R LBCL treated with anti-CD19 CART between May 2022 and October 2024 at a single academic center were included in this analysis. Their clinical characteristics are summarized in Table 1. The median age was 62 (range: 21–79) years, and 56% were male. Thirty-five patients (55%) received axi-cel, while the remaining 29 patients (45%) were treated with liso-cel (Table 1). Notable differences in baseline characteristics between liso-cel and axi-cel recipients were older median age (69 vs. 58 years; p < 0.01), greater proportion with IPI > 2 (65% vs. 17%; p < 0.01), more frequent use of systemic bridging therapy (86% vs. 60%; p = 0.03), and longer median vein-to-vein time (43 vs. 36 days; p = 0.02) in liso-cel patients (Table 1). The best overall response (ORR) and complete response rates (CRR) were 66% and 55%, respectively (Fig. 1A). No significant differences in ORR (axi-cel 63% vs. liso-cel 69%; p = 0.79) and CRR (axi-cel 51% vs. liso-cel 58%; p = 0.62) were observed between axi-cel and liso-cel. With a median follow-up of 25 months, the 24-month PFS was 46% (Fig. 1B). The 24-month overall survival (OS) was 76% (Fig. 1C). The 24-month duration of response (DOR) was 68% (Fig. 1D). Clinical correlates of CART efficacy

We performed a univariate logistic regression analysis to identify clinical factors associated with achieving CR following CART. Post-bridging imaging performed at a median time of 14 days before CART infusion was available for 94% of patients. Patients with stable disease or disease response (PR or CR) at the time of their last imaging study performed prior to infusion were considered to have achieved disease control (73% of patients). We next performed a multivariate analysis including all variables that were statistically significant in our univariate analysis. Relapsed rather than refractory disease and disease control at infusion were independently associated with higher day-90 CRRs in multivariate analysis (Fig. 2B).

Patients with disease control had significantly superior 24-month PFS (10% vs. 59%, p < 0.01; Fig. 2C) and 24-month OS (42% vs. 91%; p < 0.01; Fig. 2D) compared to those with progressive disease (PD) at infusion. Patients who were primary refractory to front-line therapy experienced significantly inferior 24-month OS compared to those with relapsed disease (66% vs. 96%; p < 0.01; Fig. 2E).

Noting that patients without disease control at the time of CART infusion had poor response and survival outcomes (Fig. 2A–D), we wanted to assess how clinical surrogates of disease burden at the time of CART infusion impacted long-term patient outcomes. We used durable response (DR), defined as patients who remained in remission at least 12 months after CART infusion, as a clinically meaningful endpoint. Stage III-IV disease (p = 0.02), END (p < 0.01), and bulky disease (maximum diameter >7.5 cm) (p < 0.01) were all significantly associated with decreased DR (Supplementary Fig. 2A–D). Notably, all nine patients with bulky disease and all five patients with LDH greater than two times the upper limit of normal (ULN) at the time of infusion failed to achieve a DR (Supplementary Fig. 2D, E). Finally, to more precisely measure disease burden, we evaluated total metabolic tumor volume (TMTV)28 at the time of CART infusion in thirty-one patients (48%) with PET/CT scans available for TMTV assessment. Among these patients, a higher TMTV was also associated with decreased likelihood of DR (p = 0.02) (Supplementary Fig. 2F). Clinical surrogates of high disease burden at the time of first relapse including stage III-IV disease (p = 0.38), END (p = 0.99), bulky disease (p = 0.73), and LDH (p = 0.15) were not significantly associated with DR (Supplementary Fig. 2G–J), highlighting the importance of disease volume at CART infusion, as opposed to the time of relapse, as a determinant of clinical outcomes.

Toxicity of second-line CART for LBCL in the real-world

In our cohort, CRS of any grade occurred in 33 patients (52%), while severe CRS (grade ≥3) occurred in 2 patients (3%). ICANS of any grade were observed in 8 patients (13%), while grade ≥3 ICANS occurred in 2 patients (3%) (Fig. 3A). By day 90 post-infusion, persistent cytopenias were infrequent, and most cytopenias were mild: anemia was seen in 47% of patients (only 5% grade ≥3), thrombocytopenia in 22% (grade ≥3 in 3%), and neutropenia in 20% (grade ≥3 in 8%) (Fig. 3B). The incidence of grade ≥3 late immune effector cell-associated hematotoxicity (ICAHT)29 was 3% (Supplementary Fig. 3A). Thirteen patients received transfusions in the 90 days after CART infusion; six received packed red blood cells (pRBC), six received platelet transfusions, and one patient received both pRBC and platelet transfusions. Fifteen (23%) patients were diagnosed with a total of 17 laboratory-confirmed infections in the first 90 days following CART infusion; six viral, ten bacterial, and one fungal infection were documented. Nine of these infections led to patient hospitalization. Clinical correlates of toxicity

In univariate analyses, patients treated with liso-cel had significantly lower rates of any grade CRS compared to those treated with axi-cel (21% vs. 80%; OR = 0.05, [95%CI = 0.01–0.19], p < 0.01; Fig. 3C). Patients with LDH greater than the ULN had higher rates of any grade ICANS compared to those with normal LDH levels (24% vs. 5%; OR = 6.33 [95%CI = 1.2–34.5], p = 0.03; Fig. 3D). The relatively low incidence of grade 3-4 CRS and ICANS precluded performing statistical analyses for predictors of high-grade toxicity. Patients who received fludarabine/cyclophosphamide lymphodepletion had higher rates of grade ≥2 late ICAHT than those receiving bendamustine (43% vs. 4%, p = 0.01; Supplementary Fig. 3B).

Immune cell composition at apheresis correlates with clinical responses

We then investigated whether the immune cell composition at apheresis was associated with clinical efficacy and toxicity in patients receiving CART. As a first step, we analyzed peripheral white blood cell (WBC) counts across the entire patient cohort. As expected, neutrophils represented the predominant WBC subset (median 75%, IQR: 67–80, p < 0.01), while lymphocytes and monocytes were present at similar levels (14% and 12%, respectively). However, the proportions of neutrophils, lymphocytes, and monocytes did not correlate with response (medians for non-responders vs. responders: neutrophils 78% vs. 73%, lymphocytes 11% vs. 15%, monocytes 12% vs. 12%; p > 0.05 for all comparisons).

Next, we analyzed T-cell subsets, as they are the source material for CART manufacturing. We found that higher CD3+ T-cell counts at the time of apheresis were associated with increased likelihood of response (p = 0.02; Fig. 4A). We performed receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis to identify a threshold predictive of ORR and identified an optimal cutoff of 401 CD3+ cells/μL. Patients with CD3+ T-cell counts above this threshold experienced longer median PFS (29.9 vs. 3.7 months, p < 0.01; Fig. 4B). We then evaluated whether CD4+ and CD8+ T-cell counts at apheresis correlated with clinical outcomes. Absolute CD8+ T-cell counts did not differ significantly between responders and non-responders (p = 0.12; Supplementary Fig. 4A). In contrast, absolute CD4⁺ T-cell counts were higher in responders than in non-responders (311 vs. 172 cells/μL, p < 0.01; Fig. 4C) and were also associated with DR (p < 0.01; Supplementary Fig. 4B). ROC analysis identified an optimal threshold of 201 CD4⁺ cells/μL, above which patients had prolonged median PFS (29.9 vs. 3.4 months, p < 0.01; Fig. 4D). These data suggest that differences in outcomes based on CD3⁺ T-cell counts at apheresis were primarily driven by CD4⁺ T-cells. While disease burden and other disease characteristics at the time of apheresis did not correlate with ORR or DR in univariate analysis (Fig. 2A, Supplementary Fig. 2G–J), CD3⁺ and CD4⁺ T-cell counts were valuable predictors of response at this early timepoint. Furthermore, CD3+ T-cell counts did not differ significantly based upon response to front-line therapy (p = 0.80; Supplementary Fig. 4C). To further investigate T-cell counts as predictors of response, we performed subgroup analyses stratified by product (axi-cel vs. liso-cel) to account for differences in manufacturing protocols. In the axi-cel subgroup, responders had higher median CD3⁺ (689 vs. 332 cells/μL), CD4⁺ (338 vs. 166 cells/μL), and CD8⁺ (296 vs. 149 cells/μL) T-cell counts as compared to non-responders (p < 0.05 for all comparisons; Fig. 4F, Supplementary Fig. 4D, E). In contrast, no significant differences were observed in the liso-cel subgroup (Supplementary Fig. 4F–H). For this reason, we performed further analyses in the axi-cel subgroup. We identified optimal thresholds for CD3⁺ (405), CD4⁺ (201), and CD8⁺ (172) T-cells using ROC analysis. CD4⁺ T-cell counts yielded the highest area under the curve, indicating that they were the most robust predictor of response. Accordingly, in axi-cel patients, high CD4⁺ counts were strongly associated with prolonged median PFS (29.9 vs. 2.5 months, p = 0.01; Fig. 4G) and remained an independent predictor of overall response in multivariate analysis including primary refractoriness and disease control (Fig. 4H). High CD3+ and CD8+ T-cell counts were also associated with improved PFS in this cohort (Supplementary Fig. 4I, J).

CD4+ naïve T-cell counts at apheresis predict axi-cel efficacy

We next performed in-depth immunophenotyping of peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMC) collected at apheresis (n = 16), day 7 (n = 19), and day 28 (n = 9) post-infusion from 21 patients treated with axi-cel (Supplementary Fig. 5, Supplementary Table 1). This subgroup was selected because of the larger sample size and the significant association between CD4+ T-cell counts and clinical outcomes within this cohort. At apheresis, CD3⁺ T-cells accounted for a median of 52% (IQR: 44–61%) of immune cells, with CD4⁺ T-cells being the predominant CD3+ subset (median 52%, IQR: 36–66%; Fig. 5A, Supplementary Fig. 6A). Absolute numbers of naïve (CD45RA⁺/CD62L⁺) CD3⁺ cells were significantly higher in complete responders, whereas no significant differences were observed for central memory, effector memory, or TEMRA T-cell subsets (Fig. 5C). For this reason, we performed subset analysis within the naïve CD3+ T-cell population, which revealed that CD4+ naïve T-cell counts were significantly higher in complete responders, while no significant difference was observed in the CD8+ T-cell subset (Fig. 5D). This suggests that the previously observed PFS differences were associated with more naïve CD4⁺ T-cells. Further, patients with high naïve CD4⁺ T-cell counts exhibited longer median PFS (not reached vs. 3.7 months, p < 0.01; Fig. 5E), whereas no PFS differences were seen when stratifying by naïve CD3⁺ T-cell counts (Supplementary Fig. 6D). Expression of exhaustion markers (PD1, LAG3, TIM3) on apheresed T-cells was not associated with CART response (Supplementary Fig. 7A–C). These findings indicate that naïve CD4⁺ T-cell counts at apheresis are potential predictors of long-term response in patients treated with axi-cel.

Immune dynamics after CART infusion shapes CART outcomes

We next examined post-infusion immune dynamics at the peak of CART expansion (day 7) and at day 28 following infusion. The relative proportions of immune cell subsets at day 7 and day 28 were not associated with clinical response (Supplementary Fig. 8A, B). Similarly, post-infusion CD4+ T-cell counts were not statistically associated with clinical outcomes (Supplementary Fig. 8C, D). B-cells were not phenotypically detectable at any post-infusion timepoint, consistent with the combined effects of lymphodepletion and CART-mediated B-cell aplasia.

We then investigated whether the phenotypic profile of T-cells changed from apheresis to after CART infusion. Dynamic changes in T-cell phenotypes from apheresis to day 7 were analyzed in 14 axi-cel patients with matched samples (Supplementary Fig. 5). Since the T-cell compartment at day 7 included both CAR⁺ and CAR⁻ populations, we profiled the T-cell phenotypes in 19 axi-cel patients (Supplementary Fig. 5), examining phenotypic differences between these subsets and their association with clinical outcomes. At day 7, CAR⁺ T-cells constituted a median of 7% (IQR, 4–20%) of total live CD3+ T-cells and showed comparable CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ representation (Fig. 6B). The percentage of CD4+CAR+ T-cells was not associated with clinical outcomes (Supplementary Fig. 10). CAR⁺ T-cell expansion was numerically higher in patients achieving DRs compared with non-responders (3% vs. 1% of PBMCs, p = 0.01; Fig. 6C). Within the CD8⁺CAR⁺ compartment, higher frequencies of naïve cells and lower frequencies of effector memory cells were associated with CR, suggesting that early proliferative potential may influence therapeutic efficacy (Fig. 6D, E). In addition, higher proportions of PD1-expressing CAR+ T-cells were associated with a lower likelihood of CR (PD-PR: 16% vs CR: 6%, p = 0.03), highlighting early CART exhaustion as a potential mechanism of resistance (Fig. 6F).

We further investigated whether the inflammatory milieu induced by CART activation influenced memory differentiation in CAR⁻ bystander T-cells. Subgroup analyses of CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ T-cells revealed largely similar memory distributions between CAR⁺ and CAR⁻ subsets apart from CD4⁺ naïve cells and CD8⁺ effector memory cells, which were enriched in the CAR⁻ and CAR⁺ compartments, respectively (Fig. 6G–I).

Finally, we analyzed PBMC samples obtained 28 days after CART infusion from 9 patients (6 CR and 3 PR/PD). At this time point, CAR+ cells represented a median of 0.72% (Range: 0.6–1.4%) of total T-cells, confirming the limited short-term persistence of axi-cel. Despite low frequencies, residual CAR+ T-cells displayed high expression of activation and immune checkpoint markers, including CD69, PD1, LAG3, and TIM3 (Supplementary Fig. 11) compared with CAR- counterparts, consistent with ongoing or prior antigen-specific engagement. Owing to the limited sample size and response distribution, formal correlations with clinical outcomes were not performed.

Collectively, these results indicate that both the magnitude of CART expansion and early phenotypic features, particularly naïve differentiation and exhaustion markers, are associated with clinical response, and that axi-cel is characterized by limited persistence, with residual CAR T-cells retaining hallmarks of prior antigen engagement.

Discussion

CART is a standard-of-care second-line treatment for R/R LBCL. We evaluated 64 patients treated at a single large academic center. In our cohort, the CRR was 55% (axi-cel 51%, liso-cel 58%), the 24-month PFS was 46% (axi-cel 47%, liso-cel 44%), and the 24-month OS was 76% (axi-cel 77%, liso-cel 75%). These rates are comparable to those seen in ZUMA-7 (CRR: 65%, PFS: 42%, OS: 61%)13,14 and TRANSFORM (ORR: 74%, PFS: 57%, OS: 68%)15,16. The longer 24-month OS in our study may be due to the approvals of improved third and later-line therapy, such as bispecific antibodies30 and fewer COVID-related deaths during the timeframe of our study31. Additionally, we observed a 24-month DOR of 68% (axi-cel 75%, liso-cel 60%), which was not reported in ZUMA-7 and was 61% in TRANSFORM. DOR is particularly relevant for CART as most patients who are in remission at 24-months remain disease-free long-term32.

Toxicity rates in our cohort were overall low, with 3% (axi-cel 5%, liso-cel 0%) of patients experiencing severe ( > grade 2) CRS or ICANS, fewer than 10% (axi-cel 9%, liso-cel 7%) of patients with severe cytopenias, and no non-relapse mortality. These rates were lower than the toxicity rates seen in ZUMA-714, TRANSFORM15, and the non-relapse mortality reported by a recent meta-analysis (6% in the LBCL population)33. This may be attributable to limited follow-up time from CART infusion to assess for non-relapse mortality and high rates of bendamustine use for lymphodepletion, which may result in a less inflammatory cytokine milieu and lower hematological toxicities34,35. In our study, the group of patients treated with liso-cel was significantly more likely to have several high-risk features, such as older age, high IPI score, and longer vein-to-vein times, than those treated with axi-cel. Despite these risk factors, survival outcomes were nearly identical between the two groups, and liso-cel patients were less likely to experience CRS and ICANS. This is in line with data from the large clinical trials14,15 and other real-world studies36 suggesting that both therapies are effective, but axi-cel is associated with higher toxicity.

We identified several clinical characteristics that were associated with decreased likelihood of achieving a CR and inferior survival outcomes, including primary refractory disease and absence of disease control at the time of CART infusion. Surrogates of high disease burden at the time of CART infusion including advanced stage, presence of END, bulky disease, and TMTV were also associated with a lower likelihood of achieving DR. Interestingly, these same clinical features identified at the time of first relapse were not predictive of DR, highlighting the greater prognostic value of disease burden at the time of CART infusion as compared to the time of the preceding relapse. In line with these findings, results from recent reports27,37 suggest that the use of effective bridging therapy prior to third or later-line CART infusion improved patient outcomes. Our data, derived from a population that frequently received bridging therapy, extends these findings to the second-line setting, which is particularly relevant given the increasing availability of effective treatment regimens that could be utilized as bridging therapies. Outcomes and toxicities differ depending on the line of therapy in which patients receive CART38. Additional lines of therapy contribute to additional toxicity and decreased T-cell fitness39. Importantly, in our cohort, disease biology, including disease control at infusion, remained a key predictor of response even in patients with presumably more fit T-cells in the second-line setting.

Higher CD3⁺ and CD4⁺ T-cell counts at apheresis were significantly associated with CR and prolonged PFS, both in the overall population and the axi-cel subgroup. Notably, this association was not observed in the liso-cel cohort, hypothetically reflecting differences resulting from the separate expansion of CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ CART subsets during the liso-cel manufacturing process40. These results expand upon similar observations seen in the third-line setting, suggesting that the efficacy of axi-cel may be influenced by the presence of more naïve CD4⁺ T-cell phenotypes at apheresis41 and support the hypothesis that naïve CD4⁺ T-cells contribute to the generation of more effective CART20,23. Also consistent with these results, higher levels of naïve T-cells have also been shown to promote improved CART efficacy in lymphoma and multiple myeloma23,42. Interestingly, a recent report noted that CD4+ T-cell reconstitution at late timepoints post-CART was not associated with PFS43, although it remains unclear if CD4+ counts at recovery are directly correlated with counts at the time of apheresis.

Consistent with prior work44,45, we showed an improved disease control in patients with better CART expansion at day 7. Moreover, the observation that complete responders had higher proportions of CD8⁺ naïve CART suggests that early memory-like CART with stemness potential contributes to durable disease control through enhanced persistence and proliferative capacity20,23,25. Our comparison of CAR⁺ and CAR⁻ T-cells revealed an enrichment of CD8⁺ effector memory cells in the CAR⁺ population, in line with past findings46, suggesting that CAR⁺ and CAR⁻ T-cells may play distinct roles post-infusion. The specific interactions between these compartments warrant further investigation. At day 7, CD4+ T-cell counts did not correlate with patient outcomes despite their importance at earlier time points. There are important differences in early in vivo CD4+ and CD8+ CART expansion, with CD8+ CART expansion dominating the early phase of CART biological activity47. CD4+ cells influence CD8+ CART function during manufacturing48, and the absence of CD4+ T-cells can lead to a hypofunctional CD8+ T-cell phenotype49. Our results are in line with these findings, suggesting CD4+ T-cells at apheresis are critical to support robust CD8+ CART manufacture and expansion. We also observed that increased exhaustion, as measured by PD1 expression on CAR⁺ T-cells at day 7, was associated with a reduced likelihood of achieving CR. These findings highlight early CART exhaustion as a potential mechanism of resistance in the axi-cel population and suggest that early identification of high-risk patients may inform rational therapeutic strategies50.

Strengths of our study include the real-world patient population, a long duration of follow-up exceeding 24 months, and robust correlative immune profiling of patient samples. However, we must acknowledge its limitations, which include its retrospective nature and relatively small sample size. Additionally, few patients from minority groups were included in our correlative analysis, which may limit our ability to generalize these findings to those groups51,52. The study only included patients from a single large academic center, further limiting generalizability. Additionally, the correlative immune profiling was conducted only in a subset of the axi-cel cohort due to limited research specimen availability, particularly in the liso-cel group. These findings are intended to be hypothesis-generating and will have to be validated using data from prospective studies with planned correlative analyses.

Overall, our data demonstrate that second-line CART is effective and safe in real-world patients with R/R LBCL. Response to first-line therapy, disease status at the time of CART infusion, and T-cell immunophenotype at the time of apheresis were strongly associated with outcomes in our cohort, and these findings may guide selection of second-line therapies for LBCL patients.

Methods

Patients

Our study was approved by the University of Pennsylvania’s institutional review board, complied with all relevant regulations regarding the use of human study participants, and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Written consent was obtained to conduct research analysis on the collected biospecimens. We retrospectively analyzed 64 consecutive patients with LBCL who were refractory to or had relapsed within 12 months of front-line therapy and were treated with standard-of-care commercial second-line axi-cel or liso-cel between May 2022 and October 2024 at the University of Pennsylvania (Philadelphia, USA). Data cutoff was October 30th, 2025. Primary refractory disease was defined as persistent disease at the time of their end-of-treatment response assessment. Treatment assignment (axi-cel vs. liso-cel) was based on physician choice. Disease status was assessed per the Lugano 2014 criteria53 using computed tomography (CT) and positron emission tomography/CT (PET/CT) when available. Cytokine release syndrome (CRS) and immune effector cell-associated neurotoxicity syndrome (ICANS) were graded per the American Society for Transplantation and Cellular Therapy (ASTCT) Consensus Grading System54. Blood counts, transfusions, and infections were manually extracted from patient charts.

Peripheral blood mononuclear cells immunophenotyping

For patient-derived samples, frozen peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMC) at apheresis Day 7 and Day 28 were thawed and enumerated. Enumeration and viability were assessed using the Moxi GO II. Cells were blocked using Human TruStainFcX (BioLegend) for 10 minutes at RT. Surface staining was then carried out with a panel of fluorochrome-conjugated antibodies targeting specific surface markers (Supplementary Table 2).

Samples were acquired on a Cytek Aurora spectral flow cytometer (Cytek Biosciences) equipped with five lasers: UV (355 nm), violet (405 nm), blue (488 nm), green (561 nm), and red (640 nm). Spectral unmixing and compensation were performed using reference controls derived from paired normal donor PBMCs, activated PBMCs, and CAR–expressing cells. Data analysis was conducted using SpectroFlo (Cytek Biosciences), FlowJo (BD Biosciences), and R software.

Flow cytometry data analysis

Flow cytometric data analysis was conducted using FlowJo v10.10 software (BD Life Sciences). For CD3+ high-dimensional analyses, viable singlet lymphocytes (CD3⁺, CD14⁻). Pre-gated populations were exported as CSV files and processed in R (v4.3) using the tidyverse package. Data were downsampled to 100,000 events per sample and transformed using the inverse hyperbolic sine (asinh) function. Subsequently, datasets were converted into Seurat objects and analyzed using the Seurat package for dimensionality reduction and clustering by Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) employing a nearest-neighbor parameter of 20. Visualization was performed using built-in Seurat functions and ggplot2. Clustering analysis was performed with the FlowSOM package, resulting in the identification of 15 metaclusters. Heatmaps were generated using the pheatmap package. The following markers were included in UMAP and FlowSOM analyses for CD3+ phenotyping: CD4, CD8, CD25, CD69, CD62L, CD27, CD45RA, CD127, PD1, LAG3, and TIM3. Unbiased clusters were manually annotated. Memory phenotype for correlation with outcomes was determined as follows: naïve (CD45RA+/CD62L+), central memory (CD45RA-/CD62L+), effector memory (CD45RA-/CD62L-), terminal effector memory (CD45RA+/CD62L-). Gating strategy is provided in Supplementary Fig. 12.

Total metabolic tumor volume calculations

Total metabolic tumor volume (TMTV) was derived from 18F-fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography combined with non-contrast computed tomography (FDG-PET/CT) performed prior to CAR T-cell therapy. All scans were acquired using standard imaging protocols. TMTV measurements in our cohort followed the framework previously outlined in this international benchmark28. Lymphomatous lesions were delineated using semi-automated segmentation in a commercially available software program – MIM (Version 7.3.4, MIM Software Inc., Ohio, USA). FDG-avid lymphomatous lesions were defined by SUV max >4 with no volume threshold. Regions of physiologic uptake and non-lymphomatous lesions were manually excluded. Lymphomatous lesions that were not captured during the automated segmentation were manually added. Extra-nodal lymphomatous lesions and splenic involvement were included only if focal hypermetabolism was observed.

Statistical analyses

Categorical variables were evaluated with Fisher’s exact or Kruskal-Wallis test as appropriate. Unpaired continuous variables were evaluated by Mann–Whitney test. Paired continuous variables were evaluated by the Wilcoxon matched-pairs test. Receiving operator curve statistics were used to calculate the optimal cut-off of CD3+, CD4+, and CD8+ T-cell counts for analysis to predict the overall response rate at three months according to Youden’s optimal threshold. Survival analyses were conducted using the Kaplan-Meier method and compared with the log-rank test. Progression-free survival (PFS) and overall survival (OS) were measured from the date of CAR T-cell infusion. PFS was defined as the time to disease progression or death from any cause, whichever occurred first, with patients alive and progression-free censored at last follow-up. OS was defined as the time to death from any cause, with censoring at the last follow-up for surviving patients. Univariate and multivariate tests for clinical variables were conducted using logistic regression. All tests were performed using a nominal significance threshold of p < 0.05. Analyses were performed using R Studio V12. and GraphPad Prism Version 10.4.2 (GraphPad Software).
