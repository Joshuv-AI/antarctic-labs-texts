Approximating Kemeny’s constant via spectral sparsification
A. Abiad, Á. Carmona, A. M. Encinas, M. J. Jiménez, Á. Samperio

Abstract
Kemeny’s constant of a network quantifies the expected time for a random walk to reach a target vertex chosen according to the stationary distribution, independently of the starting vertex. The purpose of this work is twofold. First, we introduce the class of slightly regular networks, whose orthogonal degree vector is an eigenvector of the Laplacian, and derive an exact expression for Kemeny’s constant for this class, extending previous results beyond regular networks. Second, we address the efficient approximation of Kemeny’s constant using spectral sparsification. We establish multiplicative bounds relating Kemeny’s constant of a network to that of any of its spectral sparsifiers, and use these bounds to design an approximation algorithm. While standard exact approaches based on a full spectral decomposition or on the computation of a pseudoinverse have O ⁡ ( n 3 ) O(n^{3}) complexity, our method has complexity O ⁡ ( n 2 ​ log 1 + o ⁡ ( 1 ) ​ n ) O(n^{2}\log^{1+o(1)}n) for fixed approximation parameters, and is therefore nearly quadratic in the number of vertices even for dense networks.

Introduction

Kemeny’s constant is a fundamental parameter in the theory of Markov chains that has received renewed attention in graph theory and network science. Originally defined for finite, discrete-time, time-homogeneous, irreducible Markov chains in terms of the stationary distribution and mean first passage times, it is particularly relevant in the study of random walks on networks. It quantifies the expected time needed for a random walker to reach a target vertex selected according to the stationary distribution, independently of the starting vertex.

Kemeny’s constant has found applications in several areas, including

epidemic-network analysis and testing strategies
[
10
,
32
]
, the identification and optimization of metastable states in molecular kinetic networks
[
24
]
, and the analysis of road-network dynamics
[
9
]
. More generally, it is often interpreted as a global measure of network navigability, with smaller values typically associated with more efficient exploration of the network; see, for instance,
[
5
,
6
,
14
,
15
]
.

Several expressions for Kemeny’s constant are known in the literature; see, e.g.,
[
8
,
23
,
29
]
. Our first contribution is an exact expression for Kemeny’s constant of slightly regular networks (Proposition
3.2
), namely, networks whose orthogonal degree vector is an eigenvector of the Laplacian. This expression extends
[
16
, Proposition 1]
to a broader class of networks. We also identify classes of semiregular networks satisfying the required property, including complete bipartite graphs and several generalized windmill constructions.

Standard exact approaches for computing Kemeny’s constant, based on a full spectral decomposition or on the computation of a matrix pseudoinverse, exhibit an
\mathcal{O}(n^{3})
complexity for a network with
n
vertices. This becomes computationally expensive for large networks and has motivated the development of approximation methods with lower computational cost
[
4
,
21
]
.

Kooij and Dubbeldam
[
16
]
proposed two approximations for non-regular graphs based on the Kirchhoff index and degree heterogeneity. However, a direct computation of the Kirchhoff index through standard dense spectral or pseudoinverse methods also has cubic complexity. Bini
et al.

[
4
]
derived decomposition formulas based on stochastic complements and used them to develop a divide-and-conquer strategy for computing Kemeny’s constant. They also investigated randomized trace estimation using Hutch++ combined with iterative linear-system solvers. Although they do not derive an overall asymptotic complexity bound for the divide-and-conquer algorithm comparable to the one considered here, their numerical experiments show computational savings over direct computation for most of the tested instances, although the improvement is not uniform across all networks. Li, Huang and Lee
[
21
]
proposed a Monte Carlo estimator with probabilistic accuracy guarantees and developed a parallel GPU implementation for large-scale networks.

In
[
30
]
, Xia and Zhang introduced two Monte Carlo algorithms which compute approximations to Kemeny’s constant, called
RefinedMC
and
ForestMC
. The computational complexity of the RefinedMC algorithm is asymptotically sublinear in the number of vertices, but it is asymptotically quartic in a parameter
l
that goes to infinity if the absolute value of the second or last eigenvalues of the normalized adjacency matrix (which we denote as
|\lambda_{2}|
and
|\lambda_{n}|
in Section
2
) approach
1
. Similarly, the computational cost of the ForestMC algorithm is asymptotically cubic in a parameter
l
that also goes to infinity if
|\lambda_{2}|
or
|\lambda_{n}|
approach
1
. Therefore, the complexity of those algorithms depends heavily on the structure of the network, and while they could have a very small computational cost for some families of networks, they could be prohibitively computationally costly for others. Xu
et al.

[
31
]
proposed ApproxKemeny, a theoretically guaranteed approximation algorithm for undirected graphs based on Laplacian linear systems and Hutchinson trace estimation. They approximate this expression by solving only
\mathcal{O}\big(\log n\big)
linear systems in the SDD matrix. Using the approximate SDD solver introduced in
[
20
]
, they obtain their approximation to the Kemeny constant with computational cost
\mathcal{O}\big(m\log^{5}(n)\big)
, where
m
is the number of edges. While this approach has a small computational cost for sparse graphs, for dense graphs the computational cost becomes

\mathcal{O}\big(n^{2}\log^{5}(n)\big)
.

The second contribution of our paper is the development of Algorithm
1
, which calculates a novel approximation for the Kemeny constant of any network. For dense networks, its worst-case asymptotic complexity improves on that of the comparison algorithms considered below. We should also note that the worst-case asymptotic complexity of our algorithm does not depend on the structure of the network. Our main tools are known techniques of spectral graph sparsification (see
[
18
,
26
]
), which aim to find a sparse subgraph whose Laplacian matrix can well approximate the original Laplacian matrix in terms of its quadratic form and, therefore, also its eigenvalues. We prove that the Kemeny constant of the sparse approximation is close to the Kemeny constant of the original one (see Corollary
4.2
). We then combine this sparsification step with the representation of Kemeny’s constant in terms of Laplacian linear systems used in
[
31
]
. For the complexity analysis, these systems are solved using the Sparsified Multigrid solver introduced in
[
19
]
.

Using this strategy of solving all linear systems after sparsifying the network, we achieve a computational cost of
\mathcal{O}\big(n^{2}\log^{1+o(1)}(n)\big)
, even for dense graphs, which is nearly quadratic in the number of vertices (see Theorem
4.4
for a proof of the expression of the computational cost as a function of
n
and the two parameters that control the error of our approximation,
\beta
and
\epsilon
).
Also, as we avoid using the approximation based on Hutchinson’s Monte-Carlo method used in
[
31
]
, we obtain
different accuracy guarantees. We identify a broad range of the approximation parameters for which our bounds are tighter than those of
[
31
]
(see Theorem
4.3
and the discussion on the comparison between the bounds of both approximations right before Subsection
4.1
).

To conclude, our approach is designed for the dense-network regime, where sparsification can substantially reduce the number of edges before the Laplacian systems are solved. We show that, for fixed approximation parameters, the resulting complexity is nearly quadratic in the number of vertices, and we compare both its complexity and its approximation guarantees with representative existing methods.

The two parts of the paper are connected by a common spectral viewpoint. In Section 3, we use Rayleigh-quotient arguments to identify when the degree-heterogeneity term appearing in the Laplacian representation of Kemeny’s constant is spectrally exact. This leads naturally to the class of slightly regular networks and explains several equality cases for previously known bounds. In Section 4, we use the same variational perspective in an approximation setting: spectral sparsification provides multiplicative control of Laplacian quadratic forms, which in turn yields control of the normalized Laplacian spectrum and of Kemeny’s constant.

2
Preliminaries

In this paper, we consider connected networks
G=(V,E,c)
, where

(V,E)
is a simple undirected graph with
|V|=n
vertices and
|E|=m
edges, and

c:V\times V\longrightarrow[0,\infty)

is a symmetric function satisfying
c(i,j)>0
if and only if

\{i,j\}\in E
. We write
c_{ij}=c(i,j)
and, for an edge

e=\{i,j\}\in E
, we also use the notation
c(e)=c_{ij}
. Recall that the
degree
of vertex
i
is
k_{i}=\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n}c_{ij}
, and we define
the volume
of
G
by
{\rm vol}(G)=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}k_{i}.
We denote

k_{\min}:=\min_{1\leq i\leq n}k_{i},\qquad k_{\max}:=\max_{1\leq i\leq n}k_{i}.

When
c_{ij}=1
for any
\{i,j\}\in E
, we refer to
G
as a graph; in this case,
{\rm vol}(G)=2m.

The
adjacency matrix
of the network
G=(V,E,c)
is the
n\times n
symmetric matrix
A=(c_{ij})
. We denote by
{\sf e}_{i}
the
i
-th vector of the standard basis.

Throughout this paper, vectors are represented using
sans serif
style. For instance,
{\sf k}
is the vector of degrees of vertices of
G
. A network is called
regular
if the degree vector is constant. Additionally, for any real number
\alpha
, we define
\alpha^{\#}
as
\dfrac{1}{\alpha}
when
\alpha\not=0,
and
0
otherwise. For a real symmetric matrix
M
, we denote its group inverse by
M^{\#}
. In this case,
M^{\#}
coincides with the Moore–Penrose inverse of
M
.

The
Laplacian matrix

{\sf L}_{G}
of
G
is the
n\times n
symmetric matrix given by
{\sf L}_{G}={\sf D}-{\sf A}
, where
{\sf D}=\text{diag}(k_{1},\ldots,k_{n})
is the diagonal degree matrix. The
normalized Laplacian matrix
of a network, denoted
{\cal{L}}_{G}
, is defined as

{\cal{L}}_{G}={\sf D}^{-1/2}{\sf L}_{G}{\sf D}^{-1/2}={\sf I}-{\sf D}^{-1/2}{\sf A}{\sf D}^{-1/2},

where
{\sf D}^{-\frac{1}{2}}{\sf A}{\sf D}^{-\frac{1}{2}}
is known as the
normalized adjacency matrix
.

The random walk on
G
has transition probability matrix
P=D^{-1}A
. Since
G
is connected,
P
is irreducible, and its unique stationary distribution
\boldsymbol{\pi}
is given by

\pi_{i}=\dfrac{k_{i}}{{\rm vol}(G)},

i=1,\ldots,n
.

Kemeny’s constant is a fundamental parameter in the study of Markov chains and random walks on graphs. It quantifies the expected number of steps a random walker takes to reach a randomly chosen vertex, regardless of the starting position, under the stationary distribution. Intuitively, Kemeny’s constant provides a global measure of how efficiently information or influence propagates through a network. It is particularly useful in assessing the connectivity and mixing properties of a graph. Kemeny and Snell
[
13
]
established a direct connection between Kemeny’s constant and random walks. For
i\neq j
, let
m_{ji}
denote the mean first passage time from

j
to
i
. For
i=j
, we adopt the recurrence-time convention

m_{jj}=\frac{1}{\pi_{j}}.

With this convention,

\widetilde{K}(G)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\pi_{i}m_{ji}

is independent of the starting vertex
j
. Throughout this paper, we use
the shifted convention

K(G):=\widetilde{K}(G)-1,

which is the convention corresponding to the spectral expression below.

It is known that the Kemeny constant can be expressed by means of the eigenvalues of several matrices associated with the given network. In particular, there is a way to compute Kemeny’s constant by using the eigenvalues of the
probability transition matrix.

Since
P=D^{-1}A
is similar to the symmetric normalized adjacency matrix

D^{-1/2}AD^{-1/2},

its eigenvalues are real. We denote them by

1=\lambda_{1}>\lambda_{2}\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_{n}\geq-1.

Accordingly, the eigenvalues of the normalized Laplacian

\mathcal{L}_{G}

are denoted by

0=\mu_{1}<\mu_{2}\leq\cdots\leq\mu_{n}\leq 2,

and satisfy

\mu_{i}=1-\lambda_{i},\qquad i=1,\ldots,n.

Finally, we denote the eigenvalues of the combinatorial Laplacian
{\sf L}_{G}

by

0=\gamma_{1}<\gamma_{2}\leq\cdots\leq\gamma_{n}.

Kemeny’s constant admits the spectral representation (
[
22
]
)

K(G)=\sum_{i=2}^{n}\frac{1}{1-\lambda_{i}}=\sum_{i=2}^{n}\frac{1}{\mu_{i}}=\operatorname{tr}\!\left(\mathcal{L}_{G}^{\#}\right).

(1)

Furthermore, Wang et al.
[
29
, Theorem 4]
showed that Kemeny’s constant can also be expressed in terms of the group inverse of the combinatorial Laplacian, specifically,

K(G)={\rm tr}({\sf L}_{G}^{\#}{\sf D})-\dfrac{1}{\rm{vol}(G)}{{\sf k}}^{\sf T}{\sf L}_{G}^{\#}{{\sf k}}.

(2)

An alternative derivation of this identity was given in
[
8
]
, and related formulas were subsequently extended to Schrödinger random walks in
[
7
]
.

3
Rayleigh-quotient bounds and slightly regular networks

Several bounds for Kemeny’s constant can be obtained from its
representation in terms of the group inverse of the combinatorial
Laplacian. In particular, the bounds derived in
[
29
]
depend on
the interaction between the Laplacian spectrum and the deviation of
the degree vector from regularity. This raises a natural question:
under which structural or spectral conditions do these bounds become
exact, and can such conditions lead to an explicit expression for
Kemeny’s constant beyond the regular case?

In this section, we show that the answer is governed by the spectral
position of the component of the degree vector orthogonal to the
all-ones vector. The equality cases of the known bounds correspond to
this vector lying in an extremal Laplacian eigenspace. This observation
naturally suggests considering the more general situation in which it
belongs to an arbitrary Laplacian eigenspace. We introduce the class of
slightly regular networks to capture this property and derive an exact
formula for Kemeny’s constant on this class. We then identify broad
families of semiregular networks satisfying the condition, thereby
providing a common spectral explanation for several previously known
equality cases.

The starting point of our analysis is the representation of Kemeny’s
constant in (
2
). For a network
G=(V,E,c)
, we define
the
orthogonal degree vector
by

{\sf w}={\sf k}-\frac{\operatorname{vol}(G)}{n}\mathbf{1}.

Equivalently, its
i
-th component is

w_{i}=k_{i}-\frac{\operatorname{vol}(G)}{n},i=1,\ldots,n.

Since

\mathbf{1}^{\top}{\sf k}=\operatorname{vol}(G),

we have
{\sf w}\in\mathbf{1}^{\perp}
; hence
{\sf w}
is precisely the component of
the degree vector orthogonal to the all-ones vector. Moreover,

{\sf w}=\mathbf{0}
if and only if
G
is regular. Since
{\sf L}_{G}^{\#}\mathbf{1}=\mathbf{0}
,
we have

{\sf k}^{\top}{\sf L}_{G}^{\#}{\sf k}={\sf w}^{\top}{\sf L}_{G}^{\#}{\sf w}.

Hence, (
2
) can be rewritten as

K(G)=\operatorname{tr}({\sf L}_{G}^{\#}{\sf D})-\frac{1}{\operatorname{vol}(G)}{\sf w}^{\top}{\sf L}_{G}^{\#}{\sf w}.

(3)

Thus, the second term in (
3
), which vanishes in the
regular case, is governed by the Rayleigh quotient of
{\sf L}_{G}^{\#}

evaluated at the orthogonal degree vector. Notice that

\|{\sf w}\|_{2}^{2}=\|{\sf k}\|_{2}^{2}-\frac{\operatorname{vol}(G)^{2}}{n}.

The vector
{\sf w}
was introduced in
[
29
, Eq. (25)]
for the case
of graphs, and its norm provides a natural measure of degree
heterogeneity.

Since
{\sf w}\in\mathbf{1}^{\perp}
, the Courant–Fischer min–max theorem gives

\frac{1}{\gamma_{n}}\leq\frac{{\sf w}^{\top}{\sf L}_{G}^{\#}{\sf w}}{\|{\sf w}\|_{2}^{2}}\leq\frac{1}{\gamma_{2}}

whenever
{\sf w}\neq\mathbf{0}
. Substituting these inequalities into
(
3
) yields the bounds obtained in
[
29
]
:

\displaystyle K(G)

\displaystyle\geq\operatorname{tr}({\sf L}_{G}^{\#}{\sf D})-\frac{1}{\operatorname{vol}(G)\gamma_{2}}\left(\|{\sf k}\|_{2}^{2}-\frac{\operatorname{vol}(G)^{2}}{n}\right),

(4)

\displaystyle K(G)

\displaystyle\leq\operatorname{tr}({\sf L}_{G}^{\#}{\sf D})-\frac{1}{\operatorname{vol}(G)\gamma_{n}}\left(\|{\sf k}\|_{2}^{2}-\frac{\operatorname{vol}(G)^{2}}{n}\right).

The equality cases in (
4
) have a direct spectral
interpretation.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
G
be a connected non-regular network, and let

{\sf w}={\sf k}-\frac{\operatorname{vol}(G)}{n}\mathbf{1}.

Then equality holds in the lower bound in (
4
) if and only
if
{\sf w}
belongs to the eigenspace of
{\sf L}_{G}
associated with

\gamma_{2}
. Likewise, equality holds in the upper bound if and only if

{\sf w}
belongs to the eigenspace associated with
\gamma_{n}
.

Proof.

The nonzero eigenvalues of
{\sf L}_{G}^{\#}
are

\frac{1}{\gamma_{2}},\ldots,\frac{1}{\gamma_{n}},

with the same eigenspaces as the corresponding eigenvalues of

{\sf L}_{G}
. Since
{\sf w}\in\mathbf{1}^{\perp}
, equality in the upper,
respectively lower, Rayleigh-quotient bound occurs precisely when

{\sf w}
belongs to the eigenspace associated with
\gamma_{2}
,
respectively
\gamma_{n}
. The conclusion follows from
(
3
).
∎

Proposition
3.1
suggests considering the more
general situation in which the orthogonal degree vector belongs to an
arbitrary Laplacian eigenspace, rather than necessarily to one of the
two extremal eigenspaces. This leads naturally to the following notion.

We say that a network
G
is
slightly regular
if the subspace
spanned by
{\sf w}
is invariant under
{\sf L}_{G}
. Equivalently, either

{\sf w}=\mathbf{0}
, in which case
G
is regular, or
{\sf w}
is an
eigenvector of
{\sf L}_{G}
.

For a
k
-regular network,
{\sf w}=\mathbf{0}
and
(
2
) reduces to the well-known identity

K(G)=k\,\operatorname{tr}({\sf L}_{G}^{\#}).

For non-regular slightly regular networks, the Rayleigh quotient above
is not merely bounded by its extremal values but can be evaluated
exactly.

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
G
be a non-regular slightly regular network, and let
\gamma
be
the Laplacian eigenvalue satisfying

{\sf L}_{G}{\sf w}=\gamma{\sf w}.

Then

K(G)=\operatorname{tr}({\sf L}_{G}^{\#}{\sf D})-\frac{\gamma^{\#}}{\operatorname{vol}(G)}\left(\|{\sf k}\|_{2}^{2}-\frac{\operatorname{vol}(G)^{2}}{n}\right).

(5)

Moreover,

\displaystyle K(G)

\displaystyle\geq k_{\min}\sum_{i=2}^{n}\frac{1}{\gamma_{i}}-\frac{\gamma^{\#}}{\operatorname{vol}(G)}\left(\|{\sf k}\|_{2}^{2}-\frac{\operatorname{vol}(G)^{2}}{n}\right),

\displaystyle K(G)

\displaystyle\leq k_{\max}\sum_{i=2}^{n}\frac{1}{\gamma_{i}}-\frac{\gamma^{\#}}{\operatorname{vol}(G)}\left(\|{\sf k}\|_{2}^{2}-\frac{\operatorname{vol}(G)^{2}}{n}\right).

Proof.

Since
{\sf L}_{G}{\sf w}=\gamma{\sf w}
and
{\sf w}\neq\mathbf{0}
,

{\sf L}_{G}^{\#}{\sf w}=\gamma^{\#}{\sf w}.

Therefore,

{\sf k}^{\top}{\sf L}_{G}^{\#}{\sf k}={\sf w}^{\top}{\sf L}_{G}^{\#}{\sf w}=\gamma^{\#}\|{\sf w}\|_{2}^{2}=\gamma^{\#}\left(\|{\sf k}\|_{2}^{2}-\frac{\operatorname{vol}(G)^{2}}{n}\right),

and (
5
) follows from
(
2
).

Finally, since

k_{\min}I\preceq{\sf D}\preceq k_{\max}I

and
{\sf L}_{G}^{\#}\succeq 0
, we obtain

k_{\min}\operatorname{tr}({\sf L}_{G}^{\#})\leq\operatorname{tr}({\sf L}_{G}^{\#}{\sf D})\leq k_{\max}\operatorname{tr}({\sf L}_{G}^{\#}).

Since

\operatorname{tr}({\sf L}_{G}^{\#})=\sum_{i=2}^{n}\frac{1}{\gamma_{i}},

the stated bounds follow.
∎

The path
P_{4}
provides a simple example showing that slight regularity
is not restricted to the extremal eigenspaces appearing in
Proposition
3.1
. Indeed,

{\sf w}=\frac{1}{2}(-1,1,1,-1)^{\top},\qquad{\sf L}_{P_{4}}{\sf w}=2{\sf w},

whereas the largest Laplacian eigenvalue of
P_{4}
is

\gamma_{4}=2+\sqrt{2}
and the lowest positive eigenvalue is
\gamma_{2}=2-\sqrt{2}
.
Thus, in this example the eigenvalue associated with
{\sf w}
is an
interior Laplacian eigenvalue.

We next identify a broad structural setting in which slight regularity
arises naturally. Recall that a
semiregular network
is a
network with exactly two distinct vertex degrees. The hypothesis in
the following result amounts to requiring that the two degree classes
form a weighted equitable partition.

Proposition 3.3
.

Let

G=(V_{0}\coprod V_{1},E,c)

be a
(k_{0},k_{1})
-semiregular network, where every vertex in
V_{i}
has
degree
k_{i}
and

n_{i}:=|V_{i}|,i=0,1.

Assume that, for every
i\in V_{0}
,

\sum_{j\in V_{0}}c(i,j)=r_{0},
and
\sum_{j\in V_{1}}c(i,j)=k_{0}-r_{0},

whereas, for every
i\in V_{1}
,

\sum_{j\in V_{0}}c(i,j)=k_{1}-r_{1},
and
\sum_{j\in V_{1}}c(i,j)=r_{1}.

Then
G
is slightly regular, with associated Laplacian eigenvalue

\gamma=\frac{n}{n_{0}}(k_{1}-r_{1})=\frac{n}{n_{1}}(k_{0}-r_{0}).

In particular,

\gamma=n

if and only if

k_{0}=r_{0}+n_{1},

or equivalently,

k_{1}=r_{1}+n_{0}.

Proof.

The orthogonal degree vector of
G
is

{\sf w}=\frac{k_{0}-k_{1}}{n}\left(n_{1}\chi_{V_{0}}-n_{0}\chi_{V_{1}}\right).

For every vertex in
V_{0}
,

({\sf L}_{G}{\sf w})_{i}=(k_{0}-k_{1})(k_{0}-r_{0}),

whereas, for every vertex in
V_{1}
,

({\sf L}_{G}{\sf w})_{i}=-(k_{0}-k_{1})(k_{1}-r_{1}).

Hence

{\sf L}_{G}{\sf w}=\gamma{\sf w},

where

\gamma=\frac{n}{n_{1}}(k_{0}-r_{0})=\frac{n}{n_{0}}(k_{1}-r_{1}).

The equality between these two expressions follows by double counting
the total conductance between
V_{0}
and
V_{1}
:

n_{0}(k_{0}-r_{0})=n_{1}(k_{1}-r_{1}).

The last assertion follows immediately from the expression for

\gamma
.
∎

In the unweighted case,
k_{0}-r_{0}\leq n_{1}
and

k_{1}-r_{1}\leq n_{0}
. Therefore,
\gamma=n
precisely when every vertex
of
V_{0}
is adjacent to every vertex of
V_{1}
, that is, when the cut
between the two degree classes is complete. Moreover, since the largest
Laplacian eigenvalue of an unweighted graph satisfies

\gamma_{n}\leq n

[
12
]
, in this case

\gamma=n=\gamma_{n}.

The following examples illustrate the scope of
Proposition
3.3
.

Example 3.4
.

(a)

Regular graphs are slightly regular by definition, since

{\sf w}={\sf 0}
. For a
k
-regular graph we recover

K(G)=k\,\operatorname{tr}({\sf L}_{G}^{\#}).

(b)

Let
G=K_{p,q}
be a complete bipartite graph and set
n=p+q
.
Taking

n_{0}=q,\qquad n_{1}=p,\qquad k_{0}=p,\qquad k_{1}=q,\qquad r_{0}=r_{1}=0,

Proposition
3.3
gives

\gamma=n=\gamma_{n}.

Using Proposition
3.2
together with the expression
for
{\sf L}_{G}^{\#}
obtained in
[
1
]
, we obtain

K(G)=n-1-\frac{2pq}{n^{2}}-\frac{(p-q)^{2}}{2n^{2}}=n-\frac{3}{2}.

This agrees with the value obtained directly from the spectrum of the
normalized Laplacian of
K_{p,q}
.

A broader family is obtained from join constructions studied in
[
3
]
.
For any
i=1,\ldots,s>1
, let
G_{i}=(W_{i},E_{i})
be a connected

k
-regular graph of order
n_{i}
, let
G_{0}=(W_{0},E_{0})
be a

\ell
-regular graph of order
n_{0}
and let

V=\coprod\limits_{i=0}^{s}W_{i}

be the disjoint union of
all vertex sets. Let
n=\sum\limits_{i=0}^{s}n_{i}.

The
join graph with base

G_{0}
and satellites
G_{i}
is the graph
G=(V,E)
obtained by joining the
graphs
G_{i}
,
i=1,\ldots,s
to
G_{0}
; that is,
E=\hat{E}\cup\bigcup\limits_{i=0}^{s}E_{i},
where

\hat{E}=\Big\{\{x,y\}:x\in W_{0}\mbox{ and }y\in\coprod\limits_{i=1}^{s}W_{i}\Big\}.

In this case,
V_{0}=W_{0}
,
V_{1}=\coprod\limits_{i=1}^{s}W_{i}
,
k_{0}=\ell+n-n_{0}
,
r_{0}=\ell
,
k_{1}=k+n_{0}
,
r_{1}=k
and
\gamma_{n}=n.

If
k_{0}=k_{1}
, the graph is regular and hence slightly regular as well.
Thus, every graph in this family is slightly regular.

The windmill and generalized windmill graphs considered in

[
17
]
are particular instances of this construction. A windmill
graph
W(s,k)
consists of
s
disjoint copies of
K_{k}
, every
vertex of which is adjacent to a common central vertex. In the notation
above, this corresponds to

G_{0}=K_{1},\qquad G_{i}=K_{k},\quad i=1,\ldots,s.

Kooij
[
17
]
introduced two natural generalizations obtained by
replacing the central vertex with
n_{0}
central vertices. In the
generalized windmill graph of Type I, denoted

W^{\prime}(s,k,n_{0}),

the central vertices form a clique
K_{n_{0}}
, whereas in the generalized
windmill graph of Type II, denoted

W^{\prime\prime}(s,k,n_{0}),

the central vertices are pairwise non-adjacent. Thus, in the
construction above, the base graph
G_{0}
is respectively

K_{n_{0}}
or
\overline{K}_{n_{0}}
, while the satellites are copies of

K_{k}
. Consequently, all these graphs are slightly regular.

For every non-regular member of these windmill families,
Proposition

gives

\gamma=n=\gamma_{n}.

Hence, by Proposition

, the upper bound in

explanation for the equality cases established for windmill and

extends the same

The variational viewpoint developed in this section will also be useful
in the approximation setting considered next. Here, exact alignment of
the orthogonal degree vector with a Laplacian eigenspace turns a
Rayleigh-quotient bound into an identity. In the next section, spectral
sparsification provides multiplicative control of Laplacian quadratic
forms, and Rayleigh-quotient arguments will instead be used to obtain
approximate spectral control and, ultimately, bounds for Kemeny’s
constant.

4
Approximating Kemeny’s constant via spectral sparsification

Although the results in Section
3
yield an exact
expression for Kemeny’s constant for slightly regular networks, its
direct evaluation still involves quantities associated with the group
inverse of the Laplacian and, using standard dense linear algebra,
has cubic computational cost in general. This motivates the search for
efficient approximation methods that apply to arbitrary networks.

The approach developed in this section is closely related to the
variational viewpoint used in the previous section. There,
Rayleigh-quotient arguments were used to exploit exact spectral
information on the orthogonal degree vector. Here, instead of requiring
such an exact spectral relation, we study how Kemeny’s constant behaves
when the quadratic form of the Laplacian is preserved only
approximately. This leads naturally to spectral sparsification.

More precisely, we derive multiplicative bounds relating the Kemeny
constant of a network to that of a spectral approximation. We then use
the sparsity of the resulting network to approximate Kemeny’s constant
through the solution of Laplacian linear systems, analyse the
computational cost of the resulting algorithm, and compare its
theoretical guarantees and complexity with those of the approximation
method introduced in
[
31
]
.

Spectral sparsification, introduced by Spielman and Teng
in
[
27
]
, aims to replace a network by a much sparser weighted
network while approximately preserving the quadratic form associated
with its Laplacian. This allows spectral and linear-algebraic
computations on the original network to be transferred to a
substantially sparser one. Throughout this section, we adopt the notion
of spectral approximation used in
[
26
]
.

For
0<\epsilon<1
, we say that a network
G^{\prime}=(V^{\prime},E^{\prime},c^{\prime})
is an
\epsilon
-
approximation
of a network

G=(V,E,c)
if
V^{\prime}=V
,
E^{\prime}\subseteq E
and for all
{\sf z}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
it holds that

(1-\epsilon){\sf z}^{\top}{\sf L}_{G}{\sf z}\leq{\sf z}^{\top}{\sf L}_{G^{\prime}}{\sf z}\leq(1+\epsilon){\sf z}^{\top}{\sf L}_{G}{\sf z}.

(6)

Since
G
is connected and
0<\epsilon<1
, any

\epsilon
-approximation
G^{\prime}
is connected as well.
Observe that
(V,E^{\prime})
is a subgraph of
(V,E)
, with the same vertex set and satisfying (
6
).

The sparsifier need not be a proper subgraph. For example, if

E^{\prime}=E
and

(1-\epsilon)c(e)\leq c^{\prime}(e)\leq(1+\epsilon)c(e),\,\,e\in E,

then
G^{\prime}=(V,E,c^{\prime})
is an
\epsilon
-approximation of
G
.
We denote the degree vector of
G^{\prime}
by
{\sf k}^{\prime}=(k_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,k_{n}^{\prime})^{\top}
, and we denote its diagonal degree matrix by

{\sf D}^{\prime}=\operatorname{diag}(k_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,k_{n}^{\prime}).

Given any
\epsilon
-approximation
G^{\prime}
of
G
, the Courant-Fischer min–max theorem implies that
{\sf L}_{G}
and
{\sf L}_{G^{\prime}}
have approximately the same eigenvalues, specifically

(1-\epsilon)\gamma_{i}\leq\gamma^{\prime}_{i}\leq(1+\epsilon)\gamma_{i},\hskip 7.11317pti=1,\ldots,n,

see
[
26
]
. In this paper, Spielman and Srivastava state that the same multiplicative bounds also hold for the eigenvalues of the normalized Laplacian. Since the degree matrix changes under sparsification, this conclusion does not hold in general, as the following example shows: Given
a,b>0
, consider
G=G(a,b)
, the path on
4
vertices whose conductances are
c_{12}=c_{34}=a
,
c_{23}=b
and
0
otherwise. Then, the eigenvalues of its normalized Laplacian are
\big\{0,\frac{b}{a+b},\frac{2a+b}{a+b},2\big\}
.

For
0<\epsilon<1
, we consider
G^{\prime}=G\big(a(1+\epsilon),b(1-\epsilon)\big)
and
G^{\prime\prime}=G\big(a(1-\epsilon),b(1+\epsilon)\big)
and we denote by
\mu_{2}
,
\mu_{2}^{\prime}
and
\mu_{2}^{\prime\prime}
the second eigenvalues of the normalized Laplacian of
G
,
G^{\prime}
and
G^{\prime\prime}

G^{\prime}
and
G^{\prime\prime}
are
\epsilon
-approximations of
G
and

\dfrac{\mu_{2}^{\prime}}{\mu_{2}}=\frac{(1-\epsilon)(a+b)}{a(1+\epsilon)+b(1-\epsilon)}<1+\epsilon\hskip 7.11317pt\hbox{and}\hskip 7.11317pt\dfrac{\mu_{2}^{\prime\prime}}{\mu_{2}}=\frac{(1+\epsilon)(a+b)}{a(1-\epsilon)+b(1+\epsilon)}>1-\epsilon.

However,
1-\epsilon\leq\dfrac{\mu_{2}^{\prime}}{\mu_{2}}
and
\dfrac{\mu_{2}^{\prime\prime}}{\mu_{2}}\leq 1+\epsilon
if and only if

and hence,
when

we have that
\dfrac{\mu_{2}^{\prime}}{\mu_{2}}<1-\epsilon
and
\dfrac{\mu_{2}^{\prime\prime}}{\mu_{2}}>1+\epsilon

Nevertheless, we have the following relationship between the eigenvalues of the normalized Laplacian of a network and those of the normalized Laplacian of any approximation.

Proposition 4.1
.

Given
0<\epsilon<1
, for any
\epsilon
-approximation
G^{\prime}
of a network
G
we have that

\sigma_{\epsilon}^{-1}\mu_{i}\leq\mu_{i}^{\prime}\leq\sigma_{\epsilon}\mu_{i},\hskip 7.11317pti=2,\ldots,n

where
\sigma_{\epsilon}=\frac{1+\epsilon}{1-\epsilon}
.

Proof.

Taking
{\sf z}={\sf e}_{i}
in (
6
), where
{\sf e}_{i}
denotes the
i
-th vector of the canonical basis, yields the following relationship:

(1-\epsilon)k_{i}\leq k_{i}^{\prime}\leq(1+\epsilon)k_{i},

for every
i=1,\ldots,n
. Therefore, for all
{\sf z}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

(1-\epsilon){\sf z}^{\top}{\sf D}{\sf z}\leq{\sf z}^{\top}{\sf D}^{\prime}{\sf z}\leq(1+\epsilon){\sf z}^{\top}{\sf D}{\sf z},

where
{\sf D}
and
{\sf D}^{\prime}
are the diagonal degree matrices of
G
and
G^{\prime}
, respectively.
Let

\mathcal{R}_{G}({\sf z})=\frac{{\sf z}^{\top}{\sf L}_{G}{\sf z}}{{\sf z}^{\top}{\sf D}{\sf z}},\qquad\mathcal{R}_{G^{\prime}}({\sf z})=\frac{{\sf z}^{\top}{\sf L}_{G^{\prime}}{\sf z}}{{\sf z}^{\top}{\sf D}^{\prime}{\sf z}},

for every nonzero vector
{\sf z}
.

From the spectral approximation of the Laplacians and the comparison between the degree matrices, we obtain

\sigma_{\epsilon}^{-1}\mathcal{R}_{G}({\sf z})\leq\mathcal{R}_{G^{\prime}}({\sf z})\leq\sigma_{\epsilon}\mathcal{R}_{G}({\sf z})

for every nonzero vector
{\sf z}
. The eigenvalues of the normalized Laplacian of
G
are the
generalized eigenvalues of the pair
({\sf L}_{G},{\sf D})
. Hence,

\mu_{i}=\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}\\
\dim S=i\end{subarray}}\;\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\sf z}\in S\\
{\sf z}\neq 0\end{subarray}}\mathcal{R}_{G}({\sf z}),\qquad i=1,\ldots,n,

and the analogous formula holds for
G^{\prime}
. Applying the generalized
Courant–Fischer min–max theorem to the pointwise inequalities above
gives

\sigma_{\epsilon}^{-1}\mu_{i}\leq\mu_{i}^{\prime}\leq\sigma_{\epsilon}\mu_{i},\qquad i=1,\ldots,n.

∎

The constants in Proposition
4.1
are sharp. Indeed, in the
preceding path example, when
b\to 0
we have that

\dfrac{\mu_{2}^{\prime}}{\mu_{2}}\to\sigma_{\epsilon}^{-1}
whereas
\dfrac{\mu_{2}^{\prime\prime}}{\mu_{2}}\to\sigma_{\epsilon}
.

Proposition

immediately yields a corresponding multiplicative
stability result for Kemeny’s constant.

Corollary 4.2
.

Let
0<\epsilon<1
and let
G^{\prime}
be an
\epsilon
-approximation of

G
. Then

\sigma_{\epsilon}^{-1}K(G)\leq K(G^{\prime})\leq\sigma_{\epsilon}K(G).

Proof.

By Proposition
4.1
,

\sigma_{\epsilon}^{-1}\mu_{i}\leq\mu_{i}^{\prime}\leq\sigma_{\epsilon}\mu_{i},\qquad i=2,\ldots,n.

Since all these eigenvalues are positive,

\frac{\sigma_{\epsilon}^{-1}}{\mu_{i}}\leq\frac{1}{\mu_{i}^{\prime}}\leq\frac{\sigma_{\epsilon}}{{\mu_{i}}}.

Summing over
i=2,\ldots,n
and using (
1
) gives the result.
∎

Theorem 4.3

31
, Theorem 8.5]
precisely when

\frac{1+\epsilon}{1-\epsilon}<(1+\epsilon)^{3},

which is equivalent to

0<\epsilon<\frac{\sqrt{5}-1}{2}\approx 0.618.

Similarly, the lower multiplicative factor in
Theorem
4.3
is larger than
(1-\epsilon)^{3}
precisely
when

\frac{1-\epsilon}{1+\epsilon}(1-\beta^{2})>(1-\epsilon)^{3},

or equivalently,

\beta<\sqrt{\epsilon\left(1+\epsilon-\epsilon^{2}\right)}.

In particular, if we choose
\beta=\epsilon
, the lower bound in
Theorem
4.3
is sharper for every

0<\epsilon<1
, whereas the upper bound is sharper whenever

0<\epsilon<\frac{\sqrt{5}-1}{2}.

Consequently, in this parameter range, both multiplicative bounds
provided by Theorem
4.3
are tighter than those in

[
31
, Theorem 8.5]
. Moreover, for fixed
n
and

\beta=\epsilon
, both approaches have computational cost

O\!\left(\epsilon^{-2}\log(\epsilon^{-1})\right)

with respect to the approximation parameter
\epsilon
, up to factors
that are independent of
\epsilon
.

An additional feature of our approach is that the two sources of error
are controlled independently. The parameter
\epsilon
governs the
spectral sparsification error, whereas
\beta
controls the accuracy of
the Laplacian solves. Since the computational cost of our algorithm
depends only logarithmically on
\beta^{-1}
,
\beta
can be chosen
substantially smaller than
\epsilon
, making the factor
1-\beta^{2}
in the lower bound of
Theorem
4.3
very close to one, at a relatively modest additional
cost.

4.1
Computational results

We now assess the numerical performance of Algorithm
1

on several classes of networks. The purpose of these experiments is
twofold: to examine how much sparsification can be achieved while
preserving Kemeny’s constant accurately, and to compare the observed
errors with the theoretical guarantees established above.

The complexity bound in Theorem
4.4
is obtained using
the spectral sparsification and Laplacian-solving algorithms described
in
[
18
,
19
]
. However, practical implementations of
Sparsified Multigrid are not currently available
[
33
]
. For the numerical
experiments, we therefore use efficient routines from the Julia package

Laplacians.jl

[
34
]
. The numerical implementation used
in this section should therefore be regarded as a practical realization
of Algorithm
1
, rather than as an implementation of the
specific theoretical solver used in the complexity analysis.

In particular, Laplacian linear systems are solved using the
Approximate Cholesky method, which constructs an approximate Cholesky
factorization and uses it as a preconditioner for the conjugate
gradient method; see
[
33
]
.

For each system

{\sf L}_{G^{\prime}}{\sf x}={\sf z}_{i},

the iterative solver is run with Euclidean relative residual tolerance

\frac{\|{\sf L}_{G^{\prime}}{\sf y}_{i}-{\sf z}_{i}\|_{2}}{\|{\sf z}_{i}\|_{2}}\leq 10^{-8}.

This stopping criterion is expressed using the Euclidean norm instead of the Laplacian energy seminorm, and therefore does not
directly provide the parameter
\beta
appearing in Algorithm
1
.

To quantify the actual accuracy of the computed solutions, for each

i
we also compute a high-accuracy reference solution
{\sf x}_{i}
of

{\sf L}_{G^{\prime}}{\sf x}={\sf z}_{i}

by solving a reduced nonsingular Laplacian system using a Cholesky
factorization. Since
{\sf z}_{i}\perp\mathbf{1}
, any two solutions of this
system differ by an additive multiple of
\mathbf{1}
and are therefore
equivalent in the
{\sf L}_{G^{\prime}}
-energy seminorm. We define the observed
solver accuracy by

\beta_{\mathrm{obs}}=\max_{1\leq i\leq n}\frac{\|{\sf y}_{i}-{\sf x}_{i}\|_{{\sf L}_{G^{\prime}}}}{\|{\sf x}_{i}\|_{{\sf L}_{G^{\prime}}}}.

(8)

Thus,
\beta_{\mathrm{obs}}
is the smallest value, up to numerical
accuracy, for which all computed solutions are

\beta
-approximations of their corresponding Laplacian systems.

To construct the spectral approximation
G^{\prime}
, we use the
Spielman–Srivastava sparsification routine available in

Laplacians.jl
. The routine takes an input sparsification
parameter, denoted by
\epsilon_{\mathrm{in}}
, but the resulting
network can in practice satisfy a substantially stronger spectral
approximation. We therefore report in the tables the observed value

\epsilon_{\mathrm{obs}}
, defined as the smallest
\epsilon
for which

G^{\prime}
is an
\epsilon
-approximation of
G
.

To compute this value, let
\eta_{\min}
and
\eta_{\max}
denote the
smallest and largest generalized eigenvalues of the pair

({\sf L}_{G^{\prime}}^{\mathrm{red}},{\sf L}_{G}^{\mathrm{red}}),

where the same row and column are removed from both Laplacians. Then

\epsilon_{\mathrm{obs}}=\max\left\{0,\,1-\eta_{\min},\,\eta_{\max}-1\right\}.

(9)

The generalized eigenvalues are computed using

Arpack.eigs
with tolerance
10^{-8}

[
35
]
.

For validation purposes, we also compute reference values of

K(G)
and
K(G^{\prime})
. For
H\in\{G,G^{\prime}\}
, we use

K(H)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}({\sf z}_{i}^{H})^{\top}{\sf L}_{H}^{\#}{\sf z}_{i}^{H},\qquad{\sf z}_{i}^{H}=\sqrt{k_{i}^{H}}\left({\sf e}_{i}-\frac{{\sf k}^{H}}{\operatorname{vol}(H)}\right),

evaluating the corresponding quadratic forms through high-accuracy
Cholesky solves of reduced Laplacian systems.

For each experiment, we report

K(G^{\prime})
,
K_{\beta}(G^{\prime})
, the observed values

\epsilon_{\mathrm{obs}}
and
\beta_{\mathrm{obs}}
, the number of
edges retained after sparsification, and the relative error

\frac{|K_{\beta}(G^{\prime})-K(G)|}{K(G)}.

The reference Cholesky solves and the generalized-eigenvalue
computations used to determine

\beta_{\mathrm{obs}}
,
\epsilon_{\mathrm{obs}}
,
K(G)
, and

K(G^{\prime})
are used only for validation and are not part of
Algorithm
1
.

4.1.2
Barbell graphs

A barbell graph consists of two disjoint complete graphs

K_{a}
and
K_{b}
, with
a,b\geq 2
, joined by a path

P_{\ell}
, with
\ell\geq 2
, whose endpoints are identified
with one vertex of each clique.

The graph has

n=a+b+\ell-2,\qquad m=\binom{a}{2}+\binom{b}{2}+\ell-1.

A barbell graph is called
symmetric
when the
two cliques have the same order, that is, when
a=b
.

In our experiments, we consider the symmetric family of graphs with
\ell=a+2
, which we denote as
G_{a}
. Thus,
G_{a}
consists of two copies of
K_{a}
joined by
a path with
a+2
vertices, of which exactly
a
are
outside the cliques. Consequently,

n=3a,\qquad m=2\binom{a}{2}+a+1=a^{2}+1.

This choice has a particularly interesting extremal interpretation. Breen et al.
[
37
]
showed that barbell graphs can have
Kemeny’s constant of cubic order in the number of vertices, which is
the largest possible asymptotic order for an
n
-vertex graph.
Subsequently, Faught, Kempton, and Knudson

[
11
]
obtained a simpler exact expression for
Kemeny’s constant of barbell graphs and showed that, when
n
is
divisible by
3
,
G_{a}
with
a=n/3

maximizes Kemeny’s constant among all barbell graphs on
n
vertices.
The family considered in our experiments is precisely this
extremal barbell family.

Moreover, by
[
11
, Corollary 3.6]
,

K(G)=\frac{1}{54}\left[n^{3}+3n^{2}+60n-270+\frac{297n^{2}-729n+5832}{n^{3}+9n}\right].

In particular,

K(G)=\frac{n^{3}}{54}+\mathcal{O}(n^{2})=\frac{a^{3}}{2}+\mathcal{O}(a^{2}).

We consider the case
a=400
. Thus,

n=1200,

|E|=160001,

and the exact formula above gives

K(G)=32081328.3379\ldots.

4.1.3
Barabási–Albert graphs

The Barabási–Albert model combines network growth with preferential
attachment
[
38
]
. In our implementation, based on
Graphs.jl

[
39
]
, we
start from a complete graph on
n_{0}\geq 2
vertices. Each new vertex is
then joined to
q
distinct existing vertices by degree-proportional
preferential attachment, where

1\leq q\leq n_{0}\leq n.

Thus, the resulting graph is connected and the number of edges is

m=\binom{n_{0}}{2}+q(n-n_{0}).

The classical asymptotic Barabási–Albert model is usually studied
with a fixed attachment parameter as the number of vertices tends to
infinity. In that regime, the graph is sparse and exhibits the familiar
power-law degree distribution with exponent
3
. The instance considered
here is intentionally different: we take
n_{0}
and
q
to be relatively
large in order to obtain a dense preferential-attachment network.
Therefore, this experiment is intended to test the performance of the
method on a dense network with preferential-attachment degree
heterogeneity, rather than to reproduce the classical sparse
asymptotic regime.

Kemeny’s constant has previously been investigated numerically for

Barabási–Albert graphs. In particular, Wang et
al.
[
29
]
tested their Laplacian bounds on preferential-attachment
networks and found the upper bound to be very accurate in their
experiments. More generally, Xu et al.
[
31
]
studied the
scaling of Kemeny’s constant in several sparse scale-free and
small-world network models. These results provide useful context, but
they do not directly give an asymptotic formula for the dense
finite-size variant considered here.

We generate one realization with

n=3000,\qquad n_{0}=300,\qquad q=300.

Consequently,

|E|=\binom{300}{2}+300(3000-300)=854850,

which corresponds to an edge density of approximately
0.1900
. For
this realization,

K(G)=3002.302.

This value is also very close to the general lower bound

K(G)\geq\frac{(n-1)^{2}}{n}.

Indeed, since the nonzero normalized Laplacian eigenvalues satisfy

\sum_{i=2}^{n}\mu_{i}=n
, the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality gives

K(G)=\sum_{i=2}^{n}\frac{1}{\mu_{i}}\geq\frac{(n-1)^{2}}{n},

with equality for the complete graph. For
n=3000
, this yields

\frac{2999^{2}}{3000}=2998.0003\ldots,

so the Kemeny constant of the present Barabási–Albert instance is
only about
0.14\%
above this lower bound.

4.1.5
Chung–Lu graphs with power-law weights

Finally, we consider a Chung–Lu-type random graph with heterogeneous
power-law weights. The Chung–Lu model generates independent edges from
a prescribed sequence of vertex weights
[
42
]
. We use the
capped simple-graph variant

\theta_{i}=c_{0}i^{-1/(\alpha-1)},\qquad i=1,\ldots,n,

with edge probabilities

p_{ij}=\min\left\{1,\,\frac{\theta_{i}\theta_{j}}{\sum_{h=1}^{n}\theta_{h}}\right\},\qquad i<j,

where
\alpha>1
and
c_{0}>0
. Every selected edge has unit
conductance; the quantities
\theta_{i}
are parameters of the random
graph model and should not be confused with edge conductances.

The constant
c_{0}
is determined numerically by bisection so that the
actual expected average degree of the capped model satisfies

\frac{2}{n}\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}p_{ij}=\overline{d}

to relative tolerance
10^{-8}
. Thus, the calibration explicitly
takes the probability cap into account.

We use the parameters

n=2000,\qquad\alpha=2.05,\qquad\overline{d}=0.1(n-1),

so that the expected edge density is
0.1
, up to the calibration
tolerance.

An important distinction from the standard uncapped Chung–Lu model is
that, in the present construction, in which the graph is loopless, the parameters
\theta_{i}

do not coincide exactly with the expected degrees even when the
cap is inactive, since

\mathbb{E}[k_{i}]=\theta_{i}\left(1-\frac{\theta_{i}}{\sum_{h}\theta_{h}}\right).

When the cap
p_{ij}=1
is active, the discrepancy can be much larger. For this reason,
we refer to the present model as a Chung–Lu graph with power-law
weights, without asserting that the realized degree sequence follows
an exact power law. The role of admissibility conditions and
finite-size effects in power-law Chung–Lu constructions is discussed
in
[
43
]
.

For the realization used in our experiment,

|E|=199976,

corresponding to an average degree

\frac{2|E|}{n}=199.976

and an observed edge density

\frac{2|E|}{n(n-1)}=0.100038\ldots.

Its Kemeny’s constant is

K(G)=2005.028.

As in the dense Barabási–Albert example, this value is close to the

K(G)\geq\dfrac{(n-1)^{2}}{n}.

For
n=2000
,

\dfrac{1999^{2}}{2000}=1998.0005,

so the Kemeny constant of this realization is approximately

0.35\%
above the minimum possible value for a connected graph of
the same order.
