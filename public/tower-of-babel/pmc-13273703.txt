Sympathetic Reinnervation is Associated With Antihypertensive Treatment Burden and Interstitial Fibrosis After Kidney Transplantation
Authors: Yutaro Ohki, Mayuko Kawabe, Izumi Yamamoto, Ayaka Hayashi, Go Kanzaki, Akimitsu Kobayashi, Kei Matsumoto, Fumihiko Urabe, Takafumi Yanagisawa, Jun Miki, Hiroyuki Ueda, Yudo Tanno, Nobuo Tsuboi, Hiroyasu Yamamoto, Takahiro Kimura, Takashi Yokoo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSympathetic reinnervation after kidney transplantation has been demonstrated histologically and functionally, but its clinical and molecular consequences remain unclear.MethodsIn this single-center retrospective study, 50 adult living-donor kidney transplant recipients were evaluated. Based on tyrosine hydroxylase (TH) immunostaining at 3 months and subsequent biopsies, recipients were categorized into the following: regeneration group (TH-negative at 3 months and TH-positive thereafter), nonregeneration group (persistently TH-negative), and a histologically nonevaluable group (TH-positive from 3 months onward). Cortical interstitial fibrosis was quantified by Sirius Red morphometry, and fibrosis-related gene expression was assessed using NanoString Human Fibrosis V2 Panel.ResultsAlthough all grafts showed TH-positive fibers immediately after transplantation, 58% became TH-negative at 3 months (29/50). Among these, 79% later demonstrated sympathetic reinnervation (23/29), whereas 6/29 remained persistently TH-negative. Antihypertensive agent use transiently decreased at 3 months but subsequently increased, predominantly in regeneration group. At 1 and 3 years, regeneration group required more antihypertensive medications than nonregeneration group (1 year: P = 0.066; 3 years: P = 0.012). Interstitial fibrosis progressed significantly in the regeneration group (7.78% to 10.56%, P = 0.037) but remained stable in nonregeneration group. Transcriptomic analysis showed upregulation of periostin (POSTN) and plasminogen activator inhibitor-1 (PAI-1), factors associated with sympathetic activation and senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP) (analysis performed in 8 selected cases).ConclusionSympathetic reinnervation was observed together with higher antihypertensive medication burden and greater interstitial fibrosis over time, along with transcriptomic signatures consistent with SASP-related fibrogenic pathways. However, given limited size of the nonregeneration subgroup and lack of functional validation, these findings should be interpreted cautiously and regarded as hypothesis-generating.Keywords: hypertension, kidney transplantation, renal fibrosis, SASP, sympathetic activity

Renal transplantation restores excretory and endocrine function but abolishes renal sympathetic innervation. The transplanted kidney initially undergoes complete denervation, resulting in reduced renin secretion and transient improvement in systemic blood pressure.1,2 However, studies using norepinephrine spillover, microneurography, and histologic staining have demonstrated that sympathetic reinnervation gradually occurs within 2 to 3 years after transplantation.3,4

Although partial reinnervation may help restore renal autoregulation, excessive sympathetic activation has been suggested to contribute to long-term hemodynamic instability. Experimental models have shown that chronic sympathetic stimulation increases sodium retention, activates the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system, and induces renal vasoconstriction and tissue hypoxia.5,6 In patients with kidney disease, sustained sympathetic overactivity has been associated with hypertension and cardiovascular morbidity.7 However, the histologic consequences of sympathetic reinnervation in human kidney allografts remain poorly defined.

Interstitial fibrosis is a hallmark of chronic allograft injury and a major determinant of graft survival. Recent studies have emphasized the role of SASP pathways—comprising molecules such as POSTN and plasminogen activator inhibitor-1 (PAI-1 or SERPINE1)—in fibroblast activation and matrix remodeling.8,9 Notably, several SASP factors are responsive to adrenergic stimulation, supporting the concept of a neurogenic–fibrotic interaction.10

In this retrospective observational study, we aimed to determine the relationship between sympathetic reinnervation, blood pressure control, and interstitial fibrosis in kidney allografts. Rather than establishing causality, we sought to explore whether reinnervation was associated with progressive hypertension and fibrosis, potentially in conjunction with SASP-related pathways.

Methods

Study Design and Ethics Approval

This retrospective observational study was conducted at the Department of Nephrology and Hypertension at Jikei University Hospital, Tokyo, Japan. It included 50 adult patients who underwent their first living-donor kidney transplantation between April 22, 2010, and July 15, 2021. The study aimed to examine whether sympathetic nerve regeneration occurs after kidney transplantation and whether it is associated with interstitial fibrosis.

The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the ethics committee of The Jikei University School of Medicine (approval number: 33-069 [10681]). All procedures were conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and its later amendments. Written informed consent for the use of clinical and pathological specimens was obtained before transplantation.

Data Collection

We retrospectively included 50 consecutive adult recipients (aged ≥ 18 years) who underwent their first living-donor kidney transplantation and had available post-transplant protocol or indication biopsies. Patients who underwent multiorgan transplantation, retransplantation, or had inadequate biopsy specimens for histological or molecular analysis were excluded. Clinical data—including demographics, comorbidities (e.g., hypertension), primary kidney disease, dialysis history, donor characteristics, and medication history (e.g., immunosuppressants and antihypertensives)—were collected from electronic medical records. Data extraction and verification were performed between April 2023 and March 2025. Antihypertensive medications were categorized into calcium channel blockers, renin–angiotensin system inhibitors (angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors or angiotensin II receptor blockers), beta-blockers, alpha-blockers, mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists, and diuretics.

Histological Evaluation

Kidney biopsy samples were obtained at 0 hour (implantation) and 1 hour after reperfusion, and subsequently at 3 months, 1 year, 3 years, 5 years, and 10 years after transplantation. Indication biopsies and autopsy specimens were also included. All biopsy specimens were evaluated according to the Banff classification by at least 2 experienced renal pathologists who were blinded to the clinical information.

Immunohistochemical Staining for Sympathetic Nerves

Sympathetic nerve fibers were visualized using immunohistochemical staining with an anti-TH antibody (ab112, Abcam, Cambridge, UK), a validated marker of sympathetic nerve terminals.11 Formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded tissue sections (3–4 μm thick) were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and subjected to antigen retrieval in citrate buffer (pH 6.0) at 121 °C for 10 minutes using a pressure chamber. Endogenous peroxidase activity was quenched with 3% hydrogen peroxide.

Sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with the primary anti-TH antibody (1:500 dilution), followed by a polymer-based secondary detection system and diaminobenzidine visualization. Counterstaining was performed with hematoxylin. TH-positive sympathetic fibers were identified and evaluated in the cortical interstitium under light microscopy in a blinded manner. Although TH immunostaining is widely used to identify catecholaminergic sympathetic fibers, TH positivity in this study was interpreted as morphologic evidence of catecholaminergic nerve-like structures rather than proof of functional sympathetic activity. Functional validation, such as assessment of norepinephrine release or physiological sympathetic function, was not available in this retrospective cohort.

Definition and Quantification of Sympathetic Nerve Regeneration in Transplanted Kidneys

Sympathetic nerve regeneration was defined based on TH immunostaining of renal biopsy specimens. Cases that were initially TH-negative but showed TH positivity in subsequent biopsies were classified as the nerve regeneration group (Group 1), whereas those that remained negative throughout follow-up were designated as the nonregeneration group (Group 2). Cases that were persistently TH-positive from the 3-month biopsy onward were classified as histologically nonevaluable (Group 3), because TH positivity at this early time point cannot distinguish between residual donor-derived fibers, delayed degeneration, early recipient-derived sprouting, or sampling-related detection of perivascular nerve fibers. Therefore, these cases were not used to infer the timing of sympathetic reinnervation.

Sympathetic nerve fibers enter the kidney along the renal artery and its branches, forming a renal nerve plexus12 (Supplementary Figure S1). These fibers terminate mainly on the smooth muscle cells of afferent arterioles and juxtaglomerular cells, thereby innervating the renal vasculature, juxtaglomerular apparatus, and tubular structures. Consequently, the detection of sympathetic fibers by TH immunostaining is dependent on the number of arcuate arteries, interlobular arteries, and glomeruli present in each biopsy.

For quantitative assessment of sympathetic nerve regeneration, the proportion of TH-positive structures (i.e., arcuate arteries, interlobular arteries, and glomeruli) relative to the total number of each respective structure was calculated. This morphometric approach enabled standardized comparison of sympathetic innervation across variable biopsy samples.

Morphometric Analysis for Fibrosis

Fibrotic areas were visualized using Sirius Red staining. Under polarized light, collagen type I appeared yellow-orange and type III appeared green, allowing differentiation of interstitial collagen subtypes.13,14

Quantitative assessment of cortical interstitial fibrosis was performed using polarized light microscopy images of Sirius Red–stained sections. Images were analyzed with ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD). The interstitial fibrotic area was defined as the percentage of the Sirius Red–positive area relative to the total cortical area in each biopsy specimen, as described previously.15

Comprehensive mRNA Sequencing of Interstitial Fibrosis

Because kidney fibrosis is regulated by multiple signaling cascades,16 elucidating the molecular mechanism of fibrosis associated with sympathetic nerve regeneration required minimizing histological variability between groups. Therefore, cases without statistically significant differences in fibrosis area or Banff scores were selected based on the 3-year biopsy specimens.

Representative kidney biopsy tissues from 8 cases were analyzed, including 4 3-year post-transplant samples from Group 1, 4 3-month samples from the same patients, and 4 3-year post-transplant samples from Group 2. For all analyses, RNA samples were required to have a total concentration ≥ 30 ng/μl per 10 μl and an A260/280 ratio between 1.7 and 2.3.

Ten 10-μm–thick paraffin sections were prepared per sample, and total RNA was extracted using the RNeasy FFPE Kit (QIAGEN, Venlo, Netherlands) following the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA concentration and purity were assessed using a NanoDrop 2000 Spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA). Gene expression was quantified with the NanoString nCounter Human Fibrosis V2 Panel (NanoString Technologies, Seattle, WA).

Statistical Analysis

Patient characteristics were stratified according to the presence or absence of sympathetic nerve regeneration. Continuous variables were compared using 1-way analysis of variance or the Kruskal–Wallis test, as appropriate, and categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test. For the 2 groups used in the RNA expression analysis, continuous variables were analyzed using the unpaired t test or Mann–Whitney U test, and categorical variables using the chi-square test. All statistical analyses were performed using STATA version 15.0 (StataCorp, College Station, TX). A P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant in all tests.

This study was reported in accordance with the STROBE guidelines, and the checklist is provided in the Supplementary Material.

Results

Patient and Donor Characteristics

A total of 50 kidney transplant recipients and their corresponding living donors were included in the study. Based on TH immunostaining of post-transplant biopsy specimens, recipients were categorized into 3 groups as follows: Group 1 (nerve regeneration, n = 23), Group 2 (nonregeneration, n = 6), and Group 3 (histologically nonevaluable, n = 21).

Baseline characteristics of the recipients are summarized in Table 1, and detailed pairwise comparisons are shown in Supplementary Table S1. Age, sex, body mass index, primary kidney disease, dialysis modality, and immunosuppressive regimen did not differ significantly among the 3 groups. The overall prevalence of hypertension was 80%, but its distribution varied significantly among groups (91.3%, 33.3%, and 80.9% for Groups 1–3, respectively; P = 0.007). However, this result should be interpreted cautiously given the small sample size of the nonregeneration group. Other comorbidities, including diabetes mellitus, ABO incompatibility, and HLA mismatch, were comparable across groups.

Sympathetic Nerve Regeneration After Kidney Transplantation

Sympathetic fibers were evaluated by TH immunostaining in serial renal allograft biopsies (Figure 1). TH-positive fibers were consistently present in all 0- and 1-hour biopsies, indicating intact donor-derived sympathetic nerves immediately after reperfusion.

Representative case of a 25-year-old male recipient with IgA nephropathy who received an ABOincompatible kidney transplant from his mother, categorized into the sympathetic nerve regeneration group (Group 1). At 1-hour post-transplantation, numerous TH-positive sympathetic nerve bundles were observed along arteries and around Bowman’s capsules (A–C). At 3 months, no TH-positive fibers were detected in perivascular or periglomerular areas, despite the presence of large arteries, indicating complete denervation (D–F). By 1 year, TH-positive fibers had reappeared around Bowman’s capsules, confirming sympathetic reinnervation, which persisted at 3 years (G–I). Scale bars = 100 μm.

At 3 months, 58% of grafts (n = 29) became TH-negative, whereas 42% (n = 21) remained positive (Figure 2). However, TH positivity at this time point should be interpreted with caution, as it may reflect residual donor-derived fibers, delayed degeneration, early nerve sprouting, or sampling variability rather than definitive functional reinnervation. Persistent TH positivity from the earliest post-transplant biopsy prevented determination of reinnervation timing; these cases were defined as the histologically nonevaluable group (Group 3), likely reflecting delayed degeneration or early regrowth. Among the 29 TH-negative grafts, 23 later regained TH positivity (nerve regeneration group, Group 1), whereas 6 remained negative throughout follow-up (nonregeneration group, Group 2).

Sympathetic nerve regeneration was histologically confirmed in 23 recipients (Group 1: regeneration group), absent in 6 (Group 2: non-regeneration group), and indeterminate in 21 due to persistent TH positivity from early post-transplant biopsies (Group 3: histologically non-evaluable group). At 1 and 3 years, TH-positive fibers localized mainly to perivascular and peritubular interstitial regions. Group 2 showed no TH-positive fibers (0%); thus, it was excluded from statistical analyses.

In summary, sympathetic fibers degenerated shortly after transplantation but regenerated in 23 of 29 initially TH-negative grafts within 1 to 3 years, providing histologic evidence of gradual sympathetic reinnervation.

Temporal Relationship Between Nerve Regeneration and Fibrotic Changes

To clarify the temporal association between sympathetic nerve regeneration and interstitial fibrosis, both parameters were quantitatively assessed in serial biopsy specimens obtained 1 and 3 years after transplantation (Figure 3). In the sympathetic nerve regeneration group (Group 1), the Sirius Red–positive area increased from 7.78 ± 2.84% at 1 year to 10.56 ± 3.12% at 3 years, indicating progressive interstitial fibrotic expansion. In contrast, the nonregeneration group (Group 2) showed minimal change (6.21 ± 2.07% at 1 year vs. 6.22 ± 1.98% at 3 years). The difference in fibrotic change between these 2 groups was statistically significant (P = 0.037).

Similarly, the histologically nonevaluable group (Group 3) showed a modest increase in fibrosis (6.82% ± 2.31 at 1 year vs. 9.18 ± 2.85% at 3 years), although the clinical relevance of this trend remains uncertain given persistent TH positivity from the early post-transplant period.

Collectively, these findings show that interstitial fibrosis progressed significantly in the sympathetic nerve regeneration group but not in the nonregeneration group, and may indicate a temporal association between sympathetic reinnervation and fibrotic remodeling in kidney allografts; however, this interpretation should be made with caution, given the limited number of patients in the nonregeneration subgroup.

Changes in Antihypertensive Therapy After Transplantation

The number of antihypertensive agents was evaluated at baseline, 3 months, 1 year, and 3 years after transplantation (Table 3, Figure 4). At baseline, the number of agents was comparable among the 3 groups (mean ± SD: 2.1 ± 0.5, 3.2 ± 0.4, and 3.0 ± 0.6 for Groups 1–3, respectively; P = 0.084).

Changes in antihypertensive therapy after transplantation

The mean number of antihypertensive drugs was evaluated at baseline, 3 months, 1 year, and 3 years after kidney transplantation in the Group 1: sympathetic nerve regeneration group (n = 23), Group 2: non-regeneration group (n = 6), and Group 3: histologically non-evaluable group (n = 21). All groups showed a transient decrease at 3 months, followed by a gradual increase thereafter. At 1 year, the Group 1 tended to require more antihypertensive agents than the Group 2 (∗P = 0.066). By 3 years, this difference became significant, with the Group 1 using significantly more agents than both the Group 2 (∗∗P = 0.012) and Group 3 (††P = 0.042). Data are expressed as mean ± SD. Statistical analysis was performed using the Kruskal–Wallis test followed by Holm-adjusted pairwise comparisons.

During follow-up, the number of antihypertensive medications transiently decreased at 3 months and subsequently increased in all groups, most prominently in the sympathetic nerve regeneration group (Group 1). At 1 year, Group 1 tended to require more agents than Group 2 (H = 6.12, P = 0.047), although this difference was not significant after Holm adjustment (P = 0.066). By 3 years, the difference became significant, with Group 1 requiring more agents than Group 2 (H = 8.59, P = 0.014; pairwise P = 0.012). Group 3 also required slightly more agents than Group 1 (P = 0.042).

As shown in Figure 4, the antihypertensive treatment burden increased progressively in Group 1 but remained relatively stable in Group 2. These findings are consistent with a possible association between sympathetic reinnervation and a gradual escalation in antihypertensive therapy after transplantation, which may reflect evolving blood pressure management needs, although the small size of the nonregeneration subgroup warrants cautious interpretation. Detailed class-specific and representative antihypertensive treatment profiles are provided in Supplementary Tables S2 and S3.

Gene Expression Associated With Nerve Regeneration

The 2 groups were matched for biopsy vintage (mean 3.3 ± 0.3 vs. 3.3 ± 0.4 years), Banff classification scores, and the extent of interstitial fibrosis (7.8% [6.2–13.9] vs. 6.2% [4.8–7.4]). Discussion

In this single-center retrospective cohort, we observed that histologic evidence of sympathetic reinnervation after kidney transplantation was associated with progressive cortical interstitial fibrosis, a greater requirement for antihypertensive therapy, and upregulation of senescence- and matrix-related transcripts, particularly POSTN and SERPINE1 (plasminogen activator inhibitor-1). These findings raise the possibility that sympathetic reinnervation may be associated with changes beyond structural reappearance; however, functional implications remain uncertain. Importantly, the presence of TH-positive fibers should not be interpreted as proof of functional sympathetic recovery.

Our observations extend previous reports by providing longitudinal, biopsy-based evidence of the reappearance and peripheral arborization of TH-positive fibers in human renal allografts.4,11 However, the functional and hemodynamic significance of graft reinnervation remains debated.4,17, 18, 19 A key limitation of this study is the interpretation of TH immunostaining. Although TH is a marker of catecholaminergic sympathetic fibers, it does not establish functional sympathetic activity. In particular, TH positivity at 3 months after transplantation is difficult to interpret and may reflect residual donor-derived fibers, delayed degeneration, early sprouting, or sampling variability. Therefore, such cases were classified as histologically nonevaluable rather than as evidence of early reinnervation. Previous histological studies have suggested that structural nerve sprouting may occur relatively early after transplantation, including reports of TH-positive fibers within months4; however, these findings, similar to ours, do not establish functional reinnervation. Accordingly, our results should be interpreted as morphologic associations and regarded as hypothesis-generating.

As this was an observational study, the relationships among reinnervation, fibrosis progression, and hypertension should be interpreted as associative rather than causal. Within the multifactorial landscape of chronic allograft injury—where interstitial fibrosis and tubular atrophy arise from overlapping immunologic, hemodynamic, and toxic insults20—mechanistic inference must remain cautious.

Nonetheless, neural modulation of renal remodeling is biologically plausible. Experimental data indicate that sympathetic activation has been associated with fibrogenesis, whereas renal denervation has been reported to attenuate it.21, 22, 23 The microanatomic distribution of sympathetic inputs—entering alongside segmental and interlobular arteries—may also explain biopsy-to-biopsy variability in reinnervation detectability.12

First, sympathetic reinnervation after surgical denervation is expected to occur gradually and heterogeneously across renal compartments.4,24 Peripheral nerve regeneration proceeds through sequential interactions among Schwann cells, extracellular matrix components, and growth factors rather than as an all-or-none event.25 Three-dimensional tissue-clearing analyses have revealed complex sympathetic regrowth after ischemia–reperfusion injury,11 suggesting that conventional histology may underestimate regional variability.

Accordingly, our classification into 3 categories—conversion from TH-negative to positive, persistently negative, or persistently positive from 3 months onward—was pragmatic but inevitably simplified a continuous and spatially heterogeneous process. In particular, cases with persistent TH positivity from the earliest biopsy could not be distinguished between residual donor-derived fibers and early recipient-derived reinnervation, underscoring the need for quantitative morphometry and earlier protocol sampling to better capture the onset and distribution of neural regrowth.12,25

Second, the relationship between sympathetic reinnervation and blood pressure warrants attention. Renal sympathetic activation promotes renin release, vasoconstriction, and sodium retention,2,7 and in our cohort, patients with reinnervation more frequently had pre-existing hypertension and required more antihypertensive agents. These findings raise the possibility that structural reinnervation may be linked to partial recovery of efferent sympathetic function. Two nonexclusive mechanisms could potentially account for this association: (i) direct neural–fibrotic crosstalk via upregulated matrix- and senescence-associated genes (POSTN, SERPINE1); and (ii) indirect effects through sustained hypertension and hemodynamic stress. Given the multifactorial nature of interstitial fibrosis and tubular atrophy—shaped by rejection, calcineurin inhibitor toxicity, and infection26,27—residual confounding cannot be excluded, and our results should be viewed as hypothesis-generating.

Third, the temporal association between sympathetic reinnervation and interstitial fibrosis progression may offer clues regarding a potential pathogenic role. In serial biopsies, fibrosis significantly increased between 1 and 3 years in the regeneration group but remained unchanged in the nonregeneration group. These findings are consistent with the possibility that sympathetic regrowth may coincide with—or be related to—fibrotic remodeling within the renal cortex. Experimental data support this concept— norepinephrine stimulates fibroblast proliferation and collagen synthesis through β- and α2-adrenergic receptors.28,29 Sympathetic terminals also release neuropeptides such as neuropeptide Y and adenosine triphosphate, which activate profibrotic pathways in interstitial and immune cells.30 Conversely, denervation models show that loss of sympathetic input attenuates expression of fibrogenic mediators including transforming growth factor-β1 and collagen type I alpha 1 chain and reduces myofibroblast activation.31 Collectively, these findings are compatible with the hypothesis that sympathetic reinnervation may accompany partial re-establishment of local adrenergic signaling within the graft, potentially creating a microenvironment that could favor senescence-associated and matrix-producing pathways. This neural–fibrotic coupling may represent a novel axis contributing to chronic allograft remodeling beyond traditional immune-mediated injury.

Fourth, at the molecular level, we explored how sympathetic reinnervation may be associated with chronic allograft fibrosis through modulation of senescence- and matrix-related gene expression. Sympathetic reinnervation was accompanied by increased POSTN and SERPINE1 expression, key components of the SASP and mediators of extracellular matrix remodeling. POSTN drives fibroblast-to-myofibroblast transition and matrix accumulation, whereas SERPINE1 amplifies senescence and inhibits matrix degradation through p53–p21 and NF-κB signaling.26,32,33 These molecules act not only as biomarkers but also as effectors of chronic fibrosis. As shown in Figure 6, fatty acid-binding protein4 and connective tissue growth factor expression also increased from 3 months to 3 years in the reinnervation group, indicating persistent proximal tubular stress and activation of profibrotic signaling. Together with elevated POSTN and SERPINE1, these findings suggest that sympathetic reinnervation may be accompanied by a maladaptive neuro-tubular-fibrotic response rather than restoring homeostasis.

Emerging evidence indicates that sympathetic activation has been reported to be associated with SASP-related gene expression through β-adrenergic signaling,34,35 linking neural activity to cellular senescence and sustained tissue injury. In the transplant setting, this adrenergic–SASP coupling may represent a maladaptive process in which nerve regeneration—intended to restore homeostasis—paradoxically sustains low-grade inflammation and fibrotic remodeling, even in the absence of overt rejection or immune-mediated injury.

Fifth, the clinical implications of these findings should be interpreted cautiously. Histologic detection of sympathetic reinnervation currently has no direct impact on patient management; thus, the contribution of this study is primarily conceptual. Our data are consistent with the possibility that sympathetic reinnervation may represent 1 of several contributing elements in chronic allograft remodeling and generate testable hypotheses for therapeutic intervention—such as sympathetic modulation or targeted antifibrotic therapy in patients showing early evidence of reinnervation, although prospective validation will be required.

This study has several limitations. First, it was conducted at a single center with a limited sample size, and the nonregeneration subgroup was particularly small, limiting statistical power and increasing the possibility of residual confounding. Second, the number of patients who remained persistently TH-negative was very small, which limits the robustness of comparisons between groups and increases the risk of residual confounding. Therefore, between-group differences should be interpreted as exploratory. Third, sampling bias is inherent to renal biopsy, as sympathetic fibers are distributed heterogeneously along segmental and interlobular arteries; a given core may not accurately represent overall reinnervation. Fourth, sympathetic activity was not directly measured—tissue or urinary norepinephrine levels, neuroimaging, and physiological assessments were unavailable—so our conclusions are confined to morphologic associations rather than functional confirmation. Fifth, cases with persistent TH positivity from the 3-month biopsy onward were categorized as “nonevaluable,” which may represent either residual donor-derived fibers or early recipient-derived regeneration; thus, the exact onset could not be determined. Earlier and more frequent protocol biopsies would be needed to address this limitation.

Taken together, these limitations indicate that any relationship between sympathetic reinnervation and fibrosis should be interpreted with caution. Notably, patients who remained denervated exhibited better blood pressure control and required fewer antihypertensive agents, which may indicate a potential association between persistent denervation and relative protection; however, this observation requires confirmation in larger cohorts.

In summary, sympathetic reinnervation after kidney transplantation was frequently observed and appeared to be associated with greater interstitial fibrosis and a profibrotic, senescence-related transcriptional signature. Given the heterogeneity of neural regrowth, the multifactorial nature of interstitial fibrosis and tubular atrophy, and the retrospective design, these observations should be regarded as hypothesis-generating. Although the present findings do not establish causality or functional recovery, they provide a structured histologic and molecular framework for future investigations of neuro-renal interactions in the transplanted kidney. Larger prospective studies incorporating functional and physiological assessments will be necessary to determine the true clinical impact of sympathetic reinnervation.

Conclusion

TH-positive catecholaminergic nerve-like fibers were observed in a substantial proportion of kidney transplant recipients and were associated with the findings with progressive interstitial fibrosis, senescence-related transcriptomic changes, and increased antihypertensive requirements. Although causality cannot be inferred and clinical applicability remains limited, these findings raise the possibility that persistent denervation may confer protection and that neural–fibrotic interactions could represent a novel therapeutic target to improve long-term allograft outcomes.
