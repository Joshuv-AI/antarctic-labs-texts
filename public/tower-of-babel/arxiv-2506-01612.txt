Percolation on random 2-lifts
Paul Drouvillé

Abstract
Given a graph G G , we consider a model for a random cover of G G by taking two parallel copies of G G and crossing every pair of parallel edges randomly with probability q q independently of each other. The resulting graph G q G_{q} , is a random 2 2 -lift of G G that may not be transitive but still probabilistically exhibit many properties of transitive graphs. Studying percolation in this context can help us test the reliability and robustness of our proofs methods in percolation theory. Our three main results on this model are the continuity of the critical parameter p c ​ ( G q ) p_{c}(G_{q}) , for q ∈ ( 0 , 1 ) q\in(0,1) , the strict monotonicity p c ​ ( G q ) < p c ​ ( G ) p_{c}(G_{q})<p_{c}(G) and the exponential decay of the cluster size in the subcritical regime at q = 1 / 2 q=1/2 .

Introduction

Bernoulli Percolation was introduced in 1957 by Broadbent and Hammersely
[
BH57
]
to model the propagation of a fluid through porous materials. While originally focused on Euclidean lattices
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, the model was also studied on different classes of graphs. In 1996, Benjamini and Schramm
[
BS96
]
initiated a more systematic study of percolation on transitive and quasi-transitive graphs and made connections with geometric group theory. In their article, they study the link between percolation on a graph and percolation on one of its covers.
If
G
is a graph and

one its vertex, we write

for the subgraph, induces by

, its neighbors and the edges that join

to its neighbors. We will say that a graph

is a cover of

if there is a surjective graph homomorphism

from

to

such that for any vertex

of

the restriction
\pi:H_{1}(x)\to G_{1}(\pi(x))
is an isomorphism.

Throughout this paper, our graphs will always be non-empty simple undirected graphs, connected and locally finite
. We will mostly focus (though not entirely) our study on transitive graphs, that is, graphs such that there is a group that acts transitively on the set of vertices through graph automorphism.

We want to examine a specific model for a random cover of a graph

. We construct our cover by first taking two copies of

in parallel,

Finding the possible values for the number of vertices of a Ramanujan graph, given its degree

For example, one possible solution to create

We need to adapt the definition of an exploration to discover the percolation of edges as well as the structure of the graph itself. We show that natural properties of an exploration for transitive deterministic graphs can be also be true for random covers. The challenge in exploring random graphs is finding what information we need to reveal or not at each step. For example, we may sometimes need to explore an edge without knowing one of its endpoints. We make use of gauge transformations within our random cover that reveal hidden symmetries of our constructions that exist at
q=1/2
.

We point out that the model we study is not “monotone” in the following sense : If
e=\{x,y\}
is an edge of
G
, then

and

are two lifts of

that can be present in

. However, if

is open in

, it is present in
G_{q}
and therefore

is not present and in particular is closed. This means, that any “positive” information acquired about percolation comes with a “negative” information associated. This property makes studying sharpness on the model harder and more interesting.

If

is a vertex in a graph

, then

(resp.

) will denote the set of vertices at distance at most (resp. exactly)

. The set

will denote the set of edges with one endpoint in

and the other in

. All these notations will be extended to sets of vertices

by taking the union for all the elements in

.

To give a formal definition of our random 2-lift, we consider a graph
G=(V,E)
and define the modified vertex set

. For every vertex

in
V
, we write
v_{0}
and

for the two corresponding lifts in

. If

is a lift of

, then we call

, the “twin vertex” of

.
With each edge
e=\{u,v\}
in,
E
we associate a Bernoulli variable
\eta_{e}
with parameter

, the random variables being taken independent of one another. This allows us to define a random edge set:

E(\eta)=\Big\{\{u_{0},v_{\eta_{e}}\}\cup\{u_{1},v_{1-\eta_{e}}\}\,\Big|\,e=\{u,v\}\in E\Big\}.

These two set defines our random 2-lift
G_{q}=(V^{\star},E(\eta))
. Let
\pi:G_{q}\to G
be the associated covering map. The action of
\pi
is essentially to forget the distinction between the floors
0
and

.

We then add another layer of randomness through Bernoulli percolation. Let
(\omega_{e^{\prime}})_{e^{\prime}\in E(\eta)}
be an independent family of Bernoulli variable with parameter

. We define
G_{p,q}
as the random graph of open edges for the Bernoulli percolation on
G_{q}
. Note that the measures on
\omega
are conditional on the state of
\eta
, as we need to know which edges exist in our graph to talk about open or closed. Another solution would be to consider edges as an abstract set that may randomly link vertices or not. These considerations do not matter until Section

,so we will leave this discussion for now.
In this paper, variables “
\eta
” describe the switching state of pairs of edges, while variables “
\omega
” describe a Bernoulli percolation.

Let
G
be a transitive graph. Despite the randomness of the graph

, Bernoulli percolation still exhibits a deterministic phase transition. Let
\Psi(p,q)
be the probability that there is an infinite cluster in

. As in standard Bernoulli percolation for deterministic graphs, Kolmogorov 0-1 law holds and tells us that
\Psi(p,q)\in\{0,1\}
because the event does not depend on any finite boxes in the graph. The standard coupling also shows that for every

in

,

is weakly increasing.
We can therefore conclude that for every

there is a unique critical parameter

such that:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\Psi(p,q)=1&\mbox{for }p>p_{c}(q),\\
\Psi(p,q)=0&\mbox{for }p<p_{c}(q).\end{array}\right.

Since deg
(G_{q})=

(G)<+\infty
, we have
p_{c}(q)>0
.

Finally, for a subset
S
of

, we call
\phi_{S}
, the gauge transformations, which for every vertex

, switches

with

, that is

\phi_{S}(v_{\varepsilon})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}T(v_{\varepsilon})\text{ if }v\in S,\\
v_{\varepsilon}\text{ if }v\notin S.\end{array}\right.

We also consider the extension of this mapping to edges also noted
\phi_{S}
defined by
\phi_{S}(\{x,y\})=\{\phi_{S}(x),\phi_{S}(y)\}
. The structure of

makes

a graph isomorphism.

Basic properties

In this section, we give simple properties of the function
q\mapsto p_{c}(q)
. The first one gives a crude bounds for its range.

Proposition 2.1
.

For every
(p,q)
in
(0,1)^{2}
,
\theta\left(p^{2}\right)\leqslant\theta(p,q)\leqslant\theta\left(1-(1-p)^{2}\right)
.

Proof.

With every realization of
G_{p,q}
, we can associate two percolation configurations on
G
by looking at the image of
G_{p,q}
by
\pi
. Recall that
\vee
stands for the maximum operator and
\wedge
for the minimum operator. Formally, given a family
(\omega^{+}_{e},\omega^{-}_{e})_{e\in E}
of independent Bernoulli variable with parameter
p
that create a percolation on
G_{q}
, we define
\omega^{\wedge}_{e}=\omega^{+}_{e}\wedge\omega^{-}_{e}
and
\omega^{\vee}_{e}=\omega^{+}_{e}\vee\omega^{-}_{e}
. The families
(\omega^{\wedge}_{e})_{e\in E}
and
(\omega^{\vee}_{e})_{e\in E}
are families of independent Bernoulli variables with parameter
p^{2}
and
1-(1-p)^{2}
. The existence of an infinite cluster for
\omega^{\wedge}
assures the existence of an infinite cluster for
\omega
which itself assures the existence of an infinite cluster for
\omega^{\vee}
. This means we get the inequality

\theta(p^{2})\leqslant\theta(p,q)\leqslant\theta(1-(1-p)^{2}).

∎

Remark 2.2
.

The proposition gives us an inequality for
p_{c}
:

1-\sqrt{1-p_{c}(G)}\leqslant p_{c}(q)\leqslant\sqrt{p_{c}(G)}.

Recall that a graph is bipartite if there is a partition
V_{1},V_{2}
of the vertex set such that every edge can be written as
\{v_{1},v_{2}\}
with
v_{1}\in V_{1}
and
v_{2}\in V_{2}
.

Proposition 2.3
.

If
G=(V,E)
is a bipartite graph, then

\forall\,q\in\,[0,1],\quad p_{c}(q)=p_{c}(1-q).

Proof.

Let us call
V_{1},V_{2}
a partition associated with
V
. We consider the effect of the gauge transformation
\phi_{V_{1}}
. Since every edge has one and only one endpoint in
V_{1}
, every edge changes its switching state after the application of the morphism. Therefore, if the edge is switching with probability
q
, then its image is switching with probability
1-q
. The action of
\phi_{V_{1}}
gives a coupling between
G_{q}
and
G_{1-q}
and therefore between
G_{p,q}
and
G_{p,1-q}
such that
\theta(p,q)=\theta(p,1-q)
. Proposition
2.3
follows.
∎

In the case
q=1/2
, there is a simple formula for the probability that
G_{q}
is connected for a finite graph
G
. If
C
is a cycle in
G
, we say it is
switching
in
G_{q}
if there is an odd number of edges
e\in C
such that
\eta_{e}=1
.

Proposition 2.4
.

For
G=(V,E)
a finite connected graph, the probability that
G_{1/2}
is not connected is
2^{|V|-|E|-1}
.

Proof.

First, note that at
q=1/2
, the probability of switching and not switching are the same. This means that every configuration has the same probability of happening. There are in total
2^{|E|}
possible configurations, and we need to count how many do not make
G_{1/2}
connected. In every configuration, we can use the gauge transformations
(\phi_{S})_{S\subset V}
, which preserve the connectedness of the configuration.

The kernel of the action is composed of the subsets
S=\emptyset
and
S=V
. The factorization theorem tells us that the number of non-connected configurations is
2^{|V|-1}
. We conclude by dividing this last quantity by the total number of configurations, which is
2^{|E|}
.
∎

Continuity of

This section aim at proving Theorem

, which is a consequence of the following theorem.

Theorem 3.1
.

The function
q\mapsto p_{c}(q)
is 1/2-Holderian on every compact interval of
(0,1)
.

This theorem is a consequence of the following inequality for the critical parameter.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
b,r
in
[0,1]
, then for all
a
in
[(1-r)\,b,(1-r)\,b+r]
, we have

p_{c}(b)\geqslant(1-\sqrt{r})\,p_{c}(a).

Proof of Theorem
3.1
given Lemma
3.2
.

For
I=[\alpha,\beta]\subset(0,1)
, let
q
and
q^{\prime}
be real numbers in
I
such that
q^{\prime}<q
. We can write
q^{\prime}=(1-r)q
if we set
r=\frac{q-q^{\prime}}{q}\leqslant\frac{1}{\alpha}(q-q^{\prime})
.
Using the inequality from Lemma
3.2
with

and
b=q
we get

p_{c}(q)-p_{c}(q^{\prime})\geqslant-\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha}}\sqrt{q-q^{\prime}}.

On the other side, we can write
q=(1-r)q^{\prime}+r
if we set
r=\frac{q-q^{\prime}}{1-q^{\prime}}\leqslant\frac{1}{1-\beta}(q-q^{\prime})
. Again, using the inequality of Lemma
3.2
with
a=q
and
b=q^{\prime}
, we get

p_{c}(q^{\prime})-p_{c}(q)\geqslant-\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\beta}}\sqrt{q-q^{\prime}}.

We conclude by setting
C=\max\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha}},\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\beta}}\right)
to get
|p_{c}(q)-p_{c}(q^{\prime})|\leqslant C{|q-q^{\prime}|}^{1/2}
in
[\alpha,\beta
].
∎

Proof of Lemma
3.2
.

Let
0<r<1
be a real number and
0\leqslant q\leqslant 1-r
. First, let us take a family
(\eta_{e})_{e\in E}
of independent uniform variables in

. By fixing a threshold

, we can associate a realization

by using the Bernoulli variables
\mathbb{1}_{[0,a]}(\eta_{e})
.

Let
(X_{e})_{e\in E(G)}
be a sequence of i.i.d random variables uniform in
\{-1,1\}
and independent of the variables
(\eta_{e})_{e\in E(G)}
. Let
(Y_{e})_{e\in E(G)}
be a sequence of i.i.d Bernoulli variables with parameter
A=2\frac{\sqrt{r}(1-\sqrt{r})}{1-r}=1-\frac{(1-\sqrt{r})^{2}}{1-r}<1
. The
Y_{e}
are independent of every other variables. Let us now define two random variables
\omega^{-}_{e}
and
\omega^{+}_{e}
as
(\omega^{+}_{e},\omega^{-}_{e})=f(\eta_{e},X_{e},Y_{e})
in the following way:

•

If
\eta_{e}\in\>(q,q+r)
, then
\omega^{+}_{e,}=\omega^{-}_{e}=0
.

•

Else, we look at the value of
Y_{e}
. If it equals
1
, then exactly one of the random variables
\omega^{\varepsilon}_{e}
is set to

while the other is set to

. This choice is made by looking at the sign of

.

•

If the last condition fails, we set them both equal to

.

The reason for the value of the coefficient

is that it is the value that makes

and
\omega^{+}_{e}
two independent Bernoulli variables with parameter

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\omega^{-}_{e}=\omega^{+}_{e}=1)

\displaystyle=(1-r)\left(1-2\frac{\sqrt{r}(1-\sqrt{r})}{1-r}\right)

\displaystyle=(1-r)\left(\frac{1-2\sqrt{r}+r}{1-r}\right)=(1-\sqrt{r})^{2}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(\omega^{+}_{e}=1)\mathbb{P}(\omega^{-}_{e}=1).

Note that by construction, all
\omega^{\varepsilon}_{e}
are independent when
e
varies.
let us then define a random switching variable
\hat{\eta}_{e}
independently for every edge in
G
. We set
\hat{\eta}_{e}=g(\eta_{e},Z_{e})
where
(Z_{e})_{e\in E(G)}
is a sequence of i.i.d Bernoulli variables with parameter
\frac{q}{1-r}
independent of everything else so far.
We construct
g
as follows:

•

If
\eta_{e}\leqslant q
, then we set
\hat{\eta}_{e}=1
,

•

If
\eta_{e}>q+r
, then we set
\hat{\eta}_{e}=0
,

•

If
q<\eta_{e}\leqslant q+r
, then we set
\hat{\eta}_{e}=Z_{e}
.

This ensures all
\hat{\eta}_{e}
are independent Bernoulli variables with parameter
\frac{q}{1-r}
.
Both families of variables are mutually independent, despite both“depending” on
(\eta_{e})_{e\in E}
.
For example, we can calculate

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\hat{\eta}_{e}=1,\,\omega^{-}_{e}=\omega^{+}_{e}=1)

\displaystyle=(1-q-r)\left(1-2\frac{\sqrt{r}(1-\sqrt{r})}{1-r}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(1-q-r\right)\frac{\left(1-2\sqrt{r}+r\right)}{1-r}

\displaystyle=\left(1-\frac{q}{1-r}\right)(1-\sqrt{r})^{2}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(\hat{\eta}_{e}=1)\mathbb{P}(\omega^{+}_{e}=1)\mathbb{P}(\omega^{-}_{e}=1).

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\hat{\eta}_{e}=1,\,\omega^{-}_{e}=1)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(\hat{\eta}_{e}=1,\,\omega^{+}_{e}=1)

\displaystyle=q\left(1-\frac{\sqrt{r}(1-\sqrt{r})}{1-r}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{q}{1-r}(1-\sqrt{r})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(\hat{\eta}_{e}=1)\mathbb{P}(\omega^{+}_{e}=1)=\mathbb{P}(\hat{\eta}_{e}=1)\mathbb{P}(\omega^{-}_{e}=1)

.

These are all the calculations needed to prove independence for the Bernoulli variables.
Then,
(\omega,\hat{\eta})
allows us to define a random graph

with percolation

\overline{G}
and

is such that they both agree on the switching state of the edges which are open in
\hat{G}
. Also, every edge open in
\hat{G}
is open in

and is in the same switching state. The reason for this property is that
\hat{G}
and
\overline{G}

but these edges are by definition closed in

.

Independently of everything done so far and independently for all

, we add a random percolation of parameter

and

\overline{G}

\hat{G}
and

\overline{G}

\hat{G}
. This procedure create a new coupled percolation between
\hat{G}
and
\overline{G}
.

The result retains the following coupling property:If the cluster of the origin is infinite in

G_{p,a}
. Note that the addition of a new percolation to both graphs is unambiguous, since every pair of edges that are not fully closed are in the same switching state.
We can therefore derive the inequality:

\theta\left(p\,(1-\sqrt{r}),\frac{q}{1-r}\right)\leqslant\theta(p,a).

meaning that

\displaystyle\theta\left(p\,(1-\sqrt{r}),\frac{q}{1-r}\right)>0

\displaystyle\Longrightarrow\theta(p,a)>0,

\displaystyle p\,(1-\sqrt{r})>p_{c}(\frac{q}{1-r})

\displaystyle\Longrightarrow p>p_{c}(a),

\displaystyle p_{c}\left(\frac{q}{1-r}\right)

\displaystyle\geqslant(1-\sqrt{r})\,p_{c}(a)\quad\text{for all $a$ in $(q,q+r)$}.

We conclude by setting
b=\frac{q}{1-r}\in[0,1]
.

4
Strict monotonicity

Note that while strict monotonicity was proved in
[
MS19
]
for covering graphs with strong hypothesis on the type of cover, weak monotonicity for general cover graphs was proved in
[
BS96
]
In their article, Benjamini and Schramm show that percolation in the cover graph is more probable than in the base graph. In particular, this implies weak monotonicity of the critical parameter.
We can directly use their result on every realization of our graph which shows that
p_{c}(G_{q}(\eta))\leqslant p_{c}(G)
. and we thus have,
p_{c}(q)\leqslant p_{c}(G)
. Therefore, only the strict monotonicity is a difficult problem. In this section, we adapt the arguments of

]
to prove Theorem

4.1
Preliminary lemmas

Similarly to
[
MS19
]
, we can dissect the proof of monotonicity into two main inequalities. To compare percolation on
G_{q}
and
G
, we use the notion of
enhanced percolation
— see
[
AG91
,
BBR
]
. Our enhanced percolation is defined in the following way:
If during our exploration in
G
the
r
-ball of a vertex
x
is fully open (meaning every edge with both sides lying in the
r
-ball is open) then, with probability

, we can consider every vertex at distance exactly
r+1
to be part of our connected component. The value
r
et

will be chosen later.

Formally,
(\alpha_{v})_{v\in V(G)}
is of family of independent Bernoulli variables with parameter
s
and independent of
\omega
. Then, given a configuration
(\omega,\alpha)\in\{0,1\}^{E}\times\{0,1\}^{V}
, the cluster of a vertex

in enhanced percolation is constructed recursively starting with
\mathcal{C}_{o}=\{o\}
by alternating steps.
At odd steps, we define
C_{2N+1}
to be the union of all the
\omega
-clusters (that is, the clusters for usual percolation) of the vertices in
C_{2N}
.
At even steps,
C_{2N+2}
is the union of
C_{2N+1}
and the set of vertices

such that there is a

that satisfies the following conditions :

1.

d(u,v)=r+1
,

2.

B_{r}(u)\subset C_{2N+1}
,

3.

\omega_{e}=1
for all edges with endpoints in
B_{r}(u)
,

4.

\alpha_{u}=1
.

After an infinite number of steps, the cluster of

is defined as the infinite union of the
C_{n}
.

Note that the power given by augmentation is not symmetric. If the augmentation allows one to explore the vertex
y
starting from
x
, it does not mean the vertex
x
is visited in the exploration started at
y
. For a fixed parameter
s
, there has to exist a fixed critical parameter
p_{c}(G,s)
such that

is connected to infinity with positive probability if
p>p_{c}(G,s)
and is almost surely not connected to infinity if
p<p_{c}(G,s)
. If
H
is a (potentially random) graph, we will write
C^{p}_{H}(x)
for the cluster of the vertex
x
in
H
for usual percolation and
\mathcal{C}^{p,s}_{H}(x)
for the cluster of
x
in augmented percolation.
Therefore, to prove strict monotonicity it suffices to choose a correct parameter

and prove these two inequalities

p_{c}(G_{q})\overset{(1)}{\leqslant}p_{c}(G,s)\overset{(2)}{<}p_{c}(G),

which can be summed up in these two propositions.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
q
in
(0,1)
, and
G
be an infinite transitive graph that is not a tree. Let

be a distinguished vertex of
G
and
o^{\prime}
a lift of
o
in
G_{q}
. Then there is a choice of
r
such that for every
\varepsilon>0
, we can choose
s>0
such that the following holds. For every

, there is a coupling such that if
\mathcal{C}^{p}_{G_{q}}(o^{\prime})
is infinite, then
\mathcal{C}^{p,s}_{G}(o)
is infinite.

Proposition 4.2

(Martineau-Severo)
.

Let

be an infinite transitive graph with

. Then for any choice of

, the following holds: for every
s\in(0,1]
, there exists
p_{c}(G,s)<p_{c}(G)
such that for every

, the cluster
\mathcal{C}^{p,s}_{G}(o)
is infinite with positive probability.

Proposition
4.2
is Proposition 4.2 from
[
MS19
]
. We now focus on the proof of Proposition
4.1
. But first, we want to show that these two propositions actually establish Theorem
1.2
.

Proof of Theorem
1.2
assuming Proposition
4.1
.

First set
\varepsilon=p_{c}(G)/2
and pick
s
and
r
according to Proposition
4.1
. By Proposition
4.2
, for
p>p_{c}(G,s)
the cluster
\mathcal{C}^{p,s}_{G}(o)
is infinite with positive probability. By Proposition

we know that there is a coupling such that
\mathcal{C}^{p}_{G_{q}}(o)
is infinite with positive probability meaning

. In particular,
p_{c}(G_{q})\leqslant p_{c}(G,s)
and we can conclude using
p_{c}(G,s)<p_{c}(G)
from Proposition

.
∎

Recall that a set of vertices

in

is

-dense
if any vertex can reach an element of

in at most

steps.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
G
be an infinite transitive graph that is not a tree.
Let
C=x_{0},\dots,x_{N}=x_{0}
be a cycle in
G
with no repeating vertex. Then there exists a sequence
C_{1},\dots,C_{k},\dots
of disjoint cycles and a partition
(P_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
of the vertices of
G
with
C_{i}\subset P_{i}
such that
C_{k}=\phi_{k}(C)
with
\phi_{k}\in\operatorname{Aut}(G)
, whose union is
R
-dense for some
R>0
. In addition, it is also possible to choose
(P_{i})
such that there exists
M>0
such that
|P_{k}|\leqslant M
for all

.

Proof.

\operatorname{Aut}(G)
acts on the set of cycles in the graph
G
and from the orbit of
C
, we can extract an infinite sequence of disjoint cycles that is maximal with respect to inclusion and set
A=\bigcup_{i\in\mathbb{N}}C_{i}
. If such a maximal set was not
R
-dense for any

, it would be possible to find in
G
balls of arbitrary radius that do not intersect

. In particular, one could find a ball which radius is bigger than the length of the cycle. This would go against the maximality of

as we could fit a new cycle isomorphic to

within this ball. We therefore know that such a set is
R
-dense for some
R
. We take the minimal
R
for this property.
To create a partition from this sequence, we assign each vertex to the cycle
C_{i}
it is closest to. When a vertex is at the same distance from different cycles, we arbitrarily decide it belongs to the same cell as one of its neighbors. We call
P_{i}
the set of vertices associated with
C_{i}
such that
V(G)=\bigcup_{i\in\mathbb{N}}P_{i}
. Note that
P_{i}\subset B_{R}(C_{i})
and
|B_{R}(C_{i})|=|B_{R}(C)|
so the cells have bounded cardinality. By construction, any vertex of
P_{i}
at distance

from
C_{i}
has at least one neighbor in
P_{i}
at distance

from
C_{i}
so
P_{i}
is connected since
C_{i}
is connected.
∎

The next lemma is the one that determine a suitable value of

depending on the geometry of the graph

.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
G
be an infinite transitive graph that is not a tree. Fix

and
(C_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
an

Remark 4.5
.

Note that by construction, an edge in

and

The lift of

is connected if and only if it is switching and we can calculate this probability

c=\sum_{\begin{array}[]{l}k=1\\
k\text{ odd}\end{array}}^{N}\binom{N}{k}q^{k}(1-q)^{N-k},

which is positive and independent of

. We conclude by pointing out that if

is connected, it is also the case for

. Let

.
∎

Remark 4.6
.

Following the same proof, we know that since the

Before proving Proposition

, we need one last lemma that builds a set of independent Bernoulli variables that assures us a vertex can reach its twin in a bounded number of steps.

Lemma 4.7
.

Let

be an infinite transitive graph that is not a tree. There exists

such that, on a larger probability space, there exist

and independent Bernoulli variables

with parameter

Proof.

Take
(C_{i})
and

that satisfy the conclusion of Lemma

is switching, then the

is connected with probability

at least

(\eta_{e})_{e\in E(G)}
. let us call

the independent Bernoulli variables of parameter

We then want to “distribute” the probability of connectivity to all vertices in

are independent Bernoulli variables with parameter

Reciprocally, by extending, if needed, our probability space we can suppose any Bernoulli variable

with parameter

We use this decomposition with the variables

are independent, identically distributed and if

and

is switching with

(generally, we can always decrease the parameter of a Bernoulli variable from

If the former is true, then by denoting

, we can lift the shortest path from

to

to

and

in at most

∎

Now that we have all the necessary lemmas, we can move on to the main proof.

4.2
Coupling Proposition

We prove Proposition
4.1
by constructing step by step a coupling between a percolation on
G_{q}
and an enhanced percolation on
G
. For completeness, we reproduce the totality of the argument from
[
MS19
]
, with some changes due to the fact that the distance between a vertex and one of its twin is not deterministically bounded by a constant
D
. In our case, this bound is only probabilistically true meaning during the construction of the enhancement, we need to take into account the variables
\beta_{x}
that track if a vertex can join its twin in less than
D
steps for a specific
D
. During the process, we alternate steps of pure percolation and steps of enhanced percolation. The difference from
[
MS19
]
is taken into account at steps of enhanced percolation.

Proof of Proposition
4.2
.

Let us take
(C_{i})
,
(P_{i})
,
R
and

that satisfy the conclusion of Lemmas
4.3
and
4.4
.
Let
M=|B_{r+2}(u)|+1
for some vertex

of

(because

is transitive, the choice of

does not matter). For any graph

, we define the multigraph
\hat{H}
, to have the same vertex set

and its edge set to be

where

is an edge connecting

and

. Percolation on

is done with parameter

so that percolation on
\hat{H}
with parameter

is equivalent to percolation on

with parameter

.

Let
\eta=(\eta_{e})_{e\in E(G)}
describe the switching state of edges in
G_{q}
. Setting a specific

allows us to specify the variables
\beta_{x}
described in Lemma
4.7
. Let us take
\omega
a configuration for percolation on
\hat{G_{q}}
with parameter
\hat{p}
.
We are going to describe an exploration of
\hat{G}
and

creating two other variables

and

that follow the law of augmented percolation on

. They will be coupled in such a way, that the infinite cardinality of the cluster of the origin in

with

will imply the infinite cardinality of the cluster of augmented percolation in

with
(\kappa,\alpha)
.

Edges will be explored in two possible ways:

-exploration and s-exploration.

-exploration corresponds to the usual exploration in Percolation Theory, and s-exploration means the edge has been used in an “Enhancement property”. Vertices may also be
s-
explored for the same reason. We say an edge is explored if it is either

-explored or

-explored. No edges will be both
p-
and

explored. The cluster of a vertex

in bond percolation (resp. augmented bond percolation) in the graph

will be written as
\mathcal{C}^{p}_{H}(o)
(resp.
\mathcal{C}^{p,s}_{H}(o)
).

Along the way, we will describe the sets

, that follows the law of
\mathcal{C}_{G}^{p,s}
, and

that is stochastically dominated by
\mathcal{C}_{G_{q}}^{p}(o)
.These sets will be defined as infinite unions of

and

, which will correspond to the vertices explored “at each step”. To increment the variable

by

, we will have to make a certain (potentially infinite) number of increments in

. From now on, we will refer to “step” as the set of action made in order to increment

by one. On odd steps, we will simply explore the connected clusters of some set of vertices, just like in usual percolation. On even steps, however, we will exclusively explore our graph using the special property of augmented percolation. We decide a well-ordering of the vertices and edges of
G
and
G_{q}
. In the following, by “pick an element

such that
P
”, we mean “take the smallest
a
such that
P
holds”.

Properties of the process:

A)

If an edge

in

is

-explored, then there is a lift

of

in

such that the set of the

-explored lifts of

is precisely

.

If an edge

in

is

C)

Every element of

is connected to

by an

For every edge

in

of

The map

.

Step 0 :
Initially, no edge is either

.

Step 2N+1:

.

5
Sharpness at

Our goal in this section is to prove Theorem

.
It states that the tail size of the cluster of any given vertex, decreases exponentially in the subcritical regime. To prove Theorem

, we closely follow Vaneuville’s strategy in

5.1
Random graphs and exploration

Exploring a graph in percolation means looking at the state of different edges one by one, usually only following the connected component of a specific vertex. One problem that arises when exploring random graphs, is that in order to make a decision on which edge to explore next, we need to know which edge are in our realization of the random graph. In this context, an exploration will need to make decisions based on the openness of edges but also on the partial graph structure discovered previously. We need to make a precise decision on what information to reveal when exploring our graph. We need to distinguish between exploring a vertex and exploring an edge. One consequence is that we may explore an edge without knowing one of its endpoint. Our proof strategy will need a crucial property (that is Lemma
5.10
) that in transitive deterministic graph, is related to the BK-inequality : If one explores parts of a transitive graph
G
then cuts this part of the graph, the result is a new graph where percolation is harder than in the original graph
G
. However, this property may not hold with percolation on random graphs, as exploring the cluster of a vertex also partially prescribe its geometry. Therefore, a particularly lucky geometry may favor percolation even if a part of the graph has been cut.

To account for this difficulty, we need to change our definition of a graph. Let
V
and
E
be two sets known as the vertex set and the edge set respectively. Let
P_{2}(V)
denote the set composed of subsets of
V
with
2
elements. The graph structure is embedded in a function
f:E\to P_{2}(V)
such that
f(e)=\{x,y\}
if the edge
e
links
x
and
y
. Likewise, we also make use of a function
g:V\to P(E)
such that
e\in g(x)
iff
x\in f(e)
. Given a function
f
, there is only one corresponding
g
function (but a given
g
function may not correspond to any
f
function). Therefore, we will focus only on the function
f
in our definitions. Note that whenever a function
f
is defined, we consider the function
g
to be implicitly defined as well. Note that this definition resembles Serre’s definition of a graph.

In the case of our random graphs, the randomness of the graph structure purely lies in the
f
and
g
functions, while the edge and vertex set are fixed. Therefore, a random graph is defined as a triplet
\mathcal{H}=(V,E,\nu)
where
\nu
is a probability measure on the set
\cal{F}
of functions from
E
to
P_{2}(V)
. A realization of the random graph
\mathcal{H}
is a certain function
f:\,E\to P_{2}(V)
. Throughout this section,

will always denote the measure that controls the graph structure of
G_{q}
but may refer to either the version with
\eta
variables or with the functions

.

Let us see how we adapt our new definition to the case of

constructed before from a transitive deterministic graph

. Let

and

as abstract objects such that

•

If

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}f(e_{0})=\{x_{0},y_{0}\},\,f(e_{1})=\{x_{1},y_{1}\}\\

•

If

Informally, if
