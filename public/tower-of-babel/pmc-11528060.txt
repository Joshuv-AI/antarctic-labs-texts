Innovative label-free lymphoma diagnosis using infrared spectroscopy and machine learning on tissue sections
Authors: Charlotte Delrue, Mattias Hofmans, Jo Van Dorpe, Malaïka Van der Linden, Zen Van Gaever, Tessa Kerre, Marijn M Speeckaert, Sander De Bruyne
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe diagnosis of lymphomas is challenging due to their diverse histological presentations and clinical manifestations. There is a need for inexpensive tools that require minimal expertise and are accessible for routine laboratories. Contrastingly, current conventional diagnostic methods are often found only in specialized environments. Attenuated total reflection-Fourier transform infrared (ATR-FTIR) spectroscopy offers a nondestructive and user-friendly approach in the analysis of a wide range of samples. In this paper, we determined whether the technique coupled with machine learning can detect and differentiate lymphoma within lymphoid tissue samples. Tissue sections from 295 individuals diagnosed with lymphoma and 389 individuals without the disease were analyzed using ATR-FTIR spectroscopy. The resulting spectral dataset was split using a 70:30 train-test split. Partial least Squares Discriminant Analysis (PLS-DA) models were trained to distinguish non-malignant lymphoid tissue from lymphoma samples and to differentiate between subtypes. On the training set (n = 478), significant spectral differences were mainly identified in the 1800–900 cm–1 region, attributed to fundamental biochemical constituents like proteins, lipids, carbohydrates, and nucleic acids. On the independent test set (n = 206), the trained PLS-DA model achieved a promising AUC of 0.882 (95% CI: 0.881–0.884) in the differentiation between lymphoma and non-malignant lymphoid tissue. In addition, comparative analyses revealed spectral distinctions and notable clustering between the different lymphoma subtypes. This study provides valuable insights into the application of ATR-FTIR spectroscopy and machine learning in the field of lymphoma diagnosis as a non-destructive, rapid and inexpensive tool with the potential to be easily implemented in non-specialized laboratories.Subject terms: Oncology, Lymphoma

Introduction

Lymphoma, a neoplasm characterized by clonal proliferation of lymphoid cells, comprises a diverse set of lymphoproliferative disorders with varying clinical outcomes and treatments. According to the World Health Organization’s (WHO) Classification of Hematolymphoid Tumors1, lymphomas are broadly categorized into two major groups: non-Hodgkin lymphoma (NHL), comprising families of mature B-cell neoplasms and mature T-/NK-cell neoplasm types, and Hodgkin lymphoma. It is noteworthy that NHL is ranked as the fifth to ninth most common cancer globally2,3. The diagnosis of NHL is challenging because of their diverse histological presentations and clinical manifestations at the time of diagnosis. Currently, techniques in cytopathology, immunology, and molecular biology detect aberrant lymphoma cells via morphological changes in tissue architecture and cellular shape, differences in cell-specific marker proteins using antibodies, or cytogenetic aberrations. Despite their established nature, these techniques each offer distinct advantages and limitations in terms of accuracy, cost, and time efficiency. Immunohistochemistry (IHC) markers provide high sensitivity and specificity, varying by marker and lymphoma type. IHC accuracy can be compromised by antibody specificity and staining protocol variability, leading to false positives or negatives. IHC costs include relatively inexpensive consumables like antibodies and staining kits ($50–$200 per antibody). The turnaround time for IHC is moderate, from hours to a few days. Molecular methods, including fluorescence in situ hybridization, next-generation sequencing (NGS), and polymerase chain reaction (PCR), offer extremely high sensitivity and specificity for detecting genetic and molecular abnormalities. Their accuracy depends on sample quality and preparation, and they require specialized knowledge for interpretation. Molecular methods have high initial setup expenses for equipment, especially NGS, with consumable costs ranging from $100–$500 per test. PCR results can be obtained within a few hours, while NGS requires days to weeks due to complex workflows and data analysis4–7. Consequently, there is a need for inexpensive tools that require minimal expertise, making them accessible for use in non-specialized laboratories.

Attenuated total reflection-Fourier transform infrared (ATR-FTIR) spectroscopy is sensitive to the telltale signs of cancer and fits all the characteristics for the development of an improved diagnostic tool. In ATR-FTIR spectroscopy, infrared (IR) light is directed through a crystal, undergoes internal reflection, leading to the creation of an evanescent wave entering the sample. This interaction between the evanescent wave and sample allows the identification and measurement of the biochemical constituents of the sample. ATR-FTIR is non-destructive, user-friendly, and can analyze various sample types8,9. To date, numerous pilot investigations for spectroscopic disease diagnostics have analyzed human tissues, suggesting the potential to distinguish between benign and malignant tumors, as well as healthy and diseased tissues. Studies on lesions in the breast, lungs, colon, and prostate have produced encouraging findings10–13. The present study explores the application of ATR-FTIR spectroscopy and machine learning on lymphoid tissue sections as an innovative analytical approach in the field of lymphoma diagnosis.

Materials and methods

Lymphoid tissue samples

The study was approved by the ethics committee of Ghent University Hospital (BC-11488) and is in accordance with the Helsinki Declaration. Informed consent was obtained from all participants involved in this study. All ethical regulations relevant to human research participants were followed. Lymphoid tissue samples (n = 684) were collected from the Department of Pathology of Ghent University Hospital, Belgium. The inclusion criteria were based on the CODAP code [organ (lymph node) and pathology (no tumor/lymphoma, including different types of lymphoma)], the timeframe (2016-2021), and the location (head and neck, thoracal, axillar, abdominal and inguinal) of the lymphoid tissue in the human body. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) stainings of the cases with lymphoma were revised by a pathologist (JVD) to ensure that the tissue was eligible for IR spectroscopy. Based on these criteria, 389 controls and 295 lymphoma tissue samples were included in the study. Lymphoma diagnoses were established through routine diagnostic approaches including cytomorphology, immunohistochemistry, flow cytometry, and genetics. The study encompassed the following lymphoma types: Burkitt lymphoma (n = 7), chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL, n = 45), diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL, n = 55), follicular lymphoma (n = 79), lymphoplasmacytic lymphoma (LPL, n = 3), mantle cell lymphoma (n = 13), marginal zone lymphoma (MZL, n = 12), NHL not further specified (n = 12); classical Hodgkin lymphoma consisting of nodular sclerosis Hodgkin lymphoma (n = 38), mixed cellularity Hodgkin lymphoma (n = 14) and Hodgkin lymphoma not further specified (n = 4). Nodular lymphocyte predominant B-cell lymphoma (formerly called nodular lymphocyte predominant Hodgkin lymphoma) was not included as a Hodgkin lymphoma according to the revised WHO guidelines1. Samples lacking a definitive diagnosis were excluded. The samples of non-malignant lymphoid tissue included normal (n = 336) and reactive lymphoid tissue (n = 53). The IR analysis was performed on residual formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded material received in the laboratory for routine analysis. The study samples were collected to represent a broad population of patients with different lymphoma subtypes, different ages, and varying locations of lymphoid tissue of the human body over a 5-year period. To mitigate the potential analytical bias associated with the year of sample collection and anatomical origin, samples from different years and body locations were randomized across the IR spectroscopy analysis batches. Sample preparation

All lymphoid tissues obtained through surgery were fixed with 10% neutral-buffered formalin for 6–48 h. Subsequently, lymphoid tissue samples were routinely processed using Tissue-Tek® VIP® (Sakura, Torrance, CA, USA) and embedded in paraffin. Two tissue sections were cut for each sample. First, a control tissue section of 2 µm was cut for H&E staining to determine the zone with the highest tumor percentage. Next, a sequential tissue section of 10 µm was cut and mounted on a Klinipath slide for FTIR analysis. For the normal samples, only one tissue section of 10 µm was cut for FTIR analysis. Next, all samples were incubated overnight at 37 °C and deparaffinized using Tissue-Tek Prisma® Plus and Tissue-Tek Film® according to a standard protocol1.

A total of 684 deparaffinized lymphoid tissue sections were examined using ATR-FTIR spectroscopy. FTIR spectra of all lymphoid tissue samples were obtained using a Perkin Elmer Spectrum Two ATR-FTIR spectrometer fitted with the universal ATR Accessory (ZnSe crystal of 2 × 2 mm) and Spectrum 10 software (Perkin Elmer, Waltham, MA, USA) in the range from 4000 to 400 cm–1 at a spectral resolution of 4 cm–1 (five coadded scans). Each tissue section was analyzed at three different locations and the mean spectrum was used for further data analysis.

Statistics and reproducibility

Statistical analyses were performed using Python (version 3.11.2) with the SciPy library (version 1.12.0). The Shapiro-Wilk test was used to assess data normality. Non-normally distributed data were presented as medians with interquartile ranges, whereas normally distributed data were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). The Mann–Whitney U test was used to analyze unpaired non-normally distributed data. An independent t-test was used to analyze unpaired normally distributed data. The chi-square test was used to assess associations between categorical variables. A two-sided p-value less than 0.05 was predetermined as the threshold for statistical significance.

Data preprocessing, analysis, and visualizations were performed using Python with the libraries Pandas (version 2.2.2), SciPy (version 1.12.0), Scikit-learn (version 1.4.2), NumPy (version 1.26.4), Seaborn (version 0.13.2) and Matplotlib (version 3.8.4). To remove irrelevant scatter light and standardize the spectroscopic signals, several spectral filters were employed. First of all, normalization was applied using a standard normal variate (SNV) method. Figure 1B shows the average SNV-treated absorbance spectra of lymphoma (red line) versus non-malignant lymphoid tissue (black line) on the training set samples. To enhance the resolution of overlapping peaks, spectra were converted to their first derivatives. Finally, a Savitzky–Golay filter with seven smoothing points was applied to help isolate important spectral characteristics that may be obscured by noise. The patients were randomly divided into training (70%) and test groups (30%). The test set spectra (n = 206) were predicted using partial least squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA) models that had been fine-tuned on the training set (n = 478) for the differentiation between lymphoma and non-malignant lymphoid tissue. Due to the limited number of samples, a train-test split was not conducted for the classification of the different lymphoma subtypes. Comparative analyses between different lymphoma subtypes were performed when sufficient samples were available (at least 40 per class). For training the model, the full spectral region of 4000–400 cm–1 was included. To robustly assess and improve the model’s performance, we employed leave-one-out cross-validation (LOOCV). Once the cross-validation results were deemed to be satisfactory, predictions were made on the test data to evaluate the model’s performance on unseen data. The resulting PLS-DA scores represent the distribution of samples in a multidimensional space defined by latent variables (i.e., components), which maximize the separation between predefined groups (lymphoma and non-malignant). To enhance clinical interpretability, the PLS-DA scores were converted to probability values using a sigmoid function. The optimal classification threshold (i.e., value providing the best tradeoff between sensitivity and specificity) was determined by performing a Youden index analysis on the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve generated by the LOOCV procedure. The performance of the models was evaluated using several performance metrics such as negative predictive value (NPV), positive predictive value (PPV), sensitivity, specificity, accuracy, and area under the ROC curve (AUC). Ninety-five percent confidence intervals (CI) were calculated using bootstrapping.

Results

Study samples

In this study, we compared spectroscopic signatures of 295 biopsies of individuals diagnosed with lymphoma with a control group of 389 individuals without lymphoma. The mean age at diagnosis for the lymphoma group was 64 ± 23 years, which was not significantly different from that of the control group (63 ± 18 years). However, there was a significant difference in the sex distribution between the groups. The lymphoma group comprised 176 males and 119 females, whereas the control group included 148 males and 241 females (both p < 0.0001). It is well-known, that the incidence of both Hodgkin lymphoma and NHL is higher in males compared to females across various age groups and geographic regions14.

Lymphoma versus non-malignant lymphoid tissue

During exploration of the preprocessed training data, significant alterations in peak intensities were observed between non-malignant lymphoid tissue and lymphoma in the region from 1800 to 900 cm–1, which corresponds to lipids (1735–1734 cm–1, 1709–1702 cm–1, 1471–1469 cm–1, 1466–1463 cm–1, 1460–1449 cm–1 and 1427–1423 cm–1), proteins (1679–1673 cm–1, 1643–1624 cm–1and 1589–1584 cm–1), nucleic acids (1234–1220 cm–1, 1210–1207 cm–1, 1173–1171 cm–1, 1168–1164 cm–1 and 1083–1082 cm–1), and carbohydrates (1071–1068 cm–1 and 1040–1031 cm–1, all p < 0.05, Supplementary Dataset 2). Significantly different peaks, attributed to protein absorption modes, were the most intense within the amide I region (1695–1661 cm–1). The shape of the amide I band is influenced by the secondary structure of the proteins. Specifically, peaks corresponding to α-helices, β-sheets, β-turns, and random coils were found at 1662–1645 cm–1, 1637–1613 cm–1, 1682–1662 cm–1, and 1645–1637 cm–1, respectively. The amide II band at 1595–1558 cm–1 is associated with C–N–H bending and C–N stretching in the peptide bonds. The peaks at 1478–1448 cm–1 are linked to CH2 and CH3 deformations, predominantly from lipids. Nucleic acids contribute to the IR spectra at 1237–1201 cm–1 (asymmetrical PO2–), 1176–1118 cm–1 (symmetrical PO2–), and 1096–1091 cm–1 (phosphodiester group in DNA and RNA). Peaks at 1741–1717 cm–1 (CH3 vibration) and 1478–1448 cm–1 (C=O bending) are indicative of lipids with long hydrocarbon chains. Furthermore, variations were also significant in the peaks related to saturated and unsaturated fats (3000–2800 cm–1, p < 0.05)9,15,16.

Visual examination of the IR absorption spectra of lymphoid tissues shows their complexity, characterized by a multitude of data points from various biomolecules, leading to overlapping bands. Consequently, it is crucial to integrate these vibrational spectroscopic measurements with univariate methods (such as peak assignment) and multivariate statistical analyses for an effective interpretation. For the PLS-DA model on the training data, a visible clustering between the two groups was established on the score plot of the first two out of five components of the model (Fig. 2A), resulting in an AUC of 0.974 (Fig. 2B). Evaluation of the PLS-DA model through cross-validation established its substantial efficacy in distinguishing normal lymphoid tissue from lymphoma, as evidenced by an AUC of 0.886 (95% CI: 0.885–0.887, Fig. 2C and Table 1). The Youden index analysis of the ROC curve revealed an optimal score value classification threshold of 0.450, which corresponds to a probability value of 61.0%.

Notable variations are also seen in the lipid and nucleic acid regions (900–1200 cm–1), particularly around 1150–1080 cm–1 associated with nucleic acids (phosphodiester bonds) and carbohydrates. These findings may reflect increased cellular proliferation and metabolic change

Clinical translation

As mentioned before, the original model (score threshold = 0.450, probability threshold = 61.0%) showed a sensitivity of 79.6% along with a specificity of 82.0%. To determine a probability threshold that could be useful for preliminary screening purposes in a routine clinical setting (i.e., high sensitivity in combination with an acceptable specificity), a sensitivity-tuned model was designed with a probability threshold of 58.0% (score = 0.322), resulting in an increased sensitivity of 96.8% (95% CI 96.7–96.9%) and decreased specificity of 50.4% (95% CI 50.2–50.7%). Additionally, a specificity-tuned model was also developed with a probability threshold of 65.0% (score = 0.619), resulting in a decreased sensitivity of 64.2% (95% CI 63.9–64.6%) along with a high specificity of 90.7% (95% CI 90.5–90.8%). Our proposal suggests that a model prediction below 58% probability indicates a high likelihood of accurately identifying the absence of malignant cells (blue dots, Fig. 5B). Meanwhile, when predictions exceed the 65% threshold, there is a strong confidence in the presence of malignancy within the lymphoid tissue (pink dots, Fig. 5B). Remarkably, all false positives above the 65% threshold were reactive lymphoid tissue samples with nine out of eleven samples being patients with a focus of cancer in the body (see Table S1 for more details on the sample characteristics). Samples with a probability value between 58 and 65% are then considered as a subset of cases that require further clinical investigation due to uncertainty of the model (grey dots, Fig. 5B).

Discussion

The present study unraveled significant biochemical differences between lymphoma and non-malignant lymphoid tissues. On the independent test set, our PLS-DA model achieved a promising AUC of 0.882, thereby highlighting the potential of ATR-FTIR spectroscopy as an innovative tool for lymphoma diagnosis. The technique is fast, inexpensive, and does not require reagents for analysis. Consequently, the financial commitment is limited to a one-time purchase of the equipment ($20,000–$25,000) and manual labor costs. The higher performance of the classification model on the training set (AUC 0.974) compared the cross-validation procedure (AUC 0.886) and test set (AUC 0.884) suggests a possible overfitting of the model on the training data. This discrepancy can most likely be addressed by further increasing the amount of training data. A potential approach is to generate synthetic spectra through data augmentation, which involves expanding the training dataset by creating altered versions of existing data points17–19. Implementing advanced augmentation techniques, like generative adversarial networks, can simulate a wide range of tissue characteristics and introduce additional noise, thereby enhancing the robustness and multi-center generalizability of the models17,18,20.

The reported differences in the IR spectra are primarily based on wavenumbers predominantly associated with proteins and nucleic acids. More specifically, within the MIR spectrum, the amide I and II bands spanning 1700–1500 cm–1 resonate with the peptide bonds of proteins, with subregions reflecting specific secondary structures21. Alterations in this spectral zone are indicative of dysregulated protein synthesis, which is frequently observed in lymphoma. These deviations can be attributed to the overexpression or structural modifications of proteins such as Bcl-2 in follicular lymphoma1. The spectral window of 1250–900 cm–1 encompasses vibrations from the phosphodiester linkages of nucleic acids and saccharides, offering insights into the heightened nucleic acid turnover and glycosylation patterns emblematic of lymphomas. Notably, wavenumbers within this range, such as 1000–900 cm–1 for the DNA/RNA sugar-phosphate backbone, 1120–1020 cm–1 for phosphate vibrations, and 1250–1150 cm–1 for ribose sugars, reflect cellular proliferation and metabolic alterations inherent to lymphoma9,15,16. Beyond these regions, the fingerprint region of 1500–900 cm–1 reveals C–C stretching vibrations indicative of DNA and RNA structures and C–O–C stretching in carbohydrates, potentially linked to the Warburg effect and altered lipid metabolism in cancerous cells9,15,16. The observed spectral differences could provide insights into the biochemical distinctions between cancerous and non-malignant lymphoid tissues. However, the spectral regions associated with lipids must be interpreted with caution due to the deparaffinization process in the pre-analytical phase.

In this study, several distinct lymphoma subtypes were included. Given that both pathological and non-cancerous tissues share biochemical constituents, identifying unique spectral markers for each lymphoma subtype is a rather complex endeavor. This complexity is further compounded by the inherent biological variability, not only across different lymphoma classifications but also within normal lymphoid tissues, introducing significant spectral variability. Moreover, the sample preparation process and the heterogeneous nature of lymphoma tissues, which often comprise a mixture of tumoral and normal cells, could challenge the acquisition of spectra that accurately represent the targeted cells.

Despite these challenges, comparative analysis between the different subtypes in our study revealed promising subtype-specific spectral differences. As an example, a clear distinction between aggressive DLBCL and indolent lymphoma such as CLL and follicular lymphoma could be found. Interestingly, clear spectral differences were found between Hodgkin lymphoma and reactive lymphoid tissue. Distinguishing Hodgkin lymphoma from reactive lymphoid tissue on cytomorphology can be challenging due to morphologic overlap between the two entities. In addition, spectral differences were found between mixed cellularity classical Hodgkin lymphoma and nodular sclerosis classical Hodgkin lymphoma. However, the distinction between Hodgkin lymphoma and DLBCL appeared to be less straightforward. This could suggest that there may be biochemical changes in the neighborhood associated with a factor of aggressiveness of the disease. At the molecular level, overlaps in genetic alterations and signaling pathways have been observed between these two types of lymphoma, suggesting common mechanisms of oncogenesis despite the distinct cellular origins and morphologies22,23. A possible solution may be the use of ATR-FTIR microspectroscopy to capture more precisely the IR spectra of some pathological cells. However, it has to be mentioned that the performance metrics derived from the PLS-DA subtype models should be interpreted with caution, as they are merely indicative due to the lack of sufficient samples to validate the results on an external test set.

In the evaluation of the sensitivity-tuned model designed to assist in the screening for lymphoma, only three cases were categorized as non-malignant, but actually represented a CLL, follicular lymphoma, and mantle cell lymphoma. Furthermore, for the specificity-tuned model, eleven non-malignant samples were classified as being malignant. All cases represented reactive lymphoid tissue samples and nine out of eleven cases had a focus of tumor in the body. The eleven cases originated from different locations in the body and were from another batch analysis. We hypothesize that lymphocytes may undergo biochemical or morphological changes when a tumor is present, changes that conventional histology does not reveal. In cancer, continuous exposure to antigens leads to T-cell dysfunction, manifesting as diminished proliferation, impaired effector functions, and heightened expression of various inhibitory receptors. Existing clinical practices may overlook nuanced variations in T-cell behavior, which may be detected by ATR-FTIR spectroscopy24–26. The findings of Cameron and colleagues27 bolster the evidence that there may be systemic reactions of the human body on the presence of neoplasm which may be detected by spectroscopic analysis.

Despite the promise of MIR spectroscopy in identifying the molecular hallmarks of lymphoma, its clinical adoption necessitates extensive validation, standardization of analytical protocols, and alignment with current diagnostic standards. The limitation of this study lies in its retrospective study design. To advance the application of IR spectroscopy in lymphoma diagnosis, future studies should strategically expand the diversity and number of lymphoma samples to refine the predictive accuracy of the model. Emphasizing longitudinal research to monitor disease progression and integrating spectroscopic data with genetic and clinical profiles will enrich diagnostic insights. Furthermore, executing multicenter trials will validate the efficacy of the approach across various demographics, ensuring its adaptability, and robustness in clinical settings.

In this study, we utilized a range of well-established diagnostic methodologies, including cytomorphological examination, immunohistochemical analysis, flow cytometric evaluation, and genetic profiling for comparative purposes. However, future advancements in the field may incorporate deep learning algorithms to further enhance diagnostic precision17,18. For instance, it is conceivable that a synergistic approach combining deep learning on H&E stained sections for histopathological image analysis with our IR spectroscopy method could further augment diagnostic accuracy. Notwithstanding its potential, deep learning has not yet achieved widespread adoption among clinicians due to several constraints. Challenges such as variability in specimen preparation protocols, the requirement for extensive annotated datasets for model training, and the risk of overfitting must be addressed. Furthermore, the technique necessitates rigorous validation against established diagnostic criteria and current clinical practices before it can be fully integrated into routine diagnostic workflows.

Altogether, it can be concluded that lymphoma can be detected and differentiated in an easy and fast way through the application of ATR-FTIR spectroscopy coupled with machine learning on tissue sections. Large-scale investigations on lymphoma diagnostics are imperative to establish its definite position as a potential new tool for lymphoma diagnosis compared with currently employed techniques.
