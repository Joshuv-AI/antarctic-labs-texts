Channel Choice and Customer Value
Shirsho Biswas, University of Washington

Abstract
The rapid growth of digital shopping channels has prompted many traditional retailers to invest in e-commerce websites and mobile apps. While prior literature shows that multichannel customers tend to be more valuable, it overlooks how the reason for adopting a new channel may shape post-adoption behavior. Using transaction-level data from a major Brazilian pet supplies retailer, we examine how adoption of online shopping—by previously offline-only customers—affects post-adoption spend, profitability, and channel usage. We study four distinct adoption pathways: organic adoption, adoption due to the COVID-19 pandemic, Black Friday promotions, and a newly launched loyalty program. We find that although all adopter groups increase their spending relative to offline-only customers, post-adoption behavior differs based on the reason for adoption. COVID-19 adopters behave similarly to organic adopters in terms of spending, but show greater offline stickiness consistent with consumer inertia and habit theory, yielding higher profits due to higher offline margins. In contrast, promotion-driven adopters spend less post-adoption due to forward buying and exhibit lower profitability. Our findings caution against treating all multichannel customers as equal and highlight the importance of incorporating behavioral theories into forecasting and targeting strategies. Firms should account for adoption motives when evaluating channel and promotional investments.

Retailing, Multichannel, Online Shopping, Customer value

Introduction

Online shopping has been growing in popularity over the last two decades, with the proliferation of computers and smartphones. In response, many grocery and retail stores like
Walmart, Target, Costco, etc., which earlier only operated physical retail stores, now sell their products through both physical stores and e-commerce channels such as websites and mobile apps. Given the size of the retail industry and the growth of digital channels (especially the acceleration of the adoption of online shopping during the COVID-19 pandemic), quantifying the effect of the adoption of digital shopping channels is important for managers and firms
(
Goyal 2023
;
Bloomberg 2020
)
.

In this paper, we consider two related research questions. First, we examine whether and how the adoption of digital shopping channels (e-commerce website and/or mobile app) of a retailer affects the purchase behavior of consumers who were previously purely offline-only (brick-and-mortar) shoppers. We seek to document the impact of the adoption of online shopping on three metrics of interest for managers: (1) Spend, (2) Profitability, and (3) Stickiness to the offline channels post-adoption. The first metric, spend, quantifies the total revenue/sales (across offline and online channels) generated by the customer after the adoption of online shopping, the second metric quantifies the profits (sales minus cost) generated by the customer, and the third metric denotes the extent to which consumers use offline channels even after adopting online shopping. Second, we are interested in understanding whether and how post-adoption shopping behavior differs depending on consumers’ different reasons for adopting online shopping. This is important from a managerial perspective – if the changes to consumers’ purchase behavior (post-online adoption) are largely driven by the motivations/reasons for consumers choosing to adopt online shopping, then it will be challenging to extrapolate the behavior of organic adopters (who adopt online shopping without any external incentives) to other consumers who are incentivized to adopt by marketing campaigns or other external economic or environmental factors i.e., different motivations compared to
organic adopters
. Furthermore, if the differences in post-adoption behavior relative to
organic adopters
depend on the
type
of external factor (i.e., macroeconomic or environmental vs. firm-driven promotions); forecasting post-adoption behavior would be even more complicated. On the other hand, if the consumers who adopt online shopping due to different reasons behave similarly post-adoption, then managers can be more confident in extrapolating the behavior of those who adopt organically to a broader set of adopters (and be less concerned about the reason for the adoption of online shopping).

While a large stream of prior research suggests that multichannel consumers (defined as consumers who buy from multiple channels including catalogs, mobile apps, stores, websites, etc.) tend to spend more than single-channel consumers
(
Neslin and Shankar 2009
)
, it is not obvious if
the earlier results translate directly to our setting for three reasons. We briefly discuss the reasons here and refer the reader to Table
1
for a more detailed comparison to previous literature. First, they do not specifically study the effect of the adoption of online shopping from previously only purchasing from brick-and-mortar stores. Studying the physical store to online (e-commerce or
mobile app) transition is both novel and interesting because many major retailers started out with brick-and-mortar stores, and only over the last couple of decades have been investing more heavily in developing the e-commerce and mobile app channels. E-commerce websites and mobile apps,
as channels, are potentially quite different from other channels like mail order catalogs, phone shopping, or interacting with salespeople because both these channels reduce search costs, and it is easy for customers using these channels to visit other retailers’ websites and apps to comparison shop across retailers. Second, the vast majority of these papers adopt one of two approaches – (a) a cross-sectional analysis
(
Kumar and Venkatesan 2005
;
Thomas and Sullivan 2005
;
Kushwaha and Shankar 2013
)
comparing the spend of single-channel shoppers (any single channel, not necessarily just physical stores) vs. multichannel shoppers (which can include physical stores and online channels, but also mail-order catalogs, ordering through phone, salespeople, etc.), or, (b) a within-customer analysis
(
Venkatesan and Ravishanker 2007
;
Ansari, Mela, and Neslin 2008
)
comparing the pre- and post-adoption periods of customers who start using multiple channels from being single channel only (where again, these channels include websites, catalogs, physical stores etc.). However, cross-sectional comparisons cannot answer the question of how customer behavior
changes
after the adoption of multiple channels, and within-customer analyses require the strong assumption that the entire difference between the before and after periods can be fully attributed to the adoption of multiple channels, without any other time-varying factors. More recently, some studies have used Difference-In-Differences (DID) analyses to study the effects of the adoption of mobile apps
(
Narang and Shankar 2019
;
Wang, Malthouse, and Krishnamurthi 2015
)
. However, before the adoption of the app, customers in these studies could have already been using the retailer’s website to shop online. As such, their findings may not generalize to our context, where the transition in focus is from physical stores to online channels. Third, the previous studies only examine customers who have voluntarily/organically chosen to use multiple channels for shopping or have chosen to adopt the firm’s mobile app. They completely ignore different channel adoption pathways and implicitly assume that the benefits of multichannel customers are uniform regardless of why they chose to adopt multiple channels or a particular channel. There are a couple of important theoretical reasons to expect differences between (a) organic adopters and externally incentivised adopters and (b) different types of external incentives i.e. macro-environmental shocks vs. firm driven promotions. Consumer inertia and habit theory
(
Verplanken and Wood 2006
;
Wood and Neal 2009
)
would predict that adoption driven by external shocks (e.g., macro shocks or firm-driven promotions) may lead to temporary disruptions in consumer behavior (i.e. adopting a new channels and using multiple channels to shop), but without reinforcement, consumers may revert to prior routines (i.e. not use the newly adopted channels much). Next, for promotion-induced adoption in particular, the theory of forward buying and demand distortion may apply. Promotions incentivizing online adoption may also induce forward buying, leading to reduced post-promotion consumption
(
Neslin, Henderson, and Quelch 1985
;
Ailawadi and Neslin 1998
)
, in turn leading to lower revenues for the firm post-adoption. Thus, we extend the previous literature by (a) studying a particularly important type of transition from single-channel to multi-channel (i.e. offline-only to offline-plus-online) and (b) by examining the behavior or consumers who adopted online shopping due to different reasons, and challenging the assumption that all multichannel customers are uniformly beneficial and behave the same after adopting a new channel regardless of their reason for adoption.

We use data from a large pet supplies retailer in Brazil with a large network of brick-and-mortar stores and an online presence (website and mobile app) to answer our research questions. Our data consists of all consumer purchases across all channels for the period of 2019-2024. This time period allows us to construct four groups of adopters of online shopping who previously only shopped at the firm’s physical stores – (1)

covid adopters
who started shopping online during the onset of COVID-19, (2)
Black Friday adopters
who adopted online shopping due to deep discounts available online during Black Friday, (3)
Loyalty Program
adopters
who adopted online shopping during the launch of a loyalty program which had to be activated
on the retailer’s mobile app, and (4)
organic adopters
who started shopping online fully of their own
volition, without any external stimuli. In addition, we also consider a base control group of non-adopters or
offline-only customers
(consumers who did not adopt online shopping during our observation period). We then construct consumer-level monthly panel data to quantify the revenue, profitability, and share of spend in the offline and online channels. Since we have four groups of adopters who chose to adopt for different reasons, in addition to non-adopters, we can address both our research
questions – quantify the impact of the adoption of online shopping on consumers’ spend, profitability, and
stickiness to offline channels, as well as comment on whether and how the different reasons for adoption affects the post-adoption behavior of adopters.

We now discuss our main findings. First, we find that relative to
offline-only customers
, adopters of online shopping, have higher spend and profitablility in the post-adoption period. However, the post-adoption behaviors of the different types of adopters diverge significantly from each other across the three key dimensions: total spend, profitability and the share of offline/online spending. While
covid adopters
spend similarly relative to
organic adopters
post-adoption, both promotion-driven adopters,
Black Friday adopters
and
Loyalty Program adopters
, spend less. This reduced spend is driven largely by forward buying during the promotional period, meaning they stocked up and then reduced future purchases, which doesn’t occur in the case of
covid adopters
. In terms of the share of offline/online spend post-adoption, consistent with consumer inertia and habit theory, all three adopter groups motivated by external stimuli (
covid adopters
,
Black Friday adopters
and
Loyalty Program adopters
) have a lower online spend share post-adoption relative to
organic adopters
. A combination of demographic characteristics and consumer inertia and habit theory also explain differences in how this share evolves over time. We find that
covid adopters
show a slower growth in online spend share over time. This is mainly driven by the fact that
covid adopters
are older and thus are more set in their habits.
Black Friday adopters
on the other hand, are younger and show a faster growth rate in the online spend share relative to
organic adopters
, although they still take time to catch up to the same levels. There is no difference in trends for the online spend share between
Loyalty Program adopters
and
organic adopters
. This may be due to the fact that the two adopter groups are quite similar in terms of demographics, and also the fact that the Loyalty Program involves monthly online activation, and therefore there is some reinforcement for the habit of online shopping for Loyalty Program adopters. The post-adoption profits for the different adopter groups are a function of both spend and the share of offline/online share. We find that despite similar spend,
covid adopters
are more profitable post-adoption, than
organic adopters
in our setting. This is because of their higher spend share in the offline channel, where profit margins are higher in our setting.
In contrast,
Black Friday adopters
and
Loyalty Program adopters
are less profitable due to their lower overall spending in the post-adoption period.

Our findings suggest that the reason for a consumer’s decision to adopt online shopping significantly shapes their long-term behavior. Consumers who adopt online shopping organically behave very differently from those motivated by external events or firm-led promotions, due to a combination of their demographics, consumer inertia, and forward buying during promotions. Our findings have important managerial implications. For example, had the retailer assumed that
Black Friday adopters
would behave like
organic adopters
, it would have significantly underestimated the payback period for discounts offered during the promotion. While projections based on
organic adopters
would suggest a break-even point at 13 months, the actual break-even occurred only after 39 months. Even when overall spending appears similar—as in the case of
covid adopters
—differences in channel usage (online vs. offline) can have substantial implications for profitability. Firms should avoid using
organic adopters
as the benchmark when predicting the behavior of customers adopting a new channel for other reasons. More broadly, our empirical results indicate that managers can use their knowledge of expected customer demographics, and apply the theories of consumer inertia and forward buying to forecast spend, profit and channel usage outcomes in other channel adoption scenarios. Tailoring strategies to the unique characteristics of each type of adopter group is essential for maximizing both customer value and long-term profitability.

In summary, our paper makes both substantive and managerial contributions to the literature on channel choice and consumer value. From a substantive perspective, we show that the adoption of online shopping from only using physical stores, either voluntarily or prompted by external factors like a macro-environmental shock or firm-driven promotions, causes consumers to increase their total spend with the firm relative to consumers who stay offline-only. More interestingly, we show that consumers who adopt online shopping due to different reasons, have different post-adoption outcomes in terms of their spend, channel usage and profits, and these differences can be explained using demographics, consumer inertia theory, and forward buying during promotions. From a managerial perspective, we not only quantify the impact of the adoption of online shopping on revenues and profits but also provide a cautionary tale against naively extrapolating the profitability of one group of online shopping adopters to a broader/different set of adopters. We also provide theoretical explanations of why post-adoption behaviors of different adopter groups differ, which managers can use to forecast outcomes in other settings.

Prior Empirical Literature on Multichannel Shopping in Retail Settings

There is a large empirical literature in marketing studying the effects of multichannel shopping vs. single-channel shopping. We provide a brief overview of the most closely related papers here in Table
1
and discuss how our paper differs from and contributes to this literature in the rest of this section. We refer readers to
Neslin et al. 2006
;
Neslin and Shankar 2009
;
Verhoef 2012
for more comprehensive reviews of the broader multichannel literature.

As shown in Table
1
, our paper differs from the earlier literature both substantively and methodologically. First, from a substantive perspective, we study a specific type of single-channel to multichannel transition that has not been studied before – from shopping at physical stores only to shopping both at physical stores and online through the retailer’s website/mobile app. Earlier findings from the multichannel literature largely focus on the transition from any single channel to some combination of multiple channels (including physical stores, catalogs, phones, mail orders, salespeople, mobile apps, and websites).

Studying the physical store to e-commerce transition is of particular interest to traditional brick-and-mortar retailers
(
Goyal 2023
;
Bloomberg 2020
)
like Walmart, Target, and Costco since these channels (websites and mobile apps) represent a key area of growth for them. However, since the earlier literature does not explicitly study this transition (and rather considers a broader set of channels), those findings may not generalize to this context. Indeed, there are some important differences among these different channels. For instance, websites and apps provide easy search and comparison between products within a retailer and also between competing retailers because it is easy to jump between retailer websites/apps on your device. This is not the case for other channels that have been studied before, like catalogs, salespersons, or phone shopping. Also, in many cases, prices on a retailer’s website or mobile app are lower than the prices in physical stores, which is not the case for other channels. Thus, the findings from other channels may not fully translate to the channels we study here.

Moreover, the setting of our study is a fast-moving consumer packaged goods retailer (pet supplies), whereas much of the earlier literature uses different settings like books, apparel, video games, electronics, technology, etc. These earlier settings differ from ours on many dimensions, e.g., they tend to be less frequently purchased, do not have as many competing stores, and the nature of the product is more hedonic (rather than utilitarian), which has been found to be important for the multichannel effect
(
Kushwaha and Shankar 2013
)
.

Most importantly, the existing literature overlooks the heterogeneity in channel adoption pathways, implicitly assuming that the benefits derived from multichannel customers are consistent, irrespective of the motivations underlying their adoption decisions. However, theoretical frameworks suggest important distinctions between (a)
organic adopters
and those induced through external incentives, and (b) different types of external stimuli—namely, macro-environmental shocks versus firm-initiated promotions. Consumer inertia and habit theory
(
Verplanken and Wood 2006
;
Wood and Neal 2009
)
suggests that externally triggered adoption events—such as those prompted by environmental disruptions or marketing campaigns—may temporarily disrupt existing behavioral routines, resulting in short-term channel experimentation. Without reinforcement, however, such consumers are likely to revert to prior habits, thereby potentially limiting the long-term impact of multi-channel adoption. Moreover, when adoption is driven by promotions, the literature on forward buying and demand distortion
(
Neslin, Henderson, and Quelch 1985
;
Ailawadi and Neslin 1998
)
raises concerns about post-promotion gains for firms. Specifically, promotion-induced online channel adoption may lead to intertemporal demand shifts rather than incremental sales, reducing post-promotion revenues and potentially diminishing the value of these new multichannel users.

Thus, this paper extends the literature from a substantive perspective in two key ways: First, we focus on a key type of channel transition: when customers move from shopping only in offline stores to using both offline and online channels. Second, we examine how post-adoption behavior differs depending on why consumers started using the online channel— whether organically or due to external factors like promotions or macro-environmental shocks, and provide theoretical explanations for the differences. By doing so, we challenge the implicit assumption in prior research that all multichannel customers behave similarly post-adoption and are equally valuable to firms, regardless of how or why they adopted a new channel

Cross-sectional:
Papers in this stream
(

;
Kumar and Venkatesan 2005
;

;

typically compare the revenue/profits of two distinct groups of consumers to document the value of multichannel shopping – those who use multiple channels (among catalog, brick-and-mortar, salespersons, direct mail, telephone sales, SMS or the internet) vs. those who use a single channel.
A consensus finding in this literature is that multichannel customers tend to be more valuable than single-channel customers. However, a potential drawback of this methodology is that multichannel consumers are self-selected and could be systematically different from those using single channels. As such, cross-sectional comparisons cannot answer the question of how customer behavior
changes

Within Customer:
Papers in this stream
(
Venkatesan and Ravishanker 2007
;
Ansari, Mela, and Neslin 2008
)
typically compare how a customer’s purchase behavior changes after the adoption of multiple channels (which could be different types of stores, catalogs, internet etc.) compared to their behavior when they were a single-channel customer. The key takeaway here is that customers spend more
after

First, without an appropriate control group of non-adopters, we need to assume that the entire difference between the before and after periods can be fully attributed to the adoption of multiple channels, and there are no other time-varying factors affecting purchasing behavior – which is a strong assumption. Second, it is not obvious if the estimated gains of the adopters can be extrapolated to non-adopters because the adopters self-selected into the use of multiple channels.

Setting and Data

3.1
Setting

Our data come from one of the largest Brazilian pet supplies retailers, which sells a wide variety of products for pets, such as pet food and snacks, medicines/pharmacy items, hygiene and grooming products, and various pet toys and accessories. The firm has both offline and online channels. The retailer operates more than 200 physical stores in Brazil, which serve as the offline channel, and has a website as well as a mobile app, which serve as the online channel. Pet supplies is a large and competitive industry with about $250 billion in annual revenue globally
(
Fortune 2024
)
.

3.2
Data

Our data include transaction-level records of all individual customers from both online and offline channels during the 63-month period from January 1st, 2019 to March 31st, 2024. Each transaction record is uniquely identified by an order number with one or more purchased items. For each purchased item, we have information on the unit price, quantity purchased, brand, purchased channel (online/offline), subcategory, and category (e.g., food, hygiene).

The firm tracks customer purchases using a National Identification Number (similar to a Social Security Number), which is unique to each individual in Brazil. Customers who purchase online (either through the website or the app) must be logged in to their profile, and therefore, all online purchases can be traced back to the National ID. Customers are also asked for their National ID when making an in-store purchase; however, this is not mandatory. Discussions with the retailer revealed that while most offline transactions are also linked with a customer profile, about a quarter of in-store transactions are not linked to a National ID. This number has remained fairly steady during the entire observation period and was not affected by COVID-19 or other marketing events
3
3

3

Because the number of offline transactions not linked to customer IDs remained similar through our observation period and exhibited no significant shifts in response to any of the events that spurred online adoption (including COVID-19), it is unlikely that this issue systematically biases our findings on the adoption of online shopping
.

A potential concern with any channel analysis is that the results could be driven by assortment differences across the two channels. To ensure that this is not the case, we exclude data from four product categories that were not available in both channels for the entirety of the observation period; see Table
A1
in Web Appendix
\lx@sectionsign
A
for a summary of these categories and their availability information. Together, sales from these categories account for only 0.63% of the total sales during the observation period; as such we do not expect these to play any significant role in the findings.

The total number of consumers who shopped at least once during our sample period is 5,934,248
4
4

4

. For each of these consumers, we aggregate the transaction-level data into customer-month-level panel data over the customer’s entire purchase history within the observation period (January 2019 to March 2024), starting from his or her initial purchase month and ending with the last observed purchase month. For example, if a customer purchased for the first time in our data in August 2019 and the last observed purchase is in July 2020, then the panel for this customer spans 12 months, from August 2019 to July 2020. If there are intermediate months in which a customer did not make any purchase, the values for the monthly purchase-related variables are set to zero.

4
Descriptive Analysis

As a first step, we conduct some descriptive analyses to examine (a) cross-sectional differences in demographics, monthly spending, and other purchase behaviors of
multichannel consumers
,
online-only consumers
, and
offline-only consumers
; and (b) within-customer changes to monthly spending and other purchase behaviors after adopting online shopping. We highlight the main takeaways from each analysis here and refer readers to Web Appendix

First, we observe that
multichannel consumers
are more likely to be female compared to customers who are online-only or offline-only. Of the three groups,
online-only consumers
are the youngest, and
offline-only consumers
are the oldest.
Multichannel consumers
have the highest income, and
offline-only consumers
have the lowest income. Next, we run simple cross-sectional regressions for the outcome variables, spend, quantity, unique items, brands and categories, on the explanatory variable of consumer-type (online-only, offline-only and multichannel), controlling for demographics and month fixed effects, to measure cross-sectional differences in spend and other purchase behaviors. We find that

spend more (and also purchase higher quantities, more unique items, brands, and categories) relative to both offline-only and online-only consumers. These patterns align with previous literature

;

;
Kushwaha and Shankar 2013
)
documenting that multichannel consumers are more valuable than
single-channel consumers
(though the set of channels studied in that literature is different).

Next, we conduct a within-consumer analysis to examine whether consumers who were initially only shopping offline increase their spending after adopting online shopping. We do see a significant increase in monthly spending (by about 39%) post-adoption
5

5

While these descriptive analyses confirm existing studies, they nevertheless have several shortcomings. The cross-sectional analysis does not measure changes in behavior, while the within-customer analysis assumes that the before-after changes we observe are entirely attributable to the adoption of online shopping (since there is no control group of non-adopters). Moreover, these analyses do not shed light on whether the different reasons for the adoption of online shopping affect consumers’ purchase behavior in the post-adoption period differently. We address these issues and adopt a more comprehensive empirical framework to answer our research questions in the next two sections.

5
Firm’s Problem and Sample Construction

We start by formalizing the firm’s problem. As discussed in
\lx@sectionsign
1
, our first goal is to quantify how the adoption of digital shopping channels (e-commerce website and/or mobile app) affects the purchase behavior of consumers who were previously purely offline-only (brick-and-mortar) shoppers in retail markets. Specifically, we seek to document the impact of the adoption of online shopping on the following three metrics: (1) Total Spend, (2) Profitability, and (3) Stickiness to the offline channels post-adoption. Formally, we are interested in the Average Treatment Effect on Treated (ATT) of adopting online shopping on these three metrics. A second goal is to understand whether the different reasons for adopting online shopping affect consumers’ post-adoption purchase behavior, and to what extent. To that end, we consider four types of reasons behind online adoption: (1) a macro-environmental shock (COVID-19), (2) a short-term promotion (Black Friday sales), (3) a long-term loyalty program, and (4) organic adoption, not driven by any external events. The first event is outside the firm’s control, whereas the latter two were firm-driven marketing activities. For each event, we first compare consumers who adopted online shopping
adopters
with a control group of
offline-only
consumers to study the effect of online adoption. Next, we compare consumers who adopted online shopping due to the event (i.e.,
event adopters
) with those who adopted organically (without external incentives) around the same time frame as the event (i.e.,
organic adopters
) to understand how these different external events affect post-adoption behavior relative to organic adoption. In the process, we also seek to provide theoretical underpinnings for how and why the different reasons for online adoption affect post-adoption purchase behavior differently.

We first discuss the sample construction for the three focal events in
\lx@sectionsign
5.1
and then present some summary statistics of the three samples in
\lx@sectionsign
5.2
.

5.1
Sample Construction

For each event, we first define three customer cohorts –
event adopters
,
organic adopters
, and
offline-only customers
. We then establish the pre-adoption, adoption, and post-adoption periods for each focal event. Specifically, for a given event:

\bullet

The pre-adoption period is defined as the common period during which all three groups of customers purchase only through physical stores (i.e., before the onset of the focal event).

\bullet

The adoption period is defined as the time during which adopters started purchasing online. Because each event has two sets of adopters, we have two adoption periods:

\bullet

Adoption period for
event adopters
: This is the period during which the focal event took place, and induced customers to adopt online shopping. For instance, in the case of COVID-19, this is the first month and a half since the onset of COVID-19. Similarly, for the Black Friday event, the adoption period for event adopters is simply the day of the Black Friday sale.

\bullet

Adoption period for
organic adopters
: Since
organic adopters
serve as a control group for
event adopters
, for each event, we choose consumers who organically adopted online shopping just before the onset of the focal event, and this period varies based on the event.

\bullet

The post-adoption period refers to the months
after
the adoption periods of both
event adopters
and
organic adopters
.

See Table
5
for an overview of these periods for each of the three events. In our estimation sample for each event, we only include data from the pre- and post-adoption periods for that event. We exclude the adoption periods for both event and organic adopters from the estimation sample because consumers are in flux during that time (where some adopters have adopted while others have not). Furthermore, by excluding these observations, we avoid capturing any abnormal purchase behaviors associated with the initial adoption of online shopping and the onset of specific events (i.e., COVID-19, Black Friday, Loyalty Program). As such, it allows us to cleanly identify the post-online-adoption difference in customers’ purchase behaviors
(
Gu and Kannan 2021
)
. We now clarify a few points on the choice of these three periods for each event below.

\bullet

COVID-19 event: We use one year of data before the adoption period as the pre-adoption period (the calendar year of 2019). For post-adoption period, we use all the data from May 2020 till April 2023

6.1
Model

To estimate the impact of adopting online shopping on post-adoption purchase behavior, we use the difference-in-differences approach. Unlike our descriptive analyses, this approach allows us to account for unobserved heterogeneity across customers and time-specific shocks. For each event, we use customer-month-level panel data for the estimation sample corresponding to that event, and we utilize the customer-month observations from the pre- and post-adoption periods. We exclude the adoption periods from the estimation for the reasons discussed in
\lx@sectionsign

.

7
Mechanisms, Theoretical Underpinnings and Managerial Implications

We now discuss the mechanisms and theoretical underpinnings behind our findings on spend and profit; if and how managers can translate these findings to other settings, and some general takeaways from our analysis.

7.1
Share of offline/online spend and the theory of Consumer Inertia

One of our interesting findings from
\lx@sectionsign

even though their overall spending is similar.
We now examine if this is due to differences in the post-adoption share of offline/online spending between different groups of adopters in more detail. We also discuss the theoretical underpinnings behind these differences and derive implications for managers based on theory and our empirical findings.

Consumer inertia and habit theory
(

7.3
Managerial Implications

Our main findings indicate that consumers who adopt online shopping due to different reasons (organic, macro environmental shocks, or firm-driven promotional activities) behave differently in the post-adoption period. We have discussed above that our findings are explained and supported by the theories of consumer inertia and forward buying, and we believe that our main takeaways can be abstracted to other similar situations where these same theoretical concepts may apply. An important and practically useful managerial implication is that firms should not naively use the post-adoption behavior of
organic adopters
as a proxy to forecast the behavior of consumers who adopt online shopping for other reasons such as environmental or macroeconomic shocks, or firm-run marketing campaigns, and we highlight this implication for managers evaluating promotional campaigns to increase adoption of online shopping with some numbers from our empirical context.

To assess whether to run a promotional campaign (like the Black Friday online discounts) to induce customers to adopt online shopping, firms have to forecast the expected benefits in terms of incremental sales from consumers who choose to adopt due to the promotion, and compare the benefits to the costs of running the campaign. If managers naively use organic adopters’ incremental profit as a proxy for adopters resulting from the promotional campaign (
Black Friday adopters
, in this case), they could grossly overestimate the expected benefits. This in turn would lead them to wrongly compute metrics like the time taken to break-even from such a promotional campaign. To illustrate this point, we use the Black Friday promotional campaign as an example and show some back-of-the-envelope calculations.

First, we quantify the cost of running the Black Friday promotion, by computing the difference between actual consumer spending under promotional prices on Black Friday and what consumers would have paid for the same quantities at regular prices. We find the average discount across categories is around 28%, with dry food categories discounted around 20% and hygiene categories around 33%. The average promotional cost of Black Friday sales is 299.18 MCU per customer for that day. Then, from our DID results, we obtain the average monthly incremental profit of
organic adopters
(23.57 MCU) and
Black Friday adopters
(7.81 MCU)
25
25

25

in Web Appendix
\lx@sectionsign
E.4
.
. By using the behavior of
organic adopters
to predict the behavior of
Black Friday adopters
, managers would predict that the incremental profits from
Black Friday adopters
would result in a break-even duration of 13 months post-adoption (i.e. 299.18 / 23.57). However, our analysis shows that it should actually take at least 39 months (e.g., 299.18 / 7.81) to break even, nearly three times longer than the managers’ initial forecast. Therefore, managers should appropriately calibrate their expectations of increased consumer spending when running promotions that encourage the adoption of online shopping.

9
Conclusions

In conclusion, this paper examines the impact of the adoption of online shopping, i.e., shopping on a retailer’s
e-commerce website and/or mobile app, on customers who previously only shopped at physical
stores, using data from a large pet supplies retailer in Brazil spanning the period of 2019-2024. We
study four groups of consumers who adopted online shopping for different reasons during our data period: (a)
covid
adopters
who started shopping online during the onset of COVID-19, (b)
Black Friday adopters
who adopted online shopping due to deep discounts available online during Black Friday, (c)
Loyalty Program adopters
who adopted online shopping during the launch of a loyalty program which had to be activated on the retailer’s mobile app, and (d)
organic adopters
who started shopping online fully of their own volition, without any external stimuli.
We find that for all four types of adopters of online shopping, there is an increase in spend with
the retailer post-adoption, relative to consumers who stayed offline-only.

However, there are important differences between the four groups of adopters in terms of their post-adoption behavior along three dimensions - spend, profitability and share of offline/online spend. Compared to
organic adopters
,
covid adopters
, who adopted online shopping due to a macro-environmental shock, spend similarly in the post-adoption period. However,
Black Friday adopters
and
Loyalty Program adopters
, who adopted online shopping due to firm-driven promotional activities, spend
less
. We show that this is explained by the theory of forward buying during promotions -
Black Friday adopters
and
Loyalty Program adopters
increase their spending during the promotion, (when they adopt online shopping) by pulling forward their future demand, resulting in a “post-promotion dip” in their demand. In terms of channel usage post-adoption, consistent with consumer inertia and habit theory, we find that all three groups of externally driven adopters have a lower share of online spend post-adoption relative to
organic adopters
. Further, we find that
covid adopters
also have a slower growth in the share of online spending relative to
organic adopters
. This is mainly driven by the fact that
covid adopters
are older on average, compared to
organic adopters
, and thus are more set in their habits. In contrast, the younger
Black Friday adopters
show a faster rate of growth for online spend share relative to
organic adopters
.
Loyalty Program adopters
do not differ significantly from
organic adopters
in terms of the growth in the share of online spending, probably because their demographics are quite similar and the fact that consumers have to activate the loyalty program online every month. Finally, we find that
covid adopters
are more profitable than
organic adopters
due to their higher share of offline spending, where margins are higher in our setting.
Black Friday adopters
and
Loyalty Program adopters
are less profitable relative to
organic adopters
, due to their lower spending.

Taken together, our findings indicate that consumers who adopt online shopping due to different reasons (organic, macro environmental shocks or different types of firm driven promotional activities) behave differently post-adoption, and the differences can be explained using consumer inertia and habit theory, the theory of forward buying during promotions, and the differences in demographics between these groups of consumers. Managers can use their knowledge of consumer demographics, and the theories of consumer inertia and promotional forward buying to predict spend, profit and channel usage outcomes in other situations where these same theories apply.

An important managerial implication is that firms should not naively use the behavior of
organic adopters
of a new channel as a proxy to forecast the behavior of
consumers who adopt the new channel for other reasons such as environmental or macroeconomic shocks, or
firm-run marketing campaigns. As an example, we find that the retailer only breaks-even on running the Black Friday promotion to induce more customers to adopt online shopping, after 39 months, instead of the expected 13 months it would have predicted based on the post-adoption behavior of
organic adopters
.
Further, even if post-adoption spending is similar (like in the case of
covid adopters
), differences in the usage of online channels vs. offline could cause significant differences in profitability.

Finally, we note that our study has limitations that serve as avenues for future research. First, as highlighted above, the demographics of the different types of adopters are an important input along with the theories of consumer inertia and forward buying, to predict post-adoption behavior. The external stimuli for online adoption in our setting are limited to the COVID-19 pandemic, and two specific promotional campaigns run by our retailer. Future research could investigate whether other types of events (e.g. store closures or other types of promotions or macro-shocks) lead to adopters with different demographics (and therefore potentially different interactions with these behavioral forces) than what we see in our setting. Second, we focus on one retail category (pet supplies), and it is unclear if consumers in other categories and contexts, with other forces at play, show similar behavior. For instance, recent research in the non-retail and more competitive hotel industry has found that adopters of a hotel chain’s mobile app decrease their spending at the focal hotel because they tend to also adopt competitors’ apps at the same time
(
Gu and Kannan 2021
)
. Thus, future research that studies these questions in a wider set of categories and contexts can further enhance our understanding of the consequences of consumer migration to online channels.

, and
YearMonth_{t}
is a time period fixed effect, as before. Table
A6
presents the results from this regression for Spend as the outcome variable. Model (1) shows the results without the customer and year-month fixed effects and model (2) shows the results with the fixed effects. We see that, on average, customers spend 124.2 MCU more after they adopt online shopping. Note that this result is significantly smaller than the 180.2 MCU from the previous cross-sectional analysis. These results are consistent with earlier work (studying different channel transitions) that shows that consumers who transition to shopping on multiple channels from previously using only a single channel tend to spend more
(
Venkatesan, Kumar, and Ravishanker 2007
;
Ansari, Mela, and Neslin 2008
)
post multichannel adoption.

We also perform a similar analysis on other metrics of interest (Quantity, No. of orders, Unique items, Brands, Categories, and Subcategories) and find that all of them show a positive increase after the adoption of online shopping (see Table
A7
). However, as with spend, the magnitude of these effects is much smaller than what we saw in the cross-sectional analysis.

However, while the within-consumer analysis allows us to study changes in behavior for the same consumers, there is still a strong assumption that the changes we observe between the before and after periods are entirely attributable to the adoption of online shopping, and there are no other time varying factors in play causing some of the changes in behavior we observe. As such, without including an appropriate control group of non-adopters, the effect size here could still be an inflated measure of the incremental value from converting an offline-only consumer to a multichannel consumer who also uses online channels. Moreover, these analyses do not shed light on whether the different reasons for the adoption of online shopping have any significant impact on a consumer’s purchase behavior in the post-adoption period.

C.4
Loyalty Program Control Variables

Below, we provide the definition of time-varying control variables related to customers’ participation in the loyalty program. These variables (
\boldsymbol{x}_{it}
) are included in the DID and event study analysis for the loyalty program study.

\bullet

isActivated_{it}
: 1 if customer i activates the offer during month
t
and 0 otherwise. Use to compare within-activators, how much spend differs between months with/without activation.

\bullet

isFirstActivation_{it}
: A binary variable equal to 1 if customer
i
activates the offer for the first time in month
t
, and 0 otherwise. This variable isolates the novelty (first-time) in the month of initial activation, relative to subsequent activation months.

\bullet

Months\_Since\_First\_Activation_{it}
: A non-negative integer indicating the number of months elapsed since customer
i
’s first activation of the offer, as of month
t
; equals 0 for months prior to the first activation. For example, if the first activation occurs in August 2023, then

Months\_Since\_First\_Activation_{i,September\_2023}=1
, and so on.

\bullet

\%Month\_WithActivation_{it}
: The proportion of months in which customer
i
has activated the offer up to (but not including) month
t
, calculated as the total number of activation months up to
t
divided by the number of months since first activation (excluding
t
itself). For the first month of activation, this variable is defined as 0 (since the denominator is 0); all first-month effects are captured by
isFirstActivation_{it}
. Starting from the second month post-activation, this variable reflects the historical activation rate prior to month
t
.

Appendix D
Appendix to the Profitability Analysis: Missing Value Imputation

As discussed in the main text, we have data on prices, margins, and costs for each item sold since January 2020; however, we lack cost data for 2019 due to issues with the data provider’s upgrade to a new data management platform in 2020. Thus, we need to impute the missing costs and margins for the 2019 data. We do this in two steps.

Step 1:
We impute the cost of each item in 2019 via an inflation-adjusting approach by using the unit cost and the inflation data. Based on conversations with our retail collaborator, we learned that procurement contracts are typically updated on an annual basis and that unit cost increases for the majority of items are largely due to inflation (or in sync with inflation rates). Hence, we calculate the average cost for each item
j
in each channel
c
(i.e., online and offline) and each year (i.e., 2020, 2021, and 2022), denoted as
cost_{jc,2020},cost_{jc,2021}
,
cost_{jc,2022}
.
33
33

33

We focus on cost value over a 36-month period between 2020 and 2022. We do not consider the 2023 cost value as the basis for cost imputation, as we do not have complete cost data for the full year of 2023 and cannot compute the yearly average cost.

We choose Brazil’s Extended National Consumer Price Index (IPCA) in June (the midpoint of the year) as the average level of IPCA for each year, and obtain a set of unit cost estimates for each item
j
in channel
c
, denoted as
s_{jc}
, by adjusting the ratio of IPCA index in Equation
A3
.
34
34

34

We obtain Brazil’s Broad National Consumer Index (IPCA) from the Brazil Institute of Geography and Statistics (IBGE). The IPCA data is available under the Historic Serie section of the following URL:https://www.ibge.gov.br/en/statistics/economic/prices-and-costs.

s_{jc}=\{cost_{jc,2020}\times\frac{ICPA_{2019}}{ICPA_{2020}},cost_{jc,2021}\times\frac{ICPA_{2019}}{ICPA_{2021}},cost_{jc,2022}\times\frac{ICPA_{2019}}{ICPA_{2022}}\}

(A3)

We can then take the average of the elements of set
s_{jc}
for each item-channel combination and calculate the imputed cost of item
j
in channel
c
in 2019, as
cost_{jc,2019}
.

Step 2:
Since we observe the price, we can then calculate the margin for each item-channel combination in 2019 as

margin_{jc}=price_{jc}\times(1-AvgTaxRate)-cost_{jc,2019}.

(A4)

Here, we impute missing margin using an average sales tax rate of 16% for imputing the missing margin. This average sales tax rate, 16% (or one minus average sales tax rate) is obtained by calculating the retailer’s total monthly
margin
plus
cost
divided by total monthly
sales
over a 36-month period between 2020-01-01 and 2022-12-31. This resulting 16% closely matches Brazil’s average sales tax rate of 17%
Trading Economics 2025
, and our retail collaborator has confirmed that this method is appropriate for imputing missing margin. Finally, we aggregate these margins at the customer-month level to get the customer-month panel data for the profitability measures in 2019.

F.2
Honest DID

In
F.1
, we see that when comparing
adopters
and
offline-only
customers, there are some violations of parallel trend assumption. There are also some concerns about the parallel trend assumption between
covid adopters
and
organic adopters
. To ensure that estimated ATTs of online adoption are robust to the violations in the parallel trends assumption, we perform the Honest Difference-in-Differences (Honest DID) estimation to quantify the extent to which violations in parallel trends can nullify the treatment effect
(
Rambachan and Roth 2023
)
.

To conduct this sensitivity analysis, we impose the restriction that the change in slope of the differential trend between treatment and control customers across two periods is bounded by:

\Delta^{SD}:=\{\zeta:|(\zeta_{t+1}-\zeta_{t})-(\zeta_{t}-\zeta_{t-1})|\leq M,~\forall t\}

(A6)

Here,
\zeta_{t}

covid adopters
with
organic adopters
,
\mathbbm{I}\{Treatment\}_{i}=1
if customer
i
is a
covid adopter
.
t
is the year-month for the pre-adoption period, and

is the linear trend coefficient for event
e
variable
v
. We test a wide range of
M

F.3
Appendix to DID Models with Propensity Score Matching and Inverse Propensity Score Weighting

In this section, we provide a set of robustness check under PSM and IPTW. To conduct this analysis, we label the
event adopters
as 1 and
organic adopters
as 0 and model the propensity score of customer
i
being a COVID adopter. The propensity score model is specified as follows:

PropensityScore_{i}=f(X_{i}^{T}\theta),

(A8)

where
f(\cdot)
is the logit link function where
f(X_{i}^{T}\theta)=\frac{1}{1+\exp(-X_{i}^{T}\theta)}
.
X_{i}\in\mathcal{R}^{M\times 1}
is a column vector with
M
covariates of customer
i
. These covariates include
\textit{Gender}_{i},\textit{Age}_{i},\textit{Tenure(Days)}_{i}
,
\textit{AverageSpendPerMonth}_{i}
, etc.

\theta\in\mathcal{R}^{M\times 1}
is a column vector of coefficients. Table
A39
in Web Appendix
F.3.1
presents the propensity score model estimation for covid, black friday and loyalty program analysis, respectively. Figure
A20
presents the distribution of propensity scores before matching, and we observe some differences between event adopters and organic adopters. After matching, Figure
A21
suggests that event adopters and organic adopters become more overlapped.

Next, we use these propensity scores to augment our DID anaysis in two ways.

\bullet

First, we perform a customer-level propensity score matching and then re-run the DID analysis. Matching based on propensity score is much more efficient than matching based on a set of covariates
(
Rosenbaum and Rubin 1985
)
. Based on the predicted propensity score of each customer
i
, we use
MatchIt
package in R
(
Stuart et al. 2011
)
to perform nearest-neighbor matching for each event adopter. We then run the DID analysis on the newly matched sample (see Table
A41
,
A42
and
A43
in Web Appendix
F.3.2
). We find that the results from this exercise are consistent with the main findings.

\bullet

Next, we use a slightly different approach to control for any potential selection issues. Specifically, we use an inverse propensity of treatment weight-adjusted (IPTW) DID regression. Here, the sample is the same as the one from the main analysis, but each observation is inversely weighted by its propensity score. We then perform a two-sample t-test on all the pre-treatment variables based on the weighted sample and confirm that the two samples are statistically insignificant on all the variables (see Table
A40
for the weighted sample of
organic adopters
and
event adopters
, respectively in the Web Appendix
F.3.3
). The DID results of
event adopters
and
organic adopters
from this analysis are shown in Table
A44
,
A45
and
A46
for covid, Black Friday, and Loyalty Program analysis, respectively in Web Appendix
F.3.3
. Again, we find that the results and conclusions are quite similar to those in the main section.
