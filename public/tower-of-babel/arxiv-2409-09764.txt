Abstract
. Let X o := V ⁡ ( f ¯ p ¯ ) ⊂ ( 𝕜 N , o ) X_{o}:=V({\underline{f}}_{\underline{p}})\subset(\mathbbm{k}^{N},o) be a weighted-homogeneous complete intersection germ (with arbitrary singularities, possibly non-reduced). Let { γ s ¯ } s ¯ ∈ 𝕊 \{\gamma_{\underline{s}}\}_{{\underline{s}}\in\mathbb{S}} be the corresponding foliation of 𝕜 N \mathbbm{k}^{N} by weighted-homogeneous real arcs. Take a deformation by higher order terms, X ϵ := V ⁡ ( f ¯ p ¯ + ϵ ⋅ f ¯ > p ¯ ) . X_{\epsilon}:=V({\underline{f}}_{\underline{p}}+\epsilon\cdot{\underline{f}}_{>{\underline{p}}}). Does the foliation { γ s ¯ } s ¯ \{\gamma_{\underline{s}}\}_{\underline{s}} deform compatibly with X ϵ ​ ? X_{\epsilon}? We identify the “obstruction locus”, Σ ⊂ X o , \Sigma\subset X_{o}, outside of which such a deformation does exist, and possesses exceptionally nice properties. 1.
Introduction

Let
\mathbbm{k}=\mathbb{R},\mathbb{C}.
Fix some integral positive weights of coordinates in
\mathbbm{k}^{N},
i.e.
\omega_{1}\leq\omega_{2}\leq\cdots\leq\omega_{N},

\omega_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}.

Take a regular sequence
{\underline{f}}_{\underline{p}}=(f_{p_{1}},\dots,f_{p_{c}})
of weighted-homogeneous polynomials, of
weights
{\underline{p}}=(p_{1},\dots,p_{c}),
where
p_{i}=ord_{\omega}(f_{p_{i}}).

These polynomials define the
weighted-homogeneous (complete intersection) germ
X_{o}:=V({\underline{f}}_{\underline{p}})\subset(\mathbbm{k}^{N},o).

The germ
X_{o}
can have arbitrary singularity, can be non-reduced.

Take the weighted homogeneous arcs,
\gamma_{\underline{s}}(t)=(s_{1}t^{\omega_{1}},\dots,s_{N}t^{\omega_{N}}).
Here
t\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
and
{\underline{s}}\in\mathbb{S}\subset\mathbbm{k}^{N},
the unit sphere. These arcs foliate the space
\mathbbm{k}^{N},
and this foliation is compatible with the germ
X_{o}\subset(\mathbbm{k}^{N},o).

This foliation is the first basic tool in the study of Geometry and Topology of such germs.

1.1.

Perturb
X_{o}
by higher-order terms,
X_{\epsilon}:=V({\underline{f}}_{\underline{p}}+\epsilon\cdot{\underline{f}}_{>{\underline{p}}})\subset(\mathbbm{k}^{N},o),
here
f_{>p_{i}}\in\mathbbm{k}\{x\}
and
ord_{\omega}f_{p_{i}}<ord_{\omega}f_{>p_{i}}.

One would like to deform the foliation
\{\gamma_{\underline{s}}\}_{\underline{s}}
to a foliation
\{\gamma_{\epsilon,{\underline{s}}}\}_{\underline{s}}
of
(\mathbbm{k}^{N},o),
compatibly with
X_{\epsilon}.

This is not always possible, see Example
2.1
. Such a deformation is in general obstructed by the locus
Sing(X_{o})
and by the singularities of the intersection of
X_{o}
with the flag of coordinate planes,
\mathbbm{k}^{N}\supset V(x_{1})\supset\cdots\supset V(x_{1},\dots,x_{N}).
Altogether they give “obstruction locus”
\Sigma\subset X_{o}\subset(\mathbbm{k}^{N},o)
, defined in §
2.1
.

Lemma
2.3
tells (roughly):
outside of a ‘hornic’ neighborhood of this obstruction locus,
\mathcal{U}(\Sigma)\subset(\mathbbm{k}^{N},o),

the foliation
\{\gamma_{\underline{s}}\}_{\underline{s}}
deforms to a foliation
\{\gamma_{\epsilon,{\underline{s}}}\}_{\underline{s}},
compatibly with the family
X_{\epsilon},
preserving the (non)tangency of the arcs, and with various other controlled properties.

In particular, if
\Sigma=\{o\},
then
\{\gamma_{\underline{s}}\}_{\underline{s}}
deforms to a foliation
\{\gamma_{\epsilon,{\underline{s}}}\}_{\underline{s}}
on the whole
(\mathbbm{k}^{N},o).

This construction is purely algebraic, no vector field integration is involved. It is based on the implicit function theorem of Bourbaki-Tougeron.
The functions
\gamma_{\epsilon,{\underline{s}}}(t)
are
\mathbb{R}
-analytic (in
\epsilon,{\underline{s}},t
). If the perturbations
{\underline{f}}_{>{\underline{p}}}
are Nash (e.g. polynomials), then
\gamma_{\epsilon,{\underline{s}}}(t)
are Nash functions.

The deformed “semi-weighted homogeneous” foliation
\{\!\gamma_{\epsilon,{\underline{s}}}\!\}_{\underline{s}}
is as useful for
X_{\epsilon},
as the weighted homogeneous foliation
\{\!\gamma_{\underline{s}}\!\}_{\underline{s}}
is for
X_{o}.

1.2.

A related question is the trivialization of the family
X_{\epsilon}\!:=\!V({\underline{f}}_{\underline{p}}\!\!+\!\epsilon\!\cdot\!{\underline{f}}_{>{\underline{p}}}).
Suppose the (real or complex) singular set of
X_{o}
is just one point. Then the family of germs
X_{\epsilon}
is embedded topologically trivial, while the family of functions

{\underline{f}}_{\underline{p}}\!\!+\!\epsilon\!\cdot\!{\underline{f}}_{>{\underline{p}}}
is contact topologically trivial.
Various stronger trivializations were obtained in the last 70 years (see example
3.2
for details).

​​
\bullet

The contact-Lipschitz/
C^{k}
trivializations were given (under additional assumptions) in
[
Ruas-Saia.97
]
,
[
Fernandes-Ruas.04
]
,
[
Costa-Saia-Soares.12
]
.
The construction went by integrating Lipschitz/
C^{k}
vector fields. The resulting trivialization was not subanalytic/semialgebraic.

​​
\bullet

The family
f_{p}+\epsilon\cdot f_{>p}
(where the weighted-homogeneous polynomial
f_{p}
has an isolated critical point) was right-trivialized in
[
Fukui.Paunescu.00
]

by a homeomorphism whose strict transform under a weighted-homogeneous blowup is real-analytic.
(The homogeneous case was done in
[
Kuo.80
]
.)
That trivialization does not seem to have controllable Lipschitz/
C^{1}
-properties.

The deformed foliation
\gamma_{\epsilon,{\underline{s}}}
of §
1.1
helps to trivialize the family
{\underline{f}}_{\underline{p}}\!+\!\epsilon\!\cdot\!{\underline{f}}_{>{\underline{p}}}
in a very special way.

Theorem
3.1
(roughly):
If
\Sigma=\{o\}
then the family
{\underline{f}}_{\underline{p}}\!+\!\epsilon\!\cdot\!{\underline{f}}_{>{\underline{p}}}
is contact-trivializable by a homeomorphism that is
\mathbb{R}
-analytic on
(\mathbbm{k}^{N},o)\setminus o,
differentiable at
o,
becomes globally
\mathbb{R}
-analytic in the weighted-polar coordinates, and has controlled Lipschitz/
C^{1}
-properties.

Moreover, if the perturbations
{\underline{f}}_{>{\underline{p}}}
are Nash functions (e.g. polynomials), then the trivialization is Nash on
(\mathbbm{k}^{N},o)\setminus o
and becomes globally Nash in the weighted-polar coordinates.

Remark 1.1
.

​​​
i.​

All the previous trivializations were obtained by vector field integration. This left no hope for Nash trivialization.

Recall that analyticity in weighted-polar coordinates is a much stronger property than subanalyticity.

​​​
ii.​

We can allow perturbations of the same order,
ord_{\omega}f_{p_{i}}\leq ord_{\omega}f_{\geq p_{i}},
but then one must assume
|\epsilon|\ll 1,
and not all the properties of Lemma
2.3
, Theorem
3.1
hold. See Remark
2.5
and Corollary
3.3
.

​​​
iii.​

While in this paper we work only with
\mathbbm{k}=\mathbb{R},\mathbb{C},
such trivialization/deformation questions are important over arbitrary fields, of any characteristic,
[
B.G.K.22
]
. An advantage of our method is: if
{\underline{f}}_{\underline{p}},{\underline{f}}_{>{\underline{p}}}
are defined over a field
{\tilde{\mathbbm{k}}},
then the deformed foliation (of Lemma
2.3
) is defined over the same
{\tilde{\mathbbm{k}}}.
(And similarly for the trivialization of Theorem
3.1
.)

Having such a trivialization of the family
X_{\epsilon}
(resp. the deformed foliation
\gamma_{\epsilon,{\underline{s}}}
) is extremely useful for the study of semi-weighted homogeneous germs, Newton-non-degenerate germs, and other “perturbations of weighted-homogeneous germs”.
In
[
Kerner-Mendes.23
]
we apply this deformed foliation to detect fast vanishing cycles on perturbations of weighted-homogeneous germs. More generally, this trivialization allows to extend several recent results of Lipschitz Geometry of Singularities from weighted-homogeneous germs to broader classes of germs.

1.4.
Notations and conventions

By germs we mean their small representatives.

​​
i.​

Take the standard sphere
\mathbb{S}:=\{x|\ \|x\|=1\}\subset\mathbbm{k}^{N}.
It is
S^{N-1}\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
or
S^{2N-1}\subset\mathbb{C}^{N}.

We use multi-indices,
{\underline{x}}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{N})\in\mathbbm{k}^{N},

{\underline{s}}=(s_{1},\dots,s_{N})\subset\mathbb{S},

{\underline{\omega}}=(\omega_{1},\dots,\omega_{N})\in\mathbb{Z}^{N}_{>0},

{\underline{p}}=(p_{1},\dots,p_{c}),

{\underline{f}}_{\underline{p}}=(f_{p_{1}},\dots,f_{p_{c}}).

Accordingly we abbreviate:

(1)

t^{{\underline{\omega}}}\cdot{\underline{s}}\!:=\!(t^{\omega_{1}}s_{1},\dots,t^{\omega_{N}}s_{N}),\ \ \ t^{{\underline{p}}}\cdot{\underline{f}}_{\underline{p}}\!:=\!(t^{p_{1}}f_{p_{1}},\dots,t^{p_{c}}f_{p_{c}}),\ \ \ t^{-{\underline{p}}}\cdot{\underline{f}}_{>{\underline{p}}}\!:=\!(t^{-p_{1}}f_{\!>p_{1}},\dots,t^{-p_{c}}f_{\!>p_{c}}).

We always assume:
gcd(\omega_{1},\dots,\omega_{N})=1.

Occasionally we pass to the weighted polar coordinates,
\mathbb{S}\times\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\ \lower 0.86108pt\hbox{$\mathrel{\mathop{\to}\limits^{\sigma}}$}\ \mathbbm{k}^{N},

({\underline{s}},t)\to t^{\underline{\omega}}\cdot{\underline{s}}.

For an analytic subgerm
\Sigma\subset(\mathbbm{k}^{N},o)
we get “the strict transform”,
\tilde{\Sigma}=\overline{\sigma^{-1}(\Sigma\setminus o)}\subset\mathbb{S}\times\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}.

We get also “the link at
o
”,
Link_{o}[\Sigma]:=\tilde{\Sigma}\cap\{t=o\}.

​​
ii.​

All our arcs are real-analytic.

A germ
X
is called “foliated by a set of arcs
\{\gamma_{\underline{s}}\}_{\underline{s}}
”, if there exists a representative of
X
that is covered by those arcs, and moreover (for this representative):
\gamma_{\underline{s}}\cap\gamma_{{\underline{s}}^{\prime}}=o
for
{\underline{s}}\neq{\underline{s}}^{\prime}.

The tangency order of arcs
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}\subset(k^{N},o)
is
tord_{(k^{N},o)}(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}):=ord_{t}\|\gamma_{1}(t)-\gamma_{2}(t)\|.

Here we take the length-parametrization, thus
\gamma(t)\!=\!(s_{1}\cdot t\!+\!o(t),\dots,s_{N}\cdot t\!+\!o(t)),
with
{\underline{s}}\!\in\!\mathbb{S}.

​​
iii.​

Take a weighted-homogeneous complete intersection
X_{o}:=V({\underline{f}}_{\underline{p}})\subset(\mathbbm{k}^{N},o),
here

{\underline{f}}_{\underline{p}}=(f_{p_{1}},\dots,f_{p_{c}})
is a regular sequence of polynomials,
and
ord_{\omega}f_{p_{i}}=p_{i},
i.e.
f_{p_{i}}(t^{\omega_{1}}x_{1},\dots,t^{\omega_{N}}x_{N})=t^{p_{i}}\cdot f_{p_{i}}(x_{1},\dots,x_{N}).
W.l.o.g. we assume
({\underline{f}}_{\underline{p}})\subseteq({\underline{x}})^{2}\subset\mathbbm{k}[{\underline{x}}],
thus
X_{o}
is singular. The germ
X_{o}
can be non-reduced.

The singular locus
Sing(X_{o})
is defined (inside
X_{o}
) by the determinantal ideal of the Jacobian matrix
I_{c}[f^{\prime}_{1},\dots,f^{\prime}_{c}].
Here
\{f^{\prime}_{j}\}
are the column-gradients. Thus
Sing(X_{o})
is a weighted-homogeneous
\mathbbm{k}
-algebraic subgerm.
The dimension
dim_{\mathbbm{k}}Sing(X_{o})
is taken set-theoretically. For
\mathbbm{k}\!=\!\mathbb{C}
this dimension can be computed also via the height of the defining ideal.

2.
Deforming the weighted-homogeneous foliation
\{\gamma_{\underline{s}}\}_{\underline{s}}

The arcs

\gamma_{\underline{s}}(t)=t^{\underline{\omega}}\cdot{\underline{s}}:=(t^{\omega_{1}}s_{1},\dots,t^{\omega_{N}}s_{N})
with
{\underline{s}}\in\mathbb{S},

form the weighted-homogeneous foliation of
\mathbbm{k}^{N}.
It is compatible with
X_{o}\subset\mathbbm{k}^{N},
i.e. either
\gamma_{s}\subset X_{o}
or
\gamma_{s}\cap X_{o}=\{o\}.

Take a deformation by higher order terms
X_{\epsilon}:=V({\underline{f}}_{{\underline{p}}}+\epsilon\cdot{\underline{f}}_{>{\underline{p}}})\subset(\mathbbm{k}^{N},o),
for
\epsilon\in Disc\subset\mathbbm{k}^{1},
i.e.
|\epsilon|\leq 1.

Here
ord_{\omega}(f_{p_{i}})<ord_{\omega}(f_{>p_{i}}).
This deformation is flat, as
X_{o}
is a complete intersection.
This family is not equisingular in whichever sense, as we did not assume that
X_{o}
has an isolated singularity.

One would like to deform the foliation
\{\gamma_{\underline{s}}\}
to a foliation
\{\gamma_{\epsilon,{\underline{s}}}\}
of
(\mathbbm{k}^{N},o)
(by terms of higher weights),
compatibly with
X_{\epsilon},
i.e.:
\gamma_{\underline{s}}\subset X_{o}
if and only if
\gamma_{\epsilon,{\underline{s}}}\subset X_{\epsilon}.

The minimal condition to impose is “each arc is tangent to its deformation”, i.e.:

(2)

tord_{(\mathbbm{k}^{N},o)}(\gamma_{{\underline{s}}},\gamma_{\epsilon,{\underline{s}}})>1\text{ for each }{\underline{s}}\in Link[X_{o}]=\mathbb{S}\cap X_{o}\text{ and }\epsilon\in Disc.

For
\mathbbm{k}\!=\!\mathbb{C}
such a deformation exists, e.g., if
X_{o}
has an isolated singularity, and the family
\{X_{\epsilon}\}_{\epsilon}
is equi-resolvable by a weighted blowup, and with additional assumptions on the exceptional divisor.
Just the
\mu\!=\!const
assumption on family
\{X_{\epsilon}\}_{\epsilon}
is not sufficient, due to examples of Briançon-Speder-type.

Example 2.1
.

Let
f_{\epsilon}(x,y,z)=x^{p_{x}}+z^{p_{z}}+xy^{p_{y}}+\epsilon\cdot y^{p_{y}+1},
with the weights
(\omega_{x},\omega_{y},\omega_{z})=(\frac{1}{p_{x}},\frac{1-\frac{1}{p_{x}}}{p_{y}},\frac{1}{p_{z}}).
(Up to
\mathbb{Z}
-scaling.)
Thus
V(f_{o})\subset(\mathbb{C}^{3},o)
has a weighted-homogeneous isolated singularity.
The perturbation
\epsilon\cdot y^{p_{y}+1}
is of weight
>ord_{\omega}f_{o}
if and only if
\frac{p_{y}+1}{p_{y}}(1-\frac{1}{p_{x}})>1,
i.e.
p_{x}>p_{y}+1.

In this case
f_{\epsilon}
is a
\mu=const
family. Take the arc
\gamma_{o}(t)=(0,t,0)\subset V(f_{o}).

Its deformation by higher order terms (to ensure
tord(\gamma_{o},\gamma_{\epsilon})>1
) can be presented as
\gamma_{\epsilon}(t)=(t^{d_{x}}(\dots),t,t^{d_{z}}(\dots)),
for some
d_{x},d_{z}>1.
The condition
\gamma_{\epsilon}\subset V(f_{\epsilon})
is then:

t^{p_{x}d_{x}}(\dots)+t^{p_{z}d_{z}}(\dots)+t^{d_{x}+p_{y}}(\dots)+\epsilon t^{p_{y}+1}=0.

Assuming
p_{z}>p_{y}+1
we have:
p_{x}d_{x},p_{z}d_{z},d_{x}+p_{y}>p_{y}+1.
And this contradicts the condition
f_{\epsilon}(\gamma_{\epsilon}(t))=0.

In this example “the problematic arc”
\gamma_{o}
lies inside the intersection
X_{o}\cap V(x,z).
In fact,
X_{o}\cap V(x,z)\subset\mathbbm{k}^{3}
is
a line, while the expected dimension of this intersection is zero.
We show that such intersections of unexpected dimension, or with unexpected singularities, are
the only
obstructions to deforming the foliation
\{\gamma_{\underline{s}}\}_{\underline{s}}
to
\{\gamma_{\epsilon,{\underline{s}}}\}_{\underline{s}}.

2.1.
The obstruction locus
\Sigma

Some of the weights
\omega_{\bullet}
might coincide, thus we split them:

(3)

\omega_{1}=\cdots=\omega_{r_{1}}<\omega_{r_{1}+1}=\cdots=\omega_{r_{2}}<\cdots<\omega_{r_{k}+1}=\cdots=\omega_{N}.

Intersect
X_{o}
by the coordinate planes
V(x_{1},\dots,x_{r_{j}})\subset(\mathbbm{k}^{N},o)
and take the union of the corresponding singular loci (see §
1.4
):

(4)

\Sigma\!:=\!Sing[X_{o}]\!\cup\!Sing[X_{o}\!\cap\!V(x_{1},\dots,x_{r_{1}})]\!\cup\!Sing[X_{o}\!\cap\!V(x_{1},\dots,x_{r_{2}})]\!\cup\!\cdots\!\cup\!Sing[X_{o}\!\cap\!V(x_{1},\dots,x_{r_{k}})].

Here each intersection
X_{o}\!\cap\!V(x_{1},\dots,x_{r_{j}})
is considered as a scheme with “prescribed dimension”:

•

(for
\mathbbm{k}=\mathbb{R}
)
dim_{\mathbb{R}}\leq dim_{\mathbb{R}}(X_{o})-r_{j}.

•

(for
\mathbbm{k}=\mathbb{C}
)
dim_{\mathbb{C}}=dim_{\mathbb{C}}(X_{o})-r_{j}.

Therefore
Sing[X_{o}\cap V(x_{1},\dots,x_{r_{j}})]
contains all the non-reduced components of
X_{0}\cap V(x_{1},\dots,x_{r_{j}})
, and also all the components of
dimension
>dim_{\mathbbm{k}}(X_{o})-r_{j}.

Altogether,
\Sigma\subset(\mathbbm{k}^{N},o)
is a weighted-homogeneous (
\mathbbm{k}
-algebraic) subgerm.

Example 2.2
.

i.

If
\omega_{1}=\cdots=\omega_{N}
then
r_{1}=N
and
\Sigma=Sing(X_{o}).

ii.

Suppose
\omega_{1}\!<\!\omega_{2}\!<\!\cdots\!<\!\omega_{n+1}
and let
X_{o}\!\subset\!(\mathbbm{k}^{n+1},o)
be a weighted-homogeneous hypersurface germ with isolated singularity, i.e.
Sing(X_{o})\!=\!\{o\}.
Suppose
dim_{\mathbbm{k}}(X_{o}\cap V(x_{1},\dots,x_{j}))\!\leq\!n-j,
as one could expect.
Moreover, suppose all the germs
X_{o}\!\cap\!V(x_{1}),
…,
X_{o}\!\cap\!V(x_{1},\dots,x_{n-1})
are reduced outside of
o,
and
Sing[X_{o}\!\cap\!V(x_{1},\dots,x_{j})]\!=\!\{o\}.

Then
\Sigma=\{o\}.

iii.

(Continuing example
2.1
) Let
f_{o}(x,y,z)=x^{p_{x}}+z^{p_{z}}+xy^{p_{y}},
suppose the weights satisfy:
\omega_{x}<\omega_{z}<\omega_{y},
i.e.
p_{x}>p_{z}>p_{y}.
Then
\Sigma=V(x,z)\subset(\mathbbm{k}^{3},o).

2.2.
Deforming the foliation

Having the obstruction locus,
\Sigma\subset(\mathbbm{k}^{N},o),
the naive guess is: the arcs
\{\gamma_{\underline{s}}\}_{\underline{s}}
deform to the arcs
\{\gamma_{\epsilon,{\underline{s}}}\}_{\underline{s}},

and for each
\epsilon
these later arcs foliate the germ
(\mathbbm{k}^{N},o)\setminus\Sigma.
This does not always hold. In fact, take a small representative
Ball\setminus\Sigma
of
(\mathbbm{k}^{N},o)\setminus\Sigma.
It is not necessary covered by the arcs
\{\gamma_{\epsilon,{\underline{s}}}\}_{\underline{s}},
as the radius of convergence of

\gamma_{\epsilon,{\underline{s}}}(t)\in\mathbb{R}\{t\}
can vanish when
{\underline{s}}
approaches
Link[\Sigma].

The correct statement will be: the arcs
\{\gamma_{\epsilon,{\underline{s}}}\}_{\underline{s}}
foliate the germ
(\mathbbm{k}^{N},o)\setminus\mathcal{U}(\Sigma)
for certain ‘hornic’ neighborhood

\Sigma\subset\mathcal{U}(\Sigma).

Take the change of
\omega
-orders,
\delta\!:=\!min_{i}\{ord_{\omega}f_{>p_{i}}\!-\!ord_{\omega}f_{p_{i}}\}.

If
f_{>p_{i}}=0,
one puts
ord_{\omega}f_{>p_{i}}=\infty
.
Thus
0<\delta\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}.

Lemma 2.3
.

There exists a family of arcs
\{\gamma_{\epsilon,{\underline{s}}}\}_{\underline{s}}
, for
\epsilon\in Disc
and
{\underline{s}}\in\mathbb{S}\setminus Link[\Sigma]
,

(5)

\gamma_{\epsilon,{\underline{s}}}(t):=t^{{\underline{\omega}}}\cdot({\underline{s}}+\epsilon\cdot t^{\delta}\cdot{\underline{h}}):=(t^{\omega_{1}}s_{1}+t^{\omega_{1}+\delta}\epsilon\cdot h_{1}(\epsilon,{\underline{s}},t),\ \dots\ ,t^{\omega_{N}}s_{N}+t^{\omega_{N}+\delta}\epsilon\cdot h_{N}(\epsilon,{\underline{s}},t)),\quad

with the following properties.

​​​1.​

For each
\epsilon,{\underline{s}}
the arcs are real-analytic, i.e.
\gamma_{\epsilon,{\underline{s}}}(t)\in\mathbb{R}\{t\}.

If the perturbations
{\underline{f}}_{>{\underline{p}}}
are Nash function-germs (e.g. polynomials), then the arcs are Nash, i.e.

\gamma_{\epsilon,{\underline{s}}}(t)\in\mathbb{R}\langle t\rangle.

​​​2.​

For all
\epsilon,\epsilon^{\prime}\in Disc
and
{\underline{s}},{\underline{s}}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{S}\setminus Link[\Sigma]:

​​
i.

\gamma_{\underline{s}}\subset X_{o}
if and only if
\gamma_{\epsilon,{\underline{s}}}\subset X_{\epsilon}.

​​
ii.

tord_{\mathbbm{k}^{N}}(\gamma_{\underline{s}},\gamma_{\epsilon,{\underline{s}}})\geq 1+\frac{\delta}{\omega_{N}}.

​​
iii.

In particular,
tord_{\mathbbm{k}^{N}}(\gamma_{\underline{s}},\gamma_{{\underline{s}}^{\prime}})=1
if and only if
tord_{\mathbbm{k}^{N}}(\gamma_{\epsilon,{\underline{s}}},\gamma_{\epsilon^{\prime},{\underline{s}}^{\prime}})=1.

​​
iv.

\gamma_{\underline{s}}\subset V(x_{1},\dots,x_{r_{j}})
if and only if
\gamma_{\epsilon,{\underline{s}}}\subset V(x_{1},\dots,x_{r_{j}}).

​​
v.

If
\gamma_{\underline{s}}\in X_{o}\cap X_{\epsilon}\setminus\Sigma
(for some
\epsilon>0
and
{\underline{s}}\in\mathbb{S}
), then
\gamma_{\epsilon,{\underline{s}}}=\gamma_{\underline{s}}
for each
\epsilon.

​​​3.​

(The case
\Sigma=\{o\},
thus
Link[\Sigma]=\varnothing
)

​​
i.

The arcs are analytic in
t,\epsilon,{\underline{s}},
i.e.
\gamma_{\epsilon,{\underline{s}}}(t)\in C^{\omega}(Disc\times\mathbb{S}\times[0,t_{o})),
for some
0<t_{o}.

If the perturbations
{\underline{f}}_{\!>{\underline{p}}}
​are Nash function-germs, ​then
\gamma_{\epsilon,{\underline{s}}}(t)\!\in\!Nash(Disc\!\times\!\mathbb{S}\!\times\![0,t_{o})).

​​
ii.

For each
\epsilon\in Disc
the arcs
\gamma_{\epsilon,{\underline{s}}}
foliate the whole germ
(\mathbbm{k}^{N},o).

​​​4.​

(The case
\Sigma\neq\{o\},
thus
Link[\Sigma]\neq\varnothing
) For any (
\mathbb{R}
-analytic) neighborhood
Link[\Sigma]\subset\tilde{U}\subset\mathbb{S}
there exists
0<t_{o}

and
o\in Ball_{r_{o}}\subset\mathbbm{k}^{N},
satisfying:

​​
i.​

\gamma_{\epsilon,{\underline{s}}}(t)\in C^{\omega}(Disc\times(\mathbb{S}\setminus\tilde{U})\times[0,t_{o})).

If the perturbations
{\underline{f}}_{>{\underline{p}}}
are Nash function-germs, then
\gamma_{\epsilon,{\underline{s}}}(t)\!\in\!Nash(Disc\!\times\!(\mathbb{S}\setminus\tilde{U})\!\times\![0,t_{o})).

​​
ii.​

\gamma_{\epsilon,{\underline{s}}}\cap\gamma_{\epsilon,{\underline{s}}^{\prime}}\cap Ball_{r_{o}}=\{o\}
for all
{\underline{s}},{\underline{s}}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{S}\setminus\tilde{\mathcal{U}}
with
s\neq s^{\prime}.

​​
iii.​

There exists an
\mathbb{R}
-analytic neighborhood
\Sigma\subset\mathcal{U}(\Sigma)\subset(\mathbbm{k}^{N},o)
with
Link[\mathcal{U}(\Sigma)]=\tilde{U},

Link_{o}[\mathcal{U}(\Sigma)]=Link_{o}[\Sigma],

and such that (for each
\epsilon\in Disc
) the arcs cover the complement of

\Psi_{\epsilon}\!:(\mathbbm{k}^{N},o)\!\to\!(\mathbbm{k}^{N},o)
by
\gamma_{\underline{s}}(t)\!\to\!\gamma_{\epsilon,{\underline{s}}}(t).

Explicitly,
\Psi_{\epsilon}:x_{\underline{s}}(t)\to x_{\underline{s}}(t)\!+\!\epsilon\!\cdot\!t^{\delta}\!\cdot\!t^{{\underline{\omega}}}\!\cdot\!{\underline{h}}({\underline{s}},t,\epsilon),
where
{\underline{h}}
is given by the Ansatz (
8
), while
{\underline{z}}
is determined by equation (
13
).
Thus
\Psi_{\epsilon}
is a family of (subanalytic) homeomorphisms, and their weighted-polar presentation is analytic/Nash.

The map of weighted-polar coordinates,
\mathbb{S}\times\mathbb{R}_{>0}\to\mathbbm{k}^{N}\setminus o,
is a real-algebraic isomorphism.
(Note that
gcd(\omega_{1},\dots,\omega_{N})\!=\!1.
)
Thus
\Psi_{\epsilon}
is obviously real-analytic/Nash over

(\mathbbm{k}^{N}\setminus o,o).

By the construction
\Psi
trivializes the family of zero-sets,

(17)

V({\underline{f}}_{\underline{p}}+\epsilon\cdot{\underline{f}}_{>{\underline{p}}})=\Psi_{\epsilon}(V({\underline{f}}_{\underline{p}}))=V({\underline{f}}_{\underline{p}}\circ\Psi^{-1}_{\epsilon})\subset(\mathbbm{k}^{N},o).\vskip-5.69046pt

We promote this to the contact trivialization of functions.

​​​​
\bullet

(The hypersurface case,
c\!=\!1
.) Take the weighted-polar presentation
f_{p}(t^{\underline{\omega}}\!\cdot\!{\underline{s}})\!+\!\epsilon\!\cdot\!f_{>p}(t^{\underline{\omega}}\!\cdot\!{\underline{s}})\!\in\!C^{\omega}(\mathbb{S}\!\times\![0,t_{o})).
We have the
\mathbb{R}
-analytic trivialization of the family of zero sets,
V(f_{p}(t^{\underline{\omega}}\cdot{\underline{s}})+\epsilon\cdot f_{>p}(t^{\underline{\omega}}\cdot{\underline{s}}))=V(f_{p}(\Psi^{-1}_{\epsilon}(t^{\underline{\omega}}\cdot{\underline{s}}))).
Therefore locally at each point of
\mathbb{S}\times[0,t_{o})
the family of functions

f_{p}(t^{\underline{\omega}}\cdot{\underline{s}})+\epsilon\cdot f_{>p}(t^{\underline{\omega}}\cdot{\underline{s}})
is
\mathbb{R}
-analytically contact trivial.
(See e.g. pg.113, Proposition 4.6 in
[
Mon. Nuñ.-Bal.
]
.)

Therefore the ratio
\tilde{u}(t,{\underline{s}}):=\frac{f_{p}(t^{\underline{\omega}}\cdot{\underline{s}})+\epsilon\cdot f_{>p}(t^{\underline{\omega}}\cdot{\underline{s}})}{f_{p}(\Psi^{-1}_{\epsilon}(t^{\underline{\omega}}\cdot{\underline{s}}))}

is locally analytic and invertible at each point of
\mathbb{S}\times[0,t_{o}).
Thus (by analytic uniqueness) we get the globally analytic functions

\frac{1}{\tilde{u}},\tilde{u}\in C^{\omega}(\mathbb{S}\times[0,t_{o})).

Finally we define the “scaling factor”
u:(\mathbbm{k}^{N},o)\to\mathbbm{k}^{1}
via
u(t^{\underline{\omega}}\cdot{\underline{s}})=\tilde{u}(t,{\underline{s}})
to get the contact trivialization

f_{p}+\epsilon\cdot f_{>p}=u\cdot f_{p}\circ\Psi^{-1}_{\epsilon}.

​​​​
\bullet

(The general case,
c\geq 1
.) As before we have the
\mathbb{R}
-analytic trivialization of the family of zero sets,
V({\underline{f}}_{\underline{p}}(t^{\underline{\omega}}\cdot{\underline{s}})+\epsilon\cdot{\underline{f}}_{>{\underline{p}}}(t^{\underline{\omega}}\cdot{\underline{s}}))=V({\underline{f}}_{\underline{p}}(\Psi^{-1}_{\epsilon}(t^{\underline{\omega}}\cdot{\underline{s}})))\subset\mathbb{S}\times[0,t_{o}).

As before (by Mather’s theorem), at each point of
\mathbb{S}\times[0,t_{o})
the family of functions

{\underline{f}}_{\underline{p}}(t^{\underline{\omega}}\cdot{\underline{s}})+\epsilon\cdot{\underline{f}}_{>{\underline{p}}}(t^{\underline{\omega}}\cdot{\underline{s}})
is locally
\mathbb{R}
-analytically contact trivial.
Thus the germs of the ideals
({\underline{f}}_{\underline{p}}(t^{\underline{\omega}}\cdot{\underline{s}})+\epsilon\cdot{\underline{f}}_{>{\underline{p}}}(t^{\underline{\omega}}\cdot{\underline{s}})),({\underline{f}}_{\underline{p}}(\Psi^{-1}_{\epsilon}(t^{\underline{\omega}}\cdot{\underline{s}})))\subset C^{\omega}(\mathbb{S}\times[0,t_{o}))
coincide locally at each point of
\mathbb{S}\times[0,t_{o}).

We want to deduce: these ideals coincide globally.

Consider the quotient module

(18)

M:={\left.\raisebox{6.88889pt}{\footnotesize$({\underline{f}}_{\underline{p}}({\underline{s}})+t^{-{\underline{p}}}\cdot\epsilon\cdot{\underline{f}}_{>{\underline{p}}}(t^{{\underline{\omega}}}\cdot{\underline{s}}))+({\underline{f}}_{\underline{p}}({\underline{s}}))$}\!\!\!\!{\diagup}\!\!\raisebox{-4.30554pt}{\footnotesize$({\underline{f}}_{\underline{p}}({\underline{s}}))$}\right.}\in mod\text{-}C^{\omega}(\mathbb{S}\times(-t_{o},t_{o})).\vskip-5.69046pt

We want to prove
M=0.
This module is finitely generated, and its fibres vanish at all the points of

\mathbb{S}\times(-t_{o},t_{o}).

​​​
-

Working over
\mathbb{C}
we would immediately conclude:
M=0.
(See e.g. pg. 46 of
[
Atiyah-Macdonald
]
.) Indeed, this is equivalent to

SuppM=\varnothing.
Thus it is enough to verify that the localizations of
M
at all the maximal ideals vanish.
Thus (
M
being a f.g. module) it is enough to verify that the fibres of
M
at all the f.g. maximal ideals vanish.
And finally, the f.g. maximal ideals correspond to the closed points.

​​​
-

Over
\mathbb{R}
this is not immediate, as many maximal ideals do not correspond to the real closed points of
\mathbb{S}\times(-t_{o},t_{o}).

Therefore we observe:
SuppM
is a (Zariski) closed subset of a scheme whose real points are the points of
\mathbb{S}\times(-t_{o},t_{o}).

And
Supp(M)
contains no point of
\mathbb{S}\times(-t_{o},t_{o}).
Thus
Supp(M)
is disjoint to a neighborhood of the real locus
\mathbb{S}\times(-t_{o},t_{o}).

And thus
M
vanishes along this locus.

Altogether we get the global equality of ideals:
({\underline{f}}_{\underline{p}}({\underline{s}}))\!=\!({\underline{f}}_{\underline{p}}({\underline{s}})\!+\!t^{-{\underline{p}}}\cdot\epsilon\cdot{\underline{f}}_{>{\underline{p}}}(t^{{\underline{\omega}}}\cdot{\underline{s}})).
Which means:
{\underline{f}}_{\underline{p}}({\underline{s}})\!+\!t^{-{\underline{p}}}\cdot\epsilon\cdot{\underline{f}}_{>{\underline{p}}}(t^{{\underline{\omega}}}\cdot{\underline{s}})=U\cdot{\underline{f}}_{\underline{p}}({\underline{s}}),

for a matrix
U(t^{{\underline{\omega}}}\cdot{\underline{s}},\epsilon)\in GL(c,C^{\omega}\big(\mathbb{S}\times(-t_{o},t_{o})\times Disc)\big).

Step 2.

Statement 1.
We should only verify the differentiability of
\Psi_{\epsilon}\circlearrowright(\mathbbm{k}^{N},o)
at the origin.
It is enough to present

{\underline{h}}({\underline{x}})=B(s,t)\cdot{\underline{x}},
where the matrix

is bounded.
Write the entries of
t^{\underline{\omega}}\cdot{\underline{h}}:

(19)

t^{\omega_{j}}h_{j}(\epsilon,{\underline{s}},t)=(\sum^{r_{l}}_{i=1}|s^{2}_{i}|)\cdot t^{\omega_{j}}\cdot\Big[\partial_{x_{j}}f_{p_{1}}|_{\underline{s}},\dots,\partial_{x_{j}}f_{p_{c}}|_{\underline{s}}\Big]\cdot A^{\vee}\cdot\begin{bmatrix}t^{-p_{1}-\delta}\cdot f_{>p_{1}}(t^{{\underline{\omega}}}\cdot{\underline{s}})+(h.o.t.)\\
\dots\\
t^{-p_{c}-\delta}\cdot f_{>p_{c}}(t^{{\underline{\omega}}}\cdot{\underline{s}})+(h.o.t.)\end{bmatrix}.

Here
r_{l}\geq j
and
\omega_{j}=\omega_{r_{l}},
and
s_{i}t^{\omega_{j}}=x_{i}\cdot t^{\omega_{j}-\omega_{i}},
with
\omega_{j}\geq\omega_{i}.

Therefore we can present:
t^{\omega_{j}}h_{j}({\underline{s}},t,\epsilon)=\sum^{r_{1}}_{i=1}x_{i}\cdot b_{i,j}({\underline{s}},t,\epsilon).
And one has
ord_{\omega}(h.o.t.)>ord_{\omega}(t^{-p_{k}-\delta}\cdot f_{>p_{k}})\geq 0.
Thus the coefficients
b_{i,j}({\underline{s}},t,\epsilon)
are bounded.

With this presentation we have:
\Psi_{\epsilon}({\underline{x}})-{\underline{x}}=\epsilon\cdot t^{\delta}\cdot B(s,t)\cdot{\underline{x}}=o(\|{\underline{x}}\|).
Hence
\Psi_{\epsilon}
is differentiable, and

\Psi^{\prime}|_{o}=Id.

Statement 2
is immediate.

Statement 3.
We bound the derivatives
\frac{\partial(t^{\delta+\omega_{i}}h_{i})}{\partial x_{j}}.

It is enough to verify that the polar presentation of these derivatives is bounded (resp. tend to
0
) when
t\to 0.

By compactness of
\mathbb{S}
it is enough to verify this locally at each point.
Finally, it is enough to consider the case
\mathbbm{k}=\mathbb{R}.
(For
\mathbbm{k}=\mathbb{C}
one passes to the real-polar coordinates,
s_{1},\dots,s_{2N}

It is enough to work in the chart
s_{N}\neq 0
, where the coordinates are
t,s_{1},\dots,s_{N-1}.

(Then
s_{N}
is determined by
\sum^{N}|s_{i}|^{2}=1
.) Indeed, this chart is dense on
\mathbb{S}.

We use the chain rule,

\frac{\partial(t^{\delta+\omega_{1}}h_{1},\dots,t^{\delta+\omega_{N}}h_{N})}{\partial(x_{1}\dots x_{N})}=\frac{\partial(t^{\delta+\omega_{1}}h_{1},\dots,t^{\delta+\omega_{N}}h_{N})}{\partial(t,s_{1}\dots s_{N-1})}\cdot\frac{\partial(t,s_{1}\dots s_{N-1})}{\partial(x_{1}\dots x_{N})}.

To compute the matrix
\frac{\partial(t,s_{1}\dots s_{N-1})}{\partial(x_{1}\dots x_{N})}
we write its inverse:

(20)

\frac{\partial(x_{1}\dots x_{N})}{\partial(t,s_{1}\dots s_{N-1})}=\begin{bmatrix}s_{1}t^{\omega_{1}-1}&t^{\omega_{1}}&0&\dots\\
s_{2}t^{\omega_{2}-1}&0&t^{\omega_{2}}&0\\
\dots&\dots&0&t^{\omega_{N-1}}\\
s_{N}t^{\omega_{N}-1}&\frac{\partial s_{N}}{\partial s_{1}}t^{\omega_{N}}&\dots&\frac{\partial s_{N}}{\partial s_{N-1}}t^{\omega_{N}}\end{bmatrix}.

Therefore
det[\frac{\partial(x_{1}\dots x_{N})}{\partial(t,s_{1}\dots s_{N-1})}]\ \lower 0.86108pt\hbox{$\mathrel{\mathop{=}\limits^{*}}$}\ t^{\sum\omega_{i}-1}(s_{1}\frac{\partial s_{N}}{\partial s_{1}}+\cdots+s_{N-1}\frac{\partial s_{N}}{\partial s_{N-1}}+s_{N})=s_{N}\cdot t^{\sum\omega_{i}-1}.

Here
*
is obtained by extracting
t^{\omega_{i}}
from
i
’th row and
\frac{1}{t}
from the 1’st column.

The adjugate matrix is
[\frac{\partial(x_{1}\dots x_{N})}{\partial(t,s_{1}\dots s_{N-1})}]^{\vee}\in t^{\sum\omega_{i}-1-\omega_{N}}\cdot Mat_{N\times N}(\mathbb{R}\{{\underline{s}},t\}).
The first tow of this matrix belongs to the ideal
(t^{\sum\omega_{i}-\omega_{N}}).

Thus

And the first row of this matrix belongs to the ideal

Altogether we get:

\frac{\partial(t^{\delta+\omega_{1}}h_{1},\dots,t^{\delta+\omega_{N}}h_{N})}{\partial(x_{1}\dots x_{N})}\in t^{\delta+\omega_{1}-\omega_{N}}\cdot Mat_{N\times N}(\mathbb{R}\{{\underline{s}},t\}).

\Psi_{\epsilon}

Hence

If

Hence
\Psi_{\epsilon}
is

The corresponding statements for

Corollary 3.3
.

Suppose
\Sigma({\underline{f}}_{{\underline{p}},\epsilon})=o
for each
\epsilon\in Disc.
Then the family
{\underline{f}}_{{\underline{p}},\epsilon}+\epsilon\cdot{\underline{f}}_{\geq{\underline{p}}}
is contact-trivial,

{\underline{f}}_{{\underline{p}},\epsilon}+\epsilon\cdot{\underline{f}}_{\geq{\underline{p}}}=U_{\epsilon}\cdot{\underline{f}}_{\underline{p}}\circ\Psi_{\epsilon},
where
U_{\epsilon},\Psi_{\epsilon}
satisfy properties 1,2,5 of theorem
3.1

\Psi_{\epsilon}
, but not necessarily properties 3,4.

Proof.
For the case
|\epsilon|\ll 1
the proof is the same as in theorem
3.1
. The homeomorphism
\Psi_{\epsilon}
is again defined via the deformed foliation, see Remark
2.5
.

In the general (non-local) case,
\epsilon\in Disc,
one takes a finite cover of the compact disc by small balls. For each such small ball we get
the unique coordinate change
\Psi_{\epsilon}
whose weighted-polar presentation is
\mathbb{R}
-analytic. These coordinate changes glue by analytic uniqueness.
(Note that
Disc

\Psi_{\epsilon}\in C^{\omega}(Disc\times\mathbb{S}\times[0,t_{o})).
By the same arguments as in theorem this leads to the global equality of ideals

({\underline{f}}_{\underline{p}}\circ\Psi_{\epsilon})=(({\underline{f}}_{\underline{p}}+\epsilon\cdot{\underline{f}}_{>{\underline{p}}})\circ\Psi_{\epsilon})\subset C^{\omega}(\mathbb{S}\times[0,t_{o})).
This gives the statement.

\blacksquare
