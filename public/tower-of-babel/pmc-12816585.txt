Deep learning-based detection of power transmission lines using YOLOv4 and YOLOv8
Authors: Tugce Nur Karadeniz, Sami Ekici, Engin Avci
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractReliable energy distribution is considered a cornerstone of modern society, and the maintenance of power transmission lines (PTLs) plays a vital role in achieving this goal. The accurate and rapid detection of PTLs not only enhances the efficiency of energy distribution networks but also is crucial in preventing potential failures and accidents. Traditional inspection methods often rely on the human eye, leading to time-consuming, costly, and risky processes. In this context, the emergence of unmanned aerial vehicle (UAV) technology has brought about a revolutionary change in power transmission line inspections. UAVs offer unprecedented efficiency, safety, and cost-effectiveness in monitoring energy infrastructure. The integration of deep learning-based object detection methods with UAV technology enables safe and effective monitoring of power transmission lines. Particularly, the You Only Look Once (YOLO) algorithm facilitates faster and more accurate results by combining object detection and classification processes within a single network. This study comprehensively examines the object detection applications of the YOLO algorithm. The results indicate that all versions of YOLOv8 outperform YOLOv4 in terms of object detection, demonstrating high performance. The precision, recall, and F1 score performance metrics for the tested YOLOv8 versions were found to be over 99%. The average mAP50 and mAP50-95 values were found to be 0.995 and 0.919, respectively.Keywords: Power transmission lines, Unmanned aerial vehicles, Deep learning, YOLOv8Subject terms: Energy science and technology, Electrical and electronic engineering

Introduction

Power transmission lines (PTLs) are critical for ensuring the maintenance and operation of reliable energy distribution systems, particularly as societies become increasingly dependent on electricity1. These lines are the infrastructure systems that enable the transmission of electrical energy from production sources to transformer stations and final consumers (homes, workplaces, etc.). Energy transmission lines ensure the safe and efficient transport of electrical energy at various voltage levels. Electricity is transported from power generation stations to consumption points via high and ultra-high voltage transmission lines, which stretch thousands of kilometers across mountains, forests, and hard-to-reach areas. Given the crucial role of the transmission system, system operators place great importance on maintenance activities to ensure grid availability1. Traditional methods for inspecting power transmission lines have shown little change over the years. These methods rely on visual inspections performed by human crews from the ground or via helicopters2. Visual inspections of transmission lines are often conducted by teams of two inspectors on foot. Inspectors use binoculars and sometimes infrared cameras to check the condition of the lines. This method is slow and physically demanding. In rugged terrain, under poor weather conditions, or during extreme weather events such as hurricanes and snowstorms, this approach becomes even more difficult3. While helicopter-assisted inspections offer advantages in terms of reaching hard-to-access areas and increasing the speed of inspection, they also have numerous drawbacks, including high costs, reduced accuracy due to speed, and the risk of contact with live lines. Furthermore, the detection rates of these methods are limited by the visual observation capabilities of the inspectors2,3.

Recently, as shown in Fig. 1, alternative technologies such as UAVs and climbing robots have been tested and implemented for the inspection of power transmission lines4,5. UAVs are defined as technology that can fly without a human pilot onboard and remain airborne autonomously. Smart inspection systems offer 2.5 times greater efficiency compared to traditional methods, saving significant time for grid maintenance personnel and reducing their workload, thus increasing operational efficiency. Moreover, these systems stand out due to their ability to perform critical tasks efficiently without risking human lives while offering lower operational costs compared to manned equivalents2. UAVs, with their environmentally friendly features, present promising solutions in this sector. These systems are easily and quickly deployable, scalable, and flexible with their innovative designs. Additionally, they feature capabilities such as self-organization, cost-effectiveness, and high maneuverability. UAVs are diverse in terms of the payloads they can carry, which may include communication devices, navigation equipment, sensors, cameras, and artificial intelligence (AI) technologies. They also vary widely in terms of configuration, size, range, weight, motor type, and performance characteristics. UAVs can carry various equipment such as communication devices, navigation systems, sensors, and cameras6,7.

Power transmission line monitoring scheme.

UAVs can be produced much more cheaply compared to manned aircraft due to their smaller and lighter frames. Unmanned flights make UAV accidents more financially and socially acceptable, encouraging the execution of tasks that were previously deemed impossible. Additionally, the fact that UAVs have a lifespan of over 10 years and can be operated at low costs with minimal maintenance required provides significant advantages4.

Visual inspections primarily focus on examining the condition of structural components such as transmission lines, conductors, and towers. Additionally, detecting contamination or physical damage to power insulators is a key application. Visual inspection methods are also widely used to detect violations near tower structures or on the ground. In offline applications, UAVs can record a large number of images of the transmission grid. These images can then be analyzed to detect patterns that indicate fatigue, cracks, rust, corrosion, or other damage. UAVs can also be used for surveillance against potential terrorist attacks on transmission lines8. This new approach aims to offer faster, safer, and more efficient solutions in the inspection of power transmission lines, while also having the potential to create a significant transformation in the energy sector by meeting the increasing demand for energy and ensuring the security of transmission line systems2.

Thanks to regular UAV inspections, Enel can detect faults early and optimize its maintenance operations. The distinction between types of power transmission lines is similarly important; their varying physical structures and designs present different challenges for UAV visual perception and navigation systems. Power line detection presents several challenges due to the thin and elongated structure of wires, their low contrast against complex backgrounds such as vegetation or sky, and varying lighting and weather conditions. These factors necessitate robust models capable of generalizing well across different scenarios and environments.

Overview of methodology.

Deep learning

The foundations of deep learning can be traced back to the development of artificial neural networks in the 1940s and 1950s. However, modern deep learning emerged in the early 2000s, led by researchers like Hinton and Salakhutdinov16. In the 2010s, significant advancements were made in deep learning with the use of large datasets and powerful GPUs17. Specifically, the victory of AlexNet in the ImageNet competition in 2012 marked the widespread recognition of deep learning18. As shown in Fig. 3, deep learning is a subset of machine learning, capable of learning from complex data and making predictions19. Upon reviewing the literature, it has been proven that deep learning is effective in various fields, including image and video recognition, audio processing, and natural language processing20.

Hierarchy of learning methods.

Deep learning-based remote sensing object detection has a wide range of applications, and YOLO has become a prominent model due to its fast performance and high accuracy21. YOLO is a highly popular object detection algorithm in the field of deep learning. It is a single-stage object detection algorithm known for its fast inference speed and high accuracy. It enables the detection of multiple objects in an image and CNNs to distinguish between them22. As shown in the timeline in Fig. 4, YOLOv1, developed by Joseph Redmon and Ali Farhadi in 2015, attracted attention with its fast processing speed and high accuracy23. Over time, YOLO has undergone many versions, each improving performance and addressing specific issues. For example, YOLOv2 made enhancements to detect objects of varying sizes, while YOLOv3 improved performance with features like a backbone network and spatial pyramid pooling. YOLOv4 introduced enhancements such as CSPDarknet53 as a backbone and PANet for path aggregation, resulting in improved accuracy and training stability. YOLOv5, implemented in PyTorch, offered lightweight models with better deployment flexibility. YOLOv6 introduced improvements for industrial applications, including better anchor-free detection and advanced label assignment strategies. YOLOv7 expanded the detection framework to support additional tasks such as instance segmentation and pose estimation. YOLOv8, the latest release, integrates an anchor-free design, dynamic label assignment, and auto-learning of hyperparameters for even better performance23. YOLOv8 offers the best performance in terms of both speed and accuracy compared to previous models24. YOLO frames object detection as a regression problem, simultaneously predicting bounding boxes and associated class probabilities in an image25. By passing the image through neural networks just once, YOLO delivers faster performance compared to other object detection algorithms26. In this study, the YOLOv8 algorithm was used to detect power transmission lines from UAV images.

YOLOv8 algorithm

YOLOv8 was released by Ultralytics, the company that developed YOLOv5, in 202323. YOLOv8 has become dominant in target detection due to its strong generalization capabilities and increased accuracy. In YOLO, predictions are made by passing the input image through the neural network only once. This method enables real-time object detection with a delay of less than 25 ms, providing rapid predictions. The input image is divided into grid cells of S Ã— S dimensions, with each cell associated with bounding box predictions, confidence scores, and class probabilities2.

As shown in Fig. 5, the model’s architecture consists of the backbone, neck, and head sections27. In the input part, the image serves as the input data for the model. In the backbone section, convolutional layers are present, gradually reducing the dimensions of the image. YOLOv8 uses the C2f module instead of the C3 module to enhance gradient flow and offer a lightweight design. The C2f (CSP Module) is designed to process deep feature maps and minimize information loss. This module works in integration with shortcut connections, contributing to the processing flow by directly transferring some of the features from previous layers. The Spatial Pyramid Pooling Fast (SPPF) layer pools pixels at different scales to summarize a larger area of the feature maps from the backbone. This improves the model’s ability to recognize objects of various sizes and helps it detect objects in a wider context by combining information at different scales. The SPPF layer is located near the end of the backbone, just before the neck begins.

YOLOv8 architecture.

In the neck section, there are concatenation and upsampling layers. The features from the neck are processed in these layers. These operations integrate information from previous layers and help align the dimensions23.

In the head section, detection layers perform convolutional operations on feature maps at each scale in the final stage, and these layers are used for object detection. Detecting objects at various scales increases the model’s ability to recognize objects of different sizes. Conv applies various convolutional operations and the CSP module in the final stages of detection layers. These operations play a critical role in extracting object classes and coordinates. The model presents an anchor-free architecture equipped with a head structure capable of handling object detection, classification, and regression tasks independently. This design allows each component to focus on its specific task, thus improving the model’s overall performance and accuracy23.

In the output layer of YOLOv8, the sigmoid activation function is used to determine the object score, which indicates the likelihood that the bounding box contains an object. Additionally, the softmax function is applied to determine class probabilities, representing the likelihood of the objects belonging to each potential class. It uses the CIoU loss function for calculating bounding box loss and the binary cross-entropy loss function for classification loss21. These loss functions significantly enhance the model’s performance, especially in detecting small objects. Additionally, YOLOv8 can be run via a command-line interface (CLI) or installed as a PIP package23. The model also includes Soft-NMS, a variant of the non-maximum suppression (NMS) technique found in YOLOv5. Soft-NMS processes overlapping bounding boxes by applying a soft threshold instead of completely eliminating them22.

YOLOv8 supports various computer vision tasks, including object detection, instance segmentation, pose/keypoint detection, oriented object detection, and classification. Each model type is optimized to perform a specific task in the best possible way. Moreover, it provides flexibility by working in different modes such as inference, validation, training, and exporting23.

Experimental studies

Data set

In this study, comparisons will be made using the results obtained from YOLOv4, based on the Power Transmission Line dataset prepared by Lucas Diniz and colleagues5. The dataset contains three different classes: double-circuit, simple-circuit, and ”real-circuit. Real-world data were collected via a vision-based system integrated into a UAV flying over power transmission lines. The dimensions of the 290 collected images were set at 1920 pixels in width and 1088 pixels in height. Images for the double-circuit and simple-circuit classes were generated in the Gazebo simulator by creating different scenarios. To increase the robustness of the model, some synthetic images included elements such as complex backgrounds, trees, mountains, and buildings that resemble real-world data. The dimensions of the synthetic images were defined as 320 pixels in width and 240 pixels in height. For each synthetic class, 290 different images were produced. Data augmentation techniques such as random rotation, flipping, and contrast adjustments were applied to generate additional data. Through the data augmentation process, a total of 174 new images were created, with 58 images for each class. This ensured that the sample count for each class was balanced, eliminating the issue of class imbalance. In total, the original dataset consists of 1,044 images, evenly distributed among three classes (348 per class): real-circuit, simple-circuit, and double-circuit. In this study the original dataset images were annotated using the RoboFlow platform in YOLO format, which encodes object classes and normalized bounding box coordinates. The hyperparameters used by Lucas Diniz et al.5 in the YOLOv4 algorithm are shown in Table 1. They conducted the training using a Tesla K80 GPU in the Google Colab environment. The training took approximately 35 hours, resulting 98.47% mAP in the validation image set.

Data labeling and preprocessing

To prepare the dataset for YOLO training, a series of preprocessing and augmentation steps were applied to improve data quality and model generalization. RoboFlow is a library developed for object detection and segmentation tasks, containing open-source artificial intelligence algorithms28. Each image in the dataset was labeled using the RoboFlow platform. The images labeled with RoboFlow were divided into training, validation, and test datasets for the YOLOv8 models. The training dataset was used for the model’s learning process, the validation dataset was used for model improvement, and the test dataset was used for predictions and evaluations.

In this study, a series of preprocessing steps were applied to the dataset created for transmission line detection to prepare the images for model training. Firstly, the Auto-Orient operation was applied to automatically align the images. Then, to focus on a specific region of the images and enhance the detection performance of the model, a static crop operation was performed. During this process, the 25–75% horizontal region and 30–75% vertical region of the images were cropped, and only this selected region was used as input to the model.

After the cropping process, the images were resized to the model’s input resolution of 640x640 pixels and converted to grayscale to reduce computational complexity and speed up the training process. To increase the variety of the dataset and improve the model’s capability to detect objects from different perspectives, horizontal flip and 90° rotation (both clockwise and counter-clockwise) data augmentation techniques were applied to each image, generating three additional augmented samples per training example. This method aims to enhance the model’s overall performance by increasing the diversity of the dataset28.

YOLOv8 offers five different variations depending on the depth and width of the layers: Nano (YOLOv8n), Small (YOLOv8s), Medium (YOLOv8m), Large (YOLOv8l), and Xlarge (YOLOv8x)23. The following evaluation metrics were used to analyze the performance of the model in this study: mean Average Precision (mAP), Precision, Recall, and F1 score.

False Negative (FN): If an object should have been detected but no object was detected, or if the IoU value is greater than 0.5 but the class is incorrect, it is considered an FN.

YOLOv8m is positioned as a balanced option in terms of training time and performance, making it suitable for larger datasets and complex tasks. Each model has exhibited impressive performance with high IoU thresholds as indicated by the mAP50-95 values. However, the lower mAP50-95 values for the GD class are noteworthy, suggesting the existence of areas for improvement to enhance model performance in more complex object detection scenarios. YOLOv8 versions provide flexibility for different needs and application areas.

In the reference study that served as the data source, it is observed that only the mAP metric was used for YOLOv4 performance evaluation, with a calculated value of 0.984. The most notable point in the study is that the training time took approximately 35 hours. These results clearly demonstrate that all YOLOv8 variants significantly outperform YOLOv4 in terms of both training time and performance.

Conclusion

The advancements in technology have led to the development of YOLOv8, a newer model trained with a larger dataset in a shorter training time. This model has exhibited superior performance not only in overall mAP but also in class-wise mAP values, demonstrating its robustness across various object categories. The ability to efficiently process larger datasets is critical for real-world applications dominated by variable conditions. The newer hardware used in training YOLOv8 may have contributed to this performance improvement. For example, the NVIDIA Tesla T4 graphics card not only shortens training times by providing higher computing power and efficiency but also enhances the quality of results through better resource management and faster processing speeds. YOLOv8 offers an effective solution for detecting energy transmission lines, and better results can be achieved by modifying the default parameters.

This study highlights the effectiveness of deep learning methods in the detection of energy transmission lines and underscores the potential of these technologies to transform the energy sector. Further development of this potential requires research with larger datasets and various deep learning algorithms. The integration of different datasets can enhance the model’s generalization capabilities, allowing it to perform better under diverse environmental conditions.

Additionally, the development of real-time detection systems can provide significant benefits in the management and maintenance of energy transmission lines. Such systems enable faster identification of problems, thereby reducing response times and minimizing the risk of interruptions or accidents. Integration with existing infrastructure leads to more proactive maintenance strategies, reducing operating costs and increasing safety.

The use of deep learning methods in the detection of energy transmission lines can significantly enhance the efficiency and safety of energy distribution networks. The high accuracy rates achieved with YOLOv8 demonstrate the potential for broader adoption of these methods. With the increasing global energy demand, the implementation of advanced detection technologies is critical to ensuring the reliability of energy supply systems.

Research and development in this area can strengthen the resilience of energy infrastructure by improving reliability and efficiency of energy networks and supporting the transition to smart energy management solutions. In this context, advancements in the technologies used for detecting energy transmission lines hold the potential to enhance the sustainability of the energy sector, enabling the achievement of smarter and more efficient energy management solutions.

Despite the promising results, this study has certain limitations. The dataset is primarily composed of images collected in clear weather conditions, which may restrict the modelâ€™s generalizability to foggy, rainy, or nighttime environments. Furthermore, while the current work focuses solely on power transmission lines, extending the model to detect other infrastructure components such as insulators, towers, or vegetation interference could provide a more holistic monitoring framework. Future work may explore domain adaptation techniques or multi-sensor data fusion to enhance robustness across varied conditions. Additionally, comprehensive comparisons with other state-of-the-art detectors such as YOLOv7, YOLO-NAS, and EfficientDet should be conducted to provide a more thorough evaluation of YOLOv8’s performance. Furthermore, ablation studies to systematically assess how individual preprocessing techniques (cropping, resizing, grayscale conversion) and data augmentation strategies (flipping, rotation) affect detection performance would provide valuable insights into the contribution of each component to the overall model accuracy.
