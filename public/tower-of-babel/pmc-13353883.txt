Synthesis of Patchy Particles at Phase Boundaries
Authors: Lin Zhang, Alexander Kuhn
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTFunctional patchy particles, distinguishing themselves from isotropic colloids through different discrete surface domains, are critical for advanced applications such as colloidal assembly, catalysis, and biomedical purposes. In this review, we focus on the elaboration of patchy particles, categorizing the different synthesis strategies based on the specific interfacial environments employed to induce asymmetry. Recent advances, as a function of the various interfacial constraints, are reviewed: (1) solid‐gas interfaces, which exploit physical shadowing for directional deposition; (2) liquid–gas interfaces, taking advantage of surface tension and wettability to create tunable dynamic masks for selective modification; (3) solid‐liquid interfaces, utilizing surface immobilization to restrict chemical access or induce site‐selective growth; and (4) liquid‐liquid interfaces, where soft boundaries facilitate symmetry breaking via thermodynamic partitioning, confinement induced assembly, and bipolar electrochemistry. By revealing the relationship between interfacial synthesis conditions, the triggered chemical reactions, and the final particle features, this review aims to provide practical insights and design principles for engineering next‐generation patchy particles with tailored complexity.Keywords: bipolar electrochemistry, emulsion, patchy particles, phase boundaries

Introduction

Multifunctional micro‐ and nanoparticles, which integrate several distinct physicochemical properties within a single entity, are gaining increasing prominence across diverse scientific fields. Unlike conventional isotropic colloids, patchy particles feature surfaces that are patterned into discrete domains with contrasting composition, wettability, charge, or chemical functionality. This architectural anisotropy allows for the precise integration of typically incompatible functions (e.g., hydrophobicity and hydrophilicity, magnetism, fluorescence, etc.) into a single object, often yielding synergistic properties unavailable for their isotropic counterparts, and representing, in the most extreme case, colloidal analogs of molecules [1]. As surface characteristics fundamentally govern colloidal behavior, these anisotropic particles exhibit unique capabilities in programmed colloidal assembly [2], functional interfacial engineering [3], catalysis [4, 5, 6, 7], micromotors [8, 9, 10], sensing [11], and biomedical applications [12, 13].

When the functional domains are distributed over the particle surface, these objects are commonly termed patchy particles. In the classic definition, a particle consisting of two separated domains with different properties is called a Janus particle. This terminology and physical picture are commonly attributed to De Gennes, who used the concept of “Janus grains” to describe anisotropic interfacial behavior. In contrast, patchy particles represent a broader class of anisotropic colloids, featuring multiple discrete surface patches with a controlled number, size, and spatial arrangement, a concept that emerged later in the context of research on colloidal particles. Du and O'Reilly have clarified the difference between patchy and Janus particles [14]. Based on their definition, Janus particles can be considered as the limiting case of patchy particles where the surface is divided into exactly two segregation regions. Throughout this review, we encompass both strict Janus architectures and more general patchy morphologies.

Up to now, several reviews have addressed the synthesis, assembly, and application of patchy [15, 16, 17, 18], multicomponent [19], and Janus particles [20, 21, 22], typically categorizing them either by material class such as metallic [23, 24], organic inorganic hybrid [3, 25], magnetic [26], and polymeric [22, 24, 27] objects, or by specific application fields. These reviews comprehensively cover material‐specific synthesis and emerging applications. Recent simulations and theoretical developments have further advanced the understanding of such anisotropic colloidal assemblies [22, 28].

A survey of existing synthetic strategies reveals that patchy particles can be generated through a very wide range of approaches, including spontaneous formation of block copolymer particles during solvent evaporation, site‐selective growth in homogeneous bulk solution, as well as the self‐assembly of patchy micelles in selected solvents. These methods rely on mechanisms such as internal phase separation, kinetic trapping, or molecular self‐organization, rather than externally imposed spatial constraints. A comprehensive treatment of all these different routes would, however, go beyond the scope of a single review.

Therefore, this review article especially focuses on synthesis strategies in which symmetry breaking is induced by the presence of an interface. In such approaches, the interface serves as a natural guide for symmetry breaking, enabling spatially controlled modification in a straightforward manner. To clarify this scope, we broadly define “interface‐based” synthesis and distinguish three main mechanisms: (i) phase boundaries that immobilize or partially shield the target particles, enabling anisotropic modification (e.g., solid‐gas or liquid‐gas interfaces); (ii) particle‐medium interfaces that favor site‐selective adsorption or growth in solution; and (iii) soft interfaces, such as droplets or emulsions, that provide confinement and directional environments for the formation of patchy structures. Thus, by focusing on the type of interface, this review aims to offer a complementary perspective to recent literature, categorizing synthesis routes based on the spatial origin of symmetry breaking.

Such interfaces (solid‐gas, liquid‐gas, solid‐liquid, or liquid‐liquid) serve as crucial platforms for symmetry breaking, naturally imposing environmental constraints and creating asymmetric conditions where distinct regions of a particle experience different chemical potentials, wetting conditions, or physical shielding. Thus, we categorize in this review recent advances in the synthesis of multifunctional patchy particles as a function of the specific type of phase boundary employed to induce asymmetry. We first discuss solid‐gas interfaces, which primarily utilize directional vapor deposition and shadowing effects to create well‐defined patches. We then examine liquid‐gas interfaces, which represent a tunable dynamic mask for selective modification of protruding domains, or serve as a stabilizing platform for the site‐selective functionalization of floating 2D materials. We subsequently analyze solid‐liquid interfaces, where strategies typically rely on surface immobilization and masking to restrict chemical access or to induce site‐selective growth. Finally, we explore liquid‐liquid interfaces, covering both curved (emulsion) and planar geometries, highlighting how these soft interfaces facilitate symmetry breaking through diverse mechanisms, ranging from thermodynamic partitioning and evaporation‐induced confinement in emulsions to bipolar electrochemical reactions at planar interfaces, where an external electric field drives, in a wireless way, the multicomponent deposition on particles. In addition to categorizing existing strategies, we evaluate their relative advantages and limitations for different types of interfaces using several criteria, including the control over patch geometry and spatial resolution, chemical versatility and material compatibility, as well as the achievable structural complexity.

Synthesis of Patchy Particles at Different Phase Boundaries

Solid‐Gas Interface

Solid‐gas interface strategies represent a straightforward, site‐selective approach, from a historic point of view, for synthesizing Janus particles. The fundamental principle involves immobilizing a monolayer of particles onto a planar solid support, followed by the selective modification of the exposed surface via vapor deposition or sputtering. The first critical step is the formation of a compact, ordered particle array, which can be achieved through techniques such as Langmuir‐Blodgett, spin coating, solvent evaporation, or drop casting. Once immobilized, the particles effectively mask the substrate and, in some cases, neighboring particles.

The glancing angle deposition (GLAD) method, employed for example, by Pawar et al., is a classic process to produce patchy particles using the solid‐gas interface and the shadow effect caused by neighboring particles within a particle monolayer [29]. By tilting the substrate relative to the vapor source, the particles within the monolayer cast shadows on their neighbors, blocking deposition on specific regions. Consequently, the angle of incidence and the crystallographic orientation of the monolayer dictate the specific geometry of the resulting metal patches on polystyrene (PS) particles. To increase complexity, this technique has been extended from single‐patch to multifunctional patchy particles using soft lithography. When a GLAD‐modified particle monolayer is transferred onto a polydimethylsiloxane stamp, which is then flipped upside‐down so that the initial patches face downward, a second vapor deposition results in two‐pole patchy particles with distinct materials (Figure 1A) [30]. Such asymmetric particles e.g. with deposited Pt patches, which catalyze the decomposition of hydrogen peroxide into oxygen and water, create a chemical gradient that can drive autonomous motion [31]. Recently, Raj et al. employed GLAD to generate Pt‐coated and Ti/Pt‐coated colloidal swimmers with varying catalytic patch areas by adjusting the deposition angle (10°, 30°, and 90°) (Figure 1B). Using optical tweezers, they precisely measured the self‐propulsive force and power spectral density associated with different patch geometries [32]. Furthermore, post‐functionalization allows for even more sophisticated designs. Han et al. reported the elaboration of cell‐sized (20 µm) piezoelectric magnetic Janus microparticles. These silica‐based particles, modified via solid‐gas techniques, enable clinically relevant high‐frequency neural stimulation of primary neurons under focused low‐intensity ultrasound [33].

Solid‐gas interfaces offer excellent control over patch geometry through directional deposition and shadow effects, enabling high spatial precision. However, they are inherently limited to materials compatible with vapor‐phase processing and are often constrained to planar substrates, which might restrict scalability. In addition, the fabrication of multi‐patch structures often requires multistep procedures, increasing experimental complexity.

Liquid‐Gas Interface

Solid‐gas interfaces typically offer high stability and precise anisotropic masking, however, with limited dynamic tunability. In contrast, the liquid‐gas interface (typically the air‐water boundary) offers a dynamic yet distinctively constrained environment for symmetry breaking. Unlike solid supports, the liquid‐gas interface takes advantage of the surface tension of the liquid phase to drive the self‐assembly of colloidal monolayers with long‐range order. Particles trapped at this interface equilibrate at a specific immersion depth, governed by the interplay between their intrinsic wettability and the subphase surface tension, which can be modulated via surfactant addition [35]. This will create an asymmetric masking effect, with one hemisphere shielded by the liquid phase, while the opposite hemisphere remains exposed to the gas phase. Fujii et al. showed that polystyrene grafted Au‐SiO2 Janus particles form monolayers at the air‐water boundary, with the hydrophobic PS‐grafted Au domains spontaneously oriented toward air, while the hydrophilic silica surface remained immersed in the water phase. This directed orientation could stabilize bubbles against coalescence for over a month [36], and, more importantly, provides a stable and well‐defined interfacial platform that can be advantageously exploited for subsequent patchy particle synthesis.

Utilizing the inherent asymmetry of the air‐water interface, Ji et al. utilized the liquid‐gas interface to direct the assembly of covalently bound twin single‐chain nanoparticles (TSCNPs). Similar to molecular surfactants and block copolymers, amphiphilic TSCNPs spontaneously orient at the liquid‐gas boundary to minimize surface energy. By positioning hydrophobic domains exposed to the air phase, while the hydrophilic moieties remain immersed in the aqueous phase, the water surface tension is effectively reduced, demonstrating that the phase boundary serves as a critical template for directing the orientation and subsequent assembly of complex polymeric nanostructures, thereby providing a useful platform for the controlled synthesis of patchy particles [37].

Beyond polymer systems, the liquid‐gas interface also provides an ideal platform for the asymmetric modification of two‐dimensional (2D) materials. It allows, for example, the design of Janus graphene monolayers, acting eventually as swimmers, at the water‐air interface using bipolar electrochemistry (vide infra) [34]. In this work, the 2D graphene monolayer floats at the interface, stabilized by surface tension, allowing highly site‐selective functionalization without the need for additional physical templates or direct electrical contacts. Using H2PtCl6 as a precursor, Pt metal clusters can be precisely electrodeposited onto the edges or corners of the monolayer graphene, driven by the external electric field (Figure 1C). Furthermore, by sequentially changing the precursor solution, a variety of metals or polymers can be deposited, enabling the synthesis of multifunctional monolayer graphene sheets with spatially distinct domains [38].

Compared to solid supports, liquid‐gas interfaces provide dynamic and self‐adjustable masking environments driven by wettability and surface tension, enabling scalable interfacial assembly. However, the inherently soft and fluctuating nature of these interfaces might limit spatial precision and reproducibility, particularly when aiming to construct well‐defined multi‐patch architectures.

Solid‐Liquid Interface

While solid‐gas interfaces offer precise control over patch geometry through shadow effects, they are typically restricted to materials compatible with physical or chemical vapor deposition (e.g., metals, metal oxides) and are constrained by the surface area of the 2D substrate. For the solid‐liquid interface approach, the range of applicable materials can be expanded. By conducting the synthesis in the liquid phase, a much broader variety of chemistries, including polymerization, sol‐gel processes, and bioconjugation, can be utilized to introduce complex functional patches (e.g., polymers, enzymes, surfactants) that are inaccessible via gas‐phase techniques. Typically, symmetry breaking is achieved either by physically masking a portion of the particle surface or by inducing site‐selective heterogeneous nucleation directly on the particle surface.

Surface Masking

The “masking” strategy involves partially embedding particles into a solid or semi‐solid substrate to block the diffusion of precursors to the protected interface, leaving only the exposed hemisphere available for modification. The pioneering work by Casagrande et al. utilized a planar solid interface where glass spheres were partially protected by a cellulose varnish. This preserved a hydrophilic character on one hemisphere, while the exposed hemisphere was chemically treated with octadecyltrichlorosilane, resulting in a uniform hydrophobic coating. An extension of this approach enables the formation of multi‐patch particles by repeating masking and modification steps or by controlling the extent and geometry of particle embedding. Specifically, partial embedding, combined with sequential functionalization, allows independent modification of different surface regions, yielding multiple patches with distinct properties. The aging time also plays a key role by governing the degree of embedding and the restructuring of the masking layer [39, 40]. This concept was further adapted for the bulk synthesis using wax‐based Pickering emulsions [41]. For this method, particles stabilize wax droplets in water. Upon cooling, the wax solidifies, locking the particles at the interface. Wu et al. used this strategy to mask one side of graphene oxide (GO) nanosheets, enabling asymmetric functionalization with amino‐containing chemicals [42]. Similarly, Kierulf et al. applied this technique to starch granules (Figure 2A). By embedding half of the granule in the solidified wax phase, they treated the uncovered water‐facing side with octenyl succinic anhydride before removing the wax coating to retrieve the Janus particles [43].

Apart from embedding, planar substrates can assist in asymmetric coating via solution processing. Sabapathy et al. reported a “dip‐coating” strategy to produce single‐ and multi‐patch particles (Figure 2B). A thin polymer film and a close‐packed monolayer of silica microparticles were simultaneously deposited on a substrate. Subsequent sonication disrupted the film, causing the polymer to adhere as a patch on one side of the particles, with the patch shape governed by the aging time of the polymer film prior to sonication [44].

Heterogeneous Nucleation and Surface Growth

Alternatively, the core particle itself can serve as the solid interface for site‐selective growth, relying on controlled nucleation kinetics rather than external masking. Bao et al. synthesized polystyrene nanospheres with tunable dendritic gold patches. Using surfactant‐free cationic polystyrene nanospheres as core particles, gold patches are produced through the in situ reduction of chloroauric acid with ascorbic acid [46]. Mane et al. reported TiO2‐modified asymmetric patchy particles. With asymmetric silica‐based starting materials, prepared via the Feyen approach, they obtained a site‐selective titania coating specifically on the silica portion of the particles [47]. Wang et al. reported the synthesis of Janus surface micelles on silica particles. By employing polymerization‐induced surface self‐assembly, they grew hydrophobic PS cores with positively charged poly(2‐(dimethylamino) ethyl methacrylate)/ poly(di(ethylene glycol) methyl ether methacrylate) (PDMAEMA/PDEGMA) patches directly on the silica surface, creating functional motifs suitable for enzyme immobilization [48]. Chang et al. demonstrated a simple yet effective method for synthesizing patchy particles via seed‐mediated heterogeneous nucleation (Figure 2C). The key to this procedure is the use of specific organosilane‐3‐(trimethoxysilyl)propyl methacrylate (TPM) or 3‐(trimethoxysilyl)propyl acrylate (TMSPA), which nucleate and grow into distinct patches on a variety of functional polymer seeds [45].

In addition, more sophisticated patchy particles with well‐defined anisotropic geometries have been synthesized. DNA‐functionalized patchy particles are formed via surface‐confined growth on colloidal templates, where localized regions of the particle surface are selectively exposed and functionalized. This site‐selective modification creates discrete DNA patches that enable directional and valence‐limited interactions [49]. Silica hexapods are also obtained by dispersing hematite cubic particles in a pentanol‐water system, where phase separation induces the preferential formation of water droplets on the particle faces. These droplets act as localized reaction sites, within which the silica precursor undergoes hydrolysis and condensation, leading to anisotropic growth of silica rods from each face. The resulting hexapod structure is thus formed via site‐specific heterogeneous nucleation, followed by surface growth at the solid‐liquid interface [50].

The above examples show that solid‐liquid interfaces offer high chemical versatility and compatibility with diverse material systems, enabling controlled surface functionalization and seeded growth, however, they may suffer from limited mass transport and interface‐dependent anisotropy.

Liquid‐Liquid Interface

Compared to the above discussed solid‐liquid approaches, symmetry breaking at liquid‐liquid interfaces offers the distinct advantage of allowing for the partitioning of precursors, which eventually are not compatible with each other, into separate phases, enabling independent growth or reaction kinetics in each phase. These interfaces can be topologically curved, as in emulsions and Pickering systems, or planar.

Emulsions

In emulsion systems, surfactants or emulsifiers, ranging from small molecules to polymers, adsorb at the oil‐water interface to reduce interfacial tension and provide electrostatic and/or steric stabilization. Since these interfaces can be sensitive to environmental stimuli (e.g., salt concentration, temperature, or pH), emulsion‐based techniques offer a versatile platform for synthesizing patchy particles.

Seeded Emulsion Polymerization

In a classic seeded emulsion polymerization process, the “seed” particle (a polymer latex, or an inorganic particle) is used as a confined microreactor for the growth of a second polymer phase. Typically, the method proceeds by preparing a stable seed dispersion, swelling the seed with a second monomer (M2), and then initiating polymerization so that the new polymer (P2) forms within or on the seed particle. Mechanistically, anisotropy arises when the seed particle and the new phase become thermodynamically incompatible during polymerization, leading to polymerization‐induced phase separation. As polymerization progresses, changes in composition, viscosity, and interfacial tension amplify and kinetically “lock in” this phase segregation. Meanwhile, differences in polarity and hydrophilicity govern the wetting behavior and interfacial curvature, thereby determining whether P2 evolves into a single domain or multiple surface patches. Recent studies have demonstrated various methods to manipulate this phase separation for targeted morphological control.

Lotierzo et al. showed that introducing nanogels during emulsion polymerization yields patchy latex particles decorated with multiple surface domains, with the particle size and patch surface density being adjustable via nanogel loading, pH, and salt concentration (Figure 3A) [51].

Expanding the scope to hybrid materials, Cao et al. reported a simple one‐step route to anisotropic organic‐inorganic hybrids by coupling sol‐gel chemistry with phase separation between polymer and inorganic precursors, producing patchy microcapsules where disc‐like polymer domains are dispersed within a silica matrix [52].

Furthermore, focusing on functional responsivity, Tu et al. combined polymerization‐induced phase separation with seeded emulsion polymerization to enable scalable synthesis of highly uniform, pH‐responsive Janus particles whose surface activity (wettability/amphiphilicity) can be reversibly switched with solution pH [53]. These works highlight that tailoring the interplay between phase separation thermodynamics and reaction conditions allows for the precise design of functional anisotropic colloids.

Reactions at Droplet Interfaces

Beyond internal polymerization, the oil‐water interface itself can serve as a dynamic template for directed assembly and mineralization. In these systems, anisotropy is often engineered by manipulating interfacial boundary conditions, such as controlling droplet fusion or inducing site‐specific interfacial co‐assembly. The interface acts not merely as a barrier, but as an active scaffold where chemical reactions or physical assembly are spatially confined to generate architectures.

Yan et al. reported an assembly strategy that enables precise control over the geometry of hollow nanocapsules by manipulating droplet‐fusion regions (Figure 3B). In their approach, plasmonic nanoparticles are patterned with soft polymer patches that adsorb water. These patches serve as “information sites” to direct droplet fusion upon collision. Subsequent anisotropic silica growth along the fused interfaces yields highly uniform, elongated hollow capsules [54]. Alternatively, focusing on interfacial stabilization, Luo et al. demonstrated a strategy to stabilize multiphase droplets via the co‐assembly of cellulose nanocrystals and amine‐functionalized polystyrene. The in situ formation of cellulose nanocrystal surfactants at specific interfacial regions imparts unique pH‐responsiveness, allowing the complex emulsions to reconfigure into Janus structures. These dynamic emulsions serve as versatile templates for the large‐scale synthesis of hollow, semi‐spherical, and spherical polymeric particles [55].

Solvent Evaporation

Solvent evaporation from emulsion droplets provides a pathway to generate complex morphologies via confined self‐assembly, particularly when using block copolymers. As the solvent evaporates from the dispersed phase, the increasing polymer concentration induces phase separation. However, unlike in bulk systems, the spherical curvature and the interactions at the droplet interface impose geometric constraints, forcing the separating phases into specific, often predictable, symmetry‐breaking arrangements.

Ku et al. utilized this principle to create colloidal patchy particles with diverse three‐dimensional shapes via the evaporation‐induced assembly of polystyrene‐b‐poly(4‐vinylpyridine) (PS‐b‐P4VP) block copolymers based on oil‐in‐water emulsions. They demonstrated that the final morphology is governed by the interplay between the degree of confinement (particle volume) and interfacial boundary conditions (Figure 3C). By modulating the interfacial interactions using a mixture of two surfactants, they achieved precise tuning of shapes, ranging from snowmen and dumbbells to triangles, tetrahedra, and raspberry‐like structures [56].

Extending this complexity to triblock terpolymers, Qiang et al. reported evaporation‐induced confinement assembly of polystyrene‐b‐polybutadiene‐b‐poly(methyl methacrylate) (PS‐b‐PB‐b‐PMMA) and tracked the morphological evolution during solvent‐adsorption annealing [57]. Using cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB), they first obtained ellipsoidal microparticles with axially stacked PS/PB/PMMA domains. In this case, CTAB acts as a cationic surfactant that stabilizes the emulsion droplets and promotes anisotropic confinement during solvent evaporation. After exchanging CTAB for poly(vinyl alcohol) (PVA) and applying chloroform vapor annealing, preferential interfacial localization of PMMA triggered a transition to spherical particles with concentric internal compartments and patchy surface topographies.

Pickering Emulsions

The key distinction between Pickering emulsions and classic emulsions lies in their stabilization mechanism. Unlike surfactant‐stabilized systems, Pickering emulsions are stabilized solely by the adsorption of solid micro‐ or nanoparticles at the water/organic (w/o) interface [58]. These particles form a dense, robust layer that significantly reduces interfacial energy, prevents coalescence, and slows down Ostwald ripening, a process by which smaller droplets dissolve and reorganize into larger ones due to differences in Laplace pressure, resulting in an overall increase in average droplet size. Consequently, the properties of the emulsion system and the resulting colloidal architectures are primarily determined by the characteristics of the stabilizing particles, such as their wettability, size, and surface functionality [59, 60, 61]. When solid particles adsorb at the liquid‐liquid interface, their surfaces are partitioned: one portion is exposed to the oil phase while the other resides in the aqueous phase. This spatial segregation allows for asymmetric modification, making Pickering emulsions a powerful platform for synthesizing Janus and patchy particles.

Ligand Segregation and Surface Functionalization

One strategy exploits the phase separation of immiscible ligands interacting with the particle surface. Andala et al. demonstrated that when Au nanoparticles are mixed with two competing ligands (hydrophobic dodecanethiol and hydrophilic mercaptoundecanoic acid) at a water/toluene interface, the system minimizes interfacial energy by spatially segregating the ligands (Figure 4A). This process yields nanoparticles with homogeneous cores but patchy surface chemistries, where the assembly is driven by the decrease of the liquid‐liquid interfacial energy and controlled by the ligand ratio [62].

Interfacial Chemical Reactions

A more direct approach involves triggering chemical reactions selectively at the exposed surface in either the oil or aqueous phase. Gu et al. synthesized Fe3O4‐Ag heterodimers via the self‐catalyzed reduction of Ag+ and subsequent nucleation of silver exclusively on the water‐facing side of the particles (Figure 4B) [63]. Similarly, He et al. synthesized Au patchy particles and nanocups by dissolving precursors in separate phases: HAuCl4 in water and 2‐ethoxyaniline (EOA) in hexane. At the interface, a redox reaction occurred where EOA reduced Au (III) to form Au seeds, while HAuCl4 initiated the oxidative polymerization of EOA, resulting in conjugated polymer nanoparticles attached to Au seeds [64]. Furthermore, Liu et al. reported an interface‐directed self‐assembly method where citrate‐stabilized AuNPs (aqueous) meet thiol‐terminated polystyrene (PS‐SH, in toluene) at the interface. The PS‐SH chains selectively get grafted onto the oil‐facing side of the AuNPs, rendering them amphiphilic and effectively generating Janus AuNPs [65].

Dual‐Phase Reactions

Beyond single‐sided modifications, distinct reactions can be triggered simultaneously in both phases. Kirillova et al. synthesized Janus platelets by stabilizing an emulsion with initiator‐modified kaolinite particles (Figure 4C). Upon initiation, hydrophilic and hydrophobic polymers were grafted simultaneously onto opposite faces of the platelets [66].

Assembly at Planar Liquid‐Liquid Interfaces

The above‐mentioned liquid‐liquid interfaces provide soft confinement and dynamic environments that facilitate the formation of complex and multi‐patch structures, but their inherent spatio‐temporal instability and sensitivity to interfacial conditions often compromise reproducibility and precise control. Compared to emulsion systems, which require meticulous formulation to maintain kinetic stability (often necessitating specific surfactants or solid stabilizers), the planar water‐organic interface offers a thermodynamically more stable and spatially extended 2D platform. This macroscopic stability provides more freedom to accommodate complex reaction kinetics without the constraints of droplet coalescence or Ostwald ripening, thereby broadening the scope of reactions available for patchy particle design.

The most direct strategy involves compartmentalizing reactive precursors in immiscible phases, allowing them to interact exclusively at the boundary. Li et al. reported a mass‐transfer‐assisted self‐assembly method driven by the “Ouzo effect” [67]. When a planar interface is formed between an aqueous phase and a mixed organic solution (chloroform/DMF) containing polystyrene‐b‐polyacrylic acid (PS‐b‐PAA), immediate mass transfer across the interface triggers spontaneous emulsification (Figure 5A). Depending on the organic solvent, this process yields diverse morphologies, including uniform nanowires, hollow spheres, and foam‐like structures. Such an interfacial self‐assembly provides a straightforward access to structural and chemical anisotropy, as the assembly is confined to a liquid‐liquid boundary. This spatial confinement can lead to an asymmetric distribution of components within the resulting structures, which is a key prerequisite for the formation of patchy particles, and illustrates how planar liquid‐liquid interfaces can be exploited to regulate, for example, block copolymer organization toward anisotropic and potentially patchy architectures.

(A) Schematic presentation of the formation process of PS‐b‐PAA/DAH nanowires. Reproduced with permission from [67]. (B) Synthesis of non‐spherical patchy particles at liquid‐liquid interfaces via differential deformation and their self‐assembly. Reproduced with permission from [68].

Beyond chemical synthesis, the planar interface can also induce physical symmetry breaking. As previously discussed in the context of Pickering emulsions, Sabapathy et al. utilized a flat decane‐water interface to synthesize non‐spherical Janus particles [68]. By heating charged polystyrene (PS) spheres trapped at the interface, the particles undergo unequal deformation due to partial swelling and interfacial tension. This results in particles with dual anisotropy, non‐spherical shapes, and asymmetric charge distributions, where the degree of deformation could be precisely tuned by surface‐active additives. Acorn‐ and idly‐shaped particles, as well as acorn‐shaped patchy particles, have been successfully synthesized (Figure 5B).

The planar interface also serves as an ideal template for the assembly of 2D materials. Robertson et al. demonstrated the synthesis of organic‐inorganic hybrid nanosheets using octadecanethiol (ODT)‐functionalized gold nanoparticles [69]. Through a unique biphasic monolayer adsorption and collapse mechanism at the toluene‐water interface, they achieved the assembly of nanoparticles (ranging from 10 to 80 nm) into cohesive, free‐standing hybrid nanosheets.

Bipolar Electrochemistry at a Planar Liquid‐Liquid Interface

Classic liquid‐liquid interfacial strategies for patchy particle synthesis have primarily focused on polymers, colloidal metal nanoparticles, and metal oxides. However, these methods require a careful system design to satisfy two critical conditions: first, particles must possess appropriate surface ligands to maintain a suitable contact angle at the interface; second, the reactants and solvents in both phases must be judiciously selected to ensure proper mass transfer during interfacial reactions (e.g., polymerization or aggregation). A minor change in surface chemistry or solvent polarity can drastically alter the assembly behavior, making it challenging to adapt a single protocol to diverse material classes. Due to the characteristics of planar interfaces, external physical fields (such as an electric field), can be introduced to switch the synthesis strategy. The Dryfe group has explored polarized liquid‐liquid interfaces, with a voltammetric control over interfacial charge transfer, to regulate material formation [70]. By precisely tuning the interfacial potential difference, both ion transfer and electron transfer can be directed on demand [71, 72]. This strategy has also been successfully applied to the controlled assembly of noble metals (e.g., Pd, Au) on free‐standing graphene monolayers at the interface, establishing that external electrical driving forces can offer a better control over particle growth compared to spontaneous chemical processes [73]. In this context, bipolar electrochemistry (BPE) has also been proposed more recently at planar w/o interfaces as an additional alternative and versatile route to synthesize conducting patchy particles.

In a typical BPE system, as shown in Figure 6A, a conducting bipolar electrode (BE) is placed between two feeder electrodes in an electrolyte solution. When the potential gradient induced by the feeder electrodes is sufficiently strong, simultaneous electrochemical oxidation and reduction reactions are triggered at opposite extremities of the BE [74, 77, 78]. Due to its unique features, BPE is useful for many applications ranging from materials sciences [79, 80] to analysis [81, 82, 83] and chemical or biochemical synthesis [84, 85, 86]. With respect to patchy particle synthesis, glassy carbon (GC) beads have been used as model substrates. Initially, the beads were modified with a hydrophobic gradient via electrografting of a trifluoromethyl diazonium salt in a single‐phase BPE setup. These amphiphilic beads were then positioned at a w/o interface, with their hydrophilic and hydrophobic parts spontaneously orienting toward the respective phases. By applying an electric field perpendicular to the interface, the beads get polarized, triggering simultaneous electroreduction in the aqueous phase and electrooxidation in the organic phase. As a proof of concept, Cu/polyaniline (PANI) Janus microbeads were synthesized by electroreduction of Cu2+ in water and electrooxidation of aniline in the organic phase (Figure 6B). Importantly, the functionalization ratio between the two components can be fine‐tuned by adjusting the size of the initial hydrophobic region [75].

Building up on this first set of results, the method can be extended to multicomponent modification with precise spatial control. Usually, achieving asymmetric modification of particles with various patches of different composition and size at predefined positions is quite challenging and requires multiple steps [87]. This drawback can be circumvented by applying an electric field parallel to the w/o interface, so that multiple bipolar redox reactions can be triggered simultaneously in both phases. Through an appropriate selection of precursor solutions, this configuration allows for the one‐pot single‐step preparation of patchy particles with up to four distinct components (Cu, MnO2‐PANI, Cu‐PANI, Cu‐MnO2‐PANI, and Cu‐MnO2‐Au‐PANI), which is impossible with any other conventional method (Figure 6C). In analogy to the well‐known Janus particles referring to a Roman god, such objects might be called Brahma particles, named after the Hindu god with four faces, being also considered as the creator of the Univers. Unlike classic bipolar electrodeposition, for which the areas of deposition are determined by the intrinsic threshold potential of the involved electrochemical reactions, this method enables fine‐tuning of the spatial distribution via the introduction of sacrificial reagents. The versatility of the approach can be further demonstrated by its successful application to two‐dimensional PEDOT films, establishing it as a quite universal platform for multi‐component surface modification of diverse materials [76].

Liquid–liquid interfaces provide unique opportunities for symmetry breaking through thermodynamic partitioning and dual‐phase reactions, enabling a high degree of structural complexity and multi‐component integration. However, these systems are highly sensitive to interfacial conditions, such as solvent composition and particle wettability, which indicates that precise control and scalable implementation still require further development.

Outlooks

This review discussed the role of different phase boundaries as fundamental platforms for breaking the symmetry in the frame of the synthesis of patchy particles. By exploiting the distinct constraints and thermodynamic conditions characteristic of solid‐gas, liquid‐gas, solid‐liquid, and liquid‐liquid boundaries, researchers have established a large diversity of approaches for controlling the degree of asymmetry of particles. These interfacial strategies effectively circumvent the stochastic nature of bulk synthesis, providing the necessary physical confinement or chemical partitioning to direct site‐selective modifications. However, to fully benefit from the potential of these patchy particles in practical applications, several critical limitations regarding structural complexity, scalability, and material diversity need to be addressed.

The first major task is related to the transition from mono‐functional to multi‐functional architectures. Most of the current methodologies yield particles composed of only two distinct hemispheres, but advanced applications may require more sophisticated multicomponent systems. The integration of three or more distinct domains (e.g., with catalytic, magnetic, and optical functionalities) onto a single particle is essential for enabling synergistic features, such as catalytic cascades and multiplexed signal transduction. Shifting the focus from structural asymmetry to programmable functionality should allow these particles to perform complex tasks that are currently out of reach for simple Janus particles.

A specific technical challenge in achieving this complexity is the translation of precision synthesis from the micro‐scale to the nano‐scale. Even though recent advances show that it is possible to precisely control patch topology and to elaborate multi‐patch structures on micrometer‐sized glassy carbon beads, for example, by bipolar electrochemistry at a liquid/liquid interface [76], extending this degree of controllability to sub‐micron or nano‐dimensions remains very challenging. At the nanoscale, the dominating Brownian motion, for example, severely hinders precise regional modification. This is because the thermal energy (kT) translates into higher velocity fluctuations for smaller particles, inversely proportional to their lower mass, which leads to rapid random motion. Consequently, nanosized objects experience stronger stochastic perturbations compared to their microscale counterparts, making precise spatial control and localized modification considerably more difficult. Overcoming these roadblocks to create multifunctional nanoparticles with the same level of architectural sophistication as the one at the micron‐scale is far from straightforward.

Beyond structural control, scalability represents another critical bottleneck for the practical deployment of patchy particles. Currently, most of the interfacial synthesis strategies rely on finely tuned laboratory‐scale conditions that are difficult to translate into high‐throughput or industrially relevant processes. Achieving uniformity, reproducibility, and cost‐efficiency at a large scale remains challenging, particularly when precise control over patch size, composition, and spatial distribution is required. Future efforts should therefore focus on developing continuous‐flow or parallelized interfacial systems to enable a scalable production of complex patchy particles without compromising structural integrity.

Reaching these goals most likely requires breakthroughs in terms of methodology that go beyond the simple use of interfaces as “templates”. In this context, employing additional ingredients, such as light or magnetic fields, for interfacial synthesis offers promising perspectives. These external stimuli may allow manipulating particle orientation and modulating local reaction kinetics, which should enable more precise synthesis and the design of novel objects that are otherwise inaccessible.

Continued progress in synthesis, scalability, and functional integration will be essential to move closer to practical use. Multifunctional patchy particles are expected to enable advanced applications such as cascade catalysis and multifunctional micro‐ and nanomotors by exploiting their ability to spatially segregate domains with distinct functions, thereby offering a broad potential in areas including, among others, targeted drug delivery, sensing, and smart materials.
