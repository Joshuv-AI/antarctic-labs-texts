Paradoxical coronary embolism through an atrial septal defect presenting as acute myocardial infarction: A case report
Authors: Mehraj Uid Din Khan, Aamir Rashid, Sameer Purra, Syed Bilal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Paradoxical embolism through an atrial septal defect (ASD) is a rare but important cause of acute coronary syndrome (ACS) in patients without conventional cardiovascular risk factors. We report the case of a 40-year-old male with no history of hypertension, diabetes, smoking, dyslipidemia, or family history of premature coronary artery disease who presented with acute chest pain and ischemic electrocardiographic changes. Basic thrombophilia profile screening was nonaffirmative. Troponin-T testing using a point-of-care and quantitative assay was positive. Coronary angiography revealed angiographically normal epicardial coronary arteries. Transthoracic and transoesophageal echocardiography demonstrated a large ostium secundum ASD measuring 22 mm × 24 mm, with a prominent Eustachian valve and significant right-to-left shunting on contrast study, accentuated during the Valsalva maneuver. The patient underwent successful transcatheter ASD closure with complete abolition of shunting. This case highlights paradoxical coronary embolism as a potential mechanism of ACS in young, low-risk individuals and underscores the importance of evaluating intracardiac shunts in patients presenting with ACS and nonobstructive coronaries.

INTRODUCTION

Atrial septal defect (ASD) is one of the most common congenital heart defects, accounting for approximately 25% of cases in children. Potential complications include atrial arrhythmias, pulmonary arterial hypertension, right-sided heart failure, and systemic embolic events such as transient ischemic attack or stroke.[1] The incidence of coronary embolism is unknown but is estimated to be anywhere between 0.06% and 3% of all acute coronary syndrome (ACS).[2] Paradoxical coronary artery embolism is a rare and often underdiagnosed cause of acute myocardial infarction and should be considered in patients presenting with myocardial infarction despite a low risk for atherosclerotic coronary artery disease.[3]

Coronary embolism accounts for a small proportion of ACS cases, particularly in younger patients without traditional cardiovascular risk factors. Recognition of this entity is clinically important, as management strategies differ from atherosclerotic disease and definitive treatment may involve closure of the intracardiac shunt. We describe a case of ACS in a young, low-risk individual in whom paradoxical coronary embolism through a large ASD was considered the most likely etiology.

CASE REPORT

A 40-year-old male, nonhypertensive, nondiabetic, and a nonsmoker, was referred from a peripheral hospital with sudden-onset retrosternal chest pain associated with tightness, dyspnoea, and profuse sweating. An electrocardiogram (ECG) recorded in emergency showed hyperacute T-waves in leads II, III, and aVF, with subtle ST depression in v1–v4 [Figure 1], which later normalized [Figure 2]. Troponin-T test by the kit method was positive. Quantitative troponin I assay was 92 ng/L that was available subsequently. He was admitted as a case of ACS and initiated on the standard medical management.

Follow-up ECG demonstrating normalization with Q waves in III and aVF

Coronary angiography (CAG) demonstrated normal coronary arteries [Figure 3a-c]. Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) revealed a large ostium secundum ASD, measuring 22 mm × 24 mm with predominant left-to-right shunt and a prominent Eustachian valve. There was marked dilation of the right atrium and right ventricle with mild pulmonary hypertension.

Coronary angiography, (a and b) demonstrate a normal left coronary system, while (c) shows a normal right coronary artery

Deep-vein thrombosis (DVT) screening was performed and revealed negative venous Doppler ultrasound; thrombophilia profile revealed normal protein C and S values, negative gene mutation for Factor V Leiden, and prothrombin gene. Homocysteine levels were on the upper limit of normal values.

The patient was planned for elective percutaneous ASD closure after 20 days of the myocardial infarction. During the procedure, transoesophageal echocardiography with agitated saline contrast administered through the right femoral vein confirmed a large ASD with prominent Eustachian valve with significant shunting of bubbles across the defect was seen at baseline and further increased with the Valsalva maneuver [Figures 4 and 5a, b and Videos 1 and 2]. Right heart catheterization showed a mean right atrial pressure of 8 mmHg and pulmonary artery pressures of 41/22 mmHg (mean 28 mmHg).

Transoesophageal echocardiography image demonstrates large atrial septal defect with prominent Eustachian valve

Bubble contrast study showing baseline (a) and valsalva-induced right-to-left shunt (b)

The ASD was successfully closed using a 28-mm Amplatzer septal occluder device [Figure 6]. Repeat bubble contrast study after device deployment demonstrated complete abolition of right-to-left shunt. The patient’s postprocedural recovery was uneventful.

DISCUSSION

ASD is one of the most frequently diagnosed congenital heart diseases in adults, accounting for up to 30% of adult congenital heart disease presentations.[1] While many individuals remain asymptomatic into adulthood, chronic interatrial shunting can lead to right atrial and ventricular dilatation, pulmonary arterial hypertension, atrial arrhythmias, and, less commonly, paradoxical embolic events. Cerebrovascular embolism is the most frequently reported manifestation; paradoxical coronary embolism, however, remains a distinctly rare and under-recognized cause of ACS.[23]

Coronary embolism is estimated to account for approximately 0.06%–3% of all ACS presentations and is more commonly encountered in younger patients without traditional atherosclerotic risk factors.[4] Paradoxical embolism represents a subset of these cases, most often occurring through a patent foramen ovale (PFO); however, large secundum ASDs can also serve as a conduit for embolic transit under favorable hemodynamic conditions.[5] Alenezi et al. described a case of paradoxical coronary artery embolism causing ST-elevation myocardial infarction in a patients with PFO.[3] A comparable case was described by Lin et al., who reported a paradoxical embolism leading to nonST-segment elevation myocardial infarction due to embolic occlusion of the distal right posterior descending artery in a patient with an underlying sinus venosus ASD and partial anomalous pulmonary venous drainage.[5] In most cases, emboli originate from the deep venous system; however, the source may remain unidentified.

In the present case, the patient’s young age and absence of hypertension, diabetes, smoking, dyslipidemia, or family history of premature coronary artery disease made an atherosclerotic mechanism unlikely and prompted investigation for alternative causes. Thrombophilia profile and DVT screening tests were nonaffirmative.

The diagnosis of paradoxical coronary embolism in this patient remains presumptive, based on a constellation of supportive but indirect findings. These include angiographically normal epicardial coronary arteries, ischemic electrocardiographic changes with troponin positivity, absence of conventional thromboembolic risk factors, and the presence of a large ostium secundum ASD with demonstrable right-to-left shunting on contrast echocardiography – particularly during the Valsalva maneuver. Transient reversal of the interatrial pressure gradient during coughing, straining, or Valsalva can allow venous emboli to cross into the systemic circulation and embolize the coronary arteries.[56]

The presence of a prominent Eustachian valve, as seen in this case, may further increase the risk of paradoxical embolism by directing inferior vena caval blood flow toward the interatrial septum. Although Eustachian valve-related shunting alone is unlikely to cause significant hypoxaemia or symptoms, it may act as an important facilitating structure for embolic transit in the presence of an interatrial defect.[7] Previous reports have demonstrated the resolution of shunt-related sequelae following ASD closure in patients with prominent Eustachian valves.[7]

No atrial or ventricular arrhythmias were documented on serial electrocardiograms or continuous telemetry monitoring, reducing the likelihood of in situ intracardiac thrombus formation. There was no clinical evidence of venous thromboembolism, and lower-limb Doppler screening or thrombophilia testing was negative. Failure to identify an embolic source is not uncommon in reported cases of paradoxical embolism.[5]

The patient demonstrated ischemic ECG changes with positive troponin testing, consistent with ACS. TTE did not reveal regional wall-motion abnormalities, a finding that may be explained by transient ischemia, early spontaneous reperfusion, or a small embolic infarct. Similar discordance between ECG changes and echocardiographic findings has been reported in embolic and myocardial infarction with nonobstructive coronary arteries (MINOCA)-related events.[8]

Alternative diagnoses such as MINOCA and Takotsubo cardiomyopathy were considered. Takotsubo cardiomyopathy was deemed unlikely due to the absence of characteristic apical or mid-ventricular wall-motion abnormalities and lack of an identifiable emotional or physical stress trigger. MINOCA represents a broad diagnostic entity encompassing multiple mechanisms; however, the presence of a large ASD with documented right-to-left shunting provides a plausible embolic mechanism, making paradoxical coronary embolism the most likely explanation in this clinical context.[89]

Although mild pulmonary hypertension was present (mean pulmonary artery pressure 28 mmHg), this degree of elevation is insufficient to cause fixed extrinsic coronary compression. While transient pulmonary pressure surges during Valsalva maneuvers may accentuate shunting, dynamic coronary compression is unlikely. ECG-gated computed tomography CAG with dynamic assessment was not performed, as invasive CAG demonstrated normal epicardial vessels and the patient remained clinically stable. This has been acknowledged as a diagnostic limitation.

Percutaneous device closure of the ASD was undertaken in accordance with guideline recommendations due to significant shunting and right heart volume overload.[10] Successful closure resulted in complete abolition of right-to-left shunt on postprocedural contrast study. In addition to preventing future paradoxical embolic events, closure also addresses the long-term hemodynamic consequences of ASD.

This case highlights the importance of considering structural cardiac anomalies as potential etiologies in young patients presenting with ACS and angiographically normal coronaries. While definitive proof of paradoxical coronary embolism is often elusive, recognition of this mechanism has important therapeutic implications, as closure of the interatrial defect can be both preventive and potentially curative.

CONCLUSION

Paradoxical coronary embolism through an ASD is an uncommon but important cause of myocardial infarction in patients with angiographically normal coronary arteries. Early recognition and appropriate closure of the defect can prevent recurrence and improve outcomes. A high index of suspicion for paradoxical embolism in atypical presentations of ACS, particularly in young, low-risk individuals should always be maintained.
