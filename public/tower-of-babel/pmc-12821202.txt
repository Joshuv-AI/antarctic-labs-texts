Single-cell and spatial atlas unveil tumor-specific microenvironment convergence and a prognosis-associated PLXDC1+ myofibroblast population in metastatic bladder cancer
Authors: Ziwei Wang, Jiaying Miao, Maoyu Wang, Yidie Ying, Zhensheng Zhang, Shuxiong Zeng, Chuanliang Xu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundWhile single-cell technologies have advanced bladder cancer research, the microenvironment of lymph node metastases and its role in progression remain unclear. We aimed to elucidate dynamic tumor-microenvironment interactions across metastatic and primary sites, identify poor-prognosis features, and uncover novel therapeutic targets.MethodsWe profiled matched single-cell and spatial transcriptomic samples from lymph node-metastasized bladder cancer to construct a high-resolution atlas. Bioinformatic analyses were used to inferred ligand-receptor interactions, regulatory networks, and spatial co-localization. Integration with multiple prognostic cohorts revealed key cell subpopulations linked to tumor biology and poor outcomes.ResultsOur atlas included 32 samples, 61 cell subtypes, key ligand signals (e.g., TGFB1, OSM, IFNG), 343 transcription factors, 188 co-localization events, over 1,000 spatially validated interactions, and 21 cell niches. Tumor-driven convergence was observed across distinct microenvironments. Notably, a pericyte-like myofibroblast subtype, myoCAF_PLXDC1, was enriched in malignant tissues, associated with poor prognosis, and exhibited enhanced angiogenic but reduced chemotactic activity compared to myoCAF_CCL21. Its phenotype was linked to intracellular regulators (PRRX1 and MXD4) and extracellular signals (IFNG and TGFB1). Spatially, myoCAF_PLXDC1 co-localized with ITGA8+ endothelial cells and enriched in tumor regions.ConclusionsThis study offers a comprehensive resource for lymph node-metastasized bladder cancer and identifies PLXDC1+ fibroblasts co-localized with ITGA8+ ECs as potential components of a tumor-specific vascular niche and therapeutic target.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-025-07534-8.

Background

Bladder cancer is one of the most common malignancies worldwide. Although non-muscle-invasive bladder cancer (NMIBC) has an overall favorable prognosis, the 5-year overall survival (OS) rate for patients with metastatic urothelial carcinoma (mUC) is only 27.7% [1] despite substantial endeavors to develop anti-metastatic therapies [2, 3].

Pelvic lymph nodes are the most common metastatic sites for bladder cancer, but metastases often remain occult: approximately 25% of patients are found to have lymph node involvement only after radical surgery, even among those preoperatively diagnosed with organ-confined bladder cancer. The mechanisms driving early occult metastasis in bladder cancer remain unclear. In recent years, immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), representative therapy targeting the tumor microenvironment, have been successfully incorporated into first-line treatment of metastatic bladder cancer, significantly improving upon the traditional platinum-based chemotherapy paradigm [4, 5]. In contrast, other therapeutic strategies aimed at components of the tumor microenvironment—such as anti-angiogenic therapies, targeting pro-tumor cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs), or tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs)—have yet to achieve substantial clinical success in the first-line setting, partly due to insufficient understanding of the complex tumor microenvironment (TME) characteristics in mUC.

Large-scale bulk RNA sequencing and molecular profiling studies have provided a critical foundation for precision medicine in bladder cancer. However, bulk RNA-seq is strongly influenced by tumor purity, can only capture overall tissue transcriptional characteristics, and struggles to dissect intratumoral cellular heterogeneity due to interference from stromal components. In contrast, single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) overcomes these limitations by enabling high-resolution, unbiased analysis of the phenotypes and abundances of diverse cell types within tumor tissues.

ScRNA-seq is currently widely used in microenvironmental research across multiple tumor types, driving the development of novel precision treatment strategies. Existing scRNA-seq studies in bladder cancer have preliminarily revealed complex microenvironmental heterogeneity in primary tumors, but systematic single-cell studies simultaneously covering primary and metastatic sites in metastatic bladder cancer are lacking [6, 7]. Consequently, the composition and state of the TME in metastatic lesions remain largely unknown. Additionally, current clinical treatment decisions for bladder cancer (including targeted therapies) often rely on analyses of primary tumors (PRI-T) rather than metastatic characteristics [8].

This study aims to systematically characterize the composition and features of the TME in metastatic bladder cancer by integrating scRNA-seq and spatial transcriptome sequencing (ST-seq) data from primary tumors and metastatic lesions of metastatic bladder cancer patients. By identifying key microenvironmental factors influencing treatment response in metastases, this research seeks to provide a new biological foundation and potential intervention targets for optimizing therapeutic strategies for metastatic bladder cancer.

Methods

Sample information

Fresh surgical specimens were obtained from patients undergoing radical cystectomy and lymph node dissection at our center. PRI-T samples were collected from macroscopically evident tumor regions, whereas paracancerous normal (PRI-N) samples were taken from areas at least 3 cm away from the tumor margin without visible lesions. Lymph node metastasis (LN-T) and normal lymph node (LN-N) samples were identified by palpation, and the presence of tumor infiltration was evaluated using rapid frozen-section hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining. Pathological staging was determined according to the 8th edition of the AJCC TNM classification for bladder cancer. The main clinical characteristics and treatment histories of the 32 samples from 11 patients are summarized in Table S1.

Quality control and atlas construction

Raw FASTQ files were processed with Cell Ranger v7.0 and the hg38 reference genome. Data were imported using Seurat v5 [9], followed by preprocessing with Seurat’s built-in functions including normalization, identification of highly variable genes (HVGs), scaling, and principal component analysis (PCA). To mitigate batch effects from sample sources and identify cell types consistent across samples, the Harmony algorithm [10] was applied for inter-sample integration when merging multiple samples.

To generate a high-quality single-cell atlas, two experts with biological and bioinformatics expertise performed multiple rounds of integrative iteration to form a consensus on cell types. Specifically, the first-round annotation classified cells into major categories, e.g., NKT, B cells, endothelial cells (EC), epithelial cells (Epi), myeloid cells (MonoMacro), cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAF), based on the expression of canonical marker genes. For the second round, major cell categories underwent integration and unsupervised clustering, followed by annotation of resulting clusters. Cells expressing markers of more than one lineage (based on biological knowledge) were labeled as doublets, while clusters with extremely high mitochondrial gene proportions (excluding mitochondrial genes) and lacking other marker genes were annotated as ‘low_qual’. Both doublets and ‘low_qual’cells were excluded from subsequent analyses. Cells inconsistent with their major cluster identity (based on marker gene expression) were re-annotated to their correct major cluster. This process was repeated until integration and unsupervised clustering of major cell categories no longer generated new low_qual or doublet clusters, and all subtypes were consistent with their major cluster identities. A few major cell clusters with extremely low cell numbers (n < 50) in some samples were not included in integration and unsupervised clustering and were defined as ‘undefined’. Thereafter, a total of 25,811 low-quality cells (9.0%) and 16,962 doublet cells (5.9%) were found. For the remaining 244,239 cells, 97.4% of the cells were successfully annotated, and 6191 cells were left unannotated (undefined) due to their scarcity in individual samples (Figure S1).

During cell subtype definition, ten different resolution values (0.1, 0.2, …, 0.9, 1) were tested, and marker genes of subtypes at each resolution were analyzed. Final resolutions were selected as follows: 0.7 for CD4+ T cells, 0.8 for CD8+ T cells, 0.3 for NK cells, 0.4 for iCAF, 0.4 for myoCAF, 0.1 for EC, 0.5 for B cells, and 0.4 for Mono/Macro. Subtype identities were determined based on top marker genes of each cluster. Unsupervised subtypes expressing similar markers were merged and annotated collectively (Supplementary Notes).

Microenvironment principal component analysis (PCA)

To investigate microenvironmental differences across samples, the abundances of all non-epithelial cell types were scaled to sum to 1. PCA was then performed on the scaled cell proportion matrix (sample*cell subtype), generating a sample*principal component (PC) matrix. The loadings of different cell types on PC1 and PC2 were interpreted as their contributions to the PCs, with positive/negative values indicating the direction of contribution. As a complementary approach, we performed PCA based on pseudobulk profiles of non-epithelial compartments as well as on the cluster proportion matrix derived from unsupervised clustering.

Differential expression analysis (DEA)

To identify highly specific differentially expressed genes (DEGs) for each minor cell type, we developed a stringent three-step DEG filtering strategy based on Seurat objects. The analysis was performed on an integrated single-cell transcriptomic dataset annotated with detailed subclusters and their corresponding major cell types. For each target subcluster (ident_1), we conducted the following three comparisons:

Outer Comparison: ident_1 was compared against all subclusters from other major cell types to identify genes differentially expressed from non-related cells;

Outer-similar Comparison: ident_1 was compared with functionally or transcriptionally similar subclusters in other major cell types, aiming to exclude nonspecific marker genes;

Inner Comparison: ident_1 was compared with other subclusters within the same major cell type to exclude genes that are ubiquitously expressed within the category, thereby preserving subcluster-specific markers.

Each comparison utilized Seurat’s FindMarkers function with parameters set to min.pct = 0.2 and a maximum of 2,000 cells per group. Only upregulated genes with adjusted p < 0.05 and avg_log2FC > 1 were retained. The final set of highly specific marker genes was obtained by intersecting the DEG lists from all three comparisons. This strategy maximizes specificity while minimizing false positives caused by inter-cluster similarity, cross-lineage expression, or intra-lineage ubiquity.

Cell type enrichment analysis

To assess the enrichment or depletion of different cell types across tissue types, we computed a Ro/e (ratio of observed to expected) matrix based on a contingency table of cell types versus tissue types. From the contingency table, we first calculated the total number of cells per cell type (row sum), per tissue type (column sum), and the grand total of all cells. Assuming independence between cell type and tissue type, the expected count for cell type i in tissue j is calculated as: Expectedij = (Total cells of type i × Total cells in tissue j)/Total cells; Then Ro/e values were computed by element-wise division: Ro/eij = Observedij/Expectedij. Ro/e > 1 indicates enrichment of a cell type in a tissue, while Ro/e < 1 indicates underrepresentation.

Transcriptional regulatory network analysis

We inferred transcriptional regulatory networks using pySCENIC [11] (http://github.com/aertslab/pySCENIC), which identifies regulons comprising transcription factors (TFs) and their putative target genes. The list of TFs was obtained from a published study. To improve computational efficiency on large datasets, we applied SuperCell [12] (http://github.com/GfellerLab/SuperCell) to generate metacells, with parameters set to gamma = 20, n.var.genes = 2000, k.knn = 5, and n.pc = 30. The resulting metacell-by-gene expression matrix was used as input for pySCENIC. TF activity scores (range: 0–1) for individual cells were computed using AUCell. AUCell scores were scaled and subjected to PCA. The top 50 PCs were used for UMAP dimensionality reduction and visualization. DEA was performed at both lineage and subtype levels using the Wilcoxon test. TFs were filtered out if they had a log2 fold change < 0.585, normalized mean expression < 1, or expression ratio < 0.15.

NicheNet analysis

To systematically characterize ligand-mediated regulations among cell subtypes across lineages, we applied the NicheNet [13] (http://github.com/saeyslab/nichenetr) framework to infer ligand activity at the single-cell subcluster level and constructed a weighted intercellular communication network. For each minor subtype, background genes were defined as those broadly expressed within its corresponding major lineage (avg.exp > 0.05 and pct.exp > 15%). Genes of interest (GOI) were defined as subtype-specific upregulated genes (avg_log2FC > 0.585 and FDR < 0.05) identified via DEA. For each minor subtype, ligand activities were predicted using the predict_ligand_activities() function, with GOI and background genes as input. Ligands were ranked by corrected AUPR (area under the precision-recall curve), and the top 10 were retained for downstream analysis. Top-ranked ligands and their AUPR scores were compiled across all minor subtypes. Ligands regulating more than five subtypes were defined as hub ligands.

Cell abundance estimation

For 10X Visium spatial transcriptomics (ST) data, we applied cell2location [14] (http://github.com/BayraktarLab/cell2location) to estimate the absolute abundance of each cell subtype per spot. The reference single-cell data was generated from the same center. Parameters were set to N_cells_per_location = 10 and detection_alpha = 20. cell2location outputs continuous absolute abundance values, representing the total RNA contribution from each cell subtype per spot, enabling cross-spot comparability within the same tissue section.

For bulk RNA-seq data, we used BayesPrism [15] (http://github.com/Danko-Lab/BayesPrism) to infer the relative abundance of cell subtypes in each sample. The reference was our in-house single-cell atlas, which includes ground truth proportions of each cell type. Pseudobulk expression matrices were constructed accordingly and subjected to BayesPrism deconvolution analysis. We also used marker genes of each subtype to estimate relative activity scores across samples in each cohort using GSVA package. We found the deconvolution algorithm reliably estimate the abundance levels of most cell subtypes (Figure S6A), and that the subtype-marker genes were specifically expressed in the corresponding cell subtypes (Figure S6B).

Cellular niche identification

We performed unsupervised clustering on ST spots for cellular niches based on the cell-type composition matrix deconvoluted by cell2location. The matrix was isometric log-ratio transformed before subjected to PCA for dimensionality reduction, followed by Harmony integration to correct for batch effects across samples. Clustering was then performed on the batch-corrected embeddings using the unsupervised clustering framework implemented in Seurat, thereby defining distinct spatial niches.

Based on the dominant cell-type composition and niche-specific marker genes, each spot cluster was categorized into three major classes: epithelium-associated (epi), stroma-associated (stroma), and immune-associated (lymph). To further explore intra-class heterogeneity, we performed subclustering within each major class.

To understand characteristics of cellular niches, niche-specific DEGs and differentially enriched cell types (DECTs) were identified following the same strategy as in the single-cell transcriptomic analysis. Specifically, each subcluster was compared against (i) niches from other major classes (e.g., epi vs. stroma+lymph) and (ii) other subclusters within the same class (e.g., epi_1 vs. epi_others). Genes or cell types consistently upregulated in both comparisons (p < 0.05 and mean difference > 0.01 for DECTs; logFC > 1 for DEGs) were defined as niche-specific. To evaluate site-specific differences in subniche proportions between PRI-T and LN-T samples, a heuristic annotation was applied per patient: subniches consistently higher in PRI-T or LN-T were designated as primary-specific or lymph-specific, respectively.

Cell-cell colocalization analysis

We performed cell-cell colocalization analysis using MistyR [16] (http://github.com/saezlab/mistyR) by constructing an intraview model for each spot. Cell abundances estimated by cell2location were used both as predictors and prediction targets to infer intercellular spatial relationships and quantified as ‘importance’. The analysis was conducted independently on six samples, and the inferred importance values were aggregated across all samples. Cell pairs with importance > 1 were considered colocalized. Given the directional nature of the model, a cell pair (A-B) was defined as colocalized if the importance score exceeded 1 in at least one direction (A→B or B→A). To compare PRI-T and LN-T samples, colocalizations per patient was annotated as primary-only, lymph-only, both, or neither; pairs consistently classified across patients were designated as primary-specific or lymph-specific.

Cell-cell communication analysis

Significant cell-cell interactions were identified at sample level using LIANA (http://github.com/saezlab/liana), with each interaction defined by the source cell, target cell, ligand, and receptor (SR:LR). stLearn (http://github.com/BiomedicalMachineLearning/stLearn) was used to confirm whether the LR were spatially co-localized. Only LR supported by spatial co-localization evidence were included in downstream analyses.

For bulk RNA-seq data, we adopted a previously established strategy (median-threshold ligand-receptor interaction determination). Specifically, samples were classified as “expressed” or “not expressed” based on the median expression of each gene within each cohort [17]. A ligand-receptor interaction was considered “present” in a sample only if both the ligand and the receptor were classified as expressed. This approach allowed us to determine the presence or absence of all ligand-receptor interactions in each sample. We applied this analysis to the datasets listed in Supplementary Material 5 and assessed the prognostic significance of each interaction for OS and recurrence-free survival (RFS) using univariate Cox regression.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) and multiplex immunofluorescence (mIF)

The in situ expression of proteins was detected using the following primary antibodies: Anti-PDGFRA (Abcam, ab203491, 1:2000), Anti-PDGFRB (Proteintech, 13449–1-AP, 1:8000), Anti-CSPG4 (Abcam, ab129051, 1:1000), Anti-MMP11 (Abcam, ab53143, 1:900), and Anti-CD31 (Abcam, ab218477, 1:200).

For IHC, formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) bladder cancer sections were subjected to standard deparaffinization, rehydration, antigen retrieval, and blocking, followed by incubation with primary antibodies, secondary antibodies, DAB chromogenic detection, and hematoxylin counterstaining. After dehydration and mounting, stained slides were scanned for subsequent analysis.

For mIF, FFPE sections were processed with standard deparaffinization, rehydration, antigen retrieval, and blocking, followed by sequential incubation with primary antibodies and corresponding HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for tyramide signal amplification. After each staining round, antigen retrieval and blocking steps were repeated to enable multiplex labeling. Finally, nuclei were counterstained with DAPI, and slides were mounted with an antifade medium prior to fluorescence imaging.

Public datasets

To investigate the potential role of microenvironmental cell subtypes and cell-cell interactions in mediating poor prognosis in urothelial carcinoma, we analyzed fourteen independent transcriptomic datasets. The data sources, preprocessing procedures, and available clinicopathological information are provided in Supplementary Material 5.

Survival analysis

External cohorts were obtained from the published literature, with cohort information and data preprocessing details provided in Supplementary Material 5. Gene set scores for each sample were computed using the GSVA package on normalized gene expression values. To ensure consistent interpretation of hazard ratios (HRs) across cohorts and cell types, ssGSEA scores within each cell type of each cohort were rescaled to a 0–1 range, allowing HRs to reflect the relative risk between the highest- and lowest-scoring samples. For survival analyses, univariable Cox regression models were first performed to evaluate the association between cell subtype-/subniche-specific gene set scores and patient outcomes. For multivariable analyses, only cohorts with complete clinical information on age, T stage, and N stage were included. Pooled HR and 95% confidence interval for each cell type were estimated using a random-effects model implemented in the metafor package.

Statistical analysis

The differences between two groups of continuous values were tested using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. For paired comparison, Wilcoxon signed rank test were used. Cohen’s d was used to standardize the differences across different compared features. Conventionally, a Cohen’s d of 0.2–0.5 is considered a small effect, 0.5–0.8 a moderate effect, and > 0.8 a large effect. Pearson correlation was performed in correlation analysis. Survival analysis was performed using the log-rank test for stratified variable and cox regression model for univariable and multivariable analysis. A significance level of α = 0.05 was adopted. Multiple testing corrections were applied using the Benjamini & Hochberg method when necessary.

Results

Single-cell rna sequencing and data processing

We performed single-cell transcriptome sequencing on 32 samples from 11 bladder cancer patients, covering the primary bladder cancer lesions (PRI-T, n = 9), adjacent tissues (PRI-N, n = 3), metastatic lymph nodes (LN-T, n = 11), and normal lymphoid tissues (LN-N, n = 9) (Table S1). A total of 287,012 cells were obtained and integrated to identify cell types with shared phenotypes.

Based on typical cell markers, we first identified 13 major cell categories, including B cells (n = 70,219), cycling cells (CC; n = 5,562), CD4+ T cells (n = 58,510), CD8+ T cells (n = 27,911), dendritic cells (n = 3,838), endothelial cells (EC; n = 4,543), epithelial cells (n = 28,022), inflammatory CAFs (iCAF; n = 14,008), monocyte-macrophages (MonoMacro, n = 12,038), myofibroblasts (myoCAF; n = 2,403), natural killer cells (NK; n = 2,251), mast cells (n = 2,651), and neutrophils (n = 4,310). We conducted a PCA on the abundances of the major microenvironmental cell categories in the samples and found that the microenvironmental characteristics showed differences between the bladder and lymph nodes, as well as between tumors and normal tissues. Specifically, PRI-Ns and LN-Ns were significantly separated along PC1. Tumor and normal tissues tended to separated along PC2 (Fig. 1C, Figure S1B). The tumor tissues from different sites showed a trend of microenvironmental convergence, as the difference between PC1, which represents the differences in tissue type microenvironments, was smaller in tumor-related samples compared to normal samples. The tissue enrichment scores for major cell categories also led to similar conclusions (Fig. 1D & Fig. 1E). Our analysis indicates that there are differences in the microenvironmental composition among different tissues, which can be explained by the tissue origin and the influence of oncogenesis.

Cell composition differences in primary and metastatic lesions of bladder cancer

By calculating the Bhattacharyya distance of the same major cell population among different tissues, we found that even for populations with the same annotation, there were significant phenotypic differences across tissues, suggesting distinct subtypes within the major cell populations (Figure S2). This prompted us to further define the subsets within the major cell populations. We identified subsets within B cells, CD4+ T cells, CD8+ T cells, ECs, iCAFs, MonoMacros, myoCAFs, and NKs. We repeated the PCA based on subtype information and observed histological and oncological differences similar to those in the analysis of major cell populations and also observed a trend of microenvironmental convergence (Figs. 1C, 2B)—Samples from lymph node tissues (LN-N and LN-T) and primary sites (PRI-N and PRI-T) were significantly separated along PC1, while samples from tumor tissues (PRI-T and LN-T) and normal tissues (PRI-N and LN-N) were significantly separated along PC2 (Figure S5A). Such convergence was recapitulated with two alternative methods (Figure S5B & C). Similarly, the tissue enrichment scores were calculated for each cell subtype were found hierarchically clustered into 4 group: unspecific, bladder-enriched, lymph-enriched, and tumor-enriched (Fig. 2C). “bladder-enriched” cluster exhibited higher PC1 score, corresponding to its preference for bladder tissues, “lymph-enriched” cluster exhibited lower PC1 score, indicating its preference for lymph nodes, and “tumor-enriched” cluster exhibited higher PC2 score, suggesting the preference of cell subtypes within for tumor tissues. We found most iCAF subtypes (e.g., iCAF_MATN2, iCAF_SLC14A1, iCAF_PI16) were in “bladder enriched” cluster, while iCAF_LRRC15 was in “tumor enriched” cluster. LRRC15+ fibroblasts have been reported to be associated with lymph node metastasis of tumors [18]. Most myoCAF subtypes (e.g., myoCAF_CCL21, myoCAF_PCP4, myoCAF_MYH11) preferred bladder tissues, while myoCAF_PLXDC1 preferred tumor tissues and was less abundant in PRI-Ns and LN-Ns. EC_ACKR1 showed enrichment in bladder tissues, while EC_ITGA8 was enriched in tumor tissues. Most MonoMacro subtypes (e.g., MonoMacro_PLD4, MonoMacro_VEGFA, MonoMacro_SPN, MonoMacro_FOLR2, MonoMacro_S100A8) showed a preference for PRI-Ts, while MonoMacro_SPP1 and MonoMacro_CXCL9 showed a preference for tumor tissues (Fig. 2C& 2D). Interestingly, recent studies have reported the antagonistic effects of macrophages SPP1 and CXCL9 in immunotherapeutic responsiveness [19], suggesting that the tumor microenvironment simultaneously induces pro- and anti-tumor macrophage subtypes.

As validation, we deconvoluted cell subtype populations with BayesPrism on paired PRI-Ts and PRI-Ns from TCGA-BLCA (n = 19) and SYUCC cohort (n = 22) [20] (Figure S6). Based on raw proportions, most non-epithelial subtypes appeared downregulated in tumor tissues, likely reflecting expansion of malignant epithelial compartments that compressed non-epithelial components (Figure S7A). After normalizing cell type abundances against non-epithelial compartments, iCAF_LRRC15, myoCAF_PLXDC1, and EC_ITGA8 were consistently upregulated across both cohorts, whereas iCAF_PI16, iCAF_SLC14A1, mast cells, and myoCAF_PCP4 showed consistent downregulation (Fig. 2D& 2E). Besides, myoCAF_PLXDC1 was the only cell type exhibiting consistent upregulation based on raw proportions (Figure S7B).

We next clustered spots across multiple samples based on cell abundance profiles. Three major niche categories were identified (Fig. 3B). Each exhibited increased abundance of epithelial, stromal, or immune components and increased expression of corresponding marker genes (Fig. 3C–D), and were named as tumor, stromal, and immune. In sample P02_LN, lymphoid tissue appeared compressed by tumor tissue. In P04_LN, most lymphoid tissue was preserved. In P07_LN, only limited lymphoid tissue left, with the majority replaced by fibroblasts and tumor (Fig. 3A).

Further clustering resolved three clusters into 21 subniches (Fig. 3A& 3E). To characterize each subniche and determine whether they correspond to specific tissue structures, we identified differentially enriched cell types (DECTs) within each subniche (Fig. 3F). Notably, lymph_3 represented a typical germinal center (GC) in the P04_LN sample, enriched for CD4T_CXCL13, B_MEF2B, and CC cells (Supplementary Notes). Similarly, stroma_5 in the P07_LN tissue represented a vessel-like structure and was characterized by increased endothelial subtypes (EC_CXCL12, EC_ACKR1) and myofibroblast subtypes (myoCAF_CCL21, myoCAF_MYH11) (Figure S8A). Most subniches were detected in at least one PRI-T and LN-T sample, except for lymph_3, lymph_4, and lymph_5, which were identified only in LN-T. Subniches that consistently exhibited higher proportions in PRI-T or LN-T were further explored (Figure S8B). These findings demonstrate that ST can capture tissue structures orchestrated by diverse cell subtypes.

Functional enrichment analysis of niche-specific differentially expressed genes (DEGs) further revealed distinct biological programs. Lymph_3, previously recognized as GC-related, enriched pathways such as “G2M CHECKPOINT” and “B CELL ACTIVATION INVOLVED IN IMMUNE RESPONSE”. Stroma_5 was enriched for “NOTCH SIGNALING PATHWAY” and “ENDOTHELIUM DEVELOPMENT”, reflecting its vascular characteristics. Meanwhile, tumor_9, found located within the tumor core, showed enrichment of “G2M CHECKPOINT”, indicative of enhanced cell proliferation (Figure S8C).

Altogether, our analysis revealed that myoCAF_PLXDC1 was identified as tumor-enriched (Fig. 2C–E) and was significantly associated with poor prognosis in both univariable and multivariable analyses (Fig. 4A–B, Figures S9&S10A-B). We then explored the specific TFs of myoCAF_PLXDC1. Although iCAF and myoCAF separated from each other under gene expression space, most of the TFs involved in the regulation of myoCAF and iCAF overlap (Figure S12, Figure S13). However, compared with iCAF, the gene expression and regulatory activity of TBX2 in myoCAF are higher, while the expression and regulatory activity of the PRRX2 gene are lower (Fig. 6D, Figure S12, Figure S13). TBX2 was found to be strongly and highly expressed in mesenchymal progenitors, with reduced expression in differentiated fibroblasts and SMCs [30]. Additionally, TGF-β stimulation leads to the downregulation of TBX2 in fibroblasts [31]. This suggests that TBX2+ iCAFs represent a dedifferentiated state. On the other hand, TGF-β stimulation upregulates PRRX2 to induce fibroblast differentiation and tissue fibrosis through the WNT pathway [32]. Compared with other myoCAFs, myoCAF_PLXDC1 exhibits higher activities of TFs including PRRX1, MXD4, ZEB1, and EBF1 (Fig. 6E–G). PRRX1 is a key transcription factor determining the myoCAF fate differentiation of stromal cells and is regulated by TGF-β signaling [33], while ZEB1 inhibits cytokine production and lymphocyte recruitment in fibroblasts [34].

Communication analysis to identify prognosis-related ligand-receptor (LR) pairs in bladder cancer

Finally, we explored the communication mechanisms related to the poor prognosis of bladder cancer. We separately identified communication events, jointly defined by sender-receiver (SR) cell pairs, and LR pairs, with LIANA pipeline in each sample and found 452,401 significant events. Among them, 282,676 events involved LR pairs whose spatial co-localization was confirmed by stLearn algorithm (Methods). We previously found myoCAF_PLXDC1 predicted the positions of more vascular ECs compared with myoCAF_CCL21 (Fig. 8). As proof of principle, LRs between myoCAF_CCL21 and myoCAF_PLXDC1 (source cells) and various EC subtypes (target cells) were examined, which revealed that myoCAF_PLXDC1 engages in more frequent and stronger interactions with ECs compared with myoCAF_CCL21 (Figure S15). Notably, myoCAF_PLXDC1 expresses multiple extracellular matrix-related genes (FN1, COL1A1, COL1A2, COL18A1) and angiogenesis-regulating ligands, including ANGPT2 [39], PGF [40], and SPARC [41], which interact with EC angiogenic receptors such as FLT1_KDR (VEGFR1/VEGFR2 complex), FLT4 (VEGFR3), KDR (VEGFR2), and TIE1. These results provide additional evidence that myoCAF_PLXDC1 exhibits higher angiogenic potential than myoCAF_CCL21.

We further explored the potential associations between the above interaction pairs and the poor prognosis of bladder cancer in external cohort. We adopted a median-threshold ligand-receptor interaction determination strategy [17] (Methods) and evaluated the associations between LRs and patient prognosis in an unbiased manner across multiple datasets. Our analysis identified multiple LRs that were consistently associated with prognosis of bladder cancer across multiple datasets (Table S4). Notably, several of these interactions have been previously reported, including those involving ITGA11 and WNT5A [42, 43]. LAMA4- > ITGA2–ITGB1 was identified as significantly associated with prognosis in 10 cohort-outcome combinations. LAMA4, as the ligand, was recognized as a marker gene for EC_ITGA8, EC_PROX1, and myoCAF_PLXDC1 (Table S2).

Discussion

In this study, single-cell transcriptomics and spatial transcriptomics technologies were used to systematically analyze the cellular composition, transcriptional regulatory networks, and spatial interaction patterns of the bladder cancer microenvironment, revealing the tissue-specific heterogeneity and cross-site convergence mechanisms of the tumor microenvironment.

Our study reveals a convergent pattern in the tumor-influenced microenvironments of lymph nodes and bladder tissues. Under physiological conditions, these two tissues exhibit distinct microenvironmental compositions. However, under the influence of tumor cells, their microenvironments become more similar, characterized by the enrichment of several tumor-associated cell types, such as CXCL13+ CD8+ T cells, FOXP3+ CD4+ T cells, and SPP1+ macrophages. Previous studies have demonstrated the critical roles of these cell populations in tumor progression, underscoring the importance of therapeutic strategies targeting the tumor-associated immune microenvironment.

Nonetheless, current therapies targeting the tumor microenvironment often lack specificity. For example, although targeting the CAF marker FAP has been proposed, such strategies may cause off-target toxicity to multipotent bone marrow stromal cells [44], highlighting the urgent need for more selective therapeutic targets. LRRC15 is a CAF-specific protein, and its corresponding antibody-drug conjugate, ABBV-085, has shown promising therapeutic potential in preclinical studies [45].

We identified a marked enrichment of PLXDC1+ perivascular cells in bladder cancer tissues, which was significantly associated with poor prognosis across multiple independent cohorts. While perivascular cells have received limited attention in studies of the bladder cancer microenvironment, extensive research in other solid tumors—such as liver and breast cancer—has revealed their roles in tumor angiogenesis, formation of hypoxic niches, immune tolerance, and resistance to chemotherapy. These findings suggest their potential as a selective therapeutic target in bladder cancer as well [46]. Moreover, we observed a striking spatial co-localization between PLXDC1+ CAFs and ITGA8+ endothelial cells, implying a coordinated role in constructing tumor-specific vascular structures and jointly promoting angiogenesis and immune evasion. This finding offers a novel direction for the precise modulation of the tumor microenvironment. Our findings emphasized the importance of further functional validation. However, pericyte-like cells enriched in bladder cancer tissues have not yet been isolated from primary specimens. Previous studies of pericytes have largely relied on cells derived from vascular-rich normal or tumor tissues, including lung [47], liver [48], brain [49, 50], kidney [51], bone marrow [52], and skeletal muscle [53]. These models may not fully recapitulate the precise cellular states within the bladder cancer microenvironment. Future studies involving primary isolation of myoCAF_PLXDC1 from bladder cancer tissues and the investigation of their tube-forming and pro-tumorigenic capabilities will be crucial.

IFNG participates in modulating the phenotypes of multiple tumor-associated microenvironmental cell types, including myoCAF_PLXDC1, in our ligand-receptor regulatory network analysis, while has also been reported to contribute to anti-tumor immunity, primarily through promoting tumor cell apoptosis in concert with cytotoxic effectors such as granzyme B (GZMB) and perforin (PRF1) [54], thereby underscoring the complexity of its biological functions. Several recent studies have provided mechanistic insights into this apparent paradox. For instance, interferon-induced SLC14A1+ fibroblasts (designated as iCAF_SLC14A1 in our study) were shown to secrete WNT5A, which enhanced bladder cancer stemness and contributed to chemoresistance [43]. Another study reported that IFNG secreted by exhausted T cells (Texh) promoted fibroblast plasticity and induced CXCL12 production, thereby reinforcing Texh recruitment through the CXCL12–CXCR4 axis and establishing a feed-forward loop of immune exhaustion [55]. Beyond fibroblast reprogramming, both clinical and experimental data have demonstrated divergent roles of IFNG: while some reports associate elevated IFNG levels with anti-tumor activity [56], others suggest immunosuppressive and pro-tumoral consequences [57].

Currently, although anti-angiogenic therapies have shown success in renal cell carcinoma and other genitourinary tumors, they have not yet been recommended as first-line treatments in bladder cancer. Related therapeutic strategies are still under active investigation. APL-1202, an oral MetAP2 inhibitor with anti-angiogenic activity, demonstrated superior pathological complete response rates when combined with the PD-1 antibody toripalimab in the ANTICIPATE phase I clinical trial (NCT04813107). The phase III MAIN-CAV trial is currently evaluating the efficacy of cabozantinib plus avelumab versus avelumab monotherapy as maintenance treatment in patients with advanced bladder cancer (NCT05092958). Additionally, clinical studies are underway to assess the combination of Ivonescimab, a bispecific antibody targeting PD-1 and VEGFA, with Disitamab Vedotin (NCT06957561).

This study has several limitations. First, the scRNA-seq and ST sequencing data were derived from a limited number patients. Although validation using public databases was performed, the generalizability of the conclusions to larger cohorts or different ethnic populations requires further investigation. Additionally, although key TFs and LR interaction networks were predicted through bioinformatic analyses, there is a lack of in vitro and in vivo functional experiments (e.g., co-culture systems, animal models) to directly validate the pro-tumor mechanisms and potential therapeutic targeting of specific cell subtypes such as myoCAF_PLXDC1. These limitations highlight directions for future research.

Conclusions

In this study, we constructed a comprehensive single-cell and spatial atlas of the bladder cancer microenvironment, revealing the cellular heterogeneity, spatial organization, and intercellular communication patterns that underpin tumor progression and patient prognosis. We identified a tumor-enriched myofibroblast subtype, myoCAF_PLXDC1, which is consistently associated with poor OS and RFS across multiple datasets. myoCAF_PLXDC1 exhibits a pericyte-like identity, preferentially localizes within tumor regions, and displays enhanced angiogenic and interferon-responsive programs, in contrast to other pericyte-like fibroblasts such as myoCAF_CCL21. Spatial co-localization analyses further demonstrated that myoCAF_PLXDC1 specifically interacts various EC subtypes, especially with EC_ITGA8, contributing to the formation of specialized vascular niches that lead to worse prognosis.
