Clofazimine against cerebral toxoplasmosis in diabetic and dexamethasone-immunosuppressed mice: ultrastructural and semiquantitative transmission electron microscopic study
Authors: Mennat-Elrahman A. Fahmy, Amany A. Abdel-Aal, Soad I. Hassan, Maisa A. Shalaby, Marwa Esmat
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Cerebral toxoplasmosis is a common opportunistic parasitic infection of the CNS caused by the Toxoplasma gondii parasite. Host immunosuppression can affect disease outcomes. To explore the changes in the cerebral cortical ultrastructure accompanying the infection in different immune-altered models and to find an effective treatment against the infection, we tested the possible therapeutic effect of clofazimine (CFZ) (the FDA-approved antimycobacterial drug) against the infection using 60 male CD1 Swiss Albino mice divided into 6 groups: 3 dexamethasone (DEX)- treated groups (DEX-only, DEX-infected, and DEX-infected-treated), and 3 streptozotocin (STZ)-induced type 1 diabetic groups (STZ-only, STZ-infected, STZ-infected-treated). The worst ultrastructural changes were observed in the diabetic and diabetic-infected groups, characterized by a significant increase in neuronal apoptotic and necrotic nuclei (P < 0.05) and changes in the numbers and structure of glial cells compared to the DEX and DEX-infected groups. CFZ (at a dose of 10 mg/kg/day for 3 days starting on 45th day post infection) significantly improved cortical neuronal ultrastructural changes in both models (P < 0.05), reduced microglial numbers, increased astrocyte numbers, and restored brain capillary integrity and axonal growth, in addition to significantly reducing mature cyst numbers in both models (P < 0.05). However, the drug didn’t reduce the number of atrophic and necrotic cysts in the infected-treated groups. So, in our study, CFZ showed preclinical promise in treating experimental cerebral toxoplasmosis and reducing the parasitic cyst burden, highlighting the adverse impact of the host’s altered immune status on brain tissue and the course of the infection, especially in diabetes.

Introduction

Toxoplasmosis is the most common opportunistic infection affecting over one-third of the world population and the most common cause of focal brain lesions that lead to neurological insufficiency, coma, and death. The prevalence of the infection varies significantly worldwide, reaching approximately 90% in specific demographic groups in highly endemic areas, such as parts of Africa, or 60% in some parts of Europe. The infection is caused by Toxoplasma gondii, an obligate intracellular protozoan parasite that can cross multiple biological barriers to infect, escape clearance, and persist within the host. The brain and eye are the two most clinically important sites that the parasite prefers. Clinical manifestations of the disease depend mainly on the site and number of parasites and on the host’s immune status. According to the host’s immune status, toxoplasmosis is very rarely associated with severe clinical manifestations in immunocompetent individuals. In contrast, in immunocompromised patients, cerebral toxoplasmosis is most often caused by reactivation of latent brain cysts, leading to devastating consequences in the host, especially if left untreated [1–4].

As an opportunistic infection, toxoplasmosis is linked to impaired immune function. Examples of impaired immune responses are Type 1 Diabetes and prolonged dexamethasone treatment. Type 1 diabetes mellitus (T1DM) is an autoimmune disease with a genetic and environmental background, characterized by very little or no insulin production by the pancreas. There is a mutual relationship between T1DM and toxoplasmosis. T1DM patients are found to be more prone to infections with a significantly higher T. gondii seroprevalence than non-diabetics. On the other hand, the parasite may have a possible role in inducing type 1 diabetes in subjects with a positive family history of the disease and genetic predisposition to diabetes [5–7]. Also, using adjunctive steroids may help treat cerebral toxoplasmosis presented with mass lesions or diffuse brain edema. However, long-term use of dexamethasone (DEX) can affect the immunity of the patients, leading to acute toxoplasmosis and promoting the proliferation of T. gondii tachyzoites [8, 9].

Treatment of cerebral toxoplasmosis is facing many obstacles. Resistance, allergic reactions, and adverse side effects caused by the pyrimethamine-sulfadiazine combination encourage searching for new treatment options. Clofazimine (CFZ) is a lipophilic riminophenazine antibiotic used originally for treating tuberculosis, but currently, the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) has approved it for treating leprosy. Recently, the efficacy of CFZ against acute and chronic toxoplasmosis in immunocompetent mice was tested and showed promising results [10, 11].

In this study, we aimed to assess the impact of altered host immune status on the course of the infection by observing the ultrastructural changes in the brain tissues of Type 1 diabetic mice and dexamethasone-treated mice before and after treatment with CFZ.

Materials and methods

Ethical consideration

The study was conducted according to the National Institutes of Health (NIH) guide for the care and use of laboratory animals (eighth edition) and approved by the Research Ethics Committee at Theodor Bilharz Research Institute (PT 808). Animals were monitored daily for clinical signs, and humane endpoints were applied immediately when predefined criteria were met, as approved by the ethical guidelines. Outcome assessment was conducted by an independent investigator blinded to group allocation.

Experimental mice and grouping

After excluding any diseased mice, sixty healthy male CD1 Swiss-Albino mice from Theodor Bilharz Research Institute, weighing 20–25 g and aged 6–8 weeks, were included in the study. The 60 mice were randomly allocated to 6 groups so that each group contained 10 mice based on a previously published study [12]. G1: DEX-treated only, G2: Diabetic only, G3: DEX-infected, G4: Diabetic-infected, G5: DEX-infected and treated with CFZ, and G6: Diabetic-infected and treated with CFZ. The mice were housed in ventilated cages with free access to water and ad libitum in a temperature range of 22–24 °C and maintained under a 12-hour light/dark cycle.

Dexamethasone administration

A dose of 0.25 mg/kg/day for 14 successive days of oral dexamethasone (Dexazone, Al Kahira Pharmaceutical and Chemical Industries Company, Egypt) was administered to each mouse in DEX groups by oral-gastric gavage before inoculation with Toxoplasma cysts and continued till the end of the experiment [13, 14].

Streptozotocin administration

A dose of 40 mg/kg/day for 5 consecutive days of intraperitoneal streptozotocin (STZ) (Sigma-Aldrich, Missouri, United States) was administered to each mouse in the Type 1 diabetic groups before inoculation with Toxoplasma cysts. The hyperglycemic status of mice was confirmed by measuring the blood glucose levels from tail-vein blood using a glucometer [ACCU-CHEK Active; Germany] on experimental day 14 after 6 h fasting to ensure that fasting glucose concentrations were ≥ 150 mg/dl [15].

The infection

Fourteen days after starting DEX and STZ administration, each mouse in the infected and the infected-treated groups was inoculated orally with 100 T. gondii cysts of the ME-49 strain by oral-gastric gavage. The parasite was kindly provided by The Animal House of Theodor Bilharz Research Institute from the brains of previously infected mice, according to Ashour [16].

The treatment

A dose of 10 mg/kg/day for 3 successive days of oral clofazimine (lamprene; Novartis Pharmaceuticals Corporation, New Jersey) was administered to each mouse in the infected-treated groups by oral-gastric gavage on the 45th day post inoculation of infection [17].

Mice monitoring

Fasting blood glucose levels for all mice in the study were measured on the 14th day of the experiment and at the end of the experiment [15].

The surviving mice were euthanized according to Laferriere and Pang [18] by intraperitoneal injection of an anesthetic-anticoagulant solution (500 mg/kg thiopental and 100 units/mL heparin). The mice were then decapitated, and the cortical tissues of each mouse were removed and exposed to ultrastructural processing. Animals that reached humane endpoints or died during the experiment were excluded from the final statistical analysis of data.

Cerebral tissue processing for transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

The cortical cerebral tissues (frontal and parietal lobes) of each mouse were fixed in 3% glutaraldehyde at room temperature, then rinsed and post-fixed in 1% osmium tetroxide. The specimens were dehydrated in graded concentrations of ethanol and finally in resin. For each mouse, 3 to 5 ultrathin sections were then collected on Formvar-coated copper grids, double-stained, and multiple fields were observed for inter-section and inter-mouse comparisons using a JEOL JEM 1010 TEM at 70 kV at the Regional Center for Mycology and Biotechnology (RCMB), Al-Azhar University [19, 20].

TEM examination

Evaluation of the accompanying ultrastructural changes was performed by two blinded specialists based on semiquantitative scoring of the apoptotic and/or necrotic neurons in addition to Toxoplasma cysts in random 5 fields (2500x) [21]. The score was represented as arbitrary units: 0–2, mild; 2–4, moderate; 4–6, severe; and above 6, very severe. In addition to other pathological changes related to the immune-altered status, the infection, or the treatment [14, 22].

Data analysis

All analyses were performed using SPSS (Statistical Package for Social Science) version 25 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). Fasting blood glucose levels were expressed as means ± standard deviation (SD), and the mortality rates were expressed as percentages and analyzed as an inferential outcome. The apoptotic and necrotic nuclear changes variations, in addition to types of cysts, were presented as counts (N) and percentages (%), averaged to obtain a single value per mouse, and analyzed. Comparisons between severity categories were performed using Chi-square (x2) test. Effect sizes and confidence intervals were not calculated. A P-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The number of surviving mice at the end of the experiment in each group was indicated in the tables, and all analyses were performed based on these numbers.

Ultrastructure

STZ-induced hyperglycemia affected the brain cortical ultrastructure

Compared with the DEX-only reference group, the cortical ultrastructure of the diabetic-only mice exhibited significant areas of early neuronal apoptotic changes in the form of nuclear chromatin migration, condensation, and degeneration (Table 1; Fig. 3). Reduced numbers of astrocytes with pseudopodia, with enlarged lysosomes and swollen mitochondria, were also seen with fewer swollen end feet. In addition to increased numbers of microglia and areas of demyelination.

Infection worsened the cortical ultrastructural morphology in both models

Some of the neurons in the infected non-treated groups significantly showed apoptotic features, and others showed necrotic features in the form of organelle swelling, rupture of the plasma membrane, and the release of intracellular contents compared to other study groups (Tables 1 and 2). Increased numbers of both astrocytes and microglia with pseudopodia were noticed with areas of disorganized myelin sheaths and swollen axoplasm. Infected neurons and microglial cells were also seen. Brain capillaries showed dilatation with marked perivascular edema and interrupted endothelial junctional complexes (Fig. 4). The ultrastructural changes were more pronounced in the diabetic-infected group than in the Dex-infected group.

Clofazimine treated the infection and improved the ultrastructural pathology

A marked significant improvement of ultrastructural pathology with variable degrees of recovery was observed after clofazimine administration in both models. However, the most prominent improvement was observed in the DEX model. Relatively normal neurons were seen with a significant reduction of apoptotic and necrotic neurons. Some neurons showed nuclear envelope invaginations. Improvement of neuronal ultrastructure was accompanied by a significant reduction of mature cysts and an increase of atrophic and necrotic cysts. Decreased numbers of microglia and increased numbers of astrocytes were also observed. Regaining ultrastructural integrity of the brain capillaries was noticed with areas of axonal growth (Fig. 5).

Toxoplasma cysts

Different types of cysts were seen in the different infected and infected-treated groups. Mature cysts with irregular walls enclosing bradyzoites (some of them showed escaping tachyzoites), smaller atrophic cysts with fewer bradyzoites, and necrotic cysts with disturbed morphology and large areas of debris without apparent bradyzoites or tachyzoites. The highest number of mature cysts was observed in the infected group, and the treatment could significantly lower the number of mature cysts in both models. However, CFZ couldn’t significantly reduce the number of atrophic and necrotic Toxoplasma cysts (Tables 3, 4 and 5, and Fig. 6).

Discussion

Our study aimed to evaluate the efficacy of clofazimine (the FDA-approved antileprotic) against cerebral toxoplasmosis on the ultrastructural level in two different immune-altered models (Type 1 diabetic mice and DEX-treated mice).

Regarding the cortical ultrastructural changes accompanying immune alteration in the control non-infected groups, our results revealed that within the present study design, the DEX-only group showed fewer ultrastructural abnormalities than the diabetic groups. This was in agreement with Fan et al. [23], who challenged primary hippocampal neurons with various concentrations of dexamethasone while monitoring cell morphology and death, and found no changes. However, Madalena and Lerch [24] stated that glucocorticoid exposure could affect brain cell structure and function through interacting with glucocorticoid receptors found in neurons and glial cells. In addition, an in vitro study by Zhang et al. [25] showed that chronic dexamethasone treatment of primary hippocampal rat neurons significantly induced neuronal damage, and another in vitro study using a neuroblastoma cell line by Suwanjang et al. [26] described the high dosage of dexamethasone as being neurotoxic through impairment of mitochondrial dynamics. Different results recorded depend on the dose, the duration, the route of administration of dexamethasone, and whether the used model is in vitro or in vivo.

On the other hand, the STZ-induced diabetic control group in the present study showed some neuronal apoptotic changes with areas of demyelination in addition to reduced numbers of astrocytes and increased numbers of microglia. Similar findings were reported by Zhao et al. [27], who found cortical and hippocampal neuronal nuclear pathological changes with degeneration in Type 1 diabetic (T1DM) mice, which improved after insulin and/or liraglutide. Another study by Foghi and Ahmadpour [28] documented T1DM-induced neuronal apoptosis in the dentate gyrus and CA3 regions of rats’ brains which was significantly improved in the group that received insulin and the group that received vitamin C. Changes in morphology and numbers of glial cells associated with diabetes reported in our work were similar to the studies of Nagayach et al. [29], Li et al. [30], and Luna et al. [31] who explained their observations with the neurotoxic degenerative effects of high glucose levels to neurons and glial cells through several mechanisms including glucose-driven oxidative stress and protein glycation. Astrocytes play a crucial role in maintaining blood-brain barrier (BBB) integrity, and glucose toxicity to astrocytes could be one of the causes of BBB disruption. In the present work, the neuroinflammatory process associated with STZ administration and hyperglycemia could be the main reason for the increased number and changed morphology of microglia in the diabetic control group in this study.

The pathological consequences of diabetes on cortical ultrastructure reflect the seriousness of the disease and the importance of controlling blood sugar levels. Adding antioxidants to the treatment regimen of diabetic patients would help them reduce the severity of the infection and improve cerebral complications of the disease.

The apoptotic and necrotic neurons in the infected non-treated groups in the present work significantly increased compared to the other groups. Similar neuronal nuclear changes were documented by Tedford and McConkey [32] and Ahmadpour et al. [33], who explained the morphological alteration of neurons either by invasion and formation of cysts or by modulating neuronal apoptosis by the neurotropic protozoan parasite Toxoplasma gondii. We also observed activation and increased numbers of glial cells in the infected groups. The association of glial activation and proliferation with the infection was also reported by Zhang et al. [34] and Jin et al. [35]. Astrocytes and microglia are involved in the immune responses, cytokine production, and neuroprotection against infections, so increased numbers and activity of these cells in the infected groups are expected. Moreover, microglia harboring the parasite can act as Trojan horses, facilitating the dissemination of Toxoplasma gondii within the brain [36]. Our results also revealed disruption of the BBB and demyelinated axons accompanying the infection, which was in agreement with previous studies [14, 37]. The cortical pathological changes accompanying the infection were more pronounced, with significant numbers of mature parasitic cysts and apoptotic neurons in the diabetic group than in the DEX group. Among the control negative groups, the diabetic group had the worst cortical pathological changes with BBB disruption and declined neurogenesis, which could be the reason.

Our study used CFZ after the establishment of chronic infection (about 6 weeks post-infection). Similarly, some authors used comparable study durations for testing different drugs against chronic toxoplasmosis [38, 39]. The 3-day regimen of CFZ used in our work was similarly used in previous studies against other protozoa (Babesia and Cryptosporidium) and proved efficacy [17, 40].

In our work, CFZ significantly reduced the burden of mature cortical cysts in the infected treated groups, with an increase in atrophic and necrotic cysts (which may be explained by the ability of the drug to damage or degenerate the parasitic cysts rather than eliminating them or the slow clearance of damaged cysts due to the altered immune-status of the host), as well as improvements in cortical pathology, including significantly reduced numbers of apoptotic neurons and changes in glial cells. Biglari-Moghadam et al. [11] documented the efficacy of CFZ against experimental acute and chronic cerebral toxoplasmosis, with significant reductions in parasitic burden and improved survival. They attributed their findings to the ability of CFZ treatment to significantly elevate both interferon-gamma (INF-γ) and total antioxidant capacity (TAC) levels, with a significant reduction in the malondialdehyde (MDA) assay. IFN-γ is considered the key cytokine in preventing the reactivation of Toxoplasma encephalitis in the brain and activating microglia and astrocytes to control and inhibit the parasite growth. On the other hand, oxidative stress plays a pivotal role in the pathogenesis of toxoplasmosis. One of the most important stress markers is MDA, a highly reactive aldehyde that causes oxidative damage in tissues [41, 42]. However, Ng et al. [43] documented riminophenazine-induced cardiac dysfunction in a zebrafish model by modulating oxidative stress genes. Using different drug doses and experimental models could explain the different results obtained compared to ours. Regarding the effect of clofazimine on reducing the number of microglial cells (resident macrophages) observed in our work, Fukutomi et al. [44] reported the ability of CFZ to induce apoptosis in Mycobacterium leprae-infected macrophages. This could explain our result.

Nasiri et al. [45] found a higher success rate of CFZ in treating disseminated Mycobacterium avium complex (MAC) pulmonary disease in HIV patients compared to non-HIV patients, and they explain this finding with resistance to the drug, using multi-drug regimens, and the immune modulatory effects of CFZ. So, extending research about the efficacy of CFZ in other immunocompromised models and the immunocompetent model is needed.

So, we concluded that CFZ was associated with reduced mature Toxoplasma cyst burden and improved cortical ultrastructural findings. Therefore, CFZ shows preclinical promise and warrants further evaluation.

More studies are needed to explore other mechanisms by which the drug exerts its anti-toxoplasmic effects. Adjusting the dose, combination regimens, and using nanotechnology for better drug delivery are also needed. A key limitation of our study is that a healthy untreated control group was not included, which limits interpretation of whether changes seen in the DEX-only group reflect baseline morphology or treatment-related effects. Studying the cortical cerebral ultrastructural morphological changes only was also a limitation of our study. So, functional, volumetric, molecular, and immunologic studies are needed for further investigations. Another limitation of the study is the exclusion of animals that reached humane endpoints or died during the experiment from the final statistical analysis, which may lead to underestimation of pathological changes, so using a larger number of animals per group is recommended. Finally, using a relatively small sample size in each group limited the statistical power to detect differences in the mortality rates among the groups despite the variations in the number of deaths in each group.
