The reliability of a modified three-stage cervical vertebrae maturation method for estimating skeletal growth in males and females
Authors: Tatiana Sella Tunis, Maha Masarwa, Tamar Finkelstein, Osnat Grinstein Koren, Lazar Kats, Yifat Manor, Nir Shpack, Shoshana Reiter
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundReliability of the six-stage cervical vertebrae maturation method to evaluate skeletal growth was evaluated on the combined male and female samples and revealed diverse results. The current study aimed to determine the reliability of the modified three-stage cervical vertebrae maturation method for estimating the skeletal growth of males and females.MethodsPretreatment lateral cephalometric radiographs of 137 orthodontic patients were utilized to evaluate the intra- and inter-observer reliability of a modified cervical vertebrae maturation method. Four independent examiners categorized each radiograph into one of the three skeletal growth stages according to the cervical vertebrae morphology: (1) a pre-peak group (combining the CS1 and CS2 stages), (2) a peak group (combining the CS3 and CS4 stages), and (3) a post-peak group (combining the CS5 and CS6 stages). The Kappa index and the Fleiss multirater Kappa test were carried out to determine the reproducibility of this method.ResultsThe studied sample included cephalometric radiographs of 68 males (49.6%) and 69 females (50.4%) aged between 7 and 58 years; no significant age difference was found between the sexes (p = 0.189). The overall inter-observer reliability was 0.689 (p < 0.001), and the inter-observer reliability for the post-peak stage showed only Kappa = 0.896 (p < 0.001). The Kappa values were significantly greater in males than in females during the peak stage (0.626 and 0.488, respectively).ConclusionsA modified three-stage cervical vertebrae maturation method showed substantial overall inter-examiner agreement; however, differential reliability for diverse skeletal maturation stages and sexes was found.Keywords: Cervical vertebrae, Morphology, Skeletal maturation, Reliability, Sex difference, Cephalometric radiograph

Introduction

One of the important decisions during orthodontic treatment is choosing the proper time to start the treatment. Namely, orthodontists should take advantage of an individual’s peak growth when dentofacial orthopedic treatment is needed to modify the jaw growth in adolescents (e.g., advancing the mandible in retrognathic individuals, as well as maxillary expansion and protraction, or mandibular restriction in Class III cases) [1]. Additionally, accurate diagnosis of active growth completion is very important for determining proper orthognathic surgery timing in cases with dento-facial discrepancies. Correct diagnosis of patients’ skeletal growth maturation stage improves their response to orthodontic treatment, reduces the risk of complications, provides the best outcome, and may reduce the treatment time [2].

Previous studies showed that chronological and dental ages are inaccurate and insufficient indicators of accelerated skeletal growth [3–5]. Moreover, numerous factors, such as genetic, nutritional, and hormonal factors, in addition to an individual’s ethnicity and sex, may influence his/her skeletal development [6, 7]. Several radiographic methods for assessing skeletal age are widely used in different dental disciplines [8, 9]. Evaluating skeletal growth using the hand-wrist method is a standard method of assessing skeletal maturation [8, 10]; however, this method requires performing a wrist radiograph and it exhibits sex-related differences [11].

The cervical vertebral maturation (CVM) method was first introduced by Lamparski [12] and was later used by O’Reilly and Yanniello [13]. The advantage of using this method is that the cephalometric radiograph is taken routinely prior to orthodontic treatment for morphological analysis purposes. This, of course, reduces the amount of radiation exposure by eliminating the need for a wrist radiograph. In 1995 this method was modified by Hassel and Farman by introducing skeletal maturity diagnosis based on evaluating the shape and concavity of the lower border of three cervical vertebrae (C2, C3, and C4) instead of five vertebrae [8]. This method was further modified by Baccetti et al. using five stages of CVM skeletal maturation [2], and later using the six-stage clinically improved CVM method [1] that assists the treating physician in determining the proper timing for treatment.

Previous studies showed that the CVM method has moderate to high levels of correlation with the hand-wrist method for skeletal maturation analysis [8, 14–17]. However, the literature reports inconsistent results with respect to the reliability of the CVM method, namely, its ability to reproduce the diagnosis of the skeletal maturation stage. Some studies showed almost excellent agreement on both the intra- and inter-examiner levels [8]. Perinetti et al. [18] concluded that CVM is a reliable and accurate method when proper training and precise guidelines are provided. Rainey et al. [19] suggested that the quality of the cephalometric image influences the reliability of the CVM method; they categorized it as substantial with reproducible and reliable results. Nevertheless, some researchers reported the low reliability of the CVM method for determining skeletal maturity [20–22]. To improve the reliability of the CVM method McNamara et al. [23] provided clinical guidelines to teach inexperienced practitioners how to use the CVM method; however, in their study they noted that skeletal maturation is a gradual process; thus, some of the morphological characteristics may fit the characteristics of both stages, which consequently may adversely affect the reliability of the method. Sohrabi et al. [24] conducted a study that aimed to find the causes for the low reliability of the CVM method, and additionally they performed a reliability analysis of the clinical decisions based on the CVM method. For this aim they modified the five-stage CVM method presented by Baccetti et al. [2] and reclassified the stages into three phases according to the optimum treatment timing: (1) before optimum treatment timing (combining CVMS I and CVMS II), (2) during optimum treatment timing (CVMS III), and (3) after optimum treatment timing (combining CVMS IV and CVMS V). They found that the clinical approach of the CVM method was more reliable; however, they concluded that their rationale was based on the clinical demands of determining the peak skeletal growth that should be applied for growth modification treatment solely in Class II patients. Moreover, Sohrabi et al. [24] found poor inter-examiner agreement when classifying the C3 and C4 morphological shapes, which according to the authors, could reduce the reliability of the CVM method possibly due to the difficulty in distinguishing CVMS IV from CVMS V.

In introducing the method for cervical maturation based on cephalometric radiographs, Lamparski [12] presented different standards for skeletal maturity assessment in males and females. Interestingly, Lamparski found a weaker correlation between cervical and hand-wrist methods for skeletal age evaluation in males. Previous studies also found sex differences in the relationship between the skeletal age assessment and facial growth [11, 25]. Caldas et al. [26] found significant differences between the cervical vertebrae bone age and the bone age estimated from hand-wrist radiographs in males only. Kulkarni and Dave [27] conducted a morphometric analysis of cervical vertebrae (C3 and C4) in males and females by analyzing different maturational stages. Their findings indicate that vertebrae size is sex dependent, when males possess significantly greater vertebrae dimensions. The vertebrae dimensions increase in height and size as the maturational stages progress [27]. Importantly, these dimension changes consequently induce shape changes in the vertebrae and might also be sex dependent, which subsequently may affect the reliability analysis of cervical vertebra in males and females. Previous studies evaluated the reliability of the CVM method using a combined sample of males and females, which may explain the controversial findings regarding the reliability of the CVM method for determining skeletal maturity.

Thus, using a three-stage method may reduce classification variability and supposedly, it may increase the reliability of this method. Moreover, classifying the skeletal maturation stages separately for males and females and calculating the reliability analysis for each maturational stage separately may reveal additional reasons for the low and diverse reliability of this classification method.

Materials and methods

Study aim

The aim of this study was to determine the reliability of the three-stage mCVM method and to determine whether this reliability is dependent on the patient’s sex and skeletal maturity stage.

Study sample

This study was carried out using the initial (pretreatment) records of 137 consecutively treated orthodontic patients of Caucasian origin, aged 7 to 58 years old. The sample was subdivided into three age groups according to the chronological age of the patient: (1) 7 to 11 years old, (2) 12 to 18 years old, and (3) > 18 years old. All medical records were taken between 2000 and 2017 at the Department of Orthodontics, the Maurice and Gabriela Goldschleger School of Dental Medicine, Tel-Aviv University, Israel, and from two private orthodontic clinics managed by staff members from the same Department of Orthodontics. These records were maintained for diagnostic purposes unrelated to the present study. This research was approved by the institutional ethical review board of Tel Aviv University. Inclusion criteria were as follows: age > 7 years, males and females, high-quality diagnostic lateral cephalometric radiographs including a complete morphology of the first four cervical vertebrae. Exclusion criteria included the following: congenital cranio-facial disorders or syndromes, systemic diseases, previous orthodontic treatment, previous surgery in the head and neck regions, a history of trauma to the head and neck regions, technically aberrant radiographs, and incomplete records.

Evaluation procedure

All 137 lateral cephalometric radiographs were evaluated by four independent examiners, all of whom were specialists in dental medicine and were staff members at the Maurice and Gabriela Goldschleger School of Dental Medicine, Tel-Aviv University. Each radiograph was categorized into one of the skeletal growth groups according to the cervical vertebrae morphology. All the skeletal (except for cervical vertebrae) and dental structures were hidden in the radiograph to avoid any information bias associated with the subject’s age and his/her maturity level (Fig. 1). Additionally, no information that would reveal the age and sex of the subject was provided to the examiners.

Calibration procedure

The initial stage of the study included a training procedure. A mCVM classification method was presented, and its diagnostic criteria were explained to four examiners during the calibration procedure meeting. Following the explanation stage, four examiners practiced together the diagnosis of cervical maturation using the mCVM classification method. For this purpose, 35 cephalometric radiographs were used, all of which were not included in the current study’s sample. Each radiograph lacking any patient’s information and with concealed dento-facial structures was presented using a PowerPoint slide presentation on the wall screen (Fig. 1). One minute was given for all examiners to rate each radiograph independently, after which the decision on the rating category was made by consensus.

Reliability procedure

The lateral cephalometric radiographs of 137 individuals were examined twice by each researcher (one month apart and blinded to the initial screening results). For calculating the intra-tester reliability, the first and the second ratings of each examiner were utilized, whereas for calculating the inter-tester reliability, only the first ratings of each examiner were utilized. The calculated Kappa values were interpreted following Landis and Koch [28], who developed labels for the degree of agreement based on the Kappa ranges. Kappa values between 0.81 and 1.00 were classified as an “almost perfect” degree of agreement, values between 0.61 and 0.80 were classified as a “substantial” degree of agreement, values between 0.41 and 0.60 were classified as a “moderate” degree of agreement, values between 0.21 and 0.40 were classified as a “fair” degree of agreement, values between 0.00 and 0.20 were classified as a “slight” degree of agreement, and values < 0.00 were classified as a “poor” degree of agreement.

Statistical analysis

The data were gathered and analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows (Version 27.0. Armonk, NY: IBM Corp.). One sample Kolmogorov-Smirnov test was used to determine the distribution of the patients’ age. The Mann-Whitney test was run to determine significant differences in age between males and females. The Kappa index was used to evaluate the intra-examiner reliability of the radiograph’s evaluation. The Fleiss multirater Kappa test was run to evaluate the inter-examiner reliability. The values ​​were interpreted according to Landis and Koch [28]. The level of statistical significance was set at 5%.

The sample size was calculated using the kappaSize R package (ver. 1.2): Sample size estimation functions for studies of interobserver agreement (Rotondi MA, 2018), and the R Foundation for statistical computing (Vienna, Austria): A language and environment for statistical computing (R Core Team, 2024) were used. The anticipated preliminary value of Kappa for an inter-examiner reliability of 4 raters was set at 0.8 with a precision of 0.1 on each side (i.e., Confidence Interval [CI] 95%= 0.7–0.9). The prevalence for each age group was assumed to be similar (approximately 33%).

Results

Sample size and demographics

The calculated minimal sample size was found to be 68 subjects for each gender group. The studied sample included 137 cephalometric radiographs of 68 males (49.6%) and 69 females (50.4%). The mean age of the total sample was 16.11 ± 9.534 years old, ranging between 7 and 58 years old. In males the mean age was 14.50 ± 7.286 years, and in females it was 17.70 ± 11.150 years, with no statistically significant difference found between the sexes (p = 0.189). The degree of inter-examiner agreement also was calculated for each of the three mCVM groups separately and is presented in Table 3. The greatest agreement between the examiners was found for the mCVM3 group classification; its Kappa value indicated an almost perfect degree of agreement and was 0.896 (p < 0.001) (Table 3). Regarding the mCVM1 and mCVM2 groups, only moderate agreement was found between the examiners (Kappa values = 0.589 and 0.572, respectively). Our results showed greater Kappa values for inter-examiner overall and individual stage agreement in males’ samples compared with females’ sample (Table 3). This difference was especially significant for the rating category of the mCVM2 stage, when Kappa was 0.626 (substantial agreement) and 0.488 (moderate agreement), for male and female samples, respectively.

Discussion

Previous reports showed good reliability for skeletal maturation stages using the CVM method [10, 29, 30]; however, they were criticized for their poor methodology, case, and the examiners’ selection, as well as an inappropriate statistical analysis [24, 31]. On the other hand, other studies questioned the utility of the CVM method for determining skeletal maturity due to its poor reproducibility [20–22]. Gabriel et al. [20] examined the reproducibility of Baccetti’s CVM method after addressing several of the methodological flaws reported in the previous studies [24, 31]. They found that the intra-observer agreement was 62%, whereas the inter-observer agreement was below 50%; therefore, they concluded that the CVM method should not be recommended as a strict clinical guideline for the timing of orthodontic treatment. To improve the reliability of the CVM method, Shim et al. [32] used CBCT images in addition to lateral cephalometric radiographs to diagnose the stage of skeletal growth. However, their findings confirm a poor reliability for the inter-observer assessment of CBCT scans, and a poor correlation with lateral cephalometric radiographs. Therefore, they concluded that the CBCT approach is inconsistent with the lateral cephalometric approach in assessing skeletal maturation, and due to its poor reliability, the current method is inadequate to assess the skeletal maturity stages.

In the current study we used the modified CVM method to improve the reliability of the CVM method. The aim of the modified method was to decrease the number of skeletal maturation stages from six to three (pre-peak, peak, and post-peak), thus allowing easier classification and identification and consequently improving the reliability of the technique. Additionally, we calculated the inter-observer reliability for each maturational stage separately, aiming to determine whether differences exist in the classification of certain skeletal maturation stages. We found that for intra-observer reliability using the mCVM method, the Kappa values ranged between 0.545 and 0.845, whereas for the inter-observer reliability it was 0.689 (substantial). This reliability seems to be only slighter higher than the reliability reported previously using the six-stage CVM method (moderate) [20]. Similarly, Sohrabi et al. [24] found only a slight increase in the reliability agreement using the clinical approach of the CVM method (three-stage) vs. the five-stage method. Da Rocha Duque et al. [33] compared the reliability of the CVM methods (five vs. six stages) when cervical vertebrae were evaluated by direct visual inspection and manual tracing; however, similar to our results, substantial degree of reliability was found for both methods.

Although the overall reliability was substantial, when we examined the inter-observer reliability separately for each skeletal maturation stage a different reliability was found. The pre-peak and the peak (mCVM1 and mCVM2) stages showed similar moderate reliability (Kappa values 0.589 and 0.572, respectively), whereas the post-peak stage (mCVM3) revealed a significantly higher level (almost perfect) of agreement (Kappa value = 0.896). We suggest that this differential reliability might be related to the diagnostic criteria used to classify the stage of skeletal maturation. Namely, the square or vertical rectangular shape of the vertebrae, which is used to characterize patients after the peak of their skeletal growth (stage mCVM3), is easier to distinguish from the trapezoid or horizontal rectangular shape of the vertebrae, which is used to characterize patients prior to and during the peak of their skeletal growth (stages mCVM1 and mCVM2). Previous studies similarly suggested that the difficulty in determining the morphological shapes of C3 and C4 vertebrae could possibly be a reason for the poor reproducibility of the CVM method [21, 24]. Baccetti et al. [1] claimed that distinguishing between CS4, CS5, and CS6 is possible only by using the C3 and C4 vertebrae bodies’ shape, which can explain the high reliability found in the current study for the post-peak mCVM3 stage that included both the CS5 and CS6 maturational stages. Miller et al. [34] found significant cervical vertebrae size and shape sexual dimorphism and suggested that this could explain the poor reliability of the CVM method. They pointed out that the clinical standards for cervical vertebral assessment are based on unisex standards, whereas sex dimorphism in form, shape, size, and growth differences exist between males and females.

To the best of our knowledge, the current study is the first to investigate the differences in CVM reliability analysis established separately for male and female samples. Skeletal maturation is known to be a sex-dependent process [7] and cervical vertebrae morphometrics (size and shape) was found to differ significantly between males and females [27, 34–36]. Since the CVM method is used to determine a patient’s skeletal maturation based on his/her cervical vertebrae morphometrics, the results of the reliability analysis, based on the combined samples of males and females, may be affected [34]. Indeed, the results of our study indicate that the inter-examiner reliability was greater for males’ samples compared with females’ samples for overall agreement and for each rating category separately. Kim et al. [37] developed an artificial intelligence model to predict the hand-wrist maturation stages based on the cervical vertebrae images. Similar to our findings, they found that the highest prediction accuracy was found when in addition to the cervical vertebrae characteristics, the chronological age and sex were added as prediction variables. Moreover, they found that males had a higher accuracy of prediction than females [37]. Chandrasekar et al. [35] developed a formula for objective assessment of skeletal age based on the cervical vertebrae; interestingly, they found that this formula differed between the sexes.

In our study the major difference between the sexes was found for reliability during the peak stage (mCVM2) when the reliability for males’ samples was substantial (Kappa value of 0.626) and only moderate for females’ samples (Kappa value of 0.488). This finding implies that identifying the peak growth in males is easier and more reliable than in females. Miller et al. [34] found that cervical vertebrae shapes in females were similar during the pubertal and post-pubertal period cohorts. Moreover, they suggested that females reach their mature vertebral shape during their peak growth stage. In another study, Miller et al. [36] found that significant sexual dimorphism occurs during puberty, when females’ higher estrogen levels trigger epiphyseal closure and earlier cessation in cervical height growth, whereas in males they found an additional secondary period of growth during puberty, which was absent in females. These findings can explain the lower reliability for CVM staging that we found when we compared female to male samples during the mCVM2 (the peak growth). Possibly, due to earlier vertebral shape maturation in females, the peak growth stage (mCVM2) was misclassified into the post-peak (mCVM3) growth stage.

Males possess significantly greater bone size even after matching for body size (body height and weight), compared with females [38]. Although the vertebral height was found to be similar between males and females, a significantly larger vertebral area and width was found in males [38]. In addition, Gilsanz et al. [39] found a significantly smaller vertebrae size in females even after matching it to the body size of males. These vertebrae height and width differences between the sexes affect the vertebrae size and shape differences, which consequently may play a significant role in the repeatability of the CVM classification process. Before puberty, the length and the width of the bone increase progressively in both sexes, but males enter their puberty about two years later than females; thus, their bone length at the beginning of puberty is longer [38, 40]. Moreover, during puberty, periosteal growth is inhibited by estrogens in females and is enlarged by androgen production in males, which creates skeletal sexual dimorphism during this period [40]. In addition to the longer duration of prepubertal growth, males are characterized by their greater velocity during puberty [41]. These differences also may account for better reliability in males during the mCVM2 maturational stage. However, the significant individual variations in the timing and tempo of puberty may affect the reliability results, which were significantly lower for the peak (mCVM2) stage compared with the post-peak (mCVM3) stage. Individuals’ sex identification should be an integral part of evaluating the skeletal maturational stages.

Study limitations

The current study investigated the reliability of the three-stage mCVM method in estimating skeletal growth; however, the validity of the method was not previously addressed and should be further evaluated. Although an equal distribution of the age groups was assumed when calculating the minimal sample size, fewer cephalometric radiographs for adult subjects (> 18 y) we found and the number of subjects in the 12–18 y group slightly increased, which could influence the precision of the assumed CI. Additionally, one should remember that improving the reliability of the CVM method by reducing the number of stages, as presented in the three-stage mCVM method, has a clinical advantage in determining the proper treatment time when growth modification in Class II malocclusions is indicated. However, when other malocclusions and treatment modalities are required, the three-stage mCVM method’s clinical scope can be more limited; then, precise skeletal age determination is advised, using a combination of other methods. The conclusions of the study were deduced after evaluating a Caucasian population, which was heterogeneous; thus, the generalization of the results requires populations from different geographic origins to be further investigated.

Conclusions

The inter-examiner reliability of the mCVM method was found to be patient sex dependent. Males’ samples were characterized by a higher reproducibility level, especially during the peak stage of maturational growth. The post-peak stage of maturational growth showed the highest level of reproducibility, which could be related to less inter-individual variation during this maturational stage, but it should be further investigated.
