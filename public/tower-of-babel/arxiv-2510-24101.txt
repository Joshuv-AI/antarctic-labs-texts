Traceable Signatures from Lattices Journal: Journal of Computer Standards and Interfaces
Nam Tran, Khoa Nguyen, Dongxi Liu, Josef Pieprzyk, Willy Susilo

Abstract
Traceable signatures (Kiayas et al. , EUROCRYPT’04) is an anonymous digital signature system that extends the tracing power of opening authority in group signatures. There are many known constructions of traceable signatures but all are based on number-theoretical/pairing assumptions. For such reason, they may not be secure in the presence of quantum computers. This work revisits the notion of traceable signatures and presents a lattice-based construction provably secure in the quantum random oracle model (QROM).

Introduction

In today’s world, balancing privacy with accountability has become a fundamental challenge for designing communication systems. Group signatures, introduced by Chaum and van Heyst
[
1
]
, was an attempt at resolving this problem by allowing users to sign messages anonymously with a group manager who can trace signatures to their original signers. This dual capability makes group signatures particularly valuable in applications requiring privacy protection and accountability mechanisms. Since their introduction, group signatures have evolved drastically with constructions such as
[
2
,
3
,
4
,
5
,
6
]
, offering increasingly practical solutions. However, these schemes rely on number-theoretical assumptions (either RSA-based or Diffie-Hellman in groups equipped with bilinear pairings) and are vulnerable to attacks from quantum computers. This drawback motivated the search for post-quantum candidates, with Gordon
et al.

[
7
]
giving the first lattice-based group signature scheme in 2010. Since then, this has been an active research area that has developed many constructions with security and efficiency gradually approaching that of their classical counterparts.

Lattice-based constructions are the most dominant among proposals of post-quantum group signatures and remain the most advanced regarding security and efficiency. Although the initial proposal from Gordon
et al.

[
7
]
only achieved weak anonymity with signature size linear order in the number of group members, subsequent works by Laguillaumie
et al.

[
8
]
and others
[
9
,
10
,
11
]
made significant improvement. They simultaneously reduced the signature size to be logarithmic in group size and achieved anonymity in the strongest sense, where signers’ secrets are exposed, and the adversary can open signatures of its choice. Current state-of-the-art constructions are from
[
12
,
13
]
, with a signature size of a few hundred kilobytes for a group size of roughly one million.

Alternatives other than lattices have also been explored, including isogeny-based
[
14
]
, hash-based
[
15
]
, and code-based constructions
[
16
,
17
,
18
]
. The code-based scheme from
[
17
]
achieves full-anonymity and has a logarithmic signature size in the group size. Recently,
[
19
]
proposed a zero-knowledge protocol from the VOLE-in-the-Head framework, which substantially improved upon previous code-based protocols. As an application, they reduced signature size in the scheme from
[
17
]
to roughly half a megabyte for a group size of around a billion.

Beyond basic user tracing, incorporating advanced functionalities in group signatures is a related topic that has raised much interest. The need for such features stems from specific privacy and security challenges that the existing means of group signatures may not easily deal with. For example, when the group manager expels some individuals from the group due to misbehavior, it should identify and revoke signatures produced from those. A simple method is to recollect and open every signature, but other users’ anonymity would be affected. The notion of verifier local revocable (VLR) group signatures
[
20
]
deals with such challenge, by offering a membership-revoking mechanism so that signatures remain valid as long as signers are members of the group. There is also the scenario when the group manager is not fully trusted and needs to be responsible for action. Such is addressed by the notion of accountable tracing signatures
[
21
]
. Another interesting functionality is message-dependent opening
[
22
]
, where the group manager can only open signatures on specific messages with the help of another third party (admitter). In the same spirit, many proposals of privacy-preserving signature systems generalize group signatures by enhancing the tracing power of authority while still protecting users’ privacy to a certain extent
[
23
,
24
,
25
]
. This research direction also results in a long line of work extending post-quantum group signatures by incorporating sophisticated and expressive tracing features
[
26
,
27
,
28
,
29
,
24
,
25
]
.

This work focuses on traceable signatures, proposed by Kiayias, Tsiounis, and Yung
[
30
]
. In essence, traceable signatures are group signatures but offer more tracing power to the group manager and users. In addition to standard tracing capability, group manager also has a
user-specific
tracing power that can be widely distributed among the system. Roughly speaking, the group manager possesses information called
tracing trapdoor
unique to each user. When delegated to sub-openers, the tracing trapdoor helps recognize signatures from the corresponding user while not affecting the privacy of others. In this way, user-specific tracing can be performed concurrently and without recollecting all of the previously issued signatures. In addition, traceable signatures also offer a
self-tracing
mechanism, allowing a user to claim authorship of previously issued signatures without revealing any sensitive information that may compromise its anonymity in the past or the future.

Traceable signatures may find application in anonymous systems where simple tracing is not enough to hold users accountable. For example, in financial transaction systems, there could be situations where a user conducts illegal trading activities. In such cases, the system administrator and other users should be able to invalidate the user’s previous and future transactions. A user-specific tracing method is best for this scenario, which is offered by the means of traceable signatures. Furthermore, a self-tracing capability can allow users to argue that they conducted past transactions faithfully, preventing trading assets from any ownership dispute while maintaining privacy for their other activities.

Formalization of Traceable Signatures
. Kiayias
et al.

[
30
]
defined the syntax and security requirement of traceable signatures, which we refer to as the KTY model. Informally, a traceable signature scheme in a collection of efficient algorithms (
\mathsf{Setup}
,
\mathsf{Join}
,
\mathsf{Sign}
,
\mathsf{Verify}
,
\mathsf{Open}
,
\mathsf{Reveal}
,
\mathsf{Trace}
,
\mathsf{Claim}
,
\mathsf{ClaimVerify}
). While defining a dynamic group signature scheme, these algorithms extend its functionality by formalizing tracing and claiming mechanisms. The group manager executes
\mathsf{Reveal}
to obtain a user-specific tracing trapdoor. Algorithm
\mathsf{Trace}
allows authorized parties to identify signatures from a specific user using their trapdoor. Algorithm
\mathsf{Claim}
enables users to create claims of authorship for their signatures, using their signing keys as secret input. Such claims can be publicly verified by
\mathsf{ClaimVerify}
.

The KTY security model consists of
traceability
,
non-frameability
and
anonymity
. These are similar to the security notions of group signatures but with subtleties due to the presence of tracing and claiming mechanisms. For example, non-frameability requires that a dishonest group manager or opening authority cannot create a valid signature that is traced or opened to an honest user. Moreover, users cannot illegally claim authorship of signatures they did not create, even when in coalition with the group manager and/or the opening authority. On the other hand, anonymity states that signatures reveal nothing about signers as long as users’ secrets and tracing trapdoors are not exposed. This notion is similar to
selfless-anonymity
in dynamic group signatures, put forward by
[
37
]
.

There exist extensions of the KTY security model, which generalize the above security notions in a way that resembles those of group signatures. Libert and Yung
[
34
]
considered a notion of anonymity similar to
CCA-anonymity
, when the adversary can observe claims and open signatures of its choices. The work of Blazy and Pointcheval
[
35
]
generalized the KTY model by separating the roles of opening authority and group manager similar to dynamic group signatures
[
38
]
. In their model (the BP model), the opening authority can recover tracing trapdoors from users’ public information, and users can deny authorship of signatures.

Previous Constructions.
Existing traceable signature schemes have predominantly relied on classical cryptographic assumptions. Kiayas
et al.

[
30
]
proposes the first traceable signature schemes based on pairings and RSA assumptions. Subsequently, constructions such as
[
39
,
40
,
34
,
35
]
all were based on variants of Diffie-Hellman-like assumptions
and/or required pairings. Surprisingly, to our knowledge, there are no known constructions of traceable signatures from post-quantum assumptions, except for
[
36
]
. However, there is a flaw that we will discuss shortly after.

The high-level approach to designing tracing/claiming mechanisms is including a
tracing tag
when generating a signature. Typically, this tag is computed by some pseudorandom function (PRF), evaluated on a seed (or PRF’s key) known only by the user and group manager. Constructions such as
[
39
,
40
,
34
,
35
]
implicitly employed modified variants of Dodis-Yampolskiy pairing-based verifiable random function
[
41
]
, which is a special case of PRF. In these constructions, the seed (or PRF’s key) will serve as the user-specific tracing trapdoor. Once released, anyone can “test" the tag by either re-evaluating the PRF, or verifying specific equalities the tag must satisfy.

To claim ownership, the user generates another
self-tracing
tag from signing secret and includes it in the signature. Signers create a claim by running a non-interactive zero-knowledge (NIZK) proof showing well-formedness of the tag. Such proof can be obtained from Fiat-Shamir heuristics
[
42
]
or by using specific proof systems such as Groth-Sahai proof
[
43
]
.

We stress that
[
36
]
proposed a lattice-based traceable signature scheme and proved security in the random oracle model (ROM). However, in the construction in
[
36
]
, the tracing tag is computed
deterministically
from the user’s secret and remains the same in every user’s signature. As such, this does not guarantee anonymity.

Our Contributions.
This paper proposes a traceable signature scheme from lattice assumptions and is provably secure in the quantum random oracle model (QROM). Hence, our scheme is the first traceable signature with complete post-quantum security.

Our construction implements the generic framework of dynamic group signatures
[
38
]
, building upon lattice-based cryptographic primitives including signature scheme by Jeudy
et al.

[
44
]
and GPV identity-based encryption (IBE) scheme
[
45
]
. The non-trivial task is realizing user-specific tracing and self-tracing mechanisms via lattice-based tools. To this end, we adapt the revocation technique of the VLR group signature scheme from
[
28
]
.

In our scheme, we use the ZK knowledge argument by Yang
et al.

[
46
]
to prove the well-formedness of users’ signatures. The relations defining signatures are handled with suitable transformations to make them compatible with the ZK framework. To make the argument a non-interactive argument of knowledge (
NIZKAoK
) secure in QROM, we employ generalized Unruh transformation
[
47
,
48
]
. Security of the scheme is proven under the formulation of the BP model
[
35
]
, which is more expressive and general than that of the KTY model
[
30
]
. We stress that the proof is not direct and certain details should be treated carefully using non-blackbox techniques. For example, the anonymity proof uses a technique for simulating GPV-IBE ciphertext in QROM
[
49
]
.

Overview of Our Techniques.
We sketch the high-level idea of tracing and claiming in our construction. For user-specific tracing, we turn to a technique employed by the VLR lattice-based group signature scheme in
[
28
]
. Recall that a VLR group signature scheme comes with a membership-revoking functionality. Roughly speaking, the group manager sets up a secret list of
revocation tokens
corresponding to users. A user is revoked from the group when the group manager publishes its token, and signatures are valid as long as the signers’ tokens are not revealed. In a sense, this implies user-specific tracing, with revocation tokens playing the same role as tracing trapdoors.

In the VLR group signature scheme of
[
28
]
, signer includes in signature a tag
\mathbf{t}
in the form of an LWE sample, i.e.
\mathbf{t}=\mathbf{M}\cdot\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{e}\bmod q
, where
\mathbf{e}\in\mathbb{Z}^{m}
is a randomized error term and
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{n}
is the revocation token. The matrix
\mathbf{M}\in\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{m\times n}
is also included in the signature and freshly chosen by hashing a short randomness. Based on the LWE assumption, the tag
\mathbf{t}
hides the token
\mathbf{x}
, guaranteeing the anonymity of users that are not revoked.

Looking differently,
\mathbf{t}
is a “tag" computed by an LWE-based randomized PRF with “key"
\mathbf{x}
and “input"
(\mathbf{M},\mathbf{e})
. From this observation, the revocation technique of
[
28
]
can be transformed into a user-specific mechanism, mimicking the PRF-based design in previous traceable schemes. Particularly, in our scheme
\mathbf{t}
acts as the tracing tag, and
\mathbf{x}
acts as the user’s tracing key. For the self-tracing mechanism, we bind tracing key
\mathbf{x}
to the user’s secret via a suitable one-way function. Signer can claim ownership of a signature by generating an NIZK proof proving knowledge of tracing key
\mathbf{x}
generating
\mathbf{t}
, and knowledge of the secret that is bound to
\mathbf{x}
. For this purpose, we instantiate the one-way function with the family based on the SIS problem
[
50
]
. In this way,
\mathbf{t}
also acts as the self-tracing tag.

Therefore, with the above modification to the revocation technique of
[
28
]
, we realize user-specific tracing and self-tracing functionalities using lattice-based cryptographic tools. To construct a full-fledge traceable scheme, we follow the high-level design of dynamic group signatures in
[
38
]
. The building blocks consist of an ordinary digital signature scheme for the group manager to certify members when joining the group, a CCA-secure public key encryption scheme for users to encrypt their identities, and a simulation-sound NIZK proof system for proving relations when generating signatures. For efficiency, we instantiate these components with the lattice-based signature scheme in
[
44
]
and the GPV-IBE scheme
[
45
]
, converted to a CCA-secure encryption with the help of CHK transformation
[
51
]
.

Regarding the NIZK layer, we employ the ZK argument system in
[
46
]
for handling various lattice-based relations. In addition, its inverse polynomial soundness error is an advantage compared to Stern-like ZK framework
[
52
]
employed by previous VLR schemes
[
28
,
32
]
. In particular, one needs
\mathcal{O}(\lambda/\log\mathsf{poly}(\lambda))
parallel repetitions in order to boost the soundness error of the employed ZK argument system - improving a factor
\log\mathsf{poly}(\lambda)
to that of Stern ZK protocol. Our design choices give a nearly constant signature size if the group size is a priori bounded.

Other Related Work and Further Discussion
. In
[
40
]
, a different model of traceable signatures was proposed. In this model, tracing agents can compute a set of identifying tokens from trapdoors, allowing them to recognize signatures from specific users. Abe
et al.

[
53
]
provided a generic construction of traceable signatures via a primitive called
double-trapdoor anonymous tag system
.

Although VLR group signatures and traceable signatures bear certain similarities between membership revocation and user-specific tracing, there are a few differences separating the two. First, VLR group signatures can only support static groups, while traceable signatures support dynamic groups. Regarding security, VLR group signatures can achieve the standard security notion of
full-anonymity

[
31
]
. In contrast, the result from
[
54
]
potentially suggested that selfless-anonymity is the strongest security traceable signatures can obtain.

Additionally, the syntax of VLR group signatures
[
20
]
does not require an opening algorithm. However, this can be done with the help of revocation tokens as follows: the group manager checks for which revocation token such that the signature is not valid when verified; if such token is found, the signature opens to the respective user. In traceable signatures, this is equivalent to opening signatures by trying all available tracing trapdoors instead of using the master opening key. In a sense, traceable signatures imply a form of VLR group signatures for dynamic groups.

From a certain point of view, the result of this paper provides a “conversion” of the VLR signature scheme of
[
28
]
to a traceable scheme. Nevertheless, our techniques are non-trivial and depend significantly on the mathematical structure of revocation tags and tokens. Generally speaking, it is unclear whether VLR group signatures can be transformed into traceable signatures. Even the generic bluerprint of
[
31
]
lacks a proper mechanism linking signers to its signatures to enable ownership claiming. Thus, this may suggest that such a conversion should be non-blackbox and carefully crafted from the underlying design components.

3
A Traceable Signature Scheme from Lattices

3.1
Technical Overview

At a high level, our lattice-based TS scheme is similar to the generic construction of dynamic group signatures in
[
38
]
. The building blocks include a digital signature scheme, a CCA-secure public key encryption scheme, and a simulation-sound NIZK proof system. The group manager uses the digital signature scheme to certify membership. The NIZK proof system is invoked by users when they have to prove certain relations in zero knowledge while generating signatures. For these two components, we employ the lattice-based signature scheme by Jeudy
et al.

[
44
]
and the ZK argument system by Yang
et al.

[
46
]
. For the encryption layer, we apply the CHK transformation
[
51
]
to GPV-IBE scheme
[
45
]
to obtain a CCA-secure encryption.

As explained in Section
1
, our construction adapts the revocation technique in
[
28
]
for tracing/claiming mechanisms. Recall that the user’s tracing trapdoor is a vector
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{n}
, and the tracing tag will be an LWE sample with
\mathbf{x}
as secret. In constructions following the KTY model
[
30
]
, the tracing trapdoor is sent directly to the group manager. However, in the BP model
[
35
]
, an adversary can passively observe the
\mathsf{Join}
protocol and thus may use the obtained tracing trapdoor to distinguish between users’ signatures. To hide the trapdoors, the idea of
[
35
]
is to use public key encryption, and to let the group manager certify the encryption of tracing trapdoors instead. However, rather than public key encryption, we can employ a trapdoor one-way function with sufficient pseudorandomness. An LWE-based function suffices: observe that if
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{n}
is a tracing key, then a user can “encrypt"
\mathbf{x}
as
\mathbf{B}\cdot\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{e}\mod q
where
\mathbf{e}
is an error term. Note that by the hardness of LWE, the adversary observing
\mathsf{Join}
learns nothing about
\mathbf{x}
from
\mathbf{B}\cdot\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{e}\mod q
. Additionally,
\mathbf{x}
can be recovered if we know many short vectors
\mathbf{s}\in\mathbb{Z}^{m_{\mathbf{B}}}
such that
\mathbf{B}^{\top}\cdot\mathbf{s}=0\bmod q
. From this observation, we let
\mathbf{B}
serve as the public key to the LWE-based one-way function and also as the master public key in GPV-IBE scheme as well. This design choice reduces the size of the opening authority’s secret.

When signing a message
M
, the signer encrypts its unique identifier issued by the group manager and computes the tracing tag as
\mathbf{t}=\mathbf{M}\cdot\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{e}_{\mathbf{t}}\bmod q
. The matrix
\mathbf{M}
is freshly sampled per signature by hashing a short randomness
\rho
. The user then generates an
NIZKAoK
proving that: 1) the tracing trapdoor is bound to the user’s secret, 2) an “encryption" of the tracing trapdoor is certified, 3) the “encryption" of the tracing trapdoor is correct, 4) the encryption of its identifier is correct and 5) the tag is correct. For condition 1), we let the tracing trapdoor
\mathbf{x}
be the output of an SIS-based one-way function evaluated on the user’s secret. We remark that proving condition 1 is crucial to our construction. Otherwise, malicious signers may defeat traceability and non-framebility of the system.

For the NIZK layer, the ZK argument by Yang
et al.
can be adapted to handle the signature-defining relations above, using techniques presented in
[
61
]
. To make the argument non-interactive in QROM, we apply Unruh transformation
[
47
,
48
]
.

Analysis of the Scheme

4.1
Correctness and Efficiency

4.1.1
Correctness

The sign/open/claim-correctness of the TS scheme in Section
3.2
follows from the correctness of its building blocks: the lattice-based signature scheme of
[
44
]
, GPV-IBE scheme
[
45
]
, the ZK argument system of
[
46
]
and the NIZK from generalized Unruh transformation
[
48
]
. Tracing correctness is implied by the following lemma.

Lemma 10
.

Let
\mathsf{trace}_{\mathsf{id}}=\mathbf{x},\mathsf{trace}_{\mathsf{id}^{\prime}}=\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}_{q}^{n}
be the output of
\mathsf{Reveal}
with respect to different users
\mathsf{id},\mathsf{id}^{\prime}\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
. Assume that
\Sigma
is a signature created by
\mathsf{id}
, then
\mathsf{Trace}(\mathsf{gpk},\mathsf{trace}_{\mathsf{id}^{\prime}},\Sigma)=1
with negligible probability.

Proof 1
.

First note that
\mathsf{Reveal}
correctly recovers the tracing trapdoors
\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}
with overwhelming probability. In addition,
\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}
are independent and distributed statistically close to uniform (by Lemma
5
). Therfore we have that
\mathbf{x}\neq\mathbf{x}^{\prime}
except with probability at most
2^{-\lambda}+{q^{\prime}}^{-n}=\mathsf{negl}(\lambda)
.

Parse the signature
\Sigma
as in (
2
). The condition
\mathsf{Trace}(\mathsf{gpk},\mathsf{trace}_{\mathsf{id}^{\prime}},\Sigma)=1
is equivalent to
\left\|\mathbf{t}-\mathbf{M}\cdot\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\right\|\leq B_{\mathsf{LWE}}
. It follows that

\left\|\mathbf{M}\cdot(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\right\|_{\infty}\leq\left\|\mathbf{t}-\mathbf{M}\cdot\mathbf{x}\right\|_{\infty}+\left\|\mathbf{t}-\mathbf{M}\cdot\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\right\|_{\infty}\leq 2B_{\mathsf{LWE}}.

Since
\mathbf{M}
is uniformly distributed, the above only happens with probability at most
{q^{\prime}}^{-n}=\mathsf{negl}(\lambda)
, by Lemma
4
. ∎

4.1.2
Efficiency

We analyze the asymptotic efficiency of the proposed scheme regarding key and signature sizes. The size of
\mathsf{gpk}
is dominated by the size of verification key of the signature scheme from
[
44
]
. The user secret key
\mathsf{usk}_{\mathsf{id}}
is a binary vector of length
m_{\mathbf{F}}={\mathcal{O}}(n\log q^{\prime})=\mathcal{O}(\lambda\log\lambda)
. The group manager and opening authority secrets are matrices with bit sizes of order
\mathcal{O}((n\log q)^{2})=\mathcal{O}(\lambda^{2}\log^{2}\lambda)
and
\mathcal{O}((n\log q^{\prime})^{2})=\mathcal{O}(\lambda^{2}\log^{2}\lambda)
respectively.

To estimate the signature size, we observe that in a signature
\Sigma
of the form in (
2
), the most dominant parts are the verification key
vk
and the signature
sig
in the one-time signature scheme
\mathcal{OTS}
. In turn, the size of
vk
and the size of
sig
depend on the bit-length
l_{\mathcal{OTS}}
of the message that is signed under
\mathcal{OTS}
. Note that we employ
\mathcal{OTS}
to sign the randomness
\rho
, the ciphertext
\mathbf{c}
encrypting
\mathsf{id}
, the tag
\mathbf{t}
and the NIZK proof
\pi
altogether. Among these four components, the bit-size of
\pi
dominates and is
4\kappa
times the size of prover’s response in the ZK argument
[
46
]
for
\mathcal{R}_{\mathsf{Sign}}
. As the size of prover’s response is
\mathcal{O}(\lambda\log^{3}\lambda)
(see
D.3
) and
\kappa=\Theta(\lambda/\log\lambda)
, it follows that the size of
\pi
is
\mathcal{O}(\lambda^{2}\log^{2}\lambda)
. By instantiating
\mathcal{OTS}
with the lattice-based one-time signature in
[
63
]
, we obtain one-time signature size of order
\mathcal{O}(\lambda\log\lambda)
and one-time verification key size of order
\mathcal{O}(\lambda^{2}\log^{2}\lambda+\lambda\log\lambda\cdot l_{\mathcal{OTS}})

\mathcal{O}(\lambda^{3}\log^{3}\lambda)

For the size of a claim, we note that the size of prover’s response in the ZK argument
[
46
]
for
\mathcal{R}_{\mathsf{Claim}}
is of order
\mathcal{O}(\lambda\log^{2}\lambda)

4.2

The security of our TS scheme is stated in the following theorem

Theorem 11
.

Suppose that
\mathsf{SIS}
and
\mathsf{LWE}
assumptions hold. Then the lattice-based TS scheme presented in Section
3.2
is CCA-anonymous, traceable and non-frameable in QROM.

The proof of Theorem

[
64

, which states that polynomial and truly random functions are perfectly indistinguishable against PPT quantum adversaries.

Proposition 2

(
[
64
,
48
]
)

A uniformly random polynomial function of the degree at least

is perfectly indistinguishable from a random function for any PPT quantum algorithm performing at most
q

Proposition

[
48
]

We prove Theorem

Lemma 12

B.1
Sigma Protocol

A Sigma protocol
\Sigma
for a relation
\mathcal{R}
is a tuple
\left(D_{\textrm{com}},D_{\textrm{ch}},D_{\textrm{rsp}},P_{\Sigma}^{1},P_{\Sigma}^{2},V_{\Sigma}\right)
; in which
D_{\textrm{com}},D_{\textrm{ch}},D_{\textrm{rsp}}
are domains of first, second and third message respectively;
(P_{\Sigma}^{1},P_{\Sigma}^{2})
are two PPT algorithms that share a shame state and together form a prover
\mathcal{P}
;
V_{\Sigma}
is a deterministic, PT algorithm constituting a verifier
\mathcal{V}
and outputting a single bit. On input
(x,w)\in\mathcal{R}
where
x
is a statement and
w
is a corresponding witness,
P_{\Sigma}^{1}(x,w)\rightarrow\mathsf{com}
, where the first message
\mathsf{com}
is called
commitment
and is sent to
\mathcal{V}
. The second message is called
challenge
, denoted by
\mathsf{ch}
and is uniformly chosen from
D_{\textrm{ch}}
by
\mathcal{V}
and is sent to the
\mathcal{P}
. The final message is called
response
, sent by
\mathcal{P}
to
\mathcal{V}
as
\mathsf{rsp}\leftarrow P_{\Sigma}^{2}(x,w,\mathsf{com},\mathsf{ch})
. Finally,
\mathcal{V}
runs
V_{\Sigma}
on input
(x,\mathsf{com},\mathsf{ch},\mathsf{rsp})
and accepts iff
V_{\Sigma}
outputs 1. The tuple
(\mathsf{com},\mathsf{ch},\mathsf{rsp})
is called a transcript of
\Sigma
.

A sigma protocol should satisfy the following properties:

1.

Completeness:
If
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{V}
follow the protocol with statement
x
as the common input and witness
w
as the private input to
\mathcal{P}
, such that
(x,w)\in\mathcal{R}
; then
\mathcal{V}
accepts with overwhelming probability.

2.

(Computational)
k
-special soundness:
Given a positive integer
k\geq 2
and let
(\mathsf{com},\mathsf{ch}_{1},\mathsf{rsp}_{1})
,
\ldots
,
(\mathsf{com},\mathsf{ch}_{k},\mathsf{rsp}_{k})
be
k
valid transcripts of
\Sigma
for a statement
x
, produced by some quantum PT algorithm
\mathcal{A}
, where
\mathsf{ch}_{1},\ldots,\mathsf{ch}_{k}
are pairwise distinct. Then there exists a PPT algorithm
E_{\Sigma}
called
knowledge extractor
, taking
k
valid transcripts as input and return a witness
w
such that
(x,w)\in\mathcal{R}
with overwhelming probability.

3.

Honest verifier zero-knowledge (HVZK):
There exists a PPT algorithm
S_{\Sigma}
, called
simulator
, such that for all PPT quantum algorithm
\mathcal{A}
and all
(x,w)\in\mathcal{R}
, the following quantity is negligible

\displaystyle|\Pr\left[b=1:(\mathsf{com},\mathsf{ch},\mathsf{rsp})\leftarrow(\mathcal{P}(x,w)\leftrightarrow\mathcal{V}(x)),b\leftarrow\mathcal{A}(\mathsf{com},\mathsf{ch},\mathsf{rsp})\right]-

\displaystyle\Pr\left[b=1:(\mathsf{com},\mathsf{ch},\mathsf{rsp})\leftarrow S_{\Sigma}(x),b\leftarrow\mathcal{A}(\mathsf{com},\mathsf{ch},\mathsf{rsp})\right]|.

Here
(\mathsf{com},\mathsf{ch},\mathsf{rsp})\leftarrow(\mathcal{P}(x,w)\leftrightarrow\mathcal{V}(x))
denotes the transcript obtained by executing protocol honestly with prover
\mathcal{P}
and verifier
\mathcal{V}
.

B.2
NIZK protocol in QROM

A non-interactive proof protocol
\Psi=(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{V})
for a relation
\mathcal{R}
can be described as
(\pi\leftarrow\mathcal{P}^{\mathcal{H}}(x,w),b\leftarrow\mathcal{V}^{\mathcal{H}}(x,\pi))
. The prover
\mathcal{P}
and the verifier
\mathcal{V}
are PPT algorithms,
\mathcal{H}
is a random oracle,
x
is the statement,
w
is the witness such that
(x,w)\in\mathcal{R}
and
b\in\{0,1\}
. Denote by
R
the uniform distribution of random oracles.

For a non-interactive protocol
\Psi
, the following should be satisfied:

1.

Completeness:
If
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{V}
follow the protocol with statement
x
as the common input and witness
w
as the private input to
\mathcal{P}
, such that
(x,w)\in\mathcal{R}
; then
\mathcal{V}
outputs with overwhelming probability.

2.

Zero-knowledge:
There is a pair of algorithms
(S_{P},S_{O})
(called simulator), such that for any quantum polynomial-time algorithm
\mathcal{A}
, we have that

\left|\Pr[b=1:\mathcal{H}\leftarrow R,b\leftarrow\mathcal{A}^{\mathcal{H},\mathcal{P}}()]-\Pr[b=1:\mathcal{H}\leftarrow S_{O},b\leftarrow A^{\mathcal{H},S_{P}}()\right|

is negligible, where
\mathcal{A}^{\mathcal{H},\mathcal{P}}
denotes the quantum PT algorithm
\mathcal{A}
with oracle access to
\mathcal{H}
and
\mathcal{P}
(similar for
A^{\mathcal{H},S_{P}}
).

Another property is
online-extractability
, which is a special case of
special soundness
. Roughly speaking, online-extractability captures the ability to extract a witness without rewinding a prover. In QROM, an extractor against this property is allowed to get the circuit description of random oracles, which are generated by the random oracle simulator
S_{O}
of zero-knowledge property.

Definition 18
.

\Psi
is online-extractable with respect to a simulator
S_{O}
if there exist a PPT extractor
\mathcal{E}
such that for any quantum, PT algorithm
\mathcal{A}
, the following probability

\Pr[b=1\land(x,w)\not\in\mathcal{R}:\mathcal{H}\leftarrow S_{O},(x,\pi)\leftarrow\mathcal{A}^{\mathcal{H}},b\leftarrow\mathcal{P}^{\mathcal{H}}(x,\pi),w\leftarrow\mathcal{E}(\mathcal{H},x,\pi)]

is negligible.

Simulation-sound online-extractability
is a special case of
online-extractability
, which essentially states a witness extractor succeeds with overwhelming probability, even against an adversary that adaptively gets many simulated proofs.

Definition 19
.

\Psi
is simulation-sound online-extractable, if there exist a PPT extractor
\mathcal{E}
such that for any quantum, PT algorithm
\mathcal{A}
, the following probability

\displaystyle\Pr[

\displaystyle b=1\land(x,\pi)\not\in\mathbb{X}\land(x,w)\not\in\mathcal{R}:

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}\leftarrow S_{O},(x,\pi)\leftarrow\mathcal{A}^{\mathcal{H},S_{P}},b\leftarrow\mathcal{P}^{\mathcal{H}}(x,\pi),w\leftarrow\mathcal{E}(\mathcal{H},x,\pi)]

is negligible. Here
\mathbb{X}
denotes the set of proofs produced by
S_{P}
.

) to a case of relation (

Proof of Plaintext Knowledge in GPV-IBE.

Let
\mathbf{c}\in\mathbb{Z}_{q^{\prime}}^{m_{\mathbf{B}}+1}
,
\mathbf{B}\in\mathbb{Z}_{q^{\prime}}^{n\times m_{\mathbf{B}}}
,
\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{Z}_{q^{\prime}}^{n}
,
\mathbf{r}\in\mathbb{Z}_{q^{\prime}}^{n}
,
\mathbf{e}_{\mathbf{c}}\in[-B_{\mathsf{GPV}},B_{\mathsf{GPV}}]^{m_{\mathbf{B}}+1}
and
\mathsf{id}\in\{1,\ldots,N=2^{\ell}-1\}
. We need to prove in ZK the following relation

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{\mathsf{Enc}}=

\displaystyle\left\{((\mathbf{c},\mathbf{B},\mathbf{v}),(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{e}_{\mathbf{c}},\mathsf{id})):\mathbf{c}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{B}^{\top}\\
\mathbf{v}^{\top}\end{pmatrix}\cdot\mathbf{r}+\mathbf{e}_{\mathbf{c}}+\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{0}^{m_{\mathbf{B}}}\\
\lceil q^{\prime}/(2(N+1))\rfloor\cdot\mathsf{id}\end{pmatrix}\bmod q^{\prime}\right\}.

Let

\mathbf{C}^{\prime}=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{B}^{\top}\\
\mathbf{v}^{\top}\end{pmatrix}&\mathbf{I}_{m_{\mathbf{B}}+1}&\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{0}^{m_{\mathbf{B}}}\\
\lceil q^{\prime}/(2(N+1)\rfloor\end{pmatrix}\end{array}\right)\in\mathbb{Z}_{q^{\prime}}^{(m_{\mathbf{B}}+1)\times(n+m_{\mathbf{B}}+1)}

it follows that
\mathbf{c}=\mathbf{C}^{\prime}\cdot(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{e}_{\mathbf{c}},\mathsf{id})\bmod q^{\prime}
. Write
\mathbf{c}=\mathbf{C}^{\prime}\cdot(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{e}_{\mathbf{c}},\mathsf{id})-q^{\prime}\cdot\mathbf{a}
for
\mathbf{a}\in\mathbb{Z}^{m_{\mathbf{B}}+1}
, then

q^{\prime}\cdot\left\|\mathbf{a}\right\|_{\infty}\leq\left\|\mathbf{c}\right\|_{\infty}+\left\|\mathbf{C}^{\prime}\cdot(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{e}_{\mathbf{c}},\mathsf{id})\right\|_{\infty}\leq q^{\prime}\cdot\dfrac{1+nq^{\prime}/2+B_{\mathsf{GPV}}+N}{2}.

By choosing
q>{q^{\prime}}\cdot\dfrac{1+nq^{\prime}/2+B_{\mathsf{GPV}}+N}{2}
, and letting

\displaystyle\overline{\mathbf{A}}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{C}^{\prime}&&-q^{\prime}\mathbf{I}_{m_{\mathbf{B}}+1}\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{Z}^{(m_{\mathbf{B}}+1)\times(n+2m_{\mathbf{B}}+2)}_{q};

\displaystyle\overline{\mathbf{x}}

\displaystyle=\left(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{e}_{\mathbf{c}},\mathsf{id},\mathbf{a}\right)\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}\times\mathbb{Z}^{m_{\mathbf{B}}+1}\times\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}^{m_{\mathbf{B}}+1};

we have that
\overline{\mathbf{A}}\cdot\overline{\mathbf{x}}=\mathbf{c}\bmod q
. By using the technique that transforms relation (
5
) to a case of relation (
3
), we can transform
\mathcal{R}_{\mathsf{Enc}}
to a case of relation (
3
) as well. The final quadratic relation has witness length and number of quadratic constraints approximately
n\log(q^{\prime})+(m_{\mathbf{B}}+1)\log(2B_{\mathsf{GPV}})+\log(N)+(m_{\mathbf{B}}+1)\log(1+nq^{\prime}/2+B_{\mathsf{GPV}}+N)
.

Proof of LWE Secret.
Let
\mathbf{t}\in\mathbb{Z}_{q^{\prime}}^{m_{\mathbf{M}}}
,
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{Z}_{q^{\prime}}^{n}
,
\mathbf{e}_{\mathbf{t}}\in\mathbb{Z}^{m_{\mathbf{M}}}
and
\mathbf{M}\in\mathbb{Z}_{q^{\prime}}^{m_{\mathbf{M}}\times n}
, we need to prove in ZK the following relation

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{\mathsf{LWE}}^{(2)}=

\displaystyle\left\{((\mathbf{t},\mathbf{M}),(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{e}_{\mathbf{t}})):\mathbf{t}=\mathbf{M}\cdot\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{e}\bmod q^{\prime}\land\left\|\mathbf{e}\right\|_{\infty}\leq B_{\mathsf{LWE}}\right\}.

Write
\mathbf{t}=\mathbf{M}\cdot\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{e}-q^{\prime}\cdot\mathbf{a}
for
\mathbf{a}\in\mathbb{Z}^{m_{\mathbf{M}}}
, then

q^{\prime}\cdot\left\|\mathbf{a}\right\|_{\infty}\leq\left\|\mathbf{t}\right\|_{\infty}+\left\|\mathbf{M}\cdot\mathbf{x}\right\|_{\infty}+\left\|\mathbf{e}\right\|_{\infty}\leq(q^{\prime}/2)+(q^{\prime}/2)^{2}+B_{\mathsf{PRF}}.

By choosing
q>q^{\prime}+{q^{\prime}}^{2}+2B_{\mathsf{LWE}}
and letting

\displaystyle\overline{\mathbf{A}}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{M}&&\mathbf{I}_{m_{\mathbf{M}}}&&-q^{\prime}\mathbf{I}_{m_{\mathbf{M}}}\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{Z}^{m_{\mathbf{M}}\times(n+2m_{\mathbf{M}})}_{q};

\displaystyle\overline{\mathbf{x}}

\displaystyle=\left(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{e}_{t},\mathbf{a}\right)\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}\times\mathbb{Z}^{m_{\mathbf{M}}}\times\mathbb{Z}^{m_{\mathbf{M}}};

we have that
\overline{\mathbf{A}}\cdot\overline{\mathbf{x}}=\mathbf{t}\bmod q
. By using the technique that transforms relation (
5
) to a case of relation (
3
), we can transform
\mathcal{R}_{\mathsf{LWE}}^{(2)}
to a case of relation (
8
). The final quadratic relation has witness length and number of quadratic constraints approximately
n\log q^{\prime}+m_{\mathbf{M}}\log(2B_{\mathsf{LWE}})+m_{\mathbf{M}}\log(q^{\prime})\approx 4n\log q^{\prime}
.
