An orthotopic organoid-based model to study early CD8⁺ T cell dysfunction and immunotherapy response in colorectal cancer
Authors: Lorenzo Lucantonio, Ludovica Schiano, Francesca Sozio, Andrea Kosta, Giovanna Peruzzi, Rosa Molfetta, Giuseppe Pietropaolo, Mattia Laffranchi, Antonio Francesco Campese, Giovanni Bernardini, Silvano Sozzani, Angela Gismondi, Angela Santoni, Giuseppe Sciumè, Stabile Helena, Cinzia Fionda
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTColorectal cancer (CRC) treatment represents a major clinical challenge, with immunotherapy providing durable responses only in a minority of patients. A deeper understanding of CD8⁺ T cell exhaustion and its contribution to immune checkpoint inhibitor (ICI) responsiveness is essential for the development of more effective therapeutic strategies. Preclinical models that faithfully reproduce the immune landscape of human CRC are therefore critical to address these challenges. Here, we established a syngeneic organoid-based orthotopic CRC mouse model by transplanting quadruple mutant Apc⁻/⁻KrasG12D/+Trp53R172H/⁻Smad4⁻/⁻ (AKPS) intestinal organoids into the rectal submucosa of immunocompetent mice. Single-cell transcriptomic profiling revealed that CD8⁺ T cells represent the predominant leukocyte population within the tumor infiltrate and comprise populations transitioning toward dysfunction. Functionally, CD8⁺ T cell depletion led to increased tumor burden in orthotopic AKPS implants, underscoring their antitumor activity. Importantly, anti-PD-1 treatment increased the abundance of dysfunctional CD8⁺ T cell populations within AKPS tumors and reduced tumor growth, demonstrating the responsiveness of this model to ICIs. In contrast, subcutaneous implants of AKPS were infiltrated by mixed CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ T cell subsets, with CD8⁺ T cells exhibiting a markedly less dysfunctional profile, highlighting the limitations of heterotopic tumor models for studying antitumor immune responses. Together, our findings establish the AKPS orthotopic CRC model as a platform to dissect the molecular mechanisms of early CD8⁺ T cell dysfunction and to preclinically evaluate novel immunotherapeutic interventions in CRC.Keywords: Colorectal cancer, immunotherapy, CD8+ T cell exhaustion

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is one of the most common malignancies and a leading cause of cancer-related death worldwide.1 Traditional therapeutic approaches for CRC, such as surgical resection and chemotherapy, have recently been complemented by immunotherapeutic strategies.2 These treatments are primarily based on the use of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) and are currently effective in only a small proportion of patients (approximately 10%), whose tumors exhibit high microsatellite instability (MSI) and mismatch repair deficiency (MMRd).3,4 These features are associated with a high mutational burden capable of generating numerous neo-antigens and triggering the activation of tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs). Among TILs, CD8⁺ T cells play a central role in the antitumor immune response and their abundance in the tumor microenvironment (TME) is a positive prognostic factor that correlates with improved survival in patients with advanced CRC.5-7 Conversely, lack of CD8⁺ T cell infiltration or impaired CD8⁺ T cell functionality represents a key mechanism underlying resistance to ICIs.8,9 Within the TME, immunosuppressive signals and persistent antigen exposure drive CD8⁺ T cells into a state of exhaustion.10 Compared to effector and memory T cells, exhausted CD8⁺ T cells constitute a distinct population with a unique differentiation pathway.11 They lack effector functions and are characterized by elevated expression of inhibitory receptors as well as by a profound metabolic dysregulation.12 Exhausted CD8⁺ T cells are heterogeneous and include a subset known as progenitor exhausted T cells (Tpex), which gives rise to terminally exhausted T cells (Tex).13-16 Unlike Tex cells, Tpex are defined by the expression of the transcriptional regulator T cell factor 1 (TCF1), intermediate PD-1 levels, and the absence of other inhibitory receptors as well as of CX3CR1, while retaining long-term proliferative and self-renewal capacity.14 Importantly, Tpex cells represent a compelling therapeutic target, as they can expand in response to ICIs and generate novel Tex cells.17-19 Consistently, the presence of Tpex has been proposed as a positive prognostic factor for CRC patient survival and responsiveness to immunotherapy.20,21

To date, CD8⁺ T cell exhaustion remains underexplored in CRC, partly due to the limitations of preclinical models. Immunocompetent murine models may represent a useful tool to study tumor immune infiltration and therapy efficacy; however, most CRC models are resistant to ICIs, such as the AOM/DSS model,22 Apcmin/+ mice23 and the transplantable model based on CT26 cell line.24 To overcome this issue, the mouse model based on MC38 cell line is widely used, since this cell line is highly immunogenic and can generate adenocarcinomas responsive to ICIs.25-28 However, this model suffers from several limitations. For instance, MC38 cell line lacks genetic heterogeneity of human CRC and it is often used as a subcutaneous rather than orthotopic tumor model, raising questions about physiological relevance of these observations since TME of CRC cannot be accurately recapitulated in a different anatomical site.

Tumor intestinal organoids are powerful tools to study CRC. Both in vitro (via CRISPR/Cas9 engineering) and in vivo (isolation from genetically modified mice, GEMMs) approaches can generate intestinal organoids bearing mutations commonly found across CRC subtypes.29,30 Organoid models preserve three-dimensional architecture, cellular heterogeneity, and more faithful interactions between tumor cells and their microenvironment.31,32 Moreover, they can be implanted by orthotopic or heterotopic injections to study both primary and metastatic CRC models in vivo, bridging the gap between in vitro studies and animal models.

In this study, we established a mouse model of CRC based on orthotopic transplantation of quadruple mutant (Apc-/-KrasG12D/+Trp53R172H/-Smad4-/-) intestinal organoids to investigate TILs, with a focus on CD8⁺ T cells. Using transcriptomic and flow cytometry analyzes, we delineated transcriptional states and functional properties of tumor infiltrating CD8⁺ T cells in CRC. Furthermore, we provided evidence that this model is well suited for studying the molecular mechanisms underlying initial phases of CD8⁺ T cell functional impairment, as well as for testing the efficacy of ICIs in CRC.

Methods

Animals

C57BL/6J mice (Jackson Laboratory, stock no. 008449) were obtained from Charles River Laboratories. All experiments were performed using 6–12-week-old mice, and littermates were used as controls. Sample sizes are indicated in the corresponding figure legends. Our study examined both male and female mice, and similar findings are reported for both sexes.

The study was conducted from 2024 to 2026.

Culture of murine intestinal organoids and cell lines

Apc-/-KrasG12D/+Trp53R172H/-Smad4-/- organoids were generously provided by Prof. A. Ramírez de Molina (Molecular Oncology Group, IMDEA Food Institute, Madrid, Spain)33 and embedded in Matrigel (Corning) cultured in Advanced DMEM/F12 supplemented with penicillin/streptomycin, 10 mmol/L HEPES, 1x Glutamax, 1x B27 supplement (Gibco), Y-27632 (10 µM; Stemcell), Nicotinamide (10 mM; Merck), N-Acetylcysteine (1.25 mM; Merck,), Primocin (100 µg/mL; InvivoGen). Organoids were splitted twice per week. The murine colon adenocarcinoma MC38 cell line was generously provided by Prof. Cecilia Garlanda (IRCCS Humanitas Research Hospital, Milan, Italy) and was maintained in RPMI-1640 (Euroclone) supplemented with 10% FCS. Cultures were kept at 37 °C, 5% CO2 in humidity and all cell lines were mycoplasma-free (Abcam). (See supplementary material for Immunofluorescence microscopy of Tumor Organoids and Organoid Genotyping).

Tumor histology and immunofluorescence microscopy

Tumors were formalin fixed for 24 hours, dehydrated, and paraffin embedded. For hematoxylin and eosin staining, 4 µm FFPE tumor sections were rehydrated in alcohol (100%, 90%, 70%, and 50%; 1 minute each step) stained with hematoxylin for 15 minutes, washed in tap water for 10 minutes and stained with eosin for 7 minutes. Dehydration was performed using an ascending alcohol gradient (70%, 90%, 100%, 100%; 1 minute each step), followed by xylene for clearing. Finally, sections were mounted with Eukitt (Merck). Immunofluorescence staining for CD8 was performed on 4 µm FFPE tumor sections rehydrated in alcohol (100%, 90%, 70%, and 50%; 1 minute each step) and placed in citrate buffer 0.01 M pH 6.0 (for 15 minutes in a microwave) for antigen retrieval. Unspecific binding sites were blocked for 30 minutes with 3% BSA. Samples were then incubated with primary antibody Rat Anti-Mouse anti CD8 (Life Technologies) followed by secondary antibody Alexa Fluor donkey anti-rat 594 (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Matched IgG was used as a negative control. Nuclei were stained with DAPI (Thermo Fisher Scientific, diluted 1:10002500) and. Lastly sections were mounted with Pro-Long Diamond Antifade Mountant (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Organoid intrarectal and subcutaneous injection

For tumor injections, a total of 2 × 10⁵ organoid-derived cells or 2 × 105 MC38 cells were resuspended in 50 µL of 50% Matrigel/PBS and injected into the basal membrane of the rectum (orthotopic transplantation) or into the right flank of mice (subcutaneous transplantation). Before injection, mice were anesthetized with Ketamina (100 mg/Kg) and Xylazina (10 mg/Kg). Mice were euthanized 1 week later by cervical dislocation. Tumor dimensions were measured using calipers, and excised tumors were weighed post-mortem. Tumor volume was calculated according to the following formula: [V = 1/2 (length x width2)].

To deplete CD8⁺ T cells, tumor-bearing mice received intraperitoneal injections of 150 µg anti-CD8α monoclonal antibody (clone 53–6.7; Bio X Cell) or control rat IgG2a (clone 2A3; Bio X Cell) on days −1, +3, and +6, with day 0 defined as the time of organoid implantation.

To assess ICIs responsiveness, tumor-bearing mice received intraperitoneal injections of 200 µg anti–PD-1 antibody (clone RMP1-14; Leinco Technologies) or control rat IgG2a (clone 2A3; Bio X Cell) every 2 d after organoid transplantation and were sacrificed on day +10.

Cell isolation, flow cytometry and cell sorting

Cells from intestinal lamina propria and tumors were isolated after incubation of large intestine or AKPS adenocarcinomas in RPMI with 1 mg/mL Collagenase type IV (Sigma-Aldrich) and 0.5 mg/mL DNase I (Sigma-Aldrich) at 37 °C for 1 hour and purification with 40% Percoll (Cytiva), as described before.34 Flow cytometry analysis was performed on a FACSCanto II or LSR Fortessa (Becton Dickinson). Cell sorting was performed using FACSAria III (Becton Dickinson); FACS-sorted cells were confirmed to be higher than 95% pure with post-sorting analysis. Dead cells were excluded by using Ghost dye red 780 Viability (Tombo biosciences). For IFN-γ and TNF-α production, splenic CD8+ T cells and tumor immune infiltrate were cultured for three hours in presence of the phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA)/Ionomycin-based cell stimulation cocktail (1:500) (eBioscience) and Brefeldin A (Sigma Aldrich). Antibodies used in this study can be found in Supplementary Table 2. Data were analyzed using FlowJo_v10.8.1 (Beckton Dickinson). Dimensionality Reduction: tSNE was applied for data visualization using FlowJo’s native plugin. Subset detection was performed using the FlowSom plugin, which generated clusters using a Self-Organizing Map. (See supplementary material for scRNA seq).

Statistical analysis

Statistical tests applied and sample numbers are detailed in the figure legends. For group comparisons, unpaired Student’s t test was used. One-way ANOVA was applied for experiments with multiple groups and independent variables. All analyzes were performed using Prism 9.0 software (GraphPad, La Jolla, CA). Data with P values < 0.05 were considered significant and marked with stars. SD denotes standard deviation. All data are expressed as mean ± SD. For statistical analysis of signature gene enrichment score by scRNA seq, Kruskal-Wallis test, followed by Wilcoxon test with p. adjust method Benjamini-Hochberg procedure (BH) were used. To test differences in module enrichment across murine cluster, human cluster, and MMR status, data were analyzed in R using the emmeans package. The data were reshaped to long format and analyzed using three-way ANOVA (Enrichment ~ seurat_clusters * MouseCluster * MMRStatus). Pairwise comparisons between MMRp and MMRd within each cluster combination were performed using estimated marginal means (emmeans), with Benjamini-Hochberg correction; only adjusted p-values < 0.05 were considered significant. This analysis allowed identification of conserved module enrichments and their association with MMR status.

Results

A time-efficient colorectal cancer mouse model based on orthotopic transplantation of quadruple mutant Apc-/-KrasG12D/+Trp53R172H/-Smad4-/- intestinal organoids

To investigate the role of the immune response against CRC, we sought to establish an orthotopic transplantation model based on tumor intestinal organoids. We employed organoids carrying the most common mutations found in sporadic CRC, including loss-of-function mutations in the Apc, Trp53, and Smad4 genes, as well as the oncogenic KrasG12D mutation.29,30 The intestinal organoids were derived from a genetically engineered mouse strain (Apcflox/floxKrasG12D/+Trp53R172H/floxSmad4flox/flox) and transduced with an adenovirus expressing Cre recombinase to induce the targeted mutations.33 Following CRE activation, the organoids acquired the genetic mutations described above (Figure 1A) and were thereafter referred to as AKPS organoids. These organoids expressed specific intestinal markers, including CHROMOGRANIN A for enteroendocrine cells, MUCIN 2 for goblet cells, as well as epithelial junction proteins, such as E-CADHERIN, p120-CATENIN and ZO-1, as shown by confocal microscopy analysis in Figure 1B. Next, we injected two hundred thousand cells derived from AKPS organoids into the rectal submucosa of immunocompetent wild-type C57BL/6J mice. One-week post-injection, tumor engraftment was observed in the submucosal or basolateral layer of colonic mucosa in all mice. The resulting tumors ranged in weight from 0.05 to 0.15 g and were histologically identified as localized adenocarcinomas (Figure 1C-D).

Collectively, these findings demonstrate the successful establishment of a time-efficient syngeneic organoid-based orthotopic CRC mouse model that recapitulates the molecular features of human CRC.

Single-cell transcriptome analysis reveals accumulation of CD8+ T cell clusters among lymphocytes infiltrating AKPS tumors

To evaluate whether AKPS tumors can provide a valuable platform for investigating antitumor immune response, we next dissected tumor-infiltrating cells from six AKPS tumor-bearing mice. CD45+ lymphocytes (TILs) were isolated and FACS-sorted to perform single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) analysis (Figure 2A). Following quality control, a total of 15,793 TILs were analyzed. These included clusters identified as B cells, innate lymphocytes (NK cells and ILC1), as well as multiple T cell subtypes, including CD4+, CD8+, regulatory T cells (Tregs), and γδ T cells (Figure 2B). Each cell cluster was annotated according to the expression of identity genes, functional molecules and canonical lymphocyte markers (Figure 2C, Supplementary Table 3). The top five genes defining each cluster are also shown (Figure 2D).

Among T cells, we revealed the presence of eleven clusters: seven clusters of CD8+ T cells (CD8a-g), two clusters of CD4+ T cells, and two clusters of γδ T cells. Based on the expression pattern of cytokines and lineage defining transcription factors, CD4+ T cell clusters can be mainly divided in Th1 cells, characterized by high levels of Tbx21 (encoding T-BET) as well as of Ifng and Tnf expression; and Tregs, expressing Foxp3 as well as Il7r, Il10, Lrrc32 and Itgae (CD127+IL10+GARP+CD103+) resembling an early activation phenotype indicative of increased suppressive capacity (Figure 2C).35 Differential abundance analysis showed that CD8+ T cells were the prevalent cell type among lymphocytes infiltrating AKPS tumors (Figure 2E).

Altogether we defined the transcriptional states of TILs in AKPS tumors, which are characterized by accumulation of CD8+ T cell clusters.

Heterogeneity of dysfunctional CD8⁺ T cells within the TIL compartment of AKPS tumors

Given the high prevalence of CD8⁺ T cells among TILs, we focused our analysis on these subpopulations (Figure 3A). Most CD8+ T cells displayed an effector phenotype, characterized by high Cd44 expression and very low levels or complete lack of Sell (CD62L). Notably, these CD8⁺ T cell populations also exhibited features consistent with dysfunction (Figure 3B, Supplementary Table 4). More specifically, we identified the following subsets: the CD8a, c, d and g clusters were marked by the expression of Gzmk, Cx3cr1, and Pdcd1 (PD-1), along with multiple immune checkpoint molecules including Ctla4, Lag3, Havcr2 (TIM-3), Entpd1 (CD39) and Tigit. These clusters showed reduction or lack of Tcf7 (TCF1) expression and were characterized by the presence of transcription factors driving exhaustion (Nr4a1, Nr4a2, Nr4a3) consistent with a transcriptional signature found in populations of Tex36; the CD8b and CD8f clusters (Gzmk⁺ Slamf6⁺Tcf7⁺ Pdcd1⁺ Cx3cr1⁻ Havcr2⁻) displayed a transcriptional profile resembling that of precursors of exhausted T cells (Tpex). They may be recognized as Tpex SLAMF6+ already described in both viral chronic infection and cancer37,38; the CD8e cluster expressed proliferation-associated markers, such as Mki67 (Ki-67), E2f1 and Ccnb2 (Cyclin B2), in addition to Tcf7 and genes encoding for inhibitory receptors. This profile suggests a subset of Tpex cells undergoing transition toward an early state of Tex. Consistently, all clusters expressed low levels of cytokine-related genes, mainly Tnfα and Il2, indicative of a hypofunctional state (Figure 3B).

In agreement with our manual annotation, when ranking CD8+ T cell clusters according to Tex- and Tpex-specific gene signatures,36,37 we found that CD8b and CD8f exhibited the enrichment for Tpex progenitor genes, while CD8d cluster showed elevated expression of interferon stimulated genes (ISGs, Tex ISG) (Figure 3C and Supplementary Table 4). This observation is in accordance with our idea that CD8d is a population starting the differentiation toward Tex. Indeed, early programming of TCF1-dependent CD8 progenitors was shown to be suppressed by type I IFN signaling in both viral infection and cancer.15 In contrast, CD8e displayed heightened expression of genes related to an intermediate Tex state (Tex int), while CD8a, CD8c and CD8g clusters were enriched for terminal Tex (Tex term) state signature genes, even though CD8c and CD8g clusters still retain elevated Tpex associated genes (Figure 3C, Supplementary Figure 1A-B and Supplementary Tables 5–7).

In support of these observations, we performed pseudotime analysis to investigate the developmental trajectory of CD8⁺ T cells during tumor progression by defining the CD8f cluster as the starting point. This revealed a trajectory extending from precursor Tpex clusters (CD8b and CD8f) to terminally exhausted T cells (CD8a, CD8c, CD8d and CD8g) (Figure 3D). Also, as represented in Figure 3E, expression patterns of Tpex and Tex gene signatures, found enriched in the different CD8 clusters, show dynamic processes along pseudotime that are consistent with the presence of distinct dysfunctional states of CD8⁺ T cells.

In this context, we further interrogated our scRNA-seq data to identify pathways known to be associated with CD8⁺ T cell exhaustion in cancer.39-43 Notably, we confirmed an active type I IFN response in the Tpex CD8d cluster while the Tex int CD8e cluster was enriched for genes involved in TGF-β, VEGF, hypoxia- signaling and cholesterol metabolism. Additionally, VEGF, hypoxia-, and cholesterol-associated genes were also highly expressed in the more terminally exhausted CD8a, CD8c and CD8g clusters (Supplementary Figure 2 and Supplementary Table 8).

These findings highlight the potential of our model to study how these pathways regulate CD8⁺ T cell dysfunction and to explore strategies for modulating these processes to reinvigorate antitumor immune responses.

Taken together, analysis of the transcriptional profiles of leukocytes infiltrating AKPS tumors revealed a robust presence of CD8⁺ T cells among adaptive lymphocytes, as well as the heterogeneity of this population, characterized by distinct states of Tex differentiation. These findings suggest that our CRC mouse model can be exploited to investigate the molecular mechanisms underlying CD8⁺ T cell exhaustion. Notably, Tpex are abundantly represented, highlighting the relevance of this model for testing ICIs and underscoring its translational potential.

Phenotypic and functional profiling of CD8+ T cell infiltrating orthotopic AKPS tumors

To complement our transcriptomic analyzes, we first performed immunofluorescence staining of AKPS tumor tissue sections and observed a robust infiltration of CD8⁺ T cells (Figure 4A). Consistently, phase-contrast imaging put in evidence that CD8⁺ T cells accumulated within the tumor mass, with few cells detectable at the margins. We next investigated the functional contribution of CD8⁺ T cells to the antitumor immune response in our CRC model. Mice were treated with a depleting anti-CD8α antibody starting 24 hours before orthotopic implantation of tumor-derived intestinal organoids and subsequently every two days until analysis. Efficient depletion of tumor infiltrating CD8⁺ T cells was confirmed by flow cytometry (Figure 4B), which was accompanied by a relative increase in the frequency of CD4⁺ T cells within the lymphocyte compartment. CD8⁺ T cell depletion resulted in a significant increase in tumor burden compared to control mice (Figure 4B), demonstrating that CD8⁺ T cells actively contribute to tumor containment during the early phase of tumor establishment in this model.

Based on these observations, we further characterized leukocytes infiltrating AKPS tumors and adjacent healthy tissue by flow cytometry using a combination of surface and intracellular markers.

T lymphocytes were identified as CD45⁺CD3ε⁺TCRβ⁺ cells and subsequently classified into CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ T cell subsets based on the expression of their respective coreceptors (Figure 4C and Supplementary Figure 3).

As shown in Figure 4C and D, one-week post-injection, flow cytometry analysis revealed differences in T cell distribution between the large intestinal lamina propria (liLP) and the AKPS colorectal tumors. The immune infiltrate within CRC tissue showed an increased overall T cell abundance, with a predominance of CD8⁺ T cells, in contrast to the liLP, where CD4⁺ T cells were more prevalent. Furthermore, the T cell infiltrate in AKPS tumors was predominantly composed of effector CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ T cells, defined by the expression profile CD44⁺CD62L⁻ (Supplementary Figure 4A).

In line with the scRNA-seq findings, CD8⁺ T cells infiltrating the AKPS tumors express high levels of inhibitory receptors, including PD-1, TIM-3, NKG2A, and CD94, alongside the cytolytic molecule Granzyme B (GZMB). Moreover, these cells showed a loss of TCF1 expression, which was further confirmed by immunofluorescence microscopy analysis (Supplementary Figure 4B). Although TCF1 expression was also significantly reduced in CD4+ T lymphocytes, these cells did not display an inhibitory phenotype (Figure 4E-F; Supplementary Figure 3).

To assess the functional capacity of tumor infiltrating CD8⁺ T cells, we performed short-term ex vivo restimulation assays. Tumor infiltrating lymphocytes and splenocytes were stimulated in vitro for 3 hours with phorbol myristate acetate (PMA) and ionomycin, followed by intracellular cytokine staining to evaluate IFN-γ and TNF-α production.

As shown in Figure 4G-H, intra-tumoral CD8⁺ T cells displayed a frequency of IFN-γ-producing cells comparable to that observed in splenic CD8⁺ T cells. In contrast, TNF-α production was significantly reduced in tumor-infiltrating CD8⁺ T cells relative to their splenic counterparts. Accordingly, phenotypic analysis revealed that intra-tumoral CD8⁺ T cells predominantly consisted of TCF1⁻PD-1⁺ cells and were more enriched for CXCR6⁺ cells (a marker of Tex) than for CX3CR1⁺ cells (a marker of transitory effector-like Tex) (Figure 4I).14,44,45

These data indicate a selective impairment in TNF-α production within the intra-tumoral CD8⁺ T cell compartment. Notably, previous studies have shown that during CD8⁺ T cell dysfunction, defects in TNF-α production arise earlier and are more pronounced than those affecting IFN-γ production reflecting the progressive loss of CD8⁺ T cell polyfunctionality,12,18,46,47 consistent with the functional profile observed in our model.

Collectively, these results highlight a rapid activation of adaptive lymphocytes in response to AKPS tumors, with CD8⁺ T cells representing the predominant subset acquiring features of T cell dysfunction.

Tumor microenvironment and mutational burden shape the phenotypic diversity of CD8+ T cell infiltrating CRC

Given the pivotal role of tumor location in shaping immune infiltration, we concomitantly analyzed TILs in subcutaneously implanted AKPS tumors after 1 week (Figure 5A). We found that in subcutaneous AKPS tumors T lymphocytes were equally represented by CD4+ and CD8+ subsets, with a very low percentage of exhausted CD8+ T cells (TCF1-TIM3+) (Figure 5B and C).

These observations revealed significant differences in immune infiltrate composition between tumors derived from identical AKPS organoids engrafted subcutaneously or within the colonic mucosa. Therefore, these results argue against the indiscriminate use of subcutaneous tumor models for studying antitumor immune responses across different tumor types.

Next, we sought to determine whether and how TILs in our CRC mouse model differ from those in the MC38 model, which is widely used to study CD8⁺ T cell exhaustion and responses to ICIs. To this end, we performed parallel orthotopic transplantations of AKPS organoids and MC38 cells and analyzed TILs by flow cytometry (Figure 5D). While the overall magnitude of T cell infiltration was comparable between AKPS and MC38 tumors, MC38 adenocarcinomas were characterized by a balanced distribution of CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ T cell subsets. Notably, the CD8⁺ T cell compartment in MC38 tumors contained fewer TCF1⁻ cells but they exhibited a more pronounced inhibitory phenotype (Figure 5E and F). These findings reveal substantial immunological differences between the two CRC models, likely reflecting variations in mutational burden and the ability to establish distinct TMEs.

Given that the MC38 cell line is widely recognized as a hypermutated, immunogenic model resembling MSI-H CRC,26 while AKPS organoids genetically mirror the MSS subtype,48,49 we sought to further contextualize our findings in human CRC. To this end, we independently analyzed a publicly available scRNA-seq dataset of immune cells isolated from CRC tumors from 69 patients (GSE178341),50 including both MMR-proficient (MMRp) and MMR-deficient (MMRd) samples, focusing specifically on CD8⁺ T cells.

This analysis identified eight transcriptionally distinct CD8⁺ T cell clusters (Supplementary Figure 5A and B), corresponding to effector, Tpex, or exhausted (Tex) states.51 Clusters 2, 5, 6 and 7 displayed transcriptional programs characteristic of Tpex cells, whereas clusters 0, 1, 3, and 4 were enriched for genes associated with Tex CD8⁺ T cells (Supplementary Figure 5C; Supplementary Tables 9 and 10).

To assess the correspondence between the mouse and human datasets, we mapped orthologous genes between murine CD8⁺ T cell clusters and their human counterparts. We observed that the murine Tex CD8a, CD8c, CD8d, and CD8g clusters showed high enrichment scores in human Tex-like clusters, including clusters 0, 1, and 3. In contrast, the CD8b cluster was more closely related to human clusters 2, 5, and 7, which resemble Tpex cells (Supplementary Figure 5D and Supplementary Table 11). Overall, this analysis revealed a consistent cross-species similarity, supporting the translational relevance of the AKPS model.

Notably, the degree of similarity between the murine CD8⁺ T cell clusters and their human counterparts varied according to MMR status, with some clusters showing higher enrichment in MMR-proficient (MMRp) tumors (Supplementary Table 12). This observation supports the notion that the AKPS model more faithfully recapitulates the immune microenvironment of MSS CRC.

AKPS CRC model as a preclinical platform to evaluate anti-PD-1 immunotherapy responsiveness

Gaining a deeper understanding of CD8⁺ T cell exhaustion and its role in ICI responsiveness is crucial for designing more effective therapeutic strategies. Thus, we assessed how PD-1/PD-L1 pathway blockade modulates the immune response in AKPS adenocarcinomas. To this end, mice bearing orthotopic AKPS tumors received intraperitoneal injections of an anti–PD-1 blocking antibody every two days, starting 48 hours after tumor organoid transplantation (Figure 6A). Anti-PD-1 treatment led to a significant reduction in tumor volume and an increased proportion of TCF1⁻ CD8⁺ T cells expressing the receptors TIM-3 and CXCR6, and the proliferation marker Ki-67 within AKPS tumors (Figure 6B–F), indicating that PD-1 blockade effectively promoted the differentiation of newly generated Tex cells and reinvigorated the CD8⁺ T cell-mediated antitumor response in this model of CRC.

Collectively, our findings support the utility of the AKPS orthotopic CRC model as a robust preclinical platform to assess ICI responsiveness and efficacy.

Discussion

It is well established that immunosuppression and immune cell dysfunction contribute to CRC development and progression.52 However, current immunotherapies provide durable benefit only to a limited number of CRC patients.3,4 Therefore, there is a critical need for preclinical models that can elucidate the molecular mechanisms driving antitumor immunity, evaluate drug efficacy in vivo, and support the development of more effective therapeutic strategies.

We have established a murine model of CRC that offers distinct advantages for investigating tumor-immune interactions. This model is based on tumor intestinal organoids that, in contrast to conventional tumor cell lines, more faithfully recapitulate the biological and molecular features of sporadic human CRC.30,33 The organoids harbor the most common driver mutations (Apc, Kras, Trp53, Smad4) in human CRC, thereby enhancing their translational relevance. Moreover, by implanting the organoids orthotopically into the colonic submucosa, our model preserves the anatomical, stromal, and immunological context of the intestinal microenvironment, key determinants of CRC pathogenesis and immune evasion.53 Finally, it is highly reproducible and time-efficient, generating localized adenocarcinomas within one week of transplantation.

However, a limitation of this model should be acknowledged. The rapid tumor development, occurring within approximately one week, does not fully recapitulate the chronic antigenic stimulation that characterizes tumor initiation and progression in CRC patients. As a result, it may not fully capture the long-term dynamics and stability of terminally exhausted CD8⁺ T cell populations observed in more advanced disease settings. Notably, this limitation is not unique to this model, as the chronicity and magnitude of antigenic stimulation are inherently difficult to model and standardize, even in subcutaneous transplantable systems. Nevertheless, the AKPS model provides a suitable system for dissecting the early mechanisms underlying CD8⁺ T cell dysfunction in CRC.

In epithelial tumors, tissue-resident memory (TRM) CD8⁺ T cells are thought to play a central role in antitumor immunity, contributing to cancer-immune equilibrium and tumor progression.54,55 Emerging evidence further suggests that these populations may give rise to exhausted T cells under conditions of persistent antigen stimulation as in cancer.56 Notably, TRM CD8⁺ T cells were not detected among AKPS-infiltrating lymphocytes; instead, we identified Tpex populations that may arise at early stages of the response, potentially reflecting exposure to a relatively high antigen load and/or high affinity TCR interactions in this model.57

Single-cell transcriptomic profiling of tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes revealed a heterogeneous CD8⁺ T cell compartment encompassing Tpex, Tex int, and Tex term subsets. The high frequency of Tpex cells is particularly noteworthy, as this population sustains the exhausted T cell pool through self-renewal and differentiation into Tex cells.19,37 Tpex cells are also the primary responders to ICIs, capable of proliferating and replenishing Tex progeny.18,21 In line with this, we demonstrated that PD-1 blockade in our model increased the proportion of exhausted CD8⁺ T cells, consistent with the expansion of Tpex-derived progeny following ICI, and reduced tumor volume. This responsiveness underscores the utility of the AKPS system for testing novel immunotherapeutic combinations designed to reinvigorate dysfunctional T cells in CRC.

Comparative analyzes further highlighted the importance of model design in shaping immune dynamics. Subcutaneous AKPS tumors and the orthotopic MC38 model exhibited markedly reduced CD8⁺ T cell infiltration and differences in exhaustion profile. Regarding AKPS implants, although both models originate from syngeneic sources, only the orthotopic AKPS tumors displayed robust CD8⁺ infiltration and a complex exhaustion continuum. These discrepancies likely reflect the profound influence of anatomical location on the immune landscape. Orthotopic implantation allows for physiological interactions between tumor cells, intestinal stroma, vasculature, and microbiota. Nevertheless, the importance of the genetic background of the tumor in shaping TME was underscored by differences in TILs composition in orthotopic MC38 tumors. While MC38 tumors resemble hypermutated,26 MSI-H CRC, AKPS organoids genetically mirror the MSS subtype.48,49 Our complementary analysis of a human scRNA-seq dataset further supports this interpretation: the murine AKPS CD8⁺ T cell clusters show the highest similarity to clusters enriched in MMR-proficient (MMRp) tumors, highlighting that the AKPS model faithfully recapitulates key aspects of the immune microenvironment of MSS CRC.

These findings caution against generalizing immunotherapy responses from models with distinct genetic and immunological characteristics and emphasize the AKPS organoid-based model as a robust preclinical platform to study CD8⁺ T cell responses in MSS CRC, a clinically relevant subtype for which predictive models are limited.

Mechanistically, our transcriptomic data revealed the engagement of several tumor-derived pathways, including type I IFN, TGF-β, VEGF, hypoxia, and cholesterol metabolism, in shaping CD8⁺ T cell exhaustion.39-43 The AKPS model thus provides an opportunity to dissect how these tumor-driven metabolic and signaling cues orchestrate the transition from Tpex to Tex, and how their modulation might enhance the efficacy of immunotherapy.

In conclusion, the AKPS orthotopic CRC model represents a useful and adaptable platform for studying tumor-immune crosstalk in a genetically and anatomically faithful context. By reflecting some features of the heterogeneity and functional plasticity of CD8⁺ T cells, this system enables the investigation of molecular circuits governing early phases of T cell dysfunction and provides a platform for evaluating next-generation immunotherapeutic strategies. Future studies leveraging this model may elucidate how stromal and metabolic factors drive T cell dysfunction, ultimately guiding the rational design of combination therapies aimed at restoring durable antitumor immunity in CRC.
