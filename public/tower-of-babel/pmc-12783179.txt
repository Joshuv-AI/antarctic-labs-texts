Rab23 GTPase and IFT43 regulate the trafficking of prostaglandin E receptor 4 to primary cilia
Authors: Yewei Chen, Yating Zhou, Yao Qi, Tingting Miao, Jianan Shi, Daqing Jin, Tao P. Zhong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Primary cilia are crucial organelles involved in various cellular signaling pathways. Prostaglandin E receptor 4 (EP4), a G protein-coupled receptor (GPCR), localizes to both cilia and cellular membranes, playing pivotal roles in inflammation, immunomodulation, and cancer progression. However, the mechanisms underlying EP4 trafficking to cilia remain poorly understood, hindering further exploration of its ciliary pathophysiological functions. In this study, we unveil EP4 expression in primary cilia across zebrafish embryos, mouse tissues, and human cells. We identify the third intracellular (IC3) loop and C-terminal (CT) region in EP4 as essential for its ciliary translocation, with the LPG motif serving as a key ciliary localization sequence. A single amino acid substitution in the LPG motif, introduced by CRISPR C-to-G base editing (CGBE1), blocks EP4 trafficking to cilia and reduces cAMP-PKA signaling. High-content siRNA screening identifies the small GTPase Rab23 and intraflagellar transport protein 43 (IFT43) as crucial regulators of EP4 ciliary trafficking in zebrafish and mammalian cells. Furthermore, EP4 physically interacts with IFT43 and Rab23 through its specific domains in the IC3 loop and CT region. These findings provide important insights into the conserved Rab23/IFT43-mediated ciliary trafficking mechanism, with significant implications for understanding the pathophysiological roles mediated by EP4 signaling in cilia.

Introduction

Primary cilia are microtubule-based organelles that protrude from the apical surface of most animal cells. The ciliary membrane is highly enriched with signaling receptors, including GPCRs, creating a concentrated microenvironment for signaling cascades within the ciliary compartment1. Disruption of ciliary signaling has been implicated in a broad spectrum of human disorders collectively known as ciliopathies, which present with diverse clinical features such as obesity, polydactyly, and kidney dysfunction1. At the base of the cilia, the transition zone (TZ) or ciliary gate establishes a crucial boundary that separates the ciliary membrane from the cell membrane and acts as a diffusion barrier for the entry or exit of ciliary proteins2. Although small soluble proteins may diffuse freely into cilia, the transportation of most ciliary cargo proteins from the cytoplasm to the ciliary shaft requires specialized trafficking machinery3,4.

GPCRs constitute the largest family of membrane receptors in humans, comprising ~800 members. Of these, more than 30 GPCRs have been identified to localize in cilia across a spectrum of cell types and tissues5,6. Their transportation from the cytoplasm into the cilium is facilitated by specific cargo adapters3,7. For example, Tubby-like protein-3 (TULP3) serves as a cargo adapter and orchestrates the ciliary trafficking of GPCRs to cilia, including melanin-concentrating hormone receptor 1 (MCHR1) and somatostatin receptor type 3 (SSTR3)7. Similarly, RABL2, a protein in the RAB-like family, contributes to the ciliary transport of G protein-coupled receptor 161 (GPR161) and serotonin receptor 6 (HTR6) by interacting with IFT-B complex8. The small GTPase Rab23 has important effects on Sonic hedgehog (Shh) signaling and Carpenter syndrome9,10. Rab23 deletion can prevent the D1-type dopamine receptor (D1R) from accessing the ciliary gate11. In addition, ciliary location sequences (CLSs) are crucial signals for mediating the targeting of GPCRs to cilia12,13. Although specific cargo adapters and CLSs have been identified to guide certain types of GPCRs to cilia, it remains largely unknown how other groups of GPCRs, including EP4 and MC4R, are transported to the cilium.

EP4, a member of the GPCR family, is one of four receptors for binding prostaglandin E2 (PGE2), which is widely distributed throughout diverse tissues and cell types. PGE2-EP4 signaling regulates numerous pathophysiological processes, including inflammation, immunomodulation, cardiovascular homeostasis, and cancer progression14. Our studies previously elucidated the localization of EP4 within the primary cilium of murine inner medullary collecting duct 3 (IMCD3) cells and human retinal pigment epithelial 1 (hRPE1) cells15. Similarly, EP4 is detectable in the cilium of mouse pancreatic α-cell (α-TC9) and β-cell (MIN6) lines, where it stimulates glucagon and insulin secretion16. However, little insight has been obtained about the mechanisms underlying EP4 translocation to cilia, specifically whether specific adapters and CLSs are needed for its ciliary transportation and how they function together to regulate EP4 trafficking to cilia.

In this study, we combine zebrafish embryos and mammalian cells to investigate the mechanisms underlying EP4 ciliary transportation. We identify the C-terminal LPG motif as a CLS that directs EP4 to cilia. To measure EP4-mediated cAMP signaling in cilia, we also developed a ciliary cAMP biosensor, NPHP3-G-Flamp1, with enhanced fluorescence brightness and fast response kinetics. We also show that EP4 transport to cilia requires IFT43 and Rab23, whose depletions impair EP4 ciliary localization in mammalian cells and zebrafish embryos. We thus propose a Rab23/IFT43-mediated ciliary trafficking model involving a relay delivery of EP4 through Rab23 and IFT43, shedding light on exploring the functions of EP4-generating signaling in the cilium.

Results

EP4 is localized in the primary cilia across diverse zebrafish tissues and mammalian cells

We previously observed the presence of EP4 within primary cilia in cultured mammalian cells, including IMCD3 and hRPE1 cells15. To explore whether EP4 is localized to primary cilia in diverse organs and tissues, we examined its ciliary localization in zebrafish embryos and mouse tissues. Due to genome duplication in zebrafish, three ep4 isoform genes were identified17, including ep4a, ep4b, and ep4c. We synthesized mCherry-tagged ep4a, ep4b, or ep4c mRNAs and microinjected each of fusion mRNAs into Tg(βactin2: Arl13b-GFP) transgenic zebrafish embryos, in which the fused ciliary marker Arl13b-GFP is expressed under the beta-actin2 promoter18 (Fig. 1A). Live imaging of these embryos revealed the co-localization of Ep4c-mCherry with Arl13b-GFP at primary cilia on the body surface and in the otic vesicles (Supplementary Fig. 1A–F). In contrast, Ep4a-mCherry and Ep4b-mCherry failed to display such co-localization (Supplementary Fig. 1G–R). Given the relatively subdued fluorescence signals observed in other ciliary organs, we further injected ep4c-EGFP mRNA into wild-type embryos and performed double immunofluorescent staining using an EGFP antibody and an anti-acetylated α-tubulin (AC-TUB) antibody. Remarkably, Ep4c-EGFP is localized to AC-TUB-positive cilia in the spinal cord, the pronephric duct, and the otic vesicles in zebrafish (Fig. 1B). In mice, the co-localization of endogenous EP4 and IFT88 was detectable in the primary cilia of renal tubular epithelial cells and the connecting cilia of photoreceptor cells (Fig. 1C, D). In cultured mammalian cells, native EP4 was also localized to the primary cilia of human embryonic kidney 293 T cells (HEK293T) and murine chondrogenic ATDC5 cells by double immunostaining of EP4 and ARL13B antibodies (Fig. 1E). EP4 ciliary localization in these cells was further validated by the transduction of lentiviruses encoding EP4 fused with C-terminal EGFP (EP4-EGFP) (Fig. 1F). The C-terminal region of EP4 is both necessary and sufficient for its ciliary localization in zebrafish and mammalian cells

Multiple GPCRs, including GPR161, HTR6, and SSTR3, have been reported to harbor CLSs within the third intracellular loops (IC3) or C-terminal tail (CT)12,19,20. To probe the presence of CLSs within IC3 or CT regions of the EP4 receptor, we generated IC3 deletion (212-258aa) and CT deletion (395-488aa) mutants in the EP4-EGFP construct (Fig. 2B). ATDC5 or IMCD3 cells were infected with lentivirus expressing the EP4-IC3-del-EGFP or EP4-CT-del-EGFP construct and were subsequently subjected to immunostaining for ARL13B and EGFP (Fig. 2A). We found that deletion of either IC3 loop or CT domain caused a substantial reduction in the percentage of cells exhibiting ciliary localization of EP4-EGFP (Fig. 2C-E). Next, we generated a series of deletion mutants targeting the IC3 loop and CT domain (Fig. 2F). IC3 loop deletions include IC3-del1 (212-224aa), IC3-del2 (225-238aa), and IC3-del3 (239-258aa). CT domain deletions comprise CT-del1 (395-408aa), CT-del2 (409-420aa), CT-del3 (458-474aa), and CT-del4 (475-488aa) (Fig. 2F). We found that IC3-del1 and CT-del2 exhibited the most reduction in EP4 transport to cilia compared to full-length EP4 (Fig. 2G, Supplementary Fig. 2). IC3-del2 and CT-del3 possessed reduced ciliary EP4 transport (Fig. 2G, Supplementary Fig. 2). In contrast, IC3-del3, CT-del1, and CT-del4 caused only moderate EP4 ciliary transport (Fig. 2G, Supplementary Fig. 2). Importantly, none of these deletion mutations led to EP4 protein degradation, as confirmed by immunoblotting (Supplementary Fig. 3A, B). To test whether IC3 loop or CT domain is sufficient for ciliary targeting, chimeric experiments were conducted by swapping IC3 loop or CT domain of EP4 with that of EP3, a non-ciliary localized receptor (Fig. 3A, B). Notably, translocation of EP3 to the cilium occurred only upon the replacement of CT domain, but not IC3 loop (Fig. 3B, C), suggesting that CT domain is sufficient to promote EP4 trafficking to cilia. Similarly, in zebrafish, replacing non-ciliary Ep4a-CT with Ep4c-CT also resulted in the transportation of Ep4a to cilia, whereas substituting Ep4a-IC3 with Ep4c-IC3 failed to trigger such translocation (Fig. 3D; Supplementary Fig. 4). Furthermore, microinjection of zebrafish embryos with EGFP-tagged ep4c mRNA lacking CT domain completely abolished its localization to cilia in various cell types (Fig. 3E). Next, we performed alanine (A) scan mutagenesis for every three amino acids across CT-del2 regions whose deletion caused the most reduction in EP4 localization to cilia (Figs. 2G, 3F). Western blot analysis showed that these substitution mutations did not cause degradation of the EP4-EGFP fusion protein (Supplementary Fig. 3C). Notably, we observed that CT-2-mut3 (LPG-AAA, p.415-417) completely inhibited the EP4 ciliary targeting, and CT-2-mut4 (VPG-AAA, p.418-420) resulted in a partial reduction in EP4 ciliary localization (Fig. 3G, H). However, CT-2-mut1 and CT-2-mut2 failed to impair EP4 ciliary localization (Fig. 3G, H). Notably, mutating individual amino acids in the LPG motif (p.415-417), including L415A, P416A, or G417A, almost abolished EP4 localization to cilia in both ATDC5 and IMCD3 cells (Fig. 3I-K), indicating that the LPG motif is most critical for EP4’s ciliary localization.

Endogenous EP4P416A and EP4P416S mutations abrogate its ciliary translocation and cAMP-PKA signaling

To evaluate the impact of mutations in the LPG motif on the endogenous EP4 localization, we performed a single amino acid substitution in the LPG motif of EP4 in HEK293T cells. This was achieved using the single-base editor CGBE1, which catalyzes C-to-G substitution by CRISPR-guided cytosine deaminase21. Specifically, EP4-sgRNA was designed to target the 1246 C in the exon 3 region, which corresponds to the first base codon of p416 Proline in the LPG motif (Fig. 4A). We next transfected CGBE1 with EP4 sgRNA and achieved a 45% editing efficiency for C-to-G substitution (1246 C > G) at the C4 position upstream of PAM, resulting in the P416A alteration (Fig. 4A), as confirmed by Sanger sequencing. Intriguingly, a 15% efficiency for C-to-T conversion at the same C4 position was also observed, leading to P416S conversion (Fig. 4A). These results suggest that the CGBE1 editor converts either C-to-G or C-to-T, consistent with previous studies21. Through a limiting dilution selection method, we ultimately isolated and established two mutant cell clones: EP4P416A (1246 C > G, p.416 P416A) and EP4P416S (1246 C > T, p.416 P416S). Immunofluorescence staining revealed a substantial reduction in the EP4 ciliary localization in both mutant cell lines (Fig. 4B, C), whereas the EP4 membrane localization appeared unaffected (Fig. 4B). Furthermore, cilium number and length were largely unchanged in both EP4P416S and EP4P416A mutant cells (Fig. 4D, E), and EP4 protein levels were also unaltered (Fig. 4F). As a Gαs-coupled receptor, EP4 stimulates adenylate cyclase to initiate intracellular cAMP production22. We hypothesized that disruptions in EP4 ciliary localization might alter cAMP dynamics within the cilium. To test this, we developed a ciliary cAMP biosensor, NPHP3-G-Flamp1, which combines G-Flamp1 (green fluorescent cAMP indicator 1)23 with the NPHP3 ciliary localization sequence24 (Fig. 4G). G-Flamp1 consists of a highly responsive circularly permuted GFP (cpGFP) and a cyclic nucleotide-binding domain of the MlotiK1 potassium channel (mlCNBD)23, in which cpGFP and mlCNBD had been engineered to enhance fluorescence brightness, cAMP binding affinity, and fast response kinetics23. mCherry protein was also incorporated into the biosensor to serve as an internal fluorescence reference. We infected HEK293T cells with lentivirus-packaged NPHP3-G-Flamp1 and then treated them with forskolin (FSK). The FSK administration elicited a dose- and time-dependent increase in the G-Flamp1/mCherry fluorescence index within the cilium (Fig. 4H, I). We observed that ciliary cAMP increased rapidly within 30 s by FSK treatment compared to the vehicle controls (Fig. 4H, I), and cAMP levels in cilia reached a peak plateau around 2 min (Fig. 4I).

We next treated these cells with EP4 agonist CAY10598 and observed a swift increase in G-Flamp1 fluorescence within the cilium of WT cells compared with the vehicle controls (Fig. 4J, M, Supplementary Fig. 5B). In contrast, EP4P416S cells failed to display the ciliary cAMP elevation (Fig. 4K, N, Supplementary Fig. 5C). To specifically assess cytoplasmic cAMP dynamics in EP4P416S cells, we generated a cytoplasmic cAMP biosensor by removing the NPHP3-derived ciliary localization signal (Supplementary Fig. 5A). Using this biosensor, we observed the cytoplasmic G-Flamp1 fluorescence in EP4P416S cells still gradually increased upon CAY10598 treatment, similar to the cytoplasmic cAMP response observed in wild-type HEK293T cells (Fig. 4L, O, Supplementary Fig. 5D, E), suggesting a defective cAMP response in the cilium but not in the cytoplasm of EP4P416S mutant cells. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that EP4 signaling activates cAMP production within the cilium.

Phosphorylation of the cAMP-response element-binding protein (CREB) is a crucial event in the EP4-cAMP-PKA signaling transduction14. To explore the function of ciliary EP4 in activating the pathway, we analyzed the levels of phosphorylated CREB (pCREB) following lentivirus expressing the EP4AAA mutant protein in ATDC5 cells, in which LPG residues were substituted with AAA to disrupt ciliary targeting. After inducing ciliogenesis, the cells were exposed to graded concentrations of CAY10598 to stimulate the EP4-cAMP-PKA pathway. We observed a gradual increase in pCREB protein levels in WT EP4-expressing cells compared to unphosphorylated CREB protein levels (Fig. 4P, Q). Intriguingly, ATDC5 cells expressing EP4AAA mutant protein attenuated the increased extent of pCREB protein levels (Fig. 4P, Q), suggesting that ciliary EP4 partially regulates cAMP-PKA signaling in ATDC5 cells.

High-content siRNA screen identifies Rab23 and IFT43 as critical adapters in promoting EP4 ciliary translocation

Tulp3 deletion has been shown to decrease EP4 ciliary localization in MIN6 and α-TC9 cells16. We thus tested whether TULP3 is required for EP4 translocation to cilia in ATDC5 and IMCD3 cells. Tulp3-/- cell clones in both ATDC5 and IMCD3 cells were generated and isolated using CRISPR/Cas9-mediated knockout technique. We observed a complete loss of ciliary ARL13B localization in Tulp3-/- ATDC5 and IMCD3 cell clones (Supplementary Fig. 6A–C), a known TULP3 null phenotype25–27. However, the ciliary levels of EP4 immunostaining were reduced but not absent in Tulp3-/- cells compared to WT cells (Supplementary Fig. 6D–H), suggesting that EP4 ciliary translocation does not fully rely on TULP3.

To resolve the nature of mechanisms directing EP4 to cilia, we embarked on a high-throughput and high-content siRNA screening targeting 170 genes implicated in cilia trafficking (Fig. 5A, Supplementary Table 1). This included members of the Tubby family28, Bardet-Biedl syndrome (BBS) proteins29, intraflagellar transport (IFT) proteins30, and the small GTPase family31. Each gene was targeted by a SMARTpool of four siRNAs. We then transfected the ATDC5 cells that stably express EP4-EGFP in each well with four specific siRNAs targeting each candidate gene. After inducing cilia formation through starvation, AC-TUB immunostaining were performed. The cell fluorescence images were automatically captured and quantitatively analyzed using Opera Phenix™ high-content screening and imaging system to assess EP4 ciliary localization (Fig. 5A). From this screen, we identified Rab23, a member of the small GTPase Rab family, and IFT43, the molecular stabilizer of the IFT-A base module, as key regulatory factors of EP4 ciliary trafficking (Supplementary Fig. 7). Recent studies have shown that IFT43 acts as a molecular tether to traverse the base module of IFT-A complex to stabilize its structure32. These results suggest that IFT43 not only serves as a molecular stabilizer of IFT-A complex but also participates in EP4 ciliary trafficking. To validate these findings, we specifically knocked down Rab23 or Ift43 in both ATDC5 and IMCD3 cells by siRNAs interference and observed a substantial reduction in the percentage of cells exhibiting EP4 localization (Fig. 5B, C, Supplementary Fig. 8). Furthermore, the impairment in EP4 ciliary localization in cells transfected with Rab23 siRNA was reversed by introducing a non-targetable mutant Rab23 (Rab23-NTM) that contains six synonymous mutations in the siRNA-targeted sequence (Fig. 5B, D), confirming the specificity of the siRNA-mediated knockdown effects. We next generated and isolated Rab23 knockout ATDC5 cell clones using CRISPR/Cas9 technology and a limiting dilution selection method. Western blot analysis verified the absence of Rab23 protein expression in these mutant cell clones (Fig. 5F). As expected, we observed a substantial reduction in the percentage of cells presenting EP4 ciliary localization in individual Rab23-/- clones compared to WT cells (Fig. 5E, G). Moreover, the Rab23 knockout did not interfere with ciliary formation (Fig. 5H). To validate the role of Rab23 in ciliary trafficking of Ep4c in zebrafish, we generated rab23-/- mutants using CRISPR/Cas9 gene editing technology. These mutants harbored a 5 bp deletion in exon 2, resulting in a truncated polypeptide that terminates at amino acid 69 (Fig. 5I, J). Immunoblot analysis confirmed the loss of Rab23 protein in rab23−/− embryos (Fig. 5J). We also generated a transgenic zebrafish line Tg(ef1α: ep4c-EGFP), in which ep4c-EGFP was expressed under the control of ef1α promoter. These transgenic fish were crossed with rab23+/− to generate Tg(ef1α: ep4c-EGFP)/rab23+/- zebrafish. Tg(ef1α: ep4c-EGFP)/rab23−/− and sibling control embryos were subsequently produced by in-crossing Tg(ef1α: ep4c-EGFP)/rab23+/- fish. Immunostaining of Tg(ef1α: ep4c-EGFP)/rab23−/− embryos revealed a significant reduction in the ciliary localization of Ep4c-EGFP, while ciliogenesis remained unaffected (Fig. 5K–P). This result is consistent with the findings in ATDC5 and IMCD3 cells, suggesting a conserved role for Rab23 in directing EP4 ciliary trafficking in zebrafish and mammalian cells.

EP4 interacts with Rab23 and IFT43 during ciliary trafficking

Given the involvement of Rab23 and IFT43 in EP4 ciliary localization, we explored their physical interactions. HEK293T cells were transfected with plasmids encoding Flag-tagged EP4 and HA-tagged Rab23 or HA-tagged IFT43. Immunoprecipitation assays revealed significant associations between EP4 and Rab23 (Fig. 5Q), as well as between EP4 and IFT43 (Fig. 5R). However, no interaction between Rab23 and IFT43 was detected when cells were transfected with plasmids encoding HA-tagged Rab23 and Myc-tagged IFT43 (Supplementary Fig. 9A). As Rab23 and IFT43 promote EP4 trafficking to cilia, these results suggest that EP4 ciliary transport might be through sequential contacts with Rab23 and IFT43, but not simultaneously. Unexpectedly, we found that EP4LPG-AAA mutation failed to disrupt the interaction between EP4 and Rab23 or IFT43 (Fig. 5Q, R), suggesting that additional regions in EP4 are involved in its interaction with Rab23. This finding is also reminiscent of similar phenomena observed in the interactions between HTR6 and TULP3, where the absence of the LPG motif critical for HTR6 ciliary localization did not prevent its association with TULP312. As a control, Flag-EP4 did not interact with HA-Cherry (Fig. 5Q, R).

Because the deletion of IC3d1 (212–224 aa), CTd2 (409–420 aa), or CTd3 (458–474 aa) significantly reduced EP4 ciliary targeting (Fig. 2G), we next examined the implications of these deletions on the interaction between EP4 and Rab23. Interestingly, deletion of any one of these regions individually did not disrupt the interaction between EP4 and Rab23 (Fig. 5S). However, simultaneous deletions of all three regions completely abolished the interaction between EP4 and Rab23 (Fig. 5T). Further investigation revealed that EP4-IC3d1-CTd2 deletion, but not EP4-CTd2-CTd3 deletion, interfered with the interaction between EP4 and Rab23 (Fig. 5T, Supplementary Fig. 9B). These findings suggest that EP4 interacts with Rab23 through the IC3d1 loop and the LPG-contained CTd2 domain, consistent with the functional requirement of these domains for EP4 ciliary transportation (Fig. 2G).

Discussion

In this study, we have elucidated the mechanisms underlying EP4 ciliary transportation and signaling within ciliary compartments. We reveal EP4 localization in diverse types of cilia in zebrafish embryos, mouse tissues, and mammalian cells. Both IC3 loop and CT domain are necessary for EP4 targeting to cilia. We show that single mutations in the LPG motif impede endogenous EP4 trafficking to cilia and reduce cAMP-PKA signaling. Notably, Rab23 GTPase and IFT43 promote EP4 trafficking to cilia. Furthermore, EP4 physically interacts with Rab23 or IFT43, but IFT43 fails to interact with Rab23. IFT43 has recently been shown to tether the subunits of IFT-A base module to stabilize the complex structure32. IFT-A complex has also been found to accumulate at the base of the cilia33. Based on the findings of this study and others, we propose a preliminary ciliary transport model mediated by Rab23/IFT43, in which Rab23 interacts with EP4 and promotes EP4 ciliary transportation with the assistance of the IFT43-tethered IFT-A complex (Supplementary Fig. 9C). These findings open a promising avenue for exploring the pathophysiological roles of ciliary EP4 and its potential functions in ciliopathies.

The precise targeting of GPCRs to the cilium relies on the recognition of specific ciliary localization sequences. Protein motifs, such as VxP or LxP in CT and AxxxQ in IC3, are crucial for directing multiple GPCRs to cilia, including HTR6, MCHR1, Rhodopsin, SSTR3, and others19. The LPG motif in the IC3 of melanocortin 4 receptor (MC4R) was identified to be important for its ciliary trafficking34. We have also found that the LPG motif in EP4’s CT region serves as a CLS for the EP4 ciliary translocation. Therefore, the LPG motif could be considered as a pivotal CLS for targeting a group of important GPCRs, including MC4R and EP4, to the cilium. In addition, the IC3 loop in EP4 is also required for the ciliary transportation process. This is consistent with their physical interaction, whereby EP4 interacts with Rab23 through its specific domain in the IC3 loop (IC3d1) and the LPG-contained CT region (CTd2). Deletion of these domains prevents the physical association and impedes EP4 ciliary trafficking. These results suggest that Rab23 delivers EP4 to the ciliary base via its IC3d1 and CTd2 domains (Supplementary Fig. 9C).

Our previous studies demonstrated that the EP4 receptor localizes not only to the plasma membrane but is also moderately enriched in the cilium15. Treatment with the EP4 agonist CAY10598 rapidly elevated cAMP levels in both the cilium and cytoplasm. This observation aligns with a recent study showing that activation of FFAR4 receptor by its specific agonist similarly increases cAMP in both compartments35. Likewise, stimulation of the ciliary Gαi-coupled receptor SSTR3 leads to a concurrent reduction in cAMP levels in the cilium and cytoplasm35. These findings may be attributed to the relatively modest enrichment of these GPCRs (e.g., EP4, FFAR4, and SSTR3) within the cilium compared to the cytoplasm. In contrast, several GPCRs involved in Hedgehog (HH) signaling, such as Smoothened (Smo) and GPR161, specifically modulate cAMP levels within the cilium upon agonist stimulation5,20,36,37, likely due to their active translocation from the plasma membrane to the ciliary compartment20,38.

Previous studies reported that Tulp3 depletion in MIN6 cells and α-TC9 cells caused reduced EP4 enrichment in cilia16. We found that Tulp3-/- null cells exhibited a reduction of EP4 in cilia but not an absence of EP4, suggesting that additional adapters are required for EP4 ciliary trafficking. Indeed, we discovered Rab23 and IFT43 as critical adapters for targeting EP4 to cilia. It has been shown that Rab23 directs D1R ciliary trafficking along with the IFT-B complex11. The IFT-B complex mediates the anterograde transport of ciliary proteins driven by the kinesin-2 motor39. Importantly, the IFT-A complex mediates both the retrograde trafficking of ciliary proteins and the import of various membrane proteins across the ciliary gate7,13,40,41. Our findings suggest that Rab23 acts together with IFT43 to deliver and import EP4 into the ciliary compartment (Supplementary Fig. 9C). We anticipate that the IFT-B complex, driven by the kinesin motor, is then needed to mediate the anterograde trafficking of EP4 within the cilium.

Mutations in IFT complex subunits are commonly linked to human ciliopathies42. It has been shown that IFT43 mutations are associated with cilia anomalies, including Cranioectodermal Dysplasia, Retinitis Pigmentosa, and Short-Rib Thoracic Dysplasia with Polydactyly43–45. RAB23 mutations are also implicated in Carpenter’s syndrome, a disorder characterized by craniofacial abnormalities, polydactyly, obesity, patent ductus arteriosus (PDA), and intellectual disability46,47. Given the critical role of Rab23 and IFT43 in EP4 trafficking to the cilium, further research is needed to determine whether the clinical features observed in ciliopathies and other human diseases are associated with disrupted trafficking of GPCRs (i.e., EP4) to cilia. Exploring ciliary GPCR signaling could greatly advance the understanding of etiologies of human ciliopathies and potential therapeutic development. The primary challenges are to identify GPCR-mediated signaling cascades in cilia and evaluate their effects on cellular function6. Previous research attempted to reveal GPCR ciliary signaling and function by mutating ciliary trafficking adapters16,48. However, ciliary trafficking adapters (e.g., TULP3) are capable of transporting multiple GPCRs to cilia, which may not be specific in studying distinct GPCR signaling in cilia. We have utilized the single-base editing technique to catalyze amino acid substitutions in the CLS of endogenous EP4 to block its ciliary transportation, providing a refined approach to analyze the pathophysiological roles of GPCRs in cilia.

Methods

Ethics statement

All animal experiments were performed in accordance with animal use guidelines approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at East China Normal University (Institutional Review Board No. ARXM2023085). We have complied with all relevant ethical regulations for animal use.

Zebrafish stock

All zebrafish used in this study, including the wild-type AB strain, rab23-/- mutants, Tg(ef1α: ep4c-EGFP), and Tg(βactin2: Arl13b-GFP), were raised under standard husbandry procedures49. Briefly, adult fish were maintained at 28.5 °C under a 14/10 h light/dark cycle in a recirculating water system and fed twice daily with newly hatched brine shrimp. Mating and spawning were conducted using sexually mature adults (aged 3–8 months), and embryos at 24 hpf were collected for subsequent experiments.

Plasmid construction

The plasmids used in this study are listed in Supplementary Table 2. PCR was performed using Phanta Flash Master Mix (P510; Vazyme, China). EP4 and EP3 were amplified from HEK293T cDNA. Rab23, IFT43, and NPHP3 (1-201aa) were amplified from ATDC5 or IMCD3 cDNA. LentiCRISPRv2 (#52961) and CGBE1 (#140252) were purchased from Addgene. pWPXLd-EP4-EGFP-Puro, pWPXLd-EP3-EGFP-Puro, pcDNA3.1-EP4-3xFlag, pcDNA3.1-3xHA-Rab23, and pcDNA3.1-3xHA-IFT43 were constructed using the Gibson Assembly cloning method (SB-EGR205S, Share-bio). Chimeric constructs, internal deletions, and missense mutations were generated by assembling multiple PCR fragments using Gibson Assembly. U6-sgRNA-EF1a-GFP was obtained from Dali Li’s lab50. sgRNA expression plasmids were constructed using the Golden Gate cloning method (E1602L, New England Biolabs). Briefly, oligonucleotides for sgRNA were denatured at 95°C for 1 min, followed by slow cooling to room temperature. Annealed oligonucleotides were ligated into Esp3I-linearized LentiCRISPRv2 or BbsI-linearized U6-sgRNA-EF1a-GFP for sgRNA expression. Sequences of all plasmids were confirmed by Sanger DNA sequencing (Tsingke Biotech, China). Primer sequences and PCR conditions are available on request.

Generation of rab23-/- mutant and Tg(ef1α: ep4c-EGFP) transgenic zebrafish

The rab23-/- mutants were generated by CRISPR/Cas9-mediated mutagenesis. Cas9 mRNA was synthesized using mMESSAGE mMACHINETM T7 Transcription Synthesis Kit (Ambion, AM1344). rab23 sgRNA was designed by CRISPRscan software and transcribed in vitro using T7 RNA polymerase. Cas9 mRNA (500 ng/μl) together with sgRNA (50 ng/μl) was injected into zebrafish embryos at the one-cell stage. To generate Tg(ef1α: ep4c-EGFP) transgenic zebrafish expressing Ep4c-EGFP under the ef1α promoter, Tol2 recombinant plasmid and transposase mRNA were co-injected into embryos at the one-cell stage.

Cell culture, ciliogenesis, and immunofluorescence staining

All cell lines were cultured at 37°C with 5% CO2 in a humidified atmosphere. IMCD3 (ATCC CRL-2123)15 and ATDC5 (RCB0565, Riken BioResource Center, Tsukuba, Japan)51 cells were maintained in DMEM/F12 medium supplemented with 10% FBS and 100 U/ml penicillin/streptomycin. HEK293T cells (SCSP-502, National Collection of Authenticated Cell Cultures, Shanghai, China) were cultured in DMEM (C11995500BT, Gibco) (supplemented with 10% FBS and 100 U/ml penicillin/streptomycin). All cell lines were regularly tested and confirmed to be mycoplasma-free to ensure experimental integrity. For induction of ciliogenesis, IMCD3 and ATDC5 cells were serum-starved using DMEM/F12 medium with 100 U/ml penicillin/streptomycin for 24−48 h. In contrast, HEK293T cells were serum-starved in DMEM (C11885500BT, Gibco) for 12 h to synchronize cell cycles and induce ciliary formation. For immunostaining, cells were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde at room temperature for 15 min, permeabilized in 0.1% Triton X-100 in PBS for 20 min, and blocked in 5% heat-inactivated goat serum for 60 min before staining with antibodies. Cells were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4°C, followed by incubation with secondary antibodies and DAPI for 1 h at room temperature.

Generation of stable cell lines and transfections

To generate stable cell lines, EP4-EGFP, EP4, and various EP4 mutant variants were cloned into the lentiviral vector pWPXLD. This vector was then used to produce lentivirus in HEK293T cells, which were transiently transfected with the packaging constructs pMD2.G and psPAX2. Following transfection, the cell culture medium (DMEM) was replaced. The virus-containing supernatant was collected 48 h post-transfection, filtered through 0.45 μm PES filters (Biosharp), and subsequently concentrated using a 20% polyethylene glycol (PEG) 8000 solution containing 0.3 M NaCl (Sangon Biotech). The concentrated lentivirus was then stored at -80°C until required for transducing ciliary cell lines. For transient transfections, HEK293 cells were transfected using PEI 40 K (G1802, Servicebio), and ATDC5 cells were transfected using jetPRIME Transfection Reagent (101000027, Polyplus, Inc.) by following the manufacturer’s instructions.

Generation of CRISPR/Cas9-mediated Tulp3 and Rab23 knockout cell clones

To generate Tulp3 and Rab23 knockout cell lines, ATDC5 and IMCD3 cells were infected with lentiviruses packaged with specific sgRNAs (sgTulp3: 5’-CTGCGAAGGTTAAAGCCACG-3’; sgRab23: 5’-GGAGTTTGATGCAATCACGA-3’). Lentiviral particles were produced in HEK293T cells by transfection with lentiCRISPRv2, psPAX2, and pMD2.G plasmids at a 4:3:1 ratio (8 μg in total). After 48−72 h of infection, cells underwent selection with 10 μg/mL puromycin. Knockout efficiency was assessed 6 days post-infection using Sanger DNA sequencing. Surviving cells were trypsinized, diluted, and seeded at a density of 500–1000 cells per 10 cm dish. After 1–2 weeks, individual clones were transferred to 48-well plates and expanded until they reached 70−90% confluence, followed by passage and verification through sequencing. Only cell clones with confirmed gene edits were used for subsequent experiments.

Generation and isolation of EP4P416A and EP4P416S mutant cell clones

HEK293T cells (passages < 20) were co-transfected with single-base editor CGBE1 and U6-sgEP4-EF1a-EGFP plasmids (sgEP4: CTTCCAGGTGTGCCTGGCAT) using PEI 40 K. The base editing efficiency was evaluated 3 days post-transfection by Sanger DNA sequencing. The edited cells were trypsinized, diluted, and 500−1000 cells were seeded onto four 10 cm dishes. After 1–2 weeks, individual clones were isolated, transferred to 48-well plates, and cultured to 40–60% confluence. Only clones with confirmed gene edits were used for subsequent studies.

High-throughput and high-content siRNA screening

To identify potential adapters associated with EP4 ciliary trafficking, we screened 170 genes implicated in cilia trafficking. The genes encoding these proteins are listed in Supplementary Table 1. We obtained all siRNAs targeting these genes from Dharmacon Human ON-TARGETplus siRNA Library (Thermo). Each gene was targeted by four different siRNA sequences, grouped in SMARTpools. Briefly, Lipofectamine RNAiMAX transfection reagent (Invitrogen,13778500) was automatically added to 384-well plates using a Multidrop Combi Reagent Disperser (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc.), each well containing four siRNAs targeting one specific gene. After a 20-min incubation, we seeded ATDC5 cells at a density of ~2000 cells per well. These cells stably express EP4-EGFP. The cells were cultured for 48 h, after which the medium was replaced with a serum-free formulation to further induce ciliogenesis for 24 additional hours. The cells were then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 30 min, and immunofluorescence staining was performed to assess the ciliary localization of EP4.

siRNA interference

siRNA transfection was performed using Lipofectamine RNAiMAX (Invitrogen,13778500) following the manufacturer’s instructions. The target sequences for Rab23 siRNAs and IFT43 siRNAs (GenePharma, Shanghai, China) were as follows: (Rab23 siRNAs: 5’-GAACAGACACACTCAAGTA-3’, 5’-GCAAAGGCATTTTCACAAA, 5’-GAAAGTCGAGCATGATTCA-3’, 5’-CCACAGACAGGGAATCTTT-5’; IFT43 siRNA: 5’-GAGAATTACTTAAACAGCA-3’, 5’-CGACGACGATATTCCTGTG-3’, 5’-GCATGAAGTGCGAGAGGAT-3’, 5’-GAGAAATTGACCTGA AACT-3’). A negative siRNA control is also provided by GenePharma (A06001). 30 pmol of RNA mix was transfected into cells at 60% confluence in 24-well plates. After 48 h of transfection, cells were serum-starved for 24-48 h for cilia induction before analysis.

Co-Immunoprecipitation

In HEK293T cells, co-transfection was carried out using EP4-Flag, EP4-LPG > AAA-Flag, HA-Rab23, and HA-IFT43, employing PEI 40 K (G1802, Servicebio) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. In brief, 37 h post-transfection, the cells were lysed in Beyotime lysis buffer (P0013), supplemented with 1 mM PMSF (ST507, Beyotime). The lysates were centrifuged at 1200 g for 5 min, and the supernatants were incubated with anti-Flag nanobody agarose beads (KTSM1308, Alpalifebio) or anti-HA agarose beads (26181, Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 2 h at room temperature. Following five washes with washing buffer, the beads were boiled in SDS-loading buffer for subsequent immunoblotting analysis.

Measurement of pCREB1

CREB1 phosphorylation via EP4 and its mutant EP4-LPG > AAA was assessed by measuring phosphorylation levels using immunoblotting, as previously described52. Briefly, ATDC5 cells were infected with lentivirus-packaged EP4-EGFP or EP4-LPG > AAA-EGFP vectors. After 72 h, cells underwent selection with 5 μg/mL puromycin for 48–72 h, followed by a 36-h starvation period. Subsequently, cells were stimulated with a gradient concentration of CAY10598 for 30 min. After treatment, cells were lysed in RIPA buffer (WB3100, New Cell & Molecular Biotech Co.) containing a cocktail of protease and phosphatase inhibitors (P002, New Cell & Molecular Biotech Co.). The lysates were centrifuged at 12,000 g for 10 min, and the supernatants were processed for immunoblotting after boiling in an SDS-loading buffer.

Generation of cAMP biosensor and ciliary cAMP measurement

G-Flamp1, a sensor for cAMP, was a gift from Jun Chu’s lab23. The ciliary targeting sequence, NPHP3 (1-201aa), was amplified from IMCD3 cDNA. To construct ciliary G-Flamp1, sequences for NPHP3 (1-201aa), mCherry, and G-Flamp1 were co-cloned into the pWPXLD vector using Gibson Assembly. For cytoplasmic localization, mCherry and G-Flamp1 were co-cloned into the pWPXLD vector. HEK293T cell lines stably expressing either ciliary or cytoplasmic G-Flamp1 were established as previously described. For ciliary cAMP imaging, HEK293T cells expressing NPHP3-G-Flamp1 were treated with DMSO, CAY10598 (13281, Cayman), or Forskolin (T2939, Topscience) to modulate cAMP levels. Live-cell fluorescence imaging for cAMP measurement was performed every 10 s for 400 s per experiment using an Andor Dragonfly 500 high-speed confocal microscope equipped with a Zyla sCMOS camera. This camera system does not rely on electron multiplication and provides a linear response for fluorescence intensity quantification53–55. A 488 nm laser was used to visualize G-Flamp1 fluorescence, while a 561 nm laser was used to capture mCherry fluorescence. cAMP levels in the cilia being traced were quantified with a rectangle at each time point, and the raw integrated density ratio of G-Flamp1 to mCherry was measured using ZEN software. The relative intensity change was calculated based on the fluorescence intensity ratio F/F0. A similar procedure was applied for measuring cytoplasmic levels. Results represent averages from >5 cells across three independent experiments.

Statistics and reproducibility

Data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism, and the results are presented as mean ± Standard Error of the Mean (SEM) or standard deviation (SD), as detailed in the figure legends. The quantification of fluorescence intensity was analyzed using Image J software (version 1.8.0). The sample sizes and number of replicates for each experiment were specified in the figure legends. Statistical significance was assessed using a two-tailed unpaired Student’s t-test or one-way ANOVA followed by Dunnett’s test, or Tukey’s multiple comparisons test, with a significance threshold set at p < 0.05. All experimental conditions were tested independently with at least three replicates.
