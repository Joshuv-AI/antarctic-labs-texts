Severe acute respiratory distress syndrome due to Burkholderia pseudomallei pneumonia complicated by septic shock
Authors: Paterne Michael N’kan Mobio, Vivien Hong Tuan Ha, Isidore Koffi Kouame, El Hussein Mahmoud, Houmadi Brahim, Gabriel Makeya, Eric Diomande Sogbety, Koffi Loes

Introduction

B. pseudomallei is a motile, intracellular, non-fermenting Gram-negative bacillus and the causative agent of melioidosis, an infection endemic in more than 45 countries, predominantly in Southeast Asia and northern Australia [1]. Although initially described in these regions, melioidosis has progressively expanded its geographic distribution, with increasing reports from South Asia, Africa, Central America, the Caribbean, and South America [2], [3], [4], [5].

This global spread has been linked to environmental disruption, climate change, extreme weather events, and increased population mobility, all of which heighten human exposure to contaminated soil and water [6], [7].

Clinically, melioidosis presents with a wide spectrum of manifestations, ranging from acute to delayed disease. Rare but severe forms such as neuromelioidosis and hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis have been reported [8], [9]. More commonly, patients present with cutaneous infections, parotid or prostatic abscesses, genitourinary involvement, pneumonia, or septic shock [10], [11], [12]. Pneumonia and septic shock are the most frequent and life-threatening presentations, with mortality rates ranging from 44.7% to 55% in several cohorts [13], [14].

In recent years, melioidosis has been increasingly identified in non-endemic regions. Approximately 15% of travel-associated infections are now acquired in the Americas, the Caribbean, or Africa [15]. In Europe, a growing number of imported cases has been documented, often complicated by diagnostic delays—misdiagnosis occurs in 21% of cases, and only 56% of patients receive complete and appropriate therapy, highlighting limited awareness among clinicians in non-tropical settings [16]. France has not been spared, as illustrated by a recent case diagnosed in Havre [17].

Against this backdrop of increasing international mobility and shifting environmental risk, we report a case of severe acute respiratory distress syndrome secondary to B. pseudomallei pneumonia complicated by septic shock, with the aim of raising awareness among clinicians in Western healthcare systems.

Case presentation

A 31-year-old man with no known medical history was brought to the intensive care unit (ICU) by the Mobile Emergency and Resuscitation Service (Service Mobile d’Urgence et de Réanimation, SMUR) for acute respiratory distress occurring 24 h after returning from a 2-week professional stay in Saudi Arabia. He worked in the artistic and cultural sector. During his stay abroad, he presented twice to a local emergency department with a flu-like syndrome. No laboratory investigations were performed, and he was discharged with paracetamol, which provided partial symptomatic relief and allowed him to travel back to France.

Twenty-four hours after his arrival, he developed sudden-onset dyspnea at rest, without chest pain or cough, in an afebrile context with marked asthenia. He contacted the emergency medical service (Service d’Aide Médicale Urgente, SAMU). Upon arrival, the emergency team found a confused patient (Glasgow Coma Scale score of 12), afebrile and without meningeal signs, with a blood pressure of 123/98 mmHg, a heart rate of 65 beats/min, and a peripheral oxygen saturation of 54% on room air, increasing only to 74% under 15 L/min of high-concentration oxygen. The patient exhibited signs of respiratory distress with bilateral crackles. Rapid clinical deterioration required orotracheal intubation and mechanical ventilation (FiO₂ 100%, PEEP 8 cmH₂O). Sedation with midazolam and sufentanil was initiated. Hypotension during transport prompted fluid resuscitation and initiation of norepinephrine infusion at 2 mg/h.

On ICU admission, the patient remained intubated and mechanically ventilated in controlled-assisted mode (FiO₂ 100%, PEEP 8 cm H2O). The bilateral crackles persisted. Oxygen saturation fluctuated between 76% and 79%. Arterial blood gas analysis showed severe hypoxemia (PaO₂ 49 mmHg), hypercapnia (PaCO₂ 65 mmHg), severe acidosis (pH 6.9), and low bicarbonate (7.7 mmol/L), with a PaO₂/FiO₂ ratio below 50, consistent with severe ARDS. Neurologically, the patient was sedated (Richmond Agitation–Sedation Scale [RASS] score of –4) with equal and reactive pupils. Hemodynamically, he presented with tachycardia (123 beats/min) and a mean arterial pressure of 80 mmHg under norepinephrine at 2 mg/h. The Simplified Acute Physiology Score (SAPS II) was 84, indicating extremely high severity. Electrocardiography showed no conduction or repolarization abnormalities. The remainder of the clinical examination was unremarkable.

Laboratory testing revealed neutropenia (0.7 G/L), a marked inflammatory response (CRP 501 mg/L), impaired renal function (urea 11 mmol/L; creatinine 145 µmol/L; estimated glomerular filtration rate 55 mL/min/m²), and hyponatremia. Liver and pancreatic enzymes were elevated (aspartate aminotransferase 194 U/L; alanine aminotransferase 71 U/L; gamma-glutamyl transferase 300 U/L; total bilirubin 55 µmol/L; conjugated bilirubin 54 µmol/L). Cardiac biomarkers showed normal troponin T (5 ng/L) but markedly elevated N-terminal pro-B-type natriuretic peptide (21 710 pg/mL). Human immunodeficiency virus serology was negative.

Microbiological investigations—including blood cultures, bronchial aspirate, and urine culture—identified an organism belonging to the B. pseudomallei group. The bronchial aspirate isolate was cultured on selective and non-selective media. B. pseudomallei (4 × 10⁷ colony-forming units [CFU]/mL) was identified using matrix-assisted laser desorption ionization–time of flight (MALDI-TOF) mass spectrometry (BioMérieux®, Marcy l′Etoile, France). The pathogenicity threshold for bronchial aspirates was ≥ 10⁵ CFU/mL. Blood cultures processed with the bioMérieux automated system became positive after 16 h and 50 min; the isolate was grown on nonselective media and identified as B. pseudomallei by MALDI-TOF. The result was immediately communicated by telephone. Urine culture also grew B. pseudomallei (10 ³ CFU/mL), identified by MALDI-TOF.

Molecular testing for respiratory pathogens (bacterial and viral), methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus, and extended-spectrum β-lactamase–producing bacteria was negative. Urinary antigen tests for Legionella pneumophila and Streptococcus pneumoniae were also negative. Antimicrobial susceptibility testing showed susceptibility to ceftazidime, imipenem, meropenem, and trimethoprim–sulfamethoxazole.

Chest CT showed multifocal bilateral pulmonary consolidations with areas of air bronchogram, along with ground-glass opacities and interlobular septal thickening producing a “crazy-paving” pattern, consistent with diffuse bilateral pneumonia (Fig. 1). Cardiac Doppler ultrasound showed a non-dilated left ventricle with preserved systolic function (ejection fraction 50%–60%). Lung ultrasound revealed diffuse B-lines. A diagnosis of severe ARDS secondary to B. pseudomallei pneumonia, complicated by septic shock, was established. Respiratory management included lung-protective mechanical ventilation, neuromuscular blockade, and prone positioning. Hemodynamic support consisted of fluid resuscitation and vasopressor therapy. Empirical dual antibiotic therapy (third-generation cephalosporin and a macrolide) and corticosteroids were initiated. Antibiotic therapy was adjusted immediately upon pathogen identification (16 h and 50 min), and meropenem combined with trimethoprim–sulfamethoxazole was started.

Despite optimal supportive care—including protective ventilation, prone positioning, and subsequent initiation of venovenous extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO)—the patient’s condition continued to deteriorate, and he died 72 h after admission from refractory septic shock.

Discussion

In our clinical case, one of the first questions that arises concerns the likely site of contamination. The patient’s symptom history revealed two visits to local emergency departments for a flu‑like syndrome during his two‑week stay in Saudi Arabia. Although only a few cases have been reported in the region, recent publications suggest that environmental transmission may be underestimated. The presence of confirmed indigenous cases, combined with climatic conditions favorable to the survival of B. pseudomallei and limited local surveillance, supports the likelihood that the infection was acquired during his stay rather than during transit [1]. In imported cases, recent travel to an endemic region with environmental exposure is the predominant risk factor [1], [6], [13].

The rapid deterioration of the patient’s clinical condition could suggest the presence of underlying risk factors. However, his medical history and the information collected during hospitalization revealed no pre‑existing comorbidities. Although fatal outcomes in patients without comorbidities have been described in the literature, such presentations are uncommon and were observed only once among the 10 recent cases reported between June 2023 and June 2024 [1]. This finding highlights persistent gaps in our understanding of the disease’s pathophysiology and suggests that host susceptibility may be more complex than currently appreciated [18].

Our case illustrates an exceptionally severe imported presentation of melioidosis. Although classically confined to Southeast Asia and northern Australia, the disease is increasingly recognized as a global threat, with cases now reported across South Asia, Africa, Central America, the Caribbean, and South America. Globalization, international travel, and environmental change have facilitated its emergence in Western countries, where it remains underdiagnosed owing to limited clinical awareness and insufficient microbiological preparedness. Diagnostic complexity in non‑endemic settings arises from several interconnected factors. First, low clinical suspicion frequently leads to delayed or incorrect diagnosis. In Europe, misdiagnosis has been reported in 21% of imported cases, and only 56% of patients receive complete and appropriate therapy, underscoring persistent gaps in the recognition and management of this condition [16].

Secondly, many laboratories lack standardized protocols for the identification of B. pseudomallei. Its classification as a potential biothreat agent has resulted in its exclusion from several MALDI‑TOF reference databases used in routine diagnostics, contributing to frequent misidentification or delayed recognition in non‑endemic settings [17].

Pneumonia and septic shock represent the most frequent and life‑threatening manifestations of melioidosis, with reported mortality rates ranging from 44.7% to 55% in several cohorts [13], [14]. Our patient developed septic shock with a high bacterial load, reflected by blood cultures turning positive within 16 h and 50 min, profound hypoxemia progressing to severe ARDS, and rapid multiorgan failure.

Given the initial presentation of severe ARDS complicated by septic shock, and considering the local microbial epidemiology of our unit, empirical dual antibiotic therapy with a third‑generation cephalosporin and a macrolide, along with corticosteroids, was initiated. Once B. pseudomallei was identified, and given the patient’s profound multiorgan failure, management was immediately aligned with Australian and Thai melioidosis treatment guidelines [19]. The patient received an intensive intravenous phase with meropenem for severe septicemic disease, combined from the outset with trimethoprim–sulfamethoxazole. B. pseudomallei exhibits intrinsic resistance to many commonly used empirical agents, including macrolides and most third‑generation cephalosporins. Recent genomic and antimicrobial resistance studies have demonstrated the organism’s extensive repertoire of efflux pumps and β‑lactamases, explaining the failure of standard empirical regimens and underscoring the need for early pathogen‑directed therapy in suspected melioidosis [20], [21]. In fulminant presentations such as septic shock and severe ARDS, expert recommendations advocate simultaneous administration of meropenem and trimethoprim–sulfamethoxazole to ensure both rapid extracellular bactericidal activity and enhanced intracellular penetration [19]. This combined approach is specifically recommended in life‑threatening disease because delayed or sequential therapy is associated with poorer outcomes [10], [11].

Despite early dual therapy and maximal organ support, the patient progressed to refractory septic shock and died on day three of admission. Several factors may explain this unfavorable evolution. First, diagnostic delay likely contributed: according to the history obtained, the patient presented twice to the emergency department during his stay abroad, yet no investigations were performed. Even in endemic regions, the diagnosis of melioidosis remains challenging because of its highly polymorphic clinical presentation and overlap with other tropical infections such as dengue, leptospirosis, tuberculosis, and community‑acquired bacterial pneumonia. Recent reviews emphasize that B. pseudomallei infections frequently manifest with nonspecific symptoms, leading to delayed clinical suspicion and missed early therapeutic windows [19]. In addition, rare but severe complications such as hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis have been described and may contribute to rapid deterioration [9].

Microbiological confirmation is also difficult: culture, the diagnostic gold standard, is slow, requires selective media, and is frequently misinterpreted or misidentified as Pseudomonas spp. or B. cepacia complex in laboratories with limited experience [21]. Although molecular tools such as polymerase chain reaction, loop-mediated isothermal amplification assays, and MALDI‑TOF have improved diagnostic accuracy, their availability remains limited in many endemic settings [20]. Furthermore, melioidosis is not a notifiable disease in several endemic countries, contributing to persistent underrecognition, insufficient surveillance, and substantial underdiagnosis [1]. Together, these factors explain why melioidosis continues to be frequently missed or diagnosed late, even in regions where the pathogen is well established.

Second, the fatal outcome in this case is consistent with the extreme severity of illness at presentation [1], [10], [22]. A SAPS II score of 84 corresponds to a predicted mortality exceeding 80%–90%, reflecting profound multiorgan dysfunction and severe physiological derangement. In parallel, the patient’s PaO₂/FiO₂ ratio of approximately 50 indicates ultra‑severe ARDS according to the Berlin definition, a category associated with very high mortality despite optimal ventilatory strategies. Recent studies confirm that patients presenting with refractory septic shock, severe ARDS, and multiorgan failure have the highest risk of death, even when managed with early appropriate antibiotics and advanced organ support [20]. These indicators underscore that the patient’s initial severity was beyond the threshold at which survival is typically achievable, explaining the rapid and fatal outcome despite guideline‑concordant management.

Conclusion

This case underscores the critical importance of maintaining early clinical suspicion for melioidosis when evaluating septic shock or pneumonia in patients returning from endemic regions. Delayed recognition remains a major contributor to poor outcomes. A detailed travel history is essential, particularly when exposure to soil or surface water may have occurred, even in countries where environmental transmission is likely underrecognized. Rapid initiation of targeted therapy with ceftazidime or a carbapenem, followed by prolonged eradication treatment with trimethoprim–sulfamethoxazole, is crucial to improving survival. Despite timely, guideline-based management, severe melioidosis complicated by septic shock, multiorgan failure, and severe ARDS continues to carry a high mortality risk, highlighting the need for heightened awareness and prompt diagnostic consideration in appropriate epidemiological contexts.
