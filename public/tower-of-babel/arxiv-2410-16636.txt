General Frameworks for Conditional Two-Sample Testing
Seongchan Lee, Suman Cha, Ilmun Kim

Abstract
We study the problem of conditional two-sample testing, which aims to determine whether two populations have the same distribution after accounting for confounding factors. This problem commonly arises in various applications, such as domain adaptation and algorithmic fairness, where comparing two groups is essential while controlling for confounding variables. We begin by establishing a hardness result for conditional two-sample testing, demonstrating that no valid test can have significant power against any single alternative without proper assumptions. We then introduce two general frameworks that implicitly or explicitly target specific classes of distributions for their validity and power. Our first framework allows us to convert any conditional independence test into a conditional two-sample test in a black-box manner, while preserving the asymptotic properties of the original conditional independence test. The second framework transforms the problem into comparing marginal distributions with estimated density ratios, which enables direct application of existing methods for marginal two-sample testing. We demonstrate this idea in a concrete manner with classification and kernel-based methods. Finally, simulation studies are conducted to illustrate the proposed frameworks in finite-sample scenarios.

Introduction

1.1
Problem Setup

This paper addresses the problem of conditional two-sample testing, which aims to test the equality of two conditional distributions. Statistical methods for this problem have important applications across diverse fields such as domain adaptation and algorithmic fairness. In domain adaptation, for instance, this methodology can serve as a formal framework to validate the covariate shift assumption. Under this assumption, the conditional distribution of
Y
given
X
remains unchanged, while the marginal distribution of
X
may differ. By confirming this assumption, practitioners can effectively re-weight the training data according to the marginal density ratio with respect to
X
, which potentially leads to improved predictive performance and better adaptation to new domains
(
Shimodaira, 2000
;
Sugiyama et al., 2007a
;
Sugiyama et al., 2007b
)
.
Moreover, in algorithmic fairness, conditional two-sample testing provides a principled tool for detecting and mitigating biases. Specifically, it helps identify whether a particular machine learning model unfairly favors or disadvantages specific groups based on demographic characteristics such as age, gender, or ethnicity
(
Hardt et al., 2016
;
Barocas et al., 2023
)
. Conditional two-sample testing also finds applications beyond machine learning. In genomics, for example, scientists seek to identify differences in genetic distributions conditional on various factors such as disease status and environmental exposures
(
Virolainen et al., 2022
;
Wu et al., 2023
)
. This methodology aids scientists in understanding the genetic basis of diseases and in developing strategies for personalized medicine by providing a rigorous framework for comparing conditional distributions.

With the practical motivation in mind, we now formally set up the problem. Given
n_{1},n_{2}\in\mathbb{N}
, suppose we observe two mutually independent samples

\displaystyle\{(X_{i}^{(1)},Y_{i}^{(1)})\}_{i=1}^{n_{1}}\overset{\mathrm{i.i.d.}}{\sim}P_{XY}^{(1)}\quad\text{and}\quad\{(X_{i}^{(2)},Y_{i}^{(2)})\}_{i=1}^{n_{2}}\overset{\mathrm{i.i.d.}}{\sim}P_{XY}^{(2)},

where
P_{XY}^{(1)}
and
P_{XY}^{(2)}
are joint distributions supported on some generic product space
\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
. Let
P_{Y\!\,|\,\!X}^{(1)}
and
P_{Y\!\,|\,\!X}^{(2)}
denote the conditional distributions of
Y^{(1)}\,|\,X^{(1)}
and
Y^{(2)}\,|\,X^{(2)}
, respectively. Similarly, let
P_{X}^{(1)}
and
P_{X}^{(2)}
denote the marginal distributions of
X^{(1)}
and
X^{(2)}
, respectively. Given these two samples, our goal is to test the equality of two conditional distributions

\displaystyle H_{0}:P_{X}^{(1)}\bigl\{P_{Y\!\,|\,\!X}^{(1)}(\cdot\,|\,X)=P_{Y\!\,|\,\!X}^{(2)}(\cdot\,|\,X)\bigr\}=1\;\,\text{vs}\;\,H_{1}:P_{X}^{(1)}\bigl\{P_{Y\!\,|\,\!X}^{(1)}(\cdot\,|\,X)\neq P_{Y\!\,|\,\!X}^{(2)}(\cdot\,|\,X)\bigr\}>0,

(1)

where
P^{(j)}_{Y\!\,|\,\!X}(\cdot\,|\,x)
denotes the conditional distribution of
Y^{(j)}
given
X^{(j)}=x
for
j\in\{1,2\}
. In other words, we are interested in determining whether two populations have the same distribution after controlling for potential confounding variables. Throughout this paper, we assume that
P_{X}^{(1)}
and
P_{X}^{(2)}
have the same support, satisfying
P_{X}^{(1)}\ll P_{X}^{(2)}
and
P_{X}^{(2)}\ll P_{X}^{(1)}
where the symbol
\ll
denotes absolute continuity. Since
P_{X}^{(1)}
and
P_{X}^{(2)}
have the same support, the above hypotheses (
1
) for conditional two-sample testing can be equivalently defined using
P_{X}^{(2)}
instead of
P_{X}^{(1)}
.

As pointed out by
Boeken and Mooij (2021)
, conditional two-sample testing is closely connected to conditional independence testing. To illustrate this connection, we introduce a binary variable
Z\in\{1,2\}
, and see that the conditional independence between
Y
and
Z
given
X
is equivalently expressed as

\displaystyle Y\mbox{${}\perp\mkern-11.0mu\perp{}$}Z\,|\,X~\Longleftrightarrow~(Y\,|\,X,Z=1)\overset{d}{=}(Y\,|\,X,Z=2),

(2)

where the symbol
\overset{d}{=}
denotes equality in distribution. This equivalence enables us to convert the problem of conditional two-sample testing to that of conditional independence testing based on the datasets

\{(X_{i},Y_{i}):Z_{i}=1\}
and
\{(X_{i},Y_{i}):Z_{i}=2\}
.
Consequently, we can leverage various existing methods for conditional independence testing to tackle conditional two-sample testing. However, the relationship between conditional two-sample testing and conditional independence testing has not been rigorously established in prior work.
A key challenge arises from the deterministic nature of the group indicator
Z
in the conditional two-sample problem.
Specifically, letting
n=n_{1}+n_{2}
, the quantities
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathds{1}(Z_{i}=1)
and
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathds{1}(Z_{i}=2)
are fixed in advance, corresponding to the sample sizes
n_{1}
and
n_{2}
.
This distinction prevents a direct application of existing conditional independence results and leaves a gap between the two problems. To navigate this gap while preserving readability, we primarily formulate our results using fixed distributions. However, our frameworks naturally accommodate
n
-dependent sequences, which we explicitly formalize only when theoretically necessary, as in
Section

3
.

1.2
An Overview of Our Results

In this work, we make several contributions to the field of conditional two-sample testing. First, we establish that comparing conditional distributions is intrinsically more difficult than comparing marginal distributions. For marginal two-sample testing, one can design permutation tests that control type I error, while being powerful against certain alternatives
(
Kim et al., 2022a
, e.g.,)
. However, we show that this is not the case for conditional two-sample testing.
Theorem

1
proves that any valid conditional two-sample test has power at most equal to its size against any single alternative if the conditioning random vector is of continuous type. This is reminiscent of the negative result for conditional independence testing proved by
Shah and Peters (2020)
. It is worth highlighting, however, that their negative result does not directly imply
Theorem

1
. The proof of
Shah and Peters (2020)
relies on the assumption that the data
\{(X_{i},Y_{i},Z_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
are i.i.d., which does not hold in our setup as
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathds{1}(Z_{i}=1)
and
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathds{1}(Z_{i}=2)
are deterministic. We handle this distinction through a concentration argument and show that conditional two-sample testing is as difficult as conditional independence testing. This negative result naturally motivates additional assumptions that make the problem feasible.

Our next contribution is to introduce two general frameworks for conditional two-sample testing. The first framework effectively addresses the challenge arising from the deterministic nature of the group indicator in conditional two-sample testing. In particular, we develop a generic method that converts any conditional independence test into a conditional two-sample test. This general method directly transfers the asymptotic properties of a conditional independence test computed using
\{(X_{i},Y_{i},Z_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}\overset{\mathrm{i.i.d.}}{\sim}P_{XYZ}
to the setting of conditional two-sample testing, as we show in
Theorem

2
. At the heart of this approach is the concentration property of a Binomial random variable around its mean, which facilitates the effective construction of i.i.d. samples drawn from
P_{XYZ}
(see Algorithm
1
). This development paves the way for leveraging any existing methods for conditional independence testing in the literature, thereby expanding the range of tools available to practitioners for conducting two-sample tests.

The second framework is based on density ratio estimation. To elaborate, suppose that
P_{X}^{(1)}
and
P_{X}^{(2)}
admit densities
f_{X}^{(1)}
and
f_{X}^{(2)}
, and that the conditional distributions
P_{Y\!\,|\,\!X}^{(1)}(\cdot\,|\,x)
and
P_{Y\!\,|\,\!X}^{(2)}(\cdot\,|\,x)
admit densities
f_{Y\!\,|\,\!X}^{(1)}(\cdot\,|\,x)
and
f_{Y\!\,|\,\!X}^{(2)}(\cdot\,|\,x)
, all with respect to a common base measure. Then for all
(x,y)\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
, we have the identity:

\displaystyle f_{Y\!\,|\,\!X}^{(1)}(y\,|\,x)=f_{Y\!\,|\,\!X}^{(2)}(y\,|\,x)~\Longleftrightarrow~f_{XY}^{(1)}(x,y)=\frac{f_{X}^{(1)}(x)}{f_{X}^{(2)}(x)}f_{XY}^{(2)}(x,y):=f_{XY}(x,y),

(3)

where
f_{XY}^{(1)}
is the joint density function of
(X^{(1)},Y^{(1)})
such that
f_{XY}^{(1)}(x,y)=f_{Y\!\,|\,\!X}^{(1)}(y\,|\,x)f_{X}^{(1)}(x)
, and
f_{XY}^{(2)}
is similarly defined for
(X^{(2)},Y^{(2)})
. The above equivalence (
3
) allows us to transform the problem of testing for conditional distributions into the one that compares marginal distributions with densities
f_{XY}^{(1)}
and
f_{XY}
. The latter problem has been extensively studied with various methods, ranging from classical approaches such as Hotelling’s test to modern methods such as kernel maximum mean discrepancy
(
Gretton et al., 2012
;
Liu et al., 2020
;
Schrab et al., 2023
)
and machine learning-based approaches
(
Lopez-Paz and Oquab, 2017
;
Kim et al., 2019
;
Kim et al., 2021
;
Hediger et al., 2022
, e.g.,)
. The issue, however, is that we do not observe samples from
f_{XY}
but from
f_{XY}^{(2)}
. Therefore, the success of this framework relies on how accurately one can estimate the density ratio
r_{X}(x):={f_{X}^{(1)}(x)}/{f_{X}^{(2)}(x)},
and incorporate it into the procedure to fill the gap between
f_{XY}
and
f_{XY}^{(2)}
. We demonstrate this methodology by focusing on a classification-based test in
Section

4.2
and a kernel-based test in
Section

4.3
. The theoretical results are proved in
Appendix

A
.

1.3
Literature Review

As mentioned earlier, conditional two-sample testing has critical applications in fields such as machine learning and genomics, where comparing groups while controlling for confounders is essential. Despite its significance, research on nonparametric comparisons of entire conditional distributions remains limited. While existing work has investigated testing for the equality of conditional moments and goodness-of-fit for pre-specified distributions, these methods target specific aspects rather than the distribution as a whole. Several recent works have begun to fill this gap by developing nonparametric methods that directly compare conditional distributions.

Hu and Lei (2024)
introduced a weighted rank-sum statistic relying on both marginal and conditional density ratios, a special case of our density-ratio-based framework that we derive in Appendix
C.2
.
Chen and Lei (2025)
later extended this idea using Neyman orthogonality to reduce first-order bias. Rather than estimating density ratios,
Yan et al. (2025)
proposed a kernel-based framework built on the conditional energy distance and conditional maximum mean discrepancy, where test statistics are constructed from conditional U-statistics with kernel smoothing and calibrated via a local bootstrap procedure. In a different direction,
Chatterjee et al. (2024)
considered a paired-covariate setting where both response variables are conditioned on the same covariates, which differs from our independent-covariate setting and makes their methods not directly comparable to ours.

Our first framework builds on the connection between conditional two-sample testing and conditional independence testing described in (
2
), and can be combined with a wide range of existing methods. Notable examples include the Generalized Covariance Measure
(
Shah and Peters, 2020
)
and its projected variant
(
Lundborg et al., 2024
)
, as well as kernel-based approximations
(
Strobl et al., 2019
)
, binning-based tests
(
Neykov et al., 2021
)
, and general regression frameworks
(
Williamson et al., 2023
)
.

On the other hand, our second framework relies on density ratio estimation, where directly estimating the ratio is known to be more stable than separate density estimation in high dimensions
(
Sugiyama et al., 2007b
)
. A closely related contribution is that of
Bordino and Berrett (2026)
, who proposed a permutation test for the hypothesis that the ratio of two densities is proportional to a known function. Their approach connects to our framework through the reformulation in (
3
). A more comprehensive review, including classical approaches to conditional moments and goodness-of-fit, the broader conditional independence testing literature, and density ratio estimation techniques, is provided in Appendix
C
.

2
Hardness Result

Before introducing our frameworks, we present a fundamental hardness result for conditional two-sample testing. Specifically, for a continuous random vector
X
, our result demonstrates that any valid conditional two-sample test has no power against any alternative. This finding parallels the negative result established by
Shah and Peters (2020)
for conditional independence testing, and our proof relies crucially on their work. Given the connection established in (
2
), one might argue that their negative result directly applies to the two-sample problem. However, formalizing this connection requires additional care since the sample sizes
n_{1}
and
n_{2}
are deterministic in our setting, which violates the i.i.d. assumption required in
Shah and Peters (2020)
.

While the problem is formulated on a generic product space in
Section

1
, we restrict our focus to Euclidean spaces to establish the hardness result. Let
\mathcal{P}
denote the set of all pairs of distributions
P:=(P_{XY}^{(1)},P_{XY}^{(2)})
defined on
\mathbb{R}^{d_{X}}\times\mathbb{R}^{d_{Y}}
. We write
\mathcal{P}_{0}\subset\mathcal{P}
for the subset of pairs
P
satisfying the null hypothesis
H_{0}
in (
1
), and
\mathcal{P}_{1}=\mathcal{P}\setminus\mathcal{P}_{0}
for the corresponding alternative class.
Let
\mathcal{P}^{\mathsf{ac}}\subseteq\mathcal{P}
denote the subset of pairs where each joint distribution
P_{XY}^{(j)}
(
j\in\{1,2\}
) is absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}^{d_{X}+d_{Y}}
. Furthermore, for
M\in(0,\infty]
, let
\mathcal{P}_{M}\subseteq\mathcal{P}
be the subset of pairs
P
such that the supports of both
P_{XY}^{(1)}
and
P_{XY}^{(2)}
are contained within an
\ell_{\infty}
ball of radius
M
centered at the origin. We then define the restricted null and alternative classes for the hardness result as
\mathcal{P}_{0,M}^{\mathsf{ac}}:=\mathcal{P}_{0}\cap\mathcal{P}^{\mathsf{ac}}\cap\mathcal{P}_{M}
and
\mathcal{P}_{1,M}^{\mathsf{ac}}:=\mathcal{P}_{1}\cap\mathcal{P}^{\mathsf{ac}}\cap\mathcal{P}_{M}
.

Theorem 1
.

Let
n_{1},n_{2}\in\mathbb{N}
,
\alpha\in(0,1)
, and
M\in(0,\infty]
. Suppose we observe two independent samples
\{(X_{i}^{(j)},Y_{i}^{(j)})\}_{i=1}^{n_{j}}\overset{\mathrm{i.i.d.}}{\sim}P_{XY}^{(j)}
for
j=1,2
, where the distribution pair
P=(P_{XY}^{(1)},P_{XY}^{(2)})\in\mathcal{P}^{\mathsf{ac}}_{M}
. Let
\phi
be any (possibly randomized) test based on these two samples, taking values in
\{0,1\}
. If
\phi
controls the type I error at level
\alpha
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}^{\mathsf{ac}}_{0,M}}\mathbb{E}_{P}(\phi)\leq\alpha,

then the power of
\phi
cannot exceed
\alpha
for any alternative
P\in\mathcal{P}^{\mathsf{ac}}_{1,M}
, that is,

\displaystyle\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}^{\mathsf{ac}}_{1,M}}\mathbb{E}_{P}(\phi)\leq\alpha.

Theorem

1
shows that it is necessary to impose additional assumptions (e.g., smoothness conditions on the distributions) to make the conditional two-sample problem feasible. In the next two sections, we introduce two general frameworks, which implicitly or explicitly incorporate reasonable assumptions to address this problem. The first framework converts any conditional independence test into a conditional two-sample test and is applicable whenever the chosen test is valid and consistent. In contrast, the second framework assumes that the marginal density ratio
r_{X}
is well-behaved and can be estimated with high accuracy.

3
Approach via Conditional Independence Testing

3.1
Algorithm and Asymptotic Guarantees

In this section, we introduce our first framework that converts a conditional independence test into a conditional two-sample test, while maintaining the same asymptotic guarantees. The key idea is to construct a dataset
\mathcal{D}_{\tilde{n}}
of size
\tilde{n}
, consisting of i.i.d. random vectors
(X,Y,Z)
, based on the given two samples
\{(X_{i}^{(1)},Y_{i}^{(1)})\}_{i=1}^{n_{1}}
and
\{(X_{i}^{(2)},Y_{i}^{(2)})\}_{i=1}^{n_{2}}
. To achieve this, letting
n=n_{1}+n_{2}
, we first draw a random variable
\tilde{n}_{1}
from
\mathrm{Binomial}
(
\tilde{n}

n_{1}/n
) where
\tilde{n}
is set to be smaller than
n
and
\tilde{n}/n\rightarrow 1
.
A similar idea was employed by
Neykov et al. (2021)
in a different context to eliminate Poissonization for conditional independence testing.
Since a Binomial random variable is highly concentrated around its mean, we can guarantee that
\tilde{n}_{1}\leq n_{1}
and
\tilde{n}_{2}:=\tilde{n}-\tilde{n}_{1}\leq n_{2}
with high probability. In the event that
\tilde{n}_{1}>n_{1}
or
\tilde{n}_{2}>n_{2}
, making the construction of
\mathcal{D}_{\tilde{n}}
infeasible, we simply accept the null hypothesis.
This slightly inflates the type II error in finite-sample scenarios, but it is asymptotically negligible.

Having constructed
\mathcal{D}_{\tilde{n}}
consisting of i.i.d. random samples drawn from the joint distribution of
(X,Y,Z)
, we can now implement a conditional independence test, as summarized in Algorithm

, while retaining the theoretical guarantees for conditional two-sample testing.

3.2
Coupling Stability

Despite its generality, one drawback of Algorithm
1
is that it discards the observations
\{(X_{i}^{(1)},Y_{i}^{(1)})\}_{i=\tilde{n}_{1}+1}^{n_{1}}
and
\{(X_{i}^{(2)},Y_{i}^{(2)})\}_{i=\tilde{n}_{2}+1}^{n_{2}}
whenever
\tilde{n}_{1}<n_{1}
and
\tilde{n}_{2}<n_{2}
. Provided that
n_{1}
and
n_{2}
grow proportionally with
n
, the number of discarded samples
n-\tilde{n}
is
O\bigl(\sqrt{n\log(1/\varepsilon)}\bigr)
. While this loss may degrade performance in small-sample regimes, it becomes negligible when
n
is large and
\varepsilon
decreases slowly, as supported empirically in Appendix
D.7
. Nevertheless, for a restricted class of test statistics that are
coupling-stable
, defined in
Definition

1
, the conclusion of
Theorem

2
may continue to hold without discarding samples.

To formalize this idea, we introduce a coupling construction adapted from
Chung and Romano (2013)
. Let
\bar{n}_{1}\sim\mathrm{Binomial}(n,n_{1}/n)
and
\bar{n}_{2}=n-\bar{n}_{1}
. If
\bar{n}_{1}>n_{1}
, we draw
\bar{n}_{1}-n_{1}
additional samples

\{(X_{i}^{(1)},Y_{i}^{(1)})\}_{i=n_{1}+1}^{\bar{n}_{1}}
from
P_{XY}^{(1)}
. Otherwise, we draw
\bar{n}_{2}-n_{2}
additional samples
\{(X_{i}^{(2)},Y_{i}^{(2)})\}_{i=n_{2}+1}^{\bar{n}_{2}}
from
P_{XY}^{(2)}
. In either case, we define the coupled dataset

\{(\tilde{X}_{i},\tilde{Y}_{i},\tilde{Z}_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}=\{(X_{i}^{(1)},Y_{i}^{(1)},1)\}_{i=1}^{\bar{n}_{1}}\cup\{(X_{i}^{(2)},Y_{i}^{(2)},2)\}_{i=1}^{\bar{n}_{2}}
, which, after randomly permuting indices, can be viewed as i.i.d. draws from
P_{XYZ}
.
When this newly constructed dataset is compared with the original dataset
\{(X_{i},Y_{i},Z_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}=\{(X_{i}^{(1)},Y_{i}^{(1)},1)\}_{i=1}^{n_{1}}\cup\{(X_{i}^{(2)},Y_{i}^{(2)},2)\}_{i=1}^{n_{2}}
, there are
|\bar{n}_{1}-n_{1}|
distinct data points satisfying
\mathbb{E}[|\bar{n}_{1}-n_{1}|]\leq\sqrt{(n/4)}
. This motivates the following definition.

Definition 1

(Coupling stability)
.

Let
T_{n}
be a test statistic computed on the original dataset
\{(X_{i},Y_{i},Z_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
, and let
\tilde{T}_{n}
denote its analogue computed on the coupled dataset
\{(\tilde{X}_{i},\tilde{Y}_{i},\tilde{Z}_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
constructed above. We say that
T_{n}
is
asymptotically coupling-stable
if
T_{n}-\tilde{T}_{n}
converges in probability to zero as
n\to\infty
.

This definition captures the idea that, when a test statistic
T_{n}
is asymptotically insensitive to
\sqrt{n}
-scale perturbations of the sample, its asymptotic behavior is preserved across both the original and the coupled datasets. Whether a given statistic is coupling-stable, however, depends on its specific form, as the following examples illustrate.

Example 1

(Stable case)
.

To simplify our presentation, consider the case
Y\in\mathbb{R}
, and assume
f(x):=\mathbb{E}(Y\,|\,X=x)
and
g(x):=\mathbb{E}(Z\,|\,X=x)
are known. Letting
R_{i}:=\{Y_{i}-f(X_{i})\}\{Z_{i}-g(X_{i})\}
, the generalized covariance measure introduced by
Shah and Peters (2020)
is

\displaystyle T_{n}=\frac{n^{-1/2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}R_{i}}{\sqrt{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}R_{i}^{2}-\bigl(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{r=1}^{n}R_{r}\bigr)^{2}}},

and let
\tilde{T}_{n}
be the analogue of
T_{n}
computed on
\{(\tilde{X}_{i},\tilde{Y}_{i},\tilde{Z}_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
. Focusing on the numerators of
T_{n}
and
\tilde{T}_{n}
, we see that their difference is

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(R_{i}-\tilde{R}_{i})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=n_{1}+1}^{\bar{n}_{1}}(R_{i}-\tilde{R}_{i})\cdot\mathds{1}(\bar{n}_{1}>n_{1})+\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=\bar{n}_{1}+1}^{n_{1}}(R_{i}-\tilde{R}_{i})\cdot\mathds{1}(\bar{n}_{1}\leq n_{1}).

Under the null hypothesis, the expectation of the difference is zero and the variance is bounded above by
O(n^{-1/2})
, provided
\mathbb{E}(Y_{i}^{2})<\infty
. Therefore, the difference of the numerators is asymptotically negligible. A similar argument shows that the difference of the denominators is also asymptotically negligible as detailed in Appendix
A.11
. Putting things together, we conclude that
T_{n}
is coupling-stable in the sense of
Definition

1
.

In practice,
f
and
g
are estimated from the data, which can introduce coupling-instability when these estimators are sensitive to sample perturbations. However, as the next example shows, coupling-instability is not solely an artifact of estimation error; it can also arise intrinsically from the structure of the test statistic itself.

Example 2

(Unstable case)
.

In the same univariate setting as
Example

1
, suppose
f(x)=\mathbb{E}(Y\mid X=x)
is difficult to estimate nonparametrically, whereas the labeling mechanism
g(x)=\mathbb{E}(Z\mid X=x)
is known. One might then consider the statistic
T_{n}=n^{-1/2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\zeta_{i}
, where
\zeta_{i}:=Y_{i}\{Z_{i}-g(X_{i})\}
, which does not require knowledge of
f
. Let
\tilde{T}_{n}
denote the same statistic computed on the coupled sample. Under the null hypothesis
Y\mbox{${}\perp\mkern-11.0mu\perp{}$}Z\mid X
, both
T_{n}
and
\tilde{T}_{n}
converge to centered normal limits with different variances, provided that
\mathbb{E}\{f(X)g(X)(1-g(X))\}\neq 0
(see Appendix
A.11
). Because their asymptotic distributions differ under this condition,
T_{n}
is not coupling-stable in the sense of
Definition

1
. Consequently, bypassing Algorithm
1
and applying such a test directly to the original data may fail to control the type I error, underscoring the necessity of coupling stability.

The previous examples highlight the need for caution when applying a conditional independence test directly to the two-sample data and justify our generic conversion approach in Algorithm
1
. The next section introduces an alternative framework for conditional two-sample testing based on density ratio estimation.

4
Approach via Density Ratio Estimation

4.1
General Principle and Examples

In this subsection, we present our second framework, which transforms conditional two-sample testing into a marginal comparison via density ratio estimation. Recall from (
3
) that the null hypothesis holds if and only if
f_{XY}^{(1)}=f_{XY}:=r_{X}\cdot f_{XY}^{(2)}
, where
r_{X}(x)={f_{X}^{(1)}(x)}/{f_{X}^{(2)}(x)}
. Since we only observe samples from
f_{XY}^{(2)}
rather than
f_{XY}
, we correct this sampling bias using importance weighting
(
kimura2024short
, for a survey)
. Specifically, estimating an expectation under a target density
p
using samples
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
from a source density
q
is achieved by reweighting each observation by
p(X_{i})/q(X_{i})
, since
\mathbb{E}_{Q}\bigl[\frac{p(X)}{q(X)}\varphi(X)\bigr]=\mathbb{E}_{P}[\varphi(X)]
.

Assuming
r_{X}
is known (or accurately estimated), this principle naturally extends to marginal two-sample testing. Let
V^{(j)}:=(X^{(j)},Y^{(j)})
for
j\in\{1,2\}
. For basic test statistics, such as mean comparison or rank sum statistics, one can simply reweight the transformed samples from the second distribution by
r_{X}(X^{(2)})
. Notably, the recent approach by
Hu and Lei (2024)
can be interpreted through this lens of importance-weighted rank sum statistics. Such importance-weighted statistics admit standard Gaussian calibration and are detailed in Appendix
C.2
. Below, we turn to two more involved examples.

Example 3

(Classifier-based approach)
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a class of binary classifiers
h:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}\to\{1,2\}
and let
\ell:\{1,2\}\times\{1,2\}\to\mathbb{R}
be a loss function that penalizes incorrect predictions. The core idea behind classifier-based two-sample tests
(
Lopez-Paz and Oquab, 2017
;
Kim et al., 2021
;
Hediger et al., 2022
)
is that when the null hypothesis of equality of distributions is true, any classifier will return a random guess. On the other hand, when two distributions are significantly different, the accuracy of a reasonable classifier exceeds chance level. Therefore, empirical classification accuracy serves as an effective test statistic. However, since we do not observe a sample from
f_{XY}
but a sample from
f_{XY}^{(2)}
, we must account for this discrepancy when training the classifier. Specifically, we compute a classifier

\displaystyle\widehat{h}=\argmin_{h\in\mathcal{H}}\biggl\{\frac{1}{n_{1}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{1}}\ell\bigl(h\bigl(V_{i}^{(1)}\bigr),1\bigr)+\frac{1}{n_{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{2}}r_{X}\bigl(X_{i}^{(2)}\bigr)\ell\bigl(h\bigl(V_{i}^{(2)}\bigr),2\bigr)\biggr\},

(5)

and use the empirical classification accuracy of
\widehat{h}
, similarly weighted by the density ratio, as our test statistic. We analyze this in detail in
Section

4.2
.

Example 4

(Kernel MMD)
.

The second example is a kernel MMD statistic
(
Gretton et al., 2012
)
. Given a kernel
k
, the population MMD compares the kernel mean embeddings of two distributions with density functions
f_{XY}^{(1)}
and
f_{XY}
, respectively. In kernel form, the squared MMD can be written as

\displaystyle\mathrm{MMD}^{2}=\mathbb{E}[k(V_{1}^{(1)},V_{2}^{(1)})]+\mathbb{E}[r_{X}\bigl(X_{1}^{(2)}\bigr)r_{X}\bigl(X_{2}^{(2)}\bigr)k(V_{1}^{(2)},V_{2}^{(2)})]-2\mathbb{E}[r_{X}\bigl(X_{2}^{(2)}\bigr)k(V_{1}^{(1)},V_{2}^{(2)})],

where the bias is corrected via importance weighting. The natural U-statistic estimator of
\mathrm{MMD}^{2}
is degenerate under the null hypothesis, which makes its null distribution analytically intractable in general. In
Section

4.3
, we develop calibration procedures for two distinct regimes, namely a Gaussian regime via block constructions
(
Zaremba et al., 2013
)
, and the fully quadratic regime via multiplier bootstrap.

In practice, the marginal density ratio
r_{X}
must be estimated. While various techniques exist
(
Sugiyama et al., 2010
;
Sugiyama et al., 2012a
)
, using the same dataset for estimation and testing causes plug-in bias. We thus recommend sample splitting, as in
Section

4.2
and
Section

4.3
. However, highly irregular or unbounded density ratios can destabilize the test statistic. While clipping or shrinkage
(
Shimodaira, 2000
)
mitigates this, such settings should be handled with caution, as testing performance may suffer. This issue is empirically demonstrated in
Section

5
. Although we focus on fixed subclasses of distributions for simplicity below, our framework naturally extends to sequences of null classes under uniform regularity conditions.

4.2
Classifier-based Approach

This subsection develops a classifier-based test for conditional two-sample testing. To simplify the presentation, we assume equal sample sizes
n_{1}=n_{2}=n/2
and split the dataset into two equal halves,
D_{\mathsf{infer}}:=\{V_{i}^{(1)}\}_{i=1}^{\lfloor n/4\rfloor}\cup\{V_{i}^{(2)}\}_{i=1}^{\lfloor n/4\rfloor}
and
D_{\mathsf{clf}}:=\{V_{i}^{(1)}\}_{i=\lfloor n/4\rfloor+1}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}\cup\{V_{i}^{(2)}\}_{i=\lfloor n/4\rfloor+1}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}
. The second half
D_{\mathsf{clf}}
is used to train the classifier
\widehat{h}
as in (
5
). For some positive integer
m<\lfloor n/4\rfloor
, we further divide the first half
D_{\mathsf{infer}}
into a test set
D_{\mathsf{test}}:=\{V_{i}^{(1)}\}_{i=1}^{m}\cup\{V_{i}^{(2)}\}_{i=1}^{m}
of size
2m
, and a set
D_{\mathsf{ratio}}:=D_{\mathsf{infer}}\setminus D_{\mathsf{test}}
of size
2(\lfloor n/4\rfloor-m)
. Let
\widehat{r}_{X}
denote an estimator of
r_{X}
formed on
D_{\mathsf{ratio}}
.

For
i\in\{1,\ldots,m\}
, define
\widehat{A}_{1,i}:=\mathds{1}\{\widehat{h}(V_{i}^{(1)})=1\}
and
\widehat{A}_{2,i}:=\widehat{r}_{X}(X_{i}^{(2)})\mathds{1}\{\widehat{h}(V_{i}^{(2)})=2\}
, and let
\overline{A}_{1}:=m^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\widehat{A}_{1,i}
and
\overline{A}_{2}:=m^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\widehat{A}_{2,i}
. The population-level classification accuracy of
\widehat{h}
is
\mathbb{P}\{\widehat{h}(V^{(1)})=1\}/2+\mathbb{E}[r_{X}(X^{(2)})\mathds{1}\{\widehat{h}(V^{(2)})=2\}]/2
, which equals
1/2
for any classifier
\widehat{h}
under the null hypothesis. This observation motivates the following test statistic

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathrm{Acc}}:=\frac{\sqrt{m}(\overline{A}_{1}+\overline{A}_{2}-1)}{\sqrt{{\widehat{\sigma}_{1}^{2}+\widehat{\sigma}_{2}^{2}}}},

(6)

where
\widehat{\sigma}_{1}^{2}:=(m-1)^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{m}(\widehat{A}_{1,i}-\overline{A}_{1})^{2}
and
\widehat{\sigma}_{2}^{2}:=(m-1)^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{m}(\widehat{A}_{2,i}-\overline{A}_{2})^{2}
. To establish the limiting distribution of
\widehat{\mathrm{Acc}}
, we consider the following assumptions.

Assumption 1
.

Let
m_{n}:=m
be an increasing sequence of positive integers with
\lim_{n\to\infty}m_{n}=\infty
.
Let
\mathcal{P}_{0}^{\mathsf{clf}}\subseteq\mathcal{P}_{0}
be a subclass of null distributions such that

(a)

For all sufficiently large
n
, there exist constants
0<\underline{c}_{j}<\overline{c}_{j}<1
for
j=1,2
, and constants
C,\delta>0
such that
\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}_{0}^{\mathsf{clf}}}\mathbb{E}_{P}[\{r_{X}(X^{(2)})\}^{2+\delta}]\leq C
, and with probability one,

\displaystyle\underline{c}_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\inf_{P\in\mathcal{P}_{0}^{\mathsf{clf}}}\mathbb{P}_{P}\{\widehat{h}(V^{(1)})=1\mid\widehat{h}\}\leq\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}_{0}^{\mathsf{clf}}}\mathbb{P}_{P}\{\widehat{h}(V^{(1)})=1\mid\widehat{h}\}\leq\overline{c}_{1},

\displaystyle\underline{c}_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\inf_{P\in\mathcal{P}_{0}^{\mathsf{clf}}}\mathbb{E}_{P}\big[r_{X}(X^{(2)})\mathds{1}\{\widehat{h}(V^{(2)})=2\}\mid\widehat{h}\big]\leq\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}_{0}^{\mathsf{clf}}}\mathbb{E}_{P}\big[r_{X}(X^{(2)})\mathds{1}\{\widehat{h}(V^{(2)})=2\}\mid\widehat{h}\big]\leq\overline{c}_{2}.

(b)

For any
\epsilon>0
, the density ratio estimator satisfies

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}_{0}^{\mathsf{clf}}}\mathbb{P}_{P}\bigl((n-m)\mathbb{E}_{P}[\{\widehat{r}_{X}(X^{(2)})-r_{X}(X^{(2)})\}^{2}\,|\,\widehat{r}_{X}]\geq\epsilon\bigr)=0.

Assumption
1
(a) is imposed to establish the conditional central limit theorem for the test statistic with the true density ratio. It excludes classifiers that, under the null hypothesis, return the same prediction value regardless of inputs. Assumption
1
(b) ensures that the approximation error from
\widehat{r}_{X}
is asymptotically negligible, which closely parallels the assumption in
Hu and Lei (2024)
. Although this requires a faster convergence rate than standard parametric cases (see Appendix
C.3
), we mitigate this by strategically allocating a larger sample to train
\widehat{r}_{X}
. This approach is theoretically supported by choosing a small
m
. Furthermore, in practice, we observe in
Section

5
that the empirical results closely align with the expected normal approximation under an 8:2 split.

Under Assumption
1
, the test statistic (
6
) converges to
N(0,1)
uniformly over
\mathcal{P}_{0}^{\mathsf{clf}}
.

Theorem 3
.

For the class of null distributions
\mathcal{P}_{0}^{\mathsf{clf}}
in Assumption
1
,
\widehat{\mathrm{Acc}}
in (
6
) satisfies

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}_{0}^{\mathsf{clf}}}\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\big|\mathbb{P}_{P}(\widehat{\mathrm{Acc}}\leq t)-\Phi(t)\big|=0.

By
Theorem

3
, rejecting the null hypothesis when
\widehat{\mathsf{Acc}}>\Phi^{-1}(1-\alpha)
has asymptotic validity over
\mathcal{P}^{\mathsf{clf}}_{0}
satisfying Assumption
1
. We next improve the efficiency via
K
-fold cross-fitting on
D_{\mathsf{infer}}
, keeping the classifier
\widehat{h}
fixed. We begin by considering
K
disjoint subsets
D_{\textsf{infer},1},\ldots,D_{\textsf{infer},K}
of
D_{\textsf{infer}}
, each of which contains
m_{\mathsf{cf}}:=\lfloor n/(4K)\rfloor
observations from each of the two samples. For
j\in\{1,\ldots,K\}
, let
\overline{A}_{1,j}+\overline{A}_{2,j}-1
and
\widehat{\sigma}_{1,j}^{2}+\widehat{\sigma}_{2,j}^{2}
denote quantities analogous to
\overline{A}_{1}+\overline{A}_{2}-1
and
\widehat{\sigma}_{1}^{2}+\widehat{\sigma}_{2}^{2}
, respectively, by letting
D_{\textsf{test}}=D_{\textsf{infer},j}
and
D_{\textsf{ratio}}=D_{\textsf{infer}}\setminus\!D_{\textsf{infer},j}
. We define the cross-fitted classification accuracy statistic as

\displaystyle{}^{\dagger}\widehat{\mathrm{Acc}}:=\frac{1}{K^{1/2}}\sum_{j=1}^{K}\frac{m_{\mathsf{cf}}^{1/2}(\overline{A}_{1,j}+\overline{A}_{2,j}-1)}{\sqrt{{\widehat{\sigma}_{1,j}^{2}+\widehat{\sigma}_{2,j}^{2}}}}.

(7)

Although the overlapping
D_{\mathsf{ratio}}
sets induce dependence across folds, this plug-in effect is asymptotically negligible under Assumption
1
(b).
The following corollary establishes that the cross-fitted accuracy statistic is asymptotically normally distributed under the null hypothesis.

Corollary 1
.

Consider the same setting as in
Theorem

3
. For any fixed
K\geq 2
, it holds that

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}_{0}^{\mathsf{clf}}}\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\big|\mathbb{P}_{P}\big({}^{\dagger}\widehat{\mathrm{Acc}}\leq t\big)-\Phi(t)\big|=0.

By
Corollary

1
, the test that rejects the null when
{}^{\dagger}\widehat{\mathrm{Acc}}>\Phi^{-1}(1-\alpha)
controls the asymptotic type I error.

Many practical classifiers attempt to mimic the Bayes optimal classifier. For the balanced-sample setting, the Bayes optimal classifier is
h^{\star}(x,y):=1+\mathds{1}\bigl(f_{XY}^{(1)}(x,y)/\{f_{XY}^{(1)}(x,y)+f_{XY}(x,y)\}\leq 1/2\bigr)
. The classification accuracy of
h^{\star}
can be explicitly computed in terms of the total variation (TV) distance. Specifically, twice the classification accuracy equals

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\{h^{\star}(V^{(1)})=1\}+\mathbb{E}[r_{X}(X^{(2)})\mathds{1}\{h^{\star}(V^{(2)})=2\}]=1+\mathrm{TV}(f_{XY}^{(1)},f_{XY}),

where
\mathrm{TV}(f_{XY}^{(1)},f_{XY})
denotes the TV distance between two distributions with densities
f_{XY}^{(1)}
and
f_{XY}
, respectively. Since the TV distance equals zero if and only if two distributions are identical, our classifier-based test can be powerful against general alternatives when the employed classifier approximates the Bayes classifier.

We now turn to a unified kernel-based MMD framework in the next subsection, developing parallel results that bridge the block-wise and quadratic-time statistics.

4.3
Kernel MMD

In this subsection, we introduce a unified block-wise MMD framework governed by a parameter
\gamma\in[0,1]
, encompassing both the block-wise estimator
(
Zaremba et al., 2013
)
and the quadratic-time MMD
(
Gretton et al., 2012
, Theorem 12)
. As in
Section

4.2
, we assume equal sample sizes
n_{1}=n_{2}=n/2
for simplicity. Since no auxiliary classifier needs to be trained in this setting, we allocate the entire sample to inference, so that
\tilde{D}_{\mathsf{infer}}=\{V_{i}^{(1)}\}_{i=1}^{n/2}\cup\{V_{i}^{(2)}\}_{i=1}^{n/2}
coincides with the full dataset. We partition
\tilde{D}_{\mathsf{infer}}
via sample splitting to decouple the estimation of the density ratio from the evaluation of the test statistic and thereby prevent plug-in bias. For some positive integer
m<n
, we define an evaluation set
\tilde{D}_{\mathsf{test}}:=\{V_{i}^{(1)}\}_{i=1}^{m}\cup\{V_{i}^{(2)}\}_{i=1}^{m}
of size
2m
to compute the MMD statistic, and an estimation set
\tilde{D}_{\mathsf{ratio}}:=\tilde{D}_{\mathsf{infer}}\setminus\tilde{D}_{\mathsf{test}}
of size
n-2m
to construct the density ratio estimator
\widehat{r}_{X}
.

Let
\mathcal{P}_{0}^{\mathsf{mmd}}\subseteq\mathcal{P}_{0}
be a subclass of null distributions satisfying
H_{0}
in (
1
). For each
i\in\{1,\ldots,m\}
, write
W_{i}:=(V_{i}^{(1)},V_{i}^{(2)})
. We define the pairwise kernel contribution as

\displaystyle\widehat{H}(W_{i},W_{j})

\displaystyle:=k(V_{i}^{(1)},V_{j}^{(1)})+\widehat{r}_{X}(X_{i}^{(2)})\widehat{r}_{X}(X_{j}^{(2)})k(V_{i}^{(2)},V_{j}^{(2)})

\displaystyle\qquad-\widehat{r}_{X}(X_{i}^{(2)})k(V_{i}^{(2)},V_{j}^{(1)})-\widehat{r}_{X}(X_{j}^{(2)})k(V_{i}^{(1)},V_{j}^{(2)}),

and abbreviate
\widehat{H}_{ij}:=\widehat{H}(W_{i},W_{j})
. Setting the block size
B=\max\{2,\lfloor m^{\gamma}\rfloor\}
, the number of blocks
S=\lfloor m/B\rfloor
, and the index sets
\mathcal{I}_{b}:=\{(b-1)B+1,\dots,bB\}
for
b=1,\dots,S
, we then consider the generalized block-wise
U
-statistic

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathrm{GB}}_{\gamma}=\frac{1}{S}\sum_{b=1}^{S}\bigg(\frac{1}{B-1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i,j\in\mathcal{I}_{b}\\
i\neq j\end{subarray}}\widehat{H}_{ij}\bigg).

(8)

The parameter
\gamma
controls the number of blocks
S
, which drives the asymptotic behavior. For
0\leq\gamma<1
, the growing number of blocks (
S\to\infty
) resolves the kernel degeneracy under
H_{0}
, yielding asymptotic normality. When
\gamma=1
, the data form a single block (
S=1
), recovering the standard
U
-statistic with a chi-square-type limit. To unify these two regimes, we define the empirical statistic
\widehat{\mathrm{MMD}}^{2}_{\gamma}
and its population counterpart
\mathrm{MMD}^{2}_{\gamma}
as

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathrm{MMD}}_{\gamma}^{2}:=\begin{cases}\sqrt{S}\,\widehat{\mathrm{GB}}_{\gamma}/\widehat{\sigma}_{\gamma},&0\leq\gamma<1,\\[3.99994pt]
\sqrt{\frac{m-1}{m}}\,\widehat{\mathrm{GB}}_{1},&\gamma=1,\end{cases}\qquad\mathrm{MMD}_{\gamma}^{2}:=\begin{cases}\sqrt{S}\,\mathrm{GB}_{\gamma}/\sigma_{\gamma},&0\leq\gamma<1,\\[3.99994pt]
\sqrt{\frac{m-1}{m}}\,\mathrm{GB}_{1},&\gamma=1.\end{cases}

Here, the oracle quantities
H_{ij}
and
\mathrm{GB}_{\gamma}
are obtained by replacing
\widehat{r}_{X}
with the true
r_{X}
in
\widehat{H}_{ij}
and
\widehat{\mathrm{GB}}_{\gamma}
, respectively. The variance
\sigma_{\gamma}^{2}
denotes the population variance of the oracle block statistics
\frac{1}{B-1}\sum_{i\neq j\in\mathcal{I}_{b}}H_{ij}
under
P\in\mathcal{P}_{0}^{\mathsf{mmd}}
, and
\widehat{\sigma}_{\gamma}^{2}
is its sample counterpart. Explicit forms are provided in Appendix
A.5
. To establish the asymptotic behavior of
\widehat{\mathrm{MMD}}^{2}_{\gamma}
for all
\gamma\in[0,1]
, we impose the following assumptions.

Assumption 2
.

Let
m_{n}:=m
be an increasing sequence of positive integers with
\lim_{n\to\infty}m_{n}=\infty
. Consider a class of null distributions
\mathcal{P}^{\mathsf{mmd}}_{0}
such that

(a)

There exist constants
c_{1},c_{2}>0
and some
\delta\in(0,1]
such that

\displaystyle\inf_{P\in\mathcal{P}_{0}^{\mathsf{mmd}}}\mathbb{E}_{P}\big(H^{2}_{12}\big)\geq c_{1}\quad\text{and}\quad\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}_{0}^{\mathsf{mmd}}}\mathbb{E}_{P}\big(|H_{12}|^{2+\delta}\big)\leq c_{2}.

(b)

The population density ratio satisfies
\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}_{0}^{\mathsf{mmd}}}\mathbb{E}_{P}[r_{X}(X^{(2)})^{4}]<c_{3}
for some constant
c_{3}>0
, and the corresponding estimator approximates
r_{X}
at the rate

\displaystyle\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}_{0}^{\mathsf{mmd}}}\mathbb{E}_{P}\big[\{\widehat{r}_{X}(X^{(2)})-r_{X}(X^{(2)})\}^{2}\big]=o\big((n-m)^{-1/2}\big).

(c)

The kernel is uniformly bounded, i.e.,
\|k\|_{\infty}\leq c_{4}
for some constant
c_{4}>0
.

(d)

The subsample sizes grow such that
m^{1+\gamma}/(n-m)\to c_{5}\in[0,\infty)
for
0\leq\gamma\leq 1
.

Assumption
2
(a) and (c) impose standard moment and boundedness conditions on the kernel to guarantee uniform distributional convergence. The boundedness condition (c) is readily satisfied by practical choices such as the Gaussian kernel. Assumption
2
(b) requires the plug-in error from estimating
r_{X}
to be asymptotically negligible. Compared to Assumption
1
(b), this requirement is relatively mild and attainable under broader nonparametric smoothness conditions. While a second-moment condition on the true density ratio
r_{X}
identical to that of
Hu and Lei (2024)
suffices for distributional convergence, we additionally impose a uniform fourth-moment bound on
r_{X}
to establish bootstrap consistency. Nevertheless, our overall requirement for density-ratio estimation remains strictly weaker than theirs (we provide a detailed discussion of these convergence rates in Appendix
C.3
). Finally, Assumption
2
(d) regulates the relative growth of
m
and
n-m
. Because a larger block parameter
\gamma
aggregates more kernel interactions and thereby magnifies the plug-in error,
n-m
must grow sufficiently fast to preserve the limiting distribution. This naturally motivates allowing distinct splitting proportions for test-statistic construction and density-ratio estimation
(see
Bordino and Berrett, 2026
, Example 1)
.

We now characterize the asymptotic null distribution of
\widehat{\mathrm{MMD}}^{2}_{\gamma}
for
\gamma\in[0,1]
.

Theorem 4
.

For the class of null distributions
\mathcal{P}_{0}^{\mathsf{mmd}}
satisfying Assumption
2
, the fixed
\gamma\in[0,1]
determines the asymptotic behavior of the test statistic:

1.

Regime I (
0\leq\gamma<1
):
The studentized statistic converges to
N(0,1)
as

\displaystyle{\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}_{0}^{\mathsf{mmd}}}\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\Big|\mathbb{P}_{P}\big(\widehat{\mathrm{MMD}}^{2}_{\gamma}\leq t\big)-\Phi(t)\Big|=0.}

2.

Regime II (
\gamma=1
):
The MMD statistic with
\gamma=1
satisfies

\displaystyle{\lim_{n\to\infty}\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}_{0}^{\mathsf{mmd}}}\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\Big|\mathbb{P}_{P}\big(\widehat{\mathrm{MMD}}^{2}_{1}\leq t\big)-\mathbb{P}_{P}(G\leq t)\Big|=0,}

where
G=\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{\ell}(\chi^{2}_{1,\ell}-1)
,
\{\chi^{2}_{1,\ell}\}_{\ell\geq 1}
are independent chi-square random variables with one degree of freedom, and
\{\lambda_{\ell}\}_{\ell\geq 1}
are the eigenvalues of the integral operator
g\mapsto\int H(w,\cdot)g(w)dP(w)
.

Theorem

4
shows that the asymptotic regime is determined entirely by
\gamma
: Regime I (
0\leq\gamma<1
) is asymptotically standard Normal after studentization, whereas Regime II (
\gamma=1
) yields a non-Gaussian quadratic-form limit. Meanwhile, Assumption
2
(d) requires the estimation sample
\tilde{D}_{\mathsf{ratio}}
to be sufficiently large relative to the testing sample
\tilde{D}_{\mathsf{test}}
. Under a single split, this creates an efficiency trade-off: improving density-ratio estimation leaves fewer observations for computing the MMD statistic. We therefore consider
K
-fold cross-fitting, which preserves the decoupling between estimation and evaluation while using the data more efficiently.

Fix an integer
K\geq 1
such that
K\leq\lfloor n/(2m)\rfloor
. In particular,
K=1
recovers the single-split construction above. For each fold
j\in\{1,\dots,K\}
, let
\tilde{D}_{\mathsf{test},j}:=\{V_{i}^{(1)}\}_{i=(j-1)m+1}^{jm}\cup\{V_{i}^{(2)}\}_{i=(j-1)m+1}^{jm}
and
\tilde{D}_{\mathsf{ratio},-j}:=\tilde{D}_{\mathsf{infer}}\setminus\tilde{D}_{\mathsf{test},j}
. Let
\widehat{\mathrm{GB}}_{\gamma,j}
denote the statistic in (
8
) computed on
\tilde{D}_{\mathsf{test},j}
using the density-ratio estimator
\widehat{r}_{X,-j}
constructed from
\tilde{D}_{\mathsf{ratio},-j}
. We define

\displaystyle{}^{\dagger}\widehat{\mathrm{MMD}}_{\gamma}^{2}:=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{\sqrt{K}}\sum_{j=1}^{K}\sqrt{S}\,\widehat{\mathrm{GB}}_{\gamma,j}/\widehat{\sigma}_{\gamma,j},&0\leq\gamma<1,\\[3.99994pt]
\tfrac{1}{K}\sum_{j=1}^{K}\sqrt{\frac{m-1}{m}}\,\widehat{\mathrm{GB}}_{1,j},&\gamma=1,\end{cases}

where
\widehat{\sigma}_{\gamma,j}^{2}
is the fold-
j
analogue of
\widehat{\sigma}_{\gamma}^{2}
, computed on
\tilde{D}_{\mathsf{test},j}
using
\widehat{r}_{X,-j}
.
Replacing
\widehat{r}_{X,-j}
with the true
r_{X}
yields the oracle counterpart
{}^{\dagger}\mathrm{MMD}_{\gamma}^{2}
, defined by the same expression with
H_{ij}
and
\sigma_{\gamma,j}
in place of
\widehat{H}_{ij}
and
\widehat{\sigma}_{\gamma,j}
. In Regime II, we abbreviate the fold-level oracle statistic as

\mathrm{MMD}_{1,j}^{2}:=\sqrt{\frac{m-1}{m}}\mathrm{GB}_{1,j}
,so that

{}^{\dagger}\mathrm{MMD}_{1}^{2}=K^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^{K}\mathrm{MMD}_{1,j}^{2}
. When
K=1
, both statistics reduce to their single-split counterparts. As with the classifier-based approach, while overlapping estimation sets
\tilde{D}_{\mathsf{ratio},-j}
induce dependence across folds for the plug-in statistics, this effect is asymptotically negligible under
Assumption

2
(b).

The following corollary extends
Theorem

4
to the cross-fitted statistic.

Corollary 2
.

Suppose Assumption
2
holds, and fix
K\geq 1
.

1.

Regime I (
0\leq\gamma<1
):

\displaystyle{\lim_{n\to\infty}\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}_{0}^{\mathsf{mmd}}}\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\Big|\mathbb{P}_{P}\big({}^{\dagger}\widehat{\mathrm{MMD}}_{\gamma}^{2}\leq t\big)-\Phi(t)\Big|=0.}

2.

Regime II (
\gamma=1
):
Let
G_{1},\dots,G_{K}
be independent copies of
G=\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{\ell}(\chi_{1,\ell}^{2}-1)
. Then

\displaystyle{\lim_{n\to\infty}\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}_{0}^{\mathsf{mmd}}}\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\Big|\mathbb{P}_{P}\big({}^{\dagger}\widehat{\mathrm{MMD}}_{1}^{2}\leq t\big)-\mathbb{P}_{P}\Big(\frac{1}{K}\sum_{j=1}^{K}G_{j}\leq t\Big)\Big|=0.}

For Regime I (
0\leq\gamma<1
),
Corollary

2
shows that rejecting
H_{0}
when
{}^{\dagger}\widehat{\mathrm{MMD}}_{\gamma}^{2}>\Phi^{-1}(1-\alpha)
controls the type I error at level
\alpha
uniformly over
\mathcal{P}_{0}^{\mathsf{mmd}}
.
For Regime II (
\gamma=1
), the oracle statistics
\mathrm{MMD}_{1,j}^{2}
are computed on disjoint evaluation sets and hence independent, but the limiting distribution
K^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^{K}G_{j}
has no closed-form quantile.
We therefore calibrate via multiplier bootstrap.

For each fold
j\in\{1,\ldots,K\}
, let
\{\xi_{i,j}:1\leq i\leq m\}
be independent standard Gaussian multipliers, and let
\widehat{H}_{ii^{\prime}}^{(j)}
denote the plug-in kernel computed on
\tilde{D}_{\mathsf{test},j}
using
\widehat{r}_{X,-j}
. Define

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathrm{MMD}}_{1,\mathrm{Boot},j}^{2}:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{m(m-1)}}\sum_{1\leq i\neq i^{\prime}\leq m}\xi_{i,j}\xi_{i^{\prime},j}\widehat{H}_{ii^{\prime}}^{(j)},\quad{}^{\dagger}\widehat{\mathrm{MMD}}_{1,\mathrm{Boot}}^{2}:=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{j=1}^{K}\widehat{\mathrm{MMD}}_{1,\mathrm{Boot},j}^{2}.

For
R
multiplier draws
\Xi_{R}=\big\{\{\xi_{i,j}^{(r)}:1\leq i\leq m,\ 1\leq j\leq K\}\big\}_{r=1}^{R}
, let
\widehat{q}_{1-\alpha}^{R}
denote the empirical
(1-\alpha)
-quantile of
\{{}^{\dagger}\widehat{\mathrm{MMD}}_{1,\mathrm{Boot}}^{2,(r)}\}_{r=1}^{R}
. Define the test
{}^{\dagger}\phi_{\alpha}^{R}(\tilde{D}_{\mathsf{infer}},\Xi_{R}):=\mathds{1}\{{}^{\dagger}\widehat{\mathrm{MMD}}_{1}^{2}>\widehat{q}_{1-\alpha}^{R}\}
.

The next theorem establishes that this bootstrap calibration consistently approximates the null distribution in Regime II and therefore yields an asymptotically valid test.

Theorem 5
.

Suppose Assumption
2
holds with
\gamma=1
, and fix
K\geq 1
.

(i)

Writing
\mathbb{P}_{P}^{*}(\cdot):=\mathbb{P}_{P}(\cdot\mid\tilde{D}_{\mathsf{infer}})
, for any
\varepsilon>0
,

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}_{0}^{\mathsf{mmd}}}\mathbb{P}_{P}\bigg\{\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\Big|\mathbb{P}_{P}^{*}\big({}^{\dagger}\widehat{\mathrm{MMD}}_{1,\mathrm{Boot}}^{2}\leq t\big)-\mathbb{P}_{P}\big({}^{\dagger}\mathrm{MMD}_{1}^{2}\leq t\big)\Big|>\varepsilon\bigg\}=0.

(ii)

Let
{}^{\dagger}F_{0,\scriptscriptstyle P}
denote the limiting null distribution of
{}^{\dagger}\mathrm{MMD}_{1}^{2}
under
P
, and define
{}^{\dagger}q_{1-\alpha,\scriptscriptstyle P}:=\inf\{t\in\mathbb{R}:{}^{\dagger}F_{0,\scriptscriptstyle P}(t)\geq 1-\alpha\}
. Suppose that for every
\tau>0
,

\displaystyle\inf_{P\in\mathcal{P}_{0}^{\mathsf{mmd}}}\inf_{|t-{}^{\dagger}q_{1-\alpha,\scriptscriptstyle P}|\geq\tau}\big|{}^{\dagger}F_{0,\scriptscriptstyle P}(t)-(1-\alpha)\big|>0.

Then, for any fixed
\alpha\in(0,1)
,

\displaystyle\lim_{n,R\to\infty}\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}_{0}^{\mathsf{mmd}}}\Big|\mathbb{P}_{P}\big({}^{\dagger}\phi_{\alpha}^{R}(\tilde{D}_{\mathsf{infer}},\Xi_{R})=1\big)-\alpha\Big|=0.

Theorem

5
demonstrates that the conditional distribution of the bootstrap statistic uniformly approximates the oracle null distribution. Consequently, the bootstrap critical value yields an asymptotically valid test with uniform level
\alpha
over the null class
\mathcal{P}_{0}^{\mathsf{mmd}}
, thereby obviating the need for an explicit closed-form limiting distribution.

When the kernel
k
is a characteristic kernel, the population MMD equals zero if and only if two distributions coincide
(
Fukumizu et al., 2007
)
. Hence, as with the classifier-based tests in
Section

4.2
, the MMD-based tests can be powerful against general alternatives, provided that the density ratio
r_{X}
can be accurately estimated.

Synthetic experiments are presented in Section

unbounded

)

(

Real-data experiments are presented in Section

and use two datasets, namely the diamonds dataset (

and the superconductivity dataset (

X
and

are standardized to zero mean and unit variance prior to sampling. A covariate shift between the two groups is induced by drawing

with probability proportional to

and

is drawn with probability proportional to

, which produces a detectable discrepancy between the two conditional distributions. The sample size is varied over

6
Conclusion

In this paper, we shed new light on the relatively underexplored problem of conditional two-sample testing. We begin by characterizing the fundamental difficulty of the problem and highlighting the importance of assumptions to make it feasible. We then introduce two general frameworks: (1) converting conditional independence tests into conditional two-sample tests and (2) transforming the problem of comparing conditional distributions into a problem of comparing marginal distributions based on density ratio estimation. Both approaches offer significant flexibility, allowing one to leverage well-developed tools to effectively tackle the problem.

Our work opens up several interesting directions for future research. One promising avenue is to extend our framework to conditional

K
-sample testing with a general
K\geq 2
. Such an extension would expand the applicability of our framework beyond the comparison of just two groups. This setting is related to conditional independence testing where
Z
is a categorical random variable taking values in
\{1,2,\ldots,K\}
. We expect our results established in
Section

3
to serve as a cornerstone for this extension. Another direction worth exploring is establishing a framework for conditional two-sample testing based on resampling methods. One promising approach is the Sampling Importance Resampling (SIR) algorithm
(
Givens and Hoeting, 2012
, Chapter 6.3)
, which allows us to obtain an approximate sample from the distribution with density

f_{XY}
. Future work can focus on methods that compare the sample from
P_{XY}^{(1)}
with the approximate sample obtained from the SIR algorithm. We leave these interesting topics for future investigation.

Appendix A
Proofs

A.1
Proof of Theorem
1

Proof.

To formalize the connection between the conditional two-sample problem and conditional independence testing (CIT), we first construct an auxiliary joint distribution. For any given distribution pair
P=(P_{XY}^{(1)},P_{XY}^{(2)})\in\mathcal{P}_{M}^{\mathsf{ac}}
, we define a joint distribution
P_{XYZ}
on
\mathbb{R}^{d_{X}}\times\mathbb{R}^{d_{Y}}\times\{1,2\}
as follows

\displaystyle P_{XYZ}\coloneqq\frac{n_{1}}{n}\big(P_{XY}^{(1)}\otimes\delta_{\{Z=1\}}\big)+\frac{n_{2}}{n}\big(P_{XY}^{(2)}\otimes\delta_{\{Z=2\}}\big),

(9)

where
n\coloneqq n_{1}+n_{2}
and
\delta_{\{Z=j\}}
denotes the Dirac measure at
Z=j
for
j\in\{1,2\}
.

Let
\mathcal{Q}_{M}^{\mathsf{ac}}
denote the class of all such joint distributions induced by (
9
) for any
P\in\mathcal{P}_{M}^{\mathsf{ac}}
. Within this induced space, we define the CIT null class as
\mathcal{Q}_{0,M}^{\mathsf{ac}}\coloneqq\{P_{XYZ}\in\mathcal{Q}_{M}^{\mathsf{ac}}:Y\mbox{${}\perp\mkern-11.0mu\perp{}$}Z\mid X\}
and the alternative class as
\mathcal{Q}_{1,M}^{\mathsf{ac}}\coloneqq\{P_{XYZ}\in\mathcal{Q}_{M}^{\mathsf{ac}}:Y\mathrel{\not\!\perp\mkern-11.0mu\perp}Z\mid X\}
.

Under the distribution (
9
), the condition
P_{Y|X}^{(1)}=P_{Y|X}^{(2)}

holds almost surely if and only if
P(Y\mid X,Z=1)=P(Y\mid X,Z=2)
holds almost surely, which corresponds to
Y\mbox{${}\perp\mkern-11.0mu\perp{}$}Z\mid X
. Therefore, the two-sample null class
\mathcal{P}_{0,M}^{\mathsf{ac}}
maps to the CIT null class
\mathcal{Q}_{0,M}^{\mathsf{ac}}
, and similarly, the alternative class
\mathcal{P}_{1,M}^{\mathsf{ac}}
maps to
\mathcal{Q}_{1,M}^{\mathsf{ac}}
.

Because the sample sizes
n_{1}
and
n_{2}
are deterministic in our setting, we cannot directly apply the i.i.d. hardness result of
Shah and Peters (2020)
. To bridge this gap, we embed the test
\phi
from Theorem
1
(denoted as
\phi_{\text{two}}
hereafter) into an i.i.d. sampling scheme. For an arbitrary integer
N\geq 2n
, suppose we draw
\{(X_{i},Y_{i},Z_{i})\}_{i=1}^{N}\overset{\mathrm{i.i.d.}}{\sim}P_{XYZ}
. Let
N_{1}^{\prime}\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathds{1}(Z_{i}=1)
and
N_{2}^{\prime}\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathds{1}(Z_{i}=2)
, and define the event
\mathcal{A}\coloneqq\{N_{1}^{\prime}\geq n_{1},\ N_{2}^{\prime}\geq n_{2}\}
.

Writing
\lambda_{n}\coloneqq n_{1}/n
, we have
N_{1}^{\prime}\sim\mathrm{Binomial}(N,\lambda_{n})
and
N_{2}^{\prime}\sim\mathrm{Binomial}(N,1-\lambda_{n})
.
Since
N\geq 2n
implies
N\lambda_{n}\geq 2n_{1}
, we have
n_{1}-N\lambda_{n}\leq-\tfrac{1}{2}N\lambda_{n}
, and applying Chebyshev’s inequality yields:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(N_{1}^{\prime}<n_{1})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(N_{1}^{\prime}-N\lambda_{n}<n_{1}-N\lambda_{n})\leq\mathbb{P}\Bigl(N_{1}^{\prime}-N\lambda_{n}\leq-\frac{1}{2}N\lambda_{n}\Bigr)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\Bigl(\big|N_{1}^{\prime}-N\lambda_{n}\big|\geq\frac{1}{2}N\lambda_{n}\Bigr)\leq\frac{4(1-\lambda_{n})}{N\lambda_{n}}.

Similarly,
\mathbb{P}(N_{2}^{\prime}<n_{2})\leq\tfrac{4\lambda_{n}}{N(1-\lambda_{n})}
. By the union bound,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{A})\geq 1-\frac{4(1-\lambda_{n})^{2}+4\lambda_{n}^{2}}{N\lambda_{n}(1-\lambda_{n})}\overset{\mathrm{set}}{=}1-\varepsilon_{N},

(10)

where
\varepsilon_{N}\rightarrow 0
as
N\rightarrow\infty
.

We now construct a conditional independence test
\phi_{\text{indep}}^{(N)}
based on these
N
i.i.d. samples. Whenever
\mathds{1}(\mathcal{A})=1
, this test extracts
\mathcal{D}_{1}
as exactly the first
n_{1}
observations from the subset
\{(X_{i},Y_{i}):Z_{i}=1\}

\mathcal{D}_{2}
as the first
n_{2}
observations from

Conditional on

\displaystyle\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}^{\mathsf{ac}}_{0,M}}\mathbb{E}_{P}(\phi_{\text{indep}}^{(N)})=\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}^{\mathsf{ac}}_{0,M}}\mathbb{E}_{P}(\phi_{\text{indep}}^{(N)}\mathds{1}(\mathcal{A}))\leq\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}^{\mathsf{ac}}_{0,M}}\mathbb{E}_{P}(\phi_{\text{indep}}^{(N)}\mid\mathcal{A})\leq\alpha,

making

a valid test for conditional independence with size

.

Furthermore, for any

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{P}(\phi_{\text{indep}}^{(N)}\mid\mathcal{A})(1-\varepsilon_{N})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\mathrm{i})}}{{\leq}}\mathbb{E}_{P}(\phi_{\text{indep}}^{(N)}\mid\mathcal{A})\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{A})=\mathbb{E}_{P}(\phi_{\text{indep}}^{(N)})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\mathrm{ii})}}{{\leq}}\alpha

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow\quad

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{P}(\phi_{\text{two}})=\mathbb{E}_{P}(\phi_{\text{indep}}^{(N)}\mid\mathcal{A})\leq\frac{\alpha}{1-\varepsilon_{N}},

where step (i) uses the inequality (
10

(
Shah and Peters, 2020

, and the equivalence
\mathbb{E}_{P}(\phi_{\text{two}})=\mathbb{E}_{P}(\phi_{\text{indep}}^{(N)}\mid\mathcal{A})

\mathcal{A}
.

Since

and
\varepsilon_{N}\rightarrow 0

A.3
Proof of Theorem
3

Proof.

We analyze the numerator and the denominator of
\widehat{\mathrm{Acc}}
separately. We first show that the numerator converges to a Gaussian distribution and then establish that the denominator is a ratio-consistent estimator of the population-level standard deviation under Assumption
1
.

\mathrm{(i)}
Analysis of the numerator.

Let us rewrite

\displaystyle\overline{A}_{1}+\overline{A}_{2}-1

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\bigl[\mathds{1}\{\widehat{h}(V_{i}^{(1)})=1\}+r_{X}(X_{i}^{(2)})\mathds{1}\{\widehat{h}(V_{i}^{(2)})=2\}-1\bigr]

\displaystyle\hskip 50.00008pt+\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\bigl\{\widehat{r}_{X}(X_{i}^{(2)})-r_{X}(X_{i}^{(2)})\bigr\}\mathds{1}\{\widehat{h}(V_{i}^{(2)})=2\}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\underbrace{\bigl[\mathds{1}\{\widehat{h}(V_{i}^{(1)})=1\}+r_{X}(X_{i}^{(2)})\mathds{1}\{\widehat{h}(V_{i}^{(2)})=2\}-1\bigr]}_{\coloneqq L_{i}(\widehat{h})}+o_{\mathcal{P}^{\mathsf{clf}}_{0}}(m^{-1/2}),

where the last approximation holds since

\displaystyle\bigg|\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\bigl\{\widehat{r}_{X}(X_{i}^{(2)})-r_{X}(X_{i}^{(2)})\bigr\}\mathds{1}\{\widehat{h}(V_{i}^{(2)})=2\}\bigg|\leq\sqrt{\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\bigl\{\widehat{r}_{X}(X_{i}^{(2)})-r_{X}(X_{i}^{(2)})\bigr\}^{2}},

and the upper bound is

o_{\mathcal{P}_{0}^{\mathsf{clf}}}(m^{-1/2})

due to Assumption
1
(b). Thus
\overline{A}_{1}+\overline{A}_{2}-1
is dominated by the average of
L_{i}(\widehat{h})
.
It therefore suffices to study the limiting distribution of
this average.
Under Assumption
1
(a), the conditional central limit theorem (
Lemma

3
) yields that

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}_{0}^{\mathsf{clf}}}\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\Bigg|\mathbb{P}_{P}\Biggl(\frac{m^{-1/2}\sum_{i=1}^{m}L_{i}(\widehat{h})}{\sqrt{\{\mathrm{Var}[L(\widehat{h})\,|\,\widehat{h}]\}}}\leq t\Biggr)-\Phi(t)\Bigg|=0.

\mathrm{(ii)}
Consistency of the variance estimate.
We next show the ratio consistency of the variance estimator. Observe that

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}[L(\widehat{h})\,|\,\widehat{h}]=\underbrace{\mathrm{Var}[\mathds{1}\{\widehat{h}(V_{i}^{(1)})=1\}\,|\,\widehat{h}]}_{\coloneqq\sigma_{1}^{2}}+\underbrace{\mathrm{Var}\bigl[r_{X}(X_{i}^{(2)})\mathds{1}\{\widehat{h}(V_{i}^{(2)})=2\}\,|\,\widehat{h}\bigr]}_{\coloneqq\sigma_{2}^{2}},

and

\displaystyle\bigg|\frac{\widehat{\sigma}_{1}^{2}+\widehat{\sigma}_{2}^{2}}{\sigma_{1}^{2}+\sigma_{2}^{2}}-1\bigg|\leq\bigg|\frac{\widehat{\sigma}_{1}^{2}-\sigma_{1}^{2}}{\sigma_{1}^{2}}\bigg|+\bigg|\frac{\widehat{\sigma}_{2}^{2}-\sigma_{2}^{2}}{\sigma_{2}^{2}}\bigg|.

Therefore, the ratio consistency of
\widehat{\sigma}_{1}^{2}+\widehat{\sigma}_{2}^{2}
reduces to that of
\widehat{\sigma}_{1}^{2}
and
\widehat{\sigma}_{2}^{2}
individually.
For
\widehat{\sigma}_{1}^{2}
, conditional Chebyshev’s inequality gives

\displaystyle\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}_{0}^{\mathsf{clf}}}\mathbb{P}_{P}\bigl(|\widehat{\sigma}_{1}^{2}/\sigma_{1}^{2}-1|\geq t\,|\,\widehat{h}\bigr)\leq\frac{1}{t^{2}}\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}_{0}^{\mathsf{clf}}}\mathrm{Var}_{P}(\widehat{\sigma}_{1}^{2}/\sigma_{1}^{2}\,|\,\widehat{h})\leq\frac{1}{t^{2}\underline{c}_{1}(1-\overline{c}_{1})m},

for sufficiently large

and for all
t>0
, under Assumption
1
(a). Hence
\widehat{\sigma}_{1}^{2}/\sigma_{1}^{2}
converges to one in probability uniformly over

\mathcal{P}_{0}^{\mathsf{clf}}
.

For
\widehat{\sigma}_{2}^{2}
, the analysis requires a finer decomposition. Letting
A_{2,i}:=r_{X}(X_{i}^{(2)})\mathds{1}\{\widehat{h}(V_{i}^{(2)})=2\}
, we write

\displaystyle\widehat{\sigma}_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m-1}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\biggl[A_{2,i}-\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}A_{2,j}\biggr]^{2}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{m-1}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\biggl[\bigl(\widehat{A}_{2,i}-A_{2,i}\bigr)-\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\bigl(\widehat{A}_{2,j}-A_{2,j}\big)\biggr]^{2}

\displaystyle+\frac{2}{m-1}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\biggl[A_{2,i}-\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}A_{2,j}\biggr]\cdot\biggl[\bigl(\widehat{A}_{2,i}-A_{2,i}\bigr)-\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\bigl(\widehat{A}_{2,j}-A_{2,j}\big)\biggr]

\displaystyle\eqqcolon(\mathbb{I})+(\mathbb{II})+(\mathbb{III}).

As before, the term
(\mathbb{I})/\sigma_{2}^{2}
converges to one in probability uniformly over
\mathcal{P}_{0}^{\mathsf{clf}}
under Assumption
1
(a).
The term

is

by Markov’s inequality combined with Assumption

(b). Lastly,

|(\mathbb{III})|\leq 2\sqrt{(\mathbb{I})\times(\mathbb{II})}
,
which is again

. Therefore,
\widehat{\sigma}_{2}^{2}/\sigma_{2}^{2}
converges to one in probability uniformly over
\mathcal{P}_{0}^{\mathsf{clf}}
,
which
proves that
\sqrt{\big\{(\sigma_{1}^{2}+\sigma_{2}^{2})/(\widehat{\sigma}_{1}^{2}+\widehat{\sigma}_{2}^{2})\big\}}=1+o_{\mathcal{P}^{\mathsf{clf}}_{0}}(1)

.

Putting all pieces together with

(b) proves the claim.

A.5
Proof of Theorem
4

Proof.

The proof proceeds in four steps. First, we derive a block-level decomposition of the statistic into four components. Second, we show that three remainder terms are asymptotically negligible, uniformly over
0\leq\gamma\leq 1
. Third, we analyze the leading term, distinguishing between the regimes
\gamma=1
and
0\leq\gamma<1
. Finally, for
0\leq\gamma<1
, we establish the consistency of the variance estimator and conclude by an application of Lemma
4
(b).

The remainder terms arising from density ratio estimation are controlled by Assumption
2
(b), which requires
\mathbb{E}(\{\widehat{r}_{X}(X)-r_{X}(X)\}^{2})=o((n-m)^{-1/2})
, an
o((n-m)^{-1/4})
root-mean-square error rate. To see how this compares with existing work, note that
Hu and Lei (2024)
impose the condition

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big(\{\widehat{G}_{11}-G_{11}\}^{2}\mid D_{\mathsf{ratio}}\big)=o_{P}(m^{-1}),

where
\widehat{G}_{11}
and
G_{11}
denote the plug-in and population kernel matrices in their framework. In squared-error scale, this effectively requires
(n-m)^{-1}
accuracy, whereas our condition requires only
(n-m)^{-1/2}
.

\mathrm{(i)}
Block-Level Decomposition.

All inner products and norms below are taken
in
the reproducing kernel Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{k}
, but we omit explicit reference to
\mathcal{H}_{k}
for notational simplicity. Using the feature map representation
k(x,y)=\langle\psi(x),\psi(y)\rangle
, the block-averaged statistic
\widehat{\mathrm{GB}}_{\gamma}
can be written as

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathrm{GB}}_{\gamma}=\frac{1}{S}\sum_{b=1}^{S}\underbrace{\frac{B}{\binom{B}{2}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i,j\in\mathcal{I}_{b}\\
i<j\end{subarray}}\big\langle\psi(V_{i}^{(1)})-\widehat{r}_{X}(X_{i}^{(2)})\,\psi(V_{i}^{(2)}),\;\psi(V_{j}^{(1)})-\widehat{r}_{X}(X_{j}^{(2)})\,\psi(V_{j}^{(2)})\big\rangle}_{=:\hat{\eta}_{b}}.

Letting
V_{B,(b)}
denote the Hilbert-space norm of the difference between the block-wise mean embeddings of the two samples,

\displaystyle V_{B,(b)}:=\bigg\|\frac{1}{B}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{b}}\psi(V_{i}^{(1)})-\frac{1}{B}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{b}}\widehat{r}_{X}(X_{i}^{(2)})\,\psi(V_{i}^{(2)})\bigg\|,

each block contribution
\hat{\eta}_{b}
can be expressed as

\displaystyle\hat{\eta}_{b}=\bigg(\underbrace{B\,V_{B,(b)}^{2}-\frac{1}{B}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{b}}\widehat{H}_{ii}}_{=:T_{b}}\bigg)\cdot\frac{B}{B-1},

so that
\widehat{\mathrm{GB}}_{\gamma}=S^{-1}\sum_{b=1}^{S}\hat{\eta}_{b}
. We decompose each block term
T_{b}
into a population part and an estimation-error part by writing
\sqrt{B}\,V_{B,(b)}=\|\mathbb{(I)}_{b}+\mathbb{(II)}_{b}\|
, where
\mathbb{(I)}_{b}
and
\mathbb{(II)}_{b}
denote the population and estimation-error components, respectively:

\displaystyle\mathbb{(I)}_{b}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{B}}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{b}}\bigl\{\psi(V_{i}^{(1)})-r_{X}(X_{i}^{(2)})\,\psi(V_{i}^{(2)})\bigr\},

\displaystyle\mathbb{(II)}_{b}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{B}}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{b}}\psi(V_{i}^{(2)})\{r_{X}(X_{i}^{(2)})-\widehat{r}_{X}(X_{i}^{(2)})\}.

Expanding the squared norm yields

B\,V_{B,(b)}^{2}=\|\mathbb{(I)}_{b}\|^{2}+\|\mathbb{(II)}_{b}\|^{2}+2\langle\mathbb{(I)}_{b},\mathbb{(II)}_{b}\rangle
.
By construction of
\widehat{H}
and
H
, the
diagonal correction can be written as

\displaystyle\frac{1}{B}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{b}}\widehat{H}_{ii}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{B}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{b}}H_{ii}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{2}{B}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{b}}\langle\psi(V_{i}^{(1)})-r_{X}(X_{i}^{(2)})\,\psi(V_{i}^{(2)}),\psi(V_{i}^{(2)})\rangle\cdot\bigl\{r_{X}(X_{i}^{(2)})-\widehat{r}_{X}(X_{i}^{(2)})\bigr\}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{B}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{b}}\langle\psi(V_{i}^{(2)}),\psi(V_{i}^{(2)})\rangle\cdot\bigl\{\widehat{r}_{X}(X_{i}^{(2)})-r_{X}(X_{i}^{(2)})\bigr\}^{2}\,.

Combining these expressions, the block-level statistic
T_{b}
admits the four-term decomposition

\displaystyle T_{b}

\displaystyle=T_{1,b}+T_{2,b}+T_{3,b}+T_{4,b}

where

\displaystyle T_{1,b}

\displaystyle:=\|\mathbb{(I)}_{b}\|^{2}-\frac{1}{B}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{b}}H_{ii},

\displaystyle T_{2,b}

\displaystyle:=\|\mathbb{(II)}_{b}\|^{2},

\displaystyle T_{3,b}

\displaystyle:=2\langle\mathbb{(I)}_{b},\mathbb{(II)}_{b}\rangle-\frac{2}{B}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{b}}\langle\psi(V_{i}^{(1)})-r_{X}(X_{i}^{(2)})\,\psi(V_{i}^{(2)}),\psi(V_{i}^{(2)})\rangle\cdot\bigl\{r_{X}(X_{i}^{(2)})-\widehat{r}_{X}(X_{i}^{(2)})\bigr\},

\displaystyle T_{4,b}

\displaystyle:=-\frac{1}{B}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{b}}\bigl\{\widehat{r}_{X}(X_{i}^{(2)})-r_{X}(X_{i}^{(2)})\bigr\}^{2}k(V_{i}^{(2)},V_{i}^{(2)}).

Defining
\mathcal{T}_{j}:=S^{-1/2}\sum_{b=1}^{S}T_{j,b}
for
j=1,2,3,4
, the scaled statistic becomes

\displaystyle\sqrt{S}\,\widehat{\mathrm{GB}}_{\gamma}=\frac{B}{B-1}\sum_{j=1}^{4}\mathcal{T}_{j}.

Since
B/(B-1)
is a bounded deterministic factor, it does not affect asymptotic negligibility. We therefore first analyze the remainder terms
\mathcal{T}_{2},\mathcal{T}_{3},\mathcal{T}_{4}
before returning to
\mathcal{T}_{1}
.

\mathrm{(ii)}
Control of Negligible Terms.
We now show that the remainder terms
are asymptotically negligible. Specifically, we establish that, uniformly over
0\leq\gamma\leq 1
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{2}=o_{\mathcal{P}_{0}^{\mathsf{mmd}}}(1),\qquad\mathcal{T}_{3}=o_{\mathcal{P}_{0}^{\mathsf{mmd}}}(1),\qquad\mathcal{T}_{4}=o_{\mathcal{P}_{0}^{\mathsf{mmd}}}(1).

(a)

Term
\mathcal{T}_{2}
.

Recall that
\mathcal{T}_{2}=S^{-1/2}\sum_{b=1}^{S}\|\mathbb{(II)}_{b}\|^{2}
. For a fixed block
b\in\{1,\ldots,S\}
, writing
\Delta_{i}:=r_{X}(X_{i}^{(2)})-\widehat{r}_{X}(X_{i}^{(2)})
, we have

\displaystyle\|\mathbb{(II)}_{b}\|^{2}=\frac{1}{B}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{b}}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{I}_{b}}k(V_{i}^{(2)},V_{j}^{(2)})\Delta_{i}\Delta_{j}\leq\frac{c_{4}}{B}\bigg(\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{b}}|\Delta_{i}|\bigg)^{2}\leq c_{4}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{b}}\Delta_{i}^{2},

where the first inequality uses

|k(x,y)|\leq c_{4}
and the second follows from
the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality. Summing over
b=1,\dots,S
yields

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{2}\leq\frac{c_{4}}{\sqrt{S}}\sum_{i=1}^{SB}\Delta_{i}^{2}.

By Markov’s inequality and Assumption
2
(b),

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{2}=o_{\mathcal{P}_{0}^{\mathsf{mmd}}}\big(B\,\sqrt{(S/(n-m))}\big)=o_{\mathcal{P}_{0}^{\mathsf{mmd}}}\big(\sqrt{(m^{1+\gamma}/(n-m))}\big),

where the second equality uses
S=\lfloor m/B\rfloor
and
B=\max\{2,\lfloor m^{\gamma}\rfloor\}
.
Under Assumption
2
(d),

m^{1+\gamma}/(n-m)\to c_{5}\in[0,\infty)
, and thus
\mathcal{T}_{2}=o_{\mathcal{P}_{0}^{\mathsf{mmd}}}(1).

(b)

Term
\mathcal{T}_{3}
.

Subtracting the diagonal terms from the inner product
\langle\mathbb{(I)}_{b},\mathbb{(II)}_{b}\rangle
and writing
h_{ij}:=\langle\psi(V_{i}^{(1)})-r_{X}(X_{i}^{(2)})\,\psi(V_{i}^{(2)}),\psi(V_{j}^{(2)})\rangle
, we can express
T_{3,b}=2\mathbb{C}_{b}
, where

\displaystyle\mathbb{C}_{b}:=\frac{1}{B}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i,j\in\mathcal{I}_{b}\\
i\neq j\end{subarray}}h_{ij}\,\Delta_{j}.

Then
\mathcal{T}_{3}=\frac{2}{\sqrt{S}}\sum_{b=1}^{S}\mathbb{C}_{b}.
Under the null hypothesis,
for
i\neq j
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(h_{i,j}\mid V_{j}^{(2)})=\big\langle\mathbb{E}(\psi(V_{i}^{(1)}))-\mathbb{E}(r_{X}(X_{i}^{(2)})\psi(V_{i}^{(2)})),\psi(V_{j}^{(2)})\big\rangle=0.

Hence, by the law of total expectation,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(\mathbb{C}_{b}\mid\tilde{D}_{\mathsf{ratio}})=\frac{1}{B}\sum_{i\neq j}\mathbb{E}\big\{\Delta_{j}\,\mathbb{E}(h_{i,j}\mid V_{j}^{(2)},\tilde{D}_{\mathsf{ratio}})\,|\,\tilde{D}_{\mathsf{ratio}}\big\}=0.

Since the blocks are formed from disjoint observations,
\{\mathbb{C}_{b}\}_{b=1}^{S}
are conditionally independent given
\tilde{D}_{\mathsf{ratio}}
, and

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}\bigg(\frac{2}{\sqrt{S}}\sum_{b=1}^{S}\mathbb{C}_{b}\big|\tilde{D}_{\mathsf{ratio}}\bigg)=\frac{4}{S}\sum_{b=1}^{S}\mathrm{Var}(\mathbb{C}_{b}\mid\tilde{D}_{\mathsf{ratio}}).

It therefore suffices to bound
\mathbb{E}(\mathbb{C}_{1}^{2}\mid D_{\mathsf{ratio}})
. Writing
S_{j}=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{1},\,i\neq j}h_{ij}
, we have
\mathbb{C}_{1}=B^{-1}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{I}_{1}}\Delta_{j}S_{j}
. Then by Jensen’s inequality,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(\mathbb{C}_{1}^{2}\mid\tilde{D}_{\mathsf{ratio}})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{B}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{I}_{1}}\mathbb{E}(\Delta_{j}^{2}S_{j}^{2}\mid\tilde{D}_{\mathsf{ratio}})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}(\Delta_{1}^{2}S_{1}^{2}\mid\tilde{D}_{\mathsf{ratio}})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\big\{\Delta_{1}^{2}\,\mathbb{E}(S_{1}^{2}\mid V_{1}^{(2)},\tilde{D}_{\mathsf{ratio}})\,|\,\tilde{D}_{\mathsf{ratio}}\big\}.

Conditional on
V_{1}^{(2)}
and
\tilde{D}_{\mathsf{ratio}}
, the variables
\{h_{i,1}\}_{i\geq 2}
are independent with mean zero. Hence,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(S_{1}^{2}\mid V_{1}^{(2)},\tilde{D}_{\mathsf{ratio}})=\sum_{i=2}^{B}\mathbb{E}(h_{i,1}^{2}\mid V_{1}^{(2)}).

Using
\langle\psi(x),\psi(y)\rangle=k(x,y)
and the uniform kernel bound

|k(x,y)|\leq c_{4}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[h_{i,1}^{2}\mid V_{1}^{(2)}]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\big(\langle\psi(V_{i}^{(1)})-r_{X}(X_{i}^{(2)})\psi(V_{i}^{(2)}),\psi(V_{1}^{(2)})\rangle^{2}\,|\,V_{1}^{(2)}\big)

\displaystyle\lesssim 1+\mathbb{E}(r_{X}(X_{i}^{(2)})^{2}).

Summing over
i=2,\ldots,B
yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(S_{1}^{2}\mid V_{1}^{(2)},\tilde{D}_{\mathsf{ratio}})\lesssim(B-1)\,\big\{1+\mathbb{E}(r_{X}(X_{2}^{(2)})^{2})\big\}.

Substituting back,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(\mathbb{C}_{1}^{2}\mid\tilde{D}_{\mathsf{ratio}})\lesssim(B-1)\,\big\{1+\mathbb{E}(r_{X}(X_{2}^{(2)})^{2})\big\}\cdot\mathbb{E}\big[\bigl\{\widehat{r}_{X}(X_{1}^{(2)})-r_{X}(X_{1}^{(2)})\bigr\}^{2}\,|\,\tilde{D}_{\mathsf{ratio}}\big].

By Assumption
2
(b),
\sup_{P}\mathbb{E}_{P}(r_{X}(X^{(2)})^{2})\leq\sqrt{c_{3}}
by the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality applied to the
fourth moment bound and the density ratio estimation error is
o((n-m)^{-1/2})
. Chebyshev’s inequality then gives

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{3}=o_{\mathcal{P}_{0}^{\mathsf{mmd}}}\big(\sqrt{B}(n-m)^{-1/4}\big)=o_{\mathcal{P}_{0}^{\mathsf{mmd}}}\big(m^{\gamma/2}(n-m)^{-1/4}\big)=o_{\mathcal{P}_{0}^{\mathsf{mmd}}}\big(m^{(\gamma-1)/4}\big),

which is
o_{\mathcal{P}_{0}^{\mathsf{mmd}}}(1)
for all
0\leq\gamma\leq 1
under Assumption
2
(d).

(c)

Term
\mathcal{T}_{4}
.

Since the kernel is uniformly bounded,
|k(x,x)|\leq c_{4}
, each block term satisfies
|T_{4,b}|\leq c_{4}\cdot B^{-1}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{b}}\Delta_{i}^{2}
. Hence,

\displaystyle|\mathcal{T}_{4}|

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{1}{\sqrt{S}}\sum_{b=1}^{S}\frac{1}{B}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{b}}\bigl\{\widehat{r}_{X}(X_{i}^{(2)})-r_{X}(X_{i}^{(2)})\bigr\}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{S}}{SB}\sum_{i=1}^{SB}\bigl\{\widehat{r}_{X}(X_{i}^{(2)})-r_{X}(X_{i}^{(2)})\bigr\}^{2}.

By Markov’s inequality and Assumption
2
(b),

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{4}=o_{\mathcal{P}_{0}^{\mathsf{mmd}}}\big(\sqrt{(S/(n-m))}\big)=o_{\mathcal{P}_{0}^{\mathsf{mmd}}}\bigg(\frac{m^{(1-\gamma)/2}}{\sqrt{(n-m)}}\bigg),

where the second equality uses

S=\lfloor m/B\rfloor
and

B=\max\{2,\lfloor m^{\gamma}\rfloor\}
.
Under Assumption
2
(d),

\displaystyle\frac{m^{(1-\gamma)/2}}{\sqrt{(n-m)}}=\sqrt{\bigg(\frac{m^{1+\gamma}}{n-m}\bigg)}\cdot m^{-\gamma}.

Since
0\leq\gamma\leq 1
and
m^{1+\gamma}/(n-m)
remains bounded, we conclude that
\mathcal{T}_{4}=o_{\mathcal{P}_{0}^{\mathsf{mmd}}}(1).

Therefore, these three remainder terms are asymptotically negligible regardless of the growth rate of the block size. The limiting distribution of the statistic is thus entirely determined by the leading term
\mathcal{T}_{1}
.

\mathrm{(iii)}
Analysis of the Leading Term.
Having shown that the remainder terms are negligible, it remains to analyze the leading component
\mathcal{T}_{1}
. Its limiting behavior depends on the growth rate of the block size
B
characterized by
\gamma
. We distinguish between the regimes
\gamma=1
and
0\leq\gamma<1
.

(a)

Regime II (
\gamma=1
).

For
\gamma=1
, all
m
observations belong to a single block (
B=m
,
S=1
),
so that

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{1}=T_{1,1}=\|\mathbb{(I)}_{1}\|^{2}-\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}H_{ii}\;=\;\frac{1}{m}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq i\neq j\leq m\end{subarray}}H_{ij}.

Since
\hat{\eta}_{1}=\frac{m}{m-1}T_{1,1}
and
S=1
, the full statistic satisfies
\widehat{\mathrm{GB}}_{1}=\frac{m}{m-1}\mathcal{T}_{1}+\frac{m}{m-1}(\mathcal{T}_{2}+\mathcal{T}_{3}+\mathcal{T}_{4})
, where the remainder is
o_{\mathcal{P}_{0}^{\mathsf{mmd}}}(1)
.
The leading term is a normalized degenerate U-statistic under the null hypothesis. Under Assumption
2
(a), there exist constants
c_{1},c_{2}>0
such that uniformly over
P\in\mathcal{P}_{0}^{\mathsf{mmd}}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{P}(H_{12}^{2})\geq c_{1},\qquad\mathbb{E}_{P}\big(|H_{12}|^{2+\delta}\big)\leq c_{2}.

Consequently, for
0<\delta\leq 1
, the bounding term in
Lemma

9
satisfies

\displaystyle\rho_{\delta}=\frac{\mathbb{E}\big(|H_{12}|^{2+\delta}\big)}{m^{\delta/2}\{\mathbb{E}(H_{12}^{2})\}^{(2+\delta)/2}}\leq\frac{c_{2}}{m^{\delta/2}c_{1}^{(2+\delta)/2}}\;\to\;0

uniformly over
P\in\mathcal{P}_{0}^{\mathsf{mmd}}
as
n\to\infty
(which implies
m\to\infty
).
Lemma

9
therefore yields

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}_{0}^{\mathsf{mmd}}}\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\bigg|\mathbb{P}_{P}\bigg(\sqrt{\frac{m}{m-1}}\,\mathcal{T}_{1}\leq t\bigg)-\mathbb{P}_{P}\big(G\leq t\big)\bigg|=0,

where

G=\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{\ell}\,(\chi_{1,\ell}^{2}-1)
with
\{\chi_{1,\ell}^{2}\}_{\ell\geq 1}
being independent chi-square random variables with one degree of freedom. Since the remainder terms are
o_{\mathcal{P}_{0}^{\mathsf{mmd}}}(1)
,
Lemma

4
(a) implies that
\widehat{\mathrm{MMD}}_{1}^{2}
has the same limiting distribution
G
.

(b)

Regime I
(
0\leq\gamma<1
).

Scaling
\mathcal{T}_{1}
by
\frac{B}{B-1}
yields the oracle statistic
\mathrm{GB}_{\gamma}
(which uses the true density ratio):

\displaystyle\mathrm{GB}_{\gamma}=\frac{B}{B-1}\,\frac{1}{S}\sum_{b=1}^{S}T_{1,b}=\frac{1}{S}\sum_{b=1}^{S}\underbrace{\bigg(\frac{1}{B-1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i,j\in\mathcal{I}_{b}\\
i\neq j\end{subarray}}H_{ij}\bigg)}_{=:\,U_{b}}.

Thus
\mathrm{GB}_{\gamma}
is an arithmetic mean of independent block-wise U-statistics
U_{b}
.
Under the null hypothesis,

\mathbb{E}_{P}(U_{b})=0
, and
standard
U
-statistic theory then gives the variance of each block:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{P}(U_{b}^{2})=\bigg(\frac{B}{B-1}\bigg)^{2}\mathbb{E}_{P}(T_{1,b}^{2})=\frac{2B}{B-1}\,\mathbb{E}_{P}(H_{12}^{2}).

For
B\geq 2
, the prefactor satisfies
B/(B-1)>1
. Hence, by Assumption
2
(a),

\displaystyle\inf_{P\in\mathcal{P}_{0}^{\mathsf{mmd}}}\mathbb{E}_{P}(U_{b}^{2})\;\geq\;2c_{1}.

This provides a uniform positive lower bound on the variance of
U_{b}
, independent of
B
.

For higher moments,
would introduce explicit dependence on
B
. Instead,
by
Lemma

7
, the decoupled statistic corresponding to
T_{1,b}
satisfies
\mathbb{E}\big(|T_{1,b}^{\mathrm{dec}}|^{2+\delta}\big)\leq c_{\delta}\mathbb{E}(|H_{12}|^{2+\delta})
. Since
U_{b}=\frac{B}{B-1}T_{1,b}
and
B/(B-1)\leq 2
, classical decoupling inequalities
(
de la Peña and Montgomery-Smith, 1995
;
de la Peña and Giné, 1999
, see)
ensure that the same uniform boundedness applies to
U_{b}
up to a modified universal constant. By Assumption
2
(a), there exist constants
c_{1}^{\prime},c_{2}^{\prime}>0
, depending only on
(c_{1},c_{2},\delta)
, such that

\displaystyle\inf_{P\in\mathcal{P}_{0}^{\mathsf{mmd}}}\mathbb{E}_{P}(U_{b}^{2})\geq c_{1}^{\prime},\qquad\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}_{0}^{\mathsf{mmd}}}\mathbb{E}_{P}\big(|U_{b}|^{2+\delta}\big)\leq c_{2}^{\prime}.

Let
\sigma_{\gamma}^{2}:=\mathrm{Var}_{P}[U_{1}]
denote the exact variance of the block-wise statistic.
Since the blocks are independent and identically distributed,
\sqrt{S}\,\mathrm{GB}_{\gamma}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{S}}\sum_{b=1}^{S}U_{b}
is a normalized sum of
S
mean-zero random variables with common variance
\sigma_{\gamma}^{2}
. Applying
Lemma

2
yields

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}_{0}^{\mathsf{mmd}}}\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\Big|\mathbb{P}_{P}\big(\sigma_{\gamma}^{-1}\sqrt{S}\,\mathrm{GB}_{\gamma}\leq t\big)-\Phi(t)\Big|=0.

Since
\sqrt{S}\,\widehat{\mathrm{GB}}_{\gamma}=\sqrt{S}\,\mathrm{GB}_{\gamma}+o_{\mathcal{P}_{0}^{\mathsf{mmd}}}(1)
by the negligibility of the remainder terms,
Lemma

4
(a) gives

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}_{0}^{\mathsf{mmd}}}\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\Big|\mathbb{P}_{P}\big(\sigma_{\gamma}^{-1}\sqrt{S}\,\widehat{\mathrm{GB}}_{\gamma}\leq t\big)-\Phi(t)\Big|=0.

This establishes the asymptotic distribution of the numerator. In the regime
\gamma=1
, the argument is complete. For
0\leq\gamma<1
, it remains to address the consistency of the empirical variance estimator.

\mathrm{(iv)}
Consistency of the Variance Estimator.
For
0\leq\gamma<1
, the statistic
\widehat{\mathrm{MMD}}^{2}_{\gamma}
is studentized by
\hat{\sigma}_{\gamma}
.
Denoting

\displaystyle\hat{\sigma}_{\gamma}^{2}:=\frac{1}{S-1}\sum_{b=1}^{S}\big(\hat{\eta}_{b}-\widehat{\mathrm{GB}}_{\gamma}\big)^{2},

we show that the ratio
\hat{\sigma}^{2}_{\gamma}/\sigma^{2}_{\gamma}
converges to
1
in probability uniformly over
\mathcal{P}_{0}^{\mathsf{mmd}}
, which implies
\hat{\sigma}_{\gamma}/\sigma_{\gamma}=1+o_{\mathcal{P}_{0}^{\mathsf{mmd}}}(1)
by
Lundborg et al. (2024, Lemma 7)
. The conclusion then follows from
Lemma

4
(b).

Since
the ratio
\hat{\sigma}^{2}_{\gamma}/\sigma^{2}_{\gamma}

is scale-invariant, we
may normalize

\sigma^{2}_{\gamma}=1
without loss of generality, simplifying our goal to showing
\hat{\sigma}_{\gamma}^{2}=1+o_{\mathcal{P}_{0}^{\mathsf{mmd}}}(1)
. To this end, we use the standard algebraic identity for the sample variance:

\displaystyle\hat{\sigma}_{\gamma}^{2}=\frac{S}{S-1}\bigg(\frac{1}{S}\sum_{b=1}^{S}\hat{\eta}_{b}^{2}-\widehat{\mathrm{GB}}_{\gamma}^{2}\bigg).

As
S\to\infty
, the prefactor
S/(S-1)
converges to
1
. Furthermore, the preceding analysis confirms that
\widehat{\mathrm{GB}}_{\gamma}=o_{\mathcal{P}_{0}^{\mathsf{mmd}}}(1)
, so
it suffices to show that

S^{-1}\sum_{b=1}^{S}\hat{\eta}_{b}^{2}

converges to
1
in probability.

Recalling

\hat{\eta}_{b}=\frac{B}{B-1}T_{b}
and the
normalization

\sigma_{\gamma}^{2}=(\frac{B}{B-1})^{2}\mathbb{E}_{P}(T_{1,b}^{2})=1
,
we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{S}\sum_{b=1}^{S}\hat{\eta}_{b}^{2}=\frac{1}{\mathbb{E}_{P}(T_{1,b}^{2})}\cdot\frac{1}{S}\sum_{b=1}^{S}T_{b}^{2}.

Therefore, the proof reduces to showing
S^{-1}\sum_{b=1}^{S}T_{b}^{2}=\mathbb{E}_{P}(T_{1,b}^{2})+o_{\mathcal{P}_{0}^{\mathsf{mmd}}}(1)
. Using

T_{b}=\sum_{j=1}^{4}T_{j,b}
and the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, we bound the difference as

\displaystyle\bigg|\frac{1}{S}\sum_{b=1}^{S}T_{b}^{2}-\mathbb{E}_{P}(T_{1,b}^{2})\bigg|

\displaystyle=\bigg|\frac{1}{S}\sum_{b=1}^{S}(T_{1,b}+T_{2,b}+T_{3,b}+T_{4,b})^{2}-\mathbb{E}_{P}(T_{1,b}^{2})\bigg|

\displaystyle\leq\bigg|\frac{1}{S}\sum_{b=1}^{S}{T^{2}_{1,b}}-\mathbb{E}_{P}(T_{1,b}^{2})\Bigg|+\Bigg|\frac{1}{S}\sum_{b=1}^{S}(T_{2,b}+T_{3,b}+T_{4,b})^{2}\Bigg|

\displaystyle\hskip 30.00005pt+2\sqrt{\Big({\frac{1}{S}\sum_{b=1}^{S}T_{1,b}^{2}}\Big)}\sqrt{\Big\{{\frac{1}{S}\sum_{b=1}^{S}(T_{2,b}+T_{3,b}+T_{4,b})^{2}}\Big\}}.

By the uniform law of large numbers,
S^{-1}\sum_{b=1}^{S}T_{1,b}^{2}-\mathbb{E}_{P}(T_{1,b}^{2})=o_{\mathcal{P}_{0}^{\mathsf{mmd}}}(1)
. It therefore suffices to show that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{S}\sum_{b=1}^{S}T_{j,b}^{2}=o_{\mathcal{P}_{0}^{\mathsf{mmd}}}(1)\quad\text{for }j=2,3,4.

We bound these terms using arguments analogous to those in part
\mathrm{(ii)}
.

(a)

Term
T_{2,b}^{2}
.

Since
T_{2,b}=\|\mathbb{(II)}_{b}\|^{2}
, we have
S^{-1}\sum_{b=1}^{S}T_{2,b}^{2}=S^{-1}\sum_{b=1}^{S}\|\mathbb{(II)}_{b}\|^{4}
. Using the bound
\|\mathbb{(II)}_{b}\|^{2}\leq c_{4}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{b}}\Delta_{i}^{2}
from part
\mathrm{(ii)}
,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{S}\sum_{b=1}^{S}\|\mathbb{(II)}_{b}\|^{4}

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{B^{2}}{S}\sum_{b=1}^{S}\bigg(\frac{1}{B}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{b}}\Delta_{i}^{2}\bigg)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{B^{2}}{S}\bigg(\sum_{b=1}^{S}\frac{1}{B}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{b}}\Delta_{i}^{2}\bigg)^{2}

\displaystyle=S\cdot B^{2}\bigg(\frac{1}{SB}\sum_{i=1}^{SB}\Delta_{i}^{2}\bigg)\cdot\bigg(\frac{1}{SB}\sum_{j=1}^{SB}\Delta_{j}^{2}\bigg)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ast)}}{{=}}o_{\mathcal{P}_{0}^{\mathsf{mmd}}}\big(B\sqrt{(S/(n-m))}\big)o_{\mathcal{P}_{0}^{\mathsf{mmd}}}\big(B\sqrt{(S/(n-m))}\big)

\displaystyle=o_{\mathcal{P}_{0}^{\mathsf{mmd}}}\big(B^{2}S/(n-m)\big)=o_{\mathcal{P}_{0}^{\mathsf{mmd}}}\big(m^{1+\gamma}/(n-m)\big),

and step
(\ast)
holds by

Lundborg et al. (2024, Lemma S5)
. Under Assumption
2
(d),
\frac{1}{S}\sum_{b=1}^{S}T_{2,b}^{2}=o_{\mathcal{P}_{0}^{\mathsf{mmd}}}(1).

(b)

Term
T_{3,b}^{2}
.

Since
T_{3,b}=2\mathbb{C}_{b}
, we can directly reuse the bounds established in the analysis of
\mathcal{T}_{3}
. Applying Markov’s inequality immediately yields
\frac{1}{S}\sum_{b=1}^{S}T_{3,b}^{2}=\frac{4}{S}\sum_{b=1}^{S}\mathbb{C}_{b}^{2}=o_{\mathcal{P}_{0}^{\mathsf{mmd}}}(1)
.

(c)

Term
T_{4,b}^{2}
.

By the boundedness of the kernel,
|T_{4,b}|\leq c_{4}B^{-1}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{b}}\Delta_{i}^{2}
. Squaring and summing over blocks, the same inequality chain as for
T_{2,b}^{2}
with
B^{-2}
in place of
B^{2}
yields

S^{-1}\sum_{b=1}^{S}T_{4,b}^{2}=o_{\mathcal{P}_{0}^{\mathsf{mmd}}}(S(n-m)^{-1})=o_{\mathcal{P}_{0}^{\mathsf{mmd}}}(m^{1-\gamma}(n-m)^{-1})
, which is
o_{\mathcal{P}_{0}^{\mathsf{mmd}}}(1)
under Assumption
2
(d).

Combining the above bounds completes the proof.
∎

A.6
Proof of Corollary
2
, Regime I (
0\leq\gamma<1
)

Proof.

For each
j\in\{1,\ldots,K\}
, the variance analysis in the proof of Theorem
4

shows
that
\widehat{\sigma}_{\gamma,j}/\sigma_{\gamma}=1+o_{\mathcal{P}_{0}^{\mathsf{mmd}}}(1)
.
By
Lemma

4
(b), it suffices to show the asymptotic normality of

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{K}}\sum_{j=1}^{K}\frac{\sqrt{S}\,\widehat{\mathrm{GB}}_{\gamma,j}}{\sigma_{\gamma}}.

Since all folds have the same test sample size, the block size
B=\max\{2,\lfloor m^{\gamma}\rfloor\}
and the number of blocks
S=\lfloor m/B\rfloor
do not depend on the fold index. The numerator analysis in the proof of Theorem
4
establishes that, for each fold
j
,
\sqrt{S}\widehat{\mathrm{GB}}_{\gamma,j}=\sqrt{S}\mathrm{GB}_{\gamma,j}+o_{\mathcal{P}_{0}^{\mathsf{mmd}}}(1)
, where

\displaystyle\mathrm{GB}_{\gamma,j}=\frac{1}{S}\sum_{b=1}^{S}\eta_{j,b},\qquad\eta_{j,b}:=\frac{1}{B-1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i,i^{\prime}\in\mathcal{I}_{j,b}\\
i\neq i^{\prime}\end{subarray}}H_{ii^{\prime}}.

As the folds are disjoint, the random variables

\{\eta_{j,b}:1\leq j\leq K,\,1\leq b\leq S\}
are mutually independent with
\mathbb{E}_{P}(\eta_{j,b})=0
under the null hypothesis and
\mathrm{Var}_{P}(\eta_{j,b})=\sigma_{\gamma}^{2}
.
Hence, the variance of their normalized sum satisfies

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}\bigg(\frac{1}{S}\sum_{j=1}^{K}\sum_{b=1}^{S}\sigma^{-1}_{\gamma}\eta_{j,b}\bigg)=\frac{1}{S^{2}}\cdot K\cdot S=\frac{K}{S}.

Combining the above, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{K}}\sum_{j=1}^{K}\frac{\sqrt{S}\,\widehat{\mathrm{GB}}_{\gamma,j}}{\sigma_{\gamma}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{(KS)}}\sum_{j=1}^{K}\sum_{b=1}^{S}\frac{\eta_{j,b}}{\sigma_{\gamma}}+o_{\mathcal{P}_{0}^{\mathsf{mmd}}}(1).

Since
K
is fixed and
S\to\infty
, the uniform central limit theorem (
Lemma

2
) under Assumption
2
implies that the leading term converges to
N(0,1)
.

Lemma

4
(a) then yields the desired result.
∎

A.7
Proof of Corollary
2
, Regime II (
\gamma=1
)

Proof.

We begin by reducing the cross-fitted empirical statistic to its oracle counterpart. By Assumption
2
and the proof of Theorem
4
, the plug-in error from the density ratio estimator
\widehat{r}_{X,-j}
is asymptotically negligible uniformly over
P\in\mathcal{P}_{0}^{\mathsf{mmd}}
. Thus, for each fold
j\in\{1,\dots,K\}
, we have

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathrm{MMD}}_{1,j}^{2}=\mathrm{MMD}_{1,j}^{2}+o_{\mathcal{P}_{0}^{\mathsf{mmd}}}(1),

where the
o_{\mathcal{P}_{0}^{\mathsf{mmd}}}(1)
term converges to zero in probability uniformly over
\mathcal{P}_{0}^{\mathsf{mmd}}
. Averaging over all
K
folds yields

\displaystyle{}^{\dagger}\widehat{\mathrm{MMD}}_{1}^{2}=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{j=1}^{K}\mathrm{MMD}_{1,j}^{2}+o_{\mathcal{P}_{0}^{\mathsf{mmd}}}(1)={}^{\dagger}\mathrm{MMD}_{1}^{2}+o_{\mathcal{P}_{0}^{\mathsf{mmd}}}(1).

(12)

Because each oracle statistic
\mathrm{MMD}_{1,j}^{2}
is evaluated on the disjoint test set
\tilde{D}_{\mathsf{test},j}
and uses only the true density ratio
r_{X}
, the family
\{\mathrm{MMD}_{1,j}^{2}\}_{j=1}^{K}
is mutually independent. Choose
G_{1},\ldots,G_{K}
to be independent copies of
G
. Applying
Lemma

5
to the two independent collections,

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\bigg|\mathbb{P}_{P}\bigg(\sum_{j=1}^{K}\mathrm{MMD}_{1,j}^{2}\leq t\bigg)-\mathbb{P}_{P}\bigg(\sum_{j=1}^{K}G_{j}\leq t\bigg)\bigg|\;\leq\;\sum_{j=1}^{K}\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\big|\mathbb{P}_{P}(\mathrm{MMD}_{1,j}^{2}\leq t)-\mathbb{P}_{P}(G_{j}\leq t)\big|.

The Kolmogorov distance is invariant under the deterministic affine map
x\mapsto x/K
, so dividing the two summed statistics by
K
preserves the bound. By
Theorem

4
(Regime II), each summand on the right tends to zero uniformly over
\mathcal{P}_{0}^{\mathsf{mmd}}
as
n\to\infty
. Since
K
is finite, the right-hand side vanishes uniformly, yielding

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}_{0}^{\mathsf{mmd}}}\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\bigg|\mathbb{P}_{P}\big({}^{\dagger}\mathrm{MMD}_{1}^{2}\leq t\big)-\mathbb{P}_{P}\Big(\frac{1}{K}\sum_{j=1}^{K}G_{j}\leq t\Big)\bigg|=0.

(13)

Finally, we transfer this uniform convergence from the oracle statistic to its plug-in counterpart. Let
F_{P}
denote the CDF of
K^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^{K}G_{j}
under
P
. Since
F_{P}
is the CDF of a non-degenerate infinite combination of chi-square variables, it admits a bounded density and is therefore uniformly continuous on
\mathbb{R}
, with a modulus of continuity that does not depend on
P
. For any
\eta>0
, we decompose

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\Big|\mathbb{P}_{P}\big({}^{\dagger}\widehat{\mathrm{MMD}}_{1}^{2}\leq t\big)-F_{P}(t)\Big|

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\Big|\mathbb{P}_{P}\big({}^{\dagger}\mathrm{MMD}_{1}^{2}\leq t\big)-F_{P}(t)\Big|

\displaystyle\quad+\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\big|F_{P}(t+\eta)-F_{P}(t-\eta)\big|+2\,\mathbb{P}_{P}\big(|{}^{\dagger}\widehat{\mathrm{MMD}}_{1}^{2}-{}^{\dagger}\mathrm{MMD}_{1}^{2}|>\eta\big),

where the first term vanishes uniformly over
\mathcal{P}_{0}^{\mathsf{mmd}}
by (
13
), the second by uniform continuity of
F_{P}
as
\eta\to 0
, and the third by (
12
). This is the same mechanism as in
Lemma

4
, extended from a normal limit to a uniformly continuous limit. Letting
n\to\infty
first and then
\eta\to 0
, we conclude

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}_{0}^{\mathsf{mmd}}}\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\Big|\mathbb{P}_{P}\big({}^{\dagger}\widehat{\mathrm{MMD}}_{1}^{2}\leq t\big)-\mathbb{P}_{P}\Big(\frac{1}{K}\sum_{j=1}^{K}G_{j}\leq t\Big)\Big|=0,

which completes the proof for Regime II.
∎

A.8
Proof of Theorem
5
(i)

Proof.

We proceed in two steps: we first establish the uniform consistency of the bootstrap distribution for the single-split case (
K=1
), and subsequently extend this argument to the general cross-fitted case (
K\geq 2
).

Case
K=1
.
To establish the uniform approximation of the bootstrap distribution to the null distribution in this regime, we define the conditional Kolmogorov distance

\displaystyle\mathbb{D}_{m}

\displaystyle:=\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\Big|\mathbb{P}\big(\widehat{\mathrm{MMD}}^{2}_{\mathrm{Boot}}\leq t\mid\tilde{D}_{\mathsf{infer}}\big)-\mathbb{P}\big(\mathrm{MMD}_{1}^{2}\leq t\big)\Big|,

and the oracle multiplier bootstrap statistic

\displaystyle\mathrm{MMD}^{2}_{\mathrm{Boot}}:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{m(m-1)}}\sum_{i\neq j}\xi_{i}\xi_{j}H_{ij},

where
H
uses the true density ratio
r_{X}
instead of the estimator
\widehat{r}_{X}
. By the triangle inequality, we decompose the total error into the estimation error and the oracle bootstrap approximation error:

\displaystyle\mathbb{D}_{m}

\displaystyle\leq\underbrace{\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\Big|\mathbb{P}\big(\widehat{\mathrm{MMD}}^{2}_{\mathrm{Boot}}\leq t\mid\tilde{D}_{\mathsf{infer}}\big)-\mathbb{P}\big(\mathrm{MMD}^{2}_{\mathrm{Boot}}\leq t\mid\tilde{D}_{\mathsf{infer}}\big)\Big|}_{=:\mathbb{D}_{m}^{\textsf{est}}}

\displaystyle\quad+\underbrace{\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\Big|\mathbb{P}\big(\mathrm{MMD}^{2}_{\mathrm{Boot}}\leq t\mid\tilde{D}_{\mathsf{infer}}\big)-\mathbb{P}\big(\mathrm{MMD}_{1}^{2}\leq t\big)\Big|}_{=:\mathbb{D}_{m}^{\textsf{oracle}}}.

To establish the consistency of the proposed bootstrap, it suffices to show that

\mathbb{D}_{m}^{\textsf{est}}=o_{\mathcal{P}_{0}^{\mathsf{mmd}}}(1)
and

\mathbb{D}_{m}^{\textsf{oracle}}=o_{\mathcal{P}_{0}^{\mathsf{mmd}}}(1)
.
We establish these two claims below.

Term
\mathbb{D}_{m}^{\textsf{oracle}}
.
The goal is to show that for every fixed
\varepsilon>0
,
\lim_{n\to\infty}\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}_{0}^{\mathsf{mmd}}}\mathbb{P}_{P}(\mathbb{D}_{m}^{\textsf{oracle}}>\varepsilon)=0
.
The term
\mathbb{D}_{m}^{\textsf{oracle}}
corresponds exactly to the empirical bootstrap distance
\Delta_{m,\mathrm{boot}}^{\ast}
analyzed in
Lemma

10
. To apply this lemma for uniform consistency, we verify its moment regularity condition. Under Assumptions
2
(a)–(c), the kernel is uniformly bounded and its cross-moment is bounded away from zero, so

\displaystyle\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}_{0}^{\mathsf{mmd}}}\frac{\mathbb{E}_{P}(H_{11}^{2})}{m\,\mathbb{E}_{P}(H_{12}^{2})}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C(c_{1},c_{3},c_{4})}{m}\to 0\quad\text{as }n\to\infty,

where
C(c_{1},c_{3},c_{4})
depends only on
c_{1}
,
c_{3}
, and
c_{4}
.
Since this ratio vanishes uniformly as
n\to\infty
(and thus
m\to\infty
),
Lemma

10
yields

\mathbb{D}_{m}^{\textsf{oracle}}=o_{\mathcal{P}_{0}^{\mathsf{mmd}}}(1)
.

Term
\mathbb{D}_{m}^{\textsf{est}}
.
The analysis of
\mathbb{D}_{m}^{\textsf{est}}
relies on the same technical tools used in the proof of
Lemma

10
.
The bootstrap statistics are scaled by
1/\sqrt{m(m-1)}
, and we define their
1/m
-scaled counterparts as

\displaystyle\widehat{T}_{\mathrm{Boot}}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{1\leq i\neq j\leq m}\xi_{i}\xi_{j}\,\widehat{H}_{ij},\qquad T_{\mathrm{Boot}}:=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{1\leq i\neq j\leq m}\xi_{i}\xi_{j}\,H_{ij}.

Since
\widehat{\mathrm{MMD}}^{2}_{\mathrm{Boot}}=a_{m}\,\widehat{T}_{\mathrm{Boot}}
and
\mathrm{MMD}^{2}_{\mathrm{Boot}}=a_{m}\,T_{\mathrm{Boot}}
with
a_{m}:=\sqrt{m/(m-1)}>0
, we have

\mathbb{P}(\widehat{\mathrm{MMD}}^{2}_{\mathrm{Boot}}\leq t\mid\tilde{D}_{\mathsf{infer}})=\mathbb{P}(\widehat{T}_{\mathrm{Boot}}\leq t/a_{m}\mid\tilde{D}_{\mathsf{infer}})
for any
t\in\mathbb{R}
.
As the Kolmogorov distance is invariant under scaling by a strictly positive constant,

\mathbb{D}_{m}^{\textsf{est}}
equals the corresponding distance between
\widehat{T}_{\mathrm{Boot}}
and
T_{\mathrm{Boot}}
conditionally on
\tilde{D}_{\mathsf{infer}}
. To analyze their difference, we express
\widehat{T}_{\mathrm{Boot}}
as

\displaystyle\widehat{T}_{\mathrm{Boot}}

\displaystyle=\big\|\sqrt{m}\,V_{m}^{\text{(Boot)}}\big\|^{2}-\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\xi_{i}^{2}\,\widehat{H}_{ii},

where
V_{m}^{\text{(Boot)}}:=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\xi_{i}\big\{\psi(V_{i}^{(1)})-\widehat{r}_{X}(X_{i}^{(2)})\,\psi(V_{i}^{(2)})\big\}
. As in the proof of Theorem
4
, we separate
\sqrt{m}V_{m}^{\text{(Boot)}}
into the population component
(\mathbf{I})
and the estimation-error component
(\mathbf{II})
:

\displaystyle\sqrt{m}\,V_{m}^{\text{(Boot)}}

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\xi_{i}\big\{\psi(V_{i}^{(1)})-r_{X}(X_{i}^{(2)})\,\psi(V_{i}^{(2)})\big\}}_{=:(\mathbf{I})}

\displaystyle\qquad+\underbrace{\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\xi_{i}\psi(V_{i}^{(2)})\big\{r_{X}(X_{i}^{(2)})-\widehat{r}_{X}(X_{i}^{(2)})\big\}}_{=:(\mathbf{II})},

so that
\big\|\sqrt{m}\,V_{m}^{\text{(Boot)}}\big\|^{2}=\|(\mathbf{I})\|^{2}+\|(\mathbf{II})\|^{2}+2\langle(\mathbf{I}),(\mathbf{II})\rangle
. Similarly, the diagonal correction term expands as

\displaystyle\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\xi_{i}^{2}\widehat{H}_{ii}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\xi_{i}^{2}H_{ii}

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{2}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\xi_{i}^{2}\langle\psi(V_{i}^{(1)})-r_{X}(X_{i}^{(2)})\,\psi(V_{i}^{(2)}),\psi(V_{i}^{(2)})\rangle\cdot\bigl\{\widehat{r}_{X}(X_{i}^{(2)})-r_{X}(X_{i}^{(2)})\bigr\}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\xi_{i}^{2}\langle\psi(V_{i}^{(2)}),\psi(V_{i}^{(2)})\rangle\cdot\bigl\{\widehat{r}_{X}(X_{i}^{(2)})-r_{X}(X_{i}^{(2)})\bigr\}^{2}.

Since
T_{\mathrm{Boot}}=\|(\mathbf{I})\|^{2}-\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\xi_{i}^{2}H_{ii}
, combining these expanded forms yields
\widehat{T}_{\mathrm{Boot}}-T_{\mathrm{Boot}}=T_{1}+T_{2}
, where

\displaystyle T_{1}

\displaystyle:=\|(\mathbf{II})\|^{2}-\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\xi_{i}^{2}\langle\psi(V_{i}^{(2)}),\psi(V_{i}^{(2)})\rangle\cdot\bigl\{\widehat{r}_{X}(X_{i}^{(2)})-r_{X}(X_{i}^{(2)})\bigr\}^{2},

\displaystyle T_{2}

\displaystyle:=2\langle(\mathbf{I}),(\mathbf{II})\rangle-\frac{2}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\xi_{i}^{2}\langle\psi(V_{i}^{(1)})-r_{X}(X_{i}^{(2)})\,\psi(V_{i}^{(2)}),\psi(V_{i}^{(2)})\rangle\cdot\bigl\{r_{X}(X_{i}^{(2)})-\widehat{r}_{X}(X_{i}^{(2)})\bigr\}.

We bound the expectations of
|T_{1}|
and
T_{2}^{2}
as follows:

(a)

Term
T_{1}
.
By definition,

\displaystyle T_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{1\leq i\neq j\leq m}\xi_{i}\xi_{j}k(V_{i}^{(2)},V_{j}^{(2)})\Delta_{i}\Delta_{j},

where
\Delta_{i}=r_{X}(X_{i}^{(2)})-\widehat{r}_{X}(X_{i}^{(2)})
. Since the kernel is uniformly bounded by
c_{4}
, adding the non-negative diagonal terms and applying the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality gives

\displaystyle|T_{1}|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{c_{4}}{m}\bigg(\sum_{i=1}^{m}|\xi_{i}\Delta_{i}|\bigg)^{2}\leq c_{4}m\bigg(\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\xi_{i}^{2}\bigg)\bigg(\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\Delta_{i}^{2}\bigg).

Taking the conditional expectation given
\tilde{D}_{\mathsf{infer}}
, and using the fact that
\xi_{i}\overset{\mathrm{i.i.d.}}{\sim}N(0,1)
are independent of the data, we have
\mathbb{E}(\xi_{i}^{2}\mid\tilde{D}_{\mathsf{infer}})=1
. This yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(|T_{1}|\mid\tilde{D}_{\mathsf{infer}})

\displaystyle\leq c_{4}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\Delta_{i}^{2}.

By the law of iterated expectations, taking the expectation
\mathbb{E}_{P}
over the data gives

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{P}(|T_{1}|)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{P}\big\{\mathbb{E}(|T_{1}|\mid\tilde{D}_{\mathsf{infer}})\big\}\leq c_{4}m\cdot\mathbb{E}_{P}(\Delta_{1}^{2})\lesssim m\cdot\mathbb{E}_{P}(\Delta_{1}^{2}).

(b)

Term
T_{2}
.
By definition,

\displaystyle T_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{m}\sum_{1\leq i\neq j\leq m}\xi_{i}\xi_{j}\,\underbrace{\langle\psi(V_{i}^{(1)})-r_{X}(X_{i}^{(2)})\,\psi(V_{i}^{(2)}),\psi(V_{j}^{(2)})\rangle}_{=\,h_{ij}}\,\underbrace{\bigl\{r_{X}(X_{j}^{(2)})-\widehat{r}_{X}(X_{j}^{(2)})\bigr\}}_{=\,\Delta_{j}}.

Writing
a_{ij}:=h_{ij}\Delta_{j}
, we have

T_{2}=\frac{2}{m}\sum_{1\leq i\neq j\leq m}\xi_{i}\xi_{j}a_{ij}
.
Since the multipliers
\xi_{i}
are independent of
\tilde{D}_{\mathsf{infer}}
with
\mathbb{E}(\xi_{i}\xi_{j})=0
for
i\neq j
, taking the conditional expectation gives
\mathbb{E}(T_{2}\mid\tilde{D}_{\mathsf{infer}})=0
, and hence
\mathbb{E}_{P}(T_{2})=0
. For the second moment, the independence and the fourth moment of the Gaussian multipliers yield the conditional variance

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}(T_{2}\mid\tilde{D}_{\mathsf{infer}})

\displaystyle=\frac{4}{m^{2}}\sum_{1\leq i\neq j\leq m}\big(a_{ij}^{2}+a_{ij}a_{ji}\big).

Using the elementary inequality
a_{ij}a_{ji}\leq\frac{1}{2}(a_{ij}^{2}+a_{ji}^{2})
and the law of total variance (noting that
\mathbb{E}_{P}(T_{2}^{2})=\mathbb{E}_{P}\{\mathrm{Var}(T_{2}\mid\tilde{D}_{\mathsf{infer}})\}
, since the conditional expectation is zero), we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{P}(T_{2}^{2})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{P}\big\{\mathrm{Var}(T_{2}\mid\tilde{D}_{\mathsf{infer}})\big\}\leq\frac{8}{m^{2}}\sum_{1\leq i\neq j\leq m}\mathbb{E}_{P}(h_{ij}^{2}\Delta_{j}^{2})\lesssim\mathbb{E}_{P}(\Delta_{1}^{2}),

where the last step uses
\mathbb{E}_{P}(h_{ij}^{2})\leq C(c_{3},c_{4})
uniformly under Assumptions
2
(b)–(c).

Instead of decomposing
T_{\mathrm{Boot}}
directly via empirical eigenvalues, we control the effect of
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
by bridging the conditional probabilities through the limit variable
U_{0}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{\ell}(Z_{\ell}^{2}-1)
. Since
\widehat{T}_{\mathrm{Boot}}=T_{\mathrm{Boot}}+T_{1}+T_{2}
, for any
\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{2}>0
, the union bound and monotonicity give

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\Big|\mathbb{P}(\widehat{T}_{\mathrm{Boot}}\leq t\mid\tilde{D}_{\mathsf{infer}})-\mathbb{P}(T_{\mathrm{Boot}}\leq t\mid\tilde{D}_{\mathsf{infer}})\Big|

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{P}\big(t-\varepsilon_{1}-\varepsilon_{2}\leq T_{\mathrm{Boot}}\leq t+\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon_{2}\mid\tilde{D}_{\mathsf{infer}}\big)

\displaystyle\quad+\mathbb{P}(|T_{1}|>\varepsilon_{1}\mid\tilde{D}_{\mathsf{infer}})+\mathbb{P}(|T_{2}|>\varepsilon_{2}\mid\tilde{D}_{\mathsf{infer}}).

Let
\widetilde{\Delta}_{m}^{\ast}:=\sup_{t}|\mathbb{P}(T_{\mathrm{Boot}}\leq t\mid\tilde{D}_{\mathsf{infer}})-\mathbb{P}(U_{0}\leq t)|
denote the bootstrap-to-limit Kolmogorov distance defined in
Lemma

10
. By the triangle inequality and the definition of
\widetilde{\Delta}_{m}^{\ast}
, the first term on the right-hand side is bounded by

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{P}\big(t-\varepsilon_{1}-\varepsilon_{2}\leq T_{\mathrm{Boot}}\leq t+\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon_{2}\mid\tilde{D}_{\mathsf{infer}}\big)

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{P}\big(t-\varepsilon_{1}-\varepsilon_{2}\leq U_{0}\leq t+\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon_{2}\big)+2\widetilde{\Delta}_{m}^{\ast}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sqrt{\bigg(\frac{\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon_{2}}{\sqrt{c_{1}}}\bigg)}+2\widetilde{\Delta}_{m}^{\ast},

where the last step applies the anti-concentration bound for
U_{0}
(
Lemma

8
) using
\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{\ell}^{2}=\mathbb{E}_{P}(H_{12}^{2})\geq c_{1}
. Taking expectations over
\tilde{D}_{\mathsf{infer}}
and applying Markov’s inequality to the deviation probabilities of
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
, we obtain for any
s>0
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{P}(\mathbb{D}_{m}^{\textsf{est}}>s)

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{1}{s}\bigg\{\sqrt{(\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon_{2})}+\mathbb{E}_{P}(\widetilde{\Delta}_{m}^{\ast})+\frac{\mathbb{E}_{P}(|T_{1}|)}{\varepsilon_{1}}+\frac{\mathbb{E}_{P}(T_{2}^{2})}{\varepsilon_{2}^{2}}\bigg\}.

We choose the auxiliary parameters to balance these terms

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{1}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{m\,\mathbb{E}_{P}(\Delta_{1}^{2})},\qquad\varepsilon_{2}=\big\{\mathbb{E}_{P}(\Delta_{1}^{2})\big\}^{1/3}.

With these choices, the penalty terms respectively satisfy

\displaystyle\frac{\mathbb{E}_{P}(|T_{1}|)}{\varepsilon_{1}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sqrt{m\,\mathbb{E}_{P}(\Delta_{1}^{2})},\qquad\frac{\mathbb{E}_{P}(T_{2}^{2})}{\varepsilon_{2}^{2}}\lesssim\big\{\mathbb{E}_{P}(\Delta_{1}^{2})\big\}^{1/3}.

Furthermore, by
Lemma

10
,
\mathbb{E}_{P}(\widetilde{\Delta}_{m}^{\ast})\lesssim\{\mathbb{E}_{P}(H_{11}^{2})/(m\mathbb{E}_{P}(H_{12}^{2}))\}^{1/5}\lesssim m^{-1/5}
uniformly over
\mathcal{P}_{0}^{\mathsf{mmd}}
. Taking the supremum over
P\in\mathcal{P}_{0}^{\mathsf{mmd}}
yields

\displaystyle\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}_{0}^{\mathsf{mmd}}}\mathbb{P}_{P}(\mathbb{D}_{m}^{\textsf{est}}>s)

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{1}{s}\Bigg(\frac{1}{c_{1}^{1/4}}\bigg[\Big\{m\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}_{0}^{\mathsf{mmd}}}\mathbb{E}_{P}(\Delta_{1}^{2})\Big\}^{1/2}+\Big\{\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}_{0}^{\mathsf{mmd}}}\mathbb{E}_{P}(\Delta_{1}^{2})\Big\}^{1/3}\bigg]^{1/2}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+m^{-1/5}+\Big\{m\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}_{0}^{\mathsf{mmd}}}\mathbb{E}_{P}(\Delta_{1}^{2})\Big\}^{1/2}+\Big\{\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}_{0}^{\mathsf{mmd}}}\mathbb{E}_{P}(\Delta_{1}^{2})\Big\}^{1/3}\Bigg).

By Assumption
2
(b),
\sup_{P}\mathbb{E}_{P}(\Delta_{1}^{2})=o((n-m)^{-1/2})
. Furthermore, Assumption
2
(d) yields
m/\sqrt{(n-m)}\to\sqrt{c_{5}}
. Together, these imply that
m\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}_{0}^{\mathsf{mmd}}}\mathbb{E}_{P}(\Delta_{1}^{2})\to 0
and
\sup_{P}\mathbb{E}_{P}(\Delta_{1}^{2})\to 0
. Since
m\to\infty
, all terms in the upper bound vanish, establishing
\mathbb{D}_{m}^{\textsf{est}}=o_{\mathcal{P}_{0}^{\mathsf{mmd}}}(1)
.

Combining
\mathbb{D}_{m}^{\textsf{oracle}}=o_{\mathcal{P}_{0}^{\mathsf{mmd}}}(1)
and
\mathbb{D}_{m}^{\textsf{est}}=o_{\mathcal{P}_{0}^{\mathsf{mmd}}}(1)
, we conclude that for every fixed
\varepsilon>0
,

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}_{0}^{\mathsf{mmd}}}\mathbb{P}_{P}\bigg(\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\Big|\mathbb{P}_{P}\big(\widehat{\mathrm{MMD}}^{2}_{\mathrm{Boot}}\leq t\mid\tilde{D}_{\mathsf{infer}}\big)-\mathbb{P}_{P}\big(\mathrm{MMD}_{1}^{2}\leq t\big)\Big|>\varepsilon\bigg)

\displaystyle=0.

Extension to general
K\geq 2
.
The preceding analysis establishes the uniform bootstrap consistency for a single fold (
K=1
). We now extend it to
K\geq 2
via a single application of
Lemma

5
. For each
j
, let
\widehat{F}_{m,j}^{*}(x):=\mathbb{P}_{P}\big(\widehat{\mathrm{MMD}}_{1,\mathrm{Boot},j}^{2}\leq x\mid\tilde{D}_{\mathsf{infer}}\big)
and
F_{0,\scriptscriptstyle{P}}(x):=\mathbb{P}_{P}(G_{j}\leq x)
. The case
K=1
established above gives, for every
\varepsilon>0
and every
j\in\{1,\ldots,K\}
,

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}_{0}^{\mathsf{mmd}}}\mathbb{P}_{P}\bigg(\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}|\widehat{F}_{m,j}^{*}(x)-F_{0,\scriptscriptstyle{P}}(x)|>\varepsilon\bigg)=0.

(14)

The multiplier collections
\{\xi_{i,j}\}_{i\in[m]}
for distinct folds

are mutually independent and independent of the data, so
\{\widehat{\mathrm{MMD}}_{1,\mathrm{Boot},j}^{2}\}_{j=1}^{K}
are conditionally mutually independent given

. Choose

\tilde{D}_{\mathsf{infer}}
. Applying

conditionally on

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\bigg|\mathbb{P}_{P}\bigg(\sum_{j=1}^{K}\widehat{\mathrm{MMD}}_{1,\mathrm{Boot},j}^{2}\leq t\,\big|\,\tilde{D}_{\mathsf{infer}}\bigg)-\mathbb{P}_{P}\bigg(\sum_{j=1}^{K}G_{j}\leq t\bigg)\bigg|\;\leq\;\sum_{j=1}^{K}\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}|\widehat{F}_{m,j}^{*}(t)-F_{0,\scriptscriptstyle{P}}(t)|,

where the unconditional CDF

on the left coincides with its conditional counterpart given

from the data. The Kolmogorov distance is invariant under the deterministic scaling

By

Proof of Theorem
5
(ii)

Proof.

Fix
\alpha\in(0,1)
and an integer
K\geq 1
. Let
\big\{{}^{\dagger}\widehat{\mathrm{MMD}}^{2,(r)}_{1,\mathrm{Boot}}\big\}_{r=1}^{R}
denote the
R
independent cross-fitted multiplier bootstrap replicates conditionally on
\tilde{D}_{\mathsf{infer}}
. For each
P\in\mathcal{P}_{0}^{\mathsf{mmd}}
, recall that the limiting cross-fitted null distribution and its
(1-\alpha)
-quantile are

\displaystyle{}^{\dagger}F_{0,\scriptscriptstyle{P}}(t)=\mathbb{P}_{P}\bigg(\frac{1}{K}\sum_{j=1}^{K}G_{j}\leq t\bigg),\qquad{}^{\dagger}q_{1-\alpha,\scriptscriptstyle{P}}=\inf\big\{t\in\mathbb{R}:{}^{\dagger}F_{0,\scriptscriptstyle{P}}(t)\geq 1-\alpha\big\}.

By combining the asymptotic null distribution of the cross-fitted statistic and Theorem
5
(i)
, the conditional bootstrap CDF for a single replicate,

\displaystyle{}^{\dagger}\widehat{F}_{m}^{*}(t):=\mathbb{P}_{P}^{*}\big({}^{\dagger}\widehat{\mathrm{MMD}}^{2,(1)}_{1,\mathrm{Boot}}\leq t\big)=\mathbb{P}_{P}\big({}^{\dagger}\widehat{\mathrm{MMD}}^{2,(1)}_{1,\mathrm{Boot}}\leq t\mid\tilde{D}_{\mathsf{infer}}\big),

satisfies, for every
\varepsilon>0
,

\displaystyle\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}_{0}^{\mathsf{mmd}}}\mathbb{P}_{P}\Big(\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\big|{}^{\dagger}\widehat{F}_{m}^{*}(t)-{}^{\dagger}F_{0,\scriptscriptstyle{P}}(t)\big|>\varepsilon\Big)\to 0,\quad\text{as }n\to\infty.

Define the empirical bootstrap CDF based on the
R
multiplier replicates by

\displaystyle{}^{\dagger}\widehat{F}_{m}^{R}(t):=\frac{1}{R}\sum_{r=1}^{R}\mathds{1}\Big({}^{\dagger}\widehat{\mathrm{MMD}}^{2,(r)}_{1,\mathrm{Boot}}\leq t\Big).

Conditionally on
\tilde{D}_{\mathsf{infer}}
, the bootstrap replicates are i.i.d. with distribution function
{}^{\dagger}\widehat{F}_{m}^{*}
. Hence, by the Dvoretzky–Kiefer–Wolfowitz inequality, for any
\varepsilon>0
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{P}\Big(\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\big|{}^{\dagger}\widehat{F}_{m}^{R}(t)-{}^{\dagger}\widehat{F}_{m}^{*}(t)\big|>\varepsilon\,|\,\tilde{D}_{\mathsf{infer}}\Big)\leq 2e^{-2R\varepsilon^{2}}.

Taking expectations and noting that the bound depends only on
R
and is independent of
P
and
n
, we obtain

\displaystyle\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}_{0}^{\mathsf{mmd}}}\mathbb{P}_{P}\Big(\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\big|{}^{\dagger}\widehat{F}_{m}^{R}(t)-{}^{\dagger}\widehat{F}_{m}^{*}(t)\big|>\varepsilon\Big)\leq 2e^{-2R\varepsilon^{2}}\to 0,\quad\text{as }R\to\infty.

By the triangle inequality, we have

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\big|{}^{\dagger}\widehat{F}_{m}^{R}(t)-{}^{\dagger}F_{0,\scriptscriptstyle{P}}(t)\big|

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\big|{}^{\dagger}\widehat{F}_{m}^{R}(t)-{}^{\dagger}\widehat{F}_{m}^{*}(t)\big|+\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\big|{}^{\dagger}\widehat{F}_{m}^{*}(t)-{}^{\dagger}F_{0,\scriptscriptstyle{P}}(t)\big|.

Hence, for any
\varepsilon>0
, by the union bound,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{P}\Big(\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\big|{}^{\dagger}\widehat{F}_{m}^{R}(t)-{}^{\dagger}F_{0,\scriptscriptstyle{P}}(t)\big|>\varepsilon\Big)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}_{P}\Big(\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\big|{}^{\dagger}\widehat{F}_{m}^{R}(t)-{}^{\dagger}\widehat{F}_{m}^{*}(t)\big|>\varepsilon/2\Big)+\mathbb{P}_{P}\Big(\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\big|{}^{\dagger}\widehat{F}_{m}^{*}(t)-{}^{\dagger}F_{0,\scriptscriptstyle{P}}(t)\big|>\varepsilon/2\Big).

Taking the supremum over
P\in\mathcal{P}_{0}^{\mathsf{mmd}}
, the first term vanishes as
R\to\infty
uniformly in
n
, and the second term vanishes as
n\to\infty
. Thus, taking the limits sequentially yields

\displaystyle\lim_{n,R\to\infty}\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}_{0}^{\mathsf{mmd}}}\mathbb{P}_{P}\Big(\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\big|{}^{\dagger}\widehat{F}_{m}^{R}(t)-{}^{\dagger}F_{0,\scriptscriptstyle{P}}(t)\big|>\varepsilon\Big)=0.

Fix
\tau>0
and define

\displaystyle\kappa(\tau):=\inf_{P\in\mathcal{P}_{0}^{\mathsf{mmd}}}\inf_{|t-q_{1-\alpha,\scriptscriptstyle{P}}^{\dagger}|\geq\tau}\big|{}^{\dagger}F_{0,\scriptscriptstyle{P}}(t)-(1-\alpha)\big|.

By the assumed uniform separation condition in Theorem
5
(ii)
,
\kappa(\tau)>0
for every
\tau>0
. Define the event

\displaystyle A_{n,R}:=\Big\{\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\big|{}^{\dagger}\widehat{F}_{m}^{R}(t)-{}^{\dagger}F_{0,\scriptscriptstyle{P}}(t)\big|<\kappa(\tau)/2\Big\}.

By the preceding uniform CDF convergence,

\displaystyle\lim_{n,R\to\infty}\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}_{0}^{\mathsf{mmd}}}\mathbb{P}_{P}(A_{n,R}^{c})=0.

It remains to show that on
A_{n,R}
we have
|{}^{\dagger}\widehat{q}_{1-\alpha}^{R}-q_{1-\alpha,\scriptscriptstyle{P}}^{\dagger}|<\tau
. We proceed by contradiction. Suppose that
{}^{\dagger}\widehat{q}_{1-\alpha}^{R}>q_{1-\alpha,\scriptscriptstyle{P}}^{\dagger}+\tau
. Then, by the definition of the empirical
(1-\alpha)
-quantile,

\displaystyle{}^{\dagger}\widehat{F}_{m}^{R}\big(^{\dagger}q_{1-\alpha,\scriptscriptstyle{P}}+\tau\big)<1-\alpha.

Hence

\displaystyle{}^{\dagger}F_{0,\scriptscriptstyle{P}}(q_{1-\alpha,\scriptscriptstyle{P}}^{\dagger}+\tau)-{}^{\dagger}\widehat{F}_{m}^{R}(q_{1-\alpha,\scriptscriptstyle{P}}^{\dagger}+\tau)>{}^{\dagger}F_{0,\scriptscriptstyle{P}}(q_{1-\alpha,\scriptscriptstyle{P}}^{\dagger}+\tau)-(1-\alpha).

Since
{}^{\dagger}F_{0,\scriptscriptstyle{P}}(q_{1-\alpha,\scriptscriptstyle{P}}^{\dagger})\geq 1-\alpha
by the definition of
{}^{\dagger}q_{1-\alpha,\scriptscriptstyle{P}}
and
{}^{\dagger}F_{0,\scriptscriptstyle{P}}
is non-decreasing, the right-hand side is at least
\kappa(\tau)
, which contradicts the event
A_{n,R}
. The case
{}^{\dagger}\widehat{q}_{1-\alpha}^{R}<q_{1-\alpha,\scriptscriptstyle{P}}^{\dagger}-\tau
is treated analogously. Consequently,

\displaystyle\lim_{n,R\to\infty}\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}_{0}^{\mathsf{mmd}}}\mathbb{P}_{P}\Big(\big|{}^{\dagger}\widehat{q}_{1-\alpha}^{R}-q_{1-\alpha,\scriptscriptstyle{P}}^{\dagger}\big|>\tau\Big)=0.

Let
{}^{\dagger}F_{m,\scriptscriptstyle{P}}(t):=\mathbb{P}_{P}\big({}^{\dagger}\widehat{\mathrm{MMD}}_{1}^{2}\leq t\big)
denote the exact finite-sample CDF of the cross-fitted test statistic. By the uniform distributional convergence of the cross-fitted statistic,

\displaystyle\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}_{0}^{\mathsf{mmd}}}\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\big|{}^{\dagger}F_{m,\scriptscriptstyle{P}}(t)-{}^{\dagger}F_{0,\scriptscriptstyle{P}}(t)\big|\to 0,\quad\text{as }n\to\infty.

The rejection probability equals

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{P}\big({}^{\dagger}\phi_{\alpha}^{R}=1\big)=\mathbb{P}_{P}\big({}^{\dagger}\widehat{\mathrm{MMD}}_{1}^{2}>{}^{\dagger}\widehat{q}_{1-\alpha}^{R}\big).

For any
\tau>0
, the rejection probability can be bounded from above and below by

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{P}\big({}^{\dagger}\widehat{\mathrm{MMD}}_{1}^{2}>q_{1-\alpha,\scriptscriptstyle{P}}^{\dagger}+\tau\big)-\mathbb{P}_{P}\big(|{}^{\dagger}\widehat{q}_{1-\alpha}^{R}-q_{1-\alpha,\scriptscriptstyle{P}}^{\dagger}|>\tau\big)

\displaystyle\quad\leq\mathbb{P}_{P}\big({}^{\dagger}\widehat{\mathrm{MMD}}_{1}^{2}>{}^{\dagger}\widehat{q}_{1-\alpha}^{R}\big)

\displaystyle\quad\leq\mathbb{P}_{P}\big({}^{\dagger}\widehat{\mathrm{MMD}}_{1}^{2}>q_{1-\alpha,\scriptscriptstyle{P}}^{\dagger}-\tau\big)+\mathbb{P}_{P}\big(|{}^{\dagger}\widehat{q}_{1-\alpha}^{R}-q_{1-\alpha,\scriptscriptstyle{P}}^{\dagger}|>\tau\big).

Using the relation
\mathbb{P}_{P}\big({}^{\dagger}\widehat{\mathrm{MMD}}_{1}^{2}>t\big)=1-{}^{\dagger}F_{m,\scriptscriptstyle{P}}(t)
and replacing
{}^{\dagger}F_{m,\scriptscriptstyle{P}}
by
{}^{\dagger}F_{0,\scriptscriptstyle{P}}
up to a uniformly vanishing error as
n\to\infty
, we obtain

\displaystyle\limsup_{n,R\to\infty}\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}_{0}^{\mathsf{mmd}}}\Big|\mathbb{P}_{P}\big({}^{\dagger}\phi_{\alpha}^{R}=1\big)-\alpha\Big|\leq\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}_{0}^{\mathsf{mmd}}}\sup_{|t-q_{1-\alpha,\scriptscriptstyle{P}}^{\dagger}|\leq\tau}\big|{}^{\dagger}F_{0,\scriptscriptstyle{P}}(t)-(1-\alpha)\big|.

It remains to show that the right-hand side can be made arbitrarily small. By Lemma
8
, the limiting distribution
G_{1}
satisfies the anti-concentration bound. Because the convolution of independent random variables strictly preserves bounded densities, the cross-fitted limit
\frac{1}{K}\sum_{j=1}^{K}G_{j}
inherits this anti-concentration property. Thus, for any
\varpi>0
,

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{P}_{P}\bigg(t<\frac{1}{K}\sum_{j=1}^{K}G_{j}\leq t+\varpi\bigg)\lesssim\sqrt{{\frac{\varpi}{\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{\ell}^{2}}}}.

Using the definition of the limiting CDF
{}^{\dagger}F_{0,\scriptscriptstyle{P}}
and the triangle inequality, we bound the local variation around the quantile:

\displaystyle\sup_{|t-q_{1-\alpha,\scriptscriptstyle{P}}^{\dagger}|\leq\tau}\big|{}^{\dagger}F_{0,\scriptscriptstyle{P}}(t)-(1-\alpha)\big|

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{s\in\mathbb{R}}\big|{}^{\dagger}F_{0,\scriptscriptstyle{P}}(s+\tau)-{}^{\dagger}F_{0,\scriptscriptstyle{P}}(s)\big|

\displaystyle=\sup_{s\in\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{P}_{P}\bigg(s<\frac{1}{K}\sum_{j=1}^{K}G_{j}\leq s+\tau\bigg)

\displaystyle\lesssim\sqrt{{\frac{\tau}{\mathbb{E}_{P}(H_{12}^{2})}}}.

Under Assumption
2
(a),
\mathbb{E}_{P}(H_{12}^{2})\geq c_{1}>0
uniformly over
P\in\mathcal{P}_{0}^{\mathsf{mmd}}
. Combining with the preceding bound,

\displaystyle\limsup_{n,R\to\infty}\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}_{0}^{\mathsf{mmd}}}\Big|\mathbb{P}_{P}\big({}^{\dagger}\phi_{\alpha}^{R}=1\big)-\alpha\Big|\lesssim\sqrt{{\frac{\tau}{c_{1}}}}.

Since the left-hand side does not depend on
\tau
, letting
\tau\downarrow 0
yields the desired conclusion.
∎

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}(\Delta_{1})

Proof of Example

Proof.

For simplicity, we let
Z\in\{0,1\}
(rather than
\{1,2\}
as in the main text), with
Z=1
for the first group and
Z=0
for the second, and apply the same convention to
\tilde{Z}
. We write
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{\to}}
and
\xrightarrow{d}
for convergence in probability and in distribution, respectively.

We show that
T_{n}
under the fixed group-size design is not coupling-stable with its i.i.d. counterpart
\tilde{T}_{n}
, by interpreting the fixed design as conditioning the i.i.d. statistic on the group count and comparing their asymptotic distributions. Define

\displaystyle\tilde{T}_{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\tilde{\zeta}_{i},\qquad\tilde{V}_{n}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\tilde{Z}_{i}-p),

where
\tilde{\zeta}_{i}=\tilde{Y}_{i}(\tilde{Z}_{i}-g(\tilde{X}_{i}))
and
p=\mathbb{P}(\tilde{Z}_{1}=1)
. Assuming
\mathbb{E}(|\tilde{\zeta}_{1}|^{2+\delta})<\infty
for some
\delta>0
, the multivariate central limit theorem implies that the joint distribution converges to a bivariate normal distribution

\displaystyle\binom{\tilde{T}_{n}}{\tilde{V}_{n}}\;\xrightarrow{d}\;\binom{\tilde{T}}{\tilde{V}}\sim N(\mathbf{0},\Sigma),

where the covariance matrix
\Sigma
is given by

\displaystyle\Sigma=\begin{pmatrix}\mathrm{Var}(\tilde{\zeta}_{1})&\mathrm{Cov}(\tilde{\zeta}_{1},\tilde{Z}_{1}-p)\\
\mathrm{Cov}(\tilde{\zeta}_{1},\tilde{Z}_{1}-p)&p(1-p)\end{pmatrix}.

Consequently, the unconditional limit of the i.i.d. statistic is
\tilde{T}_{n}\xrightarrow{d}N\bigl(0,\mathrm{Var}(\tilde{\zeta}_{1})\bigr)
.

Under the fixed group-size design, the data are generated as an i.i.d. sample subject to the constraint
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\tilde{Z}_{i}=n_{1}
. Assuming
n_{1}/n\to p
, this constraint is equivalent to
\tilde{V}_{n}=a_{n}
, where
a_{n}=\frac{n_{1}-np}{\sqrt{n}}\to 0
. Since
T_{n}
uses the identical functional form as
\tilde{T}_{n}
, its finite-sample distribution coincides with the conditional distribution of the i.i.d. statistic:

\displaystyle T_{n}\;\overset{d}{=}\;\bigg(\tilde{T}_{n}\;\big|\;\sum_{i=1}^{n}\tilde{Z}_{i}=n_{1}\bigg)\;\overset{d}{=}\;(\tilde{T}_{n}\mid\tilde{V}_{n}=a_{n}).

We must be careful in taking the asymptotic limit because
\mathbb{P}(\tilde{V}_{n}=a_{n})\to 0
as
n\to\infty
. To rigorously justify conditioning on this shrinking event, we invoke the conditional limit theorem for random vectors with a discrete component
(
Holst, 1979
, Theorem 2)
. The requisite regularity conditions are readily satisfied: the unnormalized sum
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\tilde{Z}_{i}
is a sufficient statistic for
p
, the Bernoulli variable
\tilde{Z}_{1}
satisfies the characteristic function bound
\sup_{\varepsilon\leq|t|\leq\pi}|\mathbb{E}(e^{it\tilde{Z}_{1}})|<1
, and the joint distribution converges to a Gaussian limit. Consequently, the exact conditional distribution of
T_{n}
weakly converges to the conditional distribution of its continuous limit, yielding
T_{n}\xrightarrow{d}(\tilde{T}\mid\tilde{V}=0)
.

For a bivariate normal vector, this conditional distribution is Gaussian with variance reduced by the projection onto
\tilde{V}
.

\displaystyle T_{n}\;\xrightarrow{d}\;N\bigg(0,\;\mathrm{Var}(\tilde{\zeta}_{1})-\frac{\mathrm{Cov}(\tilde{\zeta}_{1},\tilde{Z}_{1}-p)^{2}}{p(1-p)}\bigg).

Coupling stability in the sense of Definition
1
requires
|T_{n}-\tilde{T}_{n}|\xrightarrow{p}0
. If this convergence in probability holds, then
T_{n}-\tilde{T}_{n}=o_{p}(1)
, and hence
T_{n}
and
\tilde{T}_{n}
must share the same marginal weak limit. We now evaluate the covariance term. Under the null hypothesis
Y\mbox{${}\perp\mkern-11.0mu\perp{}$}Z\mid X
, we have
\tilde{Y}_{1}\mbox{${}\perp\mkern-11.0mu\perp{}$}\tilde{Z}_{1}\mid\tilde{X}_{1}
. Using the law of total expectation, we obtain the following expansion.

\displaystyle\mathrm{Cov}(\tilde{\zeta}_{1},\tilde{Z}_{1}-p)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\{\tilde{Y}_{1}(\tilde{Z}_{1}-g(\tilde{X}_{1}))(\tilde{Z}_{1}-p)\}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\big[\mathbb{E}(\tilde{Y}_{1}\mid\tilde{X}_{1})\cdot\mathbb{E}\{(\tilde{Z}_{1}-g(\tilde{X}_{1}))(\tilde{Z}_{1}-p)\mid\tilde{X}_{1}\}\big]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\big\{f(\tilde{X}_{1})g(\tilde{X}_{1})(1-g(\tilde{X}_{1}))\big\}.

Whenever
\mathbb{E}\{f(\tilde{X}_{1})g(\tilde{X}_{1})(1-g(\tilde{X}_{1}))\}\neq 0
, the asymptotic variance of
T_{n}
is strictly smaller than that of
\tilde{T}_{n}
. Because their marginal asymptotic distributions differ, the necessary condition
|T_{n}-\tilde{T}_{n}|\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{\to}}0
therefore fails, and
T_{n}
is not coupling-stable.
∎

, where finitely many independent (or conditionally independent) statistics are aggregated.

Lemma 5

(Kolmogorov subadditivity;
Mattner, 2024
, Lemma 5.1
)
.

Let
K\in\mathbb{N}
and let
\{Z_{j}\}_{j=1}^{K}
and
\{W_{j}\}_{j=1}^{K}
be two collections of real-valued random variables defined on a common probability space. Suppose that, within each collection, the
K
variables are mutually independent. Then

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\bigg|\mathbb{P}\bigg(\sum_{j=1}^{K}Z_{j}\leq t\bigg)-\mathbb{P}\bigg(\sum_{j=1}^{K}W_{j}\leq t\bigg)\bigg|\;\leq\;\sum_{j=1}^{K}\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\big|\mathbb{P}(Z_{j}\leq t)-\mathbb{P}(W_{j}\leq t)\big|.

(16)

Moreover, the same inequality holds when both probabilities are replaced by conditional probabilities given a common
\sigma
-field
\mathcal{G}
, provided that, within each of
\{Z_{j}\}_{j=1}^{K}
and
\{W_{j}\}_{j=1}^{K}
, the variables are mutually independent conditionally on
\mathcal{G}
.

The unconditional inequality is the case of
Mattner (2024, Lemma 5.1)
corresponding to the translation-invariant test class
\mathcal{F}=\{\mathds{1}_{(-\infty,t]}\,:\,t\in\mathbb{R}\}
, for which
\|P-Q\|_{\mathcal{F}}
coincides with the Kolmogorov distance. The conditional version follows by applying the unconditional inequality fiberwise to the regular conditional joint laws of
(Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{K})
and
(W_{1},\ldots,W_{K})
given
\mathcal{G}
, which factorize as products of conditional marginals under the stated conditional independence.

The following two lemmas provide moment bounds for canonical
U
-statistics. Lemma
6
gives a general inequality for decoupled kernels
(
Giné et al., 2000
)
, while Lemma
7
specializes this to the block statistic
T_{1,1}
, showing that its
(2+\delta)
-moment is controlled by the kernel moment. Standard decoupling results
(
de la Peña and Montgomery-Smith, 1995
;
de la Peña and Giné, 1999
)
then extend the bound to the original statistic.

Lemma 6
.

(
Giné et al., 2000
, Equation (3.3)
)

Let
h_{i,j}:=h_{i,j}(X_{i}^{(1)},X_{j}^{(2)})
be canonical kernels such that

\mathbb{E}|h_{i,j}|^{p}<\infty
for all
1\leq i,j\leq n
. Then, for any
p>2
, there exists a constant
C_{p}>0
such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}h_{i,j}\Big|^{p}\leq C_{p}\max\Biggl\{

\displaystyle p^{p}\biggl(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\,h_{i,j}^{2}\biggr)^{\!p/2},p^{3p/2}\,\mathbb{E}_{1}\max_{1\leq i\leq n}\biggl(\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}_{2}\,h_{i,j}^{2}\biggr)^{\!p/2},

\displaystyle p^{3p/2}\,\mathbb{E}_{2}\max_{1\leq j\leq n}\biggl(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}_{1}\,h_{i,j}^{2}\biggr)^{p/2},p^{2p}\,\mathbb{E}\max_{1\leq i,j\leq n}|h_{i,j}|^{p}\Biggr\}.

Here,
\mathbb{E}_{1}
and
\mathbb{E}_{2}
denote expectations with respect to
X^{(1)}
and
X^{(2)}
, respectively.

Lemma 7

(Moment bound for
T_{1,1}
in
Section

A.5
)
.

Let
\mathcal{W}
be a measurable space,
P
a probability measure on
\mathcal{W}
, and
H:\mathcal{W}\times\mathcal{W}\to\mathbb{R}
a symmetric canonical kernel.
Let
\{W_{i}^{(1)}\}_{i=1}^{B}
and
\{W_{j}^{(2)}\}_{j=1}^{B}
be mutually independent i.i.d. samples from
P
. Define the decoupled statistic

\displaystyle T_{1,1}^{\mathrm{dec}}:=\frac{1}{B}\sum_{i=1}^{B}\sum_{j=1}^{B}H(W_{i}^{(1)},W_{j}^{(2)})

Then, for any
\delta>0
, there exists a constant
C_{\delta}>0
, independent of
B
, such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigl(|T_{1,1}^{\mathrm{dec}}|^{2+\delta}\bigr)\;\leq\;C_{\delta}\,\mathbb{E}\bigl(|H(W_{1},W_{2})|^{2+\delta}\bigr).

Moreover, by classical decoupling inequalities
(
de la Peña and Montgomery-Smith, 1995
;
de la Peña and Giné, 1999
)
, the same bound holds for the block-level statistic
T_{1,1}
up to a universal constant independent of
B
, where

\displaystyle T_{1,1}=\frac{1}{B}\sum_{1\leq i\neq j\leq B}H(W_{i},W_{j}).

Proof.

Fix
p=2+\delta
for some
\delta>0
, and write
h_{ij}:=H(W_{i}^{(1)},W_{j}^{(2)})
. Applying
Lemma

6
together with
\max\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{m}\}\leq a_{1}+\cdots+a_{m}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigg|\sum_{i=1}^{B}\sum_{j=1}^{B}h_{ij}\bigg|^{p}\lesssim A_{1}+A_{2}+A_{3}+A_{4},

where multiplicative constants independent of
B
are absorbed into
\lesssim
, and

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} A_{1}&=\Big\{\sum_{i=1}^{B}\sum_{j=1}^{B}\mathbb{E}(h_{ij}^{2})\Big\}^{p/2},&\qquad A_{2}&=\mathbb{E}_{1}\Bigg[\max_{1\leq i\leq B}\Big\{\sum_{j=1}^{B}\mathbb{E}_{2}(h_{ij}^{2})\Big\}^{p/2}\Bigg],\\
A_{3}&=\mathbb{E}_{2}\Bigg[\max_{1\leq j\leq B}\Big\{\sum_{i=1}^{B}\mathbb{E}_{1}(h_{ij}^{2})\Big\}^{p/2}\Bigg],&\qquad A_{4}&=\mathbb{E}\Big(\max_{1\leq i,j\leq B}|h_{ij}|^{p}\Big).\end{aligned}

We bound each term in turn.

(a)

Term
A_{1}
.
By the i.i.d. structure,
\sum_{i=1}^{B}\sum_{j=1}^{B}\mathbb{E}(h_{ij}^{2})=B^{2}\mathbb{E}(h_{12}^{2})
, and hence

\displaystyle A_{1}\;\lesssim\;B^{p}\{\mathbb{E}(h_{12}^{2})\}^{p/2}\;\leq\;B^{p}\,\mathbb{E}(|h_{12}|^{p}),

where the last inequality uses Jensen’s inequality.

(b)

Terms
A_{2}
and
A_{3}
.
Replacing the maximum by the sum,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{1}\bigg[\max_{1\leq i\leq B}\Big\{\sum_{j=1}^{B}\mathbb{E}_{2}(h_{ij}^{2})\Big\}^{p/2}\bigg]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{B}\mathbb{E}_{1}\Big\{\sum_{j=1}^{B}\mathbb{E}_{2}(h_{ij}^{2})\Big\}^{p/2}=B\cdot\mathbb{E}_{1}\Big\{\sum_{j=1}^{B}\mathbb{E}_{2}(h_{1j}^{2})\Big\}^{p/2},

where the equality follows from the i.i.d. structure. Since
p>2
and
x\mapsto x^{p/2}
is convex, Jensen’s inequality gives

\displaystyle\Big\{\sum_{j=1}^{B}\mathbb{E}_{2}(h_{1j}^{2})\Big\}^{p/2}

\displaystyle\leq B^{p/2-1}\sum_{j=1}^{B}\{\mathbb{E}_{2}(h_{1j}^{2})\}^{p/2},

and combining these with one further application of Jensen’s inequality yields

\displaystyle A_{2}\;\leq\;B^{1+p/2}\,\mathbb{E}(|h_{12}|^{p}).

The same bound holds for
A_{3}
by interchanging the roles of
(W_{i}^{(1)})
and
(W_{j}^{(2)})
.

(c)

Term
A_{4}
.
Bounding the maximum by the sum,

\displaystyle A_{4}\;\leq\;B^{2}\,\mathbb{E}(|h_{12}|^{p}).

Combining these bounds and noting
B^{1+p/2}\leq B^{p}
and
B^{2}\leq B^{p}
for
p\geq 2
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigg|\sum_{i=1}^{B}\sum_{j=1}^{B}h_{ij}\bigg|^{p}\;\lesssim\;B^{p}\,\mathbb{E}(|h_{12}|^{p}).

Normalizing by
B^{-p}
gives
\mathbb{E}(|T_{1,1}^{\mathrm{dec}}|^{p})\lesssim\mathbb{E}(|h_{12}|^{p})
, which establishes the first bound. The corresponding bound for the block-level statistic
T_{1,1}
then follows from classical decoupling inequalities
(
de la Peña and Montgomery-Smith, 1995
;
de la Peña and Giné, 1999
)
, up to a universal constant independent of
B
.
∎

The following lemma provides a concentration inequality for an infinite sum of chi-squared random variables.

Lemma 8
.

(
Fan et al., 2026
, Lemma A.7
)

Let
\{\alpha_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
be a sequence of real numbers satisfying
\alpha_{1}\geq\alpha_{2}\geq\ldots\geq 0
and
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\alpha_{n}^{2}=1
, and let
\{\eta_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}\overset{\mathrm{i.i.d.}}{\sim}\chi_{1}^{2}
. Then, for any
\varpi>0
,

\displaystyle\sup_{z\in\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{P}\bigg\{z<\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\alpha_{n}(\eta_{n}-1)\leq z+\varpi\bigg\}\leq\sqrt{\bigg(\frac{4\varpi}{\pi}\bigg)}.

Remark.

Lemma

8
also holds for finite sums. For any finite sequence
\alpha_{1}\geq\cdots\geq\alpha_{L}>0
with
\sum_{n=1}^{L}\alpha_{n}^{2}=1
, we can simply set
\alpha_{n}=0
for all
n>L
. This directly yields

\displaystyle\sup_{z\in\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{P}\bigg\{z<\sum_{n=1}^{L}\alpha_{n}(\eta_{n}-1)\leq z+\varpi\bigg\}\leq\sqrt{\bigg(\frac{4\varpi}{\pi}\bigg)}.

The following lemma adapts the non-asymptotic distributional approximation results of
Fan et al. (2026)
, originally developed for independence testing with two random vectors, to the single-variate degenerate
U
-statistic setting used throughout our proofs.

Lemma 9

(Degenerate
U
-statistic approximation)
.

Let
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}\overset{\mathrm{i.i.d.}}{\sim}P
take values in a measurable space
\mathcal{X}
, and let
h:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
be a measurable symmetric kernel with
\mathbb{E}\{h(X_{1},X_{2})^{2}\}\in(0,\infty)
, satisfying the degeneracy condition

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\{h(x,X_{1})\}=0\qquad\text{for all }x\in\mathcal{X}.

Define the degenerate
U
-statistic

\displaystyle U_{n}:=\frac{1}{n(n-1)}\sum_{1\leq i\neq j\leq n}h(X_{i},X_{j}),

and let
Q:L^{2}(P)\to L^{2}(P)
be the Hilbert–Schmidt integral operator

\displaystyle(Qg)(x)=\int h(x,y)\,g(y)\,dP(y),

with eigenvalues
\{\lambda_{\ell\geq 1}\}
ordered so that
|\lambda_{1}|\geq|\lambda_{2}|\geq\cdots
. Define

\displaystyle U_{0}:=\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{\ell}(V_{\ell}^{2}-1),\qquad V_{1},V_{2},\ldots\overset{\mathrm{i.i.d.}}{\sim}N(0,1),

and

\displaystyle\Delta_{n}:=\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\big|\mathbb{P}\bigl(\sqrt{\{n(n-1)\}}\,U_{n}\leq t\bigr)-\mathbb{P}(U_{0}\leq t)\big|.

Then for every
0<\delta\leq 1
, there exists a constant
C_{\delta}>0
depending only on
\delta
such that

\displaystyle\Delta_{n}\leq C_{\delta}\Bigg(n^{-1/5}+\Bigg[\underbrace{\frac{\mathbb{E}(|h(X_{1},X_{2})|^{2+\delta})}{n^{\delta/2}\,\{\mathbb{E}(h(X_{1},X_{2})^{2})\}^{(2+\delta)/2}}}_{=:\,\rho_{\delta}}\Bigg]^{1/(2\delta+5)}\Bigg).

The argument parallels that of Theorem 3.3 in
Fan et al. (2026)
, with modifications confined to the reduction from the two-sample independence-testing setting of
Fan et al. (2026)
to the single-sample degenerate
U
-statistic considered here. We therefore omit the details.

Lemma

9
serves as a building block for the following non-asymptotic multiplier bootstrap approximation, which bounds the Kolmogorov distance between the conditional law of the bootstrap statistic and the non-Gaussian limit
U_{0}
.

Lemma 10

(Multiplier bootstrap consistency)
.

Adopt the setting of
Lemma

9
. Assume additionally that
h
is positive semidefinite, and admits the spectral expansion

\displaystyle h(x,y)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{\ell}\phi_{\ell}(x)\phi_{\ell}(y),

with eigenvalues ordered as
\lambda_{1}\geq\lambda_{2}\geq\cdots\geq 0
. Here, the eigenfunctions
\{\phi_{\ell}\}_{\ell\geq 1}
are mutually uncorrelated with zero mean and unit variance, meaning
\mathbb{E}\{\phi_{\ell}(X_{1})\}=0
and
\mathbb{E}\{\phi_{\ell}(X_{1})\phi_{m}(X_{1})\}=\mathds{1}(\ell=m)
for all
\ell,m
. We also assume the diagonal identity

\displaystyle h(x,x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{\ell}\phi_{\ell}^{2}(x)

holds almost surely.

Let
\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{n}\overset{\mathrm{i.i.d.}}{\sim}N(0,1)
be independent of the data
\mathbb{X}_{n}=(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})
. Define the original statistic
T_{n}
and its multiplier bootstrap counterpart
T_{n}^{\ast}
as

\displaystyle T_{n}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{1\leq i\neq j\leq n}h(X_{i},X_{j}),\qquad T_{n}^{\ast}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{1\leq i\neq j\leq n}\xi_{i}\xi_{j}h(X_{i},X_{j}).

Let
U_{0}:=\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{\ell}(Z_{\ell}^{2}-1)
with
Z_{1},Z_{2},\ldots\overset{\mathrm{i.i.d.}}{\sim}N(0,1)
. Define the relevant distance metrics as

\displaystyle\widetilde{\Delta}_{n}^{\ast}

\displaystyle:=\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\big|\mathbb{P}_{\xi}(T_{n}^{\ast}\leq t\mid\mathbb{X}_{n})-\mathbb{P}(U_{0}\leq t)\big|,

\displaystyle\Delta_{n}

\displaystyle:=\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\big|\mathbb{P}(T_{n}\leq t)-\mathbb{P}(U_{0}\leq t)\big|,

\displaystyle\Delta_{n,\mathrm{boot}}^{\ast}

\displaystyle:=\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\big|\mathbb{P}_{\xi}(T_{n}^{\ast}\leq t\mid\mathbb{X}_{n})-\mathbb{P}(T_{n}\leq t)\big|.

Then, for every
s>0
, there exists a universal constant
C>0
such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\big(\widetilde{\Delta}_{n}^{\ast}\geq s\big)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C}{s}\bigg[\frac{\mathbb{E}\{h(X_{1},X_{1})^{2}\}}{n\,\mathbb{E}\{h(X_{1},X_{2})^{2}\}}\bigg]^{1/5},

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\big(\Delta_{n,\mathrm{boot}}^{\ast}\geq s\big)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{s}\bigg\{\Delta_{n}+C\bigg[\frac{\mathbb{E}\{h(X_{1},X_{1})^{2}\}}{n\,\mathbb{E}\{h(X_{1},X_{2})^{2}\}}\bigg]^{1/5}\bigg\}.

Consequently, if
\Delta_{n}\to 0
and
\mathbb{E}\{h(X_{1},X_{1})^{2}\}/[n\,\mathbb{E}\{h(X_{1},X_{2})^{2}\}]\to 0
as
n\to\infty
, then

\displaystyle\Delta_{n,\mathrm{boot}}^{\ast}

\displaystyle\to 0

in probability.

Proof.

For
L\geq 1
, define the truncated kernel

\displaystyle h_{L}(x,y)

\displaystyle:=\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\lambda_{\ell}\phi_{\ell}(x)\phi_{\ell}(y),

and define

\displaystyle T_{n,L}^{\ast}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{1\leq i\neq j\leq n}\xi_{i}\xi_{j}h_{L}(X_{i},X_{j}),\qquad U_{0,L}:=\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\lambda_{\ell}(Z_{\ell}^{2}-1).

Let
\Phi_{L}(x):=\{\phi_{1}(x),\ldots,\phi_{L}(x)\}^{\top}
,
\Lambda_{L}:=\mathrm{diag}(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{L})
, and

\displaystyle W_{L}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\xi_{i}\Phi_{L}(X_{i}).

Conditional on
\mathbb{X}_{n}
,

\displaystyle W_{L}\mid\mathbb{X}_{n}

\displaystyle\sim N(0,\widehat{\Sigma}_{L}),\qquad\widehat{\Sigma}_{L}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Phi_{L}(X_{i})\Phi_{L}(X_{i})^{\top}.

Using the algebraic identity
\sum_{1\leq i\neq j\leq n}\xi_{i}\xi_{j}h_{L}(X_{i},X_{j})=\sum_{1\leq i,j\leq n}\xi_{i}\xi_{j}h_{L}(X_{i},X_{j})-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\xi_{i}^{2}h_{L}(X_{i},X_{i})
, we can write

\displaystyle T_{n,L}^{\ast}

\displaystyle=\bar{Q}_{n,L}^{\ast}-\bar{D}_{n,L}^{\ast},

where

\displaystyle\bar{Q}_{n,L}^{\ast}

\displaystyle:=W_{L}^{\top}\Lambda_{L}W_{L}-\mathrm{tr}(\widehat{\Sigma}_{L}\Lambda_{L}),\qquad\bar{D}_{n,L}^{\ast}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\xi_{i}^{2}-1)h_{L}(X_{i},X_{i}).

This centering uses the fact that
\mathrm{tr}(\widehat{\Sigma}_{L}\Lambda_{L})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}h_{L}(X_{i},X_{i})
.

We first compare
\bar{Q}_{n,L}^{\ast}
with
U_{0,L}
. Conditional on
\mathbb{X}_{n}
, the quadratic form
W_{L}^{\top}\Lambda_{L}W_{L}
has the same distribution as
\sum_{j=1}^{L}\hat{\gamma}_{j}Z_{j}^{2}
, where
Z_{j}\overset{\mathrm{i.i.d.}}{\sim}N(0,1)
and
\hat{\gamma}_{1}\geq\cdots\geq\hat{\gamma}_{L}
are the eigenvalues of the symmetric matrix
\sqrt{\Lambda_{L}}\widehat{\Sigma}_{L}\sqrt{\Lambda_{L}}
. Noting that
\mathrm{tr}(\widehat{\Sigma}_{L}\Lambda_{L})=\sum_{j=1}^{L}\hat{\gamma}_{j}
, we have the distributional equivalence

\displaystyle\bar{Q}_{n,L}^{\ast}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\sum_{j=1}^{L}\hat{\gamma}_{j}(Z_{j}^{2}-1).

The limit statistic can be written as
U_{0,L}=\sum_{j=1}^{L}\lambda_{j}(Z_{j}^{2}-1)
, where
\lambda_{1}\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_{L}
are the eigenvalues of
\Lambda_{L}
. Coupling them on the same probability space using the same sequence
\{Z_{j}\}_{j=1}^{L}
, we define the error

\displaystyle A_{n,L}

\displaystyle:=\sum_{j=1}^{L}(\hat{\gamma}_{j}-\lambda_{j})(Z_{j}^{2}-1),\qquad\text{so that}\quad\bar{Q}_{n,L}^{\ast}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}U_{0,L}+A_{n,L}.

This coupling is used only for the quadratic part
\bar{Q}_{n,L}^{\ast}
; no independence between
A_{n,L}
and
\bar{D}_{n,L}^{\ast}
is required.

Let
\beta_{L}:=\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\lambda_{\ell}^{2}
, and define
\omega_{L}(r):=\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{P}(t-r\leq U_{0,L}\leq t+r)
. Choose
L
large enough such that
\beta_{L}>0
. By the anti-concentration lemma for weighted sums of centered chi-square random variables (
Lemma

8
),

\displaystyle\omega_{L}(r)

\displaystyle\lesssim\sqrt{\frac{r}{\beta_{L}^{1/2}}}.

Therefore, for any
\varepsilon_{1}>0
,

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\Big|\mathbb{P}_{\xi}(\bar{Q}_{n,L}^{\ast}\leq t\mid\mathbb{X}_{n})-\mathbb{P}(U_{0,L}\leq t)\Big|

\displaystyle\leq\omega_{L}(\varepsilon_{1})+\mathbb{P}_{Z}(|A_{n,L}|>\varepsilon_{1}\mid\mathbb{X}_{n}).

Conditional on
\mathbb{X}_{n}
, the variance of
A_{n,L}
is
2\sum_{j=1}^{L}(\hat{\gamma}_{j}-\lambda_{j})^{2}
. By the Hoffman–Wielandt theorem for symmetric matrices, the sum of squared eigenvalue differences is bounded by the squared Frobenius norm of the matrix difference. Thus,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}_{Z}(A_{n,L}\mid\mathbb{X}_{n})

\displaystyle\leq 2\big\|\sqrt{\Lambda_{L}}\widehat{\Sigma}_{L}\sqrt{\Lambda_{L}}-\Lambda_{L}\big\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle=2\mathrm{tr}\Big[\big(\sqrt{\Lambda_{L}}(\widehat{\Sigma}_{L}-I_{L})\sqrt{\Lambda_{L}}\big)^{2}\Big].

Using the cyclic property of the trace, this equals
2\mathrm{tr}[((\widehat{\Sigma}_{L}-I_{L})\Lambda_{L})^{2}]
, which expands to

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}_{Z}(A_{n,L}\mid\mathbb{X}_{n})

\displaystyle\leq 2\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\sum_{k=1}^{L}\lambda_{\ell}\lambda_{k}(\widehat{\Sigma}_{L,\ell k}-\delta_{\ell k})^{2},

where
\delta_{\ell k}
is the Kronecker delta (which equals
1
if
\ell=k
and
0
otherwise). Taking expectation over
\mathbb{X}_{n}
, and using the orthonormality of
\{\phi_{\ell}\}
in
L^{2}(P)
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big\{\mathrm{Var}_{Z}(A_{n,L}\mid\mathbb{X}_{n})\big\}

\displaystyle\leq 2\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\sum_{k=1}^{L}\lambda_{\ell}\lambda_{k}\mathbb{E}\big[(\widehat{\Sigma}_{L,\ell k}-\delta_{\ell k})^{2}\big]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{n}\mathbb{E}\bigg[\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\sum_{k=1}^{L}\lambda_{\ell}\lambda_{k}\phi_{\ell}^{2}(X_{1})\phi_{k}^{2}(X_{1})\bigg]

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{n}\mathbb{E}\{h_{L}(X_{1},X_{1})^{2}\}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{n}\mathbb{E}\{h(X_{1},X_{1})^{2}\}.

The last inequality follows from
\lambda_{\ell}\geq 0
and the diagonal identity, which imply
0\leq h_{L}(x,x)\leq h(x,x)
.

Next, since
\xi_{i}\sim N(0,1)
,
\mathrm{Var}_{\xi}(\xi_{i}^{2}-1)=2
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}_{\xi}(\bar{D}_{n,L}^{\ast}\mid\mathbb{X}_{n})

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{n^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}h_{L}(X_{i},X_{i})^{2}.

Taking expectation,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big\{\mathrm{Var}_{\xi}(\bar{D}_{n,L}^{\ast}\mid\mathbb{X}_{n})\big\}

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{n}\mathbb{E}\{h_{L}(X_{1},X_{1})^{2}\}\leq\frac{2}{n}\mathbb{E}\{h(X_{1},X_{1})^{2}\}.

Now fix
\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{2}>0
. Since
T_{n,L}^{\ast}=\bar{Q}_{n,L}^{\ast}-\bar{D}_{n,L}^{\ast}
,

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\big|\mathbb{P}_{\xi}(T_{n,L}^{\ast}\leq t\mid\mathbb{X}_{n})-\mathbb{P}(U_{0,L}\leq t)\big|

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\omega_{L}(\varepsilon_{1})+\omega_{L}(\varepsilon_{2})+\mathbb{P}_{Z}(|A_{n,L}|>\varepsilon_{1}\mid\mathbb{X}_{n})+\mathbb{P}_{\xi}(|\bar{D}_{n,L}^{\ast}|>\varepsilon_{2}\mid\mathbb{X}_{n}).

By Chebyshev’s inequality and the preceding variance bounds,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigg[\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\big|\mathbb{P}_{\xi}(T_{n,L}^{\ast}\leq t\mid\mathbb{X}_{n})-\mathbb{P}(U_{0,L}\leq t)\big|\bigg]

\displaystyle\qquad\lesssim\sqrt{\frac{\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon_{2}}{\beta_{L}^{1/2}}}+\frac{\mathbb{E}\{h(X_{1},X_{1})^{2}\}}{n\varepsilon_{1}^{2}}+\frac{\mathbb{E}\{h(X_{1},X_{1})^{2}\}}{n\varepsilon_{2}^{2}}.

It remains to pass from the truncated quantities to the infinite-dimensional ones. Let
\eta>0
. A standard smoothing argument gives

\displaystyle\widetilde{\Delta}_{n}^{\ast}

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\Big|\mathbb{P}_{\xi}(T_{n,L}^{\ast}\leq t\mid\mathbb{X}_{n})-\mathbb{P}(U_{0,L}\leq t)\Big|

\displaystyle\quad+\mathbb{P}_{\xi}(|T_{n}^{\ast}-T_{n,L}^{\ast}|>\eta\mid\mathbb{X}_{n})+\mathbb{P}(|U_{0}-U_{0,L}|>\eta)

\displaystyle\quad+\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{P}(t-2\eta\leq U_{0}\leq t+2\eta)+\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{P}(t-2\eta\leq U_{0,L}\leq t+2\eta).

Since the spectral expansion holds in
L^{2}(P\times P)
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigg[\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\Big[(T_{n}^{\ast}-T_{n,L}^{\ast})^{2}\mid\mathbb{X}_{n}\Big]\bigg]

\displaystyle=\frac{2(n-1)}{n}\mathbb{E}\{(h-h_{L})(X_{1},X_{2})^{2}\}

\displaystyle=\frac{2(n-1)}{n}\sum_{\ell>L}\lambda_{\ell}^{2}\to 0,\;\text{as }L\to\infty.

and similarly,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\{(U_{0}-U_{0,L})^{2}\}

\displaystyle=2\sum_{\ell>L}\lambda_{\ell}^{2}\to 0,\;\text{as }L\to\infty.

Hence, by Markov’s inequality, the two approximation probabilities vanish after taking expectation and then letting
L\to\infty
. Also,
\beta_{L}\to\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{\ell}^{2}=\mathbb{E}\{h(X_{1},X_{2})^{2}\}
, and the anti-concentration bounds for
U_{0,L}
and
U_{0}
imply that the two smoothing terms are bounded by a constant times

\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{\eta}{\{\mathbb{E}(h(X_{1},X_{2})^{2})\}^{1/2}}}

in the limit. Letting first
L\to\infty
and then
\eta\downarrow 0
, we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(\widetilde{\Delta}_{n}^{\ast})

\displaystyle\lesssim\sqrt{\frac{\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon_{2}}{\{\mathbb{E}(h(X_{1},X_{2})^{2})\}^{1/2}}}+\frac{\mathbb{E}\{h(X_{1},X_{1})^{2}\}}{n\varepsilon_{1}^{2}}+\frac{\mathbb{E}\{h(X_{1},X_{1})^{2}\}}{n\varepsilon_{2}^{2}}.

Set
\varepsilon_{1}=\varepsilon_{2}=\varepsilon
. Balancing the two terms gives

\displaystyle\varepsilon

\displaystyle=\bigg[\frac{\mathbb{E}\{h(X_{1},X_{1})^{2}\}[\,\mathbb{E}\{h(X_{1},X_{2})^{2}\}]^{1/4}}{n}\bigg]^{2/5}.

Substitution yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(\widetilde{\Delta}_{n}^{\ast})

\displaystyle\lesssim\bigg[\frac{\mathbb{E}\{h(X_{1},X_{1})^{2}\}}{n\,\mathbb{E}\{h(X_{1},X_{2})^{2}\}}\bigg]^{1/5}.

Therefore, by Markov’s inequality,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\big(\widetilde{\Delta}_{n}^{\ast}\geq s\big)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C}{s}\bigg[\frac{\mathbb{E}\{h(X_{1},X_{1})^{2}\}}{n\,\mathbb{E}\{h(X_{1},X_{2})^{2}\}}\bigg]^{1/5}.

Finally, by the triangle inequality,

\displaystyle\Delta_{n,\mathrm{boot}}^{\ast}

\displaystyle\leq\widetilde{\Delta}_{n}^{\ast}+\Delta_{n}.

Taking expectations and applying Markov’s inequality gives

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\big(\Delta_{n,\mathrm{boot}}^{\ast}\geq s\big)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{s}\bigg\{\Delta_{n}+C\bigg[\frac{\mathbb{E}\{h(X_{1},X_{1})^{2}\}}{n\,\mathbb{E}\{h(X_{1},X_{2})^{2}\}}\bigg]^{1/5}\bigg\}.

This proves the desired result.
∎

The following lemma, adapted from
Neykov et al. (2021, Lemma A.1)
to accommodate a specified marginal distribution of
Z
, is the key technical ingredient in the proof of Theorem
1
.

Lemma 11

(Adaptation of
Neykov et al. (2021, Lemma A.1)
for Specified Marginals)
.

Let
M\in(0,\infty]
and let
(X,Y,Z)
take values in
[-M,M]^{d_{X}}\times[-M,M]^{d_{Y}}\times\{1,2\}
, with
\mathbb{P}(Z=1)=\lambda_{n}\in(0,1)
and
\mathbb{P}(Z=2)=1-\lambda_{n}
. Let
\{(X_{i},Y_{i},Z_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}\overset{\mathrm{i.i.d.}}{\sim}(X,Y,Z)
. Then, for any
\delta>0
, there exists a constant
C>0
such that for any
\varepsilon>0
and any Borel set
D\subseteq([-M,M]^{d_{X}+d_{Y}}\times\{1,2\})^{n}\times[0,1]
, one can construct i.i.d. random vectors
\{(\widetilde{X}_{i},\widetilde{Y}_{i},\widetilde{Z}_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
satisfying
\widetilde{Y}_{i}\mbox{${}\perp\mkern-11.0mu\perp{}$}\widetilde{Z}_{i}\mid\widetilde{X}_{i}
and

(i)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Big(\max_{i\in[n]}\|(\widetilde{X}_{i},\widetilde{Y}_{i})-(X_{i},Y_{i})\|_{\infty}<\varepsilon,\ \widetilde{Z}_{i}=Z_{i}\text{ for all }i\in[n]\Big)>1-\delta;

(ii)

for any
U\sim\operatorname{Unif}[0,1]
independent of
\{(\widetilde{X}_{i},\widetilde{Y}_{i},\widetilde{Z}_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Big(\big(\{(\widetilde{X}_{i},\widetilde{Y}_{i},\widetilde{Z}_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n},U\big)\in D\Big)\leq C\cdot\mu(D),

where
\mu
is the product of the counting measure on
\{1,2\}^{n}
and the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}^{(d_{X}+d_{Y})n}\times[0,1]
.

Proof.

The proof is a direct modification of Lemma A.1 in

Neykov et al. (2021)
. We spell out the modification because the
conditioning variable in the present problem is
X
, rather than
Z
.

We first reduce to the bounded-density case, exactly as in Step I of
Lemma A.1 in
Neykov et al. (2021)
. When
M=\infty
, we truncate the
distribution so that, with probability at least
1-\delta/2
, the
truncated variables coincide with the original variables. Hence it
suffices to consider the case
M<\infty
. As in Lemma A.1, we may also
replace the distribution by an arbitrarily close bounded-density
approximation. In the present setting, to guarantee membership in

\mathcal{P}_{M}^{ac}
, we additionally mix each conditional distribution
of
(X,Y)
given
Z=z
with a small uniform component on
[-M,M]^{2}
.
This modification can be made with probability at most
\delta/2
and
ensures that, for each
z\in\{1,2\}
, the conditional density of

(X,Y)
given
Z=z
is bounded above and bounded away from zero on

[-M,M]^{2}
. By standard coupling arguments, these preliminary approximations introduce an error of at most
\delta
to the final probability bounds, which can be safely absorbed or bounded.

We now describe the construction in the one-dimensional case (
d_{X}=d_{Y}=1
). The
higher-dimensional case follows by the same product-partition argument
as in Remark A.2 of
Neykov et al. (2021)
. Let

\{B_{1},\ldots,B_{m}\}
and
\{C_{1},\ldots,C_{m}\}
be equal-length partitions
of
[-M,M]
, where
B_{j}
partitions the support of
Y
and
C_{k}

partitions the support of
X
. Further divide each
C_{k}
into
m

subintervals

\displaystyle C_{1k},\ldots,C_{mk}.

The important point is that the fine subinterval
C_{jk}
is indexed by
the
Y
-bin
j
, but not by the group label
Z
.

Given a draw
(X,Y,Z)
with
X\in C_{k}
,
Y\in B_{j}
, and
Z=i
, define

\displaystyle\widetilde{Z}:=Z,

draw
\widetilde{X}
uniformly on
C_{jk}
, and draw
\widetilde{Y}

uniformly on
B_{j}
. Applying this construction independently to each
observation gives

\{(\widetilde{X}_{i},\widetilde{Y}_{i},\widetilde{Z}_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
.

Since
X
and
\widetilde{X}
both belong to
C_{k}
, and
Y
and

\widetilde{Y}
both belong to
B_{j}
, we have

\displaystyle\|(\widetilde{X},\widetilde{Y})-(X,Y)\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2M}{m}.

Also
\widetilde{Z}=Z
by construction. Hence, choosing
m
sufficiently
large so that
2M/m<\varepsilon
yields property (i), after
combining with the preliminary truncation and bounded-density
approximation.

Next, we verify conditional independence. Fix
x\in C_{jk}
. Then the
index
j
is determined by
x
. Therefore, for any Borel set

A\subseteq[-M,M]
and any
i\in\{1,2\}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\widetilde{Y}\in A\mid\widetilde{X}=x,\widetilde{Z}=i)

\displaystyle=\frac{|A\cap B_{j}|}{|B_{j}|}.

The right-hand side does not depend on
i
. Therefore

\displaystyle\widetilde{Y}\mbox{${}\perp\mkern-11.0mu\perp{}$}\widetilde{Z}\mid\widetilde{X}.

Thus the constructed law belongs to
\mathcal{P}_{0}
.

We now check the mutual absolute continuity condition. Since the
preliminary approximation gives a conditional density of
(X,Y)
given

Z=i
bounded away from zero, every cell
C_{k}\times B_{j}
has positive
probability under both labels
i=1,2
. Hence, for every
j,k
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(X\in C_{k},Y\in B_{j}\mid Z=i)>0,\qquad i=1,2.

Because the subinterval
C_{jk}
depends only on the
Y
-bin
j
and not
on the group label
Z=i
, the conditional distributions

\mathcal{L}(\widetilde{X}\mid\widetilde{Z}=1)
and

\mathcal{L}(\widetilde{X}\mid\widetilde{Z}=2)
put positive density on
exactly the same collection of intervals
\{C_{jk}:j,k\in[m]\}
.
Consequently,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\widetilde{X}\mid\widetilde{Z}=1)\ll\mathcal{L}(\widetilde{X}\mid\widetilde{Z}=2),\quad\mathcal{L}(\widetilde{X}\mid\widetilde{Z}=2)\ll\mathcal{L}(\widetilde{X}\mid\widetilde{Z}=1).

Therefore the constructed law belongs to
\mathcal{P}_{M}^{ac}
.

It remains to prove property (ii). This follows from the same
permutation-averaging and density-domination argument as in
Lemma A.1 of
Neykov et al. (2021)
. The only difference is that the
present group variable
Z
is binary with fixed marginal probabilities

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(Z=1)=\lambda_{n},\quad\mathbb{P}(Z=2)=1-\lambda_{n}.

This affects the cell-probability bound only through

\displaystyle\lambda_{n}+(1-\lambda_{n})

\displaystyle=1.

Indeed, for every pair of cells
C_{k}
and
B_{j}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(X\in C_{k},Y\in B_{j})

\displaystyle=\sum_{z\in\{1,2\}}\mathbb{P}(Z=z)\int_{C_{k}\times B_{j}}p_{X,Y\mid Z=z}(x,y)\,dx\,dy

\displaystyle\leq\lambda_{n}L|C_{k}||B_{j}|+(1-\lambda_{n})L|C_{k}||B_{j}|

\displaystyle=L|C_{k}||B_{j}|.

Thus the fixed marginal distribution of
Z
does not change the
domination argument. Applying Lemma A.1 of

Neykov et al. (2021)
with the above relabeling of variables gives
a constant
C<\infty
such that, for every Borel set
D
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\bigl(\{(\widetilde{X}_{i},\widetilde{Y}_{i},\widetilde{Z}_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n},U\bigr)\in D\right)

\displaystyle\leq C\mu(D),

where
U
is an auxiliary random variable and
\mu
is the dominating measure as defined in Lemma A.1 of
Neykov et al. (2021)
. This proves property (ii) and completes the proof.
∎

Appendix C
Extended Literature and Technical Details

C.1
Comprehensive Review of Related Literature

In the main text, we focused on recent developments directly addressing nonparametric conditional two-sample testing to maintain a concise narrative. In this subsection, we provide a broader overview of related statistical literature. Specifically, we discuss classical approaches targeting specific distributional aspects, enumerate various methodologies developed for conditional independence testing, and outline key advancements in direct density ratio estimation.

Testing Conditional Moments and Goodness-of-Fit.
While nonparametric comparisons of two conditional distributions have received limited attention until recently, related problems have been explored in the literature. These include testing for the equality of conditional moments
(
Hall and Hart, 1990
;
Kulasekera, 1995
;
Kulasekera and Wang, 1997
;
Fan and Lin, 1998
;
Neumeyer and Dette, 2003
;
Pardo-Fernández et al., 2015
)
and goodness-of-fit testing for pre-specified conditional distributions
(
Andrews, 1997
;
Zheng, 2000
;
Fan et al., 2006
)
.

Conditional Independence Testing (CIT).
The problem of testing for conditional independence has been extensively studied, resulting in a variety of methods. Beyond the methods mentioned in the main text, recent improvements to the Generalized Covariance Measure
(
Shah and Peters, 2020
)
include strategies such as weighting
(
Scheidegger et al., 2022
)
and alternative projection techniques
(
Chakraborty et al., 2024
)
. Other notable approaches include various kernel-based tests
(
Zhang et al., 2011
;
Doran et al., 2014
;
Pogodin et al., 2024
)
, binning-based tests
(
Kim et al., 2022b
;
Neykov et al., 2024
)
, regression-based tests
(
Dai et al., 2022
)
, and tests under the model-X framework
(
Candes et al., 2018
;
Berrett et al., 2020
;
Liu et al., 2022
;
Tansey et al., 2022
)
.

Density Ratio Estimation.
To address the instability of separately estimating individual densities to form a ratio,
Tsuboi et al. (2009)
developed methods that directly estimate the density ratio without involving density estimation.
Kanamori et al. (2010)
provided a comparative analysis of such methods, while
Kanamori et al. (2009)
reformulated the problem as a least-squares optimization admitting a closed-form solution. Furthermore,
Liu et al. (2017)
introduced trimmed estimation to improve robustness against extreme values. More recent developments in density ratio estimation include
Choi et al. (2021)
;
Rhodes et al. (2020)
;
Choi et al. (2022)
.

C.2
Importance-Weighted Test Statistics

The two examples below illustrate how the general principle of density ratio estimation in the main text yields basic conditional two-sample test statistics with Gaussian calibration.

Example 5

(Mean comparison)
.

Given a feature map
\psi:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}\to\mathbb{R}
, consider the test statistic

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n_{1}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{1}}\psi\bigl(V_{i}^{(1)}\bigr)-\frac{1}{n_{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{2}}r_{X}\bigl(X_{i}^{(2)}\bigr)\,\psi\bigl(V_{i}^{(2)}\bigr).

for the hypotheses in the main text. The expectation of this statistic is equal to zero under the null hypothesis. Moreover, since the test statistic is simply a linear combination of independent random variables, it can be calibrated using the Gaussian approximation.

Example 6

(Rank sum statistic)
.

Instead of comparing the mean, one can compare the stochastic order of two distributions using ranks.
Specifically, given a feature map
\psi:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}\to\mathbb{R}
, a rank sum statistic based on the transformed samples can be computed as

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n_{1}n_{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{1}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{2}}r_{X}\bigl(X_{j}^{(2)}\bigr)\,\mathds{1}\bigl\{\psi\bigl(V_{j}^{(2)}\bigr)<\psi\bigl(V_{i}^{(1)}\bigr)\bigr\}.

Under the null hypothesis, and assuming no ties among transformed samples, one can verify that the expectation is equal to 1/2. As in
Hu and Lei (2024)
, the test statistic can be shown to be asymptotically Gaussian under suitable regularity conditions. Therefore, the critical value can be determined based on this Gaussian approximation. The power, however, depends on
\psi
.
Hu and Lei (2024)
takes
\psi
as an estimate of
f_{Y\!\,|\,\!X}^{(1)}(\cdot\,|\,\cdot)/f_{Y\!\,|\,\!X}^{(2)}(\cdot\,|\,\cdot)
.

C.3
Convergence Rates of Density Ratios

Assumption
1
(b) and Assumption
2
(b) impose convergence-rate conditions on the density ratio estimator. In terms of mean squared error (MSE), the former requires the conditional MSE to decay at rate
o_{P}(m^{-1})
, while the latter requires the unconditional MSE to decay at rate
o((n-m)^{-1/2})
. The latter is the milder of the two and is attainable under nonparametric smoothness alone: if
r_{X}
belongs to a
\beta
-Hölder class on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the minimax optimal MSE rate is
O(n^{-2\beta/(2\beta+d)})

(
Nguyen et al., 2010
)
, which satisfies
o(n^{-1/2})
whenever
\beta>d/2
.

The former, matching the assumption in
Hu and Lei (2024)
, requires the stricter near-parametric rate
o_{P}(m^{-1})
, which under sample splitting with
m/n\to 0
is delivered by the following classes of estimators.

•

Parametric models.
When the density-ratio model is correctly specified (e.g., an exponential-family form for
r_{X}
itself, not necessarily for the underlying densities), least-squares estimators such as cLSIF and uLSIF achieve an MSE rate of
O(m^{-1})
under standard regularity conditions
(
Sugiyama et al., 2012b
, Theorems 13.6 and 13.7)
.

•

Kernel methods.
When
r_{X}
lies in a reproducing kernel Hilbert space (RKHS), kernel-based uLSIF attains an MSE rate of
m^{-1/(1+\gamma)}
, where
\gamma\in(0,1)
is the bracketing-entropy exponent of the RKHS ball, controlled by the eigenvalue decay of the kernel
(
Sugiyama et al., 2012b
, Theorem 14.16)
. As
\gamma\to 0
, which occurs for finite-dimensional RKHSs or sufficiently fast eigenvalue decay, the rate approaches the near-parametric
m^{-1}
.

•

Deep learning.
When
r_{X}
is
\beta
-Hölder on an approximately
d^{\ast}
-dimensional manifold embedded in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, deep ReLU network estimators attain an MSE rate of
m^{-2\beta/(2\beta+d^{\ast})}(\log m)^{c}

(
Zheng et al., 2022
, Theorem 3.1)
, mitigating the curse of the ambient dimension
d
by adapting to the intrinsic dimension
d^{\ast}
; this rate approaches
m^{-1}
up to polylogarithmic factors when
\beta\gg d^{\ast}
, and draws on the manifold-adaptive approximation machinery of
Jiao et al. (2023)
.

Assumption
1
(b) is verified in the three structural settings listed above, whereas Assumption
2
(b) is verified under nonparametric smoothness of
r_{X}
alone.

.

•

For the LLR method, we use
\boldsymbol{\beta}=(\beta_{0},\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{p})^{\top}

\displaystyle\eta(X_{i};\boldsymbol{\beta})=\mathbb{P}(\ell=1\,|\,X_{i})=\frac{1}{1+\exp\bigl(-\beta_{0}-\sum_{j=1}^{p}\beta_{j}X_{i}(j)\bigr)}.

r_{X}
and the conditional density ratio
r_{Y\,|\,X}
for the LLR and KLR estimators, under
500

On the low-dimensional diamonds dataset, both LLR and KLR attain low MSE for both density ratios, with the gap between the two methods narrowing as
n

On the high-dimensional superconductivity dataset, the two methods diverge substantially: KLR maintains low and stable MSE across all sample sizes, whereas LLR incurs orders-of-magnitude larger MSE for both density ratios, particularly at small

The density-ratio-based tests of Section
4
partition the evaluation sample into a density-ratio estimation set of size
n-2m
and a test-statistic set of size
2m
. The split proportion
(n-2m)/n
controls a trade-off: allocating a larger share to density-ratio estimation reduces the plug-in error required by Assumption
2
(b), while allocating more to the test statistic increases its finite-sample signal at a given
\widehat{r}_{X}
. We examine this trade-off in the three bounded scenarios of Section
5
, where
r_{X}
is bounded and the plug-in effect is isolated from density-ratio estimation error. For each scenario and each sample size
n\in\{200,500,1000,2000\}
, we run four density-ratio tests (MMD-
\ell
, MMD-
b
, MMD-
q
, and CP) at three proportions
(n-2m)/n\in\{0.2,0.5,0.8\}
, where
(n-2m)/n
is the fraction of the sample allocated to density-ratio estimation. The proportion
(n-2m)/n=0.8
is the
8{:}2
split used in the main experiments. Rejection rates are averaged over
500
repetitions at level
\alpha=0.05
, and the complete results are reported in Table
2
.

The sensitivity of the MMD statistics to
(n-2m)/n
increases with the block parameter
\gamma
. For MMD-
q
(
\gamma=1
), the null rejection rate is severely inflated under the small allocation
(n-2m)/n=0.2
and settles near the nominal level at
(n-2m)/n=0.8
. MMD-
b
(
\gamma=0.5
) shows the same pattern on a smaller scale, with a null rejection rate of
0.524
in S3B at
n=200
under
(n-2m)/n=0.2
that settles to
0.046
at
(n-2m)/n=0.8
. MMD-
\ell
(
\gamma=0
) is the least sensitive, with null rejection rates near
\alpha
at the larger allocations and a mild inflation only at
(n-2m)/n=0.2
under the smallest sample sizes. This gradient across
\gamma
is consistent with Assumption
2
(b): larger
\gamma
aggregates more kernel interactions and therefore requires tighter plug-in error, which is attained only at larger
(n-2m)/n
.

CP shows relatively insensitive result to
(n-2m)/n
. Its null rejection rate lies near nominal level across all scenarios, sample sizes, and proportions considered, and its power varies only marginally across proportions. CP is, however, substantially less powerful than MMD-
b
or MMD-
q
in S2B, where the alternative induces dispersion rather than a mean shift.

Under the alternative, the larger allocation
(n-2m)/n=0.8
reduces power relative to smaller allocations, most visibly for MMD-
b
and MMD-
q
at small sample sizes. The gap closes as
n
grows and is below
0.05
at
n=2{,}000
for MMD-
q
in S1B and S2B, consistent with the asymptotic negligibility of the plug-in error under Assumption
2
(b) when
(n-2m)/n
is held above the level needed to control its rate. These observations support
(n-2m)/n=0.8
as the default for the MMD family in the main experiments: it controls size under all three scenarios while retaining power at moderate and large sample sizes. For CP, which does not rely on a plug-in variance, the choice of
(n-2m)/n
does not affect size and the power differences are small.

D.4
Bandwidth Selection for MMD-based Tests

For MMD-based tests we use a Gaussian kernel with a fixed bandwidth
h=1
throughout the main experiments. A fixed bandwidth keeps the kernel deterministic and independent of the data, which simplifies implementation and is aligned with the analyses in
Gretton et al. (2012)
and
Schrab et al. (2023)
. To assess the sensitivity of test performance to this choice, we repeat the
{}^{\dagger}\mathrm{MMD}\text{-}\ell

h\in\{0.1,0.5,1,h_{\mathrm{med}},5,10\}
, where

denotes the median heuristic. We report the bounded settings so that the effect of
h
can be examined without confounding from density-ratio estimation error. Figure
6
reports the type I error rate and the power across
n\in\{200,500,1000,2000\}
.

(0,1)^{p}

\mathrm{Beta}(2,2)

D.9
A Refined Bound for
k^{\ast}

The
k^{\ast}
of Algorithm
1
is obtained by applying the multiplicative Chernoff bound (Lemma
1
) to the overflow events
\{\tilde{n}_{1}>n_{1}\}
and
\{\tilde{n}_{2}>n_{2}\}
, where
\tilde{n}_{1}\sim\mathrm{Binomial}(\tilde{n},n_{1}/n)
and
\tilde{n}=\lfloor kn\rfloor
. Because the Chernoff bound is conservative, the resulting
k^{\ast}
may be smaller than necessary, so the random subsample discards more observations than required. A tighter alternative is obtained by directly inverting the exact Binomial tail. Let

\displaystyle q_{j}(k)\coloneqq\mathbb{P}\bigl(\tilde{n}_{j}>n_{j}\bigr),\qquad\tilde{n}_{1}\sim\mathrm{Binomial}(\lfloor kn\rfloor,\,n_{1}/n),\qquad\tilde{n}_{2}=\lfloor kn\rfloor-\tilde{n}_{1},

and define

\displaystyle k^{\ast}_{\mathrm{ref}}\coloneqq\max\Bigl\{\,k\in(0,1):\;\max_{j\in\{1,2\}}q_{j}(k)\leq\varepsilon\,\Bigr\}.

A coupling argument shows that each
q_{j}(k)
is non-decreasing in
k
, so the defining set has the form
(0,k^{\ast}_{\mathrm{ref}}]
and
k^{\ast}_{\mathrm{ref}}
is computable by bisection over
(0,1)
. Because the Chernoff inequality upper bounds
q_{j}(k)
, the constraint
\max_{j}q_{j}(k)\leq\varepsilon
is weaker than the Chernoff-based constraint used in the main text, so
k^{\ast}_{\mathrm{ref}}\geq k^{\ast}
by construction. Thus
k^{\ast}_{\mathrm{ref}}
retains at least as many observations while meeting the same
\varepsilon
-tail guarantee. Table
5
compares the two values for balanced designs with
\varepsilon=1/\log(n)
. At
n=2{,}000
,
k^{\ast}_{\mathrm{ref}}
retains
\tilde{n}=3{,}930
observations out of
2n=4{,}000
, against
3{,}785
for the Chernoff-based
k^{\ast}
.

The performance of each testing method depends on the data structure and the choice of internal estimator, and our experiments confirm that no single method dominates uniformly across data-generating mechanisms or estimator choices. We therefore do not prescribe a method for each data type. Instead, this subsection reports the empirical trade-offs among computational cost, power, and type I error control observed in our real-data experiments. Empirical result is summarized in Figure
10
and Table

, with all runtimes reported as end-to-end wall-clock times averaged over

Framework Selection.

The CIT-based and DRT-based frameworks rest on different identifiability conditions. The CIT-based framework, obtained by applying
Algorithm

1
to a conditional independence test, does not require boundedness of the marginal density ratio
r_{X}
and is broadly robust across our synthetic scenarios under both the bounded and unbounded variants. The DRT-based framework relies on the importance-weighted statistics of
Section

4
, which inherit the power properties of their unconditional counterparts when
r_{X}
is bounded and accurately estimable. Its performance degrades when
r_{X}
is unbounded or the estimator is misspecified, as in
Section

5.3
and
Section

5.4
of the main text. The conditional kernel methods CMMD and CGED bypass both
r_{X}
estimation and
Algorithm

1
. They attain competitive power in our experiments but incur computational costs at
n=2{,}000
exceeding
300
seconds on both real datasets, an order of magnitude beyond the slowest CIT variant in our experiments.

Choices within the CIT Framework.

Within the CIT family, our experiments reveal substantial heterogeneity across both methods and regression backbones. GCM combined with random forests attains the highest empirical power at every sample size on both real datasets at runtimes of
4.686
and
23.760
seconds for
n=2{,}000
, respectively. RCIT attains the lowest runtimes at
n=2{,}000
,
0.132
and
0.204
seconds on the two datasets. The random-feature kernel approximation underlying RCIT also induces mild type I error inflation at
n=200
in our experiments. PCM shares the regression-based construction of GCM but attains markedly lower empirical power at comparable runtime, illustrating that the within-framework choice of test matters even when the regression backbone is held fixed. WGSC paired with gradient boosting controls type I error tightly but yields near-zero empirical power across both real datasets, suggesting that its weighting may be poorly suited to the alternatives we consider. The choice of regression backbone influences both power and runtime in the synthetic experiments of
Section

5.3
, where linear models are fast but underpower nonlinear scenarios while flexible estimators recover power at moderate computational cost.

Choices within the DRT Framework.

Within the DRT family, both the test statistic and the density ratio estimator interact with the alternative and the dimension of

in our experiments. The classifier-based test
{}^{\dagger}\widehat{\mathrm{Acc}}
of

†

(LLR) requiring only

. In the high-dimensional superconductivity setting, however, LLR alone leaves type I error inflated, and a richer estimator such as kernel logistic regression (KLR) is needed to restore size control. The cost of KLR grows rapidly with

,
†

Computational Scaling.

Three computational patterns emerge from Figure
10
and Table
7
. First, DRT methods with LLR and CIT methods with random forests scale at most polynomially in
n
with small leading constants in our experiments, and
†
MMD-
\ell
(LLR) consistently attains sub-second runtimes up to
n=2{,}000
on both datasets. Second, KLR introduce a super-linear cost. Across the four DRT tests, KLR runtime at
n=2{,}000
exceeds the corresponding LLR runtime by factors of roughly
13
to
700
, with
†
MMD-
b
and
†
MMD-

reaching
40
seconds and
†

7.369
seconds. Third, the conditional kernel methods CMMD and CGED, while bypassing
r_{X}
estimation, dominate every other method in runtime and exceed
300
seconds at
n=2{,}000
on both real datasets. In the low-dimensional setting,
†
MMD-
q
(LLR) and RCIT occupy the low-cost, high-power region of the trade-off in Figure

, whereas WGSC and the conditional kernel methods occupy the high-cost end. In the high-dimensional setting where size control is the binding concern,
†
MMD-
\ell
or

. We caution that these observations are specific to the data configurations and estimator choices considered, and that practitioners should weigh the cost-power-validity trade-off in light of their own data structure and computational budget.
