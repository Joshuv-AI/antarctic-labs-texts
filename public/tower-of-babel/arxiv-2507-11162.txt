Equality is Far Weaker than Constant-Cost Communication
Mika Göös, Nathaniel Harms, Artur Riazanov

Abstract
We exhibit an n n -bit communication problem with a constant-cost randomized protocol but which requires n Ω ⁡ ( 1 ) n^{\Omega(1)} deterministic (or even non-deterministic) queries to an Equality oracle. Therefore, even constant-cost randomized protocols cannot be efficiently “derandomized” using Equality oracles. This improves on several recent results and answers a question from the survey of Hatami and Hatami ( SIGACT News 2024 ). It also gives a significantly simpler and quantitatively superior proof of the main result of Fang, Göös, Harms, and Hatami ( STOC 2025 ), that constant-cost communication does not reduce to the k k -Hamming Distance hierarchy.

Introduction

In this paper we prove strong limits on the power of “simple
hashing” for the purpose of communication. In communication complexity, simple
hashing, i. e. the
Equality
communication problem, is the most dramatic
example of the power of randomness. Alice and Bob each have binary
strings
x,y\in\{0,1\}^{n}
and their goal is to decide whether
x=y
. With
public randomness, they can generate a random 2-bit hash
\bm{h}(z)
for every

z
and check if
\bm{h}(x)=\bm{h}(y)
. This succeeds with probability at least

3/4
and requires only 2 bits of communication regardless of the size of the
input
n

[
15
,
18
]
.

Randomized communication can often be “derandomized” by allowing Alice and Bob
access to an oracle which (deterministically) computes
Equality
. Write

\mathsf{D}^{\textsc{Eq}}(F)
for the minimum cost of a deterministic protocol computing
F
with
access to this oracle, and
\mathsf{R}(F)
for the public-coin randomized communication cost (with error probability
1/3
). Here are three examples covering a range of complexities:

Example 1

(Greater Than)
.

Alice and Bob are
given
n
-bit integers
x,y
and their goal is to decide whether
x>y
. They
can perform binary search to find the highest-order bit where

and
y

differ, by querying the

oracle (check if the first half of
their bits are equal, etc.), so
\mathsf{D}^{\textsc{Eq}}(\textsc{GT}_{n})=O(\log n)
, where
\textsc{GT}_{n}
denotes
the
Greater-Than
problem on
n
bits. Randomized communication
satisfies
\mathsf{R}(F)=O(\mathsf{D}^{\textsc{Eq}}(F))

[
16
,
11
]
, which in this case
gives the optimal randomized protocol:
\mathsf{R}(\textsc{GT}_{n})=\Theta(\log n)

[

,

Example 2

Alice and Bob are given vertices

to decide if they are adjacent. A planar graph is the edge union of 3 forests, so

Example 3

(1-Hamming Distance)

Alice and Bob are given
x,y\in\{0,1\}^{n}

\mathsf{D}^{\textsc{Eq}}(\textsc{HD}_{1}^{n})=O(\log n)

The Problem

The problem is a special case of those studied recently by Sherstov &
Storozhenko
[
20
]
. Alice and Bob are given matrices
A,B\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n\times n}
and their goal is to decide if the
\mathbb{F}_{2}
-rank of
A+B
is at most
1; i. e.
\textsc{RankOne}_{n}\colon\{0,1\}^{n\times n}\times\{0,1\}^{n\times n}\to\{0,1\}
is
defined by

\textsc{RankOne}_{n}(A,B)\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\begin{cases}1&\text{ if }A\oplus B\text{ has rank $\leq 1$}\\
0&\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

Note that a matrix
C\in\{0,1\}^{n\times n}
has rank
\leq 1
if and only if its
1-entries form a combinatorial rectangle, i. e. there are sets
X,Y\subseteq[n]
such that
C(x,y)=1
iff
(x,y)\in X\times Y
.
This problem is an XOR problem, i. e.
\textsc{RankOne}_{n}(A,B)=f(A\oplus B)
for the
boolean function
f\colon\{0,1\}^{n\times n}\to\{0,1\}
defined as
f(M)=1
if and only
if
M
has rank at most
1
.

Sherstov & Storozhenko
[
20
]
prove tight bounds for the randomized
communication complexity of computing rank
r
over any finite field. For the
sake of completeness, we include a simple proof that
\mathsf{R}(\textsc{RankOne}_{n})=O(1)
. More
specifically, we prove that the randomized parity decision tree depth of
f
is

O(1)
(which immediately implies the same for randomized
communication).

A function
g\colon\{0,1\}^{n}\to\{0,1\}
is computed by a depth-
d
parity
decision tree if, for any
x\in\{0,1\}^{n}
,
g(x)
can be computed with at most
d

adaptive queries of the form
\oplus_{i\in S}x_{i}
for chosen
S\subseteq[n]
. A function
g
is computed by a
randomized
parity decision tree if
there exists a distribution
\mu
over parity decision trees such that for every
input
x\in\{0,1\}^{n}
,
\Pr_{\bm{T}\sim\mu}[\bm{T}(x)=g(x)]\geq 2/3
.

Theorem 3
.

Let
f\colon\{0,1\}^{n\times n}\to\{0,1\}
be as above:
f(M)=1
iff
\mathrm{rk}_{\mathbb{F}_{2}}(M)\leq 1
. Then it is computed by a randomized parity decision
tree of constant depth (i. e. independent of
n
).

Proof.

Given input
M
, the tree chooses sets
\bm{A}_{1},\bm{A}_{2},\bm{B}_{1},\bm{B}_{2}\subseteq[n]

uniformly at random. For each
\alpha,\beta\in[2]
, the tree queries

\bm{C}_{\alpha,\beta}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\bigoplus_{(i,j)\in\bm{A}_{\alpha}\times\bm{B}_{\beta}}M_{ij}
, to form the
2\times 2
matrix
\bm{C}
. The tree
outputs
1
iff
\mathrm{rk}(\bm{C})\leq 1
.

The transformation from
M
to
\bm{C}
can be seen as a two-step process:
first, we generate a
n\times 2
matrix
\bm{C}^{\prime}
where column
i\in[2]

is the sum of columns
j\in\bm{B}_{i}
of
M
. Then we generate the
2\times 2
matrix
\bm{C}
where row
j
is the sum of rows
i\in\bm{A}_{j}

of
\bm{C}^{\prime}
. Neither step may increase the rank of the matrix, so if

\mathrm{rk}(M)\leq 1
, then
\Pr[\mathrm{rk}(\bm{C})\leq 1]=1
.

Now suppose
\mathrm{rk}(M)\geq 2
. Each column of
\bm{C}^{\prime}
is chosen uniformly
from the column space of
M
. The size of the column space is at least
4
,
so the probability that both columns of
\bm{C}^{\prime}
are nonzero and distinct
is at least
\tfrac{3}{4}\cdot\tfrac{1}{2}=\tfrac{3}{8}
. Conditional on
this event, by the same argument, the probability that both rows of
\bm{C}

are nonzero and distinct is at least
\tfrac{3}{8}
. Therefore, the tree
will output 0 with probability at least
9/64
. We can boost this to
2/3

by repetition.
∎

To clarify notation, observe that the inputs to this function are naturally expressed with
n^{2}

variables, whereas
Theorem

1
is stated for a function on
n

variables. So to prove
Theorem

1
we must show

\mathsf{D}^{\textsc{Eq}}(\textsc{RankOne}_{n})=\Theta(n).

(1)

The upper bound is simple:

Proposition 4
.

\mathsf{D}^{\textsc{Eq}}(\textsc{RankOne}_{n})=O(n)
.

Proof.

Suppose Alice receives a matrix
A
with rows
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\in\{0,1\}^{n}
, and Bob receives a matrix
B
with rows
b_{1},\dots,b_{n}\in\{0,1\}^{n}
. Then
\mathrm{rk}(A\oplus B)\leq 1
if and only if each pair
i,j\in[n]

with
a_{i}\neq b_{i}
and
a_{j}\neq b_{j}
satisfy
a_{i}\oplus b_{i}=a_{j}\oplus b_{j}
. Using
n
queries
\textsc{Eq}(a_{1},b_{1}),\textsc{Eq}(a_{2},b_{2}),\dots,\textsc{Eq}(a_{n},b_{n})
, Alice and Bob can find
i
such that
a_{i}\neq b_{i}
if such an
i

exists (otherwise
\mathrm{rk}(A\oplus B)=0
). Then for every
j\in[n]
such
that
a_{j}\neq b_{j}
they check that
a_{j}\oplus b_{j}=a_{i}\oplus b_{i}
by
making the query
\textsc{Eq}(a_{j}\oplus a_{i},b_{j}\oplus b_{i})
. If any

Equality
-query comes out negative, Alice and Bob output
0
,
otherwise they output
1
.
∎

Analytic Proof

We analyze the
\gamma_{2}
-norm of
\textsc{RankOne}_{n}
. The
\gamma_{2}
-norm of a matrix
M\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}

is defined as

\gamma_{2}(M)\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\min_{M=UV}\|U\|_{\text{row}}\|V\|_{\text{col}},

where the minimum is over matrices
U,V\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
(in any dimension
d
)
satisfying
M=UV
,
\|U\|_{\text{row}}
is the maximum
\ell_{2}
-norm of any row
of
U
, and
\|V\|_{\text{col}}
is the maximum
\ell_{2}
-norm of any column
of
V
. The
\gamma_{2}
-norm is related to the
Equality
oracle cost by

\forall M\in\{0,1\}^{N\times N}\;\colon\qquad\frac{1}{2}\log\gamma_{2}(M)\leq\mathsf{D}^{\textsc{Eq}}(M)\qquad\text{(\cite[cite]{[\@@bibref{}{HHH23}{}{}]})}.

(2)

To give a lower bound on
\gamma_{2}
, we use the Hölder’s inequality framework
introduced in
[
3
]
. Write
\|M\|_{F}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=(\sum_{i,j\in[N]}M_{i,j}^{2})^{1/2}

for the Frobenius norm of a matrix
M\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
. Then:

Lemma 5

(Corollary of Hölder’s inequality,
[
3
]
)
.

For any matrix
M\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
,

\gamma_{2}(M)\geq\frac{1}{N}\cdot\frac{\|M\|^{3}_{F}}{\sqrt{\mathrm{tr}((M^{T}M)^{2}})}.

In the next theorem, note that the number of bits in the input of
\textsc{RankOne}_{n}
is

n^{2}
, not
n
; by renaming the number of bits and applying
Equation

2
,
we get the bound in
Theorem

1
.

Theorem 6
.

\gamma_{2}(\textsc{RankOne}_{n})=2^{\Theta(n)}
. As a consequence,
\mathsf{D}^{\textsc{Eq}}(\textsc{RankOne}_{n})=\Omega(n)
.

Proof.

The upper bound follows from
Proposition

4
and
Equation

2
. For the lower bound, we shall apply
Lemma

5
to the matrix
\textsc{RankOne}_{n}\in\{0,1\}^{N\times N}
where
N=2^{n^{2}}
.
For convenience we write
M\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\textsc{RankOne}_{n}
.
Since this matrix is boolean, the Frobenius norm is
\|M\|_{F}=(\sum_{i,j\in[N]}M^{2}_{i,j})^{1/2}=(\sum_{i,j\in[N]}M_{i,j})^{1/2}
, so
it suffices to count the number of 1-valued entries. Rows of
M
are
identified with matrices
A\in\{0,1\}^{n\times n}
. There are
2^{2n}

rectangles
R\subseteq[n]\times[n]
and they are in 1-to-1 correspondence with rank-1 matrices
Z\in\{0,1\}^{n\times n}
; here and in what follows, let us abuse language and consider the all-0 matrix (corresponding to the empty rectangle) to also be “rank-1”. For fixed row
A
, each rank-1 matrix
Z
has a unique
B\in\{0,1\}^{n\times n}
such that
A\oplus B=Z
. Therefore the number of 1-valued entries in each row
A
is
2^{2n}
. So

\|M\|_{F}=\sqrt{N\cdot 2^{2n}}=2^{n}\cdot\sqrt{N}.

(3)

Now we bound the denominator in
Lemma

5
,

\mathrm{tr}((M^{T}M)^{2})=\sum_{x,y,z,w\in[N]}M_{x,z}M_{x,w}M_{y,z}M_{y,w}.

Rows and columns
x,y,z,w\in[N]
are identified with matrices
X,Y,Z,W\in\{0,1\}^{n\times n}
. The product
M_{x,z}M_{x,t}M_{y,z}M_{y,w}

equals
1
iff
R_{1}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=X\oplus Z
,
R_{2}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=X\oplus W
,

R_{3}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=Y\oplus Z
, and
R_{4}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=Y\oplus W
are all rank-
1
matrices in
\{0,1\}^{n\times n}
. Then the quadruple
(X,Y,Z,W)
is uniquely determined by
(X,R_{1},\dots,R_{4})
which in turn is determined by
(X,R_{1},R_{2},R_{3})
since

R_{4}=R_{1}\oplus R_{2}\oplus R_{3}
. So:

\displaystyle\sum_{x,y,z,w}M_{x,z}M_{x,t}M_{y,z}M_{y,w}

\displaystyle=N\cdot\big|\{(R_{1},R_{2},R_{3})\in(\{0,1\}^{n\times n})^{3}\mid\mathrm{rk}(R_{i})\leq 1;\;\mathrm{rk}(R_{1}\oplus R_{2}\oplus R_{3})\leq 1\}\big|.

Now it remains to bound the number of triples
(R_{1},R_{2},R_{3})
of rank-
1
matrices that
sum to a rank-
1
matrix. Recall that rank-
1
matrices over
\mathbb{F}_{2}
are
precisely the matrices whose
1
-entries form a combinatorial rectangle, let

A_{i}\times B_{i}\subseteq[n]^{2}
be this rectangle for
R_{i}
for
i\in[3]
.

We say that two sets
A,B
are in
general position
if they are not
disjoint and neither set is a subset of another. In other words
A\smallsetminus B
,
A\cap B
, and
B\smallsetminus A
are all non-empty. We first show that
there are at most
6\cdot 3^{n}\cdot 2^{4n}
triples
(R_{1},R_{2},R_{3})

where the sides of
R_{1},R_{2}
are
not
in general position. Then we
show that, if the sides of the first two rectangles
R_{1},R_{2}

are
in
general position, there is only a constant number of choices for
R_{3}
, meaning that
there are at most
9\cdot 2^{4n}
of these triples. Therefore

\mathrm{tr}((M^{T}M)^{2})=\sum_{x,y,z,w}M_{x,z}M_{x,t}M_{y,z}M_{y,w}\leq N\left(6\cdot 3^{n}\cdot 2^{4n}+9\cdot 2^{4n}\right)\leq 15\cdot N\cdot 3^{n}\cdot 2^{4n}.

Then
Lemma

5
implies

\gamma_{2}(\textsc{RankOne}_{n})\geq\frac{1}{N}\cdot\frac{N^{3/2}2^{3n}}{\sqrt{15}\cdot\sqrt{N}\cdot(3\cdot 2^{4})^{n/2}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{15}}\cdot\left(\frac{8}{\sqrt{3}\cdot 4}\right)^{n}=2^{\Omega(n)},

which concludes the proof, once we establish the claimed bounds on the number of triples.

Structured pairs.

We count the number of triples
(R_{1},R_{2},R_{3})
where
R_{1}=A_{1}\times B_{1}
and
R_{2}=A_{2}\times B_{2}
have either
A_{1},A_{2}
or
B_{1},B_{2}
not in general position. First we count
the number of choices for
A_{1},A_{2}
where
A_{1}\subseteq A_{2}
. This is at most
3^{n}
because each
i\in[n]
can be included in
A_{2}\smallsetminus A_{1}
,
A_{1}
, or neither. Therefore the number of triples
(R_{1},R_{2},R_{3})
where
A_{1}\subseteq A_{2}
is at most
3^{n}\cdot(2^{n})^{4}
, accounting for the choices of
A_{3},B_{1},B_{2},B_{3}
. Similar arguments hold for the other cases of non-general position of
A_{1},A_{2}
or
B_{1},B_{2}
. There are 6 cases in total, so the number of triples
(R_{1},R_{2},R_{3})
is at most
6\cdot 3^{n}\cdot 2^{4n}
.

General position.

We count the number of triples
(R_{1},R_{2},R_{3})
which sum to a rank-1 matrix, and where
A_{1},A_{2}
and
B_{1},B_{2}

are both in general position. We claim that there is only a constant number
options for
R_{3}=A_{3}\times B_{3}
such that
\mathrm{rk}(R_{1}\oplus R_{2}\oplus R_{3})=1
.
Let us first rule out the case where
A_{3}\supseteq A_{1}\cup A_{2}
or
B_{3}\supseteq B_{1}\cup B_{2}
. Let us take representatives
i,j,k
from
A_{1}\smallsetminus A_{2}
,
A_{1}\cap A_{2}
,
A_{2}\smallsetminus A_{1}
respectively, and

i^{\prime},j^{\prime},k^{\prime}
from
B_{1}\smallsetminus B_{2}
,
B_{1}\cap B_{2}
,
B_{2}\smallsetminus B_{1}

respectively. The submatrix of
R_{1}\oplus R_{2}
on rows
\{i,j,k\}
and
columns
\{i^{\prime},j^{\prime},k^{\prime}\}
is

P\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=(R_{1}\oplus R_{2})|_{\{i,j,k\}\times\{i^{\prime},j^{\prime},k^{\prime}\}}=J_{3}\oplus I_{3},

where
J_{3}
is
3\times 3
all-
1

I_{3}
is the
3\times 3

identity matrix. Then if
A_{3}\supseteq\{i,j,k\}
then
P\oplus R_{3}|_{\{i,j,k\}\times\{i^{\prime},j^{\prime},k^{\prime}\}}
has either two distinct columns from

J_{3}\oplus I_{3}
or two distinct columns from
I_{3}
, in both cases
\mathrm{rk}(R_{1}\oplus R_{2}\oplus R_{3})\geq 2
. The same argument shows that
B_{3}\not\supseteq\{i^{\prime},j^{\prime},k^{\prime}\}
.

Then there are a row and a column that are nonzero in
R_{1}\oplus R_{2}
and
zero in
R_{3}
. Suppose that their values in
R_{1}\oplus R_{2}
are
a\in\{0,1\}^{n}
and

, respectively. Since
\mathrm{rk}(R_{1}\oplus R_{2}\oplus R_{3})=1
, any two nonzero rows of
R_{1}\oplus R_{2}\oplus R_{3}
must be equal,
and any two nonzero columns must also be equal. Since
a,b
are nonzero,
this requires
R_{1}\oplus R_{2}\oplus R_{3}=a\cdot b^{T}
. For fixed
R_{1},R_{2}
, there are only three
possible values of
a
and

, so there are at most
9
options for the

.
Therefore there are at most
9\cdot(4^{n})^{2}
triples
(R_{1},R_{2},R_{3})
in this case.
∎

We prove a lower bound on the non-deterministic

\textsc{RankOne}_{n}

with
m+d=t

there exist matrices

satisfying:

\mathsf{D}^{\textsc{Eq}}(M_{i})\leq d

2.

If

\exists i\in[2^{m}]

If
M(x,y)=0
then
\forall i\in[2^{m}]

.

By definition,
\mathsf{ND}^{\textsc{Eq}}(M)\leq\mathsf{D}^{\textsc{Eq}}(M)
. To prove a lower bound on
\mathsf{ND}^{\textsc{Eq}}
,
we require the

.

Definition 7

(Blocky Cover)
.

A matrix

is called

Blocky covers are related to non-deterministic

. For all

\mathsf{ND}^{\textsc{Eq}}(A)\leq\log\mathrm{bc}(A)\leq O(\mathsf{ND}^{\textsc{Eq}}(A)\cdot\log\log N).

(4)

In
Appendix

Lemma 8

For any

\mathsf{ND}^{\textsc{Eq}}(A)\leq\log\mathrm{bc}(A)\leq\mathsf{ND}^{\textsc{Eq}}(A)+O(\log\log N).

We may now improve

\mathsf{ND}^{\textsc{Eq}}(\textsc{RankOne}_{n})=\Omega(n)

Theorem 9

\mathrm{bc}(\textsc{RankOne}_{n})=2^{\Omega(n)}

\mathsf{ND}^{\textsc{Eq}}(\textsc{RankOne}_{n})=\Omega(n)

Proof.

In this proof, write
M^{-1}(1)

\bm{Z}

\mathrm{H}(\bm{Z})\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=-\sum_{z\in\mathcal{Z}}\Pr[\bm{Z}=z]\log\Pr[\bm{Z}=z]

Recall from

Claim 10

There exists a 1-chromatic rectangle

with

.

Proof of claim.

For the sake of contradiction, assume each 1-chromatic rectangle

\displaystyle 2^{n^{2}}\cdot 2^{2n}

\displaystyle=|\textsc{RankOne}_{n}|\leq\sum_{i=1}^{K}|B_{i}|=\sum_{i=1}^{K}\sum_{j=1}^{b_{i}}|U_{i,j}|\cdot|V_{i,j}|

\displaystyle<\sum_{i=1}^{K}\left(\sum_{j\in[b_{i}],R_{i,j}\text{ short}}|V_{i,j}|\cdot 2^{2n}/(2K)+\sum_{j\in[b_{i}],R_{i,j}\text{ narrow}}|U_{i,j}|\cdot 2^{2n}/(2K)\right)

\displaystyle\leq\tfrac{1}{2}2^{2n}\max_{i\in[K]}\left(\sum_{j\in[b_{i}]}|V_{i,j}|+\sum_{j\in[b_{i}]}|U_{i,j}|\right)

\displaystyle\leq\tfrac{1}{2}2^{2n}\max_{i\in[K]}\left(2^{n^{2}}+2^{n^{2}}\right)=2^{2n}\cdot 2^{n^{2}}.\qed

5.1
Constant-Cost Communication vs.
k
-Hamming Distance

We will prove
Corollary

2
, which shows that constant-cost randomized
communication does not reduce to
k
-Hamming Distance
.

Remark 12
.

We must clarify a subtlety
in the definition of
\mathsf{D}^{\mathcal{Q}}(P)
, the cost of a deterministic communication protocol with access
to an oracle for problem
\mathcal{Q}
.
For the purpose of studying the class
\mathsf{BPP}_{0}
, the natural definition of

\mathsf{D}^{\mathcal{Q}}
is as follows (see e. g.
[
12
,
8
,
7
]
).
\mathcal{Q}
is a family
of boolean matrices, i. e. a communication problem, containing matrices
Q\in\{0,1\}^{N\times N}
for a variety of input sizes
N
.
\mathsf{D}^{\mathcal{Q}}(P)
is the minimum
cost of a deterministic protocol computing
P\in\{0,1\}^{N\times N}
, where on
input
x,y
, the two parties Alice and Bob in each round may construct inputs

a(x)
and
b(y)
of
arbitrary size
, and query the oracle on
Q(a(x),b(y))
, where
Q\in\mathcal{Q}
is any instance of problem
\mathcal{Q}
. The difference
between this definition and the standard definition of oracle protocols is that
the query inputs
a(x),b(y)
do not have bounded size. This is natural because
for
\mathcal{Q}\in\mathsf{BPP}_{0}
, the randomized communication cost is independent of input
size.

Recent work
[
8
]
showed that there is no complete problem for
\mathsf{BPP}_{0}
:
there is no problem
\mathcal{Q}\in\mathsf{BPP}_{0}
such that
\mathsf{D}^{\mathcal{Q}}(P)=O(1)
for all
problems
P\in\mathsf{BPP}_{0}
. The reason is that, for every
\mathcal{Q}\in\mathsf{BPP}_{0}
, there
exists a constant
k
such that
\mathsf{D}^{\mathcal{Q}}(\textsc{HD}_{k}^{n})=\omega(1)
. This raised the
question of whether the infinite hierarchy of
k
-Hamming Distance

problems
\textsc{HD}_{1},\textsc{HD}_{2},\dotsm
is complete, meaning that for every
problem
\mathcal{P}\in\mathsf{BPP}_{0}
there exists a constant
k
such that

\mathsf{D}^{\textsc{HD}_{k}}(P_{N})=O(1)
. The question was answered in
[
7
]
, whose
main result was:

Theorem 13

(
[
7
]
)
.

There exists a communication problem
F_{n}\colon\{0,1\}^{n}\times\{0,1\}^{n}\to\{0,1\}
such that

\mathsf{R}(F_{n})=O(1)
, yet for every constant
k
,
\mathsf{D}^{\textsc{HD}_{k}}(F_{n})=\omega(1)
.

The
\omega(1)
function is not given explicitly and is the result of a lengthy
Ramsey-theoretic proof. We give a simpler proof (using a different function
F
)
with a quantitative bound.

Proof of
Corollary

2
.

We prove that, for every constant
k
,
\mathsf{D}^{\textsc{HD}_{k}}(\textsc{RankOne}_{n})=\Omega(n/\log n)
.
Given a deterministic protocol computing
\textsc{RankOne}_{n}
using
\textsc{HD}_{k}
queries, we may replace each
query
Q
with a protocol computing
Q
using
Equality
queries. Then

\mathsf{D}^{\textsc{Eq}}(\textsc{RankOne}_{n})\leq\mathsf{D}^{\textsc{HD}_{k}}(\textsc{RankOne}_{n})\cdot\max_{Q}\mathsf{D}^{\textsc{Eq}}(Q),

where the maximum is over all
2^{n^{2}}\times 2^{n^{2}}
matrices
Q
which
represent oracle queries to
\textsc{HD}_{k}
; formally, these matrices
Q
are the
ones which are obtained by taking any submatrix of
\textsc{HD}_{k}^{n^{\prime}}
for arbitrarily
large
n^{\prime}
(due to
Remark

12
), and then duplicating rows or columns
to ensure the matrix is the same size as
\textsc{RankOne}_{n}
. We now require a bound on

\mathsf{D}^{\textsc{Eq}}(Q)
for arbitrary
2^{n^{2}}\times 2^{n^{2}}
submatrices of
\textsc{HD}_{k}^{n^{\prime}}

where
n^{\prime}
can be arbitrarily large. This was proved in
[
8
]
:

Theorem 14

(
[
8
, Proposition 4.1]
)
.

For any
k
and any
n,N\in\mathbb{N}
satisfying
N\leq 2^{n}
, let
M
be an
N\times N
submatrix
of
\textsc{HD}_{k}^{n}\in\{0,1\}^{2^{n}\times 2^{n}}
. Then
\mathsf{D}^{\textsc{Eq}}(M)\leq O(k\log\log N)
.

So, by
Theorem

14
,

\mathsf{D}^{\textsc{Eq}}(\textsc{RankOne}_{n})\leq O\left(\mathsf{D}^{\textsc{HD}_{k}}(\textsc{RankOne}_{n})\cdot k\log\log(2^{n^{2}})\right).

By
Theorem

1
, we conclude that
\mathsf{D}^{\textsc{HD}_{k}}(\textsc{RankOne}_{n})=\Omega\left(\frac{n}{k\log n}\right)
.
∎

5.2
Parity Decision Trees and Exact vs. Approximate Spectral Norm

We state some consequences for parity decision trees and exact vs. approximate spectral norms.
The Fourier coefficients of a function

f\colon\{0,1\}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
are defined on subsets
S\subseteq[n]
by

\hat{f}(S)\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{x}\sim\{0,1\}^{n}}[f(\bm{x})(-1)^{\sum_{i\in S}\bm{x}_{i}}].

The Fourier norm is
\|\hat{f}\|_{1}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\sum_{S\subseteq[n]}|\hat{f}(S)|
. For an XOR problem
F(x,y)=f(x\oplus y)
, it is equivalent to the
\gamma_{2}
-norm:

\gamma_{2}(F)=\|\hat{f}\|_{1}

(
[
9
, Corollary 3.9]
)

For
\epsilon\in(0,1)
, the approximate spectral norm is defined as

\|\widehat{f}\|_{1,\epsilon}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\inf_{g}\|\widehat{g}\|_{1}

where the infimum is over functions
g\colon\{0,1\}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}

which satisfy
\|f-g\|_{\infty}<\epsilon
.

We can now state the corollaries. The first is an exponential improvement over the separation given by
[
5
, Lemma 3]
.

Corollary 15
.

There exists a function
f\colon\{0,1\}^{n\times n}\to\{0,1\}
with approximate spectral norm

\|\widehat{f}\|_{1,1/3}=O(1)
but exact spectral norm
\|\widehat{f}\|_{1}=2^{\Omega(n)}
.

We remark that for some constants
\epsilon
and
\ell
the bound
\|\widehat{f}\|_{1,\epsilon}\leq\ell

[
19
, Theorem 2.5]
(known as quantitative Cohen idempotence theorem) implies that
\|\widehat{f}\|_{1}=O(1)
, so this corollary (as well as
[
5
, Lemma 3]
) gives a barrier for improving the dependency between
\ell
and
\epsilon
in this result.

The second corollary answers
[
3
, Question 8]
.

Corollary 16
.

There exists a function
f\colon\{0,1\}^{n\times n}\to\{0,1\}
with randomized parity decision tree
size
O(1)
and deterministic parity decision tree size
2^{\Omega(n)}
.

The separating function
f\colon\{0,1\}^{n\times n}\to\{0,1\}
in both cases is defined by
f(A)=1
iff
A\in\{0,1\}^{n\times n}
has
\mathrm{rk}(A)\leq 1
, so
\textsc{RankOne}_{n}(x,y)=f(x\oplus y)
.

Proof of
Corollary

15
.

By
[
9
, Proposition 4.3]
we get that
\|\widehat{f}\|_{1,1/3}=O(1)
since
1/3
-error randomized communication cost of
RankOne
is
O(1)
by
Theorem

3
. On the other hand, by
[
9
, Corollary 3.9]
combined with
Theorem

6
we get
\|\widehat{f}\|_{1}=2^{\Omega(n)}
as required.
∎

Proof of
Corollary

16
.

The upper bound on randomized parity decision tree size of
f

was proved in
Theorem

3
.
If
f
has a parity decision tree (PDT) of size
s
, then
f(x)=\sum_{i\in[s]}{A_{i}}(x)
where

A_{i}
are characteristic functions of the affine spaces corresponding to

1
-labeled leaves of the PDT. It is well known that
\|\widehat{A_{i}}\|_{1}\leq 1
. Then
\|\hat{f}\|_{1}\leq\sum_{i\in[s]}\|\widehat{{A_{i}}}\|_{1}\leq s

2^{\Omega(n)}\leq\gamma_{2}(\textsc{RankOne}_{n})=\|\hat{f}\|_{1}\leq s.\qed

)

\mathsf{ND}^{\textsc{Eq}}(\textsc{IIP}_{3}^{n})=\Theta(n)

Appendix A
Appendix: Proof of
Lemma

8

Let us now prove

To prove this, we will use the fractional blocky cover instead of the blocky cover:

Definition 20

(Fractional Blocky Cover)
.

For any
A\in\{0,1\}^{N\times N}

of

,
\lambda_{1},\dotsc,\lambda_{m}\geq 0

\displaystyle A(x,y)=0

\displaystyle\implies\sum_{i=1}^{m}\lambda_{i}B_{i}(x,y)=0

\displaystyle A(x,y)=1

\displaystyle\implies\sum_{i=1}^{m}\lambda_{i}B_{i}(x,y)\geq 1.

We write
\mathrm{fbc}(A)
for the minimum value of
\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}
over all fractional blocky covers of

We require the following properties of the fractional blocky cover:

Proposition 21

(Properties of FBC)
.

Let
P,Q\in\{0,1\}^{N\times N}
be any boolean matrices and let
J
be the all-1s matrix. Then

1.

For any blocky matrix
B
,
\mathrm{fbc}(J-B)\leq 4
.

2.

\mathrm{fbc}(P\wedge Q)\leq\mathrm{fbc}(P)\cdot\mathrm{fbc}(Q)
.

3.

\mathrm{fbc}(P\vee Q)\leq\mathrm{fbc}(P)+\mathrm{fbc}(Q)
.

4.

\mathrm{bc}(P)\leq O(\mathrm{fbc}(P)\cdot\log N)
.

We prove these properties below. First let us complete the proof of
Lemma

8
.

Proof of
Lemma

8
.

Let
A\in\{0,1\}^{N\times N}
. Due to property (4) of
Proposition

21
, it suffices to
show
\log\mathrm{fbc}(A)=O(\mathsf{ND}^{\textsc{Eq}}(A))
. Let
d,m\in\mathbb{N}
be such that
d+m=\mathsf{ND}^{\textsc{Eq}}(A)
and there exist

2^{m}
deterministic
Equality
-oracle protocols
T_{i}
of depth at most
d
such that

\forall x,y\in[N]\;\colon\qquad A(x,y)=\bigvee_{i=1}^{2^{m}}T_{i}(x,y).

By property (3) of
Proposition

21
,
\mathrm{fbc}(A)\leq\sum_{i=1}^{2^{m}}\mathrm{fbc}(M_{i})
where
M_{i}\in\{0,1\}^{N\times N}
is the matrix computed by protocol
T_{i}
. It now suffices to show that

\mathrm{fbc}(M_{i})\leq 5^{d}
for every
i
, so that
\log\mathrm{fbc}(A)\leq\log(2^{m}5^{d})<3\cdot\mathsf{ND}^{\textsc{Eq}}(A)
.

Fix any
T=T_{i}
, which is a decision tree with each inner node
v
associated with a blocky matrix

B_{v}
. Without loss of generality we may assume the depth is exactly
d
and that the tree is full.
For each node
v
, let
M_{v}\in\{0,1\}^{N\times N}
be the matrix computed by the subtree rooted at

v
, and write
\mathrm{depth}(v)
for the depth of this subtree. We prove by induction that

\mathrm{fbc}(M_{v})\leq 5^{\mathrm{depth}(v)}
. If
\mathrm{depth}(v)=1
, then
M_{v}
is either a blocky
matrix
B
or its complement
J-B
, so by property (1) of
Proposition

21
,
\mathrm{fbc}(M_{v})\leq 4
. For depth
d^{\prime}>1
, let
\ell,r
be the left and right child of
v
. Then

M_{v}=(B_{v}\wedge M_{\ell})\vee((J-B_{v})\wedge M_{r}),

so by properties (1), (2), and (3) of
Proposition

21
,

\displaystyle\mathrm{fbc}(M_{v})

\displaystyle\leq\mathrm{fbc}(B_{v})\mathrm{fbc}(M_{\ell})+\mathrm{fbc}(J-B_{v})\mathrm{fbc}(M_{r})\leq\mathrm{fbc}(M_{\ell})+4\mathrm{fbc}(M_{r})

\displaystyle\leq 5^{d^{\prime}-1}+4\cdot 5^{d^{\prime}-1}=5^{d^{\prime}},

with the last inequality by induction. This completes the proof of the lemma.
∎

Finally, we establish the properties of fractional blocky covers that we have used above.

Proof of
Proposition

21
.

Claim 22
.

Let
J
be the all-1s matrix and let
B
be any blocky matrix. Then
\mathrm{fbc}(J-B)\leq 4
.

Proof.

It suffices to prove the claim for the identity matrix
B=I_{N\times N}
, since duplicating rows
and columns does not change the value of
\mathrm{fbc}
. The claim follows from the fact that the

Equality
communication problem has a constant-cost one-sided error randomized protocol.
That is, define the following probability distribution over rectangles
R\subseteq[N]\times[N]
. We choose
\bm{R}
by including each
x\in[N]
in a set
\bm{X}\subseteq[N]
independently
with probability
1/2
, and then take
\bm{R}=\bm{X}\times([N]\smallsetminus\bm{X})
. If

x=y
, then
\Pr[(x,y)\in\bm{R}]=0
. If
x\neq y
then
\Pr[(x,y)\in\bm{R}]=1/4
.
For each rectangle
R
, set
\lambda_{R}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=4\cdot\Pr[\bm{R}=R]
. It follows that

\mathrm{fbc}(J-I_{N\times N})\leq\sum_{R}\lambda_{R}=4.\qed

Claim 23
.

Let
P,Q\in\{0,1\}^{N\times N}
. Then
\mathrm{fbc}(P\wedge Q)\leq\mathrm{fbc}(P)\cdot\mathrm{fbc}(Q)
.

Proof of claim.

Let
\alpha_{1},\dotsc,\alpha_{p}
and
B_{1},\dotsc,B_{p}
be a fractional blocky cover of
P
, and let

\beta_{1},\dotsc,\beta_{q}
and
B^{\prime}_{1},\dotsc,B^{\prime}_{q}
be a fractional blocky cover of
Q
. For any

x,y\in[N]
, consider

\sum_{i,j}\alpha_{i}\beta_{j}(B_{i}\wedge B^{\prime}_{j})(x,y)=\left(\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}B_{i}(x,y)\right)\cdot\left(\sum_{j}\beta_{j}B^{\prime}_{j}(x,y)\right)

(5)

Note that
B_{i}\wedge B^{\prime}_{j}
is itself a blocky matrix.
If
P\wedge Q(x,y)=0
then
P(x,y)=0
or
Q(x,y)=0
so
Equation

5
is 0. If
P\wedge Q(x,y)=1
then
P(x,y)=Q(x,y)=1
so
Equation

5
is at least 1. So

\mathrm{fbc}(P\wedge Q)\leq\sum_{i,j}\alpha_{i}\beta_{j}=\mathrm{fbc}(P)\cdot\mathrm{fbc}(Q).\qed

Claim 24
.

Let
P,Q\in\{0,1\}^{N\times N}
. Then
\mathrm{fbc}(P\vee Q)\leq\mathrm{fbc}(P)+\mathrm{fbc}(Q)
.

Proof of claim.

Let
\alpha_{1},\dotsc,\alpha_{p}
and
B_{1},\dotsc,B_{p}
be a fractional blocky cover of
P
, and let

\beta_{1},\dotsc,\beta_{q}
and
B^{\prime}_{1},\dotsc,B^{\prime}_{q}
be a fractional blocky cover of
Q
. It is
straightforward to check that the union of these fractional blocky covers is itself a fractional
blocky cover for
P\vee Q
, so that
\mathrm{fbc}(P\vee Q)\leq\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}+\sum_{j}\beta_{j}
.
∎

Claim 25
.

Let
P\in\{0,1\}^{N\times N}
. Then
\mathrm{bc}(P)\leq O(\mathrm{fbc}(P)\cdot\log N)
.

Proof of claim.

This is by standard randomized rounding. Let
\lambda_{1},\dotsc,\lambda_{m}\geq 0
and
B_{1},\dotsc,B_{m}
be a fractional blocky cover of
P
with
\sum_{\ell}\lambda_{\ell}=\mathrm{fbc}(P)
. Let
\bm{M}_{1},\dotsc,\bm{M}_{t}
be random blocky matrices chosen independently from the distribution
\Pr[\bm{M}_{i}=B_{j}]=\frac{\lambda_{j}}{\mathrm{fbc}(P)}
. Fix any
x,y\in[N]
and note that

P(x,y)=0\implies\Pr[\exists i\;\colon\;\bm{M}_{i}(x,y)=1]=0

since
B_{\ell}(x,y)=0
for all
\ell\in[m]
. Now suppose
P(x,y)=1
. Then

\sum_{\ell}\lambda_{\ell}B_{\ell}(x,y)\geq 1
, so, by independence of each
\bm{M}_{i}
,

\Pr[\forall i\;\colon\;\bm{M}_{i}(x,y)=0]=\Pr[\bm{M}_{1}(x,y)=0]^{t}=\left(1-\frac{1}{\mathrm{fbc}(P)}\right)^{t}\leq e^{-\frac{t}{\mathrm{fbc}(P)}}.

Setting
t=O(\mathrm{fbc}(P)\log N)
and using the union bound over at most
N^{2}
pairs
x,y\in[N]

with
P(x,y)=1
, we conclude that there exists a choice of
t=O(\mathrm{fbc}(P)\log N)
blocky matrices
which cover the 1-valued entries of
P
.
∎

This completes the proof of all of the properties from
Proposition

21
.
∎
