StrucSum: Graph-Structured Reasoning for Long Document Extractive Summarization with LLMs
Haohan Yuan, Sukhwa Hong, Haopeng Zhang

Abstract
Large language models (LLMs) have shown strong performance in zero-shot summarization, but often struggle to model document structure and identify salient information in long texts. In this work, we introduce StrucSum, a training-free prompting framework that enhances LLM reasoning through sentence-level graph structures. StrucSum injects structural signals into prompts via three targeted strategies: Neighbor-Aware Prompting (NAP) for local context, Centrality-Aware Prompting (CAP) for importance estimation, and Centrality-Guided Masking (CGM) for efficient input reduction. Experiments on ArXiv, PubMed, and Multi-News demonstrate that StrucSum consistently improves both summary quality and factual consistency over unsupervised baselines and vanilla prompting. Notably, on ArXiv, it boosts FactCC and SummaC by 19.2 and 9.7 points, indicating stronger alignment between summaries and source content. These findings suggest that structure-aware prompting is a simple yet effective approach for zero-shot extractive summarization with LLMs, without any training or task-specific tuning. Our source code is released at https://github.com/HaohanYuan01/StrucSum .

Introduction

Extractive summarization aims to identify the most salient sentences from a document to produce concise and factually reliable summaries. This approach is especially valuable in high-stakes domains such as scientific publishing, biomedical research, legal analysis, and policy documentation, where accuracy and traceability are critical
Zhang et al. 2023a
;
Ruan et al. 2022
. As long-form documents grow in length and complexity, effective summarization requires not only identifying salient content at the sentence level but also reasoning over discourse structure and capturing long-range dependencies
Zhong and Litman 2025
;
Koh et al. 2022
, which remain challenging for many existing methods.

Comparing StrucSum’s graph-structured LLM prompting with standard flat-input prompting for long-document extractive summarization.

To capture document-level structure, prior work has represented documents as sentence-level graphs based on semantic, positional, or rhetorical relationships
Mihalcea and Tarau 2004
;
Jin et al. 2020
;
Wang et al. 2020
. These graph-based approaches offer interpretable mechanisms for sentence selection via centrality and coverage
Erkan and Radev 2004
;
Cui et al. 2020
. However, most existing approaches require supervised training with transformer encoders
Li et al. 2020
;
Zhang et al. 2022
;
Zhao et al. 2024
, which limits their scalability and adaptability across domains.

In the era of advanced large language models (LLMs), systems such as GPT-4 and LLaMA3 have shown strong zero-shot performance on general summarization tasks
Yuan and Zhang 2024
. Their ability to generalize across domains without task-specific training makes them appealing alternatives to fully supervised models
Kojima et al. 2023
. However, LLMs are trained on unstructured text and often struggle to recognize salient content or maintain factual consistency in long or multi-section documents due to their lack of structural awareness
Zhang et al. 2024a
. Although prompting strategies like in-context learning and chain-of-thought have enhanced LLM reasoning in other domains
Wei et al. 2023
, prompting for structural reasoning in long-document extractive summarization remains unexplored.

To address this challenge, we propose StrucSum, a graph-structured prompting framework that enhances LLMs for long-document extractive summarization in a zero-shot setting. StrucSum builds a Text-Attributed Graph (TAG) for each document, where sentences are nodes connected by semantic or positional relationships. Instead of tuning model parameters, we inject structural signals into LLM inputs through three complementary strategies: (1)
Neighbor-Aware Prompting
(NAP) enriches each sentence with its immediate neighbors to support local reasoning; (2)
Centrality-Aware Prompting
(CAP) appends sentence importance scores derived from graph centrality to guide global selection; and (3)
Centrality-Guided Masking
(CGM) filters out low-salience content to reduce input length. These strategies jointly expose different aspects of document structure, driving LLMs to generate more faithful and context-aware summaries without additional training. We focus on extractive summarization as it provides a clearer testbed for structural reasoning, where identifying salient sentences depends more explicitly on discourse-level relationships
Zhang et al. 2023a
. While our method is designed for the extractive setting, it is model-agnostic and can be extended to abstractive summarization, which we leave for future work.

We evaluate StrucSum on two long-document summarization benchmarks (ArXiv and PubMed) and one multi-document benchmark (Multi-News). Experimental results show that our framework consistently outperforms both unsupervised methods and LLMs with standard prompting across ROUGE, BERTScore, and factual consistency metrics. Notably, on the ArXiv dataset, our NAP strategy improves FactCC scores by 19.2 points and SummaC scores by 9.7 points, showing significant gains in factual reliability. We summarize our main contributions as follows:

•

We propose
StrucSum
, a structure-aware prompting framework for extractive summarization that operates in a fully zero-shot, training-free setting with LLMs.

•

We introduce three complementary prompting strategies: NAP, CAP, and CGM, that inject local and global structural signals into LLM inputs to support more accurate and faithful sentence selection.

•

We conduct extensive experiments across three long-form summarization benchmarks. Results show that StrucSum consistently improves both summary quality and factual consistency across multiple metrics. Our findings suggest the importance of incorporating structural signals to enhance LLM reasoning in zero-shot extractive summarization.

Related Work

2.1
Graph-based Summarization

Figure 2:
Overview of the StrucSum framework: A document is encoded into a Text-Attributed Graph, from which structural signals are extracted via three prompting strategies to guide LLMs in zero-shot extractive summarization.

Graph-based models have been widely adopted in extractive summarization for their ability to capture cross-sentence relationships within a document. Their sparse structure brings scalability and flexibility, making them particularly suitable for long-form inputs. Early approaches constructed sentence-level graphs using lexical or semantic similarity, enabling algorithms such as TextRank to identify salient content
Mihalcea and Tarau 2004
. Building on this foundation, later work has incorporated Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) to better model both local and global dependencies
Chhabra et al. 2024
. MuchSUM, for example, introduces a multi-channel graph convolutional network that encodes semantic, centrality, and positional features through a bipartite word-sentence graph, outperforming BERT-based baselines
Mao et al. 2022
. Heterogeneous GNNs have further been explored to jointly model word- and sentence-level interactions
Wang et al. 2020
, while citation graphs have been used to capture cross-document relatedness in scientific summarization
Chen et al. 2022
.

2.2
Summarization with LLMs

Large Language Models (LLMs) have significantly advanced the field of text summarization, demonstrating strong performance in both zero-shot and few-shot settings. Models such as ChatGPT, and LLaMA2 have achieved state-of-the-art results on various benchmarks, including CNN/DailyMail, XSum, and Multi-News
Zhang et al. 2024a
. These models excel in generating coherent and contextually relevant summaries without the need for extensive task-specific fine-tuning. However, challenges remain. LLMs often struggle with factual consistency, especially in abstractive summarization tasks, leading to potential hallucinations or misrepresentations of source content
Pu et al. 2023
. Additionally, their performance can vary across domains, with specialized areas such as medical or legal texts posing unique difficulties
Yuan and Zhang 2024
. In response, evaluation methods leveraging LLMs themselves
Song et al. 2024
, such as G-Eval, have been proposed to provide more nuanced assessments of summary quality
Liu et al. 2023
.
Although recent work has begun exploring extractive summarization with LLMs in a training-free setting
Zhang et al. 2023a
, research in this area remains limited, particularly for long-form documents. Our work fills this gap by introducing structural signals to enhance the reasoning capabilities of LLMs, enabling more effective extractive summarization without any additional training or supervision.

3
Method

3.1
Problem Definition

In this work, we model zero-shot extractive summarization as a sentence selection task guided by structural reasoning. Each document is represented as a sequence of
N
sentences,
\mathbf{S}=(s_{1},s_{2},\dots,s_{N})
, and encoded as a Text-Attributed Graph (TAG):
\mathcal{G}=(\mathbf{V},\mathcal{E})
, where each node
v_{i}\in\mathbf{V}
corresponds to a sentence
s_{i}
, and each edge
(v_{i},v_{j})\in\mathcal{E}
captures a semantic or positional relationship between sentence pairs. The objective is to select a subset of
k
sentences from
\mathbf{S}
to form the final summary
\hat{y}=\{s_{i_{1}},s_{i_{2}},\dots,s_{i_{k}}\}
.

Our goal is to construct a structured prompt
P
that integrates both sentence content and graph structure, and use it to query a large language model (LLM) in a training-free setting. We formalize this in two steps:

P=GP(\mathbf{S},\mathcal{G}),

(1)

\hat{\mathcal{S}}=f(P).\quad\text{where }\hat{\mathcal{S}}\subseteq\mathbf{S}

(2)

Here,
GP(\cdot)
is a graph-aware prompting function that generates the structured input
P
from the sentence set
\mathbf{S}
and its associated graph
\mathcal{G}=(\mathbf{V},\mathcal{E})
. The function
f(\cdot)
denotes the LLM, which returns a subset of salient sentences
\hat{\mathcal{S}}
as the extractive summary.

3.2
Graph Construction

To capture inter-sentential relationships, we construct a sparse semantic graph for each document. Specifically, each sentence
s_{i}
is encoded using a pre-trained Sentence-BERT model to obtain a dense vector. We compute cosine similarity between all sentence pairs
(s_{i},s_{j})
and add an undirected edge
\mathbf{e}_{ij}
to the edge set
\mathcal{E}
if
\mathrm{sim}(s_{i},s_{j})>\theta
, where
\theta
is a tunable similarity threshold. This construction yields a sparse graph structure, which preserves salient relational information while maintaining scalability for long documents.

Finally, this results in a TAG,
\mathcal{G}=(\mathbf{V},\mathcal{E})
, which encodes both semantic proximity and discourse-level relationships among sentences. These TAGs provide structured signals, such as neighborhood context and sentence importance, integrated into the prompt design, allowing LLMs to reason over document structure during extraction.

3.3
Vanilla Prompting

We begin with a vanilla prompting strategy, which defines the basic mechanism for querying an LLM to perform extractive summarization. This prompt provides the LLM with an explicit task instruction and presents the document as a flat sequence of sentences, without incorporating any structural information. As such, it serves both as a fundamental way to elicit extractive behavior from the LLM and as a baseline for evaluating structure-aware extensions.

Task Description.

The LLM is instructed to identify and select the
k
most important sentences from the document. The value of
k
is a tunable hyperparameter that controls the target summary length.

Prompt Format.

The vanilla prompt is formatted as:

You are an expert in extractive summarization.

Your task is to
select the most important sentences
from a document.

On average, select around 4 key sentences.

Sentence 1: "<Text_S1>"

Sentence 2: "<Text_S2>"

…

Sentence N: "<Text_SN>"

This setup defines the summarization task purely through textual input, representing the document as an ordered list
\mathbf{S}=(s_{1},s_{2},\dots,s_{N})
. This prompt template is reused and expanded upon in all structure-aware strategies. We use this formulation as the foundation for exploring how additional structural signals—introduced in later sections—can guide LLMs toward more accurate and faithful extractive summaries.

3.4
Structure-Aware Prompting

To better guide LLMs in reasoning over document structure, we propose three structure-aware prompting strategies that incorporate both local and global graph signals into the input. These strategies leverage sentence connectivity and importance derived from the TAG, enabling LLMs to make more informed extraction decisions in a fully training-free manner.

Each strategy reflects a different perspective on structural reasoning:
(1)
Neighbor-Aware Prompting
integrates local sentence neighborhoods to enhance short-range discourse awareness;
(2)
Centrality-Aware Prompting
emphasizes globally important sentences using graph centrality scores; and
(3)
Centrality-Guided Masking
filters the input to include only structurally salient content, improving both reasoning focus and input efficiency.

The details of each prompting strategy are as follows:

Neighbor-Aware Prompting (NAP).

NAP augments each sentence by explicitly listing its 1-hop neighbors in the TAG, allowing the LLM to consider short-range semantic dependencies and local discourse flow during selection.

The prompt augments each sentence as follows:

Sentence 1: "<Text_S1>"

- 1-hop Neighbors: Sentence 2, 6, 7, 9, 13, 14, …

Sentence 2: "<Text_S2>"

- 1-hop Neighbors: Sentence 1, 4, 6, 9, 10, 15, …

By explicitly listing 1-hop neighbors, this format provides the LLM with local contextual cues that highlight semantically related sentences, helping it better assess each sentence’s role within its immediate discourse neighborhood.

Centrality-Aware Prompting (CAP).

CAP emphasizes the global structural importance of each sentence by attaching a normalized degree centrality score, computed from the TAG. This score reflects how well-connected a sentence is within the document graph, highlighting key discourse hubs.

The centrality score for sentence
s_{i}
is computed as:

\operatorname{centrality}(s_{i})=\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
j\neq i\end{subarray}}^{n}e_{ij},

(3)

where
e_{ij}=1
if an edge exists between
s_{i}
and
s_{j}
, and
0
otherwise.

The prompt format includes each sentence along with its centrality value:

Sentence 1 (Centrality: 0.67): "<Text_S1>"

Sentence 2 (Centrality: 0.42): "<Text_S2>"

Including centrality values exposes global connectivity patterns, helping the LLM distinguish structurally prominent sentences from peripheral ones.

Centrality-Guided Masking (CGM).

CGM introduces structural information by selectively including only a subset of high-centrality sentences in the prompt. Specifically, it ranks sentences by their normalized centrality scores and incrementally selects top-ranked ones until a cumulative centrality threshold
\rho
(e.g., 85%) is met. The remaining sentences are masked.

Evaluation

We evaluate summary quality using a combination of standard, faithfulness-oriented, and holistic metrics. For content overlap, we report ROUGE-2 and ROUGE-L
Lin 2004
, which capture n-gram overlap and longest common subsequence between system and reference summaries. For semantic similarity, we report BERTScore F1
Zhang et al. 2020
, which measures contextual embedding similarity between summaries and references. To assess factual consistency, we use FactCC
Kryściński et al. 2019
, a classifier-based metric that detects factual errors in generated summaries, and SummaC
Laban et al. 2022
, which estimates sentence-level consistency using natural language inference models. Finally, we report G-Eval
Liu et al. 2023
, a GPT-based evaluation framework that provides human-aligned scores across multiple dimensions, including coherence, consistency, fluency, and relevance. G-Eval offers a more comprehensive view of overall summary quality beyond traditional overlap-based metrics.

Across all datasets, structure-aware prompting consistently improves over vanilla LLM prompting, highlighting the value of incorporating document structure.

Among unsupervised baselines, Lead performs best on PubMed and remains competitive on ArXiv and Multi-News. Graph-based methods like LexRank, TextRank, and PacSum offer varying degrees of improvement, but all are outperformed by LLM-based methods—particularly on scientific texts where structural cues are more critical.

5.3
Structure-Only Prompt Format Analysis

To evaluate whether LLMs can utilize structural information without access to sentence content, we design a graph-only setting where all textual input is removed and only graph-derived structure is provided to GPT4o-mini. We compare three prompt formats that encode the same underlying Text-Attributed Graph (TAG) differently: textual neighbor lists (TNL), which list each sentence’s neighbors as in the NAP strategy but omit sentence text; numeric adjacency matrices (NAM), dense matrices with cosine similarity scores between sentence pairs; and binary adjacency matrices (BAM), which indicate edge presence based on a similarity threshold
\theta
.

As shown in Table
6
, TNL consistently outperforms both NAM and BAM across all metrics. However, its performance remains lower than systems combining structure-aware prompts with full sentence content (for example, NAP achieves 14.59 ROUGE-2 compared to 11.87 for TNL on ArXiv). Overall, these results suggest that in LLM-based summarization, structural cues, although helpful, cannot replace textual information to achieve optimal results.

6
Conclusion

This paper presents StrucSum, a prompting-based framework for extractive summarization that augments LLM prompting with structural signals obtained from sentence-level graphs. By using three complementary strategies: NAP, CAP and CGM, StrucSum enables LLMs to reason more effectively over document structure in a zero-shot setting. Experiments across multiple benchmarks show that structure-aware prompting substantially improves both summary quality and factual consistency, with NAP achieving the most consistent gains. Overall, this study shows that structure-aware prompting is an effective and scalable approach for enhancing LLM-based summarization. We hope that the findings from this work can inspire further exploration of structure-guided reasoning in summarization and related tasks.

Limitations

Instead of conducting experiments on the entire test set, we evaluate a representative subset of 200 test examples per dataset due to budget limits. Previous research efforts have similarly tested LLMs on small subsets
Goyal et al. 2023
;
Zhang et al. 2024b
. As large-scale evaluations remain resource-intensive under current LLM APIs, we leave full-dataset testing to future work.

Our evaluation primarily relies on automatic metrics to assess summary quality and factual consistency. While we include human evaluation for support, its scale is limited. A broader human study, especially involving domain experts, may provide deeper insights.

StrucSum does not involve any fine-tuning, as it is designed for the zero-shot setting. This training-free formulation promotes generality and ease of deployment, though it may limit task-specific performance gains. Exploring fine-tuned variants remains an open direction.

We also restrict our comparisons to vanilla prompting and several unsupervised baselines, without evaluating against supervised or fine-tuned models. This design isolates the effects of structure-aware prompting in a consistent zero-shot context, though broader comparisons could offer further perspective.

Finally, our experiments are conducted in English and on a selected set of LLMs (e.g., GPT-4o, LLaMA3-70B). Evaluating StrucSum across languages, domains, and additional model families is left for future exploration.
