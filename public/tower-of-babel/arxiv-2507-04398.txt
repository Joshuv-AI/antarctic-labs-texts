Do Students Write Better Post-AI Support? Effects of Generative AI Literacy and Chatbot Interaction Strategies on Multimodal Academic Writing Publication type: Original Article Journal: Journal Volume: 00
Yueqiao Jin, Kaixun Yang, Roberto Martinez-Maldonado, Dragan Gašević, Lixiang Yan

Abstract
Academic writing increasingly involves multimodal tasks requiring students to integrate visual information and textual arguments. While generative AI (GenAI) tools, like ChatGPT, offer new pathways for supporting academic writing, little is known about how students’ GenAI literacy influences their independent multimodal writing skills or how chatbot interaction strategies (passive reactive vs. proactive scaffolding) impact learning. This study examined 79 higher education students’ multimodal academic writing performance using a comparative research design. Students completed writing tasks integrating visual data under two chatbot-assisted conditions (passive vs. proactive) and subsequently without AI assistance. Their writing performance was rigorously evaluated across five dimensions, including insightfulness, visual data integration, organisation, linguistic quality, and critical thinking. Ordinal logistic regression and correlation analyses revealed that higher levels of GenAI literacy significantly predicted stronger independent multimodal writing performance immediately after AI assistance removal, particularly for students using passive chatbots requiring active prompting. These results highlight the critical role of GenAI literacy and specific chatbot interaction strategies in shaping students’ capacities for independent multimodal academic writing. Our findings emphasise the need for purposeful integration of GenAI literacy training into curricula and balancing external scaffolding support with autonomous learning opportunities. This research offers valuable recommendations for educators leveraging AI-enhanced pedagogies to optimise student writing outcomes and technological engagement strategies.

Practitioner Notes

What is currently known about this topic

•

Generative AI (GenAI) tools are increasingly used by learners in their academic writing tasks, offering new opportunities and challenges.

•

Effective use of advanced AI tools depends partly on students’ technological literacy and their ability to strategically engage with AI-generated content.

•

The academic writing process is becoming increasingly multimodal, requiring integration of textual and visual data, yet limited research exists on how GenAI tools influence learner performance in such multimodal contexts.

What this paper adds

•

Provides empirical evidence that learners’ GenAI literacy significantly predicts their independent multimodal academic writing performance, especially after AI supports are removed.

•

Demonstrates that passive chatbot interactions benefit students with higher GenAI literacy more strongly than proactive chatbot interactions, highlighting interaction style as a crucial moderating variable.

•

Confirms GenAI literacy as a distinct predictor of writing performance, separate from domain-specific knowledge, indicating its importance for successfully integrating visual information and critical-thinking components into AI-assisted writing tasks.

Implications for practitioners

•

Educators should explicitly integrate GenAI literacy training within academic curricula, focusing on skills like formulating effective prompts, critically evaluating AI-generated output, and independently integrating this assistance into academic work.

•

Practitioners need to carefully select chatbot scaffolding strategies appropriate for students’ GenAI literacy, balancing proactive guidance with opportunities for learners’ active inquiry, metacognitive awareness, and autonomous problem-solving.

•

Instructional designers should strategically balance GenAI-driven scaffolding with independent practice opportunities for students to independently apply and develop their GenAI literacy skills.

Background

3.1
Academic Writing

Academic writing is a core skill in higher education, requiring students to effectively synthesise ideas, engage critically with evidence, and communicate coherently. At the heart of successful academic writing lies the ability to provide insightful interpretations by deeply analysing the subject matter, demonstrating originality, and making sophisticated connections among multiple sources
[

,

[

,
46

[

Assessing students’ academic writing holistically thus demands evaluation across these multiple dimensions.
Insightfulness

[

,

Visual data integration
is judged by examining the accuracy and relevance of references to graphical material, as well as how effectively visual information enhances overall arguments
[

.
Organisation and coherence

[
47

.
Linguistic quality

[

. Finally, students’

[

3.3
Generative AI Literacy

Given the increasing use of GenAI in educational contexts, particularly in academic writing, it has become essential to investigate and consider students’ GenAI literacy when exploring AI-assisted writing practices. While general AI literacy has been defined and explored extensively in previous frameworks
[

Researchers have highlighted that GenAI literacy involves not only understanding how generative tools function, but also mastering practical skills to effectively prompt these tools, critically evaluate AI-generated content, and navigate ethical and contextual complexities specific to GenAI-generated outputs
[
6

Despite recognition of the importance of GenAI literacy for meaningful interaction with tools like ChatGPT and Gemini, there remains limited empirical research that explicitly examines how students’ varying levels of GenAI literacy impact their academic writing performances when GenAI assistance is provided or withdrawn
[

,

3.4
Domain Knowledge

Domain knowledge is an essential consideration when investigating AI-assisted multimodal academic writing, particularly when visual information and interpretation are integral to the task. Students’ capacity to successfully integrate visual data into meaningful written discussions depends heavily on their familiarity with relevant subject areas
[
1
,
55
]
. This underscores the necessity of evaluating and accounting for the domain knowledge possessed by individual learners, especially when examining the relationship between their GenAI literacy and writing performance
[
2
]
. Without considering domain proficiency, researchers may inadvertently attribute students’ challenges in producing coherent multimodal arguments or effectively utilising GenAI supports to deficiencies in GenAI literacy alone, rather than identifying underlying gaps in subject-specific knowledge
[
1
]
. Accordingly, research into GenAI literacy for multimodal academic writing contexts should adopt a nuanced approach, explicitly acknowledging the role of domain-specific expertise alongside GenAI literacy.

3.5
Research Gaps

The current study aimed to address the aforementioned gaps by examining how students’ GenAI literacy affects their multimodal academic writing performance with and without GenAI support. Specifically, we investigated whether these effects differ based on chatbot interaction strategies (passive vs. proactive) and account for students’ domain-specific knowledge to ensure an accurate interpretation of the effects of GenAI literacy. Specifically, the following research questions were investigated:

•

RQ1:
What is the relationship between students’ GenAI literacy and their academic writing performance at the AI-support and AI-removal stages, and does this relationship differ based on chatbot interaction type (passive vs. proactive)?

•

RQ2:
Do students with low versus high GenAI literacy differ significantly in their academic writing performance at the AI-support and AI-removal stages, and do these differences differ based on chatbot interaction type (passive vs. proactive)?

•

RQ3:
To what extent does students’ GenAI literacy predict their academic writing performance at the AI-removal stage after controlling for domain-specific knowledge, chatbot interaction type (passive vs. proactive), and writing performance at the AI-support stage?

Methods

4.1
Learning Contexts

The learning context involved writing based on visual information, a critical skill in academic writing, closely aligned with tasks such as IELTS Academic Writing Task 1
[
25
]
. Participants received three distinct visual representations (a bar chart, a network diagram, and a ward map; described in detail in Section
4.3.1
). They were instructed to interpret these visuals and compose a short written answer (150 words) addressing a question about team performance (e.g.,
”How the two nurses managed the primary patient (Amy) while attending to other beds, focusing on their task prioritisation, verbal communication, and stress levels?”
). The visuals were specifically designed using data from authentic learning contexts, such as healthcare simulations, ensuring participants had no prior exposure to these materials. Thus, any potential effects stemming from participants’ prior familiarity with the presented information were minimal. The study was conducted through an online environment (details in Section
4.3.2
).

4.2
Participants

A total of 81 participants completed the study. However, two participants were excluded from the analysis due to providing written responses shorter than 20 words, resulting in a final sample of 79 participants. This exceeded the minimum sample size (72 participants; 36 per condition) required by a priori power analysis (effect size
f=0.25
, power = 0.80,
\alpha=0.05
). Participants were recruited via Prolific
1
1

1

https://www.prolific.co/
and compensated £8 for approximately one hour of participation. Participants were randomly assigned to one of two experimental conditions: passive chatbot (39) or proactive chatbot (40). All participants were current students or graduates from medical or nursing programs. The sample comprised 45 females and 34 males, primarily aged 18-24 (28) and 25-34 (31); fewer participants belonged to age groups 35-44 (11), 45-54 (6), 55-64 (2), and 65 or older (1). Participants were distributed geographically across North/Central America (34), Europe (21), Africa (15), Australia (4), South America (2), and other regions (3). Educational backgrounds included Bachelor’s degrees (36), high school diploma or equivalent (18), Master’s degrees (11), vocational training or diplomas (7), Doctoral degrees (4), and other qualifications (3). Familiarity with GenAI tools was predominantly intermediate (42), beginner (25), advanced (9), expert (2), and none (1).

4.3
Materials and Apparatus

4.3.1
Learning Materials

The three visual information representations used for learning were generated from data collected during a high-fidelity healthcare simulation. In this simulation, students worked in teams of four (comprising two primary and two secondary nurses) to navigate acute clinical scenarios within a realistic, ward-like environment. The simulated setting included hospital beds, medical equipment, and manikins designed to replicate patient vital signs. The objective of the simulation was to enhance students’ prioritisation skills, communication strategies, and collaborative teamwork by replicating realistic clinical emergencies. Throughout the simulation, students’ positional information (x-y coordinates and body orientation) was captured using an indoor positioning system; audio interactions were recorded through wireless headset microphones; and physiological data, specifically heart rates, were collected using Fitbit Sense wristbands.

The multimodal data were used to generate three visuals (Figure
1
), each with progressively increasing complexity. First, a
bar chart
summarises time spent on different behaviours derived from positional data. This visual information illustrates team-level resource allocation and prioritisation strategies
[
64
]
. Second, a
communication network
diagram combining positional and audio data to reveal the frequency and direction of verbal interactions among nurses, the doctor, the patient, and the patient’s relative, thereby depicting participant roles and their level of involvement
[
68
]
. Third, a
ward map
integrates positional, audio, and physiological data. Inspired by visual and spatial analytics methods used in professional sports
[
20
]
, the ward map utilises heatmap visualisation techniques to show communication intensity, trace spatial movements, and highlight locations of peak physiological arousal via heart rate data.

4.3.2
Research Platform

The academic writing tasks were conducted using a custom-built platform comprising three main components. The first component, the display area (Figure
2
), presented one visual analytic at a time to minimise visual clutter and prevent information overload
[
14
]
. Participants could freely navigate between the three visual information representations using the navigation arrows positioned on either side of the component. The second component, the writing space (Figure
2
), provided participants with an area to articulate their analytical insights by synthesising information presented in the visual information. The third component varied based on the experimental condition. In the AI-supported conditions, participants had access to a chat function (Figure
2
), offering real-time support from GenAI agents, which responded either passively or proactively. Conversely, in conditions without AI support, the chat function was replaced by an instruction component providing standard task instruction without AI assistance. The overall platform design emphasised simplicity
[
52
]
, enabling participants to focus primarily on comprehending the visual information and effectively completing the tasks without being overwhelmed by complex functionalities.

4.3.3
Chatbot Interaction Design

We developed two distinct GenAI chatbots, namely the
passive
and the
proactive
, primarily differing in their interaction types and learner-support strategies (Figure
3
). The purpose behind these design choices was to explore how interaction type associates with the writing performance of learners with varying GenAI literacy levels. While both chatbots introduced users with a welcome message explaining the writing task, the
passive chatbot
provided a description of its passive and supportive features and responds only to users’ questions without following up with further interactions
[
42
,
65
]
. In contrast, the
proactive chatbot
actively initiated conversations with users and continually engaged them through domain expert-designed questions and structured narrative pathways (see Supplementary Information)
[
49
]
. Rather than simply reacting, the proactive chatbot offered structured questioning sequences and iterative feedback, progressively enhancing learners’ understanding of visual information based on
Hattie \BBA Timperley \APACyear2007
’s
Hattie \BBA Timperley \APACyear2007
feedback model (e.g., task and process-level). For example, when guiding learners through the ward map analyses, the proactive chatbot first prompted them towards interpreting the distribution of hexagons in the ward (positional data). Once it confirmed that the user had clearly understood this aspect, it then moved onto the next layer of interpretation, specifically the intensity of the colour (verbal communication). This scaffolded questioning approach aligned closely with established conceptual guidelines for interpreting visual information
Xun \BBA Land \APACyear2004
, systematically aiming to enhance learners’ critical interpretation skills.

Building upon the VizChat prototype
L. Yan, Zhao\BCBL \BOthers. \APACyear2024
, both chatbots adopted an advanced agentic architecture composed of four interconnected modules. Prompts were categorised into passive and proactive types corresponding to the aforementioned interaction strategies. At its core, the GenAI agent managed and synthesised inputs from visual information and user prompts, utilising Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) to efficiently retrieve data-informed content from its knowledge database
Gao \BOthers. \APACyear2023
. This comprehensive knowledge repository contained contextual information, visualisation details, and interaction logs that enabled personalised and relevant interactions. Finally, synthesised prompts were processed by the multimodal GPT-4o AI model, generating coherent and context-aware responses that enriched the database and progressively improved the quality of subsequent learner interactions.

\alpha=0.76

,
p>.05

[0,0.035]

4.4.4
Scoring Procedures and Inter-Rater Reliability

The scoring process followed detailed guidelines based on the rubric provided in the Supplementary Information. Two independent, trained coders rated each participant’s writing samples at each study phase (AI-support and AI-removal). Coders were trained extensively on the rubric definitions through practice scoring sessions with sample responses (n=10), followed by discussion and calibration of understanding to maximise rating consistency. All essays were then independently blind-rated by both coders. After ratings were fully completed, reliability analyses were performed (Spearman’s correlations), demonstrating high inter-rater reliability (
r_{s}\geq.75,p<.001
in all cases; Table
1
). Final participant scores for each dimension, at each stage, were averaged across the two coders, producing a single composite rating for each dimension and subsequently an overall
Composite Score
. To ensure transparency and replicability, example excerpts representing each scoring level were provided in the detailed rubric (see Supplementary Information).

4.5
Study Procedure

We conducted a two-phase, mixed-method experimental study to examine how GenAI literacy and AI interaction type (passive vs. proactive chatbot) influenced participants’ academic writing performance with visual information. Participants were recruited via Prolific and directed to a Qualtrics
2
2

2

In the AI-supported phase, participants composed a 150-word written response interpreting the provided multimodal visuals with real-time chatbot support. Subsequently, in the AI-removal phase, participants completed a similar task with new visual data from a different simulation scenario without chatbot access (Figure

4.6
Analysis

4.6.1
Correlation Analysis (RQ1)

To examine the relationship between participants’ GenAI literacy and academic writing performance (RQ1), correlation analyses were conducted separately for each chatbot engagement condition (passive, proactive), at both AI-support and AI-removal time points, and across the five writing dimensions, as well as the composite scores. GenAI literacy was first screened for normality to determine the suitable statistical test. The Shapiro-Wilk normality tests indicated a violation of normality assumptions for GenAI literacy (
p<.05
). Thus, Spearman’s rank-order correlation was used for all analyses. For each condition separately, Spearman’s correlations were calculated between participants’ GenAI literacy scores and their average writing scores at the AI-support and AI-removal stages.

4.6.2
Comparative Analysis (RQ2)

To investigate further if students at varying levels of GenAI literacy performed differently in academic writing (RQ2), we conducted comparative analyses by grouping participants into two distinct GenAI literacy categories: low and high literacy. Participants were categorised based on median splits of GenAI literacy scores within each condition separately. We chose the median split procedure to ensure balanced group sizes and improve interoperability. To ascertain whether academic writing performance differed significantly between low and high GenAI literacy groups, we used Mann-Whitney
U

6
Discussion

This study explored the association between GenAI literacy and students’ multimodal academic writing performance in conditions where either passive or proactive GenAI tools supported academic writing. By examining students’ independent writing performance immediately following the withdrawal of GenAI assistance, we sought to understand whether and how GenAI literacy shapes writing performance once external scaffolding is removed and how interactions with chatbots of different styles might moderate these relationships.

6.1
GenAI Literacy and Independent Writing Performance (RQ1)

For RQ1, our findings indicated a significant positive relationship between students’ GenAI literacy and their academic writing performance at the independent writing stage immediately after GenAI assistance was removed. Specifically, participants with higher GenAI literacy demonstrated stronger performance across multiple rubric dimensions (insightfulness, visual data integration, organisation and coherence, linguistic quality, critical thinking, and composite scores) when writing without AI support. This result provides empirical evidence underscoring the role of GenAI literacy as potentially influential in learners’ capabilities, particularly when performing independently without technological scaffolds. It appears that GenAI literacy is associated with superior performance in conditions where no external AI prompts or support mechanisms are immediately available. This finding is in line with prior theoretical positions suggesting that effective interactions with technology depend not only on the availability of tools but also on users’ understanding of how best to integrate and leverage them effectively
Nguyen \BOthers. \APACyear2024
;
L. Yan, Greiff\BCBL \BOthers. \APACyear2024
. Researchers argue that higher levels of literacy related to emerging digital tools lead to better competence, critical engagement with technology-generated output, and stronger independent strategic thinking
Jin \BOthers. \APACyear2024
;
Kim \BOthers. \APACyear2025
. Our results reinforce this perspective in the context of GenAI-assisted multimodal writing, highlighting that students with higher GenAI literacy appear better prepared, at least immediately following GenAI assistance, to produce high-quality, independent academic writing without continual external support.

6.2
The Role of Chatbot Interaction Strategies (RQ2)

Interestingly, for RQ2, significant relationships between higher GenAI literacy and independent academic writing performance emerged only within the passive (reactive) chatbot condition. In contrast, the proactive (actively scaffolding) chatbot condition did not demonstrate a significant correlation between GenAI literacy and subsequent independent writing performance. This finding suggests that chatbot interaction strategies can potentially moderate the relationship between GenAI literacy and student outcomes. One plausible explanation is that passive chatbots, which respond only when students actively query them, require greater cognitive and strategic engagement from learners. GenAI-literate students in such conditions may have had opportunities and abilities to formulate effective prompts, critically evaluate the AI-generated assistance, and transfer the insights gained into their independent writing tasks. Conversely, proactively scaffolding chatbots continuously direct and structure learner engagement, perhaps minimising the need for students to exercise or rely upon their own GenAI literacy skills explicitly. This proactive scaffolding likely enabled both high and low GenAI literacy students to allocate their cognitive resources more effectively during task completion, thus levelling performance differences related to GenAI literacy. Students in the proactive condition benefitted from structured support, reducing the cognitive demands associated with engaging directly with GenAI tools, and enabling those with lower initial GenAI literacy to perform comparably to students with higher sophistication. Previous studies suggest scaffolding can enhance learner performance by decreasing unnecessary cognitive load, especially for those less adept with novel technologies
Nguyen \BOthers. \APACyear2024
;
Sharma \BBA Hannafin \APACyear2007
. Additionally, by providing structured guidance to learners with varying technological familiarity, this scaffolding approach may help mitigate the risk of a digital divide
Celik \APACyear2023
. However, it is also possible that the proactive condition limited opportunities for learners to actively practice and further develop their GenAI literacy skills, a consideration that warrants future investigation. Consequently, educators should balance structured support from GenAI chatbots with opportunities for independent inquiry, encouraging active and strategic engagement that leverages learners’ pre-existing technological literacy.

6.3
Predictive Role of GenAI Literacy in Multimodal Writing (RQ3)

Ordinal logistic regression (RQ3) analyses further substantiated that GenAI literacy significantly predicted students’ independent writing outcomes. Even after controlling for domain-specific content knowledge, initial AI-assisted writing scores, and chatbot interaction conditions, our analyses indicated GenAI literacy uniquely predicted stronger independent writing outcomes in visual data integration, critical thinking, and overall composite scores post-scaffolding removal. By contrast, domain-specific knowledge and chatbot conditions did not significantly predict independent performance outcomes when considered alongside GenAI literacy. These findings reinforce recent arguments positioning digital tool proficiency (especially with GenAI) as a distinct skill set separate from traditional academic knowledge bases
Jin \BOthers. \APACyear2024
;
D. Yan \APACyear2023
;
Wang \APACyear2025
. Our findings suggest that training and instruction related explicitly to GenAI literacy may effectively support students in independently performing multimodal, complex writing tasks. This unique predictive influence prompts reconsideration of GenAI literacy as a key pedagogical target, warranting purposeful curriculum inclusion rather than simple assumptions of passive skill acquisition.

6.4
Implications for Research and Practice

Our findings have several implications for educational research related to the integration of GenAI in academic writing contexts. First, the identified relationships between GenAI literacy and independent multimodal writing performance highlight the importance of incorporating systematic instruction and explicit assessment of students’ technology-related literacy skills within contemporary curricula. Researchers emphasise that students do not naturally or uniformly acquire sophisticated literacy skills required to critically engage with or use advanced AI technologies in academic contexts
Jin \BOthers. \APACyear2024
;
Nguyen \BOthers. \APACyear2024
. Therefore, educators and curriculum designers should proactively embed training on critical aspects of GenAI literacy, such as effectively formulating prompts, interpreting and evaluating AI-generated outputs, and iteratively refining generated content in complex multimodal academic tasks. Future researchers might also explore longitudinal trajectories of students’ GenAI literacy development and potential transfer effects of such skills across various academic domains and types of academic tasks
Wang \APACyear2025
;
Kim \BOthers. \APACyear2025
. Additionally, careful studies into the cognitive and metacognitive strategies students employ in interaction with GenAI could yield valuable pedagogical insights for developing effective human-AI collaboration models
Yang \BOthers. \APACyear2025
;
Cheng \BOthers. \APACyear2024
.

At a practical level, our findings underscore the importance of balancing external GenAI scaffolding support with opportunities for independent exploration and inquiry. Specifically, passive or reactive chatbot interventions appear more directly beneficial to learners with high GenAI literacy, as such interaction strategies require greater student initiative, metacognitive monitoring, and strategic engagement with the technology
Fan \BOthers. \APACyear2025
. In instructional practice, educators might strategically incorporate AI-assisted tools with fading levels of scaffolding to progressively allow students to take greater control over their learning processes, aligning with established theoretical foundations (e.g., Zone of Proximal Development) and scaffolding frameworks
Van de Pol \BOthers. \APACyear2010
;
Vygotsky \BBA Cole \APACyear1978
. Practitioners should also deliberately consider design characteristics and interaction patterns of instructional chatbots, ensuring that chatbot designs offer sufficient flexibility while fostering productive human-AI interactions and learner autonomy
Nguyen \BOthers. \APACyear2024
;
L. Yan, Greiff\BCBL \BOthers. \APACyear2024
;
Xie \BOthers. \APACyear2024
. Taken together, these implications highlight the need for intentional, critical, and well-informed use of GenAI tools in educational practice, enabling learners to utilise these powerful tools effectively while continually strengthening their independent scholarly skills and cognitive competencies.

6.5
Limitations and Future Directions

Several limitations warrant attention when interpreting the present findings. First, the current study assessed students’ writing performance solely at two temporally adjacent points (with and immediately after the removal of AI scaffolding) within the same experimental session, limiting our ability to make broader claims about delayed or long-term improvements. Future longitudinal studies involving follow-up measures conducted at various time intervals can more definitively establish delayed effects or longer-term skill consolidation. Second, the task’s particular context, healthcare simulations involving multimodal academic writing and visual data interpretation, limits direct generalisability across disciplines or other academic writing types. Replicating or expanding this approach to diverse fields (such as STEM, social sciences, and the humanities) would clarify the robustness and generalisability of current findings. Third, although we distinguished passive from proactive chatbot conditions, additional chatbot features (e.g., specific scaffolding strategies, transparency, adaptivity, fairness) likely shape interactions in complex ways, warranting follow-up studies that evaluate nuanced chatbot design variables. Fourth, qualitative evidence clarifying students’ cognitive processes, metacognitive reflections, and explicit interactions with GenAI tools was beyond our current analysis scope. Future research integrating qualitative approaches (think-aloud protocols, interviews, reflection statements) or detailed log-file analyses of user-AI interactions could richly explain underlying adjustments or strategic variations driven by differing GenAI literacy levels.

7
Conclusion

As GenAI technologies like advanced chatbots become prevalent in education, questions arise regarding their impact on independent thought and student performance. Our study provides empirical evidence that GenAI literacy positively predicts students’ multimodal academic writing performance even after the removal of AI support. Significantly, these GenAI literacy skills influenced outcomes beyond domain-specific knowledge and prior academic performance, highlighting their distinct importance. Additionally, interaction style mattered; passive chatbot support effectively promoted autonomous skill application among AI-literate learners. These results underscore the importance of explicitly nurturing GenAI literacy within curricula and thoughtfully balancing AI assistance with learner autonomy. Future longitudinal studies should explore lasting impacts, informing the responsible integration of GenAI into educational settings.
