Evaluating practices toward dental health among primary school children in Rwanda
Authors: Janviere Mukabizimana, Dieudonne Kayiranga, Godfrey Katende, Jean Bosco Munyemana
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDental diseases pose a global health problem that significantly affects overall well-being and quality of life. They are frequently neglected in many regions, despite the availability of preventive measures and it remains unclear whether these measures are effectively implemented in practice. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate dental health practices among primary school children in both rural and urban areas of Rwanda.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted on 195 school children including 96 from a rural area and 99 from an urban area. Data were collected through a questionnaire and analyzed using SPSS version 21. To compare practices between the rural and urban groups, Fisher’s exact test was utilized, with a significance level set at 0.05.ResultsThe findings show significant differences in dental health practices between rural and urban areas, revealing that rural children were less likely to visit the dentist regularly (p = 0.026) and had a higher frequency of sugary drink consumption (p = 0.002). Additionally, disparities were found in the materials used for tooth brushing (p < 0.001) and the timing of toothbrush replacement (p = 0.011), indicating that rural children often use inappropriate brushing materials and replace their toothbrushes less frequently.ConclusionThis study highlights significant differences in dental health practices between rural and urban children in Rwanda. Rural children were less likely to visit the dentist, consumed more sugary drinks, and used inappropriate brushing materials with less frequent toothbrush replacement. These findings underscore the need for targeted interventions to improve oral health awareness and practices especially in rural areas.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-024-05225-2.Keywords: Dental health, Dental disease, School children, Oral health disparities

Background

Dental diseases are prevalent health issues with a significant impact on overall health and quality of life [1, 2]. In 2016, the World Health Organization (WHO) reported that over 90% of the global population experienced dental diseases, with 95% of children in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) facing untreated dental problems during their lifetime [1]. These dental diseases not only pose significant health challenges for individuals but also result in substantial financial burdens, with estimated annual international expenses predicted to reach US$298 billion, constituting 4.6% of total social expenditure [3].

The burden of oral disorders, measured in disability-adjusted life-years (DALYs), reached 15 million worldwide in 2017, accounting for 1.9% of all DALYs and indicating an average health loss of 224 annually per 100,000 residents [4]. In LMICs, the rise in sugar consumption, coupled with reduced access to dental disease prevention and care services due to poverty and higher prices of medical cost, exacerbates the challenge [5].

Untreated dental diseases can progress to serious systemic conditions with significant health and socioeconomic impacts [6]. The oral bacterial infections provide a reservoir for microorganisms to disseminate through the bloodstream, potentially causing infective endocarditis or exacerbating inflammatory conditions like arthritis [5, 6]. Moreover, aspiration of oral pathogens increases the risk of respiratory infections such as pneumonia, especially in vulnerable populations like children, and are associated with adverse prognoses and outcomes, while preventable [7].

Dental caries, commonly referred to as tooth decay or cavities, represent the most prevalent oral health disease globally. Over one-third of the world’s population suffers from untreated dental caries, impacting more than 2 billion individuals [8]. Among children, untreated caries is the most widespread chronic disease affecting deciduous teeth, with an estimated 514 million children impacted worldwide [8]. In 2019, there were 73,733,740 reported cases of dental caries in low-income countries, with a prevalence rate of 40% [8]. Moroever, the previous studies conducted in sub-Saharan Africa have demonstrated alarmingly high rates of dental caries and underscored the significant effects of dental diseases on children’s daily lives and overall well-being [9]. In East Africa, a survey conducted in Kenya revealed that 31% of children struggled to chew solid food due to painful dental conditions, 27.8% avoided smiling to prevent pain from air entering the mouth, and 18.9% were absent from school due to tooth-related issues such as dental caries [10]. These findings underscore the detrimental effects of poor oral health on essential activities and quality of life among children in the region.

Furthermore, several studies have reported alarmingly high prevalence rates of dental caries among school children. In Kenya, Gathecha et al. (2012) found a prevalence of 37.5% among 12-year-old children in the Nairobi West District [10]. In Zimbabwe, Mafuvadze et al. (2013) reported an even higher prevalence of 59.5% in urban areas and 40.8% in rural areas among 12-year-old children from low socioeconomic backgrounds [11]. Similarly, a baseline survey conducted by Wandera and Twa-Twa (2003) in Uganda revealed a high prevalence of 62% among primary school children [12]. These findings showed a higher burden of dental caries affecting school-aged children across East African Community (EAC). However, little is known about this problem in Rwandan school children.

Additionally, previous reports have identified modifiable behaviors contributing to early childhood caries (ECC) among preschool children. These include poor oral hygiene practices like infrequent toothbrushing, high sugar consumption, lack of access to dental care services, and low parental education levels, particularly maternal education [13–16]. Socioeconomic factors such as poverty and low health literacy also play a crucial role in perpetuating this cycle [15], highlighting the need for oral health promotion programs.

Despite existing preventive measures, dental diseases remain neglected, and the cost of medical care remains unaffordable [10, 17]. Recognizing the serious complications poor oral health can cause, this study aims to assess practices toward dental health among Rwandan school children in both urban and rural communities. The findings underscore the need for improved dental health and the integration of preventive activities in primary schools to promote children’s overall well-being.

Methods

Study design

A cross-sectional study design was used to assess the practices related to dental health among school children in rural and urban settings in Rwanda. The questionnaire, adapted from Zhu and Petersen [18], was used to collect data.

Study setting

Data were collected from two public primary schools, one located in a rural area (Kibari Primary School in Gicumbi District, Northern Province) with 191 students and the other in an urban area (Kacyiru Primary School in Gasabo District, Kigali City) with also 200 students. The study was conducted from 22 January 2020 to 19 March 2020 and enrolled 195 students.

Sampling strategy

The children enrolled into the study were recruited using a purposive sampling strategy. A total of 99 participants were selected from Kacyiru Public Primary School (urban) and 96 from Kibali Public Primary School (rural), based on class attendance lists. The study population was 391 children present during the sampling time. If a level had multiple classes (e.g., P1A, P1B, P1C), one class was randomly chosen to represent the rest of the grade at the same level and one class was selected at each level.

Sample size

The sample size was calculated according to the online Raosoft 2004 sample size calculation formular [19], with margin of error set at 5%, confidence interval of 95%, population size 391 and response distribution at 50%. In total 195 children were recruited and enrolled from primary (P) level 1 to level 6 (P1 to P6) [19].

Data collection tool

In this study, we used aquestionnaire with multiple-choice questions and this questionnaire had been used previously [18] and was adapted to the Rwandan context in line with the conceptual framework. Permission to adapt and use the tool was obtained from the lead author via email on April 12, 2019. The adapted quationaire consisted of three sections: Section A collected demographic information about the children, section B focused on practices related to dental health, while Section C assessed tooth brushing techniques. The questionnaire, originally in English, was translated into Kinyarwanda and was validated before data collection.

Study variables

The study categorized variables into three groups to describe participants and compare practices related to dental health among rural and urban school children, focusing on: (A) demographics such as gender, age, and school level; (B) dental health behaviors, which were assessed by evaluating factors including the frequency of tooth brushing, frequency of dentist visit, consumption of sugary drinks, habits related to drinking sugary drinks at night, frequency of eating sweets and candies in both rural and urban settings; and (C) practices related to dental health, evaluated based on tooth cleaning tools, time spent brushing, brushing techniques and frequency of changing toothbrushes among children from rural and urban areas.

Data collection

Prior to data collection, students were thoroughly briefed on the study’s objectives and procedures. Information letters and consent forms were sent home with students for parental review and signature. Children capable of understanding and responding independently completed the questionnaires themselves at school. For younger children or those requiring assistance, we provided the data collection tools to be completed at home with parental help. Subsequently, the completed questionnaires were returned to school the following day. Participants were assured that their involvement was voluntary, and they were given the option to complete the questionnaire in either Kinyarwanda or English, according to their preference. Confidentiality measures were guaranteed throughout the study.

Data analysis

Coded data were entered and analyzed using Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS) version 21.0. Frequencies and percentages were calculated and Chi-square test or fisher’s exact test were employed to examine associations between variables. Results were presented in tables. A P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant for risk behaviors and practices in both urban and rural primary schools.

Ethical considerations

The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of the University of Rwanda, College of Medicine and Health Sciences (approval reference: CMHS/IRB/046/2019). Authorization to collect data was obtained from school headmasters and local government representatives. The researchers explained the study objectives to the children, emphasizing their rights to participate voluntarily, withdraw at any time without explanation, and assured confidentiality by not collecting names. Consent forms were distributed to children to obtain parental/legal guardian consent. All children returned signed consent forms from their parents/guardians, allowing them to participate in the study. Assent was also obtained from children aged 8 years and above.

Results

Sociodemographic characteristics of participants

The study included a total of 195 participants out of 195 (100%) eligible children, with 96 (49%) from rural area and 99 (51%) from urban area. The gender distribution was relatively even, with 95 (48.7%) males and 100 (51.3%) females. The majority of participants 87 (44.6%) were between 8 and 10 years of age, followed by the age goup of 11–12 years 72 (36.9%) and 5–7 years of age 36 (18.5%). In terms of school level, 100 (51.3%) participants were in primary 1 to primary 3 (P1-P3), while 95 (48.7%) were in grades P4-P6. The p-values for gender (0.48) and school level (0.48) were not statistically significant different (Table 1).

Behaviours towards dental health in urban and rural settings

In Table 2 we present a comparison of behaviors towards dental health between urban and rural settings. The frequency of tooth brushing showed no significant difference, with 53 (55.8%) of urban participants brushing once per day compared to 66 (66.0%) in rural areas (p = 0.304). However, dental visit patterns revealed very low frequencies with a significant disparity; at least 3 (3.0%) of urban participants reported visiting the dentist regularly every 6–12 months, while none from the rural group did (p = 0.026). Conversely, a higher percentage of rural participants 48 (48.0%) reported never visiting a dentist compared to 38 (40.0%) in urban areas. The consumption of sugary drinks was significantly different, with 24 (25.3%) of urban participants consuming sugary drinks every day, compared to only 6 (6.0%) in rural areas (p = 0.002). Habits related to taking sugary drinks at night dominated, with 54 (56.8%) in urban compared to 62 (62.0%) in rural areas, showing no significant difference (p = 0.481). Similarly, the frequency of eating sweets and candies showed no significant difference (p = 0.166), with a predominance of 62 (65.3%) in urban and 56 (56.0%) in rural areas eating candies 1–3 times per day. Overall, these findings highlight notable differences in behaviors towards dental health between urban and rural populations, particularly regarding dental visits and taking sugary drinks (Table 2).

Participant practices towards dental health

The findings presented in Table 3 compares participants’ practices towards dental health in urban and rural settings. A significant difference was observed in the materials used for cleaning teeth, with 78 (82.1%) of urban participants using toothbrushes and toothpaste compared to only 57 (57.0%) of rural participants (p < 0.001). In contrast, a higher proportion of rural participants reported using charcoal 17 (17.0%) and toothpicks 15 (15.0%) as cleaning tools. Time spent brushing teeth did not show significant differences between the groups, with most participants in both settings brushing for one to two minutes (p = 0.655). Similarly, brushing techniques were comparable, with no significant preference for horizontal or systematic methods (p = 0.668). However, there was a notable difference in the frequency of changing toothbrushes; 40 (42.1%) of urban participants reported changing their toothbrushes every 1–3 months, compared to 33 (33.0%) of rural participants (p = 0.011). Additionally, a larger proportion of rural participants were unsure about how often to change their toothbrushes or did not own one at all with 20 (20.0%) and 25 (25.0%), respectively. These findings highlight significant disparities in dental health practices, particularly regarding the materials used for oral hygiene (Table 3).

Discussion

This descriptive cross-sectional study aimed to assess the practices toward dental health among primary school children, with a comparison of results between the rural and urban settings in Rwanda. The findings shed light on key factors influencing dental health in this demographic and noticed variations in dental health practices between urban and rural children. Some of the children had inadequate tooth-cleaning habits and a lack of awareness about preventive measures for maintaining oral hygiene. This finding aligns with the findings of a cross-sectional study conducted among Ethiopian primary school children [20].

Despite professional recommendations advocating for twice-daily tooth brushing, our study revealed a concerning discrepancy, with only a minority of participants (35.8%) in urban and (29.0%) in rural areas adhering to this guideline. This is consistent with findings from other regions, such as Madagascar, where infrequent tooth brushing was prevalent among school children [21]. The implications of suboptimal brushing frequency on oral health are well-documented, emphasizing the need for targeted education campaigns to promote proper oral hygiene practices [3].

The frequency and patterns of sugar consumption among our study participants also warrant attention, with a substantial proportion reporting regular (two to three times a week) intake of sugary drinks and candies in urban (31.6%) and rural (29.0%) areas. These dietary habits, coupled with inadequate tooth brushing practices, contribute significantly to dental caries and erosion, as evidenced by previous findings which showed a negative health outcome associated with excessive sugar consumption on dental diseases [22]. Another study conducted in Thailand provided evidence that supports the association between sugar consumption and high prevalence of dental caries [23].

Notably, a significant proportion of participants in the current study with 48.0% from rural and 40.0% from urban reported that they have never visited a dentist, underscoring the disparities in access to dental care between urban and rural areas. This finding aligns with existing research that indicates socioeconomic factors greatly influence dental service utilization; children from lower socioeconomic backgrounds are less likely to receive preventive care [24]. Additionally, individuals from lower socioeconomic statuses often struggle to access dental services due to high costs and a lack of awareness about available resources. Although our study revealed a significant difference in dental visits between rural and urban populations, both groups exhibited low frequencies of dental visits. This suggests either a potential shortage of dental clinics and a need for increased awareness and education about dental health. Addressing these issues is essential to improve service utilization and enhance the overall well-being of the population.

Variations in tooth brushing materials were observed between rural and urban children in this study. For example, 17% of rural children used charcoal as a cleaning tool, while 15% utilized toothpicks. In contrast, only 4.2% of urban children used charcoal, and 2.1% used toothpicks, reflecting disparities in socioeconomic status and access to oral hygiene resources. These findings highlight the urgent need for targeted interventions to enhance access to affordable dental care and promote proper oral hygiene practices, particularly among underserved populations [25].

Generally, the current findings highlight significant urban-rural disparities in various oral health behaviors and practices, and this is similar to previous findings [26, 27]. These disparities in health behaviors and practices between urban and rural populations can be attributed to various factors, including differences in socioeconomic status disparities, differential access to healthcare services, education levels, and cultural norms [27]. Addressing these urban-rural gaps is crucial for promoting equitable health outcomes and implementing targeted interventions tailored to the specific needs and challenges of rural communities.

Limitation

Despite the strengths of this study, including its comparative analysis of rural and urban populations, there are some limitations. First, the sample size was not sufficient to generalize findings to the entire country, warranting further research with larger cohorts. Second, the study focused exclusively on children attending school, potentially excluding vulnerable populations not enrolled in formal education settings. Future studies should aim to include a larger and more diverse sample, including those children not enrolled in school, to provide a comprehensive understanding of behaviors and practices toward dental health in Rwanda.

Conclusion

This study highlights significant differences in dental health practices between rural and urban children in Rwanda. Rural children were less likely to visit the dentist, consumed more sugary drinks, and used inappropriate brushing materials with less frequent toothbrush replacement. These findings underscore the need for targeted interventions to improve oral health awareness and practices especially in rural areas.
