Effect of acid etching of stainless-steel foils in aqueous rechargeable lithium-ion batteries
Authors: Kamila Maratova, Bauyrzhan Myrzakhmetov, Damira Rakhman, Aliya Toleuova, Akbar Dauletbay, Zhumabay Bakenov, Aishuak Konarov
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The influence of hydrochloric acid etching on the surface properties and electrochemical performance of stainless-steel foils as current collectors for aqueous energy storage devices was investigated. Stainless-steel foils were etched at 80 °C for 10, 20, and 30 min, and the subsequent effects on surface roughness, wettability, and battery performance were investigated in detail. Etching for 10 min provided slight variations in the surface, while etching for 30 min encompassed serious corrosion with deep pits. The roughness shifted from 0.115 µm to 0.403 µm and enhanced the hydrophilicity. The measurements of the LiFePO4 electrode demonstrated that 10-min etched foils delivered the highest capacity (~ 130 mAh g−1) and stability compared to un-etched or further etched foils. The improved performance is due to moderate roughening, oxide layer removal, and higher contact area efficiency, and hence etching at 10 min is most appropriate for stainless-steel current collectors in aqueous batteries.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-27990-y.

Introduction

Rechargeable batteries are a cornerstone of modern technology and powering systems for communication, transportation, and daily convenience. As applications expand, the demand for energy storage devices with greater safety, longer service life, and improved reliability continues to intensify1. In response, the scientific community is pursuing next-generation systems beyond conventional lithium-ion batteries (LIBs)2,3. Although LIBs dominate portable electronics and electric vehicles, their reliance on flammable organic electrolytes and limited lithium reserves has stimulated growing interest in aqueous rechargeable batteries (ARBs) as safer and more sustainable alternatives4–11.

In stationary and grid-scale applications, where the volumetric energy density is less critical than cost and safety, aqueous systems have distinct advantages. However, large-scale LIB deployments have underscored serious safety risks associated with organic carbonate electrolytes, namely, high flammability and the potential for thermal runaway12–18. In contrast, aqueous electrolytes provide intrinsic non-flammability and high ionic conductivity, enabling improved operational safety without compromising power capability. Among emerging aqueous systems, rechargeable hybrid Zn/Li-ion aqueous batteries (RHABs) represent a promising class that merges the stability and reversibility of lithium-ion intercalation cathodes with the high capacity and safety of zinc metal anodes19–23. In a typical configuration, a Li-intercalation cathode (e.g., LiFePO4) operates in an aqueous dual salt electrolyte (ZnSO4 + Li2SO4), facilitating a coupled Zn2+/Li+ storage mechanism. This design offers high-rate capability and environmental safety, but challenges remain, including Zn dendrite growth, parasitic hydrogen evolution, and interfacial instability that must be resolved for practical implementation24–38. Therefore, interfacial engineering of both electrodes and current collectors is essential to achieve stable long-term operation.

The current collector (CC) plays a crucial role in these hybrid systems, serving as the electronic pathway and mechanical support for the electrode coating. In RHABs, the CC must maintain conductivity while resisting corrosion and water decomposition within a restricted electrochemical window. Yet, compared with extensive research on electrodes and electrolytes, optimization of the current collector remains relatively overlooked. During cycling, parasitic water-splitting reactions often initiate at the CC surface, narrowing the operational potential range. Thus, developing or modifying CCs with high hydrogen- and oxygen-evolution overpotentials is a key strategy to widen the stability window and enhance performance.

Various CCs have been utilized in aqueous systems, including nickel mesh39, titanium mesh, stainless steel40–43, aluminum foil44, zinc foil45, and graphite foil. In addition, numerous modification strategies have been proposed, such as sol–gel coatings, carbonization treatments, electrochemical etching, electrodeposition, and chemical oxidation to improve adhesion, corrosion resistance, and interfacial conductivity46. Recently, modification of CCs has emerged as an increasingly effective approach to improve interfacial stability and wettability in aqueous and hybrid batteries. Early work on stainless steel showed that chemical etching introduces nanopits (100–300 nm) that substantially improve active material adhesion and reduce interfacial resistance in aqueous electrochemical systems47. Similarly, Li et al. demonstrated that FeCl3/HCl etching produces crater-like textures that enhance wettability and ion transport, yielding more uniform Zn2+ deposition and more than twice the discharge capacity of untreated foils in Zn–MnO2 cells48. Beyond chemical texturing, electro- and electroless-deposition of Ni, Ni–Al LDH, or Co-ferrite coatings have been employed to increase corrosion resistance in alkaline or mildly acidic electrolytes49. Moreover, 3D CCs, such as Ni foams or porous Ti substrates, facilitate a homogeneous charge distribution, while electroactive Ti-based collectors suppress hydrogen evolution and extend the potential window through reversible Ti4+/Ti3+ redox behavior50. Collectively, these studies underscore that rational modification of the CC surface via etching, coating, or structural engineering can effectively tailor adhesion, wettability, and reaction kinetics, directly influencing hybrid Zn/Li battery performance.

Compared with electrochemical etching and plasma/ion treatments, chemical etching provides a single-step, energy-efficient, and scalable approach that requires no external power supply and remains compatible with aqueous electrode processing. Electrochemical texturing enables fine control but requires specialized power equipment and inert environments, while coatings or polymer films, though corrosion-resistant, can add complexity and impede adhesion. In contrast, appropriately tuned chemical etching (minutes at ~ 80 °C) creates micro/nanopits that enlarge the effective surface area, enhance hydrophilicity, and lower interfacial resistance, leading to higher rate capability and cycling stability in aqueous hybrid systems47–49,51. Electrochemical etching, also known as electropolishing, removes surface oxides via anodic dissolution within a controlled potential window. However, it requires an external power supply, carefully prepared electrolytes, and more complex process regulation; it also produces a very polished and bright surface and allows for fine modification of roughness50. In contrast, HCl chemical etching is a straightforward single-step procedure that can be performed at room temperature. Rapid degradation of the chromium oxide passive layer by strong acid and chloride ions results in micro- and nanoscale roughness, along with the exposure of a Ni-enriched surface, as these ions preferentially leach Fe and Cr. This natural texture surface is chemically enhanced to facilitate scaling and reduce costs by improving adhesion or electrochemical activity, all without requiring specialized equipment50.

The working principle of HCl etching involves an initial attack of the acid on the passive chromium oxide layer, followed by penetration into the stainless steel and reaction with base metals (Fe, Ni, Cr) to form soluble metal chlorides. This process progressively breaks down the protective surface layer and increases surface roughness52. The concentration and temperature of HCl, as well as the presence of impurities and the alloy composition of stainless steel, are key factors influencing the corrosion rate53. In this study the first two factors are examined.

Compared to alternative surface treatments such as electrochemical etching, HCl-based chemical provides a simpler, cheaper, and more efficient technological solution that enables uniform surface modification over large areas without requiring complex equipment or applied voltage (electrochemical etching typically needs controlled current/voltage and advanced setups). Although the electrolytic method offers more control over the depth, pattern, and overall greater precision of the etching, it requires more complex equipment and higher operational costs compared to HCl etching54.

In this study, the hydrochloric acid (HCl) etching of stainless-steel foils under fixed composition and temperature was systematically investigated, isolating the etching time as the single controlling variable. We demonstrate the direct influence of etching duration on interfacial properties and electrochemical performance in an aqueous hybrid Zn/Li-ion battery (ZLIB) configuration that employs a LiFePO4 cathode, a zinc anode, and an electrolyte of ZnSO4–Li2SO4. By correlating surface roughness (AFM), wettability (contact angle), and electrochemical behavior, we identify an optimal etching time of 10 min, which maximizes surface activation without inducing corrosion. The resulting collector exhibits superior wettability, reduced charge-transfer resistance, and enhanced LiFePO4 performance (~ 130 mAh g−1) with improved cycling stability, making this process particularly suitable for the fabrication of scalable aqueous hybrid batteries. Unlike earlier studies such as Piao et al.51, which focused on understanding or mitigating acid-induced corrosion, our work deliberately uses HCl etching as a low-cost surface engineering strategy. We show that this simple, scalable process creates a Ni-enriched, nanostructured surface that directly enhances electrochemical performance in aqueous batteries, thereby providing both mechanistic insight and a practical route to current-collector activation.

Experimental section

Materials

Commercial stainless-steel foil (SSF) (Type 304), containing 17.5–19.5% chromium, 8–10.5% nickel, 2% manganese, 1% silicon, 0.11% nitrogen, 0.07% carbon, 0.05% phosphorus, and 0.03% sulfur, was used as both the current collector and the material for surface etching. The etching solution was prepared by diluting 83 mL of 37% hydrochloric acid (HCl, Sigma-Aldrich) in a 1000 mL volumetric flask and then filling the flask with deionized water up to the calibration mark.

Etching of SSF and material characterization

A schematic of the process and corresponding foil morphology is shown in Fig. 1. SSFs (5 cm × 8 cm) were ultrasonically cleaned in ethanol for 15 min and etched in 1 M HCl at 80 °C for 10, 20, or 30 min. After etching, the foils were rinsed with deionized water, cleaned with ethanol, and air-dried. Disk-type specimens (14 mm diameter) were then prepared for surface analysis by SEM (CrossBeam 540), AFM (SmartSPM 1000), digital optical microscopy (HIROX KH-7700), and contact angle analysis.Fig. 1Comparison of current collector materials and schematic of the HCl-etching process for stainless-steel foils.

Comparison of current collector materials and schematic of the HCl-etching process for stainless-steel foils.

Electrochemical characterization

LiFePO4 positive electrodes were prepared by mixing 90 wt% LiFePO4 (Gelon), 6 wt.% polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF, MTI Corporation) as a binder, and 4 wt.% acetylene black (MTI Corporation) as a conductive agent in 1-methyl-2-pyrrolidinone (NMP, Sigma-Aldrich). The resulting slurry was coated onto etched SSFs, followed by vacuum drying at 60 °C for 4 h. The dried electrodes were then punched into circular discs with a diameter of 14 mm for coin cells. The active-material loading was around 10–16 mg cm−2.

Zinc foil (Goodfellow, USA) was employed as the negative electrode. The electrolyte was prepared by dissolving 3.4 mol dm−3 ZnSO4 and 2.5 mol dm−3 Li2SO4 (both from Sigma-Aldrich) in deionized water. The pH of the solution was adjusted to 4.0 via titration with 0.05 mol dm−3 LiOH or 0.5 mol dm−3 H2SO4 (Sigma–Aldrich). Before cell assembly, the electrolyte solution was air-bubbled for 1 h to improve homogeneity and oxygenation.

A filter paper separator (Goodfellow, USA) was soaked in the prepared electrolyte and used to separate the electrodes in the cell. The electrochemical performance of the cells was evaluated using two-electrode coin cells, tested under galvanostatic conditions on a multi-channel battery tester (Neware Co, China) and pouch cells tested on the Arbin battery tester (USA). All specific capacities are reported per gram of active material.

A thin and stable coating of chromium oxide (Cr2O3) protects stainless steel foils. When hydrochloric acid is present, chloride ions (Cl−) and protons (H+) degrade this passive layer. In an acidic environment, iron and chromium undergo conversion into Fe2+/Fe3+ and Cr3+ chlorides, respectively. Localized pitting may also occur when chloride ions enter the defects. Nickel dissolves slowly because it is more noble than iron and chromium. Leaching iron and chromium can increase the amount of nickel traveling to certain areas. The disintegration and selective dissolution of the passive film alter the composition of the surface layer, resulting in the production of cracks and etch patterns, as well as roughening of the foil. This technique often produces a thinner oxide with a greater Ni concentration and a lower Cr concentration. This change in the micro- and nano-dimensions caused the observed changes in morphology and chemistry after the HCl etching process55.

Figure 2 illustrates the progressive evolution of the surface morphology of SSF samples etched in 1 M HCl for 10, 20, and 30 min. The optical micrographs (top row) show a distinct, time-dependent change in surface characteristics. After 10 min of etching (Fig. 2b), the surface of the SSF is still relatively smooth with minimal visible etching features. Extending the etching time to 20 min (Fig. 2c) provides a significantly rougher surface with a higher density and a clear definition of the etching lines. In 30 min (Fig. 2d), extensive corrosion was observed, producing a highly degraded surface with networked grooves and large pits.

This morphological development is seen in the lower row SEM images. The surface texture changes minimally with a few scattered defects at 10 min. At 20 min, the corrosion is more severe and extensive, showing a more complex and disordered distribution of pits. The surface is predominantly porous at 30 min, showing huge material loss and a much rougher morphology. This roughening is a significant expansion of the surface area that can be used to improve properties such as coating adhesion or electrochemical performance.

Compared to other studies that varied both the etching time and the chemical composition together48, the current study declares time the only variable for a constant composition of the etchant. The experiment design highlights etching time’s role in the resulting SSF surface morphology—a finding with implications on a wide range of applications such as catalysis, sensing, and energy storage.

The evolution of SSF surface morphology during HCl etching was further examined by AFM, as presented in Fig. 3. AFM images of SSF etched by 1 M HCl: (a) 10-min-treated SSF, (b) 20-min-treated SSF, and (c) 30-min-treated SSF (The top images provide a top-down view, and bottom images depict a 3-D representation of the surface).

After etching for 10 min (Fig. 3a), the surface showed moderately evolved grooves in a preferred direction with signs of anisotropic material removal. The surface features were quite shallow with an estimated depth variation of < 500 nm, showing the beginning of microstructural exposure.

At 20 min of etching (Fig. 3b), the surface roughness increased greatly. The morphology was less ordered, with pits and valleys randomly distributed. This suggests an increasingly active and less directionally selective etching activity at intermediate times, possibly from enhanced local reactivity and exposure of grain boundary or defect sites. The topographic depth in this instance was more than 1000 nm, indicating extensive dissolution of the material.

Additional etching for 30 min (Fig. 3c) produced a reemergence of directionally aligned grooves like those at 10 min, but with much greater surface relief. The valleys were wider and deeper, and vertical topographic variations of about 1800 nm existed. This indicates that extended etching increases anisotropy, either by microstructural domains or by preferential etching along crystal directions.

These results demonstrate that the etching time is the most important in determining the surface roughness and morphology of SSFs. Short etching times produce relatively smooth and aligned morphologies, while intermediate times achieve maximum surface roughness by isotropic corrosion. Long etching times restore structural alignment while increasing surface relief, with probable morphologies that can be adjusted.

Quantitative comparison of surface roughness (Ra) values derived from AFM analysis of SSF treated with HCl for varying durations: (a) bare SSF, (b) 10-min-treated SSF, (c) 20-min-treated SSF, and (d) 30-min-treated SSF.

The lowest Ra = 0.115 µm is in the case of a bare SSF with a relatively smooth and flat surface. A moderately developed roughness of 0.158 µm was achieved for the foil after 10 min of HCl treatment, indicating the appearance of microtextured feature growth.

There is a greater increase in surface roughness after 20 min of exposure, Ra = 0.333 µm. This is the start of a more severe topographic change and it is caused by more extensive corrosion processes or pit growth. The trend continues for the treated sample for up to 30 min, with the highest surface roughness of 0.403 µm. This phase would then be anticipated to be similar to that of deeper grooves and rougher surfaces, as suggested by the AFM images presented earlier.

In general, the findings demonstrate a definite correlation between etching time and surface roughness, considering the degree to which prolonged exposure to HCl can be used as a technique of topography control of stainless steel surfaces for certain functional applications.

The wettability of SSFs etched with 1 M HCl for varying durations was evaluated by contact angle measurements, as shown in Fig. 4. The bare SSF (Fig. 4a) exhibited a high contact angle, indicating poor wettability and a relatively hydrophobic surface. After 10 min of etching (Fig. 4b), the contact angle decreased slightly, showing surface activation and increased surface energy. The contact angle was further reduced for the etched SSF of 20 min (Fig. 4c), allowing the droplet to spread much more, showing enhanced hydrophilicity. After etching for 30 min (Fig. 4d), the contact angle was significantly reduced, indicating high hydrophilicity and excellent wettability.

This is because of the greater surface roughness and elimination of surface oxides or contaminations, enhancing the adhesion of the electrode slurry and facilitating better contact with the electrolyte. A better wettability of the current collector is beneficial for a uniform coating of the slurry and results in better electrochemical performance.

Figure 5 shows charge–discharge curves and cycling performance of LiFePO4 electrodes prepared on smooth and etched SSFs and tested at a 0.2C rate. As shown in Fig. 5a, all samples exhibit typical flat voltage plateaus, which are typical of the two-phase reaction between FePO4 and LiFePO4 corresponding to the Fe2+/Fe3+ redox couple.Fig. 5The charge–discharge profiles (a) and cycling performance (b) of bare and longer-treated samples of SSF treated with HCl for varying durations at 0.2C.

The charge–discharge profiles (a) and cycling performance (b) of bare and longer-treated samples of SSF treated with HCl for varying durations at 0.2C.

Among the tested samples, the electrode of the 10-min etched SSF yields the highest specific capacity of about 130 mAh g−1, much higher than that of the electrodes with bare SSF, and treated SSFs at 20 or 30-min. The bare SSF sample yields the lowest capacity and etching for more than 10 min seems to negatively affect the electrochemical performance.

This trend is also reflected in the cycling performance data in Fig. 5b, where the 10-min etched SSF electrode has a better initial capacitance and a further better capacitance retention after 30 cycles than the rest of the samples. In contrast, the 20- and 30-min etched SSFs exhibit a lower capacitance and relatively poorer cycling stability, which could be caused by excessive surface roughness or corrosion due to too long acid treatment.

To highlight the competitiveness of our study, Table S1 (Supplementary Material) summarizes representative performance values of state-of-the-art aqueous Li-ion, Zn-ion and Na-ion systems from the literature56–60. Despite extensive data present for various electrode and electrolyte combinations, studies employing stainless steel as a current collector is relatively limited, which makes direct comparison more challenging. Nevertheless, the observed cycling stability of our system is within the range of other studies.

The higher pore structure and surface area induced by etching in the 10-min etched SSFs are responsible for the superior performance due to a series of favorable dramatic changes induced by the weak acid treatment.

These findings demonstrate that a 10-min acid etching treatment optimizes the surface properties of stainless-steel current collectors to produce significantly enhanced electrochemical performance. Beyond optimal etching time, there is over etching, which reduces performance gains and demonstrates that control over surface modification conditions is required.

In the bare SSF cell (Fig. 6a), the charge–discharge plateaus show noticeably larger polarization, reflecting higher internal resistance and slower charge-transfer kinetics. By contrast, the 10-min etched SSF (Fig. 6b) exhibits narrower potential gaps between charge and discharge profiles, indicating enhanced interfacial contact and improved electronic conductivity of the current collector. The cyclic-voltammetry (CV) profiles of the hybrid Zn/Li pouch cell, scanned at 0.1 V s−1 (Fig. 6c), reveal distinct redox peaks centered at 1.1 V (Li+ extraction) and 1.3 V (Li+ insertion). These peaks correspond to the Fe2+/Fe3+ couple within the olivine framework of LiFePO4, confirming that lithium intercalation/deintercalation proceeds reversibly without structural degradation. The overlapping CV curves across successive cycles indicate excellent electrochemical stability and structural integrity of the active material during repeated cycles.

Long-term cycling data for the 10-min etched sample (Fig. 6d) demonstrate highly stable performance and nearly 100% coulombic efficiency throughout the 0.5C test. The specific capacity remains close to 130 mAh g−1 after prolonged cycling, further confirming the benefits of moderate acid treatment in reducing contact resistance and enhancing electrolyte wetting at the current-collector interface. Excess etching (≥ 20 min) was found to diminish these advantages by introducing deep corrosion pits that increased impedance and reduced mechanical stability.

Overall, the combination of improved surface wettability, improved electron pathways, and stronger mechanical anchoring in the 10-min-etched SSF results in superior rate capability and cycling durability, validating its suitability as an optimized current collector for aqueous hybrid Zn/Li-ion batteries.

Conclusion

Acid etching with 1 M HCl is a simple and cost-effective method to modify the surface properties of SSFs. Corrosion pits were observed even at short exposure times when the foils were treated in acidic solution at 80 °C for 10, 20, and 30 min. After 30 min of etching, the pits widened and began to coalesce, resulting in partial removal of the foil surface and uneven etching morphology.

When applied as current collectors in aqueous rechargeable batteries, the etched SSFs significantly enhanced the performance of electrical double-layer capacitors operating in an aqueous electrolyte. The voltage profiles demonstrated increased polarization for the etched SSF cells, reflecting altered electrode–electrolyte interfacial properties. Among all samples, the battery produced from the 10-min etched SSF demonstrated better electrochemical performance than the pristine and treated samples at 20 and 30 min.

The measured contact angle curve as a function of etching time showed that the contact angle decreases very rapidly, i.e., the wettability of the surface increases. It helps in spreading the slurry and in embedding the electrodes for fabrication to produce more uniform coatings and better current collection.

Since the SSF is passivated by oxides (e.g., mainly Cr2O3 and Fe2O3), the passive oxide layer would hinder electron transfer. Slight etching of this barrier layer removes some areas of this dielectric layer and improves the transport property of the interface.

The increase in roughness from the 10-min etching enhances the effective contact area between the electrode and current collector. More extreme roughening (e.g., with 20- and 30-min treatments) can lead to irregularities, poor mechanical contact, or micro-pitting that can be undesirable to performance.

When integrated into hybrid Zn/Li-ion cells, the 10 min etched SSF delivered the highest specific capacity (~ 130 mAh g−1), superior rate capability, and nearly 100% coulombic efficiency, confirming the beneficial role of moderate etching in improving interface kinetics and reducing polarization. The successful assembly and operation of the 2 Ah/6 V aqueous hybrid Zn/Li-ion pouch-cell module also shows that the system can be used in real life.

The enhanced wettability promotes uniform slurry distribution and firm adhesion of the LiFePO4 cathode, while partial removal of passive Cr2O3/Fe2O3 layers facilitates faster electron transfer across the interface.

The findings confirm that the 1 M HCl etching route provides an easily scalable surface-engineering strategy to produce corrosion-resistant, conductive, and hydrophilic current collectors. Due to its simplicity, reproducibility, and compatibility with roll-to-roll processing, this method is particularly suitable for large-scale fabrication and pouch-cell assembly in future aqueous hybrid energy storage systems.
