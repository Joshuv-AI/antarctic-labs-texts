Serum Concentration Relationships of Candesartan in Migraine Prevention
Authors: Hedda Skogum Riise, Melanie Simpson, Ketil Arne Espnes, Erling Andreas Tronvik, Andreas Kattem Husøy, Lise R Øie, Anker Stubberud, Emil Kofoed, Aurora Harsvik Alstad, Hedda Hoff Hagen, Maren Guldteig Lien, Marie Larsen Stavseng, Ella Rian Sæther, Tore Wergeland
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTMigraine is a common disabling disorder, and optimising affordable preventive treatments remains highly relevant. Candesartan is an effective and well‐tolerated preventive treatment, but little is known about its dose‐concentration–response relationships. We explored these relationships using data from the MigriNor study, a prospective observational cohort of adults with episodic or chronic migraine initiating oral preventive treatment. A total of 166 participants using candesartan were included (median age 35 years; 89% female). Participants completed a daily electronic headache diary during a 4‐week baseline and 12‐week follow‐up period with accompanying blood sampling. Dosing regimen was flexible. No differences in adverse drug reactions (ADRs) or treatment acceptability were observed between participants starting 4 versus 8 mg. Substantial interindividual variability in serum concentrations was observed. Higher serum concentrations were associated with lower odds of headache attacks (OR = 0.97, 95% CI = 0.96–0.98, p < 0.001) and migraine attacks (OR = 0.98, 95% CI = 0.97–0.99, p = 0.005). Interquartile ranges of serum concentrations among ≥ 30% and ≥ 50% responders were 62.9–141.2 and 62.3–129.3 nmol/L, respectively, suggesting preliminary therapeutic reference ranges. Serum concentration was not associated with ADRs or treatment acceptability. Interpretation is limited by the flexible dosing design, and studies with fixed dosing regimens are warranted.Keywords: candesartan, migraine, pharmacokinetics, prevention, therapeutic drug monitoring

Introduction

Migraine is a highly prevalent neurological disorder, consistently ranking among the leading causes of health loss worldwide [1]. In addition to drugs used for acute symptom relief during migraine attacks, several preventive treatment options are available. In Norway, oral preventive drugs, except for small‐molecule calcitonin gene‐related peptide (CGRP) receptor inhibitors (gepants), are used as first‐line treatment. Nonmigraine‐specific oral preventive medications belong to multiple pharmacological classes and were originally developed for indications other than migraine prevention. The choice of preventive treatment is individualised based on clinical considerations and patient‐specific factors [2]. One such option is candesartan, an angiotensin II‐receptor blocker primarily used in antihypertensive treatment. Candesartan has demonstrated effect and favourable tolerability in both randomised controlled trials (RCTs) [3, 4, 5] and observational studies [6, 7].

In recent years, drugs targeting the CGRP system have emerged as effective and well‐tolerated preventive treatment options for migraine [8, 9]. However, high costs limit their accessibility for many patients. This underscores the need to further optimise existing, affordable oral preventive treatments to improve efficacy while minimising adverse effects.

One possible strategy to improve and individualise treatment is therapeutic drug monitoring (TDM). TDM has several applications in neuropsychopharmacology [10] and is well established in Norway [11]. TDM offers several benefits, as it may improve adherence and effect, as well as reduce adverse effects [10]. In epilepsy care, TDM is an integrated part of follow‐up in both the outpatient and the emergency department settings [10]. However, its role in migraine prevention has not been explored. Studies exploring migraine‐specific dose‐concentration–response relationships, an essential prerequisite for TDM, are lacking for several migraine preventive drugs, including candesartan.

Candesartan is administered orally as the prodrug candesartan cilexetil, which is almost completely metabolised to the active compound candesartan during absorption in the intestinal wall [12]. The drug undergoes limited hepatic metabolism, with only a small fraction being converted to an inactive metabolite via CYP2C9 [13]. Oral bioavailability is approximately 14% for standard tablet formulations [13]. Peak plasma concentrations (T‐max) are typically reached within 3.6–4.3 h after intake, and the elimination half‐life is approximately 8–13 h in adults [14, 15, 16]. Candesartan demonstrates linear pharmacokinetics [13] with low pharmacokinetic variability [17]. Elimination occurs predominantly as unchanged drug, with approximately 67% faecal and 33% renal excretion [12, 16].

The MigriNor study was designed to improve the understanding of pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics in a real‐world cohort of patients using oral preventive migraine treatment. The present paper specifically investigates dose–concentration, concentration–effect and concentration–side effect relationships for candesartan in migraine prevention. In addition, dose titration patterns and differences in effectiveness, adherence and adverse drug reaction (ADR) occurrence between 4‐ and 8‐mg starting doses were explored.

Materials and Methods

The data presented in this paper were collected as part of the MigriNor study, a prospective observational study on patients starting oral preventive migraine treatment. The study was conducted from May 2021 to June 2024 at St. Olavs University Hospital, Trondheim, Norway. An overview of the study, the study sample and overall outcomes have been published separately [7]. The study was conducted in accordance with the Basic and Clinical Pharmacology and Toxicology policy for experimental and clinical studies [18]. Reporting of the study follows the STROBE guidelines for observational studies [19].

Participants and Recruitment

Participants had to have a migraine diagnosis according to the International Classification of Headache Disorders Third Edition (ICHD‐3) [20], including chronic migraine, and an a priori decision to initiate oral preventive migraine treatment made before study inclusion. Participants were excluded only if they were considered unable to comply with the study protocol. No restrictions were applied regarding previous preventive treatment failures or use of acute migraine medication. Nationwide recruitment was enabled by remote solutions. All oral preventive migraine drugs with available serum concentration measurements were included: valproic acid, topiramate, propranolol, metoprolol, candesartan, lisinopril, amitriptyline and venlafaxine. Participants were allowed to try multiple drugs during the study in cases of insufficient effectiveness or intolerable side effects, without repeating the baseline period. The present analyses include only participants who used candesartan.

No formal sample size calculation was performed because of the observational design of the study, see previously published article for details [7].

Study Outline and Follow‐Up

A 4‐week baseline period was required prior to starting the preventive treatment. Participants were then followed for 12 weeks with telephone consultations with study personnel at weeks 4 and 8, and an appointment with a neurologist at week 12. Treatment decisions were based on individual clinical needs and were independent of study participation. A neurologist determined the recommended starting dose and dosing regimen prior to inclusion, based on an individual assessment of the patient. Data on the rationale for the choice of preventive treatment and starting dose and clinical measurements obtained during these assessments (e.g., blood pressure) were not recorded for study purposes. Several neurologists were involved in the treatment initiation process. Dosing regimens were flexible, allowing participants to adjust their candesartan dose at any time within limits defined by the neurologist. Participants who changed preventive medication for any reason, including insufficient effectiveness or tolerability concerns, could start a new 12‐week follow‐up period without repeating the baseline period. No washout period was required between consecutive treatment trials. All participants who used candesartan as a preventive treatment for migraine during the study were included in the present analyses, regardless of whether candesartan was their first or a subsequent migraine preventive treatment. Only treatment periods in which candesartan was used as the sole migraine preventive medication were included in the analyses.

Headache Diary and Effectiveness Outcomes

Participants were required to complete a daily electronic headache diary using a smartphone application throughout the entire study period. The diary collected information on the presence of any headache and details on intensity (on a numerical rating scale 1–10), duration, medication intake (both acute symptomatic and preventive treatment) and whether the participant recognised the headache as a migraine attack. A headache day was defined as any day on which the participant reported the presence of headache. A moderate‐to‐severe headache day was defined as a headache with a maximum pain intensity of ≥ 4 and a duration of at least 4 h or a headache of any intensity that resulted in the use of acute rescue medication (including paracetamol, NSAIDs, triptans or other analgesics). A migraine day was defined as a headache day on which the participant identified migraine symptoms and registered it as a migraine day. Headache outcomes were defined in accordance with the current International Headache Society guidelines [21, 22].

Responder groups were defined as participants achieving a ≥ 30% or ≥ 50% reduction in moderate‐to‐severe headache days from baseline to weeks 9–12. Additionally, we defined two groups of sustained responders. Sustained responders were participants who achieved at least ≥ 30% or ≥ 50% reduction in moderate‐to‐severe headache days from baseline during any 4‐week period after treatment initiation, and who maintained this level of response in all subsequent overlapping 4‐week periods. This was generated by shifting the window forward by 1 week at a time, through the end of the 12‐week follow‐up.

Adverse Events and Acceptability

Information on adverse events (AEs) was collected at weeks 4, 8 and 12 of the follow‐up period and in response to participant inquiries. AEs were assessed in terms of severity and relationship to candesartan and coded according to the Medical Dictionary for Regulatory Activities version 26.1. AEs with a possible, probable or related relationship to candesartan were defined as ADRs. The dose level at which the participant first reported the AE was registered. The acceptability of candesartan was collected at 12 weeks or prior in case of discontinuation of treatment. Acceptability included a 0–10 grading of how much negative impact the side effects had on the quality of life and a grading of how acceptable the participant found the side effects when comparing them to the experienced effect on a 7‐point Likert scale from ‘to a very large extent unacceptable’ to ‘to a very large extent acceptable.’

Blood Sampling and Serum Concentration Analyses

Blood samples for serum concentration analysis were collected approximately 4, 8 and 12 weeks after treatment initiation. Participants were instructed to schedule blood sampling shortly before the next scheduled dose (i.e., in the drug‐fasting state) and not within the first week after a dose adjustment. Information on current dose, time of last dose intake and time of blood sampling was collected. Blood samples were collected either at the hospital laboratory's ambulatory unit or at the participant's general practitioner's office and subsequently sent to the laboratory for analysis. All serum concentration analyses were performed by the Department of Clinical Pharmacology at St. Olavs Hospital, Trondheim. Serum concentrations of candesartan were analysed on Waters Xevo TQS‐MS instruments with an ultra‐performance liquid chromatography tandem mass spectrometry (UHPLC‐MSMS) method developed in our laboratory [23]. The quantifying ion (m/z) used was 441.1 > 263.1 m/z. The internal standard was deuterated candesartan (candesartan‐d5).

The method was validated following standards and guidelines ISO/IEC 17025 [24], NA Dok. Nr. 48a [25], SOFT/AAFS Forensic Laboratory Guidelines [26] and FDA's guidance for industry bioanalytical method validation [27]. The method is accredited by Norsk Akkreditering (Norwegian Accreditation [NA]), the national accreditation body of Norway, established by the Royal Norwegian Ministry of Trade and Fisheries in accordance with EU Regulation 765. During the timespan of the study, the assay was improved and adjusted, but all modifications were cross‐validated. The analytical result area reported for candesartan was 5–1000 nmol/L. For statistical analyses, candesartan values below the lower level of reporting were set to 2.5 nmol/L.

A valid blood sample was predefined as one obtained within an acceptable time interval of 10–30 h after the last dose intake, with a minimum interval of five half‐lives of candesartan (approximately 2 days) since the last dose change. To reduce variability, a post hoc adjustment to a standardised interval of 16 h between the last dose intake and blood sampling was performed according to the method in the study by Espnes et al. [28]. This standardisation was done by using the most frequently cited elimination half‐life (9 h) of the drug and assuming first‐order elimination kinetics. Concentrations measured later than 16 h were adjusted upward, whereas concentrations measured earlier were adjusted downward to estimate the concentration expected at 16 h after dose for each sample.

The following assumptions were made prior to statistical analysis. Firstly, a measured serum concentration for a given dose was assumed to be representative of all the days on which that dose was taken. Furthermore, the mean concentration/dose‐ratio (CDR) for each participant was used to estimate the serum concentration on days with a dose level for which no corresponding measurement was available. The CDR was calculated by dividing the serum concentration of candesartan (nmol/L) by the daily dose (mg) and corresponds to the serum concentration (in nmol/L) per mg of candesartan administered daily. Finally, if a participant had multiple serum concentration measurements for the same dose, the mean CDR for that dose was used for estimating the serum concentration.

Statistical Analysis

The study was conducted according to a prespecified protocol and followed a statistical analysis plan (SAP). Assumptions for the preplanned models used were checked and adjustments made accordingly, if appropriate. A significance level of 0.05 was used for all analyses. The analyses were performed using STATA 19.5 (StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX, USA). Effectiveness analyses were based on available eDiary data without imputation.

Comparisons between participants with a starting dose of 4 and 8 mg were done using t‐tests, Cochran–Mantel–Haenszel (CMH) test and a mixed‐effects logistic regression (for comparing effectiveness). The operationalised definition of chronic migraine is previously described [7].

An overview of the distribution of serum concentration percentiles at dose levels with a minimum of 10 blood samples is presented. Within‐ and between‐individual variation was analysed using a components‐of‐variance model to estimate the coefficient of variance (CV). Correlation between dose and serum concentration and between dose and CDR was analysed with a mixed‐effects linear regression model. Comparison of CDR between subgroups were done using Mann–Whitney U tests and a Kruskal–Wallis test. The CDR can be compared directly within and between individuals without taking dose variations into account, by assuming linear pharmacokinetics. All statistical analyses of relationships with serum concentration were performed using serum concentration data adjusted to the standardised time interval. Sensitivity analyses included nonstandardised valid serum concentration measurements and total serum concentration measurements, that is, an analysis including those samples collected outside the predefined time interval after the last dose intake and too close to the most recent dose change.

A mixed‐effects logistic regression model was used to analyse effectiveness and serum concentration relationships by estimating the probability of having a headache on a single day given the serum concentration of candesartan. Analysis restricted to moderate‐to‐severe headache days and migraine days was also performed, and repeated with adjustments for sex, age and body mass index (BMI) as confounding factors. As predefined in the SAP, these analyses were also performed for the ≥ 30% responder group.

We present the median serum concentration with corresponding interquartile ranges (IQRs) for ≥ 30% and ≥ 50% responders and nonresponders as preliminary therapeutic reference ranges (TRRs) [29]. Both serum concentrations from the whole treatment period and a post hoc sensitivity analysis restricted to serum concentrations collected during the first 6 weeks of treatment are presented. The median serum concentrations with corresponding IQRs in the first week the sustained response was achieved are presented as an additional sensitivity analysis.

A logistic regression model was used to establish the relationship between serum concentration and the occurrence of ADRs in the 12‐week period, as the assumptions of pre‐planned linear regression were violated. We also present a Kaplan–Meier plot showing the ADR‐free survival over increasing serum concentration (instead of time) to explore the relationship between serum concentration and the occurrence of ADRs.

The relationship between the overall acceptability and the highest serum concentration obtained was done using a logistic regression model, only dividing between the acceptable and unacceptable categories of the scale. The relationship between serum concentration and the negative impact of experienced side effects on quality of life was first analysed with linear regression, but assumptions for the model were not fulfilled. Therefore, a generalised ordered logistic regression model was used.

Ethics

The study was approved by a regional ethics committee for Medical and Health Research Ethics (REC, nr:142619). Informed, written consent was collected from all participants prior to inclusion.

One hundred sixty‐six participants started on candesartan during their study participation and were included in the analyses. Of these, 147 (88.6%) were females, the median age was 35 and the median BMI was 25.5 kg/m2 [7].

Theoretically, the CDR should remain constant across different doses of candesartan; however, we found that the CDR varied based on the dose. For example, the CDR at 32 mg was 3.4 nmol/L/mg lower than at 8 mg (95% CI = 1.9 to 4.9). There was no statistically significant difference in CDR between age groups (over and under 45 years) (p = 0.81) and sexes (p = 0.69). The mean CDR was higher in normal‐weight participants (BMI 18–25; n = 47) than in overweight participants (BMI > 25; n = 48) (7.4 [4.9] vs. 5.4 [4.0], p = 0.0128).

CV associated with within‐ and between‐participant variability was calculated for the three dose levels with the highest number of samples; 16, 24 and 32 mg. At 16 mg (n = 98) and 24 mg (n = 37), between‐participant variability was higher (CV = 61% and CV = 40%, respectively) than the within‐participant variability (CV = 38% and CV = 26%, respectively). At 32 mg (n = 61), the between‐participant CV (33%) was lower than the within‐participant CV (58%).

Effectiveness and Serum Concentration Relationships

Among all participants, serum concentration was significantly associated with the odds of headache and migraine days, but not with moderate‐to‐severe headache days (Table 4). A similar pattern was found for all three outcomes in the ≥ 30% responder group. The odds ratio (OR) for having headache on any particular day was 0.96 (95% CI = [0.95 to 0.98], p < 0.001) for every 10 nmol/L increase in candesartan serum concentration in the responder group. Adjustment for age, sex and BMI revealed similar results (Table 4).

The Serum Concentration Levels Among Responders

Median serum concentrations among ≥ 30% and ≥ 50% responders were 95.0 nmol/L (IQR = 62.9–141.2) and 83.0 nmol/L (IQR = 62.3–129.3), respectively. In the sensitivity analysis restricted to serum concentrations collected during the first 6 weeks, the median serum concentration was 74.1 nmol/L (IQR = 50.6–111.4) among ≥ 30% responders and 74.1 nmol/L (IQR = 59.7–104.4) among ≥ 50% responders. Table S3 presents the median serum concentration with corresponding IQRs for ≥ 30% and ≥ 50% responders and nonresponders at different dose levels.

Among sustained responders, the median serum concentration in the first week of achieved response was 83.1 (IQR = 53.3–141.5) nmol/L for ≥ 30% sustained responders and 81.8 (56.3–128.0) nmol/L for ≥ 50% sustained responders.

Adverse Events and Serum Concentration Relationships

The five most common ADRs associated with candesartan were orthostatic dizziness (n = 64), dizziness (n = 48), fatigue (n = 24), headache (n = 14) and nausea (n = 12). Figure S1 shows a graphic illustration of the dose level and corresponding serum concentration level at which these five ADRs occurred.

There was no statistically significant association between serum concentration and the occurrence of ADRs, with an OR of 0.94 per 10 nmol/L increase (95% CI = 0.88 to 1.00, p = 0.066). The Kaplan–Meier analysis (Figure 2) showed that 50% of participants had experienced an ADR at a serum concentration of 93.1 nmol/L. Among the 19 participants discontinuing candesartan before the 12‐week mark due to side effects [7], 6 provided valid blood samples. The median maximum serum concentration obtained from these participants was 63.3 nmol/L (IQR = 55.4–147.5 nmol/L). The median serum concentration associated with the occurrence of ADRs with moderate severity was 84.4 nmol/L with a corresponding IQR = 75.0–179.6 nmol/L (n = 11).

There was no correlation between the highest serum concentration obtained and the overall acceptability (p = 0.710). Discussion

In this real‐world cohort, higher candesartan serum concentrations were associated with lower odds of headache and migraine days, representing a potential concentration‐response relationship in migraine prevention. The MigriNor study provides novel data on dose titration patterns and serum concentrations in clinical practice, and to our knowledge, it is the first to explore these relationships. Owing to the observational design and flexible dosing regimen, the findings are subject to confounding and potential bias. Nevertheless, they offer clinically relevant insights and support further research on these relationships.

RCTs have demonstrated the efficacy of both 8‐ and 16‐mg candesartan, with 16 mg found to be nonsuperior to 8 mg [5, 30]. The efficacy and tolerability of doses higher than 16 mg in migraine prevention have not been explored in RCTs, nor has dose titration. The flexible dosing regimen in our study, which allowed participants to aim for the lowest effective or highest tolerable dose, resulted in great variability in dosing and in many cases, higher levels than RCTs would suggest. No benefits in tolerability or acceptability of side effects from using a lower starting dose of 4 mg were observed, and there was no difference in effectiveness compared to those starting on 8 mg.

The median serum concentration levels observed were within the established reference range for candesartan and were consistent with the expected serum concentrations found in healthy volunteers [30]. However, substantial variability in serum concentrations and CDRs was observed, even after exclusion of invalid samples and the standardisation of the time interval between last dose intake and blood sampling. At the 16‐ and 24‐mg dose levels, most of the variability occurred between participants rather than within participants, with particularly pronounced between‐participant variability at 16 mg. Such a pattern indicates that CDRs may vary considerably between individuals, while remaining relatively stable within individuals.

We did not observe significant differences in CDRs between age groups, despite the known influence of age on pharmacokinetics [14]. This is likely attributed to the age distribution among participants in our study, with a median age of 35 years and few elderly participants. The predefined age cutoff of 45 years was chosen to ensure sufficient sample sizes within each age group, but is probably too low to capture age‐related pharmacokinetic differences, which typically become more prominent in older age. No significant differences in CDRs were observed between sexes, in line with existing knowledge on sex‐related differences in the pharmacokinetics for candesartan [14]. However, the study population was predominantly female, limiting the interpretability of this finding. Significantly higher CDRs were observed among participants with normal BMI compared with those with a higher BMI; however, this difference remains unexplained given the known pharmacokinetics including low distribution volume of candesartan. A potential explanation for the observed variability between individuals is CYP2C9 polymorphism. Approximately 6.5% of the European population carries a genetic variant associated with absent enzyme activity [31, 32], resulting in decreased metabolism of candesartan and, consequently, higher serum concentrations. Pharmacogenetic testing was not performed as part of the present study and should be considered in future investigations.

At the 32‐mg dose level, the variability was predominantly within participants. This finding should be interpreted cautiously given the small sample size (n = 37) and the likelihood that participants who titrated to 32 mg represent a selected subgroup. As shown in Figure 1, participants who reached 32 mg already exhibited lower serum concentrations at 8 mg than the rest, and these participants were probably more likely to be encouraged to increase the dose. Such bias may also explain why the expected linear relationship between dose and serum concentration appeared less consistent at higher dose levels and why differences in CDR were observed between 8 and 32 mg [33]. Differences in treatment adherence between dose levels may also contribute to this, as the 32‐mg dose was typically achieved later in follow‐up, with adherence generally declining over time.

There was a significant relationship between serum concentration and effectiveness, with increasing serum concentrations associated with lower odds of having headache or migraine on a given day. Based on the responder group results, a 50 nmol/L increase in serum concentration was associated with 18% lower odds of experiencing a headache day, while a 100 nmol/L increase was associated with 34% lower odds, indicating potentially clinically meaningful effects. This suggests that continued dose titration may be beneficial in selected participants, when treatment is otherwise well tolerated. Furthermore, the relationship is likely underestimated because of the flexible dosing regimen [34] and the fact that a greater proportion of nonresponders reached higher dose levels (> 16 mg) and thus higher serum concentrations. The same mechanism may also partly explain why the relationship for moderate‐to‐severe headache days did not reach statistical significance.

The interquartile ranges among ≥ 30% and ≥ 50% responders were presented as potential TRRs, which in our data were 62.9–141.2 and 62.3–129.3 nmol/L, respectively. Sensitivity analyses restricted to blood samples obtained during the first 6 weeks of treatment, when dose‐related bias was less likely to influence serum concentrations, showed largely overlapping IQRs, supporting the robustness of these findings. However, such IQR‐based TRRs should be considered preliminary [29]. Establishing a clear concentration threshold for treatment effectiveness using ROC analyses or concentration–effect curves was not feasible due to bias arising from the observational nature and flexible dosing design of our study. Future studies using fixed‐dose designs are needed to conduct such analyses and establish a TRR.

No significant associations were observed between serum concentration of candesartan and the occurrence of ADRs or the acceptability of side effects. However, participants appeared to avoid further increasing the dose or even discontinuing treatment when they experienced ADRs. Ideally, the TRR upper serum concentration limit should be defined by the risk of ADRs. Our post hoc Kaplan–Meier analysis found that 50% of participants had experienced side effects at a serum concentration of 93 nmol/L. However, the majority of participants experienced at least one ADR, most reactions being mild in severity [7]. Importantly, the Kaplan–Meier analysis did not differentiate between ADRs and included reactions that were mild, transient and/or acceptable to the participants. Among participants who discontinued treatment due to side effects, only a few provided valid blood samples. Their serum concentration levels overlapped with the range observed among responders. The relationship between serum concentration and ADRs of candesartan remains inconclusive.

At present, these findings are not sufficient to support routine measurement of candesartan serum concentrations in migraine prevention. However, they support a clinical practice of individual dose titration according to treatment response and tolerability. While RCTs have primarily demonstrated efficacy at predefined doses such as 8 or 16 mg, our findings support a more individualised approach, although this has not been specifically evaluated in RCTs. In addition, the observed concentration–effect relationship provides a rationale for further investigation of serum concentration measurements as a tool to optimise candesartan treatment in migraine. If a reliable TRR can be established, serum concentration measurements could potentially provide additional information in selected clinical situations, for example by identifying patients with insufficient treatment response and low serum concentrations who might benefit from further dose escalation. The lack of a clear concentration–ADR relationship in the present study means that an upper concentration limit based on tolerability cannot currently be defined, and this aspect requires further study. According to the proposed stepwise approach for establishing TRRs, further studies with fixed dosing regimens are first needed to better define the concentration–effect relationship and establish a robust TRR, followed by prospective, randomised and controlled studies evaluating whether TDM‐guided treatment improves clinical outcomes compared with standard care.

Strengths and Limitations

The MigriNor study was conducted as a prospective observational study with analyses following a prespecified SAP, enhancing the methodological quality of the study. The comprehensive, prospective data collection from a relatively large number of participants provides valuable insights.

Several important limitations should be acknowledged. First, the observational design inherently introduces the possibility of confounding. Furthermore, the flexible dosing regimen likely resulted in bias as previously discussed, influencing which participants reached higher and lower doses and thus, higher and lower serum concentrations. Studies with flexible dosing are not suitable for disproving relationships between serum concentrations and treatment response or to conclude that TDM is not useful [29, 34]. Conversely, any observed relationship is likely to be real but conservatively estimated. Even though we adjusted for relevant confounders and performed several sensitivity analyses to reduce bias and improve the robustness of our findings, the interpretability remains limited. The study was conducted prior to the recent publication of recommendations from a TDM task force on the determination of TRRs [29]; however, we have sought to adhere to their stepwise methodological approach as closely as possible without deviating from the prespecified SAP.

Analyses of concentration–effect relationships were partly based on calculated serum concentrations, assuming a relatively stable concentration–dose relationship within individuals during periods of unchanged dosing. This approach was used to better align with the day‐level diary data, as daily blood sampling was not feasible. Although the sensitivity analysis restricted to measured concentrations suggests that the use of calculated concentrations did not substantially bias the estimates, the method introduces uncertainty. Migraine days were self‐identified by participants and were not independently validated against IHS criteria [21, 22]. Additionally, no washout periods were implemented between consecutive preventive treatment trials, which may have introduced carry‐over effects. Furthermore, the low proportion of male participants impairs the generalisability of the findings. Because of the nature of the study design, the starting dose was not randomised and imbalances in participant characteristics between the groups cannot be excluded. However, the groups were comparable in terms of age, BMI and the proportion having chronic migraine. Blood pressure data were not collected at study inclusion, and it remains unknown whether blood pressure measurements influenced clinical decision‐making before study entry. However, routine blood pressure assessment is not considered a prerequisite for initiation of candesartan in migraine prevention [5].

Conclusions

The MigriNor study provides novel real‐world data on dose titration patterns and serum concentration relationships of candesartan in migraine prevention. No significant differences in ADRs or acceptability were observed between starting doses of 4 and 8 mg, suggesting that an 8‐mg starting dose may be preferable. Substantial interindividual variability in concentration–dose relationships supports the need for establishing a reliable therapeutic reference range (TRR). Higher serum concentrations were associated with lower odds of both headache and migraine days, although these associations may have been underestimated because of the flexible dosing design. Based on the IQRs among participants achieving ≥ 30% and ≥ 50% reductions in moderate‐to‐severe headache days, preliminary TRRs of 62.9–141.2 and 62.3–129.3 nmol/L, respectively, are proposed. Further studies, preferably using a fixed‐dose design, are needed to establish a robust TRR and subsequently evaluate the role of TDM in optimising candesartan treatment for migraine prevention.
