Invariant Extremal Projections for Operator-Ordered Families
Philip Kennerberg

Abstract
We study an extremal projection principle for families of operators ordered by domination, induced by fixed bounded linear mappings acting on a source with an additive baseline. Stability is defined through domination of second–order structure, leading to a covariance envelope of admissible sources ordered by the Löwner relation. Our main result establishes an envelope extremal principle: the maximal value of the quadratic functional over the entire envelope coincides with that of a single extremal configuration, which may lie only in the closure of the admissible class. This identification is obtained without convexity, compactness, or any global Hilbert space structure governing all components of the system, and relies instead on an operator–theoretic approximation scheme. As a consequence, minimax optimization over stability sets reduces to an ordinary quadratic minimization problem with well–posed existence and uniqueness properties for the associated minimizing operators. Structural properties of covariance envelopes are also derived, including density, closure, and spectral characterizations in stationary settings.

Introduction

Classical extremal principles in analysis describe how canonical objects arise
from order or invariance constraints imposed on quadratic forms.
A paradigmatic example is the Courant–Fischer characterization of eigenvalues

3
;
2
, which identifies extremal Rayleigh quotients of a
fixed self–adjoint operator over vectors or finite–dimensional subspaces.
More generally, operator inequalities in the Löwner order provide a framework
for comparing quadratic forms and understanding how domination relations propagate
through linear mappings
(
1

,
5
.

In variational analysis and elliptic PDE, related extremal mechanisms appear through
coercivity and Gårding–type inequalities, which ensure stability of quadratic
forms under perturbations
4
;
6
.
In a different direction, robust formulations in optimization consider worst–case
behavior over admissible families of covariance operators, often leading to minimax
problems under compactness or convexity assumptions
7

The present work fits into this landscape by identifying invariant extremal projections induced by operator domination, without relying on spectral structure,
convexity, or compactness.

We now state the main extremal principle in an informal form, highlighting the structural content of the result before presenting the precise formulation.

We emphasize that the extremal principle does not introduce a new projection, but shows that the invariant (worst–case) projection problem reduces to an ordinary quadratic minimization of the same form as in the non–invariant setting.

The envelope extremal principle identifies a canonical invariant projection

When specialized to the minimization problem over admissible operators, this mechanism yields a projection principle that extends the classical Rayleigh–Ritz paradigm beyond the Hilbertian setting.
Unlike classical orthogonal projections, the extremal projections considered here are

Remark 1.2

(On the canonical nature of the deviation functional)

The functional

This framework identifies a canonical invariant projection induced by the representation
operator, and extends classical extremal principles for quadratic forms to
families of operators ordered by domination.

All quantities entering the extremal principle depend only on second–order
structure.
In particular, the joint covariance of
(Y^{A},X^{A})
can be represented as a
block operator

\Sigma_{(Y^{A},X^{A})}=\begin{pmatrix}\Sigma_{A}^{YY}&\Sigma_{A}^{YX}\\

where

This block structure provides the operator–theoretic framework underlying both
the envelope comparison and the associated projection problem.

From a structural point of view, the result identifies an invariant extremal
projection induced by operator domination.
In contrast to classical extremal principles such as Courant–Fischer, where
quadratic forms of a fixed operator are optimized over subspaces, the present
framework optimizes over nonconvex, nonclosed families of operators ordered by
domination, yet still yields an explicit extremal representative.

The defining domination condition may be viewed as a functional–analytic
generalization of coercivity principles: rather than controlling a single
bilinear form under perturbations, the analysis compares entire families of
second–order operators through a uniform order relation.
This perspective places the envelope extremal principle within a broader theory
of extremal projections governed by operator order.

In addition to establishing the envelope extremal principle itself, we derive structural properties of the covariance envelopes (closure, dense-subset characterizations, and Fourier-analytic descriptions in the wide-sense stationary case), and we prove necessary and sufficient conditions for the existence and uniqueness of minimizers of the extremal functional. With the minimization we obtain an order–theoretic Rayleigh–Ritz analogue in our generalized setting.

Relation to operator order and extremal projections

The framework developed here admits a natural interpretation in terms of operator order
and extremal projections.
The stability sets
C_{\mathcal{A}}(A)
are defined through Loewner domination of
second–order operators and give rise to an envelope that is generally neither convex
nor closed.
The envelope extremal principle identifies a canonical extremal element within
this order structure, allowing worst–case behavior over the entire envelope to
be captured by a single representative.

From this perspective, the resulting minimax problem does not lead to a saddle–point
analysis, but instead reduces to the construction of an invariant projection induced by
the underlying linear operator.

This places the present work closer to extremal problems for ordered operator families
than to classical minimax formulations relying on convexity or compactness.

Remark 1.3
.

Operator domination under fixed linear mappings also appears in other areas of analysis,
notably in robust control and minimax formulations where uncertainty sets are defined
through covariance or operator order constraints; see, e.g.,
7
and
references therein.
In those settings, however, extremal behavior is typically analyzed through convexity,
compactness, or saddle–point arguments.
The present work differs in that it identifies a canonical extremal projection directly
from operator domination alone, without relying on convexity, compactness, or
problem–specific structure.

Challenges presented

The extremal problem studied in this paper departs in several fundamental ways
from classical quadratic minimax and Rayleigh–Ritz settings.

(i)

Envelope defined by operator domination.

The admissible class
C_{\mathcal{A}}(A)
is defined through a Löwner-type
inequality on
block covariance operators
associated with source elements
ranging over a prescribed set
\mathcal{A}
.
Such envelopes are in general neither convex nor closed and need not contain
an extremal element; the extremal configuration identified by the envelope
extremal principle typically lies only in the closure of the admissible class.
As a result, standard compactness, convexity, or arguments relying on the
existence of an extremizer within the admissible class are unavailable.

(ii)

Dual-pairing structure of the auxiliary block.

Even when
V_{2}=H^{*}
, the interaction between Hilbert–Schmidt operators and auxiliary components
is governed by the dual pairing
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{{\mathsf{H}},{\mathsf{H}}^{*}}
rather than by
a Hilbert inner product on a single ambient space. Consequently, classical Hilbert-space shortcuts based on global orthogonality,
basis-free Parseval identities, or spectral decompositions of the full block operator
are unavailable.

(iii)

Non-centered sources and failure of trace representations.

The sources are not assumed to be centered.
Without centering, quadratic deviation functionals do not reduce to purely covariance-based
expressions.
Even in a fully Hilbertian setting, the presence of mean terms destroys the identity

\int_{\Omega}\big\langle A^{\prime}(\omega),\,MA^{\prime}(\omega)\big\rangle_{\mathbb{H}_{o}^{d}}\,d\mu(\omega)=\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_{A^{\prime}}M),

so neither spectral dominance nor Rayleigh–Ritz arguments apply.
Moreover, operator domination of covariances alone does not control mixed source-baseline component terms.

(iv)

Uniform extremality across Hilbert–Schmidt operators.

The envelope extremal principle holds
for every admissible Hilbert–Schmidt operator

T\in V_{1}
, not merely for a distinguished minimizing operator.
This precludes approaches based on first characterizing a single minimizer
and instead necessitates a uniform comparison mechanism at the level of quadratic
forms.

Remark 1.4

(Why spectral and operator-theoretic shortcuts do not apply)
.

Several standard shortcuts from Hilbert-space spectral theory are unavailable
in the present framework.

•

No global Hilbert structure.

The quadratic deviation is naturally defined on

L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{H}_{o})
, while the auxiliary components interact with
Hilbert–Schmidt operators through the dual pairing with
{\mathsf{H}}^{*}
.
There is no single Hilbert space carrying all components of the model,
and hence no global spectral theorem or Parseval identity to exploit.

•

Löwner order is insufficient.

A domination constraint of the form
\Sigma_{A^{\prime}}\preceq\Sigma_{A}

controls only second-order covariance blocks.
It does not control mean contributions or mixed source–baseline terms,
and therefore does not imply trace identities or vanishing cross terms.
These properties must be established directly.

•

Absence of joint diagonalization.

The covariance blocks generated by
\widetilde{\mathcal{S}}

do not commute and need not admit a common eigenbasis.
Spectral calculus for commuting operator families is therefore inapplicable.

•

Quadratic dependence on the random source.

The relevant covariance objects depend
quadratically
on the random element
A
,
through expressions of the form

\Sigma_{A}=\int_{\Omega}(\widetilde{\mathcal{S}}A)\otimes(\widetilde{\mathcal{S}}A)\,d\mu,

rather than linearly on
A
itself.
Consequently, worst–case behavior cannot be read off directly from operator
domination relations.
In particular, a constraint of the form
\Sigma_{A^{\prime}}\preceq\Sigma_{A}

does not identify an extremal source nor reduce the problem to a spectral comparison.
This necessitates the finite–dimensional coefficient compressions
and the controlled limiting arguments developed in the proof of the extremal envelope principle.

These obstacles necessitate the approximation strategy developed in this paper:
finite-dimensional coefficient models obtained via weak operator compressions,
combined with Bochner measurability, explicit second-order expansions,
and carefully controlled limits.

Envelope extremal principle

For
A^{\prime}\in\mathcal{V}
, define the
joint environment covariance operator

\Sigma_{A^{\prime}}:\mathbb{H}_{o}^{d}\to\mathbb{H}_{o}^{d}
as follows.
For
1\leq i,j\leq d
, let

\Sigma_{ij}:=\int_{\Omega}A^{\prime}(i)\otimes A^{\prime}(j)\,d\mu\;:\;\mathbb{H}_{o}\to\mathbb{H}_{o},

where
(x\otimes y)u:=\langle u,y\rangle_{\mathbb{H}_{o}}\,x
.
The operator
\Sigma_{A^{\prime}}
is then defined by the block representation

\Sigma_{A^{\prime}}:=\begin{bmatrix}\Sigma_{11}&\cdots&\Sigma_{1,d}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\Sigma_{d,1}&\cdots&\Sigma_{d,d}\end{bmatrix}.

(2.1)

Given
A\in\mathcal{V}
and
T\in\mathcal{HS}({\mathsf{H}}^{*},\mathbb{H}_{o})

cost functional
(denoted

throughout):

\displaystyle R_{A}(T):=\int_{\Omega}\left\lVert Y^{A}-T\left(X^{A}\right)\right\rVert_{\mathbb{H}_{o}}^{2}d\mu

By linearity and boundedness of

\mathcal{S}

\int_{\Omega}\left\lVert T(X^{A})\right\rVert_{\mathbb{H}_{o}}^{2}d\mu\leq\left\lVert T\right\rVert^{2}\left\lVert J\right\rVert^{2}\left\lVert\mathcal{S}\right\rVert^{2}\left(\left\lVert A\right\rVert_{\mathcal{V}}+\left\lVert\xi^{A}\right\rVert_{\mathcal{V}}\right)^{2},

and since

is finite and well-defined.

For a given source
A

Definition 2.1
.

Stability set.

Let

be a source and

\displaystyle C_{\mathcal{A}}(A)=\Big\{A^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}:\langle\mathbf{g},\Sigma_{A^{\prime}}\mathbf{g}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}_{o}^{d}}\leq\langle\mathbf{g},\Sigma_{A}\mathbf{g}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}_{o}^{d}},\quad\forall g\in\mathbb{H}_{o}^{d}\Big\}.

This definition is the natural multivariate analogue of Mercer–type positivity

.

.

None of the structural characterizations preceding the envelope extremal principle,
such as spectral or stationarity-based descriptions of the envelope,

\displaystyle C_{\mathcal{A}}(A)=\Big\{A^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}:\langle\mathbf{g},\Sigma_{A^{\prime}}\mathbf{g}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}_{o}^{d}}\leq\langle\mathbf{g},\Sigma_{A}\mathbf{g}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}_{o}^{d}}\forall g\in\mathcal{G}\Big\}.

\mathcal{H}:=L^{2}([t_{1},t_{2}];\,H^{-1}(U)),\qquad\mathbb{H}_{o}:=L^{2}([t_{1},t_{2}];{\mathbb{R}}),\qquad\mathcal{Y}:=\mathbb{H}_{o}^{d}.

Let

A(i)(t):=(Lf_{t})_{i}=\langle Gf_{t},\psi_{i}\rangle,\qquad A^{\prime}(i)(t):=(Lf^{\prime}_{t})_{i}=\langle Gf^{\prime}_{t},\psi_{i}\rangle,\qquad 1\leq i\leq d,

Thus the covariance operators of

transfers through the bounded linear map
L=CG

Stationary subclass (restriction to

\mu={\mathbb{P}}

Proposition 2.8

(Wide-sense stationary restrictions)
.

Assume that each

We now state the main structural result, the

which shows that the supremum of the cost functional over the stability set is
attained at a canonical extremal element determined by the covariance envelope.

Theorem 2.9
.

and

then

\sup_{A^{\prime}\in C_{\mathcal{A}}(A)}R_{A^{\prime}}(T)\;=\;R_{A}(T),\qquad\forall\,T\in V_{1}.

The extremal value depends only on the closure of the admissible class: replacing

by

.

If

and

Remark 2.11

.

Although our extremal principle is formulated for linear representation operators,
a local analogue can also be formulated for non-linear
\mathcal{S}
by

Under suitable smoothness assumptions, the worst–case cost reduces

left for future work.

2.1
Broad overview of the Proof of Theorem
2.9
(Envelope extremal principle)

The proof of the envelope extremal principle does not follow from a standard
variational or compactness argument.
Instead, it relies on a sequence of structural reductions at the level of
second–order operators, together with an approximation scheme that allows the
operator order to be lifted through the underlying linear operator.
For the reader’s convenience, we first outline the main ideas of the argument
before presenting the full proof.

The first step is to expand the target
Y^{A}
in an orthonormal basis

\{\phi_{k}\}_{k}
of
\mathbb{H}_{o}
, and to use that fact that
\mathcal{HS}({\mathsf{H}}^{*},\mathbb{H}_{o})\cong\mathbb{H}_{o}\widehat{\otimes}{\mathsf{H}}
to identify an element
\beta_{T}\in\mathbb{H}_{o}\widehat{\otimes}{\mathsf{H}}
through the canonical isometric isomorphism. We can represent

\beta
in the product basis
\{\phi_{k}\otimes\psi_{l}\}_{k,l}
,
where
\{\psi_{l}\}_{l}
is an orthonormal basis of
{\mathsf{H}}
.
Next, the dual pairing between
T
and
X^{A}
is expanded in the

\{\phi_{k}\}
–basis, so that the entire deviation is expressed consistently
in terms of these coordinates.
After a sequence of preparatory expansions and limiting arguments, this yields
an explicit expansion of the cost functional in terms of random coefficients alone.

The next step is to approximate the infinite–dimensional representation
by finite–dimensional truncations. Concretely, we introduce matrices
B^{n}

encoding the action of finite–rank truncations of
\tilde{\mathcal{S}}

with respect to compatible truncations in the chosen orthonormal bases of
the underlying Hilbert spaces.
Since
\tilde{\mathcal{S}}
is not compact, these finite–rank truncations cannot
converge to
\tilde{\mathcal{S}}
in operator norm; instead they only converge
strongly (pointwise), and the resulting approximation error must be controlled
carefully in the subsequent steps.

Next, the finite–dimensional approximation is substituted into the expansion
of the cost functional, and the pointwise error is controlled by a detailed
estimate. This reduction yields a finite–dimensional quadratic form expressed
in terms of the Fourier coefficients of the input sources together with the
corresponding baseline component terms.
In the resulting quadratic form we show that the cross–term between baseline component
and input source vanishes, while the pure baseline component contribution is invariant.
This structure becomes crucial when establishing the continuity of the cost functional with respect to the source element.
Owing to the preceding expansions, we can separate the dependence on the source and the baseline component.
The orthogonality (in
L^{2}(\Omega)
) of the mixed terms associated with the source and the baseline component, together with the fixed second–order structure of the latter, implies that differences of cost functionals depend only on source–dependent components. This is the decisive step: it removes all baseline dependence and allows the envelope
comparison to be carried out entirely at the level of source covariances. As a consequence, the continuity argument proceeds via the finite–dimensional approximation apparatus developed earlier. Once we have established that the cost-functional supremum is invariant under the baseline component, we see that the supremum actually can be expressed in terms of quadratic forms in the source coefficients (with baseline contributions canceling/invariant). At this stage we are then ready to tackle the optimization problem over the envelope and we break down this final step into several sub steps.

(a)

Finite approximants inside the envelope.

Fix
\Delta>0
. Using the continuity property established in the previous step, pick
A_{\Delta}\in C_{\mathcal{A}}(A)
with

\mathrm{dist}(A_{\Delta},\,A)\leq\Delta
.
Construct finite sets
C_{m}\subset C_{\mathcal{A}}(A)
such that
(i)
\max_{A^{\prime\prime}\in C_{m}}R_{A^{\prime\prime}}(T)\to\sup_{A^{\prime}\in C_{\mathcal{A}}(A)}R_{A^{\prime}}(T)
as
m\to\infty
, and
(ii)
A_{\Delta}\in C_{m}
for all
m
. Since each
C_{m}
is finite, the convergences used below are uniform over
C_{m}
.
A crucial insight is that the envelope inequality, though defined in infinite dimension, can be transferred to compressed covariance operators, thereby allowing the rest of our machinery to apply.

(b)

Finite-dimensional compression of covariance operators.

Let
P_{N}:\mathbb{H}_{o}\to\mathbb{H}_{o}
be the orthogonal projection onto

\mathsf{span}\{\phi_{1},\ldots,\phi_{N}\}
and extend it componentwise to

\mathbb{H}_{o}^{d}
by

\mathbf{P}_{N}:=\mathrm{diag}(P_{N},\ldots,P_{N})\,:\,\mathbb{H}_{o}^{d}\to\mathbb{H}_{o}^{d}.

For each
A^{\prime\prime}\in\overline{\mathcal{A}}
define the compressed covariance operator by

\Sigma^{(N)}_{A^{\prime\prime}}:=\mathbf{P}_{N}\,\Sigma_{A^{\prime\prime}}\,\mathbf{P}_{N}.

Then for every
\mathbf{g}\in(\mathsf{span}\{\phi_{1},\ldots,\phi_{N}\})^{d}
we have

\langle\mathbf{g},\Sigma_{A^{\prime\prime}}\mathbf{g}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}_{o}^{d}}=\langle\mathbf{g},\Sigma^{(N)}_{A^{\prime\prime}}\mathbf{g}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}_{o}^{d}},

since
\mathbf{P}_{N}\mathbf{g}=\mathbf{g}
. Hence the compression does not alter the
quadratic form on the finite-dimensional test space.

(c)

Transfer of the envelope inequality to the truncated level.

By the envelope definition,

\langle\Sigma_{A^{\prime\prime}}\mathbf{g},\mathbf{g}\rangle\leq\,\langle\Sigma_{A}\mathbf{g},\mathbf{g}\rangle
for all
\mathbf{g}
.
For
\mathbf{g}\in\mathsf{span}\{\phi_{1},\ldots,\phi_{N}\}
the identities in (b) give

\langle\Sigma^{(N)}_{A^{\prime\prime}}\mathbf{g},\mathbf{g}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}_{o}^{d}}=\langle\Sigma_{A^{\prime\prime}}\mathbf{g},\mathbf{g}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}_{o}^{d}}\leq\,\langle\Sigma_{A}\mathbf{g},\mathbf{g}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}_{o}^{d}}=\,\langle\Sigma_{A}^{(N)}\mathbf{g},\mathbf{g}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}_{o}^{d}},

i.e. the envelope condition
passes
to the compressed covariance operators on the finite subspace.

(d)

Upper bound via truncation and limits.

Applying (c) with the carefully chosen
specific test functions

\mathbf{g}

that encode the finite expansion of
R(\cdot)
(depending on coefficients
v_{n}(T,B^{n})
constructed in earlier steps ),
we obtain

\max_{A^{\prime\prime}\in C_{m}}R_{A^{\prime\prime}}^{(N)}(T)\;\leq\;\,R_{A}^{(N)}(T).

Here the choice of
\mathbf{g}
is crucial: it depends on the coefficients of
\beta_{T}
and
the projection matrices
B^{n}
, so that the compressed quadratic form
matches the truncated cost functional.
Letting
N\to\infty
and using the convergence mechanism established in Step 3,
together with uniformity over the finite set
C_{m}
, we then obtain

\max_{A^{\prime\prime}\in C_{m}}R_{A^{\prime\prime}}(T)\;\leq\;R_{A}(T)+\Delta.

(e)

Lower bound via a fixed near–candidate and passage
\Delta\to 0
.

Since
A_{\Delta}\in C_{m}
for all
m
,

\max_{A^{\prime\prime}\in C_{m}}R_{A^{\prime\prime}}(T)\;\geq\;R_{A_{\Delta}}(T).

Letting
m\to\infty
gives

\sup_{A^{\prime}\in C_{\mathcal{A}}(A)}R_{A^{\prime}}(T)\geq R_{A_{\Delta}}(T)
.
Finally, by continuity property of the cost functional and
\mathrm{dist}(A_{\Delta},A)\leq\Delta
,

R_{A_{\Delta}}(T)\to R_{A}(T)
as
\Delta\to 0
.
Combining with (d) yields

\sup_{A^{\prime}\in C_{\mathcal{A}}(A)}R_{A^{\prime}}(T)\;=\;R_{A}(T).

3
Envelope extremal minimization

We study the variational problem of minimizing the
extremal (envelope) cost

\arg\min_{T\in\mathcal{HS}({\mathsf{H}}^{*},\mathbb{H}_{o})}\ \sup_{A^{\prime}\in C_{\mathcal{A}}(A)}R_{A^{\prime}}(T),

where
\arg\min
denotes the (possibly empty) set of minimizers. Our goal is to characterize this set
and to give conditions for non-emptiness and uniqueness. We refer to this problem as

envelope extremal minimization
.

As for the significance of this minimization, note that it promotes the envelope principle
from a mere
value characterization
to the identification of a
canonical operator
.
For the extremal covariance configuration identified by the envelope principle, any minimizer

T^{\ast}
is optimal against all admissible sources and depends only on the covariance envelope.
When the minimizer is unique, it identifies those directions of the
observation block
\tilde{\mathcal{S}}
that are determined by the dual pairing; non-uniqueness
exposes directions that remain undetermined. Our first result characterizes the minimizer
in an operator-theoretic manner, through normal equations.

Fix
A\in\mathcal{V}
and let
\mathcal{H}=L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{H}_{o})
. Define the bounded linear operator

\Gamma:\ \mathcal{HS}({\mathsf{H}}^{*},\mathbb{H}_{o})\longrightarrow\mathcal{H},\qquad\Gamma T:=T(X^{A}).

With this notation,

R_{A}(T)\ =\ \left\lVert\Gamma T-Y^{A}\right\rVert_{\mathcal{H}}^{2},\qquad T\in\mathcal{HS}({\mathsf{H}}^{*},\mathbb{H}_{o}).

Write
\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{H}}:\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}^{\ast}
for the Riesz isomorphism,
\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{H}}(u)(v)=\langle u,v\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}
, and let
\Gamma^{\ast}:\mathcal{H}^{\ast}\to\mathcal{HS}({\mathsf{H}}^{*},\mathbb{H}_{o})^{\ast}
be the (Banach) adjoint,
\Gamma^{\ast}\ell:=\ell\circ\Gamma
. Set

C_{XX}\ :=\ \Gamma^{\ast}\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{H}}\Gamma\ \in\mathcal{L}(V_{1},(V_{1})^{\ast}),\qquad C_{XY}\ :=\ \Gamma^{\ast}\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{H}}Y^{A}\ \in(V_{1})^{\ast}.

Theorem 3.1

(Extremal cost minimizer)
.

(i)

First-order optimality / normal equation.

There exists at least one solution
T^{*}\in V_{1}
,

T^{*}=\arg\min_{T\in V_{1}}\sup_{A^{\prime}\in C_{\mathcal{A}}(A)}R_{A^{\prime}}(T),

(3.3)

if and only if

C_{XX}T^{\ast}=C_{XY}\quad\text{in }(V_{1})^{\ast}.

(3.4)

Equivalently, a minimizer exists iff
C_{XY}\in\operatorname{ran}(C_{XX})\subset(V_{1})^{\ast}
.

(ii)

Structure/uniqueness.

If
T_{0}
is any solution of
C_{XX}T=C_{XY}
, then the set of all minimizers is the affine space

T_{0}+\operatorname{ker}(C_{XX}).

(3.5)

Uniqueness holds precisely when
\operatorname{ker}(\Gamma)=\{0\}
(equivalently, when
C_{XX}
is injective).

If we now write the cost functional as

R_{A}(T)\;=\;C_{A}\ -\ 2\,\mathcal{L}_{A}(T)\ +\ \mathcal{Q}_{A}(T,T),\qquad T\in V_{1},

where
C_{A}=\int_{\Omega}\lVert Y^{A}\rVert_{\mathbb{H}_{o}}^{2}d\mu
,

\ \mathcal{L}_{A}:V_{1}\to{\mathbb{R}}
is the continuous linear functional

\mathcal{L}_{A}(T)\;:=\;\!\int_{\Omega}\langle Y^{A},T(X^{A})\rangle_{\mathbb{H}_{o}}d\mu,

and
\ \mathcal{Q}_{A}:V_{1}\times V_{1}\to{\mathbb{R}}
is the continuous, symmetric, positive semidefinite bilinear form

\mathcal{Q}_{A}(T_{1},T_{2})\;:=\int_{\Omega}\langle T_{1}(X^{A}),T_{2}(X^{A})\rangle_{\mathbb{H}_{o}}d\mu.

To obtain an explicit representation of the normal equation, we now pass to
coordinates.
Using the canonical isometric isomorphism

V_{1}=\mathcal{HS}({\mathsf{H}}^{*},\mathbb{H}_{o})\;\cong\;\mathbb{H}_{o}\widehat{\otimes}{\mathsf{H}},

we identify each
T\in V_{1}
with its coefficient vector

v=(v_{k,\ell})_{k,\ell}\in\ell^{2}
relative to fixed orthonormal bases.
Let
\{\phi_{k}\}_{k\geq 1}
be an ON–basis of
\mathbb{H}_{o}
and
\{\psi_{\ell}\}_{\ell\geq 1}
an ON–basis of
{\mathsf{H}}
.
With respect to the chosen bases, the coefficients are given by

v_{k,\ell}=\langle T(\psi_{\ell}),\phi_{k}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}_{o}}.

We also define

b_{k,\ell}\ :=\int_{\Omega}\left\langle Y^{A}\,,\phi_{k}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{H}_{o}}\langle\psi_{\ell},X^{A}\rangle_{{\mathsf{H}},{\mathsf{H}}^{*}}d\mu,

together with the positive semidefinite operator
\Sigma:\ell^{2}\to\ell^{2}
with entries

\Sigma^{(k,\ell)\,(k^{\prime},\ell^{\prime})}\ :=\delta_{k,k^{\prime}}\int_{\Omega}\langle\psi_{\ell},X^{A}\rangle_{{\mathsf{H}},{\mathsf{H}}^{*}}\langle\psi_{\ell^{\prime}},X^{A}\rangle_{{\mathsf{H}},{\mathsf{H}}^{*}}d\mu,

here the infinite matrix
\Sigma
acts on vectors in
\ell^{2}
through left-multiplication. This operator represents the coordinate form of
C_{XX}
under the above identification.

While Theorem
3.1
characterizes the minimizer directly as a
Hilbert–Schmidt operator
T\in\mathcal{HS}({\mathsf{H}}^{*},\mathbb{H}_{o})
through an
operator-valued optimality condition, the following corollary provides a
complementary and more explicit description.
Under coercivity, the minimizer exists uniquely and admits a representation in
any fixed orthonormal basis, with Hilbert–Schmidt summability of the resulting
coefficient array. In particular, the associated coordinate expansion converges
unconditionally in
V_{1}
, yielding a concrete realization of the abstract
operator solution.
In the non-coercive case, the same representation persists upon replacing the
inverse by the Moore-Penrose pseudoinverse of the covariance operator. In this
sense, Corollary
3.2
does not introduce a new minimization
principle, but rather explicates the operator-theoretic solution of
Theorem
3.1
in a stable coordinate form.

Corollary 3.2

(Coordinate form and summability for the minimizer)
.

(i)
(Existence/uniqueness via coercivity).

If there exists
c>0
such that

\mathcal{Q}_{A}(T,T)\geq c\lVert T\rVert_{V_{1}}^{2}\qquad\forall T\in V_{1}

(coercivity of the
XX

T^{*}\in V_{1}
,

T^{*}=\arg\min_{T\in V_{1}}\sup_{A^{\prime}\in C_{\mathcal{A}}(A)}R_{A^{\prime}}(T),

characterized by the normal equation

\mathcal{Q}_{A}(T_{A}^{\star},\eta)\ =\ \mathcal{L}_{A}(\eta)\qquad\forall\,\eta\in V_{1},

and with the bound
\lVert T_{A}^{\star}\rVert_{V_{1}}\leq c^{-1}\lVert\mathcal{L}_{A}\rVert_{(V_{1})^{*}}
.

(ii)
(Coordinate form and summability).

Under the coercivity assumption above (which is equivalent to
\Sigma\succeq c\,I
on
\ell^{2}
),
the unique minimizer has coefficient vector
v^{\star}=\Sigma^{-1}b\in\ell^{2}
, hence

\sum_{k,\ell\geq 1}\big|v_{k,\ell}\big|^{2}\ <\ \infty.

Moreover, for each
x\in{\mathsf{H}}^{*}
,

T^{*}_{A}(x)=\sum_{k,\ell\geq 1}v_{k,\ell}\,\langle\psi_{\ell},x\rangle_{{\mathsf{H}},{\mathsf{H}}^{*}}\,\phi_{k},

where the series converges in
\mathbb{H}_{o}
.

(Degenerate case). If coercivity fails but
L_{A}
belongs to the range of the operator induced
by
Q_{A}
, then minimizers exist and are characterized by the normal equation above; among them, the minimal

\,|\,\cdot\,|\,_{V_{1}}
–norm solution corresponds to the
\ell^{2}
–minimal solution
v^{\star}=\Sigma^{\dagger}b

(Moore–Penrose) and satisfies
v^{\star}\in\ell^{2}
.

We now present two applications of the envelope extremal principle.
The first concerns an elliptic boundary–value problem, while the second
addresses a stochastic convolution system.
In both cases, the envelope
C_{\mathcal{A}}(A)
encodes admissible uncertainty
at the level of second-order source structure, rather than in the representation
operator itself.
The extremal elements of the envelope correspond to the most adverse
admissible source configurations, i.e. those that maximize the quadratic cost functional.
The envelope extremal principle reduces the worst–case analysis to a single
extremal covariance structure.
For this extremal configuration, the optimal predictor is a unique
Hilbert–Schmidt operator
T^{\star}\in\mathcal{HS}({\mathsf{H}}^{*},\mathbb{H}_{o})
.
In this sense, uncertainty in the source is transformed into an explicit
variational characterization of the limiting adversary and of the uniquely
optimal operator acting against it.
This operator–theoretic perspective is the key mechanism behind the
decomposition principle.

In the first example we illustrate a deterministic PDE setting,
where the baseline component is absent and the envelope reflects
pure structural uncertainty in the source family.

Example 3.3

(Elliptic Dirichlet system with parameter–averaged quadratic energy and fixed operator)
.

Setting.

Let
\mathsf{D}\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}

Let

Fix

For

By boundedness of

\lVert g_{j}(\omega,\cdot)\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathsf{D})}=\lVert\rho_{\delta}\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{m})}

\lVert F_{A}(\omega,\cdot)\rVert_{\mathbb{H}_{o}}\leq\lVert\ell\rVert_{H^{-1}}\lVert G\rVert_{L^{2}\to H^{1}_{0}}\lVert\rho_{\delta}\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{m})}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\lVert u^{A}_{j}(\omega,\cdot)\rVert_{\mathbb{H}_{o}}.

Let
{\mathsf{H}}:=H^{r}([t_{1},t_{2}])

).
Fix a bounded linear aggregation operator

\Psi:\mathbb{H}_{o}^{d}\longrightarrow V_{2},\qquad\Psi(U_{1},\dots,U_{d}):=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\alpha_{j}\,\Phi_{j}(U_{j}),

where
\Phi_{j}:\mathbb{H}_{o}\to V_{2}
are fixed bounded linear maps and

\alpha_{j}\in\mathbb{R}
.

Define the fixed linear representation operator

\mathcal{S}:\mathbb{H}_{o}^{d}\longrightarrow\mathbb{H}_{o}\times V_{2},\qquad\mathcal{S}A:=\big(\mathcal{S}_{1}A,\ \mathcal{S}_{2}A\big),

by

\mathcal{S}_{1}A:=F_{A},\qquad\mathcal{S}_{2}A:=\Psi(A).

Set
\tilde{\mathcal{S}}:=(\mathcal{S}_{1},\,J\mathcal{S}_{2})
so that

\tilde{\mathcal{S}}:\mathbb{H}_{o}^{d}\to\mathbb{H}_{o}\times{\mathsf{H}}^{*}
.

For each source
A
define

\big(Y^{A}(\omega,\cdot),\,X^{A}(\omega,\cdot)\big):=\tilde{\mathcal{S}}\big(A(\omega,\cdot)\big),

so that
Y^{A}(\omega,\cdot)\in\mathbb{H}_{o}
and
X^{A}(\omega,\cdot)\in{\mathsf{H}}^{*}
.

Cost functional.

For
T\in\mathcal{HS}({\mathsf{H}}^{*},\mathbb{H}_{o})
define

R_{A}(T):=\int_{\Omega}\lVert\,Y^{A}(\omega,\cdot)-T\!\big(X^{A}(\omega,\cdot)\big)\,\rVert_{\mathbb{H}_{o}}^{2}\,d\mu(\omega).

Second–order operators.

Define the covariance operator of
A
on
\mathbb{H}_{o}^{d}
by

\Sigma_{A}:=\int_{\Omega}A(\omega,\cdot)\otimes A(\omega,\cdot)\,d\mu(\omega).

The induced second–order operators for
(Y^{A},X^{A})
are

K^{YY}_{A}:=\int_{\Omega}Y^{A}\otimes Y^{A}\,d\mu,\qquad K^{YX}_{A}:=\int_{\Omega}Y^{A}\otimes X^{A}\,d\mu,\qquad K^{XX}_{A}:=\int_{\Omega}X^{A}\otimes X^{A}\,d\mu,

where
(y\otimes x)h:=\langle h,x\rangle_{{\mathsf{H}},{\mathsf{H}}^{*}}y
.

Envelope class and extremal principle.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be a class of admissible sources and let
A\in\overline{\mathcal{A}}
.
Define the envelope

C_{\mathcal{A}}(A):=\big\{A^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}:\ \Sigma_{A}-\Sigma_{A^{\prime}}\succeq 0\ \text{on }\mathbb{H}_{o}^{d}\big\}.

By the envelope extremal principle,

\sup_{A^{\prime}\in C_{\mathcal{A}}(A)}R_{A^{\prime}}(T)=R_{A}(T)\qquad\text{for every }T\in\mathcal{HS}({\mathsf{H}}^{*},\mathbb{H}_{o}).

Coordinate optimizer.

Applying Cor.
3.2
to
(Y^{A},X^{A})
yields the normal equation

K^{XX}_{A}\,T^{*}=K^{XY}_{A},\qquad K^{XY}_{A}:=(K^{YX}_{A})^{*},

and hence the canonical minimizer

T^{*}=\big(K^{XX}_{A}\big)^{\dagger}K^{XY}_{A},

with uniqueness if and only if
\ker K^{XX}_{A}=\{0\}
.

Remark 3.4
.

Example
3.3
fits directly into the
(\tilde{\mathcal{S}},V_{2}\hookrightarrow{\mathsf{H}}^{*})
framework with a fixed representation operator:
all admissible variability enters through the source coefficients
U_{A}
, while the envelope condition is imposed as a Loewner domination

K^{U}_{A}-K^{U}_{A^{\prime}}\succeq 0
on
\mathbb{H}_{o}^{d}
.
No compactness, kernel representations, or spectral diagonalization is required.
The coordinate corollary reduces the abstract minimizer to the concrete normal equation

K_{A}^{XX}T^{*}=K_{A}^{XY}
and the pseudoinverse formula above.

Example 3.5

(LTI system)
.

Setting.

Let
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mu)
be as in the previous example and assume

\mathbb{H}_{o}=L^{2}([t_{1},t_{2}])
.
Let
{\mathsf{H}}
be a real separable Hilbert space and let

V_{2}
be a normed space with a continuous embedding

J:V_{2}\hookrightarrow{\mathsf{H}}^{*}
.

LTI representation operator.

Fix impulse responses
h,\phi\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
and define convolution on
\mathbb{R}
by

(h*u)(t):=\int_{\mathbb{R}}h(t-s)u(s)\,ds,\qquad(\phi*u)(t):=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\phi(t-s)u(s)\,ds.

Let
\Psi:\mathbb{H}_{o}\to V_{2}
be a fixed bounded linear map.
Define the representation operator

\mathcal{S}:\ \mathbb{H}_{o}^{2}\longrightarrow\mathbb{H}_{o}\times V_{2},\qquad U=(U_{1},U_{2})\longmapsto\big(\mathcal{S}_{1}U,\ \mathcal{S}_{2}U\big),

by

\mathcal{S}_{1}U:=(h*U_{1})\big|_{[t_{1},t_{2}]}\in\mathbb{H}_{o},\qquad\mathcal{S}_{2}U:=\Psi\!\left[(\phi*U_{2})\big|_{[t_{1},t_{2}]}\right]\in V_{2}.

Then
\mathcal{S}
is bounded and

\lVert\mathcal{S}_{1}\rVert\leq\lVert h\rVert_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R})},\qquad\lVert\mathcal{S}_{2}\rVert\leq\lVert\Psi\rVert\,\lVert\phi\rVert_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R})}.

Set

\tilde{\mathcal{S}}:=(\mathcal{S}_{1},\ J\mathcal{S}_{2}):\mathbb{H}_{o}^{2}\longrightarrow\mathbb{H}_{o}\times{\mathsf{H}}^{*}.

Sources and observations.

Let
\mathcal{A}\subset\mathcal{V}
be a class of admissible sources.
For each
A\in\mathcal{A}
let
\xi^{A}\in\mathcal{V}
be a baseline component such that

\int_{\Omega}\xi^{A}\otimes\xi^{A}\,d\mu=\Sigma_{\xi},\qquad\int_{\Omega}A\otimes\xi^{A}\,d\mu=0.

Define the observed pair

(Y^{A},X^{A}):=\tilde{\mathcal{S}}(A+\xi^{A}),\qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad Y^{A}=\mathcal{S}_{1}(A+\xi^{A}),\quad X^{A}=J\mathcal{S}_{2}(A+\xi^{A}).

Thus
Y^{A}
is
\mathbb{H}_{o}
–valued and
X^{A}
is
{\mathsf{H}}^{*}
–valued.

Quadratic cost functional.

For
T\in\mathcal{HS}({\mathsf{H}}^{*},\mathbb{H}_{o})
define

R_{A}(T):=\int_{\Omega}\big\lVert Y^{A}-T(X^{A})\big\rVert_{\mathbb{H}_{o}}^{2}\,d\mu.

Envelope stability set.

For
A\in\overline{\mathcal{A}}
define

C_{\mathcal{A}}(A):=\Big\{A^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}:\ \langle\mathbf{g},\Sigma_{A^{\prime}}\mathbf{g}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}_{o}^{2}}\leq\langle\mathbf{g},\Sigma_{A}\mathbf{g}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}_{o}^{2}}\ \text{for all }\mathbf{g}\in\mathbb{H}_{o}^{2}\Big\},

where

\Sigma_{A}:=\int_{\Omega}A\otimes A\,d\mu

is the source covariance operator on
\mathbb{H}_{o}^{2}
.

Remark (Domination transfer through the fixed LTI operator).

In this example the envelope condition is imposed at the level of the source covariance

\Sigma_{A}=\int_{\Omega}A\otimes A\,d\mu
.
Since the representation operator
\tilde{\mathcal{S}}
is fixed and bounded, source-level domination

\Sigma_{A^{\prime}}\preceq\Sigma_{A}
implies domination of the induced environment covariance,

\Sigma_{(Y_{A^{\prime}},X_{A^{\prime}})}=\tilde{\mathcal{S}}\Sigma_{A^{\prime}}\tilde{\mathcal{S}}^{*}\;\preceq\;\tilde{\mathcal{S}}\Sigma_{A}\tilde{\mathcal{S}}^{*}=\Sigma_{(Y_{A},X_{A})}.

Thus the present envelope is a special case of the general covariance envelope
introduced in Section 2, obtained by lifting domination through a fixed linear
representation operator.

Extremal principle.

For every fixed
T\in\mathcal{HS}({\mathsf{H}}^{*},\mathbb{H}_{o})
,

\sup_{A^{\prime}\in C_{\mathcal{A}}(A)}R_{A^{\prime}}(T)=R_{A}(T),

with
\tilde{\mathcal{S}}
fixed as above.

Remark (Role of stationarity).

The envelope extremal principle above holds without any stationarity assumption.
Wide–sense stationarity is introduced only to obtain a diagonal (frequency–domain)
representation of the covariance operators and of the associated minimizer.
No part of the extremal identity

\sup_{A^{\prime}\in C_{\mathcal{A}}(A)}R_{A^{\prime}}(T)=R_{A}(T)

relies on stationarity or spectral representations.

WSS/LTI specialization.

Assume now that
\mu={\mathbb{P}}
and that each
A\in\mathcal{A}
admits a wide–sense stationary
extension to
\mathbb{R}
with matrix–valued covariance kernel

K_{A}(\tau)\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}
.
Then
\Sigma_{A}
is induced by convolution with
K_{A}(\cdot)
, and writing

\widehat{K}_{A}(\omega)
for its Fourier transform,

A^{\prime}\in C_{\mathcal{A}}(A)\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\widehat{K}_{A}(\omega)-\widehat{K}_{A^{\prime}}(\omega)\ \text{is positive semidefinite for a.e.\ }\omega,

cf. Proposition
2.8
.

Frequency–domain covariance blocks (representation).

In the WSS/LTI setting, the second–order structure of
(Y^{A},X^{A})

admits a diagonal representation under the Fourier transform.
Denoting by

\Sigma_{(Y^{A},X^{A})}=\begin{pmatrix}\Sigma_{A}^{YY}&\Sigma_{A}^{YX}\\
\Sigma_{A}^{XY}&\Sigma_{A}^{XX}\end{pmatrix},

the corresponding spectral densities satisfy, for a.e.
\omega
,

\displaystyle\widehat{K}_{A}^{YY}(\omega)

\displaystyle=|H(\omega)|^{2}\,[\widehat{K}_{A}(\omega)]_{11},

\displaystyle\widehat{K}_{A}^{YX}(\omega)

\displaystyle=H(\omega)\,\overline{\Phi(\omega)}\,[\widehat{K}_{A}(\omega)]_{12},

\displaystyle\widehat{K}_{A}^{XX}(\omega)

\displaystyle=|\Phi(\omega)|^{2}\,[\widehat{K}_{A}(\omega)]_{22},

where
H=\widehat{h}
and
\Phi=\widehat{\phi}
.
The baseline contribution enters additively through a fixed spectral density and is
therefore invariant under extremalization over the envelope.

Baseline invariance in the spectral domain.

The baseline component
\xi^{A}
contributes additively to the spectral densities through
a fixed positive semidefinite term determined by
\Sigma_{\xi}
.
Since this contribution is identical for all admissible sources
A^{\prime}\in C_{\mathcal{A}}(A)
,
it cancels out in differences of quadratic costs and plays no role in the extremalization
over the envelope. Consequently, the frequency–domain extremal analysis depends only on
the source–dependent spectral densities.

Coordinate characterization of the minimizer (representation).

Let
A
be extremal and consider the minimization of
R_{A}(T)
over

T\in\mathcal{HS}({\mathsf{H}}^{*},\mathbb{H}_{o})
.
By Corollary
3.2
, the unique minimum–norm minimizer

T^{*}
satisfies the normal equation

K_{A}^{XX}\,T^{*}=K_{A}^{XY},\qquad K_{A}^{XY}:=(K_{A}^{YX})^{*}.

In the WSS/LTI setting, diagonalization by the Fourier transform yields the
pointwise relation

\widehat{K}_{A}^{XX}(\omega)\,\tau(\omega)=\widehat{K}_{A}^{XY}(\omega),\quad\text{for a.e.\ }\omega.

Here
\tau\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
denotes the symbol of the Hilbert–Schmidt operator

\mathcal{F}T^{*}\mathcal{F}^{-1}
.
Equivalently,

\tau(\omega)=\widehat{K}_{A}^{YX}(\omega)\,\big(\widehat{K}_{A}^{XX}(\omega)\big)^{\dagger}\quad\text{for a.e.\ }\omega.

This representation uniquely determines
T^{*}
(up to null sets) under the inverse
Fourier transform, with uniqueness if and only if

\ker\widehat{K}_{A}^{XX}(\omega)=\{0\}
almost everywhere.

Proof of Proposition

Proof.

4.2
Proof of Proposition
2.4

Proof.

Define

C:=\Bigl\{A^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}:\langle\mathbf{g},\Sigma_{A^{\prime}}\mathbf{g}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}_{o}^{d}}\leq\langle\mathbf{g},\Sigma_{A}\mathbf{g}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}_{o}^{d}}\quad\forall\,\mathbf{g}\in\mathcal{G}^{d}\Bigr\}.

Since
\mathcal{G}^{d}\subset\mathbb{H}_{o}^{d}
, we trivially have

C_{\mathcal{A}}(A)\subseteq C
.

To prove the reverse inclusion, let
\mathbf{g}\in\mathbb{H}_{o}^{d}
be arbitrary.
Since
\mathcal{G}
is dense in
\mathbb{H}_{o}
, there exists a sequence

\mathbf{g}_{n}\in\mathcal{G}^{d}
such that

\,|\,\mathbf{g}_{n}-\mathbf{g}\,|\,_{\mathbb{H}_{o}^{d}}\longrightarrow 0.

Fix
A^{\prime}\in C
. By bilinearity of the quadratic form and the triangle inequality,

\displaystyle\big|\langle\mathbf{g},(\Sigma_{A}-\Sigma_{A^{\prime}})\mathbf{g}\rangle-\langle\mathbf{g}_{n},(\Sigma_{A}-\Sigma_{A^{\prime}})\mathbf{g}_{n}\rangle\big|

\displaystyle\leq\big|\langle\mathbf{g}-\mathbf{g}_{n},(\Sigma_{A}-\Sigma_{A^{\prime}})\mathbf{g}\rangle\big|+\big|\langle\mathbf{g}_{n},(\Sigma_{A}-\Sigma_{A^{\prime}})(\mathbf{g}-\mathbf{g}_{n})\rangle\big|

\displaystyle\leq 2\,\,|\,\Sigma_{A}-\Sigma_{A^{\prime}}\,|\,_{\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{H}_{o}^{d})}\,\,|\,\mathbf{g}_{n}-\mathbf{g}\,|\,_{\mathbb{H}_{o}^{d}}\,\,|\,\mathbf{g}\,|\,_{\mathbb{H}_{o}^{d}}.

Since
\Sigma_{A}-\Sigma_{A^{\prime}}
is a bounded operator on
\mathbb{H}_{o}^{d}
,
the right-hand side converges to zero as
n\to\infty
.

By assumption,

\langle\mathbf{g}_{n},(\Sigma_{A}-\Sigma_{A^{\prime}})\mathbf{g}_{n}\rangle\geq 0\quad\text{for all }n,

and hence, passing to the limit,

\langle\mathbf{g},(\Sigma_{A}-\Sigma_{A^{\prime}})\mathbf{g}\rangle=\lim_{n\to\infty}\langle\mathbf{g}_{n},(\Sigma_{A}-\Sigma_{A^{\prime}})\mathbf{g}_{n}\rangle\geq 0.

Since
\mathbf{g}\in\mathbb{H}_{o}^{d}
was arbitrary, this shows

A^{\prime}\in C_{\mathcal{A}}(A)
, and therefore
C\subseteq C_{\mathcal{A}}(A)
. The proof is complete.
∎

Proof of Proposition

Proof.

Denote

C=\Bigl\{A^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}:\ \widehat{K}_{A}(\omega)-\widehat{K}_{A^{\prime}}(\omega)\ \text{is positive semidefinite for Lebesgue-a.e. }\omega\Bigr\}.

and for

\displaystyle\int_{[t_{1},t_{2}]^{2}}g(s)\,K_{i,j}(s-t)\,f(t)\,ds\,dt

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}g(s)\,K_{i,j}(s-t)\,f(t)\,ds\,dt

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}g(s)\,(K_{i,j}*f)(s)\,ds

Proof of Theorem
3.1

Proof.

By Theorem
2.9
,

\sup_{A^{\prime}\in C_{\mathcal{A}}(A)}R_{A^{\prime}}(T)=R_{A}(T).

(4.42)

Let
g:\mathcal{H}\to{\mathbb{R}}
(recall
\mathcal{H}=L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{H}_{o})
) be
g(u):=\lVert u-Y^{A}\rVert_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}
.
Its Fréchet derivative is

g^{\prime}(u)[v]\ =\ 2\,\langle u-Y^{A},v\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}\ =\ 2\,\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{H}}(u-Y^{A})(v),\qquad u,v\in\mathcal{H}.

(4.43)

Since
R_{A}=g\circ\Gamma
and
\Gamma
is bounded linear, the Banach chain rule gives, for
T,h\in V_{1}
,

\displaystyle DR_{A}(T)[h]

\displaystyle=g^{\prime}(\Gamma T)(\Gamma h)

\displaystyle=2\,\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{H}}(\Gamma T-Y^{A})(\Gamma h)

\displaystyle=2\,(\Gamma^{\ast}\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{H}}(\Gamma T-Y^{A}))(h).

(4.44)

Thus
DR_{A}(T)=2\,\Gamma^{\ast}\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{H}}(\Gamma T-Y^{A})\in(V_{1})^{\ast}
.

The necessary first-order condition yields
DR_{A}(T^{\ast})=0
, i.e.

\Gamma^{\ast}\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{H}}(\Gamma T^{\ast}-Y^{A})=0,

(4.45)

equivalently
C_{XX}T^{\ast}=C_{XY}
in
(V_{1})^{\ast}
.
Conversely, if
C_{XX}T^{\ast}=C_{XY}
then for any
h\in V_{1}
,

\displaystyle R_{A}(T^{\ast}+h)-R_{A}(T^{\ast})

\displaystyle=2\,\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{H}}(\Gamma T^{\ast}-Y^{A})(\Gamma h)+\lVert\Gamma h\rVert_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\lVert\Gamma h\rVert_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}\ \geq 0,

(4.46)

so
T^{\ast}
minimizes
R_{A}
.
This proves (i).

For (ii), suppose
T_{1},T_{2}
both solve
C_{XX}T=C_{XY}
.
Then

C_{XX}(T_{1}-T_{2})=0,

(4.47)

so
T_{1}-T_{2}\in\operatorname{ker}(C_{XX})
.
Thus the full solution set is
T_{0}+\operatorname{ker}(C_{XX})
.
Moreover, since
C_{XX}=\Gamma^{\ast}J_{\mathcal{H}}\Gamma
, we have
\ker(C_{XX})=\ker(\Gamma)
.
Hence the minimizer is unique if and only if
\operatorname{ker}(\Gamma)=\{0\}
.
∎

4.6
Proof of Corollary
3.2

Proof of Corollary
3.2
.

We start with proving (i). By Theorem
3.1
,

\min_{T\in V_{1}}\sup_{A^{\prime}\in C_{\mathcal{A}}(A)}R_{A^{\prime}}(T)=\min_{T\in V_{1}}R_{A}(T)

and the minimizer satisfies

C_{XX}T^{*}=C_{XY}
. Note that

Q_{A}(T,T)=\langle v,\Sigma v\rangle_{\ell^{2}},

where
T=\sum_{k,l\geq 1}v_{k,l}\,\phi_{k}\psi_{l}
and therefore
\lVert T\rVert_{V_{1}}=\lVert v\rVert_{\ell^{2}}
. By assumption
Q_{A}(T,T)\geq c\lVert T\rVert_{V_{1}}^{2}
which is equivalent to

\langle v,\Sigma v\rangle_{\ell^{2}}\geq c\lVert v\rVert_{\ell^{2}}^{2},

i.e.
C_{XX}\succeq cI
. Recall that
V_{1}
is a Hilbert space. Define the continuous bilinear form

a(T,\eta):=\mathcal{Q}_{A}(T,\eta),\qquad T,\eta\in V_{1},

and the continuous linear functional

f(\eta):=\mathcal{L}_{A}(\eta),\qquad\eta\in V_{1}.

By hypothesis there exists
c>0
such that

a(T,T)\ \geq\ c\,\lVert T\rVert_{V_{1}}^{2}\qquad\forall\,T\in V_{1},

(4.48)

i.e.
a
is coercive on
V_{1}
.

Existence and uniqueness of a solution to the normal equation.

By the Lax–Milgram theorem, there exists a unique
T_{A}^{\star}\in V_{1}
such that

a(T_{A}^{\star},\eta)\ =\ f(\eta)\qquad\forall\,\eta\in V_{1},

(4.49)

i.e.

\mathcal{Q}_{A}(T_{A}^{\star},\eta)\ =\ \mathcal{L}_{A}(\eta)\qquad\forall\,\eta\in V_{1}.

Moreover, taking
\eta=T_{A}^{\star}
in (
4.49
) and using (
4.48
) yields

c\,\lVert T_{A}^{\star}\rVert_{V_{1}}^{2}\ \leq\ a(T_{A}^{\star},T_{A}^{\star})\ =\ f(T_{A}^{\star})\ \leq\lVert f\rVert_{(V_{1})^{*}}\,\lVert T_{A}^{\star}\rVert_{V_{1}},

hence the a priori bound

\lVert T_{A}^{\star}\rVert_{V_{1}}\ \leq\ c^{-1}\,\lVert f\rVert_{(V_{1})^{*}}\ =\ c^{-1}\,\lVert\mathcal{L}_{A}\rVert_{(V_{1})^{*}}.

(4.50)

Optimality for the quadratic functional.

Write

R_{A}(T)\ =\ C_{A}\ -\ 2\,\mathcal{L}_{A}(T)\ +\ \mathcal{Q}_{A}(T,T)\ =\ C_{A}\ -\ 2\,f(T)\ +\ a(T,T),\qquad T\in V_{1}.

The Fréchet derivative of
R_{A}
at
T
in direction
\eta
is

DR_{A}(T)[\eta]=2\left(a(T,\eta)-f(\eta)\right).

Thus
T
is a critical point iff it satisfies (
4.49
). Since
a
is
coercive,
R_{A}
is strictly (indeed, strongly) convex:

R_{A}(T+\eta)\ \geq\ R_{A}(T)\ +\ DR_{A}(T)[\eta]\ +\ c\,\lVert\eta\rVert_{V_{1}}^{2},

by (
4.48
). Therefore the unique solution
T_{A}^{\star}
of (
4.49
)
is the unique minimizer of
R_{A}
on
V_{1}
, and it satisfies the bound (
4.50
). This proves (i).

Proof of (ii).

Fix orthonormal bases
(\phi_{k})_{k\geq 1}
of
\mathbb{H}_{o}
and
(\psi_{\ell})_{\ell\geq 1}
of
{\mathsf{H}}
.
For
T\in V_{1}=\mathcal{HS}({\mathsf{H}}^{*},\mathbb{H}_{o})
define its coefficient array

v=(v_{k,\ell})_{k,\ell}\in\ell^{2}
by

v_{k,\ell}:=\big\langle T(\psi_{\ell}),\phi_{k}\big\rangle_{\mathbb{H}_{o}},\qquad k,\ell\geq 1.

Then the Hilbert–Schmidt isometry yields

\lVert T\rVert_{V_{1}}^{2}=\sum_{k,\ell\geq 1}|v_{k,\ell}|^{2}=\lVert v\rVert_{\ell^{2}}^{2}.

(4.51)

Moreover, for every
x\in{\mathsf{H}}^{*}
we have the expansion

T(x)=\sum_{k,\ell\geq 1}v_{k,\ell}\,\langle\psi_{\ell},x\rangle_{{\mathsf{H}},{\mathsf{H}}^{*}}\,\phi_{k},

(4.52)

where the series converges in
\mathbb{H}_{o}
.

Define
b\in\ell^{2}
and the bounded positive semidefinite operator
\Sigma:\ell^{2}\to\ell^{2}
by

b_{k,\ell}:=\int_{\Omega}\big\langle Y^{A},\phi_{k}\big\rangle_{\mathbb{H}_{o}}\,\big\langle\psi_{\ell},X^{A}\big\rangle_{{\mathsf{H}},{\mathsf{H}}^{*}}\,d\mu,

and

\Sigma^{(k,\ell)\,(k^{\prime},\ell^{\prime})}:=\delta_{k,k^{\prime}}\int_{\Omega}\big\langle\psi_{\ell},X^{A}\big\rangle_{{\mathsf{H}},{\mathsf{H}}^{*}}\,\big\langle\psi_{\ell^{\prime}},X^{A}\big\rangle_{{\mathsf{H}},{\mathsf{H}}^{*}}\,d\mu,\qquad k,k^{\prime},\ell,\ell^{\prime}\geq 1,

so that
\Sigma
acts on
\ell^{2}
by left multiplication.

We now rewrite the quadratic functional
R_{A}(T)=\lVert T(X^{A})-Y^{A}\rVert_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}
in coordinates.
First, expanding
T(X^{A})
in the basis
(\phi_{k})
and using (
4.52
) gives

\big\langle T(X^{A}),\phi_{k}\big\rangle_{\mathbb{H}_{o}}=\sum_{\ell\geq 1}v_{k,\ell}\,\big\langle\psi_{\ell},X^{A}\big\rangle_{{\mathsf{H}},{\mathsf{H}}^{*}},\qquad k\geq 1,

with convergence in
L^{2}(\Omega)
.
Hence, by Parseval in
\mathbb{H}_{o}
and Fubini–Tonelli,

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}_{A}(T,T)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\lVert T(X^{A})\rVert_{\mathbb{H}_{o}}^{2}\,d\mu=\sum_{k\geq 1}\int_{\Omega}\big|\langle T(X^{A}),\phi_{k}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}_{o}}\big|^{2}\,d\mu

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\geq 1}\int_{\Omega}\Big|\sum_{\ell\geq 1}v_{k,\ell}\,\langle\psi_{\ell},X^{A}\rangle_{{\mathsf{H}},{\mathsf{H}}^{*}}\Big|^{2}\,d\mu

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\geq 1}\sum_{\ell,\ell^{\prime}\geq 1}v_{k,\ell}\,v_{k,\ell^{\prime}}\,\int_{\Omega}\langle\psi_{\ell},X^{A}\rangle_{{\mathsf{H}},{\mathsf{H}}^{*}}\,\langle\psi_{\ell^{\prime}},X^{A}\rangle_{{\mathsf{H}},{\mathsf{H}}^{*}}\,d\mu

\displaystyle=\sum_{k,\ell,\ell^{\prime}\geq 1}v_{k,\ell}\,\Sigma^{(k,\ell)\,(k,\ell^{\prime})}\,v_{k,\ell^{\prime}}=\langle v,\Sigma v\rangle_{\ell^{2}}.

Similarly,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{A}(T)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\langle Y^{A},T(X^{A})\rangle_{\mathbb{H}_{o}}\,d\mu=\sum_{k\geq 1}\int_{\Omega}\langle Y^{A},\phi_{k}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}_{o}}\,\langle T(X^{A}),\phi_{k}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}_{o}}\,d\mu

\displaystyle=\sum_{k,\ell\geq 1}v_{k,\ell}\int_{\Omega}\langle Y^{A},\phi_{k}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}_{o}}\,\langle\psi_{\ell},X^{A}\rangle_{{\mathsf{H}},{\mathsf{H}}^{*}}\,d\mu=\langle v,b\rangle_{\ell^{2}}.

Therefore

R_{A}(T)=C_{A}-2\,\langle v,b\rangle_{\ell^{2}}+\langle v,\Sigma v\rangle_{\ell^{2}}=:F(v),\qquad v\in\ell^{2}.

Under the coercivity assumption of (i), equivalently
\Sigma\succeq c\,I
on
\ell^{2}
,
the functional
F
is strictly convex and Fréchet differentiable on
\ell^{2}
, with

DF(v)(h)=2\,\langle\Sigma v-b,\,h\rangle_{\ell^{2}},\qquad v,h\in\ell^{2}.

Hence
DF(v^{\star})=0
iff
\Sigma v^{\star}=b
, and by strict convexity this is also sufficient.
Thus the unique minimizer has coefficient vector
v^{\star}=\Sigma^{-1}b\in\ell^{2}
, and in particular

\sum_{k,\ell\geq 1}|v^{\star}_{k,\ell}|^{2}<\infty.

Finally, inserting
v^{\star}
into (
4.52
) yields the coordinate formula stated in (ii),
and the series converges in
\mathbb{H}_{o}
for each
x\in{\mathsf{H}}^{*}
. This proves (ii)

For the proof of (iii), let
\mathcal{R}:V\to V^{*}
be the Riesz isomorphism.
The bilinear form
\mathcal{Q}_{A}
induces a bounded self–adjoint positive semidefinite operator

K:V_{1}\to V_{1}^{*}
via

(KT)(\eta):=\mathcal{Q}_{A}(T,\eta),\qquad T,\eta\in V_{1}.

Set
B:=\mathcal{R}^{-1}K:V_{1}\to V_{1}
. Then
B
is bounded, self–adjoint and positive semidefinite. Indeed,
for
T,\eta\in V_{1}
,

\langle BT,\eta\rangle_{V_{1}}=\langle\mathcal{R}^{-1}KT,\eta\rangle_{V_{1}}=(KT)(\eta)=\mathcal{Q}_{A}(T,\eta)=\mathcal{Q}_{A}(\eta,T)=(K\eta)(T)=\langle B\eta,T\rangle_{V_{1}},

so
B
is self–adjoint, and
\langle BT,T\rangle_{V_{1}}=\mathcal{Q}_{A}(T,T)\geq 0
.

Write
\ell:=\mathcal{R}^{-1}\mathcal{L}_{A}\in V_{1}
. Then

R_{A}(T)=C_{A}-2\,\langle\ell,T\rangle_{V_{1}}+\langle BT,T\rangle_{V_{1}},

and any critical point satisfies the normal equation

BT=\ell\quad\text{in }V_{1}.

Assume
\mathcal{L}_{A}\in\overline{\mathrm{Range}(K)}\subset V_{1}^{*}
. Applying
\mathcal{R}^{-1}
yields

\ell\in\overline{\mathrm{Range}(B)}\subset V_{1}
.
By the spectral theorem, there exists a projection–valued measure
E(\cdot)
such that

B=\int_{\sigma(B)}\lambda\,dE(\lambda)
. Define the Moore–Penrose pseudoinverse

B^{\dagger}:=\int_{\sigma(B)\setminus\{0\}}\lambda^{-1}\,dE(\lambda),

which is bounded on
\overline{\mathrm{Range}(B)}
. Moreover,

BB^{\dagger}=P_{\overline{\mathrm{Range}(B)}}
, hence
BB^{\dagger}\ell=\ell
.
Therefore
T^{\star}:=B^{\dagger}\ell
solves
BT=\ell
.
The set of all solutions is
T^{\star}+\ker(B)
, and the unique solution of minimal
V_{1}
–norm is

T^{\star}=B^{\dagger}\ell
.

In the coordinate realization from (ii),
B
corresponds to the positive semidefinite operator

\Sigma
on
\ell^{2}
,
\ell
corresponds to
b
, and
B^{\dagger}
corresponds to
\Sigma^{\dagger}
.
Hence the minimal norm coefficient vector is
v^{\star}=\Sigma^{\dagger}b\in\ell^{2}
, and

T^{\star}=\sum_{k,\ell}v^{\star}_{k,\ell}\,\phi_{k}\otimes\psi_{\ell}
converges in
V_{1}
.

∎
