Mapping the global landscape of artificial intelligence in pancreatic cancer research
Authors: Pegah Rashidian, Aseel Smerat, Mohammad Ahmar Khan, Pankaj Bansal, Chandan Sharma, Djaloliddin Mansurov, Seyedsina Moghimnejadhosseini, Amirmahdi Mojtahedzadeh, Mahsa Talebzadeh, Ehsan Amini-Salehi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Artificial intelligence (AI) applications in pancreatic cancer are expanding rapidly, but the field’s global structure and emerging priorities remain incompletely characterized.Methods:The Web of Science Core Collection was searched through July 5, 2026. English-language original articles and reviews addressing AI in pancreatic cancer were eligible. After manual screening, 583 publications from 1998 to 2026 were analyzed using Biblioshiny, VOSviewer, and CiteSpace to assess publication trends, contributors, collaboration networks, co-citation patterns, keyword evolution, thematic clusters, and citation bursts.Results:Publication output accelerated after 2019 and reached 164 publications in 2025; the lower total in 2026 reflected partial-year coverage. The literature involved 59 countries, 1230 institutions, and 4038 authors. China produced the largest number of publications (n = 222), whereas the United States ranked second (n = 150) and had the highest country-level centrality (0.39). Shanghai Jiao Tong University was the most productive institution (n = 20), while Harvard University had the highest institutional centrality (0.22). Frontiers in Oncology was the most productive journal (n = 32). Influential studies focused on deep learning-based computed tomography, electronic health record-based risk prediction, exosome-based machine learning, and endoscopic ultrasonography. Research themes evolved from neural-network classification and texture analysis toward radiomics, deep learning, early detection, risk prediction, liquid biopsy, tumor biology, treatment-response prediction, and precision oncology.Conclusion:AI research in pancreatic cancer is growing rapidly and becoming increasingly multidisciplinary, although productivity and collaboration remain uneven. Clinical translation will require prospective multicenter validation, representative datasets, transparent reporting, calibration, fairness assessment, workflow evaluation, and evidence of improved patient outcomes.

1. Introduction

Pancreatic cancer remains one of the most lethal solid malignancies, largely because of its aggressive biology, nonspecific early manifestations, and frequent diagnosis at an advanced stage. Contemporary evidence indicates that overall 5-year survival remains approximately 13%, while the opportunity for curative-intent treatment is concentrated among patients diagnosed with localized or otherwise resectable disease.[1] Population-based screening is not recommended for asymptomatic average-risk adults, and current surveillance strategies are primarily directed toward individuals with substantial inherited or familial risk.[1,2] These limitations have intensified efforts to identify approaches capable of recognizing high-risk individuals, subtle early disease, and clinically relevant tumor characteristics before they are detected through conventional diagnostic pathways.

Artificial intelligence (AI) has consequently emerged as an important research direction in pancreatic cancer. Machine learning, deep learning, radiomics, and related computational methods can integrate complex information from computed tomography (CT), endoscopic ultrasonography (EUS), pathology, electronic health records (EHRs), and molecular or circulating biomarkers.[3,4] Recent studies illustrate the breadth of this potential: deep learning systems have detected pancreatic cancer on noncontrast CT at scale,[5] radiomics-based models have identified disease-associated features on prediagnostic CT examinations,[6] and longitudinal deep learning models have estimated future pancreatic cancer risk from disease trajectories recorded in national health data.[7] More recently, the international PANORAMA study provided a large-scale comparison of AI systems and radiologists for pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) detection on routine CT, reflecting the field’s movement toward clinically oriented external evaluation.[8] Nevertheless, rapid technical expansion has produced a heterogeneous literature spanning multiple disciplines, data types, clinical tasks, institutions, and geographic regions, making the overall structure and evolution of the field increasingly difficult to assess from individual studies or conventional narrative reviews alone.

Bibliometric and science-mapping approaches can address this problem by quantitatively characterizing publication growth, influential contributors, collaboration networks, knowledge foundations, and emerging research themes. A previous pancreatic cancer-specific bibliometric analysis by Yin et al evaluated AI-related publications from 1997 to 2021 and identified diagnosis, treatment, risk assessment, biology, and EUS among the major areas of interest.[9] However, that analysis preceded several influential developments in large-scale CT detection, prediagnostic imaging, longitudinal risk prediction, and more recent clinically oriented validation studies. Given the substantial expansion of AI research since 2021, an updated and methodologically broader assessment is needed to clarify how the field has changed and which directions are gaining prominence.

Accordingly, the present study maps the global landscape of AI research in pancreatic cancer using 583 manually screened publications indexed in the Web of Science Core Collection from 1998 through July 5, 2026. By integrating Biblioshiny, VOSviewer, and CiteSpace, we examine scientific production, leading countries, institutions, authors, and journals, as well as international collaboration, network centrality, co-citation structure, citation bursts, keyword evolution, thematic clusters, and temporal research trends. In addition to updating the period covered by the earlier bibliometric literature, this multidimensional approach is intended to distinguish research productivity from collaborative influence, identify the intellectual foundations of the field, and reveal emerging priorities relevant to early detection, risk prediction, biomarkers, treatment-response assessment, and precision oncology.

2. Methods

2.1. Data collection

Bibliographic records were retrieved from the Web of Science Core Collection on July 5, 2026. The Web of Science Core Collection was selected for its broad coverage of peer-reviewed literature, standardized citation indexing, and suitability for bibliometric analyses.

The search strategy targeted publications concerning the application of AI to pancreatic cancer and comprised 2 conceptual domains: AI and pancreatic cancer. AI-related terms included machine learning, deep learning, neural networks, computer vision, natural language processing, predictive modeling, and related computational techniques. Pancreatic cancer terms included pancreatic cancer, pancreatic carcinoma, pancreatic adenocarcinoma, and PDAC. The 2 domains were combined using appropriate Boolean operators. The complete search strategy, including keywords, field identifiers, truncation symbols, and Boolean combinations, is provided in Table S1, Supplemental Digital Content 1.

2.2. Eligibility criteria and study selection

Eligibility criteria were predefined before screening. Studies were included if they were indexed in the Web of Science Core Collection, published in English, and classified as original research articles or review articles. Eligible publications were required to address the development, evaluation, implementation, or clinical application of AI in pancreatic cancer, including detection, diagnosis, classification, prognosis, treatment planning, outcome prediction, imaging, pathology, or other relevant applications.

Conference proceedings, meeting abstracts, editorials, letters, book chapters, preprints, news items, early-access publications, retracted articles, and non-English publications were excluded. Restriction to full-length articles and reviews was applied to ensure a consistent and methodologically reliable bibliometric dataset.

Study selection was conducted independently by 2 reviewers using a predefined sequential process. Records were first filtered by publication type and language, followed by manual title and abstract screening for relevance to AI applications in pancreatic cancer. Disagreements were resolved through discussion or, when necessary, consultation with a third reviewer. No automated screening system was used.

The initial search identified 2249 records. During preliminary refinement, 316 records were excluded, including 304 publications that were neither original articles nor reviews and 12 non-English publications. The remaining 1933 records underwent title and abstract screening, during which 1350 records were excluded for insufficient relevance. Ultimately, 583 publications met all eligibility criteria and were included in the final bibliometric analysis. The study-selection process is presented in Figure 1.

2.3. Data analysis

Bibliometric analyses were performed using 3 complementary platforms: VOSviewer, CiteSpace, and Biblioshiny. Complete bibliographic records were exported from the Web of Science Core Collection in comma-separated values and plain-text formats to ensure compatibility across software platforms.

VOSviewer was primarily used to visualize bibliometric networks, including co-authorship and keyword relationships, and to identify collaboration patterns and conceptual linkages within the field. Association-strength normalization was applied to standardize network connections. Clustering used a resolution parameter of 1.00 and a minimum cluster size of 1. Network layout generation used a single random start and a maximum of 1000 iterations, while cluster optimization used 10 random starts and 10 iterations. Other parameters remained at their default settings.[10,11]

CiteSpace was used to investigate the intellectual structure, citation dynamics, and temporal development of research on AI in pancreatic cancer. Betweenness centrality was calculated to identify influential bridging nodes, while co-citation clustering was used to characterize major research themes. Cluster labels were generated using the log-likelihood ratio method, and cluster quality was assessed using modularity and silhouette statistics.

Temporal analyses covered 1998 to 2026 using 1-year time slices. Terms were extracted from titles, abstracts, author keywords, and Keywords Plus, and network similarity was calculated using the cosine coefficient. Node selection was performed using the g-index with k = 25, without network pruning. CiteSpace was additionally used for co-citation analysis, temporal visualization, dual-map overlays, citation-burst detection, and identification of emerging research fronts.[12,13]

Biblioshiny, the web-based interface of the Bibliometrix R package, was used for descriptive and network-based bibliometric analyses. It supported assessment of annual scientific production, citation patterns, productive sources, journal performance, country contributions, international collaboration, co-citation relationships, and thematic development. Network, clustering, and dimensionality-reduction approaches, including Louvain clustering, Walktrap community detection, and multidimensional scaling, were used to characterize scientific and thematic relationships within the dataset.

Together, the 3 platforms provided complementary perspectives: VOSviewer emphasized network visualization, CiteSpace enabled structural and temporal analysis, and Biblioshiny supported descriptive performance and thematic analyses. Their combined use enabled assessment of research productivity, influence, collaboration patterns, intellectual structure, and emerging trends in AI research related to pancreatic cancer.[14,15]

4. Discussion

This bibliometric and science mapping analysis characterizes a rapidly expanding but still translationally immature research domain at the intersection of AI and pancreatic cancer. Across 583 eligible publications produced by 4038 authors from 1230 institutions in 59 countries, scientific output remained sparse until the late 2010s and accelerated sharply after 2019, reaching 164 publications in 2025. The geographic and institutional analyses revealed an important distinction between research volume and network position: China produced the greatest number of publications, whereas the United States occupied the most prominent bridging position in the international collaboration network. A similar divergence was observed institutionally, with Shanghai Jiao Tong University leading in productivity and Harvard University displaying the highest betweenness centrality. The journal, co-citation, and dual-map analyses further showed that the field is not confined to medical imaging or computer science but is situated at the convergence of oncology, gastroenterology, radiology, diagnostics, molecular biology, genetics, epidemiology, and computational research. Finally, the citation-burst and keyword analyses suggest an intellectual progression from neural-network-assisted lesion differentiation and radiomics toward early detection, longitudinal risk prediction, biomarker integration, treatment-response assessment, and clinically oriented precision oncology.

These findings extend, rather than inaugurate, the bibliometric literature on this topic. Yin et al previously evaluated AI research in pancreatic cancer published between 1997 and 2021 and identified rapid growth after 2018, with diagnosis, survival, classification, management, risk assessment, and EUS among the major themes.[9]

The present analysis adds approximately 5 years of rapidly evolving literature, applies manual relevance screening, and combines Biblioshiny, VOSviewer, and CiteSpace to examine centrality, citation bursts, dual-map pathways, temporal overlays, and cluster timelines. It therefore provides an updated and methodologically broader map of the field, particularly of developments that occurred after the emergence of large-scale CT detection systems, longitudinal EHR models, and biologically informed AI applications. Direct numerical comparisons with the earlier analysis should nevertheless be avoided because the search terminology, screening thresholds, document eligibility criteria, database export dates, and bibliographic preprocessing were not identical.

The publication curves in Figures 2 and 3 indicate that AI in pancreatic cancer changed from an intermittent research topic into a recognizable scientific field during the past decade. The increase from only 14 publications in 2019 to 164 in 2025 is consistent with broader bibliometric studies showing steep expansion of AI research across oncology and other cancer-specific domains.[16–19]

This parallel growth suggests that the pancreatic cancer literature is participating in a larger transition in oncology from conventional statistical modeling and manually engineered imaging features toward deep learning, multimodal analysis, and data-intensive prediction. However, the growth observed here is particularly clinically relevant because pancreatic cancer presents problems for which pattern-recognition methods are intuitively attractive: subtle abnormalities may precede conventional radiological diagnosis, early symptoms are nonspecific, screening the average-risk population is not currently feasible, and therapeutic outcomes vary substantially even within apparently similar clinical stages.

Several developments may plausibly have supported the post-2019 acceleration. These include greater availability of digitized CT, magnetic resonance imaging, EUS, histopathology, and routinely collected clinical data; advances in computing infrastructure; increased accessibility of open-source machine-learning frameworks; and growing recognition that imaging and longitudinal health records contain information not fully captured by conventional interpretation. These explanations remain contextual rather than causal because bibliometric data cannot establish why individual studies were initiated. Moreover, a rising publication count does not by itself demonstrate increasing methodological quality. Rapid expansion may include exploratory, retrospective, single-center, or incompletely validated models alongside rigorous multicenter investigations.

The 104 publications recorded in 2026 should not be interpreted as evidence that interest is declining. Data were retrieved on July 5, 2026, and both incomplete calendar-year coverage and indexing delays are likely to affect the observed count. Likewise, the polynomial curves and their coefficients of determination in Figures 2 and 3 summarize the historical pattern but do not validate a forecasting model. Extrapolating them would assume that publication behavior, database indexing, terminology, funding priorities, and technological development remain stable, which is unlikely in a rapidly changing field. The defensible interpretation is therefore that recent output has accelerated substantially, not that a particular future publication trajectory has been established.

The country-level findings demonstrate that productivity and network-bridging influence represent different dimensions of scientific activity. China contributed 222 publications, compared with 150 from the United States, but the United States had substantially higher betweenness centrality than China, at 0.39 versus 0.14. England, India, and Italy also displayed relatively high centrality despite lower absolute publication volumes. In bibliometric terms, publication count reflects contribution volume, whereas centrality estimates how often a country connects otherwise less-connected parts of the collaboration network. Thus, China’s position indicates a major capacity for producing AI–pancreatic cancer research, while the United States appears to have played a more prominent role in linking national research communities. Neither measure directly evaluates methodological rigor, originality, clinical relevance, or study quality.

The prominence of China and the United States is consistent with broader AI-oncology analyses, which have repeatedly identified these countries as major contributors.[16–18] However, the present ranking differs from the earlier pancreatic cancer-specific analysis by Yin et al, in which the United States had the highest publication volume through 2021.[9]

One possible interpretation is that Chinese publication activity increased more rapidly during the subsequent period. Differences in search strategy, manual screening, country attribution, and the treatment of multicountry publications could also contribute; consequently, the change should not be framed as a definitive transfer of scientific leadership.

The collaboration map in Figure 4 and centrality network in Figure 5 also have implications beyond bibliometric prominence. AI models are especially vulnerable to dataset shift caused by differences in disease prevalence, scanner manufacturers, acquisition protocols, referral patterns, annotation practices, and patient characteristics. International partnerships can therefore provide access to scientifically important heterogeneity rather than merely increasing publication counts. Collaboration between high-output and high-centrality systems could facilitate geographically external validation, comparison across health systems, and assessment in populations that differ from the model-development cohort. The participation of 59 countries demonstrates broad international interest, but the distribution remains uneven. Because the study did not normalize output by population size, pancreatic cancer burden, research expenditure, or number of active institutions, the analysis cannot determine whether particular regions are underperforming relative to their resources or disease burden.

The institutional results reinforce the distinction between concentration of production and network connectivity. Shanghai Jiao Tong University, Zhejiang University, and the Mayo Clinic were the most productive institutions, with 20, 19, and 17 publications, respectively. In contrast, Harvard University had the highest institutional centrality at 0.22, while the University of California System, Mayo Clinic, and Johns Hopkins University each had a value of 0.13. This pattern suggests that the field combines high-output institutional groups, particularly within China, with major American academic medical centers that frequently connect collaborative communities. Because university systems, medical schools, affiliated hospitals, and cancer centers may be indexed inconsistently, small differences between institutional rankings should not be overinterpreted.

The journal distribution further illustrates the field’s interdisciplinary character. Frontiers in Oncology published the largest number of articles, followed by Cancers and Scientific Reports, whereas Cancers, Scientific Reports, and Frontiers in Oncology were the most locally cited sources. These measures describe different relationships: publication count captures the volume disseminated by a journal, while local citations measure how often each source was cited within the 583-publication corpus. Neither is equivalent to journal impact factor, peer-review quality, or the methodological quality of individual studies. The prominence of Gastroenterology, Radiology, European Radiology, Pancreas, and CA: A Cancer Journal for Clinicians among locally cited sources indicates that the intellectual base extends beyond the journals publishing the greatest number of AI-specific articles.

The dual-map overlay in Figure 10 adds conceptual information that cannot be obtained from journal rankings alone. Its principal pathways connect clinical medicine and molecular biology on the citing side with health, medicine, molecular biology, and genetics on the cited side and include additional contributions from computing and other scientific domains. This indicates bidirectional dependence: computational approaches are being applied to clinically defined problems, while model development is informed by disease biology, epidemiology, imaging, and clinical outcome literature. Future progress is therefore likely to depend on sustained collaboration among data scientists, radiologists, gastroenterologists, pathologists, surgeons, oncologists, molecular scientists, epidemiologists, and implementation researchers.

Author-level indicators should also be interpreted independently. Yun Bian, Jianping Lu, and Chengwei Shao were the most productive authors, but Adrian Săftoiu, Suresh T. Chari, and Ajit H. Goenka had the highest citation totals. R. L. Siegel position as the most frequently co-cited author reflects the importance of cancer epidemiology and disease-burden literature to the intellectual framing of the field, rather than direct authorship of the greatest number of pancreatic AI models. Figure 11 shows several relatively distinct co-authorship clusters, including a dense group around Bian, Lu, and Shao and smaller groups around other investigators. This is compatible with the existence of productive local research teams and partial fragmentation between groups, although the displayed network alone cannot establish that collaboration is inadequate. It does identify an opportunity for broader cross-cluster partnerships, especially where complementary expertise or independent datasets could support external validation.

Table 4 and Figure 12 together describe a sequence of methodological and translational milestones rather than a simple hierarchy of citation counts. Early work by Săftoiu et al demonstrated that neural-network analysis of dynamic EUS elastography could help distinguish pancreatic malignancy from chronic pancreatitis.[20] Its continued citation prominence reflects the longstanding clinical importance of differentiating malignant masses from benign inflammatory disease and shows that AI in pancreatic cancer did not originate exclusively with modern CT-based deep learning. Ko et al subsequently combined machine learning with nanofluidic exosome analysis, extending computational classification beyond imaging and providing an early link between AI and liquid-biopsy research.[21]

The strong citation bursts for Gillies et al[22] and van Griethuysen et al[23] indicate that the field’s growth depended heavily on general radiomics infrastructure and concepts. Gillies et al articulated the view that medical images can be analyzed as quantitative data rather than solely as visual pictures, while the PyRadiomics system developed by van Griethuysen et al provided an open-source platform for standardized feature extraction. Their prominence in Figure 12 therefore reflects methodological dependence on transferable tools developed outside pancreatic oncology itself. At the same time, radiomic features can be sensitive to image acquisition, reconstruction, segmentation, and preprocessing; methodological influence should not be interpreted as proof that every downstream radiomics model is reproducible or clinically valid.

The influential 2019 studies mark a second transition. Hussein et al examined supervised and unsupervised deep-learning approaches for pancreatic and pulmonary tumor characterization[24]; Kuwahara et al applied deep learning to malignancy assessment in IPMNs[25]; and Nasief et al used delta-radiomics to predict treatment response.[26] Collectively, these studies broadened the field from binary differentiation of solid masses toward representation learning, cystic-lesion risk assessment, and longitudinal evaluation of treatment-related imaging changes.

The most recent highly cited studies represent movement toward earlier and more scalable detection. Mukherjee et al investigated prediagnostic CT examinations, asking whether disease-associated patterns could be identified before clinical diagnosis.[6] Cao et al developed a large-scale noncontrast CT system,[5] while Chen et al evaluated deep-learning detection in a nationwide population-based setting.[27]

Placido et al moved beyond image interpretation by modeling longitudinal disease trajectories in national health-registry data.[7] These works collectively shift the central question from “Can AI classify an established pancreatic lesion?” toward “Can AI identify individuals or subtle abnormalities before conventional diagnosis?” Kenner et al summative review also became highly cited because it consolidated early-detection applications across imaging, biomarkers, and risk prediction.[28]

The ongoing citation bursts from 2024 through 2026 for Placido et al and for publications addressing pancreatic biology, epidemiology, and clinical applications suggest that scholarly attention is broadening beyond technical model development. Nevertheless, citation bursts identify concentrated attention, not successful clinical adoption. Recent publications also have less time to accrue citations, while older methodological papers may be cited across many cancer types. Table 4 and Figure 12 should therefore be interpreted as complementary representations of visibility, foundational dependence, and changing scientific priorities.

The keyword and timeline analyses support a 3-stage interpretation of thematic development. The earliest phase centered on artificial neural networks, EUS, elastography, diagnostic differentiation, and relatively focused lesion-classification tasks. The second phase was characterized by CT, texture analysis, radiomics, deep learning, and prediction models. The most recent phase includes early detection, longitudinal risk prediction, liquid biopsy, exosomes, prognostic modeling, tumor microenvironment, immunotherapy, molecular subtypes, metastasis, treatment response, and patient outcomes. This progression is consistent with movement from modality-specific computer-aided diagnosis toward broader precision-oncology questions.

The dominant keywords nevertheless show continuity as well as change. “Pancreatic cancer,” “machine learning,” “artificial intelligence,” “diagnosis,” “pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma,” “deep learning,” and “radiomics” remained central and highly interconnected in Figures 13 to 16. Newer terms have therefore not replaced imaging and diagnosis; rather, they have expanded the field around an established diagnostic core. Prognosis, survival, biomarkers, classification, and prediction link the earlier imaging-focused literature to newer efforts involving biological characterization and clinical outcomes.

The 13 CiteSpace clusters provide additional detail but require restrained interpretation. Clusters relating to chronic pancreatitis, artificial neural networks, deep learning, diagnostic performance, and pancreatic adenocarcinoma are coherent with the earlier emphasis on differentiation and classification. “Routine data” may represent increasing use of EHRs, clinical histories, laboratory results, or routinely acquired imaging, although study-level inspection is needed to establish its exact composition. “Using exosome,” “perineural invasion,” and “patient outcome” plausibly indicate expansion toward biomarkers, biological behavior, and clinically meaningful prediction. The pancreatic neuroendocrine tumor cluster also shows that the retrieved literature is not limited exclusively to PDAC.

The label “conversational artificial intelligence” warrants particular caution. It was generated automatically through the CiteSpace log-likelihood ratio procedure and appears as a relatively recent, small cluster in Figures 17 and 18. Without examining its constituent publications, it should not be treated as evidence that large language models, generative AI, or conversational clinical systems have become an established pancreatic cancer research front. At most, it identifies a candidate emerging theme requiring study-level confirmation.

Overall, the thematic expansion may indicate increasing clinical ambition, but it does not by itself establish clinical maturity. A literature can become more multimodal and outcome-oriented while remaining predominantly retrospective. Similarly, frequent use of terms such as “personalized management” or “early detection” does not demonstrate that models improve decisions or patient outcomes. The bibliometric evidence supports a change in research emphasis, whereas clinical effectiveness requires prospective evaluation.

The mapped research priorities closely correspond to major unresolved clinical problems in pancreatic cancer. Routine screening is not recommended for asymptomatic average-risk adults,[2] while surveillance is primarily directed toward selected individuals with substantial inherited or familial risk.[6,29] AI-based risk models could enrich a population for targeted investigation, but this role differs fundamentally from deploying an algorithm as a population screening test. Risk thresholds would need to reflect disease prevalence, downstream imaging capacity, false-positive harms, and the consequences of invasive investigation.

Imaging AI may be particularly relevant to opportunistic analysis of CT examinations performed for other indications, detection of subtle prediagnostic abnormalities, characterization of pancreatic cystic lesions, and differentiation of PDAC from chronic pancreatitis. EUS-based systems may support interpretation of diagnostically uncertain masses, whereas pathology and molecular models could contribute to subtype classification, perineural invasion assessment, or treatment selection. Prognostic and delta-radiomics studies suggest further applications in recurrence estimation and response monitoring. The bibliometric shift toward exosomes and other biomarkers raises the possibility that imaging, longitudinal clinical data, and blood-based signals may eventually be combined rather than evaluated in isolation.

However, the central translational gap is between retrospective discrimination and prospective clinical utility. A high area under the receiver operating characteristic curve does not establish adequate positive predictive value in a low-prevalence population, reliable calibration, acceptable workload, or improved survival. Models can appear highly accurate because of spectrum bias, inappropriate control selection, data leakage, overlapping patients, acquisition-specific artifacts, or unrecognized differences between development and test cohorts. Even multicenter retrospective validation may not reproduce the intended workflow because clinicians’ responses to model outputs can alter performance and downstream care.

PANORAMA represents an important step toward clinically oriented evaluation because it compared AI and radiologists in an international, confirmatory observational design.[8] Nevertheless, diagnostic performance in an observational study is not equivalent to evidence that AI changes management appropriately or improves patient outcomes. Subsequent evaluations should assess incremental benefit over expert interpretation, performance in consecutive and representative patients, calibration at clinically meaningful thresholds, time to diagnosis, unnecessary investigations, resectability at detection, treatment decisions, quality of life, cost, and survival.

Methodological and reporting standards can provide a structured pathway for this work. Prediction-model studies should follow Transparent Reporting of a multivariable prediction model for Individual Prognosis or Diagnosis–AI[30] and be appraised using Prediction model Risk of Bias ASsessment Tool–AI.[31] Imaging studies should address the Checklist for AI in Medical Imaging 2024,[32] while diagnostic-accuracy investigations should follow Standards for Reporting Diagnostic Accuracy Studies–AI.[33] Early live clinical evaluations should consider Developmental and Exploratory Clinical Investigations of Decision Support Systems Driven by AI[34] and clinical trial protocols and reports should follow Standard Protocol Items: Recommendations for Interventional Trials–AI[35] and Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials–AI.[36] Fair, Universal, Traceable, Usable, Robust, and Explainable AI additionally emphasizes trustworthy and deployable systems across the full life cycle, including fairness, universality, traceability, usability, robustness, and explainability.[37]

Beyond transparent reporting, clinical translation requires independent external validation, harmonized acquisition and preprocessing, explicit prevention of data leakage, reproducible code and model documentation, subgroup-specific performance, and calibration assessment. Decision-curve or related utility analyses should examine whether predictions produce net clinical benefit at plausible intervention thresholds. Explainability should be evaluated according to its clinical purpose rather than included as a purely visual add-on. Fairness analyses should examine performance across sex, age, ethnicity, socioeconomic circumstances, comorbidity, disease stage, scanner type, hospital setting, and geographic region where data permit.

Implementation also raises questions that conventional model-performance studies rarely answer. These include how radiologists or gastroenterologists should respond to discrepant human and algorithmic assessments; whether automation bias or alert fatigue occurs; who is responsible for monitoring performance drift; how patient consent, privacy, and secondary data use are governed; and whether the intervention is affordable relative to its clinical benefit. The relevant endpoint is therefore not simply whether an algorithm discriminates cases from controls, but whether a human–AI care pathway performs more safely, equitably, efficiently, and effectively than current practice.

4.1. Future research priorities

Near-term research should prioritize reproducible external validation and prospective multicenter evaluation of existing high-performing models rather than continued proliferation of closely related single-center algorithms. Consecutive cohorts, prespecified analysis plans, locked models, transparent exclusions, calibration measures, and clinically defined thresholds are required. International studies should intentionally include variation in scanner equipment, imaging protocols, referral pathways, patient demographics, and disease prevalence. Silent prospective deployment, in which model outputs are recorded without influencing care, may provide a practical intermediate step before interventional evaluation.

A second near-term priority is harmonization. Common data dictionaries, standardized imaging and pathology pipelines, transparent segmentation procedures, documented outcome definitions, and accessible benchmark datasets would improve comparison and replication. Code sharing, model cards, detailed reporting of hyperparameter selection, and clear identification of overlapping patient cohorts would reduce avoidable duplication. Where unrestricted data sharing is impossible, federated or other privacy-preserving approaches may support multicenter learning, although these methods still require governance, harmonization, and evaluation for site-specific bias.

Longer-term work should test genuinely multimodal systems combining imaging with EHR trajectories, pathology, genomics, transcriptomics, proteomics, metabolomics, circulating biomarkers, or exosome profiles. Potentially valuable use cases include surveillance of genetically or familially high-risk individuals, enrichment of patients with new-onset diabetes or concerning clinical trajectories, characterization of cystic lesions, prediction of neoadjuvant response, recurrence assessment, and treatment selection. These systems should be compared with simpler models to determine whether additional modalities provide meaningful incremental benefit rather than technical complexity alone.

Ultimately, prospective impact studies and, where justified, randomized trials should determine whether AI changes clinical decisions and improves outcomes. Economic evaluation should be incorporated early, particularly for low-prevalence detection pathways in which false-positive investigations may be costly or harmful. Future studies should also report algorithmic failures, subgroup inequities, workflow effects, and performance drift, not only average discrimination. Foundation models and conversational systems may eventually contribute to data integration or clinical communication, but the present cluster evidence is insufficient to identify them as mature priorities in pancreatic cancer.

Strengths, interpretive cautions, and transition to limitations. A major strength of the present study is the integration of 3 complementary bibliometric platforms with manual screening of the retrieved literature. This enabled simultaneous assessment of performance indicators, collaboration structures, centrality, co-citation, citation bursts, journal pathways, keyword evolution, and cluster timelines through July 5, 2026. The analysis therefore captures both the historical foundations and the recent expansion of the field.

Several cautions directly affect interpretation. Citation counts indicate visibility rather than quality; centrality depends on network construction; automatically generated cluster labels are approximate; and partial-year data preclude interpreting 2026 as a downturn. Exclusion of non-English publications, conference proceedings, and preprints may be especially consequential in AI, where technical work is frequently disseminated outside conventional clinical journals. Most importantly, bibliometric methods cannot determine risk of bias, calibration, fairness, reproducibility, transportability, or patient benefit. These issues are addressed more fully in the dedicated Section 4.2.

4.2. Limitations

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings of this bibliometric study. First, the analysis was based exclusively on the Web of Science Core Collection and included only English-language original research articles and reviews. Consequently, relevant publications indexed in other databases, non-English studies, conference proceedings, preprints, and other gray literature may have been missed. This is particularly important in AI research, where novel technical developments are often first reported in conference proceedings or on preprint platforms.

Second, the completeness of the dataset depended on the terminology used in the search strategy. Studies using related terms such as computational imaging, computer-aided diagnosis, digital pathology, pattern recognition, or foundation models may not have been retrieved if explicit AI-related terminology was absent. Conversely, broad search terms may have captured studies in which AI represented only a secondary component.

Third, citation-based indicators should not be interpreted as direct measures of scientific quality or clinical relevance. Citation counts are influenced by publication age, journal visibility, database coverage, self-citation, and disciplinary citation practices. Likewise, publication output, citation frequency, and network centrality reflect different dimensions of scientific activity and should not be considered interchangeable. High productivity does not necessarily indicate greater methodological quality, while high centrality primarily reflects a bridging position within collaboration networks.

Fourth, country, institution, and author-level analyses may be affected by inconsistencies in affiliation data and author-name disambiguation. Variations in institutional names, hospital or university affiliations, transliteration, organizational restructuring, shared surnames, initials, and changes in author affiliations may lead to inappropriate splitting or merging of contributors and thereby influence productivity and collaboration rankings.

Fifth, thematic and network analyses were influenced by the bibliographic fields, preprocessing decisions, and software parameters used. Related terms such as “pancreatic cancer,” “pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma,” and “PDAC” may have been treated separately, potentially fragmenting closely related themes. In addition, CiteSpace cluster labels were generated automatically and should therefore be interpreted as approximate thematic descriptions rather than definitive classifications. Network centrality estimates are similarly sensitive to node-selection criteria, thresholds, time intervals, and other analytical settings.

Sixth, data for 2026 represent only part of the calendar year and may also be affected by indexing delays. The lower publication count in 2026 should therefore not be interpreted as evidence of declining research activity. Similarly, the polynomial trend lines were used only to describe historical publication patterns and should not be regarded as validated forecasting models.

Finally, bibliometric analysis cannot determine whether individual AI models are methodologically robust, reproducible, unbiased, externally validated, or clinically effective. The present study did not assess risk of bias, data leakage, model calibration, fairness, reporting quality, external validation, or prospective clinical performance. In addition, multiple publications derived from overlapping patient cohorts or successive versions of similar AI models may have been counted separately, potentially inflating the apparent productivity or influence of particular research groups. Accordingly, the findings should be viewed as a map of the structure and evolution of the literature rather than as an assessment of the clinical validity or methodological quality of individual studies.

5. Conclusion

AI research in pancreatic cancer has expanded rapidly, particularly since 2019, and has progressed from isolated diagnostic applications toward broader uses in early detection, prognosis, treatment-response prediction, and precision oncology. The field is highly multidisciplinary and internationally active, although scientific productivity and collaborative influence remain uneven across countries and institutions.

Despite promising technical performance, most AI models are not yet ready for routine clinical use. Future studies should emphasize prospective multicenter validation, standardized and representative datasets, transparency, calibration, fairness, cost-effectiveness, and measurable improvements in patient care. The clinical value of AI will ultimately depend on whether it can safely support earlier diagnosis, personalized treatment, and better patient outcomes.
