Genome-wide association study identifies genetic variants associated with single-parity reproductive loss in three commercial pig breeds
Authors: Kefala Taye Mekonnen, Dong-Hui Lee, Befekadu Chemere Beyenssa, Ah-Yeong Son, Young-Gyu Cho, Kang-Seok Seo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundReproductive performance is a critical aspect of livestock production and sustainability. The number of stillbirths (NS) and mummified (NM) piglets significantly affect reproductive efficiency in swine production. Identifying and understanding the genetic architecture and candidate genes influencing these reproductive loss traits are essential for genetic inheritance studies and selective breeding programs in the swine breeding industry. To elucidate the genetic architecture of these traits, we performed a genome-wide association study (GWAS) using porcine 70 K BeadChip arrays from 1,162 Duroc, 1,783 Landrace, and 2,366 Yorkshire pigs.ResultSingle-parity breed-specific genome-wide association (GWA) analyses revealed that SIDT2 on Chr9 was associated with NM in Duroc pigs, whereas GTF2H3, PPP1R9A, and LMTK2 on Chr14, 9, and 3, respectively, were associated with both traits in Landrace pigs. Several genes have been linked to NS and NM in Yorkshire pigs, including GRID1 (chr14), DLGAP2 (chr15), ZZEF1 (chr12), RNF17 (chr11), SEL1L2 (chr17), ASCC3 (chr1), KCND2 (chr18), AP5B1(chr2), KAT5 (chr2), and SPATS2 (chr5). Across the breeds, five genes (DLGAP2, RNF17, SEL1L2, AP5B1, and KAT5) were exclusively associated with NS, whereas the other five (SIDT2, NDUFAF5, AKAP12, RCC1L, and BIRC6) were specific to NM. In addition, eight genes (GTF2H3, PPP1R9A, LMTK2, GRID1, ZZEF1, ASCC3, KCND2, and SPATS2) were associated with both traits, suggesting shared molecular pathways involved in piglet mortality.ConclusionsThis study showed genetic factors affecting reproductive performance loss in three commercial Korean pig breeds through first-parity GWA analysis and provided crucial genetic markers that breeders may use in marker-assisted breeding programs aimed at improving reproductive efficiency.

Background

Reproductive performance is a critical aspect of livestock production and sustainability. Sow reproductive performance is a crucial aspect of the pig industry [1], and the total number born (TNB) and the number born alive (NBA) are key measures of this performance [1, 2]. The difference between these two traits represents the number of piglets born dead, which directly quantifies the loss of litters from the fetal stage to farrowing. The number of piglets born dead included both the numbers of mummified (NM) and stillbirth (NS), which are the most significant traits for determining the loss of reproduction [3, 4]. In a typical clean environment, mummified piglets often result from large litter size, inadequate nutrient supply, delayed placental development [5], and genetic defects [6]. However, NS are typically associated with large litter size, older sow age, and slow farrowing or farrowing difficulties [5].

In swine, litter loss (reproductive loss) is a multifaceted problem, with variations in reproductive traits influenced by numerous genes, each contributing small effects, and a range of non-genetic factors [7, 8]. This complexity makes identifying the exact cause and developing effective breeding strategies challenging. The use of molecular markers has evolved with a shift from microsatellites to single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs), owing to their abundance and the development of high-throughput genotyping platforms [9]. Genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have become powerful tools for identifying DNA variants associated with complex diseases and traits in humans and animals, including swine reproductive traits [7, 8, 10]. Several linkage studies have been conducted to identify QTL and causal genes for NM and NS using molecular markers [11]. The availability of genome sequence information in recent years has revolutionized the study of complex quantitative traits, enabling researchers to explore the genomic architecture underlying these traits and conduct several GWAS using whole-genome sequence data [12]. However, the study of quantitative traits is challenging because of the substantial number of samples required, which leads to high genotyping costs for sequence-based GWAS. To overcome these issues, reduced-representation sequencing methods have been applied, which utilize restriction enzymes to digest the genome, reduce its complexity, and enable the detection of SNPs in large sample sizes with high reproducibility and minimal cost [13]. Genotyping by sequencing (GBS) is an exemplary, cost-effective, and highly efficient technology that enables the genome-wide detection of single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) and has been successfully applied to a range of organisms, including plants and animals [7–9, 11]. This reduced representation method uses restriction enzymes to simplify the genome, allowing the detection of SNPs in large sample sizes with high reproducibility and lower costs [7, 8].

NM and NS are complex traits that are directly affected by the total number of piglets born [14] and temporal gene effects in different parities [15]. In the previous study, GWAS study were used to detect genomic regions associated with TNB and NBA in three commercial pig breeds [2] and with NM and NS in different parities in two domestic pig populations [3]. However, in commercial pig breeds, a better biological understanding of these traits is required to improve selection for NM and NS. Understanding the genetic factors that contribute to loss of reproductive performance is essential for breed improvement and conservation in the pig industry, which plays a critical role in livestock productivity, sustainability, and conservation. Therefore, the present study aimed to identify the significant chromosomal regions and candidate genes associated with NM and NS across first parities in genetically different breeds to generate a list of candidate genes associated with these traits. This study provides novel insights into the genetic mechanisms underlying reproductive performance loss in three Korean commercial breeds with distinct genetic backgrounds and identifies regions of the genome for future investigation.

Materials and methods

Populations and phenotypes description

GWAS were conducted for reproductive performance loss traits, NS and NM, in three commercial Korean pig breeds (Duroc, Landrace, and Yorkshire). A total of 7,488 animals were sampled from five grand-grandparent (GGP) farms (codes 40, 45, 46, 50, and 51). The sample comprised 1,586 Duroc (126 from farm 40, 193 from farm 46, and 1,267 from farm 51), 2,256 Landrace (842 from farm 45, 716 from farm 50, 698 from farm 51, and 126 from farm 40), and 3,646 Yorkshire pigs (1,247 from farm 40, 914 from farm 45, 741 from farm 50, and 744 from farm 51). Phenotypic records were available for 1,162 Duroc, 1,783 Landrace, and 2,366 Yorkshire pigs.

Phenotypic correlation analysis

The phenotypic correlations between NS and NM traits were analyzed separately for the three Korean commercial pig breeds using the Spearman’s correlation method. The analysis was performed using R version 4.3.3 [16], released on February 29, with the “ggplot2” and “GGally” packages. The results were reported for three significant digits. The resulting visualization was enhanced through custom formatting, including optimized color schemes, clear labeling, and refined layout settings, to make the results more accessible and interpretable.

Genotyping and quality control

Genotyping was performed using porcine 70 K BeadChip arrays (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) on DNA extracted from blood samples. The study initially included 7,488 breeding pigs (1,586 Duroc, 2,256 Landrace, and 3,646 Yorkshire) and 76,756 single-nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) markers. Quality control (QC) was performed using PLINK software v1.90 [17]. SNPs were filtered based on the following criteria: minor allele frequency (MAF) >0.05, missing rate < 0.1, SNP call rate >95%, individual call rate >95%, and Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium (HWE) >1 × 10⁻⁶. Only autosomal SNPs markers were retained for genome-wide association (GWA) analysis.

Principal Component Analysis (PCA)

Population stratification within each breed group was assessed using PCA because stratification can lead to false positives in GWAS. To account for the population substructures, the vector loadings of PCs were included in our model. The optimal number of PCs was determined using a scree plot that displayed the contribution of each successive PC to the eigenvalue of the relationship matrix. PCA was performed on SNP data using PLINK v1.90 [17]. PC values and scree plots were visualized using the “ggplot2” package in R version 4.3.3 [16], released on February 29.

Genome-Wide Association (GWA) analysis

Single-parity (first-parity) GWA analysis was conducted separately for each breed to eliminate population stratification. SNP-trait association analyses for reproductive performance loss traits (NS and NM) were performed using the linear regression model in PLINK v1.90 [17]. Different numbers of principal components were included as covariates: the first principal component (PC1) for the Duroc breed, first two components (PC1 and PC2) for the Landrace breed, and first three components (PC1, PC2, and PC3) for the Yorkshire breed. The farrowing year, month, and farm location were also included as covariates. This helps mitigate potential biases [18].

Significance thresholds were determined using the Bonferroni correction method (P = α/N), where P is the corrected significance threshold, α is the single-test significance threshold (0.05), and N is the number of SNPs analyzed per breed. The final SNP datasets retained included 7,941, 8,409, and 8,394 SNPs for the Duroc, Landrace, and Yorkshire breeds, respectively. Population stratification was assessed using quantile-quantile (Q-Q) plots [19]. Manhattan and Q-Q plots were generated using the “qqman” package in R version 4.3.3 [16] to visualize the GWAS results.

Identification and candidate gene search

Genes likely to be linked to NS and NM were identified using the Ensembl Sscrofa11.1 database (www.ensembl.org; accessed June 12, 2024). This database provides comprehensive genomic information for Sus scrofa and other species. Candidate genes were functionally annotated using the Gene Ontology (GO) database (http://geneontology.org; accessed June 12, 2024), which categorizes genes based on their biological processes, molecular functions, and cellular components.

Principal Component Analysis (PCA)

PCA showed distinct patterns of genetic variation across the Duroc, Landrace, and Yorkshire breeds based on genome-wide SNP markers (Fig. 2). The first three principal components (PC1-PC3) captured the majority of the genetic variation within each breed, and their distributions are visualized in scatter plots (Fig. 2a and b, d and e, and g and h). In Duroc pigs, PC1 explained 10.97% of the total genetic variance, whereas PC2 and PC3 accounted for 2.09% and 1.95%, respectively. In Landrace pigs, a similar pattern was observed, with PC1 explaining 9.25% of the variance, followed by PC2 (5.52%), and PC3 (1.29%). In Yorkshire pigs, the variances explained by PC1, PC2, and PC3 were 6.43%, 3.00%, and 2.51%, respectively. Scree plots for each breed (Fig. 2c, f, i) demonstrated a characteristic elbow pattern, with a sharp decline in explained variance after the first few components. This pattern confirmed that the initial principal components (PCs) effectively captured the primary sources of genetic variation within each breed population. The population structure revealed using PCA was incorporated as a covariate in subsequent GWAS to minimize confounding effects and reduce false-positive associations. This approach, combined with comprehensive SNP markers across three commercial breeds, provides robust insights into the genetic architecture underlying NS and NM traits.

Single − parity GWAS for NM and NS in duroc population

GWA analysis of NS and NM in first-parity Duroc pigs identified suggestive SNPs and candidate genes associated with these traits (Table 2). On Chr13, the SNP Affx-115,053,386 was found to be significantly associated with both NS (P = 1.23 × 10⁻⁶) and NM (P = 1.07 × 10⁻⁶), with the nearest genes identified as ENSSSCG00000063239 and ENSSSCG00000063010, respectively. Further significant associations for NS were observed on Chr8 (WU_10.2_8_18342200, P = 2.32 × 10⁻⁶) with the nearest gene ENSSSCG00000056996, whereas for NM, an association was detected on Chr9 (DRGA0009521, P = 4.20 × 10⁻⁶) within the SIDT2 gene. All associations were detected at the suggested significance threshold, and SIDT2 on Chr9 emerging as a notable candidate gene for NM. Single − parity GWAS for NM and NS in landrace population

GWA analysis of SNPs and traits in Landrace sows revealed five candidate genes associated with NS and NM traits across Chr1, 3, 9, and 14 (Table 3). A significant genome-wide association was detected within an uncharacterized gene (ENSSSCG00000004081) on Chr1, where the SNP marker MARC0006874 reached genome-wide significance for NS (P = 9.35 × 10⁻⁷). A nearby SNP (WU_10.2_1_13788588) showed significant associations with both NS (P = 1.41 × 10⁻⁶) and NM (P = 1.81 × 10⁻⁶), indicating pleiotropic effects on piglet mortality traits. Three characterized genes exhibited significant chromosome-wide associations with both the traits: GTF2H3 on Chr14, which is involved in transcriptional regulation and DNA repair; PPP1R9A on Chr9, which mediates synaptic plasticity; and LMTK2 on Chr3, which regulates neuronal protein trafficking. The identification of two genes involved in neuronal function (PPP1R9A and LMTK2) indicate that neuroendocrine pathways play a crucial role in reproductive loss.

Manhattan and quantile–quantile plot

Manhattan plots illustrating SNP positions along chromosomes (X–axis) and their association significance (–log10 p–values; Y–axis) were generated for NS and NM (Figs. 3 and 4) for each breed. The Q–Q plots from the GWAS showed breed-specific patterns in the distribution of P-values relative to the expected values, with adjusted P-values (Fig. 5A–F). The Duroc breed (Fig. 5A and D) exhibited an intermediate pattern, showing excellent alignment throughout most of its distribution. However, a slight deviation in the upper tail indicated the presence of genetic associations, although these were weaker than those observed in the Landrace. The Landrace breed (Fig. 5B and E) demonstrated the most significant departure from the expected distribution. The observed log10(p values) values were significantly higher than those of the Duroc and Yorkshire breeds, indicating robust genetic associations with both NS and NM traits. This suggests that specific genomic regions significantly influence reproductive outcomes in this breed. In contrast, the Yorkshire breed (Fig. 5C and F) showed strong adherence to the expected distribution, with only slight deviations in the extreme tail, thus indicating that genetic associations with these reproductive loss traits may be more modest or evenly distributed across the genome compared to the Landrace breed.

Common and trait − specific genes associated with NM and NS

Figure 6 presents the common and trait-specific genes identified through a GWAS of NS and NM traits in pigs. Five genes (DLGAP2, RNF17, SEL1L2, AP5B1, and KAT5) were uniquely associated with NS, whereas five other genes (SIDT2, NDUFAF5, AKAP12, RCC1L, and BIRC6) were specifically associated with NM. Genes associated with NS, such as DLGAP2 and KAT5, may be involved in developmental processes and cellular mechanisms that lead to increased embryonic or fetal mortality when disrupted. NM trait-associated genes, such as SIDT2 and RCC1L, can be implicated in pathways affecting fetal development, resulting in mummification. Notably, eight genes (GTF2H3, PPP1R9A, LMTK2, GRID1, ZZEF1, ASCC3, KCND2, and SPATS2) were shared between both traits, suggesting that common biological pathways influence piglet mortality. Among these shared genes, GTF2H3 and PPP1R9A are involved in DNA repair and signal transduction and are critical for cell survival and function. The association of these genes with both NS and NM may reflect the underlying mechanisms that affect fetal viability in a more general sense.

Discussion

In swine, litter loss (reproductive loss) is a multifaceted problem, with variations in reproductive traits influenced by numerous genes, each contributing small effects, and a range of non-genetic factors [7, 8]. The identification of candidate genes is a critical step in elucidating the genetic factors that contribute to NS and NM in pig breeds. In the swine industry, NS and NM serve as significant indicators or predictive factors of performance loss associated with litter size. GWAS are effective for identifying significant genetic variants associated with various traits, including the NS and MS.

In the Duroc breed, GWA analysis identified four SNPs, two each on Chr13 and Chr8, which were within a suggested threshold, and the candidate genes and functions associated with them were uncharacterized for NS and NM. However, the SNP ‘DRGA0009521’ was identified as a suggestive marker (p = 4.20 × 10⁻⁶), associated with the candidate gene SIDT2 on Chr9, which is linked to NM in Duroc pigs. A previous study identified SIDT2 (SID1 transmembrane family member 2) as a key protein-coding gene in the autophagy-lysosomal degradation pathway, which is essential for maintaining kidney structure and filtration function in mice [20]. Recent studies have shown that SIDT2 inhibits phosphorothioate antisense oligonucleotide activity by regulating the cellular localization of lysosomes, thus suggesting its involvement in constitutive cellular RNA degradation [21]. However, further experimental validation is required to establish a direct association between SIDT2 expression and the incidence of number-born mummified piglets. The functional implications of the other three identified SNPs in NS and NM remain unclear.

A GWAS conducted on the Landrace breed identified three genes: GTF2H3, located on Chr14; PPP1R9A, located on Chr9; and LMTK2, located on Chr3, as potential candidates associated with NS and NM. A recent study identified general transcription factor IIH subunit 3 (GTF2H3) is a shared gene signature between polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) and endometrial cancer and is linked to pathways related to mitochondrial translational elongation, ribosomal subunits, and ribosome structural constituents [22]. A previous study performed whole-exome sequencing (WES) of a consanguineous Turkish family and identified a GTF2H3 mutation in the brothers with spermatogenic failure [23]. However, detailed studies on its specific functions, expression, and implications in Landrace pigs, including other pig breed reproductive traits (NS and NM), have not been documented in the currently available databases. PPP1R9A on Chr9 has been identified as a promising candidate gene associated with reproductive traits in Landrace pigs. The expression of protein phosphatase 1 regulatory subunit 9 A (PPP1R9A) was originally demonstrated to be brain specific in adult rat tissue [24, 25]. However, it was later shown to be expressed in adult skeletal muscles of mice [26]. It has also been found in both the brain and skeletal muscles of eight adult human tissues [27]. Porcine PPP1R9A is also expressed in a diverse range of tissues, including the skeletal muscle, indicating that the expression patterns of PPP1R9A are species-specific [28]. In addition, PPP1R9A is associated with porcine carcass and meat quality traits [29] and contributes to spine loss in schizophrenia and bipolar disorder through their interactions [30]. They are biallelically expressed in the imprinting of skeletal muscle, fat, pituitary gland, heart, lung, liver, kidney, spleen, stomach, small intestine, uterus, ovary, and testis in pigs [31] and are associated with the success of pregnancy and glucolipid metabolism in goats [32]. Moreover, mouse PPP1R9A shows preferential maternal expression in extraembryonic tissues and biallelic expression in neonatal tissues [33]. Subsequently, it was discovered that this gene is preferentially transcribed from maternally inherited chromosomes solely in the limb and tongue tissues, whereas it exhibits biallelic expression in other embryonic tissues [27]. Lemur tyrosine kinase 2 (LMTK2) is another candidate gene associated with NS and NM in the Landrace breeds. LMTK2 is a member of the transmembrane serine/threonine protein kinase family implicated in neurodegeneration [34] and is postulated to regulate neuroinflammation, possibly through the Nrf2 pathway [35]. Abnormalities in LMTK2 expression and function have been associated with human diseases such as neurodegeneration, cancer, and infertility [36]. Although research on LMTK2 in pigs is limited and there is no direct evidence linking LMTK2 with NS and NM, its function parallels that of other species, particularly humans, indicating its potential implications for veterinary and biomedical research. However, owing to its role in cellular development, signaling, and possibly immunity, it is plausible that LMTK2 may influence fetal survival and development under certain conditions. Although this is speculative at this stage, further genetic and functional studies are required to clarify the potential roles of LMTK2 in NS and NM in pigs.

Several candidate genes, including GRID1, ZZEF1, DLGAP2, NDUFAF5, KCND2, ASCC3, SPATS2, AKAP12, RCC1L, BIRC6, RNF17, SEL1L2, AP5B1, and KAT5 have been identified in Yorkshire pigs. Among the identified genes, three genes (GRID1, ZZEF1, and DLGAP2) were within the genome-wide significance threshold, and 11 genes were within the suggestive threshold genes that were identified to be associated with NS and NM in Yorkshire pigs. A recent study identified that glutamate receptor Ionotropic delta 1 protein (GRID1) regulates the onset of puberty in female rats [37] and is involved in synapse formation, function, plasticity in humans [38] and thalamocortical functioning [39]. Another study identified that it was associated with litter size in sheep [40] and the fecundity of Katahdin ewes [41]. Our analysis identified ZZEF1 on Chr12 as a candidate gene associated with both NS and NM traits, marked by the significant SNP ‘H3GA0034587’ (p = 1.03 × 10⁻⁶ for NS; p = 6.58 × 10⁻⁷ for NM). However a recent study identified ZZEF1 (Zinc Finger ZZ − ZZZ-type and EF − Hand Domain) protein functions remain largely unknown [42]. In Yorkshire breed, DLGAP2 on Chr15 was identified through SNP ‘WU_10.2_15_32958696’ (p = 9.87 × 10⁻⁷) as potentially associated candidate gene with NS trait and the dysregulation of DLGAP2 may contribute to altered synaptic connectivity, leading to developmental defects. In a previous study, DLGAP2 was identified as a susceptibility gene for schizophrenia in humans [43] and is involved in insulin resistance during pregnancy [44]. Further studies have indicated that DLGAP2 mutations in animal models are linked to impaired cognitive function and altered synaptic protein expression [45], suggesting a critical role in neurodevelopmental disorders. Another recent study on Mice with DLGAP2 deficiency revealed signs of intellectual disability [45]. NDUFAF5 is another gene that was identified to be associated with NM in a single-parity GWAS of the Yorkshire breed. A previous study indicated that these genes are involved in the early stages of complex I assembly, specifically in hydroxylation of the NDUFS7 subunit, which is critical for proper complex formation [46]. Studies conducted on Dictyostelium have indicated that deficiencies in NDUFAF5 lead to complex I dysfunction, which in turn causes various mitochondrial diseases [47]. Mutations in NDUFAF5 can result in a wide range of clinical manifestations, ranging from severe lactic acidosis to milder neurological symptoms [48]. Phenotypic variability observed in human cases suggests that mutations can lead to diverse health outcomes, potentially complicating diagnosis and treatment [49]. However, its role in the porcine genome has not yet been elucidated. Another GWA of the Yorkshire breed identified SPATS2 on Chr5 as a candidate gene associated with both NS and NM traits. The SNP ‘ASGA0024707’ at position 15,346,437 bp was located near SPATS2 (1,196 bp upstream for NS; 1,413 bp upstream for NM) and showed significant associations at suggestive threshold p-values with both reproductive loss traits (p = 5.14 × 10⁻⁶ for NS; p = 4.46 × 10⁻⁶ for NM). A recent study in humans indicated SPATS2 (spermatogenesis-associated serine-rich 2) is correlated with cell cycle progression and immune cell infiltration in hepatocellular carcinoma [50] and diagnostic and prognostic biomarkers in liver cancer [51]. Furthermore, our GWA analysis for Yorkshire breed identified three genes exclusively associated with NM: AKAP12 on Chr1 marked by SNP ‘WU_10.2_1_14946807’ (p = 4.70 × 10⁻⁶) and two genes on Chr3: RCC1L with SNP ‘ASGA0014253’ (p = 5.85 × 10⁻⁶) and BIRC6 with SNP ‘ASGA0097775’ (p = 5.90 × 10⁻⁶). However, a recent study also identified AKAP12 of A A-kinase anchoring Protein 12) upregulation in cardiac tissue is associated with accelerated cardiac dysfunction through the AKAP12 − PDE8 axis [52].

Conclusion

NS and NM are critical indicators of reproductive efficiency and economic losses in the swine industry. The results of our GWAS using 70 K BeadChip have revealed a complex genetic architecture underlying these traits in Korean Duroc, Landrace, and Yorkshire pig breeds. Candidate genes were selected based on their functionality and proximity to SNPs that showed significant or suggestive associations with reproductive loss traits (NS and NM, respectively). Our results identified several candidate genes associated with reproductive loss in a single-parity (first-parity) GWAS specific to each breed. This study also identified distinct sets of candidate genes for NS (DLGAP2, RNF17, SEL1L2, AP5B1, and KAT5) and NM (SIDT2, NDUFAF5, AKAP12, RCC1L, and BIRC6) along with eight shared genes, suggesting common biological pathways irrespective of breed. The identified candidate genes directly or indirectly influenced NS and NM through diverse molecular mechanisms, with both distinct and shared genetic components, suggesting a complex regulation of piglet mortality. Integration of these genetic markers into breeding strategies may help improve litter viability and reduce piglet mortality in commercial pig production, and future functional studies will further elucidate their specific roles in reproductive performance.
