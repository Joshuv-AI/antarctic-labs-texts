Numerical Investigation of Gas Migration and Spatiotemporal Evolution in Y‑Type Ventilation Working Faces with Gob-Side Entry Retaining Using COMSOL
Authors: Yaowei Zhai, Yuzhong Yang, Liyun Wu, Wanli Yang, Shicheng Liu, Lei Li, Junqi Lei
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
China has numerous gassy and outburst-prone coal mines. Y-type ventilation with gob-side entry retaining enhances resource recovery and reduces excessive gas in the upper corner but may cause gas buildup at the working face and gob-side entry retaining tail. To address this, a two-dimensional dynamic gas migration model of the goaf was developed in COMSOL to simulate gas concentration evolution under advancing speeds of 6, 9, and 12 m/d and airflow distribution ratios of 2:1, 1.5:1, 1:1, 1:1.5, and 1:2. Higher advancing speeds intensify coal disturbance and gas desorption, resulting in peak concentrations of 1.10 and 0.95% under 12 m/d. At 9 m/d, the values decrease to 0.74 and 0.48%, indicating the optimal advancing speed. Reducing to 6 m/d further lowers concentrations but shows marginal improvement, implying a threshold effect. The airflow distribution ratio significantly affects gas migration: a Z2:F1 ratio lowers working face concentration but raises gob-side entry retaining concentration, while Z1:F2 shows the opposite trend. The 1:1.5 ratio provides the most balanced result, maintaining peak concentrations at 0.62 and 0.55%. The discrepancy between simulation and field data is within 0.03, confirming model reliability. These results provide practical guidance for optimizing ventilation and controlling gas hazards in Y-type ventilated faces.

Introduction

Mine gas remains a critical bottleneck restricting the safe and efficient exploitation of coal resources in China, because its prevention and control are directly related to the intrinsic safety of mining operations. The scientific design, refinement, and optimization of ventilation systems represent a fundamental technological approach for preventing gas accumulation and mitigating disaster risks. In recent years, the widespread adoption of pillarless mining with gob-side entry retaining has significantly improved resource recovery efficiency; nevertheless, it has altered the occurrence conditions and migration environment of mine gas. Consequently, accurately characterizing and understanding the fundamental patterns of gas migration and distribution within the longwall face and gob-side entry retaining has emerged as a critical scientific challenge and a central research priority for advancing precise gas control in coal mines. Extensive research by domestic and international scholars has demonstrated strong commitment to this issue, resulting in significant progress in mine gas control technologies and theoretical understanding.

Zhou et al. studied gas leakage in gob-side entry retaining and proposed a flexible sealing method, elucidating its control on air leakage and gas migration between the main and auxiliary intake airways. Zhang et al. proposed a novel method for gob-side entry retaining in high-gas fully mechanized working faces, effectively improving roadway stability and gas migration, and providing guidance for engineering practice. Tang et al. investigated the influence of gas extraction on air leakage distribution and ventilation characteristics in the goaf using numerical simulation, providing important theoretical guidance for optimizing the coupled control of gas extraction and ventilation systems. Xie et al. analyzed the stability of gob-side entry retaining using flexible concrete formwork, revealing the stress characteristics and instability mechanisms of the surrounding rock and providing theoretical support for long-term stability control and support optimization. Zhang et al. investigated stability control of gob-side entry retaining beneath the goaf under closely spaced coal seam mining, revealing surrounding rock deformation and control mechanisms, and proposing targeted support and roadway layout optimization for safe gob-side entry retaining under complex geological conditions. Yang et al. analyzed the design principles and key techniques of roof prefracturing for gob-side entry retaining, clarifying its impact on surrounding rock stability and roadway formation, and providing guidance for safe and efficient gob-side entry retaining. Jing et al. investigated coupled gas and coal spontaneous combustion hazards during goaf retreat under top-cutting pressure relief, revealing the coupling between gas migration and oxidation heat generation, and proposing a coordinated hazard control system. Zhang et al. employed COMSOL-based numerical simulations to examine gas concentration variations within the goaf under both U-type and Y-type ventilation systems. Chen et al. applied computational fluid dynamics (CFD) techniques to simulate gas concentration and temperature in the goaf and further developed a physical model to determine the optimal ventilation rate for ensuring safe production. Zhou et al. proposed a mathematical model for gas migration in coal mines and validated its reliability through a combination of numerical simulations and field measurements. Building on this foundation, they systematically analyzed the impact of stress disturbances on gas seepage behavior and advanced a thermo–mechanical–gas coupling optimization approach for gas drainage, which was successfully implemented in field practice. Taking the fully mechanized caving face of Wuyang Coal Mine as a case study, Li et al. integrated numerical simulations with field data analysis to reveal gas migration patterns and enrichment characteristics within the caving face, while also clarifying the dominant role of irregular roof-caving structures in abnormal gas accumulation and disaster risk. Li et al. addressed the poorly understood seepage processes in the goaf by establishing a three-dimensional porosity model and simulating variations in pressure distribution and oxygen concentration. Similarly, Jia et al. conducted a systematic analysis of the spatiotemporal distribution of gas in the goaf under W-type ventilation. Their findings revealed the controlling effects of different ventilation structures and mining stages on gas migration and accumulation, thereby providing a theoretical basis and technical support for ventilation optimization and gas hazard prevention. Fu et al. adopted an experimental simulation approach to investigate the distribution of gas flow fields in the composite goaf during the transition of pillarless working faces. Their results demonstrated that changes in ventilation structures during the transition directly influenced gas migration pathways and the evolution of accumulation zones, offering critical technical support for safe face transitions and gas hazard control. To address the issue of excessive gas concentration in gob-side entry retaining, Zhang et al. analyzed a working face in the Sihe Coal Mine. Through a combined approach of numerical simulations and field measurements, they modeled gas migration in interconnected goafs and verified the validity of the simulations against on-site observations. Cheng et al. examined gas flow behavior under protective seam mining conditions in Shanxi’s Shaqu Coal Mine, where they established a gas migration model and optimized drainage borehole layout parameters, significantly improving drainage efficiency and providing both theoretical and practical support for gas management under protective seam mining. Li et al. investigated gas migration patterns under Y-type ventilation in Yangquan, comparing the results with those under conventional U-type ventilation, thereby highlighting the distinct differences in migration mechanisms between the two systems. Similarly, Wang et al. studied a working face with a gob-side entry retaining in the Xin’an Mine using ANSYS Fluent software, conducting a comparative analysis of seepage distributions in the goaf under conditions with and without drainage. Xia et al. utilized numerical simulations to explore gas migration under Y-type ventilation and demonstrated the influence of different ventilation parameter configurations on gas concentration distributions in the goaf, providing both theoretical insights and simulation support for gas control in Y-type ventilation faces. Likewise, Ma et al. and Li et al. numerically examined the effect of porosity variations on gas migration and concluded that gas migration velocity is positively correlated with porosity. , Zhao et al. focused on buried-pipe drainage in fully mechanized caving faces, clarifying the controlling effects of pipe length and placement on drainage efficiency, thereby offering practical guidance and theoretical support for optimizing pipe layouts. Finally, Gou and Han simulated gas migration patterns under Y-type ventilation in the Xiwukuang mining district of Gaozhuang Coal Mine, further enriching the understanding of gas behavior in such systems.

Although previous studies have provided valuable insights into gas migration in the goaf, limited attention has been given to the dynamic behavior of gas migration in the longwall face and gob-side entry retaining under Y-type ventilation, particularly when accounting for variations in advancing speed and air volume ratio. In this study, a fully mechanized caving face in a coal mine is taken as the engineering background, and COMSOL is employed to quantitatively investigate the spatiotemporal evolution of gas migration in both the longwall face and gob-side entry retaining. The results elucidate the variation patterns of gas concentration under different advancing speeds and air distribution ratios, thereby offering a scientific basis for optimizing gas control and improving mine safety.

Engineering Background

This study was conducted on a fully mechanized caving face in a coal mine, with a designed dip length of 284 m and a recoverable strike length of 2435 m. The panel covers an area of 691,540 m2 and contains recoverable reserves of 35.61 Mt. The coal seam has an average thickness of 8.2 m, and the working face applies the top-coal caving method with a mining-to-caving ratio of 1:1.5. The recovery rate is 85%, while the residual coal thickness ranges from 0.5 to 0.9 m. A Y-type ventilation system was employed, as illustrated in Figure . Field measurements indicated that the airflow in the main intake airway was 1324 m3/min, while that in the auxiliary intake airway was 882 m3/min. The cross-sectional areas of the main and auxiliary intake airways were both 17.5 m2, whereas that of the return airway was 14 m2. The floor elevation ranged from 687.092 to 713.124 m. The absolute gas emission rate of the working face was 3.91 m3/min, and the relative gas emission was 42.01 m3/t, thereby classifying the mine as a high-gas mine.

The underlying mechanism of this phenomenon can be attributed to the combined effects of mining-induced stress disturbance and overburden compaction. Along the margins of the goaf, the effect of stress release is relatively weak, leading to limited failure of the coal and rock mass and insufficient compaction. Consequently, porosity and permeability in these lateral regions remain notably higher. In contrast, the deeper central regions of the goaf are subjected to intense compaction from the overlying strata. The remnant coal in these regions becomes progressively denser as fractures close, resulting in consistently low porosity and permeability.

Boundary Conditions

In the velocity field of the goaf, the main and auxiliary intake airways are defined as velocity inlets, while the gob-side entry retaining is specified as a pressure outlet. The interface between the working face and the goaf is treated as an internal boundary, and all remaining boundaries are assigned no-slip wall conditions. In the gas field, the main and auxiliary intake airways are specified as gas volume fraction inlets, while the gob-side entry retaining is defined as an outflow concentration boundary. The intake airways and the working face are modeled as internal flow boundaries, with the initial gas volume fraction set to zero. All other boundaries are assigned zero-flux conditions.

Simulation Results and Analysis

Velocity Field Distribution Characteristics in the Goaf

The airflow rates of the main and auxiliary intake airways are 1324 and 882 m3/min, respectively. Both intake airways have a cross-sectional area of 17.5 m2. Accordingly, the airflow velocities are set to 1.26 m/s in the main intake airway and 1.05 m/s in the auxiliary intake airway. The working face advances at a rate of 12 m/d over a period of 24 days. The corresponding pressure field and air leakage distribution within the goaf are illustrated in Figure .

Distribution of pressure field and air leakage in the goaf.

Figure a shows that the overall pressure distribution decreases from the upper left to the lower right, with the main intake side exhibiting significantly higher pressure than the gob-side entry retaining and the central region of the goaf. Along the strike direction, pressure progressively decreases toward the deeper regions of the goaf and increases toward the working face. Along the dip direction, pressure decreases toward the gob-side entry retaining while increasing toward the main intake side. The elevated airflow rate in the main intake airway induces stronger air leakage into the adjacent goaf, resulting in higher pressure. In contrast, the auxiliary intake airway supplies a lower airflow rate, causing weaker air leakage and correspondingly reduced pressure. These pressure patterns are closely associated with the spatial distributions of porosity and permeability. The lateral regions of the goaf exhibit higher porosity and permeability, facilitating gas migration and accumulation, thereby creating localized high-pressure zones. However, in the central deep region, the caved strata gradually compact under the overlying rock weight, decreasing porosity and permeability. Consequently, air leakage becomes minimal and pressure declines, while increased flow resistance restricts inward diffusion, forming a decompression zone.

According to the leakage airflow field distribution shown in Figure b, the leakage airflow mainly enters the gob-side entry retaining from both the main and auxiliary intake airways, and the overall airflow distribution is strongly influenced by the pressure gradient. Airflow primarily occurs in regions with high porosity and permeability, where the spatial distribution of these parameters determines the pressure field structure and thus affects the leakage path. Moreover, the ventilation pressure difference directly regulates the shape and intensity of the leakage fieldlarger pressure differences enhance the pressure gradient and increase airflow velocity and penetration depth. The geometric structure of the gob-side entry retaining and the dynamic evolution of the goaf compaction zone also have notable effects. As compaction progresses, porosity and permeability decrease, restricting the leakage flow and altering its distribution.

In summary, the leakage airflow field of the gob-side entry retaining is jointly controlled by ventilation pressure difference, roadway geometry, and compaction evolution, and these coupled factors should be comprehensively considered to better understand its dynamic behavior.

Influence of Different Advancing Speeds on the Gas Concentration Field Distribution

Effect of Different Advancing Speeds on Gas Concentration in the Gob-Side Entry Retaining

Advancing speeds of 12, 9, and 6 m/d are considered, with airflow rates in the main and auxiliary intake airways set to 1324 and 882 m3/min, respectively. Gas concentration measurements are sampled along the goaf from the main intake airway to the auxiliary intake airway at 8 m intervals. Similarly, along the gob-side entry retaining, data are collected from the section nearest the working face to the far end of the entry, also at 8 m intervals. To enhance figure clarity, selected simulation data are locally adjusted. The resulting simulation outcomes are presented in Figure . For a more intuitive comparison of the effects of different advancing speeds on gas concentrations in the gob-side entry retaining and the working face, the simulation data from Figure are extracted and postprocessed to generate Figure , where Y and W denote the gas concentrations in the gob-side entry retaining and the working face, respectively.

Gas concentration distribution at different working face advancing speeds.

Variation of gas concentration along the gob-side entry retaining and working face at different advancing speeds when the working face has advanced 288 m.

As shown in Figure , the peak gas concentration in the goaf gradually decreases with decreasing face advance rate. For face advance rates of 12, 9, and 6 m/d, the overall gas concentration distribution in the goaf exhibits a fan-shaped pattern, with concentrations generally decreasing from the main intake side toward the auxiliary intake side. In the shallow region along the main intake side, gas concentrations remain relatively uniform, as indicated by widely spaced contour lines, reflecting a gradual increase from low to high values. In contrast, the shallow region along the auxiliary intake side exhibits a steeper concentration gradient, with closely spaced contour lines, indicating rapid gas accumulation. In the deep region of the goaf, both sides exhibit closely spaced contour lines, suggesting a rapid increase in gas concentrations.

As the face advance rate decreases, the spacing between adjacent gas concentration contour lines generally increases, particularly in the shallow region when the rate drops from 12 to 9 m/d, whereas changes in the deep region remain comparatively minor. When the face advance rate further decreases from 9 to 6 m/d, the contour spacing in the shallow region changes only slightly, and in the deep region, the variation is even less pronounced compared with the transition from 12 to 9 m/d. Along the strike direction, gas concentrations increase progressively from the working face toward the deeper regions of the goaf, irrespective of the face advance rate. Near the working face, the concentration gradient remains relatively gentle, as evidenced by widely spaced contour lines, whereas in the deeper region, the contour lines converge, indicating rapid gas accumulation and reduced local gas release efficiency. This trend is particularly evident when the face advance rate decreases from 12 to 9 m/d, as the spacing between adjacent contour lines increases in both the shallow and deep regions, resulting in a more stable and progressive increase in gas concentrations.

The observed phenomenon is primarily attributed to the relatively high airflow in the main intake airway, providing strong ventilation. The wide disturbance radius of this airflow enables gas in the main intake region of the goaf to be effectively diluted and removed. In contrast, airflow on the auxiliary intake side is comparatively weaker and further constrained by the cross-sectional area of the gob-side entry retaining. The resulting complex airflow pathways reduce ventilation efficiency, thereby increasing the likelihood of gas accumulation on the auxiliary intake side. Continuous advancement of the working face induces damage to the coal body and generates internal fractures, enabling gas migration from the working face into the goaf along airflow pathways. Owing to the relatively large cross-sectional area, low pressure, and limited disturbance within the gob-side entry retaining, this region serves as a preferential pathway for gas migration and accumulation.

As shown in Figure , under identical working face advancing speeds, gas concentrations at measurement points along the gob-side entry retaining are consistently higher than those on the working face. Across varying advancing speeds, higher speeds correspond to increased gas concentrations along the gob-side entry retaining. The gas concentration along the gob-side entry retaining exhibits a pronounced increasing trend with the advancing distance of the working face, particularly near the far end of the entry. At an advancing speed of 12 m/d, the peak gas concentration at the end of the gob-side entry retaining reaches approximately 1.1%, attaining the gas warning threshold. When the advancing speed decreases to 9 m/d, the peak gas concentration at the end of the entry reduces to approximately 0.75%, corresponding to a decrease of about 0.35% relative to the 12 m/d case. This indicates that reducing the working face advancing speed has a notable positive effect on gas management along the gob-side entry retaining. Further decreasing the advancing speed to 6 m/d results in a peak concentration of approximately 0.65% at the end of the entry, a reduction of about 0.05% relative to the 9 m/d case. The relatively small difference in peak concentrations between 12 and 9 m/d suggests that once the advancing speed falls below a certain threshold, its effect on gas concentration along the gob-side entry retaining becomes marginal. As illustrated in Figure , variations in advancing speed exert minimal impact on the section of the gob-side entry retaining near the working face, where gas concentration remains essentially unchanged. However, as the working face advances, gas concentration increases with distance from the face, attaining a maximum at the far end of the gob-side entry retaining.

The primary reason for this phenomenon is that higher advancing speeds accelerate coal and rock damage and fracture development, causing the goaf to progressively transition from a compacted state to a fractured state. This increase in local porosity generates temporary storage zones for gas desorbed from the working face and residual coal within the goaf. Specifically, at higher advancing speeds, the increased porosity facilitates rapid gas accumulation, concentrating in the low-velocity region near the far end of the gob-side entry retaining, thereby making this area highly susceptible to gas enrichment. Simultaneously, rapid fracture development at higher advancing speeds, coupled with the corresponding increase in permeability, enhances the connectivity between gas migration pathways in the goaf and the gob-side entry retaining, further promoting gas accumulation along the entry. Moreover, owing to the considerable length of the gob-side entry retaining, limited ventilation disturbance and low airflow velocity exacerbate the formation of localized high-concentration gas zones.

Influence of Different Advancing Speeds on Gas Concentration at the Working Face

As shown in Figure , the variation trend of gas concentration at the working face generally corresponds to that observed along the gob-side entry retaining. However, both the rate of increase and the overall concentration levels remain lower than those in the gob-side entry retaining. Specifically, the gas concentration at the working face is higher on the side adjacent to the auxiliary intake airway and gradually decreases toward the main intake side. At an advancing speed of 12 m/d, the peak gas concentration at the working face reaches approximately 0.95%, approaching the gas warning threshold. When the advancing speed decreases to 9 m/d, the peak concentration reduces to approximately 0.50%, representing a decrease of about 0.45% compared with the case at 12 m/d. Further decreasing the advancing speed to 6 m/d reduces the peak concentration to approximately 0.40%, which is only about 0.10% lower than that at 9 m/d. Compared with the decreases observed between 12 and 9 m/d, the reduction in gas concentration at the working face is relatively small, indicating that once the advancing speed drops below 9 m/d, its effect on gas concentration becomes marginal.

As the working face advancing speed increases, the coal mass experiences greater disturbance, resulting in more extensively developed fractures and enhanced local stress relief. Consequently, the gas desorption rate accelerates, resulting in higher gas concentrations at the working face. Simultaneously, the increased advancing speed intensifies coal fragmentation and enhances fracture connectivity, forming a high-permeability zone centered around the working face.

This condition facilitates the migration of gas desorbed from residual coal in the deeper regions of the goaf toward the working face, thereby further elevating gas concentrations at the working face. Gas concentration is determined not only by the intensity of gas release but also by the ventilation capacity at the working face. At relatively low advancing speeds, the airflow supplied by both the main and auxiliary intake airways is sufficient to dilute or remove the gas released from the working face. However, as the advancing speed increases, the ventilation capacity becomes insufficient to dilute the released gas, resulting in elevated gas concentrations at the working face. Moreover, further increases in advancing speed are likely to induce local airflow turbulence and vortex formation near the working face, further promoting gas accumulation.

In summary, selecting a working face advancing speed of 9 m/d not only ensures improved coal production but also maintains gas concentrations at both the working face and the gob-side entry retaining within safe and acceptable limits. As demonstrated in the previous section, the airflow intensity in the main and auxiliary intake airways exerts a dominant influence on gas concentrations at the working face. Accordingly, establishing an appropriate airflow distribution ratio between the main and auxiliary intake airways can effectively mitigate excessive gas concentrations at both the far end of the gob-side entry retaining and the auxiliary intake side near the working face. Therefore, in the subsequent analysis, a working face advancing speed of 9 m/d is adopted, and the optimal airflow distribution ratio is determined to maintain gas concentrations within safe and acceptable levels at both the working face and the gob-side entry retaining.

Influence of Different Airflow Distribution Ratios on the Distribution of Gas Concentration Fields

According to Section , the optimal working face advancing speed is 9 m/d. Therefore, this subsection investigates the variations in gas concentration under different airflow distribution ratio conditions at this advancing speed. While maintaining a constant total airflow, the gas concentration at both the working face and the gob-side entry retaining was analyzed under various airflow distribution ratios. For convenience, the airflow distribution ratio of the main intake airway is denoted as Z, and that of the auxiliary intake airway as F. In this working face, the baseline ventilation corresponds to a main intake airflow of 1324 m3/min and an auxiliary intake airflow of 882 m3/min, yielding an approximate ratio of Z1.5:F1. Based on field measurements and a review of relevant literature, five airflow distribution scenarios were considered: Z2:F1 (1470 m3/min: 736 m3/min), Z1.5:F1 (1324 m3/min: 882 m3/min), Z1:F1 (1103 m3/min: 1,103 m3/min), Z1:F1.5 (882 m3/min: 1324 m3/min), and Z1:F2 (736 m3/min: 1470 m3/min). Numerical simulations were conducted at an advancing speed of 9 m/d, and the resulting gas concentration variations at the working face and along the gob-side entry retaining after 32 days of advancement are presented in Figure .

Range of gas concentration variations under different airflow distribution ratios.

Influence of Different Airflow Distribution Ratios on Gas Concentration in the Goaf-Side Entry

As shown in Figures and a, as the proportion of airflow in the main intake airway gradually decreases, the gas concentration along the gob-side entry retaining correspondingly declines, and its spatial distribution transitions from a concentrated pattern to a more uniform configuration. Specifically, under an airflow distribution ratio of Z2:F1, the main intake airway maintains a relatively high airflow proportion, resulting in an overall pressure field inclined toward the working face. In the deep section of the gob-side entry retaining, a distinct low-pressure zone forms, leading to reduced airflow velocity and diminished gas penetration. As shown in Figure a, the high-concentration zone is mainly distributed within the 250–300 m section of the entry, where the peak gas concentration reaches approximately 1%, indicating a potential gas overlimit hazard. When the airflow distribution ratio is adjusted to Z1.5:F1, the gas concentration in the gob-side entry retaining remains comparatively high, with the peak concentration decreasing to approximately 0.74%, while the overall concentration remains elevated. This phenomenon can be attributed to the increased airflow in the auxiliary intake airway, which enhances dilution capacity, enabling gas migrating from the goaf into the gob-side entry retaining to be effectively diluted and transported out.

Range of gas concentration variations in the gob-side entry retaining and working face under different airflow distribution ratios.

When the airflow distribution ratio is set to Z1:F1, the airflow in the main and auxiliary intake airways is balanced, leading to an evenly shared ventilation load between the two roadways. Under this condition, the gas enrichment zone remains concentrated in the deeper section of the gob-side entry retaining, and the peak gas concentration decreases to approximately 0.68%. At this stage, the reduced airflow in the main intake airway results in lower leakage airflow entering the goaf from the working face. Consequently, gas desorbed from residual coal in the deeper goaf is not effectively transported and instead migrates into the gob-side entry retaining, partially diminishing the gas concentration within the entry. When the airflow distribution ratio is adjusted to Z1:F1.5, the auxiliary intake airway supplies a higher airflow than the main intake airway. The increased fresh airflow penetrates more effectively into the deeper sections of the gob-side entry retaining, diluting the accumulated gas at the tail end. As a result, the peak gas concentration reduces to approximately 0.55%. Under the Z1:F2 scenario, the auxiliary intake airway airflow reaches its maximum, resulting in a significant alteration of the overall airflow structure. In this case, the gas concentration in the gob-side entry retaining declines to the lowest level among all scenarios, with a peak value of only 0.50%. Gas concentration along the gob-side entry retaining increases steadily, forming a continuous transport pathway that effectively suppresses gas accumulation at the tail end of the entry. Conversely, when the main intake airway airflow is at its minimum, the leakage airflow entering the goaf is also the lowest compared with other airflow distribution ratios. As shown in Figure , under this condition, the peak gas concentration in the goaf reaches 15.3%, exceeding the values observed under other airflow distribution scenarios.

This indicates that the leakage airflow entering the goaf from the main intake airway is insufficient to dilute and transport gas desorbed from residual coal within the goaf. Consequently, the desorbed gas migrates into the gob-side entry retaining. Considering both the limited leakage airflow and the enhanced auxiliary intake ventilation, this specific airflow distribution ratio results in the lowest gas concentration within the gob-side entry retaining.

The variation in gas concentration along the gob-side entry retaining under different airflow ratios is primarily governed by the pressure field of the ventilation system. When the airflow proportion in the main intake airway is excessively high, the pressure center shifts toward the working face, reducing the auxiliary intake airway’s air supply capacity. Consequently, airflow velocity in the deeper sections of the gob-side entry retaining decreases, forming a low-pressure stagnation zone. Simultaneously, gas desorption from the disturbed coal mass is intensified, hindering timely dilution and triggering a compounded effect of “intense disturbance–weak ventilation–severe accumulation.” As the airflow proportion in the auxiliary intake airway increases, the airflow along the gob-side entry retaining penetrates more effectively, creating a more balanced pressure field. This enhances gas dilution capability and establishes a ventilation pattern characterized by “wide coverage–stable migration,” which effectively reduces both the peak gas concentration and its gradient of increase. When considering only the influence of airflow ratios on gas distribution within the gob-side entry retaining, the scenario with a distribution ratio of Z2:F1 should be excluded, whereas greater emphasis should be placed on the scenarios with ratios of Z1:F1.5 and Z1:F2.

Influence of Different Airflow Distribution Ratios on Gas Concentration at the Working Face

As shown in Figures and b, with the gradual increase in airflow proportion from the auxiliary intake airway, the gas concentration at the working face rises, with higher concentrations observed on the side adjacent to the auxiliary intake airway. Under the airflow distribution ratio Z2:F1, the peak gas concentration at the working face reaches 0.39%, representing the lowest value among the five distribution scenarios. At this stage, gas is primarily concentrated near the working face on the auxiliary intake side.

Gas is primarily concentrated near the working face on the side adjacent to the auxiliary intake airway. This occurs mainly because the airflow in the main intake airway is relatively high, enabling fresh air to pass through the working face and effectively dilute and remove gas released from the coal seam. Under the airflow distribution ratio Z1.5:F1, although the airflow in the main intake airway decreases, it still dominates. Under this condition, the overall gas concentration at the working face rises, with the peak reaching approximately 0.5%. The increased airflow in the auxiliary intake airway modifies the pressure difference at both ends of the working face, slightly altering the airflow direction. Consequently, local airflow velocity decreases, reducing its capacity to dilute or remove gas at the working face. Under the airflow distribution ratio Z1:F1, the peak gas concentration at the working face further rises to approximately 0.59%. The gas accumulation zone remains concentrated near the side adjacent to the auxiliary intake airway at the working face. At this point, airflow in the main and auxiliary intake airways is equal, creating a balanced pressure difference at both ends of the working face. This results in a more uniform airflow distribution across the working face, but also leads to reduced airflow velocity and relatively weaker local ventilation capacity. As a result, gas at the working face cannot be adequately diluted or transported, resulting in higher concentrations than in the previous two airflow distribution scenarios.Under the airflow distribution ratio Z1:F1.5, the peak gas concentration at the working face reaches 0.62%, representing only a slight increase compared with the previous scenario. Here, the reduced airflow in the main intake airway is insufficient to dilute or remove gas entering the working face. Nevertheless, the difference compared with the Z1:F1 scenario is minimal, with only a modest increase in gas concentration near the side adjacent to the auxiliary intake airway.

Under the airflow distribution ratio Z1:F2, the peak gas concentration at the working face reaches 0.92%, representing a substantial increase compared with the previous airflow scenario. In this scenario, airflow in the auxiliary intake airway is significantly higher than that in the main intake airway, resulting in higher air pressure on the auxiliary intake side than on the main intake side. This markedly reduces the air supply capacity of the main intake airway, while the auxiliary intake side is prone to vortex formation, altering airflow paths and generating a turbulent zone. In certain local areas of the working face, airflow may slow or even stagnate, hindering gas removal. Consequently, gas accumulates, forming concentration zones that pose significant safety risks.

Based on the above analysis, larger airflow in the main intake airway provides stronger ventilation control, enabling more effective dilution and removal of gas at the working face. Conversely, when the airflow proportion in the auxiliary intake airway is excessively high, it disrupts the dominant airflow structure of the overall ventilation system, resulting in elevated gas concentrations at the working face and increasing the risk of gas outbursts. Therefore, when evaluating the effect of different airflow ratios on working face gas concentration in isolation, the Z1:F2 scenario should be excluded. Priority should instead be given to ventilation scenarios in which the airflow proportion in the main intake airway remains relatively high.

In summary, based on the analysis in Section , both Z1:F1.5 and Z1:F2 airflow ratios maintain gas concentration in the gob-side entry retaining at approximately 0.5%. However, according to Section , the Z1:F2 scenario causes gas concentration at the working face to rapidly reach 1%, making it unsuitable. Consequently, the optimal airflow ratio for this working face is Z1:F1.5.

Based on the previous simulation analysis, the optimal advancing speed for this working face is 9 m/d, with the optimal airflow distribution ratio set at 1 for the main intake airway and 1.5 for the auxiliary intake airway. This configuration ensures effective control of gas concentrations in both the goaf and the gob-side entry retaining. Conclusions

This study established a multifield coupled numerical model for a Y-type ventilated longwall face to investigate the distribution characteristics of gas concentrations in both the working face and the gob-side entry retaining under varying face advance velocities and airflow distribution ratios. The numerical simulation results were subsequently validated against on-site measurements. The main conclusions are summarized as follows:1.The advance velocity of the working face has a significant impact on gas concentration levels at both the working face and the gob-side entry retaining. COMSOL simulations employing a moving mesh approach are conducted to investigate the effect of different advance velocities. The results show that at an advance velocity of 12 m/d, the peak gas concentrations at the working face and within the gob-side entry retaining reach approximately 0.95 and 1.1%, respectively, posing a substantial risk of gas exceedance. When the advance velocity is reduced to 9 and 6 m/d, the gas concentrations decrease markedly, reaching approximately 0.74 and 0.48%, and 0.65 and 0.38%, respectively. Notably, when the advance rate falls below 9 m/d, further reductions in gas concentration at both locations become less pronounced, indicating a critical threshold effect. Considering these results alongside operational factors, an optimal working face advance velocity of 9 m/d is recommended.2.A rational air volume distribution ratio exerts a significant influence on gas distribution. Under a constant total airflow and a working face advance velocity of 9 m/d, increasing the airflow from the main intake airway toward the auxiliary intake airway effectively reduces gas concentration at the tail end of the gob-side entry retaining, whereas the inverse configuration more efficiently lowers gas concentration at the working face. Based on the simulation results, the optimal airflow ratio is determined to be 1:1.5 for the main and auxiliary intake airways. Under this configuration, the peak gas concentrations in the gob-side entry retaining and at the working face are 0.55 and 0.62%, respectively, demonstrating a more balanced and controlled gas distribution.3.The COMSOL numerical simulation results show good agreement with field measurements, confirming the validity of the model. The discrepancies between the measured gas concentrations and the simulated values do not exceed 0.03, demonstrating that the coupled COMSOL model developed in this study can accurately capture the spatial distribution and accumulation characteristics of gas in both the working face and the gob-side entry retaining. These findings provide a robust theoretical framework and technical reference for similar Y-type ventilated working faces, offering valuable guidance for optimizing ventilation and mitigating gas-related hazards in high-gas coal mines.
