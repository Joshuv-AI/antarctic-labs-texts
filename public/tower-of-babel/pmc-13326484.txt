Polio transition in the African Region: progress, challenges and prospects for health systems integration
Authors: Terna Nomhwange, Samafilan Ainan, Modjirom Nduotabe, Usman Adamu, Ogonna Chinwike, Kennedy Adejoh, Sule Adamu, Christopher Kamugisha, Ticha Johnson, Casimir Manengu, Gedi Mohamed
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe Global Polio Eradication Initiative (GPEI) has made substantial progress towards global eradication of polio. As regions, including Africa, approach the final stages of this goal, countries must ensure that essential polio functions are sustained and effectively integrated into national health systems. To support this transition, the World Health Organization(WHO), through its regional offices, has guided countries using the Polio Transition Global Vision and its Monitoring and Evaluation Framework. This paper reviews progress made in transition planning across 15 polio transition priority countries in the WHO African Region and anticipated challenges, opportunities, and prospects for integrating polio assets and essential functions into broader health systems.MethodA secondary review of polio transition indicator performance across 15 priority countries in the African region was conducted using programme data from 2015 to 2024(or years with available data). Trends were assessed across indicators defined in the Polio Transition M&E Framework, including routine immunization coverage, emergency preparedness capacities and domestic health financing.ResultsOver the past decade, none of the 15 priority countries consistently achieved ≥ 90% routine polio containing vaccine coverage. Only Burkina Faso and Kenya have been closest to these coverage levels in the region. Timely shipment of polio samples remains a consistent challenge, particularly in countries where GPEI support has declined. The regional International Health Regulations(IHR) average capacity score remains suboptimal, averaging 51, below the global score over the last 10 years. These underscore weaknesses in epidemic preparedness and response capacities. These weaknesses, within a broader context of low domestic health prioritization, as reflected in national health accounts, further constrain transition readiness.ConclusionProgress on polio transition in the African region remains limited, with gaps in immunization, emergency preparedness and financing, gaps made riskier by declining donor support. To protect Polio eradication gains, countries must strengthen transition planning, improve coordination amongst partners and increase domestic health investments. Successful implementation of country national plans can only be achieved with increased domestic financing through innovative funding models, improved polio transition governance, and integration strategies towards safeguarding polio gains and reinforcing broader health system resilience for a polio free world.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-026-13357-9.Keywords: Polio transition, GPEI, WHO AFRO, Integration, Health system strengthening

Background

The global polio eradication initiative (GPEI) has supported polio eradication efforts since the WHA declaration to end polio in 1988 [1, 2]. Various strategies, including mass vaccination with polio vaccines, have resulted in a significant reduction in the incidence of reported polio cases [3]. Huge investments over the years have been made in the eradication efforts, including essential immunization, surveillance and outbreak response for other vaccine preventable diseases (VPDs) [4–6]. Polio eradication efforts were built on lessons learned from Smallpox eradication, the only known human disease eradicated in history [7]. Polio eradication efforts have not only prevented paralysis of millions of children in the African region but also provided wider health emergency response support as well as wider health systems strengthening [8–12]. This is important as countries in the region strive towards addressing health system challenges, including financing, workforce and governance [13, 14].

As we approach timelines set for the eradication targets, the programme has made strategic changes towards integration and leveraging the polio assets to support and strengthen overall health systems. This is even more important as global health financial resources continue to dwindle, including bilateral funding to countries and calls for greater efficiencies and fiscal resilience [15]. In 2025, alone, about $1 Billion was budgeted for polio eradication, with about $70million(reported GPEI budgets as of 16th October 2025) in these priority countries in the African Region [16].

Polio transition aims to sustain, or keep at a certain level, all the essential polio functions needed to achieve and maintain eradication, while also ensuring that these tools, resources, and expertise are deployed to support other health interventions alongside polio and, eventually, through the routine health system [17, 18]. The strategic direction for polio transition is outlined in the polio eradication endgame and the polio transition strategic framework, referred to as the Global Vision [19]. These efforts focus on priority countries based on GPEI footprints, funding and epidemiology of the polio virus. Polio transition priority countries are member states that the GPEI considers require a strategy for integrating polio assets and functions into their national health system [20]. On the other hand, health system integration is the coordination of health services and collaboration among various providers to achieve an effective health system [21]. Currently, there are 21 priority countries across three WHO regions, with most of them in Africa [22]. Polio transition is seen as a bridge between the current polio eradication efforts and the envisaged future of a polio free world [23]. Transition and integration are not considered as endpoints, but rather processes for achieving sustainability. Sustainability, in the context of polio transition, focuses on the processes and capacity to maintain or improve the health benefits achieved through the polio programme and continue programme activities through organized structures within national health systems. This is based on Scheirer and Dearing’s (2011) concept of sustainability, referring to the continuation of programmes [24].

The polio transition targets the integration of polio assets into wider health systems while ensuring leverage using best practices from the polio programme to support other health priorities as required by national governments. There have been some suggestions for the GPEI to cede control to other programmes for integration to succeed [25]. Some concerns have been raised regarding missed opportunities by the polio programme for integrated efforts in supporting other non-polio SIAs and routine immunization(RI) and health systems [26]. In the African Region, the majority of countries have prioritized essential immunization, disease surveillance and outbreak detection and response as part of the transition planning effort [25]. This approach proposes sustainable funding through national government budgets and integration towards improved efficiencies in the health system, while external funding and reliance on the GPEI gradually wanes [27]. Progress in the implementation of the global vision for polio transition is guided by a monitoring and implementation framework with strategic and process indicators: Immunization coverage trends, International Health Regulations (IHR) average scores from the e-SPAR and domestic health financing. The domestic financing assessment is expected to guide predictable funding to support bridging identified gaps [28].

The transition independent monitoring board (TIMB), an independent monitoring body, tracks progress towards these goals and makes recommendations annually. In its 2024 report, the board highlighted the challenges based on country delivery capacity and the complex environment, especially in Africa [29]. Progress towards achieving these outlined targets in the African region has been slow and far between. This has been affected by unrealistic transition planning, ongoing outbreaks of circulating vaccine-derived polio viruses (cVDPVs), including other health emergencies, amongst worsening economic conditions. Following a ministerial conference on immunization in Africa held in 2016, the region had committed to the development of transition plans, appoint government bodies and designate coordination for transition [30]. In the African region, the WHO coordinates these efforts in 15 countries regarded as priority for polio transition.

The GPEI stopped funding 37 out of the 47 countries in the WHO African region through the standard financial resource requirement (FRR) for the polio programme in 2022. The budgets for the polio programme were then included in the WHO base budget and allocated to its country offices as part of the overall funding support. This implies that countries are expected to continue to sustain these essential functions, like the surveillance network, through government budgets and external funding mobilized in the countries.

Disease surveillance is an essential function of polio eradication, with timely sample collection and shipment, a critical component. Delays in sample shipment are one of the key challenges that the region needs to address towards ensuring timely confirmation and response [31]. In the African region, the average time for VDPV2 confirmation from sample collection to result communication is 46 days, with variation between countries [32]. The Global Polio Surveillance Action Plan (GPSAP) 2022–2024 had set a time lag of 35 days between paralysis or waste work sample collection to outbreak confirmation by polio labs [33, 34]. Any timelines beyond this set threshold are considered inefficient and contribute to the continued circulation of polio viruses.

As the global health landscape continues to evolve, including donor fatigue and ongoing discussions about the possible sunsetting of GPEI at the end of the eradication goal, it is important to assess the capacities of countries in Africa and understand any associated risks of not maintaining these polio assets or ensuring their eventual integration into health systems [27]. Countries in the region will benefit from understanding what gaps exist in their current preparations for a polio free world and what risks exist to their health system when GPEI ceases to fund polio essential functions.

This paper aims to review the progress (or lack of) of polio transition efforts in the African region by tracking the performance of key essential functions required for an effective polio transition and efforts required to maintain a polio-free continent. It also examines the current challenges and opportunities available for integrating and sustaining these functions in priority countries in Africa within a changing global health donor landscape.

Methods

We conducted a descriptive review using secondary programmatic data available at the WHO Regional office for Africa over a period covering 2015–2024 across the 47 countries in the WHO region. This review focused on the designated fifteen polio transition countries in the region based on the Polio Transition Monitoring and Evaluation (M&E) framework. The indicators and variables reviewed relate to the three focus areas of polio transition, including OPV 3 vaccination coverage as an indicator for Immunization and population immunity, IHR performance scores for Emergency preparedness and response and domestic prioritization of health in national budgets using information from national health accounts. We also tracked the trends of AFP and Environmental surveillance shipments; an indicator for the quality of Polio surveillance, the key performance required for Polio eradication efforts and the number of active case searches conducted by surveillance officers using the real-time reporting of Integrated supportive supervision(ISS) visits to health facilities.

Data management

All data sets were retrieved and collated from the current polio eradication programme database at the WHO regional office in Brazzaville between April and June 2025. Data were analysed and presented using simple frequencies and proportions. All electronic program data submitted to the WHO database were downloaded in CVF format and summarized following data cleaning using the programme’s data quality tools. The results were collated into frequencies and proportions and presented in tables, maps and charts.

Results

Surveillance

Data from 44 out of 47 member states reported Acute flaccid paralysis (AFP)samples collected and shipped to national polio labs across the region, as shown in Fig. 1. The median duration for sample shipment in the region for the reviewed period was 7 days, regardless of the location of the national lab receiving the sample. The duration of shipment in five out of 44 (15%) was ≥ 14 days from sample collection to receipt of samples at the laboratory for testing. These include Comoros, Equatorial Guinea, Gabon, Mozambique and Sierra Leone. Of these five, only Mozambique is a priority country for polio transition in the region.

The median duration for the shipment of wastewater samples following collection from Environmental surveillance sites to arrive at the polio lab is 4 days. In Fig. 2, we show that only 11 out of 43(8.7%) countries conducting wastewater surveillance have reported time lags of more than 7 days. Seven of these countries have consistently, in the years reviewed, had ES samples shipment lasting > 7 days before arriving at the lab. Cumulatively over the past 5 years, only 117 districts in 17 provinces across all 46 countries have not been visited using the e-surv tool. Discussions

As the GPEI efforts towards polio eradication targets near achievements, preparations for a polio free world through a transition to a sustainable health system for polio essential functions are important. We reviewed the progress of transition planning in the WHO African region through the Monitoring and evaluation framework of its Global Vision.

The countries prioritized for polio transition in the African region continue to make minimal progress in maintaining these essential functions. Surveillance indicators for sample shipment, in particular, remain suboptimal, with AFP and environmental surveillance shipment times significantly exceeding the standards set by the GPEI. The delayed shipment times mainly occur in countries that are no longer receiving support for polio eradication from the GPEI, highlighting the challenges of surveillance gaps if these countries do not plan appropriately and sustain their resources for these critical functions. These challenges in timely sample shipment in the region relate to logistical issues as well as the availability of national polio testing labs, with a previous report of <50% of AFP samples arriving at the lab in ≤3 days. This was acknowledged in the Regional Polio eradication action plan 2024–2025 [35]. It takes a median of over 49 days for VDPV2 outbreak detection in the region, over half of the samples not arriving at the labs promptly, despite the set benchmark from the Global Surveillance action plan of ≤35 days [32, 36]. Similar optimal sub regional performance has been reported in East and Southern Africa by Mayanga et al. [37]. Addressing sample shipment delays will have a huge impact on enhancing timely outbreak detection and subsequent outbreak control. These delays ultimately affect the reliability of the surveillance system to promptly detect outbreaks and further heighten risks of missed or undetected transmission.

The ISS visits conducted across the 15 priority countries show an increasing trend over the decade, except for Burkina Faso and Niger in the last biennium (2023–2024). This decrease may be ascribed to a reduction in funding and associated resources, as well as reported challenges of access, fuelled by insecurity in the Lake Chad basin. While all the coverage of ISS visits across the 15 priority countries remains optimal at over 80%, performance in five countries (Algeria, Botswana, Eritrea, Uganda and Zimbabwe remains lower than expected. These countries, none of which received funding from the GPEI programme, highlight the risk if specific plans are not in place to sustain such essential functions like ISS health facility visits.

We have also shown that over the past decade, the cumulative immunization coverage for OPV3 and IPV1 has remained below the set targets for AI2030 within these 15 countries. OPV3 Coverage rates remain lower than the global WUENIC reported levels of 84%, still less than pre-pandemic levels. The polio eradication programme relies on strong population immunity to sustain a polio-free world following cVDPV2 interruption and subsequent elimination of all variant polio. It also anticipates cessation of bOPV use and subsequent IPV only schedules. Sustaining humoral and mucosal immunity through high levels of OPV and IPV vaccines will be necessary to achieve and maintain a polio free Africa [38]. This risk is even more concerning, reflecting from the documented gaps of the tOPV Switch in 2016 and discussions for an efficient and safe bOPV cessation and containment, currently ongoing as part of the strategy for a polio free world [39, 40].

The IHR capacity score for the African region has been consistently lower than the global score over the last 10 years. The average score for the region for the period reviewed was 51 compared to the global average score of 67. Our review also shows that over the 10 years reviewed, only two countries, Ethiopia and Mozambique, reported higher scores than the regional score and greater scores than the regional score across all 10 years. Only Ethiopia shows an average score higher than the regional and global average. These capacity assessments highlight gaps in the continent’s capacity to prepare, detect and respond to health emergencies. The largest gaps are noted in the preparedness component of the IHR assessment among the countries reviewed. If countries cannot institute systems to help prepare, detect and respond to outbreaks, the chances of increased morbidity and mortality when these emergencies occur. Similar reports showing dwindling capacity assessment in the African region were reported by Eze et al., despite an overall global improvement between 2018 and 2022 [41]. The concerns around strengthening global health security necessitate the importance of improving national leadership and ownership, as well as including the one health approach in all countries’ strategic approaches to health security [42]. Within increasing infectious disease outbreaks driven by low immunization, climate related events and population displacements, the region would benefit from better preparedness and response capacities to limit and control such disease outbreaks using all available tools [43]. This underscores the critical importance of resilient health systems and their role in global health security, while also highlighting persistent challenges in maintaining consistent and reliable country-level data on health system building blocks in Africa. and challenges in the consistent availability of country information in international databases on health systems building blocks in Africa [44]

National level financing of programmes remains essential for sustainability. The NHA account trends show that all 15 countries except Madagascar have not achieved the regional Abuja declaration target of 15% budgets for health. Domestic financing of health from national budgets has remained less than 50% in all these countries over the last two decades, ranging between 22.9%-27.5%. Amongst the priority countries reviewed, the biggest reliance on domestic assistance for health is seen in four countries(South Sudan, DR Congo, Ethiopia and Guinea), which are also ranked in the top 20 countries in the region with significant donor contribution to total spending on health [45]. This reliance on external donors and partner funding by countries in the region shows their vulnerability in changing global financing landscapes and the need to for a coordinated transition to domestic financing alongside other funding mechanisms [27]. Countries in the region must improve their allocations to health and targets towards universal coverage. This approach should also adopt a switch from vertical programmes, where necessary and adapt these programmes through a diagonal switch into strengthening health systems. Some suggestions have been made around transitional financing towards improving domestic funding for health [46]. These innovative financing models include taxes on mobile phones/telecommunication, alcohol and money transfers [47].

There has been increasing advocacy for an integration of the polio programme into the health system with increased national ownership and leadership following GPEI sunsetting in the future [25, 48]. This integrated approach should level lessons learned within and outside the region and how best the national government will address the challenges for sustaining a polio free world without external funding from the GPEI or any other globally driven governance structure. A recent review of considerations of integration of COVID-19 vaccination into the routine health system highlights challenges of coordination across programmes, while acknowledging opportunities in supply chain and information management [49, 50]. An integrated approach was also recommended in the recent 24th IMB report [51].

Initiatives like the Lusaka Agenda, which provides a path for coordination and change through a long-term vision for domestically financed health systems [52]. As countries navigate the current cliff in global health financing, the region must focus on improving efficiencies within the health system and consider more South-South collaborations [46].

Our study is limited by the utilization of secondary data currently publicly available to the programme. While we acknowledge the challenges around data quality, we believe the utilization of trends over the years across country reports addresses any challenges in the reliability of the data. Secondly, we acknowledge the use of self-reported data, especially relating to the national health accounts and IHR scores. We note the concerns around self-reporting and measuring bias in such reports. We, however, do not believe this has affected the outputs of our assessments and the utilization of our findings to guide policy decisions. Lastly, our review does not consider all the data related to the WHO health system building blocks; however, we have considered health financing data, as this is included in the M&E framework and is an indicative measurement towards sustainability.

Conclusion

Progress in planning and implementing polio transition efforts across priority countries in Africa remains limited, with persistent gaps in immunization, emergency preparedness, and health financing. These weaknesses, compounded by declining donor support, threaten the sustainability of polio assets and the broader goal of a polio-free world. To safeguard decades of eradication gains, countries must deliberately prioritize transition planning and integration of polio functions into national health systems. We also call for more flexibility, open and transparent communication, planning and collaboration at country levels within the GPEI partnership and across partners to reduce activity duplication and ensure focused system support based on country needs and peculiarities, as proposed in the draft strategy for a polio free world(SPW). Countries should also compile and submit transition planning reports based on the transition M&E framework and utilize these outputs to guide country planning and implementation processes.

Polio transition is not only about sustaining eradication gains—it is a bridge to stronger, more resilient health systems. Countries in the WHO African region must improve efforts towards securing sustainable financing by adopting innovative domestic funding models and ensuring efficient resource allocation. Innovative domestic financing models, including taxation(Alcoholic beverages, sugar-based beverages, tobacco) and contributory insurance schemes, should continue to be explored in enhancing domestic health financing. To further improve efficiencies within the health system, they also must strategically integrate polio assets—including surveillance networks, laboratories, and trained workforce—into routine immunization and broader health services. This can be achieved through a robust, realistic and implementable national polio transition planning and align with regional frameworks to guide integration and promote sustainability.
