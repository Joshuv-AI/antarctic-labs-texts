Recovery of turbulent boundary layers from pressure gradient history effects
Zefanya Bramantasaputra, Dea Daniella Wangsawijaya, Bharathram Ganapathisubramani

Abstract
The present study experimentally investigates the recovery of smooth-wall turbulent boundary layers (TBLs) following non-equilibrium pressure gradients (PGs). The imposed pressure gradient history (PGH) comprises favourable-adverse pressure gradient (FAPG) sequences of varying strength, followed by recovery to zero-pressure-gradient (ZPG) conditions. Hot-wire anemometry measurements were obtained at multiple downstream stations in the recovery region, with friction Reynolds numbers R ​ e τ Re_{\tau} ranging from 2000 to 6000 depending on downstream development. Comparative analysis at matched R ​ e τ Re_{\tau} and Clauser pressure gradient parameter β \beta enables clear assessment of history effects on TBL behaviour. Results show that increasing PGH strength enhances the wake in mean velocity profiles and amplifies turbulence intensities across the boundary layer, including the inner peak, logarithmic region, and outer peak (a signature of APG). Downstream, the mean flow gradually recovers toward a ZPG-like state, but turbulence in the outer region retains a lasting impact of PGH. Spectral analysis indicates that PGH primarily affects outer-layer scales, introducing a distinct PG peak and modifying the VLSM peak – with energy amplification dependent on PGH strength and spatial characteristics governed by history effects. Downstream recovery involves merging of large-scale wavelengths and the reorganisation of turbulence structures toward a ZPG-like state – although the ‘recovered’ VLSM streamwise length becomes shortened due to the mixing of lengthscales with the PG peak. These results demonstrate that even under matched local parameters, TBLs retain a clear imprint of their upstream history, consistent with the findings of Preskett et al. [ J. Fluid Mech. 1010 , A30 (2025) ]; moreover, this study provides new insights regarding the central role of scale interactions in the recovery mechanism of TBL subjected to complex PGH.



I
Introduction

Turbulent boundary layers (TBLs) subjected to streamwise pressure gradients (PGs) are ubiquitous in engineering applications, including flows over aircraft wings, wind turbines, and ship hulls. The similarity between these applications, from a flow perspective, is that they eventually experience high Reynolds numbers while also being exposed to strong PGs early in their evolution. While canonical zero-pressure-gradient (ZPG) smooth-wall TBLs have been extensively studied, the imposition of local PGs alters not only mean flow but also underlying turbulence structures
Lee and Sung 2009
;
Harun et al. 2013
;
Vinuesa et al. 2017a
;
Pozuelo et al. 2022
. Further complexity arises pressure gradient history (PGH):
Clauser 1956
noted that the outer portion of the boundary layer possesses a long memory, being shaped not only by local conditions but also by a prolonged history of past events. Echoing this notion, recent work by
Preskett et al. 2025
demonstrated that even after a TBL returns to a nominal
local
ZPG following exposure to PGs, it
still
deviates from the behaviour of a canonical ZPG TBL. The present study therefore seeks to experimentally investigate the recovery mechanisms of TBLs subjected to PGH.

Despite the apparent simplicity of canonical ZPG TBLs, these flows exhibit complex multiscale organisation, characterised by a hierarchy of coherent structures spanning from near-wall streaks to large-scale motions (LSMs) and very-large-scale motions (VLSMs). LSMs contribute significantly to Reynolds shear stress, with streamwise extents of 2–3
\delta
, where
\delta
is the boundary-layer thickness
Townsend 1951
;
Ganapathisubramani et al. 2003
. VLSMs emerge at sufficiently high friction Reynolds numbers (
Re_{\tau}\gtrsim 2000
, with
Re_{\tau}\equiv U_{\tau}\delta/\nu
, where
U_{\tau}
is the friction velocity and
\nu
the kinematic viscosity) and become increasingly significant as
Re_{\tau}
increases – premultiplied energy spectra show that they typically occur at
y^{+}\approx 3.9\sqrt{Re_{\tau}}
(with
y^{+}\equiv yU_{\tau}/\nu
, where
y
is the wall-normal location), corresponding to
y/\delta\approx 0.06
, with characteristic streamwise length scales of
\lambda_{x}\approx 6\delta

Kim and Adrian 1999
;
Hutchins and Marusic 2007
;
Mathis et al. 2009
. These structures modulate small-scales in the near-wall region, influencing velocity gradients and skin-friction drag
Hutchins et al. 2011
;
Ganapathisubramani et al. 2012
.

The presence of streamwise local PGs alters the mean flow: a favourable PG (FPG,
\frac{\mathrm{d}p}{\mathrm{d}x}<0
) accelerates the freestream and stabilises the flow, while an adverse PG (APG,
\frac{\mathrm{d}p}{\mathrm{d}x}>0
) has the opposite effect, where
p
is the static pressure and
x
the streamwise coordinate
Schlichting and Gersten 2018
;
Narasimha and Sreenivasan 1973
;
Simpson 1989
.
Clauser 1956
introduced the PG parameter, as a measure of PG strength:

\beta=\frac{\delta^{*}}{\tau_{w}}\frac{\mathrm{d}p}{\mathrm{d}x},

(1)

with displacement thickness
\delta^{*}=\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(1-\frac{U}{U_{\infty}}\right)\mathrm{d}y
, where
U
is the mean streamwise velocity at a given
y
,
U_{\infty}
is the freestream velocity, and
\tau_{w}
is the wall shear stress. APGs increase the shape factor
H
, reduce skin-friction
C_{f}
, and amplify the wake parameter
\mathit{\Pi}
, whereas FPGs exhibit the opposite trends
Spalart and Watmuff 1993
;
Harun et al. 2013
;
Vinuesa et al. 2017a
. From a turbulence-structure perspective, APGs generate a pronounced outer peak at sufficiently strong PGs, elevate Reynolds stresses, and amplify large-scale motions in the outer-layer
Lee and Sung 2009
;
Monty et al. 2011
;
Harun et al. 2013
;
Kitsios et al. 2016
;
Deshpande et al. 2023
;
Pozuelo et al. 2022
. These observations, reported under constant or near-equilibrium
\beta
, highlight the effects of local PG alone.

A further complication arises from PGH, whereby the downstream state of a TBL depends not only on the local PG but also on the upstream sequence of PGs. Upstream perturbations alter downstream profiles
Zarei et al. 2025
, and sequences like FPG–APG (FAPG) show distinct outer-layer characteristics compared to ZPG–APG
Parthasarathy and Saxton-Fox 2023
. PGH has been shown to influence TBL dynamics, including scale-dependent effects, spatial averaging, integral quantities, and even rough-wall flows
Mahajan et al. 2026
;
Mallor et al. 2025
;
Virgilio et al. 2025
;
Preskett and Ganapathisubramani 2025
. Notably,
Preskett et al. 2025
demonstrated that TBLs exposed to non-equilibrium PG sequences (i.e., streamwise-varying
\beta
) of varying strength remain significantly modified in skin friction, mean velocity, and turbulence structures
even
after returning to nominally ZPG conditions. These observations highlight the dominant role of accumulated PGH over instantaneous local PG alone, with history primarily influencing the outer region
Sanmiguel Vila et al. 2017
;
Tanarro et al. 2020
. The need to distinguish between local PG and history effects has been emphasised by
Vinuesa et al. 2017b
, with
Bobke et al. 2017
showing that while
Re_{\tau}
and
\beta
are sufficient to characterise TBLs under a constant PG, they are insufficient when PGH is present.

The review of previous literature leaves several important gaps. First, while PGH has been shown to strongly affect TBL state, most prior studies have focused on constant
\beta
histories, leaving the role of variations in PGH relatively unexplored. Second, although it is well established that PGH causes TBLs to deviate from local PG behaviour, there has been little systematic investigation of whether, and by what mechanisms, such flows can recover. The present study addresses two key questions: (
i
) how does varying PGH strength influence mean flow and turbulence structures during recovery, and (
ii
) What are the energy containing mechanisms in this recovery phase?

To address these questions, wind tunnel experiments were performed on smooth-wall TBLs exposed to three distinct, rapidly changing non-equilibrium FAPG histories, imposed by an aerofoil set at negative angles of attack
(\alpha)
. These sequences were designed with identical phase but varying amplitude, thereby isolating the influence of PGH strength. Hot-wire anemometry was employed in the recovery region to characterise both mean flow and turbulence structures, with measurements conducted at multiple downstream locations where the flow returns to nominally ZPG conditions, enabling assessment of recovery mechanisms. It is important to note that comparisons are made at matched
Re_{\tau}
and
\beta
, allowing an independent assessment of the local and history effects.

II
Experimental setup and parameters

II.1
Flow facility

The experiments were conducted in the Boundary Layer Wind Tunnel (BLWT) at the University of Southampton. This closed-loop facility has a test section that consists of five segments, each 2.4 m long, giving a total streamwise length of 12 m, with a spanwise width of 1.2 m and wall-normal height of 1 m, where the streamwise, wall-normal, and spanwise directions are denoted by
(x,y,z)
, respectively. Before entering the test section, the airflow passes through a settling chamber equipped with a honeycomb and three screens to reduce turbulence, resulting in a measured freestream turbulence intensity of less than 0.15%. II.2
Flow history

The streamwise PG was generated using a 2D NACA0012 wing with a chord length of 1.25 m and a span of 118.5 cm, covering 99% of the test section width (see ④ in Fig.
1
). The wing was suspended from the test section roof using four linear actuators, allowing its attitude to be adjusted by extending or retracting the actuators. Its 25% chord was positioned 280 cm from the start of the test section and set 40 cm above the floor. Three different flow histories, corresponding to varying PG magnitudes, were achieved by setting the wing angle of attack to
-4^{\circ}
,
-6^{\circ}
, and
-8^{\circ}
while keeping the quarter-chord position fixed.

To characterise the streamwise PG imposed on the TBL, the static pressure distribution along the test section floor was measured. Forty static pressure taps were installed across the first four segments of the smooth-wall test section, spaced 24 cm apart (see ② in Fig.
1
). The taps were positioned 25 cm from the side wall (35 cm off-centre) to minimise disturbances to the flow in the central region where TBL measurements were performed. Pressures were recorded using a Scanivalve ZOC 33/64Px electronic pressure scanning module with a RAD3200 system. The pressure coefficient was calculated as

(2)

where
p

p_{0}
is the reference static pressure at the tap nearest the Pitot tube measuring
U_{\infty,0}
(fifth tap, just below ③ in Fig.
1
), and
\rho
is the fluid density. The normalised streamwise PG,
\frac{\mathrm{d}C_{p}}{\mathrm{d}x}\delta_{0}
, for the three PGH cases investigated in this study is shown in Fig.
2
.

II.3
Hot-wire anemometry

Hot-wire anemometry in constant temperature mode (CTA) was employed to measure the velocity profile across the boundary layer at several streamwise positions, using a Dantec Dynamics StreamLine Pro system and an in-house repaired Dantec 55P05 boundary layer probe with a 5
\mu
m diameter
d
tungsten wire electroplated with copper. The wire had an exposed tungsten core approximately 1 mm in length
l
, achieving
l/d\geq 200
to minimise end-conduction attenuation as recommended by
Ligrani and Bradshaw 1987
. The overheat ratio was set to 0.8, corresponding to an over-temperature of approximately
220^{\circ}
C. Following square-wave testing, a 30 kHz low-pass filter was applied, and the sampling rate was set to
f_{s}=60
kHz, with a total acquisition time of 150 s per measurement point. This yielded a temporal resolution of
0.46\leq t^{+}\equiv U_{\tau}^{2}/(f_{s}\nu)\leq 0.74
and satisfied statistical convergence based on boundary layer turnover times of
TU_{\infty}/\delta>20000
as suggested by
Hutchins et al. 2009
. The viscous-scaled spatial resolution of the probe ranged from
43\leq l^{+}\equiv lU_{\tau}/\nu\leq 53
and remained below
l^{+}\lesssim 60
, ensuring the validity of the analysis in the outer region of the TBL as suggested by
Deshpande et al. 2023
, which is the region where this study will be focused on.

Hot-wire calibrations were performed before and after each measurement (approximately 4.5-hour intervals) using the
in-situ
nearby Pitot tube (see ⑤ in Fig.
1
) measuring
U_{\infty,l}
as the calibration reference. Both the hot-wire (see ⑥ in Fig.
1
) and pitot tube were mounted on an aerofoil-shaped traverse system with an encoder offering 1
\mu
m resolution. During calibration, the wing (see ④ in Fig.
1
) angle was set to zero to avoid wake effects and ensure free-stream conditions. The calibration, performed following a King’s law polynomial fit, allowed the hot-wire voltage output to be converted to velocity, with temperature correction applied as a precaution against potential temperature drift.

, the wall-normal coordinate correction

1
, the von Kármán constant was fixed at

as discussed by

. The indirect estimation of

was applied to previously published datasets: PGH smooth-wall cases from

and ZPG smooth-wall canonical cases from
Wangsawijaya et al. 2023
and
Aguiar Ferreira et al. 2024

IV
Conclusion and future works

This study experimentally examined the recovery of TBLs from PGH effects using hot-wire anemometry, with measurements taken downstream of an FAPG sequence to assess the influence of history on flow behaviour. Comparative analysis was carried out with data at
Re_{\tau}\approx 2000
,
3000
, and
5500
where the local
\beta
was approximately
1.72
,
0.57
, and
-0.04
, respectively. A
Re_{\tau}\approx 2000
was at a location closest to the PG source and
Re_{\tau}\approx 5500
furthest downstream. At each of these matched
Re_{\tau}
and
\beta
conditions, the TBL was subjected to three PGH strengths – weak, mild, and strong – and the results were compared against canonical ZPG datasets.

The mean velocity profiles,
U^{+}
, showed that the investigated PGH strongly affects the wake, with stronger PGH strength producing a more pronounced wake region. The turbulence intensity profiles,
\overline{u^{2}}^{+}
, revealed broadband modifications characterised by enhancement of the inner peak, elevation in the logarithmic layer, and the emergence of an outer peak, all of which amplified with increasing PGH strength. Downstream, the mean flow gradually recovers toward a ZPG-like state; however, even when the mean velocity appears recovered, turbulence intensity beyond the logarithmic region remains altered. Notably, this persistence occurs despite
\beta
returning to zero, highlighting the importance of understanding the energy containing processes during recovery.

Spectral analysis revealed that PGH primarily influences outer-region scales, generating a distinct PG peak – a large-scale feature smaller than canonical VLSM scales – while displacing and energising the VLSM peak. The energy amplification of these peaks depends on PGH strength, but their spatial characteristics (
\lambda_{x}
and
y
) are governed mainly by
history
effects. During downstream recovery, the VLSM peak returns to its canonical wall-normal position (
y^{+}\approx 3.9\sqrt{Re_{\tau}}
, corresponding to
y/\delta\approx 0.06
) but exhibits a progressively shorter streamwise length, reducing from
\lambda_{x}\approx 6\delta
to
\lambda_{x}\approx 4\delta
; meanwhile, the PG peak shifts outward (from
y\approx 0.3\delta
to
y\approx 0.5\delta
) and lengthens from 2–3
\delta
until it merges with the ‘recovered’ VLSM peak around
\lambda_{x}\approx 4\delta
. These observations highlight scale interactions as central to the recovery mechanism, with turbulence structures gradually reorganising toward a ZPG-like state. The VLSM lengthscale, however, is modified and remains shortened by about
2\delta
. Thus,
history
matters – raising the question of whether true recovery ever occurs. The present data cannot determine if or when full recovery happens, as no measurements exist further downstream. Furthermore, the role of PGH lengthscale (i.e., the streamwise extent over which the TBL is exposed to the PG relative to
\delta_{0}
) in governing the persistence of history effects remains an open question. Future studies should explore these points, not only to confirm the observed energy containing mechanism during recovery phase, but also to further investigate the intriguing role of history in TBL evolution.
