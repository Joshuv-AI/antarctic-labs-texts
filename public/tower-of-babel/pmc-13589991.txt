Pilot Community Health Worker-Based Intervention to Address Social Determinants of Health Among Patients with Diabetes in Ophthalmology
Authors: Bethel Joy DeGracia, Zainab Rustam, Jose Amezcua Moreno, Sophia E Yu, Diep Tran, Kenya Ferguson, James Wilson, Randi Woods, Cindy X Cai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeCommunity Health Workers (CHWs) represent a potential strategy in ophthalmology to address adverse social determinants of health (SDoH) and improve vision outcomes. In this study, we explored the feasibility, acceptability, and impact of a structured CHW intervention among patients with diabetes.Materials and MethodsThis was a prospective single-center pilot study conducted at the Johns Hopkins Wilmer Eye Institute. Adult participants (≥ 18 years) with diabetes and at least one adverse SDoH participated in the study. The CHW worked with the participant for on average 6 months to address the social barriers identified at screening. At the conclusion of the study, participants completed a follow-up SDoH survey and a semi-structured exit interview.ResultsOut of 45 enrolled participants, 23 completed the study (mean age 56 years, 52% female, and 83% non-Hispanic Black). Many participants (83%) were satisfied with the frequency of the meetings, 87% had a positive experience with the CHW and felt respected and supported (74%) throughout the program. The experience with the broader program was more mixed with 17% reporting it was negative, 22% neutral, and 57% positive. Participants found the program helpful for resource education (65%), emotional support (43%), access to medical services (43%), and self-confidence (57%). Challenges included limited resources available to address certain social needs and language barriers among the Spanish speaking participants. Participants reported high trust in CHW, but there were no significant changes in pre- and post-intervention SDoH surveys (p>0.05).ConclusionIntegration of CHWs into ophthalmology is feasible and acceptable among patients with diabetes who remained actively engaged over time. High trust in CHW and improvement in resource navigation, health knowledge, and confidence were reported. Our findings support development of CHW integration in ophthalmology as a potential intervention to address barriers to care and improve vision outcomes.Keywords: diabetic eye care, social determinants of health, community health worker

Introduction

Diabetic retinopathy (DR) remains a leading cause of preventable vision loss, with disease burden falling disproportionately on racial and ethnic minorities, low-income individuals, and the underinsured.1–5 Social determinants of health – the conditions in which people are born, live, and work – are recognized as key drivers of health disparities.6,7 Existing literature has linked SDoH to lapses in care, worse visual outcomes, and underutilization of eye care services.8–10

Community Health Workers (CHWs) have emerged as a promising strategy for addressing adverse SDoH and improving health outcomes. Existing work in diabetes management shows that CHW intervention is associated with increased glycemic control, medication adherence, and self-management behaviors.11–13 In ophthalmic settings, health workers’ integrated care has resulted in increased eye screening uptake, appointment attendance, and referral rate.14–16 Beyond clinical care, CHWs are effective in managing upstream social needs, such as coordinating food assistance, housing stability, and insurance enrollment.17,18 The success of CHW intervention is thought to stem from CHWs’ integrated position as a member of the community, understanding of community resources and barriers, and the established trust through long-term relationships. Despite promising evidence in primary care and diabetes management in lower resource countries, CHW remain to be underutilized in US specialty care settings.16,19,20

In the ophthalmic setting specifically, prior work from our group established that patients with diabetes are receptive to SDoH screening and CHW involvement in their eye care.21 Rustam et al found that 90% of patients expressed enthusiasm for integrating CHWs into ophthalmology visits, and identified transportation, scheduling conflicts, forgotten appointments, and intercurrent illness as the primary barriers to appointment attendance.21 These findings suggest that CHW deployment holds promise for improving social needs and patient outcomes in this population, and no prospective studies have evaluated the feasibility, acceptability or impact of structured CHW intervention among diabetic patients in academic ophthalmology. Understanding patient perspectives, implementation barriers, and measurable impacts is essential before broader dissemination. Through our study, we aim to evaluate the impact of this intervention, using both quantitative SDoH survey data and qualitative patient exit interviews.

Methods

Participants

This is a pilot study to assess the feasibility of the intervention; hence, a formal sample size calculation was not performed. Adult patients (≥ 18 years) with diabetes mellitus seeking care at the Wilmer Eye Institute (Patient Access Center clinic) between August 14, 2024 and January 21, 2025 were approached (N=306). All interested participants provided written consent (N=45) and were given $10–30 gift cards for their participation ($10 after completing screening SDoH survey and $10–20 after completing exit interview). Participants were excluded if they were unable to provide consent or if English or Spanish was not their primary language. This study was approved by the Johns Hopkins Institutional Review Board (IRB00444477) and followed the Declaration of Helsinki.

Adverse SDoH Screening

Enrolled participants completed a 23 question SDoH survey on sociodemographic information, economic and housing stability, food insecurity, health insurance, and receipt of monetary or nonmonetary social services9 (Supplementary Material 1). Only participants who reported at least one social barrier (N=45) were invited to the next portion of the study (Supplementary Figure 1).

CHW Intervention

The CHW worked with each participant to address the social barriers identified in the initial screening either in-person, via phone calls, or via text message. If participants were unreachable after study enrollment, the CHW was instructed to contact them a total of 3 times before excluding them from the study. On average, participants worked with the CHW for a period of 6.8 months (range 2 to 11 months) with an average of 4.7 (range 2 to 9) contact points per participant. The frequency of contact was decided by CHWs based on participants needs and interpreters were provided for participants who spoke Spanish. The CHWs were from a nonprofit organization Sisters Together and Reaching (STAR) and were certified and accredited to provide a range of services including motivation interviewing, resource navigation, and SDoH screening. The intervention protocol followed a patient-centered approach where CHWs: (1) assessed the participant’s priority needs, (2) provided resource information, (3) assisted with applications when appropriate, and (4) provided follow-up support. Fidelity was monitored through case note review and supervision.

Study Conclusion

Upon completion of the program, participants completed a repeat SDoH survey and an exit interview about their experience working with the CHW. The exit interview consisted of semi-structured and structured components (Supplementary Material 2). The semi-structured sections assessed themes of feasibility, acceptability, and impact of the CHW program based on prior literature on CHW interventions.22,23 The structured section included a previously validated 10 question survey evaluating the trust of the participants in the CHW across domains of health care competence and respectful communication.24

Data Analysis

The interview recordings were transcribed using Microsoft Word (Version 2512) and checked by team members (B.D., J.A.M., Z.R.) against the audio to ensure accuracy. Interview transcripts were analyzed using a traditional text analysis based on classic grounded theory to extract relevant themes.25 The transcripts were initially coded independently (B.D., J.A.M, Z.R.) and then discussed as a team to reconcile discrepancies. Codes were organized in Microsoft Excel (Version 2512) for analysis and accommodated multiple responses by participants.

Changes in the SDoH survey performed before and after the CHW intervention were summarized and McNemar’s test used to identify differences. Because of the small sample size and sparse cell counts, Exact McNemar’s tests were used. Statistical significance was defined at the two-sided P-value <0.05.

Additional patient sociodemographic information was extracted from the Electronic Health Record (EHR) including age, sex, self-reported race/ethnicity, and insurance. The census block group of the patients’ residential addresses was also extracted from the Johns Hopkins Precision Medicine Analytics Platform and matched to the 2021 Area Deprivation Index.26,27

Results

A total of 23 participants were included in the study (Supplementary Figure 1). The average age of the included participants was 56 years, 52% (N=12) female, and 83% (N=19) non-Hispanic Black (Supplementary Table 1).

Participants who did not complete the study (N=22) were on average younger (49 years), 64% female (N=14), and 23% Hispanic (N=5) (Supplementary Table 1). In the SDoH survey, patients who did not complete the study had fewer difficulties with transportation (47% reported they had someone to accompany them to the JHH eye clinic vs 9.1%), and fewer were on government assistance (77.3% vs 39.1%) (Supplementary Table 1). Some data were missing because participants did not answer all survey questions.

Feasibility

Most participants were satisfied with the number of times they met with the CHW (83%) which was primarily by phone (70%) mostly frequently once a month (35%). But some (18%) were unable to meet with the CHW due to other obligations such as work (50%), being away from their phones (33%), or other health reasons (17%) (Table 1).

Acceptability

In general, participants had a positive experience interacting with the CHW (87%). Participants thought the CHW was professional (61%), caring (43%), knowledgeable (48%), and most felt cared for/respected/supported (74%). The experience with the broader program was more mixed with 17% reporting it was negative, 22% neutral, and 57% positive. Participants thought the program was helpful for education about resources (65%), emotional/moral support (43%), and medical management (9%). Some of the negative experiences about the program were lack of clarity around the scope of the program, limited available resources to address certain social barriers, and language barriers primarily for the Spanish-speaking participants (Tables 2 and 3).

Impact

Participants reported an improved impact on various domains including: knowledge about their medical care and health (52%), understanding of community resources (48%), access to medical services (43%), connection to healthcare providers (52%), self-efficacy (52%) and self-confidence (57%). Fewer participants reported improvements to access to health-related social needs (30%), and social support (35%) (Table 4).

Discussion

In this single-center prospective pilot study of a structured CHW intervention for adults with diabetes receiving care in an academic ophthalmology clinic, we found that integration of a CHW into ophthalmic care was feasible, acceptable, and perceived as beneficial by many participants. Participants reported high levels of trust in the CHW and described benefits across multiple domains, including improved understanding of community resources, greater knowledge about their health and medical care, enhanced connection to healthcare providers, and increased self-efficacy and confidence. However, despite these perceived benefits, we did not observe significant changes in measured adverse SDoH before and after the intervention. This could be attributed to measurement bias, short duration of the program, and need for systemic interventions to address adverse SDoH. Together, these findings suggest that CHW programs in ophthalmology may be valuable for patient engagement, health system navigation, self efficacy, education, and support, but that larger, systemic interventions may be needed to measurably reduce structural social barriers.

A key driver of program acceptability was the relationship between patients and the CHW. Participants frequently described the CHW as professional, caring, knowledgeable, and respectful, and trust in the CHW was high at study completion. This relationship appeared to support participants in navigating resources, setting goals, and developing confidence in managing both health-related and social needs. Several participants also reported improved understanding of the importance of eye care and greater trust in available services. The high levels of trust reported by participants may reflect not only the individual CHW–patient relationship but also the community-based infrastructure supporting the intervention. The CHWs in this study were embedded within a longstanding community organization with extensive experience navigating Baltimore’s health and social service systems. This organizational context may have enhanced credibility, resource knowledge, continuity, and patient engagement. Future studies should consider the role of community-based organizational capacity as an important implementation factor influencing CHW effectiveness. Participants’ perceived benefits and positive experiences may reflect early progress rather than outcomes that can be measured immediately and can built capacity and knowledge that may lead to downstream changes. Our findings are also consistent with prior studies demonstrating that CHWs can improve diabetes-related outcomes, blood pressure control, medication adherence, and self-management skills.28 Improved knowledge, self-confidence, and connection to providers could be an important intermediate outcomes that precedes measurable changes in healthcare utilization or clinical outcomes.29,30 In the ophthalmology setting, where outcomes depend heavily on longitudinal follow-up and timely treatment, CHWs may serve as an important bridge between patients, eye care providers, and community resources.14,31

At the same time, the intervention’s impact varied by participant engagement and readiness to receive assistance. Participants who maintained communication with the CHW and expressed openness to assistance appeared to derive greater benefit than those facing competing priorities or who were less engaged. This suggests that readiness to engage may influence the effectiveness of CHW interventions.32,33 Future studies should explore methods for assessing readiness at enrollment and tailoring intervention intensity based on patient readiness, social complexity, and capacity to engage.

Several implementation lessons emerged from this pilot that may explain the high loss to follow-up rate and should inform future CHW programs in ophthalmology. First, some participants had needs that exceeded the scope of the CHW role or the availability of community resources, including housing instability, discontinuation of government programs, and limited financial resources. While CHWs can identify barriers, build trust, support goal-setting, and connect patients to resources, they cannot fully compensate for broader structural inequities or unavailable services. Second, communication and language concordance should be treated as core program design priorities. Although interpreter services were available, some Spanish-speaking participants experienced communication barriers, and non-completers included a higher proportion of Hispanic participants, suggesting that interpreter access alone may be insufficient for optimal engagement. Language-concordant and culturally responsive CHW services may improve trust, uptake, and retention. Finally, future programs should more clearly define eligibility criteria to prioritize patients with unmet needs that are actionable within the intervention, as some participants were already receiving assistance or had needs that could not be addressed through available resources. A smaller CHW-to-patient ratio may also permit more intensive follow-up for patients with multiple or complex barriers. Future studies should evaluate whether these program refinements improve both social needs and ophthalmology-specific outcomes, including appointment adherence, completion of recommended diabetic eye exams, follow-up after abnormal findings, receipt of indicated treatment, and visual outcomes.

This study has several strengths, including its prospective mixed-methods design, use of structured and semi-structured exit interviews informed by existing CHW frameworks, and inclusion of both quantitative SDoH measures and qualitative patient experiences. However, findings should be interpreted in light of several limitations. The sample size was small, which limited the study’s ability to detect changes, completion rates were modest, and the single-site academic setting limits generalizability. Participants who completed the study may have been more engaged or more satisfied than those lost to follow-up. The SDoH surveys before and after the intervention were administered by different study team members, which may have contributed to variability in responses and social desirability bias. In addition, the 6–7 month duration of CHW engagement may have been insufficient to produce measurable change in more complex structural social determinants of health. A change in CHW staffing caused a pause in the program.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this single-center pilot study suggests that CHW integration into ophthalmology care for patients with diabetes and adverse SDoH is feasible and acceptable. Participants who completed the study reported high trust in the CHW and perceived improvements in resource navigation, health knowledge, connection to care, and confidence. Although the intervention did not significantly reduce measured adverse SDoH, the findings support CHWs as a promising patient-centered strategy to address barriers to diabetic eye care. Future larger-scale studies should prioritize language concordance, clear program scope, and strategies for engaging harder-to-reach patients while evaluating downstream effects on appointment adherence, diabetic retinopathy care, and vision outcomes.
