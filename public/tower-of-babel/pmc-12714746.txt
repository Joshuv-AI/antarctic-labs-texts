Genetic variation of cutaneous leishmaniasis causative species in Ethiopia
Authors: Selfu Girma, Mesfin Tafesse Gemeda, Adugna Abdi Woldesemayat, Dawit Hailu Alemayehu, Abaysew Ayele, Semira Eshetu, Feleke Tilahun Zewdu, Eshetu Molla, Tizita Yosef, Shimelis Nigussie, Molalegne Bitew, Kidist Bobosha, Endalamaw Gadisa
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
In Ethiopia, three Leishmania species are linked to Cutaneous Leishmaniasis (CL): Leishmania aethiopica, L. tropica, and L. donovani. Among these, L. aethiopica is the most commonly reported cause. The clinical presentations of CL are broadly classified into localized (LCL), mucocutaneous (MCL), and diffuse cutaneous CL (DCL) forms. There is limited data on the genetics of these pathogens, despite the different clinical forms and estimated 50 thousand new cases of CL per year. In this research, we sequenced the ITS1 region from 72 clinical specimens. In the in-silico PCR-RFLP, 67 were identified as L. aethiopica (93.1%), and 5 were identified as L. tropica (6.9%). In the ITS1 sequence analysis, among the isolates with L. aethiopica PCR-RFLP pattern, 13.4% (n = 9) were found to be putative hybrids between L. donovani and L. aethiopica. We recommend a powered study to explore the public health implication of the different species, and interspecies hybrids.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-28928-0.

Introduction

Cutaneous leishmaniasis (CL) is a neglected tropical skin disease caused by over 20 Leishmania species transmitted by female sandflies1. Globally, an estimated 1.5 to 2 million cases are newly diagnosed each year, with nearly 350 million individuals at risk of infection2. In Ethiopia, the annual incidence and at-risk population are estimated at 50, 000 and 29 million, respectively3.

Among CL patients in Ethiopia, three Leishmania species have been identified: L. donovani, L. tropica, and L. aethiopica4–6. Of these, L. aethiopica is recognized as the primary etiological agent. Whole genome sequencing (WGS) analyses have revealed the presence of interspecies hybrids involving L. aethiopica, L. donovani, and L. tropica, alongside a notable genetic diversity among L. aethiopica strains6,7. The disease manifests in three clinical forms: localized (LCL), mucocutaneous (MCL), and diffuse (DCL), with LCL being the most prevalent8–10.

The clinical manifestation, disease severity, and treatment response of CL can vary depending on the specific parasite species or strains involved, as well as the host’s immune response and geographic factors11,12. A study in Ethiopia reported neither the parasite’s genetic factors nor the host’s immune status were linked to the clinical manifestations of CL13.

Characterizing the CL causative Leishmania species and ecotypes along with their association to clinical manifestations, will facilitate species-specific diagnosis to tailor patient management, and improve prognostic evaluations. Furthermore, understanding the impact of coendemicity of Leishmania species in the transmission and control of the disease is vital. Thus, this study analysed the ITS1 sequence from CL cases with different clinical phenotype and geographic origin.

Result

Demographics, clinical Presentations, and ITS1 analysis

Eighty-seven clinical suspects (mean age 28.8 ± 15.3 years; 35.6% female) were recruited. Of these, 72 (82.8%) tested positive for Leishmania using ITS1 PCR and were included in the sequence analysis (Table 1). Among confirmed cases, LCL was the most common clinical form (58.3%), followed by MCL (33.3%) and DCL (8.3%). The analysis revealed a modest but significant association between type of strain and clinical presentations (χ2 = 10.0, P = 0.04). (Supplementary Table 1).

In the in silico ITS1-RFLP analysis 67 samples (93.1%) had L. aethiopica pattern while 5 samples (6.9%) had L. tropica pattern. Sequence analysis of the 67 samples with L. aethiopica pattern showed that 9 (13.4%) had hybrid characteristics, 99.08 to 99.69% sequence similarity to L. aethiopica (PQ470327.1) and L. donovani (MH202979.1). Among the 9 putative hybrid samples: 3 (33.3%) were from LCL, 5 (55.6%) from MCL, and 1 (11.1%) from DCL cases.

Phylogenetic classification

Three clusters were identified in the analysis: with L. aethiopica, putative hybrids, and L. tropica. The phylogenetic tree revealed two subgroups within the L. aethiopica. In contrast, L. tropica formed a cluster alongside global L. tropica sequences from the NCBI database, appearing at different nodes (Fig. 1).

Compared to the reference strain (PQ470327.1), two point mutations were identified within the first cluster: an ‘A’ to ‘G’ at position 33 in sample Gr-193, and a ‘C’ to ‘T’ at position 49 in sample Gr-248. Samples Gr-45 and Gr-186 had an insertion of ‘AAC’ at positions 37–39. The pivotal position for the formation of the two distinct groups within this cluster appears to be variation at position 40, predominantly represented by nucleotide ‘C’ (Supplementary Data 1).

The second cluster was defined by an ‘AAC’ insertion at positions 37–39 and the presence of ‘T’ at position 40, unlike the ‘C’ in Cluster 1. The nine putative hybrid strains uniquely had an ‘A’ insertion at position 28, unlike all other clinical samples and the reference strain.

Four L. tropica sequences (Gr-42, 43, 44, 51) (Fig. 2) share a common ancestor with strains from Saudi Arabia and Iran, suggesting regional connections (Fig. 1). Samples GR-43, 44, and 45 have a ‘G’ to ‘C’ mutation at position 149, whereas GR-147 exhibits a ‘C’ to ‘T’ mutation at position 27 (Supplementary Data 2).

Discussion

The interplay of topography, climate, and geological history contributed to Ethiopia’s diverse ecosystems which played a crucial role in determining the prevalence and types of diseases. Previous studies documented CL as typical associated with L. aethiopica9 in the highland above 2000 m above sea level while L. tropica was the causative agent identified in the low lands14.

In this study, we sequenced the ITS1 region of 72 samples from CL cases in Ethiopia along with 5 control strains, and 50 sequences were retrieved from NCBI. We identified L. aethiopica, L. tropica, and putative L. donovani and L. aethiopica hybrid strains across the different clinical forms: DCL, LCL, and MCL.

L. aethiopica was the common species, a result that align with previous reports most of which were from cases in Ethiopia’s highlands9,15,16. The L. tropica strains in this study were from soldiers diploid at the Awash camp, an area close to the recent outbreaks of CL due to L. tropica and previous report5,17,18. The ecology of L. tropica in the lowland Rift Valley resembles the L. tropica endemic areas in the Maghreb, Syria, Israel, and Iran19–22. The different ecological niches occupied by L. aethiopica and L. tropica highlighting the need for further research on adaptation of Leishmania species across Ethiopia’s diverse ecology.

In Ethiopia, there are co-endemic areas of visceral leishmaniasis and CL due to L. donovani and L. aethiopica, respectively. Previous WGS studies identified hybrids involving L. aethiopica, L. donovani, and L. tropica6. Natural hybridization between L. aethiopica and L. donovani has also been reported23. The putative hybrids identified in our study corroborates previous WGS based finding that co-endemicity may facilitate natural interspecies genetic exchange (Supplementary data 1).

We observed intra species diversity in L. aethiopica (Fig. 1) consistent to previous studies which identified two distinct lineages24,25. The report that associated research also found two genetic clusters indicating some internal diversity, including putative hybrids (Fig. 1). Furthermore, L. donovani to CL4 and atypical cutaneous involvement26 the detection of the putative hybrid L. aethiopica and L. donovani in CL cases might explain the change the tropism to the skin.

L. aethiopica was considered a “distinct species” associated with all the three clinical forms of CL: LCL, DCL, and MCL. Our findings indicate the possibility that the strains of L. tropica and hybrid of L. aethiopica could cause different CL manifestations (χ2 = 10.0, P = 0.04). As our sample size is limited and ITS1 sequencing was used to infer interspecies genetic exchange we recommend a powered and systematic study to understand to the host, environmental, and parasite factors, as well as the influence of genetic diversity and hybridization on disease outcomes.

Limitation of the study

We are cognizant of the constrain in the wide generalizability of the findings. This study based on the ITS1 sequence and a small sample size (n = 72). Most of our participants (n = 65) were enrolled from the Alert Comprehensive Specialized Hospital, a tertiary level referral centre for dermatology.

Methods

Study design

This is a hospital based cross-sectional study. Enrolment was done from October 2023 to February 2024 at the Alert Comprehensive Specialized Hospital (ALERT) in Addis Ababa and Boru Meda General Hospital in the Amhara regional State (Fig. 3).

Sample size and sample collection

No formal sample size calculation was performed for this pilot study. Consecutive, self-reporting cases with microbiologically confirmed CL were enrolled. Slit skin scrape (SSS) and punch biopsy samples were collected following established method27,28.

Control strains

Reference strains, L. aethiopica (MHOM/ET/72/L100), L. donovani (MHOM/ET/67/HU3), L. tropica (MHOM/IL/1980/K27), and L. infantum (MHOM/MA/67/ITMAP263), were obtained from the London School of Hygiene & Tropical Medicine (LSHTM). L. major (MHOM/SU/73/5ASKH) was sourced from the Armauer Hansen Research Institute (AHRI) cryobank.

DNA extraction

The DNeasy Blood & Tissue Kit (QIAGEN, Lot:169035660) was used as recommended by the manufacturer (QIAGEN Strasse 1, 40724 Hilden, Germany) for purifying total DNA. DNA quality and concentration was measured using NanoDrop 2000 (Thermo Scientific, 168 Third Avenue, Waltham, MA 02451, USA).

Conventional PCR

The protocol by Schönian et al. is used to amplify the ITS1 target using the primer pairs, LITSR and L5.8 S29. In Briefly in a 50 µl reaction; 25 µl Qiagen Master Mix, 2 µl each of 10 µM primers, 19 µl molecular grade water, and 2 µl DNA template. The PCR condition included initial denaturation at 95 °C for 10 min, and 35 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C (40 s), annealing at 53 °C (30 s), and extension at 72 °C (60 s) with a final extension at 72 °C for 10 min (Fig. 4).

Targeted amplicon sequencing

The ITS1 PCR amplicon was purified using magnetic beads according to the manufacturers protocol (Beckman Coulter, AMPure XP, Lot No: 18329200). The quality and concentration was measured with NanoDrop. Library preparation was performed using Illumina DNA prep (Illumina, Cat No: 20060059). Sequencing was conducted using the Illumina iSeq100 next-generation sequencing (NGS) system30.

Phylogenetic analysis

Sequence alignment was done using the MAFFT software (v7.520) (Supplementary data_4)31. A phylogenetic tree was constructed considering the maximum likelihood method using IQ-TREE2 (version 2.0.7)32.To assess the robustness of the tree, 1,000 bootstrap replicates were conducted, and the final tree was visualized and annotated using iTOL.

In-silico ITS1- RFLP

The NEBcutter online tool (version 3.0.19) was used to analyze the consensus sequences of each sample to determine the fragment sizes produced by HhaI and HaeIII restriction enzymes33–35.

Data processing

Clinical data, including age, sex, clinical manifestations, and parasite species, were recorded in Excel and analyzed using MedCalc v20.217. Descriptive statistics was presented as figures and tables. The association between clinical manifestations and parasite species was evaluated using the Chi-square test (χ2), with a significance threshold set at P ≤ 0.05.
