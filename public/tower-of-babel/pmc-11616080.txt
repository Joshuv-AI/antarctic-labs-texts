Implementation trial I: Clinical outcomes and acceptability of an internet-delivered intervention for anxiety and depression delivered as part of routine care for university students in New Zealand
Authors: Blake F. Dear, Andreea I. Heriseanu, Bareena Johnson, David Sander, Kimberly Farmer, Nickolai Titov
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPsychological distress is highly prevalent among university students and can contribute to poor academic performance and drop-out. Help-seeking has increased in recent years, contributing to growing interest in employing internet-delivered psychological treatments to support the mental health of university students. However, few large-scale “real-world” effectiveness trials have been conducted in routine care settings for this population.AimThe aim of the current study was to examine the acceptability and effectiveness of a brief, five-week internet-delivered cognitive behavioural therapy (iCBT) intervention for anxiety and depression when delivered as part of routine care by a university counselling service in New Zealand.DesignA large, prospective, single-group Phase IV clinical trial.MethodStudents (N = 1044) engaging with the university counselling service between 2018 and 2023 were provided the option to receive the intervention based on their needs and preferences. Students completed standardised measures of depression and anxiety severity at pre-treatment, each week of the intervention, and post-treatment. A subsample (n = 405) also completed these at 3-month follow-up.ResultsOver a 5-year period, 839 students participated in the intervention. Significant reductions in symptoms of depression (% reduction = 35%, Hedges' g = 0.56) and anxiety (% reduction = 36%, Hedges' g = 0.73) were observed, alongside high levels of satisfaction (> 60%). Symptom deterioration was observed in <10% of students.ConclusionThe current results provide further support for the provision of internet-delivered psychological interventions as routine care to university students with symptoms of anxiety and depression.

Introduction

Psychological disorders are highly prevalent among university students, with 31% screening positive for at least one 12-month clinical disorder, and 35% for a lifetime disorder (Auerbach et al., 2018). Findings in young people in New Zealand are commensurate with these global figures, with a 19–21% prevalence of high to very high psychological distress in 2020–2023, having increased substantially from 5% in 2011–2012 (New Zealand Ministry of Health, 2023; New Zealand Treasury, 2022). Younger people also reported greater mental health decline during the COVID-19 pandemic in New Zealand (Norina et al., 2021).

Depression and anxiety can contribute to poor academic performance and drop-out (Auerbach et al., 2016; Bruffaerts et al., 2018), and are associated with an increased risk of suicide (Gili et al., 2019), as well as reduced workforce participation, and lower economic living standards later in life (Gibb et al., 2010). Further, most lifetime cases of mental health disorders have their onset before 24 years of age (Kessler et al., 2005), and higher psychological distress has been reported in tertiary students than in age-matched non-students (Cvetkovski et al., 2012). Hence, university students represent a crucial demographic for whom early and accessible mental health interventions could be beneficial.

Although help-seeking among students experiencing mental health difficulties has increased substantially within the last decade (LaMontagne et al., 2023), barriers to help-seeking remain. These barriers include stigmatising experiences, self-stigma, concerns regarding disclosure, limited knowledge of support options, and limited availability of services for help-seeking (Broglia et al., 2021) as well as a strong preference for self-reliance (Lui et al., 2022). Barriers to service provision also exist and meeting the growing demand for mental health support in an accessible and time-effective way remains a key challenge, prompting universities to offer a range of support services to students (Stallman, 2012), including internet-delivered treatment (Dear et al., 2019; Palacios et al., 2018; Richards et al., 2016).

Internet-delivered psychological interventions represent an innovative and promising approach for addressing the mental health treatment needs of students, while also reducing burden on university services. Internet-delivered interventions aim to provide the same mental health information and skills as in-person treatment via structured online modules, and often involve the option of brief regular contact with a mental health clinician via telephone, or written secure messages (Andersson and Titov, 2014). There is now considerable evidence demonstrating the efficacy, acceptability, and cost-effectiveness of internet-delivered psychological interventions for anxiety and depression in the general adult population (Andrews et al., 2018; Donker et al., 2015; Li et al., 2022; Mitchell et al., 2021). Reflecting this, there is also now growing evidence for this modality specifically in young adults (Benjet et al., 2023; Dear et al., 2018; Harrer et al., 2019). There is also increasing evidence that internet-based treatment such as internet-delivered cognitive behavioural therapy (iCBT) can be effectively implemented into routine care (Etzelmueller et al., 2020), including for young adults (Hobbs et al., 2017; Staples et al., 2019b). Overall, these studies have had positive findings, with evidence of meaningful reductions in symptoms of depression and anxiety.

To date, there have only been a few prospective effectiveness trials of iCBT for university students delivered as part of routine care, although the available studies have had largely positive results (Dear et al., 2019; Palacios et al., 2018; Richards et al., 2016). Large-scale effectiveness trials are essential for understanding the “real-world” effects of interventions and whether the outcomes of earlier Phase II and III trials are reproduced, when accounting for the challenges of implementation, such as organisational structures and procedures, and participant characteristics which differ to those in highly-controlled clinical trials (Mohr et al., 2017). Evidence of “real-world” clinical outcomes and acceptability is a significant component of clinical trial methodology, helping to establish real-world and longer-term benefits and risks (Australian Clinical Trials, 2023). This is especially important, as demonstrated by the fact that not all attempts to provide internet-delivered psychological interventions as part of routine care have been successful (e.g., Gilbody et al., 2015; Kenter et al., 2015).

The present study is one of two related large Phase IV trials that explore the effectiveness and acceptability of an iCBT intervention for depression and anxiety when delivered as part of routine care for students attending university counselling services. The present study employed the same design and intervention as the other trial in the series, with each focussing on the outcomes of different student counselling services (Dear et al., 2024). It was hypothesised that participants would show significant improvements in symptoms of depression and anxiety, low rates of deterioration, and high levels of satisfaction. It was also hypothesised that that these improvements would be maintained at 3-month follow-up.

Method

Participants

Participants were university students who presented to the University of Auckland Student Health and Counselling Service for counselling or psychological treatment. One-thousand and forty-four students were offered the UniWellbeing Course, an internet-delivered psychological intervention, between August 2018 and September 2023. Students were eligible if they: (1) Were a current student of the university; (2) Living in New Zealand; (3) Experiencing symptoms of stress, anxiety or depression; (4) Wished to receive psychological treatment and to participate in an internet-delivered intervention; and (5) Were not at imminent risk of suicide. No other exclusion criteria were applied, including cut-offs on questionnaires or specific diagnostic criteria. Importantly, as a routine care study, student suitability was assessed via routine clinical assessment rather than with the protocolised and standardised assessment processes typically employed within early-phase controlled clinical trials. Participant flow through the study is presented in Fig. 1. Consistent with standard practice, a modified intent-to-treat protocol was followed that excluded participants who did not complete the baseline assessment, and those who did not start the first lesson. The University of Auckland Health Counselling Service is a free service offered to students by the University of Auckland, New Zealand. It is part of the University Health and Counselling Service, a New Zealand Primary Health Organisation. The Service primarily provides face-to-face counselling and psychological treatment, with telehealth sessions also offered. Students can access services by completing an online intake questionnaire, by telephone contact with the service, or by presenting in person to the service. Referrals can also be made by service clinicians and various university staff, such as residential assistants or academic staff. During the study period, the Counselling Service employed 13 clinicians (approximately 10 full-time equivalent clinicians), and was open five days a week, between the hours of 8.30am and 4.30pm. The Counselling Service made the decision to implement an internet-delivered intervention in order to reduce barriers for students to access care by providing an alternative method of supporting students, and to increase the capacity of the service by providing a solution-focused, time-efficient resource.

Study design

A prospective longitudinal single-group open trial design was used. Students were administered standardised questionnaires at pre-treatment, each week during the intervention, at post-treatment, and a subgroup of participants were followed up to 3 months post-treatment. The study was approved by the Macquarie University Human Research Ethics Committee and the University of Auckland Human Participants Ethics Committee, and the trial was registered with the Australian New Zealand Clinical Trials registry (ACTRN12618000746235).

Recruitment and enrolment

The Counselling Service website provides information on the services offered, including the internet-delivered intervention used in the current trial, the UniWellbeing Course. When first engaging with the Counselling Service, students complete an online questionnaire indicating their presenting difficulties, their coping strategies, social support networks, risk of suicide or self-harm information, substance use difficulties, availability, and interest in the internet-delivered program. Administrative staff enter this information into the Service's patient management system, and a suitable counsellor is allocated by the Head of Counselling (KF). If students indicate their interest in the internet-delivered intervention, the primary counsellor working on the intervention is notified by email.

Counsellors who identify the internet-delivered intervention as a potentially helpful modality for students could also refer students directly to the intervention. To support this referral pathway, and provide a clear understanding of the treatment, all Counselling Service clinicians were provided with training about the internet-delivered intervention by two senior clinicians (BFD and BJ) at implementation. Subsequently DS provided further training and support to service clinicians over time, particularly when new staff joined the service or significant time had passed. This included providing information about the program, as well as access to all of the intervention resources, either as downloaded PDFs, or by navigating through the intervention on the online platform through a staff account. A majority of the service clinicians used this option to familiarise themselves with the intervention. In order to increase ease of access, students could also self-refer to the internet-delivered intervention by emailing a specific university email address. The internet-delivered intervention counsellor then sends interested students an email with more information about the program, and requests a confirmation from the students that they would like to proceed. Once students confirm, they are notified that they will be enrolled in the program.

Following enrolment, students are sent an email providing key information about the intervention (important dates, information about questionnaires, and about contact by a course clinician). On the first day of the course, enrolled students receive an automatic email with login information and instructions for accessing the course materials and completing questionnaires.

Intervention

The internet-delivered intervention employed, the UniWellbeing Course, has been previously described in detail elsewhere (Dear et al., 2019; Mullin et al., 2015). Briefly, it is a transdiagnostic and internet-delivered cognitive behaviour therapy (iCBT) intervention designed to treat anxiety and depression symptoms in young adults attending university. The intervention consists of four lessons delivered sequentially over five weeks. Each lesson is presented as a series of slides including didactic information, case stories and examples from students illustrating the principles of CBT (1. Psychoeducation; 2. Cognitive challenging; 3. Physical symptoms of depression and anxiety, behavioural activation and relaxation; 4. Behavioural symptoms and graded exposure), together with supplementary material on related topics such as sleep management, managing procrastination and assertive communication. Each lesson takes between 15 and 20 min to complete. Participants also receive regular automatic emails throughout the course notifying them of new course materials, reinforce lesson completion, encourage skills practice, and normalise common challenges.

The intervention was delivered through the eCentreClinic's secure software platform, accessible by secure login through the centre's website (www.ecentreclinic.org). The eCentreClinic is a research centre at Macquarie University specialising in the development and evaluation of internet-delivered interventions. The eCentreClinic was responsible for providing access to the intervention via its platform, initial and ongoing training and supervision of counselling service staff, and as-needed advice to the service for the management of the intervention. The integration of the internet-delivered intervention within its care framework, the delivery of the intervention and all aspects of clinical care were managed by the counselling service.

In the first week of the course, the trained university counsellor sent a message to introduce themselves, to assist the students with commencing the course, and asking students about their contact preferences. The counsellor then contacted students either via phone, or through the secure messaging system, based on student preference. The counsellor described and summarised key skills and helped students apply these to their own situation, normalised challenges, reinforced progress, and encouraged lesson access and practice of skills. The counsellor attempted to limit phone contact to approximately 10 min per phone contact, although more time was allowed if clinically required. Students were informed that they could contact the counsellor by private message at any time. If students indicated increased risk of self-harm or suicide, the counsellor attempted contact by calling, and then through their student email to check in and offer additional support.

Clinician

The primary intervention clinician (DS) was a counsellor with post-graduate qualifications in counselling (Master's Degree level), who was registered with the New Zealand Association of Counsellors and had 12 years of clinical experience. Initial training was followed by regular, weekly, supervision until the counsellor was competent and confident in delivering remote treatment. Ongoing supervision and support was subsequently provided on a monthly basis by an eCentreClinic psychologist and clinical supervisor (BJ and AIH).

Measures

Demographics

Participants provided information on their age, gender, whether they were a local or international student, marital status, employment status, level of education, current university enrolment status and mental health history (e.g., mental health care professional consults, medication use).

Clinical outcomes

Initially, the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9; Kroenke et al., 2001) and Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7 (GAD-7; Löwe et al., 2008) were administered at pre-treatment, each week of treatment (i.e., weeks 1–5), post-treatment, and at 3-month follow-up. After 2.5 years (n = 405 participants), the Patient Health Questionnaire-2 (PHQ-2; Kroenke et al., 2003) and the Generalized Anxiety Disorder-2 (GAD-2; Kroenke et al., 2007) replaced these measures, and the 3-month follow-up was discontinued; both initiatives were taken to reduce burden on participants. There is considerable research supporting the broad comparability and suitability of both the short and long-form versions of each measure (Staples et al., 2019a; Hlynsson and Carlbring, 2024).

The PHQ-2 and GAD-2 scores can be derived by summing the first two items of the PHQ-9 and GAD-7, respectively. Hence, the primary outcome measures reported for this study are the PHQ-2 and the GAD-2, which were administered to the majority of participants. The PHQ-2 consists of two items that measure symptoms of low mood and depression. The GAD-2 includes two items and measures symptoms of anxiety. For both measures, scores range from 0 to 6, with higher scores representing higher symptom severity. For both measures, the following clinical ranges were used: <1: no or minimal symptom severity; 1–2: mild severity; 3–4: moderate severity; 5–6: severe depression or anxiety symptoms. These ranges were used in the current study as none are available in the literature; however, a score of ≥3 which represents a moderate or higher range has been identified as a cut-off indicative of the presence of major depression on the PHQ-2 and an anxiety disorder on the GAD-2 (Plummer et al., 2016; Richardson et al., 2010; Spitzer et al., 2006); this is consistent with a similar cut-off of ≥10, also representing a moderate or higher severity range, for the full measures (Kroenke et al., 2001; Spitzer et al., 2006). These measures were collected at baseline, as well every week during treatment, following the end of treatment, and at 3-month follow-up for a subset of participants, as described above.

Treatment satisfaction and acceptability

Treatment satisfaction and acceptability were collected at post-treatment using a purpose-built measure, the Treatment Satisfaction Questionnaires (TSQ) used in previous studies (Dear et al., 2018; Dear et al., 2019). The TSQ includes the following questions: (1) “Overall, how satisfied were you with the course?” (with responses on a 5-point Likert scale, ranging from “Very Satisfied to ‘Very Dissatisfied’); (2) ‘Would you recommend the course to others?’ (‘Yes’/”No” response); (3) “Was it worth your time doing the course?” (“Yes”/”No” response); (4) How has participating in the Course affected your confidence in your ability to manage (i) symptoms of stress and anxiety? (ii) symptoms of low mood and depression? (iii) your emotional wellbeing while studying at university? (with responses on a 5-point Likert scale, ranging from “Greatly Increased” to “Greatly Decreased”).

Statistical analysis

All analyses were conducted using SPSS v29. Descriptive statistics were calculated for baseline demographic and clinical characteristics. To address missing data, the Multiple Imputation procedure was used, accounting for participants' baseline symptom severity and the number of lessons completed, as these variables have been identified as important missing-data mechanisms (Karin et al., 2018a). Clinical outcomes over time were analysed using Generalized Estimating Equation (GEE) models (Hubbard et al., 2010). An unstructured working correlation matrix and maximum likelihood estimation were used, and a Gamma distribution with a log link response scale was specified to address positive skewness in the dependent variable distributions (Karin et al., 2018b).

Clinical outcomes

Change in clinical outcomes between pre-treatment and post-treatment was analysed for the entire sample, and change between pre-treatment and 3-month follow-up was analysed for the subset of participants (n = 405) from whom this data was collected. Symptom changes over time were examined in three ways: (1) average percentage change across time was calculated from the GEE analyses, with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) (Karin et al., 2018b); (2) within-group Hedges' g effect sizes with 95% CIs were calculated using the estimated means derived from the GEE models (within-group Hedges' g is calculated as the difference between means at the two different time-points, divided by the pooled standard deviation, with a bias correction factor applied) (Cohen, 1988; Hedges, 1981); (3) the proportion of participants making minor symptom improvements (i.e., ≥ 30% improvements), major symptom improvements (i.e., ≥ 50% improvements), and meeting criteria for deterioration (i.e., symptom deteriorations ≥30% with resultant symptoms within the moderate or severe ranges) (Dear et al., 2019; Hiller et al., 2012).

Subgroup analyses

Subgroup analyses were conducted among participants based on the baseline severity of the clinical outcomes. For both depression and anxiety, the percentage change, effect size, and clinical improvement/deterioration (defined above) were calculated separately for participants reporting mild, moderate, and severe symptoms at baseline.

Satisfaction

Due to the distribution of the responses, five-point responses to the TSQ questions were categorised into three response categories: (1) “Satisfied”/”Very Satisfied”, “Neither Satisfied Nor Dissatisfied”, and “Dissatisfied”/”Very Dissatisfied”, and (2) “Greatly Increased”/”Increased”, “Neither Increased Nor Decreased”, and “Decreased”/”Greatly Decreased”. Satisfaction data was subsequently analysed as a series of intercept-only logistic regressions, yielding percentages and 95% CI for each of the response categories.

Discussion

The current study evaluated the effectiveness and acceptability of an internet-delivered intervention following its implementation into a major university counselling service in New Zealand. The results confirmed the hypothesis that the course is effective and acceptable for university students receiving this treatment as part of routine care. Participants showed significant and clinically meaningful reductions in symptoms of depression and anxiety, which were maintained at 3-month follow-up.

Overall outcomes were comparable to those obtained in the original randomised controlled trial examining the efficacy of the intervention used in the current study (Mullin et al., 2015), and in the Phase IV trial previously examining the same intervention when implemented into another Counselling and Psychological Service (Dear et al., 2019). Treatment satisfaction was also high and deterioration rates relatively low, consistent with research general research in internet interventions (Rozental et al., 2017). Thus, these findings are supportive of the ability to and value of implementing internet-delivered interventions into the routine services of university counselling services.

This study builds on a growing literature reporting on outcomes of internet-delivered interventions delivered as part of routine care (Etzelmueller et al., 2020; Hadjistavropoulos et al., 2017; Johansson et al., 2019; Newby et al., 2017; Nordgreen et al., 2018; Titov et al., 2018). Further, it replicates the success of the implementation of the same intervention previously deployed at an Australian university (Dear et al., 2019) and another trial (N = 700) of the same intervention implemented into another Australian university in parallel with the current trial (Dear et al., 2024). In combination, the findings of these three trials (total N = ∼2500) provide strong support for the ability to embed internet-delivered interventions into the routine services of university counselling services, and their potential to complement the traditional face-to-face psychological treatments usually provided.

In addition to being efficacious, and while not recorded in the current study, one strength of internet-delivered interventions is that they usually require relatively little clinician time compared with many other interventions. For example, a previous implementation trial of the same program, delivered the same way, which carefully recorded all therapist contact, found the treatment to require only 36 min (SD = 20 min) on average per student (Dear et al., 2019)). Thus, these interventions have the potential to increase service capacity, while also having potential to reduce some of the barriers toward help-seeking experienced by young adults, such as stigma and embarrassment, lack of time, scheduling concerns, and a greater drive for self-reliance (Broglia et al., 2021; Gulliver et al., 2010; Lui et al., 2022).

There were two other potentially interesting findings in the current study. The first was that clearer evidence of symptom improvement was found among students with more severe symptoms. For example, participants with mild anxiety were not found to improve with treatment, and the improvements of people with mild depression appeared proportionally smaller than participants with more severe symptoms. This finding is consistent with previous reports of the same intervention (Dear et al., 2019) and may suggest that people with more severe symptoms are more likely to benefit in terms of symptom reductions. This is reasonable, given that this group has more “room for improvement”, but highlights the need for further work to ensure that students with milder symptoms are still receiving some form of benefit (e.g., learning of skills, symptom prevention). One possibility is that providing such interventions may assist students from getting worse during a stressful period. Reflecting this, some studies have suggested that providing these interventions to “at risk” groups prevents mental health deteriorations (Read et al., 2021).

A second interesting finding is that there appeared to be a discrepancy in satisfaction ratings, whereby a much larger proportion of participants reported that the intervention was worth their time (85%) or that they would recommend it to others (87%), versus being satisfied or very satisfied with the intervention (63%). The same pattern was observed in the parallel implementation trial (Dear et al., 2024). While it is not clear why this difference exists, it is possible that “satisfaction” reflects whether students feel they have had their mental health needs entirely met by the intervention, in which case they may not be completely “satisfied”, even if they benefitted to some extent from the intervention. Further research is needed to explore these issues, including examining who does and does not benefit, as some groups may need different treatment modalities. However, this pattern of findings is also supportive of recent trials exploring more patient-centred approaches to the delivery of internet-delivered interventions, where users are able to select the number of modules they complete and the timeframes in which they complete them (Andersson et al., 2023; Nissling et al., 2021).

There are a few factors which potentially contributed to the positive outcomes of the current implementation trial and warrant specific mention. The first is that the current study employed an intervention that has been developed and evaluated over several years prior to implementation (Mullin et al., 2015). A second potential factor was the presence of several “champions” within the counselling service, with a champion defined as a “health care provider…who volunteers or is appointed to enthusiastically promote and facilitate implementation of an innovation”. This has been associated with the increased instrumental use of innovations by an organisation (Santos et al., 2022). Both the intervention clinician and their manager acted as “champions”, ensuring the visibility of the program within the service, and facilitating the training of other Service clinicians in the intervention.

Another possible success factor is that the intervention did not constitute a replacement for traditional face-to-face services, or as a required “first step” as part of a “stepped-care” approach for any students wishing to access services. Instead, similar to the prior Phase IV trial (Dear et al., 2019), this intervention was offered on the basis of need and preference, and as just another type of intervention available at the service. Hence, clinicians and students had the option of matching the treatments offered to the clinical needs, expectations and preferences of the students. Many of these factors align with favourable steps described in the Exploration, Preparation, Implementation, Sustainment (EPIS) Framework within the implementation science field (Aarons et al., 2011). Specifically, the EPIS Framework suggests adopting an intervention with demonstrated effectiveness, assisting with on-boarding and subsequent training and supervision, building partnerships, and using optimal technologies (Graham et al., 2020).

The positive findings of the current study need to be considered alongside some limitations. First, the current trial did not include a control group and consequently the contributions of different factors (e.g., time, natural remission, general support) to the overall outcomes cannot be ascertained. Second, the sample was comprised of relatively motivated, treatment-seeking, predominantly female local students, indicating that some caution is needed in generalising these results to university students with different characteristics. Third, limited information was collected about the reasons for not starting or not completing the intervention, only limited clinical history data was collected, only two brief self-report measures of severity could be administered, and only short-term follow-up was possible. However, extensive data collection and follow-up was not feasible in the current trial due to high participant burden, and wishing to avoid additional barriers to accessing care in this population and in a routine care context. Fourth, the short-form outcome measures used in the study do not have recognised severity bandings, which meant they had to be devised for the current study. Finally, there was considerable infrastructure and support provided by the clinical researchers from a university centre specialising in internet-delivered interventions. Thus, while all aspects of clinical care were managed by the service, it is unclear if the same outcomes might have been obtained without the training and support provided from a group specialising in this treatment approach.

Conclusion

The current study aimed to examine the effectiveness and acceptability of an internet-delivered psychological intervention for university students with anxiety and depression symptoms when provided as part of routine care by a university counselling service. The study has some notable strengths including the use of a large sample, validated measures and a best-practice approach to handling missing data with intention-to-treat analyses. Encouragingly, the intervention was found to be acceptable, and evidence of clinically meaningful improvement was observed, particularly among participants with moderate to severe symptoms. This study therefore adds further evidence to support the potential of internet-delivered psychological interventions as part of routine care within university counselling services.
