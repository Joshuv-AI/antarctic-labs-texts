Immunohistochemical and Molecular Features Distinguishing Metaplastic Breast Carcinoma With Squamous Differentiation From Cutaneous Squamous Cell Carcinoma
Authors: Cooper D. Rutland, Leandra G. Kingsley, Sabrina Zdravkovic, Poonam Vohra, Yunn-Yi Chen, Gregor Krings, Gregory R. Bean
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Metaplastic breast carcinoma (MBC) with squamous differentiation can often be diagnosed by routine histopathologic evaluation, but cutaneous squamous cell carcinoma (cSCC) may enter the differential diagnosis in selected pure squamous carcinomas involving the breast/chest, particularly in limited samples or cases lacking definitive epidermal connection, ductal carcinoma in situ, or complete clinical information. Determining the site of origin is clinically important because management strategies for breast and cutaneous carcinomas differ substantially. We sought to identify immunohistochemical and genetic features that could distinguish MBC with squamous differentiation from cSCC arising in breast/chest skin in cases of uncertain origin. To this end, we comprehensively characterized 15 MBC with pure to mixed squamous differentiation and 18 invasive/in situ cSCC arising in breast/chest skin. Comparisons between these cohorts and 48 SCC of other anatomic sites identified significant immunohistochemical differences, with CK7, SOX10, and TRPS1 expression more frequent in MBC than cSCC (P=0.0005, 0.0227, and 0.0290, respectively). HER2 overexpression was identified only in breast and esophageal carcinomas. Next-generation sequencing of the breast/chest tumors demonstrated more frequent PIK3CA/PIK3R1 alterations in MBC than cSCC (P=0.0040), whereas NOTCH1 mutations were exclusive to cSCC (P=0.0407). cSCC showed higher tumor mutational burden and percentage of ultraviolet-associated mutations than MBC (P=0.0002 and <0.0001, respectively). These distinguishing features were then applied to 9 additional breast cases of uncertain origin, demonstrating the added value of molecular data in diagnostically challenging cases. Overall, these findings support a stepwise diagnostic approach integrating immunohistochemistry, selected molecular features, and clinicopathologic context to aid in this distinction.

Metaplastic breast carcinomas (MBC) represent a heterogeneous group of invasive carcinomas that can demonstrate squamous differentiation or differentiation toward heterologous mesenchymal-like elements.1 Squamous differentiation in MBC may be present with other malignant heterologous or spindle cell components, mixed with conventional invasive breast carcinoma of no special type (IBC-NST), or occur as pure squamous cell carcinoma (SCC). Although many cases are readily classified by location, epidermal connection, or an in situ component, a subset of pure squamous carcinomas involving the breast/chest wall poses an anatomic and diagnostic challenge, particularly in limited sampling such as core needle or punch biopsy. This distinction is clinically important because MBC with squamous differentiation and cutaneous SCC (cSCC) differ significantly in staging, expected behavior, and treatment algorithms.2

Immunohistochemistry alone may not resolve this diagnostic dilemma. GATA3 and TRPS1 are markers that may be positive in MBC but are not entirely specific to mammary differentiation, as expression has also been reported in benign and neoplastic squamous epithelium.3–6 ER is also of limited diagnostic specificity in this setting. However, in contrast to the traditional view that MBC is invariably a triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC), a subset of MBC with squamous differentiation has demonstrated HER2 overexpression and amplification (up to 22% in 1 study).7,8 By contrast, HER2 overexpression/amplification in cSCC is not well documented.

Excluding rare low-grade subtypes, MBC harbors complex genomes with recurrent pathogenic mutations in TP53 and the TERT promoter, as well as PI3K/AKT and Wnt pathway alterations.9–13 By comparison, cutaneous squamous cell carcinomas are known to be associated with ultraviolet (UV)-induced aberrations—C→T or CC→TT mutations occurring at dipyrimidine sites—as well as TP53, NOTCH1/2, and CDKN2A alterations.14–17 These divergent genomic patterns suggest that selected molecular features may provide adjunctive evidence of tumor origin when interpreted with morphology, immunophenotype, and clinical context.

In the setting of a breast/chest squamous carcinoma without definitive histopathologic or clinical evidence supporting its origin from either the breast epithelium or the skin, we sought to identify features that may help distinguish MBC with squamous differentiation from cSCC and other SCC. To this end, we genetically and immunophenotypically characterized a collection of MBC with pure-to-mixed squamous differentiation and invasive and in situ cSCC arising in breast and chest skin. In addition to these known-origin cohorts, we included additional breast cases that could not be definitively classified as MBC or cSCC after initial sampling and/or complete resection, underscoring the practical relevance of this dilemma.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study Population

The pathology archives of our institutions were searched for cases from 2005 to 2025 to generate a total of 4 squamous-containing tumor cohorts. The 2 known-origin comparator cohorts were (1) MBC with pure or mixed squamous metaplastic differentiation (MBC1-15) and (2) cutaneous invasive (cSCC1-11) or in situ (cSCC12-18) SCC arising in breast or chest skin. All MBC patients underwent biopsy and subsequent surgical excision. All but 2 cSCC patients underwent shave biopsy; cSCC5 and cSCC7 underwent surgical excision. A third cohort included breast or axillary SCC for which clinicopathologic correlation could not definitively assign anatomic origin (SCC of uncertain origin cohort; U1-9). This designation required pure squamous differentiation without epidermal connection, ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS), or atypical ductal hyperplasia (ADH) in evaluable material. Cases with a prior history of squamous atypia or carcinoma and/or ADH/DCIS were excluded. In 3 of these cases (U2, U7, and U8), the patients underwent biopsy without subsequent surgery. The remaining 6 patients underwent subsequent surgical excision. A fourth cohort of invasive SCC arising at additional nonbreast/chest/axillary anatomic sites was also compiled for immunohistochemical evaluation and incorporated into tissue microarrays (TMA). Clinical information was obtained from electronic medical records.

Tissue Microarrays

TMA blocks of invasive SCC arising at nonbreast/chest/axillary anatomic sites were constructed, including the following cases: anogenital (vulva, n=6; cervix, n=6; anus, n=6; 18 total), aerodigestive (tongue, n=6; esophagus, n=6; larynx, n=6; 18 total), lung (n=6), and urothelium (n=6). This cohort contained 8 well-differentiated, 19 moderately differentiated, and 21 poorly differentiated SCC by histologic grading. For each case, a representative tumor was selected from formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissue blocks based on histologic assessment of corresponding hematoxylin and eosin (H&E)-stained slides. Each tumor was sampled in at least duplicate for TMA construction, using 2 mm diameter core punches.

Immunohistochemistry and In Situ Hybridization

The following antibodies were used for immunohistochemistry: TRPS1 (polyclonal, PAS-845874, 1:500; ThermoFisher, Waltham, MA), GATA3 (L50-823, 1:50; Biocare Medical, Concord, CA), SOX10 (BC34, 1:50; Biocare Medical), CK7 (OV-TL12/30, 1:800; DAKO, Santa Clara, CA), ER (SP1, undiluted; Ventana), and HER2 (4B5, undiluted; Ventana). Antigen retrieval was as follows: for GATA3 and SOX10, BOND ER2 (Leica Biosystems, Deer Park, IL); for TRPS1, ER, and HER2, Ventana CC1 (Ventana); and for CK7, enzyme (Leica Biosystems).

For all MBC cases, immunoreactivity for TRPS1, GATA3, CK7, SOX10, and ER was evaluated and scored only in metaplastic squamous components. Positive TRPS1, GATA3, and SOX10 staining was based on nuclear immunoreactivity and evaluated using a prespecified semiquantitative extent score: negative (<1%), low positive (1% to 10%), moderately positive (11% to 75%), or diffusely positive (>75%). An identical scoring system was used to evaluate the degree of membranous immunoreactivity for CK7. For ER and HER2, positive staining was defined according to ASCO/CAP guidelines.18,19 HER2 testing was routinely performed using both IHC and fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) upfront, regardless of the IHC result. IHC was evaluated by at least two pathologists. HER2 FISH testing was performed using the Abbott Vysis Pathvysion (Des Plaines, IL) FDA-cleared kit following the manufacturer's recommendations, and was scored and interpreted using ASCO/CAP guidelines.19

Next-generation DNA Sequencing

Targeted next-generation DNA sequencing was performed on 32/38 cases from the 2 primary cohorts. Sequencing libraries were prepared from genomic DNA extracted from punch biopsies or macrodissected unstained sections from FFPE tissue. Matched normal tissue was also selected for sequencing in 10 tumors (MBC1 to 5, 9, 13, and 14, cSCC7, and U3) and used for enhanced analysis of somatic mutations. In mixed tumors with a nonsquamous component, sequencing was always performed on the squamous metaplastic population. For 2 cases (MBC9 and 12), a separate nonsquamous portion was also sequenced. For 2 cases (MBC5 and 13), the sequenced tumor had received neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NACT); in MBC13, the squamous component was not represented in the pretreatment biopsy. The majority of cases were subjected to the Tempus xT (version 4) assay (Tempus, Chicago, IL), which detects single-nucleotide variants, indels, and translocations in 648 genes (details available at https://www.tempus.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/10/Tempus-xT-CDx_Technical-Information.pdf). Alternatively, cases MBC6 and U7 were subjected to the STAMP assay (version 4), a 130-gene targeted panel, as previously described.20 Cases MBC5, MBC8, and U6 were subjected to the UCSF500 assay, a 479-gene panel, as previously described; NGS results of MBC5 and MBC8 were previously reported in part.13,21 Case U4 was subjected to the FoundationOne CDx assay (Foundation Medicine, Boston, MA), a 324-gene panel (details available at https://www.foundationmedicine.com/sites/default/files/media/documents/2026-02/F1CDxTechSpecs_Commercial_SPEC-01197_V5.0.pdf). Case MBC11 was subjected to the MI Cancer Seek assay (Caris Life Sciences, Irving, TX), a 228-gene panel (details available at https://d35ibn74wp9llh.cloudfront.net/000d31fd-e04b-4936-be1c-11acbe19e1ec/b8abe627-f449-4ce7-93f2-bd54f87b050d/b8abe627-f449-4ce7-93f2-bd54f87b050d_source__v.pdf).

Tumor mutational burden (TMB) was calculated by each clinical assay according to its validated pipeline and reported as mutations per megabase; TMB values were not re-harmonized across platforms. To investigate UV-associated mutational patterns in cases with available mutation-level data, the number and percentage of “C→T” and “CC→TT” nucleotide alterations were determined relative to the total number of detected nonsynonymous alterations. Confirmed or probable germline variants based on allele frequency were excluded from this analysis. A UV-mutational signature was considered present using conventional thresholds of >60% UV-M (C→T + CC→TT) substitutions and >5% CC→TT substitutions, interpreted in the context of mutation count and assay platform.16,22

Statistical Analysis

Statistical comparisons were performed using 2-sided Fisher’s exact tests, Mann-Whitney U tests, or Dunn tests with Holm adjustment, as appropriate. P<0.05 was considered significant. As an exploratory analysis, binary tumor-level features were extracted from known-origin MBC and cSCC cases. Two logistic-regression models were trained: (1) an IHC-only model and (2) a combined IHC+NGS model. The IHC-only model was trained and validated on 30 known-origin tumors (12 MBC and 18 cSCC) with complete IHC data, whereas the combined IHC+NGS model was trained and validated on 23 known-origin tumors (10 MBC and 13 cSCC) with complete data. Model performance was assessed using leave-one-out cross-validation (LOOCV) on the known-origin training sets. The IHC-only model was also re-evaluated on the same 23 cases used for the combined model. For the primary binary models, IHC variables were dichotomized from the prespecified semiquantitative categories, with any staining ≥1% considered positive; sensitivity analyses were also performed using a stricter cutoff in which low-positive staining (1% to 10%) was considered negative and using ordinal extent scores (negative=0, low positive=1, moderately positive=2, diffusely positive=3). After performance assessment, final models were fit on all eligible known-origin cases and used to generate exploratory case-level probabilities for the uncertain-origin tumors (U). Predicted class assignment was defined as MBC when the predicted probability was ≥0.5, and cSCC when <0.5. Secondary exploratory models classified cases lacking IHC or UV-mutational data.

RESULTS

Summary of Cohorts

The clinicopathologic features of MBC with squamous differentiation (n=15) and SCC of uncertain origin (U, n=9) are summarized in Table 1, with corresponding cSCC cohort features summarized in Supplemental Table S1, Supplemental Digital Content 1, https://links.lww.com/PAS/C400. The majority of patients (19/24, 79%) presented with a palpable mass. Ulceration was less common (5/24, 21%). For the MBC cohort, the median patient age was 64 years (range 43 to 85), and the median tumor size was 3.5 cm (range 1.5-13). The median patient age in the U cohort was 82 years (range: 63 to 91), with a median tumor size of 5.4 cm (range: 3.5 to 10); age and size were not significantly different from those of the MBC cohort (Table 2, Supplemental Table S2, Supplemental Digital Content 1, https://links.lww.com/PAS/C400). In comparison, the median patient age of the cSCC cohort (invasive n=11, in situ n=7) was 63.5 years (range: 54 to 97), and the median invasive tumor size, excluding in situ extent, was 1 cm (range: 0.3 to 12.7); the cSCC cohort consisted of significantly younger patients with smaller tumors compared with the U cohort (P=0.0347 and 0.0023, respectively), but not the MBC cohort. More male patients (n=8) were present in the cSCC cohort compared with the U (n=2) and MBC (n=0) cohorts.

For MBC, all cases were Nottingham grade 3, and all but 1 case demonstrated poorly differentiated squamous carcinoma (Fig. 1). Four MBC cases were composed of pure (100%) squamous morphology. In tumors with mixed morphologies, the nonsquamous component ranged from 20% to 95% and consisted of IBC-NST in the majority (Table 1). High-grade DCIS was identified in most cases (9/15, 60%). One pure squamous carcinoma without DCIS demonstrated adjacent ADH. Another pure squamous case without DCIS had a concurrent, separate, radiographically adjacent IBC-NST. With additional clinical correlation, both cases were interpreted as MBC (MBC14 and MBC11, respectively). The cSCC and U cases ranged from well-differentiated to poorly differentiated pure squamous carcinoma. One case (U7) demonstrated squamous carcinoma that appeared entirely in situ, but it was uncertain whether the lesion arose within a breast duct or an epidermal inclusion cyst.

A minority of MBC cases (4/15, 27%) demonstrated low to intermediate levels of ER expression, and the remainder were ER negative (Table 1, Figs. 2, 3). Two cases (13%) exhibited HER2 positivity in the squamous cell population: one (MBC2) showed clustered heterogeneity with FISH amplification in IHC 3+ areas (and ASCO/CAP group 4 FISH results in IHC 2+ areas), and another (MBC1) was amplified without heterogeneity. Notably, one additional case (MBC12) also demonstrated HER2 heterogeneity; however, HER2 positivity was confined to the IBC-NST component (IHC 3+, FISH amplified), while the squamous component was HER2-negative (IHC 1+, FISH negative). Another case (MBC13) was group 2 by FISH (HER2/CEP17 ratio 2.22, HER2 signals/cell 2.52) and 2+ by IHC, and this was interpreted as HER2-positive based on pre-2018 guidelines. Post-treatment testing was 0 by IHC and group 5 by FISH. Among the U cases, 2 demonstrated intermediate ER expression, and 1 was positive for HER2 amplification.

Of the MBC and U patients who underwent surgery (n=21), 11 (52%) underwent mastectomy, 8 (38%) underwent lumpectomy, and 2 (10%) underwent excisional or incisional biopsy. Most patients received chemotherapy and/or immunotherapy (16/20, 80%), with 7 (35%) treated neoadjuvantly (2 of which were converted to palliative with no subsequent surgery) and 4 (20%) receiving HER2-directed therapy. Only one neoadjuvant-treated case (MBC2) demonstrated a complete pathologic response to NACT. Adjuvant endocrine therapy was used in 4/6 (67%) patients with ER-positive tumors. Of the patients with clinical follow-up (n=18, median: 24 mo, range: 3 to 112), 5 (28%) died of disease.

MBC With Squamous Differentiation and Cutaneous SCC Show Differences in Immunophenotypic Profiles

MBC, cSCC, and uncertain-origin cases were evaluated by immunohistochemistry to identify potentially distinguishing markers. Because the clinical differential diagnosis in difficult cases may extend beyond cSCC, we also surveyed SCC from additional anatomic sites (anogenital, aerodigestive, lung, and bladder) as a broader analysis (Supplemental Table S3, Supplemental Digital Content 1, https://links.lww.com/PAS/C400). The panel included TRPS1, GATA3, CK7, and SOX10, in addition to ER and HER2 (Table 2, Figs. 2, 3, Supplemental Figs. S1, Supplemental Digital Content 2, https://links.lww.com/PAS/C401 S2, Supplemental Digital Content 2, https://links.lww.com/PAS/C401).

MBC with squamous differentiation and SCC showed variable immunoreactivity for TRPS1, GATA3, and CK7. MBC with squamous differentiation was positive for TRPS1 in 77% of cases (10/13), GATA3 in 92% of cases (12/13), and CK7 in 83% of cases (10/12). SCC from other sites (excluding MBC) showed TRPS1 positivity in 28% (19/67), GATA3 positivity in 42% (28/67), and CK7 positivity in 27% (18/67) of cases, with a broad range in the degree of immunoreactivity for each marker. SOX10 positivity was observed exclusively in MBC with squamous differentiation (4/13, 31%), while all 67 SCC from nonbreast sites were negative (P=0.0005). MBC with squamous differentiation demonstrated significantly higher rates of expression for TRPS1, GATA3, and CK7 compared with SCC from all other sites (P=0.0015, 0.0015, and 0.0003, respectively). When comparing MBC to only cSCC, expression differences for TRPS1, CK7, and SOX10 remained significant, but this was not the case for GATA3 (Table 2).

Regarding ER, numerous cases of all types demonstrated some degree of ER expression (>1%), including 11/48 (23%) SCC of other anatomic sites. Notably, infrequent tumors showed significant (>10%) ER expression of low to intermediate intensity, including 2 MBC (MBC14: ER 70%, 1 to 2+, pure squamous; MBC4: ER 20%, 1+, 50% squamous differentiation) and 2 pure SCC of uncertain origin (U4: ER 75%, 1 to 2+; U8: ER 30%, 1+), as well as 5 anogenital, 2 aerodigestive, and 1 lung SCC (Supplemental Figs. S1, Supplemental Digital Content 2, https://links.lww.com/PAS/C401 and S2, Supplemental Digital Content 2, https://links.lww.com/PAS/C401).

HER2 positivity was relatively uncommon in MBC with squamous differentiation (3/15, including MBC12 with HER2 positivity only in the NST component, Supplemental Table S2, Supplemental Digital Content 1, https://links.lww.com/PAS/C400). No cSCC demonstrated HER2 overexpression/amplification (17/18 were IHC 0 to 1+, and 1 large tumor demonstrated focal 2+ with no evidence of ERBB2 amplification by NGS). Among SCCs from other anatomic sites, only one esophageal tumor was positive for HER2 overexpression (3+ by IHC; all other cases 0 to 1+).

PI3K Pathway Mutations Occur More Frequently in MBC With Squamous Differentiation Relative to Cutaneous SCC

The majority of MBC with squamous differentiation (n=14), cutaneous SCC (n=13), and SCC of uncertain origin (n=7) cases were analyzed by NGS. TP53 was the most frequently mutated gene in all 3 cohorts (MBC: 12/14, 86%; cSCC: 11/13, 85%; U: 6/7, 86%). Alterations in RB1, NF1, and the TERT promoter were also recurrently identified across groups.

In contrast to MBC, no HER2 alterations were identified in cSCC cases (0/13).

NOTCH Mutations Occur More Frequently in Cutaneous SCC Than MBC With Squamous Differentiation

Of the known-origin cases, NOTCH1/2/3 alterations (all nonsense mutations) were exclusively identified in cSCC (4/13, 31%) and not detected in MBC (0/14) (P=0.0407). Of these 4 NOTCH1/2/ 3 mutated tumors, all harbored at least one NOTCH1 mutation, most (3/4) harbored additional NOTCH2 mutation(s), and one also harbored a NOTCH3 mutation. Recurrent alterations were also identified in other NOTCH pathway genes, FAT1 (5/13, 38%, most often co-occurring with NOTCH1/2/3) and ZNF750 (3/13, 23%). Taken together, NOTCH pathway aberrations were identified in 8/13 (62%) of cSCC cases, but no MBC (P=0.0007). Similarly, ARID2 mutations (4/13, 31%) were only identified in cSCC, but this finding was not statistically significant.

Tumor Mutational Burden and UV-associated Mutational Patterns are Significantly Higher in Cutaneous SCC Than MBC With Squamous Differentiation

For a subset of tumors interrogated by NGS (32 cases total: 13 MBC, 13 cSCC, 6 U), individual nucleotide alterations and TMB were also examined (Fig. 5, Supplemental Table S6, Supplemental Digital Content 1, https://links.lww.com/PAS/C400). Overall, TMB was significantly higher in cSCC (median: 19.5 m/Mb, IQR: 6.8 to 34.7) than MBC (median: 2.6 m/Mb, IQR: 2.5 to 4) (P=0.0002). A majority of cSCC cases had TMB >10 (7/13, 54%), compared with only 1 MBC (1/12, 8%) (P=0.027).

Application of Distinguishing Features to SCC of Uncertain Origin

Univariable odds ratios of immunohistochemical and molecular features associated with MBC were calculated; a forest plot is depicted in Figure 6. These distinguishing features were evaluated in the SCC of uncertain origin (U) cases and compared with the known-origin cohorts (Supplemental Table S2, Supplemental Digital Content 1, https://links.lww.com/PAS/C400). GATA3, SOX10, and HER2 did not significantly differ between U and either comparator cohort. TRPS1 expression was more frequent in the U cohort (6/7, 86%) compared with the cSCC cohort (6/18, 33%, P=0.0302), and CK7 was less frequent in the U cohort (2/7, 29%) compared with the MBC cohort (10/12, 83%, P=0.0449). In terms of molecular features, frequencies of PI3K and NOTCH alterations did not significantly differ between U and either MBC or cSCC cases, but TMB and %UV-M were both higher in the U cohort (TMB median: 10 m/Mb, IQR: 6.3 to 17.6; UV-M median 66%, IQR: 47% to 80%) than the MBC cohort (P=0.0155 and 0.0285, respectively), but not the cSCC cases. Of the U cases, 3/7 (43%) demonstrated TMB >10, and 1 case (U6, 1/6, 17%) showed thymine dimers and fulfilled criteria for a UV-mutational signature.

As an exploratory analysis, 2 proof-of-principle binary logistic-regression models were trained: (1) an IHC-only model incorporating GATA3, SOX10, CK7, TRPS1, and HER2 status; and (2) a combined IHC+NGS model incorporating the same IHC variables plus the presence or absence of PIK3CA/PIK3R1 mutations, NOTCH1 mutations, TMB >10 m/Mb, and UV-mutational signature (Supplemental Table S7, Supplemental Digital Content 1, https://links.lww.com/PAS/C400). On LOOCV, the IHC-only model achieved an accuracy of 76.7% (23/30) and an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.843, with 66.7% sensitivity for MBC and 83.3% specificity for cSCC. The combined IHC+NGS model achieved an improved accuracy of 86.9% (20/23) and an AUC of 0.908, with 90.0% sensitivity for MBC and 84.6% specificity for cSCC, although this comparison is influenced by the smaller subset with complete data available. When both models were compared on the same 23 complete cases, the IHC-only model achieved 73.9% accuracy and an AUC of 0.862, whereas the combined IHC+NGS model achieved 86.9% accuracy and an AUC of 0.908. Sensitivity analyses using a stricter IHC cutoff (>10% expression considered positive) and ordinal IHC extent scores yielded similar model performance. These findings support a stepwise diagnostic framework in which IHC provides useful discrimination between cohorts, with additional improvement when NGS-associated features are incorporated.

The uncertain-origin cases illustrated how IHC and molecular findings may contribute to integrated interpretation in diagnostically challenging tumors. Features supporting MBC included HER2 amplification, CK7 positivity, and PIK3CA/PIK3R1 alterations, whereas features supporting cSCC included NOTCH1 alterations, high TMB, UV-mutational signature, and thymine dimers (Fig. 6). In exploratory model outputs, the IHC-only model assigned U1 and U3 to MBC and U2, U4, U5, U8, and U9 to cSCC (Supplemental Fig. S6, Supplemental Digital Content 2, https://links.lww.com/PAS/C401). The combined model assigned U1, U3, and U5 to MBC, with the notable shifting of U5 from cSCC by IHC alone to MBC after incorporation of molecular features. U4 lacked UV-signature data and was evaluated with an exploratory modified model, which classified it as MBC. U6 and U7 lacked IHC data and were evaluated using an NGS-only model, which provisionally classified both as cSCC.

DISCUSSION

Pure squamous carcinomas involving the breast without associated epidermal involvement or adjacent DCIS may create diagnostic uncertainty between MBC with squamous differentiation and cSCC, particularly with limited sampling. Although uncommon, this distinction has practical implications for staging and clinical management, as illustrated by the 9 cases of uncertain origin included in this study. We directly compared known-origin MBC with squamous differentiation and cSCC arising in breast/chest skin by immunophenotype, mutational profile, and UV-signature metrics, and our results support the utility of a combined IHC/NGS approach to aid diagnosis in this context. MBC was relatively enriched for CK7, TRPS1, and SOX10 expression and demonstrated more frequent PIK3CA/PIK3R1 mutations than cSCC. HER2 amplification was identified only in MBC with squamous differentiation, albeit in rare cases. On the other hand, NOTCH1/2/3 mutations were only identified in cSCC. Notably, we confirm a significantly higher burden of UV-induced mutations in cutaneous SCC than in MBC with squamous differentiation, underscored by the absence of thymine dimers in MBC. Immunohistochemical workup and NGS may therefore contribute valuable information when routine morphology and clinicopathologic correlation do not resolve this differential diagnosis.

Prior work has examined the molecular features of MBC and cSCC separately, but MBC studies have often analyzed metaplastic carcinomas broadly rather than focusing on squamous differentiation. PI3K/AKT pathway alterations are common in MBC, notably more frequent than in other triple-negative breast cancers.9,12,13,23,24 Relative to other MBC, MBC with spindled and/or squamous components have been reported to be enriched in PIK3CA, PIK3R1, and PTEN alterations, with PTEN alterations particularly associated with squamous differentiation.9,13,25 TP53 alterations are also frequent across subtypes.9,11–13,23 Our results corroborate these findings, with TP53 mutations detected in the majority of cases (MBC: 12/14, 86%; MBC/U/cSCC: 29/34, 85%) and PIK3CA/PIK3R1/PTEN alterations identified in most MBC with squamous differentiation (12/14, 86%). In contrast, only 2 cSCC harbored PI3K pathway alterations, both involving PIK3CA, and no PIK3R1 alterations were identified in cSCC. One of these cases, cSCC15, was exclusively in situ, arose in a man, and demonstrated thymine dimers and a UV-mutational signature in addition to the PIK3CA mutation (p.E453K), an oncogenic activating missense variant but not a canonical hotspot (E542, E545, and H1047). cSCC7 is discussed below. Thus, in this differential diagnosis, a canonical hotspot PIK3CA mutation or PIK3R1 alteration provides strong evidence favoring MBC, particularly when concordant with morphology or immunophenotype. Nonhotspot PIK3CA alterations may be less specific.

For cSCC, the canonical pathogenesis is that of UV-induced DNA damage, resulting in a UV-mutational signature and accumulation of thymine dimers. Recurrent alterations in TP53, NOTCH1/2, CDKN2A, TERT promoter, FAT1, and chromatin-remodeling genes have been reported.17,26–30 Our series confirms several of these findings, with NOTCH1/2/3, ARID2, and FAT1 aberrations limited to the cSCC cohort and not detected in MBC with squamous differentiation. Of note, FAT1 alterations have been previously reported in MBC, but these occurred either in spindle-subtype or unspecified-subtype cases without a clear association with squamous differentiation.9,31,32 Also consistent with our data, PIK3CA alterations have been rarely reported in the cSCC literature, with noncanonical hotspots predominating and hotspot mutations only occurring in <5% of cases.30,33 In contrast, RB1, NF1, CDKN2A/B, KMT2 family, HRAS, and TERT promoter alterations have been reported in both cSCC and MBC (with at least the latter 2 in squamous cases).9,11,15,25,30,34–37 Taken together with the current data, NOTCH pathway aberrations are supportive of cutaneous origin, whereas shared high-grade carcinoma alterations are less informative.

HER2 overexpression/amplification was uncommon but potentially important in this differential diagnosis. As a special type of breast cancer, MBC is classically considered triple-negative, with few reports of HER2 positivity in MBC overall (0 to 5% of cases).38–40 However, reports on MBC with squamous differentiation suggest relative enrichment for HER2 positivity in this subtype (15% to 22% of cases).7,41 Two tumors in our series showed HER2 positivity within the squamous component, and in an additional mixed tumor (MBC12), HER2 positivity was confined to the associated IBC-NST component (Supplemental Fig. S4, Supplemental Digital Content 2, https://links.lww.com/PAS/C401). Another case (MBC13) was clinically managed as HER2-positive using pre-2018 ASCO/CAP criteria, but would be considered HER2-negative by current criteria (ISH group 2). Notably, all 4 patients received HER2-directed therapy, 3 in the neoadjuvant setting and 1 adjuvantly; none of the neoadjuvant-treated cases showed residual HER2-positive carcinoma, and 1 achieved a complete pathologic response. To our knowledge, bona fide HER2 overexpression/amplification has not been reported in cutaneous SCC. Of the nonbreast/axillary/chest cases tested, HER2 overexpression was identified only in one esophageal SCC. Thus, HER2 positivity in this differential strongly favors breast origin, as metastasis of a HER2-positive nonmammary squamous carcinoma to the breast would be extraordinarily rare. These findings also underscore the heterogeneity that may occur in MBC and support the evaluation of individual components in mixed tumors when assessing breast predictive biomarkers.

Among molecular features, UV-associated mutation patterns were particularly useful for distinguishing MBC with squamous differentiation from cSCC arising in breast/chest skin, likely reflecting tumor etiology rather than lineage-marker expression. In our cohort, UV-mutational signature and thymine dimers were observed exclusively in cSCC, supporting high specificity for cutaneous origin within this differential diagnosis. When these features were incorporated into exploratory logistic-regression models, IHC alone provided useful discrimination, with additional improvement after incorporation of NGS-associated features. Model performance was driven by a relatively small number of informative variables, particularly CK7, PIK3CA/PIK3R1 alteration, and UV-mutational signature (Supplemental Table S7, Supplemental Digital Content 1, https://links.lww.com/PAS/C400). While not intended as clinically validated diagnostic classifiers, these models support the concept that lineage-associated immunophenotype and etiology-associated molecular findings contribute complementary information to classification.

One known-origin case, cSCC7, was an outlier, highlighting the lack of an independent gold standard. This patient was a 62-year-old woman with a 7.2 cm poorly differentiated squamous carcinoma initially treated as a breast primary with neoadjuvant paclitaxel/carboplatin and pembrolizumab. However, the tumor was reclassified as cSCC at mastectomy because of skin ulceration, atypical epidermis, apparent epidermal continuity, and the absence of ADH/DCIS (Supplemental Fig. S5, Supplemental Digital Content 2, https://links.lww.com/PAS/C401). There was no definite evidence of treatment effect. However, this tumor is the only one of the cSCC cohort that harbors a PIK3CA hotspot mutation, and the only invasive cSCC lacking thymine dimers. This combination of findings is highly suggestive of breast epithelial origin based on our other data. As only the deeper, poorly differentiated component was sequenced, definite conclusions cannot be drawn, but alternative possibilities include breast carcinoma with significant epidermotropic involvement and/or inducing pseudoepitheliomatous hyperplasia of the overlying skin, or an unlikely collision tumor. The patient continued adjuvant immunotherapy but developed axillary lymph node, lung, and brain metastases and died of disease 17 months after initial diagnosis. Importantly, whether cSCC7 is categorized as cSCC, MBC, or excluded from analysis, the major univariable differences between groups remain robust and statistically significant.

The application to the uncertain-origin cohort further highlighted both the value and the limitations of an integrated IHC/NGS approach. These cases could not be definitively classified after clinicopathologic review, and IHC and molecular findings provided additional support for a favored origin in several tumors. The most diagnostically persuasive examples were U1, with HER2 amplification and CK7 positivity favoring MBC (Figs. 1I, 3F); U3, with CK7 positivity and a PIK3CA hotspot mutation favoring MBC (Figs. 1D, 6B–E); and U6, with NOTCH1/2/3 alterations and thymine dimers favoring cSCC. A PIK3CA hotspot mutation detected in U5 favored MBC despite absent CK7 expression. By contrast, U7 was a squamous carcinoma that appeared entirely in situ, potentially arising in an epidermal inclusion cyst, and lacked distinguishing molecular features, resulting only in slight support for cSCC. U2 was similar to U5 (PI3K alteration and negative CK7) but with a TMB of 10.5, which resulted in slight support for cSCC. U2 is the only uncertain-origin case affected if cSCC7 is reclassified as MBC or excluded from analysis, which increases the discriminatory power of a PI3K alteration and converts U2’s prediction to MBC. Notably, both U6 and U7 arose in male patients, which also favors cSCC over MBC. Clinical follow-up in this cohort was limited, either because patients died of disease (U4, U7, U8) or were lost to follow-up (U1, U2, U5). Nevertheless, a subset was managed as breast primary tumors, with lumpectomy or mastectomy performed in 4/9 cases and chemotherapy administered in 2 patients (U3 and U4, the latter also receiving endocrine therapy).

These examples highlight the importance of an integrated interpretation, including morphology, IHC, NGS, and clinical context (eg, patient sex) when considering breast versus cutaneous origin. No single feature is sufficiently sensitive and specific for assigning origin in all uncertain cases. Although CK7 and TRPS1 were significantly more frequent in MBC with squamous differentiation than in cSCC in our cohort, caution is warranted in relying on immunohistochemistry alone. Multiple studies have documented TRPS1 expression in cutaneous tumors and non-neoplastic skin.5,6,42 In addition, although less frequently emphasized in the literature, up to 22% of cutaneous SCC have been reported to show CK7 immunoreactivity, with higher rates in poorly differentiated tumors that can mimic the high-grade morphology often seen in MBC with squamous differentiation.43 GATA3 also showed substantial overlap and has been reported in both breast carcinoma and cutaneous squamous lesions, as well as in nonlesional skin.3,4,42,44,45 SOX10 was specific for MBC in our cohort, but its limited sensitivity (4/13, 31%) reduces its utility as a stand-alone marker. A similar rate of SOX10 positivity (9/20 cases, 45%) has been reported in MBC with squamous differentiation in another study.44 ER was not consistently performed in cSCC, limiting comparison. Nevertheless, weak ER positivity in >10% of cells was seen in both MBC and nonbreast SCC (8/48, 17%), suggesting that weak to intermediate ER expression in an uncertain-origin case is not specific.

For SCC occurring in the breast/chest for which the anatomic origin is uncertain, classification has direct implications for staging, prognostication, and oncologic management. This is particularly relevant for larger tumors, as all but one case in our uncertain-origin cohort measured at least 4 cm. By National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) guidelines, a tumor of this size may prompt breast-directed neoadjuvant systemic therapy if regarded as MBC, whereas cSCC may be treated with immunotherapy in selected locally advanced or borderline resectable cases (nccn.org). In addition, identification of HER2 positivity in a subset of MBC with squamous differentiation may further alter systemic therapy.

This study has important limitations. The cohort is limited by the rarity of this diagnostic scenario, and the uncertain-origin cases, by definition, lack an independent gold standard for the site of origin. Sequencing was performed using multiple clinically validated targeted panels, which reflects real-world practice but limits direct comparison of quantitative metrics such as TMB across platforms. In addition, IHC, NGS, resection, and follow-up data were not available for all cases. Finally, the logistic-regression models are exploratory and intended as a structured way to assess the contribution of selected features, rather than as a stand-alone diagnostic tool.

In summary, our study demonstrates an integrated approach using morphology with selected immunohistochemical and molecular features to more confidently classify problematic squamous carcinomas involving the breast/chest. Although no single feature is definitive, combined assessment of CK7, SOX10, TRPS1, HER2, PIK3CA/PIK3R1 alterations, NOTCH pathway aberrations, TMB, and UV-associated mutation patterns provides a practical framework for distinguishing MBC with squamous differentiation from cutaneous SCC in this diagnostic context. Our results support a stepwise diagnostic approach in which even partial immunohistochemical or molecular data may meaningfully contribute to origin assignment when interpreted within the full clinicopathologic context.
