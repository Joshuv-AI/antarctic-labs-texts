Abstract
In this paper, we compute the Onsager-Machlup functional for distribution dependent SDEs driven by fractional Brownian motions with Hurst parameter H ∈ ( 1 4 , 1 ) H\in(\frac{1}{4},1) . In the case 1 4 < H < 1 2 \frac{1}{4}<H<\frac{1}{2} , the norm can be either the supremum norm or Hölder norms of order β \beta with 0 < β < H − 1 4 0<\beta<H-\frac{1}{4} . In the case 1 2 < H < 1 \frac{1}{2}<H<1 , the norms can be a Hölder norm of order β \beta with H − 1 2 < β < H − 1 4 H-\frac{1}{2}<\beta<H-\frac{1}{4} . As an example, we compute the Onsager-Machlup functional for the stochastic pendulum equation and derive its most probable path.



1
Introduction

Consider the following distribution-dependent stochastic differential equation in
\mathbb{R}
:

X_{t}=x+\int_{0}^{t}b(X_{s},\mathcal{L}_{X_{s}})\,ds+B^{H}_{t},

(1)

where
\mathcal{L}_{X_{s}}
denotes the law of
X_{s}
, and
B^{H}=\{B^{H}_{t},t\in[0,1]\}
is a fractional Brownian motion with Hurst parameter
H\in(0,1)
. When
H=\frac{1}{2}
, the process reduces to a standard Brownian motion. For
H>\frac{1}{2}
, the fractional Brownian motion belongs to the regular regime, characterized by smoother trajectories and positive correlation. In contrast, when
H<\frac{1}{2}
, it falls into the singular regime, exhibiting rougher paths with negative correlation and increased irregularity.

The purpose of this paper is to investigate the limiting behavior of the following ratio as

\varepsilon\to 0
:

\gamma_{\varepsilon}(\phi)=\frac{P(\|X-\phi\|\leq\varepsilon)}{P(\|B^{H}\|\leq\varepsilon)},

(2)

where
\phi
is a function such that
\phi-x
belongs to the Cameron-Martin space associtated with the fractional Brownian motion and
\|\cdot\|
is a suitable norm. When the limit exists and can be expressed as
\exp(J(\phi))
, the functional
J
is referred to as the Onsager-Machlup functional associated with equation (
1
) and the norm
\|\cdot\|
.

The Onsager-Machlup functional describes the most probable paths of diffusion processes and is analogous to the Lagrangian of a dynamical system, which is used to characterize the optimal evolution trajectory of a particle along a given path. A fundamental challenge is to understand how systems transition between states under stochastic perturbations. Given two states
x_{1}
and
x_{2}
, and
\phi
taken from an appropriate function space that satisfies the path from the initial state
x_{1}
to the final state
x_{2}
, the path that maximizes
\exp(J(\phi))
can be obtained using the variational method. This path is then the most probable path for the transition from state
x_{1}
to
x_{2}
. Therefore, the Onsager-Machlup functional is a deterministic quantity that characterizes the most probable transition path between the states of the system using the variational principle. It has significant applications in physics and chemistry (see
[
2
,
6
]
).

The Onsager-Machlup functional was first introduced by Onsager and Machlup
[
19
]
in 1953 and was subsequently solved by Ikeda and Watanabe
[
10
]
for
\phi\in C^{2}([0,1],\mathbb{R}^{d})
under the supremum norm. Shepp and Zeitouni
[
21
]
extended the result to the case where
\phi-x
belongs to the Cameron-Martin space.

In
[
3
]
, Capitaine further extended this result to a broader class of natural norms on the Wiener space, including Hölder, Sobolev, and Besov norms. Moret and Nualart
[
17
]
studied the Onsager-Machlup functional for stochastic differential equations driven by fractional Brownian motion. Maayan
[
15
]
further extended the fractional results to finite-dimensions.

Recent developments have demonstrated the expanding scope of this concept. Liu et al.
[
14
]
derived the Onsager-Machlup functional for McKean-Vlasov SDEs in a class of norms that dominate
L^{2}([0,1],\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Huang et al.
[
9
]
established a closed-form expression for the Onsager–Machlup functional for jump-diffusion processes with finite jump activity. Zhang and Li
[
24
]
investigated the Onsager-Machlup functional for stochastic differential equations with time-varying noise in the Hölder norm, where
0<\alpha<\frac{1}{4}
. Carfagnini and Wang
[
4
]
interpret Loewner energy as the Onsager-Machlup functional for
\text{SLE}_{\kappa}
loop measure for any fixed
\kappa\in(0,\frac{1}{4}]
.
Li and Li
[
12
]
proved that the
\Gamma
-limit of the OM functional on the space of curves is the geometric form of the FW functional in a proper time scale
T=T(\varepsilon)
as
\varepsilon\to 0
. Liu
[
13
]
studied the Onsager-Machlup action functional for degenerate stochastic differential equations driven by fractional Brownian motion.

The distribution dependent equation, also known as the McKean-Vlasov equation or mean-field SDE, was first introduced by Henry P. McKean in
[
16
]
. The McKean-Vlasov equation is fundamental for modeling mean-field interactions in large systems, with applications spanning physics, finance, control theory and machine learning. Galeati et al.
[
8
]
studied distribution dependent stochastic differential equations with irregular, possibly distributional drift, driven by an additive fractional Brownian motion.
Fan et al.
[
7
]
proved the well-posedness of distribution dependent stochastic differential equations driven by fractional Brownian motions, and then establish a general result on the Bismut formula for the Lions derivative by using Malliavin calculus.

This paper focuses on deriving the Onsager-Machlup functional for distribution dependent SDEs driven by fractional Brownian motions. The fractional-order distribution dependent SDE is a more general form of the standard SDE. Its advantage lies in its ability to capture memory effects and long-term dependencies within the system, as well as describe distribution-dependent randomness, thus enabling more accurate modeling of dynamic behaviors that depend on the system’s historical states in complex systems.

Compared to the standard Onsager-Machlup functional, the equation in this paper introduces a distribution dependent drift term and extends the noise from Brownian motion to the more general fractional Brownian motion. The first challenge lies in the Girsanov transformation. The distribution of a stochastic process depends on the probability measure on the probability space. Therefore, in the Girsanov transformation, we freeze the distributional coefficient of the drift term. Moreover, since the noise is fractional Brownian motion, the operator
(K^{H})^{-1}
, defined by the covariance kernel of fractional Brownian motion, involves fractional derivatives or fractional integrals due to this transformation, which requires significantly more intricate estimates.
The second challenge lies in computing the conditional expectation of the term

\exp\left[\int_{0}^{1}(K^{H})^{-1}\left(\int_{0}^{\cdot}b\left(B^{H}_{r}+\phi_{r},\mathcal{L}_{X_{r}}\right)dr\right)(s)dW_{s}\right]

under the condition
\|B^{H}\|\leq\varepsilon
. Although
\mathcal{L}_{X_{s}}
depends on
B^{H}
, the law
\mathcal{L}_{X_{s}}
remains unchanged under the small-noise constraint
\|B^{H}\|\leq\varepsilon
. To resolve this, we extend the approach in
[
17
]
by performing a Taylor expansion on
b\left(B^{H}_{s}+\phi_{s},\mathcal{L}_{X_{s}}\right)
solely with respect to the spatial variable. Notably, we avoid using the Itô formula framework in
[
14
]
due to the lack of a well-established extension of Malliavin calculus to distribution-dependent scenarios. Instead, by combining the fractional Girsanov transformation and Taylor expansion, we circumvent the need for Malliavin calculus altogether. Furthermore, by employing variational principles, we derive the Euler-Lagrange equation of the Onsager-Machlup functional.

The paper is structured as follows. In Section 2, we establish essential notations, formulate underlying assumptions, and review key preliminary results. In Section 3, we introduce a method for simplifying the Onsager-Machlup functional via the Girsanov transformation and present our main theorem. We subsequently provide rigorous proofs for both regular and singular cases. In Section 4, we extend the Onsager-Machlup functional to the finite-dimensional case. Finally, in Section 5, we derive the Euler-Lagrange equation for the Onsager-Machlup functional and verify our results through two specific examples.

2.1
Fractional calculus

We recall some definitions and results about fractional calculus .

Given
p\in[1,\infty]
and
[a,b]\subset\mathbb{R},
we denote by

L^{p}([a,b])=L^{p}([a,b],\mathbb{R})
the space of measurable functions
f:[a,b]\to\mathbb{R}
such that

\|f\|_{L^{p}([a,b])}=\left(\int_{a}^{b}|f(t)|^{p}dt\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}<\infty

with usual modification for
p=\infty.
For simplicity, we will use
\|\cdot\|_{\infty}
to denote
\|\cdot\|_{L^{\infty}}
.

The space
C^{\alpha}_{0}([0,T])
represents the set of functions in
C^{\alpha}([0,T])
that satisfy

f(0)=0
. Let
\|\cdot\|_{\alpha}
be the
Hölder norm of order
\alpha
on
C^{\alpha}_{0}([0,T])
defined by

\|f\|_{\alpha}=\sup_{0\leq s<t\leq T}\frac{|f(t)-f(s)|}{|t-s|^{\alpha}}.

The following definitions of fractional integrals and fractional derivatives are from
[
20
, Definition 2.1]
and
[
20
, Definition 2.2]
.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
f\in L^{1}([a,b])
. The integrals

\displaystyle(I_{a^{+}}^{\alpha}f)(x)

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{\Gamma(\alpha)}\int_{a}^{x}(x-y)^{\alpha-1}f(y)dy,\quad x\geq a,

\displaystyle(I_{b^{-}}^{\alpha}f)(x)

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{\Gamma(\alpha)}\int_{x}^{b}(y-x)^{\alpha-1}f(y)dy,\quad x\leq b,

where
\alpha>0
, are respectively called right and left fractional integral of the order
\alpha
.

For any
\alpha\geq 0,
any
f\in L^{p}([a,b])
and
g\in L^{q}([a,b])
where
1/p+1/q\leq\alpha,
we have:

\int_{a}^{b}f(s)(I_{a^{+}}^{\alpha}g)(s)ds=\int_{a}^{b}(I_{b^{-}}^{\alpha}f)(s)g(s)ds.

(3)

If
1\leq p<\infty
, we denote by
I_{a^{+}}^{\alpha}(L^{p})
the image of
L^{p}([a,b])
under the operator
I_{a^{+}}^{\alpha}
. Similarly,
I_{b^{-}}^{\alpha}(L^{p})
can be defined.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
f\in I_{a^{+}}^{\alpha}(L^{p})
,
g\in I_{b^{-}}^{\alpha}(L^{p})
.
Each of the expressions

\displaystyle(D_{a^{+}}^{\alpha}f)(x)

\displaystyle:=\left(\frac{d}{dx}\right)^{[\alpha]+1}I_{a^{+}}^{1+[\alpha]-\alpha}f(x),

\displaystyle(D_{b^{-}}^{\alpha}g)(x)

\displaystyle:=\left(-\frac{d}{dx}\right)^{[\alpha]+1}I_{b^{-}}^{1+[\alpha]-\alpha}g(x),

are respectively called the right and left fractional derivative.

From (
3
), we deduce the formula

\int_{a}^{b}f(s)(D_{a^{+}}^{\alpha}g)(s)ds=\int_{0}^{t}(D_{b^{-}}^{\alpha}f)(s)g(s)ds,\quad 0<\alpha<1,

(4)

which holds under the assumptions that
f\in I^{\alpha}_{b^{-}}(L^{p})
and
g\in I^{\alpha}_{a^{+}}(L^{q})
satisfy
1/p+1/q\leq 1+\alpha
.

If
f\in I_{a^{+}}^{\alpha}(L^{p})
, the function
\phi
such that
f=I_{a^{+}}^{\alpha}(\phi)
is unique in
L^{p}
. Fractional derivatives can be regarded as the inverse operation of fractional integrals.

When
\alpha p>1
, functions in
I_{a^{+}}^{\alpha}(L^{p})
are
\alpha-\frac{1}{p}
-Hölder continuous. Any Hölder continuous function of order
\beta>\alpha
has a fractional derivative of order
\alpha
, see
[
5
, Proposition 2.1]
.

Although fractional derivatives are defined as the derivatives of fractional integrals, they can also be directly expressed using the Weyl representation (see
[
20
, Remark 5.3]
):

D^{\alpha}_{0^{+}}f(x)=\frac{1}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}\left(\frac{f(x)}{x^{\alpha}}+\alpha\int_{0}^{x}\frac{f(x)-f(y)}{(x-y)^{\alpha+1}}dy\right),\quad 0<\alpha<1,

(5)

where the improper integral converges in the
L^{p}
sense. Therefore, if
f
has Hölder continuity with an exponent strictly greater than
\alpha
, then the fractional derivative of order
\alpha
exists.

Similar to the integral operator, the fractional integral operator is also a bounded linear operator on
L^{p}
spaces, see
[
20
, Theorem 2.6]
.

Theorem 2.3
.

For any
f\in L^{p}([a,b])
,
1\leq p<\infty
, one has

\|I_{a^{+}}^{\alpha}(f)\|_{L^{p}}\leq\frac{(b-a)^{\alpha}}{|\Gamma(\alpha)|}\|f\|_{L^{p}}.

(6)

2.2
Fractional Brownian motion

In this section, we will introduce the basic concepts and properties of fractional Brownian motion.

A real valued continuous process
\{B^{H}_{t},\ t\in[0,T]\}
is a fractional Brownian motion with Hurst parameter
H\in(0,1)
if it is a centered Gaussian process with covariance function

\mathbb{E}[B^{H}_{t}B^{H}_{s}]=\frac{1}{2}(|t|^{2H}+|s|^{2H}-|t-s|^{2H}).

If
H=\frac{1}{2}
, fractional Brownian motion corresponds to classical Brownian motion. But if
H\neq\frac{1}{2},
it is not a semimartingale nor a Markov process; its trajectories are
P-
a.s. in
C^{H-\varepsilon}_{0}([0,T])
for any
\varepsilon>0
.

Given a fractional Brownian motion
B^{H}
with Hurst parameter
H
on a probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},P)
, it is always possible to construct a standard Brownian motion
W
such that the following canonical representation holds. As shown in equation (42) in the work of Alòs et al.
[
1
]
, the fractional Brownian motion can be expressed through the integral representation:

B^{H}_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}K^{H}(t,s)\,dW_{s},

(7)

where
K^{H}(t,s)
is a square-integrable kernel given by:

K^{H}(t,s)=c_{H}(t-s)^{H-\frac{1}{2}}+c_{H}\left(\frac{1}{2}-H\right)\int_{s}^{t}(\theta-s)^{H-\frac{3}{2}}\left(1-\left(\frac{s}{\theta}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}-H}\right)d\theta,

(8)

with

c_{H}=\left(\frac{2H\Gamma\left(\frac{3}{2}-H\right)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{2}+H\right)\Gamma(2-2H)}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

When
H\geq\frac{1}{2},

K^{H}
can be express as

K^{H}(s,u)=c_{H}\alpha u^{-\alpha}\int_{u}^{s}(\theta-u)^{\alpha-1}\theta^{\alpha}d\theta.

(9)

For the purpose of the Girsanov transformation, we define the operator
K^{H}
corresponding to
K^{H}(t,s)
. The operator
K^{H}
, mapping from
L^{2}([0,1])
to
I_{0^{+}}^{H+\frac{1}{2}}(L^{2}([0,1]))
, is given by:

(K^{H}h)(t)=\int_{0}^{t}K^{H}(t,s)h(s)\,ds.

(10)

From
[
17
, Lemma 10]
, the operator
K^{H}
can be expressed using fractional integrals as follows:

(K^{H}h)(s)=\begin{cases}\displaystyle I^{1-2\alpha}_{0^{+}}\,s^{\alpha}I^{\alpha}_{0^{+}}\,s^{-\alpha}h,&H\leq 1/2,\\
\displaystyle I^{1}_{0^{+}}\,s^{\alpha}I^{\alpha}_{0^{+}}\,s^{-\alpha}h,&H\geq 1/2,\end{cases}

where
\alpha=|H-\frac{1}{2}|
and
h\in L^{2}([0,1])
. The inverse operator
(K^{H})^{-1}
is then defined as:

\displaystyle(K^{H})^{-1}h

\displaystyle=s^{\alpha}D^{\alpha}_{0^{+}}\,s^{-\alpha}D^{1-2\alpha}_{0^{+}}h,\quad H\leq 1/2,

\displaystyle(K^{H})^{-1}h

\displaystyle=s^{\alpha}D^{\alpha}_{0^{+}}\,s^{-\alpha}h^{\prime},\quad H\geq 1/2,

(11)

for all
h\in I^{H+\frac{1}{2}}_{0^{+}}(L^{2})
. If
h
is differentiable, the operator simplifies to:

(K^{H})^{-1}h=s^{-\alpha}I^{\alpha}_{0^{+}}\,s^{\alpha}h^{\prime},\quad H\leq 1/2.

(12)

Moreover, the operator
(K^{H})^{-1}
preserves the adaptability property.

2.3
Approximate limits in Wiener space

Let
W=\{W_{t},t\in[0,1]\}
be a Wiener process defined in the canonical probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},P)
, where

\Omega=\{f\in C([0,1],\mathbb{R}),f(0)=0\},

Let

CM=\{h\in\Omega,\text{ $h$ is absolutely continuous and $h^{\prime}$}\in L^{2}([0,1])\}.

Using the fact that the supremum norm and Hölder norm are measurable norms on the Cameron-Martin space (see
[
17
, Lemma 6]
), we derive the following two important theorems for the computation of the Onsager-Machlup functional.

The first theorem comes from
[
17

.

Theorem 2.4
.

If
\|\cdot\|
is the supremum norm or Hölder norm of order
\beta
with

0<\beta<H
, then

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\mathbb{E}\left(\exp\left(\int_{0}^{1}h(s)\,dW_{s}\right)\middle|\|B^{H}\|<\varepsilon\right)=1,\quad\forall h\in H.

(13)

Before presenting the next theorem, we first introduce the definition and properties of the trace.

Definition 2.5

(
[
17
]
)
.

An operator
K:L^{2}([0,1])\to L^{2}([0,1])
is nuclear if and only if

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}|\langle Ke_{n},g_{n}\rangle|<\infty,

for all
B=(e_{n})_{n}
,
\widetilde{B}=(g_{n})_{n}
orthonormal sequences in
L^{2}([0,1])
, where
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
denotes the inner product in
L^{2}
. We define the trace of
a nuclear operator

by

\operatorname{Tr}K=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\langle Ke_{n},e_{n}\rangle,

for any
B=(e_{n})_{n}
orthonormal sequence in
L^{2}([0,1])
.

The definition is independent of
the sequence we have chosen. Given a symmetric function
f\in L^{2}([0,1]^{2})
, the
Hilbert-Schmidt operator
K(f):L^{2}([0,1])\to L^{2}([0,1])
associated with
f
, defined by

(K(f))(h)(t)=\int_{0}^{t}f(t,u)h(u)\,du,

(14)

is nuclear if and only if
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}|\langle Ke_{n},e_{n}\rangle|<\infty
for all
B=(e_{n})_{n}
orthonormal sequences in

L^{2}([0,1])
. If
f
is continuous and the operator

is nuclear, the trace of

is defined as the trace of

, given by:

\operatorname{Tr}f=\operatorname{Tr}K(f)=\int_{0}^{1}f(s,s)\,ds.

The second theorem comes from

.

Distribution dependent SDEs

Theorem 2.10

(Girsanov transform for fractional Brownian motion)

\displaystyle e^{\delta}+e^{\delta}\exp\left(-\frac{\delta^{2}}{2k^{\prime}\varepsilon^{4}}\right)\exp(K_{2}\varepsilon^{\frac{-1}{H-\beta}})+\int_{\delta}^{+\infty}e^{\xi}\exp\left(-\frac{\xi^{2}}{2k^{\prime}\varepsilon^{4}}\right)\exp(K_{2}\varepsilon^{\frac{-1}{H-\beta}})d\xi

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle e^{\delta}+e^{\delta}\exp\left(-\frac{\delta^{2}}{2k^{\prime}\varepsilon^{4}}\right)\exp(K_{2}\varepsilon^{\frac{-1}{H-\beta}})+\exp\left(\frac{k^{\prime}\varepsilon^{4}}{2}+K_{2}\varepsilon^{-\frac{1}{H-\beta}}\right)\int_{\frac{\delta}{\sqrt{2k^{\prime}}\varepsilon^{2}}-\frac{\sqrt{2k^{\prime}}\varepsilon^{2}}{2}}^{+\infty}e^{-x^{2}}dx

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle e^{\delta}+e^{\delta}\exp\left(-\frac{\delta^{2}}{2k^{\prime}\varepsilon^{4}}\right)\exp(K_{2}\varepsilon^{\frac{-1}{H-\beta}})

\displaystyle+\sqrt{2k^{\prime}}\frac{\pi}{8}\exp\left(\frac{k^{\prime}\varepsilon^{4}}{2}+K_{2}\varepsilon^{-\frac{1}{H-\beta}}-\left({\frac{\delta}{\sqrt{2k^{\prime}}\varepsilon^{2}}-\frac{\sqrt{2k^{\prime}}\varepsilon^{2}}{2}}\right)^{2}\right)\varepsilon^{2},

when
H-\frac{1}{2}<\beta<H-\frac{1}{4}
.
Thus, when
H-\frac{1}{2}<\beta<H-\frac{1}{4}
, we have

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\mathbb{E}(\exp(cC_{3})\mid\|B^{H}\|_{\beta}\leq\varepsilon)\leq e^{\delta}.

By the arbitrariness of
\delta
, it can be concluded that

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\mathbb{E}(\exp(cC_{3})\mid\|B^{H}\|_{\beta}\leq\varepsilon)\leq 1,\quad\forall c\in\mathbb{R}.

Hence

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\mathbb{E}(\exp(A_{1})\mid\|B^{H}\|_{\beta}\leq\varepsilon)=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\mathbb{E}(\exp(C_{1}+C_{2}+C_{3})\mid\|B^{H}\|_{\beta}\leq\varepsilon)=1.

(52)

In summary, based on (
52
), (
35
), (
44
), and (
42
), and by further applying Theorem
2.9
, we can derive our result:

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{P(\|X-\phi\|_{\beta}\leq\varepsilon)}{P(\|B^{H}\|_{\beta}\leq\varepsilon)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\mathbb{E}\Big(\exp(A_{1}+A_{2}+A_{3}+A_{4})I_{\|B^{H}\|_{\beta}\leq\varepsilon}\Big)

\displaystyle\quad\cdot\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1}\big(\dot{\phi}_{s}-s^{\alpha}D_{0^{+}}^{\alpha}s^{-\alpha}b(\phi_{s},\mathcal{L}_{X_{s}})\big)^{2}ds\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1}\left(\dot{\phi}_{s}-s^{\alpha}D_{0^{+}}^{\alpha}s^{-\alpha}b(\phi_{s},\mathcal{L}_{X_{s}})\right)^{2}+d_{H}b_{x}(\phi_{s},\mathcal{L}_{X_{s}})ds\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\exp\left(J(\phi,\dot{\phi})\right).

Finally, we obtain

J(\phi,\dot{\phi})=-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1}(\dot{\phi}_{s}-s^{\alpha}D_{0^{+}}^{\alpha}s^{-\alpha}b(\phi_{s},\mathcal{L}_{X_{s}}))^{2}+d_{H}b_{x}(\phi_{s},\mathcal{L}_{X_{s}})ds,

where

∎

x_{0}
to
x_{1}

We can obtain the most probable path using the Euler-Lagrange equation.

When
