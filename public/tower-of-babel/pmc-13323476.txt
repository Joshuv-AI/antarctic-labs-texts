NAT10 drives ovarian cancer progression via NLRP3 mRNA ac4C modification
Authors: Yu Liu, Yu Yang, Chun-Chun Wang, Wen-Ying Zhang, Lei Cao, Cheng-Bin Ma, Ping Liu
Published: 2026

Introduction

Globally, ovarian cancer (OV) ranks as the eighth leading cause of cancer-related death among women [1]. It is distinguished by complex histology, aggressive behavior, and poor long-term survival. Although the disease comprises several distinct molecular subtypes, they are frequently treated as a single clinical entity. Extensive efforts have sought to classify these subtypes, delineate oncogenic pathways, and identify actionable biomarkers for precision therapy [2]; nevertheless, the fundamental mechanisms driving ovarian cancer initiation and progression remain incompletely understood.

N-acetyltransferase 10(NAT10) is the sole known enzyme that installs N4-acetylcytidine (ac4C) onto RNA, catalyzing the acetylation of the N4 position of cytidine within tRNAs, rRNAs, and mRNAs [3]. By acting as an RNA acetyltransferase, NAT10 fine-tunes gene expression through modulation of RNA transcription and stability [4–7]. Beyond its role in stabilizing mRNA, ac4C modification has been shown to influence translation efficiency, splicing, and even RNA degradation pathways [8]. Emerging evidence has linked NAT10 to the progression of several malignancies, including cervical, clear-cell renal cell, and gastric cancers [9–12]; however, its exact role in ovarian cancer remains to be fully elucidated.

The chemical modification known as N4-acetylcytidine (ac4C) is a conserved feature in tRNA [13, 14]. Research has shown that ac4C is prevalent at the wobble positions of mRNA, contributing to mRNA stability and enhancing translation efficiency [15]. Additionally, ac4C has been implicated in modulating alternative splicing events, which can generate protein isoforms with distinct functional properties [16]. This post-transcriptional modification can also affect RNA degradation pathways, thereby influencing cellular responses to stress and other stimuli [14]. The capacity of NAT10 to regulate the progression of OV through ac4C modification is not well established. This study aims to investigate how NAT10 -mediated ac4C modification influences the aggressive behavior of ovarian cancer cells.

The NLRP3 inflammasome, a central component of innate immunity, is dysregulated in numerous human diseases [17]. Emerging data highlight NLRP3 as a key mediator of inflammation-associated tissue damage and tumorigenesis [18, 19]. Whether NAT10 modulates NLRP3 expression through ac4C modification to impact OC progression has not been investigated. Here, we address this knowledge gap and delineate the mechanistic role of the NAT10–ac4C–NLRP3 axis in OV cell proliferation and metastasis.

Materials and methods

Study participants and tissue collection

Participants for this study were recruited from Changning Maternity and Infant Health Hospital, Shanghai. A collection of twenty specimens was procured, consisting of both human ovarian cancerous tissue and adjacent non-cancerous tissue samples. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki principles and was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Changning Maternity and Infant Health Hospital [CNFBLLKT-2018110604].

Cell culture

Human ovarian cancer cell lines (TOV-112D and ES-2) alongside normal ovarian surface epithelial cells (HOSEpiC) were acquired from the Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China). Cell cultures were maintained in a 37 °C humidified incubator with 5% CO2. Cultivation utilized DMEM medium (HyClone, South Logan, UT, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Invitrogen, CA, USA).

Cell counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay

Cell viability was determined using the CCK-8 assay. Cells were seeded in 96-well plates at 3 × 103 cells per well, incubated overnight, and then treated as indicated. At the designated time points (0, 24, 48, and 96 h), 10 µL of CCK-8 reagent was added to each well, followed by a 2.5-h incubation at 37 °C. Absorbance at 450 nm was measured on a microplate reader; all values were normalized to the 0-h baseline.

Transwell assays

The Transwell assay was utilized to assess cellular migration and invasion abilities. For the invasion analysis, Matrigel (Sigma) was applied to pre-coat the chambers. Then, cells were inoculated into the upper chamber and subjected to incubation at 37 °C for 48 h. Following incubation, cells that had migrated or invaded were fixed and stained using a 0.1% crystal violet solution. The stained cells were then visualized under a microscope, and the number of cells in three randomly selected fields was quantified (200 × magnification).

RT-qPCR analysis

Total RNA was obtained with TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc.). Reverse transcription to cDNA followed, using the PrimeScript RT kit (Takara, Dalian, China). RT-qPCR amplification began with a 2-min denaturation at 95 °C, then 28 cycles of 30-s denaturation at 95 °C and 60-s annealing/extension at 60 °C. β-actin was the internal control. Relative gene expression was determined by the 2−ΔΔCt method.The primer sequences used for qRT-PCR in the supplementary Table S1.

Western blot

PAGE, and transferred to a PVDF membrane (Millipore). The membrane was blocked with 5% non-fat milk in Tris-buffered saline containing 0.1% Tween-20 (TBST) for 1 h at room temperature, followed by overnight incubation at 4 °C with primary antibodies. The primary antibodies used were as follows: NAT10 (1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology), N-cadherin (1:1000, Thermo Fisher Scientific, catalog number 33-3900), Vimentin (1:1000, Abcam, catalog number ab92547), MMP2 (1:1000, Thermo Fisher Scientific, catalog number MA5-46957), MMP9 (1:1000, MyBioSource, catalog number MBS8122822), and GAPDH (1:2000, Cell Signaling Technology, #5174) as an internal loading control. After washing in TBST, the membrane was incubated with the appropriate HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (1:5000, Cell Signaling Technology) for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) system (Millipore) and quantified by ImageJ software.

acRIP-qPCR and IP-qPCR

RIP experiments were conducted with an RNA Immunoprecipitation Kit (Geneseed). Cells were rinsed twice in PBS and centrifuged at 1000 × g for 5 min. Lysis was done by adding 1 mL of RIPA buffer and incubating on ice for 10 min. Antibodies (anti-ac4C, anti-NAT10, anti-IgG) were mixed with protein A/G beads and incubated at 4 °C for 2 h. Then, 450 μL of lysis buffer was added to the beads and incubated for another 2 h at the same temperature. After washing, RNA was extracted from the mixture and analyzed by RT-qPCR.

Luciferase reporter assay

We cloned the wild-type (WT) and ac4C-mutant (MUT) 3′UTR fragments of NLRP3 into the pmirGLO dual-luciferase reporter (Promega). OV cells transfected with these constructs were subjected to NAT10 knockdown, after which Firefly luciferase activity was measured and normalized to the Renilla internal control.

Data analysis

The results derived from three separate experiments were depicted as mean ± SD. For statistical assessment, SPSS software (SPSS, Inc.) was employed. The significance was ascertained by employing one-way ANOVA or Student's t-test, where P < 0.05 was deemed indicative of statistical significance.

Results

NAT10 expression and ac4C levels are elevated in OV

The expression levels of NAT10 transcripts were initially assessed in 18 pairs of OV tissues and their corresponding non-tumorous adjacent tissues via RT-qPCR. A significant upregulation of NAT10 expression was observed in the tumor specimens compared to their non-tumorous counterparts (Fig. 1A), and ROC-curve analysis yielded an AUC of 0.865, indicating robust discriminatory power between malignant and normal tissue (Fig. 1B). Consistently, Western blotting of a representative pair confirmed marked up-regulation of NAT10 protein in OV (Fig. 1C). NAT10 mRNA was also significantly higher in two commonly used OV cell lines relative to non-transformed controls (Fig. 1D). Collectively, these data indicate that both NAT10 and global ac4C modification are substantially increased in OV.

NAT10 Knockdown reduces the proliferation, migration, and invasion of OV cells

Prompted by the marked up-regulation of NAT10 in both primary ovarian-cancer tissues and established cell lines, we stably knocked down the enzyme using short-hairpin RNAs (sh-NAT10). A non-targeting shRNA (sh-NC) served as a negative control. Efficient knockdown (> 80% reduction at the mRNA level) was confirmed by RT-qPCR (Fig. 2A). Functional interrogation revealed broad tumour-suppressive consequences. CCK-8 assays showed a time-dependent decline in viability of both TOV-112D and ES-2 cells (Fig. 2B, C), with maximal inhibition (> 55%) evident at 96 h. Transwell assays provided further mechanistic evidence that NAT10 depletion impaired both basal migration and Matrigel invasion, as the number of cells traversing the membrane per 200 × microscopic field decreased by approximately 70% relative to sh-NC controls (Fig. 2D, G). Collectively, these data establish NAT10 as a potent driver of proliferative and metastatic phenotypes in ovarian carcinoma cells.

NAT10-dependent ac4C acetylation enhances NLRP3 mRNA stability

NLRP3 is implicated in a wide range of infectious diseases and in tumorigenesis, where it influences both chemotherapeutic efficacy and clinical prognosis. Here we investigated whether NAT10 governs NLRP3 expression via N4-acetylcytidine (ac4C) modification. First, NAT10 knockdown markedly reduced NLRP3 mRNA abundance (Fig. 3A). acRIP-qPCR subsequently revealed a pronounced loss of ac4C on NLRP3 transcripts upon shRNA-mediated depletion of NAT10 (Fig. 3B, C). acRIP-qPCR confirmed a direct interaction between NAT10 and NLRP3 mRNA (Fig. 3D, E). To map the regulatory site, we cloned the wild-type (WT) NLRP3 3′UTR and a mutant (MUT) version in which candidate ac4C residues were deleted into luciferase reporters. Silencing NAT10 in two OV lines markedly attenuated luciferase activity driven by the WT 3′UTR, whereas the MUT reporter remained unaltered (Fig. 3F, G). Finally, actinomycin D chase assays demonstrated that NAT10 depletion shortened the half-life of NLRP3 mRNA (Fig. 3H, I). Collectively, our data establish that NAT10 stabilizes NLRP3 mRNA through site-specific ac4C modification of its 3′UTR.

NAT10 promotes the malignant processes of OV cells through NLRP3

To determine whether NAT10 exerts its oncogenic effects through NLRP3, we performed functional rescue experiments. We first established four experimental groups in two OV cell lines (TOV-112D and ES-2): control (sh-NC), NAT10 knockdown (sh-NAT10), NAT10 knockdown with empty vector (sh-NAT10 + vector), and NAT10 knockdown with NLRP3 overexpression (sh-NAT10 + NLRP3). RT-qPCR confirmed successful overexpression of NLRP3 in the rescue group (Fig. 4A).CCK-8 assays showed that NAT10 depletion significantly reduced cell proliferation, while enforced NLRP3 expression fully restored the proliferative capacity to levels comparable to the control group; the empty vector group showed no such recovery (Fig. 4B, C). Similarly, Transwell migration and invasion assays demonstrated that NLRP3 overexpression rescued the impaired migratory and invasive phenotypes caused by NAT10 silencing, as quantified by a significant increase in the number of cells that migrated or invaded per 200 × microscopic field (Fig. 4D, G).To further validate these functional changes at the molecular level, we examined the expression of key migratory and invasive markers (N-cadherin, Vimentin, MMP2, MMP9) via Western blot. Results showed that NAT10 knockdown led to significant downregulation of these markers, which was not reversed by empty vector transfection. In contrast, NLRP3 overexpression restored their protein levels to near-control levels (Fig. 4H), with quantitative analysis confirming these trends (Fig. 4I).

Collectively, these data demonstrate that NAT10 drives OV cell proliferation, migration, and invasion in an NLRP3-dependent manner, with both functional assays and molecular marker expression validating the rescue effect of NLRP3 over-expression.

Discussion

OV remains one of the most formidable gynecologic malignancies: it ranks as the seventh most frequently diagnosed cancer and the eighth leading cause of cancer-related death among women worldwide [1]. Its insidious onset and lack of early symptoms result in detection at advanced stages, translating into high morbidity and mortality [20]. Consequently, a deeper understanding of the molecular circuitry that drives OV progression is imperative for the identification of innovative therapeutic targets.

NAT10 is a multifunctional enzyme that plays a crucial role in normal cellular physiology by catalyzing the acetylation of cytidine at the N4 position (ac4C) in various RNA molecules, including tRNAs, rRNAs, and mRNAs. This post-transcriptional modification is essential for maintaining RNA stability, regulating gene expression, and modulating cellular processes such as proliferation, differentiation, and stress response [3–5, 21]. For instance, NAT10-mediated ac4C modification has been shown to enhance the stability and translation efficiency of specific mRNAs, thereby influencing cellular processes like cell cycle progression and metabolic regulation [6, 7].

In the context of cancer, NAT10 has emerged as a significant oncogenic factor across multiple tumor types. Elevated expression of NAT10 has been reported in various cancers, including cervical cancer, clear-cell renal cell carcinoma, and gastric cancer, where it contributes to tumor progression, metastasis, and chemoresistance [22, 23]. The oncogenic role of NAT10 is primarily attributed to its ability to stabilize specific mRNAs through ac4C modification, thereby promoting the expression of genes involved in cell proliferation, migration, and invasion [24]. NAT10-catalyzed ac4C has been shown to accelerate bladder cancer progression and to confer chemotherapeutic resistance in bladder cancer cells [23, 25]. Similarly, NAT10 promotes gastric cancer metastasis via ac4C-dependent stabilization of COL5A1 mRNA [26]. In OV, NAT10 has already been proposed as a prognostic biomarker [27]. However, the precise molecular mechanisms through which NAT10 fosters OV progression remain incompletely understood. The present study was designed to fill this knowledge gap.

To interrogate the oncogenic role of NAT10 in OV, we stably silenced its expression with shRNA (sh-NAT10) and employed two independent OV cell lines for loss-of-function assays. CCK-8 proliferation assays demonstrated a pronounced reduction in cell growth upon NAT10 depletion, and transwell migration and invasion assays revealed a corresponding impairment in cellular motility. Collectively, these data establish NAT10 as a critical driver of OV proliferation, migration, and invasion.

The NLRP3 inflammasome is a key component of the innate immune system, and its dysregulation has been implicated in various cancers [28]. The activation of NLRP3 can lead to the maturation and secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β and IL-18, which can promote tumor progression through the induction of inflammation and immune evasion [18]. In addition to its canonical role in inflammasome activation, NLRP3 has also been shown to have inflammasome-independent effects on cancer cells [29]. For instance, NLRP3 can interact with various signaling pathways, including the NF-κB pathway, which is known to regulate cell proliferation, survival, and inflammation [30]. Moreover, NLRP3 has been implicated in the epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), a process that is critical for cancer metastasis [31]. The EMT program involves the loss of epithelial markers (e.g., E-cadherin) and the gain of mesenchymal markers (e.g., N-cadherin, vimentin), which enhances the migratory and invasive capabilities of cancer cells. Given the central role of NLRP3 in these processes, it is plausible that the NAT10-mediated stabilization of NLRP3 mRNA could contribute to OV malignancy through the activation of downstream signaling pathways involved in inflammation, immune evasion, and EMT.

We first observed that NAT10 knockdown markedly reduced NLRP3 mRNA abundance. Subsequently, acRIP-qPCR and NAT10 RIP assays demonstrated that NAT10 binds directly to NLRP3 mRNA and installs ac4C modifications. Luciferase reporter assays revealed that this interaction occurs within the ac4C-bearing regions of the NLRP3 3′UTR. Consistent with the known stabilizing effect of ac4C on mRNA, actinomycin D chase assays confirmed that NAT10 depletion shortened the half-life of NLRP3 transcripts. Thus, NAT10 stabilizes NLRP3 mRNA via ac4C modification.

To determine whether NAT10-driven oncogenic phenotypes are NLRP3-dependent, we performed functional rescue experiments. Re-expression of NLRP3 fully restored proliferation, migration, and invasion in NAT10-silenced OV cells. Furthermore, Western blot analysis of migratory and invasive markers (N-cadherin, Vimentin, MMP2, MMP9) showed that their expression was significantly downregulated in NAT10-knockdown cells, which was not reversed by empty vector transfection. In contrast, NLRP3 overexpression restored the protein levels of these markers to near-control levels. These data collectively establish the NAT10-NLRP3 axis as a key regulator of OV aggressiveness and nominate ac4C-dependent stabilization of NLRP3 as a tractable therapeutic vulnerability.

Limitations and future directions

Our study provides evidence that NLRP3 overexpression rescues the oncogenic phenotypes associated with NAT10 depletion. However, we acknowledge that our current data do not definitively confirm whether NLRP3 acts through canonical inflammasome activation or through other tumor-related pathways. Future studies should focus on elucidating the specific mechanisms through which NLRP3 contributes to OV progression, including the potential involvement of inflammasome-independent pathways and interactions with key signaling molecules such as NF-κB. Additionally, further investigation into the downstream effects of NLRP3 stabilization, such as changes in cytokine secretion and immune cell infiltration, will be essential for understanding the full scope of its oncogenic role.

Conclusion

Our study identifies NAT10 as a recurrently up-regulated RNA acetyltransferase in OV specimens and implicates its ac4C-dependent stabilization of NLRP3 mRNA as a driver of malignant progression. In vitro, NAT10 depletion consistently curtailed proliferation, migration, and invasion across multiple OV cell lines; however, these observations remain to be validated in genetically engineered mouse models and in large, histologically annotated patient cohorts. The precise cis-elements within the NLRP3 3’UTR that recruit NAT10, the kinetics of ac4C turnover, and the downstream inflammatory or immune-evasive programs elicited by stabilized NLRP3 transcripts also require mechanistic dissection. Finally, prospective clinical studies with long-term follow-up are needed to determine whether combined NAT10/NLRP3 expression profiles can refine risk stratification or predict therapeutic response in women with ovarian cancer.
