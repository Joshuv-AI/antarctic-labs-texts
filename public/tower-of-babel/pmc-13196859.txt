Causal Links Between Sleep Duration and Sarcopenia-Related Phenotypes
Authors: Haibin Shang, Zhen Deng, Youzhi Lian, Youli Ai, Jie Zhang, Guozhong Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Emerging observational research reported that sleep duration was linked to sarcopenia, yet these associations have not been ascertained causally.Method:This study included data from the 2015–2018 National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey and applied multivariable logistic regression to evaluate the relationship between sleep duration and sarcopenia. A two-sample Mendelian randomization study was subsequently performed using genome-wide association study summary statistics to determine the causal relationships between sleep duration, appendicular lean mass, grip. Sensitivity analysis was performed to confirm the robustness of the results. Finally, summary data–based Mendelian randomization is used to explore their shared risk genes.Results:A total of 5243 participants were included in this study, and multivariable logistic regression analysis showed an inverse association between sleep duration and sarcopenia. Mendelian randomization analysis also supported long sleep duration are negatively correlated with appendicular lean mass, and sleep duration is negatively correlated with grip. In addition, reverse Mendelian randomization showed appendicular lean mass was negatively correlated with long sleep time. Summary data–based Mendelian randomization analysis further explored shared risk genes for each causal trait pair, and these genes are involved in biological processes.Conclusions:By integrating genetic, pharmacological, and pathological data, we revealed a potential link between sleep duration and muscular dystrophy. These results provide new ideas for potential treatments and potential guidance for clinical practice.

What Is New

Combining NHANES and Mendelian randomization analyses, this study establishes a causal inverse relationship: longer sleep duration reduces sarcopenia risk, supported by negative associations with ALM and grip strength. Bidirectional MR reveals ALM inversely affects sleep duration. Shared risk genes identified via SMR implicate neuromuscular pathways. These findings provide causal insights, position sleep as a modifiable preventive factor for sarcopenia, and propose genetic targets for therapy, directly informing clinical strategies for muscle health maintenance.

Sarcopenia is the age-related loss of mass and muscle strength and/or body function that can damage joints, cause fractures, affect organ function, and ultimately lead to death.1 Some studies have shown that sarcopenia plays a prominent role in dysfunction and disability and affects the quality of life, thereby increasing morbidity and mortality from bone and joint diseases.2 It is conservatively estimated that more than 50 million people currently suffer from clinically diagnosed osteomyelitis, and this number is expected to exceed 200 million in the next 40 yrs.3 People with sarcopenia suffer from poor health and disability, which has significant personal, social, and economic consequences. Therefore, there is an urgent need to identify modifiable risk factors for sarcopenia to develop effective preventive measures.

Among a range of putative modifiable risk factors, sleep duration has emerged as the focus of attention. Multiple studies reveal links between sleep duration, body composition, and the quality, strength, and function of skeletal muscle, which is important for an individual's vitality and health.4,5 Sleep deprivation increases cortisol levels and accelerates muscle catabolism, which can lead to muscle loss. Excessive sleep duration may also be associated with sarcopenia as it adversely affects physical activity patterns and overall health.6 Despite the prospective significance of sleep duration as a risk factor for plasticity in the development of sarcopenia, most of the evidence from epidemiologic studies may be hampered by confounding factors.

Mendelian randomization (MR), a technique that uses genetic variation as a proxy for exposures of interest, is relatively unaffected by common behavioral, physiological, and socioeconomic confounders minimizes reverse causation, and is widely used in genetic research.7,8 In the current study, we explore the association between sleep duration and sarcopenia using a data set generated by the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES). In addition, bidirectional MR was used to assess the causal nature of these associations, and summary data–based Mendelian randomization (SMR) was used to explore their shared risk genes to explore their underlying biological mechanisms and functions.

METHODS

Data Source and Study Population

The NHANES is a cross-sectional survey that provides extensive information on the nutrition and health of the noninstitutionalized United States population. The National Center approved the NHANES protocol for the Health Statistics Ethical Review Board, and all participants consented to use their information for research. In this study, we conducted analyses using publicly available data from two cycles (2015–2016 and 2017–2018), including a total of 19,225 participants.

Assessment and Definition of Sarcopenia and Sleep Duration

Dual-energy x-ray (DXA) was used to assess limb skeletal muscle mass (ASM). Body mass index (BMI, kg/m2) is defined as weight divided by height squared. According to the Foundation for the National Institutes of Health criteria, ASM/BMI was used to determine whether patients had sarcopenia. Because of physiological differences between men and women, the cutoff points are ≤0.789 for men and ≤0.512 for women, a criterion widely used in recent studies.9,10

Sleep duration was determined using the following questions: (a) “What time do you usually fall asleep on a weekday or weekdays?” and (b) “What time do you usually wake up on a weekday or weekdays?” Sleep duration was calculated as the time between the usual sleep time and the usual waking time, approximated to the nearest half hour.

Other Covariates

Based on previous literature,11,12 we incorporated clinically meaningful covariates in our study including age at interview (16–55), gender, ethnicity, BMI, education level, questionnaire data involving smoking, drinking, cancer, coronary heart disease, cardiovascular disease and diabetes, laboratory data that covered high-density lipoprotein cholesterol, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol, total cholesterol, and triglyceride. Participants were considered smokers if they had smoked ≥100 cigarettes in their lifetime, and smoking was categorized based on self-reported: smoking status (never, former, now); self-reported frequency of alcohol intake was also classified as former, heavy, mild, moderate and never.13

This study conforms to all STROBE guidelines and reports the required information accordingly.

Statistical Analyses

In cross-sectional statistical analyses, baseline characteristics of all participants were described by means (continuous variables; expressed as mean ± SD) or proportions (categorical variables; expressed as n [%]). To explore the relationship between sarcopenia and sleep duration, we established four models based on logistic regression to adjust for potential confounding factors. Model 1 was a crude model of sleep duration and sarcopenia without any factor adjustment and Model 2 includes age, gender, race, and other demographic variables as well as sleep duration. Model 3 included all variables of Model 2 plus BMI, education, and alcohol consumption, and Model 4 further added disease factors such as cardiovascular disease and diabetes. All models were conducted to stratify discussions on gender to examine the robustness of the association. P < 0.05 was considered to be statistically different. R software (version 4.1.1) was used for all analyses.

Genome-Wide Association Studies Sources

According to the consensus of the European Working Group on Sarcopenia in Older People, muscle mass and hand grip strength were used as criteria for diagnosing sarcopenia. Genome-wide association studies (GWAS) data for appendicular lean mass (ALM) were obtained from the UK Biobank study that was conducted to quantify ALM-related values by summing fat-free mass, and adjusted for age, sex, the 10 most important principal components, and other covariates involving 450,243 participants.14 We obtained GWAS summary data on low handgrip strength from a meta-analysis of 256,523 Europeans aged ≥60 yrs in 22 cohorts.15 Additionally, impaired physical performance, often operationalized as reduced walking speed, is a clinically recognized early warning signal and valuable indicator for assessing the risk of sarcopenia.16 We collected GWAS summary data on the usual walking pace of 459,915 individuals from UK Biobank. Additionally, genetic predictors for sleep duration (both long and short sleep duration) were extracted from the most up-to-date GWAS.17 Details of each GWAS dataset are provided in Table S1 (http://links.lww.com/PHM/C885).

Linkage Disequilibrium Score Regression

To explore genetic correlates of sleep duration and sarcopenia, we used linkage disequilibrium score regression (LDSC)18 to assess genome-wide genetic correlations for nine pairwise traits between three sleep duration (long/short sleep duration) and sarcopenia (ALM, grip and walk pace). We performed LDSC analysis based on linkage disequilibrium scores from European ancestry from the 1000 Genomes Project.19 P FDR < 0.05 was defined as a genetic link between two traits.

Bidirectional MR Analysis

Three assumptions that a MR study should satisfy: (1): instrumental variable is associated with exposure factor (2), instrumental variable is independent of confounder, and (3) instrumental variable contributes to outcome through exposure factors only. To construct genetic instrumental variables for exposure factors, reliable SNPs (single nucleotide polymorphisms) were obtained and trimmed to remove the effect of linkage disequilibrium (LD) (P < 5 × 10–8, r 2 < 0.001, kb = 10,000).20 We chose not to use SNP proxies, coordinated the effect alleles in the exposure and outcome datasets, and excluded all SNPs with palindromes. To obtain reliable estimates, the inverse variance weighted model, MR-Egger regression model, weighted median estimators, weighted mode, and simple mode were used to explore a causality, followed by testing for imbalance horizontal pleiotropic effects, and inverse variance weighted was considered as the main analysis method.21 It means that there is no horizontal pleiotropy when the value of the intercept term is close to zero and the P value is greater than 0.05.22 In addition, heterogeneity effects between genetic instrumental variables were also identified based on the inverse variance weighted method. Similar analytical procedures were repeatedly applied, exchanging exposure and outcome factors to enable bidirectional MR studies to assess reverse causality.

Summary Data–Based MR

SMR was originally developed to implement SMR and heterogeneity in dependent instruments (HEIDI) methods to test pleiotropic associations between gene expression levels and complex traits of interest using integrated data from GWAS and expression quantitative trait loci (eQTL) studies.23 We used the 1000 Genomes European reference data for LD calculations.19 For sarcopenia-related traits, we chose the eQTL data on muscle-skeletal from Genotype-Tissue Expression V8, and brain cortex samples from BrainMeta for sleep duration.24 We used P_SMR < 0.05 and heterogeneity HEIDI >0.01 as outcome inclusion criteria. This manuscript adheres to the STROBE-MR guidelines.

Shared Risk Genes and Enrichment Analysis

We intersect risk genes for two traits to obtain shared genes. Using gene sets derived from Gene Ontology and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes, gene set enrichment analysis was performed on shared risk genes to identify potential biological pathways based on the Molecular Signatures Database and the clusterProfiler software package. Significantly enriched pathways were declared adjusted P < 0.05.

Identification of Actionable Drugs for Risk Genes

We searched the DrugBank database to evaluate the drug development for the identified druggable genes, including obtaining drug molecule types, indications for drug targets, and clinical development activities.

Association Between Sleep Duration and Sarcopenia

Model 1, the crude model without any correction for factors, showed that sleep duration may reduce the risk of sarcopenia (odds ratio [OR] = 0.93, 95% confidence interval [CI] = 0.88–0.99). Models 2, 3, and 4 were further corrected for various confounders, respectively, and we found that sleep duration still reduced the risk of sarcopenia, and to a greater extent than uncorrected Model 1 (OR = 0.88, 95% CI = 0.82–0.95; OR = 0.84, 95% CI = 0.82–0.95; OR = 0.84, 95% CI = 0.74–0.96). After stratifying by gender, we observed the same results in females, whereas in male subjects, this association was exhaustively identified in Model 2 and Model 3 (Table 2).

Genetic Correlates of Sleep Duration and Sarcopenia

LDSC identified extensive genetic associations among six trait pairs (P adj < 0.05). In particular, there are genetic associations between short sleep duration and ALM, grip and walk, long sleep duration and walk and ALM, as well as sleep duration and grip (Table S2, http://links.lww.com/PHM/C885). These findings provided further genetic evidence for the association between sleep duration and sarcopenia.

Bidirectional MR Analysis

We initially explored the causal relationship between sleep duration and sarcopenia-related traits, and the inverse variance weighted results showed that sleep duration and long sleep duration were negatively correlated with ALM (b = −0.11, b = −1.35, respectively) as shown in Figure 1. Additionally, we found sleep duration was suggestively negatively correlated with grip (b = −0.14, P = 0.09). Horizontal pleiotropy (P > 0.05) was not found for all of the above causal relationships. Subsequently, we explored the causal relationship between sarcopenia-related traits and sleep duration in another direction. The inverse variance weighted surprisingly demonstrated that ALM was negatively correlated with long sleep duration (b = −0.01, P = 1.06E-7). Specifically, walk pace showed negative causality with both long sleep duration and short sleep duration (b = −0.08, b = −0.13, respectively), which may suggest a “U” association with them. The results of the MR analyses based on other methods are shown in detail in Table S3 (http://links.lww.com/PHM/C885).

The IVW results of the causal relationship between sleep duration and sarcopenia-related traits.

Potential Shared Risk Genes Between Sleep Duration and Sarcopenia

Based on causal effect trait pairs, we performed SMR analysis using muscle-skeletal and brain cortex eQTL data to identify potential risk genes. For sarcopenia-related traits, ALM, grip, and walk pace were identified 1626, 544, and 915 genes by SMR based on the muscle-skeletal eQTL dataset (Table S4–S6, http://links.lww.com/PHM/C885), respectively. By using brain cortex eQTL data, SMR identified 1910, 1243, and 1641 risk genes in sleep duration, long sleep duration, and short sleep duration, respectively (Table S7–S9, http://links.lww.com/PHM/C885). Intersecting risk genes with causal effect trait pairs, 122 shared risk genes were obtained in sleep duration-ALM and 80 in long sleep duration–ALM. Further shared risk genes for walk pace and sleep duration were obtained, 69 and 88 genes in long and short sleep duration, respectively (Fig. 2).

Enrichment Analysis of Shared Risk Genes

We performed enrichment analysis on the shared risk genes of the four causal effect trait pairs. Gene Ontology showed that these genes are involved in biological processes such as neurological system process, protein regulation, zinc ion balance, and cell division and significantly enriched in biological components such as cell membranes, cytoplasm, and synapses. Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes results show that it is mainly involved in protein processing in the endoplasmic reticulum and neuroactive ligand-receptor interaction pathways (Table S10, http://links.lww.com/PHM/C885, Fig. 3).

Enrichment analysis of potential shared risk genes between sleep duration and sarcopenia.

Identification of Actionable Drugs for Target Genes

We intersected risk genes for sleep-related traits with causal effects on ALM to obtain pleiotropic genes for ALM and searched for drug targets through the DrugBank database. A total of 17 genes were submitted, and MMACHC may be involved in the binding and intracellular trafficking of vitamin B12 for the treatment of vitamin B12 deficiency. FADS1, as the ligand of linolenic acid, is widely used in various supplements. We obtained 13 pleiotropic genes for walk pace, especially linolenic acid, which also targets SLC8A1 for use in drugs. Fostamatinib is a spleen tyrosine kinase inhibitor targeting MAP3K3 used to treat chronic immune thrombocytopenia after attempting one other treatment (Table S11, http://links.lww.com/PHM/C885).

DISCUSSION

In this study, we used NHANES to explore the association between sleep duration and sarcopenia, and evaluated the causality and underlying biological mechanisms of these relationships through bidirectional MR and SMR. The findings suggested that adequate sleep duration was associated with a reduced risk of sarcopenia, a finding that was consistently supported across multiple logistic regression models and further validated by sex-stratified analyses. Particularly among women, sleep duration was more strongly associated with a reduced risk of sarcopenia. At the genetic level, through LDSC and MR analyses, we found genetic correlations between sleep duration and sarcopenia-related traits such as ALM, grip strength, and walking pace. These analyses not only strengthen the associations found in observational studies but also reveal that sleep duration may influence the risk of sarcopenia through biological pathways that affect muscle function and mass. In particular, our data showed that sleep duration was negatively associated with ALM and grip strength, which may indicate potential modulatory effects of sleep quality and duration on muscle health.

Our baseline analysis identified 427 patients with sarcopenia and 4816 without sarcopenia. Although a significant difference in sleep duration was found between the groups (P = 0.033), the smaller sample size of the sarcopenia group raises the possibility of bias in the estimated mean sleep duration. We speculate that the observed difference in sleep duration might be more substantial if the sarcopenia cohort were larger. Future studies should aim to include more participants to further investigate this association.

Multiple meta-analyses confirmed independent associations between short and long sleep duration and increased risk of sarcopenia, revealing a U-shaped relationship between sleep duration and sarcopenia prevalence.2,25 Qian et al. analyzed the correlation between sleep duration, obesity, and sarcopenia in 488 adults over 65 yrs of age. Results showed a U-shaped relationship between sleep duration and sarcopenia risk. People who sleep <6 hrs have 3 times the risk of sarcopenia than those who sleep 6–8 hrs.26 In addition, there are similar results from previous studies, and there are potential gender differences in the relationship between sleep duration and the risk of sarcopenia. Compared with men, women with shorter sleep duration may be more susceptible to the risk of sarcopenia.27 Based on the results of previous studies and this research, we suggest that modification of sleep duration may be a potential intervention to prevent or slow the progression of sarcopenia. Given the significant individual health and socioeconomic impact of sarcopenia, optimizing sleep strategies may provide a practical and cost-effective way to reduce this burden. Future studies are needed to further explore how different sleep patterns (e.g., sleep quality, sleep disruption, etc.) affect the development of sarcopenia and to evaluate the long-term effects and safety of sleep-targeted interventions.

Sleep duration affects sarcopenia through different mechanisms. Restoration theory suggests that sleep is critical to the body’s repair and rejuvenation processes. During sleep, many body growth functions occur such as protein synthesis, tissue growth, muscle repair, and hormone release.25 Our enrichment analysis of shared risk genes also supports this theory, with protein processing in the endoplasmic reticulum pathway being significantly enriched. Within cells, reductions in sleep duration and quality may interfere with protein synthesis pathways by inhibiting them and inducing degradation of skeletal muscle pathways. Increased prevalence of sleep problems and circadian rhythm disruption can also cause proteolysis, alter body composition, and increase the risk of sarcopenia due to insulin resistance.28 Therefore, changes in sleep duration can alter metabolic function, leading to changes in muscle mass, function, and metabolism. Prolonged sleep deprivation may impair neuromuscular coordination and progressively impair muscle performance over time.29 Additionally, sleep disturbances have been observed to increase stress hormone levels, particularly cortisol levels, which may trigger muscle breakdown and inhibit muscle protein synthesis, leading to the onset or progression of sarcopenia.

We explored feasible drugs related to these shared risk genes through the DrugBank database. This step is of great significance for transforming basic scientific research into specific clinical applications. By analyzing these risk genes, we identified several genes that interact with known drugs that may have potential effects in treating or preventing sarcopenia. A prominent example is the MMACHC gene, a key regulator of vitamin B12 metabolism. MMACHC gene mutations are involved in the transport and intracellular processing of vitamin B12, which is essential for maintaining nerve and muscle health. Our analysis suggests that drugs targeting MMACHC, such as vitamin B12 supplements used to treat vitamin B12 deficiency, may reduce or prevent the pathological progression of sarcopenia by improving neuro-muscular function. In addition, the FADS1 gene, as a regulator of polyunsaturated fatty acid metabolism, is closely related to anti-inflammatory and tissue repair processes. Linolenic acid, a supplement related to FADS1, may combat sarcopenia by modulating the inflammatory response and promoting muscle tissue repair. Given that inflammatory states are closely associated with the development of sarcopenia, these findings suggest that linolenic acid supplementation may serve as an adjunctive treatment to reduce symptoms of sarcopenia. Further analysis also pointed out that drugs targeting SLC8A1, such as calcium channel blockers, may affect muscle function and health by regulating intracellular calcium balance. Because calcium signaling is a key factor in regulating muscle contraction and maintaining muscle health, this class of drugs may help improve muscle function in people with sarcopenia.30

Some limitations should be acknowledged. Our GWAS data were primarily driven by individuals of European ancestry, so it will be necessary to explore other ethnic groups to investigate whether our findings are replicated in more diverse ancestry. In addition, because of the nature of GWAS, it can only capture common variants associated with diseases and, in many cases, cannot reveal information about rare variants or structural variants.

CONCLUSIONS

Taken together, by integrating genetic, pharmacological, and pathological data, we not only reveal a potential link between sleep duration and sarcopenia but also point to several possible pharmacological intervention strategies by targeting specific genetic targets to improve muscle health. Future studies should further explore the effectiveness and safety of these drugs in clinical prevention and treatment of sarcopenia. These results not only provide new ideas for potential treatments for sarcopenia but also guide on how to apply these new findings in clinical practice.
