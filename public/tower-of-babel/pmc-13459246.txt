Comparison of first trimester complete blood count parameters in missed abortion and healthy pregnant women: a retrospective case-control study
Authors: Egemen Tural, Ayşe Deniz Ertürk Coşkun, Damla Demi̇r, Fisun Vural
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe aim of our study is to compare the markers and parameters obtained from complete blood count between patients diagnosed with missed abortion in the first trimester and a healthy pregnant control group.Materials and methodsThis descriptive, retrospective, case-control study included 166 patients diagnosed with missed abortion according to ultrasound criteria, and an equal number of healthy pregnant controls, who visited the Gynecology and Obstetrics Clinic at a Training and Research Hospital from January 1, 2018, to July 1, 2023. Participants, with 5 to 14 weeks of gestation, were selected using simple random sampling. Data on age, gravida, parity, gestational week, and hemogram results were retrieved from medical records and compared. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.ResultsNo significant differences were observed between the case and control groups in terms of gravida, parity, age, or gestational week (p > 0.05). Likewise, comparisons of hemogram parameters and derived values, did not show statistically significant differences (p > 0.05). However, the multivariate logistic regression analysis revealed that each unit increase in lymphocyte count was linked to a 2.075 times higher risk of missed abortion (p = 0.040).ConclusionOur study found no significant differences in complete blood count parameters between the missed abortion and healthy pregnant groups. Although lymphocyte count was significant in the multivariate model, this association should be interpreted with caution, as complete blood count values were obtained at the time of diagnosis. Therefore, it does not indicate predictive value, and prospective studies are needed to determine whether lymphocyte count may serve as a pre-diagnostic marker.Keywords: Pregnancy, Abortion, Missed, Lymphocytes, Neutrophils

Introduction

Spontaneous miscarriage is a common complication during pregnancy, often occurring without prior symptoms. Missed abortion (MA), a specific type of miscarriage, refers to the failure to naturally expel the conceptus after embryonic or fetal death, with the conceptus remaining in the uterus [1].

In cases of MA, factors such as the costs of surgery and hospital stay, complications related to surgery and/or anesthesia, infection, bleeding, and decreased fertility due to intrauterine adhesions can make the process particularly challenging or unacceptable for women who have not yet fulfilled their desire for motherhood [2].

Despite numerous studies focusing on the etiology of miscarriage, the pathophysiological mechanisms remain unclear, and an ideal marker for identifying pregnancy loss risk has not yet been identified. Consequently, research is being conducted on the effectiveness of traditional laboratory tests for assessing miscarriage risk. Evaluating complete blood count (CBC) parameters remains a popular area in medicine for assessing obstetric complications [3]. In this context, markers and parameters that can be readily obtained from a CBC, such as lymphocyte (LYM), neutrophil (NEU), mean platelet volume (MPV), neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), platelet (PLT), platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR), plateletcrit (PCT), platelet distribution width (PDW), are frequently employed in the management of complicated pregnancies [4, 5].

Research on hemogram parameters related to the etiology of miscarriage has produced diverse results. Oğlak et al. identified NLR and PLR as robust markers for early pregnancy loss [6]. Conversely, Liu et al. reported that although NLR and PLR were ineffective in predicting MA, MPV was notably lower in patients experiencing MA during the first trimester [7].

In this retrospective case-control study, we aimed to compare and assess the markers and parameters derived from CBC in patients with missed abortion versus healthy pregnant women during the first trimester.

Materials and methods

This retrospective, descriptive case-control study included women diagnosed with MA and a control group of healthy pregnant women, all of whom attended the Gynecology and Obstetrics Clinic at a Training and Research Hospital, between January 1, 2018, and July 1, 2023. The University Scientific Research Ethics Committee provided ethical approval for this study, documented under decision number 2023/14/12. Patients with MA were included in the study based on ultrasound criteria specified in guidelines and sources [8]. Data on age, gravida, parity, gestational week, and CBC results were obtained from medical records. For gestational week, measurements determined by ultrasound were used.

The study included individuals who had a CBC performed between 5 and 14 weeks of pregnancy. Exclusions were made for those who did not have a CBC within this period, multiple pregnancies, patients with insufficient or incomplete data, chromosomal abnormalities, smokers during pregnancy, uterine anomalies, known hematological disorders, anticoagulant users, individuals with a history of thrombosis, acute or chronic infectious diseases, cancer patients, those under treatment for other medical conditions (e.g., diabetes mellitus, thyroid disorders), and individuals with recurrent abortions.

During the specified period, 358 patients were diagnosed with MA at the Gynecology and Obstetrics Clinic. Of these, 79 were excluded due to the predefined exclusion criteria, leaving 279 eligible patients. A sample size calculation based on a 95% confidence level and a 5% margin of error indicated that a minimum of 162 participants would be required. Therefore, 166 patients with MA were selected from the 279 eligible cases using a computer-generated simple random sampling method. The remaining 113 eligible patients were not included, as they exceeded the required sample size and their inclusion would not have contributed additional statistical power. The study was conducted with a total of 332 participants, including 166 patients with MA and 166 healthy pregnant controls, matched for age and gestational week. Hemogram values obtained at the time of MA diagnosis were used for analysis.

Normality of continuous variables was evaluated using graphical methods and the Shapiro-Wilk test, which confirmed that none of the variables followed a normal distribution. Descriptive statistics are presented as Mean ± SD (standard deviation) and Median (Minimum-Maximum).

The Mann-Whitney U test was employed to compare various parameters between the case and control groups, including gravida, parity, age, gestational week, PLT (10³/µl), NEU (10³/µl), LYM (10³/µl), hemoglobin (HEM) (g/dl), red blood cell distribution width (RDW) (%), PDW (%), red blood cell count (RBC) (10⁶/µl), MPV (fl.), PCT, PLR, and NLR. PLR was calculated by taking the ratio of PLT to LYM. Similarly, NLR was computed by dividing the NEU by LYM.

Multivariate logistic regression analysis was utilized to evaluate potential risk factors for MA. The statistical evaluations were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 21.0 (IBM Corp., 2012) and MS Excel 2007, with significance set at a p-value of less than 0.05.

Results

In our study, the mean age ± SD of participants in the case group was 29.63 ± 6.37, while in the control group it was 28.87 ± 5.45. The mean gestational weeks ± SD were 7.96 ± 2.36 for the case group and 8.26 ± 2.74 for the control group. No statistically significant differences were found between the case and control groups regarding gravida, parity, age, or gestational week (p > 0.05) (Table 1).

Discussion

Our study did not reveal any statistically significant differences in CBC parameters between the MA and healthy pregnant groups. Recently, values obtained from CBC have been utilized to gain insights into the diagnosis and prognosis of various diseases [9]. Defective placentation, oxidative stress, and excessive maternal inflammatory response are major causes of early pregnancy loss [10]. The literature indicates that readily measurable and cost-effective values such as NLR, PLR, and MPV are effective markers for detecting underlying inflammation linked to various diseases [11].

Research on inflammatory markers and hemogram parameters related to miscarriage has shown varying results. Bas et al. suggested that markers like PLR, NLR, NEU, and LYM could be useful in assessing the risk of spontaneous miscarriage [9]. Oğlak et al. indicated that higher NLR and PLR levels might be potential indicators of early pregnancy loss [6]. Bıyık et al. observed notably higher PLR and NLR values in individuals with MA compared to those in the healthy control group [11]. Yavuz et al. also found that elevated NLR levels could be a valuable predictor for MA, especially in the initial trimester [12]. Conversely, Kim et al. observed that NLR values were significantly lower in the MA group compared to those with threatened miscarriage [13]. Similarly, Yakıştıran et al. suggested that reduced levels of NLR and PLR might be indicative of miscarriage [3]. On the other hand, Liu et al. argued that NLR and PLR were not effective predictors for MA [7]. Görkem et al. also reported no significant differences in NLR and PLR levels between spontaneous abortion cases and healthy controls [14]. Our findings are consistent with those of Görkem and Liu, as we observed no statistically significant differences in NLR and PLR levels between the MA and healthy control groups. The variations observed in different studies, may be due to differences in sample sizes, research methodologies, analytical tools, and participant demographics.

MPV, linked to platelet activation and thrombotic activity, has been associated with thrombosis, microemboli in the uteroplacental circulation, and placental inflammation [15]. Literature on MPV as a predictor for miscarriage presents mixed results. Oğlak et al. reported that MPV values were higher in the early pregnancy loss group compared to controls, though not significantly so [6]. Conversely, Liu et al. found MPV values to be lower in the MA group [7]. Bas et al. identified MPV as a negative predictive marker for spontaneous abortion [9], while Ata et al. noted reduced MPV values in cases of early pregnancy loss or threatened miscarriage [16]. In our study, MPV values were lower in the MA group relative to controls, but this difference was not statistically significant. The observed lack of significant difference may be due to the migration of larger, more active platelets to the site of inflammation, which might reduce the average platelet volume during early pregnancy losses [16].

Regarding LYM values, opinions also vary. Bıyık et al. found lower LYM values in the MA group compared to controls [11]. Wang et al. reported no significant difference in LYM values between MA and healthy control groups [17]. Conversely, Aynıoğlu et al. observed higher LYM values in the recurrent pregnancy loss group [18]. Yakıştıran et al. also reported higher LYM values in the group with spontaneous abortion compared to the healthy control group [3]. In our study, LYM values were higher in the MA group, although this difference was not statistically significant. Additionally, the multivariate logistic regression analysis indicated that each unit increase in LYM count was linked to a 2.075 times higher risk of MA. Watanabe et al. observed reductions in total lymphocyte, T cell, and B cell counts during pregnancy, attributing these changes to the influence of pregnancy-related hormones on the bone marrow and the gradual decrease in thymus size and cellularity [19]. Yuan et al. reported a decrease in the ratio of T-helper (Th)-1 to Th-2 lymphocytes and monocytes in normal pregnancy conditions [20]. These observations suggest that an elevated LYM count may be an important parameter to investigate in CBC in the context of missed abortion.

However, it is important to emphasize that although lymphocyte count remained significant in the multivariate logistic regression model, this finding must be interpreted with considerable caution. First, the explanatory power of the model is low (Nagelkerke R² = 0.075), indicating that the model accounts for only a small fraction of the variance in missed abortion status, which limits the clinical meaningfulness of the association. Second, and more critically, CBC measurements were obtained at the time of missed abortion diagnosis rather than prior to the event. Therefore, the regression model reflects a cross-sectional association rather than a temporal or causal relationship. The observed elevation in lymphocyte count may represent a consequence of the missed abortion process rather than a contributing factor. Accordingly, this result should not be interpreted as evidence of predictive value. Prospective studies incorporating pre-diagnostic CBC assessments are required to clarify whether lymphocyte count may serve as a true marker associated with missed abortion.

Studies have explored the use of various CBC parameters, such as NEU, PCT, and PLT, for predicting miscarriage. Wang et al. suggested that NEU values could be useful in predicting MA in the first trimester [17], while Aynıoğlu et al. proposed that PCT might be appropriate for assessing recurrent pregnancy losses. Their study found PLT values to be significantly higher in the group with recurrent pregnancy losses. PCT is calculated as MPV multiplied by PLT [18]. In our study, no significant differences were found between the groups concerning NEU, PCT, MPV, and PLT values. The differences between our findings and those in other studies may be due to the complex mechanisms underlying MA, which can make it challenging to identify reliable hematological markers associated with the condition [13]. Further in-depth research is required to better understand and clarify these discrepancies.

Our study found no differences between the groups in terms of age, gestational week, parity, and gravida. This lack of variation is significant, especially considering the potential negative effects of age on MA [21]. Ensuring group homogeneity and evaluating only the MA status in relation to CBC results highlights a critical distinction in our study.

The present study has several strengths and weaknesses. The homogeneity of the groups, the study’s conduction in a tertiary hospital with reliable results, the establishment and meticulous application of comprehensive exclusion criteria, and the evaluation of participants based not only on first-trimester results but also on whether they delivered a healthy baby are notable strengths. However, this study has several limitations. First, its single-center and retrospective design restricts the ability to control for confounding factors. Second, and most importantly, CBC values were obtained at the time of missed abortion diagnosis rather than prior to the event. This is a fundamental constraint, as it precludes any causal or temporal inference: the observed hematological alterations may reflect a physiological response to the missed abortion process rather than pre-existing conditions. Consequently, the findings of this study should be interpreted strictly as cross-sectional associations and not as evidence of predictive value. The lack of participants’ height and weight data, which prevented BMI calculation, and the exclusion of the abortus imminens group also represent methodological constraints.

Conclusion

In recent years, the examination of CBC parameters in the context of missed abortion has remained an area of interest. In our study, no statistically significant differences were found between the missed abortion and healthy pregnant groups in terms of CBC parameters. Although lymphocyte count appeared as a significant variable in the multivariate model, this association should be interpreted cautiously, as CBC values were obtained at the time of diagnosis rather than prior to the event. Therefore, this finding does not indicate true predictive value. Nonetheless, lymphocyte count may represent a parameter worth further investigation in future prospective studies designed to evaluate pre-diagnostic predictors of missed abortion.
