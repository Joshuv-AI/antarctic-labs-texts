Random Turán Problems for K s , t K_{s,t} Expansions
Jiaxi Nie, Sam Spiro

Abstract
Let K s , t ( r ) K_{s,t}^{(r)} denote the r r -uniform hypergraph obtained from the graph K s , t K_{s,t} by inserting r − 2 r-2 new vertices inside each edge of K s , t K_{s,t} . We prove essentially tight bounds on the size of a largest K s , t ( r ) K_{s,t}^{(r)} -subgraph of the random r r -uniform hypergraph G n , p r G_{n,p}^{r} whenever r ≥ 2 ​ s / 3 + 2 r\geq 2s/3+2 , giving the first random Turán results for expansions that go beyond a natural “tight-tree barrier.” In addition to this, our methods yield optimal supersaturation results for K s , t ( 3 ) K_{s,t}^{(3)} for sufficiently dense host hypergraphs, which may be of independent interest.

1.1
Proof Outline

Here we very briefly sketch the high-level ideas of our proof for
Theorem

1.3
. The lower bounds for the theorem follow easily from known results in the literature, so all that is needed is to prove the corresponding upper bounds.

As is often the case with random Turán problems, we will prove our upper bounds by proving a “balanced supersaturation result”, which informally says that that if an
r
-graph
H
has many edges, then we can find a collection
\mathcal{H}
of copies of
K_{s,t}^{(r)}
in
H
which are “spread out” in the sense that no set of edges of
H
lies in too many copies of
K_{s,t}^{(r)}
in
\mathcal{H}
.

In order to further discuss the details of our proof, we define for an
r
-graph
H
its
k
-shadow

\partial^{k}H
to be the
k
-graph on
V(H)
where a given
k
-set
K
is an edge of
\partial^{k}H
if and only if
K
is contained in an edge of
H
.

Balanced Supersaturation for
r\geq s+1
. At a very high-level, our previous proof of
Theorem

1.2
came from a balanced supersaturation result which itself was proven by first (I) finding a (large) collection of graph
K_{s,t}
’s in the shadow
\partial^{2}H
, and then (II) extending these copies of
K_{s,t}
into copies of
K_{s,t}^{(r)}
in
H
. Step (II) here is rather straightforward to implement, so the main difficulty lies in establishing (I).

To prove (I), i.e. to find a large collection of graph
K_{s,t}
’s in the shadow
\partial^{2}H
, we roughly speaking build our collection in one of two ways depending on the structure of
\partial^{2}H
. First, if
\partial^{2}H
has many edges, then a known balanced supersaturation result of Morris and Saxton
[
12
]
for
K_{s,t}
immediately implies that
\partial^{2}H
contains many copies of
K_{s,t}
. Otherwise if
\partial^{2}H
is sparse, then one can argue that there are many
s
-sets
\{u_{1},\ldots,u_{s}\}\subseteq V(H)
which are contained in many edges of
H
; and it is in this step here that we crucially need
r\geq s+1
(as otherwise an
s
-set will be contained in at most one edge of
H
). By using these many edges of
H
containing
\{u_{1},\ldots,u_{s}\}
, one can show that there exist many vertices
v
such that
\{u_{1},\ldots,u_{s},v\}
is contained in an edge of
H
, and taking
t
such sets implies the existence of many
K_{s,t}
’s in
\partial^{2}H
.

Slightly more generally, it is implicitly shown in
[
19
, Theorem 2.8, Lemma 4.3]
that for any graph
F
, there exists an integer
t(F)
such that for
r\geq t(F)
, an analog of (I) can be proven for
F
through an argument similar to the one outlined above. At a very high level, the parameter
t(F)
is the smallest quantity such that it is “easy” to prove
\mathrm{ex}(n,F^{(r)})=O(n^{r-1})
for
r\geq t(F)
, and intuitively this implies that it should also be “easy” to prove a corresponding bound for the random Turán problem in this same range of
r
. More precisely,
t(F)
is defined to be the smallest
t
such that
F^{(t)}
is a spanning subgraph of a “tight tree” of uniformity
t
.

Prior to the present work, tight bounds for the random Turán problem for expansions were known only in the range
r\geq t(F)
, since below this point several of the key technical results in the area fail to go through. As such, we refer to
t(F)
as the “tight-tree barrier” for random Turán problems of hypergraph expansions, with the main objective of this paper being the development of ideas to go beyond this barrier in the case of complete bipartite graphs (and possibly beyond).

Balanced Supersaturation for Smaller
r
. Our key innovation for proving our stronger bound of
r\geq 2s/3+2
in
Theorem

1.3
is that we no longer build our large collection of copies of
K_{s,t}^{(r)}
in
H
from copies of
K_{s,t}
in the
2-shadow
\partial^{2}H
, but rather from copies of
K_{s,t}^{(3)}
in the
3-shadow
\partial^{3}H
. Specifically, we will prove a balanced supersaturation result for
K_{s,t}^{(3)}
which (unlike the result of Morris and Saxton for the 2-shadow case) will be strong enough to be applied regardless of the density of
\partial^{3}H
, allowing us to build a large collection of copies of
K_{s,t}^{(3)}
without needing to rely on the existence of sets
\{u_{1},\ldots,u_{s}\}
contained in many edges, letting us go below the
r\geq s+1
barrier that was intrinsic to previous approaches.

It remains then for us to prove this balanced supersaturation result for
K_{s,t}^{(3)}
. Our proof will rely on two balanced supersaturation results for graphs: one which is effective when
\partial^{2}H
is dense and one which is effective for sparse
\partial^{2}H
. As before, the case when
\partial^{2}H
has many edges will use the result of Morris and Saxton
[
12
]
mentioned above. For the second (and far more technical) case of
\partial^{2}H
being sparse, we develop a new balanced supersaturation result for
K_{s,t}
which is effective for tripartite graphs which contain many triangles but few edges, the proof of which is based implicitly on the idea of
vertex supersaturation
as developed in
[
11
]
. Combining these two approaches gives our desired balanced supersaturation result for
K_{s,t}^{(3)}
, from which we can conclude our bounds in
Theorem

1.3
through the usage of our general theorems for expansions developed in
[
19
]
.

2.2
Dense Balanced Supersaturation

The following result was essentially proven by Morris and Saxton.

Proposition 2.1

(Theorem 7.4,
[
12
]
)
.

For all
s,t\geq 2
there exists an
L_{0}>0
such that the following holds. Let
G
be a bipartite graph with bipartition
U\cup V
which has
|U|\leq|V|
and
L|V|^{2-1/s}
edges. If
L\geq L_{0}
, then there exists a collection
\mathcal{G}
of
K_{s,t}
’s in
G
whose parts of size
s
lie in
U
such that
|\mathcal{G}|=\Omega(L^{st}|V|^{s})
, and such that every
\sigma^{\prime}\subseteq E(G)
with
|\sigma_{v}^{\prime}\cap U|=a\geq 1
and
|\sigma_{v}^{\prime}\cap V|=b\geq 1
satisfies

\deg_{\mathcal{G}}(\sigma^{\prime})\leq O((L|V|^{1-1/s})^{s-a}(L^{s})^{t-b}).

Strictly speaking, Morris and Saxton prove a slightly different result than that of
Proposition

2.1
(which would also suffice for our purposes at the cost of complicating our notation), but their proof easily adapts to prove the result above. More precisely, there are two differences between the statement of Morris and Saxton’s with
Proposition

2.1
. First, they do not consider the setting of bipartite graphs, but one can easily reduce to this case by considering a bipartite subgraph of
G
containing at least half its edges. In the bipartite setting, their result as written does not say whether the copies of
K_{s,t}
have their parts of size
s
in
U
or in
V
, but it is easy to see from their proof (which is essentially a refined version of the classic proof of the Kővári-Sós-Turán theorem) that we can guarantee that every
K_{s,t}
in
\mathcal{G}
has their set of size
s
in the smaller set
U
, from which our statement follows.

2.3
Sparse Balanced Supersaturation

Intuition
. The sparse case of our argument will require a more complicated analog of
Proposition

2.1
for tripartite graphs. We note that this result will not be needed until halfway through the proof of
Proposition

3.1
, and as such, the reader may wish to postpone fully digesting the statement of the result until this point.

The basic idea of our sparse balanced supersaturation result is roughly the same as that of
Proposition

2.1
: we want to say that if
G
is a “nice” tripartite graph (i.e. if
G
is close to regular, has many triangles, and has few edges), then we can find a collection
\mathcal{G}
of
K_{s,t}
’s in
G
such that (1) we can specify how each part of size
s
in the
K_{s,t}
’s intersects the parts of
G
, (2)
|\mathcal{G}|
is large, and (3) we can bound
\deg_{\mathcal{G}}(\sigma^{\prime})
by some function
\phi
depending only on how the vertices
\sigma^{\prime}_{v}
intersect the parts of
G
. For technical reasons, we will also need to further impose that
G
is the shadow of a “nice” hypergraph
H
.

Formal Details
. We begin by specifying the function
\phi
mentioned above.

Definition 3
.

Let
G
be a tripartite graph on
V_{1}\cup V_{2}\cup V_{3}
and
n,\delta,K,\ell,m_{1,3},m_{2,3}
a set of given parameters. We define a function
\phi:2^{V(G)}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
as follows: given
\nu\subseteq V(G)
, let
a=|\nu\cap(V_{1}\cup V_{2})|
and
b=|\nu\cap V_{3}|
. If
a=0
or
b=0
, then
\phi(\nu)=\infty
. Otherwise, if
\nu\cap V_{1}\neq\emptyset
we define

\phi(\nu)=\frac{\ell^{s-1}n^{s-1+1/s}(\ell^{3-2s}K^{s-1})^{t}}{\delta m_{1,3}(\delta\ell^{-1}Kn^{1-2/s})^{a-1}(\delta\ell^{3-2s}K^{s-1})^{b-1}}

and if
\nu\cap V_{1}=\emptyset
we define

\phi(\nu)=\frac{\ell^{s-1}n^{s-1+1/s}(\ell^{3-2s}K^{s-1})^{t}}{\delta m_{2,3}(\delta\ell^{-1}Kn^{1-2/s})^{a-1}(\delta\ell^{3-2s}K^{s-1})^{b-1}}.

We emphasize that the only difference between the two inlined expression is the usage of
m_{1,3}
and
m_{2,3}
in the denominator. We also note that having
\phi(\nu)=\infty
when either
a=0
or

Then for

2.4
Translating from Graphs to Hypergraphs

Intuition
. We will use the two results above to find a large “spread out” collection
\mathcal{G}
of graph
K_{s,t}
’s in the shadow graph of a hypergraph
H
. Given such a collection
\mathcal{G}
, we will form a collection
\mathcal{H}
of
K_{s,t}^{(3)}
’s in
H
by selecting copies
F^{\prime}\in\mathcal{G}
and then iteratively extending each edge
e\in F^{\prime}
into an edge
h\in H
containing it.
In doing this, we will want to make sure our collection of
K_{s,t}^{(3)}
’s is still “spread out”, i.e. that
\deg_{\mathcal{H}}(\sigma)
is not too large for any given set of hyperedges
\sigma
.

In order to bound
\deg_{\mathcal{H}}(\sigma)
for some given
\sigma\subseteq H
, we will need to consider all the possible ways that each of the edges
h\in\sigma
could have been generated by this procedure. That is, we will need to consider all the possible ways of picking for each edge
h\in\sigma
an edge
e_{h}\in G
contained in
h
(which we think of as the edge in a copy
F^{\prime}
that we extended to form
h
), and then work with the set
\sigma^{\prime}=\{e_{h}:h\in\sigma\}
. The set of all such
\sigma^{\prime}
that can be formed in this way will be denoted by
\mathrm{proj}(\sigma)
. More precisely, we use the following definition.

Formal Details
.

Definition 4
.

If
\sigma
is a collection of 3-sets such that
|h\cap h^{\prime}|\leq 1
for all distinct
h,h^{\prime}\in\sigma
, then we define the projection
\mathrm{proj}(\sigma)
to be the collection of all sets of 2-sets
\sigma^{\prime}
obtained by including exactly one pair from each
h\in\sigma
.

For example, if
\sigma=\{123,345\}
then

\mathrm{proj}(\sigma)=\{\{12,34\},\{12,35\},\{12,45\},\{13,34\},\{13,35\},\{13,45\},\{23,34\},\{23,35\},\{23,45\}\}.

We now state our main translation lemma from copies of
K_{s,t}
into
K_{s,t}^{(3)}
. We in fact state this in the more general context of arbitrary graphs
F
since proof and statement of the result are no more difficult in this more general setting.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
F
be a graph, let
H
be a
3
-partite
3
-graph with partition
V_{1}\cup V_{2}\cup V_{3}
, let
G_{i,j}
denote the pairs of vertices in
V_{i}\cup V_{j}
contained in at least one edge of
H
, and let
\mathcal{G}
be a collection of copies of
F
in the shadow graph of
H
. If there exist real numbers
D_{i,j},d_{i,j}\geq 2v(F)+2|F|
such that
d_{i,j}\leq\deg_{H}(e)\leq D_{i,j}
for every pair
e\in G_{i,j}
, and if there exists integers
c_{i,j}
such that every copy of
F\in\mathcal{G}
has exactly
c_{i,j}
edges in
G_{i,j}
, then there exists a collection
\mathcal{H}
of expansions
F^{(3)}
in
H
such that

|\mathcal{H}|=\Omega\left(|\mathcal{G}|\prod_{i,j}d_{i,j}^{c_{i,j}}\right)

and such that for any
\sigma\subseteq H
, we have
\deg_{\mathcal{H}}(\sigma)=0
if
|h\cap h^{\prime}|>1
for some distinct
h,h^{\prime}\in\sigma
, and otherwise.

\deg_{\mathcal{H}}(\sigma)\leq\sum_{\sigma^{\prime}\in\mathrm{proj}(\sigma)}\deg_{\mathcal{G}}(\sigma^{\prime})\prod_{i,j}D_{i,j}^{c_{i,j}-|\sigma^{\prime}\cap G_{i,j}|}.

Variants of this translation lemma have appeared in essentially every previous work on the random Turán problem for expansions, such as
[
19
, Lemma 2.1]
. We note, however, that all of these previous works only ever considered the case that each copy of
F
lied entirely within two of the parts
V_{i},V_{j}
(so that
c_{i,j}=|F|
and
c_{i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}=0
otherwise), allowing for a much simpler statement of the result. For our purposes, however, it will be crucial that we allow our copies of
F
to lie in all three parts of
H
.

Proof.

Consider the following procedure for constructing copies of
F^{(3)}
in
H
. Start by picking some
F^{\prime}\in\mathcal{G}
and let
e_{1},\ldots,e_{m}
be an arbitrary ordering of the edges of
F^{\prime}
. Iteratively pick
h_{k}\in E(H)
to be any edge of
H
which contains
e_{k}
and which is disjoint from
(V(F^{\prime})\cup\bigcup_{k^{\prime}<k}h_{k^{\prime}})\setminus e_{k}
. Observe that if this process terminates, then these
h_{k}
edges form a copy of
F^{(3)}
in
H
, and we let
\mathcal{H}
denote the set of copies of
F^{(3)}
that can be formed in this way. It remains to analyze the properties of this collection.

Observe that if
e_{k}\in G_{i,j}
, then the number of choices for
h_{k}
is at least
d_{i,j}-v(F)-|F|\geq\frac{1}{2}d_{i,j}
, so the total the number of ways of completing this algorithm is at least
\Omega(|\mathcal{G}|\prod_{i,j}d_{i,j}^{c_{i,j}})
. Moreover, since each
F^{(3)}\in\mathcal{H}
arises in at most
O(1)
ways form this procedure, we conclude that
|\mathcal{H}|
has the desired size.

Now consider any
\sigma\subseteq H
. If
|h\cap h^{\prime}|>1
for some distinct
h,h^{\prime}\in\sigma
, then
\deg_{\mathcal{H}}(\sigma)=0
(since
\sigma
can not be the subset of any expansion
F^{(3)}
), so we can assume this is not the case. Observe that every copy of
F^{(3)}\in\mathcal{H}
containing
\sigma
arose
by starting with some
F^{\prime}\in\mathcal{G}
which contained a graph edge from each
h\in\sigma
(i.e. which contained
\sigma^{\prime}\subseteq F^{\prime}
for some
\sigma^{\prime}\in\mathrm{proj}(\sigma)
), then extending each of these graph edges to form
\sigma
, and then extending the remaining graph edges
F^{\prime}\setminus\sigma^{\prime}
in an arbitrary way. Thus one can specify each copy of
F^{(3)}
containing
\sigma
through the following steps:

(a)

Choose some
\sigma^{\prime}\in\mathrm{proj}(\sigma)
.

(b)

Choose some
F^{\prime}\in\mathcal{G}
containing
\sigma^{\prime}
(which can be done in at most
\deg_{\mathcal{G}}(\sigma^{\prime})
ways).

(c)

Choose extensions of each edge in
F\setminus\sigma^{\prime}
(which can be done in at most
\prod_{i,j}D_{i,j}^{c_{i,j}-|\sigma^{\prime}\cap G_{i,j}|}
ways).

Multiplying these number of choices from (b) and (c) together and then summing over all possible choices of
\sigma^{\prime}
in (a) gives the desired degree bound, finishing the proof.
∎

Auxiliary Lemmas

We will make use of the following basic arithmetic lemma.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
\pi,A,B
be real numbers and
s,t\geq 1
integers satisfying
A\geq B
and
\pi\geq B^{-1},(A^{s-1}B^{t-1})^{\frac{-1}{st-1}}
. Then

\max_{1\leq a\leq s,\ 1\leq b\leq t}\pi^{1-ab}A^{1-a}B^{1-b}\leq 1.

Proof.

Fix some
a,b
achieving this maximum, and observe that the quantity we wish to upper bound can be rewritten as

(\pi A)^{1-a}(\pi^{a}B)^{1-b}.

Since
\pi A\geq 1
(because
\pi\geq B^{-1}\geq A^{-1}
), and since
a,b\geq 1
, we see that this quantity will be at most 1 unless
\pi^{a}B<1
. We may thus assume this holds, in which case the expression above is maximized when
b\leq t
is as large as possible, giving an upper bound of

(\pi A)^{1-a}(\pi^{a}B)^{1-t}=B^{1-t}\pi A(\pi^{t}A)^{-a}.

Observe that this quantity is maximized at either
a=1
or
a=s
. In the former case we end up with
(\pi B)^{1-t}\leq 1
(since
\pi\geq B^{-1}
), and in the latter case we end up with
\pi^{1-st}B^{1-t}A^{1-s}\leq 1
(since
\pi\geq(A^{s-1}B^{t-1})^{-1/(st-1)}
). We conclude the result.

Optimizing the choice of

Corollary 3.8
.

For all

, there exists a sufficiently large constant

such that the following holds. Let

be an

Given

To prove the result, it suffices to show that for all

that there exists an

Consider some

\ell

, there exists a non-empty collection

of copies of

in

∎

Given

, we will say that

is

if it satisfies (

there exists a valid

.

Intuition
. We will always consider
\ell
which are either at the cusp of being valid (i.e.
\ell=k^{1/2}n^{-1/2s}
or
\ell=k^{1/3}
), or which are such that the first
\mu

equals one of the latter two terms of

(i.e.
\ell=k^{\frac{t}{st+3t-2}}n^{\frac{s-2}{s(st+3t-2)}}
or
\ell=n^{\frac{s-1}{s(st-1)}}
).

In order to show our choices for
\ell
are valid, we will need to verify inequalities of the form
f(k,n)\leq g(k,n)
for all
k\in[n^{\alpha},n^{\beta}]
with
f,g
polynomials in
k,n
. We will typically do this by observing that, for example, if the exponent for
k
in
f
is larger than it is in

, then it suffices to verify this inequality at the largest possible value of
k=n^{\beta}
(since the exponent condition implies this holds for all smaller values of

). In fact, in most cases we will have
f(k,n)=g(k,n)
at

, in particular giving the desired inequality. In our proof we will often omit spelling out all of these details each time and only say things like “because equality holds for the largest possible value of
k
.”

To show
\mu\leq\pi
, we will typically show that
\mu
equals the maximum of one or two of the terms in the maximum used to define
\pi
(and hence
\pi
must be at least as large as

).

Formal Details
. First consider
k\in[K_{0}(\log n)^{100},n^{\frac{3(s-2)}{s(st-2)}}]

\ell=k^{1/3}
(which is always valid for

is sufficiently large in terms of

), which yields

\mu=\max\{k^{-1/3},k^{\frac{-1}{3(st-1)}}n^{\frac{-(s-2)}{s(st-1)}},n^{\frac{-(s-1)}{s(st-1)}}\}.

Observe that
k^{-1/3}\geq k^{\frac{-1}{3(st-1)}}n^{\frac{-(s-2)}{s(st-1)}}
for

(since this is equivalent to saying

and equality holds when

is the largest possible value). Using this with the expression for
\mu
above and the definition of
\pi
gives

giving the desired result in this case.

We next consider
k\in[n^{\frac{3(s-2)}{s(st-2)}},n^{\frac{4st-s-3t}{st(st-1)}}]

is valid. Indeed,

giving the desired result in this range.

We next consider
k\in[n^{\frac{4st-s-3t}{st(st-1)}},n^{\frac{st+2s-3}{s(st-1)}}]

\ell=n^{\frac{s-1}{s(st-1)}}

(i,j)
-bad pair. If
3\notin\{i,j\}
then
\phi(\nu)=\infty
, so no such pair exists and there is nothing to prove. Thus without loss of generality we can assume
j=3
. By definition of
\mathcal{F}
,
Claim

4.2
, and the definition of
\phi
; having
\nu\in\mathcal{F}
means

\deg_{\mathcal{G}_{v}}(\nu)\geq\lfloor\phi(\nu)\rfloor\geq\frac{1}{2}\phi(\nu)=\frac{1}{2}\delta^{-1}m_{i,3}^{-1}M.

With this, a double counting argument gives

\displaystyle\#(i,3)\textrm{-bad pairs}

\displaystyle\leq\#\left[\textrm{edges }\nu\textrm{ with }\deg_{\mathcal{G}_{v}}(\nu)\geq\frac{1}{2}\delta^{-1}m_{i,3}^{-1}M\right]

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{1}{2}\delta^{-1}m_{i,3}^{-1}M\right)^{-1}\cdot\sum_{\nu:|\nu|=2}\deg_{\mathcal{G}_{v}}(\nu)=2\delta m_{i,3}M^{-1}\cdot{s+t\choose 2}|\mathcal{G}_{v}|

\displaystyle<2\delta m_{i,3}M^{-1}\cdot{s+t\choose 2}M(12\log n)^{-16}\leq\frac{1}{6}m_{i,3}(12\log n)^{-16},

with the second to last inequality using (
13
) and the last inequality using that
\delta
is sufficiently small, proving the sub-claim.

Now define
H^{\prime}\subseteq H
to consist of all hyperedges which do not contain pairs
\{u,v\}\in\mathcal{F}
, and observe that the number of triangles in
G^{\prime}
is at least
|H^{\prime}|
, so it suffices to lower bound
|H^{\prime}|
. Also note by (e) that each
(i,j)
-bad edge is contained in at most
Kn^{3-3/s}m_{i,j}^{-1}(12\log n)^{32}
hyperedges of
H
, so by the subclaim, the number of hyperedges removed going from
H
to
H^{\prime}
is at most

\sum_{i,j}\frac{1}{6}m_{i,j}(12\log n)^{-16}\cdot Kn^{3-3/s}m_{i,j}^{-1}(12\log n)^{32}=\frac{1}{2}Kn^{3-3/s}(12\log n)^{16}.

Thus by (b),

|H^{\prime}|\geq|H|-\frac{1}{2}Kn^{3-3/s}(12\log n)^{16}\geq\frac{1}{2}Kn^{3-3/s}(12\log n)^{16},

proving the result.
∎

By working with
G^{\prime}
, we will automatically guarantee that every edge of the
K_{s,t}
’s contained in
G^{\prime}
lie outside of
\mathcal{F}
. To avoid larger subsets of
\mathcal{F}
, we define for
\nu\subseteq V(G)
the
link set

\mathcal{J}(\nu)=\{u\in V(G):\nu\cup\{u\}\in\mathcal{F}\}.

The idea here is that if we have already selected some
\mathcal{F}
-good set
\mathcal{K}^{\prime}
that we aim to include in our ultimate copy
\mathcal{K}
of
K_{s,t}
, then
\mathcal{K}^{\prime}\cup\{u\}
will continue to be
\mathcal{F}
-good if and only if
u\notin\mathcal{J}(\nu)
for any
\nu\subseteq\mathcal{K}^{\prime}
. Thus
\bigcup_{\nu\subseteq\mathcal{K}^{\prime}}\mathcal{J}(\nu)
represents the set of “bad choices” for
u
given the partially constructed copy
\mathcal{K}^{\prime}
.
The following claim gives an upper bound on the number of these “bad choices” for
u
which lie within some given set of vertices
V
.

Claim 4.4
.

Let
\nu,V\subseteq V(G)
. If
\phi(\nu\cup\{u\})=\infty
for all
u\in V
, then
\mathcal{J}(\nu)\cap V=\emptyset
, and otherwise

|\mathcal{J}(\nu)\cap V|\leq 2(s+t)\frac{\phi(\nu)}{\min_{u\in V}\phi(\nu\cup\{u\})}.

Proof.

If
\phi(\nu\cup\{u\})=\infty
, then trivially
\deg(\nu\cup\{u\})<\phi(\nu\cup\{u\})
, which means
\nu\cup\{u\}\notin\mathcal{F}
and hence
u\notin\mathcal{J}(\nu)
. This gives the result if
\phi(\nu\cup\{u\})=\infty
for all
u\in V
, so from now on we assume
\min_{u\in V}\phi(\nu\cup\{u\})<\infty
.

Observe that

\sum_{u\in\mathcal{J}(\nu)\cap V}\deg_{\mathcal{G}_{v}}(\nu\cup\{u\})\leq\sum_{u\in V(G)}\deg_{\mathcal{G}_{v}}(\nu\cup\{u\})=(s+t)\deg_{\mathcal{G}_{v}}(\nu)\leq(s+t)\phi(\nu),

where the equality used that each hyperedge
h
containing
\nu
is counted exactly
(s+t)
times in the second summation (namely, once for each
u\in h
), and the last inequality used that
\mathcal{G}_{v}
is
\phi
-bounded. On the other hand, the definition of
\mathcal{J}(\nu)
yields

\sum_{u\in\mathcal{J}(\nu)\cap V}\deg_{\mathcal{G}_{u}}(\nu\cup\{u\})\geq\sum_{u\in\mathcal{J}(\nu)\cap V}\lfloor\phi(\nu\cup\{u\})\rfloor\geq|\mathcal{J}(\nu)\cap V|\cdot\min_{u\in V}\lfloor\phi(\nu\cup\{u\})\rfloor.

Rearranging these two inlined expressions gives

|\mathcal{J}(\nu)\cap V|\leq(s+t)\frac{\phi(\nu)}{\min_{u\in V}\lfloor\phi(\nu\cup\{u\})\rfloor},

and the final result follows by using
Claim

4.2
to get rid of the floor.
∎

With these preliminaries established, we begin our main argument for finding many copies
\mathcal{K}
which are
\mathcal{F}
-good through a modification of the usual “star counting” argument of Kővári-Sós-Turán.

Claim 4.5
.

Let
\mathcal{P}
denote the set of pairs
(S,v)
such that the following holds:

•

v\in V_{3}
,

•

S\subseteq N(v)
with
|S\cap V_{1}|=1
and
|S\cap V_{2}|=s-1
,

•

The vertex
u_{1}\in S\cap V_{1}
is adjacent to every vertex of
S\cap V_{2}
, and

•

S\cup\{v\}
is
\mathcal{F}
-good.

Then

|\mathcal{P}|\geq(8s)^{1-s}\ell n^{2-1/s}\left(\ell^{-1}Kn^{1-2/s}(12\log n)^{16}\right)^{s-1}.

Proof.

Given an edge
uv
in
G^{\prime}
, let
\deg(u,v)
denote the number of common neighbors of
u,v
in
G^{\prime}
, i.e. the number of triangles containing the edge
uv
. Let
E
be the set of edges

in
V_{1}\cup V_{3}
, and let
E^{\prime}\subseteq E
be those edges with

\deg(u,v)\geq\ell^{-1}Kn^{1-2/s}\geq 4s,

(14)

with this second inequality holding since
\ell\leq\delta K
and
\delta
is sufficiently small. Note that

\sum_{uv\in E^{\prime}}\deg(u,v)\geq\frac{1}{2}Kn^{3-3/s}(12\log n)^{16}-\ell^{-1}Kn^{1-2/s}\cdot|E|\geq\frac{1}{4}Kn^{3-3/s}(12\log n)^{16},

(15)

where the first inequality used that the total number of triangles in
G^{\prime}
is at least
\frac{1}{2}Kn^{3-3/s}(12\log n)^{16}
by
Claim

4.3
and that the number of triangles not containing an edge of
E^{\prime}
is at most
\ell^{-1}Kn^{1-2/s}\cdot m_{1,3}
by definition of
E^{\prime}
; and the second inequality used (c) and (a) to get
|E|\leq m_{1,3}\leq\ell|V_{1}|^{2-1/s}\leq\ell n^{2-1/s}
and that
n
is sufficiently large.

We will now build elements of
\mathcal{P}
iteratively as follows: we start with any edge
u_{1}v\in E^{\prime}
with
u_{1}\in V_{1}
(noting that
\{v,u_{1}\}
is
\mathcal{F}
-good by definition of it being an edge in
G^{\prime}
), and given that we have already selected
v,u_{1},\ldots,u_{i-1}
with
i\leq s
such that
\{v,u_{1},\ldots,u_{i-1}\}
is
\mathcal{F}
-good, we next choose
u_{i}
to be any vertex in
N(u_{1})\cap N(v)\setminus\{u_{2},\ldots,u_{i-1}\}
such that
\{v,u_{1},\ldots,u_{i}\}
is
\mathcal{F}
-good.

Note that if
(S,v)
with
S=\{u_{1},\ldots,u_{s}\}
is the output of this procedure, then
(S,v)
is an element of
\mathcal{P}
by construction, so it remains to argue that there are many ways to complete this procedure. Observe that given
u_{1}\in V_{1},v\in V_{3}
, the number of choices for
u_{i}
with
i>1
in this algorithm is at least

\deg(u_{1},v)-(i-1)-\sum_{\nu\subseteq\{v,u_{1},\ldots,u_{i-1}\}}|\mathcal{J}(\nu)\cap(V_{2}\cap N(v))|,

where the
i-1
term denotes the number of choices
u_{i}=u_{j}
for some
j<i
, and the summation is an overestimate for the number of choices
u_{i}\in V_{2}\cup N(v)
which causes
\{v,u_{1},\ldots,u_{i}\}
to not be
\mathcal{F}
-good.

We aim to show that each term in the sum above is at most
2(s+t)\delta\ell^{-1}Kn^{1-2/s}
. Indeed, if
\nu
contains
v
and at least one vertex of
V_{1}\cup V_{2}
, then
\phi(\nu)=\delta\ell^{-1}Kn^{1-2/s}\cdot\phi(\nu\cup\{u_{i}\})\neq\infty
for all
u_{i}\in V_{2}
, so the result follows from
Claim

4.4
. Similarly if

then
\phi(\nu\cup\{u_{i}\})=\infty
for all

(since
\nu\cap V_{3}=\emptyset
), so

4.4
gives that this set is empty. The only remaining case is

. By definition of

, any

is such that

is
\mathcal{F}
-good, so
\mathcal{J}(\nu)\cap N(v)=\emptyset
in this case, again giving the result.

In total, we conclude that for

the number of choices for
u_{i}
in the algorithm is always at least

\deg(u_{1},v)-s-2^{s+t}\cdot 2(s+t)\delta\ell^{-1}Kn^{1-2/s}\geq\frac{1}{2}\deg(u_{1},v)

with the last step holding for
\delta
sufficiently small by (

). This in turn implies that the total number of ways of proceeding through the algorithm is at least

\displaystyle\sum_{uv\in E^{\prime}}\left(\frac{1}{2}\deg(u,v)\right)^{s-1}

\displaystyle\geq|E^{\prime}|\left(\frac{1}{2}|E^{\prime}|^{-1}\sum_{uv\in E^{\prime}}\deg(u,v)\right)^{s-1}

\displaystyle\geq\ell n^{2-1/s}\left(\frac{1}{2}\cdot\ell^{-1}n^{-2+1/s}\cdot\frac{1}{4}Kn^{3-3/s}(12\log n)^{16}\right)^{s-1},

where the first inequality used convexity and the second used (

) and

. This gives the desired result after noting that number of
distinct

(S,v)
pairs generated by the algorithm is at least the quantity above (i.e. the number of ways of terminating with the algorithm) divided by
s!\leq s^{s-1}
(since this is an upper bound for the number of ways a given pair
(S,v)
can be generated by this algorithm).
∎

A similar argument gives the following.

Claim 4.6
.

There exist
\Omega(\ell^{s-1}n^{s-1+1/s}(\ell^{3-2s}K^{s-1})^{t}(\log n)^{16(s-1)})

-good.

Proof.

Given a set

, define

noting that

\deg(S)\geq(8s)^{-s}\ell^{3-2s}K^{s-1}\geq 4t\textrm{ for }S\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}.

(16)

Also note that

(17)

since every

\displaystyle\geq|\mathcal{P}|-(8s)^{-s}\ell n^{2-1/s}|V_{1}|^{-1}(\ell^{-2}Kn^{1-2/s}|V_{1}|^{-1+1/s})^{s-1}\cdot|\mathcal{S}|

We will iteratively form our sets

5
Concluding Remarks

In this paper we proved a balanced supersaturation result for
K_{s,t}^{(3)}
, yielding improved ranges on the
r
for which we can give tight bounds for the random Turán number of expansions
K_{s,t}^{(r)}
. In particular, our methods yield an optimal dependency on
r
for
s=4
, and it is natural to try and extend this to other values of
s
. As discussed just after
Corollary

1.4
, the easiest
s
to solve this problem for are those of the form
1+{k\choose 2}
, motivating the following.

Problem 1
.

Establish tight bounds for
\mathrm{ex}(G_{n,p}^{r},K_{7,t}^{(r)})
whenever
r\geq 5
and
t
is sufficiently large.

Analogous to the methods used in this paper, the most natural approach for solving this problem would be to prove a balanced supersaturation result for
K_{s,t}^{(4)}
directly, after which one can use our lifting theorems to give strong bounds for all
r\geq 5
.

While the above is a natural direction to pursue, there are significant complications in going from uniformity 3 to uniformity 4 with our methods. Notably, our present proof for 3-uniform hypergraphs largely boiled down to using two distinct arguments based on whether our 3-graph
H
has a large or small number of edges in the 2-shadow
\partial^{2}H
relative to the number of edges in
H=\partial^{3}H
. For uniformity 4, one seemingly needs to consider now how the relative sizes of all the shadows
\partial^{k}H
for
2\leq k\leq 4
compare to each other, giving a much more complex set of possible parameters that one would have to build arguments around.

In
Proposition

1.5
we recorded a (non-balanced) supersaturation result for
K_{s,t}^{(3)}
. We claim without proof that one can easily lift this supersaturation result to all
r
-expansions of
K_{s,t}
using the methods from
[
19
]
to yield the following.

Proposition 5.1
.

For all
t\geq s\geq r
, there exists a sufficiently large constant
C
such that if
H
is an
n
-vertex
r
-graph with
m
edges such that

m\geq\max\{n^{r-1},~n^{r-\frac{r-1}{2}(3/s-1/st)}\}(\log n)^{C},

then
H
contains at least

\Omega^{*}(m^{st}n^{s+t-2st})

copies of
K^{(r)}_{s,t}
.

The count of
\Omega^{*}(m^{st}n^{s+t-2st})
here is tight whenever it applies. Moreover, the bound on
m
in
Proposition

5.1
is best possible whenever
n^{r-1}
achieves the maximum, which happens whenever
r\geq\frac{2st}{3t-1}+1\approx 2s/3
. In particular, it is plausible that improvements to the bound of
m
in
Proposition

5.1
for smaller values of
r
could lead towards improving our
r\approx 2s/3
bound for random Turán numbers of
K_{s,t}^{(r)}
in
Theorem

1.3
.

With an eye towards optimizing
Proposition

5.1
for smaller
r
, it is plausible that the true bound for
m
for any value of
r
should be

m=\Omega^{*}(\max\{n^{r-1},n^{r-{r\choose 2}/s}\}),

as this is the Turán number of
K_{s,t}^{(r)}
whenever
t
is sufficiently large in terms of
s
. Our inability to close this gap on
m
from
Proposition

5.1
even for the case
K_{s,t}^{(3)}
is closely related to the gap in the corresponding graph supersaturation problem stated in
[
5
]
, which asks to determine the number of copies of
K_{s,t}
guaranteed to exist in a graph with a given number of triangles.

We strongly believe that our methods used in this paper for
K_{s,t}^{(3)}
can be used to make additional progress on this corresponding graph supersaturation problem, but decided not to complicate this paper any further by including this. We note, however, that one can not use our main technical result
Proposition

2.2
as a blackbox towards this problem, as we required the graph used in this proposition to be the shadow of a nice hypergraph. As we noted in its proof, this shadow hypothesis is only needed to prove
Claim

4.3
, which itself is only needed to prove a
balanced
supersaturation result, so this in and of itself is not a significant obstacle to the problem. There are a few other additional complications that arise when trying to shift our approach to the graph setting, though again we do not believe any of them should seriously impede our methods going through in this setting.
