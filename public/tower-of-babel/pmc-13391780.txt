A temporal deep learning algorithm for prediction of extubation failures in critical care patients
Authors: Jiawei Shen, Shengye Lu, Yaqin Wu, Lihong Liu, Yue Wang, Youzhong An, Fengxue Zhu, Buzhou Tang, Huiying Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Extubation failure in ICU patients is associated with poor outcomes. Existing prediction models often rely on static data, missing dynamic disease fluctuations. This study introduces TrAcE, a deep learning-based model integrating static and temporal data for improved extubation failure prediction with explainable results. The model was trained and validated using MIMIC-III (Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care-III) data and tested on the LOCAL-Ext (a local database for extubation) dataset. A Transformer-based neural network with temporal fusion was used to screen extubation records (Patients planned for post-extubation non-invasive ventilation or tracheostomy were excluded). Model performance was assessed using AUROC (area under the receiver operating curve) and AUPRC (area under the precision recall curve). Explainability was ensured via Captum’s occlusion method, identifying feature attributions at both population and individual levels. TrAcE’s extubation timing was compared to the spontaneous breathing test (SBT) in LOCAL-Ext. From MIMIC-III, 5,895 patients (4,126 for training, 1,729 for validation) were selected. LOCAL-Ext included 6,765 test patients. TrAcE outperformed other models, achieving AUROCs of 0.823 (95% CI: 0.808—0.838) in validation and 0.859 (95% CI: 0.836—0.881) in testing, with favorable AUPRCs (0.734 and 0.757, respectively). Compared to SBT in LOCAL-Ext, TrAcE identified suitable extubation days earlier (4.32 [IQR 0—20] vs. 5.65 [IQR 0—23], p < 0.01). In the present study, we developed a deep learning model (TrAcE) for predicting extubation failure in critical care patients. This model can process both static and dynamic data, provides risk prediction and explainability in real-time. Furthermore, it demonstrated an advantage over SBT procedures in promptly identifying patients ready for extubation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s10877-026-01439-7.

Background

As a life-saving method for the critically ill, invasive mechanical ventilation (IMV) is widely used in intensive care units (ICU). Studies reported that 30—70% of ICU patients received IMV [1–3]. Despite its essential rule in ventilation support, prolonged IMV was associated with several complications, including ventilator associated pneumonia [4], ventilator induced lung injuries [5] and ICU acquired weakness [6], etc. Thus, timely weaning from IMV and successful extubation are of great importance in ICU patients.

On the other hand, there was no golden standard to guide extubation. Protocols of IMV weaning and extubation vary between institutions. Spontaneous breathing trial(SBT) was usually performed to evaluate the likelihood of successful extubation, but 10—30% patients who have passed the SBT will eventually need reintubation [7–9]. Failure of extubation was reported to be related to prolonged ICU stay and a higher mortality rate [10, 11].

Since the underlying mechanisms of extubation failure were multifactorial [12], building prediction models will facilitate risk evaluation before extubation. However, most models were derived from static data [13–15], which did not incorporate the temporal nature of patients’ status. As for ICU patients that are supported with IMV, rapid fluctuations of disease severity can affect the result of extubation. Therefore, a comprehensive algorithm that combines both the static and temporal status of IMV-supported patients is necessary for developing a prediction model that is more reflective of clinical reality. In few studies that utilized dynamic data, the necessary testing of the models in external data sets were missing. Also, no real-time explanation of features was provided, which resulting in the lack of information for interventions to risk factors. As the explainability (the capacity to use specific approaches to identify and quantify the contribution of particular input characteristics (e.g., vital signs, labs) to the model’s risk prediction) of models is gaining more attention in the field of medicine [16, 17], an explainable model for extubation failure may provide valuable information for clinical decisions and improve the outcome of extubation.

In this paper, by using a deep learning algorithm that processes both static and dynamic data, we aim to develop and validate an explainable prediction model of extubation failure in ICU patients that are supported with IMV- the TrAcE (A Temporal Deep Learning Algorithm for Prediction of Extubation Failures) model.

Methods

Databases and patient selection

Two databases were used in this study (Fig. 1a), The MIMIC-III (Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care- III) dataset, a public database that contained the multi-dimensional data of critical care patients in Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center, were used for model training and validation. The model was later tested in an external database– the LOCAL-Ext database to test its performance and compare with the spontaneous breathing test in a specified patient group. In the two databases, we screened all extubation records and excluded patients with 1) previous extubation records in this hospitalization, 2) age < 18 years or age > 89 years, 3) planned non-invasive ventilation (NIV) or tracheostomy after extubation (i.e., NIV applied within 12 h after extubation, tracheostomy before extubaton), 4) death or being transferred out of ICU before first extubation. Exudation failure was defined as reintubation within 48 h extubation. Records from MIMIC-III database were divided into training set and validation set at a ratio of 7:3. The LOCAL-Ext database was used as the test set. For collected patients, we analyzed all the static features (demographic features like age, gender, etc.) and dynamic features (laboratory and ICU records including medication, vital signs, ventilator setting and measurements, all marked with timestamps), then excluded features that were not mutually accessible in the two data sets.

Feature selection

For collected patients, we analyzed static features (demographic features like age, gender, etc.) and dynamic features (laboratory and ICU records including medication, vital signs, ventilator setting and measurements, all marked with timestamps) and excluded features that were not mutually accessible in the two data sets. There were 40 dynamic features and 8 static features that collected in the study (Appendix 1). Before feature extraction, we have explored the two data base and make sure the avaliablility of the features were equal between databases. And after extraction, the units of features were harmonized to the same (e.g., converting temperature from Fahrenheit to Celsius, ensuring creatine units were umol/L) to ensure the 40 dynamic and 8 static features were semantically and numerically consistent across MIMIC-III and LOCAL-Ext. We use down-sampling to process data that sampled more than 1 time per hour (e.g., heart rate, respiratory rates), thus generating features (median, minimum and max value of the original feature) that were more representative, at the same time control the noise from these data. And using up-sampling to impute data that were sampled less than 1 time per hour (e.g., creatine, lactate). The forward fill method was applied for data imputation in up-sampling, by replace the missing values with most recent sampled values of the features.

Development of TrAcE model

To predict extubation failure risk, we applied a Transformer-based neural network with temporal fusion which integrate two key data sources: 1) dynamic features: patient-specific data collected from the onset of intubation to the prediction time, and 2) static features: demographic features. These inputs were combined into a vectorized representation, facilitating the risk prediction process. The model workflow comprises four main stages: Embedding, Measurement Sequence Modeling, Temporal Fusion, and Prediction (Fig. 1b). Overfitting was controlled using dropout layers and early stopping based on validation loss. Hyperparameters were tuned using grid search on the validation set. See Appendix 2 for the detailed methodology of model building. Training was performed on two NVIDIA RTX 4090 with PyTorch. The total batch size was 64. Training converged within 50 epochs (for approximately 3 h).

Evaluation of model performance

Using the validation set from MIMIC-III and test set of LOCAL-Ext, the performance of TrAcE were first compared with other models (including decision tree, random forest, support vector machine, eXtreme Gradient Boosting, logistic regression, multilayer perception, K- nearest neighbors and Recurrent neural network) with Area Under the Receiver Operator Curve (AUROC) and Area Under the Precision- Recall Curve (AUPRC) as the evaluation metrics. To test the stability of the TrAcE model over time, we further investigated time-dependent AUROC in the validation and test sets. We also evaluated the calibration of the model in the two data sets. To investigate the necessity of the long-range temporal input (Full ventilation history vs. restricted time before extubation), an ablation study was performed restricting the dynamic feature input to strictly the last 30 h prior to extubation. Similarly, conducted an additional benchmark experiment comparing our temporal TrAcE model (temporal) against several static, cross-sectional models (XGBoost, Logistic regression or rapid shallow breathing index) evaluated immediately prior to extubation.

Model explainability

The Captum library was applied to obtain the explainability for the TrAcE model. Specifically, the attribution of features to the final prediction were calculated with the occlusion method [18], which was a perturbation-based method for calculating attribution. It involves substituting feature related value with a designated baseline or reference, then assessing the change in prediction output. The attribution of features was presented on both the population and case levels in a time-dependent manner.

Comparison to Spontaneous Breathing Test in the LOCAL-Ext dataset

We compared the duration of mechanical ventilation between two extubation strategies: one guided by the TrAcE model and the other by SBT alone. For patients in the LOCAL-Ext test set who underwent SBT, we analyzed their time-serial data using the TrAcE model to determine the earliest possible day for extubation. We then compared the extubation day recommendations by the TrAcE model with those suggested by SBT alone. In detail, we identified the first day the TrAcE model’s predicted risk dropped below our optimal safety threshold (using Youden Index- derived best cutoff in validation set), and compared that specific day to the chronological day the clinical team successfully passed the patient on an SBT.

Model explainability

If we interrogate the integrated influences of dynamic features to the prediction of the test set over the first 72 h of IMV support (Fig. 5a, focusing on percent of attributions), we can notice that features may have both positive and negative attributions to the model output. (except for the ventilation time, which was continuously attributed to a higher risk of extubation failure, and the effect was evolving over time). The swinging of the attribution directions of features may be neglected with a single cumulative view of dynamic features (Appendix 9). For example, the cumulative attribution of fluid balance and mean arterial pleasure were towards higher risk of extubation failure. But actually, the directions of attribution changes in the course of ventilation. In general, tidal volume, SpO2, lymphocyte count and Glasgow coma scale were attributed to a lower risk of extubation failure. While respiratory rate, PEEP and heart rate, etc. were attributed to a higher risk. TrAcE model versus SBT

From the LOCAL-Ext database, we identified 451 patients that have been evaluated with SBT, TrAcE model was applied to find the earliest extubation day (with best cutoff FPR 0.25 from validation set). When compared to the result of extubation, TrAcE achieved a better performance than SBT (Fig. 7a and 7b, AUROC 0.748 vs 0.652, AUPRC 0.691 vs 0.497). The days predicted by TrAcE for extubation were statistically earlier than the days predicted by SBT (Fig. 7c, 4.32[IQR 0—20] vs 5.65[IQR 0—23], P < 0.01), and this trend was more obvious in patients who had been through three or more attempts at SBT. Noted that 34(37.78% of SBT failure) patients failed the SBT test and extubated successfully, among them, 26 patients were identified by TrAcE as suitable for extubation. Discussion

In this study, we developed a deep learning time series prediction model (TrAcE) for predicting extubation failure in ICU patients that was supported with invasive mechanical ventilation. By processing both the static and dynamic features, the TrAcE model provides real-time risk prediction and individualized risk feature presentations for IMV supported patients. It showed good performance in both the validation set and the external test set. TrAcE demonstrated an advantage over SBT procedures in the test set in the early identification of patients who were prepared for extubation, potentially lowering the risk of complications associated with the ventilator.

Our model provided a time-sensitive method for model explainability, we noticed in this study that attributions of features to the extubation result may swing between positive and negative directions as ventilation time evolves, one may neglect this phenomenon when only looking at the cumulative result. For models [14, 15, 19–22] that only extract features at single time point, the risk of extubation failure could be miscalculated. Another interesting phenomenon was that only the weight of features in the last 30 h before extubation had significant influence on the result, indicating that the evolving of disease status before extubation had more effect on the extubation result than events that occurred a long time ago. while the full model is optimal, a 30-h restricted model is highly viable for clinical environments with limited computational resources (Appendix 7).

Although the specific strategies may vary, SBT was recommended by various guidelines as a necessary evaluation procedure before extubation [23–25]. However, 15% of patients who passed SBT may still experience extubation failure [26], which was similar to our findings. It is interesting that highly weighted features in TrAcE model (tidal volume, respiratory rate, heart rate) were also criteria for SBT termination. But when compared to SBT in retrospective data analysis, we observed a potential advantage of early extubation for TrAcE guided weaning. A possible explanation is that TrAcE focused on up to the last 30 h before extubation, while SBT prioritized parameters at the time of extubation. The extended duration between meeting extubation requirements and the actual extubation attempt has been linked to extubation failures [27], while earlier extubation can avoid IMV- associated complications (ventilator associated pneumonia, ventilator induced lung injury, etc.). Segal et,al. reported that the trend of rapid shallow index during SBT performed better than a single Rapid shallow breathing index(RSBI) value [28]. This suggests that the advancement of the patient’s condition and characteristic patterns before extubation could act as crucial guides during the weaning procedure. When evaluated at the optimal threshold (FPR = 0.25) in validation cohort, The TrAcE model exhibits a more balanced performance at the optimal cutoff point: a PPV close to 50% while maintaining an extremely high NPV (> 92%). This makes the model more practical in clinical decision support, especially in scenarios of extubation- where a balance needs to be struck between the risk of reintubation against the benefits of early weaning. In future prospective studies, intensivists could applicate a hybrid protocol: TrAcE assesses readiness first; if its high, SBT is performed; if SBT is marginal, TrAcE trajectory (improving vs. worsening) could guides the final decision.

The ability of time series data and deep learning models to forecast extubation failure has been examined in earlier research. Zeng et,al. applied recurrent neural networks in MIMIC-IV data base [29], Jia et,al. used convolutional neural networks for the same task in MIMIC-III [15]. Because the two studies only examined data from publicly available datasets and did not test them on external datasets, there was a chance that the model overfitting. Additionally, data were collected with a limited time density [29] or limited number of features [15]. The present study was developed with highest time density of 1 min and was tested in an extra data set to reflect the evolution of the patient’s condition and avoid overfitting. Besides, our transformer based model applied self-attention enables the model to capture long-range relationships without the vanishing gradient problem characteristic of RNNs. It manages the irregular time intervals of ICU data more well than fixed-window CNNs by employing timestamp embeddings. We also proved the advantage of TrAcE when compared of cross-sectional models use only static features (Appendix 8), The stepwise improvement from RSBI to logistic regression to XGBoost to TrAcE follows the progression from clinical observation to static machine learning to temporal deep learning. This gradient suggests that predictive gain derives not from feature richness alone but from the dynamic trajectory of physiological variables over time.

While TrAcE demonstrates robust statistical performance, successful ICU deployment requires clear operational boundaries to ensure clinical trust and patient safety. We emphasize that TrAcE is designed strictly as an adjunctive decision-support tool, not an autonomous guidance system. In a real-world clinical workflow, we envision the model functioning as a continuous "background monitor." Rather than dictating care, a high probability of extubation success generated by TrAcE serves as an early-warning trigger, prompting the critical care team to initiate formal clinical readiness assessments, such as an SBT. Furthermore, to safeguard against algorithmic over-reliance (automation bias), final extubation decisions must universally remain with the attending physician. When model output conflicts with bedside findings not captured by the algorithm- poor secretion clearance, weak cough strength, acute agitation, physician override is the expected and protocol-mandated response [30]. TrAcE augments bedside assessment, it does not replace it.

Our study also has several limitations. First, the model did not incorporate radiomic or many respiratory physiological data, which were reported as important predictors of extubation result [31, 32]. These features were not recorded by the training and test data sets, and could provide extra information in a more comprehensive data set. Also, we did not exclude patients with a limited time of mechanical ventilation (less than 24 h) from the data analysis. Excluding cases with limited ventilation time can reduce patient heterogeneity, as patients with longer mechanical ventilation are more likely to experience extubation failure. However, patients with short duration of IMV support (e.g., surgical patients) may also experience extubation failure [33, 34], same underlying risk factors can exist in both long- term and short- term ventilated patients [35]. Although we explored the explainability of the model, and reported the dynamic change of different features, not all the features (weight, lymphocyte count, etc.) can be intervened in clinical practice. Although this study used the occlusion method to evaluate the contribution of clinical features, due to the frequent multicollinearity among clinical indicators (such as various vital signs), the explanatory scores may be distributed among correlated features. While this dispersion of attribution might weaken the explanatory weight of individual indicators, our analysis focuses on the consistent trends and temporal directionality of risk evolution. Future research may utilize advanced attribution algorithms to more effectively disentangle the impacts of highly correlated features. Furthermore, the occlusion method used for model explainability is sensitive to multicollinearity. Because ICU variables (e.g., heart rate and respiratory rate) are often highly correlated, the specific attribution weights assigned to individual features may fluctuate. However, as demonstrated by our bootstrap stability analysis (Appendix 11), the combined directional risk trajectory of these correlated clusters remains highly stable. Thus, while the exact weight of a single parameter may vary, the model’s overall clinical risk assessment remains reliable. Future research should explore advanced attribution algorithms to better disentangle highly correlated features.

Furthermore, we are unable to assess the extent to which clinicians will utilize the model’s information to direct the weaning process and adjust treatments. Thus, a prospective study is still necessary to test the performance of the model in the real world. Finally, patients with planned NIV support and tracheostomy were excluded from the study. NIV support have been reported to greatly increase the success rate of extubation, especially for obese patients [36], while tracheostomy yields comparable results in patients with diminished consciousness [37]. However, the two modalities were only used in part of the patients and will affect the predictive power of the model.

Conclusions

In the present study, we developed a deep learning model (TrAcE) for predicting extubation failure in critical care patients. This model can process both static and dynamic data, provides risk prediction and explainability in real-time. Furthermore, it demonstrated an advantage over SBT procedures in promptly identifying patients ready for extubation.
