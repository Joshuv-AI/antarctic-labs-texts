Multifocal osseous and cutaneous Rosai-Dorfman-Destombes disease presenting as an aggressive intramedullary femoral lesion
Authors: Khaled W Aldubaiyan, Haniyya A AlRehaily, Mohammed A AlJarie, Omar A Alabdulkareem, Abdullah M Alzahrani, Abdulrahman A Aldahlawi, Abdulrahman O Alhumaydhi, Mohammed J Alsalman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRosai-Dorfman-Destombes disease (RDD) is a rare histiocytic disorder characterized by the accumulation of abnormal histiocytes in nodal and extranodal tissues. We report a 24-year-old male presenting with progressive right distal thigh pain for 1 month. MRI demonstrated a 3.5 cm intramedullary lesion in the distal right femoral metadiaphysis with minimal periosteal edema, raising concern for malignancy. Staging CT scans showed no evidence of metastatic disease. Biopsy of this lesion revealed the characteristic accumulation of CD1a-negative and S100-positive histiocytes with emperipolesis, consistent with RDD. Subsequently, PET/CT scan demonstrated multifocal osseous disease involving at least 4 sites, involving the distal right femur (SUVmax 9.0), left humerus (SUVmax 9.7), L4 vertebra, and sacrum, along with 2 cutaneous lesions and possible tonsillar and cervical lymph node involvement. The patient was initiated on the MEK inhibitor cobimetinib and follow-up PET/CT demonstrated complete metabolic resolution of the previously identified osseous disease after approximately 3 months. RDD should be considered in the differential diagnosis when imaging demonstrates concerning osseous lesions in young adults, as definitive diagnosis relies on histopathological evaluation.Keywords: Rosai-Dorfman-Destombes disease, Histiocytosis, Bone tumor mimic, MRI, PET/CT, Extranodal histiocytosis

Introduction

Rosai-Dorfman-Destombes disease (RDD) is an uncommon non-Langerhans cell histiocytosis characterized by the abnormal accumulation of S100-positive and CD1a-negative histiocytes in nodal and extranodal organs [1]. The Histiocyte Society classified RDD within the “R group” of histiocytoses [2]. More recently, due to the discovery of recurrent MAPK-pathway mutations, the fifth edition of the World Health Organization classification of hematolymphoid tumors listed RDD under histiocyte/macrophage neoplasms [3]. The clinical features of RDD depend on the location and the number of affected tissues, commonly affecting children and young males [4,5]. It is classically described as presenting with bilateral, painless cervical lymphadenopathy, which may be associated with constitutional symptoms such as fever and night sweats; however, extranodal tissues are reported to be affected in 43% of cases [4,5]. Examples of tissues that can be affected in RDD include the skin, the orbit and nasal cavity, intrathoracic and abdominal organs, and osseous structures [1,6,7]. In this case report, we present a male patient with multifocal RDD involving osseous and cutaneous structures.

Case presentation

History of present illness

A 24-years-old male with no significant past medical history presented with 4 weeks of right distal thigh pain. The symptoms began in mid-October, presenting as persistent progressive bone pain in the distal thigh just above the knee, which worsened with movements and caused partial limitation of activity, but his functional status was normal (ECOG 0) aside from the pain and a subjective fever lasting 1 month, but without night sweats. He was evaluated for the pain at an outside institution, where an MRI was done, and it raised concern as it showed a distal femoral lesion with bone marrow edema and periosteal reaction, which was concerning for an aggressive lesion. These findings, together with the patient's progressive focal bone pain, suggested an aggressive primary bone lesion or malignancy mimic. A bone scan was done, and it showed a solitary focal abnormal tracer uptake at the distal metaphysis of the right femur with no other uptake. A chest CT was performed, and it was negative for pulmonary metastasis. He then presented to our center for further evaluation and investigations.

Investigations and diagnosis

At our center, an MRI of the right femur (Philips Ingenia Ambition 1.5T) was performed using coronal T2 fat-saturated, coronal T1, and axial T1 fat-saturated post-contrast sequences (Fig. 1). It demonstrated a well-defined intramedullary lesion at the distal femoral metadiaphysis (medial aspect), measuring ∼3.5 cm, with T2 hyperintense and T1 hypointense signal. The lesion showed mild post-contrast enhancement. Minimal surrounding bone marrow edema was present. No periosteal reaction was identified. Cortical integrity was preserved. No extraosseous soft-tissue component was identified. The intramedullary location, post-contrast enhancement, and previously reported marrow edema and periosteal reaction on the outside MRI raised concern for an aggressive marrow-replacing lesion, including primary bone tumor or lymphoma. A staging CT of the chest, abdomen, and pelvis was done, and it showed no evidence of either intrathoracic or intra-abdominal metastases with no significant lymphadenopathy. Subsequently, CT-guided biopsy was performed on the distal right femoral metadiaphysis lesion in November 2025. Histopathology was consistent with extranodal RDD. The biopsy showed emperipolesis, with intact lymphocytes, plasma cells, and neutrophils present within the cytoplasm of lesional histiocytes (Fig. 4). There were no Touton-type giant cells, atypia, brisk mitotic activity, or necrosis. Immunohistochemistry showed that the lesional histiocytes were diffusely positive for S100 (Fig. 5), OCT-2, Cyclin-D1, and BCL-2, and negative for CD1a (Fig. 6) and Langerin. BRAF-V600E immunohistochemistry was inconclusive. After the biopsy, baseline FDG PET/CT was performed in December 2025 (Fig. 2), demonstrating multifocal osseous disease. The osseous lesions included the left side of the L4 vertebra with SUVmax 6.3, and a mixed sclerotic-lytic CT correlate; the posterior compartment of the upper sacrum (S2/S3) with SUVmax 5.3, with corresponding CT abnormality identified, consistent with marrow-based disease below the CT detection threshold; the midshaft of the left humerus with SUVmax 9.7, with no corresponding CT abnormality identified, consistent with marrow-based disease below the CT detection threshold; and the distal metadiaphysis of the right femur with SUVmax 9 and a mixed sclerotic-lytic CT correlate. Two hypermetabolic cutaneous foci were detected in the right gluteal region with SUVmax 4, and lower abdominal wall with SUVmax 6.2, and were interpreted as probable cutaneous involvement; no histologic confirmation was available for these lesions. Symmetrical tonsillar uptake was also noted with mild to moderate FDG-avid upper cervical lymph node involvement. These findings were not biopsied. They were initially favored to be inflammatory; however, early disease involvement could not be excluded, and neither visceral disease nor hypermetabolic lymphadenopathy elsewhere was identified. On follow-up, the tonsillar and cervical nodal uptake was favored to be inflammatory/reactive, supported by the patient's sore throat at the time of the PET/CT.

Management

Following the diagnosis of extranodal RDD and demonstration of multifocal osseous disease on PET/CT, the patient underwent evaluation at the histiocytosis clinic. Family history was notable for a mother with rheumatoid arthritis, 2 cousins who died from acute myeloid leukemia (AML), and one relative who was reported to have possible Erdheim-Chester disease. A systemic workup was done, including bone marrow evaluation, which showed no evidence of RDD with an overall cellularity of approximately 50%. BRAF-V600E immunohistochemistry was inconclusive, and next-generation sequencing was unsuccessful; therefore, mutation status remained undetermined. Due to the extent of disease and the established role of MAPK-pathway activation in RDD, MEK inhibition was recommended by the histiocytosis clinic. After the December 2025 histiocytosis clinic visit, the patient was initiated on cobimetinib 40 mg orally once daily, given for 21 days of each cycle. Baseline monitoring included ophthalmologic and cardiac evaluation. Ophthalmologic assessment was unremarkable, with OCT within normal limits.

Follow-up and outcome

Follow-up FDG PET/CT was performed in March 2026, approximately 3 months after starting cobimetinib. The scan showed complete metabolic resolution of the previously identified bone and bone marrow lesions, as well as the PET-avid cutaneous foci, with no concerning hypermetabolic lesions identified (Fig. 3). Persistent tonsillar activity with small, stable, bilateral deep cervical lymph nodes was interpreted as presumably reactive. The patient remained clinically stable on cobimetinib, with mild muscular pain as the only reported adverse effect and stable laboratory parameters. No formal pain score was documented.

Discussion

RDD is a rare histiocytic disorder that commonly affects children and young adults. Classically, it manifests as severe bilateral cervical lymph nodes enlargement accompanied by fever, night sweats, and weight loss [6]. However, RDD is not limited to the lymphatic system, and it can basically affect any organ. The most common sites involved are skin, upper respiratory tract, and bone. Osseous involvement occurs in nearly 5%-10% of cases, however, isolated bony involvement is rarely seen [5]. We report a case of femoral RDD, presenting with bone pain in a 24-years-old male patient. Additionally, the rarity of femoral involvement made the presentation a diagnostic challenge, and malignancy was initially suspected.

Diagnosing bony RDD remains a challenge due to the nonspecific clinical presentation and imaging findings. Typically, osseous lesions involve metaphysis or diaphysis, are osteolytic or mixed lytic/sclerotic, and have well-defined margins with a narrow zone of transition. Differential diagnoses of such cases include chronic osteomyelitis, lymphoma, and Ewing sarcoma [1]. Ewing sarcoma was considered given the patient's age and metadiaphyseal location within the distal femur, which is a classic site for Ewing sarcoma in young adults [8]. The outside MRI's report of bone marrow edema and periosteal reaction raised concern for an aggressive primary malignancy, since both suggest active cortical and periosteal response to an expanding intramedullary process. However, our MRI provided critical findings that argued strongly against Ewing sarcoma: the lesion demonstrated well-defined rather than permeative margins, preserved cortical integrity without focal destruction or cortical breaching, and no periosteal reaction of any type, in contrast to the outside report. Additionally, there was no substantial extraosseous soft-tissue mass, which is a feature frequently present in Ewing sarcoma at diagnosis [8]. Lymphoma was also considered, given the patient's subjective fever and a marrow-based lesion demonstrating post-contrast enhancement. Lymphoma of the bone characteristically produces marrow infiltration with disproportionately preserved cortical architecture [9]. Yet, several features argued against it: the lesion was focal rather than diffusely infiltrative, there was no soft-tissue mass disproportionate to the osseous involvement, and staging CT of the chest, abdomen, and pelvis showed no lymphadenopathy or visceral disease. Osteomyelitis was considered given the subacute pain and single febrile episode. Nevertheless, the absence of cortical remodeling, Brodie’s abscess, sequestrum, or sinus tract, which are classic signs of chronic osteomyelitis [10], significantly reduced its likelihood. On our own MRI, the main concerning feature was the lesion's post-contrast enhancement, subsequently mirrored by marked FDG avidity, despite otherwise non-aggressive morphology. In retrospect, the metadiaphyseal location and the mixed lytic-sclerotic pattern subsequently demonstrated on the CT component of PET/CT were consistent with previously reported osseous RDD [1]. Notably, however, the lesion lacked the cortical destruction and extraosseous soft-tissue component described in a substantial proportion of cases [11], illustrating that osseous RDD spans a broad imaging spectrum.

CT guided biopsy of the distal right femoral lesion was done. It showed intact lymphocytes, plasma cells, and neutrophils were within the cytoplasm of the lesional histiocytes, consistent with emperipolesis, the histologic hallmark of RDD [12]. There was no atypia, brisk mitotic activity, or necrosis, features that would have raised concern for a malignancy. Immunohistochemically, the lesional histiocytes were diffusely positive for S100, OCT-2, Cyclin-D1, and BCL-2, and negative for CD1a and Langerin. Moreover, CD68 and CD163 were not performed. Therefore, the diagnosis rested on the combination of emperipolesis with S100 positivity and CD1a and Langerin negativity, supported by cyclin D1 positivity, a recognized feature of RDD [12,13].

Langerhans cell histiocytosis (LCH) was plausible on imaging grounds due to the patient's age and a solitary lytic long bone lesion and could not be reliably distinguished from RDD radiographically. Therefore, exclusion depended on the immunophenotype described above, since CD1a and Langerin are positive in LCH [13]. Erdheim-Chester disease (ECD) was raised by the patient's family history, however, argued against on both grounds: ECD characteristically produces bilateral symmetric cortical osteosclerosis of the diaphyseal and metaphyseal parts of the long bones [14], while our patient's disease was unilateral, with an initial bone scan demonstrating solitary uptake at the distal right femur. Histologically, the absence of Touton-type giant cells and the strong S100 positivity of the lesional histiocytes further favored RDD over ECD, which is typically S100-negative and CD68-positive [14].

Regarding molecular testing, BRAF V600E immunohistochemistry was inconclusive, and next-generation sequencing, which included BRAF, was technically unsuccessful, leaving the mutational status of the lesion undetermined. Consequently, cobimetinib was initiated because of the disease extent and the established role of MAPK-pathway activation in RDD [15]. The patient was initiated on cobimetinib 40 mg orally once daily, given for 21 days of each cycle, with baseline ophthalmologic assessment and cardiac monitoring. Treatment was well tolerated, with mild myalgia the only reported adverse effect and stable laboratory parameters throughout. Follow-up FDG PET/CT (Fig. 3) was performed in March 2026, and it demonstrated complete metabolic resolution of the osseous and cutaneous disease.

In 2017, Mosheimer et al. done a systematic review on bone involvement in RDD. The authors identified 88 English-language reports (2000-April 2015) totaling 108 patients with bone involvement (55.1% female; mean age 31.1 ± 19.8 years), of whom 74.4% were classified as primary osseous RDD and 97% were symptomatic. They also mentioned that most had one (71.0%) or two (23.4%) bone regions involved; the most frequent bone sites were cranium (30.6%), facial bones (22.2%), tibia (17.6%), and femur (12%) (and imaging-when described-showed lytic lesions in 53 patients and mixed lytic-sclerotic lesions in 12 (only one purely sclerotic case) [16].

In conclusion, primary osseous RDD involving the femur is an uncommon presentation that can closely mimic malignant bone tumors on clinical and radiologic assessment. Therefore, definitive diagnosis relies on histopathology and immunohistochemistry, with emperipolesis and supportive markers such as S100, OCT-2, and Cyclin D1 helping to confirm the diagnosis. Management should be individualized based on symptoms and disease extent, and targeted therapy such as the MEK inhibitor cobimetinib may represent a useful option in selected patients.
