Cardiorenal Mortality and Safety Outcomes of GLP‐1 Receptor Agonists in Type 2 Diabetes With BMI Below 27 kg/m2: A Target Trial Emulation
Authors: Shao‐Chia Chen, Yu‐Nan Huang, Pei‐Yun Li, Jo‐Ching Chen, Gideon Meyerowitz‐Katz, Shun‐Fa Yang, Pen‐Hua Su
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimsCardiorenal evidence for glucagon‐like peptide‐1 receptor agonists (GLP‐1 RAs) derives mainly from trials in overweight or obesity; data in adults with Type 2 diabetes (T2D) and body mass index (BMI) below 27 kg/m2 are limited.Materials and MethodsWe emulated a target trial in the TriNetX US Collaborative Network (index dates 2020 to 2026; maximum follow‐up 24 months). Adults with T2D and BMI < 27 kg/m2 newly initiating non‐tirzepatide GLP‐1 RAs were compared with new users of dipeptidyl peptidase‐4 inhibitors (DPP‐4i), sodium–glucose cotransporter‐2 inhibitors (SGLT2i) or usual care. Comparisons used separate 1:1 propensity score matching. The primary outcome was all‐cause mortality.ResultsAfter matching, 7389, 8969 and 8610 GLP‐1 RA users were matched to DPP‐4i, SGLT2i and usual care. Versus DPP‐4i, GLP‐1 RA was associated with lower all‐cause mortality (hazard ratio [HR] 0.61; 95% confidence interval [CI] 0.52–0.71), major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE; HR 0.83; 95% CI 0.71–0.98) and coded heart failure (HR 0.71; 95% CI 0.60–0.85). Versus SGLT2i, MACE (HR 0.81; 95% CI 0.69–0.95) and coded heart failure (HR 0.56; 95% CI 0.47–0.65) were lower, but all‐cause mortality was not (HR 0.89; 95% CI 0.76–1.05). Gastroparesis was more frequent versus DPP‐4i.ConclusionIn people with T2D and BMI < 27 kg/m2, GLP‐1 RA use was associated with lower coded heart failure risk across all three comparisons, the most consistent finding. The lower all‐cause mortality association versus DPP‐4i and usual care, absent versus SGLT2i, should be interpreted cautiously as likely reflecting residual confounding rather than a benefit of this magnitude.

Introduction

Glucagon‐like peptide‐1 receptor agonists (GLP‐1 RAs) have become a central component of Type 2 diabetes (T2D) and obesity management [1, 2, 3]. Large cardiovascular outcome trials (CVOTs) demonstrated that liraglutide, semaglutide and dulaglutide were each associated with lower risks of major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) in patients with T2D and high cardiovascular risk [4, 5, 6]. A pooled analysis of these trials confirmed a 12%–14% relative risk reduction in MACE across the GLP‐1 RA class [7, 8]. More recently, the FLOW trial showed that semaglutide was associated with a 24% lower risk of kidney disease progression in patients with T2D and chronic kidney disease [9], and tirzepatide, a dual GIP/GLP‐1 RA, was approved for glycemic control in T2D on the basis of the SURPASS programme [10, 11]. These findings have expanded the therapeutic role of incretin‐based agents.

However, the cardiorenal benefits summarised above were derived largely from trial populations with mean baseline body mass index (BMI) generally around or above 32 kg/m2 [8]. SELECT required BMI ≥ 27 kg/m2, and the STEP programme likewise enrolled adults with overweight or obesity [12, 13]. Although SURPASS was a diabetes programme rather than an obesity trial, participants also had relatively high baseline BMI [11]. Thus, current cardiorenal use of GLP‐1 RA is supported mainly by evidence from populations with greater adiposity than the lower‐BMI adults with T2D. A recent multinational cohort study further suggested BMI‐dependent heterogeneity in cardiovascular benefit, whereas kidney protection appeared more consistent across BMI strata [14, 15].

This evidence gap is clinically relevant. T2D at lower BMI is not uncommon: approximately one in six people with T2D has a BMI below 27 kg/m2 at diagnosis [16] and 23% to 27% of US adults with diabetes were below this threshold in survey data from 1999 to 2004 [17]. Lower‐BMI T2D is increasingly recognised as a clinically important phenotype, with a greater contribution from β‐cell dysfunction than in obesity‐associated disease [18]. Normal‐weight diabetes has also been associated with higher mortality than diabetes accompanied by overweight or obesity [19]. BMI 27 kg/m2 is additionally important in the United States because semaglutide 2.4 mg is approved for chronic weight management only in adults with obesity or in those with overweight plus a weight‐related comorbidity [12]. Safety considerations may also differ in this subgroup, as GLP‐1 RAs produce substantial weight loss and reduce lean mass as well as fat mass, raising concern about underweight, sarcopenia, frailty and nutritional compromise in patients with lower BMI [20, 21].

To address these gaps, we used a target trial emulation framework [22] with propensity score matching to compare clinical outcomes among new users of GLP‐1 RAs versus three comparator groups: dipeptidyl peptidase‐4 inhibitors (DPP‐4i), sodium–glucose cotransporter‐2 inhibitors (SGLT2i) and usual care, in adults with T2D and BMI below 27 kg/m2. We examined cardiovascular, renal and mortality outcomes, along with weight‐related safety endpoints including incident underweight and sarcopenia.

Methods

Ethics, Data Source and Reporting

The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Chung Shan Medical University Hospital (CS2‐24004); informed consent was waived because TriNetX provides only de‐identified, aggregate data meeting HIPAA Safe Harbor standards. We used the TriNetX US Collaborative Network, a federated platform aggregating longitudinal electronic health records from 67 US healthcare organisations. Data were extracted in February 2026 for the main analyses and in June 2026 for the analyses added during revision, and reporting followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (RECORD) guidelines.

Target Trial Emulation and Study Population

We emulated parallel pragmatic trials comparing new use of a GLP‐1 RA (excluding tirzepatide) with new use of (i) a DPP‐4i, (ii) a sodium–glucose cotransporter‐2 inhibitor (SGLT2i) or (iii) usual care, defined as metformin and/or sulfonylurea without any GLP‐1 RA, DPP‐4i or SGLT2i. Tirzepatide was evaluated separately in exploratory analyses. Each comparison was analysed as an independent emulated trial with its own matched cohort; the seven protocol components (eligibility, treatment strategies, assignment, follow‐up, outcomes, contrasts and analysis plan) are specified in Table S1 [23, 24, 25]. A directed acyclic graph guided covariate selection (Figure S1) [26].

Eligible participants were adults aged 18 years or older with Type 2 diabetes (ICD‐10‐CM E11 or HbA1c ≥ 6.5%), a BMI below 27 kg/m2 recorded within 6 months before the index date, and at least 12 months of prior healthcare contact. Exclusions were Type 1 diabetes, HIV infection, prior bariatric or metabolic surgery and solid organ transplantation. A 6‐month washout excluded prior users of any GLP‐1 RA, DPP‐4i or SGLT2i. Cohort assignment used only index‐date information; post‐index treatment changes did not alter group membership. Concomitant metformin, sulfonylurea, insulin and other non‐assigned glucose‐lowering drugs were permitted and entered the propensity score as covariates. Before matching, patients with any study outcome recorded in the prior 6 months were excluded from all four cohorts (Figure 1). The index date was the first qualifying prescription between 1 January 2020 and 31 January 2026; full eligibility codes are in Table S2.

Final matched cohorts were formed by 1:1 propensity score matching for GLP‐1 receptor agonists (GLP‐1 RA) versus dipeptidyl peptidase‐4 inhibitors (DPP‐4i), sodium–glucose cotransporter‐2 inhibitors (SGLT2i) and usual care.

Covariates

Baseline covariates measured during the 12 months before the index date entered the propensity score model in six domains: demographics; socioeconomic hazards; healthcare utilisation; comorbid conditions; concurrent medications; and laboratory values, modelled as ordered categories with an unavailable indicator and no imputation. The full covariate list and the pre‐specified five‐condition composite negative control outcome (NCO) used to probe residual confounding after matching are provided in Table S3.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was all‐cause mortality. Secondary outcomes included a coded cardiovascular composite (MACE: myocardial infarction, ischaemic or haemorrhagic stroke or cardiac arrest), coded heart failure, a kidney failure composite (end‐stage renal disease, long‐term dialysis dependence, a dialysis or renal replacement therapy procedure or a recorded eGFR below 30 mL/min/1.73 m2), diabetic ketoacidosis or hyperosmolar hyperglycaemic state (DKA/HHS) and all‐cause hospitalisation. A pre‐specified panel of safety outcomes was also assessed, including incident underweight, sarcopenia or frailty, gastroparesis, acute kidney injury, recorded diabetic retinopathy events and suicidal ideation or attempt. Outcomes were defined by ICD‐10‐CM diagnoses with CPT procedure codes where applicable (full list in Table S4); within each matched cohort, the risk set for each outcome further excluded patients with a prior record of that outcome.

Statistical Analysis

For each pairwise comparison, 1:1 nearest‐neighbour propensity score matching was performed with the TriNetX platform default caliper of 0.1 stabdard deviations. Covariate balance was assessed with standardised mean differences (SMDs), and an SMD below 0.1 for every covariate was pre‐specified as the balance criterion. Balance was re‐confirmed within each outcome‐specific risk set.

Within each matched cohort, hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were estimated using Cox proportional hazards regression, with the proportional hazard ’s assumption evaluated using the platform's test for proportionality. Kaplan–Meier curves and log‐rank tests described survival. Absolute risk differences (ARDs) were the difference in crude 24‐month event proportions, numbers needed to treat the reciprocal of the ARD rounded up and relative risk reductions were risk based, so all three are reproducible from the reported event counts. Within each comparison, two‐sided p values were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg (BH) false discovery rate procedure across the eight outcomes in Table 1, including all‐cause mortality, and separately across the 10 safety outcomes in Table 2, with NCOs excluded (BH‐adjusted p < 0.05). E‐values were calculated for each significant outcome. Follow‐up began the day after the index date and ended at the earliest of an outcome event, death, loss to follow‐up or administrative censoring at 24 months or the data‐extraction date; patients indexed late in the accrual window contributed partial follow‐up.

Sensitivity Analyses

Pre‐specified sensitivity analyses included a 90‐day landmark, a 36‐month extended follow‐up, a 12‐month extended washout, a semaglutide‐restricted analysis and replication in the TriNetX Global Collaborative Network. At the reviewers' request we performed a per‐protocol analysis requiring persistence on the assigned index drug class within 90 days, with a 90‐day landmark (follow‐up Days 91–730); persistent patients were re‐matched 1:1 on the same covariates, with all SMDs below 0.1. Because the platform does not support time‐varying censoring at discontinuation, this persistence‐based design was the feasible as‐treated approximation, and the intention‐to‐treat analysis remained primary.

Results

Cohort Assembly and Baseline Characteristics

Among all eligible patients (Figure 1), we identified 9530 GLP‐1 RA, 13 434 DPP‐4i, 22 906 SGLT2i and 55 236 usual‐care initiators. After 1:1 matching, three cohorts were formed: GLP‐1 RA versus DPP‐4i (7389 pairs), versus SGLT2i (8969 pairs) and versus usual care (8610 pairs).

After matching, baseline characteristics were well balanced across all three cohorts (Tables S5–S7), with all post‐matching SMDs below 0.04. Mean age across cohorts ranged from 63.5 to 67.6 years, mean BMI from 24.3 to 24.6 kg/m2 and mean HbA1c from 8.2% to 8.5%; the female proportion was 52% to 54% across arms. Across cohorts, 55% to 58% of patients had a BMI of 25 kg/m2 or above, indicating a population predominantly within the 25 to 26.9 kg/m2 band rather than the normal‐weight range. Median (IQR) follow‐up in the GLP‐1 RA arm was 630 (457), 650 (448) and 637 (456) days in the DPP‐4i, SGLT2i and usual‐care matched cohorts, versus 720 (437), 549 (514) and 720 (427) days in the comparator arms (Table S8).

Primary Outcome

Compared with DPP‐4i (Table 1 and Figure 2), GLP‐1 RA use was associated with a significantly lower risk of all‐cause mortality (HR 0.61, 95% CI 0.52–0.71; p < 0.001). All‐cause mortality was comparable between GLP‐1 RA and SGLT2i (HR 0.89, 95% CI 0.76–1.05; p = 0.175) and lower with GLP‐1 RA versus usual care (HR 0.62, 95% CI 0.54–0.72; p < 0.001). Because GLP‐1 RA and SGLT2 inhibitors are both preferentially prescribed agents, the null mortality contrast versus SGLT2i serves as an internal benchmark, suggesting that the larger contrasts versus DPP‐4i and usual care are likely inflated by residual confounding by indication and frailty.

Secondary Outcomes

Compared with DPP‐4i (Table 1 and Figure 2), GLP‐1 RA was associated with lower risks of MACE (HR 0.83, 95% CI 0.71–0.98; BH‐adjusted p = 0.040), heart failure (HR 0.71, 95% CI 0.60–0.85; BH‐adjusted p < 0.001) and all‐cause hospitalisation (HR 0.84, 95% CI 0.74–0.95; BH‐adjusted p = 0.016). Versus SGLT2i, GLP‐1 RA was associated with lower heart failure (HR 0.56, 95% CI 0.47–0.65; BH‐adjusted p < 0.001) and MACE (HR 0.81, 95% CI 0.69–0.95; BH‐adjusted p = 0.034), without a difference in mortality or hospitalisation. Versus usual care, heart failure (HR 0.74, 95% CI 0.62–0.89) and all‐cause hospitalisation (HR 0.81, 95% CI 0.72–0.92) were lower, without a significant difference in MACE.

GLP‐1 RA was associated with a lower kidney failure composite risk compared with DPP‐4i (HR 0.81, 95% CI 0.69–0.96; BH‐adjusted p = 0.027), with no significant difference versus SGLT2i (HR 0.86, 95% CI 0.73–1.01; p = 0.064) or usual care (HR 0.93, 95% CI 0.79–1.11; p = 0.428).

DKA/HHS rates did not differ between GLP‐1 RA and DPP‐4i (HR 1.02, 95% CI 0.77–1.34) or SGLT2i (HR 0.89, 95% CI 0.69–1.15). Versus usual care, GLP‐1 RA showed a nominally lower DKA/HHS risk (HR 0.78, 95% CI 0.61–0.99) that did not survive BH correction (BH‐adjusted p = 0.087).

Gastroparesis was significantly higher with GLP‐1 RA versus DPP‐4i (HR 1.62, 95% CI 1.11–2.36; BH‐adjusted p = 0.027; Table 2), with a consistent trend versus usual care and no difference versus SGLT2i. Acute kidney injury and genital candidiasis were lower with GLP‐1 RA than DPP‐4i and SGLT2i, urinary tract infection was lower versus DPP‐4i and other safety outcomes did not differ after BH correction. Versus usual care, nominally higher rates of recorded diabetic retinopathy events and suicidal ideation or attempt did not survive multiple‐testing correction. These findings indicate absence of a consistent coded safety signal rather than proof that weight‐ or muscle‐related harms do not occur in practice.

Subgroup Analysis

Subgroup analyses were broadly consistent across prespecified strata (Figures S2–S9): the lower all‐cause mortality with GLP‐1 RA versus DPP‐4i and usual care was seen across most subgroups but not versus SGLT2i, and heart‐failure HRs were generally below 1.0 across subgroups for all three comparators.

A few interaction tests reached nominal significance but were not reproduced consistently, and subgroup findings for MACE, the kidney failure composite and all‐cause hospitalisation were less uniform (Figures S2–S9). In the lowest BMI stratum (22 kg/m2 or below), the lower all‐cause mortality association persisted versus DPP‐4i (102/1954 vs. 171/1973; HR 0.61, 95% CI 0.48–0.78) and usual care (103/2147 vs. 179/2164; HR 0.61, 95% CI 0.48–0.77) and was null versus SGLT2i (HR 0.87, 95% CI 0.67–1.12); the coded heart‐failure association was attenuated towards the null versus DPP‐4i (HR 0.91, 95% CI 0.66–1.24) and usual care (HR 0.94, 95% CI 0.69–1.27) and reached significance only versus SGLT2i (HR 0.68, 95% CI 0.51–0.90). Event counts were small and these subgroup estimates are correspondingly imprecise (Table S9 and Figure S9).

Sensitivity Analyses

The primary cardiorenal findings were robust across five pre‐specified sensitivity analyses (Figures S10–S14): the directions of association for all‐cause mortality versus DPP‐4i and usual care, and for coded heart failure versus all three comparators, were preserved, whereas MACE, the kidney failure composite and all‐cause hospitalisation were less uniformly robust. The 90‐day per‐protocol analysis was directionally concordant: all‐cause mortality again persisted versus DPP‐4i and usual care but not SGLT2i, and coded heart failure remained lower versus DPP‐4i and SGLT2i (borderline versus usual care), whereas MACE and the kidney failure composite were null and DKA/HHS was numerically higher versus DPP‐4i (Table S10 and Figure S15). Restricting the kidney failure composite to confirmed end‐stage renal disease, dialysis or renal replacement therapy (excluding the single eGFR criterion) reduced events by approximately 85% and rendered the association non‐significant in every comparison (Table S11 and Figure S16), indicating that the composite was substantially driven by the eGFR component.

Exploratory Tirzepatide Analysis

Tirzepatide was associated with lower all‐cause mortality than DPP‐4 inhibitors (HR 0.34, 95% CI 0.17–0.69; Figure S17), with no significant difference versus semaglutide (HR 0.68, 95% CI 0.33–1.41). These tirzepatide comparisons are exploratory and hypothesis‐generating only; given the small number of events and wide CIs, no comparative effectiveness conclusion is drawn.

Discussion

In this large target trial emulation of people with Type 2 diabetes and BMI < 27 kg/m2, initiation of non‐tirzepatide GLP‐1 RAs was associated with lower all‐cause mortality versus DPP‐4i and usual care, and with lower risk of recorded heart failure across all three comparator groups. Associations for MACE, renal outcomes, and all‐cause hospitalisation were more heterogeneous and depended on the comparator used. These findings suggest that some observed benefits associated with GLP‐1 RA use may extend to adults with lower‐BMI T2D [16, 18, 27, 28]. The comparator‐dependent pattern of the mortality association, present versus DPP‐4i and usual care but absent versus SGLT2i, an equally preferentially prescribed comparator, indicates that the larger mortality contrasts are most plausibly inflated by residual confounding by indication and frailty rather than reflecting a benefit of that magnitude; coded heart failure, the outcome concordant across all comparators, is therefore the most robust finding.

The lower mortality observed with GLP‐1 RA versus DPP‐4i and usual care in our cohort is directionally consistent with randomised trial evidence showing cardiovascular benefit of several GLP‐1 RAs in higher‐risk populations, although direct mortality benefit was most clearly demonstrated in LEADER rather than uniformly across all landmark CVOTs [4]. In contrast, SUSTAIN‐6 and REWIND primarily support reductions in major cardiovascular events rather than a consistent trial‐level reduction in all‐cause mortality [5, 6]. The lack of a mortality difference versus SGLT2i should therefore be interpreted cautiously. SGLT2 inhibitors have established benefits for heart failure outcomes and the composite of cardiovascular death or hospitalisation for heart failure, which may narrow observed differences in comparative effectiveness analyses [29]. In addition, a recent BMI‐stratified cohort study from Taiwan suggested that cardiovascular associations of GLP‐1 RA therapy may be stronger at higher BMI, whereas kidney associations were more consistent across BMI strata [14]. This interpretation was reinforced by the per‐protocol analysis, in which the mortality association again persisted against DPP‐4i and usual care but not against SGLT2i. The lower coded heart‐failure association versus SGLT2 inhibitors is biologically counterintuitive given their established heart‐failure benefit; because our endpoint captures coded rather than adjudicated heart failure and SGLT2 inhibitors are preferentially prescribed to patients at higher cardiorenal risk, this channelling would enrich the SGLT2i arm for coded events and bias the comparison towards GLP‐1 RA, so it should not be read as superiority over SGLT2 inhibitors for heart failure.

The observed associations were not limited to glycaemic endpoints but also involved mortality and cardiorenal outcomes in a lower‐BMI T2D population [30, 31], raising the possibility that mechanisms beyond weight loss may contribute. Possible explanations include reduced glucotoxicity, lower systemic and vascular inflammation, improved endothelial function and direct myocardial and renal effects of incretin‐based therapy [30, 32, 33]. These mechanisms may be especially relevant in lower‐BMI T2D with disproportionate visceral adiposity and β‐cell dysfunction [18, 34, 35]. Because these pathways cannot be measured directly in TriNetX, they should be regarded as biologically plausible explanations rather than mechanistic conclusions.

A key concern in lower‐BMI patients is the possibility that treatment‐related weight loss could contribute to underweight, loss of lean mass, sarcopenia or frailty [20, 36]. We did not observe a consistent increase in coded incident underweight or sarcopenia/frailty, although these outcomes are likely to be incompletely captured in routine EHR data and do not exclude clinically relevant body‐composition decline. Continued monitoring of nutritional status, muscle‐related decline and functional vulnerability therefore remains appropriate when initiating GLP‐1 RA in low‐BMI patients.

The significantly elevated gastroparesis risk versus DPP‐4i was the most clinically relevant safety signal and is consistent with the recognised effect of GLP‐1 RAs on gastric motility [37, 38]. GLP‐1 RA use was also associated with lower rates of genital candidiasis than SGLT2i, a pattern that may relate to the absence of treatment‐induced glycosuria [39]. The nominally higher rates of recorded diabetic retinopathy events and suicidal ideation or attempt versus usual care did not remain significant after multiple‐testing correction and should therefore be interpreted cautiously. For retinopathy, rapid glycaemic improvement remains a plausible explanation, in line with prior observations from semaglutide studies, rather than a definitive drug‐specific effect in this low‐BMI population [40]. For suicidality, current evidence remains incomplete and mixed, and the observed signal may reflect residual confounding, differential surveillance or outcome ascertainment [41]. These findings warrant confirmation in studies with outcome adjudication.

Strengths include the large multicentre EHR dataset, the active‐comparator new‐user design, matching, NCOs and multiple sensitivity analyses.

Several limitations deserve acknowledgment. Residual confounding cannot be excluded despite the new‐user active‐comparator design, matching and negative control analyses; unmeasured factors such as diabetes duration, lifestyle, intentional weight loss, adherence and body composition were not measurable in TriNetX, and prescribing may reflect clinician judgement, leaving residual channelling by indication possible. Exposure ascertainment is imperfect because prescription records do not guarantee dispensing, and discontinuation or switching was not fully captured. Outcomes relied on diagnosis codes rather than adjudicated events, and the TriNetX US network is not population‐based, which may limit generalisability. In addition, although an E value was reported for the mortality association, unmeasured determinants such as functional status, frailty and clinician‐anticipated life expectancy could be associated with both exposure and mortality strongly enough to account for it. Death ascertainment in this federated network is incomplete and not linked to a national death index, and more system‐engaged GLP‐1 RA initiators could be subject to differential ascertainment; consistent with this, the NCOs were uniformly, although non‐significantly, in the direction of apparent harm. The usual‐care comparator comprised patients maintained on metformin or sulfonylurea without escalation, who may differ in frailty, deprescribing and access, and the new‐user design largely reflects monotherapy or early‐line initiators, limiting generalisability to add‐on use. Median follow‐up differed in direction by comparator and per‐protocol retention was under half, so residual informative censoring cannot be excluded. Prior‐event exclusion before matching also removed a larger share of the SGLT2i pool than of the other cohorts, so that comparison draws on a more selected source population. Finally, the kidney failure composite was dominated by a single low eGFR value rather than confirmed kidney failure, and attenuated to non‐significance when restricted to confirmed events, so it reflects advanced kidney dysfunction subject to misclassification, and the recorded diabetic retinopathy endpoint cannot distinguish incident disease from documented progression.

These findings address a population common in practice but underrepresented in randomised evidence: adults with T2D and BMI below 27 kg/m2, in whom treatment decisions are often shaped by BMI‐based prescribing habits and reimbursement boundaries despite substantial cardiovascular and renal risk. Lower‐BMI T2D may warrant a more individualised, risk‐based framework. If confirmed prospectively, the observed associations could support broader consideration of GLP‐1 RA therapy in selected patients below the traditional BMI cutoff, with continued monitoring for gastrointestinal intolerance and nutritional compromise.

In conclusion, in adults with T2D and BMI < 27 kg/m2, GLP‐1 RA initiation was associated with lower all‐cause mortality versus DPP‐4i and usual care and lower coded heart failure across all three comparators, while other cardiorenal associations were less consistent. The all‐cause mortality association, absent against SGLT2i, should be interpreted cautiously as potentially reflecting residual confounding, whereas the concordant heart‐failure association was the most robust finding.
