H Syndrome Associated With Pure Red Cell Aplasia, Rosai–Dorfman Disease, and Sensorineural Hearing Loss: Phenotypic Variability in Three Children With the Same SLC29A3 Mutation
Authors: Mohammad Najajrah, Hadeel I. Bouzia, Nermin K. Darawi, Fatima S. Hajjaj, Nour H. Moosa, Noor M. Awad
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTH syndrome is a rare autosomal recessive disorder caused by mutations in the SLC29A3 gene. We report three pediatric patients with a homozygous c.1309G>A (p.Gly437Arg) mutation presenting with distinct phenotypic variations. These cases expand the clinical spectrum and emphasize the importance of early genetic diagnosis and family counseling.

Introduction

Mutations in the SLC29A3 gene, which encodes the human equilibrative nucleoside transporter 3 (hENT3) localized in lysosomal and mitochondrial membranes, have been implicated in a spectrum of rare hereditary disorders, including H syndrome, pigmented hypertrichosis with insulin‐dependent diabetes mellitus (PHID), Faisalabad histiocytosis (FHC), and dysosteosclerosis (DSS) [1]. Overlapping clinical manifestations among these syndromes support their unification under the term H syndrome, which exhibits remarkable phenotypic variability, emphasizing the need for early recognition and genetic confirmation [2, 3].

Here, we report three pediatric patients carrying the same homozygous mutation (c.1309G>A, p.Gly437Arg) in SLC29A3: a 17‐month‐old male whose initial presentation was PRCA, a 4‐year‐old female presenting with cervical lymphadenopathy, generalized weakness, and facial telangiectasia, and her 6‐year‐old sister with sensorineural hearing loss. This report illustrates a rare and unexpected presentation of PRCA as an initial manifestation of H syndrome, reinforcing the notable intrafamilial variability and the wide phenotypic heterogeneity associated with this condition.

Therapeutic options remain limited, with interventions including corticosteroids, methotrexate, cyclosporine, and tocilizumab; the latter has shown potential benefit in selected patients [4, 5].

Cases Presentation

Case 1

A 17‐month‐old male from Palestine, born at 38 weeks via cesarean section after an uncomplicated pregnancy, with a birth weight of 3500 g and APGAR scores of 9 and 10. The parents are first cousins. At 12 months of age, his mother noticed pallor and easy fatigability, prompting referral to the pediatric oncology department for evaluation of severe anemia. On examination, his height, weight, and head circumference were appropriate for age. Notable findings included facial telangiectasia, eyelid swelling, and large, minimally posteriorly rotated ears. No skeletal abnormalities were observed. His past medical history was significant for chronic constipation and recurrent, unexplained monthly fevers.

Laboratory tests revealed severe anemia with reticulocytopenia, elevated inflammatory markers, and mild endocrine abnormalities (Table 1). Bone marrow biopsy demonstrated normocellular marrow with severe erythroid hypoplasia, confirming a diagnosis of Pure Red Cell Aplasia (PRCA). Hemoglobin electrophoresis was normal, and viral screening for CMV, EBV, and parvovirus B19 was negative. Whole exome sequencing (WES) identified a homozygous pathogenic variant c.1309G>A (p.Gly437Arg) in the SLC29A3 gene, confirming a diagnosis of H syndrome. Additional investigations showed a normal echocardiogram and abdominal ultrasound without hepatosplenomegaly, and DPOAE testing revealed a bilateral present response.

Case 2

A 4‐year‐old female from Palestine, born to non‐consanguineous parents, presented at 2 years of age with noticeable cervical lymphadenopathy and generalized weakness. On examination, she was alert, well‐hydrated, and in no distress. Notable findings included facial telangiectasia and multiple areas of skin hyperpigmentation. Painless bilateral submandibular and deep cervical lymphadenopathy was observed. Cardiopulmonary and abdominal examinations were unremarkable, with no organomegaly or other abnormalities. The neurological examination was normal.

Laboratory tests showed normal hemoglobin and blood counts (Table 2). PCR testing was positive for Epstein–Barr Virus (EBV). Histopathology of a cervical lymph node biopsy revealed features consistent with Rosai–Dorfman Disease (RDD), with immunohistochemistry showing positivity for S‐100 and CD68, and negativity for CD1a. Whole‐body CT demonstrated enlarged bilateral cervical, axillary, and inguinal lymph nodes, hepatomegaly without focal lesions, and small hypodense splenic lesions. A whole‐body positive emission tomography (PET) scan revealed hypermetabolic activity in cervical and axillary lymph nodes, posterior nasopharynx, splenic tissue, left distal ulna, and left gluteal subcutaneous tissue.

WES identified a homozygous pathogenic variant c.1309G>A (p.Gly437Arg) in the SLC29A3 gene, confirming H syndrome. DPOAE testing showed bilateral present responses.

The patient was started on oral prednisone at the time of diagnosis, when she was 24 months old. She initially received 2 mg/kg/day for 28 days, followed by a taper to 1 mg/kg/day for 21 days, and then to 0.5 mg/kg/day for 7 days before discontinuation. During this course, she demonstrated progressive clinical improvement, with resolution of her initial presenting symptoms and normalization of inflammatory markers. Follow‐up whole‐body PET scans showed complete resolution of previously noted lymphadenopathy, and no major steroid‐related adverse effects were reported.

The child remained in apparent remission for 370 days, after which she developed sudden‐onset unilateral cervical lymph node enlargement, suggestive of disease relapse. A second course of corticosteroids was administered for 1 month. Owing to relapse and inadequate sustained response to steroid therapy, intravenous tocilizumab was initiated in Month 48 at a dose of 200 mg every 4 weeks according to pediatric protocol. Her response to tocilizumab has been closely monitored at each infusion visit through clinical examination, lymph node assessment, and laboratory evaluation of inflammatory markers.

Case 3

A 6‐year‐old female, sister of Case 2, with a known history of deafness, presented to the clinic with bilateral cervical lymphadenopathy. On examination, she was alert, well‐hydrated, and in no distress. No skin hyperpigmentation or petechiae were noted. The oropharynx showed bilateral tonsillar hypertrophy with erythema and patchy white exudates. Tender anterior cervical lymphadenopathy was present bilaterally, with nodes approximately 1 cm in size. Cardiopulmonary examination was normal, and the abdomen was soft and lax with no palpable organomegaly. The neurological examination was unremarkable.

Laboratory evaluation revealed hemoglobin 11.8 g/dL, hematocrit 35.9%, MCV 78 fL, MCH 25.7 pg., MCHC 33%, RBC count 4.6 × 106/μL, platelets 252,000/μL, WBC count 7.4 × 103/μL, CRP 3.31 mg/L, HbA1c 4.81%, ferritin 34.91 ng/mL, and TSH 1.61 mIU/mL.

Whole‐body CT scan revealed left periorbital thickening with mucosal soft tissue thickening of both sinuses, but no enlargement of cervical, axillary, abdominal, or inguinal lymph nodes. The echocardiogram was normal. Tympanometry showed bilateral negative pressure (−110 daPa in the right ear and −145 daPa in the left ear). Play tone audiometry confirmed bilateral moderate to severe sensorineural hearing loss.

Considering her family history, WES was performed, revealing a homozygous pathogenic variant c.1309G>A (p.Gly437Arg) in the SLC29A3 gene, consistent with a diagnosis of H syndrome.

Discussion

The human equilibrative nucleoside transporter 3 (hENT3), encoded by the SLC29A3 gene, is a member of the equilibrative nucleoside transporter (ENT) family within the solute carrier 29 (SLC29) protein group. It is primarily localized to lysosomal and mitochondrial membranes, where it mediates pH‐dependent nucleoside transport and maintains intracellular nucleoside homeostasis [6].

Germline mutations in SLC29A3 have been linked to four hereditary disorders: H syndrome, pigmented hypertrichosis with insulin‐dependent diabetes mellitus (PHID), Faisalabad histiocytosis (FHC), and dysosteosclerosis (DSS) [1]. Clinical features of H syndrome include cardiac anomalies, camptodactyly, short stature, hypergonadotropic hypogonadism, hepatosplenomegaly, scrotal masses, growth retardation, and sensorineural hearing loss [7, 8]. PHID presents with hyperpigmented and hypertrichotic skin, hepatosplenomegaly, hypogonadism, diabetes mellitus, camptodactyly, clinodactyly, chronic inflammatory syndrome, and growth retardation [9]. FHC is characterized by deafness, growth retardation, hypogonadism, camptodactyly, and Rosai–Dorfman disease (RDD) [10]. DSS manifests as hyperpigmented skin, frontal bossing, mid‐face hypoplasia, short stature, sclerotic platyspondyly, otosclerosis, and compression of the CNS and cranial nerves [9, 11]. Despite their distinct descriptions, overlapping clinical features have been reported in the literature, strongly suggesting that these syndromes represent a single disorder, H syndrome [1, 2, 3].

Hematological involvement in H syndrome is uncommon but comprises a broad spectrum of abnormalities, predominantly involving the bone marrow. Reported manifestations include microcytic anemia, thrombocytosis, pancytopenia, myelofibrosis, non‐clonal myeloproliferative changes with histiocytic proliferation, and pure red cell aplasia (PRCA), among other hematological abnormalities reported across the SLC29A3 spectrum [10]. Pure red cell aplasia (PRCA) is characterized by normocytic, normochromic anemia, reticulocytopenia, and marked reduction or absence of erythroid precursors in the bone marrow, while the myeloid and megakaryocytic lineages remain preserved. PRCA may be congenital, such as Diamond–Blackfan syndrome (DBS), or acquired secondary to infections, drugs, autoimmune disorders, or malignancies [17].

PRCA is an exceptionally rare manifestation within the SLC29A3 spectrum. To date, the first reported case was described by de Jesus et al. in 2013 as severe non‐regenerative anemia with erythroblastopenia confirmed by bone marrow aspiration and biopsy, representing a clinicopathological picture consistent with PRCA, although the term pure red cell aplasia was not explicitly used [11]. Subsequently, Çağdaş et al. reported steroid‐resistant, transfusion‐dependent PRCA in a patient with H syndrome accompanied by autoimmune manifestations and immunodeficiency. Complete remission following IVIG and low‐dose corticosteroids, with relapse after steroid tapering, suggested an immune‐mediated mechanism rather than a primary defect of erythropoiesis. The authors further proposed that SLC29A3 deficiency may result in immune dysregulation and recommended evaluating patients with H syndrome for clinical and laboratory evidence of immunodeficiency [16].

In our study, Case 1 presented with PRCA at 12 months of age, confirmed by complete blood count, reticulocyte count, and bone marrow biopsy, while Diamond–Blackfan syndrome was excluded by hemoglobin electrophoresis. Infectious causes of acquired PRCA, including parvovirus B19, CMV, and EBV, were also excluded. Bone marrow examination demonstrated isolated erythroid hypoplasia with preserved myeloid and megakaryocytic lineages, making marrow infiltration and leukemia unlikely. Although a comprehensive autoimmune evaluation was not performed, no clinical features suggestive of autoimmunity were present at diagnosis. In the context of a genetically confirmed pathogenic SLC29A3 variant and the limited number of previously reported cases, PRCA may represent a rare hematologic manifestation and, in some patients, an initial presentation of H syndrome. However, current evidence remains insufficient to establish a causal relationship, and additional cases with functional studies are required to determine whether this association is causal or coincidental.

Rosai–Dorfman disease (RDD), classified within the “R group” histiocytoses, is characterized by massive lymphadenopathy with possible extranodal involvement. Two main forms exist: nodal RDD and cutaneous RDD (CRDD). Histologically, RDD shows clusters of histiocytes with hypochromic nuclei, often demonstrating emperipolesis with phagocytosed lymphocytes, plasma cells, and neutrophils [18]. Immunohistochemistry typically reveals strong S100 and CD163 positivity, variable CD68 positivity, and negativity for CD1a and HLA‐DR [19]. Familial forms of RDD associated with SLC29A3 mutations have been documented by Morgan et al., Kismet et al., Jonard et al., and Chouk et al., demonstrating overlapping clinical and pathological features with H syndrome [2, 10, 20, 21].

In addition, histopathological findings across the SLC29A3 spectrum further support a shared histiocytic inflammatory process, with some lymph node specimens exhibiting features resembling RDD. In our report, Case 2 was diagnosed with Rosai–Dorfman disease, further supporting the phenotypic overlap between RDD and SLC29A3‐related disorders. The predominance of affected individuals born to consanguineous parents is also consistent with the autosomal recessive inheritance pattern of H syndrome.

In Case 1, the initial presentation was dominated by transfusion‐dependent PRCA rather than the classic manifestations of H syndrome. Consequently, WES identified a homozygous pathogenic SLC29A3 variant, c.1309G>A (p.Gly437Arg). In contrast, the clinical manifestations in Cases 2 and 3, including Rosai–Dorfman disease and sensorineural hearing loss, respectively, raised a stronger clinical suspicion of H syndrome. Single‐exome analysis identified the same homozygous pathogenic variant in both affected sisters. Segregation analysis demonstrated that their unaffected brother was heterozygous for the same variant, further supporting the autosomal recessive inheritance of H syndrome. Genetic testing and counseling were recommended for both families; however, parental testing was not performed because of financial constraints.

A large cohort of 79 patients aged 1.5 months to 27 years revealed additional uncommon manifestations, including respiratory mucosal swelling, chronic rhinitis, anemia (predominantly microcytic), renal abnormalities, skeletal involvement, and recurrent fever episodes [3]. Consistent with previous reports, our three patients carrying the same homozygous SLC29A3 variant (c.1309G>A, p.Gly437Arg) demonstrated marked phenotypic variability. The contrasting presentations observed in the two affected sisters further illustrate the poor genotype–phenotype correlation in H syndrome and suggest that additional genetic, epigenetic, or environmental modifiers contribute to disease expression [2]. Their unaffected brother was heterozygous for the same variant and had no clinical manifestations.

H syndrome has been reported worldwide but is particularly prevalent among individuals of Arab ancestry. In Arab‐Palestinian populations, the estimated combined allele frequency of the two common pathogenic SLC29A3 variants (p.Gly437Arg and p.Gly427Ser) is approximately 1%, suggesting that the disorder may be underrecognized in this population [3]. These findings highlight the importance of maintaining a high index of suspicion for H syndrome in patients with compatible clinical features, particularly in populations with a high rate of consanguinity. Early diagnosis facilitates appropriate genetic counseling, carrier detection, family screening, and informed reproductive planning.

Therapeutic experience in H syndrome remains limited, and no standardized treatment has been established [4]. In a study involving five patients with H syndrome, several immunomodulatory agents, including prednisolone, methotrexate, cyclosporine, and tocilizumab, were administered with variable clinical responses [5]. Long‐term low‐dose corticosteroids provided partial control of inflammatory symptoms; however, relapse frequently occurred during dose tapering, particularly in patients with arthritis, which remained difficult to control. Methotrexate resulted in only modest improvement, whereas cyclosporine demonstrated minimal efficacy and was subsequently discontinued because of inadequate clinical response. In contrast, tocilizumab produced the most consistent therapeutic benefit, leading to improvement in systemic inflammation, normalization of inflammatory markers, reduction of arthritis and other inflammatory manifestations, and improved growth velocity in several patients, supporting a central role for IL‐6‐mediated inflammation in disease pathogenesis and suggesting that IL‐6 blockade may represent a promising therapeutic strategy for selected patients with H syndrome [5]. Consistent with these observations, our first patient with PRCA showed no response to corticosteroid therapy, whereas our second patient demonstrated marked clinical improvement following treatment with corticosteroids and tocilizumab, highlighting the heterogeneous therapeutic response even among individuals carrying the same pathogenic SLC29A3 variant.

Conclusion

This case series expands the phenotypic spectrum of SLC29A3‐related disease by demonstrating that pure red cell aplasia may represent an uncommon initial manifestation of H syndrome. Our findings further emphasize the marked intrafamilial phenotypic variability associated with identical pathogenic variants and highlight the importance of considering SLC29A3 analysis in patients presenting with unexplained PRCA accompanied by syndromic features. Early recognition and genetic diagnosis are essential for appropriate management, family counseling, and cascade screening, while increasing awareness of H syndrome may facilitate earlier diagnosis and improve patient outcomes.
