Oxytocin agonist TGOT differentially modulates synaptic plasticity and intrinsic excitability in a sex-specific manner in the juvenile rat hippocampus
Authors: Nisha Rajan Narattil, Sailendrakumar Kolatt Chandran, Mouna Maroun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Oxytocin (OXT) regulates social behavior and cognition in the mammalian brain through its receptor, OXTR, which is widely expressed, including in the hippocampus. OXTR expression changes across development in a sex-dependent manner. We recently showed that blocking OXTR in hippocampal CA1 impairs long-term object location memory (OLM) in juvenile male rats. Here, we examine the effect of OXT in juvenile males and females using in vivo and ex vivo electrophysiology. Microinjection of the selective OXTR agonist Thr4,Gly7-oxytocin (TGOT) significantly impairs long-term potentiation (LTP) in the Schaffer collateral–CA1 pathway in juvenile males, but we find no effect in females. Whole-cell patch-clamp recordings show that TGOT increases spontaneous inhibitory postsynaptic currents (sIPSCs) in both sexes, while reducing spontaneous excitatory postsynaptic currents (sEPSCs) only in males. TGOT also reduces intrinsic excitability in juvenile males, whereas female excitability is unchanged. These findings reveal sex-specific effects of oxytocin on hippocampal synaptic plasticity and neuronal excitability during juvenile development. They highlight the importance of considering both sex and age as critical biological variables in studies of hippocampal function and neurodevelopmental disorders.

Introduction

Oxytocin (OXT) is a neuropeptide synthesized in the hypothalamic supraoptic and paraventricular nuclei and stored in the posterior pituitary1,2. It is well known for roles in childbirth, lactation, and parental behaviour1,3,4. OXT binds to its G-protein-coupled receptor (OXTR) in both the central and peripheral nervous systems.5,6, acting via neural pathways or released into blood and cerebrospinal fluid7,8. Recent advances highlight OXTR’s central role in modulating social behaviours (e.g., recognition, aggression, bonding) and non-social processes (e.g., learning, memory, mood, stress)8–13. Dysfunctions in this system are linked to neuropsychiatric conditions, including autism, schizophrenia, depression, bipolar disorder, and borderline personality disorder14,15.

The OXTR gene and its protein are widely expressed throughout the rat brain, including the hippocampus, thalamus, cortex, amygdala, and striatum16–20. In the hippocampus, OXTR has been identified in the CA1 region21–25. Electrophysiological studies have demonstrated that OXT exerts direct effects on specific neuronal populations within the CA1 area of the rat hippocampus26–29. When bound to its receptor, OXT induces a depolarizing, excitatory effect on hippocampal neurons. Notably, OXT has been shown to excite a specific class of GABAergic interneurons (INs) in the CA1 region, leading to enhanced inhibitory synaptic transmission onto CA1 pyramidal neurons in rats30,31.

In rats, females show lower OXTR binding sites than males in multiple forebrain regions like nucleus accumbens (NAc), dorsal caudate-putamen, intermediate lateral septum (LS), posterior stria terminalis (BNST), medial preoptic area (MPOA), agranular insular cortex, hippocampal CA1 region, medial amygdala (MeA), and ventral medial hypothalamus32. The density of OXT immunoreactive axons appears higher in female rats33. Maternal licking and grooming increase OXTR binding by up to 40% in female mice34. In humans, intranasal OXT administration enhanced the kindship recognition in women and competition in men35. Plasma OXT level is higher in women compared to men36.

The oxytocinergic system undergoes significant changes throughout life, with OXT-binding site distribution and density shifting during the third postnatal week and puberty37, suggesting that OXT effects may vary between young and adult animals. Research from our lab also shows differences in the mechanism of OXT throughout the development of male and female rats. Prefrontal OXT is involved in juvenile social recognition memory (SRM), as systemic OXT injection and prefrontal TGOT microinjection reversed SRM deficits in high-fat diet (HFD)-fed juvenile males38. Microinjection of OXTR agonist- Thr4, Gly7-oxytocin (TGOT) in the infralimbic medial prefrontal cortex (IL-mPFC), after fear retrieval facilitated extinction in adults39. OXT’s role in fear extinction is sex-dependent, mediating extinction in juvenile females but not males40,41. We showed that administering OXTR agonist in CA1 reverses HFD-induced object location memory (OLM) deficits in juvenile male rats42 and blocking OXTR in CA1 impairs OLM, indicating OXT’s role in mediating OLM43.

Several studies in juvenile animals have shown that OXTR activation with TGOT modulates intrinsic excitability and inhibitory synaptic transmission in hippocampal neurons using ex vivo electrophysiological approaches31,44; however, these studies have primarily been conducted in a single sex or without a direct comparison between males and females. Importantly, we recently demonstrated that blocking OXTRs in the hippocampal CA1 impairs both object location memory (OLM) and Schaffer collateral–CA1 LTP in juvenile males, but not in females, indicating that endogenous OXT signalling is required for optimal hippocampal function specifically in males45. While these findings establish a sex-specific dependence on endogenous OXT signalling, it remains unclear how direct OXTR activation influences hippocampal synaptic plasticity and neuronal excitability in juvenile males versus females, and whether such effects are mediated by differential modulation of synaptic inhibition, excitation, or intrinsic membrane properties.

In this regard, we hypothesised that activation of OXTR in the juvenile hippocampal CA1 produces sex-specific modulation of synaptic plasticity and neuronal excitability. Specifically, we predicted that TGOT application would differentially affect Schaffer collateral–CA1 long-term potentiation in juvenile males and females, and that these effects would be associated with sex-dependent changes in inhibitory and excitatory synaptic transmission and intrinsic membrane properties.

This study examined sex-specific effects of the TGOT in the hippocampal CA1 using in vivo and ex vivo electrophysiology. We first evaluated long-term potentiation (LTP) in the Schaffer collateral–CA1 pathway following TGOT application. Next, using whole-cell patch-clamp recordings from CA1 pyramidal neurons, we examined the effect of TGOT on inhibitory and excitatory synaptic transmission as well as its influence on intrinsic neuronal properties. Together, these experiments provide a comprehensive analysis of TGOT’s influence on hippocampal function in juvenile rats in a sex-specific context.

TGOT reduces the sEPSCs and increases the sIPSCs onto CA1 pyramidal neurons in juvenile male rats

Previously, studies have shown that TGOT can affect CA1 synaptic properties in juvenile male rats and mice30,44. To obtain excitatory and inhibitory post-synaptic currents from CA1 pyramidal neurons, we performed whole-cell patch clamp electrophysiology in voltage-clamp mode, holding the neurons at −70 and 0 mV, respectively, from visually identified pyramidal neurons from the CA1 region of the hippocampus (Fig. 2A). Under these experimental conditions, sEPSCs detected are inwardly flowing, and sIPSCs are outwardly (Fig. 2A and D), without applying any synaptic blockers from the same cell46. To test the effect of TGOT on the synaptic properties of CA1 pyramidal neurons, 400 nm of TGOT was infused through the perfusing ACSF. The application of TGOT reduced sEPSC frequencies in nearly all CA1 pyramidal neurons, and the effect was reversible. Representatives of averaged traces are shown in Fig. 2A. The frequencies of sEPSCs were significantly different before and after applying TGOT (Fig. 2A, B, One-Way repeated ANOVA, Friedman test, P = 0.0018), whereas the amplitudes were not significantly affected (Fig. 2C, One-Way repeated ANOVA, Friedman test, P = 0.4244). Next, we asked about the effect of TGOT in sIPSCs. Similar to the sEPSCs, the application of TGOT stimulated nearly all CA1 pyramidal neurons (Fig. 2D). The sIPSCs frequencies significantly increased in the CA1 pyramidal neurons (Fig. 2D, E, One-Way repeated ANOVA, Friedman test, P < 0.0001) following the application of TGOT without any significant change in the amplitudes (Fig. 2F, One-Way repeated ANOVA, Friedman test P = 0.1266). Interestingly, we observed a significant change in the E/I ratio of CA1 following applying TGOT (Fig. 2G, One-Way repeated ANOVA, Friedman test, P = 0.0004). TGOT reduces the excitability of CA1 pyramidal neurons in juvenile male rats

Previous studies have indicated that, unlike hippocampal CA2, OXT does not depolarize CA1 pyramidal cells; instead, it activates parvalbumin-positive fast-spiking interneurons30,44,47. The results from our voltage clamp experiments suggested an increased synaptic inhibition onto CA1 pyramidal neurons following TGOT application. Here, we hypothesize that applying TGOT will reduce the firing of CA1 pyramidal neurons and change their intrinsic properties in juvenile male rats. To test our hypothesis, another cohort of juvenile male rats was subjected to whole-cell current clamp electrophysiology and measured active and passive properties from CA1 pyramidal neurons. In agreement with our hypothesis, the firing frequencies of CA1 pyramidal neurons were significantly reduced following the application of TGOT. TGOT increases the sIPSC frequencies of CA1 pyramidal neurons without changing the intrinsic properties of juvenile female rats

Our synaptic and intrinsic investigation revealed that TGOT can modify both synaptic and intrinsic properties of CA1 pyramidal neurons in juvenile male rats. Here, we asked about a sex-dependent effect of TGOT in CA1 pyramidal neurons. To test the sex-specific effect of TGOT in CA1 pyramidal neurons, we conducted whole-cell voltage clamp and current clamp experiments in female juvenile rats, similar to juvenile male rats. In contrast to the juvenile male rats, there were no significant effects on sEPSCs frequencies (Fig. 4A, One-Way repeated ANOVA, Friedman test P = 0.6065) and amplitudes (Fig. 4C, One-Way repeated ANOVA, Friedman test P = 0.5488) onto CA1 pyramidal neurons following the application of TGOT in juvenile female rats. However, the application of TGOT significantly increased the frequencies of sIPSCs (Fig. 4D, E, One-Way repeated ANOVA, Friedman test, P = 0.0003) but not the amplitudes (Fig. 4F, One-Way repeated ANOVA, Friedman test, P = 0.1653) in female rats, which is similar to the males. In contrast, in females, the effect of TGOT was irreversible (Fig. 4D, E). Surprisingly, we did not find any significant effect on CA1 intrinsic properties following TGOT application in juvenile female rats. There are no significant differences in the firing frequency with the increasing injected currents (Fig. 5A, B, Current × Treatment F(10, 80) = 1.165, P = 0.3269, Two-way Repeated measures of ANOVA). There is no significant difference in the RMP (Fig. 5C, paired t-test t(8) = 0.7687, P = 0.4642) mAHP (Fig. 5D, Paired t-test, t(8) = 1.153, P = 0.2820), sAHP (Fig. 5E, Wilcoxon matched-pairs signed rank test, P = 0.9102), AP threshold (Fig. 5F, Paired t-test, t(8) = 0.4152, P = 0.6889), AP amplitude (Fig. 5G, Paired t-test, t(8) = 1.267, P = 0.2409), AP half width (Fig. 5H, Wilcoxon matched-pairs signed rank test, P = 0.8438), Rheobase (Fig. 5I, Paired t-test, t(8) = 1.942, P = 0.0881), input resistance (Fig. 5J, Paired t-test, t(8) = 0.7093, P = 0.4983), sag ratio (Fig. 5K, Paired t-test, t(8) = 0.2114, P = 0.8379) and time constant (Fig. 5L,Wilcoxon matched-pairs signed rank test, P = 0.0742) followed by TGOT application. Discussion

The present study examined the effect of the selective OXTR agonist TGOT on LTP, synaptic, and intrinsic properties of the CA1 region of the hippocampus in juvenile male and female rats, employing both in vivo and ex vivo electrophysiological approaches. First, to address the effect of TGOT on LTP, we microinjected the TGOT in the CA1 region of the hippocampus 30 min before the TBS, and our data revealed that the TGOT significantly impaired LTP in juvenile males without any effect on juvenile females. Next, we investigated sex-specific changes in the synaptic and intrinsic properties of the CA1 pyramidal neuron by applying TGOT through the perfusing ACSF solution. Our results show that the TGOT increases the inhibition of CA1 pyramidal neurons, reduces excitatory inputs, and changes the E/I balance and excitability in juvenile male rats. Meanwhile, increased inhibition of CA1 pyramidal neurons in juvenile females was observed without changing excitatory inputs and intrinsic properties following TGOT application.

In the hippocampus, the OXTRs are highly expressed in all subregions28,48–52. Several studies have shown that the distribution of OXTRs varies by age and sex37,53–55. Previously, we showed the differential effects of OXT on social memory, fear extinction, and OLM in juvenile male and female rats.38,40,42.

In this study, our in vivo recordings from the Schaffer collateral to CA1 pathway show that microinjection of TGOT inhibited LTP induction in juvenile males. Previous studies demonstrated that tetanic stimulation of the perforant pathway following intracerebroventricular OXT injection induced long-term depression (LTD) in the granule cells of the dentate gyrus56. A similar OXT-induced effect was also observed in the rat’s medial amygdala (MeA). Specifically, they found that intracerebroventricular (ICV) OXT administration significantly enhanced LTD induction in the accessory olfactory bulb (AOB)-medial amygdala pathway following tetanic stimulation of AOB57. In contrast, a study on 15-week-old female mice found that OXT enhances long-lasting LTP in the CA1 region, improving long-term spatial memory. They further demonstrated that this effect was mediated by activation of the MAPK cascade and phosphorylation of the cyclic AMP-responsive element binding protein (CREB), indicating that OXT-induced neuronal plasticity in the hippocampus depends on transcription58. However, it is important to note that this finding differs from the present results in several key aspects. While the above study was conducted in adult, reproductively mature mice and utilised oxytocin, our study examined juvenile rats and employed local microinjection of TGOT. These differences in species, developmental stage, physiological context, and ligand likely account for the observed difference in LTP.

As reported previously, in the CA1 region, OXTRs are highly enriched in PV+ GABAergic interneurons19,52. However, expression has also been reported in other interneuron subclasses and in a small proportion of pyramidal neurons59. Consistently, extracellular recordings in rats have demonstrated that OXT directly excites a subset of GABAergic INs26–29,31. In the present study, we examined the effects of TGOT on both sEPSCs and sIPSCs in both juvenile male and female rats. Our data revealed that the application of TGOT increases the sIPSCs frequencies of CA1 pyramidal neurons, reduces sEPSCs frequencies, and changes the E/I balance in juvenile males. In females, the TGOT increases the frequencies of sIPSCs of CA1 pyramidal neurons without any change in the sEPSCs frequencies. Similar to our results, an increase in sIPSC frequency was found following TGOT application hippocampal CA1 pyramidal neurons in rats31, and similar results were reported in mice44. Previous findings show that TGOT reduces spontaneous firing but enhances EPSP–spike coupling in CA1 pyramidal neurons. It also increases the rate and amplitude of sIPSCs, an effect abolished by the P/Q-type calcium channel blocker ω-Agatoxin IVA but unaffected by the N-type channel antagonist ω-Conotoxin GVIA, indicating a predominant contribution from fast-spiking interneurons (FSIs) with minimal input from regular-spiking (RS) interneurons30. In contrast to our report, some studies reported no change in the sEPSCs in rats and mice following TGOT application31,44.

The above-presented data suggest that TGOT influences inhibitory transmission in the CA1 stratum pyramidal. Within the hippocampus, GABAergic INs serve as the primary source of inhibition for pyramidal neurons60. Previously, it has been shown that application TGOT has a minimal or no effect on direct depolarizing response when the synaptic response is blocked47. In contrast, reports suggest that without the application of synaptic blockers, TGOT or OXT can induce hyperpolarization and change the firing rate. Suggesting the role of CA1 GABAergic INs in reducing CA1 pyramidal neuron excitability following the TGOT applications26,44,61.

The increased inhibition and reduced excitatory inputs onto CA1 neurons followed by TGOT encourage us to examine the effect of TGOT on the CA1 pyramidal neuron’s intrinsic excitability in a sex-dependent manner. To that end, we examined the intrinsic changes in the pyramidal neurons in the CA1 with TGOT application, and our results showed reduced excitability in juvenile males. We also observed reduced firing frequencies by an increased mAHP, an increased AP halfwidth, an increased sag ratio, and an increased time constant in juvenile males. The other intrinsic properties like RMP, sAHP, AP amplitude, AP threshold, input resistance, and rheobase, remain unchanged after applying TGOT. Notably, no significant changes were observed in the intrinsic properties of CA1 neurons following TGOT application in juvenile female rats.

Similar to our results in males, studies have shown a reduced excitability in CA1 with application TGOT30,44. It was also found that TGOT hyperpolarized CA1 pyramidal neurons, which leads to decreased excitability44. In contrast to this report, in our study, the reduced excitability is not mediated by membrane hyperpolarization, as we did not observe any changes in RMP in CA1 pyramidal neurons after the TGOT applications. However, the reduced excitability may be reflected by an increased AHP following the application of TGOT. An increase in AHP may limit neuronal activity by promoting frequency adaptation62. Additionally, we have observed increased AP half-width, sag ratio, and time constants, which point to the inverse regulation of different ion channels and excitability63. Conversely, it has been shown that OXTR signaling inhibits both potassium conductance (IKir) and mixed cation conductance (Ih) and can modulate CA2 pyramidal neuron firing64.

A recent study demonstrated that OXT-activated OXTR-expressing GABAergic INs in the CA1 increased firing frequency and cellular excitability, resulting in a higher firing rate for the same injected current. Additionally, OXT altered the shape of APs by modifying their threshold, reducing amplitude, afterhyperpolarization, and slowing kinetics61. In insular cortex slices, OXT depolarized pyramidal neurons, increased input resistance, reduced spike amplitude, and enhanced spiking to depolarizing currents, and these changes abolished a Protein kinase-C (PKC) inhibition65. These findings suggest TGOT may modulate neuronal excitability in the CA1 through complex mechanisms, potentially involving both directly changing the CA1 intrinsic properties and indirectly modulating the inhibitory transmission. However, the direct effect of TGOT on CA1 pyramidal neuron’s intrinsic properties in a sex-dependent manner needs to be investigated further.

The sex-specific effects of TGOT on CA1 pyramidal neuron activity and synaptic plasticity likely reflect fundamental differences in OXTR signalling and hippocampal circuit organisation between juvenile males and females. In juvenile males, TGOT decreased pyramidal neuron excitability and impaired CA1 LTP, aligning with our observations of increased inhibitory synaptic drive and changes in intrinsic membrane properties. This indicates that OXTR activation in males mainly recruits GABAergic interneuron networks, thereby enhancing inhibition and reducing pyramidal neuron output. This mechanism is consistent with earlier reports that OXTRs are predominantly found on hippocampal GABAergic interneurons and that OXT signalling can indirectly suppress pyramidal neuron excitability by strengthening interneuron-mediated inhibition30,59,66.

In contrast, in juvenile females, TGOT does not affect pyramidal neuron excitability or LTP, despite evidence of increased inhibitory transmission with TGOT application. This dissociation suggests that female CA1 circuits may possess compensatory mechanisms such as differences in interneuron subtype recruitment, chloride homeostasis, or sex-dependent intracellular signalling pathways that buffer pyramidal neurons against excessive inhibitory modulation during development. Indeed, sex differences in OXTR signalling, neuromodulatory integration, and hormone-dependent developmental trajectories are well documented and may underlie divergent circuit-level responses to oxytocinergic activation in males and females67,68.

In our recent study, we showed that blocking oxytocin receptors in CA1 impairs both OLM and LTP in juvenile males but not in females, indicating that endogenous OXT signalling is required for optimal hippocampal function specifically in males45. In the current study, we found that exogenous OXTR activation with TGOT reduces LTP and intrinsic excitability in juvenile males, accompanied by enhanced inhibitory drive. Together, these results suggest that OXTR signaling in male CA1 operates within a narrow optimal range, where both insufficient activation (antagonist) and excessive activation (TGOT) disrupt the excitatory–inhibitory balance required for stable synaptic plasticity. This circuit-level shift toward inhibition is likely to compromise CA1 output during spatial encoding, providing a mechanistic link between the observed electrophysiological effects and previously reported impairments in OLM.

Together, these findings show that OXT does not consistently exert the same facilitatory or inhibitory effect on hippocampal plasticity during juvenility; instead, it differentially influences CA1 function by sex. This highlights juvenility as a crucial developmental period during which OXT signalling can affect hippocampal excitability and synaptic plasticity in males and females. These findings have potential implications for how sex influences vulnerability or resilience to cognitive and emotional dysfunction later in life.

It is important to note that most studies used male rodents31,61 to examine the effects of TGOT on hippocampal neurons. Although some studies included both sexes, they eventually pooled their data30,44. To the best of our knowledge, this study is the first to show differences in synaptic and intrinsic properties after TGOT application in juvenile male and female rats.

Overall, our in vivo and ex vivo electrophysiological data reveal sex-specific effects of TGOT on CA1 hippocampal function in juvenile rats. TGOT microinjection impaired LTP in males but not females. In males, TGOT bath application on CA1 pyramidal neurons increased sIPSCs, reduced sEPSCs, and reduced excitability, whereas in females, it increased sIPSCs without affecting sEPSCs or excitability. These findings elucidate a sex-specific role of oxytocin in modulating hippocampal function in juveniles and underscore the importance of incorporating sex and age as biological variables in research, especially when addressing development-dependent pathologies.

Methods

Animals

We used male and female juvenile Sprague Dawley rats [post-natal day (PND) 27–30] from the psychology-neurobiology lab’s vivarium at Haifa University. The pups were weaned at PND 20–21, housed in plexiglass cages (5 rats per cage), and maintained on a free-feeding regimen with a 12-h light/dark schedule. All experiments were performed during the light portion of the cycle. The ethical committee of Haifa University approved all procedures and animal experimentation protocols (Approval number:25343U).

Electrophysiology

Juvenile (PND 27–30) male and female Sprague Dawley rats were anaesthetized with a urethane and chloral hydrate mixture (40% urethane, 5% chloral hydrate in saline; 0.5 mL/100 g, IP)38,43,69–71 and placed in a stereotaxic frame (Stoelting, USA). Urethane provides stable, long-lasting anaesthesia with minimal effects on vital functions and synaptic neurotransmission, making it particularly suitable for in vivo electrophysiological experiments. Adequate depth of anesthesia was confirmed by the absence of withdrawal reflex to paw pinch and the absence of corneal reflex. Body temperature was maintained at 37 ± 0.5 °C throughout the experiment. Anaesthetic depth was monitored periodically throughout the procedure by assessing reflex responses and respiratory pattern, and additional anaesthetic (0.1–0.2 ml) was administered when necessary to maintain a stable surgical plane of anaesthesia. Animals remained under anaesthesia throughout the electrophysiological recording procedure (approximately 3 h). All procedures were conducted as non-recovery terminal experiments, and animals did not regain consciousness.

Small holes were drilled into the skull to allow insertion of electrodes and to implant a stainless-steel guide cannula into the brain. We placed the cannula at a 30-degree angle, aiming at CA1 (Coordinates: 4.1 mm anterior, 3.1 mm lateral, and 2.1 mm ventral to bregma), and secured it with acrylic dental cement. A bipolar 125 µm stimulating electrode was inserted into the rat Schaffer collateral(Juvenile: −3 mm posterior, 0.2 mm lateral, 3.2 mm ventral to bregma). A single recording microelectrode (glass, tip diameter 2–5 mm, filled with 2 M NaCl, resistance 1–4 MO) was slowly lowered into the CA1 (Juvenile −4.1 mm anterior, 2.1 mm lateral, and 2.8 mm ventral to bregma). The evoked responses were digitized (10 kHz) and analyzed using a Cambridge Electronic Design 1401-plus data acquisition system (Cambridge, UK) and Spike2 software. Offline measurements were made of field post-synaptic potentials (PSPs) amplitude using averages of five successive responses to a given stimulation intensity applied at 0.1 Hz. Test stimuli [monopolar pulses; 100 microseconds (µs) duration] were delivered at 0.1 Hz. After positioning the electrodes, the rats were left for 30 min before commencing the experiment.

Long-term potentiation (LTP)

LTP was induced using the Theta Burst Stimulation (TBS) protocol, which was applied in all groups within 1.5 h of anesthesia. Theta-like high-frequency stimulation was delivered at Schaffer Collateral [TBS: three sets of 10 trains, each consisting of 10 pulses at 200 Hz for juvenile animals; intertrain interval: 200 ms; interest interval: 1 min]. For all experiments, baseline responses were established using delivering stimulation intensity (300–800 μA) sufficient to elicit a response representing ∼40 to 50% of the maximal amplitude of the evoked field potentials. TBS was delivered at the same intensity and pulse duration as the test stimuli while establishing the baseline responses. Evoked fPSPs at the baseline intensity were recorded from the CA1 for up to 60 min following the application of TBS. The results were presented as the mean of each 5 min to give 12 time points. LTP is defined as an increase in the amplitude of the fPSPs. Changes in fPSP amplitude were measured for each rat as a percentage of change from its baseline42,69,70.

Following completion of the electrophysiological recordings, the rats were euthanised by decapitation under deep anesthesia, and their brains were collected and stored at −80 °C. Coronal sections (50 µm) were prepared using a cryostat. Sections were subsequently Nissl-stained, and cannula and electrode placements were verified under a light microscope (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Drugs and administration

For microinjections of OXT into the CA1, we used the selective OXTR agonist [Thr4, Gly7]-(TGOT) (Biotest, Israel), delivered at a dose of 7 ng, injected at a volume of 0.5 μl over 1 min. A 28 mm gauge injection cannula was inserted into the guide cannula. The injection cannula was connected via PE20 tubing to a Hamilton micro syringe driven by a microinfusion pump (Harvard Apparatus, USA). The injection cannula was left in place for an additional 1 min to minimise the dragging of the injected solution along the injection tract.

In patch-clamp experiments, 400 nm TGOT was infused into the perfusing artificial cerebrospinal fluid (ACSF).

Brain slice electrophysiology and whole-cell patch clamp

The slice electrophysiology and recording parameters were used as described previously72,73. Briefly, the juvenile rats (PND 27–30) were deeply anesthetized using isoflurane, while brains were extracted following decapitation. 300-μm thick coronal brain slices were obtained with a Campden-1000® Vibratome. Slices were cut in an ice-cold sucrose-based cutting solution containing the following (in mM): 25 NaHCO3, 105 Choline-Chloride, 2.5 KCl, 7 MgCl2, 0.5 CaCl2, 1.25 NaH2PO4, 25 D-Glucose, 1 Na-Ascorbate, and 3 Na-Pyruvate. The slices were allowed to recover for 30 min at 37 °C in ACSF containing the following (in mM): 125 NaCl, 2.5 KCl, 1.25 NaH2PO4, 25 NaHCO3, 25 D-glucose, 2 CaCl2, and 1 MgCl2. Slices were then kept for an additional 30 min in ACSF at room temperature until electrophysiological recording. The solutions were constantly gassed with carbogen (95% O2, 5% CO2).

Recording parameters

Baseline and intrinsic properties

At the time of baseline recording, resting membrane potential (RMP) will be measured 10 s after the beginning of the whole-cell recording (rupture of the membrane under the recording pipette). The dependence of the firing rate on the injected current was obtained by injection of current steps (of 500 ms duration from −150 to 500 pA in 50 pA increments). The input resistance was calculated from a steady-state voltage in response to a hyperpolarizing current pulse (−150 pA). The sag ratio was calculated from voltage response −150 pA. The sag ratio during the hyperpolarizing steps was calculated as [(1-ΔVSS/ΔV max) × 100%] as previously reported74. The membrane time constant will be determined using a single exponential fit in the first 100 ms of the raising phase of cell response to a 500 ms, −150 pA hyperpolarization step.

For measurements of a single action potential (AP), after initial assessment of the current required to induce an AP at 15 ms from the start of the current injection with significant steps (50 pA), a series of brief depolarizing currents were injected for 10 ms in steps of 10 pA increments. The first AP that appeared on the 5 ms time point was analyzed. A curve of dV/dt was created for that trace, and the 30 V/s point in the rising slope of the AP was considered as the threshold72. AP amplitude will be measured from the equipment point of the threshold to the spike peak, whereas AP duration will be measured at the point of half amplitude of the spike. The medium after-hyperpolarization (mAHP) will be calculated using prolonged (3 s), high-amplitude (3 nA) somatic current injections to initiate time-locked AP trains of 50 Hz frequency and duration (10–50 Hz, 1 or 3 s) in pyramidal cells. These AP trains generated prolonged (20 s) AHPs, the amplitudes and integrals of which increased with the number of APs in the spike train. AHP will be measured from the equipotential point of the threshold to the anti-peak of the same spike75 fast (fAHP) and slow AHP (sAHP) measurements were identified as previously described in refs. 74,76.

To determine the effect of OXTR agonist on the intrinsic properties of CA1 pyramidal neurons, the slices were perfused with an ASCF containing OXTR agonist TGOT 400 nm following the baseline recordings. All the intrinsic properties, as mentioned above, were performed under the TGOT perfusion. A single neuron is recorded from each slice, and 3–4 slices from a single rat. The measurements were compared between the baseline and after the TGOT treatments. Series resistance, rin, and membrane capacitance were monitored during the entire experiment. Changes of at least 30% in these parameters were criteria for exclusion of data.

Synaptic properties

To determine the effect of OXT on the synaptic properties of CA1 pyramidal neurons, both spontaneous excitatory postsynaptic currents and spontaneous inhibitory postsynaptic currents, sEPSCs, and sIPSCs, respectively, from the same neuron, before and after the application of OXTR agonist TGOT in the perusing ASCF. First, a baseline recording was obtained (3 min) before the application of TGOT (400 nm for 1 min), followed by a washout (3–5 min). 60-s recordings were used to analyze the effect of TGOT on the sEPSCs and sIPSCs before, during, and after the TGOT application. A single neuron is recorded from each slice, and 3–4 slices from a single rat.

To record both sEPSCs and sIPSCs, the recording electrode was pulled from a borosilicate glass pipette (3–5 M) using an electrode puller (P-1000; Sutter Instruments®) and filled with an internal solution containing 120 mM cesium methanesulfonate, 10 mM HEPES, 0.5 mM EGTA, 8 mM NaCl, 4 mM Mg-ATP, 1 mM QX-314, 10 mM sodium phosphocreatine and 0.4 mM sodium GTP (pH 7.25, 295 mosM). To isolate both sEPSCs and sIPSCs from a single cell, the membrane voltages were held at the neuron’s reversal potential for excitatory or inhibitory currents (0 and −70 mV, respectively). The E: I ratio was calculated by dividing the sEPSCs frequencies by sIPSCs frequencies from a single cell46.

Data were acquired with SutterPatch® software (Sutter Instruments®) and recorded with a Double IPA® Integrated Patch Clamp Amplifiers with Data Acquisition System (Sutter Instruments®). All synaptic data were sampled at 20 kHz and filtered at 2 kHz. Series resistance, Rin, and membrane capacitance were monitored throughout, and experiments where resistance changed > 30% were discarded. Data was analyzed using SutterPatch® software (version 3.0, Sutter Instrument).

Statistics and reproducibility

The group size was determined for the in vivo electrophysiology experiments based on previously published results using similar methods38,40,42,43,69,71. Shapiro–Wilk test was used for identifying the normal distribution of the data. Differences among the groups were determined using a two-tailed independent t-test or ANOVA. The results were analyzed using ANOVA with drug and sex as between-subject factors with repeated measures of the time factor when appropriate. In ex vivo patch clamp electrophysiology, Two-way repeated measurements of ANOVA followed by Šídák’s or Tukey’s post hoc multiple comparison tests were performed for firing properties. The intrinsic properties were determined with two-tailed paired t-tests. Multiple comparisons were corrected post hoc with Dunn’s for one-way ANOVA. D’Agostino and Pearson tests were used to identify the normal distribution of the data. The Wilcoxon matched-pairs signed rank test was used to determine the non-normal distribution of the data.

The exact number of n is provided in the figure legends and results section, where applicable. In in vivo electrophysiology, n denotes the number of animals; in ex vivo patch-clamp experiments, n denotes the number of cells recorded, with the number of animals (N) contributing cells indicated where relevant. All experiments were independently replicated across multiple animals. Electrophysiological findings were reproduced, and key effects were observed consistently across animals and recording sessions.

All data were reported as mean ± SEM. For all tests, *P < 0.05 was considered significant. Data were analysed using Prism (GraphPad, version 10.4.1) and SPSS. Data collection and analysis were not performed blindly to the experimental conditions.
