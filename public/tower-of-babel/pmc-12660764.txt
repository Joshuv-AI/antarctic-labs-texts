An improved YOLO11 UAV toy models target detection model
Authors: Yanting Hu, Xingchen Pu, Sheng Feng, Qinyong Zeng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The challenges of Unmanned Aerial Vehicle (UAV) military target detection mainly come from the lack of training datasets and the requirements of high real-time and high precision. Due to the particularity and confidentiality of military-related data, publicly available military target datasets are very scarce. In this study, ArmoredCar, FixedWing, and Tank toy models were photographed in different environments and manually annotated to construct the Toy-3 dataset. Then, a multi-scene image enhancement method was designed based on the Toy-3 dataset. By simulating the common degradation factors in the UAV image acquisition process, more complex and diverse training data were generated, and an extended dataset Toy-3-Enhanced, which is closer to the actual mission scene, was constructed. Subsequently, the improved network Toy-Efficient-YOLO11 was proposed with YOLO11 as the benchmark model. The experimental results on Toy-3-Enhanced show that the improved model has a mAP50 of 0.982, a mAP50:95 of 0.805, and a Frames Per Second (FPS) of 333.33 frames/second, which is superior to the YOLO11n baseline model in terms of detection accuracy and real-time performance, providing a good technical solution for the UAV military target detection task.

Introduction

Unmanned Aerial Vehicle (UAVs) are increasingly used in the military field1, especially in military target detection tasks. The core task of military target detection is to identify and locate targets such as armored cars, fixed-wing aircraft, bunkers, tanks, etc. from images taken by drones2. However, this task faces some challenges3. On the one hand, target detection tasks require a large number of high-quality data sets as a training basis4–6, but due to the particularity and confidentiality brought about by the military field, public platforms lack data sets of military targets from the perspective of drones, which limits the application of models in military target detection tasks. On the other hand, when performing military target detection tasks, drones often need to detect and respond to targets in real time, which places extremely high demands on the real-time and accuracy of detection7–10. Although traditional target detection models perform well in accuracy, they often have great limitations in real-time performance11–13. At the same time, drone platforms usually have the characteristics of limited computing resources14. Therefore, how to reduce computing overhead and improve reasoning speed while ensuring high detection accuracy has emerged as a critical challenge to address in drone military target detection.

Most early detection methods relied on traditional image processing techniques. Although such methods are relatively simple and direct to implement, feature extraction relies on manual design and is easily affected by environmental interference, so their performance in complex scenes is not ideal15. For example, edge detection and template matching methods can identify simple targets, but they are less robust to changes in illumination, background complexity, and target deformation, and their accuracy is limited16. As machine learning continues to evolve, approaches such as support vector machines (SVM) and random forests have been adopted in target detection. These methods use manually designed features to classify and locate targets. Although they have improved detection accuracy to a certain extent, they are still limited by the complexity of feature extraction and insufficient generalization ability17.

The introduction of convolutional neural networks (CNNs) has brought object de-tection technology into a new stage. CNNs can automatically learn high-level features from data, get rid of the reliance on manually designed features, and significantly enhance the precision and robustness of object detection18. CNN-based object detection methods, such as the R-CNN series of models have optimized the candidate region generation mechanism by introducing the region proposal network (RPN)19–21, further improving detection efficiency. Although these methods have high detection accuracy, they have a large amount of calculation and a slow processing speed, making it difficult to meet real-time requirements. Especially in complex scenarios, computing resources become the main bottleneck22.

To solve this problem, many single-stage target detection methods have emerged over the past few years, exemplified by YOLO (You Only Look Once) and SSD (Single Shot Multibox Detector). These methods do not need to generate candidate regions first, but directly perform regression prediction of target boxes on the input image, which greatly improves the detection speed23,24. SSD uses multi-scale feature maps to im-prove the detection ability of targets of different sizes and performs well in multi-target detection23. YOLO directly predicts the bounding box and category information of the target through regression, and divides the image into multiple grids, each of which is responsible for detecting the target independently, thereby achieving efficient target detection24. Detection frameworks such as YOLO and SSD, which use a single-stage approach, typically face a trade-off between accuracy and real-time processing. In order to further improve the detection performance, the YOLO series models are constantly improved. For example, YOLOv4 and YOLOv5 have achieved higher accuracy and speed by optimizing the network structure and introducing new training strategies25,26. These improvements make the YOLO series models more accurate and efficient when facing complex environments and diverse target detection tasks. Nevertheless, ensuring high real-time performance while improving detection accuracy remains a key challenge in the field of UAV military target detection27,28.

At present, there has been some progress in aerial target detection missions, some studies have used synthetic samples obtained through offline mosaic enhancement to extract accurate boundaries of human instances from complex backgrounds by oversampling local regions of volumetric images using ISCP modules. VTSaRNet cross integrates global features and encodes the correlation between two modalities through multi head attention modules, adaptively recalibrating the channel response of some feature maps. It can be combined with anchor based detectors using transformer modules for fusion operations29. Researchers have proposed a comprehensive airborne personnel detection (APD) dataset called “VTSaR”, which provides greater diversity of scenes, different personnel behaviors, flexible capture angles, different capture heights, and includes aligned visible and infrared samples as well as synthetic samples for a wide range of potential applications related to APD30. In addition, research has proposed a lightweight distillation network (APDNet) for edge deployment of APD, which can achieve real-time inference and minimize accuracy loss during model transmission, and can locate personnel in remote and low light areas31. A study has proposed using SPD Conv instead of Conv to extract multi-scale features in the feature extraction stage of the YOLOv8 original model, which can reduce the loss of fine-grained information and introduce GAM attention mechanism in the neck to enhance the fusion of target features and improve the detection ability of the device for small targets32. Research has found that based on the YOLOv8 model, a three-layer PAFPN structure is adopted, combined with a detection head tailored for small objects using large-scale feature maps, which can significantly enhance the algorithm’s ability to detect small targets33. In terms of lightweighting, researchers have proposed a lightweight spatial pyramid pool structure for RGSPP, which uses group convolution and random channel rearrangement to reduce the computational cost of the model and improve its generalization ability34. In addition, research has proposed a Global Local Feature Enhancement Network (GLF Net) and a multi-scale feature fusion module, which can extract effective features from complex objects and solve the problem of the existence of a large number of small targets in drone images through multi-scale object detection and feature fusion35. However, there is relatively little research on aerial detection in the military field. The detection targets are mainly military vehicles. In terms of detection algorithms and optimization techniques, current research mainly focuses on deep learning algorithms, emphasizing lightweight optimization of algorithms. Single stage detection algorithms in deep learning, such as the YOLO series algorithms, are commonly used in military target detection research due to their high detection speed and ability to meet real-time detection requirements in military scenarios. At the same time, in order to adapt to the limited computing resources of drones, researchers will adopt optimization techniques such as lightweight network design, parameter pruning, and parameter quantization to compress and optimize deep learning models, in order to improve the efficiency of algorithms on drone embedded platforms, while ensuring a certain level of detection accuracy and ensuring that military target detection tasks can be executed in real-time and stably. Overall, although preliminary research has been conducted on military target detection based on UAVs, the research scale is relatively small, the depth is insufficient, and there is a lack of research on its scene specificity. Further exploration is needed to develop algorithms that adapt to the characteristics of military targets and their special environmental adaptability, in order to improve the performance of UAV military target detection.

In response to the above problems, this study proposed a method for detecting UAV military targets by improving YOLO11. First, in response to the problem of scarce military target datasets, this study selected three main types of military detection targets, purchased toy models of corresponding models, leveraged cameras to gather photographic data of toy models in varied scenarios, and manually annotated the targets in the images to construct the dataset Toy-3, which can provide high-quality data support for model training.Then, a multi-scene image enhancement method is designed for common image degradation factors in the actual image acquisition process of UAVs. An extended dataset Toy-3-Enhanced is constructed based on the Toy-3 dataset. By simulating a variety of complex scenes, the diversity and complexity of training samples are effectively increased, thereby improving the robustness and generalization ability of the detection model in the complex environment of UAV actual missions. Secondly, in terms of model selection, while the two-stage detection model performs well in accuracy, it takes a long processing time and is difficult to meet the real-time detection requirements of UAVs. The YOLO series model adopts a single-stage detection method and has significant advantages in speed. YOLO11 is one of the latest target detection algorithm models, with high detection accuracy and high inference speed. This study uses the YOLO11 model as a benchmark, optimizes and improves it, and proposes the Toy-Efficient-YOLO11 network. Finally, the results of the experiments on the Toy-3-Enhanced dataset indicate that the model performs better in efficiency and precision, meeting the requirements of UAV military object detection for efficient computing and high accuracy. Image dataset of UAV military detection targets

For training the deep learning model, we initially obtained video and image data related to wars and military exercises from public channels. However, most of these data come from parallel perspectives or remote sensing data, and lack images of military targets taken from the perspective of drones. In addition, high-quality image data is scarce, and the target details in many images are blurred, resulting in the inability to fully learn target features during model training, resulting in false positives and false negatives. Therefore, we decided to build a high-quality image dataset for drone military target detection.

Image acquisition

In order to simulate targets, we purchased small toy models of various military targets. Most of the toy models are in military green or desert camouflage to better simulate the appearance of targets in real scenes. Multiple toy models are included in one captured image. The collection scenes include asphalt roads, stone roads, grass, bushes and wooden boardwalks. During the image acquisition process, we ensured that each target in each scene has sufficient resolution and has a clear contrast with the background to enhance the model’s generalization capability in different environments. Then, for the needs of practical applications, we finally selected three most representative targets for data annotation and detection, namely: ArmoredCar, FixedWing and Tank, because these targets are more common in battlefield environ-ments. In addition, by limiting the quantity of object categories detected in the dataset, the model can better focus on these key categories of targets, thereby improving detection accuracy and training efficiency. We collected a total of 1593 qualified images. Figure 1 presents several image examples from the Toy-3 dataset.

Image annotation

After acquiring the image data, we focused on the annotation work of this image dataset. During the annotation process, we utilized the open-source annotation tool Labelme, which is widely recognized in computer vision research for its versatility and compatibility with various annotation tasks, and can accurately annotate the targets in our images.

To ensure the consistency and accuracy of all annotations, we established a set of strict guidelines. Each target object (ArmoredCar, FixedWing, Tank) was annotated with a tight bounding box. It was required that the bounding box closely fit the visible boundaries of the target and contained the minimum number of background pixels. For objects that were partially occluded or not fully captured, the bounding box was drawn to include all their visible parts. Each annotation file contains target category information and target bounding box information. Specifically, the content of the annotated file includes: target category labels (ArmoredCar, FixedWing, Tank) and the pixel coordinates of the four vertices of the target bounding box.

The initial annotation process was jointly completed by three authors. Subsequently, 20% of the annotated images were randomly selected, and the three authors conducted re-verification to identify and correct any errors. This two-stage process significantly improved the reliability of the annotations. We completed the entire annotation process within two weeks, including the initial annotation and the quality review.

Multi-Scenario image enhancement simulation

In order to more realistically approximate the imaging characteristics of drones under different environmental conditions, this study models and performs image enhancement processing for typical scenes such as night, dense fog, rain, and vibration blur. During the image enhancement process, different scene types are superimposed on the image in a random combination to generate diverse training samples.

Nighttime imaging simulation

Nighttime imaging is usually affected by the following factors: low light, color temperature shift, enhanced noise in the dark areas, and motion blur caused by long exposure. Therefore, the image is enhanced from the following aspects. Firstly, the nonlinear Gamma correction method is adopted to compress the image brightness, simulating the uneven brightness distribution and detail loss caused by insufficient illumination at night. Unlike linear transformation, when Gamma < 1, the dark parts of the image will be enhanced, which is closer to the visual characteristics of night imaging. Furthermore, based on the blackbody radiation theory, the color temperature of the image is adjusted to 4000 K through the Kelvin temperature conversion function to simulate the lighting effect that is relatively cold (blue-white) or relatively warm (orange-yellow) in the night scene. And superimpose a low-concentration blue-gray fog layer to simulate the slight fog sensation produced by the wet and cold environment at night. The intensity of the fog is adaptively calculated based on the average brightness of the image. Then, the noise in the dark areas of the image is added. Poisson noise and Gaussian noise respectively simulate the photon statistical fluctuations and the noise of the electronic system under low illumination, enhancing the authenticity of night imaging.Then, directional convolution kernels are adopted to achieve directional blurring. Random angles are selected within the Angle range of -10° to + 10° to construct linear blurring kernels, simulating the image tailing phenomenon caused by slight jitter or long exposure during night flights.Finally, a mild contrast enhancement is performed on the image to compensate for the grayscale compression caused by haze and blurriness and enhance the detail layering in the dark areas.

Haze weather simulation

Haze weather, due to the scattering effect of suspended particles (aerosols) in the air on light, leads to a decrease in image contrast, blurred details, and an overall bluish-gray tone. Therefore, the RGB fog layer is constructed first. Through the weighted fusion of the image and the fog layer, a blue-gray fog effect with a higher concentration and lower transparency is achieved. Then, mild Gaussian and Poisson noises are added to simulate the imaging fluctuations caused by the non-uniform propagation of light in haze; Finally, appropriately reduce the image contrast to present a hazy and turbid effect.

Rainy weather simulation

In rainy conditions, raindrops and rain strips in front of the lens can cause refraction, blurring and scattering effects during the imaging process, resulting in local bright spots, stripes and blurring, and reducing the clarity of the image. First, several vertical lines are randomly generated in the image to simulate the rapidly falling raindrops. Motion blur with a 90-degree direction is applied to the layer containing the raindrops to simulate the trailing effect of the raindrops, and the raindrop layer is fused with the original image. Add moderate-intensity noise to simulate the noise of the sensor on rainy days.

Motion blur simulation

Under conditions of wind speed variation or unstable flight attitude, the slight jitter generated by the UAV can lead to blurred imaging motion, affecting the edge sharpness of the target and the overall clarity. First, construct the directional blur kernel, set the kernel size to 6 (blur length) and the direction of motion (blur angle) to ± 30°, and simulate the blur effect caused by flight jitter. Then add sensor noise of moderate intensity to simulate the stability degradation of the image caused by vibration.

Multi-scenario composite image enhancement

In the practical application of UAVs, they are often jointly influenced by multiple environmental factors. For instance, rainfall may occur during night flights. Therefore, in order to simulate the imaging degradation phenomenon under complex environmental conditions more realistically and enhance the robustness of the model to complex environments, this study adopted a strategy of multi-scene combined enhancement. This strategy is to randomly combine one or more scene simulations during the image enhancement process and perform superimposed enhancement processing on the same image. This strategy aims to enhance the environmental complexity and diversity of the training samples, which is conducive to training a more robust object detection model and improving the adaptability and detection performance of the model in real complex scenarios.

Toy-Efficient-YOLO11 methodology

YOLO11

YOLO (You Only Look Once) is a real-time target detection algorithm. Its basic idea is to predict the bounding box and category information of the target directly from the image in a single forward propagation process, thereby achieving end-to-end detection. In the YOLO model, the image is divided into a fixed-size grid (such as 7 × 7 or 13 × 13). Each grid unit is responsible for predicting an object contained in the image. Each prediction contains not only the coordinates of the bounding box, but also the category probability of the object in the grid. The YOLO model processes target detection by regression, and the output includes multiple bounding boxes and correspond-ing confidence scores. These boxes may or may not contain the target. Finally, the optimal detection box is selected through non-maximum suppression (NMS). YOLO has developed multiple versions, from YOLOv2, YOLOv3, YOLOv4 to the current YOLO11, and the algorithm has been gradually optimized to improve the detection accu-racy36–41, reasoning speed and model robustness. Researchers continuously improve and optimize the YOLO series models based on practical application scenarios to enhance their performance in specific tasks42–47.

YOLO11 was released in September 2024 and was developed by Ultralytics. YOLOv5 and YOLOv8 were also developed by the company. The structure of YOLO11 is shown in Fig. 6 below. Its main innovation is the introduction of new mechanisms such as the C3k2 module and the C2PSA block. The C3k2 module is a computationally efficient implementation of the cross-stage partial (CSP) bottleneck. It replaces the C2f block in the backbone and neck and uses two smaller convolutions instead of one large convolution, thereby reducing processing time. The second point is the C2PSA module, which introduces the cross-stage partial spatial attention (C2PSA) module after the Spatial Pyramid Pooling–Fast (SPPF) module to enhance spatial attention. This attention mechanism enables the model to focus on important areas in the image more effectively, which may improve detection accuracy. The third point is to add two deep convolutions (DWconv) to the classification detection head in the original decoupling head, which significantly lowers the number of parameters and computations. YOLO11 has high accuracy and high real-time performance. We selected YOLO11n (the smallest model of YOLO11) as the benchmark model and continued to optimize and im-prove it to improve its performance.

YOLO11 structure diagram.

Recently, researchers have made the following improvements to the YOLO11 algorithm model. For example, combining the detection head of the original model with the Adaptive Spatial Feature Fusion (ASFF) module, and embedding the Convolutional Block Attention Module (CBAM) attention mechanism in the BiFPN output, can improve the efficiency of defect feature fusion in the model and be deployed on lightweight and small devices48. When studying human object detection, the introduction of the MASRCNet feature extraction module can enhance the model’s ability to extract and fuse high-dimensional and low dimensional features as well as contextual information49. In addition, researchers have used FasterNet blocks to replace bottleneck structures in traditional C3K2 networks and introduced a new efficient multi-scale attention module (EMA) that can improve computational efficiency and performance, reduce computational bottlenecks, and accelerate training and inference processes. It has been greatly applied in surface defect detection applications50.

Strengthening to backbone

For the backbone part, we learned about a simple and powerful new visual back-bone: VanillaNet51. The key advancements of this structure include: first, its minimalist design: no residual connection, no high-depth or complex modules (such as self-attention or SE blocks), only a small number of layers are used, and all convolutional layers are 1*1 kernels, only the channel dimension is adjusted, which greatly reduces the computational cost. At the same time, spatial downsampling relies entirely on maximum pooling, avoiding stride convolution. Second, its deep training strategy: nonlinear activation functions (such as ReLU) are introduced at the beginning of training, and gradually degenerate them into identity mapping (Identity). After train-ing, adjacent convolutional layers can be merged into a single layer to reduce inference latency. Third, by stacking multiple activation functions in parallel, the nonlinear ability of shallow networks is enhanced. VanillaNet enables efficient deployment due to its easy-to-understand and highly simplified architecture. A large number of experiments have shown that VanillaNet provides excellent performance comparable to the well-known deep neural network visual Transformer. We introduced a 5-layer VanillaNet backbone, and the structure is described in detail below.

The architecture of the 5-layer VanillaNet is divided into 5 stages. The initial stage uses a 4*4*3*C convolution layer with a stride of 4 to map a 3-channel image to a feature with C channels. In the next three stages, 1*1 convolution kernel convolution and a maximum pooling layer with a stride of 2 are used to extract features and reduce the size. Meanwhile, the channels are doubled at each stage. The last stage is a fully connected layer, and the convolution kernel size is still 1*1. In the entire architecture, a 1*1 kernel is employed in the convolution, which extracts image features while minimizing computational load in each stage. In the architecture, each 1*1 convolution layer is followed by an activation function and batch normalization. In this paper, we reduced the number of channels in the original architecture to 0.25 times, and then introduced this structure into the backbone part of the YOLO11 model. Strengthening to neck

EfficientViM-C3k2

EfficientViM is a series of novel lightweight visual backbone networks based on the Mamba architecture52. At its core is a fast and efficient SSD variant called HSM-SSD, and the HSM-SSD structure diagram is shown in the Fig. 8. Unlike the traditional SSD layer that performs channel mixing directly on the image feature map through linear projection and gating, this method migrates these operations to the hidden state space. These hidden states act as compact potential representations of the input, effectively alleviating the main computational bottleneck of the SSD layer while retaining the generalization ability of the model. In addition, a multi-stage fusion mechanism of hidden states is proposed, where predictions are generated by merging the original logical vector with the logical vector derived from the hidden states of each stage. This technique significantly enhances the representational power of the hidden state.

Fig. 8Schematic diagram of the HSM-SSD layer in the EfficientViM module. (a) NC-SSD layer; (b) HSM-SSD layer.

Schematic diagram of the HSM-SSD layer in the EfficientViM module. (a) NC-SSD layer; (b) HSM-SSD layer.

Through a detailed runtime analysis of the HSM-SSD layer, a macro-level design strategy is also introduced to reduce memory-intensive operations, thereby optimizing the actual performance of real applications, rather than focusing only on theoretical indicators such as FLOPs.

In summary, EfficientViM, a new lightweight architecture based on mamba, has a structural diagram as shown in the Fig. 9. It fully utilizes the linear computational efficiency of the global token mixer; and introduces HSM-SSD, which can control the computational overhead of the SSD layer by adjusting the number of hidden states; by minimizing memory-bound operations and integrating multi-stage hidden state fusion, EfficientViM sets a new standard in terms of speed and accuracy performance trade-offs.

The EfficientViM module is introduced in the neck part, integrating the efficient state space modeling mechanism HSM-SSD and the feedforward network FFN to replace the original C3k2 module to improve feature modeling capabilities and reasoning efficiency.

New concat

In YOLO series object detection models, the primary function of the neck is to effectively integrate features from various levels, typically using the Concat operation53–56. The Concat operation in the neck sections of YOLO series object detection models follow a straightforward principle: concatenation along the channel dimension. The core steps are described as follows. First is upsampling and alignment: to fuse two feature maps of different scales, the deep feature map is upsampled to make its spatial size consistent with that of the shallow feature map. Then, the two feature maps with the same spatial size are concatenated along the channel dimension. The concatenated feature map is fed into a convolutional module, whose purpose is to fuse the newly concatenated feature information from different layers and ultimately output the fused feature map.

The standard Concat module provides as much raw information as possible. It does not perform any filtering or weighting, and retains all input features without discrimination: regardless of whether the features are redundant or relevant to the target, every channel of both shallow features (rich in detailed information) and deep features (rich in semantic information) is completely preserved. This process merely stacks features from different sources together and then hands them over to subsequent convolutional layers to learn how to fuse and utilize this information.

The improved fusion module can achieve more efficient feature fusion. The specific method is as follows: first, align feature maps of different scales. Adaptive pooling is used to reduce larger feature maps, and bilinear interpolation enlarges smaller feature maps, ensuring uniform size across all maps and retain multi-scale semantic information. At the same time, multiple convolutional layers are used to map the number of channels of each feature map to the same number of channels, ensuring that all feature maps have the same channel dimension in subsequent processing, realizing channel space alignment of cross-scale features, and extracting local context information. Then, through element-by-element multiplication (multiplication of elements in the same position of two matrices or vectors, the result is still a matrix or vector of the same size), multiple channel-aligned feature maps are fused. Element-by-element multiplication can suppress low-response areas (such as background noise) and strengthen high-activation areas common to multi-scale features (such as target edges). This method can emphasize the common information in different feature maps and reduce redundant and unimportant feature information.

In the neck of the YOLO11 model, replacing the original Concat module with this module offers the following advantages.

The first advantage is the reduction of computational overhead. The standard Concat module stacks channels, leading to an exponential increase in feature dimensions. Subsequent convolutional layers or detection heads must process this expanded dimension, resulting in a significant rise in floating-point operations (FLOPs) and a slowdown in inference speed. In contrast, the new module uses channel alignment to unify the number of channels of all input feature maps to a fixed value, directly reducing the computational load of downstream networks, lowering computational complexity and the number of model parameters. This makes the model more lightweight and inference faster, which is highly suitable for edge device deployment. For UAV real-time detection tasks that are sensitive to latency, this improvement is crucial.

The second advantage is the reduction of information redundancy and the enhancement of feature fusion quality. The standard Concat module retains all features through channel stacking, which simultaneously preserves redundant information and duplicate semantic features. This forces subsequent networks to process a large amount of irrelevant data, diverting the model’s focus from target-related features. The element-wise multiplication operation of the new module is similar to an attention mechanism; inherently, it strengthens consistent features shared across multiple scales. Only regions that are highly activated across feature maps of different scales are retained and enhanced in the fusion result. In contrast, noise or background information that appears only in one scale is suppressed through multiplication with low-response values from other scales. This achieves “feature purification” and improves the quality of fused features.

Strengthening to head

In the YOLO standard architecture, larger objects are usually better detected in higher-level feature maps, while small objects are easily missed or cannot be accurately detected57,58. Therefore, we added the P2 layer to optimize the detection of small objects. By introducing lower-level convolutional features, we can better capture the details of small objects and avoid losing this information in the high-level feature maps of the network59–62.

The detection head of the original YOLO11 model usually runs on three layers (P3, P4, P5). The detection head corresponding to the P3/8 feature layer in the model is mainly used to detect relatively small objects, and its feature map size is large and the spatial resolution is high. The feature layer corresponding to the P4/16 detection head has undergone more downsampling operations. Compared with the P3/8 feature map, the spatial resolution is reduced, but the number of channels is increased. The features contain more semantic information and are mainly used to detect medium-sized objects. The P5/32 layer is the feature layer obtained after the most downsampling operations. It has the lowest spatial resolution, but the strongest semantic information and the largest global receptive field. This detection head is suitable for detecting larger objects.

The new P2/4 detection head uses early features with richer spatial information to identify smaller objects. This improvement is designed to address the limitation of the original standard architecture that may ignore smaller objects due to insufficient resolution. By adding the P2 layer, our model can effectively capture details at more scales, thereby achieving more accurate small object detection in complex scenes.

Toy-Efficient-YOLO11

As mentioned earlier, Toy-Efficient-YOLO11 optimizes and improves the back-bone, neck, and head based on YOLO11. These improvements are designed to better meet the challenges of UAV military target detection, such as the need for high-precision detection and the need for real-time processing. The overall architecture of Toy-Efficient-YOLO11 integrates a lightweight but powerful backbone and an efficient multi-scale feature fusion mechanism. The structure diagram of Toy-Efficient-YOLO11 is illustrated in Fig. 10.

Fig. 10Toy-Efficient-YOLO11 structure diagram.

Toy-Efficient-YOLO11 structure diagram.

The backbone of Toy-Efficient-YOLO11 is based on a streamlined 5-layer Vanil-laNet structure, replacing the original backbone in YOLO11. The backbone is characterized by its minimalist design, using only 1*1 convolution and maximum pooling layers to extract features, avoiding complex operations such as residual connections or self-attention mechanisms. The use of 1*1 convolution can greatly reduce the computational overhead, and spatial downsampling is achieved through maximum pooling, which further reduces the computational complexity. In addition, the architecture adopts a deep training strategy, and the nonlinear activation function gradually degenerates into an identity mapping during the training process. This allows adjacent convolutional layers to be merged into a single layer during inference, significantly reducing inference latency without compromising performance. In this study, we introduce the backbone after reducing the number of channels in the original VanillaNet to 0.25 times. This lightweight backbone ensures efficient feature extraction and is well suited for UAV platforms.

In the neck of Toy-Efficient-YOLO11, the original Concat operation is replaced by a new feature fusion module. The module first uses adaptive pooling and bilinear interpolation to align feature maps of different scales to ensure consistent spatial dimensions and retain multi-scale semantic information. This alignment is essential for processing objects of different sizes. After alignment, multiple convolutional layers are used to map the channels of each feature map to a unified dimension, thereby achieving cross-scale feature fusion and extracting local context information. The aligned feature maps are then fused using element-by-element multiplication to suppress low-response areas and enhance high-activation areas. This method highlights the common information between feature maps and reduces redundant features. Finally, the results are input into subsequent layers, further enhancing the expressive power of multi-scale features. This module can not only improve detection accuracy, but also reduce computational redundancy, making the neck more efficient and able to adapt to the needs of complex situations.

At the same time, we introduced the EfficientViM module in the neck part to replace the original C3k2 module. EfficientViM is a new lightweight visual network based on the Mamba architecture. It uses the innovative HSM-SSD layer to improve the efficiency of feature expression, and adopts efficient information compression and multi-stage fusion strategies in the hidden state space, while taking into account excellent accuracy and speed.

In the detection head of Toy-Efficient-YOLO11, we added a P2 small target detection layer. For the original YOLO model, its original structure is three detection heads (P3, P4, P5), which represent the detection capabilities of the detection head for relatively small scales, medium scales, and large scales, respectively. However, for small target objects, there are problems such as fewer features, unclear semantic features, and continuous convolution causing features to be easily submerged. Therefore, we added a P2 layer detection head to improve the detection capability of small targets. The addition of the P2 layer not only enriches the feature extraction for smaller objects, but also improves the multi-scale expression capability, making the detection results more accurate.

Experimental environment and training settings

The experiment was carried out on a computer featuring an Intel Core i9-14900KF CPU and 64GB of RAM, and an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4090 GPU with 24GB of video memory. The operating system was Ubuntu 20.04, and the Linux kernel version was 5.15.0-131. We used CUDA 12.2 for GPU accelerated training.

The specific parameters of model training in this study are as follows: batch size is 8, epochs are 300 rounds, and input image size is 640*640 pixels.

Evaluation metrics

In object detection, common evaluation indicators include precision, recall, average precision (AP), mean average precision (mAP), model parameters, frames per second (FPS), and inference time. These indicators evaluate the algorithm’s performance regarding accuracy and efficiency. Compared with precision and recall, mAP provides a more powerful accuracy measure, especially under different intersection-over-union (IoU) thresholds. Therefore, in this study, we choose mAP50 and mAP50:95 as indica-tors of algorithm effectiveness. In addition, to assess the model’s real-time performance, we also use the processing time for each item and the FPS handled by the model as evaluation metrics. These indicators enable us to comprehensively evaluate the accuracy and efficiency of our proposed method. The following is a detailed introduction to the corresponding indicators.

IoU is an indicator used to evaluate the overlap between the predicted box and the true box. The higher the IoU value, the greater the overlap between the predicted box and the true box. AP stands for the average precision of each category, which is obtained by calculating the precision and recall under all different thresholds. mAP50 means the average precision of all categories when the IoU threshold is 0.5. mAP50:95 is the average precision under multiple IoU thresholds (ranging from 0.5 to 0.95, with a step of 0.05), which represents the average value of mAP under multiple different thresholds of IoU from 0.5 to 0.95, which provides a more complete measure of the model’s detection accuracy, rather than just being limited to a fixed IoU threshold.

FPS refers to the number of image frames that the model can process per second. In object detection, FPS measures the real-time performance and the model’s execution speed. An increase in FPS directly correlates with the model’s ability to process more images per second. Among them, Preprocess refers to the processing time of each image before entering the model for inference. Inference refers to the time the model spends on object detection inference. Loss refers to the time of loss calculation (too small during inference and may not need to be considered). Postprocess refers to the post-processing time after the model is output. Discussion

In response to the challenges faced by unmanned aerial vehicle military target detection, this study proposes a set of solutions, with three core contributions: firstly, a military target dataset Toy-3 based on toy models and its extended dataset Toy-3-Enhanced were constructed, which improved data diversity by simulating various complex scenarios. Secondly, the YOLO11n model was optimized and the Toy-Efficient-YOLO11 model was obtained: VanillaNet was introduced in the Backbone section to achieve lightweighting; Designed a multi-scale dynamic feature fusion module in the Neck section and introduced the EfficientViM module to enhance feature expression capability; Added a small object detection layer to the detection head. The experimental results show that the Toy-Efficient-YOL11 model performs excellently on both our proposed dataset and publicly available drone related datasets. This validates the advantages of the model in detection accuracy and real-time performance, providing an efficient and feasible solution for unmanned aerial vehicle military target detection tasks. In the future, we will consider integrating image generation technology to create target datasets, detect more military targets, and achieve high precision and speed in more complex scenes.
