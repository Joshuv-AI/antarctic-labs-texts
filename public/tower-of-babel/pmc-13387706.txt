Knowledge and awareness of female genital cosmetic surgery among healthcare providers: A cross-sectional study
Authors: Fahad M. Algreisi, Hattan A. Bajahmom, Nada S. AlHarbi, Leena S. Abdulbadie, Ruzanah A. Almarzugi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground:Female genital cosmetic surgery (FGCS) became popular in recent decades. Many patients worldwide consider these procedures for medical or esthetic reasons. Previous literature indicates a significant gap in public understanding of the risks, benefits, and recovery processes associated with FGCS. This study aims to assess the knowledge and attitudes of healthcare providers in Makkah clusters regarding FGCS.Methods:A cross-sectional study was conducted on 142 participants from Makkah cluster facilities, including general practitioners (GPs), gynecologists, surgeons, nurses, medical interns, and medical students, from July to September 2025. Data was analyzed using SPSS software. Descriptive statistics and Chi-square tests were used to measure knowledge and awareness levels, and to assess the associations between cultural beliefs and awareness.Results:Most of the respondents were females and residents. Consultants and specialists showed a lower agreement on having adequate knowledge of FGCS. About 78.2% of workers felt they lacked adequate knowledge of FCGS. Only 26.8% felt confident about labiaplasty, and <10% in other procedures. Nearly half of the participants were not sufficiently aware of the procedures’ risks. During GPs’ consultations of FGCS, the most used information sources were anatomy books. Relationship difficulties and body dysmorphia were the most common identified psychological issues by GPs. Partners’ comments were the most significant social factor influencing women’s decisions in these procedures. Furthermore, GPs were often seen as educators.Conclusion:The study reveals a significant gap in knowledge and confidence among healthcare providers in FGCS. Creating accessible materials and educating healthcare providers can raise awareness and the quality of care.

Introduction

Female genital cosmetic surgery (FGCS), including procedures such as labiaplasty and vaginoplasty, has gained increasing attention in recent years. Patients pursue these procedures for both medical and esthetic reasons, such as addressing functional concerns or enhancing self-esteem.[12] Despite the growing popularity of FGCS, public knowledge and awareness remain limited, largely due to societal taboos and misinformation.

Research indicates a significant gap in public understanding of the risks, benefits, and recovery processes associated with FGCS. For example, a 2020 study published in the Aesthetic Surgery Journal revealed that only 37% of surveyed women were aware of medical indications for these procedures.[2] This highlights the need for accessible, evidence-based information to support informed decision-making.[13]

With increasing public discourse and media influence, FGCS is likely to gain more traction in the near future. A study by Crockett et al. found that 80% of participants were influenced by television shows to consider cosmetic surgery.[45] As societal interest grows, it becomes essential to evaluate healthcare providers’ awareness and perceptions—especially in culturally sensitive settings like Saudi Arabia.

Currently, there is limited research on the knowledge and attitudes of healthcare providers toward FGCS.[46] This study seeks to fill that gap by exploring how cultural beliefs influence their perceptions and level of awareness regarding these procedures. Therefore, our study aims to assess the knowledge and awareness of FGCS among female healthcare providers in Makkah cluster facilities. Additionally, evaluating healthcare practitioners’ knowledge of FGCS is essential for clinical decision-making and the enhancement of consultation regarding the procedure and its risks. Patients also benefit from informed decision-making, safer outcomes, and improved trust in the health system.

Materials and Methods

Study design, setting, and population

A descriptive, cross-sectional study was conducted from July 2025 to September 2025, using a non-probability, convenience sampling technique, among healthcare providers in Makkah Cluster facilities, including general practitioners (GPs), gynecologists, surgeons, and nurses. A total of 142 healthcare providers were surveyed. The questionnaire was developed based on previous literature surveys.[123]

Data extraction and study variables

Data was collected using both paper-based and online questionnaires via Microsoft Forms. The questionnaire was developed based on an extensive review of literature from similar previous studies.[78]

Data analysis

Microsoft Excel 2019 (Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, WA, USA) was used for data entry, and statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 61 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. Chi-square test was used to show the association between awareness about FGCS and sociodemographic data, to present frequencies and percentages. A P value of <0.05 was set as an indication of statistical significance.

Ethical Considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from the Research Committee of the Makkah Health Cluster (reference number H-02-K-076-0625-1369). Participation was voluntary, and informed consent was obtained from all respondents. Confidentiality and anonymity were maintained throughout the study. Collected data was stored securely and accessed only by the principal investigator.

Results

Sociodemographic data

The study surveyed 142 healthcare providers to assess their knowledge and awareness of FGCS and the influence of culture on their perspectives. Most participants were under the age of 35 (80.3%). Female respondents predominated, comprising 71.8% of the sample, while males made up 28.2%. The majority were Saudi nationals (83.1%), with 16.9% identifying as non-Saudi. Regarding education, participants were predominantly residents (35.2%), followed by medical interns (25.4%). Marital status varied, with 57.0% being single and 37.3% married [Table 1]. Most of the participants had 0–10 years of practice (87.9%) [Figure 1].

Awareness and knowledge about female genital cosmetic surgery

The study findings revealed significant gaps in knowledge and confidence among healthcare providers regarding FGCS. A substantial majority (78.2%) felt they lacked adequate knowledge, though 59.9% felt confident assessing female genital appearance, compared to only 40.1% for male patients. Sources of information on FGCS were varied, with media (33.1%) and medical training (32.4%) being the most common. Confidence in advising patients on specific procedures was low, with only 26.8% feeling confident about labiaplasty, and <10% for other procedures such as vaginal rejuvenation (6.3%) or G-spot augmentation (9.2%). Many respondents (51.4%) expressed a need for more information to form an opinion on FGCS, and 35.2% felt competent in managing patients considering such surgeries. Additionally, 56.3% had encountered patients concerned about genital normality, and 55.6% felt comfortable discussing FGCS when requested. However, 57.0% were not sufficiently aware of the risks to discuss them proactively, highlighting a clear need for enhanced education and training in this area [Table 2]. The most common overview about FGCS was a good specialty (54.9%), while 31.7% thought that FGCS is a specialty like any other medical specialty [Figure 2].

Overview about FGCS

General practitioners

The study highlighted the resources, psychosocial concerns, and perceived roles of GPs in consultations related to FGCS. Commonly used information sources included anatomy books (31.0%) and Royal Australian College of General Practitioners (RACGP) FGCS resources (23.2%). GPs often identified psychosocial issues among women seeking FGCS, such as relationship difficulties (30.3%) and body dysmorphic disorder (22.5%). Modifiable social factors influencing women’s decisions included partner comments (35.2%), beauty perceptions (27.5%), and fashion-related concerns about comfort (16.9%). Regarding the GP’s role, 24.6% viewed GPs as educators on FGCS and genital anatomy, while 23.2% saw them as key referral gatekeepers [Table 3]. The most common specialty of interest was internal medicine (71.8%) [Figure 3].

Association of awareness about female genital cosmetic surgery with sociodemographic data

The analysis examined demographic factors associated with healthcare providers’ agreement on having adequate awareness of FGCS, with significant variations observed by education level (P value = 0.003). Among education categories, medical students (100.0%) and medical interns (88.9%) were the most likely to agree they had adequate awareness, while specialists (60.9%) and consultants (20.0%) showed lower agreement levels. Differences by age, gender, and nationality did not approach significance (P value > 0.05). These results highlight the influence of educational background on perceived awareness of FGCS, suggesting targeted educational interventions may be beneficial [Table 4].

Discussion

In this study, we investigated healthcare providers’ attitudes toward FGCS. Among the 142 participants surveyed, a significant variation was found in education level. Medical students and interns demonstrated a more positive attitude than residents, specialists, nurses, and consultants. However, age, gender, and nationality did not show an impact on the results. A previous study in Jeddah measured the attitude of physicians regarding FGCS and concluded with an overall positive attitude. This contraindication is due to the difference in the sample’s education level, as Sawan et al.’s[9] study only included board-certified consultants and registrars of obstetrics and gynecology, plastic surgery, and reconstructive surgery, whereas our sample was varied and included specialties unrelated to women’s health. Moreover, our results are consistent with other studies in Saudi Arabia, which also had a diverse sample, presenting a significant gap in participants’ knowledge.[1011] These studies also found that medical students and young doctors are more knowledgeable about these procedures. However, Iqbal et al.[11] collected opinions on vulvovaginal esthetic procedures regarding medical justification, ethical consideration, and the view that such procedures should fall under the domain of gynecologists rather than plastic surgeons, general surgeons, or cosmetologists, which were not parameters in our study. Our findings suggest a potential gap in medical education. Hence, we recommend integrating training programs, workshops, or courses on FGCS into different education levels to ensure standardized knowledge across all levels of medical practice.

To our knowledge, this study is the first to measure the knowledge and attitudes of various healthcare workers while exploring the experience of GPs in consultations. The study emphasizes the available resources, psychosocial issues, and the perceived responsibilities of GPs during consultations about FGCS. Simonis et al.’s survey was the first large study to examine GPs’ experience of FGCS. Our study findings align with those of Simonis et al., indicating that GPs have limited formal training in FGCS, recognize the importance of psychosocial factors in patient decisions, perceive themselves as educators and referral gatekeepers, and require better resources and guidelines to support consultations.[12] Therefore, we recommend establishing guidelines for GPs to support them in consultation visits regarding FGCS.

Study limitations

Although this study offers valuable insights into FGCS, it has certain limitations. Using a convenience sampling technique may address potential bias, as results may not accurately reflect a wider population. Furthermore, the small sample size of the study limits the generalizability of the findings. Also, because responses were self-reported, participants’ answers may not be accurate. Lastly, because this study was cross-sectional, it does not explain causality, considering that it only presents knowledge and opinions at a single point in time.

Conclusion

This study aimed to assess the knowledge and attitudes of healthcare professionals regarding FGCS. Our study is the first to measure the knowledge of healthcare providers with different education levels, in addition to GPs’ experience in consultations. The study findings suggest that most participants lacked adequate knowledge and confidence in FGCS consultation. Consultants and specialists showed lower knowledge than medical students and interns. Thus, we recommend establishing accessible materials and guidelines on FGCS and integrating training programs and workshops across all levels of medical practice to ensure standardization. The study is limited by convenience sampling technique, small sample size, and self-reported data, which can affect the generalizability of the findings. Therefore, future studies should use longitudinal or interventional approaches with a larger and more representative sample to better assess the knowledge and awareness of FGCS among healthcare professionals.
