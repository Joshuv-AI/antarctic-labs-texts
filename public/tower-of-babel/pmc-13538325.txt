Interface engineering of UiO-66-derived ZrO2@TiO2/Ag(AgCl) multiphase heterostructure for enhanced UV-driven photocatalytic degradation and disinfection
Authors: Dev Kumar, Nelly Fitri Tampubolon, Chao Yi Ting, Tsunghsueh Wu, Yang-Wei Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Efficient separation and transport of photogenerated charge carriers remain key challenges in wide-bandgap semiconductor photocatalysts. Herein, a UiO-66-derived ZrO2@TiO2/Ag(AgCl) multiphase composite was fabricated through a solvothermal–calcination route and evaluated for UV-driven dye degradation and photo-disinfection. X-ray diffraction, electron microscopy, Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy, and UV–visible diffuse reflectance spectroscopy verified the coexistence and intimate interfacial coupling of ZrO2, TiO2, Ag, and AgCl domains. The composite exhibited an apparent optical bandgap of 4.24 eV and enhanced UV-responsive photocatalytic activity. Relative to the reference samples, the optimized composite showed a lower charge-transfer resistance (10.85 Ω), quenched photoluminescence emission, and an approximately 1.9-fold higher transient photocurrent response, supporting improved overall photoinduced carrier transport and extraction. The measured component band-edge potentials provide a thermodynamic framework for a proposed Ag-mediated interfacial charge-transfer model, while the photoelectrochemical behavior and reactive-species trapping results support improved carrier dynamics and the participation of reactive oxygen species. Within this proposed model, Ag-containing interfaces are hypothesized to facilitate selective interfacial recombination, thereby preserving charge carriers with strong redox capability. However, the present measurements do not directly resolve the phase-specific locations or migration directions of photogenerated carriers. Because the trapping experiments were conducted in the presence of H2O2, which can act as an electron acceptor and contribute to additional reactive-oxygen-species-generating pathways, the individual origins of •OH and •O2− cannot be uniquely assigned. Under the tested UV-irradiation conditions, the optimized composite achieved 97.8% methylene blue degradation within 4 h in the presence of 1.75% H2O2, exhibited activity toward methyl orange, rhodamine B, and rhodamine 6G, and showed photoinduced antibacterial activity against Escherichia coli and Staphylococcus aureus at 5 mg mL− 1. The photocatalyst maintained a degradation efficiency of 83.98% after five consecutive cycles. These results demonstrate the potential of UiO-66-derived multiphase heterostructures as multifunctional UV-driven photocatalysts for dye degradation and photo-disinfection.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s11671-026-04886-y.

Introduction

Due to their exceptional chemical stability, high water solubility, and persistence in aquatic environments, xenobiotic compounds, particularly synthetic dyes, pose a serious environmental threat when discharged without proper treatment. Effluents from textile, pharmaceutical, and paper manufacturing industries significantly contribute to this pollution burden. Among the various dye classes, methylene blue (MB) is widely used as a model cationic dye and is well known for its resistance to biodegradation, as well as its cytotoxic, mutagenic, and oxidative stress–inducing effects on living organisms [1, 2]. The environmental recalcitrance of MB is primarily attributed to its extended conjugated aromatic framework and strong molecular affinity toward organic substrates, which invulnerably shield it from degradation through conventional physicochemical and biological treatment pathways [3–7]. Consequently, the development of advanced oxidation and catalytic strategies that promote complete mineralization to innocuous byproducts remains critically important for mitigating ecological contamination.

Photocatalysis based on semiconductor materials has emerged as an environmentally benign and energy-efficient strategy for wastewater treatment, enabling redox transformation of organic contaminants through photon-induced generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), including hydroxyl radicals (•OH), superoxide anions (•O2−), and singlet oxygen [8–10]. Titanium dioxide (TiO2), regarded as a benchmark photocatalyst, exhibits superior chemical inertness, non-toxicity, and photostability; however, its application is intrinsically constrained by a wide band gap (~ 3.2 eV), limited response to visible light, and ultrafast charge carrier recombination kinetics [11, 12]. Considerable efforts have therefore been devoted to the rational engineering of TiO2-based materials through heterojunction fabrication, electronic band modulation, and noble-metal modification to promote photon absorption efficiency and suppress recombination losses [13–15]. Nonetheless, architecting interfacial structures that simultaneously accelerate charge separation and preserve redox thermodynamics remains a central challenge in photocatalyst design.

Metal organic frameworks (MOFs) represent an emerging class of crystalline porous materials characterized by highly ordered architectures, tunable chemical environments, and exceptional surface-to-volume ratios. Among them, UiO-66 possesses a robust zirconium oxygen cluster network that grants superior structural and hydrothermal stability in chemically aggressive environments [16, 17]. Pyrolytic conversion of UiO-66 produces ZrO2 nanophases that can reinforce the oxide framework and may influence interfacial electronic interactions when integrated with TiO2 matrices [3]. In addition, Metallic Ag nanoparticles may introduce localized surface plasmon resonance and metal–semiconductor interfacial effects, whereas AgCl-containing domains have been associated with antimicrobial activity in related systems. The individual contributions of these effects in the present composite were not independently quantified [18, 19].

Despite the notable merits of MOF-derived oxides, and plasmonic enhancement, their synergistic integration into a unified functional material has received limited attention. Although UiO-66-templated ZrO2–TiO2 heterostructures modified with Ag/AgCl have received increasing attention, their interfacial charge-transfer behavior and the relationship between interfacial structure and photocatalytic function remain insufficiently understood. In multicomponent photocatalytic systems, the carrier-transfer pathway critically determines whether the redox capability of the photogenerated charges can be retained. In conventional type-II heterojunctions, charge separation is often achieved at the expense of redox power because electrons and holes accumulate in bands with comparatively weaker reduction and oxidation potentials, respectively. In contrast, direct Z-scheme and S-scheme models have been proposed to preserve strongly reducing electrons and strongly oxidizing holes through selective interfacial recombination. Nevertheless, unequivocal assignment of a specific charge-transfer pathway requires phase-sensitive, illumination-dependent evidence. The possible role of metallic Ag-containing interfaces in modulating carrier dynamics warrants systematic examination in the ZrO2@TiO2/Ag(AgCl) system. Ag-containing interfacial regions may provide low-resistance pathways for charge-carrier transport; therefore, an Ag-mediated interfacial charge-transfer model is proposed and evaluated against the available structural, thermodynamic, photoelectrochemical, and reactive-species evidence. Within this model, electrons in TiO2/AgCl-related conduction-band states may undergo selective interfacial recombination with holes in ZrO2-related valence-band states through Ag-containing interfacial regions. Such a process could preserve charge carriers with strong redox capability, including electrons in ZrO2/TiO2-related conduction-band states and holes in TiO2/AgCl-related valence-band states. Based on the measured band-edge potentials, these states are thermodynamically capable of participating in O2 reduction to •O2− and surface oxidation reactions leading to •OH formation, respectively. In the presence of H2O2, additional electron-accepting and •OH-generating pathways may also contribute to the observed reactive-species profile and photocatalytic performance. Accordingly, this model is presented as a mechanistic interpretation consistent with the available data, rather than as direct proof of phase-specific carrier migration. Although many previous studies of related oxide- and AgCl-containing photocatalysts have focused primarily on dye degradation, the concurrent evaluation of photocatalytic degradation and photo-disinfection within a single UiO-66-derived multiphase material platform remains comparatively limited. The present work therefore examines the potential of this multifunctional heterostructure for UV-driven removal of organic dyes and bacterial inactivation, while recognizing that further phase-sensitive measurements are required to definitively establish the interfacial charge-transfer pathway.

In this study, we report the synthesis of a UiO-66-templated ZrO2@TiO2/Ag(AgCl) composite via a controlled solvothermal–calcination procedure and investigate its structural, optoelectronic, photocatalytic, and antibacterial properties. Advanced physicochemical characterization techniques, including X-ray diffraction, electron microscopy, UV–visible spectroscopy, and Fourier-transform infrared analysis, were employed to validate phase transformation and morphological evolution. Charge transfer dynamics were evaluated using photoluminescence spectroscopy and electrochemical impedance spectroscopy, while reactive species trapping experiments elucidated dominant oxidation pathways. The designed heterostructure demonstrated exceptional dye degradation kinetics and efficient bactericidal performance against both Gram-positive and Gram-negative strains under light irradiation. These findings provide useful insights into the fabrication of MOF-templated multiphase heterostructures and demonstrate a potentially scalable route toward multifunctional UV-driven photocatalysts for concurrent dye degradation and microbial inactivation. The proposed Ag-mediated interfacial charge-transfer model offers a thermodynamically consistent framework for interpreting the enhanced photocatalytic performance.

Materials and methods

Chemical reagents and materials

All chemicals were of analytical grade and used as received without further purification. Zirconium (IV) chloride (ZrCl4, 98%, Thermo Fisher Scientific), 2-aminoterephthalic acid (99%, Sigma-Aldrich), glacial acetic acid (CH3COOH, ≥ 99.8% Honeywell), tetrabutyl titanate (99%, ACROS Organics), silver nitrate (AgNO3, 99.5%, SHOWA), sodium chloride (NaCl, ≥ 99.5%, SHOWA), methanol (CH3OH, 99.9% MACRON), and N, N-dimethylformamide (DMF) were used as received. Agar A (70% agarose and 30% agaropectin; Bio Basic) and Luria–Bertani (LB) broth (Sigma-Aldrich) were used for antibacterial assays. Deionized (DI) water was used throughout all experiments.

Measurement techniques

Phase structure and crystallinity were analyzed by X-ray powder diffraction (XRD; LabX XRD-600, Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan). Morphological and microstructural characteristics were examined using transmission electron microscopy (TEM; JEM-2010, JEOL Ltd., Japan) and scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM; Hitachi S-4300, Tokyo, Japan). Elemental composition was determined by energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX) with a QUANTAX Annular XFlash® QUAD FQ5060 detector (Bruker Nano, Berlin, Germany). Optical properties were characterized by UV–Vis diffuse reflectance spectroscopy (UV-Vis DRS; Evolution 200, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR; Agilent Cary 600 series, Agilent Technologies, USA), and photoluminescence spectroscopy (PL; Cary Eclipse, Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). For sample processing and photocatalytic experiments, a refrigerated centrifuge (Hermle Z36HK), a hybrid multi-mode microplate reader (Synergy H1, BioTek Instruments, Winooski, VT, USA), and a muffle furnace (JH-1, Taiwan) were employed. DI water was prepared using a Millipore system (Millipore, MA, USA). A Delta DC80H sonicator and a Suntex SP-2100 pH meter were employed for sample preparation. The specific surface area and pore structure of the samples were determined by N2 adsorption-desorption measurements using a Micromeritics analyzer (TriStar II 3020, Malvern Panalytical, Taiwan) at − 196 °C. The specific surface area was calculated using the Brunauer-Emmett-Teller (BET) method.

Synthesis procedure of novel UiO-66-derived ZrO2@TiO2/Ag(AgCl) heterojunctions

Synthesis of UiO-66-NH2

UiO-66-NH2 was synthesized via a solvothermal approach. The material was prepared by dissolving 0.115 g of ZrCl4 and 0.09 g of 2-aminoterephthalic acid in 50 mL of DMF under continuous stirring for 30 min. The synthesis process was described as following, in which 10 mL of acetic acid was added as a modulator, and the mixture was stirred for an additional 30 min to ensure homogeneity. The resulting solution was transferred into a Teflon-lined autoclave and heated at 120 °C for 16 h under solvothermal conditions. After cooling to room temperature, the precipitate was separated by centrifugation, thoroughly washed with DMF and methanol to remove residual reactants and solvents, and finally dried for further use [18].

Synthesis of ZrO2@TiO2 heterostructure

ZrO2@TiO2 was synthesized from UiO-66-NH2. With constant agitation, 0.05 g of UiO-66-NH2 powder was completely dispersed in ethanol and a small amount of water. Subsequently, introduce 500 µL of tetrabutyl orthotitanate gradually to the dispersion and stir for a duration of 4 h. The precipitate was gathered, rinsed thrice with ethanol, and dried overnight at 60 °C. A muffle furnace at 600 °C was employed for 2 h to anneal the dry sample, resulting in the formation of the ZrO2@TiO2 heterostructure.

Synthesis of ZrO2@TiO2/Ag composite

The ZrO2@TiO2/Ag composite was synthesized via a solvothermal method. Typically, 0.01 g of ZrO2@TiO2 and 0.02 g of AgNO3 were dispersed in 40 mL of DMF in a 50 mL Teflon-lined stainless-steel autoclave. The mixture was sonicated for 30 min to ensure homogeneous dispersion and then heated at 160 °C for 3 h. After natural cooling to room temperature, the product was collected and washed three times with methanol and DMF, yielding the ZrO2@TiO2/Ag composite.

Synthesis of ZrO2@TiO2/Ag(AgCl) composite

The ZrO2@TiO2/Ag(AgCl) composite was also synthesized via a solvothermal method. In a typical preparation, 0.01 g of ZrO2@TiO2 and 0.02 g of AgNO3 were dispersed in 40 mL of DMF and sonicated for 30 min. Subsequently, 0.054 g of NaCl was added, and the suspension was sonicated for another 30 min to ensure complete dispersion. The mixture was then transferred into a Teflon-lined autoclave and heated at 160 °C for 3 h. After natural cooling to room temperature, the resulting product was washed three times with methanol and DMF to remove residual reactants, yielding the ZrO2@TiO2/Ag(AgCl) composite.

Photocatalytic activity and reactive species analysis

MB was chosen as a model pollutant to evaluate the photocatalytic activity of the as-prepared samples. In a typical experiment, 20 mg of catalyst was dispersed in 50 mL of an aqueous solution containing 10 ppm MB in a quartz (fused-silica) reactor. Photocatalytic experiments were conducted under irradiation from a 200 W Newport/Oriel 6291 standard mercury–xenon arc lamp with a substantial UV component. No external cutoff filter or other optical filter was used. The lamp-to-reactor distance was 5.0 cm, and the incident irradiance at the reactor surface was about 130–170 mW cm− 2. The standard 6291 Hg(Xe) lamp provides broadband UV–visible–near-infrared emission that includes a short-wavelength UV component, while the fused-silica reactor permits transmission of deep-UV radiation. At designated irradiation intervals, 3 mL aliquots were withdrawn and centrifuged to remove the photocatalyst. The residual MB concentration was quantified colourimetrically at 664 nm using a UV–Vis microplate reader. Similar procedures were applied for other dye pollutants, including methyl orange (MO), rhodamine B (RhB), and rhodamine 6G (Rh6G). To investigate the role of reactive species, scavenger experiments were conducted by introducing 1.0 mM of specific quenchers into the reaction system: 1,4-benzoquinone (BQ) as a •O2− scavenger, isopropanol (IPA) as a •OH scavenger, and ethylenedinitrilotetraacetic acid (EDTA) as a hole (h+) scavenger. The photocatalytic experiments with scavengers were performed under identical conditions to the aforementioned dye degradation tests.

Photoelectrochemical measurements and photoluminescence spectroscopy

To perform photoelectrochemical (PEC) studies a potentiostat with a standard three-electrode setup was used for electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS), transient photocurrent responses and Mott-Schottky analysis. For this purpose, the catalyst-coated indium tin oxide (ITO) glass was employed as the working electrode, a platinum (Pt) wire served as the counter electrode, and a Ag/AgCl electrode was used as the reference electrode. The supporting electrolyte used was an aqueous solution of 0.5 M Na2SO4. For the working electrode preparation, 0.1 g of catalyst powder was dispersed in 1.0 mL of ethanol to form a homogeneous slurry and drop-casted on the conductive surface of the ITO substrate (active area: ~1.0 cm2). The coated substrate was dried on a hotplate at 50 °C for 30 min to eliminate residual ethanol and then annealed at 200 °C for 2 hours to improve mechanical adherence and to ensure good electrical contact. The charge separation efficiency and transfer kinetics of the photogenerated electron–hole pairs were evaluated by recording transient photocurrent responses at a constant bias potential of 0 V under intermittent UV light irradiation (on/off cycles). The type of the semiconductors and an estimation of the flat-band potential (Efb) of the pristine ZrO2, TiO2, and AgCl films were obtained by Mott-Schottky measurements at set AC frequencies (500, 1000, and 1500 Hz) in the dark. The potentials obtained against the reference electrode were translated to the normal hydrogen electrode (NHE) scale, by utilizing the usual Nernst equation for the band alignment analysis. Moreover, the recombination rate of the electron-hole pairs was studied using steady state photoluminescence (PL) spectroscopy. The PL emission spectra of the powder samples were recorded using a fluorescence spectrometer at room temperature with an excitation wavelength of 365 nm.

Antibacterial activity

Preparation of liquid medium

Continuous stirring was used to homogenize 7.75 g of lactose broth in 500 mL of deionized water. The solution underwent autoclaving at 121 °C for 30 min and was subsequently cooled to room temperature with gentle stirring. A solitary bacterial colony cultivated on solid agar medium was introduced into sterile medium within test tubes. Fresh bacterial suspensions for antibacterial experiments were prepared by gently agitating the inoculation media to ensure uniform cell distribution, followed by incubation at 37 °C for 24 h.

Disc diffusion method

Aseptically inoculated 100 µL of freshly generated bacterial suspension onto sterile agar plates and evenly distributed it using a sterilized triangular glass spreader. Following the placement of sterile paper discs on the agar surface using forceps, 20 µL of catalyst suspension at different concentrations was applied to each disc. Plates were incubated at 37 °C for 24 h, and the diameters of inhibition zones were quantified to assess antibacterial activity.

Optical density (OD600) measurement

To inhibit liquid culture growth, 300 µL of freshly cultured bacterial suspension was incubated at 37 °C for 24 h in 3 mL of sterile growth medium. Following incubation, 100 µL of bacterial culture was combined with catalyst suspensions at concentrations of 0, 5, 10, 15, and 20 mg mL− 1, and subsequently incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. OD600 was evaluated using UV–Vis spectrophotometers following treatment. A calibration curve was established to correlate OD600 with viable bacterial cell counts, enabling the conversion of absorbance readings into CFU.

Culture method

Bacterial cultures were calibrated to an optical density of 600 nm at approximately 1 × 10⁹ CFU mL− 1. A three-step serial dilution was performed by transferring 100 µL of culture into 9.9 mL of sterile LB broth on three occasions to achieve an appropriate concentration. Following the addition of 5 mg of ZrO2@TiO2/Ag(AgCl) to the diluted sample, incubation in the dark was conducted for 30 min to achieve adsorption equilibrium. Following dark incubation, a 10 µL aliquot was transferred to agar plates as the initial (0 h) sample. Following UV irradiation, 10 µL aliquots were collected hourly (1–3 h) and cultured on fresh agar plates. The photocatalytic antibacterial activity of the nanocomposite was assessed by enumerating bacterial colonies after 24 h at 37 °C.

Results and discussion

Structural evolution and formation of the multicomponent heterojunction

The crystallographic phase evolution of the synthesized materials was analyzed via XRD, as shown in Fig. 1. The observed characteristic reflections at 2θ values of 7.2°, 8.5°, 11.9°, 17.0°, 25.8°, 28.0°, 30.8°, 43.0°, 50.1°, and 56.2° correspond to the (111), (200), (220), (400), (442), (533), (551), (771), (971), and (991) crystallographic planes, respectively, thereby confirming the synthesis of the UiO-66-NH2 framework without impurity phases [20]. All the diffraction peaks can be well matched with the previous reports, exhibiting the excellent crystallinity [21]. The UiO-66-NH2@Ti precursor displays a broadened pattern resembling titanium species, indicative of an amorphous or low-crystallinity intermediate state before thermal treatment. Upon calcination, a distinct phase transformation occurs, evidenced by the emergence of diffraction reflections corresponding to the (101) plane of tetragonal crystal structure ZrO2 (JCPDS-89-7710) [22] and the (101), (004), (200), (105), (211), and (204) planes of anatase TiO2 (JCPDS-21-1272) [23]. In the final ZrO2@TiO2/Ag(AgCl) composite, diffraction peaks at approximately 2θ = 27.8°, 32.2°, 46.2°, 54.8°, and 57.5° can be indexed to the AgCl (111), (200), (220), (311), and (222) plane reflections, respectively (JCPDS-31-1238) [24]. The peaks around the scattering angle of 2θ = 38.2°, 44.94°, 64.22°, and 78.25° belong to the (111), (200), (220), and (400) facets of Ag, respectively (JCPDS-65-2871) [25, 26]. These structural analyses validate the successful transition from the MOF template to a crystalline mixed-oxide matrix and confirm the in-situ integration of Ag and AgCl nanodomains, establishing a multiphase heterojunction essential for interfacial charge transfer. The coexistence of these phases provides a structural basis for interfacial charge transfer. The possible carrier-transfer pathway is discussed below in conjunction with the experimentally determined band-edge potentials and photoelectrochemical results.

The morphological evolution and interfacial assembly of the heterostructure composites were investigated by SEM coupled with elemental mapping and EDX analysis (Fig. S1). The pristine ZrO2@TiO2 architecture (Fig. S1A) exhibits a highly aggregated morphology with irregular particulates and pronounced surface roughness, reflecting a large interfacial area beneficial for subsequent surface modification. Elemental analysis verifies the predominance of Ti and O with trace Zr incorporation, confirming the successful integration of ZrO2 into the TiO2 framework. Upon Ag loading (Fig. S1B), a distinct transformation toward a more dispersed and homogeneous morphology is observed, suggesting that Ag nucleation effectively regulates surface energy and mitigates particle coalescence; this is further supported by the emergence of a clear Ag signal in the EDX spectra and spatial mapping. After thermal treatment at 160 °C (Fig. S1C), the composite undergoes pronounced surface reconstruction, yielding a compact and mechanically robust microstructure. Notably, the simultaneous presence of Ag and Cl in the elemental profiles indicates the in-situ formation of AgCl species within the oxide matrix. This consolidated heterointerface is expected to facilitate rapid interfacial charge transfer, suppress recombination losses, and ultimately enhance charge-carrier separation efficiency.

TEM and selected area electron diffraction (SAED) analyses (Fig. 2) were employed to interrogate the structural evolution and phase identity of the synthesized heterostructures. The (ZrO2@TiO2) core–shell nanostructure (Fig. 2A) exhibits a conformal TiO2 overlayer encapsulating the ZrO2 core, indicative of coherent interfacial growth and high crystallinity, as further supported by well-defined concentric diffraction rings in the corresponding SAED pattern. Incorporation of metallic silver yields the (ZrO2@TiO2/Ag) composite (Fig. 2B), where discrete Ag nanodomains are uniformly anchored to the oxide surface, and the emergence of additional diffraction features in the SAED pattern confirms the crystalline nature of Ag without perturbing the oxide framework. Subsequent integration of silver chloride produces the (ZrO2@TiO2/Ag(AgCl)) composite (Fig. 2C), which displays a denser morphology with heterogeneously distributed AgCl crystallites and limited aggregation [27]. The enriched SAED pattern confirms the coexistence of ZrO2, TiO2, Ag, and AgCl phases. This hierarchically integrated architecture establishes continuous heterointerface junctions that provide intimate multiphase interfaces that may facilitate interfacial charge transport, and extend light harvesting into the visible range, thereby suppressing recombination and enhancing optoelectronic and photocatalytic performance [28].

To better understand the nanoscale interface structure and confirm the production of the multiphase heterojunction, HRTEM was performed on the optimized ZrO2@TiO2/Ag(AgCl) composite. The HRTEM pictures clearly demonstrate intimate crystallographic interaction between the constituent phases as seen in Fig. S2. A careful inspection of the lattice fringes indicates the coexistence of several different crystalline domains in the same field of view. The most intense lattice fringes (interplanar distance of 0.35 nm) are attributed to the (101) crystallographic plane of anatase TiO2 (Fig. S2A) [29]. These regions are clearly next to crystalline domains with d-spacings of 0.32 nm and 0.28 nm, corresponding to the (-111) and (111) planes of monoclinic ZrO2, respectively [30]. Furthermore, highly ordered lattice fringes with interplanar spacings of 0.28 nm and 0.32 nm can be observed, which may be well indexed to the (200) and (111) planes of face-centered cubic AgCl phase, respectively [31, 32]. Crucially, the HRTEM images show that these different phases are not simply blended physically but are in direct interfacial contact. Figure S2B shows a crisp, well-defined boundary between the AgCl (200) and TiO2 (101) domains, which is a binary interface with a sudden change in lattice fringe orientation and spacing. More interestingly, a complicated triple-phase junction between ZrO2, TiO2 and AgCl domains is observed at a single interfacial location (Fig. S2C). These atomic-scale observations support the formation of an interfacially coupled multiphase heterostructure. The intimate interfacial contact provides the structural prerequisite for interfacial carrier exchange and is consistent with the proposed Ag-mediated charge-transfer model; however, it does not by itself establish the direction of carrier migration under illumination.

The chemical bonding environment and lattice vibrational modes of the composites were probed via FT-IR spectroscopy, as shown in Fig. 3A. The spectra exhibit characteristic phonon modes at 436 cm− 1 and 836 cm− 1, which are assigned to the deformation vibrations of Zr–O and the bending vibrations of Ti–O bonds, respectively. These spectral features confirm the preservation of the binary oxide lattice integrity within the heterostructure. To evaluate the modulation of the optoelectronic band structure, UV–Vis DRS was employed (Fig. 3B). The ZrO2@TiO2/Ag(AgCl) composite has a prominent absorption edge at 292 nm, which means an optical band gap (Eg) of 4.24 eV was obtained by Tauc plot analysis (Direct absorption, R2 = 0.9348). The band gap shows that the composite is mostly responsive to UV light. The visible-light absorption tail at approximately 465 nm is attributed to defect-related electronic states and Ag-associated localized surface plasmon resonance, rather than to the fundamental optical bandgap. The apparent optical bandgap of 4.24 eV, determined from the direct Tauc plot, is consistent with the predominantly UV-driven photoresponse of the composite. When considered together with the experimentally determined band-edge potentials, this result provides a thermodynamic basis for the proposed Ag-mediated interfacial charge-transfer model, in which strongly reducing electrons may be retained on ZrO2-related conduction-band states for O2 reduction. However, the optical bandgap measurement alone does not establish the direction or phase-specific location of photogenerated charge carriers.

Photocatalytic performance

The photocatalytic activity of the synthesized catalysts was evaluated using MB as a model pollutant under UV irradiation (Fig. 4A). In the absence of a photocatalyst, MB remained largely un-degraded after 10 h of UV-light irradiation, indicating negligible self-photodecomposition. The ZrO2@TiO2 composites achieved only ~ 10.2% degradation within 10 h, reflecting its limited radical generation capability. Upon Ag modification, the ZrO2@TiO2/Ag composites exhibited markedly improved activity, achieving ~ 46.4% degradation under the same conditions. Further enhancement was obtained by introducing H2O2 as an oxidant, which significantly promoted the generation of reactive oxygen species. In the combined ZrO2@TiO2/Ag with H2O2 system, MB degradation reached 95.3% within 10 h, demonstrating strong synergistic effects between the photocatalyst and H2O2. This enhancement is attributed to multiple synergistic effects of H2O2: (a) serving as an efficient electron acceptor (E° = +0.87 V vs. NHE) to promote charge separation, (b) generating additional •OH radicals through direct reduction (H2O2 + e− → •OH + OH−), and (c) participating in Haber-Weiss chemistry with photogenerated •O₂⁻ to amplify •OH generation. By contrast, H2O2 alone led to only ~ 12.0% degradation, about threefold higher than the blank condition but far less effective than the catalyst-assisted system. These results clearly highlight the importance of Ag incorporation for UV-light activation and the critical role of H2O2 in accelerating ROS-mediated photocatalysis.

Photocatalytic degradation performance of MB under various conditions: (A) Comparison of degradation efficiencies with and without the ZrO2@TiO2/Ag composite in the presence and absence of H2O2 (2.5 mL, 35%); (B) Effect of different H2O2 concentrations on the degradation efficiency

The concentration of H2O2 had a marked influence on MB degradation efficiency (Fig. 4B). At 35% H2O2, the addition of 2.5 mL yielded the optimum performance (final concentration of H2O2 is 1.75%), achieving 95.3% degradation within 10 h of UV-light irradiation. This enhancement is attributed to the efficient generation of •OH radicals, which act as the primary oxidizing species in the dye decomposition process. However, increasing the H2O2 volume to 5 mL led to a decline in activity, likely because excess H2O2 acted as a scavenger, consuming •OH radicals and reducing their effective concentration [33]. Under optimized conditions (1.75% H2O2), further improvement was observed with AgCl modification, achieving 97.8% degradation within 4 h (Fig. 5). The improved activity following AgCl incorporation is consistent with the lower PL intensity, reduced interfacial charge-transfer resistance, and enhanced photocurrent response observed for the final composite, suggesting more favorable overall carrier dynamics, which accelerates the photocatalytic degradation of MB. MB exhibits a strong absorption maximum at 664 nm. Tracking the spectral evolution during photocatalysis provides critical insight into whether degradation involves only decolorization or progresses toward complete mineralization. Under UV-light irradiation in the presence of H2O2, the photocatalyst generates electron–hole pairs that promote the formation of reactive radicals, thereby initiating dye decomposition. As shown in Fig. 5A, a gradual decrease in absorbance at 664 nm, accompanied by a slight blue shift after 3 h, indicates the breakdown of chromophoric and auxochromic groups. The emergence of weak additional absorption bands suggests the formation of intermediate degradation products. The progressive spectral changes indicate substantial degradation of the MB chromophore and formation of intermediate products; however, complete mineralization to CO2 and H2O was not independently quantified in the present study. To further confirm that the reported photocatalytic activity was not artificially boosted by the physical adsorption, a dark adsorption experiment was carried out. The ZrO2@TiO2/Ag(AgCl) catalyst was added to the MB solution and swirled in the dark for 30 min under the same circumstances as the photocatalytic degradation experiment. The data indicate that the value of C/C0 remained almost constant at ~ 1.00 during the 30 min dark phase with a maximum adsorption of just 0.27% after 30 min. The insignificant adsorption indicates that the 97.8% photocatalytic degradation efficiency in 4 h, which is better than ZrO2@TiO2/Ag composites, was mostly attributable to photocatalytic degradation rather than physical adsorption as shown in Fig. 5B. The superior activity is attributed to the in-situ formation of AgCl upon NaCl addition, which enhances charge separation and suppresses electron–hole recombination. Moreover, the stability of Ag under light, without conversion to Ag2O, preserves its function as an efficient electron mediator, thereby further improving photocatalytic efficiency [34].

Also, ZrO2@TiO2/Ag(AgCl) composites have a comparatively high specific surface area (41.36 m2 g− 1) and total pore volume (0.1415 cm3 g− 1), which is in line with their excellent photocatalytic performance. The porous architecture is important to boost the catalytic effectiveness because it allows the reactant molecules (e.g. MB) and dissolved oxygen to diffuse rapidly to the active sites, thus promoting efficient mass transport. At the same time, the increased surface area is in favor of the creation of a large number of available active sites, which is beneficial to interfacial redox processes. Moreover, the porous architecture promotes the use of light, providing numerous scattering effects and hence improving the photon capture inside the catalytic matrix. Such structural benefits are synergistically responsible for the enhanced photocatalytic activity of the composite material, in keeping with earlier publications highlighting the importance of surface area and porosity in ZrO2-TiO2-based photocatalysts.

Photodegradation experiments with different dye molecules are essential for evaluating the versatility and practical applicability of photocatalysts. Beyond MB, the photocatalytic performance of the synthesized ZrO2@TiO2/Ag(AgCl) composite was further examined against MO, RhB, and Rh6G. As shown in Fig. S3, the degradation efficiencies of these dyes under UV irradiation for 10 h in the presence of H2O2 were compared at their respective maximum absorption wavelengths. ZrO2@TiO2/Ag(AgCl) composites demonstrated consistently superior photocatalytic activity toward all tested dyes, confirming their broad-spectrum degradation capability. These findings highlight that the synthesized composite is not only effective for MB removal but also exhibits strong potential for degrading structurally diverse organic pollutants, underscoring its applicability in wastewater treatment and environmental remediation.

The as-synthesized photocatalysts were applied in cyclic degradation studies under optimized circumstances (1.75% H2O2, UV irradiation) to evaluate their practical utility. The ZrO2@TiO2/Ag(AgCl) composite showed better stability in the recycling tests (5 cycles, Fig. 6A-C), and the degradation efficiency was still high at 83.98% (85.87% retention rate) after 5 successive cycles. The causes for the modest decrease of ca. 3.0% every cycle are two-fold: (a) the unavoidable mass loss during the recovery and washing steps and (b) the possible build-up of refractory intermediate byproducts on the catalyst. On the contrary, the ZrO2@TiO2/Ag and the pure ZrO2@TiO2 samples exhibited a quick deactivation process with retention rates of 77.38% and 65.91%, respectively.

The stability of the AgCl component is a prime question. The persistence of AgCl diffraction peaks after five cycles (Fig. 6D) indicates that the AgCl phase was retained under the tested conditions; it does not quantify AgCl conversion or Ag release. The heterostructure is particularly advantageous for the photocatalytic system for the following reasons: (a) Enhanced interfacial charge-carrier transport: The Ag-containing interfacial regions may provide electronically conductive pathways between the semiconductor domains. In the proposed Ag-mediated interfacial charge-transfer model, metallic Ag may serve as a conductive interfacial region that could facilitate carrier exchange between adjacent semiconductor domains. This process is expected to promote the net spatial separation of the remaining high-redox-potential carriers. The reduced charge-transfer resistance and enhanced photocurrent response of the composite are consistent with improved interfacial carrier transport; however, direct phase-resolved carrier migration was not determined in the present study; (b) Potential optical and stability contributions of Ag/AgCl: At low loading, metallic Ag nanoparticles may modify the local optical field and photon-scattering behavior, thereby potentially contributing to improved light utilization. The visible-light absorption tail is more appropriately attributed to Ag-associated localized surface plasmon resonance and defect-related states; however, the specific contribution of Ag to UV-light absorption was not independently quantified in the present study. Under the tested UV-irradiation conditions, the AgCl-modified composite exhibited a higher MB degradation efficiency than the Ag-containing sample (97.8% versus 95.3%) and retained 83.98% of its initial degradation efficiency after five consecutive cycles. These results indicate that AgCl incorporation is associated with enhanced initial photocatalytic activity and improved cycling performance. Nevertheless, the present data do not directly establish the mechanism by which AgCl affects Ag photochemical stability or phase-specific charge transfer. The recycling results and post-reaction XRD pattern support reasonable structural and functional stability under the tested conditions, while longer-term stability and mechanistic verification require further investigation.

Reactive-species analysis and proposed charge-transfer mechanism

In photocatalysis, the photogenerated holes (h+) combine with water molecules or surface hydroxyl groups to produce •OH radicals, which are extremely reactive and mainly responsible for oxidative destruction of organic pollutants. The excited electrons (e−) can also simultaneously decrease the dissolved oxygen to form the •O2− radicals, which can also accelerate degradation, although with a much lesser efficacy than •OH. Scavenger experiments were conducted to study the role of several reactive species (Fig. 7). Radical-scavenging experiments were conducted under identical photocatalytic conditions in the presence of H2O2 to evaluate the relative contributions of individual ROS to MB degradation. Because H2O2 can itself participate in ROS generation, these experiments do not unambiguously distinguish ROS arising from the proposed Ag-mediated interfacial charge-transfer pathway from those generated through H2O2-mediated reactions. The most significant decrease in degradation efficiency (> 90%) was observed with the addition of IPA (•OH scavenger), demonstrating that •OH radicals constitute the major oxidative species. The addition of BQ (•O2− scavenger) caused a moderate decrease (~ 15%), showing that •O2− radicals contribute to the total photocatalytic activity. The detection of •O2− indicates that a fraction of the photogenerated electrons retains sufficient reducing power to activate dissolved O2. Based on the measured band-edge potentials, the conduction bands of ZrO2 (− 1.10 V vs. NHE) and TiO2 (− 0.60 V vs. NHE) are thermodynamically capable of reducing O2 to •O2−, whereas the AgCl conduction band (− 0.06 V vs. NHE) is not. This result is therefore incompatible with a model in which all photogenerated electrons accumulate at the AgCl conduction band and is consistent with the proposed Ag-mediated interfacial charge-transfer model. However, because the scavenger experiments were conducted in the presence of H2O2, the phase-specific origin of the electrons and the individual sources of ROS cannot be unambiguously assigned from these data alone. Addition of EDTA, a direct hole scavenger, exhibited little suppression of degradation, often < 1%, an unexpected result providing significant mechanistic insight. If holes were directly oxidizing dye molecules, then EDTA should have created a considerable inhibition. However, the weak response to EDTA suggests that direct hole-mediated oxidation of MB is not the dominant degradation pathway under the tested conditions. However, because H2O2 also contributes to ROS formation, the precise origin of •OH and the phase-specific location of photogenerated holes cannot be determined from the scavenger results alone. The scavenger results and the detection of •O2− demonstrate the participation of ROS in the UV-driven degradation of MB and are consistent with the proposed Ag-mediated interfacial charge-transfer model. Within this model, selective recombination of lower-energy carriers is hypothesized to preserve strongly reducing electrons on ZrO2/TiO2-related conduction-band states and strongly oxidizing holes on TiO2/AgCl valence-band states. These retained carriers are thermodynamically capable of reducing O2 to •O2− and oxidizing surface H2O/OH− to •OH, respectively. In addition, H2O2 may act as an electron acceptor and as a precursor for additional •OH-generating pathways. Accordingly, the enhanced photocatalytic degradation of MB is attributed to the combined contributions of •O2− and •OH under UV irradiation. However, because ROS detection in an H2O2-containing system does not identify their individual origins or the phase-specific locations of the charge carriers, this interpretation is presented as a mechanistic model consistent with the present data rather than as direct proof of a unique charge-transfer pathway.

A crucial factor that limits the photocatalytic efficiency is the recombination of photogenerated electron-hole pairs. To examine the charge-separation efficiency of the synthesized catalysts, PL spectra were monitored at an excitation wavelength of 365 nm (Fig. 8A). Generally, lower PL intensity indicates a lower recombination rate, hence more photocatalytic activity. The PL intensity order is ZrO2@TiO2 > ZrO2@TiO2/Ag > ZrO2@TiO2/Ag(AgCl), which means that Ag and AgCl may considerably boost the charge dissociation efficiency. To further investigate the charge transport behavior, EIS measurements were carried out (Fig. 8B). The reduced diameter of the semicircle in these Nyquist plots means lower impedance and more efficient charge movement between the catalyst surface and the electrodes. The resistance values were found to be 12.19 Ω for ZrO2@TiO2, 11.05 Ω for ZrO2@TiO2/Ag and 10.85 Ω for ZrO2@TiO2/Ag(AgCl) composites, respectively. The Ag(AgCl)-modified composite exhibits the smallest semicircle radius, indicating lower interfacial charge-transfer resistance and improved overall carrier-transport kinetics. The creation of high-performance photocatalysts, such as ZrO2@TiO2/Ag(AgCl), is a considerable step forward in photoelectrochemical efficiency (Fig. 8C). This composite system has an impressive photocurrent density of 1.56 mA cm− 2, which is1.93 times higher than the baseline ZrO2@TiO2 value of 0.81 mA cm− 2.

A proposed charge-transfer interpretation was developed by considering the structural, photoelectrochemical, band-alignment, and reactive-species results, as summarized in Scheme 1 [35–38]. Transient photocurrent measurements provide evidence that the ZrO2@TiO2/Ag(AgCl) composite exhibits enhanced photoinduced charge-carrier extraction and interfacial transport relative to the reference samples. Under chopped UV irradiation, the composite showed a higher photocurrent density and a rapid, reproducible photoresponse, which are consistent with improved overall charge-carrier dynamics. When considered together with the intimate multiphase contact observed by HRTEM, the reduced charge-transfer resistance derived from EIS, the quenched PL emission, and the measured band-edge potentials, these results are compatible with a proposed Ag-mediated interfacial charge-transfer model. In this proposed model, Ag-containing interfacial regions may act as conductive mediators that facilitate selective recombination between lower-energy electrons in TiO2/AgCl-related conduction-band states and holes in ZrO2-related valence-band states. Such a process would preserve strongly reducing electrons on the ZrO2 conduction band (− 1.10 V vs. NHE) and strongly oxidizing holes on TiO2/AgCl valence-band states (2.60 and 3.15 V vs. NHE, respectively). These retained carriers are thermodynamically capable of reducing O2 to •O2− and oxidizing surface H2O/OH− to •OH, respectively. In the presence of H2O2, additional electron-accepting and •OH-generating pathways may further contribute to the observed photocatalytic performance. Therefore, the enhanced degradation of organic contaminants is attributed to the combined action of •O2− and •OH under UV irradiation. It should be emphasized that transient photocurrent measurements do not directly resolve the phase-specific locations or migration directions of photogenerated carriers. Accordingly, the Ag-mediated charge-transfer route is presented as a mechanistic model consistent with the available structural, photoelectrochemical, and reactive-species evidence, rather than as direct proof of a unique Z-scheme pathway.

Light-activated antibacterial performance

The antibacterial efficacy of ZrO2@TiO2/Ag(AgCl) composites was first assessed using the disc diffusion method against E. coli (Gram-negative) (Fig. 9A) and S. aureus (Gram-positive) (Fig. 9B), as summarized in the antibacterial analysis. The negative control (DMSO) showed no inhibition zones, thereby confirming its lack of activity. The positive control, benzoic acid, produced inhibition zones of 1.20 cm for E. coli and 1.26 cm for S. aureus, thus confirming the validity of the assay. ZrO2@TiO2/Ag(AgCl) composites at a concentration of 5 mg mL− 1 demonstrated antibacterial activity, producing inhibition zones of 1.10 cm for E. coli and 1.15 cm for S. aureus. The inhibition zones exhibited a slight increase at 10 mg/mL (1.20 cm for E. coli, 1.17 cm for S. aureus) and reached a plateau at 15–20 mg/mL (1.20–1.25 cm), indicating a saturation effect. The Relative Standard Deviation (RSD) values were 3.79% for E. coli and 4.00% for S. aureus, demonstrating reproducibility. These results establish 5 mg/mL as the minimum effective antibacterial concentration for subsequent assays.

Conclusions

In this study, UiO-66-derived ZrO2@TiO2/Ag(AgCl) multiphase heterostructures were fabricated through an interface-engineering approach. Structural characterization verified the coexistence and intimate interfacial coupling of ZrO2, TiO2, Ag, and AgCl domains. Compared with the reference samples, the optimized composite exhibited a lower charge-transfer resistance (10.85 Ω), quenched photoluminescence emission, and an approximately 1.9-fold higher transient photocurrent response, supporting improved overall photoinduced carrier transport and extraction. Under the tested UV-irradiation conditions and in the presence of 1.75% H2O2, the optimized composite achieved 97.8% methylene blue degradation within 4 h, showed activity toward MO, RhB, and Rh6G, and exhibited photoinduced antibacterial activity against Escherichia coli and Staphylococcus aureus at 5 mg mL− 1. The material retained a degradation efficiency of 83.98% after five consecutive cycles. Furthermore, comparing with literature reports (Table 2), it is found that our composite demonstrates competitive performance especially in terms of dual functionality (photocatalytic + antibacterial activity) within a single material platform [39–47].

The measured component band-edge potentials provide a thermodynamic framework for a proposed Ag-mediated interfacial charge-transfer model, while the structural, photoelectrochemical, and reactive-species results establish intimate interfacial contact, improved carrier dynamics, and ROS participation, respectively. Within this model, Ag-containing interfaces are hypothesized to facilitate selective interfacial recombination, thereby preserving charge carriers with strong redox capability. The measured band-edge potentials indicate that the ZrO2 and TiO2 conduction bands are thermodynamically capable of reducing O2 to •O2−, whereas the TiO2 and AgCl valence-band potentials are sufficiently positive to thermodynamically permit surface oxidation reactions associated with •OH formation. However, because reactive-species trapping was performed in the presence of H2O2, which can act as an electron acceptor and contribute to additional ROS-generating pathways, the individual origins of •OH and •O2− cannot be uniquely assigned. Moreover, the present measurements do not directly resolve the phase-specific locations or migration directions of photogenerated carriers. The Ag-mediated route is therefore presented as a working model consistent with the available evidence rather than as direct proof of a unique Z-scheme pathway. Further illumination-dependent, phase-sensitive measurements, including in situ XPS, surface photovoltage/KPFM, selective photodeposition, time-resolved spectroscopy, and EPR analysis, are required to directly verify the proposed phase-specific carrier-transfer pathway. Overall, these results demonstrate the potential of UiO-66-derived multiphase heterostructures as multifunctional UV-driven photocatalysts for dye degradation and photo-disinfection.
