Unraveling diethyl phthalate-induced prostate carcinogenesis: core targets revealed by integrated network toxicology, machine learning, and structural validation
Authors: Hao Liu, Junyi Jiang, Ying Tan, Mengying Yang, Hongmei Yang, Canyong Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT

However, its molecular mechanisms beyond endocrine disruption remain poorly defined. Introduction

Diethyl phthalate (DEP), an ubiquitous synthetic phthalate ester, is extensively employed as a plasticizer in polymers, cosmetic solvents, and food packaging applications [1]. Due to non-covalent bonding with polymers, DEP readily migrates from consumer products, resulting in pervasive human exposure via multiple routes [2, 3]. The primary DEP metabolite, monomethyl phthalate (MEP), is classified by the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) as a Group 2B probable human carcinogen [4]. Experimental evidence indicates DEP and its analogues trigger reactive oxygen species (ROS) overproduction, causing DNA oxidative damage (including 8-OHdG formation) in vitro, while promoting malignant transformation via disruption of nuclear receptor signaling (notably aberrant ERβ activation) [5–7].

Phthalate esters are established endocrine disrupting chemicals (EDCs) capable of promoting prostate tumorigenesis by dysregulating critical pathways, particularly androgen receptor (AR) signaling [8, 9]. Recent advances in exposomics have highlighted the importance of integrating environmental exposure data with genomic networks to understand disease etiology [10]. In particular, the concept of genome–exposome interactions provides a powerful framework for deciphering how chemicals like DEP perturb biological systems and contribute to cancer development. Prostate development, physiological function, and oncogenesis are critically regulated by androgen receptor (AR) signaling [11–13]. Consequently, DEP’s endocrine-disrupting properties suggest mechanistic links to prostate cancer—an androgen-dependent malignancy—thereby establishing a research foundation. Our study leverages human transcriptomic data to bridge the gap between environmental exposure and genomic alterations, thereby offering a more holistic view of DEP-induced prostate carcinogenesis.

Although epidemiological data associate phthalate ester exposure with prostate carcinogenesis [14, 15], current evidence predominantly derives from macrotoxicological evaluations or reductionist molecular approaches, without systematic interrogation of multi-target mechanisms. Traditional experimental encounter prohibitive resource constraints, impeding efficient identification of pivotal oncogenic drivers. Herein, by integrating multi-platform bioinformatics, ensemble machine learning (ML), and structural validation, we propose an analytical pipeline that aligns with the emerging paradigm of ML-driven genome–exposome interaction networks.

Methods

Acquisition of disease-related targets

To ensure human relevance and translational potential, we curated seven human prostate cancer transcriptomic datasets (GSE46602, GSE55945, GSE69223, GSE71016, GSE32571, GSE62872, GSE79021) from GEO repository [16]. The detailed information of these datasets was presented in Table S1, including their platforms, sample types, and sizes. Training cohorts comprised GSE46602, GSE55945, GSE69223 and GSE71016, with remaining datasets designated validation cohorts. For microarray data, probes were mapped to gene symbols using the official platform annotation files. When multiple probes mapped to the same gene, the median expression value was taken to represent that gene. A multi-step preprocessing pipeline was implemented under a strict train–validation separation scheme to prevent data leakage. The complete data were first split into training and validation sets. For feature scaling, gene expression values were standardized to z-scores (mean = 0, standard deviation = 1). The mean and standard deviation for standardization were computed exclusively on the training set within each cross-validation fold and then applied to transform both the training and validation folds. Surrogate Variable Analysis (SVA) was applied to the training set to model latent technical artifacts. Based on the SVA outcome, the ComBat algorithm using a parametric empirical Bayes framework was used to correct for batch effects. Critically, the empirical Bayes parameters for ComBat correction were fitted solely on the training set and subsequently applied to the validation set. This entire preprocessing procedure, including normalization and batch correction, was embedded within the cross-validation loop, ensuring that all parameters were estimated exclusively from the training data. The effectiveness of normalization was visually assessed using density plots and boxplots. Principal component analysis performed after batch correction showed improved inter-batch clustering in the reduced-dimensional space, confirming successful data harmonization.

Acquisition of chemical components and targets of DEP

Diethyl phthalate (DEP) underwent integrative cheminformatics characterization, with physicochemical profiling and biological parametric data interrogated from PubMed, while canonical structural descriptors (SMILES: CCOC(= O)C1 = CC = CC = C1C(= O)OCC) were extracted from PubChem. Target prediction utilized a computational triad: ChEMBL (ligand-receptor interaction profiling), SwissTargetPrediction (chemogenomic inference; http://www.swisstargetprediction.ch), SEA (statistical ensemble algorithms; https://sea.bkslab.org). All putative targets were constrained to the Homo sapiens proteome.

Differential gene expression analysis

Transcriptomic profiling employed the limma package to identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs), defined by FDR-adjusted P < 0.05 and logFC >0.585 (1.5-fold change). Subsequent visualization utilized ggplot2. A scale-free co-expression network was constructed via Weighted Gene Co-expression Network Analysis (WGCNA) through: (1) Sample quality control implement- ing hierarchical clustering for outlier removal; (2) Soft-threshold optimization achieving scale-free topology (R2>0.9) through dynamic tree-cutting; (3) Module detection via topological overlap matrix (TOM) hierarchical clustering (minModuleSize = 60, merge-CutHeight = 0.25); (4) Module-trait association analysis correlating eigengenes with phenotypes (Pearson’s r>0.5, P<0.05); and 5 Hub gene selection by intramodular connectivity.

Identification of DEP-associated disease targets

Venn diagram analysis intersecting transcriptome-derived DEGs/WGCNA clusters with DEP-interacting targets revealed carcinogenic mediators. Subsequent functional enrichment analyses via clusterProfiler package delineated DEP-associated pathogenic mechanisms, encompassing Gene Ontology (biological processes, cellular components, molecular functions) and KEGG pathway annotations at a statistical threshold (P<0.05).

Machine learning-based core gene screening

A comprehensive machine learning framework was established to identify DEP-associated diagnostic biomarkers for PCa, integrating multiple algorithmic approaches. To ensure the reproducibility of the results, a fixed random seed (set.seed-123) was used throughout all machine learning model training and cross-validation procedures. Expression profiles from the training cohort facilitated development of 113 predictive models using classical algorithms—Lasso, SVM, RF, glmBoost, Stepglm, Ridge, Enet, GBM, LDA, XGBoost, and NaiveBayes. Hyperparameter tuning employed stratified 5-fold cross-validation with rigorous performance evaluation via ROC-AUC, accuracy, and F1 metrics. The performance of the final ensemble model was evaluated on the independent validation cohorts and reported with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) calculated using 2000 bootstrap replicates. The superiority of the ensemble over individual biomarkers was statistically assessed using DeLong’s test for comparing ROC curves. High-confidence models (AUC > 0.9) underwent stacked generaliza- tion, with feature genes prioritized by selection frequency. For the evaluation of diagnostic performance, the sensitivity and specificity for each core gene were calculated and reported at the optimal probability threshold that maximized Youden’s Index (J = Sensitivity + Specificity − 1). This approach ensures a biologically and clinically relevant balance between identifying true positives and minimizing false positives. For machine learning model input, any potential missing values would have been handled by mean imputation. The pheatmap package visualized expression patterns of candidate core genes. To address inherent model opacity, a SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP)-based quantitative interpretability framework was implemented, assigning feature-specific contribution values to delineate predictive determinants.

Molecular docking analysis

Molecular docking simulations were performed to investigate the direct binding interactions between Diethyl Phthalate (DEP) and the five core protein targets (TRPM8, CTSB, CA14, GSTM2, MYLK) using AutoDock Vina. The three-dimensional crystal structures of the targets were retrieved from the Protein Data Bank with the following accession codes: TRPM8 (9b6i), CTSB (8he9), CA14 (5cjf), GSTM2 (1hna), and MYLK (2cqv). Protein preparation involved the removal of water molecules and heteroatoms, addition of polar hydrogen atoms, and assignment of Gasteiger charges. The three-dimensional structure of DEP was obtained in SDF format and energy-minimized using the MMFF94s force field.

For each protein target, the docking grid box was defined systematically to encompass the predicted orthosteric (active) binding site. The center of the grid box was determined based on the centroid of the known native ligand or key residues lining the binding pocket, as identified from relevant literature or structural analysis. To validate and optimize this initial placement, we employed a comprehensive cavity detection algorithm. The final docking grid for each protein was parameterized based on the cavity with the most favorable (lowest) computed binding score, which consistently corresponded to the canonical orthosteric site. The definitive center coordinates and dimensions of the grid box for each target are fully detailed in Supplementary Table S2. The grid size was standardized to be sufficiently large to accommodate the DEP ligand and allow for conformational flexibility and translational freedom during the docking process. Molecular docking simulations were performed using AutoDock Vina. To ensure robust sampling and convergence, each DEP-target complex was subjected to n = 10 independent docking runs with an exhaustiveness parameter of 32. The binding pose with the most favorable (lowest) binding free energy from the most frequent cluster of conformations was selected as the representative binding mode for analysis. The binding affinity for each target was then calculated as the mean ± standard deviation across these 10 independent runs. This replication and statistical strategy is critical for verifying the reliability of the binding affinities and mitigates the risk of reporting singular, method-dependent artefacts.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses and modeling were performed within the R environment for statistical computing (version 4.5.0). The analysis leveraged numerous open-source packages; versions of the core packages used are detailed in Table S3.

Results

Identification of DEP target proteins

The molecular structure of DEP, retrieved from the PubChem database (Fig. 1A), served as the foundation for a cheminformatics-based target prediction workflow. This multi-database approach leveraged the ligand similarity-based algorithms of the ChEMBL, Similarity Ensemble Approach (SEA) and SwissTargetPrediction to computationally infer potential biological targets. Following the integration of the results, a final set of 194 unique putative targets was identified (Fig. 1B).

Identification of PCa-related target genes

To address batch effects, four prostate cancer datasets (GSE46602, GSE55945, GSE69223, and GSE71016) underwent cross-platform meta-integration with systematic normalization of expression matrices. Principal component analysis validated this preprocessing, revealing optimized data distribution and enhanced sample stratification in normalized cohorts (Fig. 2C). Subsequent differential expression analysis pinpointed 621 significantly altered prostate carcinogenesis genes, visualized via volcano plots and heatmaps (Fig. 2D). Complementarily, weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) constructed interaction networks by first establishing scale-free topology through optimal soft-thresholding power (β) determination. Topological overlap matrix computation and hierarchical clustering generated 14 discrete co-expression modules (Fig. 2E), with module-trait relationships identifying PCa-associated clusters (P < 0.05, Fig. 2F). Integration of differentially expressed genes with PCa-correlated WGCNA modules yielded a deduplicated high-confidence set of 999 unique candidates (Fig. 2G).

Identification of DEP-associated disease targets in PCa

The intersection analysis between DEP target proteins and PCa-related genes identi- fied 9 potential key targets involved in DEP-induced prostate carcinogenesis (Fig. 3A, B). Functional characterization through GO and KEGG enrichment analyses revealed that these targets are significantly enriched in (Fig. 3C, D): Biological processes: regulation of blood circulation, cardiac muscle contraction, and calcium ion transport; Cellular components: L-type voltage-gated calcium channel complexes, synaptic membranes, and ion transporter complexes; Molecular functions: calcium channel activity, G protein-coupled neurotransmitter receptor activity, and metal ion transmembrane transport; KEGG pathways: calcium signaling pathway, vascular smooth muscle contraction, cholinergic/serotonergic synapses, and endocrine secretion (insulin, renin, GnRH). These findings collectively implicate calcium dysregulation as the central mechanism, with concomitant disruption of neuroendocrine signaling and smooth muscle function in DEP-associated prostate carcinogenesis.

Identification of core genes in DEP-induced PCa

Machine learning interrogation of 9 candidate targets generated 113 predictive models for DEP-associated prostate carcinogenesis, with the glmBoost + RF ensemble exhibiting superior accuracy across training/validation cycles (Fig. 4A). This analysis distilled five pivotal regulators: TRPM8, CTSB, CA14, GSTM2, and MYLK. Individually, these core genes demonstrated modest to good diagnostic potential as standalone biomarkers in the validation cohorts, with AUCs ranging from 0.743 to 0.777 (AUC>0.75, Fig. 4B and Table S4), while differential expression profiling in PCa tissues revealed distinct transcriptional signatures (Fig. 4C). Critically, the integration of these five genes into an ensemble model (glmBoost + RF) yielded a significantly more robust and powerful diagnostic classifier. The ensemble achieved a mean AUC of 0.994 on the training set and generalized effectively to independent validation cohorts, with AUCs of 0.883, 0.854, and 0.854 for GSE32571, GSE62872, and GSE79021, respectively (see Table S4 for complete ensemble metrics). A DeLong’s test confirmed that the ensemble model’s performance was significantly superior to that of any single gene across all validation sets. SHAP interpretable modeling identified TRPM8 (SHAP = 0.093) and CA14 (0.0813) as dominant predictors (Fig. 4D-E), uncovering critical non-linear dynamics: synergistic TRPM8-MYLK co-expression and CTSB’s peak contribution threshold (> 10TPM, Fig. 4F). Force-directed analysis further established CTSB (Δ = − 0.147) and MYLK (Δ = − 0.0685) as primary negative modulators, depressing prediction values (f(x) = 0.32) below expected baselines (E[f(x)] = 0.572, Fig. 4G).

Discussion

Environmental endocrine-disrupting chemicals (EDCs) such as diethyl phthalate (DEP) have been increasingly implicated in prostate carcinogenesis [17, 18], yet the precise molecular mechanisms underlying this association remain incompletely char- acterized. Our study illustrates a potential approach for integrating exposomic and genomic data to unravel the mechanisms of environmental carcinogens. By combining these data, we propose a genome–exposome interaction model for DEP-induced prostate cancer. This mechanism may represent an emerging paradigm in precision environmental health, where exposome-driven analyses of human data are essential for identifying actionable mechanisms.

Conventional perspectives primarily attribute DEP’s carcinogenic effects to its endocrine-disrupting actions, such as interactions with the androgen receptor (AR) [19]. However, our findings reveal that the overlapping genes between DEP targets and PCa-associated genes are significantly enriched in pathways related to dysregulated calcium ion signaling, aberrant neuroendocrine signaling, and smooth muscle dysfunction. This discovery suggests that DEP may influence prostate carcinogenesis and progression through a complex, multidimensional network rather than a singular endocrine pathway. These results provide a novel theoretical framework for the toxicological assessment of DEP and suggest a new research direction for investigating the role of other environmental pollutants in cancer development.

The pronounced enrichment of calcium signaling pathways underscores the pivotal role of calcium in oncogenic transformation, where disrupted calcium homeostasis drives key hallmarks of cancer, including persistent proliferative signaling, resistance to growth suppression, and heightened invasive–metastatic potential [20, 21]. Furthermore, The marked enrichment of neuroendocrine signaling pathways offers mechanistic insight into the well-documented neuroendocrine differentiation observed in advanced prostate cancer [22, 23], suggesting a potential role for DEP in driving malignant progression toward more aggressive phenotypes. The identification of L-type voltage-gated calcium channel and ion transporter complexes as key cellular components indicates that DEP primarily perturbs membrane-associated calcium regulatory machinery, thereby disrupting the spatiotemporal control of intracellular calcium dynamics critical for normal prostate epithelial function [24].

TRPM8, a well-characterized oncogenic driver in prostate cancer biology [25], is computationally predicted in our study to be directly involved in the action network of DEP exposure. As an androgen-regulated nonselective cation channel, TRPM8 demonstrates elevated expression in prostate cancer cells, correlating with enhanced invasiveness and poor clinical prognosis [26, 27]. Through SHAP-based interpretable modeling, we not only quantified the dominant contribution of TRPM8 to DEP-induced prostate cancer prediction (SHAP value = 0.093) but also untangled a novel synergistic co-expression pattern with MYLK. This finding transcends the conventional single-target paradigm, revealing that DEP orchestrates a coordinated network effect rather than isolated TRPM8 activation.The mechanistic cascade likely involves DEP-mediated activation of TRPM8, leading to enhanced calcium influx, which subsequently triggers MYLK activation through a calcium-calmodulin-dependent mechanism [28]. This results in phosphorylation of myosin light chains and enhanced cellular contractility and migratory capacity. Such cross-pathway synergy represents a sophisticated multi-target coordination mechanism, providing a more nuanced biological insight into the role of DEP in promoting prostate cancer progression. We identified that the MYLK-TRPM8 synergistic axis supports a multi-tiered mechanism wherein DEP exposure concurrently activates calcium signaling (via TRPM8) and cytoskeletal regulatory pathways (via MYLK), establishing a positive feedback loop that markedly enhances the migratory and invasive capacities of prostate cancer cells.

Our study challenges the conventional understanding of CTSB function in prostate cancer by untangling an unexpected negative regulatory contribution (Δ = − 0.147) in the predictive model [29, 30]. This finding aligns with emerging evidence suggesting that low CTSB expression levels may participate in cellular homeostasis maintenance or even exert tumor-suppressive functions, whereas high expression shifts toward pro-oncogenic activity [31]. This biphasic characteristic implies that the effect of DEP on CTSB is complex and dependent on either exposure dosage or specific stage of prostate cancer progression. The carbonic anhydrase (CA) family catalyzes the hydration reaction of CO and plays a pivotal role in pH regulation, ion exchange, and cellular metabolism [32]. In malignant tissues, elevated CA expression frequently induces extracellular acidification, which not only facilitates cancer cell invasion but may also promote tumor immune evasion by suppressing T lymphocyte activity [33, 34]. Our findings suggest that DEP exposure may modulate CA14 expression, thereby exacerbating acidification of the prostate cancer microenvironment and creating favorable conditions for tumor cell proliferation, invasion, and immune evasion. This mechanism represents a sophisticated environmental carcinogenic strategy wherein toxicants not only directly affect cancer cells but also remodel the surrounding microenvironment to support malignant progression.

Our molecular docking results predicted high-affinity binding between DEP and all five core targets. It is noteworthy that the computed binding energy for MYLK was particularly strong (ΔG − 15 kcal/mol). Although such a low value is atypical and should be interpreted with caution in the context of classical docking scoring functions, its consistent replication across multiple independent simulations lends credibility to the finding that DEP binds strongly to MYLK. This strong interaction may be attributed to the optimal shape complementarity and extensive hydrophobic/electrostatic interactions within MYLK’s catalytic cleft. Nevertheless, we acknowledge that this computational prediction requires validation through experimental techniques such as Isothermal Titration Calorimetry (ITC) or Surface Plasmon Resonance (SPR).

Our findings indirectly untangle that DEP may promote cancer progression through immune microenvironment remodeling. Multiple enriched pathways, including calcium signaling and vascular smooth muscle contraction pathways, are closely associated with immune cell function and tumor angiogenesis [35]. Calcium signaling is essential for T-cell activation and immune synapse formation, while smooth muscle contraction pathways participate in tumor vasculature formation and functionality, thereby influencing immune cell infiltration patterns [36, 37]. These findings are consistent with previous studies reporting that environmental pollutants can influence both cancer cell intrinsic pathways and immune cell functionality, thereby amplifying tumor progression.

The outstanding advantage of this study lies in its rigorous multi-level closed-loop research framework, which exemplifies the application of AI and explainable ML in translational exposomics. This framework integrates multi-platform target prediction, cross-dataset meta-analysis, machine learning (particularly SHAP interpretable modeling)-driven core network mining, and final molecular docking structural validation, forming a self-validating analytical loop. This closed-loop design not only significantly enhances the reliability and credibility of results by establishing multiple lines of evidence but also bridges the gap between theoretical prediction and clinical/experimental validation, offering a reproducible model for environmental toxicology. The triangulation method combining prospective prediction with retrospective clinical analysis and prospective structural validation, along with the powerful capability of interpretable machine learning in untangling complex exposure-disease mechanisms, not only offers a systematic model for understanding the action networks of specific environmental carcinogens (e.g., DEP) but also contributes to a shift toward ”mechanism-informed” predictive models in environmental health risk assessment, demonstrating broad application potential.

However, despite the high confidence level of our model, its biological significance and clinical relevance require direct validation through ex vivo cellular experiments and in vivo animal models. Meanwhile, the off-target effects of DEP (diethyl phthalate), such as those mediated by epigenetic modifications (including DNA methylation and histone acetylation) or metabolite actions (e.g., monoethyl phthalate), remain systematically unevaluated in our analysis. Furthermore, future studies should employ single-cell sequencing and immunohistochemical techniques to systematically assess the impact of DEP exposure on the immune microenvironment of prostate cancer. Understanding these immunomodulatory effects could guide immunotherapy strategies and inform combinatorial approaches addressing both direct cellular effects and immune system restoration.

Conclusion

Our study proposes a genome–exposome interaction model for DEP-induced prostate cancer, highlighting the central role of calcium signaling, neuroendocrine disruption, and microenvironment remodeling. These findings underscore the importance of integrating human genomic data with environmental exposure assessment to advance precision environmental health.
