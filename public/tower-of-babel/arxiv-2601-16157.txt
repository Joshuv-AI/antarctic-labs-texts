A saturation bound for cumulative responses under local linear relaxation
Sanjeev Kumar Verma

Abstract
Saturation of cumulative observables is widely observed in systems with propagating or spreading signals and is commonly modeled using system-specific mechanisms such as scattering statistics, coherence functions, or phenomenological decay laws. This work shows that such saturation follows directly from linear local relaxation alone. Any linear observable accumulated over the lifetime of a relaxing signal is bounded by a scale set by the relaxation time, independent of geometry, dimensionality, or microscopic dynamics. When relaxation is mapped to space through transport or spreading, this temporal bound yields a corresponding spatial saturation scale. A closed-form expression reveals a two-regime behavior: linear growth at short times followed by saturation beyond the relaxation time. The result provides a minimal and unified explanation for cumulative saturation across transport, diffusive, and stochastic systems.

Introduction

Many physical systems involve signals that propagate or spread while undergoing local relaxation. Examples include attenuated waves, diffusive transport with decay, and stochastic processes with finite memory

Saturation of these cumulative responses is widely observed: as propagation time or path length increases, the accumulated signal approaches a finite limit. In practice, this behavior is typically modeled using detailed properties of the medium, including scattering parameters, coherence statistics, or empirical decay models. In radiative transfer and kinetic theory, attenuation is characterized through optical depth and mean free path
[
1
,
4

. As a result, explanations of saturation are often formulated in domain-specific terms rather than as consequences of a common underlying principle.

The purpose of this work is to show that saturation of cumulative observables does not require system-specific modeling. Instead, it follows directly and generically from local linear relaxation alone. Once a signal decays exponentially in time due to a local decay mechanism, any linear cumulative observable must saturate beyond the relaxation time. Transport, diffusion, or stochastic dynamics determine only how this temporal limitation is translated into spatial extent. By isolating this minimal explanation, the work reframes a familiar phenomenon as a general consequence of linear relaxation.

2
Minimal assumptions

Consider a scalar signal
\psi(t)
that evolves in time and undergoes linear local relaxation. The framework rests on three minimal assumptions:

1.

The signal relaxes exponentially in time with rate
\nu>0
,

\frac{d\psi}{dt}=-\nu\psi.

(1)

2.

The observable of interest is a linear cumulative response accumulated over time,

\mathcal{A}(T)=\int_{0}^{T}\psi(t)\,dt.

(2)

3.

When spatial extent is of interest, it arises through a transport or spreading process that maps time into distance.

Here, a “cumulative observable” refers to any linear quantity defined as a time
or path integral of the signal amplitude, such as accumulated phase, amplitude,
or response. No assumptions are made regarding geometry, dimensionality, or the
detailed form of the transport mechanism. This abstraction isolates the
consequences of local relaxation for cumulative observables.

3
Saturation of cumulative response

Solving the relaxation equation gives

\psi(t)=\psi_{0}e^{-\nu t},

(3)

where
\psi_{0}
is the initial amplitude. The cumulative response is then

\mathcal{A}(T)=\int_{0}^{T}\psi_{0}e^{-\nu t}\,dt=\frac{\psi_{0}}{\nu}\left(1-e^{-\nu T}\right).

(4)

This immediately implies the bound

\mathcal{A}(T)\leq\frac{\psi_{0}}{\nu}\quad\text{for all }T.

(5)

Thus, local exponential relaxation alone ensures the existence of a finite upper bound on any linear cumulative observable. Once the accumulation time exceeds the relaxation time
\tau=1/\nu
, further extension in path length does not increase the response.

4
Short-time and long-time behavior

Introducing the dimensionless parameter

\Pi=\nu T,

(6)

the response exhibits a two-regime behavior:

•

Short-time regime
(
\Pi\ll 1
):

\mathcal{A}(T)\simeq\psi_{0}T,

(7)

corresponding to linear accumulation.

•

Long-time regime
(
\Pi\gg 1
):

\mathcal{A}(T)\simeq\frac{\psi_{0}}{\nu},

(8)

corresponding to saturation.

This behavior is fixed entirely by the relaxation rate and does not depend on the spatial realization of the dynamics.

5
Relation between relaxation and spatial accumulation

Local linear relaxation fixes a finite time scale
\tau=1/\nu
. Any spatial limitation on cumulative response arises only through the mechanism by which the dynamics relate time to distance. Consequently, different transport processes map the same relaxation time onto different characteristic spatial scales, without affecting the existence of a finite saturation bound itself.

For transport with a well-defined propagation speed
v
, distance grows linearly with time,
x=vt
. Over the relaxation time
\tau
, the signal explores a characteristic length

L=v\tau=\frac{v}{\nu},

(9)

which sets the spatial scale at which path-integrated responses saturate along the propagation direction.

For diffusive dynamics with decay,

\partial_{t}\psi=D\nabla^{2}\psi-\nu\psi,

(10)

there is no fixed propagation speed. Spatial extent instead grows through spreading, with
\langle x^{2}\rangle\sim Dt
. Over the relaxation time
\tau
, diffusion explores a characteristic length

L\sim\sqrt{\frac{D}{\nu}}.

(11)

Local exponential relaxation therefore suppresses contributions from distances much larger than
L
, so that cumulative responses expressed in spatial terms saturate once this scale is exceeded
[
2
]
.

In finite-memory stochastic dynamics,

\dot{\psi}=-\nu\psi+\eta(t),

(12)

with
\eta(t)
a zero-mean noise term, accumulation occurs along the temporal trajectory rather than in space. The relaxation rate
\nu
fixes a finite memory duration
\tau=1/\nu

[
3
]
. The mean response decays exponentially,
\langle\psi(t)\rangle=\psi_{0}e^{-\nu t}
, and integrating the exponentially relaxing mean response yields a saturated cumulative value
\psi_{0}/\nu
for times much longer than
\tau
, independent of the noise statistics.

These examples share a common mathematical structure: linear evolution combined with local exponential relaxation. This can be expressed in a transport-independent way by introducing an effective time–distance mapping. For a given process, let
t_{\mathrm{eff}}(x)
denote the characteristic time required for the signal to influence a point a distance
x
away, as determined by the transport scaling law
x\sim\ell(t)
via inversion,

t_{\mathrm{eff}}(x)=\ell^{-1}(x)
. Local relaxation then implies

\psi(x)=\psi_{0}e^{-\nu\,t_{\mathrm{eff}}(x)}.

(13)

Within this framework, two distinct linear readouts of the same relaxing signal
naturally arise. A
local (boundary) readout
samples the signal amplitude
at a given distance
L
and is given directly by
\psi(L)
, which decays
exponentially with increasing
L
as a consequence of local relaxation. By
contrast, an
integral (accumulative) readout
aggregates the signal along
the path up to distance
L
and therefore takes the form

\mathcal{A}_{x}(L)=\psi_{0}\int_{0}^{L}e^{-\nu\,t_{\mathrm{eff}}(x)}\,dx.

(14)

The former characterizes the end-point signal amplitude, while the latter measures
the cumulative response accumulated along the path. Local exponential relaxation
ensures convergence of the integral readout
\mathcal{A}_{x}(L)
, so that it
saturates once the accumulation distance exceeds the relaxation-controlled
scale, as the boundary amplitude
\psi(L)
continues to decay
monotonically with distance.

For constant-speed transport one has
t_{\mathrm{eff}}(x)=x/v
, which gives

\mathcal{A}_{x}(L)=\psi_{0}\int_{0}^{L}e^{-\nu x/v}\,dx=\psi_{0}\,\frac{v}{\nu}\left(1-e^{-\nu L/v}\right).

(15)

The cumulative response therefore saturates at

\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{sat}}=\frac{\psi_{0}v}{\nu}\qquad(L\gg v/\nu),

(16)

with the saturation scale set by the relaxation length
v/\nu
.

For diffusive dynamics, the transport scaling
t_{\mathrm{eff}}(x)\sim x^{2}/D
leads instead to

\mathcal{A}_{x}(L)\sim\psi_{0}\int_{0}^{L}e^{-(\nu/D)x^{2}}\,dx=\psi_{0}\,\frac{\sqrt{\pi D}}{2\sqrt{\nu}}\,\mathrm{erf}\!\left(\sqrt{\frac{\nu}{D}}\,L\right).

(17)

In this case the cumulative response saturates at

\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{sat}}=\frac{\psi_{0}\sqrt{\pi D}}{2\sqrt{\nu}}\qquad(L\gg\sqrt{D/\nu}),

(18)

with the saturation scale determined by the diffusive relaxation length

\sqrt{D/\nu}
.

Thus, although different transport mechanisms map relaxation time to distance in different ways, local linear relaxation alone guarantees the existence of a finite bound on any linear path-integrated response. Transport determines the scale at which saturation is reached, but does not affect the existence of the bound itself.

Within this framework, sustained growth of a linear cumulative observable in a regime where local exponential relaxation is otherwise established signals the involvement of additional effects, such as effective nonlinearity, nonlocal coupling, or deviations from exponential relaxation. Conversely, saturation observed in the absence of identifiable local relaxation suggests that accumulation is controlled by mechanisms distinct from linear local decay, including long-memory or nonlinear processes.

6
Relation to existing results and structural implications

The result derived here makes explicit a general constraint implied by linear local relaxation: any linear cumulative observable obtained by integrating a relaxing signal is bounded by a finite scale set by the relaxation time. Although this limitation is often assumed implicitly in practical modeling, it is rarely stated or derived in a form that is independent of geometry, dimensionality, or the specific details of the transport mechanism.

In several established physical frameworks, attenuation and relaxation play a central role, yet their implications for observables defined by time or path integration are typically left implicit. In radiative transfer and kinetic theory, attenuation is characterized through optical depth and mean free path
[
1
,
4
]
, which describe the local decay of propagating signals. These quantities, however, are primarily local descriptors of attenuation and are not usually formulated as statements about the boundedness of cumulative observables obtained by integrating a relaxing signal over arbitrarily long times or paths.

In linear control theory, stability is often formulated through the requirement that finite inputs produce finite system outputs under sustained driving
[
5
]
. This notion, however, concerns instantaneous responses and does not explicitly address observables defined through cumulative integration over time or along propagation paths.

In wave-based settings, similar considerations arise. In radio-frequency propagation through extended media, coherence lengths and path-loss models empirically encode signal decay with distance
[
6
]
. In interferometric systems—radio, optical, or seismic—phase variance and fringe visibility degrade with increasing baseline length, commonly described using coherence measures such as the Fried parameter in optical astronomy
[
7
,
8
]
. In atmospheric wave–optics coupling, including laser propagation and adaptive optics, wavefront distortions accumulate along extended paths while attenuation and decorrelation act locally
[
9
]
. In all these cases, saturation of cumulative effects is well recognized in practice, but is typically incorporated through system-specific models or empirical parameters rather than derived as a direct and general consequence of local linear relaxation acting on cumulative observables.

The present result clarifies that the boundedness of linear cumulative responses follows directly from local relaxation itself, rather than from detailed modeling assumptions. Transport and spreading mechanisms determine how the relaxation time is mapped onto a spatial or path-length scale, but they do not affect the existence of a finite upper bound on accumulation. Persistent growth of a linear cumulative observable in a regime where local exponential relaxation is established therefore signals the involvement of additional physical ingredients, such as effective nonlinearity, nonlocal coupling, or departures from exponential relaxation. Observing such behavior provides a clear indicator of regimes in which mechanisms beyond local linear relaxation influence cumulative response. Local relaxation alone fixes the existence of a finite bound on linear cumulative response, while transport sets its scale, making persistent growth a clear signature of additional dynamical structure. Departures from linearity, locality, or exponential relaxation therefore define natural directions in which new models of cumulative response can arise.
