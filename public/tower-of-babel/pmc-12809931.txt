Assessment of cone-beam computed tomography indications in pediatric patients: a retrospective radiographic analysis
Authors: Ceylan Çağıl ERTUĞRUL, Burak Kerem APAYDIN
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) provides high-quality, three-dimensional imaging of the maxillofacial region. However, its use in pediatric patients remains controversial due to radiation dose concerns and the sensitivity of immature anatomical structures to radiation damage.This study aims to determine the prevalence and indications of dental CBCT usage in children.MethodsAll CBCT images of pediatric patients under 15 years old, obtained from 2018 to 2025 at a university hospital, were retrospectively reviewed. Demographic data, indications for CBCT, imaging field of view (FOV), and average kVp and mA values were evaluated. Descriptive statistics and Chi-square tests were used for data analysis.ResultsA total of 266 CBCT images from children aged 5–14 years (mean age 11.06; 128 females, 138 males) were included. The prevalence of CBCT among all pediatric patients in this age group was found to be 2.1%. Of the CBCT scans, 175 were for the pathologies from maxilla, 88 from the mandible, and 3 involving both jaws. Imaging was most commonly performed in the anterior (incisor) region for both sexes, with a statistically significant higher rate in males (p<0.001). Surgical indications accounted for 81.6% of cases, while orthodontic indications comprised 18.4%. The most common reasons for CBCT imaging were impacted teeth (29.3%), pathological lesions (21.4%), supernumerary teeth (17.3%), and mesiodens (15.8%). The most frequently used FOV was 15x12 cm.ConclusionsThe prevalence of CBCT use in pediatric patients is low and is primarily preferred before surgical interventions for impacted teeth and cyst/tumor-like lesions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-025-07523-9.

Background

Cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) has become a frequently utilized imaging modality in dentistry, especially for diagnostic and treatment planning purposes in recent years. This technology enables detailed three-dimensional visualization of complex anatomical structures, providing more comprehensive information compared to conventional two-dimensional imaging. CBCT is commonly preferred for evaluating pathological conditions in the maxillofacial region, locating impacted teeth, diagnosing temporomandibular joint disorders, planning dental implants, and performing skeletal analysis prior to orthodontic treatment [1].

CBCT enables precise measurement of the maxillofacial region in axial, sagittal, and coronal planes from various angles, facilitating both diagnosis and treatment planning. Moreover, CBCT images can be digitally stored, allowing for comparative assessments between multiple imaging sessions over time.

Despite these advantages, the use of CBCT in pediatric patients raises certain ethical and clinical concerns [2]. The pediatric population is more sensitive to ionizing radiation due to rapidly dividing cells and longer life expectancy, which increases the potential for radiation-induced adverse effects. Ongoing organ development and cellular growth render children more susceptible to the harmful effects of radiation. [3] Additionally, image quality may be compromised by artifacts and increased scatter radiation. [2]

The International Commission on Radiological Protection (ICRP) reports that children have a 2 to 3 times higher radiation risk compared to adults [4]. Therefore, indications for CBCT in children must be carefully determined, adhering to principles of necessity, minimal dose, and limited field of exposure.

Studies have reported that the mortality risk associated with radiation exposure is higher in children compared to adolescents [5]. Therefore, it is strongly recommended that dental professionals adhere to the ALARA principle (As Low As Reasonably Achievable), ensuring that radiation exposure is minimized while still achieving diagnostic adequacy [6].

CBCT is most commonly indicated in children for the localization of impacted teeth, assessment of malocclusions, cleft lip and palate, dentomaxillofacial anomalies, foreign body localization, trauma evaluation, and identification of cysts, tumors, and other pathologies. Nonetheless, there are limited studies in the literature addressing the frequency of CBCT use, types of indications, and technical parameters in pediatric patients [7–9].

According to relevant guidelines, CBCT should only be used when conventional two‑dimensional imaging is deemed inadequate and the three‑dimensional information obtained will significantly influence patient management [10]. Despite the increasing use of CBCT in pediatric dentistry, there remains a significant gap in the literature regarding how frequently it is used, for which specific indications, and with what imaging parameters in children under 15 years of age. Furthermore, most existing data are derived from studies in different countries with varying clinical practices and guidelines. This creates a need for region-specific evidence to inform local decision-making. The clinical relevance of the present study lies in its potential to provide a comprehensive overview of CBCT usage trends in a real-world pediatric population over a seven-year period. By identifying common indications, anatomical regions imaged, and technical parameters employed, this study aims to contribute to the optimization of CBCT practices and promote safer, more justified use of radiation in pediatric dental care.

The present study retrospectively analyzes CBCT images from children under 15 years at a university hospital to identify usage patterns, prevalance among pediatric population, common clinical reasons and principal indications, thereby providing a basis for improving CBCT practices in pediatric dentistry.

The classification of CBCT indications was primarily based on the textbook Oral Radiology: Principles and Interpretation (7th ed.) by White and Pharoah [10], which served as a key reference in this study and based on the evidence-based guidelines [11]. In alignment with these recommendations, indications in our study were grouped under two main categories. Cases involving mesiodens, pathological lesions, temporomandibular joint (TMJ) disorders, internal resorption, odontomas, dental trauma, impacted supernumerary teeth, and external resorption were categorized under surgical indications, while cases involving impacted teeth (no surgical intervention is planned), ankylosed teeth, and cleft lip and palate were classified under orthodontic indications.

The FOV was determined by the radiology department at the time of image acquisition. The referring clinician provided the clinical indication and relevant anatomical area on the imaging request form, but the radiologist selected the most appropriate FOV size to ensure sufficient diagnostic coverage while minimizing radiation exposure, in accordance with radiation safety guidelines.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS 25.0 software. Descriptive statistics (mean, standard deviation) were calculated. Differences between gender and age groups were assessed using Chi-square tests, with significance set at p < 0.05.

Results

A total of 266 CBCT images from pediatric patients (128 females, 138 males) who were under 15 years of age were analyzed in the study. The patients’ ages ranged from 5 to 14 years, with an overall mean age of 11.06 years; the mean age was 11.45 for females and 10.71 for males. No instances of repeated CBCT scans were identified within the examined age group. The prevalence of CBCT was determined to be 2.1% among the total number of pediatric patients in the same age group who visited our hospital.

Among the CBCT indication findings, surgical indications accounted for 81.6% (217 patients) of all cases, while orthodontic indications accounted for 18.4% (49 patients). CBCT images were more frequently taken for surgical reasons in both females and males, with no statistically significant difference between genders in this regard (p = 0.617).

CBCT images were most frequently taken from the incisor region in both females and males, with the difference being statistically significant in males (p < 0.001). Patients included in the study were divided into three groups based on age. Patients aged 5–6 were categorized as Group 1, those aged 7–12 as Group 2, and those aged 13–15 as Group 3. No statistically significant difference was found between age groups in terms of indication type (surgical or orthodontic) (P = 0.539).

All six patients in Group 1 (ages 5–6) underwent CBCT imaging due to surgical indications (3 for mesiodens and 3 for pathological lesion). CBCT scans of the small number of patients in Group 1 (n = 6) were found to be equally distributed between the maxilla and mandible, and most commonly obtained from the anterior teeth region (66.6%).

Of the 169 patients in Group 2 (ages 7–12), 139 underwent CBCT imaging for surgical indications. Similarly, 72 out of 91 patients in Group 3 had CBCT imaging for surgical reasons. Patients in Group 2 most commonly underwent CBCT due to impacted teeth, pathological formations, mesiodens, and impacted supernumerary teeth, respectively. Patients in Group 3 most commonly underwent CBCT due to impacted teeth, pathological formations, impacted supernumerary teeth, and temporomandibular joint (TMJ) problems, respectively.

Out of the CBCT images, 175 (65.8%) were taken for the pathologies from the maxilla, 88 (33.1%) for the pathologies from the mandible, and 3 (1.1%) for the pathologies involving both jaws. Imaging was more frequently performed on the maxillary pathologies in both females (56.8%) and males (75.3%), and this difference was statistically significant in males (p = 0.001). CBCT scans were most commonly performed in the maxilla among both patients aged 7–12 years (Group 2; 73.9%) and those aged 13–15 years (Group 3; 51.6%). This pattern was significantly more evident in Group 2 (p = 0.001).

In terms of age groups, CBCT imaging was most frequently performed in the anterior teeth region in both Group 2 (61.5%) and Group 3 (34.0%). This pattern was significantly more evident in Group 2 (p < 0.001). Discussion

In this retrospective study, the rate of CBCT usage among pediatric patients under the age of 15 who visited a university hospital between 2018 and 2025 was found to be 2.1%. This relatively low prevalence indicates that while CBCT is rarely used in children, it holds significant value in clinical decision-making when indicated. Similar studies from different regions reporting CBCT usage patterns in pediatric populations, but no data on the rate of CBCT usage among pediatric patients was observed to be comparable with the results of this study [12–15]. The relatively low prevalence of CBCT usage in pediatric patients in our study may be attributed to several contributing factors. First, clinicians at our institution appear to follow a conservative approach in line with the ALARA principle, reserving CBCT imaging for cases in which conventional radiographs are insufficient. Second, the use of alternative low-radiation imaging methods such as panoramic and intraoral radiographs remains widespread in pediatric dental diagnostics and treatment planning. Additionally, institutional policies and practitioner training regarding radiation safety may further limit unnecessary CBCT referrals in children.

In the majority of similar studies, the use of CBCT in children aged 0–6 has been reported as either nonexistent or negligible [7, 12, 16]. This consistency across studies underscores a global sensitivity toward minimizing radiation exposure in children. Similarly CBCT imaging was observed to be least frequent in the 5–6 age group and most frequent in the 7–12 age group. Additionally, CBCT indications involving the maxilla and anterior teeth were found to be significantly more frequent in the 7–12 age group compared to the other groups. The observed differences in CBCT utilization among the age groups in this study highlight important considerations regarding diagnostic imaging practices in pediatric and adolescent populations.

The mean age of children who underwent CBCT in this study was 11.06 years, which is relatively low compared to findings reported in the existing literature. 7,12,13,16 This discrepancy is likely attributable to the age range of the study population, which was limited to 15 years, whereas other studies typically included individuals up to 18 years of age.

Our findings revealed that 81.6% of CBCT scans were performed for surgical purposes, whereas only 18.4% were for orthodontic purposes. This highlights the predominant role of CBCT in surgical planning within pediatric dentistry. Among the most common surgical indications were impacted teeth and pathological lesions, followed by supernumerary teeth and developmental anomalies such as mesiodens. Similar studies have reported that CBCT is most commonly performed for surgical purposes [7]—such as cysts and other pathologies, followed by dental trauma—and, as in the present study, for the evaluation of impacted teeth [12]. However, in some studies, malocclusion and dentofacial anomalies have been reported as the primary indications for CBCT use [13, 16]. The ability of CBCT to accurately localize impacted teeth and assess their relationship to adjacent anatomical structures significantly improves preoperative planning and may help reduce the risk of complications [17–19].

According to present study the lower rate of orthodontic indications suggests that two-dimensional radiographic methods are generally sufficient for diagnosis and treatment planning in most pediatric orthodontic cases. Nevertheless, in complex scenarios—such as skeletal asymmetries, tooth impactions, or dental crowding not clearly visible in conventional radiographs—CBCT proves to be a valuable tool [20, 21]. These findings are consistent with the current literature [11] and recommendations of the European Commission’s SEDENTEXCT guidelines, which advocate for CBCT use in children only when conventional imaging is insufficient and when it is expected to significantly influence treatment decisions [22].

Gender-based analysis revealed a statistically significant difference in the anatomical regions imaged, with male patients more frequently undergoing imaging of the incisor region (p < 0.001). This could be attributed to gender-related variations in dental development. Previous research supports this finding, noting that impacted teeth and cysts, are more frequently encountered in boys [7, 13]. Conversely, some studies have indicated that CBCT is more commonly used in female patients [12, 16].

As in many studies in the literature,12,13,16 CBCT was most frequently utilized in this study for the evaluation of pathologies located in the maxilla. The significantly higher frequency of CBCT imaging in the 7–12-year age group, particularly in the maxilla and anterior teeth regions, may reflect the unique clinical challenges and diagnostic needs characteristic of this developmental period. During the mixed dentition phase, which typically occurs between the ages of 7 and 12, dental practitioners often encounter complex cases such as impacted teeth, supernumerary teeth, and pathological formations. Notably, supernumerary teeth such as mesiodens are predominantly found in the anterior maxillary region, contributing to the higher diagnostic demand for imaging in this area. The increased prevalence of these anomalies in the maxillary anterior region explains, in part, the more frequent use of CBCT scans targeting this zone.

Restricting the FOV to only the region of interest is considered one of the most efficient approaches to reducing radiation dose, and represents a key opportunity for optimization. Regarding imaging parameters, the 15 × 12 cm field of view (FOV) was the most commonly used across both genders and jaw regions. This finding is consistent with the results of similar studies [7, 16]. This FOV size offers an optimal balance between diagnostic utility and radiation dose, which is particularly important in pediatric patients. The frequent use of the 15 × 12 cm FOV in pediatric CBCT imaging may be due to its ability to provide sufficient anatomical coverage while maintaining a balance with radiation exposure. In many clinical situations, especially when evaluating impacted teeth or anterior maxillary regions, this FOV size ensures that relevant structures are captured without requiring repeat scans. Its consistent use may also reflect a standardized protocol adopted to simplify imaging procedures in pediatric patients.

The use of lower mA and kVp values further contributes to dose optimization. Existing literature recommends that CBCT protocols for children should utilize radiation doses up to 60% lower than those applied in adult imaging [5, 23].

CBCT should clearly be avoided in routine diagnostic evaluations where conventional two-dimensional imaging (e.g., panoramic, periapical radiographs) can provide sufficient information, such as in most cases of caries detection, initial orthodontic assessments, or follow-up of previously diagnosed conditions without complications. On the other hand, CBCT is strongly preferred in situations where detailed three-dimensional evaluation is critical—such as in the localization of impacted or supernumerary teeth with complex anatomical positions, assessment of cystic or tumoral lesions with uncertain margins, pre-surgical planning for anomalies like cleft palate, or evaluation of trauma involving suspected fractures. In these cases, the diagnostic benefit of CBCT outweighs the radiation risk, especially when imaging is limited to the smallest possible field of view with optimized exposure settings [24, 25].

Overall, the findings of this study suggest that CBCT is used judiciously and appropriately in pediatric dental practice. However, to ensure safe and consistent imaging protocols, there is a need to standardize the clinical decision-making process for CBCT utilization by promoting greater adherence to national and international guidelines. Furthermore, it is ethically essential to ensure that pediatric patients and their guardians receive thorough information prior to imaging and that informed consent is obtained in all cases.

Conclusions

CBCT use in pediatric patients under 15 years is relatively low (2.1%) and mainly driven by surgical indications, especially impacted teeth and pathological lesions. CBCT imaging was more frequent in male children, particularly in the maxillary anterior teeth region. CBCT should be reserved for cases where it provides diagnostic benefit and conducted under optimized protocols to minimize radiation.

Clinical decisions regarding CBCT use in children should be individualized to avoid unnecessary radiation. Two-dimensional imaging modalities remain the first choice, with CBCT reserved for situations where additional diagnostic information is essential.

Strengths and limitations

One of the main strengths of this study is its comprehensive analysis of CBCT usage in a pediatric population over a 7-year period, which provides valuable insight into real-world clinical practices. Additionally, the inclusion of various parameters—such as FOV, indications, and gender-based distributions—adds depth to the findings.

However, this study also has some limitations. Being a retrospective, single-center study, the results may not fully represent broader trends across different institutions or regions. Furthermore, the lack of detailed radiation dose records per patient limits the evaluation of actual exposure levels. Future prospective, multi-center studies incorporating dose monitoring would provide more generalizable and clinically actionable data.
