Association between NHHR and depression symptoms among U.S. adults with hypertension: a cross sectional
Authors: Jie Lu, Xiaojing Zheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe non-HDL cholesterol to HDL cholesterol ratio (NHHR) is an emerging and reliable lipid marker for assessing the risk of cardiovascular disease. Previous studies have demonstrated a correlation between lipid metabolism and clinically significant depressive symptoms, while also identifying hypertension as a potential risk factor for this condition. This study aims to investigate the association between NHHR and clinically significant depressive symptoms in patients diagnosed with hypertension.MethodsThe study was conducted over a period from 2007 to 2018, utilizing data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES). The study population comprised participants aged 18 years and older from across the United States. Depressive symptoms were evaluated using the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9), while NHHR values were derived from laboratory cholesterol test results. The cross-sectional analysis included a total of 10,754 participants. Various methods were employed, including multiple logistic regression, curve analysis, threshold effect modeling, and subgroup analysis, to investigate the relationship between NHHR and depressive symptoms.ResultsThe curve-fitting model demonstrated a distinct U-shaped relationship among the variables examined. Threshold analysis indicates that a cutoff point of 1.25 exists in the association between log-transformed NHHR and clinically significant depressive symptoms. Below this threshold, NHHR was negatively correlated with the prevalence of depressive symptoms (OR = 0.19, 95% CI: 0.07–0.52, P = 0.001), whereas above the threshold, a positive correlation was observed (OR = 1.14, 95% CI: 1.09–1.19, P < 0.001).ConclusionsThe study findings suggest a U-shaped association between NHHR and depressive symptoms in adults with hypertension in the United States. NHHR appears to serve as a succinct indicator of depressive symptoms in individuals aged 18 and older with hypertension. Consequently, further exploration into the correlated factor NHHR may offer an innovative perspective for research on depressive symptoms among this population.

Introduction

Hypertension represents a major global public health concern that substantially elevates the risk of severe complications, including renal failure, cardiovascular disease, stroke, and cognitive impairment. Moreover, hypertension is frequently comorbid with mental health disorders, most notably depressive symptoms. Research indicates that approximately one in four individuals with hypertension also experience clinically significant depressive symptoms, suggesting a concerning interplay between these two conditions [1]. Depression symptoms is a prevalent mental health disorder characterized by symptoms including anhedonia, loss of interest in daily activities, insomnia, and in severe cases, suicidal ideation. Data from the Global Burden of Disease study reveals that the prevalence of depressive symptoms rose from 172 million in 1990 to 264 million in 2017, representing a 53.49% increase [2]. Despite advances in treatment approaches, clinically significant depressive symptoms remains associated with a high recurrence rate. Studies indicate that roughly 50% of people who have a single depressive episode will go on to experience further episodes [3].

The non-high-density lipoprotein cholesterol to high-density lipoprotein cholesterol ratio (NHHR) is increasingly recognized as a valuable marker for evaluating atherosclerotic lipid profiles. Recent studies suggest that NHHR may outperform traditional lipid parameters in predicting cardiovascular and cerebrovascular risks, thereby strengthening its clinical utility in the monitoring of such diseases [4, 5]. However, existing evidence remains inconsistent: some studies report a negative association, with lower high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) linked to more severe depressive symptoms, while others have found that elevated HDL-C levels may also be associated with clinically significant depressive symptoms [6, 7].

However, the association between NHHR and depression symptoms among adults with hypertension remains insufficiently studied. We therefore analyzed data from the 2007–2018 National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) to explore their potential relationship. We aim to provide a new approach for assessing depressive symptoms risk for healthcare practitioners and related professionals.

Methods

Source of research data

NHANES is a critically important cross-sectional health survey conducted in the United States. NHANES employs a intricate, multistage sampling design and combines data collection via interviews, physical examinations, and biospecimen analysis to generate a nationally representative dataset regarding the health of American adults and kids [8, 9]. The use of NHANES data is formally approved by the National Center for Health Statistics’ Research Ethics Review Board, with every subjects providing informed consent, signifying their understanding and agreement to participate in studies involving data collection through interviews, health examinations, and laboratory testing [10, 11]. For detailed information, access official website: www.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes/.

Study population

Our research drew upon data spanning six NHANES survey cycles between 2007 and 2018 to examine the potential relationship between NHHR levels and depressive symptoms. In this study, relatively complete data that meet our research requirements are only available for these six cycles. All other cycles suffer from critical limitations, including missing core indicators and study populations that fail to align with the inclusion criteria of this research. The initial sample pool comprised 59,842 participants; however, after rigorous screening, the final sample was reduced to 10,754 eligible individuals. We applied four key exclusion criteria: (1) Participants without hypertension (46,727 excluded); (2) Individuals under the age of 18 (62 excluded); (3) Participants with incomplete or missing PHQ-9 depression symptoms assessment data (11,341 excluded); and (4) Participants lacking complete NHHR measurement data (587 excluded). Hypertension assessment

Hypertension is defined as follows: 1. The diagnosis of hypertension is based on the NHANES standardized questionnaire, specifically the ‘Blood Pressure and Cholesterol Questionnaire’ (BPQ020). Participants were asked, ‘Have you ever been diagnosed with hypertension by a doctor or specialist?’ Those who answered ‘yes’ were classified as having hypertension [12]. 2. Participants were considered to have hypertension if the average of three measurements indicated a systolic blood pressure of ≥ 140 mmHg or a diastolic blood pressure of ≥ 90 mmHg. Data were recorded by trained medical personnel using standard mercury sphygmomanometers at Mobile Examination Centers (MECs). Participants remained seated throughout the process, with measurements typically taken on the right arm unless otherwise specified. After a five-minute rest period, blood pressure was measured three consecutive times, and researchers calculated the average of these readings for further evaluation [13]. 3. Participants currently taking antihypertensive medication were also classified as having hypertension. Participants are classified as having hypertension if they meet any one of the three criteria listed above [14].

Definition of NHHR

Calculations of NHHR were based on the methodology described in earlier research. NHHR is calculated by subtracting high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) from total cholesterol (TC) and then dividing the result by HDL-C [15].

Assessment of depression symptoms

NHANES employs the widely recognized Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) to evaluate the prevalence of depressive symptoms among participants over the preceding two weeks [16, 17]. The PHQ-9 categorizes the severity of depression symptoms based on the total score: individuals scoring below 5 are classified as not depressed; scores ranging from 5 to 9 suggest mild depression symptoms; while scores between 10 and 27 indicate moderate to severe levels of depression symptoms [18, 19]. According to the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fourth Edition (DSM-IV), a diagnosis of clinically significant depressive symptoms is established when the total score reaches or exceeds 10 points [20].This study uses a PHQ-9 score of 10 or higher as the primary outcome measure.

Covariates

We referred to previous studies, we concentrate on identifying the elements that could influence the link between NHHR and depression symptoms [21, 22]. This encompasses sociodemographic factors, health statuses, and residents’ lifestyles. A wide range of variables is considered, including age, sex, race/ethnicity, educational attainment, marital status, poverty income ratio (PIR), body mass index (BMI), daily behaviors involving tobacco use and alcohol consumption, TC, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), HDL-C, non-HDL cholesterol, triglyceride levels (TG), diabetes, and coronary heart disease (CHD). We classify races into four major categories: Mexican American, non-Hispanic Black, non-Hispanic White, and Other. Education is divided into three stages: compulsory education, high school, and tertiary education. Marital status is categorized into three types: married or living with a partner (cohabiting), never married (single), and widowed, orphaned, or lonely (discrete). To calculate BMI, weight (kg) is divided by height squared (m²). Smoking is classified as having consumed at least 100 cigarettes in a lifetime. Daily alcohol intake over the past year is averaged at more than one drink. Data on diabetes and coronary heart disease were obtained from the DIQ010 questionnaire and the MCQ160c medical status questionnaire, respectively.

Statistical analyses

Given the complex survey design of NHANES and the observed non-response rate, official, validated sample weights were adopted in all analyses. In this study, all variables were collected through household interviews and Mobile Examination Center (MEC) assessments. Accordingly, the two-year MEC weight (WTMEC2YR) was ultimately applied as the weighting variable for logistic regression analysis. Stratification variables (STRATA) and primary sampling unit (PSU) variables were further incorporated to reconstruct the survey design framework, offsetting biases stemming from the complex sampling strategy. To generate standardized weights for pooled survey cycles, the two-year examination weight of each NHANES cycle was divided by two. Considering that the NHHR value shows a skewed distribution, we log-transformed the variable before analysis. For continuous variables like age, BMI, PIR, HDL-C, LDL-C, TC, and TG, we indicate them as the mean ± SD; categorical variables like sex, race/ethnicity, educational attainment, etc. are shown as percentages.The two variable types undergo distinct testing methods: Student’s t-test and chi-square test. We explored the relationship between NHHR and depression symptoms among hypertensive individuals using logistic regression techniques. In our research, we employed multivariable logistic regression to determine the odds ratio (OR) and included the associated 95% confidence interval (CI) to examine their association. These multivariate logistic regression models incorporate both continuous and categorical variables. Among the categorical data, NHHR is divided into four equivalent segments, with the lowest segment serving as the baseline for comparison. For further analysis, three models were developed. Model 1 is the base model, untouched by any variables. Model 2, however, considers age, sex, and racial/ethnic background. Model 3, the comprehensive model, goes a step further by incorporating additional factors such as educational attainment, marital status, smoking habits, alcohol consumption, and BMI, based on the adjustments made in Model 2. The association between NHHR and depression symptoms was investigated using curve fitting techniques. When nonlinear patterns emerged, threshold effect analysis was employed to identify potential tipping points, followed by the development of a two-part multivariate logistic regression model on either side of these critical thresholds. Subsequently, we split the participants into eight distinct subgroups based on sex, racial and ethnic background, age, marital status, educational attainment, BMI, diabetes status, and history of coronary heart disease to further explore the relationship between the two. Following subgroup analyses, an interaction test was conducted to assess the uniformity across different subgroups.

R (version 3.4.3 available at www.r-project.org) and EmpowerStats (Version 2.0 available at www.empowerstats.com) were employed for all statistic analysis; statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05.

Results

Baseline characteristics of the participants

Table 1 presents the baseline characteristics and demographic data of study participants stratified by log-transformed NHHR quartiles. After applying the inclusion and exclusion criteria, the final analysis included 10,754 eligible participants, of whom 11.83% were diagnosed with depressive symptoms. The study revealed a nearly balanced gender distribution, with males comprising 48.92% and females 51.08%. Among the 1,272 individuals diagnosed with depressive symptoms, females were significantly overrepresented, accounting for 64.00%, while males constituted just over one-third of the total cases at 36.00%. The mean age of participants was 59.4 ± 15.0 years. The mean NHHR for all participants was 3.0 ± 1.5, with median values of 1.5 ± 0.3, 2.4 ± 0.2, 3.2 ± 0.3, and 5.0 ± 1.5.

The association between NHHR and depression symptoms

Table 2 illustrates the relationship between NHHR and depressive symptoms. Our analysis of continuous variables revealed a significant association between log-transformed NHHR and depressive symptoms. The odds ratios for each model are as follows: Model 1 (OR = 1.35, 95% CI: 1.19–1.53, P < 0.001), Model 2 (OR = 1.32, 95% CI: 1.15–1.51, P < 0.001), and Model 3 (OR = 1.28, 95% CI: 1.12–1.47, P < 0.001). To further evaluate the association between NHHR and depressive symptoms, log-transformed NHHR was categorized into four quartiles for the subsequent analyses. Model 1 represented the unadjusted baseline model. Model 2 was adjusted for age, sex, and race/ethnicity. Model 3 incorporated the factors from Model 2 and further included educational attainment, marital status, smoking status, alcohol consumption, BMI, physical activity, sleep status, and antihypertensive medication use. In the highest quartile, the OR of NHHR was higher than that in the lowest quartile (OR = 1.36, 95% CI = 1.13–1.62, P < 0.001). Elevated NHHR values were associated with a progressive increase in the odds of developing depressive symptoms among this population.

In addition, curve fitting and threshold effect analysis were employed to further elucidate the association between log-transformed NHHR and depressive symptoms. Figure 2 illustrates the nonlinear relationship between log-transformed NHHR and depressive symptoms through a smoothed curve fit. According to Table 3, threshold effect analysis identified 1.25 as the cutoff point. When NHHR is below 1.25, it is negatively associated with the prevalence of depressive symptoms (OR = 0.19, 95% CI: 0.07–0.52, P = 0.001). Conversely, when NHHR exceeds 1.25, it shows a positive association with the prevalence of depressive symptoms (OR = 1.14, 95% CI: 1.09–1.19, P < 0.001).

Discussion

This research investigated the association between NHHR and depressive symptoms among adults with hypertension in the U.S. In the fully adjusted model, a cross-sectional analysis of 10,754 participants revealed a U-shaped association between NHHR and depression symptoms among individuals with hypertension. The threshold effect’s inflection point is located at 1.25. In individuals with hypertension, depression symptoms levels show an inverse relationship with NHHR below the point, whereas above this threshold, NHHR exhibits a positive association with depressive symptoms.

This is the only research currently known that evaluates the association between NHHR and depressive symptoms among hypertensive adults. NHHR is an emerging lipid parameter that has garnered attention for its potential as a biomarker of lipid spectra related to various health conditions. Research indicates a strong interrelationship between cardiovascular disease and dyslipidemia. Dyslipidemia is not only a significant risk factor for cardiovascular disease but also exacerbates depressive symptoms through mechanisms such as inflammation and oxidative stress [23]. Research indicates that lower concentrations of HDL-C, rather than LDL-C, are strongly associated with an increased prevalence of depressive symptoms, particularly among specific populations such as postmenopausal women [24, 25]. This association suggests that HDL-C may serve as a potential biomarker for major depressive disorder and related suicidal tendencies. Furthermore, a meta-analysis has shown that decreased HDL-C levels significantly correlate with a heightened risk of clinically significant depressive symptoms across diverse populations [26]. The findings from these preliminary studies may indirectly support our outcomes. However, some studies have reported conflicting results. Jones et al. [27]. reviewed potential pathways through which metabolic dysfunction, evidenced by changes in cholesterol levels, could contribute to the pathogenesis of mood disorders. This includes alterations in brain structure and cognitive function, both of which are interconnected with depressive states. Additionally, research suggests that cytokine activation during depressive episodes interferes with cholesterol synthesis, resulting in lower serum cholesterol levels and establishing a cyclical relationship between mood and metabolic health [28]. The conflicting findings in the study may arise from variations in participant demographics, racial backgrounds, and methods for measuring depressive symptoms.

Several hypotheses are currently under investigation to unpack the biological mechanisms behind depressive symptoms. Chief among them is chronic inflammation: elevated levels of inflammatory markers have long been correlated with depression symptoms, underscoring inflammation’s pivotal role in the disorder’s pathogenesis. Beyond this, cytokines such as interleukin−6 have been shown to alter neural plasticity and emotional regulation, in turn giving rise to depressive symptoms [29]. The link between inflammation and neurobiology suggests that dysregulated immune responses may trigger neuroinflammation, a condition closely tied to both the emergence and persistent presence of depressive symptoms [30]. Recent studies indicate that HDL-C possesses anti-inflammatory properties that can significantly alter macrophage behavior. For instance, HDL-C has been shown to inhibit the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines by macrophages, a mechanism crucial for limiting excessive inflammatory responses and tissue damage [31, 32]. HDL-C interacts with pathogen-associated molecular patterns, effectively inhibiting their ability to activate inflammatory pathways, which is particularly significant in the context of infections [31, 33]. This interaction modulates macrophage responses, steering them towards a less inflammatory phenotype and facilitating the resolution of inflammation [34, 35]. The complex role of cytokines in modulating neurotransmitter systems has garnered substantial attention in recent years, especially regarding their influence on gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA), serotonin (5-HT), and dopamine (DA) biosynthesis and metabolism, which are crucial for mood regulation. Elevations in pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as interleukin-6 (IL-6) and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), have been consistently linked to mood disorders through their effects on neurotransmitter levels and neuroendocrine functions. Importantly, these cytokines can inhibit the activity of enzymes involved in the synthesis of key monoamines, contributing to the neurotransmitter dysregulation that underlies depressive symptoms [36, 37].

Building on the biological basis whereby inflammation and lipid metabolism disorders are implicated in depression pathogenesis, this study further identifies a pronounced nonlinear relationship between NHHR and depression symptoms risk. Detailed analyses show that populations with NHHR below 1.25 exhibit a relatively low risk of depression symptoms onset. This finding aligns with conclusions from multiple cross-sectional studies: low NHHR levels are generally associated with a low risk of depression symptoms, with only marginal threshold variations observed across different populations [38–40]. In contrast, depression symptoms risk rises substantially when NHHR exceeds 1.25. Once past this critical threshold, the rate of increase in depression symptoms risk gradually moderates and plateaus. This nonlinear pattern has been validated among elderly Chinese populations and NHANES participants in the Americas. Although threshold definition can be affected by sample characteristics and statistical models, the clear critical effect of NHHR in transitioning from low to high risk is well established [41, 42]. In conclusion, the nonlinear association between NHHR and depression symptoms risk holds considerable clinical value. It suggests that NHHR can serve as a promising biological marker for depression risk assessment. Clinically, precise monitoring of NHHR level fluctuations is imperative to prevent abnormal variations from exacerbating the risk of depression symptoms.

Our research has the following strengths. Firstly, the NHANES database we utilized is a nationally representative, large-scale dataset comprising average American adults. Its substantial sample size significantly enhances the reliability and robustness of our findings. Secondly, we controlled for numerous covariates and analyzed diverse variable types to further strengthen the reliability of our results. However, our research also has several limitations. Firstly, due to the nature of cross-sectional research, it is not possible to establish a cause-and-effect association between NHHR and depressive symptoms. To fully understand how initial NHHR levels influence the likelihood of depressive symptoms over time, more longitudinal investigations are necessary. Moreover, our research excluded participants from countries outside the United States, acknowledging the substantial disparities in depression symptom rates and blood lipid concentrations across various countries and regions [43, 44]. While the PHQ-9 questionnaire is an excellent tool for detecting depressive symptoms, it does not provide a definitive clinical diagnosis. Numerous studies suggest that its results may be skewed [45, 46].

Conclusion

Our study confirms a U-shaped association between NHHR and clinically significant depressive symptoms in the U.S. adult population with hypertension. NHHR appears to be a potential biological marker for depressive symptoms related to hypertension; however, the underlying mechanisms warrant further investigation. Additionally, establishing a causal relationship between NHHR and depressive symptoms will necessitate further prospective studies and randomized trials to validate our findings.
