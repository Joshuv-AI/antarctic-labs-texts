All-inorganic CeO2-TiO2 artificial photosynthesis system with forced heterojunctions for methane to C-C coupled products
Authors: Subhashree S Kanungo, Akshay Murali, Divya Verma, Chinnakonda S Gopinath
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummarySustainable and efficient photocatalytic conversion of small molecules to liquid fuels/chemicals needs a large extent of charge separation at heterojunctions and equal charge utilization at redox sites. Toward achieving this, visible-light absorbing CeO2 quantum dots (CeQDs) are directly assembled from the precursors (Ce3+ and OH−) into the TiO2 nanopores by the successive ionic layer adsorption and reaction method, and are subsequently integrated structurally and electronically. Assembly of CeQDs into TiO2 leads to a sub-trillion number of heterojunctions in 1 mg photocatalyst material. Concurrent activation of water and methane generates active radicals that help in selective ethanol and a minor amount of acetone formation. Product generation is further enhanced by deliberate reduction of the photocatalyst device, which enhances Ce3+ content and broadens the valence band with a significant change in electronic structure. The detailed analysis of the photocatalyst device and photocatalysis results reveals the charge transfer mechanism and the reaction pathway.Keywords: energy conversion, natural gas, solar energy, photocatalysis, heterojunctions, carbon neutral economy, climate neutral, sustainable development goal, SDG

Introduction

Methane serves as one of the prime sources of fossil fuel-based energy that propels the wheels of industrial growth. However, it also carries a significant environmental burden, including non-viability from off shore locations, climate changes, and cascading adverse effects.1,2 The need to develop methodologies that utilize the methane source for the generation of value-added products (VAPs) under ambient or near-ambient conditions drives the momentum of the field of heterogeneous catalysis. Elevated temperatures/pressures and high-throughput energy are indispensable in currently available methodologies that employ noble metal-based catalyst systems.3,4 Of particular interest lately is the direct generation of C2 products, viz., ethanol and ethylene.5,6 Although conventional processes of ethanol production yield significant industrial output, the yields are still far from meeting the needs, considering the vital input energy in terms of operational conditions and expenses.7,8

Harnessing solar energy to upgrade methane into C-C coupled products using water, a naturally abundant oxidant, offers the most attractive clean energy solutions. A significant surge in enriching the optical properties of rare earth oxides for photocatalytic reactions has been observed in recent years. In the mid-20th century, CeO2’s role as a catalyst, mainly in oxidation reactions and three-way catalytic converters, proliferated due to its readily switchable Ce3+ and Ce4+ oxidation states, which act as an oxygen storage/buffer in the catalytic reactions.9,10,11 Moreover, CeO2 being a semiconductor, it can photoexcite electrons under visible light through 4f-5d and/or 4f-4f (VB to CB) transitions to drive various photocatalytic reactions. While holes would be consumed for oxidation, electrons need to be separated in the exciton and consumed for reduction via suitable heterojunctions. If excited electrons could be selectively injected into another wide-bandgap semiconductor, which does not absorb visible light (like TiO2, ZnO), the extent of charge carrier utilization would be enhanced to a large extent. A similar work has been reported by Kanungo et al.,12 which demonstrates the successful assembly of a binary metal-oxide CeVO4-QDs within TiO2 nanopores for the selective photocatalytic conversion of methane to C-C coupled products (ethanol/ethylene). Crucially, to bridge the gap areas existing in materials engineering and/for energy conversion, attempts to create heterojunctions between two semiconductor materials have been explored in the present and earlier12 studies. Containment of visible light absorbing semiconductor particles in the pores of a wide bandgap material with structural/electronic integration and proper VB/CB energies is likely to exhibit desired charge separation/dispersion along with charge utilization.

From the above discussion, containing simple metal-oxide CeO2 quantum dots (CeQDs) in the pores of a wide bandgap TiO2 would be more challenging compared to a binary (CeVO4) photocatalytic system in which both V and Ce can undergo redox changes, reported earlier.12 Especially, hydroxyl groups would need a highly basic pH, while it is not the same for cation introduction into the pores. Nonetheless, they are expected to exhibit remarkable properties that help stir the sunlight-driven reactions: (1) surface modifiability by creating defect engineering, especially at the heterojunctions (CeO2-TiO2), along with tuneable redox capability of CeQDs, which enables better electronic interactions; (2) significant activity and scalability for oxidation reactions, and (3) long-term stability.13,14,15 The current work presents a coherent methodology to directly convert methane to C-C coupled products, i.e., ethanol and/or acetone, by methodically strategizing the engineering aspects of catalyst design. Herein, an integrated photocatalyst system has been developed, which entails the incorporation of a visible light absorbing semiconductor (CeO2) with tuneable oxidation states in the pores of TiO2. Particularly, growth of CeQDs is strategically designed by restricting them in the nanopores (2–3 nm) of commercial TiO2. The SILAR (successive ionic layer adsorption and reaction) method developed by Nicolau in 1985 is employed tactfully to form CeQDs, rather than CeO2 layers, in TiO2 pores and integrate the binary semiconductors; in fact, this method helps in generating trillions of nano heterojunctions (or interfaces) and band-edge modulation.16 The method has been strategized to facilitate the seeding and growth of CeQDs in the pores of TiO2 that renders a good heterostructure interface; this procedure also manifests the possibility of fine-tuning a range of photocatalysts that can help in tailoring the band edge positions of the semiconductors for photocatalytic activation of methane or any other molecule.17,18

CeQDs integrated in the TiO2 (CeT) nanopores were evaluated for partial oxidation of methane to value-added C-C coupled products by photocatalysis in visible light and direct sunlight. The photo-generated charge carriers are utilized to produce active species essential for the oxidation reaction to occur. The substantial in-situ generation of active oxygen species, such as superoxide (O2·-), hydroxyl radicals (·OH), by the photo-generated charge carriers propels the activation pathway. The present system thus meets the requirements to generate active oxidative species along with activating methane under ambient conditions under visible light. The proof of concept has been demonstrated and reported by our group wherein BiVO4 QDs were integrated into the pores of TiO2 for CO2 reduction to HCHO and MeOH, achieving high solar-to-fuel efficiency (30–38%).19

Results and discussion

CeQDs integrated TiO2 (CeT) photocatalyst device fabrication

SILAR method was employed to assemble the CeQDs into the pores of TiO2 (CeT) for the synthesis of the integrated binary semiconductor photocatalyst device system, and the synthesis steps are shown in Figure 1. SILAR is a multifaceted technique used for the versatile synthesis of thin films, especially for semiconductors and solar cell materials.12 The precursor taken for the cationic solution was Ce(NO3)3.6H2O, while NaOH was chosen as the anionic solution from amongst various other precursors. 40 mL of 25 mM solutions of each were taken in separate beakers and kept at room temperature. The as-prepared TiO2 films (by doctor blade method) were fitted to the automated SILAR instrument and subjected to the precursor solutions in consecutive cycles for 30 s each, followed by washing in deionized water to remove excess (loosely held or physisorbed) ions adsorbed to the surface of the film. This process is repeated for several cycles to populate a significant number of nanopores with ionic precursors of CeQDs throughout the entire film thickness, i.e., ∼10 microns to optimize maximum light penetration across the film thickness. The method facilitates the diffusion of the cation (Ce3+) and anion (OH−), leading to the gradual seeding toward the growth of CeQDs in the size range of TiO2 nanopores. One set of films was then dried in an oven at 333 K for 1 h, followed by calcination at 723 K for 4 h at a ramping rate of 5 deg/min. In a muffle furnace. It is noteworthy that the CeQDs are formed due to the calcination step; in fact, no ceria features were observed in XRD before calcination. On the other hand, another set of films was deliberately reduced under a 5% H2 atmosphere at 773 K for 1 h at a ramping rate of 5 deg/min. To induce the generation of oxygen vacancies (Ov). The CeQD size observed in both cases was nearly 2 nm, while there is an observable difference in the respective coloration, indicating the effect of calcination under two different atmospheres. While the air-calcined film exhibits a pale-yellow color, the reduced film exhibits a marginally greyish coloration (Figure 1). After optimizing the number of SILAR cycles to 10, it was observed that 1 cm2 of the area holds ∼1 ± 0.1 mg of the integrated binary semiconductor photocatalyst, i.e., CeO2/TiO2 (CeT). Upon careful analysis and calculations, obtained from the BET and EDAX analysis, it was observed that 1 cm2 of photocatalyst device holds ∼60 μg of CeQDs (6 wt%) integrated in ∼940 μg of TiO2. This value is an average arrived at from at least six different batches of CeT photocatalysts. Many characterization and photocatalytic reactions were also repeated at least three times with photocatalysts prepared from the same and different batches, and the results are reproducible within an error margin of 10%. For comparative studies, a CeO2 thin film was also synthesized directly on an FTO plate following the same SILAR method that led to the formation of bigger-sized CeO2 particles, and this is considered the bulk counterpart.

Unique architectural, optoelectronic, and photocatalytic activity features observed are attributed to the procedure adopted for photocatalyst device making, especially by the SILAR method for assembling constituents of CeQDs. Hence, the material properties arising from the interactions at the heterojunction interface have been carefully scrutinized. The effect of deliberate reduction of the CeT photocatalyst device under 5% H2 in Ar atmosphere on manipulating the oxygen vacancies and hence an increase in the ratio of Ce3+:Ce4+, which in turn populates Ce 4f1 level. The manipulation of the band gap energy by broadening the VB due to Ce 4f1, and thereby the effect on product selectivity, has been thoroughly investigated and elucidated.

Structure and morphology

The first structural dissection of the photocatalysts, namely pure TiO2, and CeT (both as prepared [or oxidized, due to final air calcination treatment) and H2 reduced], was carried out by XRD analysis, and the results are shown in Figure 2. As displayed in Figure 2A, there is no observable peak for CeO2 in the integrated CeT photocatalysts, but only the prominent peaks observed for TiO2. This could suggest the formation of small-sized [few nm (≤5 nm) regime] CeQDs and low CeO2 content. Even though no diffraction features of CeQDs are observed, the shift in peak positions of TiO2 suggests the same occurring after the introduction of CeQDs into the TiO2, which leads to disruption in the orderly arrangement of the unit cells of TiO2, inducing some lattice re-arrangement. The interaction between certain facets of CeQDs and TiO2 is reflected in the change in diffraction angle and the full width at half maximum (FWHM) of the CeT photocatalysts. It is to be underscored that no Ce ions are doped in TiO2; rather, CeQDs are integrated into the pores, establishing an intimate physical and chemical contact along the periphery of the latter with TiO2, and the same reflects in a change in the FWHM, which is to be noted; direct evidence of CeQDs will be discussed later from HRTEM and other analyses. The FWHM value for pristine TiO2 and CeT-OX is measured to be 0.40, while it changes to 0.38 for CeT-RED. The change in the TiO2 environment upon assembling the QDs leads to some structural rearrangement to accommodate the foreign particles. Nonetheless, the temperature treatment mainly influences the integration aspects. The shift in the peak positions of anatase TiO2 is observed in this modulation. The TiO2 (101) diffraction peak at 25.13° shifts to a lower angle of 25.04° and 24.88° (by 0.09° and 0.25°) for CeT-RED and CeT-OX, respectively. Similar shifts to lower diffraction angles are observed for peaks at 27.29° (for rutile TiO2 (101)), 37.70°, and 47.92° of TiO2. Further careful analysis of the shift observed between CeT-OX and CeT-RED, the latter (former) shows a marginal (large) shift to a lower angle compared to TiO2 features; this trend is observed with all features in Figure 2. Upon reduction, more oxygen vacancies and Ce3+ are created concurrently, and hence volume expansion is minimized to some extent, compared to CeT-OX (with maximum oxygen occupancy). This leads to a significantly lower shift in diffraction angle for CeT-RED compared to CeT-OX. These observations suggest an increase in the interplanar spacing, as well as the elongation of associated bonds, after CeQDs integration, irrespective of oxidation/reduction treatment. The shift in the diffraction patterns underscores the structural integration of CeQDs with TiO2.

The assessment of structural integration has been carried out by employing electron microscopy. As the integrated CeQDs are expected to be in the low-nm size regime, HRTEM was expected to provide direct evidence for the same. The QDs with an approximate size range of ∼2–3 nm appear strikingly formed (and calculated from statistical particle size distribution (histogram) shown in Figure S1), with a homogeneous distribution of both size and concentration. These images were obtained by drop-casting a minute amount of CeT photocatalyst material on a lacey-carbon grid for improved resolution and contrast. The images displayed in Figures 3 and 4 provide nanoscale views at different magnifications/resolutions. A close examination of the images reveals that the integrated particles do not aggregate even after prolonged calcination and sonication. While most of the QDs are embedded within the pores, some are partially embedded on the surface pores of TiO2. In such cases, a difference in their lattice fringes is observed. CeQDs that are inside the TiO2 pores, indicated by dashed yellow circles in Figures 3D, 3E, and 4D–4F do not display distinct lattice fringes; while most of them do not show clear lattice fringes, a few exhibit intersected/grid-like patterns, possibly due to pores that are close to the surface. However, a distinct change in contrast is observed that underscores the presence of QDs within the bulk of the TiO2 particles. These are clear indications of heterostructure formation that led to the formation of trillions of heterojunctions at the CeQDs-TiO2 interface. On the other hand, the QDs partially embedded into the TiO2 surface pores display clear lattice fringes (0.207 nm and 0.179 nm for (200) and (220) planes of CeT-OX and CeT-RED, respectively), distinguishable from that of the (101) plane of TiO2 (0.345 nm).20,21

The elemental mapping demonstrates a homogeneous distribution of CeQDs. A careful view shows that the particles are well separated from one another, as they are restricted by the thick walls of TiO2; while TiO2 shows bigger particles than CeQDs. Also, the CeQDs can be seen present over the entire area where TiO2 particles are available; in other words, no agglomerated CeO2 particles are observed anywhere, underscoring the efficient integration of CeQDs with TiO2. CeQDs that are partly embedded on the surface of TiO2 do not undergo agglomeration, testifying to the integrated nature of CeT.

Opto-electronic features

UV-visible spectra were recorded for CeT-OX and CeT-RED and compared with parent TiO2 materials; the results obtained are shown in Figure 5. It is known from literature reports that bulk CeO2 and Ce2O3 exhibit band gaps of ∼2.9 and 2.4–2.7 eV, respectively.14,22 CeT-OX demonstrates significantly improved absorption in the visible light wavelength range (compared to TiO2), and thus, the designed structure and the interface create heterojunctions that help harness more solar energy and charge separation/dispersion, respectively; this is in good correspondence with the pale yellow color observed with CeT-OX. In other words, more visible light photons can be absorbed from the same area as compared to pristine TiO2. TiO2 is a well-known semiconductor widely explored for photocatalytic reactions but under UV irradiation. The CeQD integration thus leads to a coherent interaction that enhances the absorption features of the photocatalyst device, making it suitable to be evaluated under direct sunlight.23 A distinct red-shift in the absorption spectrum can be observed after assembling the CeQDs into the micro and meso pores of TiO2. Furthermore, the structural integration of CeQDs in the micro and meso pores of TiO2 is substantiated by the optoelectronic property exhibited here by the photocatalyst device material. Collating the infamous Planck’s quantum theory with Einstein’s re-interpretation, it is well established that a particle’s band gap energy is inversely proportional to the absorption wavelength of light; in simple terms, the lower the absorption λ, the higher the bandgap. This theory can thus be applied to vividly illustrate the quantum dots formation of the integrated light absorbers. As the size of a particle is scaled down to only a few nanometers (or a few tens of unit cells), it makes the dispersed bands (CB and VB) lean or narrow in width; in other words, the band structure or band dispersion of the bulk material disappears and moves toward a single unit cell, which leads to an increase in the band gap. This is demonstrated by the illustration in Figure S2.

The factor γ is dependent on the nature of the electronic transition and is equal to ½ or 2 for direct and indirect bandgap transitions, respectively. Thus, the calculated band gaps for the CeT-RED and CeT-OX are 2.87 and 3.02 eV, respectively; these values are significantly higher than the bulk CeO2, displaying a band gap of ∼2.6 eV and Ce2O3 (2.4 eV). It is to be noted that perfect stoichiometric Ce2O3 formation requires a higher reduction temperature and longer reduction time, and the same is expected to change the phase and nature of TiO2. Also, reduction treatment was restricted, and CeT-RED is partially reduced. Hence, the band gap reduction in CeT-RED is due partly to reduction and partly to the QD size regime. These observations reinforce a facile and sturdy electronic interaction between the semiconductor materials. The narrowing of the band gap in the photocatalyst may be partly ascribed to the improved crystallinity of the CeQDs as compared to the bulk counterpart. In the case of reduction treatment, the generation of Ov leads to the formation of mid-gap states due to the formation of Ce3+. As the 4f1 of Ce3+ serves as the highest occupied orbital/band, an electronic transition occurs from occupied 4f1 to unoccupied 4f (not 5d), in addition to O 2p to 4f for the Ce4+ state.26 Some extent of Ti3+ mid-gap states and their influence cannot be ruled out in light absorption.

A primary limitation in photocatalysis is the inefficient utilization of photogenerated charge carriers, leading to significant photon energy loss as excited electrons recombine with holes and relax via radiative and non-radiative pathways. Establishing heterojunction interfaces can effectively mitigate this issue by enhancing exciton separation and utilization. The CeT photocatalysts demonstrate a substantial reduction in charge recombination rates with the incorporation of CeQDs into the TiO2 pores, as evident from photoluminescence (PL) results shown in Figure S3. Besides, the CeT photocatalyst material displays an extended emission at higher wavelengths (∼580 nm) due to the availability of mid-gap states (∼2.13 eV) as also inferred from the UV-Vis spectral analysis.15 A prolonged lifetime of the photogenerated charge carriers thus leads to concomitant charge transfer and utilization at the redox sites, improving the photocatalytic activity.

Efficient photon-induced charge separation pathway

An enhanced charge separation/transfer property of the integrated CeT system was investigated by photo-electrochemical (PEC) measurements, including chronoamperometric study and wavelength-dependent IPCE (incident photon-to-current efficiency) performance. Figure 6A displays the graphical illustration of the photo-response study carried out while chopping the light intermittently.27,28 The experiment was carried out under one-sun conditions at an applied potential of 1.23 V (vs. RHE) using Calomel as the reference electrode, graphite as a counter electrode, and 0.5 M Na2SO3 electrolyte. After stabilizing the electrodes under illumination, the readings were obtained at an interval of 20 s with consecutive light-on and light-off conditions. The plot shows an enhanced photoresponse for CeT-RED (CeT-OX) with a current density of nearly 34 (22) μA/cm2, significantly higher than that of TiO2 (6.2 μA/cm2). Critically, the potential gradient between CeQDs and TiO2 at the type-II heterojunction interface facilitates charge separation and their diffusion to the electrodes, underscoring efficient light absorption and charge separation. Reduced TiO2, as that of CeT-RED, shows a marginally enhanced current density compared to TiO2, shown in Figure 6A; while gray color appears for reduced TiO2 too, the integration with CeQDs (as in CeT) helps for better charge separation/transport toward high current density. Efficient utilization of the photons to generate energy through improved charge separation at the heterojunction is elucidated by the IPCE performance (Figure 6B). The wavelength-dependent IPCE experiment was carried out at 298 K without any applied potential. High to moderate IPCE performance is observed over the entire visible range for the integrated CeT photocatalyst devices. Pristine TiO2 exhibits nearly 5% efficiency at wavelengths ≤400 nm; however, IPCE increases up to 9-fold to 48% when integrated with CeQDs, specifically in the visible light range between 400 and 460 nm. Among CeT-OX and CeT-RED, the latter shows significantly higher performance due to efficient light absorption, which is in agreement with the light absorption results. Indeed, the IPCE performance of CeT-RED is twice that of CeT-OX. The inset image shows the cumulative current density obtained by integrating the IPCE results; an impressive conversion is seen for the integrated CeT. An observation worth underscoring is the second steep increase in the cumulative current density from ∼550 nm, which depicts an increase in the light absorption as well as charge separation. Indeed, an efficient absorption of longer-wavelength photons is observed around 600 nm, indicating possible mid-gap states in CeT-OX/RED materials, but with different population. Thus, the results corroborate that the smaller-sized CeQDs (with higher Eg) at lower wavelengths boost the photocurrent generation below 460 nm, thereby efficiently contributing to the charge carriers for the photo-driven activation of methane over the visible spectrum. It is also to be noted that no light absorption around 600 nm could be seen in the UV-Vis spectrum (Figure 5), hinting at the possible creation of new trap states under illumination, which are long-lived and hinder charge recombination. It is worth studying transient absorption to explore excited-state dynamics; however, this is beyond the scope of the current manuscript.

Spectroscopic insights

Raman spectral analysis was carried out for the photocatalyst materials, primarily to provide information typically for the change in the vibrational energy modes of TiO2 before and after the integration of the visible light-absorbing CeQDs. Very similar to XRD, the Raman peaks (Figure 7) did not exhibit any features for CeO2, likely due to its low content as well as small QDs.29 The peaks observed at 144, 198, 399, 515, and 640 cm−1 are typical of the anatase phase of TiO2.30,31,32 The red shift in the Eg band by 6.15 cm−1, after ceria integration in TiO2, directly suggests the electronic integration of Ce QDs with TiO2. An increase in the FWHM value to 16.7 from 15.6 in pristine TiO2 to the integrated photocatalyst also supports electronic integration due to QD nature. An increase in the FWHM value is also evident in structural disorderliness, reduced crystallite size, and internal strain arising from the CeQDs-TiO2 interactions.

To elaborate on the electronic integration of the visible-light-absorbing CeQDs, XPS analysis was conducted, and the results are shown in Figure 8 and Table S1. In Figure 8A, the deconvoluted spectra for Ce 3d are composed of multiplets of spin-orbit 3d5/2 and 3d3/2 doublet core levels. There exist five sets of sub-signals (v0, u0, v, u, v’, u’, v’’, u’’, v’’’, u’’’).33,34,35 The sub-signals (u0, v0, u’ and v’) result from configurational interaction arising from charge transfer between Ce and oxygen in the final state of photoelectron emission, relating to Ce3+. These peaks with the highest binding energies appearing at 904.2 ± 0.1 eV (u’) and 886 ± 0.1 eV (v’) indicate the 3d9 4f1 O2p6 final state of Ce, while the peaks (u, v) at 882.8 ± 0.1 eV and 901.4 ± 0.1 eV indicate the 3d9 4f2 O2p5 states in CeT-OX. The presence of 4f0 signals (fingerprint region for Ce4+) can typically be observed at 917.3 ± 0.2 eV.10,34,36,37 All the observed XPS signals later show a shift toward positive B.E upon the reduction treatment, i.e., in the CeT-RED photocatalyst device. Nonetheless, the Ce3+-O to Ce4+-O ratio observably increased from CeT-OX to CeT-RED, corroborating an increase in the oxygen vacancy sites induced by the reduction atmosphere. Further, the appearance of the O 1s core level (Figure 8B) signal corresponding to lattice oxygen at a higher binding energy, i.e., 530.3 ± 0.1 eV, supports the presence of Ce(III) and suggests the presence of O-Ti3+. The latter suggested bond can be corroborated by increased oxygen vacancy upon integration of CeQDs into TiO2 in CeT-OX as well as CeT-RED, whereby the Ce4+:Ce3+ ratio (v’:v0) decreased from 11.1 (CeT-OX) to 2.4 (CeT-RED) as calculated from the Ce 3d spectra.35,38

It is also interesting to note the change in Ti 2p spectra (Figure 8C) for CeT-OX and CeT-RED; the latter one shows extra broadening on the lower B.E side, while the former shows a relatively narrow Ti 2p feature. Deconvolution highlights an increase in Ti3+ content with CeT-RED very significantly, compared to CeT-OX. This particular observation underscores an extensive electronic integration between ceria and TiO2, and possibly this occurs along the heterojunctions, particularly at the periphery of ceria QDs and inner pore walls of TiO2. XPS results from all three core levels reiterate the presence of concomitant mixed valent states of Ti and Ce, along with oxygen vacancies in different quantities in both CeT-OX and CeT-RED.

X-ray VB (XVB) spectra were explored to further ascertain the changes observed in core levels.36 XVB spectra depicted in Figure 9 unfurl the band-edge positions of the pristine and integrated TiO2 photocatalyst materials.18,39 This significant change in the VB position at lower energy is mainly contributed by the Ti3+ (Ti 3d) state, while it also overlaps with a significant contribution arising from the Ce3+ (Ce 4f1). While Ce 4f1 is considered an impurity state due to the partial reduction of CeO2 to CeO2-x, the Ti3+ state leads to the energy overlap of Ti 3d1 with O 2p of TiO2, effectively leading to a further decrease in the bandgap of CeT-RED. XVB spectra provide direct evidence for the broadening of the VB of CeT. Table 1 summarizes the CB-VB positions of the photocatalyst materials after oxidation and reduction. Elucidation of the band edge positions provides a vivid understanding of the flow of photo-generated charge carriers and their consecutive utilization for the redox reactions to proceed. Clearly, a shift in the VB potentials toward lower energy is observed after the integration of CeQDs into the TiO2 nanopores. The CB edge or the CBMIN of the integrated system (−0.65 and −0.82 eV) lies at a higher negative potential than that of TiO2 (−0.24 eV). This facilitates better and more efficient injection of electrons from the CB of CeQDs to that of TiO2, making it the reduction site, while the holes are retained in the VB of the CeQDs that serve as the oxidation site. Thus, a type-II heterojunction between the two semiconductors formed, as inferred from the aforementioned observations. A doublet structure observed for the O 2p band in XVB for TiO2 changes to a single broad O 2p band after integration with CeQDs, also supports the integration of components of CeT-OX/RED.

Visible light-driven PCMO activity

Having demonstrated the containment of CeQDs in nanopores of TiO2 by various methods, with the possibility of tuning the oxygen vacancy or Ce 4f1 population by oxidation/reduction treatment, the photocatalyst was evaluated for C-H activation of methane to VAPs. The thin-film photocatalyst system offers an advantage over its powder counterpart catalyst by improving solar energy conversion.17,19,40,41 It offers a larger surface area for effective light illumination/absorption with a minimum amount of material, enhancing photoexcitation of charge carriers while significantly reducing recombination rates due to an abundant number of heterojunctions.42 These heterojunctions facilitate efficient electron transfer from the CB of CeO2 to TiO2, while holes are retained in CeO2. This spatial separation of charge carriers as well as active sites optimizes the activation and conversion of methane, enabling efficient photocatalytic performance.

CeT-OX was evaluated for PCMO (photocatalytic methane oxidation) as described in the experimental section. The C-C coupled product, namely, ethanol, was observed as the major product, along with the formation of minor yields of methanol and acetone after 3 h of illumination, and the results are shown in Figure 10 and Table 2. The products formed were confirmed by spiking experiments with standard samples, whereby a known amount (∼20 μL) of ethanol or acetone was added to the NMR samples to confirm the signals (see Figure S4). This generation of C2 and C2+ products under such ambient conditions makes the methodology more impressive. The product quantification was carried out using potassium hydrogen phthalate (KHP) as a standard with 1 mM concentration in the NMR analysis.12,35,43 Various control experiments were carried out to study the effectiveness of visible light, pristine catalysts (bulk CeO2 and TiO2; bulk TiO2 refers to pristine nanoporous TiO2), and other reaction parameters on the extent and nature of the reaction. As mentioned earlier, the amount of light-absorbing QDs in the binary system was optimized by evaluating different concentrations (5, 10, and 25 mM) and varying numbers of SILAR cycles (5, 10, 15, and 20). Finally, the optimized synthesis parameters, including a 25 mM concentration of the ionic precursors and 10 SILAR cycles, yielded the most efficient photocatalytic results. Further, reactions using bulk TiO2 and bulk CeO2 on an FTO plate exposed to the solar simulator were also carried out, where they failed to form ethanol or any C-C coupled VAPs, which confirms that the heterojunctions between CeO2 and TiO2 is critically important for the formation of VAPs. Reactions under dark conditions yield no products, confirming that the oxidation of methane to any VAP is predominantly a light-driven catalytic process.

As per the mechanistic understanding from previous reports, along with the availability of dissolved methane, the generation of active oxygen species during photo-illumination plays an important role in the production of partially oxidized VAPs.44,45,46 Thus, the optimal generation of superoxide, peroxide, and/or hydroxyl radicals enhances the product selectivity and activity. Two different experiments were carried out with CeT-OX to explore this aspect. First, a deliberate addition of 30% H2O2 (1 mL) to the solution (9 mL deionized water saturated with methane) was solar irradiated for 3 h. It is well-known that H2O2 generates peroxide radicals, which can further transform into hydroxyl species, enhancing the production of oxygenates. The post-reaction sample analysis (Figure 10C) revealed an enhancement in ethanol (acetone) yield to 3.39 (1.83) μmol/cm2 in line with the notion above; deliberate peroxide addition helps for a larger amount of acetone, which may be noted, and this aspect may be explored further. In another experiment, the introduction of an electron scavenger was intended to capture the photoexcited electrons to limit the reduction of water to generate active oxygen species and, thus, retard the activity/product formation. For this, 1 mM FeCl3 was added deliberately to the CH4-saturated solution and irradiated for 3 h. Negligible product formation was observed, indicating two valuable pieces of information: (1) the absence of photoexcited electrons (due to their scavenging by Fe3+) retards the photocatalytic activity; (2) without Fe3+, photoexcited electrons were utilized for the formation of active oxygen species, such as superoxide radicals, which can transform to generate OH radicals to yield the partially oxidized products. EPR studies were carried out with a CH4-saturated solution containing the CeT device in the custom-made quartz reactor, along with DMPO, indicating the formation of trapped radicals, as shown in Figure S5, substantiating the proposed pathway. First, a spectrum of the aliquot was recorded before one-sun illumination, and no EPR signal for radicals was detected, except DMPOX. Then it was irradiated under a Xe lamp for 15 min, and the collected aliquot was measured for trapped radicals by EPR. Signals for trapped methyl and hydroxyl radicals were observed, and they are assigned on the spectra; due to the limited amount of CeT material (1 mg), the amount of radical generation is also limited. With the addition of H2O2, a hydroxyl signal was detected; it is to be noted that, compared to actual reaction conditions with peroxide, a minute amount (1/100) of peroxide was used for EPR measurements to avoid broadening/overlap with other species. Some broadening observed without peroxide is attributed to larger modulation factor and some unresolved hyperfine coupling. Nevertheless, the pattern observed with and without peroxide is similar, underscoring the formation of methyl and hydroxyl radical species.

The effect of the addition of a co-catalyst and its influence on the activity was carried out with CeT-OX under one-sun conditions, and the improvement in the utilization of photogenerated electrons, which was explored next. For this, the Pd nanocubes (PdNC) were coated along the edges of the fabricated photocatalyst on two opposite sides.42,47 This system is denoted as PdNC-CeT-OX. The approximate PdNC amount coated on all sides together is ∼20 ± 5 μg. PdNC -CeT-OX was evaluated for PCMO as that of CeT-OX. The yield further improved the generation of coupled products. Table 2 summarizes the yield of coupled products observed under different experimental conditions with the CeT photocatalyst.

The methane activation using the photocatalyst device was also conducted under direct sunlight for comparative analysis and to assess commercial viability. The average temperature during the sunlight exposure was 41 ± 5°C, with a solar power density of around 65 mW/cm2. The yield is higher under direct sunlight than simulated conditions. Under direct sunlight, the experimental temperature rose to 40°C–50°C range, while the simulated “one sun” condition was conducted in an air-conditioned laboratory at ∼25°C. Despite this, a notable yield was achieved in direct sunlight, demonstrating the potential for further improvement through advanced engineering on a larger scale, especially with significantly higher methane partial pressure in the dissolved state in a closed reactor and recycling the reaction solution with gas-phase CH4.

Curiously, acetone was also produced as a minor/secondary product with CeT; however, acetone concentration increases 15 times to 1.83 μmol/cm2 after 3 h of irradiation, in the presence of H2O2 (compared to without H2O2), underscoring the influence of hydroxyl radicals with enhanced oxidation. This is advantageous for making the C3 product, which was not possible with the CeVO4-TiO2 system;12 indeed, the rational approach to make a longer chain with/without chain branching is required. Based on the above experimental fact, we speculate a few possibilities, such as (a) ethanol/ethoxy species reacting with methyl/methoxy/methanol, (b) formation of CO (from methoxy due to high oxidation), and interacting with methyl radicals; however, they need in situ experimental verification by methods such as IR spectroscopy and isotopic labeling methods.

The aforementioned experiments identify the key factors that determine the efficiency of photocatalytic methane conversion to platform chemicals under one-sun conditions. The approach of incorporating a visible-light absorbing semiconductor in the nanopores of TiO2 redefines the path of exciton flow and utilization for redox reactions. Rational material design and establishing suitable operational conditions are key to developing a sustainable photocatalyst device. Although significant activity was achieved, the yield obtained was low. Thus, the devised photocatalyst was further fine-tuned to create defect sites with H2-reduction that can act as active sites for the activation and conversion of methane under ambient conditions. The activity aligned with expectations, as it increased ethanol production more than five times, with a yield of ∼11.2 μmol/cm2 in 3 h under one-sun conditions and ∼15 μmol/cm2 under direct sunlight for CeT-RED.

The reported ethanol yield is per cm2 of the photocatalyst device, which contains 1 ± 0.1 mg of the CeT photocatalyst. To facilitate comparisons with other photocatalyst systems reported in the literature, the catalyst amount was normalized to 1 g (with 60 mg CeQDs in 940 mg TiO2 as CeT-RED spread over 1000 cm2), and the yield obtained for CeT-RED changed from 3.73 (5) μmol/mgCeT·h to 3.73 (5) mmol/gCeT·h under one sun (direct sunlight) conditions, assuming that the activity increases linearly with area. Furthermore, all studies in photocatalysis report weight-normalized activity based on the active visible-light-absorbing material, which is CeQDs in the present case. Hence, scaling the photocatalyst device further to an area of ∼1.7 m2 (with 1 g CeQDs in 15.7 g TiO2) is expected to increase the ethanol yield to 62.2 (83.3) mmol/gCeQDs·h under one sun conditions (direct sunlight), which far exceeds the performance of top literature reports (Table 3). It is worth noting that a larger area device evaluation needs to be carried out, and engineering problems (such as mass transfer) need to be tackled. Additionally, literature reports indicate that simple weight normalization was applied to the results without conducting experiments on a 1 g scale.

A major limitation of methane partial oxidation remains the possible uncontrolled over-oxidation of the desired oxygenates to undesirable CO2. Thus, the gas samples were collected after the completion of the reaction duration and analyzed by GC equipped with a TCD detector, Carbosieve S II column, and He as carrier gas. It is likely that the C-C coupled products formed are stable under the experimental conditions.

Mechanism

The remarkable PCMO activity exhibited by the CeT-OX and CeT-RED photocatalyst devices leading to the formation of C-C coupled products can be attributed to the carefully structured heterojunctions between the CeQDs and TiO2 by the SILAR method that, as derived from the spectroscopy studies, helps in the reconstruction of the band edge positions of the binary photocatalyst. This, in turn, renders a better flow of the photo-generated charge carriers, especially the photoexcited electrons from CeQDs to TiO2 CB, lowering the C-H activation barrier for methane and selective production of coupled products. Addition of sacrificial Fe3+ (to Fe2+) fully stops methane activation, highlighting the consumption of photoexcited electrons in the CB of CeQDs and confirming the electron-transfer pathway and consequent hydroxyl radical formation. Retaining all the holes in CeQDs enhances the availability of minority charge carriers and helps for water activation. The formation and availability of a range of CeQDs with different sizes provide a potential gradient across the interface of the binary-semiconductor device. This is evident from the IPCE plot, which suggests that the smallest QDs propel the photocurrent generation at lower wavelengths, with accelerated values with violet-indigo photons. Better charge separation and consecutive utilization for methane activation are thus inferred. Further, as displayed in Figure 11 the CeT photocatalyst material exhibits a type-II heterojunctions. Thereby, upon visible light irradiation, the CeQDs get excited, generating electron-hole pairs. According to the charge flow mechanism, the excited electrons from the CB of CeQDs are transferred to the CB of TiO2, which are later consumed to produce the active oxygen-containing radicals.14 On the other hand, the holes present in the VB of CeQDs are utilized to oxidize water, generating hydroxyl radicals. These OH radicals activate the C-H of CH4 to generate methyl radicals, which is likely to be the rate-determining step.51 Although no oxygen is deliberately added in the reaction solution, in-situ generation of oxygen from water splitting cannot be ruled out, as the VB potential of CeQDs and TiO2 favor the same. The availability of active sites in near proximity enables the coupling of methyl radicals and forms ethanol after combining with hydroxyl species. The deliberate addition of an oxidizing species, like H2O2, can provide surplus active oxygen species, which increases the PCMO by almost doubling the ethanol yield while driving the coupling reaction further to improve acetone formation. Thus, the active oxygen species play an important role in deciding the selectivity of the coupled product from PCMO.

Conclusions

The appealing synthetic artifacts of integrating CeQDs into the micro and mesopores of TiO2 act as a stimulus for band structure alignments with concomitant generation of abundant heterojunctions. This enables plenty of active sites that are capable of absorbing photons in the visible light range to produce photoexcited charge carriers responsible for the redox reaction. The transfer/pathway of photogenerated charge carriers, especially electrons, plays a pivotal role in lowering the activation barrier of methane, thereby rendering photocatalysis advantageous over thermo-catalysis and/or electrochemical methods. The generation of active radical species, such as hydroxyl radicals and superoxide radicals, initiates the activation of methane. An impressive yield of ethanol achieved with the present metal oxide-based semiconductor materials can be claimed to make its mark amongst the best-performing photocatalytic systems for selective methane oxidation and set a new benchmark. The novelty in the synthetic method to integrate QDs into the pores of TiO2 enables lower recombination of photogenerated charge carriers, modulates the band alignment, generates active species, and finally improves selectivity toward an upgraded fuel, ethanol, simultaneously. Further, the present work offers a more innovative, green, and feasible methodology for the formation of homogeneously distributed, smaller-sized (2–4 nm) simple metal oxide CeO2 QDs assembled from the controlled transformation and nucleation of CeO2 from its hydroxyl precursor, by following a bottom-up approach; this is in contrast to our earlier work on the CeVO4 -TiO2 photocatalyst.12 Nonetheless, the present work demonstrates the effect of reduction treatment on the nature of active sites and Ov concentration attributed to the feasible redox nature of cerium, which can tune both activity and selectivity of methane photo-oxidation beyond C2 product, i.e., formation of acetone. These aspects are summarized in Figure 12. The reported system is indeed superior to the benchmark catalysts and thus opens up a vast horizon for the innovation, incorporation, and investigation of photocatalytic systems in a custom-built reactor (Figure S7) set-up. The present work also provides further scope for improvement in AQE, TOF, and TON, which remain below the bar for the majority of photocatalysts. It can be envisioned that the intellectual combination/selection of semiconductor materials with strategic engineering designs can provide a recipe for further scaling up productivity and selectivity.

Limitations of the study

Some dynamic methane desorption from the dissolved state into the gaseous state occurs under irradiation conditions, due to a slow increase in temperature, which limits the reaction time. Volatility of the products, especially under illumination and direct sunlight conditions, introduces possible loss of some products while performing the reaction as well as product(s) collection for quantitative analysis.

Method details

The CeT integrated photocatalyst was thoroughly characterized employing spectral and microscopic analytical tools. A Shimadzu spectrophotometer (model-UV2550) was employed to record diffuse reflectance UV–Vis measurement and converted into absorbance. Powder XRD data were collected using a Pan Analytic X’pert pro dual goniometer diffractometer using CuKα (1.5418 Å) radiation with a Ni-filter. Microstructural analysis was carried out with a JEOL JEM F-200 HRTEM operating at 200 kV and FEI NOVA NANOSEM 450 system equipped with energy-dispersive X-ray (EDX) spectroscopy. N2 adsorption−desorption isotherm measurements, for obtaining the textural properties, were carried out at 77 K using a Quantachrome Quadrasorb gas sorption system. BET and BJH models were employed to get the surface area and pore size distribution. Electronic structure was studied with X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) (Thermo Fisher), equipped with an Al Kα (monochromatic) X-ray radiation source operating at 6 mA beam current and 12 kV. A TechnoS IndiRAM CTR 500C Micro Raman spectrometer was used to obtain the Raman spectra with an excitation laser wavelength of 532 nm. Photoluminescence (PL) measurements were conducted using a Horiba FluoroMax Plus spectrophotometer with an excitation and emission slit width of 3 with 1220 grating density. Electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) spectra were measured by a Bruker A300 X-band spectrometer. The data was collected at a scanning power, 0.998 mW at 298 K. Electrochemical measurements herein have been carried out with a Gamry 3000 potentiostat. IPCE measurements were performed in a Newport IPCE system (UUX 1404565).

Calculation method for number of heterojunctions: Semi-quantitative method

Enhancement in photocatalytic activity of CeT can be attributed to the synthetic scaffolds of the SILAR method, propelling the formation of a structured heterojunction between CeQDs and TiO2. A quasi-quantitative look at the number of heterojunctions has been calculated based on TEM and BET analyses along with some valid assumptions. Prior art suggests it can be safely assumed that TiO2 has two major pore sizes, i.e., with diameters 2.5 nm and 4 nm, wherein the CeQDs are assembled.12,18,19 This is mainly to simplify the calculations, as the exact quantitative calculation of filled nanopores by CeQDs is difficult; nonetheless, we expect this representative calculation to be closer to the actual number of filled pores within an error margin of 10–20%. Next, assuming that 60 μg CeQDs are accommodated in 1:2 ratio (20:40 μg) in the 2.5 and 4 nm mesopores, respectively, of TiO2. The number of unit cells of CeO2 required to fill the 2.5 and 4 nm mesopores is calculated. This is followed by the number of mesopores required to accommodate 20 and 40 μg in 2.5 and 4 nm pores, respectively. Indeed, it is possible that different weight ratios of CeQDs could be accommodated in different-sized pores of TiO2; however, the above calculation is mainly to provide an idea of calculation of number of heterojunctions and the numbers associated with.

The volume (V = 4/3 πr3) of one 4 nm spherical pore is calculated to be 33.51 nm3. One CeO2 (fluorite) unit cell occupies a volume of 0.16 nm3 (a = b = c = 0.541 nm; volume = a3). So, the number of CeO2 unit cells that can occupy a 4 nm pore is ∼209. One unit cell of CeO2 contains 4 molecules and thus, 836 molecules are present in one 4 nm mesopore. Similarly, it is calculated for 2.5 nm pore diameter. 1 mol of CeO2 (6.023 × 1023 molecules) weighs 172.115 g, and hence, 836 molecules to weigh 23.88 × 10−20 g. It is assumed that a single TiO2 mesopore is integrated with one CeQD and, consequently, one heterojunction forms; there is a possibility that some pores might be filled partially and still it gives a heterojunction.

Similarly, no. of heterojunctions by 2.5 nm pore= (20 × 10−6)/16 × 10−20 = 125 × 1012

This suggests, that ∼167 (125) trillion heterojunctions are formed by filling and integration 40 (20) μg of CeQDS in 4 (2.5) nm mesopores of TiO2. This implies that 1 mg of CeT photocatalyst in 1 cm2 area with 10 μm thickness could form nearly 292 trillion (∼0.3 quadrillion) heterojunctions. Similar calculations were extended for other systems in the manuscript. A generous 20% error is considered, due to the assumptions made above and hence the numbers reported are approximate. Thus, the calculation suggests a vast scope for improving and exploiting the photon conversion efficiency by tactfully creating a heterojunction between a transition metal-based oxide and a semiconductor material, put together in the form of an artificial leaf device. The method explained above is a generic method and can be extended to any set of pore sizes and for any material combinations.

Photocatalysis measurements and solar-to-fuel efficiency (STFE)

The photocatalytic methane oxidation reaction was carried out with the fabricated photocatalyst coated as a thin film over 1 × 1 cm2 on the conductive side of an FTO plate.
