Plug-and-play computational method for advancing natural and biomedical image representation
Authors: Haifan Gong, Tianyu Han, Guanbin Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Visual information is central to human perception, and how we represent images critically shapes downstream analysis. While recent years have witnessed remarkable advances in deep learning for image processing, Valous et al. now introduce a non-data-driven framework grounded in hypercomplex algebras for natural and biomedical image processing. This plug-and-play approach operates without training data and shows its effect across tasks such as re-colorization, de-colorization, contrast enhancement, re-staining, and integration into machine-learning pipelines.

Main text

Visual information ranks among the most vital resources for human perception and fuels an ongoing quest for superior representation in image creation and analysis.1 A crisp photograph heightens engagement, and a high-contrast, low-noise computed tomography (CT) scan helps clinicians reduce diagnostic errors. Even state-of-the-art learning frameworks such as the nnU-Net pipeline,2 which relies on carefully tailored preprocessing steps like Z score normalization, achieve substantial gains from clearer, more consistent visual inputs.

In recent years, deep-learning-based techniques have greatly improved image quality, including super-resolution, contrast adjustment, and de-colorization.3 However, these methods often require extensive task-specific training datasets and depend on graphics processing unit (GPU) acceleration for training and inference. Such dependencies underscore the enduring potential of mathematically grounded image-processing approaches, which can provide efficient, data-agnostic solutions without the overhead of specialized hardware or curated data.

Recently, Valous et al.4 proposed a plug-and-play workflow for natural and biomedical image processing based on hypercomplex algebras (Figure 1). Hypercomplex-algebra-based image processing offers a unified paradigm that weaves together algebraic elegance and geometric insight to tackle the rich complexity of color imagery. Four-dimensional numbers are ideally suited to encode the three-color channels by harnessing quaternions. Using the two-dimensional orthogonal planes split framework,5 this approach treats each pixel as a hypercomplex entity. The result is a suite of versatile workflows that span natural image re-colorization and de-colorization, contrast enhancement, computational re-staining and stain separation in histological slides, and even accelerated learning in deep-network pipelines for digital pathology. In natural photography, these quaternion-based methods unlock alternative color renditions and robust grayscale conversions, permitting artists and vision-system designers to tailor color appearance with unprecedented control.4 Meanwhile, in the biomedical realm, the workflows excel at isolating and enhancing biomarker signals: from separating overlapping histological stains to producing color-blind-friendly visualizations that preserve diagnostic fidelity.

Remarkably, these advances rely solely on elementary arithmetic and matrix operations, sidestepping the data-hungry training regimes of many modern algorithms. Despite their non-data-driven nature, the proposed techniques match or surpass established benchmarks, underscoring the power of well-founded mathematical frameworks. This also hints at a new frontier where hypercomplex analysis becomes an indispensable tool for natural-image enhancement and cutting-edge biomedical imaging.

One of the most significant contributions of this work is its applicability to machine-learning and deep-learning pipelines. The authors show that applying the hypercomplex workflow as a preprocessing step can substantially improve ResNet-1016 sensitivity from 94.1% to 96.6% on an image-classification task. Meanwhile, its computational complexity remains low enough to allow near-real-time processing of images containing up to one million pixels. This efficiency suggests potential for further improving the performance of recent foundation models across both natural and biomedical domains.

Despite the above advantages, several limitations call for further improvement. Parameter selection currently requires choosing among 91 quaternion combinations; although the authors have validated some patterns, these may not be optimal for real-world scenarios and introduce a potential tuning burden. Processing gigapixel pathology images remains computationally challenging, with runtime scaling roughly linearly with image size; a CUDA-based implementation or other hardware acceleration could help mitigate this bottleneck. Although the method achieves a 90.8% structural similarity index measure (SSIM) score on de-colorization, it struggles with stain separation when colors have visually similar hues (e.g., red and pink). In many practical settings, data-driven approaches outperform this method, suggesting that further hybridization between this framework and up-to-date learning-based pipelines may be necessary to achieve state-of-the-art results.

There are several specific approaches to develop this work further. The most intriguing possibility is extending the proposed framework to popular natural and medical foundation models, such as the Segment Anything Model (SAM)7 and its medical counterpart, MedSAM.8 One strategy is to apply the hypercomplex algebra workflow at inference time. In real-world settings, images for inference often come from different centers and imaging devices. Normalizing these inputs with the hypercomplex workflow can help mitigate potential domain gaps.9 Another strategy is to use the workflow to preprocess images before training. In that scenario, each image undergoes the quaternion split during the data preparation phase to test whether the resulting representations improve foundation model performance. While full retraining of large models can be resource intensive, this preprocessing step remains feasible for smaller datasets on tasks like segmentation, classification, or landmark detection.10

Another promising direction is to validate the clinical benefits of this methodology. Computational re-staining and stain separation could become powerful tools for pathological data analysis. These techniques may address long-standing challenges such as inferring immunological information from standard H&E slides without additional immunohistochemical staining.11 Achieving these advances will require close collaboration between engineers and clinicians. Specifically, pathologists trained on established, standardized protocols will need substantial retraining to integrate these analyses into their workflow. Securing regulatory approval for these systems could take several years. And since no user studies with practicing clinicians have been conducted, questions about real-world usability and diagnostic impact remain challenging.

Generally, enhanced visual representations empower a broad spectrum of applications:(1)In biomedical imaging, they sharpen the contrast of subtle tissue structures and isolate overlapping stains to improve diagnostic accuracy and facilitate automated analysis.(2)In natural photography and computer vision, they enable more faithful color reproduction, stylistic re-colorizations, and adaptive grayscale conversions that bolster both aesthetic appeal and scene understanding.(3)For accessibility, they deliver color-blind-friendly renditions that preserve critical information.(4)And across all domains, they provide richer, more discriminative inputs for machine-learning and deep-learning pipelines, ultimately driving more reliable feature extraction, faster model convergence, and better overall performance in tasks ranging from image salient-object detection12 to quantitative biomarker assessment.While efforts to elevate visual representation continue, the authors introduce a robust, plug-and-play workflow grounded in hypercomplex analysis and quaternion algebra, enabling seamless color re-rendering, contrast enhancement, and stain separation via simple arithmetic and matrix operations. By open-sourcing the complete implementation, the authors invite AI researchers, clinicians, and mathematicians to adopt and extend the framework, catalyzing rapid interdisciplinary innovation. Such collaboration promises refined diagnostic imaging, accessible color-blind-friendly renditions, and improved feature extraction in automated vision systems. This work also paves the way for richer, more informative visual data across biomedical and natural-image applications.
