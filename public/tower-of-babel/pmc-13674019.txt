Central Message

A low-level aortotomy for self-expanding TAVR explantation improves the ability to remove the degenerated valve and facilitates surgical replacement.

Transcatheter heart valve (THV) explantation is increasingly relevant as indications for transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) expand to patients who will likely outlive their prostheses and require reintervention.1 Redo TAVR is not feasible for all patients, and explantation may be required in the lifetime management of aortic stenosis.

Published TAVR explant techniques tailor aortotomy location on the basis of the THV platform and prosthesis frame height. Balloon-expandable valves (BEVs) can often be approached through a standard low aortotomy, whereas self-expanding valves (SEVs) are commonly performed above the upper edge of the THV frame.2,3

On the basis of our high-volume center's experience, we opt for a lower aortotomy than previously described in the literature for self-expanding valves, performing our incision at the location of the aortotomy for native surgical aortic valve replacement (SAVR). Our approach prioritizes annular exposure for device removal and easier subsequent valve replacement. Institutional review board approval and consent were not required.

Surgical Technique

Preoperative Planning

We review the preoperative computed tomography angiography to define annular and aortic anatomy, as well as the relation between the THV and cardiac structures. Further, we plan cannulation and aortic crossclamp placement on the basis of the computed tomography angiography findings.

Bypass, Cardioplegia, and Aortotomy

After central cannulation and standard cardiopulmonary bypass, we palpate or use epiaortic ultrasound to identify and mark the valve frame height and planned aortotomy. After cardioplegic arrest and venting, the THV frame is fully defined. We prepare a lower aortotomy near the sinotubular junction through a transverse incision. Self-Expanding Valves

We use a Freer elevator or a scalpel to separate the neo-endothelialized frame from the aorta, with dissection for areas of dense incorporation (Figure 1, B). We dissect circumferentially until the crown of the SEV can be delivered through the low aortotomy without injuring the aorta or surrounding tissue (Figure 1, C). In our experience, the upper portion of the SEV frame can consistently be freed and delivered through this exposure, without the need for upward extension onto the ascending aorta, although superior extension can be performed if necessary. To improve annular exposure for explant of the bottom THV, the incision is typically carried down into the noncoronary sinus or the left/noncoronary commissure, which also allows root enlargement if necessary after SEV explant.

We irrigate cold saline over the exposed nitinol frame of the SEV, reducing the nitinol's radial force and improving frame mobility (Video 1). If the frame is resistant to delivery, we use a lasso technique whereby we pass silk sutures through the SEV frame cells at 120° intervals, then thread each suture through a chest tube (Figure 1, D). These sutures are then pulled as the chest tube slides over the THV, enabling easy delivery of the degenerated device through the low aortotomy while preserving the aorta, aortomitral curtain, and leaflets for a safe SAVR (Figure 1, E and F).

Postvalve Removal

We inspect the annulus, aortic wall, and aortomitral curtain, then perform SAVR and concomitant procedures when necessary. We close the aortotomy directly with sutures when the aortic wall is healthy and the closure is tension-free; otherwise, we perform patch reconstruction.

Discussion

TAVR explant is among the fastest-growing categories of cardiac surgical procedures in the United States, and its volume is expected to increase as TAVR indications expand to younger, lower-risk patients who may outlive their prostheses.1 The choice between redo TAVR and explant depends on individual anatomy, physiologic reserve, and clinical trajectory, and for many patients, particularly those whose anatomy precludes redo TAVR, explant is a necessary component of lifelong management. Explant carries greater operative risk than native SAVR, and refining its technique remains important to improving patient safety.

The high-aortotomy approach is anatomically constrained by the SEV's design. Given that the SEV frame extends into the ascending aorta, the conventional approach places a transverse aortotomy high on the ascending aorta, above the upper edge of the frame. The neoendothelialized frame is then mobilized and delivered superiorly through incorporated tissue, and the annular complex is visualized through a high exposure for the subsequent valve replacement. We instead begin the aortotomy at the native SAVR level, leaving the ascending aorta intact and extending the incision obliquely into the noncoronary sinus or the left/noncoronary commissure when additional exposure or root enlargement is required. This delivers the frame inferiorly and provides direct annular exposure for valve replacement.

Published series using the conventional high aortotomy have reported substantial rates of concomitant aortic root replacement during SEV explant.3 In the EXPLANT-TAVR registry, root replacement was required more frequently after SEV than BEV (15.3% vs 7.4% P = .016), which the investigators attributed to the taller SEV frame and the higher aortotomy used to extract it.4 In the Society of Thoracic Surgeons database, ascending aortic replacement was more frequent with SEV than BEV explant (22% vs 9%; P < .001), whereas root replacement did not differ.5 By incising at the native SAVR level rather than high on the ascending aorta, the frame is delivered inferiorly without dissection along the ascending aorta, minimizing shear injury to the aorta and preserving annular exposure for SAVR.

This report describes a single-center experience and is subject to the limitations of that design. The described outcomes reflect the experience of a high-volume center and experienced operators. Comparative study will be required to determine whether a SAVR-level aortotomy affects rates of aortic root replacement or mortality relative to the conventional approach.

Comments

A SAVR-level aortotomy is a reproducible technique for SEV extraction and subsequent surgical valve replacement. As TAVR use expands, refined explant techniques will remain relevant to the lifelong management of valvular heart disease.
