RGS20 reduces glioma stemness and temozolomide resistance by intrinsically inhibiting the WNT/β-catenin signaling pathway
Authors: Yang Xie, Qi Li, Yecheng Ma, Yongchang Yang, Xingyu Jin, Tailong Yi, Xianglian Ge, Xing Xu, Xuejun Yang, Qiang Huang, Yalin Lu, Peng Li, Wenliang Li, Xiaoguang Wang, Yingzhe Piao, Xun Jin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
G protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs) play a pivotal role in maintaining the stemness of both normal and cancer stem cells. However, the function of the regulator of G protein signaling (RGS) family, particularly in tumor stem cells, remains poorly under-stood. Through bioinformatics analysis of clinical data, we identified RGS20 as a potential regulator of glioma stemness and temozolomide (TMZ) resistance, which may significantly influence patient prognosis. Subsequent in vitro and in vivo experiments demonstrated that RGS20 inhibition markedly enhanced tumor sphere formation and upregulated stem cell markers by intrinsically activating the WNT/β-catenin signaling pathway, thereby promoting tumorigenesis and ultimately leading to TMZ resistance. Furthermore, in human glioblastoma specimens, β-catenin signaling associated with low RGS20 expression was significantly enriched in hypoxic regions, suggesting that this mechanism may support the maintenance of glioma stem cells (GSCs) and drive TMZ resistance within the hypoxic niche. Our findings reveal that low RGS20 expression sustains WNT/β-catenin signaling in a ligand-reduced manner within hypoxic niches, unveiling a novel intracellular mechanism that drives glioma progression. Targeting this mechanism could provide new therapeutic strategies for glioma treatment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-30291-z.

Introduction

Glioblastoma is the most aggressive tumor in human central nervous system, and the clinical efficacy of current treatment strategies (including surgical resection, radiotherapy, and chemotherapy) remains unsatisfactory, with a five-year recurrence rate exceeding 90%1–3. One of the primary factors contributing to this high recurrence rate is the presence of glioblastoma stem cells (GSCs). GSCs are a subpopulation of tumor cells within glioblastoma that not only exhibit self-renewal and multipotent differentiation abilities similar to normal neural stem cells but also possess strong tumor initiating ability4. Moreover, GSCs display remarkable resistance to radiotherapy and chemotherapy due to their enhanced DNA damage repair capacity and robust anti-apoptotic mechanisms5,6. Spatially, GSCs are preferentially localized in hypoxic and perivascular regions7. Therefore, investigating their distribution characteristics and underlying molecular mechanisms is critical for developing effective therapeutic strategies targeting GSCs.

In fact, neural stem cells (NSCs) are predominantly located in subventricular zone (SVZ) and subgranular zone (SGZ)8. Within these specialized regions, multiple signaling pathways, including WNT, Hedgehog, TGF-β, and Notch, are involved in maintaining the stemness of NSCs9–12. Notably, many of these signaling pathways can be categorized under the G protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs) family13,14, which also plays a role in maintenance of tumor stem cells and therapy resistance15,16. GPCRs, characterized by their seven-transmembrane structure, transduce extracellular signals into intracellular responses by activating G proteins. This process involves the exchange of GDP for GTP in the Gα subunit, initiating downstream signaling cascades17. Interestingly, GPCR activity can be regulated not only through receptor-ligand interactions but also via intracellular modulation by the Regulator of G-protein Signaling family. The RGS family comprises over 20 members, each containing a structural domain known as the RGS box. This domain binds to the Gα subunit through a conserved asparagine/serine residue, functionally regulating the activity of GTPase-activating proteins. This accelerates GTP hydrolysis, ultimately inactivating the G protein18,19. Thus, the RGS family may serve as an alternative to extracellular ligands in regulating GPCR activity, potentially enabling tumor stem cells to adapt to specific microenvironments, such as hypoxic and perivascular niches.

Consistent with the inhibitory function of RGS family on G proteins, the RGS family canonically exhibits suppressive effects on various types of tumors. For instance, in liver cancer, RGS5 curtails proliferation and invasion by repressing the WNT/β-catenin pathway20. Similarly, in non-small cell lung cancer, RGS6 suppresses epithelial-mesenchymal transition via TGF-β signaling21. However, RGS proteins have also been found to promote tumor progression in noncanonical ways. For example, mutated RGS7 enhances tumor aggressiveness in melanoma22, while RGS19 drives malignancy in hepatocellular carcinoma via a MYH9/β-catenin/c-Myc positive feedback loop23. Despite these insights, research on the role of the RGS family in glioma stemness and resistance to therapy is currently limited. Further comprehensive studies are needed to fully understand the impact of the RGS family on glioma progression.

Materials and methods

Establishment of patient-derived glioma cells

Primary glioma cells were isolated from fresh tumor tissue samples obtained from patients undergoing surgical resection, with written informed consent and approval from the Institutional Ethics Committee. The tumor tissue was washed in cold DPBS containing 2% penicillin-streptomycin, mechanically dissociated using sterile scalpels, and enzymatically digested with Accutase (Sigma-Aldrich) at 37 °C for 20–30 min. The cell suspension was filtered through a 70 μm cell strainer and centrifuged at 800 ×g for 5 min. The pellet was resuspended in DMEM/F-12 medium supplemented with B27 (1×), EGF (20 ng/mL), FGF (20 ng/mL), penicillin (100 U/mL), and streptomycin (100 µg/mL). Cells were seeded in culture flasks pre-coated with poly-L-lysine and maintained at 37 °C in a humidified 5% CO₂ incubator. Medium was changed every 3–4 days, and cells were passaged at 80–90% confluence using Accutase. Primary glioma stem cell (GSC) enrichment was confirmed via tumorsphere formation under serum-free conditions and expression of stemness markers (e.g., CD15, KLF4) using qPCR.

Cell lines and cell culture

The human glioma cell lines A1207 and A172 were obtained from the Cancer Institute & Hospital of Tianjin Medical University. Cells were maintained in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco, USA) at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO₂. For the TMZ treatment, cells were cultured under FBS-containing conditions. For tumorsphere culture, cells were grown in serum-free stem cell medium consisting of DMEM/F12 (Gibco, USA) supplemented with 20 ng/mL epidermal growth factor (EGF; Invitrogen, USA), 20 ng/mL basic fibroblast growth factor (bFGF; Invitrogen, USA), and 1× B27 supplement (Invitrogen, USA).

Cell culture under hypoxic conditions

To simulate the hypoxic tumor microenvironment in vitro, cells were cultured under controlled hypoxic conditions. Briefly, cells were seeded at an appropriate density and allowed to adhere overnight under normoxic conditions (5% CO₂, 95% air, 37 °C). The culture medium was then replaced with fresh pre-warmed medium, and the cells were transferred to a tri-gas hypoxia workstation (Thermo Scientific Forma Series II Water Jacketed CO₂ Incubator) set to 1% O₂, 5% CO₂, and 94% N₂ at 37 °C. The cells were maintained under hypoxic conditions for the designated treatment periods, with all subsequent experimental manipulations performed inside the hypoxia chamber to minimize oxygen exposure. Normoxic control cells were cultured in parallel under standard conditions (5% CO₂, 95% air, 37 °C) for the same duration.

Lentiviral transfection

Lentiviral shRNA constructs were obtained from Genechem (China). shRNA sequences were as follows: GAGAAATCTATTGAAGCAT, shRGS20-1; TCGTGTCTCACTGTTAGAA, shRGS20-2. Cells were transfected with the lentiviral particles, according to the manufacturer’s recommendations. After infection, stable cell clones expressing the shRNA constructs were isolated by selection with 2 µg/ml puromycin solution. Cells were collected for further experiments at 48 h after the transfection.

Tumor sphere formation assay and in vitro limiting dilution assay

For tumorsphere formation assay, A1207 and A172 cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 40 cells per well. Tumorsphere formation was assessed by quantifying both the number and size of spheres on day 7 post-seeding. For the limiting dilution assay, glioma cells were plated in 96-well plates at serial dilutions ranging from 10 to 50 cells per well (10, 20, 30, 40, and 50 cells/well), with 16 replicates for each cell density. Following 7 days of incubation at 37 °C with 5% CO₂, tumorsphere formation was evaluated in each well. Sphere-forming efficiency was calculated using Extreme Limiting Dilution Analysis (ELDA) software (http://bioinf.wehi.edu.au/software/elda).

Western blot

Protein extraction was performed using RIPA lysis buffer (Solarbio, China) supplemented with PMSF protease inhibitor at a 1:100 dilution (Solarbio, China). Protein concentration was quantified using a BCA protein assay kit (Solarbio, China) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Equal amounts of protein samples (40–50 µg) were separated by 10% SDS-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and subsequently transferred onto PVDF membranes (Millipore, USA). The membranes were blocked and then incubated with primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight. After three washes with TBST buffer, the membranes were probed with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated goat anti-rabbit/mouse secondary antibodies (1:5000 dilution) for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) detection system (Millipore, USA).

qPCR analysis

Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. cDNA was synthesized from 1 µg of total RNA with HiScript III RT SuperMix (Vazyme). Quantitative PCR was performed using ChamQ SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme) on a StepOnePlus Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems). The reaction conditions were as follows: 95 °C for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 10 s and 60 °C for 30 s. Gene expression levels were normalized to 18 S and calculated using the 2^−ΔΔCt method. The following primer sequences were used: CD133-F: TTCTTGACCGACTGAGACCCA; CD133-R: TCATGTTCTCCAACGCCTCTT; CD15-F: GATCTGCGCGTGTTGGACTA; CD15-R: GAGGGCGACTCGAAGTTCAT; 18 S-F: TGCATGGCCGTTCTTAGTTG;18 S-R: AGTTAGCATGCCAGAGTCTC.

Intracranial xenografting

Animal experiments were approved by the Ethical Committee of Tianjin medical university cancer institute & hospital. A1207 with scrambled lentivirus and RGS20 shRNA lentivirus were respectively co-infected with a luciferase-expressing lentivirus. Mice were anesthetized with isoflurane (3% induction, 1.5% maintenance in 100% oxygen) via a calibrated vaporizer and placed in a stereotactic frame, and injected with a total of 100,000 A1207 in 10 µL of PBS using a 27-gauge needle at 2 mm lateral and posterior to the bregma and 3 mm below the dura. Tumor dimensions were measured every week. Cell suspension was injected slowly in 20 min. Then the needle was kept in the injection site for 5 min before removing it. Bioluminescence imaging was performed to assess xenograft formation at 7 days after implantation by using the IVIS Spectrum Live Imaging System.

Flow cytometry

Cells were treated with TMZ (100 µM, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) alone or with JW67(10 µM, (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) for 3 days. Then the cells were collected and resuspended in 400µL of cold PBS for apoptosis analysis. An apoptosis detection kit (US Everbright, USA) was used to stain the cells, and Annexin V-PI was detected using a flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, USA).

TUNEL staining

TMZ/JW67 treatment procedure was described above. After then cells were incubated for an hour at the temperature of 37℃ using a TUNEL apoptosis assay kit (Beyotime, China). After washing with PBS and staining with DAPI, the samples were observed using a fluorescence microscope finally. The number of TUNEL-positive cells (red) in each treatment condition was counted from 10 random fields per well by an individual who was blinded as to the treatment.

Immunohistochemical quantification

Immunohistochemical data from the Human Protein Atlas (HPA; https://www.proteinatlas.org/) were automatically quantified using the IHC Profiler plugin (https://sourceforge.net/projects/ihcprofiler/) for ImageJ software (https://imagej.nih.gov/ij/). The analysis objectively scored staining intensity (negative, weak, moderate, strong) and the percentage of positive tumor cells. Based on these parameters, expression was classified as: strong positive (> 75%, strong staining), moderate positive (25–75%, moderate staining), weak positive (< 25%, weak staining), or negative (no staining).

Single-cell RNA sequencing data processing

Raw single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data were processed using the R package Seurat (version 4.1.0). Potential doublets were identified and removed using DoubletFinder with an optimised pK parameter. Low-quality cells were filtered out by retaining only those cells that expressed a minimum of 200 genes; genes detected in fewer than three cells were also excluded. Normalized data underwent dimensionality reduction via principal component analysis (PCA) with 30 principal components (npcs = 30). The top 10 principal components were utilized for non-linear dimensionality reduction using uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP). Initial cell clustering was performed with the FindNeighbors and FindClusters functions, resulting in 19 distinct cell clusters. For cluster annotation, marker genes for each cluster were identified using the FindAllMarkers function (parameters: only.pos = TRUE, min.pct = 0.25, logfc.threshold = 0.5). Cell types were annotated based on canonical marker gene expression and copy number variation (CNV) profiles inferred with the inferCNV package (v1.8.0). CNV scores were calculated per cell through quadratic summation across genomic regions.

Spatial transcriptomics data processing

Spatial transcriptomics data were processed according to the standard Seurat spatial analysis workflow. After initial quality control, gene expression data were normalized using the SCTransform function. Dimensionality reduction was carried out using PCA, followed by visualization with UMAP. Clustering was performed using the first 30 principal components under the default resolution setting.

Integration of datasets

Integration of scRNA-seq and spatial transcriptomic data was performed using the Seurat alignment workflow. Both datasets were preprocessed and reduced in dimension independently. The FindTransferAnchors function (normalization.method = “SCT”) was employed to identify cross-modal anchors. Cell type predictions were subsequently transferred from the scRNA-seq reference to the spatial data using the TransferData function (dims = 1:30), enabling the quantification of prediction scores for each single-cell subcluster within spatial domains.

Bioinformatic analysis

All the transcriptional and clinical databases were purchased from: http://gliovis.bioinfo.cnio.es. The Single cell sequencing data (GSE271959) were obtained from Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO, http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/) database. The Spatial transcriptomics data were purchased form the 10X genomics datasets (https://www.10xgenomics.com/cn) and GSE235672. The single sample enrichment analysis of signaling pathway was calculated using the GSVA package in R 4.4.1. The ssGSEA analysis, UMAP analysis and Spatial transcriptomic analysis were performed on R 4.4.1. Pearson correlations between RGS20 expression and these pathways were computed across Rembrandt, CGGA, and TCGA-GBM datasets, identifying consistently negative associations (FDR < 0.05).

Statistical analysis

All quantitative data are presented as mean ± standard deviation from at least three independent experiments. Statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism software (version 9.0). For comparisons between two groups, unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-tests were employed. Multiple group comparisons were analyzed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with appropriate post-hoc tests. Correlation analyses were performed using Pearson’s correlation coefficient. Survival analysis was conducted using Kaplan-Meier curves, and statistical significance was assessed by the log-rank test. For the K-M survival analysis, the median expression level was used as the cutoff for stratification in the Ducray cohort due to its relatively small sample size. In contrast, for the larger TCGA and CGGA cohorts, a median ± SEM-based stratification approach was adopted. The threshold for statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Results

RGS20 as a prognostic marker: enhancing sensitivity to TMZ and modulating tumor stemness in glioma

To investigate the clinical relevance of the RGS family, we first examined their expression in the Rembrandt database. We found that eight RGS members were upregulated in non-tumor tissues compared to tumor tissues, with seven of them were downregulated (Fig. 1a). Notably, RGS20 consistently showed higher expression in non-tumor tissues across multiple databases including Rembrandt, TCGA-GBM, Gravendeel, and Kamoun (Fig. 1b, c). Further univariate and multivariate regression analyses revealed that RGS20 acts as an independent protective factor in glioma, similar to IDH mutation and 1p19q codeletion (Table S1 and 2). Indeed, studies indicate that patients with IDH mutation/1p19q codeletion generally have better survival outcomes and are more responsive to treatments such as TMZ and radiotherapy24–27. Therefore, we stratified patients based on 1p19q codeletion and IDH mutant status, revealing that 62% of patients in the IDH mutant/1p19q codeletion group exhibited high RGS20 expression, compared to 43% in the IDH mutant/1p19q non-codeletion group and 49% in the IDH wild-type/1p19q non-codeletion group (Fig. 1d). Interestingly, RGS20 expression was significantly lower in pseudopalisading and microvascular regions (Fig. 1e), which are areas predominantly populated by GSCs (Fig. 1f). This prompted us to further explore RGS20’s potential role in enhancing sensitivity to TMZ and radiotherapy. We performed a survival analysis on glioma patients receiving single-modality treatment with either radiotherapy or chemotherapy in the Ducray database28. Surprisingly, high RGS20 expression correlated with better responsiveness to chemotherapy (Fig. 1 g) and improved prognosis (Fig. 1h). However, no similar correlation was observed in patients receiving radiotherapy alone (Fig. 1i, j). Overall, patients with high RGS20 expression showed improved prognosis compared to those with low expression. (Fig. 1k, l). In summary, RGS20 may influence glioma patient outcomes by modulating tumor stemness and sensitivity to TMZ.

RGS20 inhibits canonical WNT/β-catenin pathway in glioma

Based on the established role of RGS20 in inhibiting GPCR signaling18,29–31, we analyzed the correlation between RGS20 expression and various GPCR-related pathways using the Rembrandt database. Among the 89 GPCR-associated pathways examined, the WNT signaling pathway showed the strongest negative correlation with RGS20 expression (Fig. 2a; Table S3). This inverse correlation was consistently observed across multiple independent datasets, including TCGA-GBM, CGGA, and Rembrandt (Fig. 2b, c). Further analysis using the Ivy-GAP database—a multi-region repository of glioblastoma samples—revealed that in pseudopalisading and microvascular regions characterized by low RGS20 expression, WNT signaling was markedly activated. These regions also exhibited enriched stem cell signatures and elevated expression of stemness markers such as SOX2, NANOG, OCT4, and KLF4, despite low expression of canonical WNT ligands (Fig. 2 d). These findings suggest that downregulation of RGS20 may promote WNT pathway activation and support glioma stem cell (GSC) maintenance even in ligand-reduced niches. To functionally validate the regulatory role of RGS20 in WNT signaling, we performed RGS20 knockdown in glioma cell lines A1207 and A172. This resulted in significantly increased phosphorylation of GSK-3α/β and elevated expression of β-catenin (Fig. 2e), indicating enhanced WNT pathway activity. Together, these results demonstrate that reduced RGS20 expression promotes WNT signaling activation, potentially enabling GSCs to sustain stem-like properties within microenvironmental niches that lack canonical WNT ligands.

RGS20 regulates WNT signaling to control glioma stem cell maintenance, neuronal differentiation, and tumorigenicity

To further explore the impact of RGS20 on stem cell properties of glioma cells, we first observed the morphological changes in cells following RGS20 knockdown. The results showed that RGS20 knockdown enabled A1207 and A172 to acquire stem-like spheroid formation capabilities, while treatment with the WNT inhibitor JW67 significantly suppressed this spheroid formation (Fig. 3a). Additionally, the stem-like spheroids formed after RGS20 knockdown exhibited high self-renewal capacity, which was effectively inhibited by JW67 treatment (Fig. 3b). This negative correlation between RGS20 expression and maintenance of GSCs was validated across multiple databases (Fig. 3c, d), while the activation of the WNT signaling pathway showed a strong positive correlation with maintenance of stem cell properties (Fig. 3e). Consistent with the changes in self-renewal capacity, RGS20 knockdown induced the expression of GSC markers CD15 and CD133, and this induction was effectively suppressed by JW67 treatment (Fig. 3f, Fig. S1). Interestingly, the expression of neuronal marker Tuj-1 and NeuN was suppressed by RGS20 knockdown but restored by JW67 treatment, whereas no significant changes were observed in the expression of astrocyte marker GFAP or oligodendrocyte marker MBP (Fig. 3f). Analysis of glioma databases further revealed a positive correlation between RGS20 expression and neuronal differentiation (Fig. 3 g), and in GSC-enriched regions with low RGS20 expression (pseudopalisading and microvascular regions), neuronal distribution was significantly reduced (Fig. 3 h), suggesting that the RGS20-associated WNT signaling pathway may regulate the differentiation of GSCs into neurons. To assess the impact of RGS20 inhibition on tumorigenicity of glioblastoma cells, we conducted orthotopic transplantation experiments in nude mice. The results demonstrated that RGS20 inhibition significantly promoted tumor growth (Fig. 3i-k, Fig. S2) and shortened the survival time of tumor-bearing mice (Fig. 3 l). These findings indicate that the RGS20 not only plays a role in the maintenance of GSCs and their differentiation into neurons but also is crucial for the tumorigenicity of GSCs.

RGS20 modulates TMZ resistance in glioma cells via WNT signaling pathway

To validate whether RGS20 knockdown-induced glioma cells exhibit TMZ resistance, we treated RGS20-knockdown glioma cells with TMZ. The results showed that RGS20 knockdown reduced TMZ-induced AnnexinV-PI positive cells by nearly half (Fig. 4a, Fig. S3a), and TMZ-induced TUNEL-positive cells were also significantly reduced by more than half (Fig. 4b). However, this TMZ resistance was reversed upon treatment with the WNT inhibitor JW67, restoring sensitivity to TMZ (Fig. 4c, Fig. S3b). Additionally, the cleavage levels of TMZ-induced apoptosis-related proteins caspase 9 and caspase 3 were significantly reduced following RGS20 knockdown, while JW67 treatment restored their cleavage levels (Fig. 4 d, Fig. S3c). In line with the results, the HALLMARK_DNA_REPAIR signature was significantly enriched in RGS20 low expression group in various databases (Fig. 4e). These findings suggest that the RGS20-associated WNT signaling pathway is involved in the mechanism of TMZ resistance.

RGS20 modulates hypoxia-dependent WNT/β-catenin signaling to drive glioma stem cell maintenance and patient survival

To further validate the RGS20-associated WNT signaling pathway, we analyzed the protein expression levels of RGS20, β-catenin, and WNT ligands in a glioma tissue microarray database. The results showed that RGS20 was generally lowly expressed in glioma samples, while β-catenin was highly expressed. Except for WNT4, other WNT ligands were also generally lowly expressed in glioma samples (Fig. 5a). Interestingly, consistent with findings in the Ivy-GAP database (Fig. 2 d), RGS20 protein was not expressed in tumor necrotic regions, yet β-catenin remained highly expressed despite the low distribution of WNT ligands (e.g., WNT4) (Fig. 5b). In contrast, in perivascular regions, a small amount of RGS20 expression was observed, accompanied by high enrichment of WNT4 and β-catenin proteins (Fig. 5c). These results suggest that WNT signaling activation is regulated through distinct mechanisms across different GSC niches; notably, low RGS20 expression is associated with marked activation of the intrinsic WNT signaling pathway within hypoxic niches.

To further understand the cell populations with activated RGS20-associated intrinsic WNT signaling in glioma samples, we analyzed a single-cell glioma database. We found that Olig2-high GSC populations generally exhibited low RGS20 expression and high activation of the WNT/β-catenin pathway (Fig. 5 c), indicating that the RGS20-associated intrinsic WNT signaling pathway plays a crucial role in maintaining GSCs in tumor tissues. To further validate the spatial distribution characteristics of the RGS20-WNT/β-catenin signaling pathway, we analyzed spatial transcriptomic data. Consistent with protein expression patterns (Fig. 5b), RGS20 expression was low in hypoxic tumor regions, while the WNT/β-catenin signaling pathway and stem cell-related pathways were highly enriched (Fig. 5 d), further highlighting the importance of the RGS20-associated intrinsic WNT signaling pathway in hypoxic niches. To directly validate the hypoxic regulation linking RGS20 expression and WNT signaling, we utilized patient-derived glioma stem cells (MGSC3) cultured under normoxic and hypoxic conditions. As shown in Fig. 5e, hypoxia slightly reduced the expression of RGS20, which was associated with enhanced WNT downstream signaling—including increased phosphorylation of GSK-3α/β and elevated β-catenin expression. Interestingly, RGS20 knockdown-induced activation of WNT signaling was sustained under hypoxic conditions, suggesting that downregulation of RGS20 may help maintain amplified WNT signaling in a hypoxic environment.

To assess the impact of the RGS20-associated intrinsic WNT signaling pathway on patient survival, we stratified patients in the CGGA database into four groups based on RGS20 expression levels and WNT signaling activation and performed survival analysis. The results showed that patients with low RGS20 expression and high WNT signaling activation (RGS20 low/WNT high) had significantly shorter survival compared to other groups (Fig. 5f), underscoring the importance of the RGS20-associated intrinsic WNT signaling pathway in glioma treatment.

In summary, we demonstrated that the spatial distribution of the RGS20-WNT/β-catenin signaling axis is hypoxia-dependent, and this regulatory axis significantly impacts the survival outcomes of glioma patients (Fig. 6). This study provides critical insights for further exploration of its role in tumor progression and therapeutic resistance.

Discussion

Although numerous studies have shown that RGS20 promotes malignant progression in various epithelial-derived tumors (such as non-small cell lung cancer, breast cancer, squamous cell carcinoma, and liver cancer)32–36, other reports indicate that RGS20 is involved in neuronal functional degeneration and apoptosis in neurodegenerative diseases (such as Alzheimer’s disease and Parkinson’s disease)37. Based on the classical mechanism of RGS proteins inhibiting GPCR signaling pathways, RGS20 may suppress tumor progression by inhibiting GPCR signaling. In fact, we found that knocking down RGS20 activates endogenous WNT signaling, enabling glioma cells to acquire glioma stem cell (GSC) characteristics and inhibiting neuronal differentiation. This suggests that RGS20 may play opposing roles in cells with different genetic and epigenetic backgrounds.

Notably, approximately 10% of epithelial-derived tumors harbor point mutations in the conserved domains of RGS proteins19,38. These mutations may lead to abnormal activation of G proteins39,40 or regulate downstream signaling through alternative pathways (such as long non-coding RNAs and NF-κB signaling) in a GPCR-independent manner35,36, thereby promoting cancer progression in epithelial-derived tumors. However, to date, there have been no reports of RGS20 mutations in neurodegenerative diseases or brain tumor tissues, suggesting that RGS20 in gliomas likely retains its intrinsic GPCR-inhibitory function. Additionally, studies have shown that RGS20 can undergo alternative splicing under epigenetic regulation, but the effects of its different splice variants on cells of different origins remain to be further explored41. Given that glial-derived and epithelial-derived tumor cells may have distinct epigenetic regulatory programs, further investigation into the epigenetic regulation of RGS20 in tumors of different origins is warranted.

The maintenance of normal stem cells relies on exogenous signals provided by specific microenvironments (niches) composed of diverse cell types8. However, the hypoxia niche or perivascular niche enriched with GSCs, due to differences in cellular composition, cannot provide the same exogenous signals as the niche for normal neural stem cells. Therefore, GSCs require alternative mechanisms to maintain their stemness42,43. In the hypoxic regions of glioblastoma, we observed that low expression of RGS20 leads to activation of the WNT signaling pathway, which may precisely substitute for the exogenous WNT ligand stimulation needed for GSC maintenance in this environment.

Furthermore, in tumor cells, the WNT signaling pathway drives chemotherapy (TMZ) resistance through multiple mechanisms. For instance, β-catenin binding to Tcf/Lef sites enhances the transcription of MGMT44, while the interaction between β-catenin and SCL26A9 suppresses the expression of cleaved caspase-9, cleaved caspase-3, and apoptosis-inducing factor45. Additionally, WNT signaling can disrupt transcytosis in endothelial cells, thereby hindering drug delivery46. These mechanisms collectively contribute to patient resistance to TMZ therapy and poor prognosis.

In conclusion, our study reveals that in hypoxic microenvironments, GSCs maintain their stemness and chemoresistance by downregulating RGS20 to drive activation of the WNT signaling pathway, along with the underlying mechanisms (Fig. 5 F). We believe that intrinsic regulators of GPCRs, such as the RGS family, may play a critical role in the malignant progression of a broader range of cancers. In-depth research and targeted exploration of these regulators will provide important theoretical insights and potential strategies for cancer treatment.
