A case of giant cholangioblastic cholangiocarcinoma (12 kg) with left hepatectomy and hilar reconstruction: a surgical and pathological challenge
Authors: Doaa Abdulaziz, Navneet Tiwari, Akshadar Koneti, Viktorija Nara, Jennifer Watkins, Joerg Matthias Pollok
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction:Cholangioblastic cholangiocarcinoma is a rare variant of intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma affecting young adults. Fewer than 30 cases have been reported. We describe a giant tumor in a 32-year-old man with morbid obesity (BMI of 54).Case presentation:The patient presented with a 34 cm mass, pain, persistent vomiting, inability to tolerate oral intake, and a 30 kg weight loss. Biopsy confirmed cholangioblastic intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma. As mass-effect symptoms and malnutrition precluded initial systemic treatment, he underwent high-risk palliative cytoreduction. A fresh 12 kg tumor was removed by left hepatectomy. Inadvertent hilar stapling in severely distorted anatomy required immediate portal vein and hepatic artery reconstruction and Roux-en-Y hepaticojejunostomy. Hepatic inflow was compromised for approximately 75 minutes. Estimated blood loss was 20 L. He was discharged on postoperative day 14.Pathology:The specimen measured 53 × 33.5 × 22 cm and weighed 7.914 kg after fixation. Histology showed extensive venous and lymphovascular invasion. An involved transection margin and residual intrahepatic and nodal disease constituted an R2 resection; six of nine nodes were metastatic (pT2, pN1).Discussion:This case illustrates the diagnostic, surgical, and anesthetic hazards of palliative resection for a giant hilar tumor, including the need for anatomical confirmation before hilar division and for preparedness for vascular reconstruction and massive hemorrhage.Conclusion:Palliative cytoreduction relieved obstruction and enabled systemic therapy. The procedure was neither radical nor curative, and its potential symptomatic benefit must be balanced against substantial operative risk.

Introduction

Cholangiocarcinoma (CCA) is a heterogeneous group of malignancies arising from the biliary epithelium. Intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (iCCA) accounts for approximately 10–15% of all primary liver cancers[1]. While several histological subtypes of iCCA are recognized, the cholangioblastic variant is exceedingly rare, with fewer than 30 cases reported in the literature[2,3].

First[4] described in 2005, cholangioblastic CCA is characterized by a distinctive morphology resembling fetal bile duct development, with solid, trabecular, and tubulocystic patterns[5]. It typically affects young adults, with a female predominance, and often presents as a large, solitary liver mass[2,6]. A key diagnostic feature is the strong and diffuse expression of inhibin, an immunohistochemical marker not typically associated with other forms of CCA[7]. More recently, a characteristic NIPBL::NACC1 gene fusion has been identified as a defining molecular feature of this entity[8,9].

Surgical resection remains the only potentially curative treatment for selected patients with iCCA. However, the management of giant hepatic tumors (≥10 cm) presents significant technical challenges, including massive hemorrhage, post-hepatectomy liver failure, and incomplete resection[10]. The case presented here is exceptional due to the unprecedented tumor size (12 kg), severe mass-effect symptoms, the need for palliative cytoreduction, and the complexity of hilar reconstruction. We describe the multidisciplinary surgical and anesthetic challenges of managing a 32-year-old man with morbid obesity and giant cholangioblastic CCA. This case report has been prepared in line with the SCARE 2025 checklist[4]

Case presentation

A 32-year-old man was transferred from a peripheral hospital for management of a large abdominal mass that had been diagnosed at the local hospital as a neuroendocrine tumor (NET) of unknown primary. His past medical history included diabetes and morbid obesity, with a BMI of 54. An initial CT scan in October 2025 revealed a 34 cm mass within the abdomen, with additional liver lesions (Fig. 1). Subsequent PET scans (FDG and Ga-68 DOTATATE) showed heterogeneous, low-grade uptake. No relevant family history was reported in the available clinical record. Detailed social history, regular medication list, and allergy status were not available in the submitted case material and should be confirmed against the clinical record before final submission.

The referred endoscopic ultrasound biopsy of an abdominal mass was reviewed, and the histology showed a tumor composed of trabecular and tubulocystic structures, and confluent islands of monotonous cells with a moderate amount of eosinophilic cytoplasm and medium-sized, round or oval nuclei with inconspicuous nucleoli. On review of the referred immunohistochemistry, tumor cells showed nearly diffuse positivity for cytokeratin 7 and synaptophysin with variable intensity, but only patchy positivity for chromogranin A, and they were negative for cytokeratin 20 and site-specific markers (CDX2, TTF1, PAX8). Further immunohistochemical analysis showed no expression of INSM1 but revealed strong, diffuse positivity for Inhibin A. Following RNA-based NGS testing, an NIPBL::NACC1 gene fusion was detected, confirming the diagnosis of the cholangioblastic variant of iCCA.

The patient was experiencing severe symptoms due to the mass effect of the tumor, including persistent vomiting and an inability to tolerate oral intake, which led to a significant weight loss of 30 kg over a 3-month period. His nutritional status was poor, with an albumin level of 2.5 g/dL. Given the severity of the gastric outlet obstruction and the patient’s poor nutritional status, primary chemotherapy was not considered a viable option. Therefore, palliative debulking surgery was deemed necessary to alleviate the obstruction and improve his quality of life, potentially to be followed by chemotherapy. Preoperative liver function tests were within normal limits.

Following a multidisciplinary team discussion, the patient was scheduled for an elective palliative debulking resection. The primary goals of the surgery were to alleviate mass effect and relieve gastrointestinal obstruction, with the clear understanding that the procedure was not intended to be curative.

Operative procedure

The patient underwent laparotomy through an inverted L incision in a specialist hepatopancreatobiliary center. Upon entering the abdomen, a massive tumor arising from the left lobe of the liver was identified, occupying the majority of the abdominal cavity. The anatomy was severely distorted, with compression and displacement of the stomach, spleen, and transverse colon. The hilar structures were encased and displaced by the tumor, making standard hilar dissection unsafe and technically limited.

The tumor was mobilized, and parenchymal transection for left hepatectomy was completed. Because of the extreme distortion and encasement of the hilar structures, the intended left-sided hilar pedicle control resulted in inadvertent stapling across the hepatic hilum. The structures involved included the portal venous inflow, hepatic arterial inflow, and biliary drainage to the remnant liver, necessitating immediate reconstruction. Portal venous and hepatic arterial continuity were restored by end-to-end anastomoses, and biliary drainage was reconstructed with a Roux-en-Y hepaticojejunostomy. The period of temporary hepatic inflow compromise, from hilar interruption to restoration of hepatic arterial inflow, was approximately 75 minutes. The procedure was complicated by massive intraoperative hemorrhage, with an estimated blood loss of 20 L. A major hemorrhage protocol was activated, including 20 units of packed red cells, 3 units of fresh frozen plasma, 24 units of platelets, and 6 units of cryoprecipitate, with the use of 16 g of fibrinogen and tranexamic acid, with thromboelastogram-guided resuscitation where applicable. Epoprostenol (Flolan) was commenced intraoperatively to support arterial flow.

Postoperative course

The patient was transferred to the intensive care unit for postoperative monitoring. He required mechanical ventilation and vasopressor support for the first 48 hours. He was successfully extubated on postoperative day 3 and remained in the ICU for monitoring and for management of pain and initial agitation, which resolved gradually. His liver function tests peaked on postoperative day 2 (bilirubin 4.5 mg/dL, AST 1500 U/L, ALT 1200 U/L) and gradually returned to normal over the next week. His INR peaked at 2.5 and normalized by day 5. Drain output decreased gradually until it ceased on day 11. He was transferred to the surgical ward on day 10. He was tolerating an oral diet and was under the care of the nutrition, pain, and physiotherapy teams for optimization of his condition. He was discharged home on postoperative day 14.

He was reviewed 2 weeks after discharge in the outpatient clinic and was progressing well clinically. He had superficial wound dehiscence at the right and epigastric edges of the wound, managed conservatively. At the most recent available follow-up, CT imaging showed no early postoperative surgical complications. Longer-term oncological follow-up is ongoing, and the patient remains under oncology review for systemic therapy. The exact duration of follow-up and early response to postoperative immunochemotherapy should be updated from the oncology record before final submission.

The case was discussed at the HPB Cancer MDT. Given the R2 margin and pN1 disease, the operation was not considered curative; an oncology referral was recommended for systemic treatment planning.

At his 1-month follow-up, he was seen in the oncology clinic and was managing well, with improved ability to eat. He was started on systemic therapy, 8 weeks post-surgery, in the form of gemcitabine–cisplatin and durvalumab.

His follow-up in the surgical clinic was 6 months later, where no surgical complaints were noted and he was managing well and tolerating chemotherapy.

Pathological findings

The distorted left hepatectomy specimen measured 53 × 33.5 × 22 cm (Fig. 2), weighed 12 kg post-resection, with a post-fixation weight of 7.914 kg. Macroscopic examination revealed an exophytic, subcapsular and intrahepatic, multinodular whitish tumor with cystic spaces, measuring 53 cm in maximum dimension, with multiple satellite lesions. Microscopic examination showed tumor nodules with morphological features similar to those seen in the previously biopsy-proven cholangioblastic variant of iCCA, as well as extensive venous and lymphovascular invasion. Some areas exhibited biphasic morphology, composed of smaller cells with scanty cytoplasm and larger cells with a moderate amount of eosinophilic cytoplasm. On immunohistochemistry, tumor cells showed patchy positivity for cytokeratin 7 and chromogranin A, and strong, diffuse positivity for synaptophysin and inhibin A (Fig. 3). No capsular breach was seen. The tumor was present at the resection margin (R2). The background liver showed mild to moderate steatosis and delicate septa of bridging fibrosis. Discussion

This case report describes the palliative surgical management of a giant (12 kg) cholangioblastic CCA in a 32-year-old man, a presentation that appears unprecedented in the available literature. Cholangioblastic CCA is an exceedingly rare variant of iCCA, with most reported cases occurring in young women and presenting as significantly smaller tumors[2,6]. The present case is unique in several aspects: the patient’s sex, the size and weight of the tumor, the severe mass-effect symptoms requiring palliation, the complexity of hilar reconstruction, and the massive intraoperative hemorrhage.

The surgical management of giant hepatic tumors is fraught with challenges. As described by Wee et al, resection of super-giant HCCs (≥15 cm) is technically demanding and associated with poor long-term outcomes[10]. Our case, a cholangioblastic CCA of 12 kg and over 50 cm in diameter, far exceeds the dimensions of previously reported giant tumors. The surgical strategy employed, including a generous reverse-L incision, an anterior approach to avoid tumor rupture, and meticulous dissection, aligns with the principles outlined for the safe resection of giant hepatic tumors[10].

The most significant intraoperative challenge was the management of the distorted hilar anatomy. The tumor encased the portal triad, necessitating a stapled transection of the hilum followed by a complete reconstruction of the portal vein, hepatic artery, and biliary system. This complex reconstruction, combined with a 75-minute anhepatic phase, placed the patient at high risk for postoperative complications, including liver failure and biliary leaks. The duration of the anhepatic phase, while shorter than the 100-minute threshold associated with a higher incidence of graft dysfunction in liver transplantation, still represented a significant ischemic insult to the remnant liver[11]. This highlights the importance of careful dissection and positive identification of structures before irreversible maneuvers such as stapling, although we acknowledge this may be extremely challenging with giant tumors.

Massive intraoperative hemorrhage, with an estimated blood loss of 20 L, is another remarkable feature of this case. This is likely attributable to the large tumor size, the extensive raw surface area of the liver resection, and the complex vascular reconstruction. The successful management of such a massive hemorrhage required a coordinated effort between the surgical and anesthetic teams, including the activation of a massive transfusion protocol and the maintenance of hemodynamic stability.

The histopathological diagnosis of cholangioblastic CCA can be challenging due to its rarity and overlapping morphological and immunohistochemical features with those of NETs. Cholangioblastic CCA can exhibit patchy expression of neuroendocrine markers or, sometimes, even diffuse synaptophysin expression[3]. Strong, diffuse positivity for inhibin is a key differentiating feature, with the presence of the NIPBL::NACC1 gene fusion confirming a cholangioblastic variant of iCCA[7–9]. This case highlights the importance of considering cholangioblastic CCA not only in the biopsy of a liver lesion, especially in a young adult, but also in a tumor initially presenting as an abdominal mass and resembling a NET of unknown primary site.

The R2 resection margin and the presence of lymph node metastases (pN1) are poor prognostic factors, consistent with the aggressive nature of this disease. This was intended to be a debulking surgery, and therefore, knowing that it would be an R2 resection has little relevance.

The exceptional nature of this case questions the reproducibility of this technique. We recommend that patient selection for similar procedures be highly selective and that such procedures be performed only in high-volume centers with extensive experience in complex hepatobiliary surgery and liver transplantation.

Conclusion

We present an extraordinary case of giant cholangioblastic CCA in a young man managed with palliative left hepatectomy and complex hilar reconstruction. The case illustrates that, in highly selected patients with severe mass-effect symptoms, palliative cytoreductive surgery may provide meaningful symptom relief and create an opportunity for systemic therapy, but it should not be presented as radical or curative when R2 margins and nodal disease are present. Key lessons include the need for explicit preoperative definition of surgical intent, meticulous planning for distorted hilar anatomy, avoidance of irreversible hilar stapling without complete anatomical control, early availability of vascular reconstruction expertise, and preparedness for massive hemorrhage with close anesthetic collaboration.
