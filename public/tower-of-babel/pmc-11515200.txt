Identification and validation of key extracellular proteins as the potential biomarkers in diabetic nephropathy
Authors: Wei Pan, Qiankun Zhang, Xiaohua Gong, Wenjun Wu, Qi Zhou
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveAccumulation of extracellular matrix (ECM) proteins in the glomerular mesangial region is a typical hallmark of diabetic nephropathy (DN). However, the molecular mechanism underlying ECM accumulation in the mesangium of DN patients remains unclear. The present study aims to establish a connection between extracellular proteins and DN with the goal of identifying potential biomarkers for this condition.MethodsDifferentially expressed genes (DEGs) between DN kidney tissue and healthy kidney tissue were analyzed using the public data GSE166239. Two gene lists encoding extracellular proteins were then utilized to identify extracellular protein-differentially expressed genes (EP-DEGs). Functional enrichment analyses, including Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) analyses, were performed on these EP-DEGs. A protein–protein interaction (PPI) network was established to identify key EP-DEGs. Furthermore, the diagnostic ability, immune cell infiltration, and clinical relevance of these EP-DEGs were investigated. Immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining of paraffin-embedded renal tissues was performed to validate the accuracy of the bioinformatic results.ResultsA total of 1204 DEGs were identified, from which 162 EP-DEGs were further characterized by overlapping with extracellular protein gene lists. From the PPI network analysis, five EP-DEGs (e.g., TNF, COL1A1, FN1, MMP9, and TGFB1) were identified as candidate biomarkers. TNF, COL1A1, and MMP9 had a high diagnostic accuracy for DN. Assessment of immune cell infiltration revealed that the expression of TNF was positively associated with resting dendritic cells (DCs) (r = 0.85, P < 0.001) and M1 macrophages (r = 0.62, P < 0.05), whereas negatively associated with regulatory T cells (r = − 0.62, P < 0.05). Nephroseq v5 analysis demonstrated a negative correlation between the estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) and TNF expression (r = − 0.730, P = 0.025). Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) revealed significant enrichment of glycosaminoglycan (GAG) degradation in the high-TNF subgroup. IHC staining of renal tissues confirmed significantly elevated TNF-a expression and decreased hyaluronic acid (HA) levels in the DN group compared to controls (both P < 0.05), with a negative correlation observed between TNF-a and HA (r = − 0.691, P = 0.026).ConclusionOur findings suggest that TNF may play a pivotal role in the progress of DN by driving ECM accumulation, and this process might involve GAG degradation pathway activation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40001-024-02120-y.Keywords: TNF, Extracellular matrix, Dendritic cell, Diabetic nephropathy, Glycosaminoglycan degradation

Introduction

Diabetic nephropathy (DN) is a major microvascular complication in individuals with diabetes mellitus (DM), affecting millions worldwide and serving as the leading cause of end-stage renal disease (ESRD) [1, 2]. The hallmark of DN pathology is mesangial expansion, characterized by the abnormal proliferation of mesangial cells and the accumulation of extracellular matrix (ECM) within the glomerular mesangial region, eventually leading to glomerulosclerosis and renal failure [3, 4].

The increased deposition of ECM, detectable through biopsy, serves as a vital diagnostic and prognostic marker for DN progression [5]. Key ECM components elevated in DN include collagen, fibronectin, laminin, and proteoglycans. Extensive research has focused on the mechanisms and regulatory molecules involved in ECM upregulation in diabetic kidneys [6, 7]. Hyperglycemia is widely recognized as the principal driver of mesangial expansion, with hyperglycemia-induced ECM accumulation largely mediated by Transforming Growth Factor-beta (TGF-β), which is significantly upregulated in diabetes [8–10]. In addition to hyperglycemia, advanced glycation end products (AGEs), mechanical stress, and reactive oxygen species (ROS) are critical contributors to ECM deposition. AGEs, formed through non-enzymatic glycation of proteins and lipids, accumulate in diabetic tissues and interact with their receptors, leading to the activation of pro-fibrotic pathways [11]. Mechanical stress, resulting from increased intraglomerular pressure, activates signaling pathways such as the mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) pathway, which upregulates ECM protein production and enhances the expression of TGF-β [12, 13]. Furthermore, mechanical stretch has been shown to directly increase the synthesis of ECM components in mesangial cells [14].

Despite these insights, the fundamental molecular mechanisms underlying ECM accumulation in the mesangium, leading to mesangial expansion in DN, remain incompletely understood. This research aims to identify novel extracellular protein biomarkers of DN through bioinformatics analysis and to elucidate the potential molecular mechanisms involved. Identifying these biomarkers could enhance diagnostic accuracy and provide new therapeutic targets, ultimately improving patient outcomes.

Materials and methods

Raw data acquisition and processing

Gene expression profiles used in this study were sourced from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database, specifically from dataset GSE166239. This dataset was generated using the expression profiling array platform GPL18573, which facilitates the high-throughput measurement of gene expression levels across numerous genes. We analyzed paraffin-embedded kidney biopsy samples obtained from adult patients registered in the Norwegian Kidney Biopsy Registry. The study focused on two distinct groups: normal renal biopsies and biopsies from patients with Type 2 Diabetic Nephropathy (T2DN). Each group comprised six samples (n = 6 per group). Gene symbols were identified through a standardized transformation process, ensuring all probes were accurately mapped to their corresponding genes based on platform annotation data. Probes that mapped to more than one gene were removed to maintain data integrity and avoid ambiguity. For genes associated with multiple probes, the mean value of these probes was computed to provide a single, representative value for each gene. Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was performed using the “PCA” package to reduce the dataset's dimensionality while preserving most of the data's variability. As a result, a final set of 14,884 genes was selected for subsequent analyses.

Identification of differentially expressed genes (DEGs)

The analysis of DEGs between DN and control groups was conducted using the “DESeq2” package in R version 4.2.2. DEGs were identified based on two criteria: an adjusted p-value of less than 0.05 to ensure statistical significance, and an absolute log2 fold-change (|log2 FC|) greater than 1 to highlight genes with substantial changes in expression levels.

Screening of EP-DEGs

Two lists of extracellular protein (EP) genes were obtained from two prominent databases: UniProt (using the Gene Ontology term GO:0005576 for extracellular region) and the Human Protein Atlas (HPA) (refer to Supplementary Data S1 for detailed lists) [15, 16]. To identify the overlap between these EP gene lists and DEGs, we used the "VennDiagram" package. This tool allowed us to visualize the intersections between each EP gene list and the set of DEGs separately. After determining the intersecting genes (EP-DEGs) from both the UniProt and HPA datasets, we focused on those EP-DEGs that were common to both intersections. These common EP-DEGs were then visualized using volcano plots to display their significance and magnitude of change, and heatmaps to illustrate their expression patterns across samples.

Function enrichment analysis

To elucidate the potential functions and pathways associated with these extracellular protein differentially expressed genes (EP-DEGs), we conducted Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses using the "clusterProfiler" package. GO enrichment analysis allowed us to categorize the EP-DEGs into three distinct aspects: cellular components (CC), molecular functions (MF), and biological processes (BP).

Establishment of PPI network and identification of key EP-DEGs

The previously identified EP-DEGs were mapped onto the STRING database to explore potential protein–protein interactions (PPIs). STRING, a comprehensive resource for predicted and experimental PPIs, helped in identifying interactions among these genes, providing a network context for their functions. Subsequently, we utilized Cytoscape version 3.8.2, a powerful software platform for visualizing complex networks, to construct and visualize the transcriptional regulatory network of the common EP-DEGs. Within Cytoscape, we employed the CytoHubba plug-in to identify key hub genes within the network. CytoHubba ranks nodes in a network based on several centrality measures. Specifically, we used three methods: Maximum Neighborhood Component (MNC), Maximal Clique Centrality (MCC), and Degree, to compute scores for each gene. These methods assess the importance of genes based on their connectivity and centrality in the network.

Diagnostic value analysis of EP-DEGs

We employed the "pROC" package to evaluate the diagnostic significance of the key EP-DEGs in DN. This package facilitated the generation of receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves, which are essential for assessing the performance of diagnostic markers. By calculating the area under the curve (AUC) for each ROC curve, we quantified the diagnostic accuracy of the EP-DEGs. An AUC value closer to 1.0 indicates a higher diagnostic value, demonstrating a gene's strong ability to distinguish between DN and control samples.

Immune cell infiltration correlation analysis

We uploaded the gene expression matrix to CIBERSORTx, a powerful tool for deconvoluting complex tissue mixtures and estimating immune cell infiltration levels. Through CIBERSORTx, we obtained a comprehensive immune cell infiltration matrix. To visually represent the relationships between different immune cell types, we employed the "corrplot" package in R. This package facilitated the generation of a correlation heatmap depicting the relationship among different immune cell infiltrates.

Clinical correlation analysis

We utilized the Nephroseq v5 online database, accessible at http://v5.nephroseq.org, as a comprehensive resource for conducting an in-depth analysis of the interrelations among TNF expression, eGFR, and proteinuria in patients diagnosed with DN. Utilizing the rich repository of transcriptomic and clinical data within this platform, we conducted a thorough investigation to elucidate the correlations between these key parameters.

Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA)

Tumor necrosis factor (TNF) emerged as the predominant hub gene through GSEA. To further investigate its functional significance, we classified six renal biopsies from patients with T2DN and DN kidney tissues into low and high-expression groups based on the median expression level of TNF. Subsequently, we conducted GSEA to explore the differential enrichment of functions or pathways between these two expression groups. The analysis was performed using the "clusterProfiler" package in R. A significance threshold of adjusted p-value < 0.05 was applied to identify statistically meaningful disparities in enriched functions or pathways.

Immunohistochemistry validation

The paraffin-embedded renal tissues utilized in our research, sourced from the pathology department, underwent immunohistochemistry (IHC) validation. Approval for this study was obtained from the Human Ethics Committee of the Fifth Affiliated Hospital of Wenzhou University under protocol number 2023-319, with all participating patients providing written informed consent. The disease group comprised individuals diagnosed with DN through kidney puncture, while the control group included non-tumor renal tissue adjacent to renal tumors. To prepare the paraffin slides, they were initially immersed in xylene for 15 min, followed by washing with anhydrous ethanol for 30 min. Subsequently, the slides underwent treatment with citrate buffer (pH 6.0) for 20 min. Following this, the slides were incubated in 3% hydrogen peroxide for 25 min at room temperature. The slides were washed thrice with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) solution, each wash lasting 5 min. Blocking was then carried out using 3% bovine serum albumin for 30 min. Primary antibodies against TNF-a (Bioss, BS-10802R) and hyaluronic acid (HA) (Beyotime, AH158) were selected. Incubation with these primary antibodies was performed overnight at 4 ℃. Subsequently, horseradish peroxidase-labeled secondary antibody solution was added to each paraffin slide and incubated at room temperature for 1 h. Following five washes with PBS solution, the paraffin slides were stained with 3,3′-diaminobenzidine. Representative photos were captured using the PANNORAMIC SCAN II (3DHISTECH Ltd., Pannoramic MIDI). Quantitative analysis of TNF-a and HA expression was conducted using the Densito quant module of Quant Center 2.3 software.

Statistics analysis

Statistical tests were performed using R v4.2.2 and GraphPad Prism v9. To compare differences between the two groups, Student’s t-test was employed. Spearman’s correlation analysis was utilized to explore correlations between clinical data and gene expression levels. A significance level of P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Identification of DEGs

In this study, we performed an in-depth analysis of gene expression profiles from patients with T2DN and normal controls using data from the GSE166239 dataset. Specifically, we examined RNA expression data from six T2DN patients and six matched normal controls (Fig. 1A). We then conducted differential expression analysis using DESeq2, a robust statistical tool for detecting changes in gene expression levels. This analysis identified 1204 DEGs, comprising 865 up-regulated and 339 down-regulated genes in the T2DN group compared to the controls, as detailed in Supplementary Data S2. The distribution of these DEGs is visualized in volcano plots (Fig. 1B), which highlight the genes with the most significant changes in expression.

Identification of DEGs between the DN and control groups. A PCA plot after batch correction. B Volcano plot of DEGs in the expression profiling datasets

Screening of EP-DEGs

In total, we screened 332 DEGs from the UniProt database and 207 DEGs from the HPA, identifying 162 overlapping genes (EP-DEGs) exhibiting consistent expression patterns (Fig. 2A). The distribution of these EP-DEGs was visualized using a volcano plot (Fig. 2B), which revealed 124 up-regulated and 38 down-regulated genes (Supplementary Data S3). Additionally, Fig. 2C provides a detailed illustration of the expression patterns of the EP-DEGs.

Identification of DEGs that encode EP-DEGs. A Venn diagrams showing a total of 162 EP-DEGs identified. B Volcano plot displaying EP-DEGs. C Heatmap illustrating EP-DEGs

Function enrichment analysis of EP-DEGs

GO analysis revealed significant enrichment of EP-DEGs in BP (e.g., extracellular matrix organization), CC (e.g., collagen-containing extracellular matrix), and MF (e.g., extracellular matrix structural constituent) (Fig. 3A, Supplementary Data S4). Concurrently, KEGG analysis indicated that EP-DEGs were predominantly enriched in pathways involving complement and coagulation cascades, cytokine–cytokine receptor interaction and protein digestion and absorption (Fig. 3B, Supplementary Data S5).

Functional enrichment analysis of EP-DEGs. A Enrichment analysis results of EP-DEGs corresponding to three GO levels: BP, CC, and MF. B KEGG pathways analysis of EP-DEGs

Establishment of the PPI network and identification of key genes

A PPI network comprising 162 EP-DEGs was constructed using the STRING database and visualized with Cytoscape software. The resulting PPI network, shown in Fig. 4A, includes 27 nodes and 105 edges, representing 21 up-regulated genes and 6 down-regulated genes. To identify key hub genes, we applied three topological methods, namely, MCC, MNC, and Degree, using the CytoHubba plug-in (Fig. 4B–D). Figures 4B–D illustrate the top ten hub genes identified by each method. The intersection of these three methods revealed five key genes: FN1, TNF, MMP9, TGFB1, and COL1A1.

Screening the key EP-DEGs via the PPI network. A PPI network constructed using Cytoscape, with network nodes representing proteins. Top ten EP-DEGs obtained using the MCC (B), MNC (C), and Degree (D) methods

ROC curves for the diagnostic value of key EP-DEGs in DN patients

As shown in Figs. 5A, the AUC values for TNF, COL1A1, and MMP9 were close to 1, indicating high diagnostic accuracy for DN. In contrast, TGFB1 (AUC = 0.7778) demonstrated moderate diagnostic accuracy, while FN1 (AUC = 0.5) exhibited limited diagnostic capability for DN. We conducted a further validation study using another independent dataset to confirm the accuracy of the ROC analysis (GSE142153), showing a similar results (Figs. 5B).

A, B ROC curves for the diagnostic values of five key EP-DEGs in DN patients. A The same dataset (GSE166239); B another dataset (GSE142153)

Correlations among key EP-DEGs and infiltrating immune cells

Based on the immune cell infiltration matrix, the expression of TNF exhibited strong positive correlations with resting dendritic cells (r = 0.85, P < 0.001) and M1 macrophages (r = 0.62, P < 0.05), while exhibiting a negative correlation with regulatory T cells (r = − 0.62, P < 0.05). FN1 expression was positively correlated with M1 macrophages (r = 0.73, P < 0.05) and also showed another positive correlation with M1 macrophages (r = 0.6, P < 0.05). COL1A1 expression was negatively correlated with resting CD4 memory T cells (r = − 0.66, P < 0.05). MMP9 expression exhibited positive correlations with monocytes (r = 0.8, P < 0.01) and B cell memory (r = 0.64, P < 0.05). Lastly, TGFB1 expression was negatively correlated with naive B cells (r = − 0.65, P < 0.05). These correlations are illustrated in Fig. 6 and detailed in Supplementary Data S6.

Associations of the expression of five key EP-DEGs and infiltrating immune cells

Correlation analysis between key EP-DEGs and eGFR

To further elucidate the clinical relevance of key EP-DEGs in DN, we performed a correlation analysis between their expression and estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) using Nephroseq v5. The results demonstrated a significant negative correlation between TNF expression and eGFR (r = − 0.7300, P = 0.0255, Fig. 7A). Although TGFB1, FN1, and COL1A1 showed a negative trend, their correlations with eGFR were not statistically significant. Conversely, MMP9 exhibited a mild positive correlation with eGFR (Fig. 7B–E).

Correlations of the expression of TNF, FN1, COL1A1, MMP9 and TGFB1 with eGFR

GSEA based on TNF expression

Based on diagnostic value, correlations with infiltrating immune cells and eGFR, TNF was identified as a key gene, so six kidney tissues from DN patients were categorized into two subgroups based on the median expression level of TNF for GSEA. Notably, the high-TNF subgroup displayed significant enrichment in pathways related to glycosaminoglycan (GAG) degradation, the Fanconi anemia pathway, steroid biosynthesis, and glycosylphosphatidylinositol (GPI)-anchor biosynthesis (Fig. 8, Supplementary Data S7). This suggests a potential association between elevated TNF expression and alterations in these key metabolic pathways, highlighting their potential role in the pathogenesis of DN.

GSEA based on TNF expression levels

Immunohistochemistry validation

A total of 10 paraffin-embedded renal tissue samples from patients with DN and 10 samples from non-tumor renal tissue adjacent to renal tumors, matched for gender and age, were collected for IHC staining. As illustrated in Fig. 9A–D, TNF-α immunostaining was prominently observed in mesangial regions and tubular epithelial cells, with only sparse TNF-α detected in mesangial cells in the control group. Conversely, HA immunostaining showed abundant presence in the nuclei of glomerular interstitial cells and tubulointerstitial cells in the control group, while reduced staining was observed in the mesangial regions of the DN group. The expression levels of TNF-α and HA were significantly higher and lower, respectively, in the DN group compared to the control group (Fig. 9E, both P < 0.05). Furthermore, a negative correlation was found between TNF-α and HA expression in the DN group (r = − 0.691, P = 0.026, Fig. 9F).

Immunohistochemistry validation. A–D Immunostaining of TNF-a and HA in DN tissues and normal renal tissues (magnification ×400). E The expression of the TNF-a and HA in the DN and control group. (F)TNF-a was negatively correlated with HA in the DN group

Discussion

In this study, five key EP-DEGs (FN1, MMP9, TNF, TGFB1, and COL1A1) were identified as candidate biomarkers based on the PPI network. Among these, TNF, COL1A1, and MMP9 demonstrated high diagnostic value, with AUC values reaching almost 1 according to ROC curve analysis. Immune cell infiltration analysis revealed that TNF expression positively correlated with resting dendritic cells and M1 macrophages, while negatively correlating with regulatory T cells. Additionally, the Nephroseq v5 online platform indicated a negative correlation between TNF expression and eGFR. These findings suggest that TNF may hold greater clinical significance in DN compared to the other EP-DEGs.

TNF, a multifaceted cytokine, along with its receptors, TNFR1 and TNFR2, play crucial roles in inflammation, immunity, and cellular organization [17, 18]. Significant advances have elucidated the contribution of TNF to the common pathways of DN development and progression. For instance, Ioanna et al. [19] demonstrated the activation of T-cell immunity and the TNF-α pathway at the onset of DN in T2DM patients. Skupien et al. reported that circulating TNFR2 levels predicted the risk of ESRD among patients with diabetes and macroalbuminuria, especially those experiencing a rapid decline in eGFR [20]. Furthermore, an animal study by Nakamura et al. showed a progressive increase in TNF-α mRNA levels in the glomeruli of diabetic rats over time [21]. Despite the plethora of studies highlighting the pivotal role of TNF in DN progression, its precise physiological mechanism remains incompletely understood.

Our findings indicated a strong correlation between TNF expression and resting dendritic cells, based on immune cell infiltration analysis. Dendritic cells (DCs) represent the most potent antigen-presenting cells in the immune system, and the activation of resting DCs is a critical step in initiating adaptive immunity [22]. Evidence suggests a link between the activation of resting DCs and TNF. For example, coordinated activation of TLRs and prostaglandin receptors with TNF induces transcriptomes in DCs, monocytes, and macrophages reminiscent of chronic inflammatory states [23]. Moreover, deficiency in the X-linked inhibitor of apoptosis protein has been shown to restrain TNF-driven intestinal inflammation and dysbiosis by enhancing innate immune responses of Paneth and DCs [24]. In renal ischemia–reperfusion injury, resident DCs are primary TNF-secreting cells in the early stages [25]. These findings underscore the pivotal role of TNF in DC activation, which may contribute to the development of DN. There were substantial evidences to suggest the role of innate immunity in the DN-related inflammatory pathways [26]. During inflammation, toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) endogenous ligand and high mobility group box 1 (HMGB1) were released into the extracellular fluid by dendritic cells, macrophages, and necrotic cells. TLR4 and HMGB1 levels have been seen in the renal tissues and serum of people with DN [27, 28]. Furthermore, in patients with DN, renal fibrosis and albuminuria were significantly connected with the development and activation of macrophages in the kidneys [29].

In our study, GSEA based on TNF expression revealed a significant enrichment of GAG degradation in the high-TNF subgroup. This suggests that TNF may activate the GAG degradation pathway in the development of DN. Immunohistochemical staining of renal tissues validated the bioinformatic results. Diabetes mellitus can affect the metabolism of various extracellular matrix components, including GAGs. Previous research has shown that treatment with GAG formulations improved experimental DN outcomes [30]. Furthermore, Anna et al. found that urinary GAG excretion positively correlated with the duration of DN, suggesting that assessing GAG excretion values could be useful for detecting early stages of DN and monitoring disease progression [31]. Although studies on TNF-activated GAG degradation have primarily focused on osteoarthritis [32, 33], our findings warrant further investigation into TNF-mediated GAG degradation in DN.

Recent advancements in single-cell RNA sequencing have further illuminated the role of TNF in the renal microenvironment of diabetic nephropathy. Studies utilizing this technology have demonstrated that TNF expression is notably elevated in specific kidney cell populations, including mesangial cells and podocytes, contributing to inflammatory and fibrotic responses [34, 35]. These findings align with our observations of TNF's involvement in immune cell interactions and GAG degradation pathways. Additionally, the application of CRISPR-Cas9 gene editing to selectively knock out TNF in renal cells has shown promise in reducing inflammation and preserving renal function in diabetic mouse models [36]. This innovative approach highlights the potential of targeted TNF therapies in mitigating DN progression.

Further exploration into the mechanistic pathways of TNF in DN, including its interactions with other cytokines and growth factors, could reveal new therapeutic targets. For example, the interplay between TNF and IL-6 has been implicated in the exacerbation of renal inflammation and fibrosis [37]. Investigating the crosstalk between these signaling molecules might offer insights into combinatory therapeutic strategies. Moreover, the role of epigenetic modifications in regulating TNF expression and activity presents another promising avenue for research [38]. Epigenetic interventions, such as DNA methylation inhibitors or histone deacetylase inhibitors, have shown efficacy in modulating inflammatory responses in DN [39]. Integrating these approaches with conventional anti-TNF therapies could enhance treatment outcomes for patients with DN.

Nevertheless, our study has several limitations. Firstly, based on bioinformatics analysis, we established TNF as a key extracellular proteins in DN, however, it was not an innovative finding, because previous studies have also found an important role for TNF in DN. Secondly, in the experimental verification section, we only did a HIC study, which was semi-quantitative, a animal model or cell line validation to analyze detailed signalling pathway or detailed biological process will be better. Thirdly, while the correlation between TNF and immune cell infiltration is compelling, the causal relationships and underlying mechanisms require further exploration. Future studies, including both animal model or cell line, should be conducted to elucidate these mechanisms and explore the therapeutic potential of targeting TNF in DN.

In conclusion, we identified five key EP-DEGs (TNF, FN1, MMP9, TGFB1, and COL1A1) as candidate biomarkers through bioanalysis. According to the ROC curve and Nephroseq v5 online platform, TNF demonstrated greater clinical significance in DN compared to the other four EP-DEGs. Through immune cell infiltration analysis and GSEA, we further explored the potential mechanism of TNF in DN, revealing its potential key role in DN development, potentially involving the activation of the GAG degradation pathway.
