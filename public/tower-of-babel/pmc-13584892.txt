A case of tic improvement following tipepidine administration in a boy with Tourette syndrome comorbid with attention‐deficit/hyperactivity disorder and autism spectrum disorder
Authors: Masumi Tachibana, Tsuyoshi Sasaki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTourette Syndrome (TS) is defined by motor and vocal tics that begin in childhood and persist for over a year. TS causes difficulties in daily life and lowers self‐esteem, yet pharmacological treatment options for TS remain limited. Tipepidine, administered in this case report, is generally used as a safe and inexpensive cough suppressant. Recent studies have suggested that tipepidine may improve symptoms of attention‐deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) and depression.Case PresentationWe report the clinical course of a boy with TS, ADHD, and autism spectrum disorder (ASD), in whom the addition of tipepidine to aripiprazole was associated with improvement in both motor and vocal tics. The patient had tried several antipsychotics before he was prescribed tipepidine. However, the effectiveness of those drugs was temporary, and the patient experienced tolerability issues such as drowsiness. The addition of tipepidine was well tolerated, and the improvement in tic symptoms was maintained during long‐term follow‐up.ConclusionThis case suggests that adjunctive tipepidine may be well tolerated and associated with sustained improvement in motor and vocal tics during long‐term follow‐up in a patient with TS receiving aripiprazole.

BACKGROUND

Tourette syndrome (TS) is defined by motor and vocal tics. It has a high rate of comorbidity. 1 , 2 Its tic symptoms and concomitant symptoms are diverse and change with growth. 1

Tipepidine is used widely as a safe and inexpensive non‐opioid cough suppressant. In recent years, tipepidine has been found to have antidepressant effects as well as effects on attention‐deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD). 3 , 4 , 5 The pharmacological effects of tipepidine have been reported to include the inhibition of G protein‐activated inwardly rectifying potassium (GIRK) channels, which are involved in the metabolism of dependent substances and consequently the reward system, and the activation of adenosine monophosphate‐activated protein kinase (AMPK), which is involved in feeding behavior and energy metabolism. 6

There have been few reports to date on the long‐term course of TS, and almost no reports on the efficacy or long‐term prognosis of its pharmacotherapy.

In this report, we describe the clinical course of a boy with TS comorbid with ADHD and ASD in whom the addition of tipepidine to ongoing aripiprazole treatment was associated with improvement in motor and vocal tics (Tables 1 and 2).

CASE PRESENTATION

The patient was a 6‐year‐old boy. There were no abnormalities during the perinatal period. He started walking at 10 months, and his first word was at one and a half years of age. From early childhood, he did not cling to his mother or seek affection. He preferred stereotypical play involving stacking blocks or plates. He had his own specific way of doing things and would panic if his method was corrected to a “better” one. He persistently pulled on other children and was unable to interact with them properly. His hearing was normal, but he sometimes failed to notice when spoken to while engaged in an activity. Around age 3, he began exhibiting a throat‐clearing‐like tic. Gradually, a vocal tic producing a “hic” sound developed. In kindergarten, other children disliked this vocal tic, leading him to refuse to go to school. When excited, he would emit strange cries, run around, throw sand at other children, and otherwise struggle to interact appropriately. During this time, his mother had repeatedly consulted the pediatrician during visits for physical ailments, but no therapeutic intervention was provided. When the patient started school, he was not able to sit still during class and would make strange noises when excited. Because his vocalizations made it impossible for him to make friends, his parents consulted the Ear, Nose, Throat department at the nearby medical center during a visit in February 2016, where they were referred to Chiba University Hospital. The patient was then seen at Chiba University Hospital in August 2016. During the examination, vocal tics like “ah,” “uh,” and “huh” were constantly present, along with frequent motor tics such as clicking his tongue, grimacing and then relaxing his face, and shrugging his shoulders.

Intellectual evaluation of the patient was conducted by a certified assessor using the Japanese version of the Wechsler Intelligence Scale for Children, Fourth Edition (WISC‐IV). We also evaluated the patient using the Autism‐Spectrum Quotient‐Children's Version (AQ‐Child) and the ADHD Rating Scale (ADHD‐RS). His tic symptoms were assessed using the Yale Global Tic Severity Scale (YGTSS). The YGTSS consists of the Total Tic Severity Score, Impairment Rating, and Total YGTSS Score. The patient's parents were interviewed using the Parent‐Interview ASD Rating Scale‐Text Revision (PARS‐TR). WISC‐IV (October 17, 2016) showed FSIQ128, VCI117, PRI136, WMI120, and PSI107. PARS‐TR (October 19, 2016) yielded a total Early Childhood peak score of 29, with Sociality, 4; Communication, 8; Obsession, 4; Stereotyped behavior, 0; Difficulties, 9; and Hypersensitivity, 4; and a total Childhood score of 21, with Sociality, 3; Communication, 8; Obsession, 6; Stereotyped behavior, 1; Difficulties, 1; and Hypersensitivity, 2. Although the patient was able to remain seated during the examination, he was restless throughout, fidgeting with his body and clothes, and kicking his legs. At school, he was constantly moving during class and would repeatedly stomp his feet. He was extremely irritable; at home, he would spit at his parents and younger sister, and at school, his irritability made it impossible for him to pay attention in class. He would hit his own arms or make a commotion even during class. Furthermore, due to his high impulsivity, he would frequently run out of the classroom at the slightest sign of something he did not like. Based on these observations, the patient's total ADHD‐RS score was set at 46, with both Inattentive and hyper/impulsive subscores of 23.

We diagnosed the patient with TS, ADHD, and ASD based on these results together with a life history showing lack of imagination, difficulties in communication, obsession, hyperactivity, and hyper‐impulsivity.

Initial treatment with aripiprazole 1.5 mg/day was started for TS in November 2016. Aripiprazole was initially effective for the patient's tic symptoms, but its effects diminished within a few months. However, discontinuing aripiprazole caused his ADHD symptoms to worsen. It was decided to maintain the aripiprazole dose between 1.5 and 6 mg/day. In May 2018, the patient participated in a clinical trial at Chiba University Hospital to evaluate the efficacy of tipepidine for ASD. He received 30 mg/day of tipepidine for 2 weeks as an initial short‐term treatment. While no changes were observed in his ASD symptoms, a clear effect was evident on both motor and vocal tics during this period. After the 2‐week trial ended and tipepidine administration ceased, the tic symptoms returned to their original level. Other TS medications were subsequently tried, including perospirone 8 mg/day in July 2019 and risperidone 2 mg/day from October to December 2019, but neither was effective. Therefore, after explaining to the mother and the patient that tipepidine was an off‐label drug and obtaining their consent, and after submitting an ethics review application to Chiba University Hospital and receiving approval, we initiated long‐term adjunctive treatment with tipepidine at 30 mg/day in November 2020. At that time, the patient's total YGTSS score was 86 (total tic severity score, 46; impairment rating, 40). After 1 month at this dose, the patient's total YGTSS score had decreased to 61 (total tic severity score, 31; impairment rating, 30). We then increased the dosage to 60 mg/day, and the patient's total YGTSS score decreased to 29 (total tic severity score, 19; impairment rating, 10) by April 2021.

From May to October 2021, the patient's total YGTSS score temporarily worsened from 44 to 69. This was likely due to stress caused by the patient not understanding the tested material on his first regular exam after entering junior high school, taking time to adjust to a new homeroom teacher, and experiencing difficulties in friendships. His symptoms later improved and stabilized following collaborative environmental adjustments with the school.

When aripiprazole was discontinued in September 2022, the patient's inattention and hyperactivity worsened markedly despite continued treatment with tipepidine. Consequently, aripiprazole was resumed. Additionally, when he self‐discontinued tipepidine for 3 weeks between September and October 2022, his total YGTSS score remained at 47 (total tic severity score, 22; impairment rating, 25), but then improved to 24 (total tic severity score, 14; impairment rating, 10) after tipepidine was resumed. At the last, the beneficial effect of tipepidine on tic symptoms remained consistent.

DISCUSSION

Since the efficacy of haloperidol for TS was first demonstrated in 1961, pharmacotherapy has played an important role in the management of tic disorders. However, it is now widely recognized that pharmacological treatment is not always necessary or effective, as tic symptoms often improve spontaneously over time. Accordingly, current treatment strategies emphasize careful assessment before initiating medication.

The American Academy of Neurology (AAN), the European Society for the Study of Tourette Syndrome (ESSTS), and the Japanese Society of Child Neurology (JSCN) have each published clinical practice guidelines for the management of TS. 7 , 8 Despite some differences in their recommendations, these guidelines consistently emphasize assessing the natural course of tic symptoms and the degree of functional impairment before initiating pharmacotherapy. They also recommend providing psychoeducation for patients and their families and considering behavioral therapy, environmental modifications, and pharmacological treatment according to symptom severity and functional impairment.

Regarding pharmacological treatment, the AAN guideline recommends α2‐adrenergic agonists, botulinum toxin injections, topiramate, and cannabis‐based medications as therapeutic options. The ESSTS pharmacotherapy guideline recommends aripiprazole, risperidone, and tiapride as first‐line agents, in that order. In patients with comorbid ADHD, clonidine and guanfacine are considered appropriate treatment options because they may improve both ADHD and tic symptoms. For treatment‐resistant cases, haloperidol, pimozide, topiramate, botulinum toxin injections, and cannabinoid‐based medications are recommended. Similarly, the JSCN guideline strongly recommends aripiprazole and risperidone for the pharmacological treatment of tic disorders (strong recommendation, moderate‐certainty evidence) and weakly recommends guanfacine or atomoxetine for patients with both ADHD and tic symptoms (weak recommendation, low‐certainty evidence). In the present case, because the patient had substantial functional impairment, pharmacotherapy was initiated while simultaneously providing education to promote understanding of TS.

Additionally, the patient received aripiprazole throughout most of the treatment period, although it was temporarily discontinued in September 2022.

Although its beneficial effect on tic symptoms diminished after several months, aripiprazole was continued because its discontinuation resulted in worsening of the patient's ADHD symptoms. Previous studies evaluating the efficacy of tipepidine for ADHD have also included patients receiving aripiprazole, in whom adjunctive treatment with tipepidine was associated with improvements in ADHD symptom scores. These findings suggest that tipepidine may exert an adjunctive effect when used in combination with aripiprazole.

Tics most commonly develop between the ages of 4 and 6 years, and the initial presentation is often as simple motor tics. Typically, simple vocal tics emerge 1–2 years after the onset of motor tics, and both motor and vocal tics tend to become more complex with increasing age. Tic severity often peaks between the ages of 10 and 12 years, when complex tics are more likely to be observed, after which tics frequently show spontaneous improvement. In this patient, tic symptoms improved following the initiation of oral tipepidine in combination with aripiprazole in 2020 and remained improved during the subsequent follow‐up period. Temporary worsening occurred after discontinuation of aripiprazole and after self‐discontinuation of tipepidine, suggesting that both medications may have contributed to symptom control. However, because tic symptoms naturally fluctuate over time and often improve with age, it remains difficult to distinguish the effects of treatment from the natural course of the disorder.

Tipepidine, used in this case, is a non‐opioid cough suppressant widely used for treating colds. 9 Common side effects include loss of appetite (1.1%) and constipation (0.5%). 10 Rare side effects include allergy and delirium. 11 Thus, it is considered a relatively safe and inexpensive drug with no fatal side effects. In recent years, in addition to its antitussive effects, its antidepressant properties and efficacy for ADHD have garnered attention. 3 , 4 , 12 In a double‐blind study involving humans, the administration of tipepidine as an adjunct therapy to the antidepressant citalopram demonstrated antidepressant effects. 5 Its pharmacological action involves AMPK activation, similar to antidepressants such as atomoxetine and fluvoxamine 13 as well as stimulation of dopamine D1 receptors and adrenergic α2 receptors, and an increase in extracellular dopamine levels in the nucleus accumbens (NAc). 6 , 14 The NAc is involved in switching actions and controlling all movements, and is implicated in ADHD hyperactivity, impulsivity, and vocal and motor tics. 15 In a 4‐week open‐label trial using tipepidine, ADHD‐RS scores (total score, impulsivity, and inattention) improved. 3 Furthermore, a primate model capable of reproducing vocal tics by exciting the NAc was created. Positron emission tomography (PET) measurements were taken, and electrophysiological recordings of neural activity in the NAc, anterior cingulate cortex, and primary motor cortex regions involved in vocalization (particularly the oromandibular area) revealed that vocal tics occurred when neural activity in these regions synchronized. 16 This result suggests the potential for deep brain stimulation therapy for TS. However, if similar effects can be achieved with the oral administration of tipepidine, which is generally well tolerated, it may represent a promising therapeutic option for patients with TS. Although these proposed mechanisms of action may contribute to the pharmacological effects of tipepidine, it is not possible to determine which mechanism is primarily responsible. Furthermore, an apparent paradox exists in that conventional antipsychotics exert their therapeutic effects by blocking dopamine receptors, whereas tipepidine has been reported to increase dopamine levels. One possible explanation is that maintenance of dopaminergic homeostasis, rather than simple dopamine receptor blockade or dopamine enhancement, may be important. Thus, modulation of dopaminergic balance may contribute to the improvement of tic symptoms. However, the precise mechanisms by which these effects influence tic symptoms remain unclear. This report has several limitations. First, this case report describes the administration of tipepidine in one patient. Second, it should be noted that, since this case involved comorbid ASD and ADHD symptoms, the effect of tipepidine on TS alone remains unclear. Third, because the patient received combination therapy with aripiprazole and tipepidine, the contribution of tipepidine alone cannot be clearly determined, and a synergistic or pharmacodynamic interaction between the two agents cannot be excluded. Further prospective studies involving larger cohorts are warranted to clarify the efficacy, safety, and mechanisms underlying the therapeutic effects of tipepidine in patients with TS.

CONCLUSION

This case suggests that adjunctive tipepidine may be a well‐tolerated and potentially effective therapeutic option for improving motor and vocal tics in selected patients with TS receiving aripiprazole.
