Adding artificial intelligence to the pulmonary embolism response team improves time to diagnosis and treatment of pulmonary embolism
Authors: Emily Austin, Adam Reichard, Patrick Muck, Angela Fellner
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThere is limited data demonstrating the benefit of artificial intelligence (AI) technology for the diagnosis and triage of pulmonary embolism (PE). This study aimed to demonstrate improved time to diagnosis of PE and subsequent anticoagulation and intervention, with the goal of reducing in-hospital mortality. We hypothesized that implementation of AI-assisted computed tomography pulmonary angiogram (CTPA) detection would reduce time to diagnosis, anticoagulation, and intervention compared with the standard radiology-based workflow.MethodsA single institution retrospective review from July 2018 to March 2025 was performed to identify patients diagnosed with PE who underwent pulmonary angiogram with mechanical thrombectomy and/or thrombolytics. Patients were divided into a pre-AI cohort (July 2018-2022) and a post-AI cohort (2022-March 2025) corresponding to the institutional implementation of Viz.ai PE (Viz.ai), a Food and Drug Administration-cleared, Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act-compliant AI platform for automated PE detection on CTPA. Time to diagnosis was defined as the interval from CTPA scan completion to AI-generated alert (post-AI cohort) or to the final radiology report issuance (pre-AI cohort). Time to anticoagulation and intervention was measured from the time of confirmed PE diagnosis. In-hospital mortality was also evaluated.ResultsFrom July 2018 to March 2025, a total of 148 patients were diagnosed with PE and underwent endovascular intervention. Twenty-four patients were excluded. Forty-two patients were diagnosed in the pre-AI era and 82 in the post-AI era. The median age was 65 [interquartile range (IQR), 53-73] and 65.5 (IQR, 52-73.5) years, respectively. Time to diagnosis improved significantly from 72.5 (IQR, 44.5-93.3) to 40 (IQR, 28-68.2) minutes (P = .00005). Time to anticoagulation was 76 (IQR, 48.3-99.8) vs 61.5 (IQR, 46.8-110) minutes (P = .824, not significant). Time to intervention improved from 1360 (IQR, 1075.5-1790.2) to 1224 (IQR, 601.6-1563) minutes (P = .036). Two in-hospital deaths occurred, both in the pre-AI cohort.ConclusionsImplementation of AI-assisted CTPA detection using Viz.ai PE significantly improved time to diagnosis and intervention in patients with acute PE requiring catheter-directed therapy. Time to anticoagulation was not significantly different between groups; this was study was insufficiently powered to detect differences in clinical outcomes including mortality.Keywords: Pulmonary embolism, Artificial intelligence, Anticoagulation, Mechanical thrombectomy, PERT, Viz.ai

Pulmonary embolism (PE) is defined as an obstruction in the pulmonary artery secondary to clot, tumor, air, or fat.1 PE is the third most common cause of death among hospitalized patients and the third leading cause of cardiovascular death behind myocardial infarction and stroke.1,2 Risk factors for venous thromboembolism (VTE) include prolonged immobilization, postoperative state, recent hospitalization, obesity, and active cancer. A previous episode of VTE increases the risk of recurrent VTE with an incident of 11.2% within 2 weeks of initial diagnosis despite adequate anticoagulation.1

Computed tomographic pulmonary angiography (CTPA) is the imaging modality of choice for diagnosis of PE, allowing evaluation of the pulmonary arteries to the subsegmental level. Laboratory biomarkers including troponin and brain natriuretic peptide and echocardiography, in addition to CTPA, are used to risk stratify patients into low, intermediate, or high risk. Right ventricular dysfunction on echocardiography, an enlarged right ventricle (RV) defined as a RV-to-left ventricular (LV) ratio ≥0.9 on CTPA, and elevated plasma troponins levels are associated with a worse prognosis.3 The mainstay of treatment for PE is anticoagulation, typically with low-molecular-weight heparin or unfractionated heparin initially.4 In intermediate- and high-risk patients, treatment modalities include percutaneous catheter-directed therapies, surgical embolectomy, and systemic thrombolysis.

There is an annual incidence for PE ranging from 39 to 115 per 100,000 population.3 The Center for Disease Control and Prevention estimates 60,000 to 100,000 deaths/year from PE in the United States.4 Early recognition and treatment reduces the risk of death.2 Pulmonary embolism response teams (PERTs) provide multidisciplinary, time-sensitive management of acute PE.1 With the use of artificial intelligence (AI) technology, PERT evaluation and initiation of therapy may be expedited.5 AI technology has been studied most extensively in acute ischemic stroke, where its use has improved time to treatment, 5-day National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale scores, and 90-day modified Rankin Scale scores.6 However, there is limited data demonstrating the benefit of AI technology in the workflow diagnosis and triage of PE.

In the present study, we evaluated our institution’s experience using AI technology in the diagnosis and treatment of PE. We hypothesized that implementation of AI-assisted CTPA detection would reduce time to diagnosis, anticoagulation, and intervention compared with standard radiology workflow.

Methods

The Institutional Review Board approved this study and waived the requirement for patient-informed consent. A total of 148 patients were identified from July 2018 to March 2025 who were diagnosed with PE and underwent pulmonary angiogram with mechanical thrombectomy and/or thrombolytics. Patients were divided into a pre-AI cohort (before 2022) and a post-AI cohort (2022-2025) based on the institutional implementation of Viz.ai PE (Viz.ai, Inc). The time to diagnosis, time to anticoagulation, time to intervention, and in hospital mortality were evaluated between the two groups.

AI technology description

Viz.ai PE (Viz.ai, Inc) is a Food and Drug Administration 510(k)-cleared, Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act-compliant AI software platform for automated detection of PE on CTPA. The platform uses a deep learning algorithm trained on CTPA imaging to automatically analyze scans for filling defects consistent with PE as they are completed, without requiring manual routing or radiologist pre-review. Upon detection of a probable PE, the algorithm calculates the RV/LV ratio from the axial CTPA images (see Figs 1 and 2).

A real-time push notification is sent via the Viz.ai mobile application to all enrolled care team members – including vascular surgery, interventional radiology, pulmonology, cardiology, and emergency medicine – simultaneously and before finalization of the radiology report. The notification includes access to the CTPA images and pertinent laboratory values within the platform. Any enrolled practitioner can initiate a PERT alert directly through the application. The platform also facilitates real-time multidisciplinary discussion regarding anticoagulation, systemic thrombolysis, and catheter-directed therapy (see Fig 3).

Pre-AI notification workflow

Before AI implementation, CTPA scans were read in standard radiology workflow order. Upon finalization of the radiology report, the ordering physician received an electronic notification through the electronic medical record. PERT activation required individual provider outreach via pager or telephone. There was no automated mechanism for simultaneous multidisciplinary notification before report finalization.

AI oversight and physician review

All CTPA scans flagged by the AI algorithm were subsequently reviewed and confirmed by an attending radiologist before definitive treatment decisions. Cases, in which the treating physician did not proceed with intervention following an AI alert were not included in the study.

Patient population

Age, sex, location of PE, risk stratification of PE, RV/LV ratio, and VTE risk factors including sedentary lifestyle, recent long-distance travel, obesity, previous VTE, history of cancer, recent surgery, thrombophilia, and hormone replacement therapy were evaluated. Twenty-four patients were excluded because of contraindication to immediate anticoagulation, imaging delay, or initiation of anticoagulation before imaging confirmation. Of these excluded patients, 13 were in the pre-AI cohort and 11 were in the post-AI cohort. Three patients in the pre-AI cohort and five in the post-AI cohort had a contraindication to immediate anticoagulation; two patients in each cohort had an imaging delay; and eight patients in the pre-AI cohort and four in the post-AI cohort were initiated on anticoagulation before imaging confirmation.

All patients were started on unfractionated heparin or low-molecular-weight heparin once the diagnosis was confirmed. Risk stratification into intermediate-high- or high-risk was performed using cardiac biomarkers, RV/LV ratio on CTPA, and echocardiographic evidence of right ventricular strain. Once intervention was determined to be appropriate, patients underwent catheter-based pulmonary angiogram via common femoral or internal jugular venous access under ultrasound guidance. Mechanical thrombectomy and/or catheter-directed thrombolysis were performed based on clot burden and clinical status.

Time metric definitions

Time to diagnosis was defined as the interval from CTPA scan completion to the Viz.ai AI alert in the post-AI cohort and the final radiology report issuance in the pre-AI cohort. Time to anticoagulation was defined as the interval from confirmed diagnosis to administration of the first dose of anticoagulation. Time to intervention was defined as the interval from confirmed diagnosis to venous puncture in the angiography suite. Within the pre-AI cohort, confirmed diagnosis was defined by the final radiology report. In the post-AI cohort, confirmed diagnosis was defined by the Viz.ai alert, which is the time of evaluation of the computed tomography scan by a member of the PERT team.

Statistical analysis

Since continuous data were not normally distributed, median and interquartile range (IQR) were used to describe the variables. The Mann-Whitney U-test was used for nonparametric comparisons.

Results

Of the 148 patients who were diagnosed with PE and underwent endovascular intervention, 42 patients were included in the pre-AI cohort and 82 in the post-AI cohort. Twenty-four patients were excluded because of contraindication to immediate anticoagulation, delay in imaging, or initiation of anticoagulation before confirmation of PE with imaging.

The median (IQR) age in the pre-AI and post-AI cohort were 65 (53, 73) and 65.5 (52, 73.5) years, respectively. Baseline VTE risk factors differed between cohorts, with higher rates of sedentary lifestyle (47.6% vs 21.4%), obesity (63.4% vs 47.6%), and lower rates of active cancer (15.9% vs 33.3%) in the post-AI cohort.

Risk stratification was similar between cohorts: 92.9% intermediate-high and 7.1% high-risk in the pre-AI cohort; 92.7% intermediate-high and 7.3% high-risk in the post-AI cohort. Centrally located thrombus was present in 97.6% and 93.9% of patients, respectively. RV/LV ratio >1.5 was present in 64.3% (pre-AI) and 69.5% (post-AI) of patients.

The median (IQR) time to diagnosis was 72.5 (44.5-93.3) minutes in the pre-AI cohort and 40 (28-68.2) minutes in the post-AI cohort (P = .00005). Time to anticoagulation was 76 (48.3-99.8) minutes in the pre-AI cohort and 61.5 (46.8-110) minutes in the post-AI cohort (P = .824). Time to intervention was 1360 (1075.5-1790.2) minutes in the pre-AI cohort and 1224 (601.6-1563) minutes in the post-AI cohort (P = .036). Two in-hospital deaths occurred, both in the pre-AI cohort.

Discussion

This single-center retrospective study demonstrates that implementation of Viz.ai PE AI technology within a PERT-integrated workflow was associated with a significant reduction in time to diagnosis and intervention in patients with acute PE requiring catheter-directed therapy. These findings are consistent with and extend our institution’s previous published experience using AI technology in the detection and management of PE, in which we demonstrated a shorter time to PERT consult in AI-evaluated patients.5

The time to diagnosis in the pre-AI cohort was 72.5 minutes compared with 40 minutes in the post-AI cohort. Kline et al7 demonstrated that delayed PE diagnosis is independently associated with adverse in-hospital outcomes, supporting the clinical importance of the diagnostic improvement observed here. With AI technology, the CTPA can be evaluated by the PERT team simultaneously with – and in some cases before – the radiologist’s final read, which directly compresses the time from imaging to multidisciplinary awareness. This improved awareness then shifts responsibility to the members of the PERT team to initiate treatment including anticoagulation and determining the need for intervention, which can be done in an expedited fashion compared with the workflow in the pre-AI cohort when consultants had to wait for a diagnosis from a radiologist to be involved in the care of the patient.

Although time to anticoagulation was shorter in the post-AI cohort (61.5 vs 76 minutes), this difference did not reach statistical significance (P = .824). This finding warrants direct discussion rather than being minimized.

This study was insufficiently powered to detect a statistically significant difference in time to anticoagulation. The observed numerical trend may reflect a real effect that is obscured by sample size, or alternatively, anticoagulation timing at our institution may already be optimized by existing protocols independently of AI diagnosis. Smith et al8 demonstrated that earlier anticoagulation is associated with reduced in-hospital and 30-day mortality; Soh et al9 confirmed this relationship specifically in high-risk PE. Future studies should prospectively evaluate whether AI-driven earlier diagnosis translates to statistically significant improvements in anticoagulation timing.

There were two in-hospital deaths in the pre-AI cohort and none in the post-AI cohort. This difference is hypothesis-generating only. With only two deaths in total, this study is profoundly underpowered to support any causal inference regarding AI and mortality. Both deaths occurred in the high-risk PE subgroup of the pre-AI cohort; the temporal difference in extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) utilization patters between eras may also have contributed to the mortality difference independently of AI. Larger prospective studies are needed to evaluate the impact of AI-assisted PE management on clinical outcomes including mortality.

In the post-AI cohort, three of four patients cannulated for ECMO were high-risk. Benfor et al10 demonstrated that aspiration thrombectomy can precipitate cardiovascular collapse in high-risk patients and that ECMO can be used as a salvage strategy. Our practice of performing ECMO evaluation before endovascular intervention in high-risk and intermediate-high-risk patients with concerning echocardiographic findings reflects this evidence and likely contributed to the absence of intraoperative mortality in the post-AI cohort, independent of AI implementation.

Limitations

The study has several important limitations. First, this is a single-center retrospective review, limiting generalizability of the findings. Second, and critically, there is significant potential for temporal confounding. The pre-AI cohort spans July 2018 through 2022, whereas the post-AI cohort spans 2022 through March 2025. Over this interval, institutional PERT maturity increased, operator experience with catheter-directed therapy grew, and management protocols evolved. These changes are independent of AI implementation and may have contributed to improved time metrics in the post-AI era. We cannot separate the effect of AI from concurrent improvements in institutional PE care infrastructure.

Third, the cohort is limited to patients who underwent pulmonary angiogram with mechanical thrombectomy and/or thrombolytics – that is, intermediate-high and high-risk patients judged to require intervention. Lower-risk patients managed conservatively are not represented, and results should not be generalized to the broader PE population.

Fourth, baseline characteristics differed between cohorts, including higher rates of obesity and sedentary lifestyle and lower rates of active cancer in the post-AI group. No multivariable adjustment was performed, and it is possible that these differences confounded time metric comparisons.

Fifth, the in-hospital mortality analysis is underpowered. With only two deaths, no meaningful statistical comparison is possible, and the observed mortality difference should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating only.

Sixth, the exclusion of 24 patients is not fully characterized in terms of distribution between time periods. If exclusions were unequally distributed, this could introduce selection bias.

Conclusions

Implementation of Viz.ai PE AI technology within a PERT-integrated workflow was associated with significant reduction in time to diagnosis and time to intervention for patients with acute PE requiring catheter-directed therapy. Time to anticoagulation was not statistically different between groups; this study was insufficiently powered to detect differences in clinical outcomes including mortality. These findings contribute to the growing evidence based supporting AI-assisted workflow in acute PE management and highlight the need for larger prospective studies to evaluate the impact of AI on clinical outcomes.
