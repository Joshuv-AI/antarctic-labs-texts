Celecoxib adjunct to antidepressants for major depressive disorder: Systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Manit Srisurapanont, Chawisa Suradom, Sirijit Suttajit
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized, placebo-controlled trials evaluated the efficacy, acceptability, and tolerability of celecoxib as an adjunct to antidepressants for major depressive disorder (MDD). PubMed, Embase, the Cochrane Library, Google Scholar, and ClinicalTrials.gov were searched from inception to April 11, 2026. The primary outcome was change in depressive symptom severity. Secondary outcomes included all-cause dropouts and adverse-effect dropouts. The risk of bias in trials was assessed using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 tool. Standardized mean differences (SMDs) and risk ratios (RRs) were pooled using a random-effects model. Evidence certainty was rated using the Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development, and Evaluations (GRADE) approach. Seven trials were included (N = 365; 183 received celecoxib, 182 received placebo). Most trials administered celecoxib 400 mg/day for 6 weeks in patients without treatment-resistant depression. Adjunctive celecoxib significantly reduced depressive symptom severity compared with placebo (SMD = −0.63, 95% CI: −1.25 to −0.01; I2 = 81.3%; 7 trials). All-cause dropouts (RR = 0.77; 95% CI: 0.14 to 4.07; I2 = 57.6%; 5 trials) and adverse-effect dropouts (RR = 0.83; 95% CI: 0.19 to 3.64; I2 = 16.9%; 5 trials) did not differ between groups. The certainty of evidence was very low for efficacy, low for acceptability, and moderate for tolerability. The antidepressant efficacy of adjunctive celecoxib remains highly uncertain due to high risk of bias in several trials, substantial heterogeneity of reported outcomes, and small sample sizes. However, celecoxib adjunctive therapy appears acceptable and likely well tolerated over the short-term treatment.

Introduction

First-line antidepressants, such as selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) and serotonin-norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors (SNRIs), frequently fail to achieve remission and often leave residual depressive symptoms. In the Sequenced Treatment Alternatives to Relieve Depression (STAR∗D) trial, only 30% of patients with major depressive disorder (MDD) achieved remission following initial SSRI treatment. Although the cumulative remission rate increased to approximately 67% after four sequential steps, relapse remained common (Rush et al., 2006). Even among responders, residual symptoms, particularly cognitive symptoms and anhedonia, often persist (McClintock et al., 2010). Nonresponse, incomplete remission, and residual symptoms are associated with substantial functional disability and increased healthcare costs (Johnston et al., 2019). Collectively, these findings highlight the ongoing challenges in improving treatment response, achieving sustained remission, and reducing residual symptoms in MDD.

Evidence from the past two decades implicated immune dysregulation in major depressive disorder (MDD). Inflammatory markers, including C-reactive protein (CRP) and interleukin-6 (IL-6), correlate with an increased risk of depression, poor treatment response, and illness chronicity (Arteaga-Henríquez et al., 2019; Strawbridge et al., 2017; Vogelzangs et al., 2014). Consequently, interest in repurposing anti-inflammatory agents—such as minocycline, celecoxib, omega-3, and aspirin—as antidepressant treatments has increased (Kittur et al., 2022; Simon et al., 2023). A meta-analysis of celecoxib across diverse populations, including those with MDD, bipolar disorder, and cancer-related depression, also demonstrated its moderate treatment effect (Wang et al., 2022).

Celecoxib, an anti-inflammatory agent that selectively inhibits cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2), has been investigated as an adjunctive treatment for MDD. Its proposed antidepressant mechanisms include attenuation of neuroinflammation through reduced prostaglandin E2 synthesis and downstream proinflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL-6 and tumor necrosis factor-α), potentially facilitating restoration of monoaminergic neurotransmission (Dantzer et al., 2008; Miller and Raison, 2016). In addition, celecoxib may inhibit activation of the indoleamine-2,3-dioxygenase (IDO)-mediated tryptophan-kynurenine pathway, thereby limiting the production of neurotoxic metabolites implicated in depression (Dantzer et al., 2008).

Despite supportive findings from several systematic reviews and meta-analyses (SRs/MAs), celecoxib has not been recommended as a routine treatment for MDD. An early randomized trial suggested that adjunctive celecoxib enhanced the antidepressant efficacy of reboxetine (Müller et al., 2006). Subsequent SRs/MAs reported higher odds of response or remission and greater symptom reduction with adjunctive celecoxib (Gędek et al., 2023; Na et al., 2014). Although celecoxib has a strong potential antidepressant effect (Zhang et al., 2025), recent reviews consistently conclude that no clear recommendation can be made for this immunomodulatory treatment for depression (Baune and Fromme, 2025; Simon et al., 2023).

The discrepancy between SR/MA findings and experts’ recommendations may reflect limits in the existing evidence base. Earlier SRs/MAs by Köhler et al. (2014) and Na et al. (2014) included only four trials, all published by 2012. In contrast, large randomized controlled trials (RCTs) published thereafter reported negative results (Baune et al., 2021; Simon et al., 2021). Although Gędek et al. (2023) reported a large treatment effect, their analysis included only four trials and did not incorporate more recent RCTs (Sakhvidi et al., 2024; Simon et al., 2021). To date, no systematic review has comprehensively incorporated recent large RCTs while also formally grading the certainty of evidence, limiting confidence in existing conclusions.

To address this knowledge gap, we conducted an SR/MA of RCTs evaluating celecoxib as an adjunct to antidepressants in adults with MDD. Adjunctive celecoxib was compared with adjunctive placebo for efficacy, acceptability, and tolerability. Potential moderators were also explored, including celecoxib dose, study duration, and TRD status.

Methods

Protocol registration and reporting standards

The protocol for this SR/MA was registered prospectively in the Open Science Framework (OSF) registry (https://osf.io/bptcg/). This report adheres to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 guideline (Page et al., 2021).

Eligibility criteria

RCTs were eligible if they met the following criteria: (i) Population—participants diagnosed with MDD using a standard diagnostic classification; (ii) Intervention—celecoxib as an adjunct to antidepressants; (iii) Comparator—placebo as an adjunct to antidepressants; (iv) Outcomes—depressive symptom severity, all-cause dropouts, and adverse-effect dropouts.

Trials were excluded if they met any of the following criteria: (i) Population—participants with depressive symptoms secondary to bipolar disorder or somatic disorders; (ii) Intervention—celecoxib used as an adjunct to treatments other than antidepressants (e.g., electroconvulsive therapy, cognitive-behavioral therapy); and (iii) Study design—crossover trials.

Search, trial selection, and data collection

PubMed, Embase, and the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL) were searched from inception to April 11, 2026. The search strategy combined the terms “((celecoxib) OR (coxib) OR (cox-2 inhibitor) OR (celebrex)), (depress∗ OR mood), and (random∗)”. ClinicalTrials.gov was also searched for complete trials with posted results evaluating celecoxib for depression, depressive disorder, or depressive symptoms. For Google Scholar, screening was limited to the first 200 records. No restrictions were imposed on language or publication dates. Full search strategies are provided in SMethods 1.

After duplicate removal, two reviewers (MS and CS) independently screened titles and abstracts, assessed full-text eligibility, and extracted data. Disagreements were resolved through discussion.

Data items and risk of bias assessment

The primary outcome was efficacy, measured as the mean change from baseline in depressive symptom severity. Secondary outcomes were acceptability and tolerability, defined as all-cause dropouts and adverse-effect dropouts, respectively.

The following trial characteristics were extracted: first author, publication year, study location, prior nonresponse to antidepressants, sample size per arm, trial duration, celecoxib dose, mean age, proportion of female participants, concomitant antidepressant, and depression rating scale.

The following data were extracted for each treatment arm: (i) the number of randomized participants; (ii) the number of all-cause dropouts; (iii) the number of dropouts due to adverse effects; (iv) the means at baseline and final assessment, along with the mean change from baseline; and (v) the standard deviations (SDs) at both baseline and final assessment, as well as the SDs for the change scores. When reported, the trial-reported mean changes were used; otherwise, they were calculated as the final mean minus the baseline mean. For trials with multiple assessment time points, the assessment closest to the end of treatment was selected. For outcomes reported only graphically, values were extracted using WebPlotDigitizer (https://automeris.io/).

Standard errors (SEs) or 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were converted to SDs. Study authors were contacted to obtain missing SDs. When unavailable, change-score SDs were imputed using the ImputeSD (.) function from the MKInfer R package (version 1.2) (Kohl, 2024), which implements methods recommended in the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions (Higgins et al., 2019). This approach first estimated a correlation coefficient from trials reporting SDs at baseline, final assessment, and change scores, followed by using this coefficient to impute change-score SDs for trials reporting only baseline and final SDs.

MS and SS independently assessed risk of bias using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 (RoB 2) tool (Sterne et al., 2019). RoB 2 evaluates five domains: (i) randomization process, (ii) deviations from intended interventions, (iii) missing outcome data, (iv) outcome measurement, and (v) selection of the reported results. The overall risk of bias was determined using the RoB 2 algorithm: low if all domains were rated low risk; some concerns if at least 1 domain raised some concerns and none were high; and high if at least 1 domain was rated high risk or multiple domains raised some concerns. Disagreements were resolved through discussion.

Effect measures, synthesis methods, and reporting bias

Standardized mean differences (SMDs) were used to quantify between-group effects, defined as the difference in mean change in depression symptom severity between celecoxib and placebo, both administered as adjuncts to antidepressants. All depression rating scales were oriented such that higher scores indicated greater symptom severity. SMDs were calculated as Hedges' g, with negative values favoring celecoxib, and were interpreted using Cohen's thresholds: 0.2 (small), 0.5 (medium), and 0.8 (large) (Cohen, 1992). All-cause and adverse-effect dropouts were expressed as risk ratios (RR).

SMDs were pooled using a frequentist random-effects model with inverse-variance weighting and restricted maximum likelihood (REML) estimation. RRs were pooled using a random-effects Mantel-Haenszel method, with a continuity correction of 0.5 applied to zero cells. The random effects models incorporated the Hartung-Knapp adjustment with an ad hoc correction, specifically the Hartung-Knapp-Sidik-Jonkman (HKSJ) approach (IntHout et al., 2014). Prediction intervals (PIs) were calculated using the t-distribution. All analyses and plots were conducted in R (version 4.5) using the meta package (version 8.2) (Balduzzi et al., 2019; R Core Team, 2025).

Statistical heterogeneity was quantified using I2. Values of 50–75% indicated substantial heterogeneity, and values > 75% indicated considerable heterogeneity (Higgins et al., 2003). Prespecified subgroup analyses were conducted according to celecoxib dose (≤200 mg/day vs > 200 mg/day), study duration (<8 vs ≥ 8 weeks), and TRD status (yes/no). Robustness was assessed through sensitivity analysis excluding trials at high risk of bias. Reporting bias, including small-study effects and publication bias, was assessed using funnel plot asymmetry. Egger's test was conducted when ≥10 trials were available (Higgins et al., 2019).

Assessing the certainty of results

MS and CS independently assessed the certainty of evidence using the Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development, and Evaluations (GRADE) approach (Balshem et al., 2011). Evidence certainty was initially rated as high because only RCTs were included.

Five GRADE domains were assessed. Certainty was downgraded by one or two levels based on seriousness or likelihood using the following scheme: not serious/unlikely = 0; serious/likely = −1; very serious/very likely = −2. Prespecified downgrading criteria were as follows: (i) risk of bias: 25–50% of data derived from high-risk trials = serious, >50% = very serious; (ii) inconsistency: I2 of 50–75% = serious, I2 > 75% = very serious; (iii) indirectness: 25–50% of data obtained from patients without MDD, >50% = very serious; (iv) imprecision: total sample size <800 = serious; and (v) publication bias: funnel plot asymmetry or statistical tests suggesting possible bias = likely, marked bias = very likely. Imprecision was assessed in accordance with GRADE guidance (Guyatt et al., 2025), using a total sample size of <800 as a rule-of-thumb alternative to optimal information size. Very serious imprecision was not applied because no outcome jointly assessed benefits and harms. Each outcome was rated as high, moderate, low, or very low. Disagreements were resolved by discussion. GRADE guidance for informative statements was used to communicate findings according to the certainty of evidence (Santesso et al., 2020).

After excluding 1096 records, 20 full-text articles plus 2 ClinicalTrials.gov records were assessed. Fifteen articles did not meet the inclusion criteria (STable 1). Seven trials were included in the meta-analysis (Abbasi et al., 2012; Akhondzadeh et al., 2009; Baune et al., 2021; Majd et al., 2015; Müller et al., 2006; Sakhvidi et al., 2024; Simon et al., 2021).Fig. 1The PRISMA flow diagram presents the identified records, excluded records, included articles, and included trials in the systematic review and meta-analysis of celecoxib adjunctive to antidepressants for major depressive disorder.

Three trials used sertraline (50-200 mg/day) as the background antidepressant; one trial each used escitalopram (10 mg/day), fluoxetine (40 mg/day), reboxetine (4-10 mg/day), or vortioxetine (20 mg/day). All trials, except one (Sakhvidi et al., 2024), had washout periods at least 3 days or started study medications in drug-naïve participants. Celecoxib was administered at 400 mg/day in all trials except one, which used 200 mg/day (Majd et al., 2015). Two trials had a duration of 8 weeks (Majd et al., 2015; Sakhvidi et al., 2024), while the remaining trials lasted 6 weeks.

The medians [interquartile ranges, IQRs] of participant characteristics were as follows: mean age, 35.3 years [34.6 – 41.7]; proportion of female participants, 50.0% [45.4% - 57.1%]. Five trials assessed depressive symptom severity using the Hamilton Rating Scale for Depression (HAM-D), while two others used the Montgomery–Asberg Depression Rating Scale (MADRS). Baune et al. (2021) reported MADRS change scores, whereas Simon et al. (2021) reported the percentage change in MADRS scores.

In the meta-analysis of depressive symptoms, three of seven trials did not report change-score SDs. For two trials (Majd et al., 2015; Müller et al., 2006), we extracted change-score SDs from a previous meta-analysis in which the authors had obtained these data directly from the original investigators (Na et al., 2014). For the remaining trial (Sakhvidi et al., 2024), missing SD were imputed using the ImputeSD function from the MKinfer R package.

Risk of bias

Among the seven trials included in the meta-analysis of depressive symptoms, three were judged to be at high risk of bias due to missing outcome data (SFig. 1A). Müller et al. (2006) experienced substantial attrition, with 55% of participants discontinuing by week 6. Akhondzadeh et al. (2009) and Sakhvidi et al. (2024) did not report dropout data, which may have inflated estimates of treatment efficacy. Simon et al. (2021) changed the depression outcome measure during the trial, raising additional concerns. Overall, 42.9% of data were at high risk of bias, and 14.3% had some concerns (SFig. 1B).

Akhondzadeh et al. (2009) and Sakhvidi et al. (2024) were excluded from the dropout analyses because dropout outcomes were not reported. Consequently, five trials contributed to the meta-analyses of all-cause dropouts and adverse-effect dropouts (SFig. 2A). Müller et al. (2006) was judged to be at high risk of bias, resulting in 20% of dropout data being classified as high risk (SFig. 2B).

Results of individual trials

Celecoxib, when used as an adjunct to antidepressants, significantly reduced depressive symptoms compared with placebo in four of the seven trials (Abbasi et al., 2012; Akhondzadeh et al., 2009; Müller et al., 2006; Sakhvidi et al., 2024). The remaining three trials found no significant between-group differences (Baune et al., 2021; Majd et al., 2015; Simon et al., 2021).

In five trials reporting dropout outcomes (Abbasi et al., 2012; Baune et al., 2021; Majd et al., 2015; Müller et al., 2006; Simon et al., 2021), there were no significant differences between celecoxib and placebo in either all-cause or adverse-effect dropouts.

Results of syntheses

Data were synthesized to compare celecoxib and placebo as an adjunct to antidepressants in patients with MDD.

Depressive symptom reduction

Celecoxib significantly outperformed placebo in reducing depressive symptom severity (SMD = −0.63, 95% CI: −1.25 to −0.01, I2 = 81.3%, 7 trials) (Fig. 2). However, the 95% PI was wide and crossed the null value (−2.23 to 0.97). Subgroup analysis by TRD status suggested significantly lower efficacy in TRD samples (χ2 = 4.33, p = 0.04). Subgroup analyses by dose (χ2 = 0.04, p = 0.84) and duration (χ2 = 1.45, p = 0.23) showed no significant differences. All-cause dropouts

There was no significant difference between celecoxib and placebo for all-cause dropouts (RR = 0.77, 95% CI: 0.14 to 4.07, I2 = 57.6%, 5 trials) (Fig. 3A). In addition, the 95% PI was wide and crossed the null value (0.04 to 14.63). Subgroup analyses by dose and duration were identical because only one trial used celecoxib 200 mg/day for 8 weeks. These analyses showed significantly fewer dropouts with celecoxib 200 mg/day for 8 weeks compared with 400 mg/day for 6 weeks (χ2 = 4.12, p = 0.04) (Fig. 3A and SFig. 5A). Adverse-effect dropouts

One trial reported zero adverse-effect dropout events in both arms (Abbasi et al., 2012) (Fig. 3B). Overall, celecoxib and placebo did not differ significantly (RR = 0.83; 95% CI: 0.19 to 3.64, I2 = 16.9%, 5 trials). In addition, the 95% PI crossed the null value (0.21 to 3.19). As with all-cause dropouts, subgroup analyses by dose and duration were identical and showed no significant difference (χ2 = 2.55, p = 0.11) (Fig. 3B and SFig. 5A). Subgroup analysis by TRD status was also not significant (χ2 = 0.04, p = 0.84) (SFig. 5B).

Reporting bias

Visual inspection of funnel plots revealed no obvious asymmetry for any outcomes (Fig. 4). Based on percentage of participants without MDD: <25% = Not serious, 25%-50% = Serious, >50% Very serious.

Based on total sample size: ≥800 = Not serious, <800 = Serious.

Efficacy

Evidence certainty for depressive symptom reduction was downgraded one level for serious risk of bias (42.9% of data from high-risk-of-bias trials) and two levels for very serious inconsistency (I2 = 81.3%). Combined with one-level downgrading for imprecision, certainty was expected to decrease by four levels. However, it was downgraded by only three levels, reaching the very lowest grade.

Acceptability

Evidence certainty for all-cause dropouts showed no serious risk of bias (20% high-risk-of-bias trials). It was downgraded one level for serious inconsistency (I2 = 57.6%). Together with one-level downgrading due to the serious imprecision, this certainty decreased by two levels from high to low.

Tolerability

Evidence certainty for adverse-effect dropouts had no serious risk of bias (20% high-risk-of-bias trials) or inconsistency (I2 = 16.9%). Together with one-level downgrading due to the serious imprecision, this certainty decreased by one level, from high to moderate.

Discussion

Main findings

This SR/MA includes seven randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trials evaluating celecoxib as an adjunct to antidepressants in MDD. Adjunctive celecoxib was associated with a reduction in depressive symptom severity compared to placebo. However, the evidence certainty regarding efficacy was very low due to a high risk of bias in several trials, very high heterogeneity of reported outcomes, and small sample sizes. In addition, the observed efficacy was nonsignificant after exclusion of trials with a high risk of bias. Taken together, the antidepressant efficacy of adjunctive celecoxib remains highly uncertain. However, celecoxib adjunctive therapy appears acceptable and likely well tolerated over the short-term treatment.

Comparison with prior research

The present findings are broadly consistent with earlier SRs/MAs but present a more cautious interpretation of efficacy. Previous meta-analyses with fewer included trials reported markedly optimistic results, including a 6.5-fold increase in response and remission rates with adjunctive celecoxib (Na et al., 2014). A recent review reported a large effect size for overall evidence (SMD = −0.85) and a substantial effect size in patients without TRD (SMD = −1.12) (Gędek et al., 2023). In contrast, the present meta-analysis yielded smaller effect estimates: a medium effect size for overall evidence (SMD = −0.63) and a large effect size in patients without TRD (SMD = −0.75).

Variations in included trials likely explain the differing results between Gędek et al. (2023) and the present study. Gędek et al. (2023) rated Simon et al. (2021) as having a high-risk of bias in outcome measurement and excluded it from their meta-analysis. In contrast, this SR/MA considered Simon et al. (2021) as having low risk of bias in outcome measurement because: (i) it was appropriate to measure outcomes (depression) using MADRS; (ii) no difference of outcome measures between the intervention and the control groups; and (iii) the double-blind design ensured that outcome assessors were masked to participant interventions. The inclusion of negative results from Simon et al. (2021) resulted in a lower SMD in the present study than in Gędek et al. (2023).

Two factors likely explain the discrepancies of those efficacy results. First, the present meta-analysis included more trials, notably one clearly negative trial (Simon et al., 2021), which attenuated pooled estimates. Second, it employed the REML method with HKSJ approach rather than the DerSimonian and Laird (DL) approach to estimate between-study heterogeneity. REML is currently recommended because it provides more accurate heterogeneity estimates, particularly when heterogeneity is substantial or the number of studies is small (Tanriver-Ayder et al., 2021). The DL method is the traditional approach but frequently underestimates uncertainty, leading to narrow CIs and inflated false-positive rates, particularly when few studies are included. In contrast, the HKSJ approach utilizes a t-distribution to produce more conservative and reliable CIs by better accounting for the estimation error of between-study heterogeneity (IntHout et al., 2014). Consequently, the present results likely offer a more conservative and methodologically robust reflection of the current evidence base.

The present findings for acceptability and tolerability were consistent with prior meta-analyses. Similar to an earlier meta-analysis (Na et al., 2014), this study found no significant difference in all-cause dropouts between celecoxib and placebo. However, these findings are limited by short study durations (predominantly 6 weeks), high heterogeneity, and small sample sizes. This SR/MA excluded trials of depressed patients with physical illness. Because this study omitted participants susceptible to adverse effects, the results may overestimate treatment acceptability and tolerability.

The findings of subgroup analyses by celecoxib doses, study duration, and TRD status should be interpreted cautiously. The subgroup finding suggesting fewer all-cause dropouts with celecoxib 200 mg/day for 8 weeks compared with 400 mg/day for 6 weeks was driven by a single trial (n = 23) using a lower dose and longer duration. The results related to TRD status were also drawn from one TRD trial, in which 76% of the participants had TRD.

Mechanisms and result interpretation

To our knowledge, this is the first meta-analysis to explore potential moderators of celecoxib efficacy, acceptability, and tolerability in patients with TRD. Adjunctive celecoxib appeared less efficacious in TRD; however, this finding was based on a single trial in which 76% of participants met criteria for TRD (Baune et al., 2021). Accordingly, the role of celecoxib in TRD remains unclear. Neither dose nor study duration significantly moderated efficacy. The observed higher acceptability with celecoxib 200 mg/day for 8 weeks may reflect improved tolerability at lower doses, but additional trials using this regimen—particularly in TRD populations and with longer follow-up—are needed to substantiate this hypothesis.

This is also the first SR/MA of adjunctive celecoxib that formally applies the GRADE framework. Unlike earlier reviews that focused primarily on pooled effect sizes and reached optimistic conclusions, the GRADE assessment in this study identified multiple serious and very serious limitations affecting efficacy outcomes, including high risk of bias in included trials, very high heterogeneity of reported outcomes, and small sample sizes. These limitations downgraded the evidence certainty from high to very low. The sensitivity analysis excluding high-risk-of-bias trials yielded nonsignificant efficacy, suggesting that the antidepressant effects of adjunctive celecoxib remains highly uncertain.

Including participants without high inflammation may introduce heterogeneity and affect the results of this SR/MA. Approximately 27% of patients with depression exhibit low-grade inflammation (e.g., CRP >3 mg/L) (Osimo et al., 2019). However, only one trial in this SR/MA stratified participants by pro-inflammatory markers using 3 mg/L CRP cutoff) (Baune et al., 2021). While a lack of stratification may mask the efficacy of anti-inflammatory treatments (Miller and Raison, 2023), studies using this method have yielded mixed results. A recent meta-analysis found that anti-inflammatory treatments reduced depressive symptoms but did not improve response or remission rates (Mac Giollabhui et al., 2026). For celecoxib in MDD, baseline CRP levels did not predict treatment response (Kavakbasi et al., 2024). Furthermore, clinic trials have not confirmed the association between celecoxib response and inflammatory parameters observed in preclinical studies (Gędek et al., 2023).

Growing interest in the inflammatory MDD subtype has prompted several studies on the association between depression and inflammation (Jha et al., 2025). These include those of the Advanced Stratification of People with Depression Based on Inflammation (ASPIRE) consortium, which focuses on this association, and the ongoing studies of INflammation-based Stratification for Immune-Targeted Augmentation in Major Depressive Disorder (INSTA-MD) and Treating Immuno-metabolic Depression With Anti-inflammatory Drugs (INFLAMED) (Wessa et al., 2024; Worrell et al., 2026; Zwiep et al., 2023). Findings from these studies will clarify the link between depression and inflammation, including the efficacy of anti-inflammatory drugs such as celecoxib in depressed patients with low-grade inflammation.

Potential drug interactions between celecoxib and antidepressants warrant clinical attention. Although celecoxib is primarily a CYP2C9 substrate, it inhibits CYP2D6 (Gong et al., 2012). This enzyme metabolizes several antidepressants, such as fluoxetine and escitalopram (Austin-Zimmerman et al., 2021). Furthermore, CYP2D6 inhibition may decrease sertraline metabolism in patients with high CYP2D6 activity (Sproule et al., 1997). This inhibition suggests that celecoxib may increased the serum levels of certain antidepressants, potentially enhancing the antidepressant effects of these combinations.

This SR/MA provided 95% PIs, which indicate the expected results in future samples (Contini et al., 2024) While the CI reflects the precision of the pooled effect size, the PI incorporates both pooled effect uncertainty and potential heterogeneity between new and existing studies (Al Amer and Lin, 2021). Although the 95% CI of the present SMD of efficacy (−1.25 to −0.01) did not cross the null value, its corresponding 95% PI (−2.23 to 0.97) was wide and crossed the null. This PI result further supports the high uncertainty of treatment efficacy.

Implications for practice and research

Given the very low confidence in its efficacy, current data do not support the routine clinical use of celecoxib as an adjunct to antidepressants in adults with MDD. If considered, its use should align closely with the characteristics of the existing evidence base—namely, patients without TRD, a dose of 400 mg/day, and a treatment duration of approximately 6 weeks. Extrapolation to other populations, dosing regimens, or longer treatment durations should be avoided. Moreover, tolerability was assessed primarily through short-term adverse-effect dropout rates; broader adverse-effect profiles remain insufficiently characterized. Clinicians should therefore interpret any potential symptomatic benefit with caution, and current evidence does not justify patient-specific or prescriptive recommendations.

Future research should address several key gaps. First, adequately powered, multicenter RCTs with prespecified endpoints and intention-to-treat analyses are needed to confirm efficacy across diverse clinical settings. Second, patient selection strategies should incorporate stratification by TRD status and prespecify inflammatory biomarkers, such as C-reactive protein and cytokine profiles, to evaluate whether inflammatory phenotypes moderate treatment response. Third, studies should directly compare doses (e.g., 200 vs. 400 mg/day), extend treatment durations (≥8–12 weeks), and standardize background antidepressant therapy to reduce heterogeneity. Fourth, trials should systematically collect adverse events and patient-reported outcomes and assess longer-term endpoints, including remission and relapse. Finally, transparent reporting of outcome data, rigorous handling of missing data, adherence monitoring, preregistration, and protocol sharing are essential to reduce bias and enhance reproducibility.

Limitations

This SR/MA has several limitations. First, risk of bias was frequently high, primarily due to missing outcome data, which may inflate efficacy estimates if attrition or outcome reporting favored celecoxib. Second, heterogeneity in efficacy was substantial (I2 = 80.4%), likely reflecting differences in patient populations (TRD vs. non-TRD), background antidepressant use, outcome measures (HAM-D vs. MADRS), celecoxib dosage, study duration, and geographic location. This heterogeneity reduces confidence in the pooled estimate and limits clinical application. Third, small sample sizes (n = 365 for efficacy, n = 265 for acceptability and tolerability) and short follow-up periods (mostly 6 weeks) further increase uncertainty regarding both benefits and harms. Fourth, three trials did not report change-score SDs, necessitating imputation or data extraction from secondary sources. Although methodologically appropriate, this imputation may have affected variance estimates. Fifth, most included trials were conducted in a limited number of countries, restricting generalizability. Finally, subgroup analyses were exploratory and underpowered, which should be considered hypothesis-generating.

Conclusions

The antidepressant efficacy of adjunctive celecoxib remains highly uncertain due to high risk of bias in several trials, substantial heterogeneity of reported outcomes, and small sample sizes. The sensitivity analysis excluding high-risk-of-bias trials yielded nonsignificant efficacy, confirming the high uncertainty of the efficacy. However, celecoxib adjunctive therapy appears acceptable and likely well tolerated over the short-term treatment. Well-designed, adequately powered, biomarker-informed randomized trials with longer follow-up are required before adjunctive celecoxib can be recommended for routine treatment of MDD.
