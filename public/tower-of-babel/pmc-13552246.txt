Preservation of positive emotion recognition in patients with multiple sclerosis: Artefact related to methodological issues or real feature of the disorder?
Authors: Laurent Zikos, Antonio Pinti, Henrique Sequeira, Cécile Donze, Arnaud Kwiatkowski, Béatrice Degraeve, Bruno Lenne
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPrior studies in multiple sclerosis (MS) suggest preserved recognition of positive emotions despite deficits for negative ones, but this dissociation may reflect methodological limitations (valence‐asymmetry: positive‐valence being limited to happiness/joy in basic‐emotion sets). This study tested whether emotion–recognition deficits in MS are valence‐specific or more generalized using dynamic stimuli and reduced valence‐asymmetry, assessed across two sensory modalities. Exploratory analyses also examined cognitive status and correlations with cognitive/affective measures. Fifty relapsing–remitting persons with MS (pwMS) and 50 matched controls completed an emotion–recognition task involving dynamic audio and video stimuli. The task assessed recognition of all six basic emotions and 14 complex emotions. Cognitive and affective measures were collected; exploratory analyses examined cognitive status and associations with these variables. PwMS showed significantly lower recognition accuracy across multiple emotions compared to controls. In the video condition, group differences were stronger for negative emotions (OR = .62, p = .001), whereas no significant group difference was observed for positive emotions. Only a single significant deficit (anger: OR = .52, p = .027) was found in the audio condition. Video subgroup analyses indicated reduced recognition in cognitively impaired pwMS for selected items, whereas pwMS without cognitive impairment did not differ significantly from controls. Findings support a predominant vulnerability for negative emotion–recognition in MS. In the absence of a significant group‐level difference, the frequently reported preservation of positive emotion–recognition cannot be attributed to methodological artefacts alone. More broadly, individual patient characteristics, including cognitive functioning, may contribute to heterogeneity in emotion–recognition performance in MS.

INTRODUCTION

Multiple sclerosis (MS) is a chronic inflammatory and neurodegenerative disease of the central nervous system, characterized by multifocal demyelination and axonal loss (Margoni et al., 2023), and represents the leading cause of non‐traumatic neurological disability in young adults (Noseworthy et al., 2000). MS is associated with a wide and unpredictable range of symptoms, including motor, visual, neuropsychiatric and cognitive impairments (Chiaravalloti & DeLuca, 2008). These manifestations often result in substantial functional consequences, notably in social functioning and employment (Lancia et al., 2024).

Cognitive dysfunction affects approximately 40%–70% of people with multiple sclerosis (pwMS) and can occur at all stages and across all disease subtypes (Langdon, 2011; Prakash et al., 2008). The most frequently reported deficits involve information processing speed, episodic memory and executive functions (Piacentini et al., 2023) and contribute significantly to daily functional limitations (Jansa et al., 2022).

Beyond cognitive impairment, evidence converges in demonstrating the presence of emotion–recognition impairments in MS (Roheger et al., 2024). Notably, several studies have reported a dissociation between impaired recognition of negative emotions and preserved recognition of positive emotions (Henry et al., 2011, 2017; Lenne et al., 2014; Prochnow et al., 2011; Sever Aktuna et al., 2024), not only in advanced stages of the disease (Henry et al., 2017) but also in early MS (Lenne et al., 2014). Collectively, these findings have led to the widespread notion that emotion–recognition difficulties in MS predominantly affect negative emotions, whereas positive emotion–recognition appears relatively preserved.

Importantly, the emotional stimuli used in most facial emotion–recognition tasks derive from Ekman's model of basic universal emotions, which distinguishes happiness as the sole positive category among several negative emotions (Ekman, 1992). Although this theoretical framework has strongly shaped task construction in the field, Bora et al. (2016) pointed out that such designs may introduce an asymmetry in task difficulty across emotional valence. Indeed, commonly used emotion–recognition paradigms (such as the Ekman 60‐Faces Test) rely on static facial photographs and forced‐choice formats in which recognizing a stimulus as positive typically leads directly to a single response option, whereas recognizing a negative stimulus requires further discrimination among several competing alternatives. This structural asymmetry mechanically reduces task‐difficulty for positive emotion–recognition (Frank & Stennett, 2001) and may contribute to the apparent ‘preservation’ of positive emotion–recognition observed in pwMS. This effect may be further amplified by the high perceptual salience of happiness, which is characterized by distinctive and relatively unambiguous facial features (e.g., the smile), in contrast to the greater feature overlap observed among negative emotions (Calvo & Beltrán, 2013).

Consistent with this interpretation, happiness is generally recognized more rapidly and more accurately than other emotions, is rarely confused with alternative categories, and frequently reaches ceiling levels in healthy participants (Palermo & Coltheart, 2004). Similar patterns have been reported across multiple neurological and psychiatric conditions, including Parkinson's disease, Alzheimer's disease and schizophrenia, where happiness recognition often appears relatively preserved compared with negative emotions (Argaud et al., 2018; Goodkind et al., 2015; Johnston et al., 2006). Importantly, several authors have cautioned against interpreting this apparent preservation as evidence of selective positive‐emotion sparing, emphasizing instead the potential contribution of forced‐choice task structure and ceiling effects to observed valence‐specific patterns. In line with this view, Goodkind et al. (2015) demonstrated that previously reported advantages for positive emotion–recognition in neurodegenerative disorders were substantially attenuated when task design asymmetries were reduced, supporting a methodological rather than functional interpretation.

In addition to valence imbalance, other methodological characteristics may further influence performance patterns. Emotion–recognition beyond facial expressions has received comparatively limited attention in MS, despite evidence of impairments in affective prosody and body language processing (Beatty et al., 2003; Cecchetto et al., 2014; Kraemer et al., 2013). Auditory emotion–recognition, in particular, is generally more demanding than facial emotion–recognition (Adolphs, 2002) and may reveal deficits that remain undetected in visually based tasks.

Moreover, the predominant use of static stimuli raises concerns regarding ecological validity. Posed facial expressions provide exaggerated and temporally frozen emotional signals that only partially reflect the dynamic and multimodal nature of real‐life social interactions. In MS, emotion–recognition has been almost exclusively assessed using static facial material, with very few studies employing dynamic stimuli. To date, only one study (Phillips et al., 2011) has compared static and dynamic facial expressions in pwMS (i.e., video clips), without examining the role of emotional valence.

Taken together, previous research in MS has largely relied on unimodal, static emotion–recognition tasks that include several negative emotions but only a single positive one. These combined factors introduce an asymmetrical level of difficulty across emotional valence, making it unclear whether the preserved recognition of positive emotions reported in earlier studies reflects a genuine feature of MS or a methodological artefact. The present study aimed to clarify this issue by assessing emotion–recognition in pwMS by using complementary visual and auditory paradigms and expanding the set of positively valenced categories to reduce forced‐choice valence asymmetry. By doing so, we sought to determine whether the commonly reported preservation of positive emotion–recognition persists under conditions that reduce task‐design artefacts and limit ceiling effects. Because positive and negative facial expressions rely on partly distinct neurocognitive systems (with negative expressions more consistently engaging salience/threat‐related circuits, including amygdala–insula, and positive expressions more consistently engaging reward/valuation circuits, including ventral striatum–orbitofrontal regions, Adolphs, 2002), testing valence effects under improved methodological conditions is necessary to determine whether reported dissociations in MS reflect task structure or differential neural vulnerability.

Specifically, we examined recognition performance for visual and auditory emotional stimuli in pwMS and matched healthy controls, including six basic emotions and 14 complex emotions. Our primary objective was to determine whether emotion–recognition difficulties in MS reflect a valence‐specific impairment or a more generalized deficit that has been obscured by task design in previous studies. To our knowledge, no previous study in MS has examined valence effects using a paradigm that includes complex emotions (i.e., emotions beyond Ekman's six basic categories). A secondary objective was to examine whether emotion–recognition performance is associated with cognitive and affective functioning in patients.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study design and procedure

The experiment was conducted in a single session including (a) a clinical and demographic assessment, (b) an emotion–recognition task and (c) cognitive and affective assessments. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants after full explanation of the procedures. The study was promoted by GHICL and ethical approval was obtained from the French committee Ile de France XI (RC‐P00107, 2020‐A03312‐37).

The study protocol, including hypotheses, inclusion criteria and planned analyses, was preregistered on the Open Science Framework prior to data collection (https://osf.io/gv9q5).

Clinical and demographic assessment

Demographic and clinical data were collected through a structured interview and included age, sex, years of education, laterality and medical history. For pwMS, disease subtype, disease duration, current disease‐modifying treatment and date of the most recent relapse were recorded. Physical disability was assessed using the Expanded Disability Status Scale (EDSS). These variables were used to characterize the sample and verify inclusion and exclusion criteria.

Emotion–recognition task

Emotion–recognition was assessed using two types of nonverbal dynamic stimuli: musical audio excerpts and facial video clips.

The auditory task consisted of 10 randomized musical excerpts selected from Vuoskoski and Eerola (2011), expressing four basic emotions (anger, fear, happiness and sadness; two excerpts per emotion) as well as two neutral stimuli.

The visual task comprised 42 randomized dynamic facial expressions drawn from O'Reilly et al. (2016), depicting six basic emotions (anger, fear, happiness, sadness, disgust and surprise; two exemplars per emotion) and 14 complex emotions (boredom, deception, excitement, frustration, hurt, interest, jealousy, joking, kindness, pride, shame, sneakiness, unfriendliness and worry; two exemplars per emotion), in addition to two neutral stimuli. To discourage response strategies, three additional videos were included as fillers, for which the correct response was ‘None of the proposed labels’.

All 45 video clips (duration: 4–8 s) featured a single actor filmed from the shoulders up and conveyed no semantic or verbal information.

Following each stimulus, participants identified the expressed emotion using the forced‐choice recognition procedure described by O'Reilly et al. (2016). The primary outcome measure was emotion–recognition accuracy. A detailed description of task structure and procedures is provided in Data S1 (Supplementary Methods; Figure S1).

Cognitive and affective assessment

Cognitive functioning was assessed using the Brief International Cognitive Assessment for Multiple Sclerosis (BICAMS; Benedict et al., 2012), including measures of verbal learning and memory, visuospatial learning and memory and information processing speed. To complement the BICAMS and better capture executive functioning, additional tests assessed attention, cognitive flexibility, inhibitory control and verbal fluency. In addition, cognitive reserve was assessed using The Cognitive Reserve Index questionnaire (CRIq; Nucci et al., 2012), a standardized tool designed based on lifetime educational attainment, occupational complexity and leisure activities. It provides a global score and domain‐specific indices reflecting the contribution of life experiences to cognitive resilience.

Depressive symptoms, anxiety and alexithymia were assessed using validated self‐report questionnaires (BDI‐FS, STAI and TAS‐20; Benedict et al., 2003; Degraeve et al., 2025; Kvaal et al., 2005; Loas et al., 1996). Cognitive and affective measures were included to examine their contribution to performance on the emotion–recognition task. A detailed description of these measures is provided in Data S1.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were performed using R. Group differences in demographic, cognitive, and affective variables were assessed using Student's t‐tests or Mann–Whitney U tests, as appropriate. Emotion–recognition responses were coded as correct or incorrect for each stimulus and analysed using logistic mixed effect models with a logit link function, fitted with the lme4 package (Bates et al., 2015). Effect sizes are reported as odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals. Associations between emotion–recognition performance and cognitive measures were examined using point–biserial correlations and are reported in Data S1.

RESULTS

Participants

A total of 50 pwMS (86% female; mean age = 43.0 years, SD = 8.19) and 50 healthy controls (HC; 88% female; mean age = 41.7 years, SD = 8.57; see Table 1) participated in the study. Among pwMS, the mean disease duration since diagnosis was 9.6 years (SD = 6.48), and the median EDSS score was 2. All participants completed affective and cognitive assessments. Because musical emotion–recognition can be influenced by musical expertise, we recorded participants' musical background (practice and training; see Table 1). Exploratory binomial logistic regressions conducted for each auditory item showed no significant effect of musician status or music training on recognition accuracy (all p > .05). Overall, model fit was low across analyses (McFadden's R 2 = .001–.04), providing no evidence for a robust contribution of musical experience to auditory‐task performance. Moreoever, no significant associations were found between total emotion–recognition performance and clinical variables in the MS group (disease duration, time since last relapse and EDSS; see Table S11).

Finally, to formally test whether the relative difference between positive and negative emotion–recognition differed between groups, we fitted a logistic mixed effects model including Group (MS vs. controls), Valence (positive vs. negative) and their interaction, with random intercepts for participants and stimuli.

The model revealed a significant main effect of Group (OR = .54, 95% CI [.38, .77], p < .001), indicating overall lower recognition performance in pwMS. The main effect of Valence was not significant (OR = 1.36, 95% CI [.56, 3.32], p = .49). Importantly, the Group × Valence interaction did not reach statistical significance (OR = 1.35, 95% CI [.95, 1.91], p = .092), indicating no reliable evidence that the relative difference between positive and negative emotion–recognition differed between groups. Post hoc contrasts showed that pwMS performed significantly worse than controls for negative emotions (OR = 1.86, p < .001), whereas the group difference did not reach significance for positive emotions (OR = 1.38, p = .13).

DISCUSSION

This study aimed to determine whether the commonly reported preservation of positive emotion–recognition in pwMS reflects a distinctive feature of the disease or a methodological artefact related to stimulus design. Specifically, because forced‐choice basic‐emotion paradigms typically include only one positive response label (happiness/joy), valence‐based responding (i.e., identifying a stimulus as positive) can be sufficient to select the correct answer. By using dynamic stimuli that include a wider range of positive (and negative) emotions and response labels, we tested whether the commonly reported ‘positive sparing’ pattern persists when the positive response option is not mechanically advantaged by design. We also investigated whether deficits varied according to emotion complexity and sensory modality, and whether they were associated with cognitive or affective functioning in patients.

Negative emotion recognition in MS

Our findings indicate reduced recognition accuracy in the MS group for a subset of specific emotions, with significantly lower performance observed for disgust, deception, boredom and sneakiness, and a general trend towards impaired recognition across both basic and complex emotional expressions. Odds ratios for most emotions ranged from .4 to .7, meaning that pwMS had about 30%–60% lower odds of recognizing these emotions correctly compared to controls. These results are consistent with previous studies reporting selective impairments in the recognition of negative emotions in MS (Henry et al., 2017; Lenne et al., 2014; Lin et al., 2021; Roheger et al., 2024), particularly for basic emotions such as anger, fear or disgust.

Beyond accuracy deficits, our error pattern analysis revealed that pwMS frequently misclassified certain negative emotions with others: disgust was often confused with sadness (75%), whereas deception was primarily misinterpreted as worry (75%). Similarly, sneakiness was misidentified as other in 77.5% of cases, and boredom was often labelled as frustration (60%). Further, pwMS frequently selected the ‘other’ option when misclassifying certain negative emotions, notably for sneakiness (77.5%), deception (75%) or unfriendliness (40%).

Thus, rather than mistaking these expressions for a specific alternative emotion, patients often failed to identify them within the proposed response options. This tendency possibly indicates atypical perception or difficulty in mapping emotional cues onto conventional labels. This interpretation remains limited by the nature of the forced‐choice task: When participants selected ‘other’, we were unable to determine which emotion they perceived instead. This constitutes a methodological limitation and invites future studies to include open‐ended response options or post hoc verbal justifications.

Notably, error distributions in healthy controls were largely comparable to those observed in pwMS for several complex negative emotions (i.e., deception, sneakiness and unfriendliness), suggesting similar misclassification patterns across groups. However, this comparability did not extend to all categories: disgust, happiness and neutral expressions were associated with different response distributions between groups.

Valence asymmetries in emotion recognition in MS

To address our primary research question—whether the frequently reported preservation of positive emotion recognition in MS reflects a genuine feature or a methodological artefact—we examined group differences across a wide range of emotional expressions, including several positive emotions. Overall, our results indicate that emotion–recognition difficulties in MS primarily concern negative emotions, which show consistent vulnerabilities across analyses, modality and emotional categories. In contrast, recognition of positive emotions does not exhibit a clear or systematic impairment, despite the use of dynamic stimuli and a broader range of positive emotional expressions and response labels.

While methodological asymmetries in previous tasks represent a legitimate concern, correcting for these factors did not uncover additional or more pronounced deficits in positive emotion–recognition in our sample. Thus, the frequently reported preservation of positive emotions in MS cannot be fully dismissed as a simple methodological artefact.

Overall, these findings suggest that the absence of a clear positive deficit after methodological corrections cannot be accounted for by task‐related artefacts alone, and instead points to the possible contribution of additional cognitive or motivational processes shaping emotion–recognition in MS. Notably, performance for kindness was paradoxically enhanced among cognitively impaired patients (see Supplementary Results; Figure S2), an effect not observed in controls.

This inverse association may reflect a processing bias rather than preserved emotional functioning per se. Indeed, one plausible interpretative framework is provided by Socioemotional Selectivity Theory (SST), which posits that when individuals perceive their future as limited, attention and cognitive resources shift towards emotionally meaningful information, often resulting in a positivity bias in attention and memory (Carstensen, 2021; Mather & Carstensen, 2005; Reed et al., 2014). Importantly, SST is driven by subjective time perspective rather than chronological age. In MS, disease progression can be unpredictable and disability may accumulate, which may shape perceptions of future time in some individuals. Recent work (Zikos et al., 2024) suggests that such perceptions could promote a motivational shift towards positive emotional content, potentially earlier than in typical aging.

Such a motivational shift could also affect emotion–recognition. For example, Kellough and Knight (2012) demonstrated that a more limited future time perspective is associated with a positivity bias in facial‐emotion perception. A similar mechanism could therefore be consistent with the advantage observed here for kindness, particularly in the context of cognitive vulnerability. Converging evidence comes from the error profile for neutral expressions: unlike controls, pwMS more frequently misclassified neutral items as kindness (Table 3), consistent with a bias in categorical judgements towards more positively valenced labels.

Importantly, such a strategy may not be uniformly present across all patients but may depend on how each individual perceives their illness and future. Patients who experience a strong sense of life constraint or anticipate progressive loss (including cognitive decline) may be particularly prone to prioritize positive information and interpretations, in line with SST predictions, which could, in turn, manifest as improved recognition of positive emotional expressions and/or a tendency to make more positive judgements of ambiguous expressions (e.g., neutral faces). However, these relatively circumscribed effects provide only tentative support for SST and call for direct testing. Future studies should directly assess time perspective/motivational priorities and examine whether they moderate recognition of positive emotions in pwMS.

To sum up, the vulnerability observed for negative emotion–recognition, together with the absence of a clear positive deficit after methodological corrections, suggests that the observed pattern cannot be attributed to task‐related artefacts alone. Rather, it raises the possibility that context‐dependent cognitive or motivational factors may contribute to shaping the processing of positive emotional information in MS. Such influences may vary across individuals, depending on how patients perceive and experience their disease, potentially contributing to heterogeneity and obscuring group‐level effects for positive emotions. Further studies are needed to clarify the underlying mechanisms of these positivity‐related effects, as well as how they interact with emotion–recognition processes and broader aspects of social cognition in pwMS.

Basic versus complex emotion recognition

Beyond individual emotion categories, we investigated whether the observed deficits extended to broader emotional dimensions such as valence and complexity. Significant group differences were found for both basic and complex emotions, with odds ratios ranging from .59 to .65. This indicates that MS participants were approximately 35% to 40% less likely to correctly recognize these emotions compared to controls. Similarly, in the valence‐based analysis, odds ratios were consistently below 1 for all categories, with particularly strong effects observed for negative and neutral stimuli. These results suggest a broad pattern of reduced emotion–recognition accuracy in the MS group, which extended across both the complexity and valence dimensions. Even when not statistically significant, the direction and size of the effects point to a general underperformance relative to healthy controls. These findings extend previous work on emotion–recognition deficits in MS by demonstrating impairments not only for basic but also for complex emotional expressions (Cecchetto et al., 2014; Lenne et al., 2014).

Video versus audio emotion recognition

A surprising dissociation was observed between modalities for anger recognition. While pwMS with cognitive impairment showed reduced performance in the video condition, the opposite pattern emerged in the audio (musical) modality, where only pwMS without cognitive impairment showed significantly lower odds of correct identification. This discrepancy may reflect modality‐specific demands or statistical noise.

The distribution of incorrect responses for this emotion across both subgroups suggests that the dissociation in recognition accuracy may not reflect distinct emotional processing strategies, but rather a quantitative difference in performance. Indeed, this analysis indicated that, although the overall recognition accuracy differed between groups, the qualitative pattern of errors was rather similar. Both subgroups tend to confuse musical expressions of anger with fear, while very few errors involved confusion with more distant emotional categories such as sadness or neutrality. This suggests that the divergence in performance is quantitative rather than qualitative, and points to a general reduction in accuracy among patients without cognitive impairment, rather than a shift in response strategy.

Given the similar qualitative error profiles, it is possible that pwMS with early or subtle cognitive changes not captured by standard criteria already exhibit difficulties with musical emotion recognition. Further research is needed to determine whether the apparent dissociation in performance between cognitively impaired and non‐impaired pwMS reflects meaningful differences in emotional processing or is due to random variability or task‐related biases.

Relationship between cognition and emotion recognition

Regarding the relationship between cognition and emotion–recognition, our results suggest that processing speed, verbal memory and executive functioning (particularly cognitive flexibility) are associated with emotion recognition in pwMS across both the video and audio modalities. Specifically, executive functions were implicated across the board: Stroop performance (inhibition) was associated with recognition accuracy in the audio task, while the TMT index (cognitive flexibility) showed correlations in the video condition. Moreover, episodic memory played a role in both modalities, with visuospatial memory (BVMTR) contributing to performance in the audio task and verbal memory (CVLT‐II) in the video task. Processing speed (SDMT) and verbal fluency may be particularly relevant to emotion–recognition accuracy, especially for complex emotions. In line with this possibility, exploratory analyses in controls suggested positive associations between SDMT/verbal fluency and recognition accuracy for several emotions, including basic negative emotions and complex emotions (see Tables S3–S6). In this context, the lower SDMT and verbal fluency levels observed in pwMS may plausibly contribute to vulnerability to poorer emotion–recognition performance. However, none of these associations remained statistically significant after Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons, and these correlational patterns should therefore be interpreted cautiously.

Overall, the role of cognition in emotion–recognition difficulties in MS remains debated. Although some studies report links between cognitive efficiency and socio‐cognitive processing (Ciampi et al., 2018; Grothe et al., 2021; Henry et al., 2017), others find weak or inconsistent associations across samples and tasks (Batista et al., 2018; Golde et al., 2020; Pottgen et al., 2013). Such heterogeneity likely reflects differences in paradigms (static vs. dynamic; complexity and intensity of stimuli; response format), clinical characteristics (phenotype, disability level, fatigue and mood burden) and statistical power. Ongoing research protocols (Degraeve et al., 2024) are currently addressing this question and may help clarify the nature and extent of these associations.

Relationship between affective symptomatology and emotion recognition

In examining the relationship between affective symptomatology and emotion recognition in the audio task, we observed only a limited number of nominal associations. Higher trait anxiety was associated with reduced accuracy for anger‐related cues, in line with the idea that anxiety may interfere with the decoding of high‐arousal negative emotions. In contrast, both trait and state anxiety showed positive correlations with recognition of neutral items. Higher alexithymia scores were also associated with better recognition of some audio items (notably happiness‐related cues), with several additional trend‐level associations. However, none of these associations remained statistically significant after Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons (see Tables S9 and S10), and they should therefore be interpreted cautiously.

Limitations

Although the present study addresses several methodological gaps of prior research by employing an expanded set of positively valenced categories (beyond a single ‘happiness’ label) and complementary visual and auditory tasks, certain limitations should be acknowledged. First, although the study was adequately powered to detect the primary effects of interest (moderate group‐level effects), the sample size remains relatively modest, which may limit the statistical power to detect subtle effects, particularly in analyses involving subgroups or correlations with cognitive and affective variables. Additionally, our sample was restricted to individuals with relapsing–remitting MS (RR‐MS). This approach ensured clinical homogeneity but may limit the generalizability of our findings to other MS phenotypes or to broader, more diverse patient populations.

A further limitation is that the present protocol did not include dedicated controls to fully isolate alternative contributors to emotion–recognition performance, particularly affective symptomatology and more general face‐processing abilities. Although severe affective symptoms were excluded (with affective symptom levels remaining low on average in the MS group) and exploratory associations between affective measures and emotion–recognition performance did not remain significant after correction for multiple comparisons, a partial contribution of affective symptomatology cannot be entirely excluded. This remains relevant because depression and anxiety have been associated with reduced emotion–recognition accuracy (Demenescu et al., 2010) and a tendency to interpret ambiguous or neutral faces more negatively (e.g., Leppänen et al., 2004). Future studies would benefit from including a non‐MS clinical control group with depression and/or anxiety in order to better disentangle affective contributions from MS‐related neurological and cognitive factors.

Similarly, although prior MS studies suggest that emotion–recognition impairments may occur even when basic face‐processing abilities are preserved (Berneiser et al., 2014), the absence of a dedicated facial‐identity control task in the present protocol prevents us from definitively ruling out some contribution of more general face‐processing factors. Emotion–recognition studies would benefit from systematically including a control measure of basic face‐processing abilities in order to distinguish impairments in emotion decoding from more general facial perceptual difficulties.

Furthermore, the cross‐sectional design of this study precludes conclusions regarding the evolution of emotion–recognition deficits over the course of the disease or their potential responsiveness to intervention. Longitudinal research would be necessary to determine whether the observed deficits are stable, progressive or reversible.

Finally, although dynamic stimuli offer greater ecological validity than static, they may still not fully capture the complexity of real‐life social interactions. Future studies could benefit from incorporating more naturalistic or interactive paradigms.

CONCLUSION

Using dynamic stimuli in complementary visual and auditory tasks, and a broader set of positively valenced categories, this study re‐examined the widely reported dissociation between impaired recognition of negative emotions and preserved recognition of positive emotions in MS. Consistent with previous findings, pwMS showed a predominant vulnerability for negative emotions. At the same time, recognition of positive emotions showed no robust impairment, even when methodological asymmetries commonly present in emotion–recognition tasks were reduced. These findings indicate that the frequently reported preservation of positive emotion recognition in MS cannot be dismissed as a simple methodological artefact but also should not be interpreted as evidence that positive emotion recognition is fully intact. Rather, they suggest a more complex pattern shaped by individual patient characteristics, including cognitive functioning.

Taken together, this argues against a single, uniform mechanism of socio‐cognitive vulnerability in MS, where differences in lesion burden and lesion location, disease duration/progression and broader neurobehavioral manifestations (cognitive efficiency, fatigue, affective symptoms, disability) are likely to modulate socio‐cognitive outcomes. Accordingly, future studies could extend prior cluster‐analytic work in MS (e.g., Henry et al., 2022) by applying a profile‐based approach to multimodal characterization (cognition, affective symptoms, clinical disability and ideally MRI metrics) to derive data‐driven subgroups (e.g., predominantly cognitive‐, affective‐ or disability‐driven profiles) and assess whether these profiles show distinct patterns of emotion–recognition performance.
