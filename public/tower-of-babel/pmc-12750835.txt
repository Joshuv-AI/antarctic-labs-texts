Symptom course of PFAPA syndrome
Authors: Mathilde Labouret, Inès Elhani, Sébastien Cavelot, Glory Dingulu, Maurine Jouret, Ai Tien Nguyen, Adrien Subervie, Anne-Sophie Tavernier, Caroline Vinit, Véronique Hentgen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPeriodic fever, aphthous stomatitis, pharyngitis and cervical adenitis (PFAPA) syndrome is the most common cause of autoinflammatory periodic fever in children. It is generally considered to be a self-limiting condition that resolves spontaneously over time.ObjectivesTo evaluate age and delay to recovery of patients with PFAPA syndrome.MethodsWe retrospectively reviewed the medical records of patients diagnosed with PFAPA syndrome at the Versailles Hospital (Paris, France) and included in the Juvenile Inflammatory Rheumatism (JIR) cohort between 2016 and February 2023. Recovery was defined as the absence of any febrile PFAPA episode in the past year. Patients with either no reported febrile episode or insufficient information on fever status over the last 12 months were contacted by telephone.Results209 patients with PFAPA syndrome were included. Overall, 56 (27%) patients experienced resolution of periodic fever, 119 (57%) were still active and 34 (16%) patients were lost to follow-up. Among recovered patients, the median duration of symptoms was 5.43 years (Q1-Q3: 2.97–8.65) and the median age at last febrile episode was 8.34 years (Q1-Q3: 5.44–10.24). Of the 119 patients with persistent fever, 10 patients were initially declared cured but relapsed.ConclusionIn patients whose fever resolved, most experienced their last febrile episode before adolescence. The identification of relapses after at least 12 months without a febrile episode raises questions about the definition of recovery.Keywords: PFAPA, Periodic fever, Outcome, Recovery, Relapse

Introduction

Periodic fever, aphthous stomatitis, pharyngitis, and cervical adenitis (PFAPA) syndrome is the most common cause of autoinflammatory periodic fever in children. Its incidence was estimated in a 2013 Norwegian study as 2.3/10,000 children up to 5 years old [1].

Marshall et al. first described this syndrome in 1987 [2]. PFAPA syndrome is characterized by recurrent episodes of fever associated with at least one of the eponymous features of aphthous stomatitis, pharyngitis, and/or cervical adenitis. Inflammatory markers rise significantly during episodes but normalize in symptom-free intervals.

Diagnosing PFAPA is challenging due to the lack of specific biomarkers or genetic tests. Instead, diagnosis relies on clinical criteria set by experienced physicians, differentiating it from infectious or other auto-inflammatory diseases (AIDs). Thomas et al. were the first to suggest diagnostic criteria in 1999 [3], commonly used in subsequent studies. More recently, PRINTO/EUROFEVER classification criteria was published [4]. However, none of these classifications has been definitively validated.

It is established that the first symptoms appear usually before the age of 5 but there are few studies on the age of recovery and duration of this syndrome [5]. PFAPA is often described as a self-limiting condition, but cases of onset in older children, adolescents, and adults have been documented. Moreover, symptoms can resolve for prolonged periods and then recur, highlighting that the natural course of the syndrome is still not fully established [5, 6]. Its evolutive pattern varies, with most children experiencing a gradual decrease in episode frequency and/or severity before recovery [1, 7]. The unpredictable evolution of PFAPA syndrome is challenging for physicians, patients, and their families, both in terms of managing the disease and its impact on quality of life [8].

Treatment essentially focuses on minimize episode duration and improving patient comfort. Single dose oral corticosteroid given at episode onset access seems highly effective [9, 10]. Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs and antipyretics are used with partial efficacy. Tonsillectomy is usually presented as the only curative treatment, however, its efficiency seems variable according to different studies [11–13]. Colchicine might decrease episode frequency, especially in patients with MEFV gene mutations, though its effectiveness is inconsistent [6]. Thus, many patients rely on symptomatic treatment while waiting for spontaneous resolution.

The aim of our study is to evaluate the age and delay to recovery in a retrospective cohort of PFAPA patients followed in a French pediatric AIDs reference center.

Methods

Patients were identified from the Juvenile Inflammatory Rheumatism (JIR) cohort database, an international data repository established in 2013 to collect data on patients with juvenile-onset inflammatory or rheumatological diseases (https://www.jircohorte.org - NTC02377245). The JIR cohort operate through a web-secured platform organized into different modules, one of which is dedicated to AIDs. This study was conducted in Versailles Hospital Center (France) a reference center for AIDs, participating to the JIR Cohort since 2016. We included patients diagnosed with PFAPA syndrome, who were followed in Versailles Hospital Center and had at least one visit recorded in the JIR database from January 1, 2016, to February 1, 2023. PFAPA was diagnosed clinically by experienced pediatric rheumatologists, based on the presence of recurrent febrile episodes with typical associated features (aphthous stomatitis, pharyngitis and/or cervical adenitis), with normal growth and development, and after exclusion of alternative causes of recurrent fever. No formal diagnostic or classification criteria were applied. As they may have PFAPA-like clinical manifestations, patients with a heterozygous class 4 (likely pathogenic) or 5 (pathogenic) variants in the gene MEFV were excluded. Genetic variants were classified according to the Infevers database (https://infevers.umai-montpellier.fr), affiliated to the International Society for Systemic Auto-Inflammatory Disease. Patients for whom the diagnosis of PFAPA was subsequently ruled out in the JIR Cohort were also excluded.

Recovery was defined by the absence of any febrile episode for at least the past 12 months. To determine the status of patients, we conducted telephone calls to the families of all patients who either had no documented febrile episodes or lacked fever-related data for the 12 months preceding February 1, 2023. These structured follow-up telephone interviews were carried out from February 1st to August 2023. A re-emergence of febrile episodes in patients clearly stated to be recovered in the JIR cohort after 12 months free of febrile episodes was defined as a relapse.

Demographic information, familial history of unexplained fever or AIDs, age at first and last symptoms, information about puberty, laboratory results and genetic tests when performed were collected. Prophylactic treatment (colchicine, tonsillectomy, interleukin-1 inhibitors) and their efficacy were recorded. Missing data are indicated alongside the relevant variables. The duration of follow-up was determined from the first contact with the center to February 1, 2023, for patients reached by telephone, or to the date of their last visit for those who were not recontacted. Predicting the lack of data on puberty in current practice, patients were pragmatically considered to be under the age of normal puberty if they were under 8 years old for girls and 9 years old for boys, according to the classic definition of precocious puberty [14].

Among the still active patients, those who were older than 14 years at the last follow-up date (February 1, 2023) were analyzed. The age of 14 years was chosen on the basis that at this age all patients, regardless of sex, should have started puberty, unless they had a pubertal delay [15–17].

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The JIR cohort protocol was approved by the French Ethics Committee (CCTIRS) on April 21, 2015 (decision number 14.302). The electronic form of the JIR cohort was approved by the National Commission of data processing and liberties (CNIL) on March 27, 2015 (decision number DR-2015-218). All patients or their guardian were not opposed for inclusion and the storage of their medical data in the JIR cohort. They were informed that collected data might be used for research studies in compliance with privacy rules.

Statistical analysis

Quantitative variables were expressed as medians and quartiles. Qualitative variables were presented as numbers (n) and percentages (%). Normality and heteroskedasticity were assessed with Shapiro-Wilk and Levene’s tests respectively. Continuous outcomes were compared with Mann-Whitney U test, according to data distribution. Fisher’s exact test was used for categorical data. Correlation between continuous data was tested using Spearman’s coefficient after assessing normality using Shapiro-Wilk test. For multivariable linear regression, multicollinearity was checked with the Belsley-Kuh-Welsch technique, heteroskedasticity and normality of residuals were assessed respectively by the Breusch-Pagan test and the Shapiro-Wilk test. The Newey West correction for heteroskedasticity was applied. The alpha risk was set to 5%. Statistical analyses were performed using EasyMedStat (version 3.44; www.easymedstat.com).

Results

Description of the total cohort

Two hundred twenty-two patients diagnosed with PFAPA syndrome were identified in the JIR cohort. From this group, we excluded 10 patients with a heterozygous variant class 4 or 5 in the MEFV gene, and 3 other patients whose diagnosis of PFAPA syndrome was subsequently discarded by physicians within the JIR cohort. These three patients were reclassified as syndrome of unexplained recurrent fever: one carried a non-referenced ADAM17 variant and developed arthritis during febrile episodes, one experienced non-stereotyped febrile attacks with perianal aphthous lesions and symptoms outside of flares, and one presented with diarrhea, musculoskeletal pain, probable arthritis between episodes, and prolonged fever. This resulted in a study sample of 209 patients. Forty-six patients still had active disease at the time of their last visit to the center in the year before the end of the study period. We contacted by phone the remaining 163 patients – patients without reported febrile episode during the last visit or without available information about fever episodes in the last 12 months. We identified 56 patients with resolution of periodic fever (27%) and 119 patients (57%) with a still ongoing disease. 34 patients (16%) patients were lost to follow-up could not be reached for an update (Fig. 1). Resolved fever group

The median duration of symptoms was 5.43 years (2.97; 8.65) with a median age at first symptoms of 1.71 years (1.0; 3.27) and at the last febrile episode of 8.34 years (5.44; 10.24) (Fig. 2). Last febrile episode occurred before reaching normal age of puberty in 31 patients (55%). The median time from first contact with center to last follow up was 6.93 years (4.91; 8.94) while the time from last febrile episode to current follow up was 3.46 years (2.44; 5.03).

Distribution of age at last febrile episode (resolved group)

Although most patients experienced resolution during childhood, a minority (n = 9) remained symptomatic beyond 14 years of age, with a median age of 16.62 years (14.54; 18.62) at last follow-up (February 1, 2023). Notably, 2 of these patients remained active due to relapse following a remission period. Their median ages at PFAPA symptoms onset and diagnosis seems higher than in the resolved fever group (respectively p = 0.217 and p = 0.019) (Table 1). One patient underwent tonsillectomy 11 years before last follow-up.

Genetic testing for AIDs was conducted in nine patients, targeting specific genes: MEFV (n = 6), MVK (n = 5), TNFRSF1A (n = 2) (Table 1). For 1 of these patients, a panel of 59 other genes involved in AIDs were tested. Two patients had a heterozygous variant of uncertain significance (class 3) in the MEFV gene, while no other genetic abnormalities were identified. One patient exhibited a slightly elevated urine mevalonate level during a fever episode, but genetic testing ruled out mevalonate kinase deficiency.

Tonsillectomy was conducted in 8 patients. Of these, 3 patients had no subsequent febrile episodes. In the remaining 5 patients the median duration between tonsillectomy and the last febrile episode was 5.49 years (4.14; 7.76). Notably, one patient reported a last single febrile episode 3 months post-surgery, followed by a full recovery. Tonsillectomy resulted in the prolonged disappearance of episodes in 2 patients, with remission periods of 4 and 10 years respectively. According to the delay of 12 months without febrile episode used to define recovery in this study, they could have been declared cured earlier and therefore be considered as relapsed patients. Only 2 (4%) patients in this group had been temporarily treated with colchicine with no observed efficacy (Table 2).

Predictors of disease duration

When comparing patients with resolved versus ongoing episodes, median disease duration did not differ significantly between groups (respectively 65.1 (35.64-103.79) vs. 62.87 (39.18-104.08) months, p = 0.927). In contrast, follow-up duration was markedly shorter in the ongoing group (33.93 (20.52–63.47) vs. 83.2 (58.89-107.28) months, p < 0.001) (Fig. 3).

Distribution of symptom duration (A) and follow-up time (B) in the resolved fever and still active groups. Horizontal bars indicate medians; boxes, interquartile ranges; whiskers, ranges

In resolved fever group, a low negative correlation was observed between age of onset and symptom duration (ρ = -0.42; r2 = 0.116; p = 0.001). However, in multivariable analysis including all patients with ongoing symptoms this association disappeared after adjusting for follow-up duration, which remained the only independent predictor of disease duration (β = 0.94, [0.76; 1.13], p < 0.0001).

Comparison of treatment modalities between the resolved fever group and the ongoing episodes group revealed no statistically significant differences (Table 2). Tonsillectomy was performed in 8 patients (14%) in the resolved fever group versus 7 patients (6%) in the ongoing episodes group (p = 0.083), while colchicine was used in 2 patients (4%) and 3 patients (3%), respectively (p = 0.656). No patients in either group received IL-1 inhibitors. In multivariable analysis, tonsillectomy was not associated with disease duration (β = 12.64, [-11.66; 36.95], p = 0.3059). In contrast, follow-up duration remained independently associated with longer disease duration (β = 0.98, [0.8; 1.15], p < 0.0001). For colchicine, the number of treated patients was too small for multivariable modelling. Median disease duration was 43.87 months (41.07–79.23) in patients who had received colchicine compared with 63.47 months (38.21-104.31) in those who had not, with no significant difference (p = 0.671).

Identification of patients who relapse

Among the 119 patients with persistent fever, we identified 10 (8.4%) patients who were initially considered recovered during follow-up but subsequently relapsed. The median age at their last febrile episode before relapse was 7.68 years (5.82; 9.61). For nine of them (missing data for one patient), relapse occurred after a median remission period of 30.5 months (24.47; 39.2) with a median age at the time of relapse of 10.16 (8.74; 11.55).

Discussion

This follow-up study examines the resolution of PFAPA syndrome in a significant pediatric cohort, highlighting the natural course of the disease.

Given the absence of specific biomarkers, diagnosis remains a clinical challenge. A limitation of this retrospective study is that no formal classification criteria were applied. Nevertheless, diagnosis relies on the expertise of a team of physicians belonging to the same reference center, specialized in pediatric AIDs, thereby reducing diagnostic variability that may arise from disparate clinical approaches.

Most of the other follow-up studies, have shown that resolution of PFAPA typically occurs during early adolescence. In this study, the last febrile episode was observed at a median age of 8.34 years (5.44; 10.24). Comparatively, Wurster et al. [5] reported a mean age of 9.2 (95% CI, 8.9–10.5) for complete resolution of symptoms while Padeh et al. documented a mean age of 10.9 ± 2.5 years [18]. There was no significant difference in the duration of active disease between patients who achieved remission and those with persisting episodes, although follow-up was significantly shorter in the latter group. This suggests that the apparent absence of remission in patients with still active disease may partly reflect limited follow-up, and that their overall disease course is likely to be longer once observation is complete.

There is no standard definition of recovery in PFAPA syndrome. However, Førsvoll et al. defined resolution as at least 6 months without any febrile episode, which is may be too short a period as 8 of 37 cured children relapsed after a median febrile-attack-free period of 20 months (quartiles: 11, 24) [1]. As in Wurster et al. study, we defined resolution of fever as the absence of periodic fever episodes in the past year [5]. Among 119 patients with persistent fever, we identified 10 patients (8.4%) who relapsed after a median remission period of 30.5 months (24.47; 39.2) for 9 of them. Similarly, Wurster et al. reported 5 patients relapsing after an average remission duration of 13 months (range, 4–24 months). This finding raises questions about the adequacy of this timeframe and suggests a more complex disease course. In addition, it may be necessary to have a nuanced understanding of recovery, as patients may continue to experience PFAPA symptoms without fever, as observed in clinical practice [5, 19].

Onset of PFAPA typically occurs before 5 years of age, 51 patients (91%) of the recovery group met this time point. While younger age at onset initially appeared associated with longer disease duration, this association was no longer significant after adjusting for follow-up time. Neither tonsillectomy nor colchicine was associated with disease duration in our cohort.

Tonsils, a site of immune dysregulation may trigger PFAPA episodes [20], with differences in histologic features and microbial composition reported in PFAPA patients [21, 22]. However, the effectiveness of tonsillectomy remains inconclusive [11, 23]. Among 8 fever-resolved patients in our study who underwent tonsillectomy, 3 recovered fully, 1 had final episode at 3 months, and 2 relapsed after long remission periods. Reports of PFAPA in adults with prior tonsillectomy during childhood also suggests the need of long-term evaluation of its efficacy [24]. The limited use of tonsillectomy and colchicine in our cohort allowed us to observe the natural course and spontaneous resolution of PFAPA.

More than half of the patients experienced their last febrile episode before the conventional age thresholds for puberty. As no individual pubertal stage data were available, we cannot determine whether hormonal changes contribute to PFAPA resolution. Nevertheless, pubertal development may represent a relevant factor influencing disease course, which warrants further study.

Some patients may experience symptoms beyond childhood. While reclassification of their PFAPA diagnosis as syndrome of undifferentiated recurrent fever, defined as recurrent inflammation that does not fit the clinical picture of a well-defined AID or without a pathogenic mutation causing a known hereditary AID, may be considered. This remains hypothetical given the absence of biological markers and the current inability to predict which patients will have prolonged symptoms. Nevertheless, distinguishing between the typical self-limiting childhood form of PFAPA and cases with persistence into adolescence or adulthood may be useful for follow-up strategies, patient counseling, and future research.

The retrospective design and reliance on parental reporting and physician interpretation introduce potential biases [19]. The subjective nature of PFAPA episode recognition could lead to a wrong estimation of the disease activity and relapse rates. It underscores the need for long term follow-up prospective studies with objective criteria and standardized reporting methods in PFAPA research. Despite these limitations, it is apparent that, in patients whose fever resolved, most experienced their last febrile episode before adolescence.

Conclusion

We present the data from a large cohort of PFAPA patients followed in a reference center for AIDs. In most patients with resolved fever, the final febrile episode occurs before adolescence. The identification of relapses after at least 12 months without a febrile episode raises questions about the definition of recovery. Further studies with longer follow-up are needed to confirm these data and to identify predictive factors for recovery.
