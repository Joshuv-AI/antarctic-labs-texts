Bacterial Infections in US Adults with Firearm Injuries
Authors: Jerry Yang, Chih Chun Tung, Melike N. Harfouche, Jonathan D. Baghdadi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This cohort study identifies risk factors for bacterial infection after firearm injury and assesses the association between bacterial infection and mortality among adults with these injuries in the US.

Introduction

Firearm injuries frequently lead to complications.1,2 Although guidelines recommend less than 24 hours of antibiotic prophylaxis for most patients,3 bacterial infection is a recognized risk.4,5 This study’s objectives were to explore risk factors for bacterial infection after firearm injury and evaluate the association between bacterial infection and mortality across a broad sample of US adults with firearm injury. We hypothesized that firearm injuries requiring lower abdominal surgery would be at highest risk for bacterial infection.

Methods

This cohort study of adults with firearm injuries between January 2019 and May 2021 used data from the Premier Healthcare Database, an all-payer administrative database covering a geographically diverse set of hospitals across the US.6 Hospitalizations for firearm injury were identified using International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems, Tenth Revision diagnosis codes.7 The University of Maryland School of Medicine Institutional Review Board deemed this study exempt from ethics review and informed consent because it did not include private health information. The STROBE reporting guideline was followed.8

Bacterial infection was defined by positive culture results from any source and antibiotics initiated within 2 days of these results that were continued for at least 4 days or until discharge.1,2 Culture results positive for fungal pathogens or elements (eg, hyphae) were excluded. Early infections were present during the index hospitalization, whereas late infections developed within the 90-day follow-up. Firearm type, intent of injury, and New Injury Severity Score (range, 0-50, with higher scores indicating greater severity) were ascertained using diagnosis codes. Surgical procedure codes localized the site of injury and were used as a proxy for injury severity based on a standard mapping provided by the National Healthcare Safety Network. Baseline comorbidity was represented by the Elixhauser Comorbidity Index. There were no missing values for any variables included in the analysis.

Characteristics of patients with and without bacterial infection were compared by χ2 or t test as appropriate for categorical and continuous variables. Multivariable logistic regression was used to evaluate associations between clinical characteristics and bacterial infection among patients who survived at least 1 day during the index hospitalization. Variables included patient demographics, insurance type, firearm type, intent and severity of injury, Elixhauser Comorbidity Index, and whether surgery involving different body regions was performed. Separate models were fit for early and late infection. Individual coefficients were evaluated for significance using the Wald test. Statistical tests were 2-tailed, with a statistical significance threshold of P < .05. Analyses were performed from June and December 2025 using SAS Studio, version 3.81 (SAS Institute).

Results

Across 323 hospitals that contributed microbiologic data to Premier Healthcare Database during the study period, we identified 24 024 patients (mean [SD] age at initial presentation, 32.9 [15.2] years; 20 488 males [85.3%]) with hospitalization for firearm injury (Table 1). Median (IQR) New Injury Severity Score was 2 (1-8), and median (IQR) duration of antibiotic therapy was 1 (0-1) day. Surgical procedures were performed in 5798 patients (24.1%), and 2007 patients (8.4%) died.

Bacterial infection developed in 1236 patients (5.1%), with 1034 early infections (4.3%) and 306 late infections (1.3%) identified. Patients with bacterial infection received antimicrobial therapy for a median (IQR) of 9 (5-17) days. The most common pathogens identified were Escherichia coli (258 patients [20.9%] ), Staphylococcus aureus (154 patients [12.4%]), and enterococci (153 patients [12.4%]). Mortality occurred in 102 of 1134 patients (8.3%) with bacterial infection and 1905 of 22 778 patients (8.4%) without infection (P = .89).

Among patients who survived at least 1 night in the hospital, the clinical factors associated with increased risk of early infection were surgical procedures of the head, neck, and/or spine (OR, 5.18; 95% CI, 3.96-6.78) and lower abdomen (OR, 1.77; 95% CI, 1.40-2.24). Handgun injury (OR, 0.58, 95% CI 0.48-0.75) and surgical procedures for extremity injuries had lower risk of early infection (OR, 0.58; 95% CI, 0.46-0.73) (Table 2). Clinical factors associated with increased risk of late infection were liver surgical procedures (OR, 3.83; 95% CI, 2.25-6.51) and head, neck, and/or spine procedures (OR, 2.30; 95% CI, 1.27-4.16). Among patients who survived at least 1 night in the hospital, infection during the initial hospitalization was not associated with mortality (OR, 0.86; 95% CI, 0.64-1.16; P = .33).

Discussion

To our knowledge, this cohort study is the largest to date examining patterns of bacterial infection among hospitalized patients with firearm injury in the US. Bacterial infection during index hospitalization was present in 1 of 23 patients, whereas new infections rarely developed during follow-up. The patients at greatest risk of infection were those who required head, neck, spine, or colon surgical procedures. Antibiotic exposure among patients without infection was low.

Study limitations included use of administrative data that relied on diagnosis codes. We used a relatively specific definition for bacterial infection that required positive culture results, since many patients with traumatic injuries are treated empirically with antibiotics for nonspecific inflammation. Type of surgical procedure and New Injury Severity Score were used as proxies for acute severity of illness. Complete follow-up data may not have been available for all patients.

Bacterial infection during initial hospitalization for firearm injury is common, but development of new infection during follow-up is rare. Among patients who survive at least 1 night in the hospital, bacterial infection was not independently associated with mortality.
