Design and validation of revised medication assessment tool for community-acquired Pneumonia: rMAT-CAP
Authors: Sahar Elnajjar, Sabariah Noor Harun, Semira Beshir, Ali Qureshi, Dujana Al Hamed, Hebah Al Jaghoub, Hamzeh Al Subbah, Danish Khowaja
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEvaluating therapeutic appropriateness is a cornerstone of antimicrobial stewardship. The original Medication Assessment Tool for Community-Acquired Pneumonia (MAT-CAP), developed in Norway, requires adaptation to account for evolving IDSA/ATS recommendations and region-specific antimicrobial resistance patterns in the UAE. To revise, adapt, and validate a Medication Assessment Tool for Community-Acquired Pneumonia (rMAT-CAP), adapted to the UAE healthcare context.MethodsA methodological study was conducted in three phases: (1) systematic revision of MAT-CAP indicators in accordance with 2019 IDSA/ATS guidelines and UAE resistance data; (2) content validation through a multidisciplinary Delphi panel of 10 experts; and (3) psychometric validation using inter-rater reliability and criterion validity assessments based on 30 anonymized CAP cases.ResultsThe rMAT-CAP comprised 13 explicit, guideline-aligned criteria covering empirical therapy, microbiological diagnostics, dose adjustment, IV-to-oral switch, and treatment duration. Content validity indices were excellent (mean I-CVI = 0.95; S-CVI/Ave = 0.95). Inter-rater reliability demonstrated almost perfect agreement for the drug domain (κ = 0.87; absolute agreement 90.0%, 95% CI: 73.5–97.9%), substantial agreement for the dose domain (κ = 0.64; absolute agreement 73.3%, 95% CI: 54.1–86.6%), and moderate agreement for the duration domain (κ = 0.45; absolute agreement 80.0%, 95% CI: 61.4–91.1%). Criterion validity showed strong diagnostic performance: sensitivity 100% (95% CI: 83.9–100%), specificity 62.5% (95% CI: 30.6–86.3%), PPV 88.0% (95% CI: 69.0–96.7%), NPV 100% (95% CI: 56.6–100%), and overall accuracy 90.0% (95% CI: 73.5–97.9%).ConclusionThe rMAT-CAP is a valid, reliable, and contextually relevant tool for evaluating therapeutic appropriateness in CAP management. Its structured approach supports antimicrobial stewardship initiatives and facilitates standardized audit and feedback in UAE hospitals.Trial registrationClinical trial number: not applicable.Keywords: Antimicrobial stewardship, Community-acquired pneumonia, Medication assessment tool, Validation, rMAT-CAP, Therapeutic appropriateness

Introduction

Inappropriate antimicrobial prescribing remains a major driver of antimicrobial resistance (AMR), highlighting the need for structured, evidence-based tools to evaluate therapeutic appropriateness [1]. Although several medication assessment tools (MATs) have been developed to evaluate prescribing quality, such as the Medication Appropriateness Index (MAI), Beers Criteria, and STOPP/START, these tools mainly target chronic or geriatric pharmacotherapy and do not address the time-critical and pathogen-directed decisions required in acute infectious diseases [2, 3]. In antimicrobial therapy, tools such as the Gyssens algorithm, the Global Point Prevalence Survey (Global-PPS), the National Antimicrobial Prescribing Survey (NAPS), and the Antimicrobial Rationality Assessment Tool (AmRAT) offer structured approaches for evaluating antimicrobial appropriateness [4]. However, these frameworks are broad, non-disease-specific, and may not capture key clinical nuances relevant to community-acquired pneumonia (CAP), such as severity-based empiric regimens, microbiological testing triggers, IV-to-oral switch timing, and duration of therapy.

To address the need for an infection-specific assessment tool, Hogli et al. (2014) developed the Medication Assessment Tool for Community-Acquired Pneumonia (MAT-CAP). This explicit, criterion-based tool translates CAP guidelines into measurable indicators covering empirical treatment, diagnostics, dose adjustment, and treatment duration. MAT-CAP demonstrated high interrater reliability and feasibility for clinical audit in Norway, supporting its potential value for targeted ASP interventions [5].

However, several limitations restrict the direct application of MAT-CAP outside its original context. The tool was developed prior to the release of the updated 2019 IDSA/ATS CAP guidelines [6], which introduced key changes in treatment duration, IV-to-oral switch criteria, and empirical therapy recommendations. MAT-CAP also does not account for local AMR patterns, formulary availability, or region-specific diagnostic practices.

Among available tools, MAT-CAP was selected for adaptation because it is the only validated, disease-specific instrument designed to assess therapeutic appropriateness in CAP. Its structure directly aligns with stewardship priorities for pneumonia management, unlike broader tools such as AmRAT [7] or NAPS [8], which are not tailored to specific infections. Adapting MAT-CAP to the UAE context, therefore, offers a unique opportunity to create a standardized, evidence-based mechanism for evaluating CAP prescribing quality and supporting structured post-prescription review.

Methods

Study design

This methodological validation study consisted of three sequential phases: (1) revision and adaptation of the original MAT-CAP, (2) content validation through Delphi consensus, and (3) psychometric validation involving inter-rater reliability and criterion validity assessments within a 786-bed general medical–surgical hospital in Dubai.

Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Board/Ethics Committee of Dubai Health Authority, Dubai, United Arab Emirates (Ref.: DSREC-SR-01/2025_06).

Phase I: revision and adaptation of MAT-CAP

The original MAT-CAP instrument was comprehensively reviewed and updated to ensure alignment with contemporary evidence and clinical practice [5]. In particular, revisions were guided by the 2019 IDSA/ATS CAP guidelines and by national antimicrobial resistance trends reported in the UAE [6, 9]. The 2019 IDSA/ATS guidelines were selected as the primary reference because: (1) they represent the most current comprehensive evidence-based CAP guideline internationally endorsed at the time of tool development; (2) they have been formally adopted as the foundational framework for CAP management at the study institution; and (3) the empiric regimens they recommend for hospitalized CAP are consistent with UAE antimicrobial resistance patterns and local formulary [6, 9].

This revision phase occurred before the Delphi validation and followed a structured, methodical workflow. Multidisciplinary core research team; comprising an infectious disease (ID) clinical pharmacist as the primary tool developer, two infectious diseases physicians, and two pulmonologists collaboratively examined each indicator in the original tool. Every indicator was reformulated into a qualifying statement to determine whether it should apply to a given case, followed by an audit standard specifying how adherence would be assessed.

The team reviewed each of the 15 original indicators against several evidence sources, including updated international guidelines, local antimicrobial prescribing protocols, UAE formulary availability, and regional antimicrobial resistance patterns. During this process, the team considered whether each indicator remained clinically relevant, reflected current CAP management practices, required updated terminology or definitions, or needed adaptation for feasibility within UAE hospital settings. Any differences in interpretation among team members were resolved throughout the meeting, and all revisions were documented in a detailed change log outlining the justification for each modification. Some indicators required extensive update due to changes in recommended empiric regimens, diagnostic criteria, or dosing standards, whereas others were removed because they no longer aligned with evidence-based practice or could not be meaningfully operationalized in the UAE clinical environment.

Upon completing this structured revision process, the refined rMAT-CAP consisted of 13 indicators organized into three domains, retained the same domains of MAT-CAP: initial empirical therapy, microbiological diagnostics and pathogen-directed therapy, and dose optimization, including IV-to-oral switch and treatment duration. In addition, the tool retained the same audit response categories as the original MAT-CAP: Yes (adherence), No (non-adherence), Justified Non-adherence, and Insufficient Data, to ensure continuity and facilitate consistent interpretation during subsequent reliability and validity testing.

Phase II: content validity

Content validity of the rMAT-CAP tool was established using a structured, modified Delphi process. A multidisciplinary panel of ten clinical experts was recruited through purposive sampling. Experts were invited via email based on specialized clinical experience in the management of community-acquired pneumonia (CAP) or antimicrobial therapy. The panel included infectious diseases physicians, pulmonologists, clinical pharmacists, and emergency medicine physicians.

The Delphi procedure consisted of a single round, conducted electronically using Microsoft Forms. Experts independently reviewed each preliminary rMAT-CAP item and rated its relevance, clarity, and clinical applicability using a four-point Likert scale for each of the three domains, with the scale anchors adapted per domain: (1) Relevance: 1 = not relevant to 4 = highly relevant; (2) Clarity: 1 = not clear to 4 = very clear; (3) Clinical applicability: 1 = not applicable to 4 = highly applicable. The I-CVI was calculated separately for each domain, and items were required to meet the ≥ 0.78 threshold across all three. Although additional rounds are typically used to obtain consensus, the predefined agreement threshold was reached in the first round; therefore, further rounds were not required.

Item-level Content Validity Index (I-CVI) was calculated using RStudio (version 4.5.2) by determining the proportion of experts who rated each item as either 3 or 4. The overall adequacy of the scale was assessed using the Scale-level Content Validity Index (S-CVI/Ave), computed as the average of all I-CVI values. A minimum I-CVI threshold of 0.78, as recommended by Lynn (1986), Polit and Beck (2006), and Yusoff (2019), was applied to identify items requiring revision [10, 11, 12].

Following expert review, the final structure of rMAT-CAP was established according to the consensus achieved during the Delphi process.

Phase III: psychometric validation

Inter-rater reliability (IRR)

To evaluate the reproducibility of the rMAT-CAP tool, inter-rater reliability was assessed using a subset of experts from the Delphi panel. All 10 experts who participated in the content validity round were invited to take part in the reliability assessment; however, only seven experts accepted and completed the evaluation. These raters included infectious diseases physicians, pulmonologists, internal medicine physicians, and clinical pharmacist.

Prior to scoring, raters received a structured training session, which included orientation to the rMAT-CAP domains, definitions, qualifying statements, and audit standards. Training was conducted by the primary investigator and included case-based examples and group clarification of potential ambiguities to ensure consistent interpretation. After training, all raters independently applied the tool without further discussion. To prevent bias, raters were blinded to each other’s assessments.

Inter-rater reliability was assessed using 30 anonymized CAP cases, selected randomly from adult (> 18 year old and </= 65 year old) inpatients diagnosed with community-acquired pneumonia [13]. Each rater completed the full rMAT-CAP scoring for all cases using the categorical audit responses (Yes, No, Nj, ID). For the purpose of IRR analysis, ratings were converted into a binary appropriateness classification, in which “Yes” and “Nj” were coded as appropriate, and “No” as inappropriate. Cases with “ID” ratings were excluded pairwise from kappa calculations.

Fleiss’ kappa (κ) was used to quantify agreement among the seven raters, with interpretation based on the Landis and Koch benchmark scale. The κ statistic was calculated for overall appropriateness classification, as the intent of the reliability analysis was to examine reproducibility at the tool level rather than for individual indicators. All statistical analyses were performed in RStudio (version 4.5.2), with significance set at p < 0.05.

Criterion validity

Criterion validity examined the extent to which rMAT-CAP classifications aligned with independent expert clinical judgment. For this analysis, a separate set of 30 CAP cases, distinct from those used in the inter-rater reliability assessment, was randomly sampled. A senior infectious diseases consultant, serving as the reference standard, evaluated these cases independently using clinical judgment informed by institutional and international CAP guidelines, without access to the rMAT-CAP tool. A second expert reviewer, a pulmonologist, then applied the rMAT-CAP to the same 30 cases. Both reviewers were blinded to each other’s assessments, and the pulmonologist was blinded to the expert reference classification. Criterion validity was assessed by comparing rMAT-CAP appropriateness classifications with the expert’s judgments.

Analysis method

Sensitivity, specificity, Positive Predictive Value (PPV), Negative Predictive Value (NPV), and overall accuracy were calculated to assess the tool’s diagnostic performance, and all proportions are reported with 95% confidence intervals calculated using the Wilson score interval method. Therapeutic appropriateness refers to the extent to which prescribed antimicrobial therapy aligns with evidence-based clinical practice guidelines in terms of drug selection, dose, and duration. In this study, therapeutic appropriateness was conceptualized using a graded adherence framework as per MAT-CAP methodology, adherence < 50%, 50–75% and > 75% have been defined as low, intermediate and high adherence, respectively. High adherence = Appropriate, whereas intermediate and low adherence = Inappropriate [5].

The revised version (rMAT-CAP) retained the core structure of the original MAT-CAP but introduced substantial updates in empirical therapy and dose optimization.

Phase II: content validity

Ten experts were invited to participate in the Delphi process, and all ten completed the single round with no attrition. Because consensus thresholds were achieved in the first round, no additional rounds were required.

Across all items, the I-CVI values ranged from 0.80 to 1.00 with a mean I-CVI of 0.92, exceeding the predefined acceptability criterion of ≥ 0.78. All 13 items met with the cut-off for relevance, clarity, and applicability. Thirteen items were retained without modification. No items were removed, and no new criteria were added.

The final rMAT-CAP tool consisted of 13 criteria organized into three domains: (1) initial empiric therapy selection, (2) microbiological diagnostics and pathogen-directed therapy, (3) dosing, IV-to-oral transition, and treatment duration.

Phase III: psychometric validation

Inter-rater reliability

Fleiss’ kappa analysis demonstrated κ = 0.84, indicating almost perfect agreement among all raters. In addition to Fleiss’ kappa, absolute percentage agreement was calculated for each domain to provide a clinically interpretable measure of concordance. Absolute agreement was 90.0% (95% CI: 73.5–97.9%) for the drug domain, 73.3% (95% CI: 54.1–86.6%) for the dose domain, and 80.0% (95% CI: 61.4–91.1%) for the duration domain. These figures complement the kappa values reported in Table 3 and confirm that the level of raw concordance across raters is acceptable for routine clinical use following structured training. No significant differences were observed between physicians and pharmacists, suggesting that the tool can be reliably applied across different professional backgrounds following brief, structured calibration training.

Criterion validity

Criterion validity was assessed to determine the accuracy of rMAT-CAP in classifying appropriate and inappropriate prescribing compared with expert clinical judgment. Thirty CAP cases were independently evaluated by both an infectious diseases specialist (serving as the reference standard) and a pulmonologist applying rMAT-CAP. The classification outcomes were as follows: True Positives (n = 22), True Negatives (n = 5), False Positives (n = 3), and no False Negatives (n = 0).

From these data, the following indices were calculated: Sensitivity 100% (95% CI: 83.9–100%), specificity 62.5% (95% CI: 30.6–86.3%), PPV 88.0% (95% CI: 69.0–96.7%), NPV 100% (95% CI: 56.6–100%), and overall accuracy 90.0% (95% CI: 73.5–97.9%).

These findings confirm that rMAT-CAP exhibits excellent sensitivity and predictive accuracy in identifying appropriate prescribing decisions, with no false negatives and high overall concordance with expert evaluation. The moderate specificity suggests a slight tendency toward over-classification of appropriateness, which is acceptable within the context of antimicrobial stewardship, where emphasis is placed on patient safety and the prevention of under-treatment.

Discussion

This study presents the design and validation of rMAT-CAP, a revised, explicit assessment tool developed to evaluate therapeutic appropriateness in CAP management by systematically assessing adherence to evidence-based clinical guidelines. The tool demonstrated strong content validity, inter-rater reliability, and criterion validity, confirming its suitability for use within the UAE healthcare setting. These findings support the utility of rMAT-CAP as a practical instrument that operationalizes guideline-concordant prescribing, aligning directly with the Department of Health Abu Dhabi Antimicrobial Stewardship Guidelines, which emphasize standardized prescribing practices, consistent application of treatment pathways, and routine audits to ensure rational antimicrobial use [16].

The content validity indices obtained (mean I-CVI = 0.95; S-CVI = 0.95) exceeded the recommended thresholds (≥ 0.78) established by Lynn (1986), Polit & Beck (2006), and Yusoff (2019), and were comparable to previous MAT validations such as MAT-CHDSP and MAT-Cancer Pain [17, 18]. This high level of expert consensus indicates that the updated indicators are both clear and reflective of essential clinical decision points in CAP management. Despite the strengths of the Delphi process, a methodological limitation should be acknowledged. While experts evaluated each revised indicator for relevance, clarity, and clinical applicability, they were not explicitly asked to assess whether the revision process as a whole preserved the construct validity of the original MAT-CAP - that is, whether the revised instrument continues to measure the same underlying construct of therapeutic appropriateness. Revisions to individual indicators may alter the conceptual structure of an instrument in ways that item-level content validity assessment alone cannot detect. Ideally, this could be addressed through comparative validation, such as formal expert evaluation of the original and revised tools against the same case set or empirical head-to-head application. As this was not undertaken in the present study, further validation work is warranted to confirm preservation of construct validity. The high I-CVI values achieved across a multidisciplinary expert panel - including the same clinical specialties as the original MAT-CAP validation (infectious diseases physicians, pulmonologists, and clinical pharmacists) - provide indirect evidence that the revisions were considered clinically sound, but do not substitute for a formal construct validity-of-revision assessment.

On the other hand, the rMAT-CAP operationalizes stewardship goals across multiple domains: Indicator 1 enforces timely antimicrobial initiation within four hours of admission, reducing the risk of clinical deterioration and empirical escalation; Indicators 4 and 5 apply severity-stratified prescribing thresholds that explicitly restrict MRSA-active and antipseudomonal agents to patients with documented risk factors, preventing inappropriate escalation in the majority of cases; Indicator 3 mandates documented clinical justification for any deviation from the recommended first-line regimen, promoting prescriber accountability; Indicator 10 enforces prompt intravenous-to-oral switch once clinical stability criteria are met, minimizing unnecessary parenteral therapy; Indicator 12 ensures that total treatment duration is concordant with guideline recommendations, directly addressing one of the highest-yield targets for antibiotic overuse reduction; and Indicator 13, when levofloxacin is clinically indicated, enforces the high-dose short-course strategy (750 mg for five days) in preference to prolonged lower-dose regimens. Collectively, these criteria position the rMAT-CAP not as a tool that endorses broad-spectrum therapy, but as a structured, auditable instrument that identifies deviations from evidence-based, context-appropriate practice in either direction—whether under-treatment (inadequate empiric coverage, delayed therapy) or over-treatment (unjustified escalation, excessive duration). This dual sensitivity to both types of prescribing failure is precisely the function a stewardship tool must serve to meaningfully improve antimicrobial use.

The inter-rater reliability results (κ = 0.87 for the drug domain) indicate almost perfect agreement, consistent with the high reliability reported for the original MAT-CAP and with subsequent MAT validation studies [6, 19]. The substantial and moderate agreement observed for dosing (κ = 0.64) and duration (κ = 0.45) likely reflects the intrinsic clinical variability in dose optimization and determining appropriate therapy length findings also noted in large antimicrobial audits such as the Global-PPS [20]. These domains commonly involve individualized decision-making, including renal dose adjustments and the timing of therapy discontinuation based on patient stability, which naturally introduces discrepancy in assessor interpretation.

To mitigate this variability and ensure consistent application within our setting, the rMAT-CAP tool incorporates explicit, operationally defined criteria for dosing, IV-to-oral switch, and duration. During the validation phase, in accordance with Department of Health Abu Dhabi stewardship guidance advocating structured training, all raters underwent standardized training and calibration sessions, which included reviewing case scenarios, clarifying definitions, and standardizing interpretations of clinical stability, renal function thresholds, and recommended durations based on guideline updates [16]. Embedding these definitions within the tool reduces ambiguity and minimizes subjective judgment.

Furthermore, the structured format of rMAT-CAP, combined with its alignment to the 2019 IDSA/ATS CAP guidelines and UAE stewardship protocols, supports uniform application across clinicians and pharmacists, improving reproducibility during post-prescription review. In routine practice, such standardization is essential to reducing inter-clinician variation and ensuring that therapeutic decisions consistently adhere to best-practice guidelines; an outcome explicitly prioritized in Department of Health Abu Dhabi antimicrobial stewardship policy [16].

In the pilot study, rMAT-CAP systematically identified recurrent deficiencies in CAP management, particularly delays in IV-to-oral transition and unnecessarily prolonged antibiotic durations. These patterns are consistent with findings from previous CAP audits and reinforce the tool’s utility in detecting persistent deviations from guideline recommendations [6, 20]. These insights support the tool’s dual role in performance evaluation and quality improvement. In the UAE, prior antimicrobial-use studies and audits have reported frequent deviations from guideline-recommended antibiotic prescribing (in terms of choice, dosing, and overall days of therapy), highlighting the potential for overuse or inappropriate regimen selection in various infections [21, 22, 23]. rMAT-CAP could address these gaps and support standardized antimicrobial stewardship practices across different hospitals.

The criterion validity analysis yielded excellent diagnostic accuracy; sensitivity 100% (95% CI: 83.9–100%), specificity 62.5% (95% CI: 30.6–86.3%), PPV 88.0% (95% CI: 69.0–96.7%), NPV 100% (95% CI: 56.6–100%), and overall accuracy 90.0% (95% CI: 73.5–97.9%), consistent with validation metrics reported for other antimicrobial rationality tools such as AmRAT and Hospital-NAPS [7, 8]. The moderate specificity of 62.5% (95% CI: 30.6–86.3%) warrants critical consideration. This finding indicates that the rMAT-CAP misclassified three of eight expert-identified inappropriate cases as appropriate - a pattern that carries meaningful implications for stewardship practice. In the context of post-prescription audit, a false-positive classification of appropriateness may generate false reassurance, allowing genuinely suboptimal prescribing to go undetected and unchallenged. This risk is not trivial: if auditors or stewardship teams rely on rMAT-CAP classifications without supplementary clinical review, prescribing deficiencies in borderline cases - particularly those involving subtle deviations in duration, de-escalation timing, or dose optimization - may be systematically missed. To mitigate this risk, a regular multidisciplinary review sessions should be embedded into the audit workflow to calibrate tool application and capture cases where guideline-concordance and clinical appropriateness may diverge [19]. Future iterations of the tool should consider whether additional specificity-enhancing criteria - such as more granular duration thresholds or explicit de-escalation triggers - can be incorporated without compromising feasibility. Such findings underscore the importance of structured training and regular multidisciplinary review to refine tool application over time [19].

The key advantage of the rMAT-CAP tool when compared to global antimicrobial assessment tools, lies in its explicit CAP-specific indicators and its adaptation to regional AMR patterns. Tools like AmRAT and NAPS are not disease-specific and offer broad antimicrobial evaluation [7, 8]. The rMAT-CAP fills this gap by incorporating severity-based empiric recommendations, updated IV-to-oral switch criteria, and dosing refinements that reflect local clinical practice in the UAE. This makes the tool more relevant for hospitals facing distinct resistance trends, formulary constraints, and prescribing behaviors.

A notable strength of this study is its multi-step validation process, which included content validity assessment, inter-rater reliability testing, and criterion validity analysis, ensuring that rMAT-CAP is both methodologically and clinically relevant. In addition, its structured format allows completion within 11 min per case, supporting its routine use in post-prescription review and stewardship interventions. The tool requires minimal training and short completion time, which enhances its feasibility for integration into daily clinical practice. In the future, this tool can be embedded within electronic health records, further streamlining its use and scalability.

Despite these strengths, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the criterion validity sample included only 30 cases from a single tertiary care center in Dubai, which limits statistical power and generalizability. The wide confidence intervals observed - particularly for specificity (95% CI: 30.6–86.3%) - reflect the small number of expert-classified inappropriate cases (n = 8) and indicate substantial uncertainty in the estimates. Accordingly, these findings should be interpreted with caution and considered exploratory, serving to establish preliminary psychometric benchmarks rather than definitive evidence of criterion validity. Further validation in larger, multicenter cohorts across diverse healthcare settings in the UAE - including community hospitals, private institutions, and centers with varying antibiotic formularies and resistance patterns - is warranted before broader implementation can be recommended.

Conclusion

The rMAT-CAP demonstrated acceptable content validity, inter-rater reliability, and preliminary evidence of criterion validity for evaluating therapeutic appropriateness in CAP management. However, given the single-center design and exploratory sample size, these findings should be interpreted as preliminary psychometric benchmarks that establish the tool’s feasibility and initial validity rather than definitive evidence for broad implementation. The instrument has the potential to bridge the gap between international guidelines and local practice realities, thereby facilitating standardized audit and supporting integration into antimicrobial stewardship initiatives across hospitals in the UAE. Future research should focus on longitudinal evaluation of rMAT-CAP implementation and its impact on prescribing patterns, clinical outcomes, and antimicrobial resistance trends.
