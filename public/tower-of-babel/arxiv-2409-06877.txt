Positive equilibria in mass action networks: geometry and bounds
Murad Banaji 1 and Elisenda Feliu 2

Abstract
. Any mass action network gives rise to a parameterised family of polynomial equations whose positive solutions are the positive equilibria of the network. Here, we consider alternative systems of equations, whose solutions are in smooth, one-to-one correspondence with positive equilibria of the network, and capture degeneracy or nondegeneracy of the corresponding equilibria. The construction leads us to consider partitions of networks in a natural sense, and we explore the implications of choosing different partitions. The alternative systems are in some situations simpler than the original mass action equations, which allows us to rapidly identify various algebraic and geometric properties of the positive equilibrium set. This includes the characterisation of toricity and local toricity, bounds on the number of positive nondegenerate equilibria on stoichiometric classes, semialgebraic descriptions of the parameter regions for multistationarity, and the study of bifurcations. After discussing the construction of the alternative systems, various consequences for particular classes of networks and numerous examples are presented. We also develop additional techniques specifically for quadratic networks, the most common class of networks in applications, and use these techniques to derive strengthened results for quadratic networks. Introduction

Often motivated by problems in systems biology, the question of when chemical reaction networks (CRNs) admit multiple equilibria, and if so how many, has been studied extensively. For example: there are many results giving necessary conditions for multistationarity; some results with sufficient conditions for multistationarity; results providing explicit bounds on the number of equilibria; results on bifurcations leading to multistationarity; and results on properties of the parameter set for multistationarity. Much of the work focuses on CRNs with mass action kinetics (
mass action networks
for short), and this is the setting of this work. A small selection of references on counting equilibria, both in mass action networks and networks with other kinetics, includes: classical early work
[
hornjackson
,
Feinberg1995class
]
; a survey on conditions for multistationarity
[
joshi-shiu-III
]
; papers on injectivity-based approaches to multistationarity
[

,
banajicraciun2
,
feliuwiufSIADS2013
,
MR12
,
Muller2015injectivity
,
shinarfeinbergconcord1
]
; papers on parameter regions for multistationarity
[
conradi2019total
,
Conradi.2016aa
,
Telek2023
]
; and papers on systems with special properties
[
Wang.2008aa
,
Millan2012toricsteadystates
,
Tang2020MultistabilityOS
,
hernandez2023independent
,
Craciun2009toric
,
CraciunHeltonWilliams
]
.

Underlying questions about multistationarity are a deeper set of questions about the geometry of the positive equilibrium set of a mass action network. In particular, what can we infer about this geometry based on the number of species, the number of reactions, the rank of the network, the configuration of its sources, the way that it “factors” over various partitions, and other purely combinatorial properties of the network? In this paper, we derive bounds on the number of positive nondegenerate equilibria and show that easily computed combinatorial properties of a network can be used to infer and characterise toricity or local toricity of the equilibrium set. On the way to these results we explore partitions of a network, defined in a natural way, and how properties of the equilibrium set of a network relate to the way it factors over various partitions. We remark that the main construction in
[
regensburger:gale
]
, and several consequences presented by the authors, are closely related to some of the results we present here.

The broad starting point we take follows the principles of Gale duality
[
bihan-sottile-complete-gale
,
sottile:book
]
, adapted to the specific context of mass action networks. While positive equilibria of a mass action network are, naturally, the positive solutions of a system of polynomial equations, we use these principles to write down
alternative
systems of equations whose solutions on some polyhedron are in smooth, one-to-one correspondence with positive equilibria of the network, perhaps on some stoichiometric class, see in particular
3.14
and
3.21
. The basic idea of the construction is as follows (details are given in
Section

3
): positive equilibria satisfy a polynomial equation of the form
\Gamma(\kappa\circ x^{A})=0
, where
\Gamma
is a matrix,
x^{A}
is a vector of monomials and
\kappa
a vector of positive constants multiplying the monomial vector componentwise. For any fixed
\kappa
, a positive vector
x
satisfies this equation if and only if
\kappa\circ x^{A}=z
for some
z
in the positive part of
\ker\Gamma
.
Given any positive
z
, one can easily recover all
x
satisfying
\kappa\circ x^{A}=z
as, after applying logarithms, this system becomes a linear system. Thus, imposing that
z
lies in the positive part of
\ker\Gamma
, and that
\ln(z/\kappa)
lies in
\mathrm{im}\,A
, one obtains a description of all solutions to the original system using linear algebra alone.

This approach to solving polynomial systems, which extends to zeros over the nonzero complex numbers, appears in many studies (see e.g. the review in
[
dickenstein-binomials
]
), and has proven useful for the study of fewnomial systems
[
bihan:descartes:2
,
bihan:gale
]
. In the context of reaction networks, this idea appears in disguise for example in the deficiency one theorem of Feinberg
[
Feinberg1995class
]
, and explicitly in the language of algebraic geometry in several works of Gatermann and coauthors e.g.
[
gatermann-sparse
, Section 7]
and
[
gatermann-hopf
, Section 3.2]
. The construction provided the basis to study parameter regions for multistationarity in
[
conradi-flockerzi
]
, and to count positive equilibria in
[
helmer:gale
]
. It was used in
[
banajiborosnonlinearity
,
BBH2024smallbif
]
to write down parameterisations of equilibria with a view to studying bifurcations, and it plays a central part in various algorithms used in the study of mass action networks gathered in the library
[
muradgithub
]
. In recent work
[
regensburger:gale
]
, using these ideas, the authors give an explicit description of the solution set of a system of generalised polynomial inequalities of the form
\kappa\circ x^{A}\in C
, where
\kappa
is a positive vector,
C
is a convex cone in the positive orthant, and the exponents in
A
are allowed to be real numbers. Additionally, the authors exploit possible direct product decompositions of the cone
C
. In this work, we restrict our attention to polynomial systems arising from reaction networks, but also consider decompositions of the positive part of
\ker\Gamma
. We arrive at explicit descriptions of the positive equilibrium set of an arbitrary reaction network, and hence at the alternative systems of equations.

Our main concern is to use properties of the alternative systems to make claims about various classes of mass action networks. The alternative systems may include fewer variables and equations and/or have lower degree, than the original systems while, crucially, capturing the cardinality of the equilibrium set and degeneracy or nondegeneracy of equilibria. In some important cases, even when the network has many species and reactions, the alternative system becomes trivial, or reduces to a single polynomial equation in one real variable, allowing one to use techniques such as Descartes’ rule of signs and its generalisations, or Sturm’s theorem (see Chapter 1 of
[
Sturmfels02solvingsystems
]
, for example) to make claims about the positive equilibrium set. Even when not so simple, the alternative system may be easier to analyse than the original mass action equations using tools of computational algebra such as Bézout’s theorem, the Bernstein–Khovanskii–Kushnirenko (BKK) theorem
[
bernstein
]
, or cylindrical algebraic decomposition
[
BasuAlgorithms
, Chapter 11]
.

Particular classes of networks for which we derive bounds on positive nondegenerate equilibria include networks with relatively few reactions or sources compared to the number of species, networks with few or no conservation laws, and networks with few affine dependencies amongst their sources (

and
for example). Specialising to quadratic networks, we get sharper or deeper results (

As discussed above, and also highlighted in
[
regensburger:gale
]
, the construction often leads to explicit parameterisations of the equilibrium set of a mass action network. The theory we develop here is useful in the analysis of bifurcations in mass action networks both because explicit parameterisations of equilibria simplify many computations, and because, as we will show, the alternative equations preserve degeneracy/nondegeneracy of solutions of the original mass action equations. In fact, the analyses of bifurcations in mass action networks developed in
[
banajiborosnonlinearity
,
BBH2024smallbif

relied on special cases of the construction presented here. Moreover, as we show in examples, the approach here can be directly used to identify potential bifurcation sets for bifurcations such as fold and cusp bifurcations
[
kuznetsov:2023

near which the cardinality of the equilibrium set changes. The role of the alternative equations in the study of bifurcations will be explored further in forthcoming work.

Also touched on in examples, but not explored in depth, is the potential of these techniques for characterising the parameter set for multistationarity. In some cases, the results allow an explicit semialgebraic characterisation of the set of rate constants at which a given mass action network admits a given number of equilibria on some stoichiometric class. Deriving such a characterisation by direct examination of the mass action equations can be quite nontrivial even for relatively simple networks.

We present numerous examples to illustrate the power of the techniques we develop; some of these are drawn from the biological literature (see

In particular, nondegenerate multistationarity, and transversally unfolded bifurcations, identified using the techniques described here, can be “lifted” to larger, more complex, networks, see, e.g.,
[
banaji:boros:hofbauer:2024b

Preliminaries on chemical reaction networks

We write
\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\geq 0}:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\,:\,x_{i}\geq 0,\,\,i=1,\ldots,n\}
and
\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\,:\,x_{i}>0,\,\,i=1,\ldots,n\}
for the nonnegative and positive orthants in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
respectively; similar notation is used for these orthants in
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
. Vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\geq 0}
are termed
nonnegative
and vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}
are termed
positive
. For
v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
D_{v}
is the
n\times n
diagonal matrix with diagonal given by
v
.
\mathbb{C}^{*}
denotes the complex torus
\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
.

For a matrix
A=(a_{ij})\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{m\times n}
and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}
, we let
x^{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}_{+}
denote the vector whose
i
th entry is
\prod_{j=1}^{n}x_{j}^{a_{ij}}
. Observe that
A
encodes the exponents in
rows
. Throughout the text, “
\bm{1}
” will denote a column vector of ones whose length is inferred from the context. The symbol “
\circ
” will denote the entrywise product, and we will freely apply various functions and operations entrywise; in particular,
\ln v=(\ln v_{1},\dots,\ln v_{n})
for
v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}
and
u/v=(u_{1}/v_{1},\dots,u_{n}/v_{n})
for
u\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
,
v\in(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{n}
. Given a differentiable map
F
between subsets of Euclidean space,
J_{F}
refers to the Jacobian matrix of
F
.

Throughout, we say a property holds
generically
in
X\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{n}
, if it holds in a nonempty Zariski open subset of
X
, that is, in a nonempty set of the form
X\setminus V
with
V
being the zero locus of a set of polynomials.

Projections and partitions.
Given
X\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
and
B\subseteq\{1,\ldots,m\}
, write
X_{B}
for the orthogonal projection of
X
onto the coordinate hyperplane
\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{m}\,:\,x_{i}=0\mbox{ if }i\not\in B\}
. When
X
is a singleton, say
X=\{v\}
, we write
v_{B}
rather than
\{v\}_{B}
, and note that
v_{B}=\bm{1}_{B}\circ v\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
. Given a partition
\mathcal{P}=\sqcup_{i=1}^{p}P_{i}
of
\{1,\ldots,m\}
, we refer to the subsets
P_{i}
as
blocks
of
\mathcal{P}
. We refer to a partition with
p
blocks as a “
p
-partition”. Given two partitions
\mathcal{P},\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}
of
\{1,\ldots,m\}
, we write
\mathcal{P}\leq\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}
to mean that
\tilde{\mathcal{P}}
is a refinement of
\mathcal{P}
.

Given
X,Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{p}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
, we abuse notation slightly and write
X=Y_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus Y_{p}
to mean that each element of
X
can be written uniquely as a sum
y_{1}+\cdots+y_{p}
where
y_{i}\in Y_{i}
. We say that
X\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}

factors over
\mathcal{P}
if
X=X_{P_{1}}\oplus\cdots\oplus X_{P_{p}}
.
It is easily seen that if
X
factors over two partitions, say
\mathcal{P}
and
\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}
, then it factors over their common refinement.
Assuming that
X\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
is nonempty and nonzero, we refer to the (unique) finest partition over which
X
factors as the
finest partition
associated with
X
. We may also observe that if
X
is a relatively open subset of a linear subspace
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
, then if
X
factors over a partition
\mathcal{P}
,
\mathcal{S}
must also factor over
\mathcal{P}
.
Writing
\bm{1}_{\mathcal{P}}
for the
m\times p
matrix whose
i
th column is
\bm{1}_{P_{i}}
, we will often use the easy fact that a linear subspace
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
factors over
\mathcal{P}
if and only if
(\bm{1}_{\mathcal{P}}\lambda)\circ x\in\mathcal{S}
for all
x\in\mathcal{S},\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
. Consequently, if
\mathcal{S}
includes a vector none of whose components are zero, it must have dimension at least
p
. Another immediate consequence is that if
\mathcal{S}
factors over
\mathcal{P}
, then so does its orthogonal complement
\mathcal{S}^{\perp}
.

Basics on reaction networks.
To avoid extensive repetition, basic notation and terminology on reaction networks are presented only briefly, and the reader is referred to
[
banajiborosnonlinearity
,
BBH2024smallbif
]
for more detail. These concepts will be illustrated in examples throughout the forthcoming sections.

A (chemical)
complex
is a formal linear combination of (chemical) species with nonnegative integer coefficients termed
stoichiometries
. The
molecularity
of a complex is the sum of its coefficients. A (chemical)
reaction
is the conversion of one complex, here termed the
source
, into another distinct complex, here termed the
product
. Mathematically, a reaction is simply an ordered pair of distinct complexes. A
(chemical) reaction network
(
CRN
for short) is a collection of reactions, and when the reactions are assumed to have mass action kinetics we refer to the CRN as a
mass action network
(also sometimes referred to as a
mass action system
in the literature). Henceforth, whenever we refer to a “reaction network”, or “network”, without further qualification, this will mean a mass action network.

Having made some choices of orderings on species and reactions, a mass action network with
n
species and
m
reactions can be characterised by three objects:

•

The
stoichiometric matrix

\Gamma\in\mathbb{Z}^{n\times m}
whose
(i,j)
th entry is the net production of species
i
in reaction
j
.

•

The
exponent matrix

A\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{m\times n}
whose
(i,j)
th entry is the stoichiometry of species
j
in the source of the
i
th reaction.

•

A vector of
rate constants

\kappa\in\mathbb{R}^{m}_{+}
.

The associated mass action ODE system can be written briefly as

(2.1)

\dot{x}=g_{\kappa}(x):=\Gamma(\kappa\circ x^{A})

where
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\geq 0}
is the vector of species concentrations. A standard computation shows that the Jacobian matrix of the right hand side of (
2.1
) evaluated at
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}
is

(2.2)

J_{g_{\kappa}}(x)=\Gamma\,D_{\kappa\circ x^{A}}\,A\,D_{1/x}\,.

A CRN is
quadratic
if all its sources have molecularity two or less; equivalently, each polynomial on the right hand side of (
2.1
) has degree two or less. It is
bimolecular
if all sources
and products
have molecularity two or less.

The
rank

r
of a CRN is defined as

r:=\rank\Gamma\quad\leq\min\{n,m\}\,.

If
r=n
, we say the network has
full rank
. We refer to a CRN with
n
species,
m
reactions and rank
r
as an
\bm{(n,m,r)}
network
.

The
source rank

s
of a CRN is the dimension of the affine hull of its source complexes, considered as points in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Equivalently,

s:=\rank\,[A\,|\,\bm{1}]-1\quad\leq\min\{n,m-1\}\,.

The intersections of cosets of
\mathrm{im}\,\Gamma
with
\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\geq 0}
(resp.,
\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}
) are termed
stoichiometric classes
(resp.,
positive stoichiometric classes
) of the CRN. Positive stoichiometric classes are easily seen to be locally invariant under (
2.1
). A stoichiometric class is defined by equations

(2.3)

Zx-K=0\,,\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\geq 0}\,,

where
K\in\mathbb{R}^{n-r}
is a vector of real constants, called
total amounts
, and
Z\in\mathbb{R}^{(n-r)\times n}
is a matrix whose rows form a basis of
\ker\Gamma^{\top}
called a
matrix of conservation laws
. When referring to total amounts, we implicitly assume a choice of
Z
has been made.

We refer to
\mathcal{C}:=\ker\Gamma\cap\mathbb{R}^{m}_{+}
as the
positive flux cone
of the CRN
[
Clarke:1980tz
]
. A CRN is termed
dynamically trivial
if
\mathcal{C}=\emptyset
and
dynamically nontrivial
otherwise. Dynamically trivial CRNs admit no positive equilibria (and, in fact, no omega-limit sets intersecting the positive orthant). On the other hand, dynamically nontrivial mass action networks admit positive equilibria for at least some choices of rate constants
[
banajiCRNcount
]
. Dynamically nontrivial CRNs are also sometimes called “consistent” (e.g.,
[
JoshiShiu2017
]
).

If
\mathcal{C}\neq\emptyset
, then the closure
\overline{\mathcal{C}}
of
\mathcal{C}
, namely
\ker\Gamma\cap\mathbb{R}^{m}_{\geq 0}
, is a closed, pointed, convex, polyhedral cone, satisfying
\mathrm{dim}\,\mathcal{C}=\mathrm{dim}\,\overline{\mathcal{C}}=m-r
. If
m-r\leq 2
, then
\overline{\mathcal{C}}
is simplicial, i.e., it has exactly
m-r
extremal vectors (unique up to scaling); however, if
m-r\geq 3
, then
\overline{\mathcal{C}}
need not be simplicial.

Positive, nondegenerate equilibria.
For fixed
\kappa\in\mathbb{R}^{m}_{+}
, we consider the set of positive equilibria of (
2.1
), namely,

\mathcal{E}_{\kappa}:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}:\Gamma(\kappa\circ x^{A})=0\}.

It is clear that, for any fixed
\kappa\in\mathbb{R}^{m}_{+}
,
x\in\mathcal{E}_{\kappa}
if and only if

(2.4)

\kappa\circ x^{A}\in\mathcal{C}\,.

We say that
x^{*}\in\mathcal{E}_{\kappa}
is
nondegenerate
if
J_{g_{\kappa}}(x^{*})
acts as a nonsingular transformation on
\mathrm{im}\,\Gamma
, i.e.,
\ker J_{g_{\kappa}}(x^{*})\cap\mathrm{im}\,\Gamma=\{0\}
; and it is
degenerate
otherwise
[
banajipantea
]
. Equivalently,
x^{*}
is nondegenerate if it is a nondegenerate equilibrium, in the usual sense, of the
restriction
of (
2.1
) to the stoichiometric class on which
x^{*}
lies. Note that, by the implicit function theorem, a positive nondegenerate equilibrium is necessarily isolated on its stoichiometric class.

It is convenient also to define
x^{*}\in\mathcal{E}_{\kappa}
to be
strongly degenerate
if
\mathrm{rank}\,J_{g_{\kappa}}(x^{*})<r
. Note that a strongly degenerate equilibrium is degenerate, but the converse is not necessarily true; however, for networks of full rank, “strongly degenerate” and “degenerate” are clearly equivalent.

A reaction network is termed
nondegenerate
if it admits a positive nondegenerate equilibrium for some rate constants, and
degenerate
otherwise. A network is
strongly degenerate
if every positive equilibrium of the network is strongly degenerate. Dynamically trivial networks are included amongst the strongly degenerate (hence also the degenerate) networks.

From the general form (
2.2
) of the Jacobian matrix of a mass action network evaluated at a positive equilibrium, and using (
2.4
),
a mass action network is strongly degenerate if

(2.5)

\mathrm{rank}(\Gamma\,D_{v}\,A)<r

for all
v\in\mathcal{C}
; and is nondegenerate if and only if
\Gamma D_{v}A
has an
r\times r
principal minor which is not identically zero. The latter is easily inferred using the Cauchy-Binet formula (see e.g.
[
banajipantea
, Appendix A]
, or
[
feliuwiufSIADS2013
, Prop. 5.3]
). Equivalently, the network is nondegenerate if and only if the matrix

(2.6)

\begin{pmatrix}\Gamma\,D_{v}\,A\,D_{x}\\
Z\end{pmatrix}\,

has rank
n
for some
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}
and
v\in\mathcal{C}
, with
Z
being any matrix of conservation laws,
see e.g.
[
feliuwiufSIADS2013
, Prop. 5.2]
. As this condition holds for almost all values of
x,v
if it holds for one, the condition can be easily verified by considering a random choice.

We recall the following results on dimension, nondegeneracy and emptiness of equilibrium sets from
[
feliu:dimension
, Thm 3.1, 3.4]
; see also
[
feliu:vertical
, Thm 3.7]
.

Proposition 2.1
.

Given a reaction network, let
\mathcal{Z}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}_{+}
be the set of rate constants
\kappa
for which
\mathcal{E}_{\kappa}\neq\emptyset
, and let
\mathcal{Z}_{sc}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{n-r}
be the set of rate constants and total amounts
(\kappa,K)
for which the corresponding stoichiometric class intersects
\mathcal{E}_{\kappa}
.
Then:

•

The network is strongly degenerate if and only if
\mathcal{Z}
has empty Euclidean interior, equivalently
\mathcal{E}_{\kappa}
is generically empty.

•

The network is degenerate if and only if
\mathcal{Z}_{sc}
has empty Euclidean interior, equivalently for generic choices of
(\kappa,K)
,
\mathcal{E}_{\kappa}
does not intersect the stoichiometric class defined by
K
.

Additionally:

(i)

If the network is not strongly degenerate, then, for generic
\kappa
in
\mathcal{Z}
, the dimension of
\mathcal{E}_{\kappa}
is
n-r
.

(ii)

If the network is nondegenerate, then (i) applies and also, for generic
(\kappa,K)
in
\mathcal{Z}_{sc}
, the stoichiometric class defined by
K
contains a finite number of positive equilibria in
\mathcal{E}_{\kappa}
, and these are all nondegenerate.

We emphasise that
Proposition

2.1
tells us that the existence of a positive nondegenerate equilibrium for some choice of rate constants is the only requirement for the conclusions of (ii) to hold. A degenerate network that is not strongly degenerate will have
\mathcal{Z}_{sc}
with empty Euclidean interior while the interior of
\mathcal{Z}
will be nonempty. In particular (i) will hold but (ii) will not.

In
Section

4.1
we consider a class of (strongly) degenerate networks, which we will refer to as “overdetermined”, for which the dimension of
\mathcal{E}_{\kappa}
is always larger than
n-r
when nonempty. The latter might not hold for an arbitrary strongly degenerate network, see
[
feliu:dimension
, Example 3.10]
.

Naive Bézout and BKK bounds.
For any fixed
\kappa
, the right hand side of (
2.1
) is a vector of
n
polynomials
g_{\kappa,1}(x),\dots,g_{\kappa,n}(x)
in the variables
x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
. Given any
r\times m
matrix
\widehat{\Gamma}
whose rows form a basis of the row-span of
\Gamma
, let
\hat{g}_{\kappa}(x):=\widehat{\Gamma}(\kappa\circ x^{A})
. Then positive equilibria on the stoichiometric class with total amount
K
are precisely the positive solutions to the square system

(2.7)

\displaystyle\hat{g}_{\kappa,j}(x)

\displaystyle=0\qquad

\displaystyle j=1,\ldots,r,

\displaystyle Z\,x-K

\displaystyle=0\,,

where
Z\in\mathbb{R}^{(n-r)\times n}
is a matrix of conservation laws.
By Bézout’s theorem, the product of degrees of
\hat{g}_{\kappa,j}
(
j=1,\ldots,r
) provides an upper bound on the number of nondegenerate solutions to (
2.7
) in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
, hence on the number of positive nondegenerate equilibria of (
2.1
) on any stoichiometric class. We refer to any such bound as a
naive Bézout bound
. In general, the choice of
\widehat{\Gamma}
is not unique, and so naive Bézout bounds are not in general unique.

We may also consider the BKK theorem, which establishes that the mixed volume of the polynomials on the left hand side of (
2.7
) provides an upper bound on the number of nondegenerate solutions in
(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{n}
, hence of positive nondegenerate equilibria on any stoichiometric class. This bound, here called a
naive BKK bound
, is no larger than the naive Bézout bound for the same choice of rows of
\Gamma
.

If we know the sources and the rank of a network, but not its products, then we know all the monomials that might appear in (
2.7
) but not their coefficients. In particular, if
d
is the maximum degree of the monomials in
x^{A}
, then all naive Bézout bounds are clearly bounded above by
d^{r}
; we refer to
d^{r}
as the
naive Bézout source bound
of the network.
Similarly, by letting
P
be the convex hull of all sources of the network, and
\Delta
the standard
n
-dimensional simplex with vertices the origin and the standard vectors of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the BKK theorem tells us that the number of positive nondegenerate equilibria is at most the mixed volume of
r
copies of
P
and
n-r
copies of
\Delta
. We refer to this bound as the
naive BKK source bound
which, by the monotonic properties of mixed volumes, is not lower than any naive BKK bound.

Fewnomial bounds on positive equilibria.

The previous are bounds on the number of nondegenerate solutions to (
2.7
) in
(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{n}
or
\mathbb{C}^{n}
. We give now, for later use, some bounds on the number of nondegenerate
positive
equilibria based on the number of monomials of (
2.7
). The main technique behind these bounds is the rewriting of the system of equations using
Gale duality
. We will employ these same principles throughout the remainder of this work.

Proposition 2.2
.

For a system of
n\geq 2
real polynomial equations in
n
variables and with
n+k+1
monomials, the following holds:

(i)

If
k<0
, then the system has no positive solution for generic choices of the coefficients.

(ii)

If
k=0
, then the system has either no positive solution, one positive nondegenerate solution, or infinitely many positive solutions.

(iii)

If
k=1
, then the system has at most
n+1
positive nondegenerate solutions.

The first statement is a consequence of the BKK bound, while a proof of the second statement can be found e.g. in
[
Bihan:positive_decorated
, Prop. 3.3]
. Bihan gave the case
k=1
in
[
Bihan:circuit
, Prop. 2.1]
, and sharper bounds in the spirit of the univariate Descartes’ rule of signs and which take into account the exponents and the coefficient matrix, are given in
[
bihan:descartes:1
,
bihan:descartes:2
]
. Bounds for
k\geq 2
are given in
[
bihan:gale
]
, but these are too large to be of interest for the purposes of this work.

3.
Existence and number of positive equilibria: general theory

In this section, we follow the principles of Gale duality (see
[
bihan-sottile-complete-gale
,
sottile:book
]
) to provide a parameterisation of the set of positive equilibria of a mass action network, namely, Equation (
3.11
) in
Theorem

3.14
(i). We then explore properties of this parameterisation, and use it to derive the alternative systems of equations in Equation (
3.18
) in
Theorem

3.21
, which are used heavily in subsequent results on various special classes of networks. We use the language and exposition from
[
BBH2024smallbif
, Section 2]
, but note that closely related ideas have been exploited in several previous works about reaction networks
[
helmer:gale
,
kaihnsa:connectivity
]
and in the recent framework for systems of polynomial inequalities
[
regensburger:gale
]
. We consider decompositions of the positive flux cone as in
[
bryan:partition2
,
bryan:partition
,
feliu:toric
]
. In
[
regensburger:gale
]
, decompositions of cones are also considered.

While the construction we present below closely follows the template developed in
[
banajiborosnonlinearity
,
BBH2024smallbif
]
, where special cases of this construction were considered, it can also be derived from
[
bihan-sottile-complete-gale
]
when considering the trivial partition. It is also closely related to the construction in
[
regensburger:gale
, Theorem 5]
, and in
Remark

3.15
, we elaborate further on this relationship. Despite the existence of related results, we present the construction in full here, keeping the paper self-contained and allowing us to develop machinery and notation which are subsequently reused in various other claims, including about nondegeneracy of equilibria.

Throughout the section, we consider an arbitrary dynamically nontrivial
(n,m,r)
mass action network
\mathcal{N}
with stoichiometric matrix
\Gamma
, exponent matrix
A
, and positive flux cone
\mathcal{C}:=\ker\Gamma\cap\mathbb{R}^{m}_{+}
.

3.1.
Partition subnetworks

Let us assume that
\mathcal{C}
, hence, by our earlier remarks on partitions,
\ker\Gamma
, factor over some
p
-partition
\mathcal{P}=\{P_{1},\ldots,P_{p}\}
of
\{1,\ldots,m\}
. Note that this assumption is without loss of generality as we may always choose
\mathcal{P}
to be the trivial partition, in which case
p=1
. In this case, we say that
\mathcal{N}

factors over
\bm{\mathcal{P}}
. (Whenever this terminology is used, it will always be implicitly assumed that the network in question is dynamically nontrivial.) As
\dim\ker\Gamma=m-r
and
\mathcal{C}\neq\emptyset
, we have
m-r\geq p
. Each subnetwork
\mathcal{N}_{i}
having the same species as
\mathcal{N}
but only the reactions indexed by
P_{i}
is called a
partition subnetwork
of
\mathcal{N}
.

The partition
\mathcal{P}
induces a partition on the set of reactions, hence on the sets of rate constants, of columns of
\Gamma
, and of rows of
A
. However, the partition need not induce a partition on the set of sources, as the same source may occur in reactions in distinct blocks of
\mathcal{P}
.

Set
m_{i}:=|P_{i}|
, and write
\Gamma^{(i)}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n\times m_{i}}
for the submatrix of
\Gamma
with columns indexed by
P_{i}
;
A^{(i)}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{m_{i}\times n}
for the submatrix of
A
consisting of rows indexed by
P_{i}
; and
\kappa^{(i)}\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{i}}_{+}
for the subvector of
\kappa
with entries indexed by
P_{i}
.
An important and easy observation is that
\ker\Gamma
factors over
\mathcal{P}
if and only if

(3.1)

\mathrm{im}\,\Gamma=\mathrm{im}\,\Gamma^{(1)}\oplus\cdots\oplus\mathrm{im}\,\Gamma^{(p)}\,.

Consequently the rank of a network is the sum of the ranks of its partition subnetworks. Equilibria of the network are solutions of
0=\Gamma(\kappa\circ x^{A})=\sum_{i=1}^{p}\Gamma^{(i)}(\kappa^{(i)}\circ x^{A^{(i)}})
. From (
3.1
), this is equivalent to

\Gamma^{(i)}(\kappa^{(i)}\circ x^{A^{(i)}})=0,\,\qquad i=1,\ldots,p\,,

namely, equilibria of
\mathcal{N}
are precisely the common equilibria of the partition subnetworks
\mathcal{N}_{i}
. If, for example, some
\mathcal{N}_{i}
has no positive equilibria, the same holds for
\mathcal{N}
. We will later make use of the following result.

Lemma 3.1
.

Assume that a dynamically nontrivial
(n,m,r)
network
\mathcal{N}
factors over a
p
-partition
\mathcal{P}
of
\{1,\ldots,m\}
. Fix
\kappa\in\mathbb{R}^{m}_{+}
, and suppose that some positive equilibrium
x
of
\mathcal{N}
is a strongly degenerate equilibrium of the partition subnetwork
\mathcal{N}_{j}
for some
j
.
Then
x
is also strongly degenerate as an equilibrium of
\mathcal{N}
. Consequently, if, for some
\kappa
and some
j
, all equilibria of
\mathcal{N}_{j}
are strongly degenerate, then this holds for
\mathcal{N}
. If, for some
j
,
\mathcal{N}_{j}
is strongly degenerate, then so is
\mathcal{N}
.

Proof.

With the notation above we write
\Gamma=[\Gamma^{(1)}\,|\,\cdots\,|\,\Gamma^{(p)}]
. Fix
\kappa\in\mathbb{R}^{m}_{+}
and a positive equilibrium
x
of
\mathcal{N}
. Recall that
x
is also an equilibrium of
\mathcal{N}_{j}
for each
j
. Let
r_{i}=\rank\Gamma^{(i)}
so that, by (
3.1
), we have
r=r_{1}+\dots+r_{p}
.
Using (
2.2
) we obtain

J:=J_{g_{\kappa}}(x)=\Gamma\,D_{\kappa\circ x^{A}}\,A\,D_{1/x}=\sum_{i=1}^{p}J_{i}\,,\quad\mbox{where}\quad J_{i}:=\Gamma^{(i)}\,D_{\kappa^{(i)}\circ x^{A^{(i)}}}A^{(i)}D_{1/x}\,.

But
\rank J_{i}\leq\rank\Gamma^{(i)}=r_{i}
for all
i
, and if
x
is a strongly degenerate equilibrium of
\mathcal{N}_{j}
, then
\mathrm{rank}\,J_{j}<r_{j}
. As
\mathrm{im}\,J=\mathrm{im}\,J_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus\mathrm{im}\,J_{p}
, and hence
\mathrm{rank}\,J=\sum_{i}\mathrm{rank}\,J_{i}
, it follows that
\mathrm{rank}\,J<r
, and so
x
is strongly degenerate as an equilibrium of
\mathcal{N}
. The remaining claims now follow immediately.
∎

3.2.
The main equation for positive equilibria

We present an important equation satisfied by positive equilibria of a mass action network, a special case of which appeared as Equation 2 in
[
BBH2024smallbif
]
. We assume that the network factors over a
p
-partition
\mathcal{P}
.

We begin by parameterising the positive flux cone
\mathcal{C}
in a natural way. Write
\mathcal{C}_{i}:=\mathcal{C}_{P_{i}}
for tidiness, so that
\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{C}_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus\mathcal{C}_{p}
. For each
i=1,\ldots,p
, choose a flat cross section of
\mathcal{C}_{i}
, say
\widehat{\mathcal{C}}_{i}
, defined as the intersection of
\mathcal{C}_{i}
with any hyperplane in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
which intersects each ray of
\mathcal{C}_{i}
exactly once. Write down an affine injective map

h^{(i)}\colon Y_{i}\longrightarrow\widehat{\mathcal{C}_{i}}

with domain
Y_{i}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{\dim\mathcal{C}_{i}-1}
an open polyhedron. We can now write a general point in
\mathcal{C}
uniquely as
\sum_{i=1}^{p}\lambda_{i}\,h^{(i)}(\alpha^{(i)})
with
(\lambda_{i},\alpha^{(i)})\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\times Y_{i}
. Writing

Y:=Y_{1}\times\cdots\times Y_{p}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m-r-p},\quad\quad\widehat{\mathcal{C}}:=\widehat{\mathcal{C}}_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus\widehat{\mathcal{C}}_{p}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}_{+}\,,

and

\alpha:=(\alpha^{(1)},\ldots,\alpha^{(p)})=(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{m-r-p})
for an element of
Y
,
we define the affine injective map

(3.2)

h:=h^{(1)}\oplus\cdots\oplus h^{(p)}\colon\quad Y\longrightarrow\ \widehat{\mathcal{C}}\,\qquad(\alpha^{(1)},\ldots,\alpha^{(p)})\mapsto\sum_{i=1}^{p}\,h^{(i)}(\alpha^{(i)})\,.

We may naturally extend the domain of
h
to all of
\mathbb{R}^{m-r-p}
if needed. In the special case where
\mathcal{C}_{i}
is one dimensional, and so
\widehat{\mathcal{C}_{i}}
consists of a single point, say
v^{(i)}
, we may simply write
h^{(i)}(\alpha^{(i)})=v^{(i)}
.

Write
\lambda:=(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{p})^{\top}
. For given
\kappa\in\mathbb{R}^{m}_{+}
, (
2.4
) is satisfied by some
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}
if and only if there exist
(\lambda,\alpha)\in\mathbb{R}^{p}_{+}\times Y
such that

(3.3)

\kappa\circ x^{A}=\sum_{i=1}^{p}\lambda_{i}\,h^{(i)}(\alpha^{(i)})=(\bm{1}_{\mathcal{P}}\lambda)\circ h(\alpha)\,.

We remark that, by construction, each set of the form
\{(\bm{1}_{\mathcal{P}}\lambda)\circ h(\alpha)\,:\,\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{p}_{+}\}
intersects
\widehat{\mathcal{C}}
exactly once, namely at
h(\alpha)
, and that these sets partition
\mathcal{C}
.

Remark 3.2
.

Using (
2.2
) and (
3.3
), the Jacobian matrix of
g_{\kappa}
at
x\in\mathcal{E}_{\kappa}
, can be written

(3.4)

J_{g_{\kappa}}(x):=\Gamma\,D_{(\bm{1}_{\mathcal{P}}\lambda)\circ h(\alpha)}\,A\,D_{1/x}\,,

where
\lambda
and
\alpha
are (uniquely) determined by (
3.3
).

We can take logarithms and rearrange (
3.3
) to get

(3.5)

\ln\kappa+[A\,|\,\bm{1}_{\mathcal{P}}]\,\left(\begin{array}[]{r}\ln x\\
-\ln\lambda\end{array}\right)=\ln h(\alpha)\,.

Equation (
3.5
) is the crucial equation satisfied by positive equilibria of a mass action network. The special case of Equation (
3.5
) where
\mathcal{P}
is trivial, namely
\bm{1}_{\mathcal{P}}=\bm{1}
, appeared in
[
BBH2024smallbif
]
.
A similar equation appeared in
[
conradi-flockerzi
, Eq. (12a)-(12l)]
, where the authors consider a parameterisation of the positive flux cone, instead of restricting to a section.

Remark 3.3

(The geometry of Equation (
3.5
))
.

As
(\kappa,x,\lambda)
varies in
\mathbb{R}^{m}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{p}_{+}
, the left hand side of (
3.5
) defines a family of cosets of the hyperplane
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{P}}:=\mathrm{im}\,[A\,|\,\bm{1}_{\mathcal{P}}]
in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
. As
\ln\,h(\cdot)
is a smooth, injective, immersion of
Y
into
\mathbb{R}^{m}
, the right hand side of (
3.5
) defines an
(m-r-p)
-dimensional manifold

(3.6)

\mathcal{Y}:=\big\{\ln h(\alpha)\,:\,\alpha\in Y\big\}\,\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}\,.

For any fixed
\kappa\in\mathbb{R}^{m}_{+}
, points of intersection of
\mathcal{Y}
and
\ln\kappa+\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{P}}
correspond to solutions of (
3.5
) and hence to positive equilibria of (
2.1
). This correspondence is one-to-one if and only if
\ker\,[A\,|\,\bm{1}_{\mathcal{P}}]
is trivial.

Remark 3.4

(Decompositions of Equation (
3.5
))
.

Observe that solutions to (
3.5
) are precisely the common solutions to the corresponding set of equations for each block, namely, for
i=1,\ldots,p
,

(3.7)

\ln\kappa^{(i)}+[A^{(i)}\,|\,\bm{1}]\,\left(\begin{array}[]{r}\ln x\\
-\ln\lambda_{i}\end{array}\right)=\ln\,h_{i}(\alpha^{(i)})\,.

where
h_{i}(\alpha^{(i)})
refers to the subvector of
h^{(i)}(\alpha^{(i)})
indexed by
P_{i}
.
More precisely,
(x,\lambda,\alpha,\kappa)
together solve (
3.5
), if and only if
(x,\lambda_{i},\alpha^{(i)},\kappa^{(i)})
together solve (
3.7
) for each
i=1,\ldots,p
. Of course, the
i
th equation is precisely the equation we would derive if our goal was to find positive equilibria of the
i
th partition subnetwork (using the trivial partition).

The next lemma tells us that associated with the parameterisation
h
are natural decompositions of
\ker\Gamma
.
Recall that
h\colon Y\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m-r-p}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}_{+}
is an affine injective map with constant Jacobian matrix, say
J_{h}
. Define
\mathcal{H}
to be the unique
(m-r-p)
-dimensional linear subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{m}
parallel to
\mathrm{im}\,h
, i.e.,
\mathcal{H}:=\mathrm{im}\,J_{h}
when
m>r+p
and
\mathcal{H}=\{0\}
when
m=r+p
.

Lemma 3.5
.

Consider a dynamically nontrivial
(n,m,r)
network which factors over a
p
-partition
\mathcal{P}
. Given
c\in\widehat{\mathcal{C}}
, define
\mathcal{S}_{c}:=\{c\circ(\bm{1}_{\mathcal{P}}\lambda):\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{p}\}
.

(i)

\mathcal{H}\subseteq\ker\Gamma
and
\mathcal{H}
factors over
\mathcal{P}
.

(ii)

Given
c\in\widehat{\mathcal{C}}
,
\mathcal{H}
and
\mathcal{S}_{c}
have trivial intersection.

(iii)

For any
c\in\widehat{\mathcal{C}}
, we have
\ker\Gamma=\mathcal{H}\oplus\mathcal{S}_{c}
, so that, for any fixed
\alpha\in Y
,

(3.8)

\ker\Gamma=\{J_{h}z+(\bm{1}_{\mathcal{P}}\lambda)\circ h(\alpha):(z,\lambda)\in\mathbb{R}^{m-r-p}\times\mathbb{R}^{p}\}\,.

Proof.

In the case when
m=r+p
,
\widehat{\mathcal{C}}
is a singleton,
\mathcal{H}=\{0\}
, and all the claims become immediate; so assume
m>r+p
.

(i) That
\mathcal{H}\subseteq\ker\Gamma
is obvious. To see that
\mathcal{H}
factors over
\mathcal{P}
, we observe that
J_{h}
has block diagonal structure, namely
\Pi_{P_{i}}J_{h}=J_{h}\Pi_{i}
, where for each
i
,
\Pi_{P_{i}}
is the natural projection of
\mathbb{R}^{m}
onto
\mathbb{R}^{m}_{P_{i}}
, and
\Pi_{i}
is the natural projection of
\mathrm{span}\,Y
onto
\mathrm{span}\,Y_{i}
, regarded as a subspace of
\mathrm{span}\,Y
.

(ii) Suppose the claim is false, and
(\bm{1}_{\mathcal{P}}\hat{\lambda})\circ h(\alpha)=J_{h}z
for some
\alpha\in Y
,
\hat{\lambda}\neq 0
and
z\neq 0
. Choose
\varepsilon>0
small enough that (a)
\beta:=\alpha+\varepsilon z\in Y
and (b)
\bm{1}+\varepsilon\hat{\lambda}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}_{+}
. Define
\lambda:=\varepsilon\hat{\lambda}
. Then
(\bm{1}_{\mathcal{P}}\lambda)\circ h(\alpha)=J_{h}(\varepsilon z)=h(\beta)-h(\alpha)
; consequently
h(\beta)=(\bm{1}_{\mathcal{P}}(\bm{1}+\lambda))\circ h(\alpha)
. The fact that
h(\alpha),(\bm{1}_{\mathcal{P}}(\bm{1}+\lambda))\circ h(\alpha)\in\widehat{\mathcal{C}}
with
\bm{1}_{\mathcal{P}}(\bm{1}+\lambda)\neq\bm{1}
contradicts the definition of
\widehat{\mathcal{C}}
.

(iii) Fix
c\in\widehat{\mathcal{C}}
. By (i),
\mathcal{H}\subseteq\ker\Gamma
is an
(m-r-p)
-dimensional linear subspace, while
\mathcal{S}_{c}\subseteq\ker\Gamma
is a
p
-dimensional linear subspace. Moreover,
\mathcal{H}
and
\mathcal{S}_{c}
have trivial intersection from (ii).
∎

3.3.
Four matrices associated with a general mass action network

Consider a dynamically nontrivial
(n,m,r)
network that factors over a
p
-partition
\mathcal{P}
of
\{1,\dots,m\}
. Define

s_{\mathcal{P}}:=\mathrm{rank}\,[A\,|\,\bm{1}_{\mathcal{P}}]-p\ \geq 0\,.

In the case
p=1
,
s_{\mathcal{P}}
is the source rank of the network. Clearly
s_{\mathcal{P}}\leq\min\{n,m-p\}
.

We will be interested in the following matrices
W
,
Q
,
Z
and
G
which are not, in general, uniquely defined. Moreover,
W
,
Q
and
G
depend on the partition
\mathcal{P}
; when we wish to emphasise this, we may write
W_{\mathcal{P}}
, and so forth.

•

An
(m-p-s_{\mathcal{P}})\times m
full rank matrix
W
, termed a
solvability matrix
for reasons to become clear below, whose rows are a basis of
\ker\,[A\,|\,\bm{1}_{\mathcal{P}}]^{\top}
, i.e., such that

W\,[A\,|\,\bm{1}_{\mathcal{P}}]=0\,.

•

An
(n+p)\times(n-s_{\mathcal{P}})
full rank matrix
Q
whose columns are a basis of
\ker\,[A\,|\,\bm{1}_{\mathcal{P}}]
, i.e.,

[A\,|\,\bm{1}_{\mathcal{P}}]\,Q=0\,.

Also define
\widehat{Q}
to be the
n\times(n-s_{\mathcal{P}})
matrix consisting of the first
n
rows of
Q
, which we will refer to as a
toricity matrix
for reasons to become clear below. It is easily confirmed that
\ker\widehat{Q}=\{0\}
, hence
\rank\widehat{Q}=\rank Q=n-s_{\mathcal{P}}
.

•

An
(n+p)\times m
matrix
G
which is a
generalised inverse
of
[A\,|\,\bm{1}_{\mathcal{P}}]

[
James1978TheGI
]
, i.e., such that

[A\,|\,\bm{1}_{\mathcal{P}}]\,G\,[A\,|\,\bm{1}_{\mathcal{P}}]=[A\,|\,\bm{1}_{\mathcal{P}}]\,.

Also define
\widehat{G}
to be the
n\times m
matrix consisting of the first
n
rows of
G
.

•

Recall the
(n-r)\times n
full rank matrix
Z
of
conservation laws
, whose rows form a basis of
\ker\Gamma^{\top}
, i.e., such that

Z\,\Gamma=0\,.

Remark 3.6

(Empty matrices)
.

Some or all of
W
,
Q
and
Z
may be empty:

•

Z
is empty if and only if the network has full rank, i.e.
r=n
. We explore this special case in some detail in the sections to follow.

•

W
is empty if and only if
[A\,|\,\bm{1}_{\mathcal{P}}]
is a surjective matrix, i.e.
m=\mathrm{rank}\,[A\,|\,\bm{1}_{\mathcal{P}}]=s_{\mathcal{P}}+p
. In this case we will say that the sources are
\mathcal{P}
-independent
. In the case
p=1
,
\mathcal{P}
-independence is precisely affine independence of the sources.

•

Q
is empty if and only if
[A\,|\,\bm{1}_{\mathcal{P}}]
is an injective matrix, i.e.
n=s_{\mathcal{P}}
. In this case,
G
is a left inverse of
[A\,|\,\bm{1}_{\mathcal{P}}]
.

•

If
W
and
Z
are empty, then so is
Q
:
m=s_{\mathcal{P}}+p
and
r=n
together give
s_{\mathcal{P}}=m-p\geq r=n
, hence
s_{\mathcal{P}}=n
.

•

If
W
and
Q
are empty, then
m=n+p
, consequently
[A\,|\,\bm{1}_{\mathcal{P}}]
is an invertible square matrix, and
G
is its ordinary inverse.

In what follows we will assume that
W,Q
and
Z
have been chosen to be integer matrices whenever they are nonempty.
On the other hand,
G
has rational entries, and it may or may not be possible to choose it to be an integer matrix.

Remark 3.7
.

Only
Z
requires knowledge of reaction vectors of the network. The matrices
W
,
Q
and
G
are associated only with the multiset of sources of the network and the partition
\mathcal{P}
. This observation will allow us often to make simultaneous claims for all networks with a given multiset of sources.

Remark 3.8

(Geometrical interpretation of the toricity matrix
\widehat{Q}
)
.

Geometrically, each column of
\widehat{Q}
is orthogonal to the affine hull of the sources of the reactions in each block of
\mathcal{P}
. In particular, the columns of
\widehat{Q}
form a basis of the intersection of the orthogonal complements of the directions of each of these affine hulls. Note that the latter may be strictly larger than the orthogonal complement of the direction of the affine hull of the sources.

Example 3.9

(When
p=1
,
W
encodes affine dependencies amongst the sources)
.

Consider the trivial partition and a
2
-species and
5
-reaction network with sources,
[A\,|\,\bm{1}]
, and choice of
W
as shown:
\bullet
\bullet
\bullet
\bullet
\bullet
\mathsf{0}
\mathsf{X}
2\mathsf{X}
\mathsf{X}+\mathsf{Y}
2\mathsf{Y}
[A\,|\,\bm{1}]=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}0&0&1\\
1&0&1\\
2&0&1\\
1&1&1\\
0&2&1\end{array}\right)
W=\left(\begin{array}[]{rrrrr}-1&2&-1&0&0\\
0&0&1&-2&1\end{array}\right)
.

The first row of
W
tells us about the affine dependency amongst
\mathsf{0}
,
\mathsf{X}
and
2\mathsf{X}
, while the second tells us about the affine dependency amongst
2\mathsf{X}
,
\mathsf{X}+\mathsf{Y}
and
2\mathsf{Y}
. These choices for rows of
W
are minimal in the sense that
\{\mathsf{0},\mathsf{X},2\mathsf{X}\}
and
\{2\mathsf{X},\mathsf{X}+\mathsf{Y},2\mathsf{Y}\}
are the smallest affinely dependent subsets of the sources.
∎

Affine independence of the sources implies
\mathcal{P}
-independence, but if
p>1
, the converse does not necessarily hold as we see in the following example.

Example 3.10

(Affinely dependent but
\mathcal{P}
-independent sources)
.

Consider the following dynamically nontrivial
(2,4,2)
network

The following lemma on the choice of
W
will be used frequently. Given an
(n,m,r)
network which factors over a partition
\mathcal{P}
, let us say that indices
i
and

-equivalent

\mathcal{P}
and correspond to reactions with the same source.

Lemma 3.11

(A useful choice of
W
)
.

Consider an
(n,m,r)
network
\mathcal{N}
which factors over a
p
-partition
\mathcal{P}
. Let
m_{i}:=|P_{i}|
,
\ell_{i}
be the number of distinct sources in
P_{i}
, and
\ell:=\sum_{i}\ell_{i}
. Define
s_{i}
to be the source rank of the partition subnetwork
\mathcal{N}_{i}
, namely,
s_{i}:=\mathrm{rank}[A^{(i)}\,|\,\bm{1}]-1
, and let
\widehat{s}:=\sum_{i}s_{i}
. Then there exists a matrix
W
whose rows form a basis
\mathcal{B}=\mathcal{B}_{1}\sqcup\mathcal{B}_{2}\sqcup\mathcal{B}_{3}
of
\ker\,[A\,|\,\bm{1}_{\mathcal{P}}]^{\top}
such that:

•

\mathcal{B}_{1}
consists of
m-\ell
“trivial” vectors, each with two nonzero entries,
1
and
-1
, supported on a pair of
\mathcal{P}
-equivalent indices.

•

\mathcal{B}_{2}
consists of
\ell-\widehat{s}-p
vectors, each supported on exactly one block of
\mathcal{P}
and with support not including a pair of
\mathcal{P}
-equivalent indices.

•

\mathcal{B}_{3}
consists of
\widehat{s}-s_{\mathcal{P}}
vectors, each supported on more than one block of
\mathcal{P}
, and with support not including a pair of
\mathcal{P}
-equivalent indices.

Proof.

For each
i
, we can clearly choose
m_{i}-\ell_{i}
linearly independent trivial vectors in
\ker\,[A\,|\,\bm{1}_{\mathcal{P}}]^{\top}
, each supported on
P_{i}
. Together, these give the
m-\ell
vectors in
\mathcal{B}_{1}
.

For each
i
, choose any additional
\ell_{i}-s_{i}-1
vectors in
\ker\,[A\,|\,\bm{1}_{\mathcal{P}}]^{\top}
supported on
P_{i}
such that the total of
m_{i}-s_{i}-1
vectors supported on
P_{i}
are a linearly independent set. Let
\mathcal{B}_{2}^{\prime}
be the set formed by the union of these vectors.
Choose any additional
\widehat{s}-s_{\mathcal{P}}
vectors forming a set
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}_{3}
, such that
\mathcal{B}_{1}\cup\mathcal{B}^{\prime}_{2}\cup\mathcal{B}^{\prime}_{3}
is a basis of
\ker\,[A\,|\,\bm{1}_{\mathcal{P}}]^{\top}
.

By adding linear combinations of trivial vectors in
\mathcal{B}_{1}
to the vectors in
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}_{2}
(resp.,
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}_{3}
) if necessary, we obtain sets of vectors
\mathcal{B}_{2}
(resp.,
\mathcal{B}_{3}
) whose support does not include
\mathcal{P}
-equivalent indices. (Note that the support of a vector in
\mathcal{B}_{3}
may still include indices of two reactions with the same source belonging to different blocks of
\mathcal{P}
.)
∎

The following technical lemma simplifies several proofs.

Lemma 3.12
.

Consider an
(n,m,r)
network which factors over a partition
\mathcal{P}
.

(i)

Given
u\in\mathrm{im}\,[A\,|\,\bm{1}_{\mathcal{P}}]
,
v\in\mathbb{R}^{n-s_{\mathcal{P}}}
, and
z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, satisfying
\widehat{G}u+\widehat{Q}v=z
, there exists
t\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
such that
u=Az+\bm{1}_{\mathcal{P}}t
.

(ii)

Given
(z,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{p}
, there exists a unique
v\in\mathbb{R}^{n-s_{\mathcal{P}}}
such that
\widehat{G}(Az+\bm{1}_{\mathcal{P}}t)+\widehat{Q}v=z
.

Proof.

(i) We may “augment” the equation
\widehat{G}u+\widehat{Q}v=z
by adding to
\widehat{G}
and
\widehat{Q}
the final
p
rows of
G
(resp.,
Q
), to obtain

Gu+Qv=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}z\\
t\end{array}\right)\,,

where this equation defines
t\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
. Multiplying on the left by
[A\,|\,\bm{1}_{\mathcal{P}}]
, and observing that
[A\,|\,\bm{1}_{\mathcal{P}}]Q=0
, and that
G
is a generalised inverse of
[A\,|\,\bm{1}_{\mathcal{P}}]
, now gives the first result. (ii) The second claim follows by setting
u=Az+\bm{1}_{\mathcal{P}}t
and considering again the augmented equation

G[A\,|\,\bm{1}_{\mathcal{P}}]\left(\begin{array}[]{c}z\\
t\end{array}\right)-\left(\begin{array}[]{c}z\\
t\end{array}\right)=-Qv\,.

The existence of
v
satisfying this equation follows as the left hand side lies in
\mathrm{im}\,Q=\ker[A\,|\,\bm{1}_{\mathcal{P}}]
, by the definition of
G
. The uniqueness of
v
follows as
\widehat{Q}
is an injective matrix.
∎

3.4.
Parameterising the equilibrium set

With a
p
-partition
\mathcal{P}
and the matrices
W,Q
and
G
in place, we revisit the main equation (
3.5
) describing the set of positive equilibria.
For given
(\alpha,\kappa)\in Y\times\mathbb{R}^{m}_{+}
, (
3.5
) has a solution
(x,\lambda)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{p}_{+}
if and only if

\ln h(\alpha)-\ln\kappa\in\im[A\,|\,\bm{1}_{\mathcal{P}}]
, hence if and only if the (Fredholm) solvability condition
W(\ln h(\alpha)-\ln\kappa)=0
, equivalently,

(3.9)

h(\alpha)^{W}=\kappa^{W}

is satisfied. We refer to system (
3.9
) as the
solvability system
of the network, and equations of this system as
solvability equations
.

We consider (
3.9
) with
\kappa\in\mathbb{R}^{m}_{+}
fixed and
\alpha
varying: it is then a system of
m-s_{\mathcal{P}}-p
equations for
m-r-p
unknowns. Define
\mathcal{A}_{\kappa}
to be the set of solutions to (
3.9
), i.e.,

\mathcal{A}_{\kappa}:=\{\alpha\in Y\,:\,h(\alpha)^{W}=\kappa^{W}\}\,.

As
h(\alpha)
and
\kappa
are positive vectors, we may clear denominators in
h(\alpha)^{W}=\kappa^{W}
to obtain a system of
polynomial
equations. Thus
\mathcal{A}_{\kappa}
is semialgebraic, being the intersection of an algebraic variety in
\mathbb{R}^{m-s_{\mathcal{P}}-p}
with the open polyhedron
Y\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m-s_{\mathcal{P}}-p}
. Three special cases of importance are:

(i)

if
s_{\mathcal{P}}=m-p
(sources are
\mathcal{P}
-independent), so that
W
is empty and solvability of (
3.5
) is automatic, we have
\mathcal{A}_{\kappa}=Y
;

(ii)

if
s_{\mathcal{P}}<r
, then we will see later that
\mathcal{A}_{\kappa}
is generically empty and we will refer to the network as
\mathcal{P}
-overdetermined
;

(iii)

if
s_{\mathcal{P}}=r
, we will see later that
\mathcal{A}_{\kappa}
is generically finite, in which case we will refer to the network as
\bm{\mathcal{P}}
-toric
.

Remark 3.13

(Subsystems of the solvability system)
.

Let us choose
W
as in
Lemma

3.11
, and define
W^{(i)}
to be the submatrix of
W
consisting of columns indexed by
P_{i}
and rows supported only on
P_{i}
. With
h_{i}
defined as in
Remark

3.4
, (
3.9
) consists of a set of subsystems (some or all possibly empty) of the form

(3.10)

h_{i}(\alpha^{(i)})^{W^{(i)}}=(\kappa^{(i)})^{W^{(i)}},\quad i=1,\ldots,p

along with further equations
h(\alpha)^{\widetilde{W}}=\kappa^{\widetilde{W}}
, where
\widetilde{W}
consists of rows of
W
supported on more than one block of
\mathcal{P}
. For any
i\in\{1,\ldots,p\}
, the
i
th equation of (
3.10
) is precisely the solvability system for the
i
th partition subnetwork.

In what follows, we allow functions and matrices to be empty, and adopt the natural convention that any system of equations which is empty is trivially satisfied. In particular, given a
0\times m
matrix
M
, the equation
Mz=0
is satisfied by all
z\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
. And if
n=s_{\mathcal{P}}
, so that
Q
is an
(n+p)\times 0
matrix, we interpret
\mu^{\widehat{Q}}
as
\bm{1}
.

For fixed
\kappa\in\mathbb{R}^{m}_{+}
, define
T_{\kappa}\colon Y\times\mathbb{R}^{n-s_{\mathcal{P}}}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}^{m-s_{\mathcal{P}}-p}
and
F_{\kappa}\colon Y\times\mathbb{R}^{n-s_{\mathcal{P}}}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
by

T_{\kappa}(\alpha,\mu)=h(\alpha)^{W}-\kappa^{W}\,,\qquad F_{\kappa}(\alpha,\mu)=(h(\alpha)/\kappa)^{\widehat{G}}\circ\mu^{\widehat{Q}}\,,

so that
T_{\kappa}^{-1}(0)=\mathcal{A}_{\kappa}\times\mathbb{R}^{n-s_{\mathcal{P}}}_{+}
. The next result, which tells us that there is a smooth bijection between
\mathcal{A}_{\kappa}\times\mathbb{R}^{n-s_{\mathcal{P}}}_{+}
and the positive equilibrium set of (
2.1
), is key to the results to follow. Special cases of Equation (
3.11
) in
Theorem

3.14
(i), derived in a closely related way, appear in
[
banajiborosnonlinearity
, Section 3.1]
, and
[
BBH2024smallbif
, Section 2]
. Equation (
3.11
) is structurally equivalent to the system of equations presented in
[
regensburger:gale
, Theorem 5]
, and can be deduced from
[
bihan-sottile-complete-gale
]
for the trivial partition.

Theorem 3.14
.

Consider a dynamically nontrivial
(n,m,r)
network which factors over a
p
-partition
\mathcal{P}
, and fix
\kappa\in\mathbb{R}^{m}_{+}
. Then:

(i)

The map
F_{\kappa}
is smooth on
Y\times\mathbb{R}^{n-s_{\mathcal{P}}}_{+}
, and an injective, semialgebraic, mapping on
\mathcal{A}_{\kappa}\times\mathbb{R}^{n-s_{\mathcal{P}}}_{+}
. The set
\mathcal{E}_{\kappa}
of positive equilibria of (
2.1
) is
F_{\kappa}(\mathcal{A}_{\kappa}\times\mathbb{R}^{n-s_{\mathcal{P}}}_{+})
, namely,

(3.11)

\mathcal{E}_{\kappa}=\{(h(\alpha)/\kappa)^{\widehat{G}}\circ\mu^{\widehat{Q}}:\alpha\in\mathcal{A}_{\kappa},\,\,\mu\in\mathbb{R}^{n-s_{\mathcal{P}}}_{+}\}\,\,=\bigsqcup_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}_{\kappa}}(h(\alpha)/\kappa)^{\widehat{G}}\circ\{\mu^{\widehat{Q}}:\mu\in\mathbb{R}^{n-s_{\mathcal{P}}}_{+}\}\,.

(ii)

The map
\widetilde{F}_{\kappa}\colon Y\times\mathbb{R}^{n-s_{\mathcal{P}}}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}^{m-s_{\mathcal{P}}-p}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
(\alpha,\mu)\mapsto(T_{\kappa}(\alpha,\mu),F_{\kappa}(\alpha,\mu))
, is a smooth injective map such that
J_{\widetilde{F}_{k}}(\alpha,\mu)
has trivial kernel for all
(\alpha,\mu)\in Y\times\mathbb{R}^{n-s_{\mathcal{P}}}_{+}
.

(iii)

Given
(\alpha,\mu)\in\mathcal{A}_{\kappa}\times\mathbb{R}^{n-s_{\mathcal{P}}}_{+}
,
\ker J_{T_{\kappa}}(\alpha,\mu)\cong\ker J_{g_{\kappa}}(x)
, where
x:=F_{\kappa}(\alpha,\mu)
. In particular,
J_{F_{\kappa}}(\alpha,\mu)
takes
\ker J_{T_{\kappa}}(\alpha,\mu)
isomorphically to
\ker J_{g_{\kappa}}(x)
.

Proof.

(i) Smoothness of
F_{\kappa}
is obvious. Given
\alpha\in\mathcal{A}_{\kappa}
, we have
\ln(h(\alpha)/\kappa)\in\mathrm{im}\,[A\,|\,\bm{1}_{\mathcal{P}}]
, hence

(3.12)

[A\,|\,\bm{1}_{\mathcal{P}}]\,G\,\ln(h(\alpha)/\kappa)=\ln(h(\alpha)/\kappa)\,.

Suppose that
F_{\kappa}
fails to be injective on
\mathcal{A}_{\kappa}\times\mathbb{R}^{n-s_{\mathcal{P}}}_{+}
, namely, there exist
(\alpha_{1},\mu_{1}),(\alpha_{2},\mu_{2})\in\mathcal{A}_{\kappa}\times\mathbb{R}^{n-s_{\mathcal{P}}}_{+}
such that
(h(\alpha_{1})/\kappa)^{\widehat{G}}\circ\mu_{1}^{\widehat{Q}}=(h(\alpha_{2})/\kappa)^{\widehat{G}}\circ\mu_{2}^{\widehat{Q}}
. Taking logarithms we obtain

(3.13)

\widehat{G}\big(\ln h(\alpha_{1})-\ln h(\alpha_{2})\big)+\widehat{Q}\ln(\mu_{1}/\mu_{2})=0\,.

By (
3.12
),

\ln h(\alpha_{1})-\ln h(\alpha_{2})\in\mathrm{im}\,[A\,|\,\bm{1}_{\mathcal{P}}]
, hence, (
3.13
) gives by
Lemma

3.12
(i) that
\ln h(\alpha_{1})-\ln h(\alpha_{2})=\bm{1}_{\mathcal{P}}t
for some
t\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
, namely
h(\alpha_{1})=(\bm{1}_{\mathcal{P}}e^{t})\circ h(\alpha_{2})
implying that
h(\alpha_{1})=h(\alpha_{2})
, hence
\alpha_{1}=\alpha_{2}
. As
\widehat{Q}
is an injective matrix it immediately follows from (
3.13
) that
\mu_{1}=\mu_{2}
, proving that
F_{\kappa}
is injective on
\mathcal{A}_{\kappa}\times\mathbb{R}^{n-s_{\mathcal{P}}}_{+}
.

If
x=(h(\alpha)/\kappa)^{\widehat{G}}\circ\mu^{\widehat{Q}}
for
(\alpha,\mu)\in\mathcal{A}_{\kappa}\times\mathbb{R}^{n-s_{\mathcal{P}}}_{+}
, then, taking logarithms and observing that by (
3.12
)
\ln(h(\alpha)/\kappa)\in\mathrm{im}\,[A\,|\,\bm{1}_{\mathcal{P}}]
,
Lemma

3.12
(i) gives that
A(\ln x)=\ln(h(\alpha)/\kappa)-\bm{1}_{\mathcal{P}}t
for some
t
. With
\lambda=e^{-t}
,
(x,\lambda,\alpha,\kappa)
together satisfy (
3.5
), and thus
x\in\mathcal{E}_{\kappa}
. On the other hand, if
x\in\mathcal{E}_{\kappa}
, namely, there exists
(\alpha,\lambda)\in Y\times\mathbb{R}^{p}_{+}
such that
(x,\lambda,\alpha,\kappa)
together satisfy (
3.5
). Then
\alpha\in\mathcal{A}_{\kappa}
by the solvability condition and, using (
3.12
), there exists
\mu\in\mathbb{R}^{n-s_{\mathcal{P}}}_{+}
such that

(3.14)

\left(\begin{array}[]{r}\ln x\\
-\ln\lambda\end{array}\right)-G\ln(h(\alpha)/\kappa)=Q\ln\mu\,.

Exponentiating gives that
x=(h(\alpha)/\kappa)^{\widehat{G}}\circ\mu^{\widehat{Q}}
. Clearly
\mathcal{A}_{\kappa}\times\mathbb{R}^{n-s_{\mathcal{P}}}_{+}
and
\mathcal{E}_{\kappa}=F_{\kappa}(\mathcal{A}_{\kappa}\times\mathbb{R}^{n-s_{\mathcal{P}}}_{+})
are, when nonempty, both semialgebraic sets. That the graph of
\left.F_{\kappa}\right|_{\mathcal{A}_{\kappa}\times\mathbb{R}^{n-s_{\mathcal{P}}}_{+}}
is a semialgebraic set now follows by raising both sides of the defining equation
y=(h(\alpha)/\kappa)^{\widehat{G}}\circ\mu^{\widehat{Q}}
to the lowest common multiple of the denominators appearing in
\widehat{G}
and
\widehat{Q}
and clearing denominators in the resulting rational system to get a system of polynomial equations with the same positive zero set.

(ii) Smoothness is obvious. To see injectivity, suppose that
\widetilde{F}_{\kappa}(\alpha_{1},\mu_{1})=\widetilde{F}_{\kappa}(\alpha_{2},\mu_{2})
. Then it follows from
T_{\kappa}(\alpha_{1},\mu_{1})=T_{\kappa}(\alpha_{2},\mu_{2})
that
\ln h(\alpha_{1})-\ln h(\alpha_{2})\in\mathrm{im}\,[A\,|\,\bm{1}_{\mathcal{P}}]
. With this fact in place,
F_{\kappa}(\alpha_{1},\mu_{1})=F_{\kappa}(\alpha_{2},\mu_{2})
implies that
(\alpha_{1},\mu_{1})=(\alpha_{2},\mu_{2})
exactly as in (i). It remains to prove that
\widetilde{F}_{\kappa}
is an immersion. Writing
x=(h(\alpha)/\kappa)^{\widehat{G}}\circ\mu^{\widehat{Q}}
, we have

J_{T_{\kappa}}(\alpha,\mu)=(D_{h(\alpha)^{W}}WD_{1/h(\alpha)}J_{h},\,0)
and
J_{F_{\kappa}}(\alpha,\mu)=(D_{x}\widehat{G}D_{1/h(\alpha)}J_{h},\,D_{x}\widehat{Q}D_{1/\mu})
. Hence
the Jacobian matrix of
\widetilde{F}_{\kappa}
at
(\alpha,\mu)\in Y\times\mathbb{R}^{n-s_{\mathcal{P}}}_{+}
is

J_{\widetilde{F}_{\kappa}}:=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}D_{h(\alpha)^{W}}WD_{1/h(\alpha)}J_{h}&0\\
D_{x}\widehat{G}D_{1/h(\alpha)}J_{h}&D_{x}\widehat{Q}D_{1/\mu}\end{array}\right)\,.

This matrix is singular if and only if there exists
(z_{1},z_{2})\neq(0,0)
satisfying the pair of equations

(3.15)

WD_{1/h(\alpha)}J_{h}z_{1}=0,\qquad\widehat{G}D_{1/h(\alpha)}J_{h}z_{1}+\widehat{Q}D_{1/\mu}z_{2}=0\,,

where we have used the fact that
D_{h(\alpha)^{W}}
and
D_{x}
are nonsingular. The first equation of (
3.15
) tells us that
D_{1/h(\alpha)}J_{h}z_{1}\in\ker W=\mathrm{im}\,[A\,|\,\bm{1}_{\mathcal{P}}]
. Using
Lemma

3.12
(i), the second equation of (
3.15
) gives that
D_{1/h(\alpha)}J_{h}z_{1}=\bm{1}_{\mathcal{P}}t
for some
t
. By
Lemma

3.5
(ii),
t=0
, hence
z_{1}=0
(as
D_{1/h(\alpha)}J_{h}
is injective). It follows that
z_{2}=0
(as
\widehat{Q}D_{1/\mu}
is injective). Thus
J_{\widetilde{F}_{\kappa}}
has trivial kernel.

(iii) For notational convenience write
J_{F_{\kappa}}=J_{F_{\kappa}}(\alpha,\mu)
,
J_{T_{\kappa}}=J_{T_{\kappa}}(\alpha,\mu)
and
J_{g_{\kappa}}=J_{g_{\kappa}}(x)
.
By part (ii),
J_{F_{\kappa}}
is injective when restricted to
\ker J_{T_{\kappa}}
. It thus suffices to show that
J_{F_{\kappa}}(\ker J_{T_{\kappa}})=\ker J_{g_{\kappa}}
.

For a vector
z=(z_{1},z_{2})\in\ker J_{T_{\kappa}}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m-r-p}\times\mathbb{R}^{n-s_{\mathcal{P}}}
, write
u:=D_{1/h(\alpha)}J_{h}z_{1}
.
As above
z\in\ker J_{T_{\kappa}}
if and only if
u\in\im\,[A\,|\,\bm{1}_{\mathcal{P}}]
. Using
J_{F_{\kappa}}z=D_{x}\big(\widehat{G}u+\widehat{Q}D_{1/\mu}z_{2}\big)
, we obtain

(3.16)

J_{F_{\kappa}}(\ker J_{T_{\kappa}})=\Big\{D_{x}\big(\widehat{G}u+\widehat{Q}D_{1/\mu}z_{2}\big):z_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{n-s_{\mathcal{P}}},u\in\im\,[A\,|\,\bm{1}_{\mathcal{P}}]\cap\im D_{1/h(\alpha)}J_{h}\Big\}.

Using formula (
3.4
) in
Remark

3.2
, and the fact that
\ker\Gamma
factors over
\mathcal{P}
,
z^{*}\in\ker J_{g_{\kappa}}
if and only if
D_{h(\alpha)}AD_{1/x}z^{*}\in\ker\Gamma
. By
Lemma

3.5
(iii) this is in turn equivalent to the existence of
z^{\prime},t
such that

D_{h(\alpha)}AD_{1/x}z^{*}=J_{h}z^{\prime}-(\bm{1}_{\mathcal{P}}t)\circ h(\alpha),\quad\text{
equivalently}\quad AD_{1/x}z^{*}=D_{1/h(\alpha)}J_{h}z^{\prime}-\bm{1}_{\mathcal{P}}t\,.

Hence

(3.17)

\ker J_{g_{\kappa}}=\big\{z^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:AD_{1/x}z^{*}\in\im D_{1/h(\alpha)}J_{h}\oplus\im\bm{1}_{\mathcal{P}}\big\}.

We show that
J_{F_{\kappa}}(\ker J_{T_{\kappa}})=\ker J_{g_{\kappa}}
using (
3.16
) and (
3.17
).
The inclusion
\subseteq
follows from
Lemma

3.12
(i) which gives that
A(\widehat{G}u+\widehat{Q}D_{1/\mu}z_{2})=u-\bm{1}_{\mathcal{P}}t
for some
t
.

To show
\supseteq
, for
z^{*}\in\ker J_{g_{\kappa}}
, write
AD_{1/x}z^{*}=u-\bm{1}_{\mathcal{P}}t
with
u\in\im D_{1/h(\alpha)}J_{h}
. As
\mu
is positive, from
Lemma

3.12
(ii), there exists a (unique)
z_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{n-s_{\mathcal{P}}}
which solves
\widehat{G}u+\widehat{Q}D_{1/\mu}z_{2}=D_{1/x}z^{*}
. Multiplying both sides by
D_{x}
and noting that
u\in\im\,[A\,|\,\bm{1}_{\mathcal{P}}]\cap\im D_{1/h(\alpha)}J_{h}
we see from (
3.16
) that
z^{*}\in J_{F_{\kappa}}(\ker J_{T_{\kappa}})
. This completes the proof.
∎

Remark 3.15

(Relationship with results in
[
regensburger:gale
]
)
.

As indicated above, equation (
3.11
) in
Theorem

3.14
(i) follows from recent work
[
regensburger:gale
, Theorem 5]
, after some minor adjustments. In terms of the notation developed here, the authors of
[
regensburger:gale
]
consider the image of
h
(here referred to as
\widehat{\mathcal{C}}
, and referred to in
[
regensburger:gale
]
as the
coefficient set
) as the base space for
\mathcal{A}_{\kappa}
, instead of the domain
Y
of

used here. In addition, the authors impose the constraint that the entries of

add up to one, effectively fixing a particular choice of
\widehat{\mathcal{C}}
. They obtain the matrix
\widehat{G}
(called
E
in that work) via a slightly different construction: however, both approaches lead to the same matrix, in the sense that
E
, like
\widehat{G}
, is formed of the top rows of a generalized inverse of
[A\,|\,\bm{1}_{\mathcal{P}}]
. Our choice to use
Y
, rather than
\widehat{\mathcal{C}}
, as the base space for
\mathcal{A}_{\kappa}
is better suited for the purposes of this work, allowing us to write down equations explicitly, and obtain bounds on the number of positive nondegenerate equilibria in various classes of networks.

We note that
Theorem

3.14
(i) expresses
\mathcal{E}_{\kappa}
as a disjoint union of
toric sets
, that is, sets admitting a monomial parameterisation in the positive orthant. These sets are parameterised by
\mu
and have dimension equal to
\rank\widehat{Q}
, namely
n-s_{\mathcal{P}}
, explaining the terminology “toricity matrix” for
\widehat{Q}
. We will get back to toricity in
Section

4.2
.

Remark 3.16

(Dimension of
\mathcal{E}_{\kappa}
)
.

By
Theorem

3.14
(i),
\left.F_{\kappa}\right|_{\mathcal{A}_{\kappa}\times\mathbb{R}^{n-s_{\mathcal{P}}}}
is an injective semialgebraic map. Consequently
\dim\mathcal{E}_{\kappa}=\dim(\mathcal{A}_{\kappa}\times\mathbb{R}^{n-s_{\mathcal{P}}}_{+})=\dim\mathcal{A}_{\kappa}+n-s_{\mathcal{P}}
, where the first equality follows from the dimension-preserving property of an injective semialgebraic map, and the second equality follows as the dimension of the Cartesian product of semialgebraic sets is the sum of their dimensions (see
[
costesemialgebraic
, p56ff]
.)

We will frequently manipulate systems of equations on some domain to obtain polynomial systems with the same zero sets. For example, we often clear denominators in the solvability system
h(\alpha)^{W}=\kappa^{W}
, equivalent to multiplying both sides of each equation by a positive (polynomial) function. We observe in the following lemma that such manipulations, applied to a square system, preserve degeneracy and nondegeneracy of solutions. Henceforth we will refer to systems of equations obtained via any sequence of manipulations which preserve degeneracy or nondegeneracy of solutions, such as those in
Lemma

3.17
, as
equivalent
.

Lemma 3.17

(Equivalent systems of equations)
.

Let
Y\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{k}
be open, let
d_{1},\ldots,d_{k}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
, and let
f_{i},g_{i},h_{i}\colon Y\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
,
(i=1,\ldots,k)
be smooth functions. Let
F_{1},F_{2},F_{3}\colon Y\to\mathbb{R}^{k}
be defined by

\displaystyle F_{1}(y)

\displaystyle:=(f_{1}(y)-g_{1}(y),\ \ldots,\ f_{k}(y)-g_{k}(y))

\displaystyle F_{2}(y)

\displaystyle:=(h_{1}(y)(f_{1}(y)-g_{1}(y)),\ \ldots,\ h_{k}(y)(f_{k}(y)-g_{k}(y)))

\displaystyle F_{3}(y)

\displaystyle:=(f_{1}(y)^{d_{1}}-g_{1}(y)^{d_{1}},\ \ldots,\ f_{k}(y)^{d_{k}}-g_{k}(y)^{d_{k}})\,.

Let
F_{1}(y^{*})=0
, equivalently,
F_{2}(y^{*})=0
, equivalently,
F_{3}(y^{*})=0
. Then
\ker J_{F_{1}}(y^{*})\cong\ker J_{F_{2}}(y^{*})\cong\ker J_{F_{3}}(y^{*})
. Hence
y^{*}
is a degenerate zero of
F_{1}
if and only if it is a degenerate zero of
F_{2}
if and only if it is a degenerate zero of
F_{3}
.

Proof.

We check that
J_{F_{2}}(y^{*})=D_{h(y^{*})}J_{F_{1}}(y^{*})
and
J_{F_{3}}(y^{*})=\widehat{D}J_{F_{1}}(y^{*})
, where
\widehat{D}
is a diagonal matrix with
\widehat{D}_{ii}=d_{i}f_{i}^{d_{i}-1}(y^{*})=d_{i}g_{i}^{d_{i}-1}(y^{*})
. As
D_{h(y^{*})}
and
\widehat{D}
are positive diagonal matrices, the claim is immediate.
∎

While
Theorem

3.14
provides a smooth injective parameterisation of the equilibrium set, it has the drawback that a factor of the parameter space, namely
\mathcal{A}_{\kappa}
, is implicitly defined (via some linear inequalities and polynomial equations). Nevertheless, as we will see in examples and when we examine special cases, we can fairly often pass to an explicit, perhaps piecewise, parameterisation of the equilibrium set. Cases (i) and (iii) of the next corollary provide instances where this holds.

Corollary 3.18
.

We have the following special cases:

(i)

(
\mathcal{P}
-independence:
W
is empty.) If
m=s_{\mathcal{P}}+p
, then
W
is empty, in which case
\mathcal{A}_{\kappa}=Y
and

\mathcal{E}_{\kappa}=\bigsqcup_{\alpha\in Y}(h(\alpha)/\kappa)^{\widehat{G}}\circ\{\mu^{\widehat{Q}}:\mu\in\mathbb{R}^{n-s_{\mathcal{P}}}_{+}\}\,.

(ii)

(
\widehat{Q}
is empty.) If
n=s_{\mathcal{P}}
, then
Q
is empty and

\mathcal{E}_{\kappa}=\bigsqcup_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}_{\kappa}}(h(\alpha)/\kappa)^{\widehat{G}}\,.

(iii)

(
Y
is a singleton.) If
r=m-p
, then
Y
is a singleton, say
Y=\{\alpha\}
. If, further,
s_{\mathcal{P}}\geq r
(i.e., the network is not
\mathcal{P}
-overdetermined), then
W
is also empty, and

\mathcal{E}_{\kappa}=(h(\alpha)/\kappa)^{\widehat{G}}\circ\{\mu^{\widehat{Q}}:\mu\in\mathbb{R}^{n-s_{\mathcal{P}}}_{+}\}\,.

In particular
\mathcal{E}_{\kappa}
is toric,
s_{\mathcal{P}}=r
, and the sources are
\mathcal{P}
-independent.

(iv)

(Conditions (i)–(iii) hold simultaneously.) In this case,
n=s_{\mathcal{P}}=r=m-p
, and
\mathcal{E}_{\kappa}
consists of exactly one equilibrium for all
\kappa\in\mathbb{R}^{m}_{+}
, namely
(h(\alpha)/\kappa)^{\widehat{G}}
, where
\widehat{G}
is now a submatrix of
[A\,|\bm{1}_{\mathcal{P}}]^{-1}
. Moreover this unique equilibrium is nondegenerate.

Proof.

(i) and (ii) are merely applications of
Theorem

3.14
(i). In case (iii), we observe that
m=r+p
implies that
Y
is a singleton, say
Y=\{\alpha\}
, while
m=r+p
and
s_{\mathcal{P}}\geq r
together imply
s_{\mathcal{P}}=r
(
\mathcal{P}
-independence), i.e., that
W
is empty and hence
\mathcal{A}_{\kappa}=\{\alpha\}
. (iv) When
n=s_{\mathcal{P}}=r=m-p
,
[A\,|\,\bm{1}_{\mathcal{P}}]
is square and invertible, and we can solve (
3.5
) to get the unique positive equilibrium
(h(\alpha)/\kappa)^{\widehat{G}}
for each
\kappa
. Nondegeneracy follows immediately from
Theorem

3.14
(iii) (see also
[
banajiborosnonlinearity
]
for the direct calculations in the special case where
\mathcal{P}
is trivial.)
∎

Remark 3.19

(Full rank networks with
\mathcal{P}
-independent sources)
.

A full rank network with
\mathcal{P}
-independent sources satisfies condition (iv) of
Corollary

3.18
, as

r\leq m-p=s_{\mathcal{P}}\leq n=r

where the first equality follows from
\mathcal{P}
-independence. Hence any such network has a unique, positive, nondegenerate, equilibrium for all rate constants. The case of
p=1
was covered in
[
banajiborosnonlinearity
]
.

Remark 3.20

(Posynomials)
.

In general,
(h(\alpha)/\kappa)^{\widehat{G}}
is a vector of
posynomial
expressions
[
boyd2004convex
]
in the variables
h_{i}
with rational (not necessarily positive) exponents. Consequently,
(h(\alpha)/\kappa)^{\widehat{G}}
is a vector of
generalised posynomials

[
boyd2004convex
, Exercise 4.37]
in
\alpha
. When solving for equilibria, we can obtain corresponding systems of
polynomial
equations in various ways as we will illustrate by example.

3.5.
Positive equilibria in stoichiometric classes

Recall that for networks not of full rank, i.e., such that
r<n
,
Z
is nonempty and each stoichiometric class is defined by a linear system of the form
Zx=K
for some constant
K\in\mathbb{R}^{n-r}
. For the following theorem, recall the notation and definitions surrounding
Theorem

3.14
. We adopt the natural convention that a (constant) map on a zero dimensional vector space is nondegenerate.

Theorem 3.21
.

Consider a dynamically nontrivial
(n,m,r)
network which factors over a
p
-partition
\mathcal{P}
. For fixed
\kappa\in\mathbb{R}^{m}_{+}
,
K\in\mathbb{R}^{n-r}
, define
H_{\kappa,K}\colon Y\times\mathbb{R}^{n-s_{\mathcal{P}}}\to\mathbb{R}^{m-s_{\mathcal{P}}-p}\times\mathbb{R}^{n-r}
by

H_{\kappa,K}(\alpha,\mu):=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}T_{\kappa}(\alpha,\mu)\\[4.0pt]
ZF_{\kappa}(\alpha,\mu)-K\end{array}\right)=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}h(\alpha)^{W}-\kappa^{W}\\
Z[(h(\alpha)/\kappa)^{\widehat{G}}\circ\mu^{\widehat{Q}}]-K\end{array}\right)\,.

Let
\mathcal{S}_{K}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}
be the positive stoichiometric class defined by
Zx=K
. Then

(i)

F_{\kappa}
is a smooth injective map from the zero set of
H_{\kappa,K}
on
Y\times\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n-s_{\mathcal{P}}}
, namely from solutions to

(3.18)

h(\alpha)^{W}=\kappa^{W},\quad Z[(h(\alpha)/\kappa)^{\widehat{G}}\circ\mu^{\widehat{Q}}]=K\,\qquad(\alpha,\mu)\in Y\times\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n-s_{\mathcal{P}}}\,,

onto
\mathcal{E}_{\kappa}\cap\mathcal{S}_{K}
, the set of positive equilibria on
\mathcal{S}_{K}
.

(ii)

Fix
\kappa\in\mathbb{R}^{m}_{+}
and
(\alpha,\mu)\in\mathcal{A}_{\kappa}\times\mathbb{R}^{n-s_{\mathcal{P}}}_{+}
, and define
x:=F_{\kappa}(\alpha,\mu)=(h(\alpha)/\kappa)^{\widehat{G}}\circ\mu^{\widehat{Q}}
and
K:=Zx
. Then

\ker J_{H_{\kappa,K}}(\alpha,\mu)\cong\ker J_{g_{\kappa}}(x)\cap\mathrm{im}\,\Gamma\,,

with
J_{F_{\kappa}}(\alpha,\mu)
providing the isomorphism. Consequently,
x
is degenerate as an equilibrium of (
2.1
) if and only if
(\alpha,\mu)
is degenerate as a solution of (
3.18
).

Proof.

The first claim is immediate from
Theorem

3.14
(i). In order to show the second claim, for notational convenience write
J_{F_{\kappa}}=J_{F_{\kappa}}(\alpha,\mu)
,
J_{T_{\kappa}}=J_{T_{\kappa}}(\alpha,\mu)
,
J_{H_{\kappa}}=J_{H_{\kappa,K}}(\alpha,\mu)
and
J_{g_{\kappa}}=J_{g_{\kappa}}(x)
(observe that
J_{H_{\kappa,K}}(\alpha,\mu)
is independent of
K
). Define
\mathcal{T}:=\ker J_{H_{\kappa}}=\ker J_{T_{\kappa}}\cap\ker(ZJ_{F_{\kappa}})
, and observe that, as
\mathrm{im}\,\Gamma=\ker Z
,
\mathcal{T}
is the subspace of
\ker J_{T_{\kappa}}
mapped by
J_{F_{\kappa}}
into
\mathrm{im}\,\Gamma
. Recalling, from
Theorem

3.14
(iii), that
J_{F_{\kappa}}
maps
\ker J_{T_{\kappa}}
isomorphically onto
\ker J_{g_{\kappa}}
, clearly
\left.J_{F_{\kappa}}\right|_{\mathcal{T}}\colon\mathcal{T}\to\ker J_{g_{\kappa}}\cap\mathrm{im}\,\Gamma
is an isomorphism. But now the following four statements are equivalent: (a)
(\alpha,\mu)
is a degenerate solution of (
3.18
); (b)
\mathcal{T}\neq\{0\}
; (c)
\ker J_{g_{\kappa}}\cap\mathrm{im}\,\Gamma\neq\{0\}
; (d)
x
is a degenerate equilibrium of (
2.1
). The equivalences (a)
\Leftrightarrow
(b) and (c)
\Leftrightarrow
(d) are by definition, while (b)
\Leftrightarrow
(c) uses the isomorphism between
\mathcal{T}
and
\ker J_{g_{\kappa}}\cap\mathrm{im}\,\Gamma
.
∎

We refer to (
3.18
) as the
alternative system of equations
for equilibria on the positive stoichiometric class defined by
Zx=K
.

Remark 3.22

(The alternative system of equations)
.

Note that (
3.18
) is a square system, consisting of
(m-s_{\mathcal{P}}-p)+(n-r)
equations in the same number of unknowns
(\alpha,\mu)
. Recall that if
W
is empty we take the first equation of (
3.18
) to be trivially satisfied, and if
Q
is empty we define
\mu^{\widehat{Q}}=\bm{1}
. Similarly, if
Z
is empty, we take the second equation of (
3.18
) to be satisfied.
Corollary

3.18
(iv) covers the case where both
W
and
Z
are empty.
Theorem

3.21
(ii) tells us that the level of degeneracy of a solution of the alternative system is precisely the level of degeneracy of the corresponding equilibrium. This remains true if we manipulate the alternative system of equations to obtain an equivalent system as in
Lemma

3.17
.

Remark 3.23

(Noninteger entries in
G
)
.

Even when
G
has noninteger entries (see
Remark

3.20
), we can always write down a system of polynomial equations, perhaps with additional variables and equations, whose solutions (if necessary projected onto
Y\times\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n-s_{\mathcal{P}}}
) include the solutions of system (
3.18
). The most natural or efficient way to do this may not be clear. In the results and examples to follow, all such manipulations lead to equivalent systems as in
Lemma

3.17
, hence preserve degeneracy and nondegeneracy of solutions.

3.6.
Alternative Bézout and BKK bounds

Whenever solutions of (
3.18
) are also solutions of some equivalent polynomial system, we obtain a new Bézout bound on the number of positive nondegenerate equilibria on any stoichiometric class. We refer to any such bound as an
alternative Bézout bound
. If the polynomial system is defined on a domain not intersecting the coordinate hyperplanes, we can apply the BKK theorem to improve on the alternative Bézout bound: such a bound is termed an
alternative BKK bound
. Any such bound derived using knowledge of the sources and rank of a CRN alone is termed a
source bound
.

Remark 3.24

(Nonuniqueness of the matrices
Z
,
W
,
Q
and
G
)
.

Different choices of the various matrices lead to different alternative systems of equations hence, potentially, different alternative bounds. Obviously the choices leading to the lowest alternative bounds are desired. In various special cases, the best choice is obvious, but this is not always true.

The following examples illustrate the construction and use of the alternative systems of equations, and alternative bounds.

Example 3.25
.

Consider any dynamically nontrivial
(3,5,3)
network with sources

\mathsf{0},\quad 2\mathsf{X},\quad 2\mathsf{Y},\quad\mathsf{X}+\mathsf{Z},\quad\mathsf{Y}+\mathsf{Z}\,.

The naive Bézout source bound is
8
and the naive BKK source bound is
6
(we may check the latter claim, for example, using the
Mathematica
function
BKKRootBound
with the
Toric
option).
We have

[A\,|\,\bm{1}]=\left(\begin{array}[]{rrrr}0&0&0&1\\
2&0&0&1\\
0&2&0&1\\
1&0&1&1\\
0&1&1&1\end{array}\right)\,,\qquad W=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccccc}0&1&-1&-2&2\end{array}\right)\,.

The solvability system
h(\alpha)^{W}=\kappa^{W}
is thus equivalent to the single polynomial equation

h_{2}(\alpha)h_{5}(\alpha)^{2}-\kappa^{W}h_{3}(\alpha)h_{4}(\alpha)^{2}=0\,,

which is at-most-cubic in
\alpha
, giving an alternative Bézout source bound of
3
, a considerable improvement on the naive BKK source bound. Moreover, for any particular network with these sources, identifying parameter regions for multistationarity, and parameter sets for bifurcations such as fold and cusp bifurcations, reduces to examining a single univariate, at-most-cubic equation, considerably simplifying the analysis.
∎

Example 3.26
.

Consider the following
(3,5,2)
network

\mathsf{0}{}\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{0.94722pt}{$\mathrel{\mathop{\makebox[0.0pt]{\to}}\limits^{\mkern 9.0mu\kappa_{1}\mkern 9.0mu}_{\makebox{\raisebox{3.76735pt}[0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle\mkern 9.0mu\hphantom{\kappa_{2}}\mkern 5.0mu$}}}}$}\hss}\raisebox{-0.94722pt}{$\mathrel{\mathop{\makebox[0.0pt]{\to}}\limits^{\mkern 5.0mu\hphantom{\kappa_{1}}\mkern 9.0mu}_{\makebox{\raisebox{3.76735pt}[0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle\mkern 9.0mu\kappa_{2}\mkern 9.0mu$}}}}$}}{}2\mathsf{X}\,,\qquad 2\mathsf{Y}{}\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{0.94722pt}{$\mathrel{\mathop{\makebox[0.0pt]{\to}}\limits^{\mkern 9.0mu\kappa_{3}\mkern 9.0mu}_{\makebox{\raisebox{3.76735pt}[0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle\mkern 9.0mu\hphantom{\kappa_{4}}\mkern 5.0mu$}}}}$}\hss}\raisebox{-0.94722pt}{$\mathrel{\mathop{\makebox[0.0pt]{\to}}\limits^{\mkern 5.0mu\hphantom{\kappa_{3}}\mkern 9.0mu}_{\makebox{\raisebox{3.76735pt}[0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle\mkern 9.0mu\kappa_{4}\mkern 9.0mu$}}}}$}}{}2\mathsf{Z}\,,\qquad\mathsf{Z}{}\mathrel{\mathop{\makebox[0.0pt]{\rightarrow\displaystyle}}\limits^{\mkern 5.0mu\kappa_{5}\mkern 9.0mu}}{}\mathsf{X}+\mathsf{Y}\,.

The best possible naive Bézout bound is
4
, as is the naive BKK bound.
We have

\Gamma=\left(\begin{array}[]{rrrrr}2&-2&0&0&1\\
0&0&-2&2&1\\
0&0&2&-2&-1\end{array}\right)\quad\mbox{and}\quad A=\left(\begin{array}[]{cccc}0&0&0\\
2&0&0\\
0&2&0\\
0&0&2\\
0&0&1\end{array}\right)\,.

In this case
\ker\Gamma
is spanned by the vectors
\{(1,1,0,0,0),(0,0,1,1,0),(0,1,1,0,2)\}
, and
\ker\Gamma
, hence
\mathcal{C}
, factor only over the trivial partition. We set

h(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})=(\alpha_{1},\,1-\alpha_{2},\,1-\alpha_{1},\,\alpha_{2},\,2(1-\alpha_{1}-\alpha_{2}))^{\top}\,,

defined on the triangle
Y:=\{(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}\,:\,\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}<1\}
. As
[A\,|\,\bm{1}]
is an injective matrix,
Q
is empty. Setting
W=(1,0,0,1,-2)
, the solvability system rearranges to

h_{1}(\alpha)h_{4}(\alpha)=\hat{\kappa}\,h_{5}(\alpha)^{2},\quad\mbox{i.e.,}\quad\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}=4\hat{\kappa}\,(1-\alpha_{1}-\alpha_{2})^{2}

where
\hat{\kappa}=\kappa_{1}\kappa_{4}/\kappa_{5}^{2}
. So, in this case,

\mathcal{A}_{\kappa}=\{(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})\in Y:\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}=4\hat{\kappa}\,(1-\alpha_{1}-\alpha_{2})^{2}\}\,.

For the following choice of generalised inverse of
[A\,|\,\bm{1}]
, the set of positive equilibria
\mathcal{E}_{\kappa}
is parameterised by

\left(\begin{array}[]{c}x\\
y\\
z\end{array}\right)=\left(\frac{h(\alpha)}{\kappa}\right)^{\widehat{G}}=\kappa^{-\widehat{G}}\circ\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\alpha_{1}^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}(1-\alpha_{2})^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\\
\alpha_{1}^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}(1-\alpha_{1})^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\\
\alpha_{1}^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\alpha_{2}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\end{array}\right)\,,\quad\text{where}\quad G=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccccc}-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}&\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}&0&0&0\\
-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}&0&\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}&0&0\\
-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}&0&0&\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}&0\\
1&0&0&0&0\end{array}\right)\,,

for
(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})\in\mathcal{A}_{\kappa}
. Choosing
Z=(0,1,1)
, positive equilibria on the stoichiometric class
y+z=K
correspond to the common solutions, in
Y
, to

\theta_{2}\alpha_{1}^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}(1-\alpha_{1})^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}+\theta_{3}\alpha_{1}^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\alpha_{2}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}=K,\qquad\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}(1-\alpha_{1}-\alpha_{2})^{-2}=4\hat{\kappa}\,,

where
\theta=\kappa^{-\widehat{G}}
. These are equivalent to a pair of quadratic equations in
(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})
, and so we obtain an alternative Bézout bound of
4
, which is precisely the naive bound in this case. The alternative BKK bound corresponding to this pair of quadratic equations is also
4
.
∎

In the next example, the source rank and rank of the networks considered are equal, namely the networks are
\mathcal{P}
-toric with the trivial partition. (
\mathcal{P}
-toric networks are treated in more generality in
Section

4.2
.)

Example 3.27
.

Consider any
(3,4,2)
network with sources

\mathsf{X}+\mathsf{Y},\quad 2\mathsf{Y},\quad 2\mathsf{X},\quad\mathsf{Y}+\mathsf{Z}\,.

The naive Bézout source bound in this case is
4
, and the naive BKK source bound can be checked to be
2
.
Corresponding to the trivial partition we have
W=(-2,1,1,0)
and
Q=(1,1,1,-2)^{\top}
. The solvability system
h(\alpha)^{W}=\kappa^{W}
is thus equivalent to

(3.19)

h_{2}(\alpha)h_{3}(\alpha)-\kappa^{W}h_{1}(\alpha)^{2}=0\,,

which is at-most-quadratic in
\alpha
. For each
\alpha^{*}\in Y
which solves (
3.19
), we have a ray of equilibria

\left(\begin{array}[]{c}x\\
y\\
z\end{array}\right)=\left(\frac{h(\alpha^{*})}{\kappa}\right)^{\widehat{G}}\circ\mu^{\widehat{Q}}=\left(\frac{h(\alpha^{*})}{\kappa}\right)^{\widehat{G}}\circ\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\mu\\
\mu\\
\mu\end{array}\right)\,.

The equation for positive equilibria on a stoichiometric class, corresponding to
\alpha=\alpha^{*}
, takes the form
Z[\left(h(\alpha^{*})/\kappa\right)^{\widehat{G}}\circ\mu^{\widehat{Q}}]=K
, which is clearly linear in
\mu
. We obtain an alternative Bézout source bound of
2
, and the mass action network admits at most two positive nondegenerate equilibria on any stoichiometric class. (Observe that we did not actually need to compute
G
in order to arrive at this bound, see also
Remark

4.10
below.)

The parameter region for multistationarity is easily determined in any particular
(3,4,2)
network with these sources. For example, consider the CRN

\mathsf{X}+\mathsf{Y}{}\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{0.94722pt}{$\mathrel{\mathop{\makebox[0.0pt]{\to}}\limits^{\mkern 9.0mu\kappa_{1}\mkern 9.0mu}_{\makebox{\raisebox{3.76735pt}[0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle\mkern 9.0mu\hphantom{\kappa_{2}}\mkern 5.0mu$}}}}$}\hss}\raisebox{-0.94722pt}{$\mathrel{\mathop{\makebox[0.0pt]{\to}}\limits^{\mkern 5.0mu\hphantom{\kappa_{1}}\mkern 9.0mu}_{\makebox{\raisebox{3.76735pt}[0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle\mkern 9.0mu\kappa_{2}\mkern 9.0mu$}}}}$}}{}2\mathsf{Y},\quad 2\mathsf{X}{}\mathrel{\mathop{\makebox[0.0pt]{\rightarrow\displaystyle}}\limits^{\mkern 5.0mu\kappa_{3}\mkern 9.0mu}}{}\mathsf{X}+\mathsf{Z},\quad\mathsf{Y}+\mathsf{Z}{}\mathrel{\mathop{\makebox[0.0pt]{\rightarrow\displaystyle}}\limits^{\mkern 5.0mu\kappa_{4}\mkern 9.0mu}}{}2\mathsf{X}\,.

In this case,
we may set
Z=(1,1,1)
and
h(\alpha)=(1,\,\alpha,\,1-\alpha,\,1-\alpha)^{\top}
with
\alpha\in(0,1)
. The solvability system is equivalent to

\alpha^{2}-\alpha+\kappa^{W}=0,\quad\mbox{so that}\quad\mathcal{A}_{\kappa}=\{\alpha\in(0,1)\,:\,\alpha^{2}-\alpha+\kappa^{W}=0\}\,.

Clearly,
\mathcal{A}_{\kappa}
consists of two points if and only if
\kappa^{W}=\kappa_{2}\kappa_{3}/\kappa_{1}^{2}<1/4
. The parameter region for multistationarity is thus
\{\kappa\in\mathbb{R}^{4}_{+}\,:\,4\kappa_{2}\kappa_{3}<\kappa_{1}^{2}\}
, and we can see via
Theorem

3.21
that in fact
every
stoichiometric class contains two positive nondegenerate equilibria whenever
4\kappa_{2}\kappa_{3}<\kappa_{1}^{2}
, a unique positive degenerate equilibrium whenever
4\kappa_{2}\kappa_{3}=\kappa_{1}^{2}
, and no positive equilibria otherwise. The equation
4\kappa_{2}\kappa_{3}=\kappa_{1}^{2}
thus defines the parameter set for potential fold bifurcations on each stoichiometric class. We remark that despite the simplicity of this network, these conclusions are not entirely trivial if we work directly with the mass action and conservation equations.
∎

3.7.
The finest partition

In the constructions above, the matrices
W
,
Q
and
G
depend on choosing a partition over which a network factors. Different choices thus give rise to different parameterisations of the set of positive equilibria. We now discuss further the role of the partition, and highlight the finest partition as an advantageous choice.
[
regensburger:gale
, Section 2]
already hints briefly at this fact.

As we will use several partitions in this subsection, we indicate the choice by subscripting the relevant matrices. We start with a basic observation.

Lemma 3.28
.

Let
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}\leq\mathcal{P}
be two partitions over which a network factors, of sizes
p^{\prime}
and
p
respectively. Then
s_{\mathcal{P}}\leq s_{\mathcal{P}^{\prime}}
, and further

\rank\widehat{Q}_{\mathcal{P}}\geq\rank\widehat{Q}_{\mathcal{P}^{\prime}},\qquad\rank\widehat{Q}_{\mathcal{P}}-\rank W_{\mathcal{P}}=\rank\widehat{Q}_{\mathcal{P}^{\prime}}-\rank W_{\mathcal{P}^{\prime}}+(p-p^{\prime})\,.

Proof.

The matrix
[A\,|\,\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{P}}]
has
p-p^{\prime}
more columns than

[A\,|\,\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{P}^{\prime}}]
and
\im[A\,|\,\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{P}^{\prime}}]\subseteq\im[A\,|\,\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{P}}]
.
Hence for some
0\leq\ell\leq p-p^{\prime}
we have

\rank\,[A\,|\,\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{P}}]=\rank\,[A\,|\,\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{P}^{\prime}}]+\ell.

It follows immediately that
s_{\mathcal{P}}\leq s_{\mathcal{P}^{\prime}}
, and that

\rank\widehat{Q}_{\mathcal{P}}=\rank\widehat{Q}_{\mathcal{P}^{\prime}}+(p-p^{\prime})-\ell,\qquad\rank W_{\mathcal{P}}=\rank W_{\mathcal{P}^{\prime}}-\ell

from where the remaining statements are immediate.
∎

Remark 3.29

(The effect of choosing a finer partition on the parameterisation of
\mathcal{E}_{\kappa}
)
.

For a finer partition, the dimension of
Y
, namely, the number of variables in the solvability system (
3.9
), decreases.
Lemma

3.28
tells us that a finer partition may provide a description of the set of positive equilibria where the toricity matrix
\widehat{Q}
has larger rank and hence a larger part of the equilibrium set is explained by a toric set. If this does not occur, then the rank of
W
must decrease, and hence the solvability system has fewer equations. In either case, choosing the finest possible partition can be advantageous (see
Examples

3.31
and
3.32
below).

The
finest partition
of the network, namely the unique finest partition over which a dynamically nontrivial network factors, is termed the
matroid partition
in
[
feliu:toric
]
where partitions are considered in the general setting of vertically parameterised systems. It has previously appeared in the context of reaction networks, see
[
bryan:partition
, Remark 3.4]
. We denote the finest partition by
\mathcal{P}_{f}
and its cardinality by
p_{f}
.

Given a dynamically nontrivial network with stoichiometric matrix
\Gamma
, by our remarks on partitions, the positive flux cone of the network, being a relatively open subset of
\ker\Gamma
, factors over some partition
\mathcal{P}
if and only if
\ker\Gamma
factors over
\mathcal{P}
, equivalently,
(\ker\Gamma)^{\perp}
, namely the row space of
\Gamma
, factors over
\mathcal{P}
. The finest partition of a network is thus precisely the finest partition over which
\ker\Gamma
factors, and can be computed as follows. Let
M
be a row-reduced matrix whose rows form a basis of
\ker\Gamma
, or of the row span of
\Gamma
.
Then
M
is, up to permutation of its rows and columns, a block-diagonal matrix, and two indices
i,j\in\{1,\dots,m\}
are in the same block of
\mathcal{P}_{f}
if and only if
i
and
j
are both in the support of some row of
M
. It is shown in
[
feliu:toric
, Lemma 4.6]
that the partition obtained in this way does not depend on the choice of
M
.
The following lemma gathers together some of the observations above.

Lemma 3.30
.

The positive flux cone
\mathcal{C}
of a dynamically nontrivial network factors over a
p
-partition
\mathcal{P}
if and only if the same holds for
\ker\Gamma
. There is a unique partition, denoted
\mathcal{P}_{f}
and termed the finest partition of the network, over which
\ker\Gamma
and
\mathcal{C}
factor, and which refines any other partition
\mathcal{P}
over which
\ker\Gamma
and
\mathcal{C}
factor.

We conclude with two examples illustrating the effects of the choice of partition on the resulting parameterisation, in particular, the two scenarios discussed in
Remark

3.29
.

Example 3.31

(Finest partition, increased toricity)
.

Consider the following
(3,4,2)
network

2\mathsf{X}{}\mathrel{\mathop{\makebox[0.0pt]{\rightarrow\displaystyle}}\limits^{\mkern 5.0mu\kappa_{1}\mkern 9.0mu}}{}\mathsf{X}+\mathsf{Y}\,,\qquad\mathsf{Y}{}\mathrel{\mathop{\makebox[0.0pt]{\rightarrow\displaystyle}}\limits^{\mkern 5.0mu\kappa_{2}\mkern 9.0mu}}{}\mathsf{X}\,,\qquad 2\mathsf{Y}{}\mathrel{\mathop{\makebox[0.0pt]{\rightarrow\displaystyle}}\limits^{\mkern 5.0mu\kappa_{3}\mkern 9.0mu}}{}\mathsf{Y}+\mathsf{Z}\,,\qquad\mathsf{Y}+\mathsf{Z}{}\mathrel{\mathop{\makebox[0.0pt]{\rightarrow\displaystyle}}\limits^{\mkern 5.0mu\kappa_{4}\mkern 9.0mu}}{}2\mathsf{Y}\,.

A basis of
\ker\Gamma
is
\{(1,1,0,0),(0,0,1,1)\}
, and so there are two partitions to consider: the finest partition
\mathcal{P}_{f}=\{\{1,2\},\{3,4\}\}
and the trivial partition. We can set
Z=(1,1,1)
.

For the trivial partition, say
\mathcal{P}
,
W_{\mathcal{P}}
and
Q_{\mathcal{P}}
are empty and
G=G_{\mathcal{P}}=[A\,|\,\bm{1}]^{-1}
. Setting
h(\alpha)=(\alpha,\alpha,1-\alpha,1-\alpha)
the equilibrium set is

\mathcal{E}_{\kappa}=\{(h(\alpha)/\kappa)^{\widehat{G}}:\alpha\in(0,1)\}=\Big\{(\tfrac{\sqrt{1-\alpha}\,\kappa_{2}}{\sqrt{\alpha}\sqrt{\kappa_{1}\,\kappa_{3}}},\tfrac{(1-\alpha)\,\kappa_{2}}{\alpha\,\kappa_{3}},\tfrac{(1-\alpha)\,\kappa_{2}}{\alpha\,\kappa_{4}}):\alpha\in(0,1)\Big\}.

Defining
q\colon(0,1)\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
by
q(\alpha):=\sqrt{(1-\alpha)/\alpha}
, the alternative system (
3.18
) takes the form
a\,q(\alpha)^{2}+b\,q(\alpha)-c=0
with
a,b,c>0
;
as the quadratic
at^{2}+bt-c
has one positive (nondegenerate) zero, and
q
is a smooth, strictly decreasing function, clearly the alternative system has exactly one nondegenerate solution. The network thus has exactly one positive equilibrium on each stoichiometric class for any choice of rate constants, and this equilibrium is nondegenerate.

For the finest partition
\mathcal{P}_{f}
,
Y
is a singleton and we readily realise that
\mathcal{E}_{\kappa}
is toric (see
Corollary

3.18
(iii)). As we may choose
Q_{\mathcal{P}_{f}}=(1,2,2,-2)^{\top}
, without computing
\widehat{G}_{\mathcal{P}_{f}}
we see that system (
3.18
) takes the form
a\mu^{2}+b\mu-K=0
with
a,b,K>0
, which clearly always has exactly one positive nondegenerate solution (see also
(iii) below).
∎

Example 3.32

(Finest partition, no increase in toricity)
.

Consider the following
(2,4,2)
network:

\mathsf{0}{}\mathrel{\mathop{\makebox[0.0pt]{\rightarrow\displaystyle}}\limits^{\mkern 5.0mu\kappa_{1}\mkern 9.0mu}}{}\mathsf{Y}\,,\qquad\mathsf{X}+\mathsf{Y}{}\mathrel{\mathop{\makebox[0.0pt]{\rightarrow\displaystyle}}\limits^{\mkern 5.0mu\kappa_{2}\mkern 9.0mu}}{}\mathsf{X}\,,\qquad\mathsf{X}{}\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{0.94722pt}{$\mathrel{\mathop{\makebox[0.0pt]{\to}}\limits^{\mkern 9.0mu\kappa_{3}\mkern 9.0mu}_{\makebox{\raisebox{3.76735pt}[0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle\mkern 9.0mu\hphantom{\kappa_{4}}\mkern 5.0mu$}}}}$}\hss}\raisebox{-0.94722pt}{$\mathrel{\mathop{\makebox[0.0pt]{\to}}\limits^{\mkern 5.0mu\hphantom{\kappa_{3}}\mkern 9.0mu}_{\makebox{\raisebox{3.76735pt}[0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle\mkern 9.0mu\kappa_{4}\mkern 9.0mu$}}}}$}}{}2\mathsf{Y}\,.

A basis of
\ker\Gamma
is
\{(1,1,0,0),(0,0,1,1)\}
and so the finest partition is
\mathcal{P}_{f}=\{\{1,2\},\{3,4\}\}
.
For the trivial partition, say
\mathcal{P}
,
Q_{\mathcal{P}}
is empty and hence solutions to the solvability system correspond to positive equilibria. Choosing
W_{\mathcal{P}}=(1,2,-2,-1)
, gives an alternative Bézout source bound of
3
. However, in this case, the solvability system takes the form

\frac{\kappa_{1}\kappa_{2}^{2}}{\kappa_{4}\kappa_{3}^{2}}=\left(\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}\right)^{3}\,,\quad\alpha\in(0,1)\,,

which has a unique nondegenerate solution for any choice of rate constants. Thus the network has a unique positive nondegenerate equilibrium for all
\kappa\in\mathbb{R}^{4}_{+}
. On the other hand,
[A\,|\,\bm{1}_{\mathcal{P}_{f}}]
has full rank, hence
W_{\mathcal{P}_{f}}
and
Q_{\mathcal{P}_{f}}
are empty,
Corollary

3.18
(iv) applies, and we conclude without further analysis that there is a unique positive nondegenerate equilibrium for all
\kappa\in\mathbb{R}^{4}_{+}
.
∎

4.
Special cases

With the mathematical framework in place, we derive further results for several particular classes of networks, using
3.14
and
3.21
.

4.1.
\mathcal{P}
-overdetermined and source deficient networks

Let us fix an
(n,m,r)
mass action network and a partition
\mathcal{P}
. If
s_{\mathcal{P}}<r
, then the solvability system (
3.9
) has more equations than variables: recall that in this case we refer to the network as
\mathcal{P}
-overdetermined. We show that positive equilibria of a
\mathcal{P}
-overdetermined mass action network exist only for an exceptional set of rate constants and, when they do exist, are always strongly degenerate (i.e., the network is strongly degenerate). Moreover, for parameters at which positive equilibria exist, the positive equilibrium set has dimension greater than
n-r
. We remark that a network being
\mathcal{P}
-overdetermined is sufficient, but not necessary, for these conclusions (see
Example

4.5
below).

In the special case where
\mathcal{P}
is the trivial partition, we refer to a
\mathcal{P}
-overdetermined network as
source deficient
. Importantly, to decide if a network is source deficient, we require knowledge only of its sources, and not its flux cone.
Source deficient networks are ones with too many affine dependencies amongst their sources, i.e., whose sources span an affine space of dimension less than the dimension of the span of the reaction vectors. For example, a rank-
2
network is source deficient if its sources lie on a line.

We remark that in the case of
(n,m,m-1)
networks, source deficiency is equivalent to affine dependence amongst the sources of the network (see also
[
banajiborosnonlinearity
, Remark 3.2]
for the special case of
(n,n+1,n)
networks.)

The main result on
\mathcal{P}
-overdetermined networks, extending Lemma 6 in
[
BBH2024smallbif
]
, is
Theorem

4.2
. We start with a well-known lemma. Hausdorff dimension is denoted
\mathrm{dim}_{\mathrm{H}}
.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{k}
be open and let
f\colon U\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
be a
C^{1}
diffeomorphism (i.e., a
C^{1}
map with
C^{1}
inverse) of
U
onto
f(U)
. Then given any
A\subseteq U
,
\mathrm{dim}_{\mathrm{H}}f(A)=\mathrm{dim}_{\mathrm{H}}A
.

Proof.

Note first that, restricted to any compact subset of
U
,
f
is clearly bi-Lipschitz onto its image. Let
\{U_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
be any sequence of compact
k
-dimensional subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{k}
such that
\cup_{i}U_{i}=U
. Let
A_{i}=A\cap U_{i}
and
\mathcal{A}_{i}:=f(A_{i})
so that
\mathcal{A}:=f(A)=\cup_{i}\mathcal{A}_{i}
. Then, by the bi-Lipschitz stability of Hausdorff dimension
[
falconerfractal
, Prop 3.3(b)]
,
\mathrm{dim}_{\mathrm{H}}\mathcal{A}_{i}=\mathrm{dim}_{\mathrm{H}}A_{i}
for each
i
, and hence

\mathrm{dim}_{\mathrm{H}}\mathcal{A}=\mathrm{dim}_{\mathrm{H}}\cup_{i}\mathcal{A}_{i}=\sup\nolimits_{i}\left\{\mathrm{dim}_{\mathrm{H}}\mathcal{A}_{i}\right\}=\sup\nolimits_{i}\left\{\mathrm{dim}_{\mathrm{H}}A_{i}\right\}=\mathrm{dim}_{\mathrm{H}}\cup_{i}A_{i}=\mathrm{dim}_{\mathrm{H}}A\,,

where the second and fourth equalities follow from the countable stability of Hausdorff dimension
[
falconerfractal
, p49]
.
∎

We now prove
Theorem

4.2
, the main theorem on
\mathcal{P}
-overdetermined networks. We note that Claim (iv), which follows easily from
Theorem

3.14
(i), already implies Claims (i)-(iii) by
[
feliu:dimension
, Thm 3.1]
. However, we present a self-contained proof.

Theorem 4.2
.

Consider a
\mathcal{P}
-overdetermined
(n,m,r)
mass action network, i.e., a network which factors over a partition
\mathcal{P}
of
\{1,\ldots,m\}
, and such that
s_{\mathcal{P}}<r
. Then

(i)

The set of
\kappa\in\mathbb{R}^{m}_{+}
for which
\mathcal{E}_{\kappa}\neq\emptyset
has
m
-dimensional Lebesgue measure
0
.

(ii)

For generic
\kappa\in\mathbb{R}^{m}_{+}
,
\mathcal{E}_{\kappa}=\emptyset
.

(iii)

The network is strongly degenerate.

(iv)

For
\kappa\in\mathbb{R}^{m}_{+}
such that
\mathcal{E}_{\kappa}\neq\emptyset
,
\mathcal{E}_{\kappa}
has dimension at least
n-r+1
.

Proof.

(i) Let
p:=|\mathcal{P}|
. Observe that
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{P}}:=\mathrm{im}\,[A\,|\,\bm{1}_{\mathcal{P}}]=\ker W
has dimension
s_{\mathcal{P}}+p<r+p
, so its orthogonal complement
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{P}}^{\perp}
has dimension
q:=m-s_{\mathcal{P}}-p
. For brevity, let
k:=m-r-p
, so that by assumption
k<q
. Recall that
Y\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{k}
, the domain of
h
, is open and note that clearly
\hat{h}\colon Y\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
defined by
\hat{h}(y)=\ln h(y)
is a
C^{1}
diffeomorphism onto its image. Then, by
Lemma

4.1
,
\mathrm{dim}_{\mathrm{H}}\mathcal{Y}=k
, where recall
\mathcal{Y}=\hat{h}(Y)
. Let
\Pi\colon\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{P}}^{\perp}
be the orthogonal projection onto
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{P}}^{\perp}
. As
\Pi
is clearly Lipschitz, and
\mathrm{dim}_{\mathrm{H}}
is nonincreasing under Lipschitz maps
[
falconerfractal
, Prop 3.3(a)]
,
\mathrm{dim}_{\mathrm{H}}\Pi(\mathcal{Y})\leq k<q
.

Now note that the set of rate constants for which solutions to (
3.5
) exist is

\{\kappa\in\mathbb{R}^{m}_{+}:(\ln\kappa+\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{P}})\cap\mathcal{Y}\neq\emptyset\}=\{\kappa\in\mathbb{R}^{m}_{+}:(\ln\kappa+\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{P}})\cap\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{P}}^{\perp}\in\Pi(\mathcal{Y})\}=\mathrm{exp}(\Pi(\mathcal{Y})\times\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{P}})\,.

We identify
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{P}}
with
\mathbb{R}^{m-q}
and note that
\mathrm{dim}_{\mathrm{H}}\left(\Pi(\mathcal{Y})\times\mathbb{R}^{m-q}\right)=\mathrm{dim}_{H}\Pi(\mathcal{Y})+m-q
(see, for example,
[
Tricot_1982
]
).
As a consequence,

\mathrm{dim}_{\mathrm{H}}\left(\mathrm{exp}(\Pi(\mathcal{Y})\times\mathbb{R}^{m-q})\right)=\mathrm{dim}_{\mathrm{H}}\left(\Pi(\mathcal{Y})\times\mathbb{R}^{m-q}\right)=\mathrm{dim}_{H}\Pi(\mathcal{Y})+m-q<m\,,

where the first equality follows from
Lemma

4.1
, and the final inequality from above. Hence
\mathrm{exp}(\Pi(\mathcal{Y})\oplus\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{P}})
has
m
-dimensional Hausdorff measure
0
, and consequently
m
-dimensional Lebesgue measure
0
. This confirms that the system admits positive equilibria for only an exceptional set of rate constants.

(ii) As the set
\{(x,\kappa)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}_{+}:\Gamma(\kappa\circ x^{A})=0\}
is semialgebraic, the Tarski-Seidenberg theorem
[
RAG
, Prop. 5.2.1]
tells us that so is its projection onto
\mathbb{R}^{m}_{+}
; namely, the set
\mathcal{Z}
of rate constants for which positive equilibria exist is semialgebraic. As
\mathcal{Z}
has zero Lebesgue measure by (i), it does not contain an open ball and consequently its semialgebraic dimension is strictly smaller than
m
. The same holds for its closure in
\mathbb{R}^{m}_{+}
in the Zariski topology
[
RAG
, Prop. 2.8.2]
. Therefore
\mathcal{Z}
is contained in a proper algebraic subvariety of
\mathbb{R}^{m}_{+}
giving (ii).

(iii) The claim that all positive equilibria are degenerate would be an easy corollary of (ii) via the implicit function theorem. To show strong degeneracy, recall from (
2.5
) that it is enough to show that
J^{\prime}:=\Gamma\,D_{v}\,A
has rank strictly smaller than
r
for each fixed
v\in\mathcal{C}
. The condition
\mathrm{rank}\,[A\,|\,\bm{1}_{\mathcal{P}}]<r+p
, holds if and only if either (1)
\mathrm{rank}\,A<r
; or (2)
\mathrm{rank}\,A:=\hat{r}\geq r
and
\mathrm{dim}(\mathrm{im}\bm{1}_{\mathcal{P}}\cap\mathrm{im}\,A)>\hat{r}-r
. We consider each case:

(1)

If
\mathrm{rank}\,A<r
, then clearly
\mathrm{rank}\,J^{\prime}<r
, since
J^{\prime}
includes
A
as a factor.

(2)

Assume that
\mathrm{rank}\,A:=\hat{r}\geq r
and there exist linearly independent
\{w_{1},\ldots,w_{\hat{r}-r+1}\}\subseteq\mathrm{im}\bm{1}_{\mathcal{P}}\cap\mathrm{im}\,A
.
The first assumption gives the existence of linearly independent vectors
v_{1},\dots,v_{n-\hat{r}}\in\ker A\subseteq\ker J^{\prime}
. For each
i\in\{1,\ldots,\hat{r}-r+1\}
, choose
\hat{v}_{i}
such that
w_{i}=A\hat{v}_{i}
. Clearly
\{\hat{v}_{1},\ldots,\hat{v}_{\hat{r}-r+1}\}
are linearly independent. The fact that
w_{i}\in\mathrm{im}\bm{1}_{\mathcal{P}}
and
\ker\Gamma
factors over
\mathcal{P}
implies that
D_{v}w_{i}\in\ker\Gamma
for each
i
. Thus
\hat{v}_{i}\in\ker J^{\prime}\backslash\ker A
for each
i
, and so
v_{1},\dots,v_{n-\hat{r}},\hat{v}_{1},\ldots,\hat{v}_{\hat{r}-r+1}
are linearly independent vectors in
\ker J^{\prime}
. This implies that
\mathrm{dim}\,\ker J^{\prime}\geq n-r+1
, and hence
\rank J^{\prime}<r
as claimed.

(iv) Let
\kappa
be a rate constant such that positive equilibria exist, namely, (
3.5
) admits a solution
(x_{0},\lambda_{0})
for some
\alpha\in Y
. By
Theorem

3.14
(i), the equilibrium set then includes the branch

\{x_{0}\circ\mu^{\widehat{Q}}\,:\,\mu\in\mathbb{R}^{n-s_{\mathcal{P}}}_{+}\}\,,

which has dimension
n-s_{\mathcal{P}}>n-r
by
Lemma

4.1
as the map
\mu\mapsto x_{0}\circ\mu^{\widehat{Q}}
is a smooth
C^{1}
diffeomorphism onto its image.
∎

Remark 4.3

(Dimension of the equilibrium set in
\mathcal{P}
-overdetermined networks)
.

Theorem

4.2
(iv) tells us that the positive equilibrium set
\mathcal{E}_{\kappa}
of a
\mathcal{P}
-overdetermined network has dimension greater than
n-r
when it is nonempty. In the special case
r=n
(networks of full rank), this means that, when nonempty,
\mathcal{E}_{\kappa}
has positive dimension, and it immediately follows that positive equilibria are not isolated and hence
must
be degenerate, equivalently, strongly degenerate. In the case where
r<n
, when
\mathcal{E}_{\kappa}
is nonempty,
\dim\mathcal{E}_{\kappa}+r>n
, and so we expect intersections of
\mathcal{E}_{\kappa}
and stoichiometric classes generally to have positive dimension when nonempty.
Example

4.4
demonstrates that a zero dimensional intersection is possible, though
Theorem

4.2
tells us that even in this case equilibria on this class are strongly degenerate.

Example 4.4

(A source deficient network with an isolated equilibrium on a stoichiometric class)
.

The following dynamically nontrivial
(3,4,2)
network is source deficient, having rank
2
, but only two distinct sources:
\mathsf{X}+2\mathsf{Y}+\mathsf{Z}
{}\mathrel{\mathop{\makebox[0.0pt]{\rightarrow\displaystyle}}\limits^{\mkern 5.0mu\kappa_{1}\mkern 9.0mu}}{}
{}\mathrel{\mathop{\makebox[0.0pt]{\rightarrow\displaystyle}}\limits^{\mkern 5.0mu\kappa_{2}\mkern 9.0mu}}{}
{}\mathrel{\mathop{\makebox[0.0pt]{\rightarrow\displaystyle}}\limits^{\mkern 5.0mu\kappa_{3}\mkern 9.0mu}}{}
2\mathsf{X}+2\mathsf{Y}
2\mathsf{X}+\mathsf{Y}+\mathsf{Z}
2\mathsf{Y}+2\mathsf{Z}
2\mathsf{X}+3\mathsf{Y}+2\mathsf{Z}{}\mathrel{\mathop{\makebox[0.0pt]{\rightarrow\displaystyle}}\limits^{\mkern 5.0mu\kappa_{4}\mkern 9.0mu}}{}2\mathsf{X}+4\mathsf{Y}+\mathsf{Z}\,.

Stoichiometric classes have equation
x+y+z=K
for
K\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
. Setting
\kappa_{1}=\kappa_{2}=\kappa_{4}=1
,
\kappa_{3}=2
the reader may confirm that positive equilibria are the positive solutions to
x\,y\,z=1
. Hence there is a unique equilibrium on the stoichiometric class defined by
x+y+z=3
. By
Theorem

4.2
, this equilibrium is strongly degenerate.
∎

It is not necessary for a network to be
\mathcal{P}
-overdetermined for it to be strongly degenerate, as illustrated by the next example.

Example 4.5

(A strongly degenerate network which is not
\mathcal{P}
-overdetermined)
.

Consider the dynamically nontrivial
(2,4,2)
network

2\mathsf{X}{}\mathrel{\mathop{\makebox[0.0pt]{\rightarrow\displaystyle}}\limits}{}3\mathsf{X},\quad 2\mathsf{X}{}\mathrel{\mathop{\makebox[0.0pt]{\rightarrow\displaystyle}}\limits}{}\mathsf{X}+\mathsf{Y},\quad 0{}\mathrel{\mathop{\makebox[0.0pt]{\rightarrow\displaystyle}}\limits}{}\mathsf{Y},\quad 2\mathsf{Y}{}\mathrel{\mathop{\makebox[0.0pt]{\rightarrow\displaystyle}}\limits}{}\mathsf{Y}\,.

The reader may immediately check that
\mathcal{P}_{f}
is the trivial partition and the network is not source deficient. However, the network is degenerate; equivalently, as the network has full rank, it is strongly degenerate.
∎

Remark 4.6

(
\mathcal{P}
-overdetermined networks are
\mathcal{P}_{f}
-overdetermined)
.

By
Lemma

3.28
,
s_{\mathcal{P}_{f}}
is minimal amongst
\{s_{\mathcal{P}}\}
for all partitions
\mathcal{P}
over which the network factors, as
\mathcal{P}_{f}
refines them all. It follows that if a network is
\mathcal{P}
-overdetermined for any partition
\mathcal{P}
, then it also is
\mathcal{P}_{f}
-overdetermined. The converse is not true as illustrated by the following example.

Example 4.7

(A
\mathcal{P}_{f}
-overdetermined network that is not source deficient)
.

Consider the following dynamically nontrivial
(2,4,2)
network:

\mathsf{X}{}\mathrel{\mathop{\makebox[0.0pt]{\rightarrow\displaystyle}}\limits}{}\mathsf{X}+\mathsf{Y},\quad 2\mathsf{X}{}\mathrel{\mathop{\makebox[0.0pt]{\rightarrow\displaystyle}}\limits}{}\mathsf{X}+\mathsf{Y},\quad 2\mathsf{Y}{}\mathrel{\mathop{\makebox[0.0pt]{\rightarrow\displaystyle}}\limits}{}\mathsf{X}+\mathsf{Y},\quad\mathsf{Y}{}\mathrel{\mathop{\makebox[0.0pt]{\rightarrow\displaystyle}}\limits}{}\mathsf{0}\,.

The reader may easily confirm that the finest partition is
\mathcal{P}_{f}=\{\{1,4\},\{2,3\}\}
and the network is
\mathcal{P}_{f}
-overdetermined as
s_{\mathcal{P}_{f}}=1<2=r
. However, the network is not source deficient as the source rank is
s=2=r
.
∎

Even if a network is not
\mathcal{P}
-overdetermined, it may still occur that some subnetwork is overdetermined, giving rise to conclusions similar to those of
Theorem

4.2
. Recall from
Lemma

3.1
that a network is strongly degenerate if it has a strongly partition subnetwork. This occurs, in particular, if some partition subnetwork is source deficient.

Theorem 4.8
.

Assume that an
(n,m,r)
network
\mathcal{N}
factors over a
p
-partition
\mathcal{P}
of
\{1,\ldots,m\}
. Choose
W
as in
Lemma

3.11
, fix
i
, and let
w_{i}
denote the number of rows of
W
supported on
P_{i}
. If, for some
i
,
\dim\mathcal{C}_{i}\leq w_{i}
, then
\mathcal{N}
does not admit positive equilibria for generic
\kappa
in
\mathbb{R}^{m}_{+}
, and is strongly degenerate.

Proof.

Let
m_{i}=|P_{i}|
, and consider the
(n,m_{i},m_{i}-\dim\mathcal{C}_{i})
partition subnetwork
\mathcal{N}_{i}
corresponding to
P_{i}
. The source rank of
\mathcal{N}_{i}
satisfies

s_{i}:=\rank\,[A^{(i)}|\bm{1}]-1\leq m_{i}-w_{i}-1\,,

as, by assumption,
\ker\,[A^{(i)}\,|\,\bm{1}]^{\top}
contains at least

linearly independent vectors. As the rank of

is
r_{i}:=m_{i}-\dim\mathcal{C}_{i}
, we have

s_{i}\leq m_{i}-w_{i}-1\leq m_{i}-\dim\mathcal{C}_{i}-1<r_{i}\,,

\mathcal{N}_{i}
is source deficient. As a consequence, by
Theorem

(ii),
\mathcal{N}_{i}
does not admit positive equilibria for generic

, and the same holds for

for generic
\kappa
. By

4.2
(iii),
\mathcal{N}_{i}
is strongly degenerate, and the same holds for

by
Lemma

3.1
.
∎

Corollary 4.9
.

Assume that an
(n,m,r)
network factors over a
p
-partition
\mathcal{P}
of

. If, for some

\dim\mathcal{C}_{i}\leq m_{i}-\ell_{i}
, where
\ell_{i}
is the number of distinct sources among the reactions in

, then the network does not admit positive equilibria for generic
\kappa
in
\mathbb{R}^{m}_{+}
, and is strongly degenerate.

4.2.
\mathcal{P}
-toric networks

If
s_{\mathcal{P}}=r
, then the solvability system
h(\alpha)^{W}=\kappa^{W}
has the same number of equations as unknowns. Recall that we refer to such networks as
\mathcal{P}
-toric. This terminology is explained by the fact that if the solvability system has a finite number of solutions, then by
Theorem

3.14
(i)
\mathcal{E}_{\kappa}
is a finite union of toric sets, a notion referred to as
local toricity
in
[
feliu:toric
]
.

Remark 4.10

(
\mathcal{P}
-toric networks automatically give alternative Bézout bounds)
.

For any fixed
\alpha
, the system
Z[(h(\alpha)/\kappa)^{\widehat{G}}\circ\mu^{\widehat{Q}}]=K
can be written as a system of
n-r

polynomial
equations in the
n-r
parameters
\mu
. Thus the equations
h(\alpha)^{W}=\kappa^{W}
and
Z[(h(\alpha)/\kappa)^{\widehat{G}}\circ\mu^{\widehat{Q}}]=K
give an alternative Bézout bound regardless of whether
G
is an integer matrix or not: this is the product of the bounds obtained from
h(\alpha)^{W}=\kappa^{W}
considered as a (square) polynomial system in
\alpha
, and
Z(h(\alpha)/\kappa)^{\widehat{G}}\circ\mu^{\widehat{Q}}=K
considered as a (square) polynomial system in
\mu
and treating
\alpha
as constant. The alternative Bézout bound in
Example

3.27
was of this form.

Recall that
\mathcal{A}_{\kappa}
, the set of
\alpha\in Y
solving
h(\alpha)^{W}=\kappa^{W}
, depends, via
W
, on the chosen partition
\mathcal{P}
. The following result confirms that under the assumption of nondegeneracy,
\mathcal{A}_{\kappa}
cannot be generically finite unless the network is
\mathcal{P}
-toric. See
[
feliu:toric
, Thm 5.3]
for a more algebraic approach where toricity properties are extended to
\mathbb{C}
.

Theorem 4.11

(Finiteness of
\mathcal{A}_{\kappa}
)
.

Consider an
(n,m,r)
network which factors over a partition
\mathcal{P}
.

(i)

If
s_{\mathcal{P}}\leq r
, then
\#\mathcal{A}_{\kappa}<+\infty
generically for
\kappa\in\mathbb{R}^{m}_{+}
.

(ii)

If
s_{\mathcal{P}}>r
, then
\#\mathcal{A}_{\kappa}<+\infty
generically for
\kappa\in\mathbb{R}^{m}_{+}
if and only if the network is strongly degenerate.

In particular, if the network is not strongly degenerate, then
\#\mathcal{A}_{\kappa}<+\infty
generically for
\kappa\in\mathbb{R}^{m}_{+}
if and only if
s_{\mathcal{P}}=r
.

Proof.

We start with three observations: (1) If the network is strongly degenerate, then by
Proposition

2.1

\mathcal{E}_{\kappa}=\emptyset
generically for
\kappa\in\mathbb{R}^{m}_{+}
and hence
\#\mathcal{A}_{\kappa}=0<+\infty
generically; (2)
\dim\mathcal{E}_{\kappa}=\rank(\widehat{Q})=n-s_{\mathcal{P}}
if and only if
\mathcal{A}_{\kappa}
is finite and nonempty: this follows as
\dim\mathcal{E}_{\kappa}=\dim\mathcal{A}_{\kappa}+n-s_{\mathcal{P}}
(see
Remark

3.16
). (3) If the network is not strongly degenerate, then the set
\mathcal{Z}
of rate constants for which
\mathcal{E}_{\kappa}\neq\emptyset
has nonempty interior, and
\dim\mathcal{E}_{\kappa}=n-r
for generic
\kappa\in\mathcal{Z}
by
Proposition

2.1
.

With this in place, (1) covers: the
\mathcal{P}
-overdetermined case, namely,
s_{\mathcal{P}}<r
(recall from
Theorem

4.2
that
\mathcal{P}
-overdetermined networks are strongly degenerate); the case
s_{\mathcal{P}}=r
with the network strongly degenerate; and the reverse implication in (ii).

If
s_{\mathcal{P}}=r
and the network is not strongly degenerate, then (3) tells us that
\dim\mathcal{E}_{\kappa}=n-s_{\mathcal{P}}
for generic
\kappa
in
\mathcal{Z}
, and hence generically
\#\mathcal{A}_{\kappa}<+\infty
by (2). This completes the proof of (i).

If
s_{\mathcal{P}}>r
and the network is not strongly degenerate then
\dim\mathcal{E}_{\kappa}=n-r>n-s_{\mathcal{P}}
generically for
\kappa\in\mathcal{Z}
by (3), and hence by (2),
\mathcal{A}_{\kappa}
is infinite generically in
\mathcal{Z}
. This completes proof of (ii).

The last statement is immediate from (i), (ii) and
Theorem

4.2
.
∎

We say that a network
admits a monomial parameterisation
if there exists a nonnegative integer
\ell
and a matrix
B\in\mathbb{Z}^{n\times\ell}
such that for all
\kappa\in\mathbb{R}^{m}_{+}
, either
\mathcal{E}_{\kappa}=\emptyset
or
\mathcal{E}_{\kappa}=\{x^{*}_{\kappa}\circ\mu^{B}:\mu\in\mathbb{R}^{\ell}_{+}\}
for some
x^{*}_{\kappa}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}
. We say the network
admits generically a monomial parameterisation
, if this holds for
\kappa
in a nonempty Zariski open subset of
\mathbb{R}^{m}_{+}
. Observe that by definition, a strongly degenerate network is considered here to admit generically a monomial parameterisation.

Networks which admit a monomial parameterisation include deficiency zero networks
[
Craciun2009toric
,
Feinberg1972complex
]
, networks satisfying the hypotheses of the deficiency one theorem
[
Feinberg1995class
]
, networks with toric steady states
[
Millan2012toricsteadystates
]
, and the networks studied in
[
conradi2019total
]
. By
[
Millan2012toricsteadystates
]
, see also
[
Muller2015injectivity
,
sadeghimanesh:multi
]
, deciding upon multistationarity (the existence of multiple positive equilibria in a stoichiometric class), reduces to a simple check involving only the exponent matrix
B
and
\Gamma
.

Clearly, if
\#\mathcal{A}_{\kappa}\leq 1
for all
\kappa\in\mathbb{R}^{m}_{+}
, then the network admits a monomial parameterisation with exponent matrix being the toricity matrix
\widehat{Q}
by
Theorem

3.14
(i). The next result shows that for a network that is not strongly degenerate, and working with the finest partition, the existence of a monomial parameterisation in fact implies that
\#\mathcal{A}_{\kappa}\leq 1
for all
\kappa\in\mathbb{R}^{m}_{+}
.

Theorem 4.12
.

A
(n,m,r)
network that is not strongly degenerate admits a monomial parameterisation if and only if, with the finest partition
\mathcal{P}_{f}
,
\#\mathcal{A}_{\kappa}\leq 1
for all
\kappa\in\mathbb{R}^{m}_{+}
. Additionally, in this case the exponent matrix is any choice of
\widehat{Q}_{\mathcal{P}_{f}}
.

Proof.

The reverse implication follows from
Theorem

3.14
. For the forward implication, as the network is not strongly degenerate,
[
feliu:toric
, Sec 4]
gives that if the network admits a monomial parameterisation, then necessarily the exponent matrix
B
consists of the first
n
rows of any matrix whose columns form a basis of
\ker\,[A\,|\,\bm{1}_{\mathcal{P}_{f}}]
. Hence the result follows again by
Theorem

3.14
.
∎

Remark 4.13
.

When
\mathcal{P}_{f}

.

Consider a

-toric,
