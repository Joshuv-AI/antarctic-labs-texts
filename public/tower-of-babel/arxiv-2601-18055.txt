Large Coupling Convergence Beyond Definiteness
Christian Koke

Abstract
. We study convergence of operator families of the form A β = A + β ​ B A_{\beta}=A+\beta B towards an effective operator defined on ker ⁡ ( B ) \ker(B) , as the coupling constant β \beta tends to infinity. Crucially, we focus on the setting where neither A A nor B B can be assumed to be positive- (or negative-) semi-definite. We are hence outside the classical form-theoretic framework, where results based on Kato’s monotone convergence theorem would be applicable. Thus, instead of form methods, our approach builds on classical resolvent identities to study convergence of the family { A β } β \{A_{\beta}\}_{\beta} . Our findings are that: (i) Strong resolvent convergence holds (without further spectral assumptions) if A + β ​ B A+\beta B is self-adjoint and the compression of A A onto ker ⁡ ( B ) \ker(B) is well behaved. (ii) Under the more detailed assumption that 0 ∈ σ ⁡ ( B ) 0\in\sigma(B) is isolated, norm resolvent convergence can be established even if A + β ​ B A+\beta B is merely closed, provided the quasinilpotent part of B B at zero vanishes and certain conditions on the interplay of A A and B B are met. Importantly, if B B is not self-adjoint we find that the limit operator not only depends on ker ⁡ ( B ) \ker(B) as a Hilbert space, but crucially also on the precise form of the Riesz projector at 0 ∈ σ ⁡ ( B ) 0\in\sigma(B) onto ker ⁡ ( B ) \ker(B) .



1.
Introduction

The asymptotic analysis of operator families of the form
A_{\beta}=A+\beta B
, where
A
is a densely defined non-negative self-adjoint operator and
B
is a non-negative perturbation, is by now well established. Arguably most attention has been paid to the fundamental example of
A_{\beta}
being an indexed family of Schrödinger operators, with
A=-\Delta
the free Hamiltonian and
B=V
a non-negative interaction potential
[
22
,
21
,
20
,
17
]
.
In order to account for point interactions, as they appear in certain physical settings
[
1
]
, the condition that
B
be a true operator was subsequently relaxed: Instead of arguing at the level of operators
A,B
, the problem was lifted to the level of (densely defined) quadratic forms
\mathfrak{a},\mathfrak{b}
. When bounded from below, the form
\mathfrak{a}_{\beta}=\mathfrak{a}+\beta\mathfrak{b}
is then canonically associated a densely defined operator
A_{\beta}
via the Friedrichs extension. A rich detailed theory of such large coupling perturbations of quadratic forms – as well as resolvent convergence of their associated operators – has since been established
[
24
,
50
,
16
,
14
,
15
,
2
,
3
,
11
]
. Most of these works take as their starting point the abstract framework of strongly perturbed quadratic forms
a_{\beta}=\mathfrak{a}+\beta\mathfrak{b}
initially developed by Kato
[
31
]
and later extended by Simon
[
46
]
who in their monotone convergence theorems established (generalized) strong resolvent convergence of the operator
A_{\beta}
associated to
\mathfrak{a}_{\beta}
to a limit operator
\underline{A}
defined on
\ker(\mathfrak{q})
.

Common among most of these works is the need for the considered quadratic forms
\mathfrak{a},\mathfrak{b}
(and hence also their associated operators
A,B
) be positive semi-definite. Surveys into the terrain outside the realm of positive definiteness assumptions have so far been limited: In
[
12
]
e.g. the case where
\mathfrak{a}
and
-\mathfrak{b}
(as opposed to
\mathfrak{b}
) are assumed to be non-negative, is treated. The series of works
[
28
,
29
,
27
]
considered the setting of Schrödinger operators with strong magnetic fields, and
[
10
,
23
]
considered the example of convergence of two dimensional Dirac operators perturbed by an increasing mass term supported only outside of a domain. In particular, the author is not aware of results on large coupling convergence in the sectorial- or more general closed setting.

In this paper, we initiate a systematic study of
large coupling convergence beyond positivity assumptions
, where neither the perturbing operator
B
nor the background operator
A
is assumed to be associated with a semi-definite form. In contrast to most prior approaches, we hence avoid form methods altogether and instead directly work at the abstract operator level
A_{\beta}=A+\beta B
. Under suitable assumptions, we then establish convergence of the family of resolvents
\{(A+\beta B-z)^{-1}\}_{\beta}
as
\beta\rightarrow\infty
. A necessary condition for the resolvents in this family to be well-defined, is that the operators in the family
\{A_{\beta}\}_{\beta}
are closed, at least for sufficiently large
\beta\gg 1
. Outside of lower-boundedness assumptions, there exist two typical settings that guarantee that the sum of two operators
A,B
is closed
[
31
,
51
]
, which we will hence also consider here:

(1)

A
is bounded relative to
B
:
If
A
is bounded with respect to a closed operator
B
, then by definition
\mathscr{D}(B)\subseteq\mathscr{D}(A)
and there are constants
a,b\geq 0
so that

(1.1)

\displaystyle\quad\|A\psi\|\leq a\|B\psi\|+b\|\psi\|,\quad\forall\psi\in\mathscr{D}(B).

Clearly then,
A
is also relatively bounded with respect to
\beta B
and by rescaling
\beta
if necessary, we may assume without loss of generality, that
a<1
as soon as (
1.1
) is true for
any
generic
a>0
. Standard results (e.g.
[
31
, Theorem IV.1.1]
) then
guarantee that
A+\beta B
is closed on
\mathscr{D}(B)
.

(2)

B
is bounded and
A
is closed:
Clearly adding a bounded operator to
(A,\mathscr{D}(A))
yields an operator that is closed on the same domain.

In these settings, we then determine conditions under which the resolvent family
\{(A+\beta B-z)^{-1}\}_{\beta}
converges to the resolvent of a limit operator
\underline{A}
densely defined on
\ker{B}
:

In Section
2
we consider the setting where both
A
and
B
are still self-adjoint, but no longer positive- (or negative-) semi-definite. For self-adjoint
B
, the spectral projection
P_{\{0\}}
then provides a canonical projection onto
\ker{B}
, and we show that as soon as the compression
\underline{A}=PA
on
\mathscr{D}(A)\cap P\mathscr{A}
of
A
onto
\ker{B}
is well-behaved, we have
strong
resolvent convergence.
In Section
3
, we then consider the more general non-self-adjoint setting: At a fundamental level, if
B
is no longer self-adjoint (or more generally normal), we lose access to the Borel functional calculus and only retain the holomorphic one. In order to still define a spectral projection onto
\ker(B)
, we thus need to assume that
0\in\sigma(B)
is isolated, as only then the Riesz-projector onto
\{0\}
is well defined. This is a stronger assumption on the spectrum of
B
around
0
than in the preceding section, which explains why it
already allows to establish the stronger notion of
norm
-resolvent convergence in many settings. Crucially, we find that within this general non-self-adjoint setting, we need to assume that the quasi-nilpotent part of
B
at
0\in\sigma(B)
vanishes, if we want to hope for convergence at all. Additionally, we find that the limit operator
\underline{A}
towards which
A_{\beta}
convergence in a generalized norm resolvent sense not only depends on
\ker{B}
as a linear space, but also on the way the Riesz projector
P
of
B
at
0\in\sigma(B)
projects onto
\ker(B)
.

Throughout our paper, we illustrate our results using examples from partial differential equations and abstract operator theory (Examples
2.3
,
3.5
,
3.12
), high energy physics (Examples
2.4
,
3.6
,
3.10
)
and
graph theory (Examples
3.4
,
3.14
). In fact a main motivation in writing this paper was to develop tools that can be applied to the study of effective descriptions of directed graphs that contain high-connectivity clusters, which has applications to graph based machine learning
[
32
]
. We briefly illustrate this point in Example
3.14
and pursue the study of the graph setting in full depth in a companion paper
[
42
]
, using the tools developed in this note.

2.
Strong Resolvent Convergence when
A+\beta B
is self-adjoint

In this section, we assume that
B
is symmetric bounded and the operator
A
is self adjoint on its dense domain
\mathscr{D}(A)\subseteq\mathscr{H}
. We are then interested in strong generalized resolvent convergence convergence of the family
A_{\beta}=A+\beta B
.

As we discuss below, the limit operator to which this family converges is defined via the notion of compression of an operator
[
49
,
44
,
5
]
, which we hence briefly revisit here: Given a linear operator
A
densely defined on the Hilbert space
\mathscr{H}=\mathscr{V}\oplus\mathscr{V}^{\perp}
, its compression onto the linear subspace
\mathscr{V}\oplus\mathscr{V}^{\perp}
is the induced operator

(2.1)

P_{\mathscr{V}}A:\mathscr{D}(A)\cap\mathscr{V}\longrightarrow\mathscr{V}.

If
A
is unbounded, it is generically possible that
\mathscr{D}(A)\cap\mathscr{V}=\{0\}
, so that in-particular the compressed operator is not densely defined, let alone self-adjont
[
5
]
. If however for example
\dim(\mathscr{V}^{\perp})=\text{codim}(\mathscr{V})<\infty
, density of the linear manifold
\mathscr{D}(A)\cap\mathscr{V}\subseteq\mathscr{V}
can be established
[
25
]
. Furthermore, if in this case
A
is self adjoint on
\mathscr{D}(A)
, the compression
P_{\mathscr{V}}A
of
A
is in fact also self-adjoint on its domain
\mathscr{D}(A)\cap\mathscr{V}

[
49
]
. Interestingly, this holds even in the extreme case, where
\mathscr{V}^{\perp}\cap\mathscr{D}(A)=\{0\}
.
Beyond finite codimension, well behaved settings of self-adjoint operators compressed onto subspaces with infinite codimension are easily construced: As a simple example take e.g. a generic densely defined self adjoint operator
(A,\mathscr{D}(A))
on the Hilbert space
\mathscr{H}
. For some
d\geq 2
and a a Hermitian Matrix
\mathbf{M}\in\mathds{C}^{d\times d}
, consider then the operator

(2.2)

\mathbf{M}\otimes A:\mathds{C}^{d}\otimes\mathscr{D}(A)\longrightarrow\mathds{C}^{d}\otimes\mathscr{H},

on the Hilbert space
\tilde{\mathscr{H}}=\mathds{C}^{d}\otimes\mathscr{H}
.
It is not hard to verify that
\mathbf{M}\otimes A
is self-adjoint on its domain. Set now
\mathscr{V}=V\otimes\mathscr{H}
for some linear subspace
V\subseteq\mathds{C}^{d}
. Then if
V
is a true subspace of
\mathds{C}^{d}
, we have
\text{codim}(\mathscr{V})=\infty
and the compression of
\mathbf{M}\otimes A
onto
\mathscr{V}
is given by

(2.3)

[P_{V}\mathbf{M}P_{V}]\otimes A:V\otimes\mathscr{D}(A)\longrightarrow V\otimes\mathscr{H},

with the orthogonal projection
P_{V}
onto
V
in
\mathds{C}^{d}
.

We are now interested in the general setting where
\mathscr{V}
is the kernel of an operator
B
, and the compression of
A
onto
\mathscr{V}=\ker(B)
is self adjoint. In this setting, we can establish generalized strong resolvent convergence of the family
A_{\beta}=A+\beta B
to an operator
\underline{A}
, exactly given as the compression of
A
onto
V
:

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
A:\mathscr{D}(A)\rightarrow\mathscr{H}
be a densely defined, self-adjoint operator, and let
B
be self-adjoint and either bounded or so that
A
is relatively bounded with respect to
B
. Let
P:=P_{\ker(B)}\equiv\chi_{\{0\}}(B)

be the orthogonal projection onto
\ker(B)
.
Assume that the compression of
\underline{A}
onto
\ker(B)
is self-adjoint.
Then for all
z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{R}
and
\psi\in\mathscr{H}
:

(2.4)

\displaystyle\left\|(A+\beta B-z)^{-1}\psi-P(\underline{A}-z)^{-1}P\psi\right\|\longrightarrow 0\quad\text{as }\beta\to\infty

Proof.

The proof proceeds by first establishing
weak
generalized resolvent convergence and then making use of the first resolvent formula to elevate this to strong resolvent convergence. Let
z\in\mathds{C}\setminus\mathbb{R}
be arbitrary.
We begin by establishing that with
Q=Id_{\mathscr{H}}-P
we have
Q(A+\beta B-z)^{-1}\rightarrow 0
weakly. We will make use of the fact that since
(A+\beta B-z)(A+\beta B-z)^{-1}\psi=\psi
, we have

(A-z)^{-1}B(A+\beta B-z)^{-1}\psi=\frac{1}{\beta}\left[(A-z)^{-1}-(A+\beta B-z)^{-1}\right]\psi.

Let us take
\eta\in\text{Ran}\{B\}=\text{Ran}\{Q\}=\text{Ran}\{P\}^{\perp}
. Choose
\xi\in\text{Ran}\{B\}
such that
B\xi=\eta
. Then we have

\langle\eta,(A+\beta B-z)^{-1}\psi\rangle=\langle B\xi,(A+\beta B-z)^{-1}\psi\rangle=\langle\xi,B(A+\beta B-z)^{-1}\psi\rangle.

Fix
\epsilon>0
. Since
\text{Ran}\{(A-\overline{z})^{-1}\}=\mathscr{H}
,
we may find
\zeta\in\mathscr{H}
so that

(A-\overline{z})^{-1}\zeta=\xi.

Thus:

(2.5)

\displaystyle\left|\langle\eta,(A+\beta B-z)^{-1}\psi\rangle\right|=\left|\langle\xi,B(A+\beta B-z)^{-1}\psi\rangle\right|

(2.6)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left|\langle(A-\overline{z})^{-1}\zeta,B(A+\beta B-z)^{-1}\psi\rangle\right|=\left|\langle\zeta,\left[(A-z)^{-1}-(A+\beta B-z)^{-1}\right]\psi\rangle\right|/\beta\rightarrow 0.

Since
z\in\mathds{C}\setminus\mathds{R}
above was arbitrary, and
(A+\beta B-z)^{-1}Q=\left[Q(A+\beta B-\overline{z})^{-1}\right]^{*}
, this also proves that
(A+\beta B-z)^{-1}Q\rightarrow 0
weakly.
Next we prove that
(A+\beta B-z)^{-1}P\rightarrow(\underline{A}-z)^{-1}P
weakly. We recall that we assume that
PA:\mathscr{D}(A)\rightarrow P\mathscr{H}\rightarrow P\mathscr{H}
is self adjoint. Thus
(PA-z)\mathscr{D}(A)=P\mathscr{H}
. Fix
\phi\in\mathscr{D}(A)\rightarrow P\mathscr{H}
and set
\psi=P(A-z)\phi
.
We clearly have

(\underline{A}-z)^{-1}\psi=(\underline{A}-z)^{-1}P(A-z)\phi=\phi.

We also find for all
\eta\in\mathscr{H}
:

(2.7)

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{\beta\rightarrow\infty}\langle\eta,(A+\beta B-z)^{-1}\psi\rangle=\lim\limits_{\beta\rightarrow\infty}\langle\eta,(A+\beta B-z)^{-1}P(A-z)\phi\rangle

(2.8)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{\beta\rightarrow\infty}\langle\eta,(A+\beta B-z)^{-1}(P+Q)(A-z)\phi\rangle=\lim\limits_{\beta\rightarrow\infty}\langle\eta,(A+\beta B-\overline{z})^{-1}(A-z)\phi\rangle

(2.9)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{\beta\rightarrow\infty}\langle\eta,(A+\beta B-\overline{z})^{-1}(A+\beta B-z)\phi\rangle=\langle\eta,\phi\rangle

In total we have thus proved the weak convergence

(A+\beta B-z)^{-1}\rightarrow P(\underline{A}-z)^{-1}P
.
Next we want to promote this to strong resolvent convergence. To achieve this, we make use of the first resolvent formula

(A-z)^{-1}-(A-y)^{-1}=(z-y)(A-z)^{-1}(A-y)^{-1}
:

(2.10)

\displaystyle\left\|\left[(A+\beta B-z)^{-1}-P(\underline{A}-z)^{-1}P\right]\psi\right\|^{2}

(2.11)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle\psi,\frac{1}{\overline{z}-z}\left[(A+\beta B-\overline{z})^{-1}-(A+\beta B-z)^{-1}\right]\psi\rangle

(2.12)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\langle\psi,\frac{1}{\overline{z}-z}P\left[(\underline{A}-\overline{z})^{-1}-(\underline{A}-z)^{-1}\right]P\psi\rangle

(2.13)

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\langle(A+\beta B-z)^{-1}\psi,P(\underline{A}-z)^{-1}P\psi\rangle-\langle P(\underline{A}-z)^{-1}P\psi,(A+\beta B-z)^{-1}\psi\rangle.

This converges to zero as terms cancel each other in the limit.
∎

In fact, it suffices to assume that the compression of
A
onto
\ker(B)
is only
essentially
self-adjoint on a dense subset of
\ker(B)\cap\mathscr{D}(A)
, as our next corollary shows:

Corollary 2.2
.

In the setting of Theorem
2.1
, assume that
B
is bounded,
A
is closed and that
there only is a dense domain
\mathscr{D}\subseteq P\mathscr{H}\cap\mathscr{D}(A)\rightarrow P\mathscr{H}
such that
(PA,\mathscr{D})
is essentially self adjoint. Setting
\underline{A}=\overline{PA}
the claim of the previous Thoerem still holds.

Proof.

To establish
Q(A+\beta B-z)^{-1}\rightarrow 0
weakly, we note that since
\text{Ran}\{(A-\overline{z})^{-1}\}
is dense,
we may find
\zeta\in\mathscr{H}
so that

\left\|(A-\overline{z})^{-1}\zeta-\xi\right\|\leq\epsilon\cdot|\text{Im}(z)|/(\|\psi\|\cdot\|B\|).

Thus:

(2.15)

\displaystyle\left|\langle\eta,(A+\beta B-z)^{-1}\psi\rangle\right|=\left|\langle\xi,B(A+\beta B-z)^{-1}\psi\rangle\right|

(2.16)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left|\langle\xi-(A-\overline{z})^{-1}\zeta,B(A+\beta B-z)^{-1}\psi\rangle\right|+\left|\langle(A-\overline{z})^{-1}\zeta,B(A+\beta B-z)^{-1}\psi\rangle\right|

(2.17)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\epsilon+\left|\langle(A-\overline{z})^{-1}\zeta,B(A+\beta B-z)^{-1}\psi\rangle\right|

(2.18)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\epsilon+\left|\langle\zeta,(A-z)^{-1}B(A+\beta B-z)^{-1}\psi\rangle\right|

(2.19)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\epsilon+\frac{1}{\beta}\left|\langle\zeta,\left[(A-z)^{-1}-(A+\beta B-z)^{-1}\right]\psi\rangle\right|\leq 2\epsilon,

where the last inequality holds for
\beta
sufficiently large.
To establish the weak convergence
(A+\lambda B-z)^{-1}\rightarrow P(\underline{A}-z)^{-1}P
,
we note we assume that
PA:\mathscr{D}\rightarrow P\mathscr{H}
is essentially self adjoint, we know that
\text{Ran}(PA-z)=P\mathscr{H}
. In other words we have the dense inclusion
\text{Ran}(P(A-z))\subseteq P\mathscr{H}
. Let
\phi\in\mathscr{D}
and set
\psi=P(A-z)\phi
. Then

(\underline{A}-z)^{-1}\psi=(\underline{A}-z)^{-1}P(A-z)\phi=\phi.

We also find for any
\eta\in\mathscr{H}
that

(2.20)

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{\lambda\rightarrow\infty}\langle\eta,(A+\lambda B-z)^{-1}(P+Q)(A-z)\phi\rangle=\lim\limits_{\lambda\rightarrow\infty}\langle\eta,(A+\lambda B-z)^{-1}(A-z)\phi\rangle

(2.21)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{\lambda\rightarrow\infty}\langle\eta,(A+\lambda B-z)^{-1}(A+\lambda B-z)\phi\rangle=\langle\eta,\phi\rangle.

Thus we have found for
\eta\in\mathscr{H}
arbitrary and
\psi
in the dense set
(PA-z)\mathscr{D}\subseteq\mathscr{H}
that

\lim_{\lambda\rightarrow\infty}\langle\eta,(A+\lambda B-z)^{-1}\psi\rangle=\langle\eta,P(\underline{A}-z)^{-1}P\psi\rangle
.
Let us now extend this result to arbitrary
\tilde{\psi}\in P\mathscr{H}
. With

\psi\in\text{Ran}P(A-z)
such that
\|\tilde{\psi}-\psi\|\leq|\text{Im}(z)|\epsilon/3
:

(2.22)

\displaystyle\left|\langle\eta,(A+\beta B-z)^{-1}\tilde{\psi}-\langle\eta,P(\underline{A}-z)^{-1}P\tilde{\psi}\rangle\right|

(2.23)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left|\langle\eta,(A+\beta B-z)^{-1}[\tilde{\psi}-\psi]\rangle\right|+\left|\langle\eta,(\underline{A}-z)^{-1}[\tilde{\psi}-\psi]\rangle\right|

(2.24)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\left|\langle\eta,\left[(A+\beta B-z)^{-1}-P(\underline{A}-z)^{-1}P\right]\psi\rangle\right|

(2.25)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{2}{3}\epsilon+\left|\langle\eta,\left[(A+\beta B-z)^{-1}-P(\underline{A}-z)^{-1}P\right]\psi\rangle\right|

For sufficiently large
\beta
, this can in total thus be bounded by
\epsilon
.
In total we have thus proved the weak convergence

(A+\beta B-z)^{-1}\rightarrow P(\underline{A}-z)^{-1}P
.
From here on, we may follow the proof of the main theorem again.
∎

As an initial example, we consider a self adjoint operator
A
perturbed by a large but finite-dimensional perturbation
B
:

Example 2.3
.

Let
(A,\mathscr{D}(A))
be an arbitrary selfadjoint operator and let
B
be a symmetric operator with finite dimensional range. Then by Stenger’s Lemma
[
49
]
the compression of
A
onto
\ker{B}
is self-adjoint. Since
B
is bounded, Theorem
2.1
guarantees generalized strong resolvent convergence of the family
A_{\beta}=A+\beta B
towards the compression
\underline{A}
for any
z\in\mathds{C}\setminus\mathds{R}
. Importantly, the range of
B
need not lie inside the domain of
A
for the result to hold.

As a ”real world example” about which Theorem
2.1
allows to draw conclusions, we consider a large coupling limit inside the weak sector of the standard model of particle physics:

Example 2.4
.

In the Standard Model of particle physics, the weak interaction is mediated by an
\mathrm{SU}(2)
gauge field

[
45
,
26
]
. This gauge interaction is responsible for processes such as beta decay and neutrino scattering
[
13
,
48
]
. In this example, we consider this weak-sector structure in a classical gauge background, and investigate what happens in the limit where the coupling constant
\beta
mediating the strength of the interaction between Fermions and the gauge fields is taken to infinity.
To focus on the underlying conceptual behaviour and reduce superfluous complexity, we here consider the setting of
1+1
-dimensional Minkowski space.
1
1

1

Appendix
A.2
contains the analogous but technically slightly more involved discussion of the
1+3
-dimensional real world setting.
In this setting, the spinor and gauge structure remain nontrivial, but the technical complexity is reduced.

Mathematically speaking, we here hence consider a system of spinor fields on Minkowski space
\mathbb{R}^{1+1}
, interacting with a fixed, classical
\mathrm{SU}(2)
gauge field. The unknown is a field
\Psi:\mathbb{R}^{1+1}\to\mathbb{C}^{4}
, which decomposes as a doublet of Dirac spinors:

(2.27)

\Psi=\begin{pmatrix}\psi^{(1)}\ \psi^{(2)}\end{pmatrix}^{\top},\quad\psi^{(a)}=\begin{pmatrix}\psi_{L}^{(a)}\ \psi_{R}^{(a)}\end{pmatrix}^{\top},\quad a=1,2,

where each
\psi_{L}^{(a)}
and
\psi_{R}^{(a)}
is a complex-valued function of spacetime. These represent the two so called ’chiral’ components (
L
eft and
R
ight) of a two-component Dirac spinor in
1+1
dimensions
[
26
]
.
The gauge field
W
maps from spacetime into the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{su}(2)
.
This is a three dimensional Lie algebra, whose generators
\{\tau^{1},\tau^{2},\tau^{3}\}
may simply be taken to be given by the Pauli matrices
\{\sigma_{x},\sigma_{y},\sigma_{z}\}
:

(2.28)

\displaystyle\sigma_{x}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix},\quad.\sigma_{y}=\begin{pmatrix}0&-i\\
i&0\end{pmatrix},\quad\sigma_{z}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&-1\end{pmatrix}.

Each gauge field
W_{\mu}:\mathds{R}^{1+1}\rightarrow\mathfrak{su}(2)
may thus be written as
W_{\mu}=W_{\mu}^{b}\frac{\tau^{b}}{2}
, with the functions
W_{\mu}^{c}
representing the components of the fixed background gauge field
W_{\mu}
.
Crucially, in the weak sector of the standard model, the gauge coupling acts only on the left-chiral components of the spinor field
[
45
]
. The corresponding Hamiltonian (derived in Appendix
A.1
for the completeness) then takes the following :

(2.29)

\displaystyle i\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\begin{pmatrix}\psi_{L}\\
\psi_{R}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}-i\partial_{x}\mathbb{I}_{2}+g(W_{0}^{c}-W_{1}^{c})\frac{\tau^{c}}{2}&m\mathbb{I}_{2}\\
m\mathbb{I}_{2}&i\partial_{x}\mathbb{I}_{2}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\psi_{L}\\
\psi_{R}\end{pmatrix}.

To illustrate Theorem
2.1
, we now take each
W^{c}_{\mu}\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})\cap L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
to be a smooth, bounded function of the spatial coordinate
x\in\mathbb{R}
. Then it is easy to check that for any value of the coupling constant
\beta
, the Hamiltonian
H_{\beta}
is self-adjoint on the dense domain
\mathbb{C}^{4}\otimes H^{1}(\mathbb{R})\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{4}\otimes L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
.
For simplicity in presentation (similar results hold in the general case), let us assume that all gauge fields vanish except for
W_{0}^{3}
; i.e.
W_{\mu}^{a}=0
unless
\mu=0
and
a=3
. Then we may write the
\beta
-dependent Hamiltonian
H_{\beta}
as a sum

H_{\beta}=A+\beta B

where the unperturbed and interacting parts are given by

(2.30)

\displaystyle A=\begin{pmatrix}-i\partial_{x}\mathbb{I}_{2}&m\mathbb{I}_{2}\\
m\mathbb{I}_{2}&i\partial_{x}\mathbb{I}_{2}\end{pmatrix},\qquad B=\frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}W_{0}^{3}(x)\otimes\tau^{3}&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}=\frac{W_{0}^{3}(x)}{2}\begin{pmatrix}I_{2}\otimes\tau^{3}&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}.

Assuming that
W_{0}^{3}(x)\neq 0
except on a set of measure zero, the kernel of
B
consists precisely of the right-handed spinors
\psi_{R}\in\mathbb{C}^{2}\otimes L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
. Compressing
A
onto the space of right-handed spinors,

\mathscr{V}\equiv 0\oplus\mathbb{C}^{2}\otimes L^{2}(\mathbb{R})\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{4}\otimes L^{2}(\mathbb{R}),

yields the compressed operator

\underline{A}=i\partial_{x}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{2},

which is self-adjoint on the domain
\mathbb{C}^{2}\otimes H^{1}(\mathbb{R})
.
Thus,
Theorem
2.1
establishes strong resolvent convergence of
A_{\beta}
to the effective Hamiltonian

(2.31)

\underline{A}=i\partial_{x}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{2}.

This limiting Hamiltonian describes a
decoupled
model of two massless right-handed Weyl spinors, each governed by a free transport-type evolution equation: As the coupling constant is taken to infinity (
\beta\to\infty
), the left-handed degrees of freedom are projected out, and only the right-handed sector remains dynamically relevant.

Crucially, we note that the spectrum of
B
generically contains both positive and negative values: If we for simplicity e.g. take
W_{0}^{3}\equiv 1
, then simply
\sigma(B)=\{\pm\tfrac{1}{2}\}
. Likewise, the spectrum of
A
also contains both positive and negative components. In fact, it is readily verified that

\sigma(A)=(-\infty,-m]\cup[m,\infty)

[
52
]
.
Thus, methods reliant on definiteness of either
A
or
B
are indeed not applicable here.

3.
Norm Resolvent convergence when
A+\beta B
is closed

In the previous section, we established weak resolvent convergence from first principles and then used the fact that for self-adjoint operators the notions of weak- and strong resolvent convergence coincide.
In this section, we now want to establish (conditions for) the stronger notion of
norm
resolvent convergence. Since self-adjointness does not aid beyond establishing
strong
resolvent convergence, we drop this assumption and generically consider operators
A
,
B
that are not necessarily self adjoint.

In Section
2
, the limit operator was naturally
defined as a dense operator on the Hilbert space
\ker(B)\subseteq\mathscr{H}
.
While it is reasonable to expect this property to persist beyond the self-adjoint setting, some care has to be taken in determining when exactly convergence can be expected: As an example
to illustrate what can go wrong in the non-self-adjoint setting consider the case of
A_{\beta}=A+\beta B
, with

(3.1)

A=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix},\quad B=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}.

For any
\beta\in\mathbb{R}
, the operator
A_{\beta}-zI=[A+\beta B]-zI
is invertible for all
z\in\mathds{C}\setminus\{1\}
, with the corresponding resolvent given as

(3.2)

\displaystyle(A_{\beta}-zI)^{-1}=\frac{1}{(1-z)^{2}}\begin{pmatrix}1-z&-\beta\\
0&1-z\end{pmatrix}.

In the self-adjoint setting,
the resolvents of
A_{\beta}
were bounded as
\|(A+\beta B-z)^{-1}\|\leq 1/|\text{Im}(z)|
uniformly in
\beta
.
In contrast, here
the norm of (
3.2
) grows without bound as
\beta\to\infty
. Thus the resolvent family
(A+\beta B-zI)^{-1}
in particular may never converge.
Crucially, this pathology stems from the fact that the perturbation
\beta B
in (
3.2
) is nilpotent. Indeed, clearly
B^{2}=0
holds true in our example.

To allow for convergence instead, we will have to impose that the nilpotent part of
B
at zero vanishes.
To formalize this also for potentially unbounded non-self-adjoint operators, recall the Riesz projection
P_{\{\lambda\}}
associated to an isolated spectral point
\lambda\in\sigma(B)
of a closed, densely defined operator
(B,\mathscr{D}(B))
:

(3.3)

\displaystyle P_{\{\lambda\}}:=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_{\Gamma}(B-zI)^{-1}\,dz.

Here
\Gamma
is a positively oriented, closed contour in the resolvent set
\rho(B)
encircling only the point
\lambda
. The projection
P_{\{\lambda\}}
and its complement
Q_{\{\lambda\}}:=I-P_{\{\lambda\}}
satisfy
P_{\{\lambda\}}Q_{\{\lambda\}}=Q_{\{\lambda\}}P_{\{\lambda\}}=0
and commute with the resolvent of
B

[
30
]
. Moreover, since
P_{\{\lambda\}}
arises via a Bochner integral over an analytic family of bounded operators, its range lies in the domain of
B
(endowed with the graph norm), Furthermore, since
B
commutes with its resolvent, it also commutes with
P_{\{\lambda\}}
on
\mathscr{D}(B)
.

We next note that for
P=P_{\{0\}}
the projection onto the isolated point
0\in\sigma(B)
, the operator
PB
satisfies
\sigma(PB)=\{0\}
. This – in turn – is exactly the definition of a quasi-nilpotent operator
[
30
,
53
]
, as its spectrum reduces to
\sigma(PB)=\{0\}
. This motivates the following definition:

Definition 3.1
.

We say the
quasi-nilpotent part
of
B
at zero vanishes if

P_{\{0\}}B=0.

As will become clear e.g. from the proof of Theorem
3.3
below, the key to preventing pathological growth of the resolvent lies in ensuring the convergence

(3.4)

-z(\beta B-zI)^{-1}\longrightarrow P_{0},

where
(\beta B-zI)^{-1}
denotes the resolvent of the scaled operator
\beta B
. The conditions imposed on
B
precisely ensure this convergence holds:

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
B:\mathscr{D}(B)\rightarrow\mathscr{H}
be closed with isolated eigenvalue
0\in\sigma(B)
for which the quasi-nilpotent part of
B
vanishes. Then
\exists C\geq 0
so that for any
z\neq 0
we have for sufficienly large
\beta\gg 1
that
\beta B-z
is boundedly invertible. Furthermore we have the following convergence towards the Riesz projector
P=P_{\{0\}}
:

(3.5)

\displaystyle\left\|(\beta B-z)^{-1}-P/(-z)\right\|\leq C/\beta\longrightarrow 0.

If
BP\neq 0
, we have
\|(\beta B-z)^{-1}\|\rightarrow\infty
instead.

Proof.

First, by assumption, since
0\in\sigma(B)
is isolated,
there exists some
\epsilon>0
such that the punctured disk

\mathbb{D}_{\epsilon}\setminus\{0\}=\{\lambda\in\mathbb{C}:0<|\lambda|<\epsilon\}

lies entirely within the resolvent set
\rho(B)
of
B
. Bounded invertibility of
\beta B-z
is equivalent to bounded invertibility of

B-z/\beta

and for
|z|\leq\beta\epsilon
,
B-z/\beta
is boundedly invertible.

To prove the second claim, we leverage the spectral decomposition induced by the Riesz projection
P
:
On its domain
\mathscr{D}(B)
, we can decompose
B
into a sum of closed operators as

B=PBP+QBQ,

where
Q=I-P
is the complementary projection.
Since
PQ=QP=0
, this decomposition induces a corresponding block decomposition of the resolvent as

(\beta B-z)^{-1}=P(\beta PBP-z)^{-1}P+Q(\beta QBQ-z)^{-1}Q.

By assumption, the quasi-nilpotent part of
B
vanishes, so
PBP=0
. Hence,

(3.6)

P(\beta PBP-z)^{-1}P=P(-z)^{-1}P=-P/z.

Therefore

-z(\beta B-z)^{-1}=P+(-z)Q(\beta QBQ-z)^{-1}Q.
To conclude the proof, it suffices to show that there exists
C\geq 0
such that

\left\|Q(\beta QBQ-z)^{-1}Q\right\|\leq C/\beta

for sufficiently large
\beta
.
To verify this estimate, note that

Q(\beta QBQ-z)^{-1}Q=\frac{1}{\beta}Q\left(QBQ-\frac{z}{\beta}\right)^{-1}Q.

Since
0
is isolated in
\sigma(B)
, and
P
projects onto the eigenspace of
0
, the operator
QBQ
has a resolvent set containing a neighborhood of
0
. In particular, there exists
\epsilon>0
such that the closed disk of radius
\epsilon
centered at
0
is contained in the resolvent set of
QBQ
.
For
|\frac{z}{\beta}|<\epsilon
we can thus – by analyticity of the resolvent – expand it in a Neumann series:

(3.7)

Q\left(QBQ-\frac{z}{\beta}\right)^{-1}Q=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{z}{\beta}\right)^{n}\left[Q\left(QBQ\right)^{-1}\right]^{n+1}Q,

Thus,

\left\|Q(\beta QBQ-z)^{-1}Q\right\|\leq\frac{1}{\beta}\left\|Q(QBQ)^{-1}\right\|\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\left|z/\beta\right|^{n}\left\|Q(QBQ)^{-1}\right\|^{n}
.
For sufficiently large
\beta
, the geometric series converges and is bounded by
2
.
Thus we find

\left\|Q(\beta QBQ-z)^{-1}Q\right\|\leq 2\left\|Q(QBQ)^{-1}\right\|/\beta.

Setting

C:=2\left\|Q(QBQ)^{-1}\right\|

completes the proof.

It remains to prove that whenever
BP\neq 0
, we have
\|(\beta B-z)^{-1}\|\rightarrow\infty
instead. We note
\|(\beta B-z)^{-1}\|\geq[\|(\beta B-z)^{-1}P\|/\|P\|=\|(\beta BP-z)^{-1}P\|/\|P\|
. Suppose
BP\neq 0
and take
\psi\in\mathscr{D}(B)
so that
\|P\psi\|=1
and
BP\psi\neq 0
. Set
\eta_{\beta,z}=(\beta BP-z)^{-1}P\psi
. Then
BP\psi=(\eta_{\beta,z}+z\psi)/\beta
. Thus we find
\|\eta_{\beta,z}\|/\beta\rightarrow\|BP\psi\|\neq 0
as
\beta\rightarrow\infty
and hence
\|\eta_{\beta,z}\|\rightarrow\infty
. Thus, we have
\|(\beta B-z)^{-1}\|\gtrsim\|(\beta BP-z)^{-1}P\|\geq\|\eta_{\beta,z}\|\rightarrow\infty
.

∎

In the non-selfadjoint setting (or more generally the non-normal setting), we have to assume that
0\in\sigma(B)
is isolated, in order to be able to define the Riesz Projector
P_{0}
: Otherwise the interior of any path
\Gamma\subseteq\rho(B)
encircling zero
would necessarily also contain other points in the spectrum of
B
.
However when determining conditions for
norm
resolvent convergence, assuming that
0\in\sigma(B)
is isolated is not a strong limitation in the self-adjoint setting: If
B
is self-adjoint,
P_{0}
can alternatively be defined via the Borel functional calculus as
P_{0}=\chi_{\{0\}}(B)
. Hence it may be calculated also if
0\in\sigma(B)
is isolated. As it turns out, if
0\in\sigma(B)
is not isolated, we however do not even have
norm
convergence in the simplest setting:

Proposition 3.1
.

If
\beta B
is self-adjoint and
0\in\sigma(B)
is not isolated, we do not have
norm
convergence of the resolvent:

\left\|-z(\beta B-z)^{-1}-P_{0}\right\|\nrightarrow 0.

Proof.

This straightforward result follows directly from the spectral theorem and the fact that
-z/(\beta\lambda-z)\rightarrow\chi_{\{0\}}(\lambda)
pointwise, but not in
L^{\infty}
.
∎

Hence if
0\in\sigma(B)
is not isolated, we could not even establish
norm
-resolvent convergence of the family
A_{\beta}:=0+\beta B
towards the zero operator on
\ker(B)
(whose resolvent is clearly given as
(0-z)^{-1}=-P_{0}/z
on
\ker(B)=P\mathscr{H}
).

3.1.
Norm Resolvent Convergence when
A
is bounded relative to
B

We begin by discussing the setting of relative boundedness of
A
relative to
B
, for which we find the following:

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
B:\mathscr{D}(B)\rightarrow\mathscr{H}
be closed and let
A
be relatively bounded with respect to
B
. Suppose that zero is an isolated spectral point of
B
at which the nilpotent part vanishes. Denote by
P
the Riesz projection of
B
onto zero. Then
AP\in\mathscr{B}(\mathscr{H})
and for any
z\in\mathbb{C}
with
|z|>\|AP\|,\|PAP\|

we have
z\notin\sigma(A+\beta B)
for all sufficiently large
\beta
. Furtheremore there exists a constant
C\geq 0
such that

(3.8)

\displaystyle\left\|(A+\beta B-zI)^{-1}-P(PAP-zI)^{-1}P\right\|\leq C/\beta\xrightarrow[\beta\to\infty]{}0.

Consequently, the family
A_{\beta}:=A+\beta B
converges in the generalized norm resolvent sense to the operator
\underline{A}:=PAP
.

Proof of Theorem
3.3
.

The relative boundedness condition
3.2
ensures that
A(\beta B-z)^{-1}
is a bounded operator. Since
AP=-zA(\beta B-z)^{-1}P
, also
AP
is bounded.
Thus for any
z
with
|z|>\|AP\|
, we have that
AP-z
is invertible by a Neumann series argument. Similarly for
|z|>\|PAP\|
and we find

(3.9)

\displaystyle P(AP-z)^{-1}=P\frac{1}{z}\sum\limits_{k=0}^{\infty}\left[\frac{AP}{z}\right]^{k}=P\frac{1}{z}\sum\limits_{k=0}^{\infty}\left[\frac{PAP}{z}\right]^{k}=P(PAP-z)^{-1}.

From this it is also clear that
P(AP-z)^{-1}Q=0
.
Our goal is now to approximate
P(AP-z)^{-1}
with
[-z(\beta B-z)^{-1}](A[-z(\beta B-z)^{-1}]-z)^{-1}
. To this end, we first note

(3.10)

\displaystyle\|P(AP-z)-(AP-z)^{-1}(-z)(\beta B-z)^{-1}\|\leq\|(AP-z)^{-1}\|\frac{C|z|}{\beta}\leq\frac{1}{\beta}\frac{C|z|}{|z|-\|AP\|}.

Next we note that for
\beta\gg 1
sufficiently large, we have that
-zA(\beta B-z)^{-1}-z=AP-z+A[-z(\beta B-z)^{-1}-P]
is boundedly invertible. Indeed, by relative boundedness:

(3.11)

\displaystyle\|A[-z(\beta B-z)^{-1}-P]\|\leq a\|B[-z(\beta B-z)^{-1}-P]\|+b\|[-z(\beta B-z)^{-1}-P]\|.

The second term on the right hand side may be estimated using Lemma
3.2
.
For the first term, we note that:

(3.12)

\displaystyle\|B[-z(\beta B-z)^{-1}-P]\|=\|QBQ(\beta QBQ-zQ)^{-1}Q\|

(3.13)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\|Q(Id-z[QBQ]^{-1}/\beta)^{-1}Q\|/\beta\leq\frac{1}{\beta}\frac{|z|}{1-|z|\cdot\|[QBQ]^{-1}\|/\beta}\leq\frac{2|z|\|Q\|^{2}}{\beta}.

Thus in total
\|A[-z(\beta B-z)^{-1}-P]\|\lesssim 1/\beta
. A Neumann argument then establishes invertibility of
-zA(\beta B-z)^{-1}-z
:

(3.14)

\displaystyle(-zA(\beta B-z)^{-1}-z)^{-1}=(AP-z)^{-1}\sum\limits_{k=0}^{\infty}[-(AP-z)^{-1}A[-z(\beta B-z)^{-1}-P]]^{k}

Hence for
\beta\gg 1
sufficiently large,
-zA(\beta B-z)^{-1}(-zA(\beta B-z)^{-1}-z)^{-1}
is well defined and

[A+\beta B-z]\cdot[-z(\beta B-z)^{-1}(-zA(\beta B-z)^{-1}-z)^{-1}]=Id_{\mathscr{H}}
.
Thus
z
is indeed in the resolvent set of
A+\beta B
. Furthermore we may estimate:

(3.15)

\displaystyle\left\|(A+\beta B-zI)^{-1}-P(AP-zI)^{-1}P\right\|

(3.16)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\|[-z(\beta B-z)^{-1}(-zA(\beta B-z)^{-1}-z)^{-1}]-P(AP-zI)^{-1}\|

(3.17)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\|[-z(\beta B-z)^{-1}(-zA(\beta B-z)^{-1}-z)^{-1}]-[-z(\beta B-z)^{-1}(AP-zI)^{-1}]\|

(3.18)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\|[-z(\beta B-z)^{-1}(AP-zI)^{-1}-P(AP-zI)^{-1}\|

(3.19)

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle 1/\beta.

Thus we have also proved
3.8
and hence established generalized norm resolvent convergence.
∎

In the self adjoint setting, the spectral projection
P=\chi_{\{0\}}(B)
onto
\ker(B)
is orthogonal, so
\underline{A}
is completely determined by
A
and
\ker(B)\subseteq\mathscr{H}
.
If
B
is not self-adjoint however, the Riesz projector (
3.3
) is generically not orthogonal.
Crucially, the operator
\underline{A}
then not only depends on
A
and the space
\ker(B)
as a set, but also the Riesz projector
P=P_{\{0\}}
itself:

Example 3.4
.

Let us consider a Graph
G
on a vertex set
V=\{1,2,3\}
consisting of three nodes, with directed edges
E=\{(1,2),(2,1),(2,3),(3,2)\}
. Consider now edge weights
a_{12}=a_{21}=a
and
b_{2,3}\neq b_{3,2}
. After scaling up the edge weights of edges
(2,3),(3,2)
by a factor of
\beta\gg 1
, the corresponding (in-)degree Graph Laplacian is given as

(3.20)

L_{\beta}=\begin{pmatrix}a&-a&0\\
-a&a&0\\
0&0&0\end{pmatrix}+\beta\begin{pmatrix}0&0&0\\
0&b_{23}&-b_{23}\\
0&-b_{32}&b_{32}\end{pmatrix}.

It is not hard to see that in this setting

(3.21)

\displaystyle P=(1,0,0)^{\intercal}(1,0,0)+\frac{1}{1+\frac{b_{23}}{b_{32}}}(0,1,1)^{\intercal}(0,1,b_{23}/b_{32})

Identifying
\ker(B)=P\mathds{R}^{3}\cong\mathds{R}^{2}
via
(1,0,0)^{\intercal}\mapsto(1,0)^{\intercal}
and
(0,1,1)^{\intercal}\mapsto(0,1)^{\intercal}
we find

(3.22)

\displaystyle\underline{L_{0}}=\begin{pmatrix}a&-a\\
-\frac{a}{1+\frac{b_{23}}{b_{33}}}&\frac{a}{1+\frac{b_{23}}{b_{33}}}\\
\end{pmatrix},

which clearly depends not only on the kernel
\ker(B)=\text{span}\{(1,0,0)^{\intercal},(0,1,1)^{\intercal}\}
but also on the projection
P
itself (or equivalently on the left-kernel of
B
as well).

Next let us consider a straightforward example from partial differential equations where both
A

B
are unbounded:

Example 3.5
.

Set
\mathscr{H}=L^{2}(\mathds{R}^{d})
and consider the multiplication operator
A=\frac{1}{|x|}
. By Hardy’s inequality,
A
is relatively bounded with respect to the Laplacian
-\Delta
if
d\geq 3

[

]
. Set now
f:[0,\infty)\rightarrow\{0\}\cup[1,\infty)
with
f(x)=0
if
0\leq x\leq 1
and
f(x)=x
if
x>1
. It is not hard to prove that
B
is also bounded relative to

0\in\sigma(f(B))
is isolated with
\ker(B)=\mathscr{F}^{-1}(L^{2}(B^{d})))
; i.e. those functions whose momentum support is contained within the unit ball
B^{d}
. Thus for any
\alpha\in\mathds{C}\setminus\{0\}
we have the generalized norm resolvent convergence of
A_{\beta}=1/|x|+\beta\cdot\alpha f(-\Delta)
towards the integral operator
\underline{A}
acting on
\psi\in\mathscr{F}^{-1}(L^{2}(B^{d})))
as

(3.23)

\displaystyle[\underline{A}\psi](x)=\int_{\mathds{R}^{d}}\frac{J_{\frac{d}{2}}(x-y)}{\|x-y\|^{\frac{d}{2}}\cdot\|y\|}\psi(y)\,d^{d}y.

Here

to apply:

Example 3.6
.

Consider the discretized (massless) Dirac Hamiltonian in one spatial dimension (c.f. e.g.

\mathds{C}^{2}\otimes\ell^{2}(\mathds{Z})
.
Specifically, we are interested in a discrete approximation using a
non-symmetric
finite difference approximation to the spatial derivative: Replacing the derivative
\partial_{x}\psi(x)
by the forward difference
\partial_{x}\psi_{n}\approx\psi_{n+1}-\psi_{n},

the Hamiltonian acts on the two-component spinor wavefunction

as

(H_{0}\psi)_{n}=-i\sigma_{x}(\psi_{n+1}-\psi_{n})
,

(H_{0}\psi)_{n}=-i\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\

It is not hard to see, that the spectrum of the operator

\sigma(H_{0})=\{\pm i(e^{ik}-1):k\in[0,2\pi]\}

is a exactly the restriction of

to these sites.

3.2.
Norm Resolvent Convergence when
B
is bounded

Let us now consider the setting where
B
being bounded ensures
A+\beta B
is closed.

3.2.1.
Convergence if hermitianized anticommutators are bounded from below

We begin our discussion without any assumptions on how

A
interacts with kernel and range of
B
. Instead we note that since
\mathscr{B}(\mathscr{H})
is Banach, a sufficient condition for generalized resolvent convergence is that the family
\{(A+\beta B)^{-1}\}_{\beta}
forms a Cauchy net. As we show now, this can be ensured if the ’hermitianized’ anti-commutators
(AB^{*}+BA^{*})
and
(BA^{*}+AB^{*})
are bounded from below:

Theorem 3.7
.

Let
A:\mathscr{D}(A)\rightarrow\mathscr{H}
be a closed operator. Let
B\in\mathscr{B}(\mathscr{H})
be bounded. Assume further that
0\in\sigma(B)
is an isolated point of
\sigma(B)
at which the quasi-nilpotent part of
B
vanishes. Assume the Riesz projection
P_{0}
(associated to
B
) onto
0\in\sigma(B)
is an orthogonal projection. Let
Q_{0}=Id_{\mathscr{H}}-P_{0}
be the corresponding complementary projection. Assume
\exists\gamma\geq 0
so that for all
\phi\in\mathscr{D}(A)
and
\psi\in\mathscr{D}(A^{*})
we have

(3.25)

\displaystyle-\gamma\|Q\phi\|^{2}\leq\langle A\phi,B\phi\rangle+\langle B\phi,A\phi\rangle,\ \ -\gamma\|Q\psi\|^{2}\leq\langle A^{*}\psi,B^{*}\psi\rangle+\langle B^{*}\psi,A^{*}\psi\rangle.

Then there exists a closed operator
\underline{A}:\mathscr{D}(\underline{A})\to P\mathscr{H}
such that

(3.26)

\left\|(A+\beta B-z)^{-1}-P(\underline{A}-z)^{-1}P\right\|=\mathscr{O}\left(1/\beta\right).

If
\mathscr{D}(A)\cap P\mathscr{H}
is dense in

P\mathscr{H}
, then
\underline{A}
is densely defined on
P\mathscr{H}
and a closed extension of
PA:\mathscr{D}(A)\cap P\mathscr{H}\rightarrow P\mathscr{H}
.

Before conducting the proof, let us first separate out a discussion of the implications of the conditions in Theorem
3.7
. We begin with discussing the implications of assuming that the Riesz projector is orthogonal:

Lemma 3.8
.

If the Riesz projector
P
is orthogonal, there is a constant

|(\eta,B\eta)|\leq C\|B\eta\|^{2}

Proof.

Setting
\eta=By
shows we may set

∎

Next we discuss the implications of the assumption on lower boundedness in (

):

Lemma 3.9
.

The lower-boundedness of the forms in (
3.25
) together with the assumption
P=P^{*}
implies the exitence of
a constant

C_{z}\geq 0
so that
\|Q(B+\delta(A-z))^{-1}\|
,
\|(B+\delta(A-z))^{-1}Q\|\leq C_{z}
uniformly as
\delta\to 0
.

Proof.

We begin by establishing the existence of
C_{z}
so that
\|Q(B+\delta(A-z))^{-1}\|\leq C_{z}
. This condition is equivalent to

\left\|Q(B+\tilde{\delta}(A-z))^{-1}\psi\right\|^{2}\leq\tilde{C}\|\psi\|^{2}
,
\forall\psi\in\mathscr{H}
.
This in turn is true if

\|Q\eta\|^{2}\leq\tilde{C}\|B\eta+\tilde{\delta}(A-z)\eta\|^{2}
,
\forall\eta\in\mathscr{D}(A).

Expanding yields

(3.27)

\|Q\eta\|^{2}\leq\tilde{C}\left[\tilde{\delta}^{2}\|(A-z)\eta\|^{2}+\|B\eta\|^{2}+\tilde{\delta}\left(\langle(A-z)\eta,B\eta\rangle+\langle B\eta,(A-z)\eta\rangle\right)\right].

Since
\tilde{\delta}^{2}\|(A-z)\eta\|^{2}
is non-negative, the above inequality is certainly true, if we know that the following tightened inequality holds:

(3.28)

\|Q\eta\|^{2}\leq\tilde{C}\left[\|B\eta\|^{2}+\delta\left(\langle A\eta,B\eta\rangle+\langle B\eta,A\eta\rangle\right)-\delta\left(\langle z\eta,B\eta\rangle+\langle B\eta,z\eta\rangle\right)\right].

This – in turn – is true if the following three inequalities hold uniformly as
\delta\to 0
:

(3.29a)

\displaystyle\|Q\eta\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C_{z}}{3}\|B\eta\|^{2}

(3.29b)

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\|B\eta\|^{2}}{3}+\delta\left(\langle A\eta,B\eta\rangle+\langle B\eta,A\eta\rangle\right)

(3.29c)

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\|B\eta\|^{2}}{3}-\delta\left(\langle z\eta,B\eta\rangle+\langle B\eta,z\eta\rangle\right)

By the proof of Lemma
3.8
, we know that (
3.29a
) holds if
C_{z}\geq 3\|[QBQ]^{-1}\|^{2}
. Again by Lemma
3.8
, we know that (
3.29c
) is true for
C_{z}\geq 3|z|\|B\|^{2}\|[QBQ]^{-1}\|
. Finally, we note that with our assumption

(3.30)

\displaystyle\frac{\|B\eta\|^{2}}{3}+\delta\left(\langle A\eta,B\eta\rangle+\langle B\eta,A\eta\rangle\right)\geq\frac{\|B\eta\|^{2}}{3}-\delta\gamma\|Q\eta\|^{2}

(3.31)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\left(\frac{1}{3}-\delta\gamma\|[QBQ]^{-1}\|^{2}\right)\|B\eta\|^{2},

which is eventually non-negative uniformly in
\eta\in\mathscr{H}
and
\delta\to 0
, so that (

) holds as well. Hence we may choose
C_{z}=3\cdot\max\left\{1,|z|\|B\|^{2}\right\}\cdot\|[QBQ]^{-1}\|^{2}
.

To establish the existence of

so that

uniformly, we note
\|(B+\delta(A-z))^{-1}Q\|=\|[Q]^{*}\cdot[(B+\delta(A-z))^{-1}]^{*}\|=\|Q\cdot[(B^{*}+\delta(A^{*}-\overline{z}))^{-1}]|

From here we may proceed in complete analogy to above, using the remaining condition in (

Equipped with the above two Lemmata, we are now ready prove Theorem

Proof of Theorem
3.7
.

We begin by establishing that
\left\{(A+\beta B-z)^{-1}\right\}_{\beta\geq 0}
is a Cauchy net. Indeed, by the second resolvent formula, we have

(3.32)

\displaystyle\left\|(A+\beta B-z)^{-1}-(A+\tilde{\beta}B-z)^{-1}\right\|=|\beta-\tilde{\beta}|\cdot\left\|(A+\beta B-z)^{-1}B(A+\tilde{\beta}B-z)^{-1}\right\|

(3.33)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{|\beta-\tilde{\beta}|}{\beta\tilde{\beta}}\cdot\left\|(B+\delta(A-z))^{-1}QBQ(B+\tilde{\delta}(A-z))^{-1}\right\|,

where we have set
\delta=\beta^{-1}
,
\tilde{\delta}=\tilde{\beta}^{-1}
. By Lemma
3.9
, there is a
C\geq 0
so that

(3.34)

\displaystyle\left\|(B+\delta(A-z))^{-1}QBQ(B+\tilde{\delta}(A-z))^{-1}\right\|

(3.35)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left\|(B+\delta(A-z))^{-1}Q\right\|\cdot\|B\|\cdot\left\|Q(B+\tilde{\delta}(A-z))^{-1}\right\|\leq C^{2}\|B\|.

Hence the net
\left\{(A+\beta B-z)^{-1}\right\}_{\beta}
is indeed Cauchy, and
since
\mathscr{B}(\mathscr{H})
is Banach, there thus exists
T_{z}\in\mathscr{B}(\mathscr{H})
such that

\left\|(A+\beta B-z)^{-1}-T_{z}\right\|\to 0.
.
W.l.o.g. take now
\tilde{\beta}\geq\beta
. Then

\|(A+\beta B-z)^{-1}-(A+\tilde{\beta}B-z)^{-1}\|\leq C^{2}\|B\|/\beta.

Next we note

(3.36)

\displaystyle\left\|(A+\beta B-z)^{-1}-T_{z}\right\|\leq

\displaystyle\left\|(A+\beta B-z)^{-1}-(A+\tilde{\beta}B-z)^{-1}\right\|

(3.37)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\left\|(A+\tilde{\beta}B-z)^{-1}-T_{z}\right\|

(3.38)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\tilde{C}\|B\|/\beta,

with the last inequality holding because the second term on the previous line can be made arbitrarily small (as
\tilde{\beta}\to\infty
).
Next we note that the mapping
z\mapsto T_{z}
inherits the first resolvent formula. The first resolvent formula for
(A+\beta B-z)^{-1}
yields

(3.39)

(A+\beta B-z)^{-1}-(A+\beta B-y)^{-1}=(z-y)(A+\beta B-z)^{-1}(A+\beta B-y)^{-1}.

The claim then follows since the left side converges in norm to
T_{z}-T_{y}
, while the right side converges to
(z-y)T_{z}T_{y}
.
Indeed, for any two nets of bounded operators
A_{\beta}\to A
,

B_{\beta}\to B
,

\|A_{\beta}B_{\beta}-AB\|\leq\|A\|\|B_{\beta}-B\|+\|B_{\beta}\|\|A_{\beta}-A\|.

Next we establish, that
T_{z}:P\mathscr{H}\to P\mathscr{H}
is injective: Suppose
T_{z}P\psi=0
. Set
\eta_{\beta}:=(A+\beta B-z)^{-1}P\psi
. Then
T_{z}P\psi=\lim_{\beta\to\infty}\eta_{\beta}
.

Clearly,
P(A-z)\eta_{\beta}=P(A+\beta B-z)\eta_{\beta}=P\psi
. Since
PA\eta_{\beta}=P\psi+z\eta_{\beta}
and
\eta_{\beta}\rightarrow 0
, the sequence
\{PA\eta_{\beta}\}_{\beta}
converges to
P\psi
. However, since
\eta_{\beta}\rightarrow 0
and
PA
is closed, we have for the limit
P\psi
of
PA\eta_{\beta}
that

P\psi=0.

This establishes injectivity of
T_{z}
.

By
[
55
, Chapter VIII.4, Theorem 1]
, since
z\mapsto T_{z}
is a pseudo-resolvent with dense range on the Hilbert space
\overline{\text{Ran}(T_{z})}

and
\ker(T_{z})=\{0\}
,
T_{z}
is generated by a closed, densely defined operator
\underline{A}
on
\overline{\text{Ran}(T_{z})}
.

Suppose now
\mathscr{D}(A)\cap P\mathscr{H}
is dense in
P\mathscr{H}
. For
\phi\in\mathscr{D}(A)\cap P\mathscr{H}
,

(3.40)

(A+\beta B-z)^{-1}(A-z)\phi=(A+\beta B-z)^{-1}(A+\beta B-z)\phi=\phi,

so
\phi\in\operatorname{Ran}(T_{z})=\mathscr{D}(\underline{A})
. Hence
\underline{A}
is densely defined (
\overline{\mathscr{D}(\underline{A})}\equiv\overline{\operatorname{Ran}(T_{z})}=P\mathscr{H}
).
∎

From the proof may also understand the necessity of the condition that the Riesz projector

3.2.2.

In the previous subsection, we had to restrict ourselves to the setting where the Riesz projector is orthogonal, and the quadratic forms of (

If we know more about the behaviour of

is denely defined on

as well as closed. The closedness of

implies that

equipped with the graph norm is a Hilbert space in which

is a closed subspace. We denote the orthogonal complement of

With these preparations we are now ready to state our result:

Theorem 3.11
.

Let
B\in\mathscr{B}(\mathscr{H})
have zero as an isolated eigenvalue at which the corresponding nilpotent part vanishes. Let
P
be the Riesz projector associated to
0\in\sigma(B)
and let
Q=Id-P
be its complementary projection. Let
A:\mathscr{D}(A)\rightarrow\mathscr{H}
be a closed operator whose compression
PA:\mathscr{D}\rightarrow P\mathscr{H}
is also closed, so that there exists
z\in\rho(A)\cap\rho(PA)
. Futher assume that
QA
and

extend to bounded operators on the closure of their domains. Finally assume

Then for sufficiently large
\beta\gg 1
the operator

is boundedly invertible and we have the generalized norm resolvent convergence

(3.44)

\displaystyle\|(A+\beta B-z)^{-1}-P(PA-z)^{-1}P\|\lesssim 1/\beta\xrightarrow{\beta\rightarrow\infty}0.

Proof.

We note that we may view
A_{\beta}=A+\beta B-z
as a bounded operator between the Hilbert spaces
D\oplus D^{\intercal}\rightarrow P\mathscr{H}+Q\mathscr{H}
, with the latter sum not necessarily being orthogonal. This induced a block decomposition of
A_{\beta}-z
as

(3.45)

\displaystyle(A_{\beta}-z)=\begin{pmatrix}[PA-zP]\restriction_{\mathscr{D}}&[PA-zP]\restriction_{\mathscr{D}^{\intercal}}\\
QA\restriction_{\mathscr{D}}&[QA+\beta QB-zQ]\restriction_{\mathscr{D}^{\intercal}}\end{pmatrix}.

By assumption, the off-diagonal entries constitute bounded operators.
We now use a Schur complement approach to show that the operator in (
3.45
) is boundedly invertible. To build a candidate inverse
T
let first define
S:=[QA+\beta QB-zQ-QAPRPA]\restriction_{\mathscr{D}^{\intercal}}\in\mathscr{B}(\overline{\mathscr{D}^{\intercal}}^{\|\cdot\|_{\mathscr{H}}},Q\mathscr{H}),

and
R:=(PA\restriction_{\mathscr{D}}-zP)^{-1}\in\mathscr{B}(P\mathscr{H},\mathscr{D})
.
Since
z\notin\sigma(PAP)
by assumption, we know that
R
is well-defined. We then define the candidate inverse as:

(3.46)

\displaystyle T=\begin{bmatrix}R+RP[A\restriction_{\mathscr{D}^{\intercal}}]S^{-1}Q[A\restriction_{\mathscr{D}}]PR&-RP[A\restriction_{\mathscr{D}^{\intercal}}]S^{-1}\\
-S^{-1}Q[A\restriction_{\mathscr{D}}]R&S^{-1}\end{bmatrix}.

To show that
S:\overline{\mathscr{D}^{\intercal}}^{\|\cdot\|_{\mathscr{H}}}\rightarrow Q\mathscr{H}
has an inverse
S^{-1}\in\mathscr{B}(Q\mathscr{H},\overline{\mathscr{D}^{\intercal}}^{\|\cdot\|_{\mathscr{H}}})
, we use the assumption that
Q:\overline{\mathscr{D}^{\intercal}}^{\|\cdot\|_{\mathscr{H}}}\rightarrow Q\mathscr{H}
is boundedly invertible. Denoting this inverse by
Q^{-}\in\mathscr{B}(Q\mathscr{H},\overline{\mathscr{D}^{\intercal}}^{\|\cdot\|_{\mathscr{H}}})
, we have

(3.47)

\displaystyle S=\beta QBQ\cdot Q\cdot\left[Id_{\overline{\mathscr{D}^{\intercal}}^{\|\cdot\|_{\mathscr{H}}}}+\frac{1}{\beta}Q^{-}(QBQ)^{-1}(QA-zQ-Q[A\restriction_{\mathscr{D}}]RPA))\right].

We note that
(QBQ)^{-1}\in\mathscr{B}(Q\mathscr{H})
and the term

(QA-zQ-Q[A\restriction_{\mathscr{D}}]RPA))
extends to a bounded operator defined on all of
\overline{\mathscr{D}^{\intercal}}^{\|\cdot\|_{\mathscr{H}}}
. Thus for sufficiently large
\beta\gg 1
the term in square brackets is invertible by a Neumann series as

(3.48)

\displaystyle S^{-1}=\frac{1}{\beta}\left[\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\left[-Q^{-}(QBQ)^{-1}(QA-zQ-Q[A\restriction_{\mathscr{D}}]RPA)\right]^{k}/\beta^{k}\right]\cdot Q^{-}\cdot[QBQ]^{-1}.

Verifying that
T(A+\beta B-z)=(A+\beta B-z)T=Id_{\mathscr{H}}
boils done to block matrix multiplication; included in Appendix
C
for completeness.
From (
3.48
) we also infer
\|S^{-1}\|\lesssim 1/\beta\rightarrow 0
. The convergence in (
3.44
) is then established by noting that due to this fact, all but the top-left block in (
3.46
) are
\mathscr{O}(1/\beta)
.
∎

as

(3.53)

\displaystyle[L_{b}f](x)=\frac{1}{m(y)}\sum\limits_{x\in V}b(x,y)(f(x)-f(y)),

which after extending by zero outside of

clearly defines a bounded operator on
\ell^{2}(V)
.
Since the range of
L_{b}
is finite dimensional, its spectrum is discrete. By standard results in graph theory, the algebraic multiplicity of the eigenvalue

is equal to its geometric multiplicity

]
, so that the nilpotent part of the corresponding Riesz projector

vanishes. In fact, since we assumed that there is a node from which all other nodes in

is even an orthogonal projection, with its action given as
[Pf](x)=\chi_{G\setminus W}(x)f(x)+\langle\chi_{W},f\rangle_{\ell^{2}(V)}\chi_{W}(x)

[

]
. We now consider the setting
A+\beta B=L_{a}+\beta L_{b}
where we scale up the weights in the subgraph
W
.
We are then interested in the effective Laplacian
\underline{L_{a}}
describing the graph in the infinite weight limit.

We first note that
P\mathscr{H}
is precisely comprised of those functions

that are constant on

. We nay thus defined a new node set
\underline{V}=V\setminus W\cup\{W\}
obtained by removing all nodes of

from

and adding back only a single node

representing the cluster
W
. We may thus unitarily identify

with the Hilbert space
\ell^{2}(\underline{V})

is determined as

(3.54)

\displaystyle\underline{m}(w)\overline{\underline{f}(w)}\underline{g}(w)+\sum\limits_{x\in V\setminus W}m(x)\overline{f(x)}g(x)=:\langle\underline{f},\underline{g}\rangle_{\ell^{2}(W)}\equiv\langle Pf,Pg\rangle_{\ell^{2}(V)},

with
f,g\in\ell^{2}(V)
arbitrary functions in the pre-images
P^{-1}(f),P^{-1}(g)
. Thus we have
\underline{m}(w)=\sum_{x\in W}m(x)
and
\underline{f}(w)=\frac{1}{\underline{m}}\sum_{x\in W}f(x)
.

We will now show that the limit effective operator guaranteed to exist by Corollary
3.13

(3.55)

\displaystyle[\underline{L_{a}}\underline{f}](x)=\frac{1}{\underline{m}(x)}\sum\limits_{y\in\underline{V}}\underline{a}(x,y)(\underline{f}(x)-\underline{f}(y)),

\underline{a}(w,y)=\sum_{x\in W}a(x,y)

\underline{a}(y,w)

locally within

\underline{V}

\ell^{2}(\underline{V})

Next we note that the complementary projection

Appendix A
Chiral Dirac Equation in an
\mathrm{SU}(2)
Gauge Background

In this appendix, we provide further context and details for Example
2.4
. The derivations below are standard, as general references a number of books on particle physics or the Dirac equation may serve
[
45
,
26
,
13
,
48
,
52
]
.

In the Standard Model of particle physics, the weak interaction is mediated by an
\mathrm{SU}(2)
gauge field acting on left-handed fermion doublets. This chiral gauge interaction is responsible for processes such as beta decay and neutrino scattering. The
\mathrm{SU}(2)
symmetry acts only on the left-chiral components of fermions, organized into doublets (e.g.,
(\nu_{e},e)_{L}
), while the right-chiral components transform trivially under the gauge group. In this section, we isolate this weak-sector structure in a classical gauge background and derive the associated Dirac equation in an explicit, componentwise form suitable for mathematical analysis.

A.1.
1 + 1 Dimensions

Mathematically speaking, we here hence consider a system of spinor fields on Minkowski space
\mathbb{R}^{1+1}
, interacting with a fixed, classical
\mathrm{SU}(2)
gauge field. The unknown is a field
\Psi:\mathbb{R}^{1+1}\to\mathbb{C}^{4}
, which decomposes as a doublet of Dirac spinors:

(A.1)

\Psi=\begin{pmatrix}\psi^{(1)}\ \psi^{(2)}\end{pmatrix}^{\top},\quad\psi^{(a)}=\begin{pmatrix}\psi_{L}^{(a)}\ \psi_{R}^{(a)}\end{pmatrix}^{\top},\quad a=1,2,

where each
\psi_{L}^{(a)}
and
\psi_{R}^{(a)}
is a complex-valued function of spacetime. These represent the two so called ’chiral’ components (
L
eft and
R
ight) of a two-component Dirac spinor in
1+1
dimensions
[
26
]
.
The gauge field
W
consists of functions
W_{\mu}
(
\mu=0,1
) where each function
W_{\mu}
maps from spacetime
\mathds{R}^{1+1}
into the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{su}(2)
. This is a three dimensional Lie algebra, whose generators
\{\tau^{1},\tau^{2},\tau^{3}\}
may simply be taken to be given by the Pauli matrices
\{\sigma_{x},\sigma_{y},\sigma_{z}\}
:

(A.2)

\displaystyle\sigma_{x}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix},\quad.\sigma_{y}=\begin{pmatrix}0&-i\\
i&0\end{pmatrix},\quad\sigma_{z}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&-1\end{pmatrix}.

Each gauge field
W_{m}u:\mathds{R}^{1+1}\rightarrow\mathfrak{su}(2)
may thus be written as
W_{\mu}=W_{\mu}^{b}\frac{\tau^{b}}{2}
, with the functions
W_{\mu}^{c}
representing the components of the fixed background gauge field
W_{\mu}
.

Crucially, in the weak sector of the standard model, the gauge coupling acts only on the left-chiral components of the spinor field
[
45
]
. The corresponding covariant derivative
D_{\mu}
acting on each left-handed component is given as
[
48
]

(A.3)

\displaystyle D_{\mu}\psi_{L}^{(a)}:=\partial_{\mu}\psi_{L}^{(a)}-ig\sum_{b=1}^{2}\sum_{c=1}^{3}\left(\frac{\tau^{c}}{2}\right)_{ab}W_{\mu}^{c}\psi_{L}^{(b)},

and on the right-handed components by the ordinary derivative

(A.4)

\displaystyle D_{\mu}\psi_{R}^{(a)}:=\partial_{\mu}\psi_{R}^{(a)}.

Using the chiral (Weyl) representation of the Dirac gamma matrices in
1+1
dimensions
\gamma^{\mu}=(\gamma^{0},\gamma^{1}),\gamma^{0}=\sigma^{1},\quad\gamma^{1}=-i\sigma^{2},

where
\sigma^{i}
are the Pauli matrices,
the Dirac equation is given as

(A.5)

\displaystyle(i\gamma^{\mu}D_{\mu}-m)\Psi=0.

Unpacking this equation for each component
a=1,2
, and separating left- and right-handed parts, we obtain the two-component systems

(A.6)

\displaystyle i(\partial_{t}+\partial_{x})\psi_{R}^{(a)}

\displaystyle=m\psi_{L}^{(a)},

(A.7)

\displaystyle i(\partial_{t}-\partial_{x})\psi_{L}^{(a)}

\displaystyle=g\sum_{b=1}^{2}\sum_{c=1}^{3}\left(\frac{\tau^{c}}{2}\right)_{ab}(W_{0}^{c}-W_{1}^{c})\psi_{L}^{(b)}+m\psi_{R}^{(a)}.

This system represents an evolution equation for the pair
(\psi_{L}^{(a)},\psi_{R}^{(a)})
with non-abelian coupling on the left-handed part and mass mixing between left- and right- chiralities. The gauge field couples the left-handed components across the
\mathrm{SU}(2)
indices
a=1,2
, while the right-handed components evolve freely except for mass coupling.
Collecting the spinor doublets into 4-component vectors

(A.8)

\psi_{L}:=\begin{pmatrix}\psi_{L}^{(1)}\ \psi_{L}^{(2)}\end{pmatrix}^{\top}\quad\text{and}\quad\psi_{R}:=\begin{pmatrix}\psi_{R}^{(1)}\ \psi_{R}^{(2)}\end{pmatrix}^{\top},

the coupled system takes the matrix form

(A.9)

\displaystyle i\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\begin{pmatrix}\psi_{L}\\
\psi_{R}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}-i\partial_{x}\mathbb{I}_{2}+g(W_{0}^{c}-W_{1}^{c})\frac{\tau^{c}}{2}&m\mathbb{I}_{2}\\
m\mathbb{I}_{2}&i\partial_{x}\mathbb{I}_{2}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\psi_{L}\\
\psi_{R}\end{pmatrix}.

A.2.
1 + 3 Dimensions

We now extend the previous analysis to spinor fields on four-dimensional Minkowski space
\mathbb{R}^{1+3}
, interacting with a fixed, classical
\mathrm{SU}(2)
gauge field. As in the
1+1
case, the unknown field
\Psi:\mathbb{R}^{1+3}\to\mathbb{C}^{8}
is a doublet of Dirac spinors:

(A.10)

\Psi=\begin{pmatrix}\psi^{(1)}\\
\psi^{(2)}\end{pmatrix},\quad\psi^{(a)}=\begin{pmatrix}\psi_{L}^{(a)}\\
\psi_{R}^{(a)}\end{pmatrix},\quad a=1,2,

where
\psi_{L}^{(a)}
and
\psi_{R}^{(a)}
are now two-component Weyl spinors.

The
\mathrm{SU}(2)
gauge field is specified by
W_{\mu}=W_{\mu}^{b}\,\tau^{b}/2
, where
\tau^{1},\tau^{2},\tau^{3}
are the Pauli matrices. As before, the gauge coupling acts only on the left-chiral components, so the covariant derivative acts on left-handed components as:

(A.11)

D_{\mu}\psi_{L}^{(a)}:=\partial_{\mu}\psi_{L}^{(a)}-ig\sum_{b=1}^{2}\sum_{c=1}^{3}\left(\frac{\tau^{c}}{2}\right)_{ab}W_{\mu}^{c}\psi_{L}^{(b)},

and on right-handed components by the ordinary derivative:

(A.12)

D_{\mu}\psi_{R}^{(a)}:=\partial_{\mu}\psi_{R}^{(a)}.

We adopt the Weyl (chiral) representation for the Dirac gamma matrices in
1+3
dimensions:

(A.13)

\gamma^{\mu}=\begin{pmatrix}0&\sigma^{\mu}\\
\bar{\sigma}^{\mu}&0\end{pmatrix},\quad\text{with }\sigma^{\mu}=(I_{2},\sigma^{1},\sigma^{2},\sigma^{3}),\quad\bar{\sigma}^{\mu}=(I_{2},-\sigma^{1},-\sigma^{2},-\sigma^{3}),

where the
\sigma^{i}
are the Pauli matrices.

The Dirac equation in this chiral basis takes the form:

(A.14)

(i\gamma^{\mu}D_{\mu}-m)\Psi=0.

Expanding by components, we obtain:

(A.15)

\displaystyle i\sigma^{\mu}\partial_{\mu}\psi_{R}^{(a)}

\displaystyle=m\psi_{L}^{(a)},

(A.16)

\displaystyle i\bar{\sigma}^{\mu}\left(\partial_{\mu}\psi_{L}^{(a)}-ig\sum_{b=1}^{2}\sum_{c=1}^{3}\left(\frac{\tau^{c}}{2}\right)_{ab}W_{\mu}^{c}\psi_{L}^{(b)}\right)

\displaystyle=m\psi_{R}^{(a)}.

Rewriting in Hamiltonian (time-evolution) form:

(A.17)

\displaystyle i\partial_{t}\psi_{L}^{(a)}

\displaystyle=-i\sigma^{i}\left(\partial_{i}\delta_{ab}-ig\sum_{c=1}^{3}\left(\frac{\tau^{c}}{2}\right)_{ab}W_{i}^{c}\right)\psi_{L}^{(b)}+g\sum_{c=1}^{3}\left(\frac{\tau^{c}}{2}\right)_{ab}W_{0}^{c}\psi_{L}^{(b)}+m\psi_{R}^{(a)},

(A.18)

\displaystyle i\partial_{t}\psi_{R}^{(a)}

\displaystyle=-i\sigma^{i}\partial_{i}\psi_{R}^{(a)}+m\psi_{L}^{(a)}.

This system describes a coupled evolution of left- and right-chiral spinors, where only the left-handed components interact with the gauge field. As in the
1+1
case, the mass term introduces a chiral mixing, but the gauge field couples only the left-handed components across the
\mathrm{SU}(2)
indices
a=1,2
.

We now collect the spinor doublets into four-component vectors:

(A.19)

\psi_{L}:=\begin{pmatrix}\psi_{L}^{(1)}\\
\psi_{L}^{(2)}\end{pmatrix},\quad\psi_{R}:=\begin{pmatrix}\psi_{R}^{(1)}\\
\psi_{R}^{(2)}\end{pmatrix},

so that the coupled evolution system becomes:

(A.20)

i\partial_{t}\begin{pmatrix}\psi_{L}\\
\psi_{R}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}-i\sigma^{i}\left(\partial_{i}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{2}-ig\sum_{c=1}^{3}W_{i}^{c}\otimes\frac{\tau^{c}}{2}\right)+g\sum_{c=1}^{3}W_{0}^{c}\otimes\frac{\tau^{c}}{2}&m\mathbb{I}_{4}\\
m\mathbb{I}_{4}&-i\sigma^{i}\partial_{i}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{2}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\psi_{L}\\
\psi_{R}\end{pmatrix}

This matrix equation has the same structure as its lower-dimensional analog, but now reflects the full spatial complexity of three dimensions. The system remains first-order in both time and space, with matrix-valued coefficients arising from the fixed non-Abelian background gauge field.

Example:

As an example for the setting of Theorem
2.1
, let us consider the Hamiltonian operator that governs the time evolution of spinor fields subject to a chiral
\mathrm{SU}(2)
gauge interaction. This type of structure arises in the electroweak sector of the Standard Model of particle physics, where certain spin-
\tfrac{1}{2}
particles (such as electrons and neutrinos) are grouped into pairs called
\mathrm{SU}(2)
doublets. These doublets interact with a background gauge field through their left-handed components only. A spinor is said to be
left-handed
or
right-handed
based on how it transforms under spatial rotations and Lorentz boosts: roughly, these terms correspond to projections onto eigenspaces of the Dirac operator under a so called chirality operator,
separating the spinor into two components that evolve differently under relativistic symmetry transformations. The term “chiral coupling” refers to the fact that in the standard model only the left-handed component of each spinor field interacts with the gauge field, while the right-handed component evolves independently except for mass coupling.

In the setting we consider, the gauge field is treated as a smooth, matrix-valued, externally prescribed background—that is, a fixed coefficient field entering the differential operator, not subject to any dynamical evolution. The resulting system describes the evolution of a pair of coupled spinor fields via a Dirac-type equation with internal matrix structure. The evolution equation takes the form

(A.21)

\displaystyle i\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\begin{pmatrix}\psi_{L}\\
\psi_{R}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=H_{\beta}\begin{pmatrix}\psi_{L}\\
\psi_{R}\end{pmatrix},

with the coupling constant
\beta
dependent Hamiltonian operator

(A.22)

\displaystyle A_{\beta}=\begin{pmatrix}-i\sigma^{i}\left(\partial_{i}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{2}-i\beta\sum_{c=1}^{3}W_{i}^{c}\otimes\frac{\tau^{c}}{2}\right)+\beta\sum_{c=1}^{3}W_{0}^{c}\otimes\frac{\tau^{c}}{2}&m\mathbb{I}_{4}\\
m\mathbb{I}_{4}&-i\sigma^{i}\partial_{i}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{2}\end{pmatrix}

Here
\psi_{L}
and
\psi_{R}
are column vectors of two-component spinor fields representing the left- and right-chiral components of an
\mathrm{SU}(2)
spinor doublet. The functions
W_{\mu}^{c}
represent the components of the fixed background gauge field, and the
\tau^{c}
are the standard Pauli matrices generating the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{su}(2)
. The mass term
m
couples the left- and right-handed fields.

To ilustrate Theorem
2.1
, we now take each
W^{c}_{i}\in C^{\infty}(\mathds{R})\cap L^{\infty}(\mathds{R})
. Then it is easy to check that for any value of the coupling constant
\beta
the Hamiltonian
H_{\beta}
is self adjoint on the dense domain
\mathds{C}^{8}\otimes H^{1}(\mathds{R})^{3}\subseteq\mathds{C}^{8}\otimes L^{2}(\mathds{R})^{3}
.

For simplicity in presentation (similar results hold in the general case), let us assume that besides
W^{3}_{0}
all other gauge fields vanish (
W_{\mu}^{a}=0
). Then we may write the coupling constant dependent Hamiltonian
H_{\beta}
as
H_{\beta}=A+\beta B
, with

(A.23)

\displaystyle A=\begin{pmatrix}-i\sigma^{i}\partial_{i}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{2}&m\mathbb{I}_{4}\\
m\mathbb{I}_{4}&-i\sigma^{i}\partial_{i}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{2}\end{pmatrix}\quad\text{and}\quad B=\frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}W_{0}^{3}\otimes\sigma_{3}&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}=\frac{W_{0}^{3}}{2}\begin{pmatrix}I_{2}\otimes\sigma_{3}&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}.

Assuming
W_{0}^{3}(x)=0
holds only on a set of measure zero, the kernel of
B
consists precisely of the right handed spinors
\psi_{R}\in\mathds{C}^{4}\otimes L^{2}(\mathds{R}^{3})
. Compressing
A
onto the space of right handed spinors
\mathscr{V}\equiv 0\oplus\mathds{C}^{4}\otimes L^{2}(\mathds{R}^{3})\subseteq\mathds{C}^{8}\otimes L^{2}(\mathds{R}^{3})
yields
\underline{A}=-\sigma^{i}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{2}
,
which is clearly self adjoint on its compressed domain
\mathds{C}^{4}\otimes H^{1}(\mathds{R}^{3})
.

Thus we may apply Theorem
2.1
to establish strong resolvent convergence of
H_{\beta}
to the effective hamiltonian
\underline{A}=-i\sigma^{i}\partial_{i}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{2}
. This limit Hamiltonian describes a
decoupled
model of two right handed massless Weyl spinors. Each such spinor is respectively governed by a Dirac equation.

Bottom-left block:

(C.18)

\displaystyle T_{21}A_{11}+T_{22}A_{21}=-S^{-1}A_{21}RA_{11}+S^{-1}A_{21}=-S^{-1}A_{21}+S^{-1}A_{21}=0.

Bottom-right block:

(C.19)

\displaystyle T_{21}A_{12}+T_{22}A_{22}=-S^{-1}A_{21}RA_{12}+S^{-1}A_{22}=S^{-1}(A_{22}-A_{21}RA_{12})=S^{-1}S=I.

Let us also explicitly verify that
T
is a left-inverse:

Using the same block notation, we compute:

(C.20)

\displaystyle(A_{\beta}-z)T=\begin{bmatrix}A_{11}T_{11}+A_{12}T_{21}&A_{11}T_{12}+A_{12}T_{22}\\
A_{21}T_{11}+A_{22}T_{21}&A_{21}T_{12}+A_{22}T_{22}\end{bmatrix}.

Top-left block:

\displaystyle A_{11}T_{11}+A_{12}T_{21}

\displaystyle=A_{11}(R+RA_{12}S^{-1}A_{21}R)+A_{12}(-S^{-1}A_{21}R)

\displaystyle=A_{11}R+A_{11}RA_{12}S^{-1}A_{21}R-A_{12}S^{-1}A_{21}R

(C.21)

\displaystyle=I+A_{12}S^{-1}A_{21}R-A_{12}S^{-1}A_{21}R=I.

Top-right block:

\displaystyle A_{11}T_{12}+A_{12}T_{22}

\displaystyle=A_{11}(-RA_{12}S^{-1})+A_{12}S^{-1}

\displaystyle=-A_{11}RA_{12}S^{-1}+A_{12}S^{-1}

(C.22)

\displaystyle=-A_{12}S^{-1}+A_{12}S^{-1}=0.

Bottom-left block:

\displaystyle A_{21}T_{11}+A_{22}T_{21}

\displaystyle=A_{21}(R+RA_{12}S^{-1}A_{21}R)+A_{22}(-S^{-1}A_{21}R)

\displaystyle=A_{21}R+A_{21}RA_{12}S^{-1}A_{21}R-A_{22}S^{-1}A_{21}R

(C.23)

\displaystyle=A_{21}R\left(I+A_{12}S^{-1}A_{21}R-S^{-1}A_{22}\right).

Using again the identity

(C.24)

\displaystyle S^{-1}A_{22}=I+S^{-1}A_{21}RA_{12}\Rightarrow I+A_{12}S^{-1}A_{21}R-S^{-1}A_{22}=0,

we find the bottom-left block is zero.

Bottom-right block:

\displaystyle A_{21}T_{12}+A_{22}T_{22}

\displaystyle=A_{21}(-RA_{12}S^{-1})+A_{22}S^{-1}

(C.25)

\displaystyle=-A_{21}RA_{12}S^{-1}+A_{22}S^{-1}=SS^{-1}=I.

Hence,
(A_{\beta}-z)T=I
.
This completes the verification that
T
is both a left and right inverse of
A_{\beta}-z
, and thus,
T=(A_{\beta}-z)^{-1}
.
