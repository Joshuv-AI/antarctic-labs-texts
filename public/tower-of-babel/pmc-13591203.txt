Reversible Modulation of Glycan Recognition in C‐Type Lectins by Calcium/Lanthanide Exchange
Authors: Pablo Valverde, Paola Oquist‐Phillips, Ángeles Canales, Ana Diniz, Filipa Marcelo, Francisco Javier Alonso, Ana Arda, Jesús Jiménez‐Barbero, Francisco Javier Cañada
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractC‐type lectins are Ca2+‐dependent glycan‐binding proteins involved in immune recognition and host–pathogen interactions. Previous structural studies suggested that lanthanide ions can replace Ca2+ while preserving protein structure, raising the possibility of exploiting paramagnetic lanthanides as NMR probes for studying glycan recognition. Here, we investigated Ca2+/Ln3+ exchange in the immune lectins DC‐SIGN and MGL using T2‐filtered 19F‐NMR binding assays with monofluorinated monosaccharides, together with heteronuclear NMR analysis of 15N‐labeled proteins. Chemical shift perturbations, pseudocontact shifts, and paramagnetic relaxation enhancements showed preferential replacement of the Ca2+ ion at the glycan‐binding site, while the remaining Ca2+‐binding sites were less susceptible to exchange. Unexpectedly, substitution with La3+ or Yb3+ suppresses carbohydrate binding in both lectins, despite preserving the folded structure of the carbohydrate‐recognition domains. Glycan recognition was restored upon EDTA‐mediated removal of lanthanides in excess Ca2+, demonstrating the reversibility of the process. These findings show that lanthanides do not necessarily behave as functional Ca2+ mimetics in C‐type lectins but instead act as reversible disruptors of glycan recognition. Beyond highlighting the need to assess the functional consequences of Ca2+ substitution, this work establishes Ca2+/Ln3+ exchange as a strategy for controlled modulation of lectin activity and for developing new tools to investigate C‐type lectin‐mediated biological processes.Keywords: biochemistry, chemistry, fluorine‐19 NMR, glycan, lanthanide, lectin, monosaccharide, protein NMR spectroscopy, protein structure

Introduction

Lectins are glycan‐binding proteins that mediate a wide variety of biological recognition processes and play central roles in cell communication, immune regulation, host–pathogen interactions, and glycoprotein trafficking [1, 2, 3]. As key mediators of molecular recognition at the cell surface, lectin–glycan interactions are of major relevance in chemical biology, not only for understanding fundamental biological processes but also for the development of diagnostic tools, antiviral strategies, and glycan‐targeted therapeutics [4]. Among lectin families, C‐type lectins constitute a large group of Ca2+‐dependent receptors in which carbohydrate recognition relies on the coordinated binding of calcium ions within specialized carbohydrate‐recognition domains (CRDs) [5, 6]. Some C‐type lectins participate in immunological functions and pathogen recognition, either as membrane‐bound receptors on immune cells, as for example the cases of the Dendritic Cell‐Specific Intercellular adhesion molecule‐3‐Grabbing Nonintegrin (DC‐SIGN) or the human macrophage galactose‐type lectin (MGL) [7], or in soluble forms such as collectins [8], being mannose‐binding lectin (MBL), one of its most known receptors, a key component of the lectin pathway of complement activation [9].

Glycan recognition in these C‐type lectins occurs within the so‐called C‐type lectin‐like domain, a carbohydrate recognition domain (CRD) of ca. 130 amino acids with two/three conserved disulfide bonds and up to 4 potential Ca2+ binding sites but with diverse occupation among family members [7]. In most of the cases, the glycan binding site contains one bound Ca2+ and a highly conserved WND tripeptide sequence involved in its coordination [5]. Along with this, another tripeptide in the Ca2+ coordination sphere is present in one of two alternative conserved sequences: EPN or QPD [10]. Direct interactions between Ca2+, these conserved sequences, and specific hydroxyl group patterns on the carbohydrate ligand establish a general monosaccharide selectivity: EPN motifs are typically associated with mannose and L‐fucose binding lectins, like DC‐SIGN, whereas QPD motifs are characteristic of galactose‐binding lectins, as it is the case for MGL. Additional structural features in individual C‐type lectins further refine their ligand specificity [5, 11, 12].

Interestingly, early structural studies of C‐type lectins, particularly on rat mannose‐binding protein (MBP), explored the substitution of Ca2+ with various lanthanide cations to improve crystallographic analysis. These studies revealed a higher affinity of the protein for lanthanide ions compared to Ca2+, while also confirming that the lanthanide‐substituted proteins retained structural and functional properties comparable to the native Ca2+‐bound form [13, 14]. Such observations suggested that lanthanides could act as functional as well as structural surrogates for calcium in at least some members of this protein family.

Paramagnetic lanthanide ions have been extensively employed in biomolecular NMR because they induce pseudocontact shifts (PCS) and paramagnetic relaxation enhancements (PRE), thereby providing long‐range structural and dynamic information [15, 19]. In chemical glycobiology, lanthanide‐binding tags attached to glycans have enabled the characterization of glycan conformations and glycan–protein interactions by NMR spectroscopy [20, 25]. These approaches highlight the potential of lanthanides as valuable probes for investigating molecular recognition processes involving carbohydrates.

The possibility that lanthanides may substitute for Ca2+ in C‐type lectins is particularly attractive in this context. Those early observations on MBP that lanthanide ions could occupy Ca2+‐binding sites while preserving the overall fold of the lectin CRD raise the possibility of introducing paramagnetic centers directly into the glycan‐binding site of C‐type lectins through Ca2+/Ln3+ exchange, potentially enabling direct NMR investigation of lectin–glycan recognition. However, whether such substitution preserves the functional properties of immune C‐type lectins has not been systematically examined.

To address this question, we selected two representative immune C‐type lectins: DC‐SIGN, an EPN‐type MBL, and human MGL, a QPD‐type galactose‐binding lectin [7‍,‍ 26, 33]. Using ligand‐ and protein‐observed NMR approaches, including 19F relaxation‐filtered experiments and heteronuclear NMR spectroscopy using the 15N‐labeled lectin forms, respectively, we have investigated the structural and functional consequences of Ca2+/Ln3+ exchange in both lectins. Our results show that lanthanides preferentially replace the Ca2+ ion at the glycan‐binding site while preserving the overall fold of the CRD but unexpectedly abolish carbohydrate recognition in a reversible manner.

Results

Monosaccharide Binding by DC‐SIGN and MGL in the Presence of Lanthanum

The effect of Ca2+/La3+ exchange on glycan recognition by DC‍‐‍SIGN and MGL was investigated using ligand‐observed 19F NMR transverse relaxation (T 2) experiments with monofluorinated monosaccharides, following previously established methodologies [26, 34]. Selected fluorinated monosaccharides acting either as binders or nonbinders for each lectin were employed to monitor changes in carbohydrate recognition upon lanthanide substitution. Where indicated, apparent T 2 values were obtained from signal‐intensity decays recorded over a series of Carr–Purcell‍–‍Meiboom–Gill (CPMG) relaxation delays.

Dendritic Cell‐Specific Intercellular Adhesion Molecule‐3‐Grabbing Nonintegrin

It is well known that DC‐SIGN recognizes D‐mannosides (Man) and L‐fucosides (Fuc), as well as some of its monofluorinated monosaccharide derivatives [26].The anomeric mixture of 2‍‐‍deoxy‐2‐fluoro‐L‐fucose, 2F‐α‐Fuc and 2F‐β‐Fuc, was used as model ligands of the CRD of DC‐SIGN, while the 3‐deoxy‐2‐fluoro‐D‐mannose anomeric mixture, 3F‐α‐Man and 3F‐β‍‐‍Man, lacking the key OH group at C3 for interaction with DC‐SIGN [26, 28], was used as a negative control.

As expected, both fluorinated fucose anomers, in comparison with the 3‐F‐mannose anomers, exhibited a pronounced NMR signal decrease in the presence of the protein when the spectra were acquired under the 400 ms T 2 filter (Figure 1A,B). Sequential addition of an excess LaCl3 over protein concentration resulted in a recovery of the signals of fluorinated fucoses, indicating the loss of interaction with the CRD of DC‐SIGN (Figure 1C). These observations suggest that substitution of the native Ca2+ ion by La3+ perturbs the coordination environment required for productive carbohydrate recognition. Considering the higher affinities of some chelating agents for trivalent ions over divalent ones [35], and to evaluate whether this effect was reversible, EDTA was subsequently added to selectively chelate the lanthanide ions while maintaining excess Ca2+ in solution. Addition of EDTA restored the strong attenuation of the fluorinated fucose signals in the T 2‐filtered spectra, consistent with recovery of glycan binding to DC‐SIGN (Figure 1D). These results indicate that removal of La3+ enables reoccupation of the binding site by Ca2+, thereby restoring the native carbohydrate‐recognition state of the lectin.

Quantitative analysis of the relaxation decay curves under the different cation‐exchange conditions (Figures 2 and S1 and Table S1) confirmed the conclusions drawn from the single‐point T 2‐filtered spectra (Figure 1). In the presence of DC‐SIGN, both fluorinated fucose anomers displayed a marked decrease in apparent T 2 values, consistent with the enhanced transverse relaxation associated with lectin binding (Figures 1 and 2 and Table S1). Progressive addition of La3+ resulted in gradual recovery of the T 2 values toward those of the free ligands, indicating disruption of carbohydrate recognition upon Ca2+/La3+ exchange. Subsequent addition of EDTA, which selectively chelates La3+ in the presence of excess Ca2+, restored the decrease in T 2 values characteristic of the bound state, consistent with regeneration of the functional glycan‐binding site. In contrast, the 3F‐mannose anomers showed only minor variations in T 2 values throughout the experiment, supporting their negligible interaction with DC‐SIGN under the conditions employed.

Macrophage Galactose‐Type Lectin

The same experimental strategy was used to examine Ca2+/La3+ exchange in MGL. For these studies, 2‐deoxy‐2‐fluoro‐D‐galactose (2F‐Gal) was employed as a reference ligand, whereas the corresponding 3‐ and 4‐fluorinated Gal derivatives were used as nonbinding controls.

Upon addition of MGL to the fluorinated galactose mixture and application of a 240 ms T 2 filter, the signals corresponding to the α and β anomers of 2F‐Gal underwent a pronounced attenuation, becoming nearly undetectable relative to those of the 3F‐ and 4F‍‐‍Gal derivatives (Figure 3A,B). This behavior is consistent with the known recognition mode of MGL, in which the hydroxyl groups at positions 3 and 4 of galactose are directly involved in lectin binding, such that fluorine substitution at these positions strongly impairs interaction [34]. Analogously to DC‐SIGN, subsequent addition of 200 μM of La3+ ions to the MGL/sugars mixture led to a recovery in the relative T 2 values of 2‐F‐Gal anomers, pointing out the loss of binding to MGL upon metal exchange (Figure 3C). Addition of EDTA (1 mM) in the presence of excess Ca2+ (20 mM) restored the attenuation of the 2F‐Gal resonances, consistent with recovery of glycan binding after removal of the lanthanide ions and reoccupation of the binding site by Ca2+ (Figure 3D).

Binding of MGL selectively reduces the signals of the 2F‐Gal anomers, whereas La3+ substitution restores signal intensity, indicating loss of glycan recognition. Subsequent EDTA addition restores carbohydrate binding by removal of the lanthanide and reoccupation of the binding site by Ca2+.

Together, the ligand‐observed NMR experiments demonstrate that both DC‐SIGN and MGL undergo reversible Ca2+/Ln3+ exchange within their CRDs. This behavior is consistent with previous observations reported for rat MBP A, another member of the C‐type lectin family, in which several Ca2+ ions were shown to be replaceable by lanthanide cations, in some cases with even higher affinity than calcium itself [13, 14]. Indeed, the crystal structure of MBP containing holmium ions in place of calcium (PDB: 1MSB) represented one of the earliest structural characterizations of a C‐type lectin [9]. Interestingly, in MBP the lanthanide‐substituted protein retained carbohydrate‐binding activity. In contrast, our data indicate that, although La3+ interacts reversibly with the CRDs of DC‐SIGN and MGL, its incorporation suppresses glycan recognition.

To establish the structural basis of this functional effect and determine which calcium‐binding sites were affected, Ca2+/Ln3+ exchange was next examined from the protein perspective. For this purpose, 1H,15N‐HSQC spectra were recorded for uniformly 15N‐labeled DC‐SIGN and MGL samples upon titration with increasing concentrations of either diamagnetic La3+ or paramagnetic Yb3+ ions, taking advantage of their complementary spectroscopic properties [36].

Ca2+/Ln3+ Exchange in DC‐SIGN and MGL: NMR Analysis From the Protein Perspective

For both lectins, addition of either lanthanide ion induced clear chemical shift perturbations (CSPs), even at low stoichiometric ratios and despite the large excess of Ca2+ present in solution. Representative regions of the 1H,15N‐HSQC spectra recorded in the presence of La3+ are shown in Figure 4A,B for DC‐SIGN and MGL, respectively. The observed perturbations demonstrate that both lectins efficiently accommodate lanthanide ions within their CRDs under the experimental conditions employed.

La3+‐induced CSPs in DC‐SIGN and MGL. Selected regions of 1H and 15N‐HSQC spectra of DC‐SIGN CRD (A) and MGL CRD (B) recorded in the absence (black) and presence (red) of La3+. Spectra correspond to samples containing 3 equiv of La3+ for DC‐SIGN and 2 equiv for MGL. Representative residues displaying significant CSPs are labeled. The observed changes indicate that Ca2+/La3+ exchange alters the local environment of residues mainly placed around the glycan‐binding calcium site within the CRDs.

The signal perturbations occurred predominantly in the slow‐exchange regime on the NMR chemical shift timescale, since the different protein states arising from the Ca2+/Ln3+ equilibria appeared simultaneously as distinct sets of resonances throughout the titrations. C‐type lectin‐like domains can contain up to four Ca2+‐binding sites, conventionally numbered 1–4, although their occupancy varies among lectins [5]. Typically, site 2 is the one containing the Ca2+ ion directly involved in glycan binding. In DC‐SIGN, sites 1, 2, and 3 are the ones occupied [37], while in MGL are sites 1, 2, and 4 [30] (see 3D models in Figure 5A,B, respectively). During the initial stages of the titrations (up to 2–3 equivalents of lanthanide), resonances corresponding both to the initial Ca2+‐bound state and to a second “altered” state arising from Ca2+/Ln3+ exchange could be simultaneously observed, especially for Yb3+ (Figure S2). In DC‐SIGN, the addition of 2 equivalents of La3+ was sufficient to generate a final altered spectral fingerprint, whereas 3–5 equivalents of Yb3+ were required to completely shift the equilibrium and suppress the resonances corresponding to the original Ca2+‐bound state (Figure S2A). A similar behavior was observed for MGL: The La3+‐substituted state was rapidly populated upon addition of 1 equivalent of La3+, while up to 4 equivalents of Yb3+ were necessary to fully generate the corresponding Yb3+‐substituted state (Figure S2B).

In both lectins, saturation of the La3+‐substituted state was achieved rapidly, requiring only 1 equivalent of La3+. In contrast, Yb3+ titrations revealed the coexistence of cross‐peaks corresponding to both the original Ca2+‐bound state and the Yb3+‍‐‍substituted state, even after the addition of more than 2–3 equivalents of lanthanide. Qualitatively, these observations indicate that La3+ replaces Ca2+ more readily than Yb3+, suggesting a higher relative affinity of La3+ for the accessible lectin Ca2+‐binding sites (Table S3). Importantly, saturation of the altered states occurred well before reaching the stoichiometry expected for occupation of all three Ca2+ sites, suggesting that only a subset of the calcium‐binding sites undergoes efficient lanthanide exchange in both lectins.

To identify the sites affected by La3+ substitution, the CSPs observed in the presence of La3+ were quantified and mapped onto the three‐dimensional structures of DC‐SIGN (PDB: 1SL5 [38], Figure 5A) and MGL (PDB: 6PY1 [30], Figure 5B) as well as onto their corresponding amino acid sequences (Table S2).

Interestingly, for both lectins the most relevant perturbations were all located around the calcium site 2, which, as mentioned, harbors the calcium ion responsible for sugar coordination and biological activity [5]. For DC‐SIGN, the most shifted cross‐peaks belonged to amino acids located on the long loop and β‐strands β4 and β3, which are all regions closely involved in the 3D architecture of site 2 (Figure 5A). Some of these included acidic and polar residues like Glu347, Asn349, Glu354, Asn365, Asp366, and Asp367, which directly coordinate the metal through their respective side chain carbonyl groups [38], and also residues known to assist in sugar binding, namely Val351 and Lys368 [27, 38]. Due to the extremely large CSP and the slow exchange, some amino acid pairs, like Asp367/Asn349, were difficult to track. Even considering these ambiguities, all these CSPs fit closely with perturbations arising from metal swapping at site 2. Additionally, a few notable CSPs were also detected at residues on nearby β‐strands β2, β2′ and the small loop that connects them and establishes contacts with Ca‐sites 1 and 3. These latter CSPs might likely result from medium‐ or long‐range perturbations transmitted from the La3+ presumably replacing Ca2+ at site 2. Indeed, the presence of mostly unperturbed or barely perturbed amino acids around sites 1 and 3, including those directly involved in metal coordination, suggested that no metal replacement was taking place at these positions along the titration.

For MGL, a similar scenario was found. The largest CSPs mapped to residues surrounding Ca‐site 2, mainly within the long loop and β‐strands β2, β3, and β4 (Figure 5B). To highlight, remarkable CSP were measured for Gln267, Glu269, and Asp280, which are actively involved in the calcium coordination sphere [30], and also for Trp271, which plays a key role in GalNAc binding [30]. Moderately high CSPs were also detected around site 1, at the hinge between β‐strands β2 and β2′. These CSPs were not as strong as those observed around site 2; therefore, a Ca2+/La3+ replacement at this site was expected to be unlikely, yet a transient substitution with weaker affinity could not be completely ruled out. At one equivalent of La3+, a distinct intermediate spectral state was observed that differed from the final La3+‐saturated state reached at two or more equivalents of lanthanide (Figure S3). One possible interpretation is that La3+ initially replaces Ca2+ at site 2, consistent with the CSP distribution, whereas weaker substitution at site 1 occurs only at higher La3+ concentrations. Because the individual affinities and occupancies of the two sites are not known, however, the precise origin of this intermediate state cannot be established. Different combinations of Ca2+‐ and La3+‐occupied sites may coexist before the final state is reached. In contrast, no significant CSP was observed around Ca‐site 4, indicating that this site is largely insensitive to Ca2+/La3+ exchange under the conditions employed.

To further characterize the metal‐exchanged states, both lectins were independently titrated with paramagnetic Yb3+ ions and the resulting spectra were compared with those obtained in the presence of diamagnetic La3+. Ca2+/Yb3+ exchange induced pronounced perturbations in the 1H, 15N‐HSQC fingerprints of both CRDs. A substantial number of resonances displayed moderate to severe line broadening, and in many cases complete signal disappearance was noticed because of PRE effects associated with Yb3+ incorporation into the lectin structures (Table S2 and Figure S4). As a result, several resonances could no longer be reliably tracked during the titrations. In addition, many of the remaining observable signals exhibited clear paramagnetic PCS, particularly in the 1H dimension, relative to the corresponding La3+‐substituted reference spectra (Figures 6A and S4A for DC‍‐‍SIGN and 6B and S4B for MGL).

Remarkably, for both lectins, residues whose resonances disappeared due to strong PRE effects were localized within an approximately 14 Å radius around Ca‐site 2 (green spheres in models in Figure 6A,B), strongly supporting incorporation of Yb3+ at this position. The extent of line broadening observed for the remaining resonances, together with the magnitude and sign of the corresponding PCS, correlated well with their spatial proximity to Ca‐site 2, further supporting selective metal exchange at this glycan‐binding calcium site. In contrast, residues located farther from the binding pocket displayed only minor broadening and negligible PCS values. Importantly, resonances corresponding to residues surrounding the other calcium‐binding sites remained largely observable in both lectins, particularly around Ca‐sites 1 and 3 in DC‐SIGN and sites 1 and 4 in MGL. Altogether, the PRE and PCS distributions strongly indicated that Yb3+ preferentially replaces the calcium ion located at Ca‐site 2, directly involved in glycan recognition at the respective CRDs of DC‐SIGN and MGL.

Having established that lanthanide exchange preferentially affects the glycan‐binding calcium site, we next investigated whether the resulting lectin states retained any detectable capacity to recognize carbohydrates. For DC‐SIGN, L‐Fuc, and D‐Man [28, 39] were employed as reference ligands, whereas for MGL the trifluoroacetylated derivative of N‐acetylgalactosamine (GalNAc‐CF3) was used as a probe ligand.

For DC‐SIGN, addition of 50 equivalents of L‐Fuc to the La3+‐substituted protein (DC‐SIGN:La3+ 1:10 ratio) did not induce detectable CSPs in the 1H,15N‐HSQC spectrum, indicating complete loss of glycan recognition in the presence of lanthanum (Figure S5). An analogous result was obtained for the Yb3+‍‐‍substituted protein upon addition of D‐Man. Consistent with the ligand‐observed 19F relaxation experiments, the process proved to be reversible upon addition of EDTA. Chelation of Yb3+ restored the characteristic HSQC fingerprint of the folded CRD, including resonances previously broadened beyond detection by PRE effects (Figures 7A and S6). Moreover, comparison with the spectrum of the native lectin confirmed the presence of few, specific CSPs associated with weak Man binding, demonstrating recovery of glycan recognition after reoccupation of the binding site by Ca2+.

A similar behavior was observed for MGL. In this case, GalNAc‐CF3 was first added to confirm ligand binding, followed by addition of La3+. Incorporation of the lanthanide generated a markedly different HSQC spectrum (Figures 7B and S7), which closely resembled that of the La3+‐substituted lectin in the absence of ligand. These results demonstrate that Ca2+/La3+ exchange remains strongly favored even in the presence of any bound carbohydrate ligand and that lanthanide incorporation suppresses glycan recognition while preserving the folded structure of the CRD. Treatment with EDTA restored the original ligand‐bound spectral pattern, confirming reversible removal of La3+ and recovery of MGL function after Ca2+ reoccupation of the binding site.

Overall, these data demonstrate that Ca2+/Ln3+ exchange is strongly favored in both DC‐SIGN and MGL for both La3+ and Yb3+, even in the presence of excess Ca2+ or carbohydrate ligand. Under a simplified global 1:1 protein‐to‐lanthanide model, the spectra provide approximate affinity estimates in the low‐micromolar range for Yb3+ and potentially lower for La3+ (Table S3). These apparent affinities are substantially stronger than the reported global Ca2+ affinities of approximately 1 mM for DC‐SIGN [40] and 0.2 mM for MGL [41].

Together, these experiments establish that trivalent lanthanide ions preferentially replace the glycan‐binding Ca2+ ion while preserving the overall folded structure of the CRDs, yet abolishing carbohydrate recognition in both DC‐SIGN and MGL.

Discussion

The present work provides a detailed analysis of the consequences of Ca2+/Ln3+ exchange on glycan recognition by C‐type lectins. Using complementary ligand‐ and protein‐observed NMR approaches, we demonstrate that lanthanide substitution in the CRDs of DC‐SIGN and MGL leads to a marked loss of carbohydrate‐binding activity while preserving the overall folded structure of the proteins. CSP, PCS, and PRE analyses consistently indicate that the exchange occurs preferentially at Ca‐site 2, the calcium‐binding site directly involved in glycan recognition.

These findings contrast with earlier studies on rat MBP, in which lanthanide substitution was reported to preserve both structural integrity and carbohydrate‐binding functionality [13, 14]. In that system, lanthanides were therefore considered effective functional mimetics of Ca2+ and with around 30 times more tight affinity than Ca2+, whose dissociation constant was estimated around 1.2 mM [14].

In contrast, our data clearly show that, in DC‐SIGN and MGL, with Ca2+ affinities on the order of 1 mM for DC‐SIGN [40] and 0.2 mM for MGL [41], the lanthanides, also showing higher apparent affinity in the low mM range, behave as reversible disruptors of glycan recognition and favor cation replacement (Table S3), rather than as functional substitutes for calcium.

Lanthanides are rarely encountered in biological systems [42, 43], although their ability to replace Ca2+ in calcium‐binding proteins has been previously documented [44, 45, 46]. Their ionic radii and preferred coordination distances are similar to those of Ca2+ [47, 50], which likely explains their efficient incorporation into the lectin Ca2+‐binding sites. In agreement with this interpretation, no detectable exchange was observed for Sc3+ (Figure S8), whose significantly smaller ionic radius likely prevents productive accommodation within the CRD metal‐binding environment [49, 51]. Despite these similarities, lanthanide differences from Ca2+ in charge density, coordination preferences, and electrostatic properties [46, 50] can alter local coordination geometry and hydrogen‐bonding networks within the glycan‐binding site, thereby disrupting the precise arrangement required for productive carbohydrate recognition. The functional defect therefore appears to arise from local changes at the glycan‐binding site rather than from global protein unfolding. Because the carbohydrate directly participates in the coordination sphere of the Ca2+ ion, even subtle alterations in metal–ligand distances, coordination number, or orientation could prevent formation of the precise geometry required for productive binding. Our data support this interpretation, since glycan binding is strongly suppressed even though the CRDs remain folded, and the exchange process is reversible upon EDTA‐mediated removal of the lanthanide ions.

An additional relevant observation is that lanthanide substitution appears to occur mainly at Ca‐site 2, whereas the remaining Ca2+‐binding sites are significantly less susceptible to exchange. This selective behavior likely reflects differences in coordination geometry and local electrostatic environment among the calcium‐binding sites of the lectin CRDs. The low lanthanide stoichiometries required to reach the final altered spectral state, together with the limited perturbations observed around the other calcium sites, support the conclusion that only a subset of the occupied sites undergoes efficient exchange under the conditions employed. In fact, attempts to promote extensive Ca2+/Ln3+ replacement by exhaustive dialysis resulted in protein precipitation and aggregation, strongly suggesting that full substitution of all calcium ions is structurally unfavorable in these lectins. Thus, although lanthanides can occupy the glycan‐binding site while preserving the global CRD fold, the remaining Ca2+ ions may still be required to maintain local stability and structural integrity. This observation further argues against treating lanthanide substitution as a simple, uniform replacement of calcium throughout the domain.

The intermediate spectral state detected during the MGL La3+ titration may reflect the coexistence of different metal‐occupancy states. One possible explanation is that Ca2+ at site 2 is replaced first, followed by a weaker or less favorable exchange at site 1 as the La3+ concentration increases. However, because the individual affinities and occupancies of these sites are not known, the precise composition of this intermediate state cannot be assigned from the present data. More generally, the slow‐exchange behavior observed in the NMR spectra indicates that several discrete Ca2+‐ and Ln3+‐containing species may coexist during the exchange process.

The apparent lanthanide affinities estimated from the titration data are substantially stronger than the reported global Ca2+ affinities for DC‐SIGN and MGL. These estimates should nevertheless be interpreted cautiously. The observed behavior reflects multiple coupled equilibria involving Ca2+ binding, lanthanide exchange, carbohydrate binding, and potentially cooperative interactions among metal‐binding sites. Accordingly, the simplified affinity estimates reported here are useful for qualitative comparison but do not represent site‐specific equilibrium constants.

Previous structural analyses have shown that, although lanthanides can adopt coordination modes similar to those of Ca2+, subtle changes in coordination arrangement and hydrogen‐bonding networks are common [46]. In addition, theoretical studies suggest that lanthanide substitution is strongly favored in negatively charged binding pockets, explaining their ability to outcompete Ca2+ in many systems [50]. However, these studies do not address whether such substitution preserves the precise coordination geometry required for functional carbohydrate recognition or the implications for the direct coordination contacts between the cation and the monosaccharide functional groups [52].

This study has been performed using isolated CRDs rather than full‐length receptors. This distinction is important because DC‍‐‍SIGN and MGL function as oligomeric cell‐surface proteins, and multivalent glycan recognition can substantially enhance apparent affinity through avidity effects. The consequences of Ca2+/Ln3+ exchange in intact receptors may therefore differ quantitatively from those observed for the isolated domains. Nevertheless, the preferential occupation of the canonical glycan‐binding calcium site suggests that lanthanide substitution could also influence multivalent recognition in the full‐length proteins. Dedicated studies will be required to determine how this process affects receptor clustering, avidity, and signaling.

Conclusions

The present work illustrates that lanthanide substitution should not simply be viewed as a structural surrogate for calcium. Instead, it provides a reversible chemical switch capable of selectively modulating glycan recognition while preserving the native lectin fold. This concept establishes Ca2+/Ln3+ exchange as a versatile chemical biology strategy for interrogating and transiently controlling C‐type lectin‐mediated recognition events in increasingly complex biological settings.

Beyond its mechanistic implications, the reversibility of the process provides a potentially useful means of modulating lectin function. Lanthanide substitution abolishes glycan recognition without globally unfolding the CRD, and carbohydrate binding is restored after EDTA‐mediated removal of the lanthanide in the presence of excess Ca2+. Ca2+/Ln3+ exchange therefore acts as a reversible chemical switch that selectively controls the functional state of the lectin.

Material and Methods

Chemicals and Proteins

Compounds LaCl3, YbCl3, Tris‐d11 1 M D2O solution, D2O, CaCl2, EDTA, mannose, L‐fucose were from commercial sources (Sigma, Aldrich, Merk, Spain), and monofluorinated monosaccharides, 2‐deoxy‐2‐fluoro‍‐‍D‍‐‍galactose (2F‐Gal), 3‐deoxy‐3‐fluoro‍‐‍D‍‐‍galactose (3F‐Gal), 4‐deoxy‐4‐fluoro‐D‐galactose (4F‍‐‍Gal), 3‐deoxy‐3‐fluoro‐D‐mannose (3F‐Man), and 2‐deoxy‐2‍‐‍fluoro‐L‐fucose (2F‐L‐Fuc), were also from commercial sources (Biosynth, UK). N‐trifluoroacetyl Galactosamine was provided by Dr. Niels Reichardt (CICbiomaGUNE, Donostia/San Sebastian, Spain) by acylation with trifluoroacetic anhydride in the presence of triethylamine, following a previously described procedure [53], with an added recrystallization step in acetone. The crystalline α‐anomer was allowed to equilibrate in solution before use. The full synthesis procedure will be published elsewhere. The structure and purity of the compound were confirmed by NMR (Figure S9).

The CRD of MGL (amino acids C181−H316) protein and its 15N‐labeled form were expressed and purified as previously described [31]. Briefly, MGL was expressed in E. coli BL21(DE3) grown in LB medium and induced with 1 mM IPTG overnight at 25 °C. For the 15N‐labeled samples, cells were grown in M9 minimal medium with 15NH4Cl as the nitrogen source. Cells were lysed in 50 mM Tris buffer, pH 8.0, 150 mM NaCl. MGL was recovered from insoluble inclusion bodies that were solubilized with 2 M NH4OH, and the solution obtained was dialyzed against 10 mM Tris buffer, pH 7.5, containing 150 mM NaCl and 20 mM CaCl2. The soluble recombinant MGL was purified using affinity chromatography on an α‐lactose agarose column (Sigma–Aldrich L7634), and the protein was eluted with buffer containing 150 mM lactose. Lactose was removed by a desalting step (HiTrap Desalting, GE Healthcare) followed by dialysis against 10 mM Tris‐HCl, pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, and 20 mM CaCl2. The protein was kept in this buffer until further use.

The CRD of DC‐SIGN (amino acids H254‐A404) and its 15N‍‐‍labeled form were also expressed and purified as reported [27]. Briefly, DC‐SIGN was expressed in E. coli BL21(DE3) cells. The culture was grown overnight at 37°C in LB medium, and protein expression was then induced with IPTG and allowed to proceed for 3 h. For 15N‐labeled samples, cells were grown in M9 minimal medium with 15NH4Cl as the nitrogen source. Cells were lysed by sonication in 10 mM Tris‐HCl, pH 8, buffer. DC‐SIGN was recovered from insoluble inclusion bodies that were solubilized in 100 mM Tris‐HCl buffer (pH 8) with 6 M urea and 0.01% (v/v) β‍‐‍mercaptoethanol. The protein was refolded by successive dialysis at 4°C against decreasing concentrations of urea, down to a final buffer of 100 mM Tris‐HCl, pH 8, 100 mM NaCl, 4 mM CaCl2, and 0.01% (v/v) β‐mercaptoethanol. The soluble, folded protein was purified by mannose‐Sepharose affinity chromatography, eluted with 25 mM Tris‐HCl, 150 mM NaCl, and 10 mM EDTA, pH 8, and then by size‐exclusion chromatography on a HiLoad 16/60 Superdex 75 column in the same buffer. The fractions containing the pure monomeric CRD were washed by successive cycles of 1:10 concentration‐dilution with 25 mM Tris, 150 mM NaCl, 4 mM CaCl2, and 2 mM DTT at pH 8 to remove the EDTA, followed by an additional concentration step by ultrafiltration using 10 kDa molecular‐weight‐cutoff membrane filters (Amicon). The protein was kept in this buffer until further use.

NMR Experiments From the Ligand Perspective: 19F‐T2 NMR Relaxation Filtering

All 19F T2‐filtered NMR experiments were performed at 298 K on a Bruker AVANCE 500 MHz spectrometer equipped with a 5 mm SEF 19F/1H probe with z‐axis gradients (Z‐GRD Z8278/0016). Titration and competition experiments were carried out by sequential addition of reagents directly into the same 3 mm NMR tube, followed by mixing and equilibration prior to data acquisition at each step.

Stock solutions of the purified MGL CRD and DC‐SIGN CRD (200 μM) were prepared by buffer exchange into deuterated buffer (10 mM Tris‐d11, 75 mM NaCl, 20 mM CaCl2, pH 7.8), performed by five repetitive ultrafiltration cycles of 10:1 dilution‐concentration, with a final concentration step up to 200 µM using 10 kDa molecular‐weight‐cutoff membrane filters (Amicon, Merk Millipore). Monofluorinated monosaccharide mixtures were prepared in the same buffer at 1.1 mM concentration for each compound. For MGL experiments, mixtures contained 2F‐Gal, 3F‐Gal, and 4F‐Gal, whereas DC‐SIGN experiments employed mixtures of 3F‐Man and 2F‐L‐Fuc.

Samples were prepared by mixing 20 μL of protein stock solution with 180 μL of the corresponding monosaccharide mixture, resulting in final protein concentrations of 20 μM and approximately 1 mM of each fluorinated monosaccharide. Control samples were prepared analogously by replacing the protein solution with buffer. Spectra were acquired in 3 mm NMR tubes. LaCl3 and EDTA stock solutions were prepared in the same Tris‐d11 buffer at concentrations of 20 and 100 mM, respectively.

For MGL, La3+ was added sequentially to final concentrations of 100 and 200 μM (5 and 10 equivalents relative to protein concentration, respectively). EDTA was then added stepwise to final concentrations of 1, 2, and 3 mM, corresponding to 5, 10, and 15 equivalents relative to the highest lanthanide concentration employed. For DC‐SIGN, LaCl3 was sequentially added to final concentrations of 60, 160, 320, and 420 μM (3, 8, 16, and 21 equivalents relative to protein concentration, respectively). EDTA was subsequently added stepwise to final concentrations of 0.42, 1.26, and 2.1 mM, corresponding to 1, 3, and 5 equivalents relative to LaCl3.

19F transverse relaxation measurements were performed using a CPMG pulse sequence consisting of a 90° excitation pulse followed by a train of 180° refocusing pulses separated by an inter‐echo delay of 2τ = 2 ms. For each relaxation delay, 64 scans were recorded using a recycle delay (d1) of 5 s. Transverse relaxation behavior was monitored using Bruker parameter lists containing either 5 or 16 effective CPMG delays. For MGL experiments, effective delays of 0, 104, 160, 240, and 400 ms were employed, whereas DC‐SIGN experiments used delays of 8, 16, 32, 64, 80, 160, 240, 320, 400, 800, 1200, 1880, 2400, 3000, 3600, and 4400 ms.

NMR Experiments From the Protein Perspective: 1H,15N‐HSQC Spectroscopy

For both C‐type lectins, 1H,15N‐HSQC experiments were recorded at 310 K on a Bruker AVANCE 600 MHz spectrometer equipped with a TXI triple‐resonance cryoprobe using the standard Bruker pulse sequence hsqcetfpf3gpsi with J NH = 90 Hz. Spectra were acquired using 500 μL samples with 64 scans and 128 increments in the indirect dimension. The 15N spectral width was set to 35 ppm and centered at 120 ppm.

DC‐SIGN samples were prepared in H2O/D2O 9:1 buffer containing 20 mM Tris, 150 mM NaCl, 4 mM CaCl2, and 2 mM DTT at pH 7.6–7.8, resulting in final protein concentrations of 41 and 56 μM for the La3+ and Yb3+ titrations, respectively. MGL samples were prepared in H2O/D2O 9:1 buffer containing 10 mM Tris, 75 mM NaCl, and 11 mM CaCl2 at pH 7.4–7.6, yielding a final protein concentration of 144 μM.

HSQC‐based titrations were performed by sequential acquisition of spectra for the native Ca2+‐bound proteins followed by successive additions of either La3+ or Yb3+ ions. Lanthanide salts, carbohydrate ligands, and EDTA were added from concentrated stock solutions prepared in D2O (approximately 18 mM LaCl3 or YbCl3; 25–50 mM D‐mannose, L‐fucose, or D‐GalNAc‐CF3; and 80 mM EDTA adjusted to pD 7–8).

HSQC spectra were analyzed using the software CARA (Computed Aided Resonance Assignment, version 1.9.1.5). Previously reported assignments were employed as references for signal identification, using the deposited DC‐SIGN assignment (BMRB 27854) and the published assignment for MGL [31]. For MGL, titration spectra were recorded at 0.5, 1, 2, 4, and 8 equivalents of La3+ or Yb3+ relative to protein concentration. For DC‐SIGN, spectra were acquired at 0.5, 1, 2, 3, 5, 7, and 10 equivalents of Yb3+ and at 2, 3, 5, 7, and 10 equivalents of La3+.
