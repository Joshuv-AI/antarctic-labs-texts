Clustering of quantum correlations at low temperature
Arka Adhikari, Joscha Henheik, Marius Lemm, Tom Wessel

Abstract
Identifying conditions for the clustering of correlations in thermal states is a central problem in quantum many-body physics. In the low temperature regime, the problem is subtle, because it is intimately connected to phase transitions. Here we investigate quantum lattice Hamiltonians that are sums of on-site terms and relatively bounded perturbations. We present a direct proof that the thermal state satisfies exponential decay of correlations at low temperature, with a correlation length that stays uniformly bounded as T → 0 T\to 0 . The proof develops a quantum many-body adaptation of a probabilistic swapping trick of the first author and Cao [ AC2024 ] for lattice gauge theories.

Introduction

A central problem about thermal quantum many-body systems is to determine the spatial behaviour of their correlations, particularly their exponential decay, which is known as
“clustering”
.
As in classical statistical mechanics, clustering of correlations is connected to the absence of phase transitions, a longstanding and notoriously difficult topic in mathematical physics (see e.g.
[
ginibre1969existence
,
robinson1969proof
,
dyson1978phase
]
).
A new perspective has developed in the past 10 years with the rise of quantum information science, which has identified clustering results for correlations and related information measures as key inputs in numerous proofs of fundamental results.
These celebrated applications of clustering conditions include the efficient simulability of thermal states
[
temme2011quantum
,
molnar2015approximating
,
anshu2016concentration
,
cirac2021matrix
,
8
]
, their efficient tomography
[
rouze2024efficient
]
and sampling
[
ding2025efficient
]
, the rapid equilibration of open quantum systems
[
kuwahara2020eigenstate
,
bardet2023rapid
,
bardet2024entropy
,
kochanowski2025rapid
]
, which is connected to the efficient preparation of thermal states on quantum devices
[
11
,
kato2019quantum
,
chen2023quantum
,
rouze2025efficient
]
.
Moreover, at zero temperature, clustering is used to prove quantization of the Hall conductance
[
hastings2015quantization
,
bachmann2020many
,
kapustin2020hall
,
48
]
, and a strong form of clustering was shown to imply the area law
[
10
]
.
Structurally, at positive temperature, clustering of correlations was shown to be equivalent to the stability against local perturbations as well as its local indistinguishability (also known as
“locality of temperature”
)
[
31
,
12
]
.
The reviews
[
wolf2008area
,
32
,
alhambra2023quantum
]
contain further background and applications of these concepts.
In a nutshell, clustering results are useful in quantum information and condensed-matter theory because they allow to translate locality of the Hamiltonian into locality properties of the associated thermal state.

Due to the broad importance of clustering results, their derivation has developed into a large subfield in its own right.
Most results treat the case of zero temperature for gapped ground states
[
25
,
39
,
17
,
47
]

or the high temperature regime by cluster expansions
[
park1982cluster
,
31
,
21
,
32
,
kuwahara2020clustering
]
, or 1D systems
[
4
,
8
,
41
,
kuwahara2024clustering
]
.
By contrast, clustering at low temperatures in general dimensions is connected to the absence of phase transitions and therefore more subtle
[
46
,
messager1996low
,
borgs1996low
,
15
,
16
,
frohlich2001quantum
]
.
We describe the literature in more detail below.
While the above references focus on quantum spin systems, clustering of correlations for fermions has also been considered
[
24
,
25
,
17
,
frohlich2001quantum
,
23
]
.

In this paper, we aim to derive uniform clustering of correlations at low temperatures, i.e. with a decay rate (inverse localization length) that stays finite as
T\to\infty
, for suitable quantum spin systems.
Consider a quantum many-body Hilbert space
\bigotimes_{x\in\varLambda}\mathbb{C}^{q}
over a finite graph
\varLambda
and a quantum state
\rho
on this Hilbert space, i.e. a linear operator
\rho\geq 0
with
\Tr\lparen\rho\rparen=1
.
Its locality properties can be captured through the associated
covariance

\Cov_{\rho}(A,B):=\Tr\lparen A\,B\,\rho\rparen-\Tr\lparen A\,\rho\rparen\,\Tr\lparen B\,\rho\rparen

of two bounded observables
A
and
B
with local supports
X
and
Y\subset\varLambda
, respectively.
We say that the state
\rho
satisfies
Decay of Correlations
(DoC) if the covariance
\Cov_{\rho}(A,B)
decays
exponentially
in the distance
d\lparen X,Y\rparen
of the supports
X
and
Y
of the observables
A
and
B
.
Throughout the entire paper, all such DoC bounds are understood to hold uniformly in the number of vertices of
\varLambda
and are thus stable in the thermodynamic limit where
\varLambda\to\infty
in a suitable way.
We are interested in deriving DoC for thermal states (
“Gibbs states”
)

\rho^{\beta}=\frac{{\mathrm{e}}^{-\beta H}}{\Tr\lparen{\mathrm{e}}^{-\beta H}\rparen},

where the quantum many-body Hamiltonian
H
is a sum of local terms.
We shall focus on the regime of low temperature which means that the inverse temperature
\beta=1/T
will be a large parameter.

The traditional interest in DoC for Gibbs states arises from its intimate connection to the existence of phase transitions
[
ginibre1969existence
,
robinson1969proof
,
dyson1978phase
]
.
As in classical statistical mechanics, if exponential DoC for
\rho^{\beta}
breaks down at a certain critical
\beta_{\mathrm{c}}
, then this indicates a phase transition occurring at this inverse temperature.
This explains why proving DoC for Gibbs states is necessarily a temperature-dependent, and thus somewhat delicate, task – especially in higher dimensions.
Therefore, to prove DoC, it is necessary to identify a condition which excludes a phase transition in the temperature regime under consideration.
There are different approaches to this with the following two the most common.

In arbitrary dimensions, results are usually about sufficiently
high temperature
, i.e. for
\beta
below a certain (universal) critical inverse temperature
\beta_{*}
, no phase transitions are possible and DoC can be derived by cluster expansion techniques
[
park1982cluster
,
31
,
21
,
32
]
.

A special case arises in
one spatial dimension
, where it is
“folklore wisdom”
that phase transitions can only occur at zero temperature.
Accordingly, as already pioneered by Araki
[
4
]
in 1969, it can be shown that exponential DoC holds at any positive temperature in one-dimensional, finite-range, translation-invariant systems; see
[
8
,
41
,
30
]
for recent extensions.
While the one-dimensional case covers any positive temperature, the possibility of a phase transitions at zero temperature affects the
\beta\to\infty
behaviour of the bound.
This is expressed through the
localization length

\xi(\beta)
which features as the inverse decay rate in

\lvert\Cov_{\rho^{\beta}}(A,B)\rvert\lesssim{\mathrm{e}}^{-d\lparen X,Y\rparen/\xi(\beta)}.

The best known upper bound
\xi(\beta)\lesssim\exp\lparen c\beta\rparen
was proved recently in
[
30
]
and divergence as
\beta\to\infty
is a distinct possibility.
For example, in the classical one-dimensional Ising model with coupling strength
J>0
, the localization length of the two-point spin-spin covariance can explicitly be computed to be given by
\xi(\beta)=-1/\log(\tanh(\beta J))\sim\tfrac{1}{2}{\mathrm{e}}^{2J\beta}
as
\beta\to\infty
.
This shows that deriving a bound on the correlation length at small temperature is subtle even in one dimension.

Recall that we are after deriving uniform clustering, i.e. a uniform bound on the correlation length in any dimension.
Another idea to exclude phase transitions is to suitably
“perturb”
a stable classical phase with a non-commuting perturbation.
A stable classical phase can be implemented for example by breaking a symmetry.
Indeed, taking another hint from the classical one-dimensional Ising model, notice that its behaviour drastically changes when breaking the symmetry through a constant external field of strength
h>0
.
This breaks the two-fold ground state degeneracy present for
h=0
and leads to a correlation length that is uniformly bounded in the low temperature regime.
The idea of adding quantum perturbations and deriving DoC was first implemented for special models
[
46
,
messager1996low
]
.
A general approach was then developed around the same time by Borgs, Kotecký, and Ueltschi
[
borgs1996low
]
and Datta, Fröhlich, and Fernandez
[
15
,
16
]
through quantum versions of the machinery of Pirogov-Sinai theory.
These works obtain DoC
in dimension at least two for translation-invariant quantum perturbations of classical Hamiltonians with finitely many periodic gapped ground states.
Intermediate temperature ranges have been considered as well
[
frohlich2001quantum
]
, also by a modified Pirogov-Sinai theory.
Our model, which we discuss next, is also a quantum perturbation of a classical Hamiltonian as in
[
borgs1996low
,
15
,
16
]
, as this is a natural way to ensure absence of phase transitions in higher dimensions.
However, since we are only after proving DoC, we are able to develop a more direct and robust proof method, as we describe next.

1.1
Discussion of our main result and proof ideas

In this paper, we prove exponential DoC at all temperatures
\beta\in[0,\infty\rparen
for lattice systems on
\varLambda\Subset\mathbb{Z}^{D}
with finite-dimensional on-site Hilbert spaces.
The considered Hamiltonians are of the form

H=H^{0}+V=\sum_{x\in\varLambda}h_{x}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in\varLambda:\\
B_{R}(x)\subset\varLambda\end{subarray}}v_{x}.

Here, every
h_{x}\geq 0
acts only on the site
x\in\varLambda
and has a unique ground state separated from the rest of the spectrum by a gap of size at least one, and every
v_{x}
is a small finite range interaction supported on
B_{R}(x)
, a ball of radius
R
around site
x
.
In particular, we do not assume any sort of periodicity or translation invariance.

Additionally, we assume that every
v_{x}
is relatively form bounded with respect to the sum of all
h_{x}
in
B_{R}(x)
in the following sense: There exists a small
a\in(0,1)
such that

\lvert\langle\psi,v_{x}\,\psi\rangle\rvert\leq a\,\Big\lvert\Big\langle\psi,\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}z\in B_{R}(x)\end{subarray}}h_{z}\,\psi\Big\rangle\Big\rvert\qquad\text{for all states $\psi$}.

(1)

Roughly speaking, the form bound (
1
) ensures that the spectrum of
H
is contained in
[0,\infty\rparen
(as is the case for
H^{0}
), the ground state of
H
remains a gapped product state, and all the (non-negative) eigenvalues
\{E_{j}^{0}\}
of
H^{0}
remain
“of the same order”
when perturbed by
V
.
In fact, a simple application of the min-max-principle (or alternatively of
[
29
, Theorem 3.6 in Chapter VII]
) shows that the
j^{\mathrm{th}}
eigenvalue of
H
lies in the interval
[(1-a)\,E_{j}^{0},(1+a)\,E_{j}^{0}]
.
This ensures, in particular, that the many-body density of states (mbDoS) of
H
behaves similarly to the mbDoS of the non-interacting Hamiltonian
H^{0}
: The low- and high-energy states have a small mbDoS, while for intermediate energies the mbDoS is large.
This intuitive picture may be understood by considering (for simplicity) a non-interacting translation invariant Hamiltonian on
n
lattice sites, where each on-site Hamiltonian has simple eigenvalues
\{0,1\}
.
In this case, the eigenvalues of the total Hamiltonian are given by
k\in\{0,\dotsc,n\}
with degeneracy
\binom{n}{k}
.
Hence, the mbDoS is essentially given by a binomial distribution.

We point out that, on a heuristic level, a small mbDoS at low-energy excited states for a Hamiltonian with a (unique) gapped ground state basically ensures that its low temperature Gibbs state is fairly close to the ground state.
In fact, writing out the Gibbs state in spectral decomposition,
\sum_{j\geq 0}\Pi_{j}\,{\mathrm{e}}^{-\beta E_{j}}/Z
, with
E_{j}<E_{j+1}
and
\Pi_{j}
being associated spectral projectors, we see the following:
At low temperature,
\beta\to\infty
, the contribution from excited states,
j\geq 1
, can be small for two reasons: Either the rank of
\Pi_{j}
is small (small mbDoS), or the exponential weight
{\mathrm{e}}^{-\beta E_{j}}
is suppressed (spectral gap above
E_{0}
, high energies).
Hence, given the validity of DoC for gapped ground states
[
25
,
39
]
, our assumptions put us in a favourable position for proving DoC at low temperatures.
We stress that the above reasoning is merely an intuitive explanation of our assumptions and their relevance for DoC, and
not
an informal description of our actual proof.

As our main result, we obtain the following (see Theorem
2
):

For small enough
a>0
as in (
1
), there exists a constant
c>0
, independent of
\varLambda
and
\beta
, such that

\left\lvert\Cov_{\rho^{\beta}}(A,B)\right\rvert\leq C_{\lvert X\rvert,\lvert Y\rvert}\,\lVert A\rVert\,\lVert B\rVert\,{\mathrm{e}}^{-c\,d\lparen X,Y\rparen}

for a constant
C_{\lvert X\rvert,\lvert Y\rvert}
depending only on the size of the supports
X
and
Y
of the observables
A
and
B
.

Our proof of DoC relies on three main ingredients: In the first step, we employ an inclusion-exclusion decomposition of Gibbs states and estimate the contributions using analyticity bounds by Yarotsky
[
50
]
, which were originally applied to prove DoC for ground states in systems similar to ours.
Second, we develop a quantum many-body version of the probabilistic cluster expansion technique and swapping trick from
[
AC2024
]
in order to (algebraically) unravel cancellations in the covariance.
The remaining, uncancelled terms naturally carry ratios of partition functions of restrictions of
H
and
H^{0}
to subsystems
S\subset\varLambda
, which are finally estimated in the third step using the form bound (
1
).
These three ingredients are combined with combinatorial estimates on the terms from the probabilistic cluster expansion.

In terms of the model considered, our article stands in the broad tradition of
[
46
,
borgs1996low
,
15
,
16
]
: We consider a quantum perturbation of an on-site (
“classical”
) Hamiltonian.
However, we consider the case that the classical Hamiltonian has a unique ground state and our new proof method of DoC is completely different than those of
[
borgs1996low
,
15
,
16
]
.
In particular, it does not use any heavy machinery such as (quantum versions of) Pirogov-Sinai theory.
The reason is that our method tackles DoC directly, instead of deriving it as one of several consequences of the phase diagram that one obtains from convergence of a cluster expansion.
More precisely, we extract cancellations in the covariance by a quantum version of the swapping trick from
[
AC2024
]
.
This direct approach leads to a fully self-contained and, we believe, conceptually simple proof of exponential DoC.

Another advantage of the fact that our new method is very direct, is that it is robust in new ways:
First, our proof also works in
one spatial dimension
.
This allows us to refine the recent DoC results from quantum information theory in the low-temperature regime
[
8
,
41
,
34
]
for our class of Hamiltonians by providing the first uniform bound on the correlation length.
Second, we do not assume that the classical Hamiltonian and the quantum perturbation are
translation-invariant
, which was needed for the Pirogov-Sinai theory
[
borgs1996low
,
15
]
.
In particular, we can treat for the first time
disordered
Hamiltonians, like the disordered XXZ chain that we describe now.

Example 1
.

A paradigmatic example Hamiltonian satisfying our conditions is the XXZ model with (random) external field on some
\varLambda\Subset\mathbb{Z}^{D}
.
More precisely, let
\sigma^{i}_{x}
be the
i
-th Pauli matrix acting only on site
x\in\varLambda
, and define the
ladder operators
and the
number operator
acting on
x\in\varLambda
as
\sigma^{\pm}_{x}=\frac{1}{2}(\sigma^{1}_{x}\pm\mathrm{i}\sigma^{2}_{y})
and
\mathcal{N}_{x}=\frac{1}{2}(\mathbf{1}_{x}-\sigma^{3}_{x})
, respectively.
Then the XXZ Hamiltonian with (random) external field acting on the Hilbert space
\bigotimes_{x\in\varLambda}\mathbb{C}^{2}
is given by

H_{\mathrm{XXZ}}=H^{0}+V

with

\begin{gathered}H^{0}:=\sum_{x\in\varLambda}(1+\lambda\,\omega_{x})\,\mathcal{N}_{x}\mathrlap{\qquad\text{and}}\\
V:=\sum_{x,y\in\varLambda}J_{12}(x,y)\,(\sigma^{+}_{x}\sigma_{y}^{-}+\sigma_{x}^{-}\sigma_{y}^{+})+\sum_{x,y\in\varLambda}J_{3}(x,y)\,\mathcal{N}_{x}\,\mathcal{N}_{y},\end{gathered}

(2)

where
\{\omega_{x}\}_{x\in\varLambda}
is a collection of random variables supported on
[0,1]
and the parameter
\lambda\geq 0
modulates the strength of the randomness.
The self-adjoint coupling matrices
J_{12}
and
J_{3}
, i.e. satisfying
J_{12}(y,x)=\overline{J_{12}(x,y)}
and
J_{3}(y,x)=\overline{J_{3}(x,y)}
, are assumed to be of
finite range
.
That is, there exists
R>0
such that
J_{12}(x,y)=J_{3}(x,y)=0
whenever
d\lparen x,y\rparen\geq R
.

By construction, every summand of
H_{0}
in (
2
) satisfies
(1+\lambda\,\omega_{x})\,\mathcal{N}_{x}\geq 0
with ground state eigenvalue
0
and excited eigenvalue
1+\lambda\,\omega_{x}\geq 1
.
Moreover, if
\sup_{x,y\in\varLambda}\lvert J_{12}(x,y)\rvert
and
\sup_{x,y\in\varLambda}\lvert J_{3}(x,y)\rvert
are small enough, we find a relative form bound as in (
1
) for the interaction
V
in (
2
) in terms of
H_{0}
.
Hence, the XXZ Hamiltonian
H_{\mathrm{XXZ}}
as in (
2
) satisfies all the assumptions in our main result, and hence satisfies DoC.

2
Main result

After introducing the necessary mathematical framework in Section
2.1
, we present our main result on decay of correlations in Theorem
2
in Section
2.2
.

2.1
Mathematical setup

Consider the regular lattice
\mathbb{Z}^{D}
, for fixed
D\in\mathbb{N}
, equipped with the
\ell^{1}
-metric
d\colon\mathbb{Z}^{D}\times\mathbb{Z}^{D}\rightarrow\mathbb{N}_{0}
.
We denote arbitrary subsets as
\varLambda\subset\mathbb{Z}^{D}
(including equality) and finite subsets by
\varLambda^{\prime}\Subset\varLambda
(again including equality if
\varLambda
is finite).
The set of all finite subsets is denoted
\mathcal{P}_{0}(\varLambda)
.
The cardinality of a set
\varLambda\Subset\mathbb{Z}^{D}
is denoted by
\lvert\varLambda\rvert
.
Given any two subsets
X
,
Y\subset\mathbb{Z}^{D}
we denote by
d\lparen X,Y\rparen
their distance with respect to the metric
d
.
Likewise, we denote by
\operatorname{diam}\lparen X\rparen:=\sup_{x,y\in X}d(x,y)
the diameter of
X
.

With every site
x\in\mathbb{Z}^{D}
we associate a finite-dimensional local Hilbert space
{\mathcal{H}}_{x}:=\mathbb{C}^{q}
, with
q\geq 2
, and the corresponding space of linear operators
\mathcal{A}_{x}:=\mathcal{B}\lparen{\mathcal{H}}_{x}\rparen
.
For each
\varLambda\Subset\mathbb{Z}^{D}
we define the Hilbert space
{\mathcal{H}}_{\varLambda}:=\bigotimes_{x\in\varLambda}{\mathcal{H}}_{x}
, and denote the algebra of bounded linear operators on
{\mathcal{H}}_{\varLambda}
by
\mathcal{A}_{\varLambda}:=\mathcal{B}({\mathcal{H}}_{\varLambda})
.
Due to the tensor product structure, we have
\mathcal{A}_{\varLambda}=\bigotimes_{x\in\varLambda}\mathcal{B}({\mathcal{H}}_{x})
.
Hence, for
X\subset\varLambda\Subset\mathbb{Z}^{D}
, any
A\in\mathcal{A}_{X}
can be viewed as an element of
\mathcal{A}_{\varLambda}
by identifying
A
with
A\otimes\mathbf{1}_{\varLambda\backslash X}\in\mathcal{A}_{\varLambda}
, where
\mathbf{1}_{\varLambda\backslash X}
denotes the identity in
\mathcal{A}_{\varLambda\backslash X}
.
Using this identification, we define the algebra of local operators

\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{loc}}:=\bigcup_{\varLambda\Subset\mathbb{Z}^{D}}\mathcal{A}_{\varLambda}.

We consider Hamiltonians composed of two parts
H_{\varLambda}:=H^{0}_{\varLambda}+V_{\varLambda}
.
The first is a sum of on-site terms
h_{x}\in\mathcal{A}_{\{x\}}
, each with unique gapped ground state
\varOmega_{x}\in{\mathcal{H}}_{x}
with gap at least
1
, i.e. we have

\langle\varOmega_{x},h_{x}\,\varOmega_{x}\rangle=0\quad\quad\text{and}\quad\quad\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}\psi\in{\mathcal{H}}_{x}\\
\langle\psi,\varOmega_{x}\rangle=0\end{subarray}}\frac{\langle\psi,h_{x}\,\psi\rangle}{\langle\psi,\psi\rangle}\geq 1.

In particular,

H^{0}_{\varLambda}:=\sum_{x\in\varLambda}h_{x}

has ground state
\varOmega_{\varLambda}=\bigotimes_{x\in\varLambda}\varOmega_{x}
with gap
1
.

For the second part of the Hamiltonian, let

B_{r}(x):=\big\{\nonscript\,y\in\mathbb{Z}^{D}\nonscript\,\big|\allowbreak\nonscript\,\mathopen{}d\lparen x,y\rparen\leq r\nonscript\,\big\}

denote the ball in
\mathbb{Z}^{D}
.
We also abbreviate
B_{r}=B_{r}(0)
which will mainly be used for the size
\lvert B_{r}\rvert=\lvert B_{r}(x)\rvert\leq(2r+1)^{D}
.
Then, fix a range
R\in\mathbb{N}
and define

V_{\varLambda}:=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in\varLambda\mathpunct{\ordinarycolon}\\
B_{R}(x)\subset\varLambda\end{subarray}}\,v_{x}

as a sum of local terms
v_{x}\in\mathcal{A}_{B_{R}(x)}
.
We denote
\lVert v\rVert_{\infty}=\sup_{x\in\mathbb{Z}^{D}}\lVert v_{x}\rVert
.
Moreover, we assume that there exists
a<1
such that each
v_{x}
is relatively form bounded w.r.t.
H^{0}_{B_{R}(x)}
in the sense that

\lvert\langle\psi,v_{x}\,\psi\rangle\rvert\leq\frac{a}{\lvert B_{R}\rvert}\,\lvert\langle\psi,H^{0}_{B_{R}(x)}\,\psi\rangle\rvert\qquad\text{for all $\psi\in{\mathcal{H}}_{B_{R}(x)}$.}

(3)

Then, it immediately follows that
V_{\varLambda}
is relatively form-bounded w.r.t.
H^{0}_{\varLambda}
, namely

\lvert\langle\psi,V_{\varLambda}\,\psi\rangle\rvert\leq\frac{a}{\lvert B_{R}\rvert}\,\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in\varLambda\mathpunct{\ordinarycolon}\\
B_{R}(x)\subset\varLambda\end{subarray}}\,\lvert\langle\psi,H^{0}_{B_{R}(x)}\,\psi\rangle\rvert\leq a\,\lvert\langle\psi,H^{0}_{\varLambda}\,\psi\rangle\rvert\qquad\text{for all $\psi\in{\mathcal{H}}_{\varLambda}$.}

We point out that, at the cost of adjusting the constant
a
in the form bound, we can assume w.l.o.g. that
v_{x}\leq 0
for all
x\in\varLambda
.
In fact, denoting
\tilde{a}:=a/\lvert B_{R}\rvert
, we can write

H^{0}_{B_{R}(x)}+v_{x}=(1+\tilde{a})\,H^{0}_{B_{R}(x)}+(-\tilde{a}\,H^{0}_{B_{R}(x)}+v_{x}),

where now
(-\tilde{a}\,H^{0}_{B_{R}(x)}+v_{x})\leq 0
.
Moreover,
(1+\tilde{a})\,h_{x}
satisfies the same conditions as
h_{x}
and
(-a\,H^{0}_{B_{R}(x)}+v_{x})
satisfies the same conditions as
v_{x}
, although the form bound is now satisfied with
2\,\tilde{a}/(1+\tilde{a})
instead of
a
.
We will henceforth assume that
v_{x}\leq 0
without further mentioning this.

Finally, we denote the partition functions

Z^{0}_{\varLambda}=\Tr\big\lparen{\mathrm{e}}^{-\beta H^{0}_{\varLambda}}\big\rparen\quad\quad\text{and}\quad\quad Z_{\varLambda}=\Tr\big\lparen{\mathrm{e}}^{-\beta H_{\varLambda}}\big\rparen

(4)

and the Gibbs states

\rho^{0}_{\varLambda}=\frac{{\mathrm{e}}^{-\beta H^{0}_{\varLambda}}}{Z^{0}_{\varLambda}}\quad\quad\text{and}\quad\quad\rho_{\varLambda}=\frac{{\mathrm{e}}^{-\beta H_{\varLambda}}}{Z_{\varLambda}},

(5)

of the unperturbed and the full system, respectively.
Here and in the following, unless specified differently, every trace (e.g., the ones used in the definition of the partition functions in (
4
)) is understood to be taken over
\mathcal{A}_{\varLambda}
.

2.2
Main result

We are now ready to formally state our main result.
We recall that
D
denotes the spatial dimension and
q
denotes the dimension of the local Hilbert space.

Theorem 2

(Decay of correlations)
.

Let
D
,
q
,
R\in\mathbb{N}
and
C_{\mathrm{int}}>0
.
Then there exist
a\in\lparen 0,1\rparen
and
C_{1}
,
C_{2}
,
C_{3}>0
such that the following holds.
Consider the lattice
\varLambda\Subset\mathbb{Z}^{D}
and a Hamiltonian
H^{0}_{\varLambda}+V_{\varLambda}
as defined in Section
2.1
with
\lVert h\rVert_{\infty}
,
\lVert v\rVert_{\infty}\leq C_{\mathrm{int}}
,
v_{x}
of range
R\in\mathbb{N}
, and
v_{x}
relatively
a
-bounded w.r.t.
H^{0}_{\varLambda}
in the sense (
3
).
Then the Gibbs state
\rho_{\varLambda}
at any inverse temperature
\beta\in(0,\infty)
satisfies

\displaystyle\lvert\Tr\lparen A\,B\,\rho_{\varLambda}\rparen-\Tr\lparen A\,\rho_{\varLambda}\rparen\Tr\lparen B\,\rho_{\varLambda}\rparen\rvert

(6)

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}\,\lVert A\rVert\,\lVert B\rVert\,\exp\big\lparen C_{2}\,(\lvert X\rvert+\lvert Y\rvert)\big\rparen\,\exp\big\lparen-C_{3}\,d\lparen X,Y\rparen\big\rparen

for all
X
,
Y\subset\varLambda
and observables
A\in\mathcal{A}_{X}
and
B\in\mathcal{A}_{Y}
.

We stress that all the constants
a
,
C_{1}
,
C_{2}
and
C_{3}
in the above theorem are
independent
of the lattice
\varLambda
and in particular its size.
Therefore, our result on decay of correlations can easily be extended to the thermodynamic limit.
More precisely, for any weak
∗
limit (i.e. when tested on
\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{loc}}
) of the finite volume states
\rho_{\varLambda}(\cdot)\equiv\Tr(\cdot\,\rho_{\varLambda})
, given by
\rho_{\varLambda}\rightarrow\rho_{\mathbb{Z}^{d}}
as
\varLambda\nearrow\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, the same statement (
6
) holds as well.
Moreover, the constants are also independent of the inverse temperature
\beta
and hence the correlation length
1/C_{3}
is uniformly bounded away from zero and infinity, indicating the absence of any kind of phase transition.
In particular, we have decay of correlations, uniformly for all temperatures.

3
Proofs

The proof of Theorem
2
is conducted in three main steps.
First, in Section
3.1
, we rewrite the exponential of the Hamiltonian via an inclusion-exclusion argument and estimate the terms in the decomposition exploiting analyticity of
\mathbb{C}\ni z\mapsto z\,v_{x}
for every
x\in\varLambda
, as done in
[
50
]
.
Next, in Section
3.2
, we adapt the probabilistic cluster expansion technique from
[
AC2024
]
to the quantum setting in order to (algebraically) unravel cancellations in the difference
\Tr\lparen A\,B\,{\mathrm{e}}^{-\beta H_{\varLambda}}\rparen\,Z_{\varLambda}-\Tr\lparen A\,{\mathrm{e}}^{-\beta H_{\varLambda}}\rparen\,\Tr\lparen B\,{\mathrm{e}}^{-\beta H_{\varLambda}}\rparen
.
The remaining terms then naturally carry ratios of partition functions of
H_{S}
and
H_{S}^{0}
for some
S\subset\varLambda
, which are then estimated in Section
3.3
.
Finally, the arguments provided in Sections
3.1
–
3.3
are combined in Section
3.4
, where we complete the proof of Theorem
2
, additionally using some combinatorial percolation estimate.

3.1
Analyticity bound

Throughout the proof, we consider both the inverse temperature
\beta
and the underlying lattice
\varLambda\Subset\mathbb{Z}^{D}
to be fixed.
In particular, every trace is understood to be taken over
\mathcal{A}_{\varLambda}
.
To start, we introduce the following notations: For any
M\subset\varLambda
we define their
interior
and
closure
(relative to
\varLambda
) as

{M}^{\circ}:=\big\{\nonscript\,x\in M\nonscript\,\big|\allowbreak\nonscript\,\mathopen{}B_{R}(x)\subset\varLambda\nonscript\,\big\}\quad\quad\text{and}\quad\quad\overline{M}:=\varLambda\cap\bigcup_{x\in M}B_{R}(x),

such that, in particular,

V_{\varLambda}=\sum_{x\in{\varLambda}^{\circ}}v_{x}\quad\quad\text{and}\quad\quad\sum_{x\in M}v_{x}\in\mathcal{A}_{\overline{M}}\quad\text{for all $M\subset{\varLambda}^{\circ}$}.

We now follow the expansion from
[
50
]
.
First, note that for any function
f
defined on
\mathcal{P}_{0}(\mathbb{Z}^{D})
and every
\varGamma\Subset\mathbb{Z}^{D}
it holds that

f(\varGamma)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}I\subset\varGamma\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}M\subset I\end{subarray}}(-1)^{\lvert I\rvert-\lvert M\rvert}\,f(M),

because on the right hand side all terms except
f(\varGamma)
cancel exactly.
We now apply this to

f(M)=\exp\bigg\lparen-\beta\,\Big\lparen H^{0}_{\varLambda}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in M\end{subarray}}v_{x}\Big\rparen\bigg\rparen

to obtain

{\mathrm{e}}^{-\beta H_{\varLambda}}=f({\varLambda}^{\circ})=\sum_{I\subset{\varLambda}^{\circ}}T_{I}\quad\quad\text{with}\quad\quad T_{I}=\sum_{M\subset I}(-1)^{\lvert I\rvert-\lvert M\rvert}\,f(M).

In
T_{I}
, the second term in the exponential is supported on
\overline{M}\subset\overline{I}
and thus commutes with
H^{0}_{\varLambda\mathbin{\mathchoice{\displaystyle\setminus}{\textstyle\setminus}{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\setminus$}}{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\setminus$}}}\overline{I}}=H^{0}_{\varLambda}-H^{0}_{\overline{I}}
, which can thus be factored out of the exponential.
Hence,

T_{I}={\mathrm{e}}^{-\beta H^{0}_{\varLambda\mathbin{\mathchoice{\displaystyle\setminus}{\textstyle\setminus}{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\setminus$}}{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\setminus$}}}\overline{I}}}\,T^{\overline{I}}_{I}\quad\quad\text{with}\quad\quad T^{I_{2}}_{I_{1}}=\sum_{M\subset I_{1}}(-1)^{\lvert I_{1}\rvert-\lvert M\rvert}\,{\mathrm{e}}^{-\beta\,\big\lparen H^{0}_{I_{2}}+\sum_{x\in M}v_{x}\big\rparen}\in\mathcal{A}_{I_{2}\cup\overline{I_{1}}}.

With this notation,
T_{I}=T^{\varLambda}_{I}
.

Lemma 3
.

Using the above notations, it holds that

\lVert T^{\overline{I}}_{I}\rVert\leq(2a)^{\lvert I\rvert}.

Proof.

For
M\subset I\subset{\varLambda}^{\circ}
consider the function

\mathbb{C}^{\lvert M\rvert}\to\mathcal{A}_{\overline{I}},\quad z=(z_{x})_{x\in M}\mapsto g_{M}(z)=H^{0}_{\overline{I}}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in M\end{subarray}}z_{x}\,v_{x},

which agrees with
H^{0}_{\overline{I}}+\sum_{x\in M}v_{x}
for
z=(1,\dotsc,1)
and is analytic.
Moreover, by (
3
)

\Big\lvert\Big\langle\psi,\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in M\end{subarray}}z_{x}\,v_{x}\,\psi\Big\rangle\Big\rvert\leq\sum_{x\in M}\,\lvert z_{x}\rvert\,\frac{a}{\lvert B_{R}\rvert}\,\big\lvert\big\langle\psi,H^{0}_{B_{R}(x)}\,\psi\big\rangle\big\rvert\leq\max_{x\in M}\,\lvert z_{x}\rvert\,a\,\big\lvert\big\langle\psi,H^{0}_{\overline{I}}\,\psi\big\rangle\big\rvert

for all
\psi\in{\mathcal{H}}_{\overline{I}}
.
Hence, for all
z
such that
\max_{x}\,\lvert z_{x}\rvert<1/a
it holds that

\big\lvert\big\langle\psi,g_{M}(z)\,\psi\big\rangle\big\rvert\geq 0,

which implies that

\lVert{\mathrm{e}}^{-\beta g_{M}(z)}\rVert\leq 1.

Now, for
I\subset{\varLambda}^{\circ}
consider the function

\mathbb{C}^{\lvert I\rvert}\to\mathcal{A}_{\overline{I}},\quad z=(z_{x})_{x\in I}\mapsto T^{\overline{I}}_{I}(z)=\sum_{M\subset I}(-1)^{\lvert I\rvert-\lvert M\rvert}\,{\mathrm{e}}^{-\beta\,g_{M}\big\lparen(z_{x})_{x\in M}\big\rparen}.

This function is also analytic, satisfies
\lVert T^{\overline{I}}_{I}(z)\rVert\leq 2^{\lvert I\rvert}
if
\max_{x\in I}\,\lvert z_{x}\rvert<1/a
by the above bounds, and
T^{\overline{I}}_{I}\big\lparen(1)_{x\in I}\big\rparen=T^{\overline{I}}_{I}
.
Moreover, if
z_{y}=0
for some
y\in I
, then
T^{\overline{I}}_{I}(z)=0
.
To understand this, note that for any
M\subset I\mathbin{\mathchoice{\displaystyle\setminus}{\textstyle\setminus}{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\setminus$}}{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\setminus$}}}\{y\}

g_{M\cup\{y\}}\big\lparen(z_{x})_{x\in M\cup\{y\}}\big\rparen=g_{M}\big\lparen(z_{x})_{x\in M}\big\rparen

and due to the different signs in
T^{\overline{I}}_{I}(z)
all terms cancel.
The result follows by applying
[
50
, Lemma 2]
.
∎

3.2
Cluster expansion

Using Yarotsky’s decomposition from last section, for any
\varOmega\subset\varLambda
and
O\in\mathcal{A}_{\varOmega}
we can decompose

\displaystyle\frac{\Tr\big\lparen O\,{\mathrm{e}}^{-\beta\,H_{\varLambda}}\big\rparen}{Z^{0}_{\varLambda}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{I\subset{\varLambda}^{\circ}}\frac{\Tr\big\lparen O\,T_{I}\big\rparen}{Z^{0}_{\varLambda}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{I\subset{\varLambda}^{\circ}}\frac{\Tr\big\lparen O\,T^{\overline{I}}_{I}\,{\mathrm{e}}^{-\beta H^{0}_{\varOmega\mathbin{\mathchoice{\displaystyle\setminus}{\textstyle\setminus}{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\setminus$}}{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\setminus$}}}\overline{I}}}\,{\mathrm{e}}^{-\beta H^{0}_{\varLambda\mathbin{\mathchoice{\displaystyle\setminus}{\textstyle\setminus}{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\setminus$}}{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\setminus$}}}\smash[b]{(\overline{I}\cup\varOmega)}}}\big\rparen}{\Tr\big\lparen{\mathrm{e}}^{-\beta H^{0}_{\overline{I}}}\,{\mathrm{e}}^{-\beta H^{0}_{\varOmega\mathbin{\mathchoice{\displaystyle\setminus}{\textstyle\setminus}{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\setminus$}}{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\setminus$}}}\overline{I}}}\,{\mathrm{e}}^{-\beta H^{0}_{\varLambda\mathbin{\mathchoice{\displaystyle\setminus}{\textstyle\setminus}{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\setminus$}}{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\setminus$}}}\smash[b]{(\overline{I}\cup\varOmega)}}}\big\rparen}

\displaystyle=\sum_{I\subset{\varLambda}^{\circ}}\frac{\Tr\big\lparen O\,T^{\overline{I}\cup\varOmega}_{I}\big\rparen}{Z^{0}_{\overline{I}\cup\varOmega}},

where due to
O
, the trace only factorizes between
\mathcal{A}_{\overline{I}\cup\varOmega}
and
\mathcal{A}_{\varLambda\mathbin{\mathchoice{\displaystyle\setminus}{\textstyle\setminus}{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\setminus$}}{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\setminus$}}}(\overline{I}\cup\varOmega)}
.
For
O=\mathbf{1}
, one can choose
\varOmega=\emptyset
and obtain

\frac{\Tr\big\lparen{\mathrm{e}}^{-\beta\,H_{\varLambda}}\big\rparen}{Z^{0}_{\varLambda}}=\sum_{I\in{\varLambda}^{\circ}}\frac{\Tr\big\lparen T^{\overline{I}}_{I}\big\rparen}{Z^{0}_{\overline{I}}}.

At this point, we would like to interpret the subset
I
that is being summed over on the right-hand side of the equations above as a
“configuration”
that is produced by the partition function.
This interpretation allows us to phrase the methods we will later use in this paper in the language of statistical physics.
Accordingly, we will also give the following definition to link our quantities to concepts from statistical physics.

Definition 4

(Configuration, Support, and Weight)
.

We defined a configuration
I
to be any subset of
{\varLambda}^{\circ}
.
The support of the configuration is equivalent to the subset itself.
The weight of the configuration is
\Tr\lparen T^{\overline{I}}_{I}\rparen/Z^{0}_{\overline{I}}
.

Remark 5

(On the
h_{x}
’s being on-site operators)
.

In implementing the decomposition above and using the concepts introduced in Definition
4
, we crucially used that the operators
h_{x}
act only on individual sites.
This is necessary to introduce the notion of
“weights”
and use that the weight of union
I\cup J
factorizes, if
I
and
J
are not
R
-connected
(see Definition
7
later).

Remark 6

(Comparison to the probabilistic setting)
.

The only notion presented above that would distinguish our present setup from standard statistical physics is the fact that the weight associated to each configuration is not necessarily positive.
Moreover, we remark that the notion of
“support”
is only mention to complete the analogy, but not used in the argument.

A critical idea in the paper
[
50
]
and statistical physics in general is the idea of cluster and the compatibility between clusters.
Indeed, one can very strongly characterize properties of the Gibbs measures in statistical physics if one knows that configurations can be broken apart into distinct clusters and the weight of a configuration is a simple product of the weight of each individual cluster.

Definition 7

(Clusters and
R
-connectedness)
.

Let
x
and
y
be two points of
\varLambda
.
We say that
x
and
y
are
R
-connected
if
B_{R}(x)\cap B_{R}(y)\neq\emptyset
.
Similarly, we say that two sets
I_{1}
,
I_{2}\subset\varLambda
are
R
-connected to each other
, if there exist
R
-connected points
x_{1}\in I_{1}
and
x_{2}\in I_{2}
.

Given this
R
-connectedness, we define clusters as
R
-connected sets.

Definition 8

(Cluster)
.

A subset
I\subset\varLambda
is a
cluster
or equivalently an
R
-connected set
, if for every two points
x
,
y\in I
there exists a sequence of points
z_{1},\ldots,z_{m}\in I
such that
z_{i}
is
R
-connected to
z_{i+1}
for
i\in\{1,\ldots,m-1\}
and
x
is
R
-connected to
z_{1}
and
y
is
R
-connected to
z_{m}
.

The following Lemma shows that our configurations and weights satisfy the fundamental properties of a cluster expansion.

Lemma 9
.

Let
I_{1}
,
I_{2}\subset{\varLambda}^{\circ}
and
\varOmega_{1}
,
\varOmega_{2}\subset\varLambda
.
Assume that
I_{1}\cup\varOmega_{1}
and
I_{2}\cup\varOmega_{2}
are not
R
-connected to each other.
Then, for every
O_{1}\in\mathcal{A}_{\varOmega_{1}}
and
O_{2}\in\mathcal{A}_{\varOmega_{2}}
, we have that

\frac{\Tr\lparen O_{1}\,O_{2}\,T^{\overline{I_{1}\cup I_{2}}\cup\varOmega_{1}\cup\varOmega_{2}}_{I_{1}\cup I_{2}}\rparen}{Z^{0}_{\overline{I_{1}\cup I_{2}}\cup\varOmega_{1}\cup\varOmega_{2}}}=\frac{\Tr\lparen O_{1}\,T^{\overline{I_{1}}\cup\varOmega_{1}}_{I_{1}}\rparen}{Z^{0}_{\overline{I_{1}}\cup\varOmega_{1}}}\,\frac{\Tr\lparen O_{2}\,T^{\overline{I_{2}}\cup\varOmega_{2}}_{I_{2}}\rparen}{Z^{0}_{\overline{I_{2}}\cup\varOmega_{2}}}.

(7)

Proof.

We first write

T^{\overline{I_{1}\cup I_{2}}\cup\varOmega_{1}\cup\varOmega_{2}}_{I_{1}\cup I_{2}}=T^{\overline{I_{1}\cup I_{2}}}_{I_{1}\cup I_{2}}\,{\mathrm{e}}^{-\beta H^{0}_{(\varOmega_{1}\cup\varOmega_{2})\mathbin{\mathchoice{\displaystyle\setminus}{\textstyle\setminus}{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\setminus$}}{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\setminus$}}}\overline{I_{1}\cup I_{2}}}}.

Since
I_{1}
and
I_{2}
are not
R
-connected to each other, we must have that
\overline{I_{1}}\cap\overline{I_{2}}=\emptyset
.
Thus,
\overline{I_{1}\cup I_{2}}={\overline{I_{1}}\cup\overline{I_{2}}}
is a disjoint union.
Furthermore,
I_{1}
and
I_{2}
are disjoint and any subset
M\subset I_{1}\cup I_{2}
can be uniquely decomposed as
M=M_{1}\cup M_{2}
, where
M_{1}\subset I_{1}
and
M_{2}\cup I_{2}
.
As a consequence, we see that

\displaystyle T_{I_{1}\cup I_{2}}^{\overline{I_{1}\cup I_{2}}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{M\subset I_{1}\cup I_{2}}(-1)^{\lvert I_{1}\cup I_{2}\rvert-\lvert M\rvert}\,\exp\bigg\lparen-\beta\,\Big\lparen H^{0}_{\overline{I_{1}\cup I_{2}}}+\sum_{x\in M}v_{x}\Big\rparen\bigg\rparen

\displaystyle=\begin{aligned} \sum_{M\subset I_{1}\cup I_{2}}(-1)^{\lvert I_{1}\rvert-\lvert M_{1}\rvert}\,(-1)^{\lvert I_{2}\rvert-\lvert M_{2}\rvert}\,&\exp\bigg\lparen-\beta\,\Big\lparen H^{0}_{\overline{I_{1}}}+\sum_{x\in M_{1}}v_{x}\Big\rparen\bigg\rparen\\
&\times\exp\bigg\lparen-\beta\,\Big\lparen H^{0}_{\overline{I_{2}}}+\sum_{x\in M_{2}}v_{x}\Big\rparen\bigg\rparen\end{aligned}

\displaystyle=T^{\overline{{I_{1}}}}_{I_{1}}\,T^{\overline{{I_{2}}}}_{I_{2}}.

In the second line, we first decomposed the subset
M=M_{1}\cup M_{2}
as above.
The operators
\sum_{x\in M_{1}}v_{x}
and
H^{0}_{\overline{I_{1}}}
are in
\mathcal{A}_{\overline{I_{1}}}
while
\sum_{x\in M_{2}}v_{x}
and
H^{0}_{\overline{I_{2}}}
are in
\mathcal{A}_{\overline{I_{2}}}
.
Thus, the operators
H^{0}_{\overline{I_{1}}}+\sum_{x\in M_{1}}v_{x}
and
H^{0}_{\overline{I_{2}}}+\sum_{x\in M_{2}}v_{x}
commute with each other.
Since
H^{0}
has only on-site terms, we also have

\displaystyle\,{\mathrm{e}}^{-\beta H^{0}_{(\varOmega_{1}\cup\varOmega_{2})\mathbin{\mathchoice{\displaystyle\setminus}{\textstyle\setminus}{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\setminus$}}{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\setminus$}}}\overline{I_{1}\cup I_{2}}}}={\mathrm{e}}^{-\beta\,H^{0}_{\varOmega_{1}\mathbin{\mathchoice{\displaystyle\setminus}{\textstyle\setminus}{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\setminus$}}{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\setminus$}}}\overline{I_{1}}}}\,{\mathrm{e}}^{-\beta\,H^{0}_{\varOmega_{2}\mathbin{\mathchoice{\displaystyle\setminus}{\textstyle\setminus}{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\setminus$}}{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\setminus$}}}\overline{I_{2}}}}

and

\displaystyle{\mathrm{e}}^{-\beta\,H^{0}_{\overline{I_{1}\cup I_{2}}\cup\varOmega_{1}\cup\varOmega_{2}}}={\mathrm{e}}^{-\beta\,H^{0}_{\overline{I_{1}}\cup\varOmega_{1}}}\,{\mathrm{e}}^{-\beta\,H^{0}_{\overline{I_{2}}\cup\varOmega_{2}}}.

Using factorization of the trace between
\overline{I_{1}}\cup\varOmega_{1}
and
\overline{I_{2}}\cup\varOmega_{2}
gives (
7
).
∎

With these consequences in hand, we can now get to the crux of the matter.

Definition 10

(Supercluster)
.

Let
I
and
J
be two configurations. A supercluster decomposition of
I\cup J\cup X\cup Y
is a decomposition

I\cup J\cup X\cup Y=S_{1}\cup S_{2}\cup\ldots\cup S_{n},

where the sets
S_{i}
are all maximally
R
-connected subsets, i.e. the
S_{i}
are
R
-connected sets that are not
R
-connected to each other.

We can provide configuration expansions for
\Tr\lparen{\mathrm{e}}^{-\beta\,H_{\varLambda}}\rparen/Z^{0}_{\varLambda}
,
\Tr\lparen A\,B\,{\mathrm{e}}^{-\beta\,H_{\varLambda}}\rparen/Z^{0}_{\varLambda}
as well as
\Tr\lparen A\,{\mathrm{e}}^{-\beta\,H_{\varLambda}}\rparen/Z^{0}_{\varLambda}
and
\Tr\lparen B\,{\mathrm{e}}^{-\beta\,H_{\varLambda}}\rparen/Z^{0}_{\varLambda}
.
The product of the configuration decompositions of
\Tr\lparen{\mathrm{e}}^{-\beta\,H_{\varLambda}}\rparen/Z^{0}_{\varLambda}
and
\Tr\lparen A\,B\,{\mathrm{e}}^{-\beta\,H_{\varLambda}}\rparen/Z^{0}_{\varLambda}

will lead to a supercluster decomposition which will, in some instances, cancel with appropriate terms that appear in a supercluster decomposition involving
\Tr\lparen A\,{\mathrm{e}}^{-\beta\,H_{\varLambda}}\rparen/Z^{0}_{\varLambda}
and
\Tr\lparen B\,{\mathrm{e}}^{-\beta\,H_{\varLambda}}\rparen/Z^{0}_{\varLambda}
.
This is the content of the following theorem.

Theorem 11
.

Let
X
and
Y\subset\varLambda
each be an
R
-connected set and
A\in\mathcal{A}_{X}
and
B\in\mathcal{A}_{Y}
.
Let
I
be a configuration produced in the decomposition of
\Tr\lparen{\mathrm{e}}^{-\beta\,H_{\varLambda}}\rparen/Z^{0}_{\varLambda}
and
J
be a configuration produced in the decomposition of
\Tr\lparen A\,B\,{\mathrm{e}}^{-\beta\,H_{\varLambda}}\rparen/Z^{0}_{\varLambda}
.
We consider the supercluster decomposition of
I\cup J\cup X\cup Y
and let
E(X,Y)
be the event that the associated supercluster decomposition does not contain
X
and
Y
in the same supercluster.
As a shorthand, we write such terms in the product as

\frac{\Tr\lparen{\mathrm{e}}^{-\beta\,H_{\varLambda}}\rparen}{Z^{0}_{\varLambda}}\,\frac{\Tr\lparen A\,B\,{\mathrm{e}}^{-\beta\,H_{\varLambda}}\rparen}{Z^{0}_{\varLambda}}\bigg|_{E(X,Y)}.

We consider a similar supercluster decomposition for

\frac{\Tr\lparen A\,{\mathrm{e}}^{-\beta\,H_{\varLambda}}\rparen}{Z^{0}_{\varLambda}}\,\frac{\Tr\lparen B\,{\mathrm{e}}^{-\beta\,H_{\varLambda}}\rparen}{Z^{0}_{\varLambda}}\bigg|_{E(X,Y)}.

The two quantities above are equal.

Proof.

Consider a supercluster expansion for

\frac{\Tr\lparen{\mathrm{e}}^{-\beta\,H_{\varLambda}}\rparen}{Z^{0}_{\varLambda}}\,\frac{\Tr\lparen A\,B\,{\mathrm{e}}^{-\beta\,H_{\varLambda}}\rparen}{Z^{0}_{\varLambda}}\bigg|_{E(X,Y)}.

This supercluster expansion can be written as
S_{1}\cup S_{2}\cup\ldots\cup S_{m}
and without loss of generality we can assume that
X\subset S_{1}
and
Y\subset S_{2}
.
We now define

\displaystyle I^{\prime}

\displaystyle=(J\cap S_{1})\cup\big\lparen I\cap(S_{2}\cup S_{3}\cup\dotsb\cup S_{m})\big\rparen

and

\displaystyle J^{\prime}

\displaystyle=(I\cap S_{1})\cup\big\lparen J\cap(S_{2}\cup S_{3}\cup\dotsb\cup S_{m})\big\rparen.

On an intuitive level, we switch the parts of the configurations
I
and
J
that are part of the first supercluster
S_{1}
, which contains
X
.

We remark that the supercluster decomposition of
X\cup Y\cup I^{\prime}\cup J^{\prime}
is still
S_{1}\cup S_{2}\cup\ldots\cup S_{m}
.
The union of
I^{\prime}\cup J^{\prime}
must be the same as the union of
I\cup J
by our construction and, thus, we could not have changed the maximally
R
-connected subsets that appear in the decomposition.
Furthermore,
Y
will still be contained in the supercluster
S_{2}
and
X
is contained in the supercluster
S_{1}
.
As a result, we see that this construction is an involution.
We only have to check that

\frac{\Tr\lparen T^{\overline{I}}_{I}\rparen}{Z^{0}_{\overline{I}}}\,\frac{\Tr\lparen A\,B\,T^{\overline{J}\cup X\cup Y}_{J}\rparen}{Z^{0}_{\overline{J}\cup X\cup Y}}=\frac{\Tr\lparen A\,T^{\overline{I^{\prime}}\cup X}_{I^{\prime}}\rparen}{Z^{0}_{\overline{I^{\prime}}\cup X}}\,\frac{\Tr\lparen B\,T^{\overline{J^{\prime}}\cup Y}_{J^{\prime}}\rparen}{Z^{0}_{\overline{J^{\prime}}\cup Y}}.

(8)

Since the superclusters
S_{1},\ S_{2},\ldots,\ S_{m}
are mutually not
R
-connected to each other and
X\subset S_{1}
and
Y\subset S_{2}
, we can apply
Lemma

9
to each of the fractions, yielding

\displaystyle\frac{\Tr\lparen T^{\overline{I}}_{I}\rparen}{Z^{0}_{\overline{I}}}

\displaystyle=\prod_{k=1}^{m}\frac{\Tr\lparen T^{\overline{{I\cap S_{k}}}}_{I\cap S_{k}}\rparen}{Z^{0}_{\overline{I\cap S_{k}}}},

\displaystyle\frac{\Tr\lparen A\,B\,T^{\overline{J}\cup X\cup Y}_{J}\rparen}{Z^{0}_{\overline{J}\cup X\cup Y}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\Tr\lparen A\,T^{\overline{{J\cap S_{1}}}\cup X}_{J\cap S_{1}}\rparen}{Z^{0}_{\overline{J\cap S_{1}}\cup X}}\,\frac{\Tr\lparen B\,T^{\overline{{J\cap S_{2}}}\cup Y}_{J\cap S_{2}}\rparen}{Z^{0}_{\overline{J\cap S_{2}}\cup Y}}\,\prod_{k=3}^{m}\frac{\Tr\lparen T^{\overline{{J\cap S_{k}}}}_{J\cap S_{k}}\rparen}{Z^{0}_{\overline{J\cap S_{k}}}}

for the left-hand-side and

\displaystyle\frac{\Tr\lparen A\,T^{\overline{I^{\prime}}\cup X}_{I^{\prime}}\rparen}{Z^{0}_{\overline{I^{\prime}}\cup X}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\Tr\lparen A\,T^{\overline{{I^{\prime}\cap S_{1}}}\cup X}_{I^{\prime}\cap S_{1}}\rparen}{Z^{0}_{\overline{I^{\prime}\cap S_{1}}\cup X}}\,\frac{\Tr\lparen T^{\overline{{I^{\prime}\cap S_{2}}}}_{I^{\prime}\cap S_{2}}\rparen}{Z^{0}_{\overline{I^{\prime}\cap S_{2}}}}\,\prod_{k=3}^{m}\frac{\Tr\lparen T^{\overline{{I^{\prime}\cap S_{k}}}}_{I^{\prime}\cap S_{k}}\rparen}{Z^{0}_{\overline{I^{\prime}\cap S_{k}}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\Tr\lparen A\,T^{\overline{{J\cap S_{1}}}\cup X}_{J\cap S_{1}}\rparen}{Z^{0}_{\overline{J\cap S_{1}}\cup X}}\,\frac{\Tr\lparen T^{\overline{{I\cap S_{2}}}}_{I\cap S_{2}}\rparen}{Z^{0}_{\overline{I\cap S_{2}}}}\,\prod_{k=3}^{m}\frac{\Tr\lparen T^{\overline{{I\cap S_{k}}}}_{I\cap S_{k}}\rparen}{Z^{0}_{\overline{I\cap S_{k}}}},

\displaystyle\frac{\Tr\lparen B\,T^{\overline{J^{\prime}}\cup Y}_{J^{\prime}}\rparen}{Z^{0}_{\overline{J^{\prime}}\cup Y}}

We begin the algorithm by setting

,

q_{1}=v_{1}
.
