Atorvastatin Prevents Early Lipotoxic Remodelling in Right Ventricular Pacing–Induced Cardiomyopathy
Authors: Yu-Sheng Lin, Wan-Chun Ho, Meng-Hung Lin, Ting-Yu Kuo, Chang-Min Chung, Pei-Chun Yen, Yung-Lung Chen, Huang-Chung Chen, Mien-Cheng Chen, Tzu-Hsien Tsai, Wei-Chieh Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRight ventricular (RV) pacing can induce metabolic and structural alterations in left ventricular (LV) myocardium, including lipid accumulation, glycogen depletion, and increased endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress. This study evaluated protective effects of atorvastatin on RV pacing–induced metabolic dysfunction.MethodsFifteen male Lanyu miniature pigs were randomized into sham control (n = 5), RV pacing (n = 5), and RV pacing with atorvastatin treatment (n = 5) groups. RV pacing was achieved via ventricular lead implantation at the RV outflow tract, followed by complete atrioventricular block, with ventricular pacing maintained for at least 80 beats/min in dual-chamber, dual-sensing, dual-response, rate-responsive mode to provide atrial-synchronous ventricular pacing while allowing atrial sensing or pacing. The RV pacing with atorvastatin treatment group received atorvastatin (40 mg/d) starting 2 weeks before surgery until euthanasia at 6 months. Metabolic, histologic, and molecular parameters were assessed in LV myocardial tissue.ResultsCompared with sham controls, RV pacing significantly increased myocardial lipid accumulation, with higher triglyceride (2.43 ± 0.17 vs 1.74 ± 0.14 μg/mg, P < 0.01) and diacylglycerol (2902 ± 567.7 vs 977.5 ± 182.5 μg/mg, P < 0.01) levels, and significantly reduced glycogen content (0.09 ± 0.03 vs 0.21 ± 0.03 μg/mg, P < 0.05). ER stress markers binding immunoglobulin protein and cleaved caspase-9 were markedly increased in the RV pacing group (P < 0.0001). Atorvastatin significantly reduced lipid accumulation (triglycerides: 1.83 ± 0.08 μg/mg; P < 0.05), ER stress, and apoptosis, but did not significantly restore glycogen levels.ConclusionsRV pacing induces lipid accumulation, glycogen loss, and increased ER stress in the LV myocardium. Atorvastatin partially mitigated these effects by normalizing fatty acid metabolism and reducing ER stress and apoptosis, suggesting a potential role in attenuating early/subclinical RV pacing–induced metabolic remodelling.

With the aging of the population, an increasing number of patients are receiving permanent pacemakers (PPMs) for conduction disorders.1,2 However, PPMs can also lead to long-term complications, such as pacing-induced cardiomyopathy (PICM), particularly in patients with a high pacing burden and a wide paced QRS duration.3 PICM is defined as a reduction in left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) of ≥ 10% to a value < 50%, without a clear alternative explanation, in the context of significant right ventricular (RV) pacing, with an incidence of approximately 10%-20% in real-world registries.3, 4, 5 PICM is recognized increasingly as a consequence of not only mechanical dyssynchrony but also substantial alterations in myocardial metabolic pathways, as chronic RV pacing leads to asynchronous myocardial contraction that disrupts normal energy utilization and substrate metabolism.6 Mechanistically, pacing-induced metabolic ventricular remodelling leads to downstream consequences, including lipid accumulation, oxidative stress, and structural deterioration of the myocardium. Previous studies have further demonstrated that chronic RV pacing activates the liver X receptor/retinoid X receptor (LXR/RXR) signalling pathway, which plays a key role in regulating myocardial lipid homeostasis and contributes to pacing-induced lipotoxicity.7

Pharmacologic interventions with statins, such as atorvastatin, have shown promise in mitigating these maladaptive processes by reducing lipid accumulation and endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress, alleviating fibrosis, and enhancing mitochondrial and metabolic signalling.8,9 These findings are supported by clinical studies showing that statin therapy is associated with improved outcomes in heart failure (HF) patients, including those with PICM, irrespective of ischemic etiology.10,11 Atorvastatin is a widely used statin that demonstrates beneficial effects in HF, even in nonischemic cases, beyond its lipid-lowering properties.12 These benefits are attributed to pleiotropic actions, including a reduction in systemic inflammatory markers such as C-reactive protein and soluble tumour necrosis factor receptor-1, as well as an improvement in endothelial function and inhibition of neurohormonal activation.13,14 However, whether pharmacologic modulation of metabolic remodelling can mitigate pacing-induced myocardial injury remains unknown.

In the present study, we extend previous findings by demonstrating that RV pacing induces early lipotoxic metabolic remodelling prior to overt systolic dysfunction, and that atorvastatin mitigates myocardial lipid accumulation and ER stress–mediated apoptosis, effects consistently observed in both a porcine pacing model and paced cardiomyocytes. Building on these observations, we further investigated the molecular mechanisms through which atorvastatin may attenuate metabolic remodelling and delay the development of pacing-induced cardiomyopathy.

Methods

Animal model

Our animal model included 15 male Lanyu miniature pigs, randomly assigned to 3 groups: sham control (n = 5), RV pacing (n = 5), and RV pacing treated with atorvastatin (RVS pacing group; n = 5). All animals received care in accordance with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals published by Taiwan’s National Institutes of Health. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC: 2018030801) of Chang Gung Memorial Hospital. All pigs underwent a surgical procedure for pacemaker lead implantation, with the atrial lead placed in the right atrial appendage and the ventricular lead positioned in the right ventricular outflow tract (RVOT). After confirming secure placement of the ventricular lead, a complete atrioventricular (AV) block was induced via radiofrequency ablation of the AV node in both the RV pacing and RVS pacing groups. In the sham control group, the same anesthesia and pacemaker implantation procedures, including atrial and ventricular lead placement, were performed; however, AV nodal ablation was not performed, and the pacemaker was not activated for ventricular pacing during the follow-up period. Following AV nodal ablation, the pacemaker was programmed in dual-chamber, dual-sensing, dual-response, rate-responsive mode to provide atrial-synchronous ventricular pacing while allowing atrial sensing or pacing. The lower rate limit was set at 80 beats/min. Because a complete AV block had been created, this programming resulted in a high ventricular pacing burden while preserving atrial sensing and AV tracking. The procedures were performed on pigs aged 8-10 months, and all animals were sacrificed 6 months after surgery. Throughout the study period, all pigs were maintained on a standard diet. In addition, pigs in the RVS pacing group received atorvastatin at a dose of 40 mg/d, starting 2 weeks prior to surgery and continuing until the end of the study. General anesthesia was induced with intramuscular Zoletil 50, dexmedetomidine, and atropine, followed by inhaled isoflurane via mask and endotracheal intubation. Animals were mechanically ventilated with 100% FiO2, and anesthesia was maintained with 2%-4% isoflurane. At the end of the study, animals were euthanized under deep general anesthesia according to the institutional animal care protocol, followed by immediate harvesting of left ventricular myocardial tissue for histologic and molecular analyses. Body surface area (BSA) in the pigs was calculated using the Kelley formula, which was developed for swine: BSA (cm2) = 734 × body weight (kg)∧0.656. BSA-indexed echocardiographic parameters, including LV mass index, were calculated using this species-specific formula.

Specimen storage

After euthanasia under general anesthesia, the LV myocardial tissues at the level of the papillary muscles were harvested from the pigs. Portions of the ventricular tissue were immediately snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80°C for subsequent protein analysis. Additional tissue samples were fixed in 10% formalin, dehydrated through a graded series of ethanol solutions (75%, 85%, 90%, and 100%), and embedded in paraffin wax. These samples were preserved for histologic analysis.

Histologic, biochemical, and molecular assays

All histologic, biochemical, and molecular analyses were conducted in accordance with previously published protocols and/or the manufacturers’ instructions, as detailed below.

Oil red O stain

LV myocardial tissues were sectioned into 8-μm-thick slices and stained using an Oil Red O staining kit (ScyTek Laboratories, Logan, UT) following the manufacturer’s instructions. The stained sections were then mounted and visualized using an Olympus BX51 microscope. Lipid-positive areas were analyzed using CellSens Dimension software (Olympus Corporation, Tokyo, Japan), and the extent of lipid accumulation in each cardiomyocyte was quantified and expressed as a percentage of the total cell area. Quantification was performed on ≥ 10 randomly selected fields per sample.

Lipid assessment

LV myocardial tissues were homogenized in NP-40 Substitute Assay Reagent (Cayman Chemical Company, Ann Arbor, MI) supplemented with protease inhibitors (Complete Mini, Roche Diagnostics, Basel, Switzerland). The homogenates were then centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 10 minutes at 4°C. The resulting supernatants were collected and analyzed using a triglyceride (TG) colorimetric assay kit (Cayman Chemical Company, Ann Arbor, MI), following the manufacturer’s instructions. Diacylglycerol (DAG) concentration was measured using a competitive inhibition enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kit (MyBioSource, San Diego, CA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. LV myocardial tissues were homogenized in freshly prepared lysis buffer using a glass homogenizer on ice. The homogenates were then centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 5 minutes at 4°C. The resulting supernatants were collected and processed for ELISA. Absorbance was measured at a wavelength of 450 nm using a microplate reader.

Western blot

Protein extracts from LV myocardial tissues were prepared using CelLytic MT Cell Lysis Reagent (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO) supplemented with a protease/phosphatase inhibitor cocktail. The tissue homogenates were centrifuged at 14,000 revolutions per minute for 20 minutes at 4°C, and the resulting supernatants were collected. Protein concentrations were determined using the bicinchoninic acid assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Equal amounts of protein (30-100 μg) were separated on 10%-12% sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) gels by electrophoresis at room temperature for 1 hour and transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride membranes on ice for 1 hour. Membranes were blocked at room temperature for 1 hour with Tris-buffered saline containing 0.1% Tween-20 (TBST) and either 5% (w/v) nonfat dry milk or 5% (w/v) bovine serum albumin. The membranes were then incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies against binding immunoglobulin protein (BiP), hormone-sensitive lipase (HSL), pyruvate dehydrogenase (PDH), cleaved caspase-9, adipose triglyceride lipase (ATGL), and cluster of differentiation 36 (CD36) (all at a 1:1000 dilution; Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, unless otherwise noted), diluted in TBST containing 5% nonfat dry milk. After 3 washes in TBST, membranes were incubated for 1 hour at room temperature with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:10,000 dilution) in TBST containing 5% nonfat dry milk. Following another 3 washes, signal detection was performed using Immobilon Western Chemiluminescent HRP Substrate (Millipore, Burlington, MA). Protein expression levels were normalized to glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH; 1:50,000 dilution; Millipore) as a loading control.

Periodic acid-Schiff staining

Paraffin-embedded LV myocardial tissue blocks were sectioned into 4-μm-thick slices. The sections were deparaffinized by immersing them in xylene 3 times for 5 minutes each, followed by rehydration through a graded series of ethanol solutions (100%, 90%, 85%, and 75%, each for 5 minutes). Glycogen content in the myocardial tissue was then visualized using periodic acid–Schiff staining.

Glycogen measurements

Glycogen concentration was measured using 10 mg of LV myocardial tissue. Tissue samples were homogenized and diluted with distilled water, after which 50 μL of each homogenate was loaded in duplicate into a 96-well plate. Glycogen levels were then determined using a colorimetric glycogen assay kit (ab65620; Abcam, Cambridge, UK), following the manufacturer’s protocol.

Histology and cell surface area measurements

To quantify individual myocyte size, LV myocardial tissue sections were stained with Alexa Fluor 555-conjugated wheat germ agglutinin (WGA; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Rockford, IL). Histologic sections (5-μm thick) were prepared and stained with WGA at a concentration of 50 μg/mL to delineate cell membranes, allowing measurement of myocyte cross-sectional area. ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD) was used for image analysis. For each sample, 10 random fields were analyzed, with 100 myocytes measured per field, yielding a total of 1000 cells per sample. Each experimental group included 5 samples.

Masson trichrome stain

LV myocardial tissue sections were stained using a Modified Masson’s Trichrome Stain Kit (ScyTek Laboratories) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, sections were first fixed in Bouin’s fluid, then stained with Weigert’s hematoxylin. This step was followed by sequential incubation with Biebrich Scarlet/Acid Fuchsin, differentiation in phosphomolybdic/phosphotungstic acid solution, staining with Aniline Blue, and a final incubation with acetic acid. After dehydration, the sections were mounted and visualized under an Olympus DP70 microscope. Fibrotic areas stained by Masson’s Trichrome were quantified using Image-Pro Plus 6.0 software (Media Cybernetics, Rockville, MD), which calculated the positive staining area (μm2) to assess the extent of fibrosis.

Cell model

Rat ventricular cardiomyocytes (SV-40 strain) were cultured in Prigrow III medium (Applied Biological Materials, Richmond, Canada) supplemented with 15% (v/v) fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin/streptomycin. Cells were maintained at 37°C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2 and 95% air. Once the cells reached approximately 80% confluence, they were subjected to either non-pacing conditions or electrical pacing at a frequency of 1.5 Hz to induce reproducible cellular pacing stress using a C-PACE EP culture pacer (IonOptix Corporation, Milton, MA). The pacing parameters included an output of 1 V and a pulse width of 0.4 ms, applied continuously for 16 hours. Each pacing condition was performed in duplicate per experiment, and the entire experiment was repeated 3 times. To investigate the effect of statins on lipid metabolism in cardiomyocytes under pacing conditions, cells were pretreated with atorvastatin (Sigma-Aldrich, Oakville, Canada) at a concentration of 20 μM for 1 hour. The treatment with atorvastatin was maintained throughout the 16-hour pacing period in the atorvastatin group. The 16-hour pacing protocol was used to induce acute pacing-related metabolic stress while maintaining cell viability. Atorvastatin was applied at 20 μM based on previous cardiovascular cell-culture studies using micromolar concentrations to investigate direct intracellular stress and metabolic signalling.15,16 This in vitro protocol was intended to assess cell-autonomous responses to pacing and atorvastatin treatment, rather than to replicate the chronic pharmacokinetic exposure of the in vivo animal model.

Flow cytometry and immunofluorescence staining

The intracellular lipid content was assessed using both flow cytometry and immunofluorescence analysis. Cells were stained with BODIPY 493/503, a lipophilic fluorescent dye specific to neutral lipids (Cayman Chemical). For flow cytometry, stained cells were analyzed to quantify lipid accumulation based on fluorescence intensity. For immunofluorescence, cells were fixed and stained with BODIPY 493/503, followed by imaging under a fluorescence microscope to visualize and qualitatively assess lipid distribution within the cells.

Statistical analysis

All data analyses were performed using Prism 5.0 (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA). Normality was assessed for each group prior to statistical comparisons. Longitudinal echocardiographic parameters were analyzed using 2-way repeated-measures analysis of variance with group and time as factors, followed by multiplicity-adjusted post hoc comparisons. End-point comparisons among independent groups were analyzed using 1-way analysis of variance followed by Tukey’s test. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD); a P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics and electrophysiological effects of RV pacing and echocardiographic parameters

The study included 15 male Lanyu miniature pigs, randomly assigned to 3 groups: sham control (n = 5; average age 8.2 ± 0.8 months), RV pacing (n = 5; average age 8.3 ± 0.9 months), and RVS pacing (n = 5; average age 8.5 ± 0.7 months), with no significant difference in age among groups (P = 0.843). During the perioperative period, heart rates were 100.8 ± 13.44 beats/min in the sham group, 99.4 ± 9.63 beats/min in the RV pacing group, and 110.4 ± 16.41 beats/min in the RVS group (P = 0.400). Just prior to euthanasia, heart rates were 86.0 ± 8.75, 85.0 ± 15.36, and 89.8 ± 17.11 beats/min in the sham, RV pacing, and RVS pacing groups, respectively. At the time of pacemaker implantation, average body weights were 27.8 ± 4.19 kg (sham), 29.37 ± 2.55 kg (RV pacing), and 30.97 ± 2.34 kg (RVS pacing), with no significant differences (P = 0.315). On the day of euthanasia, body surface areas were nearly identical across all groups (0.99 ± 0.07 m2 for sham, 0.99 ± 0.04 m2 for RV pacing, and 0.99 ± 0.04 m2 for RVS pacing; P = 0.970). After 6-months of RV pacing, a significant prolongation of septal-to-posterior wall motion delay (SPMWD) showed pronounced ventricular dyssynchrony in the RV pacing group, compared to the sham group, compatible with the RVS pacing group (P < 0.05; Fig. 1A). The LV mass index and left ventricular end-diastolic diameter (LVEDD) were significantly elevated in the RV pacing group post 6 months RV pacing (P < 0.05; Fig. 1, B and C), reflecting marked hypertrophic remodelling and chamber dilation, whereas the RVS pacing group showed no such increase. A modest but not statistically significant decline in LVEF occurred in the RV pacing group, with preserved systolic function in the sham and RVS pacing groups (Fig. 1D). Although no overt clinical HF phenotype was observed during follow-up, RV pacing resulted in significant intraventricular dyssynchrony (Supplemental Fig. S1), increased LV mass index, and LV dilation, with only a modest and nonsignificant reduction in LVEF. RV pacing induces metabolic dysregulation and structural remodelling in the LV myocardium, attenuated by atorvastatin

Histologic and biochemical analyses (Fig. 2) were conducted to evaluate the mechanism of remodelling. Glycogen content (Fig. 2A) was significantly reduced in the RV pacing group (0.09 ± 0.03 μg/mg) compared to the sham group (0.21 ± 0.03 μg/mg; P < 0.05). Masson's trichrome staining (Fig. 2B) revealed extensive interstitial fibrosis in the RV pacing group relative to both the sham and the RVS pacing groups (P < 0.001), and WGA staining (Fig. 2C) demonstrated significantly increased cardiomyocyte cross-sectional area, indicative of hypertrophic remodelling (P < 0.001). Oil Red O staining (Fig. 2D) showed a marked accumulation of lipid droplets in the RV pacing group (16.06% ± 1.31%), compared to the sham (0.43% ± 0.14%) and RVS pacing groups (4.06% ± 2.61%; P < 0.001), indicating lipid overload after RV pacing. RV pacing induces myocardial lipotoxicity characterized by TG and DAG accumulation

To further characterize RV pacing–induced myocardial lipotoxicity, myocardial TG, DAG, and cholesterol levels were quantified in LV tissue. The RV pacing group exhibited significantly elevated myocardial TG levels (2.43 ± 0.17 μg/mg) compared to both the sham (1.74 ± 0.14 μg/mg; P < 0.01) and RVS pacing groups (1.83 ± 0.08 μg/mg; P < 0.05), indicating enhanced lipid storage (Fig. 3A). Similarly, DAG content was markedly higher in the RV pacing group compared to the sham group (P < 0.01), whereas the RVS pacing group showed nonsignificantly lower DAG accumulation (Fig. 3B). In contrast, myocardial cholesterol levels remained statistically unchanged across all groups (Fig. 3C). These findings indicate that RV pacing leads to significant lipid accumulation in the LV myocardium, primarily through increased TG and DAG levels, whereas cholesterol levels remain unaffected. RV pacing induces metabolic and stress-related protein dysregulation in the LV myocardium, ameliorated by RVS pacing

To investigate the molecular mechanisms underlying lipid and glycogen alterations in the LV myocardium following RV pacing, key proteins involved in fatty acid and glucose metabolism were analyzed (Fig. 4). In the fatty acid metabolism pathway, CD36—a membrane transporter responsible for fatty acid uptake—was significantly upregulated in the RV pacing group, compared to both the sham and the RVS pacing groups (1.09 ± 0.08 in RV vs 0.80 ± 0.06 in sham, P < 0.05; vs 0.62 ± 0.03 in RVS pacing, P < 0.001; Fig. 4A). In contrast, HSL, an enzyme involved in TG hydrolysis, was markedly downregulated in the RV pacing group relative to both the sham and RVS pacing groups (0.60 ± 0.08 in RV vs 0.88 ± 0.07 in sham, P < 0.05; vs 1.20 ± 0.07 in RVS pacing, P < 0.001; Fig. 4B), indicating impaired lipid catabolism. Similarly, ATGL, another key lipolytic enzyme, was significantly elevated in the RV pacing group compared to the sham group (1.64 ± 0.04 vs 0.71 ± 0.05, P < 0.001) but was reduced relative to the RVS pacing group (vs 1.19 ± 0.15, P < 0.05; Fig. 4C). In the glucose metabolism pathway, PDH—a critical enzyme that catalyzes the conversion of pyruvate to acetyl-CoA—was significantly upregulated in the RV pacing group compared to the sham group (1.59 ± 0.29 vs 0.53 ± 0.03, P < 0.01), but was not significantly different from the RVS pacing group (1.26 ± 0.18, P = n.s. [not significant]; Fig. 4D). In addition, markers of cellular stress and apoptosis were notably altered. Levels of cleaved caspase-9, an indicator of mitochondrial apoptotic signalling, were significantly increased in the RV pacing group compared to both the sham and RVS pacing groups (1.87 ± 0.15 vs 1.26 ± 0.09 in the sham group, P < 0.05; vs 1.19 ± 0.15 in the RVS pacing group, P < 0.01; Fig. 4E). Additionally, expression of BiP, a marker of ER stress, was markedly elevated in the RV pacing group compared to both control groups (2.91 ± 0.19 vs 1.74 ± 0.06 in the sham group, P < 0.001; vs 1.41 ± 0.11 in RVS pacing, P < 0.001; Fig. 4F). Atorvastatin restores metabolic balance and reduces cellular stress in pacing-stressed cardiomyocytes from a cell model

Key proteins involved in fatty acid metabolism, lipolysis, ER stress, and glucose metabolism were assessed in cardiomyocytes after 16 hours of pacing, with or without treatment with 20 μM atorvastatin (Supplemental Fig. S3). Consistent with vivo findings, pacing markedly increased the expression of CD36, ATGL, BiP, and PDH, while significantly reducing HSL levels. Atorvastatin treatment attenuated the pacing-induced upregulation of CD36, BiP, and PDH, and restored HSL expression. These results indicate that atorvastatin effectively counteracts pacing-induced metabolic dysregulation and cellular stress, highlighting its potential therapeutic role in preserving metabolic homeostasis in stressed cardiomyocytes.

Discussion

Our findings support a model in which RV pacing–induced mechanical dyssynchrony promotes maladaptive lipid handling in the myocardium, leading to DAG accumulation that may contribute to activation of ER stress, a process that appears to be partially attenuated by atorvastatin. In our study, RV pacing induced significant metabolic and pathologic alterations in the LV myocardium, including intraventricular dyssynchrony, increased LV mass index and LVEDD, myocardial lipid accumulation, glycogen depletion, fibrosis, cardiomyocyte hypertrophy, ER stress, and apoptosis. The heterogeneous contraction timing and wall stress caused by RV pacing may promote cardiomyocyte growth, extracellular matrix deposition, and progressive chamber dilation. Thus, the increased LV mass index, cardiomyocyte cross-sectional area, and LVEDD likely represent coordinated features of maladaptive eccentric remodelling rather than isolated concentric LV hypertrophy. The nonsignificant reduction in LVEF may reflect an early functional consequence of dyssynchrony and chamber remodelling, rather than established systolic dysfunction. Biochemical analyses demonstrated increased myocardial TG and DAG levels, whereas cholesterol levels remained unchanged. These changes were accompanied by upregulation of CD36 and ATGL, downregulation of HSL, increased PDH expression, and elevated markers of ER stress and apoptosis, including BiP and cleaved caspase-9. An important finding is that atorvastatin markedly reduced RV pacing–induced lipid accumulation by rebalancing lipolysis (partially restoring ATGL and enhancing HSL), normalizing metabolic and stress-related protein expression, and alleviating ER stress and apoptosis, whereas glycogen content remained largely unchanged. In vitro studies using paced cardiomyocytes further confirmed that atorvastatin effectively counteracts pacing-induced metabolic dysregulation and cellular stress, supporting its potential role in mitigating RV pacing-–induced metabolic remodelling. However, although the present findings support a plausible link between RV pacing–induced lipid accumulation, ER stress, and apoptosis, this study was not designed to prove direct causality among these pathways. Atorvastatin may exert protective effects through multiple pleiotropic mechanisms, and further mechanistic studies using pathway-specific inhibition or genetic modulation are required.

Lipid metabolism as a key therapeutic target in pacing-induced cardiomyopathy

Lipid metabolism is essential for maintaining cardiac function; however, disturbances in this process—especially lipid overload—can result in lipotoxicity and contribute to the development of cardiac dysfunction.17,18 Direct evidence of myocardial lipid accumulation after chronic RV pacing in patients remains limited, as myocardial biopsy or quantitative lipid imaging is not routinely performed in pacemaker recipients. Abnormalities in lipid and glucose metabolism are implicated in the pathogenesis of HF, including etiologies such as dilated cardiomyopathy (DCM), ischemic heart disease, and tachyarrhythmia.19, 20, 21 PICM arises from the suppression of myocardial lipid homeostasis, which disrupts lipid metabolism and promotes lipid accumulation, oxidative stress, and progressive myocardial remodelling.7 In our study, RV pacing induces significant lipid accumulation in the LV myocardium, as demonstrated by markedly elevated levels of TG and DAG. Previous work from our group demonstrated that RV pacing suppresses LXR/RXR signalling, a key pathway regulating myocardial lipid homeostasis. Although the present study did not directly assess LXR/RXR activity, the observed upregulation of CD36 and accumulation of TG and DAG may represent downstream consequences of impaired LXR/RXR signaling. This lipid buildup is associated with the upregulation of CD36, a key fatty acid transporter, and the downregulation of HSL, a crucial enzyme responsible for TG breakdown, indicating impaired lipid catabolism.22 Elevated ATGL, possibly reflecting a compensatory response to lipid overload, together with RV pacing–induced alterations, contributes to lipotoxic stress and may promote myocardial dysfunction.23 DAG accumulation has been shown to be a major source of ER stress in lipid metabolism disorders.24 In our study, RV pacing increased myocardial DAG levels, which may lead to ER stress and apoptosis. Atorvastatin significantly reduced TG accumulation, whereas DAG levels showed a nonsignificant numerical reduction and normalized CD36 and HSL expression, which also attenuated ER stress and apoptosis, as demonstrated by the reduced expression of BiP and cleaved caspase-9 in the atorvastatin-treated group compared to the RV pacing group (Fig. 6). Incomplete correction of glucose metabolism highlights the need for multi-targeted interventions

Although atorvastatin effectively reduces lipid accumulation and ER stress, its impact on glycogen metabolism appears limited. In the LV myocardium, glycogen levels in the atorvastatin-treated group did not significantly differ from those in the RV pacing group, suggesting that atorvastatin's primary cardioprotective effect may involve modulation of fatty acid metabolism rather than glucose metabolism. RV pacing increases myocardial energy demand, which impairs lipolysis and reduces the efficiency of fatty acid oxidation for adenosine triphosphate (ATP) production, thereby shifting the myocardium’s reliance on glycogen as an alternative energy source.25,26 In our study, glycogen content and PDH expression were not reversed by atorvastatin treatment. RV pacing can exert multifaceted effects on cardiac energy metabolism, and atorvastatin may help partially restore metabolic balance in this context. Future studies are needed to explore the specific mechanisms behind these selective metabolic effects and identify additional strategies that could restore glucose-related energy handling and support comprehensive metabolic recovery in RV pacing–induced cardiac stress.

Atorvastatin in PICM and nonischemic HF

According to 2 meta-analyses, atorvastatin improved LV performance and reduced HF readmission and mortality, with effects superior to those of other statins.27,28 In the context of PICM, statin therapy has been associated with reduced HF readmission and mortality; however, the comparative efficacy among different statins remains insufficiently explored.11 In other nonischemic etiologies of HF, low-dose atorvastatin has been shown to significantly reduce levels of inflammatory cytokines and uric acid, improve hemodynamic parameters, and enhance 5-year survival in patients with DCM.29 Our findings underscore the importance of metabolic regulation in the pathogenesis of PICM. Atorvastatin may help preserve LV function by reducing apoptosis and fibrosis, as well as mitigating lipotoxicity. Although further clinical studies are warranted, these results provide a rationale for considering statins as an adjunctive strategy to reduce the risk of PICM.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the sample size was relatively small and limited to male Lanyu miniature pigs, which may not fully represent the sex-specific responses or complex human cardiac physiology, limiting the generalizability of the study. Despite the limited sample size, the consistency across histologic, biochemical, and molecular endpoints supports the robustness of the findings. Second, the 6-month pacing duration, although sufficient to observe early metabolic and structural changes, may not capture the long-term progression of pacing-induced cardiomyopathy or the sustained effects of atorvastatin therapy. Third, although the study demonstrated beneficial effects of atorvastatin on lipid metabolism and ER stress, it did not restore glycogen content, indicating incomplete metabolic correction and suggesting that additional pathways may be involved. In addition, the associations among lipid accumulation, ER stress, and apoptosis should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating, because direct causal links were not tested using pathway-specific inhibition or genetic modulation. Fourth, the in vitro pacing model employed a simplified mechanical stress protocol and lacked the full hemodynamic and neurohormonal environment of in vivo systems. Fifth, invasive hemodynamic measurements were not performed; therefore, subclinical elevation of LV filling pressure in the RV pacing group cannot be excluded. Accordingly, this model should be interpreted as showing early/subclinical RV pacing–induced remodelling rather than a purely pre-HF state. Sixth, although all groups underwent the same anesthesia, euthanasia, and tissue-harvesting procedures, acute effects of anesthesia or terminal procedures on myocardial metabolic parameters cannot be completely excluded. In addition, AV nodal ablation followed by atrial-synchronous ventricular pacing with a lower rate limit of 80 beats/min was used to establish a stable, high-burden ventricular pacing model. Although atrial sensing and AV tracking were preserved, the selected lower rate was higher than the basal pacing rate commonly used in human pacemaker recipients. Therefore, the model primarily reflects the biological effects of a sustained high ventricular pacing burden and may not fully reproduce routine clinical pacemaker programming. Lastly, only one dose of atorvastatin was tested; thus, dose-response relationships and potential class effects of other statins remain unexplored. The in vitro pacing model used short-term stimulation and micromolar atorvastatin, which do not fully reproduce the chronic in vivo exposure in the 6-month pig model. Thus, these experiments should be interpreted as supportive mechanistic assays rather than a direct simulation of the in vivo condition. Future studies with longer follow-up periods, inclusion of female animals, and exploration of additional metabolic targets are warranted to better translate these findings into clinical practice. In particular, although previous work from our group implicated LXR/RXR signalling in RV pacing–induced lipid remodelling, LXR/RXR pathway activity was not directly assessed in the present study. Therefore, the mechanistic relationships among RV pacing, atorvastatin treatment, LXR/RXR signalling, and downstream lipid-handling proteins remain inferential and require further investigation.

Conclusion

In this study, RV pacing induces lipid accumulation, glycogen loss, and increased ER stress in the LV myocardium. Atorvastatin partially mitigated these effects by normalizing fatty acid metabolism and reducing ER stress and apoptosis, suggesting a potential role in attenuating early/subclinical RV pacing–induced metabolic remodelling.
