Abstract
. The phenomena that cause a value of a polynomial function to be a bifurcation one are yet to be described when the fibers have dimension higher than 1 1 . In this note, the main result is the construction of a polynomial submersion function of ℝ 3 \mathbb{R}^{3} with connected fibers having a bifurcation value such that close enough to it the fibers are mutually diffeomorphic. We also present an example of a polynomial submersion function of ℝ 2 \mathbb{R}^{2} having a bifurcation value such that close enough to it the fibers are mutually diffeomorphic.

Introduction

It recently came to our knowledge a question raised by Joi

t

,

a and Tibăr
[
1
]
whether a polynomial function of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
must be a locally trivial fibration at a given regular value whose nearby fibers are connected and diffeomorphic.
Here we negatively answer this question by presenting an explicit example.

To be more precise, let
f:\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a polynomial map.
We say that
f
is a
locally trivial fibration at
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
if there exist a neighborhood
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
of
\lambda
such that the restriction
f|_{f^{-1}(U)}\to U
is a
trivial fibration
, that is, the diagram

\textstyle{f^{-1}(\lambda)\times U\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\varphi}
\scriptstyle{\pi_{2}}
\textstyle{p^{-1}(U)\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{f}
\textstyle{U}

is commutative, where
\varphi
is a homeomorphism and
\pi_{2}
is the projection at the second variable.
A value
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is said to be a
bifurcation value
when
f
is not a locally trivial fibration at
\lambda
.
We denote by
B(f)
the set of bifurcation values of
f
.

It is clear that when
\lambda\notin B(f)
the fibers
f^{-1}(t)
are mutually homeomorphic for any
t
nearby
\lambda
.
But the converse is far from be true even when
\lambda
is a regular value in the “simplest” case
m=2
and
n=1
, as pointed out by Tibăr and Zaharia
[
3
]
by means of an explicit example, although not being a submersion in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.
The submersion assumption shows to be irrelevant, because in Example
2.1
below we present a submersion
f:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
with a bifurcation value at
0
and such that all the fibers
f^{-1}(t)
, for
t
in a neighborhood of
0
, are diffeomorphic, althought not connected.

The set
B(f)
is only partially understood.
When
m=2
and
n=1
it was completely characterized by Tibăr and Zaharia in the already mentioned paper
[
3
]
by means of the phenomena of
vanishing
and
splitting
.
Joi

2
]
extended results of

3
]
for the case
m=n+1
.
So for fibers of dimension
1

B(f)
is done.
But when the fibers have dimension greater than
1
it seems that vanishing and splitting alone do not characterize
B(f)
anymore.

In the already mentioned paper

f:\mathbb{R}^{4}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
having connected and mutually diffeomorphic fibers nearby

In particular, nor vanishing nor spliting can occur here.

Naturally, one can ask the same for functions.

So we negatively answer this question.

We finish the paper by taking advantage of our construction to deliver the above mentioned Example

2.
The answer is no

The objective of this section is to present a polynomial function
f:\mathbb{R}^{3}\to\mathbb{R}
such that

(1)

f
is a submersion

(2)

0\in B(f)

(3)

f^{-1}(t)
is connected and diffeomorphic to
\mathbb{R}^{2}
minus
3
points for all
t
close enough to
0
.

Let
p:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be a polynomial function having a local minimum
x_{m}>0
and a local maximum
x_{M}>0
both with value
0
, such that
p(c)\neq 0
for all
c\notin\{x_{m},x_{b},x_{M}\}
, where
x_{b}
is in between
x_{m}
and
x_{M}
and
p(x_{b})=0
.
In particular, the graphic of
p
at
(x_{b},c_{0})
is transversal to the
x
axis and
p(0)\neq 0
.
As an example one can take

p(x)=(x-3)^{2}(x-2)(x-1)^{2},

which has local minimum at
3
and local maximum at
1
, both with value
0
, and annihilates again only at
2
.

Consider now
\tilde{f}:\mathbb{R}^{3}\to\mathbb{R}
given by

(1)

\tilde{f}(x,y,z)=y-p(x),

where
p
is a polynomial function as above.
This function
\tilde{f}
is a submersion with fibers diffeomorphic to
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.
Moreover, for every
c
in a small enough interval
(-\epsilon,\epsilon)
, the fiber
\tilde{f}^{-1}(c)
intersects

\Gamma=\{(x,0,0)\ |\ x\in\mathbb{R}\}

at exactly three points.

We will work from now on only with
c
’s in
(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)
, assuming further that
p(0)\notin(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)
.
In particular,
\tilde{f}^{-1}(c)\cap\left(\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus\Gamma\right)
is diffeomorphic to
\mathbb{R}^{2}
minus
3
points.
If
c\neq 0
, then the intersections of
\tilde{f}^{-1}(c)
with
\Gamma
are transversal.
And the intersections of
\tilde{f}^{-1}(0)
with
\Gamma
are tangent at
(x_{m},0,0)
and
(x_{M},0,0)
, and transversal at
(x_{b},0,0)
.

Now we consider the diffeomorphism
T:\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus\Gamma\to\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus\Gamma
given by

T(x,y,z)=\left(x(y^{2}+z^{2}),y,z\right),\quad T^{-1}(x,y,z)=\left(\frac{x}{y^{2}+z^{2}},y,z\right).

In particular, the set
T^{-1}\left(\tilde{f}^{-1}(c)\cap(\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus\Gamma)\right)
is diffeomorphic to
\mathbb{R}^{2}
minus
3
points.

Finally we define
f:\mathbb{R}^{3}\to\mathbb{R}
by

f(x,y,z)=\tilde{f}\left(x(y^{2}+z^{2}),y,z\right).

Then
f|_{\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus\Gamma}=\tilde{f}\circ T
.
In particular,
\nabla f
is nowhere zero away from
\Gamma
.
Moreover, since
f_{y}(x,0,0)=\nabla\tilde{f}(0)\cdot(0,1,0)=1
for all
x
, it follows that
\nabla f
is nowhere zero in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, and hence
f
is a submersion.

On the other hand,

(2)

f^{-1}(c)=\left(f^{-1}(c)\cap\Gamma\right)\cup T^{-1}\left(\tilde{f}^{-1}(c)\cap(\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus\Gamma)\right)=T^{-1}\left(\tilde{f}^{-1}(c)\cap(\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus\Gamma)\right),

because
|f(x,0,0)|>|c|
.
In particular, the fiber
f^{-1}(c)
is diffeomorphic to
\mathbb{R}^{2}
minus
3
points.

Now we prove that
0
is a bifurcation value of
f
.
First we prove that
0
is a bifurcation value of

g=\tilde{f}|_{\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus\Gamma}.

If this is not the case, that is,
g
is a locally trivial fibration at
0
, then there exists
\delta>0
, a homeomorphism
\varphi:\mathcal{F}\times(-\delta,\delta)\to g^{-1}(-\delta,\delta)
, where
\mathcal{F}
is
\mathbb{R}^{2}
minus three points, and
g\circ\varphi
is the projection at the second coordinate.
Then we can select a small neighborhood of
(x_{m},0,0)
, and consequently an open set
V
of
\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus\Gamma\subset g^{-1}(-\delta,\delta)
.
It is easy to get a contradiction now because
V\cap\tilde{f}^{-1}(c)
has no holes when
c>0
but
V\cap\tilde{f}^{-1}(0)
does have a hole and so
\varphi^{-1}(V)\times\left(\mathcal{F}\times\{c\}\right)
will have a hole for all
c\in(-\delta,\delta)
.

But (
2
) gives that

f^{-1}(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)=T^{-1}\left(g^{-1}(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)\right).

From this and from (
2
) again we conclude that
0
is a bifurcation value of
f
as well.

Example 2.1
.

Let
\tilde{h}:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
be defined by

\tilde{h}(x,y)=\tilde{f}(x,y,0),

with
\tilde{f}
given in (
1
).
Function
\tilde{h}
is a submersion but now the fiber
\tilde{h}^{-1}(c)
when intersected with the complement of

\Gamma=\{(x,0)\ |\ x\in\mathbb{R}\}

has
4
connected components.
We consider

h(x,y)=\tilde{h}(xy^{2},y),

which is a submersion, as above, whose fiber
h^{-1}(c)
satisfies

h^{-1}(c)=J^{-1}\left(\tilde{h}^{-1}(c)\right),

where
J:\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\Gamma\to\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\Gamma
is the diffeomorphism given by

J(x,y)=\left(xy^{2},y\right).

In particular, the fiber
h^{-1}(c)
has
4
connected components for all
c
nearby
0
.
That
0
is a bifurcation value follows easily because
h
has both vanishing and splitting at
0
.
