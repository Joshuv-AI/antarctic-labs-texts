Profiling of main regulators of angiogenesis and inflammation in human PBMC and plasma revealed associations with lower extremity artery disease
Authors: Daniel Zalewski, Paulina Chmiel, Przemysław Kołodziej, Marcin Feldo, Jacek Bogucki, Janusz Kocki, Anna Bogucka-Kocka
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Lower extremity artery disease (LEAD) represents the most common atherosclerosis-related disease in the group of peripheral artery diseases. A growing amount of evidence suggests that dysregulations in signaling pathways associated with angiogenesis and inflammation can contribute to the progression and onset of LEAD; however, this association is still poorly understood. Therefore, in our study, we investigated expression levels of 18 genes that encode key regulators of angiogenesis and inflammation, as well as plasma levels of their protein products, in patients with LEAD in comparison with non-LEAD controls. Gene expression levels were determined in peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMC) using real-time PCR, and protein plasma levels were measured using ELISA. The expression levels of TGFB1, VEGFA, and VEGFB, as well as the plasma concentrations of ANG-1, CCL2, PDGF-AB, TGF-beta 1, and TNF-alpha, significantly differentiated the LEAD group from the control group. A strong correlation was found between the expression levels of TGFB1 and VEGFA. Significant relationships between VEGFB expression and blood cholesterol levels, as well as between CCL2 plasma concentrations and C-reactive protein blood levels, were demonstrated. Identified dysregulations in key angiogenesis and inflammation-related factors could reflect abnormal regulation of these processes in patients with LEAD, shedding new light on the molecular mechanisms underlying the development of LEAD and indicating candidates for circulatory biomarkers of this disease.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-25719-5.

Introduction

Atherosclerosis is one of the most common cardiovascular diseases caused by the continuous growth of atherosclerosis plaque in the lumen of the arteries leading to disturbance and restriction of blood flow1. Depending on the arterial localization, various adverse manifestations of atherosclerosis are observed, including coronary artery disease (CAD), cerebral artery disease, and a heterogeneous group of peripheral artery diseases (PAD) mostly represented by lower extremity artery disease (LEAD) caused by atherosclerosis developing in the arteries that supply the lower limbs2. LEAD is estimated to affect 230 million adults worldwide and is associated with an increased risk of various adverse clinical outcomes, including critical limb ischemia and amputation3. LEAD is the third leading cause of atherosclerotic morbidity, following coronary heart disease and stroke. Despite the prevalence and clinical importance of LEAD, the majority of attention is paid to heart-related atherosclerotic diseases and clinical outcomes (such as CAD and myocardial infarction), leading to limited awareness of this disease. For this reason, currently LEAD is considered an underdiagnosed and undertreated condition that requires more effort to increase awareness and research in order to gain new knowledge about the pathophysiology, risk factors, and molecular pathways involved in LEAD, as well as identify new biomarkers that can be used to improve the diagnosis, prognosis, and treatment of LEAD4.

LEAD is a progressive, multifactorial disease associated with many risk factors (e.g., sex, age, smoking, hypertension, dyslipidemia, and genetic predisposition) and involved various physiological and pathophysiological processes, such as endothelial dysfunction, response to hypoxia, inflammation, angiogenesis, vascular calcification, and thrombosis. Despite many efforts made to date, the molecular mechanisms underlying LEAD still constitute a large knowledge gap5. The studies performed to date proposed many candidates for LEAD biomarkers, either in genetics, epigenetics, transcriptomics, metabolomics, proteomics, and gut microbiome areas, providing potential diagnostic, prognostic, and therapeutic targets; however, evidence is insufficient to implement any of these biomarkers in clinical practice6. Special attention is paid to circulating biomarkers of LEAD, among which interleukin 6, interleukin 8, CRP (C reactive protein), and tumor necrosis factor α seem to exhibit the most promising properties7,8.

Given the evidence that increased inflammatory response and angiogenesis play a crucial role in the development and progression of LEAD, the presented study aims to identify dysregulations in key regulators of signaling pathways that control inflammation and angiogenesis in patients with LEAD. Obtained results may provide new information on the molecular mechanisms involved in LEAD and provide targets that can be useful in the diagnosis and therapy of LEAD.

The presented study joins the trend of research on the identification of circulatory biomarkers of LEAD in response to the urgent need for new and more effective diagnostic and therapeutic methods for this disease. LEAD is often asymptomatic, especially in the early stages of development; therefore, it is considered a globally underdiagnosed and undertreatment disease, which, if not recognized, can progress and lead to the development of serious complications. The identification and successful validation of easy-accessible circulatory biomarkers of LEAD would provide new diagnostic targets that can be used in laboratory tests for the early detection of this disease. It could result in an increase in the rate of disease detection, improve the outcome of treatment, decrease mortality due to complications, and reduce the costs of medical care. Furthermore, modulation of dysregulated inflammation or angiogenesis pathways could exert a therapeutic effect and expand the currently used treatment options.

Results

Subjects qualified for the study were age- and sex-matched, but differed in some other risk factors and biochemical parameters

The subjects enrolled in the study were divided into two groups: the group of 40 patients diagnosed with LEAD (LEAD group) and the group of 20 subjects without LEAD (control group). The main clinical characteristics were compared between the studied groups and, as expected, some statistically significant differences (P < 0.05) in the typical risk factors for LEAD were observed (refer to ‘Statistical analysis’ section for methodological details). The LEAD group contained statistically significantly more subjects who smoke and have hypertension and higher blood levels of cholesterol, CRP, and fibrinogen (Table 1). All of these parameters are strongly associated with LEAD. No statistically significant differences were found between these groups in age, sex, and body mass index (BMI), as well as in blood levels of LDL, HDL, creatinine, and homocysteine (Table 1).

Genes TGFB1, VEGFA, and VEGFB were dysregulated in PBMC of patients with LEAD

The presented study aimed to investigate the differences in the expression levels of 18 genes associated with angiogenesis and inflammation (ANGPT1, ANGPT2, CCL2, CSF2, CXCL8, FGF2, FGF23, IL1A, IL1B, IL6, PDGFA, PDGFB, TGFA, TGFB1, TNF, VEGFA, VEGFB, and VEGFC) in PBMC between the group of 40 patients with LEAD (LEAD group) and 20 healthy controls (control group). However, during the multistep data quality control procedure, five genes (ANGPT2, CSF2, FGF23, IL1A, and IL6) were excluded from the study due to the low expression levels (refer to the Methods section for more details).

Differences in the expression levels of 13 remaining genes in the PBMC samples between the LEAD and control groups were determined using the delta Ct method for relative quantification9,10. The distributions of normalized 2− dCt-transformed expression levels of these genes in the studied samples are presented in Fig. 1. Three genes (ANGPT1, CCL2, and TNF) exhibited lower expression and the other genes (CXCL8, FGF2, IL1B, PDGFA, PDGFB, TGFA, TGFB1, VEGFA, VEGFB, and VEGFC) had higher expression in the LEAD group in relation to the control group. Genes with P < 0.05 and with at least 50% change in expression in the LEAD group versus the control group were considered as significant. Three genes met these criteria: TGFB1, VEGFA, and VEGFB (Table 2).

The accuracy in classifying the samples into appropriate groups was analyzed using ROC analysis. The obtained values of aeras under the ROC curves (ROC-AUC) obtained for genes characterized by statistical significance were above 0.7, showing good classification accuracy (Table 2 and S1, Fig. S1). The differential character of the studied genes was further evaluated using logistic regression that was used to illustrate the chance that the LEAD condition will occur when the mean expression of the genes will double. The analysis was performed in univariate and multivariate modes, and the odds ratios (OR) and associated P values were calculated. Univariate logistic regression shows that only TGFB1 and VEGFA had statistically significant OR values (Table 2 and S2, Fig. S2). The OR value obtained for VEGFB had a borderline statistical significance (P = 0.052). In the multivariate mode of logistic regression, in which sex, age, BMI, and smoking were used as additional variables, any of the genes exhibits statistical significance. Therefore, the differences in the expression of the analyzed genes between the compared groups are probably associated with these variables, and therefore the analysis of such associations was also performed (see the paragraph ‘Relationships with risk factors and biochemical parameters’).

The performed study has shown that increased expression of TGFB1, VEGFA, and VEGFB could be indicative of LEAD and altered course of their associated regulatory pathways can contribute to the development of this disease. These findings should be further elucidated in future studies to better understand the pathology of LEAD.

Proteins encoded by some of the analyzed genes were also dysregulated in LEAD group

The ELISA method was used to analyze plasma levels of 19 proteins (ANG-1, ANG-2, CCL2, GM-CSF, IL-8, IL-1 alpha, IL-1 beta, IL-6, FGF-2, FGF-23, PDGF-AA, PDGF-AB, PDGF-BB, TGF-alpha, TGF-beta 1, TNF-alpha, VEGF-A, VEGF-B, and VEGF-C) encoded by genes analyzed in this study (see Methods section for details). Analysis was performed in plasma samples from 40 patients with LEAD (LEAD group) and 20 healthy controls (control group), the same as that used for gene expression analysis. The amounts of samples in which the concentrations of the analyzed proteins were determined or were too low to be measured (below the quantification range) were quite similar in the compared groups (Fig. S3). However, in the case of TGF-beta 1, concentrations were not detected in the entire control group, but were successfully determined in most LEAD patients.

Hierarchical clustering and PCA analysis showed the presence of two outlier samples in the data; therefore, they were excluded from the analysis (Fig. S4 and S5). The distributions of plasma levels of all analyzed proteins are compared between the studied groups in Fig. 2. The mean concentrations of ANG-1, CCL2, PDGF-AA, PDGF-AB, TGF-beta 1, TNF-alpha, VEGF-B, and VEGF-C were statistically significantly different (P < 0.05) between the LEAD and control groups (Table 3). In the ROC analysis, the obtained ROC-AUC values are consistent with statistical significance: proteins with the lowest P values had the highest values of the ROC-AUC parameter. For five proteins (ANG-1, CCL2, PDGF-AB, TGF-beta 1, and TNF-alpha), the obtained ROC-AUC values were higher than 0.75, suggesting good precision in the classification of the analyzed samples to the studied groups (Tab. S1, Fig. S6). Univariate odds ratios calculated for these five proteins characterized by statistical significance (Table 3 and S2, Fig. S7), except for TGF-beta 1, for which the odds ratio was not calculated, because in all samples of the control group, the concentrations of this protein were below the quantification range, and the obtained odds ratio values are difficult to be properly interpret. After adjustment by sex, age, BMI, and smoking variables, none of the analyzed proteins was statistically significant.

Genes and proteins dysregulated in LEAD group were associated with some risk factors and biochemical parameters

The relationships with risk factors and biochemical parameters were investigated for three genes (TGFB1, VEGFA, and VEGFB) and five proteins (ANG-1, CCL2, PDGF-AB, TGF-beta 1, and TNF-alpha) that showed the highest potential to be a biomarker of LEAD. Associations regarding continuous-type characteristics (age, BMI, and blood levels of cholesterol, LDL, HDL, homocysteine, urea, fibrinogen, creatinine, and CRP) were investigated using correlation analysis and univariate linear regression. No strong correlations have been found (Tab. S3), and those with (|R| > 0.40) were between VEGFB expression and blood cholesterol levels (R = 0.53, P = 3.09 × 10− 5), between TGF-beta 1 plasma concentrations and CRP blood levels (R = 0.45, P = 3.52 × 10− 4), as well as between CCL2 plasma concentrations and CRP blood levels (R = -0.42, P = 9.89 × 10− 4) (Fig. S8). In the linear regression analysis, only relationships between VEGFB expression and blood cholesterol levels (P = 6.47 × 10− 4), as well as between CCL2 plasma concentrations and CRP blood levels (P = 1.03 × 10− 3), were confirmed as statistically significant.

For categorical-type characteristics (sex, smoking, hypertension), the evaluation of the relationships with the analyzed genes and proteins was performed using the Mann Whitney U test and the Student’s t-test, depending on the normality of the data. Obtained results are provided in Table 4 and Fig. S9 and S10. All analysed factors were associated with hypertension, and also all factors, except TGFB1 expression, were associated with smoking. Only plasma levels of TNF-alpha were significantly different between men and women (P = 0.040).

Obtained results showed that genes and proteins found as potentially indicative of LEAD are associated with risk factors such as CRP and blood cholesterol levels, as well as sex, smoking, and hypertension status, which are the main risk factors for this disease. More studies are required to deeper elucidate these mechanisms.

Genes TGFB1 and VEGFA were strongly correlated with each other

To investigate the relationships between genes and proteins, a pairwise correlation analysis was performed for three genes (TGFB1, VEGFA, and VEGFB) and five proteins (ANG-1, CCL2, PDGF-AB, TGF-beta 1, and TNF-alpha) that statistically significantly differentiated the LEAD group from the control group (Fig. 3, Tab. S4). The strongest correlation was found between the expression levels of TGFB1 and VEGFA (R = 0.70, P = 5.18 × 10− 9) that were up-regulated in the LEAD group. Also positive, but moderate correlations were obtained between plasma levels of TNF-alpha and ANG-1 (R = 0.50, P = 1.35 × 10− 4), and between TNF-alpha and PDGF-AB (R = 0.57, P = 9.00 × 10− 6). These results suggest regulatory and functional associations between correlated factors and will be further discussed in the Discussion section.

No correlation was found between the expression of TGFB1 gene and plasma levels of the encoded TGF-beta 1 protein (R = -0.04, P = 0.79); therefore, the expression of this gene in PBMC does not correspond to its protein product in plasma.

The expression of the analyzed factors has a low ability to predict the LEAD advancement

To assess whether the expression of selected genes and proteins could be useful in predicting the clinical features of LEAD, associations with ABI, length of occlusion, and claudication distance were explored using correlation analysis (Tab. S5). Only two statistically significant, weak and positive correlations were found: between TGF-beta 1 plasma levels and length of occlusion (R = 0.40, P = 0.012), as well as between TNF-alpha plasma levels and ABI (R = 0.36, P = 0.026). The results show that analyzed factors have a low ability to predict the clinical parameters of LEAD, but more studies are needed to validate this conclusion.

Discussion

In the presented study, the expression levels of 18 genes associated with angiogenesis and inflammation (ANGPT1, ANGPT2, CCL2, CSF2, CXCL8, FGF2, FGF23, IL1A, IL1B, IL6, PDGFA, PDGFB, TGFA, TGFB1, TNF, VEGFA, VEGFB, and VEGFC) in PBMC samples, and plasma levels of their protein products were compared between the group of 40 patients with LEAD and 20 healthy controls (control group). Three genes, TGFB1, VEGFA, and VEGFB exhibited a statistically significant higher expression in the LEAD group (Fig. 1; Table 2). Regarding proteins, plasma levels of ANG-1, CCL2, PDGF-AB, and TNF-alpha were lower, while TGF-beta 1 was higher in the LEAD group compared to the control group (Fig. 2; Table 3). Therefore, the abnormal course of signaling pathways associated with genes and proteins that were found to be dysregulated in LEAD probably contributes to the development of this disease and should be further studied in future studies. Furthermore, these genes and proteins can be considered as potential candidates for LEAD biomarkers; however, the obtained results need to be validated in larger cohorts. Differential expression of these potential biomarkers was found to be related to a main risk factors for LEAD, such as sex, smoking, hypertension, and blood cholesterol levels; thus, they should be further studied in the context of these features. However, any of the selected genes and proteins exhibited a strong relationship with the clinical parameters of LEAD such as ABI, length of occlusion, and claudication distance; therefore, the usefulness of these factors in the predicting of the progression of LEAD has not been confirmed.

Among the 18 analyzed genes, only three of them (TGFB1, VEGFA, and VEGFB) met the criteria established for biomarker candidates: P value < 0.05 and at least 50% change in expression between compared groups. These genes are also characterized by the highest accuracy of the classification of the subjects into groups (ROC-AUC values ranged between 0.788 and 0.721), as well as they had the most statistically significant odds ratio values in the logistic regression analysis (Table 2). The results obtained by all used statistical methods agree that the differences in the expression of these genes have the greatest ability to distinguish LEAD patients from healthy subjects.

VEGF-A and VEGF-B are well-known regulators of blood vessel growth and the inflammatory response that play a crucial role in the development of atherosclerosis. VEGF-A exerts both beneficial and harmful effects related to atherosclerosis. VEGF-A stimulates NO synthesis in endothelial cells and inhibits their apoptosis; however, it can also induce atherogenesis by promoting monocyte adhesion, endothelial permeability, and transendothelial migration and activation of leukocytes11. The increase in VEGF-A-related adhesion of leukocytes to endothelial layers appears to be a vital mechanism of atherosclerosis in LEAD, since VEGF-A enhances the progression of atherosclerosis in various animal models11. In PBMCs, the same material as used in the current study, the VEGF145 isoform of VEGF-A was previously associated with ICAM-1 and L-selectin, well-known cellular adhesion molecules12. In the other study, VEGF-A increased neutrophil and T cell adhesion to the activated colonic endothelium13. In the study in which the concentrations of VEGF isoforms were predicted using a three-compartment model of the VEGF distribution in LEAD, the expression and secretion of the antiangiogenic VEGF-A165b isoform was higher than the proangiogenic VEGF-A165a isoform14. Furthermore, increased expression of VEGF-A165b was found in PAD muscle biopsies, and was demonstrated to inhibit endothelial VEGFR1 and VEGFR2 receptors, reducing the bioavailability of proangiogenic VEGF-A isoforms and inducing an antiangiogenic M1-like phenotype of macrophages that directly impairs angiogenesis, ischemic muscle revascularization, and perfusion recovery15–17. These results support the contribution of the VEGF-A165b isoform to the pathogenesis of LEAD and indicate inhibition of VEGF-A165b to exert a therapeutic effect in LEAD18. Based on these results, it could be concluded that the higher expression levels of VEGFA found in our study may reflect an increased expression of the antiangiogenic VEGF-A165b isoform, indicating reduced proangiogenic VEGF-A signaling leading to impaired angiogenesis and reduced revascularization ability in patients with LEAD. This conclusion could be supported by found in our study higher PBMCs and plasma levels of TGF-beta, which was previously shown to drive VEGFA alternative splicing to selection of distal splice site and thus favour the production of antiangiogenic VEGF-A165b isoform19. However, the mechanisms are more complex, since VEGF-A-mediated therapeutic angiogenesis trials to improve limb outcome are of limited benefit20–22.

VEGF-B is another member of the VEGF family that was found to have higher expression in PBMC of LEAD patients when compared to healthy controls. Although VEGF-B was shown to have some pro-angiogenic properties by binding to VEGF-R1 receptor23,24, a previous study demonstrated a negligible role of VEGF-B in revascularization after limb ischemia25, but rather is associated with promoting cell survival23,26. Decreased pro-angiogenic signaling mediated by VEGF-B can be caused by higher expression of VEGF-A165b isoform that binds to VEGF-R1 and represses its activation and downstream signaling in LEAD models16. The elevated expression of VEGFB found in LEAD patients in the presented study may represent a compensatory mechanism (such as negative feedback loop) caused by impaired VEGF-B signaling; however, this hypothesis needs to be verified in further studies.

Another gene found in our study to be upregulated in LEAD is TGFB1. TGF-beta 1, together with two other isoforms, TGF-beta 2 and TGF-beta 3, was evidenced to exhibit anti-inflammatory and antiproliferative properties by inhibiting chemotaxis, leukocyte adhesion to vascular endothelium, and macrophage activity. Studies have shown that inhibition of TGF-beta signaling resulted in acceleration of lesion progression and accumulation of inflammatory cells, promoting the development of an unstable plaque phenotype. Furthermore, TGF-beta isoforms levels were significantly lower in patients with atherosclerosis, and low serum levels of TGF-beta 1 are a risk factor for atherosclerosis. Due to these effects, TGF-beta 1 is regarded as atheroprotective factor associated with a lower inflammatory status and increased plaque stability, thus enhancing TGF-beta activity is a promising therapeutic option27–33.

In light of the above findings, the intriguing result observed in our study is that in PBMC of LEAD patients the expression of atheroprotective TGFB1 and plasma levels of TGF-beta 1 is higher than in the control group (Figs. 1 and 2; Tables 2 and 3). This phenomenon can be explained by the presence of ischemia-reperfusion cycles characteristic for this disease, which was previously associated with a significant increase in TGF-beta 1 levels and TGF-beta pathway signaling in endothelial cells and muscle cells, and contributed to fibrosis in the gastrocnemius34,35.

Apart of the TGF-beta signaling genes, identified core fibrosis genes associated with critical limb ischemia also include genes that are the main regulators of collagen synthesis and VEGF signaling36. In addition, TGF-beta signaling was shown to induce VEGF-A expression in various cell types37–40. This relationship may explain the strong and positive correlation between the expression of TGFB1 and VEGFA demonstrated in our study (Fig. 3).

Another factors with different plasma levels between LEAD and non-LEAD subjects were ANG-1, CCL2, PDGF-AB, and TNF-alpha. The concentrations of all these agents were found to be lower in LEAD samples. Since all of these factors contribute to stimulation of angiogenesis, the obtained results suggest a lower proangiogenic status of patients with LEAD, and can be linked to reduced ability to ischemic revascularization.

In particular, TNF-alpha has previously been shown to modulate VEGF splicing preferentially at the proximal splice site, leading to an increase in the expression of pro-angiogenic VEGF isoforms19. Therefore, lower plasma levels of TNF-alpha may be responsible for the reduction of proangiogenic signalling in LEAD patients; however, more studies are required to validate the impact of this mechanism on LEAD development. In contrast to our study, higher circulatory concentrations of TNF-alpha (and also CCL2) were previously found in subjects with LEAD compared to controls41–46. The observed discrepancy can be determined by differences in aspects of the methodology and the characteristics of the patients between studies. TNF-alpha and CCL2 appear as important factors involved in the development and complications of lower limb ischemia46,47; however, their role in the pathogenesis of this disease needs to be elucidated in further studies.

ANG-1 is involved in the crosstalk between blood vessels and muscle cells during regeneration from ischemic myopathy48, and promotes vessel assembly and stability via activation of the TIE2 receptor49. In previous studies, this factor was found to be required for functional neovascularization in animal models50,51, and its lower serum levels were found in LEAD patients and were associated with leg pain in non-diabetic patients52. Similarly, our study also demonstrated lower concentrations of ANG-1 in plasma from LEAD patients that could contribute to impaired vessel maturation and stability, leading to disease progression. Similarly to ANG-1, the plasma levels of PDGF-AB were also lower in LEAD patients. The role of PDGF-AB in LEAD pathogenesis has been poorly studied; however, it was shown that PDGF-AB, acting together with FGF-2, induces arteriogenesis and stabilizes the newly formed vasculature in the ischemic tissue53. It could be concluded that dysregulations of biomarkers identified in our study suggest that in patients with LEAD angiogenesis is impaired either in a phase of the formation of new vessels or in the mechanisms responsible for their maturation and stabilization (Fig. 4). Indeed, novel pro-angiogenic therapies proposed for LEAD include two components that address both problems. For example, therapeutic angiogenesis induced by the PDGF-BB and VEGF codelivery led to improved revascularization and reduced tissue damage in an ischemic hindlimb model54. Other, cell-based therapy containing autologous venous smooth muscle cells expressing VEGF and venous endothelial cells with high expression of ANG-1 was shown to improve amputation-free survival and wound healing rates in patients with critical limb ischemia55.

Identification of the circulatory hallmarks of localized diseases raises the question of to what extent the identified changes reflect the pathological processes ongoing in affected tissues. If lesions occur in a vascular system, such as atherosclerotic plaques, circulatory cells interact directly with the pathologically changed vascular wall and can easily acquire new features useful for disease detection and characterization. Furthermore, factors secreted by lesions can influence the functionality of circulatory and other cells, providing disease biomarkers. Our study demonstrated dysregulations in certain key regulators of inflammation and angiogenesis, processes closely associated with LEAD pathophysiology. However, in performed study, only selected, main regulators of these processes were investigated, providing a new paths and directions for further studies, in which downstream pathways, effectors, and receptors of proposed biomarkers should be further studied. Furthermore, the nature of the study does not allow to determine whether the obtained dysregulations are the cause or effect of the disease. Finally, the number of the analyzed samples in both groups was limited; therefore, the obtained results must be validated in studies with larger cohorts. However, our study provides a valuable primary insight into the LEAD signatures carried by the PBMC and plasma compartments, and associated with dysregulations of angiogenesis and inflammation. The results presented in this study can be further validated and extended in future studies in order to evaluate the possibility of using the presented dysregulations as diagnostic or therapeutic targets in clinical practice.

Although the analyzed genes/proteins are known contributors to the development of atherosclerosis, the performed study provides novel results in various aspects. The presented work utilized PBMCs, which is a biological material that has been poorly studied in the context of gene expression biomarkers of LEAD. The majority of studies conducted to date involved arterial tissues or plasma/serum samples, while PBMCs were studied much less frequently. Furthermore, the design of our study goes beyond typical procedures by integrated analysis of mRNA and protein levels, measured parallelly in the same individuals. Applied study design gives a unique opportunity not only to identify biomarkers of LEAD in poorly studied biological material, but also to provide new information about differences in the expression of revealed biomarkers at different stages of genetic expression (mRNA level, protein level).

Conclusions

In the presented study, we identified dysregulations of key regulators of angiogenesis and inflammation in patients with LEAD. These findings indicate that the abnormal course of these processes is probably involved in the pathophysiology of this disease. The presented differences in gene expression and plasma levels of angiogenesis and inflammation regulators can be considered as candidates for biomarkers of LEAD; however, more studies are required to validate and extend the results obtained in our study.

Methods

Subjects enrolled in the study

The study was carried out according to the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki and the study procedure was approved by the Bioethics Committee at Medical University of Lublin (Decision No. KE-10-0254/148/2021). Two groups of subjects were included in the study: 40 patients with LEAD (LEAD group) and 20 healthy volunteers (control group). The participants were qualified for the study in the Chair and Department of Vascular Surgery and Angiology of the Medical University of Lublin by a vascular surgeon. Informed and signed consent was obtained from all study subjects.

The LEAD group consisted of patients diagnosed with LEAD based on a medical interview, evaluation of peripheral pulses, ankle-brachial index (ABI) measurements, treadmill test, and vascular imaging by color duplex ultrasonography. The included individuals had atherosclerotic plaques diagnosed with a Trans-Atlantic Inter-Society Consensus score B or C. The atherosclerotic lesions were localized in the femoral, iliac, or popliteal arteries. Symptoms of the disease were classified into Rutherford category 2 or 3 (claudication without critical ischemia or tissue loss). Exclusion criteria were previous vascular surgery or percutaneous transluminal angioplasty/stent placement of the lower limbs, chronic venous disease, myocardial infarction, stroke, type 2 diabetes mellitus, and pregnancy.

The control group included 20 healthy non-smoking volunteers without cardiovascular diseases and events in the medical history. No atherosclerotic plaques or hemodynamic blood flow disturbances were observed in the lower limb region during color flow duplex ultrasound scanning.

Real-time PCR experiments

Expression levels of 18 genes associated with the regulation of angiogenesis and inflammation (ANGPT1, ANGPT2, CCL2, CSF2, CXCL8, FGF2, FGF23, IL1A, IL1B, IL6, PDGFA, PDGFB, TGFA, TGFB1, TNF, VEGFA, VEGFB, and VEGFC) were investigated in peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMC) derived from included subjects. The investigations were performed using the real-time PCR method as previously described56. Briefly, venous blood samples were collected from the included subjects and subjected to PBMC isolation using gradient centrifugation with Gradisol L reagent (Aqua-Med, Łódź, Poland) according to the procedure described in57. Subsequently, total RNA was isolated from the PBMC samples using TRI Reagent Solution (Ambion, Austin, TX, USA), according to the manufacturer’s procedure. The quality and quality of the total RNA samples were assessed using the NanoDrop ND-1000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and the Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer with the Agilent RNA 6000 Pico Kit (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). The total RNA samples were approved for further experiments if the 260/280 ratio was higher than 1.8 (RNA without significant contamination) and RNA integrity number was greater than 7 (RNA with high integrity).

The concentrations of the total RNA samples were equilibrated at 100 ng/µL and then reverse transcribed using the High Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol.

The real-time PCR reactions were prepared in 96-well plates and contain 2 µL of cDNA, 10 µL of TaqMan Gene Expression Master Mix (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA), 7 µL of nuclease‑free water, and 1 µL of TaqMan Gene Expression Assay (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA) specific to the target gene (Tab. S6). GAPDH was used as a reference gene. The real-time PCR reactions were performed in triplicates.

Real-time PCR experiments were performed using the 7900HT Real-Time Fast System (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA). Amplification conditions were used as follows: initial denaturation (95 °C for 10 min) and 40 amplification cycles (95 °C for 15 s and 60 °C for 1 min in each cycle).

The expression levels of the analyzed genes were determined using the ExpressionSuite v1.3 software (Life Technologies Corporation, Carlsbad, CA, USA) and defined as Ct values (the numbers of amplification cycle achieved at the intersection between an amplification curve and a threshold line defining the linear phase of the signal growth).

The Ct data were analyzed using R 4.3.2 programming software (https://www.r-project.org) and the RQdeltaCT 1.3.0 package (https://CRAN.R-project.org/package=RQdeltaCT). In the quality control of the raw Ct data, Ct values higher than 35, flagged as ‘undetermined’ or ‘inconclusive’, or with the AMPSCORE parameter < 1 were labeled as unreliable and removed from the data. The amounts of reliable and unreliable Ct values for each gene and each sample are presented in Fig. S11 and S12, respectively. Furthermore, genes with unreliable Ct data in more than half of the subjects in at least one of the study groups were excluded from the study (ANGPT2, CSF2, FGF23, IL1A, and IL6). One sample with unreliable Ct values in more than half of the analyzed genes was also removed. The filtering procedure resulted in the presence of missing values in the data (5.2% of the data), which were imputed using the multivariate linear regression method based on genes with complete data.

The imputed Ct data set was further analyzed using the delta Ct method for relative quantification9,10. The Ct values of GAPDH (reference gene) were used to normalize the Ct values of the target genes, obtaining delta Ct (dCt) values. Subsequently, the dCt values were transformed using the 2− dCt formula to achieve a linear form of the data. The homogeneity of the transformed dCt data was evaluated using hierarchical clustering (Fig. S13) and PCA analysis (Fig. S14). Four outlier samples were detected and removed from the data. The retained data have good uniformity and were subjected to further steps of the analysis.

Differences in expression of target genes between the LEAD and control groups were determined as fold change values that were calculated for each gene by dividing the mean of the transformed dCt values of the LEAD group by the mean of the transformed dCt values of the control group. Statistical analysis of the obtained differences was performed according to the procedure described in the paragraph ‘Statistical analysis’.

ELISA experiments

The concentrations of 19 proteins (Tab. S7) encoded by the analyzed genes were determined in plasma collected from 40 patients with LEAD (LEAD group) and 20 healthy volunteers (control group), the same as for the gene expression analysis. Plasma was separated from venous blood samples by centrifugation, aliquoted, and stored at -80 °C. Plasma protein quantification was carried out using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits purchased from Biorbyt (Cambridge, United Kingdom), according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Tab. S7).

Before adding to the ELISA plate, the thawed plasma samples were centrifuged at 2,000 g for 10 min at 4 °C to remove residual platelets and cell remains. If necessary, the samples were diluted using a diluent buffer provided with the ELISA kit. Eight standard concentrations (for the standard curve) and blank wells that contain a dilution buffer were used for each ELISA experiment. The DTS-2 incubator (ELMI, Riga, Latvia) was used to incubate ELISA plates during the experimental procedure. The prepared ELISA plates were read at the appropriate wavelength using the Synergy H1 microplate reader (BioTek, Winooski, VT, USA).

Gen5 version 3.10 software (BioTek, Winooski, VT, USA) was used to subtract the background absorbance values obtained from the blank wells from the values obtained from other wells, generate the standard curves, and calculate protein concentrations by interpolation. If the samples were diluted, the concentrations obtained from interpolation were multiplied by the dilution factor to obtain the final concentration.

The numbers of samples in and below the quantification range of the ELISA kits are shown in Fig. S3. If the interpolated protein concentration turns out to be below the quantification range, the values were set to the lowest values in the quantification range. The homogeneity of the data was assessed by hierarchical clustering (Fig. S4) and PCA analysis (Fig. S5). Two outlier samples were identified and removed from the data. Differences in plasma protein levels between the LEAD and control groups were evaluated using statistical testing (see the next paragraph).

Statistical analysis

The entire statistical analysis of the data was performed using the computational software R 4.3.2 (https://www.r-project.org/) and using appropriate statistical tests and methods. The normality of the data was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test (shapiro.test function in R). If the variables were normally distributed in both compared groups (P > 0.05 in the Shapiro-Wilk test), the parametric, two-sided Student’s t test was used (t.test function in R), otherwise the nonparametric, two-sided Mann-Whitney U test was used (wilcox.test function in R). The Fisher’s exact test was performed using the fisher.test function in R.

Linear regression models and logistic regression models were constructed using the lm and glm base functions in R, respectively. The correlation analysis was performed using the Spearman rank correlation test implemented in the Hmisc 5.1-0 package (https://cran.r-project.org/web/packages/Hmisc/index.html), and the correlation plot was generated using the corrplot 0.92 package in R. For ROC (Receiver Operating Characteristics) analysis, the pROC package 1.18.058 (https://cran.r-project.org/web/packages/pROC/index.html) was used.

Results obtained with P < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. The visualizations presented in the study were created using the ggplot2 3.4.2 package (https://ggplot2.tidyverse.org/) in R unless otherwise specified.
