Abstract
. We extend the classical theorems of Khintchine and Schmidt in metric Diophantine approximation to the context of self-similar measures on ℝ d \mathbb{R}^{d} . For this, we establish effective equidistribution of associated random walks on SL d + 1 ⁡ ( ℝ ) / SL d + 1 ⁡ ( ℤ ) \SL_{d+1}(\mathbb{R})/\SL_{d+1}(\mathbb{Z}) . Our result strengthens that of [ 7 ] which requires d = 1 d=1 and restricts Schmidt-type counting estimates to approximation functions which decay fast enough. Novel techniques include a bootstrap scheme for the associated random walks despite algebraic obstructions, and a refined treatment of Dani’s correspondence. We also establish non-concentration properties of self-similar measures near algebraic subvarieties of ℝ d \mathbb{R}^{d} .

1.
Introduction

Let
d\geq 1
be an integer. Denote by
\Sim(\mathbb{R}^{d})
the group of similarities of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, i.e. transformations
\phi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
of the form
\boldsymbol{s}\mapsto\mathtt{r}_{\phi}O_{\phi}\boldsymbol{s}+\mathtt{b}_{\phi}
where
\mathtt{r}_{\phi}>0
and
O_{\phi}\in O(d)
is a linear orthogonal transformation of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and
\mathtt{b}_{\phi}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
A probability measure
\lambda
on
\Sim(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is called a
randomized self-similar iterated function system
(IFS).
We say that
\lambda
is
strongly irreducible
if
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the only finite union of affine subspaces of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
which is invariant under
\lambda
-almost every
\phi
. We also say that
\lambda
has a
finite exponential moment
if there exists
\varepsilon>0
such that

\int_{\Sim(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\mathtt{r}_{\phi}^{\varepsilon}+\mathtt{r}_{\phi}^{-\varepsilon}+\|\mathtt{b}_{\phi}\|^{\varepsilon}\,\mathrm{d}\lambda(\phi)\,<+\infty.

Throughout this paper, we consider a probability measure
\sigma
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
which is
self-similar
in the sense that it is stationary under some randomized self-similar IFS
\lambda
which is strongly irreducible and has a finite exponential moment. Recall that stationarity means

\sigma=\int_{\Sim(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\phi_{\star}\sigma\,\mathrm{d}\lambda(\phi).

Although we do not impose the maps in the support of
\lambda
to be contractive, it turns out that the existence of
\sigma
implies that
\lambda
is
contractive in average
, i.e.

\int_{\Sim(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\log\mathtt{r}_{\phi}\,\mathrm{d}\lambda(\phi)<0,

and vice versa, see
[
12
, Theorem 2.5]
.

Example
.

On
\mathbb{R}
, classical examples of self-similar measures are the Lebesgue measure, the normalized Hausdorff measure on a missing digit Cantor set, or Bernouilli convolutions. In higher dimension, one may consider powers of missing digit Cantor measures, the normalized Hausdorff measure on a Sierpiński triangle, Sierpiński carpet, etc. In general, every randomized self-similar IFS with finite exponential moment and which is contractive in average, has a unique stationary probability measure
[
38
, Theorem 3.1]
.

The goal of the paper is to study the Diophantine properties of typical points chosen by a self-similar measure on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. This topic originates from a question of Mahler
[
43
]
, asking how well points in the middle-thirds Cantor set can be approximated by rationals. Mahler’s question is later recast by Kleinbock-Lindenstrauss-Weiss
[
33
]
who ask whether self-similar measures may satisfy a dichotomy in the spirit of Khintchine’s theorem. We first recall this theorem and present our main result, then we discuss how it connects to earlier works.

Given a non-increasing function
\psi:\mathbb{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
, we write
W(\psi)
the set of
\psi
-approximable
vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. In other terms, a vector
\boldsymbol{s}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
belongs to
W(\psi)
if for infinitely many
(\boldsymbol{p},q)\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}\times\mathbb{N}
, one has

(1.1)

\|q\boldsymbol{s}-\boldsymbol{p}\|_{\infty}<\psi(q),

where
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{\infty}
denotes the supremum norm on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. The celebrated Khintchine theorem
[
30
,
31
]
states that
W(\psi)
has either null or full Lebesgue measure, and that each scenario can be read simply on the function
\psi
, as they respectively correspond to
\sum_{q\in\mathbb{N}}\psi(q)^{d}<\infty
and
\sum_{q\in\mathbb{N}}\psi(q)^{d}=\infty
. In the divergent case, Khintchine’s theorem has been further refined by Schmidt
[
49
]
who provided an asymptotic estimate for the number of solutions to the Diophantine inequality (
1.1
) with bounded
q
.

In this paper, we extend the theorems of Khintchine and Schmidt to the context of self-similar measures. Our main theorem is the following.

Theorem 1.1

(Khintchine and Schmidt for self-similar measures)
.

Let
\lambda
be a probability measure on
\Sim(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, and assume
\lambda
is strongly irreducible with finite exponential moment.
Let
\sigma
be a
\lambda
-stationary probability measure on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Let
\psi:\mathbb{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
be a non-increasing function.

Then we have the dichotomy

(1.2)

\displaystyle\sigma(W(\psi))=\begin{cases}0&\text{ if }\sum_{q\in\mathbb{N}}\psi(q)^{d}<\infty,\\
1&\text{ if }\sum_{q\in\mathbb{N}}\psi(q)^{d}=\infty.\end{cases}

Moreover, in the divergent case
\sum_{q\in\mathbb{N}}\psi(q)^{d}=\infty
, we have the following asymptotic:
for
\sigma
-a.e.
\boldsymbol{s}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, as
n\to+\infty
:

\displaystyle\big|\{(\boldsymbol{p},q)\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}\times\llbracket 0,n\rrbracket\,:\,\|q\boldsymbol{s}-\boldsymbol{p}\|_{\infty}<\psi(q)\}\big|\sim_{\boldsymbol{s},\psi}2^{d}\sum_{q=0}^{n}\psi(q)^{d}.

Here we use the notation
\llbracket 0,n\rrbracket=[0,n]\cap\mathbb{N}
.
An asymptotic estimate of the number of
primitive
solutions of the Diophantine inequality is also provided, see (
8.5
).

Let us now explain how the topic has evolved from Mahler’s question in the 80’s to the above
1.1
. In the early literature, the convergence and divergence aspects of
1.1
were addressed separately for specific approximation functions. The first significant result in this direction was obtained by Weiss
[
56
]
for
\psi(q)=1/q^{1+\varepsilon}
and measures on the real line satisfying a certain decay condition, including the middle thirds Cantor measure. This work was later generalized by Kleinbock-Lindenstrauss-Weiss
[
33
]
to a broader class of measures on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, known as friendly measures. Subsequent important developments, adopting similar terminology, include
[
45
,
17
,
18
]
. The study of the case
\psi(q)=\varepsilon/q
was conducted by Einsiedler-Fishman-Shapira
[
21
]
for missing digit Cantor measures, and generalized significantly by Simmons-Weiss
[
52
]
to arbitary self-similar measures.

Without restriction on the non-increasing function
\psi
, Khalil-Luethi
[
28
]
obtained the Khintchine dichotomy (
1.2
) under the condition that the self-similar measure
\sigma
has a large enough dimension, and
\lambda
is rational, finitely supported, contractive, and satisfies the open set condition. For instance, the Cantor measure with single-missing digit in base
5
is in this class, but not the middle thirds Cantor measure, see also the subsequent related works
[
58
,
19
]
which all assume large dimension for
\sigma
in some sense.
In our previous paper
[
7
]
, we proposed a new approach, based on projection theorems à la Bourgain, which led to the Khintchine dichotomy (
1.2
) in the case where
d=1
, and with no further restriction, i.e. dealing with any self-similar measure
\sigma
and any non-increasing function
\psi
. The assumption
d=1
is however needed at the most crucial dimension bootstrapping step in the proof. We also obtained Schmidt’s counting theorem for
primitive
solutions under the additional assumption
\psi(q)<1/q
.
Pushing one step further,
1.1
establishes the Khintchine dichotomy for self-similar measures in
arbitrary dimension
, along with a
full Schmidt counting theorem
, i.e without any restriction on
\psi
nor asking solutions of (
1.1
) to be primitive.

Other related topics
. Mahler
[
43
]
also suggested the study of intrinsic Diophantine approximations on fractal sets, i.e. approximation by rationals sitting in the fractal itself. For works in this direction, see e.g.
[
54
,
14
]
. Beside fractal sets, Diophantine approximation on embedded submanifolds has also attracted much attention over the past years, see e.g.
[

,

,

,

]
.

1.1
will be deduced from a dynamical statement which we now present.
Let
G=\SL_{d+1}(\mathbb{R})
, let
\Lambda\subseteq G
be a lattice.
Consider the quotient space
X=G/\Lambda
equipped with the standard Riemannian metric and Haar probability measure
m_{X}
.
For
x\in X
, we write
\inj(x)
the injectivity radius of
x
.
For
l\in\mathbb{N}
, we denote by
B^{\infty}_{\infty,l}(X)
the collection of smooth functions on
X
whose derivatives up to order
l
are bounded, and set
{\mathcal{S}}_{\infty,l}(\cdot)
the associated norm (see
Section

2
for more details on these conventions).
For
t>0
and
\boldsymbol{s}=(s_{1},\cdots,s_{d})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, consider
a(t),u(\boldsymbol{s})\in G
given by

\displaystyle a(t)=\begin{pmatrix}t^{\frac{1}{d+1}}&&&\\
&\ddots&&\\
&&t^{\frac{1}{d+1}}&\\
&&&t^{-\frac{d}{d+1}}\end{pmatrix},\,\,\,\,\,\hskip 11.74988pt\hskip 11.74988ptu(\boldsymbol{s})=\begin{pmatrix}1&&&s_{1}\\
&\ddots&&\vdots\\
&&1&s_{d}\\
&&&1\end{pmatrix}.

We show

Theorem 1.2

(Effective equidistribution of expanding fractals)
.

Let
\sigma
be as in
1.1
. For every
x\in X
,
t>1
,
f\in B^{\infty}_{\infty,l}(X)
, we have

\left\lvert\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f\bigl(a(t)u(\boldsymbol{s})x\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}\sigma(\boldsymbol{s})-m_{X}(f)\right\rvert\leq C{\mathcal{S}}_{\infty,l}(f)\inj(x)^{-1}t^{-c}.

where the constants
C,c>0
only depend on
\Lambda,\sigma
, and
l=\lceil\frac{1}{2}\dim\SO(d+1)\rceil
.

1.2
establishes the exponential equidistribution of the measure
\sigma
, viewed along a unipotent orbit based at an arbitrary point
x
and expanded under the associated diagonal flow. The term
\inj(x)^{-1}
in the error term reflects that equidistribution takes longer when the basepoint
x
is high in a cusp.

The qualitative convergence (i.e. without rate) implied by
1.2
resonates with Khalil-Luethi-Weiss
[
29
]
, which establishes equidistribution under expansion by a broader class of diagonal flows, but at the cost of restricting the IFS to be “carpet”, i.e. rational, with equal contraction ratios, and no rotation part.

From a quantitative point of view,
1.2
can be seen as an effective version of Ratner’s theorem for the multiparameter unipotent flow
(u(\boldsymbol{s}))_{\boldsymbol{s}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}
in the context of fractal measures. The non-fractal case (i.e.
\sigma
is absolutely continuous with respect to Lebesgue) is due to Kleinbock-Margulis, in the broader context of expanding translates of horospherical subgroups
[
35
,
37
]
. Related works in this direction for the non-horospherical case include
[
53
,
15
,
32
,
41
,
57
,
42
]
. In the context of fractal measures, Khalil-Luethi
[
28
]
obtained
1.2
under the additional assumption the point
x
lies in a certain countable set determined by the IFS. They also required the IFS to be rational, contractive, finitely supported, to satisfy the open set condition and has large enough dimension. See also
[
19
]
for a different approach for
d=1
. Provided
d=1
, those constraints were eliminated in our previous work
[
7
]
. We now generalize
[
7
]
to arbitrary dimensions, achieving the theorem in full generality.

The connection between
1.2
and the Khintchine dichotomy in
1.1
comes from
Dani’s philosophy

[
16
]
. Roughly speaking, it claims that the Diophantine properties of a vector
\boldsymbol{s}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
can be read in the dynamics of the trajectory
(a(t)u(\boldsymbol{s})x_{0})_{t>1}
on
\SL_{d+1}(\mathbb{R})/\SL_{d+1}(\mathbb{Z})
, where
x_{0}
is the identity coset. The correspondence is rooted in the simple computation

\displaystyle a(t)u(\boldsymbol{s})(-\boldsymbol{p},q)=(t^{\frac{1}{d+1}}(q\boldsymbol{s}-\boldsymbol{p}),\,t^{-\frac{d}{d+1}}q),

which yields that for every
I\subseteq\mathbb{R},J\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the statement

\exists(\boldsymbol{p},q)\in\mathbb{Z}^{d+1}\,:\,q\in I\text{ and }q\boldsymbol{s}-\boldsymbol{p}\in J

is equivalent to the lattice
a(t)u(\boldsymbol{s})\mathbb{Z}^{d+1}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
intersecting the product set
t^{\frac{1}{d+1}}J\times t^{-\frac{d}{d+1}}I
. Now, identifying
\SL_{d+1}(\mathbb{R})/\SL_{d+1}(\mathbb{Z})
to the space of covolume
1
lattices in
\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
and provided the product set
t^{\frac{1}{d+1}}J\times t^{-\frac{d}{d+1}}I
looks like a ball, the latter condition can be interpreted dynamically, at which point
1.2
can be used. Since Dani’s insight
[
16
]
, many works have exploited this connection, e.g.
[
36
,
34
,
15
,
28
,
7
]
. In
Section

8
, we will show how effective decorrelation of expanding translates (consequence of
1.2
) can be utilized to obtain the divergent case of the Khintchine dichotomy, along with a rate as in Schmidt’s counting theorem. This extends
[
7

which assumed

and restricted counting to primitive solutions, both assumptions being required to deal with
bounded
Siegel transforms. In this paper, we will tackle Siegel transforms which are not even

.

To prove
1.2
, we exploit the self-similarity of

to see that the translate

\lambda

is orientation preserving (i.e.

), write

, and let
\mu
be the probability measure on

given by

(1.3)

\mu=\int_{\Sim(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\delta_{k^{-1}_{\phi}a(\mathtt{r}^{-1}_{\phi})u(\mathtt{b}_{\phi})}\,\mathrm{d}\lambda(\phi).

We show

Theorem 1.3

(Effective equidistribution of the
\mu
-random walk)
.

Let
\lambda
be as in
1.1
and orientation preserving. Let
\mu
be the associated probability measure on
\SL_{d+1}(\mathbb{R})
as in (

).
Then for every

,

and

\left\lvert\mu^{*n}*\delta_{x}(f)-m_{X}(f)\right\rvert\leq C{\mathcal{S}}_{\infty,l}(f)\inj(x)^{-1}e^{-cn}

where the constants
C,c>0
only depend on

, and

.

Let us explain the steps of the proof of
1.3
.
The overall strategy is similar to that in
[
7
]
, which itself is inspired by
[
6
]
. It is done in three phases, each analyzing the dimension of the

[
7
]
, the present higher dimensional setting imposes new difficulties in the second phase, as we explain below.

The first phase is to show that the random walk acquires some (small) positive dimension (
Proposition

5.1
): there exists
A,\kappa>0
depending on
\Lambda,\lambda
such that for any
\rho>0
small,
x,y\in X
, and any

, we have

\mu^{*n}*\delta_{x}(B_{\rho}y)\leq\rho^{\kappa}.

The proof of this statement closely follows the argument in
[
7

, and is based on effective recurrence of the random walk (

4.1
).

In the second phase, we bootstrap the initial dimension

Proposition

6.2
). However, unlike the one-dimensional case in
[

, the multislicing method proposed in

is never satisfied for

6.3
). To resolve this limitation, we promote a mild non-concentration hypothesis (MNC) which also enables the dimensional bootstrapping (

)

\displaystyle k_{g}^{-1}a(\mathtt{r}_{g}^{-1})\in H_{\mu},\hskip 11.74988pt\hskip 11.74988pt\hskip 11.74988ptu(\mathtt{b}_{g})\in H_{\mu}.

6.
Dimensional Bootstrap

In this section, we show that the
n
-step distribution
\mu^{*n}*\delta_{x}
becomes high dimensional in
X
exponentially fast as

goes to infinity. The next definition will be useful.

Definition 6.1

(Robust measure)
.

Let
\alpha>0,\tau\geq 0
and
I\subseteq(0,1]
.
A Borel measure
\nu
on
X
is said to be
(\alpha,{\mathcal{B}}_{I},\tau)
-robust
if
\nu
can be written as the sum of two Borel measures
\nu=\nu^{\prime}+\nu^{\prime\prime}
such that
\nu^{\prime\prime}(X)\leq\tau
, and
\nu^{\prime}
satisfies

(6.1)

\nu^{\prime}\{\inj<\sup I\}=0,

as well as for all
\rho\in I
,
y\in X
,

(6.2)

\nu^{\prime}(B_{\rho}y)\leq\rho^{\alpha\dim X}

If
I
is a singleton
I=\{\rho\}
, we simply say that
\nu
is
(\alpha,{\mathcal{B}}_{\rho},\tau)
-robust.

We aim to show the following.

Proposition 6.2

(High dimension)
.

Let
\kappa\in(0,1/10)
. For
\eta,\rho\lll_{\kappa}1
and
for all
n\ggg_{\kappa}\lvert\log\rho\rvert+\lvert\log\inj(x)\rvert
, the measure
\mu^{*n}*\delta_{x}
is
(1-\kappa,B_{\rho},\rho^{\eta})
-robust.

6.1.
Non-concentration inequalities

The strategy to prove
Proposition

6.2
is to show that convolution by
\mu
(or a suitable power
\mu^{*n}
) improves the dimensional properties of any given Frostman measure
\nu
on
X
. Iterating this phenomenon allows to reach high dimension. The dimensional increment property for random walks is rooted in the following key observation:

\mu^{*n}*\nu(B_{\rho}x)=\int_{G}\nu(g^{-1}B_{\rho}x)\,\,\mathrm{d}\mu^{*n}(g)

and

g^{-1}B_{\rho}x
can be seen, in some exponential chart, as a Euclidean box
\Ad(g^{-1})B^{\mathfrak{g}}_{\rho}
, varying randomly with
g\sim\mu^{*n}
. Provided this random box satisfies suitable non-concentration properties, we can then derive a small dimensional increment via a multislicing theorem (which itself boils down to the sum product phenomenon).

The required non-concentration concerns the partial flag carrying the box.
Let us see what it is in our setting. Consider the weight spaces decomposition
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{g}_{-}\oplus\mathfrak{g}_{0}\oplus\mathfrak{g}_{+}
for
A^{\prime}
.
More precisely,
\mathfrak{g}_{+},\mathfrak{g}_{-}
are respectively the Lie algebras of
U
and
U^{-}
, where
U^{-}
denotes the transpose of
U
, and
\mathfrak{g}_{0}
is their orthogonal complement in
\mathfrak{g}
. Recalling
g=k_{g}^{-1}a(\mathtt{r}_{g}^{-1})u(\mathtt{b}_{g})
and the norm on
\mathfrak{g}
is
\Ad(K^{\prime})
-invariant,
the box
\Ad(g^{-1})B^{\mathfrak{g}}_{\rho}
can be written

\displaystyle\Ad(g^{-1})B^{\mathfrak{g}}_{\rho}

\displaystyle=\Ad(u(-\mathtt{b}_{g}))\Ad(a(\mathtt{r}_{g}))B^{\mathfrak{g}}_{\rho}=\Ad(u(-\mathtt{b}_{g}))\left(B^{\mathfrak{g}_{-}}_{\mathtt{r}^{-1}_{g}\rho}\oplus B^{\mathfrak{g}_{0}}_{\rho}\oplus B^{\mathfrak{g}_{+}}_{\mathtt{r}_{g}\rho}.\right)

(6.3)

\displaystyle\overset{L_{g}}{\simeq}B^{\Ad(u(-\mathtt{b}_{g}))\mathfrak{g}_{-}}_{\mathtt{r}^{-1}_{g}\rho}+B^{\Ad(u(-\mathtt{b}_{g}))\mathfrak{g}_{\leq 0}}_{\rho}+B^{\mathfrak{g}}_{\mathtt{r}_{g}\rho}

where
\mathfrak{g}_{\leq 0}:=\mathfrak{g}_{-}\oplus\mathfrak{g}_{0}
,
L_{g}=O((1+\|\mathtt{b}_{g}\|)^{d+1})
, and the notation
A\overset{L}{\simeq}B
means that
A
can be covered by less than
L
additive translates of
B
, and conversely.
Note the norm
\|\mathtt{b}_{g}\|
is controlled via
Lemma

3.5
(i). We are left to examine the non-concentration properties of the partial flag given by
V_{1}(g):=\Ad(u(-\mathtt{b}_{g}))\mathfrak{g}_{-}
and
V_{2}(g):=\Ad(u(-\mathtt{b}_{g}))\mathfrak{g}_{\leq 0}
as
g
varies with law
\mu^{*n}
.

In
[
7
]
about the case
d=1
(as well as in
[
6
]
), a similar approach is exploited, but the non-concentration at disposal therein is very strong, namely: for
i=1,2
, any subspace
W\subseteq\mathfrak{g}
with
\dim V_{i}+\dim W=\dim\mathfrak{g}
, for
\mu^{*n}
-many
g
, we have
V_{i}(g)\cap W=\{0\}
with a large angle between
V_{i}(g)
and
W
. This non-concentration requirement is natural, as it is the hypothesis of the projection theorems à la Bourgain which are at the heart of the multislicing estimates from
[
6
]
. Unfortunately, such property fails for
d\neq 1
, as we see in the next lemma.

Lemma 6.3

(Obstacle)
.

Assume
d\geq 2
.
Let
W=\{\,M\in\mathfrak{g}\,:\,Me_{1}=0\,\}
be the subspace of matrices in
\M_{d}(\mathbb{R})
with zero trace and null first column. Then
\codim W=d+1>\dim\mathfrak{g}_{-}
but
for every
g\in G
, we have

\Ad(g)\mathfrak{g}_{-}\cap W\neq\{0\}.

Proof.

Observe that

\mathfrak{g}_{-}=\oplus_{j=1}^{d}\mathbb{R}E_{d+1,j}=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&\cdots&0\\
*&*&\cdots\,*&0\end{pmatrix}.

Therefore,
\Ad(g)\mathfrak{g}_{-}
corresponds to the collection of endomorphisms of
\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
which are
2
-step nilpotent and with image in
g\mathbb{R}e_{d+1}
. Consider
m,m^{\prime}\in\Ad(g)\mathfrak{g}_{-}
non colinear. As
me_{1},m^{\prime}e_{1}
are colinear, there must exist
(t,t^{\prime})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\smallsetminus\{0\}
with
(tm+t^{\prime}m^{\prime})e_{1}=0
, whence
tm+t^{\prime}m^{\prime}\in W
. This justifies that any
2
-dimensional subspace of
\Ad(g)\mathfrak{g}_{-}
intersects
W
, whence
\dim(\Ad(g)\mathfrak{g}_{-}\cap W)\geq d-1
.
∎

In this section, we consider an arbitrary
d\geq 1
and show that the random subspaces
V_{1}(g),V_{2}(g)
where
g\sim\mu^{*n}
still satisfy a
weak
form of non-concentration. It is presented as
Proposition

6.4
below. We will explain afterward how this can be utilized to perform the dimensional bootstrap.

Given subspaces
F_{1},\dots,F_{k}\in\Gr(\mathfrak{g})
, we write

\|F_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge F_{k}\|:=\|v_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge v_{k}\|

where
v_{i}\in\bigwedge^{*}\mathfrak{g}
is a unit vector spanning the line
\bigwedge^{\dim F_{i}}F_{i}.

Proposition 6.4

(Mild non-concentration)
.

Let
W\in\Gr(\mathfrak{g},d)
. Then for
n\geq 1
,
r\geq e^{-n}
,

(\mu^{*n})^{\otimes d+1}\left\{(g_{i})_{i=1}^{d+1}\,:\,\|V_{1}(g_{1})\wedge\dots\wedge V_{1}(g_{d+1})\wedge W\|\leq r\right\}\leq Cr^{c}

where
C,c>0
are constants depending on
\mu
only.

Observing
\dim\mathfrak{g}=d(d+2)
,
Proposition

6.4
means that for most parameters
(g_{i})_{i=1}^{d+1}
selected by
(\mu^{*n})^{\otimes d+1}
, we have
\mathfrak{g}=\oplus_{i}V_{1}(g_{i})\bigoplus W
, and each subspace makes a rather large angle with the complementary sum.

We may derive a similar non-concentration property for
V_{2}(g)^{\perp}
as
g\sim\mu^{*n}
.

Corollary 6.5
.

Let
W\in\Gr(\mathfrak{g},d)
. Then for
n\geq 1
,
r\geq e^{-n}
,

(\mu^{*n})^{\otimes d+1}\left\{(g_{i})_{i=1}^{d+1}\,:\,\|V_{2}(g_{1})^{\perp}\wedge\dots\wedge V_{2}(g_{d+1})^{\perp}\wedge W\|\leq r\right\}\leq Cr^{c}

where
C,c>0
are constants depending on
\mu
only.

Proof of
Corollary

6.5
.

Recall that the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{sl}_{d+1}(\mathbb{R})
is equipped with the scalar product given by
\langle A,B\rangle=\tr(A^{T}B)
where
A^{T}
denotes the transpose of the matrix
A
. Using
\tr(AB)=\tr(BA)
, it is direct to check for every
g\in G
, the adjoint of
\Ad(g)\in\End(\mathfrak{g})
for this Euclidean structure is given by
\Ad(g)^{*}=\Ad(g^{T})
. Moreover, the eigenspaces
\mathfrak{g}_{-}
,
\mathfrak{g}_{0}
and
\mathfrak{g}_{+}
are mutually orthogonal. It follows that for
g\in P^{\prime}
, we have

V_{2}(g)^{\perp}=(\Ad(u(-\mathtt{b}_{g}))\mathfrak{g}_{\leq 0})^{\perp}=\Ad(u(\mathtt{b}_{g})^{T})\mathfrak{g}_{+}=\left(\Ad(u(-\mathtt{b}_{g}))\mathfrak{g}_{-}\right)^{T}.

As the map
\mathfrak{g}\mapsto\mathfrak{g},A\mapsto A^{T}
is an isometry, the claim follows from
Proposition

6.4
.
∎

We now focus on establishing
Proposition

6.4
. We first reduce to a purely geometric version of that result.

Proposition 6.6

(Geometric reduction)
.

Let
W\in\Gr(\mathfrak{g},d)
. Then there exists
(u_{i})_{i=1}^{d+1}\in U^{d+1}
such that

\Ad(u_{1})\mathfrak{g}_{-}\oplus\Ad(u_{2})\mathfrak{g}_{-}\oplus\dots\oplus\Ad(u_{d+1})\mathfrak{g}_{-}\oplus W=\mathfrak{g}

Proposition

6.6
is geometric in the sense that no random variable is involved. It turns out to be equivalent to
Proposition

6.4
.

Proof that
Proposition

6.4

\iff

Proposition

6.6
.

The direct implication is clear, therefore we assume
Proposition

6.6
and check
Proposition

6.4
. We write
P(\boldsymbol{s}):=\Ad(u(-\boldsymbol{s}))
for conciseness. Observe that the angle function
(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{d}\rightarrow\bigwedge^{\dim\mathfrak{g}}\mathfrak{g}\simeq\mathbb{R},(\boldsymbol{s}_{i})_{i}\mapsto\lVert P(\boldsymbol{s}_{1})\mathfrak{g}_{-}\wedge\dots\wedge P(\boldsymbol{s}_{d+1})\mathfrak{g}_{-}\wedge W\rVert
is Lipschitz continuous.
In view of
Lemma

2.1
, it suffices to show the existence of constants
C,c>0
depending only on
\sigma
such that for every
r>0
,

\sigma^{\otimes d+1}\left\{(\boldsymbol{s}_{i})_{i=1}^{d+1}\,:\,\|P(\boldsymbol{s}_{1})\mathfrak{g}_{-}\wedge\dots\wedge P(\boldsymbol{s}_{d+1})\mathfrak{g}_{-}\wedge W\|\leq r\right\}\leq Cr^{c}.

Let
v_{-},w\in\bigwedge^{*}\mathfrak{g}
be unit vectors spanning respectively the lines
\bigwedge^{\dim\mathfrak{g}_{-}}\mathfrak{g}_{-}
and
\bigwedge^{\dim W}W
. Note that

(6.4)

\displaystyle\|P(\boldsymbol{s}_{1})\mathfrak{g}_{-}\wedge\dots\wedge P(\boldsymbol{s}_{d+1})\mathfrak{g}_{-}\wedge W\|=\frac{\|P(\boldsymbol{s}_{1})v_{-}\wedge\dots\wedge P(\boldsymbol{s}_{d+1})v_{-}\wedge w\|}{\|P(\boldsymbol{s}_{1})v_{-}\|\dotsm\|P(\boldsymbol{s}_{d+1})v_{-}\|}.

As the map
\boldsymbol{s}\mapsto P(\boldsymbol{s})
is polynomial, and
\sigma
has finite moment of positive order (
Lemma

3.1
), we have for some
\gamma=\gamma(\sigma)>0
,

(6.5)

\displaystyle\sigma^{\otimes d+1}\{\|P(\boldsymbol{s}_{1})v_{-}\|\dotsm\|P(\boldsymbol{s}_{d+1})v_{-}\|\geq r^{-1/2}\}\ll_{\sigma}r^{\gamma}.

On the other hand,
Proposition

6.6
guarantees that the polynomial map
(\boldsymbol{s}_{i})_{i}\mapsto P(\boldsymbol{s}_{1})v_{-}\wedge\dots\wedge P(\boldsymbol{s}_{d+1})v_{-}\wedge w
is non-zero. As it depends continuously on
w
and
\Gr(\mathfrak{g},d)
is compact, it must have the supremum norm on the coefficients bounded below by a constant
c_{d}>0
depending only on
d
.
Combined with
Corollary

3.4
, this yields

(6.6)

\displaystyle\sigma^{\otimes d+1}\{\|P(\boldsymbol{s}_{1})v_{-}\wedge\dotsm\wedge P(\boldsymbol{s}_{d+1})v_{-}\wedge w\|<r^{1/2}\}\ll_{\sigma}r^{\gamma}

up to taking
\gamma
smaller.
Proposition

6.4
follows from the combination of (
6.4
), (
6.5
), (
6.6
).
∎

We further reduce to the case where the subspace
W
is invariant under a Borel subgroup of
G
. We denote by
B
the upper triangular subgroup of
G
.

Proposition 6.7

(Borel-invariant reduction)
.

Let
W\in\Gr(\mathfrak{g},d)
be a subspace which is
\Ad(B)
-invariant. Then there exists
(g_{i})_{i=1}^{d+1}\in G^{d+1}
such that

\Ad(g_{1})\mathfrak{g}_{-}\oplus\Ad(g_{2})\mathfrak{g}_{-}\oplus\dots\oplus\Ad(g_{d+1})\mathfrak{g}_{-}\oplus W=\mathfrak{g}.

Let us check that that
Proposition

6.6
and
Proposition

6.7
are equivalent.

Proof that
Proposition

6.6

\iff

Proposition

6.7
.

The direct implication is clear.
We establish the converse. Assume by contradiction that
Proposition

6.6
fails for some
W\in\Gr(\mathfrak{g},d)
. Write
Z_{G}(A^{\prime})
the centralizer of
A^{\prime}
in
G
. Noting that
\mathfrak{g}_{-}
is
Z_{G}(A^{\prime})U^{-}
-invariant, we obtain for every
(g_{i})_{i}\in(UZ_{G}(A^{\prime})U^{-})^{d+1}
that

(6.7)

\Ad(g_{1})\mathfrak{g}_{-}+\Ad(g_{2})\mathfrak{g}_{-}+\dots+\Ad(g_{d+1})\mathfrak{g}_{-}+W\neq\mathfrak{g}.

This is a Zariski-closed condition in the variable
(g_{i})_{i=1}^{d+1}
.
By looking at Lie algebras, we see that
UZ_{G}(A^{\prime})\supseteq B
, so by Bruhat’s decomposition,
UZ_{G}(A^{\prime})U^{-}
is Zariski-dense in
G
.
It follows that (
6.7
) holds for all
(g_{i})_{i=1}^{d+1}\in G^{d+1}
.
Applying another
\Ad(g)
on both side we see the set

\bigl\{\,W\in\Gr(\mathfrak{g},d)\,:\,\text{\eqref{eq:sumAdgkgW} holds for all }(g_{i})_{i=1}^{d+1}\in G^{d+1}\,\bigr\}

is preserved under the action of
\Ad(G)
, whence of
\Ad(B)
.
It is moreover Zariski-closed. More precisely, it is the set of
\mathbb{R}
-points of a complete
\mathbb{R}
-variety. On the other hand,
B
is the set of
\mathbb{R}
-points of a
\mathbb{R}
-split connected solvable linear algebraic group which acts
\mathbb{R}
-morphically on this variety.
Thus, by a version of the Borel fixed point theorem (
[
11
, Proposition 15.2]
), the above set contains a fixed point for
\Ad(B)
. This contradicts
Proposition

6.7
, thus finishing the proof of the converse implication.
∎

The advantage of reducing to
Proposition

6.7
is that it constrains
W
to belong to a finite explicit family of subspaces.

Lemma 6.8
.

Let
W\subseteq\mathfrak{g}
be a subspace of dimension at most
d
. Then
W
is
\Ad(B)
-invariant if and only if we can write

W=\oplus_{(i,j)\in S}\mathbb{R}E_{i,j}

where
S\subseteq\{(i,j)\,:\,1\leq i<j\leq d+1\}
and
S
is stable by the operations
(i,j)\mapsto(i-1,j)
and
(i,j)\mapsto(i,j+1)
(provided
i\geq 2
and
j\leq d
respectively).

In words,
W
must be a sum of elementary subspaces that are strictly above the diagonal, and stable by moving upward or to the right in the matrix representation.

Proof.

Recall
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{sl}_{d+1}
. Write
\mathfrak{b}
the Lie algebra of
B
, i.e. the subspace of upper triangular matrices in
\mathfrak{g}
. Note that
W
is
\Ad(B)
-invariant if and only if it is
\ad(\mathfrak{b})
-invariant.
Observe the relation
[E_{i,j},E_{k,l}]={\mathbbm{1}}_{j=k}E_{i,l}-{\mathbbm{1}}_{i=l}E_{k,j}
for all
i,j,k,l\in\{1,\dots,d+1\}
.
In particular, for
i<j
and
k<l
, the matrix
[E_{i,j},E_{k,l}]
is either
0
or up to a sign an elementary matrix located either to the right or above
E_{i,j}
. This justifies the “if” direction in
Lemma

6.8
.

We now assume
W
to be
\Ad(B)
-invariant and establish the announced decomposition. By invariance under diagonal matrices,
W
must be of the form
W=E\bigoplus\oplus_{(i,j)\in S}\mathbb{R}E_{i,j}
where
E
is a subspace of diagonal matrices, and
S
does not intersect the diagonal. If
E\neq\{0\}
, then by
\ad(\mathfrak{b})
-invariance,
W
must contain a line
\mathbb{R}E_{i,i+1}
for some
i\in\{1,\dots,d\}
. But the
\ad(\mathfrak{b})
-invariant subspace spanned by such a line has dimension at least
d
, which is absurd because
\dim W\leq d
. Hence
E=\{0\}
. Noting that for every
i>j
, the
\ad(\mathfrak{b})
-invariant subspace spanned by
\mathbb{R}E_{i,j}
intersects the diagonal subspace, we further deduce
S\subseteq\{(i,j)\,:\,1\leq i<j\leq d+1\}
. The final claim on
S
follows from
\ad(\mathfrak{b})
-invariance and the bracket relation exhibited in the first paragraph.
∎

We are finally able to show
Proposition

6.7
, thus completing the proofs of Propositions
6.4
,
6.6
.

Proof of
Proposition

6.7
.

We shall prove this proposition by induction on
d
.

Base case
d=1
. Here
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
and
\mathfrak{g}_{-}=\mathbb{R}E_{21}
. By assumption,

W=\mathbb{R}E_{12}.

Take
g_{1}=\Id,g_{2}=\Id+E_{12}\in\SL_{2}(\mathbb{R})
. By direct computation, we can verify that

\Ad(g_{1})\mathfrak{g}_{-}\oplus\Ad(g_{2})\mathfrak{g}_{-}\oplus W=\mathfrak{g}.

Induction step.
Let
d\geq 2
be an integer. We assume the proposition has been proved for
\SL_{d}(\mathbb{R})
and establish it for
\SL_{d+1}(\mathbb{R})
. Throughout the proof, we write
M_{d+1}
the space of all
d+1
by
d+1
real matrices. We keep the notations
G,\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{g}_{-}
related to
\SL_{d+1}(\mathbb{R})
. We write
G^{\prime}=\SL_{d}(\mathbb{R})
, which we view as a subgroup of
G
by embedding it in the lower-right corner (and imposing
1
on the first diagonal entry). Accordingly, we define
\mathfrak{g}^{\prime}
(resp.
\mathfrak{g}_{-}^{\prime}
) to be the intersection of
\mathfrak{g}
(resp.
\mathfrak{g}_{-}
) with the lower-right
d
by
d
block of
M_{d+1}
. In particular,

\mathfrak{g}_{-}^{\prime}=\mathbb{R}E_{d+1,2}\oplus\cdots\oplus\mathbb{R}E_{d+1,d}.

We denote by
\Proj_{\mathfrak{g}^{\prime}}:M_{d+1}\to M_{d+1}
the projection onto the lower right
d
by
d
block, and by
\Proj_{R_{1}}:M_{d+1}\to M_{d+1}
the projection onto the the subspace of matrices with nonzero entries only on the first row.

Let
W\subseteq\mathfrak{g}
be a
d
-dimensional linear subspace that is
\Ad(B)
-invariant. To make use of the induction hypothesis, our strategy is to choose a suitable element
g_{0}\in G
such that
\Ad(g_{0})\mathfrak{g}_{-}\oplus W
fills up the first row of
\mathfrak{g}
. This will enable us to work on
\mathfrak{g}^{\prime}
and apply the induction hypothesis.

To begin with, we let
k=\dim\Proj_{R_{1}}(W)\geq 1
. We choose

g_{0}=(\Id+E_{d+1,1})\cdot\omega_{1,d+1}.

where
\omega_{1,d+1}
is an element in the standard Weyl group of
G
satisfying that left multiplication by
\omega_{1,d+1}
exchanges the first and
(d+1)
-th row.
Then by direct computation, we have

\displaystyle\Ad(g_{0})\mathfrak{g}_{-}=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}a_{1}&a_{2}&\cdots&a_{d}&-a_{1}\\
0&0&\cdots&0&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots\\
0&0&\cdots&0&0\\
a_{1}&a_{2}&\cdots&a_{d}&-a_{1}\end{pmatrix}:a_{1},\cdots,a_{d}\in\mathbb{R}\right\}.

Decompose
\mathfrak{g}_{-}=V_{0}\oplus V_{1}
, where
V_{0},V_{1}
are linear subspaces defined by

\displaystyle V_{0}=\mathbb{R}E_{d+1,1}\oplus\mathbb{R}E_{d+1,2}\oplus\cdots\oplus\mathbb{R}E_{d+1,d+1-k};

\displaystyle V_{1}=\mathbb{R}E_{d+1,d+2-k}\oplus\cdots\oplus\mathbb{R}E_{d+1,d}.

Using
Lemma

6.8
, we also decompose
W=\Proj_{R_{1}}(W)\oplus\mathrm{Proj_{\mathfrak{g}^{\prime}}}(W)
. Then we have

(6.8)

\displaystyle\Ad(g_{0})\mathfrak{g}_{-}\oplus W=\Ad(g_{0})V_{0}\oplus\mathrm{Proj_{R_{1}}(W)}\oplus V_{1}\oplus\mathrm{Proj_{\mathfrak{g}^{\prime}}(W)}.

Here we used that
V_{1}
and
\Ad(g_{0})V_{1}
coincide modulo
W
. Let

W^{\prime}=V_{1}\oplus\mathrm{Proj_{\mathfrak{g}^{\prime}}(W)}.

Note that
W^{\prime}\subseteq\mathfrak{g}^{\prime}
and

\dim W^{\prime}=k-1+d-k=d-1.

Hence, we can apply the induction hypothesis (more precisely its equivalent version from
Proposition

6.6
) to the pair
(G^{\prime},W^{\prime})
to obtain
g^{\prime}_{1},\cdots,g^{\prime}_{d}\in G^{\prime}
such that

(6.9)

\displaystyle\Ad(g^{\prime}_{1})\mathfrak{g}^{\prime}_{-}\oplus\Ad(g^{\prime}_{2})\mathfrak{g}^{\prime}_{-}\oplus\cdots\oplus\Ad(g^{\prime}_{d})\mathfrak{g}^{\prime}_{-}\oplus W^{\prime}=\mathfrak{g}^{\prime}.

Let
C\subseteq\mathfrak{g}
be the linear subspace defined by

C=\mathbb{R}E_{2,1}\oplus\mathbb{R}E_{3,1}\oplus\cdots\oplus\mathbb{R}E_{d+1,1}.

Observe that
C
is
\Ad(G^{\prime})
-invariant, and the adjoint representation of
G^{\prime}
on
C
is isomorphic to the standard representation
\mathbb{R}^{d}
of
G^{\prime}=\SL_{d}(\mathbb{R})
. Therefore, we may find
g_{1}^{\prime\prime},g_{2}^{\prime\prime},\cdots,g_{d}^{\prime\prime}\in G^{\prime}
such that

(6.10)

\displaystyle\Ad(g_{1}^{\prime\prime})\mathbb{R}E_{d+1,1}\oplus\Ad(g_{2}^{\prime\prime})\mathbb{R}E_{d+1,1}\oplus\cdots\oplus\Ad(g_{d}^{\prime\prime})\mathbb{R}E_{d+1,1}=C.

Observe that the collection of elements
(g^{\prime}_{i})_{i=1}^{d}
and
(g^{\prime\prime}_{i})_{i=1}^{d}
satisfying respectively
(
6.9
) and (
6.10
) are (non-empty) Zariski-open subsets of
G^{\prime d}
. By irreducibility of
G^{\prime d}
for the Zariski topology, they are dense, whence must intersect. This allows to choose
(g^{\prime}_{1},\cdots,g^{\prime}_{d})=(g^{\prime\prime}_{1},\cdots,g^{\prime\prime}_{d})
in (
6.9
) and (
6.10
). As

\mathfrak{g}_{-}=\mathfrak{g}^{\prime}_{-}\oplus\mathbb{R}E_{d+1,1},

we obtain

(6.11)

\displaystyle\Ad(g^{\prime}_{1})\mathfrak{g}_{-}\oplus\Ad(g^{\prime}_{2})\mathfrak{g}_{-}\oplus\cdots\oplus\Ad(g^{\prime}_{d})\mathfrak{g}_{-}\oplus W^{\prime}=\mathfrak{g}^{\prime}\oplus C.

On the other hand, observing that the restriction of
\Proj_{R_{1}}
to
\Ad(g_{0})V_{0}\oplus\mathrm{Proj_{R_{1}}}(W)
is injective while its restriction to
\mathfrak{g}^{\prime}\oplus C
vanishes, we have
(\Ad(g_{0})V_{0}\oplus\mathrm{Proj_{R_{1}}}(W))\cap(\mathfrak{g}^{\prime}\oplus C)=\{0\}
, or equivalently

(6.12)

\displaystyle\Ad(g_{0})V_{0}\oplus\mathrm{Proj_{R_{1}}}(W)\oplus\mathfrak{g}^{\prime}\oplus C=\mathfrak{g}

because dimensions match. Combining (
6.8
), (
6.11
), (
6.12
), we obtain

\Ad(g_{0})\mathfrak{g}_{-}\oplus\Ad(g^{\prime}_{1})\mathfrak{g}_{-}\oplus\Ad(g^{\prime}_{2})\mathfrak{g}_{-}\oplus\cdots\oplus\Ad(g^{\prime}_{d})\mathfrak{g}_{-}\oplus W=\mathfrak{g}.

This validates the induction step and completes the proof.
∎

6.2.
Linear multislicing

It remains to see how the non-concentration property established for the subspace
\Ad(u(-\mathtt{b}_{g})\mathfrak{g}^{-})
in the previous subsection can be exploited to obtain a dimensional gain. In this subsection, we study this question in an abstract linear setting. We place ourselves in
\mathbb{R}^{D}
where
D\geq 2
. We encapsulate the non-concentration property via the following definition.

Definition 6.9
.

Let
k\in\llbracket 1,D-1\rrbracket
, let
C,c,\rho>0
.
Let
\Xi
be a probability measure on
\Gr(\mathbb{R}^{D},k)
. We say
\Xi
satisfies the
mild non-concentration property

\MNC
with parameters
(\rho,C,c)
if there exist integers
q,m\in\mathbb{N}
such that
D=qk+m
and for every
W\in\Gr(\mathbb{R}^{D},m)
,
r\geq\rho
,

\Xi^{\otimes q}\left\{(F_{i})\,:\,\|F_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge F_{q}\wedge W\|\leq r\right\}\leq Cr^{c}.

We also say
\Xi
satisfies
\MNC^{\perp}
with parameters
(\rho,C,c)
if its image under
F\mapsto F^{\perp}
satisfies
\MNC
with parameters
(\rho,C,c)
.

Our aim is to show that
\MNC
or
\MNC^{\perp}
allow for a
supercritical multislicing estimate
, see
Proposition

6.14
. For that, we first present a submodular inequality for covering numbers (
Lemma

6.10
) and use it to connect
\MNC
and
\MNC^{\perp}
with the properties of the individual projectors
\pi_{F_{i}}
(
Lemma

6.11
). We then deduce that a random subspace
F
whose law
\Xi
has the property
\MNC
or
\MNC^{\perp}
must enjoy both supercritical and subcritical projection theorems (Lemmas
6.12
,
6.13
). Those estimates refine that of Bourgain
[
13
]
and He
[
26
]
which were established under the stronger non-concentration condition that
\Xi
satisfies
\MNC
with
q=1
. From there, we use
[
6
]
to combine our projection theorems into the multislicing estimate
Proposition

6.14

We now introduce the submodular inequality for covering numbers that we need. Let
{\mathcal{P}}
and
{\mathcal{Q}}
denote partitions of
\mathbb{R}^{D}
, let
A
be a subset of

Lemma 6.10

(Submodular inequality)
.

Let

, and

Assume that

\displaystyle{\mathcal{N}}_{\mathcal{P}}(A){\mathcal{N}}_{\mathcal{Q}}(A)\geq\frac{c^{2}}{4L^{3}}{\mathcal{N}}_{\mathcal{R}}(A){\mathcal{N}}_{{\mathcal{S}}}(A^{\prime}).

Proof.

We start with a few general observations on the relation

Now consider

8.
Quantitative Khintchine dichotomy in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
from equidistribution

We show that an arbitrary probability measure
\xi
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
obeys the Khintchine dichotomy provided that the pushfoward

satisfies certain effective equidistribution properties in
\SL_{d+1}(\mathbb{R})/\SL_{d+1}(\mathbb{Z})
for large

. Combined with
Proposition

7.3
, this yields
1.1
.

Throughout the section, notations refer to
Section

2
, and we further specify
\Lambda=\SL_{d+1}(\mathbb{Z})
, in particular
X=\SL_{d+1}(\mathbb{R})/\SL_{d+1}(\mathbb{Z})
.
We also set
x_{0}=\Lambda/\Lambda\in X
.

Definition 8.1
.

Let
\xi
be a probability measure on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
We say that
\xi
satisfies the
effective single equidistribution property on
X
if there are constants
C,c>0
and
l\in\mathbb{N}
such that

(8.1)

\forall f\in B^{\infty}_{\infty,l}(X),\,\forall t>1,\\
\left\lvert\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f\bigl(a(t)u(\boldsymbol{s})x_{0}\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}\xi(\boldsymbol{s})-m_{X}(f)\right\rvert\leq C{\mathcal{S}}_{\infty,l}(f)t^{-c}.

We say that
\xi
satisfies the
effective double equidistribution property on
X
if for every
\eta>0
, there are constants
C,c>0
and
l\in\mathbb{N}
such that

(8.2)

\forall f_{1},f_{2}\in B^{\infty}_{\infty,l}(X),\,\forall t_{1}>1,\,\forall t_{2}>t_{1}^{1+\eta},\\
\Delta^{\xi}_{f_{1},f_{2}}(t_{1},t_{2})\leq C{\mathcal{S}}_{\infty,l}(f_{1})\lvert m_{X}(f_{2})\rvert t_{1}^{-c}+C{\mathcal{S}}_{\infty,l}(f_{1}){\mathcal{S}}_{\infty,l}(f_{2})t_{2}^{-c}.

where the notation
\Delta^{\xi}_{f_{1},f_{2}}(t_{1},t_{2})
is defined in (
7.8
).

Taking
f_{2}=1
and
t_{2}\to+\infty
, we see that effective double equidistribution (
8.2
) implies effective single equidistribution (
8.1
). Note that (
8.2
) assumes some separation
t_{2}>t_{1}^{1+\eta}
between
t_{1}
and
t_{2}
. As it turns out, (
8.2
) (with small enough
\eta
) can in fact be automatically upgraded to the following full range estimate:
there exist (potentially different) constants
C,c>0
,
l\in\mathbb{N}
, such that for every
f_{1},f_{2}\in B^{\infty}_{\infty,l}(X)
and all
t_{2}\geq t_{1}>1
.

(8.3)

\Delta^{\sigma}_{f_{1},f_{2}}(t_{1},t_{2})\leq C{\mathcal{S}}_{2,l}(f_{1}){\mathcal{S}}_{2,l}(f_{2})t_{1}^{c}t_{2}^{-c}\\
+C{\mathcal{S}}_{\infty,l}(f_{1})\lvert m_{X}(f_{2})\rvert t_{1}^{-c}+C{\mathcal{S}}_{\infty,l}(f_{1}){\mathcal{S}}_{\infty,l}(f_{2})t_{2}^{-c}.

The implication from (
8.2
) to (

) (again, parameters

may differ) is explained in
[
7
, Section 7.1]
. The idea is that (

) implies single equidistribution, which in turn, by decay of matrix coefficients, yields effective double equidistribution in the short range regime

for sufficiently small

.

The following result of Khalil-Luethi

guarantees that effective single equidistribution implies the convergent case of the Khintchine dichotomy.

Theorem 8.2

(Convergent case

Let

Lemma 8.6

Let

Lemma 8.13

Let

\sum_{k\in\Ks(n)}\varphi^{+}_{k}(a(t_{k})u(\boldsymbol{s})x_{0})\leq(\tau-1+C(d)\varepsilon)\sum_{k\in\Ks(n)}\psi(\tau^{k})^{d}\tau^{k}.

where
C(d)>0
is a constant depending on
d
only.
The estimate (
8.28
) follows mutatis mutandis from the argument establishing the lower bound for partial sums over
\Ks
, in which we replace
R_{k}^{-}
by
P_{k}^{+}
.

We have thus established
Lemma

8.16
, whence the asymptotic upper bound

\limsup_{N\to+\infty}\frac{\mathscr{T}_{N}(\boldsymbol{s})}{\sum_{q=1}^{N}\psi(q)^{d}}\leq 1

of
8.3
(case
d\geq 2
).

8.3.
The case
d=1
.

It remains to establish
8.3
in the case where
d=1
. The proof is similar to the higher dimensional case but a certain number of refinements are required due to poorer moment estimates for Siegel transforms.

Let us start with the
lower bound
. Keep the notations
\tau
,
R_{k}
,
\gamma_{1}
,
\Kb
,
\Ks
,
\gamma_{2}
,
\chi_{k}
,
\varepsilon
,
R_{k}^{-}
,
\theta
, from §
8.1
. The asymptotic lower bound for
\sum_{k\in\Kb(n)}\mathscr{S}_{k}(\boldsymbol{s})
given in
Lemma

8.7

is still valid because the argument works without restriction on
d
.

We thus focus on the lower asymptotic for
\sum_{k\in\Ks(n)}\mathscr{S}_{k}(\boldsymbol{s})
, more precisely on extending
Corollary

8.15
to the case
d=1
. The difference with the higher dimensional case is that
the Siegel transform of a ball in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
does not have finite second moment
, in particular (
8.11
) is not valid anymore. To deal with this obstacle, we restrict the Siegel transform by counting only lattice points
(p,q)
which are bounded multiple of a primitive point. Namely, given
m>0
, we set

{\mathcal{P}}^{(m)}(\mathbb{Z}^{2}):=\{(p,q)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}\smallsetminus\{0\}\,:\,\GCD(p,q)\leq m\}

where
\GCD(p,q)\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 1}
denotes the greatest common divisor of
p
and
q
.
Given a measurable function
f:\mathbb{R}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
, we define its restricted Siegel transform
\widetilde{f}^{(m)}:X\rightarrow[0,+\infty]
by:
\forall g\in G
,

\widetilde{f}^{(m)}(gx_{0}):=\sum_{v\in{\mathcal{P}}^{(m)}(\mathbb{Z}^{2})}f(gv).

In this context, we have the following moment estimates. Their vocation is to replace
8.8
from the higher dimensional case.

Proposition 8.17

(Moments of restricted Siegel transforms)
.

Let
m\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 1}
, let
k\in\Ks
. Let
c_{m}:=\zeta(2)^{-1}\sum_{t=1}^{m}t^{-2}
and let
R\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a rectangle such as
R_{k}^{-}
here and
P_{k}^{+}
further below.
We have

(8.29)

\displaystyle\int_{X}\widetilde{{\mathbbm{1}}}^{(m)}_{R}\,\mathrm{d}m_{X}=c_{m}\Leb(R)

(8.30)

\displaystyle\int_{X}(\widetilde{{\mathbbm{1}}}^{(m)}_{R})^{2}\,\mathrm{d}m_{X}=c_{m}^{2}\Leb(R)^{2}+O\left(\Leb(R)\log(1+m)\right).

Proof.

Those estimates appear in the litterature for primitive Siegel transforms, i.e. for
m=1
. We explain how to deduce the case
m\geq 1
. Note that

\widetilde{{\mathbbm{1}}}^{(m)}_{R}=\sum_{q\in\llbracket 1,m\rrbracket}\widetilde{{\mathbbm{1}}}^{(1)}_{R/q}.

The Siegel summation formula for primitive Siegel transform
[
50
, Equation (
8
)]
guarantees
\int_{X}\widetilde{{\mathbbm{1}}}^{(1)}_{R/q}\,\mathrm{d}m_{X}=\zeta(2)^{-1}\Leb(R/q)
, whence (
8.29
).

To justify (
8.30
), we note that

\displaystyle\int_{X}(\widetilde{{\mathbbm{1}}}^{(m)}_{R})^{2}\,\mathrm{d}m_{X}=\sum_{q,q^{\prime}\in\llbracket 1,m\rrbracket}\int_{X}\widetilde{{\mathbbm{1}}}^{(1)}_{R/q}\widetilde{{\mathbbm{1}}}^{(1)}_{R/q^{\prime}}\,\mathrm{d}m_{X}.

By Rogers
[
48
, Theorem 5]
, we have

\displaystyle\int_{X}\widetilde{{\mathbbm{1}}}^{(1)}_{R/q}\widetilde{{\mathbbm{1}}}^{(1)}_{R/q^{\prime}}\,\mathrm{d}m_{X}

\displaystyle=\zeta(2)^{-2}q^{-2}q^{\prime-2}\Leb(R)^{2}+\zeta(2)^{-1}\Leb(R/q\cap R/q^{\prime})+\zeta(2)^{-1}\Leb(R/q\cap(-R/q^{\prime})).

Summing over
q,q^{\prime}\in\llbracket 1,m\rrbracket
the first term of the right hand side gives

\sum_{q,q^{\prime}\in\llbracket 1,m\rrbracket}\zeta(2)^{-2}q^{-2}q^{\prime-2}\Leb(R)^{2}=c_{m}^{2}\Leb(R)^{2}.

On the other hand, looking at the second coordinate of points in
R
, we find for any
q\geq 1
,

|\{q^{\prime}\geq 1\,:\,R/q\cap R/q^{\prime}\neq\varnothing\}|\ll q

Using the trivial bound
\Leb(R/q\cap R/q^{\prime})\leq\Leb(R)/q^{2}
, we deduce

\sum_{q,q^{\prime}\in\llbracket 1,m\rrbracket}\Leb(R/q\cap R/q^{\prime})\ll\sum_{q\in\llbracket 1,m\rrbracket}\Leb(R)/q\simeq\Leb(R)\log(1+m).

As
R/q\cap(-R/q^{\prime})=\varnothing
, the last term does not contribute, and this concludes the proof of (
8.30
).
∎

From there, the proof of the higher dimensional case goes through with a few adaptations.
Let
(m_{k})_{k\in\Ks}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{\Ks}
be the sequence satisfying

(8.31)

\forall k\in\Ks,\hskip 11.74988pt\log(1+m_{k})=\Bigl(\sum_{j\in\Ks(k)}\psi(\tau^{j})\tau^{j}\Bigr)^{1/8}.

Set
\varphi_{k}:=\theta*(\chi_{k}\widetilde{{\mathbbm{1}}}^{(m_{k})}_{R_{k}^{-}})
where
\chi_{k}
is as in (
8.9
),
and define positive constants

y_{k}:=m_{X}(\varphi_{k}),\hskip 23.49976pty^{\prime}_{k}:=m_{X}(\varphi_{k})\log(1+m_{k}).

Noting that by hypothesis, we have
\lim_{k}m_{k}=+\infty
as
k
tends to infinity along
\Ks
, we find that
y_{k}\leq y^{\prime}_{k}
for all large
k\in\Ks
.

Replacing
8.8
by
Proposition

8.17
in the proof of
Lemma

8.10
, we obtain the following.

Lemma 8.18
.

If
\gamma_{1}\lll\gamma_{2}
, then for some
M=M(d)>1
, every
k\in\Ks
, we have

(8.32)

\displaystyle y_{k}=c_{m_{k}}\Leb(R_{k}^{-})-O(t_{k}^{-\gamma_{2}/M}),

\displaystyle{\mathcal{S}}_{\infty,l}(\varphi_{k})\ll t_{k}^{M\gamma_{2}},

\displaystyle{\mathcal{S}}_{2,l}(\varphi_{k})\ll y_{k}+\sqrt{y^{\prime}_{k}}+O(t_{k}^{-\gamma_{2}/M}).

Next, writing for
\boldsymbol{s}\in\mathbb{R}
,

Y_{k}(\boldsymbol{s})=\varphi_{k}\bigl(a(t_{k})u(\boldsymbol{s})x_{0}\bigr),\hskip 23.49976ptZ_{k}(\boldsymbol{s})=Y_{k}(\boldsymbol{s})-y_{k},

Lemma

8.11
becomes (with a similar proof) the following.

Lemma 8.19
.

Assume
\gamma_{1}\lll\gamma_{2}\lll 1
. Then for every
k\in\Ks
such that
y^{\prime}_{k}\geq 1
, we have

\mathbb{E}[Z_{k}^{2}]\ll y^{\prime}_{k}.

We deduce the following replacement for
Proposition

8.12
.

Proposition 8.20
.

Assume
\gamma_{1}\lll\gamma_{2}\lll 1
and

\psi(q)\geq q^{-1}\log^{-2}(q),\hskip 11.74988pt\forall q\in\Ks.

Then for every finite subset
J\subseteq\Ks
with
\inf J\ggg_{\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}}1
, we have

\mathbb{E}\bigl[Z_{J}^{2}\bigr]\ll\left(1+y^{\prime}_{J}\right)^{3/2}.

We recall that
Z_{J}=\sum_{j\in J}Z_{j}
and
y^{\prime}_{J}=\sum_{j\in J}y^{\prime}_{j}
. Similar notations
Y_{J},y_{J}
will be used below.

Proof.

Same as for
Proposition

8.12
but using
y_{j}^{\prime}
to define the partition
J=J_{1}\sqcup J_{2}\sqcup J_{3}
, and noting that all the upper bounds in the proof of
Proposition

8.12
are valid with
y_{j}^{\prime}
at the place of
y_{j}
thanks to Lemmas
8.18
,
8.19
, and the inequality
y_{j}^{\prime}\geq y_{j}
(which is valid thanks to the assumption
\inf J\ggg_{\gamma_{1}}1
).
∎

We can now combine
Proposition

8.20
and
Lemma

8.13
(note here that we allow
(y^{\prime}_{j})
to be different from
(y_{j})
in the latter) to obtain the following.

Lemma 8.21
.

For
\xi
-almost every
\boldsymbol{s}\in\mathbb{R}
, for large enough
n
, we have

(8.33)

\lvert Z_{\Ks(n)}(\boldsymbol{s})\rvert\leq\left(y^{\prime}_{\Ks(n)}\right)^{4/5}.

We now claim that the right-hand side in (
8.33
) is negligible compared to
y_{\Ks(n)}
.
To see why, note first that by definition, the sequence
(m_{k})_{k\in\Ks}
is non-decreasing, therefore

(8.34)

y^{\prime}_{\Ks(n)}\leq y_{\Ks(n)}\log(1+m_{\max\Ks(n)}).

Moreover, by (
8.7
), (
8.32
), we have
\psi(\tau^{k})\tau^{k}\ll y_{k}+t^{-\gamma_{2}/M}_{k}
, so using the definition of
m_{k}
, we get

(8.35)

\forall k\in\Ks,\hskip 11.74988pt\log(1+m_{k})\ll\left(y_{\Ks(k)}+O_{\gamma_{2}}(1)\right)^{1/8}.

Equations (
8.34
) and (
8.35
) together justify the claim.

Lemma

8.21
and the above claim yield in particular that for
\xi
-almost every
\boldsymbol{s}\in\mathbb{R}
, for sufficiently large
n
,

Y_{\Ks(n)}(\boldsymbol{s})\geq(1-\varepsilon)y_{\Ks(n)}.

By construction, we know that
\mathscr{S}_{k}(\boldsymbol{s})\geq Y_{k}(\boldsymbol{s})
. On the other hand, it follows from (
8.32
) that
y_{\Ks(n)}\geq\sum_{k\in\Ks(n)}c_{m_{k}}\Leb(R_{k}^{-})-O_{\gamma_{2}}(1)
, where
c_{m_{k}}\to_{k}1
as
k
goes to infinity along
\Ks
. Using (
8.7
) to see that
\Leb(R_{k}^{-})=(1-\tau^{-1}-2\varepsilon)(1-2\varepsilon)\psi(\tau^{k})\tau^{k}
, we infer that for
\xi
-almost every
\boldsymbol{s}\in\mathbb{R}
, for all large enough
n
,

\sum_{k\in\Ks(n)}\mathscr{S}_{k}(\boldsymbol{s})\geq(1-\tau^{-1}-6\varepsilon)\sum_{k\in\Ks(n)}\psi(\tau^{k})\tau^{k}.

This concludes the proof of the lower bound.

Let us now justify the
upper bound
in the case
d=1
. Similarly to the higher dimensional case, we can mimic the proof of the lower bound estimate in the case
d=1
to establish an upper bound estimate. However, this upper bound concerns the restricted Siegel transforms which are used in the proof, while we aim for an upper bound without restriction on
\GCD(p,q)
when counting solutions
(p,q)
of the Khintchine inequality. We explain below how to deal with this obstacle.

We keep the notations of §
8.2
, in particular we fix
\tau\in(1,2]
, and consider for
k\geq 0
,
\boldsymbol{s}\in\mathbb{R}
,

\displaystyle\mathscr{S}_{k}^{+}(\boldsymbol{s})

\displaystyle=\big|\{(p,q)\in\mathbb{Z}\times\llbracket\tau^{k},\tau^{k+1}\llbracket\,:\,0\leq q\boldsymbol{s}-p<\psi(\tau^{k})\}\big|

\displaystyle=\widetilde{{\mathbbm{1}}}_{P_{k}}\bigl(a(t_{k})u(\boldsymbol{s})x_{0}\bigr).

where
P_{k}=[0,r_{k})\times[r_{k},\tau r_{k})
.
The goal is to show
Lemma

8.16
when
d=1
. Provided
\sum_{k\in\Kb}\psi(\tau^{k})\tau^{k}=+\infty
, the argument for
Lemma

8.7
yields the desired upper bound for the partial sums
\sum_{\Kb(n)}\mathscr{S}_{k}^{+}(\boldsymbol{s})
.
Therefore we only need to deal with
\sum_{\Ks(n)}\mathscr{S}_{k}^{+}(\boldsymbol{s})
, and under the assumption
\sum_{k\in\Ks}\psi(\tau^{k})\tau^{k}=+\infty
(as noted for
Lemma

8.16
).

Recall from §
8.2
that
\varepsilon>0
is an arbitrarily small number and
P_{k}^{+}
denotes the
\varepsilon r_{k}
-thickening of
P_{k}
.
Define for
k\in\Ks
and
m>0
,

\varphi^{+(m)}_{k}=\theta*(\chi_{k}\widetilde{{\mathbbm{1}}}^{(m)}_{P_{k}^{+}})\hskip 11.74988pt\text{and}\hskip 11.74988ptY^{+(m)}_{k}(\boldsymbol{s})=\varphi^{+(m)}_{k}\bigl(a(t_{k})u(\boldsymbol{s})x_{0}\bigr).

Let
(m_{k})_{k\in\Ks}
be as in (
8.31
). We use the shorthand
\varphi^{+}_{k}:=\varphi^{+(m_{k})}_{k}
,
Y^{+}_{k}:=Y^{+(m_{k})}_{k}
. The argument used for the lower bound (case
d=1
) then shows that, provided
\gamma_{1}\lll 1
, we have for
\xi
-almost every
\boldsymbol{s}\in\mathbb{R}
, for large enough
n
,

(8.36)

\sum_{k\in\Ks(n)}Y^{+}_{k}(\boldsymbol{s})\leq(\tau-1+10\varepsilon)\sum_{k\in\Ks(n)}\psi(\tau^{k})\tau^{k}.

We now compare the left hand side of (
8.36
) with
\sum_{k\in\Ks(n)}\mathscr{S}_{k}(\boldsymbol{s})
. In other terms, we need to show that the truncation of the cusp induced by
\chi_{k}
, and most importantly the reduction of the Siegel transform to counting
m_{k}
-primitive lattice points does not affect too much the asymptotic of the partial sums. The next lemma is a first step to replace
m_{k}
by a term
m^{\prime}_{k}
which grows exponentially in
k
.

Lemma 8.22
.

Let
m^{\prime}_{k}:=\max\{m_{k},t_{k}^{\gamma_{2}}\}
.
Provided that
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}\lll 1
, we have for every
k\in\Ks
,

\mathbb{E}\bigl[Y^{+(m^{\prime}_{k})}_{k}-Y^{+}_{k}\bigr]\ll r_{k}^{2}m^{-1}_{k}+t_{k}^{-\gamma_{2}}.

Proof.

We may assume
m_{k}\leq t_{k}^{\gamma_{2}}
.
Unfolding definitions, then using effective single equidistribution (
8.1
) while noting that
{\mathcal{S}}_{\infty,l}(\varphi_{k}^{+(m^{\prime}_{k})}-\varphi_{k}^{+})\ll t_{k}^{M\gamma_{2}}
for some
M
as in
Lemma

8.18
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigl[Y^{+(m^{\prime}_{k})}_{k}-Y^{+}_{k}\bigr]

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\theta*[\chi_{k}(\widetilde{{\mathbbm{1}}}^{(m^{\prime}_{k})}_{P_{k}^{+}}-\widetilde{{\mathbbm{1}}}^{(m_{k})}_{P_{k}^{+}})]\bigl(a(t_{k})u(\boldsymbol{s})x_{0}\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}\xi(\boldsymbol{s})

\displaystyle\ll m_{X}(\widetilde{{\mathbbm{1}}}_{P_{k}^{+}}-\widetilde{{\mathbbm{1}}}^{(m_{k})}_{P_{k}^{+}})\,+\,t_{k}^{-c+M\gamma_{2}}

\displaystyle\ll r_{k}^{2}m^{-1}_{k}+t_{k}^{-\gamma_{2}},

where the last inequality uses (
8.29
), the definition of
c_{m_{k}}
and assumes
\gamma_{2},\gamma_{1}
small enough depending on
c
.
∎

We deduce that (
8.36
) is still valid for
Y^{+(m^{\prime}_{k})}_{k}
in the place of
Y^{+}_{k}
.

Lemma 8.23
.

For
\xi
-almost every
\boldsymbol{s}\in\mathbb{R}
, for large enough
n
, we have

\sum_{k\in\Ks(n)}Y^{+(m^{\prime}_{k})}_{k}(\boldsymbol{s})\leq(\tau-1+11\varepsilon)\sum_{k\in\Ks(n)}\psi(\tau^{k})\tau^{k}.

Proof.

Let
j\geq 1
, set
I_{j}:=\{n\in\Ks\,:\,\sum_{\Ks(n)}r_{k}^{2}\in(j,j+1]\}
.
We have by
Lemma

8.22

\mathbb{E}[\sum_{k\in I_{j}}(Y^{+(m^{\prime}_{k})}_{k}-Y^{+}_{k})]\ll\sum_{k\in I_{j}}(r_{k}^{2}m^{-1}_{k}+t_{k}^{-\gamma_{2}})\leq\frac{j+1}{e^{j^{1/8}}-1}+\sum_{k\in I_{j}}t_{k}^{-\gamma_{2}},

where the last inequality follows from the definition of
m_{k}
and
I_{j}
.
Therefore, the right hand side is summable over
j\geq 1
.
Hence, for
\xi
-almost every
\boldsymbol{s}
, the total sum
\sum_{k\in\Ks}(Y^{+(m^{\prime}_{k})}_{k}-Y^{+}_{k})
is
\xi
-almost-surely finite.
Then the result follows from (
8.36
).
∎

To conclude, we show the reduction prescribed by
m^{\prime}_{k}
is loose enough not to affect the counting.

Lemma 8.24
.

Assume
\gamma_{1}\lll\gamma_{2}
.
For
\xi
-almost every
\boldsymbol{s}\in\mathbb{R}
, for large enough
k
, we have

\mathscr{S}^{+}_{k}(\boldsymbol{s})\leq Y^{+(m^{\prime}_{k})}_{k}(\boldsymbol{s}).

Proof.

Note that we have
\theta*(\widetilde{{\mathbbm{1}}}^{(m^{\prime}_{k})}_{P_{k}^{+}})\geq\widetilde{{\mathbbm{1}}}^{(m^{\prime}_{k})}_{P_{k}}
, because
gP^{+}_{k}\supseteq P_{k}
for all
g\in\supp\theta
. Therefore, recalling the truncation function
\chi_{k}={\mathbbm{1}}_{\{\inj\geq t_{k}^{-\gamma_{2}}\}}
, and writing
\chi^{\prime}_{k}
the characteristic function of the set of points
x
such that
B_{1}x\subseteq\supp\chi_{k}
, we have
\theta*(\chi_{k}\widetilde{{\mathbbm{1}}}^{(m^{\prime}_{k})}_{P_{k}^{+}})\geq\chi^{\prime}_{k}\widetilde{{\mathbbm{1}}}^{(m^{\prime}_{k})}_{P_{k}}
.
It follows that

(8.37)

\displaystyle Y^{+(m^{\prime}_{k})}_{k}(\boldsymbol{s})\geq\bigl(\chi^{\prime}_{k}\widetilde{{\mathbbm{1}}}^{(m^{\prime}_{k})}_{P_{k}}\bigr)\bigl(a(t_{k})u(\boldsymbol{s})x_{0}\bigr).

By
Lemma

8.5
, for
\xi
-almost every
\boldsymbol{s}\in\mathbb{R}
, for large enough
k\in\Ks
, we have
\dist(a(t_{k})u(\boldsymbol{s})x_{0},x_{0})\ll\log\log t_{k}
, meaning that

(8.38)

a(t_{k})u(\boldsymbol{s})\in g_{k}\SL_{2}(\mathbb{Z})\text{ for some $g_{k}\in G$ satisfying }\lVert g_{k}\rVert\leq(\log t_{k})^{O(1)}.

In particular,
a(t_{k})u(\boldsymbol{s})x_{0}\in\supp\chi^{\prime}_{k}
for
k
sufficiently large, whence the truncation
\chi^{\prime}_{k}
plays no role.
Moreover, as
k\in\Ks
, we have that
P_{k}
is included in the
t_{k}^{O(\gamma_{1})}
-neighborhood of the origin.
Combined with (
8.38
), this yields that
every point
(p,q)\in g_{k}^{-1}P_{k}\cap\mathbb{Z}^{2}
satisfies
\GCD(p,q)\leq t_{k}^{\gamma_{2}}\leq m^{\prime}_{k}
(provided
\gamma_{1}\lll\gamma_{2}
). It follows that

(8.39)

\displaystyle\bigl(\chi^{\prime}_{k}\widetilde{{\mathbbm{1}}}^{(m^{\prime}_{k})}_{P_{k}}\bigr)\bigl(a(t_{k})u(\boldsymbol{s})x_{0}\bigr)=\widetilde{{\mathbbm{1}}}_{P_{k}}\bigl(a(t_{k})u(\boldsymbol{s})x_{0}\bigr)=\mathscr{S}^{+}_{k}(\boldsymbol{s}).

Equations (
8.37
) and (
8.39
) together finish the proof.
∎

The combination of Lemmas
8.23
,
8.24
yields for
\xi
-almost every
\boldsymbol{s}\in\mathbb{R}
, for large enough
n
,

\sum_{k\in\Ks(n)}\mathscr{S}^{+}_{k}(\boldsymbol{s})\leq(\tau-1+11\varepsilon)\sum_{k\in\Ks(n)}\psi(\tau^{k})\tau^{k}.

This concludes the proof of the asymptotic upper bound in
8.3
in the case
d=1
.
