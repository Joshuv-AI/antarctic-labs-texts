Untreated Thalassemia Associated With Facial and Dental Changes in Adulthood
Authors: Miranda Lin, Ivana Ho, Richard C. Godby
Published: 2026

A female patient in her 40s with transfusion‐dependent hemoglobin E/β0 thalassemia, osteoporosis, and insulin‐dependent diabetes presented with severe hyperglycemia and profound fatigue. On admission, notable laboratory tests included hemoglobin 5.2 g/dL (baseline 6 g/dL), platelet count 605 × 109/L, sodium 124 mmol/L, glucose 849 mg/dL, lactate elevated to 2.3 mmol/L, and beta‐hydroxybutyrate elevated to 1.2 mmol/L. Venous blood gas showed a pH of 7.41, pCO2 50 mmHg, and bicarbonate of 31 mmol/L. Fructosamine was > 800. She had new elevation in liver function tests, including ALT 227 U/L, AST 150 U/L, alkaline phosphatase 421 U/L, and bilirubin 2.7 mg/dL. Ferritin was elevated to 3014 mcg/L (compared to 394 mcg/L 2 years prior) with transferrin saturation > 90%, consistent with iron overload. Hemoglobin electrophoresis demonstrated 51.4% hemoglobin F, 4.3% hemoglobin A2, and 44.3% hemoglobin E, consistent with the genetically confirmed diagnosis of hemoglobin E/β0 thalassemia.

The patient had been lost to follow‐up due to lack of insurance for approximately 2 years and had not received transfusions, iron chelation therapy, or insulin during that time. The patient reported progressive changes in her facial features and dentition since her last medical evaluation (Figure 1). Although these skeletal changes are classically associated with undertreated thalassemia during childhood, clinically appreciable craniofacial progression may also occur in adulthood following the interruption of chronic transfusion therapy [1, 2]. Examination revealed frontal bossing, nasal bridge depression, lateral displacement of the orbits, maxillary overgrowth, and dental malocclusion with gingival abnormalities and tooth discoloration, which became more apparent when compared to prior photos (Figure 1).

In beta‐thalassemia, ineffective erythropoiesis can lead to iron overload and marrow expansion, resulting in characteristic craniofacial changes [1, 2, 3, 4, 5]. For patients presenting with craniofacial changes, additional considerations include Paget disease of the bone, acromegaly, and renal osteodystrophy resulting from secondary hyperparathyroidism. In untreated thalassemia, while higher transfusion thresholds (e.g., > 10 g/dL) can suppress marrow expansion and extramedullary hematopoiesis, they exacerbate iron accumulation and necessitate chelation therapy to prevent end‐organ damage such as endocrinopathies (e.g., diabetes), heart failure, and cirrhosis.

She was treated with insulin for hyperglycemia and received a red blood cell transfusion for worsening anemia compared to her baseline. Transthoracic echocardiogram was negative for signs of pulmonary hypertension; cardiac MRI and liver MRI were deferred to the outpatient setting. The patient was discharged on deferasirox, obtained through the Mark Cuban Cost Plus Drug Company, and will follow up with local charity care while reestablishing insurance coverage. At her follow‐up visit, she will likely be considered for initiation of mitapivat therapy. This case highlights the need for continuous, lifelong medical care in thalassemia and the influence of social determinants of health.
