Redescription and embryonic developmental dynamics of Aphelenchoides bicaudatus with implications for nematode taxonomy and ecology
Authors: Min Li, Ying Pei, Yanmei Shi, Fengmao Chen, Lifeng Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryAphelenchoides bicaudatus is widely reported yet frequently confused with morphologically similar congeners. We redescribe A. bicaudatus recovered from declining Pinus massoniana and associated Monochamus alternatus in eastern China using integrated light microscopy, SEM, and rDNA markers (ITS and SSU). Key diagnostic characters, particularly the bifurcated female tail terminus and SEM-resolved lip annulation, matched prior descriptions and were supported by congruent maximum-likelihood phylogenetic placement with reference sequences. We further document embryogenesis under controlled conditions and provide a time-resolved staging series from oviposition to hatching (approximately 60 h) in a parthenogenetic line, highlighting an extended one-cell phase relative to related aphelenchoids. These data expand the currently limited baseline of developmental information for Aphelenchoides and provide practical morphological and molecular references for identification. Our field sampling records co-occurrence with pine decline and beetles, but does not establish causality or vectoring.

Introduction

The genus Aphelenchoides Fischer, 1894 (Nematoda: Aphelenchida: Aphelenchoididae) comprises nematodes with broad ecological strategies, including plant parasitism, fungal feeders, and occasional associations with insects.1 Several species are important pests of crops, ornamentals, and mushrooms, making accurate identification essential for management, quarantine, and global biosecurity.2,3 Despite this importance, species-level delimitation within Aphelenchoides remains challenging because diagnostic characters can be subtle, environmentally variable, and partially overlapping among congeners, which has contributed to persistent taxonomic uncertainty in the genus.4

Aphelenchoides bicaudatus is a widely reported species originally described from Japan and subsequently recorded from diverse habitats and hosts in multiple geographic regions.4,5,6,7,8 However, comparative interpretation of published descriptions has been hindered by inconsistencies and incomplete reporting of key morphological features, including characters of the lateral field, lip region, and tail terminus, as well as by the limited integration of high-resolution imaging with the molecular data.4,6,7,9 These issues reduce diagnostic reliability and complicate the separation of A. bicaudatus from morphologically similar Aphelenchoides species. An integrative redescription that combines robust morphometrics with scanning electron microscopy and independent molecular evidence is therefore valuable to refine stable diagnostic characters and improve taxonomic resolution.

In forest systems, nematode communities associated with declining pines have received increasing attention in the context of pine wilt disease (PWD), which is primarily caused by Bursaphelenchus xylophilus and involves the pine sawyer beetle Monochamus alternatus.10,11 Previous reports have noted the occurrence of A. bicaudatus in pine-associated environments, including in association with Monochamus adults, but the biological nature of such associations remains insufficiently characterized.10 Importantly, observations of co-occurrence do not in themselves establish specificity to symptomatic trees or imply causality in tree decline, emphasizing the need for careful interpretation and for rigorous baseline characterization before ecological roles can be tested. Beyond taxonomy and occurrence records, the developmental biology of Aphelenchoides species remains poorly documented. For A. bicaudatus in particular, systematic descriptions of early embryogenesis and developmental timing are scarce, despite their potential relevance to reproductive biology and their value as a baseline phenotype for comparative studies.12 Establishing a standardized developmental timeline can therefore complement integrative taxonomy and motivate future work on reproductive and developmental diversification within the Aphelenchoididae.

Here, we provide an integrative characterization of A. bicaudatus isolated from adult M. alternatus and symptomatic Pinus massoniana collected in Jiangsu and Zhejiang provinces, China. We combine detailed morphological observations using light and scanning electron microscopy with molecular identification based on ITS-rDNA and SSU (18S rDNA) sequences and an ITS-RFLP profile as a supplementary screening tool. We further document embryonic development under controlled conditions to establish a developmental timeline for this species. Together, these data refine diagnostic characters for reliable identification and provide a reproducible foundation for future ecological and evolutionary studies on A. bicaudatus.

Results

Morphological characterization

Among 23 pine wood samples and 40 M. alternatus adults examined, A. bicaudatus was recovered from 5 wood samples (∼21.7%) and multiple beetles. Three beetles carried pure populations, while others harbored A. bicaudatus alongside different nematodes. This repeated detection in declining trees and associated beetles shows that A. bicaudatus can reproducibly colonize both wood and insect-associated habitats in the surveyed stands. However, because healthy pines were not sampled in this study, these data cannot determine whether their occurrence is specific to declining trees or reflects a broader distribution in pine forests. Only adult females were observed and measured in the established cultures; no males were detected during routine screening, and male morphometrics are therefore not provided for this population. Adult females of A. bicaudatus exhibited a slender body shape, slightly ventrally curved upon heat fixation, with a distinctly constricted head region (Figures 1A and 2A). The lip region was rounded with clear annulations, bearing six symmetrically arranged labial papillae (Figures 1B, 2B, 3A, and 3B). The lateral field contained four incisures, comprising two outer incisures and two inner incisures forming a refractive band (Figures 3D–3G). The stylet was slender and clearly differentiated, including a pointed conus (approximately 4–5 μm), narrow shaft (approximately 5–6 μm), and basal swellings (approximately 2–3 μm wide) (Figures 1B and 2B). The median bulb (metacorpus) was oval to spherical, approximately 8.7–11.9 μm long by 6.2–9.1 μm wide, occupying roughly two-thirds of the corresponding body width (Figures 1B and 2B). The excretory pore was located about half a median bulb width posteriorly, occasionally opening at the base of the median bulb. The reproductive system was monodelphic-prodelphic, featuring a single anteriorly extended ovary. Oocytes were arranged in a single row, sometimes extending posteriorly toward the esophageal gland region (Figures 1C and 2C). The vulval opening was posteriorly situated, appearing as a simple transverse slit without an epiptygma. The vagina extended anteriorly, and the postuterine sac was short, measuring approximately 1.2 times the vulval body width (Figures 1C, 2C, and 3C). The rectum and anus were prominent. The tail was elongate-conoid, measuring 31.1–39.4 μm long, ending in a distinctly bifurcated terminus, with the ventral bifurcation typically longer than the dorsal one, clearly observable under both LM and SEM (Figures 1D, 2D–2F, 3H, and 3I). Molecular identification and phylogenetic relationships

PCR amplification of the ITS rDNA region yielded a single product of approximately 723 bp. The ITS sequence obtained in this study was deposited in GenBank under accession number HQ259064. Pairwise comparison against GenBank sequences indicated that HQ259064 shares 99% sequence identity with an existing A. bicaudatus ITS sequence (GU984233). Phylogenetic inference based on ITS sequences further supported the species assignment. Using B. mucronatus (KT581990) and B. xylophilus (KT581989) as outgroups, the maximum-likelihood tree placed HQ259064 within the A. bicaudatus lineage and recovered a sister relationship with GU984233 with strong bootstrap support (bootstrap = 100; Figure 4). Other related taxa (e.g., Aphelenchoides sp., A. fragariae, A. varicaudatus, A. arachidis, A. besseyi, and A. ritzemabosi) formed distinct clades separated from the A. bicaudatus cluster (Figure 4). PCR-ITS rDNA-RFLP analysis generated a reproducible restriction profile consistent with the sequence-based identification (Figure 5). The ∼723 bp ITS amplicon was not digested by RsaI, HaeIII, or MspI (single band at ∼723 bp). Digestion with HinfI yielded fragments of approximately 198, 182, 81, 70, 51, 49, 47, and 45 bp (sum = 723 bp). Digestion with AluI produced fragments of approximately 386, 218, 105, and 14 bp; as expected, the smallest fragment is below the practical resolution of routine agarose electrophoresis, and the observed banding pattern corresponded to the larger predicted fragments within the sizing limits of a 100-bp DNA ladder (Figure 5). To provide an independent nuclear marker, the SSU (18S rDNA) region was also analyzed. Using B. mucronatus (AY284648) and B. xylophilus (KJ636306) as outgroups, the 18S maximum-likelihood phylogeny recovered our sequence (shown in bold in Figure 6) within the A. bicaudatus assemblage alongside reference A. bicaudatus sequences (AY284643 and KX345119). In particular, the node uniting our sequence with KX345119 received strong bootstrap support (bootstrap = 99; Figure 6). Embryonic developmental stages

Embryonic development of A. bicaudatus was observed from oviposition to juvenile hatching under constant laboratory conditions (25 ± 1 °C). Across three independent trials involving more than 50 eggs in total, the entire embryogenesis from oviposition to the emergence of second-stage juveniles (J2)—required, on average, approximately 60 h, with observed durations ranging from 58 to 62 h. Newly deposited eggs were elongated-oval, approximately 30–40 μm long by 15–20 μm wide (Figure 7A). Within the first 2–4 h post-oviposition, a distinct female pronucleus became evident, asymmetrically positioned at one pole of the egg (Figure 7B). Subsequent embryonic development progressed through a series of clearly identifiable stages, thoroughly documented by morphological observations: Two-cell stage (Figure 7C): Approximately 6–8 h post-oviposition, symmetrical longitudinal cleavage occurred, resulting in two equal-sized blastomeres aligned parallel to the long axis of the egg. Three-cell stage (Figure 7D): Observed consistently between 8 and 12 h after oviposition. Embryos at this stage exhibited clearly distinguishable quadrants. Four-cell stage (Figure 7E): Appeared approximately 12–16 h post-oviposition, with blastomeres regularly arranged and easily observable. Morula stage (Figures 7F and 7G): Around 20–24 h post-oviposition, embryos were characterized by approximately 16–32 tightly packed cells without a central cavity. Blastula stage (Figures 7H and 7I): Occurred at about 30–36 h post-oviposition, with a recognizable internal cavity (blastocoel) beginning to form, marking the initiation of internal structural differentiation. Gastrula stage (Figures 7J and 7K): Observed at approximately 40–48 h post-oviposition. Gastrulation was characterized by clear invagination and differentiation into ectoderm, mesoderm, and endoderm layers, indicating the initiation of organogenesis. Early vermiform embryo stage (Figures 7L and 7M): Around 48–52 h post-oviposition, the embryo elongated, showing an early vermiform shape and distinct developmental organization, with recognizable head and tail ends. Late vermiform embryo stage (Figures 7N and 7O): At approximately 52–56 h post-oviposition, embryos further elongated and exhibited clearly visible internal organs and structures, such as the digestive tract and stylet primordium. Fully developed second-stage juvenile (J2) (Figure 7P): Emerged consistently from eggs at approximately 60 h post-oviposition. At this stage, juveniles exhibited fully developed morphological characteristics, actively hatched, and began their independent life outside the egg. Discussion

This study strengthens the diagnosis of A. bicaudatus by integrating high-resolution morphology with independent molecular evidence and developmental observations. Within Aphelenchoides, species delimitation is frequently complicated by partial overlap in key morphometrics and by environmentally influenced variation, which can undermine morphology-only identification.1,6 Morphological traits, especially the bifurcated female tail terminus emphasized in classical redescriptions and the SEM-resolved lip annulation pattern, are consistent with prior descriptions and represent stable diagnostic characters for routine identification.4,8 At the same time, modest population-level deviations in body size or other measurements should be interpreted cautiously as descriptive variation rather than taxonomically decisive characters, consistent with previous reports that highlight variability across geographic isolates.4,6 Notably, males were not observed in our cultures, suggesting that the sampled population may reproduce predominantly by parthenogenesis, which provides a biological context for the developmental phenotype described later in discussion.8

Sequence-based evidence provides an independent line of support for species identity and helps mitigate the ambiguity inherent to morphology-only comparisons in closely related Aphelenchoides.1,4 Importantly, we do not interpret the restriction profile as a universally species-specific test across all congeners; broader taxon sampling and multilocus approaches would be required to rigorously evaluate uniqueness and refine species limits within Aphelenchoides.1,4 In the study, ITS and SSU sequence similarity, together with congruent phylogenetic placement, constitute the primary molecular evidence supporting identification. We report the ITS-RFLP profile only as a supplementary, low-cost screening signature for the present isolates and do not imply universal uniqueness across congeners without broader comparative validation. This framing is consistent with the historical use of rDNA-RFLP as an adjunct tool in nematode diagnostics, while recognizing that sequencing provides higher information content for species delimitation.1,13,14

A major contribution of this work is a detailed account of embryogenesis in A. bicaudatus. Embryogenesis from oviposition to hatching required about 60 h at 25 °C, whereas published work on B. xylophilus at 25 °C reported a substantially shorter oviposition-to-hatching interval, indicating that the timeline observed here is comparatively extended under similar laboratory temperature conditions.15 In contrast to amphimictic nematodes such as C. elegans and the B. xylophilus, which typically display an asymmetrical first cleavage,15,16 A. bicaudatus exhibited a symmetrical first division producing two similarly sized blastomeres. Rather than treating this as a demonstrated adaptation, we interpret it as a baseline developmental phenotype that motivates testable hypotheses in the context of reproduction. Comparative studies in parthenogenetic nematodes show that polarity establishment and symmetry breaking can follow alternative routes relative to amphimictic models, including meiosis-linked asymmetry and maternal inheritance of centrosomal components.17,18 In apomictic plant-parasitic nematodes, embryogenesis can also follow modified but stereotyped programs.19 Within this comparative framework, the developmental timeline and cleavage patterns reported here provide a reproducible reference for future experiments that explicitly test whether cleavage symmetry and developmental timing correlate with reproductive mode, environmental conditions, or lineage history within Aphelenchoididae.

Ecologically, A. bicaudatus was recovered from declining P. massoniana and from adults of M. alternatus collected in the same regions, that is, within forest stands where components of the PWD system are present.10,12 However, our data document only co-occurrence and habitat overlap; they do not demonstrate a causal relationship with pine decline, nor do they show that Monochamus functions as a biological vector for Aphelenchoides. Given the lack of evidence that A. bicaudatus parasitizes conifers and its reported mycophagous habit, we interpret it conservatively as a free-living component of forest ecosystems that opportunistically colonizes fungus-rich, beetle-affected wood and may be incidentally or passively carried on beetles between suitable substrates.13,14,20 Aphelenchoides spp. have also been intercepted from wood packaging materials in international trade, underscoring that accurate diagnosis remains relevant for forest health monitoring and forest product-related inspections.21 At present, the specific dispersal stage(s) of A. bicaudatus on M. alternatus (e.g., dauer juveniles versus other stages) and its microhabitat localization on or in the beetle remain unknown. In the broader PWD context, ophiostomatoid fungi and associated fungal communities can influence the reproductive success and carriage dynamics of B. xylophilus, and these fungal shifts may create or modify niche opportunities for other fungus-associated nematodes.22,23,24,25 Consistent with this view, interactions among co-occurring pine-associated nematodes, including non-pathogenic congeners, have been proposed to influence invasion dynamics and vector loads, although outcomes vary across systems.26,27 Any stronger ecological or epidemiological interpretation will require targeted evidence, including surveys that incorporate asymptomatic tree controls, determination of the developmental stage(s) and microhabitat localization of nematodes on or in insects, and direct tests of transport and persistence under field-relevant conditions.

A. bicaudatus can be identified most reliably by the combination of a distinctly bifurcated female tail terminus, LM/SEM-resolved lip annulation and lateral-field characters, and congruent ITS and SSU phylogenetic placement. The developmental timeline reported here provides a comparative baseline for future work on reproductive biology and ecological diversification within Aphelenchoididae. Our field records document co-occurrence with declining pine tissues and M. alternatus adults, but do not demonstrate causality or biological vectoring.

Limitations of the study

Sampling focused on symptomatic trees and did not include healthy pine controls, and we did not determine the developmental stage or specific localization of nematodes on or in insects. Accordingly, our ecological conclusions are restricted to documented co-occurrence rather than causality or biological vectoring. Future work should include asymptomatic tree controls, stage-specific localization of nematodes on or in M. alternatus, and direct tests of transport and persistence under field-relevant conditions.

Method details

Nematode isolation and culturing

Specimens of A. bicaudatus were isolated from adults of the M. alternatus and symptomatic P. massoniana collected from Jiangsu and Zhejiang provinces, China. In total, 23 declining pine samples (wood tissues) and 40 adult beetles were collected during the surveys. Because the original survey specifically targeted declining or PWD-suspect stands, no asymptomatic trees were sampled as controls in this study. Declining trees were identified based on visible field symptoms; because such symptoms can result from multiple factors (e.g., bark beetle attack, fungal pathogens, drought stress, or other abiotic conditions), the term “declining” is used descriptively throughout and does not imply any causal link to nematode presence. Nematodes were extracted from samples using the Baermann funnel technique, as previously described, with slight modifications.30 Briefly, beetle bodies and pine wood samples were separately cut into small pieces (approximately 1-2 cm) and placed in funnels lined with filter paper. Nematodes actively migrated into water at the bottom of the funnels overnight (approximately 12-24 h), after which they were collected and examined under a stereoscopic microscope (Leica DFC320). Pure cultures were established by transferring individual female nematodes onto potato dextrose agar (PDA) plates previously inoculated with the fungus Botrytis cinerea and maintained at 25 °C for population expansion. Males were not observed during routine microscopic examination of the established cultures; therefore, isofemale lines were initiated using single adult females to ensure culture purity. Because we did not conduct a targeted sex-ratio survey in the original field extractions, we cannot exclude that males may occur at very low frequency in natural populations. All nematode cultures used in subsequent morphological and molecular analyses were maintained under these conditions.

Morphological examination

Morphological observations were performed using both light microscopy (LM) and scanning electron microscopy (SEM). For LM observations, live nematodes were heat-killed gently on glass slides, fixed in TAF fixative (triethanolamine-formalin), dehydrated gradually to anhydrous glycerin following Kumari’s rapid method, and mounted permanently in glycerin slides for microscopic examination.31 Morphometric measurements were recorded using a Nikon Eclipse Ni-U microscope equipped with Nikon NIS-Elements imaging software. Key morphological characters, including body length, stylet length, esophageal bulb dimensions, vulval position, and tail shape, were measured from at least 20 adult females to ensure accuracy.

For SEM analyses, nematodes were fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde buffered with 0.1 M phosphate buffer (pH 7.2) for 24 h at 4 °C, and then post-fixed in 1% osmium tetroxide in the same buffer for 1-2 h. Specimens were dehydrated through a graded ethanol series (30%, 50%, 70%, 90%, and 100%), critical-point dried, sputter-coated with gold, and examined using a scanning electron microscope (Hitachi S 3400N). For figure preparation, images were cropped and adjusted only for linear brightness/contrast; no feature-level manipulation was performed. Original micrographs are available via the data repository (see data and code availability).

Molecular characterization and phylogenetic analysis

Genomic DNA was extracted from nematodes obtained from a pure culture established from a single adult female (isofemale line; see nematode isolation and culturing) using the FastPure Universal Plant DNA Isolation Mini Kit (Vazyme, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. To ensure sufficient DNA yield for multiple downstream assays (PCR amplification, cloning/Sanger sequencing, and restriction digestion), each extraction used approximately 50-100 individuals harvested simultaneously from a single isofemale culture plate. Because the line was initiated from a single adult female and males were not observed in culture (see nematode isolation and culturing), the pooled individuals represent clonal descendants and the risk of mixing different haplotypes is therefore negligible. This approach was applied for species identification rather than population genetic inference, and within-line haplotype diversity was not assessed. Although designed for plant tissues, this silica-column protocol provided DNA that was readily amplifiable for nematode ITS and SSU (18S rDNA), as evidenced by the successful amplification and sequencing of these regions in all isolates. The ITS region of rDNA was amplified by polymerase chain reaction (PCR) using universal primers (forward: 5’-TTGATTACGTCCCTGCCCTTT-3’; reverse: 5’-TTTCACTCGCCGTTACTAAGG-3’) 21. The SSU (18S rDNA) region was amplified using primers 988F (5′-CTCAAAGATTAAGCCATGC-3′) and 1912R (5′-TTTACGGTCAGAACTAGGG-3′) 22. PCR amplifications were performed in a 50 μL reaction mixture containing 2 μL template DNA (20-50 ng), 25 μL 2x PCR Master Mix (Vazyme, China), 1 μL each primer (10 μM), and ddH2O to final volume. Amplification conditions were as follows: initial denaturation at 94 °C for 5 min; 35 cycles of 94 °C for 30 s, 55 °C for 45 s, 72 °C for 1 min; and final extension at 72 °C for 10 min.

PCR products were purified, cloned, and Sanger sequenced. The ITS sequence generated in this study was deposited in GenBank under accession number HQ259064; the SSU (18S rDNA) sequence was deposited under accession number MH722388. For phylogenetic analyses, ITS and SSU datasets were compiled separately by including representative sequences of Aphelenchoides and related taxa retrieved from GenBank. Multiple sequence alignments were performed using MUSCLE implemented in MEGA 5.2, followed by manual inspection and trimming to remove ambiguously aligned regions. Phylogenetic trees were inferred using the maximum-likelihood (ML) method in MEGA 5.2 under the best-fit nucleotide substitution model selected by model testing, and branch support was assessed using 1,000 bootstrap replicates. Trees were rooted using B. xylophilus and B. mucronatus as outgroups. Additionally, PCR–RFLP analysis of the ITS amplicon was performed using restriction enzymes RsaI, HaeIII, MspI, HinfI, and AluI (New England Biolabs, USA) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Restriction fragments were separated by agarose gel electrophoresis alongside a 100-bp DNA ladder for approximate size estimation.

Embryonic development

To document embryogenesis, nematode eggs were obtained from fresh culture plates by dissecting gravid females under a stereomicroscope. Eggs were transferred individually onto slides containing a thin layer of 2% water agar, sealed to prevent desiccation, and incubated at a constant temperature of 25 ± 1 °C. Images were captured at defined time intervals post-oviposition until juvenile hatching (hereafter referred to as the hatchling/J2 stage). Key developmental stages, including pronuclear appearance, cleavage patterns, and cell division processes, were recorded and photographed using a Nikon Eclipse Ni-U microscope equipped with Nomarski differential interference contrast (DIC) optics.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Morphometrics were measured from at least 20 adult females using calibrated microscopy imaging software and are reported as mean ± standard deviation (range). Embryogenesis timing (oviposition to hatchling) was recorded across three independent trials involving more than 50 eggs in total and is reported descriptively (mean and range). No formal statistical hypothesis testing was performed.
