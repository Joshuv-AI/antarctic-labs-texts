When SVT is Not Benign: Troponin Elevation Revealing Coronary Vasospasm, A Case Report
Authors: Elias El Murr, Antonio Al Hazzouri, Katherine Atallah, Philippe Attieh, Maroun Braidy, Adel Daas, Nabil El Murr
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTroponin elevation is frequently observed in patients presenting with supraventricular tachycardia (SVT) and is commonly attributed to transient demand ischemia. However, its clinical significance remains uncertain, and alternative mechanisms such as coronary vasospasm may be underrecognized.Case PresentationWe report the case of a 77-year-old male presenting with a first episode of SVT, successfully terminated with adenosine. Adenosine successfully restored sinus rhythm with partial regression of the ischemic ST-segment changes. Initial high-sensitivity troponin was elevated but demonstrated a significant rise on the following day, accompanied by new T-wave inversions in the lateral leads. Given the dynamic electrocardiographic changes and biomarker progression, coronary angiography was performed, revealing coronary vasospasm of the left anterior descending artery without obstructive coronary disease. The spasm resolved with intracoronary nitroglycerin administration, suggesting the diagnosis of coronary vasospasm.ConclusionCoronary vasospasm should be considered in patients with SVT and evolving troponin elevation, particularly when accompanied by dynamic electrocardiographic changes. Recognition of this mechanism is essential, as it has important diagnostic and therapeutic implications and requires a tailored management approach.Keywords: case report, coronary vasospasm, SVT, troponin, demand ischemia

Introduction

Supraventricular tachycardia (SVT) is a common arrhythmia encountered in emergency departments, defined as a tachyarrhythmia originating at or above the His bundle with a heart rate exceeding 100 beats per minute at rest and encompassing a heterogeneous group of re-entrant and focal atrial rhythms. Although generally associated with a benign prognosis and often terminating with vagal maneuvers or pharmacologic cardioversion, SVT frequently presents with symptoms that overlap with acute coronary syndrome, including chest pain, dyspnea, and palpitations, which often prompts routine evaluation with cardiac biomarkers such as high-sensitivity troponin. However, multiple studies have shown that troponin elevation in SVT is common and poorly specific for obstructive coronary artery disease, reflecting instead transient myocardial stress due to tachycardia-induced oxygen supply–demand mismatch, reduced diastolic coronary perfusion, and increased myocardial workload.1,2 Despite this, elevated troponin levels frequently lead to hospital admission and invasive coronary evaluation, although the prevalence of significant coronary artery disease in this population remains low and outcomes are generally favorable. 3 Emerging evidence also suggests that alternative mechanisms such as coronary vasospasm and atrial tachyarrhythmia–induced sympathetic surges may contribute to myocardial injury and troponin release in selected patients, further complicating interpretation.4,5 We report a case of an elderly male patient presenting with first-episode SVT and progressive troponin elevation, ultimately diagnosed with coronary vasospasm on coronary angiography, highlighting the importance of a comprehensive and individualized diagnostic approach when interpreting troponin elevation in SVT.

Case Presentation

A 77-year-old male patient, with no past medical history, presented to the emergency department for palpitations, chest discomfort, blurry vision and dizziness of same day history. The patient reports no dyspnea, chest pain, fever, cough, weakness, diaphoresis or left arm numbness. He reports that this is his first time experiencing such symptoms. His past surgical history includes lithotripsy and left inguinal hernia repair. He is a smoker, and has no known food or drug allergy. He also reports that he has a stressful life. Electrocardiography (ECG) was done upon arrival, a regular narrow complex tachycardia with a heart rate of 200 bpm, with diffuse horizontal ST segment depression and aVR ST elevation, and no retrograde P waves seen. Findings were consistent with SVT (AVNRT type) (Figure 1). Blood pressure was 110/70 mmHg, oxygen saturation was 95%, and the patient had no signs of shock. He was given 6mg followed by 12mg of adenosine, after which he converted back to sinus rhythm. The ECG showed a sinus rhythm with a prolonged PR (280ms), due to the adenosine effect, diffuse ST depression with aVR ST elevation (Figure 2). Thirty minutes after adenosine administration, an ECG was performed showing a sinus rhythm with a heart rate of 80 bpm, normal PR interval (180ms), 1mm upsloping ST depression in the inferior leads, with flat T waves in the lateral leads V5, V6 and aVL (Figure 3). Laboratory results are summarized in Table 1. It showed a normal complete blood count, creatinine, electrolytes and TSH. High sensitivity troponin was elevated (3.34), CPK and CPK-MB were negative. The patient was admitted to the cardiac care unit for monitoring. The second day, the patient had a drastic increase in the troponin level from 3.34 to 10.57. ECG was done showing T wave inversion in leads V4, V5 and V6 (Figure 4). He was experiencing active ischemic chest paint along with the ECG changes. The patient was scheduled for coronary angiography. Transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated preserved left ventricular systolic function with no regional wall motion abnormalities. Coronary angiography demonstrated a severe focal narrowing of approximately 80–90% and coronary vasospasm involving the mid segment of the left anterior descending artery (LAD), with a thrombolysis in myocardial infarction (TIMI) flow of 2, and normal remaining coronary arteries, with no significant stenosis or lesions (Figure 5). While visualizing the spasm during coronary angiography, the patient was actually experiencing chest pain. Nitroglycerin (200 μg) was administered during angiography. No stents were placed. Coronary angiography post nitroglycerin administration showed restoration of LAD diameter and blood flow with a TIMI flow of 3 (Figure 6). The chest pain resolved 30 seconds post administration of nitroglycerin, supporting the diagnosis of coronary vasospasm rather than catherter induced spasm. An ECG done post coronary angiography showed a normal sinus rhythm with resolution of ST segment changes and T wave inversions (Figure 7). Provocative testing using acetylcholine or ergonovine was not performed. Administration of intracoronary nitroglycerin resulted in complete angiographic resolution of the lesion and chest pain within 30 seconds, strongly supporting the diagnosis of coronary vasospasm. Given the temporal sequence of events, it is plausible that the initial SVT episode, through increased sympathetic activation and myocardial oxygen demand, acted as a trigger for delayed coronary vasospasm, subsequently manifesting as evolving electrocardiographic changes and troponin elevation. The patient was discharged on verapamil 240 mg once daily, which was selected both for prevention of recurrent SVT episodes and as first-line therapy for coronary vasospasm owing to its coronary vasodilatory properties. At 3 and 6 month follow-up, the patient remained asymptomatic with no recurrence of arrhythmia or chest pain. The patient was satisfied with the management as his case was taken with careful consideration with good clinical judgment that led to the discovery of the underlying vasospasm.

Discussion

SVT is a common cardiac dysrhythmia defined as any atrial rhythm exceeding 100 beats per minute due to abnormal electrical conduction at or above the atrioventricular node. Patients typically present with palpitations, chest pain, and dyspnea, and less frequently diaphoresis, nausea, presyncope, or syncope. SVT accounts for an estimated 50,000 emergency department (ED) visits annually, with an incidence of 35 per 100,000 persons. Although generally associated with a favorable prognosis and often managed with medical or electrical cardioversion, approximately 25% of cases get admitted to the hospital. Such admissions increase healthcare resource utilization, with average costs of $3,800 per admission and annual costs exceeding $190 million in one study. 2

Several mechanisms have been proposed to explain troponin elevation during SVT in the absence of acute plaque rupture. Supply–demand mismatch (type 2 myocardial infarction), in which rapid heart rates increase myocardial oxygen demand while shortening diastolic coronary perfusion time, resulting in transient subendocardial ischemias, is the most widely accepted mechanism.3,6,7 This is supported by the frequent observation of rate-related ST-segment depression and the correlation between higher heart rates and the magnitude of troponin elevation. In the context of SVT, troponin elevation is most plausibly explained by transient myocardial stress rather than obstructive CAD. Additional contributing factors may include tachycardia-induced myocardial stretch, beta-adrenergic stimulation, increased sarcolemmal permeability, and release of cytosolic troponin from an early releasable pool without irreversible myocyte necrosis. 1 Less commonly, coronary vasospasm has been proposed as a potential mechanism, although its causal relationship with SVT remains incompletely understood. 2

Elevated cardiac troponin (cTn) levels are frequently observed in patients presenting with supraventricular tachycardia (SVT), with contemporary studies reporting biomarker elevation in approximately 46–48% of cases.1,2 Several clinical factors have been associated with troponin release, including chest pain, absence of a prior history of SVT, higher heart rates, and lower systolic blood pressure. Despite its frequent occurrence, troponin elevation in SVT has limited specificity for obstructive coronary artery disease (CAD). Multiple studies have demonstrated that neither the presence nor the magnitude of troponin elevation, nor associated ST-segment depression, reliably predicts significant CAD.1,6 In one cohort, more than two-thirds of patients with biochemical or electrocardiographic evidence of myocardial injury had no angiographic evidence of obstructive coronary disease, 1 while Dorenkamp et al reported a CAD prevalence of only 4% among patients with SVT, with no association between CAD and either troponin elevation or ST-segment. 6 Similarly, a recent systematic review and meta-analysis found that although nearly half of patients presenting with SVT had elevated troponin levels, the overall prevalence of major adverse cardiovascular events remained low, suggesting that troponin elevation most often reflects transient tachycardia-related myocardial stress rather than acute coronary occlusion. 2 Consistent with these findings, elevated troponin levels have been associated with increased hospital admissions, cardiology consultations, and additional diagnostic testing without a corresponding increase in adverse cardiac outcomes, highlighting the potential for unnecessary healthcare utilization when biomarker elevation is interpreted in isolation. 3

Although most studies report a low prevalence of obstructive CAD among patients with SVT and elevated troponin, recent data suggest that significant coronary disease may still be encountered in selected higher-risk individuals. Higher troponin concentrations, abnormal ECG findings, previous CAD, and traditional cardiovascular risk factors appear to increase the likelihood of underlying coronary disease. These findings support an individualized rather than uniform approach to invasive evaluation. 8 Similarly, a case report has described SVT as the initial manifestation of severe multivessel CAD, emphasizing that troponin elevation should not be universally attributed to demand ischemia, especially in patients with cardiovascular risk factors. 9

Clinically, these findings support a selective approach to troponin testing. In low-risk patients without ischemic symptoms, significant ECG changes, or cardiovascular risk factors, routine troponin measurement is unlikely to be informative and may lead to unnecessary investigations. In contrast, patients with high-risk features such as typical chest pain, ischemic ECG changes, hemodynamic instability, age, or known coronary disease—require further evaluation, as troponin elevation in this context may reflect underlying acute coronary syndrome rather than SVT alone. 7

Emerging evidence suggests that coronary vasospasm may represent an additional and clinically relevant pathophysiological process. Coronary vasospastic angina is characterized by transient, reversible coronary artery constriction in the absence of significant fixed obstructive disease, resulting in episodic myocardial ischemia. 4 Its pathogenesis involves endothelial dysfunction with reduced nitric oxide bioavailability, hyperreactive vascular smooth muscle, heightened sympathetic tone, and inflammatory and oxidative stress pathways. These mechanisms can lead to transient ischemia, electrocardiographic changes, and reversible troponin release even without permanent myocardial injury. 4

A potential relationship between coronary vasospasm and atrial tachyarrhythmias has also been described, with reports of atrial fibrillation presenting as the initial manifestation of coronary spasm, 5 suggesting a bidirectional relationship between atrial arrhythmias and coronary vasomotor instability. In this context, SVT or atrial fibrillation could possibly act as a trigger for coronary spasm through increased sympathetic activation and catecholamine surge, thereby creating overlapping clinical presentations of tachyarrhythmia-induced troponin elevation and vasospastic ischemia. Clinically, both entities can mimic acute coronary syndrome, presenting with chest discomfort, transient ischemic ECG changes, and mild to marked troponin elevation. Coronary vasospasm is particularly important to consider in patients with disproportionate biomarker elevation or recurrent ischemic symptoms despite the absence of significant obstructive coronary disease on angiography. Diagnosis relies on coronary angiography with provocative testing, while treatment is centered on calcium channel blockers and nitrates. 4 In selected patients, troponin elevation during SVT may therefore reflect dynamic coronary vasomotor dysfunction rather than isolated tachycardia-induced myocardial strain, with important implications for both diagnosis and management. 4 Coronary spasm leads to sinoatrial (SA) node ischemia, hindering its maestro role in rhythm control. Ischemia also increases the automaticity of the ectopic foci that will have the upper hand over the SA node, initiating atrial tachycardia. 5

Alternative explanations for troponin elevation in the setting of non-obstructive coronary arteries include type 2 myocardial infarction secondary to tachycardia-induced oxygen supply–demand mismatch, myocardial infarction with non-obstructive coronary arteries (MINOCA), myocarditis, Takotsubo syndrome, and catheter-induced coronary spasm. In our patient, type 2 myocardial infarction related to SVT was initially considered; however, the progressive rise in troponin levels, evolving ischemic electrocardiographic changes after arrhythmia termination, and the demonstration of a focal severe coronary narrowing that resolved promptly following intracoronary nitroglycerin administration suggested an active coronary vasomotor process rather than isolated tachycardia-induced myocardial stress. Myocarditis and Takotsubo syndrome were considered less likely given the absence of suggestive clinical features, regional wall-motion abnormalities, or diffuse angiographic abnormalities. Furthermore, the angiographic appearance and rapid reversibility of the lesion favored spontaneous coronary vasospasm over catheter-induced spasm. Taken together, these findings support coronary vasospasm as the most plausible explanation for the patient’s presentation and biomarker elevation.

In our case, an elderly patient presenting with a first episode of SVT demonstrated progressive elevation of high-sensitivity troponin, prompting further evaluation despite the absence of clear ischemic symptoms at presentation. While initial interpretation could have favored tachycardia-induced demand ischemia, coronary angiography ultimately revealed coronary vasospasm as the underlying mechanism. The patient’s smoking history represents a well-established risk factor for coronary vasospasm and may have contributed to underlying endothelial dysfunction. Coronary angiography demonstrated a severe, focal narrowing of approximately 80–90% involving the mid segment of the LAD, with no evidence of fixed obstructive atherosclerotic disease in the remaining coronary circulation. The spasm occurred distal to the site of catheter engagement, making a catheter-induced mechanism less likely, as iatrogenic vasospasm typically involves proximal segments at the coronary ostia. Importantly, the lesion resolved completely following intracoronary nitroglycerin administration, confirming its dynamic and reversible nature. The degree of transient luminal narrowing, its distal localization, and its prompt reversibility strongly support the diagnosis of true coronary vasospasm rather than mechanical artifact. This angiographic finding correlated with the patient’s evolving electrocardiographic changes and progressive rise in troponin levels, supporting the clinical significance of the vasospasm as the underlying mechanism of myocardial injury. While demand ischemia related to SVT was initially considered, the subsequent development of ischemic ECG changes and angiographically demonstrated reversible coronary narrowing raises the possibility that the arrhythmic episode contributed to a vasospastic response. Although a direct causal relationship cannot be established, the temporal sequence suggests a potential interaction between SVT-related sympathetic activation and coronary vasomotor dysfunction Although a bidirectional relationship between atrial tachyarrhythmias and coronary vasospasm has been described, the temporal sequence in our case, SVT onset followed by delayed troponin elevation and evolving ischemic electrocardiographic changes, favors the possibility of SVT being the precipitating trigger rather than a consequence of primary ischemia. This supports the hypothesis that tachycardia-induced sympathetic activation and increased myocardial oxygen demand may promote coronary vasomotor instability, leading to subsequent vasospasm. This finding is clinically important, as it demonstrates that troponin dynamics in SVT should not be attributed exclusively to benign supply–demand mismatch, particularly when elevations are rising or disproportionate. Our observation adds to growing evidence that SVT-associated troponin elevation may unmask dynamic coronary pathology rather than fixed obstructive disease and highlights coronary vasospasm as a key but underrecognized diagnosis in this context. Importantly, recognition of this entity has direct therapeutic implications, as it shifts management away from unnecessary workup for atherosclerotic coronary disease toward targeted vasodilator therapy. This case therefore reinforces the need for careful clinical reassessment when troponin elevation persists or evolves in SVT, ensuring that alternative coronary mechanisms are not overlooked.

This report has several limitations. First, provocative testing with acetylcholine or ergonovine was not performed; therefore, the diagnosis of coronary vasospasm was based on angiographic appearance and rapid resolution following intracoronary nitroglycerin rather than formal provocation. Second, as a single-case report, a definitive causal relationship between SVT and coronary vasospasm cannot be established. Although the temporal sequence suggests a possible association, it remains uncertain whether the arrhythmia triggered the vasospasm or whether both phenomena shared a common underlying autonomic mechanism. Finally, alternative causes of myocardial injury with non-obstructive coronary arteries cannot be completely excluded, although the clinical presentation and angiographic findings strongly favored coronary vasospasm.

Conclusion

Troponin elevation in supraventricular tachycardia is most often attributed to transient demand ischemia and is therefore frequently considered a benign and self-limited finding. However, this assumption can obscure alternative and clinically important mechanisms. Our case emphasizes coronary vasospasm as a key diagnostic consideration in patients with SVT-associated troponin rise, particularly when biomarker elevation is progressive or disproportionate to the arrhythmic burden. Coronary vasospasm can closely mimic acute coronary syndrome, producing dynamic ischemic changes and myocardial injury despite the absence of obstructive coronary disease, yet it remains a reversible and treatable condition once identified. This case therefore, highlights that SVT should not be viewed in isolation when troponin elevation is present and that clinicians and cardiologists should keep in mind and raise suspicion for underlying coronary vasomotor disorders, especially in older patients or those with cardiovascular risk factors. Early recognition is essential, as it directly influences management strategy and prevents both underdiagnosis of vasospastic disease and unnecessary treatment directed toward fixed atherosclerotic coronary pathology.
