Associated mortality and time spent in hospital following mitral valve intervention for mitral regurgitation according to frailty status: a nationwide Danish study
Authors: Lauge Østergaard, Jeppe K Petersen, Christian L Carranza, Simon Melchior, Liv Borum Schöps, Sofie Truong, Kamillia Baker, Jarl Emanuel Strange, Lars Køber, Emil Fosbøl
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsLittle is known about how patient frailty affects mortality and hospitalization following mitral valve intervention for mitral regurgitation (MR).Methods and resultsUsing Danish nationwide registries, we identified patients undergoing first-time mitral valve intervention for MR during the period 1996–2022. Patients were categorized as frail or non-frail based on the Hospital Frailty Risk Score. We assessed 1 year mortality with the reverse Kaplan–Meier estimator, and adjusted comparisons were computed using multivariable-adjusted Cox regression analysis. A composite outcome of death or time in hospital >14 days within 1 year was examined using multivariable-adjusted logistic regression analysis. A total of 7000 patients (90.0%) were considered non-frail (median age 67.0 years, 66.3% male) and 782 (10.0%) were considered frail (median age 71.0 years, 56.3% male). One year mortality was 8.2% among non-frail patients and 13.4% among frail patients (P-value <0.0001), but no statistically significant difference was observed [hazard ratio = 1.16, 95% confidence interval (CI): 0.88–1.54, reference: non-frail patients] in the adjusted analysis. Within the first year after mitral valve intervention, 42.8% of non-frail patients were never admitted, 42.1% were admitted for 1–14 days, 6.2% for 14–28 days, and 5.1% for >28 days. Among frail patients, the corresponding proportions were 26.6%, 46.3%, 9.1%, and 9.8%. In the adjusted analysis, frail patients were associated with higher odds of the composite outcome [odds ratio = 1.65 (95% CI: 1.37–2.00)].ConclusionFrail patients were associated with more time spent in hospital within the first year following surgery compared with non-frail patients, but no statistically significant difference was found in the 1 year mortality according to frailty status in adjusted analysis.

Introduction

Mitral valve intervention for mitral regurgitation (MR) is associated with severe perioperative complications, as well as a high risk of rehospitalization within the first year following intervention.1 Tools for the assessment of frailty are increasingly used across several cardiovascular disease categories, and has been widely investigated and incorporated into clinical practice for patients with severe aortic stenosis undergoing transcatheter aortic valve intervention.2, 3 Frailty is defined as a clinically identified increased vulnerability in which several physiological systems are involved, resulting in a reduced ability to maintain everyday functioning.4 Some studies have emerged focusing on patients undergoing mitral valve transcatheter edge-to-edge repair (TEER)5–7; however, limited data exist for all patients undergoing mitral valve intervention.8 Whether frailty encompasses a prognostic marker of worse outcomes in both patients undergoing mitral valve surgery and TEER for MR is yet to be elucidated in detail. Although some studies have identified factors associated with poor prognosis following mitral valve intervention, few studies have assessed how frailty correlates with rehospitalization and mortality in this population.1, 9 Careful patient selection is needed for mitral valve intervention, and information on frailty status and its association with outcomes following surgery may add another important aspect in this process. Therefore, we aimed to examine the association between frailty and rate of rehospitalization and mortality in patients undergoing mitral valve intervention for MR.

Methods

Data sources

In Denmark, every citizen is provided with a unique identifier, enabling linkage across several national health registries. For this study, the following registries were used: The Danish National Patient Registry, Civil Registration System, and The Danish Pharmacy Registry. The Danish National Patient Registry was initiated in 1977, and since 1996, surgical procedure codes have been included. A primary discharge diagnosis is provided for every hospital admission, and up to several secondary diagnosis codes can be registered by the treating physician. Diagnosis codes have since 1994 been coded according to the International Classification of Diseases 10th revision (ICD), and surgical procedures codes according to the Nordic Medical-Statistical Committee (NOMESCO) classification. The Civil Registration System provides data on sex, date of birth, and date of death, while the Danish Pharmacy Registry provides data on all prescriptions redeemed from a Danish pharmacy since 1994, coded according to the Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical classification. The Danish health registries have been described in detail previously.10–12

Study population and exposure

The study population comprised all patients undergoing first-time mitral valve intervention for MR in Denmark between 1 January 1996 and 31 December 2022. Mitral valve intervention included the following surgical procedure codes: KFKB, KFKC, and KFKD, encompassing patients undergoing TEER (for procedure codes, see Supplementary material online, Table S1). These codes have been validated with a positive predictive value of 100%.13 Exclusion criteria included the following: (i) tourists or insufficient data, (ii) patients <18 years of age, (iii) no diagnosis of MR in relation to the surgical procedure, (iv) diagnosis of infective endocarditis in relation to the surgical procedure, and (v) MR considered as a complication to acute myocardial infarction (Figure 1).

Flow chart of the patient selection process.

The study population was categorized as frail or non-frail based on the Hospital Frailty Risk Score, which is a risk score assessed from hospital discharge codes based on the International Classification of Diseases, 10th revision, of up to 5 years prior to mitral valve intervention.14 The Hospital Frailty Risk Score was developed in the UK in older patients and has been shown to predict prolonged hospital stay, hospital readmission, and mortality.14 The Hospital Frailty Risk Score showed a fair overlap with Fried and Rockwood Scales, which are some of the most frequently used methods of frailty assessment.14 Patients with a Hospital Frailty Risk Score ≥5 were considered frail. The discharge codes applied for the calculation of the Hospital Frailty Risk Score are depicted in Supplementary material online, Table S2, accompanied by the frailty risk score.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was mortality within 1 year from date of mitral valve intervention. Patients were followed from the date of mitral valve intervention until date of death, 31 December 2023, or a maximum of 1 year after mitral intervention, whichever occurred first. Additionally, mortality with a follow-up of maximum 5 years was also investigated. Secondary outcomes were as follows: (i) time in hospital during the first year following discharge for mitral valve intervention and (ii) a composite outcome of time in hospital >14 days or death. For the analysis of time in hospital during the first year following discharge, patients who died during hospitalization for mitral valve surgery were excluded. In two supplementary analyses, we examined (i) time in hospital from date of surgery up until discharge and (ii) time in hospital from date of surgery with 1 year of follow-up. Time in hospital was categorized as (i) never admitted, (ii) admitted for 1–14 days, (iii) 15–28 days, and (iv) >28 days. Patients who died during follow-up did not contribute to any of the other hospitalization groups. The cumulative time in hospital was calculated based on nationwide records from all hospitals in Denmark.

Covariates

Covariates included in- and outpatient diagnosis codes from the Danish National Patient Registry prior to admission date for mitral valve intervention. Information on medical therapy was obtained from the Danish Pharmacy Registry, based on prescriptions redeemed within the 6 months prior to intervention. Patients with either a relevant diagnosis code or a redeemed prescription for an antidiabetic drug were classified as having diabetes mellitus.

Concomitant surgical interventions were identified from surgical procedure codes and were categorized as follows: (i) coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG), (ii) tricuspid valve intervention, (iii) intervention for atrial fibrillation/flutter, and (iv) aortic valve intervention. For procedure codes, see Supplementary material online, Table S1.

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics were shown in counts and percentages for categorical variables, and medians with 25th and 75th percentiles for continuous variables. Mortality was computed using the reverse Kaplan–Meier estimator, and the log-rank test was used for the assessment of crude differences in mortality between study groups. Two multivariable-adjusted Cox proportional hazards analyses were used to compute relative differences in mortality between study groups. The first model included the following covariates: frailty risk group, age, and sex. The second model also included calendar year, type of mitral valve intervention (repair, bioprosthesis, mechanical prosthesis, and TEER), malignancy, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, chronic heart failure, prior acute myocardial infarction, and liver disease. For the assessment of proportional hazards, Martingales residuals were used and reported if violated. Further, an a priori landmark sub-analysis was conducted, assessing mortality (i) from date of intervention and up to 90 days of follow-up and (ii) from day 90 to 1 year among patients surviving the first 90 days after mitral valve intervention. This landmark analysis was conducted to examine mortality excluding early perioperative mortality. Two multivariable-adjusted logistic regression analyses were computed for the comparison between study groups of a composite outcome of death or total time spent in hospital >14 days within the first year following mitral valve intervention. The same covariates were included in the logistic regression analyses as in the Cox regression analyses. For the logistic regression model, the variance inflation factors were assessed and all were found below <5 indicating no multicollinearity. Results were reported using hazard ratio (HR) or odds ratio (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). The Kruskal–Wallis test was used to examine differences in time in hospital as a continuous variable for the two study groups. A P-value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using the SAS statistical software, version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Inc., Cary, NC, USA).

In a subgroup analysis, outcomes (mortality and hospitalization) were stratified according to type of mitral valve intervention. Additionally, a subgroup analysis of burden of hospitalization was conducted by stratifying patients according to age group: (i) <70 years of age and (ii) ≥70 years. A third sub group analysis was conducted, investigating differences in the proportion of frail patients according to calendar group (1996–2002, 2003–07, 2008–12, 2013–17, and 2018–22) and related outcomes.

Ethics

In Denmark, registry-based studies conducted for the purpose of statistics or research do not require approval from ethics committee or informed consent by law.15 In accordance with the General Data Protection Regulation, the study was approved by the data-responsible institute (Capital Region of Denmark—approval number: P-2019-348). All personal identifiers were pseudonymized and subgroup classifications with three or fewer patients were not reported to ensure anonymity, in accordance with the regulations of Statistics Denmark.

Results

A total of 7782 patients were included, of which 7000 (90.0%) were considered non-frail (median age 67.0 years, 66.3% male) and 782 (10.0%) were considered frail (median age 71.0 years, 56.3% male) (Table 1). The burden of comorbidities was higher among frail patients. Notably, frail patients more often suffered from chronic heart failure (44.2 vs. 27.2%), atrial fibrillation/flutter (44.5 vs. 34.2%), and stroke (18.2 vs. 4.3%) when compared with non-frail patients (Table 1). Non-frail patients more commonly underwent mitral valve repair (63.0%) compared with frail patients (46.2%) (Table 1). On the other hand, frail patients more often underwent TEER (19.2%) compared with non-frail patients (5.0%) (Table 1).

Mortality

The 1 year mortality was 8.2% among non-frail patients and 13.4% among frail patients (crude P-value <0.0001 for difference) (Figure 2). In the age- and sex-adjusted analysis, frail patients were associated with a statistically significant higher associated mortality with HR = 1.42 (95% CI: 1.16–1.76); however, no statistically significant difference was found in the fully adjusted model [HR = 1.13 (95% CI: 0.91–1.41)] with non-frail patients as reference. With a maximum of 5 years of follow-up, mortality was 19.7% among non-frail patients and 37.3% among frail patients. Compared with non-frail patients, frail patients had a higher risk of mortality compared with non-frail patients, with HR = 1.76 (95% CI: 1.54–2.01) given adjustment for age and sex, and HR = 1.35 (95% CI: 1.17–1.55) in the fully adjusted analysis.

Mortality according to frailty status. The figure shows the cumulative incidence of all-cause mortality from date of discharge with 1 year of follow-up according to frailty status.

Examining mortality within the first 90 days of follow-up, mortality was 5.6% among non-frail patients and 8.2% among frail patients (crude P-value 0.004 for difference) (see Supplementary material online, Figure S1A). In the age- and sex-adjusted analysis, no statistically significant difference was observed in the associated mortality [HR = 1.26 (95% CI: 0.97–1.65) and HR = 1.16 (95% CI: 0.88–1.54)] in the fully adjusted analysis, with non-frail patients as reference. For patients surviving the first 90 days of follow-up, the 1 year mortality was 2.8% among non-frail patients and 5.7% among frail patients (crude P-value <0.0001 for difference) (see Supplementary material online, Figure S1B). In the adjusted analysis, the estimated HR for mortality was 1.78 (95% CI: 1.26–2.50) when adjusted for age and sex and 1.10 (95% CI: 0.77–1.58) in the fully adjusted analysis, with non-frail patients as reference.

Hospitalization

For the examination of hospitalization within the first year following discharge after mitral valve intervention, patients not alive at discharge were excluded (N = 379). Among non-frail patients, 57.2% (N = 3813) were hospitalized compared with 73.4% (N = 542) of frail patients. Time in hospital during the first year is depicted in Figure 3, overall showing that the burden of hospitalization was higher among frail patients compared with non-frail patients. The median time spent in hospital within the first year following mitral valve intervention was 5.0 days (25th and 75th percentiles: 2.0–13.0) for non-frail patients, and 8.0 days (25th–75th percentiles: 3.0–19.0) for frail patients (P < 0.0001 for difference between groups).

Time in hospital within 1 year following discharge for mitral valve intervention in pre-specified categories according to frailty status.

In a supplementary analysis examining time in hospital from date of surgery, the median length of hospital stay for mitral valve surgery was 9.0 days (25th and 75th percentiles: 6.0–14.0) for non-frail patients and 9.0 (25th and 75th percentiles: 5.0–16.0) (P-value = 0.63 for difference between groups). Time in hospital from date of surgical intervention and up until discharge are depicted in Supplementary material online, Figure S2, while Supplementary material online, Figure S2B shows time spent in hospital within 1 year following surgery illustrating a similar trend that frail patients spent more time in hospital as compared with non-frail patients.

Compared with non-frail patients, frail patients were associated with a higher odds of experiencing the composite outcome of death or >14 days spent in hospital within the first year after mitral valve intervention: OR = 1.96 (95% CI: 1.64–2.34) when adjusted for age and sex and OR = 1.65 (95% CI: 1.37–2.00) in the fully adjusted analysis.

Subgroup analysis

In a subgroup analysis, we stratified patients according to type of mitral valve intervention and identified 4634 patients who underwent repair (7.5% frail), 1067 patients who received bioprosthetic valve replacement (14.3% frail), 1214 who received mechanical valve replacement (7.7% frail), and 488 who underwent TEER (29.7% frail). Despite that the proportion of frail patients varied across the intervention types, frail patients were consistently associated with an increased burden of hospitalization regardless of intervention type (P for interaction = 0.27) (Supplementary material online, Figure S3A–D).

Burden of hospitalization and mortality within 1 year was also stratified according to age group [(i) <70 years, (ii) ≥70 years] (Supplementary material online, Figure S4). A significant interaction with age group was identified (P for interaction = 0.0002). Among patients aged <70 years (N = 4,468, 92.1% non-frail and 8.0% frail), the age- and sex-adjusted analysis showed that frail patients were associated with higher odds of experiencing the composite outcome with an OR = 2.68 (95% CI: 2.10–3.43) and OR = 2.24 (95% CI: 1.71–2.92) in the fully adjusted analysis. Among patients aged ≥70 years (N = 2,935, 86.9% non-frail and 13.1% frail), no statistically significant difference in the odds of the composite outcome was observed: OR = 1.38 (95% CI: 1.07–1.79) when adjusted for age and sex and OR = 1.20 (95% CI: 0.91–1.57) in the fully adjusted analysis, with non-frail patients as reference.

A third sub group analysis was conducted in which the main findings were stratified according to calendar groups. The proportion of frail patients was 3.9% from 1996 to 2002, 6.5% in 2003–07, 10.6% from 2008 to 2012, 14.3% from 2013 to 2017, and 12.9% from 2018 to 2022. Supplementary material online, Figure S5 displays time spent in hospital and mortality within 1 year according to calendar groups with no major differences from the main findings.

Discussion

This Danish nationwide study examined the burden of hospitalization and mortality in patients undergoing mitral valve intervention for MR according to frailty status. The study yielded three major findings: First, 10% of patients who underwent mitral valve intervention were classified as frail. Frail patients were older, had a higher burden of comorbidities, and more often underwent TEER, compared with non-frail patients. Second, a higher crude 1 year mortality was observed among frail patients (13.4 vs. 8.2%); however, adjusted analysis identified no statistically significant difference between groups. With 5 years of follow-up, frail patients were associated with a higher mortality compared with non-frail patients. Third, both the proportion of hospitalized patients and the length of hospitalization were higher among frail patients compared with non-frail patients. In adjusted analyses, frail patients were associated with higher odds of experiencing the composite outcome of hospitalization >14 days or mortality within 1 year following mitral intervention.

Among patients with aortic valve stenosis undergoing transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI), frailty has been assessed in several studies and has been associated with higher risk of readmission and mortality, and higher resource use.2, 3, 16 Frailty assessment has been suggested in risk stratification in the selection process for TAVI.17, 18 However, for patients undergoing mitral valve intervention the evidence base remains more limited. Our findings align with previous studies in TAVI populations that frail patients are associated with increased rates of hospitalization and mortality compared with non-frail patients.2, 3, 16 For patients undergoing mitral valve surgery, few studies have assessed the impact of frailty. One study from the USA (including patients from 2010 to 2014) reported that 7.9% of patients undergoing mitral valve replacement were considered frail, and frailty was associated with increased in-hospital mortality (12% for frail patients and 4% for non-frail), 30 day readmission (28 vs. 20%), and increased hospitalization costs.8 For patients undergoing TEER, some studies have assessed how frailty impacts the prognosis following intervention. These studies reported similar findings, in that patient frailty was associated with higher resource use, hospitalization rate, and mortality.5–7, 19–21 As TEER is mostly considered in patients deemed inoperable or at high surgical risk,18 it is not surprising that the proportion of frail patients was identified to be higher among patients who underwent TEER. Our findings suggest that even for a less invasive procedure (such as TEER) compared with mitral valve surgery, frailty remains an important prognostic marker. Also, minimal invasive cardiac surgery has addressed these findings, and the techniques have a second era of implementation these years, also in Denmark. Most data have been presented in younger, non-frail patients.22–24 Yet, a multi-centre study from Italy assessed minimally invasive mitral valve surgery in elderly frail patients and the same positive findings have been shown in minimally invasive cardiac surgery.25

Identifying frailty is a complex assessment, and several aspects may be needed to provide a full picture of this multidimensional syndrome.26 It has previously been suggested that one or more of the following criteria are associated with frailty: unintentional weight loss/sarcopenia, weakness, poor endurance/exhaustion, slowness, and low physical activity level.27 The Clinical Frailty Scale is also a widely used tool, grading the patient’s dependence on others to perform everyday activities28 Functional tests such as gait speed and chair raise time are also commonly used in frailty assessment.29 A large international, multi-centre, prospective cohort study compared seven frailty risk scales for the prediction of poor outcomes following aortic valve replacement and identified that the Essential Frailty Toolset (encompassing four instruments: lower extremity weakness, cognitive impairment, anaemia, and hypoalbuminemia) outperformed other frailty scales.30 In our study, we identified frail patients from hospital diagnosis codes using the Hospital Frailty Risk Score. A limitation of this approach is that ICD-codes may not capture all aspects of functional capacity and changes in frailty within short time periods may be difficult to detect/assess. Further, patients with low functional capacity who have not been in contact with the health care system (hence not given an ICD-10 code) would be misclassified as patients with low frailty. This may incorporate younger patients but also patients presenting with acute illness. From the registries used in our study we were not able to assess frailty from a bedside evaluation as the Clinical Frailty Scale or the Fried Frailty Phenotype, which is a limitation to our study. Nevertheless, the Hospital Frailty Risk Score has been well-validated and has shown a fair agreement with the Fried frailty phenotype and the Rockwood Clinical Frailty Scale.14 Further, the Hospital Frailty Risk score is a consistent tool requiring few resources when reliable, administrative registries are available. Our study does not identify whether assessment of frailty prior to intervention can improve patient selection for mitral valve intervention—but it does identify a subgroup of patients at higher risk of worse outcomes compared with non-frail patients.

In a subgroup analysis, we identified age as an effect modifier for the association between frailty and the composite outcome of hospitalization burden and mortality. The association was most pronounced among young patients (<70 years). This finding may be interpreted in the light of the fact that, among older patients (≥70 years), competing risks (i.e. higher burden of comorbidities) for hospitalization and mortality are more prevalent and thus may attenuate the association between frailty and increased rates of hospitalization and mortality. Usually, systematic assessment of frailty is more commonly conducted in older patients; however, our finding suggest that this assessment could be a clinical parameter of interest in patients <70 years undergoing mitral valve intervention. However, other frailty measures could possibly provide further insights for the association.

This study encompasses patients over a period of 26 years and during this period there have been changes in surgical techniques and characteristics of patients undergoing mitral valve surgery. However, when our findings were stratified according to calendar groups, we were not able to detect major changes from the main findings.

Our study has some limitations. First, frailty was assessed according to the Hospital Frailty Risk Score. This risk score has been validated in a large cohort; however, clinical measures such as timed up and go, hand grip strength, sarcopenia, and dependence on others for daily function were not available from the registries used. Second, we were not able to categorize patients according to mitral valve regurgitation aetiology from the registries used. Further, the following data were not available from the registries used: echocardiographic characteristics prior to mitral valve intervention, urgency of mitral valve intervention, laboratory values prior to intervention (including plasma albumin and N-terminal pro-B-type natriuretic peptide), sarcopenia, and preoperative scoring systems such as the EuroSCORE-II. In particular, the absence of data on MR aetiology, including echocardiographic data, urgency of the surgical intervention limits further risk stratification. Third, we identified a higher, though statistically insignificant difference in 1 year mortality between groups but the risk of a Type II error cannot be ruled out for this finding. Fourth, the observational design of the study precludes that causal relations can be made.

Conclusions

In this nationwide Danish cohort of patients undergoing mitral valve intervention for MR, we categorized patients according to frailty status based on the Hospital Frailty Risk Score. The overall proportion of frail patients was 10%, with significant variation across mitral intervention type, and the highest proportion was observed among patients who underwent TEER (30%). Frail patients were associated with more time in hospital within the first year following surgery compared with non-frail patients, but no statistically significant difference was found in the 1 year mortality. This association was irrespective of intervention type. Our study highlights the need for studies investigating pre- and post-discharge interventions aimed at improving outcomes among frail patients undergoing mitral valve intervention for MR.
