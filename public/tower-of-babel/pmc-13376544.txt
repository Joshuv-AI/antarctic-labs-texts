Foundation and early survival of Atta sexdens rubropilosa (Hymenoptera: Formicidae) colonies in contrasting environments
Authors: R. S. Camargo, L.C Forti, C.A.O. Matos, F. A. G. Guilherme, N.C. Oliveira, A.A. Moreira, J.E. Serrao, M.A. Castellani, J.C. Zanuncio
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Leaf-cutting ant queens of the genus Atta (Hymenoptera: Formicidae) leave mature nests during nuptial flights to establish new colonies, but the factors influencing patterns of colony foundation remain poorly understood. This study describes foundation and evaluates early colony survival of Atta sexdens rubropilosa under contrasting environmental conditions. The research was conducted in Botucatu, São Paulo state, Brazil, in two 700 m² areas: (A) an open, sun-exposed, urban site with bare soil, and (B) a shaded area near a river, with riparian vegetation and native trees. Nine hundred and six queens were recorded founding nests, 556 (61.4%) in the open area and 350 (38.6%) in the shaded area, during the nuptial flight in November 2024, resulting in 0.794 and 0.500 queens/m², respectively. After four months, the survival of 300 marked nests in each area was evaluated. Thirty and 135 nests remained active in the open (0.043 nests/m²) and shaded (0.193 nests/m²) sites, respectively (p < 0.001). These results indicate that colony foundations were not evenly distributed between the two environments and that early survival differed markedly between them. Because the study is observational and based on two non-replicated sites, the findings should be interpreted as descriptive evidence of habitat-associated differences rather than definitive proof of active habitat selection.

Introduction

Nests older than 36 months of leaf-cutting ants of the genus Atta (Hymenoptera: Formicidae) produce winged males and females1, which leave the nests in large numbers during warm and rainy periods to perform nuptial flights and mating2. Flight patterns vary among species; for example, the nuptial flight of Atta texana is a single, large, and precisely synchronized event before dawn, whereas that of Atta sexdens may last several hours beginning around midday3. After mating, queens land on the ground and initiate colony foundation3. At this stage, mortality is extremely high, as adverse environmental conditions, predation, and pathogens eliminate more than 95% of founding queens4.

Once on the ground, queens must excavate a chamber in the soil, where they remain claustral and rely on internal reserves to rear the first brood and cultivate their symbiotic fungus1,2,5,6. The excavation process, its depth, duration, and energetic cost, is influenced by soil physical properties and temperature, and has been studied mainly under laboratory conditions7–11. These factors are closely associated with the success of colony foundation, as they affect physiological stress and energy expenditure. However, they do not necessarily explain how queens arrive at particular microsites in the field12[,13.

In contrast, field evidence on the environmental context of nest establishment remains scarce. Available observations suggest that nest densities of Atta sexdens may be higher in forested environments, particularly in tree-fall clearings, than in adjacent open pastures14. This indicates that landscape structure may influence the spatial distribution of newly founded nests, although the mechanisms involved—behavioral preference, differential settlement, or post-establishment survival—remain difficult to disentangle in natural conditions15.

In the present study evaluates whether colonies foundations are spatially non-random between two contrasting environments and whether early colony survival differs between them. The results are interpreted conservatively, recognizing that the observational design does not allow causal inference about habitat preference. For that, the study was conducted in two areas: in an urban perimeter with bare, open soil (10 m × 70 m = 700 m²) (A), and another near a river with riparian vegetation 50 m away and 20 m of native tree plantations providing shade (B), also with 700 m², both suitable for A. sexdens rubropilosa16,17.

Results

PHASE 1 – Number of founding queens in two areas (A and B).

The total number of dealate founding queens in the process of initiating nest excavation recorded in the two sampled areas was 906, with 556 in the urban perimeter (A) and 350 near the river, characterized by 50 m of riparian vegetation and a 20 m strip of native tree plantation (B) (Table 1), differing significantly between these areas (χ² = 46.385, df = 1, p = 9.715 × 10⁻¹²). In the urban area, with exposed and open soil (700 m²), the 556 records (61.37% of the total) resulted in a founding density of 0.794 queens/m². In contrast, the area near the river, with riparian vegetation and native tree plantations (700 m²), had 350 records (38.63% of the total), a lower number than in the urban site, corresponding to a founding density of 0.500 queens/m² (Table 1).

Discussion

The survival of the queen and the success of nest establishment depend strongly on the site where the nest is initiated (Souza et al., 2022). During colony foundation, queens face severe energetic constraints: carbohydrate reserves are largely depleted during the nuptial flight, while lipid reserves fuel excavation and the claustral phase, with body proteins also being mobilized to support survival and early colony development22a, b10,23;. At the same time, mortality rates are extremely high during flight and excavation due to intense predation pressure from birds, amphibians, reptiles, mammals, and arthropods such as Canthon spp. (Coleoptera: Scarabaeidae) and ants (Solenopsis, Paratrechina, and Nomamyrmex)4,24–26. Together, these physiological and ecological constraints make the nest-founding phase one of the most critical periods in the life cycle of leaf-cutting ants.

Beyond predation, abiotic conditions at the nesting site can further influence queen survival. Excavation in denser or drier soils may increase cuticular abrasion and water loss, elevating physiological stress and potentially increasing oxidative damage due to the greater physical effort required for digging27,28; Sohal & Buchan, 198129;. Therefore, microhabitat characteristics that affect soil structure and microclimate are expected to play a central role in determining early colony survival.

Within this context, our results reveal an apparent paradox. A greater number of colony foundations occurred in the open, sunny, and exposed area than in the more vegetated and shaded area near the river, even though shaded environments are generally considered more favorable for early colony development and survival (Souza et al., 2022). A similar pattern has been observed in other Atta species, in which founding queens concentrate nests in particular microhabitats, such as forest clearings, where nest densities may exceed those in surrounding open pastures14. Thus, nest-site distribution does not necessarily align with the microhabitats that maximize early survival.

The survival data reinforce this mismatch between foundation frequency and success. Of the 300 nests initially recorded in each area, only 30 remained active in the exposed site after four months, compared with 135 in the area with riparian vegetation. This pattern is consistent with previous findings for Atta sexdens rubropilosa, which showed markedly higher mortality of incipient colonies in sunny environments (91%) than in shaded ones (58%), along with reduced brood production and fungal biomass under higher irradiance and greater soil temperature fluctuations11. Together, these studies indicate that increased solar exposure negatively affects the microclimatic stability required for successful claustral development.

The question that emerges, therefore, is why queens frequently initiate nests in environments associated with poorer survival prospects. One possibility is that founding queens respond to surface cues such as higher soil temperature or radiant energy, which may be more detectable or attractive during their diurnal activity period3. Another non-mutually exclusive explanation involves soil biotic factors. Sunnier areas may harbor lower densities of soil microorganisms, potentially reducing antagonistic interactions with the symbiotic fungus during the earliest stages of colony development, as suggested for Atta laevigata in soils with lower fertility and microbial abundance30. Similar ecological mechanisms could influence nest-site selection in A. sexdens rubropilosa, although targeted studies are needed to test this hypothesis directly.

Finally, it is important to interpret these patterns with caution. The greater occurrence of colony foundations in exposed sites, despite their association with lower early survival, does not necessarily indicate that Atta sexdens rubropilosa queens fail to select suitable environments. Because our study was observational, we cannot disentangle active preference from constraints imposed by landscape structure, soil availability, or queen dispersal behavior. Therefore, our results should not be viewed as evidence against the “mother knows best” principle31, but rather as evidence that nest distribution and early survival differ between contrasting habitats, while the mechanisms driving these patterns remain unresolved.

Conclusion

The higher ocurrence of colony foundations in exposed sites, despite their association with lower early survival, indicates that nest establishment of Atta sexdens rubropilosa was spatially non-random between the two environments studied. However, because the study was observational, these patterns cannot be interpreted as evidence of active habitat preference or maladaptive choice by queens. Instead, the results show that colony foundation and early survival differed between open and shaded habitats, highlighting the importance of environmental context during the initial stages of colony development without allowing causal inference about queen decision-making.
