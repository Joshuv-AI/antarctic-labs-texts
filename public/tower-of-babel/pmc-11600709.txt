Is polytrauma really just a simple accident? Recurrent characteristic of polytrauma
Authors: Cong Zhang, Peidong Zhang, Deng Chen, Qian Wan, Gang Yin, Yang Liu, Jialiu Luo, Shunyao Chen, Zhiqiang Lin, Shuaipeng Gu, Hui Li, Liming Dong, Teding Chang, Zhaohui Tang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPolytrauma was defined as a severe traumatic injury and believed that it was a sudden and unpredictable incident. Is polytrauma really just a simple accident? In order to comprehensively answer this question, the study sought to determine the frequency and specific risk factors associated with polytrauma recidivism, while also comparing the initial and subsequent injury events among recidivists.MethodsA multicenter, retrospective cohort study was conducted at four Advanced Trauma Centers’ emergency surgery or traumatic intensive care units (TICUs) between August 2020 and July 2023. A total of 2490 consecutive trauma patients who met the criteria consecutively were recruited and analyzed in the study. Risk factors for recurrent polytrauma were identified through the use of logistic regression analysis. A nomogram was created using the results from a multivariate logistic regression analysis and the rms package in R.ResultsIn polytrauma, the rate of recidivism was 44.6% (672/1507), then recidivists were predominantly male (80.4%) and frequently within the 45 to 54 year-old age range (51.3%). Recidivists in polytrauma patients overall had a median time to reinjury of 27 months, as measured by the interquartile range (IQR). The polytrauma patients in the recent traumatic event were often more severe than that in the initial event, as patients had higher ISS scores and lower GCS scores (P < 0.01). Moreover, polytrauma recidivism were associated with poorer prognosis and increased healthcare costs. Polytrauma patients with the specific characteristics were found to have a higher likelihood of experiencing a subsequent recurrence, including being male (OR = 3.82,95% CI: 2.21–6.83), aged 45–54 years old (OR = 2.62,95% CI: 2.13–6.32),experiencing sleep deprivation (OR = 2.38,95% CI: 1.32–4.25), working in construction (OR = 2.72,95% CI: 1.44–5.42), working as delivery staff (OR = 3.65,95% CI: 1.51–7.96) and being involved in an electric bicycle collision (OR = 2.85,95% CI: 1.31–5.64).ConclusionPolytrauma recidivism is associated with a high recurrence rate, poorer clinical outcomes, and elevated healthcare costs. Key predictive markers for recidivism include being male, aged 45–54 years, experiencing sleep deprivation, employment in construction or delivery roles, and involvement in electric bicycle collisions. These findings highlight polytrauma recidivists as a critical target for primary prevention efforts. Public health strategies should prioritize tailored interventions to reduce recidivism, aiming to mitigate morbidity, mortality, and associated healthcare burdens in this high-risk population.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13690-024-01460-9.Keywords: Polytrauma, Recurrence, Recidivism, Multicenter cohort study, Risk factors

Contributions to the literature• A premiere, multicenter, retrospective cohort study was performed to ascertain the rate and independent risk factors associated with polytrauma recidivism.• The study indicate that polytrauma recidivism represents a substantial public health concern, as evidenced by its frequency of recidivism.• The study emphasized that polytrauma recidivist patients represent a viable target for primary trauma prevention efforts and it is imperative for public health initiatives to prioritize the development of interventions aimed at mitigating polytrauma recidivism and preventing avoidable fatalities.

Introduction

Trauma is currently the fifth leading cause of death in China, following cancer, stroke, heart disease, and respiratory illnesses [1, 2]. Advancements in the treatment and prevention of cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases as well as respiratory disease, driven by societal development and progress in science and technology have left trauma as a leading cause of mortality and morbidity [3]. Based on statistical data published by the National Bureau of Statistics of China (NBS), a total of 256,409 traffic accidents occurred in 2022, resulting in approximately 263,621 injuries and 60,676 fatalities. The overall economic impact of these traffic accidents in China for the year 2022 is estimated to be 1.2 billion Chinese Yuan (CNY). Productivity loss represents the largest component of this economic burden, accounting for 72% of the total cost [4]. Polytrauma was defined as severe traumatic injuries and accounted for only 10% of total trauma incident. However, polytrauma results in 30–50% of traumatic fatalities, necessitating comprehensive medical care and extended stays in the hospital and recovery times because of severe injuries and difficult complications, leading to significant societal and financial consequences [5–7]. Polytrauma is often classified as a “disease set” composed of different types of traumatic diseases, rather than independent diseases, with a prevailing belief that it is an unpredictable incident that occurs suddenly. Unfortunately, trauma surgeons frequently express frustration at repeatedly encountering the same patients suffered severe traumatic injury in surgical emergency unit (SEU) or traumatic intensive care unit (TICU). This begs the question, is polytrauma really just a simple accident? Previous research indicates that in certain populations, trauma is not isolated incidents but rather a recurring phenomenon [8–11]. This repetitive pattern of trauma, known as ‘trauma recidivism,’ is defined as any prior trauma (AIS ≧ 2) requiring hospital care within the past 5 years or older trauma resulting in functional impairments in daily activities [12, 13]. It may account for up to half of trauma visits among the urban vagrants, alcohol addicts or drug addicts, who predominantly attributed to gunshot wounds, knife stabbings, and other forms of violent injuries [14, 15]. However, the cause of injury for polytrauma populations are primarily resulting from high-energy incidents such as road traffic accidents, falls from heights or work-related crushing injuries. Unfortunately, there is currently limited research and little known about the recurrence of polytrauma.

One of the responsibilities of trauma care systems is prevention, although trauma care systems usually focus on tertiary prevention, which aims to improve prognosis and minimize mortality after severe traumatic injury has occurred. It is widely acknowledged that primary prevention, or the prevention of trauma occurrence, is preferable [16]. Previous studies revealed that trauma recidivists (such as, urban vagrants or drug addicts, et al.) have an average time to re-injury of 18.4 months, suggesting a potential opportunity for public health intervention [17]. As such, polytrauma recidivists represent a viable target for primary trauma prevention efforts. However, until now the modifiable risk factors linked to polytrauma recidivism and potential intervention targets remain inadequately understood.

To the best of our knowledge, there is a notable gap in the literature regarding large-sample cohort studies on polytrauma recidivism, which remains an understudied yet critical public health issue. Consequently, we conducted a retrospective, multicenter cohort study aimed at identifying the frequency and specific risk factors linked to polytrauma recidivism. The underlying assumption of the study is that polytrauma recidivism represents a substantial public health concern, as evidenced by its frequency of recidivism. This study’s primary objective is to identify intrinsic patient characteristics that may predispose individuals to polytrauma recidivism, thus filling a crucial research gap and providing valuable insights. These findings can inform the development of enhanced preventive measures at both population and individual levels, ultimately benefiting healthcare providers, policy makers, and researchers seeking to reduce the recurrence of polytrauma and improve patient outcomes.

Materials and methods

Design of the study and participants

From August 2020 to July 2023, we conducted a consecutive sampling of all trauma patients admitted to four Level I trauma centers verified by the China Trauma Rescue & Treatment Association (CTRTA). The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) age ≥ 18 years; (2) Patients with traumatic injury; (3) a first set of assessments within the first 48 hours after injury. Exclusion criteria for this analysis were (1) physical disabilities (included: Paraplegia, total paralysis, total loss of sight, et. al.), epileptic seizure, organic mental disorders (dementia, delirium), or Alzheimer’s disease.; (2) missing clinical records. Polytrauma patients are classified according to the ‘new Berlin’ definition as having AIS ≥ 3 in two or more body regions, along with at least one additional parameter such as hypotension, unconsciousness, acidosis, coagulopathy, or being over 70 years old [18]. Monotrauma is defined as an AIS severity of ≥ 2 in one body region with no injuries in other regions [19, 20]. A total of 2,597 patients were consecutively enrolled across the four trauma centers, of whom 2490 consecutive trauma patients met the inclusion criteria were enrolled. After data query, all enrolled trauma patients were separated into different subgroups based on their site of injury: monotrauma (MT) group versus polytrauma (PT) group; or according to with/without trauma recidivism: trauma recidivist (TR) group versus non-recidivist (NR) group.

Data collection

Each patient was followed up with a question to determine the mechanism of trauma, marital status, employment and other baseline data (Attached Fig. 1). Patients with no recorded response underwent analysis of the electronic medical record (EMR) to identify any previous visits to our facility. In cases where patients could not be approached in person, efforts were made to contact them during outpatient follow-up visits or via telephone. The clinical characteristics of each patient were gathered from electronic medical and nursing records within the initial 48 h of admission, encompassing data such as Glasgow coma scale (GCS), Injury Severity Score (ISS), Shock index (SI), laboratory values, and discharge record. The hospital course was documented, noting the ICU stay length, ventilator use duration, and mortality rates [21, 22]. References 21 and 22 are included here to cite standard methodologies for documenting ICU stay length, duration of ventilator use, and mortality rates.

Case identification procedure

Study end points

The primary outcome of this research was to evaluate the frequency of polytrauma recidivism, with the secondary end points including identifying risk factors associated with polytrauma recidivism and describing the clinical characteristics of affected individuals.

Ethical statement

The Tongji Hospital Institutional Review Board at Huazhong University of Science and Technology approved this study on 22 July 2020.Approval was obtained from the local review boards at each study site, including Xiantao First People’s Hospital Institutional Review Board (approved on January 10, 2023), Tianmen First People’s Hospital Institutional Review Board (approved on January 8, 2020), and Xiangyang Central Hospital Institutional Review Board (approved on October 10, 2021). The research was carried out in compliance with the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. Consent was obtained from every patient or their legally authorized representative participating in the study.

Statistical analysis

Prior to analyzing the data, the normality of the distribution for continuous variables was assessed by using the Shapiro-Wilk test and equal variance was evaluated with Levene’s test. Categorical variables were analyzed using frequency counts and percentages, while continuous variables were summarized using median and interquartile ranges (IQR) for non-normally distributed data, or mean ± standard deviation (SD) for normally distributed data. To compare differences between groups, the Student’s t-test for normally distributed continuous variables and the Mann-Whitney U test for non-normally distributed variables were utilized. Categorical variables were compared using the Chi-squared (χ²) test. Risk factors for recurrent polytrauma were identified through logistic regression analysis, which included both univariate and multivariate analyses to control for potential confounders. A nomogram was developed based on the results of the multivariate logistic regression analysis, using the rms package in R to facilitate visual interpretation of the risk factors. The nomogram’s performance was evaluated through a calibration plot and its predictive accuracy was assessed using ROC curve analysis, with the AUC calculated to determine discrimination ability. All statistical analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.4.0) and GraphPad Prism software (version 9.3.1) from GraphPad Software Inc., San Diego, California, USA. A significance level of p < 0.05 was set for all statistical tests.

Results

Characteristics of trauma patients

Between August 2020 and July 2023, the trauma centers received 2597 patients with trauma injuries. 107 patients were excluded due to lack of complete information and a total of 2490 consecutive trauma patients met the inclusion criteria and were divided into monotrauma group (n = 983) and polytrauma group (n = 1507). Based on there is recidivist or not, the monotrauma group is further divided into a recidivist group (n = 70) and a n-recidivist group (n = 913), which for polytrauma group was a recidivist group (n = 672) and a n-recidivist group (n = 835) (Fig. 1).

Table 2 displays the patient demographics and traits. In polytrauma group, the mean value of ISS was 21.1, while for GCS and SI were 11.2 and 0.9 respectively. Overall, 57.2% (862/1507) polytrauma patients suffered head injury, 62.3% (939/1507) polytrauma patients had a thorax injury, 19.3% (204/1507) suffered abdomen injury, 33.2% (501/1507) suffered spine injury, and 14.2% (150/1507) suffered pelvis injury, while lower-extremity and upper-extremity were recorded for 55.3% (833/1507) and 45.3% (683/1507) patients, respectively. The injury was mainly caused by car collision (28.6%, 431/1507), followed by electric bicycle collision (23.2%, 350/1507), low-energy fall (16.5%, 248/1507) and high-energy fall (14.2%, 214/1507) (Table 2).

Distribution of the trauma recidivism rate

The recidivism rate was 7.1% (70/983) in monotrauma patients (MT) group, and was 44.6% (672/1507) in polytrauma patients (PT) group (Fig. 1). As shown in Table 1, of these 70 monotrauma recidivists, 91.4% (64/70) patients were readmitted for the second time and 8.6% (6/70) patients were readmitted for the third time. And of these 672 polytrauma recidivists, 81.4% (547/672) recidivists patients were readmitted for the second time and 18.5% (124/672) recidivists patients were readmitted for the third time. The individual who experienced four separate instances of trauma was a 48-year-old male who initially suffered a work-related head and upper-extremity injury from a fall and was subsequently admitted to a nearby trauma center. over a span of 26 months, this recidivist patient was readmitted to the trauma center for three additional times due to motor vehicle accidents, resulting in re-injury to his head and thorax on two occasions, and to his abdomen, pelvis, and lower extremities during the final readmission. Despite the severity of these injuries, the patient survived each incident and was discharged with functional independence (Table 1).

Characteristics of trauma recidivists

Sociodemographic characteristics and injury profile between recidivist and N-recidivist in trauma patients

As shown in Table 2, in monotrauma group, recidivists were predominantly male (82.9% vs. 54.8%; p < 0.01), and frequently within the 18 to 29 year-old age range (45.7% vs. 20.0%; p < 0.01) when compared with n-recidivists. In polytrauma group, recidivists were predominantly male (80.4% vs. 48.3%; p < 0.01), and frequently within the 45 to 54 year-old age range (51.3% vs. 26.9%; p < 0.01). Polytrauma recidivists had higher rate of sleep deprivation than n-recidivists (59.1% vs. 28.3%; p < 0.01), and then the difference is not significant (15.6% vs. 12.4%, p > 0.05) in monotrauma group. As for the rate of married, there was an opposite results between monotrauma group and polytrauma group. In polytrauma group, recidivists had higher rate of married (82.3% vs. 54.4%; p < 0.01), and then in monotrauma group, recidivists had lower rate (21.4% vs. 48.3%; p < 0.01). Furthermore, in monotrauma group, those recidivists were mostly student (28.5%) and unemployment (20.0%), and then in polytrauma group, those were mostly construction workers (27.8%) and delivery staff (21.4%). Regarding the cause of injury, in polytrauma group, electric bicycle collision was more common among recidivists (27.7% vs. 19.6%, p < 0.01), and then in monotrauma group, bicycle was more common (32.9% vs. 22.1%, p < 0.01) when compared with n-recidivists (Table 2).

The hospital course and outcomes of recidivists versus non-recidivists following trauma

In polytrauma, there was no significant difference in Injury Severity Score (ISS), Shock Index (SI) scores and Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) scores between recidivists and n-recidivists (p > 0.05). In addition, the duration of ICU, the rate of mechanical ventilation and use of vasoactive drug were significantly different (P < 0.01). The recidivists had higher ratio of mechanical ventilation (39.4% vs. 21.4%, P < 0.01), use of vasoactive drug(61.2% vs. 40.5%, P < 0.01) and longer of ICU stays (15days vs. 6days, P < 0.01) (Table 2). Unfortunately, in-hospital mortality was also higher among recidivists (15.3% vs. 7.1%, p < 0.01). As to disease burden, the recidivists had higher rate of ADL/IADL disability (43.8% vs. 12.7%, p < 0.01) and hospitalization cost (10.6 (ten thousand) vs. 3.8 (ten thousand), p < 0.01) (Table 2).

Trend of changes in reinjure time

As depicted in Fig. 2, the typical time to reinjury for recidivists was 27 months, with an interquartile range of 34 months. The analysis categorized by mechanism revealed that patients experiencing an electric bicycle collision had a median (IQR) time to reinjury of 13 (18) months during their initial admission, while patients involved in a high-energy fall had a median (IQR) time to reinjury of 37 (43) months.

Trend of changes in the cause of injury

Polytrauma recidivists experienced more electric bicycle collision (31.3% vs. 13.8%, p < 0.01) during their recent traumatic event than during their initial event. High-energy fall exhibited the opposite result (14.7% vs. 31.4%, p < 0.01). 58.1% of recidivists with electric bicycle collision during their initial traumatic event returned to the hospital for the same type of injury. Nearly half of the recidivists (46.4%) returned with the same mechanism of injury on their recent traumatic event. Electric bicycle injuries had the highest rate of same-mechanism return (Table 3; Fig. 3).

The evolution trend in traumatic condition

As showed in Table 3, the polytrauma recidivists in the recent traumatic event were often more severe than that in the initial event, as patients had higher ISS scores and lower GCS scores (P < 0.01). And those patients in recent traumatic event may have gone through a more severe shock as patients had higher SI scores (P < 0.01). Furthermore, the need for mechanical ventilation (MV) was significantly greater (56.8% vs. 26.3%; P < 0.01). The rate of use of vasoactive drug was significantly higher (71.1% vs. 41.2%; P < 0.01) and duration of ICU was marginally longer [median (IQR): 16 (19) days vs. 7 (13) days; P < 0.01] during the recent trauma-related admission. As to laboratory indicators, those in the recent traumatic event had a higher levels of serum lactate, IL-6, APTT and higher ratio of ABE≤-6 and INR ≥ 1.4, which were retrospectively collected in the first 48 h after admission. As to disease burden, polytrauma recidivists in the recent traumatic event had higher hospitalization cost than in the initial event (15.3 vs. 8.7; P < 0.01) (Table 3).

Risk factors in polytrauma recidivists

Risk factors for recidivism in polytrauma patients were examined through group comparisons (Table 2) and further analyzed in both univariate and multivariate models (Table 4). As shown in Table 2, males, aged 45–54, married individuals, those experiencing sleep deprivation, construction workers, delivery staff, electric bicycle collisions, and low-energy falls were identified as variables significantly associated with recidivism (p < 0.01). Table 4 presents the independent factors linked to recidivism based on multivariate regression analysis. Men had a 3.82 (95% CI 2.21–6.83) higher risk of experiencing a second injury, while those in the 45–54 age group had a 2.62 (95% CI 2.13–6.32) higher likelihood. Moreover, patients with sleep deprivation were 2.38(95% CI: 1.32–4.25) times more likely to experience another injury. Construction workers(OR = 2.72; 95% CI: 1.44–5.42), delivery staff(OR = 3.65; 95% CI: 1.51–7.96),and electric bicycle collision (OR = 2.85, 95% CI: 1.31–5.64) had also been demonstrated to be independent risk factors for polytrauma recidivism (Table 4).

When utilizing the nomogram, locate the position of every variable on the appropriate axis, connect a line to the points axis based on the number of points, sum up the points from all variables, and then draw a line from the total points axis to ascertain the probabilities of recidivism at the bottom line of the nomogram (Fig. 4A). Discussion

Previous research has demonstrated that trauma, once thought to be an isolated incident, is in fact a recurrent condition in certain populations [11, 15, 23]. Our study found that polytrauma patients (44.6%) had the higher rates of trauma recidivism compared with monotrauma patients (7.1%). Trauma recidivist, characterized by repeated trauma incidents, has been shown to result in functional impairment, which heightened socioeconomic burdens following the initial trauma event [24]. Moreover, trauma recidivism is associated with elevated mortality rates and increased healthcare costs [25]. Our study indicated that in-hospital mortality rates were significantly higher among recidivists compared with n-recidivists in polytrauma patients (15.3% vs. 7.1%), and also exhibited a higher prevalence of ADL/IADL disability (43.8% vs. 12.7%) and incurred greater hospitalization costs (12.6 vs. 8.3 (ten thousand CNY)). To mitigate the risk of polytrauma recidivism, the primary strategy should focus on prevention and the implementation of educational interventions. Thus, this study examines the demographics, injury mechanisms, patterns of injury, and outcomes in individuals with repeated polytrauma to recognize trends and patterns for more effective prevention strategies.

This study aimed to compare sociodemographic characteristics, injury profile, and hospital outcomes between recidivist and non-recidivist patients with monotrauma and polytrauma. The finding revealed that 7.1% of monotrauma patients and 44.6% of polytrauma patients experienced recidivism. Consistent with previous research, a majority of recidivists in trauma patients were male [12, 14, 26]. The analysis of characteristics indicates that a higher percentage of recidivists among monotrauma patients present at a younger age, with 45.7% of incidents occurring in the 18–29 age group. However, a higher percentage of recidivists in polytrauma patients present at a middle age, with 51.3% of incidents occurring in the 45–54 age group. One potential rationale for this disparity is that individuals in distinct age brackets may be engaged in diverse occupations and work settings, resulting in varying mechanisms of injury. The younger demographic is more inclined to be students (28.5%) or unemployed (20.0%), with a higher likelihood of sustaining injuries from bicycle accidents (32.9%) or low-energy falls (30.0%). Conversely, the middle-aged cohort is more likely to be employed as manual laborers such as construction workers (27.8%) and delivery staff (21.4%), with a greater propensity for injury due to electric bicycle accidents (27.7%) or car accidents (25.1%). Meanwhile, Our study indicates that a higher percentage of recidivists among monotrauma patients were unmarried. In contrast, the recidivists among polytrauma patients were more likely to be married, probably leading to greater financial strain and forcing them to engage in more jobs, even more dangerous ones [27].

We underscored the importance of distinguishing between recidivist and n-recidivist among polytrauma patients in our analysis. In n-recidivist among polytrauma, the leading mechanism of injury was car collision, which in recidivist among polytrauma was electric bicycle accidents. Moreover, a prior investigation demonstrated that individuals with a history of repeated traumatic events were prone to experiencing the same type of injury in both occurrences, with a prevalence as high as 75% [28]. Within our sample, 46.4% of recidivists returned with the same mechanism of injury on their recent traumatic event. Additional examination indicates that individuals with a history of polytrauma are more likely to be involved in electric bicycle accidents in their most recent event compared to their initial event, whereas the opposite trend was observed with high-energy falls.

In the context of the Injury Severity Score (ISS), Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS), and Shock Index (SI), it was found that there is no significant difference between recidivists and n-recidivists among polytrauma. However, there were the higher rates of vasoactive drug use (61.2% vs. 40.5%), mechanical ventilation (39.4% vs. 21.4%), and longer durations of ICU stay (15 vs. 6) in polytrauma recidivist group. Meanwhile, the mortality rate among polytrauma patients with a history of recidivism is notably elevated compared with those without a history of recidivism, potentially due to a decline in physiological reserves including gastrointestinal, cardiovascular, and immune function, as well as an increase in chronic complications following repeated trauma [29, 30]. This ultimately results in an unfavorable prognosis for the patient.

In the present study, the median time to reinjury for polytrauma recidivists patients overall was 27 months. Research on a group of repeat offenders in Canada found that, on average, it took 41 months for them to commit another offense [31], and Kaufmann et al. showed an average time of around 10 months before experiencing another injury [11]. A diverse range of reinjury timelines have been documented in the scientific literature, potentially influenced by factors such as sample size and access to trauma care facilities of varying capabilities. Additionally, the timing of reinjury may be influenced by the specific cause of the initial injury, as suggested by our research findings. For instance, our study revealed a discrepancy of approximately 24 months in the time to reinjury between electric bicycle collisions (the median time: 13 months) and high-energy falls (the median time: 37 months), with electric bicycle collisions resulting in earlier recidivism. This disparity may be attributed to the reduced mobility typically experienced by individuals following a high-energy fall and greater control over factors that could contribute to reinjury. Possible factors could involve reducing the risk of falls by keeping the floor, stairs, and ladders clear, as well as using safety measures.

In our study, we identified several early indicators of following injury, including the ISS score, GCS score, shock index, serum lactate, IL-6, and APTT, which were able to differentiate disease severity between initial traumatic events and recent traumatic events in recidivist polytrauma patients. Our study found that recidivist among polytrauma patients involved in recent traumatic events exhibited significantly higher levels of illness, as indicated by higher ISS scores, shock index, and lower GCS scores. These recidivist patients also had longer stay in the ICU, higher rates of mechanical ventilation, and a greater use of vasoactive drugs compared with patients involved in their initial traumatic event. This finding aligns with clinical observations and suggests that individuals who experience repeated traumatic injuries are at an increased risk for severe consequences following polytrauma. Specifically, severe shock following polytrauma can result in poor organ perfusion, as evidenced by elevated serum lactate levels. Furthermore, severe polytrauma results in heightened stress, an exaggerated inflammatory response (evidenced by elevated levels of IL-6), and disrupted coagulation function (indicated by prolonged APTT). The activation of hypoperfusion and inflammatory cascade in polytrauma patients experiencing severe shock leads to the development of metabolic acidosis and hypothermia, exacerbating coagulopathy through platelet and coagulation factor consumption and dysfunction [32]. Unfortunately, this condition worsens in recidivist polytrauma patients due to diminished physiological reserves, including renal and cardiovascular frailty [33]. Longer stay in the ICU and higher rates of mechanical ventilation in trauma patients have been linked to prolonged hospital stays, and substantial financial burden [34]. Our findings indicates that individuals experiencing polytrauma recidivism following a recent traumatic event incurred higher hospitalization expenses compared to their initial trauma event (P < 0.01).

In terms of predictors of reinjury, the male gender emerged as a significant risk factor for all forms of recidivism, consistent with existing literature on the subject [26, 31]. Prior research indicated that individuals aged 15–24 were nearly four times more likely to experience recidivism [31]. In contrast, our study found that individuals aged 45–54 were three times more likely to experience recidivism. This variance may be attributed to the focus of our study on non-violent traumatic exposures, as opposed to previous studies which concentrated on violent traumatic events. It is widely acknowledged that teenagers are the primary contributors to violent trauma. Our research indicates that individuals with sleep deprivation are twice times more likely to be repeat offenders. In the realm of occupational sectors, it has been observed that construction workers and delivery staff are associated with elevated levels of risk. Specifically, individuals employed in construction occupations exhibit a 2.72-fold increased likelihood of recurrent offenses, whereas those in delivery roles demonstrate a 3.65-fold higher risk. Moreover, individuals who sustain injuries from electric bicycles are 2.85 times more prone to subsequent injuries. Given the burgeoning electronic commerce in China, a substantial workforce will be involved in express delivery industry (including food delivery and express delivery service), predominantly utilizing electric bicycles for transportation, thereby warranting attention and concern. Early trauma prevention for such high-risk individuals is very necessary.

A nomogram has been created and tested to predict recidivism in polytrauma patients on an individualized basis. This nomogram includes six variables: Male gender, Age 45–54, Sleep deprivation, Construction workers, Delivery staff, and Electric bicycles collision. The risk score’s predictive accuracy was considered satisfactory, achieving an AUC-based accuracy of 0.899. Clinicians can utilize this nomogram to assess the risk of recidivism in individual hospitalized patients. The six factors needed to assess the likelihood of reoffending can usually be obtained easily when a patient is admitted to the hospital, and the nomogram is simple to use. The calibration curves of the recidivist-predicting nomogram indicate its utility. Early identification of potential recidivist polytrauma patients through thorough history assessments, including factors such as occupation, could enhance our understanding and facilitate further research. This knowledge could be utilized to develop targeted prevention strategies for road safety, particularly for vulnerable populations. These findings have the potential to significantly influence trauma prevention efforts by identifying individuals at high risk for recurrent trauma incidents. By targeting education and counseling resources towards these individuals during their initial hospitalization, there is a potential to decrease the likelihood of future traumatic events [35].

Limitations and strengths

This study leverages a comprehensive provincial trauma registry, which enhances both the validity and generalizability of its findings. The substantial sample size facilitates rigorous statistical analysis, allowing for the identification of significant risk factors associated with polytrauma recidivism. Furthermore, the multicenter design ensures a diverse patient population, reinforcing the relevance and applicability of the result. However, there are notable limitations to consider. Despite the thoroughness of the trauma registry, certain variables were found to have missing values. Additionally, it is possible that returning patients may have had their information mistakenly entered with a different ID, resulting in mislabeling as non-repeat offenders. Moreover, the occurrence of patient deaths either at the scene or shortly after the initial injury, as well as those resulting from comorbidities, may have influenced the accuracy and completeness of the available information and subsequent results. Furthermore, it is possible that certain repeat patients were not detected due to the limited duration of the follow-up period.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this retrospective cohort study underscores that polytrauma is not merely a series of isolated events but a recurrent public health challenge, with over 40% of affected individuals experiencing recidivism. The findings identify critical risk factors for trauma recidivism, including sex, age, and occupational hazards, which correlate with poorer clinical outcomes and escalating healthcare costs. The median interval to reinjury of 27 months among recidivist patients highlights the urgent need for targeted, evidence-based interventions. To mitigate the impact of polytrauma recidivism, it is essential for public health initiatives to prioritize the development and implementation of preventive strategies tailored to high-risk populations. These strategies should focus on addressing the identified risk factors—such as male sex, age group 45–54 years, and high-risk occupations—and enhancing resilience in vulnerable groups. By focusing on these areas, we can improve patient outcomes, reduce preventable injuries, and ultimately alleviate the associated healthcare burden.
