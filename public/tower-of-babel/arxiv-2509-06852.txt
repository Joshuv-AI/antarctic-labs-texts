Using Erdős’s methods to study Yorke’s problems
Eran Igra and Valerii Sopin

Abstract
. In this paper, we study the possible bifurcations of periodic orbits by reducing them to graphs. The aforementioned allows to study the genericity of routes to chaos, as well as to analyze their possible complexity. In particular, our results show that there is no upper bound on the possible complexity for dimension d > 3 d>3 . Moreover, it suggests general fermionic description via the virial expansion and universal description via the Rado graph.



1.
Introduction

Let
S
be a closed, smooth, orientable manifold of dimension
d>1
, and let
f_{t}:S\to S
,
t\in[0,1]
, be an orientation-preserving smooth isotopy. Moreover, assume the dynamics of
f_{0}
is ”simple”, while the dynamics of
f_{1}
is ”complex” - for example, a.e. initial condition on
S
is attracted to a stable periodic orbit under
f_{0}
, while
f_{1}
includes infinitely many sinks, as per the Newhouse Phenomenon
[
15
]
. Can we characterize the bifurcations that lead from the orderly states of
f_{0}
to the complex behavior of
f_{1}
? Or, more precisely, can we tell which bifurcations we should expect for a generic choice of such an isotopy?

One famous approach to studying this problem is that of the Orbit Index, originally introduced in
[
20
]
, inspired by ideas from
[
10
]
,
[
11
]
,
[
12
]
, and
[
13
]
(among others). This notion, as developed further in
[
21
]
,
[
22
]
, and
[
24
]
, was well suited to the study of such questions. One important application is
[
26
]
, where it was proven that when
f_{1}
is a Smale Horseshoe map, one should generically expect the bifurcations from
f_{0}
to
f_{1}
to include infinitely many period-doubling cascades. Other interesting examples are given in works
[
27
]
,
[
30
]
and
[
32
]
, where the Orbit Index was generalized from periodic orbits to homoclinic trajectories and used to study the bifurcations of flows.

).

Based on the Orbit Index Theory (and in particular by the results of
[
26
]
), in this paper we tackle these problems using Graph Theory. In detail, given any dimension
d>1
and
k>0
we construct
G^{A}_{k,d}
, a collection of colored acyclic graphs - i.e., colored trees - which encode the transition from order to chaos (where the number
k
describes the maximal number of distinct periodic orbits every periodic orbits is allowed to split into along the isotopy). Following that, we consider the relative dimension of
G^{A}_{k,d}
within
G^{A}_{k+1,d}
, which we denote by
\frac{|G^{A}_{k+1,d}|}{|G^{A}_{k,d}|}
and treat as direct limit. As we will prove, for all
d>3,k>1
we have
\frac{|G^{A}_{k+1,d}|}{|G^{A}_{k,d}|}=\frac{|G^{A}_{k+1,d}|}{|G^{A}_{2,d}|}=\frac{|G^{A}_{k,d}|}{|G^{A}_{1,d}|}=\infty
, see Theorem
3.3
for the precise details. The meaning of this result is that for all closed, smooth manifolds
S
of dimension
4
and higher and all
k>0
, there are much more routes to chaos involving ”degenerate” bifurcations where periodic orbits split into arbitrarily many distinct orbits than there are routes to chaos involving only saddle-node and period-doubling bifurcations. In other words, in higher dimensions the period-doubling route to chaos is just one out of many, many others.

As will be made clear, our results apply only to four- and higher-dimensional systems. In detail, we show that in dimensions
2
and
3
the arguments used to prove Theorem
3.3
no longer apply. Nevertheless, as we will prove, the relative dimension in these cases strongly depends on how much the two- and three-dimensional bifurcations ”fill up”
G^{A}_{k,4}
,
k>0
(see Corollary
3.4
for the precise formulation). Intuitively, these results should be understood as a quantification of how much three-dimensional bifurcations are an intermediate stage between two- and four-dimensional bifurcation scenarios.

At this point we remark our results correlate well with observations made in several numerical and theoretical studies. For example, in
[
36
]
it was numerically observed how many routes to chaos need not be the classical period-doubling one. Similarly, in
[
57
]
it was observed how the introduction to mixed convection in a flow could alter the type of route to chaos. In a more theoretical direction, in
[
33
]
it was concluded that there exist routes to chaos whose dynamics can be much more complex than the regular period-doubling route. Finally, as noted in
[
43
]
, as far as one can talk of a ”likely” route to chaos, in higher-dimensional systems these routes to chaos appear to be based on a Neimark-Sacker bifurcations, as opposed to the usual period-doubling one.

This paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
we remind several basic facts about the Orbit Index, use it to translate families of isotopies into colored graphs (see Propositions
2.3
-
2.5
), and rigorously define bifurcation diagrams (see Definition
2.1
). In Subsection
3.1
we study the relative size of
G^{A}_{k,d}
in
G^{A}_{r,d}
,
k<r
, proving Theorem
3.3
, and discussing what happens in dimensions
2
and
3
. Following that, in Subsection
3.2
we consider an intermediate scenario between general graphs and trees. Finally, we discuss the wider context of our results, both in Topological Dynamics and possibly also in Statistical Mechanics.

Before we begin, we would like to state that even though it is not clear from the text, our ideas were very much inspired by the results of
[
35
]
. To elaborate, recall the problem studied in
[
35
]
was constructing a measure that quantifies the size of generic families of diffeomorphisms on manifolds. As proven in
[
35
]
, even though there is no complete answer to this question, one can, in general, check when a certain collection of such diffeomorphisms is negligible. These ideas inspired to study the analogous question for generic bifurcations, which eventually led to Theorem
3.3
. A final source of inspiration comes from the ways graphs are used in tropical geometry
[
51
]
, which gave us the motivation to apply these methods to bifurcation diagrams.

2.
Bifurcation Laws for Diffeomorphisms

From now on,
S

. Now, consider an orientation preserving diffeomorphism
f:S\to S
. Let

\phi(x)=\begin{cases}(-1)^{\sigma^{+}},&\text{if $\sigma^{-}$ is even}\\

(4)

•

•

As we vary

to

,

(or alternatively,

),

(5)

Finally,

We will refer to these bifurcations as the

throughout this paper. The connection between the types of periodic orbits, the admissible bifurcations, and the Orbit Index is given by the following result, proven in

:

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
f_{t}:S\to S
be a
C^{3}
-isotopy,
t\in(0,1)
, let
Per=\{(y,t)\in S\times(0,1)|\exists n>0,f^{n}_{t}(y)=y\}
, and let
Bif\subseteq Per
denote the collection of
n
-junction and type
m\geq 1
orbits inside

. Then, the Orbit Index (when defined) is a bifurcation invariant - that is, we have the following:

•

The Orbit Index is constant on every component of
Per\setminus Bif
.

•

Assume
(x_{0},t_{0})\in Bif
is a bifurcation periodic point where
k>1
components
C_{1},...,C_{k}
of
Per\setminus Bif
meet. Let
C_{l}
denote the components meeting
(x_{0},t_{0})
from
(t_{0}-\epsilon,t_{0})
, and let
C_{r}
denote the components meeting it from
(t_{0},t_{0}+\epsilon)
. Then, for all sufficiently small
\epsilon
and every

,

, we have the equality:

\sum_{(x,t)\in C_{l}\cap(S\times\{t\})}\phi(x)=\sum_{(x,t)\in C_{r}\cap(S\times\{t\})}\phi(x)

In other words, the Orbit Index is a bifurcation invariant (see the illustration in Figure

Having recalled the basic definitions of the Orbit Index and its bifurcation invariance properties, we are ready to begin. As stated in the introduction we are interested in measuring the size of different bifurcation scenarios within the space of all ”admissible” bifurcations - which for us will be the type
1,2,m
and
k
-junctions orbits (where
m,n\geq 3
). To this end, given a periodic point
x
of minimal period
n
for a diffeomorphism
f:S\to S
, we say
x
is

if there exists a neighborhood
N
of
x
s.t.
\{y\in N|f^{n}(y)=y\}=\{x\}
. We now use this idea to introduce the following families of isotopies:

•

The set
F_{1}(S)
will be defined as the collection of
C^{3}
-isotopies in
S\times[0,1]
s.t. every periodic orbit is isolated, and its type is
0,1,
or
2
. Moreover, the Orbit Index is defined as either
0,1,-1
for all periodic orbits which are not bifurcation orbits.

•

The set
F_{2}(S)
will be defined as the collection of
C^{3}
-isotopies in
S\times(0,1)
whose periodic orbits are all isolated and of type
0,1,2
and
m\geq 3
. Again, the Orbit Index is defined as either

for all periodic orbits which are not bifurcation orbits.

•

The set
F_{n}(S)
,
n\geq 3
will be defined as the collection of
C^{3}
-isotopies in
S\times(0,1)
whose periodic orbits are all isolated and are either of types
3\leq m
, or
k
-junction bifurcation orbits for
3<k\leq n+1
.

By definition, for all
1\leq m<k
we have
F_{m}(S)\subseteq F_{k}(S)
. As
F_{1}(S)
is

–generic (see the appendices in both
[
20
]
and
[

]
), it follows that each

is generic in the collection of
C^{3}
-isotopies
F:S\times(0,1)\to S
. Before moving on, we remark that in general, it is not known whether
F_{k}(S),k>2
is non-empty for some arbitrary

. Bbt by the results of
[

]
we know there are good indications for

As stated in the introduction, we will prove the results of this paper by reducing the families
F_{k}(S)
,
k>0
, to a collection of colored graphs. In detail, given an isotopy
f_{t}:S\to S
,

in

, we will define a graph which represents the bifurcation diagram of periodic orbits along this isotopy, and color the said graph using Theorem
2.1
. To this end, we first study the dependence of the admissible bifurcations on
d
, the dimension of
S
- in detail, we will consider how
d
and the Orbit Index constrain the bifurcations a periodic orbit can undergo. To begin, we first introduce the following notation: given a periodic orbit of type
0
with Orbit Index
i
that splits along a
C^{3}
– isotopy
f_{t}:S\to S
,
t\in(0,1)
into
k\in\mathbb{N}
distinct orbits of indices
i_{1},...,i_{k}
, we denote this transition by
i\to(i_{1},...,i_{k})
. By Theorem
2.1
, we know
i=\sum_{j=0}^{k}i_{j}
. With this notation in mind, we prove:

Lemma 2.2
.

Assume
x\in S
is periodic for
f_{t_{0}}
,
t_{0}\in(0,1)
, and that
\phi(x)=0
. Then, for all dimensions
d
,
x
cannot split in a period-doubling bifurcation of the type
0\to(0,0)
.

Proof.

Assume
x
has a minimal period
n
, and recall that
\phi(x)=0
when
\sigma^{-}
, the number of multipliers of the differential
D_{f_{t_{0}}}(x)
in
(-\infty,-1)
, is odd. Now, assume
x
undergoes a period-doubling bifurcation at some
t_{1}>t_{0}
, where
x
splits to
x^{\prime}
and
x^{\prime\prime}
s.t. the period is doubled at, say,
x^{\prime\prime}
. We will prove the lemma by counting the eigenvalues of
D_{f^{2n}_{t}}(x^{\prime\prime})
in
(-\infty,-1)
and showing it is even - this will imply the Orbit Index of
x^{\prime\prime}
is non-zero, thus by Theorem
2.1
the Orbit Index of
x
and
x^{\prime}
cannot both be
0
.

Without loss of generality, assume
x
goes through the period-doubling bifurcation at some

. As
-1
is the only root of unity of
D_{f^{n}_{t_{1}}}(x)
and since every negative eigenvalue for
D_{f^{n}_{t_{1}}}(x)
becomes positive for
D_{f^{2n}_{t_{1}}}(x)
, the differential
D_{f^{2n}_{t}}(x^{\prime\prime})
,

cannot inherit any negative eigenvalues from
D_{f^{n}_{t_{1}}}(x)
(at least when

is sufficiently close to

). Moreover, since complex conjugate eigenvalues for
D_{f^{n}_{t_{1}}}(x)
only come in pairs, it follows

has at most an even number of negative eigenvalues - consequentially, the same is true for
D_{f^{2n}_{t}}(x^{\prime\prime})
. This proves

cannot have Orbit Index

and the assertion follows.

–isotopy

,

•

Consider the differential of

Assume

,

be a

–isotopy. Then, the possible saddle-node, period-doubling, and type

(1)

A saddle-node bifurcation where two periodic orbits of Orbit Index
0
collide with one another and disappear.

(2)

A period-doubling bifurcation of either one of the forms -
0\to(-1,1)
,
-1\to(-1,0)
.

(3)

Type
m\geq 3
bifurcations of the form
-1\to(-1,1,-1)
or
1\to(1,-1,1)
.

In other words, the possible bifurcation laws when
S
is three-dimensional are precisely the admissible rules from the two-dimensional case in Proposition
2.3
, plus the additional
5
forms of bifurcations given above. Consequentially, the possible
n>3
-junctions are as in Figure
7
- or, in other words, every
n
-junction takes one of the following forms:

•

A cycle of Orbit Index
\pm 1
which splits into
n-1
cycles of Orbit Index
0
and one cycle of Orbit Index
\pm 1
(respectively).

•

A cycle of Orbit Index
\pm 1
which splits either into

cycles of Orbit Index
-1
and

, or the opposite (where

).

Proof.

Let
x
be a periodic orbit - for simplicity, we assume it is a fixed point. We study the admissible bifurcations it can undergo depending on whether
\phi(x)=0,1,-1
. In each case, we denote the eigenvalues of the differential by
(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\lambda_{3})
, and recall that as the isotopy
f_{t}:S\to S
,
t\in(0,1)
is orientation–preserving, we always have
\lambda_{1}\cdot\lambda_{2}\cdot\lambda_{3}>0
. Therefore, if there are negative multipliers, there are precisely two - and if there are complex-conjugate multipliers, again, there are exactly two. In particular, whatever the case, there always exists at least one positive eigenvalue.

We prove the statement on a case-by-case basis. We denote the multipliers by their values. Therefore, for brevity, we now introduce the following notations:

•

\lambda=\alpha
if
\lambda\in(0,1)
.

•

\lambda=\beta
if
\lambda>1
,

•

\lambda=\gamma
if
\lambda\in(-1,0)
.

•

\lambda=\delta
if
\lambda<-1
.

Remark 2.6

At this point we remark that the proofs of Propositions

We color the edges of

For the remainder of this paper we will study the properties of bifurcation diagrams for families of isotopies in

, where

This Corollary motivates us to define the following:

.

. Furthermore, due to the known involvement of period-doubling cascades in the formation of Horseshoes, many colored graphs in

[

6

2.4

In other words,

3.1.
Trees

From now on, a
tree
will denote a graph in which every pair of distinct vertices is connected by exactly one path - or equivalently, a connected acyclic graph. A
forest
is a graph s.t. each component of it is a tree. The
degree of a tree
refers to the highest number of children any node in the tree has. For example - a binary tree is degree
2
, a ternary tree is degree
3
, and an
m
-ary tree,
m>0
is one where each node has no more than
m
children. In this subsection we will use trees to answer the following question - can we find an upper bound on the number of possible routes to chaos?

To motivate, let
S
be a closed,
d
-dimensional smooth manifold where
d>1
. We first recall that by
[
26
]
, given a
d
-dimensional cube
ABCD
and a
C^{3}
-isotopy
f_{t}:ABCD\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
t\in[0,1]
s.t.
f_{0}(ABCD)\cap ABCD=\emptyset
and
f_{1}(ABCD)
is a Horseshoe map, then all the periodic orbits in
ABCD
arise through period-doubling cascades as in Figure
1
. As can easily be seen in this setting, every cascade corresponds to a colored tree while the bifurcation diagram of the isotopy would correspond to a colored forest. This shows we can think of colored trees with infinitely many vertices and colored forests with infinitely many components in
G_{k,d}
as routes to chaos. Using this formalism, in this subsection we prove there is no upper bound to the possible complexities of a given route to chaos.

Before we proceed (and to better motivate the proof below), we remark our approach which concentrates on trees and forests as opposed to more general graphs is also motivated by Graph Theory. To see why, recall a spanning tree of a connected graph is a subgraph which includes all the original graph’s vertices, forms a tree (i.e., it has no cycles), and contains only edges from the original graph. Spanning trees are graph invariants, because properties like the number of vertices and edges remain the same for all spanning trees of a given graph (see Appendix
7
for more details). Moreover, if the collection of spanning trees includes all trees even if they are isomorphic as graphs but having different labels, then a graph isomorphism will induce an isomorphism on spanning trees and vice-versa. Hence, a graph may have exponentially many spanning trees. This shows that even in a graph-related context, trees are the most important object.

\end{overpic}

Figure 16.
On the left - a collection of (colored) star graphs. In the middle - a graph constructed by gluing vertices with the same color. On the right - the corresponding bifurcation diagram, which is admissible when

To make our statements precise, given
k>0
, let
G^{A}_{k,d}
denote the collection of all possible connected acyclic diagrams from
G_{k,d}
- i.e., the collection of all trees in
G_{k,d}
. To continue, for any natural
n
denote by
P^{n}G^{A}_{k,d}
the set consisting of all possible connected acyclic diagrams of degree
k+1
on
n
vertices in the star representation of
G^{A}_{k,d}
. Finally, given
r>k>0
, by the
relative dimension of
G^{A}_{k,d}
in
G^{A}_{r,d}
, we will always mean the expression
\frac{|G^{A}_{r,d}|}{|G^{A}_{k,d}|}=\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{|P^{n}G^{A}_{k+1,d}|}{|P^{n}G^{A}_{k,d}|}
. In Theorem
3.3
we will prove that for all
r
and
k
and every dimension
d\geq 4
, we always have
\frac{|G^{A}_{r,d}|}{|G^{A}_{k,d}|}=\infty
. To this end, we first prove the following Lemma:

Lemma 3.2
.

Assume
d\geq 4
and let
k>0
be some natural number. Then, if we remove the colors from the vertices of the trees in
P^{n}G^{A}_{k,d}
, the resulting collection of graphs is all the trees of degree
k+1
on
n
vertices.

Proof.

By Proposition
2.5
, we know it would suffice to prove the assertion for
d=4
. Now, recall that the trees in
P^{n}G^{A}_{k,4}
are created by gluing its components together only at vertices with the same colors. It follows from Proposition
2.5
that given any
1\leq j\leq k+1
, we can split any orbit, regardless of its Orbit Index, into
j
distinct periodic orbits. This implies
P^{n}G^{A}_{k,4}
includes any tree of degree
k+1
on
n
vertices, and Lemma
3.2
follows.
∎

Having proven Lemma
3.2
, we now use it to prove Theorem
3.3
where we show that whenever
d\geq 4
, the relative dimension of
G^{A}_{k,d}
in
G^{A}_{k+1,d}
is infinite. In light of the discussion above, the dynamical meaning of this statement is that the collection of routes to chaos where orbits are allowed to split to up to
k+2
orbits at every bifurcation is infinitely larger than the collection of routes to chaos where periodic orbits are allowed to split to up to
k+1
periodic orbits at every bifurcation. As a consequence, it follows that in dimension
4
and up there is no upper bound for the possible complexity of routes to chaos.

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
S
be a closed, smooth manifold of dimension
d\geq 4
. Then, for any natural
k>1
, we have
\frac{|G^{A}_{k+1,d}|}{|G^{A}_{k,d}|}=\frac{|G^{A}_{k+1,d}|}{|G^{A}_{2,d}|}=\frac{|G^{A}_{k,d}|}{|G^{A}_{1,d}|}=\infty
.

Proof.

To begin with, we first recall the formula for the number of
k
-ary (uncolored) trees with
n
vertices
[
39
]
:

\frac{1}{(k-1)n+1}\binom{kn}{n}=\frac{1}{(k-1)n+1}\frac{(kn)!}{((k-1)n)!n!}.

Now, choose
k>1
. By Lemma
3.2
we know
P^{n}G^{A}_{k-1,d}
, after forgetting colors, is the set of all
k
–ary trees of degree
k
on
n
vertices - therefore, since every vertex is colored by one of three colors we conclude
|P^{n}G^{A}_{k-1,d}|\leq 3^{n}N(k)
, where
N(k)
is the number of
k
–ary trees with
n
vertices. This yields the following inequality:

\frac{|P^{n}G^{A}_{k,d}|}{|P^{n}G^{A}_{k-1,d}|}\geq\frac{N(k+1)}{3^{n}N(k)}.

By using the Stirling approximation (i.e.
n!\sim n^{n+1/2}e^{-n}
;
(1\pm 1/a)^{b}\sim e^{\pm b/a}
), we now get:

\frac{N(k+1)}{3^{n}N(k)}\approx\frac{(k+1)^{(k+1)n}(k-1)^{(k-1)n}}{3^{n}k^{2kn}}=\frac{1}{3^{n}}(1-\frac{1}{k^{2}})^{(k-1)n}(\frac{k+1}{k})^{2n}.

We now note that by
k>1
we have
(1-\frac{1}{k^{2}})^{(k-1)n}>\frac{1}{2^{(k-1)n}}
, therefore we conclude:

\frac{N(k+1)}{3^{n}N(k)}\approx\frac{1}{3^{n}}(1-\frac{1}{k^{2}})^{(k-1)n}(\frac{k+1}{k})^{2n}\geq\frac{1}{6^{(k-1)n}}(\frac{k+1}{k})^{2n}.

Write
c=6^{k-1}
and note
c^{n}=(\frac{k+1}{k})^{n\log_{c}(\frac{k+1}{k})}
. Consequently,
\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}(\frac{k+1}{k})^{2n}c^{-n}=\infty
, which implies

\frac{|G^{A}_{k,d}|}{|G^{A}_{k-1,d}|}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{|P^{n}G^{A}_{k,d}|}{|P^{n}G^{A}_{k-1,d}|}=\infty.

∎

Having studied the case where
d\geq 4
, we are now led to ask the following question - do these results also hold for lower-dimensional systems? In other words, assuming
d=2,3
, do we still have
\frac{|G^{A}_{k+1},d|}{|G^{A}_{k,d}|}=\infty
? Unfortunately, when
d<4
there is no clear analogue of Lemma
3.2
, so one cannot perform approximations as above. To better illustrate this point, we note it is easy to see, based on Proposition
2.3
and Proposition
2.4
that the following occurs:

•

For all
k>0
,
\cup_{n>0}P^{n}G^{A}_{k,2}
does not include all binary and ternary trees (after forgetting the graph coloring).

•

For all
k>0
,
\cup_{n>0}P^{n}G^{A}_{k,3}
includes all binary trees, but not all
4
-ary trees (after forgetting the graph coloring).

That being said, when
d=2,3
the relative dimensions are dominated, in a sense, by higher-dimensional bifurcation diagrams. To make this statement precise, we make the following computation:

\frac{|P^{n}G^{A}_{k+1,d}|}{|P^{n}G^{A}_{k,d}|}=\frac{|P^{n}G^{A}_{k+1,d}|}{|P^{n}G^{A}_{k,d}|}\frac{|P^{n}G^{A}_{k+1,d+1}|}{|P^{n}G^{A}_{k+1,d+1}|}=\frac{|P^{n}G^{A}_{k+1,d}|}{|P^{n}G^{A}_{k+1,d+1}|}\frac{|P^{n}G^{A}_{k+1,d+1}|}{|P^{n}G^{A}_{k,d}|}.

By Proposition
2.3
and Proposition
2.4
we have the following two inequalities:

\frac{|P^{n}G^{A}_{k+1,d+1}|}{|P^{n}G^{A}_{k,d}|}\geq\frac{|P^{n}G^{A}_{k+1,d+1}|}{|P^{n}G^{A}_{k,d+1}|}\geq 1\text{ and }0<\frac{|P^{n}G^{A}_{k+1,d}|}{|P^{n}G^{A}_{k+1,d+1}|}\leq 1.

When
d=3
, we already know
\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\frac{|P^{n}G^{A}_{k+1,d+1}|}{|P^{n}G^{A}_{k,d+1}|}=\infty
, which shows that the quantity
\frac{|G^{A}_{k+1,d}|}{|G^{A}_{k,d}|}=\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\frac{|P^{n}G^{A}_{k+1,d}|}{|P^{n}G^{A}_{k,d}|}
depends on
\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\frac{|P^{n}G^{A}_{k+1,d}|}{|P^{n}G^{A}_{k+1,d+1}|}
. From now on, for
d=2,3
and
k>0
we denote
\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\frac{|P^{n}G^{A}_{k+1,d}|}{|P^{n}G^{A}_{k+1,d+1}|}=\frac{|G^{A}_{k,d}|}{|G^{A}_{k,d+1}|}
, and refer to this as the
relative share of
G^{A}_{k,d}
inside
G^{A}_{k,d+1}
- by definition,
\frac{|G^{A}_{k,d}|}{|G^{A}_{k,d+1}|}\leq 1
. Much like how the relative dimension measures the growth rate of routes to chaos, the relative share measures how much of the routes to chaos in dimension
d+1
are essentially
d
-dimensional. Now, recalling
\frac{|G^{A}_{k+1,4}|}{|G^{A}_{k,4}|}=\infty
for all
k>0
, see Theorem
3.3
, these calculations imply the following:

Corollary 3.4
.

Assume
d=2,3
. Given any natural number
k>0
, if the relative dimension
\frac{|G^{A}_{k+1,d}|}{|G^{A}_{k,d}|}
is finite, then the relative share
\frac{|G^{A}_{k+1,d}|}{|G^{A}_{k+1,4}|}
is
0
.

In other words, the growth rates of routes to chaos in dimension
3
w.r.t. dimension
4
depends on how many bifurcation scenarios in dimension
4
are essentially three-dimensional, i.e., can be realized along a three-dimensional isotopy. At this point we remark that something similar happens in dimension
2
with respect to dimension
1
- but to introduce it, we first have to extend our setting. To do so, recall the Orbit Index can be extended to periodic orbits in dimension
1
(see, for example,
[
48
]
). However, as one-dimensional isotopies
f_{t}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
where
t\in[0,1]
cannot have routes to chaos (or non-trivial dynamics, in general), in order to get meaningful results one has to replace these isotopies with
C^{3}
-homotopies
h_{t}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
,
t\in[0,1]
.

\end{overpic}

On the left - admissible bifurcations in

. On the right - their star representations.

In this new setting, one can define the Orbit Index of a periodic point
y
in the exact same way as in higher dimensions. However, it is also easy to see that in this setting the only admissible bifurcations would be saddle node, period-doubling, and
n
-junctions modeled on them (see the illustration in Figure
17
). This implies that given any
k>0
, by analogously defining
G^{A}_{k,1}
we always have
G^{A}_{k,1}\subseteq G^{A}_{k,2}
. Or, in other words, every one-dimensional bifurcation diagram is also an admissible two-dimensional diagram. In particular, as there are no type
m
bifurcations in
G^{A}_{1,2}
, we have
G^{A}_{1,1}=G^{A}_{1,2}
.

It is well known that the dynamics of two-dimensional maps are essentially those of ”stretched out” one-dimensional maps (see, for example,
[
37
]
for a survey of these ideas). Moreover, it is also known that four-dimensional flows can have three-dimensional sub-systems - which in turn can have two-dimensional sub-systems with chaotic behavior (with the restricted three body problem being one such famous example -
[
29
]
). We believe the relative dimension and relative share possibly explain why we encounter such phenomena in two- and three-dimensional systems. Motivated by these facts, we ask the following:

Question 3.5
.

For all
k>1
, is the relative share
\frac{|G^{A}_{k,1}|}{|G^{A}_{k,2}|}
necessarily finite? And, conversely, can the relative shares
\frac{|G^{A}_{k,2}|}{|G^{A}_{k,3}|}
and
\frac{|G^{A}_{k,3}|}{|G^{A}_{k,4}|}
be non-zero?

Before concluding this subsection, we recall it is possible to convert any uncolored
m
-ary tree to an uncolored binary tree - which, intuitively, is like saying the trees in
G^{A}_{k,d}
,
k>1
can be converted to trees in
G^{A}_{1,d}
. In detail, recall that to form a binary tree from an arbitrary
m
-ary tree one needs to do the following:

(1)

The root of the tree is made the root of the binary tree.

(2)

Starting with the root, each node’s leftmost child in the tree is made its left child in the binary tree.

(3)

Any subsequent children of that node in the tree are treated as siblings. The second child becomes the right child of the first child, the third child becomes the right child of the second child, and so on.

This combinatorial argument tells us that even if for all
d>0
we have
\frac{|G^{A}_{k,d}|}{|G^{A}_{1,d}|}=\infty
, we should still expect period-doubling routes to chaos to play a prominent role.

3.2.
Block graphs

Having studied routes to chaos in the form of trees, one is naturally led to ask if we can generalize these ideas to bifurcation diagrams whose structure is more complex. To see why, note that given a
C^{3}
-isotopy
f_{t}:S\to S
,
t\in[0,1]
with a bifurcation diagram
\Gamma
, there is no reason to believe
\Gamma
consists only of trees and forests. Or, in other words, the bifurcations of the periodic orbits along this isotopy can be far more complex than only routes to chaos.

To give a concrete example, let
f:S\to S
be a
C^{3}
-diffeomorphism and let
x\in S
be a hyperbolic fixed point with a homoclinic tangle. As proven in
[
8
]
, this forces the existence of Horseshoe like dynamics around
S
. Now, let
f_{t}:S\to S
be a
C^{3}
-isotopy s.t.
f_{1}=f
, and the Horseshoe dynamics around
x
are non-existent for
f_{0}
- i.e., the tangle is created at some
0<t_{0}<1
and persists as
t\to 1
. By the results of
[
26
]
, we would expect the bifurcation diagram
\Gamma
of this isotopy to include a forest
\Gamma^{\prime}
composed of period-doubling cascades, corresponding to the periodic orbits around
x
. However, as these periodic orbits are restricted to some neighborhood of
x
, analyzing
\Gamma^{\prime}
possibly gives only partial information on
\Gamma
. This implies that there is no good reason to assume that all its components are trees.

So far we have used only the star representation, while in the beginning of this section we also defined the representation using complete graphs. By definition, the last never gives trees and forests, but let us again notice that the 2-star graph is the complete graph on two vertices. In this subsection we will show that two representations are related, see Theorem
3.8
.

\begin{overpic}[images/diamond.png]
\end{overpic}

Figure 18.
The transformation from
K_{4}
to the Diamond.

We first recall several notions. Any connected graph decomposes into a tree of biconnected components (that is, if any one vertex were to be removed, the graph would remain connected). We refer to such components as the
block-cut tree
of the graph. These block-cut trees are attached to each other at shared vertices called
cut vertices
, i.e. a cut vertex is any vertex whose removal increases the number of connected components. Finally, a
block graph
or
clique tree
is a type of connected graph in which every biconnected component is a clique (that is, a complete graph). Block graphs can also be described via a forbidden graph characterization, as the graphs that do not have the following components:

•

The diamond graph, that is, the complete graph
K_{4}
minus one edge (see the illustration in Figure
18
).

•

A cycle of four or more vertices as an induced subgraph, which is formed from a subset of the vertices of the graph and all of the edges from the original graph, connecting pairs of vertices in the said subset.

It is easy to see any tree satisfies the condition above - yet it is also easy to see that it allows for a more general structure. As with trees, the numbers of connected block graphs on
1,2,\dots
nodes are known and are given in
[
41
]
:

Proposition 3.6
.

Let
(n_{2},n_{3},\dots)
be a sequence of non-negative
integers and
n=\sum\limits_{i\geq 2}n_{i}(i-1)+1
. Then the number
Hu(n_{2},n_{3},\dots)
of block graphs on
n
vertices having
n_{i}
blocks of size i for each
i
, is given by

Hu(n_{2},n_{3},\dots)=\frac{(n-1)!}{\prod\limits_{j\geq 1}(j!)^{n_{j+1}}n_{j+1}!}n^{k-1},

where
k=\sum\limits_{j\geq 2}n_{j}
is the total number of blocks.

Remark 3.7
.

Block graphs are exactly the graphs in which for every four vertices
u,v,x,
and
y,
the largest two of the three distances
d(u,v)+d(x,y)
,
d(u,x)+d(v,y)
, and
d(u,y)+d(v,x)
are always equal - i.e., 0-hyperbolic in sense of Gromov (see
[
18
]
and
[
58
]
).

We further recall that the
line graph
of a graph
G
is another graph
L(G)
that represents the adjacencies between edges of
G
. In detail, the line graph
L(G)
is constructed in the following way:

•

For each edge in
G
, assign a vertex in
L(G)
.

•

For every two edges in
G
that have a vertex in common, there exists an edge connecting their corresponding vertices in
L(G)
.

The most important fact for our purposes on line graphs is the following - for connected graphs with more than four vertices, there is a one-to-one correspondence between the isomorphisms of the graphs and the isomorphisms of their line graphs
[
1
]
. Using this fact, we now prove that representations using star graphs and complete graphs are dual in a sense (see Figure
12
, which already exhibits this phenomenon):

Theorem 3.8
.

Let
\Gamma
be a bifurcation diagram, which is a tree. Then the line graph of
\Gamma
is a block graph. Consequently, everything we prove for bifurcation diagrams using the star representation is also true w.r.t. the clique representation.

Proof.

The line graphs of trees are exactly the block graphs in which every cut vertex is incident to at most two blocks, or equivalently the claw-free block graphs (i.e., the graphs without an induced subgraph in the form of a three-leaf tree). For the proof, see
[
14
]
,
[
7
]
and
[
9
]
.
∎

To illustrate another relation between two representations, recall that block graphs are the intersection graphs for the blocks of arbitrary undirected graphs - where by an ”intersection graph” we mean a graph that represents the pattern of intersections of a family of sets (for the details, see
[
5
]
). Let
G
be an arbitrary graph, the block graph of
G
denoted
B(G)
is the intersection graph of the blocks of
G
. In detail,
B(G)
has a vertex for every biconnected component of
G
, and two vertices of
B(G)
are adjacent if the corresponding two blocks meet at an articulation vertex. It is easy to see that
B(G)
is a block graph: it has one biconnected component for each vertex of
G
, and each biconnected component formed in this way must be a clique (note the clique number as well as the maximal clique size is an invariant).

In light of the discussion above (and despite the abstract formulation), Theorem
3.8
means that for practical purposes, it doesn’t matter if one uses the star or clique representation. Finally, we would like to state these ideas could possibly be generalized to other types of bifurcation diagrams possessing a ”mixed” structure - see Appendix
6
, where we consider cactus graphs.

4.
Discussion

Before we conclude this paper, we would like to say a few words on how this work can possibly be continued. Despite Theorem
3.3
, we still believe one can use Graph Theory to describe ”universal” properties of bifurcation diagrams. We describe these ideas below - needless to say, at present we do not know how to prove them. That being said, provided these ”universal” properties are shown to exist, they can probably shed light on the universal mechanisms governing the evolution from order into chaos.

The first conjecture, which is motivated by our results (and by
[
47
]
) is the existence of a ”universal” bifurcation diagram. To motivate this conjecture, let us recall the
Rado graph
(the said graph arises via the Erdős–Rényi model of a random graph on countably many vertices - see
[
4
]
). The Rado graph can also be characterized in a deterministic way by the following properties (see
[
6
]
):

•

Universality
- it has countably many vertices, and any graph with countably many vertices is isomorphic to an induced subgraph of the Rado graph.

•

Homogeneity
- any isomorphism between finite induced subgraphs can be extended to an isomorphism between the Rado graph to itself.

•

Robustness
- removing any finite set of its vertices and edges produces a graph isomorphic to the whole Rado graph

These properties define the Rado graph uniquely up to isomorphism
[
6
]
. The universality of the Rado graph can be extended to edge-colored graphs - in other words, to some universal ”bifurcation diagram”, realizing all possible bifurcation diagrams. Furthermore, the first-order logic sentences (
0-1
laws) that are true for the Rado graph are also true of almost all random finite graphs - while the sentences that are false for the Rado graph are also false for almost all finite graphs (for the precise details, see
[
16
]
).

We conjecture that something similar happens in Dynamical Systems. To elaborate, we recall the
Theorem of Multiverse chaos
, proven in
[
47
]
. That theorem states the existence of a dynamical system
R:X\to X
(where
R
is a renormalization operator - see
[
47
]
) s.t. the following holds:

•

The periodic orbits for
R
are dense in
X
.

•

if
g:S\to S
is a dynamical system on some finite-dimensional set
S
(deterministic or stochastic), then there exist infinitely many invariant sets
X^{\prime}\subseteq X
s.t.
R|_{X^{\prime}}
is conjugate to
g:S\to S

In light of this result, we conjecture a bifurcation analogue of the Theorem of Multiverse chaos - namely, that much like all dynamical systems are governed by a fixed rule (i.e.,
R:X\to X
), so do all bifurcations. Specifically, we conjecture the following:

Conjecture 4.1
.

There exists a homotopy
R_{t}:X\to X
,
t\in[0,1]
s.t. the following is true:

•

R_{1}=R
, while

has ”simple” dynamics (for example, a finite number of periodic orbits). In particular, the bifurcation diagram

•

Let

Provided Conjecture

can be proven, its implications would be that there exists a universal theory of bifurcations for homeomorphisms. More specifically, it would show that in order to study all possible bifurcations, one has to study the graph

(although in many ways, this could prove much more difficult then studying separately the isotopy

,

).

A second conjecture is motivated by statistical physics - and specifically, by

. In brief, fermions are defined by equations similar to differential forms (see
[
55
]
for the complete details). A fermion structure, in general, allows to simplify the counting of states for statistical models
[

]
. For example, Mayer’s theory of ”cluster integrals” for the virial expansion for imperfect gases has such structure (see Section 2 in
[
41
]

[

). In that theory, the calculations use graphs. Therefore, considering the deep connection between Statistical Physics and Dynamical Systems (see, for example,
[
28
]
), we are led to wonder if our results form a part of the interplay between these two theories.

Before we conclude this discussion, we remark that we have not touched upon the question of whether our results can be used to study flows generated by differential equations. As our results are strongly dependent on the notion of the Orbit Index which was originally introduced to study persistent periodicity for flows
[
20
]
, intuitively, one would expect the answer to be true. Moreover, our results easily generalize to

Finally, we would like to state we believe there are additional ways to apply Graph Theory to study genericity in bifurcation diagrams. For example, consider the notion for the limit of a sequence of graphs, also known as

[

. It tells us that graphs themselves can be treated as measurable functions on

This allows us to treat graphs and random graphs as the same kind of object and analyze the limiting behavior of the sequence by considering the limiting behavior of the functions. We think the study of such objects can lead to new results about relative dimensions and generic bifurcation phenomena.

Similarly to what we did in Subsection

cactus
is a connected graph in which every edge belongs to at most one cycle - i.e., the cactus graphs are graphs in which every nontrivial biconnected component is a cycle. The family of such graphs can be characterized by a single forbidden minor (formed by deleting edges, vertices and by contracting edges), the diamond graph, see Figure
18
. To illustrate, given by bifurcation diagram
\Gamma
and a component
\gamma\subseteq\Gamma
, the cactus condition says that every two cycles on
\gamma
are at most tangent, see Figure
20
.

Similarly to block graphs, one can also count the number of cactus graphs. In detail, as proven in
[
41

, the numbers of cactus graphs on
1,2,\dots
nodes are:

Proposition 6.1
.

Let
(n_{2},n_{3},\dots)
be a sequence of non-negative

where

is the number of polygons and a polygon of size 2 is simply an edge.

Remark 6.2
.

A triangular cactus is a special type of cactus graph such that each cycle has length three and each edge belongs to a cycle. Since cacti are planar graphs, the largest triangular cactus can be used as an approximation to the largest planar subgraph: the largest triangular cactus in any graph may be found in polynomial time using an algorithm for the matroid parity problem
[
45

. In this case, the number of triangles in the largest triangular cactus is an invariant. It follows, the number of triangular cactus graphs with

Following
[
34

, we now recall the notion of a
Matroid
. There are many equivalent ways to define a matroid, we will use the one in terms of independent sets. A matroid is defined by a set
V
of elements and a family
\mathcal{E}
of subsets of
V
, called independent subsets, which satisfy the following axioms:

(1)

The empty set is independent.

(2)

Any subset of an independent set is also independent.

(3)

For any
A\subseteq V
, all the independent subsets which are contained in
A
and are maximal w.r.t. inclusion relative to
A
have the same number of elements.

Every finite graph yields a matroid: to see how, take the set of all edges and consider a set of edges independent if and only if it is a forest (that is, if it does not contain a cycle). Thus, the procedure basically works with spanning trees of a graph. For more details about spanning trees, see Appendix
7
.

As matroid theory was originally developed by examining the properties of independence and dimension in vector spaces, one can also find instances of matroids using vectors. To illustrate, if
E
is any finite subset of a vector space

, we can define a matroid

by taking the independent sets of

to be the linearly independent subsets of

- in other words,

is a lattice. This leads to

Matroid representations are analogous to group representations; both types of representations provide abstract algebraic structures (matroids and groups, respectively) with concrete descriptions in terms of linear algebra. It is easy to see that any matrix
A
gives rise to a matroid
M
on its set of columns. The dependent sets of columns in the matroid are those that are linearly dependent as vectors (
A
is said to represent
M
).

One open problem in matroid theory is to characterize all the matroids that can be represented over a given field. Therefore, we now consider which bifurcation diagrams can be represented in this way. To make this notion precise, from now on we will say a (finite) bifurcation diagram is

if its graph representation gives rise to a representable matroid over some field with a five or more elements. Note that a matroid is representable over all fields if and only if it is representable over the fields with

[
34

. We now prove:

Theorem 6.3
.

A finite bifurcation diagram is non-representable if its graph representation has a forbidden minor which is the eight vertices of a cube – i.e., a cube, in which the only 4-circuits are the 4 “sides” and just the one “diagonal” plane.

Proof.

The

[
34

has two elements for each vertex of the diamond graph (Figure

[

7.
Appendix - Spanning trees

In this appendix we review some facts about spanning trees, as defined at the beginning of Subsection
3.1
. Firstly, the number of spanning trees in a graph is an invariant and it is addressed by Kirchhoff’s matrix tree theorem proven in
[
2
]
. This theorem relies on the
Laplacian matrix
of a graph, which is equal to the difference between the graph’s degree matrix (the diagonal matrix of vertex degrees that is, the number of edges attached to each vertex) and its adjacency matrix (a (0,1)-matrix with 1’s at places corresponding to entries where the vertices are adjacent and 0’s otherwise). In detail, there is the following result:

Proposition 7.1
.

For a given connected graph with
n
vertices, let
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\dots,\lambda_{n-1}
be the non-zero eigenvalues of its Laplacian matrix. Then the number of spanning trees is
\frac{1}{n}\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}\dots\lambda_{n-1}
.

Remark 7.2
.

The graph Laplacian matrix can be seen as a matrix form of the negative discrete Laplace operator on a graph approximating the negative continuous Laplacian obtained by the finite difference method
[
46
]
.

The
Tutte polynomial

[
40
]
of a graph is a two variable polynomial with many equivalent definitions, but one of them comes from a sum over the spanning trees of the graph. Namely, its value at the arguments (1,1) is the number of spanning trees or, in a disconnected graph, the number of maximal spanning forests. It is the most general graph invariant that can be defined by a deletion–contraction recurrence (an edge contraction is an operation which removes an edge from a graph while simultaneously merging the two vertices that it previously joined). For example, along the hyperbola
xy=1
, the Tutte polynomial of a planar graph specializes to the Jones polynomial of an associated alternating knot. Note that an alternating knot diagram is in one-to-one correspondence with a planar graph: each crossing is associated with an edge and half of the connected components of the complement of the diagram are associated with vertices in a checker board manner. Moreover, any prime, non-split alternating link is hyperbolic, i.e., the complement has a hyperbolic geometry unless it is a torus link
[
25
]
. At this point we recall the Tutte polynomial is essentially a generating function for the number of edge sets of a given size and for the number of connected components with immediate generalizations to matroids. The Kazhdan-Lusztig polynomial of a matroid is in many ways analogous to the classical Kazhdan-Lusztig polynomial associated with an interval in the Bruhat poset of a Coxeter group, i.e., it is the intersection cohomology Poincaré polynomials of a certain variety
[
52
]
.

In the end, we remark that every finite connected graph has a spanning tree. However, for infinite connected graphs, the existence of spanning trees is equivalent to the axiom of choice. Indeed, in the other direction, given a family of sets it is possible to construct an infinite connected graph such that every spanning tree of the graph corresponds to a choice function of the family of sets. Therefore, if every infinite connected graph has a spanning tree, then the axiom of choice is true.
