Characterizing resources for multiparameter estimation of SU(2) and SU(1,1) unitaries
Shaowei Du, Shuheng Liu, Frank E. S. Steinhoff, Giuseppe Vitagliano

Abstract
We analyze the task of estimating a multi-parameter unitary belonging to the S ​ U ​ ( 2 ) SU(2) or S ​ U ​ ( 1 , 1 ) SU(1,1) groups, in a two-bosonic-mode scenario and investigate the scaling of the precision in terms of the total particle number. For the S ​ U ​ ( 2 ) SU(2) case, the total particle number is conserved by the evolution and we discuss optimal states in fixed- n n subspaces, identifying eigenstates of J z 2 J_{z}^{2} as useful resources, even allowing simultaneous Heisenberg precision scaling for all three parameters. In the S ​ U ​ ( 1 , 1 ) SU(1,1) case instead, the conserved quantity is the particle number difference between the two modes, and we identify useful probe states in the sector with an equal number of particles in the two modes. These states are analogous to the S ​ U ​ ( 2 ) SU(2) case and would also allow simultaneous Heisenberg precision scaling for all three parameters. We then consider the more pragmatic scenario of an estimation via expectation values of time-evolved observables, which we restrict to be the first two moments of the generators. We analyze the maximal precision achievable in this setting and we find that the twin-Fock state emerges in both the S ​ U ​ ( 2 ) SU(2) and the S ​ U ​ ( 1 , 1 ) SU(1,1) cases as the only one potentially allowing Heisenberg scaling for the estimation of two out of the three parameters. As a complement, we also consider other probe states with fluctuating number of particles, with measurements restricted to quadratic expressions in the mode operators. In this scenario, simultaneous Heisenberg scaling in multiple parameters seems mostly forbidden, with the only exception being an input two-mode squeezed state for the estimation of a two-parameter S ​ U ​ ( 2 ) SU(2) . This extends to the multiparameter scenario the well-established intuition that the performance of a S ​ U ​ ( 2 ) SU(2) interferometer can be enhanced by a prior S ​ U ​ ( 1 , 1 ) SU(1,1) operation.

Methods

2.1
Introduction to multiparameter estimation

We consider the task of estimating a vector of phases
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=(\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{K})
encoded with a unitary evolution on a probe state:

\varrho_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}=U({\boldsymbol{\theta}})\varrho_{0}U^{\dagger}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}),

(1)

where
\varrho_{0}
is the initial state and
U({\boldsymbol{\theta}})
is the unitary encoding. We consider a
local
estimation problem, in which the phases are considered in an infinitesimal neighborhood of a given point
\boldsymbol{\theta}
. To estimate such a vector of phases one needs to make measurements, which in quantum mechanics are described by a Positive Operator Valued Measure (POVM), i.e., a set of positive operators
\{M_{q}\}
with
M_{q}\geq 0
and
\sum_{q}M_{q}=\mathbbm{1}
, while
q
labels the outcomes. Here we assume that the vector of parameters can be associated to real numbers that are prior to and independent of the measurements, which is often called the
frequentist
approach in literature, and is suitable for our local estimation task. An alternative, more general approach, which is typically termed Bayesian, is usually employed for a global estimation, when a
prior
probability distribution
p(\boldsymbol{\theta})
is assigned to each point
\boldsymbol{\theta}
and is then updated to a
posterior

p(\boldsymbol{\theta}|\boldsymbol{q})
after some measurements with outcomes
\boldsymbol{q}
are performed
[
81
,
77
,
126
]
.

Thus, collecting statistics with
\nu
independent measurements, one in principle samples a probability distribution

{\rm p}({\bf q}|{\boldsymbol{\theta}})=\prod_{j}{\rm p}(q_{j}|{\boldsymbol{\theta}})=\prod_{j}\tr[M_{q_{j}}\varrho_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}],

(2)

where
{\bf q}=(q_{1},\dots,q_{\nu})
is a set of
\nu
outcomes, and the outcome probability depends on the encoded parameters
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
. Then, one has to find a good vector of estimators
\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\bf q)
, possibly
unbiased
, i.e.
\mathbb{E}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}[\hat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}]={\boldsymbol{\theta}}
, the accuracy of which is most commonly quantified via its covariances

[{\mathcal{C}ov}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}})]_{ij}=\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[\hat{\theta}_{i}\hat{\theta}_{j}]-\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[\hat{\theta}_{i}]\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[\hat{\theta}_{j}],

(3)

where we used the notation
\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}[f(\boldsymbol{q})]=\sum_{\boldsymbol{q}}f(\boldsymbol{q})\,{\rm p}(\boldsymbol{q}|{\boldsymbol{\theta}})
for the expectation value of a function of the outcomes
1
1

1

In case the estimator is biased, the appropriate figure of merit becomes the
mean square error
, defined as
\mathrm{MSE}[\hat{\theta}]=\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[(\hat{\theta}-\theta)^{2}]
.
.

2.2
Quantum Cramér-Rao bound and its saturation

In general, lower bounds can be found as matrix inequalities for the covariance matrix, the most commonly used of which is the celebrated Cramér-Rao bound:

{\mathcal{C}ov}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}})\geq\mathcal{F}^{-1}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})/\nu,

(4)

where the matrix on the right hand side is called
Fisher information matrix

\mathcal{F}_{ij}:=\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[(\partial_{\theta_{i}}\ln{\rm p}({\bf q}|\boldsymbol{\theta}))(\partial_{\theta_{j}}\ln{\rm p}({\bf q}|{\boldsymbol{\theta}}))].

(5)

In turn, the classical Fisher information matrix can be optimized over all POVMs, so to get the additional bound
[
97
,
12
]

{\mathcal{C}ov}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}})\geq\mathcal{F}^{-1}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})/\nu\geq\mathcal{F}^{-1}_{Q}[\varrho_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}]/\nu.

(6)

The second inequality is referred to as the quantum (multiparameter) Cramér-Rao bound (QCRB), which defines the

quantum Fisher information matrix
(QFIM)

[\mathcal{F}_{Q}[\varrho_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}]]_{jk}:=\tr[\varrho_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}(L_{j}L_{k}+L_{k}L_{j})/2],

(7)

where the
L_{i}
are the symmetric logarithmic derivatives (SLDs) relative to each of the phases, i.e., they satisfy

\partial_{j}\varrho_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}=\frac{1}{2}(L_{j}\varrho_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}+\varrho_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}L_{j}),

(8)

where we used the simplified notation
\partial_{j}=\partial_{\theta_{j}}
. Note that a more general version, termed Holevo-Cramér-Rao bound can be also found, which is generally tighter than
Eq.

6

[
137
,
13
,
67
,
5
,
117
]
. When the probe state is pure, i.e.,
\varrho_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}=|\Psi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\rangle\langle\Psi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}|
, the QFIM is proportional to the covariance matrix of the vector
{\bf\tilde{H}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
of generators
[
12
,
91
]

\tilde{H}_{j}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}):=iU^{\dagger}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})\partial_{j}U({\boldsymbol{\theta}})=\int_{0}^{1}e^{is{\bf H}\cdot{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}H_{j}e^{-is{\bf H}\cdot{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}{\rm d}s,

(9)

calculated on the initial state
\ket{\Psi_0}
.
Namely, we have

\mathcal{F}_{Q}[\Psi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}]=4{\Gamma}_{\Psi_{0}}[{\bf\tilde{H}}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})],

(10)

where the (symmetric) covariance matrix of a vector of operators
{\boldsymbol{A}}=(A_{1},\dots,A_{K})
on a quantum state
\varrho
is defined as

[{\Gamma}_{\varrho}[{\boldsymbol{A}}]]_{jk}:=\tfrac{1}{2}\left\langle{A_{j}A_{k}+A_{k}A_{j}}\right\rangle_{\varrho}-\left\langle{A_{j}}\right\rangle_{\varrho}\left\langle{A_{k}}\right\rangle_{\varrho}.

(11)

From the matrix inequality in
Eq.

6
one can derive scalar figures of merit for the performance of the estimator from any positive semidefinite weight matrix
P
via
\tr[P\mathcal{F}_{Q}[\varrho_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}]]
. A common choice is just to take
P=\mathbbm{1}
and thus obtain

\sum_{i}(\Delta\hat{\theta}_{i})^{2}\geq\frac{1}{\nu}\tr[\mathcal{F}_{Q}^{-1}[\varrho_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}]].

(12)

In some cases where the group structure of the unitaries is relevant, e.g., in the
SU(2)
case that we are going to discuss, another approach is
to consider the metric as a group-covariant weight matrix
[
57
]
.

Note also that the matrix inequality in

Eq.

6
is saturated if and only if all possible inequalities for the scalar figures of merit are saturated. Thus, the saturation of one single scalar condition is in general a necessary but not sufficient requisite for the saturation of the matrix inequality.

In the multiparameter case the quantum Cramér-Rao bound in
Eq.

6
is in general harder to saturate than in the single-parameter case, even in the asymptotic limit, due to the fact that the SLDs associated to the different parameters might not commute. In fact, a simple condition for saturation that is in general only sufficient but not necessary is given by
[L_{j},L_{k}]=0
for all
j
and
k
, which implies that all the SLDs share a common eigenbasis and thus the optimal measurement is simply given by a projective measurement onto that basis
[
97
,
111
,
90
]
.
A simple necessary and sufficient condition can be instead given for pure states
\varrho_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}=|\Psi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\rangle\langle\Psi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}|
and reads

\bra{\Psi_0}[\tilde{H}_{j}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}),\tilde{H}_{k}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})]\ket{\Psi_0}=0\qquad\text{for all }j,k.

(13)

Whenever the saturability condition is satisfied, concrete conditions for POVMs to saturate the QFIM bound have been derived in
[
12
]
, while in
[
103
]
it was shown how measurements that saturate the QCRB for a pure probe state can be constructed by means of projectors into the probe state plus a set of projectors into its orthogonal subspace.

2.3
Method of moments estimator and accuracy

When the Cramér-Rao bound is saturated, there are well-known estimators that saturate it in the limit of large
\nu
, one of which is given by the maximum-likelihood estimator
\hat{\theta}=\mathrm{argmax}_{\theta}\,{\rm p}(q|\theta)
, which, however, requires sampling extensively the likelihood function
{\rm p}(q|\theta)
(seen as a function of
\theta
). A common alternative, which only requires determining lower-order moments of
{\rm p}(q|\theta)
, is the so-called
method of moments
(MoM)
[
104
,
50
,
81
]
, which we are going to consider in the following. Let us illustrate it first in the single parameter scenario. One performs projective measurements
\Pi_{q}=|q\rangle\langle q|
in the eigenbasis of an observable
M=\sum_{q}m_{q}\,|q\rangle\langle q|
and uses as an estimator for
\theta
the expectation value

\left\langle{M}\right\rangle_{\varrho_{\theta}}=\sum_{q}m_{q}\tr[\Pi_{q}\rho_{\theta}]=\sum_{q}m_{q}\tr[U^{\dagger}(\theta)\Pi_{q}U(\theta)\rho_{0}].

(14)

More precisely, the estimator in this case will be given by the value
\hat{\theta}_{M}
such that

\mu(\hat{\theta}_{M}):=\bar{M}_{\nu}=\left\langle{M}\right\rangle_{\varrho_{\theta}},

(15)

where
\bar{M}_{\nu}=\tfrac{1}{\nu}\sum_{i=1}^{\nu}m_{q_{i}}
is the measured average, calculated from
\nu\gg 1
measurement outcomes
\{q_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\nu}

[
108
,
104
,
50
]
.
In other words, this estimator is given by

\hat{\theta}_{M}:=\mu^{-1}(\bar{M}_{\nu}),

(16)

where
\mu^{-1}(\cdot)
is the inverse of the function given above. This estimator becomes unbiased in the large
\nu
limit and its accuracy can be calculated from the error-propagation formula, and, to the first order in
1/\nu
, is given by:

(\Delta\hat{\theta}_{M})^{2}=\frac{1}{\nu}\frac{(\Delta M)_{\varrho_{\theta}}^{2}}{|\partial_{\theta}\left\langle{M}\right\rangle_{\varrho_{\theta}}|^{2}}=\frac{1}{\nu}\frac{(\Delta M)_{\varrho_{\theta}}^{2}}{|\left\langle{[M,\tilde{H}]}\right\rangle_{\varrho_{\theta}}|^{2}}.

(17)

This formula (once again, valid in the limit of
\nu\gg 1
) is calculated from a Taylor expansion of the function
\mu^{-1}(x)
in the neighborhood of
\langle M\rangle_{\varrho_{\theta}}
. More details about this method are provided in
appendix

A
.

This method of moments can be generalized to the multiparameter scenario
[
50
]
. In this case, one calculates the expectation value of a set of observables
{\boldsymbol{M}}=(M_{1},\dots,M_{K})
and again estimates
\boldsymbol{\theta}
from the corresponding sample means after
\nu\gg 1
rounds:
\langle{\boldsymbol{M}}\rangle_{\varrho_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}=\bar{\boldsymbol{M}}_{\nu}
. The accuracy can be again calculated via a Taylor series expansion of the multivariate estimator around
\langle{\boldsymbol{M}}\rangle_{\varrho_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}
, but it also becomes important to distinguish whether the observables
\boldsymbol{M}
commute with each other or not. If they all commute, their joint expectation value can be obtained from a single measurement in the common eigenbasis
|q\rangle\langle q|
and the accuracy becomes (at first order in
1/\nu
):

\displaystyle{\mathcal{C}ov}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{M}_{\varrho_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}}^{-1}[{\tilde{\boldsymbol{H}}},{\boldsymbol{M}}]/\nu

(18)

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{\nu}\Omega_{\varrho_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}}^{-1}[{\tilde{\boldsymbol{H}}},{\boldsymbol{M}}]{\Gamma}_{\varrho_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}}[{\boldsymbol{M}}](\Omega_{\varrho_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}}^{-1}[{\tilde{\boldsymbol{H}}},{\boldsymbol{M}}])^{T},

where
{\Gamma}_{\varrho_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}}[{\boldsymbol{M}}]
is the covariance matrix of the set of observables
{\boldsymbol{M}}
and

[\Omega_{\varrho_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}}[{\tilde{\boldsymbol{H}}},{\boldsymbol{M}}]]_{kl}=-i\left\langle{[M_{k},\tilde{H}_{l}]}\right\rangle_{\varrho_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}}

(19)

is the commutation matrix, both of them calculated on the evolved probe
\varrho_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}
. When the observables
\boldsymbol{M}
do not commute, their expectation values have to be obtained either from separated subsets of the samples, or by performing a POVM that approximates a joint measurement of all the
M_{k}
, which, however, has generally increased uncertainty
[
23
]
. See
section

A.2
for further details. In
[
50
]
, it has also been investigated how to look for bounds on the precision reached by the method of moments, when fixing the set of available observables
\boldsymbol{A}
, which includes the generators of the dynamics, as well as additional observables that are considered as measurements, which is what we are also going to do in what follows. For finding this overall bound, one defines the moment matrix of the available observables as

\mathcal{M}_{\varrho}[\boldsymbol{A}]:=\Omega^{T}_{\varrho}(\boldsymbol{A})\;{\Gamma}^{-1}_{\varrho}(\boldsymbol{A})\;\Omega_{\varrho}(\boldsymbol{A}),

(20)

where
\bigl[\Omega_{\varrho}(\boldsymbol{A})\bigr]_{kl}:=-\mathrm{i}\,\langle[A_{k},A_{l}]\rangle_{\varrho}
is the matrix of expectation values of commutators. The following upper-bound then holds:

\mathcal{M}_{\varrho}\bigl[\tilde{\boldsymbol{H}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}),\boldsymbol{M}\bigr]\;\leq\;R(\boldsymbol{\theta})\,\mathcal{M}_{\varrho}[\boldsymbol{A}]\,R(\boldsymbol{\theta})^{T},

(21)

where
R
is the matrix such that
\tilde{\boldsymbol{H}}=R\boldsymbol{A}
. See
section

A.3
for more details.

3
Intermezzo: Estimation of Heisenberg-Weyl unitaries

As a preliminary discussion on the idea we want to employ, let us consider now arguably the simplest scenario of estimation of multiple phases from non-commuting generators, which is the case in which the generators form the Heisenberg-Weyl algebra:

[x,p]=i\mathbbm{1}.

(22)

This essentially means that we consider a single-mode system and want to estimate a two-parameter phase-space displacement, i.e.:

U(\boldsymbol{r})=e^{-i(r_{1}x+r_{2}p)}.

(23)

Let us imagine that we want to estimate
\boldsymbol{r}=(r_{1},r_{2})
by measuring the quadratures
\boldsymbol{X}=(x,p)
and using the method of moments. For these observables, the expectation value of the commutator is actually constant for all states and thus the commutator matrix is given by the canonical symplectic form

\Omega_{\varrho}[\boldsymbol{X}]=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
-1&0\end{pmatrix}:=\Omega,

(24)

independent of
\varrho
.
For a given probe state
\varrho
, we then consider the moment matrix of the quadrature vector
\boldsymbol{X}
which is given by

\mathcal{M}_{\varrho}[{\boldsymbol{X}}]=\Omega^{T}\;\Gamma^{-1}_{\varrho}[\boldsymbol{X}]\;\Omega,

(25)

which for Gaussian states
\varrho_{G}
actually coincides with the QFIM
[
50
]
. In particular, for pure Gaussian probe states
\ket{\Psi_{\vec r}}=U(\boldsymbol{r})\ket{\Psi_{\vec 0}}
we can use
Eq.

10
and also obtain directly

\mathcal{F}_{Q}[\Psi_{\boldsymbol{r}}]=4{\Gamma}_{\Psi_{\boldsymbol{0}}}[\tilde{\boldsymbol{X}}].

(26)

For simplicity, let us consider the case
\boldsymbol{r}\simeq\boldsymbol{0}
, which implies
\tilde{\boldsymbol{X}}=\boldsymbol{X}
.
Note that in this case the saturability condition in
Eq.

13
is not met for any pure state, since the commutator between the generators is
a nonzero constant.

On the other hand, because the commutator between the generators does not vanish, we can take the vector of available observables to coincide with the generators, i.e:

\boldsymbol{H}=\boldsymbol{X}\implies R=\mathbbm{1}.

(27)

Correspondingly, we can see that a vector of optimal measurements is given by

\boldsymbol{M}=\Omega\;{\Gamma}^{-1}_{\varrho}[\boldsymbol{X}]\;\boldsymbol{X},

(28)

which follows from the general argument discussed in
section

A.3
(see
Eq.

136
with
G=R=\mathbbm{1}
).

Now it just remains to analyze the probe state. As a first example, let us consider the vacuum state
\ket{0}
.
For such a state the covariance matrix is just proportional to the identity and we have

\mathcal{M}[{\boldsymbol{X}}]=\Omega^{T}\;{\Gamma}^{-1}_{0}[\boldsymbol{X}]\;\Omega=2\;\mathbbm{1},

(29)

while the optimal observables whose expectation values have to be measured are

\boldsymbol{M}=\Omega\boldsymbol{X}=(p,-x),

(30)

which is essentially the same as the vector
\boldsymbol{X}
itself.

The same reasoning would hold for all coherent states, defined by

\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(x+ip)\ket{\alpha}=\alpha\ket{\alpha},

(31)

where
\alpha
is a complex number.

Let us now instead consider a squeezed state, defined by

\ket{\xi}=e^{\tfrac{1}{2}\xi(a^{2}-(a^{\dagger})^{2})}\ket{0},

(32)

where
\xi
is a real parameter which gives the squeezing factor.

In this case we have
{\Gamma}_{\xi}[\boldsymbol{X}]=\tfrac{1}{2}{\rm diag}(e^{-2\xi},e^{2\xi})
and consequently

\mathcal{M}_{\xi}[{\boldsymbol{X}}]=2{\rm diag}(e^{-2\xi},e^{2\xi}),

(33)

while the optimal observables can be again taken to be those in
Eq.

30
.

The question then arises how to directly estimate from a single measurement run the expectation values of both
x
and
p
quadratures. For that, in this case one can employ a heterodyne measurement, which is essentially a POVM into the (non-orthogonal) coherent states projectors
|\alpha\rangle\langle\alpha|
.
Formally, we have that the resolution of identity is given by

\mathbbm{1}=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{\mathbb{C}}{\rm d}^{2}\alpha\ |\alpha\rangle\langle\alpha|.

(34)

Such a measurement gives as an outcome a phase-space point labelled by
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}
and is such that its real and imaginary parts provide the values of
x
and
p
. Thus, by estimating the expectation value of
\alpha
, we get an estimate of both the expectation values of
\left\langle{x}\right\rangle
and
\left\langle{p}\right\rangle
through its real and imaginary parts respectively. This essentially amounts to evaluate the Husimi function of the quantum state, namely:

\mathcal{Q}_{\varrho}(\alpha)=\frac{1}{\pi}\bra{\alpha}\varrho\ket{\alpha}.

(35)

Note also that for the MoM estimator we do not need the full statistics of
\alpha
, but just its first order moments, i.e., the expectation value
\left\langle{\alpha}\right\rangle
, which may be obtained also from such an imperfect measurement. Still, in order to evaluate the accuracy one needs to calculate the moment matrix, which in this case is essentially given by the covariance matrix
\Gamma_{\varrho}[\boldsymbol{X}]
. This can be also reconstructed from heterodyne measurements (i.e., from a partial reconstruction of the Husimi function) or other imperfect joint measurements of the quadratures, but with additional noise coming from the non-commutativity of the quadratures and the non-existence of a perfect joint measurement.

We remark that in this case the QCRB is not saturated, and this example just provides an illustration of how a multiparameter estimation can be performed using the method of moments, also
for the case of two noncommuting generators.

4.1
Probe states reaching Heisenberg scaling for three parameters

To analyze potentially good candidate (pure) probe states, we first consider sectors with fixed number of particles
n
and take this as the resource with respect to which we investigate the precision scaling. This is a common approach, which relates to the energy or intensity of the probe. Moreover, in this context where the evolution preserves the particle number, components of the states in each fixed-
n
sector will be invariant during the evolution, and the question also arises on whether the measurement can detect superpositions between different sectors or not. Later in
section

6
we will discuss more extensively probe states with a non-fixed particle number. In this scenario, it is well-known that the best single-parameter probe state is the NOON state
[
124
,
104
,
34
]
(see also
appendix

B
), which has a non-singular QFIM and is an eigenstate of
J_{z}^{2}
. On a deeper inspection one can then see that, while it provides Heisenberg scaling for estimating one phase, say
\theta_{z}
, its QCRB for the other two phases scales as the SQL. Another well-known good candidate state is the twin-Fock state, which is also an eigenstate of
J_{z}
and
J^{2}_{z}
at the same time and thus, while it is useful only for the estimation of two phases,
\theta_{x}
and
\theta_{y}
, it allows achieving Heisenberg scaling in both of them. In general, eigenstates of
J_{z}^{2}
with
\langle\boldsymbol{J}\rangle=0
have a diagonal QFI matrix and the QCRB is saturated in their case, since
Eq.

13
is satisfied.

This is also an indication that eigenstates of
J_{z}^{2}
can be considered as good candidate probe states in the multiparameter case. In fact, other eigenstates of
J_{z}^{2}
present sort of intermediate scalings for the three phases and, in particular one can find some that allow Heisenberg scaling for all three directions. Specifically, these have the form

\ket{\Psi_k}:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\ket{n-k,k}+\ket{k,n-k}\right),

(39)

with
k=\left\lfloor\frac{n-\sqrt{\frac{n(n+2)}{3}}}{2}\right\rceil:=k_{\rm opt}
and
\lfloor x\rceil
denoting the closest integer to
x

3
3

3

Note that here, and similarly later in
Eq.

67

1/\sqrt{2}
normalization of the state should be modified in order to obtain the twin Fock state as a special case in the class.
.

The QFIM of states of the form (
39
) is given by

\mathcal{F}_{Q}[\Psi_{k}]={\rm diag}\left(F_{\parallel},F_{\parallel},F_{\perp}\right),

(40)

with
F_{\parallel}=2k(n-k)+n
and

, which reaches Heisenberg scaling for all parameters already for

.

In the case of

the state becomes close to those called two-anti-coherent in literature
[

\hat{\rm n}

\left\langle{J^{2}_{\hat{\rm n}}}\right\rangle=j(j+1)/3
is satisfied for all directions
\hat{\rm n}

Note that anti-coherent states are defined for any order

In

two-anticoherent states were analyzed in detail for the task of estimating three rotation angles from a geometrical point of view and also optimal measurements were studied, following the approach of

that generates an optimal PVM from the probe state and its orthogonal subspace. Furthermore, they also analyzed the case of a POVM of the form analogous to that in

Thus, we can see that the states from the class

SU(2)

Similarly, we would expect that the set of projectors onto coherent spin states that need to be measured to approach the saturation of the QCRB bound would be similar. In the next subsection, we will discuss an approach that is partly analogous, but focused on the method of moment estimator, and we restrict to an estimation that is based on just the reconstruction of the first two moments of the generators. In that case, as we will see, it is not possible to saturate the QCRB for the full three-parameter estimation.

As a further comparison with two-anticoherent states, we mention that the three-parameter
SU(2)
estimation can also be seen as a rotation sensing scenario, where the rotation direction is not known, i.e, a unitary encoding of the form
U_{{}_{\hat{\boldsymbol{\rm n}}}}(\theta)=\exp(-i\theta J_{\hat{\vec{\rm n}}})

is an arbitrary direction. With such a viewpoint, a natural benchmark figure of merit is the average performance over all directions

\overline{\Delta^{2}\hat{\theta}}\geq\frac{1}{4\pi}\int{\rm d}\hat{\boldsymbol{\rm n}}\frac{1}{F_{Q}[\ket{\Psi_{\theta}},J_{\hat{\boldsymbol{\rm n}}}]}.

(41)

In this respect, it is known that two-anticoherent states are optimal
[

Once again, in analogy to this reasoning, considering as probe states those in

\bar{F}_{Q}[\ket{\Psi_k},J_{\hat{\boldsymbol{\rm n}}}]:=\frac{1}{4\pi}\int{\rm d}\boldsymbol{n}\ F_{Q}[\ket{\Psi_k},J_{\hat{\boldsymbol{\rm n}}}]=\frac{1}{3}n(n+2),

(42)

which is exactly the same as the two-anticoherent states

\displaystyle\overline{\Delta^{2}\hat{\theta}}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{4\pi}\int{\rm d}\boldsymbol{n}\ \frac{1}{F_{Q}[\ket{\Psi_k},J_{\hat{\boldsymbol{\rm n}}}]}

4.2
Estimation via multiparameter method of moments

As we have seen, states are known that can be useful to estimate three phases with Heisenberg scaling, and also measurements saturating such bounds can be worked out. However, these schemes can be quite challenging in some setups. Also, from a purely theoretical perspective, it is interesting
to look for alternative protocols and, at the same time, uncover other aspects that might also be related to the group-structure of the unitaries to be estimated.
Here, we have already discussed that states of the form (
39
) can be a viable alternative to those discussed in literature for the potential achievement of Heisenberg scaling, which in some sense is also constructed from the algebraic structure forming the unitary group. In fact, we will observe a somewhat similar structure later for the case of
SU(1,1)
unitaries.

On top of that, we can also try to observe whether a pragmatic estimation scheme, namely the method of moments implemented with reasonable observables, also allows the saturation of Heisenberg scaling, or alternatively what is the best scaling achievable in that case. In particular, we ask the question: What is the best achievable precision (scaling) when only the first two moments of the generators are available? Clearly, more in general this question can be asked for different sets of observables, which may be relevant in different experimental contexts. The case we consider here can be seen as being of relatively general relevance, as it involves the generators of the dynamics themselves. Moreover, the first two moments are what is most typically estimated in actual experiments, especially those where the particle number is conserved, e.g., atomic ensembles
[
104
]
, while larger and larger order moments become quickly very tedious to consider and also more difficult to reconstruct in practical circumstances.

Thus, overall for the estimation of an
SU(2)
unitary we consider the following vector of observables:

\boldsymbol{A}_{\mathfrak{su}(2)}=(\boldsymbol{J},\boldsymbol{Q}),

(44)

where

\boldsymbol{Q}=(J_{x}^{2},J_{y}^{2},J_{z}^{2},\{J_{x},J_{y}\},\{J_{x},J_{z}\},\{J_{y},J_{z}\})

(45)

is the vector of quadratic products of spin operators.

It is also useful to express these observables in a two-index tensor notation from the quadratic products

A_{kl}=J_{k}J_{l},

(46)

such that by taking the symmetric and anti-symmetric part we can write

\displaystyle J_{i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{kl}\epsilon_{ikl}A_{kl},

(47)

\displaystyle Q_{kl}

\displaystyle=A_{kl}+A_{lk},

with
\epsilon_{ikl}
being the usual Levi-Civita symbol and we see that basically
\boldsymbol{A}_{\mathfrak{su}(2)}
is just a vectorized form of the operator matrix
A
.

Furthermore, the expectation value of the symmetric operator matrix
Q
on a quantum state gives the correlation matrix of the spin operators. Thus, estimating the expectation values of the vector of observables
\boldsymbol{A}_{\mathfrak{su}(2)}
essentially amounts to estimating the average vector
\left\langle{\boldsymbol{J}}\right\rangle=(\left\langle{J_{x}}\right\rangle,\left\langle{J_{y}}\right\rangle,\left\langle{J_{z}}\right\rangle)
and the covariance matrix
{\Gamma}_{\Psi_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}}[{\boldsymbol{J}}]
on the probe state
\ket{\Psi_{\Bth}}
. Here, we work in the Heisenberg picture, in which the probe state remains fixed and the elements of our vector of observables
\boldsymbol{A}
are transformed as

A_{i}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=U^{\dagger}(\boldsymbol{\theta})A_{i}U(\boldsymbol{\theta}).

(48)

Group-theoretically speaking, these observables transform under the adjoint representation of
SU(2)
, and in particular the vectors
\boldsymbol{J}
and
\boldsymbol{Q}
are transformed as

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{J}(\boldsymbol{\theta})

\displaystyle=U^{\dagger}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\boldsymbol{J}U(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\mathcal{O}_{\bf 3}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\,\boldsymbol{J},

(49)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{Q}(\boldsymbol{\theta})

\displaystyle=U^{\dagger}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\boldsymbol{Q}(\boldsymbol{\theta})U(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\mathcal{O}_{\bf 6}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\,\boldsymbol{Q},

where
\mathcal{O}_{\bf 3}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
and
\mathcal{O}_{\bf 6}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
are
3\times 3
and
6\times 6
orthogonal matrices respectively which are obtained from the adjoint representation of
SU(2)
(see
appendix

C
for details). The matrix
\mathcal{O}_{\bf 3}
gives basically a three-dimensional rotation of the spin axes, while the six-dimensional matrix is constructed from vectorizing the quadratic relation

Q(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\mathcal{O}_{\bf 3}\,Q\,\mathcal{O}^{T}_{\bf 3},

(50)

where
Q
is the symmetric operator matrix given in
Eq.

47
, which is transformed quadratically with the three-dimensional orthogonal rotation of the spin axes. Further details are provided in
appendix

C
.

Let us now consider the moment matrix
\mathcal{M}_{\Psi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}[\boldsymbol{A}_{\mathfrak{su}(2)}]
coming from
Eq.

18
for the vector of observables as in
Eq.

44
. Here, the probe states are the eigenstates
\ket{\Psi_k}
of
J_{z}^{2}
as in
Eq.

39
for all possible values of
k
. As we mentioned, this class contains states that can be potentially good resources for multiparameter
SU(2)
estimations. Then, we can calculate what the optimal observables
\boldsymbol{M}
are among the vector
\boldsymbol{A}_{\mathfrak{su}(2)}
, which can be done following the methods described in
appendix

A
(cf.
Eq.

136
). In summary, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{\Psi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}[{\boldsymbol{H}},{\boldsymbol{M}}]

\displaystyle\leq R\mathcal{M}_{\Psi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}[{\boldsymbol{A}}]R^{T}

(51)

\displaystyle=R\,\Omega^{T}_{\Psi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}[{\boldsymbol{A}}]\,\Gamma^{-1}_{\Psi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}[{\boldsymbol{A}}]\,\Omega_{\Psi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}[{\boldsymbol{A}}]R^{T},

where
R
is the matrix such that

\boldsymbol{J}=R\boldsymbol{A}.

(52)

This way, we get from
Eq.

18
an upper bound to the precision matrix given by

\displaystyle{\mathcal{C}ov}^{-1}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}})

\displaystyle\leq\nu R\,\Omega^{T}_{\Psi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}[{\boldsymbol{A}}]\,\Gamma^{-1}_{\Psi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}[{\boldsymbol{A}}]\,\Omega_{\Psi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}[{\boldsymbol{A}}]R^{T}.

(53)

Note that the saturability of this bound is not trivial in general, and eventually can be reached only in the asymptotic limit
\nu\gg 1
. Note also that this relation assumes that the moment matrix is invertible. However, if there are some zero eigenvalues of
\mathcal{M}_{\Psi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}(\boldsymbol{A})
we should discard the corresponding observable from the vector
\boldsymbol{A}
since it will simply not contribute at all to the estimation.

Now let us consider probe states of the form
Eq.

39
, and again for simplicity an infinitesimal estimation with
\boldsymbol{\theta}\simeq\boldsymbol{0}
. Because all of the states in our family
\ket{\Psi_k}
are eigenstates of
J_{z}^{2}
(the corresponding eigenvalue being
\frac{1}{4}(n-2k)^{2}
for a given value of
k
) it turns out that the expectation values of
J_{z}
and
J_{z}^{2}
are insensitive to the (infinitesimal) rotation we want to estimate. Thus, first of all with such states it is impossible to estimate
\theta_{z}
by measuring expectation values of
\boldsymbol{A}_{\mathfrak{su}(2)}
; secondly, the observables
J_{z}
and
J_{z}^{2}
can be eliminated from
\boldsymbol{A}_{\mathfrak{su}(2)}
entirely since they are not useful also for the estimation of
\theta_{x}
and
\theta_{y}
.
Consequently, for the states in
Eq.

39
we can consider the reduced vector of observables

\boldsymbol{A}_{\mathfrak{su}(2)}^{\prime}=(J_{x},J_{y},\{J_{x},J_{z}\},\{J_{y},J_{z}\}),

(54)

and the reduced problem of estimating just
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime}=(\theta_{x},\theta_{y})
. Moreover, as we already briefly observed before, for the two-parameter estimation problem it is actually the twin-Fock state that gives the best precision scaling (which corresponds to the state with
k=n/2
in
Eq.

39
). What we observe here is that Heisenberg-scaling precision can in principle be maintained also via the method of moments with the observable set in
Eq.

54
. Note, however, that in practice it might still be challenging to make a suitable (potentially imperfect) reconstruction of the moments needed for the estimation, as the observables do not commute.

Making a more precise statement, we find that none of the states of the form
Eq.

39
have a moment matrix
\mathcal{M}_{\Psi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}[{\boldsymbol{A}}]
that allows to reach Heisenberg scaling for all three parameters
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=(\theta_{x},\theta_{y},\theta_{z})
. We find that only the twin-Fock state (or states close to it, e.g., with
k=n/2\pm 1
) allow for Heisenberg-scaling precision and only for the reduced problem of estimating
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime}
. In particular, for the twin-Fock state we get the following precision matrix (for small
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
, but analogous statement can be made for arbitrary
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
in this case, see
appendix

C
):

\lim_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\rightarrow(0,0,0)}{\mathcal{C}ov}^{-1}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}})\leq\nu\ {\rm diag}\left(\frac{n(n+2)}{2},\frac{n(n+2)}{2},0\right),

(55)

and thus we see that in this case the theoretical Heisenberg scaling of the QFIM could be in principle saturated via method of moments.

Furthermore, we can also obtain the optimal observables from which to evaluate the expectation values, which are (cf.
Eq.

136
)

\boldsymbol{M}^{\prime}=(\{J_{x},J_{z}\},\{J_{y},J_{z}\})^{T},

(56)

i.e., essentially one needs to estimate cross-covariances between the spin operators. Note that in
[
50
]
it was also discussed how to saturate the QCRB (i.e., achieve simultaneous Heisenberg scaling for
\theta_{x}
and
\theta_{y}
) for the twin-Fock state via the method of moments. It was pointed out that a set of two commuting observables can be found, which are
A_{x}=J_{x}|\Psi_{n/2}\rangle\langle\Psi_{n/2}|J_{x}
and
A_{y}=J_{y}|\Psi_{n/2}\rangle\langle\Psi_{n/2}|J_{y}
. But they involve the projector onto the twin-Fock state itself.

We conclude this discussion by briefly commenting on another candidate state that was proposed to achieve the Heisenberg limit for the three parameters, namely the state that is a superposition of three NOON states relative to three orthogonal directions
[
5
,
12
]
. For this, one can observe that the method of moments involving only quadratic spin observables does not work, essentially because the moment matrix would be entirely zero for that state. In fact, as we already mentioned, optimal measurements that achieve Heisenberg scaling for such a state contain correlators among all the
n
particles. Concerning the two-anticoherent states instead, as they are close to the state
\ket{\Psi_k}
for
k=\left\lfloor\frac{n-\sqrt{\frac{n(n+2)}{3}}}{2}\right\rceil
(even though a bit more difficult to characterize analytically), we would expect a similar conclusion on the impossibility to reach Heisenberg scaling with only the observables in
\boldsymbol{A}_{\mathfrak{su}(2)}
.

Let us consider here the estimation of a phase generated by an

[

. For better clarity, let us first consider the single-parameter case. First of all, the two-mode bosonic generators obtained from the fundamental representation of

\displaystyle K_{x}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(a_{1}^{\dagger}a_{2}^{\dagger}+a_{1}a_{2}),

(57)

\displaystyle K_{y}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2i}(a_{1}^{\dagger}a_{2}^{\dagger}-a_{1}a_{2}),

\displaystyle K_{z}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(1+a_{1}^{\dagger}a_{1}+a_{2}^{\dagger}a_{2}).

We can see that
K_{x}
and
K_{y}
are two-mode squeezing generators, while
K_{z}
is essentially the total number operator.

Thus, a single-parameter
SU(1,1)
estimation can be the estimation of a real squeezing parameter
\xi
in an evolution of the form

U(\xi)=e^{-i\xi K_{x}}.

(58)

In this setting it is clear that the total number of particles will not be conserved, since
SU(1,1)
operations do not commute with
N
. Thus, the question also arises how to properly identify the resources. An argument similar to the previous section is that the total average number of particles
\left\langle{N}\right\rangle=:\bar{n}
will be still bounded in practical evolutions, since it is related to the total energy of the system. In fact, a typical benchmark for indefinite-
n
states in the Mach-Zehnder interferometer is provided by a coherent-state input
\ket{\Psi_{\vec 0}}=\ket{\alpha, 0}

[
102
,
34
]
, which allows one to reach only shot-noise scaling in terms of the average particle number (see also
section

6
).

Another quantity that might make sense to keep fixed in the context of
SU(1,1)
estimations, at least from a group-theoretical point of view, is the eigenvalue
m
of
J_{z}
, which is a quantity that is left invariant by
SU(1,1)
unitaries and labels the corresponding irreducible representations. In other words,
SU(1,1)
unitaries always map eigenstates of
J_{z}
with a given eigenvalue
m
into eigenstates with the same eigenvalue and the question arises as to which sector are states that give the best scaling of precision with
\bar{n}
.

As a practical example, let us consider the estimation of an infinitesimal squeezing parameter
\xi\simeq 0
generated by
K_{x}
as above. First, it is easy to see that a Fock-state input reaches SQL scaling:

\begin{gathered}\ket{\Psi_{\vec 0}}=\ket{n,0}\quad\rightarrow\quad 4(\Delta K_{x})^{2}_{0}=n+1\\
\rightarrow\quad(\Delta\hat{\xi})^{2}=O(1/n),\end{gathered}

(59)

while for example once again an input twin-Fock state reaches Heisenberg scaling:

\begin{gathered}\ket{\Psi_{\vec 0}}=\ket{n/2,n/2}\quad\rightarrow\quad 4(\Delta K_{x})^{2}_{0}=(n^{2}/2)+n+1\\
\rightarrow\quad(\Delta\hat{\xi})^{2}=O(1/n^{2}).\end{gathered}

(60)

Similarly, it is also easy to see that the NOON state gives a SQL scaling with
\bar{n}=n
.

Note also how the Fock state has
m=n
, while the twin-Fock state is in the sector
m=0
. Other natural probe states with fluctuating number of particles are again the Heisenberg-Weyl coherent states, as well as squeezed states. The former gives SQL scaling completely analogously as the Fock state because it satisfies

4(\Delta K_{x})^{2}_{0}=|\alpha|^{2}+1=\bar{n}+1,

(61)

while inputting some initial squeezing allows to reach Heisenberg scaling in
\bar{n}
. For example, a natural probe state to consider in our context is given by a two-mode squeezed-vacuum

\ket{\Psi_0}=e^{-i\zeta K_{y}}\ket{0,0}=\sqrt{1-|q|^{2}}\sum_{n}q^{n}\ket{n,n}\ ,

(62)

where
q=-\tanh(\zeta/2)
. This probe state is also in the sector with
m=0
and satisfies

4(\Delta K_{x})^{2}_{0}=\bar{n}^{2}+2\bar{n}+1\rightarrow(\Delta\hat{\xi})^{2}=O(1/\bar{n}^{2}),

(63)

where the average particle number is given by

\bar{n}=\left\langle{N}\right\rangle_{0}=2\sinh^{2}{\frac{\zeta}{2}}.

(64)

Let us now consider the three-parameter estimation of an arbitrary
SU(1,1)
unitary (cf.
fig.

2
), and let us focus once again on the case of an infinitesimal unitary:

U(\boldsymbol{\xi})\simeq\mathbbm{1}-i(\xi_{x}K_{x}+\xi_{y}K_{y}+\xi_{z}K_{z})=\mathbbm{1}-i\boldsymbol{\xi}\cdot\boldsymbol{K},

(65)

which also means that
\tilde{\boldsymbol{H}}=\boldsymbol{K}
in this case. Here the case of an infinitesimal unitary, besides being possibly the most relevant from a practical perspective, is also the most tractable from a mathematical point of view.

\xi_{x}\simeq 0

5.2
Probe states reaching Heisenberg scaling for three parameters

Let us now consider superpositions of such Fock states, in the special case
n_{1}=n_{2}
, so as to restrict to the subspace

(69)

\displaystyle=(\Delta K_{y})^{2}_{\tilde{\Psi}_{k}}.

Thus, states of the form in

can reach Heisenberg scaling for the estimation of

and

5.3
Estimation via multiparameter method of moments

Following the same approach as in the
SU(2)
case, we now consider the scenario of an estimation via multiparameter method of moments with the following vector of observables:

\boldsymbol{A}_{\mathfrak{su}(1,1)}=(\boldsymbol{K},\tilde{\boldsymbol{Q}}),

(72)

where the vector of generators is given by

\tilde{\boldsymbol{H}}=\boldsymbol{K}=(K_{x},K_{y},K_{z}),

(73)

and corresponds to the first three elements, while

\tilde{\boldsymbol{Q}}=(K_{x}^{2},K_{y}^{2},K_{z}^{2},\{K_{x},K_{y}\},\{K_{x},K_{z}\},\{K_{y},K_{z}\})

(74)

is the vector of quadratic products.

Analogously as in the

estimation case, for states of the form (
67
) we have that the expectation values of
K_{z}
and
K_{z}^{2}
are insensitive to infinitesimal
SU(1,1)

\boldsymbol{A}^{\prime}_{\mathfrak{su}(1,1)}=(K_{x},K_{y},\{K_{x},K_{z}\},\{K_{y},K_{z}\})^{T},

(75)

and again for the same reason we will be able to estimate only phases generated by

\lim_{\boldsymbol{\xi}\rightarrow(0,0,0)}{\mathcal{C}ov}^{-1}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\xi}})\leq\nu\,{\rm diag}(\kappa,\kappa,0),

(76)

where

\kappa=1+\bar{n}+\tfrac{1}{2}{\bar{n}}^{2},

(77)

which would in principle allow the QFIM scaling given by
Eq.

66
. The observables for which one has to estimate the expectation values are given by

\boldsymbol{M}=(hK_{y}-\{K_{y},K_{z}\},hK_{x}-\{K_{x},K_{z}\})^{T},

(78)

where
h=1+\bar{n}
. Clearly, this is a more abstract scenario, in which the measurements are not necessarily possible to implement in practice. Furthermore, as before it is unclear whether the precision bound that we found can be saturated, even just theoretically, with an imperfect joint measurement of the observables in
\boldsymbol{M}
, since they do not commute (besides being potentially unrealistic to implement). Nevertheless, we do find some analogy between the
SU(1,1)
and the
SU(2)

\varrho_{0}=\sum_{n}|c_{n}|^{2}|\Psi_{n}\rangle\langle\Psi_{n}|,

(80)

\sum_{n,n^{\prime}}c_{n}^{*}c_{n^{\prime}}\bra{\Psi_n}J_{k}^{l}\ket{\Psi_{n^\prime}}=\sum_{n}|c_{n}|^{2}\bra{\Psi_n}J_{k}^{l}\ket{\Psi_n},

(81)

which can also be understood in terms of a superselection rule
[
11

SU(2)
unitaries via moments of
su(2)
generators
[
124

.

Still, one can consider the more general framework of fluctuating-
n
states, perhaps with physical restrictions on the allowed superpositions and measurements. A typical benchmark for indefinite-
n

\ket{\alpha, 0}=e^{-|\alpha|^{2}/2}\sum_{n}\frac{\alpha^{n}}{\sqrt{n!}}\ket{n,0},

and has an average number of particles given by

.
Thus, the reference for “classical” probe states for fluctuating

Several ideas have been explored to surpass this limit in the fluctuating-

7
Conclusion and outlook

In conclusion, we have investigated the multiparameter estimation of
SU(2)
and
SU(1,1)
unitaries and the limit to their precision scaling. First, for each of the two estimation tasks we identified a class of pure states containing ideal probes that would potentially allow for reaching Heisenberg precision scaling simultaneously in all three parameters according to the quantum Fisher information matrix and the relative multiparameter quantum Cramér-Rao bound. In the
SU(2)
case, we consider subspaces (sectors) of states with fixed particle number, as the operator
N
itself is left invariant by the parameter-encoding evolution. Furthermore, the value of
\left\langle{N}\right\rangle=n
can be seen as a resource connected with the total energy of the system. As an ideal class of probe states we identified eigenstates of
J^{2}_{z}
with different fixed-
n
eigenvalues. Such a class contains paradigmatic states, such as the NOON state, the twin-Fock state and also states close to those which have been called two-anti-coherent in recent literature
[
15
,
29
,
56
,
96
,
55
,
116
]
.

In the
SU(1,1)
case instead, the particle number is not anymore fixed by the evolution, but the analogous invariant becomes the value of
J_{z}
. Still, the average particle number, even if not conserved by the encoding evolution, can be seen as the total energy resource of the system. Therefore, we consider subspaces of states with different values of
\left\langle{J_{z}}\right\rangle=m
and identify a class of states in the
m=0
sector that contains states that would allow Heisenberg scaling of precision in terms of
\left\langle{N}\right\rangle=\bar{n}
in all three parameters according to their QFIM. Again, motivated by the fact that the sector with
m=0
contains ideally useful states, we consider a class of eigenstates of
J_{z}^{2}
which is analogous to the
SU(2)
case.

Afterwards, we considered a more pragmatic scenario in which the estimation is performed via signal-to-noise ratio of first and second moments of the (
su(2)
and
su(1,1)
) generators on the probe states. Analyzing such a scenario with the ideal class of pure probe states that we have identified, we found that the twin-Fock state emerges in both
SU(2)
and
SU(1,1)
estimations as the only state that allows one to achieve Heisenberg limit for at least two out of the three parameters. As a comparison, we also investigated the case of other paradigmatic probe states with a fluctuating particle number, such as Gaussian states and cat states, allowing only the measurement of expectation values of operators quadratic in the mode operators. In this case we find that only the two-mode squeezed state emerges for allowing simultaneous Heisenberg scaling in a two-parameter
SU(2)
estimation, which confirms the resourceful nature of such a state as discussed in the single-parameter scenario
[
24
,
14
,
138
]
and also generalizes to the multiparameter case the arguments on the enhancement of
SU(2)
estimations with prior
SU(1,1)
operations
[
20
,
18
,
19
,
46
,
25
,
87
,
136
,
140
,
37
]
.

As an outlook, we anticipate that our methods are readily generalizable to unitary groups containing more parameters, such as the
SU(d)
and the
SU(p,q)
groups. Also in those cases it is possible to identify ideal classes of states in sectors left invariant by the encoding evolution and analyze the estimation precision with the error-propagation formula from measurements of (low) powers of the generators. Still, the open question remains on how to saturate the asymptotic bounds coming from the method of moments when the measured observables do not commute and one is forced to do either independent or imperfect measurements of the noncommuting observables. We believe these and related questions, for example, further connections between optimal probe states for larger subgroups of unitaries, and the notion of anti-coherent states
[
116
]
, would deserve further investigation. Moreover, from a practical point of view, it could be interesting to explore a more general framework, for example including non-ideal preparations, practical measurement schemes, as well as going towards global estimations (at least in the
SU(2)
or other compact groups cases) and consider a Bayesian approach. We thus hope that our work could trigger subsequent investigation with group-theoretic methods in the estimation of multiparameter unitaries.

(89)

Assume that the theoretical mean

\mu(\theta):=\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[M]=\sum_{q}m_{q}\,{\rm p}(q|\theta)

(90)

is known and (locally) invertible. The MoM estimator is defined implicitly by matching the empirical and theoretical moments,

\mu(\hat{\theta})=\bar{M}_{\nu}\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad\hat{\theta}=\mu^{-1}(\bar{M}_{\nu}).

(91)

For large
\nu
, the central limit theorem (CLT) implies that the random variable
\sqrt{\nu}\,(\bar{M}_{\nu}-\mu(\theta))
is normally distributed with zero mean and variance given by

\mathrm{Var}_{\theta}[\bar{M}_{\nu}]=\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[M^{2}]-\mu^{2}(\theta).

(92)

Then, from a first-order Taylor expansion one obtains

\hat{\theta}-\theta\;\simeq\;\frac{1}{\mu^{\prime}(\theta)}\bigl(\bar{M}_{\nu}-\mu(\theta)\bigr),

(93)

where
\mu^{\prime}(\theta):=\partial_{\theta}\mu(\theta)
.

Because of this, the estimator is asymptotically unbiased and has a mean-squared error (MSE) given by

\mathrm{MSE}[\hat{\theta}]=\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[(\hat{\theta}-\theta)^{2}]\;=\;\frac{1}{\nu}\,\frac{\mathrm{Var}_{\theta}[M]}{\mu^{\prime}(\theta)^{2}}+O(\nu^{-2}).

(94)

In the quantum setting, if
M
corresponds to a Hermitian operator measured on a density matrix
\varrho_{\theta}
, then the mean is
\mu(\theta)=\langle M\rangle_{\varrho_{\theta}}
and the variance is
\mathrm{Var}_{\theta}[M]=(\Delta M)^{2}_{\varrho_{\theta}}
.

Unbiased estimators satisfy the (classical) Cramér–Rao bound,

\mathrm{MSE}[\hat{\theta}]\geq\frac{1}{\nu\,\mathcal{F}(\theta)},

(95)

where
\mathcal{F}(\theta)
is the Fisher information for a single shot. Pre-asymptotic corrections to (
94
) typically enter at
O(\nu^{-2})
and depend on higher cumulants as well as higher derivatives of
\mu(\theta)
; see, e.g., Refs.
[
84
,
65
]
. The method of moments estimator is asymptotically unbiased
[
108
]
so that in this regime the MSE coincides with the variance
(\Delta\hat{\theta}_{M})^{2}
.

A.2
Multiparameter case

Let
\boldsymbol{\theta}=(\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{r})
be the unknown parameter vector. First, consider a
single joint measurement
described by a PVM
\{|q\rangle\langle q|\}
, and let
\boldsymbol{M}=(M_{1},\dots,M_{k})
be a collection of
commuting
Hermitian operators that are diagonal in that same basis,

M_{i}=\sum_{q}m_{i}(q)|q\rangle\langle q|,\qquad[M_{i},M_{j}]=0.

(96)

The joint outcome
q
is distributed as
{\rm p}(q|\boldsymbol{\theta})=\mathrm{Tr}[\varrho_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}|q\rangle\langle q|]
, and it induces a classical random vector

\boldsymbol{M}(q):=\bigl(m_{1}(q),\dots,m_{k}(q)\bigr)^{T}.

(97)

The empirical mean vector and the theoretical mean vector are

\bar{\boldsymbol{M}}_{\nu}:=\frac{1}{\nu}\sum_{t=1}^{\nu}\boldsymbol{M}(q_{t}),\qquad\boldsymbol{\mu}(\boldsymbol{\theta}):=\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[\boldsymbol{M}]=\bigl(\langle M_{1}\rangle_{\varrho_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}},\dots,\langle M_{k}\rangle_{\varrho_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}\bigr)^{T}.

(98)

The MoM estimator is defined by the vector equation

\boldsymbol{\mu}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}})=\bar{\boldsymbol{M}}_{\nu}.

(99)

Define the
k\times r
Jacobian

C_{ij}(\boldsymbol{\theta}):=\partial_{\theta_{j}}\mu_{i}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\partial_{\theta_{j}}\langle M_{i}\rangle_{\varrho_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}.

(100)

The multivariate CLT yields that the vector of random variables
\sqrt{\nu}\,(\bar{\boldsymbol{M}}_{\nu}-\boldsymbol{\mu}(\boldsymbol{\theta}))
is normally distributed with mean zero and covariance matrix given by
\mathrm{Cov}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{M})
. Linearizing (
99
) around
\boldsymbol{\theta}
gives

\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}-\boldsymbol{\theta}\;\simeq\;C^{+}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\bigl(\bar{\boldsymbol{M}}-\boldsymbol{\mu}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\bigr),

(101)

where
C^{+}
denotes a left-inverse (for
k=r
and
\det C\neq 0
, this is
C^{-1}
). Hence, once again the MoM is asymptotically unbiased. Its MSE matrix following from the CLT (i.e., valid in the large
\nu
limit) is given by:

\mathrm{MSE}[\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}]:=\mathbb{E}_{\theta}\bigl[(\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}-\boldsymbol{\theta})(\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}-\boldsymbol{\theta})^{T}\bigr]=\frac{1}{\nu}\,C^{+}\,\mathrm{Cov}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}[\boldsymbol{M}]\,\bigl(C^{+}\bigr)^{T}+O(\nu^{-2}).

(102)

In this case of commuting observables, since each repetition produces a joint outcome
q_{t}
that gives the value of all observables, the empirical covariance of the sampled statistics converges to the covariance matrix of the measured observables, i.e., we have:

\displaystyle[\mathrm{Cov}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}[\boldsymbol{M}]]_{ij}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{\nu-1}\sum_{t=1}^{\nu}\bigl(m_{i}(q_{t})-(\bar{M}_{\nu})_{i}\bigr)\bigl(m_{j}(q_{t})-(\bar{M}_{\nu})_{j}\bigr)=\langle M_{i}M_{j}\rangle_{\varrho_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}-\langle M_{i}\rangle_{\varrho_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}\langle M_{j}\rangle_{\varrho_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}=[\Gamma_{\varrho_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}[\boldsymbol{M}]]_{ij}.

(103)

If
\varrho_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=U(\boldsymbol{\theta})\varrho\,U^{\dagger}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
and we work in the Heisenberg picture
M_{i}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=U^{\dagger}(\boldsymbol{\theta})M_{i}U(\boldsymbol{\theta})
, then

C_{ij}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\partial_{\theta_{j}}\langle M_{i}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\rangle_{\varrho}=-\mathrm{i}\,\bigl\langle[M_{i}(\boldsymbol{\theta}),\tilde{H}_{j}(\boldsymbol{\theta})]\bigr\rangle_{\varrho}:=[\Omega_{\varrho}[\boldsymbol{M}(\boldsymbol{\theta}),\tilde{\boldsymbol{H}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})]]_{ij},

(104)

with
\tilde{H}_{j}(\boldsymbol{\theta}):=\mathrm{i}\,U^{\dagger}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\,\partial_{\theta_{j}}U(\boldsymbol{\theta})
, which connects the Jacobian directly to commutators with the (local) generators
\tilde{H}_{j}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
. Here we also defined the commutator matrix
\Omega_{\varrho_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}[\boldsymbol{M},\tilde{\boldsymbol{H}}]=\Omega_{\varrho}[\boldsymbol{M}(\boldsymbol{\theta}),\tilde{\boldsymbol{H}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})]
. Compactly, one can introduce the moment matrix

\mathcal{M}_{\varrho_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}[\boldsymbol{M}]:=\Omega^{T}_{\varrho_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}[\boldsymbol{M},\tilde{\boldsymbol{H}}]\,\Gamma^{-1}_{\varrho_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}[\boldsymbol{M}]\,\Omega_{\varrho_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}[\boldsymbol{M},\tilde{\boldsymbol{H}}],

(105)

such that the MSE matrix can be written as
[
50
]

\mathrm{MSE}[\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}]=\frac{1}{\nu}\,\mathcal{M}^{-1}_{\varrho_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}[\boldsymbol{M}]+O(\nu^{-2}).

(106)

Note that this argument can be extended also to the case of
non-commuting
quantum observables
\boldsymbol{M}
. With the same reasoning, one can resort to the CLT to find the asymptotic expression for the MSE matrix as in
Eq.

106
. In that case, we have to consider the
real
covariance matrix, which is given by the symmetrized version
[\Gamma_{\varrho_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}[\boldsymbol{M}]]_{ij}=\tfrac{1}{2}\langle M_{i}M_{j}+M_{j}M_{i}\rangle_{\varrho_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}-\langle M_{i}\rangle_{\varrho_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}\langle M_{j}\rangle_{\varrho_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}
. However, when the observables of interest do
not
commute, one cannot in general associate them to a single joint PVM on the original system. A natural alternative is to perform an
imperfect joint measurement
described by a (generally non-projective) POVM; operationally, this produces joint classical data from which one may extract (noisy) estimates. An exemplary scenario is the extraction of Husimi type of quasi-probability distributions, as it is done via heterodyne measurements of quadrature operators, or non-orthogonal projectors onto spin-coherent states for the case of spin variables.

Let
\{E_{u}\}
be such a noisy POVM, where
u
can be discrete or continuous, and we use a different letter to emphasize that it is not associated to the spectrum of the quantum operators
\boldsymbol{M}
that we want to measure. In this case, the outcome probability is given again by the Born rule:

{\rm p}(u|\boldsymbol{\theta})=\tr[\varrho_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}E_{u}],

(107)

and we can take a vector of (real) processing functions
\boldsymbol{M}(u)=(m_{1}(u),\dots,m_{k}(u))^{T}
to define the classical random vector

\boldsymbol{M}:=\boldsymbol{M}(u),

(108)

and repeat the same reasoning as above to define the MoM estimator and find its asymptotic unbiasedness and its MSE.

From
\nu
independent repetitions we obtain outcomes
q_{1},\dots,q_{\nu}
and hence the sampled mean vector

\bar{\boldsymbol{M}}_{\nu}:=\frac{1}{\nu}\sum_{t=1}^{\nu}\boldsymbol{M}(q_{t}),

(109)

analogously as before, which defines the MoM estimator. The theoretical mean can be also still computed analogously as before:

\boldsymbol{\mu}(\boldsymbol{\theta}):=\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}[\boldsymbol{M}]=\int{\rm d}u\,\boldsymbol{m}(u)\,{\rm p}(u|\boldsymbol{\theta}),

(110)

where we used a continuous variable notation for the outcomes for generality.

Similar relation holds for other moments like

\langle{M_{i}M_{j}}\rangle_{\varrho_{\theta}}=\int{\rm d}u\,m_{i}(u)m_{j}(u)\,{\rm p}(u|\boldsymbol{\theta}),

(111)

which can be expressed also by defining operators associated to the moments in terms of the POVM elements:

F_{i}:=\int{\rm d}u\,m_{i}(u)\,E_{u},\qquad F_{ij}:=\int{\rm d}u\,m_{i}(u)\,m_{j}(u)\,E_{u},

(112)

so that

\mu_{i}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\tr[\varrho_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}F_{i}],\qquad[\Gamma_{\varrho_{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{M})]_{ij}=\tr[\varrho_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}F_{ij}]-\tr[\varrho_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}F_{i}]\tr[\varrho_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}F_{j}].

(113)

If the goal is to jointly infer expectation values of a non-commuting operator vector
\boldsymbol{A}=(A_{1},\dots,A_{k})
, a standard notion of an
unbiased
(first-moment preserving) joint measurement is

F_{i}=A_{i}\qquad\text{for all }i.

(114)

In this case, the POVM outcomes reproduce the target first moments exactly,

\mu_{i}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\tr[\varrho_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}A_{i}]=\langle A_{i}\rangle_{\varrho_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}.

(115)

The price for joint access to non-commuting observables is then paid at the level of second moments: the measurement is necessarily noisy, and the covariance of the sampled statistics contains additional noise
[
23
]
.

Alternatively, one may estimate different components by measuring different POVMs on disjoint subsets of the
\nu
copies. In this case, the corresponding sample means are formed from
independent
datasets, which in turn requires a large number of samples for each independent observable, as well as additional ones to estimate the MSE from the covariances.

A.2.1
Example: heterodyne measurement of two quadratures

A canonical way to
jointly, but imperfectly
access two non-commuting quadratures is heterodyne detection on a single bosonic mode. Let
a
be the annihilation operator and define dimensionless quadratures

x:=\frac{a+a^{\dagger}}{\sqrt{2}},\qquad p:=\frac{a-a^{\dagger}}{i\sqrt{2}},\qquad[x,p]=i\mathbbm{1}.

(116)

The heterodyne POVM is the coherent-state POVM

E(\alpha)=\frac{1}{\pi}|\alpha\rangle\langle\alpha|,\qquad\alpha\in\mathbb{C},\qquad\int_{\mathbb{C}}d^{2}\alpha\,E(\alpha)=\mathbbm{1},

(117)

so that the outcome distribution is the Husimi
Q
function:

{\rm p}(\alpha|\boldsymbol{\theta})=\tr[\varrho_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}E(\alpha)]=\frac{1}{\pi}\bra{\alpha}\varrho_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\ket{\alpha}=:\mathcal{Q}_{\varrho_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}(\alpha).

(118)

Writing the complex outcome as

\alpha=\frac{x+ip}{\sqrt{2}}\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad x=\sqrt{2}\,\real\alpha,\;\;p=\sqrt{2}\,\imaginary\alpha,

(119)

each experimental run returns a
pair

\alpha_{t}=(x_{t},p_{t})
from a single shot. We then define the empirical means and empirical covariance matrix from the data stream

\bar{x}=\frac{1}{\nu}\sum_{t=1}^{\nu}x_{t},\qquad\bar{p}=\frac{1}{\nu}\sum_{t=1}^{\nu}p_{t},

(120)

\widehat{\Gamma}=\begin{pmatrix}\widehat{\mathrm{Var}}(x)&\widehat{{\Gamma}}(x,p)\\
\widehat{{\Gamma}}(p,x)&\widehat{\mathrm{Var}}(p)\end{pmatrix},\qquad\widehat{{\Gamma}}(x,p):=\frac{1}{\nu-1}\sum_{t=1}^{\nu}(x_{t}-\bar{x})(p_{t}-\bar{p}),

(121)

(and similarly for
\widehat{\mathrm{Var}}(x),\widehat{\mathrm{Var}}(p)
). Since each repetition produces
joint
outcomes
(x_{t},p_{t})
, we can resort to the law of large numbers to assume

\bar{x}\xrightarrow{\nu\to\infty}\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}[x],\qquad\bar{p}\xrightarrow{\nu\to\infty}\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}[p],\qquad\widehat{\Gamma}\xrightarrow{\nu\to\infty}\Gamma_{\varrho_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}(\boldsymbol{X}),

(122)

where
\Gamma_{\varrho_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}(\boldsymbol{X})
can be also drawn from heterodyne statistics, i.e., from the moments:

\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}[f(x(\alpha),p(\alpha))]=\int_{\mathbb{C}}{\rm d}^{2}\alpha\,f(x(\alpha),p(\alpha))\,{\rm p}(\alpha|\boldsymbol{\theta}),

(123)

for suitable functions
f(x(\alpha),p(\alpha))
. The
Q
distribution reproduces
first moments
of the quadratures exactly:

\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}[\boldsymbol{X}]=\langle\boldsymbol{X}\rangle_{\varrho_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}.

(124)

For
second moments
, heterodyne detection is a noisy joint measurement: it adds one half quantum of vacuum noise to each marginal quadrature. Concretely,

\mathrm{Var}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(x)=\mathrm{Var}_{\varrho_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}(X)+\frac{1}{2},\qquad\mathrm{Var}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(p)=\mathrm{Var}_{\varrho_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}(P)+\frac{1}{2}.

(125)

Equivalently, one may think of the heterodyne outcomes as an effective classical model

x=X+\xi_{x},\qquad p=P+\xi_{p},

(126)

where
\xi_{x},\xi_{p}
are independent, zero-mean Gaussian noises with variance
1/2
(the “vacuum penalty”), independent of the state.

For the
cross covariance
, no additional constant term appears; rather one obtains the symmetrized quantum covariance:

{\Gamma}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(x,p)=\frac{1}{2}\bigl\langle XP+PX\bigr\rangle_{\varrho_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}-\langle X\rangle_{\varrho_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}\langle P\rangle_{\varrho_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}\;=:{\Gamma}_{\varrho_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}^{(\mathrm{sym})}(X,P).

(127)

Thus, the heterodyne covariance matrix can be written compactly as

\Gamma(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\Gamma^{(\mathrm{sym})}_{\varrho_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}(X,P)+\frac{1}{2}\,\mathbbm{1}_{2},\qquad\Gamma^{(\mathrm{sym})}_{\varrho}(X,P):=\begin{pmatrix}\mathrm{Var}_{\varrho}(X)&{\Gamma}^{(\mathrm{sym})}_{\varrho}(X,P)\\
{\Gamma}^{(\mathrm{sym})}_{\varrho}(P,X)&\mathrm{Var}_{\varrho}(P)\end{pmatrix}.

(128)

A.3
Optimization over an accessible operator set and attainability of the moment-matrix bound

In many practical scenarios one has access only to a restricted set of observables

\boldsymbol{A}:=(A_{1},\dots,A_{n})^{T},

(129)

from which both the (local) generators and the measured observables are chosen as linear combinations. In particular, we write (local) generators as

\tilde{\boldsymbol{H}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=R(\boldsymbol{\theta})\,\boldsymbol{A},

(130)

with a (generally
\boldsymbol{\theta}
-dependent) real matrix
R(\boldsymbol{\theta})
, and the measured observables as

\boldsymbol{M}=S\boldsymbol{A},

(131)

for some real matrix
S
.

Given a state
\varrho
, define the covariance matrix of
\boldsymbol{A}
and the commutator matrix

\bigl[{\Gamma}_{\varrho}(\boldsymbol{A})\bigr]_{kl}:={\Gamma}_{\varrho}(A_{k},A_{l}),\qquad\bigl[\Omega_{\varrho}(\boldsymbol{A})\bigr]_{kl}:=-\mathrm{i}\,\langle[A_{k},A_{l}]\rangle_{\varrho},

(132)

(where for noncommuting operators we take the
symmetrized
covariance

{\Gamma}_{\varrho}(A_{k},A_{l}):=\tfrac{1}{2}\langle A_{k}A_{l}+A_{l}A_{k}\rangle_{\varrho}-\langle A_{k}\rangle_{\varrho}\langle A_{l}\rangle_{\varrho}
, which corresponds to the real part of the non-symmetrized covariance matrix).
The associated “accessible” moment matrix is

\mathcal{M}_{\varrho}[\boldsymbol{A}]:=\Omega^{T}_{\varrho}(\boldsymbol{A})\;{\Gamma}^{-1}_{\varrho}(\boldsymbol{A})\;\Omega_{\varrho}(\boldsymbol{A}).

(133)

For any choice of measured observables
\boldsymbol{M}
and generators
\tilde{\boldsymbol{H}}
of the form
(
130
)–(
131
), the moment matrix associated with the MoM protocol obeys the bound

\mathcal{M}_{\varrho}\bigl[\tilde{\boldsymbol{H}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}),\boldsymbol{M}\bigr]\;\leq\;R(\boldsymbol{\theta})\,\mathcal{M}_{\varrho}[\boldsymbol{A}]\,R^{T}(\boldsymbol{\theta}),

(134)

as shown in Ref.
[
50
]
.
Moreover, the bound is saturated whenever there exists an invertible matrix
G
such that

G\,S\;=\;R(\boldsymbol{\theta})\,\Omega_{\varrho}(\boldsymbol{A})\,{\Gamma}^{-1}_{\varrho}(\boldsymbol{A}),

(135)

in which case one may choose an optimal set of observables

\boldsymbol{M}_{\rm opt}=G^{-1}\,R(\boldsymbol{\theta})\,\Omega_{\varrho}(\boldsymbol{A})\,{\Gamma}^{-1}_{\varrho}(\boldsymbol{A})\,\boldsymbol{A}.

(136)

The key point is that Eq. (
21
) is an
algebraic
optimization over linear combinations of the accessible operators and is therefore independent of how (or whether) the components of
\boldsymbol{M}_{\rm opt}
can be measured jointly in a single shot.

Whether the upper bound (
21
) is operationally saturable depends on the measurement model, i.e. on what is allowed in collecting the classical data used to form the empirical moments.
The bound (
21
) is automatically attainable by a single PVM whenever the optimal observables commute. If the optimal observables do not commute, then attainability becomes model-dependent: a single-shot joint POVM typically adds noise (hence one may fail to saturate the bound), while separate measurements avoid joint-measurement noise but require a larger number of samples.

Beyond the CLT regime, the covariance admits a systematic expansion in
1/\nu
, whose next-to-leading terms depend on higher-order cumulants of the measurement outcomes and on higher derivatives of the mean-value map
\boldsymbol{\mu}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\langle\boldsymbol{M}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\rangle
. Such pre-asymptotic corrections can be addressed using higher-order asymptotic methods (see, e.g.,
[
84
,
98
,
17
]
) and, in the quantum setting, may be viewed as multiparameter generalizations of corrections beyond the QCRB
[
65
]
. In the present work we restrict to the leading
1/\nu
behavior captured by the moment-matrix formalism.

Appendix B
Recap of single-parameter
SU(2)
estimation

Here, we reconsider some known results about the precision limits of estimation schemes where the evolution belongs to
SU(2)
.
As we mentioned, it is well known that the Mach-Zehnder Interferometer can be described in terms of the Lie-group
SU(2)

[
138
,
24
,
82
,
132
]
. Mathematically, applying the so-called Jordan-Schwinger map
to the spin-
1/2
operators
J_{k}=\mathbf{a}^{\dagger}\cdot\tfrac{1}{2}\sigma_{k}\cdot\mathbf{a}
where
\sigma_{k}
are the usual Pauli matrices with
k=x,y,z
and
\mathbf{a}=(a_{1},a_{2})^{T}
, we obtain

\displaystyle J_{x}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(a^{\dagger}_{1}a_{2}+a_{1}a^{\dagger}_{2})

(137)

\displaystyle J_{y}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2i}(a^{\dagger}_{1}a_{2}-a_{1}a^{\dagger}_{2})

\displaystyle J_{z}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(a^{\dagger}_{1}a_{1}-a^{\dagger}_{2}a_{2}),

which generate a
su(2)
algebra.
In an optical setup,
J_{x}
and
J_{y}
correspond to the interaction of a beam-splitter and in general two-mode systems (e.g., a Bose-Einstein Condensate with two internal states or in a double-well), these operators correspond to tunneling between the modes. In both cases,
J_{z}
is half the population difference between modes.

The
su(2)
operators are observables that can be measured via the action of a suitable
SU(2)
unitary followed by measurements of local number operators
n_{1}=a_{1}^{\dagger}a_{1}
and
n_{2}=a_{2}^{\dagger}a_{2}
. For example, the observable
J_{y}
satisfies

J_{y}=e^{i(\pi/2)J_{x}}J_{z}e^{-i(\pi/2)J_{x}}.

(138)

One can then measure
J_{y}
by sending the input state through a balanced beam-splitter and measure
\frac{1}{2}(n_{1}-n_{2})
on the resulting state. A similar reasoning allows to measure
J_{x}
and indeed any
su(2)
observable. More generally, any polynomial in
J_{x}
,
J_{y}
and
J_{z}
can be brought to a polynomial in
J_{z}
via suitable
SU(2)
unitaries
[
135
]
.

These
su(2)
operators commute with the total particle number operator
N=a^{\dagger}_{1}a_{1}+a^{\dagger}_{2}a_{2}
, and as such it might be useful to partition the state space into sectors with different fixed values of
N
. The latter, or its expectation value
\langle{N}\rangle
, can be also seen as a resource that is connected with the total energy of the system, which is bounded in practical applications. Furthermore, when the total number of particles is bounded by some
n
, one can easily see that the most useful states are in the corresponding fixed-
n
subspace.

We also remark that a subspace with fixed
n
corresponds to the permutationally symmetric subspace of
n
qubits, or equivalently to the space of a single particle with total spin
j=n/2
, which has dimension
d=n+1
. In such a space, states that are eigenstates of the spin
J_{\hat{\boldsymbol{\rm n}}}
along a direction
\hat{\boldsymbol{\rm n}}=(\hat{\rm n}_{x},\hat{\rm n}_{y},\hat{\rm n}_{z})
with eigenvalue
n/2
are called
SU(2)-coherent
. From those states, which are not orthogonal to each other, one can construct a resolution of the identity similar to that in
Eq.

34
which reads

\frac{2j+1}{4\pi}\int_{S_{2}}{\mathrm{d}}\hat{\boldsymbol{\rm n}}\ |\hat{\boldsymbol{\rm n}}\rangle\langle\hat{\boldsymbol{\rm n}}|=\mathbbm{1},

(139)

where
\ket{\hat{\vec{\rm n}}}
denotes the state pointing in the direction
\hat{\boldsymbol{\rm n}}
and the integral is performed on the two-dimensional (Bloch) sphere
S_{2}
of a spin-
j
particle with
j=n/2
.

In the usual MZI (cf.
fig.

3
), an input state enters a balanced beam-splitter followed by arbitrary phase shift operations and then by a recombining beam-splitter operation, which corresponds to the unitary encoding:

\ket{\Psi}\mapsto e^{i(\pi/2)J_{x}}e^{i(\phi_{1}n_{1}+\phi_{2}n_{2})}e^{-i(\pi/2)J_{x}}\ket{\Psi}.

(140)

It is easy to see that

\begin{gathered}\phi_{1}n_{1}+\phi_{2}n_{2}=\frac{\phi_{1}+\phi_{2}}{2}N+(\phi_{1}-\phi_{2})J_{z}\\
\implies e^{i(\phi_{1}n_{1}+\phi_{2}n_{2})}=e^{i\Phi N}e^{-i\theta J_{z}}\\
\text{with}\quad\Phi=\frac{\phi_{1}+\phi_{2}}{2}\quad\text{and}\quad\theta=\phi_{2}-\phi_{1}.\end{gathered}

(141)

For states with a definite value of the total number of particles
n
the term
e^{i\Phi N}
corresponds to a negligible global phase. Thus, the usual MZI corresponds to a single-parameter estimation scheme with generator given by
J_{y}
:

\ket{\Psi_\theta}=e^{-i\theta J_{y}}\ket{\Psi_0}.

(142)

Taking a Fock state
\ket{n,0}
as the input corresponds to a
SU(2)
-coherent state pointing in the
\hat{\boldsymbol{\rm z}}
direction and results in the so-called
Standard Quantum Limit
(SQL) (or
shot-noise scaling
):

\begin{gathered}\ket{\Psi_{\vec 0}}=\ket{n,0}\quad\rightarrow\quad 4(\Delta J_{y})^{2}_{0}=n\quad\rightarrow\quad(\Delta\hat{\theta})^{2}\geq 1/[4(\Delta J_{y})^{2}_{0}]=1/n,\end{gathered}

(143)

where the QFI is calculated as the variance of the generator
J_{y}
since it is a single-parameter estimation (cf.
Eq.

9
). Such a scaling is saturated, in the vicinity of a reference point
\theta=0
, by a measurement of
J_{x}
and the usual error-propagation formula for the accuracy coming from the method of moments:

\displaystyle(\Delta\hat{\theta})^{2}=\frac{(\Delta J_{x})^{2}_{\theta}}{(\partial_{\theta}\left\langle{J_{x}}\right\rangle)^{2}}\bigg|_{\theta\simeq 0}=\frac{(\Delta J_{x})^{2}_{0}}{\left\langle{J_{z}}\right\rangle_{0}^{2}}=\frac{1}{n}.

(144)

On the other hand, using a so-called
twin-Fock state
as an input gives
Heisenberg scaling

\ket{\Psi_0}=\ket{n/2,n/2}\quad\rightarrow\ 4(\Delta J_{y})^{2}_{0}=n(n/2+1),

(145)

which is also saturated by the method of moments, by measuring the second-order observable
\{J_{x},J_{z}\}
, where
\{\cdot,\cdot\}
denotes the anti-commutator.

The theoretically optimal state is given by the NOON state

\ket{\Psi_0}=e^{-i(\pi/2)J_{x}}\left(\frac{\ket{n,0}+\ket{0,n}}{\sqrt{2}}\right)\ \rightarrow\ 4(\Delta J_{y})^{2}_{0}=n^{2},

(146)

which needs the measurement of observables such as
M=|0,n\rangle\langle n,0|+|n,0\rangle\langle 0,n|
, which essentially corresponds to parity measurements, or in the multi-qubit language to
n
-qubit correlators like
\sigma_{x}^{\otimes n}

[
124
]
.

A Mach-Zehnder interferometer. An input state
\ket{\Psi_{\vec 0}}
passes through BS-1, PS-1 and PS-2, resulting in the evolved state
\ket{\Psi_{\theta}}=U(\theta)\ket{\Psi_{\vec 0}}
, where
U(\theta)\in

Appendix C
More details on three-parameter
SU(2)
estimations with the method of moments

C.1
Local estimation and parametrizations

Here we provide some details on the more general problem of locally estimating three
SU(2)
phases with arbitrary values by measuring moments of the
su(2)
generators. Beyond infinitesimal unitaries, the formulation of the problem depends on the chosen parametrization of the
SU(2)
group. Let us first consider the fundamental representations, given by

U(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\exp(-i\frac 12\sum_k \theta_k \sigma_k)=\begin{pmatrix}\cos(\theta/2)-i\frac{\theta_{3}\sin(\theta/2)}{\theta}&-\frac{\theta_{2}\sin(\theta/2)}{\theta}-i\frac{\theta_{1}\sin(\theta/2)}{\theta}\\
\frac{\theta_{2}\sin(\theta/2)}{\theta}-i\frac{\theta_{1}\sin(\theta/2)}{\theta}&\cos(\theta/2)+i\frac{\theta_{3}\sin(\theta/2)}{\theta}\end{pmatrix},

(147)

where
\sigma_{k}
are the usual Pauli matrices and we called
\theta=\sqrt{\theta_{1}^{2}+\theta_{2}^{2}+\theta_{3}^{2}}
.

Given any element of the algebra
G\in su(2)
, and any element of the group
U\in SU(2)
, we have also the relation
UGU^{-1}\in su(2)
, which
also means that, given a basis
\{J_{1},J_{2},J_{3}\}
of
su(2)
we can write

UGU^{-1}=\sum_{k}c_{k}J_{k},

(148)

for some coefficients
c_{k}
.
In particular, a change of
su(2)
basis is obtained as

J^{\prime}_{k}=UJ_{k}U^{-1}=\sum_{l}(\mathcal{O}_{\bf 3})_{kl}J_{l},

(149)

where
\mathcal{O}
is a
3\times 3
orthogonal matrix.
For a generic change of basis
U(\theta)=\exp(-i\frac 12\sum_k \theta_k \sigma_k)
parametrized by three angles
\boldsymbol{\theta}
such an orthogonal matrix is given by

\mathcal{O}_{\bf 3}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{\theta_{1}^{2}+(\theta_{2}^{2}+\theta_{3}^{2})\cos\theta}{\theta^{2}}&\frac{\theta_{1}\theta_{2}(1-\cos\theta)-\theta_{3}\theta\sin\theta}{\theta^{2}}&\frac{\theta_{1}\theta_{3}(1-\cos\theta)+\theta_{2}\theta\sin\theta}{\theta^{2}}\\
\frac{\theta_{1}\theta_{2}(1-\cos\theta)+\theta_{3}\theta\sin\theta}{\theta^{2}}&\frac{\theta_{2}^{2}+(\theta_{1}^{2}+\theta_{3}^{2})\cos\theta}{\theta^{2}}&\frac{\theta_{2}\theta_{3}(1-\cos\theta)-\theta_{1}\theta\sin\theta}{\theta^{2}}\\
\frac{\theta_{1}\theta_{3}(1-\cos\theta)-\theta_{2}\theta\sin\theta}{\theta^{2}}&\frac{\theta_{2}\theta_{3}(1-\cos\theta)+\theta_{1}\theta\sin\theta}{\theta^{2}}&\frac{\theta_{3}^{2}+(\theta_{1}^{2}+\theta_{2}^{2})\cos\theta}{\theta^{2}}\end{pmatrix}.

(150)

A similar relation holds if we consider the generators from
Eq.

9
, namely

\tilde{H}_{j}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})=iU^{\dagger}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})\partial_{j}U({\boldsymbol{\theta}})=\int_{0}^{1}\mathrm{d}s\ \mathcal{O}_{\bf 3}(s\theta_{1},s\theta_{2},s\theta_{3})\boldsymbol{J}:=\tilde{\mathcal{O}}_{\bf 3}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})\,\boldsymbol{J},

(151)

and the matrix
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}_{\bf 3}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})
can be calculated explicitly:

\tilde{\mathcal{O}}_{\bf 3}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{\theta_{1}^{2}+(\theta_{2}^{2}+\theta_{3}^{2})\sin\theta/\theta}{\theta^{2}}&\frac{\theta_{1}\theta_{2}(1-\sin\theta/\theta)-\theta_{3}(1-\cos\theta)}{\theta^{2}}&\frac{\theta_{1}\theta_{3}(1-\sin\theta/\theta)+\theta_{2}(1-\cos\theta)}{\theta^{2}}\\
\frac{\theta_{1}\theta_{2}(1-\sin\theta/\theta)+\theta_{3}(1-\cos\theta)}{\theta^{2}}&\frac{\theta_{2}^{2}+(\theta_{1}^{2}+\theta_{3}^{2})\sin\theta/\theta}{\theta^{2}}&\frac{\theta_{2}\theta_{3}(1-\sin\theta/\theta)-\theta_{1}(1-\cos\theta)}{\theta^{2}}\\
\frac{\theta_{1}\theta_{3}(1-\sin\theta/\theta)-\theta_{2}(1-\cos\theta)}{\theta^{2}}&\frac{\theta_{2}\theta_{3}(1-\sin\theta/\theta)+\theta_{1}(1-\cos\theta)}{\theta^{2}}&\frac{\theta_{3}^{2}+(\theta_{1}^{2}+\theta_{2}^{2})\sin\theta/\theta}{\theta^{2}}\end{pmatrix}.

(152)

Therefore, in a given parametrization
U(\boldsymbol{\theta})
, the QFIM of generic
SU(2)
unitaries of a pure state can be written as

\mathcal{F}^{Q}[\Psi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}]=4\Gamma_{\Psi_{0}}[\boldsymbol{\tilde{H}}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})]=4\tilde{\mathcal{O}}_{\bf 3}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})\,\Gamma_{\Psi_{0}}[\boldsymbol{J}]\,\tilde{\mathcal{O}}_{\bf 3}^{T}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}).

(153)

In turn, this gives an asymptotic precision bound for the local estimation of
U(\boldsymbol{\theta})
around the point given by
\boldsymbol{\theta}
(and again in the given parametrization).

In particular, a common parametrization that is alternative to
U(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\exp[-i(\theta_{x}J_{x}+\theta_{y}J_{y}+\theta_{z}J_{z})]
is given by Euler angles, namely:

U(\Phi,\Theta,\Psi)=\exp(-i\Phi J_z)\exp(-i\Theta J_y)\exp(-i\Psi J_z),

(154)

where
\boldsymbol{\eta}:=(\Phi,\Theta,\Psi)
are three independent parameters just as
\boldsymbol{\theta}=(\theta_{x},\theta_{y},\theta_{z})
.
The transformation from the
\boldsymbol{\theta}
representation to
\boldsymbol{\eta}
reads

\begin{cases}\Phi=\arctan(\frac{\frac{\theta_z}\theta\tan\frac\theta 2-\frac{\theta_x}{\theta_y}}{1+\frac{\theta_x}{\theta_y}\frac{\theta_z}\theta\tan\frac\theta 2})\\
\Theta=2\arcsin(\frac{\sqrt{\theta_x^2+\theta_y^2}}{\theta}\sin\frac\theta 2)\\
\Psi=\arctan(\frac{\frac{\theta_z}\theta\tan\frac\theta 2+\frac{\theta_x}{\theta_y}}{1-\frac{\theta_x}{\theta_y}\frac{\theta_z}\theta\tan\frac\theta 2})\end{cases}.

(155)

A change of parametrization then impacts the QCRB, and in particular the QFIM is transformed via the Jacobian matrix:

\mathcal{F}(\Phi,\Theta,\Psi)=\mathcal{J}^{T}\,\mathcal{F}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\,\mathcal{J},

(156)

where the Jacobian matrix of the transformation
\boldsymbol{\eta}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
is defined as

\mathcal{J}_{k\alpha}=\frac{\partial\theta_{k}}{\partial\lambda_{\alpha}},\ \ k\in\{x,y,z\},\ \ \alpha\in\{\Phi,\Theta,\Psi\}.

(157)

Besides considering different parametrization, one can also find a parametrization-invariant QCRB
[
57
]
. Following Ref.
[
57
]
one can consider the Cartan metric:

\boldsymbol{g}=\tilde{\mathcal{O}}_{\bf 3}\,\tilde{\mathcal{O}}_{\bf 3}^{T}=\frac{\theta^{2}+2\cos\theta-2}{\theta^{4}}\boldsymbol{\theta}\cdot\boldsymbol{\theta}^{T}+\frac{2(1-\cos\theta)}{\theta^{2}}\mathbbm{1}_{3},

(158)

where
\mathbbm{1}_{3}
is the
3\times 3
identity matrix. Then, considering
g
as the weight matrix on the multiparameter QCRB, one gets

\tr[\boldsymbol{g}\,{\mathcal{C}ov}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}})]\geq\frac{1}{4}\tr[\Gamma_{\psi}^{-1}[\boldsymbol{J}]],

(159)

which is valid when the probe state is pure and is independent on the parametrization.
Applied to our investigation, we find that for the states in
Eq.

39
, the r.h.s is given by

\frac{1}{4}\tr[\Gamma_{\Psi_{k}}^{-1}[\boldsymbol{J}]]=\frac{2}{2k(n-k)+n}+\frac{1}{(n-2k)^{2}}.

(160)

C.2
Estimation via moments of the generators

Let us now consider a vector of observables constructed by vectorizing the tensor

A_{i_{1},\dots,i_{K}}=J_{i_{1}}J_{i_{2}}\dots J_{i_{K}},

(161)

where
K
is the maximal degree of the monomial of spin observables and we consider all the different such monomials for each given order
K
. Again, working in the Heisenberg picture the moment matrix of such a vector can be written as

\mathcal{M}_{\Psi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}[\boldsymbol{A}]=\mathcal{O}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})\,\mathcal{M}_{\Psi_{\boldsymbol{0}}}[\boldsymbol{A}]\,\mathcal{O}^{T}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}),

(162)

where
\mathcal{O}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})
is an orthogonal matrix that can be constructed iteratively in the order
K
of the monomial terms.

For first order moments, i.e., the vector
\boldsymbol{J}
, we already know that the transformation is given by
\mathcal{O}_{\bf 3}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})
. We now illustrate how this construction works for the case
K=2
, i.e., monomials of second order. For the vector of second order monomials, which we call
\boldsymbol{Q}=(J_{1}^{2},J_{2}^{2},J_{3}^{2},\{J_{2},J_{3}\},\{J_{1},J_{3}\},\{J_{1},J_{2}\})
, we have that the orthogonal transformation matrix is given by

\mathcal{O}_{\bf 6}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})=\begin{pmatrix}o_{11}^{2}&o_{12}^{2}&o_{13}^{2}&2o_{12}o_{13}&2o_{11}o_{13}&2o_{11}o_{12}\\
o_{21}^{2}&o_{22}^{2}&o_{23}^{2}&2o_{22}o_{23}&2o_{21}o_{23}&2o_{21}o_{22}\\
o_{31}^{2}&o_{32}^{2}&o_{33}^{2}&2o_{32}o_{33}&2o_{31}o_{33}&2o_{31}o_{32}\\
o_{21}o_{31}&o_{22}o_{32}&o_{23}o_{33}&o_{22}o_{33}+o_{32}o_{23}&o_{21}o_{33}+o_{31}o_{23}&o_{21}o_{32}+o_{31}o_{22}\\
o_{31}o_{11}&o_{32}o_{12}&o_{33}o_{13}&o_{12}o_{33}+o_{32}o_{13}&o_{11}o_{33}+o_{31}o_{13}&o_{11}o_{32}+o_{31}o_{12}\\
o_{11}o_{21}&o_{12}o_{22}&o_{13}o_{23}&o_{12}o_{23}+o_{22}o_{13}&o_{11}o_{23}+o_{21}o_{13}&o_{11}o_{22}+o_{21}o_{12}\end{pmatrix},

(163)

where
o_{ij}:=o_{ij}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})
are the entries of
\mathcal{O}_{\mathbf{3}}
. Thus, in total, the vector
\boldsymbol{A}=(\boldsymbol{J},\boldsymbol{Q})
transforms with the matrix
\mathcal{O}_{\bf 3}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})\oplus\mathcal{O}_{\bf 6}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})
. For higher order moments of the generators, the transformation matrix can be calculated iteratively with similar methods.

C.2.1
Reconstructions of moments via spin-
j
Husimi measurement

In order to reconstruct the moments of the generators, one can employ a spin-coherent-state measurement, also known as the Husimi-
Q
measurement, which is described by a continuous POVM on the sphere. The POVM elements are given by

E(\Omega)=\frac{2j+1}{4\pi}|\Omega\rangle\langle\Omega|,\qquad\Omega=(\vartheta,\varphi)\in S^{2},\qquad\int_{S^{2}}E(\Omega)\,\mathrm{d}\Omega=\mathbbm{1},

(164)

where
\mathrm{d}\Omega=\sin\vartheta\,\mathrm{d}\vartheta\,\mathrm{d}\varphi
and
\ket{\Omega}
denotes the spin-
j
coherent state pointing in the direction specified by
\Omega
. The associated unit vector is
\mathbf{n}(\Omega)=(\sin\vartheta\cos\varphi,\sin\vartheta\sin\varphi,\cos\vartheta)
. For a quantum state
\varrho_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
encoding the parameters to be estimated, the outcome distribution is the normalized Husimi function

{\rm p}(\Omega|\boldsymbol{\theta})=\mathrm{tr}[\varrho_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}E(\Omega)]=\frac{2j+1}{4\pi}\,\langle\Omega|\varrho_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}|\Omega\rangle.

(165)

Choosing suitable functions
\boldsymbol{f}(\Omega)
(e.g., components of the unit vector
\mathbf{n}(\Omega)
or other spherical harmonics / symbols), one obtains
\boldsymbol{\mu}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\langle{\boldsymbol{M}(\boldsymbol{\theta})}\rangle_{\varrho}
for the chosen set of observables
\boldsymbol{M}
directly from the Husimi statistics and can apply the method of moments as described in
section

A.2
. In this sense, the Husimi POVM plays for spins the same conceptual role as heterodyne detection for continuous variables: it provides a single-shot, joint (non-projective) measurement whose covariances determine the asymptotic MoM precision.

To connect the continuous measurement outcomes directly to the method-of-moments employed throughout this work, we use the contravariant symbols dual to the Husimi-
Q
POVM
[
83
]
. For any operator
A
, the contravariant symbol
A^{\vee}(\Omega)
is defined such that

\langle A\rangle_{\varrho}=\int_{S^{2}}\mathrm{d}\Omega\,A^{\vee}(\Omega)\,\tr[\varrho E(\Omega)].

(166)

Given
\nu
independent samples
\{\Omega_{\ell}\}_{\ell=1}^{\nu}
from the Husimi distribution, the sample mean
\bar{A}_{\nu}=\nu^{-1}\sum_{\ell=1}^{\nu}A^{\vee}(\Omega_{\ell})
provides an unbiased reconstruction of
\langle A\rangle_{\varrho_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}
.

For the first- and second-order moments relevant to our analysis, the contravariant symbols take explicit forms
[
83
]
:

\displaystyle(J_{i})^{\vee}(\Omega)

\displaystyle=(j+1)\,n_{i}(\Omega),

(167)

\displaystyle(\{J_{i},J_{k}\})^{\vee}(\Omega)

\displaystyle=(j+1)(2j+3)\,n_{i}(\Omega)n_{k}(\Omega)-(j+1)\,\delta_{ik}.

(168)

These relations allow us to map the measurement data onto the moment vector
\boldsymbol{A}
used in the main text. Define the processed outcomes as
M_{i}(\Omega_{\ell}):=(M_{i})^{\vee}(\Omega_{\ell})
. The sample means
\mathbb{E}_{\theta}\left[M_{i}\right]
constructed from
Eqs.

167
and
168
yield unbiased estimates of
\langle\boldsymbol{M}\rangle_{\varrho_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}
, while the sample covariance matrix can be used to find the MSE.
Note that this measurement was also used in the context of estimation of three-parameter rotations in
[
55
]
, where the classical Fisher information of this POVM was investigated.
