The mediating role of health-related behaviors between social capital and primary healthcare physicians’ health-related quality of life
Authors: Zonghao Wu, Zhuoqi Gu, Yukun Wei, Yuankai Huang, Ennan Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study aimed to evaluate the correlation between social capital and physicians’ health-related quality of life(HRQoL) in primary healthcare institutions. Simultaneously, we examined the mediating role of health-related behaviors in the process. Data was obtained from a nationwide cross-sectional survey conducted in 2021. Demographic characteristics and health-related behaviors were collected by a self-administered questionnaire. We used the social capital of healthcare organizations reported by employees (SOCAPO-E) scale to measure the social capital and the EuroQol-five dimension-five level (EQ-5D-5L) to measure physicians’ HRQoL. The Tobit model was performed to evaluate the association between social capital and HRQoL. Spearman’s test was applied to investigate the relationship between social capital, health-related behaviors and HRQoL. A bootstrap approach was used to assess the mediating effect of health-related behaviors. The regression results showed that social capital (β = 0.605, p<0.001) was significantly and positively associated with physicians’ HRQoL. Spearman’s correlation analysis showed that social capital was positively correlated with health-related behaviors (r = 0.211, p < 0.01), health-related behaviors were positively correlated with HRQoL (r = 0.134, p < 0.01), and social capital was positively correlated with HRQoL (r = 0.253, p < 0.01). Health-related behaviors moderately mediated the relationship between social capital and HRQoL (BootSE = 0.006, BootCI=[0.005,0.030]). Social capital not only directly affected physicians’ HRQoL, but also indirectly affected HRQoL through health-related behaviors. Our findings suggested that future intervention measures could complement the strengthening of social capital with health behavior promotion programs to achieve more comprehensive health improvements.Keywords: Social capital, Health-related quality of life, Health-related behavior, Primary healthcare, Physician, Occupational healthSubject terms: Health policy, Health services, Public health, Quality of life, Occupational health

Introduction

In China, primary healthcare (PHC) is designed to provide universally accessible basic healthcare to individuals and families in the community. With the enhanced accessibility of PHC institutions, PHC assumes a pivotal role in fostering regional health equity1. Primary healthcare physicians assume primary responsibility for patients’ health, entrusted with the pivotal task of safeguarding public health. However, they faced mounting pressure stemming from the aging population and the burden of non-communicable diseases(NCDs)2. Moreover, the health status of the physician cohort presents a disquieting picture, beset by both physical and mental health problems including illness, sleep disturbances, anxiety, and depression3,4. These afflictions not only compromise the efficacy of healthcare professionals but also precipitate a diminution in overall productivity5. PHC physicians serve as the mainstay of the PHC workforce, and their physical and psychological health status directly influence the quality and safety of PHC services6. Therefore, enhancing the health status of PHC physicians is crucial for elevating the quality of PHC services.

Patient-reported outcome(PRO) is reported directly by the patient, without the interpretation of the patient’s responses by a physician or anyone else7. PRO offers a comprehensive assessment of a patient’s health status by employing standardized measures to evaluate any aspect of a patient’s health status8. As an important branch of patient-reported outcomes, health-related quality of life (HRQoL) describes a person’s perceived health in the domains of physical, mental, and social health9. In summary, evaluating the HRQoL of PHC physicians contributes to obtaining a comprehensive understanding of their actual health status.

The concept of social capital originates from sociological research10. Putnam defines social capital as the networks, norms and social trust within social organizations, characteristics that facilitate coordination and cooperation for the mutual benefit of the organization11. In public health, social capital is acknowledged for its positive influence on health outcomes12. Kawachi elucidated the correlation between social capital and health outcomes through a comprehensive synthesis of extant literature13. It is notable that he hypothesized health-related behavior may act as a mediating variable between social capital and health outcomes14. In 2010, Hyyppä formally proposed the classical hypothesis, suggesting that health behaviors act as a mediator between social capital and individual health outcomes15. The significance of social capital within health research is underscored by accumulating evidence linking it to a wide range of health behaviors and outcomes in various populations and contexts in last 20 years16. Additionally, some demographic variables have been shown to correlate with health outcomes, such as gender, age and income17–19. It was necessary to control for potential bias that these variables could introduce to the results. In summary, social capital and health-related behavior may be the potential pathways to promote physician health. Nevertheless, there remains a dearth of studies conducted among groups of PHC physicians. This leaves uncertainty about the applicability of the health-promoting effects of social capital to the physician population.

The objective of this study was to evaluate the correlation between social capital and HRQoL among PHC physicians. Simultaneously, we examined the mediating role of health-related behaviors in the process. Our study was the attempt to introduce relevant theories from the field of social capital into the PHC system, providing new ideas for enhancing the occupational health of the PHC physicians.

Literature review and hypothesis development

Social capital and HRQoL

Social capital, recognized as a significant social determinant of health, has consistently demonstrated a favorable influence on enhancing overall quality of life20. There is evidence that social capital exerts a protective influence on an individual’s HRQoL. The promotion of social capital emerges as a potentially viable strategy for enhancing HRQoL21. In multiple studies involving older populations, a consistent finding has emerged: even after adjusting for pertinent confounding variables, a strong association persists between increased levels of social capital and enhanced HRQoL22–24. Furthermore, studies focusing on adolescent populations have revealed the enduring influence of social capital on children’s oral HRQoL25. High levels of social capital are expected to exert a positive influence on children’s oral HRQoL. To summarize, extant literature provides ample evidence supporting a positive correlation between social capital and HRQoL. However, there is a scarcity of studies concerning groups of physicians in PHC. Thus, drawing from the evidence delineated in the literature, we posit novel hypotheses:

H1: Higher social capital in PHC institutions is associated with higher health-related quality of life for physicians.

Health-related behaviors as mediator

Social capital has been shown to be associated with a wide range of health-related behaviors, such as smoking, drinking, diet and physical activity26–29. Social capital demonstrates a constraining effect on health risk behaviors30. In addition, it is widely recognized that health-related behaviors play a crucial role in influencing HRQoL31. Individuals who adopt healthy behaviors tend to exhibit higher levels of HRQoL compared to those who do not32. Similarly, it has been found that better health-related behaviors among Chinese PHC physicians tend to be associated with higher HRQoL.

There have been several studies that have focused on the mediating role of health-related behaviors, but with mixed results. An examination employing nationally representative data from China revealed that health-related behaviors played a moderate mediating role in the correlation between social capital and health outcomes33. Mohnen’s study into various health-related behaviors revealed that only physical activity was the mediating variable between social capital and self-rated health outcomes34. Poortinga’s study found no mediation effect of health behaviors between social capital and health35. The mediating function of health-related behaviors has been corroborated not only in investigations pertaining to self-assessed health, but also in quality of life(QoL) studies36. Nevertheless, a research lacuna persists, with only one study documenting the mediating effect of health-related behaviors in the relationship between social capital and HRQoL37.

Building upon the findings of the aforementioned literature review, our study endeavors to corroborate the mediating influence of health-related behaviors within a cohort of physicians at a PHC institutions in China. The following hypotheses will be tested:

H2: Health-related behavior serves as a mediator between social capital and physicians’ health-related quality of life.

Methods

Data source and participants

The data for this study were obtained from a cross-sectional investigation conducted across several cities in 2021. The PHC institutions in this study mainly include township (street) health centers, community health service centers (stations), village health offices, infirmaries and clinics. This study used a multi-stage sampling strategy: (1) 31 provinces and administrative regions in mainland China were divided into three groups according to their GDP per capita in 2018. (2) At least 2 PHC institutions were selected using convenient sampling in each group based on approval of administrators. (3) At least 2 physicians were selected based on convenience and voluntariness. Respondents meeting the following criteria were included in the analysis: (1) full-time physicians working in a PHC institutions, aged 18 years or older; (2) willing to participate in the survey and provide informed consent. Based on the above criteria, we received a total of 1227 questionnaires. After excluding invalid samples with missing data, 1211 valid questionnaires with complete responses were included in this study.

Measurements

Social capital

This study used the social capital of healthcare organizations reported by employees(SOCAPO-E) instrument to assess social capital within primary healthcare organizations38. The SOCAPO-E is a succinct, validated, and theory-based measurement tool tailored for assessing social capital within healthcare organizations. The scale includes all core elements of community outlined by Bauman, including mutual understanding, warm circles, trust, “we-feeling”, mutual help and shared values39. Participants rated a total of 6 items on a 4-point scale, ranging from 1 (“I strongly disagree”) to 4 (“I strongly agree”). Higher scores indicate greater levels of social capital.

Health-related behaviors

We used the total number of low-risk health-related behaviors to measure the overall health-related behavior score, with higher scores indicating healthier behaviors40. The following health-related behaviors and low-risk criteria included in our study were based on previous studies and Dietary Guidelines for Chinese Residents41–43: non-smoking (within the past 6 months), non-drinking (within the past 6 months), adherence to a regular diet, and weekly physical activity (at least 30 min of exercise per week). All questions were responded to with either YES or NO. 1 point for a YES answer to each behavior and 0 points for NO. The total personal health-related behavior score is the sum of the scores for these four behaviors, ranging from 0 to 4.

Health-related quality of life

Our study used health utility scores measured by the Chinese version of the EuroQol-five dimension-five level(EQ-5D-5L) to represent physicians’ HRQoL44. The EQ-5D-5L is a widely used universal measurement tool that consists of two parts: a health descriptive system and a visual analogue scale (VAS). The descriptive system comprises five dimensions: Mobility (MO), Self-Care (SC), Usual Activities (UA), Pain/Discomfort (PD) and Anxiety/Depression(AD). Each dimension has 5 levels. The respondents’ answers will be transformed into utility scores using the Chinese value set45.

Sociodemographic

Based on previous research related to social capital, the following socio-demographic variables were collected in this study: age, sex, marital status, number of children, residence, annual income, education background, professional title, employment forms, and chronic diseases46. The above socio-demographic variables will be incorporated into the study as covariates.

Questionnaire test

All questionnaires were written in Chinese and verified by experts and researchers. The validity, reliability, comprehensibility, and readability of the questionnaire had been verified by pilot surveys conducted in PHC institutions in Nanjing, Jiangsu Province. Participants in the pre-study met the general inclusion criteria for the study. Revisions were made at the end of pilot surveys based on feedback.

Data analysis

Data analyses were conducted using Stata 15.0 and SPSS PROCESS Macro version 4.3. All effects were considered statistically significant at p < 0.01.

Testing of Hypothesis H1

Descriptive statistics were employed to summarize the demographic characteristics and scale scores. The EQ-5D utility index was skewed and it was censored at 1, so we chose the Tobit regression to explore the potential effects of social capital on HRQoL47. Two models were constructed: Model 1 assessed the impact of health-related behaviors and demographic variables on HRQoL. Model 2 incorporated the independent variable to assess its impact on the model outcomes. The variance inflation factor (VIF) was employed to assess multicollinearity within the model.

Testing of Hypothesis H2

Spearman’s correlation analysis was employed to measure bivariate correlations between social capital, health-related behaviors and HRQoL. Following Preacher and Hayes’ recommendation, the bootstrap approach demonstrates superior performance in terms of statistical power and controlling Type I error rates compared to the causal steps approach and the Sobel test48. The bootstrap method empirically approximates the sampling distribution through data resampling, which provides accurate confidence intervals for the indirect effect49. The mediation analysis in our study was conducted using the PROCESS macro with 5,000 bootstrap resamples. Because the PROCESS macro does not automatically standardize variables measured in different units, we standardized all continuous variables and converted all categorical variables into dummy variables prior to the mediation analysis. The macro then constructed two regression models (Model 3 and Model 4) to test the mediation paths and generated bootstrap estimates for the direct, indirect, and total effects with 95% confidence intervals (CI). The indirect effect was considered significant if 95% bootstrap confidence intervals did not include zero. Demographic variables were also included in the model as control variables. 5,000 bootstrapping samples were generated from the original dataset via random sampling.

These results indicated that Hypothesis 1 (H1) is supported.

Discussion

Our study examined the positive correlation between social capital and physicians’ HRQoL, while validating the mediating role of health-related behaviors in the process. Our findings demonstrated that the theoretical framework linking social capital to health outcomes held true among the population of PHC physicians. These results not only broadened the scope of research on social capital theory but also provided hospital administrators with a new approach to improving the health status of physicians. The characteristics of the participants in this study closely mirrored that of the authoritative survey published in the same year, indicating that our sample is representative50. As the attempt at social capital in PHC physicians’ HRQoL, our study provided valuable evidence for improving physicians’ occupational health.

H1: positive association between social capital and physicians’ HRQoL

The regression results demonstrated that social capital was significantly and positively associated with physicians’ HRQoL. Social capital in PHC institutions describes the network of relationships built up through interactions between employees of the organization51. This network of relationships is recognized as a key feature of an effective and sustainable healthcare team, which is important for employees’ general work engagement and job satisfaction in healthcare workplace52. Greater work engagement is associated with a set of positive outcomes, including lower levels of depression, anxiety, and burnout, alongside better perceived physical health and more frequent positive emotions53. Health-related quality of life comprehensively reflects performance across these dimensions. Our results provided evidence that social capital improved employee well-being at the health-related quality of life level. The results could be useful to implement in education programs for professionals in hospital settings to translate the theoretical mechanisms of social capital into practical initiatives, thereby creating a resource for sustainable organizational development.

Beyond the primary variables, our study revealed that chronic disease emerged as the most significant risk factor impacting physicians’ HRQoL. In our sample, 71.7% of primary care physicians were diagnosed with chronic diseases. Therefore, it is critically important to find opportunities to carry out health promotion and chronic disease prevention activities in the workplace for PHC physicians54. In response to the challenge of chronic disease, studies have suggested that adopting healthier lifestyle choices can serve as a proactive measure against the rising prevalence of chronic conditions55. Notably, the fact that social capital remained a significant and substantial variable even after accounting for such a powerful confounder greatly strengthens the validity and robustness of our main finding.

H2: the mediating role of health-related behaviors

Social capital played a crucial role in individual health-related behaviors. Social capital provided the structural resources to supportive relationships, health-related knowledge, and information, fostering positive health behaviors in the community. The richer the structural resources of social capital, the greater the prevalence of positive health behaviors among individuals56. These positive health-related behaviors contributed to better individual health quality. In summary, alongside the direct impact of social capital on health, health-related behaviors played a key mediating role. Our study validated the role of health-related behaviors as a mediator between social capital and physicians’ HRQoL. These findings offered a new perspective for enhancing physicians’ HRQoL.

However, it should be noted that our results differed from prior research regarding the proportion of the total effect that was accounted for by the mediating pathway. Our evidence indicated that the direct effect played a dominant role throughout the entire process(92.3%). The relational networks described by social capital created a positive work environment for physicians in primary healthcare institutions. These characteristics (such as We-feeling and Trust) may have a direct impact on a physicians’ mental health. In our study, PHC physicians reported relatively more problems in the Anxiety/Depression(AD) and Pain/Discomfort(PD) dimensions. Anxiety and depression were the most reported mental health disorders among physicians in previous studies. Furthermore, the Anxiety/Depression (AD) dimension, one of the five dimensions of the EQ-5D, effectively captures the outcomes of this direct effect. Therefore, we believed that the relatively significant proportion of direct effects may be attributed to this influence mode, which aligned with the results reported by Hassanzadeh57. Our results were consistent with Yang’s findings but divergent from those of Poortinga. The variance in results could potentially be attributed to the specific health-related behaviors included in the respective studies. Poortinga’s study examined three behaviors—smoking, alcohol intake, and fruit/vegetable consumption, while overlooking the significant factor of physical activity. Our study employed a comprehensive total score to characterize physicians’ overall health-related behaviors. Unlike previous studies that focused on the single specific behavior, our findings reflect the mediating effects of overall health-related behaviors.

It is noteworthy that the mediating effect accounted for 7.7% of the total effect in this study. Although the indirect effect of health-related behaviors accounted for a relatively small proportion, it did not mean that it was unimportant in the theory of social capital. The pathway of health-related behaviors was one of the most studied branches in the multi-pathway model of how social capital influenced health. PHC physicians in our study worked long hours and were largely desk-based. The health-related behaviors of physicians may be limited due to their busy work schedules58. However, health-related behaviors remained a non-negligible mediating factor. Changing the busy work schedules of PHC physicians could have been a potential breakthrough for enhancing this pathway in the future. In similar studies, the magnitude of the mediating effect varied from 6.10% to 24.82% in Xue’s study. Several potential explanations exist for the disparities between our findings and those reported in previous studies. Firstly, it is important to note that the population under study differs. Xue’s study involved the general population and our study specifically focused on PHC physicians. There exists discernible variations in health-related behaviors and health status between PHC physicians and the general population; Secondly, variations in the manifestation of health status may also contribute to the disparities observed. Our study focused on physicians’ HRQoL, which differs somewhat from self-rated health in previous studies. Self-rated health is commonly assessed using a five-point scale, where individuals evaluate their own health status based on subjective perceptions59. However, the majority of these measurements fail to incorporate an assessment of the patient’s mental health. Compared to self-rated health outcomes, HRQoL provides a more comprehensive assessment of health performance, including both physical and mental health components of an individual. Results can also be converted into utility scores, facilitating comparisons across multiple studies60. Finally, we speculated that the share of direct effects may also be related to other potentially unknown mediators. Previous research has reported that depression served as a mediator between social capital and HRQoL among older adults in the context of the COVID-19 pandemic61. We hypothesize that there may exist multiple mediators in the pathway through which social capital influences physicians’ HRQoL, beyond the health-related behaviors delineated in this study. Future studies may need to further investigate potential mediating variables to yield more reliable conclusions.

Limitations and future research

There are still several limitations in our study. First, as a cross-sectional study, we can only establish associations between social capital and HRQoL. However, causality cannot be determined62. Second, the data for this study were derived from questionnaire survey responses. Factors such as the length of the questionnaire, recall period, and respondents’ comprehension ability may introduce some degree of inaccuracy into the results. Lastly, health-related behaviors were limited to the four commonly observed behaviors within our study. There is a clear need for further exploration into additional potential health-related behaviors.

Conclusion

This study found that social capital not only directly affected physicians’ HRQoL, but also indirectly affected HRQoL through health-related behaviors. Our findings suggested that future intervention measures could complement the strengthening of social capital with health behavior promotion programs to achieve more comprehensive health improvements. Overall, our study had a significant impact on enhancing the occupational health of PHC physicians.
