Exponential Advantage from One More Replica in Estimating Nonlinear Properties of Quantum States
Qi Ye, Zhenhuan Liu, Dong-Ling Deng

Abstract
Inferring nonlinear features of quantum states is fundamentally important across quantum information science, but remains challenging due to the intrinsic linearity of quantum mechanics. It is widely recognized that quantum memory and coherent operations help avoid exponential sample complexity, by mapping nonlinear properties onto linear observables over multiple copies of the target state. In this work, we prove that such a conversion is not only sufficient but also necessary. Specifically, we prove that the estimation of tr ⁡ ( ρ k ​ O ) \mathrm{tr}(\rho^{k}O) for a broad class of observables O O is exponentially hard for any protocol restricted to ( k − 1 ) (k-1) -replica joint measurements, whereas access to one additional replica reduces the complexity to a constant. These results establish, for the first time, an exponential separation between ( k − 1 ) (k-1) - and k k -replica protocols for any integer k > 2 k>2 , thereby introducing a fine-grained hierarchy of replica-based quantum advantage and resolving an open question in the literature. The technical core is a general indistinguishability principle showing that any ensemble constructed from large Haar random states via tensor products and mixtures is hard to distinguish from its average. Leveraging this principle, we further prove that k k -replica joint measurements are also necessary for distinguishing rank- k k density matrices from rank- ( k − 1 ) (k-1) ones. Overall, our work delineates sharp boundaries on the power of joint measurements, highlighting resource–complexity trade-offs in quantum learning theory and deepening the understanding of quantum mechanics’ intrinsic linearity.

Our results

A general indistinguishability principle.

A common approach to proving lower bounds in quantum learning theory is to reduce the learning task to a distinguishing problem between two ensembles of quantum states. In a nutshell, if two ensembles are hard to distinguish, then any property that differs significantly between them must also be hard to learn, otherwise one could distinguish the ensembles by learning that property.
A fruitful example is pseudorandom states
[
43
]
, which are easy to generate yet indistinguishable from Haar-random states by any polynomial-time algorithm under standard computational assumptions. Building on this, prior works have shown that it is computationally hard to test whether a state has imaginary amplitudes
[
33
]
, negative amplitudes
[
28
,
42
]
, high entanglement
[
2
]
, or high magic
[
30
]
.
Another example, more closely related to our setting, is to test purity using restricted measurements.
In
[
15
,
17
]
, the authors show the hardness of distinguishing the Haar-random ensemble
\mathcal{E}=\{\psi\leftarrow\mu_{\text{H}}(d)\}
from the maximally mixed state
\rho_{0}=\mathds{1}/d
with 1-replica protocols, thereby implying the hardness of purity estimation.
These examples illustrate a general paradigm that new indistinguishability results often translate directly into new lower bounds for quantum learning tasks.

In this work, we establish an indistinguishability principle that substantially generalizes the purity testing results in
[
15
,
17
]
. We prove that any ensemble constructed from large Haar-random states via tensor products and mixtures is indistinguishable from its average without joint measurements.

Depending on the choice of

1.2
Overview of techniques

Here, we provide a high-level discussion of techniques.

Lower bounds for estimating nonlinear properties.

A standard approach to proving lower bounds is to reduce estimation to a distinguishing problem between two carefully designed ensembles
\mathcal{E}_{1}
and
\mathcal{E}_{2}
.
The construction must satisfy two conditions that

(a)

no
k
-replica protocol can efficiently distinguish them,

(b)

we can efficiently distinguish them given the ability to estimate
\mathrm{tr}(\rho^{k+1}O)
.

These two conditions together imply the hardness of estimating
\mathrm{tr}(\rho^{k+1}O)
under
k
-replica protocols.
The first condition implies that
\rho^{\otimes i}
has the same distribution under both ensembles for every
i\leq k
, otherwise one could distinguish them by estimating some linear observable on
\rho^{\otimes i}
. The second condition demands that
\mathrm{tr}(\rho^{k+1}O)
differ significantly between the two ensembles.

To construct such ensembles, we start with two distributions
\bm{p}=(p_{1},\dots,p_{m})
and
\bm{q}=(q_{1},\dots,q_{m})
that match on all moments up to degree
k
but differ as much as possible on the
(k+1)
-th moment, that is,
\sum_{r=1}^{m}p_{r}^{i}=\sum_{r=1}^{m}q_{r}^{i}
for
i\leq k
but
\absolutevalue{\sum_{r=1}^mp_r^{k+1}-\sum_{r=1}^m q_r^{k+1}}
is as large as possible.
Using Chebyshev polynomials, we achieve a gap of
2(2(k+1))^{-k}
in the
(k+1)
-th moment (
Lemma

36
). We believe this to be optimal, and in fact it is the only obstacle to extending the lower bound beyond
\varepsilon=\mathcal{O}((2(k+1))^{-k})
.
Given
\bm{p}
and
\bm{q}
, we define

\mathcal{E}_{1}\coloneqq\left\{\sum_{r=1}^{m}p_{r}\psi_{r}\right\}_{\psi_{1},\cdots,\psi_{m}\leftarrow\mu_{\text{H}}(d)},\quad\mathcal{E}_{2}\coloneqq\left\{\sum_{r=1}^{m}q_{r}\psi_{r}\right\}_{\psi_{1},\cdots,\psi_{m}\leftarrow\mu_{\text{H}}(d)},

(2)

where
\psi_{1},\dots,\psi_{m}
are independent Haar-random pure states.
To prove condition (a), observe that distinguishing
\mathcal{E}_{1}
and
\mathcal{E}_{2}
with a
k
-replica protocol is equivalent to distinguishing
\mathcal{E}_{1}^{(k)}\coloneqq\{\rho^{\otimes k}\}_{\rho\leftarrow\mathcal{E}_{1}}
and
\mathcal{E}_{2}^{(k)}\coloneqq\{\rho^{\otimes k}\}_{\rho\leftarrow\mathcal{E}_{2}}
using
1
-replica protocols on
k
qudits.
A key observation is that
\mathcal{E}_{1}^{(k)}
and
\mathcal{E}_{2}^{(k)}
are both Haar-assembled ensembles satisfying Definition
1
.
Furthermore,
\mathcal{E}_{1}^{(k)}
and
\mathcal{E}_{2}^{(k)}
have the same average.
Indeed,
\mathop{\mathbb{E}\/}_{\rho\sim\mathcal{E}_{1}}[\rho^{\otimes k}]
is symmetric in the entries of
\bm{p}
, and hence can be expressed solely in terms of the moments of
\bm{p}
up to degree
k
, which equal those of
\bm{q}
by construction.
By our indistinguishability principle for Haar-assembled ensembles, both
\mathcal{E}_{1}^{(k)}
and
\mathcal{E}_{2}^{(k)}
are hard to distinguish from this common average using 1-replica protocols, which is defined on
k
qudits.
The triangle inequality then implies that
\mathcal{E}_{1}^{(k)}
and
\mathcal{E}_{2}^{(k)}
are indistinguishable under 1-replica protocols, establishing condition (a).

Now we explain the condition (b). Direct calculation gives
\mathop{\mathbb{E}\/}_{\rho\leftarrow\mathcal{E}_{1}}[\mathrm{tr}(\rho^{k+1}O)]-\mathop{\mathbb{E}\/}_{\rho\leftarrow\mathcal{E}_{2}}[\mathrm{tr}(\rho^{k+1}O)]\approx 2(2(k+1))^{-k}\absolutevalue{\tr(O)}/d
. Therefore, we can distinguish the two ensembles given estimators of
\mathrm{tr}(\rho^{k+1}O)
within error
(2(k+1))^{-k}\absolutevalue{\tr(O)}/d
. However, this argument fails when
O
is traceless. The reason is that the positive and negative parts of
O
cancel out in the calculation. To circumvent this issue, for a possibly traceless
O
, we select
d/2
eigenvalues of
O
such that the absolute value of their sum is at least
\norm{O}_{1}/4
. Then we define
\mathcal{E}_{1},\mathcal{E}_{2}
inside the
(d/2)
-dimensional subspace spanned by these eigenvalues. In this subspace, the
d/2
eigenvalues will not cancel out completely so the argument above follows.

Indistinguishability principle for Haar-assembled ensembles

We next sketch the proof of the indistinguishability principle. The simplest Haar-assembled ensemble is
\mathcal{E}=\{\psi\}_{\psi\sim\mu_{\text{H}}(d)}
, i.e., a single Haar-random state. In
[
15
]
, it was shown that any 1-replica protocol requires
\sqrt{d}
samples to distinguish
\mathcal{E}
from its mean, namely the maximally mixed state
\rho_{0}
. Although this is already a special case, the proof is highly nontrivial and contains the main ideas of the general argument. So we sketch its proof here.

Suppose a protocol applies 1-replica measurements to
T
copies of
\rho
. These measurements can be combined into a single measurement
\{F_{s}\}_{s}
on
\rho^{\otimes T}
, where each
F_{s}
has product form. For every
F_{s}
, the one-sided likelihood ratio satisfies

\frac{\mathrm{tr}(F_{s}\mathop{\mathbb{E}\/}_{\psi}[\psi^{\otimes T}])}{\mathrm{tr}(F_{s}\rho_{0}^{\otimes T})}=\frac{d^{T}}{d(d+1)\cdots(d+T-1)}\frac{\mathrm{tr}(F_{s}\sum_{\pi\in S_{T}}\pi)}{\mathrm{tr}(F_{s})}\overset{*}{\geq}\frac{d^{T}}{d(d+1)\cdots(d+T-1)}\geq 1-\frac{T^{2}}{d}.

(3)

Here
(*)
is a property of permutation operators proved in
[
15
, Lemma 5.12]
. Thus, when
T=o(\sqrt{d})
, the outcome distributions under
\mathop{\mathbb{E}\/}_{\psi}[\psi^{\otimes T}]
and under
\rho_{0}^{\otimes T}
are statistically close so that no algorithm can distinguish the two cases. This yields the
\Omega(\sqrt{d})
lower bound.

The general indistinguishability principle follows a similar strategy but with two additional complications. First, in our setting the state is a mixture rather than a pure state, so we must expand
\psi^{\otimes T}
and bound the likelihood ratio term by term. Second, each term is now a tensor product of multiple Haar-random states, making the Haar integral more involved. To overcome this, we prove a generalized version of
(*)
in
Lemma

30
.

Lower bounds for spectrum testing.

We now show how to apply the technique to spectrum testing.
For two spectra
\bm{p}
and
\bm{q}
that need to test, we construct
\mathcal{E}_{1}
and
\mathcal{E}_{2}
as in (
2
). They are hard to distinguish for the same reason.
The only problem is that the spectrum of
\sum_{r}p_{r}\psi_{r}
is not exactly
\bm{p}
because Haar random states may not be orthogonal, although with high probability they are approximately orthogonal. We address this with a rounding lemma (
Lemma

41

\bm{p}
. This ensures that replacing the ensembles with their rounded versions introduces only a small error.

Upper bounds for estimating nonlinear properties.

For
Theorem

5

k_{1}=\lceil\frac{k+1}{2}\rceil

Performing a generalized swap test on

The state
\rho_{2}
is defined in the same way with
k_{2}
.
With high probability, we can prepare a sufficient number of copies of
\rho_{1}
and
\rho_{2}
. As a byproduct, we also get estimators for
\mathrm{tr}(\rho^{k_{1}})
and
\mathrm{tr}(\rho^{k_{2}})
by counting the number of successful preparations. Now we apply protocols in
[
6
,
21
]
to estimate
\mathrm{tr}(\rho_{1}\rho_{2}O)
in a distributed way.
Using the identity
\mathrm{tr}(\rho^{k+1}O)=(1+\mathrm{tr}(\rho^{k_{1}}))(1+\mathrm{tr}(\rho^{k_{2}}))\mathrm{tr}(\rho_{1}\rho_{2}O)-\mathrm{tr}(\rho^{2}O)-\mathrm{tr}(\rho^{k_{1}+1}O)-\mathrm{tr}(\rho^{k_{2}+1}O)
, we reduce the task of estimating
\mathrm{tr}(\rho^{k+1}O)
to estimating
\mathrm{tr}(\rho^{2}O)
,
\mathrm{tr}(\rho^{k_{1}+1}O)
, and
\mathrm{tr}(\rho^{k_{2}+1}O)
, all of which involve strictly smaller degrees (except in the base cases
k=1,2,3
, which we handle separately). By applying this reduction one more time, i.e.,
k_{1}+1=\lceil\frac{k_{1}+1}{2}\rceil+\lfloor\frac{k_{1}+1}{2}\rfloor
and
k_{2}+1=\lceil\frac{k_{2}+1}{2}\rceil+\lfloor\frac{k_{2}+1}{2}\rfloor
, we ensure that all degrees are eventually not larger than
k_{1}
, which can be estimated directly using
k_{1}
-replica protocols.
The total sample complexity is
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(\max\{\frac{k\sqrt{d}}{\varepsilon},\frac{k}{\varepsilon^{2}}\}\right)
due to the distributed estimation of
\mathrm{tr}(\rho_{1}\rho_{2}O)
.

1.3
Future directions

Error dependence.

Our lower bound for estimating
\mathrm{tr}(\rho^{k+1}O)
exhibits a poor error dependence of
\varepsilon^{-1/(k+1)}
. Can this be improved? We note that the optimal dependence is unclear even for purity estimation (i.e.,
\mathrm{tr}(\rho^{2})
). In fact, it is only known that with 1-replica protocols the sample complexity is
\mathcal{O}(\max\{\tfrac{\sqrt{d}}{\varepsilon},\tfrac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\})
and
\Omega(\max\{\tfrac{\sqrt{d}}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}},\tfrac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\})

[
29
]
.

Error regime.

Theorem

3
shows that estimating
\mathrm{tr}(\rho^{k+1})
is hard when
\varepsilon=\mathcal{O}((2(k+1))^{-k})
, while
[
62
]
shows that it becomes easy when
\varepsilon>2(k+1)e^{-k}
.
A nontrivial gap therefore remains. Pinpointing the precise threshold of this phase transition is an interesting open problem.

More applications in spectrum testing.

Our application to spectrum testing (
Theorem

40
) is meaningful only in the regime
m<\sqrt{d}
. The main obstacle is that when
m
is large, the spectrum of
\sum_{r}p_{r}\psi_{r}
can deviate significantly from
(p_{1},\dots,p_{d})
. A possible workaround is to consider ensembles of the form
\{U\operatorname{diag}(p_{1},\dots,p_{d})U^{\dagger}\}
for Haar-random unitaries
U
. However, analyzing such ensembles requires heavy use of representation theory and Weingarten calculus. Can the indistinguishability principle be established for this more natural class of ensembles?

Considering state resetting.

While our results show that
(k+1)
replicas are necessary for efficiently estimating
\mathrm{tr}(\rho^{k+1}O)
, this does not imply that
n(k+1)
working qubits are strictly required, since replicas can be loaded sequentially.
For instance, there exists a 3-replica protocol that estimates
\mathrm{tr}(\rho^{3}O)
using only
(2n+1)
working qubits: one applies a swap test on two replicas, resets one register to a fresh copy, and then applies another swap test.
Therefore, a practically meaningful question is that, can we identify a quantum learning task, such as the estimation of
\mathrm{tr}(\rho^{\otimes(k+1)}O_{k})
with a general form of
O_{k}
, that is exponentially hard for all protocols utilizing
nk
working qubits?

Estimating nonlinear properties of quantum states

The celebrated swap test
[
11
]
estimates
\mathrm{tr}(\rho^{2}O)
using a simple quantum circuit consisting of a controlled-SWAP gate and two Hadamard gates.
Since then, various methods have been developed to estimate
\mathrm{tr}(\rho^{k}O)
for general
k
and to reduce the overhead, including the generalized swap test
[
23
]
, the two-copy test
[
63
]
, and multivariate trace estimation
[
59
]
.
For a detailed review of existing methods, we refer the reader to
[
62
]
, which also provides an improved algorithm for the low-rank case.
On the lower-bound side, recent works
[
13
,
71
]
establish a sample complexity of
\Omega(k/\varepsilon^{2})
, matching the known upper bounds.

Besides the sample-access model, some papers also study the “purified query access model”, where one additionally has access to a purification of the unknown state or the oracle to prepare the purification
[
26
]
.
This model can sometimes reduce the overhead in predicting nonlinear quantum properties.
For instance,
[
13
,
71
]
show that with purification, one can estimate
\mathrm{tr}(\rho^{k}O)
using
\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{k})
queries, achieving a quadratic improvement. Similarly,
[
51
]
proves that if
\rho
can be purified with a constant number of ancilla qubits, then
\mathrm{tr}(\rho^{k}O)
can be estimated with only a constant number of queries.

More general nonlinear properties have also been investigated. A particularly important class includes the von Neumann and Rényi entropies
[
4
,
69
,
68
,
26
,
32
,
67
]
. Recently,
[
50
]
studied the sample, query, and time complexity of estimating
\mathrm{tr}(\rho^{q})
for non-integer

. Further improvements were obtained in
[
12
]
, which showed that the sample complexity is
\tilde{\Theta}(1/\varepsilon^{2})
for

, and lies between
\tilde{O}(1/\varepsilon^{2/(q-1)})
and
\Omega(1/\varepsilon^{\max\{1/(q-1),2}\})
for
1<q<2
.
Moreover, complexities of quantum spectrum learning and rank testing problems have also been thoroughly studied in
[
57
]
without limitations in allowed quantum operations.

Learning with restricted measurements and quantum replica advantage

Because implementing joint measurements across many replicas is experimentally challenging, recent works have explored learning properties under restricted measurement models.
One prominent example is the classical shadow protocol
[
39
]
, which recovers quantum properties of a state from the outcomes of random 1-replica measurements, including
\mathrm{tr}(\rho^{k}O)

[
37
,
61
]
.
Another example is the randomized measurement protocol, which is initially developed to estimate state moments
\mathrm{tr}(\rho^{k})
also with 1-replica measurements
[
66
,
24
,
25
,
9
]
.
It reaches the provable optimal sample complexity in estimating state purity
\mathrm{tr}(\rho^{2})
among all 1-replica protocols
[
15
]
.
This approach has also been applied to estimating
\mathrm{tr}(\rho^{2}O)

[
21
]
.
In
[
58
]
, another 1-replica protocol was proposed to estimate
\mathrm{tr}(\rho^{k})
, using
\mathcal{O}(d^{3-2/k})
copies to achieve constant multiplicative error.

However, it is already known that 1-replica protocols cannot efficiently estimate purity in the worst case
[
5
,
15
,
29
]
, establishing an exponential separation between 1- and 2-replica protocols.
Another 1-versus-2 separation arises in shadow tomography
[
1
,
15
,
17
,
45
]
, where the goal is to estimate
\mathrm{tr}(P\rho)
for all Pauli strings
P
.
In
[
14

, the authors construct a learning task that is hard for
k
-replica protocols but easy for
\mathrm{poly}(n,k)
-replica protocols.

Even for 1-replica protocols, one already assumes that arbitrary quantum operations can be performed on a state
\rho
.
In the real world, quantum circuits are limited by factors including topology, noise, and physical characteristics.
Recently, researchers started to consider quantum learning with restricted circuit structures.
In
[
36
,
8
,
35
]
the authors consider using shallow random circuits to perform classical shadow protocol, demonstrating intriguing scaling behavior in estimating certain nonlocal properties.
In
[
3

, the authors give the optimal sample complexity of quantum tomography using Pauli measurements.
Compared with the known performance bound for 1-replica quantum tomography
[
18
]
, they concluded that Pauli measurement is not the best method for quantum tomography.

Discussion about the concurrent work

As introduced above, there are two known sources of 1-vs-2 replica separations: the linearity of quantum mechanics and Heisenberg’s uncertainty principle. The first leads to the separation in estimating
\mathrm{tr}(\rho^{2})

[
5
,
15
,
29
]
, while the second underlies the separation in Pauli shadow tomography
[
15
,
17
,
45
]
. In the latter case, since Pauli strings

are not pairwise commuting, one cannot estimate all
\mathrm{tr}(P\rho)
simultaneously due to the uncertainty principle. However,
P\otimes P
are pairwise commuting, which makes it possible to estimate all
\mathrm{tr}((P\otimes P)(\rho\otimes\rho))=\mathrm{tr}(P\rho)^{2}
simultaneously.

Our work generalizes the first type of separation to all nonlinear properties
\mathrm{tr}(\rho^{k}O)
with
\norm{O}_{\infty}=1
and
\norm{O}_{1}=\Omega(d)
, establishing
(k-1)
-vs-

extend the second type of separation: by generalizing Pauli tomography on qubits to Weyl–Heisenberg tomography on qudits, they obtained
(k-1)

3.2

To learn the desired properties of an unknown quantum state
\rho
, we need to perform measurements to the state. General quantum measurements are represented as positive operator-valued measures (POVMs).

Definition 11

(POVMs)
.

A
D
-dimensional positive operator-valued measure (POVM) is a set of positive-semidefinite matrices
\{F_{s}\in\mathbb{C}^{D\times D}\}_{s}
that satisfies
\sum_{s}F_{s}=\mathds{1}_{D}
. When we measure a
D
-dimensional state
\sigma
, we obtain a classical outcome
s
with probability
\mathrm{tr}(F_{s}\sigma)
.

A
k
-replica joint measurement on
k
copies of a
D
-dimensional state
\sigma
is a
D^{k}
-dimensional POVM on the state
\sigma^{\otimes k}
.

A
k
-replica protocol is a learning protocol that can only perform joint measurements on at most
k
copies of the unknown states at a time. We give a formal definition as follows.

Definition 12

(
k
-replica protocols)
.

Given copy access to a
D
-dimensional unknown quantum state
\sigma
, a
k
-replica protocol with
T
rounds works as follows: In each round of the protocol, it gets
k
copies of
\sigma
and measure
\sigma^{\otimes k}
with a
D^{k}
-dimensional POVM
\{F_{s}\}_{s}
, obtaining some classical outcome
s
with probability
\mathrm{tr}(F_{s}\sigma^{\otimes k})
. The choice of the POVM can depend on all previous measurement outcomes. After
T
measurements, the algorithm predicts the desired properties of
\sigma
according to all measurement outcomes. The sample complexity of the protocol is
kT
.

Alternatively, we can merge the
T
rounds of measurements into one giant POVM on
kT
qudits with a tree structure.

Definition 13

(
k
-replica
T
-round POVMs)
.

A
k
-replica
T
-round POVM with local dimension
D
is a
D^{kT}
-dimensional POVM with the form
\{F_{s_{1}}\otimes F_{s_{1},s_{2}}\otimes\cdots\otimes F_{s_{1},s_{2},\cdots,s_{T}}\}_{s_{1},s_{2},\cdots,s_{T}}
, where for each
t\leq T
and
s_{1},\cdots,s_{t-1}
,
\{F_{s_{1},s_{2},\cdots,s_{t-1},s_{t}}\}_{s_{t}}
is a
D^{k}
-dimensional POVM.

Fact 14
.

We can redefine a
k
-replica protocol with
T
rounds as follows: It performs a
k
-replica
T

s_{1},\cdots,s_{T}
.

In this paper, we do not utilize the tree structure of
k
-replica protocols. Instead, we only need the fact that every POVM element has a product form.

Definition 15

(

A
(D,T)

Fact 16

A

The main tool to prove lower bounds is Le Cam’s two-point method.

Task 17

(
\mathcal{E}_{1}

.

Given copy access to an unknown quantum state
\sigma
, promised to be drawn from one of two ensembles
\mathcal{E}_{1}
and
\mathcal{E}_{2}
, the goal is to determine which ensemble
\sigma

Lemma 18

(Le Cam’s two-point method)
.

Let
M=\{F_{s}\}_{s}
be a
D
-dimensional POVM and
\mathcal{E}_{1},\mathcal{E}_{2}
be two
D
-dimensional ensembles. The success probability of distinguishing
\mathcal{E}_{1},\mathcal{E}_{2}
by performing

d_{M}(\mathop{\mathbb{E}\/}_{\sigma\leftarrow\mathcal{E}_{1}}[\sigma],\mathop{\mathbb{E}\/}_{\sigma\leftarrow\mathcal{E}_{2}}[\sigma])\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{s}\absolutevalue{\tr(F_s\E_{\sigma\leftarrow\cE_1}[\sigma])-\tr(F_s\E_{\sigma\leftarrow\cE_2}[\sigma])}.

(5)

Here
d_{M}(\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2})
is the total variation distance between the outcome distributions obtained by measuring the two states using
M
.

Therefore, the problem of proving lower bounds for sample complexity reduces to upper bounding the total variation distance. We recapitulate the reduction in the following lemma.

Lemma 19
.

Let
T_{0}\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
and
c_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
. For two

-dimensional ensembles
\mathcal{E}_{1},\mathcal{E}_{2}
, if we can prove that

\max_{M\in\mathcal{M}_{D,T}}d_{M}(\mathop{\mathbb{E}\/}_{\sigma\leftarrow\mathcal{E}_{1}}[\sigma^{\otimes T}],\mathop{\mathbb{E}\/}_{\sigma\leftarrow\mathcal{E}_{2}}[\sigma^{\otimes T}])\leq c_{0},\penalty\ \forall T<T_{0},

(6)

then the sample complexity of distinguishing
\mathcal{E}_{1}
and
\mathcal{E}_{2}
with success probability
c_{0}
using 1-replica protocols is at least
T_{0}
.

In particular, for two
D
-dimensional ensembles
\mathcal{E}_{1}^{\prime},\mathcal{E}_{2}^{\prime}
, if we can prove that

\max_{M\in\mathcal{M}_{D^{k},T}}d_{M}(\mathop{\mathbb{E}\/}_{\sigma\leftarrow\mathcal{E}_{1}^{\prime}}[\sigma^{\otimes kT}],\mathop{\mathbb{E}\/}_{\sigma\leftarrow\mathcal{E}_{2}^{\prime}}[\sigma^{\otimes kT}])\leq c_{0},\penalty\ \forall T<T_{0}.

(7)

then the sample complexity of distinguishing
\mathcal{E}_{1}^{\prime}
and
\mathcal{E}_{2}^{\prime}
with success probability
c_{0}
using
k
-replica protocols is at least
kT_{0}
.

One useful trick to upper bound the total variation distance is through the one-sided likelihood ratio.

Lemma 20

(One-sided likelihood ratio bound suffices, adapted from
[

)

Let
\delta>0

3.3
Haar measure and permutation operators

Random instances are common tools to prove lower bounds on the sample complexity of learning problems. In quantum information, the uniform distributions over quantum states and unitaries are captured by Haar measure, denoted by
\psi\leftarrow\mu_{\text{H}}(D)
and
U\leftarrow\mu_{\text{H}}(D\times D)

Definition 21

(Permutation operators)
.

For
N\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
, let
S_{N}
be the symmetric group on
N
elements. For each permutation
\pi\in S_{N}
, we can define a corresponding permutation operator on
N

\pi^{(D)}\ket{\psi_1}\otimes\cdots\otimes\ket{\psi_N}=\ket{\psi_{\pi^{-1}(1)}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\ket{\psi_{\pi^{-1}(N)}}.

(8)

Denote the symmetry operator
S_{N}^{(D)}\coloneqq\sum_{\pi\in S_{N}}\pi^{(D)}
and the normalized symmetry operator
\tilde{S}_{N}^{(D)}\coloneqq\frac{1}{D^{\uparrow N}}S_{N}^{(D)}
. We sometimes omit the superscriptions
(D)
in
S_{N}^{(D)}
and
\tilde{S}_{N}^{(D)}
.

Lemma 22

(Haar integrals for pure states)
.

\mathop{\mathbb{E}\/}_{\psi\leftarrow\mu_{\text{H}}(D)}\psi^{\otimes k}=\frac{1}{D(D+1)\cdots(D+k-1)}\sum_{\pi\in S_{k}}\pi=\tilde{S}_{k}
.

Another desirable property of Haar measure is the strong concentration of measure given by Levy’s lemma. We say a function
f:\mathbb{H}_{d}\to\mathbb{R}
is

\absolutevalue{f(\psi)-f(\phi)}\leq L\norm{\psi-\phi}_{2}.

(9)

Lemma 23

(Levy’s lemma)
.

Let

be an
L

\delta>0

\Pr_{\psi\leftarrow\mu_{\text{H}}(d)}\left[\absolutevalue{f(\psi)-\E_{\phi\leftarrow\mu_\Haar(d)}[f(\phi)]}>\delta\right]\leq 2\exp(-\frac{2d\delta^2}{9\pi^3 L^2}).

(10)

There is a stronger concentration for inner products

Lemma 24

(See
[
47
, Proposition 4.8]
or
[
54

)
.

Let
\psi

\Pr_{\psi}[\absolutevalue{\norm{\braket{\psi}{\phi}}^2}\geq\delta]\leq 2\exp(-\frac{d}{2}\delta).

3.4
Subroutines

Generalized swap test

Given a state
\sigma
and a unitary
U
, the generalized swap test (adapted from
[
23
]
) goes as follows: Apply a controlled-
U
to
\ket{+}\otimes\sigma
and measure the first qubit in the
X

\frac{1}{4}\mathrm{tr}((\mathds{1}\pm U)\sigma(\mathds{1}\pm U^{\dagger}))
, the outcome is
\ket{\pm}
and the (unnormalized) post-measurement state is
(\mathds{1}\pm U)\sigma(\mathds{1}\pm U^{\dagger})
.

When
\sigma=\rho^{\otimes k+1}
for a single-qudit state
\rho
and
U=\pi
is a
(k+1)
-qudit cyclic permutation operator. The generalized swap test outputs
\ket{\pm}
with probability
\frac{1}{4}\mathrm{tr}((\mathds{1}\pm\pi)\rho^{\otimes k+1}(\mathds{1}\pm\pi))=\frac{1\pm\mathrm{tr}(\rho^{k+1})}{2}
. Tracing out the last
k

\frac{\rho\pm\rho^{k+1}}{1\pm\mathrm{tr}(\rho^{k+1})}
upon the outcome
\ket{\pm}
.

Direct estimation of nonlinear observables

Given a
d
-dimensional state
\rho
and a
d
-dimensional observable
O
, estimating
\mathrm{tr}(\rho^{k+1}O)
is easy if we can perform
(k+1)
-replica joint measurements. Indeed, we can write
\mathrm{tr}(\rho^{k+1}O)
as the linear function of
\rho^{\otimes k+1}
:
\mathrm{tr}(\rho^{\otimes k+1}\cdot\frac{1}{2}[(O\otimes\mathds{1}_{d^{k}})\pi+\pi^{\dagger}(O\otimes\mathds{1}_{d^{k}})])
for any
(k+1)
-body cyclic permutation. Thus, it suffices to measure
\rho^{\otimes k+1}
in the eigenbasis of the Hermitian operator
\frac{1}{2}[(O\otimes\mathds{1}_{d^{k}})\pi+\pi^{\dagger}(O\otimes\mathds{1}_{d^{k}})]
. The overall sample complexity is
O(k/\varepsilon^{2})
, where

is the estimation error.

However, this method requires the diagonalization of
\frac{1}{2}[(O\otimes\mathds{1}_{d^{k}})\pi+\pi^{\dagger}(O\otimes\mathds{1}_{d^{k}})]
. A more clever way is to use a variety of generalized swap test. Apply a controlled-
\pi

\ket{+}\otimes\rho^{\otimes k+1}
. We can verify that the expectation of

under the resulting state is equal to
\mathrm{tr}(\rho^{k+1}O)
, thus it can be estimated directly using
O(k/\varepsilon^{2})
samples.

Their techniques actually can be easily combined and applied to the estimation of

Lemma 25

.

There exists an algorithm for estimating
\mathrm{tr}(\rho\sigma O)+\mathrm{tr}(\sigma\rho O)
with arbitrary observable
\norm{O}_{\infty}=1
with sample complexity
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(\max\{1/\varepsilon^{2},\sqrt{d}/\varepsilon\})
. The algorithm performs 1-replica measurement to each copy of
\rho
and
\sigma
separately and outputs the estimation based on all measurement outcomes. Moreover, the algorithm is non-adaptive, meaning that all measurements are pre-determined and are independent of intermediate measurement outcomes.

This lemma is crucial for proving the upper bound result in Theorem
5
, where we mainly consider the case
\rho
and
\sigma
commute with each other.
Therefore, we can simplify
\mathrm{tr}(\rho\sigma O)+\mathrm{tr}(\sigma\rho O)
into
\mathrm{tr}(\rho\sigma O)
.

4
Indistinguishability principle for Haar-assembled ensembles

In this section, we prove the hardness of distinguishing a Haar-assembled ensemble from its average. We restate the definition of Haar-assembled ensembles here for convenience. We slightly generalize the definition in
Definition

1
by allowing Haar random states of different dimensions. This generalization is not needed for our main results but may be useful for future applications.

Definition 26

(Haar-assembled ensembles)
.

For
m,D\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
, a
D
-dimensional Haar-assembled ensemble
\mathcal{E}_{\text{H}}
assembled by
m
Haar-random states
\psi_{1},\cdots,\psi_{m}
with local dimensions
d_{1},\cdots,d_{m}
is defined as

\mathcal{E}_{\text{H}}\coloneqq\left\{\sum_{j=1}^{J}p_{j}U_{j}(\psi_{1}^{\otimes a_{j1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\psi_{m}^{\otimes a_{jm}}\otimes\tau_{j})U_{j}^{\dagger}\right\}_{\psi_{r}\leftarrow\mu_{\text{H}}(d_{r}),r\in[m]}\,,

(13)

where
J\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
,
\{p_{j}\}_{j=1}^{J}
is a probability distribution. For each
j\in[J]
and
r\in[m]
,
a_{jr}\in\mathbb{N}
,
U_{j}
is a fixed unitary, and
\tau_{j}
is a fixed state such that the dimension of each term in the summation is
D
.

For
r\in[m]
, define
a_{r}\coloneqq\sum_{j=1}^{J}p_{j}a_{jr}
as the average occurrence of
\psi_{r}
, and
a^{\prime}_{r}\coloneqq\sum_{j=1}^{J}p_{j}a_{jr}^{2}
as the average squared occurrence of
\psi_{r}
. Denote
d_{\min}\coloneqq\min_{r\in[m]}d_{r}
and
a_{\max}\coloneqq\max_{r\in[m]}a_{r}
.

By
Lemma

22
, the average of the Haar-assembled ensemble is given by

\mathop{\mathbb{E}\/}_{\sigma\leftarrow\mathcal{E}_{\text{H}}}[\sigma]\coloneqq\sum_{j=1}^{J}p_{j}U_{j}(\tilde{S}_{a_{j1}}^{(d_{1})}\otimes\cdots\otimes\tilde{S}_{a_{jm}}^{(d_{m})}\otimes\tau_{j})U_{j}^{\dagger}\,.

(14)

We will prove indistinguishability from two aspects:

•

In
Theorem

27
, we prove the concentration of
\mathrm{tr}(O\sigma)
for
\sigma\leftarrow\mathcal{E}_{\text{H}}
. Therefore, it is hard to distinguish a random instance from its mean by estimating the expectation of a single observable
O
.

•

In
Theorem

29
, we show that any 1-replica protocol cannot distinguish
\sigma\leftarrow\mathcal{E}_{\text{H}}
from
\mathop{\mathbb{E}\/}_{\sigma^{\prime}\leftarrow\mathcal{E}_{\text{H}}}[\sigma^{\prime}]
efficiently with nontrivial advantage. We call this the indistinguishability principle for Haar-assembled ensembles.

4.1
Concentration of Haar-assembled ensembles

We first prove the concentration of Haar-assembled ensembles.

Theorem 27
.

Let
\mathcal{E}_{\text{H}}
be a Haar-assembled ensemble defined in (
13
) and
O
be a
D
-dimensional observable. For all

,

\Pr_{\sigma\leftarrow\mathcal{E}_{\text{H}}}\left[\absolutevalue{\tr(O\sigma)-\tr(O \E_{\sigma'\leftarrow\cE_\Haar}[\sigma'])}>\delta\right]\leq 2\sum_{r=1}^{m}\exp(-\frac{d_r\delta^2}{18\pi^3 m^2 a_r^2 \norm{O}_{\infty}^2}).

(15)

We apply Levy’s lemma recursively to prove the theorem. The following lemma establishes the Lipschitzness of
\mathrm{tr}(O\sigma)
as a function of each Haar-random state
\psi_{i}
.

Lemma 28
.

Let
O
be a
D
-dimensional observable and
\sigma=\sum_{j=1}^{J}p_{j}U_{j}(\psi_{1}^{\otimes a_{j1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\psi_{m}^{\otimes a_{jm}}\otimes\tau_{j})U_{j}^{\dagger}
as in (
13

\mathrm{tr}(O\sigma)
is
2a_{r}\norm{O}_{\infty}
-Lipschitz as a function of
\psi_{r}
, while fixing all other
\psi_{i}
s.

Proof.

We write
\sigma=\sigma(\psi_{r})
to emphasize the dependence on
\psi_{r}
. For any two
d_{r}
-dimensional states
\phi,\phi^{\prime}
,

\displaystyle\absolutevalue{\tr(O\sigma(\phi))-\tr(O\sigma(\phi'))}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\norm{O}_{\infty}\norm{\sigma(\phi)-\sigma(\phi')}_{1}

(
Lemma

9
(a))

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\norm{O}_{\infty}\sum_{j=1}^{J}p_{j}\norm{(\phi^{\otimes a_{jr}}-(\phi')^{\otimes a_{jr}})\otimes\tau_j\otimes\bigotimes_{i\neq r}\psi_i^{\otimes a_{ji}}}_{1}

(triangle inequality)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\norm{O}_{\infty}\sum_{j=1}^{J}p_{j}\norm{\phi^{\otimes a_{jr}}-(\phi')^{\otimes a_{jr}}}_{1}

(
Lemma

9
(b))

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\norm{O}_{\infty}\sum_{j=1}^{J}p_{j}a_{jr}\norm{\phi-\phi'}_{1}

(
Lemma

9
(d))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle a_{r}\norm{O}_{\infty}\norm{\phi-\phi'}_{1}

(
a_{r}=\sum_{j=1}^{J}p_{j}a_{jr}
)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2a_{r}\norm{O}_{\infty}\norm{\ket{\phi}-\ket{\phi'}}_{2}.

(
Lemma

9
(c))

∎

Now we are ready to prove
Theorem

27
.

Proof of
Theorem

27
.

The randomness of
\sigma\leftarrow\mathcal{E}_{\text{H}}
comes from
m

\psi_{1},\cdots,\psi_{m}
. We write
\mathrm{tr}(O\sigma)
as
f(\psi_{1},\cdots,\psi_{m})
to emphasize this dependence. By telescoping,

\displaystyle\absolutevalue{f(\psi_1, \cdots, \psi_m)-\E_{\psi_1',\cdots, \psi_m'}[f(\psi_1',\cdots, \psi_m')]}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{r=1}^{m}\absolutevalue{\E_{\psi_1', \cdots, \psi_{r-1}'}f(\psi_1', \cdots, \psi_{r-1}', \psi_r, \cdots, \psi_m) - \E_{\psi_1', \cdots, \psi_{r}'}f(\psi_1', \cdots, \psi_{r}', \psi_{r+1}, \cdots, \psi_m)}.

(16)

Regard
\mathop{\mathbb{E}\/}_{\psi_{1}^{\prime},\cdots,\psi_{r-1}^{\prime}}f(\psi_{1}^{\prime},\cdots,\psi_{r-1}^{\prime},\psi_{r},\cdots,\psi_{m})
as a function of
\psi_{r}
for fixed
\psi_{r+1},\cdots,\psi_{m}
. By the previous lemma, it is
2a_{r}\norm{O}_{\infty}
-Lipschitz. Applying Levy’s lemma (
Lemma

), the
r
-th term in (

) is at most
\delta/m
with probability at least
1-2\exp(-\frac{d_r\delta^2}{18\pi^3 m^2 a_r^2 \norm{O}_{\infty}^2})
.
By union bound, with probability at least
1-2\sum_{r=1}^{m}\exp(-\frac{d_r\delta^2}{18\pi^3 m^2 a_r^2 \norm{O}_{\infty}^2})
, every term in (

) is at most

. Therefore, with the same probability,
\absolutevalue{\tr(O\sigma)-\tr(O \E_{\sigma'\leftarrow\cE_\Haar}[\sigma'])}\leq\delta
.
∎

4.2
Haar-assembled ensemble versus its average

We now show the hardness of distinguishing in a stronger sense. Suppose we have copy access to
\sigma
that is either drawn from a Haar-assembled ensemble
\mathcal{E}_{\text{H}}
or is equal to the averaged state
\mathop{\mathbb{E}\/}_{\sigma^{\prime}\leftarrow\mathcal{E}_{\text{H}}}[\sigma^{\prime}]
. In this section, we lower bound the sample complexity of distinguishing the two cases using
1

By
Lemma

19
, we only need to upper bound

\max_{M\in\mathcal{M}_{D,T}}d_{M}(\mathop{\mathbb{E}\/}_{\sigma\leftarrow\mathcal{E}_{\text{H}}}[\sigma^{\otimes T}],\mathop{\mathbb{E}\/}_{\sigma\leftarrow\mathcal{E}_{\text{H}}}[\sigma]^{\otimes T}).

(17)

Theorem 29

(Indistinguishability principle for Haar-assembled ensembles)
.

Let
T\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
and
\mathcal{E}_{\text{H}}
be a
D
-dimensional Haar-assembled ensemble defined in (

\mathcal{E}_{\text{H}}\coloneqq\left\{\sum_{j=1}^{J}p_{j}U_{j}(\psi_{1}^{\otimes a_{j1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\psi_{m}^{\otimes a_{jm}}\otimes\tau_{j})U_{j}^{\dagger}\right\}_{\psi_{r}\leftarrow\mu_{\text{H}}(d_{r}),r\in[m]}\,.

(18)

1
-replica

(19)

As a consequence,

Proof.

Write
\sigma_{j}=U_{j}(\psi_{1}^{\otimes a_{j1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\psi_{m}^{\otimes a_{jm}}\otimes\tau_{j})U_{j}^{\dagger}

\sigma=\sum_{j=1}^{J}p_{j}\sigma_{j}

(20)

and

(21)

in

\displaystyle d_{M}(\mathop{\mathbb{E}\/}[\sigma^{\otimes T}],\mathop{\mathbb{E}\/}[\sigma]^{\otimes T})\leq\sum_{j_{1},\cdots,j_{T}\in[J]}\lparen\prod_{t=1}^{T}p_{j_{t}}\rparen d_{M}(\mathop{\mathbb{E}\/}[\otimes_{t=1}^{T}\sigma_{j_{t}}],\otimes_{t=1}^{T}\mathop{\mathbb{E}\/}[\sigma_{j_{t}}]).

(22)

Fix a term

(23)

Let us first assume (
23

d_{M}(\mathop{\mathbb{E}\/}[\sigma^{\otimes T}],\mathop{\mathbb{E}\/}[\sigma]^{\otimes T})\leq\sum_{j_{1},\cdots,j_{T}\in[J]}\left\lparen\prod_{t=1}^{T}p_{j_{t}}\right\rparen\sum_{r=1}^{m}\frac{1}{d_{r}}\left\lparen\sum_{t=1}^{T}a_{j_{t},r}\right\rparen^{2}.

Let

\displaystyle d_{M}(\mathop{\mathbb{E}\/}[\sigma^{\otimes T}],\mathop{\mathbb{E}\/}[\sigma]^{\otimes T})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle T(T-1)\sum_{r=1}^{m}\frac{1}{d_{r}}\left\lparen\sum_{j=1}^{J}p_{j}a_{jr}\right\rparen^{2}+T\sum_{r=1}^{m}\frac{1}{d_{r}}\sum_{j=1}^{J}p_{j}a_{jr}^{2}\,.

(
Lemma

This gives (

Now we prove (
23

M=\{F_{s}\}_{s}
By definition,

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{tr}(F\mathop{\mathbb{E}\/}[\otimes_{t=1}^{T}\sigma_{j_{t}}])}{\mathrm{tr}(F\otimes_{t=1}^{T}\mathop{\mathbb{E}\/}[\sigma_{j_{t}}])}

Lemma 30
.

Let
I
be a set of qudits,
T,m\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
, and let
\{I_{t,r}\}_{t\in[T],r\in[m]}
be a partition of
I
. Write
P_{t}=\bigcup_{r=1}^{m}I_{t,r}
and
Q_{r}=\bigcup_{t=1}^{T}I_{t,r}
, so

\{P_{1},\dots,P_{T}\}
and
\{Q_{1},\dots,Q_{m}\}
are two partitions of
I
. Suppose
G
is a positive semidefinite operator on
I
that factorizes across the partition
\{P_{t}\}_{t\in[T]}
, i.e.,

G=G_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes G_{T}\quad\text{with }G_{t}\text{ acting on }P_{t}.

(31)

Then

\mathrm{tr}(G\otimes_{r=1}^{m}S^{Q_{r}})\geq\mathrm{tr}(G\otimes_{t=1}^{T}\otimes_{r=1}^{m}S^{I_{tr}})=\prod_{t=1}^{T}\mathrm{tr}(G_{t}\otimes_{r=1}^{m}S^{I_{tr}})\,.

(32)

Here we do note require that all qudits have the same local dimension. We only require that qudits in each
Q_{r}
have the same local dimension.

When
I=[T]
,
m=1
,
I_{t,1}=\{t\}
, this lemma reduces to
\mathrm{tr}((G_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes G_{T})S_{T})\geq\mathrm{tr}(G_{1})\times\cdots\times\mathrm{tr}(G_{T})
, which is
[
15
, Lemma 5.12]
. When
I=[a+b]
,
T=2
,
m=1
,
I_{1,1}=[a]
,
I_{2,1}=[a+1,\cdots,a+b]
, this lemma reduces to
\mathrm{tr}((G_{1}\otimes G_{2})S_{a+b})\geq\mathrm{tr}(G_{1}S_{a})\mathrm{tr}(G_{2}S_{b})
, which is
[
17
, Lemma 16]
. Both lemmas are used to lower bound one-sided likelihood ratios in state discrimination problems involving Haar random states.
Lemma

30
goes beyond these two special cases and can be useful in more general scenarios.

Proof.

We prove the inequality by induction on
T
. When
T=1
, the inequality is a trivial equation. We now focus on the case

. The inequality becomes

\mathrm{tr}((G_{1}\otimes G_{2})\cdot(\otimes_{r=1}^{m}S^{I_{1r}\cup I_{2r}}))\geq\mathrm{tr}(G_{1}\cdot\otimes_{r=1}^{m}S^{I_{1r}})\mathrm{tr}(G_{2}\cdot\otimes_{r=1}^{m}S^{I_{2r}}).

(33)

For
i_{r}\leq\min\{\absolutevalue{I_{1r}},\absolutevalue{I_{2r}}\}
, define
S^{I_{1r}\cup I_{2r}}_{i_{r}}
as the set of permutations that cross
I_{1r}
and
I_{2r}

i_{r}
times (in other words, the permutation maps
i_{r}
qudits from
I_{1r}
to
I_{2r}
). Then
S^{I_{1r}\cup I_{2r}}=\sum_{i_{r}}S^{I_{1r}\cup I_{2r}}_{i_{r}}
. The LHS of (

) is the summation of
\mathrm{tr}((G_{1}\otimes G_{2})\cdot(\otimes_{r=1}^{m}S^{I_{1r}\cup I_{2r}}_{i_{r}}))
over all possible
\{i_{r}\}_{r=1}^{m}
, while the RHS of (
33
) is
\mathrm{tr}((G_{1}\otimes G_{2})\cdot(\otimes_{r=1}^{m}S^{I_{1r}\cup I_{2r}}_{0}))
. Therefore, we only need to prove that
\mathrm{tr}((G_{1}\otimes G_{2})\cdot(\otimes_{r=1}^{m}S^{I_{1r}\cup I_{2r}}_{i_{r}}))\geq 0
for all
\{i_{r}\}
.

The key observation is that every permutation in
S^{I_{1r}\cup I_{2r}}_{i_{r}}
can be decomposed to three steps: we first apply some permutation on
I_{1r}
and some permutation on
I_{2r}
. Then we swap
i_{r}
qudits in
I_{1r}
with
i_{r}
qudits in
I_{2r}
. Finally apply some permutation on
I_{1r}
and some permutation on
I_{2r}
. Therefore, up to a positive factor due to repeated counting,
S_{i_{r}}^{I_{1r}\cup I_{2r}}\propto(S^{I_{1r}}\otimes S^{I_{2r}})(\textnormal{SWAP}_{r}\otimes I_{r})(S^{I_{1r}}\otimes S^{I_{2r}})
, where
\textnormal{SWAP}_{r}
is a SWAP operator between
i_{r}
qudits in
I_{1r}
and
i_{r}
qudits in
I_{2r}
(the choice can be arbitrary), and
I_{r}
is the identity on other qudits. Let
S_{1}=\otimes_{r=1}^{m}S^{I_{1r}}
,
S_{2}=\otimes_{r=1}^{m}S^{I_{2r}}
, and
F
be the product of the
m
SWAP operators (acting on
\sum_{r=1}^{m}i_{r}
qudits in total). Let
R_{1},R_{2}
be the set of qudits in
P_{1},P_{2}
that are not swapped by
F
, respectively. We have

\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}((G_{1}\otimes G_{2})\cdot(\otimes_{r=1}^{m}S^{I_{1r}\cup I_{2r}}_{i_{r}}))

\displaystyle\propto\mathrm{tr}((S_{1}G_{1}S_{1}^{\dagger}\otimes S_{2}G_{2}S_{2}^{\dagger})\cdot(F\otimes\mathds{1}^{R_{1}}\otimes\mathds{1}^{R_{2}}))

\displaystyle=\mathrm{tr}((\mathrm{tr}_{R_{1}}(S_{1}G_{1}S_{1}^{\dagger})\otimes\mathrm{tr}_{R_{2}}(S_{2}G_{2}S_{2}^{\dagger}))\cdot F)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{tr}(\mathrm{tr}_{R_{1}}(S_{1}G_{1}S_{1}^{\dagger})\cdot\mathrm{tr}_{R_{2}}(S_{2}G_{2}S_{2}^{\dagger}))\geq 0.

Therefore, the lemma holds for
T=2
. We now prove the lemma from the
T-1
case to the
T
case. Write
G^{\prime}_{1}=G_{1}\otimes\cdots G_{T-1}
,
G^{\prime}_{2}=G_{T}
,
I_{1r}^{\prime}=I_{1r}\cup\cdots\cup I_{T-1,r},I^{\prime}_{2r}=I_{T,r}
. Using the results for
2
and
T-1
,

\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}(G\otimes_{r=1}^{m}S^{\cup_{t=1}^{T}I_{tr}})

\displaystyle=\mathrm{tr}((G_{1}^{\prime}\otimes G_{2}^{\prime})\cdot(\otimes_{r=1}^{m}S^{I_{1r}^{\prime}\cup I_{2r}^{\prime}}))

\displaystyle\geq\mathrm{tr}(G_{1}^{\prime}\cdot\otimes_{r=1}^{m}S^{I_{1r}^{\prime}})\mathrm{tr}(G_{2}^{\prime}\cdot\otimes_{r=1}^{m}S^{I_{2r}^{\prime}})

\displaystyle\geq\left(\prod_{t=1}^{T-1}\mathrm{tr}(G_{t}\otimes_{r=1}^{m}S^{I_{tr}})\right)\mathrm{tr}(G_{T}\otimes_{r=1}^{m}S^{I_{Tr}})

\displaystyle=\prod_{t=1}^{T}\mathrm{tr}(G_{t}\otimes_{r=1}^{m}S^{I_{tr}}).\qed

5
Lower bounds

In this section, we leverage Haar-assembled ensembles to construct hard instances for estimating nonlinear properties and testing spectrum. In this section, all qudits have the same local dimension
d
equal to the dimension of the unknown state
\rho
.

5.1
Constructing hard instances

For two probability distributions
\{p_{r}\}_{r=0}^{m},\{q_{r}\}_{r=0}^{m}
, define two single-qudit ensembles

\mathcal{E}_{1}\coloneqq\left\{p_{0}\rho_{0}+\sum_{r=1}^{m}p_{r}\psi_{r}\right\}_{\psi_{1},\cdots,\psi_{m}\leftarrow\mu_{\text{H}}(d)},\quad\mathcal{E}_{2}\coloneqq\left\{q_{0}\rho_{0}+\sum_{r=1}^{m}q_{r}\psi_{r}\right\}_{\psi_{1},\cdots,\psi_{m}\leftarrow\mu_{\text{H}}(d)},

(34)

where
\rho_{0}
is the maximally mixed state, and
\psi_{r}
s are Haar random states. Compared to (
2
), here we include an extra term of the maximally mixed state. This is used to tune the
\varepsilon
-dependence.

Write
\bm{p}=(p_{1},\cdots,p_{m}),\bm{q}=(q_{1},\cdots,q_{m})
(note that
p_{0}
and
q_{0}
are not included). We will choose
p_{0}=q_{0}
and choose
\bm{p},\bm{q}
such that the moments of
\bm{p}
and
\bm{q}
are consistent up to degree
k
, but differ at degree
k+1
. In this setting, we aim to prove that,

•

\mathop{\mathbb{E}\/}_{\rho\leftarrow\mathcal{E}_{1}}[\rho^{\otimes k}]=\mathop{\mathbb{E}\/}_{\rho\leftarrow\mathcal{E}_{2}}[\rho^{\otimes k}]
. Furthermore,
\mathcal{E}_{1}
and
\mathcal{E}_{2}
are hard to distinguish using
k
-replica protocols.

•

\mathop{\mathbb{E}\/}_{\rho\leftarrow\mathcal{E}_{1}}[\rho^{\otimes k+1}]\neq\mathop{\mathbb{E}\/}_{\rho\leftarrow\mathcal{E}_{2}}[\rho^{\otimes k+1}]
. Therefore, we can distinguish between
\mathcal{E}_{1}
and
\mathcal{E}_{2}
if we can learn some
(k+1)
-degree properties of
\rho
efficiently.

We formalize and prove the two points in
Lemma

33
and
Lemma

34
.

To express
\mathop{\mathbb{E}\/}_{\rho\leftarrow\mathcal{E}_{1}}[\rho^{\otimes k}]
, we introduce monomial symmetric polynomials.

Definition 31

(Monomial symmetric polynomials)
.

For
t\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
, we use
\lambda\vdash t
to denote that
\lambda
is a partition of
t
, that is, a multiset of positive integers
\lambda=\{\lambda_{1},\cdots,\lambda_{l(\lambda)}\}
such that
\lambda_{1}+\cdots+\lambda_{l(\lambda)}=t
. Denote the monomial symmetric polynomial

m_{\lambda}(\bm{p})\coloneqq\sum_{i_{1},\cdots,i_{l(\lambda)}\in[m],\text{distinct}}p_{i_{1}}^{\lambda_{1}}\cdots p_{i_{l(\lambda)}}^{\lambda_{l(\lambda)}}.

(35)

Expanding
\mathop{\mathbb{E}\/}_{\rho\leftarrow\mathcal{E}_{1}}[\rho^{\otimes k}]
, each term is of the form

\rho_{0}^{\otimes A_{0}}\otimes\mathop{\mathbb{E}\/}_{\psi_{1}}[\psi_{1}^{\otimes A_{1}}]\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathop{\mathbb{E}\/}_{\psi_{m}}[\psi_{m}^{\otimes A_{m}}]=\rho_{0}^{\otimes A_{0}}\otimes\tilde{S}^{A_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\tilde{S}^{A_{m}},

(36)

where we write
\tilde{S}^{A}\coloneqq\frac{1}{d^{\uparrow\absolutevalue{A}}}S^{A}
. Notice that this formula is not changed if we permute
\psi_{1},\cdots,\psi_{m}
. After collecting like terms, the coefficient of (
36
) is
p_{0}^{\absolutevalue{A_0}}
multiplying a symmetric polynomial in
\bm{p}
. To explicitly write down the polynomial, we select nonempty subsets in
A_{1},\cdots,A_{m}
. The cardinalities of them give a partition
\lambda
of
k-\absolutevalue{A_0}
. The coefficient of (
36
) is
p_{0}^{\absolutevalue{A_0}}m_{\lambda}(\bm{p})
. Therefore,

\mathop{\mathbb{E}\/}_{\rho\leftarrow\mathcal{E}_{1}}[\rho^{\otimes k}]=\sum_{a=0}^{k}\sum_{\lambda\vdash k-a}p_{0}^{a}m_{\lambda}(\bm{p})\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}A,B_{1},\cdots,B_{l(\lambda)}\text{ partition }[k]\\
\absolutevalue{A}=a,\absolutevalue{B_i}=\lambda_{i}\end{subarray}}\rho_{0}^{\otimes A}\otimes\tilde{S}^{B_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\tilde{S}^{B_{l(\lambda)}}.

(37)

Since
m_{\lambda}(\bm{p})
is a symmetric polynomial, it can be written as a polynomial in elementary symmetric polynomials, which in turn can be written as a polynomial in power sums by Newton’s identities. The full formula is complicated. Here we provide a self-contained way to calculate the leading coefficient. The proof is delayed to
Section

7.2
.

Lemma 32
.

Let
t\in\mathbb{N}
and let
\lambda=\{\lambda_{1},\cdots,\lambda_{l}\}
be a partition of
t
.
Denote by
m_{\lambda}(\bm{p})
the monomial symmetric polynomial defined in (
35
), and let
s_{u}(\bm{p})\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{m}p_{i}^{u}
be the degree-
u
power sum.
Then there exists a polynomial
g_{\lambda}
such that

m_{\lambda}(\bm{p})=(-1)^{\,l-1}(l-1)!\,s_{t}(\bm{p})\;+\;g_{\lambda}(s_{1}(\bm{p}),s_{2}(\bm{p}),\dots,s_{t-1}(\bm{p})).

(38)

Equivalently, in the expansion of
m_{\lambda}(\bm{p})
in the power-sum basis, the term
s_{t}(\bm{p})
occurs with coefficient
(-1)^{\,l-1}(l-1)!
, and all remaining terms involve only
s_{1},\dots,s_{t-1}
.

With (
37
) and
Lemma

32
, we are ready to compare the moments of
\mathcal{E}_{1}
and
\mathcal{E}_{2}
in
Lemma

33
.

Lemma 33
.

Let
\mathcal{E}_{1},\mathcal{E}_{2}
be two single-qudit ensembles defined in (
34
). Suppose
p_{0}=q_{0}
and
\sum_{r=1}^{m}p_{r}^{i}=\sum_{r=1}^{m}q_{r}^{i}
for
i\in[k]
. Then
\mathop{\mathbb{E}\/}_{\rho\leftarrow\mathcal{E}_{1}}[\rho^{\otimes k}]=\mathop{\mathbb{E}\/}_{\rho\leftarrow\mathcal{E}_{2}}[\rho^{\otimes k}]
,

\mathop{\mathbb{E}\/}_{\rho\leftarrow\mathcal{E}_{1}}[\rho^{\otimes k+1}]-\mathop{\mathbb{E}\/}_{\rho\leftarrow\mathcal{E}_{2}}[\rho^{\otimes k+1}]=(\sum_{r=1}^{m}p_{r}^{k+1}-\sum_{r=1}^{m}q_{r}^{k+1})\Delta_{k+1},

where

\Delta_{k+1}\coloneqq\sum_{l=1}^{k+1}(-1)^{l-1}(l-1)!\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}B_{1},\cdots,B_{l}\text{ partition }[k+1]\\
\text{all nonempty}\end{subarray}}\tilde{S}^{B_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\tilde{S}^{B_{l}}.

(39)

Proof.

Since
s_{i}(\bm{p})=s_{i}(\bm{q})
for
i\in[k]
,
Lemma

32
implies that

m_{\lambda}(\bm{p})-m_{\lambda}(\bm{q})=\begin{cases}0,&\lambda\vdash k^{\prime},k^{\prime}\leq k\\
(-1)^{l(\lambda)-1}(l(\lambda)-1)!(s_{k+1}(\bm{p})-s_{k+1}(\bm{q})),&\lambda\vdash k+1\end{cases}.

(40)

By (
37
),
\mathop{\mathbb{E}\/}_{\rho\leftarrow\mathcal{E}_{1}}[\rho^{\otimes k}]=\mathop{\mathbb{E}\/}_{\rho\leftarrow\mathcal{E}_{2}}[\rho^{\otimes k}]
and

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{E}\/}_{\rho\leftarrow\mathcal{E}_{1}}[\rho^{\otimes k+1}]-\mathop{\mathbb{E}\/}_{\rho\leftarrow\mathcal{E}_{2}}[\rho^{\otimes k+1}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\lambda\vdash k+1}(-1)^{l(\lambda)-1}(l(\lambda)-1)!(s_{k+1}(\bm{p})-s_{k+1}(\bm{q}))\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}B_{1},\cdots,B_{l(\lambda)}\text{ partition }[k]\\
\absolutevalue{B_i}=\lambda_{i}\end{subarray}}\tilde{S}^{B_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\tilde{S}^{B_{l(\lambda)}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(s_{k+1}(\bm{p})-s_{k+1}(\bm{q}))\sum_{l=1}^{k+1}(-1)^{l-1}(l-1)!\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}B_{1},\cdots,B_{l}\text{ partition }[k]\\
\text{all nonempty}\end{subarray}}\tilde{S}^{B_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\tilde{S}^{B_{l}}.\qed

(41)

Now we prove that
\mathcal{E}_{1}
and
\mathcal{E}_{2}
are hard to distinguish using
k
-replica protocols.

Lemma 34
.

Let
\{p_{r}\}_{r=0}^{m},\{q_{r}\}_{r=0}^{m}
be two probability distributions such that
p_{0}=q_{0}
and
\sum_{r=1}^{m}p_{r}^{i}=\sum_{r=1}^{m}q_{r}^{i}
for
i\in[k]
. Define two single-qudit ensembles
\mathcal{E}_{1},\mathcal{E}_{2}
by

\mathcal{E}_{1}\coloneqq\{p_{0}\rho_{0}+\sum_{r=1}^{m}p_{i}\psi_{i}\}_{\psi_{1},\cdots,\psi_{m}\leftarrow\mu_{\text{H}}(d)},\quad\mathcal{E}_{2}\coloneqq\{q_{0}\rho_{0}+\sum_{r=1}^{m}q_{i}\psi_{i}\}_{\psi_{1},\cdots,\psi_{m}\leftarrow\mu_{\text{H}}(d)},

(42)

where
\rho_{0}
is the maximally mixed state,
\psi_{1},\cdots,\psi_{m}
are Haar random. Then the sample complexity of distinguishing
\mathcal{E}_{1}
and
\mathcal{E}_{2}
with a constant success probability using
k
-replica protocol is at least
\Omega(\frac{\sqrt{d}}{1-p_{0}})
.

Proof.

By
Lemma

18
, the success probability of distinguishing
\mathcal{E}_{1}
and
\mathcal{E}_{2}
using
k
-replica protocol is at most

\max_{M\in\mathcal{M}_{d^{k},T}}d_{M}(\mathop{\mathbb{E}\/}_{\rho\leftarrow\mathcal{E}_{1}}[\rho^{\otimes kT}],\mathop{\mathbb{E}\/}_{\rho\leftarrow\mathcal{E}_{2}}[\rho^{\otimes kT}]).

Notice that

\{\rho^{\otimes k}\}_{\rho\leftarrow\mathcal{E}_{1}}=\left\{\sum_{A_{0},A_{1},\cdots,A_{m}\text{ partition }[k]}\left\lparen\prod_{r=0}^{m}p_{r}^{\absolutevalue{A_r}}\right\rparen\rho_{0}^{\otimes A_{0}}\otimes\psi_{1}^{\otimes A_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\psi_{m}^{\otimes A_{m}}\right\}

(43)

is a
k
-qudit Haar-assembled ensemble (where the rotation
U_{j}
is used to allocate positions of
\psi_{r}
’s). We now apply
Theorem

29
to upper bound
d_{M}(\mathop{\mathbb{E}\/}_{\rho\leftarrow\mathcal{E}_{1}}[\rho^{\otimes kT}],\mathop{\mathbb{E}\/}_{\rho\leftarrow\mathcal{E}_{1}}[\rho^{\otimes k}]^{\otimes T})
.
Recall that in (
19
), the “
a_{r}
” is the average occurrence of
\psi_{r}
and the “
a^{\prime}_{r}
” is the average squared occurrence of
\psi_{r}
. Here, with
Lemma

43
, they become

a_{r}=\sum_{b_{0}+b_{1}+\cdots+b_{m}=k}\binom{k}{b_{0},\cdots,b_{m}}(\prod_{r=0}^{m}p_{r}^{b_{r}})b_{r}=kp_{r},

(44)

a^{\prime}_{r}=\sum_{b_{0}+b_{1}+\cdots+b_{m}=k}\binom{k}{b_{0},\cdots,b_{m}}(\prod_{r=0}^{m}p_{r}^{b_{r}})b_{r}^{2}=k(k-1)p_{r}^{2}+kp_{r}.

(45)

Plugging them into (
19
), we obtain

\displaystyle\max_{M\in\mathcal{M}_{d^{k},T}}d_{M}(\mathop{\mathbb{E}\/}_{\rho\leftarrow\mathcal{E}_{1}}[\rho^{\otimes kT}],\mathop{\mathbb{E}\/}_{\rho\leftarrow\mathcal{E}_{1}}[\rho^{\otimes k}]^{\otimes T})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{T(T-1)}{d}\sum_{r=1}^{m}(kp_{r})^{2}+\frac{T}{d}\sum_{r=1}^{m}(k(k-1)p_{r}^{2}+kp_{r})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{k^{2}T^{2}-kT}{d}\sum_{r=1}^{m}p_{r}^{2}+\frac{Tk}{d}\sum_{r=1}^{m}p_{r}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{d}\left\lparen(kT\sum_{r=1}^{m}p_{r})^{2}+(kT\sum_{r=1}^{m}p_{r})\right\rparen.

(46)

Similarly,

\max_{M\in\mathcal{M}_{d^{k},T}}d_{M}(\mathop{\mathbb{E}\/}_{\rho\leftarrow\mathcal{E}_{2}}[\rho^{\otimes kT}],\mathop{\mathbb{E}\/}_{\rho\leftarrow\mathcal{E}_{2}}[\rho^{\otimes k}]^{\otimes T})\leq\frac{1}{d}\left\lparen(kT\sum_{r=1}^{m}p_{r})^{2}+(kT\sum_{r=1}^{m}p_{r})\right\rparen.

(47)

By
Lemma

33
,
\mathop{\mathbb{E}\/}_{\rho\leftarrow\mathcal{E}_{1}}[\rho^{\otimes k}]=\mathop{\mathbb{E}\/}_{\rho\leftarrow\mathcal{E}_{2}}[\rho^{\otimes k}]
. By triangle inequality, we obtain

\max_{M\in\mathcal{M}_{d^{k},T}}d_{M}(\mathop{\mathbb{E}\/}_{\rho\leftarrow\mathcal{E}_{1}}[\rho^{\otimes kT}],\mathop{\mathbb{E}\/}_{\rho\leftarrow\mathcal{E}_{2}}[\rho^{\otimes kT}])\leq\frac{2}{d}\left\lparen(kT\sum_{r=1}^{m}p_{r})^{2}+(kT\sum_{r=1}^{m}p_{r})\right\rparen.

(48)

According to
Lemma

19
, the sample complexity of distinguishing
\mathcal{E}_{1}
and
\mathcal{E}_{2}
with a constant success probability using

-replica protocols is at least
\Omega(\sqrt{d}/\sum_{r=1}^{m}p_{r})=\Omega(\sqrt{d}/(1-p_{0}))
.
∎

5.2

(k+1)
-body observables

We now apply
Lemma

33
and

to prove lower bounds for estimating
(k+1)

Theorem 35
.

Let
k,d\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
,
O
be a
(k+1)
-qudit observable with the operator norm
\norm{O}_{\infty}\leq 1
and the nontrivial
(k+1)
-body weight
w(O)\coloneqq\absolutevalue{\sum_{\pi\in S_{k+1}^{\text{circ}}}c_\pi(O)}\geq 2(k+1)^{2k}/d
. Let
\varepsilon
be the error parameter such that
30(k+1)^{3}d^{-1/2}\leq\varepsilon\leq(2(k+1))^{-k}w(O)/5
.
Given access to a
d
-dimensional mixed quantum state
\rho\in\mathbb{C}^{d\times d}
, the sample complexity of estimating
\mathrm{tr}(\rho^{\otimes k+1}O)
within additive error
\varepsilon
with probability
0.9
is at least
\Omega(\frac{\sqrt{d}}{k\varepsilon^{1/(1+k)}})
for any
k
-replica protocol.

We need the following two lemmas. The proofs of them are left in
Section

7
.

Lemma 36
.

For
k\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
, there exists two probability distributions
\{p_{i}\}_{r=1}^{k+1}
and
\{q_{i}\}_{r=1}^{k+1}
such that
\sum_{r=1}^{k+1}p_{r}^{i}=\sum_{r=1}^{k+1}q_{r}^{i}
for all
0\leq i\leq k
, and
\sum_{r=1}^{k+1}p_{i}^{k+1}-\sum_{r=1}^{k+1}q_{i}^{k+1}=\frac{2}{2^{k}(k+1)^{k}}
.

Lemma 37
.

Let
O
be a
(k+1)
-qudit observable and
\Delta_{k}
be the operator defined in (
39
). Then we have

\absolutevalue{\sum_{\pi\in S_{k+1}^{\text{circ}}}c_\pi(O)-\tr(O\Delta_{k+1})}\leq\frac{(k+1)^{k}(k+1)!}{d}\leq\frac{(k+1)^{2k}}{d}.

(49)

Proof of
Theorem

35
.

Write
w\coloneqq w(O)
and
\delta_{k}\coloneqq(2(k+1))^{-k}
. Let
\{p_{r}^{\prime}\}_{r=1}^{k+1}
and
\{q_{r}^{\prime}\}_{r=1}^{k+1}
be the sequence given by
Lemma

36
. Let
p_{0}=q_{0}=1-(5\varepsilon/(w\delta_{k}))^{1/(k+1)}
,
p_{r}=(5\varepsilon/(w\delta_{k}))^{1/(k+1)}p_{r}^{\prime}
, and
q_{r}=(5\varepsilon/(w\delta_{k}))^{1/(k+1)}q_{r}^{\prime}
. Then
\sum_{r=1}^{k+1}p_{r}^{k+1}-\sum_{r=1}^{k+1}q_{r}^{k+1}=10\varepsilon/w
.

Consider the two single-qudit ensembles
\mathcal{E}_{1},\mathcal{E}_{2}
in
Lemma

34
with the
\{p_{r}\}_{r=0}^{k+1},\{q_{r}\}_{r=0}^{k+1}
defined above. By
Lemma

34
, to distinguish between
\mathcal{E}_{1}
and
\mathcal{E}_{2}
with probability at least
0.6
, the sample complexity is at least
\Omega(\frac{\sqrt{d}}{1-p_{0}})=\Omega(\frac{\sqrt{d}}{k\varepsilon^{1/(1+k)}})
.

Now assume we can estimate
\mathrm{tr}(\rho^{\otimes k+1}O)
within additive error
\varepsilon
with probability
0.9
. Denote the estimator by
\hat{E}
and let
E_{1}\coloneqq\mathop{\mathbb{E}\/}_{\rho^{\prime}\leftarrow\mathcal{E}_{1}}[\mathrm{tr}(\rho^{\prime\otimes k+1}O)]
,
E_{2}\coloneqq\mathop{\mathbb{E}\/}_{\rho^{\prime}\leftarrow\mathcal{E}_{2}}[\mathrm{tr}(\rho^{\prime\otimes k+1}O)]
. We design a simple algorithm that distinguishes
\mathcal{E}_{1}
and
\mathcal{E}_{2}
: Check whether the estimator of
\mathrm{tr}(\rho^{\otimes k+1}O)
is closer to
E_{1}
or
E_{2}
, and output the corresponding ensemble. We now show that this method succeeds in distinguishing
\mathcal{E}_{1}
and
\mathcal{E}_{2}
with probability at least 0.8.

Without loss of generality, suppose the ground truth is
\mathcal{E}_{1}
. By definition, with probability at least 0.9,

\absolutevalue{\hat{E}-\tr(\rho^{\otimes k+1}O)}\leq\varepsilon.

(50)

Since

is a
(k+1)
-qudit Haar-assembled ensemble, by the concentration of Haar-assembled ensemble (

, where

and the average occurrence

of each

),

Therefore, with probability at least

, we have

.

On the other hand,

\displaystyle\absolutevalue{E_1-E_2}

\displaystyle=\absolutevalue{\sum_{r=1}^{k+1}p_r^{k+1}-\sum_{r=1}^{k+1}q_r^{k+1}}\cdot\absolutevalue{\tr(O\Delta_{k+1})}

(

)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{10\varepsilon}{w}\cdot(w-\frac{(k+1)^{2k}}{d})

(

)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{10\varepsilon}{w}\cdot(w-w/2)

(

)

\displaystyle=5\varepsilon.

(51)

Therefore, with probability at least 0.8,

is closer to

and our algorithm gives the correct answer. So an algorithm that estimates

within additive error

with probability 0.9 will solve the distinguishing task with probability at least

. But we already know that the distinguishing task requires

samples for
k
-replica protocols. Hence estimating
\mathrm{tr}(\rho^{\otimes k+1}O)
requires
\Omega(\frac{\sqrt{d}}{k\varepsilon^{1/(1+k)}})
samples for
k
-replica protocols.

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{E}\/}_{U\leftarrow\mu_{\text{H}}}[U^{\otimes k+1}O^{\prime}(U^{\dagger})^{\otimes k+1}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}[(\mathop{\mathbb{E}\/}[UOU^{\dagger}]\otimes\mathds{1}_{d^{k}})\pi+\pi^{\dagger}(\mathop{\mathbb{E}\/}[UOU^{\dagger}]\otimes\mathds{1}_{d^{k}})]

(

Apply

to this

Lemma 38
.

Let
k,d\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
,

be a

\varepsilon
be the error parameter such that
30(k+1)^{3}d^{-1/2}\leq\varepsilon\leq(2(k+1))^{-k}\absolutevalue{\tr(O)}/(5d)
.
Given access to a
d
-dimensional mixed quantum state
\rho\in\mathbb{C}^{d\times d}
, the sample complexity of estimating
\mathrm{tr}(\rho^{k+1}O)
within additive error
\varepsilon
with probability

is at least
\Omega(\frac{\sqrt{d}}{k\varepsilon^{1/(1+k)}})
for any
k
-replica protocol.

However, this result is not satisfactory because it require
\absolutevalue{\tr(O)}=\Omega(d)
, so it is not applicable to traceless observables including Pauli observables. The reason is that the positive and negative parts of

cancel out when we calculate
\mathop{\mathbb{E}\/}_{\rho\leftarrow\mathcal{E}_{1}}[\mathrm{tr}(\rho^{k+1}O)]-\mathop{\mathbb{E}\/}_{\rho\leftarrow\mathcal{E}_{2}}[\mathrm{tr}(\rho^{k+1}O)]

Theorem
3
.

Let
k,d\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
,
O
be a
d
-dimensional observable with the operator norm
\norm{O}_{\infty}\leq 1
and
\norm{O}_{1}\geq 2(k+1)^{2k}
. Let
\varepsilon
be the error parameter such that
50(k+1)^{3}d^{-1/2}\leq\varepsilon\leq(2(k+1))^{-k}\norm{O}_{1}/(10d)
.
Given access to a
d
-dimensional mixed quantum state
\rho\in\mathbb{C}^{d\times d}
, the sample complexity of estimating
\mathrm{tr}(\rho^{k+1}O)
within additive error
\varepsilon
with probability

is at least
\Omega(\frac{\sqrt{d}}{k\varepsilon^{1/(1+k)}})
for any
k
-replica protocol.

Proof.

The first step is to find
d/2
eigenvalues of
O
such that the absolute value of their sum is at least
\norm{O}_{1}/4
.
Without loss of generality, we assume that the number of nonnegative eigenvalues of

is at least
d/2
. Sort the eigenvalues of
O
as
\lambda_{1}\geq\lambda_{2}\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_{s}\geq 0>\lambda_{s+1}\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_{d}
, where

. If
\sum_{i=1}^{d/2}\lambda_{i}\geq\norm{O}_{1}/4
, we are done. Otherwise if
\sum_{i=1}^{d/2}\lambda_{i}<\norm{O}_{1}/4
, then

\absolutevalue{\sum_{i=d/2+1}^{d}\lambda_i}\geq\sum_{i=s+1}^{d}\absolutevalue{\lambda_i}-\sum_{i=d/2}^{s}\lambda_{i}=\norm{O}_{1}-\sum_{i=1}^{d/2}\lambda_{i}-2\sum_{i=d/2}^{s}\lambda_{i}\geq\norm{O}_{1}-3\sum_{i=1}^{d/2}\lambda_{i}>\frac{\norm{O}_{1}}{4}.

Therefore, we can always find
d/2
eigenvalues of
O
such that the absolute value of their sum is at least
\norm{O}_{1}/4
. Let
O_{0}
be the diagonal matrix whose diagonal entries are these

eigenvalues and
O_{1}
be the diagonal matrix whose diagonal entries are the remaining
d/2
eigenvalues. Then

for some unitary

By definition,

, so
\norm{O}_{1}/(10d)\leq\absolutevalue{\tr(O_0)}/(5(d/2))
. Applying
Lemma

38
to
O_{0}
(where the local dimensional is
d/2
), we know that for
\absolutevalue{\tr(O_0)}\geq 2(k+1)^{2k}
and
30k^{3}(d/2)^{-1/2}\leq\varepsilon\leq(2(k+1))^{-k}\norm{O}_{1}/(10d)
, the sample complexity of estimating
\mathrm{tr}(\rho_{0}^{k+1}O_{0})
within additive error
\varepsilon
with probability
0.9
is at least
\Omega(\frac{\sqrt{d/2}}{k\varepsilon^{1/(1+k)}})=\Omega(\frac{\sqrt{d}}{k\varepsilon^{1/(1+k)}})
for any
k
-replica protocol, where
\rho_{0}
is a
d/2
-dimensional mixed quantum state.

Now assume that there is a
k
-replica protocol
\mathcal{A}
that estimates
\mathrm{tr}(\rho^{k+1}O)
. We construct a
k
-replica protocol
\mathcal{A}_{0}
that estimates
\mathrm{tr}(\rho_{0}^{k+1}O_{0})
with the same precision, success probability, and sample complexity.

\mathcal{A}_{0}
works as follows: given access to
(d/2)
-dimensional state
\rho_{0}
, it construct a
d
-dimensional state

, where
\mathds{O}_{d/2}
is the
d/2
-dimensional zero matrix. Then it runs
\mathcal{A}
on

\mathrm{tr}(\rho^{k+1}O)=\mathrm{tr}(U^{\dagger}(\rho_{0}\oplus\mathds{O}_{d/2})^{k+1}UO)=\mathrm{tr}((\rho_{0}^{k+1}\oplus\mathds{O}_{d/2})(O_{0}\oplus O_{1}))=\mathrm{tr}(\rho_{0}^{k+1}O_{0}),

(52)

the output of

is also an estimate of
\mathrm{tr}(\rho_{0}^{k+1}O_{0})
with the same precision and success probability. According to the hardness of estimating
\mathrm{tr}(\rho_{0}^{k+1}O_{0})
, the sample complexity of estimating
\mathrm{tr}(\rho^{k+1}O)
is at least
\Omega(\frac{\sqrt{d}}{k\varepsilon^{1/(1+k)}})
for any
k
-replica protocol.
∎

5.3
Applications in spectrum testing

As another illustrative application of our framework, we establish lower bounds for spectrum testing and rank testing under

-replica protocols.
A

refers to a probability distribution with entries arranged in nonincreasing order (sometimes the length of the sequence is smaller than

The rank of a spectrum is the number of its nonzero entries, and the rank of a quantum state is defined as the rank of its spectrum.

Task 39

(
\bm{p}

Given copy access to an unknown state

When

-replica measurements are indeed necessary.

Theorem 40
.

Let
\bm{p}=(p_{1},\cdots,p_{d})
and
\bm{q}=(q_{1},\cdots q_{d})

\bm{q}

k
-replica protocol, where

and

It seems a direct corollary of

Lemma 41
.

Let
\bm{a}=(a_{1},\cdots,a_{m})
be a spectrum and
\psi_{1},\cdots,\psi_{m}
be
m
Haar random states. With probability at least

We leave the proof to

7.5
.

In this section, we prove the upper bound results

Theorem
5
.

Let

,
\varepsilon>0
, and

Denote

Define two states

Apply the generalized swap test to

and a

-qudit cyclic permutation operator

after normalization. Therefore, we obtain a copy of state

with probability at least

. With high probability, we successfully obtain

\rho_{1}
after
3M
runs. Similarly, with high probability, we successfully obtain
M
copies of
\rho_{2}
.

If
k=1
, we can estimate
\mathrm{tr}(\rho^{2}O)
using
Lemma

25
directly. If
k=2
, then
\rho_{1}=(\rho+\rho^{2})/(1+\mathrm{tr}(\rho^{2}))
. Since
\mathrm{tr}(\rho^{3}O)=(1+\mathrm{tr}(\rho^{2}))\mathrm{tr}(\rho_{1}\rho O)-\mathrm{tr}(\rho^{2}O)
, once we get estimators for
\mathrm{tr}(\rho^{2}),\mathrm{tr}(\rho_{1}\rho O)

\mathrm{tr}(\rho^{2})

\varepsilon/10
, we can obtain an estimator of
\mathrm{tr}(\rho^{3}O)
within error
\varepsilon
. Here
\mathrm{tr}(\rho^{2})

\mathrm{tr}(\rho_{1}\rho O)
can be estimated in a distributed way using
Lemma

25
, and
\mathrm{tr}(\rho^{2}O)
can be estimated directly as it is a linear property of

. All of these estimations can be done by
2
-replica protocols. Therefore, we obtain a
2
-replica protocol for estimating
\mathrm{tr}(\rho^{3}O)
.

Now we assume

7.1
Expectations under multinomial distributions

In this paper, we run into the following calculation several times

\sum_{x_{1}+\cdots+x_{m}=N}\binom{N}{x_{1},\cdots,x_{m}}p_{1}^{x_{1}}\cdots p_{m}^{x_{m}}f(x_{1},\cdots,x_{m}),

Lemma 42
.

Let

\sum_{x_{1}+\cdots+x_{m}=N}\binom{N}{x_{1},\cdots,x_{m}}\left\lparen\prod_{i=1}^{m}p_{i}^{x_{i}}\right\rparen\prod_{i=1}^{m}x_{i}^{\downarrow c_{i}}=N^{\downarrow c_{1}+\cdots+c_{m}}\prod_{i=1}^{m}p_{i}^{c_{i}}.

(57)

Proof.

Notice that
\binom{N}{x_{1},\cdots,x_{m}}\prod_{i=1}^{m}x_{i}^{\downarrow c_{i}}=N^{\downarrow c_{1}+\cdots+c_{m}}\binom{N-(c_{1}+\cdots+c_{m})}{x_{1}-c_{1},\cdots,x_{m}-c_{m}}
. Let
x_{i}^{\prime}=x_{i}-c_{i}
. We have

\displaystyle\sum_{x_{1}+\cdots+x_{m}=N}\binom{N}{x_{1},x_{2},\cdots,x_{m}}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{m}p_{i}^{x_{i}}\right)\prod_{i=1}^{m}x_{i}^{\downarrow c_{i}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle N^{\downarrow c_{1}+\cdots+c_{m}}\sum_{x_{1}+\cdots+x_{m}=N}\binom{N-(c_{1}+c_{2}+\cdots+c_{m})}{x_{1}-c_{1},x_{2}-c_{2},\cdots,x_{m}-c_{m}}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{m}p_{i}^{x_{i}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle N^{\downarrow c_{1}+\cdots+c_{m}}\prod_{i=1}^{m}p_{i}^{c_{i}}\sum_{x_{1}^{\prime}+\cdots+x_{m}^{\prime}=N-(c_{1}+\cdots+c_{m})}\binom{N-(c_{1}+c_{2}+\cdots+c_{m})}{x_{1}^{\prime},x_{2}^{\prime},\cdots,x_{m}^{\prime}}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{m}p_{i}^{x_{i}^{\prime}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle N^{\downarrow c_{1}+\cdots+c_{m}}\prod_{i=1}^{m}p_{i}^{c_{i}}\left(p_{1}+\cdots+p_{m}\right)^{N-(c_{1}+\cdots+c_{m})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle N^{\downarrow c_{1}+\cdots+c_{m}}\prod_{i=1}^{m}p_{i}^{c_{i}}.\qed

For a general polynomial

to compute the expectation. For convenience, we list quadratic calculations below.

Lemma 43

\displaystyle m_{\{\lambda_{1},\cdots,\lambda_{l-1}\}}(\bm{p})s_{\lambda_{l}}(\bm{p})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i_{1},\cdots,i_{l-1}\in[m],\text{distinct}}\sum_{i_{l}}p_{i_{1}}^{\lambda_{1}}\cdots p_{i_{l}}^{\lambda_{l}}

\displaystyle=m_{\lambda}(\bm{p})+\sum_{i_{1},\cdots,i_{l-1}\in[m],\text{distinct}}\sum_{j=1}^{l-1}p_{i_{1}}^{\lambda_{1}}\cdots p_{i_{l-1}}^{\lambda_{l-1}}\cdot p_{i_{j}}^{\lambda_{l}}

\displaystyle=m_{\lambda}(\bm{p})+\sum_{j=1}^{l-1}m_{\lambda^{j\leftarrow l}}(\bm{p}),

(59)

where
\lambda^{j\leftarrow l}\coloneqq\{\lambda_{1},\cdots,\lambda_{j-1},\lambda_{j}+\lambda_{l},\lambda_{j+1},\cdots,\lambda_{l-1}\}
is a partition of length
l-1
obtained by absorbing
\lambda_{l}
into
\lambda_{j}
. By induction hypothesis,
m_{\lambda^{j\leftarrow l}}(\bm{p})
is
(-1)^{l-2}(l-2)!s_{t}(\bm{p})
plus some polynomials in
s_{1},\cdots,s_{t-1}
. The left-hand side
m_{{\lambda_{1},\cdots,\lambda_{l-1}}}s_{\lambda_{l}}(\bm{p})
is also a polynomial in
s_{1},\cdots,s_{t-1}
. Therefore,
m_{\lambda}(\bm{p})
is
-(l-1)\times(-1)^{l-2}(l-2)!s_{t}(\bm{p})=(-1)^{l-1}(l-1)!s_{t}(\bm{p})
plus some polynomials in
s_{1},\cdots,s_{t-1}
.
∎

7.3
Proof of Lemma
36

Lemma
36
.

For
k\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
, there exists two probability distributions
\{p_{r}\}_{r=1}^{k+1}
and
\{q_{i}\}_{r=1}^{k+1}
such that
\sum_{r=1}^{k+1}p_{i}^{m}=\sum_{r=1}^{k+1}q_{i}^{m}
for all
0\leq m\leq k
, and
\sum_{r=1}^{k+1}p_{i}^{k+1}-\sum_{r=1}^{k+1}q_{i}^{k+1}=\frac{2}{2^{k}(k+1)^{k}}
.

To prove the lemma, we introduce the Chebyshev polynomial.
Let
T_{k+1}(x)
be the
(k+1)
-th Chebyshev polynomial of the first kind, namely the polynomial that satisfies
T_{k+1}(\cos\theta)=\cos((k+1)\theta)
.
T_{k+1}(x)
has the following properties:

1.

When
x\in[-1,1]
,
T_{k+1}(x)\in[-1,1]
.

2.

The leading two terms of
T_{k+1}(x)
is
2^{k}x^{k+1}+0x^{k}
.

3.

Equation
T_{k+1}(x)=1
has
k+1
roots in
[-1,1]
. When
k+1
is even, the roots are
\cos\frac{2r\pi}{k+1}
for
r=0,1,\cdots,(k+1)/2
, with multiplicity
1
for
r=0,(k+1)
and multiplicity
2
for other
r
. When
k+1
is odd, the roots are
\cos\frac{2r\pi}{k+1}
for
r=0,1,\cdots,k/2
, with multiplicity
1
for
r=0
and multiplicity
2
for other
r
.

4.

Equation
T_{k+1}(x)=-1
has
k+1
roots in
[-1,1]
. When
k+1
is even, the roots are
\cos\frac{(2r+1)\pi}{k+1}
for
r=0,1,\cdots,(k-1)/2
with multiplicity 2 for all
r
. When
k+1
is odd, the roots are
\cos\frac{(2r+1)\pi}{k+1}
for
r=0,1,\cdots,k/2
, with multiplicity 1 for
r=k/2
and multiplicity
2
for other
r
.

Proof of
Lemma

36
.

We rescale
T_{k+1}
by defining
T(x)\coloneqq\delta T_{k+1}((k+1)(x-1))
, where
\delta=\frac{1}{2^{k}(k+1)^{k+1}}
, so that

1.

The leading two terms of
T(x)
is
x^{k+1}-x^{k}
,

2.

Both
T(x)-\delta
and
T(x)+\delta
have
(k+1)
nonnegative roots.

Let
\{p_{r}\}_{r=1}^{k+1}
and
\{q_{r}\}_{r=1}^{k+1}
be roots of
T(x)-\delta
and
T(x)+\delta
, respectively. By Vieta’s formulas and the properties above,
\{p_{r}\}_{r=1}^{k+1},\{q_{r}\}_{r=1}^{k+1}
are probability distributions such that all elementary symmetric polynomials up to degree
k
are the same. By Newton’s identities, it implies that
\sum_{r=1}^{k+1}p_{r}^{m}=\sum_{r=1}^{k+1}q_{r}^{m}
for all
0\leq m\leq k
. The difference between
(k+1)
-th moments can be bounded by

\sum_{r=1}^{k+1}p_{r}^{k+1}-\sum_{r=1}^{k+1}q_{r}^{k+1}=\sum_{r=1}^{k+1}(T(p_{r})-T(q_{r}))=2(k+1)\delta.

(60)

This completes the proof the lemma. Moreover, since
-1
is the root of either
T_{k+1}(x)=-1
or
T_{k+1}(x)=1
, one of
\{p_{r}\}_{r=1}^{k+1}
and
\{q_{r}\}_{r=1}^{k+1}
contains a zero entry. And the smallest nonzero entropy is

\frac{1-\cos\frac{\pi}{k+1}}{k+1}=\frac{2}{k+1}\sin^{2}\frac{\pi}{2(k+1)}\geq\frac{2}{(k+1)^{3}},

(61)

where we use the fact
\sin x\geq 2x/\pi
for
x\in[0,\pi/2]
.
∎

7.4
Proof of Lemma
37

To ease the notation, here we replace
k+1
in the original lemma by
k
.

Lemma
37
.

Let
O
be a
k
-qudit observable and
\Delta_{k}
be the operator defined in (
39
). Then we have

\absolutevalue{\sum_{\pi\in S_{k}^{\text{circ}}}c_\pi(O)-\tr(O\Delta_{k})}\leq\frac{k^{k-1}k!}{d}.

(62)

We rewrite the definition of
\Delta_{k}
here for convenience

\Delta_{k}\coloneqq\sum_{l=1}^{k}(-1)^{l-1}(l-1)!\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}B_{1},\cdots,B_{l}\text{ partition }[k]\\
\text{all nonempty}\end{subarray}}\tilde{S}^{B_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\tilde{S}^{B_{l}}.

We introduce Stirling numbers of the second kind,
S(k,l)
, defined as the number of ways to partition a set of
k
elements into
l
nonempty subsets. A useful property of
S(k,l)
is the polynomial identity
x^{k}=\sum_{l=1}^{k}S(k,l)x^{\downarrow l}
. So
x^{k-1}=\sum_{l=1}^{k}S(k,l)(x-1)^{\downarrow l-1}
. Setting
x=0
, we obtain that

\sum_{l=0}^{k}S(k,l)(-1)^{l-1}(l-1)!=\begin{cases}1,&k=1\\
0,&k>1\end{cases}.

(63)

Proof of
Lemma

37
.

By definition of
\Delta_{k+1}
,
(U^{\dagger})^{\otimes(k+1)}\Delta_{k+1}U^{\otimes(k+1)}=\Delta_{k+1}
. So

\mathrm{tr}(O\Delta_{k+1})=\mathop{\mathbb{E}\/}_{U\leftarrow\mu_{\text{H}}}[\mathrm{tr}(U^{\otimes(k+1)}O(U^{\dagger})^{\otimes(k+1)}\Delta_{k+1})]=\sum_{\pi}c_{\pi}(O)\mathrm{tr}(\Delta_{k+1}\pi).

(64)

The goal is to show that
\mathrm{tr}(\Delta_{k}\pi)\approx 1
if
\pi\in S_{k+1}^{\text{circ}}
and
\mathrm{tr}(\Delta_{k}\pi)\approx 0
otherwise.
Fix a
\pi
, to calculate
\mathrm{tr}(\Delta_{k}\pi)
, we divide terms
B_{1},\cdots,B_{l}
in
\Delta_{k}
into two cases.

Case 1:
There exists an
i\in[k]
such that
i
and
\pi(i)
are in different parts of the partition, say,
i\in B_{r_{1}}
and
\pi(i)\in B_{r_{2}}
for
r_{1}\neq r_{2}
.

Apparently, such
i
cannot be unique. So there is another
j\in[k]
such that
j
and
\pi(j)
are in different parts, say,
j\in B_{r_{3}}
and
\pi(j)\in B_{r_{4}}
for
r_{3}\neq r_{4}
.
In this case

\displaystyle\absolutevalue{\tr(\tilde{S}^{B_1}\otimes\cdots\otimes\tilde{S}^{B_{l}}\cdot\pi)}

\displaystyle\leq\mathop{\mathbb{E}\/}_{\psi_{1},\cdots\psi_{l}\leftarrow\mu_{\text{H}}}[\absolutevalue{\tr(\psi_1^{\otimes B_1}\otimes\cdots\psi_l^{\otimes B_l}\cdot\pi)}]\leq\mathop{\mathbb{E}\/}[\absolutevalue{\braket{\psi_{r_1}}{\psi_{r_2}}\braket{\psi_{r_3}}{\psi_{r_4}}}].

(65)

If
\{r_{1},r_{2}\}=\{r_{3},r_{4}\}
, then the RHS is just
\frac{1}{d}
. If
\{r_{1},r_{2}\}\cap\{r_{3},r_{4}\}=\varnothing
, then by Cauchy’s inequality, the RHS is

\mathop{\mathbb{E}\/}[\absolutevalue{\braket{\psi_{r_1}}{\psi_{r_2}}}]\cdot\mathop{\mathbb{E}\/}[\absolutevalue{\braket{\psi_{r_3}}{\psi_{r_4}}}]\leq\sqrt{\mathop{\mathbb{E}\/}[\absolutevalue{\braket{\psi_{r_1}}{\psi_{r_2}}}^{2}]}\cdot\sqrt{\mathop{\mathbb{E}\/}[\absolutevalue{\braket{\psi_{r_3}}{\psi_{r_4}}}^{2}]}=\frac{1}{d}.

If
\absolutevalue{\{r_1,r_2\}\cap\{r_3, r_4\}}=1
, say
r_{1}=r_{3}
, then by Cauchy’s inequality, the RHS of (
65
) is at most

\sqrt{\mathop{\mathbb{E}\/}_{\psi_{r_{1}}}\mathop{\mathbb{E}\/}_{\psi_{r_{2}},\psi_{r_{4}}}[\absolutevalue{\braket{\psi_{r_1}}{\psi_{r_2}}}^{2}\cdot\absolutevalue{\braket{\psi_{r_1}}{\psi_{r_4}}}^{2}]}=\sqrt{\mathop{\mathbb{E}\/}_{\psi_{r_{1}}}\frac{1}{d^{2}}}=\frac{1}{d}.

In summary, the RHS of (
65
) is always at most
1/d
. So the total contribution of this case is at most

\frac{1}{d}\sum_{l=1}^{k}(l-1)!S(k,l)\leq\frac{1}{d}\sum_{l=1}^{k}(k-1)^{\downarrow l-1}S(k,l)=\frac{k^{k-1}}{d}.

(66)

Case 2:

i
and
\pi(i)
are in the same part for every
i
.

The cycle decomposition of
\pi
gives a partition of
[k]
, denoted by
A_{1},\cdots,A_{t}
. Case 2 happens if and only if each
A_{j}
is contained in one of
B_{j}
. The number of such partitions

(the number of ways to divide

into
l
parts). For each such partition

(67)

So the total contribution of this case is

\sum_{l=1}^{k}(-1)^{l-1}(l-1)!S(t,l)=\begin{cases}1,&t=1\\
0,&t>1\end{cases}.

(68)

Here

if and only if
\pi\in S_{k}^{\text{circ}}
.
In summary, we have shown that, up to error

and

7.5
Proof of Lemma
41

Lemma
41
.

Let
\bm{a}=(a_{1},\cdots,a_{m})
be a spectrum and
\psi_{1},\cdots,\psi_{m}
be
m
Haar random states. With probability at least
0.99
,
\rho\coloneqq\sum_{r=1}^{m}a_{r}\psi_{r}
is
\mathcal{O}(m\sqrt{\ln m}/\sqrt{d})
-close to a state with spectrum
\bm{a}
in trace distance.

Proof.

Let
\Psi
be a
d\times m
matrix whose
r
-th column is
\ket{\psi_r}
. Let
A_{0}
be the
m\times m
diagonal matrix with diagonal
\bm{a}
. Then
\rho=\Psi A_{0}\Psi^{\dagger}
.
Define the Gram matrix
G\coloneqq\Psi^{\dagger}\Psi
.

\norm{G-\Id}_{F}^{2}=\sum_{i\neq j}\absolutevalue{\braket{\psi_i}{\psi_j}}^{2}.

(69)

By
Lemma

24
and union bound, with probability at least
1-2m^{2}\exp(-d\delta/2)
,
\norm{G-\Id}_{F}^{2}\leq\frac{m^{2}}{d}+m^{2}\delta
. Setting
\delta=\frac{2\ln(200m^2)}{d}
, we obtain that with probability at least
0.99
,
\norm{G-\Id}_{F}^{2}=\mathcal{O}(\frac{m^{2}\ln m}{d})
and
\norm{G-\Id}_{F}=\mathcal{O}(m\sqrt{\ln m}/\sqrt{d})

Write the
QR
decomposition
\Psi=QR
, where
Q
is a
d\times d
unitary and
R
is a
d\times m
upper triangular matrix. Since
d>m
,
R
consists of a
m\times m
upper triangular matrix
R_{0}
and a
(d-m)\times m
zero matrix. Since
G=\Psi^{\dagger}\Psi=R^{\dagger}R=R_{0}^{\dagger}R_{0}
and
R_{0}
is upper triangular,
R_{0}^{\dagger}R_{0}
is the Cholesky decomposition of
G
. According to perturbation bounds for Cholesky decomposition
[
64
, Theorem 1.1]
,
\norm{R_0-\Id_m}_{F}\leq\sqrt{2}\norm{G-\Id_m}_{F}
.

Let
A
be the
d\times d
diagonal matrix with diagonal
(a_{1},\cdots,a_{m},0,0,\cdots)
. Define
\sigma\coloneqq QAQ^{\dagger}
.
Then
\sigma
is a state with spectrum
\bm{a}
.
\norm{\rho-\sigma}_{1}=\norm{Q(RA_0R^\dagger-A)Q^\dagger}_{1}=\norm{RA_0R^\dagger-A}_{1}=\norm{R_0A_0R_0^\dagger-A_0}_{1}
. Write
E=R_{0}-\mathds{1}
. By Holder’s inequality
\norm{XY}_{1}\leq\norm{X}_{F}\norm{Y}_{F}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\norm{R_0A_0R_0^\dagger-A_0}_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\norm{EA_0}_{1}+\norm{A_0E}_{1}+\norm{EA_0E}_{1}

(triangle inequality)

\displaystyle\leq 2\norm{E}_{F}\norm{A_0}_{F}+\norm{EA_0}_{F}\norm{E}_{F}

(Holder’s inequality)

\displaystyle\leq 2\norm{E}_{F}+\norm{E}_{F}^{2},

(
\norm{A_0}_{F}\leq 1
,
\norm{EA_0}_{F}\leq\norm{A_0}_{F}
)

where
\norm{EA_0}_{F}\leq\norm{A_0}_{F}
because
A_{0}
is a diagonal matrix with all entries in
[0,1]
. Hence, with probability at least
0.99
,
\norm{\rho-\sigma}_{1}\leq\mathcal{O}(m\sqrt{\ln m}/\sqrt{d})
.
∎
