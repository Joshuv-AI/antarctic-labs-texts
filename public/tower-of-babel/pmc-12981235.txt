Effects of group trauma-sensitive yoga on inflammatory markers and psychological well-being in women veterans with PTSD: A randomized controlled trial☆
Authors: Belle Zaccari, Ursula A Kelly, Travis I Lovejoy, Kimberly Hubbard, Aurora Newman, Jennifer M Loftis
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:Lifetime prevalence of post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) is double among veterans compared to civilians and women compared to men. Inflammatory factors are increasingly implicated in symptoms of PTSD. Yoga shows promise to reduce psychological symptoms of PTSD and positively impact inflammatory responses. The present study aimed to examine the effectiveness of yoga to reduce symptoms of PTSD and depression in addition to investigating the impact of treatment on inflammatory markers in women veterans with PTSD secondary to military sexual trauma.Methods:We collected dried blood spot samples, self-report and clinician administered measures of PTSD, and self-reported depression symptoms at baseline, 2 weeks, and 3 months post-intervention from a subset of women veterans diagnosed with PTSD (N = 27) who were randomized to either Trauma Center Trauma-Sensitive Yoga (TCTSY; a movement therapy) or cognitive processing therapy (CPT; a talk therapy) as part of a larger multisite RCT. Concentrations of interleukin (IL)-6, IL-10, and C-reactive protein (CRP) were measured using multiplex bead-based immunoassay at baseline and post-intervention (2 weeks and 3 months). Generalized estimating equations examined changes in symptoms of PTSD, depression, and inflammatory markers over time. We hypothesized decreases in IL-6 and CRP and increases in IL-10 in TCTSY participants compared to CPT participants and that PTSD and depression symptoms would improve over time in both groups.Results:From baseline to 3 months post-intervention, IL-6 (β = 0.10, p < 0.05), IL-10 (β = 0.68, p < 0.05), and CRP (β = 0.77, p < 0.05) increased in TCTSY participants relative to those randomized to CPT. PTSD and depression symptoms reduced in both groups over time (CAPS-5 β = −3.96, PCL-5 β = −4.66, and BDI-II β = −2.70, all p < 0.05); groups did not differ in magnitude of symptom reduction.Conclusions:Findings indicate that TCTSY has the potential to improve symptoms of PTSD and depression and alter inflammatory markers. The findings are limited by our sample size and the immune factors we examined. Future directions for related research would benefit from measuring a wider array of stress response components.Keywords: Posttraumatic stress disorder, Trauma-sensitive yoga, Inflammatory markers, IL-6, IL-10, CRP, Cognitive processing therapy, Women veterans, Sexual trauma

Introduction

The lifetime prevalence of post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) among military populations is an estimated 7.7–17.0% (Schein et al., 2021). Women veterans are nearly twice as likely to develop PTSD as men (Schein et al., 2021). Military sexual trauma in women veterans is associated with greater PTSD and related mental health symptoms (Webermann et al., 2023; Sumner et al., 2021). Elevated markers of inflammation (e.g., interleukin-6 [IL-6], C-reactive protein [CRP]) are associated with PTSD and have long-term effects that further exacerbate PTSD symptoms (O’Donovan et al., 2015). Psychotherapy and medications are each recommended for PTSD improvement, (VA/DOD, 2023) though both have limitations in acceptability and uptake (Hoskins et al., 2015; Maguen et al., 2019). Elevated inflammation predicts treatment nonresponse, (Rhein et al., 2021; Renner et al., 2022) which suggests a need for anti-inflammatory treatment strategies. Yoga shows promise as a complementary and integrative health approach to improve PTSD and comorbid mental health symptoms such as depression. Psychoneuroimmunologic mechanisms have been theorized, including the potential that yoga might decrease the immunologic dysregulation associated with PTSD (Kelly et al., 2018, 2021; Zaccari et al., 2020).

Cytokines and other inflammatory factors have been examined for the role they play in PTSD and associated symptoms, like depression (Dell’Oste et al., 2023; Pivac et al., 2023). Prior research has demonstrated increased inflammation is associated with greater PTSD symptom severity, although some studies have shown inflammatory factors (e.g., IL-6, tumor necrosis factor-alpha [TNF-α], CRP) are similar or even lower in individuals with PTSD compared with controls (O’Donovan et al., 2015; McCanlies et al., 2011; Plantinga et al., 2013). Nevertheless, inflammatory factors IL-6, IL-1β, TNF-α, and CRP have been identified as key in understanding the relationship between inflammation and PTSD (de Oliveira et al., 2018; Hori and Kim, 2019; Passos et al., 2015). The most studied pro-inflammatory cytokine associated with PTSD is IL-6 (Hori and Kim, 2019); a meta-analysis on inflammatory markers in PTSD showed elevated IL-6 levels among individuals with PTSD compared to controls (Passos et al., 2015). Also, CRP, an acute-phase protein, is a marker of immune dysregulation and inflammation with a pattern of elevation among those with childhood trauma exposure and individuals with PTSD (O’Donovan et al., 2012; Eraly et al., 2014; Maples-Keller et al., 2022). A meta-analysis found similar levels of CRP between groups of individuals with PTSD and controls (Passos et al., 2015), but inconsistent results have been reported (Baumert et al., 2013). More recently, a large longitudinal study in women (n’s ranged from 628 to 2797) found that the combination of PTSD symptoms and depressed mood was associated with increasing CRP levels over time (Lawn et al., 2022). However, the relationship between anti-inflammatory markers (e.g., IL-10) and PTSD has been less conclusive and less empirical data exist. In a cross-sectional study, IL-10 showed significant elevation among those with PTSD compared to healthy controls (de Oliveira et al., 2018). Importantly, higher levels of IL-10 before treatment for PTSD is associated with improved outcomes, including general symptom burden and less depressive symptoms (Renner et al., 2022).

Complementary and integrative health approaches, such as yoga, have emerged as desirable, feasible interventions for PTSD that have additional benefits to inflammatory responses (Kelly et al., 2018; Falkenberg et al., 2018; Yeun and Kim, 2021; Zaccari et al., 2022a). In yoga studies examining biological outcomes, treatment response has been measured in the sympathetic nervous system, HPA-axis, and inflammatory response system with mixed findings (Kelly et al., 2018; Zaccari et al., 2020; Falkenberg et al., 2018; Yeun and Kim, 2021; Nguyen-Feng et al., 2019; Oka et al., 2019). There is evidence that yoga positively impacts the inflammatory response system, (Rajbhoj, 2015; Naderi et al., 2022) which has implications for mental health conditions, such as depression, anxiety, and PTSD (Falkenberg et al., 2018; Yeun and Kim, 2021). A systematic review found that IL-6, IL-1β, and TNF-α decreased in RCTs that measured these pro-inflammatory cytokines when yoga interventions were provided to healthy individuals (Falkenberg et al., 2018). Conversely, patients who received 12 weeks of inpatient psychiatric treatment showed increased pro-inflammatory cytokines alongside decreased symptoms of psychological distress indicating a unique pattern of immunological change among traumatized populations engaged in recovery (Toft et al., 2018).

The objectives of this RCT were to examine the effectiveness of Trauma Center Trauma-Sensitive Yoga (TCTSY) compared to a first-line psychotherapy (cognitive processing therapy; CPT) to reduce symptoms of PTSD and associated symptoms, including depression. We also sought to investigate the impact of treatment on pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory markers in a sample of women veterans with PTSD secondary to military sexual trauma. In this sub-analysis, we hypothesized that participants who received TCTSY would show (1) decreases in PTSD and depression over time, commensurate with those who participated in CPT, and (2) greater decreases in IL-6 and CRP (pro-inflammatory factors) and increases in IL-10 (anti-inflammatory factor) over time compared to those who participated in CPT.

Materials and methods

Design

This study included an initial subsample of participants (n = 28) from a single site of a multi-site RCT that compared the effectiveness of TCTSY to CPT in women veterans with PTSD secondary to military sexual trauma (Fig. 1). Our sample included the participants of a cohort that used the dried blood spot collection method described below. As such, a sample size was determined by the methods rather than calculated a priori.

CONSORT flow diagram. a One participant who completed TCTSY was discovered to have a history of a moderate traumatic brain injury, and therefore was enrolled in error.

In this RCT, we recruited from a Pacific Northwestern US VA Health Care System. An in-depth description of the larger RCT study design, recruitment, consent/enrollment, data collection, interventions, and their rationale are published elsewhere (Kelly et al., 2021; Zaccari et al., 2022b). For participants in this subsample, all study procedures and interventions were delivered virtually due to the COVID-19 pandemic (Zaccari et al., 2022b).

The research was carried out in accordance with The Code of Ethics of the World Medical Association (Declaration of Helsinki) and approved by the VA Institutional Review Board (IRB) and relevant research oversight committees. Informed consent was obtained from all study participants.

Participants

We included women veterans diagnosed with PTSD secondary to military sexual trauma. We excluded women veterans who were engaged in trauma-focused treatment, yoga practice, or any treatment/intervention at odds with the study interventions, with moderate or severe traumatic brain injuries, active suicidal ideation with intent or plan, medical conditions that can contribute significantly to psychiatric symptoms (e.g. hypo/hyper-thyroidism, kidney or liver failure) or symptoms of psychosis that precluded meaningful engagement in the study intervention and assessments. We requested at least 2 weeks of stability following changes to medications and tracked medication changes at each assessment timepoint. Following delivery of all interventions and assessments, one participant disclosed status of a traumatic brain injury, which was a study exclusion criterion. This participant’s data were retained for intent-to-treat analyses. Additional sensitivity analyses were conducted excluding this participant’s data. See Statistical Analysis section for additional details.

Participants were recruited between December 2019 and June 2020 via self-referral, provider referral, or chart review of the roster of recent mental health appointments conducted by clinicians in relevant clinics. Groups were expected to begin in spring 2020; however, due to the COVID-19 pandemic, this study was amended to be conducted virtually, delaying the start of groups until summer 2020. Participants were phone screened, consented, and then completed an in-depth assessment to determine study eligibility, prior to study enrollment. Enrolled participants completed a baseline assessment and then were randomized to TCTSY or CPT using block randomization generated by the study statistician, stratified by site, using PASS v.15 (NCSS, LLC, 2017). Intervention allocation was determined by a randomization schedule; group assignment was communicated to participants by a study team member via a phone call.

Interventions

Group interventions were conducted between July and September 2020 via video teleconferencing. Interventions were delivered by two facilitators and a third non-facilitating study team member present to assist participants with technological issues that arose. Treatment fidelity was monitored using methods described in Zaccari et al. (2022a).

Trauma Center Trauma-Sensitive Yoga (TCTSY)

TCTSY utilizes Hatha yoga style which employs physical forms and movement, and emphasizes interoception, choice, and experiences of empowerment. TCTSY was developed for women with complex trauma, specifically childhood sexual abuse. Ten sessions of group TCTSY were provided in weekly, 60–75 minute sessions. Interventions were co-facilitated by two registered yoga teachers certified in TCTSY.

Cognitive processing therapy (CPT)

CPT is an evidence-based cognitive behavioral trauma-processing therapy. Twelve sessions of group CPT were provided in weekly, 90-minute sessions co-facilitated by two certified VA psychologists.

Procedures and outcomes

Data were collected at baseline, 2 weeks post-intervention, and at 3 months post-intervention. The data collection included a structured diagnostic interview for PTSD (Clinician Administered PTSD Scale for DSM-5; CAPS-5) administered by a trained assessor, self-report measures of PTSD (PTSD Checklist for DSM-5; PCL-5) and depression (Beck Depression Inventory-Second Edition; BDI-II) collected via REDCap online survey, and inflammatory markers (IL-6, IL-10, CRP) measured via dried blood spots. To avoid in-person contact, all mental health data were collected virtually using online survey. Dried blood spots were self-collected by participants who were guided by a study team member either by phone or video teleconference. Data collection methods for PTSD, depression symptoms, and inflammatory markers are described in greater detail in the following subsections.

Structured diagnostic interviews

The CAPS-5 is a gold standard diagnostic instrument for assessing PTSD (Patel and Youssef, 2022). The CAPS-5 is a 30-item structured interview that yields a continuous measure (score range 0–80) of PTSD symptom severity over the past month (Weathers et al., 2018).

Self-reported PTSD symptoms

The PCL-5 is a 20-item self-report measure (score range 0–80) designed to assess trauma symptoms and monitor symptom change over time (Blevins et al., 2015). The PCL-5 has demonstrated excellent psychometrics within the veteran population (Bovin et al., 2016).

Self-reported depression symptoms

The BDI-II is a 21-item, self-report measure designed to assess depression symptoms. Higher scores indicate more severe depressive symptoms (score range 0–63) (Beck et al., 1996).

Dried blood spot collection

Dried blood spots (DBS) were collected using methods previously described (Zaccari et al., 2022b). A finger stick blood collection method was used during the baseline and post-intervention (2 weeks and 3 months) assessments. Supplies for DBS collection, return shipping materials, and postage were mailed to participants prior to their virtual data collection visit. Participants were provided written and verbal instructions to collect their blood samples at home. Guided, self-collection of capillary whole blood involved cleaning a finger with isopropyl alcohol then using a sterile, single-use lancet (BD Microtainer™ Contact-Activated Lancet, Franklin Lakes, NJ) to obtain drops of blood that were dried on a filter card (Whatman #903, GE Healthcare, Piscataway, NJ). The lancet was designed to reduce pain while delivering a uniform and controlled puncture to stimulate capillary blood flow for up to five drops of blood (approximately 50 µL each) that were absorbed onto filter paper (Whatman #903, GE Healthcare, Piscataway, NJ). Up to three lancets were included in the data collection kits provided to participants as instances of lancet failure resulting in the finger not being punctured would occasionally occur. Additionally, as participants intermittently experienced difficulty stimulating blood flow, advice for blood collection was provided to participants (i.e., using gauze to wipe the puncture site, massaging the palm, running the finger under warm water prior to the puncture).

After collecting and drying the blood spots, the filter paper was stored in air-tight plastic bags with desiccant (VWR-61161 Humidity Sponges, Desiccator in a Bag, VWR, Radnor, PA) and returned to the study team by drive-through/drop-off or mail using appropriate COVID-19 guidelines and shipping requirements, respectively. Written instructions for participants provided guidance on properly repackaging biospecimens using shipping materials that were complaint with all Department of Transportation and International Air Transport Association guidelines for Category B specimens.

Inflammatory marker measurements

All DBS samples were stored at −20 °C until analysis. Samples were shipped on dry ice to AssayGate, Inc. (Ijamsville, MD, USA) for DBS protein extractions and immune factor measurements. Concentrations of IL-6, CRP, and IL-10 were measured using a multiplex bead-based immunoassay, as previously reported (Loftis et al., 2018). Given that the most studied pro-inflammatory cytokine associated with PTSD is IL-6, IL-6 was selected as opposed to TNF-α, for example. IL-10 was selected as the anti-inflammatory factor and CRP was selected for its putative role in PTSD (Pace and Heim, 2011).

Statistical analysis

The data were analyzed in SPSS V 29.0. To examine between-group changes in PTSD, depression, and inflammatory markers, we used generalized estimating equations (GEE), specifying model-based variance estimation and unstructured correlation matrices. We used intent-to-treat procedures and all available data to model the effects of Time (baseline, 2-week, and 3-month post-intervention), Treatment (TCTSY vs. CPT), and the Time X Treatment interaction on the PTSD and depression outcomes (CAPS-5, PCL-5, BDI-II total severity scores) and three inflammatory marker outcomes (IL-6, IL-10, CRP). GEE provides statistical estimates using all available data. Consistent with analyses of cytokine data, we applied natural log transformations to address non-normality (Lawn et al., 2022; Hou et al., 2019).

In addition to analyzing changes in circulating levels of single inflammatory markers, the relative balance of anti- and pro-inflammatory markers is also considered to be an important determinant of health (e.g. Karvonen-Gutierrez et al., 2017). We thus analyzed the ratios of IL-10 to IL-6 and IL-10 to CRP. All inferential analyses utilized 2-tailed tests of significance and α = 0.05. We conducted sensitivity analyses for all outcomes that excluded the one ineligible participant who reported a TBI history after all interventions and follow-up assessments were complete. Results were unchanged, and we thus present intent-to-treat findings below.

Results

Following delivery of all interventions and assessments, one participant disclosed status of a traumatic brain injury resulting in removal of her data from analysis, yielding a final analytic sample of 27. Participants on average were 47.5 years old (SD = 11.7 years). Most participants were white (89%) and non-Hispanic (85%). Nearly all participants (93%) had completed some college, and 82% had monthly incomes greater than $2000. Approximately half of participants (49%) were partnered or in a relationship. Inflammatory marker changes over time

There were no significant changes in the total sample over time for IL-6 or IL-10 (Table 2; Fig. 2A, B). However, participants who received the TCTSY intervention had increases in both IL-6 and IL-10 over time, relative to participants who received the CPT intervention (IL-6 β = 0.68 [95% CI: 0.37, 1.00]; IL-10 β = 0.77 [95% CI: 0.11, 1.43]; Table 2 and Fig. 2A, B). CRP increased significantly from baseline to 3-month followup (β = 0.21 [95% CI: 0.20, 0.22]) and to a greater extent in participants who received the TCTSY intervention, relative to those who received the CPT intervention (β = 0.10 [95% CI: 0.09, 0.11]; Table 2; Fig. 2C).

PTSD and depression symptom changes over time

PTSD and depression symptoms reduced significantly in all participants over time (CAPS-5 β = −3.96 [95% CI: −6.63, −1.30]; PCL-5 β = −4.66 [95% CI: −7.90, −1.41]); BDI-II β = −2.70 [95% CI: − 5.34, − 0.05]; Table 3 and Fig. 3A–C). The observed reductions in symptoms were commensurate for both groups, as evidenced by non-significant interaction effects (Table 3; Fig. 3A–C).

Discussion

In this RCT, we compared changes in inflammatory markers, PTSD and depression symptoms before and after participation in a yoga intervention (TCTSY) or a CPT intervention in a sample of women veterans diagnosed with PTSD related to military sexual trauma. We found that levels of pro-inflammatory (IL-6 and CRP) and anti-inflammatory (IL-10) factors increased over time in women who received the yoga intervention, as compared to those who received CPT (Fig. 2A–C). Although increases in IL-6 and CRP were not hypothesized, the overall increase in the balance of anti- and pro-inflammatory factors (Fig. 2D) suggests some beneficial change in immune regulation (Karvonen-Gutierrez et al., 2017; Girndt et al., 2003). PTSD and depression symptoms reduced similarly over time in both the yoga and CPT groups, as hypothesized (Fig. 3A–C).

While other published studies have described similar methods, this is the first to publish findings that consider psychological and somatic aspects of mental health conditions and treatment effects using an RCT design comparing yoga to a gold standard psychotherapy for PTSD (Kelly et al., 2021). Of particular value, this study also demonstrates the feasibility of self-sampling methods for specimen collection.

This study contributes important findings to the wider body of literature examining the complex relationships between inflammatory factors and symptoms of PTSD and depression and changes to both following treatment. Our results indicated that pro- and anti-inflammatory factors increased following the yoga intervention, which was somewhat unexpected. A systematic review of yoga’s effects on changes to immune system functioning identified a general pattern of pro-inflammatory down-regulation following yoga (Falkenberg et al., 2018). Others have described methodological challenges associated with investigating yoga and inflammatory factors, particularly in the context of mental health comorbidities (Estevao, 2022). One RCT of women endorsing psychological distress that examined cytokine changes following a yoga intervention or waitlist control found no significant changes from baseline to post-intervention in both groups (n = 28) (Harkess et al., 2016). It has been suggested that there may be a distinct pattern of neuroimmune changes among traumatized populations during recovery (Toft et al., 2018).

Consistent with our hypothesis, PTSD and depression symptoms reduced among TCTSY participants, commensurate with CPT. A meta-analysis examining the effect of mind-body interventions (e.g. yoga, MBSR) on symptoms of PTSD, depression, and anxiety found large effect sizes for PTSD and depression following mind-body interventions. The studies included largely had “regular daily life” control conditions and the quality of the studies was low (Zhu et al., 2022). Similar findings were demonstrated in a meta-analysis examining yoga interventions specifically, with most studies including waitlist control conditions as comparators (Nguyen-Feng et al., 2019). In our sample of women veterans who received the yoga intervention, PTSD and depression symptoms reduced alongside increased cytokines, and one explanation for this may be the physical component of the yoga intervention. Physical interventions are known to have some inflammatory effects—depending on the intensity, duration, and frequency of the interventions. For example, both injurious and therapeutic stretching can induce aspects of inflammatory responses (Muñoz-Vergara et al., 2022).

Drawing from a wider perspective of the inflammatory stress response system (Fig. 4) (Bovin et al., 2016), Guilliams et al. (Guilliams and Edwards, 2010) described how low cortisol states, such as PTSD (Sbisa et al., 2023), result in increased production of pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g. IL-6) suppressing production of anti-inflammatory cytokines (e.g. IL-10). Elevated IL-6 and CRP over time have been found among women with PTSD and depression, compared to those without trauma or depression (Lawn et al., 2022). Further, in a large study of individuals with PTSD and depressive disorders, a decrease in IL-10 levels was found to be associated with the onset and exacerbation of depression symptoms (Ogłodek, 2022). It is possible that these findings bear some implication on the results of the immediate study. The increase in IL-10 levels that we observed may be associated with the decrease in depression symptoms reported in our study.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. The modest sample size included a subset of women US veterans with PTSD and a history of military sexual trauma from a single site of the full RCT, and findings may not generalize to non-veterans or persons with PTSD related to other traumas. Secondly, three inflammatory markers were selected for this RCT and did not include others that would warrant examination in this context (e.g., chemokines) (Pan et al., 2021), as well as other factors influencing immune response. Finally, given that our sample size was relatively small, the potential effects of demographic variables were not determined.

Conclusion

Yoga has been proposed as an alternative treatment for mental health disorders like PTSD and depression, has shown efficacy for reducing symptoms of these and other mental health conditions when compared with first-line psychotherapy treatments, and has shown steady increase in use among veterans (Kelly et al., 2021; Zaccari et al., 2020; Nguyen-Feng et al., 2019; Bayley et al., 2022; Taylor et al., 2023). We add to this promising literature and report for the first time that for women veterans with PTSD secondary to military sexual trauma, participation in a yoga intervention (TCTSY) was associated with changes to inflammatory markers and reductions in PTSD and depression symptoms. Future replication studies would benefit from measuring a broader array of biological components of the stress response system (Fig. 4) in order to better define immune regulation in the context of yoga and PTSD symptom improvement.
