Unraveling the role of Cullin3 of E3 ubiquitin ligase in salt stress tolerance via proteomics
Authors: Bagus Herwibawa, Chakkree Lekklar, Sittiruk Roytrakul, Toshiro Ito, Supachitra Chadchawan, Teerapong Buaboocha
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAn association of OsCUL3c with salt tolerance has been found by a genome-wide association studies (GWAS) in rice. However, its functions and mechanisms remain unexplored. We aimed to examine the role of Cullin3 of E3 ubiquitin ligase in salt stress responses.ResultsProteomic analyses were performed to compare the Atcul3a mutant and wild type (WT) plants under salt stress. Our results demonstrated that an Arabidopsis mutant of Cullin3a (Atcul3a), an OsCUL3c homolog, exhibits a more sensitive phenotype under salt stress than the WT. Differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) that were non-salt-responsive demonstrated an overrepresentation of Gene Ontology (GO) terms associated with photosynthesis. Conversely, salt-responsive DEPs exhibited enriched GO terms linked to responses to toxic substances and involvement in glutathione metabolism. The role of OsCUL3c in salt tolerance was further supported by its heterologous expression in Arabidopsis. We cloned the rice OsCUL3c gene and expressed it in Arabidopsis, both in WT and Atcul3a mutant backgrounds. Complementation and over-expression lines featuring OsCUL3c expression displayed a higher germination rate, enhanced growth, and elevated photosynthetic pigment content under salt stress compared to the mutant line. Over-representation analysis on the combined proteomic datasets revealed major processes affected by the Cullin3 mutation and overexpression, which included glutathione metabolism, carbon metabolism, photosynthesis, and translation. Upregulation of several key genes in glutathione metabolism and carbon metabolism; and downregulation of specific photosynthetic and ribosomal proteins indicate a shift toward enhanced oxidative defense and metabolic reprogramming under salt stress conditions.ConclusionsThese findings highlight Cullin3 as a key salt tolerance regulator by coordinating multiple pathways.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07993-7.

Background

Saline and sodic soils, which affect approximately 1 billion ha of land globally, contribute to annual agricultural losses exceeding USD 27.3 billion [1]. Salt stress disrupts ion homeostasis, hinders nutrient uptake, increases reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels, and damages membranes and cellular structures [2], which can lead to damaged protein accumulation, compromising plant health [3]. The ubiquitin-proteasome system (UPS) plays a crucial role in selectively degrading damaged proteins and regulating plant stress responses [4]. The UPS is a complex proteolytic machinery composed of several proteins and enzymes, including ubiquitin (Ub), ubiquitin activating enzyme (E1), ubiquitin conjugation enzyme (E2), ubiquitin ligase enzyme (E3), deubiquitinating enzymes (DUB), and the 26S proteasome [5]. A key component is the E3 ubiquitin ligase, which functions as either a single protein or a multisubunit complex [6]. The four main E3 ligases are HECT, RING, U-box, and Cullin-RING ligases (CRLs). CRLs comprise various subtypes, including SCF, CUL3-BTB, CUL4-DDB, and APC [7]. Although SCF complexes have been extensively studied in plants [8–11], recent studies have highlighted the increasing importance of CUL3-BTB complexes [12, 13].

Cullin (CUL) proteins are essential components of the CRL complexes in plants [14]. These complexes include Cullin1, Cullin3 (subtypes 3a, 3b, and 3c), and Cullin4 [15–18]. Each CUL protein serves as a scaffold to facilitate the assembly of the multi-subunit CRL complex. These complexes function as E3 ubiquitin ligases responsible for recruiting target proteins for ubiquitination [19]. In CUL3, the BTB domain-containing adaptor proteins act as bridges, linking CUL3 to the substrate within a single polypeptide chain [20]. Simultaneously, ring box proteins (RBX1/RBX2) perform this role in the CRL, establishing interactions with the E2 enzyme, and ultimately enabling the addition of ubiquitin to the target protein [21]. Upon building a polyubiquitin chain on the target protein, the ubiquitinated protein is degraded via the 26 S proteasome [6].

There are two CUL3 genes in Arabidopsis thaliana, AtCUL3a and AtCUL3b. Single mutants, Atcul3a or Atcul3b, did not show any significant growth issues compared to the wild type; however, a double mutant, Atcul3a/b, caused embryo death, indicating that AtCUL3a and AtCUL3b have significant overlapping functions [16, 17]. Rice (Oryza sativa L.) contains three CUL3 genes, OsCUL3a, OsCUL3b, and OsCUL3c [18, 22, 23]. Previous reports have shown that a mutation in OsCUL3a leads to cell death, suggesting that OsCUL3a negatively regulates cell death and immunity by degrading OsNPR1 via the 26 S proteasome [24–26]. It has also been reported that OsCUL3b influences the number of crown roots [23]. Furthermore, based on a genome-wide association study (GWAS), we found that OsCUL3c is one of the candidate genes associated with the salt stress response, containing significant SNPs in the promoter and exon regions related to the number of panicles per plant under salt stress [27] and reported amino acid substitutions and an OsCUL3c haplotype potentially associated with the salt stress response [28]. Notably, salt stress causes significant upregulation of all OsCUL3 genes in both shoots and roots [22]. However, the precise mechanisms by which OsCUL3 proteins contribute to the response to salt stress remain unclear, and the role of ubiquitination and deubiquitination mediated by DUB function in regulating salt stress responses remains an open question [29].

Here, we aimed to examine the role of Cullin3c of E3 ubiquitin ligase in salt stress responses. We cloned the rice OsCUL3c gene and expressed it in Arabidopsis, both in wild-type and Atcul3a mutant backgrounds and subjected them to proteomic analyses. The findings of the molecular mechanisms regulated by OsCUL3c not only broaden our understanding of the adaptive capability of plants mediated by the ubiquitin-proteasome system in response to salt stress but also facilitate further studies for improving salt tolerance in plants.

Methods

Plant materials and growth condition

The Arabidopsis ecotype Columbia (Col-0) was used as wild type (WT), and Atcul3a mutant germplasm (SALK_050756) was obtained from the Arabidopsis Biological Resource Center (ABRC). Two independent lines expressing OsCUL3c in a WT background (CW-L16 and CW-L19) and two independent lines expressing OsCUL3c in an Atcul3a mutant background (CC-L5 and CC-L6) were used. The seeds were decontaminated with 1 ml ethanol 70% (v/v) for 1 min, 1 ml NaOCl 3% (v/v) for 13 min and rinsed with 1 ml sterile water five times. Seeds were grown in 25 ml of Murashige and Skoog (MS) medium containing 0.8% (w/v) agar and sucrose 1% (w/v). After stratification at 4 °C for 2 days, seeds were moved to the growth room at 22 °C under 16 h-light/8 h-dark photoperiod for 7 days and the seedings were then moved to an 8-cm pot containing peat moss, perlite, and vermiculite. Seeds were then harvested and stored at 4 °C.

Cloning of OsCUL3c

Arabidopsis lines expressing OsCUL3c were generated using recombinant plasmids containing the gene under the control of a 35SCaMV promoter. Specific primers were designed (CACCATGAGTTCCAGGAAGAAGCC, and TTATGCAAGATACCGGTACAGTTT), based on the Phytozome v12.1 database (https://phytozome-next.jgi.doe.gov/) for amplification of the OsCUL3c CDS, with a sequence of CACC nucleotide added into 5′ from forward primer to facilitate the directed cloning. The amplified product was purified using a TIANquick Mini Purification Kit (TIANGEN, China) and cloned into pENTR/D-TOPO (Invitrogen, USA). Competent cells (Escherichia coli DH5α) were transformed with the construct using the heat shock method. Plasmid DNA was isolated using the TIANprep Mini Plasmid Kit (TIANGEN, China). The entry clone was then subcloned into pGWB2 (addgene; https://www.addgene.org/), which carries a CaMV 35 S promoter for constitutive gene expression and a hygromycin resistance gene for plant selection, using the Gateway LR Clonase Enzyme mix (Invitrogen, USA).

Construction of transgenic Arabidopsis

Agrobacterium tumefaciens GV3101 competent cells were transformed with the OsCUL3c expression clone. Arabidopsis was transformed by floral dipping [30] in both the WT and Atcul3a mutant backgrounds, resulting in CW and CC lines, respectively. Selection of T1 seeds was performed using the MS agar containing 50 µg/ml hygromycin. Subsequent generations of seeds were generated until homozygous lines were obtained. Finally, four independent lines, CW-L16 and CW-L19 (WT background), and CC-L5 and CC-L6 (Atcul3a mutant background), were generated. Gene-specific primers for conventional PCR (Table S1) and RT-qPCR (Table S2) were used to verify selected lines.

Phenotypic analysis

For the agar plate system, seeds were surface-sterilized as described and sown on MS agar supplemented with or without 150 mM NaCl. After stratification at 4 °C for 2 days, the germinated seeds were cultured at 22 °C under a 16 h-light/8 h-dark photoperiod. Five-day-old seedlings were transferred to MS agar supplemented with or without 150 mM NaCl and grown under the same conditions for 7 days. For the hydroponic system (Fig. S1), seeds were surface-sterilized and then germinated on half-strength MS agar with stratification at 4 °C for 3 days. The resulting seedings were grown in a growth room at 25 °C under 16 h light/8 h dark photoperiod. When the seedlings reached 15 days old, the salt stress treatment was initiated by supplementing the nutrient solutions A and B [31] with or without 200 mM NaCl for 4 days. Root length (RL) was measured using ImageJ (https://imagej.nih.gov/ij/) . For both agar plate and hydroponic systems, germination rate (GR), fresh weight (FW), and dry weight (DW) were measured.

Photosynthetic pigment quantification

For photosynthetic pigment (chlorophyll a, chlorophyll b, total chlorophyll, and carotenoid) measurements, the plants grown on MS agar plates were treated with 120 mM NaCl for 5 days, Chlorophyll a, chlorophyll b, and carotenoid were extracted with 80% acetone from leaf tissue. Their concentrations were then measured spectrophotometrically at 470 nm, 646.9 nm, and 663.2 nm, respectively [32].

Statistical analysis

The experimental setup followed a Randomized Complete Block Design (RCBD) with three biological replicates for each treatment group. All phenotypic data were subjected to statistical analysis using R 4.3.0 (https://cran.r-project.org/). The Agricolae package [33] was used for post-hoc analysis. Differences between treatments and genotypes were evaluated using one-way ANOVA. A p-value less than 0.05 (p < 0.05) was considered to indicate statistical significance.

Transcriptomic analysis

Germinated seeds were grown on MS agar and 7-day-old seedlings were transferred to an MS agar supplemented with or without 150 mM NaCl. After 1 day of salt stress, total RNA was isolated from the aerial part of the plant using TRI reagent (Molecular Research Center, USA) and quantified using a Qubit Fluorometer (Invitrogen, USA). mRNA was isolated using a Dynabeads mRNA purification kit (Invitrogen) and converted to cDNA using SuperScript III (Invitrogen). The cDNA was fragmented using dsDNA fragmentase (New England Biolabs, UK). DNA fragments of approximately 300 bp were selected and purified using the AMPure beads (Beckman Coulter, USA). DNA fragments were amplified using PCR for 10 cycles. Unique DNA barcodes (Bioo Scientific, USA) were ligated to the fragments, and the resulting cDNA libraries were qualified using a CFX96 Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad, USA). All 16 libraries were pooled and loaded onto a HiSeq 2000 Genome Analyzer (Illumina, USA). All the retrieved reads were demultiplexed into individual cDNA libraries [34]. Three independent biological replicates were used for each genotype under every treatment condition.

Proteomic analysis

The germinated seeds were grown on a half-strength MS agar. Salt stress and control conditions were applied hydroponically to 15-day-old seedlings using nutrient solutions A and B supplemented with or without 200 mM NaCl, respectively. Seedlings were harvested after 1 and 3 days of salt stress. Protein extraction, in-solution digestion, and LC-MS/MS analysis were conducted as previously described, with minor modifications, using the in-house method of the Proteomics Research Laboratory, National Center for Genetic Engineering and Biotechnology, Thailand (BIOTEC) [35–37]. The tryptic peptide samples were quantified using the Ultimate 3000 Nano/Capillary LC System (Thermo Scientific, UK) coupled to Hybrid Quadrupole Q-TOF Impact II™ (Bruker Daltonics, USA) and equipped with a Nano-captive spray ion source. LC-MS/MS analysis of each sample was performed in triplicate. Each LC-MS/MS sample was prepared from independent biological replicates for each genotype and treatment combination. The MaxQuant v2.4.7.0 (https://www.maxquant.org/) was used to quantify proteins in each sample using the Andromeda search engine to correlate the MS/MS spectra with the data deposited in the UniProt Arabidopsis thaliana database. Maximum peptide intensities were log2 transformed. Proteomic data was acquired from 15-day-old seedlings, grown in a hydroponic system while the transcriptomic data originated from 7-day-old seedlings grown on MS medium. Although the ages differed, the 15-day-old hydroponic seedlings exhibited a slower growth rate following transfer and system adjustment. This resulted in plants of a comparable size to the younger, MS-grown seedlings, thereby minimizing size-related developmental differences between the datasets.

Bioinformatics analysis

For transcriptomic analysis, RNA-seq data were generated only from the Atcul3a mutant and WT plants under both control and salt stress conditions. This targeted approach aimed to examine Cullin3a-dependent transcriptional responses to salt stress. The sequence reads were mapped to the Arabidopsis genome using the Bowtie aligner [38] and TopHat [39]. Mapped reads were counted using HTSeq [40], and differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using the DESeq package [41]. The number of significant DEGs (p-adj ≤ 0.05) was tabulated and visualized using scatterplots. The shared DEGs between these two genotypes under control and salt-stress conditions were visualized using Venn diagrams. GO and KEGG enrichment analyses were performed using the ClueGo Cytoscape plug-in (https://apps.cytoscape.org/apps/cluego) [42].

For the proteomic analysis, quantitative data were obtained from six genotypes: Atcul3a mutant, WT, two OsCUL3c complementation lines (CC-L5 and CC-L6), and two OsCUL3c overexpression lines (CW-L16 and CW-L19). Log2 transformed peak intensity data were processed using MetaboAnalyst 5.0 (https://www.metaboanalyst.ca/). Before data submission, missing values within technical replicates were estimated using in-house VBA scripts for the average function. Features with > 50% missing values were removed, and the remaining missing values were estimated using K-Nearest Neighbor (KNN) (feature-wise) [43]. Auto-scaling data were used to build a supervised classification using PLS-DA. The DEPs between Atcul3a mutant and WT, and among complementation lines and over-expression lines under control and salt stress conditions were visualized in volcano plots. CC is the average of CC-L5 and CC-L6, and CW is the average of CW-L16 and CW-L19. DEPs were analyzed for intersections using a Venn diagram (http://bioinformatics.psb.ugent.be/webtools/Venn). The GO and KEGG enrichment analyses were performed using ClueGo with medium network specificity; the connectivity of the pathways in the network was described with a kappa score of 0.4, and the enrichment demonstrated only significant pathways with p-value ≤ 0.05. STITCH 5.0 (http://stitch.embl.de/) was used to analyze the common and forecasted enrichment functional network of chemical-protein interactions, and we also added NaCl, PEG, and H2O2 to the list of names. Heat maps of the overlapping DEP regions were constructed using the ComplexHeatmap of the R package [44]. To identify key biological processes and pathways associated with the salt stress responses among the genotypes, the proteomic dataset was analyzed using the WEB-based GEne SeT AnaLysis Toolkit (WebGestalt; https://www.webgestalt.org/). We designated ID type as gene symbol and defined the reference set as genome-protein coding. We chose over-representation analysis (ORA) as the method of interest, Arabidopsis thaliana as the organism, and KEGG pathway as the functional database. Redundancy among the enriched terms was reduced using the affinity propagation algorithm. The minimum and maximum numbers of analytes for a category was set to 10 and 5000, respectively, while all other parameters remained at their default settings. GO and KEGG enrichment analyses were performed on the proteomic dataset to identify overrepresented pathways related to salt stress.

Results

Impact of AtCUL3a mutation on salt stress response in Arabidopsis

We investigated the role of Cullin3 in salt stress tolerance using a T-DNA insertion line of AtCUL3a, the Arabidopsis homolog of OsCUL3c. PCR validation confirmed the T-DNA insertion in the Atcul3a mutant (approximately 800 bp) compared with the wild-type (WT) (approximately 1200 bp) using specific oligonucleotide primers (Table S1). This genetic difference resulted in phenotypic variations, including delayed flowering, in Atcul3a (Fig. 1a). Seed germination assays revealed no significant differences between the WT and Atcul3a plants under control conditions. However, under salt stress, the WT exhibited a higher germination rate than the mutant, suggesting a role of AtCUL3a in salt tolerance during seed germination (Fig. 1b). Seedling growth on the agar medium (Fig. 1c) demonstrated no significant difference in fresh weight under either control or salt stress conditions, which reduced fresh weight by approximately 50% in both genotypes (Fig. 1d). However, WT seedlings accumulated significantly more dry matter under salt stress compared to Atcul3a (Fig. 1d), suggesting a potential role for AtCUL3a in regulating seedling growth during salt stress. These findings suggest that AtCUL3a plays a role in seed germination and seedling growth under salt-stress conditions.

Proteomic changes of wild-type and Atcul3a mutant under salt stress

We transferred 15-day-old WT and Atcul3a mutant seedlings to a hydroponic system with or without 200 mM NaCl for 1 and 3 days and used them for proteomic profiling. The protein data for each line and treatment were grouped in the PLS-DA plot (Fig. 2a). Table S3 shows the number of differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) identified under control and salt stress conditions. After 1 day, salt stress treatment induced contrasting protein expression patterns in the WT and Atcul3a mutants. WT comparisons (S_WT vs. C_WT) revealed 359 DEPs, with a higher proportion of 275 downregulated proteins, suggesting a stress response focused on resource conservation. Conversely, the Atcul3a mutant (S_Atcul3a vs. C_Atcul3a) exhibited an opposite trend for 314 DEPs, dominated by 263 upregulated proteins, indicating a potential shift towards stress sensitivity response mechanisms. Notably, the Atcul3a mutant also displayed protein profiles distinct from those of the WT under control conditions (C_Atcul3a vs. C_WT), with 300 DEPs (34 highly expressed, 266 less expressed in the mutant). This difference became even more pronounced under salt stress (S_Atcul3a vs. S_WT), especially in the number of upregulated DEPs, with 309 DEPs detected (197 highly expressed and 112 less expressed in the mutant). After 3 days, WT plants (S_WT vs. C_WT) displayed 338 DEPs, with a bias towards downregulation (269), similar to 1 day of treatment. Conversely, the Atcul3a mutant (S_Atcul3a vs. C_Atcul3a) exhibited an opposing trend with 239 DEPs, which were dominated by upregulation (174) and downregulation (65). Notably, the Atcul3a mutant displayed distinct profiles under control conditions (C_Atcul3a vs. C_WT) with 576 DEPs (only 9 with higher expression and 567 with lower expression in the mutant). This difference was similar under salt stress (S_Atcul3a vs. S_WT), with 427 DEPs detected (79 upregulated, 348 downregulated in the mutant); however, the upregulation proportion was slightly higher.

Analysis of DEPs following salt stress exposure revealed distinct patterns between WT and Atcul3a mutants. Salt stress had a more pronounced effect on the upregulated proteins in the Atcul3a mutant compared to WT after both 1 and 3 days of treatment (Fig. 2b). Focusing on non-salt-responsive proteins after 1 day of treatment (Fig. 2c, blue dashed circle), 179 DEPs between WT and Atcul3a mutants were identified. In contrast, we observed that 362 DEPs were revealed in the overlapping regions of at least two of the four comparisons (Figs. 2c, red dashed circle). These overlapping DEPs represent proteins that respond to salt stress and were differentially expressed between the two lines. After 3 days of treatment, 400 non-salt-responsive DEPs (Fig. 2c, blue dashed circle) and 407 salt-responsive DEPs (Figs. 2c, red dashed circle) were observed.

GO enrichment and KEGG pathway analyses revealed distinct metabolic pathways regulated by AtCUL3a (Table S4). Enrichment analyses of the non-salt-responsive proteins list (Fig. 2c, blue circle) revealed GO terms in the BP category (Figs. S2a and S2b) and KEGG pathways (Figs. S2c and S2d) associated with photosynthesis, with several GO terms in the CC (Figs. S3a and S3b) and MF (Fig. S3c and S3d) categories supporting this involvement. In contrast, enrichment analyses of the salt-responsive proteins list (Fig. 2c, red circles) revealed GO terms in the BP category (Figs. S4a and S4b) and KEGG pathways (Fig. S4c and S4d) related to response to toxic substance and glutathione metabolism, accompanied by several supporting GO terms in the CC (Fig. S5a and S5c) and MF (Fig. S5b and S5d) categories.

A transcriptomic analysis comparing the WT and Atcul3a mutant under both control and salt stress conditions was performed (Fig. S6a, Table S5). This analysis revealed that the Atcul3a mutant exhibited a stronger transcriptional response to salt stress. Specifically, the differences in gene expression between the Atcul3a mutant and WT plants were more pronounced under salt stress than under control condition, suggesting that Cullin3a potentially regulates several genes critical for salt stress response. Conversely, the WT maintained a more stable transcriptional profile during salt stress.

Venn diagrams categorized the DEGs based on their salt stress responses (Fig. S6b). A set of 86 DEGs (blue dashed circle) demonstrated no response to salt stress but differed in expression between genotypes. Another set of 118 DEGs (red dashed circles) responded to salt stress and exhibited altered expression patterns across at least two comparisons, suggesting a potential role for AtCUL3a under salt stress. Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis revealed distinct functional roles of DEGs in these gene sets. In agreement with the GO enrichment analysis of the non-salt-responsive DEPs (Fig. S2), photosynthesis-related biological process (BP) terms (e.g., electron transport chain, ATP synthesis) were also enriched in non-salt-responsive DEGs (Fig. S6c). Cellular component (CC) and molecular function (MF) further supported their roles in photosynthesis (Figs. S6d and S6e). In contrast, DEGs responding to salt stress were enriched for BP terms associated with defense mechanisms (glucosinolate biosynthetic process, response to toxic substance, glutathione metabolism, etc.) (Fig. S6f). Consistently, the salt-responsive DEPs showed enrichment for the GO terms related to response to toxic substance and glutathione metabolism (Fig. S4a). Further corroborating these results, similar enriched GO terms in CC and MF, along with the KEGG pathway analysis were observed (Fig. S6g-i). Overall, these transcriptomic patterns provided supportive evidence consistent with the proteomic findings presented previously.

Functional characterization of OsCUL3c in Arabidopsis under salt stress

This study investigated OsCUL3c, a candidate gene in rice, for salt response identified via genome-wide association (GWA) mapping [27]. OsCUL3c comprises 3,909 bp genomic sequences and a 2,196 bp coding sequence (CDS), which encodes a protein of 731 amino acids in length. AtCUL3a comprises 3,065 bp of genomic sequences and 2,199 bp of CDS, which encode 732 amino acid residues in length. The OsCUL3c amino acid sequence demonstrated high identity (69.8%) and similarity (83.6%) with AtCUL3a (Fig. S7). To understand OsCUL3c role in the salt stress response, Arabidopsis lines expressing OsCUL3c in both Atcul3a mutant backgrounds (complementation lines, CC-L5 and CC-L6) and WT backgrounds (overexpression lines, CW-L16 and CW-L19) were generated. By PCR using specific primers (Table S2), we found that the complementation and overexpression lines demonstrated heterologous OsCUL3c expression (Table 1). Their phenotypes were examined and compared to those of the WT and the Atcul3a mutant (Fig. 3). Germination rates were similar under control conditions (Fig. 4a) but significantly lower in the Atcul3a mutant under salt stress (Fig. 4b). Both the complementation and overexpression lines exhibited similar or little difference in germination rates compared to the WT. The photosynthetic pigment content demonstrated no significant differences among the lines under control conditions. However, under salt stress, over-expression lines exhibited higher chlorophyll and carotenoid content than the WT, while the Atcul3a mutant demonstrated the lowest (Figs. 4c–f). Similar trends were observed for plant growth in hydroponic systems. The complementation lines demonstrated restored root length compared with the Atcul3a mutant under salt stress (Fig. 4g). Overexpression lines tended to have higher fresh and dry weights than the WT plants under salt stress, whereas the Atcul3a mutant tended to exhibit lower biomass (Fig. 4h and i), suggesting that OsCUL3c overexpression enhanced salt tolerance in Arabidopsis.

Proteomic changes of OsCUL3c expressed lines under salt stress

Protein data from the complementation lines (CC-L5 and CC-L6) and overexpression lines (CW-L16 and CW-L19) under normal and salt stress conditions were visualized in the PLS-DA plot (Fig. S8). The overlapping DEPs of CC-L5 and CC-L6 and those of CW-L16 and CW-L19 were selected (Figs. S9 and S10), and their average values were used for further analysis. The total number of overlapping DEPs comparing the control and salt stress conditions of CC and CW is summarized in Table S6. The Venn diagram shows the number of significant DEPs among Atcul3a mutant, WT, and CC or CW under normal and salt stress conditions. These results revealed that DEPs were found more frequently in overlapping regions between the WT and CC or CW than in overlapping regions between Atcul3a mutant and CC or CW. For overlapping salt-responsive DEPs among Atcul3a mutant, WT, and CC (red dashed triangles), we found no DEPs after 1 day of treatment (Fig. 5a), however, six overlapping DEPs were identified after 3 days of treatment (Fig. 5c). AtCUL3a/OsCUL3c also appears to regulate energy metabolism. Expression of osmotically responsive genes 2 (LOS2), a glycolysis and gluconeogenesis regulator, showed no significant changes in Atcul3a but was upregulated in WT and further increased in CC. In addition, glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPC2), a key enzyme in glycolysis and the Calvin cycle, remained unchanged in Atcul3a but exhibited upregulation in WT and CC, reinforcing the role of AtCUL3a/OsCUL3c in regulating energy metabolism. In addition, levels of formate dehydrogenase (FDH), which participates in carbon metabolism, were upregulated in WT and CC, but remained unchanged in Atcul3a. Similarly, the expression of lipoxygenase 2 (LOX2), an enzyme involved in lipid oxidation and stress signaling, was upregulated only in WT and CC. This further strengthens the connection between AtCUL3a/OsCUL3c function and carbon and energy metabolism.

In addition, AtCUL3a/OsCUL3c possibly influences photosynthetic efficiency. Levels of several photosystem II components, including photosystem II subunit QA (PSBQA) and photosystem II reaction center protein H (PSBH), were strongly downregulated in WT and CC. In contrast, photosystem II reaction center protein B (PSBB), showed a contrasting trend, with a slight increase in WT and a more pronounced induction in CC. Meanwhile, photosystem I reaction center subunit N (PSAN) followed a similar downregulation pattern, indicating broad adjustments in light-harvesting mechanisms.

Protein homeostasis regulation appeared to be influenced by AtCUL3a/OsCUL3c. Levels of nearly all identified ribosomal proteins, including 40S ribosomal proteins S14-3 (AT3G52580), 40S ribosomal proteins S25-4 (AT4G39200), 60S ribosomal protein L32-2 (AT5G46430), ribosomal protein S7p/S5e (RPS7.1), and ribosomal protein L9 (RPL9), as well as embryo defective 3137 (EMB3137) were substantially downregulated in WT and CC while remained unchanged in Atcul3a. In contrast, the significant upregulation of luminal-binding protein 1 (BIP1) only in WT and CC suggests that AtCUL3a/OsCUL3c enhances protein folding and endoplasmic reticulum (ER) processing. Similarly, eukaryotic initiation factor 4A-2 (EIF4A-2), a key mRNA surveillance and nucleocytoplasmic transport component, showed increased expression in WT and CC, suggesting the role of AtCUL3a/OsCUL3c in maintaining translational efficiency.

When comparing CW lines with WT, and Atcul3a under salt stress (Fig. 6c), key metabolic alterations were observed as heatmap visualization of log₂ fold changes (Fig. 6d) highlighted significant downregulation patterns of several key proteins in WT and CW, while Atcul3a exhibited unchanged levels. Carbon metabolism is one of the processes affected as photosynthesis-related proteins showed significant alterations under salt stress. The levels of photosystem II subunit QA (PSBQA) and photosystem II reaction center protein H (PSBH) were significantly downregulated under salt stress in WT and CW but remained unchanged in Atcul3a, while the levels of Rubisco activase (RCA), which facilitates CO₂ fixation, showed a decrease at a higher degree in WT and CW, when compared with Atcul3a. Similarly, phosphoglycerate kinase (AT1G56190), which is involved in glycolysis and carbon fixation, was downregulated in WT and CW but remained unchanged in Atcul3a. In addition, malate synthase (MLS3), a key enzyme in the glyoxylate cycle, was significantly downregulated only in WT and CW.

In agreement with carbon metabolism, energy metabolism was possibly also affected. The levels of male gametophyte defective 1 (MGP1), associated with mitochondrial biogenesis, were significantly downregulated in WT and CW, while remaining unchanged in Atcul3a. A crucial enzyme of energy production, ATP synthase subunit beta (PB), showed a mild reduction in WT but a more pronounced decrease in CW. Lipid metabolism also appeared to be affected, with ATP-binding cassette A1 (ABCA1) exhibiting a consistent downregulation in WT and CW. In contrast, adenosylhomocysteinase 1 (HOG1), a key enzyme in methionine metabolism, exhibited an increase, with a higher degree of upregulation in WT and CW than in Atcul3a. Overall, these findings reflect a shift in energy metabolism regulated by Cullin3 under salt stress.

Similar to those in CC plants, the expression of multiple ribosomal proteins was significantly reduced in WT and CW plants, while remained largely unaffected in Atcul3a. Several 40 S ribosomal proteins exhibited consistent downregulation in WT and CW, such as 40S ribosomal protein S13A (RPS13A), 30S ribosomal protein S20 (PRPS20), 40S ribosomal protein S18 (AT1G34030), and 40S ribosomal protein S30 (AT2G19750). Similarly, several 60S ribosomal proteins were repressed in WT and CW, such as 60S ribosomal protein L9 (RPL9), 60S ribosomal protein L23AB (RPL23AB), 60S ribosomal protein L35-1 (AT3G09500), 60S ribosomal protein L4-1 (AT3G09630), and 60S ribosomal protein L4-2 (AT5G02870). Beyond core ribosomal proteins, breast basic conserved 1 (BBC1) and piggyback1 (PGY1), both implicated in ribosome-associated processes, also displayed significant downregulation. The consistent reduction of ribosomal components in WT and CW highlights the impact of AtCUL3a disruption and OsCUL3c overexpression on ribosomal function.

The combined proteomic datasets revealed Cullin3-mediated oxidative defense and detoxification under salt stress

The biological processes underlying plant stress adaptation are inherently complex and dynamically regulated. To elucidate the molecular mechanisms governed by Cullin3 under salt stress, we performed an over-representation analysis (ORA) using the WEB-based GEne SeT AnaLysis Toolkit (WebGestalt). The analysis combined several datasets (Table S7), including the salt-responsive proteins differently expressed between the WT and Atcul3a mutant (Fig. 3c, red dashed circle); and the salt-responsive DEPs shared between WT and OsCUL3c complementation lines (CC) or OsCUL3c overexpression lines (CW) (Fig. Figure 5a-d, red and blue dashed triangles). This analysis identified the major KEGG pathways that were significantly enriched in the salt-responsive proteins. The most prominent functional categories were associated with photosynthesis, carbon metabolism, the ribosome, glutathione metabolism, and ABC transporters (Fig. 7a; Tables S8-S12). Among these pathways, photosynthesis and carbon metabolism were the most significantly enriched (FDR < 0.05). This indicates that Cullin3 may influence both energy production and carbon flux regulation under salt stress.

The integrative pathway map (Fig. 7b) visually demonstrates how these metabolic processes are interconnected. Salt stress induces changes in the photosynthetic electron transport chain (PSI and PSII complexes), subsequently impairing carbon assimilation through the Calvin-Benson cycle and affecting glycolysis-TCA intermediates, and photorespiration. The result of these changes is a significant impact on redox homeostasis. Enhanced flux through the glutathione metabolism pathway supports detoxification and antioxidant defense. This defense is facilitated by key enzymes including ascorbate peroxidase (APX1), two dehydroascorbate reductases (DHAR1, DHAR3), and nine glutathione S-transferases (GSTF2, GSTF8, GSTF9, GSTF10, GSTF11, GSTF14, GSTU5, ERD9 (GSTU17), and GSTU20) (Table S11). Collectively, these pathways illustrate a coordinated metabolic adjustment encompassing energy metabolism, redox regulation, and protein synthesis. This adjustment enables plants to maintain cellular homeostasis and mitigate oxidative damage during salt stress.

Discussion

Rice Cullin3, OsCUL3c (Os08g0170900), was identified by GWAS to be associated with salt response [27]. In Arabidopsis, AtCUL3a is an OsCUL3c homolog (Fig. S7). Using an Atcul3a mutant, we elucidated the function of this gene by comparing it to that of the WT, in which the flowering time was earlier than that of the Atcul3a mutant (Fig. 1a). AtCUL3a assembles the multi-subunit of E3 ligase and interacts with MYB56, a negative flowering regulator, by controlling the expression of FLOWERING LOCUS T (FT), a key flowering initiation regulator in Arabidopsis [45]. By targeting MYB56 for ubiquitination and subsequent degradation, AtCUL3a likely promotes flowering under normal conditions. Notably, the Atcul3a mutant also exhibited increased sensitivity to salt stress compared to the WT. To gain insight into the underlying mechanisms, we performed proteomic analyses to elucidate the regulation of abiotic stress signaling by Cullin3. Analysis of non-salt-responsive DEGs and DEPs revealed over-representation in GO terms, mostly involving photosynthesis (Fig. S2 and S3). These findings suggest a novel role for AtCUL3a that could involve the regulation that negatively influences the expression of photosynthesis-related genes. Consequently, the inability to form functional E3 ligase complexes in the Atcul3a mutant allows the expression of genes associated with photosynthesis to be maintained even under salt-stress conditions. The limitation of water and CO2 availability may lead to excessive energy production and ROS formation, which can damage photosynthetic components. This was probably reflected in the photosynthetic pigment content in the Atcul3a mutant, which tended to be lower than that of the WT under control conditions (Fig. 4).

Our analysis of salt-responsive DEPs revealed that they were overrepresented in glutathione metabolism and detoxification (Fig. S4 and S5). These findings highlight the ability of the WT to effectively activate the glutathione metabolic pathway upon exposure to salt stress, which is likely compromised in the Atcul3a mutant. In response to salt-induced ROS overproduction, plant cells have evolved effective mechanisms to regulate steady-state ROS levels both inside and, to some extent, outside cells. Glutathione serves as an electron donor to reduce harmful ROS such as H2O2 and lipid hydroperoxide (LPO) to water and alcohol, respectively [46]. Consequently, the lower capacity for glutathione upregulation in the Atcul3a mutant probably resulted in a diminished ability to scavenge ROS under salt stress conditions, which likely contributed to the increased salt stress sensitivity observed in the Atcul3a mutant. Although the proteomic data were generated from 15 days old seedlings, comparisons with transcriptomic data from an earlier developmental stage provided independent evidence supporting similar biological patterns.

The complementation and overexpression lines with OsCUL3c expression generated in both Atcul3a mutant and WT backgrounds exhibited significantly higher OsCUL3c expression levels compared to the WT and Atcul3a mutants, as determined by RT-qPCR analysis (Table 1). Complementation lines (CC-L5 and CC-L6) and over-expression lines (CW-L16 and CW-L19) exhibited significantly enhanced germination rates, fresh weight, dry weight, root length, and photosynthetic components under salt stress compared to the Atcul3a mutant (Fig. 4), reinforcing the previously stated understanding on the role of Atcul3a in enhancing salt tolerance. Our findings are consistent with those of Moin et al. (2019), who reported that salt stress significantly upregulated the OsCUL3a, OsCUL3b, and OsCUL3c transcript levels in rice. In rice, OsCUL3a (Os02g0746000) has been identified as a regulator of a specific target in response to biotic stress. The Oscul3a mutant displayed a smaller overall plant size than the WT (Zhonghua 11) [24–26]. However, this mutation results in increased resistance to rice pathogens. In agreement with our speculation, the accumulation of H2O2, a stress signal in plants, was higher in the Oscul3a mutant than in the WT.

Proteomic profiling provided a detailed overview of the complex regulatory network involving Cullin3c in orchestrating responses to salt stress through the ubiquitin-proteasome system. The identified salt-responsive proteins were enriched in several metabolic pathways, including photosynthesis, carbon metabolism, the ribosome, glutathione metabolism, and ABC transporters (Fig. 7). Our results highlight upregulation of metabolic pathways associated with stress adaptation. Beyond general GO term categories, our conclusions are reinforced by two main findings; first, the consistent upregulation of specific stress-related genes, including ascorbate peroxidase, dehydroascorbate reductase, and glutathione-S-transferase; and second, the phenotypic data showing improved salt stress tolerance, evidenced by better maintenance of pigments contents in the overexpressing OsCUL3c lines. The increased expression of genes related to carbon metabolism suggests an enhanced metabolic flux, facilitating sustained ATP production and maintaining energy homeostasis despite salt-induced stress [47]. Similarly, nitrogen metabolism activation implies an enhanced nitrogen assimilation process, which is essential for amino acid biosynthesis and protein turnover under stress conditions [48].

Glutathione metabolism appears to be highly important in salt tolerance responses mediated by Cullin3 (Fig. 7a). Cullin3 promotes glutathione metabolism by upregulating key genes, strengthening the plant’s antioxidant defense system and mitigating oxidative damage caused by salt-induced reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation [49]. In this study, a proteomic analysis, coupled with KEGG pathway enrichment analysis, identified several proteins in glutathione metabolism overrepresented including ascorbate peroxidase, dehydroascorbate reductase, and glutathione-S-transferase (Fig. 7b). Previous studies have shown that salt stress increases glutathione levels in Arabidopsis and rice [50, 51]. Glutathione supports antioxidant enzyme systems by serving as a cofactor, maintains the reduced form of ascorbate and recycling of other antioxidants as well as serves as an antioxidant by itself in ROS detoxification. In Arabidopsis and rice, the glutathione content is related to the developmental stages of plants and profiling provided significant evidence of glutathione participation and glutathione metabolizing genes in response to various abiotic stress conditions [52, 53].

Based on our findings, we summarize the processes and proteins that are potentially regulated Cullin3 in salt stress responses (Fig. 8). In contrast to the upregulated glutathione metabolism in dealing with ROS accumulation under salt stress, Cullin3 downregulates several photosynthetic proteins. The suppression of photosynthetic proteins indicates a metabolic trade-off, where the plant prioritizes stress-responsive metabolic pathways over maximizing light-dependent energy production. This regulatory adjustment may function as a protective strategy to minimize excess ROS generation under stress [54, 55]. In addition to these changes, Cullin3 influences carbon metabolism, particularly enzymes involved in Calvin-Benson cycle, glycolysis, the TCA cycle and photorespiration This suggests that Cullin3 plays a role in rerouting carbon flux toward redox balance and energy efficiency. Cullin3 also significantly affects ribosomal components and probably the translational capacity, suggesting translational reprogramming under stress. This is supported by previous findings showing that Cullin-RING ligases, including CUL3-KBTBD8 complexes, can regulate translation-related processes via ubiquitin-dependent ribosome remodeling [56]. Furthermore, CUL3 has been shown to modulate translational activity more directly by targeting translation-associated substrates such as eEF1A1 and Pdcd4 through BTB-domain adaptors like BPOZ-2 and IBTK, respectively [57]. Such modulation may contribute to the selective translation of stress-responsive proteins, a hallmark of translational reprogramming in adverse conditions. Given its role in coordinating diverse metabolic responses, Cullin3 presents a promising target for genetic engineering aimed at improving salt tolerance in crops. Future studies should focus on elucidating its direct regulatory mechanisms, particularly its interactions with transcription factors and ubiquitination targets within salt stress pathways.

Conclusions

Our study demonstrates that Cullin3 expression through complementation or overexpression enhances salt stress tolerance in Arabidopsis. By combining the proteomic datasets from the mutant, the complementation and overexpression lines, we identified a consistent set of stress-responsive genes and proteins. This analysis revealed that Cullin3 regulates key stress-responsive pathways, particularly those involved in glutathione metabolism, carbon metabolism, photosynthesis, and translation. The enhanced abundance of antioxidant enzymes highlights the central role of Cullin3 in coordinating multiple core metabolic processes and regulating reactive oxygen species (ROS) detoxification under salt stress. These findings underscore Cullin3 as a promising molecular target for engineering salt-tolerant crops.
