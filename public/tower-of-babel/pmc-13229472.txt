More Than Just an Abscess: An Unusual Case of Anal Squamous Cell Carcinoma
Authors: Lan Nguyen, Clark Zhang, Erin Ly
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAnal squamous cell carcinoma remains a rare malignancy of the lower gastrointestinal tract. We report a 39-year-old woman who presented with recurrent perianal abscesses initially suspected to be from fistulizing Crohn's disease. Despite appropriate antibiotics and drainage, her symptoms persisted. Multiple endoscopic evaluations were performed, with initial biopsies showing only chronic inflammation. Repeat sigmoidoscopy ultimately revealed moderately differentiated squamous cell carcinoma. The patient underwent a diverting colostomy for symptom relief and was treated with chemoradiation, resulting in significant tumor reduction. This case highlights the importance of considering malignancy in patients with persistent perianal disease, particularly when inflammatory bowel disease has been ruled out.

INTRODUCTION

Squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) of the gastrointestinal tract most commonly affects the esophagus or anal canal due to the presence of squamous epithelial cells in these regions. Anal SCC accounts for 4% of all lower gastrointestinal cancers and less than 1% of all new cancer diagnoses annually.1 Its clinical presentation is variable and may mimic other anorectal conditions, often leading to delays in diagnosis. We present an atypical presentation of anal SCC characterized by recurrent perianal abscesses initially suspected to be due to Crohn's disease. This case highlights the importance of considering anal SCC in the differential diagnoses for recurrent perianal abscesses, especially after inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) has been ruled out.

CASE REPORT

A 39-year-old woman with no medical history presented with 9 months of painful perianal swelling associated with spontaneous mucous drainage and minimal bleeding. She reported recurrent pimples on the buttocks but denied previous perianal lesions. Her vital signs were notable for tachycardia with a heart rate of 115 bpm but were otherwise unremarkable. Physical examination revealed 2 perianal openings at the 7–8 o'clock position with mild skin maceration; no erythema, fluctuance, or active drainage were noted. Laboratory evaluation was unremarkable, including a white blood cell count of 10.8 × 109/L, C-reactive protein of 3.59 mg/L, and fecal calprotectin of 94.6 µg/g. Abdominal and pelvic computed tomography demonstrated 2 perirectal fluid collections, raising concerns for infectious or inflammatory proctitis, abscesses, or fistulizing disease. Flexible sigmoidoscopy revealed a focal area of erythema and edema in the rectum, with biopsies showing mild chronic inflammation. The patient was treated with a 10-day course of doxycycline while awaiting outpatient colorectal surgical evaluation for definitive drainage.

One week later, she developed a new area of painful swelling between the rectum and labia. Pelvic magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) showed a perirectal abscess and a left labial abscess (Figures 1 and 2). Owing to worsening pain, the patient presented to the Emergency Department and was admitted to the hospital. Laboratory findings at the time revealed a white blood cell count of 14 × 109/L, C-reactive protein of 10.75 mg/L, and fecal calprotectin of 223 µg/g. Antibiotics were initiated, and colorectal surgery performed a bedside incision and drainage with seton placement. Through examination under anesthesia, repeat rectal biopsies again showed chronic inflammation with focal hyperplastic changes. Gastroenterology was consulted and raised concern for isolated perianal Crohn's disease or subclinical proximal IBD. The patient was discharged with plan for close outpatient gastroenterology follow-up to diagnose and treat suspected fistulizing Crohn's disease.

Pelvic magnetic resonance imaging: Axial view showed a multilobulated left labial abscess (arrows).

An outpatient colonoscopy with terminal ileum intubation was deferred as the patient did not tolerate the bowel preparation. Instead, a magnetic resonance enterography was obtained, showing no active bowel inflammation. Repeat pelvic MRI identified a 7.8 × 7.4 × 5.2 mass in the anal canal with extension to the right lower rectum and posterior vaginal wall invasion. Approximately 3 months after her initial endoscopy, a repeat sigmoidoscopy was performed and revealed a focal lesion of granulation tissue in the distal rectum, just proximal to the anal verge (Figure 3). Subsequent biopsy unexpectedly demonstrated moderately differentiated SCC. Metastatic work up, including a positron emission tomography scan and inguinal node biopsy, was negative. The patient underwent laparoscopic diverting colostomy for symptom relief and initiated chemoradiation. After 6 weeks of therapy, a repeat positron emission tomography imaging showed a marked reduction in both tumor size and its metabolic uptake.

DISCUSSION

Anal SCC is strongly associated with chronic inflammation driven by persistent infection with high-risk human papillomavirus (HPV) subtypes, such as HPV-16 and HPV-18.1 HPV viruses infect the basal epithelial cells of the squamocolumnar transition zone within the anal canal.1 Viral oncoproteins E6 and E7 disrupt tumor suppressor pathways and promote cellular proliferation.1 This results in malignant transformation and subsequently, dysplastic changes in the squamous epithelium, known as intraepithelial neoplasia.1 Progression from intraepithelial neoplasia to SCC occurs in approximately 10% to 11% of cases, with higher risks among immunocompromised patients.2 In addition to HPV infection, studies have identified changes in the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway and mutations in chromatin-modifying genes of MLL2 and MLL3. Moreover, CDKN2A mutations, more commonly seen in patients without HPV infection, are associated with worse clinical outcomes.1 Notably, PD-L1 expression has been detected in 30%–40% of anal SCC cases, highlighting a potential therapeutic role for immunotherapy.3–5

Clinically, anal SCC often presents with pruritus, pain, bleeding, tenesmus, mucopurulent discharge, changes in stool caliber, or palpable mass.1 However, symptoms may overlap with benign anorectal conditions, such as anal fissures or fistulas.6,7 In this case, recurrent perianal abscesses closely mimicked fistulizing Crohn's disease, posing a diagnostic challenge. Crohn's disease typically demonstrates skip lesions on endoscopy and histologic findings of active inflammation, crypt distortion, or granuloma formation. However, the patient's endoscopic evaluations and biopsies lacked these characteristic features, prompting repeat evaluation and ultimately leading to the diagnosis of anal SCC. Tissue biopsies via anoscopy, rigid proctoscopy, or sigmoidoscopy remain essential in confirming anal SCC.1 Meanwhile, transanal ultrasound and pelvic MRI are useful for assessing tumor extent and regional nodal involvement.1

Historically, the treatment of anal SCC involved abdominoperineal resection, with 5-year survival rates of 50%–70%.1 In 1974, Nigro et al reported tumor regression in 3 anal SCC patients treated with chemoradiation, shifting the paradigm of management.8 In 1985, a study by Leichman et al demonstrated that 84% of cases achieved complete remission after chemoradiation, with residual disease occurring at distant sites in the remainder.9 Subsequent studies have demonstrated that chemoradiation not only improves survival, but also preserves sphincter function for patients with anal SCC.1,10 Thus, chemoradiation has been the standard of care for stage I to III disease, with abdominoperineal resection reserved for patients with a residual disease after radiation.11 Metastatic disease is managed with systemic chemotherapy, with reported 1-year and 5-year survival rates of 60% and 32%, respectively.12 More recently, PD-1 inhibitors have been approved for locally recurrent or metastatic anal SCC, and ongoing trials are exploring their role in earlier-stage disease.13–15

This case illustrates an unusual presentation of anal SCC masquerading as recurrent perianal abscesses and presumed Crohn's disease. Clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion for anal SCC in patients with persistent or progressive perianal disease, particularly after ruling out IBD. Endoscopic evaluation with biopsies is essential for accurate diagnosis. While chemoradiation remains the cornerstone of treatment of localized disease, systemic chemotherapy is indicated for metastatic cases. Future research should further define the role of immunotherapy in anal SCC, including its optimal combination strategies and long-term outcomes.
