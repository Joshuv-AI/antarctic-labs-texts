Do elite soccer players meet nutritional requirements? A scoping review and meta-analysis
Authors: Pedro Miguel Mendes, Diogo Vicente Martinho, Alain Massart, César Leão, Rodrigo Abreu, Cristian Petri, Vítor Hugo Teixeira, Adam Field, Néstor Ordoñez-Saavedra, Hugo Sarmento
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis scoping review and meta-analysis synthesised current evidence on energy and macronutrient intake and energy expenditure in elite male and female soccer players. Despite established nutritional guidelines, concerns persist regarding chronic low energy availability and its implications for both performance and health.MethodsA total of 57 studies involving professional and semi-professional athletes aged 16 years and older were included. Meta-analyses were conducted for energy intake and total energy expenditure, with analyses performed separately for male and female players. Evidence was also synthesised for dietary intake, including carbohydrate, protein, and fat consumption.ResultsEnergy intake and expenditure comparisons were based on 11 studies in male players and 6 in female players. Across both sexes, findings consistently demonstrated negative energy balance. Male players exhibited significant intake deficits relative to expenditure, averaging up to − 741 kcal.day⁻1, while female players showed deficits of − 544 kcal.day⁻1 (both p < 0.01). Even after adjustment for estimated underreporting (~ 19%), substantial energy gaps persisted, particularly among males. Carbohydrate intake was derived from 26 studies in male players (pre-season: 5.42 g.kg⁻1.day⁻1; in-season: 4.62 g.kg⁻1.day⁻1) and 7 studies in female players (3.79 g.kg⁻1.day⁻1). Protein intake was based on 25 studies in male players (1.78 g.kg⁻1.day⁻1) and 8 studies in female players (1.57 g.kg⁻1.day⁻1). Overall, carbohydrate and protein intakes generally aligned with the lower end of UEFA recommended ranges, with more pronounced inadequacies observed in female players, while fat intake was consistent with established nutritional guidelines.ConclusionThese findings emphasise the need for more rigorous, sex-specific investigations to underpin evidence-based nutritional strategies in elite soccer, thereby bridging the gap between research and practice while safeguarding both performance and athlete well-being.

Introduction

Soccer has evolved over recent decades into a sport of increasing physiological [1–3], technical [4], and tactical demands [5]. There have been changes in training loads and competition schedules, which have become more congested, along with increased international travel due to multiple competitions — all of which heighten the physiological stress placed on players [6, 7]. Elite soccer players may cover distances of 10–13 km per match, including repeated high-intensity efforts such as sprints, accelerations, and decelerations, placing substantial demands on energy systems and recovery processes [8, 9]. Furthermore, congested competition schedules—often involving multiple matches per week—limit recovery time and increase cumulative physiological load. As such, nutrition has become an essential tool for optimizing not only training and competition preparation, but also recovery between sessions and the maintenance of performance levels throughout an athlete’s career [10].

The application of tailored nutritional strategies has gained increasing relevance in the context of elite soccer, aiming to keep pace with the evolving physiological demands of players and striving to optimise both performance and long-term health. Effective implementation requires the translation of scientific evidence into practice by specifying appropriate food types, quantities, and timing of intake, in addition to fluid consumption [11]. These all contribute to optimising health, body composition, performance and enhancing the recovery process between matches and training sessions and decreasing the risk of injury/illness [12].

Although there is widespread recognition among sport professionals of the influence of nutrition on key components of soccer performance—such as physical factors, including body composition and energy availability, as well as cognitive function [13]—a gap persists between empirical knowledge and the practical or clinical implementation of nutritional strategies. It is important to note that the very body of scientific knowledge and the established nutritional recommendations for this population may be called into question, owing to the methodological challenges inherent in conducting research within elite sporting contexts and the complexities of translating theoretical guidelines into practice within the multifaceted environment of professional soccer [14].

In this context, one of the critical steps for developing and applying empirically supported nutritional strategies is the accurate assessment of energy expenditure [15], alongside a comprehensive energetic and nutritional evaluation [16]. However, several methodological limitations have been identified in the tools currently employed for these assessments, including reliance on memory, under-reporting of intake, and behavioural changes during data collection. Retrospective tools such as 24-h recalls and FFQs often fail to capture habitual intake or the timing and context of meals. Prospective methods, while potentially more accurate, impose a high burden on the athlete and may alter eating behaviour. Nutrient analysis is further complicated by errors in food coding and inconsistencies in food composition databases [15, 17]. In addition, variability in the interpretation of dietary data between practitioners, as well as inconsistencies in the quality and completeness of self-reported intake, may further compromise the reliability of dietary assessments. Emerging methods, such as remote food photography, have shown promise but are still subject to practical challenges and user-dependent variability [18, 19].

In response to these challenges, UEFA published a consensus statement in 2020 [10], which reviewed existing literature on nutrition and supplementation specifically within the context of soccer, with the objective of translating this body of knowledge into context-specific guidelines. The primary goal was to develop evidence-based recommendations to assist performance nutritionists in the implementation of tailored nutritional strategies within soccer clubs. The document provided a series of recommendations regarding nutritional intake across various phases of the soccer season and within weekly microcycles. Key recommendations included carbohydrate intake ranges adjusted to seasonal phases (3–8 g.kg⁻1 during the in-season and 4–8 g.kg⁻1 during the off-season), protein intake (1.6–2.2 g.kg⁻1), and fat intake (20–35% of total daily energy).

Subsequently, additional review articles and consensus statements have emerged [11, 20, 21]. However, many of these reviews present important limitations. In several cases, the competitive level of participants is not clearly specified, or studies include mixed samples of non-professional and semi-professional players, limiting the applicability of findings to elite contexts. Additionally, the accuracy of dietary intake assessments is often compromised by methodological constraints such as underreporting and reliance on self-reported data. Furthermore, the inclusion of mixed age groups, without distinguishing between youth and adult players, may obscure meaningful differences in nutritional requirements [11]. In addition, certain reviews fail to account for the methodological limitations associated with the tools used to assess energy and nutritional intake [21, 22], including the potential underreporting of dietary intake—estimated in the literature to be as high as 19% [23]. These limitations restrict the applicability of current findings to senior elite-level soccer and underscore the importance of context-specific research. Given the substantial barriers to conducting rigorous studies in elite settings, there is a pressing need for more high-quality investigations within these environments.

Unlike previous reviews, the present study combines a scoping approach with quantitative meta-analysis to provide pooled estimates of energy intake and expenditure specifically in elite soccer populations. Additionally, this review focuses exclusively on professional and semi-professional players aged 16 years and older, thereby reducing the heterogeneity associated with mixed-level or youth samples frequently observed in previous literature. Its objectives are twofold: (1) to examine current practical recommendations for nutritional intake in elite soccer in relation to the available evidence; and (2) to establish average values for energy intake, expenditure, and macronutrient consumption based on available evidence, contextualized against established guidelines.

Methods

This review adhered to established methodological frameworks, including the Cochrane guidelines [24], the PRISMA 2020 guidelines [25] and corresponding extensions for scoping reviews [26]. The protocol was registered by the first author (PMM) on the Open Science Framework (https://doi.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/8QJDV), during the analysis phase of the study. Although registration did not occur prior to study commencement, all eligibility criteria and analytical procedures had been defined before data extraction to minimise potential bias. Nevertheless, this should be acknowledged as a methodological limitation.

Eligibility criteria

Original manuscripts or ahead-of-print articles in English, Portuguese, or Spanish were eligible for inclusion in the review, with no date limitations. Semi-professional players were also included because, in many competitive leagues, professional and semi-professional players frequently compete within the same competitive structures, and some teams may include both fully professional and semi-professional athletes within the same squad. In instances where essential information was not available in the original publications, corresponding authors were contacted for clarification. Studies were excluded if they did not meet key eligibility criteria, including unclear participant characteristics (e.g., age or competitive level), involvement of non-elite populations (e.g., recreational, university, or mixed-sport samples), or lack of relevant nutritional outcomes (e.g., absence of data on energy intake, energy expenditure, or macronutrient intake). Additionally, conference abstracts, duplicate reports, and articles not published in English, Portuguese, or Spanish were excluded. Studies for which full data could not be obtained after contacting the authors were also excluded.

Information sources and search strategy

On November 22, 2024, we conducted a comprehensive search across three electronic databases: PubMed, Scopus and Web of Science. The search strategy employed was as follows: ((Soccer* OR Soccer OR "Soccer player*" OR "Soccer player*") AND (Nutrition* OR Food* OR "Food Intake" OR "Energ* intake" OR "Nutrition* intake" OR "nutrition* needs" OR "nutrition* demand*" OR "Nutritional intervention*" OR Nutri* OR Micronutrient* OR Macronutrient* OR Carb* OR Protein* OR Fat OR “Fatty acids” OR “fatty acid” OR Fats OR Vitamin* OR Mineral* OR Eletrolyt* OR Hydrat* OR OR “food*")). Additionally, we reviewed the reference list from two previous reviews (a scoping review and a systematic review) [21, 27] to identify any additional relevant manuscripts for inclusion.

Selection process

EndNote™ 21.0 (Clarivate™, Philadelphia, Pennsylvania, USA) was used to facilitate the identification, screening, and inclusion steps of our systematic review. Duplicate records were initially removed automatically by EndNote's "Find Duplicates" feature. Subsequently, the first and second authors (PMM/DVM) manually confirmed and resolved any remaining duplicates. An initial screening was performed based on titles and abstracts, followed by full-text assessments to ensure studies met the inclusion criteria. Two researchers (PMM/DVM) conducted the screenings, and in cases of disagreement, a third author (HS) was consulted to reach a consensus.

Data extraction and items

Microsoft Excel® (version 2501, Microsoft) was employed to systematically extract and organise relevant data from each manuscript. This process was conducted independently by two authors (PMM/DVM). Any discrepancies identified during data extraction were resolved through discussion and consensus, with a third researcher (HS) consulted when necessary. Two detailed datasets were developed in accordance with the study’s objectives: one focused on nutrition and energy intake in female players, and the other in male players.

For studies addressing dietary intake, data were collected on both absolute and relative values of dietary consumption and energy expenditure. With respect to macronutrient intake, the extracted data were contextualised according to the phase of the competitive season, the specific time point within the microcycle (e.g., match day [MD], MD-1, MD + 1), and the player’s field position (where reported). Intake values for carbohydrates, protein and fats were recorded in grams per kilogram of body weight (g.kg⁻1) and in percentage of total energy intake. Whenever carbohydrate and protein intake values were reported in kilocalories, total daily grams, or as a percentage of total energy intake, conversions to g.kg⁻1 were performed, provided that the necessary information—such as the sample’s mean body weight and total daily energy intake—was available. Similarly, fat intake values were converted to percentage of total daily energy intake when originally presented in other units.

To facilitate comprehensive comparison and interpretation, two separate datasets were created: one dedicated to women’s soccer and the other to men’s soccer, each structured according to the variables outlined above. For data presented exclusively in graphical format, GetData Graph Digitizer (getdatagraph-digitizer.software.informer.com) was used to accurately extract means and standard deviations from figures included in the original publications [28].

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were carried out separately for male and female soccer players aged 16 and older, competing at professional or semi-professional levels. The meta-analyses focused on energy intake and total energy expenditure using reported means, standard deviations, and sample sizes. When data were only presented in graphical form, values were extracted using GetData Graph Digitizer (version 2.26).

To avoid over-representing studies that reported multiple data points—such as those providing intake values by playing position or by seasonal phase (e.g., pre-season vs. in-season)—a reductionist approach was applied specifically in the analysis of macronutrient intake [29]. This strategy ensured that multiple entries from the same study did not distort the pooled estimates by consolidating subgroup data into a single representative value. While this approach reduced the likelihood of over-representing individual studies within pooled analyses, it may also have limited the variability captured in the dataset and reduced ecological validity.

When standard deviations for within-subject differences between energy intake and expenditure were not available, they were estimated using correlation coefficients from previous research [17], as detailed in the sensitivity analyses.

Energy balance was evaluated using two methods: one based on the raw reported values, and another using intake values adjusted upward by 19% to account for potential underreporting [23]. All analyses were performed using a random-effects model to account for methodological variation between studies.

Publication bias was assessed visually through funnel plots and statistically via Egger’s regression intercept. When bias was suspected, adjustments were made using the Duval and Tweedie trim-and-fill method [30]. A random-effects model was applied to accommodate variability across different methods used to assess the variables of interest [31]. Subgroup analyses were conducted to explore macronutrient intake—specifically carbohydrates, protein, and fat—with carbohydrate and protein reported in grams per kilogram of body weight (g/kg), and fat as a percentage of total energy intake. When possible, data were further stratified by sex, phase of the season, and playing position.

All statistical procedures were conducted using Comprehensive Meta-Analysis software (version 2; Biostat, Englewood, NJ, USA) and GraphPad Prism (version 5.00 for Windows; GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA), with statistical significance set at p < 0.05.

Risk of bias

The risk of bias and methodological quality of the included studies was independently assessed by two reviewers (PMM and DVM). Any disagreements were discussed between reviewers, with a third author (HS) consulted to achieve consensus when necessary. The choice of assessment tool was based on study design. Observational cohort and cross-sectional studies were evaluated using the full 14-item Quality Assessment Tool for Observational Cohort and Cross-Sectional Studies [32]. This checklist covers key domains such as the clarity of the research question, definition and representativeness of the study population, uniform application of eligibility criteria, sample size justification, timing of exposure and outcome measurements, reliability and validity of measurement tools, blinding of outcome assessors, follow-up rates, and appropriateness of statistical analyses. Each item was assessed individually and rated as “Yes”, “No”, or “Cannot Determine/Not Reported”, without producing a cumulative score.

All risk of bias assessments were compiled into structured tables, reported separately by sex. These evaluations were intended to characterise the methodological quality of the available evidence and were not used as exclusion criteria during the study selection process.

Results

Study identification and selection

A total of 15,228 records were initially identified across the three databases. After removal of duplicates (n = 4,564), 10,664 records were screened, of which 312 were assessed for full-text eligibility. Following full-text screening, 57 studies met the inclusion criteria and were included in the review. Period of season, energy expenditure & energy intake assessment

Figure 3 presents four bar charts related to the reviewed studies addressing the assessment of total daily energy expenditure, resting energy expenditure, and energy or nutrient intake. Relevant topics explored across the studies are summarised accordingly.Fig. 3Number of studies by season period (A), dietary assessment method (B), REE assessment (C), and daily energy expenditure assessment (D) in female and male soccer players

Number of studies by season period (A), dietary assessment method (B), REE assessment (C), and daily energy expenditure assessment (D) in female and male soccer players

Panel A illustrates the competitive season period during which each study was conducted, including studies with both male and female soccer players. Accordingly, thirty-one studies involving male soccer players were conducted during the in-season period, while thirteen studies involving female players were conducted in the same period. Seven studies were conducted during the pre-season period—two in women’s soccer and five in men’s soccer. One study on female soccer players was conducted during the off-season. Additionally, several studies on male soccer players spanned multiple seasonal phases: one study covered both pre-season and in-season, one covered off-season and in-season, and one included all three phases—off-season, pre-season, and in-season. Three studies did not specify the period during which data were collected.

Panels C and D present the methodologies used to assess resting energy expenditure (REE) and total daily energy expenditure (TDEE), as reported in the reviewed studies. In this context, eleven studies involving male soccer players and five involving female players estimated REE (or basal metabolic rate) using predictive equations. Indirect calorimetry was employed in three studies with female players and one with male players. Bioelectrical impedance analysis was used in one study for each sex, while one study involving female players used a combination of methods. TDEE was assessed using various methods across the included studies. The factorial method was the most applied, featuring in 11 studies involving male players and six involving female players. The doubly labelled water method, considered the gold standard for measuring energy expenditure, was used in three studies with male players and two with female players. A combination of methods was reported in two studies on male players and 3 on female players. Additionally, one study involving male players utilised an activity record, while one study on female players employed a multisensor armband. Approximately thirty-one studies did not report the methodology used to assess REE (n = 25 for men’s soccer and n = 6 for women’s soccer). Likewise, around twenty-nine studies did not report the methodology used to assess TDEE (n = 25 and n = 4 for men’s and women’s soccer, respectively).

Panel B presents the methodologies used to assess nutritional intake as reported in the reviewed studies. The most commonly used method overall was the 24-h dietary recall (n = 16 for men’s soccer and n = 5 for women’s soccer). In men’s soccer specifically, the most frequently reported methods for assessing nutritional intake were the 7-day food diary (n = 7), 3-day food diary (n = 6), and food frequency questionnaire (n = 4). A similar trend was observed in women’s soccer, with five studies reporting the use of a 7-day food diary and three studies employing a 3-day food diary. Additionally, five studies in men’s soccer reported the use of combined tools (n = 3) or a 3-day weighed food diary (n = 2). Other methodologies reported in men’s soccer included a 10-day food diary (n = 1), 10-day weighted food diary (n = 1), 7-day weighted food diary (n = 1), and 6-day weighted food diary (n = 1). With regard to women’s soccer, further methods reported included a food photography diary (n = 1), an 8-day weighted food diary (n = 1), and a 5-day weighted food diary (n = 1).

Differences by position

Figure 10 presents a bar chart illustrating positional differences in energy and macronutrient intake. Panel A displays energy intake by playing position [44, 54, 62, 67, 74, 75, 79, 89], while Panels B, C, and D show carbohydrate, protein, and fat intake, respectively [44, 67, 74, 75, 78, 79]. Given the limited number of studies assessing nutritional intake by playing position—particularly in female players—and the substantial methodological heterogeneity across studies, the following findings should be interpreted cautiously and considered primarily exploratory in nature. Notably, only one study specifically investigated female players [44].Fig. 10Dietary and energy intake by playing position in soccer players

Dietary and energy intake by playing position in soccer players

To ensure consistency across the findings reported in the literature, playing positions were grouped into five categories: (1) Goalkeeper (GK), (2) Defender/Full-Back (D), (3) Winger/Sides/Wide Midfielders (WM), (4) Midfielder/Central Midfielders (CM), and (5) Forward/Attacker (F).

Energy intake

With regard to energy intake (Panel A), no clear positional differences were observed between playing positions, including between goalkeepers and outfield players. (GK: 2954 ± 629 kcal, D: 2857 ± 364 kcal, WM: 3012 ± 386 kcal, CM: 2778 ± 373 kcal, F: 2806 ± 458 kcal). However, substantial within-group variation was noted, with particularly large standard deviations observed in goalkeepers. The highest reported energy intake was 3903 kcal [54], while the lowest mean intake was 1985 kcal, the latter being reported in female players [44].

Carbohydrates

Panel B displays carbohydrate intake across playing positions. Of the macronutrients analysed, carbohydrate intake appeared to show the greatest positional variation. Wingers/Sides/Wide Midfielders (WM) appeared to present higher average carbohydrate intake values (5.20 ± 0.65 g.kg⁻1.day⁻1), whereas Goalkeepers (GK) tended to present lower values (4.15 ± 1.28 g.kg⁻1.day⁻1). Goalkeepers also showed notable within-group variability, a pattern not observed to the same extent in other positions: Defenders (4.68 ± 0.62 g.kg⁻1.day⁻1), Central Midfielders (4.45 ± 0.48 g.kg⁻1.day⁻1), and Forwards (4.52 ± 0.82 g.kg⁻1·day⁻1).

Protein

Panel C shows the variation in protein intake across playing positions. Although no clear positional differences were evident between positions, Forwards/Attackers recorded the lowest intake values (F: 1.52 ± 0.11 g.kg−1) when compared with other positions on the field (GK: 1.78 ± 0.44 g.kg−1, D: 1.78 ± 0.13 g.kg−1, WM: 1.83 ± 0.28 g.kg−1 and CM: 1.85 ± 0.26 g.kg−1). Similar to the trend observed for carbohydrate intake, considerable within-group variability was noted among goalkeepers.

The lowest value was reported in a study involving female players was 1.30 g.kg−1 [44], whereas the highest was reported in a study involving male players was 2.40 g.kg−1 [78].

Fats

As shown in Panel D, and similarly to the findings for protein intake, no clear positional differences were evident between positions regarding fat intake (GK: 30.32 ± 11.32%; D: 27.70 ± 4.43%; WM: 31.83 ± 6.25%; CM: 30.52 ± 4.46%; F: 29.78 ± 5.53%). In this context, as with the other macronutrients, goalkeepers displayed the greatest within-group variation. The lowest fat intake value was 22.30 ± 3.10% of total energy intake [75], while the highest was 45.90 ± 7.40% [78].

Sixteen observational studies involving female players were assessed (Table 5). The majority articulated their research questions clearly and adequately defined both the study population and exposures. Nonetheless, recurring methodological limitations were identified, most notably the lack of sample size justification and the absence of repeated exposure assessments over time. Blinding of outcome assessors was rare, and potential confounding variables were frequently not accounted for, thereby limiting the internal validity of findings and the strength of any causal inferences.

In the case of male players (Table 6), 46 observational studies were appraised. Most studies clearly stated their objectives and appropriately characterised their study populations. However, methodological weaknesses were again evident, including limited adjustment for confounders, inadequate reporting of sample size calculations, and restricted temporal assessment of exposures. Although follow-up rates and consistency in outcome measurement were generally satisfactory, the overall risk of bias remains moderate due to these prevalent shortcomings.

In sum, the methodological quality of the included studies was mixed. While many fulfilled key quality indicators, persistent issues such as lack of longitudinal design and inadequate control of confounding factors constrain the overall reliability and interpretability of the evidence base.

Discussion

This review synthesises the current evidence on nutrition practices in professional and semi-professional soccer players, providing important insights for practitioners working with elite male and female athletes. Overall, the findings are consistent with previous literature highlighting ongoing challenges in achieving adequate energy and macronutrient intake in elite soccer settings, despite the availability of established nutritional guidelines [10, 23, 92]. The findings highlight several key themes warranting further consideration. First, male players consistently exhibit energy intakes below their total energy expenditure, a discrepancy that remains evident even after adjusting for potential under-reporting of dietary intake, a well-recognised limitation in dietary assessment research [23]. Female players similarly demonstrate inadequate energy intake relative to expenditure, although this difference is attenuated when measurement error is accounted for, in line with previous observations in athletic populations at risk of low energy availability [92]. Second, heterogeneity in the terminology used to describe training periodisation within the microcycle limits comparability across studies and constrains the interpretation of energy and nutrient intake relative to training demands. Third, while average carbohydrate intakes in male players generally align with UEFA recommendations, overall consumption, across both pre-season and in-season periods, tends to be positioned toward the lower end of recommended ranges, a pattern also noted in applied sport nutrition contexts [10]. Female players show carbohydrate intakes consistent with recommendations during the in-season phase but at levels lower than their male counterparts. Protein intake is frequently reported as insufficient among female players and exhibits considerable variability in male players. Fat intake appears consistent across sexes and aligns with existing nutritional guidelines [10]. Fourth, there remains a notable scarcity of research exploring positional differences in energy and nutrient intake, particularly in women’s soccer. Collectively, these findings underscore the need for more rigorous, standardised research protocols and greater focus on female-specific nutritional demands within soccer.

Energy expenditure vs energy intake

The average daily energy expenditure reported for professional or semi-professional male soccer players in this review was 3529 kcal.day⁻1, with individual study values ranging from approximately 3188 to 4440 kcal.day⁻1. Both the highest and lowest reported values corresponded to studies conducted during the in-season period, whereas the only study that assessed energy expenditure during a different phase of the season reported a mean value of 3770 kcal.day⁻1 [58], carried out during the pre-season.

Given the well-documented differences between the pre-season and in-season phases in variables such as total distance covered, percentage of maximum heart rate (%HRmax), and session rating of perceived exertion (s-RPE) [93]—which reflect the distinct training objectives and periodisation strategies associated with each phase—it is reasonable to anticipate significant fluctuations in daily energy expenditure across the season. For example, during the pre-season phase, players exhibited consistently high training loads over a six-week period, with average total distances exceeding 6000 m per session and %HRmax around 70%, reflecting an emphasis on re-establishing aerobic and physical conditioning following the off-season. In contrast, the in-season phase was characterised by a progressive decrease in total distance and session duration, particularly towards the end of the competitive calendar, while %HRmax values peaked mid-season—suggesting a transition to shorter, more intense training sessions focused on tactical refinement and recovery. Moreover, session-RPE values declined markedly from the pre-season to the latter stages of the season (e.g., from approximately 447 AU to 259 AU), further supporting the notion of a reduced subjective training load. Despite these well-established seasonal variations in training demands, the current evidence base fails to adequately capture corresponding fluctuations in energy expenditure. This represents a critical gap in the literature, highlighting a mismatch between physiological demands and the temporal resolution of energy expenditure assessments.

These documented fluctuations suggest changes in energy expenditure across the competitive season. However, such variations were not reflected in the present review. The scarcity of studies assessing daily energy expenditure during the pre-season phase undermines the methodological robustness of any comparative analysis. As a result, the pre-season and off-season phases remain largely underexplored, limiting the potential for a more comprehensive understanding of energy expenditure dynamics throughout the soccer season. This underrepresentation of certain seasonal phases restricts the generalisability of findings and may mask important period-specific nutritional requirements, thereby limiting practical applicability for practitioners aiming to optimise player conditioning and recovery.

With regard to elite female soccer, the average daily energy expenditure reported in this review was 2570 kcal.day⁻1, with reported values ranging between 2300 and 3000 kcal.day⁻1 across studies. Similar to the findings observed in male players, most studies involving female athletes were conducted during the in-season period, limiting the ability to comprehensively examine variations across different phases of the competitive calendar. Nevertheless, this review identified one study conducted during the pre-season [39] and another during the off-season [33], which reported mean daily energy expenditures of 2701 kcal.day⁻1 and 2251 kcal.day⁻1, respectively. These findings appear consistent with expected fluctuations associated with training periodisation, whereby higher training loads during pre-season may increase energy demands, while reduced training volumes during the off-season are likely associated with lower energy expenditure [93]. However, the limited number of studies conducted outside the in-season period highlights an important gap in the literature and underscores the need for future longitudinal investigations examining seasonal variations in energy expenditure among elite female soccer players.

It should also be noted that the methodologies used to assess and estimate energy expenditure across the included studies were heterogeneous (as illustrated in Fig. 3). In most studies, both REE and TDEE were estimated, whereas others reported only total energy expenditure without clearly describing the calculation method. Some studies failed to report the methodology altogether. Given this methodological variability, the interpretation of energy expenditure data should be approached with caution. Such heterogeneity in assessment techniques introduces considerable uncertainty and complicates cross-study comparisons, thereby undermining the reliability of pooled estimates and calling for standardised, validated methodologies in future research.

In relation to energy intake, the mean reported in-season intake was 2758 kcal.day⁻1 for male players and 2019 kcal.day⁻1 for female players. However, these values should be interpreted with caution, as dietary intake assessment in athletic populations is subject to well-documented methodological limitations, including reliance on self-reported data and underreporting of energy intake [23]. In addition, the methodologies used to assess energy intake varied considerably across studies, which may further limit comparability and contribute to potential misestimation. Consequently, these methodological considerations should be taken into account when interpreting energy intake values and their implications for energy balance in elite soccer players.

Nonetheless, based on the data reviewed, the energy balance of both male and female elite players appeared to be negative, with estimated deficits ranging between –737 and –741 kcal.day⁻1 in male players and between –312 and –544 kcal.day⁻1 in female players, depending on the values reported across studies. These findings suggest that energy intake may not always be sufficient to match the physiological demands of elite soccer. Although direct evidence examining energy balance in this population remains limited, previous studies in professional soccer players have reported suboptimal energy and carbohydrate intakes relative to training and match demands, which may contribute to a mismatch between energy intake and expenditure [72].

However, according to Capling et al. (2017), dietary intake assessments may be subject to underreporting, with estimates suggesting a potential rate of approximately 19% [23]. When this adjustment was applied, the estimated deficits decreased to between –146 and –212 kcal.day⁻1 in male players and between –153 and –158 kcal.day⁻1 in female players. After adjustment, the negative energy balance remained statistically significant in male players, whereas in female players it was no longer significant. Taken together, these findings may indicate that, during the competitive season, energy intake among elite male players is, in some cases, lower than their estimated energy expenditure. Due to the limited scientific research analysing energy balance specifically on match days, this review did not include a dedicated analysis of this variable. However, UEFA consensus indicates that daily energy expenditure on match days in male soccer players is approximately 3500 kcal [10], which is considerably higher than the average energy intake reported in this review (2758 kcal.day⁻1). Nevertheless, it is expected that energy intake is higher on match days; therefore, this average intake is unlikely to be representative of those specific days in elite soccer. However, this correction assumes a relatively uniform degree of underreporting across studies and populations, which may oversimplify a complex and context-dependent issue.

In this context, insufficient energy intake relative to expenditure may increase the risk of low energy availability (LEA), the primary driver of Relative Energy Deficiency in Sport (REDs), a syndrome associated with impaired physiological function across multiple systems [94]. In soccer-specific contexts, a high prevalence of LEA has been reported in both male and female players, raising concerns regarding the adequacy of current nutritional practices in elite settings [95]. From a performance perspective, inadequate energy intake has been associated with impaired recovery, reduced training adaptation, and an increased risk of injury, which may negatively affect players’ availability and match performance [10, 94, 95]. From a health perspective, chronic energy deficiency may impair body composition management and disrupt endocrine function, bone health, and metabolic regulation [96, 97]. Despite these recognised risks, the behavioural and environmental factors contributing to insufficient energy intake in elite soccer players remain poorly understood and warrant further investigation.

Energetic periodization

Moreover, the literature indicates that training load is periodised across the microcycle [98, 99], with fluctuations depending on the number of matches played during the week [9]. Accordingly, daily energy expenditure is expected to vary throughout the microcycle. However, this review did not analyse outcomes according to specific training-day characteristics due to a lack of studies making such distinctions. Furthermore, the nomenclature used to describe the days within the microcycle varies across studies—some classify days relative to the match (e.g., MD–1, MD, MD + 1, MD + 2) [45, 87], while others characterise them based on training content or purpose (e.g., rest day, light training day, heavy training day, match day) [42, 44]. This inconsistency in terminology and lack of granular data limit the capacity to precisely map energy expenditure fluctuations to training load variations, thus constraining the applicability of nutritional periodisation strategies. Such gaps highlight the need for standardised definitions and more detailed monitoring within future research.

Macronutrient intake

Based on UEFA guidelines, carbohydrate (CHO) intake should range from 3 to 8 g.kg⁻1.day⁻1 during the in-season period and from 4 to 8 g.kg⁻1.day⁻1 during the pre-season. In this context, the results of this review demonstrate that both elite male and female soccer players present in-season relative CHO intakes that fall within the UEFA-recommended range, although both show potential for further optimisation. Similarly, the average relative CHO intake among male players during the pre-season was also found to align with UEFA guidelines. Despite meeting the theoretical recommendations, CHO intake appears to be closer to the lower end of the recommended range, suggesting room for improvement. Given that carbohydrate (CHO) intake is closely linked to glycogen availability and players’ energy status, increasing CHO consumption towards the upper limits of current recommendations [10] may support performance optimisation, particularly in high-intensity intermittent activities such as soccer [8, 9]. Adequate glycogen stores are recognised as critical for sustaining repeated high-intensity efforts and delaying fatigue during match play [9]. However, the tendency for CHO intake to cluster near the lower end of recommendations may reflect practical barriers faced by players, such as dietary preferences, time constraints, or lack of tailored nutritional support—factors rarely captured in quantitative assessments but critical for effective intervention [100]. Given these findings, it is important to reflect on the factors that may contribute to suboptimal CHO intake. Specifically, it is worth considering whether: 1) the 19% underreporting typically associated with daily energy intake assessments also applies to CHO intake, highlighting the need to interpret these data with such adjustment in mind [15, 23]; 2) elite players experience practical challenges in achieving higher CHO intakes, necessitating the development of individualised strategies to optimise intake; 3) UEFA’s current CHO recommendations are indeed well-aligned with the demands of elite soccer.

According to UEFA guidelines, protein intake should range between 1.6 and 2.2 g.kg⁻1.day⁻1 [10]. With regard to protein intake, this scoping review demonstrates that, similarly to CHO intake, it remains below the optimal level. While male players were found to meet the UEFA-recommended intake range, their average intake tended to cluster near the lower theoretical threshold. It is important to highlight the heterogeneity observed across studies, with considerable variation in reported protein intake. For instance, professional players in Brazil were reported to consume an average of 1.23 g.kg⁻1·day⁻1 [63], whereas another study from the same country reported a mean intake of 2.43 g.kg⁻1.day⁻1 [77]. Similarly, a goalkeeper from the English Premier League demonstrated a protein intake of 2.40 g.kg⁻1.day⁻1 [78]. It is worth noting that the methodologies employed across studies differed, thereby limiting the comparability of nutritional intake values. According to the literature, the use of combined dietary assessment tools—such as that employed in the study on the Premier League goalkeeper [79], which utilised food diaries, photographic records, and 24-h recalls—appears to reduce relative measurement error when compared with studies relying on a single tool [17, 101].

Regarding the average relative protein intake among female players, this review found it to be insufficient according to UEFA recommendations. Nonetheless, a degree of heterogeneity comparable to that observed in male studies was also evident. For instance, a study conducted on players from the Spanish first division reported an average intake of 1.20 g.kg⁻1.day⁻1 [35], while another involving professional players in Brazil reported an average of 2.00 g.kg⁻1.day⁻1 [39]. It is worth noting that the dietary assessment tools used in these studies differed, with the former relying on a 24-h recall and the latter on a 7-day food diary. The limited number of studies involving elite female players may also reflect potential structural and methodological challenges within women’s soccer research, including broader contextual factors related to the development and professionalisation of the women’s game. In addition, methodological constraints such as smaller sample sizes, limited longitudinal monitoring, and inconsistent consideration of female-specific factors may further contribute to the scarcity and heterogeneity of the available evidence. These considerations should be taken into account when interpreting the current literature on nutrition in elite female soccer players.

In contrast, the findings of this scoping review concerning average fat intake suggest that elite soccer players—both male and female—generally meet UEFA guidelines, with fat intake appearing to be the only macronutrient consistently aligned with current theoretical recommendations. This observation is consistent with previous literature indicating that fat intake in athletic populations is often adequate, likely due to its natural inclusion in a broad range of commonly consumed foods and the absence of strict nutritional periodisation strategies compared with carbohydrate intake [10, 102]. Unlike carbohydrate intake, which is closely linked to training load, glycogen availability, and match demands, fat intake tends to be less deliberately manipulated and more reflective of habitual dietary behaviours [102]. Consequently, athletes may achieve recommended fat intake levels without targeted nutritional intervention. However, while total fat intake appears adequate in quantitative terms, this does not necessarily indicate optimal dietary quality or composition. Most studies included in this review focused exclusively on total macronutrient intake, providing limited information regarding qualitative aspects such as fatty acid profile, saturated versus unsaturated fat intake, or meal composition. These factors may have important implications not only for performance and recovery, but also for long-term cardiovascular and metabolic health [102]. Therefore, although average fat intake appears aligned with current recommendations, future research should place greater emphasis on the qualitative characteristics of dietary fat intake within elite soccer populations.

Based on the reported findings regarding dietary intake in elite soccer players, it may be considered that further optimisation of both carbohydrate and protein intake is warranted. In this regard, it is essential to improve dietary assessment methodologies by combining multiple assessment tools, as was implemented in several studies included in this review [60, 78, 87]. Current approaches to dietary assessment in athletic populations are subject to several well-recognised limitations, including reliance on self-reported data [23], underreporting of intake [23], recall bias [103], and behavioural changes during recording periods [104]. Additionally, variability in practitioner interpretation and inconsistencies in data collection methods may further compromise the accuracy and reliability of dietary data [18, 19]. These limitations can lead to misestimation of energy and macronutrient intake, thereby affecting the interpretation of energy balance and nutritional adequacy. The use of combined methods—such as food diaries, photographic records, and recalls—may help mitigate some of these issues by providing complementary sources of information, improving data completeness, and reducing measurement error [17, 101]. As such, adopting multi-method approaches may enhance the methodological robustness of future research in elite soccer populations.

Moreover, while current nutritional recommendations, such as the UEFA consensus statement [10], are specifically developed for soccer and account for variations in training load and competition phases, they are primarily intended as general frameworks. As such, they may not fully capture the individual and contextual variability inherent to elite-level environments, including differences in match demands, playing position, and competition schedules. This interpretation is supported by the findings of the present review, which indicate that, although carbohydrate and protein intake generally fall within recommended ranges, values often cluster toward the lower end of these recommendations, with protein intake appearing suboptimal in female players. This pattern may suggest that there is still scope for optimisation of nutritional practices in applied settings, including closer and more individualised support from performance nutrition practitioners to better align intake with the upper limits of current recommendations when appropriate. At the same time, it may also indicate that additional research conducted directly within elite populations is warranted to further refine current recommendations and ensure their applicability to the specific demands of elite soccer.

Playing position

Daily energy expenditure is also likely to vary across playing positions, given the distinct physical demands associated with each role. According to Sarmento et al. (2024), central midfielders and wide midfielders tend to cover greater distances during matches (11,012 m and 10,894 m, respectively), which may be associated with higher energy expenditure when compared to other positions, including goalkeepers [105]. However, the findings of this scoping review did not identify clear differences in energy intake across positions, despite these theoretical differences in expenditure (GK: 2954 ± 629 kcal; D: 2857 ± 364 kcal; WM: 3012 ± 386 kcal; CM: 2778 ± 373 kcal; F: 2806 ± 458 kcal). Notably, there was no significant difference in energy intake between goalkeepers and outfield players. This apparent disconnect between position-specific energy expenditure and uniformity of energy intake suggests that players may not be adjusting their nutritional intake to match positional demands, which could have implications for optimising individualised nutrition strategies and performance outcomes.

In this regard, as carbohydrates represent the primary energy substrate utilised by soccer players [10], one would expect CHO intake to vary in accordance with differences in total distance covered and high-intensity activities, particularly high-speed running and sprinting. Indeed, external midfielders have been shown to exhibit greater physical and physiological demands, including higher sprint distances (≈330 m compared to ~ 180–280 m in other positions), as well as greater high-speed running outputs (≈ + 106 to + 191 m compared to other positions), and higher average heart rate responses during matches [105].

Consistent with these demands, this review found that average carbohydrate intake appeared to be higher among Wingers/Sides/Wide Midfielders (5.20 ± 0.65 g.kg⁻1.day⁻1), while goalkeepers tended to report lower intake values (4.15 ± 1.28 g.kg⁻1.day⁻1). These observations may reflect the greater engagement of wide players in high-intensity activities and the potentially higher carbohydrate demands associated with these roles in elite soccer. While CHO intake appeared to reflect positional demands to some extent, similar differentiation was not observed for the remaining macronutrients. This may suggest that position-specific variation in protein and fat intake is either less pronounced or insufficiently investigated within the current literature. With regard to the remaining macronutrients, no clear positional differences were evident that would allow robust conclusions to be drawn. Therefore, the positional findings presented in this review should be considered exploratory rather than definitive, particularly given the limited number of studies available and the considerable methodological heterogeneity across investigations. These findings highlight the need for future targeted research to better understand position-specific nutritional demands and to inform more individualised nutritional strategies in elite soccer.

Risk of bias

A range of methodological limitations was identified across the observational studies included in this review, which should be considered when interpreting the current body of evidence. Only a small proportion of studies provided justification for sample size—specifically, 11 of the 16 studies involving female players [33, 34, 38–42, 44, 45, 47, 48] and 3 of the 42 studies involving male players [74, 76, 85]. Furthermore, exposure was assessed more than once over time in 14 female studies [33, 35–47] and approximately half of the studies in male players [51–53, 55, 58–60, 62, 64–66, 70, 74–76, 78, 82, 83, 85, 86], limiting the ability to infer temporal relationships in several cases. In addition, several studies failed to adequately control for confounding variables or to report blinding of outcome assessors—issues that may have introduced systematic bias and compromise the internal validity of findings. In summary, while several studies exhibited sound methodological characteristics—such as well-defined outcomes and the application of appropriate statistical analyses—there remain recurring concerns that compromise overall validity. These include the absence of adequate justification for sample sizes, limited assessment of exposures over time, and insufficient control of confounding variables. To enhance the reliability of future findings on nutritional practices in soccer performance, these methodological shortcomings must be systematically addressed.

Limitations and future research

This scoping review aimed to examine the literature on nutrition exclusively within the elite soccer context, including only professional and semi-professional players aged 16 years or older. Consequently, the extrapolation of these findings to non-professional settings—such as university or amateur-level sport—is limited. In addition, the protocol was registered during the data analysis phase rather than prospectively, which should be considered when interpreting the methodological robustness of the review. Furthermore, only studies with outcomes directly related to performance were included, while studies focusing exclusively on recovery outcomes were excluded. However, recovery may indirectly influence athletic performance through its effects on physiological readiness and training capacity. It should also be noted that studies in which the competitive level of the sample was not clearly specified—and for which clarification requests to the authors went unanswered—were excluded from this review. As a result, it is possible that some studies involving elite players may not have been represented in the final analysis.

Moreover, comparisons between studies should be made with caution, given the considerable methodological heterogeneity observed across the literature included in this review. Specifically, studies differed substantially in the methods used to assess dietary intake (e.g., 24-h recalls, food diaries of varying duration, and weighed versus non-weighed records), as well as in the approaches used to estimate energy expenditure (e.g., predictive equations, doubly labelled water, and wearable-based methods). Inconsistencies in the reporting of nutritional data, including the presentation of absolute versus relative intake values, further contributed to inter-study variability. Additionally, differences in the classification and nomenclature of training days across studies (e.g., match day minus formats such as MD-1 or MD-3 versus generic classifications such as “high-load” or “intense training days”) may limit direct comparisons regarding nutritional periodisation within the weekly microcycle. Consequently, the interpretation and extrapolation of pooled findings should be approached with caution.

A further limitation identified in this review relates to the imbalance in scientific research between male and female soccer players. There remains a clear scarcity of studies focusing specifically on professional and semi-professional female players, as well as limited data concerning energy intake and energy expenditure during match days and competitive matches in both sexes. These gaps restrict the current understanding of nutritional demands across different competitive contexts and highlight the need for more longitudinal and context-specific investigations within elite populations, particularly across complete competitive microcycles and different phases of the season. Future research should prioritise the standardisation of training-day nomenclature and dietary assessment methodologies, while also promoting the use of combined dietary assessment tools to minimise measurement error and improve comparability across studies. In particular, there is an urgent need for more research focused on elite female players in order to strengthen the evidence base informing nutritional strategies in women’s soccer.

Conclusion

This scoping review provides a comprehensive synthesis of dietary intake and nutritional practices among elite soccer players, encompassing both male and female cohorts. The findings suggest a tendency towards insufficient energy intake relative to the physical demands of the sport, although these observations should be interpreted with caution given the methodological limitations associated with dietary intake and energy expenditure assessment. This pattern appeared to be more evident among male players.

Both CHO and protein intakes were frequently below recommended levels, suggesting that increasing the intake of these macronutrients may be a viable strategy to improve energy availability, support recovery, and enhance physiological adaptation. Although average CHO intake aligns with the lower end of UEFA's recommended range, this may reflect either a need for more effective dietary strategies implemented by practitioners or a mismatch between current guidelines and the practical demands of elite soccer. Research in this specific context remains limited and methodologically complex, hindering the development of precise and context-sensitive nutritional recommendations.

Protein intake appeared to be particularly suboptimal among female players, underscoring the need for greater attention to sex-specific requirements. Significant heterogeneity across studies was also observed, likely stemming from differences in methodological quality, sample characteristics, and contextual variables such as competition phase or playing level. Methodological limitations in the assessment of energy expenditure, dietary intake, and overall energy availability continue to constrain the interpretation of current evidence. The adoption of multi-method assessment approaches, integrating objective and subjective tools, may improve the accuracy and reliability of nutritional data within elite sporting contexts.

In conclusion, practitioners working within elite soccer environments should aim to bridge the gap between scientific knowledge and practical implementation. This will require an emphasis on individualised, evidence-informed nutritional strategies and closer integration with multidisciplinary performance teams to optimise both performance outcomes and player well-being. In practical terms, practitioners should place particular emphasis on optimising carbohydrate intake according to training load, match demands, and different phases of the competitive season, while also ensuring adequate daily protein intake, particularly among female players.
