Improving cognitive performance through adaptive audiovisual perceptual training in community-dwelling older adults
Authors: Yanna Ren, Zhihan Xu, Danbo Xia, Heyuan Xue, Mengmeng Yang, Shuting Huang, Yuanhua Wu, Weiping Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesAge-related declines in visual and auditory function are closely linked to cognitive deterioration in older adults. While cross-modal audiovisual training has shown potential for enhancing perceptual sensitivity, its underlying neural mechanisms and transfer effects on higher-order cognitive functions remain largely underexplored. This study aimed to investigate the effects of adaptive audiovisual perceptual training (APT) on cognitive performance and neural activation in older adults.MethodsNinety-two older adults (mean age = 66.3 years, range = 60–77) were assigned to a training group (TG; n = 47) or a control group (CG; n = 45). The TG completed a 7-day APT program, while the CG received no intervention. All participants underwent pretest and posttest assessments, including a training task, audiovisual discrimination task, attention network test (ANT), and audiovisual associative 2-back.ResultsTraining led to significant behavioral improvements in the TG, as reflected by higher accuracy and faster responses. At the neural level, the TG exhibited enhanced P3 amplitudes over parietal region in the training task, increased P3-AVI amplitudes over central and centro-parietal regions in the audiovisual discrimination task, greater P3 amplitudes over parietal region in the ANT, and increased P2 amplitudes over frontal, fronto-central, and central regions in the 2-back task.ConclusionsThese findings demonstrate that adaptive APT effectively enhances audiovisual integration, attentional processing, and working memory in older adults. The results highlight its potential as a non-pharmacological intervention for mitigating age-related cognitive decline.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-026-07646-w.Keywords: Perceptual training, Older adults, Audiovisual integration, Executive attention, Working memory

Introduction

Aging is accompanied by marked deterioration in sensory functions, including vision, hearing, touch, taste, and olfaction [1]. Memory decline in older adults has been closely associated with impairments in cross-modal information processing, which not only underpins memory encoding and retrieval but also reinforces memory performance [2]. Cross-modal integration and memory processes are believed to share common neural substrates and overlapping network structures [3]. Among all sensory modalities, vision and hearing are the most critical, responsible for processing over 80% of external information [4]. The concurrent decline of these senses in older adults has been linked to an increased incidence of Alzheimer’s disease (AD) and an accelerated trajectory of cognitive deterioration [5, 6].

Audiovisual integration (AVI) refers to the neural and cognitive mechanisms by which the brain combines visual and auditory stimuli into a coherent percept, facilitating efficient interpretation of complex multisensory environments [4]. AVI plays a critical role in older adults’ environmental awareness, information processing, and social interaction, serving as a fundamental mechanism for maintaining functional independence and overall quality of life [4, 7]. Emerging evidence suggests that despite age-related losses in visual and auditory acuity, older adults exhibit enhanced cross-modal integration of audiovisual stimuli [8–11]. This enhanced integration is thought to reflect a compensatory mechanism that helps maintain perceptual and cognitive functioning in the face of sensory decline [12, 13]. Furthermore, recent findings suggest that audiovisual perceptual training (APT) can enhance visual and auditory sensitivity in older adults, potentially strengthening multisensory integration [14, 15] and mitigating cognitive decline [16, 17]. For example, Ren et al. (2024) and Yang et al. (2018) examined changes in the P3 component related to AVI before and after APT, demonstrating improved audiovisual processing. However, these studies did not explore the transfer effects of training on higher-order cognitive functions. Anguera et al. (2013) developed a video game–based audiovisual training paradigm (NeuroRacer) combining visual discrimination (colored geometric signs) and visuomotor tracking. The training significantly enhanced cognitive control in older adults—reducing multitasking costs to levels comparable with younger adults—and these gains transferred to untrained domains such as sustained attention and working memory. Follow-up studies further revealed that the cognitive benefits were sustained for up to seven years [17]. Despite these promising findings, the neural mechanisms underlying these transfer effects have not been directly investigated, as electroencephalography (EEG) measures in Anguera et al. (2013) were obtained only during the training task, whereas transfer effects were assessed behaviorally.

In addition, other numerous APT approaches have also shown improvements in sensory sensitivity [18–24], but the transfer effects on high-order cognition have not been systematically examined. Evidence from both human [25] and animal studies [7, 26] further supports the benefits of cross-modal stimulation in enhancing perceptual sensitivity and high-order cognition. Notably, Hampton et al. (2019) reported that audiovisual stimulation in a dementia mouse model reduced amyloid-β accumulation and significantly improved working memory performance [27]. Taken together, these findings suggest that APT represents a promising and accessible cognitive intervention for community-dwelling older adults. However, its neural mechanisms and transfer effects on higher-order cognition remain inadequately understood and warrant further investigation.

Adaptive perceptual training provides a personalized and scalable approach to cognitive enhancement in older adults [28, 29]. By continuously adjusting task difficulty in response to individual performance, it sustains optimal cognitive engagement and ensures that participants are consistently challenged within their capacity. This dynamic adjustment promotes deeper cognitive processing and may enhance neuroplasticity by activating and reinforcing brain networks related to attention, working memory, and multisensory integration. Furthermore, its individualized design allows for the accommodation of diverse cognitive profiles, making it particularly well-suited for community-dwelling older adults with varying levels of functional capacity [30]. Therefore, to maximize the training effect, an adaptive APT program was designed. Moreover, previous studies have shown that older adults exhibit enhanced AVI comparing with younger adults during semantic processing [31, 32], which might induce age-related compensatory mechanism, therefore, the semantically standardized visual images and their corresponding auditory stimuli were applied in the present training paradigm (audiovisual semantic matching task). We hypothesize that, consistent with previous findings, adaptive APT may lead to significant improvements in perceptual sensitivity [18–22] and AVI capacity [14, 15]. Adaptive perceptual training has been shown to engage widespread neural circuits involved in higher-order cognitive processes [33–35]. Notably, audiovisual processing recruits fronto-parietal networks that substantially overlap with those underlying executive function [36, 37] and working memory [33, 38, 39]. Therefore, we hypothesize that improvements in audiovisual processing capacity may also generalize to enhanced attentional control and working memory performance.

To systematically evaluate the cognitive effects of adaptive APT in older adults, event-related potential (ERP) components were selected across tasks in the current study. In the training task, participants judged whether simultaneously presented visual and auditory stimuli conveyed semantically consistent information. This task engages high-level cognitive processes such as cross-modal integration, semantic evaluation, and decision-making, which are known to modulate P3 amplitudes [37, 40]. Therefore, the P3 component was used as a neural index to assess training-related cognitive changes. For the audiovisual discrimination task, the N2 and P3 components were examined as previous study [14, 41]. The N2, reflecting early conflict detection and attentional control, is sensitive to cross-modal mismatch, while the P3 is associated with later-stage stimulus evaluation and decision-making. To evaluate the effects of training on attention, the attention network test (ANT) was employed. The N1 component, which is sensitive to early sensory processing and cue-related attentional orienting, was used to index neural activity associated with the alerting and orienting networks. The P3 component, reflecting stimulus evaluation and attentional resource allocation, was used to assess neural processes related to executive attention [42, 43]. These components together offer a comprehensive view of how adaptive training influences attentional networks. For working memory, an audiovisual associative 2-back task was used. The P3 component, closely related to stimulus evaluation, memory updating, and decision-making [40, 41], was analyzed during match trials to assess the transfer effects of training on working memory performance. We further hypothesize that adaptive APT would be associated with training-related modulations of the N1, N2, and P3 components during the attention, multisensory integration, and working memory tasks, respectively.

Methods

Participants

An priori power analysis was conducted using G*Power 3.1 (test family: F tests; statistical test: ANOVA, Repeated measures, within-between interaction). The analysis assumed two groups and two measurements, with a correlation among repeated measures of r = 0.50 and nonsphericity correction ε = 1.00. The results indicated that a sample size of at least 64 participants would be required to detect a Group × Time interaction with a medium effect size (f = 0.25), α = 0.05, and power (1 − β) = 0.975. To ensure sufficient power and account for potential attrition, we targeted a minimum of 85 participants and ultimately enrolled 94 community-dwelling older adults, randomly assigned 1:1 to a training group (TG) or a passive control group (CG). After two participants in the CG withdrew, 47 participants in the TG (M = 66.2 ± 6.1 years, range = 60–77, 29 female) and 45 participants in the CG (M = 66.5 ± 7.6 years, range = 60–77, 28 female) completed the study. All participants demonstrated normal cognitive function, with screening scores above 26 on a cognitive screening module within the Xinyue Neuropsychological Cognitive Assessment System (Shanghai Xinyue Yiliao S&T Co., Ltd., China; Software Copyright Registration No. 2019SR0364607). None of the participants reported the use of central nervous system-related medications, and all provided written informed consent. The study was approved by the ethics committee of the Second Affiliated Hospital of Guizhou University of Traditional Chinese Medicine.

Design and procedure

All participants in the TG completed a 7-day consecutive APT independently in a sound-attenuated room, with video monitoring from the main control room to ensure protocol adherence. On Day 1, prior to the onset of training, participants underwent a baseline cognitive assessment (pretest), which included a Level 1 training task (used to assess training effects), an audiovisual discrimination task, the ANT, and an audiovisual associative 2-back (used to assess transfer effects). Following completion of the pretest, participants began the adaptive training program on the same day. On Day 7, after completion of the final training session, the same test battery was administered as a posttest. Before the posttest, participants were given a rest period of at least 1 h (with the duration adjusted based on individual needs) to minimize potential fatigue effects. All stimuli were presented, and behavioral data were collected using E-Prime 3.0 software (Psychology Software Tools, Inc., Pittsburgh, PA, USA). The CG completed the same pretest and posttest cognitive assessments over a 7-day interval without receiving any training intervention.

Adaptive APT

The visual stimuli consisted of black-and-white line drawings (5.2 cm × 3.8 cm) selected from the Snodgrass and Vanderwart (1980) standardized image set, including images of a cow, dog, bird, fly, frog, horse, mouse, pig, goat, fox, rooster, cat, trumpet, piano, bell, car, telephone, and gun. The corresponding auditory stimuli were matched 65-dB sounds downloaded from http://www.tuke88.com. Difficulty of identification was systematically manipulated by concurrently degrading visual and auditory stimuli. For visual stimuli, Gaussian blur was applied with increasing kernel sizes: no blur at level 1, a kernel size of 5 at level 2, 10 at level 3, 15 at level 4, and so on. For auditory stimuli, white noise was proportionally added to the original sound: no noise at level 1, 5% noise at level 2, 10% at level 3, and so forth. The manipulations were synchronized across levels and implemented using custom scripts in MATLAB (Fig. 1A).

A Adaptive audiovisual perceptual training protocol showing the mean difficulty level achieved across seven training days. The assessment tasks before and after perceptual training, including audiovisual discrimination task (B), attention network test (C), and audiovisual associative 2-back task (D)

The experiment began with the presentation of a central fixation (+) for 3000 ms on a white background, followed by visual and auditory stimuli presented in a randomized order, each lasting 800 ms. Interstimulus interval (ISI) varied randomly between 2000 and 3000 ms. The participants were instructed to judge whether the stimuli were matched by pressing the right mouse button for a match trial and the left button for a non-match trial. To ensure balanced responses, each difficulty level included 20 match trials and 20 non-match trials. All participants began training at level 1. Task difficulty increased after the successful performances (accuracy > 90%) and decreased after a failure (accuracy < 70%) [14]. If performance fell between these thresholds (70% < accuracy < 90%), the same difficulty level was repeated. Training lasted for one hour per day, with self-paced breaks between blocks.

Cognitive assessment

Cognitive function was assessed at pretest and posttest using measures of audiovisual perceptual ability, audiovisual integration, attention, and working memory to examine training and transfer effects of adaptive APT. The Level 1 training task (administered at a fixed difficulty level, in contrast to the adaptive training sessions) assessed training effects, whereas the audiovisual discrimination task, ANT, and audiovisual associative 2-back task assessed transfer effects. Assessments were conducted before training on Day 1 and after completion of the final training session on Day 7, following a rest period of at least 1 h. Behavioral performance and EEG data were collected for all tasks.

All EEG data were recorded using the BrainVision system (Brain Products GmbH, Germany) with 32 Ag/AgCl electrodes mounted on a standard electrode cap. Vertical eye movements and blinks were monitored via electrooculography (EOG) using an electrode positioned approximately 1 cm below the participant’s left eye (VEOG). Horizontal eye movements were recorded using an electrode placed approximately 1 cm lateral to the outer canthus of the left eye (HEOG). Electrode impedance was maintained below 5 kΩ throughout the recording, and the EEG signals were digitized at a sampling rate of 1000 Hz.

Training effect

Training effects were evaluated using the Level 1 training task, which included 68 match trials and 68 nonmatch trials (Fig. 1A).

Transfer effect

Audiovisual discrimination task: The visual stimuli (V) consisted of 10% contrast Gabor patches (1.5 cycles/degree) with a visual angle of 5° (52 mm × 52 mm), presented either vertically (V target) or horizontally (V non-target) on a 60-cm gray background monitor (RGB: 192, 192, 192). The auditory stimuli (A) were sinusoidal tones of 1000 Hz (A target) and 500 Hz (A non-target), delivered at 60 dB with a 10-ms cosine ramp (rise/fall) via centrally positioned speakers located behind the monitor. Audiovisual stimuli (AV) paired corresponding auditory and visual targets or non-targets (Fig. 1B). Each trial began with a central fixation “+” displayed for 3000 ms, followed by the presentation of A, V, or AV stimuli for 100 ms, with a randomly varied ISI ranging from 1800 to 3000 ms. Participants were instructed to press the left mouse button as quickly and accurately as possible in response to target stimuli. This task comprised 360 trials, including 30 target trials and 90 nontarget trials for each of the A, V, and AV. The total task was divided into two sessions with a self-paced break in between.

Attention network test: The ANT was adapted from Fan’s classical design [44]. The experiment began with the presentation of a central fixation “+” with the display duration randomly varying between 400 and 1600 ms. Then, a visual cue “*” (cue type: no, center, double or spatial) appeared for 100 ms. Four hundred milliseconds later, the target stimulus (target type: congruent, incongruent or neural) was randomly presented either above or below the fixation cross. The participants were instructed to respond to the direction of the central arrow in the sequence as quickly and accurately as possible by pressing the left key if the arrow pointed left and the right key if it pointed right (Fig. 1C). The target stimulus disappeared either upon the participant’s response or automatically after 1700 ms if no response was made. Following the disappearance of the stimulus, the fixation cross remained on the screen for a short period, ensuring that the total duration of each trial was 4000 ms. The formal experiment was conducted only if the accuracy in the practice session exceeded 80%. The ratio for each cue type trial was 1:1:1:1, and the ratio for each target type trial was 1:1:1. There were 384 trials in total, which were divided into two blocks with a self-timed break between them.

Audiovisual associative 2-back task: Adapted from our previous study [45], the audiovisual stimuli was similar with training task, including bird, cat, rooster, cow, dog, elephant, frog and horse. Semantically congruent visual and auditory stimuli were presented simultaneously for 500 ms, followed by a 2500 ms interstimulus interval (Fig. 1D). Participants were instructed to press the left mouse button if the current stimulus matched the one presented two trials earlier as accurately as possible; otherwise, they were to withhold their response. The ratio of matched to non-matched trials was 1:1. Each block consisted of 22 trials, and a total of 6 blocks were administered, with self-paced breaks between blocks.

Data analysis

Behavioral data

Adaptive APT

Changes in task difficulty over seven consecutive days were analyzed using a linear mixed-effects model, with Satterthwaite’s approximation for degrees of freedom.

Cognitive assessment

Accuracy and response time (RT) were computed separately for each participant in the training task, the audiovisual discrimination task, and the ANT. For the audiovisual associative 2-back task, accuracy, miss rates, and false alarm rates were analyzed. RTs were calculated as the mean of all responses within ± 2.5 standard deviations of each participant’s average. The data were analyzed using mixed-design ANOVA, with group as a between-subject factor and test (and conditions, where applicable) as within-subject factors, with Greenhouse–Geisser corrections applied when necessary, followed by Bonferroni-adjusted pairwise comparisons. Additionally, for the ANT, attention network indices were calculated from RTs as follows: alerting (RTno cue – RTcenter cue), orienting (RTcenter cue – RTspatial cue), and executive control (RTincongruent cue – RTcongruent cue) [42, 46], and analyzed via 2 (Group) × 2 (Test) mixed-design ANOVA, with group as a between-subject factor and test as within-subject factors .

EEG data

Preprocessing

EEG data were processed using MATLAB R2021b and the EEGLAB toolbox (Swartz Center for Computational Neuroscience, La Jolla, CA, USA). Data were recorded from a 32-channel system following the international 10/20 configuration. HEOG and VEOG artifacts were removed, and data were re-referenced to TP9 and TP10. A 0.1–100 Hz bandpass filter and a 49–51 Hz notch filter were applied. Data were epoched into 1000 ms segments (− 200 to 800 ms relative to target onset), and baseline-corrected using the − 200 to 0 ms window. Independent component analysis (ICA) was subsequently applied to identify and remove artifacts, including ocular activity, line noise, muscle activity, head movements, and electrocardiographic contamination. In addition, trials with EEG amplitudes exceeding ± 100 µV were automatically rejected from further analyses. Grand-average waveforms were computed per stimulus type and electrode.

Statistical analysis

The peak latency of each ERP component was first identified from the grand-average waveforms across both pretest and posttest sessions in the TG and CG. Analysis time windows were then defined around the peak latency to capture the component activity across the maximum number of overlapping conditions. Mean amplitudes were calculated within these time windows. A 20-ms window (± 10 ms) was used for the early components (N1, P2, and N2), whereas a 40-ms window (± 20 ms) was used for the later component (P3) due to its broader temporal characteristics. Lateralization effects were examined within each region of interest (ROI). When no significant lateralization was observed, the electrode showing the maximal mean amplitude within the ROI was selected as the representative electrode for subsequent analyses and visualization. ERP amplitudes were analyzed using mixed-design ANOVAs, with group as a between-subject factor and test (and ROI, where applicable) as within-subject factors. Greenhouse–Geisser corrections were applied when necessary, and Bonferroni-adjusted post hoc tests were conducted for pairwise comparisons.

Training effect

The P3 associated with attentional allocation and stimulus evaluation [40], was analyzed at parietal electrodes (P7, P3, Pz, P4, P8), where audiovisual congruency effects are typically maximal [14, 37, 40]. P3 peaked at ~ 550 ms, with the largest mean amplitude observed at electrode Pz. Accordingly, a 530–570 ms time window was selected for analysis. Mean amplitudes within the predefined time window at representative electrode Pz were submitted to a 2 (Group: TG, CG) × 2 (Test: Pre, Post) × 2 (Stimulus: Congruent, Incongruent) mixed-design ANOVA, with group as a between-subject factor and test and stimulus as within-subject factors.

Transfer effect

Audiovisual discrimination task: N2 and P3 components were analyzed to assess early detection and multisensory integration [41, 47, 48]. N2 peaked at ~ 300 ms (290–310 ms window) and P3 at ~ 410 ms (390–430 ms window). ROIs for N2 included frontal (F7, F3, Fz, F4, F8), fronto-central (FC5, FC1, FC2, FC6), and central regions (C3, Cz, C4); for P3: central (C3, Cz, C4), centro-parietal (CP5, CP1, CP2, CP6), and parietal regions (P7, P3, Pz, P4, P8). Mean amplitudes within the predefined time window were analyzed via one-way ANOVAs within each ROI. As no lateralization effects emerged, the representative electrodes with the maximal mean amplitude (N2: Fz, FC1, Cz; P3: Cz, CP1, Pz) were used in subsequent analysis. Audiovisual integration was quantified using difference waveforms calculated as [ERPAV – (ERPA + ERPV)] (Supplementary Figure S1). The N2-AVI and P3-AVI amplitudes were defined as the mean amplitudes of the difference waveform within the corresponding time windows.

Attention network test: The N1 component was analyzed to index alerting and orienting functions, whereas the P3 component was analyzed to index executive control processes [42, 46]. Unlike later ERP components that exhibited a focal parietal maximum, the N1 component in the ANT showed a broad and bilateral parieto-occipital distribution. Accordingly, mean amplitudes were extracted from a cluster of bilateral parieto-occipital electrodes (P3, P4, P7, P8, O1, O2), consistent with established ANT–ERP studies [42, 46], to improve signal stability and reduce inter-individual variability. the N1 peaked at ~ 200 ms; therefore, mean amplitudes were extracted within a 190–210 ms time window. The P3 peaked at ~ 500 ms; accordingly, mean amplitudes were extracted within a 480–520 ms window from parietal electrodes, with maximal activity observed at the representative parietal electrode Pz. ERP-based attention network effects were computed following previous studies [42, 46]: alerting amplitude = amplitude center cue -amplitude no cue, orienting amplitude = amplitude spatial cue -amplitude center cue, and executive control amplitude = amplitude congruent target -amplitude incongruent target. Mean values within the predefined time windows were submitted to 2 (Group) × 2 (Test) mixed-design ANOVA, with group as a between-subject factor and test as within-subject factors.

Associative Audiovisual 2-Back: The P2 linked to perceptual and attentional processing [49, 50], was analyzed from match trials. P2 peaked at ~ 150 ms (140–160 ms window). Based on pointwise running t-tests, three ROIs were selected: frontal (F7, F3, Fz, F4, F8), fronto-central (FC5, FC1, FC2, FC6), and central regions (C3, Cz, C4). Mean amplitudes within the predefined time window were analyzed via one-way ANOVAs within each ROI. As no lateralization effects were observed, electrodes with the maximal mean amplitude (Fz, FC1, Cz) were selected as representative electrodes for a 2 (Group) × 2 (Test) × 3 (ROI) mixed-design ANOVA, with group as a between-subject factor and test and ROI as within-subject factors.

Results

Behavioral results

Adaptive APT

A significant positive effect of training day on task difficulty was found, indicating that task difficulty levels increased over time, B = 0.67, SE = 0.02, z = 29.77, p <0.001. The intercept was estimated at B = 1.82, SE = 0.18, z = 10.33, p < 0.001. The variance of the random intercepts was 0.98, suggesting substantial between-subject variability in baseline difficulty (Fig. 1A).

Training effect

2(group: TG, CG) × 2(Test: Pre, Post) × 2(Stimulus: Congruent, Incongruent) mixed-design ANOVA for accuracy revealed a significant main effect of stimulus (Table 1). Mixed-design ANOVA for RTs, there was a significant main effect of test, stimulus, and significant interactions of test × group and stimulus × group (Table 1). Post hoc pairwise comparisons of the test × group interaction revealed significantly faster responses at posttest than at pretest for both groups (Fig. 2A). In the TG, RT decreased significantly in both the congruent trials [t(46) = 7.197, p < 0.001, d = 1.047] and incongruent trials [t(46) = 9.343, p < 0.001, d = 1.363]. Similarly, in the CG, RT was significantly lower at posttest than at pretest in both the congruent trials [t(44) = 4.121, p < 0.001, d = 0.614] and incongruent trials [t(44) = 4.990, p < 0.001, d = 0.744]. However, two-tailed t-tests revealed that the RT reduction (ΔRT) was significantly greater in the TG than in the CG for both congruent trials (84 ms vs. 44 ms, p = 0.008) and incongruent trials (96 ms vs. 45 ms, p < 0.001).

Transfer effect

Audiovisual discrimination task

A 2 (Group: TG, CG) × 2 (Test: Pre, Post) × 3 (Stimulus: A, V, AV) mixed-design ANOVA on accuracy revealed a significant main effect of test and stimulus (Table 1). Significant interactions were observed for group × test, group × stimulus, and group × test × stimulus (Table 1; Fig. 3A). To clarify the nature of the group × test × stimulus interaction (Supplementary Figure S2), follow-up post hoc analyses were conducted separately for the TG and CG, with pairwise comparisons adjusted using the Bonferroni correction. Within the TG, accuracy was significantly higher at posttest than at pretest across all stimulus conditions (all ps ≤ 0.007), including A [t(46) = -3.044, p = 0.004, d = − 0.444], V [t(46) = -2.793, p = 0.007, d = − 0.440], and AV [t(46) = -3.989, p < 0.001, d = − 0.582]. Whereas no significant pretest–posttest differences were observed in CG (all ps ≥ 0.088). Within TG at pretest, accuracy for AV was significantly higher than for A [t(46) = 2.935, p = 0.005, d = 0.428] and V [t(46) = 3.624, p < 0.001, d = 0.529]. At posttest, accuracy in TG was higher for AV [t(46) = 3.516, p = 0.002, d = 0.498] and A [t(46) = 1.476, p = 0.012, d = 0.384] than for V, with no significant difference between AV and A [t(46) = 1.027, p = 0.462, d = 0.094]. Within CG, accuracy for AV was significantly higher than for A (all ps ≤ 0.013) and V (all ps ≤ 0.001) at both pretest and posttest. Analysis of RTs revealed a significant main effect of stimulus (Table 1), with pairwise comparisons indicating faster responses to AV than to both A (p < 0.001) and V (p < 0.001).

Attention network test

A 2 (Group: TG, CG) × 2 (Test: Pre, Post) × 4 (Cue type: No, Center, Double, Spatial) × 2 (Target type: Congruent, Incongruent) mixed-design ANOVA for accuracy revealed a significant main effect of cue type and target type (Table 1). Discussion

This study investigated the effects of adaptive APT on cognitive performance and neural processing in community-dwelling older adults. Overall, the findings suggest training-related modulations across multiple behavioral and neural measures. As training progressed, task difficulty was adaptively increased, accompanied by consistent performance improvements across training sessions. At the behavioral level, overall improvements from pretest to posttest were observed across assessment tasks, with the exception of the 2-back task. At the neural level, the TG exhibited robust pretest–posttest changes across all cognitive assessment tasks. Specifically, in the training task, enhanced P3 amplitudes were observed over parietal region. In the audiovisual discrimination task, P3-AVI amplitudes were increased over central and centro-parietal regions. In the ANT, greater posttest P3 amplitudes were evident over parietal region. In the audiovisual associative 2-back task, match trials were associated with increased P2 amplitudes over frontal, fronto-central, and central regions.

Consistent with previous findings [14, 15, 18, 20, 22], participants demonstrated progressive improvement over the course of training. Aligned with prior work [14], the present study employed a self-adaptive training program, that dynamically adjusted task difficulty in real time based on individual performance. This design ensured that task demands were continuously tailored to each participant’s capabilities, maintaining an optimal level of challenge conducive to sustained cognitive engagement and efficient learning [28–30]. Such individualized pacing likely contributed to enhanced attentional focus while minimizing disengagement [29]. Furthermore, older adults retain a degree of neuroplasticity, enabling functional reorganization of cognitive networks in response to training [51]. Repeated and progressively demanding exposure to audiovisual tasks may have reinforced neural pathways associated with multisensory integration, executive control, and working memory [52]. The inclusion of real-time feedback at each difficulty level likely enhanced learning by allowing participants to monitor their performance and iteratively refine cognitive strategies [30]. With increased familiarity with the task structure, participants may have gradually adopted more efficient cognitive strategies, potentially contributing to reduced response times and enhanced accuracy. Additionally, the perception of incremental improvement throughout the training process might have fostered motivation and self-efficacy, which have been associated with greater learning persistence and performance enhancement [53].

To further assess training-related effects, behavioral and neural responses were evaluated at pretest and posttest in the Level 1 training task. Both the TG and CG showed improvements in behavioral performance at posttest; however, the ΔRT was significantly greater in the TG than in the CG. Additionally, only the TG exhibited significantly enhanced P3 amplitudes following training, whereas no corresponding neural changes were observed in the CG. The behavioral gains observed at posttest in both groups suggest the presence of practice-related effects, such as increased task familiarity and motor speeding. However, the larger improvement in the TG compared to the CG, accompanied by a selective increase in P3 amplitude, may reflect training-related enhancement beyond general practice effects. Given that the P3 component has been widely associated with attentional resource allocation and stimulus evaluation processes [40, 41], the selective enhancement of P3 amplitude in the TG may indicate training-related modulation of late-stage attentional and evaluative processing rather than generalized behavioral improvement. During the adaptive training phase, the TG was repeatedly exposed to progressively degraded visual and auditory stimuli, requiring continuous adjustment to increased perceptual and attentional demands [28, 29]. Such sustained exposure may have facilitated experience-dependent neural adaptation, resulting in more efficient or more robust engagement of attentional and evaluative mechanisms that became detectable even under simplified task conditions at posttest [54, 55]. Although the TG exhibited both greater behavioral improvement and enhanced P3 amplitudes, the correlation between changes in P3 amplitude and RT gain was not significant. This dissociation between behavioral and neural outcomes may suggest that repeated task exposure is sufficient to facilitate overt response performance, but may not necessarily be accompanied by proportional changes in the underlying neural processing mechanisms.

Training-related improvements were observed across all transfer tasks at either the behavioral or neural level; however, in the 2-back task, the effects appeared to be primarily reflected in ERP measures (e.g., P2 amplitude), with no significant changes observed in behavioral performance. In the audiovisual discrimination task, the TG showed higher accuracy when response to untrained visual Gabor patches and auditory tones at posttest, suggesting that training effects may have generalized beyond the specific stimuli used during intervention [56]. These gains point to potential enhancements in perceptual sensitivity [14], which could support more efficient multisensory detection and integration. This interpretation is further supported by ERP evidence: P3-AVI amplitudes were elevated in central and centro-parietal regions. The increased P3 may indicate greater engagement in stimulus evaluation and more effective allocation of attentional resources to integrated audiovisual inputs [40, 41]. Previous study have evidenced that the AVI was higher in attended condition than unattended condition [48, 57], therefore, it is reasonable for higher P3-AVI mean amplitude for TG at posttest. The convergence of behavioral and electrophysiological findings suggests that adaptive APT is associated with enhanced perceptual sensitivity and strengthened cross-modal processing in older adults.

In the ANT, the TG exhibited increased orienting efficiency, as indexed by RT-based difference measures, potentially reflecting more effective spatial attentional allocation at the behavioral level. Prior studies suggest that adaptive training maintains attentional engagement by continuously adjusting task difficulty based on individual performance, which may enhance the functioning of attentional networks involved in spatial cueing [28, 29]. Notably, the training-related behavioral effect was specific to orienting, which in the ANT is explicitly defined as the RT benefit conferred by spatial cues [44, 58]. At the neural level, significantly increased executive control-related ERP effects were observed at posttest, indexed by P3 amplitude difference measures, possibly reflecting enhanced attentional allocation and stimulus evaluation processes [40]. It is worth noting that the training-related effects observed at the behavioral and neural levels did not fully converge across attentional networks. Whereas behavioral improvements were specific to orienting, neural modulation was primarily evident for executive control. This dissociation is not unexpected, given that RT-based difference measures and ERP-based amplitude difference scores index different stages and aspects of attentional processing. RT measures primarily reflect the efficiency of the final response output, whereas ERP indices capture component-specific neural processing related to sensory gain, stimulus evaluation, and attentional resource allocation [42, 43]. Consequently, training-related changes may manifest at the neural level without a one-to-one correspondence at the behavioral level, or vice versa, particularly following short-term interventions. Taken together, these findings suggest training-related modulation of executive control processes at the neural level. Neuroimaging evidence further supports this interpretation, showing that cognitive training can preserve caudate volume and white matter integrity in regions linked to cognitive control [59]. Additionally, given that attention modulates AVI across processing stages, with greater integration under attended conditions [57], the observed enhancements in P3-AVI may reflect the combined effects of improved attentional regulation and strengthened multisensory integration.

In the audiovisual associative 2-back task, no behavioral improvement was observed in either the TG or the CG; however, the TG exhibited a significant posttest increase in P2 amplitude, whereas no significant change was observed in the CG. Given the partial overlap in stimulus features between the 2-back task and the adaptive training paradigm, it is plausible that perceptual or associative processing strategies established during training were reused during task performance. Although the cognitive demands of the two tasks differ, both rely on early perceptual encoding and cross-modal integration processes, which may give rise to overlapping neural engagement rather than reflecting broad transfer across cognitive domains [56]. Importantly, the overlap in stimulus categories suggests that the observed ERP-related modulation may reflect changes in perceptual or strategy-based processing, rather than clear evidence of generalized improvements in working memory function. The P2 component has been linked to early stimulus discrimination and attentional selection processes [60], and the observed enhancement may reflect altered allocation of attentional resources or changes in early stage perceptual processing. The absence of similar P2 modulation in the CG further suggests that the effect cannot be fully explained by stimulus familiarity alone, but may instead index training related changes in early stage neural processing [56]. One possible interpretation is that APT engaged neural mechanisms associated with experience that support early stage processing, which may be particularly relevant in older adults. Consistent with prior work suggesting that associative memory deficits in aging are not readily alleviated by multimodal presentation alone [61], the present findings may indicate the involvement of compensatory processes at early stages of information processing, as reflected by enhanced P2 responses. Alternatively, because the audiovisual stimuli were semantically congruent and identical to those used during training, the enhanced P2 responses may also reflect facilitative effects of semantic integration [45]. Previous studies have shown that semantically congruent audiovisual inputs can facilitate perceptual processing and support memory encoding through shared conceptual representations [62], which may contribute to the observed neural modulation in the training group. However, it remains unclear whether the increased amplitude reflects greater processing efficiency, compensatory recruitment, or increased task related demands, and future studies are needed to clarify the functional significance of these neural changes.

Taken together, converging behavioral and neural evidence suggests that adaptive APT improves task-specific performance and is associated with training-related modulation of multiple cognitive processing stages in older adults. ERP indices indicated enhanced perceptual integration and attentional control, suggesting improved efficiency in early-stage information processing under working memory demands. Although partial stimulus overlap cannot be ruled out, the absence of comparable changes in the control group and the specificity of the observed neural modulation argue against a simple familiarity-based explanation. At the same time, although effect size measures were reported across all tasks, the present findings primarily reflect task-specific and neural-level modulation. Accordingly, interpretations regarding clinical or real-world significance should be made with caution, as the current study did not include clinical endpoints or functional outcome measures. The magnitude of the observed effect sizes appears to be broadly comparable to those reported in prior cognitive training studies [14, 15, 45]. Therefore, the present findings highlight the potential of adaptive APT as a non-pharmacological approach for supporting cognitive vitality in aging, while underscoring the need for future studies to establish the functional and long-term significance of these effects.

Limitations

Several limitations of the present study should be acknowledged. First, the use of a passive control group limits the ability to fully separate training-specific effects from non-specific influences such as expectancy, placebo-like effects, or general task engagement. This design choice was based on practical constraints and the intensive nature of the training protocol. However, future studies should include a structurally matched but cognitively neutral active control group and incorporate explicit assessments of expectancy and motivation to improve the interpretability and internal validity of the findings. Second, the lack of a younger comparison group precludes conclusions about the age specificity of the observed neural modulations. Including younger adults or modeling age as a moderating factor would help clarify whether training effects differ across the lifespan. Finally, training effects were assessed only at the immediate post-test, leaving open the question of long-term retention. Future research should incorporate follow-up assessments (e.g., 1–3 months post-training) to evaluate the durability and translational value of adaptive audiovisual perceptual training in older adults.
