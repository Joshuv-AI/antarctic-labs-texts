Predicting risk of postpartum hemorrhage associated with vaginal delivery of twins: A retrospective study
Authors: Zhaodong Liu, Rongxin Chen, Huihui Huang, Jianying Yan, Caihong Jiang
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Many studies have only focused on the risk factors for postpartum hemorrhage (PPH) in singleton vaginal deliveries and twin cesarean deliveries. We analyzed the factors of influencing PPH occurrence in twin vaginal deliveries and developed a nomogram for clinical application. This retrospective study included 274 pregnant women with twin pregnancies who were hospitalized for delivery from January 2014 to December 2018. The patients opted for vaginal delivery and experienced spontaneous labor. Univariate analysis of PPH risk factors was performed. Multivariate analysis was performed using the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) to obtain relevant factors and build a prediction model, which was presented as a nomogram. The model was internally validated by bootstrap self-sampling method. Model accuracy was evaluated with the concordance index (C-index). There were 36 (13.14%) and 238 (86.9%) patients in the PPH and no PPH groups, respectively. Univariate analysis identified twin chorionicity, hypertensive disorders complicating pregnancy (HDCP), anemia in pregnancy, delivery mode of the second twin, oxytocin use during labor, postpartum curettage, cervical laceration, intrapartum fever, fibrinogen degradation products (FDP), and platelet count (PLT) as significant PPH factors. On multivariate analysis, HDCP, anemia in pregnancy, intrapartum fever, oxytocin use during labor, fetal distress, PLT, direct bilirubin, and FDP were noted as significant PPH factors and were included in the prediction model. A C-index of 0.816 was noted after internal validation, and the calibration curve showed good consistency. We developed a model to predict PPH risk in the vaginal delivery of twin pregnancies and visualized it with a nomogram that can be applied clinically to assess PPH risk and aid PPH prevention.

1. Introduction

Postpartum hemorrhage (PPH) is the most common cause of obstetric bleeding and one of the leading causes of maternal mortality. According to the World Health Organization in 2014, obstetric hemorrhage accounted for 27% of maternal deaths globally, while data from the China Maternal and Child Health Surveillance conducted in 2017 showed that obstetric hemorrhage accounted for 29% of deaths. Twin pregnancies are a high-risk factor for PPH.[1] Multiple studies showed that twin pregnancies were more prone to PPH than singleton pregnancies.[2,3] With the improvements in assisted reproductive technology, the incidence of twin pregnancies has significantly increased. A study of twin pregnancies in Canada showed no significant differences in adverse perinatal outcomes, neonatal deaths, and long-term neurodevelopmental follow-up between vaginal and planned cesarean delivery groups.[4,5] Thus, vaginal delivery is recommended for twin pregnancies when conditions are suitable to reduce the rate of cesarean section in China.[6] A previous study by Suzuki S points out that gestational age, birth weight, induction of labor, oxytocin administration during labor are risk factors for postpartum hemorrhage after vaginal delivery of twins. However, the existing researches do not establish a predict model to decrease the PPH of vaginal delivery of twins, this study aims to develop and validate a model to decrease the PPH of vaginal delivery of twins. To reduce the incidence of PPH associated with the vaginal delivery of twins, it was important to develop a model to assess the risk of PPH and apply this model clinically to prevent PPH. In this study, we analyzed the factors influencing the occurrence of PPH in the vaginal delivery of twins, developed a prediction model, and visualized the model with a nomogram. We aimed to guide obstetricians in assessing the risk of PPH and to develop therapeutic strategies to reduce the occurrence of severe PPH and maternal death associated with the delivery of twins.

2. Methods

2.1. Study design and patients

In this retrospective study, we included 274 pregnant women with twin pregnancies who were admitted to Fujian Maternity and Child Health Hospital, Affiliated Hospital of Fujian Medical University (a tertiary obstetric center in southeast China) from January 2014 to December 2018. The women opted for vaginal delivery and had spontaneous contractions prior to labor. We excluded women with fetal anomalies, placenta previa, intrauterine fetal death, cardiovascular diseases, immune diseases, and hematologic diseases. We also excluded women who were transferred to cesarean section before delivery.

The pregnant women were divided into PPH and no PPH groups based on whether PPH eventually occurred. PPH was defined as excessive bleeding ≥ 500 mL after vaginal delivery and ≥ 1000 mL after cesarean delivery within 24 hours post birth. The estimation of bleeding volume was by the weighing method or volumetric method.[7] Anemia in pregnancy was defined as a hemoglobin (Hb) concentration during pregnancy < 110 g/L. According to the Hb level, anemia was classified as mild (100–109 g/L), moderate (70–99 g/L), or severe (40–69 g/L).[8] Routine blood count, coagulation profile, and biochemistry parameters were obtained after admission to the hospital and before delivery. The medical data of the 274 pregnant women were collected and recorded, including general information, chorionicity, complications and comorbidities, delivery, intrapartum interventions, blood loss monitoring, and maternal outcome.

We aimed to identify high-risk factors for PPH associated with the vaginal delivery of twins by analyzing the following: mode of conception, chorionicity, complications, presence of premature rupture of membranes, scarred uterus, hypertensive disorders complicating pregnancy (HDCP), blood pressure before delivery, laboratory test results, presence of placental abruption, fetal distress, fetal previa in both fetuses, mode of delivery, whether manual intervention was performed for the second infant, presence of postpartum perineal laceration, cervical laceration, whether a curettage was performed, and whether fever was present during delivery. Logistic regression was performed with the least absolute shrinkage, and selection operator (LASSO) was used to establish the prediction equation, which was presented as a nomogram.

2.2. Statistical analysis

R software version 4.0.2 was used for data cleaning, statistical analysis, and graphing. The mean ± standard deviation was used for data that conformed to a normal distribution, while the median [interquartile range] was used for non-normally distributed data. Frequency (percentage) was used to represent categorical data. Continuous data were analyzed with the t-test or Wilcoxon rank test, as appropriate. Categorical data were analyzed with the chi-square test, corrected chi-square test, or Fisher exact test. A P-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. LASSO logistic regression was performed using the glmnet package to build the prediction model. Thereafter, the rms package was used to build the nomogram. Missing data were handled using appropriate methods. For variables with missingness below 10%, multiple imputation was performed using the “mice” package in R software. Multiple imputation was a widely accepted method that generated multiple plausible values for missing data based on observed values and their relationships. By creating several complete datasets with imputed values, we obtained more robust estimates and accounted for uncertainty introduced by the missing data. The model was internally validated using the Bootstrap sampling method with 1000 replicate samples, and the C-index of the model was calculated using the Harrell C-statistic to verify the discriminative power of the model. The C-index ranged from 0.5 (no discriminative power) to 1.0 (good discriminative power).

4. Discussion

Globally, the incidence of twin pregnancies was increasing and more attention was being paid to the vaginal delivery of twins.[9] Twin pregnancy was one of the main causes of PPH, and the incidence of PPH in vaginal deliveries was higher than that in cesarean deliveries in twin pregnancies. Easter et al reported that the probability of PPH for vaginal delivery in twin pregnancies was 9.1%, which was significantly higher than that noted for elective cesarean delivery (4.9%; P < .01).[10] If the occurrence of PPH in twin pregnancies can be predicted, strategies can be utilized to reduce its occurrence and any associated complications, which will help reduce maternal mortality. However, many studies have only focused on the risk factors for PPH in singleton vaginal deliveries and twin cesarean deliveries. Thus, our study aimed to fill this gap in the literature.

One advantage of our study was that the model was constructed by LASSO logistic regression. LASSO regression was achieved by generating a penalty function to compress the coefficients of the variables in the regression model to prevent overfitting and mitigate the effects of notable covariance. LASSO regression was initially proposed by Tibshirani and was suitable for fitting models with numerous variables and a small number of instances of each variable. LASSO regression could treat all independent variables simultaneously compared to traditional stepwise regression, and this improvement made the model much more stable.[11] Our study also visualized the model in the form of a nomogram to facilitate clinical application.[12]

PPH and HDCP caused nearly 40% of all maternal deaths in China. Several studies have reported that HDCP significantly increases the risk of PPH in singleton pregnancies and was also a risk factor for PPH in twin pregnancies.[13–18] Some scholars found that the risk of PPH in pregnant women with preeclampsia was 1.53 times higher than in those without hypertension, and the risk of PPH was 2.29 times higher in pregnant women with preeclampsia who delivered vaginally.[19] Women with HDCP in our study had a 2.28-fold increased risk of PPH, which was consistent with the results of other studies. The mechanism might be related to an imbalance between angiogenic and antiangiogenic factors in the maternal circulation, impaired vascular endothelial function, coagulation dysfunction due to the activation of coagulation factors, and thrombosis.[13,18] Coagulation dysfunction was 1 of the 4 major causes of PPH. Compared to singleton pregnancies, twin pregnancies were associated with increases in coagulation-fibrinolytic activity, conversion of fibrinogen to fibrin, and FDP levels, and decreased fibrinogen levels in late pregnancy. Therefore, twin pregnancies were more prone to PPH.[20] Yamada et al[21] also found that fibrinogen levels in twin pregnancies significantly decreased in the third trimester and before delivery. In our study, the risk of PPH was 1.06 times higher in pregnant women with elevated prenatal FDP than in those without elevated FDP. FDP was a degradation product produced by the action of fibrinolytic enzymes on fibrinogen or fibrin, and its level reflected the functional status of the fibrinolytic system. Elevated prenatal FDP indicated that the body was in a hypercoagulable state in preparation for postpartum hemostasis, but excessive hypercoagulation could lead to thrombosis and massive consumption of coagulation factors, causing postpartum coagulation dysfunction and even inducing disseminated intravascular coagulation; these then resulted in PPH.[22] Japanese scholars found that the FDP level during normal delivery was 0.35 ± 0.28 mg/dL, and the range of FDP in patients with PPH was 0.8 to 3.2 mg/dL.[23] The median prenatal FDP in the PPH group in our study was 12.99 µg/mL (1.299 mg/dL), indicating that prenatal FDP level was a predictor of PPH.

During pregnancy, PLT might mildly decrease (by approximately 10%) due to relative hemodilution and accelerated platelet destruction caused by increased blood volume, which was a physiological occurrence. Pathological thrombocytopenia might also occur due to pregnancy-specific diseases such as preeclampsia, HELLP syndrome, and acute fatty liver during pregnancy.[24] The main function of platelets was to clot and stopped bleeding and to repair broken blood vessels. A decrease in platelets caused an increased risk of bleeding. Blitz et al found that PLT < 100,000/µL was a risk factor for PPH in women with twin pregnancies.[25] Another study also showed that prenatal PLT was significantly lower in the PPH group than in the control group.[26] The risk of PPH in our study also increased with the decrease in PLT. A South African study found that women with a prenatal PLT of 51,000 to 10,000/µL and 101,000 to 150,000/µL were 3 and 2 times more likely, respectively. To be transfused postpartum than those with PLT ≥ 200,000/µL, with reduced PLT increasing the probability of PPH.[27]

Our study found that the risk of PPH during labor was 4.19 times higher in those who received oxytocin induction than in women who did not use oxytocin, which was consistent with previous studies.[17] A study showed that in women who did not receive prophylactic oxytocin after delivery, the use of oxytocin induction at delivery increased the risk of severe PPH in the postpartum period (odds ratio [OR] 1.8, 95% CI 1.3–2.6) and the OR increased progressively with the total dose of induction oxytocin. In women treated with prophylactic oxytocin after delivery, the association between the induction of labor and severe PPH was significant only at the highest exposure level. It might be that women who use prophylactic oxytocin after delivery have a reduced risk of PPH during labor at the risk of oxytocin overdose.[28] Erickson et al[29] found that the incidence of PPH was significantly higher in pregnant women who used oxytocin for ≥ 4 h during spontaneous labor than in those who did not use oxytocin. The use of oxytocin for > 7 h during induced labor significantly increases the incidence of PPH. Oxytocin receptor (OXTR) was a member of the G-protein-coupled receptor family, and molecular desensitization occurs upon activation. After the agonist binds to OXTR, the receptor was immediately uncoupled from its associated G protein, limiting further oxytocin signaling until the receptor was recirculated back into the cell membrane. Thus, the prolonged use of oxytocin led to OXTR desensitization, which limited the oxytocin-mediated contractile response.[30]

Anemia was one of the most common complications during pregnancy, and prenatal anemia was a risk factor for PPH.[31] Our study showed that the risk of PPH was 2.09 times higher in pregnant women with prenatal anemia than in those without anemia. A Peruvian study[32] showed a higher incidence of PPH in the moderate-to-severe anemia group (Hb < 8 g/dL), and no difference in the incidence of PPH was observed between the mild anemia and normal Hb groups. A British study showed that pregnant women with severe prenatal anemia (Hb < 8 g/dL) lost an average of 80 mL more blood at delivery than controls (Hb > 11 g/dL) and had a higher incidence of PPH (P = .012), but the effect of severe anemia on PPH might have been underestimated because anemic pregnant women were treated with iron before delivery.[33] This finding was consistent with the results of 2 other studies.[34,35] Anemia, especially iron deficiency anemia, led to tissue ischemia and hypoxia and resulted in increased nitric oxide (NO) production, and NO activates smooth muscle cell ornithine cyclase, which dephosphorylated uridine triphosphate to cyclic guanosine monophosphate, causing smooth muscle relaxation and sluggish contractions leading to PPH. This was also supported by Soltan et al[36] who reported that the mean NO level was significantly higher in anemic women (185.7 ± 1.0 μm/L) than in non-anemic women (147.4 ± 1.0 μm/L).

The risk of PPH was 7.48 times higher in pregnant women with fever before or during delivery than in those without fever in our study. This might be related to the cause of the fever, such as chorioamnionitis. Wetta et al[37] found chorioamnionitis to be an independent risk factor for weak contractions (OR 2.8, 95% CI 1.6–5.0). Fever was associated with the production of inflammatory factors such as IL-6 and IL-1b. Prolonged exposure to inflammatory factors led to impaired oxytocin signaling and decreased intracellular calcium ions in myocytes, which increased the risk of PPH.[38] A US study verified that after fever lasting 2 hours, uterine systolic pressure began to decrease significantly and steadily, and the decrease in contractility was not due to a decrease in oxytocin exposure. There was no significant change in oxytocin dose during the contractility decrease. Oxytocin increased by 0.6 ± 0.1 MVU/mU (subunits of Mondevi, equal to the peak amplitude of each contraction minus resting uterine tone) before fever and by 0.4 ± 0.1 MVU/mU after fever, suggesting that uterine responsiveness to oxytocin is reduced in the presence of fever.[39]

Fetal distress was a risk factor for PPH in this study. Considering the different modes of delivery at the time of univariate analysis between the 2 groups, it was possible that fetal distress increased the probability of surgically assisted delivery and soft birth canal laceration, which led to increased PPH.

In the univariate analysis, the cervix laceration rate in the PPH group was significantly higher than that in the no PPH group. Because birth canal injury was 1 of the 4 major causes of PPH and has no predictive value being an intra-delivery occurrence, it was excluded from the LASSO regression. The rate of postpartum curettage was significantly higher in the PPH group than in the no PPH group. This was because the residual placenta-fetal membranes caused PPH, making curettage a necessity.

The limitations of our study were as follows: firstly, this study was a single-center and retrospective design, which might lead to selection bias. Secondly, several clinical data were missing or insufficient. Thirdly, only internal validation was performed. Finally, the study population consisted exclusively of Asian Han Chinese individuals, and generalizing the findings to other ethnic groups requires further exploration. The predictive model presented in this study had the potential to assist healthcare providers in assessing the risk of bleeding during vaginal delivery in twin pregnancies. For pregnant women at high risk of bleeding, timely referral or seeking assistance could potentially reduce the occurrence of severe postpartum hemorrhage. As data accumulates and the model was further validated, it ight serve as a valuable reference for the development of risk stratification and management strategies in the future. In a subsequent study, we aimed to prospectively apply the nomogram and test its external validity.

4.1. Conclusions

Our risk prediction model based on a nomogram helped to rapidly calculate the risk of PPH with convenient clinical application. Internal validation was performed by bootstrap self-sampling, with a C-index of 0.816, which indicates that the model has good stability. The risk factors in the model are readily available prenatally via medical records and test results. These factors can facilitate the individualized assessment of the risk of PPH at any stage during labor in conjunction with changes in condition and aid the adoption of appropriate preventive measures. Moreover, the risk factors are all objective indicators, which can reduce subjective reliance on clinical experience, ensure the accuracy of prediction, and facilitate the application by young clinicians and midwives.
