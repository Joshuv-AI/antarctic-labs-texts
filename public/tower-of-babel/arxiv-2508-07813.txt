Fulde-Ferrell-Larkin-Ovchinnikov States and Topological Bogoliubov Fermi Surfaces in Altermagnets: an Analytical Study
Zhao Liu, Hui Hu, Xia-Ji Liu

Abstract
We present an analytical study of the ground-state phase diagram for dilute two-dimensional spin-1/2 Fermi gases exhibiting d d -wave altermagnetic spin splitting under s s -wave pairing. Within the Bogoliubov-de Gennes mean-field framework, four distinct phases are identified: a Bardeen-Schrieffer-Cooper-type superfluid, a normal metallic phase, a nodal superfluid with topological Bogoliubov Fermi surfaces (TBFSs), and Fulde-Ferrell-Larkin-Ovchinnikov (FFLO) states with finite center-of-mass momentum. Among these, the FFLO states and TBFSs exemplify two unconventional forms of superconductivity. Considering the simplicity of this model, with only one band, zero net magnetization, and s s -wave paring, the emergence of both unconventional phases underscores the pivotal role of altermagnetic spin splitting in enabling exotic pairing phenomena. This analytical study not only offers a valuable benchmark for future numerical simulations, but also provides a concrete experimental roadmap for realizing FFLO states and TBFSs in altermagnets.

Introduction

The Fulde-Ferrell-Larkin-Ovchinnikov (FFLO) state

{\bf q}
to minimize the
free energy, therefore the translational symmetry of the
homogeneous superfluidity (SF) is broken. Over the past six decades, extensive
efforts have been devoted to realizing the FFLO phase across a wide
range of platforms, including high-density quark matter
(

;

)
,
ultracold Fermi gases
Hu2006
;

)
.
However, conclusive evidence-namely, spatial modulation of either
the phase or amplitude of the SF order parameter-remains elusive.

Altermagnetism (AM)
(

)
, a novel type of collinear
magnetism beyond the traditional ferromagnetism (FM) and antiferromagnetism
(AFM), has recently attracted considerable attention

;

;

;

,

AM also provides a promising platform for the FFLO state for two main
reasons. First, FFLO phases typically require a nonzero spin imbalance,
which is usually achieved via external Zeeman fields. However, in
conventional superconductors, the orbital effect induced by magnetic
fields can suppress the superconductivity before the FFLO emerges

(
Maki1966
;
Gruenberg1966
)
. In contrast, AM naturally hosts
NRSS without the need of external magnetic field, thereby avoiding
detrimental orbital effects. Second, conventional FFLO state involves
spontaneously breaking of the continuous
U(1)
rotational symmetry
of the Fermi surfaces, which, according to Nambu-Goldstone theorem

(
Nambu1960
;
Goldstone1961
;
Goldstone1962
)
, leads to gapless
modes associated with fluctuations between equivalent
{\bf q}
.
In AM, the continuous rotational symmetry is reduced to a discrete
one
Wu2007
, significantly suppressing such fluctuations. These
unique advantages have motivated several theoretical investigations
of FFLO phases in altermagnetic metals
(
Feiguin2009
;
Soto-Garrido2014
;
Gukelberger2014
;
Sumita2023
;
Chakraborty2024-1
;
ZhangS2024
;
Hong2025
;
Hu2025-1
;
Hu2025-2
)
.

Despite these efforts, the existence of FFLO state in two-dimensional
(2D)
d
-wave AM Fermi gas with
s
-wave pairing remains debated.
Using a continuum model, Soto-Garrido and co-workers
(
Soto-Garrido2014
)

reported the absence of FFLO state in a 2D
d_{x^{2}-y^{2}}
-wave
AM Fermi gas. This conclusion is corroborated by Feiguin
et
al.

(
Feiguin2009
)
and Chakraborty
et al.

(
Chakraborty2024-1
)

through a square lattice model. Nevertheless, more recent studies
challenge this result. Investigating a similar lattice model to that
of Chakraborty
et al.

(
Chakraborty2024-1
)
, Hong and
co-workers
(
Hong2025
)
found that FFLO state does emerge under
purely
s
-wave pairing. Subsequently, Hu and co-workers
(
Hu2025-1
)

conducted a numerical calculation on the continuum model close to
Soto-Garrido
et al.

(
Soto-Garrido2014
)
and confirmed
the existence of FFLO state, aligning with the findings of Hong
et al.

(
Hong2025
)
. These conflicting reports raise a fundamental
question: can 2D
d
-wave AM Fermi gas supports the FFLO state driven
solely by
s
-wave pairing?

In this work, we give a definitive and affirmative answer by
analytically

determining the ground-state phase diagram. We consider two physically
relevant situations: (i) fixed
\mu/\varepsilon_{B}
, where
\mu

is the total chemical potential and
\varepsilon_{B}
is the two-body
binding energy which is experimentally controllable in the ultracold
atom systems; and (ii) fixed total particle number
N
, which is
appropriate for describing electronic superconductors. In both situations,
we demonstrate that FFLO phases do indeed exist. What’s more, another
exotic unconventional form of superconductivity - nodal
SF hosting topological Bogoliubov Fermi surface (TBFS) - also
emerges in both situations.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. In the next section
(Sec. II), we outline the model Hamiltonian that describes a
d
-wave
altermagnetic metal. In Sec. III, we briefly discuss how to solve
the model Hamiltonian by using the standard Bogoliubov de-Gennes (BdG)
mean-field theory, with an inhomogeneous FF order parameter. In Sec.
IV, we present the phase diagram between normal (N) phase, nodal SF,
and SF. The nontrivial
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
topology of BFS is characterized
by the Pfaffian of the BdG Hamiltonian. In Sec. V, we discuss in details
the FFLO phase boundary driven by the NRSS. Finally, we conclude in Sec.
VI and present some outlooks for future works.

To simplify the notation, the Fermi wavevector
k_{F}
and Fermi
energy
\varepsilon_{F}
are taken as the units for wavevector and
energy, respectively. This means that we can directly set
2m=\hbar=k_{B}=k_{F}=\varepsilon_{F}=1
.

II
Model Hamiltonian

The model Hamiltonian describing two-dimensional spin-1/2 Fermion
gas is:

\displaystyle{\mathscr{H}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int d{\bf x}({\cal H}_{0}+{\cal H}_{\textrm{int}}),

(1)

\displaystyle{\cal H}_{0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\sigma=\uparrow,\downarrow}\psi_{\sigma}^{\dagger}\left({\bf x}\right)\left[\hat{\xi}_{{\bf k}}+s(\sigma)\hat{J}_{\mathbf{k}}\right]\psi_{\sigma}\left({\bf x}\right),

(2)

\displaystyle{\cal H}_{\textrm{int}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle g\psi_{\uparrow}^{\dagger}\left({\bf x}\right)\psi_{\downarrow}^{\dagger}\left({\bf x}\right)\psi_{\downarrow}\left({\bf x}\right)\psi_{\uparrow}\left({\bf x}\right).

(3)

For the non-interacting part
{\cal H}_{0}
,
\hat{\varepsilon}_{{\bf k}}\equiv-{\bf\nabla}^{2}

is the operator for kinetic energy where
\hat{k}_{x}=-i\partial_{x}
,

\hat{k}_{y}=-i\partial_{y}
.
\hat{\xi}_{{\bf k}}\equiv\hat{\varepsilon}_{{\bf k}}-\mu

is the modified kinetic energy operator where
\mu
is the chemical
potential. The chemical potential is tuned to yield the given total
particle density
n=n_{\uparrow}+n_{\downarrow}
.
\hat{J}_{\mathbf{k}}\equiv t_{\textrm{AM}}2\hat{k}_{x}\hat{k}_{y}

where
s(\uparrow)=+1
and
s(\downarrow)=-1
is the
d_{xy}
-wave
altermagnetic spin splitting, whose strength is controlled by the
dimensionless parameter
t_{\textrm{AM}}
(without the loss of generality,

t_{\textrm{AM}}
is set to be positive here). In 2D, there are two independent
d
-wave spin splitting:
d_{xy}
and
d_{x^{2}-y^{2}}
. As
d_{x^{2}-y^{2}}
can be obtained from
d_{xy}
by
\pi/4
rotation, the results obtained here can be generalized to
d_{xy}
-wave altermagnetic spin splitting straightforwardly.
Without
\hat{J}_{\mathbf{k}}
,
the spin degenerated Fermi surface is a circle with radius
\sqrt{\mu}
,
which conserves
U(1)
rotational symmetry (see the dashed circle in
Fig.
1
(a)). The introduction of
\hat{J}_{\mathbf{k}}

distorts the Fermi surface into two orthogonal ellipses (denoted as
Fermi ellipse here) with equal semi-major axis
a\equiv\sqrt{\mu/(1-t_{\textrm{AM}})}

and semi-minor axis
b\equiv\sqrt{\mu/(1+t_{\textrm{AM}})}
(see
Fig.
1
(a)), in this regard,
t_{\textrm{AM}}
also
measures the eccentricity of the Fermi ellipses at a given
\mu
.
These two Fermi ellipses together exhibit
d_{xy}
-wave form (see
Fig.
1
(a)): spin up and down ellipse has major axis
along
\left(1,-1\right)
and
\left(1,1\right)
direction, respectively.
Since the two Fermi ellipses always have the same area even with finite
momentum boost in the FFLO phase, the system remains spin-population balanced in the whole phase diagram. Such a circle-to-ellipse distortion breaks
the continuous
U(1)
to the discrete
C_{4z}
symmetry, leaving
four degeneracies along both
(1,0)
and
(0,1)
directions. As
mentioned before, such continuous symmetry breaking would stabilize
the FFLO state.

For the inter-particle interaction
{\cal H}_{\textrm{int}}
, we
consider a short-range
s
-wave contact potential. Such a consideration
is straightforward as pair states like
\left|\mathbf{k},\uparrow\right\rangle

and
\left|-\mathbf{k},\downarrow\right\rangle
in Fig.
1
(a)
are susceptible to
s
-wave pairing according to BCS theory
(
BCS1957
)
.
In two dimensions, its interaction strength
g
can be conveniently
regularized by using a two-body binding energy
\varepsilon_{B}

(
He2015
)
:

\frac{1}{g}=-\frac{1}{\mathcal{A}}\sum_{\mathbf{k}}\frac{1}{2\varepsilon_{{\bf k}}+\varepsilon_{B}},

(4)

where
\mathcal{A}
is the area of the 2D Fermi system.

III
Mean-field theory

To search for the FF superconductivity, we assume a FF-like order
parameter
\Delta({\bf x})=-g\left\langle\psi_{\downarrow}\left({\bf x}\right)\psi_{\uparrow}\left({\bf x}\right)\right\rangle=\Delta\exp[i\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{x}]

at positive
\mathbf{x}
with the center-of-mass momentum
\mathbf{q}
,
so
\left|\Delta({\bf x})\right|^{2}=\Delta^{2}
(below we take
\Delta

positive) and consider the standard mean-field decoupling of the interaction
Hamiltonian density
(
Hu2006
;
Liu2013
)
,

{\cal H}_{\textrm{int}}\simeq-\left[\Delta({\bf x})\psi_{\uparrow}^{\dagger}\left({\bf x}\right)\psi_{\downarrow}^{\dagger}\left({\bf x}\right)+\text{H.c.}\right]-\frac{1}{g}\Delta^{2}.

(5)

In the framework of BdG mean-field theory, the total Hamiltonian can
be rewritten into the form
(
Hu2006
;
Liu2013
)
,

{\cal\mathscr{H}}_{\textrm{MF}}=\int d{\bf x}\Phi^{\dagger}\left({\bf x}\right){\cal H}_{\textrm{BdG}}\Phi\left({\bf x}\right)-\frac{\Delta^{2}}{g}\mathcal{A}+\mathcal{E}_{0}^{\prime},

(6)

where
\Phi\left({\bf x}\right)\equiv[\psi_{\uparrow}\left({\bf x}\right),\psi_{\downarrow}^{\dagger}\left({\bf x}\right)]^{T}

is the Nambu spinor and

{\cal H}_{\textrm{BdG}}\equiv\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\hat{\xi}_{{\bf k}}+\hat{J}_{\mathbf{k}}&-\Delta\left({\bf x}\right)\\
-\Delta^{*}\left({\bf x}\right)&-\left(\hat{\xi}_{{\bf k}}-\hat{J}_{\mathbf{k}}\right)\end{array}\right],

(7)

and
\mathcal{E}_{0}^{\prime}=\sum_{\mathbf{k}}(\hat{\xi}_{{\bf k}}-\hat{J}_{\mathbf{k}})

is an energy shift (to be specified below), due to the abnormal order
in the field operators
\psi_{\downarrow}(\mathbf{x})
and
\psi_{\downarrow}^{\dagger}({\bf x})

taken in the Nambu spinor representation.

III.1
Bogoliubov quasiparticles

As the FF order parameter takes a plane-wave form, it is convenient
to diagonalize the mean-field Hamiltonian in momentum space, with
the following Bogoliubov equations
(
Liu2013
)
,

{\cal H}_{\textrm{BdG}}\Phi_{{\bf k}}\left({\bf x}\right)=E_{{\bf k}}\Phi_{{\bf k}}\left({\bf x}\right),

(8)

where

\Phi_{{\bf k}}\left({\bf x}\right)\equiv\left[\begin{array}[]{c}u_{{\bf k}\uparrow}e^{+i{\bf q}{\bf x}/2}\\
v_{{\bf k}\downarrow}e^{-i{\bf q}{\bf x}/2}\end{array}\right]e^{i{\bf kx}}

(9)

and
E_{{\bf k}}
are the wavefunction and energy of the Bogoliubov
quasiparticles, respectively. With this wavefunction, the energy shift
is
\mathcal{E}_{0}^{\prime}=\sum_{\mathbf{k}}(\xi_{{\bf k}-\mathbf{q}/2}-J_{\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{q}/2})
,
where
\xi_{\mathbf{k}}\equiv k^{2}-\mu
, and
J_{\mathbf{k}}\equiv t_{\textrm{AM}}2k_{x}k_{y}
.
Then the Bogoliubov equations becomes,

\left[{\cal H}_{\textrm{BdG}}\right]\left[\begin{array}[]{c}u_{{\bf k}\uparrow}\\
v_{{\bf k}\downarrow}\end{array}\right]=E_{{\bf k}}\left[\begin{array}[]{c}u_{{\bf k}\uparrow}\\
v_{{\bf k}\downarrow}\end{array}\right],

(10)

where
\left[{\cal H}_{\textrm{BdG}}\right]
is a 2 by 2 matrix given
by,

\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\xi_{{\bf k}+\mathbf{q}/2}+J_{\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}/2}&-\Delta\\
-\Delta&-(\xi_{{\bf k}-\mathbf{q}/2}-J_{\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{q}/2})\end{array}\right].

(11)

\left[{\cal H}_{\textrm{BdG}}\right]
can be diagonalized with eigenvectors

[u_{{\bf k}\uparrow}^{(\eta)},v_{{\bf k}\downarrow}^{(\eta)}]^{T}

and corresponding eigenvalues
\eta E_{{\bf k}\eta}
, labeled by
the branch index
\eta=\pm
. Explicitly, we have the Bogoliubov quasiparticle
energy spectrum,

E_{\mathbf{k}\pm}=\sqrt{A_{\mathbf{k}}^{2}+\Delta^{2}}\pm B_{\mathbf{k}},

(12)

where

\displaystyle A_{\mathbf{k}}

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\xi_{{\bf k}}+\frac{{\bf q}^{2}}{4}+t_{\textrm{AM}}(k_{x}q_{y}+k_{y}q_{x}),

(13)

\displaystyle B_{\mathbf{k}}

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle{\bf k}\cdot{\bf q}+J_{\mathbf{k}}+\frac{t_{\textrm{AM}}}{2}q_{x}q_{y}.

(14)

Here, as usual we have reversed the sign for the eigenvalue
-E_{\mathbf{k}-}
,
in order to write the corresponding field operators of Bogoliubov
quasiparticles
\alpha_{\mathbf{k}-}
and
\alpha_{\mathbf{k}-}^{\dagger}

in the normal order. This introduces another energy shift
\mathcal{E}_{0}^{\prime\prime}=-\sum_{\mathbf{k}}E_{\mathbf{k}-}

in the mean-field Hamiltonian. When
t_{\textrm{AM}}=0
,
our quasiparticle energy spectrum reduces to the results of Ref.
(
Sheehy2015
)
. In the end, we obtain the
diagonalized mean-field Hamiltonian,

{\cal\mathscr{H}}_{\textrm{MF}}=\sum_{{\bf k},\eta=\pm}E_{{\bf k}\eta}\alpha_{{\bf k\eta}}^{\dagger}\alpha_{{\bf k}\eta}-\frac{\Delta^{2}}{g}\mathcal{A}+\mathcal{E}_{0},

(15)

where

\mathcal{E}_{0}=\mathcal{E}_{0}^{\prime}+\mathcal{E}_{0}^{\prime\prime}=\sum_{\mathbf{k}}\left(A_{\mathbf{k}}-\sqrt{A_{\mathbf{k}}^{2}+\Delta^{2}}\right).

(16)

III.2
Thermodynamic potential and ground state energy

From the diagonalized Hamiltonian, we can straightforwardly write
down the grand thermodynamic potential,

\Omega=-\frac{\Delta^{2}}{g}\mathcal{A}+\mathcal{E}_{0}-T\sum_{{\bf k},\eta=\pm}\ln\left(1+e^{-\frac{E_{{\bf k\eta}}}{T}}\right),

(17)

where
T
is the temperature, and the last term is the standard contribution
of non-interacting fermionic particles to the thermodynamic potential.

In the ground state at zero temperature, referred to as the ground-state
energy,
\Omega
takes the following form,

E_{G}=-\frac{\Delta^{2}}{g}\mathcal{A}+\mathcal{E}_{0}+\sum_{{\bf k},\eta=\pm}E_{{\bf k\eta}}\Theta\left(-E_{{\bf k\eta}}\right),

(18)

where
\Theta(x)
is the Heaviside step function. Converting the
momentum sum to an integration, the ground-state energy density becomes:

\begin{split}\frac{E_{G}}{\mathcal{A}}&=\int\frac{d{\bf k}}{\left(2\pi\right)^{2}}\left[\sum_{\eta=\pm}E_{{\bf k\eta}}\Theta\left(-E_{{\bf k\eta}}\right)\right.\\
&\left.+\frac{\Delta^{2}}{2\varepsilon_{{\bf k}}+\varepsilon_{B}}+A_{\mathbf{k}}-\sqrt{A_{\mathbf{k}}^{2}+\Delta^{2}}\right],\end{split}

(19)

where the renormalization condition Eq. (
4
) is used.
At a given chemical potential, the phase boundary between SF and N
phase is obtained by equating the ground-state energies of these two
phases:

E_{G,\textrm{SF}}=E_{G,\textrm{N}}.

(20)

III.3
Gap equation

During the phase transition from N to FFLO SF phase,
E_{G}
should
decrease as the onset of
\Delta
. The minimization of
E_{G}
with
respect to
\Delta
defines a function
S\left(t_{\textrm{AM}},{\bf q},\Delta\right)
:

-2\Delta S\left(t_{\textrm{AM}},{\bf q},\Delta\right)=\frac{\partial E_{G}}{\partial\Delta},

(21)

with the explicit expression

\frac{S\left(t_{\textrm{AM}},{\bf q},\Delta\right)}{\mathcal{A}}=\int\frac{d{\bf k}}{\left(2\pi\right)^{2}}\left[\frac{1-\sum_{\eta}\Theta\left(-E_{{\bf k\eta}}\right)}{\sum_{\eta}E_{{\bf k\eta}}}-\frac{1}{2\varepsilon_{{\bf k}}+\varepsilon_{B}}\right].

(22)

Equilibrium FFLO states satisfy
S\left(t_{\textrm{AM}},{\bf q},\Delta\right)=0
,
which is also known as the gap equation. In the following, this gap
equation is utilized to find the phase boundary between N and FFLO
SF phase. We note that,
S(t_{\textrm{AM}},{\bf q},\Delta)
can alternatively
be understood as the inverse vertex function (i.e., the Cooper-pair
propagator in the normal state with frequency
\omega=\Delta
) in
the ladder approximation
Sheehy2015
;
Hu2025-2
. Therefore, the
condition
S(t_{\textrm{AM}},{\bf q},0)=0
is precisely the well-known
Thouless criterion for the onset of the N to SF phase
transition.

IV
Phase diagram at
{\bf q}={\bf 0}

In this section, we determine the phase boundaries assuming
{\bf q}={\bf 0}

pairing, which amounts to neglecting the possibility of the FFLO state.
Within this approximation,
A_{{\bf k}}=\xi_{{\bf k}}
and
E_{{\bf k\pm}}=\sqrt{\xi_{{\bf k}}^{2}+\Delta^{2}}\pm J_{\mathbf{k}}
.
In our terminology,
E_{{\bf k\pm}}
is non-negative. If
E_{{\bf k\pm}}>0

for all
{\bf k}
, such a SF phase is called BCS SF. If
E_{{\bf k\pm}}=0

at certain
{\bf k}
, then the Bogoliubov quasiparticle has a “Fermi
surface” structure, which is also known as BFS
(
Volovik1993
)
.
As discussed below, we have nodal SF with BFS protected by
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
topological invariant. In the following, we first investigate the
BCS SF phase.

IV.1
BCS SF phase

Since
E_{{\bf k\pm}}
is positive, the Heaviside step function in
Eq. (
22
) vanishes and
S\left(t_{\textrm{AM}},{\bf 0},\Delta\right)/\mathcal{A}

becomes

\begin{split}\frac{S\left(t_{\textrm{AM}},{\bf 0},\Delta\right)}{\mathcal{A}}&=\int\frac{d{\bf k}}{\left(2\pi\right)^{2}}\left[\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\xi_{{\bf k}}^{2}+\Delta^{2}}}-\frac{1}{2\varepsilon_{{\bf k}}+\varepsilon_{B}}\right],\\
&=\frac{1}{8\pi}\ln\left[\frac{\varepsilon_{B}}{\sqrt{\mu^{2}+\Delta^{2}}-\mu}\right].\end{split}

(23)

And the gap equation leads to the stationary pairing amplitude

\Delta=\sqrt{\varepsilon_{B}\left(\varepsilon_{B}+2\mu\right)},

(24)

so we must have
\mu>-\varepsilon_{B}/2
for a stable SF phase.

The total particle number of the SF state at a fixed
\mu
is given
by calculating
N=-\frac{E_{G,\textrm{SF}}}{\partial\mu}
, yielding,
for the total density

n=\frac{1}{2\pi}\left(\mu+\sqrt{\mu^{2}+\Delta^{2}}\right).

(25)

Inserting Eq. (
24
) into Eq. (
25
)
gives results for
\Delta
and
\mu
for a system at a fixed total
particle number

\begin{split}\frac{\Delta}{\varepsilon_{F}}&=\sqrt{\frac{\varepsilon_{B}}{\varepsilon_{F}}},\\
\frac{\mu}{\varepsilon_{F}}&=1-\frac{1}{2}\frac{\varepsilon_{B}}{\varepsilon_{F}}.\end{split}

(26)

Here, we want to emphasize that
t_{\textrm{AM}}
does not appear
in Eq. (
24
), Eq. (
25
), and Eq.
(
26
). This is in agreement with the earlier result
in Ref.
(
Sheehy2015
)
, where a small magnetic field does not
appear in the stationary pairing amplitude of a BCS SF.

IV.2
Fixed chemical potential

At the SF phase, the effect of NRSS disappears. The integration over
momentum and the insertion of the stationary gap-equation solution
leads to

\frac{E_{G,\textrm{SF}}}{\mathcal{A}}=-\frac{1}{16\pi}(\varepsilon_{B}+2\mu)^{2}

(27)

The effect of NRSS does appear in the normal phase. By setting
\Delta=0

in Eq. (
19
), we find that,

\frac{E_{G,\textrm{N}}}{\mathcal{A}}=\int\frac{d{\bf k}}{\left(2\pi\right)^{2}}\left[\sum_{\eta=\pm}E_{{\bf k\eta}}\Theta\left(-E_{{\bf k\eta}}\right)+\left(\xi_{{\bf k}}-\left|\xi_{{\bf k}}\right|\right)\right],

(28)

where
E_{{\bf k\eta}}=\left|\xi_{{\bf k}}\right|\pm J_{{\bf k}}
,
i.e. the energies of Bogoliubov quasiparticles reduce to the standard
single-particle excitation energies.

The integration over the second term in the above equation is straightforward:

\int\frac{d{\bf k}}{\left(2\pi\right)^{2}}\left(\xi_{{\bf k}}-\left|\xi_{{\bf k}}\right|\right)=-\frac{1}{4\pi}\mu^{2}

(29)

Next we integrate over the first term. Introducing polar coordinate

{\bf k}=[k\cos\left(\phi\right),k\sin\left(\phi\right)]
and setting

E_{{\bf k\eta}}=0
, the boundary
k_{\pm}(\phi)
is already shown
in Fig.
1
(a) with the following expression:

k_{\pm}(\phi)=\sqrt{\frac{\mu}{1\mp t_{\textrm{AM}}\sin\left(2\phi\right)}}.

(30)

Here we see
t_{\textrm{AM}}<1
.
E_{{\bf k+}}
(
E_{{\bf k-}}
)
becomes negative only when
\phi\in\left[\frac{\pi}{2},\pi\right]\cup\left[\frac{3\pi}{2},2\pi\right]

(
\left[0,\frac{\pi}{2}\right]\cup\left[\pi,\frac{3\pi}{2}\right]
),
which is marked as the cyan (blue) region in Fig.
1
(b).
Due to the
C_{4z}
symmetry, it is clear that
\int\frac{d{\bf k}}{\left(2\pi\right)^{2}}E_{{\bf k+}}\Theta\left(-E_{{\bf k+}}\right)

and
I_{1}\equiv\int\frac{d{\bf k}}{\left(2\pi\right)^{2}}E_{{\bf k-}}\Theta\left(-E_{{\bf k-}}\right)

are the same, so only
I_{1}
is evaluated here. What is more, the
contribution from
\phi\in\left[0,\frac{\pi}{2}\right]
and
\phi\in\left[\pi,\frac{3\pi}{2}\right]

are also the same, therefore, we have

I_{1}=\frac{2}{\left(2\pi\right)^{2}}\int_{0}^{\pi/2}d\phi\int_{k_{-}(\phi)}^{k_{+}(\phi)}\left(\left|\xi_{{\bf k}}\right|-J_{{\bf k}}\right)kdk.

(31)

By noticing that
\left|\xi_{{\bf k}}\right|=-\xi_{{\bf k}}
for

k\in\left[k_{-}(\phi),\sqrt{\mu}\right]
and
\xi_{{\bf k}}
for

k\in\left[\sqrt{\mu},k_{+}(\phi)\right]
, the integration over
k

can be calculated as

2\int_{k_{-}(\phi)}^{k_{+}(\phi)}\left(\left|\xi_{{\bf k}}\right|-J_{{\bf k}}\right)kdk=-\frac{\left[t_{\textrm{AM}}\sin(2\phi)\right]^{2}}{1-\left[t_{\textrm{AM}}\sin(2\phi)\right]^{2}}\mu^{2},

(32)

and
I_{1}
becomes the integration over
\phi
only:

\begin{split}I_{1}&=-\frac{\mu^{2}}{\left(2\pi\right)^{2}}\int_{0}^{\pi/2}d\phi\left[\frac{1}{1-\left[t_{\textrm{AM}}\sin(2\phi)\right]^{2}}-1\right],\\
&=-\frac{\mu^{2}}{\left(2\pi\right)^{2}2t_{\textrm{AM}}^{2}}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{1}{\frac{2-t_{\textrm{AM}}^{2}}{t_{\textrm{AM}}^{2}}+\cos(\phi)}d\phi+\frac{\mu^{2}}{8\pi}.\end{split}

(33)

With
t_{\textrm{AM}}<1
, we have
\frac{2-t_{\textrm{AM}}^{2}}{t_{\textrm{AM}}^{2}}>1
,
the standard integration gives us

\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{1}{\frac{2-t_{\textrm{AM}}^{2}}{t_{\textrm{AM}}^{2}}+\cos(\phi)}d\phi=\frac{2\pi}{\sqrt{\left(\frac{2-t_{\textrm{AM}}^{2}}{t_{\textrm{AM}}^{2}}\right)^{2}-1}},

(34)

and finally
I_{1}
is expressed as:

I_{1}=\frac{\mu^{2}}{8\pi}\left(1-\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-t_{\textrm{AM}}^{2}}}\right).

(35)

Therefore, we obtain

\frac{E_{G,\textrm{N}}}{\mathcal{A}}=-\frac{1}{4\pi}\frac{\mu^{2}}{\sqrt{1-t_{\textrm{AM}}^{2}}}.

(36)

It is worth noting that the area of the ellipse in Fig.
1
(a)
is given by
\pi ab=\pi\mu/\sqrt{1-t_{\textrm{AM}}^{2}}
, therefore,
the ground-state energy density of the N phase is proportional
to the area of the ellipse. Such a geometric meaning can also be understood
by setting
t_{\textrm{AM}}=0
, the above ground-state energy density
becomes the well-known result
E_{G,\textrm{N}}/\mathcal{A}=-\mu^{2}/(4\pi)
.

By equating
E_{G,\textrm{SF}}
and
E_{G,\textrm{N}}
, we have

(\varepsilon_{B}+2\mu)^{2}=\frac{\left(2\mu\right)^{2}}{\sqrt{1-t_{\textrm{AM}}^{2}}},

(37)

and the critical
t_{\textrm{AM}}
separating SF and N phase is given
by:

t_{\textrm{AM, c}}=\sqrt{1-\left(\frac{2\mu/\varepsilon_{B}}{1+2\mu/\varepsilon_{B}}\right)^{4}}.

(38)

This determines the black solid curve in Fig.
2
(a).
When
t_{\textrm{AM}}
is small, i.e. anisotropic spin splitting
is weak, we have SF phase, even to the high chemical potential regime.
Such a feature is in accordance with the earlier result that SF phase
exist at high chemical potential regime when Zeeman field is small
Sheehy2015
. Such SF becomes
unstable when the anisotropic NRSS becomes large.

IV.3
Fixed total particle number

After determining the phase boundary at fixed chemical potential,
our next task is to consider the case of a fixed total particle number.
By approaching the phase transition from the N phase,
\mu=\sqrt{1-t_{\textrm{AM}}^{2}}\varepsilon_{F}

according to Eq. (
36
). Then Eq. (
38
) leads
to

t_{\textrm{AM, c}}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\varepsilon_{B}}{2\varepsilon_{F}}-\sqrt{\frac{1}{4}-\frac{\varepsilon_{B}}{2\varepsilon_{F}}}},

(39)

which gives the black solid curve in Fig.
2
(b) for
\varepsilon_{B}/\varepsilon_{F}<1/2
. When
t_{\textrm{AM}}=0
,
arbitrarily weak attraction interactions are enough to drive the system
into SF phase, as expected. The introduction of
t_{\textrm{AM}}

will destabilize SF and the system transits to a normal state.

IV.4
Nodal SF

Now we study the nodal SF. Since the Bogoliubov quasiparticle energy
is

E_{k,\pm}\left(\phi\right)=\sqrt{\xi_{{\bf k}}^{2}+\Delta^{2}}\pm t_{\textrm{AM}}k^{2}\sin\left(2\phi\right),

(40)

it is highly anisotropic: along
(1,0)
and
(0,1)
directions,
i.e.,
\phi=0
and
\pi/2
, the pairing of the two states
\left|\mathbf{k},\uparrow\right\rangle

and
\left|-\mathbf{k},\downarrow\right\rangle
in Fig.
1
(a)
leads to an excitation energy
E_{{\bf k},\pm}=\sqrt{\xi_{{\bf k}}^{2}+\Delta^{2}}

with minimum
\Delta
. Such a positive excitation energy follows
the spirit of BCS theory
BCS1957
. Along
(-1,1)
and
(1,1)

direction, i.e.,
\phi=\frac{3\pi}{4}(\frac{7\pi}{4})
and
\frac{\pi}{4}(\frac{5\pi}{4})
,

E_{\mathbf{k},+}
and
E_{\mathbf{k},-}
reach the same minimum
value

E_{\mathbf{k}}=\sqrt{\xi_{{\bf k}}^{2}+\Delta^{2}}-t_{\textrm{AM}}k^{2}.

(41)

Fig.
1
(c) shows the typical behavior of
E_{\mathbf{k}}

with respect to different
t_{\textrm{AM}}
values:
E_{\mathbf{k}}

acquires a global minimum at
\xi_{{\bf k}}=\frac{t_{\textrm{AM}}}{\sqrt{1-t_{\textrm{AM}}^{2}}}\Delta

with the value
E_{\mathbf{k},\textrm{min}}=\frac{1-t_{\textrm{AM}}}{\sqrt{1-t_{\textrm{AM}}^{2}}}\Delta-\mu t_{\textrm{AM}}
.

BFS exists when
E_{\mathbf{k},\textrm{min}}=0
has real positive
root (
\bar{t}_{\textrm{AM}}
) within
[0,1)
. As
E_{\mathbf{k},\textrm{min}}=0

is equivalent to the cubic equation

t_{\textrm{AM}}^{3}+t_{\textrm{AM}}^{2}+\left(\frac{\Delta}{\mu}\right)^{2}t_{\textrm{AM}}-\left(\frac{\Delta}{\mu}\right)^{2}=0,

(42)

it is obvious that Eq. (
42
) has only one real root
within the range
[0,1)
as shown in Fig.
1
(d). Therefore,
when
t_{\textrm{AM}}>\bar{t}_{\textrm{AM}}
,
E_{k,\pm}\left(\phi\right)=0

always have solutions and we have nodal SF with the typical BFS shown
in Fig.
1
(e)-(f). The closed BFS separates the whole
momentum space into two regions, in the following the momentum inside
(outside) BFS is marked as
\mathbf{k}_{-}
(
\mathbf{k}_{+}
).

By inserting Eq. (
24
) into Eq. (
42
),
we obtain the equation for determining
\bar{t}_{\textrm{AM}}
at
fixed
\mu
, which is displayed as the black dashed line in Fig.

2
(a). At the same time, by plugging Eq. (
26
)
in Eq. (
42
), we obtain the equation for determining

\bar{t}_{\textrm{AM}}
at fixed total particle number, which is
displayed as the black dashed line in Fig.
2
(b). With

\bar{t}_{\textrm{AM}}<t_{\textrm{AM, c}}
, the nodal SF phase does
exist in both fixed chemical potential and fixed total particle number
cases. From Fig.
2
(b), we know SF /nodal SF is preferred
for a strong/weak pairing interactions, in accordance with Feiguin

et al.

(
Feiguin2009
)
.

IV.5
Topological BFS

The fact that the BFS shown in Fig.
1
(e)-(f) shares
the same dimension as the normal state Fermi surface indicates it
has a topological origin. To reveal the topological invariant, here
we hinges on the Pfaffian Pf(
\mathbf{k}
) of Eq.
7
.
Since Eq.
7
preserves both charge conjuration and parity
symmetry, as suggested by Agterberg and co-workers
(
Agterberg2017
)
,
we can always find a suitable basis to transform Eq. (
7
)
into a screw form whose Pf(
\mathbf{k}
) is given by

\mathrm{Pf}(\mathbf{k})=\xi_{{\bf k}}^{2}+\Delta^{2}-\left[t_{\textrm{AM}}k^{2}\sin\left(2\phi\right)\right]^{2}.

(43)

The solution of Pf
(\mathbf{k})
= 0 is already given by Eq. (
42
).
For BFS shown in Fig.
1
(e)-(f), Pf
(\mathbf{k})

is positive and negative for
\mathbf{k}_{+}
and
\mathbf{k}_{-}
,
respectively. Hence we can identify
(-1)^{l}=\mathrm{sgn}[\mathrm{Pf}(\mathbf{k}_{-})\mathrm{Pf}(\mathbf{k}_{+})]

as the
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
invariant
Zhao2016
.

Here we would like to compare TBFS realized here with existing approaches.
It is well known that BFS can not be realized in conventional 2D
s
-wave
superconductors through external magnetic field alone, as the required
magnetic field already drives the system into N phase before BFS can
form. The replacement of the nodeless
s
-wave pairing by the nodal

d_{x^{2}-y^{2}}
-wave pairing has shown to enable TBFS

(
Yang1998
;
Setty2020-1
)
. Alternatively, inducing supercurrent
in
s
-wave superconductors with high Fermi velocity can also give
rise to TBFS
(
Zhu2021
)
. A further route involves multiband
superconductors that preserve inversion symmetry but breaks time-reversal
symmetry, as demonstrated in recent theoretical studies
(
Brydon2018
;
Lapp2020
;
Setty2020-2
)
.
In contrast to these approaches, AM introduces a new but simple mechanism
for realizing TBFS. This scenario requires neither external magnetic
field nor spin-orbit coupling, but only a single band and
s
-wave
pairing, offering a minimal yet realistic platform for TBFS in superconducting
systems.

V
FFLO phase boundary driven by NRSS

Now we turn to the FFLO phase. To study the FFLO phase, we assume
a continuous transition, with decreasing
t_{\textrm{AM}}
, from
the N phase to the FFLO phase, occurring when the curvature of
E_{G}

versus
\Delta
, at
\Delta=0
, becomes 0 for
{\bf q}\neq(0,0)
.
This equals to
S(t_{\textrm{AM}},{\bf q},0)=0
with

\frac{S(t_{\textrm{AM}},{\bf q},0)}{\mathcal{A}}=\int\frac{d{\bf k}}{\left(2\pi\right)^{2}}\left[\frac{N({\bf k})}{2A_{{\bf k}}}-\frac{1}{2\varepsilon_{{\bf k}}+\varepsilon_{B}}\right]

(44)

where we have defined the form factor
N({\bf k})=1-\Theta\left(-\left(A_{{\bf k}}+B_{{\bf k}}\right)\right)-\Theta\left(-\left(A_{{\bf k}}-B_{{\bf k}}\right)\right)
.
It is clear that
N({\bf k})
exhibits discontinuities whenever an
argument of one of the Heaviside functions vanishes.

We write
S(t_{\textrm{AM}},{\bf q},0)/\mathcal{A}
as the sum of
the following three integrals,

\begin{split}I_{2}&=\int\frac{d{\bf k}}{\left(2\pi\right)^{2}}\left[\frac{1}{2A_{{\bf k}}}-\frac{1}{2\varepsilon_{{\bf k}}+\varepsilon_{B}}\right],\\
I_{3}&=-\int\frac{d{\bf k}}{\left(2\pi\right)^{2}}\frac{\Theta\left[-\left(A_{{\bf k}}+B_{{\bf k}}\right)\right]}{2A_{{\bf k}}},\\
I_{4}&=-\int\frac{d{\bf k}}{\left(2\pi\right)^{2}}\frac{\Theta\left[-\left(A_{{\bf k}}-B_{{\bf k}}\right)\right]}{2A_{{\bf k}}}.\end{split}

(45)

Among them, the evaluation of
I_{2}
is straightforward:

I_{2}=\frac{1}{8\pi}\left(\ln\frac{\varepsilon_{B}}{2}-\ln\left|\left(1-t_{\textrm{AM}}^{2}\right)\left(\frac{{\bf q}}{2}\right)^{2}-\mu\right|\right)

(46)

The evaluation of
I_{3}
and
I_{4}
is highly nontrivial as the
region of integration (
A_{{\bf k}}\pm B_{{\bf k}}<0
) is an ellipse
while the integrand (
1/A_{{\bf k}}
) is circular. By coordinate
transformation,
I_{3}
and
I_{4}
can be expressed as,

\begin{split}I_{3}&=-\frac{ab}{2\left(2\pi\right)^{2}}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{kdkd\phi}{f_{2}^{+}(\phi)k^{2}+f_{1}^{+}(\phi)k+f_{0}^{+}},\\
I_{4}&=-\frac{ab}{2\left(2\pi\right)^{2}}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{kdkd\phi}{f_{2}^{-}(\phi)k^{2}+f_{1}^{-}(\phi)k+f_{0}^{-}},\end{split}

(47)

where the coefficients are

\begin{split}f_{2}^{\pm}(\phi)&=a^{2}\cos^{2}\left(\phi\right)+b^{2}\sin^{2}\left(\phi\right),\\
f_{1}^{+}(\phi)&=\frac{(1+t_{\textrm{AM}})(q_{y}-q_{x})}{\sqrt{2}}a\cos\left(\phi\right)\\
&+\frac{(-1+t_{\textrm{AM}})(q_{y}+q_{x})}{\sqrt{2}}b\sin\left(\phi\right),\\
f_{1}^{-}(\phi)&=\frac{(1+t_{\textrm{AM}})(q_{y}+q_{x})}{\sqrt{2}}a\cos\left(\phi\right)\\
&+\frac{(1-t_{\textrm{AM}})(q_{y}-q_{x})}{\sqrt{2}}b\sin\left(\phi\right),\\
f_{0}^{\pm}&=\frac{{\bf q}^{2}}{2}\pm t_{\textrm{AM}}q_{x}q_{y}-\mu.\end{split}

(48)

There is a
duality
between
I_{3}
and
I_{4}
. By changing

t_{\textrm{AM}}
to
-t_{\textrm{AM}}
and
\phi
to
\phi+\frac{3\pi}{2}

simultaneously, then
a,b
becomes
b,a
and
\sin(\phi),\cos(\phi)

becomes
-\cos(\phi),\sin(\phi)
, therefore
f_{2}^{+}(\phi)
becomes

f_{2}^{-}(\phi)
,
f_{1}^{+}(\phi)
becomes
f_{1}^{-}(\phi)
,
and
f_{0}^{+}
becomes
f_{0}^{-}
. Since the translation of integral
variable
\phi\rightarrow\phi+\frac{3\pi}{2}
does not change the
integration over
\phi
,
I_{3}
and
I_{4}
should be connected
by
t_{\textrm{AM}}\rightarrow-t_{\textrm{AM}}
. Such a fact simplifies
the integrations. In the following, we firstly evaluate
I_{4}
by
contour integration, then changing
t_{\textrm{AM}}
to
-t_{\textrm{AM}}

to obtain
I_{3}
.

For
d_{xy}
-wave altermagnetic spin splitting, there are two characteristic
high-symmetric directions for the FF momentum
\mathbf{q}
: along
the
\left(1,0\right)
direction (or
\left(0,1\right)
), and along
the
\left(1,1\right)
direction (or equivalently
\left(1,-1\right)
)
according to the
C_{4z}
rotational symmetry. In the following,
we first analyze the case of the
\left(1,1\right)
direction for
concreteness, and then comment on the situation along the
\left(1,0\right)

direction, which appears to be more difficult. As discussed in Appendix
A, the angular dependence of the peak value of
S(t_{\textrm{AM}},{\bf q},0)/\mathcal{A}

as a function of
q
turns out to be weak. We thus anticipate that
the analysis along the
\left(1,1\right)
direction will provide
a reasonably accurate phase boundary for the onset of the FFLO state.

V.1
Contour integration of
I_{4}

There are two integral variables
k
and
\phi
in
I_{4}
. The
integration order can be first
k
then
\phi
, or first
\phi

then
k
. We find that the second choice is much more convenient
than the first one and thus adopt it. Since
{\bf q}
is along the

(1,1)
direction, we have
(q_{x},q_{y})=(\frac{q}{\sqrt{2}},\frac{q}{\sqrt{2}})

(
q>0
for simplicity) and the denominator of the integrand of
I_{4}

is,

\begin{split}D_{I_{4}}&=a^{2}k^{2}\cos^{2}(\phi)+b^{2}k^{2}\sin^{2}(\phi)+a(1+t_{\textrm{AM}})kq\cos(\phi)\\
&+\frac{1+t_{\textrm{AM}}}{2}q^{2}-\mu.\end{split}

(49)

Applying Euler’s formula
\cos(\phi)\rightarrow\frac{1}{2}(z+\frac{1}{z})
,

D_{I_{4}}
becomes

D_{I_{4}}=\frac{(a^{2}-b^{2})k^{2}}{4}\left(z^{2}+\alpha z+\beta+\alpha z^{-1}+z^{-2}\right),

(50)

where

\begin{split}\alpha&=\frac{(1+t_{\textrm{AM}})^{2}\sqrt{1-t_{\textrm{AM}}}}{t_{\textrm{AM}}}\frac{Q}{k},\\
\beta&=\frac{2k^{2}+(1+t_{\textrm{AM}})^{2}(1-t_{\textrm{AM}})Q^{2}-2(1-t_{\textrm{AM}}^{2})}{t_{\textrm{AM}}k^{2}},\end{split}

(51)

and
Q\equiv\frac{q}{\sqrt{\mu}}
is the dimensionless momentum.
The integral over
\phi
now becomes a contour integral over the
unit circle:

\begin{split}I_{4}&=-\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{2}}\frac{2ab}{a^{2}-b^{2}}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{dk}{k}\oint_{|z|=1}f_{4}(z)dz,\\
f_{4}(z)&\equiv\frac{iz}{z^{4}+\alpha z^{3}+\beta z^{2}+\alpha z+1}.\end{split}

(52)

The four poles (denoted as
z_{1/2/3/4}
) of
f_{4}(z)
is determined
by the equation
z^{2}+\alpha z+\beta+\alpha z^{-1}+z^{-2}=0
. Such
an equation exhibits the reciprocal symmetry: if
z
is a solution,
so is
\frac{1}{z}
. Therefore, when considering relative position
of
z_{1/2/3/4}
with respect to the unit circle, there are three
situations: 1) all
z_{1/2/3/4}
are on the unit circle; 2) two poles
are on the unit circle, one inside and one outside the unit circle.
Here we always choose
z_{1}
on the unit circle (so
z_{2}=\frac{1}{z_{1}}

is also on the unit circle) and
z_{3}
inside the unit circle (so

z_{4}=\frac{1}{z_{3}}
is outside the unit circle). 3) two poles
are inside the unit circle and two outside the unit circle. We choose

z_{1}
and
z_{3}
inside the unit circle, then
z_{2}=\frac{1}{z_{1}}

and
z_{4}=\frac{1}{z_{3}}
are outside the unit circle.

V.2
Positions of the four poles

Defining
w=z+\frac{1}{z}
, then
w^{2}+\alpha w+\beta-2=0
. The
discriminant is
\mathcal{D}=(1-t_{\textrm{AM}})C(k)/(t_{\textrm{AM}}^{2}k^{2})

with

C(k)\equiv\left(1-t_{\textrm{AM}}^{2}\right)^{2}Q^{2}+8t_{\textrm{AM}}\left(1+t_{\textrm{AM}}-k^{2}\right).

(53)

With
k\in[0,1]
and
t_{\textrm{AM}}\in(0,1)
, we have
C(k)>0

and
\mathcal{D}>0
. Then

w_{\pm}=\frac{\sqrt{1-t_{\textrm{AM}}}}{2t_{\textrm{AM}}k}\left[-\left(1+t_{\textrm{AM}}\right)^{2}Q\pm\sqrt{C(k)}\right],

(54)

so both
w_{+}
and
w_{-}
are purely real.

Suppose
z_{1/2}
(
z_{3/4}
) corresponds to the solution
z=\frac{w_{+}\pm\sqrt{w_{+}^{2}-4}}{2}

(
z=\frac{w_{-}\pm\sqrt{w_{-}^{2}-4}}{2}
). Since
w_{+}
is purely
real, if
w_{+}^{2}-4>0
, then we have
z_{1}=\frac{w_{+}-\sqrt{w_{+}^{2}-4}}{2}

inside the unit circle and
z_{2}=\frac{w_{+}+\sqrt{w_{+}^{2}-4}}{2}

outside the unit circle. Nevertheless, if
w_{+}^{2}-4<0
, then we
have
z_{1/2}=\frac{w_{+}\pm i\sqrt{-w_{+}^{2}+4}}{2}
both on the
unit circle. The same analysis applies for
w_{-}
and
z_{3/4}
.

The two discriminants are calculated as
w_{\pm}^{2}-4=(1+t_{\textrm{AM}})D_{\mp}(k)/(2t_{\textrm{AM}}^{2}k^{2})

with

\begin{split}D_{\pm}(k)&\equiv\left(1-t_{\textrm{AM}}^{4}\right)Q^{2}\pm\left(1-t_{\textrm{AM}}^{2}\right)Q\sqrt{C(k)}\\
&+4t_{\textrm{AM}}\left(1-t_{\textrm{AM}}-k^{2}\right).\end{split}

(55)

If
k^{2}<1-t_{\textrm{AM}}
, then
D_{+}(k)>0
and
w_{-}^{2}-4>0
.
For the left cases, the condition
D_{\pm}(k)=0
gives two critical

Q
curves:

\begin{split}Q_{c1}(k)&=\left|\sqrt{\frac{2-k^{2}}{1+t_{\textrm{AM}}}}-\sqrt{\frac{k^{2}}{1-t_{\textrm{AM}}}}\right|,\\
Q_{c2}(k)&=\sqrt{\frac{2-k^{2}}{1+t_{\textrm{AM}}}}+\sqrt{\frac{k^{2}}{1-t_{\textrm{AM}}}},\end{split}

(56)

which are shown in Fig.
3
(a)-(b). These two critical

Q
values separate the
(k,Q)
-parameter space into different regions
according to the sign of
w_{\pm}^{2}-4
. In Fig.
3
(a)
(Fig.
3
(b)), the region marked by “+” sign means

w_{+}^{2}-4>0
(
w_{-}^{2}-4>0
), while
w_{+}^{2}-4<0
(
w_{-}^{2}-4<0
)
in the region marked by “-” sign.

At given
(k,Q)
, there are three different possibilities according
to sign of
w_{\pm}^{2}-4
: (a) both are positive, so poles
z_{1}

and

We first evaluate

V.7
Geometric interpretation

In previous section, we have shown that, for
\Delta=0
,
S(t_{\textrm{AM}},Q,0)/\mathcal{A}

exhibits a nonanalytic dependence on
Q
according to the contour
integration of complex analysis. The nonanalyticity occurs at
Q_{c}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{1-t_{\textrm{AM}}}}-\sqrt{\frac{1}{1+t_{\textrm{AM}}}}

which corresponds to
q_{c}=a-b
. As shown in Fig.
5
(a),
such a
q_{c}
is the critical momentum that two of the four crossings
between the two Fermi ellipses (
E_{{\bf k}\pm}=A({\bf k})\pm B({\bf k})=0
)
and the black dashed circle (
A({\bf k})=0
) becomes one tangent
point.

We note that the black dashed circle in Fig.
5
(a)
is where the denominator of the first term in Eq. (
44
)
vanishes. i.e. it represents gapless excitations. Therefore the onset
of FFLO phase here is accompanied by the decrease of gapless excitations.
Such a fact is in huge contrast with FFLO phase driven by magnetic
field, where FFLO phase emerges when the crossing point increase from
0 to 1.

The physical meaning of
q_{c}
can also be understood through the
robustness of the BFS. Since the BFS is protected by the topological
invariant
l
, it remains stable until a topological phase transition
occurs. This transition is precisely what happens at
q_{c}
: as
shown in Fig.
5
(b), the number of BFS decreases from
4 to 3 at this critical momentum.

Motivated by the above analysis, we may determine
q_{c}
when
\mathbf{q}

is along the
(1,0)
(or equivalently
(0,1)
) direction, where
obtaining a closed form of
S(t_{\textrm{AM}},Q,0)/\mathcal{A}
is
difficult. Fig.
5
(c) demonstrates the condition when
the number of crossings decreases from 4 to 2, and the corresponding

q_{c}
is

q_{c}=\frac{\sqrt{2}(a^{2}-b^{2})}{\sqrt{a^{2}+b^{2}}}=2\sqrt{\frac{\mu t_{\mathrm{AM}}^{2}}{1-t_{\mathrm{AM}}^{2}}}.

(72)

As elaborated in Appendix A, our numerical simulation verifies that
Eq. (
72
) indeed gives a good estimate of the actual
q_{c}

when
\mathbf{q}
is along the
(1,0)
direction.

VI
CONCLUSIONS AND OUTLOOKS

In summary, we have analytically investigated the ground-state phase
diagram of 2D spin-1/2 Fermi gases exhibiting
d
-wave altermagnetic
spin splitting in the presence of
s
-wave pairing, within the framework
of BdG mean-field theory. Under both fixed chemical potential and
fixed total particle number conditions, we identify four distinct
phases as illustrated in Fig.
2
. Our results definitely
confirm the existence of FFLO states under
s
-wave pairing, thereby
resolving previous contradictory conclusions in the literatures. In
addition, we provide a geometric interpretation of the onset
\mathbf{q}_{c}

of FFLO states, offering intuitive insight into its origin. Quite
unexpectedly, we also discover a nodal SF, characterized by the TBFS.
Given the simplicity of the system - a single band, without external
magnetic field, and under nodeless
s
-wave pairing - the presence
of TBFS is both surprising and compelling.

Fig.
2
illustrates the fundamental relationship between
anisotropic NRSS and attractive pairing. When the attractive pairing
is strong, BCS state is expected with a zero-momentum and fully gapped
Fermi surface. Meanwhile, for a very large anisotropic NRSS, the metallic
normal phase is anticipated. Nodal SF and FFLO occur when anisotropic
NRSS and attractive pairing are both at intermediate strength. All
phases can be realized in either ultracold gases or electronic superconductors
by choosing an appropriate
t_{\textrm{AM}}
. In the context of ultracold
gases, a large
t_{\textrm{AM}}
is required as shown in Fig.
2
(a),
so we can access to all four phases via tuning the two-body interactions

\varepsilon_{B}
through Feshbach resonance
Chin2010
. For
electronic superconductors, a small
t_{\textrm{AM}}
suffices, as
shown in Fig.
2
(b). By increasing the Fermi energy

\varepsilon_{F}
via techniques like electrostatic gating
Wu2023
,
we can sequentially observe BCS, nodal SF, FFLO, and normal phases.

We conclude this section by noting several natural extensions of this
work. First, incorporating additional physical ingredients - such
as external magnetic fields, spin-orbit coupling,
d
-wave pairing,
and finite temperature - could significantly enrich the phase diagram
and reveal new emergent pairing phenomena. Second, while our analysis
is based on the simplest single-plane-wave ansatz here, higher plane-wave
FFLO ansatz such as the LO state with
\Delta(\mathbf{x})=\Delta\cos(\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{x})

are expected to be energetically more favorable. Finally, the topological
BFS uncovered in this work may provide a fertile platform for realizing
Majorana bound states, opening the door to potential applications
in topological quantum computation.
