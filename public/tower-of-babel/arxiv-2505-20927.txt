Uncertainty Partitioning with Probabilistic Feasibility and Performance Guarantees for Chance-Constrained Optimization
F. Cordiano, M. Jafarian, B. De Schutter

Abstract
We propose a novel distribution-free scheme to solve optimization problems where the goal is to minimize the expected value of a cost function subject to probabilistic constraints. Unlike standard sampling-based methods, our idea consists of partitioning the uncertainty domain in a user-defined number of sets, enabling more flexibility in the trade-off between conservatism and computational complexity. We provide sufficient conditions to ensure that our approximated problem is feasible for the original stochastic program, in terms of chance constraint satisfaction. In addition, we perform a rigorous performance analysis, by quantifying the distance between the optimal values of the original and the approximated problem. We show that our approach is tractable for optimization problems that include model predictive control of piecewise affine systems, and we demonstrate the benefits of our approach, in terms of the trade-off between conservatism and computational complexity, on a numerical example.

Introduction

Optimization problems with uncertainty are of central aspect in several fields, ranging from control
[
1
]
, to operation research
[
2
]
, and mathematical finance
[

Under these circumstances, neglecting uncertain components in the underlying optimization problem may significantly degrade the quality of the solution, in terms of performance, safety, and reliability.

Among the diverse paradigms proposed in the literature, stochastic optimization
[
4
]
has recently gained a lot of interest, thanks to the possibility of optimizing an expected cost while guaranteeing constraint satisfaction with a desired probability level, leading to solutions that are less conservative compared to purely robust approaches
[
5
]
. On the other hand, dealing with probabilistic constraints or expected cost functions is difficult: First, it generally leads to non-convex, or even intractable, optimization problems, even for simple cost and constraint functions
[
6
]
; Second, it requires a probabilistic description of the uncertainty; Third, even when the two previous points are satisfied, e.g., by suitable assumptions or approximations, the solved problem should deliver guarantees in terms of performance and probability of constraint violations, while not resulting in excessively conservative solutions.

These aspects have constantly motivated significant research efforts within this field. For example, several approaches assume knowledge of the uncertainty distribution
[
7
,
8
,
9

, or of the statistical moments
[
10

. Although these approaches provide tractable approximations of the original problem and feasibility guarantees, assumptions on the specific type of the distribution are often not met in practice. For this reason, sampling-based approaches gained a lot of success in the recent years. Initially, a sample-average approximation for stochastic optimization was proposed in
[
11
]
, and feasibility guarantees for chance-constrained problems were given in
[
12

. However, a common drawback of this approach is that the computational complexity grows rapidly in the number of samples, and typically many samples are required to provide feasible solutions. In this regard, randomized approaches
[
13
]
,
[
14
]
replace a chance constraint with a number of independent hard constraints, offering an elegant theory to control the risk of constraint violation while leading to more tractable schemes compared to the sample-average approximation. However, a common feature of randomized approaches is that the sample complexity depends on the number of decision variables; hence, they may result in high computational complexity and conservatism
[
15
]
, especially for high-dimensional or non-convex settings
[
16
]
.

As most of the papers in the literature focus on feasibility, less attention has been devoted to performance guarantees, i.e., the distance between the optimal value of the original problem and the approximated one. Continuity results for the optimal value of a stochastic optimization problem are proposed in
[
17

19

In contrast, the work
[
12

proposes an approach to practically generate a lower bound to the optimal value of a chance-constrained problem by solving an auxiliary optimization problem based on a sample-average approximation, which, however, may not be computationally efficient to solve. In the context of randomized algorithms, the sampling-and-discarding approach proposed in
[
21
]
provides a significant contribution in this direction, but explicit performance bounds are missing. In
[
16

, explicit performance bounds are given for randomized solutions of chance-constrained programs with deterministic cost functions, but the resulting sample complexity scales exponentially with the dimension of the uncertainty, and explicit computation requires knowledge of the distribution.

From a practical viewpoint, both feasibility and performance guarantees are relevant in several applications. For example, in stochastic optimal control problems,
probabilistic constraint satisfaction is crucial for the safety of the system against uncertainty

In view of these considerations, a tractable approximation for chance-constrained optimization problems, with expected performance minimization, that is computationally well-tractable and not too conservative, and endowed with non-conservative feasibility and performance guarantees, is missing. To tackle this issue, in this paper we propose a novel distribution-free method to solve such stochastic optimization problem, which lies in between a sampling-based method and a robust approach. We provide a lower bound on the number of samples for probabilistic feasibility guarantees, and we derive informative performance bounds. The novel contributions of this paper are summarized as follows:

Preliminaries and problem formulation

With

\mathcal{X}\oplus\mathcal{Y}=\{z:z=x+y,x\in\mathcal{X},y\in\mathcal{Y}\}
. The symbol
\mathbb{B}
denotes the unit ball with center in the origin.
A probability space is denoted by
(\Xi,\mathfrak{B}(\Xi),\mathbb{P})
, where

II-B

Problem formulation

Consider
\mathcal{X}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n_{x}}
,
\Theta\subset\mathbb{R}^{n_{\theta}}
, and the probability space
(\Theta,\mathfrak{B}(\Theta),\mathbb{P})
. Given the measurable functions
J:\mathcal{X}\times\Theta\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
and
g:\mathcal{X}\times\Theta\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, let us consider the following chance-constrained optimization problem:

\displaystyle\text{CP}_{\varepsilon}:\quad\min_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\

\displaystyle\E[J(x,\theta)]

s.t.

\displaystyle\ProbOp\left(g(x,\theta)\leq 0\right)\geq 1-\varepsilon,

with optimal value
J^{\star}_{\text{CP}_{\varepsilon}}
and feasible set
\mathcal{F}_{\text{CP}_{\varepsilon}}
. Also,
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\theta}}
is stochastic uncertainty, and
\varepsilon\in[0,1)
is the risk parameter.

Let

. In this article, we consider a class of constraint functions described by

\displaystyle g(x,\theta):=\min_{h\in\{1,...,Z\}}g_{h}(x,\theta)

(1)

where

are measurable functions. Together with (
1
), the feasible set of CP
ε
can be equivalently written as

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{\text{CP}_{\varepsilon}}=\{x\in\mathcal{X}:\ProbOp\left(\lor_{h=1}^{Z}(g_{h}(x,\theta)\leq 0)\right)\geq 1-\varepsilon\},

(2)

that is, we require that the probability that at least one of the functions
g_{h}
is non-positive is at least
1-\varepsilon
. Note that this formulation is similar to the one considered in
[
16
]
, and includes in particular chance constraints with integer variables.
Also, as we will discuss in Sections
V
and
VI
, a wide class of optimal control problems, e.g. for dynamical systems with logical constraints, can be formulated as CP

, for a suitable design of the functions
J
and

For the sake of simplicity of the exposition, we assume the functions

to have values in
\mathbb{R}
, which is without loss of generality
1
1

1

To model multiple constraints,

can be designed as

, which again has values in

.

As previously mentioned, solving CP

can be challenging even if

is known, since, in general, it would require to evaluate multidimensional integrals over the distribution describing

. For these two reasons, problems such as CP

III
Uncertainty partitioning

The underlying idea to solve CP
ε
relies on partitioning the uncertainty domain
\Theta
. Our approach does not require assumptions on the distribution that induces the probability measure
\mathbb{P}
, nor its explicit knowledge. Instead, we require that a number
N\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
of uncertainty realizations is available, with the intuition that a sufficiently large number of samples provides a good description of
\mathbb{P}
.

III-A

Partitioning scheme

Throughout the paper, we consider the following assumptions:

Assumption 1

A dataset of
N
independent and identically distributed realizations
\{\theta^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^{N}
, drawn from
\mathbb{P}
, is available.

Assumption 2

There exists a set
\mathcal{D}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n_{\theta}}
such that: 1)
\Theta\subseteq\mathcal{D}
; 2)
\mathcal{D}
can be partitioned into
K
non-empty sets, i.e.:
\exists\mathcal{D}^{(j)}\subseteq\mathcal{D},\forall j\in\{1,...,K\}
, with
K\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
, such that:
\mathcal{D}^{(j)}\neq\emptyset,\forall j\in\{1,...,K\},

\ProbOp(\theta\in\mathcal{D}^{(i)}\cap\mathcal{D}^{(j)})=0
, for all
i,j\in\{1,...,K\},i\neq j
, and
\bigcup_{j=1}^{K}\mathcal{D}^{(j)}=\mathcal{D}
. The sets
\mathcal{D}^{(j)}
are assumed to be known,
\forall j\in\{1,...,K\}
.

We observe two aspects in Assumption
2
. First, we introduce a set
\mathcal{D}
that contains the uncertainty domain
\Theta
because, in some cases, we may not know the actual uncertainty domain exactly. However, depending on the application, we may be able to compute an over-approximation. For example, this can be the case if the uncertainty has a physical meaning, e.g., in temperature control
[
25
]
. Note that we have

\ProbOp(\theta\in\Theta)=1
and
\ProbOp(\theta\in\mathcal{D}\setminus\Theta)=0
;
hence, considering the superset
\mathcal{D}
instead of
\Theta
will not affect the analysis (by requiring the functions
J,g_{h},h\in\{1,...,Z\}
, to be defined on the superset
\mathcal{D}
).
In addition, while
\Theta
can be any set, we can design
\mathcal{D}
with some convenient shape for computational reasons, as we will see later. Second, note that the condition
\ProbOp(\theta\in\mathcal{D}^{(i)}\cap\mathcal{D}^{(j)})=0
requires the sets
\mathcal{D}^{(j)}
to be pairwise disjoint, up to 0-probability events. Thus, if
\mathbb{P}
admits a density, which is the case of continuous distributions, any partition whose sets have pairwise disjoint interiors satisfies Assumption
2

In Section
V
, we will discuss three options to provide such a partition.

We denote the sets of the indices corresponding to the realizations that belong to a given set by

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}^{(j)}:=\{i\in\{1,...,N\}:\theta^{(i)}\in\mathcal{D}^{(j)}\},\ \forall j\in\{1,...,K\},

where, in view of Assumption
2
, they are pairwise disjoint with probability 1, and
\cup_{j=1}^{K}\mathcal{C}^{(j)}=\{1,...,N\}
.
For each set
\mathcal{D}^{(j)}
, we select a representative element
\hat{\theta}^{(j)},\forall j\in\{1,...,K\}
. Although the choice is free, a possible one can be the average of the realizations that belong to
\mathcal{D}^{(j)}
, i.e.

\displaystyle\hat{\theta}^{(j)}:=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{C}^{(j)}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{C}^{(j)}}\theta^{(i)},\ \forall j\in\{1,...,K\},

(3)

for which we observe that
\hat{\theta}^{(j)}\in\mathcal{D}^{(j)}
if

is convex.
We define the probabilities
\hat{p}^{(j)}
as the empirical probability mass associated to region

, i.e.

\displaystyle\begin{split}\hat{p}^{(j)}:&=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbf{1}_{i\in\mathcal{C}^{(j)}},\ \forall j\in\{1,...,K\},\end{split}

(4)

for which we have
\sum_{j=1}^{K}\hat{p}^{(j)}=1
with probability 1, in view of Assumption
2
.
Since the realizations
\{\theta^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^{N}
are drawn from
\mathbb{P}
independently, the probability

is the approximation, obtained via counting, of the actual probability that

, i.e.,

\displaystyle\hat{p}^{(j)}\approx\ProbOp(\theta\in\mathcal{D}^{(j)}),\quad\forall j\in\{1,...,K\},

(5)

where the accuracy of the approximation increases with

.

In the proposed approach we approximate the objective function in CP
ε
by only employing the probabilities
\hat{p}^{(j)}
and the representative elements
\hat{\theta}^{(j)}
. Thus, we optimize

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{K}\hat{p}^{(j)}J(x,\hat{\theta}^{(j)}),

(6)

which can be seen as the expectation of the cost function
J
, taken over the discrete distribution with domain on the set
\{\hat{\theta}^{(j)}\}_{j=1}^{K}
and occurrence probabilities
\{\hat{p}^{(j)}\}_{j=1}^{K}
.
Concerning the chance constraint, we approximate (
2
) by

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{K}\hat{p}^{(j)}\mathbf{1}_{[\lor_{h=1}^{Z}(g_{h}(x,\theta)\leq 0,\forall\theta\in\mathcal{D}^{(j)})]}\geq 1-\varepsilon,

(7)

where we optimally select for which of the sets in
\{\mathcal{D}^{(j)}\}_{j=1}^{K}
the constraint is robustly satisfied at least by a function
2

2

If
g_{h}
represents multidimensional constraints, i.e.
g_{h}:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{D}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n_{\mathrm{constr}}}
, the robustification is performed equivalently element-wise, and the argument of the indicator function is:
\lor_{h=1}^{Z}\left(g_{h,i}(x,\theta)\leq 0,\forall\theta\in\mathcal{D}^{(j)},\forall{i\in\{1,...,n_{\mathrm{constr}}\}}\right)
.

g_{h},h\in\{1,...,Z\}
, in a way that the empirical probability mass contained in such sets is at least
1-\varepsilon
.
Then, by putting together (

\displaystyle\text{PP}_{\varepsilon}:\quad\min_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{K}\hat{p}^{(j)}J(x,\hat{\theta}^{(j)})

s.t.

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{K}\hat{p}^{(j)}\mathbf{1}_{[\lor_{h=1}^{Z}(g_{h}(x,\theta)\leq 0,\forall\theta\in\mathcal{D}^{(j)})]}\geq 1-\varepsilon,

with optimal value denoted by

and feasible set

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{\text{PP}_{\varepsilon}}:=\{

\displaystyle x\in\mathcal{X}:

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{K}\hat{p}^{(j)}\mathbf{1}_{[\lor_{h=1}^{Z}(g_{h}(x,\theta)\leq 0,\forall\theta\in\mathcal{D}^{(j)})]}\geq 1-\varepsilon\}

(8)

Given the previous considerations, we observe that PP
ε
provides a discrete approximation, in terms of cost function and chance constraint, of the original CP
ε
, regardless of the nature of

. Thus, in contrast to CP

In particular, the computational complexity of PP

is determined also by

, which is user-defined. Intuitively, a large

results in a very accurate discrete approximation of the original problem, making the solution of PP

very close to the original CP

, at the expenses of increased computational complexity.

Although its intuitive interpretation, there is no direct link between the optimal solution of PP

and the optimal solution of CP

. This is due to the partitioning approximation, and due to the fact that

III-B

Probabilistic error

As previously stated,
\mathbb{P}
does not need to be known. Thus, the feasibility analysis is purely data-driven, and it only relies on the accuracy of the estimated probabilities
\hat{p}^{(j)},\forall j\in\{1,...,K\}
, which become more and more accurate as more samples are available. Let the following assumption hold:

Assumption 3

Let
\delta\in(0,\varepsilon]
,
\beta\in(0,1]
. We assume that

\displaystyle N\geq\frac{K\log 2+\log\frac{1}{\beta}}{2\delta^{2}}.

Intuitively, the parameter
\delta
represents the level of accuracy that we would like to achieve in the estimates for the probabilities
\ProbOp(\theta\in\mathcal{D}^{(j)}),\forall j\in\{1,...,K\}
, which, as for
K
, is user-defined, provided that the given condition for
N
is satisfied. The parameter
\beta
, instead, is a confidence parameter, which encodes the fact that the probabilities
\{\hat{p}^{(j)}\}_{j=1}^{K}
are estimated empirically, thus their computation is affected by the variability of the available data. Note that smaller values for
\delta
and
\beta
require a larger

.

The condition on

in Assumption

resembles other common assumptions on the sample complexity, especially in the field of sampling-based methods
[

]
, or randomized algorithms for non-convex programs
[

31

. However, an important difference is that our requirement for

is linear in

, which is a user-defined parameter. On the other side, in
[
14
]
,
[

,

]
, the sample complexity is affected by the number of decision variables in the optimization problem, which can be high especially in non-convex settings

IV-B

Probabilistic performance bounds

In several applications, e.g. when the objective function represents a cost or a consumption, optimal performances are also of interest in addition to feasibility guarantees.
Thus, the goal of this section is to provide an interval

\displaystyle J^{\star}_{\text{CP}_{\varepsilon}}\in[\underline{c},\bar{c}],

\displaystyle\text{RP}_{\varepsilon}:\ \min_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{K}\hat{p}^{(j)}\mathbf{1}_{[\lor_{h=1}^{Z}(g_{h}(x,\hat{\theta}^{(j)})\leq\gamma^{(j)}_{h}(x,\hat{\theta}^{(j)}))]}\geq 1-\varepsilon

\displaystyle\begin{split}&g_{h}(x,\hat{\theta}^{(j)})-g_{h}(x,\theta)\leq\gamma^{(j)}_{h}(x,\hat{\theta}^{(j)}),\\
&\forall x\in\mathcal{X},\forall\theta\in\mathcal{D}^{(j)}.\end{split}

IV-C

Local continuity of the optimal value

Theorem
2
provides the sought bound (
11
), which can be obtained by solving
\text{PP}_{\varepsilon-\delta}
and
\text{RP}_{\varepsilon+\delta}
. In this section, we derive a theoretical bound for

\displaystyle|J^{\star}_{\text{PP}_{\varepsilon-\delta}}-\ J^{\star}_{\text{CP}_{\varepsilon}}|,

(19)

in order to find a mathematical expression that characterizes the source of the optimality gap between the problem that we actually solve, i.e. PP
ε-δ
, and the problem that we would ideally solve, i.e. CP
ε
, without going through the solution of the auxiliary problem RP
ε+δ
.

Bounding (
19
) is in principle difficult, since a direct study of
|J_{\text{CP}_{\varepsilon}}-J_{\text{PP}_{\varepsilon-\delta}}|
requires to evaluate the distance between
\mathcal{F}_{\text{CP}_{\varepsilon}}
and
\mathcal{F}_{\text{PP}_{\varepsilon-\delta}}
, which can be difficult since
\mathcal{F}_{\text{CP}_{\varepsilon}}
can be a very complex set (possibly non-convex) for a general distribution
\mathbb{P}
. However, we observe that deriving an optimality bound for

\displaystyle J^{\star}_{\text{PP}_{\varepsilon-\delta}}-J^{\star}_{\text{RP}_{\varepsilon+\delta}},

(20)

will lead to a bound for (
19
), by combining (
16
) with the bound for (
20
) (note that (
20
) is always non-negative). In particular, since problems PP
ε-δ
and RP
ε+δ
optimize the same cost function, the performance bound (
20
) depends only on the distance between the constraint sets, i.e. respectively
\mathcal{F}_{\text{PP}_{\varepsilon-\delta}}
and
\mathcal{F}_{\text{RP}_{\varepsilon+\delta}}
, which have a similar structure in view of the discrete approximation in PP
ε-δ
and RP
ε+δ
.

Leveraging tools from variational analysis
[
32
]
, in order to evaluate how much the constraints set differ from each other, we consider the following definition:

Definition 1

(Hausdorff distance, cf.
[
32
]
, Chapter 4).
Let
\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be closed and non-empty.
The Hausdorff distance between
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
is defined as

\displaystyle\mathbb{d}(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}):=\inf\left\{\rho\geq 0:\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathcal{B}\oplus\rho\mathbb{B},\mathcal{B}\subseteq\mathcal{A}\oplus\rho\mathbb{B}\right\}.

As we will see in the next lemma, (
20
) can be bounded in terms of the Hausdorff distance between
\mathcal{F}_{\text{RP}_{\varepsilon+\delta}}
and
\mathcal{F}_{\text{PP}_{\varepsilon-\delta}}
.

Lemma 4

Let us assume that
\mathcal{F}_{\text{RP}_{\varepsilon+\delta}},\mathcal{F}_{\text{PP}_{\varepsilon-\delta}}
are non-empty.
Then, it holds that

\displaystyle 0\leq J^{\star}_{\text{PP}_{\varepsilon-\delta}}-J^{\star}_{\text{RP}_{\varepsilon+\delta}}\leq L_{x}\mathbb{d}(\mathcal{F}_{\text{PP}_{\varepsilon-\delta}},\mathcal{F}_{\text{RP}_{\varepsilon+\delta}}).

(21)

Proof:

See Appendix A.
∎Note that this bound is deterministic, i.e., it holds for any possible sample set
\{\theta^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^{N}
, since once a sample set
\{\theta^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^{N}
is given, PP
ε-δ
and RP
ε+δ
are deterministic optimization problems.

The Hausdorff distance between sets is not always easy to compute or bound. Thus, in this section, we focus on a specific type of constraint function defined by

\displaystyle g_{h}(x,\theta)=C_{h}x+D_{h}\theta+b_{h},\ \forall h\in\{1,...,Z\},

(22)

where
C_{h},D_{h}
, and
b_{h}
, are matrices and vectors of appropriate dimension,
\forall h\in\{1,...,Z\}
. As we will see in Section
VI
, this class of constraints includes, e.g., controlled dynamical systems with logical constraints (e.g., PWA systems), with additive uncertainty.

In this setting, we observe that the robustified chance constraint in PP
ε
reads as:

\sum_{j=1}^{K}\hat{p}^{(j)}\mathbf{1}_{[\lor_{h=1}^{Z}(C_{h}x+D_{h}\theta+b_{h}\leq 0,\forall\theta\in\mathcal{D}^{(j)})]}\geq 1-\varepsilon,

and for constraint functions as in (

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{\text{PP}_{\varepsilon}}=\{

\displaystyle x\in\mathcal{X}:

We observe the following aspects deriving from Lemma

1.

In general, the value of a chance-constrained program with a discrete distribution can be discontinuous with respect to changes in
\varepsilon

2.

Interestingly, (

3.

and the corresponding
\mathcal{F}_{\text{RP}_{\varepsilon}}^{k}

Tractability of the proposed approach

Problem PP

8
).
Such constraint can be solved by a branch-and-bound algorithm whenever the robust constraint

\displaystyle g_{h}(x,\theta)\leq 0,\forall\theta\in\mathcal{D}^{(j)}

(33)

admits an equivalent deterministic reformulation, for
j\in\{1,...,K\}
and
h\in\{1,...,Z\}
.
In the following we fix, for simplicity, two indices
j
and
h
. In several cases, an equivalent deterministic reformulation for (
33
) exists, which is the case, e.g., when the function
g_{h}
is affine in
\theta
and the set
\mathcal{D}^{(j)}
, is compact and convex (see
[
5
]
, Chapter 2 and 5). More generally, the robust constraint appearing in (
33
) can be solved whenever the function
g_{h}
is convex or monotone with respect to
\theta
, and
\mathcal{D}^{(j)}
is a polytope. Then, according to Bauer’s Maximum Principle
[
33
]
, the maximum with respect to
\theta
is attained at least at one of the vertices of the uncertainty set. Note that, although this induces an equivalent deterministic reformulation of (
33
), vertex enumeration can be computationally expensive for high-dimensional sets
\mathcal{D}^{(j)}
. In addition, there are some specific classes of nonlinear functions, e.g., concave with respect to the uncertainty, for which an equivalent tractable reformulation can be derived (see
[
5
]
, Chapter 13). Last, we also mention the case in which
g_{h}
is
L
-Lipschitz with respect to
\theta
, uniformly in
x
. Constraint tightening based on Lipschitz bounds is possible (see e.g.
[
34
]
), but these bounds are typically very conservative unless the size of the set
\mathcal{D}^{(j)}
is small enough.

Last, we observe that if only feasibility is of interest, the computational complexity of PP
ε
can be reduced. Indeed, in
\mathcal{F}_{\text{PP}_{\varepsilon}}
, defined as in (
8
), we optimally choose for which of the sets in
\{\mathcal{D}^{(j)}\}_{j=1}^{K}
the constraint must be robustly satisfied, which is a combinatorial problem. An alternative would be to select such sets greedily, e.g., by ordering
\{\tilde{p}^{(j)}\}_{j=1}^{K}
in decreasing order, selecting the first elements whose sum is at least
1-\varepsilon
, and enforcing robust constraint satisfaction with respect to the corresponding uncertainty sets. This idea essentially fixes the value of the indicator function in
\mathcal{F}_{\text{PP}_{\varepsilon}}
to some predefined values, thus it might significantly reduce the computational complexity, but it may yield a suboptimal solution. Note, however, that the logical “or” in
\mathcal{F}_{\text{PP}_{\varepsilon}}
is still a combinatorial constraint, which, however, depends on the problem of interest (e.g., control of dynamical systems with logical constraints), rather than on the proposed approach.

V-B

Application: Optimal control of PWA systems

The class of constraint functions described in (
22
) is often encountered in several optimization problems of practical interest, i.e., optimization problems with a class of logical constraints. In this section, we show how to implement our algorithm for MPC problems for discrete-time PWA systems with chance constraints. PWA systems are a special class of nonlinear system, where the function describing the dynamics is indeed piecewise affine in state, input, and uncertainty. This class of systems includes linear systems, and they are often used to model physical systems with logical or discrete components (e.g., energy storage systems), or to approximate nonlinear systems with a user-defined level of accuracy
[
35
]
.

V-B
1
Modeling

The dynamics of a general PWA system with additive uncertainty are described by

\displaystyle s_{t+1}=A_{l}s_{t}+B_{l}u_{t}+v_{l}+C_{l}\eta_{t}\ \Leftrightarrow\ \begin{bmatrix}s_{t}\\
u_{t}\\
\eta_{t}\end{bmatrix}\in\Omega_{l},

(34)

where
A_{l},B_{l},C_{l}
, and
v_{l}
are the system matrices and vectors of appropriate dimension, for
l=1,...,m
, with
m\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
. Also,
s_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{s}}
and
u_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{u}}
are, respectively, the state and input of the system at time step
t
, and
\Omega_{l}
,
l=1,...,m
, are non-empty polytopic sets such that
\{\Omega_{l}\}_{l=1}^{m}
is a polytopic partition of the state, input, and uncertainty space.
The variable
\eta_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\eta}}
is an exogenous additive stochastic signal, and it is described by a distribution inducing the probability measure
\mathbb{P}
.
Given a stage cost function
\ell:\mathbb{R}^{n_{s}}\times\mathbb{R}^{n_{u}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
and a prediction horizon
N_{\text{pred}}\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
, the goal of the controller is to employ the system dynamics (
34
) to find a sequence of inputs
u^{\star}_{t},...,u^{\star}_{t+N_{\text{pred}}-1}
such that the expected performance of the system over the prediction window

\displaystyle\E\textsubscript{$\eta_{t},...,\eta_{t+N_{\text{pred}}-1}$}\left[\sum_{k=1}^{N_{\text{pred}}}\ell(s_{t+k},u_{t+k-1})\right]

is minimized, while satisfying the following constraints on the state and on the input of the system:

\displaystyle\ProbOp(s_{t+k}\in\mathcal{S},\ k=1,...,N_{\text{pred}})\geq 1-\varepsilon,

\displaystyle u_{t+k}\in\mathcal{U},\ k=0,...,N_{\text{pred}}-1,

where
\mathcal{S}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n_{s}},\mathcal{U}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n_{u}}
are polytopes.

Now, to match the constraint formulation in (
22
), we introduce the following compact notation:
\mathbf{s}_{t}=[s_{t}^{\top}\ ...\ s_{t+N_{\text{pred}}}^{\top}]^{\top},\mathbf{u}_{t}=[u_{t}^{\top}\ ...\ u_{t+N_{\text{pred}}-1}^{\top}]^{\top},\boldsymbol{\eta}_{t}=[\eta_{t}^{\top}\ ...\ \eta_{t+N_{\text{pred}}-1}^{\top}]^{\top}
, and

\mathbf{F}_{h}=\begin{bmatrix}I\\
A_{h_{0}}\\
A_{h_{1}}A_{h_{0}}\\
\vdots\\
\Pi_{k=0}^{N_{\text{pred}}-1}A_{h_{k}}\end{bmatrix},\ \ \mathbf{v}_{h}=\begin{bmatrix}v_{h_{0}}\\
v_{h_{1}}\\
\vdots\\
v_{h_{N_{\text{pred}}-1}}\end{bmatrix}

\mathbf{G}_{h}=\begin{bmatrix}0_{n_{s}\times n_{u}}&0_{n_{s}\times n_{u}}&\ldots&0_{n_{s}\times n_{u}}\\
B_{h_{0}}&0_{n_{s}\times n_{u}}&\ldots&0_{n_{s}\times n_{u}}\\
A_{h_{1}}B_{h_{0}}&B_{h_{1}}&\ddots&\vdots\\
\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&0_{n_{s}\times n_{u}}\\
\Pi_{k=1}^{N_{\text{pred}}-1}A_{h_{k}}B_{h_{0}}&\Pi_{k=2}^{N_{\text{pred}}-1}A_{h_{k}}B_{h_{1}}&...&B_{h_{N_{\text{pred}}-1}}\end{bmatrix}

\mathbf{\Gamma}_{h}=\begin{bmatrix}0_{n_{s}\times n_{\eta}}&0_{n_{s}\times n_{\eta}}&\ldots&0_{n_{s}\times n_{\eta}}\\
C_{h_{0}}&0_{n_{s}\times n_{\eta}}&\ldots&0_{n_{s}\times n_{\eta}}\\
A_{h_{1}}C_{h_{0}}&C_{h_{1}}&\ddots&\vdots\\
\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&0_{n_{s}\times n_{\eta}}\\
\Pi_{k=1}^{N_{\text{pred}}-1}A_{h_{k}}C_{h_{0}}&\Pi_{k=2}^{N_{\text{pred}}-1}A_{h_{k}}C_{h_{1}}&...&C_{h_{N_{\text{pred}}-1}}\end{bmatrix}

with
h\in\{1,...,m^{N_{\text{pred}}-1}\}
.
Then, the system dynamics (
34
) over the horizon are:

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\mathbf{s}_{t}=\mathbf{F}_{h}s_{t}+\mathbf{G}_{h}\mathbf{u}_{t}+\mathbf{v}_{h}+\mathbf{\Gamma}_{h}\boldsymbol{\eta}_{t}\\
&\Leftrightarrow s_{t}\in\Omega_{h_{0}},...,s_{t+N_{\text{pred}}-1}\in\Omega_{h_{N_{\text{pred}}-1}}.\end{split}

(35)

Let
\mathbf{L}_{u},\boldsymbol{l}_{u}
describe the polytopic representation of
\mathcal{U}^{N_{\text{pred}}}
, i.e.,
(u_{t},...,u_{t+N_{\text{pred}}-1})\in\mathcal{U}^{N_{\text{pred}}}\Leftrightarrow\mathbf{L}_{u}\mathbf{u}_{t}\leq\boldsymbol{l}_{u}
, and similarly let
\mathbf{L}_{s,h},\boldsymbol{l}_{s,h}
describe the polytopic representation of
(\Omega_{h_{0}}\cap\mathcal{S})\times...\times(\Omega_{h_{N_{\text{pred}}-1}}\cap\mathcal{S})
, the final chance constrained optimal control problem can be written as

\displaystyle\begin{split}\min_{\mathbf{u}_{t}}\ &\E\left[\mathscr{L}(s_{t},\mathbf{u}_{t},\boldsymbol{\eta}_{t})\right]\\
\text{s.t.}\ &\ProbOp(\lor_{h=1}^{Z}(\mathbf{L}_{s,h}\mathbf{s}_{t}\leq\boldsymbol{l}_{s,h}))\geq 1-\varepsilon\\
&\mathbf{L}_{u}\mathbf{u}_{t}\leq\boldsymbol{l}_{u},\end{split}

(36)

with
\mathscr{L}(s_{t},\mathbf{u}_{t},\boldsymbol{\eta}_{t}):=\sum_{k=1}^{N_{\text{pred}}}\ell(s_{t+k}(s_{t},\mathbf{u}_{t},\boldsymbol{\eta}_{t}),u_{t+k-1})
, and where
s_{t+k}(s_{t},\mathbf{u}_{t},\boldsymbol{\eta}_{t})
indicates that the predicted state
s_{t+k}
is a function of the current state, the applied input sequence, and the uncertainty, according to (
35
) (thus, by explicitly substituting the PWA dynamics in the cost function).
Note that (
36
) matches the formulation of CP
ε
with
x=\mathbf{u}_{t},\mathcal{X}=\{\mathbf{u}:\mathbf{L}_{u}\mathbf{u}\leq\boldsymbol{l}_{u}\},\theta=\boldsymbol{\eta}_{t},Z=M^{N_{\text{pred}}-1},J(x,\theta)=\mathscr{L}(s_{t},\mathbf{u}_{t},\boldsymbol{\eta}_{t})
, and
g_{h}(x,\theta)=\mathbf{L}_{s,h}\mathbf{s}_{t}-\boldsymbol{l}_{s,h}
, for
h\in\{1,...,Z\}
. In particular, each function
g_{h}
is linear, thus matching the special case considered in (
22
). However, despite the linearity of
g_{h}
, optimal control problems for PWA systems are non-convex also in the deterministic case (due to the “or” constraint, which is intrinsic of the PWA dynamics), and typically they are formulated as mixed-integer problems
[
27
,
36
]
.

V-B
2
Constraint modifications and Lipschitz constants

In order to apply the proposed approach to MPC problems for PWA systems, we need some ingredients. The tightened and robust constraints are of the type discussed in (
23
), (
25
), and the tightening and relaxation parameters can be computed as in (
24
), (
26
).
Note that, in the case of PWA systems, in the resulting problem PP
ε-δ
we require that all the uncertainty values in a set
\mathcal{D}^{(j)}
activate the same mode sequence (since a given sequence of control and uncertainty uniquely determines the mode sequence). Thus, we may require the sets in the uncertainty partition to have non-overlapping boundaries, to allow that different mode sequences can be activated by sets whose boundaries may have non-empty intersections. This, in practical implementation, can be achieved by subtracting an arbitrarily small positive term to (
24
) (and adding it to (
26
)).

For the performance bounds, the Lipschitz constants of the PWA dynamics with respect to
\mathbf{u}_{t}
and
\boldsymbol{\eta}_{t}
are needed. Note that generic vector-valued continuous PWA functions (thus also the ones in (
35
)) are automatically Lipschitz, with constant equal to the largest induced matrix norm defining the slope (this is evident in the scalar case, and can be derived by using the triangle inequality for the multi-dimensional case). Then, if the stage cost is also Lipschitz, the function
\mathscr{L}
will be Lipschitz due to composition of Lipschitz functions.

For example, suppose the stage cost is the 1-norm, which is commonly used for control of PWA systems (see e.g.
[
37
]
, as the resulting problem can be then solved as a mixed-integer linear program). Thus, by considering
Q\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{s}\times n_{s}}
and
R\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{u}\times n_{u}}
with non-negative entries, the stage cost is

\ell(s,u)=\|Qs\|_{1}+\|Ru\|_{1}.

Since any norm is 1-Lipschitz (in view of the reverse triangle inequality), the Lipschitz constants of the cost function with respect to
\boldsymbol{\eta}_{t}
and with respect to
\mathbf{u}_{t}
are, respectively

\displaystyle\begin{split}&L_{\eta}=\|Q\|_{1}\max_{h\in\{1,...,Z\}}\|\mathbf{\Gamma}_{h}\|_{1},\\
&L_{u}=\left(\|Q\|_{1}\max_{h\in\{1,...,Z\}}\|\mathbf{G}_{h}\|_{1}+\|R\|_{1}\right)\sqrt{N_{\text{pred}}n_{u}},\end{split}

(37)

where we have leveraged the composition of the PWA dynamics with the 1-norm, i.e.:
\|Q\|_{1}
is the Lipschitz constant of the map
s\rightarrow\|Qs\|_{1}
,
\|R\|_{1}
is the Lipschitz constant of the map
u\rightarrow\|Ru\|_{1}
, and
\max_{h\in\{1,...,Z\}}\|\mathbf{\Gamma}_{h}\|_{1}
and
\max_{h\in\{1,...,Z\}}\|\mathbf{G}_{h}\|_{1}
are the Lipschitz constants of the PWA dynamics (
35
) with respect
\boldsymbol{\eta}_{t}
and
\mathbf{u}_{t}
, respectively. Last,
L_{u}
is rescaled in accordance to the equivalence between 1-norm and 2-norm in view of Assumption
4
.

V-B
3
Uncertainty partitioning

So far, a partition that satisfies Assumption
2
was assumed to be given. We now discuss three options to derive such a partition.

Clustering

A partition of
\mathcal{D}
may be derived from a clustering algorithm, followed, e.g., by a Voronoi partition
[
38
]
based on the resulting centroids. This method has the benefit of capturing a pattern in the uncertainty (as explored in
[
9
]
), but it requires additional data to perform the clustering algorithm. Also, modern implementations for clustering and Voronoi partitioning allow for iterative algorithms with polynomial complexity, and in practice can be very fast.

Iterative gridding

Second, if no data to perform clustering is available, or if the partitioning has to be computed fast, an idea could be to just partition the uncertainty domain such that the sets
\{\mathcal{D}^{(j)}\}_{j=1}^{K}
are with a convenient shape, e.g. box uncertainty sets.
That is, assume an initial box that contains the domain
\Theta
is available:
\mathcal{D}:=\{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\theta}}:\underline{\rho}_{i}\leq\theta_{i}\leq\bar{\rho}_{i},\forall i\in\{1,...,n_{\theta}\}\}
. Then we can find the entry
i
for which the interval
[\underline{\rho}_{i},\bar{\rho}_{i}]
is the largest, and split it into half. This procedure can be repeated iteratively, by splitting into half the largest interval among the sets currently present in the partition, until a maximum number
K
is reached.

Adaptive splitting

Third, a variant of the previous idea can be tailored based on the receding-horizon nature of MPC. Note that instead of considering all the entries of the uncertainty in this gridding procedure, an interesting variant consists of partitioning the dimensions of
\boldsymbol{\eta}_{t}
that may push the state of the system close to the constraint boundaries. For example, from the predicted state trajectories at time step
t
we can infer at which time step
\bar{k}\in\{1,...,N_{\text{pred}}\}
the predicted state
x_{t+\bar{k}}
is closest to the constraint boundaries. Then the idea could be to derive a partition for
\eta_{t+\bar{k}}
only (e.g. by using the idea in Section
V-B3
by starting from a box containing
\eta_{t+\bar{k}}
) for the solution of the MPC problem at the next time step, with the intuition that having a refined partition on the uncertainty elements that may cause a constraint violation is likely to lead significant performance improvements (since high system performance is often achieved in the vicinity of the constraint boundaries).

VII
Conclusions

This paper has presented a new strategy for solving chance-constrained optimization problems. The proposed approach is based on partitioning the uncertainty domain in a user-defined number of sets, allowing for a trade off between conservatism and computational complexity. Rigorous feasibility and performance analyses have been provided, and the results have been tailored for a class of optimization problems that includes optimal control of dynamical systems with probabilistic logical constraints. Finally, the effectiveness of the approach has been tested on a model predictive control problem for PWA systems.

Ongoing and future work consists of making the approach more sample-efficient, as well as exploring methods to speed up the solution of MPC problems for chance-constrained PWA systems.
