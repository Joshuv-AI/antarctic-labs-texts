Future Application of Virtual Reality Exercise to Various Types of Congenital Heart Disease
Authors: Lucy Youngmin Eun
Published: 2026

The essential finding of this study is that virtual reality (VR) exergaming can effectively alleviate postprandial vascular and metabolic impairments in adolescents with congenital heart disease (CHD) who consume a high-fat meal (HFM) and engage in prolonged sitting.1)

Adolescents with CHD are at a higher risk of cardiovascular disease compared to their healthy peers.2) This risk is exacerbated by behaviors like consuming HFMs and prolonged sedentary time.3),4),5) Traditional exercise programs may present participation barriers for these individuals.

The authors investigated whether VR exergaming could mitigate these adverse effects. They employed a randomized crossover design where 10 adolescents with CHD underwent two conditions after consuming an HFM: either 30 minutes of VR exercise or simply sitting for an extended period. The VR exercise group maintained their postprandial vascular function, measured by Flow-Mediated Dilation (FMD), whereas the sitting group showed a significant impairment. Furthermore, the VR exercise group experienced a reduction in systolic blood pressure and the augmentation index (AIx), an indicator of arterial stiffness. The VR exercise group also showed an attenuated increase in the triglyceride-glucose index (TyG index) post-meal. A single session of VR exergaming was sufficient to counteract the acute vascular and metabolic impairments induced by an HFM and prolonged sitting in adolescents with CHD. This suggests that VR exercise is a promising and feasible alternative for protecting vascular health in this vulnerable population.

This study highlights that VR exercise can serve as an engaging and accessible intervention to promote vascular health and mitigate the negative physiological consequences of post-meal insults in adolescents with CHD.1)

However, this randomized crossover trial did not explore how VR exercise should be tailored to different types of CHD. We expect timely discussion how VR interventions might be individualized across the heterogeneous CHD spectrum. We can simplify from its findings and the general characteristics of CHD to explore potential considerations and suggest future research directions.

This study demonstrated that even a single VR exercise session yielded positive effects. This suggests that VR exercise has the potential to offer immediate benefits to adolescents with various types of CHD, even though they have unique cardiopulmonary capacity and very individualized physical condition. The study noted that participants were New York Heart Association functional class I, indicating relatively stable cardiac function. This implies that the intensity, duration, or type of VR game might need to be adjusted based on the severity and functional classification of their CHD. For instance, patients with more restricted cardiac function might benefit more from lower intensity VR games or shorter exercise durations.

The participants had complex congenital heart defects and prior surgical treatments. This suggests that anatomical changes post-surgery or the presence of residual lesions could influence the effectiveness of VR exercise. For example, individuals with specific CHDs or hemodynamic abnormalities might respond differently to certain movements or heart rate changes during VR exercise.

The improvements observed in FMD, AIx, and TyG index in this study are related to vascular and metabolic dysfunctions that can be present across various CHD types. Therefore, VR exercise could potentially target these common vulnerabilities, regardless of the specific CHD subtype.

In the future, we should investigate the customized VR exercise application by CHD type. For major CHD cohorts, such as Fontan circulation with single ventricle physiology, transposition of great arteries, complex shunt diseases or obstructive left- or right-sided lesions, CHD with pulmonary hypertension, further study should compare the effects of VR exercise, for vascular function, metabolic markers, cardiopulmonary exercise capacity, etc.6) This approach will reveal if VR exercise is more pronounced in certain CHD subtypes or if specific subtypes require particular precautions.

We need to organize the intensity of VR exercise, target heart rate, duration, game difficulty, for different CHD subtypes or functional classifications and compare the resulting vascular and metabolic responses.7),8) This process can contribute to developing the safest and most effective VR exercise protocols for each patient group.

Moreover, we should evaluate the combined effects of VR exercise with existing cardiac rehabilitation programs or physical therapy,9) stratified by CHD patient groups, then determine if combined approaches yield higher synergistic benefits or if there are specific considerations for combining them in certain CHD categories.

It would be helpful if we can create personalized VR game content that accounts for the patient’s specific disease characteristics, physical capabilities, and psychological preferences.10) For instance, content that reduces stress on specific exercise movements or enhances elements that engage the patient. This could improve patient adherence and motivation, leading to greater long-term health outcomes.

Beyond acute effects, we can track the long-term impact of regular VR exercise on the cardiovascular health, exercise capacity, and quality of life of adolescents with CHD. This also helps to involve enhanced monitoring for potential risks specific to each disease type including arrhythmias, worsening heart failure, etc.

In conclusion, while this study presents a promising avenue for VR exercise in adolescents with CHD, further research is essential to modify its application based on the diverse characteristics of CHD and to scientifically validate these approaches. Such projects will be foundational for effectively integrating VR exercise into cardiac rehabilitation and health management strategies for CHD patients.
