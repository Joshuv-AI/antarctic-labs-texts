Quantum Liouville Cosmology
Dionysios Anninos, Thomas Hertog, Joel Karlsson

Abstract
We provide a detailed analysis of the disk path integral of timelike Liouville theory, conceived as a tractable and precise toy-model quantum cosmology in two dimensions. Disk path integrals with the insertion of matter field operators, taken along a judiciously chosen complex contour, yield states akin to the Hartle–Hawking wavefunction. Working in the fixed K K -representation, where K K is the trace of the extrinsic curvature, we compute the one-loop wavefunctions and put forward a conjecture for the all-loop expressions. A suitable pairing of Liouville disk path integrals yields a K K -independent quantity that may form the basis for a well-defined inner product on the space of Euclidean histories. We also consider other ensembles, including one with fixed area, and provide a static patch perspective with a timelike feature.

Introduction

Ample evidence indicates that cosmological theory must ultimately be based on quantum gravity.
Indeed, the quantum dynamics of the early universe is the arena par excellence where our theories of quantum gravity are put to the test.
Current models of quantum cosmology, however, remain on somewhat shaky grounds, both conceptually and computationally.
This in turn hinders one’s ability to derive precise predictions for cosmological observables, let alone verify these.
The measure problem in inflation and, relatedly, the quantum physical modelling of the observer as part of the theory, provide a case in point.

Hence, there is a need for tractable toy models of quantum cosmology that are on solid theoretical footing and from which certain core principles could be gleaned or sharpened.
Those principles concern first and foremost whether and how a quantum mechanical framework is applicable to cosmology
[
1
,
2
]
.
This matters both in the ultraviolet, where a big bang singularity may wipe out the very notion of classical spacetime, and in the infrared, where continued accelerated expansion can give rise to puzzling if not outright paradoxical phenomena
[
3
]
.
Further, if the universe is a quantum system it must have a state, which may or may not be living in Hilbert space, and which enters at least at some level in any detailed predictions
[
4
,
5
]
.

In this paper, we consider a precise toy model that, we aim to demonstrate, elucidates some of the theoretical underpinnings of quantum cosmology.
To make progress, we forgo — for now — phenomenological constraints.
On the other hand, inspired by earlier work
[
6
,
7
,
8
,
9
,
10
]
, we retain a number of realistic features.
These include the presence of a large number of locally propagating degrees of freedom, a tunable parameter that allows us to take a semiclassical limit where quantum fluctuations of the geometry are small, the existence of classical big bang/big crunch cosmologies (as well as bouncing solutions), an analogue of the conformal mode problem in Euclidean signature, a systematically calculable Euclidean gravitational path integral, and a Wheeler–DeWitt equation with a wrong sign kinetic term that supports a large number of solutions.
The price we pay is that we work in two spacetime dimensions.
This being said, we take the perspective that the richness of the above list compensates for this.

Given this considerable list of requirements, one might wonder whether such a model even exists.
Fortunately, it turns out it is rather straightforward to construct and describe.
The model we consider is given by quantum mechanically coupling a unitary two-dimensional conformal field theory, with a discrete spectrum of states, to a fluctuating metric field governed by an action that contains a cosmological term with
\Lambda>0
.
We take the central charge
c
of the conformal field theory — which is a measure of local degrees of freedom — to be positive and large, but finite.
The semiclassical limit of the theory corresponds to a large-
c
expansion, whereby the small parameter
1/c
suppresses the size of gravitational quantum fluctuations.

We work in conformal gauge
[
11
,
12
]
in which the physical metric takes the form
g_{\mu\nu}=e^{2\varphi}\tilde{g}_{\mu\nu}
, with the reference metric
\tilde{g}_{\mu\nu}
some fixed metric around an open neighbourhood about a point.
Upon fixing the conformal gauge and further integrating out the conformal matter, the problem takes the form of a specific Liouville theory known as the timelike Liouville conformal field theory.
Though nonunitary, when viewed as a theory in and of itself, timelike Liouville theory can be analysed with standard conformal bootstrap methods
[
13
]
.
Though Liouville theory is often employed in the context of the noncritical string worldsheet, here we view it as part of a quantum gravitational theory in its own right
[
14
]
.
What makes the Liouville theory timelike is the fact that the kinetic term governing the Liouville field
\varphi
has the ‘wrong’ sign, directly mimicking the unbounded nature of the conformal mode in Euclidean gravity
[
15
]
.
Despite this, recent mathematical work
[
16
,
17
]
has made progress toward a rigorous construction of the timelike Liouville path integral.
A crucial element in this construction is the complexification of the path integral contour for the timelike Liouville field
[
8
,
9
,
17
]
.

Our main purpose is to analyze the quantum mechanical structure of disk one-point functions of timelike Liouville theory.
We take the perspective that this disk path integral produces a solution to the Wheeler–DeWitt equation of the underlying gravitational theory.
The disk partition function of spacelike Liouville theory was computed at one loop in a particular limit in
[
18
]
, and more generally in
[
19
]
.
Here, we focus on the disk path integral of timelike Liouville theory
[
20
,
9
,
17
]
and mainly consider a disk decorated, in a gauge invariant manner, with the insertion of matter field operators.
We find that by carefully choosing the complexified contour, such disk path integrals produce states reminiscent of the Hartle–Hawking no-boundary wavefunction
[
21
]
.
In particular, we recover the elegant condition that the wavefunction is well-behaved and decaying in the small spatial-volume limit.
In our toy model, however, the states are definable and calculable at all orders in the quantum loop expansion.
Exploiting this, we analyse the disk integral with a range of methods and choices of boundary conditions
[
22
,
23
,
24
]
, to reveal its detailed structure in various forms.

The paper is structured as follows.
In §
2
, we review how timelike Liouville theory arises from two-dimensional quantum gravity coupled to conformal matter.
We also introduce the wavefunctions we study as disk one-point functions and review
[

,

,

]
to put forward a hypothesis for the exact, all-loop wavefunctions.
In §

We reinterpret the FZZT
[

,
24
]
boundary conditions geometrically as fixing the trace of the extrinsic curvature of the boundary (
K
).
In addition, we demonstrate how the conformal structure of the Liouville one-point functions arise.
This is known to stem from the integral over the moduli space of saddles when the insertion is light and does not backreact on the geometry
[
22
]
, but we elaborate also on the case of a heavy insertion, which does backreact and has no moduli space.
One-loop quantum fluctuations of the disk are discussed in §
4

Due to the boundary conditions, which we treat with care throughout, the Liouville field fluctuates both in the bulk and at the boundary of the disk.
Moreover, we analyse the number of negative and zero modes, which turns out to be subtler in timelike Liouville theory as compared to the spacelike case.
In §

Lastly, in §

We also comment on a thermal perspective, in contrast to the wavefunction point of view we employ throughout most of the paper.

2
Gravity as a Liouville theory

We study Euclidean two-dimensional gravity with
\Lambda>0
coupled to a matter conformal field theory (CFT).
On a fixed manifold
M
at fixed genus, the partition function reads

Z^{M}_{\text{grav}}=\int\ \frac{\mathcal{D}g}{\operatorname{Vol}(\mathrm{Diff})}{\mkern 2.0mu}e^{\vartheta\chi_{M}-\Lambda A}Z_{\mathrm{CFT}}[g]{\mkern 2.0mu},

(2.1)

where
\chi_{M}
is the Euler characteristic of
M
, and
A\equiv\int_{D}\!\mathrm{vol}
the area of the spacetime metric
g_{\mu\nu}
.
We consider
\Lambda>0
and take the matter CFT to be unitary with a discrete spectrum and a normalizable vacuum state.
Taking
\Lambda>0
, in addition to being of physical interest, has the effect of suppressing large area configurations.
Note that, although contributions from higher-genus topologies are formally suppressed at large
\vartheta
, in this case there are divergencies related to the integral over radii of the contractible cycles
[
25
]
.
(The situation might be improved by introducing supersymmetry
[
26
,
27
]
or other deformations
[
28
]
.)
Here, as a first step, we consider fixed topology, focusing on the disk.

In this section, we review how Liouville theory arises by gauge fixing the above path integral
[
29
]
(see also e.g.
[
30
]
).
Taking a unitary matter CFT, one is led to consider timelike Liouville theory
[
14
]
.
As in spacelike Liouville theory
[
19
]
, Dirichlet boundary conditions are inconsistent with conformal symmetry but it is possible to fix the physical length of the boundary.
Alternatively, one can introduce a boundary cosmological constant as a chemical potential for the boundary length
[
22
,
20
]
.
This differs from the treatment of semiclassical timelike Liouville theory on the disk in
[
9
]
.
We elaborate further on these points in §
3
and §
5
.
We also review exact results in spacelike Liouville theory on the disk
[
22
,
24
]
as well as previous analyses of the timelike theory in the same setting
[
9
,
20
]
, on the basis of which we put forward a hypothesis for the semiclassical all-loop one-point function in timelike Liouville theory, which we will verify at one loop in subsequent sections.

2.1
The Weyl gauge and timelike Liouville theory

The path integral can be gauge fixed to the Weyl gauge by inserting

1=\Delta_{\mathrm{FP}}[g]\int\ \mathcal{D}f{\mkern 2.0mu}\mathcal{D}\varphi\,\delta(g-e^{2\varphi_{f}}\tilde{g}_{f}){\mkern 2.0mu},

(2.2)

into (
2.1
), where
\Delta_{\mathrm{FP}}
is the associated Faddeev–Popov determinant.
Here, there would be an additional integral over modular parameters for higher-genus topologies.
Above, we have chosen a fiducial background metric
\tilde{g}
, acted on by a Weyl transformation parametrized by
\varphi
and a diffeomorphism
f
.
With this insertion, the integral over physical metrics
g
is readily performed due to the delta functional.
Since
\Delta_{\mathrm{FP}}
and all quantities in (
2.1
) are diffeomorphism invariant, the integral over
f
cancels against
\operatorname{Vol}(\mathrm{Diff})
.
The Faddeev–Popov determinant is the same as in bosonic string theory
[
31
]
and hence given by the standard
bc
ghost CFT of central charge
-26
,
\Delta_{\mathrm{FP}}[g]=Z_{bc}[g]
.
Upon this gauge fixing, and recalling that the physical metric is
g=e^{2\varphi}\tilde{g}
, there is a redundancy under Weyl transformations

\tilde{g}\mapsto e^{2\omega}\tilde{g}{\mkern 2.0mu},\qquad\quad\varphi\mapsto\varphi-\omega{\mkern 2.0mu},

(2.3)

with
\omega
some smooth function.
The observables of the gravitational theory have to respect this, since they should not depend on the chosen gauge.

Although there is no kinetic term for the metric in (
2.1
), dynamics for the conformal mode
\varphi
is generated by the conformal anomaly
[
29
]

Z_{\mathrm{CFT}}[e^{2\varphi}\tilde{g}]=e^{\frac{c}{24\pi}S_{\mathrm{anom}}[\varphi;\tilde{g}]}Z_{\mathrm{CFT}}[\tilde{g}]{\mkern 2.0mu},\qquad S_{\mathrm{anom}}=\int_{\mathrlap{M}}\ \mathrm{v\tilde{o}l}{\mkern 2.0mu}\bigl(\tilde{g}^{\mu\nu}\partial_{\mu}\varphi{\mkern 2.0mu}\partial_{\nu}\varphi+\tilde{R}\varphi\bigr)+2\int_{\mathrlap{\partial M}}\ \mathrm{v\tilde{o}l}{\mkern 2.0mu}\tilde{K}\varphi{\mkern 2.0mu},

(2.4)

where
\tilde{R}
is the Ricci scalar and
\tilde{K}
the trace of the extrinsic curvature of the fiducial background.
Putting the above together, the theory, on fixed topology, factorizes into the ghost CFT, the matter CFT and Liouville theory

Z_{\text{grav}}^{M}=e^{\vartheta\chi_{M}}Z_{bc}[\tilde{g}]Z_{\mathrm{CFT}}[\tilde{g}]Z_{\mathrm{L}}[\tilde{g}]{\mkern 2.0mu},\qquad Z_{\mathrm{L}}=\int\ \mathcal{D}\varphi{\mkern 2.0mu}e^{\frac{c-26}{24\pi}S_{\mathrm{anom}}[\varphi;\tilde{g}]-\Lambda A[e^{2\varphi}\tilde{g}]}{\mkern 2.0mu}.

(2.5)

In this path integral, only the physical length of the boundary,
\ell\equiv\int_{\partial M}\!\mathrm{vol}=\int_{\partial M}\!\mathrm{v\tilde{o}l}{\mkern 2.0mu}e^{\varphi}
, is fixed.
We discuss the boundary conditions in more detail below.
Since
Z_{\text{grav}}^{M}
is independent of the choice of fiducial background
\tilde{g}
, it follows that Liouville theory is itself a CFT with central charge
c_{\mathrm{L}}
such that

c_{\mathrm{L}}+c-26=0{\mkern 2.0mu}.

(2.6)

The path integral measure,
\mathcal{D}\varphi
, for the Liouville field is background independent, diffeomorphism invariant and ultralocal.
As such, it can be taken as proportional to the volume form associated to

\|\delta\varphi\|_{g}^{2}=\int_{\mathrlap{M}}\ \mathrm{vol}{\mkern 2.0mu}(\delta\varphi)^{2}=\int_{\mathrlap{M}}\ \mathrm{v\tilde{o}l}{\mkern 2.0mu}e^{2\varphi}(\delta\varphi)^{2}{\mkern 2.0mu}.

(2.7)

The relation between this field space metric and the continuum limit of a matrix model is discussed in
[
32
]
.
The above metric is not flat; it depends on the field value
\varphi
when written in terms of the fiducial background
\tilde{g}
on which the Liouville theory is defined.
This leads to complications, e.g. in the semiclassical expansion beyond the one-loop order, but also has its virtues, as we will see.
For instance, at the formal level it follows immediately that Liouville theory satisfies (
2.4
) with central charge
c_{\mathrm{L}}=26-c
from the above measure and

S_{\mathrm{anom}}[\varphi;e^{2\omega}\tilde{g}]+S_{\mathrm{anom}}[\omega;\tilde{g}]=S_{\mathrm{anom}}[\varphi+\omega;\tilde{g}]{\mkern 2.0mu}.

(2.8)

The latter is a consistency relation that follows from (
2.4
) and can be verified using the explicit form of the anomaly action.

The above shows that, in contrast to many familiar CFTs, the classical action of Liouville theory is not Weyl invariant but contributes to the conformal anomaly.
Moreover, in the above formulation, the conformal anomaly is completely captured by the classical action with no contribution from the measure.
Note also that, although conformally invariant,

(2.9)

Here, we have focused on the timelike flavour of the theory.
The action for spacelike Liouville theory is recovered by
\phi\to\pm i\phi
,

In (

The parameters

q=\beta^{-1}-\beta{\mkern 2.0mu},\qquad c_{\mathrm{L}}=1-6q^{2}{\mkern 2.0mu}.

(2.10)

In this formulation, the conformal anomaly receives contributions both from the action and the measure.
Note that, for real

can take any real value and
c_{\mathrm{L}}\leq 1
.
In the spacelike theory,
c_{\mathrm{L}}=1+6Q^{2}

Both formulations above are useful and we will distinguish them by using

Similarly, abusing notation, the vertex operator

2.2
Wavefunctions of the Liouville universe

Having introduced the model, we turn to the object of interest: the wavefunction of the universe.
Adapting the proposal by Hartle and Hawking
[
21
]
, we are prompted to consider a gravitational path integral over metrics on a manifold with a single
S^{1}
boundary.
We may generalize slightly by exciting the matter sector through an insertion.
More concretely, after fixing the Weyl gauge, we are led to consider the following class of wavefunctions:

\Psi^{\text{grav}}_{O}=\biggl\langle\int_{\mathrlap{D}}\ \mathrm{v\tilde{o}l}{\mkern 2.0mu}V_{\alpha}O\biggr\rangle_{\!\text{grav}}{\mkern 2.0mu},

(2.11)

since we fix the topology to that of a disk,
D
.
Here, the expectation value is evaluated in the full gravity theory,
O
is a scalar primary of the matter CFT and
V_{\alpha}=e^{2\alpha\phi}
a Liouville vertex operator such that

\Delta_{\alpha}+\Delta_{O}=1,

(2.12)

to ensure Weyl invariance.
2

2

Under a Weyl transformation
\tilde{g}\mapsto e^{2\omega}\tilde{g}
, a primary transforms as
O\mapsto e^{-2\Delta\omega}O
, following the notation of e.g.
[
22
]
.
Hence,
\Delta

This integrated one-point function should be interpreted as the wavefunction of the state created by the insertion in the basis provided by the boundary conditions.
Such an interpretation is customary for so called ‘physical states’ with
\alpha=Q/2+iP
for real

[

]

The physical states of timelike Liouville theory have
\alpha=-q/2+P

, such that
\Delta_{\alpha}=-q^{2}/4+P^{2}

13
]

In the gravity theory, on the other hand, we are led to consider

\alpha=-\frac{q}{2}\pm\sqrt{\frac{q^{2}}{4}+1-\Delta_{O}}{\mkern 2.0mu},

(2.13)

since
\Delta_{\alpha}=\alpha(q+\alpha)

[

For
\Delta_{O}\leq q^{2}/4+1

\Delta_{O}\geq 0
).
However, the gravity theory also allows for
\alpha=-q/2+ip
, with

, when
\Delta_{O}>q^{2}/4+1
.

To be interpreted as a wavefunction, the one-point function in (
2.11
) should not be normalized.
That is, it is given simply by a path integral with an insertion,
not
divided by the zero-point function
Z_{\text{grav}}
.
In Liouville theory, this is typically how one-point functions are defined but in the matter CFT sector, one should multiply the normalized one-point function by the disk partition function.

For the theory to factorize as in (
2.5
), it is important that the boundary conditions of the matter CFT are conformal.
Hence, conformal boundary conditions should be used for the Liouville sector as well.
As will be explained in §
3.3
, this does not allow for Dirichlet boundary conditions for
\varphi
.
Instead, one can fix the physical length of the boundary,
\ell=\int_{\partial D}\!\mathrm{v\tilde{o}l}{\mkern 2.0mu}e^{\varphi}
.
This respects conformal invariance since the physical metric
g=e^{2\varphi}\tilde{g}
is Weyl invariant.
Alternatively, one can introduce a boundary cosmological constant
\Lambda_{\mathrm{b}}
, acting as a chemical potential for
\ell

[
22
]
.
We will see below that, similarly to how
\Lambda
(the chemical potential for area) fixes the curvature,
\Lambda_{\mathrm{b}}
fixes the extrinsic curvature of the boundary of the semiclassical geometry.

With conformal boundary conditions, the physical Hilbert space, and the wavefunction, factorizes into the matter CFT and Liouville sectors.
We will focus on the Liouville sector but first comment on the integral in (
2.11
).
Choosing a flat fiducial background of radius
\tilde{L}
and using a complex coordinate with
\mathopen{|}z\mathclose{|}\leq 1

\mathrm{d}\tilde{s}^{2}=\tilde{L}^{2}{\mkern 2.0mu}\mathrm{d}\bar{z}{\mkern 2.0mu}\mathrm{d}z{\mkern 2.0mu},\qquad\quad\langle O(z)\rangle\propto(1-\bar{z}z)^{-2\Delta_{O}}{\mkern 2.0mu}.

(2.14)

Hence, the integral in (
2.11

\int\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}z}{(1-\bar{z}z)^{2}}\propto\operatorname{Vol}(\mathbb{H}^{2}){\mkern 2.0mu},

(2.15)

which diverges due to the contribution close to the boundary.
However, there are zero modes in the ghost sector associated to the conformal group of the background,
\operatorname{Conf}(D)=\mathrm{PSL}(2,\mathbb{R})

As we will see in §
3.3

\mathrm{PSL}(2,\mathbb{R})
also span

Any remaining finite factor that can arise from differently normalized measures depends only on
\tilde{L}
and the UV scale needed to define ultralocal path integral measures.
This dependence is captured by the
\tilde{L}^{2}
from
\mathrm{v\tilde{o}l}
and the conformal anomaly of the ghost sector.
We will not be concerned with the remaining finite normalization.

The wavefunction of the full theory takes the form

\ket{\Psi^{\text{grav}}_{O}}=\ket{\Psi_{\alpha}}\otimes\ket{O}\otimes\ket{\text{ghost}}{\mkern 2.0mu},

(2.16)

Going forward, we focus on the Liouville sector and the associated wavefunction

If we fix the physical length of the boundary,

\langle V_{\alpha}(z_{0})\rangle_{\ell}=\frac{\Psi_{\alpha}(\ell)}{(1-\bar{z}_{0}z_{0})^{2\Delta_{\alpha}}}{\mkern 2.0mu},

(2.17)

where the expectation value now only refers to the Liouville sector and

is a point in the interior of the disk.
This completely captures the dependence of
\Psi^{\text{grav}}_{O}(\ell)
on

and

Even though the dependence of the one-point function on

is fixed by conformal invariance, we will use it as a consistency check of the semiclassical computation.

The wavefunctions
\Psi_{\alpha}(\ell)

9
]

Motivated by the functional Wheeler–DeWitt (WDW) equation, upon gauge fixing the semiclassical field
\varphi(\theta)=\varphi_{0}

\biggl[\frac{\beta^{2}}{2}\biggl(\ell\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\ell}\biggr)^{2}+\frac{\Lambda}{2\pi}\ell^{2}-\frac{1}{2}(q+2\alpha)^{2}\biggr]\Psi_{\alpha}=0\quad\implies\quad\Psi_{\alpha}^{\pm}=J_{\pm(q+2\alpha)/\beta}\Bigl(\sqrt{\tfrac{\Lambda}{\pi\beta^{2}}}{\mkern 2.0mu}\ell\Bigr){\mkern 2.0mu},

(2.18)

here expressed in terms of the boundary length
\ell=2\pi e^{\varphi_{0}}
.
Although not derived rigorously, it is expected that this WDW equation is correct in the semiclassical limit
\beta\to 0
since this suppresses quantum fluctuations of the conformal mode
\varphi=\beta\phi
.

The sign of the first term is the Lorentzian version of the conformal mode problem and is responsible for the oscillatory behaviour of the wavefunction at large
\ell

When
(q+2\alpha)/\beta
is an integer, the two solutions above are linearly dependent and one can use
Y_{(q+2\alpha)/\beta}
as the second solution.
It was stressed in
[
9
]

When the matter fields are highly excited, such that

3

We now turn to the semiclassical one-loop analysis of the Liouville one-point functions, related to the wavefunction by (

We will consider both ‘light’ operators that do not backreact and ‘heavy’ ones that do.

3.3
Conformal structure of the one-point functions

Having discussed the saddle points, we turn to the conformal structure of the one-point functions.
This comes about in slightly different ways depending on whether the insertion is light or heavy.
In the former case, it is related to zero modes while in the latter it comes from how the saddle point depends on
z_{0}
.
In both cases, it has to do with the conformal group of the disk,
\mathrm{PSL}(2,\mathbb{R})
(see Appendix
A
for a brief review).

A zero-mode analysis similar to the one below can be found in
[
22
]
.
Here, we refine that analysis slightly by integrating only over the moduli space
\mathrm{PSL}(2,\mathbb{R})/\mathrm{U}(1)
of saddles instead of the full
\mathrm{PSL}(2,\mathbb{R})
.
The distinction is important to be able normalize the measure by comparing with the normalization of the infinitesimal zero modes (
3.8
), as well as to keep track of finite factors that will play a role for the consistency check of the results.
In addition, we perform the analysis on the disk instead of the upper half-plane.

Liouville theory is invariant under conformal transformations since the anomaly action
S_{\mathrm{anom}}[\omega;\tilde{g}]
vanishes for

such that

is related to

by a diffeomorphism.
Explicitly, with
\mathrm{d}\tilde{s}^{2}=\Omega(\bar{z},z)\mathrm{d}\bar{z}{\mkern 2.0mu}\mathrm{d}z
, a conformal transformation
z\mapsto z^{\prime}=g(z)
maps

10

10

Note that these conventions yield a left-action since

\varphi\mapsto g\cdot\varphi=\varphi^{\prime}+\omega_{g}{\mkern 2.0mu},\qquad\varphi^{\prime}=\varphi\circ g^{-1}{\mkern 2.0mu},\quad\omega_{g}=\log\biggl(\mathopen{|}\partial g^{-1}\mathclose{|}\frac{\Omega\circ g^{-1}}{\Omega}\biggr){\mkern 2.0mu},

where, by abuse of notation, we use

At the infinitesimal level, a diffeomorphism

with vanishing normal component at the boundary, and
\delta\tilde{g}_{\mu\nu}=-2\tilde{\nabla}_{(\mu}\xi_{\nu)}

For there to be a corresponding conformal transformation,

This implies that

the covariant derivative associated to

Note that only nonisometric conformal Killing vectors of the semiclassical background correspond to zero modes since Killing vectors, associated to isometries, have
\nabla_{\!\ast\mu}\xi^{\mu}=0
.
It is of course expected that there is a zero mode for every generator of the conformal group that does not leave the saddle point
\varphi_{\ast}
invariant, analogous to the phenomenon of spontaneous symmetry breaking.

A short calculation shows that, in two dimensions,
(-\square-R)\nabla_{\mu}\xi^{\mu}=0
for any conformal Killing vector, consistent with them corresponding to zero modes on compact manifolds without boundary.
When there is a boundary, we see that they satisfy the bulk condition in (

Generically, they are nonvanishing on the boundary, which explains why Dirichlet boundary conditions break conformal invariance.
The only exception is when
(-\square-R)\delta\varphi=0
admits a nonvanishing solution with homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions, in which case our decomposition into bulk and boundary modes breaks down.
As we explain below, this only happens on the hemisphere.

Light insertion

Due to the conformal invariance,
g\cdot\varphi_{\ast}

\varphi_{\ast}
the reference solution (

The zero modes are, thus, dealt with by introducing collective coordinates and integrating over the moduli space of saddles

Since the on-shell action is invariant, it need not be included in the integral and we focus on

The measure on the coset space is obtained by disintegrating a bi-invariant Haar measure on
\mathrm{PSL}(2,\mathbb{R})

Its normalisation is fixed by demanding that it agrees with the path integral measure for the fluctuations when evaluated at the identity or, equivalently, can be chosen arbitrarily as long as the corresponding Jacobian is included.
Note that the moduli space is noncompact.
Hence, without an insertion, the integral would diverge.

The integrand can be simplified by using that the measure is left-invariant and judiciously choosing a group element to act with.
A general element of

Using the left-invariance of the measure, the integral is

\displaystyle I

\displaystyle=\int\ \mathrm{d}\mu[g\mathrm{U}(1)]{\mkern 2.0mu}e^{2a(g_{0}\cdot g\cdot\varphi_{\ast})(z_{0})}=\biggl(\frac{2\gamma L/\tilde{L}}{1-\bar{z}_{0}z_{0}}\biggr)^{2a}\int\ \frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}w}{(1-\bar{w}w)^{2}}\biggl(\frac{1-\bar{w}w}{1+\eta\gamma^{2}\bar{w}w}\biggr)^{2a}

Quantum fluctuations

Having analysed the saddle points of the one-point function

Recall that these are split into boundary fluctuations

Boundary fluctuations of spacelike Liouville theory have been studied before in

Here, we generalize

We find that, although the number of negative modes is independent of

In addition, we calculate the zero-mode Jacobian in case of a light vertex operator by matching the fluctuations induced by conformal transformations, discussed in §

A similar analysis for timelike Liouville theory on the sphere can be found in
[

The bulk fluctuation determinant was recently computed in

It turns out that there is always one negative mode in total but whether it is a bulk or boundary mode depends on whether the spherical cap is large or small.

Summary of negative and zero modes

We have seen that, in the spacelike theory, there is one negative boundary mode for every

We compare the result to the Bessel functions after writing the one-loop result for a light insertion.

In the semiclassical path integral, there is an additional factor

(5.19)

At one loop, this creates a mixing between

\int\frac{\ell{\mkern 2.0mu}\mathrm{d}\delta\Lambda_{\mathrm{b}}}{2\pi i}e^{\frac{6\pi}{c_{\mathrm{L}}}\ell\lambda_{\mathrm{b},0}^{-1}\delta\Lambda_{\mathrm{b}}^{2}}=\sqrt{\frac{-c_{\mathrm{L}}}{\pm 12\pi\sqrt{1-\ell^{\prime{\mkern 2.0mu}2}}}}{\mkern 2.0mu},

As mentioned above, the standard integration contour for

In the timelike theory, the integral converges on that contour only when the saddle is a small spherical cap (

Around a small spherical cap, the expression in (

\pm i

\delta\varphi_{\mathrm{b},0}

Using a measure
\mathrm{d}\mu(x)

Note that

\pm i

If we were to, in addition, fix the orientation of the

The positive orientation result in a negative mode contributing

That the phases are opposite is related to the fact that negative modes have to be rotated back from an imaginary to a real contour in the timelike case.

Putting the above together, the one-loop result for the wavefunction in timelike Liouville is

\Psi_{a}(\ell)=-(\pm i)^{(3\mp 1)/2}\frac{\mathopen{|}c_{\mathrm{L}}\mathclose{|}^{3/2}}{\sqrt{12\pi}}B(\gamma_{\ell})\frac{(\Lambda_{\mathrm{UV}}\tilde{L})^{\frac{7}{6}}}{2a-1}\biggl(\frac{2L}{\tilde{L}}\gamma_{\ell}\biggr)^{-\frac{c_{\mathrm{L}}}{6}+\frac{7}{6}+2a}\frac{e^{\frac{c_{\mathrm{L}}}{6}\bigl(1\mp\sqrt{1-\ell^{\prime{\mkern 2.0mu}2}}\bigr)}}{(1-\ell^{\prime{\mkern 2.0mu}2})^{1/4}}[1+\mathcal{O}(1/c_{\mathrm{L}})]{\mkern 2.0mu},

\pm i

Both of these saddles give rise to a wavefunction that solves the WDW equation.
Note that we focus on the regime

When

5.3
Fixed area and boundary length

Fixing not only the boundary length but also the area of the bulk, the one-point functions are related by

\langle V_{a}\rangle_{\Lambda,\ell}=\int_{\mathcal{C}_{A}}\frac{\mathrm{d}A}{A}e^{-\Lambda A}\langle V_{a}\rangle_{A,\ell}{\mkern 2.0mu},

(5.28)

or

\langle V_{a}\rangle_{A,\ell}=\int\mathcal{D}\varphi{\mkern 2.0mu}e^{-S_{\mathrm{L}}}\delta(1-\hat{A}/A)\delta(1-\hat{\ell}/\ell)V_{a}=\int_{\mathcal{C}_{\Lambda}}\frac{A{\mkern 2.0mu}\mathrm{d}\Lambda}{2\pi i}\int_{\mathcal{C}_{\Lambda_{\mathrm{b}}}}\!\frac{\ell{\mkern 2.0mu}\mathrm{d}\Lambda_{\mathrm{b}}}{2\pi i}{\mkern 2.0mu}e^{\Lambda A+\Lambda_{\mathrm{b}}\ell}\langle V_{a}\rangle_{\Lambda,\Lambda_{\mathrm{b}}}{\mkern 2.0mu}.

(5.29)

Below, we present first the semiclassical analysis and then discuss the expected exact result and integration contours.

Semiclassical analysis

At the saddle point level, the Legendre transform simply removes the
\Lambda A
and
\Lambda_{\mathrm{b}}\ell
terms in (
3.18
) and the result is

\Psi_{a}^{(A)}(\ell)=(\Lambda_{\mathrm{UV}}\tilde{L})^{\frac{c_{\mathrm{L}}}{12}k^{2}}\biggl(\frac{2(1+k)A}{\tilde{L}\ell}\biggr)^{-\frac{c_{\mathrm{L}}}{6}(1+k)}e^{\frac{c_{\mathrm{L}}}{6}\bigl(1+k-\frac{\ell^{2}}{4\pi A}\bigr)+\mathcal{O}(\log c_{\mathrm{L}})}{\mkern 2.0mu},

(5.30)

where we use a superscript to indicate that the area is fixed and have, again, stripped off the kinematic dependence on the insertion point to extract the wavefunction, as in (
5.1
).
Note that, in contrast to §
5.2
, there is only one real saddle geometry.
The power of
\tilde{L}
is the same as in §
5.1
and hence consistent with conformal invariance.

At the one-loop level, in addition to (
5.19
), there is now an additional factor

\exp\biggl[-\delta\Lambda\int_{\mathrlap{D}}\ \mathrm{vol}_{\ast}(e^{2\delta\varphi}-1)\biggr]{\mkern 2.0mu},

(5.31)

in the semiclassical path integral.
Unmixing
\delta\Lambda
and
\delta\varphi
at the quadratic level now requires a constant shift of
\delta\varphi
in the bulk.
This shift is best performed before splitting
\delta\varphi
into bulk and boundary pieces since, otherwise, it would disrupt the homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions of the bulk fluctuations.
The bulk action is unmixed by

\delta\varphi\quad\longrightarrow\quad\delta\varphi+\frac{24\pi}{c_{\mathrm{L}}}R_{\ast}^{-1}\delta\Lambda{\mkern 2.0mu},

(5.32)

which produces a term
\delta\Lambda^{2}
and mixed boundary terms
\delta\Lambda{\mkern 2.0mu}\delta\Lambda_{\mathrm{b}}
and
\delta\Lambda{\mkern 2.0mu}\delta\varphi_{\mathrm{b}}
.
Because of this, after splitting into bulk and boundary fluctuations, the shift of
\delta\varphi_{\mathrm{b}}
is modified to (c.f. (
5.21
))

\delta\varphi_{\mathrm{b}}\quad\longrightarrow\quad\delta\varphi_{\mathrm{b}}-\frac{12\pi}{c_{\mathrm{L}}}\lambda_{\mathrm{b},0}^{-1}\biggl(\delta\Lambda_{\mathrm{b}}-\frac{2K_{\ast}}{R_{\ast}}\delta\Lambda\biggr){\mkern 2.0mu}.

(5.33)

The shifts leave the quadratic action for
\delta\varphi
invariant, so the analysis in §
4
again goes through, but produces an additional factor

\int\frac{A{\mkern 2.0mu}\mathrm{d}\delta\Lambda}{2\pi i}\int\frac{\ell{\mkern 2.0mu}\mathrm{d}\delta\Lambda_{\mathrm{b}}}{2\pi i}{\mkern 2.0mu}e^{-\frac{24\pi}{c_{\mathrm{L}}}\Bigl[\bigl(\frac{A}{R_{\ast}}-\frac{K_{\ast}\ell}{R_{\ast}^{2}}-\frac{K_{\ast}^{2}\ell}{R_{\ast}^{2}}\lambda_{\mathrm{b},0}^{-1}\bigr)\delta\Lambda^{2}+\bigl(\frac{\ell}{R_{\ast}}+\frac{K_{\ast}\ell}{R_{\ast}}\lambda_{\mathrm{b},0}^{-1}\bigr)\delta\Lambda_{\mathrm{b}}\delta\Lambda-\frac{\ell}{4}\lambda_{\mathrm{b},0}^{-1}\delta\Lambda_{\mathrm{b}}^{2}\Bigr]}=\pm i\frac{\mathopen{|}c_{\mathrm{L}}\mathclose{|}}{12\pi^{2}}{\mkern 2.0mu},

(5.34)

which, remarkably, is independent of
A
and
\ell
.
Here, we focus on a light insertion and have used (
4.5
) for
\lambda_{\mathrm{b},0}
.
Note that the quadratic form in the exponent is of indefinite signature, i.e. precisely one of the two integrals converge along the imaginary contour.
We have already seen in (
5.22
) that the integration contour of
\delta\Lambda_{\mathrm{b}}
has to be rotated when the saddle is a large spherical cap, but not when it is a small cap.
Hence, the opposite holds for the integration contour of
\delta\Lambda
.
Relatedly, as shown in §
4
, the negative mode is a boundary mode on the small cap but a bulk mode on the large cap.
The contour should be rotated to produce the same phase,
\pm i
, as a negative mode, as explained in §
5.2
, see in particular footnote
18

In this ensemble, including the factor discussed above, the result for a light insertion, to one-loop order, is

\Psi_{a}^{(A)}(\ell)=\frac{c_{\mathrm{L}}^{2}}{12\pi^{2}}B(\gamma_{A})\frac{(\Lambda_{\mathrm{UV}}\tilde{L})^{\frac{7}{6}}}{2a-1}\biggl(\frac{2A}{\tilde{L}\ell}\biggr)^{-\frac{c_{\mathrm{L}}}{6}+\frac{7}{6}+2a}e^{\frac{c_{\mathrm{L}}}{6}\bigl(1-\frac{\ell^{2}}{4\pi A}\bigr)}[1+\mathcal{O}(1/c_{\mathrm{L}})]{\mkern 2.0mu},

(5.35)

where
B
is the scheme-dependent factor in (
5.9
) and

\gamma_{A}\equiv\sqrt{\frac{4\pi A}{\ell^{2}}-1}{\mkern 2.0mu},

(5.36)

for a light insertion in the timelike theory.
Note that
\Psi_{a}^{(A)}
is real and positive for

since the phases from the negative mode and Laplace transforms cancel those from the zero modes.

Exact expectation

We expect that the dependence on
\ell

\Psi_{\alpha}^{(A)}(\ell)\propto(2A/\ell)^{(q+2\alpha)/\beta}\exp\biggl[\frac{\ell^{2}}{4\sin(\pi\beta^{2})A}\biggr]{\mkern 2.0mu}.

(5.37)

This result is obtained by analytic continuation of the corresponding formula in spacelike Liouville theory

Although such a continuation is in general not valid

To compare this with the semiclassical result, note that it follows from (

Explicitly, the counterterms produced by

In spacelike Liouville theory, the Laplace transform (

In the timelike theory, in contrast, there is a severe small-area divergence due to the sign of the argument of the exponential.

The contour

described above and

For the Hartle–Hawking-like wavefunction

6
Outlook

We have thoroughly and systematically analysed the disk path integral of timelike Liouville theory.
At one loop we have obtained the expression (

Further to this, we have provided a variety of arguments indicating that the all-loop expression should be given by

\Psi_{\alpha}(K)=\mp i\beta^{-2}\bigl(\Lambda_{\mathrm{UV}}\tilde{L}\bigr)^{\frac{c_{\mathrm{L}}}{6}-2\alpha(q+\alpha)}\bigl(2\Lambda_{\mathrm{UV}}L\gamma_{K}/e\bigr)^{(q+2\alpha)/\beta}{\mkern 2.0mu},

Here, we have dropped an

Apart from this rescaling and normalization, (

We note that although both spacelike and timelike Liouville theory on a disk without heavy insertions exhibit a phase in the fixed
K
ensemble, the origins of the phases differ.
In the former, it stems from a single universal negative boundary mode.
In the latter, it follows from the one-loop calculation upon rotating the Liouville mode to an appropriate complex contour and can be due to either a bulk or boundary mode.
The unbounded nature of real boundary fluctuations (
4.6
) in timelike Liouville theory is a boundary counterpart of the conformal mode problem of Euclidean gravity.

The gravitational disk path integrals can be related to solutions of the Wheeler–DeWitt equation of two-dimensional quantum gravity with
\Lambda>0
, coupled to a conformal field theory with large and positive central charge.
The full Wheeler–DeWitt wavefunction, with the matter in a conformal primary state
\ket{O_{\alpha}}

\Psi^{\text{grav}}_{\alpha}(K)=\Psi_{\alpha}(K)\otimes\ket{O_{\alpha}}\otimes\ket{\text{ghost}}{\mkern 2.0mu},

(6.2)

2.13

To label the gravitational wavefunction by
\alpha

The matter CFT Hilbert space has a positive definite norm, and the ghost vacuum
\ket{\text{ghost}}
is taken to have unit norm.
Generally, one can build superpositions of the above states.

It is worth emphasizing that many of the features of our wavefunctions bear strong resemblance to properties encountered in higher spacetime dimensions.
For example, there exist both Hartle–Hawking-like and Vilenkin-like states when
\Delta_{O_{\alpha}}
is not too large

The former has vanishing amplitude in the limit of small spatial size and oscillates in a WKB-like fashion at large size, in line with Hartle and Hawking’s defining principle
[
21
]

In our setup, however, these states can be systematically explored to all loops, and perhaps even nonperturbatively, without resorting to a minisuperpsace approximation.
Indeed, in many ways the minisuperspace approximation becomes exact in the lower-dimensional theories we consider
[

What is less understood in the Wheeler–DeWitt literature is the gravitational inner product governing the

We now discuss some potentially interesting structures that have appeared along the way in our analysis that may shed some light on this.

6.2
Static patch perspective

A different perspective to the one we have taken throughout our discussion, is that the gravitational disk path integral, at least in the absence of any insertions, is computing a thermodynamic partition function for the Euclidean static patch of

At the boundary, we further imagine placing the matter CFT theory in some Cardy state.
This follows from the fact that the two-sphere metric can also be viewed as the Euclidean continuation of the static patch metric, if we Wick rotate the static patch time coordinate.

Appendix A
Conformal transformations of the disk

Here, we briefly review the conformal transformations of the disk and derive the corresponding fluctuations of the Liouville field.
As is well known, using a complex coordinate
z
, a vector field
\vec{\xi}=\xi{\partial}+\bar{\xi}\bar{\partial}
is a
local
conformal Killing vector if
\bar{\partial}\xi=0
.
On the sphere,
\xi_{n}=z^{n+1}
gives a global conformal Killing vector only for
n\in\{0,\pm 1\}
with complex coefficients, since vector fields with smaller
n
are singular at the south pole
z=0
and those with greater
n
are singular at the north pole
z=\infty
.
This gives rise to the familiar six-dimensional conformal group
\mathrm{PSL}(2,\mathbb{C})
.

On the disk,
n>1
cannot be excluded in this way since the north pole is not part of the geometry.
However, since the vector field should generate a diffeomorphism, its normal component has to vanish at the boundary.
To satisfy this, one has to take a particular linear combination of
\xi_{n}
and
\xi_{-n}
, which renders the conformal group finite-dimensional since
n\geq-1
from regularity at the south pole.
Explicitly, the holomorphic components are

\xi_{0}=iz{\mkern 2.0mu},\qquad\xi_{\mathrm{R}}=\frac{-1+z^{2}}{2}{\mkern 2.0mu},\qquad\xi_{\mathrm{I}}=i\frac{1+z^{2}}{2}{\mkern 2.0mu}.

(A.1)

Only real linear combinations of these satisfy the constraint of not moving the boundary, so the conformal group of the disk is three-dimensional.
One can show that it is isomorphic to
\mathrm{PSL}(2,\mathbb{R})
by mapping the disk to the upper half-plane.
We have normalised the generators such that

[\vec{\xi}_{0},\vec{\xi}_{\mathrm{R}}]=\vec{\xi}_{\mathrm{I}}{\mkern 2.0mu},\qquad[\vec{\xi}_{0},\vec{\xi}_{\mathrm{I}}]=-\vec{\xi}_{\mathrm{R}}{\mkern 2.0mu},\qquad[\vec{\xi}_{\mathrm{R}},\vec{\xi}_{\mathrm{I}}]=-\vec{\xi}_{0}{\mkern 2.0mu}.

(A.2)

Although the conformal group of the disk is independent of the metric, since all metrics are related by Weyl transformations, we still need it to compute the corresponding fluctuations of the Liouville field,
\delta\varphi=-\frac{1}{2}\nabla_{\!\ast\mu}\xi^{\mu}
.
When the insertion is light, the semiclassical geometry is a hyperbolic or spherical cap

\mathrm{d}s_{\ast}^{2}=\frac{4\gamma^{2}L^{2}}{(1+\eta\gamma^{2}\bar{z}z)^{2}}{\mkern 2.0mu}\mathrm{d}\bar{z}{\mkern 2.0mu}\mathrm{d}z{\mkern 2.0mu},

(A.3)

without a conical singularity, here in coordinates
\mathopen{|}z\mathclose{|}\leq 1
.
A short calculation shows that

\nabla_{\!\ast\mu}\xi_{0}^{\mu}=0{\mkern 2.0mu},\qquad\nabla_{\!\ast\mu}\xi_{\mathrm{R}}^{\mu}=\frac{1+\eta\gamma^{2}}{1+\eta\gamma^{2}z\bar{z}}(z+\bar{z}){\mkern 2.0mu},\qquad\nabla_{\!\ast\mu}\xi_{\mathrm{I}}^{\mu}=i\frac{1+\eta\gamma^{2}}{1+\eta\gamma^{2}z\bar{z}}(z-\bar{z}){\mkern 2.0mu}.

(A.4)

These are indeed the two
\mathopen{|}n\mathclose{|}=1
zero modes (
4.3
) of §
4.1
with
k=0
.
We also see that the vector field in (
3.26
) is

Appendix B
Zeta function regularization of boundary determinant

Here, we compute the boundary fluctuation determinant in (
4.6
) by zeta function regularization for generic
k
, such that there are no zero modes.
In this scheme,

D_{\mathrm{b}}(k)\equiv\prod_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\frac{2\pi}{\Lambda_{\mathrm{UV}}\ell}\frac{n^{2}-(1+k)^{2}}{\mathopen{|}n\mathclose{|}+u-(1+k)}=\biggl(\frac{\Lambda_{\mathrm{UV}}\ell}{2\pi}\biggr)^{-\zeta_{\mathrm{b}}(0)}e^{-\zeta_{\mathrm{b}}^{\prime}(0)}{\mkern 2.0mu},

(B.1)

where we have defined
u\equiv\ell^{2}/(2\pi A)
,

\zeta_{\mathrm{b}}(s)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\biggl(\frac{n^{2}-(1+k)^{2}}{\mathopen{|}n\mathclose{|}+u-(1+k)}\biggr)^{-s}{\mkern 2.0mu},

(B.2)

for sufficiently large
\operatorname{Re}(s)
such that the sum converges, and
\zeta_{\mathrm{b}}
is defined in a neighbourhood of
s=0
through analytic continuation.

It is useful to define
\tilde{\zeta}_{\mathrm{b}}
by

\zeta_{\mathrm{b}}(s)=\biggl(\frac{-(1+k)^{2}}{u-(1+k)}\biggr)^{-s}+2\tilde{\zeta}_{\mathrm{b}}(s){\mkern 2.0mu},\qquad\quad\tilde{\zeta}_{b}(s)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\biggl(\frac{(n+1+k)(n-1-k)}{n+u-(1+k)}\biggr)^{-s}{\mkern 2.0mu}.

(B.3)

The latter may be computed, again for sufficiently large
\operatorname{Re}(s)
, as

\displaystyle\tilde{\zeta}_{\mathrm{b}}(s)

\displaystyle=R_{\mathrm{b}}(s)+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(n+1+k)^{-s}+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(n-1-k)^{-s}-\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(n+u-1-k)^{-s}

(B.4)

\displaystyle=R_{\mathrm{b}}(s)+\zeta(s,2+k)+\zeta(s,-k)-\zeta(s,u-k){\mkern 2.0mu},

where
\zeta(s,a)
is the Hurwitz zeta function and the residue term is

R_{\mathrm{b}}(s)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\biggl[\biggl(\frac{(n+1+k)(n-1-k)}{n+u-(1+k)}\biggr)^{-s}-(n+1+k)^{-s}-(n-1-k)^{-s}+(n+u-1-k)^{-s}\biggr]{\mkern 2.0mu},

(B.5)

The point of this rewriting is that the terms in
R_{\mathrm{b}}(s)
scale as
\mathcal{O}(n^{-s-2})
for large
n
and, hence, the sum converges absolutely for
\operatorname{Re}(s)>-1
.
The expansion in large
n
is uniform on compact regions of
\mathbb{C}\ni s
for fixed
u
and
k
.
Since we are only interested in the value and first derivative of
R
at
s=0
, we may compute these by differentiating and evaluating before summing.
This shows that
R_{\mathrm{b}}(0)=R^{\prime}(0)=0
, i.e. there is no multiplicative anomaly.
Hence,

\biggl(\frac{\Lambda_{\mathrm{UV}}\ell}{2\pi}\biggr)^{-\tilde{\zeta}_{\mathrm{b}}(0)}e^{-\tilde{\zeta}_{\mathrm{b}}^{\prime}(0)}=\sqrt{2\pi}\biggl(\frac{\Lambda_{\mathrm{UV}}\ell}{2\pi}\biggr)^{-(u-k-3/2)}\frac{\Gamma(u-k)}{\Gamma(-k)\Gamma(2+k)}{\mkern 2.0mu},

(B.6)

which is consistent with the result in the Appendix of
[
19
]
.
As a further consistency check, it is straightforward to verify that

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial u^{2}}\log\frac{n^{2}-(1+k)^{2}}{n+u-(1+k)}=-\frac{\partial^{2}\tilde{\zeta}_{\mathrm{b}}^{\prime}(0)}{\partial u^{2}}{\mkern 2.0mu},

(B.7)

where the right-hand side converges without regularization due to the
u
-derivatives.

Accounting for the single
n=0
mode and the two-fold degeneracy of the
\mathopen{|}n\mathclose{|}\geq 1
eigenvalues in
\zeta_{\mathrm{b}}
, c.f. (
B.3
), we get the result (
4.7
)

D_{\mathrm{b}}(k)=-\frac{2\pi}{(1+k)^{2}}\biggl(\frac{\Lambda_{\mathrm{UV}}\ell}{2\pi}\biggr)^{-2[u-(1+k)]}\bigl(u-(1+k)\bigr)\frac{\Gamma\bigl(u-(1+k)\bigr)^{2}}{\Gamma(1+k)^{2}\Gamma(-1-k)^{2}}{\mkern 2.0mu}.

(B.8)

Appendix C
Hemisphere bulk determinant

As a consistency check, we verify the result (
4.19
) of
[
44
]
on the hemisphere.
The result in (
4.19
) vanishes there due to the breakdown of the split into bulk and boundary fluctuations, as discussed in §
4
.
Here, we lift the zero mode by varying either the position of the boundary
\gamma
or the mass of the fluctuation, remove it and then take limits to recover the hemisphere and
m^{2}=-2/L^{2}
.

Lifting the zero mode by shifting the boundary

We begin by deriving the hemisphere determinant with the zero mode removed by shifting the boundary, starting from (
4.19
).
Recall that the zero mode is the eigenmode

f(\rho)=\sqrt{3}\frac{1-\rho^{2}}{1+\rho^{2}}{\mkern 2.0mu},

(C.1)

of the Sturm–Liouville problem (
4.15
).

22

22

Since the mass term was abosrbed into the eignevalue in the Sturm–Liouville problem, this mode has
\lambda=2
.

This eigenmode comes from (
4.3
) with
n=0
, here normalized such that
\int_{0}^{\gamma}\mathrm{d}\rho{\mkern 2.0mu}wf^{2}=1
.
It manifests as a term
\log(1-\gamma)
when expanding
\log D_{\mathrm{B}}
around
\gamma=1
.

By taking a derivative of the Sturm–Liouville problem with respect to the location
\gamma
of the boundary and integrating the result against
f
, it is straightforward to show that

\partial_{\gamma}\lambda=-p(\gamma)f^{\prime}(\gamma)^{2}=-3{\mkern 2.0mu}.

(C.2)

Hence, the eignevalue entering in the bulk determinant is
\lambda_{\mathrm{B},0}=-3(\gamma-1)/L^{2}+\mathcal{O}(\gamma-1)^{2}
and

D^{\prime}_{\mathrm{B}}(\gamma=1)=\lim_{\gamma\to 1}\frac{\Lambda_{\mathrm{UV}}^{2}D_{\mathrm{B}}(\gamma)}{\lambda_{\mathrm{B},0}}=\frac{e^{\frac{9}{4}-2\zeta^{\prime}(-1)}}{3\sqrt{2\pi}}(\Lambda_{\mathrm{UV}}L)^{-1/3}{\mkern 2.0mu}.

(C.3)

Lifting the zero mode by shifting the mass

We will now verify (
C.3
) by computing the hemisphere determinant for a massive scalar of generic mass and then take the limit
m^{2}\to-2
, corresponding to the effective mass of the bulk fluctuation of the timelike Liouville field, see (
4.12
).
Here, for convenience, we denote by
m^{2}
the mass in units of the radius of the sphere, i.e. the physical mass is
m^{2}/L^{2}
.

Consider first the Klein–Gordon eigenvalue problem
(-\square+m^{2}/L^{2})\phi=\lambda_{\ast}\phi
on the full sphere.
Using coordinates (
3.10
) and decomposing
\phi=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\phi_{n}e^{in\theta}
, the solution (
4.17
) respecting regularity at the south pole can be rewritten as

\phi_{n}=\biggl(\frac{\rho}{1+\rho^{2}}\biggr)^{\mathopen{|}n\mathclose{|}}{{\mkern 2.0mu}{}_{2}F_{1}}\biggl(\Delta_{\lambda}+\mathopen{|}n\mathclose{|},1-\Delta_{\lambda}+\mathopen{|}n\mathclose{|};1+n;\frac{\rho^{2}}{1+\eta\rho^{2}}\biggr){\mkern 2.0mu},\qquad\Delta_{\lambda}=\frac{1}{2}+\sqrt{\frac{1}{4}+\lambda}{\mkern 2.0mu}.

(C.4)

Here,
\lambda
is related to the eigenvalue of the massive Klein–Gordon operator by
\lambda_{\ast}=(\lambda+m^{2})/L^{2}
.
Note that the prefactor is invariant under the reflection
\rho\to\rho^{-1}
across the equator and, hence, smooth both at the south pole (
\rho=0
) and the north pole (
\rho=\infty
).
The full fluctuation, including the hypergeometric function, is regular at the north pole if
1-\Delta_{\lambda}+\mathopen{|}n\mathclose{|}=-\tilde{n}
for a nonnegative integer
\tilde{n}
.
This can be understood from the fact that spherical harmonics can be constructed by restricting homogeneous polynomials in embedding space to the sphere.
It follows that the eigenvalues are the well-known
\lambda=l(l+1)
, with
l=\mathopen{|}n\mathclose{|}+\tilde{n}
, with corresponding multiplicities
2l+1
.

The homogeneous Dirichlet problem on the hemisphere can be solved by noting that one may extend to the full sphere by reflecting across the equator by
\phi(\rho,\theta)=-\phi(\rho^{-1},\theta)
.
Hence, the eigenmodes on the hemisphere are the restrictions of the odd eigenmodes on the full sphere.
The expression in (
C.4
) is odd under this reflection for odd
\tilde{n}=l-\mathopen{|}n\mathclose{|}
and even otherwise.
Hence, the eigenvalues are the same as on the full sphere,
\lambda=l(l+1)
, but the corresponding multiplicities are
l
instead of
2l+1
.

To be able to compare to (
C.3
), we have to use the same scheme as in
[
44
]
since the normalization of the determinant is scheme dependent.
Hence, we employ zeta function regularization, as in Appendix
B
.
The eigenvalues can conveniently be written as
\lambda_{\ast}=[l(l+1)+m^{2}]/L^{2}=(l+\Delta)(l+1-\Delta)/L^{2}
, where we parametrize the mass by
m^{2}=\Delta(1-\Delta)
.
Hence, the zeta-regularized hemisphere determinant is

D_{\mathrm{B}}(m^{2})=\prod_{l\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}}\biggl(\frac{(l+\Delta)(l+1-\Delta)}{\Lambda_{\mathrm{UV}}^{2}L^{2}}\biggr)^{l}=(\Lambda_{\mathrm{UV}}^{2}L^{2})^{-\zeta_{\mathrm{B}}(0)}e^{-\zeta_{\mathrm{B}}^{\prime}(0)}{\mkern 2.0mu},

(C.5)

where

\displaystyle\zeta_{\mathrm{B}}(s)=\sum_{l=1}^{\infty}l{\mkern 2.0mu}[(l+\Delta)(l+1-\Delta)]^{-s}=R_{\mathrm{B}}(s)+\zeta_{\Delta}(s)+\zeta_{1-\Delta}(s){\mkern 2.0mu},

(C.6a)

\displaystyle\zeta_{\Delta}(s)=\sum_{l=1}^{\infty}(l+\Delta)^{-s}=\zeta(s-1,1+\Delta)-\Delta\zeta(s,1+\Delta){\mkern 2.0mu},

(C.6b)

\displaystyle R_{\mathrm{B}}(s)=\sum_{l=1}^{\infty}r_{l}(s){\mkern 2.0mu},\qquad r_{l}=l{\mkern 2.0mu}\bigl[\bigl((l+\Delta)(l+1-\Delta)\bigr)^{-s}-(l+\Delta)^{-s}-(l+1-\Delta)^{-s}\bigr]{\mkern 2.0mu}.

(C.6c)

In contrast to Appendix
B
, the residue term does not converge in a neighbourhood of
s=0
.
However, it can be written as

R_{\mathrm{B}}(s)=\sum_{l=1}^{\infty}[r_{l}(s)-a_{l}(s)]+\sum_{l=1}^{\infty}a_{l}(s){\mkern 2.0mu},

(C.7)

where
a_{l}
is constructed from the large
l
expansion of
r_{l}
,

a_{l}(s)=\frac{l^{-s}}{2}\bigl[-4l+2s-s(s+1)(1-2m^{2})l^{-1}+\mathcal{O}(l^{-2})\bigr]+\frac{l^{-2s}}{2}\bigl[2l-2s+s(s+1-m^{2})l^{-1}+\mathcal{O}(l^{-2})\bigr]{\mkern 2.0mu},

(C.8)

such that
r_{l}(s)-a_{l}(s)=\mathcal{O}(l^{-2-s})+\mathcal{O}(l^{-2-2s})
.
Importantly, this makes the first term on the right-hand side of (
C.7
) converge in a neighbourhood of
s=0
and, much like
R_{\mathrm{b}}
from Appendix
B
, it is
\mathcal{O}(s^{2})
and hence does not contribute to the hemisphere determinant in (
C.5
).
Computing the last sum,

R_{\mathrm{B}}(s)=\sum_{l=1}^{\infty}a_{l}(s)+\mathcal{O}(s^{2})=\frac{3m^{2}-1}{6}+\frac{4m^{2}-1}{4}s+\mathcal{O}(s^{2}){\mkern 2.0mu}.

(C.9)

Putting the above pieces together, we get

D_{\mathrm{B}}(m^{2})=(\Lambda_{\mathrm{UV}}L)^{-\frac{1}{3}+m^{2}}2\pi\Gamma(1+\Delta)^{\Delta}\Gamma(2-\Delta)^{1-\Delta}e^{-\frac{3}{4}-2\zeta^{\prime}(-1)-\psi^{(-2)}(1+\Delta)-\psi^{(-2)}(2-\Delta)}{\mkern 2.0mu},

(C.10)

where
\psi^{(n)}(z)
is the polygamma function of order
n
.

Lastly, we take the limit
m^{2}\to-2
.
The lowest mode, with eigenvalue
\lambda_{\ast,0}=(m^{2}+2)/L^{2}
, becomes a zero mode in this limit, as expected.
Removing it, we find

D_{\mathrm{B}}^{\prime}(m^{2}=-2)=\lim_{m^{2}\to-2}\frac{\Lambda_{\mathrm{UV}}^{2}D_{\mathrm{B}}(m^{2})}{\lambda_{\ast,0}}=\frac{e^{\frac{9}{4}-2\zeta^{\prime}(-1)}}{3\sqrt{2\pi}}(\Lambda_{\mathrm{UV}}L)^{-1/3}{\mkern 2.0mu},

(C.11)

which agrees with (
C.3
) and concludes the consistency check of (
4.19
) on the hemisphere.
