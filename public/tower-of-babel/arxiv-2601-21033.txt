Predict-Project-Renoise: Sampling Diffusion Models under Hard Constraints
Omer Rochman-Sharabi, Gilles Louppe

Abstract
Neural emulators based on diffusion models show promise for scientific applications, but vanilla models cannot guarantee physical accuracy or constraint satisfaction. We address this by introducing a constrained sampling framework that enforces hard constraints, such as physical laws or observational consistency, at generation time. Our approach defines a constrained forward process that diffuses only over the feasible set of constraint-satisfying samples, inducing constrained marginal distributions. To reverse this, we propose Predict-Project-Renoise (PPR), an iterative algorithm that samples from the constrained marginals by alternating between denoising predictions, projecting onto the feasible set, and renoising. Experiments on 2D distributions, PDEs, and global weather forecasting demonstrate that PPR reduces constraint violations by over an order of magnitude while improving sample consistency and better matching the true constrained distribution compared to baselines.



1
Introduction

Diffusion models
(
37
;
15
;
41
)
have emerged as the state-of-the-art framework for deep generative modeling, showing strong performance in approximating high-dimensional data across modalities. These models learn to reverse a gradual corruption process, transporting a simple prior distribution to a complex data distribution
p
. However, in many scientific and engineering domains, such as medical imaging, fluid dynamics, or climate modeling, sampling
p
is insufficient. Instead, the samples must satisfy hard constraints, such as exact consistency with observations or governing equations, zero divergence in incompressible fluids, conservation laws, boundary and initial conditions, or geometric constraints.

Although training-based approaches can incorporate constraints
(
47
;
20
;
28
)
, they often lack flexibility, requiring retraining for different constraints, and enforce the constraints only softly. For this reason, some recent efforts focus on
training-free constrained sampling
, where a pre-trained unconditional model is guided to satisfy constraints during inference. Current methods typically rely on treating constraints as observations
(
9
)
or implement projection-based corrections
(
6
;
31
)
.

When treating the constraint as an observation, Bayesian decomposition can be used to express the posterior score as a prior score plus a likelihood score. While effective for noisy inverse problems
(
7
)
, especially in the context of images, these methods are ill-suited for hard constraints: the implicit likelihood approaches a Dirac delta, causing gradients to become unstable and informative only in the immediate vicinity of the feasible set. On the other hand, projection methods (Section
4
) can enforce feasibility explicitly but they come with both theoretical and practical pitfalls. The core problem is that the true constrained distribution induced by the constraints on the prior is not correctly sampled. This can happen for three reasons: (i) the constraints are not respected, (ii) the probability mass distribution is incorrect, and (iii) the sample lies outside of the support of the original distribution
p
.

These problems are due to a number of factors. For nonlinear constraints, the projection typically has no closed-form solution, so it must be approximated with an iterative solver at every step. Moreover, it is often unclear what exactly should be projected. Directly projecting the noisy state onto the constraint set can be ill-posed or numerically unstable, since many constraints are only meaningful for clean data. Projecting an estimate of the clean sample is not a clear fix either. Sample consistency with
p
is often broken, for example, by introducing discontinuities into otherwise continuous samples. Finally, repeated projection steps can systematically bias the sampler. In the extreme, this behaves like an attractor toward “nearest” feasible points, reducing diversity and causing mode collapse rather than sampling from the full constrained distribution.

Contributions.

In this work, we address these limitations by formalizing the constrained forward process and arguing that correct constrained sampling requires approximating the constrained marginal distributions
p_{t}^{\mathcal{C}}
induced by a forward diffusion SDE starting from the constrained set
\mathcal{C}
. We define the constrained forward process, derive the associated constrained marginals and backward process, and design the Predict-Project-Renoise (PPR) sampling algorithm that approximates the constrained marginals at every step. This approach achieves orders of magnitude lower constraint violations and better mass distribution while remaining consistent with
\mathcal{C}
.

Score-based generative modeling

Forward process.

The goal of generative modeling is to approximate the data distribution
p(x)
of
x\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
. In score-based diffusion
(
41
)
, this is done by learning to reverse a forward corruption process. Following
23
, we define the
forward corruption process
as the Stochastic Differential Equation (SDE)

\mathrm{d}x_{t}=f(x_{t},t)\mathrm{d}t+g(t)\mathrm{d}w_{t},

(1)

where
f:\mathbb{R}^{D+1}\to\mathbb{R}^{D}
is a drift term,
g:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is a scalar diffusion coefficient, and
w_{t}
is a standard Wiener process. Starting from
p_{0}=p
, this SDE defines a series of marginal densities
\{p_{t}(x_{t})\}_{t\in[0,T]}
, with
x_{t}\sim p_{t}(x_{t})
for all
t
, which interpolate between the data distribution and
p_{T}
. Choosing
f
and
g
defines the perturbation or
forward kernels

p(x_{t}|x_{0})
, inducing the marginals

p_{t}(x_{t})=\int p(x_{t}\mid x_{0})\,p(x_{0})\,\mathrm{d}x_{0}.

(2)

If
f
and
g
are chosen appropriately, then
p(x_{t}|x_{0})
and
p_{T}
become Gaussian
(
33
)
. In particular,
p_{T}
can become
\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{T}I)
. We also note that the forward kernels
p(x_{t}|x_{0})
depend only on
f
and
g
, and are independent of the data distribution
p
.

Backward process.

2
shows that given a forward process (Eq.
1
), there exists a reverse-time or
backward process
from
T
to
0
transporting
p_{T}
back to
p_{0}
given by

\mathrm{d}x_{t}=\left[f(x_{t},t)-g^{2}(t)\nabla_{x_{t}}\log p_{t}(x_{t})\right]\mathrm{d}t+g(t)\mathrm{d}\tilde{w}_{t},

(3)

with
x_{T}\sim p_{T}(x_{T})
and where
\tilde{w}_{t}
is the reverse-time Wiener process. Remarkably, in this reverse process, the marginal distributions of
x_{t}
are identical to those of the forward process. This allows for sampling from
p
by generating a sample from
p_{T}
and then simulating the reverse process. However, the score
s(x_{t})=\nabla_{x_{t}}\log p_{t}(x_{t})
is unknown because we do not know
p(x)
.

Training and sampling.

As the score is unknown, it has to be learned from data.
In this work, we follow
19
and parameterize the score using Tweedie’s formula
s_{\theta}(x_{t})=(d_{\theta}(x_{t})-x_{t})/\sigma_{t}^{2}

(
42
;
10
)
. The denoiser
d_{\theta}(x_{t})=\mathbb{E}[x_{0}|x_{t}]
predicts the posterior mean of the clean sample given the noisy input.
The resulting denoising objective minimizes

\mathbb{E}_{x_{0}\sim p(x_{0})}\mathbb{E}_{x_{t}\sim p(x_{t}|x_{0})}\|d_{\theta}(x_{t})-x_{0}\|^{2}.

(4)

Once the denoiser is available, a way to sample from the reverse process is to use the predictor-corrector (PC) sampler (
41
, Algorithm
1
). This algorithm alternates between a
Predict
step (e.g. DDPM
(
15
)
, DDIM
(
39
)
), which moves the samples from
p_{t}
to
p_{t-1}
and a number of
Correct
steps (e.g. Langevin steps based on the score), which corrects the marginals
p_{t-1}
. The
Predict
step provides an update along the reverse dynamics, but discretization error and imperfect scores can leave the resulting samples slightly off-distribution. The
Correct
step reduces this mismatch by pulling samples toward higher-density regions of
p_{t-1}
while re-injecting controlled noise to preserve diversity.

Method

The reverse process (Eq.
3
) allows us to sample (approximately) from the data distribution
p
.
However, in many domains, we are interested in imposing additional constraints on the samples. Let
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{D}
be the support of
p(x)
and
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathcal{S}
be a (constrained or feasible) set corresponding to samples with certain characteristics.
In practice,
\mathcal{C}
can be represented using the nonnegative constraint function
c(x)\geq 0
as

\mathcal{C}\coloneqq\{x\in\mathcal{S}:c(x)=0\}.

(5)

The constraint strictly restricts the prior to the feasible set, inducing the density

p^{\mathcal{C}}\propto p(x_{0})\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{C}}(x_{0}),

(6)

where
\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{C}}(x)
is the indicator function of
\mathcal{C}
. This is a key point: under hard constraints, the target distribution is simply the prior truncated to
\mathcal{C}
.

Constrained forward process.

We define the constrained forward process similarly to Eq.
1
, but with
x_{0}\sim p^{\mathcal{C}}(x_{0})
. This constrained process induces a new sequence of marginals
\{p_{t}^{\mathcal{C}}\}_{t\in[0,T]}
, given by

p_{t}^{\mathcal{C}}(x_{t})\propto\int p(x_{t}\mid x_{0})\,p(x_{0})\,\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{C}}(x_{0})\,\mathrm{d}x_{0}.

(7)

Our main insight is that the unconstrained and constrained forward processes share the same dynamics and transition kernels, while their different initial densities lead to distinct marginals and, consequently, a new reverse process.

Constrained backward process.

Mirroring the unconstrained backward process in Eq.
3
, the constrained reverse process is

\mathrm{d}x_{t}=\left[f(x_{t},t)-g^{2}(t)\nabla_{x_{t}}\log p_{t}^{\mathcal{C}}(x_{t})\right]\mathrm{d}t+g(t)\mathrm{d}\tilde{w}_{t}.

(8)

This process involves a different score function, which is unknown. It can be computed by taking the log and then the gradient of the constrained marginals (Eq.
7
)

\nabla_{x_{t}}\log\int_{\mathcal{C}}p(x_{t}|x_{0})p(x_{0})\mathrm{d}x_{0},

(9)

dropping the normalization constant because it vanishes under the derivative and absorbing the indicator function into the integral. Then, from Bayes’
p(x_{t}|x_{0})p(x_{0})=p(x_{0}|x_{t})p(x_{t})
, we get

\nabla_{x_{t}}\log p_{t}^{\mathcal{C}}(x_{t})=\nabla_{x_{t}}\log\int_{\mathcal{C}}p(x_{0}|x_{t})\mathrm{d}x_{0}+\nabla_{x_{t}}\log p_{t}(x_{t}).

(10)

The constrained score is thus the unconstrained score plus the gradient of the log-feasibility
\nabla_{x_{t}}\log\int_{\mathcal{C}}p(x_{0}|x_{t})\mathrm{d}x_{0}=\nabla_{x_{t}}\log\mathbb{P}(x_{0}\in\mathcal{C}|x_{t})
, a term pointing in the direction (in
x_{t}
-space) of increasing probability of
x_{0}
being in
\mathcal{C}
.

Predict-Project-Renoise (PPR) sampling.

The prior score
\nabla_{x_{t}}\log p_{t}(x_{t})
is available from the unconstrained model, but the feasibility term
\nabla_{x_{t}}\log\mathbb{P}(x_{0}\in\mathcal{C}\mid x_{t})
is unknown. PPR approximates the effect of this feasibility with a projection-based correction that enforces the constraint on the denoised estimate and restores the correct noise level via a forward kernel reapplication (renoising). As the constraint is defined on clean variables
x\in\mathcal{S}
, we work in the data domain with the denoised estimate
x_{0}=d_{\theta}(x_{t})\approx\mathbb{E}[x_{0}\mid x_{t}]
.

Concretely, given the current iterate
x_{t}
, we compute the
projection

x_{t}^{*}=\Pi_{d}(x_{t}):=\arg\min_{x}\left[c(d_{\theta}(x))+\lambda_{t}\|x-x_{t}\|_{2}^{2}\right],

(11)

where
\lambda_{t}\geq 0
is a penalty parameter. The first term in the projection encourages
x_{t}^{*}
to be such that
d_{\theta}(x_{t}^{*})\in\mathcal{C}
, while the second encourages
x_{t}^{*}
to remain close to
x_{t}
. However, the projection usually does not preserve the marginals of
p_{t}
or
p_{t}^{\mathcal{C}}
. In particular,
x_{t}^{*}
might move to regions that are unlikely under the prior, or incorrect mass might be assigned to
x_{0}\in\mathcal{C}
that are strong attractors of
c(\cdot)
.

To compensate for these issues, we
renoise
by sampling a new
x_{t}
using the
unconstrained
forward diffusion kernel

p\left(x_{t}\mid x_{0}=d_{\theta}(x_{t}^{*})\right).

(12)

The idea is that by repeated projections and renoising, the mixing will approximate the constrained marginals
p_{t}^{\mathcal{C}}
.

As projecting the clean estimate of
x_{t}
into
\mathcal{C}
through the denoiser makes the procedure more expensive, other works project either
x_{t}
or the clean estimate
x_{0}
into
\mathcal{C}
, without the denoiser.
However, there are a number of advantages in using the denoiser: (i)
p(x_{0}|x_{t})
(unknown term in the constrained score Eq.
10
) is better approximated with
\mathcal{N}(\mathbb{E}(x_{0}|x_{t}),\sigma_{t}I)
than with
\mathcal{N}(x_{t},\sigma_{t}I)
, (ii) constraints are usually defined on the data manifold
\mathcal{S}
and
x_{t}\notin\mathcal{S}
almost surely, (iii) pushing through the denoiser helps maintain
x_{0}\in\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathcal{S}
, for example by propagating the local constraint information to the rest of the sample through its correlations, (iv) it makes it harder for
x_{t}
to move to pathological regions in
x_{t}
-space, as
d_{\theta}(x_{t})
in those regions will not be a sample from the prior, and (v) it solves the problem of
x_{t}
getting stuck in local minima of
c(x_{t})
.
Problems (ii), (iv), and (v) are common when projecting
c(x_{t})
with respect to
x_{t}
directly, while problem (iii) appears when projecting
c(x_{0})
with respect to
x_{0}
.

The projection and renoise together result in the
Predict-Project-Renoise
algorithm. First,
Predict
decreases time by one step, mapping
x_{t}
to
x_{t-1}
using the standard unconstrained predictor update. This step follows the reverse-time dynamics implied by the prior score and corresponds to the second term in Eq.
10
. Second,
Project
modifies the current iterate to encourage the feasibility of its denoised estimate. Concretely, it applies the nonlinear projection
\Pi_{d}
in Eq.
11
, producing
x_{t-1}^{*}
whose denoised estimate
d_{\theta}(x_{t-1}^{*})
better satisfies the constraint. This step targets the feasibility term in Eq.
7
, but it can move the iterate off the diffusion manifold at noise level
t-1
. Third,
Renoise
restores consistency with the diffusion noise level by reapplying the forward kernel at time
t-1
, sampling a new iterate so that feasibility is enforced through the denoised estimate while the iterate remains aligned with the intended marginal distribution at
t-1
. We repeat the
Project
and
Renoise
pair for
M
inner iterations to encourage mixing, and avoid strong attractors of
c(x)
. At the final step
t-1=0
, we omit renoising, and do only one projection.

Algorithm
2
summarizes the full Predict-Project-Renoise sampler. In practice, the computational cost is dominated by
Project
, since
\Pi_{d}
is solved with standard optimizers (e.g., gradient descent or L-BFGS
(
25
)
). The projection budget (e.g., number of optimization steps or higher-order updates) provides a direct tradeoff between compute and constraint satisfaction.

Related Work

Some research has addressed constrained sampling in diffusion models, which we summarize in Table

. First, we focus on projection-based constrained samplers and then discuss a related body of work on posterior sampling under noisy observation processes. To help understand how the works relate to each other, we annotate each projection-based method according to the space in which the projection is done. If done with respect to

x_{0}

-space
and use

-space
projection is done using

and “renoise” if any noise is added after the projection.

Limitations of existing projection methods.

Across these approaches, the following limitations typically appear:

1.

Projecting
x_{0}
.
Direct projection in data space can cause
x_{0}\notin\mathcal{S}
, such as discontinuities.

2.

Projecting
x_{t}
directly.
Operating directly on
c(x_{t})
may disrupt the marginal distributions
p_{t}(x_{t})
. Further, constraints may be ill-conditioned or undefined when applied to noisy variables.

3.

Renoising and marginal mismatch.
Renoising in
x_{t}
-space is claimed to alleviate disruptions to
p_{t}(x_{t})
, but in general renoising in
x_{t}
space does not recover the correct marginal. Similarly, if renoising in
x_{0}
-space with a transition different from the forward kernel, the constrained marginal is not recovered.

4.

Coverage.
Simply projecting either
x_{t}
or
x_{0}
can lead to projections collapsing onto the minimizers of
c(x_{t})
or
c(x_{0})
rather than faithfully representing the constrained distribution.

Posterior sampling under observation processes.

A related yet distinct body of work focuses on sampling from posteriors derived from observation processes. While constrained sampling enforces strict adherence to a feasible set, observation-based methods typically condition generation on external measurements, often assuming an underlying Gaussian likelihood
p(y|x)=\mathcal{N}(y\mid A(x),\sigma^{2})
, where
A
is the observation process and
y
is the observation value.
27
address linear observation processes, while other approaches extend to general nonlinear observations
(
40
;
5
;
32
)
. Additionally,
12
propose learning guidance weights to properly balance the likelihood term. Theoretically, hard constraints can be framed as a special case of inverse problems by modeling the observation likelihood as a Dirac delta distribution
\mathcal{N}(y\mid A(x),\sigma^{2})\xrightarrow{\sigma\to 0}\delta(y-A(x))
. However, applying standard posterior sampling algorithms in this regime presents challenges, as these methods are generally designed for more informative likelihoods rather than the narrow distributions required for strict constraint satisfaction.
7
provide a comprehensive survey of this field, and
45
propose a unified framework for training-free guidance. Related to PPR within this approach is
38
(
x_{0}
-space
with renoising). They solve the projection and renoise for a subset of the diffusion steps. We elaborate on this approach in Appendix
A
.

Experiments

We evaluate both the constraint violation in generated samples and the fidelity of the resulting constrained distribution, or proxies thereof. We consider three experimental settings:
Data2D
,
KS
, and
Weather
.
Data2D
comprises 2D problems where the ground truth constrained distribution can be approximated.
KS
is based on the 1D Kuramoto-Sivashinsky equation
(
22
)
and assesses our method and the baselines in a high-dimensional setting. Finally,
Weather
uses
Appa
, a global latent weather model
(
3
)
, to test PPR in a large-scale, realistic setting. In both
Data2D
and
KS
, we generate the constrained distribution by sampling the diffusion prior
p_{0}
instead of the true data distribution

. This disentangles the error from the constrained sampling process and the error from the data density approximation, as now the ground truth data density is the diffusion prior. We used
\lambda_{t}=0
for
KS
and
Appa
. For
Data2D
we used
\lambda_{t}=\frac{t^{2}}{4\sigma_{t}^{2}+4}
.

PPR uses
64
diffusion steps,
M=2
projection + renoising steps,
N=0

Correct
steps, and
8
iterative steps inside of
\Pi_{d}
. To account for the cost of projecting through the denoiser, all baselines use an increased computational budget, with between
64
and
1024
diffusion steps and
64

Ablations.

In Appendix
E
, we ablate
PPR
on
Data2D
and
KS
over the number of renoising steps
M
, unconstrained
Correct
steps
N
, and iterative projection steps. Across both datasets, renoising at least one step is needed for sample and distribution quality. Without renoising, the projection can distort the marginals and yield samples
x_{0}\notin\mathcal{S}
. Adding renoising improves distribution metrics, continuity, and reduces constraint violation. Increasing
M
offers a clear compute-quality tradeoff, with the largest gains for the harder
KS
multi-time sine constraint. In contrast, unconstrained
Correct
provides at most a small benefit at
N=1
and quickly plateaus or degrades performance as
N
grows, often increasing constraint violation. Additionally,
Correct
involves model calls, while renoising simply samples Gaussian noise. Lastly, increasing the number of iterative projection steps reduces violation but saturates quickly in low dimensions and for easier constraints. Overall, these results support projection + renoising as an effective mechanism for reliable constrained sampling.

Data2D.

In
Data2D
, we first train diffusion models on two distinct 2D distributions, a uniform checkerboard (Fig.
3
, top), and a mixture of Gaussians, which serve as priors. We then generate
12
random functions
f(x)
by sampling cosine series. For each function, we define the constraint
c(x)=1-e^{-f(x)^{2}}
and approximate the ground-truth (GT) constrained distribution by rejection sampling from the diffusion model prior with a small tolerance
\epsilon
, that is,
\mathrm{GT}=\{x\in\mathcal{S}\,:\,c(x)\leq\epsilon\}
. The accepted samples are then refined with a few deterministic gradient steps to better satisfy the constraint. This results in
12
constraints for each of the
2
distributions, giving
24
total experimental settings. For each of these, we generated ensembles with PPR and the baselines to approximate the GT constrained distribution. More details on the data in Appendix
C
.

To assess the quality of the resulting constrained distributions, we use two metrics: the Wasserstein distance
(
44
)
, and the
k
-nearest-neighbor (
k
-NN) cross-edge rate
(
34
)
. The Wasserstein distance computes the optimal transport cost of moving a point cloud of samples into the ground truth cloud. The
k
-NN cross-edge rate constructs a graph on the union of GT and model samples, and counts the number of cross edges, i.e., edges that connect a GT sample to a model sample. The cross-edge rate (cross edges divided by total edges) is expected to be
0.5
when the two distributions match. In practice, we generate point clouds from each distribution and repeat the test over multiple random subsets to reduce variance. The results are averaged across all prior distributions and all
12

In the Kuramoto-Sivashinsky (

\mathbb{R}^{128\times 128}

Evaluating the quality of approximations of conditional distributions for high-dimensional systems is an open problem
(
24
)
, so we rely on proxy quantities. To test the distribution of samples, we frame perfect nonlinear observations as constraints. We use the constraint

32

4

The skill measures how far the ensemble mean is from the ground truth, that is, the bias of the ensemble. The spread measures the deviation of the ensemble from said mean, i.e., how close it is to the delta. Lastly, the continuous-ranked-probability-score (CRPS) penalizes both bias and misrepresentation of uncertainty (too much or too little spread). See Appendix
B
for their definitions. Eq.
11
was solved using L-BFGS
(
25
)
.

x_{0}

Even with the increased computational budget afforded to the baselines, PPR outperforms them.

6
Conclusion

To address the problem of constrained sampling in score-based models, we defined a constrained forward process with its corresponding reverse process and introduced PPR as a way to sample following that reverse process. Over a series of experiments, we showed that PPR achieves lower constraint violation and closer approximation to the constrained distribution compared to the state of the art.
Additionally, it offers a meaningful way of trading compute for sample and constrained distribution quality.

Limitations.

While PPR offers a meaningful way of trading computation for approximation quality, the iterative nonlinear projection problem through the denoiser is expensive. Further, we rely on approximations to sample the marginals
p_{t}^{\mathcal{C}}
and there is no guarantee of unbiased sampling from the true constrained distribution. Lastly, PPR relies on the differentiability of the constraint function
c(x)
to propagate information from
x_{0}
to
x_{t}
. This dependency precludes the direct application of the framework to non-differentiable, discrete, or black-box constraints without further work.

Future work.

The clearest direction is to reduce the computational cost of PPR. A possible way of accomplishing that is to only project at a subset of diffusion steps. While we preliminarily found that it gives poorer constraint satisfaction and constrained distribution approximation, it is an additional lever to be tuned by the user. Further, extending beyond equality constraints to mixed and inequality constraints is a promising direction. Inequality constraints could be handled by a projection with momentum or Langevin projection that stops once inside
\mathcal{C}
. Additionally, extending the framework to explicitly handle discrete constraints and domains such as inverse folding in constrained protein design remains open for further research. Finally, while we presented a formulation for the constrained forward process, establishing theoretical bounds for the approximation of the constrained marginals
p_{t}^{\mathcal{C}}
via the proposed projection-renoising would strengthen the foundation of the method.

Operator splitting.

A useful analogy for the

Using the constrained score decomposition in Eq.
10

\displaystyle F_{t}(x)

\displaystyle\coloneqq f(x,t)-g^{2}(t)\nabla_{x}\log p_{t}(x),

(13)

\displaystyle G_{t}(x)

\displaystyle\coloneqq-g^{2}(t)\nabla_{x}\log\mathbb{P}\!\left(x_{0}\in\mathcal{C}\mid x_{t}=x\right),

(14)

\dot{x}_{t}=F_{t}(x_{t})+G_{t}(x_{t})

Let
\Phi_{F,t}(h)

\Phi_{F,t}

\Phi_{G,t}

The feasibility field
G_{t}

\Pi_{d}(x_{t})=\arg\min_{x}\Big[c\big(d_{\theta}(x)\big)+\lambda_{t}\|x-x_{t}\|_{2}^{2}\Big],

When

While this splitting viewpoint explains why repeated feasibility updates help, it does not by itself yield correct hard constraint sampling: projection steps can distort the marginal at noise level

PPR therefore interleaves projection with

This refresh restores the intended noise level while keeping feasibility enforced through

p_{t}^{\mathcal{C}}
(Eq.

Appendix B
Metrics

We use the same metrics as in
3
, and partially reproduce their text for the definitions here. The evaluation is performed using WeatherBench2
(
30
)
for
Appa
data. For the
KS
experiments, the metrics are the same, but with one spatial dimension removed.

Skill.

Skill is computed as the root mean square error of the posterior mean of an ensemble compared to the ground-truth trajectory. For
K
ensembles each consisting of
M
predicted states
\hat{x}
of resolution
H\times W
, ground truth
x
, the skill of a single time step is computed as

\text{Skill}=\sqrt{\dfrac{1}{KHW}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{i=1}^{H}\sum_{j=1}^{W}\left(x_{i,j}^{k}-\dfrac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\hat{x}_{i,j}^{k,m}\right)^{2}}.

Spread.

Ensemble spread is computed as the square root of the ensemble variance
(
11
)
:

\text{Spread}=\sqrt{\dfrac{1}{KHW}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{i=1}^{H}\sum_{j=1}^{W}\dfrac{1}{M-1}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\left(\hat{x}_{i,j}^{k,m}-\dfrac{1}{M}\sum_{n=1}^{M}\hat{x}_{i,j}^{k,n}\right)^{2}}.

Spread-skill ratio.

A well-calibrated forecast should have a (corrected for ensemble size) spread-skill ratio of 1, which is a necessary but not sufficient condition. Ratios below one indicate overconfident estimations. The correct ratio is defined as

\text{Ratio}=\sqrt{\dfrac{M+1}{M}}\dfrac{\text{Spread}}{\text{Skill}}.

Continuous ranked probability score (CRPS).

The CRPS
(
13
)
is defined as

\text{CRPS}=\dfrac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left(\dfrac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}||\hat{x}^{k,m}-x^{k}||_{L_{1}}-\dfrac{1}{2M(M-1)}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\sum_{n=1}^{M}||\hat{x}^{k,m}-\hat{x}^{k,n}||_{L_{1}}\right).

The first term penalizes the average divergence from the ground truth, while the second term encourages spread. Therefore, the CRPS is lowest when the distribution of the ensemble matches the ground-truth distribution.

A word on the Wasserstein distance.

The Wasserstein distance, approximated through the Sinkhorn algorithm
(
35
)
, can be an imperfect measure of ‘structural similarity’ between two point clouds because it only measures the minimal transport cost needed to move mass from one distribution to the other, not whether the source cloud reproduces the target’s geometry in a meaningful way. In particular, a cloud that is more diffuse and broadly spread can sometimes achieve a comparable or lower transport cost than a cloud that matches the target’s structure but has mass misplaced. For instance, if the target consists of two thin filaments, a point cloud drawn from a Gaussian mixture that forms a hazy envelope around both filaments may incur modest transport because every target point can be reached from nearby diffuse mass, even though the cloud does not concentrate on the filaments themselves. Meanwhile, a cloud that lies almost exactly on the filaments but has a small imbalance in how much mass sits on each filament can be penalized more, because correcting that imbalance requires transporting mass along longer paths between filaments. 7
shows the distance
d
and transport maps computed on subsampled and zoomed constrained distributions from
Data2D
, one obtained through DPS and one through projection.

C.1
Data2D

We generated i.i.d. samples in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
from two parametric distributions: a uniform checkerboard distribution and a “banana” Gaussian mixture. Unless otherwise stated, reported samples are standardized by using an empirically estimated mean
\mu\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
and standard deviation
\sigma\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
, i.e.,

\tilde{x}\;=\;\frac{(x-\mu)}{\sigma}.

The normalization statistics are estimated from Monte Carlo samples drawn from the
non-standardized
distribution.

Checkerboard.

Fix an integer grid size
m
and a jitter scale
\eta\geq 0
. We draw
i\sim\mathrm{Unif}\{0,\dots,m-1\}
and then draw
j
uniformly from
\{0,\dots,m-1\}
subject to the parity constraint
(i+j)\bmod 2=0
(equivalently, points occupy alternating squares). Given
(i,j)
, we draw
u_{1},u_{2}\sim\mathcal{U}(0,1)
independently and set

x\;=\;\begin{bmatrix}i+u_{1}\\
j+u_{2}\end{bmatrix}.

If
\eta>0
, we add isotropic Gaussian jitter,
x\leftarrow x+\eta\,\xi
with
\xi\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{2})
.

Banana Gaussian mixture model (Banana-GMM).

Let
\{w_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}
be nonnegative mixture weights with
\sum_{k}w_{k}=1
, and let
\mu_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
and
s_{k}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2}
be component means and diagonal standard deviations. We first sample a component index
k\sim\mathrm{Categorical}(w_{1},\dots,w_{K})
and then draw a Gaussian sample

z\sim\mathcal{N}\!\bigl(\mu_{k},\;\mathrm{diag}(s_{k}^{2})\bigr),\qquad z=\begin{bmatrix}z_{1}\\
z_{2}\end{bmatrix}.

We then apply a “banana” shear in the second coordinate,

x_{1}=z_{1},\qquad x_{2}=z_{2}+b\bigl(z_{1}^{2}-\mathbb{E}[z_{1}^{2}]\bigr),

where
b\in\mathbb{R}
controls the curvature and the centering term
\mathbb{E}[z_{1}^{2}]
is computed analytically under the mixture,

\mathbb{E}[z_{1}^{2}]\;=\;\sum_{k=1}^{K}w_{k}\bigl((\mu_{k})_{1}^{2}+(s_{k})_{1}^{2}\bigr).

GRF Constraints.

We generated a collection of
12
independent scalar constraint functions on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
as random draws from an approximate Gaussian Random Field (GRF)
(
1
)
with an RBF kernel, using random Fourier features. All constraints are defined by the same hyperparameters. Fix the number of features
M
(here
M=64
), lengthscale
\ell>0
(here

\omega_{m}\sim\mathcal{N}\!\left(0,\ell^{-2}I\right),\qquad\phi_{m}\sim\mathcal{U}(0,2\pi),\qquad m=1,\dots,M,

independently, and we sample coefficients

a_{m}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1),\qquad m=1,\dots,M.

We define the random feature map for
x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
as

\varphi_{m}(x)\;=\;\cos(\omega_{m}^{\top}x+\phi_{m}),

and the corresponding random function

f(x)\;=\;\sqrt{\frac{2\sigma_{f}^{2}}{M}}\sum_{m=1}^{M}a_{m}\,\varphi_{m}(x)\;+\;\beta,

where the bias term is sampled as

\beta\sim\mathcal{N}\!\left(0,\;\sigma_{b}^{2}\,\sigma_{f}^{2}\right),

with
\sigma_{b}=0.05
. In the limit
M\to\infty

\ell

\sigma_{f}^{2}
. Each constraint is obtained by smoothing

We formed the
12

by sampling

independently for each
i

\epsilon=10^{-2}
for the creation of the constrained distributions.

M
, the number of unconstrained
Correct
steps
N
from the PC algorithm (Algorithm
1
), and the number of optimization iterations used inside the projection
\Pi_{d}
(reported as
Proj. steps
). Tables
3
(
Data2D
) and
4
(
KS
) list all settings.

Across both datasets, renoising has the clearest and most consistent impact. Without renoising, projecting onto
\mathcal{C}
can break the model marginals and introduce artifacts (including reduced continuity), which shows up as worse distribution-proxy metrics and often increased constraint violation. Adding renoising largely mitigates this mismatch, improving distributional fidelity while typically also reducing violation. This makes
M
a reliable compute-quality knob, with larger benefits when the constraint is harder.

Increasing iterative projection steps in
\Pi_{d}
(
Proj. steps
) generally improves feasibility by producing a better local solution for
\Pi_{d}
, which can also improve distribution metrics and continuity, but with diminishing returns once the optimizer reaches local minima. Finally, increasing the number of unconstrained
Correct
steps
N
is not a substitute for
ConstrainedCorrect
: while
N=1
can stabilize sampling, larger
N
tends to move samples away from feasibility, increasing violation and degrading downstream metrics, whereas projection+renoising directly targets feasibility while preserving marginals.

Data2D
.

On
Data2D
, we use batch size
256^{2}
and energy tolerance
\epsilon=0.01
. Due to memory limits, we estimate Wasserstein distance and the
k
-NN cross-edge rate using
4096
samples drawn without replacement from each cloud. We repeat this subsampling
10
times, and report the mean. In this low-dimensional setting, a single
Correct
step can improve both marginals and the constrained distribution, but additional
Correct
steps quickly plateau and can increase constraint violation. At least one renoising step is needed for distributional fidelity, while additional renoising has limited impact on Wasserstein and cross-edge metrics. Increasing
Proj. steps
reduces constraint violation, but the benefits saturate quickly, consistent with
\Pi_{d}
typically reaching a good local minimum in few iterations.

KS
.

On
KS
, in addition to the main-text constraint

c(u_{0})=\sum_{i}\|\sin(u_{0}^{i})-\sin(u^{i})\|^{2}
with
i\in\{0,64,127\}
, we also consider the single-time sine constraint

c(u_{0})=\|\sin(u_{0}^{0})-\sin(u^{0})\|^{2}
and the linear observation

c(u_{0})=\|u_{0}^{0}-u^{0}\|^{2}
, which are substantially less challenging. The qualitative trends above are amplified by constraint difficulty: renoising yields the strongest gains for the multi-time sine constraint, providing a meaningful compute-quality tradeoff. Increasing
Proj. steps
further reduces violation with the most visible improvements on harder constraints. Varying
N
shows that
N=1
can stabilize sampling, but larger
N
typically degrades performance by pulling samples away from feasibility. Overall, these results support
ConstrainedCorrect
as the most effective mechanism for improving constrained sampling on
KS
, and explain why increasing
M
is generally more beneficial than increasing
N
.
